Cumulative effects of excess weight, sleep quality, and depressive and anxiety symptoms in Chinese adolescents
Authors: Youguo Tan, Hui Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLittle is known about the association of the cumulative effects of excess weight with depressive and anxiety symptoms in Chinese adolescents and the role of sleep quality in the above-mentioned association. This study aimed to assess this issue.MethodsA cohort study was conducted annually during 2021 (wave 1), 2022 (wave 2) and 2023 (wave 3). The cumulative effects of excess weight, based on body mass index, from wave 1 to wave 3 were calculated using the trapezoid rule. We evaluated sleep quality, depressive symptoms, and anxiety symptoms based on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire, and 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Questionnaire in wave 3, respectively. We performed sensitivity analyses using overweight/obesity-years to define the cumulative effects of excess weight.ResultsThis study included 39,442 participants (male: 51.6%; mean age: 13.7 years) without depressive and anxiety symptoms in wave 1 and 2. Logistic models with restricted cubic splines showed a significant linear association between the cumulative effect of excess weight and depressive symptoms. We noted a mediating role of sleep quality in the above-mentioned association (percentage of mediation = 9.7%; P < 0.001). Compared with participants without the incident cumulative effect of excess weight and sleep disturbance, those with the above-mentioned two conditions were more likely to have anxiety symptoms (odds ratio = 5.94; P < 0.001). The sensitivity analyses yielded similar results.ConclusionOur results indicate that the cumulative effect of excess weight may directly affect depressive symptoms, and indirectly affect anxiety symptoms through sleep quality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04697-x.Keywords: Excess weight, Sleep quality, Depressive symptoms, Anxiety symptoms

Introduction

Health in adolescents has received increasing attention because of its effects on their lifelong well-being, the health of their offspring, and the future of global development. Health includes physical and mental health. Excess weight and depressive/anxiety symptoms negatively impact the physical and mental health of adolescents, respectively. Globally, it is estimated that approximately 20%, 21%, and 25% of adolescents are affected by excess weight, depressive symptoms, and anxiety symptoms, respectively [1–3]. Excess weight in adolescents is related to immediate poor cardiovascular effects (e.g. elevated blood pressure) and long-term adverse outcomes in adulthood [4]. Depressive and anxiety symptoms in adolescents increase the risk of psychiatric disorders and impairments in social functioning [5].

China has a large adolescent population [6]. In Chinese adolescents, excess weight and depressive/anxiety symptoms are major public health issues [6]. The prevalence of excess weight, depressive and anxiety symptoms in Chinese adolescents is approximately 19.6%, 28.2%, and 29.8%, respectively [7–9]. Some publications report that excess weight causes the depressive/anxiety symptoms through various potential mechanisms (e.g. stigma) [10]. Epidemiologically, previous studies have showed the association of excess weight with depressive/anxiety symptoms in adolescents in developed countries [11, 12]. A cross-sectional study in Chinese adolescents analyze the association of weight with mental health [13]. Based on the aforementioned evidence, cohort studies are needed to further establish causal relationships. However, relevant evidence based on cohort studies with large samples in Chinese adolescents is limited. Considering the concurrent high prevalence of excess weight and depressive/anxiety symptoms in China, it is necessary to explore the relationships among them based on cohort studies to provide evidence for the establishment of national prevention and control strategies [7–9]. In addition, given that weight status is constantly changing, this study considered the cumulative effects of excess weight as the exposure of interest, to capture the effects of long-term exposure. Furthermore, despite previous studies, there is still a paucity of knowledge about the mediators of the association between obesity and depressive symptoms, with confirmation only available for the psychological mediators [14]. Sleep disorders are prevalent among adolescents [15]. Previous studies have indicated that excess weight is related to sleep quality, and sleep quality has been proven to contribute to depressive/anxiety symptoms [16–18]. Taken together, this cohort study aimed to assess the association of the cumulative effects of excess weight with depressive and anxiety symptoms and the role of sleep quality in the above-mentioned association in Chinese adolescents.

Materials and methods

Study population

A school-based open cohort study was initiated in 2020 in the city of Zigong, Sichuan province, China [19]. In 2020, the survey was conducted in only a subset of areas of Zigong. Three subsequent survey waves were conducted in all areas of the city at one-year intervals, i.e. 2021 (wave 1), 2022 (wave 2), and 2023 (wave 3). Students were recruited from all middle and high schools in the surveyed areas of Zigong. The participants completed an electronic questionnaire in the schools’ computer rooms. The Ethics Committee of Zigong Mental Health Center approved the study [No. 2020-8-01] [19, 20]. The study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The trained investigators and head teachers of the school were responsible for introducing the study details [20]. The participants and their guardians were comprehensively informed about the purpose, procedures, measurements, potential risks, and benefits of the research before the survey [20]. The participants and their guardians were aware that they had the right to refuse or withdraw from participation at any time [20]. Then, informed consent was obtained from the participants and their guardians [19, 20].

The study included only the survey waves from wave 1 to wave 3 as they covered all areas of Zigong. To assess the association of the cumulative effects of excess weight with depressive and anxiety symptoms and the role of sleep quality in the above-mentioned association, we collected sex, age, 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) scores, 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Questionnaire (GAD-7) scores, height, and weight from wave 1 to wave 3, sleep quality in wave 3, and covariates in wave 1.

Initially, 47,415 adolescents were included, for whom data were available on sex, age, PHQ-9, and GAD-7 scores. Additionally, these adolescents had taken part in all three survey waves and had both PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores less than 10 in wave 1 and wave 2. We excluded 499 adolescents who were younger than 11 years or older than 18 years in any wave from 1 to 3. We also excluded 4,040 adolescents for whom there had not been a 1-year interval between any two consecutive survey waves, and 3,434 adolescents without data on height, weight, or sleep quality. Finally, 39,442 adolescents were included in this study. The inclusion and exclusion of participants and flowchart of current study (Figure S1) were provided in Supplementary Material.

Cumulative effects of excess weight

Weight and height were self-reported by participants. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated [BMI=weight (kg) /height (m)2]. The cumulative effects of excess weight from wave 1 to wave 3 were evaluated using the trapezoid rule, as described in a previous study [21]. In detail, under the trapezoid rule, the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated as the mean BMI value from two consecutive survey waves multiplied by the corresponding time interval. The cumulative effects of excess weight were then calculated as the difference between the AUC for personal actual BMI and the AUC for the corresponding sex–age-specific overweight thresholds [22]. By using the trapezoid rule, the corresponding calculation formula is as follows: ; n is the number of measurements; i represents the i-th measurement; BMIi is the BMI value at the i-th measurement; timei is the time value at the i-th measurement; thresholdsi are the corresponding sex–age-specific overweight thresholds at the i-th measurement. In cases where the difference was below 0, the cumulative effects of excess weight were given a value of 0. In cases where the difference was greater than or equal to 0, the cumulative effects of excess weight were the actual calculated value. The effects calculated in this way are presented in Fig. 1a.

We also calculated the cumulative effects of excess weight based on overweight/obesity-years, as reported in a previous study [23]. In detail, if at two consecutive survey waves both the corresponding BMI values were greater than or equal to the corresponding sex–age-specific overweight thresholds, the cumulative effects of excess weight were calculated as the BMI at the previous wave minus the corresponding sex–age-specific overweight threshold plus 0.1 (to assess the cumulative effects when participants’ BMI was equal to the threshold), multiplied by the corresponding time interval [22, 23]. By using the overweight/obesity-years, the corresponding calculation formula is as follows: ; n is the number of measurements; i represents the i-th measurement; BMIi is the BMI value at the i-th measurement; timei is the time value at the i-th measurement; thresholdsi are the corresponding sex–age-specific overweight thresholds at the i-th measurement. In all other cases, the cumulative effects of excess weight were given a value of 0. The effects calculated in this way are presented in Fig. 1b.

Sleep quality in wave 3

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was used to assess sleep quality in wave 3. The PSQI is calculated based on a self-reported questionnaire with 19 items classified into seven components [24]. Each component’s score ranges from 0 to 3, yielding a global PSQI score from 0 to 21. Higher global PSQI scores suggest worse sleep quality. Sleep disturbance is defined as PSQI score > 5 [24]. Previous studies assessed the validity of PSQI in Chinese adolescents and supported the use of this instrument [25]. The PSQI showed good internal reliability in the current study (Cronbach’s α = 0.81) [26].

Outcome assessment in wave 3

The PHQ-9 and GAD-7 were used to assess depressive and anxiety symptoms, respectively, in wave 3. The PHQ-9 and GAD-7 were described in detail in previous studies [27, 28]. The PHQ-9 and GAD-7 included nine and seven self-report items, respectively. For each item of both PHQ-9 and GAD-7, responses are recorded on a 4-point scale: 0 (not at all), 1 (several days), 2 (more than half the days), and 3 (nearly every day). In this study, for both depressive and anxiety symptoms, their presence was defined as a total score of ≥ 10 [27, 28]. Previous studies showed that the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 were the valid self-reported measures in Chinese adolescents [29, 30]. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 in the current study was 0.78 and 0.84, respectively, indicating satisfactory internal consistency [26].

Covariates assessment in wave 1

Self-rated information on the covariates in wave 1 was collected, including sex (male/female), age, urban residence, single child, left-behind child, nuclear family type, school administrative region (Ziliujing and Gaoxing district, Gongjing district, Da’an district, Yantan district, Rong county, or Fushun county), parental education and occupation, smoking status, alcohol drinker, and video gaming time [19]. Father education (mother education) was defined as below high school, and high school or above. Father occupation (mother occupation) was defined as worker, farmer, public institution employee, company employee/driver/salesclerk/waiter/self-employed, and others. Smoking status was defined as never smoking (lifetime smoking exposure less than 100 cigarettes), previous smokers (lifetime smoking exposure more than 100 cigarettes but none in the past 28 days), and current smokers (lifetime smoking exposure more than 100 cigarettes and smoking in the past 28 days). Alcohol drinker was defined as weekly alcohol consumption more than 30 g (900mL beer or 300mL wine or 30mL of Chinese spirits) for at least one year. The average weekly video game time in the past year was calculated as (average weekday gaming time × 5) + (average weekend gaming time × 2).

Statistical analysis

The trapezoid rule is often used to assess the cumulative effect of time-dependent variables, and is simple to calculate and easy to understand [21]. Additionally, overweight/obesity-years were specifically designed to assess the cumulative effect of excess weight [23]. This indicator has received attention and been put into practical use in recent research. Consequently, those two methods were used to assess cumulative effects in this study.

After adjustment for the baseline variables of sex, age, urban residence, school administrative region, single child, left-behind child, nuclear family type, sleep quality, parental education and occupation, smoking status, alcohol drinker, and video gaming time, logistic models with restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to assess the dose–response association of the cumulative effects of excess weight (based on the trapezoid rule; continuous variable) with depressive and anxiety symptoms. We used RCS with four knots to flexibly model the above-mentioned association, as recommended in a previous study [31]. Wald test was used to test for non-linearity [32].

If the logistic models with RCS suggested a linear association, we further performed mediation analyses to investigate the mediating role of sleep quality (continuous variable) in the association between the cumulative effects of excess weight (based on the trapezoid rule; continuous variable) and anxiety and depressive symptoms (dichotomous variable). The mediating effect models consisted of two sub-models: a linear regression model (model 1) and a logistic regression model (model 2). Model 1 evaluated the association of the cumulative effects of excess weight (based on the trapezoid rule) with sleep quality. Model 2 assessed the impact of the cumulative effects of excess weight (based on the trapezoid rule) and sleep quality on outcomes. Subsequently, the proportion of mediation was obtained based on the above-mentioned two models. A series of sensitivity analyses were conducted as follows: (1) using the cumulative effects of excess weight (based on overweight/obesity-years; continuous variable) and sleep quality (continuous variable) to repeat the above-mentioned analyses; (2) using the incident cumulative effects of excess weight (based on the trapezoid rule; dichotomous variable) and sleep disturbance (dichotomous variable); (3) using the incident cumulative effects of excess weight (based on overweight/obesity-years; dichotomous variable) and sleep disturbance (dichotomous variable).

If the logistic models with RCS suggested the lack of a dose–response association, we assessed the incident cumulative effects of excess weight (dichotomous variable) on sleep disturbance. Then, we investigated the association of the combined effect of the incident cumulative effects of excess weight plus sleep disturbance with anxiety and depressive symptoms. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using overweight/obesity-years to define the cumulative effects of excess weight or excluding the participants with sleep disturbance (PSQI score > 5) in wave 1.

R software version 4.5.1 was used to perform the statistical analysis. We performed the dose-response association and mediating effect models using “rms” and “mediation” R packages, respectively. Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Population characteristics

This study included 39,442 participants who were free of depressive and anxiety symptoms in wave 1 and 2. Table 1 presents the characteristics of the participants. Males accounted for 51.6% of all participants, and the mean ages on wave 1 was 13.7 years. Mean cumulative effect of excess weight based on overweight/obesity-year and trapezoid rule was 0.5 and 0.5 kg/m2×year, respectively. The corresponding incidence of cumulative effect was 8.9% and 9.2%. Mean Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index scores in wave 3 were 3.2, and the corresponding prevalence of sleep disturbance was 19.0%. The incidence of depressive and anxiety symptoms was 8.6% and 3.0%, respectively.

Dose–response association

Figure 2 depicts the dose–response relationship between the cumulative effect of excess weight and depressive and anxiety symptoms. Regardless of depressive or anxiety symptoms, the four nodes of RCS correspond to 0.004, 0.301, 3.989, and 15.325 kg/m2×year of cumulative excess weight using the trapezoid rule while 0.101, 0.434, 4.187, and 15.631 kg/m2×year using overweight/obesity-years. A significant linear association between the cumulative effect of excess weight (based on the trapezoid rule) and depressive symptoms was identified. In contrast, no dose–response association between the cumulative effect of excess weight (based on the trapezoid rule) and anxiety symptoms was detected. Sensitivity analyses using the cumulative effect of excess weight based on overweight/obesity-years yielded similar results.

Mediating effect analyses

Table 2 summarizes the mediating effect of PSQI scores on the association of the cumulative effect of excess weight with depressive symptoms. After adjustment for the covariates, we noted a mediating role of sleep quality in the association of the cumulative effect of excess weight (based on the trapezoid rule) with depressive symptoms (percentage of mediation = 9.7%; P < 0.001). Similar results were obtained in sensitivity analyses using the cumulative effect of excess weight based on overweight/obesity-years (continuous variable), the trapezoid rule (dichotomous variable), and overweight/obesity-years (dichotomous variable) (Tables S1–S2).

Combined effect analyses

Table S3 shows the association of the incident cumulative effect of excess weight with sleep disturbance. Compared with participants without the cumulative effect of excess weight (based on the trapezoid rule), participants with this cumulative effect were more likely to have sleep disturbance (odds ratio = 1.12; P = 0.008 ). Table 3 reports the combined effect of the incident cumulative effect of excess weight and sleep disturbance on anxiety symptoms. The incidence of anxiety symptoms was higher in the group with the incident cumulative effect of excess weight (based on the trapezoid rule) and sleep disturbance than in all three other groups (10.3% vs. 1.9%, 7.7%, and 1.7%). Compared with participants without the incident cumulative effect of excess weight (based on the trapezoid rule) and sleep disturbance, those with both conditions were more likely to have anxiety symptoms (odds ratio = 5.94; P < 0.001). We obtained similar results when we conducted sensitivity analyses using the cumulative effect of excess weight based on overweight/obesity-years (Table S3 and Table S4). Discussion

Principal findings

This study found a significant linear association between the cumulative effect of excess weight and depressive symptoms, and a mediating role of sleep quality in the above-mentioned association. Compared with participants without the incident cumulative effect of excess weight and sleep disturbance, those with both conditions were more likely to have anxiety symptoms.

Comparison with other studies on prevalence and incidence

A meta-analysis report that the prevalence of sleep disturbances in Chinese adolescents is 26%, which is slightly higher than our results (19%) [33]. Additionally, evidence regarding the incidence of depressive and anxiety symptoms is limited. A cohort study, which is also conducted in Sichuan Province, China, shows that the incidence of adolescent depressive symptoms is 9.4% during the approximately 1.5-year follow-up, which is similar to our findings [34]. Both the existing evidence and our findings indicate that the close attention should be paid to sleep disorders, depressive and anxiety symptoms in Chinese adolescents.

Interpretation of dose–response association analyses

This study found a significant linear dose–response association between the cumulative effect of excess weight and depressive symptoms. Epidemiologically, a systematic review also shows that persistent and/or increasing adiposity from childhood to adulthood is related to depressive symptoms [35]. Mechanistically, the association between the cumulative effect of excess weight and depressive symptoms has been explained by pathophysiological mechanisms (e.g. inflammatory responses), behavioral mechanisms (e.g. repeated dieting), cognitive mechanisms (e.g. body image dissatisfaction), and social mechanisms (e.g. stigma) [10].

On the contrary, our study found no dose–response association between the cumulative effect of excess weight and anxiety symptoms. This can be explained by the following reasons. First, the GAD-7 primarily focuses on generalized anxiety symptoms and may not capture other subtypes of anxiety disorders [28]. Second, previous studies have also showed no association of excess weight with anxiety symptoms in adolescents, which indicate that excess weight may be an upstream factor in the causal chain [12].

Interpretation of mediating and combined effect analyses

Our study builds on established knowledge by showing that the association between the cumulative effect of excess weight and depressive symptoms is partly mediated by sleep disturbance. Mechanistically, obesity promotes sleep disorders via inflammation, hormonal dysregulation, and autonomic dysfunction [16]. Furthermore, sleep disorders may predispose individuals to depressive symptoms via inflammation, biochemical pathways, genetic correlations, and circadian rhythm [17]. Additionally, the percentage of mediation is 9.7% (Table 2), suggesting that cumulative effect of excess weight may directly affect depressive symptoms and indirectly affect this condition through sleep quality. The indirect effect plays a partial but not primary role.

We further detected that the cumulative effect of excess weight was related to sleep disturbance and those with both conditions were more likely to have anxiety symptoms, indicating that the cumulative effect of excess weight indirectly affects anxiety symptoms through sleep disorders. Poor sleep quality is associated with anxiety symptoms among adults with extreme obesity, which partly supports our findings [36]. In addition, the evidence about obesity-induced sleep disorders has been reported previously [16]. Sleep disruption and anxiety symptoms share some common genetic background and neurocircuitry, and sleep disruption can cause anxiety symptoms though altering epigenetic regulation and elevating inflammation levels [37].

Public health implications

This study has significant public health implications. On the one hand, preventing the further spread of excess weight in adolescents and implementing weight loss interventions for those with excess weight are effective strategies for the prevention and control of depressive symptoms. On the other hand, for the prevention and control of anxiety symptoms, it may be important to focus on and intervene downstream factor in the causal chain (e.g. sleep quality).

Strengths and limitations of study

The key strengths of this study include the large sample size and cohort study design. However, there are some limitations. First, weight and height were self-reported, which may have introduced bias. However, a previous study revealed that self-reported and objectively measured weight and height were strongly correlated [38]. Additionally, students undergo health checks every year in Zigong, and most of them are aware of their height and weight. Second, this study was only conducted in one single city in China. Our results should be generalized cautiously. Third, some unconsidered confounders (e.g. academic stress and fruit and vegetable intake) may have affected our results. For example, fruit and vegetable intake is associated with lower risks of excess weight and depressive symptoms [39, 40]. However, academic stress is related to increased risks of excess weight and depressive symptoms [41, 42]. The influence of confounders on our findings is complex, and further research may be needed to confirm our results. Fourth, to assess the cumulative effects of excess weight, we used the BMI from wave 1 to wave 3. Giving the chronological order, we assessed sleep quality and depressive and anxiety symptoms in wave 3. Consequently, when we conducted the mediating effect models, the temporal sequence between the mediator (sleep quality) and outcome (depressive symptoms) was not well established. The mediation analysis in this study is exploratory in nature. While epidemiological evidence and mechanistic studies demonstrate that sleep disturbances contribute to the depressive symptoms, future research is still needed to confirm our findings [17].

Conclusion

In conclusion, the cumulative effect of excess weight may directly affect depressive symptoms, and indirectly affect anxiety symptoms through sleep quality. This study highlights the importance of maintaining healthy weight and good sleep quality over a long period of time to prevent mental disorders in adolescents.
