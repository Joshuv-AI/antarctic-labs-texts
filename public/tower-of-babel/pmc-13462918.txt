Making microbes matter: evidence for the use of digital storytelling in antimicrobial resistance public engagement
Authors: Becky McCall, Andrew Hayward, Michael Lang, Michael Wilson, Kate Grailey, Laura Shallcross
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a major global health threat, yet public engagement remains limited because AMR is often perceived as distant and abstract. Digital storytelling (DST) may offer a more accessible approach by situating AMR within lived experience. In this qualitative study, eight members of the public participated in a facilitated screening discussion of five digital stories (short videos created by people depicting their experiences of resistant infection and antibiotic-related harm). Analysed using reflexive thematic analysis, three themes were generated from the data. First, DST made AMR visible, personal, and emotionally grounded through lived experience rather than information alone. Second, engagement was shaped by storytelling devices including metaphor, imagery, voice, and framing. Third, viewers interpreted stories through pre-existing beliefs, social norms, and personal experience. As an exploratory outcome, mapping themes to a behaviour change model suggested DST engagement may influence determinants of behaviour change including social opportunity, psychological capability, and reflective and automatic motivation.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is largely driven by inappropriate antimicrobial use in human and animal health, with resistant infections undermining the foundations of modern medicine and contributing to millions of deaths worldwide each year1–4. However, despite the scale and urgency of the issue, public engagement with AMR and its relevance to both individuals and society, remains relatively poor5–7. Unlike acute public health crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic, which rapidly mobilised public attention and behaviour, AMR is often perceived as distant and abstract, which contributes to this sense of public disconnection from the issue8–10. This highlights the need for more meaningful public engagement interventions that shape understanding and behaviour in everyday decision-making around the prevention and management of non-serious infections, ultimately supporting appropriate behaviours such as using antibiotics only when clinically indicated11,12.

To date, most public health AMR communication and stewardship efforts have relied heavily on informational and biomedical framings, emphasising knowledge transmission, clinical guidelines, and risk statistics, as exemplified by campaigns such as ‘Keep Antibiotics Working’, and European Antibiotic Awareness Day campaign material13–15. Even for those interventions that improve knowledge and attitudes, there remains little robust evidence that these gains translate into sustained change in beliefs and behaviour related to reduced antibiotic use for non-serious infections14,16. The behaviour of public, patients and healthcare prescribers is recognised as an important contributor to the AMR crisis, including poor compliance with antimicrobial prescriptions, and requests, use or provision of antibiotics when not clinically indicated17. Existing public AMR communication has also shown limited ability to address the emotional and lived-experience dimensions of antibiotic use, such as fear, uncertainty and prior illness experiences, or to overcome the perceived irrelevance of AMR in everyday life. Accordingly, there is increasing recognition of the need for more affective and relatable approaches that engage the public with the human impact of AMR and antibiotic use7,18.

In response to this research gap - the need for more affective and relatable approaches to public engagement with AMR that may encourage behaviours to mitigate its development - the StoryBug study examined digital storytelling (DST) as an innovative and under-explored approach. Building on earlier conceptual work proposing DST as a potential tool for AMR communication and engagement, and as a means to ‘make microbes matter’ in public engagement with AMR, the present qualitative analysis examines the impact of digital stories created through a facilitated process in which individuals produce short, first-person videos combining images with a voiced script representing their lived experience19. In addition, as an exploratory outcome, this study also considered the extent to which public engagement with digital stories could be situated on the causal pathway to behaviour change20.

Behavioural science frameworks increasingly recognise that health-related behaviours are shaped not only by knowledge and information but also by emotional, social, cultural and contextual influences. The Capability, Opportunity, Motivation–Behaviour (COM-B) model provides a framework for behavioural diagnosis by identifying the capability, opportunity and motivational factors that influence behaviour20. The Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) also synthesises a broad range of behavioural determinants and is widely applied in healthcare implementation and behaviour-change research21. Such frameworks may therefore offer a useful lens through which to explore how emotionally resonant communication grounded in lived experience, such as DST, shapes public engagement with AMR and antibiotic use beyond awareness alone.

The study was guided by the following overall research question: What is the value of DST in enriching public engagement with concepts of AMR? Specifically, this analysis addresses Part 2 of the two-part StoryBug study, asking: how do digital stories enrich public engagement with antibiotic use, and AMR as a threat to health and well-being, and, exploratorily, how might such engagement sit on the causal pathway to behaviour change? Part 1 explored the DST process from the storytellers’ perspective and will be reported separately in a future publication. To our knowledge, the StoryBug study represents the first application of DST in the context of AMR.

Results

Six participants completed the full screening (viewing of the digital stories) and associated discussion and were included in the final analysis after two participants withdrew part way through the screening for reasons unrelated to the study. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 70 years and represented diverse educational and occupational backgrounds (Table 1). Theme 1: Viewers of digital stories recognise and realise the personal and often unacknowledged impacts and associated knowledge of lived experiences of AMR and antibiotic adversity

Across the discussion, participants consistently described how the digital stories transformed AMR from a distant or abstract concept into a more humanised and personally meaningful health issue. Rather than reporting acquisition of new factual knowledge, viewers described moments of realisation in which the consequences of antibiotic use and AMR became more tangible and personally meaningful when embedded in everyday life contexts.“I thought it was the cancer that would kill me, but now I think the resistant infection is the more imminent threat. That for me was the dramatic twist.” (BO, False Peak)

“I thought it was the cancer that would kill me, but now I think the resistant infection is the more imminent threat. That for me was the dramatic twist.” (BO, False Peak)

Participants acknowledged that the stories did not deliver a comprehensive explanation for AMR but instead fostered shifts in perception by presenting the subject matter as situated, humanised, and unpredictable.‘I really think that these videos tell us about the human impact of AMR rather than the science or medicine behind AMR and antibiotics.’ (BO, general)

‘I really think that these videos tell us about the human impact of AMR rather than the science or medicine behind AMR and antibiotics.’ (BO, general)

Emotional responses, including empathy for fear, vulnerability, and uncertainty portrayed in the stories, were central to this shift. Participants described moving from perceiving AMR as a problem affecting ‘other people’ to recognising its relevance within more familiar circumstances, suggesting a reduction in psychological distance22.“It could happen to anyone…he was a healthy man and it is very relatable.” (LK, Stiletto)

“It could happen to anyone…he was a healthy man and it is very relatable.” (LK, Stiletto)

‘…the pictures in the hospital [chair in waiting room] were really good because everybody, patient or not, has been in hospital and experienced the feelings that go with that’ (LK, Fight to Breathe)

“I thought it was really good, especially having it from the mother’s perspective…the relationship between the mother and the child is so important.” (JH, Fight to Breathe)

Taken together, these responses indicate that DST grounded understanding of AMR in lived experience, combining emotional engagement with contextualised sense-making rather than didactic information delivery.

Theme 2: Viewers relate by DST devices that nurture immersion and connectedness with the AMR story

This theme captures how engagement can be shaped not only by story content, but by DST devices and techniques such as setting, story structure, metaphor, imagery, and voice. Viewers reflected on how these elements nurtured immersion in the story and shaped perceived relevance of the subject matter, while also recognising that the same techniques could resonate differently across viewers. Responses were contingent on individual experience and interpretation, reflecting a transactional interpretative process between storyteller and viewer.

Stories depicting ordinary people in recognisable situations were widely described as accessible and relevant. Reflecting on Toxic Turn, one participant noted:‘This is a regular guy, he has kids, went skiing, and had an accident.’ (JN, Toxic Turn)

Theme 3: Psychosocial concepts, and wider societal values underpin viewer engagement with antibiotic use and AMR as a threat to health

Theme 3 extends the ideas introduced in Theme 2 by recognising that meaning does not reside solely within the digital story. Instead, it is co-constructed through drawing upon viewers’ own life experiences, social roles, and beliefs about illness, medicine, and risk, giving rise to diverse and sometimes contrasting interpretations.

For example, responses to Hidden Embers varied according to viewers’ sociocultural perspectives and personal beliefs. Behavioural interpretation of the themes

To explore the behavioural relevance of the findings, the completed themes were subsequently interpreted using the COM-B model, the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF), and Behaviour Change Technique Taxonomy v1 (BCTTv1). This conceptual mapping is presented below.

A COM-B behavioural diagnosis was developed to define the behavioural influences underpinning unnecessary antibiotic use and public engagement with AMR20,23.

DST mechanisms of action identified from literature on DST/storytelling, narrative persuasion, and health communication, included, for example, emotional engagement, social influence, relatability, identification, and narrative reframing. These mechanisms were interpretively aligned with selected BCTs and TDF domains using the BCT Theory and Techniques Tool and the Storytelling Framework by Brooks et al. 24–30.

The final themes and supporting participant extracts were then examined to explore whether viewer responses reflected the COM-B behavioural diagnosis, TDF domains, BCTs and proposed DST mechanisms. The resulting conceptual links are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Mapping the thematic findings to the COM-B model showed that four domains were most clearly reflected in the data: Reflective Motivation, Automatic Motivation, Psychological Capability, and Social Opportunity. Theme 1 primarily reflected Psychological Capability and Reflective Motivation, as participants described increased awareness of the lived consequences and human impacts of AMR and antibiotic use. Theme 2 aligned most closely with Automatic Motivation and Social Opportunity, with viewers describing emotional immersion, relatability, metaphor, and identification with storytellers and familiar scenarios. Theme 3 captured Reflective Motivation and Social Opportunity through participants’ discussion of wider social norms, cultural beliefs, vulnerability, uncertainty, and assumptions surrounding antibiotics and modern medicine.

The findings suggested that DST engaged participants through several interrelated psychological and behavioural processes, including emotional engagement, social influence, beliefs about consequences, knowledge acquisition/meaning and sense-making, and self-relevance24,31. Together, these shaped how participants engaged with, interpreted, and made sense of the stories. These mechanisms were interpreted as showing conceptual correspondence with several BCT categories described within the BCT Taxonomy and Theory and Techniques Tool, particularly those relating to information about emotional and health consequences, social comparison, framing and reframing, prompts and cues, and credible source. Relevant TDF domains included emotion, beliefs about consequences, knowledge, and social influences21. Overall, this suggests that engagement with DST is shaped through interacting cognitive, emotional, and social processes, rather than through the transfer of information alone.

Importantly, the findings suggest that DST may help reduce psychological distance by making AMR feel more immediate and relevant to people’s own lives. Through relatable digital stories, first-person perspectives, and metaphor, abstract concepts about AMR were translated into something more tangible and easier to connect with. In this sense, DST appears to work as an upstream, sense-making intervention that supports reflection, challenges assumptions, and reframes how antibiotic use is understood, rather than directly changing behaviour.

Discussion

Digital storytelling (DST) offered a distinctive mode of engagement by making AMR more visible, personal, and emotionally grounded through authentic first-person accounts of lived experience. Rather than learning new facts, participants described moments of recognition and realisation, in which AMR became tangible and meaningful when embedded in stories of ordinary lives disrupted by antibiotic-resistant infection, treatment failure, or antibiotic-related harm. As one of the first empirical examinations of DST as a public engagement approach to AMR – here focusing on viewers rather than storytellers - the findings highlight how engagement emerges through interpretation rather than information transmission. Taken together, the findings suggest DST can reposition AMR from a distant biomedical issue to an immediate, humanised health concern.

The findings also emphasise the transactional and immersive nature of DST engagement, in which meaning is co-constructed through interaction between story and viewer rather than transmitted one-way26,32. Responses were shaped not only by story content, including metaphor, imagery, voice, and framing, but also by viewers’ own identities, experiences, and beliefs. This helps explain why the same stories resonated differently across individuals, supporting DST as a dialogic and interpretive process grounded in personal frames of reference. In doing so, these findings extend beyond conventional evaluations focused on message recall or attitude change, suggesting instead that DST may activate affective, relational, and social pathways often under-recognised in antibiotic stewardship communications. Toxic Turn illustrates this: some viewers related to the skiing context, while others found it socio-culturally distant. Engagement therefore emerges through this dynamic relationship, fostering a viewer's sense of individual ownership and highlighting the value of developing diverse stories on a single topic to broaden resonance across audiences.

In contrast to conventional campaigns that present knowledge as isolated facts, DST embeds biomedical information within storytelling, for example, conveying the seriousness of infection for cancer survival in False Peak. This addresses a gap in AMR communication research, which has largely prioritised information provision over interpretive, two-way engagement. A scoping review by Park et al. found DST to be a well-received, engaging approach to knowledge translation for patients, caregivers, and professionals33. Through this process, audiences do not simply receive information but interpret and internalise it in personally meaningful ways. Rather than tightly controlling interpretation, DST invites multiple readings and stimulates conversation, representing a departure from information-deficit, transmission-based public health messaging.

In addition, the DST approach used here prioritised an open-ended, storyteller-led process over predetermined messaging, consistent with psychologist, Arthur Frank’s account of storytelling in illness34. This preserved the meaning-making character of DST for both storytellers and viewers and aligned with the study’s interpretivist paradigm. Storyteller agency is evident in Fight to Breathe, where the mother shaped the story to emphasise reflection on what mattered most to her family and her son, informing treatment decisions beyond a purely clinical framing. This interplay between storyteller agency and viewer interpretation helps explain the diversity of responses observed and sheds light on how DST operates as an engagement mechanism.

These findings are supported by broader theories of narrative communication. Storytelling can induce narrative transportation (Green and Brock's Narrative Transportation Theory), where attention, emotion, and imagination are absorbed in the story world, reducing counter-arguing and enabling experiential engagement26,28,35. Participants’ accounts of being ‘drawn in’, emotionally connected, and prompted to reflect on their own experiences indicate that DST operates through similar mechanisms, supporting personally meaningful interpretation and reducing perceived distance from AMR. Narrative devices such as tone, pacing, imagery, and voice further support this immersion36. For example, Hidden Embers and False Peak use metaphor to convey emotional and clinical risk. Similar effects are reported elsewhere; Difulvio et al. found that shared storytelling elicited empathy and solidarity through shared experience37.

In the context of AMR, DST reduces psychological distance by immersing viewers cognitively and emotionally in lived experience, reframing AMR as an immediate, shared vulnerability rather than a distant threat. Vulnerability is depicted across a spectrum, from healthy individuals to those living with serious illness, challenging assumptions that AMR affects only high-risk groups. The stories also show that AMR-related vulnerability extends beyond physical illness to psychosocial impacts, including stigma, uncertainty, and isolation, as reflected in prior DST research38,39. These dimensions are illustrated in Fight to Breathe, where a child describes feeling ‘under attack’ and unsure ‘who was now the enemy’, or in Hidden Embers the storyteller describes feeling trapped psychosocially as well as physically by her urinary tract infection. Through these portrayals, DST fosters emotional resonance and personal relevance, addressing a key limitation of AMR communication, which often struggles when risks are framed in abstract or population-level terms7,11,40.

Participant viewers were largely drawn from the UK general public and did not necessarily share lived experience of AMR and this contrasts with much of the existing DST literature, where audiences often have shared illness, community, or cultural ties with storytellers, and as such are already personally invested; for example, DST work with Inuit women on HIV and sexually transmitted infection (STI) prevention draws on shared social and cultural experience to support engagement33,41. This distinction is important, as it highlights how DST functions in more targeted settings, and conversely, how engagement may differ when addressing a broader, less invested public audience, as in the StoryBug study. In these cases, additional outreach may be needed to attract initial attention and bring viewers to the stories.

The evidence base linking DST to behaviour change is limited and mixed, particularly in relation to sustained effects. A systematic review of narrative interventions for health-promoting behaviours found mixed results, with moderate short-term effects on intentions but less consistent evidence of sustained behaviour change42. As such, to situate the thematic findings from the StoryBug study within a behaviour change context, an exploratory mapping to the COM-B model suggested that DST may influence Psychological Capability, Social Opportunity, Reflective Motivation and Automatic Motivation. Social Opportunity, for example, was evident through processes related to role, identity, and social comparison, as evidenced by a viewer-mother identifying strongly with the parent in Fight to Breathe. This aligns with wider narrative communication literature, which emphasises the importance of relatable characters and audience characteristics in shaping engagement and interpretation28,43.

However, the relationship between DST and behaviour change is likely indirect. StoryBug was designed to prompt reflection rather than prescribe behaviour, and existing evidence suggests only modest and short-term effects on behavioural intentions. In practice, antibiotic stewardship messages and intentions may also be limited by structural and clinical constraints, highlighting the complexity of AMR behavioural change44.

This study has several strengths and limitations. A key strength lies in its interpretivist design, which enabled exploration of collective meaning-making through open-ended discussion. The use of RTA supported identification of patterns of engagement while acknowledging the researcher’s role45. A diverse participant viewer sample enriched the range of perspectives. However, limitations include a small, self-selected sample, a single screening session, and the potential influence of negative (as well as positive) group dynamics. Analysis by a single researcher may also limit interpretive breadth, and the exploratory nature of COM-B mapping constrained the depth of behavioural inference. Importantly, DST was not evaluated as a direct behaviour change intervention, but rather as a reflective and sense-making approach that may support precursors relevant to behavioural engagement with AMR and antibiotic use.

The study also drew predominantly on UK-based literature, reflecting the context-specific nature of DST, in which audience identification and narrative resonance are shaped by local prescribing cultures, healthcare structures, and sociocultural understandings of antibiotic use. At the same time, existing literature suggests that antibiotic use and prescribing are influenced not only by biomedical considerations but also by wider sociocultural and relational dynamics, including hierarchy, clinician-patient communication, social expectation, and tolerance of uncertainty46–48. These factors may shape not only prescribing decisions, but also how individuals interpret and emotionally engage with AMR stories. While the present study focused on lived experiences of resistant infection within a UK audience context, future DST research could explore how differing prescribing cultures, healthcare systems, and sociocultural norms influence narrative interpretation and behavioural engagement across settings.

Future research should build on these findings by examining how DST can contribute to antimicrobial stewardship in real-world settings and at scale. Prospective studies incorporating behavioural or clinical endpoints are needed, alongside pragmatic trials in healthcare and public campaign contexts. Methodological development will also be important, including mixed-methods evaluation, comparative studies of narrative framing, and exploration of different complementary and/or comparative formats such as podcasts or short-form media. Further work could explore targeted approaches for specific populations and strategies for wider digital dissemination, while preserving the authenticity central to the fundamental fabric of DST. Future work may also further explore and validate the connections between DST and behaviour change.

In conclusion, this study provides insight into how DST can support public engagement with complex health issues such as AMR. Rather than transmitting information didactically, DST fosters engagement through a transactional, interpretive, and emotional process that makes abstract threats more visible and personally meaningful. By situating AMR within lived experience, it enables reflection on vulnerability, responsibility, and the consequences of antibiotic resistance in ways conventional messaging may not achieve. These findings extend prior conceptual work on storytelling as a means to ‘make microbes matter’ and suggest that DST can play a valuable complementary role in antimicrobial stewardship by reducing psychological distance and enabling personal meaning-making around antibiotic use and AMR.

Methods

Design and overview

Part 2 of the StoryBug study that is reported in this analysis comprised the screening and facilitated discussion of the five stories created in Part 1 of the study. Part 1 involved the co-creation of digital stories depicting lived experiences of antibiotic-resistant infection and antibiotic-related harm, while Part 2 explored how members of the public engaged with, interpreted, and responded to these stories as a public engagement intervention around AMR and antibiotic use. The study adopted an interpretivist qualitative design using facilitated group discussion and RTA to explore collective meaning-making in response to DST.

Participants comprised members of the general public drawn from across London to support demographic diversity within a small exploratory qualitative study. The screening discussion was recorded to capture how viewers engaged with, interpreted and responded to the digital stories as an engagement intervention that aimed to enrich meaning-making around AMR and antibiotic adversity.

The digital story creation process followed the ‘Four Phases’ method designed for health and wellness contexts and taught by Common Language Digital Storytelling (CLDST). In Part 1 of the study, each storyteller worked virtually one-to-one with a trained facilitator and researcher (BM), who has a background in medical journalism, qualitative research, and DST facilitation, to develop a 3–5-minute video based on lived experience of antibiotic-resistant infection or antibiotic-related harm. Events and associated emotions were iteratively explored (the Finding phase), then scripted and recorded as a voiceover (the Telling phase). The narration was subsequently integrated with authentic images and video using a range of editing techniques (the Crafting phase), before being screened to a small group of lay participants with no specialist knowledge of AMR or DST beyond that of the general public (the 'Sharing' phase)49. Reflexive awareness of the facilitator-researcher role was maintained throughout data collection and analysis, particularly with respect to interpretation, questioning style, and familiarity with the DST process.

The DST intervention was designed to frame antibiotic use and AMR within everyday lived experience and thereby challenge assumptions and misunderstandings surrounding AMR through highly contextualised personal storytelling. For example, misconceptions that AMR primarily affects people who frequently use antibiotics or who have significant underlying illness may be challenged through digital stories depicting resistant infection in otherwise healthy individuals whose lives have nevertheless been profoundly impacted by AMR. In this way, DST was hypothesised to reduce psychological distance around AMR, shift perceptions of AMR from an abstract public health issue to a personally relevant concern and encourage more reflective engagement with antibiotic use.

Viewers were members of the general public who were unknown to the storytellers (who did not attend the screenings to minimise potential bias in viewer responses). Viewers were encouraged to reflect on their interpretations, emotional responses and understanding of concepts and issues around AMR and antibiotic use raised by the stories. Recruitment and methods for the screening and discussion

Participants for the digital story screening and discussion were recruited via social media (StoryBug Project and Institute of Health Informatics Twitter/X accounts) and local community groups in London, alongside snowball sampling to support demographic diversity across age, education, ethnicity and occupation. Sampling was therefore primarily convenience-based, with purposive efforts to achieve diversity in demographic and social backgrounds relevant to a lay public audience. The StoryBug stories aimed to address members of the general population with respect to understanding AMR and antibiotic use, and as such, the study sample aimed to represent this as far as reasonably possible in a small qualitative study that had to balance representation with costs. Individuals with medical backgrounds were excluded to preserve a lay public perspective on AMR. Each participant received an information sheet and provided written informed consent. Eight participants attended the five-hour screening held at the Institute of Health Informatics (IHI), University College London (UCL), London on 8 April 2024, and received £100 to cover time and travel. Inclusion criteria were age ≥16 years, adequate English proficiency, and willingness to be audio recorded.

While the sample size was small (eight reduced to six part way through the screening and discussion event), this was considered appropriate for an exploratory interpretivist study focused on depth of discussion, collective meaning-making, and nuanced interpretation rather than breadth or representativeness. The extended five-hour screening format generated rich qualitative data across multiple stories and discussion points, supporting sufficient information power for the study aims.

The digital stories were screened and discussed both immediately following the viewing of each story individually, as well as after viewing all the stories as a collection. One of the five digital stories, Stiletto, concerned a healthy young man who acquired a resistant toe infection. Because the storyteller withdrew from the study for personal reasons before the digital story was completed, the script was read aloud by the facilitator. Questions asked at the end of each video viewing were deliberately open in approach to avoid bias through leading participants towards researcher views, and the same initial question about what resonated with the viewers was asked for each video to ensure consistency. This was followed by two specific questions tailored to each video but again, these aimed for openness in response. Participants were also asked a small set of open, reflective questions after viewing the story collection to explore how perceptions were formed over a series rather than one story. The questions elicited expectations, comparisons with traditional health communication, and any shifts in perception of antibiotic use and AMR, without directing responses.

Analysis

The discussion was audio-recorded using a UCL-provided Zoom digital recorder, with iPhone Voice Memos used as a backup recording method. Audio files were subsequently transferred to password-protected storage compliant with UCL and NHS data governance requirements. The discussion was then analysed using Braun and Clarke’s RTA, an inductive, interpretivist, and reflexive approach that prioritises participant-led meaning through open coding and theme generation grounded in the data.

Braun and Clarke’s six-part RTA process comprised familiarisation with the transcript, detailed line-by-line coding, and the development and iterative refinement of themes through repeated movement between coded extracts, the wider dataset, and evolving interpretations45. Analytical rigour was supported through iterative engagement with the data, reflexive memo-writing, transparent documentation of analytical decisions, and use of NVivo 15 software to organise data. Patterns were generated in how viewers engaged with AMR through DST across the story collection, while drawing on specific examples from different stories and retaining divergent responses to preserve interpretive complexity rather than seeking consensus across participants.

Following completion of the inductive RTA, a secondary and exploratory deductive mapping exercise was undertaken. The behavioural mapping process occurred in three stages: COM-B behavioural diagnosis, followed by DST mechanism linkage and finally mapping themes (from the RTA) to behavioural domains. This mapping process examined how the thematic findings might relate to behavioural science frameworks commonly used in healthcare implementation and behaviour-change research, here comprising COM-B and the TDF20,21. BCT Taxonomy v1 was subsequently used to explore conceptual correspondence between identified DST mechanisms and recognised BCTs. These models and frameworks were applied only after theme generation (the RTA process) as sensitising and interpretive lenses, rather than as primary coding frameworks.They also enabled structured consideration of cognitive, emotional, social, and contextual influences on behaviour that may be engaged through DST50.

The behavioural mapping process occurred in three stages. First, a COM-B behavioural diagnosis was undertaken to identify the behavioural influences relevant to antibiotic use and public engagement with AMR. This provided the conceptual behavioural diagnosis used in the subsequent mapping exercise20. Related BCTs were subsequently explored using the BCT Theory and Techniques Tool, while relevant TDF domains were identified to support interpretation of the thematic findings30.

Second, DST-related mechanisms identified from the storytelling and narrative persuasion literature were then interpretively related to BCTs using the BCT Theory and Techniques Tool and Brooks et al.’s Storytelling Framework study24–28. These stages informed an expanded COM-B mapping framework incorporating DST-related mechanisms that was subsequently used to interpret the thematic findings (see COM-B diagnosis Supplementary Table 1).

Finally, the thematic findings were mapped onto this integrated COM-B–DST MoA–BCT framework to identify corresponding COM-B, BCT and TDF domains and to explore whether the findings suggested that DST may utilise mechanisms relevant to a plausible pathway toward behaviour change. This approach aimed to translate thematic insights into hypothesised behavioural mechanisms, rather than to assess behavioural outcomes directly.

Theoretical orientation

Both the creation of the digital stories and the subsequent screening and discussion were theoretically guided by philosophical hermeneutics, which aligns with the interpretivist paradigm by positioning understanding as an interpretive process through which human experience is made meaningful. Beyond interpretivism, philosophical hermeneutics emphasises that understanding is always historically and linguistically mediated, shaped by the social, cultural and experiential backgrounds of those involved and understanding is arrived at through negotiation of meaning51.

Ethical issues pertaining to human subjects

Health Research Authority approval for the StoryBug study granted 9 May 2023. REC reference: 23/WS/0059. Sponsor UCL (date obtained 28/-02/23). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent for both the Story Creation process and the screening-and-discussion event (as reported here). Participants had the right to withdraw at any time without repercussions. Data security measures included anonymisation and storage on password-protected systems compliant with NHS data protection requirements.
