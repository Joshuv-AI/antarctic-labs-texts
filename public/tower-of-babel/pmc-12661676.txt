Knowledge, attitudes, practices and determinants of mosquito repellent use for malaria prevention in Nangabo Ward, Wakiso District, Uganda
Authors: Grace Kemigisha, David Musoke, Abel W. Walekhwa, Adoke Yeka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Background

Malaria remains a significant global public health challenge, especially in regions where the disease is endemic, such as sub-Saharan Africa [1]. Transmitted by Anopheles mosquitoes, malaria is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, with an estimated 263 million cases and 59,7000 deaths reported worldwide in 2023 [2]. Malaria is the leading cause of illness and death in Uganda, accounting for 40% of outpatient visits and 25% of hospital admissions [3]. Despite ongoing efforts to combat the disease through measures like the widespread use of long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLIN), indoor residual spraying (IRS), and environmental management, malaria transmission persists and continues to impact vulnerable populations [4, 5]. This ongoing burden highlights the need for additional and complementary strategies to mitigate malaria transmission and its associated public health consequences.

Mosquito repellents are an effective, cost-efficient, and environmentally-friendly strategy for preventing mosquito bites, and consequently, malaria transmission both indoors and outdoors [6, 7]. While synthetic repellents, such as DEET, picaridin, and permethrin-treated clothing, are commonly used, natural repellents derived from plants such as citronella, neem oil, and lemongrass offer promising alternatives due to their eco-friendly and safe properties, especially for vulnerable people [6–9]. In Uganda, mosquito repellents are available in various forms, including sprays, coils, creams, and oils [10]. Some households have also adopted traditional methods, such as planting mosquito-repellent plants and burning cow dung, although these practices are not widespread and not formalized [4, 6]. Despite efforts to control malaria primarily through indoor interventions, such as LLIN and IRS, there remains a significant gap in knowledge, policy and practice. Relatedly, more studies in other countries like Kenya, Tanzania, and Malawi also showed that the same gap was significantly impacting their communities in the area of malaria prevention [11–13]. The knowledge versus practice gap leaves outdoor malaria transmission largely unaddressed, especially as mosquitoes began to bite both indoors and outdoors, and both during the day and night. Recent entomological data confirmed the adaptive biting behaviours in Ugandan Anopheles mosquitoes, which underscores the urgency of repellent-based interventions [14]. However, the use of mosquito repellents has not been sufficiently promoted or integrated into national malaria control strategies, which predominantly focus on interventions targeting indoor mosquito bites, such as bed nets and IRS [15, 16]. This explicit linkage between policy and interventions highlights a critical gap, which hinders the smooth adoption and use of mosquito repellents.

Although LLINs and IRS are central to malaria strategies, in Uganda, there have been no investigations into knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding mosquito repellent use in Uganda, nor in Wakiso District, where 30% of mosquito bites occur outdoors [17, 18]. Another Wakiso report showed that only 68% of the households owned only one net per household, which underscores the need for complementary strategies like mosquito repellents [19]. Studies indicated that while some Ugandan households were aware of mosquito repellents, there was limited understanding of the various types, their correct application, and their effectiveness in preventing malaria [20]. Furthermore, concerns regarding the safety of chemical repellents, particularly for vulnerable populations such as pregnant women, breastfeeding mothers, and young children, contributed to hesitancy and inconsistent use [18]. Additionally, factors such as cost, accessibility, cultural beliefs, and a lack of awareness further hindered the widespread adoption of mosquito repellents in malaria-endemic communities [11, 21]. Although there is increasing recognition of mosquito repellents as an effective malaria prevention strategy, there is a lack of empirical data on their practical use in Uganda, especially in rural areas. This underscores the need for more targeted public health interventions to raise awareness and improve mosquito repellent use. This study found it crucial to assess the knowledge, attitudes, practices, and social determinants of repellent use, given the gaps in knowledge and practices, the absence of formal integration of mosquito repellents into the national malaria control policy and the inadequacy of current preventive measures [22]. The findings aim to inform policy development, encourage greater adoption of repellents, and enhance malaria prevention strategies in Uganda. Ultimately, this study will contribute to improving malaria control efforts and may prove beneficial for similar malaria-endemic regions globally.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted in Nangabo ward, located in Nangabo subcounty, Wakiso District, in the Central Region of Uganda, which is approximately 14.4 km from Kampala, the capital city. Nangabo ward is one of the 9 wards (local council IIs) in Wakiso district and is subdivided into 6 cells, which include: Nangabo, Kitagobwa, Kitegombwa, Bamba, Makenke, and Gita-Nabutaka. The study area was selected due to its peri-urban location, which exhibits a unique and complex malaria transmission dynamic characterized by a mix of rural and urban features [12]. This setting is particularly significant for malaria research due to the rapid and often unplanned expansion. This leads to altered land use, including the conversion of natural wetlands for construction and agriculture, which creates numerous productive mosquito breeding sites ideal for Anopheles vectors [23, 24]. Additionally, rapid population growth driven by migration and natural increase of land use results in high human density, increasing human mosquito contact rates and facilitating malaria parasite spread [23]. Unlike highly endemic rural areas or low transmission urban centres, this peri-urban zone experiences a high yet heterogeneous malaria burden, making it an ideal case study for understanding transmission patterns that are increasingly common in Uganda and across sub-Saharan Africa [23, 25].

Study population

The study population consisted of 185 participants aged 18 years and above, who were recruited to ensure informed consent autonomy as guided by Uganda’s ethical guidelines, in the Uganda National Council of Science and Technology ethical regulations (UNCST, 2020, Sect. 4.4) for adult-focused public health research. [26]. For the qualitative study, in-depth interviews were conducted with 15 community members who were expected to have lived experiences in malaria preventive interventions. These included two local council chairmen, two VHTs, a health educator, and 10 members of the community who were 60 years and above, who could give us insights on malaria prevention trends over the years, making a total of 200 participants.

Study design

This was a cross-sectional study that employed mixed methods of data collection. Quantitative data were collected through a semi-structured questionnaire administered to household heads or their designees, on Knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions. A descriptive phenomenological design was used for the qualitative component of the study. This approach enabled participants to share their stories and perspectives in their own words, producing rich and detailed narratives essential for understanding the phenomenon of repellent use within the community. Since the study aimed at identifying common themes and patterns across the narratives, forming a collective understanding of the community's perceptions, the approach was appropriate for the study [27].

Sampling methods

The Kish-Leslie formula (1965) was used to determine the sample size [28] with a confidence interval of 95%, an accepted error of 5%, and a 14% prevalence of mosquito repellent use [6]. For quantitative data, a sample size of 185 household heads was used, while for the qualitative study, 15 participants were interviewed. In Nangabo ward, there are six cells, and two of these cells were randomly selected using the ballot method, whereby villages were assigned numbers one to six on pieces of paper. The well-folded papers containing numbers were placed in a box and mixed thoroughly for one minute. Each of the two research assistants was then allowed to draw one piece of paper. The numbers chosen corresponded to the villages selected for the study. For the study participants, A two-stage sampling method was used, primarily based on a lottery system. First, households in the two selected cells were listed using the local council registers, and each household was assigned a number from 1 to 100 for each village. The numbered pieces of paper were then cut, folded, and placed into two boxes, 100 slips in each box. The chairpersons of the cells took turns drawing numbers from their respective boxes, one at a time. The drawn numbers were then used to select the study participants. To finalize the required sample, a simple random sampling method was employed by generating a list of random numbers from the numbered papers arranged in ascending order to select the final households. In each selected household, the head of the household was interviewed. If the head was unavailable, a designated representative was interviewed instead.

For the qualitative aspect, 15 participants were purposively selected from the original sample size of 200, where the first five (two LC chairmen, two VHTs, one Health educator) were already known due to their positions in the community, while the other 10 were selected with the guidance of the LC chairmen. These participants were interviewed to the point of data saturation.

Study variables and measurements

This study assessed knowledge, attitudes, practices, and determinants regarding mosquito repellents used for malaria prevention using structured and synthetic tools. Knowledge was measured through a 15-question survey addressing various aspects of repellents, including effectiveness, type, and safety. Correct answers scored one point, while incorrect or I don’t know responses scored zero. Participants scoring ≥ 9 were classified as having high knowledge, while those scoring below 9 were considered to have low knowledge. Attitudes were evaluated using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Five statements assessed perceptions of repellents' safety, effectiveness, and acceptability. Scores of ≥ 4/5 indicated positive attitudes, while scores of ≤ 2/5 reflected negative attitudes. Practices were assessed through questions on repellent use frequency, type (creams, sprays), timing (day or night, indoor or outdoor), and application methods (on the skin, clothing, burning or spraying in space). Good practices were determined based on regular use, whereby one who chose 2 options day and night, daily, and appropriate usage aligning with product recommendations.

Inclusion criteria

Household heads or their designees aged 18 years and above, who could ethically give consent, were interviewed. Only residents who had lived in the area for six months or longer were interviewed, as they were perceived to have a better understanding of local customs and the social environment. Short-term residents may not accurately represent the long-term community’s views and could introduce bias in the findings.

Exclusion criteria

Those who were not present during the scheduled data collection days were excluded for practical and logistical reasons. Additionally, individuals unable to provide informed consent, including those with cognitive impairments, severe mental health conditions affecting decision making or those under the influence of substances like alcohol at the time of data collection, were also excluded due to ethical and legal constraints. Individuals with physical conditions that prevented their participation, such as being bedridden or otherwise unable to engage in data collection activities like interviews or surveys, were excluded as well.

Tools development and pre-testing

For quantitative data, a semi-structured questionnaire composed of closed-ended questions was developed, informed by existing literature, and was deployed on the Kobo toolbox for a pilot study, resulting in efficient data collection management [29–31] Two research assistants proficient in Luganda, the most spoken language in the area, were then selected and trained on how to use the questionnaire, understand the study goals, collect data, and adhere to research ethics. After the training, they engaged in role-playing exercises to ensure consistent questioning before being assigned specific locations for data collection.

To ensure robustness, the study tools underwent pre-testing among 20 randomly selected households in Nangabo cell, which was not part of the study area. Since the sample size for pre-testing was small (20 participants), qualitative feedback methods were used to test for consistency and reliability, particularly cognitive interviews, to gather insights from participants as they navigated through each survey item. This process allowed the researchers to capture the participants' views and insights, highlighting any misunderstandings, the presence of jargon, and identifying any gaps in the questions. The tool was then revised for length to ensure that it does not go beyond 30 min, to avoid keeping participants for long, which would lead to biased answers. The final approved tool by the research team was deployed to Kobo Toolbox, and the link was shared with research assistants to go to the field for the actual data collection process, ensuring both robustness in the methods and contextual appropriateness for the study population.

To assess validity, the tool was submitted to the School of Public Health, Department of Infectious Diseases and Environmental Health Science, who convened an expert meeting of three public health specialists and local field workers with experience in malaria prevention and community engagement. This panel conducted a systematic content validity evaluation, rating each questionnaire item for relevance using a 4-point scale. Items that achieved a content validity of 0.80 and above were retained as valid for the final tool, and those that did not were replaced with the more appropriate items. In terms of reliability, the study’s pre-test sample size was below the minimum required for conducting the Cronbach's alpha test (30 participants). However, several safeguards were implemented to enhance reliability. These included standardized training for the research assistants to ensure consistent administration, iterative pretesting with cognitive interviews to improve question clarity, and peer debriefing sessions to verify interpretive accuracy.

Data collection

The research team conducted a preliminary visit to Nangabo ward to establish rapport and seek permission from the relevant authorities. During this visit, important information was gathered, like what time is appropriate for carrying out data collection, as engagements were going on with health workers, village health team members (VHTs), and local council chairpersons. Upon arrival at the data collection units, the research assistants introduced the study to the participants, establishing rapport and ensuring a comfortable environment. They then presented the consent form, seeking approval from each participant to audio record their responses. After obtaining consent, the research assistants began the questioning process, reading the guiding questions aloud and documenting the participants' answers.

Quantitative data were collected through a semi-structured questionnaire composed of closed-ended questions, which was administered to 185 participants. Data on social demographics like age, level of education, and employment, among others, which would later inform us about the association of variables with mosquito repellent use. Furthermore, data were collected on Knowledge, attitudes and perceptions on mosquito repellent use. Research assistants ensured to cross-check that the questions were all answered before they left one study unit for another to avoid missing data.

Qualitative data were collected through in-depth Interviews, focusing on participants’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices, as well as the determinants of mosquito repellent use for malaria prevention. Consent was sought from each participant to audio record their responses before each interview started. Participants were also instructed not to disclose their names during the discussions, and all transcripts were anonymized to protect their identities. Data saturation was achieved through an iterative approach involving 15 in-depth interviews with five community leaders, and 10 community members 60 years and above, with varied use of mosquito repellents (ever used, and never used). Early interviews were transcribed and coded to identify preliminary themes, and subsequent interviews were analysed to compare new data against emerging patterns. Saturation was confirmed when three consecutive interviews no longer yielded new themes, indicating redundancy. To ensure methodological rigour, peer debriefings were held with the research team to validate the coding framework and conclusions. [32].

Data analysis

The dataset was then downloaded from the Kobo Toolbox server and loaded into an MS Excel spreadsheet for data cleaning and stabilization to ensure it was accurate and consistent. The cleaned Excel file was then imported into Stata MP V14 for descriptive and inferential analysis. Quantitative descriptive statistics of socio-demographics, knowledge, attitudes, and practices were computed and presented in terms of frequencies and percentages, with results reported in tables. Inferential statistics were conducted using robust modified Poisson regression to identify the association between categorical independent variables and the dependent categorical variable of never-used mosquito repellents. The bivariate analysis explored the relationship between social demographic factors, knowledge levels, and attitude levels of household heads with the never use of mosquito repellents. Crude Prevalence Ratios (CPR) were generated for variables that showed a statistically significant association (P-value ≤ 0.1) at a 90% confidence level. At the multivariable level, modified robust Poisson regression was also used to calculate adjusted prevalence ratios (APR), to identify factors significantly associated with never using mosquito repellents at P-value ≤ 0.05 within a 95% confidence level. Poisson regression was preferred over logistic regression because it provides direct estimates of adjusted prevalence ratios (APR), which are more interpretable and less prone to overestimation than odds ratios (OR) produced by logistic regression.

For qualitative data, audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and translated into English using MS Word, and transferred to Atlas.ti. Data were analysed through content analysis, whereby the audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and supplemented by comparing the notes that were taken during the interviews. The research team read through the transcripts several times, while comparing them with each other to ensure consistency in meanings. Key expressions were evaluated, and those with similar meanings were grouped into codes. The codes were then examined for patterns to generate themes and sub-themes, which were then compared with transcripts, to ensure that they presented what participants said. To ensure the trustworthiness of the qualitative data and findings, several key strategies were implemented, which included credibility through member checking, allowing participants to review the findings for accuracy and employing triangulation by cross-verifying data from multiple sources. For dependability, a detailed audit trail was maintained that documented the research design and data collection methods. To enhance confirmability, peer debriefings were utilised to solicit feedback on the content analysis and interpretations, thereby addressing potential biases. These results were then used to supplement the quantitative data represented as participants quotations.

Ethical considerations

Permission to conduct the study was obtained from Makerere University School of Public Health as part of the Bachelor's programme of Environmental Health Science (#SPH-2024–3–28) on 28th March 2024. This letter was presented to the public health department of Kasangati sub-county, as well as to the local council members in Bamba and Kitagobwa cells, for their consent to conduct the study. Furthermore, the written consent of all respondents, as well as a thumbprint from those who didn’t know how to write, was obtained before involving them in the study. In the same way, verbal consent was requested from the IDIs before they were audio recorded. The data collection tools did not include the respondent's name, but code numbers were used, and all information obtained from each respondent was kept confidential and accessed only by the principal investigator.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of participants

The study enrolled 185 participants with a mean age of 40.5 years (12.7) (Table 1), and over half, 96/185 (51.9%), were aged between 20 and 39 years. The majority of the participants, 102/185 (55.1%) had attained a tertiary education, and the predominant occupations held included: professional/managerial roles by 59/185 (31.9%), as well as business/sales by 44/185 (23.8%). Almost two-thirds of the participants, 119/185 (64.3%), had a size of 2–4 members, while over half of the participants, 104/185 (56.2%), had between 0 and 1 child aged under five years.

Knowledge of participants towards mosquito repellent use

Generally, over 63.8% (118/185) of participants had high knowledge regarding mosquito repellent use (Table 2). Over 61.6% (114/185) of participants had ever heard about mosquito repellents, whereas the majority of participants, 71.4% (132/185), had heard from family members.

From the key informants, it was discovered that several community members knew some mosquito repellent products, including plants that were good and effective.

“People in the community know those particular plants that repel mosquitoes. I met two homes which have planted a variety of plants around their homes that are meant to repel mosquitoes, including citronella, mint, rosemary, and sage.” (An IDI participant, VHT 1)

“We once used a mosquito repellent called Pico, which was very good. It had no bad smell, and no reactions. I could even use it on my babies, and none of them got any reactions to it”. (An IDI participant, Health Educator).

Only 38.4% (71/185) of the participants recognized that repellents can be used by children under five, and 35.7% (66/185) by pregnant women. Consequently, knowledge about existing government regulations was notably low at 8.1% (15/185).

Notably, the majority of the KIIs and the IDIs participants pointed out that if the government could employ a robust campaign about COVID prevention, and many people listened, the same strategy could be employed for the use of mosquito repellents in malaria prevention.

"Just like the government sensitized people about COVID-19, let them do the same for mosquito repellents. Make people know about them" (An IDI participant, Chairman 1).

Furthermore, it was discovered through key informants and in-depth interviews that although many participants had ever heard about mosquito repellents, it did not guarantee that they had sufficient knowledge about them regarding types and formulations.

“I don’t know much about mosquito repellents, although I have heard about things like coils being used to repel mosquitoes. what about these house-spraying chemicals? Do they kill mosquitoes? Or are they also repellents? (He meant Indoor Residual Spraying (An IDI participant, 66 years old).

Attitudes of participants towards mosquito repellent use

Generally, the majority, 59.5% (110/185) of the participants had a positive attitude towards mosquito repellent use for malaria control (Table 3). Over half, 55.7% (103/185) of participants believed that mosquito repellents were safe to use, whereas 53% (98/185) of participants believed that naturally based mosquito repellents were better than synthetic and chemical ones.

Similarly, the qualitative findings emphasized that mosquito repellents are a good alternative in controlling mosquito bites for malaria prevention. They further pointed out that from ancient times, homes used to employ naturally based means of controlling mosquitoes, and they were effective.

"Long ago, every home had a fireplace, and in the fire, we mixed leaves of bitter leaf plants. Mosquitoes can’t be where there is smoke. That’s why we used to burn cow dung or sit around fireplaces in the evenings" (An IDI participant, 74 years old).

However, less than a third, 27.6% (51/185) of the participants agreed that repellents were readily available.

Similarly, from the key informants, it was discovered that people had good attitudes towards mosquito repellents, and the majority accepted that they are effective and safer than synthetic repellents in terms of side effects and reactions, although some disagreed with them being readily available.

"Availability and affordability are the biggest problems we have concerning those mosquito repellents. If people don’t see them in shops, they won’t know about them, and so they won't use them." (An IDI participant, Chairman 2).

When asked if they could gladly recommend the use of repellents to other people, almost all the participants were positive. There was an overwhelming willingness of people to accept and take up this alternative of malaria prevention if it could take care of both the indoor and outdoor mosquito bites.

“Now, like me, I usually talk on night vigils, and I like hanging out at night with friends. We cannot be sitting under mosquito nets outside during all those night activities, yet mosquitoes are always present, biting us. So, if we can get that alternative, that can be easily used both outside and inside houses, which I can carry in my pocket or bag, it would be very recommendable” (An IDI participant, 62 years old).

It was also discovered from the KII and the IDIs that mosquito repellents had no known side effects on pregnant women and babies, through a few of the key responses, and this could not guarantee that they are safe in such conditions.

“We once used a repellent which was so good, even for babies, Family care, it was made of natural herbs. It had no bad smell, and no reactions, but when it was done, we looked for it, and never found it again up to today. Now that I am pregnant, we are using Balle, because I hate mosquito nets, although it smells bad for me”. (An IDI Participant VHT 2).

Practices of participants regarding mosquito repellent use

The study discovered that three-quarters, 77.3% (143/185) of the participants had ever used mosquito repellents for malaria control at least once. Among these, only 8.2% (12/143) had always used mosquito repellents (Table 4).

Findings from the IDIs and KIIs also reflected the same, where most participants agreed to have ever used them once, or just heard about them, and never used them at all.

"I used mosquito repellents during my army training many years ago because we were exposed to mosquitoes in the bush. They were very helpful, but I have never used them again." (An IDI participant, 61 years old).

During nighttime, 95.1% (136/143) and while inside the house, 55.2% (79/143) had higher scores for the times and places where mosquito repellents were used. The majority of the participants who had ever used mosquito repellents reported using them in a spray form, 39.2% (56/143).

From the key informants, it was discovered that the majority of the participants had poor practices regarding the use of mosquito repellents for malaria prevention. This was due to most of them responding to either having ever used them, but not always used, or never used after a one-time use, although they admitted to their effectiveness and safety.

I last used it 10 years ago; we were supplied with tubes of cream to smear ourselves, which were very helpful. They were given to my husband at his workplace, but I have not used them again, because I can't find those tubes, and they are also expensive, about 5.44 USD one tube. (An IDI participant 68).

Factors associated with not using mosquito repellents

At the multivariable level of analysis, five factors, including age and the highest education attained, were found to be statistically significant with the dependent variable (Table 5). Participants aged 60 and above were 2.5 times more likely to never use mosquito repellents compared to those aged 20–39 years. [Adjusted Prevalence Ratio (APR) (95% CI) = 2.50 (1.43–4.38)]. Participants who had attained a university-level education were 58% less likely to never use mosquito repellents than those who had no formal education [APR (95% CI) = 0.42 (0.22–0.79)]. Individuals who had a negative attitude were 74% more likely to have never used mosquito repellents compared to those with a positive attitude [APR (95% CI) = 1.74 (1.04–2.91)].

From the key informants’ interviews, the study found that several barriers affected the use of mosquito repellents, and they included high costs, limited availability, and poor sensitization about them.

“These creams and sprays are also very expensive if you are lucky enough to find them. A tube of cream goes for between 54.43 USD, and 81.65 USD. How many people can willingly part with that amount to buy a cream? For example, the common Odomos costs 4.08 USD, and the more favourable ones are even more expensive”. (An IDI participant, Health educator).

“Another problem is availability and affordability. If they were available and accessible in our shops or drug shops, people would at least know about them, talk about them, and in turn use them. The way people behave is that when one person finds something interesting, they will always tell another person about it. So, the fact that we don’t hear about them means they are not available. I only insist on one main thing, sensitisation”. (An IDI participant, 71 years old).

A general finding for the whole study was also noted from the key informants, whereby the majority complained of how short-acting the mosquito repellents were, among those who had ever used them.

“The only challenge with them is that they are not so long-lasting, so you would have to reapply after about 5 to 6 hours. Another challenge that I faced personally was allergies. The smell would make me sneeze a lot, but I still had to use it. (An IDI participant, 65 years old).

Discussion

The findings of this study highlight a critical gap in malaria prevention efforts in Uganda. While knowledge about mosquito repellents is relatively high (63.8%), their consistent use remains alarmingly low, with only 8.2% of households applying them regularly. This is particularly concerning given the persistent malaria burden despite existing interventions such as LLINs and IRS, which mainly focus on indoor transmission control. The low adoption of repellents raises significant implications, particularly in addressing outdoor transmission, which remains a largely unmitigated risk in Uganda. The study also uncovered key sociodemographic factors influencing repellent use, including education, household size, and age, reinforcing the need for targeted intervention strategies. Given the growing evidence from global studies on the efficacy of repellents, integrating them into Uganda's malaria control policies could provide a much-needed complementary strategy [3, 4]. Therefore, the subsequent sections delve deeper into the implications of these key findings, particularly about knowledge gaps, attitudes, practices, barriers, and factors associated with the non-use of repellents.

The study findings revealed that a significant portion of participants had heard of mosquito repellents as a means to prevent malaria. This aligns with previous studies indicating general awareness of repellents in malaria-endemic regions [20, 33]. The primary sources of this information in the study were family and friends, 71.4% (132/185), followed by radio and television. This suggests that both interpersonal communication and mass media play crucial roles in reaching a broader audience, highlighting the importance of informal social networks in disseminating mosquito repellent knowledge. This aligns with studies from other endemic regions, which discovered that when malaria prevention strategies incorporate community-based education, household adoption of interventions improves significantly [14, 18, 34]. Uganda could benefit from similar approaches, ensuring that information on repellent safety, application methods and effectiveness is widely disseminated through trusted local networks. Consequently, the finding that 60.0% (114/185) of participants knew that mosquito repellents were effective in keeping mosquitoes away when used either inside or outside the house indicates a reasonable understanding of the preventive benefits of repellents. The most commonly known effective types of mosquito repellents included mosquito repellent cream, with 31.35% (58/185) of participants mentioning it, followed by mosquito coil at 26.49% (49/185), and spray-on repellents with DEET at 22.16% (41/185). This indicated a level of familiarity with different types of repellent products. However, significant knowledge gaps were identified, particularly regarding the safe use of repellents among vulnerable populations. Only 38.4% of respondents knew that repellents could be used by children under five, and 35.7% recognized their safety for pregnant women. These gaps were concerning, as they could lead to underutilization of repellents among populations most at risk of severe malaria outcomes. This reflects findings in other studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa, where safety concerns, coupled with misinformation, contributed to low adoption rates [35] (Table 1). The majority of participants (59.5%) held positive attitudes towards mosquito repellents, with 55.7% believing that they are safe to use. This is encouraging as positive attitudes are a critical driver of behaviour change. However, concerns about affordability and accessibility were prominent, with only 40.5% of the respondents finding repellents affordable and 27.6% considering them readily available. These findings echo studies from other low-resource settings, where cost and availability hindered repellent adoption even when attitudes were generally favourable [16, 18, 36]. Additionally, a considerable preference for natural repellents over synthetic ones emerged, driven by worries about chemical exposure and a desire for environmentally friendly options, which can inform product development. This preference could be leveraged to promote the use of plant-based repellents, which are often more culturally acceptable and perceived as safer, especially for vulnerable groups, as other studies in malaria endemic areas found [37, 38] (Table 4). This preference was found not only to be unique to Uganda, but other similar studies from Ghana and Tanzania also reported rising demand for plant-based repellents, largely due to fears about the long-term health effects of chemical-based products [6, 8, 39] (Table 5). Many participants recognized the effectiveness of mosquito repellents in preventing malaria, although a significant number expressed uncertainty, highlighting the need to address misconceptions and promote accurate information. While there was moderate confidence in the safety of these products, concerns about potential side effects, such as skin reactions and allergies, indicated the importance of providing clear guidance on safe usage. Moreover, issues related to the affordability and availability of mosquito repellents were frequently mentioned, illustrating the need for government intervention. To address these challenges, integrating mosquito repellents into national malaria prevention programmes could significantly increase access, particularly for low-income and high-risk populations. Some African countries, like Madagascar and Nigeria have successfully piloted repellent distribution through community health initiatives, demonstrating increased uptake when repellent is subsidized or bundled with existing malaria prevention tools [36, 40, 41]. Uganda could explore similar approaches, such as including repellents in maternal and child health programmes, where their adoption could be targeted toward the most vulnerable groups. In addition, partnerships between the government and the private sector could ensure that both natural and synthetic repellents are available in local markets at affordable prices. A related study from Kenya showed that removing import taxes on essential malaria prevention commodities significantly reduced costs for consumers (15–20%), leading to higher adoption rates, which could, in the same way, be applied in Uganda and other countries, making them a viable strategy [42].

The study revealed a complex relationship between knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding mosquito repellents among participants. While a notable 63.8% demonstrated a high-level understanding of malaria prevention, and 59.5% exhibited positive attitudes towards such measures, the actual use of mosquito repellents tells a different story. Although 77.3% participants reported ever using repellents, a concerningly low 8.2% indicated that very few participants used these products consistently. This disparity presents a troubling paradox that having good knowledge and maintaining good attitudes does not necessarily lead to effective practice and behaviour change. This has been seen in other interventional implementations, like the use of condoms for HIV/AIDS prevention, where people had good knowledge about the use of condoms, but with poor practices, and health care professionals' knowledge about the electronic tool, but with poor use of it [43, 44]. This low, inconsistent use of mosquito repellents highlights a critical public health implication, emphasizing the need for comprehensive behavior change strategies that account for social norms, accessibility, and personal attitudes. This extreme contrast between awareness and actual behaviour reflected the practical barrier that limited repellent adoption in Uganda. Similar studies were done in Myanmar and Ethiopia, where knowledge and willingness to use repellents were found not to always translate into consistent practice due to economic, cultural, and educational level constraints [28]. The timing and context of repellent use further illustrate the gap between awareness and practice. Most participants in this study (95.1%) suggested that they perceived malaria transmission as an exclusively nighttime, indoor risk. However, this perception is increasingly outdated as evidence from recent entomological studies in Uganda and across Africa indicates that malaria-carrying mosquitoes are adapting their biting behaviours with more species now biting outdoors and during the early evenings and morning hours [14, 35]. This shift necessitates a paradigm change in malaria prevention strategies, moving beyond the traditional reliance on bed nets and IRS to interventions that offer protection in multiple settings, including outdoor environments. The most common methods of repellent application were spraying (39.2%) and applying on the skin (32.2%). While these methods are effective, the low frequency of use and inconsistent application practices suggest that many households may not be maximizing the protective benefits of repellents. This highlighted the need for education on proper application techniques and the importance of consistent use, particularly during peak mosquito activity periods. The finding that very few participants (8.2%) had ever used repellents consistently suggested that barriers or challenges may hinder the regular use of mosquito repellents among the population.

From the qualitative findings, participants raised concerns about several barriers to the usage of mosquito repellents. These included the scarcity of mosquito repellents in the market and the high costs of the available ones, indicating challenges in accessibility and affordability, respectively. This finding was consistent with studies from Nigeria and Ghana, where repellent affordability was identified as a major hindrance to widespread use [6, 20]. Understanding these barriers is crucial for developing targeted interventions to promote consistent and effective use of mosquito repellents. Consequently, the fact that 89.2% of participants reported using mosquito repellents during nighttime highlighted that those participants had a good understanding of the importance of using repellents during peak mosquito biting times, which can contribute to more effective prevention of malaria. This study also found that the majority, 90.3% (167/185) of the participants would recommend other people to use mosquito repellents. The high recommendation of mosquito repellents to others indicated an increased level of satisfaction and belief in the effectiveness of these products. This positive endorsement can contribute to increased acceptance and uptake of repellents among other community members. Studies in Tanzania and Malawi have shown that when repellents are integrated into community health programmes and made available through local vendors, uptake improves significantly [45, 46]. Uganda could benefit from similar distribution models, ensuring that repellents are stocked in health centres, supermarkets, and local drug shops alongside other malaria prevention products like bed nets and insecticides.

Several factors were found to influence the non-use of mosquito repellents, including age, education level, household size, and attitudes towards repellents. Older participants (60 years and above) were significantly more likely to have never used the repellents, with an adjusted prevalence ratio (APR) of 5.67. This suggests that older individuals could have been less aware of repellents, have entrenched habits that prioritize traditional methods over modern interventions, or faced barriers related to accessibility and affordability. Similar trends were observed in studies from Tanzania and Ghana, where older adults were less likely to adopt new malaria prevention measures unless they were directly engaged through targeted community health programmes [4, 47]. This underscores the need for tailored health promotion strategies that specifically target older populations, ensuring that they are not left behind. Education level also emerged as a significant determinant of repellent use. The study found that household heads with a tertiary education were 58% more likely to use mosquito repellents compared to those with no formal education. This aligns with findings from previous research indicating that higher education levels are positively associated with better health-seeking behaviours, including the adoption of preventive measures [18, 48]. However, given that Uganda also has a substantial portion of its population with limited formal education, relying solely on literacy-based approaches for repellent promotion may be ineffective [49]. Instead, community-based education efforts such as visual demonstrations, radio campaigns and peer-led interventions could play a crucial role in bridging this gap and ensuring that knowledge translates into practice. Households (8–10 members) were 6.4 times more likely to have never used repellents. This could have been likely due to the financial burden associated with protecting multiple family members, as repellent costs increase exponentially with household size. In low-resource settings, households with limited income often prioritize essentials such as food and school fees over malaria prevention products, particularly those that require repeated purchases. This highlights the urgent need for cost-reduction strategies, including subsidized repellent distribution programmes or government-led bulk purchasing initiatives that could lower prices for consumers. Countries such as Kenya and Rwanda have successfully implemented malaria prevention subsidies for bed nets and insecticides, leading to increased adoption rates [15, 39]. A similar approach could be applied to mosquito repellents in Uganda, ensuring that large families who are at higher risk of malaria due to shared living spaces can afford effective protection. Negative attitudes towards repellents also played a major role in determining non-use. The study found that individuals with negative perceptions of repellents were three times more likely to have never used them. This finding is consistent with research from Malawi and Nigeria, where scepticism about repellent effectiveness, concerns about potential side effects and general preferences for other malaria prevention methods contributed to low uptake [12, 47, 48]. Addressing these negative perceptions requires a comprehensive communication strategy that emphasizes the proven benefits of repellents while dispelling myths about their safety and effectiveness. While natural repellents are often perceived as a safer alternative, some participants pointed out that they typically require more frequent application, making them less practical for long-term use. This presents an opportunity for innovation in product formulation, with researchers and manufacturers working to develop longer-lasting, more user-friendly repellent options tailored to local preferences. To translate these findings into actionable policy, the study suggests a 3-tier framework, which was conceptualized based on identified gaps in knowledge, cost and accessibility, and policy [50]. First, to address affordability, the model calls for government-led subsidies to reduce the cost of repellents, a crucial step given that 36.8% of participants cited cost as a barrier. Second, targeted community education and sensitization campaigns are needed to close knowledge gaps, particularly since only 38.4% of participants knew repellents were safe for children under five years and pregnant women. Finally, the model recommends the formal policy integration of repellents into the NMCP’s strategic documents, as a staggering 91% of the participants were unaware of any existing regulations governing their use. This framework bridges the study's KAP findings with scalable interventions aligning with Uganda's health system structures and global best practices for malaria prevention [41].

Strengths and limitations

In terms of strengths, a mixed methods approach was applied, which enhanced the study's robustness. This mixed methods approach contributed to the validity of the findings, as it allowed for triangulation of data and a richer contextual analysis. For example, the qualitative data enabled us to explain the numbers that were obtained from the survey. During data analysis, traditional statistical approaches were employed, specifically Poisson regression, which provides direct estimates of adjusted prevalence ratios (APR), which were more interpretable and less prone to overestimation than odds ratios (OR) produced by logistic regression. Another strength was that this study was the first one in Uganda to exclusively study KAP on the use of mosquito repellents for malaria prevention, which makes it crucial in informing policy.

The study faced limitations; first, this was a time-constrained project (three months) for the corresponding authors' undergraduate degree at Makerere University, Uganda, which itself limited the depth of exploration on this important subject. Second, the study had a small sample size, which could have limited the statistical power of this study. Third, key informants were not interviewed; people directly in charge and responsible for repellent use and regulation in Uganda. For example, the officials from the national malaria control programme in the Ministry of Health and the national drug authority could have provided more insights into the knowledge levels. This study was conducted in a small geographical area (a ward), which limits the generalizability of the findings to the entire country or the whole of sub-Saharan Africa, where malaria is prevalent. Moving forward, conducting a similar study but in a wider setting would improve the study's vigor. Additionally, recall bias was a concern, as the study relied on participants’ memories of past behaviours, which could result in inaccuracies, particularly for irregular practices. Missing out on the sub-groups analysis was another limitation, which, when carried out, would have informed us more about the mosquito repellents. Future studies should consider this for better and more representative results.

Conclusion

While awareness and attitudes towards repellents were generally positive, usage was low due to high costs, limited availability, and inadequate government support. A significant association was found between age, education, household size, and repellent use. Qualitative data showed a preference for natural repellents. To bridge the knowledge-practice gaps, this study proposes a 3-tier framework for actionable policy, including government subsidies for affordable repellents, targeted community education on safety for children and pregnant women, and formal integration of repellents into the NMCP's strategic documents to enhance malaria prevention efforts in Uganda.
