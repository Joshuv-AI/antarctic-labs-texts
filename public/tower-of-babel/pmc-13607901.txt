Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion beyond radiculopathy: Improvement in vertebral artery flow and dizziness relief
Authors: Youssef Ahdi Sawiris, Mahmoud Mohammed Ragheb, Ahmed Samir Barakat, Wael Mohammed Tawfik Koptan, Yasser Hassan ElMiligui, Ahmed Maher Sultan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is widely used for cervical disc disease, primarily for neural decompression. However, its potential vascular effects – particularly on vertebral artery hemodynamics and cervicogenic dizziness – remain underexplored.Objective:The objective of this study was to evaluate whether ACDF improves vertebral artery flow and alleviates dizziness symptoms associated with impaired posterior circulation perfusion.Methodology:A prospective case series of 40 patients with cervical radiculopathy or myelopathy underwent ACDF with polyetheretherketone (PEEK) cages. Vertebral artery peak systolic velocity (PSV) was assessed preoperatively and at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively using duplex ultrasonography. Functional outcomes were measured with the Visual Analog Scale for neck and arm pain, the Neck Disability Index, and patient-reported dizziness. Radiographic fusion was confirmed at serial follow-up.Results:PSV decreased significantly, indicating improved vertebral artery flow: right side from 48.85 ± 12.71 to 39.0 ± 7.45 and left side from 48.20 ± 12.02 to 41.54 ± 7.56 (both P < 0.001). Symptomatic dizziness also improved in parallel. Fusion was achieved in 92.5% by 12 months, with minor complications in 12.5%.Conclusion:ACDF may alleviate both neural compression and extrinsic vertebral artery compromise, particularly in younger patients. Given the absence of a control group and reliance on surrogate vascular measures, these results should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

INTRODUCTION

Cervical spondylosis is a common degenerative condition of the spine and remains the leading cause of nontraumatic cervical myelopathy in older adults. Patients typically present with a combination of neck pain (NP), radiculopathy, or signs of spinal cord compression, which in progressive cases necessitate surgical intervention. Among the available surgical options, anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is one of the most frequently performed procedures, offering reliable decompression of the neural elements and favorable long-term functional outcomes.[12]

While the neurological consequences of cervical spondylosis are well established, less attention has been given to its vascular implications.[3] Vertebral artery stenosis is a clinically important but often underrecognized condition, most commonly affecting the proximal segment of the artery.[4] It is implicated in up to 20% of posterior circulation ischemic strokes and may arise from atherosclerosis, arterial dissection, congenital anomalies, or extrinsic compression by adjacent osseous and soft tissue structures.[56] Patients with symptomatic vertebral artery stenosis can present with a wide range of posterior circulation symptoms, including vertigo, dizziness, visual disturbances, syncope, and ataxia, which can significantly impair daily activities and quality of life.[78]

Cervicogenic dizziness, in particular, is frequently overlooked in patients with cervical degenerative disease.[9] Although its exact mechanisms remain debated, disturbed cervical proprioception and impaired vertebrobasilar circulation are thought to contribute. Importantly, some patients report improvement in dizziness following ACDF, suggesting that neural decompression and restoration of cervical alignment may also enhance vertebral artery hemodynamics. Despite these observations, there is limited systematic evidence evaluating changes in vertebral artery flow after ACDF and their relationship to symptom relief.[1011]

The present study aims to address this gap by prospectively assessing both neurological and vascular outcomes in patients undergoing ACDF. Specifically, we evaluated changes in vertebral artery flow using duplex ultrasonography alongside clinical measures of pain, disability, and dizziness across a 12-month follow-up. By exploring this dual effect, our study highlights a potential vascular benefit of ACDF that extends beyond its well-established neurological role.

METHODOLOGY

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board/Ethics Committee of our institute. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to enrollment.

Materials and Methods

This prospective study was conducted on patients diagnosed with cervical spondylosis and indicated for ACDF.

Patient selection

Inclusion criteria were adult patients presenting with symptomatic cervical spondylosis, confirmed by radiological imaging, and demonstrating either radiculopathy, myelopathy, or significant NP refractory to conservative treatment. Patients with a history of previous cervical spine surgery, traumatic cervical injuries, intracranial vascular pathology, or systemic conditions affecting vascular flow were excluded to minimize confounding factors.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia by the same surgical team using a standard anterior cervical approach. Following discectomy, neural decompression was achieved, and interbody fusion was performed using a cage with or without plating, depending on intraoperative requirements. The primary surgical aim was adequate decompression of the neural elements and restoration of cervical alignment.

Clinical evaluation

Clinical assessment was carried out at baseline (preoperatively) and subsequently at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. Pain was evaluated using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for both NP and radicular upper limb pain (ULP). Functional disability was assessed with the Neck Disability Index (NDI), a validated tool adapted from the Oswestry Low Back Pain Disability Index.[1213] In addition, symptoms of cervicogenic dizziness and manifestations potentially related to vertebral artery stenosis were documented as patient-reported symptoms using VAS-based assessment, without application of dedicated vestibular scoring systems.

Radiological and vascular assessment

All patients underwent preoperative and postoperative duplex ultrasonography of the vertebral arteries, with particular attention to peak systolic velocity (PSV) measurements. Vertebral artery duplex ultrasonography was selected due to its noninvasive nature, availability, and suitability for repeated follow-up assessments. PSV was used as a surrogate hemodynamic parameter potentially reflecting changes in arterial compression. Advanced vascular imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance (MR) angiography were not routinely performed. Examinations were performed by the same experienced radiologist to ensure consistency and reduce interobserver variability. PSV was recorded for both right and left vertebral arteries, and comparisons were made across follow-up intervals to determine changes over time.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software (version XX; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Paired t-tests and repeated measures analysis of variance were used to assess changes in continuous variables over time. Associations between PSV improvement and demographic factors such as age, sex, and number of affected levels were evaluated using Chi-square tests and independent t-tests where appropriate. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Patient demographics and baseline characteristics

A total of 40 patients (26 females, 14 males; mean age: 42.55 ± 6.5 years, range: 30–60) were included [Table 1]. Twelve patients (30%) were younger than 40 years, while 28 (70%) were 40 years or older. Twenty-eight patients (70%) had single-level disease, 8 (20%) had two levels affected, and 4 (10%) had three levels. The most commonly involved levels were C5–6 (50%) and C4–5 (45%).

Clinical outcomes

Neck and arm pain improved significantly following ACDF [Table 2 and Figure 1]. The mean VAS for NP decreased from 5.15 ± 0.98 preoperatively to 1.43 ± 0.46 at 12 months (P < 0.001). Similarly, ULP improved from 6.33 ± 1.37 to 1.54 ± 0.45 at 12 months (P < 0.001). The NDI declined from 33.53 ± 6.69 preoperatively to 12.68 ± 2.68 at 12 months (P < 0.001).

Vascular outcomes

Vertebral artery PSV showed significant postoperative reduction [Table 2 and Figure 2]. Right PSV decreased from 48.85 ± 12.71 cm/s preoperatively to 39.00 ± 7.45 cm/s at 12 months (P < 0.001). Left PSV decreased from 48.20 ± 12.02 to 41.54 ± 7.56 cm/s at 12 months (P < 0.001). These hemodynamic improvements paralleled the clinical relief of vertigo and gait disturbance reported by several patients.

Radiological fusion and complications

Radiographic fusion was achieved in 92.5% of patients at 12 months [Table 5]. Ten patients (25%) fused at 3 months, 34 (85%) at 6 months, and 37 (92.5%) at 12 months. Three patients (7.5%) developed nonunion at 1 year.

Radiological fusion and complications

Perioperative complications included dysphagia in 3 patients (7.5%), hematoma in 1 (2.5%), recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy in 1 (2.5%), wound infection in 1 (2.5%), and adjacent segment syndrome in 2 (5%). No cases of vertebral artery injury or stroke occurred.

DISCUSSION

The present study evaluated both clinical and vascular outcomes following ACDF in patients with symptomatic cervical spondylosis. In addition to confirming the well-established benefits of ACDF in reducing pain and disability,[14] our findings provide evidence that the procedure can also enhance vertebral artery hemodynamics, particularly through improvements in PSV. This dual neural and vascular effect underscores the multifactorial benefits of ACDF and expands the scope of its therapeutic implications.[15]

Consistent with previous reports, patients in this study experienced significant reductions in NP, radicular ULP, and disability as measured by the NDI. Improvements were observed as early as the 1st postoperative month and were sustained throughout the 12-month follow-up period. These findings align with earlier studies that have established ACDF as an effective intervention for pain relief, functional recovery, and quality-of-life improvement in cervical spondylosis.[1415] The durability of these benefits highlights the role of ACDF not only in neural decompression but also in restoring cervical alignment and stability, which together contribute to long-term symptomatic relief.[6]

A novel aspect of this work is the systematic assessment of vertebral artery flow dynamics before and after surgery. Three-fourths of the patients demonstrated postoperative improvement in PSV, suggesting that decompression of cervical structures may alleviate extrinsic compression or distortion of the vertebral artery.[16] The association between cervical spondylosis and vascular compromise has been reported in isolated case studies and smaller cohorts,[171819] but few studies have incorporated duplex ultrasonography into routine postoperative evaluation. Our findings provide supportive evidence that ACDF may have vascular as well as neurological effects.

Age was a significant factor in predicting vascular improvement. All patients younger than 40 years showed measurable PSV improvement, compared to only 64.3% of those older than 40 years. This disparity may reflect age-related differences in arterial elasticity, vascular remodeling, and the extent of underlying atherosclerotic disease.[20] Younger patients are less likely to have advanced vascular calcification or diffuse atherosclerotic changes, making them more responsive to mechanical decompression. This observation suggests that early surgical intervention may not only improve neurological symptoms but may also prevent or mitigate vascular compromise, particularly in younger populations.[212223]

Sex did not significantly influence postoperative vascular outcomes, with both males and females demonstrating comparable rates of PSV improvement.[24] Similarly, the number of cervical levels involved did not significantly alter the likelihood of vascular recovery. Although patients with three-level involvement all demonstrated improvement, the sample size in this subgroup was small, limiting statistical interpretation. Nonetheless, these findings suggest that vascular recovery is less dependent on anatomical disease extent and more closely tied to patient-specific vascular resilience and age-related factors.[252627]

The relationship between cervical spine pathology and vertebral artery stenosis has gained increasing attention, particularly in cases of cervicogenic dizziness and posterior circulation ischemic events. Prior studies have shown that extrinsic compression of the vertebral artery can result from osteophytes, disc protrusion, or malalignment. However, the majority of literature has been descriptive, focusing on pathophysiology rather than outcomes of surgical intervention.[28] Our study contributes to bridging this gap by demonstrating that ACDF, beyond its neural benefits, can improve vertebral artery flow and potentially reduce symptoms attributable to vertebrobasilar insufficiency.

The novelty of this study lies in integrating vascular assessment into the evaluation of ACDF outcomes. While pain reduction and functional improvement are expected surgical endpoints, the demonstration of improved vertebral artery hemodynamics offers a new dimension to understanding the benefits of ACDF. This may be particularly relevant in patients presenting with cervicogenic dizziness or posterior circulation symptoms, where vertebral artery stenosis may be a contributing factor. By showing that ACDF can positively impact both neural and vascular domains, this work underscores the importance of a comprehensive preoperative and postoperative assessment strategy.

While postoperative reduction in vertebral artery PSV was observed in a majority of patients, PSV should not be interpreted as a direct measure of flow volume. Instead, reduced PSV may reflect relief of extrinsic arterial compression or altered vessel geometry following decompression.

Age-related differences in vascular response were observed, with younger patients demonstrating more consistent PSV improvement. This may be explained by greater arterial compliance and a lower burden of atherosclerotic disease, making mechanical decompression more effective in this group.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The lack of a control group precludes causal inference, dizziness assessment was subjective, and potential confounders such as blood pressure and hydration were not formally controlled. Inclusion of patients with both cervical radiculopathy and myelopathy introduces clinical heterogeneity, which may influence symptom perception and vascular response, and limits disease-specific inference.

Additionally, the sample size, though adequate for detecting clinical significance, was relatively small for subgroup analyses, particularly in patients with three-level disease. Duplex ultrasonography, while noninvasive and reliable, may not fully capture the complexity of vertebral artery flow dynamics compared to advanced imaging modalities such as CT or MR angiography. The study was limited to a 12-month follow-up period; longer-term evaluation is needed to determine whether vascular improvements are sustained and whether they translate into reduced risk of posterior circulation ischemic events.

CONCLUSION

ACDF is associated with sustained improvement in pain and disability and may contribute to favorable changes in vertebral artery hemodynamics and dizziness symptoms. These findings expand the understanding of ACDF beyond neural decompression alone. However, given the study design and limitations, the results should be interpreted as exploratory, supporting the need for controlled studies incorporating validated vascular and vestibular endpoints.
