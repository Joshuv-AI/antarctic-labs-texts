Effects of Ferrosilicon Fineness on the Multiphase Flow Field and Separation Performance of Dense Medium Cyclones for Low-Grade Ores
Authors: Shuli Wang, Jun Wang, Guanzhou Qiu, Li Shen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
As natural resources continue to be exploited, dense medium cyclones (DMCs) are increasingly utilized for the preconcentration of low-grade ores to meet the demands for higher feed grade, increased processing capacity, and reduced energy consumption. However, determining the optimal fineness of ferrosilicon remains ambiguous for different types of ores and is often described as more of an art than a science. This paper investigates the subtle effects of ferrosilicon fineness on flow field characteristics, medium classification, and the ore separation process using a validated numerical approach, which integrates a two-fluid model, a turbulence dispersion model, and a discrete phase model. The results reveal that, under consistent operating conditions, increasing the ferrosilicon fineness enhances the tangential velocity and pressure drop while having minimal impact on axial velocity and water split. The ferrosilicon fineness also influences the distribution of the turbulence field, and in combination with the changes in the particle size distribution (PSD) itself, it modifies the characteristics of the density field. This results in a reduction in the density difference between the overflow and underflow with finer ferrosilicon particles, thereby lowering the cut density but improving the separation accuracy. Moreover, the residence time of particles with different densities in the DMC also varies significantly with changes in separation density. The study underscores that even minor changes in ferrosilicon fineness can impact separation performance, highlighting the need for careful consideration of this factor in process design and medium recovery.

Introduction

As mining resources are increasingly developed, high-quality and easily accessible deposits are becoming increasingly scarce, leading to more challenging and costly extraction processes. For example, China stands as one of the world’s largest producers and consumers of mineral resources, with a rich reserve of strategic metal minerals that are, however, characterized by being “low grade, fine grain, and complex symbiosis”.1 Traditional beneficiation methods, such as full grinding and flotation, are energy-intensive and generate abundant fine tailings, posing significant environmental2 and economic challenges.3,4 Hence, the preselection (or preconcentration) of ores before grinding has emerged as a critical strategy to address these issues by reducing the load on subsequent processes and enhancing overall mineral processing efficiency.5 This approach also allows for the utilization of discarded coarse gangue minerals as construction materials, promoting resource efficiency and sustainability.6 Among various preselection methods7−9 used in mineral processing, dense medium separation (DMS) has been widely employed in industries like coal,10 phosphate,11 diamond,12,13 lithium,14 and iron15 ores, due to its high adaptability and efficiency. In DMS, the ore-medium mixture is fed into separation vessels where separation occurs based on the relative densities of medium and ores. These vessels can be categorized as either static, relying on gravity as the primary driving force, or dynamic, utilizing centrifugal forces. The dense medium cyclone (DMC), patented in 1942 by the Dutch State Mines, is a typical piece of dynamic separation vessel. Its geometric structure is just like a hydrocyclone but involves a more complex separation process due to the heterogeneous suspension of water and solids used as the dense medium.16 Subject to the centrifugal force, the DMC involves both the classification of dense medium particles and the separation of ore particles within a nonuniform viscosity-density field, with interactive effects between these processes.17 Therefore, effective DMC operation highly depends on matching the medium properties with ore characteristics.

Over the years, heterogeneous suspensions have been created using materials such as sand, galena, magnetite, and milled/atomized ferrosilicon, with the latter two being most widely used for low-density and high-density separation, respectively.18 Research by institutions such as JKMRC19,20 and PESCO16 has shown that these suspensions are unstable and exhibit non-Newtonian fluid characteristics, including yield stress and shear-thinning behaviors. The stability and rheology of these suspensions are oppositely affected by the concentration, fineness, density, and shape of suspended solids, as well as contaminant content and shear rate. For instance, increasing the density or fineness of DMS powder can significantly improve suspension stability but also increase apparent viscosity and flow resistance. Compared to milled ferrosilicon, atomized ferrosilicon has higher sphericity, and lower viscosity but poorer stability.21 In practice, an unstable or overly viscous medium due to improper PSD can lead to severe efficiency losses. Therefore, maintaining a balance between the stability and viscosity of the dense medium is crucial, especially for high-density separation.22 International suppliers offer various types and fineness of DMS powders, like 75D, 270D, Cyclone 40, etc., to meet the requirements of different operational requirements. However, this does not imply a fixed recommended fineness for a specific ore, and the selection remains a complex blend of art and science.23 Physical experiments can help determine the optimal type of ferrosilicon, but setting up and calibrating the test apparatus, as well as recycling the medium, is extremely labor- and resource-intensive. These experiments yield macroscopic results highly dependent on the properties of the ore, lacking insights into the internal flow field, making the conclusions often lack general applicability. Consequently, those empirical models developed by the Dutch State Mines,24 Collins,25 Davis,26 and Wood27 are often limited to coal or specific ores.

In recent years, numerical modeling has become an essential tool for enhancing our fundamental understanding of fluid–solid flows in recent years. For DMCs, simulation accuracy predominantly hinges on the selection of the turbulence model and multiphase flow model.28 Due to the inherent anisotropy of turbulence within a DMC, the choice often falls between the Reynolds stress model (RSM) and the large eddy simulation (LES), with the latter demonstrating superior predictive capabilities for fluctuating velocities and turbulence dispersion effects.29 For the complex water–air–ferrosilicon–ore multiphase flow, many modeling approaches are available and usually involve several steps. For the simulation of dense medium flow, the two-fluid model (TFM) stands out as the most comprehensive Eulerian-Eulerian model suitable for varying solid concentrations,30 but its computational demands become quite pronounced when dealing with a broad PSD. Hence, a more prevalent alternative in the literature is a simplified TFM, the algebraic slip mixture (ASM) model.31,32 While the ASM model33 is generally used to simulate the dense medium flow field, few studies34 have used it to simulate the separation of ore particles, as they are typically in millimeter scale and the basic assumption of slip velocity in ASM is not suitable. It is important to note that in DMCs, the concentration of solid particles is very high, especially for metallic ores, and the effects of collision and friction between particles are significant. Using the original ASM combined with a simplified kinetic theory for granular flow (KTGF) makes it challenging to produce meaningful results.35 Therefore, previous studies31,35−38 often use an empirical viscosity model to correct medium viscosity. For example, Wang et al.31 used a modified Ishii model with a fixed correction factor (3.8), which worked well for coal beneficiation. However, by checking the original study39 involving viscosity measurements, ferrosilicon powder was used to make suspensions, whereas coal beneficiation typically uses magnetite as the dense medium, making the model’s applicability questionable. Narasimha et al.40,41 also used an empirical viscosity equation calibrated by fine fractions and the Saffman-Mei42 lift force model to successfully simulate the density field in a DMC, achieving good agreement with Gamma-Ray test results.43 Nevertheless, the above method assumes that the dense medium is a Newtonian fluid, which is also debatable.35

As ore particles are typically on a millimeter scale, the discrete element method (DEM) is more appropriate than TFM. It simulates the separation process by tracking the motion of individual particles according to Newton’s laws of motion, which combines the strengths of CFD and DEM to provide a comprehensive understanding of complex multiphase systems. To enhance model robustness and reduce computational load, coarse-grained DEM (CG-DEM)32 or Lagrangian particle tracking (LPT)44 models are more widely used. Scholars have employed these models to investigate the impact of various design parameters,45 operating conditions,46 and feed properties.47,48 Regarding the influence of ore PSD, studies by Chen et al.49,50 and Chu et al.47,51 explored the performance of DMCs when handling coal particles of varying sizes and densities. Their findings indicated that both particle and medium flows exhibit sensitivity to the ore PSD, as particles of different sizes follow distinct trajectories, significantly altering bulk particle-fluid interactions. However, the effect of ferrosilicon PSD was not considered in the above studies.

This paper aims to address these limitations by exploring the intricate relationship between ferrosilicon fineness, flow field characteristics, and separation performance in DMCs. First, experimental trials were conducted on a carefully designed DMC setup using milled ferrosilicon. The resulting data serve as a basis for validating and refining numerical models. The Eulerian-Eulerian framework enhanced by a turbulent dispersion model (TDM) was utilized to accommodate a wider range of ferrosilicon PSD variations. The LPT model was used to track the trajectories of different ore particles. This approach provides valuable insights into flow patterns, ferrosilicon segregation, and underlying mechanisms within the DMC.

Methodology

Experimental Work

This section provides a detailed description of the laboratory test rig’s construction, material properties, and operating conditions used in this study.

Test Rig

The physical experiments were designed and carried out in the semi-industrial laboratory of Weihai Haiwang Hydrocyclone Co., Ltd. Figure 1 shows the schematic diagram and implementation of the laboratory test rig, which consists of a measurement and control system, a feed system, and a separation system. The feed flow rate is controlled by a KFJ-35 centrifugal pump and measured by an MGG-50 magnetic flowmeter produced by Azbil Corporation. The feed pressure is measured by a Y-100 pressure transmitter. The slurry is prepared and maintained in a homemade 1200L mechanical stirring tank equipped with a frequency converter. The signals from all these electronic devices are brought together into an electric cabinet and controlled by an embedded micro-PC. A bypass pipe was mounted on the main pipe to ensure pulp circulation and feed sampling. The test platform contains a set of two-product Φ250 mm DMC and a set of three-product DMC, while only the former is used in the current study to investigate the performance of water–air two-phase flow and medium segregation.

Properties of Dense Medium

The separation of low-grade ores usually requires ferrosilicon with a higher density as the heavy medium. Similar to hydrocyclones or cyclones,52,53 particle shape and particle size play an important role in the stability/viscosity of ferrosilicon suspensions. Physical tests used 270D ferrosilicon with a silicon content of 14–16%, which has proven to provide the best corrosion protection without too great a reduction in particle density.19 The density and particle size of ferrosilicon were measured by using an Anton Paar DMA 4200 M powder density meter and a Malvern 3000 laser particle size analyzer, respectively. The results indicate that the average density of ferrosilicon is 6900 kg/m3, with an error of 15 kg/m3. Practically, the percentage passing 325 mesh (45 μm) sieve is often used to differentiate fineness. The actual ferrosilicon fineness is about 88%, with most ferrosilicon particles falling within the 20–30 μm size range. Test Procedure and Data Collection

The laboratory tests were designed to assess the effects of feed pressure on water–air flow and medium segregation results. A typical test consists of three key steps: material preparation, sampling, and postprocessing.

Material Preparation

Initially, 400 L of tap water was injected into the stirring tank. To examine the medium segregation performance, 560 kg of 270D ferrosilicon powder was added to prepare the dense medium. It is important to note that the computed slurry density may not exactly match the actual feed medium density (ρf); in fact, it tended to be slightly higher. After thorough stirring for 10 min, the centrifugal pump was initiated at a frequency of 30 Hz, and the bypass pipeline was opened to allow for feed sampling. Continuous recirculation ensured that all slurry streams returned to the stirring tank, maintaining consistent feed qualities. Throughout this process, readings from both the pressure transmitter and the electromagnetic flowmeter were transmitted to a remote-control panel.

Sampling

Gradually increase the pump frequency to achieve desired feed pressure readings. Once a quasi-steady state is reached, samples from the underflow, overflow, and feed streams are gathered, with minimal temporal discrepancies. Note that the sampling locations are marked in Figure 1. The sampling mass is approximately 1.5 kg to ensure the representativeness of samples. For each operating condition, three samples were taken in parallel

Postprocessing

The wet mass of each sample was measured and then subjected to filtration, drying, and weighing to establish mass flux ratios. Note that in the following analyses, the medium or particle density is expressed as the relative density (RD), which is defined as the density ratio of medium (ρm) or particle (ρp) to water (ρw). A subset (5–10g) of samples was used to determine the PSD. After computing the mass balance and analyzing the PSDs in the input and output products, the partition curve can be drawn.

For each test, three parallel runs were conducted to obtain an average result, which served as validation data for numerical experiments. Trail tests have shown that the measurements error was less than 3%. Numerical Simulation

Model Description

The internal flow field of the DMC is highly complex, and direct solutions for gas–liquid–solid multiphase flow often encounter convergence difficulties. To address this, the study employs a modeling strategy, as illustrated in Figure 3, to systematically explore the flow field characteristics, ferrosilicon classification, and motion behavior of ore particles in three steps.

Modeling strategy used in this study.

In the first step, the RSM-TFM model is used to obtain a preliminary water–air two-phase flow field, focusing on forming a stable air core. In the second step, ferrosilicon particles of all sizes are introduced, and the RSM-TFM coupled with the TDM is employed to examine the spatial distribution of ferrosilicon particles, thereby characterizing the density field within the DMC. In the final step, based on the time-averaged gas–liquid–solid multiphase flow field, the DPM is used to track the motion behavior of individual ore particles in the slurry, with an emphasis on analyzing their separation performance. Further details regarding these models are provided below.

Multiphase Model

The TFM treats both fluids and solids as interpenetrating continua through the implementation of the volume fraction concept. The equations of continuity and momentum are satisfied individually by each phase, which can be expressed as23where αq, ρq, and symbolize the volume fraction, density, and velocity of the phase q, respectively. In general, q = 1 pertains to water, q = 2 relates to air, and q ≥ 3 refers to ferrosilicon particles.

Turbulence Model

It is well recognized that both RSM and LES can predict the turbulent flow field of cyclones, such as air cyclones, hydrocyclones, and DMC. Despite that the LES gives more accurate predictions on fluctuating velocities and pressure, the RSM shows advantages in memory requirements and computation efficiency as well as good compatibility with other models such as the TFM.52 Hence, the RSM is used to model turbulent flows. model turbulent flows. The equations have been well documented in the literature58,59 and are not repeated here. It should be noted that although the secondary phase volume fraction is comparable to the primary phase in some positions, their velocity differences are not that significant. Hence, a more stable option called the RSM mixture turbulence option is used which shares the same turbulence field for all phases.

Turbulent Dispersion Model

The turbulent dispersion force essentially acts as turbulent diffusion in particle motions, which is crucial for accurately predicting the spatial distribution of ferrosilicon particles, i.e. the density field. In numerical simulations, the immediate momentum transfer between dispersed and continuous phases can be expressed as the sum of the mean drag force and the resulting turbulent dispersion force from the averaging process11where and are the mean phase velocity vectors and Kpq is the interphase exchange coefficient. The second term, Kpq, accounts for the dispersion of the secondary phases due to transport by turbulent fluid motion.

DMCs involve three phases of air, water, and solid, where a distinct interface exists between the air and water phases. As in previous studies33,60 concerning hydrocyclones, the coefficients Kpq used for fluid–fluid and fluid–solid momentum exchanges are determined by the Schiller–Naumann model61 and the Gidaspow model,55 respectively.1213For the turbulent dispersion force, this study applied a model62 based on Favre averaging of the drag term, and the final expression is like14where CTD is a user constant, Dq is the dispersion scalar estimated by turbulent viscosity of the continuous phase, σpq is the Prandtl number, and is the gradient of dispersed phase volume fraction.

Ore particles are relatively coarse, typically a few millimeters—comparable to the grid size. Meanwhile, their sphericity has a significant impact on particle movement. Hence, it seems inappropriate to continue using the TFM to simulate ore separation. In this study, the LPT model is used for ore particle tracking in a fixed dense medium flow field. Note that, when using the TFM, the discrete phase can only interact with the primary phase (here the water phase) rather than the mixture phase. The water density and viscosity in eq 18 must be replaced with mixture properties (density and viscosity) before conducting particle tracking. These mixture properties can be calculated via volume averaging, like the algebraic slip mixture model.

Additional forces include the pressure gradient force, virtual mass force, Saffman lift force, and others. Due to the large pressure gradient along the radial direction, the pressure gradient force should be considered, while other forces are negligible for DMCs. To account for the effect of turbulence dispersion, the discrete random walk model is used to predict the instantaneous velocity along the particle path. The particle is assumed to interact with the fluid phase eddy over the smaller of the eddy lifetime and the eddy crossing time. When this time is reached, a new value for the instantaneous velocity is obtained.

Simulation Conditions

The geometric parameters and computational mesh of Φ250 mm DSM are shown in Figure 4. It assembled a standard 20° cone and an involute feed body. The inclination angle of the axis is 15°. The 3D geometry was created on the Ansys platform using SCDM. The type and quantity of grids determine the calculation speed and precision of numerical tests. In this study, the ICEM meshing software is employed to generate high-quality hexahedral grids using a multiple O-shaped meshing method. This can enhance the orthogonality between the fluid flow direction and mesh surface, thus improving the convergence speed. The grids at the bottom and wall of the vortex finder are locally refined. This ensures that the first layer of grids is in the turbulent logarithmic zone, meaning the Y+ value is between 30 and 300. A growth rate parameter of 1.2 was utilized, indicating that the maximum increase in the size of a neighboring element cannot exceed 20%.64 Grid independence tests were conducted with grid numbers of 100k, 193k, 410k, and 830k, respectively. The tangential and axial velocities at the Z = 0 mm line were used to determine the most appropriate grid scheme, as shown in Figure 5. When the grid size exceeds 400k, further reducing the grid size has a minimal impact on the calculation results. Therefore, the appropriate number of grids for the Φ250 mm DMC is determined to be 400k.

The inlet velocity is set according to the flow rates specified in Table 1. The static pressure at both outlets is maintained at 1 atm, representing ambient atmospheric pressure. Simulations are conducted using a transient solver, with pressure–velocity coupling achieved through the phase-coupled SIMPLE scheme. A fixed time step of 5 × 10–4 s, determined based on the Courant number and calculation speed, is used for all simulations, yielding converged results within 20–30 s of physical time. Results and Discussion

The effects of ferrosilicon fineness are analyzed from three perspectives, including the flow field, the classification of different-sized ferrosilicon particles, and the partitioning of ore particles.

Effects of Ferrosilicon Fineness on Flow Field

Velocity Profile and Strain Rate

Although the density field is the most critical characteristic of the flow field in a DMC, it is fundamentally the velocity profile that dictates the distribution of ferrosilicon particles. Therefore, this section examines the impact of ferrosilicon fineness on tangential and axial velocities and compares them with the flow field in the water–air system, as shown in Figure 7. Two characteristic lines at Z = 0 and Z = −200 mm were selected for comparison. The addition of ferrosilicon significantly reduces the tangential velocity, with variations observed at different axial positions. At the cylinder-cone junction, i.e. line 1, the velocity change outside the quasi-free vortex is minimal, while in other regions, tangential velocity decreases as ferrosilicon fineness reduces. This is because larger particles exhibit poor fluid followability and lower momentum exchange efficiency with the fluid, leading to an overall reduction in tangential velocity. The PSD of ferrosilicon has a negligible effect on axial velocity, with the location of the zero-velocity remaining almost unchanged. The ferrosilicon particle size distribution (PSD) has a similar impact on the velocity profile at line 2. However, throughout the entire radial scope, the tangential velocity decreases as the ferrosilicon gets coarser. This trend is further illustrated in Figure 8, where decreasing ferrosilicon fineness results in a drop in feed pressure, but the water split ratio of the underflow remains relatively stable, aligning with the changes in tangential and axial velocities shown in Figure 7.

For incompressible non-Newtonian fluids, the strain rate or shear rate is crucial for determining apparent viscosity. It has been well-recognized that the dense medium made by ferrosilicon suspension is a typical non-Newtonian fluid. However, when the shear rate exceeds 200 s–1, the medium approximates Newtonian behavior, meaning the apparent viscosity remains constant with the shear rate. Figure 9 presents the distribution of strain rate on the Y–Z plane in DMCs with different ferrosilicon PSDs. The results demonstrate that the strain rate is generally larger than 200 s–1 near the vortex finder, along the whole chamber wall, and in the lower cone section. However, in the majority of the separation space, it mainly lies in the range of 80–120 s–1, which can lead to shear-thinning depending on the local volume fraction and PSD of ferrosilicon in each cell. This phenomenon underscores the superiority of the KTGF oversimpler Newton viscosity models. As the ferrosilicon fineness increases, the shear rate inside the cone increases, corresponding to the variations of the tangential velocity shown in Figure 7.

The results demonstrate that the addition of ferrosilicon significantly alters the turbulence characteristics of the flow field. Maximum values shift from the lower part of the vortex finder in the cylinder section to the lower cone section. This shift suggests that ferrosilicon particles in the lower cone are less likely to segregate by size and instead exhibit improved dispersion. As the fineness of ferrosilicon increases, both the turbulent intensity and turbulent viscosity of the entire flow field rise correspondingly. It is foreseeable that the degree of dispersion of fine particles will also increase.

Density Field

The motion behavior of ore particles depends on the density field characteristics of the DMC, particularly its density and uniformity. Theoretically, a uniform dense medium is beneficial for improving separation accuracy.67 However, different sizes of ferrosilicon particles introduce classification effects within a DMC, resulting in axial and radial density differences. Figure 12 illustrates the density field changes with variations in the ferrosilicon fineness. As the axial position decreases or the radial position increases, the density of the medium gradually rises, peaking at the cone apex. Although increasing ferrosilicon fineness leads to an overall decrease in the density of the separation space, the distribution pattern remains largely unchanged. Notably, as the proportion of fine particles increases, the uniformity of the density field improves, which may enhance ore separation efficiency.

Effects of Ferrosilicon Fineness on Medium Segregation

Medium Partition Curve

Subject to the centrifugal force, ferrosilicon particles will be classified, with the coarser particles reporting to the underflow and the finer particles to the overflow. Figure 14 shows the impact of ferrosilicon fineness on the partition curves of ferrosilicon. As ferrosilicon fineness increases, the partition curve shifts to the left, which means the recovery to underflow of each size of ferrosilicon particles increases. This shift can be attributed to two main factors. First, finer ferrosilicon particles result in larger pressure drops and tangential velocities, which increase the centrifugal force acting on the particles. Second, a reduction in the overall content of coarse particles with finer ferrosilicon diminishes the congestion effect near the spigot, facilitating easier entry of coarse particles into the underflow. However, this does not necessarily translate to a higher overall yield of ferrosilicon in the underflow. To better understand the seemingly contradictory changes in the density field, Figure 16 contrasts the spatial distribution of ferrosilicon particles of different sizes. The concept of enrichment ratio is introduced here to facilitate comparison of their spatial distribution patterns, given their varying percentages in the feed. Different sizes of ferrosilicon particles exhibit distinct motion behaviors in the DMC. Finer particles are more easily carried by the fluid and evenly distributed, while coarser particles tend to concentrate in specific areas, affecting the uniformity of the density field. As the particle size increases to 17 μm, particles start to concentrate in the inner vortex and near the cyclone wall. With a further increase in particle size, there is a notable rise in particle concentration near the wall, while the concentration in the inner vortex decreases. Effects of Ferrosilicon Fineness on Ore Separation

Ore Partition Curve

Low-grade metallic ores and gangue minerals are typically in the range of 2000 and 3000 kg/m3 in density and between 0.5 and 10 mm in size after crushing. LPT simulations were conducted where particles in this size range were injected at the inlet and tracked. Each simulation was repeated 3 times and 800 particles were injected per run. The particles (parcels) reported to the underflow were recorded and used to construct partition curves as a function of particle densities and particle sizes. Figure 19 shows the partition curves so generated using a TFM simulation with a feed RD of 1.94 and the original ferrosilicon PSD.

Ore particle partition curve as a function of ore size.

As shown in Figure 19, for ore particles of different sizes, the partition number shows a gradual increase with the increase in ore particle density. The cut density significantly decreases as the mineral particle size increases. This implies that a larger particle size is beneficial for improving the separation accuracy. The pivot phenomenon, where partition curves for different sizes of ore intersect at a pivot point, has been successfully modeled. The predicted pivot parameters slightly deviate from the feed density, which is a common phenomenon for DMC with high densities.69

Taking 3 mm particles as an example, Figure 20 shows the impact of ferrosilicon particle size composition on the ore partition curves. As the fineness of ferrosilicon increases, the cut density initially decreases slowly and then more rapidly, while the Ecart probable (EP) first decreases and then increases when ferrosilicon fineness exceeds 85%. By comparing Figures 12, 15, and 20, it is evident that the cut density is closely related to the distribution of the density field, particularly aligning with the trend of the density difference between the overflow and underflow. This indicates that a uniform density field formed by finer ferrosilicon promotes high-precision separation, whereas a high-density difference field formed by coarser ferrosilicon can achieve high-density separation even under lower feed densities. It should be noted that the effect of the latter gradually weakens and significantly impacts the separation efficiency of fine ore particles. Additionally, when the ferrosilicon is too fine, the apparent viscosity of the density medium increases, the flow resistance becomes greater, and EP will also increase inevitably. Therefore, optimizing the fineness of ferrosilicon is crucial for improving separation performance, and must be tailored to specific ore and separation conditions.

Effects of ferrosilicon fineness on partition curves of ore particles.

Particle Trajectory

Figures 21 and 22 compare the movement trajectories and residence time distributions of 3 mm ore particles with a RD = 2.5 in different density fields. From Figure 21, it is noticed that with the increase in ferrosilicon fineness, the residence time of particles in the cyclone gradually increases, reaching a maximum under the F90 condition. Examining the trajectories reveals that near-density particles undergo multiple separations in the conical section, which are difficult to separate.44 When the fineness of ferrosilicon is further increased, particles quickly enter the underflow, with residence times approximately equal to the system’s response time, which is simply the quotient of volume and flow rate (1.46 s).

Effects of ferrosilicon fineness on trajectories of ore particles.

Effects of ferrosilicon fineness on the residence time of ore particles.

From Figure 22, it can be observed that in both the overflow (light product) and underflow (heavy product), the residence time of particles initially increases and then decreases with the increase in ore density, reaching a maximum at near-density, approximately 2–3 times the system’s response time. As the fineness of ferrosilicon increases, the residence time of low-density particles in the DMC gradually increases, and the peak residence times of both light and heavy products shift toward lower densities. Conversely, the residence time of high-density particles gradually decreases and approaches the system’s response time. These results are mainly related to the properties of the density field, which corresponds well with the observations in Figure 20.

Conclusions

This study investigates the impact of ferrosilicon fineness on multiphase flow characteristics, ferrosilicon classification, and the overall separation performance of dense medium cyclones (DMCs) through laboratory experiments and numerical simulations based on a TFM-TDM-DPM model. Five different ferrosilicon samples were considered, with their −45 μm contents being 75%, 80%, 85%, 80%, and 95% respectively. The key findings are as follows.

1The TFM-TDM model demonstrates good predictive capabilities for ferrosilicon segregation, with relative errors less than 10% in pressure drop and RD. As ferrosilicon fineness increases, the tangential velocity gradually increases but is still smaller than in the absence of ferrosilicon. The axial velocity is barely affected by the existence of ferrosilicon. Under a constant flow rate, finer ferrosilicon particles cause a higher pressure drop (from 78 to 106 kPa), while having a negligible impact on the Rf, which remains around 15%. In most separation space, the strain rate lies in the range of 80–120 s–1, which results in the slurry retaining non-Newtonian fluid characteristics. The interaction between ferrosilicon particles and flow field causes changes in turbulence characteristics and significantly reduces the medium density in cone section as ferrosilicon fineness increases.
