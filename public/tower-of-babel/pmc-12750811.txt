A comparative 3D finite element analysis of mandibular advancement with clear aligners and twin block appliances
Authors: Muhteber Durmus, Musa Bulut, Yasin Hezenci
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study was to compare the effects of two commonly used functional orthodontic appliances, Twin Block and Mandibular Advancement appliances with clear aligners, on dentoskeletal structures and the temporomandibular joint in individuals with Class II malocclusion using a three-dimensional finite element method.MethodsSeparate three-dimensional finite element models were created for each appliance using ANSYS SpaceClaim software (Version 22.0). The maxillomandibular complex, craniofacial sutures, TMJ structures, and dentition were modeled. 12 N force value for temporalis muscle activity during mandibular advancement was applied to simulate forward mandibular positioning in both models. Displacement and stress distribution values were analyzed in various anatomical regions, including maxillary and mandibular bones, dental arches, and TMJ components.ResultsThe Twin Block appliance produced greater displacement in mandibular incisors and resulted in increased skeletal changes, particularly in the mandibular body and condylar regions. It also caused higher stress levels in the TMJ disk and condyle compared to Mandibular Advancement appliance with clear aligners. Additionally, greater displacement was observed at craniofacial suture points in the Twin Block group.ConclusionsWhile both appliances demonstrated orthopedic and orthodontic effects beneficial for Class II correction, the Twin Block appliance exhibited stronger skeletal influence and more favorable TMJ stress patterns. The MA appliance, however, may offer more controlled dentoalveolar effects and improved patient comfort, making it a practical alternative in cases requiring esthetic and compliance-focused treatment strategies.Keywords: Class II malocclusion, Twin Block, Mandibular advancement, Finite element analysis

Introduction

Class II malocclusion is one of the most frequently encountered skeletal and dental discrepancies in orthodontics, affecting approximately one-third of the population [1]. It is typically characterized by a distal relationship of the mandible relative to the maxilla, often resulting from mandibular retrognathia [1]. This condition may present with varying combinations of dental and skeletal components and requires a comprehensive diagnostic approach to determine the appropriate treatment strategy [2].

Functional orthopedic appliances have long been used to correct skeletal Class II malocclusion by promoting mandibular growth and repositioning, utilizing muscle function and biomechanical forces [3–5]. Among these, the Twin Block appliance is widely accepted due to its ability to provide mandibular advancement and correct the sagittal discrepancy between the jaws. Its design, consisting of upper and lower bite blocks with inclined planes, facilitates forward positioning of the mandible during functional movements, contributing to both skeletal and dentoalveolar adaptations [6].

Despite the effectiveness of traditional appliances, aesthetic and comfort concerns, especially among adolescent and adult patients, have led to the increasing popularity of clear aligner systems [7]. Recent technological advances have introduced clear aligners with integrated mandibular advancement features, offering a more esthetically pleasing and comfortable alternative. These systems are particularly attractive for patients seeking less visible and removable options for Class II correction [8, 9].

The mandibular advancement (MA) feature of Invisalign® (Align Technology, San Jose, California, USA) incorporates bilateral “precision wings” located on the buccal surfaces of the upper and lower aligners. These wings are designed to engage upon occlusion, posturing the mandible forward in a fixed sagittal position. This mechanism aims to mimic the functional orthopedic effects of traditional appliances, such as the Twin Block, while offering improved esthetics and patient comfort associated with clear aligners. In our study, this appliance design was digitally modeled to replicate the functional engagement of the advancement wings at maximal intercuspation, thereby enabling the simulation of force distribution and stress patterns during mandibular advancement [9]. Although early clinical studies [10, 11] have shown promising results in correcting Class II malocclusions with this system, direct biomechanical comparisons with conventional appliances remain limited.

Patient compliance plays a critical role in the success of functional treatments, particularly for removable appliances. Twin Block appliances require consistent wear and patient cooperation, whereas clear aligner systems offer better acceptability due to their esthetics and comfort [11]. However, the force delivery mechanisms and mechanical efficiency of these two systems may differ substantially, potentially influencing their treatment outcomes.

Although clinical studies have investigated the dentoskeletal effects of both appliance types, finite element analysis (FEA) offers a unique advantage by enabling precise biomechanical assessment of stress distribution and displacement within craniofacial structures under controlled, simulated conditions. This computational method allows for detailed comparisons of how different appliances affect anatomical regions such as the maxilla, mandible, teeth, and temporomandibular joint (TMJ) [12].

The TMJ plays a critical role in mandibular advancement therapy, and its biomechanical response can have significant implications for treatment stability and joint health. Moreover, the craniofacial complex as a whole is affected by mandibular repositioning, and evaluating its mechanical behavior can offer a more holistic understanding of treatment effects. To address this gap, the present study incorporates a refined finite element model that includes detailed TMJ structures as well as key craniofacial components.

In the present study, both appliances were modeled to advance the mandible under identical mandibular advancement conditions. This design allows for a direct comparison of the mechanical environment induced by the two appliances under identical mandibular repositioning. By analyzing TMJ loading patterns and craniofacial stress–strain distribution under controlled advancement, this study aims to test the hypothesis that, under equivalent mandibular advancement, Twin Block and MA appliances generate different biomechanical environments in the TMJ and craniofacial complex, which may influence skeletal and dentoalveolar adaptation. By comparing their effects on skeletal displacement, dental movement, TMJ stress, and craniofacial sutures using three-dimensional finite element analysis, this study seeks to provide clinically relevant insight into when each appliance may be more advantageous for Class II correction.

Materials and methods

This study employed a three-dimensional finite element method (3D-FEM) to evaluate and compare the mechanical effects of two commonly used functional orthodontic appliances—the Twin Block and the MA appliance—on the maxillomandibular complex and TMJ. All simulations and modeling were performed using ANSYS SpaceClaim (Version 22.0, Canonsburg, Pennsylvania, USA), a widely recognized software for biomechanical and engineering applications.

To develop accurate anatomical models, cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) data of a representative Class II patient were used. The DICOM data were segmented to isolate the craniofacial bones, including the maxilla, mandible, and cranial base, as well as dentition and relevant soft tissue structures such as the TMJ disk, condyle, articular cartilage, and periodontal ligaments (PDL). These structures were modeled in detail to ensure the mechanical properties and interactions of each tissue could be accurately simulated under load.

The cortical and trabecular bones of both the maxilla and mandible were modeled separately to reflect their distinct biomechanical properties. Similarly, PDL was simulated as a 0.25 mm layer surrounding each tooth root, and was assigned nonlinear, anisotropic properties to best represent its biological behavior. The TMJ components—including the condylar cartilage, disk, and joint capsule were also included in the models, allowing for a comprehensive assessment of joint stress and displacement during mandibular advancement. Based on the literature [13–18] that was accessible, the mechanical parameters of each component were determined (Table 1).

Two distinct appliance models were constructed: one for the Twin Block and one for the MA appliance. The MA appliance was simulated as a pair of custom-made clear aligners fitted with integrated advancement wings (or precision attachments) that guided the mandible forward upon closure (Fig. 1A). The Twin Block appliance was modeled with inclined occlusal bite planes placed in the posterior regions of both arches, allowing mandibular advancement upon occlusion (Fig. 1B). Both appliances were fitted to the same dental arch geometry to standardize comparisons. In the simulation, the mandible was advanced 7 mm anteriorly and 4.5 mm inferiorly to represent a forward postured rest position. In this position, 12 N horizontal force was applied to the posterior fibers of the temporalis muscle based on data from previous biomechanical studies [19], and the appliance was modeled to counteract this force, maintaining the advanced mandibular posture. No additional occlusal bite forces were applied.

This force was used in both appliance models to replicate the muscle activity during mandibular forward positioning. The force vectors were applied symmetrically, and boundary conditions were standardized to restrict unnecessary head movement while allowing physiologic mandibular translation.

Material properties for each modeled structure were obtained from published literature. Cortical bone, trabecular bone, enamel, dentin, PDL, TMJ cartilage, and the disk were each assigned specific elastic moduli, Poisson’s ratios, and density values according to validated biomechanical references [13–18]. The appliances themselves were assigned material characteristics matching polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) for the Twin Block and polyethylene terephthalate glycol (PETG) for the MA appliance aligners [17, 18].

Meshing of the model was carried out with tetrahedral elements using adaptive refinement to ensure accurate results, especially in regions of interest such as the condyle, incisors, and craniofacial sutures. Convergence tests were performed to confirm mesh sensitivity. Contact interactions between different anatomical components (e.g., between condyle and disk, or between aligner and teeth) were defined as frictionless or bonded depending on the clinical reality being modeled.

After the modeling was completed in ANSYS SpaceClaim (Version 22.0, Canonsburg, Pennsylvania, USA) software, the models were mathematically processed and prepared for analysis using ANSYS Workbench (Version 22.0, Canonsburg, Pennsylvania, USA) software. In order to perform the analyses, the mathematical models prepared in ANSYS Workbench were transferred to the LS-DYNA solver.

Post-processing was conducted to evaluate stress distribution (using Von Mises criteria), displacement magnitudes, and strain energy across critical anatomical landmarks. Specific focus was placed on the anterior teeth, mandibular body, maxillary sutures, and the TMJ components. Comparative evaluations were made between the Twin Block and MA simulations, allowing identification of regional differences in biomechanical response to functional mandibular advancement.

Results

Finite element simulations were completed for both the Twin Block and MA appliance models. The simulations enabled a detailed comparison of displacement magnitudes and stress distributions across dental structures, craniofacial bones, and TMJ components under identical loading conditions.

Tooth displacement

In the Twin Block simulation, mandibular incisors exhibited significantly greater proclination and anterior displacement compared to the MA model. The magnitude of displacement in the mandibular anterior region was higher in Twin Block. Both appliances showed distalization of maxillary teeth and mesialization of mandibular teeth; however, these effects were more substantial with the Twin Block appliance (Figs. 2, 3, 4 and Table 2).

Craniofacial suture displacement

The Twin Block appliance induced greater displacement across key craniofacial sutures, including the frontozygomatic, zygomaticomaxillary, and midpalatal regions. Von mises stress in dental structures

Stress distribution analysis in both appliances demonstrated elevated Von Mises stress in the incisor and premolar regions, with the Twin Block group exhibiting higher overall stress levels. Notably, the mandibular incisors in the Twin Block simulation experienced increased tensile and compressive forces, attributed to the forward mandibular positioning induced by the inclined bite blocks (Fig. 6, Table 2).

Both appliances generated stress in the condylar cartilage and articular disk. The Twin Block appliance, however, produced markedly higher stress concentrations, particularly in the posterior region of the condyle and the superior aspect of the articular disk. In contrast, the MA appliance induced measurable but more evenly distributed stress, reflecting a more moderate biomechanical impact on the TMJ structures (Figs. 7, 8 and Table 3).

Mandibular and maxillary bone stress

Cortical and trabecular bone structures exhibited differential responses to each appliance. The Twin Block appliance generated higher Von Mises stress values in both the maxillary and mandibular cortical bone, with increased concentrations in the alveolar processes and the posterior region of the mandibular body. In contrast, the MA appliance produced lower stress magnitudes, with peak values localized to the anterior alveolar ridge (Figs. 9, 10 and Table 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to compare the biomechanical effects of two distinct functional orthodontic appliances—Twin Block and MA—on the maxillomandibular complex and TMJ using three-dimensional finite element modeling. The results revealed clear differences in dental displacement, skeletal adaptation, and TMJ stress distribution, highlighting the unique design philosophies and force-delivery mechanisms of each appliance.

According to our results, both appliances caused more pronounced labial movement at the crown of the lower incisors compared to the root apex. This suggests an increase in the inclination of the lower incisors relative to the mandibular plane. The increased anterior displacement and proclination of the mandibular incisors in the Twin Block model are consistent with previous studies that report strong dentoalveolar effects associated with this appliance [10, 11, 20, 21]. The inclined planes of the Twin Block appliance actively posture the mandible forward, generating increased forces on the anterior teeth. This may explain the higher Von Mises stress values observed in the lower anterior region. In contrast, the MA appliance, with its more passive and distributed force system through aligner trays and advancement wings, provided a more controlled and localized force application, resulting in milder incisor movement.

In a study by Sidlauskas et al., cephalometric analysis was conducted on 34 patients with Class II Division 1 malocclusion to evaluate skeletal and dentoalveolar changes before and after treatment with the Twin Block appliance. All analyses were performed using a consistent reference system. To account for natural growth, longitudinal data from individuals with ideal occlusion, consistent with Bolton standards, were used as the control group. The results showed that approximately 40% of the overjet correction achieved with Twin Block therapy was due to skeletal changes, while the remaining 60% was attributed to dentoalveolar adaptations. Additionally, of the 1 mm distal movement observed in the upper molars, 0.7 mm was reported to be of dentoalveolar origin [22]. In our study, complementing the findings of previous research, we observed that the cusp tips of the maxillary posterior teeth moved more distally than the root apices in both simulations. This movement was more pronounced in the Twin Block appliance compared to the MA appliance. Although both treatment modalities resulted in distalization of the upper posterior teeth through tipping, our analysis indicated that the Twin Block appliance induced greater tipping in the maxillary posterior teeth. Moreover, the more substantial distal movement observed with the Twin Block suggests a more pronounced headgear effect on the maxilla compared to the MA appliance.

In a retrospective study comparing the effects of the MA appliance and the Twin Block appliance, the reduction in the ANB angle was associated with a more pronounced retroclination effect in patients treated with the Twin Block. The greater change in the ANB angle was interpreted as a consequence of this effect. Based on these findings, the authors concluded that the MA appliance may be a more advantageous option for patients presenting with Class II Division 2 malocclusion [23].

The skeletal displacement patterns also differed significantly between the two appliances. The Twin Block appliance induced more anterior-inferior mandibular movement and greater displacement in the maxillary sutures. These findings are consistent with the literature highlighting the orthopedic effectiveness of Twin Block therapy, particularly in growing patients [24–26]. However, even in this FEM-based simulation without consideration of growth, the structural response was more significant with the Twin Block appliance, suggesting that its biomechanical design inherently favors skeletal changes.

Khan et al. investigated and compared the dentoskeletal effects of Twin Block appliance therapy in patients with Class II malocclusion and untreated controls. The study analyzed pre-treatment (T1) and post-treatment (T2) lateral cephalograms of 30 patients treated with the Twin Block appliance alongside 30 untreated Class II control patients. Both groups were evaluated for dentoskeletal changes using 24 angular and linear cephalometric measurements. The findings demonstrated a "headgear effect," characterized by restricted maxillary growth and distalization of the maxillary molars in the Twin Block group [26]. These findings are corroborated by the posterior displacement of the A-point, ANS, and PNS landmarks along the Y-axis observed in our study.

The posterior displacement along the Y-axis supports these findings. In a case report and literature review by Liu et al., a patient with Class II malocclusion treated with the MA appliance was examined. The report highlighted that the MA appliance promotes mandibular growth but does not inhibit maxillary development [27]. Accordingly, Spalj et al. noted that whether extraoral forces—such as those applied by an Activator Headgear—can effectively restrict maxillary growth remains an issue warranting further investigation [28]. Consistent with these observations, our study found less posterior displacement of Point A in the MA appliance scenario compared to the Twin Block appliance.

A and B points are commonly used skeletal landmarks for the maxilla and mandible, but their positions can be affected by the torque of the upper and lower incisors [29]. In our study, we also evaluated craniofacial landmarks as skeletal references. Since our study mainly compares findings, the smaller displacement observed at Point A with the MA appliance can be interpreted alongside Liu et al.’s results [27]. Accordingly, it can be concluded that the skeletal effect of the MA appliance may be less significant than that of the Twin Block appliance.

The observed differences in stress and displacement within the craniofacial sutures possess significant clinical implications. Twin Block therapy elicited greater mechanical effects in the zygomaticomaxillary, frontozygomatic, and midpalatal sutures, suggesting its potential to modulate both transverse and sagittal craniofacial dimensions. This is particularly relevant for patients exhibiting mild maxillary constriction or those requiring orthopedic expansion in conjunction with sagittal correction. Conversely, the MA appliance demonstrated minimal involvement of the sutures, indicative of its predominantly dentoalveolar mechanism of action.

The Twin Block appliance has been shown to stimulate condylar growth, resulting in remodeling of the condyle. Several studies have investigated the effects of the rigid functional Twin Block appliance on condylar growth, particularly in individuals undergoing active growth phases [30, 31]. Research on the MA appliance has indicated that the majority of changes occur at the dentoalveolar level, with skeletal modifications being comparatively minimal and less pronounced than those observed with the Twin Block appliance [23, 32]. Based on these findings, the higher Von Mises stress values observed with the Twin Block appliance may suggest a greater mechanical stimulus that could potentially favor condylar growth, although direct biological remodeling cannot be conclusively inferred from mechanical data alone.

Another study reported in the literature demonstrated that the group treated with the Twin Block appliance exhibited significant anterior displacement of the condylar head compared to untreated control groups. Additionally, bone formation was observed in the posterosuperior region of the condyle, contributing to condylar growth [24]. Similarly, in our study, stress values recorded in the condylar head region for both appliances were elevated, consistent with condylar growth in the posterosuperior direction. Since anterior displacement of the condyle is associated with bone formation in the posterior region, interpretation of similar studies suggests that the higher stress values observed in the condylar head region with the Twin Block appliance, compared to the MA appliance, may indicate greater stimulation of bone formation. Blackham’s study supports the finding that the skeletal effects of the MA appliance do not differ significantly from those of the Twin Block appliance [20]. However, the MA appliance is generally considered more comfortable, less conspicuous, and easier to wear than the Twin Block appliance, which may contribute to improved patient compliance [33].

In the study conducted by Dong et al., the highest Von Mises stress values in the mandibular bone and joint region were reported to occur at the condylar neck [34]. Similarly, another study reported in the literature also identified higher Von Mises stress values at the condylar neck [16]. In both simulations conducted in our study, the maximum Von Mises stress values in the mandible and condyle were localized at the condylar neck, corroborating previous findings. The higher values observed in the Twin Block simulation suggest a greater contribution to condylar bone formation.

These results may assist orthodontists in understanding the importance of preventing impingement in the condyle–glenoid fossa region during Class II malocclusion treatment. Compared to other functional orthodontic appliances used in Class II therapy, the Twin Block appliance appears to reduce pressure on the temporomandibular joint more effectively, thereby potentially promoting new condylar bone formation [13].

In our study, Von Mises stresses in the condylar bone, as well as in the maxillary cortical, maxillary spongious, mandibular cortical, and mandibular spongious bones, were observed at higher values and over larger areas in the Twin Block scenario. This finding is believed to be associated with the greater skeletal effects provided by the Twin Block appliance. Similar studies in the literature also suggest the effectiveness of the MA appliance in treating mild to moderate skeletal mandibular retrusion. From a clinical perspective, these biomechanical differences emphasize the importance of individualized treatment planning. While the Twin Block may be more effective for significant skeletal discrepancies in younger patients, the MA appliance offers improved esthetics, better patient compliance, and a more conservative force system, making it suitable for mild to moderate Class II cases or patients with high esthetic demands. Our study supports the findings of Caruso et al. [23].

The tensile and compressive stresses observed in the condylar neck region occur in a manner analogous to the bending effects induced by force applications in long bones. In the mandible, this bending effect, particularly around the condylar neck, plays a crucial role in the remodeling process, which is the mechanism responsible for bone reshaping. The influence of bending forces on the mandible directs the morphological adaptation of the bone and is considered one of the key biomechanical factors governing the dynamic development of the jaw skeleton [35, 36].

In the study conducted by Zhou et al., it was reported that forces applied to the mandible 8 mm anteriorly and 4 mm inferiorly resulted in the highest maximum tensile stresses concentrated in the posterosuperior region of the condyle [37]. In our current study, finite element analysis performed using the Twin Block and MA appliances demonstrated that the highest tensile stresses were observed in the posterior region of the condyle, while maximum compressive stresses were concentrated in the anterior region. These findings suggest that both types of functional appliances produce similar stress distribution patterns on the temporomandibular joint, sharing biomechanical loadings in specific areas.

In our study, both simulation models demonstrated that stress distribution in the mandible was particularly concentrated in the condylar neck region. This observation aligns with the findings of Panigrahi et al., who also reported that maximum stress values occurred in both the condylar neck and condylar head. Such consistency across studies highlights the critical role of the condylar neck in bearing functional orthopedic forces and contributing to mandibular remodeling [38]. This similarity, despite the use of different analysis models and methodologies, underscores that certain regions of the mandibular condyle are more susceptible to biomechanical loading during functional treatment. These findings further emphasize that the condylar neck represents a critical site for remodeling during functional therapy, with the effects of applied forces being predominantly concentrated in this region.

The higher compressive and tensile stresses observed in the condylar bone with the Twin Block appliance are believed to stimulate bone formation in this region more effectively, thereby contributing to the more pronounced skeletal effects associated with this appliance. The findings of our study are consistent with those reported by Zhang et al. [31]. Zhang and colleagues reported that both the Twin Block and MA appliances effectively support condylar growth, as well as sagittal and vertical development of the mandible in adolescent patients with Class II malocclusion. However, they emphasized that a significantly greater increase in mandibular ramus length was observed in patients treated with the Twin Block appliance compared to those treated with clear functional aligners.

In a study conducted by Dong et al., the stress and shear distribution in the cranio-mandibular complex were evaluated using the finite element method to simulate different angulations of the inclined planes in the Twin Block appliance. The results of all simulations demonstrated that the stress generated by the Twin Block appliance was distributed primarily over the teeth and the periodontal ligaments [34].

This stress pattern facilitated the forward movement of the mandibular teeth and the backward displacement of the maxillary teeth. In the TMJ region, a reduction in compressive forces on the superior and posterior surfaces of the condyle was observed, consistent with a distribution that promotes condylar and mandibular growth. Stress was homogeneously distributed on the anterior surface of the condyle and the articular disc, while significant stress concentrations were noted in the posterior part of the glenoid fossa.

The maximum and minimum principal stress values of the condyle were reported as 0.36 MPa and − 0.15 MPa, respectively. These values are within the physiologic range (0.1–1.0 MPa) reported to stimulate adaptive condylar remodeling and are well below overload thresholds (> 2–3 MPa). This indicates that while Twin Block produces higher stress than MA, these magnitudes are biomechanically favorable rather than harmful, supporting controlled functional stimulation of the TMJ [34].

In our study, consistent with these findings, the simulation involving the Twin Block appliance also demonstrated a decrease in compressive forces on the superior and posterior surfaces of the condyle, supporting a biomechanical environment favorable for condylar and mandibular growth. The compressive stress observed in our model similarly showed increased intensity on the anterior surfaces of the condyle, reaching a peak value of − 0.42 MPa.

In the first scenario of our study, the minimum principal stress values for the MA appliance were observed on the same condylar surfaces as those in the Twin Block simulation but with lower magnitudes. These lower stress levels are believed to correspond to a reduced contribution to bone remodeling, suggesting a lesser skeletal effect. Overall, we consider the stress values obtained in our simulations to be compatible with functional growth modification.

Overall, the Twin Block appliance demonstrated a stronger orthopedic effect, characterized by greater displacement and higher stress levels across both skeletal and TMJ structures. In contrast, the MA appliance exhibited a more conservative biomechanical profile, with milder—yet clinically meaningful—effects, particularly in the control of anterior dental positioning. These biomechanical differences may inform appliance selection based on the desired emphasis on skeletal versus dentoalveolar correction.

A limitation of this study is that the finite element model was based on the anatomy of a single patient, which may not fully represent the variability in craniofacial morphology and skeletal maturity observed in clinical populations. Future studies incorporating multiple patient geometries or statistical shape modeling could enhance the generalizability of these findings. Also, the TMJ disk and cartilage were modeled as isotropic and linearly elastic, which does not fully reflect their viscoelastic nature. This simplification may affect absolute stress magnitudes; therefore, the TMJ stress results should be interpreted in relative rather than absolute terms. Future studies using nonlinear viscoelastic properties may provide more realistic insights into joint biomechanics. Nonetheless, finite element modeling remains a powerful tool for isolating and comparing the mechanical responses of different appliances under controlled conditions.
