Reduction in membrane AMPA receptor subunit 2 in left prefrontal cortex correlates with post-stroke depression
Authors: Yipeng Yu, Rong Fu, Haokun Rong, Qi Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPost-stroke depression (PSD) is a prevalent and debilitating complication of stroke. While ischemic lesion in left prefrontal cortex (PFC) is a recognized risk factor, the underlying mechanisms linking this locus to PSD remain poorly defined. The α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptor subunit 2 (GluA2) plays a critical role in neuroplasticity and mood regulation. In this study, a PSD rat model was used to investigate the pathophysiological mechanisms in left PFC through behavioral testing, biochemical assays, and pharmacological interventions targeting protein kinase C alpha (PKCα) and protein interacting with C kinase 1 (PICK1)-GluA2 binding. The results demonstrated that PSD reduced membrane GluA2 in left PFC. Mechanistically, PICK1 participated in the phosphorylation of GluA2 by PKCα, which in turn facilitated the reduction of membrane GluA2. Crucially, both inhibition of PKCα and disruption of PICK1-GluA2 binding rescued membrane GluA2 in left PFC without affecting total GluA2 expression and alleviated depressive-like behaviors of PSD rats. These findings suggest that reduced membrane GluA2 in left PFC correlates with PSD and may represent a potentially therapeutic target.Keywords: Post-stroke depression, Left prefrontal cortex, AMPA receptor trafficking

Introduction

Depression is the most prevalent psychiatric complication after stroke, with an estimated incidence ranging from 18% to 33% [1]. It is associated with cognitive impairment, suicidal ideation, and long-term disability, ultimately leading to poor quality of life and increased mortality [2,3].

Altered neuroplasticity has received increasing attention in the study of affective disorder-like symptoms following stroke [4,5]. The suppression of long-term potentiation (LTP) has been detected in prefrontal cortex (PFC) during the chronic phase following stroke [6]. The expression of neuroplasticity involves the physical transport of α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptor in and out of the synaptic membrane, the changes in subunit composition and the phosphorylation state of AMPA receptors [7,8].

Subunit-specific mechanisms exist in neurons for the synaptic trafficking of AMPA receptors [9]. AMPA receptor subunit 2 (GluA2) defines the receptor’s permeability to calcium (Ca2+), serving as a critical subunit in synaptic neuroplasticity [10,11]. Recent review has shown that the dynamic regulation of AMPA receptors during LTP is mediated by scaffolding molecules containing the PSD-95/discs large/zonula occludens-1 (PDZ) domain [12]. Protein interacting with C kinase 1 (PICK1) was initially identified as a protein interacting with protein kinase C alpha (PKCα) and specifically colocalizes with GluA2 at synaptic membranes [13,14]. However, the role of GluA2 and its affinity with PDZ proteins in PSD remains to be further elucidated.

In this study, we demonstrated that PSD reduced the membrane expression of GluA2 in left PFC. Remarkably, inhibition of PKCα and disruption of PICK1-GluA2 binding both rescued membrane GluA2 and ameliorated the depressive-like behaviors of PSD rats. These results suggest that reduction in membrane GluA2 in left PFC correlates with PSD, which could potentially serve as a therapeutic target.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Male Sprague-Dawley (S-D) rats weighing 330–380 g were housed under a specific pathogen-free condition, with a humidity of 40%–60%, a 12-h light/dark cycle, and a temperature of 20 °C–26 °C. The animals had ad libitum access to standard diet and water unless otherwise specified. All experimental procedures adhered to the National Institutes of Health guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Soochow University (Approval No. SUDA20251211A04) on December 11, 2025.

Establishment of PSD rat model

Clinical evidence indicates that ischemic injury in left hemisphere is associated with a higher risk of PSD, particularly during the subacute phase post-stroke [15]. Furthermore, PSD is shown to arise from the interplay between ischemic injury and subsequent psychological stress, a condition that cannot be fully recapitulated by ischemia alone [16]. Therefore, the middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) + chronic restraint stress (CRS) method was employed to establish PSD in rats.

We performed the MCAO procedures based on a previously reported method with minor modifications [17]. Briefly, after anesthetizing the rats, the left common carotid artery, external carotid artery (ECA), and internal carotid artery were carefully isolated. After ligating the distal end of ECA, a suture (3040A2, CINONTECH) was advanced from the proximal segment of ECA into the middle cerebral artery (MCA). After 1.5 h of MCA occlusion, the suture was partially withdrawn to restore blood flow. Sham-operated control rats underwent ECA ligation only.

The CRS procedure was initiated 3 days after the surgery. Animals were placed in restraint devices from 10:00 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. for 3 weeks [18]. During the restraint period, access to diet and water was restricted in both modeling and control groups. The depressive-like behaviors were tested weekly.

Triphenyl tetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining

TTC staining was performed to evaluate the ischemic infarct after the surgery. Rats were sacrificed after MCAO, and the brains were carefully removed. Brains were then rapidly frozen at −80 °C and sectioned coronally into a series of 2 mm sections. The sections were incubated in 2% TTC solution (D025-1-2, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) at 37 °C for 30 min protected from light [19]. After acquiring section images, the ischemic infarct was assessed using Image J software (Fig. 1a and b).

Neurological deficit assessment

The Longa score was adopted to assess the neurological deficit of rats after the surgery [20]. The higher the score, the more severe the neurological deficit. The score ranges from 0 to 4, a score of 1–3 indicating successful establishment of MCAO model [21]. Longa scores of the experimental groups were assessed 24 h after the surgery (Fig. 1c).

Intracranial microinjection

Rats were anesthetized with 1.5–2.5% isoflurane in 2% oxygen, and secured with a brain stereotaxic apparatus (71,000, RWD) [22]. We shaved the scalp and made a longitudinal incision to expose the bregma. Using the bregma as the origin, a hole was drilled at coordinates targeting the prefrontal cortex (x = 1.5 mm, y = 1.1 mm, z = 4.5 mm) [23]. Rats received stereotaxic injections into the left PFC of either (1) 1.0 μL of green fluorescent protein (GFP)-expressing lentivirus for GRIP1 overexpression (or Vector, 0.1 μL/min), or (2) 0.5 μL fluorescein Isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled pep2-EVKI (or pep2-SVKE, 0.1 μL/min) [24].

Behavioral tests

The depressive-like behaviors were assessed using sucrose preference test (SPT), tail suspension test (TST), and forced swimming test (FST) [25]. The behavioral tests were performed weekly (Fig. S1).

SPT was conducted based on a previously reported protocol with minor modifications [23]. For acclimatization, rats were exposed to a bottle of 1% sucrose solution and a bottle of pure water for 24 h; the position of bottles were switched midway. The formal test was initiated after a 24-h water deprivation. During the formal test, each rat was presented with a bottle of 1% sucrose solution and a bottle of pure water for 1 h. The PSD group exhibited significantly decreased sucrose preference index than the SHAM group, indicating the symptom of anhedonia (Fig. 1d).

TST was performed based on a previously reported protocol with minor modifications [26]. Prior to the formal test, rats were placed in the testing room 1 h in advance for habituation. During the formal test, the distal end of tail was securely fixed to the horizontal bar of the sound-attenuating chamber using medical adhesive tape, ensuring the rat could not contact the walls of chamber. The test session last 6 min and was recorded using an animal behavior analysis system (SA210, SansBio). Rats were returned to cages after the test. The immobility time of the middle 5-min period was automatically calculated by the system. The PSD group exhibited significantly increased immobility time than the SHAM group, indicating the symptom of behavioral despair (Fig. 1e).

FST was conducted based on a previously reported protocol with minor modifications [27]. This test was performed in a transparent cylindrical tank (height: 50 cm, diameter: 30 cm) filled with water to a depth of 30 cm. One day prior to the formal test, rats underwent a 15-min habituation session in the water tank. During the formal test, rats were placed in the water tank, ensuring them could not touch the bottom or edge of the tank. The test session last 6 min and was recorded using an animal behavior analysis system (SA209, SansBio). Rats were then dried in a warming incubator and returned to cages. The immobility time of the middle 5-min period was automatically calculated by the system. The PSD group exhibited significantly increased immobility time than the SHAM group, indicating the symptom of behavioral despair (Fig. 1f).

Primary cortical neuron culture and lentiviral transduction

Primary cortical neurons were isolated from the cerebral cortex of rat embryos (pregnant S-D rat) [28]. After the brains had been dissected, the cerebral cortex was treated with 0.25% trypsin (25,200,056, Gibco) for 15 min at 37 °C. Digestion was terminated by adding 10% FBS/DMEM-F12 (11,320,033, Gibco). Cells were seeded on poly-d-lysine (P0899, Sigma-Aldrich) coated 6-well plates and maintained in Neurobasal medium (21,103,049, Gibco) supplemented with B-27 (17,504,044, Gibco), GlutaMAX (35,050,061, Gibco) and an antibiotic mixture (15,240,096, Gibco). The cultured neurons were maintained in a humidified 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator, with half of the medium replaced every 3 days.

Lentivirus (LV)-GFP expressing shRNA targeting PICK1 (shPICK1), PKCα (shPKCα), or a scramble control (shCtrl) were purchased from Genechem (China). After 3 days in vitro, neurons were transduced with these lentiviral particles in the presence of polybrene (5 μg/mL, TR-1003, Sigma-Aldrich). At 72 h post-transduction, successfully transduced neurons (GFP-positive) were harvested for immunofluorescence and Western blot analysis (Fig. S2).

Western blot analysis

Proteins extracted from left PFC tissue and primary cortical neurons were separated by SDS-PAGE (7.5% gel) and then transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane (HATF00010, MERCK). The membrane was then blocked with 5% BSA (BS114, Biosharp) to prevent non-specific binding, followed by incubation at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies for GluA1 (1:1000, ab174785, abcam), GluA2 (1:1000, 13,607, Cell Signaling Tech-nology), phospho S880 (1:1000, ab254103, abcam), GluA3 (1:2000, ab40845, abcam), Na+-K+-ATPase (1:10,000, ab76020, abcam), PICK1 (1:1000, 85,325, Cell Signaling Technology), GRIP1 (1:1000, sc-365,937, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), PKCα (1:1000, ab11723, abcam), and Tublin (1:1000, 5568, Cell Signaling Technology). On the next day, the membrane was incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody (1:3000, 7074 S or 7076 S, Cell Signaling Technology) for 1 h RT. Blots were detected using a chemiluminescence kit (KF8003, Affinity). Densitometric analysis of blots was performed using Image J software. The optical density of target protein was normalized to the corresponding loading control and then expressed as fold change relative to SHAM or Control group.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Co-IP combined with Western blot analysis were performed to examine the binding affinity between GluA2 and PDZ proteins. The total protein extraction of left PFC tissue and primary cortical neurons was incubated with the corresponding IP antibodies and magnetic beads (B26302, Selleck) within the binding buffer (P0013, Beyotime) at 4 °C overnight [29]. Non-specific IgG (2729 S, Cell Signaling Technology) was used as a negative control. Then proteins were eluted from the beats and subjected to Western blot analysis.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Rats were transcardially perfused with 4% formaldehyde (BL539A, Biosharp) upon sacrifice, followed by brain dehydration using 30% sucrose (1245, BioFroxx) solution [30]. Then the brains were embedded in Neg-50 (6502, Epredia) for solidification and sectioned coronally. After antigen retrieval with citrate buffer (MVS-0101, MXB Biotechnologies), the sections were incubated with primary antibodies for NeuN (1:300, ab177487, abcam) overnight at 4 °C in a wet box. On the next day, the sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor™ 647 linked secondary antibody (1:500, A32733TR, Invitrogen) for 1 h RT. Finally, the sections were sealed with the mounting media (0100–20, SourthernBiotech) and examined under an upright fluorescence microscope (Axio Imager 2, Zeiss) for FITC-labeled peptides or LV-GFP in left PFC.

Statistical analysis

The GraphPad Prism (10.0, GraphPad Software) software was used for statistical analysis. The sample size for behavioral tests was set at n = 10 per group to ensure adequate statistical power. For biochemical analysis, tissue samples were collected from n = 6 rats per group, while in vitro experiments were performed with n = 3 independent primary neuronal cultures [31,32]. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test [33]. Comparisons between multiple groups of data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA [34]. All data are presented as mean ± SEM in this study. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

PSD reduces membrane GluA2 in left PFC

As the main excitatory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system, glutamate is released at a level of potential toxicity, leading to overactivation and increased intra-cellular Ca2+ influx [35]. Previous study indicated that GluA2-lacking AMPA receptors exhibit a high permeability to Ca2+, which plays a critical role in the pathophysiology of ischemic injury [36,37]. First, we isolated the membrane protein fractions using a membrane protein and cytoplasmic protein extraction kit and examined the membrane AMPA receptors expression in left PFC [38]. Western blot analysis exhibited that PSD significantly reduced the membrane expression of GluA2. Importantly, this reduction was not attributable to ischemia or stress alone, as the membrane GluA2 in PSD group was significantly lower than that of MCAO and SHAM + CRS groups, underscoring the PSD-specific nature of this downregulation (Fig. 1g and h). No significant differences were observed in total GluA2 expression or in the membrane expression of GluA1 and GluA3 (Fig. S3) [39].

To validate the regional and temporal specificity of membrane GluA2 reduction, we further examined GluA2 expression in different brain regions and at different time points (Fig. S4). While membrane GluA2 was markedly reduced in left PFC of PSD rats, no apparent changes were observed in right PFC or bilateral hippocampus. Regarding temporal dynamics, membrane GluA2 expression progressively declined from the first week of restraint and reached the nadir at week 3. Notably, no significant recovery was observed within 2 weeks after restraint removal, which is consistent with the previous finding that depressive-like behaviors persist for 1–2 weeks after model establishment [40].

PSD increases PICK1-GluA2 binding in left PFC

Protein interacting with C kinase 1 (PICK1) is known to mediate the internalization of GluA2 during synaptic plasticity [41]. Previous study has indicated that the ischemic injury induced internalization of GluA2 may be mediated by the PICK1 pathway [42]. We next examined the role of PICK1 during the GluA2 plasticity in the context of PSD. Co-IP results exhibited that neither SHAM + CRS nor MCAO group showed a statistically significant change relative to SHAM. Notably, the PSD group exhibited a marked increase in PICK1-GluA2 binding, which was also significantly stronger than that in SHAM + CRS and MCAO groups (Fig. 2a and b).

PSD increases the phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880 in left PFC

PICK1 was initially identified as a protein interacting with protein kinase C alpha (PKCα) and co-localizes with GluA2 through the PDZ domain interaction [13,43]. The binding affinity between PICK1 and PKCα is highly dependent on kinase activation [44]. In the next step, we examined the binding affinity between PICK1 and PKCα to characterize the activity of PKCα in the context of PSD. Co-IP results demonstrated the PSD group exhibited a marked elevation in PICK1-PKCα binding compared to SHAM, SHAM + CRS, and MCAO groups. In contrast, neither the MCAO nor the SHAM + CRS group showed a significant change relative to SHAM (Fig. 2c and d).

Previous study indicated that serine 880 (Ser-880) of GluA2 is a substrate of PKCα [45]. Since the activation state of PKCα determines its binding affinity with PICK1, it is likely that the PICK1-PKCα complex contains the activated form of PKCα. We next examined the phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880 using a site-specific antibody. Western blot results demonstrated the phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880 was significantly increased in PSD group compared to SHAM, SHAM + CRS, and MCAO groups. Although a mild upward trend was observed in MCAO group, it did not reach statistical significance relative to SHAM (Fig. 2e and f). Therefore, we supposed that PICK1 may recruit both activated PKCα and GluA2 to a common surface, thereby facilitating the phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880.

Glutamate receptor interacting protein 1 (GRIP1) is a 7-PDZ domain-containing adaptor protein interacting with GluA2 [46]. Recent study has indicated that the primary role of GRIP1 is supporting the surface pool of AMPA receptors [47]. To further elucidate the reduction in membrane GluA2 in PSD, we examined the binding affinity between GluA2 and GRIP1 in left PFC. The PSD group exhibited a marked reduction in GluA2-GRIP1 binding. Importantly, the extent of GluA2-GRIP1 binding in PSD group was also significantly lower than that observed in both SHAM + CRS and MCAO groups (Fig. S5).

PICK1 and PKCα regulate membrane GluA2 expression via phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880

To clarify the upstream-downstream regulatory relationship among PICK1, PKCα, and GluA2 at cellular level, we performed shRNA-mediated knockdown of PICK1 and PKCα in primary rat cortical neurons.

Firstly, the knockdown efficiency was validated by Western blot analysis. Transduction with shPICK1 and shPKCα significantly reduced the protein expression, comparing with the shCtrl group respectively (Fig. 3a-d).

Knockdown of PICK1 or PKCα regulates membrane GluA2 and Ser-880 phosphorylation in primary rat cortical neurons (a) Representative blots of PICK1.(b) Quantitative analyses of PICK1 expression, n = 3.(c) Representative blots of PKCα.(d) Quantitative analyses of PKCα expression, n = 3.(e) Representative blots of phosphorylated GluA2 following knockdown of PICK1.(f) Quantitative analysis of phosphorylated GluA2 following knockdown of PICK1, n = 3.(g) Representative blots of membrane GluA2 following knockdown of PICK1.(h) Quantitative analysis of membrane GluA2 following knockdown of PICK1, n = 3.(i) Representative blots of phosphorylated GluA2 following knockdown of PKCα.(j) Quantitative analysis of phosphorylated GluA2 following knockdown of PKCα, n = 3.(k) Representative blots of membrane GluA2 following knockdown of PKCα.(l) Quantitative analysis of membrane GluA2 following knockdown of PKCα, n = 3.Data are presented as mean ± SEM. ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01.

We next examined the effects of these knockdowns on GluA2 trafficking. Knockdown of PICK1 significantly decreased the phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880, accompanied by a significant increase in membrane GluA2 (Fig. 3e-h). Similarly, knockdown of PKCα significantly reduced phosphorylated GluA2 (Fig. 3i and j). Importantly, this was associated with a significant increase in the membrane expression of GluA2 (Fig. 3k, l). Collectively, these results indicate that PKCα phosphorylates GluA2 at Ser-880 to facilitate PICK1-mediated internalization, thereby reducing membrane GluA2 expression.

GRIP1 overexpression restores membrane GluA2 and alleviates depressive-like behaviors in PSD rats

To verify the functional role of GRIP1 in membrane GluA2 neuroplasticity, LV-GFP overexpressing GRIP1 (GRIP1-OE) or empty control (Vector) were stereotaxically injected into the left PFC of PSD rats. The lentiviral transduction was verified by GFP immunofluorescence, as shown in Fig. S6.

Western blot analysis confirmed GRIP1 overexpression, as GRIP1 expression was significantly increased in left PFC of the PSD + GRIP1-OE group compared with the PSD + Vector group (Fig. 4a and b). Notably, membrane protein fractionation followed by Western blot analysis revealed that the membrane GluA2 was markedly elevated in the GRIP1-overexpressing group relative to the control group (Fig. 4c and d), suggesting that overexpression of GRIP1 promotes GluA2 membrane localization in the context of PSD.

GRIP1 overexpression increases membrane GluA2 and alleviates depressive-like behaviors in PSD rats (a) Representative blots of GRIP1.(b) Quantitative analyses of GRIP1 expression, n = 6.(c) Representative blots of membrane GluA2 in left PFC.(d) Quantitative analysis of membrane GluA2 in left PFC, n = 6.(e) Quantitative analysis of sucrose preference index, n = 10.(f) Quantitative analysis of tail suspension immobility time, n = 10.(g) Quantitative analysis of forced swimming immobility time, n = 10.Data are presented as mean ± SEM. ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01.

Behavioral tests were performed weekly to monitor the temporal progression of depressive-like behaviors in experimental groups (Fig. S7). Longitudinal analysis revealed that the PSD + GRIP1-OE group showed a gradual reversal of behavioral deficits from the first week post-intervention onward. In contrast, the PSD + Vector group displayed no significant improvement relative to the levels observed at the end of the modeling period. Further analysis of the behavioral data at 2 weeks post-intervention demonstrated that the PSD + GRIP1-OE group exhibited a significantly increased sucrose preference index and decreased immobility times in both the TST and FST compared with the PSD + Vector group (Fig. 4e-g). Collectively, these behavioral data indicate that GRIP1 overexpression effectively rescues the depressive-like behaviors in PSD rats.

Inhibition of PKCα alleviates depressive-like behaviors of PSD rats

Increased PICK1-PKCα binding affinity could be attributed to enhanced activity of PKCα. To further examine the role of PKCα on GluA2 plasticity in PSD, we next employed the PKCα specific inhibitor Gö6976 (365,250, Sigma-Aldrich) and tested its effects on the experimental groups (Fig. S8) [48].

The drug administration was initiated after CRS. Rats were intraperitonially injected with Gö6976 (2.5 mg/kg) or solvent (0.1% DMSO in 0.1 M PBS) once daily for two weeks [49]. Behavioral tests were performed weekly (Fig. S9). Further analysis of the behavioral data at 2 weeks post-intervention demonstrated that Gö6976 significantly increased the sucrose preference index of PSD rats and decreased the immobility time in both TST and FST compared with the PSD + solvent group (Fig. 5a-c). Rats were then sacrificed for subsequent biochemical assays.

Gö6976 alleviates depressive-like behaviors of PSD rats and rescues membrane GluA2 in left PFC (a) Quantitative analysis of sucrose preference index, n = 10.(b) Quantitative analysis of tail suspension immobility time, n = 10.(c) Quantitative analysis of forced swimming immobility time, n = 10.(d) Representative blots of membrane GluA2 in left PFC.(e) Quantitative analysis of membrane GluA2 in left PFC, n = 6.Data are presented as mean ± SEM. ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001.

Inhibition of PKCα rescues membrane GluA2 of PSD rats

Given PSD reduced the membrane GluA2 in left PFC, we next evaluated the effect of Gö6976 on GluA2 expression. Following the drug administration, the membrane fraction was isolated from left PFC tissue and examined for membrane GluA2. Western blot analysis indicated that Gö6976 significantly increased membrane GluA2 expression in left PFC of PSD rats (Fig. 5d and e).

PSD increased the PICK1-GluA2 binding, which could facilitate the internalization of membrane GluA2 [37]. We next examined whether the therapeutic effect of Gö6976 was mediated by the PICK1-GluA2 binding. Co-IP results demonstrated that Gö6976 did not exert significant effect on the PICK1-GluA2 binding in the experimental groups (Fig. 6a and b).

Gö6976 decreases the phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880 in left PFC (a) Representative blots of GluA2 precipitated with PICK1.(b) Quantitative analysis of PICK1-GluA2 binding, n = 6.(c) Representative blots of PKCα precipitated with PICK1.(d) Quantitative analysis of PICK1-PKCα binding, n = 6.(e) Representative blots of phosphorylated GluA2 in left PFC.(f) Quantitative analysis of phosphorylated GluA2 in left PFC, n = 6.Data are presented as mean ± SEM. ns, not significant, ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001.

Inhibition of PKCα decreases the phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880 of PSD rats

PSD increased the PICK1-PKCα binding in left PFC, suggesting an increased PKCα activity. The PICK1-PKCα binding was examined after the administration of Gö6976. Consistent with our expectation, Co-IP analysis demonstrated that inhibition of PKCα markedly decreased PICK1-PKCα binding in left PFC of PSD rats (Fig. 6c and d).

A decreased binding affinity between PICK1 and PKCα corresponds to the successful inhibition of PKCα activity. We next examined the phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880 after the drug administration. Western blot analysis demonstrated that Gö6976 significantly decreased the phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880 in left PFC of PSD rats (Fig. 6e and f). Since the administration of Gö6976 did not reduce the PICK1-GluA2 binding, we further examine the binding affinity between GluA2 and GRIP1 to elucidate the retention of membrane GluA2 in PSD + Gö6976 group. Co-IP analysis demonstrated inhibition of PKCα markedly increased GluA2-GRIP1 binding in left PFC of PSD rats (Fig. S10).

Disruption of PICK1-GluA2 binding alleviates depressive-like behaviors of PSD rats

In the next part of the experiments, peptides (GL Biochem) targeting PICK1-GluA2 binding were employed to further elucidate the GluA2 plasticity in PSD. Pep2-EVKI (GRYGIERI) mimics the C terminus residues of GluA2 to specifically disrupt PICK1-GluA2 binding, with pep2-SVKE (NVYGIESVKE) serving as a nonfunctional control [50]. After CRS, a single dose of the peptides was delivered to left PFC of the experimental groups (Fig. S11). A two-week period was allowed for the peptides to take effect. The distribution of peptides was verified by FITC immunofluorescence, as shown in Fig. S12.

To exclude the non-specific effects of peptide, we supplemented additional comparisons among SHAM, SHAM + pep2-SVKE, and SHAM + pep2-EVKI groups. Statistical analysis revealed no significant differences among these three groups in relevant behavioral or biochemical parameters (Fig. S13), confirming that the peptide did not significantly affect membrane GluA2 expression or depressive-like behaviors under physiological conditions.

Behavioral tests were performed weekly (Fig. S14). Further analysis of the behavioral data at 2 weeks post-intervention demonstrated that pep2-EVKI significantly increased the sucrose preference index in PSD rats and decreased the immobility time in both TST and FST (Fig. 7a-c). Rats were then sacrificed for subsequent biochemical assays.

Disruption of PICK1-GluA2 binding rescues membrane GluA2 of PSD rats

Firstly, the binding affinity between PICK1 and GluA2 was examined after the administration of peptide. Consistent with our expectation, Co-IP results demonstrated that pep2-EVKI markedly decreased PICK1-GluA2 binding in left PFC of PSD rats (Fig. 7d and e).

Reduction in membrane GluA2 in left PFC correlates with the pathophysiological process of PSD. The membrane protein fraction was then isolated from left PFC tissue and examined for GluA2. Western blot analysis demonstrated that pep2-EVKI significantly increased membrane GluA2 in left PFC of PSD rats (Fig. 7f and g).

Disruption of PICK1-GluA2 binding decreases phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880

PSD increased the phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880, which could be attributed to increased PKCα activity [41]. It has been shown that increased PKCα activity is in parallel with reduction in membrane GluA2. The binding affinity between PICK1 and PKCα was then measured to characterize the PKCα activity after the peptide administration. Co-IP results demonstrated that pep2-EVKI did not exert significant effect on the PICK1-PKCα in the experimental groups (Fig. 8a and b).

Pep2-EVKI decreases the phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880 in left PFC (a) Representative blots of PKCα precipitated with PICK1.(b) Quantitative analysis of PICK1-PKCα binding, n = 6.(c) Representative blots of phosphorylated GluA2 in left PFC.(d) Quantitative analysis of phosphorylated GluA2 in left PFC, n = 6.Data are presented as mean ± SEM. ns, not significant, ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗∗P < 0.001.

Increased phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880 impaired its binding affinity with GRIP1, which could facilitate the internalization of GluA2 and its disassociation from the synaptic membrane [51]. The phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880 was then measured after the peptide administration. Western blot results demonstrated that pep2-EVKI significantly decreased the phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880 in left PFC of PSD rats (Fig. 8c and d), suggesting the PICK1-GluA2 binding is necessary for the PKCα-mediated phosphorylation of GluA2.

Discussion

In this study, we reported the membrane GluA2 plasticity in PSD. Firstly, we observed PSD reduced the membrane GluA2 in left PFC, which could be attributed to increased phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880. Then, inhibition of PKCα alleviated the depressive-like behaviors of PSD rats and rescued membrane GluA2 in left PFC, suggesting an elevated PKCα activity in the pathophysiological process of PSD. On the other hand, disruption of PICK1-GluA2 binding also preserved membrane GluA2 in left PFC, without inhibiting the PKCα activity. These results suggest that reduction in membrane GluA2 in left PFC correlates with the pathophysiological process of PSD, which is regulated by the phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880 and its binding affinity with PDZ proteins.

Dysfunction of the glutamate system during ischemic stroke can cause neuronal damage, making it a key mechanism in the development of PSD [52]. Previous studies have documented widespread GluA2 reduction as a generalized response to ischemic brain injury [53,54], yet whether such alteration persists or diverges in the context of PSD has remained unexplored. Here we revealed a PSD-specific decrease in membrane GluA2 that is selectively confined to left PFC, which is significantly more pronounced than that observed in ischemic injury alone.

Previous studies have reported that the internalization of GluA2 is associated with the activation of protein kinases [55,56]. Although our results indicated reduction in membrane GluA2 is parallel to increased PKCα activity, no consensus has been reached regarding the identification of specific kinases or phosphatases during the AMPARs synaptic plasticity. There is report suggesting non-receptor Src family kinases are involved in GluA2 internalization and suppression of AMPA receptor EPSCs [57]. Future studies are needed to characterize the AMPA receptor pathway associated kinases or phosphatases.

The pathophysiology of PSD correlates with the activation of PKCα. Our results demonstrated that PSD increased the PICK1-PKCα binding affinity in left PFC, indicating an enhanced activity of PKCα [58]. Meanwhile, the phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880 was significantly increased in the PSD group, which could subsequently facilitate the internalization of GluA2-containing AMPA receptors from the synaptic surface [47]. Inhibition of PKCα alleviated the depressive-like behaviors of PSD rats and rescued the membrane GluA2 in left PFC, suggesting that PKCα could potentially serve as a reversible therapeutic target of PSD. Meanwhile, GluA2-containing AMPA receptors stand as crucial part of synaptic neuroplasticity. Recent study has shown that decreased synaptic GluA2 could impair both LTP and LTD [59]. Our results demonstrated the plasticity of GluA2 not only determined by its membrane level but also regulated by its phosphorylation status.

Next, the phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880 differentially regulates its binding affinity with PDZ domain proteins, inducing the impairments of synaptic neuroplasticity [60]. The C-terminal domain of GluA2 contains the type II PDZ ligands, which could interact directly with PICK1 and GRIP1 [61]. Our results demonstrated that PSD significantly increased the PICK1-GluA2 binding, which is considered as a therapeutic target for the synaptic plasticity linked neurological disorders [62]. The binding affinity between GluA2 and GRIP1 was then measured to further characterize the relationship between the phosphorylation of GluA2 and its internalization in PSD. Co-IP results demonstrated that PSD significantly decreased the GluA2-GRIP1 binding, which is parallel to increased phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880. Administration of PKCα inhibitor Gö6976 increased GluA2-GRIP1 binding without affecting PICK1-GluA2 binding, suggesting GluA2 phosphorylation reduces its binding affinity with GRIP1 instead of PICK1. On the other hand, disruption of the PICK1-GluA2 binding using pep2-EVKI reduced the phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880 and rescued its membrane expression, without inhibiting the PKCα activity. These findings indicate that disrupting the PICK1-GluA2 binding could bypass PKCα to retain membrane GluA2, offering an alternative way to regulate the GluA2-containing AMPA receptors trafficking.

Reduced membrane GluA2 in left PFC may partly result from altered scaffolding interactions, such as those mediated by GRIP1. Additionally, the membrane plasticity of GluA2 may also be influenced by chaperone-like factors such as NSF. NSF interacts directly with GluA2 and has been shown to regulate the surface expression and synaptic localization of AMPA receptors [63]. In the context of our findings, the observed reduction in membrane GluA2 raises the possibility that chronic stress or ischemic insult may impair the NSF-GluA2 binding, although this was not directly examined in the present study [64]. While NSF binding stabilizes GluA2 at the synaptic membrane, it has been reported that LTD reflects a reduction in synaptic AMPA receptor content through clathrin-mediated endocytosis, which may be regulated by the dephosphorylation of GluA2 C-terminus [65,66]. Taken together, these observations imply that reduced membrane GluA2 in PSD may result from a convergence of altered scaffolding, trafficking, and endocytic mechanisms.

Preclinical evidence has demonstrated that the PICK1-specific inhibitory peptide pep2-EVKI confers neuroprotection against ischemic injury by completely blocking the oxygen/glucose deprivation-induced decrease in AMPA receptor rectification and preventing the loss of surface GluA2 [67]. To date, few studies have quantitatively reported the blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability coefficient of pep2-EVKI or its TAT conjugated parent peptide. Notably, a dimeric PICK1 inhibitor conjugated to TAT has been reported to cross the BBB and accumulate in the brain upon systemic administration, suggesting that the brain delivery of peptide may depend on the specific molecular design or physicochemical properties of the cargo rather than on TAT conjugation alone [68]. Gö6976 serves as a potent inhibitor of conventional PKC isoforms PKCα and PKCβ1 and has been shown to block HIF-VEGF signaling-mediated angiogenesis after cerebral ischemia [49]. However, the PKC family comprises multiple isoforms with opposing roles in ischemic injury, and inhibition of conventional isoforms may disrupt the balance between pro-injury and protective signals, suggesting that the therapeutic utility of Gö6976 or other PKC inhibitors require more thorough evaluation before clinical translation [69].

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Our findings are derived from a single animal model using only male rats, leaving the reproducibility of our findings across other stroke paradigms, both sexes, and human PSD unconfirmed. Future studies incorporating complementary models, female subjects, and clinical samples are needed to establish the general significance of AMPA receptor trafficking in PSD. Although we examined bilateral PFC and hippocampus to examine region-specific reduction in membrane GluA2, other emotion-related regions such as the amygdala remains unexplored [70]. We acknowledge that Gö6976 also exhibits inhibitory activity against PKCβ1, and future studies employing PKCα-specific genetic knockdown would provide valuable validation. Additionally, the proposed downstream effects of reduced membrane GluA2 on calcium permeability and synaptic plasticity remain to be substantiated by future calcium imaging and electrophysiological experiments.

In summary, our study demonstrated the reduction of membrane GluA2 in left PFC correlates with the pathophysiological process of PSD. In membrane trafficking of GluA2, PICK1 acting as a bridging molecule facilitating the phosphorylation of GluA2 at Ser-880 by PKCα, consequently leading to the internalization of membrane GluA2 in left PFC and the expression of depressive-like behaviors of PSD rats. Given the complexity of the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying PSD, it is particularly important to develop therapeutic strategies that can simultaneously target to multiple sites.
