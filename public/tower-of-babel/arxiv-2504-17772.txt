Demagnetization in micromagnetics: magnetostatic self-interactions of bulk chiral magnetic skyrmions
Paul Leask, Martin Speight

Abstract
We develop a theoretical and numerical framework for three-dimensional bulk chiral magnets that includes the full magnetostatic dipole-dipole interaction and its back-reaction on the magnetization. Assuming translational invariance along one spatial direction, we analyze the effect of dipolar interactions on three Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction (DMI) terms – Dresselhaus, Rashba, and Heusler –corresponding to Bloch, Néel, and antiskyrmion textures. In the absence of the dipolar interaction, these three DMI terms are gauge-equivalent and yield degenerate skyrmion energies. Incorporating the non-local dipole-dipole interaction breaks this degeneracy: Bloch skyrmions remain unaffected, Néel skyrmions shrink slightly, and Heusler antiskyrmions lose axial symmetry and stabilize into square-lattice crystals. The system is solved using a non-local numerical relaxation method that self-consistently computes the magnetostatic potential from Poisson’s equation. Our results show that long-range dipolar interactions can stabilize bulk antiskyrmion crystals in translationally invariant three-dimensional chiral magnets.

I
Introduction

Topological spin textures arise in magnetic systems due to the competition between the Heisenberg exchange interaction and the Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction (DMI).
The Heisenberg exchange energy promotes parallel alignment of spins whereas the DMI energy favours non-collinear alignment of spins
[
5
]
.
Magnetic skyrmions are one such type of topological spin texture.
They are topological defects in the magnetization configuration and behave as magnetic quasi-particles.
Such vortex-like configurations were first observed in
[
8
,
6
]
in the context of condensed matter systems and, in particular, they found skyrmions in easy-axis chiral magnetic materials with the Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction.
The easy-axis, or Zeeman, interaction is essential for stability as it provides a scale for the magnetic skyrmion.

There are three different skyrmion types investigated in the literature, each associated to a particular DMI term.
The first two are the Bloch and Néel skyrmions, corresponding to the Dresselhaus and Rashba spin-orbit couplings (SOC), respectively.
The final type is the antiskyrmion, which appears in Heusler compounds
[
33
,
12
,
30
]
, so we label the associated DMI term as the Heusler DMI.
The chiral magnet can also be formulated as a
\textup{U}(1)\times\textup{SU}(2)
gauge theory with a constant background gauge field
[
2
]
.
Within this formulation, the three DMI terms are related by gauge transformations, which are controlled by a single rotation parameter
[
4
]
.
The energy functional is invariant under the relevant gauge transformations, so the three skyrmion types are degenerate in energy.
An in-depth review on the current state of the theoretical and experimental realization of magnetic skyrmions in condensed matter systems can be found in
[
3
]
.

The inclusion of the magnetic dipole-dipole interaction (DDI) introduces frustration into a ferromagnetic state, turning it into a frustrated spin system.
This frustration between the DDI and Heisenberg exchange interaction can lead to interesting phenomena.
The nucleation of skyrmions using an external magnetic dipole, which modifies the Zeeman interaction term, was demonstrated numerically by Garanin
et al
.
[
14
]
.
Depending on how the magnetic dipole approaches the film, a skyrmion-antiskyrmion pair can be created in the bulk or a skyrmion can be created at the edge of the film.
It was also shown that magnetic biskyrmions can be stabilized by dipolar interactions in centrosymmetric materials where the DMI term is forbidden
[
15
]
.

Kashuba and Pokrovsky
[
19
]
presented a theory on the effect of long-range dipole interactions in thin ferromagnetic films.
In particular, they studied stripe domain structures in ordinary ferromagnets with no interfacial DMI.
An analytical investigation into the stabilization of magnetic skyrmions by dipolar interactions in thin films was conducted by Ezawa
[
11
]
.
He considered both ordinary and chiral ferromagnets, and employed the Dresselhaus DMI term in the latter, which favours Bloch skyrmions.
In both ferromagnet types, domain wall stripes and skyrmions were constructed analytically.
However, in his analysis, Ezawa considers the spin texture of a Néel skyrmion, which is incompatible with the Dresselhaus DMI.

A numerical study on the effect of the dipolar interaction on chiral ferromagnets was performed by Kwon
at al
.
[
21
]
.
They also considered the Dresselhaus DMI term, for which they investigated stripe domains and Bloch skyrmion lattices in the presence of an applied external magnetic field and perpendicular uniaxial anisotropy.
As the external magnetic field is increased, the microscopic spin configurations transform from a spin-spiral stripe to a skyrmion crystalline structure.
Sui and Hu
[
36
]
conducted a similar investigation (but with no anisotropies), wherein they considered the DMI term corresponding to Rashba SOC, which gives rise to Néel type skyrmions.
In both studies
[
21
,
36
]
, magnetic skyrmion crystals were investigated using simulated annealing with a Monte-Carlo method on small grids.
Furthermore, they both include a tuneable constant that allows them to alter the strength of the dipolar interaction, and Kwon
et al
. chose to set the dipolar interaction strength comparable to the anisotropy.
Sui and Hu
[
36
]
find that, as the DDI strength increases, the skyrmion crystal structure changes from a hexagonal arrangement to one of a square form.

Our approach differs from that of
[
21
,
36
]
.
Instead of considering a tuneable dipole interaction parameter, we focus on the effect the dipolar interaction has on the different DMI-related skyrmion types in the bulk of a chiral ferromagnet.
That is, we do not model a thin film, instead we model a three dimensional bulk system and impose translation invariance along the
z
-direction.
We consider three DMI terms and their associated skyrmion type - Dresselhaus (Bloch), Rashba (Néel) and Heusler (antiskyrmion).
Without the dipolar interaction, the magnetic skyrmion types are equivalent up to redefinitions of the field.
However, the dipole-dipole interaction breaks this equivalence and there is a splitting in the energies.
Furthermore, the axial symmetry is broken for the antiskyrmion (Heusler DMI), resulting in a change in shape of both isolated antiskyrmions, and their crystal structure.

II
The model

The system we wish to model is a large three-dimensional chiral ferromagnet whose magnetization field is assumed to vary smoothly and have constant magnitude, and so can be written
\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{x})=M_{s}\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})
where
|\mathbf{n}|=1
and
M_{s}
is a constant parameter called the magnetic saturation density.
The total energy of such a field is assumed to take the form

E=E_{\textup{exch}}+E_{\textup{DMI}}+E_{\textup{pot}}+E_{\textup{DDI}},

(1)

where the exchange, DMI and potential energies are

\displaystyle E_{\textup{exch}}=\,

\displaystyle\frac{J}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\textup{d}^{3}x\,|\textup{d}\mathbf{n}|^{2},

(2)

\displaystyle E_{\textup{DMI}}=\,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\textup{d}^{3}x\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathbf{d}_{i}\cdot(\mathbf{n}\times\partial_{i}\mathbf{n}),

(3)

\displaystyle E_{\textup{pot}}=\,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\textup{d}^{3}x\left(K_{m}(1-n_{3}^{2})+M_{s}B_{\textup{ext}}(1-n_{3})\right),

(4)

and
E_{\textup{DDI}}
is the dipole-dipole interaction energy, which we will consider in detail in section
III
. The potential has two terms: an intrinsic anisotropy term, with coupling constant
K_{m}
(which can be any real constant) and a Zeeman term representing the interaction of
\mathbf{m}
with a constant external applied magnetic field
\mathbf{B}_{\textup{ext}}=(0,0,B_{\textup{ext}})
.

The ground state configuration is found by minimizing the potential energy
E_{\textup{pot}}
, which yields the vacuum configuration
\mathbf{n}_{\textup{vac}}=\mathbf{n}_{\uparrow}=(0,0,1)
.
In general, for the energy to remain finite, we must impose the vacuum boundary condition
\lim_{|\mathbf{x}|\rightarrow\infty}\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{n}_{\uparrow}
on configurations.
Throughout we will consider configurations invariant under translation in the
x_{3}
direction (the direction of the applied magnetic field), so that the domain effectively reduces to the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
For these translational invariant cases, with the vacuum boundary condition, there is a compactification of space
\mathbb{R}^{2}\cup\{\infty\}\cong S^{2}
such that the magnetization can be interpreted as a map
\mathbf{n}:S^{2}\rightarrow S^{2}
.
Associated to this map is an integer valued topological degree
N_{\textup{Sk}}\in\pi_{2}(S^{2})=\mathbb{Z}
, which is given by

N_{\textup{Sk}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\textup{d}^{2}x\,\rho(\mathbf{x}),\quad\rho(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\mathbf{n}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{n}}{\partial x_{1}}\times\frac{\partial\mathbf{n}}{\partial x_{2}}\right).

(5)

Following the usual convention of the literature, magnetization fields with
N_{\textup{Sk}}<0
are referred to as skyrmions while those with for
N_{\textup{Sk}}>0
are antiskyrmions.
The skyrmion type is determined by the choice of DMI term.
Magnetic skyrmions with
N_{\textup{Sk}}=0
, such as a chiral magnetic bag enclosing a domain wall, are referred to as skyrmionium
[
20
]
or a
2\pi
-vortex
[
7
]
.
We will, however, not consider skyrmion bags in this article.

In addition to magnetic skyrmions having a topological degree, they also have an associated size
[
38
]
. There is some choice in how this is
defined
[
1
]
.
For a field theory permitting topological solitons, one can introduce the root-mean-square (RMS) radius of the soliton, defined by

R_{\textup{Sk}}=\left(\frac{1}{N_{\textup{Sk}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\textup{d}^{2}x\,r^{2}|\rho(\mathbf{x})|\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(6)

We will use this definition of skyrmion size throughout to compare what happens to the size of the skyrmion as the DDI is included for various DMI terms.

The DMI energy is determined by three constant vectors
\mathbf{d}_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
.
Having restricted attention to translation invariant fields, only
\mathbf{d}_{1}
,
\mathbf{d}_{2}
are relevant.
The overall
DMI strength
\mathcal{D}
is set by choosing the longer of these two vectors, without loss of generality
\mathbf{d}_{1}
, to have length
1
.
We will consider three different choices of the DMI vectors
\mathbf{d}_{1},\mathbf{d}_{2}
.
The most standard choice, deriving from Dresselhaus spin-obit coupling, yields the DMI vectors
\{\mathbf{d}_{1}=-\mathbf{e}_{1},\,\mathbf{d}_{2}=-\mathbf{e}_{2}\}
, and the corresponding DMI term is

\sum_{i}\mathbf{d}_{i}\cdot(\mathbf{n}\times\partial_{i}\mathbf{n})=\mathbf{n}\cdot\left(\bm{\nabla}\times\mathbf{n}\right)=\Lambda_{xz}^{(y)}-\Lambda_{yz}^{(x)},

(7)

where we have introduced the Lifshitz invariants
[
27
]
,

\Lambda_{ij}^{(k)}=n_{i}\frac{\partial n_{j}}{\partial x_{k}}-n_{j}\frac{\partial n_{i}}{\partial x_{k}}.

(8)

\{\mathbf{d}_{1}=\mathbf{e}_{1},\,\mathbf{d}_{2}=-\mathbf{e}_{2}\}
and corresponds to tetragonal compounds with
D_{2d}
symmetry (such as Heusler compounds)
[

,

\sum_{i}\mathbf{d}_{i}\cdot(\mathbf{n}\times\partial_{i}\mathbf{n})=\Lambda_{xz}^{(y)}+\Lambda_{yz}^{(x)}.

(10)

In the absence of
E_{\textup{DDI}}
all these choices are equivalent up to field redefinitions. That is,

\begin{split}E_{\textup{Dresselhaus}}(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})=E_{\textup{Rashba}}(n_{2},-n_{1},n_{3}),\\

(11)

The Rashba DMI term favours skyrmions of Néel type, that is,

\mathbf{n}_{\textup{N\'{e}el}}(r,\theta)=(\sin f(r)\cos\theta,\sin f(r)\sin\theta,\cos f(r)),

(12)

where
f(r)
decreases monotonically from
\pi
to
0
.
Note that this field has degree
N_{\textup{Sk}}=-1
, so we call it a skyrmion, following the standard convention in the magnetism literature.
It follows from (
11
) that the Dresselhaus DMI term favours Bloch skyrmions

\mathbf{n}_{\textup{Bloch}}(r,\theta)=(-\sin f(r)\sin\theta,\sin f(r)\cos\theta,\cos f(r)),

(13)

while the Heusler DMI term favours
antiskyrmions
of the form

\mathbf{n}_{\textup{Heusler}}(r,\theta)=(-\sin f(r)\sin\theta,-\sin f(r)\cos\theta,\cos f(r)),

(14)

and that these three solutions are exactly degenerate in energy.
As we will see, dipole-dipole interactions break this degeneracy, producing particularly striking changes to the Heusler system.

III
The interaction energy of a distribution of magnetic dipoles

In this section we define the magnetostatic self-energy, or dipole-dipole interaction energy,
E_{\textup{DDI}}
and compute its first variation.
The magnetic field induced by an isolated dipole of moment
\mathbf{m}
at
\mathbf{0}
is
[
31
]

\mathbf{B}=-\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi r^{3}}\left(\mathbf{m}-3\frac{\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{x}}{r^{2}}\mathbf{x}\right),

(15)

where
\mu_{0}
is the permeability of free space.
This is often referred to as the
demagnetizing
field and can be expressed as the gradient of a magnetic potential
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, that is,

\mathbf{B}=-\bm{\nabla}\psi

(16)

where

\psi=-\mu_{0}\mathbf{m}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\left(\frac{1}{4\pi r}\right).

(17)

Consider now a continuous distribution of magnetic dipole density
\mathbf{m}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
, where
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
is some domain.
The magnetic field it induces, at a point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, is given by integrating the field induced at
\mathbf{x}
by
\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{y})
at
\mathbf{y}\in\Omega
over
\mathbf{y}\in\Omega
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}\int_{\Omega}\textup{d}^{3}\mathbf{y}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{3}}\left\{\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{y})\right.\\
\left.-3\frac{\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{y})\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\right\}.\end{split}

(18)

Again, this is the gradient of the magnetic potential
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

\psi(\mathbf{x})=-\mu_{0}\int_{\Omega}\textup{d}^{3}\mathbf{y}\,\left\{\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{y})\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{x}\left(\frac{1}{4\pi|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|}\right)\right\}.

(19)

We now note that

G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{4\pi|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|}

(20)

is the Green’s function for the Laplacian
\Delta=-\nabla^{2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, that is

\Delta_{x}G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}).

(21)

Comparing with (
19
), we see that the magnetic potential
\psi
satisfies the PDE
[
13
]

\Delta\psi=-\nabla^{2}\psi=\mu_{0}\rho,\quad\rho=-(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{m}),

(22)

that is, Poisson’s equation with source
\rho=-(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{m})
. Up to a multiplicative constant, the magnetic field induced by the dipole distribution
\mathbf{m}
coincides, therefore, with the magnetic field induced by the charge distribution
-(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{m})

[
28
]
.
Roughly speaking, we may think of
-(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{m})
as “magnetic charge density”.

The interaction energy of a pair of magnetic dipoles
\mathbf{m}^{(1)}
,
\mathbf{m}^{(2)}
, is
-\mathbf{m}^{(1)}\cdot\mathbf{B}^{(2)}
, where
\mathbf{B}^{(2)}
is the magnetic field induced by
\mathbf{m}^{(2)}
at the position of
\mathbf{m}^{(1)}
. Hence, the total dipole-dipole interaction energy of a continuous dipole density distribution is the magnetostatic energy

E_{\textup{DDI}}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\textup{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\,\left(\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{x})\right),

(23)

where
\mathbf{B}
is the demagnetizing field (
18
) induced by the magnetization
\mathbf{m}
.
(The factor of
1/2
arises since we sum over dipole pairs.)
It is useful to rewrite this as a functional of the magnetic potential
\psi
:

\displaystyle E_{\textup{DDI}}=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\textup{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\,\mathbf{m}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\psi

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\textup{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\,\left(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{m}\right)\psi+\frac{1}{2}\oint_{\partial\Omega}\textup{d}\mathbf{s}\cdot\left(\psi\mathbf{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\int_{\Omega}\textup{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\,\psi\Delta\psi+\frac{1}{2}\oint_{\partial\Omega}\textup{d}\mathbf{s}\cdot\left(\psi\mathbf{m}\right),

(24)

by the Divergence Theorem. We will use this formula only in situations where the boundary conditions ensure that the boundary term vanishes.
In this case,
E_{\textup{DDI}}
coincides with the “electrostatic” self-energy of the “charge” distribution
-(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{m})
.

We now specialize to the case of immediate interest: the dipole-dipole interaction energy of a chiral ferromagnet in a translation invariant configuration. The dipole field has constant length
M_{s}
, so
\mathbf{m}=M_{s}\mathbf{n}
where
\mathbf{n}
is valued on the unit sphere. Further, we impose translation invariance in the direction
\mathbf{e}_{3}=(0,0,1)
, so
\mathbf{n}
is independent of
x_{3}
. The boundary conditions require some care. We assume that some anisotropy in the system (either intrinsic or generated by an external applied magnetic field) imposes an energetic preference for the dipole orientation
\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{e}_{3}
, and consider fields
\mathbf{n}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow S^{2}
which have compact support in the sense that there exists
R_{0}>0
such that, for all
r:=|(x_{1},x_{2})|\geq R_{0}
,
\mathbf{n}(x_{1},x_{2})=\mathbf{e}_{3}
. Since the field
\mathbf{n}
is translation invariant, the total dipole interaction energy either vanishes (for example, if
\mathbf{n}
is constant) or diverges. The energy per unit length (in the
\mathbf{e}_{3}
direction) may be finite however, and this coincides with the total energy of the slab

\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{2}\times[0,1]
, which we compute as the limit of the energy of the thick disk

\Omega_{R}=\{\mathbf{x}\>:\>x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\leq R^{2},\>0\leq x_{3}\leq 1\}
as
R\rightarrow\infty
. Note that, in this case, the boundary term in (
24
) vanishes identically for all
R>R_{0}
: the flux of
\psi\mathbf{m}
through the cylindrical wall vanishes since
\mathbf{m}
has no normal component on this part of the boundary, and the flux through the top disk (at
x_{3}=1
) is exactly canceled by the flux through the bottom disk (at
x_{3}=0
) since
\psi\mathbf{m}
is translation invariant. Hence

E_{\textup{DDI}}=\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\textup{d}^{2}x\,\psi\Delta\psi,

(25)

where
\Delta=-\partial_{1}^{2}-\partial_{2}^{2}
is the Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and
\psi
satisfies

\Delta\psi=-\mu_{0}M_{s}\left(\frac{\partial n_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}+\frac{\partial n_{2}}{\partial x_{2}}\right).

(26)

Consider the large
r
behaviour of the magnetic potential
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Any solution of the Poisson equation
\Delta\psi=\mu_{0}\rho
on the plane has a multipole expansion

\psi=-\frac{q\mu_{0}}{2\pi}\log r+O(r^{-1})

(27)

where
q=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\textup{d}^{2}x\,\rho
is the total “charge”.
In general, such functions are logarithmically unbounded.
In our case

\displaystyle q=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\textup{d}^{2}x\,\rho=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\textup{d}^{2}x\,(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{m})=0,

(28)

and we conclude that
\psi
is (at least)
1/r
localized.

IV
Evading the Hobart–Derrick theorem

So far we have shown how to include the dipolar interaction and computed its variation with respect to the magnetization.
Now, we want to understand how the dipolar interaction transforms under a coordinate rescaling, to see the role it plays in evading the Hobart–Derrick theorem
[
9
]
.

Let us consider an energy and length rescaling with
E=\frac{J^{2}}{\mathcal{D}}\hat{E}
and
x=\frac{J}{\mathcal{D}}\hat{x}
.
Further, let us also consider the rescaling of the magnetic potential
\psi=\frac{\mathcal{D}}{M_{s}}\hat{\psi}
.
Then the dimensionless energy
\hat{E}
is (having dropped all
\hat{}
decorations)

\begin{split}E=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}|\textup{d}\mathbf{n}|^{2}+\mathbf{d}_{i}\cdot(\mathbf{n}\times\partial_{i}\mathbf{n})+K(1-n_{3}^{2})\right.\\
\left.+h(1-n_{3})+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{n}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\psi\right\}\textup{d}^{3}x,\end{split}

(29)

where the magnetic potential satisfies the dimensionless Poisson equation

\Delta\psi=-\mu\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{n}.

(30)

The dimensionless coupling constants are

K=\frac{K_{m}J}{\mathcal{D}^{2}},\quad h=\frac{M_{s}B_{\textup{ext}}J}{\mathcal{D}^{2}},\quad\mu=\frac{\mu_{0}JM_{s}^{2}}{\mathcal{D}^{2}}.

(31)

We now return to the case of interest where we have translation invariance in the
\mathbf{e}_{3}
direction.
Before, we were considering the energy per unit length in the translation invariant direction.
So, our domain was
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{2}\times[0,1]
, which now becomes
\Omega^{\prime}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\times[0,t]
where
t=L_{0}^{-1}
is the thickness of the slab under the rescaling.
To maintain adimensionality, let us consider the energy per unit thickness
E/t
.
Therefore, the adimensional energy functional we are really considering is

\displaystyle\begin{split}E=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}|\textup{d}\mathbf{n}|^{2}+\mathbf{d}_{i}\cdot(\mathbf{n}\times\partial_{i}\mathbf{n})+K(1-n_{3}^{2})\right.\\
\left.+h(1-n_{3})+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{n}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\psi\right\}\textup{d}^{2}x.\end{split}

(32)

With the dimensionless total energy (
32
) in hand, we now turn to evading the Hobart–Derrick theorem
[
9
]
.
Let us consider, for a given field
\mathbf{n}:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\rightarrow S^{2}
, the one parameter variation
\mathbf{n}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}):=\mathbf{n}(\lambda\mathbf{x})
,
\lambda>0
, obtained by isotropic dilation. The “magnetic charge density” of
\mathbf{n}_{\lambda}
is

\rho_{\lambda}(x)=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})=-\lambda\rho(\lambda\mathbf{x})
, so its magnetic potential
\psi_{\lambda}
satisfies

(\Delta\psi_{\lambda})(\mathbf{x})=\mu\lambda\rho(\lambda\mathbf{x}).

(33)

Comparing with (
30
), we see that

\psi_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})=\psi(\lambda\mathbf{x})/\lambda
. Combining this with the well-known scaling behavior of the exchange, DMI and potential terms, we see that

E(\mathbf{n}_{\lambda})=E_{\textup{exch}}(\mathbf{n})+\frac{1}{\lambda}E_{\textup{DMI}}(\mathbf{n})+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\left(E_{\textup{pot}}(\mathbf{n})+E_{\textup{DDI}}(\mathbf{n})\right).

(34)

So the dipole-dipole interaction energy behaves like a potential term under the spatial scaling.

If
\mathbf{n}
is a static solution of the model, it extremizes
E
locally with respect to all variations, including
\mathbf{n}_{\lambda}
, so

\left.\frac{\textup{d}E}{\textup{d}\lambda}\right|_{\lambda=1}=E_{\textup{DMI}}+2\left(E_{\textup{pot}}+E_{\textup{DDI}}\right)=0,

(35)

the Derrick scaling constraint.
Therefore, it is possible to evade the Hobart–Derrick theorem without inclusion of the anisotropy and Zeeman interactions, provided the dipolar interaction is present.

We note that the energy contribution from the DMI term must be negative semi-definite as both the potential and DDI energies are positive semi-definite.
To see this, we use the general identity
\psi\Delta\phi=\bm{\nabla}\psi\cdot\bm{\nabla}\phi-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left(\psi\bm{\nabla}\phi\right)
and the divergence theorem to express the DDI energy as the electrostatic self-energy of the charge distribution
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{n}
, that is

E_{\textup{DDI}}=\frac{1}{2\mu}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\textup{d}^{2}x\,|\bm{\nabla}\psi|^{2}\geq 0.

(36)

Hence, the dipolar interaction can stabilize magnetic skyrmions in chiral ferromagnets where the DMI is present, but does not provide stability in ordinary ferromagnets.

V
The Euler-Lagrange equations

We seek fields
\mathbf{n}:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\rightarrow S^{2}
which (at least locally) minimize
E
so, for all smooth variations
\mathbf{n}_{t}
of
\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{n}_{0}
through fields of compact support, we require that

\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}E(\mathbf{n}_{t})=0.

(37)

This condition is equivalent to the Euler-Lagrange equations for
E
, which we now derive.

Let

\bm{\varepsilon}=\partial_{t}\mathbf{n}_{t}|_{t=0}
, the generator of the variation
\mathbf{n}_{t}
, and note that
\bm{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
everywhere since
|\mathbf{n}|=1
.
The induced variations of
E_{\textup{exch}}
,
E_{\textup{DMI}}
and

E_{\textup{pot}}
are easily computed:

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}E_{\textup{exch}}(\mathbf{n}_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\textup{d}^{2}x\,\bm{\varepsilon}\cdot\Delta\mathbf{n}

(38)

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}E_{\textup{DMI}}(\mathbf{n}_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\textup{d}^{2}x\,\bm{\varepsilon}\cdot(-2\mathbf{d}_{i}\times\partial_{i}\mathbf{n})

(39)

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}E_{\textup{pot}}(\mathbf{n}_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\textup{d}^{2}x\,\bm{\varepsilon}\cdot(0,0,-h-2Kn_{3}).

(40)

We will also need the variation of
E_{\textup{DDI}}
which is rather more subtle.

Denote by
\psi_{t}
the unique solution of (
30
)
with source
-{\rm div}\,\mathbf{n}_{t}
decaying to
0
at infinity, and
\dot{\psi}=\partial_{t}\psi_{t}|_{t=0}
. It is important to note that, while

\bm{\varepsilon}
has compact support, neither
\psi=\psi_{0}
nor
\dot{\psi}
do: as argued above, they are
1/r
localized. Care must be taken with boundary terms when computing the variation of
E_{\textup{DDI}}
, therefore. The variation of
E_{\textup{DDI}}
induced by
\mathbf{n}_{t}
is

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}\>}{\textup{d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}E_{\textup{DDI}}(\mathbf{n}_{t})=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mu}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\textup{d}^{2}x\left(\dot{\psi}\Delta\psi+\psi\Delta\dot{\psi}\right)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\textup{d}^{2}x\,\psi\Delta\dot{\psi}

\displaystyle+\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2\mu}\int_{\partial B_{R}(0)}(\dot{\psi}*\textup{d}\psi-\psi*\textup{d}\dot{\psi})

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\textup{d}^{2}x\,\psi\Delta\dot{\psi}

\displaystyle+\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\frac{R}{2\mu_{0}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}(\dot{\psi}\psi_{r}-\psi\dot{\psi}_{r})\textup{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\textup{d}^{2}x\,\psi\Delta\dot{\psi}

(41)

since
\psi,\dot{\psi}=O(r^{-1})
and
\psi_{r},\dot{\psi}_{r}=O(r^{-2})
. Differentiating (
30
) with respect to
t
, we deduce that

\Delta\dot{\psi}=-\mu\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\varepsilon},

(42)

and hence

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}\>}{\textup{d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}E_{\textup{DDI}}(\mathbf{n}_{t})=\,

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\textup{d}^{2}x\,\psi\left(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\textup{d}^{2}x\,{\bm{\varepsilon}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\psi

\displaystyle-\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\partial B_{R}(0)}\psi*\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\textup{d}^{2}x\,\bm{\varepsilon}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\psi

(43)

by Stokes’s Theorem, since
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{1}\textup{d}x_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}\textup{d}x_{2}
has compact support.

Combining (
38
), (
39
), (
40
) and (
43
), we see that

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}E(\mathbf{n}_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\textup{d}^{2}x\,\bm{\varepsilon}\cdot(\Delta\mathbf{n}-2\mathbf{d}_{i}\times\partial_{i}\mathbf{n}

(44)

\displaystyle\qquad-(h+2K\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{3})\mathbf{e}_{3}+\bm{\nabla}\psi),

and this must vanish for arbitrary
\bm{\varepsilon}
pointwise orthogonal to
\mathbf{n}
.
Hence, the Euler-Lagrange equation for
E
is

\grad E(\mathbf{n}):=P_{\mathbf{n}}(\Delta\mathbf{n}-2\mathbf{d}_{i}\times\partial_{i}\mathbf{n}-(h+2Kn_{3})\mathbf{e}_{3}+\bm{\nabla}\psi)=0,

(45)

where the projector
P_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{u}):=\mathbf{u}-(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n})\mathbf{n}
accounts for the constraint that

|\mathbf{n}|\equiv 1
and
\psi
is determined by the Poisson equation
(
30
). Our notation calls attention to the fact that
\grad E
is formally the
L^{2}
gradient of
E
, that is, the direction in field space in which
E(\mathbf{n})
grows fastest.

VI
Numerical method

We now detail our method for finding magnetization fields
\mathbf{n}
that simultaneously minimize the energy (
32
) and solve Poisson’s equation (
30
).
This method is implemented for NVIDIA CUDA architecture and is adapted from a similar non-local method developed to determine (nuclear) skyrmion crystals stabilized by
\omega
-mesons
[
18
,
23
]
.
Similar methods have also recently been developed to study knot solitons in an extended version of the standard model of particle physics
[
10
]
and the back-reaction of the Coulomb force in an extension of the Skyrme model
[
17
]
.
We follow the same methodology, separating our algorithm into two stages, detailed as follows.

Magnetic skyrmions are local minimizers of the energy functional (
32
).
We choose to numerically relax the energy (
32
) using an accelerated gradient descent based method with flow arresting criteria, known as arrested Newton flow.
It consists of a fourth order central finite difference method, yielding a discrete approximation
E_{\textup{dis}}(\mathbf{n})
to the energy
E(\mathbf{n})
, and is performed on a meshgrid of
N^{2}
lattice sites with lattice spacing
h_{\textup{latt}}
, so the field may be thought of as a point in the discretised configuration space
\mathcal{C}=(S^{2})^{N^{2}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3N^{2}}
. Hence our discrete approximant of the energy functional is a function
E_{\textup{dis}}:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. To find a minimum of

E_{\textup{dis}}
, we start at some initial field
\mathbf{n}(0)\in\mathcal{C}
and solve Newton’s equation for the potential
E_{\textup{dis}}

\ddot{\mathbf{n}}(t)=-\grad E_{\textup{dis}}(\mathbf{n}(t))

(46)

with initial velocity
\dot{\mathbf{n}}(0)=0
using an appropriate time stepping scheme (we use a 4th order Runge-Kutta scheme). This curve will accelerate “downhill” initially, but will not converge to a minimum of
E_{\textup{dis}}
(since the flow conserves total energy, including kinetic energy). To remedy this, after each time step, we check whether
E_{\textup{dis}}(t+\delta t)>E_{\textup{dis}}(t)
: if it is, we set
\dot{\mathbf{n}}(t)=0
and restart the flow at
\mathbf{n}(t)
.
The flow is deemed to have converged when
\lVert\grad E_{\textup{dis}}(\mathbf{n})\rVert_{\infty}<\varepsilon_{\textup{tol}}
, where
\varepsilon_{\textup{tol}}
is some threshold tolerance (we use
\varepsilon_{\textup{tol}}=10^{-6}
).
This scheme is simple, robust, and converges much faster than conventional gradient flow.

In practice, we compute
\grad E_{\textup{dis}}(\mathbf{n})
by discretizing our formula for

\grad E
, (
45
). Of course, constructing

\grad E_{\textup{dis}}(\mathbf{n}(t))
requires us to compute the magnetic potential
\psi(t)
induced by
\mathbf{n}(t)
, that is, we must solve the PDE (
30
) on our numerical mesh. We achieve this by thinking of (
30
) for
\mathbf{n}
fixed as the Euler-Lagrange equation for the functional

F(\psi)=\frac{1}{2}\lVert\textup{d}\psi\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\mu\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\textup{d}^{2}x\,\psi\left(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{n}\right)

(47)

which we minimize using a conjugate gradient method. (Such methods are particularly efficient for minimizing quadratic functionals such as this.)

To summarize, the full numerical algorithm is implemented as follows:

1.

Perform a step of the arrested Newton flow method for the magnetization
\mathbf{n}
using a 4th order Runge-Kutta method.

2.

Solve Poisson’s equation for the magnetic potential
\psi
using nonlinear conjugate gradient descent with the Polak-Ribiere-Polyak method.

3.

Compute the total energy of the configuration
(\mathbf{n}_{n},\psi_{n})
and compare to the energy of the previous configuration
(\mathbf{n}_{n-1},\psi_{n-1})
. If the energy has increased, arrest the flow.

4.

Check the convergence criterion:
\lVert E_{\textup{dis}}(\mathbf{n})\rVert_{\infty}<\varepsilon
. If the convergence criterion has been satisfied, then stop the algorithm.

5.

Repeat the process (return to step 1).

VI.1
Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert dynamics

As a consistency check, we verify our numerics by comparing a selcetion of our results with those obtained using Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert (LLG) dynamics.
The dimensionless LLG equation is

\frac{\textup{d}\mathbf{n}}{\textup{d}\tau}=-\frac{\gamma}{1+\alpha^{2}}\mathbf{n}\times\left(\mathbf{h}_{\textup{eff}}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{h}_{\textup{eff}}\right)

(48)

where
\gamma
is the gyromagnetic ratio,
\alpha
is the Gilbert damping parameter, the effective magnetic field is

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}_{\textup{eff}}=\,

\displaystyle-\frac{\delta E}{\delta\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle-\Delta\mathbf{n}+2\mathbf{d}_{i}\times\partial_{i}\mathbf{n}+(h+2K\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{3})\mathbf{e}_{3}+\mathbf{h}_{\textup{demag}},

(49)

and
\mathbf{h}_{\textup{demag}}=-\bm{\nabla}\psi
is the demagnetization field.
The first term in the LLG equation (
48
) represents precession and the second term damping.
To find static solutions we set
\alpha=1
and
\gamma=1
.
We evolve this using a fourth order Runge–Kutta method.
During the evolution we determine the optimal/appropriate magnetostatic potential
\psi
at each intermediate RK step, i.e. when we compute the slopes
\mathbf{k}_{i}
.
We find that the results are consistent, with the LLG method being significantly slower than the arrested Newton flow method (roughly
1\sim 2
orders of magnitude slower)

VIII
Conclusion

We have developed a theory of magnetic skyrmions that includes the effect of the dipole-dipole interaction in the bulk of chiral ferromagnets.
In other words, we have included the demagnetizing field and its associated back-reaction on the magnetization.
The theory is formulated in terms of an arbitrary DMI and can be extended easily to include any choice of potential.
We focus on three DMI terms, these being the Dresselhaus, Rashba and Heusler, and include the Zeeman interaction and anisotropy.
In conventional chiral magnets without the DDI, all three DMI terms (and their associated skyrmion types) are equivalent up to redefinitions of the fields.
Therefore, they are energy degenerate and, for the parameter set we have chosen, do not form a stable crystalline structure.
The inclusion of the dipolar interaction, and its back-reaction, breaks the energy degeneracy and the symmetry group of the individual magnetic skyrmion.
Furthermore, it provides stability to Heusler antiskyrmions, allowing them to form into a crystalline arrangement.

The micromagnetic crystal simulation method we employed was very simple.
We restricted the geometry of our unit cell to be equianharmonic/lemniscatic, which would normally favour a hexagonal/square crystalline structure.
In both cases, the Dresselhaus and Rashba skyrmions preferred to be infinitely separated, however, the Heusler DMI preferred antiskyrmions in a square lattice arrangement.
Additionally, this remained to be the case for a different choice of parameter set, one which results in larger skyrmions of size
\sim 100\textup{nm}
and is typically used to model Heusler compounds.

An applied external magnetic field was also considered, but the results were qualitatively the same for our parameter set.
However, an external magnetic field in the absence of anisotropies was not considered in this study.
For this case, as the applied magnetic field strength is increased, one would expect to see the transition from the spin-stripe domain to a lattice of skyrmions as found in
[
21
]
.

This method can be adapted to the analogue problem of skyrmions in chiral liquid crystals
[
26
]
.
That is, to study the flexoelectric polarization effect on skyrmions in liquid crystals.
The problem there is slightly more difficult as the Poisson equation depends on the divergence of the polarization
\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{n})
instead of the divergence of the order parameter
\mathbf{n}\in S^{2}

[
24
]
.

Appendix A
Lattice criticality

We have described a numerical scheme that, for a given fixed period lattice

\Lambda=\{\alpha_{1}\mathbf{v}_{1}+\alpha_{2}\mathbf{v_{2}}:\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}

(51)

and topological degree, produces the minimizer among all fields
\mathbf{n}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow S^{2}
periodic with respect to
\Lambda

\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{v}_{1})\equiv\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{v}_{2})\equiv\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})

(52)

of the energy of a unit cell,

\displaystyle E_{\textup{cell}}=\,

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\textup{d}^{2}x\,\left\{\frac{1}{2}|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{n}|^{2}+\mathbf{d}_{i}\cdot(\mathbf{n}\times\partial_{i}\mathbf{n})+V(\mathbf{n})\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{1}{2\mu}\psi\Delta\psi\right\}.

(53)

Here
\Sigma=\mathbb{R}^{2}/\Lambda\equiv T^{2}
is the torus with period lattice
\Lambda
(or, in more elementary terms, its unit cell, that is the parallelogram spanned by the vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2}
). We have left the potential terms
V(\mathbf{n})
arbitrary, since our argument does not rely on the specific potential (
4
) used in this paper.
One should note that such a minimizer is likely to exist for
any
choice of period lattice
\Lambda
. If this solution is to have any physical significance, it should be a local minimizer of
E_{\textup{cell}}
with respect to variations of the period lattice
\Lambda
also. Previous studies of soliton crystals in other systems show that, in general, the energetically optimal period lattice may have unexpectedly low symmetry, being neither square nor triangular
[
35
,
22
]
, so this issue is not negligible.
In this appendix, we formulate this criticality condition as a set of explicit integral constraints, suitable for numerical verification, adapting ideas introduced in
[
34
]
.

Let
\mathbf{n}:\Sigma\rightarrow S^{2}
be a fixed field on the torus
\Sigma={\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\Lambda
, assumed to minimize
E_{\textup{cell}}
among all fields in its homotopy class. Denote by
\psi:\Sigma\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
its associated magnetic potential. Any other period lattice
\widetilde{\Lambda}
is generated by
M\mathbf{v}_{1}
,
M\mathbf{v}_{2}
, where
M\in\textup{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})
is some invertible matrix. Furthermore there is a canonical magnetization field on the new torus
\widetilde{\Sigma}={\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\widetilde{\Lambda}
, namely
\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{x}):=\mathbf{n}(A\mathbf{x})
where
A=M^{-1}
. This has an associated magnetic potential
\widetilde{\psi}:\widetilde{\Sigma}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
, which, by definition satisfies the equation

-(\partial_{1}^{2}+\partial_{2}^{2})\widetilde{\psi}=-\mu(\partial_{1}\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}+\partial_{2}\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{2}).

(54)

Note that
\widetilde{\psi}
is
not
simply
\psi(A\mathbf{x})
in general.
Having fixed the mapping
\mathbf{n}
,
E_{\textup{cell}}
is a smooth function of
A\in\textup{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})
. We require that this function is locally minimal at
A={\mathbb{I}}_{2}
. In particular,
{\mathbb{I}}_{2}
must be a critical point of
E_{\textup{cell}}:\textup{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
.

All tori are diffeomorphic, and it is helpful to identify the new torus
\widetilde{\Sigma}
with the reference torus
\Sigma
via the linear diffeomorphism
M:\Sigma\rightarrow\widetilde{\Sigma}
. From this viewpoint, the magnetization field
\mathbf{n}:\Sigma\rightarrow S^{2}
remains fixed, but the metric on
\Sigma
, its area form, the DMI vectors and the source of the magnetic potential
\widetilde{\psi}
depend on
M
. The metric is the pullback of the Euclidean metric on
\widetilde{\Sigma}
by
M
,

\widetilde{g}=(M^{T}M)_{ij}\textup{d}x_{i}\textup{d}x_{j},

(55)

its area form is
\det M\,\textup{d}x_{1}\wedge\textup{d}x_{2}
, the DMI vectors are

\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{i}=A_{ij}\mathbf{d}_{j},

(56)

and
\widehat{\psi}:=\psi\circ L:\Sigma\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies Poisson’s equation on
(\Sigma,\widetilde{g})

\widetilde{\Delta}\widehat{\psi}=\widehat{\rho}

(57)

with source

\widehat{\rho}=-\mu A_{ij}\partial_{i}n_{j}.

(58)

In equation (
57
),

\widetilde{\Delta}=-A_{ik}A_{jk}\frac{\partial^{2}\>}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}

(59)

is the Laplacian on
\Sigma
with respect to the deformed metric
\widetilde{g}
.
One finds that

\displaystyle E_{\textup{exch}}^{\textup{cell}}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\det A}\tr(A^{T}HA)

(60)

\displaystyle E_{\textup{DMI}}^{\textup{cell}}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\det A}\tr(A^{T}{\mathscr{D}})

(61)

\displaystyle E_{\textup{pot}}^{\textup{cell}}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\det A}\int_{\Sigma}\textup{d}^{2}x\,V(\mathbf{n})=\frac{E_{\textup{pot}}^{\textup{cell}}({\mathbb{I}}_{2})}{\det A}

(62)

\displaystyle E_{\textup{DDI}}^{\textup{cell}}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mu}\int_{\Sigma}\frac{\textup{d}^{2}x}{\det A}\,\widehat{\psi}\widetilde{\Delta}\widehat{\psi},

(63)

where
H
and
{\mathscr{D}}
are the matrices

\displaystyle H_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\textup{d}^{2}x\,\partial_{i}\mathbf{n}\cdot\partial_{j}\mathbf{n}

(64)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{D}}_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\textup{d}^{2}x\,\mathbf{d}_{i}\cdot(\mathbf{n}\times\partial_{j}\mathbf{n}).

(65)

Although we do not have an explicit formula for
E_{\textup{DDI}}^{\textup{cell}}(A)
, as we do for each of the other terms, we will still be able to compute its derivative at
{\mathbb{I}}_{2}
explicitly, and this will suffice for our purposes.

As a consistency check, let us derive the condition for
E_{\textup{cell}}
to be critical with respect to isotropic dilations of the period lattice. These are generated by the curve
A(t)=e^{-t}{\mathbb{I}}_{2}
. The corresponding magnetic potential satisfies

e^{-2t}\Delta\widehat{\psi}=-\mu e^{-t}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{n},

(66)

so
\widehat{\psi}=e^{t}\psi
.
Hence the condition for criticality is

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2}\tr H+e^{t}\tr{\mathscr{D}}+e^{2t}E_{\textup{pot}}^{\textup{cell}}({\mathbb{I}}_{2})\right.

(67)

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{1}{2\mu}\int_{\Sigma}\textup{d}^{2}x\,e^{t}\psi e^{-2t}\Delta e^{t}\psi e^{2t}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tr{\mathscr{D}}+2E_{\textup{pot}}^{\textup{cell}}({\mathbb{I}}_{2})+\frac{1}{2\mu}\int_{\Sigma}\textup{d}^{2}x\,\psi\Delta\psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{\textup{DMI}}^{\textup{cell}}({\mathbb{I}}_{2})+2(E_{\textup{pot}}^{\textup{cell}}({\mathbb{I}}_{2})+E_{\textup{DDI}}^{\textup{cell}}({\mathbb{I}}_{2}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

which coincides precisely with the Derrick scaling constraint (
35
) evaluated on a single lattice cell. This constraint is automatically satisfied by our numerical solutions since our scheme minimizes
E_{\textup{cell}}
with respect to the area of the lattice cell.

We now analyze the 3 dimensional space of lattice variations complementary to isotropic dilations. Let
A(t)
be a curve in
\textup{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
with
A(0)={\mathbb{I}}_{2}
generating a variation of
\Lambda
through lattices whose unit cells have fixed area. Then
\varepsilon:=\dot{A}(0)
is traceless. The induced variations of the various terms in
E_{\textup{cell}}({\mathbb{I}}_{2})
are

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}E_{\textup{exch}}^{\textup{cell}}(A(t))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tr(\varepsilon^{T}H)

(68)

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}E_{\textup{DMI}}^{\textup{cell}}(A(t))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tr(\varepsilon^{T}{\mathscr{D}})

(69)

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}E_{\textup{pot}}^{\textup{cell}}(A(t))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(70)

The variation of
E_{\textup{DDI}}^{\textup{cell}}
is considerably subtler. Let us denote by
\Delta_{t}
the Laplacian on
\Sigma
with respect to the metric
\widetilde{g}(t)
, that is,

\Delta_{t}=-(A(t)A(t)^{T})_{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j},

(71)

and by
\psi_{t}
the solution of

\Delta_{t}\psi_{t}=\rho_{t},

(72)

where

\rho_{t}=-\mu A_{ij}(t)\partial_{i}n_{j}.

(73)

Note that
{\psi}_{0}=\psi
. Further, let an overdot denote the derivative with respect to
t
at
t=0
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}E_{\textup{DDI}}^{\textup{cell}}(A(t))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mu}\int_{\Sigma}\textup{d}^{2}x\left(\dot{\psi}\Delta\psi+\psi\Delta\dot{\psi}+\psi\dot{\Delta}\psi\right)

(74)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu}\int_{\Sigma}\textup{d}^{2}x\left(\psi\Delta\dot{\psi}+\frac{1}{2}\psi\dot{\Delta}\psi\right).

Differentiating (
72
), we see that

\Delta\dot{\psi}=\dot{\rho}-\dot{\Delta}\psi=-\mu\varepsilon_{ij}\partial_{i}n_{j}-2\varepsilon_{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi.

(75)

Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}E_{\textup{DDI}}^{\textup{cell}}(A(t))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu}\int_{\Sigma}\textup{d}^{2}x\,\varepsilon_{ij}\psi\left(-\mu\partial_{i}n_{j}+\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi\right)

(76)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tr(\varepsilon^{T}Q),

where

Q_{ij}=\int_{\Sigma}\textup{d}^{2}x\,\psi\left(\frac{1}{\mu}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi-\partial_{j}n_{i}\right).

(77)

Note that
Q
is traceless by (
30
).

The condition that
E_{\textup{cell}}
is critical with respect to all area preserving lattice variations is therefore that

\tr\left(\varepsilon(H+{\mathscr{D}}+Q)\right)=0

(78)

for all traceless matrices
\varepsilon
. This is equivalent to the condition that

H+{\mathscr{D}}+Q=\lambda{\mathbb{I}}_{2}

(79)

for some
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Taking traces of both sides we see that

\lambda=E_{\textup{exch}}^{\textup{cell}}+\frac{1}{2}E_{\textup{DMI}}^{\textup{cell}}.

(80)

To summarize, a doubly periodic map
\mathbf{n}:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\Lambda\rightarrow S^{2}
, inducing magnetic potential
\psi
, is critical with respect to all variations of the period lattice
\Lambda
if it satisfies the scaling identity
(
67
) and the angular virial constraint (
79
), where the matrices
H,{\mathscr{D}},Q
are defined in (
64
), (
65
), (
77
), and all integrals are over a single unit cell of the lattice
\Lambda
. These conditions are easily checked numerically.

To numerically verify the extended virial constraints [(
67
),(
79
)], we compute the energy normalized constraints
\{V_{1}=0,V_{2}=0\}
, where

\displaystyle V_{1}=\,

\displaystyle\frac{E^{\textup{cell}}_{\textup{DMI}}+2\left(E^{\textup{cell}}_{\textup{pot}}+E^{\textup{cell}}_{\textup{DDI}}\right)}{E_{\textup{cell}}},

(81)

\displaystyle V_{2}=\,

\displaystyle\frac{H+{\mathscr{D}}+Q-\lambda{\mathbb{I}}_{2}}{E_{\textup{cell}}}.

(82)

We find that both of these normalized constraints are satisfied to within
\textup{err}=10^{-5}
for the square Heusler antiskyrmion lattice at a lattice parameter of
L_{*}=39\,\textup{nm}
.
