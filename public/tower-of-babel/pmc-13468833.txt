The significance of the ketolytic gene OXCT1 in metabolism, intracellular signaling and disease development
Authors: Karolina Górecka, Kamila Soboska, Marcin Pacholczyk, Luciano Pirola, Aneta Balcerczyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
OXCT1 (3-oxoacid CoA-transferase 1), encoding the mitochondrial enzyme SCOT (succinyl-CoA:3-ketoacid CoA transferase), is a key enzyme in ketone body utilization. It catalyzes the reversible transfer of CoA from succinyl-CoA to acetoacetate, generating acetoacetyl-CoA, which is subsequently converted into acetyl-CoA for entry into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and mitochondrial energy production. Although classically regarded as a metabolic enzyme, emerging evidence indicates that OXCT1 participates in broader regulatory networks. In addition to its protein-coding transcript, the OXCT1 locus produces regulatory non-coding RNAs, including circ-OXCT1 and lncRNA OXCT1-AS1, which provides additional levels of regulation, as for now reported in various types of cancer. Moreover, the review summarizes current knowledge on OXCT1 biochemical function, regulation, and tissue distribution, with emphasis on transcriptional control, post-translational modifications such as lysine succinylation and redox-dependent regulation, and integration with nutrient-sensing and stress-response pathways. By combining recent literature with bioinformatics analysis, we demonstrate that OXCT1 displays highly dynamic expression across diverse cancer types and metabolic states, consistent with a role in metabolic plasticity and disease progression. Furthermore, we discuss here the OXCT1 role in other pathological conditions including metabolic disorders, neurological disease, and cardiomyopathy, implicating both metabolic dysfunction and aberrant protein modification in disease mechanisms. Collectively, these findings establish OXCT1 as a central regulator of metabolic adaptation and a potential therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06264-4.

Introduction

Metabolism of ketone bodies has been known for decades. Discovered in diabetic urine in the mid-19th century, ketone bodies were long viewed as by-products of incomplete fat oxidation [1]. Over the years it became apparent that ketone bodies utilization constitutes an important contributor in providing the energy substrates for extrahepatic tissues, especially during starvation, increased physical activity or on the course of maintaining certain types of low-carbohydrate diets [2]. Recently, there was a noticeable increase of interest on how ketone bodies and their metabolism can interact with intracellular signaling or causes and progression of broad spectrum disorders, ranging from obesity [3, 4], certain types of cancers [5] and immune responses [6, 7]. Although considerable attention is often devoted to the main ketone body, β-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) and its signaling properties [8], it is likely that the role of two rate-limiting enzymes for the metabolism of ketone bodies – HMGCS2 (3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA synthase 2) and OXCT1 could be far more complex than simply controlling the synthesis and utilization of these metabolites.

The following review aims to provide a thorough analysis regarding role of OXCT1 beyond ketolysis. Based on both available literature data and bioinformatics analyses, we present the potential involvement of OXCT1 in the development of broad spectrum of pathologies, including OXCT1 deficiency and mutation syndromes. Our discussion in this review is focused on three systems within which OXCT1 expression is particularly abundant. We aim to describe the role of OXCT1 in the contribution to cardiovascular complications, its impact on muscular metabolism and nervous system. The final section will address the role of OXCT1 in cancer biology, describing its role in several types of cancer, potential links to chemotherapy resistance and the oncogenic/tumor suppressing role of OXCT1-related non-coding RNAs.

Methodology of literature search

Medline and Scopus databases were surveyed for original articles and reviews with the following keywords and terms, used alone or in combination: β-hydroxyburyrate, β-hydroxybutyrylation, calorie restriction, cancer, cardiovascular diseases, circ-OXCT1, diabetes, diabetic cardiomyopathy, energy production, heart failure, inflammation, ketogenic diet, ketone bodies, ketolysis, ketogenesis, lncRNA OXCT1-AS1, neurological diseases, OXCT1, SCOT, succinyl-CoA, succinyltransferase, succinylation. The figures were created with BioRender.com.

Data acquisition and processing

To enable direct comparison between primary tumor (TP) and normal tissue (NT) samples expression data (RSEM-normalized count) for the OXCT1 gene were retrieved from TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas, at https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga) datasets using the FirebrowseR (R) package (ver. 1.1.35). The samples were filtered to include only those with available OXCT1 expression. The resulting datasets comprising OXCT1 expression in TP and NT samples were exported (as.pzfx package) to save the processed data in the GraphPad Prism-compatible format.

Overall survival (OS) analyses were conducted based on OXCT1 expression in TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas, at https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga) datasets using the Bioconductor (R) package cBioPortalData (ver. 2.16.0). The relevant TCGA study was retrieved as a MultiAssayExperiment object, providing both RNA-Seq gene expression and comprehensive clinical data integration. Samples were filtered to include only those with available OXCT1 expression and complete OS information. OXCT1 mRNA expression values were extracted from TCGA RNA-Seq profiles (RSEM-normalized counts). Patients were stratified into “high” and “low” OXCT1 expression groups using the cohort median as the threshold. Overall survival time and status were defined using the clinical OS_MONTHS (time in months) and OS_STATUS (censored if living, event if deceased) variables. The resulting datasets comprising OXCT1 groupings and survival information were exported (as.pzfx package) to save the processed data in the GraphPad Prism-compatible format.

Consensus expression levels of the OXCT1 gene across a broad range of normal human tissues were retrieved from the GTEx database (release v10; accessed on 03/24/2026) and are presented as median normalized expression values (nTPM).

Statistical analysis, survival analysis and visualization

Expression levels of the OXCT1 gene in primary tumor (TP) and solid tissue normal (NT) samples were compared to assess differential expression between cancerous and non-cancerous tissues. For statistical analysis, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney test (also known as the Wilcoxon rank-sum test) was performed using GraphPad Prim 10.3.1 (GraphPad, San Diego, California, USA) to compare the distribution of OXCT1 expression values between the two groups.

All survival analysis and visualizations were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.3.1 (GraphPad, San Diego, California, USA). Kaplan-Meier analyses were conducted to compare overall survival between the “high” and “low” OXCT1 expression groups. Median survival, hazard ratios, confidence intervals, and statistical significance were calculated by Prism’s built-in tools, with results visualized as survival curves. The results of statistical analyses are indicated on the graphs, with significance levels as follows: **p < 0.01 and ***p < 0.001; N/A - not assessed due to insufficient sample number in control (normal) tissue group.

OXCT1: expression, regulation, and functional versatility

The OXCT1 gene product, known as OXCT1, but also SCOT (succinyl-CoA:3-ketoacid CoA transferase), is a mitochondrial enzyme that plays a central role in ketone body utilization by catalyzing the transfer of CoA from succinyl-CoA to acetoacetate, generating acetoacetyl-CoA (see Fig. 1). This reaction constitutes a rate limiting step in ketolysis, linking ketone body metabolism with mitochondrial energy production. Beyond this canonical metabolic function, emerging evidence indicates that OXCT1 may also act as a protein succinyltransferase, thereby contributing to the regulation of the cellular “succinylome.” Recent work [9] demonstrated that OXCT1 can directly catalyze lysine succinylation of LACTB (serine beta-lactamase-like protein), implicating it in the modulation of mitochondrial function and cellular metabolism. In parallel, additional findings suggest that OXCT1 itself can be regulated by succinylation (e.g., via SUCLA2; succinyl-CoA synthetase ADP-forming beta subunit), highlighting a potential feedback mechanism linking succinyl-CoA metabolism with post-translational modification dynamics [10]. Despite these advances, the scope and specificity of OXCT1-mediated succinylation remain largely undefined. It is unclear whether OXCT1 selectively targets specific proteins or acts more broadly depending on cellular context. Furthermore, an additional, unresolved question is whether OXCT1 indirectly influences global protein succinylation by modulating intracellular succinyl-CoA availability, thereby coupling its enzymatic role in ketolysis with wider regulatory effects on post-translational modification landscapes. Together, these findings position OXCT1 at the intersection of both metabolic and epigenetic-like regulation.

According to GTEx data, OXCT1 shows especially high expression in the brain structures (e.g. cerebellum, cortex) and cardiovascular system (e.g. left ventricle). Interestingly, expression of the OXCT1 gene may vary across different tissues within specific systems/organs. For example, the left ventricle exhibits significantly higher OXCT1 expression compared to the atrial appendage (median expression level 58.66 vs. 45.29 (nTPM); Mann-Whitney test, p = 0.0026), similarly to the esophageal muscularis in comparison to the mucosa (median expression level 44.44 vs. 28.92 (nTPM); Mann-Whitney test, p < 0.0001). Observed variations reflect the critical role of ketone body oxidation in supporting mitochondrial energy production in these tissues. Nevertheless, OXCT1 is also moderately expressed in nearly all extrahepatic tissues, including skeletal muscle, pancreas, and the gastrointestinal tract, underscoring its systemic importance in energy homeostasis during metabolic stress or fasting.

Ketogenic diets, characterized by low carbohydrate intake and a high proportion of fatty acids, promote a state of ketosis and adherence to this type of diet can be associated with improved insulin sensitivity [22]. In general, composition of the ketogenic diet and related metabolic outcomes of this dietary regimen can be linked to GLUTs and PPARs expression and action. Unsaturated and saturated fatty acids provided by a ketogenic diet may be the ligands for PPARs [23, 24] while alteration in insulin signaling can result in changes in translocation of GLUT4 [25]. For example, in C2C12 cells, supplementation with BHB was associated with regulation of Akt/Gsk3β pathway and the increase of GLUT4 levels on the cellular membrane [26], while mice with cardiac-specific overexpression of the insulin-independent glucose transporter Glut1 were characterized by downregulated expression of Oxct1 in myocardium [27]. Although, multiple studies have determined that the expression of HMGCS2 is regulated through PPARs [28]; [29]; [30], it is not known whether this type of regulation also applies to Oxct1 ([29]). This issue is worth investigating given the noticeable expression of OXCT1 in adipose tissue (Fig. 2) and the importance of PPARγ in adipogenesis [31]. However, there is a possibility that expression of OXCT1 might be regulated by PGC-1α (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor c coactivator 1α). Silencing of Pparg1a reduce transcript levels of Oxct1 (as well as Bdh1 and Acat1) in brain, kidney, heart and skeletal muscle, whereas overexpression of PGC-1α in C2C12 myotubes enhance expression of aforementioned genes [32]. The overexpression of Pparg1a was connected to increase in transcript of Esrra (estrogen-related receptor alpha). Intriguingly, knockdown of Esrra reduced transcript levels of both Oxct1 and Acat1 (with no effect on Bdh1). It is highly likely that ERRα constitute an important factor facilitating induction of ketolytic gene transcription by PGC-1α in muscle cells. OXCT1 could be also modified post-translationally, via either nitration or succinylation. The aspects of nitration of Oxct1 were studied in detail in rodents’ models (as discussed in the Sect. Diabetic cardiomyopathy). Tyrosine nitration or simultaneous addition of nitro and hydroxy groups to tryptophan could either impair the catalytic activity of the protein [14, 15] or render it more susceptible to in vitro proteolysis [19]. Succinylation of OXCT1 was described only in one study [10]. OXCT1 interacts with SUCLA2 upon insulin like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) stimulation in HCC cells. Upon association with SUCLA2, OXCT1 generates succinyl-CoA, which could not only serve as its substrate, but could also directly succinylate OXCT1 at K421 of the amino acid sequence. This consequently activates the protein, leading to increased ketolysis, production of acetyl-CoA and ATP, and HCC cell proliferation.

OXCT1 deficiency and related disorders of ketone body metabolism

There are several hereditary metabolic disorders stemming from mutations in genes that code for proteins with metabolic functions. MCAD (medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase) deficiency is relatively well known and, due to its prevalence and the high mortality risk it poses to newborns, it is part of routine infant screening in many countries. However, mutations in genes crucial to the metabolism of ketone bodies are less prevalent and less threatening; therefore, they are often underdiagnosed or overlooked. Moreover, clinical features of patients with OXCT1 and ACAT1 mutations are inconsistent and depend on the type of the modified amino acids, which creates another challenge to proper diagnosis. For example, although, mutations in ACAT1 gene may alter acylcarnitine profiles in blood, this feature is not consistently observed in some individuals, creating problems in establishing a uniform diagnostic marker [33].

OXCT1 deficiency follows an autosomal recessive mode of inheritance. The majority of the OXCT1 mutations have been identified in compound-heterozygosity [36], therefore it is not possible to directly assess the impact of single mutation in vivo and characterize its clinical features. Nevertheless, in most reported cases, patients experience episodes of ketoacidosis, which usually starts during the neonatal period and often coexist with infection or develop during it [37, 38]. Intriguingly, several of the identified mutations, create temperature-sensitive mutated proteins, which may explain why the patients developed ketoacidosis during febrile illness [34, 38].

In case of OXCT1 deficiency, it is recommended to avoid prolonged fasting and excessive fat intake and restrict protein intake to reduce ketogenesis [51]. In other words, ketogenic diet may lead to potential adverse effects in individuals with inborn dysfunction in ketone bodies metabolism. This could be potentially alarming, given the possibility of underdiagnosis of OXCT1 deficiency in the population, since this type of genetic defect usually presents itself with mild symptoms and could be confirmed only via enzyme studies or mutation analysis and, at present, newborns are not screened for this type of deficiency [48, 49]. Moreover, given the increasing popularity of the ketogenic diet for the treatment of obesity and related disorders, it is possible that mildly symptomatic individuals with undiagnosed OXCT1 deficiency could be at risk if a ketogenic diet is improperly managed.

Contribution of OXCT1 to cardiovascular complications

Overview of cardiac energy metabolism

The mammalian heart is a highly energy-demanding organ, due to the continuous need for ATP to support its functions. Therefore, myocardium demonstrates remarkable metabolic flexibility, ranging from avid utilization of fatty acids (FAs) as the predominant substrate, (60–90% of cardiac ATP), with glucose, lactate and ketone bodies supplying the remainder [52, 53]. However, this balance is quite dynamic and fluctuates depending on substrate availability, hormonal regulation, physical activity or changes in plethora of pathological stages (Fig. 3). Metabolic perturbations in glucose and fatty acid metabolism observed in diabetes and heart failure have deleterious effects. In both forms of diabetes, an overreliance on fatty acids as an energy source, due to either lack of insulin production (type 1 diabetes) or impairments in glucose uptake due to increased fatty acid availability (type 2 diabetes) results in the development of hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia [54]. Similarly, in non-diabetic heart failure, a decrease in cardiac mitochondrial oxidative metabolism and glucose oxidation leads to an energy deprived state, which as a consequence impact proper functions of the heart, rendering it unable to adequately pump enough blood to meet the body’s needs for nutrients and oxygen [53, 55].Fig. 3Cardiac substrate utilization in health, diabetes, and heart failure. Recent evidence suggest that the increased myocardial ketone utilization is an adaptive mechanism providing cardioprotection, which has led to growing interest in strategies to enhance ketone oxidation, such as SGLT2 (Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2) inhibitors and ketone esters [55]. Despite the relatively high expression of OXCT1 in the heart compared with other tissues and organs (Fig. 2), which signify its importance for cardiac metabolism, only a few studies on ketone bodies in the heart specifically addressed OXCT1 function. In a recent study, ketone catabolism in heart was shown to be essential for maintaining cardiac function during aging [57]. OXCT1 deletion resulted in significant impairments, such as contractile dysfunction and fibrosis. Intriguingly, ketogenic diet partially restored myocardial functions, suggesting that cardioprotective effects of ketogenic diet do not solely depend on their oxidation. However, the role of OXCT1 beyond ketolysis, including potential signaling functions (see sections: Heart failure; Circ-OXCT1 and lncRNA OXCT1-AS1) remains largely unknown. Diabetic cardiomyopathy

Due to impaired glucose utilization in the diabetic heart, ketone bodies may serve as an alternative energy fuel. This is reflected by the decreased uptake of glucose, lactate and pyruvate accompanied by increased uptake of total ketone bodies and BHB in the diabetic vs. non-diabetic patients [58].

Although OXCT1 has been proposed as a potential prognostic marker in diabetic cardiomyopathy, thus far, this has been demonstrated only by bioinformatic approach [59]. Nevertheless, experimental studies show that the expression of both Bdh1 and Oxct1 is reduced in the murine diabetic myocardium in models of streptozotocin-induced type 1 diabetes as well as diet-induced glucose intolerance. Moreover, expression of Bdh1, but not Oxct1, appears to be regulated by protein O-GlcNAcylation [60]. Noteworthy, PTMs of OXCT1 such as tyrosine nitration (Table 1) may also contribute to cardiovascular complications in diabetes. Elevated blood glucose levels increase intracellular ROS production in diabetic tissues, which in turn may promote various protein modifications including sulfonation, nitration, and nitrosylation, ultimately contributing to mitochondrial dysfunction and cardiac impairment [17].

Heart failure

The failing heart is an avid consumer of ketone bodies, which is marked by an increased myocardial utilization of BHB and upregulation of expression of ketolytic enzymes [61]; [62]. According to Byrne et al. [63] elevation in circulating BHB reduces cardiac inflammation in HF (heart failure), likely through anti-inflammatory properties of BHB. Although the increase in BHB concentration was achieved by skeletal muscle specific deletion of Oxct1, no changes in its cardiac expression levels were observed. These findings suggest that the beneficial effects of therapeutic strategies aimed at elevating ketone body levels in HF largely depend on the signaling properties of BHB rather than solely on its role as an energy substrate. A common feature observed in heart failure, cardiomyopathy, and myocardial infarction is the presence of hypoxia, which leads to energetic insufficiency, due to lack of oxygen [64]; [65]. Although ketone bodies have been identified as an alternative fuel source for the failing heart, their effects on cardiomyocytes under hypoxic conditions are poorly understood. According to Chen et al. [66] hypoxia contributes to the elevated expression of the OXCT1, ACAT1, and BDH2 while reducing the levels of succinyl-CoA in human cardiomyocyte cell line H9C2. Importantly, the treatment with the HIF-1α (Hypoxia Inducible Factor 1 Subunit Alpha) inhibitor LW6 restored the levels of succinyl-CoA and to some extend downregulated the expression of ACAT1 and BDH2, however with no impact on OXCT1. It is worth mentioning that this study did not address the functional consequences of elevated ketone bodies metabolism under hypoxia, leaving the regulatory mechanisms underlying this response unresolved. However, succinate, the product of the reaction catalyzed by OXCT1 (Fig. 1), is a well-known inhibitor of prolyl hydroxylases and its accumulation leads to the stabilization and activation of HIF-1α when SDH (succinate dehydrogenase) is inhibited [67]. Although not yet studied, whether overexpression of OXCT1 could have similar effects on HIF-1α stabilization as SDH inhibition could be a valuable question worth exploring, particularly in the aspect of highly vascular tumors, given the potential oncogenic role of OXCT1 in some types of cancer (see section: Oncogene or tumor suppressor? The impact of OXCT1 on cancer progression). Intriguingly, heart failure is often connected to abnormalities in succinyl-CoA metabolism [68]. Consistent with Chen et al. [66] findings, mice with myocardial infarction (MI) are characterized by a significant decrease in myocardial succinyl-CoA levels alongside with impaired mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) capacity [68]. Succinyl-CoA in the myocardium could be utilized for both ketolysis and heme synthesis. Indeed, MI mice presented with an increase in the expression of Oxct1 and myocardial heme levels. Given the abundance of succinyl-CoA within the heart and mentioned in our review possibility of OXCT1 acting as a succinyl-transferase (see sections: OXCT1: expression, regulation, and functional versatility; Hepatocellular carcinoma), it may be valuable to further investigate the interplay between ketone bodies metabolism, succinyl-CoA availability, and epigenetic regulation in the failing heart. Finally it is noteworthy that succinylation of numerous proteins is altered in ischemic HF [69]. However, the precise consequences of these alterations remainunknown, which advocate for the need for further studies into the role of succinylation in cardiac dysfunction.

Impact of OXCT1 on muscle metabolism

Multiple studies indicate that disturbances in skeletal muscle metabolism, whether involving glucose, fatty acid, and branched-chain amino acid (BCAAs: leucine, isoleucine, and valine), contribute to pathological states such as obesity-induced hyperglycemia often followed by or coexisting with insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes [70]; [71]; [72]. Although many data support the beneficial effects of the ketogenic diet on glycemic normalisation and mitigation of inflammation, increasing evidence suggests that enhanced ketone body oxidation in skeletal muscle may instead promote insulin resistance, consequently leading to T2D [73].

Skeletal muscle specific silencing of Oxct1, as well as OXCT1 pharmacological inhibition, lead to the increase in the activity of pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) in obese animals, and reversed obesity-induced hyperglycemia [70]. Lean mice, however, while not exhibiting differences in glucose and fat metabolism, likely due to their overall metabolic fitness, nevertheless display alterations in BCAA catabolism under low-fat feeding conditions [74]. OXCT1 can markedly diminish the expression of the Bcat2 gene (encoding mitochondrial Branched Chain Amino Acid Transaminase 2), whereas overexpression of Oxct1 results in an opposite effect. Dysregulation within BCAA metabolism is often defective in obese humans and animals and can contribute to the pathogenesis of insulin resistance and type II diabetes [75]; [76].

Silencing or pharmacological inhibition of OXCT1 elevates ketone body levels by blocking their oxidation. This suggests that some glycemic effect of ketogenic diet or exogenous ketone esters/salts administration may be mediated by signalling roles of ketone bodies, such as inhibition of class I HDACs (histone deacetylases) by BHB [77]. Inhibition of class I HDACs has been shown to improve glucose homeostasis in diabetic mice, which was associated with enhancement in skeletal muscle oxidative metabolism [78]. Moreover, interventions targeting HDACs may be promising for the treatment of sarcopenia [79]. Nevertheless, HDACs activity and signaling have not been studied in the aspects directed on inhibition of ketone metabolism in the skeletal muscle. Therefore whether it would be possible to prevent muscle histone and protein deacetylation by targeting ketone body metabolism remains to be determined. Moreover, the role of BHB as an HDACs inhibitor remains controversial, since several studies did not confirm it [80, 81].

With respect to muscle function, OXCT1 has recently been identified as a positive regulator of muscle mass [82]. Accordingly, OXCT1 expression is significantly downregulated in skeletal muscle of mice across three sarcopenia models: cancer cachexia, fasting and diabetes. Reduced OXCT1 expression may impair the ketolytic capacity and contribute to muscular dysfunction. Moreover, bioinformatic analyses further support this role: OXCT1 is downregulated in muscle after spinal cord injury [83] and in patients with primary Sjögren’s syndrome (SS), a chronic, autoimmune rheumatic disease [84]. In SS, reduced OXCT1 expression was linked to impaired mitochondrial fatty acid beta -oxidation and increased adipose tissue deposition within salivary glands. SS is also often associated with muscle weakness and inflammatory myopathies due to the chronic inflammation [85]; [86]. Moreover, patients with primary SS may experience widespread pain and functional disability which also contributes to the sarcopenia development [86].

Impact of ketone bodies metabolism on the nervous system. The significance of OXCT1

The brain is one of the organs that largely depends on ketone bodies utilization, particularly during starvation or the neonatal period, as proven by the development of modest hyperketonemia among murine neonates with neuron-specific Oxct1 knockout [87]. In the absence of neuronal ketone body oxidation, glycolysis and oxidative glucose metabolism are upregulated, likely to meet the energetic demands of the developing neonatal brain.

To date, numerous clinical studies as well as in vivo rodent models have demonstrated potential effectiveness of ketogenic diet or ketone administration in curbing a broad spectrum of neurological disorders (Table 4), including neurodegenerative diseases and depression. For example, in rats, ketogenic diet was proven to be effective in curbing chronic pain [88] and restricting chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy [89] by activation of PPARγ signaling pathway.

Neurological effects of ketogenic diet or ketone administration in preclinical models

Synthesis and utilization rate of ketone bodies is also probably significant in the proper functioning of hippocampus, via the increase of autophagy rate in response to ketogenic diet feeding [100]. It is likely that some reported neuroprotective effects of ketogenic diet may partially mediated by autophagy mechanisms [101, 102]. Compared with the frontal cortex, the hippocampus exhibits a stronger response to nutritional ketosis, reflected in higher levels of BDH1 and OXCT1. Recent findings further highlight the role of OXCT1 in hippocampal neurogenesis: Qiu et al. [103] demonstrated that Oxct1 promotes neurogenesis via the Akt/GSK-3β/β-catenin pathway in a subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) model, consequently leading to the improvement in cognitive function. To this date, increased hippocampal neurogenesis has been reported to boost cognitive function in both Alzheimer’s disease [104] as well as traumatic brain injury [105]. Regarding the latter, it could be achieved via intermittent fasting. Additionally, according to Zhuang et al. [106] Oxct1 displays neuroprotective effects via the SIRT3-SOD2 pathway after traumatic brain injury. Oxct1 was found to significantly increase the expression of SIRT3 and reduce the proportion of acetylated SOD2, thereby blunting the production of ROS in the injured hippocampal neurons, reducing neuronal death, and consequently improving cognitive function. Beyond injury models, Oxct1 was identified as one of the genes linked to chronic mild stress response being significantly downregulated in the pituitary of rats susceptible to chronic mild stress-induced depression [107].

OXCT1 also plays a role in the peripheral nervous system. Enders et al. [108] demonstrated that sensory neuron-specific Oxct1 knockout in mice causes profound neurological changes, including sensory deficits, gait dysfunction, axonal abnormalities, and myelination pathology. Myelination defects were characterized by reduced intraepidermal nerve fiber (IENF) density among Oxct1 knockout mice. Dermal biopsies of IENFs are at present used as a clinical tool for diagnosing peripheral neuropathies in humans [109] and IENF density was previously described as significantly lower among Friedreich’s ataxia (FRDAs) patients [110]. Given that ketone-derived acetyl groups represent a preferred substrate for myelin sterol biosynthesis in rodent brain and nerves, the study also found that Oxct1 deficiency was associated with enlargement of myelinated peptidergic DRG soma, suggesting that reduced axonal ketone utilization may increase ketone availability for myelin production [108].

Alterations in ketone metabolism have also been linked to major neurodegenerative disorders. In early stages of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), BDH1 and OXCT1 are strongly downregulated in mouse motor neurons, while ACAT2 is upregulated [111]. This shift may direct acetoacetyl-CoA toward cholesterol synthesis via the mevalonate pathway, consistent with reports of elevated cholesterol levels in the cerebrospinal fluid of ALS patients.

Even more compelling is the connection between OXCT1 and FRDA, an autosomal recessive neurodegenerative disorder caused by deficiency of the mitochondrial protein frataxin. Although the primary symptoms of FRDA include progressive ataxia, such as limb and gait ataxia and other neurological features like absent of reflex, loss of vibration and proprioception sensation [112] the onset of the disease often leads to a hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [113] or even diabetes and diabetic ketoacidosis [114]; [115]. According to Dong et al. [116] frataxin-deficient mice displayed decreased Oxct1 expression in multiple tissues, including cerebellum and skeletal muscle, elevated plasma ketone levels, and increased succinyl-CoA in vitro. Biochemical analyses confirmed a direct physical interaction between frataxin and OXCT1, with regulation mediated by frataxin-dependent suppression of ubiquitin–proteasome-driven OXCT1 degradation.

Collectively, these observations underscore the importance of OXCT1 in both central and peripheral nervous system homeostasis. Through its roles in neuronal energy metabolism, autophagy, oxidative stress defense, neurogenesis, and myelination, OXCT1 emerges as a critical regulator whose dysfunction may contribute to diverse neurological disorders. Further studies dissecting OXCT1-mediated pathways could provide valuable insights into novel therapeutic strategies targeting neurometabolic health.

Oncogene or tumor suppressor? The impact of OXCT1 on cancer progression

The overall role of ketone bodies metabolism and the impact of OXCT1 on cancer progression is worth exploring, mainly due to prospects of implementing ketogenic diet as an additional (adjuvant) therapy for many types of cancer [117]; [118]. Besides uncontrolled growth and proliferation, one of the defining hallmark of cancer is also reprogrammed cellular metabolism [119]; [120]. Most tumors favor aerobic glycolysis over oxidative phosphorylation, even in the presence of oxygen, which is known as the Warburg effect [121]. This metabolic phenomenon is to some extent connected to the main arguments supporting beneficial effects of ketogenic diet implementation in cancer treatment. Carbohydrate or calorie restriction provoked by a ketogenic diet regimen restricts glucose supply, thereby curbing cancer growth and progression [122]. Additionally, the utilization of ketone bodies for energy purposes requires oxidative phosphorylation. Since many cancer cells harbor impairments in their mitochondria, and cancerous tumors often develop hypoxic regions as a result of their rapid rate of proliferation, it is likely that this could render them unable to efficiently metabolize ketone bodies. Therefore, this metabolic weakness could potentially be turned into a therapeutic strategy.

Despite promising results from clinical trials [123], there is still controversy regarding the safety and efficacy of using a ketogenic diet to curb cancer. Some fundamental aspects such as the optimal duration of the ketosis state, patient condition and cancer type (stage, grade) or differential expression of key ketogenic/ketolytic enzymes within tumor, still need to be uncovered.

A growing body of evidence indicates that OXCT1 may act as an oncogene in numerous human cancers stemming from diverse tissues (Fig. 4). The results of our meta-analysis further support this notion (Fig. 5). Differential expression analyses reveal that OXCT1 levels are significantly altered in multiple tumor types compared with corresponding normal tissues (Fig. 5a). Importantly, the increase or decrease in the expression of this gene appears to be dependent on the type of cancer. Therefore, is OXCT1 an oncogene or a tumor suppressor? The answer to that question remains ambiguous and dependent on the tumor type. In the following sections, to discuss the matter in more detail, we summarize the available literature data with additional reference to our survival analyses for different types of cancer (Fig. 5).

Hepatocellular carcinoma

To date, multiple studies have documented the possible connection between either HMGCS2 or OXCT1 and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). It is likely that these genes may have antagonistic effects on HCC progression, where HMGCS2 overexpression restricts tumor growth, whereas overexpression of OXCT1 is considered to be one of the factors contributing to HCC development.

In the healthy liver, OXCT1 expression is normally suppressed (see Table 1), effectively excluding ketone body utilization as a major energy source in this tissue [20]. However, under nutrient-limiting conditions such as those commonly present in the tumor microenvironment, HCC cells may acquire the ability to consume ketone bodies as an adaptive survival strategy. Huang et al. [12] demonstrated that OXCT1 expression can be reinduced upon serum deprivation in several HCC cell lines (HepG2, Hep3B, PLC) through activation of the mTORC2–AKT–SP1 signaling axis. Notably, OXCT1 had previously been identified as a direct transcriptional target of SP1, due to the presence of GC-box sequences within its promoter region, which are potential binding sites for SP1 [13]. SP1 itself is a transcription factor for myriads of genes, and its overexpression contributes to the malignant phenotype of various cancers via upregulation of genes involved in proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [124]. Consistent with this, both shRNA-mediated SP1 knockdown and pharmacological inhibition using mithramycin A reduced OXCT1 expression in HepG2 cells, whereas SP1 overexpression augmented OXCT1 levels [12]. Clinically, high OXCT1 expression has been correlated with poor prognosis in HCC patients [12]; [9], supporting its potential as a prognostic biomarker.

Recent findings by Zhu et al. [125] combine the contribution of OXCT1 to HCC progression with its involvement in shaping the tumor microenvironment. OXCT1 deficiency in tumor-associated macrophages (TAM) suppresses HCC progression by reprogramming TAMs toward an anti-tumor phenotype via reducing CD8+ T-cell exhaustion and enhancing cytotoxicity. Since OXCT1 catalyzes the reaction of transferring the CoA from succinyl-CoA to acetoacetate and thus producing acetoacetyl-CoA (Fig. 1), elevated OXCT1 expression in TAMs induced the accumulation of succinate. Succinate, in turn, was linked to the transcriptional upregulation of Arg1, likely due to the increase in the H3K4me3 level in the Arg1 promoter. This finding aligns with prior evidence regarding the activity of Jumonji-family histone demethylases, which depends on α-ketoglutarate/succinate ratio [126].

Given this interplay, OXCT1 may participate not only in metabolic adaptation but also in epigenetic regulation. Two possible mechanisms can be envisioned. On the one hand OXCT1 may act directly as a succinyltransferase [9], modifying proteins such as metabolic enzymes or histones. Alternatively, by channeling succinyl-CoA into ketone metabolism, OXCT1 could indirectly restrict the intracellular succinyl-CoA pool, thereby reducing global lysine succinylation.

Lysine succinylation is increasingly recognized as an important post-translational modification (PTM) governing metabolism [127] and with alterations in cancer [128]. Elevated succinylation levels were reported in diverse cancers and frequently associated with poor prognosis [129]; [130]; [131]. Ma et al. [9] specifically reported that OXCT1 promotes HCC progression by succinylating LACTB at lysine 284, leading to its inactivation. While LACTB has been proposed as a tumor suppressor regulating mitochondrial lipid metabolism and potentially ferroptosis [132], the mechanisms of its regulation remain incompletely understood. However, contribution of OXCT1 to succinylation in HCC is controversial. Although Bai et al. [131] identified several succinylation-associated genes in HCC, the contribution of OXCT1 turned out to be not significant. Interestingly, links between lysine succinylation and tumor progression extend beyond HCC. Succinylation has been implicated in renal cancer [133]; [134] and prostate cancer [135]. However, the potential involvement of OXCT1 was not addressed in these three studies.

Prostate cancer

In prostate cancer, OXCT1 expression appears to correlate with disease progression. At the cellular level, OXCT1 mRNA is more abundant in LNCaP-SF cells (an androgen-independent subline resembling castration-resistant prostate cancer) than in the hormone-sensitive LNCaP parental line [136]. This pattern is reflected in clinical samples, where immunohistochemistry demonstrates higher OXCT1 expression in high-grade tumors compared to normal or low-grade tissues. Similarly, Labanca et al. [137] reported upregulation of ketolytic enzymes (ACAT1, BDH1, OXCT1) in castration-resistant tumors, suggesting that ketones may fuel tumor progression after androgen-deprivation therapy (ADT), which presents a critical clinical concern given that ADT-resistant prostate cancer remains incurable.

From a therapeutic perspective, OXCT1 inhibition is of particular interest. Interestingly, the OXCT1 inhibitor pimozide has been proven to be effective against prostate cancer in preclinical models [138]; [139], including LNcaP cells [140]. However, since pimozide had been identified as an OXCT1 inhibitor relatively recently, it remains unknown whether these effects are mediated specifically through OXCT1 inhibition, nevertheless studies focusing on that matter may reveal new therapeutic opportunities.

Colorectal cancer

OXCT1 may also hold promise as a biomarker and therapeutic target in colorectal cancer (CRC). Elevated OXCT1 mRNA expression has been detected in patient fecal samples, suggesting a potential utility as a non-invasive diagnostic marker for late stages of disease. At the cellular level, metastatic CRC lines (CC-M3) display ~ 2.8-fold higher OXCT1 expression compared to non-metastatic SW480 cells [141], possibly implicating OXCT1 in the metastatic process.

Moreover, colon cancers can be divided into ketolytic or glycolytic subtypes based on expression of OXCT1, ACAT1, GLUT1, and PFKFB3 [142]. For example, HT29 and SW620 cells represent the glycolytic subtype due to deficiencies in OXCT1 or BDH1, respectively, whereas SW480 cells could be defined as the hybrid subtype. This division not only highlights the heterogeneity of metabolic programming in CRC but also underscores the potential clinical importance of OXCT1 expression in predicting tumor behavior and therapy response.

Breast cancer

Enhanced ketolysis is also a probable cause of increased tumor growth and metastatic potential in breast cancer. In a murine xenograft model using MDA-MB-231 cells, overexpression of OXCT1/2 accelerated tumor progression and metastasis [143]. However, conflicting evidence complicates the interpretation. Enhanced ketolysis may promote tumor growth and metastatic potential in breast cancer. In a murine xenograft model using MDA-MB-231 cells, overexpression of OXCT1/2 accelerated tumor progression and metastasis [143]. Zhang et al. [144] reported downregulation of OXCT1 in breast cancer epithelial cells relative to normal breast tissue. Similarly, Maldonado et al. [145] observed no significant change in OXCT1, BDH1, or ACAT1 expression upon glucose deprivation or BHB treatment in MCF-7 and T47D cells. In contrast, Fulman-Levy et al. [146] documented upregulation of OXCT1 in MCF-7 cells following BHB treatment, which was accompanied by enhanced cell survival and proliferation. Given all the above, the involvement of OXCT1and overall ketone bodies metabolism in breast cancer progression requires further investigation.

Lung cancer

In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), OXCT1 appears to function as an oncogene. According to Lu et al. [147] OXCT1 activates NF-κB signaling pathway in H1299 cells, which consequently promotes tumor initiation and progression. Conversely, OXCT1 knockdown combined with BHB treatment induced expression of SREBP1 and its target TRIM21 (E3 ubiquitin ligase), presumably due to unspecified signaling mechanism mediated by BHB. As a result, TRIM21 promoted the ubiquitination and degradation of p65, thereby inhibiting the tumorigenesis of NSCLC.

OXCT1 and chemotherapy resistance

Recently, OXCT1 was also identified as a key gene in conferring the resistance to gemcitabine (GEM) in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) by activating NF-κB signaling pathway both in vivo and in vitro [148]. Nevertheless, there are also studies that successfully combined ketogenic diet with GEM therapy to restrain PDAC progression [149] and advocated for implementation of ketogenic diet as an adjuvant dietary treatment for pancreatic cancer due to lack of hepatotoxic effect after combining this diet with GEM [150].

In bladder cancer, OXCT1 knockout restored GEM sensitivity in the intrinsically resistant cell line UM-UC3 [151]. Similarly, OXCT1 may be involved in conferring the sensitivity to cisplatin in ovarian cancer. Yang et al. [152] reported that OXCT1 expression was suppressed in cisplatin-resistant cell lines due to hypermethylation of CpGs in its promoter region. Treatment with 5-aza-2’-deoxycytidine, a DNA methyltransferase inhibitor, reactivated OXCT1 expression in resistant lines but not in sensitive ones.

Although altered methylation patterns are thought to be one of the causes of cisplatin resistance due to possibility of silencing of genes required for cytotoxicity, at presence there is no direct evidence that OXCT1 could be one of those genes. However, silencing (via shRNA) of another key enzyme for ketone bodies metabolism- ACAT1 in ovarian cancer cells (OC-314, SKOV-3 and IGROV-1) renders them more sensitive to cisplatin treatment, via a mechanisms that remains still unknown [153].

Circ-OXCT1 and lncRNA OXCT1-AS1 involvement in cancer progression

OXCT1 can also contribute to cancer progression at the non-coding RNA level. OXCT1-related noncoding RNAs are expressed as both circRNA (circ-OXCT1) [154]; [155] and lncRNA (OXCT1-AS1) [156].

CircRNAs are highly stable RNA molecules with regulatory roles, often acting as miRNA sponges or modulators of gene expression [157]. Luo et al. [155] showed that circ-OXCT1 is upregulated in NSCLC tissues and cells (A549, H1975, H1650), where it promotes malignancy by interacting with miR-516b-5p and promoting SLC1A5 expression. Moreover, circ-OXCT1 silencing reduced PCNA expression and increased Bax and E-cadherin in NSCLC cells, suppressing proliferation and endothelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Conversely, Liu et al. [154] reported that circ-OXCT1 is downregulated in gastric cancer, where it inhibits EMT and metastasis via sponging miR-136 and restoring SMAD4 expression. Additionally, circ-OXCT1 was significantly downregulated within tissues samples obtained from patients with gastric cancer, which was related to poor overall survival rate and metastasis to the lymph nodes. These opposing findings suggest tissue-specific roles for circ-OXCT1 in tumor biology.

Similar to circRNAs, numerous LncRNAs, have been associated with either the cause or progression of cancers [158, 159]. Elevated OXCT1-AS1 expression has been documented in several types of cancer. In NSCLC, knockdown of OXCT1-AS1 significantly increased the expression of miR-195 (a tumor-suppressing miRNA) with subsequent reduction in expression of its target genes: CCND1 and CCNE1 [160]. A similar link between OXCT1-AS1 and miR-195 was also noted in a model of glioma [156]. OXCT1-AS1 by acting as a ceRNA (competing endogenous RNA) of miR-195 vicariously enhanced CDC25A expression and consequently promoted cancer progression, whereas knockdown of OXCT1-AS1 attenuated the severity of glioma. Moreover, lncRNA OXCT1-AS1 was reported to be significant in bladder cancer [161] and osteosarcoma [162] via interaction with either miR-455-5p or miR-886.

According to our analysis (Fig. 5) expression of OXCT1 is elevated within CHOL tissues (cholangiocarcinoma) in comparison to the normal controls (Fig. 5). In cholangiocarcinoma, several microRNAs, including miR-195 have been implicated in its progression [163]. While no direct link has been established between OXCT1-related RNAs (circ-OXCT1, OXCT1-AS1) and cholangiosarcoma, such interactions represent an intriguing direction for future studies (Fig. 4).

Summary and future perspectives

The molecular functions of OXCT1 extend far beyond its canonical role in ketone body catabolism, suggesting its involvement in diverse signaling pathways and in a broad spectrum of pathological conditions. Given the importance of ketone bodies in maintaining the energy homeostasis during nutrient deprivation, together with the popularity of ketogenic diets and exogenous ketone supplementation, further investigation into the regulation and function of key ketogenic and ketolytic enzymes represents an important and timely area of research. This review outlined the complex and dynamic landscape linking OXCT1 to multiple disease states including cardiovascular disorders and cancer. However several limitations should be acknowledged, particularly regarding the heterogeneity of the available data and the challenges associated with their interpretation. A considerable proportion of current evidence is based solely on bioinformatics analyses that require further experimental validation, such as the identification of OXCT1 as a prognostic marker in diabetic cardiomyopathy [59], or its proposed association with muscular atrophy [83].

Progress in the development of in vivo models with tissue-specific OXCT1silencing has been limited since the study by Cotter et al. [87]. Expanding such approaches to additional tissues remains an important objective. Notably, recent findings from heart-specific OXCT1 knockout models highlight the critical role of ketone body catabolism in cardiac function [57], supporting the need for analogous studies in other organs or specific cell lineages.

Emerging evidence linking OXCT1 to tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) [125] suggests a potential role in modulating the tumor microenvironment. This area warrants further investigation, as it may provide mechanistic insight into the context-dependent effects of OXCT1 observed across different tumor types.

Another promising direction involves the interplay between OXCT1 and epigenetic regulation, particularly protein post-translational modifications. Recent studies implicate OXCT1 in pathways associated with lysine succinylation, although this field remains in its early stages. In addition, lysine β-hydroxybutyrylation [80], a modification in which BHB serves as a substrate, represents a potential link between ketone metabolism and epigenetic control. Although there is currently no direct evidence that OXCT1 regulates this modification, this hypothesis warrants experimental evaluation. Given that OXCT1 catalyzes the conversion of AcAc to AcAc-CoA (Fig. 1), alterations in its activity may influence intracellular BHB availability and, consequently, the extent of lysine β-hydroxybutyrylation.
