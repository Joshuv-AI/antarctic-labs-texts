Forward-Time Equivalent of a “Retrocausal” Diffusion Hidden Variable Model for Quantum Mechanics
William S. DeWitt, Benjamin H. Feintzeig

Abstract
A recently proposed stochastic hidden variable model for quantum mechanics has been claimed to involve “retrocausality” due to the appearance of equations of motion with future-time boundary conditions. We formulate an equivalent system of forward-time equations of motion that gives rise to the same trajectories as solutions, but involves only initial-time boundary conditions. The forward-time dynamics involves a guidance term for the dynamical variables, determined by the phase-space distribution corresponding to a quantum wavefunction. We show, however, that this particular guidance term can be recovered as the mean-field limit of averaged pairwise interactions among an ensemble of finitely many particles.



I
Introduction

In a series of recent papers, Drummond and Reid
(
Drummond and Reid 2020
;
Drummond 2021
;
Drummond and Reid 2021
)
have proposed a new hidden variable model for quantum mechanics and argued that it helps solve interpretive and foundational problems associated with quantum measurement
(
Joseph et al. 2024
;
McGuigan et al. 2024
)
.
Drummond and Reid describe their model as including “retrocausal” effects because the defining stochastic differential equations (SDEs) involve a boundary condition at a future time, resulting in a reverse-time diffusion process.
In this letter, we show that “retrocausality” need not be understood as an inherent feature of the model. Using a classic result on time-reversed diffusion processes
(
Anderson 1982
)
(of recent acclaim for its key role in score-based generative modeling through SDEs
Song et al. 2020
) we formulate an equivalent forward-time stochastic process that gives rise to the same solutions as the model from Drummond and Reid.
Our dynamical model has some resemblance to older pilot wave hidden variable theories
(
Bohm 1952
;
Nelson 1966
)
, crucially adjusted to provide a stochastic dynamics on phase space.
Our stochastic formulation on phase space generalizes pilot wave theories on phase space
(
García de Polavieja 1996
)
and also admits an interpretation in terms of
propagation of chaos
in an interacting particle system
Chaintron and Diez 2022a
;
Chaintron and Diez 2022b
;
Sznitman 1991
that resembles many-interacting-worlds proposals
(
Hall et al. 2014
)
.

II
“Retrocausal” Diffusion Model

We summarize Drummond and Reid’s “retrocausal” stochastic hidden variable model through the concrete example of a single particle quantum system with state space consisting of square-integrable normalized wavefunctions in one dimension.
The model employs the
Husimi Q-function

Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}
, an integrable function on the two-dimensional phase space with canonical coordinates
(p,q)
.
The Husimi function
Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}
is defined for any wavefunction
\psi
by

Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}(p,q)=\frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}\left|\left\langle{\phi^{p,q}_{\hbar}}|\psi\right\rangle\right|^{2},

(1)

where

{\phi^{p,q}_{\hbar}}(x)=\frac{1}{(\pi\hbar)^{1/4}}e^{-\frac{ipq}{2\hbar}}e^{\frac{ipx}{\hbar}}e^{-\frac{(x-q)^{2}}{2\hbar}}

(2)

is a
coherent state
.
The Husimi function
Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}
is a probability density on the phase space corresponding with the Berezin quantization scheme.
Berezin quantization
(
Landsman 2017
)
associates a suitable phase space function
f
(e.g., smooth and compactly supported) with an operator or “symbol”
\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(f)
, determined by the prescription

\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}q}{2\pi\hbar}f(p,q)\left|{\phi^{p,q}_{\hbar}}\right\rangle\left\langle{\phi^{p,q}_{\hbar}}\right|,

(3)

where the integral is understood in the weak operator sense.
For each suitable phase space function
f
, the operator
\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(f)
is compact and satisfies

\left\langle\psi|\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(f)|\psi\right\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(p,q)\,Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}(p,q)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}q

(4)

for all wavefunctions
\psi
, thus recovering quantum mechanical expectation values for the operator
\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(f)
from the classical expectation value of
f
relative to the Husimi probability density.
Those unfamiliar with the Husimi function can compare its role as a phase space probability density with that of the perhaps more familiar Wigner function
(
Wigner 1932
;
Moyal 1949
)
, which is a phase space quasi-probability density associated with the Weyl “symbols” determined by the Weyl quantization scheme
(
Weyl 1950
;
Landsman 1998
)
.

Drummond and Reid’s model
(
Drummond 2021
;
Drummond and Reid 2020
)
is motivated by the observation that, for a class of Hamiltonians with potential up to fourth order in
x
, the Schrödinger equation
\psi(t)=e^{-iHt/\hbar}\psi_{0}
(for initial condition
\psi(0)=\psi_{0}
) gives rise to a generalized Fokker-Planck equation for the Husimi function (which here, and in what follows, is understood as a flow of phase-space densities due to the time dependence of
\psi
):

\partial_{t}Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}=-\partial_{p}(a_{1}Q_{\psi}^{\hbar})-\partial_{q}(a_{2}Q_{\psi}^{\hbar})\\
+\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{p}^{2}\left(b^{2}Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}\right)-\partial_{q}^{2}\left(b^{2}Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}\right)\right).

(5)

Here,
a_{1},a_{2}
are positive
drift coeffecients
, and
b
is a positive
diffusion coefficient
(but note the negative sign on the
\partial_{q}^{2}
term), all of which may vary as a function of the phase-space variables.
The Fokker-Planck type Eq. (
5
) resembles standard diffusion dynamics, but, whereas dissipative diffusion involves positive diffusivity, the time symmetry of the quantum dynamics in the Schrödinger equation force the use of some nonstandard negative entries to maintain traceless diffusivity.

Drummond and Reid’s model in principle allows for a traceless matrix of diffusion coefficients acting on second-order derivatives of phase-space variables, where the matrix may be diagonal in a coordinate system different from the standard one for
(p,q)
.
We restrict ourselves to the simpler form in Eq. (
5
) because this encompasses our examples and suffices to illustrate the main ideas.

Drummond and Reid suggest to interpret the Fokker-Planck type Eq. (
5
) as the bulk evolution of a probability density corresponding to some underlying microdynamics for the “hidden variables”
p
and
q
represented by an SDE.
They propose the coupled SDEs written in integral form as

\displaystyle p(t)

\displaystyle=p(t_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}a_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}b\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w_{1},

(6)

\displaystyle q(t)

\displaystyle=q(t_{f})-\int_{t}^{t_{f}}a_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}-\int_{t}^{t_{f}}b\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w_{2},

(7)

where
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are independent Gaussian white noise processes characterized by

\left\langle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w_{i}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w_{j}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\delta_{ij}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime}),\quad i,j=1,2

(8)

The equations are, in general, coupled through the functional form of the drift terms, since
a_{1}
may depend on
q
and
a_{2}
may depend on
p
.
The Langevin-type stochastic Eqs. (
6
)-(
7
) involve boundary conditions consisting of both an
initial
time
t_{0}
and a
future
time
t_{f}
, thus leading Drummond and Reid to interpret them as representing a “retrocausal” model of quantum theory.
According to Drummond and Reid, these SDEs recover the bulk evolution of the distribution
Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}
in Eq. (
5
), thus yielding the standard quantum mechanical predictions.

III
Equivalent Forward-Time Process

We motivate our alternative approach by noticing that the “retrocausal” aspects of Drummond and Reid’s model do not appear at all for certain systems.
Well-known results concerning Berezin quantization
(e.g.,
Landsman 1998
, Cor. II.2.4.5)
imply immediately that for a class of Hamiltonians up to quadratic in the phase space variables
(p,q)
, the Fokker-Planck equation can be recovered from a completely deterministic microdynamics.

For example, for a free particle Hamiltonian

H=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\partial_{x}^{2},

(9)

one finds the Fokker-Planck equation for
Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}
in the form

\partial_{t}Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}=-\frac{p}{m}\partial_{q}Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}.

(10)

This can be recovered from the microdynamics

\displaystyle p(t)

\displaystyle=p(t_{0}),

(11)

\displaystyle q(t)

\displaystyle=q(t_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{p(t^{\prime})}{m}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}

\displaystyle=q(t_{0})+\frac{p(t_{0})}{m}(t-t_{0})

(12)

which does not require a future boundary condition.
This example raises the question: is the use of a future boundary condition
necessary
to reproduce a Fokker-Planck equation for
Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}
as in Drummond and Reid’s model?

While Drummond and Reid analyze only one possible equation for the microdynamics underlying the bulk Fokker-Planck type evolution of the probability density in Eq. (
5
), generally such microdynamics may be non-unique.
In this case, we will now define an alternative microdynamics reproducing the same bulk evolution.
Whereas Drummond and Reid’s microdynamics involves a reverse-time diffusion process for one of the dynamical variables, our proposed equivalent microdynamics involves only forward-time evolution equations.
To proceed, we invoke a classic result concerning reverse-time diffusion dynamics from Anderson
(
Anderson 1982
)
, which states that every suitable diffusion process has an equivalent time-reversed process giving rise to a one-to-one correspondence between forward-time and backward-time trajectories.
By a “suitable” diffusion process, we mean one whose Fokker-Planck equation has a well-defined initial value problem giving rise to a unique smooth evolution of the probability density, as well as a unique smooth joint evolution with the modified noise term below.

In our case, we carry over Drummond and Reid’s microdynamics in Eq. (
6
) for the evolution of
p
, but we reverse the time in Eq. (
7
) for the evolution of
q
to obtain the forward-time SDE in integral form:

q(t)=q(t_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\overline{a}_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}b\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overline{w}_{2}.

(13)

In this equation, we employ the new drift term

\overline{a}_{2}(p,q)=-a_{2}(p,q)+\frac{\partial_{q}\left(b^{2}(p,q)\ \mu_{t}(q)\right)}{\mu_{t}(q)},

(14)

where

\mu_{t}(q)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}(p,q)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p

(15)

is the marginal probability density over the variable
q
at time
t
.
The new diffusion term involves the modified Gaussian white noise process with increments that relate to those of the forward process according to

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overline{w}_{2}(t)=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w_{2}(t)+\frac{\partial_{q}\left(b(p,q)\ \mu_{t}(q)\right)}{\mu_{t}(q)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

(16)

which likewise is characterized by

\left\langle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overline{w}_{2}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overline{w}_{2}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

(17)

Crucially, the forward-time microdynamics defined in Eq. (
13
) is a forward-time Itô stochastic process as long as (i)
\mu_{t}(q)
satisfies suitable regularity or smoothness assumptions required for the existence and uniqueness of a smooth solution to the Fokker-Planck Eq. (
5
), and (ii) there is a unique smooth solution to the Kolmogorov equation corresponding to the evolution of the joint probability density associated with
q(t)
and
\overline{w}_{2}(t)
.

Notice that the new drift term in Eq. (
14
) couples to the marginal probability distribution in a similar form to pilot wave guidance equations.
For instance, the de Broglie-Bohm pilot wave theory
(
Bohm 1952
;
Dürr et al. 1992
)
contains a velocity term of the form
\Im(\partial_{x}\psi/\psi)
for a pilot wavefunction
\psi(x)
, and likewise Nelson’s stochastic mechanics
(
Nelson 1966
)
contains a velocity term of the form
\partial_{x}\rho/\rho
for a probability density
\rho(x)=\left|\psi(x)\right|^{2}
.
Thus, Eq. (
13
) can be understood as a stochastic guidance equation for a particle undergoing diffusion guided by the Husimi function.
The familiar form of these new drift terms in pilot wave theories can now be understood as arising from the general considerations regarding the generation of time-symmetric differential equations using the procedure from Anderson
(
Anderson 1982
)
for defining time-reversed processes.

IV
Mean-Field Interpretation

One potential virtue of the forward-time stochastic guidance diffusion model in Eq. (
13
)-(
14
) is that it is of McKean-Vlasov type
(
McKean 1966
;
Chaintron and Diez 2022a
)
, with coefficients that depend on the distribution of the solution of the Fokker-Planck equation.
Hence, the model can be interpreted as the mean-field limit of an ensemble of
N
particles
\left(q^{N}_{i}(t)\right)_{i=1}^{N}
interacting weakly via their empirical measure

\mu^{N}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{q^{N}_{i}(t)},

(18)

and driven by independent noise processes
\left(\overline{w}^{N}_{2,i}(t)\right)_{i=1}^{N}
of intensity
b
characterized by

\left\langle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\overline{w}^{N}_{2,i}}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\overline{w}^{N}_{2,j}}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\delta_{ij}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime}),\quad i,j=1,\dots,N.

(19)

Since the positions
q^{N}_{i}(t)
are random variables sampled from a distribution satisfying an SDE, the empirical measure
\mu^{N}_{t}
is a
measure-valued
stochastic process.

We specify the so-called
N
-system

\left(q^{N}_{i}(t)\right)_{i=1}^{N}
to satisfy
N
coupled SDEs

q_{i}^{N}(t)=q_{i}^{N}(t_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\overline{a}^{N}_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}b\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\overline{w}^{N}_{2,i}}.

(20)

The N-system SDEs are coupled via the appearance of the empirical measure in their drift term

\bar{a}^{N}_{2}(p,q)=-a_{2}(p,q)+\frac{\partial_{q}\left(b^{2}(p,q)\,\tilde{\mu}_{t}^{N}(q)\right)}{\tilde{\mu}_{t}^{N}(q)},

(21)

with
mollified
(kernel smoothed) empirical measure

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}_{t}^{N}(x)=\left(\mu_{t}^{N}\ast K^{N}\right)(x)=\int K^{N}(x-y)\mu_{t}^{N}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.

(22)

Here,
\ast
denotes convolution and we choose
K^{N}
as a positive symmetric
mollifier
(smoothing kernel) with bandwidth
\frac{1}{N}
. As a concrete example,
K^{N}(\cdot)
could be a Gaussian with variance
\frac{1}{N^{2}}
and mean
0
.

If the initial states
\left(q^{N}_{i}(0)\right)_{i=1}^{N}
are sampled from an iid (or more generally, exchangeable) distribution, then it follows from standard results on propagation of chaos for McKean-Vlasov systems that, in the limit
N\to\infty
(so long as relevant derivatives are bounded), we have
\mu^{N}_{t}\to\mu_{t}
in the sense of convergence in the Wasserstein metric
(
Chaintron and Diez 2022b
, Thm. 3.1)
, which also implies weak* convergence, or convergence in distribution.
Further, a defining feature of the mollifier is that
K^{N}\to\delta
(weak* convergence as tempered distributions), so we also have
\tilde{\mu}_{t}^{N}\to\mu_{t}
.
Thus we have
\bar{a}^{N}_{2}\to\bar{a}_{2}
(convergence in distribution), matching the guidance term in Eq. (
14
).

In this mean-field limit, the stochastically evolving empirical measure
\mu^{N}_{t}
converges to the
deterministically
evolving measure
\mu_{t}
, whose flow is determined by the Fokker-Planck Eq. (
5
).
Furthermore, the
N
-system
decouples
, so that each particle in the ensemble can be treated as evolving independently only taking into account interactions with the background field
\mu_{t}
.
These features characterize
propagation of chaos
(See
(
Chaintron and Diez 2022a
;
Chaintron and Diez 2022b
;
Sznitman 1991
)
for comprehensive reviews of the theory and many applications of this vast topic).
The picture provided by our mean-field interpretation also bears an interesting resemblance to the proposal of
Hall et al. 2014
, which aims to recover quantum dynamics from the interaction of many classical worlds.

V
Example: Amplifier Dynamics

We now illustrate the preceding comparison between reverse-time and forward-time diffusion hidden variable models in a concrete example.
Drummond and Reid
(
Drummond and Reid 2020
)
consider the “amplifier” dynamics as a toy model of measurement given by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H=-\frac{i\hbar^{2}}{2}\left(x\partial_{x}+\frac{1}{2}I\right),

(23)

which they argue leads to the Fokker-Planck type equation for the Husimi function:

\partial_{t}Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}=p\partial_{p}Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}-q\partial_{q}Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}+\partial_{p}^{2}Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}-\partial_{q}^{2}Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}.

(24)

In other words, the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
23
) gives rise to drift coefficients
a_{1}=-p
,
a_{2}=q
and constant diffusion coefficient
b=1
.
The “retrocausal” diffusion microdynamics in Drummond and Reid’s model is

\displaystyle p(t)

\displaystyle=p(t_{0})-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}p(t^{\prime})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}+\sqrt{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w_{1}

(25)

\displaystyle q(t)

\displaystyle=q(t_{f})-\int_{t}^{t_{f}}q(t^{\prime})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}+\sqrt{2}\int_{t}^{t_{f}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w_{2}.

(26)

As in the general case, one of the microdynamical equations describes evolution determined by a future boundary condition.

The alternative forward-time stochastic guidance model proposed in this letter keeps Eq. (
25
), but reverses the time in Eq. (
26
) to obtain

q(t)=q(t_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\left(-q(t^{\prime})+2\frac{\partial_{q}\mu_{t^{\prime}}(q(t^{\prime}))}{\mu_{t^{\prime}}(q(t^{\prime}))}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}+\sqrt{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overline{w}_{2}.

(27)

These dynamics now involve a drift provided by a guidance term.

Next, we will show that the forward-time guidance dynamics in Eq. (
27
) arises as the mean-field limit of a weakly interacting particle system.
The Fokker-Planck Eq. (
24
) has a solution of the form

\displaystyle Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}(p,q)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(q-q_{0}e^{t}\right)^{2}+\left(p-p_{0}e^{-t}\right)^{2}\right)\right)

(28)

for some initial value
(p_{0},q_{0})
.
Eq. (
28
) describes a product of Gaussians with fixed variance and independently evolving means, each mean obeying classical dynamics.
In this case, we have the marginal distribution

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(q)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(q-q_{0}e^{t}\right)^{2}\right),

(29)

which gives rise to the guidance term

\displaystyle 2\frac{\partial_{q}\,\mu_{t}\left(q\right)}{\mu_{t}(q)}=2\,\partial_{q}\log\mu_{t}(q)=-2\left(q-q_{0}e^{t}\right).

(30)

We may identify the guidance term as arising from the pairwise harmonic potential

\displaystyle V(q,x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(q-x\right)^{2},

(31)

where we identify
\log\mu_{t}
with the expectation value of the potential relative to the distribution
\mu_{t}
:

\displaystyle\langle V(q,\cdot)\rangle_{\mu_{t}}

\displaystyle=\int V(q,x)d\mu_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(q-\langle q\rangle_{\mu_{t}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\log\mu_{t}(q).

(32)

The form of the harmonic potential makes apparent that the guidance term generally gives rise to a force on the particle toward the mean of the distribution, counteracting the diffusive behavior.

In the mean-field interpretation, we can also understand the restorative force as arising in the
N\to\infty
limit from a pairwise potential for the
N
-system.
To see this, define the mollified pairwise interaction potential

\tilde{V}^{N}(q,x)=\left(V(q,\cdot)\ast K^{N}\right)(x)=\int V(q,y)K^{N}(x-y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.

(33)

Then, since for each fixed
q
and
y
,
V(q,x)K(x-y)
vanishes fast enough as
x\to\infty
, one has

\displaystyle\int V(q,x)\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int\int V(q,x)K^{N}(x-y)\mu_{t}^{N}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int\tilde{V}^{N}(q,y)\mu_{t}^{N}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y,

(34)

which implies

\langle V(q,\cdot)\rangle_{\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}}=\langle\tilde{V}^{N}(q,\cdot)\rangle_{\mu^{N}_{t}}.

(35)

It follows that
\tilde{V}^{N}\to V
, and hence

\displaystyle|\log\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}-\tilde{V}^{N}|\to 0,

(36)

with convergence in distribution as
N\to\infty
.
Thus, the guidance term for the focal particle in Eq. (
21
) is approximated for large
N
by the force from an average energy of pairwise interactions with all particles of the
N
-system. Thus, one can interpret the restorative mean-reverting force in the bulk harmonic potential as arising from attractive pairwise interactions among particles in the mean-field limit of the
N
-system.

Note that the mean-reverting form of the guidance term can recover a mean-field interpretation for the dynamics of a wide range of Hamiltonians. The guidance term will arise from a pairwise harmonic potential whenever the Fokker-Planck equation for
Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}
admits a solution that is a Gaussian of fixed variance, but whose mean evolves according to a classical dynamics—for example, Gaussian solutions of
Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}
to the free particle Fokker-Planck Eq. (
10
). We conjecture this holds more generally for Hamiltonians up to quadratic in the position and momentum operators whose Fokker-Planck equation contains no coupling between
p
and
q
in the drift terms.

VI
Conclusion

We have shown that the “retrocausal” hidden variable model of Drummond and Reid
(
Drummond 2021
;
Drummond and Reid 2020
;
Drummond and Reid 2021
)
is equivalent to a set of forward-time SDEs.
In the forward-time formulation, results from Anderson
(
Anderson 1982
)
allow one to recover the reverse-time diffusion by adding an extra force, expressed as a guidance term determined by a distribution that solves the Fokker-Planck equation.
We showed that the guidance term can be understood as a restorative force
toward the mean
of the distribution.
The qualitative picture is that while the reverse-time diffusion model involves dissipation away from the mean, the forward-time equivalent compensates with precisely the force required to move the particle back along the same path.
We showed that this qualitative picture is born out exactly in the mean field limit, so that one can think of the particle as interacting with an ensemble via a restorative coupling to the (smoothed) empirical measure. In this sense, the phase-space pilot wave of our model loses it’s mystery; it can be understood as only a convenient summary arising in the
N\to\infty
limit of averaging over pairwise interactions of particles in an ensemble.
Thus, in expressing stochastic hidden variable models underlying a Fokker-Planck equation for
Q_{\psi}^{\hbar}
, we find a general trade-off between “retrocausality” (captured by reverse-time equations) and a mean-field interaction picture.
