Performance of 3D-printed concrete columns with GFRP rebars under axial compression
Authors: Rhys Bates, Farhad Aslani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract3D concrete printing (3DCP) offers transformative potential in the construction industry by enabling rapid, cost-effective, and sustainable fabrication, yet its application to structural components is limited by concrete’s brittleness and challenges in reinforcement integration. This study investigates the axial compression performance of 3D-printed concrete columns reinforced with glass fibre reinforced polymer (GFRP) bars. A hybrid construction approach was employed, in which 3D-printed concrete formed a permanent outer shell, while GFRP reinforcement cages were post-installed prior to casting the concrete core. Four columns, including conventionally cast counterparts of identical dimensions, were tested to assess load–displacement response, strain distribution, and failure mechanisms. The 3D-printed columns exhibited approximately 50% of the peak load of the mould-cast columns but demonstrated progressive, ductile failure, in contrast to the brittle failure observed in conventional specimens. These results suggest that 3D-printed columns could provide advantages in energy absorption, controlled failure, and modular construction, particularly where ductility is critical. The study further identifies interlayer bonding and reinforcement integration as key factors requiring optimization to enable reliable structural applications of 3DCP in the future.Keywords: 3D concrete printing, Glass fibre reinforced polymer rebars, Columns, Axial compression

Introduction

Recent advancements in 3D concrete printing (3DCP) have introduced transformative potential within the construction industry. As an additive manufacturing technique, 3DCP offers numerous benefits including customization, reduced material waste, shorter construction times, and increased automation. These features contribute to enhanced sustainability, safety, and design flexibility1–6. While current applications of 3DCP are largely architectural, structural uses remain limited, primarily due to the challenges of reinforcing printed concrete, which is inherently brittle and possesses low tensile strength7.

In conventional reinforced concrete construction, steel reinforcement is critical to ensuring ductility and crack resistance. However, integrating reinforcement into 3D-printed components remains a major obstacle. Furthermore, traditional construction methods rely heavily on temporary formwork, which poses safety risks. It is estimated that up to 60% of construction failures are related to formwork collapse, inadequate shoring, or insufficient lateral bracing. Additionally, formwork contributes significantly to construction costs, often comprising 35% to 60% of total concrete work expenses8,9.

This research addresses two key challenges in structural 3DCP: the integration of reinforcement and the elimination of temporary formwork10,11. One promising approach involves printing a hollow concrete shell that functions as permanent formwork. Reinforcement cages can be installed after printing, followed by conventional concrete casting to form the structural core. This hybrid method provides a feasible solution to reinforcement placement while eliminating the need for traditional formwork23-26.

Among alternative reinforcements, glass fibre reinforced polymer (GFRP) rebars offer compelling advantages over steel, including corrosion resistance, a high strength-to-weight ratio, and compatibility with concrete in terms of thermal expansion12,13. However, GFRP also has limitations, including low ductility, poor performance under compression, and unidirectional mechanical properties. These factors have historically led to conservative design approaches. For example, ACI 440 recommends neglecting GFRP’s contribution under compression15.

Experimental investigations on GFRP-reinforced concrete (GFRP-RC) columns have shown mixed results17-19. Some studies, including that by Abed et al.16, report that while GFRP-RC columns demonstrate lower ultimate capacities than steel-reinforced counterparts, their failure modes, strain responses, and displacement profiles are comparable. However, concerns persist regarding ductility, axial stability, and buckling resistance, particularly in slender columns20–22. These findings underscore the need for further study and refinement of design standards to accommodate the unique mechanical behaviour of GFRP.

To date, only a limited number of studies have explored the structural potential of 3DCP columns reinforced with GFRP. Notably, Zhu et al.27 demonstrated the feasibility of using 3D-printed permanent formwork in combination with steel reinforcement, reporting improved performance over conventionally cast columns. However, the influence of concrete mix design and confinement effects made direct performance attribution difficult.

In the context of 3DCP, rheological properties of fresh concrete such as yield stress, viscosity, and thixotropy are critical to successful printing. These properties determine the workability (flowability and extrudability) and buildability of the mix6,28. Achieving an optimal balance between these characteristics is essential for print stability and structural performance. Mix design parameters such as the water-to-binder (W/B) ratio, the use of chemical admixtures (e.g., superplasticizers and viscosity-modifying agents), and the choice of binder materials (e.g., GGBFS, silica fume) significantly affect both fresh and hardened properties28–36.

This study builds on previous work by investigating 3D-printed permanent formwork for square concrete columns reinforced with GFRP rebars. Unlike earlier studies, it maintains consistent concrete mix compositions and geometries across both 3D-printed and traditionally cast specimens to enable a controlled comparison.

Research significance

This research evaluates the structural performance of 3D-printed concrete columns reinforced with GFRP rebars under axial compression, and directly compares them with equivalent traditionally cast GFRP-reinforced concrete (GFRP-RC) columns. The study examines ultimate load capacity, ductility, and failure mechanisms, while referencing current design provisions such as AS 360037 and ACI 440.1R-1513.

The novelty of this work lies in the experimental integration of GFRP reinforcement within 3D-printed concrete using a hybrid permanent formwork approach, providing insights into interlayer bonding, progressive failure, and structural ductility, aspects that have not been systematically investigated in prior studies. By addressing practical challenges associated with reinforcement placement and printed formwork, this research expands the potential structural applications of 3D concrete printing and lays the foundation for future innovations in reinforced additive manufacturing.

Three cube specimens and three prism specimens were tested at each of the 7-day and 28-day curing intervals to determine mechanical performance.

Compression tests were conducted on the cube specimens using a U-Test Compression Testing Machine, operated at a controlled loading rate of 1.25 kN/s. The compressive strength of the concrete was determined from the average 28-day test results, calculated by dividing the maximum applied load by the cross-sectional area of the specimen, in accordance with AS 1012.943. The compressive stress and strain were derived from the recorded load and displacement data, with the compressive strength defined as the maximum observed stress. Testing was terminated once the applied load dropped below 40% of the peak value. Flexural strength tests were also performed on the prism specimens using standard procedures. While flexural strength is not a primary factor influencing the compressive performance of concrete columns, the results offer supplementary insight into the flexural behaviour of the mix, providing a useful performance baseline.

Column preparation

The printed columns were fabricated using the HC1008 Desktop Concrete 3D Printer at the University of Western Australia (UWA). The printer, shown in Fig. 4a, comprises a print bed, user interface, and an extrusion system capable of movement along all three axes. The extrusion system features a manually fed hopper with an interchangeable nozzle, through which a rotating screw-type extruder delivers the concrete. A 10 mm diameter nozzle was selected to match the mix composition and to minimise the total volume of material extruded.

a 3D Concrete printer38 and b Print trial.

Prior to printing, the columns were modelled using computer-aided design (CAD) software to generate a print code. This code, transferred via USB and read by the printer’s user interface, includes information such as column geometry, printing speed, extrusion speed, and the designated starting position, critical due to the raised, removable print bed visible in Fig. 4b. Printing speed was controlled by adjusting the gantry system’s movement, while extrusion speed was regulated through the rotational speed of the screw extruder. The layer-by-layer extrusion process is depicted in Fig. 4b.

Using a 10 mm diameter nozzle, each layer was printed with a double-wall configuration to enhance structural stability during the printing process. This resulted in an average wall thickness of approximately 40 mm, attributed to the lateral spreading behaviour of the fresh concrete. The initial design specified a wall thickness of 20 mm and an external width of 200 mm; however, these dimensions were adjusted to improve print stability and accommodate the available print code.

The flow rate of the extrusion system was sensitive to the rate at which concrete was manually fed into the hopper. Efforts were made to maintain a consistent feed rate, though minor inconsistencies in the printed layers were observed, particularly in the early prints, as illustrated in Fig. 4b. Owing to the height limitations of the 3D printer, columns were fabricated in 260 mm tall hollow segments, as shown in Fig. 5. The final printed sections exhibited an average external width of 250 mm and an internal width of 170 mm. While this segmentation allowed fabrication within the printer’s capacity, it also introduced cold joints and potential printing-discontinuity effects.

Printed sections.

Each column included a printed base section, matching the height of the hollow segments but with the bottom two layers (20 mm) fully closed to encapsulate the base of the column. A total of ten column segments were printed, comprising two base sections, three segments for the short-columns, and seven for the long-columns. Following printing, all sections were cured for 28 days. During this curing period, reinforcement cages were assembled for all four columns, as shown in Fig. 2.

After curing, the printed segments were stacked over the reinforcement cages and joined using grout prepared from the same concrete mix. As mechanical vibration of the poured concrete was not feasible, the superplasticiser (SP) dosage was increased from 4 to 7–8 mL per unit to enhance flowability without significantly altering the hardened properties of the mix. The poured concrete was manually agitated using a concrete scraper to release trapped air and ensure uniform distribution. A pan mixer was employed to accommodate the larger volumes of concrete required for casting. The assembly process and the fully constructed columns are illustrated in Fig. 6. Once assembled, the columns were cured for an additional 28 days prior to testing.

Column heights were initially chosen to facilitate testing of both short and slender columns, as defined by the slenderness ratios specified in AS 360037. However, due to factors such as printing height limitations, extrusion spreading, and an initial misinterpretation of the bracing conditions of the testing apparatus, all four columns ultimately fell below the slenderness threshold for braced columns.

Testing of columns

After curing for 28 days, the columns were transported to the UWA Materials Testing Laboratory (MTL) via forklift and positioned in the testing apparatus using an overhead crane integrated into the laboratory roof. Testing was conducted using an Instron UT-05–3000, an Instron Magnum High Force Fatigue Testing Machine with a maximum load capacity of 3000 kN44. Each column was subjected to static axial compression until failure, applying a controlled displacement rate of 0.5 mm/min. The top platen displaced downward at this rate and was constrained to axial movement only, providing pinned boundary conditions at both ends of the column.

Lateral displacements at the column mid-height were monitored using string potentiometers attached on two orthogonal sides, capturing displacements along the local x- and y-axes. The applied load was recorded by a load cell integrated within the top platen, which also measured the total axial displacement. Photographs of two test setups are presented in Fig. 9a, b, with string potentiometer attachments clearly visible in Fig. 9b. Testing was automatically terminated once the load dropped below 20% of the peak value.

Theoretical capacity

The theoretical capacities of the columns were determined based on the 28-day compressive strength results, following the guidelines set out in AS 360037 and ACI 440.1R-1513. According to ACI 440.1R-15, the compressive contribution of any fibre-reinforced polymer (FRP) reinforcement is neglected in design calculations; therefore, the compressive strength is derived solely from the concrete properties.

Section 1.1.2 of AS 3600 specifies that the standard is applicable to members with concrete compressive strengths between 20 and 120 MPa and reinforcement classified as ductility class N or E. To estimate the theoretical capacity of a conventional column in accordance with AS 3600, an equivalent column model is adopted wherein the GFRP rebars are replaced with grade D500N steel of the same bar diameter. This reinforcement corresponds to deformed steel bars with ductility class “N” and a characteristic yield strength of 500 MPa.

The cross-sectional strength of the column can be represented using an interaction diagram. The squash load, which corresponds to the ultimate axial compressive capacity without bending, is calculated assuming a rectangular concrete stress block and uniform compressive strength. This squash load represents the theoretical capacity of the column cross-section. Additionally, the buckling load is determined based on the balanced failure condition. The theoretical axial capacity of the equivalent steel-reinforced column is then taken as the lesser of the squash load and the buckling load.

The parameters used in the relevant equations1,2 were calculated according to the formulae provided in AS 360037. Detailed explanations of the column capacity calculations, including definitions of parameters and their computational methods, are available in this standard.

Results and discussion

Section “Results and discussion” presents the results of the experimental investigation into the structural performance of 3D-printed concrete columns reinforced with GFRP rebars under pure axial compression. The primary objective of this study is to evaluate whether columns produced using 3D concrete printing (3DCP) technology with printed permanent formwork can achieve sufficient load-bearing capacity for structural applications. The experimental program compares 3D-printed columns of two different lengths with equivalent GFRP-reinforced columns fabricated through conventional mould-casting methods using temporary formwork, with a focus on analysing the structural responses of each column type.

Section “Compressive strength results” first establishes the mechanical properties of the concrete mix, which serve as the basis for estimating the theoretical capacity of both the column cross-section and equivalent steel-reinforced columns in Section “Flexural strength results”. These theoretical predictions are then compared with the experimentally measured compression capacities of the columns in Section “Theoretical capacity results”.

Compressive strength results

The complete results from the cube compression tests conducted using the U-Test machine are summarised in Table 3 and failure modes of the tested cubes have been presented in Fig. 10. In this dataset, the reported peak displacement and peak strain refer to the values corresponding to the peak load, rather than the absolute maximum values recorded. At 7 days, the mean peak load was 121.1 kN, corresponding to an average compressive strength of 48.43 MPa. Unexpectedly, the 28-day specimens exhibited a slightly lower mean peak load of 120.6 kN, with a corresponding compressive strength of 48.24 MPa. This atypical decrease suggests significant variability, which must be attributed to experimental or specimen-related inconsistencies rather than true material behaviour. Possible contributing factors include the 3D printing procedure, batch-to-batch differences in mix proportions, the presence of entrapped air voids, inconsistent vibration during casting, and variations in curing conditions.

The variability in compressive strength is reflected in the relatively high standard deviations observed in both datasets: 11.1 MPa at 7 days and 11.4 MPa at 28 days. These variations are most likely due to imperfections in specimen preparation and testing, with the 3D printing procedure playing a dominant role. Factors such as surface defects, voids, inconsistent consolidation, or misalignment during loading also contributed. In particular, entrapped air bubbles were visible on several tested specimens, indicating inadequate vibration and consolidation.

Within the 28-day group, specimens 1 and 3 yielded similar results, whereas specimen 2 exhibited a significant deviation, indicating it may be an outlier. Similarly, specimen 3 in the 7-day group displayed an unusually low peak stress and atypical stress–strain behaviour. This specimen had been oriented differently in the testing machine, loaded perpendicular to the casting direction, which may have introduced a plane of weakness at the interlayer interface. This highlights the critical role of the 3D printing process, especially orientation and interlayer bonding, in determining the strength of 3D-printed mixes.

Due to the load-controlled nature of the test, the U-Test machine maintained a constant loading rate of 1.25 kN/s until the load dropped below 40% of the peak. In the case of specimen 3 (7-day test), this resulted in an anomalous negative strain reading post-failure, likely due to an overcorrection by the machine. The mean peak displacements were 0.580 mm (1.16% strain) at 7 days and 0.916 mm (1.83% strain) at 28 days, representing a 58% increase in strain capacity. While this may suggest enhanced ductility with curing time, the high variability limits confidence in this conclusion. The corresponding standard deviations were 0.12% (7 days) and 0.733% (28 days), with the coefficient of variation increasing from 10 to 40%, respectively.

Confidence intervals were calculated for the 28-day compressive strength using a t-distribution with two degrees of freedom. The 90% confidence interval ranged from 29.08 to 67.39 MPa, and the 95% confidence interval ranged from 20.01 to 76.46 MPa. These wide intervals underscore the uncertainty inherent in the dataset and highlight the difficulty in establishing a reliable characteristic strength for this 3D-printed mix.

The compressive strength results were unexpected, as concrete typically gains strength with age. For normal-strength concrete, the 28-day to 7-day strength ratio generally falls between 1.3 and 1.7. Based on the 7-day strength results, a typical 28-day strength would be approximately 63 MPa (assuming a 1.3 ratio), which contrasts markedly with the measured 48.24 MPa. One possible explanation is that the mix exhibited high early strength development, resulting in a lower-than-expected strength gain between 7 and 28 days.

Such early strength development can result from the use of chemical admixtures, low water-to-binder (W/B) ratios, and controlled curing conditions. The inclusion of superplasticiser (SP), viscosity-modifying admixture (VMA), and silica fume in the mix may have enhanced particle packing, stability, and early-age hydration. As noted by Aïtcin in High-Performance Concrete36, a W/B ratio of 0.30–0.35, combined with curing at ambient temperatures around 20°C, can produce high early strength. These factors may have contributed to the limited strength increase observed in this study. However, they do not fully explain the slight decrease in compressive strength at 28 days, which is more reasonably attributed to variability in the 3D printing procedure, specimen preparation, curing, and testing procedures rather than a material property.

Overall, the results highlight the need for improved quality control in 3D printing applications, particularly with regard to the printing procedure, mix consistency, curing uniformity, casting procedures, and interlayer bonding, to ensure reliable mechanical performance.

While this provides a preliminary measure of material strength, it is acknowledged that the reliability of this estimate is limited by the variability observed in the test data. To improve the accuracy of strength characterisation in future research, it is recommended that additional specimens be tested. Theoretical capacity results

The theoretical axial load capacities of the concrete columns were evaluated using the provisions of AS 360038 and ACI 440.1R-1514. In accordance with ACI guidelines, the compressive strength contribution of GFRP reinforcement was neglected. To provide a comparative baseline, an equivalent column with conventional steel reinforcement was also analysed.

Slenderness considerations

Slenderness ratios were calculated. The resulting ratios were 10.7 and 24 for the short (800 mm) and long (1800 mm) columns, respectively. As both values are below the slenderness threshold of 25 for braced columns, the columns are classified as non-slender. However, buckling calculations were still performed for the long-column due to its proximity to the slenderness limit.

Cross-sectional capacity

Using the 28-day compressive strength of the concrete mix (48.24 MPa), the axial compressive capacity of the column sections was calculated. For the GFRP-reinforced columns, only the contribution of the concrete was considered, resulting in an ultimate capacity of approximately 2563 kN. For the equivalent steel-reinforced sections, an ultimate axial capacity of 2789 kN was obtained.

Buckling load

Although the columns were not technically slender, the buckling load was calculated to evaluate potential instability effects, particularly for the long-column. The analysis incorporated a moment–axial interaction approach based on AS 3600 design principles. For the short-column, the calculated buckling load significantly exceeded the squash load, confirming axial compression as the governing limit state. In contrast, the buckling load for the long-column was lower than its squash load, and thus controls its theoretical capacity.

A summary of the theoretical load capacities is provided in Table 5. All values are unfactored ultimate loads. For the long-column with steel reinforcement, buckling was the governing failure mode, while for the short-column, the squash load was critical.

Compression test results of mould-cast columns

Compression tests were conducted on mould-cast columns using crane and forklift assistance for placement. String potentiometers were fastened to the centre of two adjacent faces using superglue. For long-columns, a crane was additionally employed as a safety measure to prevent ejection from the testing apparatus under high loads (Figs. 11, 12).

Mould-cast short-column a moment of failure and b after failure.

Mould-cast long-column a moment of failure and b after failure.

During the initial loading phase, the cast columns exhibited no visible deformation. Vertical cracking initiated at loads of approximately 600–700 kN and was accompanied by audible popping sounds at around 1000 kN and 1700 kN for the long and short-columns, respectively. These events marked the onset of failure, evidenced in Fig. 13 by discontinuities in the otherwise smooth load–displacement curves. At approximately 2000 kN, tests were observed from a safe distance due to the potential for sudden failure. Catastrophic failure occurred shortly thereafter, without significant visual warning apart from an increased frequency of cracking sounds. Testing was terminated immediately once the applied load dropped below 20% of the peak value.

Axial load–displacement curves for mould-cast columns.

Failure events were recorded using a video setup, with selected frames presented in Figs. 11, 12, showing both the moment of failure and post-failure column conditions. The peak load capacities recorded for the short and long mould-cast columns were 2429 kN and 2137 kN, respectively. Both tests had comparable durations of approximately 10–11 min and exhibited similar peak displacements. As illustrated in Fig. 13, the short-column transitioned more gradually into elastic behaviour but ultimately achieved a peak load approximately 300 kN greater than that of the long-column. This outcome aligns with theoretical expectations, as reduced slenderness typically enhances compressive strength by mitigating buckling effects. These results are consistent with previous studies that report increased load-bearing capacity in columns with lower slenderness ratios.

String potentiometers were mounted on the sides of each column to monitor lateral displacement, providing insight into buckling, stability, and deformation behaviour under axial loading. However, significant challenges were encountered in capturing reliable data. The raw displacement signals exhibited considerable irregularity and noise. Even after applying filtering and smoothing techniques, the lateral displacement curves shown in Fig. 14 remain affected by data fluctuations.

Recorded lateral displacement over time for mould-cast a short column and b long-column.

Despite this, the Y-direction displacement of the long-column shows an upward trend with increasing load, consistent with expected buckling behaviour. The magnitude of this displacement was minimal and not visually perceptible during testing. In contrast, the short-column exhibited issues with sensor attachment: the hook began to partially detach around 240 s into the test and fully separated at approximately 540 s, compromising the reliability of its displacement data.

Compression test results of printed columns

The compression tests for the printed columns were conducted using the same procedure as for the mould-cast specimens. Due to difficulties in attaching string potentiometer hooks to the printed surfaces, additional tape was used to secure the sensors. As with the cast columns, no visible deformations were observed during the initial loading phase, particularly for the short-column. However, cracking occurred significantly earlier in the printed specimens, initiating at approximately 300–400 kN for the short-column and around 150 kN for the long-column. These early signs of damage are reflected in the load–displacement curves in Fig. 15, where deviations from smooth behaviour are evident.

Axial load–displacement curves for printed columns.

Cracking continued to develop with increasing load, primarily along interlayer interfaces and at the joints between printed column sections. This suggests insufficient bonding between layers and sections. Progressive detachment of small fragments was observed throughout loading, culminating in major material separations at peak loads of 1412 kN and 1014 kN for the short and long-columns, respectively, achieved within timeframes similar to those of the mould-cast specimens. However, total test durations were substantially longer, extending to nearly one hour per column.

Unlike the mould-cast columns, failure of the printed columns was significantly less violent, as illustrated in Figs. 16, 17. Observed failure modes included delamination and peeling of printed layers, with the outer shell progressively separating from the cast concrete core. This degradation process generated falling debris, which led to the detachment of the string potentiometers; the sensors for the short-column had already detached prior to ultimate failure (Fig. 17). Following the initial detachment of a substantial portion of the printed shell, the columns continued to carry load, but with diminishing capacity as additional pieces separated. The load–displacement curve in Fig. 15 shows a series of minor load recoveries as remaining segments temporarily resisted loading, followed by renewed drops as further delamination occurred. This cyclical deterioration persisted until the applied load fell below 20% of the peak, marking the end of the test.

Figure 18 provides a detailed view of the exposed reinforcement cage from the printed long-column after testing. The GFRP rebar is visibly split and compressed against an adjacent steel ligature, with another ligature found completely fractured. These observations highlight the substantial internal stresses imposed on the reinforcement as the concrete matrix failed and the column’s structural integrity deteriorated. Under increasing axial load, the GFRP reinforcement experienced significant lateral strain, resulting in material splitting and deformation against the surrounding steel ties. The fractured ligature indicates that the internal stresses exceeded the confinement capacity of the reinforcement system, likely accelerating the loss of lateral stability and contributing to the progressive failure of the column.

GFRP rebar and steel ligature on printed long-column.

This behaviour underscores a critical limitation of GFRP reinforcement in high-compression scenarios, particularly where lateral confinement is compromised. Figure 19 showing the full column after testing, reveals that the lower segments remained relatively intact compared to the upper regions, which suffered more extensive damage. This non-uniform damage pattern suggests that the upper sections were subjected to greater stress concentrations or experienced more severe interlayer delamination during loading. Several factors may explain this differential damage. The base of the column may have benefited from increased confinement due to the weight of the overlying sections during curing, resulting in stronger interfacial bonding. Additionally, variations in the properties of the fresh concrete, caused by sequential batching in the pan mixer, could have led to inconsistent bonding quality between sections. Another potential factor is rebar eccentricity. The method of stacking prefabricated column segments could have allowed slight misalignments in the vertical placement of the reinforcement cage, leading to increased eccentricity and reduced confinement effectiveness in the upper sections. These observations point to the importance of construction sequence, material consistency, and reinforcement alignment in the performance of 3D-printed reinforced concrete columns under axial compression.

Although the printed columns demonstrated significantly lower peak load capacities, approximately half that of their mould-cast counterparts, the less violent and more gradual failure mode may be advantageous in applications where controlled degradation and serviceability are priorities. This form of failure could offer improved warning prior to collapse and better accommodate deformation without sudden loss of structural integrity. However, the early onset and extensive development of cracking at relatively low load levels raise concerns regarding serviceability, particularly with respect to crack control in 3D-printed elements.

Overall, the performance of the printed columns highlights both the potential and the current limitations of 3DCP for structural applications. While the reduced strength, especially when printed in discrete sections, remains a critical challenge, the observed failure characteristics suggest that 3DCP with GFRP reinforcement may provide benefits in energy absorption and progressive failure behaviour. It should be noted, however, that the current study does not include axial compression tests on GFRP-only specimens, and thus the isolated compressive contribution of GFRP rebars cannot be directly assessed. Future studies will incorporate such tests to better quantify the role of GFRP reinforcement in 3D-printed columns. These findings support the continued investigation of 3DCP for structural use, particularly where gradual failure and ductility are desirable.

Comparison with theoretical capacities

Theoretical axial capacities for the GFRP-reinforced columns, as well as hypothetical steel-reinforced counterparts, were calculated based on the AS 360037 and ACI 440.1R-1513 guidelines. These values, presented in Table 6, served as benchmarks for evaluating the structural performance of both the mould-cast and 3D-printed columns. Corresponding load–displacement behaviour is shown in Fig. 21.

Axial load–displacement curves for all columns.

The mould-cast columns exhibited performance closely aligned with theoretical predictions. In particular, the short mould-cast column reached 95% of its predicted capacity, indicating reliable structural behaviour and validating the applicability of design models for conventional casting methods. In contrast, the 3D-printed columns achieved approximately half the peak load of the mould-cast specimens, primarily due to interlayer delamination and weak bonding at the interfaces between printed segments.

Among the theoretical estimates, the capacity derived from ACI 440.1R-15, which neglects the axial contribution of GFRP reinforcement, yielded the lowest values and was closest to the experimental data. All theoretical predictions overestimated the actual capacity, including the unreinforced concrete capacity, suggesting that the assumed 28-day compressive strength was likely higher than the actual material strength achieved during testing. Additionally, the steel-reinforced column capacities, included for comparative purposes, were expectedly higher due to the superior compressive strength of steel compared to GFRP.

Failure modes also differed notably between specimen types. The mould-cast columns failed abruptly and in a brittle manner at peak load, consistent with the behaviour of monolithic concrete under compression. Conversely, the 3D-printed columns displayed a more gradual, stepwise failure characterised by progressive delamination and shell separation. While this reduced ultimate strength, it suggests an alternative energy dissipation mechanism and potential for more controlled failure, which may be beneficial in specific structural applications.

These findings highlight the predictability and structural efficiency of traditional casting techniques, which closely conform to theoretical models. In contrast, 3DCP offers advantages in material usage and energy dissipation but requires significant improvements in interlayer bonding and reinforcement strategies to meet the compressive strength demands of structural applications. Future research should prioritise enhancing interfacial adhesion and investigating hybrid or alternative reinforcement systems to improve the structural viability of 3D-printed concrete elements.

Conclusions

This study investigated the structural performance of 3D-printed concrete columns reinforced with GFRP bars under axial compression and compared their behaviour with that of conventionally mould-cast GFRP-reinforced columns. The 3D concrete printing (3DCP) method was used to fabricate permanent formwork in sectional units, into which reinforcement was placed and filled with the same concrete mix. Based on the experimental results, the following conclusions can be drawn:

Column Performance by Height: In both construction methods, short columns consistently outperformed long columns. The mould-cast short and long columns reached peak loads of 2429 kN and 2137 kN, respectively, while the corresponding 3D-printed columns reached 1412 kN and 1014 kN. These results highlight the influence of slenderness on axial capacity.

The mould-cast short and long columns reached peak loads of 2429 kN and 2137 kN, respectively, while the corresponding 3D-printed columns reached 1412 kN and 1014 kN. These results highlight the influence of slenderness on axial capacity.

Structural Capacity and Failure Mode: 3D-printed columns achieved approximately 50% of the peak load capacity of their mould-cast counterparts. This reduced performance is attributed to weak interlayer bonding and delamination between printed sections. However, the printed columns exhibited gradual, progressive failure modes rather than the brittle, sudden failure seen in the mould-cast columns, suggesting potential advantages in ductility and energy dissipation.

For critical structural applications, 3D-printed columns in their current form are not yet viable; however, with improvements in mix design, interlayer bonding, and continuous printing techniques, they could become competitive alternatives for lightweight, durable, and corrosion-resistant structural systems in the future.

Further work should also investigate optimized mix designs and reinforcement strategies tailored for hybrid 3DCP-cast systems.
