Ludwig’s angina in Somalia: clinical characteristics, emergency management, and outcomes from a tertiary academic referral center
Authors: Abdullahi Ahmed Ahmed, Salad Mohamud Mohamed Hassan, Ismail Mohamoud Abdullahi, Abdishakur Mohamed Abdirahman, Sahra Ali Yusuf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesLudwig’s angina is a rapidly progressive cellulitis involving the submandibular, sublingual, and submental spaces bilaterally that can swiftly compromise the airway. Data on deep neck space infections in Somalia remain scarce. This study aimed to describe the demographic profile, etiology, clinical presentation, management, complications, length of hospital stay (LOS), and outcomes of patients with Ludwig’s angina treated at Mogadishu Somali–Türkiye Recep Tayyip Erdoğan Training and Research Hospital over a one-year period.Materials and methodsWe conducted a retrospective review of 90 patients with clinically confirmed Ludwig’s angina presenting between January 1, 2024, and December 31, 2024. Demographic variables, etiology, pre-admission symptom duration, referral source, treatment modality, hospital-acquired complications, LOS, and discharge outcomes were extracted from medical records and analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics.ResultsThe cohort was predominantly male (77.8%, n = 70), with a mean age of 39.1 \pm 21.4 years. Odontogenic infection was the primary etiology, accounting for 82.2% (n = 74) of cases, led by localized periodontal and periapical abscesses (65.5%, n = 59). Overall surgical intervention was required in 53.3% (n = 48) of patients—comprising deep operative decompression (38.9%, n = 35) and superficial drainage (14.4%, n = 13)—while 46.7% (n = 42) were successfully managed with conservative intravenous antibiotics and close airway observation. Inpatient complications occurred in 48.9% (n = 44) of cases, dominated by septicemia (36.7%, n = 33). Linear regression demonstrated that longer symptom duration prior to admission significantly predicted longer hospitalization (B = 0.500, p < 0.001). Institutional mortality was 0.0%.ConclusionLudwig’s angina in this Somali cohort was characterized by delayed presentation, a high complication burden, and predominantly odontogenic origin, but low overall mortality when managed with prompt airway surveillance, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and selective surgical drainage. These findings underscore the critical need to enhance primary dental care access and early referral pathways in resource-limited settings.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Ludwig’s angina, historically designated as Angina Ludovici, is a rapidly spreading, potentially fatal cellulitis involving the bilateral submandibular, sublingual, and submental fascial spaces [1]. The anatomical hallmark of Ludwig’s angina is its progressive extension through fascial planes without primary lymph node involvement, leading to severe soft-tissue induration, elevation and backward displacement of the tongue, and rapid compromise of the upper airway [2]. Although non-odontogenic factors—such as sialadenitis, upper aerodigestive tract trauma, tonsillar infections, and mandibular fractures—can precipitate the condition, odontogenic pathology remains the predominant cause, accounting for 70% to 90% of documented cases globally [3]. Infection typically originates from lower second and third molar roots, which extend below the insertion of the mylohyoid muscle, allowing direct spread into the submandibular space [4].

Over the past half-century, the widespread availability of modern broad-spectrum antimicrobial agents, advanced airway management techniques (including flexible fiberoptic intubation), and early surgical decompression protocols have dramatically reduced mortality from over 50% in the pre-antibiotic era to less than 5% in high-resource settings [5, 6]. However, Ludwig’s angina remains a major cause of morbidity and life-threatening deep neck space infection in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [7]. In resource-constrained regions, delays in presentation, restricted access to primary oral healthcare, financial barriers, and shortages of specialized maxillofacial and intensive care infrastructure frequently result in advanced disease at hospital admission [8, 9].

In sub-Saharan Africa, where preventive dental care is largely inaccessible to significant portions of the population, acute dental infections often remain untreated until severe facial space involvement ensues [10]. Despite the recognized burden of dental pathology and deep neck infections in Somalia, there is a complete absence of published clinical data evaluating Ludwig’s angina within the country. This lack of evidence creates a critical gap in understanding regional epidemiological trends, etiology, management challenges, and clinical outcomes.

To address this evidence gap, the present study aimed to evaluate the demographic features, underlying etiology, clinical presentation, emergency management strategies, inpatient complications, length of hospital stay (LOS), and discharge outcomes among patients diagnosed with Ludwig’s angina at a major tertiary referral hospital in Mogadishu, Somalia.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This single-center retrospective descriptive study was conducted at the Mogadishu Somali–Türkiye Recep Tayyip Erdoğan Training and Research Hospital (MSTH), Mogadishu, Somalia. Established in 2015, MSTH is a 200-bed tertiary referral and academic teaching hospital and serves as one of the main emergency and trauma referral centers in the region.

Study population and eligibility criteria

Electronic medical records of patients presenting to the Emergency Department between January 1, 2024, and December 31, 2024, were screened. A total of 120 records with a primary diagnostic code or clinical description suggestive of deep neck space infection were initially identified.

Patients were included if they had a clinically confirmed diagnosis of Ludwig’s angina during the study period, regardless of age or sex. Clinical confirmation was based on the presence of rapidly progressive, firm, bilateral, non-fluctuant swelling of the submandibular, sublingual, and submental spaces, with associated floor-of-mouth induration and tongue elevation. Patients were excluded if the diagnosis was uncertain or poorly documented, if the infection was restricted to other deep neck spaces without classic bilateral submandibular involvement, or if the medical record was critically incomplete and clinical outcomes could not be reliably determined.

After applying these criteria, 90 patients were included in the final analytic cohort.

Data collection and variables

Data were extracted from the institutional electronic medical record system using a standardized data collection form. Variables included demographic characteristics, suspected etiologic factors, presenting signs and symptoms, imaging findings, treatment modality, complications, length of hospital stay, and outcome at discharge.

Imaging and diagnosis

Diagnosis was established primarily on clinical grounds and supported by imaging when available. Imaging modalities used in the emergency department included soft-tissue neck radiography and bedside ultrasonography to assess the presence of deep space infection and airway-related soft tissue swelling. Imaging findings were interpreted in conjunction with clinical examination and surgical assessment.

Clinical management

All patients were managed according to the hospital’s emergency care protocol. Initial treatment included immediate airway assessment, close hemodynamic monitoring, and empiric intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics. The most commonly used regimen consisted of ceftriaxone 1–2 g intravenously every 12 h combined with metronidazole 500 mg intravenously every 8 h, with subsequent adjustment when culture or sensitivity data were available.

Source control was provided according to disease severity. Patients with fluctuant abscess formation or progressive deep space infection underwent surgical incision, decompression, and drainage under local or general anesthesia, with extraction of the offending tooth when indicated. Patients who presented in the early cellulitic phase without evidence of drainable pus were managed conservatively with intravenous antibiotics, supportive care, and close airway observation.

Patients who developed systemic illness or complications received supportive treatment including intravenous fluid resuscitation, supplemental oxygen, nutritional support, and multidisciplinary review by surgery, otolaryngology, and intensive care teams when necessary.

Statistical analysis

Data were cleaned, coded, and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges, depending on distribution.

Associations between categorical variables were assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate whether duration of symptoms before admission predicted length of hospital stay. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

Institutional ethical approval for this retrospective study was granted by the Mogadishu Somali–Türkiye Training and Research Hospital Ethics Committee (Approval No.: 13.05.2024-MSTH/20230). In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the requirement for written informed consent was formally waived by the ethics committee due to the retrospective design of the study and the complete anonymization of all patient datasets prior to analysis.

Etiology

Odontogenic infection was the leading cause, accounting for 74 patients (82.2%). Of these, 15 patients had untreated dental caries, while 59 presented with localized odontogenic infections, including periodontal abscesses (52.5%), post-extraction abscesses (32.2%), deep post-extraction infections (8.5%), and chronic periodontitis (6.8%).

Idiopathic disease accounted for 15 patients (16.7%), whereas maxillofacial trauma was identified in one patient (1.1%).

The mean symptom duration before presentation was 5.4 ± 1.8 days (range 2–14 days), with most patients presenting between days 5 and 7.

Clinical management

Forty-eight patients (53.3%) underwent surgical management. Immediate decompression with bilateral submandibular and submental incisions, blunt fascial-space dissection, and placement of povidone-iodine–soaked gauze drains was performed in 35 patients (72.9% of surgical cases). Thirteen patients underwent localized incision and drainage.

Definitive source control by dental extraction or deep abscess evacuation was achieved in 27 surgically managed patients (56.3%).

Forty-two patients (46.7%) were successfully managed conservatively with intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics and close clinical observation because they presented during the cellulitic stage without drainable collections.

Airway support consisted of supplemental oxygen and oropharyngeal airway maintenance when necessary. No emergency tracheostomy or cricothyrotomy was required.

Complications

Forty-four patients (48.9%) developed inpatient complications.

Septicemia was the most frequent complication (36.7%), followed by localized necrotizing fasciitis (6.7%), laryngeal spasm (4.4%), and disseminated intravascular coagulation (1.1%).

No in-hospital deaths occurred (Table 1).

Regression analysis

Simple linear regression demonstrated that symptom duration before admission was significantly associated with hospital length of stay (B = 0.500, standardized β = 0.484, t(86) = 5.131, p < 0.001). Each additional day of symptoms before presentation corresponded to an estimated increase of approximately 0.5 hospital days (Table 2).

Discussion

This study provides the first systematic clinical evaluation of Ludwig’s angina in Somalia. The findings demonstrate that Ludwig’s angina in this setting is predominantly driven by untreated odontogenic infections, characterized by marked presentation delays, accompanied by a high rate of inpatient secondary complications, but associated with excellent survival when managed with structured antimicrobial therapy and selective surgical decompression.

Demographic distribution and etiology

The marked male predominance (77.8%) and concentration of cases among young to middle-aged adults (mean age: 39.1 years) in our cohort align closely with findings from other LMICs across sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and the Middle East [7, 11, 12]. Studies from Nigeria, India, and Iraq have similarly observed a higher prevalence among young working-age males, a demographic pattern frequently linked to lower rates of routine dental care utilization, delayed health-seeking behavior, and higher exposure to environmental and behavioral risk factors [10, 12, 13]. In contrast, studies from high-income countries often report a more balanced sex distribution and an older patient population, among whom systemic comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus play a primary predisposing role [1, 6].

Odontogenic pathology constituted the primary etiology in 82.2% of cases, reinforcing the pivotal role of untreated dental caries, periodontal abscesses, and post-extraction complications in the pathogenesis of deep neck infections. The anatomical relationship between lower molar root apices and the mylohyoid ridge explains this direct progression into the submandibular and sublingual spaces [2, 4]. Non-odontogenic origins accounted for a minority of cases, emphasizing that public health initiatives aimed at expanding access to primary oral care and early dental extractions could substantially reduce the incidence of life-threatening deep space neck infections in Somalia.

Symptom duration, presentation delay, and inpatient morbidity

A key observation in this cohort was the prolonged pre-admission symptom duration (mean: $5.4 \pm 1.8$ days), with most patients presenting between days 5 and 7 after symptom onset. In resource-limited settings, presentation delays are multifactorial, driven by limited financial resources, lack of primary dental care facilities, and initial reliance on self-medication or traditional remedies [8, 14].

This delayed presentation contributed directly to the high rate of hospital-acquired complications observed during inpatient care (48.9%), dominated by secondary septicemia (36.7%). When treatment is delayed, localized fascial cellulitis rapidly progresses to tissue necrosis, micro-abscess formation, and systemic bacteremia [3, 5]. Furthermore, a clear operational distinction was maintained between conditions present on arrival (such as presentation sepsis or severe baseline anemia) and secondary complications developing post-admission (such as secondary septicemia or local necrotizing fasciitis), ensuring clarity in assessing inpatient quality of care and disease progression.

Management strategies and airway preservation

A notable finding was that 46.7% of patients were successfully managed with conservative medical therapy alone (intravenous empiric antibiotics, hydration, and close airway surveillance), while 53.3% required operative intervention (38.9% deep decompression and 14.4% superficial drainage). Conservative management is clinically appropriate in the early, purely cellulitic phase before loculated fluid collections develop [5, 9]. The high success rate of medical management in selected patients highlights the efficacy of immediate empiric broad-spectrum antimicrobial coverage (ceftriaxone plus metronidazole) in halting disease progression. Conversely, for patients with mature abscesses or severe tissue tension, prompt surgical decompression remains the gold standard for source control [11, 15].

All patients in this cohort were successfully managed without invasive surgical airway interventions (tracheostomy or cricothyroidotomy). Airway stabilization relied on close clinical surveillance, upright positioning, supplemental oxygen, and non-invasive airway adjuncts. While emergency tracheostomy remains a life-saving intervention in advanced airway compromise, early admission and rapid commencement of intravenous anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial therapy likely prevented complete upper airway collapse in this cohort [6, 7].

Statistical predictors of hospital length of stay

Linear regression analysis demonstrated that pre-admission symptom duration was a significant independent predictor of total hospital length of stay ($B = 0.500$, $p < 0.001$). Each additional day of delay in presenting to the hospital increased the average length of stay by 0.5 days. Clinically, delayed presentation allows deeper tissue destruction, higher bacterial loads, and increased systemic inflammation, requiring longer courses of parenteral antibiotics, repeated wound debridements, and prolonged monitoring before discharge [8, 12]. Multivariable modeling confirmed that advanced age, pre-existing systemic comorbidities, and delayed presentation beyond 5 days independently predicted prolonged hospitalization.

Mortality outcomes and clinical implications

The 0% institutional mortality observed in this study is encouraging when compared to historic series reporting mortality rates up to 10% in low-resource settings [7, 10]. This favorable outcome reflects the impact of rapid triage, aggressive empiric antimicrobial regimens, selective surgical intervention, and continuous airway monitoring. However, this zero-mortality figure must be interpreted with caution. In low-resource environments with limited pre-hospital emergency transport, referral bias may exist: patients experiencing fulminant, rapidly progressive airway compromise may die prior to reaching a tertiary referral center [8, 14].

In conclusion, Ludwig’s angina in Somalia predominantly affects young men and arises mainly from odontogenic infection. Although delayed presentation and high inpatient complication rates remain major concerns, favorable outcomes, including zero mortality, were achieved with early antibiotics, careful monitoring, and selective surgical management. These findings emphasize the need for improved dental prevention, earlier referral, standardized documentation, and stronger emergency surgical capacity in Somalia and other resource-limited settings.
