Novel approach for vaccine delivery using mRNA lipid nanoparticles for in vivo production of monoclonal antibodies
Authors: Kenneth Lundstrom
Published: 2026

Main text

The COVID-19 pandemic triggered an unprecedented development of prophylactic and therapeutic approaches to conquer the disease. In this context, monoclonal antibodies1 (mAbs) and mRNA-based vaccines2 have proven efficient against COVID-19 in clinical trials and mAbs have been approved for the treatment of individuals with high-risk for severe COVID-193 and mRNA-based vaccines have been subjected to mass vaccinations.4 However, due to issues with waning vaccine effectiveness with time and the continuous identification of emerging SARS-CoV-2 variants, it has been necessary to explore new possibilities to ensure proper protection against recurrence of coronavirus and other viral respiratory infections. In the recent publication in Molecular Therapy Nucleic Acids entitled “Delivery of monoclonal antibodies using mRNA lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) confers protection against SARS-CoV-2 and influenza,” Wheatley and co-workers presented an interesting alternative for vaccine development, where mRNA encapsulated in LNPs produced monoclonal antibodies in vaccine recipients in vivo.5

Applications of mAbs for the treatment of respiratory diseases including COVID-19 have been well established; although the drawback has been the high cost and time-consuming need for recombinant manufacturing of mAbs and their administration to patients. In attempts to accelerate the mAb production and to make the process more cost effective, Wheatley and co-workers introduced mRNA carrying the sequences for both heavy chain (HC) and light chain (LC) of SARS-CoV-2 and influenza virus mAbs into LNPs targeting SARS-CoV-2 and influenza viruses, respectively.5 In the case of SARS-CoV-2, the previously isolated neutralizing human IgG1 mAb PDI204 targeting the receptor binding domain (RBD) of the spike (S) protein6 was compared to mRNA-LNP-based delivery of either a mixture of PDI204 encoding separately HC and LC mRNA constructs or in a single mRNA construct linked by a furin cleavage site and a self-cleaving P2A motif (HC-P2A-LC).7 The mRNA-LNP formulations were analogous to the formulation in the BNT162b2 vaccine. There were no differences in the expression levels of functional PDI204 mAbs in transfected HEK293T cells between the two mRNA formulations. In contrast, intravenous administration of the HC-P2A-LC mRNA-LNPs into C57BL/6 mice generated significantly higher serum levels of PDI204 and higher neutralization activity compared to the HC and LC mRNA-LNP mixture. Biodistribution studies demonstrated that high PDI204 mAb levels were detected in mouse serum and mucosal fluids after both intravenous and intramuscular administration. However, only minimal expression levels were obtained after intranasal delivery. The pharmacokinetic comparison of intravenous administration of HC-P2-LC mRNA-LNPs to recombinant PDI204 protein in mice showed peak expression 1-day post-injection for recombinant PDI204, while the levels continued to increase for 3–5 days for mRNA-LNP delivery. As the PDI204 protein remained detectable for 70 days, the mRNA-LNP delivery resulted in a rapid decline of mAb levels after 7 days. The rapid reduction in mAb titers was postulated to be related to the induction anti-drug antibodies (ADA).8 Previously, strong immune responses against encoded antigens have been detected after BNT162b2 vaccine administration. Likewise, significant antibody responses against PDI204 were found in all animals immunized with PDI204 mRNA-LNPs, and the rapid reduction in mAb titers was likely due to ADA responses. However, based on neutralization assays, approximately 50% of the PDI204 activity was retained in the serum from mice immunized with mRNA/LNPs, suggesting that functional antibody activity was retained.

Protection efficacy of recombinant PDI204 protein and PDI204 mRNA-LNPs was evaluated in a transgenic human ACE2 mouse model intranasally infected with the SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.1 variant 7 days after intravenous injection of mAbs.6 Robust protection of lungs with no viral load detected was obtained after injection of 10 μg PDI204 protein. A dose-dependent reduction in viral loads was seen in animals immunized with PDI204 mRNA-LNPs. However, the degree of protection was inferior to PDI204 administration, which was most likely due to the ADA.

In the case of influenza virus, mRNA HC and LC of the prototypic HV B10 neutralizing human IgG1 mAb targeting hemagglutinin (HA) on pH1N1 influenza virus were linked by a P2A motif.9 Similar to SARS-CoV-2, administration of HV B10 protein elicited rapid mAb production, which showed a slow decay over more than 50 days, whereas mRNA-based delivery induced higher mAb concentrations in the serum but a rapid clearance by day 7 post-administration. Induction of anti-HV B10 antibodies was detected at 7 and 14 days after mRNA-LNP administration. Mice treated with either HV B10 protein or mRNA-LNPs were challenged with lethal doses of the influenza A/California/04/2009 virus, which resulted in 60% survival for animals receiving a 10 μg dose of HV B10 protein, but no protection was obtained after administration of 1 μg or 5 μg doses. In contrast, all mice receiving 1, 5, or 10 μg of mRNA-LNPs were protected against challenges. Moreover, when the intervals between vaccine administration and challenge were extended, lower HV B10 serum concentrations and higher ADA titers were detected. However, mRNA-LNP-based delivery provided superior protection against influenza virus challenges overall compared to recombinant protein administration.

The recent COVID-19 pandemic has clearly provided an excellent example of safe and efficient vaccine development, which has made a difference from being at the brink of a global health disaster to relatively quickly downgrading COVID-19 to an endemic status and allowing life to return to normal pre-pandemic conditions. Moreover, supplemental therapeutic and protective activity was documented for mAbs engineered against SARS-CoV-2, particularly in the elderly and high-risk immunocompromised individuals. The formulation of mRNA-LNPs expressing mAbs as described by Wheatley and co-workers6 confirmed efficient delivery and expression of mAbs both in vitro and in vivo. The design of a single mRNA hosting both HC and LC showed superior mAb titers compared to the delivery of separate HC and LC mRNA-LNPs, which most likely indicated that the HC and LC mRNA did not end up in the same cells. Formulation and manufacturing of single mRNA-LNPs are also simpler. Moreover, it is important to point out that the lipid components present in LNP formulations have been demonstrated to have a clear impact on mRNA delivery, trafficking, and immunostimulatory activity.10 As demonstrated by Wheatley and co-workers, LNP formulations used for the BNT162b2 generated high mAb accumulation in mice after intravenous and intramuscular administration but not after intranasal delivery.6 In contrast, intranasal delivery of LNP encapsulated self-replicating Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus (VEE) RNA expressing the SARS-CoV-2 S protein resulted in induction of key respiratory mucosal T cell populations and prevention and transmission of COVID-19 in hamsters.11 Another issue with mRNA-LNP-based mAb delivery is the reduced in vivo half-life and rapid serum clearance of mAbs compared to the administration of mAbs as recombinant protein. This difference might be due to the adjuvant properties of LNPs. The interval between vaccination and challenge can also have an impact as in the case of SARS-CoV-2. Wheatley and co-workers challenged mice 11 days post-vaccination leading to inferior protection after mRNA-LNP administration compared to recombinant PDI204 protein.6 In contrast, the challenge with influenza virus took place only 1-day post-vaccination resulting in a high and potentially more robust protection after mRNA-LNP delivery than recombinant PDI204 protein administration. Another strategy to consider for mRNA-LNP delivery is the utilization of self-replicating RNA vectors, which can provide effective expression at 100- to 1,000-fold lower doses compared to conventional mRNA12 generating a better balance between mAb expression and reduced immunogenicity.

Although the promising alternative approach of mRNA-based mAb expression described previously has been achieved, additional engineering and formulation of LNPs are required to enhance delivery and mucosal localization of prophylactic and therapeutic mAbs. This approach should provide a cost-effective delivery of mAbs and should aid and strengthen in being prepared for emerging new respiratory infections and the prevention of future pandemics.
