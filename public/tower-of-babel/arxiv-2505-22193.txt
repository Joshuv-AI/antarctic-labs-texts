Physics-inspired Generative AI models via real hardware-based noisy quantum diffusion
Marco Parigi, Stefano Martina, Francesco Aldo Venturelli, Filippo Caruso

Abstract
Quantum Diffusion Models (QDMs) are an emerging paradigm in Generative AI that aims to use quantum properties to improve the performances of their classical counterparts. However, existing algorithms are not easily scalable due to the limitations of near-term quantum devices. Following our previous work on QDMs, here we propose and implement two physics-inspired protocols. In the first, we use the formalism of quantum stochastic walks, showing that a specific interplay of quantum and classical dynamics in the forward process produces statistically more robust models generating sets of MNIST images with lower Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) than using totally classical dynamics. In the second approach, we realize an algorithm to generate images by exploiting the intrinsic noise of real IBM quantum hardware with only four qubits. Our work could be a starting point to pave the way for new scenarios for large-scale algorithms in quantum Generative AI, where quantum noise is neither mitigated nor corrected, but instead exploited as a useful resource.

Generative Diffusion Models, Quantum Machine Learning, Quantum Noise, Quantum Computing, Quantum Stochastic Walks.

2.1
Quantum, hybrid and classical stochastic diffusion

In classical

after
T

. For a more detailed description of the
dm

Section

4
.

Next, we consider the family of

K

x_{t}\in 1,\dots,K
at the time step
t\in[0,T]

;
Hoogeboom et al.
. In the following, we denote by
\mathbf{x}
the one-hot version of

and

for

at time

is obtained drawing from the transition kernel:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t}

\displaystyle\sim q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1}),

(1)

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})

\displaystyle=\text{Cat}(\mathbf{x}_{t};\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mathbf{Q}_{t}),

(2)

where Cat

with probability
\mathbf{p}
,

is the matrix that contains the transition probabilities of

The diffusion transition chain after

q(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1}).

(3)

In this section, we study the performance in the image generation task when the classical stochastic dynamics in the forward chain are replaced or interplay with quantum stochastic diffusion processes. For this purpose, we decide to adopt the formalism of
qsw
that provides a useful tool to study the transition from classical to quantum diffusion dynamics. This decision is inspired by previous works where the

;
Caruso et al. 2016
;

Formally, a continuous-time

dynamics is described by the Kossakowski–Lindblad-Gorini master equation

;
Lindblad 1976
;
Gorini et al. 1976
:

\frac{d\rho}{dt}=(1-\omega)i[H,\rho]+\omega\sum_{j}L_{j}\rho L_{j}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{j}L_{j}^{\dagger},\rho\},

(4)

where
\rho
is the walker density matrix,
H
is the Hamiltonian of the system describing the coherent evolution, and
L_{j}
are Lindblad operators responsible for the incoherent dynamics, which represent the interactions of the system with an external environment. The continuous parameter
\omega\in[0,1]
quantifies the interaction between coherent and incoherent evolution.
For
\omega=0
,
Eq.

4
describes the evolution of a pure
qw
. Instead, setting
\omega=1
and choosing
L_{ij}=S_{ij}\outerproduct{i}{j}
,

4
describes the motion of a
crw
where
\ket{i}
is the quantum basis state associated with node
i
of a graph and
S_{ij}
is the transition matrix of the walker from node
j
to
i
. A more complete description on
qw
and
crw
is given in
Section

4
.

In the following, a categorical data sample is represented by a quantum stochastic walker that evolves on a cycle graph of
8
nodes, which represent the categories of the sample. In this approach, the density operator
\rho
of
Eq.

4
describes the state evolution of the walker on the graph: the diagonal elements
\rho_{ii}
give the probabilities that the walker is at the node
i
, while the off-diagonal elements
\rho_{ij}
describe and contain information on the peculiar quantum mechanical effects (coherence) during the diffusion process. Next, we refer to the diagonal and off-diagonal elements of the density matrix
\rho

and

In

2.3
Implementation on
nisq
Devices

A hybrid
qsw
dynamics can be interpreted as a
qw
interacting with an external environment introducing noise. In this section, we therefore perform image generation by implementing a
qw
that exploits the intrinsic noise of a
nisq
device in the forward process. This dynamic is efficiently implemented on a cycle graph with the quantum circuit of Razzoli et al.

The efficiency of the algorithm allows us to modulate the amount of noise introduced in the forward chain, which has low values by default and is increased by delays. As in
Section

2.2
, we represent each pixel by a
qw

8
nodes. Furthermore, the rotation-invariant property of the cycle graph allows us to run each walker as if starting from the same initial state, and then re-mapping the outcome to the specific value of the pixel color by a shift operation. In this way, the forward chain is run only once for all the
qw

More precisely, our model requires only
4

8

8

3
Discussion and Conclusions

In this work, we show that the impact of quantum noise within the forward
dm
dynamics influences the effectiveness of the backward generation process. In particular, we find that a model with a hybrid
qsw
diffusion dynamics produces sets of samples with a lower
fid
and is also statistically more solid with respect to its classical counterpart.

Successively, we propose a model that allows one to generate images of any size by harnessing the noise of real
nisq
devices taking into account the topology and connectivity of the
qpu
. More precisely, we generate
28\times 28
gray-scale MNIST images using only
4
qubits by efficiently implementing a quantum walk dynamics on a cycle graph whose nodes are the color values of a single image pixel. The invariant property of the graph allows one to independently run the walkers for the single pixels starting from the same initial state and then remapping the outcome to the specific value of the pixel color. Furthermore, our forward protocol enables the implementation of multiple walkers in parallel, requiring a maximum
3
degree of connectivity between qubits of a
qpu
. This allows for the implementation on the currently available
nisq
devices.

In conclusion, we show how noise can be used as a resource in the context of
q-gen-ai
and not only be a detrimental factor for quantum algorithms.
Some future research directions can focus on a major integration of the
qpu
topology with the
qw
implemented at the circuit level. In particular, we can take more advantages from the connectivity to increase the range of the pixel values, and thus generate high-quality images. Moreover, error correction or mitigation techniques could be used to better control the level of noise of the quantum forward to further improve the capabilities of the backward network. Another interesting outlook can be the possibility of a physical realization of the quantum walk making our model directly applied to quantum data, without performing any quantum embedding in the first stage of the algorithms and then using a quantum
ann
in the reverse process. In conclusion, we believe that the latter can be fruitful where it is necessary to learn unknown quantum phenomena, for instance, in quantum sensing, metrology, chemistry, and biology scenarios.

4.1
Diffusion Models

dm
(
dm
) are a class of latent variable generative models trained to learn the underlying unknown data distribution of a finite dataset in order to generate new similarly distributed synthetic data. The core idea is to use a classical Markov chain to gradually convert an unknown (data) distribution, called
posterior
, to a simple well-known distribution, e.g. Gaussian or uniform, called
prior
.
The most generic pipeline of
dm
is characterized by a forward (or diffusion) and a backward (or denoising) process.
In the forward process an initial sample
\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim q(\mathbf{x}_{0})
is corrupted in a sequence of
T
increasingly noisy latent variables
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{T}
. Formally, the diffusion process is described by a classical random process via a Markov chain that gradually injects noise on the initial sample
\mathbf{x}_{0}
. More precisely, this is realized by using the Markov transition kernel:

q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})=K_{\pi}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1};\beta_{t}),

(10)

q(\mathbf{x})=\int d\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\ K_{\pi}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\beta)q(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),

(11)

where
\beta_{t}\in(0,1)
is an hyper-parameter at time
t
of the model (or in physical terms the diffusion rate) describing the
level of noise
added at each time step, and
\mathbf{x}_{t}
and
\mathbf{x}_{t-1}
are the random noisy latent variables at the time steps
t
and
t-1
, respectively.
The scheduling of
\beta_{t}
is chosen and fixed such that the initial data distribution
q(\mathbf{x}_{0})
convergences to a well-known stationary distribution
\pi(\mathbf{x}_{T})
in the limit
T\rightarrow\infty
.
The forward trajectory after performing
T
time steps of diffusion can be written as:

q(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1}),

(12)

where the chain rule of probability and the Markov property of the process are used to factorize the joint distribution
q(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})
. In addition, in the diffusion process there are no trainable parameters and therefore it does not involve the use of any learning model.
The idea of the backward process is to reverse the forward dynamics moving from a pure noise sample
\mathbf{x}_{T}\sim\pi(\mathbf{x}_{T})
towards a sample of the initial distribution
q(\mathbf{x}_{0})
. The denoising is implemented by an
ann
that is trained to learn the reverse trajectory of the diffusion process:

p(\mathbf{x}_{T})=\pi(\mathbf{x}_{T})

(13)

p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})=p(\mathbf{x}_{T})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t}),

(14)

where
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t})
is a parameterized transition kernel having the same functional form of
q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})
.
A deep
ann
, usually with a U-Net architecture
Ronneberger et al. 2015
, is used to estimate the parameters
\theta
at each time step.
The denoising network is trained to optimize the negative log-likelihood on the training data writing the evidence lower bound (ELBO)
Kingma and Welling 2014
as follow:

\begin{split}L&=D_{\text{KL}}(q(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})||p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}))\\
&=-\mathbb{E}[\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})]+\text{const}\\
&=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[-\log p(\mathbf{x}_{T})-\sum_{t\geq 1}\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})}{q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})}\Bigg]+\text{const}\\
&\geq\mathbb{E}[-\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0})]+\text{const},\end{split}

(15)

where the Jensen’s inequality holds in the last line, and the
D_{\text{KL}}(\cdot||\cdot)
is the
kl
divergence, which computes the difference between two probability distributions. The objective of the optimization procedure is to minimize the loss function
L
to reduce the difference between the probability distribution
q(\mathbf{x}_{0})
and the parameterized distribution
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
.

4.2
Diffusion models in discrete state-spaces

dm
in
discrete state-spaces
has been introduced by Sohl-Dickstein et al. for binary random variables
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
, and then generalized to categorical random variables characterized by uniform transition probability distribution by Hoogeboom et al.
Hoogeboom et al.
. In the general framework, given a scalar discrete
K
-categories random variable
X
taking values
x_{t}\in 1,\dots,K
for
t\in[0,T]
, the forward transition probabilities from the category
i
to the category
j
at time
t
can be realized by matrices:

[\mathbf{Q}_{t}]_{i,j}=q(x_{t}=j|x_{t-1}=i).

(16)

Denoting the one-hot version of
x
with the row vector
\mathbf{x}
, i.e. a vector whose elements for the category
k
are
x_{k}=1
and
x_{j}=0
for
j\not=k
, forward transition kernel can be written as:

q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})=\text{Cat}(\mathbf{x}_{t};\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mathbf{Q}_{t}),

(17)

where Cat
(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{p})
is the categorical distribution over the one-hot row vector
\mathbf{x}
with probability
\mathbf{p}
.
Starting from an initial data
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, the data
\mathbf{x}_{t}
after
t
time steps can be sampled from the transition kernel:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t}

\displaystyle\sim q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{0}),

(18)

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\text{Cat}(\mathbf{x}_{t};\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{x}_{0}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{t}),

(19)

where
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{t}:=\mathbf{Q}_{1}\mathbf{Q}_{2}\dots\mathbf{Q}_{t}
is the cumulative product of the transition matrices.
The rows of the matrix
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{t}
must satisfy two constraints: i) must sum to one to conserve the probability mass, and ii) must converge to a known stationary distribution in the limit
t\rightarrow\infty
. Moreover, it can be shown
Hoogeboom et al.
;
Austin et al.
that a closed-form of the categorical
posterior

q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0})
can be computed:

q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0})=\dfrac{q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_{0})q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{0})}{q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{0})},

(20)

where
q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_{0})=q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})
due to the Markov property and all the terms can be calculated from
Eqs.

17
and
19
.
The denoising process is implemented via an
ann
predicting the logits of the parameterized distribution:

p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t}),

(21)

which has the functional form of a categorical distribution
Hoogeboom et al.
;
Austin et al.
.
The optimization is realized by minimize the loss function:

L=D_{\text{KL}}(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0})||p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t})).

(22)

4.3
Classical Random Walk on Graph

A graph is a pair
G=(V,E)
, where
V
is a finite and non-empty set whose elements
v_{i}
,
i=1,\dots\absolutevalue{V}
, are called vertices (or nodes), and
E
is a non-empty set of unordered pairs of vertices
e_{ij}=\{v_{i},v_{j}\}
, called edges (or links). The
order
and the
size
of a graph are the cardinality
\absolutevalue{V}
and
\absolutevalue{E}
of the sets
V
and
E
, respectively. The number
d_{v}
of edges connected to the vertex
v
is called
degree
of the vertex.
A graph is called
undirected
if the edges of the graph do not have a direction, or
directed
otherwise.
A graph is completely defined by its
adjacency matrix

A
that contains information on the topology of the graph, and whose element are defined as follow:

[A]_{i,j}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if}\quad e_{ij}\in E.\\
0,&\text{if}\quad e_{ij}\not\in E.\end{cases}

(23)

In a discrete-time
crw
on an undirected graph
G
, at each step
t\in\mathbb{N}
a walker jumps between two connected nodes
v_{i},v_{j}
with some probability that is described by the stochastic transition matrix
S=[S]_{i,j}

Weiss and Rubin
;
Weiss
;
Caruso 2014
.
S
is related to the adjacency matrix by the equation:
[S]_{i,j}=[A]_{i,j}/d_{i}
.
Formally, a walker is represented by a discrete random variable
X
and its trajectory after the
T
time steps is the set
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{T}\}
, where the value
x_{t}
, with
t=1,\dots,T
, corresponds to the node occupied by the walker at time
t
.
A
crw
is a Markov process, that is, the distribution at time
t+1
depends only on the distribution at time
t
.
Given
\mathbf{p}_{t}=\{p_{i}^{(t)}\}
the occupation probability distribution of a walker over the nodes
v_{i}
after
t
time steps, the evolution of the distribution at time
t+1
is given by:

\mathbf{p}_{t+1}=S\mathbf{p}_{t}.

(24)

The distribution of a
crw
converges to a limiting
stationary
solution:

\pi=\Bigg\{\dfrac{d_{i}}{2\absolutevalue{V}}\Bigg\},\quad t\rightarrow\infty,

(25)

independently of the initial distribution
\mathbf{p}_{0}
.
For a
d-
regular graph (all vertices have the same degree
d
) the limiting stationary distribution is
uniform
over the nodes of the graph.

4.4
Quantum Walks

In quantum information theory,
qw
were introduced by Aharonov et al.
Aharonov et al. 1993
as quantum analogues of classical random walks. However, in
crw
the dynamics is purely stochastic at each time step, while
qw
evolve via a deterministic dynamics, and the stochasticity comes out only when a measurement is performed on the quantum state of the walker
Aharonov et al. 1993
;
Kempe 2009
. Moreover,
qw
involve peculiar quantum mechanical properties such as
coherent superposition
,
entanglement
, and
quantum interference
, resulting in a faster spread (ballistic for the quantum case, while diffusive for the classical one).
There exist two different formulations of
qw
: i)
ctqw
and ii)
dtqw
. In the former, the unitary evolution operator can be applied at any time
t
, while in the latter, the operator can be applied only in discrete time steps. Furthermore,
dtqw
need an extra degree of freedom, called “coin”, which stores directions and speeds up the dynamics of the walker
Ambainis et al. 2005
.

4.4.1
Discrete-Time Quantum Walks on Graph

Given a graph
G(V,E)
, let
\mathcal{H}_{V}
be the Hilbert space spanned by the position states
\{\ket{v}:v=1,\dots,\absolutevalue{V}\}
, and let
\mathcal{H}_{A}
be an auxiliary Hilbert space spanned by the coin states
\{\ket{\downarrow},\ket{\uparrow}\}
.
The total Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
associated to a
qw
is obtained by the tensor product between the auxiliary space and the position space:

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{V}.

(26)

In general, a state is written as:

\ket{\psi}=\ket{a}\otimes\ket{v},\quad\ket{a}\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\quad\ket{v}\in\mathcal{H}_{V}.

(27)

The dynamics of the quantum walker is governed by the unitary single time-step operator
\hat{U}
acting on the total Hilbert space:

\hat{U}=\hat{S}\cdot(\hat{C}\otimes\hat{I}),

(28)

where
\hat{I}
is the identity on position space,
\hat{C}
is the coin operator acting on the auxiliary space, and
\hat{S}
is shift operator acting only on position space and moving the walker from state
\ket{v}
to state
\ket{v+1}
, or
\ket{v-1}
. Formally, the shift operator is given by:

\hat{S}=\sum_{v}\outerproduct{\uparrow}{\uparrow}\otimes\outerproduct{v+1}{v}+\outerproduct{\downarrow}{\downarrow}\otimes\outerproduct{v-1}{v}.

(29)

The coin operator
\hat{C}
can be chosen in the family of unitary operators and its choice leads to symmetric (unbiased walk) or asymmetric (biased walk) distributions. A common choices for
\hat{C}
is the Hadamard coin:

\hat{H}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&1\\
1&-1\end{array}\right),

(30)

which leads to an unbiased walk.
The state of a quantum walk after
t
discrete time steps is given by:

\ket{\psi(t)}=\hat{U}^{\otimes t}\ket{\psi(0)}.

(31)

In general,
qw
do not converge to any limiting distribution in contrast to the classical ones:
the limit
\lim_{t\to\infty}\ket{\psi(t)}
does not exist due to the unitary evolution
Aharonov et al. 2001
. Moreover, the interference effects lead a quantum walker to spread
quadratically
faster with respect to its classical counterpart. Namely, in the classical case after
t
time steps the expected distance from the origin is of order
\sigma\sim\sqrt{t}
, while in the quantum case it is of order
\sigma\sim t
, as shown in
Fig.

9

Comparison between the Gaussian probability distribution (violet squares) obtained from a
crw
and probability distribution of a
qw
with Hadamard coin (red circles).
