Irisin promotes intestinal epithelial cell proliferation via Wnt/β-catenin and focal adhesion kinase signaling pathways
Authors: Arong Gaowa, Supasuta Leangpanich, Eun Jeong Park, Eiji Kawamoto, Motomu Shimaoka
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe regeneration of epithelia is crucial for maintaining intestinal homeostasis. Irisin is an exercise-induced hormone originally found to be secreted by skeletal muscles, thereby regulating energy metabolism. Recent studies have revealed that irisin protected against gut inflammation. However, the direct effects of irisin on the intestinal epithelial cells remain to be elucidated. In this study, mouse intestinal organoids were used to assess the effects of irisin on the proliferation of the intestinal epithelial cells. At a concentration of 100 ng/mL irisin significantly increased the growth of the intestinal organoids and upregulated the Wnt/β-catenin and focal adhesion kinase (FAK) signaling pathway genes. Notably, a FAK inhibitor 14 blocked the effects of irisin on the proliferation of the intestinal epithelial cells by inhibiting FAK phosphorylation, as well as the expressions of Wnt target genes. Furthermore, irisin (100 ng/mL) improved the recovery of the intestinal organoids from cellular damages caused by TNF-α, and markedly increased the expression of Wnt target genes in the intestinal epithelial cells. Taken together, irisin activates Wnt/β-catenin and FAK signaling pathways in the intestinal epithelial cells, thereby promoting intestinal epithelial self-renewal under normal homeostatic conditions and intestinal epithelial regeneration upon damages.Keywords: Irisin, Epithelial regeneration, Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, Focal adhesion signaling pathway, Mouse intestinal organoidSubject terms: Cell biology, Drug discovery, Molecular biology, Physiology, Gastroenterology, Medical research, Molecular medicine

Introduction

The intestinal epithelial monolayer plays important roles in the digestion of food, absorption of nutrients, and protection of the body from microbial infections. The intestinal epithelium is one of the most rapidly renewing tissues in the body and the renewal is driven by stem cells at the base of the crypts1. Multiple pathways including Wnt/β-catenin, Notch, and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) are involved in the self-renewal and expansion of stem cells. Among these pathways, Wnt signaling is indispensable for crypt proliferation and the regulation of the intestinal stem cell identity. Key genes such as LGR5, cyclin D1, and c-Myc are well-known downstream targets of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and play a crucial role in the regulation of cell proliferation and cell cycle events in the intestinal epithelial cells2,3. Furthermore, numerous studies have shown that focal adhesion kinase (FAK), which is an integrin-associated protein tyrosine kinase, is also involved in maintaining intestinal homeostasis and promoting mucosal regeneration4,5. A previous study indicated that FAK was essential downstream player of activated Wnt signaling for intestinal regeneration and tumorigenesis6. Generally, after a cellular injury, the intestinal epithelium rapidly undergoes a wound healing process. However, the dysregulated immune response and the extensive crypt distortion or loss of the crypt compartments during inflammation result in the impaired regeneration of damaged intestinal epithelial cells as seen in inflammatory bowel diseases7–9. Therefore, the development of suitable and effective pharmacological therapies that could induce the proliferation of the intestinal epithelial cells is essential for regenerating the damaged intestinal mucosa.

Irisin is a recently identified exercise-responsive myokine, which is produced by the proteolytic cleavage of fibronectin-type III domain-containing 5 (FNDC5) protein10. Irisin is mainly secreted by the skeletal muscles and the adipose tissues. Irisin is kwon to be involved in many of the beneficial effects of exercise, such as enhanced glucose and lipid metabolism11,12, anti-inflammatory responses13,14, tissue protective effects15, and anti-tumor activities16. Furthermore, previous studies revealed that irisin is also involved in cell proliferation. Irisin promoted the proliferation and the differentiation of osteoblast and neural progenitor cells17,18. Recently, it was reported that irisin restored gut barrier function after intestinal ischemia and reperfusion injury by ameliorating oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction19. Mazur-Bialy et al. demonstrated that voluntary exercise could improve the colon injury by the release of irisin and the restoration of plasma adiponectin in an experimental colitis20. Additionally, Narayanan et al. found that exogenic irisin alleviated chronic inflammatory bowel disease by blocking the intestinal inflammatory responses21. On the one hand, many studies have indicated that irisin acts mostly through mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathways11,12,18. On the other hand, increasing evidence suggests that the effects of irisin are also triggered by integrin-mediated FAK signaling pathways19,22,23.

However, the direct effects of irisin on the proliferation of the intestinal epithelial cells and underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated. This study aimed to investigate the effects of irisin on the epithelial cell proliferation and the cell signaling pathways in an intestinal organoid model in vitro.

Results

Irisin promotes the proliferation of intestinal epithelial cells in organoids

To study the effects of irisin on the intestinal organoid growth under physiological conditions, recombinant irisin-Fc protein was added to immature organoids. The growth and viability of the organoids were then monitored for 3 days. Morphological analysis showed that irisin at a concentration of 100 ng/ mL, which is close to the physiological levels in human blood samples reported in most studies10,24, significantly induced the growth of the intestinal organoids, thereby increasing the surface areas and the number of the crypt-like structures showing budding in normal organoids (Fig. 1a and b; Supplementary Fig. S1). Moreover, EdU incorporation experiments showed that 100 ng/mL irisin treatment markedly increased the number of EdU-positive cells, which was also verified with significantly enhanced proliferating cells stained with Ki67 in the crypts (Fig. 1c and d; Supplementary Figs. S2 and S3). In contrast, 50 ng/mL of irisin-treated organoids and the Fc protein-treated control organoids exhibited less expansion and low densities of EdU-positive or Ki67-positive cells in the crypts. Furthermore, qPCR analysis revealed that 100 ng/mL of irisin significantly upregulated the expression levels of stem cell markers (Leucine-rich repeat-containing G protein-coupled Receptor 5: LGR5; and Olfactomedin 4: OLFM4), differentiated cell markers (Paneth cell marker: lysozyme; enterocyte cell marker: CDX2; goblet cell marker: Muc2), Wnt target genes (Achaete-scute homolog 2: ASCL2; and cyclin D1), and a Wnt activator gene (B cell-specific Moloney-MLV integration site 1: Bmi-1) (Fig. 2).

To gain further insight into the dose–response effects of irisin treatment, the organoids were treated with increasing concentrations of irisin (100 ng/mL, 250 ng/mL, and 1000 ng/mL) and analyzed for 3 days. In 250 and 1000 ng/mL irisin-treated organoids, there were no dose–response effects on cell proliferation or gene expression in the epithelial cells. Consistent with our preliminary studies, the effects of irisin-Fc protein on organoid growth and the gene expression levels of intestinal epithelial specific genes peaked at 100 ng/mL (Supplementary Figs. S4 and S5), suggesting that irisin at 100 ng/mL could constitute the optimal dose to effectively promote the proliferation and differentiation of the intestinal epithelial cells in the organoids. Therefore, treatment with 100 ng/mL irisin was considered as the optimal condition for further experiments.

Irisin alleviates TNF-α-induced damage of intestinal epithelial cells in organoids

To further investigate the proliferation effect of irisin under pathological conditions, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) was used to induce an intestinal injury in the organoid model, and the effects of irisin were assessed. As shown in Fig. 3, the number of the buds and the surface areas of the intestinal organoids decreased significantly after TNF-α treatment and more than 40% of the organoids were disrupted (Supplementary Fig. S6). Moreover, both EdU incorporation assay and Ki67 staining assays showed that TNF-α treatment led to a reduction in the number of proliferating cells in the intestinal organoids. However, irisin significantly reduced the percentage of the disrupted organoids, and ameliorated the loss of the organoid buds (Fig. 3a and b). Consistently, irisin effectively enhanced the proliferating cells stained with EdU or Ki67 in the intestinal epithelial cells (Fig. 3c and d; Supplementary Figs. S7 and S8).

Additionally, TNF-α stimulation significantly reduced the gene expression of the Wnt target genes (LGR5, OLFM4, and cyclin D1) and intestinal epithelial specific genes (Muc2, lysozyme, CDX2 and CHGA). On the other hand, irisin significantly suppressed the TNF-α gene expression, and reversed the reduced expression of the Wnt target genes and epithelial specific genes after TNF-α treatment (Fig. 4). These results indicated that irisin exerted anti-inflammatory effects and proliferative effects that promoted the repair of damaged epithelial cells under pathological states. Taken together, our findings strongly suggest that 100 ng/mL irisin could stimulate the cell proliferation and repair the epithelial damage caused by TNF-α in the intestinal organoid model in vitro.

Irisin promotes the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway

To explore the possible mechanisms by which irisin enhances the cellular proliferation of the intestinal epithelial organoids, we examined the effects of irisin on the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which plays a central role in the regulation of the cell proliferation in the intestinal epithelia2,3. We found that the protein levels of total β-catenin and cyclin D1 in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway significantly increased in the 100 ng/mL irisin-treated organoids, but not in the medium-, Fc-protein-, and 50 ng/mL irisin-treated organoids (Fig. 5; Supplementary Fig. S9). These results demonstrated that irisin at 100 ng/mL activated the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and possibly promoted cell proliferation and differentiation in the intestinal epithelial organoids.

Irisin promotes the activation of the focal adhesion signaling pathway

Focal adhesion kinase (FAK) is a cytoplasmic tyrosine kinase that plays critical roles in integrin-mediated cell adhesion and cell growth signaling in the intestinal epithelial cells4,5. It is known that most studies examining FAK activation have focused on rapid phosphorylation events, especially autophosphorylation at tyrosine 397 (FAK-pY397), which is critical for activating signaling pathways4,5. Thus, we focused on the effects of irisin on FAK expression and/or its autophosphorylation. We performed Western blotting on cell lysates and found that FAK-pY397 protein levels were upregulated by irisin treatments, whereas the total levels of FAK protein remained unchanged in all organoid treatment groups (Fig. 6; Supplementary Fig. S10). Next, in order to investigate whether FAK signaling affected the epithelial cell proliferation of the organoids, we tested the effect of a FAK inhibitor 14 on cell proliferation and FAK phosphorylation in the intestinal organoids. Treatment with the FAK inhibitor 14 specifically blocked phosphorylation of FAK at Y397. Consistently, the inhibition of FAK phosphorylation blocked the effects of irisin on organoid growth (Fig. 7; Supplementary Figs. S11 and S12), as well as the gene expressions of cyclin D1 and c-Myc (Supplementary Fig. S13).

We also explored the effects of irisin on the integrin expression in the organoids, since FAK is a key downstream signaling molecule triggered by the integrin activation. Cell surface expression of integrins were determined by flow cytometry analysis using indicated antibodies. We found that β1 integrin was expressed at higher levels in the epithelial cells of the organoids (Supplementary Fig. S14). Expression of the β1 integrin was not significantly altered by the irisin treatment. Previous studies have reported that irisin acts as a ligand for a subset of integrins including αVβ5, αVβ1 and α5β1 and plays an important role in the tissue remodeling19,22,23. Thus, these results suggested that irisin may exert its promoting effects on the intestinal organoid growth partly via β1 integrin -mediated FAK signaling pathway.

Discussion

Since first reported in 2012, irisin has shown various functions in many organs. Its principal function is to regulate glucose and lipid metabolism and mitochondrial function10. Recent studies indicated that both exogenic irisin and exercise-induced endogenic irisin could exert beneficial ameliorating effects on inflammatory bowel diseases and improve the healing of an experimental animal colitis20,21,25,26. However, the direct effects of irisin on the intestinal epithelial cells have yet to be shown. Therefore, in this study, the ability of irisin to induce intestinal epithelial proliferation and its underlying mechanism were evaluated. We found that irisin (100 ng/mL) promoted intestinal organoid growth and upregulated the Wnt target genes under physiological conditions (Figs. 1 and 2). TNF-α plays a crucial role in regulating immunity and as a key mediator of various autoimmune diseases, including inflammatory bowel diseases27,28. Thus, we also established and characterized an in vitro model of intestinal epithelial damage by exposing the intestinal organoids to TNF-α. The results showed that TNF-α exposure decreased the viability of the organoids and reduced the population of Ki67-positive (or EdU-positive) proliferating cells in the crypts. Notably, these pathological phenomena, such as morphological alterations in the loss of the cryptic buds were prominently ameliorated by the treatment with irisin (Fig. 3). Furthermore, irisin completely reversed the TNF-α-induced reduction of proliferation-related genes (LGR5, OLFM4, and cyclin D1) in the intestinal organoids (Fig. 4). Therefore, these data indicate that irisin could promote the proliferation of the intestinal epithelial cells and facilitate the intestinal epithelial cell repair from TNF-α induced damages in the organoids under pathological states.

Huh et al. first identified circulating irisin using ELISA and reported a serum level of 113.1 ± 20.6 ng/mL among a population of typically healthy women after exercise training29. Similarly, Moreno et al. measured circulating irisin levels in the blood of healthy subjects after exercise, thereby reporting hundreds of ng/mL30. Afterward, many animal experiments and clinical studies have provided evidence regarding the association between circulating irisin levels and health status. Irisin levels were reported to be decreased in patients with cardiovascular disease, hypertension, and chronic kidney disease31–33. Moreover, circulating irisin levels were found to be lower in dialysis patients than in non-dialysis patients32,33. A previous study reported that irisin levels in the serum and intestine are downregulated during the intestinal ischemia–reperfusion19. However, Mazur-Bialy et al. showed no difference in the serum levels of irisin between mice with colitis and normal controls20. Despite several reports on the efficacy of irisin in treating intestinal inflammation in vitro and in vivo studies19–21,25,26,34, there are still no reports on changes in blood irisin levels in patients with intestinal inflammation. Previous studies indicate that regular exercise training promotes tissue regeneration, including neural regeneration, osteoblast and muscle regeneration17,18. Our study is the first to report that irisin at 100 ng/mL, which is close to the physiological levels in human blood samples, significantly induced the proliferation of the intestinal epithelial cells.

Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity is the major driving force for the intestinal stem cell renewal and the epithelial differentiation2,3. In our study, qPCR analysis of the intestinal organoid samples showed that irisin increased the expression levels of several Wnt target genes, including LGR5, ASCL2, cyclin D1, and c-Myc (Fig. 2). Importantly, Western blot analysis of the intestinal organoid samples showed that β-catenin stabilization in the intestinal epithelial cells upon irisin treatment (Fig. 5). According to these results, it is thought that irisin promoted intestinal stem cell proliferation and differentiation via activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. These results are consistent with previous reports that the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway maintains stemness and enhances the proliferation of the intestinal epithelial cells2,3.

As a component of the integrin-mediated signaling axis, FAK regulates intestinal epithelial cell survival and proliferation during mucosal wound healing4,5. The organoid growth experiments and Western blot analysis revealed that the FAK inhibitor 14 significantly suppressed the intestinal epithelial cell proliferation by inhibiting p-FAK expression and decreasing cyclin D1 and c-Myc genes (Fig. 7). This suggests that the potential effects of irisin on the intestinal epithelial cells occurs partly via the integrin/FAK-dependent pathway. Integrin β1 is highly expressed in the intestinal epithelial organoids in our study. Indeed, it is possible that irisin can increase FAK activation by acting as a ligand for integrin β1, as previously reported19,22,23.

We also performed a preliminary study to examine the therapeutic effect of irisin on the regeneration of the inflamed epithelial cells in mice with dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis. Mice were treated with 2% DSS solution for 5 days followed by normal drinking water for 7 days. On day 5, mice were injected daily with irisin (1 mg/kg) for 5 days. Control mice were injected with saline. The results showed that irisin slightly ameliorated DSS-induced colitis in mice (Supplementary Fig. S15). During the study, four mice died (three in the DSS + Saline group and one in the DSS + irisin group). As in our previous study35, the death of the mice in the present study was caused by severe diarrhea, severe blood in the stool, dehydration, and continuous weight loss. This result suggest that irisin may extend the median survival of mice. However, as the sample size of our preliminary study is small, we need to conduct experiments on large samples in the future for further studies.

This study has two major limitations. First, this study mainly evaluated the potential effects of exogenous irisin using an in vitro mouse intestinal organoid model, additional animal studies and human intestinal organoid experiments are needed to confirm these findings. Second, as we used only exogenous irisin, the effects of exercise-induced endogenous irisin efficacy are also important to study.

In conclusion, irisin exerts potential effects on the Wnt/β-catenin and FAK signaling pathways in the intestinal epithelial cells, thereby promoting capacity of intestinal epithelial cells to self-renew under normal homeostatic conditions and to regenerate upon damages.

Materials and methods

Materials

Irisin-Fc protein was recombinantly expressed as a Fc-fusion protein in the human embryonic kidney cell line 293 T (HEK293T, ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and purified using the Amicon® Pro purification system (Millipore-Japan, Tokyo, Japan) as previously described36. IntestiCult™ Organoid Growth Medium (Mouse; Catalog #06,005), Gentle Cell Dissociation Reagent (Catalog #07,174), and Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline (DPBS; Catalog #37,350) were purchased from STEMCELL Technologies (Vancouver, BC, Canada). Matrigel (Catalog #356231) was obtained from Corning Inc. (Corning, NY, USA). Anti-rabbit FAK antibody (Catalog #3285), phospho-β-catenin (Ser33/37/Thr41) antibody (Catalog #9561), anti-phospho-FAK (Y397) antibody (Catalog #3283), anti-mouse and anti-rabbit IgG HRP antibodies (Catalog #7076 and #7074) were all purchased from Cell signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). Recombinant mouse TNF-alpha (aa 80–235) protein (Catalog #410-MT) and anti-h/m/r beta-catenin antibody (Catalog #3285) were obtained from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN, USA). Anti-cyclin D1 mouse monoclonal antibody (A-12) (Catalog #SC-8396) was purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, TX, USA). FAK-inhibitor 14 (Catalog #SML0837) and anti-beta-actin monoclonal antibody (A-5316) were purchased from Sigma (Saint Louis, MO, USA). EdU Staining Proliferation Kit (iFluor 488) (ab219801) and recombinant rabbit anti-Ki67 antibody (SP6) (ab16667) were obtained from Abcam (Tokyo, Japan).

Mouse intestinal crypt isolation and organoid culture

Seven-week-old female C57BL/6 mice were purchased from SLC Japan (Shizuoka, Japan). The mice were maintained in the Mie University Experimental Animal Facility under specific pathogen-free conditions with ad libitum access to water and food and with a standard 12-h light–dark cycle. Mice were allowed to acclimatize for at least one week before being used for experiments. The mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation under anesthesia with isoflurane, and small intestinal tissue were harvested. Crypt isolation and culture were performed as previously described35,37. We only used freshly isolated primary tissue organoids for all experiments. To prevent bacterial contamination, 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, MA, USA) were added to organoid growth medium. The culture medium was changed every 3 days.

Under physiological conditions, intestinal organoids were co-cultured with increasing concentrations of irisin (50 ng/mL, 100 ng/mL, 250 ng/mL, and 1000 ng/mL) or Fc protein in organoid growth medium for 3 days. To further evaluate the potent effect of irisin on intestinal epithelial barrier damage, we established a TNF-α-stimulated intestinal organoid model as described previously38. The intestinal organoids were pretreated with recombinant mouse TNF-α (60 ng/mL) for 12 h to induce intestinal epithelial damage and then treated with irisin (100 ng/mL) for 24 h. The growth of the crypts was monitored continuously under the microscope, and the expression of target genes were analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR, Western blotting and immunofluorescent staining. All animal studies were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. The animal study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

5-Ethynyl-20-deoxyuridine (EdU) cell proliferation assay

Organoids were harvested from 24-well culture plate and transferred to 4-well cell culture chambers for 24 h. The organoids were incubated in an intestinal organoid growth medium containing 10 µM EdU for 4 h, followed by incubation in an organoid growth medium with 10 µg/mL of Hoechst 33,342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) for 15 min prior to fixation with 4% cold paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Incorporation of EdU into DNA was detected using a Click-iT EdU Alexa Fluor 488 kit, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The proportion of EdU positive cells was measured as the number of green-stained (proliferating) cells relative to the total number of nuclei (blue).

Immunofluorescence staining

Organoids were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma), then blocked with 1% BSA for 30 min. Sections were incubated with monoclonal anti-EpCAM-antibody and recombinant rabbit anti-Ki67 antibody at 4 °C for overnight, followed by the corresponding secondary fluorescence-conjugated antibodies for 1 h. The sections were further incubated with Hoechst 33,342 for 15 min and mounted in a mounting medium and then visualized under a confocal laser imaging microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Qualitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis

RNA expression was detected by qPCR analysis as described previously35,37. Total RNA was extracted from organoids using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and reverse transcription was conducted with a Prime Script RT reagent Kit (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). Quantitative RT-PCR was performed with PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix using a StepOne Plus Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Relative fold changes in gene expression were calculated using the comparative 2−ΔΔCT method and normalized to the expression levels of the corresponding housekeeping gene (GAPDH).

Western blot analysis

Protein expression was detected by western blot analysis as described previously36,37. First, organoids were harvested from Matrigel and lysed with ULTRA RIPA Lysis Buffer containing a protease-inhibitor cocktail and a phosphatase-inhibitor cocktail for 10 min on ice, followed by 10,000 × g centrifugation for 5 min. Protein concentrations of samples were determined by their absorbance at 280 nm. The protein samples were mixed with sample buffer and boiled for 3 min at 95 °C. The proteins were separated on 7.5% SDS-PAGE gel, and transferred to PVDF membranes (Immun-Blot™, Bio-Rad). The membranes were then blocked with 4% (w/v) nonfat dry milk powder or 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) powder in 1 × Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween ® 20 detergent (TBST) buffer (Santacruz Biotechnology, Texas, USA). The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight and washed three times with 1xTBST buffer. Membranes were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h, followed by a second washing with 1 × TBST buffer. The proteins were then visualized using the enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection reagent (Waka, Osaka, Japan). Protein images were taken with the ImageQuant LAS 4000 mini and AMERSHAM ImageQuant 800 (GE Healthcare, Tokyo, Japan). The intensity of each band was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, USA).

Flow cytometry analysis

Organoids were harvested from Matrigel and washed once with cold D-PBS, then incubated with fluorescently labeled antibodies at 4 °C for 1 h. The samples were washed twice with PBS buffer containing 2% FBS and 2 mM EDTA (Wako, Osaka, Japan), and then analyzed by using BD Accuri C6 flow cytometer and software (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA).

Monoclonal antibodies to mouse integrins were purchased as follows: β1 (HMb1-1) (Biolegend), β3 (HMb3-1 ) (Biolegend), β5 (KN52) (Thermo Fisher Scientific), β7 (FIB27) (Biolgened), α1 (HMa1) (Biolegend), α2 (P1H5) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), α4 (9C10) (Biolegend), α5 (MFR5) (Biolegend), α6 (BQ16) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), αL (2D7) (BD Biosciences), αV (RMV-7) (Biolegend). Isotype-matched control antibodies were also obtained as follows: mouse IgG1 (MOPC-21, Biolegend), mouse IgG2a (MOPC-173) (Biolegend), mouse IgG2b (MPC-11) (Biolegend), Armenian hamster IgG (HTK888) (Biolegend), and rat IgG1 (RTK2071) (Biolegend).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 6.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). All values are expressed as the means ± SD. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s test was used to determine whether the differences among multiple groups were significant. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.
