Acute fibrinous and organising pneumonia (AFOP): a case series highlighting diverse aetiologies and clinical outcomes in three patients
Authors: Shakeel Ahmed, Farhana Shora, Mousa Hussein, Mansoor Hameed, Irfan Ul Haq, Tasleem Raza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAcute fibrinous and organising pneumonia (AFOP) is a rare histopathological pattern of acute lung injury characterised by intra-alveolar fibrin deposition and organising pneumonia. It has diverse aetiologies and highly variable clinical outcomes.Case presentationsWe report three cases of AFOP from Qatar. Case 1: A 71-year-old man developed AFOP following influenza A (H1N1) infection and achieved complete clinical and radiological resolution with corticosteroid therapy. Case 2: A 52-year-old man with anti-MDA5-positive clinically amyopathic dermatomyositis developed rapidly progressive AFOP and died despite aggressive multi-modal immunosuppression. Case 3: A 51-year-old woman presented with AFOP in the setting of Staphylococcus aureus bacteraemia, which unmasked underlying chronic myeloid leukaemia; she achieved full radiological resolution with corticosteroids.ConclusionsThis case series highlights the wide clinicopathologic spectrum of AFOP, the critical importance of early histological diagnosis, identification of underlying triggers, and the highly variable prognosis depending on aetiology. Early lung biopsy and tailored therapy remain essential in the management of this uncommon condition.

Introduction

Acute fibrinous and organising pneumonia (AFOP) is a rare histopathological pattern of acute lung injury first described by Beasley et al. [1]. According to the ATS/ERS international multidisciplinary classification of the idiopathic interstitial pneumonias (2013 statement, with 2025 update), AFOP is recognised as a rare histologic pattern of acute/subacute lung injury, often categorised under rare or unclassifiable patterns, distinct from diffuse alveolar damage (DAD) [2]. It is characterised by intra-alveolar fibrin deposition forming ‘fibrin balls’ and organising pneumonia, typically patchy and lower-lobe predominant [1,2]. Unlike DAD, AFOP lacks hyaline membranes [1].

AFOP has been associated with diverse aetiologies including infections (particularly viral), autoimmune diseases, drug reactions, occupational exposures, haematological malignancies, and idiopathic cases [1–4]. The condition predominantly affects individuals between 50 and 70 years of age with a slight male predominance [2,3]. Clinical presentation varies from subacute respiratory symptoms to fulminant respiratory failure, with mortality rates ranging from 30% to over 80% depending on the underlying aetiology and disease pattern [1,4].

Diagnosis requires histopathological confirmation via lung biopsy, as clinical and radiological features are non-specific and often mimic community-acquired pneumonia or other interstitial lung diseases [5]. Treatment typically involves corticosteroids, with variable response rates depending on the clinical context and disease severity [1,4].

We present three cases of AFOP with distinct aetiologies – post-viral H1N1 infection, anti-MDA5-positive CADM, and CML-associated disease – highlighting the diagnostic challenges, therapeutic approaches, and prognostic variability of this uncommon condition.

Case presentations

Case 1: post-influenza a (H1N1) acute fibrinous and organising pneumonia

A 71-year-old Indian male with a medical history of type 2 diabetes mellitus, stable coronary artery disease, and former smoking presented with a 10-day history of fever, productive cough with greenish sputum, and progressive dyspnoea. These symptoms were preceded by one week of upper respiratory tract symptoms including rhinorrhoea and sore throat. He also reported generalised weakness, anorexia, and unintentional weight loss of approximately 6 kilograms.

On admission, vital signs revealed a temperature of 37.8°C and oxygen saturation of 88% on room air, which improved to 94% with 2 L/min supplemental oxygen via nasal cannula. Physical examination demonstrated bilateral diffuse coarse crackles on chest auscultation. There was no cyanosis, digital clubbing, lymphadenopathy, pedal oedema, or cardiovascular abnormalities.

Laboratory investigations showed marked elevation of inflammatory markers: C-reactive protein 267 mg/L (normal less than 10 mg/L), procalcitonin 3.4 ng/mL (normal less than 0.5 ng/mL), and pro-brain natriuretic peptide 3,737 pg/mL. Complete blood count revealed thrombocytopenia with a platelet count of 75 × 103/μL, while white blood cell count was within normal limits. Chest radiography demonstrated bilateral upper and mid-zone infiltrates. (Figure 1(C)) Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) confirmed influenza A (H1N1) infection. Additional microbiological workup, including tuberculosis cultures, fungal cultures, and bacterial cultures, was negative. Transthoracic echocardiography showed normal cardiac function, effectively excluding significant cardiac contribution to the respiratory failure. Patient was initially managed as viral pneumonia with possible bacterial superinfection and received intravenous ceftriaxone 2 g daily, azithromycin 500 mg daily, and oseltamivir 75 mg twice daily. Despite partial improvement in oxygenation over the following week, he continued to require supplemental oxygen with no significant reduction in inflammatory markers. High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the chest revealed diffuse bilateral infiltrates with a ‘crazy paving’ pattern characterised by ground-glass opacities with superimposed interlobular septal thickening (Figure 1(A,B)). Given the lack of clinical improvement and atypical radiological findings, bronchoscopy with transbronchial lung biopsy (TBLB) was performed. Bronchoalveolar lavage demonstrated neutrophilic predominance with 70 % with negative cultures. The patient continued intravenous antibiotics for a total of 7–10 days alongside oseltamivir.

Histopathological examination revealed intra-alveolar fibrin deposition with organising pneumonia, consistent with AFOP. Autoimmune serological screening including antinuclear antibody (ANA), anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA), and rheumatoid factor was negative, supporting a post-viral aetiology.

Oral prednisolone was initiated at 1 mg/kg/day (60 mg daily) along with calcium and vitamin D supplementation and proton pump inhibitor prophylaxis. The patient exhibited marked clinical improvement within one week, including resolution of dyspnoea and successful weaning from supplemental oxygen. Corticosteroids were tapered gradually: reduced to 40 mg daily after two weeks and continued for four weeks, then further tapered by 5–10 mg every two weeks. Complete clinical and radiological resolution was achieved by four months, (Figure 1(D)) and corticosteroids were discontinued after five months of therapy. The patient remained asymptomatic with no recurrence at 12-month follow-up. No baseline pulmonary function tests (PFTs) were performed due to acute presentation and later during follow up.

Case 2: anti-MDA5-positive clinically amyopathic dermatomyositis with AFOP

A 52-year-old Filipino male with no significant past medical history and no smoking history presented with a one-month history of generalised vasculitic rash, polyarthralgia, oral ulcers, and unintentional weight loss. Physical examination revealed severe proximal muscle weakness, diffuse scaling over the face and scalp, and patches of alopecia, raising suspicion for an underlying autoimmune disorder.

Laboratory investigations demonstrated markedly elevated liver transaminases (alanine aminotransferase 286 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase 362 U/L), proteinuria, and creatine kinase level of 2,269 U/L. Autoimmune serological evaluation was notable for positive antinuclear antibody (ANA), anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene 5 (anti-MDA5) antibody, and anti-Ro antibody. Other myositis-specific and myositis-associated antibodies were negative. Computed tomography of the chest and abdomen ruled out occult malignancy. Renal biopsy findings were inconclusive. Whole-body magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated patchy signal intensity in proximal muscle groups, and electromyography confirmed generalised myopathy. Transthoracic echocardiography was performed on admission to exclude cardiac causes and was normal. No baseline PFTs due to rapid progression; no follow-up possible due to fatal outcome. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of anti-MDA5-positive clinically amyopathic dermatomyositis (CADM) was established.

The patient was treated with pulse methylprednisolone 1 g intravenously daily for three days and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) 2 g/kg over five days, followed by high-dose oral prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day. Initial clinical improvement was noted; however, his condition deteriorated within two weeks with the onset of progressive dyspnoea, pleuritic chest pain, high-grade fevers, hypotension, and oxygen desaturation. Chest radiography revealed diffuse bilateral infiltrates. The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit for management of septic shock, requiring mechanical ventilation and vasopressor support. Repeated blood cultures, sputum cultures, viral PCR panel, and Pneumocystis jirovecii staining were all negative.

Subsequent HRCT demonstrated new diffuse ground-glass opacities and patchy consolidation predominantly in bilateral lower lobes. (Figure 2(A,B)). Flexible bronchoscopy revealed neutrophilic infiltrates on bronchoalveolar lavage and excluded alveolar haemorrhage. Microbiological studies including bacterial, fungal, and mycobacterial cultures remained negative. Given the clinical deterioration despite broad-spectrum antimicrobial coverage, an open lung biopsy via video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) was performed, which confirmed acute fibrinous and organising pneumonia (AFOP) with features suggestive of diffuse alveolar damage but distinct from the typical DAD pattern associated with anti-MDA5 dermatomyositis. Despite aggressive multi-modal immunosuppressive therapy including additional pulse methylprednisolone, a second course of IVIG, cyclophosphamide 750 mg/m2 intravenously, rituximab 1 g intravenously, and mycophenolate mofetil 1.5 g twice daily, the patient’s respiratory status continued to deteriorate. He developed refractory hypoxaemia and multiorgan failure, ultimately succumbing to the illness three weeks after ICU admission.

Case 3: chronic myeloid leukaemia-associated AFOP

A 51-year-old Arab-Canadian female with a complex medical history including hypertension, severe COVID-19 pneumonia in 2021 requiring intubation complicated by post-intubation tracheal stenosis (managed with multiple balloon dilatations and tracheostomy), cardiomyopathy, and prior pulmonary embolism (anticoagulation completed) presented with a 10-day history of right-hand dorsum pain, erythema, swelling, fever, cough, and back pain.

On admission, vital signs showed temperature 38.1°C, haemodynamic stability, and oxygen saturation 98% on 2 L/min supplemental oxygen. Physical examination confirmed right-hand cellulitis with surrounding erythema and fluctuance. Laboratory investigations revealed marked leucocytosis (37 × 103/μL with neutrophil predominance) and elevated C-reactive protein (257 mg/L). Renal and hepatic function were normal.

Chest radiography demonstrated bilateral infiltrates, multiple nodules, and a small right-sided pleural effusion. Ultrasound confirmed a loculated right pleural effusion with septations. HRCT of the chest revealed multiple bilateral well-defined pulmonary nodules, one with cavitation in the left lower lobe, along with sub-centimetre mediastinal lymph nodes and a right-sided pleural effusion with adjacent atelectasis. Blood cultures grew methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus. The presumed source was identified as a hand abscess, which underwent incision and drainage. Transoesophageal echocardiography showed no valvular vegetations or endocarditis. The patient was initially treated with intravenous piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g every 8 hours for presumed cellulitis and community-acquired pneumonia, later switched to intravenous cloxacillin 2 g every 6 hours following positive blood cultures. Despite clinical improvement and normalisation of inflammatory markers, concern for metastatic disease persisted.

A CT-guided core needle biopsy of a pulmonary nodule was performed, which unexpectedly revealed acute fibrinous and organising pneumonia (AFOP) with no evidence of malignancy. Antibiotics were discontinued after completion of a 14-day course, and oral prednisolone 40 mg daily was initiated. Follow-up HRCT at one month demonstrated complete resolution of pulmonary nodules. (Figure 3(B)). The patient was clinically well, and mycophenolate mofetil 500 mg twice daily was added for maintenance immunosuppression with gradual steroid taper.

Several weeks later, the patient presented with tracheostomy-site cellulitis and was noted to have marked thrombocytosis (1,014 × 103/μL). Haematology consultation was obtained, and bone marrow aspiration and biopsy confirmed chronic-phase chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML) with BCR-ABL1 fusion gene. Retrospective review of laboratory records revealed persistent moderate thrombocytosis (450–650 × 103/μL) over the preceding six months, which had been attributed to recurrent infections. The patient was started on nilotinib 300 mg twice daily, chosen due to drug-drug interactions between other tyrosine kinase inhibitors and mycophenolate mofetil. The patient continues on maintenance immunosuppression and tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy with stable disease at six-month follow-up.

Discussion

This case series highlights the clinical heterogeneity, diagnostic challenges, and variable outcomes of acute fibrinous and organising pneumonia (AFOP) across three distinct clinical contexts: post-viral infection, autoimmune disease, and haematological malignancy. AFOP remains an underrecognised histopathological entity that requires high clinical suspicion and tissue diagnosis for accurate identification [1,2].

Clinical and epidemiological considerations

AFOP represents an unusual form of acute lung injury characterised by intra-alveolar fibrin deposition forming distinctive ‘fibrin balls’ and organising pneumonia, typically in a patchy distribution with lower lobe predominance [1,2]. The condition most commonly affects individuals between 50 and 70 years of age with a male predominance [2,3]. The ages of our patients (51, 52, and 71 years) align with this reported epidemiology.

The aetiopathogenesis of AFOP remains incompletely understood. It has been associated with collagen vascular diseases [4–6], drug reactions, occupational or environmental exposures, infections [1,3,7], and haematological malignancies [4,8–10]. Several cases have been described as idiopathic when no underlying trigger is identified [3,4].

Post-viral H1N1-associated AFOP

Our first case represents a rare presentation of AFOP following influenza A (H1N1) infection in an immunocompetent patient. Through a comprehensive literature search of PubMed over the past 20 years, we identified only two other case series reporting H1N1-associated AFOP: Beasley et al. [1] and Gomes et al. [8]. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of H1N1-related AFOP in an immunocompetent patient outside of the transplant setting in the past two decades.

H1N1 influenza can trigger AFOP through direct viral-mediated damage to lung epithelial and endothelial cells, leading to increased vascular permeability and fibrin leakage into alveolar spaces [7]. The infection induces a robust immune response with proinflammatory cytokine release (cytokine storm), which amplifies inflammation and tissue injury. Unlike typical diffuse alveolar damage (DAD), AFOP features deposition of fibrin ‘balls’ without hyaline membrane formation, coupled with fibroblast proliferation characteristic of organising pneumonia [1]. This pathological process disrupts normal alveolar-capillary gas exchange, potentially resulting in severe respiratory failure.

Comparative analysis with published literature reveals important differences in outcomes based on immune status. Otto et al. reported a 67% mortality rate in post-lung transplant patients with H1N1-associated AFOP [7], whereas our immunocompetent patient demonstrated excellent response to corticosteroid therapy with complete resolution as described in Table 1. Our case illustrates a subacute presentation of AFOP following H1N1 infection, which often mimics community-acquired pneumonia and may show delayed response to antiviral and antibiotic therapy. The ‘crazy paving’ pattern on HRCT, while non-specific, should prompt consideration of AFOP in the appropriate clinical context. Histopathological confirmation via lung biopsy remains essential for accurate diagnosis.

Anti-MDA5-positive CADM-associated AFOP

Our second case represents a unique and severe presentation of AFOP in the context of anti-MDA5-positive clinically amyopathic dermatomyositis (CADM). Interstitial lung disease (ILD) is a common and often early manifestation of polymyositis/dermatomyositis (PM/DM), which may occur even without respiratory symptoms [11,12]. In CADM, anti-MDA5 antibodies are strongly associated with rapidly progressive ILD (RP-ILD), characterised by acute onset, severe respiratory failure, and high mortality despite intensive immunosuppression [11,12].

High-resolution computed tomography in MDA5-positive CADM-ILD typically demonstrates ground-glass opacities, consolidation, reticulations, and traction bronchiectasis, with consolidation predominating in acute or subacute presentations [13]. Histologically, CADM-ILD is most commonly characterised by nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP), usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP), diffuse alveolar damage (DAD), or organising pneumonia; AFOP as a histological pattern is exceedingly rare [14,15]. AFOP in anti-MDA5-positive CADM-ILD is exceptionally rare and associated with mortality rates exceeding 80% in rapidly progressive cases [11,12]. While most CADM-ILD cases exhibit NSIP or DAD histopathology, our case identifies AFOP as a rare and aggressive histological variant. Despite combined immunosuppressive therapy including corticosteroids, IVIG, cyclophosphamide, rituximab, and mycophenolate mofetil, the disease progressed to fatal respiratory failure.

No consensus exists on optimal treatment for anti-MDA5-positive RP-ILD, though combined immunosuppressive regimens initiated early in the disease course may offer the best chance for stabilisation [13]. Our case underscores the fulminant nature of AFOP in this context and the need for early palliative care discussions when appropriate.

Haematological malignancy-associated AFOP

Our third case demonstrates AFOP in the setting of chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML), presenting with a subacute clinical course initially mimicking septic pulmonary emboli secondary to Staphylococcus aureus bacteraemia. The patient exhibited symptoms resembling community-acquired pneumonia, and HRCT revealed bilateral nodular infiltrates with cavitation, raising initial concern for metastatic disease or septic emboli.

AFOP in haematological malignancies such as CML and acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) has been associated with drug-induced lung injury, opportunistic infections in immunocompromised hosts, and as a paraneoplastic phenomenon [4,8–10]. The condition presents in two distinct forms: an acute, rapidly progressive variant causing respiratory failure and multiorgan dysfunction (average time to diagnosis approximately 29 days), and a subacute, more indolent form [1,8].

Imaging findings in AFOP are non-specific. Acute AFOP resembles diffuse alveolar damage with diffuse bibasilar consolidation and ground-glass opacities, while subacute AFOP mimics organising pneumonia with focal or diffuse parenchymal opacities [1,8]. Pleural effusions may be present. Definitive diagnosis requires lung biopsy, typically via surgical methods (open biopsy or VATS), though CT-guided core needle biopsy or transbronchial lung biopsy may occasionally be diagnostic [5,8]. Bronchoalveolar lavage is generally non-diagnostic but useful for excluding infection and haemorrhage.

Gomes et al. described 13 cases of AFOP in patients with haematological malignancies, reporting a 38% mortality rate, primarily linked to opportunistic infections and disease progression [8]. AFOP carries a high overall mortality rate (30–50%), influenced by the disease pattern (acute vs. subacute) and underlying aetiology [1,8]. Corticosteroids are the mainstay of treatment, with additional immunosuppressants such as cyclophosphamide or mycophenolate mofetil employed in severe or steroid-refractory cases [1,4].

Our case represents a subacute presentation diagnosed by CT-guided biopsy, which responded favourably to corticosteroid therapy with complete radiological resolution. The subsequent diagnosis of CML was incidental but highlights the importance of comprehensive haematological evaluation in patients with unexplained thrombocytosis or leucocytosis.

Key diagnostic and therapeutic insights

Several important lessons emerge from this case series:

AFOP is frequently misdiagnosed as community-acquired pneumonia or septic emboli, leading to diagnostic delay.

Early lung biopsy (transbronchial, CT-guided, or VATS) is essential for definitive diagnosis [5,8].

Histological features may overlap with diffuse alveolar damage, requiring experienced pulmonary pathology review.

Prognosis is highly variable: subacute post-infectious and malignancy-associated cases generally respond well to corticosteroids, whereas anti-MDA5-associated AFOP carries a poor prognosis.

Comprehensive evaluation for underlying triggers and multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussion are crucial for optimal management.

Early palliative care discussions are appropriate in rapidly progressive cases.

Need for Early Aggressive Therapy in Appropriate Contexts: In rapidly progressive cases, particularly those associated with autoimmune diseases, early aggressive immunosuppression may be warranted despite variable efficacy [13]. However, the poor outcomes in fulminant cases underscore the importance of early palliative care discussions and realistic goal-setting with patients and families.

Multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussions were held for all cases involving pulmonologists, rheumatologists, haematologists, and radiologists, in line with current ILD guidelines emphasising MDT as the cornerstone of complex ILD management.

Limitations

This case series has several limitations. The small sample size (n = 3) limits generalisability of findings. The retrospective nature of case reporting introduces potential selection bias. Long-term follow-up data are limited, particularly for Cases 2 and 3. Additionally, the lack of standardised treatment protocols across cases makes it difficult to draw definitive conclusions about optimal therapeutic approaches.

Conclusions

Acute fibrinous and organising pneumonia (AFOP) is a rare and clinicopathologically underdiagnosed disease with variable presentation and prognosis. This case series expands AFOP’s clinicopathologic spectrum by documenting three distinct aetiologies: post-viral H1N1 infection in an immunocompetent patient (a rare presentation), anti-MDA5-positive CADM with fulminant respiratory failure, and CML-associated disease with complete recovery.

Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion in atypical pneumonia unresponsive to antimicrobials, particularly with suggestive HRCT patterns. Early histological diagnosis, trigger identification, and MDT-guided management are critical. While corticosteroids remain the mainstay, outcomes vary widely by aetiology. Multicentre collaborations and registry studies are needed to refine diagnostic criteria, identify prognostic factors, and establish evidence-based protocols. Further research is required to elucidate AFOP pathogenesis and develop targeted therapies.
