Berberine alleviates radiation-induced intestinal injury by inhibiting cellular senescence
Authors: Xiaofeng Niu, Zheng Ren, Lijuan Deng, Siyu Yang, Jingyan Gao, Chengliu Bi, Ruolan Wang, Shaomei Sun, Ao Chen, Qinqing Li, Jun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe aim was to study the inhibitory effect of berberine (BBR) on intestinal senescence induced by radiation and explore its mechanism.MethodsMale C57BL/6J mice and SD rats were randomly divided into three groups: Control, irradiation (IR), and IR + BBR. Except for the normal control group, which received sham irradiation, the other two groups were subjected to abdominal X-ray irradiation (12 Gy for mice and 15 Gy for rats). Mesenteric blood flow was detected using laser speckle blood flow contrast imaging. Senescence markers were evaluated using RT-qPCR, Western blot analysis, and SA-β-Gal staining. 16S rRNA sequencing technology was used to study the changes in the intestinal microbiota. MRI was used to evaluate the efficacy of BBR on intestinal injury.ResultsOur results showed that BBR can alleviate radiation-induced DNA damage and senescence in mouse intestines, mitigate both acute and chronic radiation-induced intestinal damage, and is accompanied by an improvement in intestinal flora imbalance. In addition, BBR enhances the survival and proliferative capacity of irradiated intestinal epithelial cells, inhibits radiation-induced cellular senescence and SASP in vitro, and prevents cells from entering senescence. The underlying mechanism may involve the activation of the Nrf2-related pathway.ConclusionOur findings suggest that BBR can effectively alleviate the intestinal cellular senescence caused by radiation, concomitant with the remodeling of gut microbiota, which provides potential implications for using BBR as a drug against radiation-induced intestinal injury.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13014-026-02851-8.

Introduction

Radiotherapy is a common treatment for malignant tumors, which can alleviate the tumor load and prolong the survival time of patients. However, while radiation kills cancer cells, it can also damage the normal tissues around the tumor, causing serious adverse reactions [1]. Radiation-induced intestinal injury (RIII) occurs during radiotherapy for abdominal and pelvic malignancies, and is an important limiting factor of radiotherapy dose in clinic [2, 3]. Although three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy and intensity-modulated radiotherapy can prevent the healthy organs near the tumor from being irradiated to some extent, the high radiation sensitivity of the intestine usually leads to radiation toxicity [4, 5], seriously affecting the subsequent quality of life of patients. Around the world, about 300,000 patients receive pelvic and abdominal radiotherapy every year to treat cancer [6]. It is reported that 60–80% of patients with abdominal or pelvic tumors receiving radiotherapy will have early symptoms of intestinal toxicity, such as nausea, abdominal pain, diarrhea, and fatigue [7]. At present, there is no effective intestinal radioprotective agent in the clinic. Therefore, it is urgent to find potential treatment schemes to reduce intestinal damage caused by radiation, which has important research significance and clinical value for improving the prognosis of patients with abdominal and pelvic tumors undergoing radiotherapy.

Ionizing radiation is a primary driver of DNA damage, which subsequently triggers the onset of cellular senescence. The accumulation of DNA damage and the reduction of DNA repair efficiency after radiation will eventually lead to the continuous increase of senescent cells, which will lead to the accumulation of senescent cells when they exceed the immune clearance capacity or senescent cells cannot be effectively cleared [8, 9]. Senescent cells secrete numerous proteins and various cytokines, which is senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). It can induce tissue dysfunction and deterioration in a paracrine manner [10]. Studies have shown that radiation-induced cellular senescence plays an important role in the occurrence and development of RIII [11, 12], which leads to the impairment of intestinal endothelial cell function and intestinal ecological imbalance [13, 14]. Therefore, targeting radiation-induced intestinal senescence may be a potential therapeutic approach that promises to stop radioactive intestinal damage from entering an irreversible fibrotic phase, thus addressing the current clinical challenge of limited symptom relief.

Berberine (BBR) is a kind of isoquinoline alkaloid extracted from plants, which is common in the plant kingdom and is the main active ingredient in Coptis [15]. It has a variety of pharmacological effects, such as antiviral, anti-inflammatory, anti-diabetic, and cardioprotective activities [16–19]. Previous studies found that BBR plays a crucial role in resisting retinal pigment epithelial aging and cardiac aging [20, 21]. BBR can also extend the lifespan and restore the health of doxorubicin-induced aged mice by protecting against DNA damage and downregulating the expression of cell cycle proteins [22]. Similarly, BBR has been regarded as an important nutrient for the treatment of gastrointestinal diseases. BBR treatment can delay mortality in mice after total abdominal irradiation and alleviate intestinal injury by reducing the levels of tumor necrosis factor-α, diamine oxidase, and intestinal fatty acid-binding protein in plasma, as well as decreasing intestinal apoptosis [23]. In both healthy and irradiated mice, BBR promotes the proliferation and differentiation of intestinal stem cells (ISCs) and enhances their function, suggesting its potential value in the treatment of RIII [24]. These findings highlight the enormous potential of BBR in addressing these complex health challenges.

As mentioned above, BBR has been demonstrated to alleviate RIII and to resist aging and aging-related diseases. However, the effect of BBR on radiation-induced intestinal senescence has not been reported to date. In this study, we will investigate the therapeutic effect of BBR on radiation-induced intestinal senescence in mice. On this basis, we further studied the anti-radiation aging effect of BBR on intestinal cells and its potential mechanism in vitro (Fig. 1). These results will provide theoretical support for the treatment of intestinal injury induced by ionizing radiation.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

The IEC-6 cells were purchased from Kunming Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, and cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FQ), 1% streptomycin/penicillin (HyClone) and 10ug/ml insulin (Pronoxair). The cells were cultured in a cell incubator containing 5% carbon dioxide at 37 °C, and the medium was changed every 2 days to ensure that the cells had sufficient nutrients. IEC-6 cells were exposed to a biological X-ray irradiator (RS2000pro-225TR, 225.0 kV, 17.7 mA) with a single dose of 0, 4, 8, 16, or 20 Gy. BBR (Chengdu DeSiTe Biological Technology Co., Ltd) was dissolved in DMSO to prepare a stock solution, which was diluted to different concentrations with culture medium in subsequent use. The cells were pretreated with BBR 48 h before radiation exposure.

Animal irradiation and BBR administration

Healthy male C57BL/6J mice and SD rats (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of Kunming Medical University, and all animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of our Experimental Center. To study the effects of BBR on RIII, C57BL/6J mice and SD rats were randomly divided into a control group (Control), irradiation group (IR), and BBR group (IR + BBR). After intraperitoneal injection of anesthesia, mice in the irradiation group and BBR group were irradiated with 12 Gy total abdominal irradiation (TAI) by biological X-ray irradiator (RS2000pro-225TR, 225.0 kV, 17.7 mA, 3.38 Gy/min), and other parts were covered with lead plates. Radiotherapy equipment (Elekta) was used to irradiate rats in the irradiation group and BBR group with 15 Gy TAI. According to previous studies [25, 26], animals in the IR + BBR group were gavaged 40 mg/kg BBR (Shanghai yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd) 4 h after irradiation and orally administered for 7 consecutive days.

Cell toxicity and proliferation

The effect of BBR on the proliferation of IEC-6 cells was evaluated by cell count Kit 8 (CCK-8, Beyotime). In short, IEC-6 cells were inoculated into 96-well plates with an initial density of 5 × 103cells/well and were first cultured in BBR with different concentrations for 48 and 72 h to evaluate the toxic effect of BBR on cells. Secondly, the cells were irradiated after being pretreated with 8 μm BBR for 48 h to evaluate their radiation protection. Then, 10 µl CCK-8 solution and 100 µl medium were added to each well at each time point and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance of the sample was measured at 450 nm with an enzyme-labeled instrument.

Clonogenic survival assay

IEC-6 cells were inoculated into 6-well plates at a density of 800 cells per well. The original medium was discarded after cell adhesion growth and incubated with 8 μm BBR for 48 h. Then, X-ray irradiation (8 Gy) was performed. The medium was changed every 2 days until the colonies were visible, and then the medium was discarded, the cells were washed with PBS twice, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution, and then stained with crystal violet (0.5%). Wash the 6-well plate with clean water and turn the 6-well plate upside down to dry naturally.

SA-β-galactosidase staining

The cells were inoculated into a six-well plate and treated with BBR for 48 h, then irradiated with or without 8 Gy X-ray, and the cell aging status was assessed at 7 days after irradiation. According to the manufacturer’s protocol, staining is performed using the SA-β-Gal staining kit (Beyotime) for dyeing Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and incubated with fresh SA-β-gal solution (PH = 6.0) at 37 °C. Senescent cells were identified as blue-stained cells under light microscopy, and the cells were counted in six random fields to determine the percentage of SA-β-gal cells. The staining method for SA-β-gal in the intestine is the same as above, and finally, the nucleus is stained with hematoxylin.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using an RNA-easy Isolation Reagent (Vazyme). cDNA synthesis follows the manufacturer’s protocol (Evo M-MLV reverse transcription premix kit, Accurate Biology). According to the manufacturer’s protocol, real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green qPCR premix (Accurate Biology). Primers for RT-qPCR are listed in the supplementary table. All data was normalized to a control using βactin or GAPDH as an internal control.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted using a cold RIPA buffer (Beyotime). Concentrations were determined using the BCA kit (Beyotime). An equal amount of protein from each sample was soaked in a TRIS-glycine SDS-PAGE gel and then transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore). The membrane was sealed with 5% skimmed milk and incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C, then incubated with horseradish peroxide-coupled anti-IgG (Beyotime) for 1 h at room temperature. An Enhanced chemiluminescence detection system (Bio-Rad Laboratories) was used to detect protein expression. The primary antibodies used were: γ-H2AX (MERCK), p21 (MCE), p16 (HUABIO), NRF2, HO-1, NQO1 (Cell Signaling), Actin, GAPDH (Beyotime), and the dilution ratio was 1: 1000.

Laser speckle contrast imaging (LSCI)

On the 7th day after irradiation, the mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and placed on the operating table, made a small incision along the midline of the abdomen, the body position of the mice into a lateral position, exposed the mesenteric vascular system, and collected mesenteric blood perfusion images using RFLSI ZW Laser Speckle Imaging System. Throughout the experiment, the body temperature of the mice was maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C.

Magnetic resonance imaging

In vivo MR imaging of rats was performed on a 3.0T MRI scanner (PHILIPS). During the MRI scanning, rats were anesthetized by isoflurane, and small-diameter animal coils were used to fix the rats’ bodies and restrict their movements. The scanning sequence includes T1WI (TE=shortest, TR=510ms, Slice thickness=2 mm) and T2WI (TE=shortest, TR=2000ms, Slice thickness=2 mm).

Histological analysis

After the last administration, the intestinal tissues of each group were collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution overnight, embedded in paraffin, and then stained with H&E and Masson. After antigen repair and sealing treatment, the glass slide was incubated with a primary antibody at 4 °C overnight, followed by a secondary antibody. Then we stained the nucleus with DAPI, and the image was observed and recorded by fluorescence microscope. The quantitative analysis was completed by ImageJ software.

Gut microbiota analysis

The DNA library was sequenced by LC Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China) on the Illumina NovaseqTM 6000 platform to analyze the composition of intestinal microflora in feces. DNA was extracted by magnetic bead method using a fecal genomic DNA extraction kit (Bio Teke, AU46111-96). We preprocessed the original sequencing data to get Clean Data, and then denoised the sequence by QIIME2 D2DA2 [27] to get the representative correct biological sequence ASVs (Amplicon Sequence Variants), and further analyzed it.

Statistical analysis

The data are shown as mean ± standard deviation. Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA were used to evaluate statistical significance. The asterisk indicates that the difference is statistically significant (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001). The SPSS27.0 software package was used for statistical analysis.

Results

BBR alleviates RIII in mice

In this study, we established acute and chronic mice models to evaluate the therapeutic effect of BBR. Firstly, we irradiated the entire abdomen of mice with different doses of X-rays. The results showed that the weight and survival rate of mice decreased after irradiation (Fig. 2a-b). Combined with other literature reports [28, 29], we subsequently used 12 Gy to irradiate mice to construct a RIII model in mice. Histological experiments were performed to examine the intestinal structure. In mice exposed to radiation, the intestinal structure was damaged, and BBR effectively reversed this change (Fig. 2c). Vascular endothelial cells are very sensitive to ionizing radiation, and damage will lead to a lack of nutrient supply to cells and tissues, accelerate cell death and increase tissue inflammation [30]. Studies have found that vascular endothelial cell injury and subsequent ischemia are the key factors contributing to X-ray-induced intestinal injury [31]. As shown by the results of laser speckle blood flow imaging (Fig. 2d-e), mesenteric blood flow in the IR group was significantly reduced 7 days post-irradiation, while it increased after BBR treatment. In addition, 12 Gy TAI can lead to normal colon shortening, which is effectively inhibited by BBR therapy (Fig. 2f-g). Previous studies have found that one of the long-term effects of radiotherapy is fibrosis [32, 33]. We observed mice for a long time after radiation and found that there was no obvious fibrosis in the intestine of the BBR treatment group at 4 months post-radiation (Fig. 2h-i). Similarly, at 4 months post-radiation, we also observed that BBR inhibited the shortening of the colon caused by radiation to some extent (Fig. 2j-k). Therefore, oral BBR may provide potential sustained protection against radiation damage.

MRI to evaluate the therapeutic effect of BBR on RIII in rats

To clarify the therapeutic effects of BBR on RIII and to identify an noninvasive method for assessing its efficacy, MRI can serve as an ideal tool. With its high resolution and lack of radioactivity, MRI is well-suited for diagnosing inflammatory bowel disease and monitoring treatment effectiveness. We utilized MRI to analyze the pathological lesions macroscopically 1–2 weeks post-radiation. MRI images indicated that the T2WI signal intensity and intestinal wall thickness of the irradiated rats increased (Fig. 3a). This finding aligns with histological changes, as we observed edema and mucosal thickening in the gut after radiation, which was accompanied by some degree of fibrosis (Fig. 3b). Compared with the IR group, there was no obvious intestinal injury in BBR group. During this period, we monitored the weight change, survival rate, and defecation of the rats. The weight and defecation of the rats in the BBR group were superior to those in the IR group (Fig. 3c-e). In addition, the mRNA expression levels of fibrosis-related genes and inflammatory factor IL-6 in the BBR group were significantly reduced (Fig. 3f-g). These results suggest that BBR can improve intestinal damage in rats after radiation by reducing the expression level of inflammatory-related factors and reducing collagen deposition, and MRI can be used to evaluate its efficacy.

BBR attenuates IR-induced intestinal DNA damage and senescence in mice

γH2AX is commonly associated with the DNA damage response and is widely considered a marker for this process [34]. As shown in the figure (Fig. 4a-b), compared with Control group, the specific fluorescence signal intensity of γH2AX in IR group was significantly increased, and a large amount of DNA DSB accumulated in tissues after irradiation. While the γH2AX phosphorylation level in BBR group was decreased, indicating that BBR could effectively reduce DNA damage. Similarly, SA-β-Gal staining showed that BBR alleviated radiation-induced intestinal senescence on day 7 after a single 12 Gy exposure (Fig. 4c). SASP is an important hallmark of cellular senescence [35], characterized by the upregulation of various cytokines and inflammatory mediators. The results showed that IR resulted in significantly elevated levels of multiple SASP factors (IL1β, TNFα) and p21 in the intestine, both 7 days and 4 months post-radiation (Fig. 4d-e). And, BBR exerts radiation protection by reducing the levels of SASP and p21. Furthermore, the mice in the IR group exhibited signs of aging, including graying and darkening of the hair on their backs, along with a non-smooth texture (Fig. 4f). Notably, these aging characteristics were significantly improved in the BBR-treated group. Impairment of the intestinal barrier is a common pathophysiological feature observed during the aging process of organisms [36]. In our study, we examined the gene expression of tight junction proteins in the mouse intestine. As shown in Fig. 4g, the tight junction function between epithelial cells in the IR group was impaired, as manifested by the decreased expression levels of ZO-1, Occludin, Claudin3, and Claudin4, while BBR intervention could improve intestinal barrier dysfunction. The above results suggest that BBR has a certain therapeutic effect on radiation-induced intestinal senescence.

BBR reduces cell senescence and SASP in vitro

Based on the above, we established a radiation-induced senescence model of intestinal epithelial cells in vitro and further observed the radiation-protective effect of BBR in vitro. First, the response of IEC-6 cells to different doses of X-ray irradiation was evaluated by CCK8 method, and the results showed that cell growth was inhibited after irradiation in a radiation dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5a). BBR is a natural compound, and we found that it did not produce cytotoxicity to IEC-6 cells within a range of concentrations (Fig. 5b). Subsequently, we pretreated IEC-6 cells with 8 µM BBR for 48 h before administering 8 Gy of radiation to evaluate whether BBR could mitigate the harmful effects of ionizing radiation in vitro. At 7 days after radiation, we observed an increase in SA-β-gal activity, while the SA-β-gal activity decreased to some extent in the BBR group (Fig. 5c-d). Similarly, Western blotting results showed reduced levels of senescence markers p16 and p21 proteins in the BBR-treated IEC-6 cells (Fig. 5e-h). At 3 and 7 days after radiation exposure, we observed a decrease in SASP in the BBR group (Fig. 5i-k). Oxidative stress is widely recognized as a major driver of aging and age-related diseases [37]. To investigate whether BBR inhibits cell senescence by resisting oxidative stress, we examined the expression levels of Nrf2 and its downstream proteins HO-1 and NQO1 (Fig. 5l-q). Compared with IR group, BBR enhanced the expression of Nrf2 and antioxidant enzymes (HO-1 and NQO1) to protect IEC-6 cells from oxidative damage caused by radiation. These results suggest that BBR can prevent cells from entering a senescent state by reducing SASP and improving oxidative stress, thereby enhancing cell proliferation. To evaluate the effect of BBR on cell proliferation, we performed a cell survival assay and clonal formation assay. Compared with the control group, the cell survival rate in the irradiated group was significantly reduced, and the cell survival rate in the BBR-treated group was higher than that in the irradiated group (Fig. 5r). After radiotherapy, cell proliferation was greatly reduced, however, we found that BBR improved the colony-forming efficiency of irradiated intestinal epithelial cells (Fig. 5s). These findings suggest that BBR increases the cell’s ability to survive and reproduce. In summary, BBR can stimulate cell clonal proliferation and enhance antioxidant capacity, while inhibiting the initiation of specific aging mechanisms in cells.

BBR altered gut microbiota in irradiated mice

Ionizing radiation can damage crypt cells and intestinal barrier repair mechanism, promote the expression of inflammatory factors, cause intestinal microbiota imbalance, and affect the structure, function and species of intestinal flora metabolites [38]. And disorder of gut microbiota induces excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to inflammation and DNA damage, thereby increasing cellular senescence [39]. Because BBR was administered orally, we hypothesized that its protective effect against RIII may be associated with alterations in the gut microbiome. First, we used Venn diagrams to visually display the numbers of shared and unique amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) among different groups. As shown in Fig. 6a, a total of 5049 ASV sequences were obtained from this sequencing. Regarding the unique sequences, there were 1536 in the Control group, 594 in the IR group, and 2005 in the IR + BBR group. The results of alpha diversity (Fig. 6b-d) showed that the Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices in the IR group were significantly lower than those in the Control group. However, the abundance and diversity of intestinal microbiota were restored to some extent by BBR treatment after radiation. The results of beta diversity (Fig. 6e, f) indicated that the distribution range of the gut microbiota in the irradiation group decreased significantly, and abdominal irradiation could alter the composition of the gut microbiota in mice.

Regarding the composition of the gut microbiota, irradiation changed the composition of the main gut microbiota at the phylum and class levels. At the phylum level, compared with the Control group, the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes decreased, while the relative abundances of Firmicutes and Proteobacteria increased 7 days after radiation. The Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes (B/F) ratio was reduced (Fig. 7a, d, f). At the class level, compared with the normal control group, the irradiation group showed a significant increase in Bacilli and a decrease in Bacteroidia and Clostridia (Fig. 7b, c). BBR intervention improved the disrupted microbiota state after irradiation. Similarly, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEFSe) of the ASVs also revealed significant changes in the gut microbiota (Fig. 7e). These results indicated that BBR is closely related to the improvement of intestinal flora disorder in irradiated mice.

Discussion

Radiotherapy has always been an integral part of cancer management, but the potential harm to normal tissues caused by this treatment should not be ignored [40]. The intestine is a highly radiation-sensitive tissue, ionizing radiation can cause severe DNA damage, which in turn induces apoptosis and cell senescence in vivo and in vitro [41]. In this study, we demonstrated that the natural compound BBR has a therapeutic effect on radiation-induced intestinal aging by reducing oxidative stress damage, inhibiting intestinal SASP. At the same time, BBR can restore the normal function of intestinal vascular endothelial cells, and is accompanied by an improvement in intestinal flora imbalance. Above all, these findings carry important implications for the application of BBR as a promising therapeutic agent to mitigate radiation-induced intestinal damage.

In previous studies, BBR has been observed to have significant pharmacological benefits in a variety of aging-related diseases, including hyperlipidemia, hyperglycemia, neurodegeneration (such as Alzheimer’s disease) [42–44]. Meanwhile, in radiation-induced skin injury, Huang et al. reported that BBR could accelerate radiation-induced wound healing and exert protective effects against radiation-induced skin damage by downregulating the expression of senescence markers and inhibiting inflammatory pathways including p53, ROS, and JAK-STAT signaling [45]. However, whether BBR can regulate cellular senescence in RIII remains unclear. Based on these findings, we subsequently investigated the effects of BBR on RIII and its related mechanisms. The intestinal senescence was confirmed through SA-β-Gal staining and detection of detection of SASP and p21 expression. We also observed characteristic age-related changes in mice, including increased DNA damage and graying of hair. We innovatively found that BBR exerts therapeutic efficacy against IR-induced intestinal senescence, with previous studies providing evidence of its inhibitory effects on aging in various tissues, including the hepatic and cardiac [21, 46]. Our findings align harmoniously with these established results and provide novel insights into IR-induced senescence. Subsequently, we further confirmed BBR’s anti-radiation senescence effects in in vitro cell experiments, with the potential mechanism involving the Nrf2-related pathway. The Nrf2 is a critical transcription factor that regulates the expression of numerous genes under both healthy and diseased conditions [47]. Upon exposure to endogenous or exogenous insults, including oxidative stress, Nrf2 activates the expression of antioxidant and cytoprotective genes [48]. Regarding Nrf2, previous studies have reported that BBR against MTX-induced intestinal injury through modulating the GSK-3β/NRF2 pathway, and hence an improvement of the endogenous antioxidant defense system [49]. Incongruent with these results, we observed that BBR prevented radiation-induced cellular senescence and activated Nrf2 and its downstream antioxidant proteins (HO-1,NQO1). In a fructose-induced rat liver injury model, Cheng et al. also found that BBR could significantly upregulate the protein expression of Nrf2 and HO-1, thereby alleviating oxidative stress [50], which is consistent with our findings. They also demonstrated that BBR markedly reduced intracellular levels of ROS and MDA, increased SOD enzymatic activity and GSH content. The hepatoprotective effect of BBR was closely associated with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant actions mediated via the ADK/AMPK/Nrf2 signaling pathway.

Vascular endothelial cells are very sensitive to ionizing radiation, and radiation-induced vascular endothelial injury may upset the balance of inflammatory cytokines, resulting in a lack of nutrient supply to cells and tissues, which plays an important role in the progression of radiation bowel injury [51, 52]. According to previous literature reports, LSCI was used to monitor mesenteric blood flow to evaluate ischemia secondary to vascular endothelial cell injury [53]. Our results showed that mesenteric blood flow in mice was significantly reduced after TAI, indicating injury to vascular endothelial cells. Studies have reported that Geranylgeranylacetone protects against RIII by inducing VEGF/eNOS signaling and inhibiting the expression of inflammatory cytokines, thereby promoting the angiogenic activity of injured endothelial cells [54]. Similarly, Mu et al. demonstrated that Lonicerae japonicae flos and its main component chlorogenic acid can improve vasodilation of the mesenteric artery, restore blood perfusion, and prevent abdominal radiotherapy-induced vascular endothelial dysfunction via GTPCH1/BH4/eNOS pathway [55]. Our findings are consistent with these results, and further in-depth studies will be conducted to explore the specific mechanism by which BBR restores vascular endothelial cell function.

In addition to directly damaging intestinal cells, radiation disrupts the original balance of intestinal microecology. Preclinical research and clinical studies have shown that ionizing radiation can alter the intestinal microbiota [56, 57], affecting the maintenance of intestinal health and the absorption and digestion of nutrients. In this study, we used 16S rRNA sequencing to compare the composition of the intestinal microbiota of irradiated mice, with detailed analyses identifying notable alterations in its structural composition, α diversity, and β diversity. Fortunately, we found that BBR is associated with the restoration of gut microbiota dysbiosis. Numerous previous studies have also demonstrated that BBR can treat various diseases by modulating gut microbiota dysbiosis, highlighting its important role in maintaining intestinal symbiotic balance. In ulcerative colitis (UC) mice, gut microbiota analysis showed that the relative abundances of Bacteroides, Alistipes, and unclassified_Clostridia_vadinBB60_group were significantly higher in the UC + low-dose BBR 14-day intervention group than in the UC group [58]. BBR significantly alleviated UC symptoms in mice, regulated the intestinal microecology, and reshaped the composition of intestinal tissue metabolites. Similarly, our study also found that BBR could upregulate the abundance of Bacteroidetes after radiation exposure. However, studies by Wu et al. and Chen et al. found that the beneficial effects of BBR on intestinal barrier function are mainly attributed to Akkermansia, and BBR can alleviate intestinal mucosal injury and reduce the levels of inflammatory factors [59, 60]. Members of the genus Akkermansia are the only cultured representatives of Verrucomicrobiota within the gastrointestinal tract [61], and it influences host metabolism, strengthens gut barrier integrity, modulates microbial composition, and regulates immune responses [62]. Therefore, future studies may focus on this key player in the disease regulation of the gut microbiota—Akkermansia. Furthermore, we employed MRI to assess the therapeutic effects of BBR in a rat model of RIII, specifically focusing on the thickness of the colorectal wall and T2WI signal intensity. In our study, we observed a notable increase in colorectal wall thickness and T2WI hyperintensity in irradiated rats, which aligns with previous research findings [63]. While BBR can effectively reverse this change. At the same time, we also found some association between these changes and histopathology. Histopathology results showed that these findings on MR imaging were related to submucosal edema. This suggests that MR imaging can be used to assess intestinal damage and response to treatment in this animal model, and it may also provide a potential marker for histopathological scoring.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings indicate that BBR can effectively alleviate radiation induced intestinal senescence in mice. Meanwhile, BBR has certain effects on alleviating oxidative stress damage and restoring the disordered intestinal flora following radiation exposure. The therapeutic effects of BBR may be related to the regulation of the NRF2, HO-1, and NQO1 pathways. At the same time, combined with MRI, the pathological lesions of radiation-induced intestinal damage can be analyzed macroscopically, allowing for the non-invasive evaluation of BBR’s efficacy. These findings highlight the potential use of BBR as a treatment for radiation-induced intestinal damage.
