Towards Sharp Minimax Risk Bounds for Operator Learning
Ben Adcock, Gregor Maier, Rahul Parhi

Abstract
We develop a minimax theory for operator learning, where the goal is to estimate an unknown operator between separable Hilbert spaces from finitely many noisy input-output samples. For uniformly bounded Lipschitz operators, we prove information-theoretic lower bounds together with matching or near-matching upper bounds, covering both fixed and random designs under Hilbert-valued Gaussian noise and Gaussian white noise errors. The rates are controlled by the spectrum of the covariance operator of the measure that defines the error metric. Our setup is very general and allows for measures with unbounded support. A key implication is a curse of sample complexity which shows that the minimax risk for generic Lipschitz operators cannot decay at any algebraic rate in the sample size. We obtain essentially sharp characterizations when the covariance spectrum decays exponentially and provide general upper and lower bounds in slower-decay regimes.

Introduction

A new paradigm in machine learning for scientific computing is focused on designing learning algorithms and methods for continuum problems. This paradigm is referred to as
operator learning
and has received considerable interest in the last few years

,

,

,

,

,

,

. The basic task may be posed as learning a map between infinite-dimensional function spaces, i.e., learning an operator

F:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y},

where, for example,
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are real, separable Hilbert spaces. Operator learning naturally arises in many scientific problems where one wants to learn how a continuum model, often described by partial differential equations (PDEs), maps inputs, such as parameters or boundary conditions, to outputs, such as states or observables. A prototypical example to keep in mind is learning parameter-to-solution maps of parametric PDEs
[
2
,
11
,
1
]
. In contrast to more classical surrogate modeling, which typically focuses on learning finite-dimensional parameter-to-solution maps for some
fixed discretization
, operator learning directly aims to learn/approximate the continuum map
F:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
itself. Thus, the inputs and outputs are functions (not vectors) and the goal is to directly design
discretization-invariant
methods
[
23
,
7
]
.

From a statistical perspective, this naturally leads to a nonparametric regression problem in which both the object of interest (the operator) and the observations (finite number of noisy samples) are infinite-dimensional. Suppose that we observe the noisy input-output pairs

\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m},\quad Y_{i}=F(X_{i})+\sigma E_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,m,

where the design points
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
are either
fixed
or
random
elements of
\mathcal{X}
, the noise terms

are i.i.d. noise terms which may or may not take values in
\mathcal{Y}
(e.g., our setup can account for Gaussian white noise that almost surely does not take values in
\mathcal{Y}
),

\sigma>0

F
belongs in some prescribed model class
\mathcal{F}
of operators. The goal is then to use the data
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
to design an
estimator

\widehat{F}:=\widehat{F}_{m}
that is reasonably close to
F
. The performance of an estimator is quantified via the
minimax risk

\inf_{\widehat{F}}\sup_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\|F-\widehat{F}\|}_{L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})}}}\right],\quad 1\leq p<\infty.

(1.1)

The precise dependence of (
1.1
) on

minimax rate
.
In finite-dimensional nonparametric regression (i.e.,
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces), the optimal behavior of (
1.1
) is well-understood. Indeed, for many finite-dimensional model classes, e.g., the unit ball of the Besov space
B^{s}_{p,q}[-1,1]^{d}
that satisfies the condition

B^{s}_{p,q}[-1,1]^{d}

15
,
16
,
41
,
42

43

. For operator learning, however, the situation is much less clear (see Section

Given a model class
\mathcal{F}
of operators
F:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
and the error metric of the
L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})
-norm,
what is the optimal rate at which the risk can decay as a function of
m
?

In this paper, we make a step towards closing this gap for one of the most natural and widely studied operator classes, the class of
uniformly bounded Lipschitz operators
. We focus on the nonparametric regression setting where the noise is either Hilbert-valued Gaussian noise (trace-class covariance operator) or Gaussian white noise. For this model class, we study the minimax risk for both fixed and random designs and quantify the decay rates as a function of the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
of the covariance operator of the measure
\mu
. Our main results give general upper and lower bounds on this quantity for classes of bounded Lipschitz operators, and we derive sharp rates in several important regimes. In particular, we remark that our setting is very general as
\mu
could have bounded or unbounded support and includes practical cases such as the uniform measure on a compact set or an (unbounded support) Gaussian measure. This generality is in stark contrast with most works in minimax estimation.

At a qualitative level, our findings show that operator learning in the Lipschitz setting is subject to a
curse of sample complexity
: Regardless of how fast the eigenvalues decay, the minimax risk cannot decrease at an algebraic rate in
m
.
In particular, in the case of algebraically-decaying eigenvalues,
\lambda_{i}=i^{-\alpha}
, we establish general upper and lower bounds for the minimax risk, with the former being algebraically-decaying in
\log(m)
and the latter exponentially-decaying in
\sqrt{\log(m)}
(note that this subalgebraic in
m
, as must be the case). For exponentially decaying eigenvalues,
\lambda_{i}=\exp(-\alpha i^{\beta})
, we obtain essentially matching upper and lower bounds for the minimax risk, leading to a precise characterization of the minimax rate in terms
of
m
. Overall, these results make precise, in a minimax sense, the “curse of sample complexity” that has been identified in previous data-complexity and approximation-theoretic analyses of operator learning, which we discuss below.

1.1
Motivations

There are multiple motivations for developing a minimax theory of operator learning for Lipschitz classes. For example, Lipschitz operators provide a flexible and natural model class for many operator learning problems arising in applications. Maps defined by solution operators of PDEs, parameter-to-state maps, and parameter-to-observable maps often enjoy a Lipschitz (or locally Lipschitz) dependence on the input in appropriate norms. Such Lipschitz bounds play a central role, for example, in stability analyses of PDEs and in the well-posedness theory for inverse problems. Furthermore, it is well-known that solution operators of elliptic variational inequalities, which arise, for instance, in obstacle and optimal control problems, are Lipschitz continuous but generally not Gâteaux differentiable
[
9
,
33
,
10
]
. It is therefore natural to ask how difficult it is, from a statistical point of view, to learn a generic Lipschitz operator from noisy input-output data.

There has also been recent work in operator learning on revealing various notions of “complexity” for operator classes, including
parametric complexity
(the number of parameters required by a neural operator architecture to achieve a given approximation error)
[
26
,
38
]

and
data complexity
(the number of samples required by a particular estimation procedure to achieve a prescribed accuracy)
[
3
,
4
,
22
]
. Approximation-theoretic results for neural operators have shown that approximating general Lipschitz operators may require an exponentially large number of parameters in the target accuracy. Data-complexity analyses, on the other hand, suggest that sample requirements for operator learning can be extremely unfavorable for Lipschitz operators
[
4
,
22
,
40
]
. A minimax perspective complements these analyses by asking a more fundamental question: Even if we ignore computational and architecture choices, what is the best sample complexity that
any
estimator can achieve over a given operator class from a set of
m
noisy observations?

Thus, the results of this paper contribute to this agenda by providing the first, to the best of our knowledge, general minimax bounds (upper and lower) for bounded Lipschitz operators and by identifying regimes where these bounds are sharp. Along the way, our analysis clarifies how the difficulty of operator learning depends on the geometry induced by the underlying measure
\mu
(encoded in the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
).
Our analysis also reveals many nuances when studying statistical estimation where the inputs and outputs are both infinite dimensional when compared to the classical statistical paradigm of finite-dimensional inputs and outputs, which we recover as a special case (see Section
6.1
).

1.2
Implications and related work

This paper makes several contributions that are statistical in nature. Although operator learning has been studied extensively from approximation-theoretic and algorithmic perspectives, there is comparatively little work that characterizes its fundamental statistical difficulty. We develop a minimax theory for learning uniformly bounded Lipschitz operators from noisy input-output samples, providing both information-theoretic lower bounds and matching (or near-matching) upper bounds across fixed and random designs and across two canonical infinite-dimensional noise models.
The arguments draw together ideas that are typically disparate: Operator learning, minimax estimation under general (possibly unbounded) measures, nonparametric functional data analysis, and estimation of objects with Hilbert-valued outputs. Thus, the results of this paper can also be viewed as yielding a single framework that subsumes several previously studied settings as special cases. We outline these previous settings in the context of our results in the sequel.

Data complexity and minimax rates for operator learning.

There has recently been a large interest in studying various statistical and data-complexity properties for operator learning problems
[
12
,
21
,
22
,
32
,
39
,
40
]
. Outside of a few notable examples
[
29
,
37
]
, there is almost no work on minimax estimation for operator learning. The authors of
[
29
]
analyze ERM solutions over deep network classes for estimating Lipschitz operators between separable Hilbert spaces and derive nonasymptotic upper bounds for the risk. They note that sharp determination of minimax rates (and, in particular, matching lower bounds) is an open problem. On the other hand, the authors of
[
37
]
develop a general statistical theory for determining upper bounds on operators using tools from empirical-process theory. As a special case, they obtain algebraic rates for learning holomorphic operators, but note that lower bounds are unknown in the infinite-dimensional setting. In contrast, we study the minimax risk over uniformly bounded Lipschitz operators and prove information-theoretic lower bounds together with matching (or near-matching) upper bounds, implying in particular that the minimax risk cannot decay algebraically in the sample size for generic Lipschitz opertors. We find sharp rates in regimes such as exponential eigenvalue decay.

Nonparametric estimation beyond bounded domains.

While classical minimax theory is often stated for compact domains, our framework is formulated for error measured with respect to a general measure
\mu
(possibly unbounded support) and recovers the usual bounded-domain Lipschitz minimax rate as a special case (e.g.,
\mu
uniform on
[-1,1]^{d}
yields
m^{-\frac{1}{2+d}}
, cf. Section
6.1
). Recently, a finite-dimensional counterpart on unbounded supports appeared in
[
6
]
. There, the authors study scalar regression of
1
-Lipschitz functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
under isotropic log-concave
\mu
and analyze various estimators. In contrast, we consider the operator learning problem and characterize the minimax rate in terms of the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
, giving information-theoretic lower bounds and matching upper bounds in infinite-dimensional settings.

Nonparametric functional data analysis.

A close analogue of operator learning is nonparametric functional regression, where one estimates a regression operator from functional designs and possibly Hilbert-valued responses; see, e.g., the book
[
17
]
and references therein. In this literature, rates for kernel/Nadaraya–Watson and
k
-nearest-neighbor methods are typically controlled by local small-ball probabilities. For instance, the authors of
[
8
,
28
]
prove almost-sure convergence rates (only upper bounds) for Nadaraya–Watson and nearest-neighbor estimators with scalar-valued
[
8
]
and Hilbert-valued
[
28
]
observations (i.e., the noise is
\mathcal{Y}
-valued, meaning that Gaussian white noise is excluded) under boundedness and Hölder/Lipschitz regularity assumptions on the regression operator. However, sharpness of their bounds, i.e., characterization of the minimax rate, was not addressed. In the opposite direction, the authors of
[
31
]
derive minimax lower bounds for estimating functionals
F:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. They show that, in general, for infinite-dimensional settings, the minimax risk at
a single point
is necessarily subalgebraic in the number of data points
m
, but, as above, the sharpness of their bounds was not addressed. Our results, on the other hand, provide
Hilbert-valued
and
global-risk
counterparts of these results with matching (or near-matching) upper and lower bounds in several important regimes.

Estimation of objects with Hilbert-valued outputs.

A recurring technical issue in infinite-dimensional-output regression/operator learning is the noise model. Many analyses assume that the noise is
\mathcal{Y}
-valued (e.g., sub-Gaussian or trace-class Gaussian)
[
28
]
. This ensures that the observations satisfy
Y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
almost surely, in which case least-squares objectives are well-defined. Unfortunately, this assumption does not include the Gaussian white noise errors as soon as
\dim(\mathcal{Y})=\infty
. As a result, naive least-squares objectives are not well-defined, and so one must work with alternative formulations. In this paper we treat both of these canonical regimes (Assumptions
2.1
and
2.1
). In the case of Gaussian white noise errors we take inspiration from
[
12
,
19
,
35
,
37
]
and consider Hilbert scales to “tame” the white noise, cf. (
2.6
). Correspondingly, our results allow for a unified minimax theory for Hilbert-valued errors and white noise errors.

Main results

Our main results consider classes of uniformly bounded Lipschitz operators. Let

\mathcal{F}_{B,L}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{F:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}:{\|F(X)\|}_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq B,\ {\|F(X)-F(X^{\prime})\|}_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq L{\|X-X^{\prime}\|}_{\mathcal{X}},\ \forall X,X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}}\right\}.

These two results are consequences of general lower (Theorem
3.1
) and upper (Theorems

,

) bounds we establish later. These general bounds hold for arbitrary eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
and for both finite- and infinite-dimensional spaces

and

and

satisfying Assumption

. Later, in Section

follow as simple consequences of these general upper and lower bounds.

Our tight characterization only holds for

. For
0<\beta<1
, we have the non-tight bounds

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log(m/\sigma^{2})}}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}}\lesssim_{\alpha,\beta,\iota,p}\mathcal{L}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})\lesssim_{\alpha,\beta,\iota,p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log(m/\sigma^{2})}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

in the case of Hilbert-valued noise. An analogous result holds for white noise. In particular, the upper bound for
\mathcal{L}_{m}
(i.e., lower bound for
\mathcal{M}_{m}
) has exponent

independent of

. We believe this is an artefact of the proof. See Remark
5.1
for further discussion.

While our results tightly characterize the minimax risk in the case of exponentially decay, for slower-decaying eigenvalues we have non-matching upper and lower bounds. We now summarize these results in the case of algebraic decay.

Theorem 2.9

(Algebraically-decaying
\lambda_{i}
; Hilbert-valued Gaussian noise)

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
and Assumption
2.1

\frac{\alpha-1}{2}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log(m/\sigma^{2})}{\log(\log(m/\sigma^{2}))}}}\right)+\mathcal{O}_{\alpha,\beta,\iota,\Upsilon,B,L,p}(1)\leq\mathcal{L}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})\lesssim_{\iota,p}\sqrt{\log(m/\sigma^{2})},

as
m\rightarrow\infty
, where
\mathcal{L}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})
is as in (
2.4
), with
\mathcal{M}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{B,L};,L^{p}_{\mu})
given by either (
2.2
) or (
2.3
).

Theorem 2.10

(Algebraically-decaying
\lambda_{i}
; white noise)
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
and Assumption
2.1
hold. Let
1\leq p<\infty
and suppose that
\lambda_{i}=i^{-\alpha}
for some
\alpha>1
. Then

\frac{\alpha-1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log(m/\sigma^{2})}{\log(\log(m/\sigma^{2}))}}}\right)+\mathcal{O}_{\alpha,\beta,\iota,t,B,L,p}(1)\leq\mathcal{L}_{m}(\mathcal{F}^{t}_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})\lesssim_{\iota,p}\sqrt{\log(m/\sigma^{2})}.

\mathcal{L}_{m}(\mathcal{F}^{t}_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})

\mathcal{M}_{m}(\mathcal{F}^{t}_{B,L};,L^{p}_{\mu})

) or (

These theorems imply the minimax risk satisfies, for either
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{B,L}
or
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}^{t}_{B,L}

C_{1}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-C_{2}\sqrt{\log(m/\sigma^{2})}}}\right)\leq\mathcal{M}_{m}(\mathcal{F};L^{p}_{\mu})\leq C_{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log(m/\sigma^{2})}{\log(\log(m/\sigma^{2}))}}}\right)^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{2}}

\alpha
of the eigenvalues—notably, it is the same rate as holds in the case of exponentially-decaying eigenvalues with

Namely, it is polylogarithmic in

To summarize, in this section we have shown that minimax rates for Lipschitz operators are necessarily subalgebraic, and have giving a series of upper and lower bounds for different eigenvalues decay profiles. We remark in passing that, while impossible, rates that are close to algebraic can be obtained for sufficiently-fast decaying eigenvalues. In Section

we present a tight characterization for the log-minimax risk in the case of double-exponential decay, i.e.,

3
Lower bounds on the minimax risk

In this section, we establish the following lower bound on the minimax risk, from which the corresponding upper bounds in our main results will subsequently follow.

Theorem 3.1

Suppose that Assumption

3.1
Key ingredients for the proof Theorem
3.1

Our lower bound follows the standard reduction of minimax estimation to a multi-hypothesis testing problem (see, e.g., Chapter 2 in
[
41
]
). Fix a semidistance
d(\cdot,\cdot)
on the model class
\mathcal{F}
. In this paper, we take

d(F,G):=\|F-G\|_{L^{p}_{\mu}(X;Y)},\quad 1\leq p<\infty.

(3.3)

For a finite subset of hypotheses
\{F_{0},\dots,F_{M}\}\subset\mathcal{F}
, let
P_{j}
denote the law of the observations under
F_{j}
. In the fixed-design setting,
P_{j}
is the law of
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m})
. In the random-design setting,
P_{j}
is the joint law of
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
. If the hypotheses are well-separated, i.e.,

d(F_{j},F_{k})\geq 2s^{*},\quad j\neq k,

(3.4)

and simultaneously the induced distributions are close in the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence, i.e.,

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}D(P_{j}\|P_{0})\leq\alpha\log M,\quad 0<\alpha<1/8,

(3.5)

then no estimator can reliably distinguish the hypotheses. In which case, Fano’s inequality (reproduced below) provides a uniform lower bound of order
s^{*}
for the minimax risk.

It remains to construct the well-separated set
\{F_{0},\dots,F_{M}\}\subset\mathcal{F}
. We do this by combining (i) a geometric construction of many localized “bump” functions in the first
d
eigencoordinates of
\mu
with (ii) the Varshamov–Gilbert bound (also reproduced below) to extract a subset of
\{0,1\}^{n}
with large Hamming separation, which translates into
L^{p}_{\mu}
-separation of the corresponding operators. We now recall the two standard tools (Fano’s inequality and the Varshamov–Gilbert bound) that formalize this recipe.

Theorem 3.3

(Fano’s inequality)
.

Let
M\geq 2
and let
\{F_{0},\ldots,F_{M}\}\subset\mathcal{F}
be such that

1.

d(F_{j},F_{k})\geq 2s^{*}
, where
d
is a semidistance; and

2.

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}D(P_{j}\|P_{0})\leq\alpha\log M
, with
0<\alpha<1/8
.

Then

\inf_{\widehat{F}}\sup_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d(\widehat{F},F)\geq s^{*}}}\right]\geq\frac{\sqrt{M}}{1+\sqrt{M}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-2\alpha-2\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{\log M}}}}\right)>0

(3.6)

and, in particular,

\inf_{\widehat{F}}\sup_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d(\widehat{F},F)}}\right]\geq\frac{\sqrt{M}}{1+\sqrt{M}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-2\alpha-2\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{\log M}}}}\right)s^{*}.

(3.7)

Note that (
3.7
) follows directly from (
3.6
) and Markov’s inequality.
Next, let
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. We define their
Hamming distance
as

H(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\theta_{j}-\theta_{j}^{\prime}|=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\theta_{j}-\theta_{j}^{\prime}|^{2}.

Lemma 3.4

(Varshamov–Gilbert bound)
.

Let
n\geq 8
. Then there exists a subset
(\theta_{0},\ldots,\theta_{M})
of
\{0,1\}^{n}
such that

(i)

\theta_{0}=(0,\ldots,0)

(ii)

M\geq 2^{n/8}
; and

(iii)

H(\theta_{j},\theta_{k})\geq n/8
for all

4.1
Construction of the estimator

A standard approach for establishing upper bounds for Lipschitz functions in finite dimensions and for compactly-supported measures
\mu
involves constructing a
histogram
estimator. We follow a similar approach, extending this estimator to infinite-dimensional Lipschitz operators and measures with potentially noncompact supports.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}\in\mathbb{N}
and
R>0
be parameters.
Let
\lambda_{i}
and
\phi_{i}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
, be as in Assumption
2.1
and define the set

D=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{X\in\mathcal{X}:|\langle X,\phi_{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}|\leq\sqrt{R\lambda_{i}},\ \forall i=1,\ldots,d}}\right\}\subset\mathcal{X}.

Now subdivide each interval
[-\sqrt{R\lambda_{i}},\sqrt{R\lambda_{i}}]
into
n_{i}
equally-spaced intervals
I_{i,j}
,
j=1,\ldots,n_{i}
, and consider the cells

C=C_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{d}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{X\in\mathcal{X}:\langle X,\phi_{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}\in I_{i,j_{i}},\ \forall i=1,\ldots,d}}\right\},

Let
\mathcal{C}
denote the collection of cells. Note that

|\mathcal{C}|=n_{1}\cdots n_{d}=:n.

Given
F:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
, in the case of Hilbert-valued Gaussian noise, we define the estimator

\widehat{F}=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}Y_{C}\mathbb{I}_{C},

(4.1)

where
\mathbb{I}_{C}
denotes the indicator function of the set
C
and

Y_{C}=\frac{1}{N_{C}}\sum_{i:X_{i}\in C}Y_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad N_{C}=|\{i:X_{i}\in C\}|.

For completeness, if
N_{C}=0
we define
Y_{C}=0
.
In the case of white noise, we modify the estimator as follows. Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
and define the operator

\mathcal{S}_{r}:\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y},\ Y\mapsto\sum^{r}_{i=1}\langle Y,\psi_{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}\psi_{i},

where
\{\psi_{i}\}^{\infty}_{i=1}

is the orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{Y}
that defines the spaces
\mathcal{Y}^{t}
in (
2.6
).
Then we set

\widehat{F}=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}Y_{C,r}\mathbb{I}_{C},\quad\text{where }Y_{C,r}=\mathcal{S}_{r}(Y_{C}).

(4.2)

4.2
Error bounds for the estimator

Lemma 4.3
.

Consider the fixed design setting, where the
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
are such that
N_{C}=m/n
(assumed to be an integer) and
\langle X_{i},\phi_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}=0
,
\forall j>d
. In the Hilbert-valued Gaussian noise setting, the estimator (
4.1
) satisfies, for any
F\in\mathcal{F}_{B,L}
,

\mathbb{E}{\|F-\widehat{F}\|}_{L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})}\lesssim_{p}\sqrt{\frac{n}{m}}\sigma+L\sqrt{R}\sqrt{\sum^{d}_{j=1}\frac{\lambda_{j}}{n^{2}_{j}}}+L\sqrt{\sum_{j>d}\lambda_{j}}+B\mu(D^{c})^{\frac{1}{p}}.

In the white noise setting, the estimator (
4.2
) satisfies, for any
t\geq 0
and
F\in\mathcal{F}^{t}_{B,L}
,

\mathbb{E}{\|F-\widehat{F}\|}_{L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})}\lesssim_{p}Bw^{-t}_{r}+\sqrt{\frac{rn}{m}}\sigma+L\sqrt{R}\sqrt{\sum^{d}_{j=1}\frac{\lambda_{j}}{n^{2}_{j}}}+L\sqrt{\sum_{j>d}\lambda_{j}}+B\mu(D^{c})^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{S}_{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}
converges strongly to the identity operator on
\mathcal{Y}
, we may consider the estimator (
4.1
) as a special case of (
4.2
) with
r=\infty
and
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i}
being the orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of the covariance of Hilbert-valued Gaussian
noise distribution. Assuming
r\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, we first write

\widetilde{F}=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\widetilde{Y}_{C,r}\mathbb{I}_{C},\quad\widetilde{Y}_{C,r}=\frac{1}{N_{C}}\sum_{i:X_{i}\in C}\mathcal{S}_{r}(F(X_{i})),

and then write

{\|F-\widehat{F}\|}_{L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})}\leq{\|F-\mathcal{S}_{r}\circ F\|}_{L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})}+{\|\mathcal{S}_{r}\circ F-\widetilde{F}\|}_{L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})}+{\|\widetilde{F}-\widehat{F}\|}_{L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})}.

(4.3)

Consider the first term. We have

\displaystyle{\|F(X)-\mathcal{S}_{r}\circ F(X)\|}^{2}_{\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i>r}|\langle F(X),\psi_{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq w^{-2t}_{r}\sum_{i>r}w^{2t}_{r}|\langle F(X),\psi_{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq w^{-2t}_{r}{\|F(X)\|}^{2}_{\mathcal{Y}^{t}}

\displaystyle\leq B^{2}w^{-2t}_{r}.

Therefore

{\|F-\mathcal{S}_{r}\circ F\|}_{L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})}\leq Bw^{-t}_{r}.

(4.4)

Consider the third term of (
4.3
). We have

\displaystyle{\|\widetilde{F}-\widehat{F}\|}^{p}_{L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\mu(C){\|Y_{C,r}-\widetilde{Y}_{C,r}\|}^{p}_{\mathcal{Y}}=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\mu(C)\frac{\sigma^{p}}{N^{p}_{C}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\sum_{i:X_{i}\in C}\mathcal{S}_{r}(E_{i})}}\right\|}^{p}_{\mathcal{Y}}.

Consider the Hilbert-valued noise case. Let
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{Y}
be the orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of the covariance operator
\Upsilon
of the noise and
\upsilon_{1}\geq\upsilon_{2}\geq\cdots\geq 0
be the eigenvalues. Let

I_{C}=\mathbb{E}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\sum_{i:X_{i}\in C}E_{i}}}\right\|}^{p}_{\mathcal{Y}}=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum^{\infty}_{j=1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sum_{i:X_{i}\in C}\langle E_{i},\psi_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}}}\right|^{2}}}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}.

Now observe that
\langle E_{i},\psi_{j}\rangle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\upsilon_{j})

and therefore we can write

\sum_{i:X_{i}\in C}\langle E_{i},\psi_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}=\sigma\sqrt{\upsilon_{j}}Z_{j},\quad\text{where }Z_{j}=\sum_{i:X_{i}\in C}n_{ij}.

and
n_{ij}\sim_{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. We now apply Hölder’s inequality to get

\displaystyle I_{C}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum^{\infty}_{j=1}\upsilon_{j}|Z_{j}|^{2}}}\right]^{\frac{p}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum^{\infty}_{j=1}\upsilon^{(1-1/p)q}_{j}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum^{\infty}_{j=1}\upsilon_{j}|Z_{j}|^{2p}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}}}\right]^{\frac{p}{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}(\Upsilon)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum^{\infty}_{j=1}\upsilon_{j}|Z_{j}|^{2p}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Tr}(\Upsilon)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum^{\infty}_{j=1}\upsilon_{j}\mathbb{E}|Z_{j}|^{2p}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where
1<q\leq\infty
satisfies
1/p+1/q=1
. Now
Z_{j}
is a sum of
N_{C}
i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
random variables. Therefore, it is subgaussian with parameters
\beta=2
and
\kappa=1/(2N_{C})
. By a standard moment bound, we have

[\mathbb{E}(|Z_{j}|^{2p})]^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lesssim_{p}\sqrt{N_{c}}.

We deduce that

I_{C}\lesssim_{p}\mathrm{Tr}(\Upsilon)^{\frac{p}{2}}N^{\frac{p}{2}}_{C}.

Therefore

\mathbb{E}{\|\widetilde{F}-\widehat{F}\|}_{L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})}\lesssim_{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma^{p}\mathrm{Tr}(\Upsilon)^{\frac{p}{2}}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{\mu(C)}{N^{\frac{p}{2}}_{C}}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\Upsilon)n}{m}},

(4.5)

where we recall that
N_{C}=m/n
. In the Hilbert-valued noise case, since
\mathrm{Tr}(\Upsilon)=1
, this gives

\mathbb{E}{\|\widetilde{F}-\widehat{F}\|}_{L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})}\lesssim_{p}\sigma\sqrt{\frac{n}{m}}.

(4.6)

However, this analysis also applies to the white noise case, upon setting
\upsilon_{1}=\cdots=\upsilon_{r}=1
and
\upsilon_{i}=0
otherwise. We deduce that

\mathbb{E}{\|\widetilde{F}-\widehat{F}\|}_{L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})}\lesssim_{p}\sigma\sqrt{\frac{rn}{m}}

(4.7)

in the white noise case.

Now consider the second term in (
4.3
). We first write

{\|\mathcal{S}_{r}\circ F-\widetilde{F}\|}^{p}_{L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})}=\int_{D}{\|\mathcal{S}_{r}\circ F(X)-\widetilde{F}(X)\|}^{p}_{\mathcal{Y}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(X)+\int_{D^{c}}{\|\mathcal{S}_{r}\circ F(X)\|}^{p}_{\mathcal{Y}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(X).

For the second term, since
\mathcal{S}_{r}
is a projection, we have

{\|\mathcal{S}_{r}\circ F(X)\|}_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq{\|F(X)\|}_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq{\|F(X)\|}_{\mathcal{Y}^{t}}\leq B

and therefore

\int_{D^{c}}{\|\mathcal{S}_{r}\circ F(X)\|}^{p}_{\mathcal{Y}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(X)\leq\mu(D^{c})B^{p}.

Next, let
X\in C
for some
C\in\mathcal{C}
. Then, since
\mathcal{S}_{r}
is a projection and
F
is
L
-Lipschitz, we have

\displaystyle{\|\mathcal{S}_{r}\circ F(X)-\widetilde{F}(X)\|}_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq\frac{1}{N_{C}}\sum_{i:X_{i}\in C}{\|\mathcal{S}_{r}(F(X))-\mathcal{S}_{r}(F(X_{i}))\|}_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq L\max_{i:X_{i}\in C}{\|X-X_{i}\|}_{\mathcal{X}}.

Now, by construction,

{\|X-X_{i}\|}^{2}_{\mathcal{X}}=\sum^{d}_{j=1}|\langle X-X_{i},\phi_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}|^{2}+\sum_{j>d}|\langle X,\phi_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}|^{2}\leq 4R\sum^{d}_{j=1}\frac{\lambda_{j}}{n^{2}_{j}}+\sum_{j>d}|\langle X,\phi_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}|^{2}.

Therefore

\displaystyle{\|\mathcal{S}_{r}\circ F-\widetilde{F}\|}^{p}_{L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})}\lesssim_{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(L^{2}R\sum^{d}_{j=1}\frac{\lambda_{j}}{n^{2}_{j}}}}\right)^{p/2}+L^{p}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j>d}|\langle X,\phi_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}|^{2}}}\right)^{p/2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(X)+\mu(D^{c})B^{p}.

Consider the second term, which we denote as
I
for convenience. By assumption,
\langle X,\phi_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}=\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\xi_{j}
. Hence we may write this as

I=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j>d}\lambda_{j}|\xi_{j}|^{2}}}\right)^{p/2},

where the expectation is with respect to the
\xi_{j}
. Suppose first that
p\geq 2
. Then, by Minkoswki’s inequality and the moment assumption on the
\xi_{j}
, we have

I^{\frac{2}{p}}\leq\sum_{j>d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbb{E}(\lambda_{j}|\xi_{j}|^{2})^{\frac{p}{2}}}}\right]^{\frac{2}{p}}=\sum_{j>d}\lambda_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbb{E}|\xi_{j}|^{p}}}\right]^{\frac{2}{p}}\lesssim_{\xi,p}\sum_{j>d}\lambda_{j}

and therefore

I^{\frac{1}{p}}\lesssim_{\xi,p}\sqrt{\sum_{j>d}\lambda_{j}}.

Now suppose that
1\leq p\leq 2
. Then

I\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j>d}\lambda_{j}|\xi_{j}|^{2}}}\right)}}\right]^{\frac{p}{2}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j>d}\lambda_{j}\mathbb{E}|\xi_{j}|^{2}}}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}\lesssim_{\xi,p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j>d}\lambda_{j}}}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}},

and therefore, once more,

I^{\frac{1}{p}}\lesssim_{\xi,p}\sqrt{\sum_{j>d}\lambda_{j}}.

Substituting this into the previous expression, we see that

{\|\mathcal{S}_{r}\circ F-\widetilde{F}\|}_{L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})}\lesssim_{\xi,p}L\sqrt{R}\sqrt{\sum^{d}_{j=1}\frac{\lambda_{j}}{n^{2}_{j}}}+L\sqrt{\sum_{j>d}\lambda_{j}}+B(\mu(D^{c}))^{\frac{1}{p}}.

(4.8)

Substituting this, (
4.4
) and either (
4.6
) or (
4.7
) into (
4.3
) now gives the result.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Consider the probability distribution
\nu
on
\mathcal{X}
given by the law of the random variable

X=\sum^{d}_{i=1}\sqrt{R\lambda_{i}}\zeta_{i}\phi_{i},\quad\text{where }\zeta_{i}\sim_{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{U}([-1,1]).

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\sim_{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}\nu
.
Then the same conclusions as Lemma
4.3
hold, where the expectation is now also taken with respect to design points
X_{i}
in addition to the noise.

Proof.

We modify the proof of the previous lemma.
Consider the three terms on the right-hand side of (
4.3
). It follows from the derivations leading to (
4.4
) and (
4.8
) that these bounds for first and second terms of (
4.3
) are unaffected choice of design points. Therefore, we only need to show that the bounds (
4.6
),(
4.7
) for the third term remain valid for this choice of random design. Following the same arguments, we see that

\mathbb{E}{\|\widetilde{F}-\widehat{F}\|}^{p}_{L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})}\lesssim\sigma^{p}\mathrm{Tr}(\Upsilon)^{\frac{p}{2}}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\mu(C)\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{N^{\frac{p}{2}}_{C}}}}\right),

where we observe that
N_{C}
is now the random variable given by
N_{C}=|\{i:X_{i}\in C\}|
whenever at least one
X_{i}
belongs to
C
and
+\infty
otherwise. Let

p_{C}=\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\nu}(X\in C)=\frac{1}{n},

where the second equality follows from the definition of
\nu
. Then
N_{C}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(m,p_{C})
. A standard Chernoff bound gives that

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N_{C}\leq\frac{m}{2n}}}\right)\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{m}{8n}}}\right).

Therefore, we may write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{N^{p/2}_{C}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{N^{p/2}_{C}}\Bigg|N_{C}\leq\frac{m}{2n}}}\right)\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N_{C}\leq\frac{m}{2n}}}\right)+\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{N^{p/2}_{C}}\Bigg|N_{C}>\frac{m}{2n}}}\right)\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N_{C}>\frac{m}{2n}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{m}{8n}}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{m}{2n}}}\right)^{-\frac{p}{2}}

In other words, (
4.5
) remains valid in this setting. We immediately deduce that (

),(
4.7
) also hold. This completes the proof.

G(k,d(k))\asymp_{\alpha}(\log(k))^{\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}},

and therefore

\mathcal{L}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})\lesssim_{\alpha}(\log(m/\sigma^{2}))^{\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}},\quad m\rightarrow\infty,

as required.
∎

Proof of Theorems
2.9
and
2.10
; lower bounds.

The argument is similar to the previous case. Let
\mu_{d}=\sum^{d}_{j=1}1/\lambda_{j}
,
k=L^{2}m/(c\sigma^{2})
and use Theorem
3.1
to write

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{m}(\mathcal{F};L^{p}_{\mu})

\displaystyle\lesssim\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sqrt{\mu_{d}}}{L}}}\right)+\frac{d}{p}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{p+1}{\iota}}}\right)+\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{8}{a}}}\right),\frac{1}{2+d}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{k}{\mu_{d}}}}\right)-\frac{d}{2+d}\log(a)}}\right\}

\displaystyle=:G(k,d)

for all sufficiently large
d
. Now suppose once more that (
5.1
) holds, so that
G
satisfies (
5.2
). Observe that
\mu_{d}=\sum^{d}_{j=1}j^{\alpha}\sim\frac{d^{\alpha+1}}{\alpha+1}
,
d\rightarrow\infty
, and therefore

G(k,d)\sim\frac{d}{p}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{p+1}{\iota}}}\right)+\frac{\log(k)}{d}.

We now set (
5.3
) once more and argue in the same way to obtain

\mathcal{L}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})\lesssim_{\iota,p}\sqrt{\log(m/\sigma^{2})},\quad m\rightarrow\infty,

as required.
∎

As noted, we currently lack tight characterizations of the minimax risk for exponentially-decaying eigenvalues with
0<\beta<1
(see Remark
2.2
) or algebraically-decaying eigenvalues for any
\alpha>1
.
This stems from the factor
(\iota/(p+1))^{d}

in
3.1
. Since
\iota\leq 1
(see Assumption
2.1
), this factor is always exponentially-small in
d
. When the eigenvalues decay algebraically or exponentially with
0<\beta<1
, the decay of this term dominates in the asymptotic behaviour of the function
G(k,d)
. This is seen in the previous proofs. For exponential decay with
\beta\geq 1
this term no longer dominates, with the consequence being a tight characterization. We believe the factor
(\iota/(p+1))^{d}

is an artefact of the proof of Theorem
3.1
. Removing it is an objective of future work.

5.2
Upper bounds

Proof of Theorem
2.5
; upper bound.

We may assume without loss of generality that
B,L\geq 1
, since this only potentially increases the size of the set
\mathcal{F}_{B,L}
.
Suppose that
\lambda_{i}=\exp(-\alpha i^{\beta})
. Then

\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{d}\leq\exp(-c_{\alpha,\beta}d^{\beta+1}),

for some constant
c_{\alpha,\beta}>0

and

\displaystyle\sum_{j>d}\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\int^{\infty}_{d}\exp(-\alpha t^{\beta})\,\mathrm{d}t.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta\alpha^{1/\beta}}\int^{\infty}_{\alpha d^{\beta}}u^{1/\beta-1}\exp(-u)\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta\alpha^{1/\beta}}\Gamma(1/\beta,\alpha d^{\beta}),

where
\Gamma
denotes the upper incomplete Gamma function. It is known that
\Gamma(a,z)\sim z^{a-1}\mathrm{e}^{-z}
as
z\rightarrow\infty
. We deduce that

\sum_{j>d}\lambda_{j}\lesssim_{\alpha,\beta}d^{1-\beta}\exp(-\alpha d^{\beta}).

Let
k=m/\sigma^{2}
. Then Theorem
4.1
gives that

\mathcal{M}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})\lesssim_{\alpha,\beta,B,L,p}d^{\frac{4}{2(p+2)}}k^{-\frac{2}{(p+2)d+4}}\exp(-c_{\alpha,\beta,p}d^{\beta})+d^{1/2-\beta/2}\exp(-c_{\alpha,\beta,p}d^{\beta})

for some constant
c_{\alpha,\beta,p}>0
, provided

k\geq\exp(c^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta,p}d^{\beta+1})

for some constant
c^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta,p}>0
. Here we used the facts that
B,L\geq 1
and
d\geq 1
.
Set

d=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log(k)}{c^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta,p}}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta+1}}}}\right\rfloor.

For sufficiently large
m
, we have
k,d\geq 1
and therefore

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{m}(F_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})

\displaystyle\lesssim d\exp(-c_{\alpha,\beta,p}d^{\beta})\lesssim_{\alpha,\beta,p}(\log(m/\sigma^{2}))^{\frac{1}{\beta+1}}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-c^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha,\beta,p}(\log(m/\sigma^{2}))^{\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}}}}\right),\quad m\rightarrow\infty.

Consequently,

\mathcal{L}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})\gtrsim_{\alpha,\beta,p}(\log(m/\sigma^{2}))^{\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}},\quad m\to\infty,

as required.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.6
; upper bound.

We proceed as in the previous proof, using Theorem
4.2
in place of Theorem
4.1
and with
k=m/(r\sigma^{2})
.
Choosing the same value for
d
and noting that
w_{r}=r
by assumption, we deduce that

\mathcal{M}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})\lesssim_{\alpha,\beta,B,L,p}r^{-t}+\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-c^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha,\beta,p}(\log(k))^{\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}}}}\right),\quad k\rightarrow\infty,

and therefore

\mathcal{L}_{m}(\mathcal{F}^{t}_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})\gtrsim_{\alpha,\beta,p}\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(\log(k))^{\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}},t\log(r)}}\right\}=\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log(m/\sigma^{2})-\log(r)}}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}},t\log(r)}}\right\}.

This holds for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Now let
r=r(m)
the smallest integer such that

\log(r)\geq(\log(m/\sigma^{2}))^{\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}}.

Notice that
\log(r)=o(\log(m/\sigma^{2}))
as
m\rightarrow\infty
. The result now follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.9
; upper bound.

We proceed in a similar manner. Let
\lambda_{i}=i^{-\alpha}
and notice that

\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{d}=(d!)^{-\alpha}\leq(d/\mathrm{e})^{-\alpha d}

and

\sum_{j>d}\lambda_{j}\lesssim_{\alpha}d^{1-\alpha}

Hence Theorem
4.1
gives that

\mathcal{M}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})\lesssim_{\alpha,B,L,p}dk^{-\frac{2}{(p+2)d+4}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d}{\mathrm{e}}}}\right)^{-\frac{\alpha d}{(p+2)d+4}}+d^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}},

provided

k\geq(d/\mathrm{e})^{-\alpha d/2}d^{\alpha\frac{(p+2)d+4}{2(p+2)}-\frac{4}{p+2}}.

This is equivalent to

\log(k)\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}d+\frac{2(\alpha-2)}{p+2}\log(d).

(5.4)

Set

d=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor\frac{4}{(p+2)+\alpha p}\frac{\log(k)}{\log(\log(k))}}}\right\rfloor

and observe that (
5.4
) holds for all sufficiently large
k
. Also note that
d\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
. Consequently, substituting this value into the above error bound, we deduce that

\mathcal{M}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})\lesssim_{\alpha,B,L,p}dk^{-\frac{2}{(p+2)d}}d^{-\frac{\alpha}{p+2}}+d^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\lesssim_{\alpha,p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log(k)}{\log(\log(k))}}}\right)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}},\quad k\to\infty,

as required.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.10
; upper bound.

We proceed as in the previous proof, to deduce that

\mathcal{L}_{m}(\mathcal{F}^{t}_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})\lesssim_{\alpha,B,L,p}r^{-t}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log(k)}{\log(\log(k))}}}\right)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}},

as
k\rightarrow\infty
, where
k=m/(r\sigma^{2})
. Therefore,

\mathcal{M}_{m}(\mathcal{F}^{t}_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})\geq\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log(k)}{\log(\log(k))}}}\right),t\log(r)}}\right\}+\mathcal{O}_{\alpha,B,L,p}(1).

Now let
r=r(m)
be the smallest integer such that

t\log(r)\leq\frac{\alpha-1}{2}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log(m/\sigma^{2})}{\log(\log(m/\sigma^{2}))}}}\right).

Observe that
k\rightarrow\infty
as
m\rightarrow\infty
with this choice and also that

\frac{\log(k)}{\log(\log(k))}\sim\frac{\log(m/\sigma^{2})}{\log(\log(m/\sigma^{2}))},\quad m\rightarrow\infty.

We deduce that

\mathcal{L}_{m}(\mathcal{F}^{t}_{B,L};\mathcal{L}^{p}_{\mu})\geq\frac{\alpha-1}{2}\log(\log(m/\sigma^{2}))+\mathcal{O}_{\alpha,\beta,B,L,p}(1),

as required.
∎

The above proofs of the upper bounds in Theorems
2.6
and
2.10
indicate how the choice of the weights
w_{i}
in the smoothness spaces (
2.6
) can influence the bounds. In general, we have that the log-minimax risk
\mathcal{L}_{m}(\mathcal{F}^{t}_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})
in the white noise case is bounded below by (up to potential constants)

\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\log(w_{r}),\mathcal{R}(\log(m/\sigma^{2})-\log(r))}}\right\},

where
\mathcal{R}:(0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
is some increasing function which provides the
analogous term in the lower bound for
\mathcal{L}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})
in the case of Hilbert-valued Gaussian noise. Proposition
2.4
asserts that
\mathcal{R}
must grow sublinearly, i.e.,
\mathcal{R}(z)=o(z)
as
z\rightarrow\infty
.
We are interested in when the lower bound in the white noise case matches that in the Hilbert-valued Gaussian noise case. This holds whenever it is possible to choose
r=r(m)
such that

\log(r)\leq c\log(m/\sigma^{2}),\quad\log(w_{r})\gtrsim\mathcal{R}(\log(m/\sigma^{2})),

(5.5)

for some
0<c<1
and all sufficiently large
m
.
This clearly holds when
w_{i}=i
, as considered in this work. Slower growth of
w_{i}
is also possible, depending on behaviour of
\mathcal{R}
. For example, if
\mathcal{R}(z)\asymp\log(z)/\log(\log(z))
,
as in the case of algebraically-decaying eigenvalues (see Theorem
2.9
), then the choice
w_{r}=\log(r)
also satisfies (
5.5
).

6
Further discussion

Before concluding, we now discuss several further topics.

6.1
Finite-dimensional rates

Although it is not the primary focus of this paper, we now briefly show that our main upper and lower bounds yield the well-known minimax risks for finite-dimensional Lipschitz functions on bounded domains. To this end, let
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}
, both equipped with the Euclidean inner product, and suppose that
\mu
is the uniform probability measure on
[-1,1]^{d}
. Notice that
a=1
,
b=1/2
and
\lambda_{1}=\cdots=\lambda_{d}=1
in this case.
Theorem
3.1
gives the lower bound

\mathcal{M}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})\gtrsim_{d,p,L,B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{m}{\sigma^{2}}}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2+d}},\qquad m\rightarrow\infty.

Technically speaking, this is only true if
(
3.2
) holds, i.e.,
B\geq L(8\sqrt{d})
. However, this poses no issue thanks to Remark
3
.

We now establish the matching upper bound. On the fact of it, Theorem
4.1
would yield a suboptimal rate depending on
p
. This stems from the general types of measures
\mu
allowed in this theorem. However, applying Lemma
4.3
or
4.4
with
R>1
and the choice
n_{1}=\cdots=n_{d}=\lfloor n^{\frac{1}{d}}\rfloor
immediately yields the upper bound

\mathcal{M}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})\lesssim_{d,p,L,B}\sqrt{\frac{n}{m}}\sigma+n^{-\frac{1}{d}},\qquad m\rightarrow\infty,

valid for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Optimizing with respect to
n
we deduce that

\mathcal{M}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})\lesssim_{d,p,L,B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{m}{\sigma^{2}}}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2+d}},\qquad m\rightarrow\infty.

Combining with the lower bound above, we now recover the known minimax rate

\mathcal{M}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{B,L};L^{p}_{\mu})\asymp_{d,p,L,B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{m}{\sigma^{2}}}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2+d}},\qquad m\rightarrow\infty.

6.2
Double-exponential decay

Suppose that
\lambda_{i}=\exp(-\exp(\alpha i))
has double-exponential decay. Then, working through similar calculations to those shown in Section
5
, one can establish the tight characterization of the log-minimax risk as

\mathcal{L}_{m}(\mathcal{F};L^{p}_{\mu})\asymp_{\alpha,p}\frac{\log(m/\sigma^{2})}{\log(\log(m/\sigma^{2}))},\quad m\rightarrow\infty,

for either
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{B,L}
or
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}^{t}_{B,L}
depending on the noise assumption.
This means that algebraic rates, while impossible due to Proposition
2.4
, can ‘nearly’ be achieved for sufficiently fast-decaying eigenvalues. Indeed, the above result implies that

\mathcal{M}_{m}(\mathcal{F};L^{p}_{\mu})\asymp C_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{m}{\sigma^{2}}}}\right)^{-\frac{C_{2}}{\log(m/\sigma^{2})}},\quad m\rightarrow\infty,

for constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
depending on the various parameters. For succinctness we omit the details of this calculation.

7
Conclusion

We developed a minimax theory for learning uniformly bounded Lipschitz operators from noisy input-output data when both the inputs and outputs may be infinite dimensional. Our general upper and lower bounds cover fixed and random designs and accommodate both Hilbert-valued Gaussian noise and Gaussian white noise. Our results make precise how statistical difficulty is governed by the eigenvalues of the covariance of
\mu
. Our results also recover classical finite-dimensional Lipschitz rates as a special case.

There are several open problems that remain. Most importantly, our lower bounds are not sharp for algebraically decaying eigenvalues and, more generally, for exponential decay with
0<\beta<1
. A key technical obstacle is the
d
-dependence in the current lower-bound argument. Removing it would likely lead to a sharp characterization in these regimes. In the algebraic case, we conjecture that the true minimax rate is polylogarithmic in the sample size, with an exponent determined by the decay rate of the eigenvalues. Even in regimes where our bounds match, such as exponential and double-exponential eigenvalue decay, identifying the optimal constant in the exponent appears nontrivial. Finally, for very rapidly decaying eigenvalues (such as double-exponential decay) one can achieve ‘nearly’ algebraic performance. Beyond Lipschitz operators, extending the framework to higher-regularity operator classes (e.g., the full scale of Hölder and Besov classes) is a natural direction for future work.
