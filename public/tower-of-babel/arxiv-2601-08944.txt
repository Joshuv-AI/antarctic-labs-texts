Multiple cluster algebra structures for triple crossing diagram maps I: theoretical framework
Niklas Affolter, Terrence George, Max Glick, Sanjay Ramassamy

Abstract
. We introduce triple crossing diagram (TCD) maps, which encode projective configurations of points and lines, as a unified framework for constructions arising in various areas of geometry, such as discrete differential geometry, discrete geometric dynamics and hyperbolic geometry. We define two types of local moves for TCD maps, one of which is governed by the discrete Schwarzian KP (dSKP) equation, and establish their multi-dimensional consistency. We construct two distinct cluster structures on the space of TCD maps, called projective and affine cluster structures, and show that they are related via an operation called section. This framework organizes and unifies a wide range of examples, including Q-nets, Darboux maps, line complexes, T-graphs, t-embeddings, triangulations and geometric discrete integrable systems such as the pentagram map and cross-ratio dynamics, which are further developed in the companion paper [ 10 ] and in [ 8 ] .

Introduction

Cluster algebras
, introduced by Fomin and Zelevinsky

Let us mention three major directions of research that have involved such cluster structures:

•

Grassmannians:
the parametrization of the totally positive Grassmannian and its positroid cells via plabic graphs

•

[

•

Recently, the notion of a

was introduced in
[

. VRCs possess a cluster strcuture and provide a unified framework encompassing both the Grassmannian and discrete dynamics cases. However, the cluster structures arising from dimer embeddings exhibit fundamentally different behavior and do not fit in the VRC framework. The purpose of this paper is to introduce a modification of VRCs, which we call

In this way, TCD maps also encompass a fourth direction of research, thereby also establishing the existence of cluster structures in these systems.

to a fixed projective space
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}
such that, at every black vertex, the images of the three adjacent white vertices lie on a common projective line. The name reflects that BTB graphs are in bijection with Thurston’s triple crossing diagrams (TCDs)

TCD maps are the projectivized form of VRCs together with the additional trivalence condition at black vertices (whereas VRCs are defined on general planar bipartite graphs called
plabic graphs
). VRCs have several local moves: the
spider move
and the mutually inverse operations of
splitting
and
contracting
vertices. When restricted to BTB graphs, only two moves remain: the
spider move
and the
resplit
, which is the composition of a contraction followed by a split (see Figure
2
). Moreover, any plabic graph can be transformed into a BTB graph through a sequence of splits and contractions; thus, BTB graphs, although being a subclass, possess the same scope as plabic graphs. On the level of TCD maps, the spider move leaves all white vertices fixed, acting merely as a reparametrization of the TCD map, while the resplit replaces a white vertex with a new one which is mapped to the intersection of the two lines associated with the adjacent black vertices.

As mentioned earlier, TCD maps possess two cluster structures. The first cluster structure, which we call the
projective cluster structure
, is the cluster structure for VRCs was introduced in
[
11
]
reinterpreted in the framework of TCD maps. The
projective quiver
of a BTB graph
{G}
is the oriented dual graph of
{G}
, so the cluster variables are naturally assigned to the faces of
{G}
(see Figure
1
(left)). For a face

projective cluster variable

X_{f}
is defined in terms of a multi-ratio:

X_{f}:=-\prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\mathsf{T}(w_{i-1})-\mathsf{T}(v_{i})}{\mathsf{T}(w_{i})-\mathsf{T}(v_{i})},

with the labeling of vertices as in Figure
3
(left) and the expression on the right is computed in any affine chart of
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}
. The terminology “projective” reflects that the cluster variables, being multi-ratios, are invariant under projective transformations. It was shown in
[

that the projective cluster variables transform under the spider move according to the mutation formula for

1.2.
Affine cluster structures

Our first contribution is the introduction of a second cluster structure for TCD maps, which we call the
affine cluster structure
. Fix a hyperplane
{\mathsf{H}}\subset\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}
and consider a TCD map
\mathsf{T}
in the affine chart of
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}
for which
{\mathsf{H}}
is the hyperplane at infinity. The
affine quiver
has a vertex for each white vertex of
{G}
(see Figure
1
(center)) and the associated
affine cluster variable
is defined by the following
star-ratio
:

Y_{w}:=-\prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\mathsf{T}(w_{i})-\mathsf{T}(w)}{\mathsf{T}(w^{\prime}_{i})-\mathsf{T}(w)},

with the labeling of vertices as in Figure
3
(right). The terminology “affine” reflects that these cluster variables are invariant under affine transformations (i.e. projective transformations that fix
{\mathsf{H}}
). We show that affine cluster variables transform under the resplit according to the mutation formula for
X
cluster variables but are invariant under spider moves. In this sense, affine and projective cluster structures exhibit opposite behavior: the affine structure mutates under resplits but not spider moves, whereas the projective structure mutates under spider moves but not resplits. Since the definition of affine cluster variables requires black vertices to be trivalent, this provides an additional reason for working with BTB graphs rather than general plabic graphs.

The affine cluster structure is what brings embeddings, Grassmannians and discrete dynamics under a unified framework: in particular, we show that any t-embedding (a special case of a dimer embedding) can be realized as a TCD map
\mathsf{T}
whose known cluster structure coincides with the affine cluster structure of
\mathsf{T}
.

1.3.
Sections and the correspondence between cluster structures

Our second contribution is the introduction of a geometric operation on TCD maps called a
section
. As before, let
{\mathsf{H}}\subset\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}
be a hyperplane. Informally, a section is a map
\sigma_{\mathsf{H}}(\mathsf{T})
that assigns to each black vertex
b
of
{G}
the point where the line associated with
b
meets
{\mathsf{H}}
. We show that a section is again a TCD map on one of several possible related graphs
\sigma({G})
, where the different choices of
\sigma({G})
are related by resplits. Closely related constructions also appear in
[
35
,
36
]
. There, it is shown that two iterations of the section preserves positivity of the cluster variables. The combinatorial operation sending
{G}
to
\sigma({G})
is also called the
shift-by-1 map

[
34
]
and
T
-duality

[
61
,
18
]
.

If
\mathsf{T}
is a map to
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}
, we refer to
d
as its
rank
. Since a section of
\mathsf{T}
has rank
d-1
, we can iteratively take
d-2
sections, each of which possesses a projective and an affine cluster structure. Our third contribution is the following correspondence between projective and affine cluster structures:

The affine cluster structure of a TCD map agrees with the projective cluster structure of a section.

See also Figure
1
(center and right) and Theorem
9.1
. Therefore, TCD maps have
d
cluster structures and every affine cluster structure is also a projective cluster structure though of a different TCD map, except in the case of TCD maps of rank 1, where no nontrivial sections exist; notably, this case includes the important example of t-embeddings.

1.4.
Multi-dimensional consistency

Given a BTB graph
{G}
, its
move graph
is the graph whose vertices represent all BTB graphs obtained from
{G}
by sequences of local moves, with edges corresponding to individual moves. Cycles in the move graph based at
{G}
thus correspond to sequences of moves that return
{G}
to itself. Thurston
[
59
]
conjectured that all such cycles are generated by three elementary ones, a result recently proved by Balitsky and Wellmann
[
13
]
. Our fourth main contribution is that TCD maps exhibit the following property:

TCD maps are invariant under any sequence of moves forming a cycle.

The proof is a consequence of the classical Desargues’ theorem. This property is known as
multi-dimensional consistency
; see Theorem
6.8
.

Typically, in discrete integrable systems multi-dimensional consistency is studied on maps defined on lattices. There is a well-known way to label vertices of
{G}
, introduced in the context of the cluster structure on the Grassmannian
[
58
]
and studied in relation to weak separation
[
53
]
. In our context, this labeling realizes TCD maps as maps on the root lattice of type
A_{n}
. This lattice perspective allows us to compare TCD maps with other geometric maps defined on
A_{n}
lattices studied in discrete integrable systems, which results in our fifth contribution:

TCD maps are the restrictions of
Desargues maps
. Moreover, coordinate projections of TCD maps form
dSKP lattices
.

See also Theorem
6.15
and Theorem
6.18
, where we show that this statement is also compatible with local moves in an appropriate sense.
Desargues maps were introduced by Doliwa
[
23
,
24
]
as maps to
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}
defined by incidence relations, while dSKP lattices arise as a discretization of the Schwarzian KP equation
[
25
,
52
,
46
]
.

Background

In this section, we recall the necessary background on projective geometry, triple crossing diagrams, black-trivalent bipartite graphs and vector-relation configurations.

2.1.
Projective and affine geometry

To simplify exposition, we work over
\mathbb{C}
in this paper. However, all results and definitions remain valid over
\mathbb{R}
(and, in fact, over any field, though in some cases the statements may become trivial).

For
d\geq 1
,
projective space

\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}
is defined as the space of all lines through the origin in
\mathbb{C}^{d+1}
. A point in
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}
is represented by an equivalence class

[x]=[x_{1},\cdots,x_{d+1}],

where
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d+1})\in\mathbb{C}^{d+1}\setminus\{0\}
, and two vectors are identified if they are linearly dependent, that is,

(x_{1},\dots,x_{d+1})\sim\lambda(x_{1},\dots,x_{d+1})\quad\text{for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}=\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}.

A
projective transformation
is an invertible linear transformation of
\mathbb{C}^{d+1}
modulo scalars, that is, an element of

\mathrm{PGL}(d+1,\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{GL}(d+1,\mathbb{C})/\mathbb{C}^{*}.

An
affine chart
on
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}
is given by choosing a hyperplane
{\mathsf{H}}\subset\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}
and identifying the complement
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}\setminus{\mathsf{H}}
with affine space
\mathbb{C}^{d}
via a choice of coordinates. We assume in the following that
{\mathsf{H}}
is the hyperplane
x_{d+1}=0
, giving the
standard affine chart

\{[x]\in\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}:x_{d+1}\neq 0\}

which is identified with
\mathbb{C}^{d}
via the map

[x_{1},\cdots,x_{d+1}]\longmapsto\left(\frac{x_{1}}{x_{d+1}},\dots,\frac{x_{d}}{x_{d+1}}\right).

An
affine transformation
is an element of
\mathrm{PGL}(d+1,\mathbb{C})
of the form

\begin{bmatrix}A&b\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{mod }\mathbb{C}^{*},

with
A\in\mathrm{GL}(d,\mathbb{C})
and
b
a
d
-dimensional column vector. The subgroup of affine transformations is precisely the stabilizer of the hyperplane
{\mathsf{H}}
. In other words, affine transformations preserve the standard affine chart.

Let
{\mathsf{Z}}\in\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}
be a point called the
center
and let
{\mathsf{H}}\subset\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}
be a hyperplane not containing
{\mathsf{Z}}
. The
central projection
from
{\mathsf{Z}}
onto
{\mathsf{H}}
is the rational map

\pi_{{\mathsf{Z}},{\mathsf{H}}}:\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}\dashrightarrow{\mathsf{H}},

defined by sending a point
{\mathsf{P}}\in\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}\setminus\{{\mathsf{Z}}\}
to the intersection point with
{\mathsf{H}}
of the line joining
{\mathsf{Z}}
to
{\mathsf{P}}
. More generally, we define central projection from a subspace as follows.

Definition 2.1

(Central projection)
.

Let
\mathsf{U}\subset\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}
be a projective subspace of dimension
d-r-1
(the
center
),
and let
\mathsf{V}\subset\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}
be a complementary subspace of dimension
r
(i.e.
\mathsf{U}\cap\mathsf{V}=\varnothing
). The
central projection

\pi_{\mathsf{U}}
from
\mathsf{U}
onto
\mathsf{V}
is the rational map

\pi_{\mathsf{U}}:\ \mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}\dashrightarrow\mathsf{V}\cong\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{r},

that sends every point
{\mathsf{P}}\in\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}\setminus\mathsf{U}
to the intersection of
\mathsf{V}
with the projective span of
\mathsf{U}
and
{\mathsf{P}}
.

Since we will quotient by the action of
\operatorname{PGL}(r{+}1)
on the target, the specific choice of
\mathsf{V}

and of the identification
\mathsf{V}\cong\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{r}

\pi_{\mathsf{U}}
instead of
\pi_{\mathsf{U},\mathsf{V}}
.
It is also useful to describe central projections in homogeneous coordinates. Specifically, if
\tilde{\mathsf{U}}\subset\mathbb{C}^{d+1}
is the linear subspace corresponding to
\mathsf{U}
, then
\pi_{\mathsf{U}}
is the projectivization of the linear quotient

(2.1)

\tilde{\pi}_{\tilde{\mathsf{U}}}:\mathbb{C}^{d+1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d+1}/\tilde{\mathsf{U}}\cong\mathbb{C}^{r+1}.

The following geometric ratios will be used to define cluster variables associated to TCD maps.

Definition 2.2

(Oriented length ratio)
.

Let
{\mathsf{P}}_{1},{\mathsf{P}}_{2},{\mathsf{P}}_{3}
be points on a line
{\mathsf{L}}
in
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}
and assume we have a fixed affine chart

\lambda({\mathsf{P}}_{1},{\mathsf{P}}_{2},{\mathsf{P}}_{3})

\displaystyle\lambda({\mathsf{P}}_{1},{\mathsf{P}}_{2},{\mathsf{P}}_{3})=\frac{{\mathsf{P}}_{1}-{\mathsf{P}}_{3}}{{\mathsf{P}}_{2}-{\mathsf{P}}_{3}},

where subtraction and division are in the affine chart

induced by the intersection
{\mathsf{L}}\cap\mathbb{C}^{d}

The oriented length ratio is an affine invariant. However, it depends on the choice of affine chart and is not invariant under general projective transformations.

Definition 2.3

(Multi-ratio)
.

Let
{\mathsf{P}}_{1},{\mathsf{P}}_{1,2},{\mathsf{P}}_{2},{\mathsf{P}}_{2,3},\dots,{\mathsf{P}}_{m},{\mathsf{P}}_{m,1}

\displaystyle\mr({\mathsf{P}}_{1},{\mathsf{P}}_{1,2},{\mathsf{P}}_{2},\dots,{\mathsf{P}}_{m},{\mathsf{P}}_{m,1})=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{{\mathsf{P}}_{i}-{\mathsf{P}}_{i,i+1}}{{\mathsf{P}}_{i,i+1}-{\mathsf{P}}_{i+1}}=(-1)^{m}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\lambda({\mathsf{P}}_{i},{\mathsf{P}}_{i+1},{\mathsf{P}}_{i,i+1}).

\cro({\mathsf{P}}_{1},{\mathsf{P}}_{1,2},{\mathsf{P}}_{2},{\mathsf{P}}_{2,1})=\frac{{\mathsf{P}}_{1}-{\mathsf{P}}_{1,2}}{{\mathsf{P}}_{1,2}-{\mathsf{P}}_{2}}\frac{{\mathsf{P}}_{2}-{\mathsf{P}}_{2,1}}{{\mathsf{P}}_{2,1}-{\mathsf{P}}_{1}}.

2.2.
Triple crossing diagrams

What we refer to as a
triple crossing diagram
was originally introduced by Dylan Thurston
[
59
]
under the names
triple-point diagram
and
triple diagram
. These diagrams are also sometimes called
Thurston diagrams

[
27
]
, and the term triple crossing diagram has appeared in the literature in
[
17
,
13
]
. In what follows, we recall several basic definitions and results from Thurston’s paper, before relating triple crossing diagrams to vector-relation configurations in the next section.

Definition 2.5

(Triple crossing diagrams)
.

A
triple crossing diagram
or
TCD
for short is a collection
\mathcal{T}
of oriented closed intervals and oriented circles immersed smoothly into a disk
\mathbb{D}
. The image of a connected component is called a
strand
; it is either an
arc
(the image of an interval) or a
loop
(the image of a circle). The immersions are required to satisfy:

(1)

Exactly three strands cross at each point of intersection.

(2)

The endpoints of arcs are distinct points on the boundary of the disk, and no other points map to the boundary.

(3)

The orientations of the strands induce consistent orientations on faces of
\mathcal{T}
(that is, on the complementary regions of
\mathcal{T}
).

Let
{\mathcal{T}_{\bm{\circlearrowright}}}
(resp.
{\mathcal{T}_{\bm{\circlearrowleft}}}
) denote the set of clockwise (resp. counterclockwise) faces of
\mathcal{T}
, including the boundary faces.

TCDs are considered up to ambient isotopies of
\mathbb{D}
. Due to Condition (3), the endpoints of strands have to alternate in orientation between sources and sinks around the boundary of the disk. Suppose there are
n
strands that are arcs, so there are
n
endpoints that are sources and
n
that are sinks. We label the sources (resp. sinks) by
{1},\dots,{n}
(resp.
\overline{1},\dots,\overline{n}
) in clockwise order such that source
{i}
is between sinks
\overline{i}
and
\overline{i+1}
. We label arcs by the labels of their sources.

Definition 2.6

(Strand permutation of a TCD)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a TCD with
n
strands that are arcs. Let
C_{\mathcal{T}}\in S_{n}
be the permutation defined by

C_{\mathcal{T}}(i)=j\text{ if there is a strand from source $i$ to sink $\overline{j}$}.

We call
C_{\mathcal{T}}
the
strand permutation
of
\mathcal{T}
.
If
1\leq k\leq n
, we denote by
{\mathcal{S}_{k}^{n}}
the permutation sending any
i
to
i+k\mod n
.

Definition 2.7

(Minimal TCDs)
.

A
minimal
TCD is a TCD with no loops and the fewest triple crossings among all TCDs with the same strand permutation.

Minimal TCDs can be characterized by the absence of certain forbidden configurations of strands.

Theorem 2.8

(
[
59

A TCD is minimal if and only if

(1)

No strand is a loop.

(2)

No strand has a self-intersection.

(3)

No pair of strands forms a parallel bigon, that is, there are no two strands
\alpha,\beta
and two triple crossings
x

with both strands oriented from

For the remainder of this paper, we will assume without further mention that

Theorem 2.9

(
[
59
, Theorem 3]
)
.

All
n!

Definition 2.10

(2-2 move)
.

A
2-2 move

s:\mathcal{T}\rightsquigarrow\tilde{\mathcal{T}}
is one of the two local moves shown in Figure

\mathcal{T}
with the same portion rotated by an angle of

The 2-2 move plays a central role in the geometric dynamics that we study. Note that the orientation of the bigon is preserved under the move. Since we distinguish between orientations, we will treat the 2-2 move applied to a clockwise bigon as different from the move applied to a counterclockwise bigon. Definition 2.12

Definition 2.13

(Black-trivalent bipartite graphs)

Definition 2.17

3.
Triple crossing diagram maps and vector-relation configurations

In this section, we introduce TCD maps and describe their relationship with VRCs.

Definition 3.1

(Triple crossing diagram maps)
.

Let
d\geq 1
and let
\mathcal{T}
be a TCD and
{G}
its BTB graph. A
triple crossing diagram map
or
TCD map
is a map

\mathsf{T}:{\mathcal{T}_{\bm{\circlearrowright}}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d},

that assigns a point in
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}
to each clockwise face of
\mathcal{T}
such that at every triple crossing, the three points associated to the three adjacent clockwise faces are distinct and lie on a line.

Equivalently, identifying clockwise faces with white vertices in the BTB graph associated to
\mathcal{T}
, a TCD map is a map

\mathsf{T}:W\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d},

assigning a point in
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}
to each white vertex, such that if a black vertex
b
is incident to white vertices
w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}
, then the points
\mathsf{T}(w_{1}),\mathsf{T}(w_{2}),\mathsf{T}(w_{3})
are distinct and lie on a line
{\mathsf{L}}_{b}
.

Let us also fix notation for the moduli space of TCD maps.

Definition 3.2

(Rank and moduli space of TCD maps)
.

The
rank
of a TCD map
\mathsf{T}
, denoted
\operatorname{rk}(\mathsf{T})
, is the dimension of the span of its image points.
We denote by

\mathrm{Moduli}(\mathcal{T},d):=\Bigl\{\text{TCD maps $\mathsf{T}$ on $\mathcal{T}$ with $\operatorname{rk}(\mathsf{T})=d$}\Bigr\}\big/\operatorname{PGL}(d+1),

the
moduli space of rank-
d
TCD maps on
\mathcal{T}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

There is a one-to-one correspondence between TCD maps and gauge-equivalence classes of circuit VRCs.

Proof.

Given a circuit configuration
({\mathsf{v}},{\mathsf{R}})
on a BTB graph
{G}
, we obtain a TCD map by setting

\mathsf{T}(w):=[{\mathsf{v}}(w)]\in\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d},

for all white vertices
w
, where
[{\mathsf{v}}(w)]
is the projectivization of
{\mathsf{v}}(w)
. If
w,w^{\prime}
are adjacent to the same black vertex, then
\mathsf{T}(w)
and
\mathsf{T}(w^{\prime})
are different points due to the circuit condition. Gauge-equivalent circuit configurations give rise to the same TCD map, hence,
\mathsf{T}
is well-defined.

Conversely, let
\mathsf{T}
be a TCD map. For each white vertex
w\in W
, choose a lift
{\mathsf{v}}(w)\in\mathbb{C}^{d+1}\setminus\{0\}
of the point
\mathsf{T}(w)\in\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d}
.
For each black vertex
b
adjacent to three white vertices
w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}
, the condition that
\mathsf{T}(w_{1}),\mathsf{T}(w_{2}),\mathsf{T}(w_{3})
lie on a line is equivalent to the existence of a linear dependence relation

{\mathsf{R}}(b):\quad\alpha_{1}{\mathsf{v}}(w_{1})+\alpha_{2}{\mathsf{v}}(w_{2})+\alpha_{3}{\mathsf{v}}(w_{3})=0,\qquad\text{for some $\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}\in\mathbb{C}$},

on lifts. The condition that the points are distinct implies that
\{{\mathsf{v}}(w_{1}),{\mathsf{v}}(w_{2}),{\mathsf{v}}(w_{3})\}
forms a circuit and in particular that
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}
are nonzero.
Since different choices of lifts and different rescalings of the relations are gauge transformations, this defines a VRC on
{G}
uniquely up to gauge. ∎

We regard the TCD maps as the fundamental theoretical objects instead of the VRCs, since we are primarily interested in objects and maps in projective geometry. Many of the interesting structures we study later on, such as cluster structures and partition functions, are defined modulo gauge transformations of VRCs.
Considering TCD maps avoids having to quotient out by gauge transformations. Furthermore, local moves have a simpler geometric interpretation for TCD maps than for VRCs, see Section
5
. The bipartite graphs carrying VRCs may look more general, because their black vertices have arbitrary degree. However, contracting all the degree-two black vertices and repeatedly splitting degree-two white vertices to reduce the degree of black vertices that have more than three neighbors, as in Figure
8
, one obtains a bipartite graph with trivalent black vertices carrying a VRC equivalent to the original one.

Remark 3.4
.

In general, we attempt to minimize calculations and rely on incidence theorems and a flexible collection of lemmas. However, on occasions where we need to resort to explicit calculations, working with VRCs is the most practical approach.

There is a particular gauge that is useful both for explicit calculations and for the affine cluster structures that we introduce in Section
7.3
.

Definition 3.5

(Affine gauge)
.

Consider a VRC
({\mathsf{v}},{\mathsf{R}})
on a BTB graph
{G}
. The edge weights
\mu
of the VRC are said to be in

if

(3.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{bw\in E}\mu(bw)=0

for every

.

Let us explain why we call this affine gauge.

Definition 3.6

(Generic hyperplane with respect to a TCD map)
.

Let
\mathsf{T}
be a TCD map. We say that a hyperplane

is

if it does not contain any of the points of
\mathsf{T}
.

Suppose
\mathsf{T}
is a TCD map and

is a hyperplane that is generic with respect to

be a VRC of

so that their

The perfect orientation

By induced, we mean that

if and only if there is an oriented path from

By Lemma

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a TCD. Every TCD map
\mathsf{T}
on

\operatorname{rk}(\mathsf{T})\leq\operatorname{rk}(\mathcal{T}).

Proof.

In the orientation

. Let

be the other two neighbors of

∎

Motivated by Proposition

\operatorname{rk}(\mathsf{T})=\operatorname{rk}(\mathcal{T}).

Definition 4.6

(1)

(3)

Let

\mathsf{T}(w_{1})

(a)

(b)

Proposition 4.7

The moduli space
\mathrm{Moduli}(\mathcal{T},d)

(d-1)(\operatorname{rk}(\mathcal{T})+1)-(d+1)^{2}+1+|W|.

Proof.

Theorem 6.8
.

Move-generic TCD maps on a TCD
\mathcal{T}
are consistent. Moreover, they form a non-empty Zariski-open subset of the space of TCD maps on
\mathcal{T}
.

Proof.

Recall from Remark
5.12
that the move graph
\mathfrak{F}_{1}
of
\mathcal{T}
is finite. Fix a spanning tree
\mathfrak{T}
of
\mathfrak{F}_{1}
rooted at
\mathcal{T}
. For each vertex
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, there is a unique path along
\mathfrak{T}
from
\mathcal{T}
to
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
. The set of TCD maps on
\mathcal{T}
such that all the TCD maps defined along these paths are valid forms a nonempty Zariski-open subset of the space of TCD maps on
\mathcal{T}
.

Considering an arbitrary path in
\mathfrak{F}_{1}
from
\mathcal{T}
to
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
does not lead to the appearance of any additional TCD maps. Indeed, the cycle in the move graph obtained by concatenating the latter path with the unique path in
\mathfrak{T}
from
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
to
\mathcal{T}
forms a cycle in
\mathfrak{F}_{1}
, which can be decomposed into finitely many elementary cycles by Theorem

. Lemma
6.7
then guarantees consistency along each elementary cycle, thus along the whole cycle.
∎

Remark 6.9
.

By Theorem

, it is natural and well-defined to label the points and lines of a TCD map by

like we did in the proof of Lemma

Remark 6.10
.

It is not surprising that consistency is governed by Desargues’ theorem. For instance, in the case of fundamental line complexes, it was already noted that multi-dimensional consistency is due to Desargues’ theorem in
[

. In the next section, we will study Desargues maps, which are so named because their consistency also follows from Desargues’ theorem. Moreover, by considering TCD maps in

[

]
.
Later, King and Schief showed that the consistency of the dSKP equation follows geometrically from a conformal version of Desargues’ theorem
[

6.3.
Desargues maps

Consider a TCD
\mathcal{T}
with corresponding BTB graph
{G}
and let
k=\operatorname{rk}(\mathcal{T})+1
. Recall that, via the map
{\bm{B}}
, we identify the set of white vertices of
{G}
with a subset of
A_{n-1}^{k-1}
.
The 2-cells of the
A_{n-1}^{k-1}
lattice can be partitioned into two families usually referred to as white triangles and black triangles. In this section, we need the
black triangles
, which are given by three lattice points of the form

\{z+e_{i}:i\in I\}\subset A_{n-1}^{k-1},

where
z\in A_{n-1}^{k-2}
and
I\subset\{1,2,\dots,n\}
is a
3
-element subset.

Definition 6.11

(Desargues map)
.

Let
L\subset A_{n-1}
. A
Desargues map
is a map

\mathsf{D}:L\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{d},

such that for every black triangle in
L
, the images of its three vertices under
\mathsf{D}
lie on a common line.

Desargues maps were originally introduced by Doliwa on
\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}
in a different form
[
23
]
. The equivalence with Definition
6.11
was established in
[
24
, Proposition 3.1]
.
The goal of this section is to establish a relationship between TCD maps and Desargues maps. In order to do this, we need to introduce some combinatorial notions that will only be used in the proofs of Lemmas
6.14
and
6.17
.
The following notion was introduced by Leclerc and Zelevinsky
[
49
]
.

Definition 6.12

(Weak separation)
.

We say that
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
are
weakly separated
if there does not exist four indices
i_{1}<i_{2}<i_{3}<i_{4}
, such that

z_{i_{1}}<z^{\prime}_{i_{1}},\quad z_{i_{2}}>z^{\prime}_{i_{2}},\quad z_{i_{3}}<z^{\prime}_{i_{3}},\quad z_{i_{4}}>z^{\prime}_{i_{4}}.

Theorem 6.13

(Oh, Postnikov and Speyer
[
53
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a minimal TCD with BTB graph
{G}
, and let
L\subset A_{n-1}^{k-1}
be the set of
{\bm{B}}
-labels of all white vertices that appear in all BTB graphs move-equivalent to
{G}
. Then every pairwise weakly separated collection in
L
that is maximal by inclusion is also maximal by size. Moreover, such a collection is the
{\bm{B}}
-image of the white vertices of a BTB graph
\tilde{G}
, unique up to spider moves, move-equivalent to
{G}
, and conversely.

Proof.

In
[
53
]
, the result is formulated in terms of face labels; however, it translates directly to white vertex labels, since, after reversing the orientation of all strands, faces and white vertices play equivalent roles in the context of TCDs.
∎

We also require the explicit construction of the graph
\tilde{G}
appearing in Theorem
6.13
due to
[
53
]
which we now describe. However, we rephrase their construction using white vertex labels. Let
\mathcal{D}
be a maximal by inclusion pairwise weakly separated collection in
L
. For
z\in\mathcal{D}
, let
S(z)
be the corresponding
(k-1)
-element subset of
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. For
K
a
k
-element subset of
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, define the corresponding
face clique

\mathcal{F}(K):=\{z\in\mathcal{D}:S(z)\subset K\}.

Similarly, for
K
a
(k-2)
-element subset, define the corresponding
black clique

\mathcal{B}(K):=\{z\in\mathcal{D}:S(z)\supset K\}.

A clique is called
non-trivial
if it contains at least
3
elements.
Note that
\mathcal{F}(K)
is of the form

\{z_{K}-e_{i_{1}},\dots,z_{K}-e_{i_{r}}\},

where
z_{K}:=\sum_{i\in K}e_{i}
and
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}
. Define its
boundary

\partial\mathcal{F}(K):=\{(z_{1},z_{2}),\dots,(z_{r},z_{1})\},\qquad z_{j}:=z_{K}-e_{i_{j}}.

Similarly, every black clique
\mathcal{B}(K)
is of the form

\{z_{K}+e_{i_{1}},\dots,z_{K}+e_{i_{r}}\}

for some
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}
. Define its
boundary

\partial\mathcal{B}(K):=\{(z_{1},z_{2}),\dots,(z_{r},z_{1})\},\qquad z_{j}:=z_{K}+e_{i_{j}}.

Let
\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the vertices of a convex
n
-gon and define the point
\bm{v}(z):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}\bm{v}_{i}
. The
plabic tiling
corresponding to
\mathcal{D}
is the
2
-dimensional complex
\Sigma(\mathcal{D})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that:

•

The vertices of
\Sigma(\mathcal{D})
are
\{\bm{v}(z):z\in\mathcal{D}\}
;

•

The edges of
\Sigma(\mathcal{D})
are of the form
\bm{v}(z)\bm{v}(z^{\prime})
where
(z,z^{\prime})
belongs to the boundary of a non-trivial clique;

•

The faces of
\Sigma(\mathcal{D})
are polygons whose vertices form a non-trivial clique.

The BTB graph
\tilde{G}
is obtained from

as follows. Triangulate the faces of
\Sigma(\mathcal{D})
that correspond to black cliques. Place a black vertex inside each triangular black face and a white vertex at each vertex of
\Sigma(\mathcal{D})
. Connect each black vertex to every white vertex lying on the boundary of the corresponding triangular face. Different choices of triangulations give rise to different

that are related by spider moves.

Lemma 6.14
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a minimal TCD with BTB graph
{G}
. Suppose that for some

and
\{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\}\subset\{1,2,\dots,n\}
,

Then there exists a BTB graph
\tilde{{G}}
move-equivalent to
{G}
containing a black vertex

such that

{\bm{B}}(b)=z,\qquad{\bm{B}}(w_{j})=z+e_{i_{j}},\qquad j=1,2,3.

Proof.

Since the collection
\{z+e_{i_{1}},z+e_{i_{2}},z+e_{i_{3}}\}
is weakly separated, it can be extended to a maximal weakly separated collection which corresponds to a BTB graph
\tilde{G}
by Theorem

. Moreover,
\{z+e_{i_{1}},z+e_{i_{2}},z+e_{i_{3}}\}\subset\mathcal{B}(K)
where

. Choose a triangulation of black cliques such that they form the vertices of a triangle and let

be the corresponding black vertex.
∎

With these combinatorial preliminaries in place, we can now state and prove a close connection between TCD maps and Desargues maps.

Theorem 6.15
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a minimal TCD with BTB graph
{G}
, and let

denote the set of

is a TCD map on a BTB graph

By Lemma

A consequence of Theorem

is that TCD maps can be interpreted as initial data for a Desargues map. The global propagation of a Desargues map from this initial data is governed by sequences of resplits and spider moves.

Recall from Section

Instead of Lemma

7.
Projective and affine cluster structures

In this section we endow TCD maps with two complementary cluster structures. The first, the projective cluster structure, is inherited from VRCs and is invariant under projective transformations. The second is new: the affine cluster structure depends on a choice of hyperplane at infinity and is invariant under affine transformations.

7.1.
Cluster algebras

We begin with a brief reminder on cluster algebras. Cluster algebras are a class of commutative algebras that were introduced by Fomin and Zelevinsky
[
30
]
. Unlike typical presentations of algebras in terms of generators and relations, a cluster algebra is described by providing a set of generators as follows. We start with an initial finite set of generators and produce recursively new generators via a combinatorial operation called mutation. In this section, we give an informal introduction to cluster algebras in the restricted setting arising from BTB graphs.,

Definition 7.1

(Quivers)
.

A
quiver
is an oriented graph without self-loops and oriented
2
-cycles. We denote the vertices and
arrows
(oriented edges) of a quiver
Q
by
Q_{0}
and
Q_{1}
respectively. The vertex set
Q_{0}
is partitioned into

Q_{0}=Q_{0}^{\text{mut}}\sqcup Q_{0}^{\text{fr}},

where the vertices in
Q_{0}^{\text{mut}}
(resp.
Q_{0}^{\text{fr}}
) are called
mutable
(resp.
frozen
).

In the present article, we only deal with the following special class of quivers.

Definition 7.2

(Planar dual-bipartite quivers)
.

A
planar dual-bipartite quiver
(or
PDB quiver
) is a quiver
Q
with a planar embedding such that around each vertex the arrows are alternatingly pointing outwards and inwards.

As a consequence of the definition, the faces of a PDB quiver may be be colored in a bipartite manner, that is, adjacent faces get different colors (for this, we do not count the outer face as a face of the quiver). Equivalently, the dual graph of a PDB quiver is a bipartite graph.

Figure 17
.
Mutation at a degree-4 vertex
v
in a PDB quiver.

Definition 7.3

(Mutation of a PDB quiver)
.

Let
Q
be a PDB quiver, and let
v\in Q_{0}
be a mutable vertex of degree
4
with cyclically ordered neighbors
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4}
, such that the arrows incident to
v
are

v_{1}\to v,\quad v\to v_{2},\quad v_{3}\to v,\quad v\to v_{4}.

The
mutation
of
Q
at
v
produces a new quiver
\tilde{Q}
as follows:

•

Reverse all arrows incident to
v
.

•

Add new arrows

v_{1}\to v_{2},\quad v_{1}\to v_{4},\quad v_{3}\to v_{2},\quad v_{3}\to v_{4}.

•

Remove any oriented
2
-cycles that are created.

See Figure
17
for an illustration.

Remark 7.4
.

In this paper, we restrict to mutations at degree-
4
mutable vertices only, as these correspond to spider moves in the associated BTB graph. On the other hand, in the general theory of cluster algebras, mutations are defined at all vertices of a quiver. A mutation at an arbitrary vertex of a PDB quiver may give quivers that are not PDB.

It is also possible to encode the orientations of the arrows in
Q
via an antisymmetric bilinear form

\displaystyle\nu\colon\mathbb{Z}^{Q_{0}}\times\mathbb{Z}^{Q_{0}}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle(v_{1},v_{2})

\displaystyle\longmapsto\#\{\text{arrows }v_{1}\to v_{2}\}-\#\{\text{arrows }v_{2}\to v_{1}\}.

Then
\nu
transforms under mutation at a vertex
v
according to the rule:

\tilde{\nu}({v_{1},v_{2}})=\begin{cases}-\nu({v_{1},v_{2}})&\text{if $v\in\{v_{1},v_{2}\}$},\\
\nu({v_{1},v_{2}})+\max(0,\nu({v_{1},v}))\max(0,\nu({v,v_{2}}))-\max(0,\nu({v_{2},v}))\max(0,\nu({v,v_{1}}))&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Definition 7.5

(
X
-cluster variables and their mutations)
.

The
X
-cluster variables
of a quiver
Q
are a collection of variables
X=\{X_{v}\}_{v\in Q_{0}^{\text{mut}}}
associated to the mutable vertices and taking values in
\mathbb{C}^{*}
. The
X
-cluster variables transform under mutation at a vertex
v
according to the following rule:

(7.1)

\tilde{X}_{v^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}X_{v}^{-1}&\text{if }v^{\prime}=v,\\
X_{v^{\prime}}(1+X_{v})^{\nu(v,v^{\prime})}&\text{if }\nu(v,v^{\prime})>0,\\
X_{v^{\prime}}(1+X_{v}^{-1})^{-\nu(v^{\prime},v)}&\text{if }\nu(v^{\prime},v)>0,\\
X_{v^{\prime}}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

A pair
(Q,X)
is called an
X
-seed
.

By starting from an initial seed and iterating mutations at every possible mutable vertex (regardless of its degree), one can generate all
X
-seeds of the cluster algebra, which may be infinite in number. Thus, a single initial
X
-seed
(Q,X)
determines the entire structure, and we refer to this data as a
cluster structure
.

Remark 7.6
.

The
X
-cluster variables define coordinates on an algebraic torus

\mathcal{X}_{Q}:=(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{Q_{0}^{\text{mut}}},

which comes equipped with a canonical Poisson structure. The mutation formula (
7.1
) defines Poisson birational transition maps between the tori
\mathcal{X}_{Q}
and
\mathcal{X}_{\tilde{Q}}
. These maps can be used to glue together the tori corresponding to all quivers obtained from an initial quiver by mutation, producing a space
\mathcal{X}
known as a
cluster Poisson variety

[
26
]
.
While the cluster variety and its Poisson structure on
\mathcal{X}
plays a crucial role in the connection between TCD maps and cluster integrable systems studied in
[
8
]
, it will not play a role in the present paper.

7.2.
The projective cluster structure of a TCD map

A cluster structure for VRCs was introduced in
[
11
]
; it induces the projective cluster structure of a TCD map.

(Projective cluster structure of a TCD map)

Let

be a VRC of

. For

Recall that we were able to write the projective cluster variables as products of oriented length-ratios. Similarly, we show that the affine cluster variables can be written in terms of (other) products of oriented length-ratios.

Definition 7.17

(Star-ratio)
.

Let
{\mathsf{P}},{\mathsf{P}}_{1},{\mathsf{P}}^{\prime}_{1},{\mathsf{P}}_{2},{\mathsf{P}}^{\prime}_{2},\dots,{\mathsf{P}}_{m},{\mathsf{P}}^{\prime}_{m}

(2)

(1) follows directly from the construction.

For (2), if

Remark 8.7

The following proposition shows how strands behave under taking a section of a TCD map.

Proposition 8.8
.

Let
\mathsf{T}
be a TCD map with BTB graph
{G}
. Assume that no two consecutive boundary white vertices of
{G}
are glued. Then:

(1)

There is a natural bijection between the strands of
{G}
and those of
\sigma({G})
.

(2)

The strand permutation of
\sigma(\mathcal{T})
is given by:

C_{\sigma(\mathcal{T})}(i)=C_{\mathcal{T}}(i)-1,

with indices taken modulo
n
.

(3)

The BTB graph
\sigma({G})
is minimal.

Proof.

By prepending (resp. appending) the boundary edge
b_{i,i+1}^{\partial}w_{i}^{\partial}
(resp.
w_{j}^{\partial}b^{\partial}_{j-1,j}
), a zig-zag path in
{G}
from
w_{i}^{\partial}
to
w_{j}^{\partial}
corresponds to a zig-zag path in
{{G}^{\mathrm{int}}}
from
b^{\partial}_{i,i+1}
to
b^{\partial}_{j-1,j}
; see Figure
21
(B). Now let
f
be a face of
{{G}^{\mathrm{int}}}
with vertices
b_{1},w_{1},\dots,b_{m},w_{m}
in counterclockwise cyclic order.
Then a zig-zag segment
b_{i}\rightarrow w_{i+1}\rightarrow b_{i+1}
in
{{G}^{\mathrm{int}}}
gets replaced with the segment
w^{\star}_{b_{i}}\rightarrow b^{\star}_{f}\rightarrow w^{\star}_{b_{i+1}}
in
{\star({G})}
. Gluing these replacements face-by-face gives a bijection between zig-zag paths in
{{G}^{\mathrm{int}}}
and
{\star({G})}
; see Figure
21
(C). There is a natural bijection between zig-zag paths in
{\star({G})}
and
\sigma({G})
induced by splits and contractions; see Figure
21
(D). Composing all these bijections gives the bijection between the strands of
{G}
and
\sigma({G})
in (1).

Under the bijection in (1), the zig-zag path in
{G}
from
w_{i}^{\partial}
to
w_{j}^{\partial}
becomes the zig-zag path in
\sigma({G})
from
b_{i,i+1}^{\partial}
to
b_{j-1,j}^{\partial}
, giving (2).

For (3), we use the characterization of minimality in Theorem
2.8
. We have already seen that every strand in
\sigma({G})
starts and ends at the boundary; there are no strands that are loops in
\sigma({G})
.

The correspondence between edges in
{G}
and
{\star({G})}
is compatible with zig-zag paths in the following sense: a zig-zag path of
{G}
uses
bw
in a given direction (black-to-white or white-to-black) if and only if the corresponding zig-zag path of
{\star({G})}
uses
b^{\star}_{f}w^{\star}_{b}
in the same direction. See Figure
21
for an illustration. Additionally,
{\star({G})}
has
n
boundary edges of the form

b^{\star}_{f_{k,k+1}^{\partial}}w^{\star}_{b_{k,k+1}^{\partial}}
, where
f_{k,k+1}^{\partial}
is the boundary face of
{G}
between

w_{k}^{\partial}
and
w_{k+1}^{\partial}
.

In terms of zig-zag paths, a parallel bigon arises when two zig-zag paths traverse two distinct edges in the same order. From the correspondence of zig-zag paths and edges above, no pair of internal edges of
{\star({G})}
can form a parallel bigon since it would mean that the corresponding zig-zag paths form a parallel bigon in
{G}
. Furthermore the boundary edges, that is, edges of the form
b^{\star}_{f_{k,k+1}^{\partial}}w^{\star}_{b_{k,k+1}^{\partial}}
are traversed exactly once in each direction by some zig-zag path: in the white-to-black (resp. black-to-white) direction as the first (resp. last) edge of a zig-zag path. Thus boundary edges cannot participate in parallel bigons.

Similarly, a self-intersection arises when a zig-zag path traverses an edge in both directions. This cannot happen at an internal edge of
{\star({G})}
since it would correspond to a self-intersection of the corresponding zig-zag path in
{G}
. At a boundary edge, a self-intersection of a zig-zag path in
{\star({G})}
would require a zig-zag path of
{G}
with
C_{\mathcal{T}}(i)=i+1
. Indeed, the corresponding zig-zag path in
{{G}^{\mathrm{int}}}
must use the edge from
b^{\partial}_{i,i+1}
to
w^{\partial}_{i}
, which can only occur at the beginning of the path, as well as the edge from
w^{\partial}_{i+1}
to
b^{\partial}_{i,i+1}
, which can only occur at the end of the path. If
C_{\mathcal{T}}(i)=i+1
, the strand
i
does not intersect any other strands. Indeed, one can construct a minimal TCD
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
in which strand
i
has no crossings by first taking a minimal TCD of the remaining strands and then adding strand
i
without introducing any intersections.
By Theorem
2.11
,
\mathcal{T}
is related to
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
by 2-2 moves, and strand
i
cannot participate in such a move.

Consequently, strand
i
must be a part of the boundary of a clockwise region of
\mathcal{T}
that contains both
w_{i}^{\partial}
and
w_{i+1}^{\partial}
. This forces these two vertices to be
glued, a contradiction.
∎

Since the section of a TCD map is again a TCD map, one may also consider iterated sections of a TCD map. Moreover, because the rank of
\sigma(\mathsf{T})
is at most the rank of
\mathsf{T}
minus one, one can take at most
\operatorname{rk}(\mathsf{T})
iterated sections. A priori, this would suggest that up to
2\operatorname{rk}(\mathsf{T})
distinct cluster structures can be associated to a TCD map. However, Theorem
9.1
implies that there are in fact at most
\operatorname{rk}(\mathsf{T})+1
distinct cluster structures.

Remark 9.2
.

Note that, under our standing minimality assumption, we have defined sections only for TCD maps on minimal TCDs. Nevertheless, the same definition applies to non-minimal TCDs as well. However, in that case, some information may be lost when taking sections but Theorem
9.1
continues to hold away from local violations of minimality.
