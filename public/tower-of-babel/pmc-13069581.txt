Comparing the Effects of Lavender Aromatherapy With Virtual Reality on the Quality of Sleep in Type 2 Diabetic Patients: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Melika Jafari Dehnayebi, Hooman Mohammad talebi, Hossein Soltaninejad, Pouya Zeidy, Mehdi Safarabadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsSleep disturbances frequently affect patients with type 2 diabetes, impairing glycemic control and overall health. Safe, non‐pharmacological approaches such as aromatherapy and virtual reality (VR) are increasingly utilized to enhance sleep. This study compared the effectiveness of lavender aromatherapy and VR interventions in improving sleep quality among individuals with type 2 diabetes.MethodsIn this double‐blind, randomized controlled trial, 90 patients with type 2 diabetes were randomly assigned to one of three groups: lavender aromatherapy, virtual reality (VR) and control (routine care). Interventions were performed nightly for three consecutive nights. Sleep quality was assessed before and after the intervention using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). Data were analyzed using appropriate statistical tests based on the distribution of the data.ResultsMedian (IQR) PSQI scores decreased from 7.5 (6–9) to 4.0 (3–5) in the aromatherapy group and from 8.0 (6–9) to 4.0 (3–6) in the VR group (both p < 0.001, r = 0.81–0.83), whereas no significant change occurred in the control group (p = 0.225). ANCOVA adjusting for baseline PSQI confirmed a significant group effect, F (2, 86) = 171.06, p < 0.001, η² = 0.799. Overall, 65%–70% of participants in both interventions achieved good sleep quality (PSQI < 5) compared with 5% in controls.ConclusionLavender aromatherapy and virtual reality improved sleep quality in patients with type 2 diabetes, but results should be interpreted cautiously due to the short, single‐center study design.Keywords: aromatherapy, lavender, sleep quality, type 2 diabetes, virtual reality

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes is a serious public health issue that imposes a significant economic burden on healthcare systems, patients, and their families [1]. Genetic factors play a crucial role in predisposing individuals to type 2 diabetes, while unhealthy dietary habits and sedentary lifestyles act as strong triggers [2, 3]. Type 2 diabetes is characterized by elevated blood glucose levels due to peripheral insulin resistance and decreased insulin production [4].

This condition is associated with multiple complications that impact not only the physical well‐being of individuals but also their psychological health, significantly affecting their quality of life [5]. Among the psychological effects of diabetes that have received relatively less attention are stress, anxiety, depression, and sleep disturbances, all of which require appropriate therapeutic interventions [6, 7].

Sleep is a period of physical and mental rest and is considered a vital human need essential for health and quality of life [8]. Patients with type 2 diabetes often report sleep disturbances. The prevalence of sleep disorders and sleep deprivation has increased in recent years, and a bidirectional relationship between sleep disturbances and diabetes has been established [1]. While diabetes and its complications can lead to sleep disorders, poor sleep quality and quantity can, in turn, worsen diabetes control and significantly increase complications and mortality [1, 8, 9].

The use of non‐invasive therapeutic interventions to improve both physical and mental health in patients with type 2 diabetes is essential [6]. Effective non‐invasive approaches for managing sleep disorders include pharmacological treatments, psychotherapeutic strategies (such as virtual reality), and complementary medicine methods such as aromatherapy [10].

In recent years, aromatherapy has emerged as one of the most widely used complementary therapies due to its cost‐effectiveness, non‐invasiveness, and low risk of side effects [11, 12]. Aromatherapy works by stimulating the olfactory system, influencing the connection between body and mind [13]. Commonly used essential oils in aromatherapy include rose, eucalyptus, lemon balm, tangerine, bergamot, and lavender. Among these, lavender is known for its minimal toxicity and low allergenic potential. It possesses multiple therapeutic properties that make it an ideal choice for aromatherapy, including anxiolytic, antispasmodic, anti‐fatigue, calming, and refreshing effects [14, 15]. Inhaling lavender aroma can stimulate the limbic system, regulate emotions, enhance relaxation, and thereby facilitate sleep induction [15]. Recent research shows aromatherapy with essential oils like lavender can reduce anxiety and improve sleep quality in people with diabetes, suggesting a beneficial complementary approach to enhance well‐being and support holistic diabetes care [16].

Another non‐pharmacological and non‐invasive method is virtual reality (VR) [17]. VR consists of a computer‐generated interactive environment that operates through the principles of immersion and presence. The sense of “being there” creates a believable illusion, making events in VR appear real [18]. Essentially, VR provides realistic, three‐dimensional experiences [19].

With advancements in technology, the application of VR in various fields has expanded. Currently, VR is being used in integrated meditation to enhance mental stability, improve sleep quality, overcome spatial and temporal limitations, increase user comfort, maximize immersion, minimize environmental distractions, and promote deep focus [18, 20]. Today, a variety of VR‐assisted sleep interventions are available. By presenting calming visuals and sounds, VR can divert the mind from stressful thoughts, fostering a sense of tranquility and readiness for sleep [18]. A 2025 scoping review highlights that virtual reality technologies are increasingly applied across healthcare for clinical interventions, education, rehabilitation, and patient support, reflecting rapid growth and diverse benefits of VR in improving care outcomes and training [21].

Several studies have shown that both lavender aromatherapy and virtual reality can improve sleep quality. However, few have directly compared them in patients with type 2 diabetes. This study aims to fill that gap and help healthcare providers choose the best treatment by evaluating their effects on sleep in these patients.

Materials and Methods

This study was a double‐blind, three‐arm randomized clinical trial conducted to compare the effects of lavender aromatherapy and VR on sleep quality in patients with type 2 diabetes. After receiving ethical approval from the Research Ethics Committee (IR.KHOMEIN. REC.1404.004) and registering the trial prospectively in the Iranian Registry on Clinical Trials (IRCT20241125063850N1, https://irct.behdasht.gov.ir/trial/82115), ninety patients with type 2 diabetes admitted to the internal ward of Fatemeh Zahra Hospital, Khomein, were recruited using convenience sampling from May to June 2025.

A total of 101 participants were enrolled to ensure that 90 participants (30 per group) completed the study. The first patient enrolled in the study on May 12th 2025. The sample size was calculated based on the formula for comparing the means of two independent groups:

According to this formula and based on data from previous studies, the following parameters were used: Z1‐α/2=1.96 for a 95% confidence level, 1‐β=1.63 corresponding to a power of 90%, S₁ = 2.46, S₂ = 3.7, μ₁ = 12, and μ₂ = 8.96. Substituting these values into the formula resulted in a required sample size of 27 participants for each group. Considering a possible 10% dropout rate, the sample size was increased to 30 participants per group. Therefore, with one control group and two intervention groups (aromatherapy and virtual reality), the total sample size was calculated to be 90 participants [22].

Study objectives were clearly explained before enrollment. Written informed consent was obtained from literate participants. For illiterate individuals, consent was read aloud, documented with a fingerprint, and confirmed by a legal guardian when required. After obtaining informed consent, participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio to one of three groups (A, B, or C). The random allocation sequence was generated in advance using SPSS software by an independent team member who was not involved in participant recruitment, enrollment, or data collection. This team member also prepared sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes containing the group assignments. To ensure allocation concealment, envelopes were opened sequentially only after a participant had been enrolled in the study. The enrolling investigator opened the next envelope in sequence to reveal the assigned intervention group (control, aromatherapy, or virtual reality). No further allocation steps occurred.

Eligible participants included individuals over 18 years of age diagnosed with type 2 diabetes by an endocrinologist, scoring 5 or higher on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), with no known allergy to lavender, no history of migraine or respiratory disorders, no use of hypnotics or antidepressants, and no use of complementary medicine within the past month. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Any unforeseen physical or psychological crisis during the intervention or withdrawal of consent led to exclusion from the study.

To control potential confounding factors, participants in all three groups were educated to avoid consuming stimulating substances such as caffeine and refrain from engaging in intense physical activity during the intervention period. This precaution aimed to minimize external influences on sleep quality outcomes.

To maintain double blinding, the allocator and the statistician responsible for data analysis was blinded to group allocation, remaining unaware of which data belonged to which group.

The intervention sessions were conducted individually in a quiet room with controlled ambient conditions. Background noise was minimized and lighting was kept at a comfortable, non‐glare level. No external audio or headphones were used during the procedure. In the aromatherapy group, each night before sleep, two drops of lavender essential oil (Effecting substance: Linalyl acetate, produced by GiahKala Company, approved by the Ministry of Health and international standards) were applied to a piece of cotton. Participants inhaled the aroma through ten deep breaths, and the cotton was then attached to their clothing until 8:00 AM the following morning. This intervention was conducted for three consecutive nights [23, 24]. The intervention was administered nightly within the same pre‐sleep time window for all participants. Participants were closely monitored for allergies and unfavorable reactions to the lavender oil during the sessions.

In the VR group, each night before sleep at 10 p.m., participants watched a 15‐min high‐quality relaxing nature video via a VR headset, model VR Box 3D, manufactured by Shenzhen Microcase Technology Co. Ltd. The videos were selected in consultation with a psychologist. After the session, the headset was gently removed. This intervention also continued for three consecutive nights [17, 25]. Participants were monitored for cyber sickness or any other unfavorable reactions to the virtual reality.

In the control group, participants received routine care without any additional interventions related to sleep. They were not informed about the nature of the interventions provided to the other groups to minimize expectancy bias. Furthermore, like the other groups, they were instructed to avoid caffeine and strenuous activity during the intervention period [24].

Demographic data, including age, gender, marital status, number of children, education level, history of chronic and psychological illnesses, type of residence, and presence or absence of a roommate, were collected via a demographic questionnaire before the intervention. Sleep quality was assessed using the standard Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), completed both before and after the three‐night intervention period. The PSQI consists of seven components, with a global score ranging from 0 to 21; scores below 5 indicate good sleep quality, while scores above 11 reflect poor sleep quality. The psychometric properties of the Persian version of the PSQI have been validated in the Iranian population, with reported sensitivity of 100%, specificity of 93%, and Cronbach's alpha of 0.89 [23].

Finally, changes in sleep quality scores before and after the intervention were calculated for each group and analyzed using appropriate statistical tests, performed with SPSS software (Version 22).

Results

Participant flow indicated that of 101 enrolled patients, 90 (30 per group) completed all assessments. Demographic and clinical characteristics of these 90 participants are shown in Table 1. The mean age was 55.04 years (SD = 8.71; range 38–74). Age was normally distributed among the groups according to the Shapiro–Wilk test. The test results were non‐significant for all groups: aroma (W = 0.958, p = 0.272), virtual reality (W = 0.974, p = 0.654), and control (W = 0.961, p = 0.324), indicating no violation of the normality assumption. Sixty‐one percent were male, and 39% were female, with no missing cases for those who completed the study. Educational attainment was distributed as 10.0% having no formal education, 50.0% with a diploma, 30.0% holding a bachelor's degree, and 10.0% having a master's degree. Employment status included 6.7% jobless, 23.3% freelancers, 15.6% employees, 28.9% retirees, and 25.6% householders. Thirty‐three percent were single, 67% married, and 60% reported at least one chronic disease. Forty percent had a history of surgery, and 60% resided in a villa compared to 40% in an apartment. The number of children ranged from 1 to 7, with 10.0% having one child, 31.1% two, 31.1% three, 20.0% four, and the rest having five or more children. No group differed significantly on any demographic characteristic, indicating comparability at baseline.

Between‐group comparisons at pre‐test confirmed equivalence in baseline sleep disturbance (Kruskal–Wallis H = 0.360, p= 0.835), with no significant differences among the aroma, VR, and control groups (all p > 0.05; r = 0.03–0.15). At post‐test, the Kruskal–Wallis test indicated a significant overall group effect (H = 54.218, p < 0.001). Pairwise Mann–Whitney analyses showed that both the aroma (U = 20.0, Z = −6.43, p < 0.001, r = 0.81) and VR (U = 16.5, Z = −6.47, p < 0.001, r = 0.83) interventions produced substantially greater reductions in PSQI scores compared with the control condition. The median improvement in PSQI was –3.5 (95% CI −4.2 to −2.7) for the aroma group and −3.7 (95% CI −4.4 to −3.0) for the VR group, versus –0.2 (95% CI −0.8 to 0.4) for the control. No significant difference was observed between the two active treatments (U = 446.0, Z = −0.06, p= 0.951, r = 0.01), indicating comparable efficacy. Clinically, 65%–70% of participants in the aroma and VR groups achieved good sleep quality (PSQI < 5), compared with only 5% in the control group (Table 4 and Figure 2).

A one‐way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted to evaluate differences in post‐intervention sleep quality among the aroma, VR, and control groups while controlling for baseline PSQI scores. Before analyzing, ANCOVA assumptions were met, including normality of residuals, linearity between the covariate and dependent variable, homogeneity of regression slopes, and inspection of residuals for outliers and homoscedasticity. The covariate (pre‐test PSQI) was evaluated at its mean value of 8.04. After adjustment, there was a significant main effect of group on post‐test PSQI, F(2, 86) = 171.06, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.799, indicating a large effect size. Adjusted mean post‐test scores were lowest in the VR group (M = 4.10, SE = 0.17, 95% CI [3.76, 4.45]), followed closely by the aroma group (M = 4.36, SE = 0.17, 95% CI [4.02, 4.71]), and highest in the control group (M = 8.13, SE = 0.17, 95% CI [7.79, 8.48]). Pairwise Bonferroni‐adjusted comparisons showed that both the lavender aromatherapy and VR interventions resulted in significantly lower post‐test PSQI scores compared with the control condition (p < 0.001 for both), while no significant difference was found between the two active treatments (p = 0.866). These results confirm that both interventions were comparably effective in improving sleep quality relative to the control group (Table 5).

Discussion

The present study aimed to compare the effects of lavender aromatherapy and virtual reality (VR) on sleep quality in patients with type 2 diabetes. The findings revealed that both interventions significantly improved sleep quality compared to the control group, with no statistically significant difference observed between the two intervention groups. These results indicate that both non‐pharmacological approaches are effective in enhancing sleep quality among diabetic patients.

The aroma group demonstrated a significant reduction in sleep disturbance scores. Aromatherapy, particularly with essential oils like lavender, has been shown to improve sleep quality by promoting relaxation and reducing stress and anxiety levels [26, 27]. A meta‐analysis concluded that inhalation aromatherapy is effective in improving sleep problems such as insomnia [26]. The therapeutic effects are attributed to the influence of aromatic compounds on the limbic system, which regulates emotions and sleep. Similarly, the VR group experienced a significant improvement in sleep quality. VR interventions often incorporate mindfulness and relaxation techniques within immersive environments, which can alleviate anxiety and promote better sleep [28]. A recent study found that VR‐based meditation effectively enhances sleep quality, lowers stress levels, and improves autonomic nervous system balance [29].

The findings of this study are consistent with previous research on the effectiveness of lavender aromatherapy in improving sleep. Earlier studies have shown that inhalation of lavender essential oil can help reduce stress and anxiety and improve sleep by influencing the brain's nervous system [15, 23]. For instance, Nasiri Lari et al. demonstrated that lavender inhalation could enhance the quality and quantity of sleep, as well as quality of life and mood in diabetic patients suffering from insomnia, without significantly affecting their metabolic status [30]. Similarly, Can et al. reported comparable effects on improving sleep quality in elderly patients with diabetes [31]. Moreover, our aromatherapy result coheres with an RCT in diabetic neuropathy showing lavender aromatherapy massage reduced neuropathic pain and improved quality of life mechanisms (pain and autonomic arousal) that plausibly mediate sleep gains in diabetes [32].

On the other hand, virtual reality technology provides immersive sensory experiences that help divert the mind from stressful thoughts, thereby facilitating better sleep. This is supported by a study conducted by Goudman et al., which confirmed the effectiveness of VR in improving sleep quality among patients with painful diabetic neuropathy [18], in line with the current study's results. Additionally, Wan et al. provided preliminary evidence indicating that VR therapy not only improves sleep quality but also reduces symptoms of depression and anxiety while enhancing cognitive and autonomic functioning in patients with chronic insomnia [33]. Conversely, in a proof‐of‐concept randomized crossover study in painful diabetic polyneuropathy (PDPN), 2 weeks of bedtime VR did not improve actigraphy‐based sleep efficiency or awakenings versus usual care, though more participants reported global improvement after VR (suggesting perceived benefit despite limited objective change in this very small sample) [18].

The lack of a significant difference between the aromatherapy and VR groups in this study suggests comparable effectiveness of these two interventions. This is of great clinical importance, as it allows nurses and healthcare providers to select the most appropriate intervention based on patients' individual preferences, environmental factors, and resource availability. For example, patients without access to technological equipment may prefer aromatherapy, whereas others may find VR more suitable due to its interactive and visual features.

It is noteworthy that the control group in this study showed no significant change in sleep quality, further highlighting the effectiveness of both interventions. Moreover, the use of the validated PSQI questionnaire and the randomized clinical trial design enhances the internal validity of the findings.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. One of the main limitations was the short duration of the intervention (three nights), which may not fully reflect the long‐term effects of the treatments. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting short‐term responses, and further studies with extended monitoring periods are needed to evaluate the persistence and clinical significance of the observed outcomes. Additionally, the lack of participant blinding may have introduced response bias, as participants' awareness of their assigned intervention could have influenced their self‐reported sleep quality. The use of convenience sampling from a single hospital also limits the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. It is recommended that future studies employ longer intervention periods, triple‐blind designs, placebo‐controlled groups, and larger, more diverse sample sizes to confirm and expand upon these findings. Incorporating objective sleep measures, such as actigraphy or polysomnography, may also enhance the validity of sleep quality assessments.

In conclusion, both lavender aromatherapy and virtual reality represent safe, practical, and effective methods for improving sleep quality in patients with type 2 diabetes. Incorporating these complementary interventions into routine diabetes care may contribute to better physical and psychological health and enhance patients' overall quality of life.

Conclusion

This randomized clinical trial demonstrated that both lavender aromatherapy and virtual reality significantly improved sleep quality in patients with type 2 diabetes compared to the control group. No significant difference was found between the two interventions, indicating that both are equally effective non‐pharmacological options. These findings support the clinical use of either approach based on patient preference and resource availability. The use of validated tools and robust statistical analysis strengthens the reliability of these results. Results should be interpreted cautiously due to the short, single‐center study design.
