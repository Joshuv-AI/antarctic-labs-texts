On an initial value problem describing the small oscillations of a floating cylinder Thanks: ∗ Corresponding author


Abstract
. We study a coupled PDE-ODE system modeling the small oscillations of a floating cylinder interacting with small water waves. We consider the case when the floating body is an infinite circular cylinder, so that the equations of the free surface of the fluid can be written in one space dimension. The governing equations are formulated as an abstract evolution equation in a suitable Hilbert space, and we establish the well-posedness of the associated initial value problem. A key element of the proof is the analysis of a partial Dirichlet-to-Neumann map on an unbounded domain with a non-smooth boundary.

1.
Introduction

Understanding the dynamics of a floating body interacting with ocean waves is a problem of major interest for many application fields, such as naval engineering, floating wind turbines or wave energy converters.
The mathematical analysis of the models describing such interactions is a complex problem. Indeed, in addition to the difficulties raised by the equations of water waves, new challenges are added by the presence of the fluid-solid interface and to the coupling of the equations describing the various components of the system. In particular, the possible appearance of corners in the fluid domain induces difficulties which are not usually present in the study of water waves in smooth (possibly periodic) domains. We refer to the recent work of Lannes and Ming
[
11
]
for a more detailed description of the state of the art in this field. When the full water system is replaced by some reduced models valid within the shallow water approximation or the Boussinesq regime, several results are available in the literature, see, for instance, Lannes and Iguchi
[
8
]
, Bresch, Lannes and Métivier
[
2
]
and the references therein.

Most of the existing works on wave-structure interactions are based on the linear potential flow approach, which has been introduced in a rigorous manner in John
[
9
]
and then used in various articles. These works consider essentially time harmonic motions, for both rigid or deformable bodies (see, for instance, Kuznetsov and Mazya
[
10
]
or Hazard and Lenoir
[
7
]
). Much less has been done on the analysis of the corresponding systems in the time domain. As far as we know, within the linear potential flow approach,
the well-posedness of the initial value problem describing the coupled motion of the floating object and of the free surface of the fluid has not been explicitly considered in the literature. Some of the difficulties raised by this problem, namely those connected to the simultaneous presence of corners and of an unbounded fluid domain, have been recently tackled in
[
11
]
. In the above mentioned reference, the authors consider the linear potential flow approach, when the horizontal dimension is
d=1
, the fluid is supposed of finite depth and the floating solid is replaced by a partially immersed obstacle.

Our work tackles a much simpler geometric context, introduced in Ursell
[
16
]
. The considered system describes the coupled vertical oscillations of a floating infinite circular cylinder and of the free surface of an ideal fluid of infinite depth. The equilibrium position of the cylinder is assumed to be attained when half of its lateral surface is immersed, see Figure
1
.

Figure 1.
Floating cylinder

Our main contribution consists in proving that the initial value problem describing the vertical oscillations of this coupled system is well-posed in standard Sobolev spaces. Due to the particular geometric configuration, some of the main technical difficulties can be solved in a quite elementary manner. More precisely, an important point of our methodology consists in deriving explicit formulas for the two potentials of the fluid velocity (one generated by the oscillations of the free surface of the fluid and a second one by those of the floating cylinder). Moreover, these explicit formulas are exploited to obtain the main ingredient of our approach: the proof of the fact that an associated Dirichlet-to-Neumann map defines an unbounded positive operator on an
L^{2}
space. This fact allows us to construct a
C^{0}
group of operators to describe the dynamics of the considered system.

The remaining part of this paper is organized as described below. Firstly, Section
2
is devoted to the precise formulation of the considered system and to the statement of our main result. In Section
3
, we recall some basic properties on the classical Dirichlet-to-Neumann map for the Laplacian in the upper half-plane. In Section
4
, we consider a problem with mixed boundary conditions for the Laplacian in a domain
\Omega
obtained by intersecting the upper half plane with the exterior of the unit disk in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In Section
5
, we construct and study the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
\Lambda_{\Omega}
associated with the boundary value problem introduced in Section
4
. Finally, in Section
6
, we prove our main result.

2.
Statement of the main result

In this section we give, following
[
16
]
(which, in turn, follows
[
9
]
), the precise formulation of the governing equations. Moreover, we give the precise statement of our main result.

Within the geometric context in Figure
1
above, the transverse section of the cylinder is supposed to be a disk of radius
1
.
We assume that, at equilibrium, the center of this disk lies on the free surface of the fluid (roughly speaking, this means that the density of the solid is half of the density of the fluid).
We consider a Cartesian coordinate system
(x,y)
with the origin fixed at the center of this disk.
The
x
-axis is taken to be horizontal, while the
y
-axis increases with depth. The polar coordinates
(r,\theta)
of a point in the fluid domain are defined by
x=r\sin\theta,\quad y=r\cos\theta
, with
\theta\in\left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]
and
r>1
. Moreover, at equilibrium, the surface of the cylinder is described by
r=1
and
\theta\in\left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]
.

Let
h(t)
denote the vertical displacement, at time
t\geqslant 0
, of the centre of the disk. The depth of the fluid is assumed to be infinite. Moreover, the amplitude of the motion is supposed to be sufficiently small so that we can use a linearized version of the governing equations. Neglecting viscosity, assuming that the flow is irrotational and that the fluid density is a constant
\rho>0
, the velocity field of the fluid is
\nabla\varphi
, where the velocity potential
\varphi(t,x,y)
satisfies

(2.1)

\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial x^{2}}(t,x,y)+\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial y^{2}}(t,x,y)=0\qquad\qquad(t\geqslant 0,x^{2}+y^{2}>1,y>0).

The linearized condition on the free surface, following from Bernoulli’s law, is

(2.2)

\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial t^{2}}(t,x,0)-g\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y}(t,x,0)=0\qquad\qquad(t\geqslant 0,\ |x|>1).

On the cylinder the radial components of the velocities
of the body and of the fluid are equal,
i.e.
,

(2.3)

\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial r}(t,\sin\theta,\cos\theta)=\dot{h}(t)\cos\theta\qquad\qquad\left(t\geqslant 0,\ -\frac{\pi}{2}\leqslant\theta\leqslant\frac{\pi}{2}\right).

The equation of motion for
h(t)
is given by

(2.4)

\frac{1}{2}\pi\rho\ddot{h}(t)=-2\rho gh(t)+\rho\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}(t,\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\cos\theta\,{\rm d}\theta+f(t),

where on the right-hand side the first term stands for the hydrostatic restoring force, the second term is the resultant of the hydrodynamic pressures, whereas the third term describes a possible applied vertical force.

As usual in wave dynamics the fluid equations can be reformulated as an equation on the free boundary. More precisely, let
\Omega
denote the water domain and
\mathcal{E}
the free surface, i.e.,

(2.5)

\Omega=\left\{\left.\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ \ \right|\ \ x^{2}+y^{2}>1,y>0\right\}.

(2.6)

\mathcal{E}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\ \ |\ \ |x|>1\}.

The PDE-ODE system formed by (
2.1
), (
2.2
), (
2.3
) and (
2.4
), of unknowns
\varphi
and
h
, can be reformulated in terms of the trace of the potential on the free surface
v(t,x)=\varphi(t,x,0)
(defined for
t\geqslant 0
and
|x|>1
) and of
h
. Indeed, by linearity, we can write

(2.7)

\varphi(t,x,y)=(D_{\Omega}v)(t,x,y)+\dot{h}(t)\varphi_{1}(x,y)\qquad\left(t\geqslant 0,\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}\in\Omega\right),

where

(2.8)

\varphi_{1}(x,y)=-\frac{y}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\qquad\qquad\left(\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}\in\Omega\right),

which satisfies

(2.9)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi_{1}}{\partial x^{2}}(x,y)+\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi_{1}}{\partial y^{2}}(x,y)=0&\left(\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}\in\Omega\right)\\
&\frac{\partial\varphi_{1}}{\partial r}(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)=\cos\theta&(-\pi/2\leqslant\theta\leqslant\pi/2)\\
&\varphi_{1}(x,0)=0&(x\in\mathcal{E}),\end{aligned}\right.

and
D_{\Omega}
is
the Dirichlet operator
on
\Omega
. This means that for each
v
in an appropriate function space,
D_{\Omega}v
solves

(2.10)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial^{2}(D_{\Omega}v)}{\partial x^{2}}(t,x,y)+\frac{\partial^{2}(D_{\Omega}v)}{\partial y^{2}}(t,x,y)=0&\left(\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}\in\Omega\right)\\
&\frac{\partial(D_{\Omega}v)}{\partial r}(t,\sin\theta,\cos\theta)=0&(-\pi/2\leqslant\theta\leqslant\pi/2)\\
&(D_{\Omega}v)(t,x)=v(t,x)&(x\in\mathcal{E}).\end{aligned}\right.

We refer to Section
4
for a detailed construction of
D_{\Omega}
.

From (
2.7
) it follows that

(2.11)

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}(t,\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\cos\theta\,{\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\frac{\partial(D_{\Omega}v)}{\partial t}(t,\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\cos\theta\,{\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle-\ddot{h}(t)\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos^{2}\theta\,{\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\frac{\partial(D_{\Omega}v)}{\partial t}(t,\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\cos\theta\,{\rm d}\theta-\frac{\pi}{2}\ddot{h}(t).

An important role in the remaining part of this paper will be played by the
Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator
on
\Omega
. This operator, denoted
by
\Lambda_{\Omega}
, will be precisely defined in Section
5
. At this stage, we formally define the action of
\Lambda_{\Omega}
on
every suitable
v:\mathcal{E}\to\mathbb{R}
by

(2.12)

(\Lambda_{\Omega}v)(t,x)=-\frac{\partial(D_{\Omega}v)}{\partial y}(t,x,0)\qquad(t\geqslant 0,x\in\mathcal{E}),

where
D_{\Omega}v
is the solution of (
2.10
).

With the above notation, equations (
2.2
) and (
2.4
) can be written as

(2.13)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial t^{2}}(t,x)+g(\Lambda_{\Omega}v)(t,x)+\frac{g}{x^{2}}\dot{h}(t)=0\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\quad\quad(t\geqslant 0,x\in\mathcal{E})\\
&\ddot{h}(t)=-\frac{2g}{\pi}h(t)+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\frac{\partial(D_{\Omega}v)}{\partial t}(t,\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\cos\theta\,{\rm d}\theta+\frac{f(t)}{\pi\rho}\quad(t\geqslant 0).\end{aligned}\right.

Our study will thus focus on system (
2.13
).
With
\mathcal{E}
given in (
2.6
), and for
s\geqslant 0
, we denote by
W^{s,2}(\mathcal{E})
the usual Sobolev space on
\mathcal{E}
.
These spaces are often denoted by
H^{s}(\mathcal{E})
, but in this paper we choose the notation
W^{s,2}(\mathcal{E})
to avoid confusion with other notation involving the letter capital
H
(the Hilbert transform or abstract Hilbert spaces) frequently encountered in this work.

The main result of this paper is:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
f\in L_{{\rm loc}}^{1}([0,\infty))
,
h_{0},h_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
,
v_{0}\in W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathcal{E})
and
v_{1}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
. Then, the system (
2.13
) with initial data
h(0)=h_{0},\dot{h}(0)=h_{1},v(0,x)=v_{0}(x)
and
\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}(0,x)=v_{1}(x)
has a unique solution
(v,h)
satisfying

(2.14)

\displaystyle v\in C([0,\infty),W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathcal{E}))\cap C^{1}([0,\infty),L^{2}(\mathcal{E})),

(2.15)

\displaystyle h\in C^{1}([0,\infty)).

Moreover, if
v_{0}\in W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
and
v_{1}\in W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathcal{E})
and
f
is of class
C^{1}
, then we have

(2.16)

\displaystyle v\in C([0,\infty),W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E}))\cap C^{1}([0,\infty),W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathcal{E}))\cap C^{2}([0,\infty),L^{2}(\mathcal{E})),

(2.17)

\displaystyle h\in C^{2}([0,\infty)).

3.
Some background on the Dirichlet problem and on the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map in the half plane

The precise construction of the operators
D_{\Omega}
and
\Lambda_{\Omega}
introduced in the previous section is based, within our approach, on known results on similar operators in the upper half-plane. This is why in this section we recall, with no claim of originality, some basic facts on the Dirichlet problem and on the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map in the half plane and we refer to the existing literature (see, for instance, Axler, Bourdon and R. Wade
[
1
]
for more details on this subject).

Let
\mathbb{H}
be the upper half-plane

(3.1)

\mathbb{H}=\left\{\left.\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ \ \right|\ \ y>0\right\}.

Consider the Poisson kernel for
\mathbb{H}
defined by

(3.2)

P_{\mathbb{H}}(x,y,\tilde{x})=\frac{y}{\pi\left[(x-\tilde{x})^{2}+y^{2}\right]}\qquad\qquad\qquad(x,\ \tilde{x}\in\mathbb{R},\ y>0).

We recall the following classical result, see, for instance,
[
1
, Theorems 7.3,7.4,7.5]
).

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\eta:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous and bounded function on
\mathbb{R}
. Then the function
\tilde{\psi}:\overline{\mathbb{H}}\to\mathbb{R}
, defined by

(3.3)

\tilde{\psi}(x,y)=(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta)(x,y):=\begin{cases}\int_{\mathbb{R}}P_{\mathbb{H}}(x,y,\tilde{x})\eta(\tilde{x})\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}&\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathbb{R},y>0),\\
\eta(x)&\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathbb{R},y=0),\end{cases}

is the unique solution, in the class of functions which are continuous and bounded on
\overline{\mathbb{H}}
, of the Dirichlet problem

(3.4)

\Delta\tilde{\psi}(x,y)=0\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathbb{R},\ y>0),

(3.5)

\tilde{\psi}(x,0)=\eta(x)\qquad\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathbb{R}).

Moreover, we have

(3.6)

\max_{x\in\overline{\mathbb{H}}}|\tilde{\psi}|=\max_{x\in\overline{\mathcal{E}}}|\eta|

Remark 3.2
.

Let
\theta
be a continuous function on
\overline{\mathbb{H}}
such that for every
y\geqslant 0
the function
x\mapsto\theta(x,y)
lies in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
. We consider the partial Fourier transform of
\theta
with respect to
x
defined by

\mathcal{F}\theta(\xi,y)=\widehat{\theta(\cdot,y)}(\xi)\qquad\qquad\qquad(\xi\in\mathbb{R},\ y\geqslant 0),

where
\hat{f}
stands for the Fourier transform of
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
.
With the above notation, formulas (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) can be gathered in

(3.7)

\left(\mathcal{F}{D_{\mathbb{H}}}\eta\right)(\xi,y)=\exp(-|\xi|y)\hat{\eta}(\xi)\qquad\qquad\qquad(\xi\in\mathbb{R},\ y\geqslant 0).

Proposition 3.3
.

With the notation in Proposition
3.1
, for every
\eta\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})

we have that
\nabla(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{H})
and

(3.8)

\left\|\nabla(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{H})}\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\|\eta\|_{W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R})}.

Proof.

We first note that from (
3.7
) it follows that

(3.9)

\int_{\mathbb{H}}\xi^{2}\left|\mathcal{F}{D_{\mathbb{H}}}\eta(\xi,y)\right|^{2}\,{\rm d}\xi\,{\rm d}y=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\xi^{2}\exp(-2|\xi|y)|\hat{\eta}(\xi)|^{2}\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}\xi\\
=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|\ |\hat{\eta}(\xi)|^{2}\,{\rm d}\xi\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+|\xi|)|\hat{\eta}(\xi)|^{2}\,{\rm d}\xi.

Moreover, from (
3.7
) it also follows that

(3.10)

\frac{\partial\left(\mathcal{F}{D_{\mathbb{H}}}\eta\right)}{\partial y}(\xi,y)=-|\xi|\exp(-|\xi|y)\hat{\eta}(\xi)\qquad\qquad\qquad(\xi\in\mathbb{R},\ y\geqslant 0),

so that

(3.11)

\int_{\mathbb{H}}\left|\frac{\partial\left(\mathcal{F}{D_{\mathbb{H}}}\eta\right)}{\partial y}(\xi,y)\right|^{2}\,{\rm d}\xi\,{\rm d}y=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{\infty}|\xi|^{2}\exp(-2|\xi|y)|\hat{\eta}(\xi)|^{2}\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}\xi\\
=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|\ |\hat{\eta}(\xi)|^{2}\,{\rm d}\xi\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+|\xi|)|\hat{\eta}(\xi)|^{2}\,{\rm d}\xi.

Putting together (
3.9
), (
3.11
) and Plancherel’s identity we obtain the conclusion (
3.8
).
∎

We next recall some background on the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}
of the upper half plane
\mathbb{H}
. Roughly speaking, this map associates to every
\eta\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})
the Neumann trace on the real axis of
the unique solution, in the class of functions which are continuous and bounded on
\overline{\mathbb{H}}
, of
the Dirichlet problem (
3.4
), (
3.5
). To give the precise definition of this map
we introduce the Hilbert transform
\mathcal{H}
defined by

(3.12)

(\mathcal{H}f)(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}{\rm P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(\tilde{x})}{x-\tilde{x}}\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}\qquad\qquad\qquad(f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})),

where

{\rm P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(\tilde{x})}{x-\tilde{x}}\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\int_{|x-\tilde{x}|\geqslant\varepsilon}\frac{f(\tilde{x})}{x-\tilde{x}}\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}\qquad\qquad(f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}),\ x\in\mathbb{R}).

We recall (see, for instance, pp. 109-110 in
[
6
]
) that
\mathcal{H}\in\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
and

(3.13)

\widehat{\mathcal{H}f}(\xi)=-i{\rm sign}\,(\xi)(\hat{f}(\xi))\qquad\qquad(f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}),\ \xi\in\mathbb{R}).

We next introduce the operator

\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}\in\mathcal{L}(W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}),L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
, called
Dirichlet-to-Neumann map in

\mathbb{H}
,
defined by

(3.14)

(\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}\eta)(x)=-(\mathcal{H}\eta^{\prime})(x)\qquad\qquad\qquad(\eta\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})).

Let us also note that from (
3.13
) and (
3.14
) it follows that

\widehat{\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}\eta}(\xi)=i{\rm sign}\,(\xi)(\widehat{\eta^{\prime}}(\xi))\qquad\qquad(\eta\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}),\ \xi\in\mathbb{R}).

From the above formula we obtain the alternative definition of
\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}
as

(3.15)

\widehat{\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}\eta}(\xi)=|\xi|\hat{\eta}(\xi)\qquad\qquad(\eta\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}),\ \xi\in\mathbb{R}).

From (
3.15
) and Plancherel’s theorem it follows that for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
I+\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}
can be restricted (or extended) to a boundedly invertible operator from
W^{s,2}(\mathbb{R})
to
W^{s-1,2}(\mathbb{R})
. This fact, together with (
3.15
), yields that

\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}
is an unbounded self-adjoint positive operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, with domain
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})
.

The result below justifies the term
Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
to denote
\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}
.

Proposition 3.4
.

For every
\eta\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})
the solution
D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta
of (
3.4
), (
3.5
)
satisfies

(3.16)

\lim_{y\to 0+}\left\|\frac{\partial(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta)}{\partial y}(\cdot,y)+\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}\eta\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=0,

where
\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}\in\mathcal{L}(W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}),L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
is the operator defined in
(
3.14
) (or, alternatively, in (
3.15
)).

Moreover, we have

(3.17)

\int_{\mathbb{H}}\nabla(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta_{1})\cdot\overline{\nabla(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta_{2})}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}\eta_{1}\right)\,\overline{\eta_{2}}\,{\rm d}x\qquad\qquad(\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})).

Proof.

Using formula (
3.10
) in the proof of Proposition
3.3

(3.18)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\frac{\partial\left(\mathcal{F}{D_{\mathbb{H}}}\eta\right)}{\partial y}(\xi,y)+\widehat{\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}\eta}(\xi)\right|^{2}\,{\rm d}\xi\\
=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{2}(1-\exp(-|\xi|y))^{2}|\hat{\eta}(\xi)|^{2}\,{\rm d}\xi\qquad\qquad\qquad(\xi\in\mathbb{R},\ y>0).

The conclusion (
3.16
) follows from the above formula by combining the dominated convergence theorem with the fact that
\eta\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})

and with Plancherel’s theorem.

In order to prove (
3.17
) we first note that the left hand side of (
3.17
) is well defined thanks to Proposition

3.3
. Moreover,

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{H}}\nabla(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta_{1})\cdot\overline{\nabla(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta_{2})}{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}x\,=\int_{\mathbb{H}}\mathcal{F}\left(\nabla(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta_{1})\right)\cdot\overline{\mathcal{F}\left(\nabla(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta_{2})\right)}\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}\xi\\
=\int_{\mathbb{H}}\xi^{2}\mathcal{F}\left({D_{\mathbb{H}}}\eta_{1}\right)(\xi,y)\overline{\mathcal{F}\left({D_{\mathbb{H}}}\eta_{2}\right)(\xi,y)}\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}\xi\\
+\int_{\mathbb{H}}\frac{\partial\left(\mathcal{F}{D_{\mathbb{H}}}\eta_{1}\right)}{\partial y}(\xi,y)\,\overline{\frac{\partial\left(\mathcal{F}{D_{\mathbb{H}}}\eta_{2}\right)}{\partial y}(\xi,y)}\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}\xi.

Combining the above formula with (
3.7
) and (
3.10
) it follows that

(3.19)

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{H}}\nabla(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta_{1})\cdot\overline{\nabla(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta_{2})}\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}x\,=\int_{\mathbb{H}}\xi^{2}\exp(-2|\xi|y)\hat{\eta}_{1}(\xi)\overline{\hat{\eta}_{2}}(\xi)\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}\xi\\
+\int_{\mathbb{H}}\xi^{2}\exp(-2|\xi|y)\hat{\eta}_{1}(\xi)\overline{\hat{\eta}_{2}}(\xi){\rm d}y\,{\rm d}\xi=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|\hat{\eta}_{1}(\xi)\overline{\hat{\eta}_{2}}(\xi){\rm d}y\,{\rm d}\xi,

which clearly yields the conclusion (
3.17
).
∎

Remark 3.5
.

It is not difficult to check that if
\eta\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R})
then
\nabla(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta)
is continuous on
\overline{\mathbb{H}}

and

(3.20)

(\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}\eta)(x)=-\frac{\partial(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta)}{\partial y}(x,0)\qquad\qquad(\eta\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}),\ x\in\mathbb{R}).

Indeed, from (
3.7
) and (
3.10
) we have

(3.21)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(\mathcal{F}D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta)}{\partial\xi}(\xi,y)

\displaystyle=-y\,{\rm sgn}(\xi){\rm exp}(-|\xi|y)\hat{\eta}(\xi)+{\rm exp}(-|\xi|y)\widehat{\eta^{\prime}}(\xi)

\displaystyle=-y\,{\rm sgn}(\xi){\rm exp}(-|\xi|y)\hat{\eta}(\xi)+i\xi{\rm exp}(-|\xi|y)\hat{\eta}(\xi)

\displaystyle=(-y\,{\rm sgn}(\xi)+i\xi){\rm exp}(-|\xi|y)\hat{\eta}(\xi)\qquad\qquad(y>0,\xi\in\mathbb{R})

and

(3.22)

\frac{\partial(\mathcal{F}D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta)}{\partial y}(\xi,y)=-|\xi|{\rm exp}(-|\xi|y)\hat{\eta}(\xi)\qquad\qquad(y>0,\xi\in\mathbb{R}).

Since
\eta\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R})
,
\eta^{\prime}\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})
, so that
\eta^{\prime}
is continuous, and thus, so is
\xi\hat{\eta}(\xi)
. It follows from the above formulas that
\nabla(\mathcal{F}(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta))
(and thus
\nabla(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta)
) is continuous on
\overline{\mathbb{H}}
.
Moreover, using (
3.10
) again we have

(3.23)

\lim_{y\to 0^{+}}\frac{\partial(\mathcal{F}D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta)}{\partial y}(\xi,y)=-\lim_{y\to 0^{+}}|\xi|{\rm exp}(-|\xi|y)\hat{\eta}(\xi)=-|\xi|\hat{\eta}(\xi)=-\widehat{\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}\eta}(\xi),

where we used (
3.15
) for the last equality.
Thus the operator
\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}
can also be seen as the extension to
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})
of the operator on
W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R})
defined by the right-hand side of (
3.20
).

4.
A boundary value problem for the Laplacian

In this section we construct in a rigorous manner the operator
D_{\Omega}
defined in Section
2
and we explicitly compute a Poisson type kernel describing this operator.

Let
\Omega
and
\mathcal{E}
be given by (
2.5
) and (
2.6
) respectively. We are interested in the mixed problem which consists of determining
\psi:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial x^{2}}(x,y)+\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial y^{2}}(x,y)=0&&\qquad(x^{2}+y^{2}>1,y>0),\\
&\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}(x,y)=0&&\qquad(x\in\mathcal{E},\ y>0,\ x^{2}+y^{2}=1),\\
&\psi(x,0)=v(x)&&\qquad(|x|>1),\end{aligned}\right.

where the radial derivative of
\psi
is defined by

(4.2)

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}(x,y)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}(x,y)+\frac{y}{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}(x,y)\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathcal{E},\ y>0).

Proposition 4.1
.

For
v\in W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
, the problem (
4.1
) has a unique solution
\psi=D_{\Omega}v
in the class of functions which are continuous and bounded on
\overline{\Omega}
. Moreover,
for every
\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}\in\Omega
we have

(4.3)

(D_{\Omega}v)(x,y)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathcal{E}}\left[\frac{y}{(x-\tilde{x})^{2}+y^{2}}+\frac{y}{(x\tilde{x}-1)^{2}+y^{2}\tilde{x}^{2}}\right]v(\tilde{x})\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}.

Finally,
D_{\Omega}
is a linear bounded operator from
W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
to
C_{b}(\overline{\Omega})
, where
C_{b}(\overline{\Omega})

stands for the space of continuous bounded functions on
\overline{\Omega}
, endowed with the
L^{\infty}
norm.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{H}
be the upper half-plane introduced in (
3.1
)
and let
P_{\mathbb{H}}
be the Poisson kernel for
\mathbb{H}
defined in (
3.2
).
We next extend
v
to
\eta(v):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, with

(4.4)

(\eta(v))(x)=\begin{cases}v(x)&\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathcal{E})\\
v\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)&\qquad\qquad(x\in(-1,1)\setminus\{0\}),\\
0&\qquad\qquad(x=0).\end{cases}

Since
v\in W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
, a classical Sobolev embedding implies that
\eta(v)
is continuous and bounded on
\mathbb{R}
. For notational simplicity, we write
\eta
instead of
\eta(v)
in the remaining part of this proof.

We claim that the restriction
\psi
of
\tilde{\psi}=D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta
, where
\tilde{\psi}=D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta
has been introduced in Proposition
3.1
, to
\overline{\Omega}
is a classical solution of (
4.1
). To prove this claim, we first note that from
the properties established in Proposition
3.1
for
\tilde{\psi}
it
follows that
\psi
is harmonic on
\Omega
, as well as continuous and bounded on
\overline{\Omega}
.
We thus just need to check that
\psi
satisfies the Neumann boundary condition in (
4.1
).
To this aim we note that from (
3.2
) it follows that
for
x,\ \tilde{x}\in\mathbb{R}
and
y>0
we have

\displaystyle\pi\left[x\frac{\partial P_{\mathbb{H}}}{\partial x}(x,y,\tilde{x})+y\frac{\partial P_{\mathbb{H}}}{\partial y}(x,y,\tilde{x})\right]

\displaystyle=x\frac{2(\tilde{x}-x)y}{((\tilde{x}-x)^{2}+y^{2})^{2}}+y\frac{(\tilde{x}-x)^{2}-y^{2}}{((\tilde{x}-x)^{2}+y^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\tilde{x}xy-2x^{2}y+\tilde{x}^{2}y-2\tilde{x}xy+x^{2}y-y^{3}}{(\tilde{x}^{2}-2x\tilde{x}+x^{2}+y^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{x}^{2}y-y(x^{2}+y^{2})}{(\tilde{x}^{2}-2x\tilde{x}+x^{2}+y^{2})^{2}}.

From the above formula it follows that

\frac{\partial P_{\mathbb{H}}}{\partial r}(x,y)=\frac{\tilde{x}^{2}y-y}{\pi(\tilde{x}^{2}-2x\tilde{x}+1)^{2}}\qquad\qquad(y>0,\ x^{2}+y^{2}=1).

Combining the above formula with (
3.3
) and the dominated convergence theorem it follows that for
x\in\mathbb{R},\ y>0,\ x^{2}+y^{2}=1
, we have

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}(x,y)=\frac{\partial\tilde{\psi}}{\partial r}(x,y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\partial P_{\mathbb{H}}}{\partial r}(x,y,\tilde{x})\eta(\tilde{x})\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\tilde{x}^{2}y-y}{(\tilde{x}^{2}-2x\tilde{x}+1)^{2}}\eta(\tilde{x})\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}\\
=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathcal{E}}\frac{\tilde{x}^{2}y-y}{(\tilde{x}^{2}-2x\tilde{x}+1)^{2}}\eta(\tilde{x})\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\tilde{x}^{2}y-y}{(\tilde{x}^{2}-2x\tilde{x}+1)^{2}}\eta(\tilde{x})\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}.

Using a change of variable and the fact that
\eta(x)=\eta\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)
for
x\in(-1,1)\setminus\{0\}
in the last integral in the formula above, we obtain that

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathcal{E}}\frac{\tilde{x}^{2}y-y}{(\tilde{x}^{2}-2x\tilde{x}+1)^{2}}\eta(\tilde{x})\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}\\
+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathcal{E}}\frac{y-\tilde{x}^{2}y}{(\tilde{x}^{2}-2x\tilde{x}+1)^{2}}\eta(\tilde{x})\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}=0\qquad\qquad(x^{2}+y^{2}=1,\ y>0).

We have thus shown that the function
\psi
defined by (
4.3
) defines a classical solution of (
4.1
).

To prove the uniqueness of the solution constructed above,
we use an argument inspired by the Schwarz reflection principle for harmonic functions.
More precisely, let
\Phi
be a harmonic function on
\Omega
, which is continuous and bounded on
\overline{\Omega}
and with

\Phi(x,0)=v(x)\qquad\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathcal{E}),

\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial r}(x,y)=0\qquad\qquad\qquad(x^{2}+y^{2}=1,\ y>0),

with the last equality understood in a weak sense.
We extend
\Phi
to a function
\tilde{\Phi}
defined on
\mathbb{H}
by setting

\tilde{\Phi}(x,y)=\begin{cases}\Phi(x,y)&\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathbb{R},y\geqslant 0,x^{2}+y^{2}\geqslant 1)\\
\Phi\left(\frac{x}{x^{2}+y^{2}},\frac{y}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\right)&\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathbb{R},y\geqslant 0,x^{2}+y^{2}<1).\end{cases}

Then
\tilde{\Phi}
is continuous and bounded on
\overline{\mathbb{H}}
. Moreover, since
\frac{\partial\tilde{\Phi}}{\partial r}

is also continuous on
\mathbb{H}
, it follows that
\tilde{\Phi}
is harmonic on
\mathbb{H}
. Moreover, we clearly have
\Phi(x,0)=\eta(x)

for
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
where
\eta
is the function defined in (
4.4
). Thus, according to
[
1
, Theorem 7.5]

\tilde{\Phi}
coincides with the Poisson integral of
\eta
, i.e., with the function
\tilde{\psi}
defined in (
3.3
).
From this it follows that
\Phi=\psi
on
\overline{\Omega}
, which ends the uniqueness proof.

We still have to prove (
4.3
). To this aim, we first note that from (
3.3
)
it follows that for every

\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{H}
we have

(4.5)

\tilde{\psi}(x,y)=\int_{\mathcal{E}}P_{\mathbb{H}}(x,y,\tilde{x})\eta(\tilde{x})\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}+\int_{-1}^{1}P_{\mathbb{H}}(x,y,\tilde{x})\eta(\tilde{x})\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}.

We next remark that from the definition (
3.2
) of the Poisson kernel and the fact that
\eta(x)=\eta\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)
on
\mathbb{R}^{*}

it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}P_{\mathbb{H}}(x,y,\tilde{x})\eta(\tilde{x})\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{y}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\eta(\tilde{x})}{(x-\tilde{x})^{2}+y^{2}}\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{y}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\eta(\tilde{x})}{{\tilde{x}}^{2}\left[\left(\frac{x}{\tilde{x}}-1\right)^{2}+\frac{y^{2}}{\tilde{x}^{2}}\right]}\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{y}{\pi}\int_{\mathcal{E}}\frac{\eta(\tilde{x})}{\left(x\tilde{x}-1\right)^{2}+y^{2}\tilde{x}^{2}}\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}\qquad\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathbb{R},\ y>0).

Combining the above formula with (
4.5
), (
3.2
) and the fact that
\psi
is the restriction to
\overline{\Omega}
of

\tilde{\psi}
yields the conclusion (
4.3
).

Finally, the fact that
D_{\Omega}
is a linear bounded operator from
W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
to
C_{b}(\overline{\Omega})
follows from
(
3.6
), (
4.4
), (
4.5
) and the continuity of the embedding of
W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
into

C_{b}(\overline{\mathcal{E}})
.
∎

5.
A Dirichlet-to-Neumann map on
\Omega

In this section we construct and study a Dirichlet-to-Neumann map for the domain
\Omega

defined in (
2.5
). More precisely, we study the map
\Lambda_{\Omega}
, associating to a function
v

defined on
\mathcal{E}
, with
\mathcal{E}
defined in (
2.5
), the trace on
\mathcal{E}

of the normal derivative of the solution
\psi
of (
4.1
).
Rather than defining this map by duality, as is usually done in the literature (see, for instance, Lannes
[
11
]

and references therein), we use below the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}
of the half-plane, introduced in Section
3
, and a symmetry argument.
Our aim being to define
\Lambda_{\Omega}
on
W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
, we first define this operator
on the subspace
V_{0}(\mathcal{E})
of
W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
formed by the functions whose support is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
.
Note that for
v\in W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
the function
\eta(v)
defined in (
4.4
) lies in
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Definition 5.1
.

The Dirichlet-to-Neumann
map associated to the boundary value problem (
4.1
),
denoted by
\Lambda_{\Omega}
, is defined on
V_{0}(\mathcal{E})
by

(5.1)

(\Lambda_{\Omega}v)(x)=\left[\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}(\eta(v))\right](x)\qquad\qquad\qquad(v\in V_{0}(\mathcal{E}),x\in\mathcal{E}),

where
\eta(v)
has been defined in (
4.4
).

Remark 5.2
.

If
v\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})
then
\eta(v)
clearly lies in
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R})

and, according to (
3.20
),
\left[(\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}\eta(v))\right](x)=-\frac{\partial(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta(v))}{\partial y}(x,0)

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
, in a classical sense. Thus, using the fact, already used in the proof of Proposition
4.1
, that

D_{\Omega}v=D_{\mathbb{H}}(\eta(v))
on
\Omega
it follows that for
v\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})
, we have that

\left[(\Lambda_{\Omega}v)\right](x)=-\frac{\partial(D_{\Omega}v)}{\partial y}(x,0)
for every
x\in\mathcal{E}
, in a classical sense.

The result below plays an important role in the remaining part of this paper.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\Omega
be defined in (
2.5
) and let
\Lambda_{\Omega}
be the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
introduced in Definition
5.1
.
Then
\Lambda_{\Omega}
extends to an operator, still denoted by
\Lambda_{\Omega}
, in
\mathcal{L}(W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E}),L^{2}(\mathcal{E}))
. Moreover, we have

(5.2)

\left(\Lambda_{\Omega}v\right)(x)=-(\mathcal{H}(g(v))(x)-\frac{1}{x^{2}}(\mathcal{H}(g(v))\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)\\
-\frac{v(1)-v(-1)}{\pi x}\qquad\qquad(v\in W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E}),\ x\in\mathcal{E}),

where
g(v)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is the extension of
v^{\prime}
by zero outside
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{H}
is the Hilbert transform defined in (
3.12
).

Proof.

Let
v\in W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
be such that there exists
a>0
with
v(x)=0
for
|x|\geqslant a
. Then the function
\eta(v)
defined by
(
4.4
) lies in
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})
. For the sake of simplicity, this function will simply be denoted by
\eta
in the remaining part of this proof.

As seen in the proof of Proposition
4.1

the solution
\psi
of (
4.1
) is the restriction to
\overline{\Omega}
of the
solution
\tilde{\psi}
of (
3.4
)-(
3.5
). We thus have

(5.3)

\left(\Lambda_{\Omega}v\right)(x)=\left(\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}\eta\right)(x)\qquad\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathcal{E}\ {\rm a.e.})\,.

Using the fact that
\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}\in\mathcal{L}(W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}),L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
it follows that
\Lambda_{\Omega}v\in L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
.
Moreover, using (
3.14
) it follows that

(5.4)

\left(\Lambda_{\Omega}v\right)(x)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\int_{|\tilde{x}-x|\geqslant\varepsilon}\frac{\eta^{\prime}(\tilde{x})}{x-\tilde{x}}\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}\\
=-\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\left(\int_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(x)}\frac{\eta^{\prime}(\tilde{x})}{x-\tilde{x}}\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}+\int_{\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(x)}\frac{\eta^{\prime}(\tilde{x})}{x-\tilde{x}}\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}\right)\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathcal{E}),

where

(5.5)

\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(x)=\{\tilde{x}\in\mathcal{E}\ \ |\ \ |x-\tilde{x}|>\varepsilon\}\qquad\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathcal{E}).

(5.6)

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(x)=\{\tilde{x}\in(-1,1)\ \ |\ \ |x-\tilde{x}|>\varepsilon\}\qquad\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathcal{E}).

It is easily seen that

(5.7)

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(x)=(-1,1)\qquad\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathcal{E},\varepsilon<|x|-1),

so that (
5.4
) can be rewritten

(5.8)

\left(\Lambda_{\Omega}v\right)(x)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\left(\int_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(x)}\frac{\eta^{\prime}(\tilde{x})}{x-\tilde{x}}\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}+\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\eta^{\prime}(\tilde{x})}{x-\tilde{x}}\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}\right)\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathcal{E}).

We next remark that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(x)}\frac{\eta^{\prime}(\tilde{x})}{x-\tilde{x}}\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\int_{|\tilde{x}-x|>\varepsilon}\frac{g(\tilde{x})}{x-\tilde{x}}\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}=\pi(\mathcal{H}g)(x)\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathcal{E}).

Denote

(5.9)

h_{1}(x)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(x)}\frac{\eta^{\prime}(\tilde{x})}{x-\tilde{x}}\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathcal{E}).

Using the fact that the Hilbert transform, as defined in (
3.12
), is an isometry of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we obtain that

(5.10)

\|h_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E})}=\pi\|\mathcal{H}g(v)\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E})}\leqslant\pi\|\mathcal{H}g(v)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\\
=\pi\|g(v)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant\pi\|v\|_{W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})}.

Let

(5.11)

h_{2}(x)=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\eta^{\prime}(\tilde{x})}{x-\tilde{x}}\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}\qquad\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathcal{E}).

Then

(5.12)

h_{2}(x)=\frac{\eta(1)-\eta(-1)}{x}+\frac{1}{x}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\tilde{x}\eta^{\prime}(\tilde{x})}{x-\tilde{x}}\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}\\
=\frac{v(1)-v(-1)}{x}-\frac{1}{x^{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{v^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{x}}\right)}{\frac{1}{\tilde{x}}-\frac{1}{x}}\frac{1}{{\tilde{x}}^{2}}\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}\\
=\frac{v(1)-v(-1)}{x}-\frac{1}{x^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{E}}\frac{v^{\prime}(s)}{s-\frac{1}{x}}\,{\rm d}s\\
=\frac{v(1)-v(-1)}{x}+\frac{1}{x^{2}}(\mathcal{H}g)\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathcal{E}).

From the last formula it clearly follows that

(5.13)

\|h_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E})}\leqslant c\|v\|_{W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})},

where
c
is a universal constant.

On the other hand, from (
5.8
) we have

(5.14)

\left(\Lambda_{\Omega}v\right)(x)=-\frac{1}{\pi}(h_{1}(x)+h_{2}(x))\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathcal{E}).

Combining the last formula with (
5.10
) and (
5.13
) it follows that there exists a universal
constant
c>0
such that the inequality

\|\Lambda_{\Omega}v\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E})}\leqslant c\|v\|_{W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})}

holds for every
v\in W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
which vanishes on
(-\infty,-a]\cup[a,+\infty)
for some
a>0
.
By a density argument, it follows that the last inequality holds for every
v\in W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
, which implies the first conclusion of the proposition.

Finally, (
5.2
) follows from (
5.9
),(
5.12
) and (
5.14
).
∎

The result below shows, in particular, that the solution
(D_{\Omega}v)
of (
4.1
) given by (
4.3
)
is also the solution of (
4.1
) in the variational sense introduced in
[
11
]
. Moreover, this result provides an important identity involving
\Lambda_{\Omega}
.

To precisely state this result we introduce the Beppo-Levi space

\dot{W}^{1,2}(\Omega):=\{\varphi\in L_{\rm loc}^{1}(\Omega),\nabla\varphi\in L^{2}(\Omega)\}.

Proposition 5.4
.

With the notation in Proposition
5.3
, for every
v\in W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
the operator
D_{\Omega}
defined in (
4.3
) satisfies

(5.15)

D_{\Omega}v\in\dot{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)\qquad\qquad(v\in W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E}))

with

(5.16)

\|\nabla(D_{\Omega}v)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leqslant C\|v\|_{W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})},

for a constant
C>0
. Moreover,
\Lambda_{\Omega}
(defined in (
5.2
)) satisfies

(5.17)

\int_{\Omega}\nabla(D_{\Omega}v)\cdot\overline{\nabla(D_{\Omega}u)}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y=\int_{\mathcal{E}}\left(\Lambda_{\Omega}v\right)\,\overline{u}\,{\rm d}x\qquad\qquad\qquad(v,u\in W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})).

Proof.

First, let
\mathcal{D}(\overline{\mathcal{E}})
be the space of restrictions to
\overline{\mathcal{E}}
of functions in
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R})
. This space is obviously contained in
W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
. Let

u,v\in\mathcal{D}(\overline{\mathcal{E}})
and let
\eta(v),\eta(u)
be their extensions defined by (
4.4
). We denote by
\mathbb{D}^{+}
the upper unit half-disk, i.e.,

(5.18)

\mathbb{D}^{+}=\left\{\left.\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ \ \right|\ \ x^{2}+y^{2}<1,y>0\right\}.

Using (
3.17
) we get

(5.19)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla(D_{\Omega}v)\cdot\overline{\nabla(D_{\Omega}u)}{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{H}}\nabla(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta(v))\cdot\overline{\nabla(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta(u))}{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{D}^{+}}\nabla(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta(v))\cdot\overline{\nabla(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta(u))}{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}\eta(v)\,\overline{\eta(u)}{\rm d}x-\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}^{+}}\frac{\partial(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta(v))}{\partial\nu}\overline{\eta(u)}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}\eta(v)\,\overline{\eta(u)}{\rm d}x+\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\partial(D_{\mathbb{H}}\eta(v))}{\partial y}(x,0)\overline{\eta(u)}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle-\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\underbrace{\frac{\partial(D_{\Omega}v)}{\partial r}(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)}_{\text{$0$}}\cos\theta{\rm\ d}\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}\eta(v)\,\overline{\eta(u)}{\rm d}x-\int_{-1}^{1}\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}\eta(v)\,\overline{\eta(u)}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{E}}\Lambda_{\mathbb{H}}\eta(v)\,\overline{\eta(u)}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{E}}\Lambda_{\Omega}v\,\overline{u}{\rm d}x,

so we get (
5.17
) for
u,v\in\mathcal{D}(\overline{\mathcal{E}})
. Now let
u,v\in W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
. Since
\mathcal{D}(\overline{\mathcal{E}})
is dense in
W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
, there exist sequences
(v_{n}),(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{D}(\overline{\mathcal{E}})
such that
v_{n}\to v
and
u_{n}\to u
in
W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(5.20)

\int_{\Omega}\nabla(D_{\Omega}v_{n})\cdot\overline{\nabla(D_{\Omega}u_{n})}{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y=\int_{\mathcal{E}}\Lambda_{\Omega}v_{n}\,\overline{u_{n}}{\rm d}x.

By Proposition
5.3
,
\Lambda_{\Omega}
is a bounded operator from
W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
to
L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
, so that, taking
u_{n}=v_{n}
, we obtain that for every
m,\ n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(5.21)

\|\nabla(D_{\Omega}(v_{n}-v_{m}))\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\langle\Lambda_{\Omega}(v_{n}-v_{m}),v_{n}-v_{m}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E})}\leqslant C\|v_{n}-v_{m}\|_{W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})}^{2}.

This implies that
(\nabla(D_{\Omega}v_{n}))
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, so that it converges to a certain
{\bf\Psi}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. On the other hand, by Proposition
4.1
, we have
D_{\Omega}v_{n}\to D_{\Omega}v
in
C_{b}(\overline{\Omega})
, so that
\nabla(D_{\Omega}v_{n})\to\nabla(D_{\Omega}v)
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
.
It follows that
\nabla(D_{\Omega}v)={\bf\Psi}
, so that
\nabla(D_{\Omega}v)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. We have thus shown that
D_{\Omega}v\in\dot{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
and that
D_{\Omega}v_{n}\to D_{\Omega}v\in\dot{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
. We can thus pass to the limit in (
5.20
) and use again Proposition
5.3
to obtain (
5.16
) and (
5.17
).
∎

A direct consequence of Proposition
5.4
is

Corollary 5.5
.

The operator
\Lambda_{\Omega}:W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
is symmetric on
L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
.

The main result of this section is:

Theorem 5.6
.

With the assumptions and notation in Proposition
5.3

the operator
\Lambda_{\Omega}
, with domain
\mathcal{D}(\Lambda_{\Omega})=W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
,
is an (unbounded) self-adjoint operator on
H=L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
.

In order to prove Theorem
5.6
we introduce, following an idea
already exploited in Friedman and Shinbrot
[
5
]
,
the boundary value problem

(5.22)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Delta\phi(x,y)=0&&\left(\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}\in\Omega\right)\\
&-\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}(x,0)+\phi(x,0)=f(x)&&(x\in\mathcal{E})\\
&\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial r}(x,y)=0&&(x\in\mathcal{E},\ y>0,\ x^{2}+y^{2}=1),\end{aligned}\right.

where
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
is given. We define weak solutions of (
5.22
).
as follows:

Definition 5.7
.

Let
V(\Omega)
be the closure of
\mathcal{D}(\overline{\Omega})
with respect to the norm

\|\Psi\|_{V(\Omega)}^{2}=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\Psi|^{2}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y+\int_{\mathcal{E}}|\Psi(x,0)|^{2}\,{\rm d}x\qquad\qquad(\Psi\in\mathcal{D}(\overline{\Omega})).

A function
\phi\in V(\Omega)
is called solution of (
5.22
) if

(5.23)

\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\Psi\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y+\int_{\mathcal{E}}\phi(x,0)\Psi(x,0)\,{\rm d}x=\int_{\mathcal{E}}f(x)\Psi(x,0)\,{\rm d}x\qquad(\Psi\in V(\Omega)).

Proposition 5.8
.

For every
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
the system (
5.22
) admits a unique solution
\phi\in V(\Omega)
, in the sense
of Definition
5.7
. Moreover, the operator
T
defined by

(Tf)(x)=\phi(x,0)\qquad\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathcal{E}),

is bounded from
L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
into
W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
.

Proof.

The existence and uniqueness result is directly obtained by the Riesz representation theorem.

Taking next
\Psi=\phi
in (
5.23
) it follows that

\int_{\mathcal{E}}|\phi(x,0)|^{2}\,{\rm d}x\leqslant\int_{\mathcal{E}}f(x)\phi(x,0)\,{\rm d}x.

The above estimate clearly implies that
T\in\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{E}))
and

(5.24)

\|T\|_{\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{E}))}\leqslant 1.

On the other hand, since
\phi
is a solution to the weak formulation (
5.23
), it follows that
\Delta\phi=0
in

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
. Consequently, the trace of the exterior normal derivative
\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\nu}
of
\phi
is
defined as an element of
\left[W^{1,2}(\partial\Omega)\right]^{\prime}
(the dual of

W^{1,2}(\partial\Omega)
with respect to the pivot space
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
)
by the formula

\left\langle\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\nu},v\right\rangle_{\left[W^{1,2}(\partial\Omega)\right]^{\prime},W^{1,2}(\partial\Omega)}=\langle\nabla\phi,\nabla(D_{\Omega}v)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\qquad(v\in W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})).

Moreover, by Proposition
5.4
, we have
D_{\Omega}v\in V(\Omega)
for every
v\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)
.
Comparing the above formula and (
5.23
) (with
\psi=D_{\Omega}v
) it follows that
\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\nu}\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
, that this trace vanishes on
\partial\Omega\setminus\mathcal{E}
and that

\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\nu}=f-\phi=f-Tf\qquad\qquad{\rm in}\quad L^{2}(\mathcal{E}).

Using next (
5.24
) it follows that the map
x\mapsto\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}(x,0)
lies in
L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
and

\int_{\mathcal{E}}\left|\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}(x,0)\right|^{2}\,{\rm d}x\leqslant 2\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E})}.

Using next Theorem A from Brown
[
4
]
we obtain the announced conclusion.
∎

We are now in a position to prove the main result of this section.

Proof of Theorem
5.6
.

According to a classical result (see, for instance, Tucsnak and Weiss
[
15
, Proposition 2.3.4]
),
it suffices to prove that
\Lambda_{\Omega}:\mathcal{D}(\Lambda_{\Omega})\to H
is symmetric and that
\mathbb{I}+\Lambda_{\Omega}
is onto.
The symmetry of
\Lambda_{\Omega}
has already been established in Corollary
5.5
.
To prove the surjectivity of
\mathbb{I}+\Lambda_{\Omega}
, consider
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{E})

and denote
v=Tf\in W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
, where
T
is the operator introduced in Proposition
5.8
.
Then the corresponding solution
\phi
of (
5.22
) clearly satisfies

(\Lambda_{\Omega}v)(x)=-\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}(x,0)\qquad\qquad\qquad(x\in\mathcal{E}),

so that

v+\Lambda_{\Omega}v=f.

We have thus shown that
\mathbb{I}+\Lambda_{\Omega}
maps
W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
onto
L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
, which ends the proof.
∎

Remark 5.9
.

We have seen that the operator
\Lambda_{\Omega}
is self-adjoint on
L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
. Moreover,
from (
5.17
) we have

(5.25)

\langle\Lambda_{\Omega}v,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E})}=\|\nabla(D_{\Omega}v)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\geqslant 0\qquad\qquad\qquad(v\in W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})).

The above inequality is strict for every
v\neq 0
since
\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\to\infty}(D_{\Omega}v)(x,0)=0
for every
v\in W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
.
From this it follows that the map
v\mapsto\sqrt{\langle\Lambda_{\Omega}v,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E})}}
defines a norm on
W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
.
However, due to the fact that the Poincaré inequality does not hold in
\dot{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, the completion of
W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})

with respect to the norm
\sqrt{\langle\Lambda_{\Omega}v,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E})}}
is not contained in
L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
.
To avoid this inconvenience we use below the operator
\mathbb{I}+\Lambda_{\Omega}
instead of
\Lambda_{\Omega}
to define the function spaces appearing in the statement of our main result. This operator is clearly strictly positive. Thus, if we define
\mathcal{D}((\mathbb{I}+g\Lambda_{\Omega})^{\frac{1}{2}})
as the completion of
\mathcal{D}(\Lambda_{\Omega})=W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
with respect to the norm

(5.26)

\|v\|_{\mathcal{D}((\mathbb{I}+g\Lambda_{\Omega})^{\frac{1}{2}})}=\|(\mathbb{I}+g\Lambda_{\Omega})^{\frac{1}{2}}v\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E})},

we have

\mathcal{D}((\mathbb{I}+g\Lambda_{\Omega}))\subset{\mathcal{D}((\mathbb{I}+g\Lambda_{\Omega})^{\frac{1}{2}})}\subset L^{2}(\mathcal{E}),

with continuous and dense embeddings.

The space
\mathcal{D}((\mathbb{I}+g\Lambda_{\Omega})^{\frac{1}{2}})
introduced in the remark above can be described in a more precise way. To this aim, we recall the definition of the Sobolev space
W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(I)
, for a given interval
I
, which is:

(5.27)

\displaystyle W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(I)=\left\{v\in L^{2}(I)\ \ |\ \ \|v\|_{W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(I)}<\infty\right\},

(5.28)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(I)}^{2}=\|v\|_{L^{2}(I)}^{2}+\iint_{I\times I}\frac{|v(x)-v(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y.

When
I=\mathbb{R}
, this norm is given by

(5.29)

\|v\|_{W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+|\xi|)|\hat{v}(\xi)|^{2}{\rm d}\xi.

For more details, see
[
3
,
12
]
.

Lemma 5.10
.

With the above notation, we have
\mathcal{D}((\mathbb{I}+g\Lambda_{\Omega})^{\frac{1}{2}})=W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathcal{E})
.

Proof.

According to
[
13
, Theorem 4.36]
, since
\mathbb{I}+g\Lambda_{\Omega}
is a strictly positive operator, we have

(5.30)

\mathcal{D}((\mathbb{I}+g\Lambda_{\Omega})^{\frac{1}{2}})=(\mathcal{D}((\mathbb{I}+g\Lambda_{\Omega})^{0}),\mathcal{D}((\mathbb{I}+g\Lambda_{\Omega}))^{1})_{\frac{1}{2},2}=(L^{2}(\mathcal{E}),W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E}))_{\frac{1}{2},2}

where
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\frac{1}{2},2}
denotes the real interpolation space. On the other hand, according to
[
14
, Theorem B.8]
, we have

(5.31)

(L^{2}(\mathcal{E}),W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E}))_{\frac{1}{2},2}=W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathcal{E}),

which ends the proof.
∎

6.
Proof of the main result

In this section we combine the results in the previous sections with a semigroup theoretic approach to prove the main result in Theorem
2.1
.

Recall from Remark
5.9
that
\Lambda_{\Omega}
is a positive operator from
W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
to
L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
. Let us now introduce the spaces

(6.1)

\displaystyle H=L^{2}(\mathcal{E})\times\mathbb{C},

(6.2)

\displaystyle H_{1}=\mathcal{D}(\Lambda_{\Omega})\times\mathbb{C}=W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})\times\mathbb{C}

with the norms

(6.3)

\displaystyle\left\|\begin{bmatrix}v\\
h\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{H}^{2}=\|v\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E})}^{2}+|h|^{2}

(6.4)

\displaystyle\left\|\begin{bmatrix}v\\
h\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{H_{1}}^{2}=\|(\mathbb{I}+g\Lambda_{\Omega})v\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E})}^{2}+|h|^{2}.

Let
\mathcal{D}(A_{0})=H_{1}
and
A_{0}:\mathcal{D}(A_{0})\to H
be given by

(6.5)

A_{0}\begin{bmatrix}v\\
h\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}g\Lambda_{\Omega}v\\
\frac{2g}{\pi}h\end{bmatrix}.

Next, we introduce
H_{\frac{1}{2}}=\mathcal{D}((\mathbb{I}+g\Lambda_{\Omega})^{\frac{1}{2}})\times\mathbb{C}
with the norm

(6.6)

\left\|\begin{bmatrix}v\\
h\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{H_{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}=\|(\mathbb{I}+g\Lambda_{\Omega})^{\frac{1}{2}}v\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E})}^{2}+|h|^{2}.

Since
\Lambda_{\Omega}
is self-adjoint and positive from
W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})
to
L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
, it clearly follows that
A_{0}
is self-adjoint and
\langle A_{0}z,z\rangle_{H}\geqslant 0

for every
z\in\mathcal{D}(A_{0})
, i.e.,
A_{0}
is a positive operator on
H
, with domain
H_{1}
. From Lemma
5.10
, we have
H_{\frac{1}{2}}=W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathcal{E})\times\mathbb{C}
and

(6.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(A_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}})=H_{\frac{1}{2}}

(6.8)

\displaystyle A_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\begin{bmatrix}v\\
h\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{g}\Lambda_{\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}v\\
\sqrt{\frac{2g}{\pi}}h\end{bmatrix}\qquad\qquad\left(v\in W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathcal{E}),h\in\mathbb{C}\right).

Next, we define

(6.9)

X=H_{\frac{1}{2}}\times H=W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathcal{E})\times\mathbb{C}\times L^{2}(\mathcal{E})\times\mathbb{C}

with the norm

(6.10)

\displaystyle\left\|\begin{bmatrix}v\\
h\\
u\\
\ell\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{X}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|A_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\begin{bmatrix}v\\
h\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{H}^{2}+\left\|\begin{bmatrix}u\\
\ell\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|(\mathbb{I}+g\Lambda_{\Omega})^{\frac{1}{2}}v\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E})}^{2}+\frac{2g}{\pi}|h|^{2}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E})}^{2}+|\ell|^{2}.

Lemma 6.1
.

With the above notation,
let
A:\mathcal{D}(A)\to X
be the operator defined by

(6.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(A)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}(A_{0})\times\mathcal{D}(A_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}})=W^{1,2}(\mathcal{E})\times\mathbb{C}\times W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathcal{E})\times\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle A\begin{bmatrix}v\\
h\\
u\\
\ell\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}u\\
\ell\\
-A_{0}\begin{bmatrix}v\\
h\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}u\\
\ell\\
-g\Lambda_{\Omega}v\\
-\frac{2g}{\pi}h\end{bmatrix},

where
A_{0}
has been defined in (
6.5
).
Then,
A
generates a
C^{0}
group on
X
.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{A}:\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A})\to X
be the operator defined by
\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A})=\mathcal{D}(A)

\tilde{A}=A+Q,

where
Q\in\mathcal{L}(X)
is defined by

Q\begin{bmatrix}v\\
h\\
u\\
\ell\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
-v\\
-h\end{bmatrix}\qquad\qquad\qquad\left(\begin{bmatrix}v\\
h\\
u\\
\ell\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{D}(A)\right).

Since
A_{0}
is a positive operator from
H_{1}
to
H
, we can apply Proposition 3.7.6 in
[
15
]
, with
\mathbb{I}+A_{0}
instead of
A_{0}
, it follows that
\tilde{A}
is skew-adjoint, thus it generates a unitary group on
X
. Since
A=\tilde{A}-Q
, with
Q\in\mathcal{L}(X)

the announced conclusion follows from a standard result, see, for instance, Theorem 2.11.2 in
[
15
]
.
∎

Let us define
P:X\to X
as

(6.12)

P\begin{bmatrix}v\\
h\\
u\\
\ell\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
-g\ell\sigma\\
\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}(D_{\Omega}u)(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\cos\theta\,{\rm d}\theta\end{bmatrix},

where
\sigma\in L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
is given by
\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{x^{2}}
, for
x\in\mathcal{E}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

With the above notation, we have
P\in\mathcal{L}(X)
.

Proof.

Let
[v,h,u,\ell]^{T}\in X
. From Proposition
4.1
we have

(6.13)

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}(D_{\Omega}u)(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\cos\theta\,{\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle=2\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\int_{\mathcal{E}}\frac{u(\tilde{x})\cos^{2}\theta}{\tilde{x}^{2}-2\tilde{x}\sin\theta+1}\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}\,{\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle=2\int_{\mathcal{E}}u(\tilde{x})\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\frac{\cos^{2}\theta}{\tilde{x}^{2}-2\tilde{x}\sin\theta+1}\,{\rm d}\theta\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}.

We note that

(6.14)

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\frac{\cos^{2}\theta}{\tilde{x}^{2}-2\tilde{x}\sin\theta+1}\,{\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi/2}^{0}\frac{\cos^{2}\theta}{\tilde{x}^{2}-2\tilde{x}\sin\theta+1}\,{\rm d}\theta+\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{\cos^{2}\theta}{\tilde{x}^{2}-2\tilde{x}\sin\theta+1}\,{\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{\cos^{2}\theta}{\tilde{x}^{2}+2\tilde{x}\sin\theta+1}\,{\rm d}\theta+\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{\cos^{2}\theta}{\tilde{x}^{2}-2\tilde{x}\sin\theta+1}\,{\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle=:I_{1}(\tilde{x})+I_{2}(\tilde{x}).

We show below that the map
\tilde{x}\mapsto I_{1}(\tilde{x})
is in
L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
. To this aim, we note that

(6.15)

\displaystyle I_{1}(\tilde{x})

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{\cos\theta}{\tilde{x}^{2}+2\tilde{x}\sin\theta+1}{\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{{\rm d}s}{\tilde{x}^{2}+2\tilde{x}s+1}=\frac{1}{2\tilde{x}}\ln\left(\tilde{x}^{2}+2\tilde{x}s+1\right)\Bigg|_{s=0}^{s=1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\tilde{x}}\ln\left((\tilde{x}+1)^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2\tilde{x}}\ln(\tilde{x}^{2}+1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tilde{x}}\ln|\tilde{x}+1|-\frac{1}{2\tilde{x}}\ln(\tilde{x}^{2}+1)

For
\varepsilon>0
small enough we have

(6.16)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{-1}\frac{1}{\tilde{x}^{2}}\ln^{2}|\tilde{x}+1|\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{-2}\frac{1}{\tilde{x}^{2}}\ln^{2}|\tilde{x}+1|\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}+\int_{-2}^{-1-\varepsilon}\frac{1}{\tilde{x}^{2}}\ln^{2}|\tilde{x}+1|\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}

\displaystyle+\int_{-1-\varepsilon}^{-1}\frac{1}{\tilde{x}^{2}}\ln^{2}|\tilde{x}+1|\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}

For the first integral on the right-hand side, a straightforward change of variables yields

(6.17)

\int_{-\infty}^{-2}\frac{1}{\tilde{x}^{2}}\ln^{2}|\tilde{x}+1|\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}=\int_{-2}^{-1}\frac{1}{\tilde{x}^{2}}\ln^{2}|\tilde{x}+1|\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}<+\infty

As for the second integral, taking the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
leads to the same value as the first one. It only remains to estimate the third integral as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

(6.18)

\displaystyle\int_{-1-\varepsilon}^{-1}\frac{1}{\tilde{x}^{2}}\ln^{2}|\tilde{x}+1|\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{-1-\varepsilon}^{-1}\ln^{2}|\tilde{x}+1|\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\varepsilon}^{0}\ln^{2}|s|\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=s\ln^{2}|s|\Bigg|_{-\varepsilon}^{0}-2\int_{-\varepsilon}^{0}\ln|s|\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\ln^{2}(\varepsilon)-2\varepsilon\ln(\varepsilon)+2(1-\varepsilon)\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0^{+}]{}2.

On the other hand, we have

(6.19)

\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\tilde{x}^{2}}\ln^{2}|\tilde{x}+1|\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}<+\infty.

Using (
6.17
), (
6.18
) and (
6.19
) in (
6.15
) we obtain

(6.20)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{E}}|I_{1}(\tilde{x})|^{2}\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathcal{E}}\frac{1}{\tilde{x}^{2}}\ln^{2}|\tilde{x}+1|\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}+\int_{\mathcal{E}}\frac{1}{4\tilde{x}^{2}}\ln(\tilde{x}^{2}+1)\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}<+\infty,

so that indeed
I_{1}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
. Similarly, we get
I_{2}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
. Going back to (
6.13
) and using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we get

(6.21)

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}(D_{\Omega}u)(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\cos\theta\,{\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle=2\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\int_{\mathcal{E}}\frac{u(\tilde{x})\cos^{2}\theta}{\tilde{x}^{2}-2\tilde{x}\sin\theta+1}\,{\rm d}\tilde{x}\,{\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle=2\int_{\mathcal{E}}u(\tilde{x})\left(I_{1}(\tilde{x})+I_{2}(\tilde{x})\right){\rm d}\tilde{x}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{1}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E})},

where
C_{1}=2\|I_{1}+I_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E})}
.
Finally, we get

(6.22)

\displaystyle\left\|P\begin{bmatrix}v\\
h\\
u\\
\ell\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{X}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|g\ell\sigma\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E})}^{2}+\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\left|\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}(D_{\Omega}u)(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\,\cos\theta\,{\rm d}\theta\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(\frac{2}{3}g^{2}|\ell|^{2}+\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E})}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(\|(\mathbb{I}+g\Lambda_{\Omega})^{\frac{1}{2}}v\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E})}^{2}+\frac{2g}{\pi}|h|^{2}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E})}^{2}+|\ell|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left\|\begin{bmatrix}v\\
h\\
u\\
\ell\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{X}^{2}

where
C>0
absorbed the other constants in each line.
∎

We are now in a position to prove Theorem
2.1
.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

We first note that, denoting
z_{0}=[v_{0},h_{0},v_{1},h_{1}]^{T}
and
z(t)=\left[v(t,\cdot),h(t),\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}(t,\cdot),\dot{h}(t)\right]^{T}
,
the system (
2.13
) can be rephrased as

(6.23)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{z}(t)&=A_{1}z(t)+F(t)\qquad(t\geqslant 0),\\
z(0)&=z_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

where
\mathcal{D}(A_{1})=\mathcal{D}(A)
,
A_{1}=A+P
, with
A
and
P
defined in (
6.11
) and (
6.12
), respectively, and

(6.24)

F(t)=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\\
\frac{f(t)}{\pi\rho}\end{bmatrix}.

By Lemma
6.1
, the operator
A
generates a group
\mathbb{T}=(\mathbb{T}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
on
X
.
From Lemma
6.2
we know that
P\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. A standard perturbation argument (see, for instance, Theorem 2.11.2 in
[
15
]
) implies that
A_{1}
generates a
C^{0}
group
\mathbb{T}^{P}=(\mathbb{T}_{t}^{P})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
on
X
. Since
f\in L_{{\rm loc}}^{1}([0,\infty))
we have (see Theorem 4.1.6 in
[
15
]
) that for every
z_{0}\in X
the Cauchy problem (
6.23
) has a unique solution
z\in C([0,\infty),X)
given by

(6.25)

z(t)=\mathbb{T}_{t}^{P}z_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{T}_{t-s}^{P}F(s)\,{\rm d}s\qquad(t\geqslant 0).

This fact clearly implies the first assertion of Theorem
2.1
. The second assertion of Theorem
2.1
simply follows from
the well known fact that, if
z_{0}\in\mathcal{D}(A_{1})
and
F\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}([0,\infty),\mathcal{D}(A))
, then the function
z
defined by (
6.25
) is continuous from
[0,\infty)
to
\mathcal{D}(A_{1})
.
∎
