A multi-objective particle swarm algorithm based on hierarchical clustering reference point maintenance
Authors: Siwan Chen, Yanmin Liu, Jie Yang, Aijia Ouyang, Jiang Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn multi-objective particle swarm optimization (MOPSO), challenges persist, including low diversity in external archives, ambiguous individual optimal choice mechanisms, high sensitivity to parameter settings, and the arduous task of balancing global exploration and local exploitation capabilities. To address these issues, this paper introduces a novel multi-objective particle swarm optimization algorithm named HCRMOPSO. The proposed algorithm innovatively leverages hierarchical clustering based on Ward’s linkage to generate the center of mass as reference points, which are then combined with the ideal point and crowding distance. This effectively maintains the external archive, thereby resolving the diversity deficiency commonly found in traditional MOPSO archives. Additionally, HCRMOPSO fuses multiple particles to update the personal best positions. It also adaptively tunes the flight parameters according to the diversity information within each particle’s neighborhood, enhancing the algorithm’s adaptability. Notably, a new strategy is designed for two specific types of particles, further optimizing the search process. The performance of HCRMOPSO is rigorously evaluated against ten existing algorithms on 22 standard test problems. Experimental results demonstrate that HCRMOPSO outperforms its counterparts on multiple benchmarks, showcasing superior effectiveness in handling multi-objective optimization tasks.Keywords: Multi-objective particle swarm optimization, Individual optimal choice, Hierarchical clustering, Flight parametersSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In practical applications, optimization problems constitute a vast and intricate category of challenges. Examples include UAV path planning1,2, energy system optimization3, production line scheduling optimization4, and engine performance optimization5, etc. These problems are often characterized by the need to optimize multiple objectives simultaneously, which frequently conflict with one another—such challenges are defined as Multi-Objective Optimization Problems (MOPs). Owing to the inherent conflict between objectives, it is impossible to identify a single solution that optimizes all objectives concurrently. Typically, enhancing the performance of one objective may lead to a decline in another. Thus, the core goal of MOPs is to discover a set of solutions that balance these conflicting objectives. This solution set is referred to as the non-dominated set or Pareto-optimal set (PS), while the corresponding objective values in the objective space are termed the Pareto-optimal frontier (PF). Meta-heuristic algorithms demonstrate significant potential in addressing MOPs. Consequently, scholars have proposed numerous swarm intelligence-based stochastic optimization algorithms, including Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)6, Artificial Lemming Algorithm (ALA)7, Birds of prey-based optimization (BPBO)8, and Animated Oat Optimization Algorithm (AOO)9. Among these, PSO stands out for its minimal parameters, easy tunability, rapid convergence, parallel computation capability, and robustness, leading to its extensive application. This has motivated researchers to extend particle swarm algorithms from single-objective to multi-objective frameworks for solving MOPs. MOPSO10 has been widely used in efficiency management of engineering projects11, path planning12,13, intelligent parameter optimization14, and many other fields.

When extending from single-objective to multi-objective scenarios, particle swarm algorithms encounter numerous challenges. For instance, with the continuous evolution of the population, a large number of non-dominated solutions will emerge. Since the capacity of the external archive storing these elite solutions is limited, how to retain high-quality solutions to more accurately and uniformly approach the actual Pareto Front (PF) and maintain the archive becomes a key issue. Additionally, challenges include the selection strategy for the individual best particles (pbest) and the tuning of flight parameters. These factors significantly affect the overall performance of the current algorithm. Up to now, researchers have proposed various variants to tackle these challenges.

In terms of archive maintenance, an adaptive grid15 is employed, which preferentially removes regions with higher particle density. However, the adaptive grid suffers from high computational complexity and difficulty in determining the grid interval division. Alternatively, crowding distance16 is used, but in high-dimensional spaces, relying solely on crowding distance fails to accurately measure particle density. To address this limitation, Wang et al.17 designed the adaptive Parallel Cell Coordinate System (PCCS) . Leveraging parallel coordinates—a popular technique for high-dimensional geometric visualization and multivariate data analysis—PCCS maps the archive from a Cartesian to a parallel cell coordinate system, calculating the density of non-dominated solutions based on parallel cell distances18. Zhang et al.19 further integrated PCCS with other methods, effectively preserving solution diversity and convergence within the archive. Yang et al.20 proposed a novel approach for external archive maintenance, which leverages the vector angle values of individual solutions. Many researchers also use metrics to maintain the archive, and representative MOPSOs using metrics are e.g., R2 metrics-based ANMPSO21and hypervolume-based MOPSOhv22 and IGD metrics-based MaOPSO-CA23, MaOEA/IGD24 . However, metrics-based computations suffer from exponential computational complexity as the number of solutions increases. Moreover, if the metrics are ill-suited, they may introduce bias toward specific properties during archive maintenance, thereby compromising the diversity and convergence of the final solution set.Reference vectors and reference point methods represent a newer and more parsimonious category of archive management techniques compared to metric-based approaches. These methods maintain archive diversity and convergence by leveraging predefined, uniformly distributed vectors or points as references. The method has been used in MaOPSO/vPF25, IDMOPSO26, PRMOPSO27 and other studies. However, the generation of reference vectors or points is highly sensitive to problem characteristics. If their distribution does not align with the geometry of the true Pareto front—such as concave, convex, or discontinuous structures—it can cause solution distribution deviation. Consequently, reference vectors and points typically require predefined design; otherwise, they may fail to effectively guide the search process. Clustering techniques also represent noteworthy technological approaches. Palakonda et al. proposed the Pre-DEMO28 algorithm within the differential evolution (DE) framework. This algorithm employs adaptive clustering to identify local knee points, providing preference-guided search processes. Additionally, the Ar-RGDEMO29 algorithm similarly adopts clustering strategies, retaining elite solutions to prevent the loss of high-quality solutions. Consequently, clustering methods offer significant technical support for enhancing the archive maintenance mechanisms of MOPSO algorithms.

In terms of the personal best particle (pbest) selection strategy, most approaches follow the Pareto domination principle of the original MOPSO10, which involves comparing solutions across two consecutive generations. However, in high-dimensional spaces, particles often fail to dominate each other, and the researchers, in order to solve the problem, fused the fitness values of multiple targets into a single metric to make comparisons27; or compared the two candidate pbest and gbest distances for comparison25. If only two consecutive generations of particles are considered, and if the particles are in an evolutionary state where the latest generation particle is chosen as pbest, this leads to a zero value in the individual cognition term of the updating formula. Consequently, pbest fails to provide any guiding effect for particle exploration during flight, rendering it ineffective in directing the search process. To address this issue, Huang et al.30 proposed storing particle positions from the most recent iterations, forming a memory interval that serves as the candidate pool for pbest selection.

Balancing particle exploration and exploitation during flight remains a challenging issue. Currently, researchers have implemented a range of adaptive parameter-tuning strategies to address this balance, leveraging evolutionary dynamics or particle diversity metrics. Notable examples include: AMOCSO31, AMOPSO32, MOPSO-AMS33, DAMOPSO34. However, these approaches often rely on single updating modes, which can compromise particle diversity and exploration capability. To address this, Kang et al.35 developed a particle kinetic equation, while Lin et al.36 introduced a multi-strategy search framework. While these strategies enhance learning modalities, particle evolution may require multiple consecutive generations to manifest, necessitating the integration of information across iterations. Thus, integrating information across multiple iterations for particle processing becomes essential to optimize evolutionary efficiency.

Clustering algorithms

Clustering is the process of grouping samples with similar characteristics and assigning samples with different characteristics to distinct categories. In recent years, clustering has been widely applied to multi-objective optimization problems. For instance, in ClGrMOEA37, the K-means clustering core is employed during the mating selection phase, forming a dual-layer strategy of cluster partitioning and convergence screening. In MSEMOEA38, it is utilized in the final environmental selection stage to address problems that cannot be decomposed by the voting allocation mechanism. However, K-means clustering presents challenges. Its key drawbacks include the need for manual specification of the number of clusters (k) and high sensitivity to initial cluster center assignments. Another clustering approach—hierarchical clustering—starts with “each sample as an individual cluster,” progressively merging the most similar clusters to ultimately form a top-down tree structure. Users need not predefine k; instead, they can split the tree at any “height” (corresponding to a cluster similarity threshold) based on business requirements or data structure. The resulting number of branches becomes the final k value. Its core logic is “iterative merging of similar clusters,” where each step is based on “inter-cluster similarity” (e.g., Euclidean distance, Manhattan distance, intra-cluster error sum of squares) rather than random initial points. The hierarchical clustering approach, characterized by “no manual specification of k” and “insensitivity to initial conditions,” effectively addresses the core shortcomings of K-means clustering. These features constitute its fundamental advantages in flexibility and stability.

Maintenance of the archive

The global optimal gbest is derived from the external archive, which comprises non-dominated solutions generated in each iteration. As iterations progress, an increasing number of non-dominated solutions are produced. Due to the limited archive capacity, a maintenance strategy is essential when the number of non-dominated solutions exceeds the preset threshold—this strategy must prioritize retaining solutions with superior diversity and convergence while removing suboptimal ones to ensure efficient archive management. This paper employs a reference point-based archive maintenance strategy, diverging from conventional reference point generation methods. We utilize a hierarchical clustering based on Ward’s linkage39 to generate reference points, which offer two key advantages adaptively: Reference points are dynamically generated according to the distribution of non-dominated solutions, eliminating the need for predefined parameters. The clustering approach aligns reference points with the geometric characteristics of the solution set, thereby enhancing the efficiency of archive maintenance.The following will provide a detailed explanation.

Update strategy for pbest

Update strategy for pbest

In traditional MOPSO, pbest update primarily relies on the Pareto dominance principle, which compares particles across successive generations. If a dominance relationship exists between two particles, the dominant one updates the pbest for the next generation; otherwise, a random selection is made. However, choosing the newest generation’s particle position as pbest will render the individual cognitive term in the particle update formula zero, leading to a meaningless pbest during flight. Conversely, selecting the previous generation’s position as pbest may trap the particle in a degraded or stagnant state, prone to local optima.

To address the aforementioned issue, this paper proposes a pbest update strategy. Unlike traditional methods that rely solely on a particle’s own evolutionary information, this new strategy integrates the particle with the best convergence within its neighborhood and the globally optimal particle gbest. The specific details of the update strategy are as follows:

First, based on the fitness of each particle, the number of dominated particles in the population that dominate that particle is calculated according to the principle of pareto domination, and then ranked according to this number. Obviously, the larger the number of dominated particles, the lower the ranking. There is no other particle to dominate except itself, so the ranking is 1. Compare the ranking of each particle in the current iteration with that in the previous iteration, and the higher-ranked one is selected. If the rankings of the previous and previous iterations are the same, a particle is randomly selected, which is noted as iebest. Then for each particle in the current iteration, calculate the distance from the particle to each particle in the population, select the nearest 10 particles as the solution in the neighborhood of the particle, then compare the rankings of these 10 particles, select the one with the highest ranking, i.e., the one with the best convergence in the neighborhood, and then select the particle corresponding to iebest, which is recorded as nbriebest. Multi-strategy learning

Multi-strategy learning

In traditional MOPSO, all particles in the population adopt the same learning strategy. This uniform learning mode can lead to slower convergence for certain particles. For instance, some particles may consistently rank at the bottom of convergence metrics across consecutive iterations. In such cases, continuing to update their learning process using pbest can hinder their search efficiency and fail to accelerate convergence. Alternatively, particles near the global optimal region might fall into local optima during subsequent learning phases, influenced by pbest and gbest. To address the aforementioned challenges, this paper proposes a novel learning mode for two categories of particles exhibiting special evolutionary behaviors. Since the evolutionary status of particles cannot be accurately judged by merely comparing two consecutive generations, this study integrates information from three iterations to enhance evaluation accuracy. Performance analysis of HCRMOPSO across various test functions

Analysis in the preceding two sections indicates that HCRMOPSO exhibits varying performance across different test functions: it significantly outperforms comparison algorithms on some functions while showing slightly inferior performance on others. This section provides a brief analysis of this discrepancy. ZDT Series Functions: Overall Superior Performance HCRMOPSO significantly outperforms the 10 comparison algorithms on the ZDT series functions, primarily due to three key factors. First, the ZDT problem features a regular and continuous Pareto Front (PF). Hierarchical clustering precisely generates reference points, and combining rational points with congestion distance effectively eliminates suboptimal solutions. When the archive size falls below the threshold, the mechanism of first deleting some solutions and then restoring high-quality ones proves highly effective for this type of problem. The deleted solutions are mostly approximations of the true PF, and their restoration rapidly improves convergence and distribution. Second, the search space for ZDT problems is relatively smooth. Particles update their pbest by integrating their position, domain optimality, and gbest information, enabling rapid guidance toward the global PF while enhancing solution diversity and convergence momentum. Third, particle distribution in ZDT problems is relatively uniform. Diversity metrics accurately reflect the search state, allowing dynamic parameter adjustment to balance exploration and exploitation. The core challenge in extending from two-dimensional to three-dimensional objectives lies in maintaining solution distribution within high-dimensional space—where solution convergence is typically easier to achieve than distribution. The adaptive archiving mechanism of hierarchical clustering performs dimensionality reduction and structural analysis in high-dimensional spaces, robustly identifying and retaining solutions distributed across different regions of the three-dimensional objective space. Concurrently, multi-source fusion and specialized mechanisms guide particles to explore distinct regions of the three-dimensional space, enabling HCRMOPSO to outperform comparison algorithms on UF8-10.

HCRMOPSO underperforms compared to benchmark algorithms on certain UF and DTLZ functions, primarily due to mismatches between the problem’s PF characteristics and the algorithm’s mechanism. This can be categorized into four scenarios: Complex PF Geometric Structure: Some UF and DTLZ problems exhibit non-convex, degenerate, or multimodal PFs. The hierarchical clustering based on the Ward method uses Euclidean distance, making it difficult to accurately capture the geometric structure of such PFs. For instance, on the degenerate PFs of UF5 and UF6, clustering may erroneously classify key solutions as suboptimal ones, leading to performance degradation. Local Optima Interference: DTLZ1-3 contain multiple local PFs. When particle distributions are uneven, the guidance direction toward domain-optimal solutions is prone to deviation. Particularly in DTLZ3, gbest may become trapped in local optima, subsequently misleading the entire population. In such cases, integrating multiple information sources increases the risk of “groupthink,” reducing global exploration and trapping the algorithm in local optima. Parameter Adjustment Bias: DTLZ4 exhibits a biased decision space where particles may cluster in specific regions. However, diversity metrics may erroneously indicate “good distribution,” leading to parameter adjustments favoring exploitation over exploration and failing to cover a broader search space. Failure of Special Particle Updates: In DTLZ1, the top 1% of particles are prone to getting stuck in local optima. The algorithm’s small perturbation mechanism for special particles is insufficient to break them out of local optima, ultimately affecting overall performance.

To evaluate the performance of HCRMOPSO in high-dimensional objective spaces, this section compares it with five state-of-the-art multi-objective particle swarm algorithms on a series of five-objective DTLZ test problems. Ablation study

The HCRMOPSO algorithm optimizes the original MOPSO algorithm through four improvement methods. To validate the effectiveness of each improvement method, controlled experiments must be designed to quantify the impact of a single method on algorithm performance. Such experiments are commonly referred to as ablation studies in academic research. Therefore, this section systematically verifies the feasibility and necessity of the proposed improvement methods through ablation research. The core design principle of ablation experiments is the variable control method: by removing a specific improvement component from the algorithm, we observe the overall performance trend to precisely assess that component’s contribution to the algorithm’s functionality. This design ensures that even when a component is removed, the algorithm retains its basic operational capability. Consequently, experimental results are dominated solely by the absence or presence of the target component, eliminating interference from other factors.

Based on this design philosophy, this section establishes four experimental groups. Each group removes one improvement method from HCRMOPSO to validate the importance of the removed method. The specific grouping is as follows: Experimental Group 1 (HCRMOPSO-234): Does not employ the “hierarchical clustering reference point maintenance archive” method; Experimental Group 2 (HCRMOPSO-134): Does not employ the “fused multiple particle update pbest” method; Experimental Group 3 (HCRMOPSO-124): Does not employ the “adaptive parameter strategy”; Experimental Group 4 (HCRMOPSO-123): Does not employ the “specific particle update strategy”.

To ensure the reliability and statistical significance of the experimental results, the algorithms of the four experimental groups and the original HCRMOPSO algorithm were each independently run 30 times on various problems within the three major classical test suites: ZDT, UF, and DTLZ. The final performance metrics were determined by the mean and standard deviation of the IGD and HV scores. The experimental judgment logic is as follows: if the IGD and HV metrics of a test group are significantly worse than those of the original HCRMOPSO, or if performance shows a noticeable decline, it indicates that the removed improvement method in that test group plays a crucial role in enhancing algorithm performance. Detailed experimental results are recorded in Tables 9 and 10, with specific data as follows: HCRMOPSO-234 achieved the best HV value for only one test problem; HCRMOPSO-134 achieved the optimal IGD value for 2 test problems and the optimal HV value for 2 test problems; HCRMOPSO-124 achieved the optimal IGD value for only 1 test problem and the optimal HV value for only 1 test problem; HCRMOPSO-123 achieved the optimal IGD value for 3 test problems and the optimal HV value for 3 test problems; The original HCRMOPSO demonstrated significantly superior overall performance across all 22 test problems compared to the four variant algorithms. Based on the combined experimental results, it can be concluded that all four proposed improvements in the HCRMOPSO algorithm significantly enhance its performance. Furthermore, when these four methods synergize, the algorithm achieves optimal multi-objective optimization results.

Algorithm sensitivity analysis

This section conducts a sensitivity analysis on the HCRMOPSO algorithm, focusing on investigating the influence patterns of four key parameters—number of centroids, number of recovered deleted solutions, neighborhood size, and population size—on algorithm performance. Additionally, it further examines the fluctuation characteristics of the algorithm’s overall performance under different flight parameter settings.

In this study, the population size was set to 200, with the corresponding number of centroid reference points set to 100. Should future expansion of the population size be required, the number of centroid reference points should also be increased accordingly. The selection of centroid count directly impacts the effectiveness of archive maintenance and ultimately determines the quality of the output solution set. When the number of reference points is set too low, overcrowding errors are likely to occur, leading to the erroneous deletion of diverse non-dominated solutions. Due to sparse reference points, most non-dominated solutions tend to cluster around a few popular reference point directions. Although these solutions are actually dispersed across the objective space and contribute significantly to diversity, they are associated with the same crowded reference point. Consequently, the algorithm treats them as redundant and eliminates them, thereby reducing the population’s true diversity. Conversely, setting too many reference points increases computational cost and may degrade frontier quality. When reference points are overly dense, some solutions—despite being far from the true Pareto frontier (poor convergence)—may be retained if they happen to occupy a direction toward an unoccupied reference point. This occurs because they fill the “gap” at that reference point. In such cases, the algorithm retains solutions with poor convergence but unique positions, thereby compromising the overall solution set quality.Sensitivity analysis conducted using 60, 80, 100, 120, and 140 reference points (experimental results shown in Table 11) clearly indicates that the algorithm performs optimally when the number of reference points is set to 100, regardless of whether the problem is two-dimensional or three-dimensional.

To prevent potential distribution imbalances that may persist after archive maintenance using the reference point strategy, this algorithm incorporates a specific recovery mechanism during archive maintenance: when deletion operations reach a preset threshold, the system continues deleting a fixed number of solutions (e.g., 20), then re-selects the top 20 performing solutions from all deleted individuals to re-include them in the archive. This mechanism provides a “revival” opportunity for potentially misdeleted high-quality solutions, helping prevent excellent individuals from being permanently eliminated due to accidental crowding. This enhances the convergence and diversity of the solution set. However, if the recovery size is set too small, some high-quality solutions may still fail to return to the archive, rendering the recovery mechanism ineffective. Conversely, setting the recovery size too large significantly increases the computational overhead of archive maintenance, impacting algorithm efficiency. Therefore, rationally setting the recovery size is crucial for balancing solution quality and computational cost. Sensitivity analysis conducted with recovery sizes of 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 (results shown in Table 12) clearly demonstrates that setting the recovery size to 20 achieves the optimal balance between maintaining solution set quality and computational efficiency, yielding superior overall performance.

In research on optimizing the performance of the HCRMOPSO algorithm, the population size N is a critical parameter whose value directly impacts the algorithm’s overall performance. Selecting an appropriate N value can effectively enhance both the accuracy of the algorithm’s solutions and computational efficiency. Therefore, conducting a sensitivity analysis on this parameter holds significant theoretical and practical importance, laying a solid foundation for the scientific selection of algorithm parameters. When N is less than 200, the solution quality falls below the level achieved at N=200. When N exceeds 200, the IGD value tends to stabilize or even fluctuate slightly, no longer decreasing significantly with increasing population size. This indicates that further increasing the population size yields extremely low marginal benefits for solution quality improvement while incurring extremely high computational costs. This conclusion provides clear guidance for parameter configuration of the HCRMOPSO algorithm.

HCRMOPSO integrates information from multiple particles when updating the particle best (pbest), including the best solution among the 10 particles selected from the current particle’s neighborhood (nbriebest). The setting of the neighborhood size significantly impacts the algorithm’s performance. If the neighborhood size is set too small, the selected neighborhood best solution (nbriebest) may lack representativeness, potentially representing only a random outlier of low quality. Using such an nbriebest to guide pbest updates then provides limited guidance, effectively causing the algorithm to degrade into being primarily—or even entirely—driven by the global best solution (gbest). This weakens the algorithm’s ability to conduct refined searches using local information, leading to excessive reliance on gbest. Once gbest becomes trapped in a local optimum, the particles’ search process is likely to stagnate. Conversely, if the neighborhood size is set too large, although individual particles can gather more information, the overall search coverage of the population may narrow. This also increases unnecessary computational overhead. The neighborhood size directly impacts the quality of pbest updates and is crucial for balancing the algorithm’s exploration and exploitation capabilities. Sensitivity analysis experiments with neighborhood sizes of 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14 (results shown in Table 13) clearly demonstrate that the algorithm achieves optimal overall performance when the neighborhood size is set to 10.

Limitations and future work

Although the proposed HCRMOPSO algorithm demonstrates superior comprehensive performance in the two core evaluation metrics—IGD and HV—through comparative experiments with ten advanced algorithms on the ZDT, UF, and DTLZ series standard test problems, fully proving its significant advantages in convergence speed and solution distribution uniformity, this study still has three limitations: When tackling high-dimensional multi-objective optimization challenges, the algorithm is susceptible to the “curse of dimensionality.” This manifests in two ways: first, the search space expands dramatically, reducing convergence efficiency; second, the proportion of effective non-dominated solutions within the solution cluster decreases, ultimately making it difficult to balance convergence and distribution. It has not been validated on real-world multi-objective optimization problem (MaOPs) testbeds (e.g., Ma-RW55, CSID56), limiting its assessment for practical applicability; There is a lack of practical engineering application validation. Moreover, in dynamic/real-time optimization scenarios, its computational complexity and response time may become key limiting factors for widespread adoption. Particularly in highly time-sensitive scenarios like real-time production control or emergency resource allocation, algorithmic delays could render optimization results practically worthless.

To address these limitations, subsequent research will focus on “strengthening weaknesses, enhancing performance, and promoting application.” Specifically, within the hierarchical clustering approach for adaptive archiving strategies, introducing dimension reduction techniques or sparse representation strategies can improve processing efficiency for high-dimensional solution sets. Diversity parameter adjustment mechanisms can also be reconstructed based on high-dimensional spatial distribution characteristics to enhance the algorithm’s search capabilities in ultra-high-dimensional environments. Furthermore, comprehensive experiments will be conducted on complex MaOPs testbeds, focusing on specialized particle update mechanisms and multi-source fusion strategies to evaluate robustness under non-uniform Pareto fronts, multimodal distributions, and biased data scenarios. Simultaneously, to advance the transition from theory to practice, the algorithm will be validated in typical engineering scenarios such as mechanical structure design and power system dispatch. Addressing common challenges in practical applications—including constraint handling and computational efficiency—the algorithm structure will undergo scenario-specific tailoring and acceleration optimization.

Conclusion

In this paper, to address the problems of insufficient archive diversity, individual optimal choice mechanisms are ambiguous, parameter sensitivity, and the difficulty of balancing the global exploration capability and local exploitation capability of the algorithm in multi-objective particle swarm optimization, we propose a HCRMOPSO with significantly improved performance, and the core innovation lies in the systematic introduction of four key improvement strategies. Generating centers of mass as reference points based on hierarchical clustering, which are able to represent points in different pareto frontier regions, and combining archival ideal points with their respective crowding distances, significantly improves the distribution uniformity and coverage of the external archival solution set, and mitigates the problem of insufficient diversity of traditional archives. Fusing multi-source information to update the pbest, this strategy enriches the diversity of particle learning samples and enhances the potential of jumping out of the local optimum and approximating the true pareto frontier. Adaptive parameter tuning based on domain diversity information can effectively balance the exploration and development of algorithms, and enhance the robustness and optimization efficiency of algorithms under different problem characteristics. The differential update for special particles effectively solves the problem of slow and ineffective evolution of specific particles under the traditional update rule.

To comprehensively evaluate the overall performance of the proposed algorithm, this study systematically compares HCRMOPSO with ten advanced multi-objective optimization algorithms across three major categories of classical test functions. Through multidimensional validation methods—including IGD and HV metric analysis, Pareto frontier visualization, box plot statistics, convergence curve tracking, and Friedman and Wilcoxon tests—experimental results consistently demonstrate HCRMOPSO’s superior performance. The conclusion further analyzes the current limitations of the algorithm and outlines future research directions, aiming to advance its precise and efficient adaptation to a broader range of complex multi-objective optimization problems.
