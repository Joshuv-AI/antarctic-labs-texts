Pseudoalteromonas sp. SRSBD1_22: A potent prophylactic candidate mediating lipopolysaccharide (LPS) based humoral immune response in Penaeus monodon against WSSV infection
Authors: H M Rakibul Islam, Mizanur Rahman Washim, Md. Shoebul Islam, Mst. Aziza Begum, Shikder Saiful Islam, Md. Iqramul Haque, Md. Shariful Islam, Nilufa Begum, Md. Harunor Rashid, Yahia Mahmud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Pseudoalteromonas sp. SRSBD1_22 LPS boosts shrimp immunity against WSSV.•Cellular and humoral defense in shrimp increased at 7 µg LPS/g feed supplementation.•TLR gene expression spikes with high LPS dosage, signalling robust immune activation.•LPS diet provides 100% WSSV protection for up to four days, while the control group showed over 50% mortality.

Introduction

Black tiger shrimp (Penaeus monodon Fabricius, 1798) is a popular aquaculture commodity due to its high nutritional value, growth, and economic benefits. Shrimp alone contributing significantly (> 70%) to the export income of Bangladesh from the fisheries sector in 2018–2019, with a total production of 63,171 tons and export value of US$500.97 million (DoF, 2020) [1]. Thus, tiger shrimp culture sector has an immense contribution to the country’s economy, resolving unemployment problem, alleviating poverty, and improving political economy and social wellbeing in Bangladesh [2,3]. Despite its great potential, sudden outbreak of diseases in shrimp aquaculture has led this industry under the risk of production and economic loss, and leading towards unsustainability. White spot disease (WSD) is one of the major detrimental events to collapse shrimp industry in Bangladesh as well as other countries in the world [[4], [5], [6]].

Antibiotics and chemotherapeutics are widely used for culture intensification and to control diseases in tiger shrimp culture, which have detrimental impacts on environment and wildlife, favor proliferation of diverse pathogens, and often arise antibiotic resistant bacterial strains (Kumar et al., 2016; Le Moullac and Haffner, 2000; Naiel et al., 2021; Silvestre et al., 2010). Therefore, appropriate alternatives should be explored for sustainable and eco-friendly disease management. Uplifting shrimp resistance and immune enhancement by applying biological control measures are becoming more promising options for bacterial and viral disease management in tiger shrimp culture ([7]; Naiel et al., 2021; [8]). The lipopolysaccharides (LPS) are microbial origin based immunostimulant, which strengthen the non-specific defense systems of shrimp [9,10].

LPS are capable of triggering cytokine released from cells of different origin. For example, it is widely known as an inducer of TLR-4 signaling pathway [11], mediator of dendritic cell maturation [12], and activator of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 [13,14]. LPS isolated from Pseudoalteromonas sp. SRSBD1_22 outer cell membrane showed significantly higher immune modulation in tiger shrimp compared to two locally isolated Gram-negative bacteria (viz., Vibrio parahaemolyticus SRSBD1_18 and Shewanella khirikhana SRSBD1_22) and commercial strain Escherichia coli BL21 (our unpublished data). The aptitude of Pseudoalteromonas spp. as probiotic candidate and immunomodulators bearing the capability of producing antibacterial compounds is robustly assessed in earlier studies [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. A previous report indicated that Pseudoalteromonas can enhance shrimp immunity and protect them against WSSV infection [19]. However, no maiden study has been done yet on the application of LPS derived from Pseudoalteromonas bacterium in modulating shrimp immunity. This study aimed at identifying the potency of LPS from Pseudoalteromonas sp. SRSBD1_22, a new Gram-negative bacteria candidate isolated from local aquaculture environment as therapeutic agent. This study also tested the prophylactic mechanism of Pseudoalteromonas sp. SRSBD1_22 on the humoral immune response in tiger shrimp against WSSV infection.

Materials and methods

Extraction of LPS from Pseudoalteromonas sp. SRSBD1_22 outer membrane

Tiger shrimp samples were collected from local culture environment near Shrimp Research Station (SRS), Bagerhat, Bangladesh. Pseudoalteromonas sp. SRSBD1_22 strain was isolated from the hepatopancreas of the samples and identified by sanger sequencing with 16S rRNA gene (Table 1). The isolated bacterial strain was stored at - 85 °C in Ultra Low Temperature Freezer (New Brunswick Scientific, England) for future use. The bacterial isolate was grown in nutrient broth (HIMEDIA; Peptone 10 g/L, Beef extract 10 g/L, Sodium chloride 5 g/L, pH-7.3 ± 0.1) and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Around 1.38-gram LPS (Yield 13.8%) was extracted from 10-gram bacterial lyophilized cells following the methods of Westphal and Jann [20] and Rezania et al. [21] with slight modification. At first, 10 g bacterial pellet was obtained by cultivating the bacterial strain in a nutrient broth for 24 h at 37 °C with continuous shaking. Specifically, approximately 18 liters of broth culture were grown for 24 h. Afterward, the culture was subjected to centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C. Then, this 10 g of bacterial pellet was taken in a 500 ml glass beaker, 150 ml 2 M CaCl2 added drop by drop and homogenized. The mixture was transferred into a centrifuge tube, centrifuged (Hanil, SUPRA21K, Korea) at 7134 g for 30 min at 4 °C, supernatant taken, and pellet discarded. Then 95% ethanol was added at 25% volume of the supernatant, mixed for 30 min using magnetic stirrer in iced cold condition and centrifuged at 7134 g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube, equal volume of 80% ethanol added and stored at - 20 °C for 1 h. Experimental animals, test diet preparation and LPS dose optimization

The black tiger shrimp juveniles (P. monodon) with an average weight of 6.04 ± 0.51 g was obtained from the grow-out pond of SRS, Bagerhat. Before initiating the supplementation treatment, we verified shrimp health by stocking SPF Post Larvae (PL) in rearing ponds, visually inspecting for physical abnormalities, and conducting basic diagnostic tests. During the three-day acclimatization period, shrimp were monitored for stress or disease, ensuring only healthy, active individuals with normal feeding behavior and consistent growth rates were selected for the study. Shrimp were randomly divided under the treatment of three different doses of LPS, such as 3 µg LPS/g diet (T1), 5 µg LPS/g diet (T2), 7 µg LPS/g diet (T3). The selection of the three different doses of LPS (3 µg, 5 µg, and 7 µg LPS/g diet) for this experiment is grounded in a comprehensive review of existing literature and previous studies involving LPS application in aquaculture [[8], [26], [27]]. A control group without LPS application was maintained (Fig. 1). Twenty juveniles were stocked in 40 L glass aquarium tanks with three replications for each treatment and acclimatized for 3 days before feeding. The experimental shrimps were fed a commercial pelleted feed (Quality Shrimp Feed, Special, Nursery 2) with a composition of 32% protein, 7% fat, and 3.5% fiber. To coat the feed with lipopolysaccharides (LPS), a solution of LPS was prepared according to Rungrassamee et al. [8] by dissolving LPS in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at a concentration corresponding to 3, 5, and 7 µg LPS/g diet. The LPS solution was then sprayed onto the surface of the pelleted feed to ensure uniform distribution. After spraying, the coated pellets were allowed to air dry at room temperature for 1 h to facilitate the adsorption of LPS onto the feed matrix. The drying process at ambient temperature helps to preserve the stability and bioactivity of LPS without denaturing its immune-stimulatory properties. The doses of 3, 5, and 7 µg LPS/g diet were selected based on earlier studies that optimized LPS supplementation in shrimp diets for immune modulation. In this study, we chose to spray a PBS solution combined with LPS, as this method effectively promoted adhesion without the need for external binders. PBS not only aided in the coating process but also preserved the bioactivity of the LPS. During the feeding trial, we observed that the pellets remained intact, indicating that this approach was successful in minimizing LPS leaching. Diets were stored in plastic bags at 4 °C before feeding. Shrimps were fed twice daily at 08:00 and 18:00 h and the feeding rate was 5–6% of total body weight. After 5 days of feeding trial, hemolymph and tissue samples (n = 5) from each treatment group were collected for further immunological studies. We used 5-days feeding period because LPS was applied here as a medicated immunostimulatory feed intended for short-term prophylactic priming. We also chose this duration conservatively to avoid possible confounding effects of prolonged endotoxin exposure, since LPS can act as a physiological toxicant in shrimp depending on its source, dose, and duration [[27], [28]].Fig. 1Experimental layout of LPS dose optimization and challenge test with WSSV infection.Fig 1 dummy alt text

Preparation of WSSV stock inoculum for challenge experiment

The viral stock solution was prepared following the method of Chang et al. [29]. Briefly, 0.5 g of the frozen WSSV infected P. monodon were minced, homogenized in 4.5 ml of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, 2 mM KH2PO4), centrifuged at 400 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant filtered through 0.45 μm membrane filter. This supernatant was used as the viral stock solution.

Challenge experiment was carried out in 40 L glass aquarium containing UV treated water. Experimental animals were divided into two groups in triplicates having 20 individuals (mean weight = 6.06 ± 0.45 g) per replication. Considering the overall impact of different doses of LPS, we used the diet coated with 7 µg LPS/g feed for the challenge test experiment. After 3 days of acclimatization, the treatment group was fed LPS coated commercial pelleted diet (Quality shrimp feed, Special, Nursery 2) twice daily for 7 days at 5% of body weight. On the other hand, LPS free commercial pellets were provided to the control group. After 7 days of feeding trial, experimental animals from both groups were subjected to WSSV challenge by immersion method [30] with moderate aeration. Equal volume (5 ml) of viral stock solution was inoculated per 40 L of rearing water. All water quality and environmental parameters were maintained same in both groups during the entire challenge test period. Removal of waste and 30% water exchange were performed daily. Test shrimps were monitored for 168 h post infection (hpi) and mortalities checked daily. Dead shrimps were recorded, removed from the system, and subjected to polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis to confirm presence of WSSV. Specific primer sets for WSSV were used (Table 1).

DNA/RNA extraction

For DNA extraction 15 shrimp samples from each group were collected, washed with sterile distilled water, finely chopped and pooled. From the chopped samples 20 mg of tissue samples were collected using a sterile forceps in a 2 ml centrifuge vial (Eppendorf, USA) to extract DNA using DNA purification Kit (Invitrogen, USA) according to manufacturer’s guideline. Genomic DNA of Pseudoalteromonas sp. SRSBD1_22 was extracted using the same protocol from the pure culture of this strain grown in nutrient broth (HIMEDIA; Peptone 10 g/L, Beef extract 10 g/L, Sodium chloride 5 g/L, pH 7.3 ± 0.1). The culture tubes were incubated overnight at 37 °C, 1.5 ml culture transferred to a clean microtube, centrifuged at 7134 g for 30 min (Hanil, SUPRA21K, Korea). The supernatant was discarded, and the bacterial pellet used for DNA extraction. Extracted DNA was stored at - 80 °C for further use.

For RNA extraction, considering WSSV susceptibility, gills and hepatopancreases of 15 live shrimp (5 from each replication) were taken from the control and each treatment group. The gill and hepatopancreas samples that were collected from each treatment were then quickly chopped, mixed, and finally 20 mg of the chopped tissue was taken to extract RNA using an RNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA with Verso cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Baltics UAB, Lithuania). Synthesized cDNA was stored at - 80 °C for further use.

PCR assay

Conventional PCR

Polymerase chain reactions for all samples were performed using Bio-Rad T100 thermal cycler with an initial denaturation of DNA at 94 °C for 2 mins, followed by 30 cycles, denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 60 – 62 °C for 30 s (Table 1), extension at 72 °C for 30 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 2 mins. Amplified PCR products were checked through electrophoresis on a 0.8% agarose gel containing 0.06 g/ml DNA stain (Midori Green Advance, Nippon Genetic GmbH EU). The bands were visualized on a Gel-documentation system (UVITEC, Firereader V10, UK).

RT-qPCR

Gene expression analysis was performed through RT-qPCR by a HYRIS bCUBE qPCR System (London, UK). The reaction was done in 20 μl reaction mixture, containing 1x (10 µl) of Luna Universal qPCR mix (BioLabs, New England), 2 μl of cDNA template, and 7.6 μl of sterile ultrapure nuclease free water (HyClone, Australia). The final concentration of both forward and reverse primers were 0.1 µM. The PCR cycling comprised of initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 mins, followed by 40 cycles, denaturation at 95 °C for 5 sec, annealing at 53–60 °C for 11 sec (Table 1), and extension at 72 °C for 19 sec.

Expression of immune genes

Toll Like Receptor (TLR), Prophenoloxidase (ProPO), and Photorhabdus-like Insect A (PIR A) gene were considered for the gene expression analysis. A set of specific primers were used (Table 1) to determine the level expression from pooled sample using HYRIS bCUBE QPCR System (London, UK). Relative gene expression was measured following the formula as previously described by Nagy and colleagues [31].

Total haemocyte count (THC)

Hemolymph was collected from the ventral sinus of the first abdominal segment of each shrimp by using a 1 ml syringe equipped with 26-gauage needle. A 100 µl of hemolymph were collected with the syringe containing 200 µl anticoagulant solution (0.03 M tri sodium citrate, 0.45 M sodium chloride, 0.01 M EDTA, 0.1 M glucose, and 0.026 M citric acid; pH 7.5). A 20 µl aliquot of each sample was taken into a tube containing 5 µl of 1.2% Rose Bengal Dye Solution (Sigma–Aldrich, USA). A drop (≈ 15 µl) of the anticoagulant-haemolymph mixture was placed in a haemacytometer to count the number of hemocytes using an inverted phase-contrast microscope (Euromex, iScope IS-1153PLi/SLC, Netherlands). Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity assay

Superoxide dismutase activity of LPS treated and WSSV challenged tiger shrimp juvenile was assayed following the modified protocol of Chandra et al. [32]. Briefly pyrogallol autoxidation, an enhanced Marklund method, was utilized in this assay to measure enzyme activity. 1 g shrimp muscle was finely ground and mixed with 9 ml distilled water (Dl). Then the sample was centrifuged at 1430 g at 10 min. The supernatant was taken for further analysis. For analysis, solution 1 was prepared using 0.1 M Tris–HCl buffer solution with 1 mM EDTA (pH 8.20). Then 1.2114 g Tris and 37.2 mg disodium salt of EDTA was dissolved in 62.4 mL solution of 0.1 M HCl. The solution was then transferred into a 100 mL volumetric flask and Distilled water was added to 100 mL. Solution 2 was prepared using 4.5 mM pyrogallol solution in 10 mM HCl. For blank, 2.35 mL Solution 1- and 2 mL DI water into a 10 mL test tube. After that 0.15 mL solution 2 was added, vortexed and absorbance was measured at 325 nm. Another aliquot was taken after 1 min. The different in absorbance between the two aliquot, ΔA325 (min-1), indicates the rate of pyrogallol autoxidation. For sample, the procedure was same except addition of 20 µl sample solution. One unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of SOD required to inhibit the rate of xanthine reduction by 50% [33]. Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis

All sequence data handling and alignment was conducted with the software package Geneious Prime (versions 2020 and 2022, Biomatters Ltd; Kearse et al. (2012)). Poor quality sequences were trimmed, and base call errors and discrepancies resolved manually from the assembled forward and reverse sequences. We successfully retrieved a 314 bp partial sequence of the 16S rRNA gene from the de novo assembly of the forward and reverse sequences. Assembled and corrected sequences were then aligned with published sequences of Pseudoalteromonas species available from the NCBI GenBank database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nucleotide/). Sequence alignment used the Clustal Omega algorithm with Cluster Size for mBed Guide Trees set to 100 (Baxevanis et al., 2020). Phylogenetic trees were built with maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI). Trees with ML were calculated with bootstrapping (BT) (= 1000 BS) by Hasegawa-Kishini-Yano model using PhyML software version 3.3.20180621 (Guindon et al., 2010). The general time reversal model with an estimated transition/transversion (Ti/Tv) rate and gamma distribution were used in this analysis. Trees with BI were based on posterior probabilities (PP) with Markov chain Monte Carlo simulations (Huelsenbeck and Ronquist, 2001) using HKY85 substitution model with three heated and one cold chain(s), the chain length 1100,000, and heated chain temperature at 0.2. The trees were sampled every 200 generation within the 1,100,000 generations generated.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8.0, Microsoft Excel, and SPSS version 28.0. One-way and Two-way analysis of variance and unpaired t-test was used to assess differences between the treatment and control. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, with the log-rank test, evaluated probability of cumulative survival and hazard function. Probit mortality analysis determined the 50% mortality of shrimp juveniles.

Discussion

This study examined the potential application of lipopolysaccharides in shrimp feed from a locally isolated Gram-negative bacteria Pseudoalteromonas sp. SRSBD1_22. LPS from this bacterium acted as an immunomodulator and increased the disease resistance of P. monodon against pathogenic WSSV. LPS is an indispensable component of the cell wall of Gram-negative bacteria, which play important roles in bacterial virulence, drug resistance and protecting them from unfavorable conditions [34]. LPS induces immune responses via pathways like the TLR4-NF-κB signaling pathway to enhance inflammation and the AMPK-mTOR pathway to induce autophagy in epithelial cells [35,36].

We did the dose optimization of LPS by oral administration with commercial pelleted feed for the immune enhancement in P. monodon juveniles. Intravenous administration of LPS might cause lower survival and cytotoxicity in animals, which is less likely for the oral administration [[10], [37], [38]]. Diet coated with 7 µg/g feed revealed highest level of THC, SOD activity and expression of TLR (Fig. 3A, B, and Fig. 4A). One of the key reasons for selecting the LPS dose of 7 µg/g feed was that it provided the maximum increase in THC during the dose optimization trial. This is because the LPS from Gram-negative bacteria can be recognized by pattern recognition proteins such as lipopolysaccharide and β−1,3-glucan binding protein (LGBP), which can trigger immune responses [39]. A previous study reported that animals with higher haemocyte count can successfully outperform WSSV infection in Litopenaeus vannamei [40]. TLR gene expression level increased in an equal rhythm with LPS dose intensity. The TLR expression level in T3 (7µg/g feed) fed shrimp was upregulated by 19, 6 and 12.5-folds in comparison to T1, T2 and control group, respectively. In contrast, LPS did not seem to affect the transcript level with the same manner for PIR A and ProPO genes. Thus, this study suggested that upregulation of different immune genes in tiger shrimp is not specific to LPS treatment. However, considering the importance of the TLR gene as well as the Toll-pathway for regulation of innate immune response in tiger shrimp (P. monodon) against WSSV infection [41,42], we prioritize the TLR gene expression results to select the dose of LPS for further experiment. On the other hand, these results indicated that LPS at a higher dose (≤ 7 µg/g feed) can enhance the THC and SOD enzyme activity in shrimp approximately 52-folds and 10-folds, respectively. This higher dose might cause little cytotoxicity, resulting a lower expression of ProPO and PIR A genes. Expression of PirA gene in animal has been reported as the measure of toxicity due to bacterial invasion in animal body. For example, the acute hepatopancreatic necrosis disease (AHPND) in shrimp, caused by Vibrio parahaemolyticus when the bacteria produce a binary toxin, PirABvp. As we used LPS as immune modulator, it was also required to measure the level of toxicity produced by these foreign particles in animals. With the increasing concentration of LPS (3,5 &7 µg LPS/g feed), the expression of PirA gene showed sequential increase up to 5 µg LPS/g feed. Interestingly, the toxin gene expression significantly reduced at 7 µg LPS/g feed, which might be happened due to the procaryotic cells toxin antitoxin (TA) systems. This event has been reported in bacterial cell protection against phase/toxin [43,44], but no relevant study found mentioning TA system working in shrimp. In the present study, the T3 diet treatment may have produced a stronger or earlier immune priming effect, allowing the shrimp to control the pathogen challenge more efficiently. As a result, the need for prolonged proPO up-regulation may have been reduced by the time of sampling. Therefore, lower proPO expression in T3 does not necessarily indicate weak immune response; rather, it may indicate controlled immune regulation after effective stimulation [10]. Therefore, in the conventional way, this study used the diets coated with 7 µg LPS/g feed for the challenge test experiment. However, several studies reported a wide range of doses to evaluate the efficacy of LPS extracted from different bacteria. Much lower dose (0.02, 0.04, and 0.1 µg/g) of LPS used from Pantoea agglomerans was reported to protect viral disease of P. japonicus [45]. Another study used 0.4 to 0.5 ng of lipopolysaccharide like molecule per ml in a suspension method to stimulate the hemocytes of P. monodon [46]. The highest reported dose of LPS was 50 mg/kg diet (Genio et al., 2014).

In the challenge test, juveniles fed an LPS-coated diet showed 80±5% survival against WSSV for up to four days, compared to 50±10% in the control group. Mortality stabilized around 5 days (120 hpi), significantly shorter than the 25 days reported for Bacillus sp. Mk22 in a similar study [47]. Probit regression and cumulative survival probability confirmed that the LPS-coated diet significantly protected juveniles. Hazard function analysis indicated that LPS-coated juveniles faced lower WSSV risk. These results suggest that LPS from Pseudoalteromonas sp. SRSBD1_22 either directly inhibits WSSV through structural or biochemical mechanisms or enhances immunity, protecting shrimp from WSSV-induced mortality (70±5%). A similar study reported 75% survival with 50 mg/kg LPS from V. harveyi (Genio et al., 2014), which is seven times higher than our dose. LPS may activate the anti-LPS factor (ALF), inhibiting WSSV-induced coagulation and improving viral resistance [[48], [49], [50]]. ALFPm6 gene expression in P. monodon has been linked to immunity and WSSV protection [51].

The LPS-coated diet (7 µg/g) induced immunity, increasing expression of TLR, ProPO, and PIR A genes. Gene expression of TLR, ProPO, and PIR A increased 18-fold, 11-fold, and 15-fold, respectively (Fig. 9a, b, c). Toll-like pathways, known to regulate immunity in shrimp, can boost survival against viral infections [[52], [53], [54],53]. Shrimp immune pathways involving TLR/MyD88/Tube/Pelle/TRAF6/NF-κB can be activated by WSSV449 protein, upregulating immune genes in shrimp (P.-H [55]). Li et al. [56] reported that Toll-4 confers resistance to WSSV by inducing the NF-κB transcription factor Dorsal, producing AMPs with antiviral activity. Therefore, LPS from Pseudoalteromonas sp. SRSBD1_22 may directly enhance TLR expression and immunity, protecting shrimp from WSSV infection.

LPS-fed shrimp also exhibited higher SOD activity under WSSV exposure. SOD activity increased 1.5-fold in treated shrimp compared to controls. SOD is a key humoral immune parameter that mediates oxidative stress in hemocytes, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) like superoxide anions (O2-), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and hydroxyl ions (OH-), which are involved in pathogen destruction through phagocytosis, encapsulation, and melanization [[57], [58], [59]]. These findings support the role of LPS in boosting immune response and resistance to WSSV infection.

Conclusion

LPS supplementation in the diet showed induction of some crucial immune-related transcripts such as TLR, ProPO, PIR A genes and SOD enzyme activity in shrimp. These findings suggest that LPS of Pseudoalteromonas sp. SRSBD1_22 strain isolated from the native source would be a promising candidate to increase disease resistance in black tiger shrimp farming in Bangladesh. However, Pseudoalteromonas sp. SRSBD1_22 requires accurate species identification and finding functional genes through whole genome sequencing to provide a conclusive assumption about their defence mechanism against WSSV infection. Application of this potential candidate as an effective immune modulator can significantly uplift the drowning shrimp industry. Extensive investigation of the immunity system of tiger shrimp juveniles is needed focusing on the development of ready to use LPS products for the mass shrimp producers.
