The added value of contrast-enhanced ultrasonography to BI-RADS in the evaluation of breast lesions presenting with pathological nipple discharge
Authors: Jiaqian Zhong, Jia Luo, Shaohong Wu, Manying Li, Jinyu Liang, Fushun Pan, Xiaoyan Xie, Yanling Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPathological nipple discharge (PND) is a frequent presentation in breast clinics, yet its accurate differentiation between benign and malignant lesions remains difficult due to the inherent limitations of current imaging tools. There is still a lack of a practical, non‑invasive approach that incorporates contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) features with the conventional Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) to improve the diagnostic yield. This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility and diagnostic performance of a rerated BI-RADS system combining ultrasound (US) and CEUS versus US BI-RADS alone for breast lesions causing PND.MethodsA total of 217 consecutive patients with PND underwent conventional US and CEUS between January 2009 and August 2025. Each lesion was evaluated with US BI-RADS categories, then CEUS BI-RADS categories were re-rated based on US BI-RADS categories and CEUS scores. All responsible lesions were confirmed by pathological results through surgery or biopsy. To account for the potential correlation among multiple lesions from the same patient, we used generalized estimating equations with an exchangeable correlation structure for multivariate analysis to identify independent risk factors. The diagnostic performance of US BI-RADS and CEUS BI-RADS was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis.ResultsThere were 258 breast lesions from 217 patients consecutively evaluated in this retrospective study. A total of 57 lesions were pathologically confirmed to be malignant, and 201 were confirmed to be benign. There was a statistically significant difference in multiple clinical and ultrasonic features between malignant and benign lesions (P<0.05). Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis based on generalized estimating equations identified age, size, enhancement range, enhancement order and spiculate sign as independent predictors of malignant breast lesions with discharge (all P<0.02). The BI-RADS categories based on CEUS showed higher sensitivity, negative predictive value (NPV) and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) compared to US BI-RADS, which were 0.930 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.822–0.977], 0.945 (95% CI: 0.858–0.982) and 0.637 (95% CI: 0.575–0.695), respectively.ConclusionsIn conclusion, CEUS BI-RADS demonstrates high sensitivity and NPV in evaluating breast lesions associated with PND. Although its overall discriminatory ability, as measured by AUROC, was not significantly different from that of conventional US BI-RADS when BI-RADS 4a lesions were considered test-positive, CEUS provides complementary functional information that aids in risk stratification. These findings position CEUS as a valuable adjunct to conventional US, particularly in refining the assessment of low-suspicion lesions and guiding biopsy decisions in patients with PND.

Introduction

Nipple discharge (ND) is the third most common complaint after breast pain and mass (1,2). Pathological nipple discharge (PND), spontaneous and unrelated to pregnancy or lactation, can be serous, serosanguineous, bloody, or purulent. It is mostly induced by benign diseases in the breast duct, such as ductal ectasia, intraductal papilloma, intraductal hyperplasia, and ductitis. Otherwise, a proportion of patients, ranging from 3% to 23%, present with malignant lesions, such as ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) and invasive ductal carcinoma (1,3,4), in which their secretions are frequently either bloody or serous, occurring in 5% to 21% of cases (5,6). Consequently, imaging screening for PND-related breast lesions is crucial.

Mammography is recommended for evaluating PND in women aged 40 years or older (2), for screening possible malignant lesions. However, it actually has limited value in detecting responsible lesions, especially small benign lesions. Galactography is an alternative method in patients presenting with a single orifice discharge. Common manifestations are ductal dilatation, ductal filling defect or complete obstruction and duct wall irregularities (7,8). These findings are inconsistently detected on galactography and may be found in both benign and malignant breast lesions, complicating the differential diagnosis. Moreover, its invasiveness may induce discomfort and it is associated with various limitations, including challenges in cannulating the duct, the potential for periductal contrast extravasation, and adverse effects related to contrast administration. Ultrasound (US) is a widely utilized tool suggested to be a complementary technology for mammography in the evaluation of ND. It provides radiologists with important sonographic characteristics, including shape, echogenicity, margin properties and calcification to help identify benign and malignant lesions. It is reported that subareolar US has a high sensitivity and specificity equal to or superior to galactography in screening for suspected ductal breast disease, and the sensitivity of US is up to 97% (9,10). However, diagnosis based on US is subjective for assessment and is heavily dependent on the radiologists’ experience. The American College of Radiology Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) based on US has been routinely used as standardized diagnostic criteria to assess breast lesions and facilitate clinical decision-making (11). Application of BI-RADS improves the diagnostic efficacy for breast lesions and thus reduces unnecessary biopsies of benign lesions. Nevertheless, US struggles with a miss rate of up to 50% for PND-related lesions (12). Efforts to integrate clinical and conventional imaging features, such as the nomogram developed by Mei et al., have sought to improve risk stratification, yet they still relied solely on morphological parameters and did not leverage perfusion information (13).

Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) is a promising diagnostic method that has advanced rapidly over the past several decades and is emerging as an alternative imaging modality for the diagnosis of breast diseases (14-17). It visualizes microvascular architecture using microbubble agents, highlighting differences between visible lesions and the surrounding breast tissue, thereby providing more comprehensive information for diagnosis (18-21). Hu et al. revealed a good overall sensitivity [0.87, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.81–0.90] and specificity [0.79, 95% CI: 0.73–0.84) of CEUS in the characterization of breast lesions (17). Also, it highlights the suspicious areas by exhibiting visible hyperenhancement for observation, so as to guide pathological biopsy accurately (22). Recently, a study used CEUS to improve the BI-RADS classification on the basis of US, which enhanced the specificity and positive predictive value (PPV) of diagnosing PND-related malignant tumors. However, this study did not refine the scoring criteria of CEUS in the improvement process (23). Xiao et al. created a new scoring system called rerated BI-RADS by reforming the US BI-RADS with the application of CEUS (24), which was verified to improve diagnostic efficiency in breast lesions (25). The rerated BI-RADS scoring system was adopted in this study as it provides a comprehensive assessment of CEUS features relevant to breast lesion characterization. The system incorporates many parameters such as enhancement pattern, margin and intensity, etc., which collectively capture the microvascular features associated with benign and malignant lesions. This multidimensional approach aligns with current clinical practice in CEUS interpretation and allows for a standardized evaluation across all included lesions.

This study aims to evaluate the feasibility and diagnostic performance of CEUS combined with US BI-RADS (rerated BI-RADS) for breast lesions causing PND, comparing it against US BI-RADS alone. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-0592/rc).

Methods

Study population

This single-center, retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University [No. (2008)34-1, date: 26 September 2008]. Individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Between January 2009 and August 2025, 217 consecutive patients with single-duct bloody/serosanguineous PND, who underwent US, CEUS, and subsequent surgical or biopsy confirmation, were enrolled. The exclusion criteria included: (I) patients who had received any previous interventional procedures; (II) surgery history of related malignant breast tumors; (III) US failed to clearly visualize ductal lesions; (IV) low-quality US images. A total of 258 lesions were finally included.

US and CEUS examination

Baseline US, color Doppler flow imaging (CDFI) and CEUS were performed. The CEUS instruments included Siemens ACUSON (10L4 linear array probe), Mindray Resona 7 (L9-3 linear array probe), Mindray Resona A20 (SL10-3U linear array probe) and Philips iU22 (L9-3 linear array probe). The CEUS agent used was SonoVue (produced by Bracco, Italy), a freeze-dried powder (containing 59 mg of sulfur hexafluoride microbubbles). It was mixed with 5 mL of normal saline to form a suspension, which was then left to oscillate for 30 seconds. Then a dosage of 4.8 mL of the agent suspension was bolus-injected into the antecubital vein. During the entire CEUS procedure, we used pulse inversion harmonic imaging with a mechanical index <0.15, adjusted the depth and enlarged the lesion properly according to the location and size of the lesion, and converted to dual image mode for real-time synchronous observation of US and CEUS characteristics of the lesion. The dynamic images up to 90 s after administration of the contrast agent were recorded for further analysis. For patients with bilateral lesions, CEUS was performed sequentially. First, one breast lesion was examined after contrast injection, and imaging data were acquired and analyzed. Subsequently, a high-mechanical-index flash sequence was applied to destroy the microbubbles within the imaging field, ensuring complete clearance of contrast agent from the previously examined lesion. After confirmation of contrast washout, a second bolus of contrast agent was administered to evaluate the contralateral breast lesion. This approach ensured that each lesion was assessed independently without interference from residual contrast from the prior injection.

Image analysis

Two radiologists, each with over 10 years of experience in breast US, independently reviewed all images. To evaluate the reproducibility of the assessment, they independently reviewed a randomly selected subset of 50 lesions (19.4% of the total cohort). Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ) was used to assess inter-reader agreement for each CEUS feature. A κ value of <0.20 indicated poor agreement, 0.21–0.40 fair, 0.41–0.60 moderate, 0.61–0.80 good, and 0.81–1.00 excellent agreement. Both of them were blind to any clinical information and final histological results.

Lesion size (maximal diameter on US images), characteristics on baseline US and Alder grading of blood flow signals on CDFI, together with enhancement characteristics in CEUS, were recorded. Among them, the morphologic type of intraductal lesions was classified as follows based on the US features: type I: duct ectasia with a mass inside the duct (a mass incompletely or completely filling the duct); type II: a regular hypoechoic nodule without a definite dilated duct around it; type III: a regular hypoechoic nodule with a definite dilated duct around it and type IV: an irregular hypoechoic nodule with or without a definite dilated duct around it. CEUS parameters of the lesion were recorded, including enhancement time, enhancement intensity, enhancement boundary, enhancement distribution, enhancement order, enhancement range (whether enlarged), perfusion defect, and spiculate sign (26).

US BI-RADS category and 5-point scoring of CEUS were performed successively for the analyzed breast lesions, and then were reconciled to a rerated BI-RADS category (11). The US BI-RADS categories were determined as follows: category 3, probably benign (with a probability of malignancy <2%); category 4, suspicious (with a probability of malignancy between 2 and 95%); category 5, highly suggestive of malignancy (≥95% likelihood of malignancy). Lesions classified in category 4 were subcategorized further into 4a (>2% to ≤10% likelihood of malignancy), 4b (>10% to ≤50% likelihood of malignancy) or 4c (>50% to <95% likelihood of malignancy).

All dynamic CEUS images were analyzed, and the enhancement patterns of the target lesion were evaluated and scored according to the 5-point system established in prior research (15,27,28). The detailed scoring criteria are shown in Table 1 and Figure 1, and the US BI-RADS classification will be adjusted accordingly based on the corresponding CEUS scores (24), as detailed in Table 2. According to the ACR BI-RADS Atlas, category 4a lesions have a low suspicion of malignancy (2% to 10%) and require biopsy. Therefore, in the analysis, US and CEUS BI-RADS 4a, 4b, 4c, and 5 lesions were considered test-positive (i.e., clinically suspicious requiring tissue diagnosis), while BI-RADS 3 was considered test-negative.

The reference standards

All target lesions were pathologically confirmed by surgical resection or core needle biopsy within three weeks after US examination. For lesions initially diagnosed as high-risk (e.g., atypical ductal hyperplasia) or suspicious on biopsy, surgical excision was recommended for definitive diagnosis. All BI-RADS 3 lesions included in this study underwent pathological confirmation because they presented with PND. At our institution, during the study period, surgical duct excision or image-guided biopsy was routinely performed for PND patients even when imaging findings were low-suspicion (BI-RADS 3), in order to rule out occult malignancy or high-risk lesions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical software package SPSS 25.0 software and MedCalc 20.1 were used. Measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, using a two-sided t-test. The lesion patterns of US and CEUS were presented as percentages, and the differences were examined using chi-square or Fisher’s exact probability method. To account for the potential correlation between multiple lesions within the same patient, we used generalized estimating equations with an exchangeable working correlation matrix. Patient identification was set as the subject variable. This approach was applied to all lesion-level analyses, including univariate comparisons and multivariate logistic regression. Then, this analysis screened independent factors for identifying malignant lesions and the relevant odds ratio (OR) was calculated with 95% CI. In the analysis, BI-RADS 4a, 4b, 4c, and 5 lesions were considered test-positive, while BI-RADS 3 was considered test-negative. The sensitivity, specificity, PPV, negative predictive value (NPV), and accuracy of US BI-RADS and CEUS BI-RADS scoring were calculated. DeLong test was conducted to compare the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). The difference was considered statistically significant when P<0.05.

Results

Clinical and pathological information of enrolled PND patients

A total of 258 breast lesions of 217 female patients were finally enrolled (Figure 2). Thirteen patients had bilateral breast discharge, and the other 204 subjects had unilateral breast discharge. With positive breast symptoms, 65.4% (142/217) women showed bloody ND or initial non-bloody changed to bloody discharge; the other cases with abnormal ND presented non-bloody overflow.

Inter-reader agreement for CEUS features

In the randomly selected subset of 50 lesions, inter-reader agreement for CEUS features was moderate to good. The κ values were 0.60 (95% CI, 0.42–0.78) for enhanced range, 0.58 (95% CI, 0.38–0.78) for enhancing order, 0.46 (95% CI, 0.24–0.67) for enhanced homogeneity, 0.65 (95% CI, 0.20–1.00) for enhanced level, 0.64 (95% CI, 0.43–0.85) for enhanced edge, 0.73 (95% CI, 0.38–1.00) for enhanced time, 0.68 (95% CI, 0.49–0.87) for perfusion defect, and 0.45 (95% CI, 0.11–0.79) for spiculate sign. The κ values for the key CEUS features ranged from 0.45 to 0.73, indicating moderate to good inter-reader agreement. These findings indicate that the CEUS feature assessment was reproducible, supporting the reliability of the single-reader evaluation for the full cohort.

Independent predictors of malignant breast lesions in US and CEUS

Comparisons of baseline US and CEUS characteristics between benign and malignant lesions were shown in Table 5. The univariate analysis showed that lesion size, morphology type, internal echo distribution, boundary, internal calcification, posterior echo, and blood flow in US were statistically different between benign and malignant (all P<0.04). However, there was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of internal echogenicity and aspect ratio (anteroposterior dimension/width) (P=0.753 and 0.780, respectively). Besides, CEUS features, including enhancement range, enhancement order, enhancement mode, enhanced boundary, enhancement time and spiculate sign, differed significantly between malignant and benign lesions (all P<0.005). However, there was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of enhancement amplitude and perfusion defect (P=0.143 and 0.328, respectively). In the multivariate analysis, age (P=0.013), size (P=0.013), enhancement range (P=0.002), enhancement order (P=0.007), and spiculated sign (P=0.003) were independent predictors (Table 6).

US BI-RADS and CEUS BI-RADS category correlated with pathology were exhibited in Table 7. Compared to US BI-RADS, the CEUS BI-RADS obtain a higher sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, accuracy, which are 0.930 (95% CI: 0.822–0.977), 0.343 (95% CI: 0.279–0.414), 0.286 (95% CI: 0.224–0.358), 0.945 (95% CI: 0.858–0.982) and 0.473 (95% CI: 0.411–0.536), respectively (Table 8). Reclassification of BI-RADS 4A lesions by CEUS

A total of 132 lesions were initially classified as BI-RADS 4a on conventional US. Among these, pathological examination confirmed 109 as benign and 23 as malignant. Following CEUS assessment, no lesions were downgraded to CEUS BI-RADS 3. 9 malignant lesions (6.8% of all 4a) were upgraded to CEUS BI-RADS 4b or higher, ensuring appropriate biopsy.

Analysis of rerating and treatment decision changes

Nine cases of malignant lesions diagnosed by original US BI-RADS category 4a were adjusted to CEUS rerated BI-RADS category 4b (n=4) and 4c (n=5), according to the features of enlarger hyper-enhancement area compared with that on the US image, and enhancement earlier than surrounding tissue, with (n=5) or without (n=4) marginal burr sign. Discussion

This study revealed different CEUS enhancement characteristics between benign and malignant PND-related breast lesions. We used the 5-point scoring system and re-rated the CEUS BI-RADS lexicon developed by Xiao et al. to evaluate causative breast lesions in PND (24). CEUS may provide complementary functional information and may influence selected reclassification patterns, but the manuscript should not imply overall diagnostic superiority.

ND represents the third most presenting symptom in breast specialty practice, after pain and lumps (1,2). High-resolution superficial US was used as a primary clinical work-up tool in etiologic examination of ND (29), with the advantages of being convenient, inexpensive and non-invasive. It depicts the morphological features for differential diagnosis and provides precise location for invasive treatment or follow-up observation. The US BI-RADS provides a standard categorization for lesion morphology that has acceptable diagnostic efficiency for clinical evaluation (30).

However, conventional US has inherent limitations. There is considerable overlap in morphological features between benign and malignant breast lesions, which results in the low specificity of US BI-RADS. This is particularly evident for category 4 lesions, which encompass a broad range of malignancy probability (2% to 95%) (31,32). Moreover, compared with CEUS, they fail to capture important biological behaviors, such as tumor neovascularization.

In our study, intraductal papilloma was the leading cause of PND, accounting for 47.8% (96/201) of benign lesions and 37.2% (96/258) of total lesions, which was consistent with the previously reported range of 35% to 48% (33). Our results showed that the features of calcification, shape type, boundary, internal echogenicity, posterior echo, and blood flow were important indicators in differential diagnosis, conforming to the previous studies (16,34). However, the aspect ratio (<1 or ≥1) showed no statistically significant difference between benign and malignant lesions in our study, which was inconsistent with previous studies (11,35). We deduced that the possible reason was that all malignant observations included in this study were relatively large in size (2.27±2.01 cm), yet increased aspect ratio mostly appeared in relatively small tumors (36).

Under CEUS, most malignant breast tumors in our study exhibited indistinct enhancement boundaries, enlarged enhancement ranges, and spiculate margins, with significant differences compared to benign lesions. These features likely reflect the micro-infiltration of adjacent tissues and heterogeneous stromal distribution characteristic of infiltrative tumor growth, as opposed to the expansive growth pattern typical of benign lesions (37). Multiple enhancement characteristics, including enhancement range, order, mode, time, boundary and spiculate sign, were significantly different between benign and malignant lesions. It has been clarified that vessels in benign lesions are always singular, circumferential, regular, and tapering, whereas tortuous, peripheral vessels and vessel knots are more often detected in malignant lesions (38,39). By using microbubble contrast agents, CEUS provides real-time dynamic assessment of blood perfusion with excellent spatial and temporal resolution, thereby offering valuable pathophysiological information to complement morphological imaging (40). Our results further confirmed the benefit of CEUS in the differential diagnosis of breast lesions that was revealed in previous research (15,24,26).

The findings of this study have several important implications for clinical practice. First, the high sensitivity (93.0%) and NPV (94.5%) achieved by CEUS when BI-RADS 4a lesions were considered test-positive suggest that CEUS may serve as a valuable rule-out test. In clinical settings, a negative CEUS finding could provide sufficient reassurance to consider short-interval follow-up instead of immediate biopsy for carefully selected patients with low-suspicion lesions. This approach has the potential to reduce associated patient anxiety and maintain diagnostic safety. Second, CEUS demonstrated particular value in reclassifying lesions initially categorized as US BI-RADS 4b or higher. In our study, 20 lesions were over-diagnosed as US BI-RADS category 4b; after CEUS evaluation, 13 of these were downgraded to CEUS BI-RADS category 3 (n=2) or 4a (n=11), thereby reducing the priority for immediate invasive intervention or supporting individualized follow-up strategies. This finding highlights the potential of CEUS to improve specificity and reduce false-positive rates in clinical practice. Third, the ability of CEUS to visualize tumor vascularity and infiltration patterns may aid in surgical planning. Lesions showing indistinct enhancement boundaries and spiculate margins on CEUS may require wider excision margins, whereas those with distinct enhancement boundaries may be amenable to more conservative approaches. Nevertheless, the optimal integration of CEUS into clinical workflows requires further validation through prospective multi-center studies with standardized protocols and long-term follow-up outcomes.

When BI-RADS 4a lesions were classified as test-positive in accordance with ACR guidelines, the overall diagnostic performance of CEUS BI-RADS, as measured by AUROC, did not significantly surpass that of conventional US (AUC: 0.635 vs. 0.637, P>0.05). This finding warrants careful interpretation. Several factors may account for this observation. First, BI-RADS 4a lesions inherently carry a low risk of malignancy (2–10%), and in our cohort, the majority of 4a lesions were pathologically benign. This low disease prevalence within the 4a category may dilute the overall diagnostic gain of CEUS when 4a is included in the test-positive group. Second, CEUS primarily provides functional information regarding tumor vascularity, which may be less pronounced in low-grade or early-stage malignancies that often present as 4a lesions. For instance, low-grade malignancies, particularly small DCIS, may not induce substantial neoangiogenesis and may instead rely on pre-existing surrounding vessels for blood supply, resulting in benign-appearing enhancement patterns (41). Furthermore, some malignant lesions may exhibit enhancement features that overlap with those of benign lesions such as distinct boundary or homogeneous enhancement, especially in early-stage disease (42).

Despite the lack of superiority in overall AUROC, CEUS demonstrated clinically valuable advantages in specific aspects, as outlined above. These findings suggest that CEUS may be best positioned not as a standalone diagnostic tool, but as a complementary adjunct to conventional US, particularly in refining the management of BI-RADS 4a lesions. Its strength lies in its ability to rule out malignancy with high confidence, thereby potentially reducing unnecessary biopsies in carefully selected patients, rather than in improving overall diagnostic accuracy across all lesion categories.

In the current study, 3 malignant lesions were misdiagnosed by CEUS. All of these lesions were DCIS. They showed hyperenhancement earlier compared with the surrounding gland tissue, and no visual expansion of enhancement or spiculated sign was seen, and were assigned CEUS score 3. We inferred that the absence of malignant signs for that low-grade carcinoma in situ may grow without inducing substantial neovascularization when the surrounding capillary network is sufficient to nourish its merisis, and the deficiency of malformed neovascularity may lead to misdiagnosis (15,43). Conversely, 20 benign lesions (including 12 cases of intraductal papilloma, 6 cases of adenopathy, 1 case of atypical ductal hyperplasia and 1 case of inflammation) were over-diagnosed as US BI-RADS category 4b. CEUS reclassified 2 of these as CEUS BI-RADS category 3, thereby potentially avoiding unnecessary biopsy. Therefore, CEUS was an effective means to improve the ability to distinguish benign lesions in the absence of enlarged enhancement induced by infiltrative growth (27,44).

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study, the total number of cases and pathological categories included was limited. Second, we excluded patients without identifiable lesions on conventional US, which introduces selection bias and limits the generalizability of our findings to the broader PND population. In real-world practice, many patients with PND may have no US-detectable abnormalities. As such, our results regarding the diagnostic value of CEUS should be interpreted with caution when applied to patients lacking visible lesions on initial US. Further research incorporating imaging modalities capable of detecting occult lesions is warranted to address this gap. Third, comparative analysis of CEUS with MRI or mammography was not performed, as a considerable number of patients did not undergo those examinations. Fourth, we acknowledge that PPV and NPV are prevalence-dependent parameters. In our study, the calculated PPV and NPV reflect diagnostic performance specifically in a consecutive cohort of PND patients with a malignancy prevalence of 22.1%. Therefore, these values should be interpreted within this specific clinical context and may not be directly generalizable to populations with different disease prevalences, such as screening cohorts or patients with palpable masses. Nevertheless, our findings provide valuable reference data for the use of CEUS in the PND population, and future multicenter studies with larger and more diverse cohorts are warranted to validate the reproducibility and generalizability of our results.

Notably, the classification of BI-RADS 4a lesions as test-positive in accordance with clinical guidelines may have influenced our diagnostic performance analysis. As discussed above, this approach may have inadvertently underestimated the true diagnostic value of CEUS by including numerous benign 4a lesions as false positives, thereby lowering specificity for both modalities. Future studies with larger sample sizes of BI-RADS 4a lesions are warranted to further validate the role of CEUS in this specific subgroup. Additionally, combining CEUS with other US techniques, such as elastography or super-resolution US imaging, may further enhance diagnostic accuracy and the value of these complementary techniques requires future investigation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, CEUS demonstrates high sensitivity and NPV in evaluating breast lesions associated with PND. Although its overall discriminatory ability, as measured by the area under the ROC curve, was not significantly different from conventional US BI-RADS when 4a lesions were considered test-positive, CEUS provides complementary functional information that may aid in risk stratification. These findings support the role of CEUS as a useful adjunct to conventional US, particularly in refining the assessment of low-suspicion lesions and guiding biopsy decisions in patients with PND.
