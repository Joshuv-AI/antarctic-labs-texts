Optimizing the factors affecting the quality condition of 4WD tractors’ engine oil using the hybrid RSM-GRA method
Authors: Seyed Mojtaba Faregh, Fatemeh Afsharnia, Rasoul Memar Dastjerdi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Oil condition monitoring is vital for enhancing engine performance, reducing fuel consumption, and preventing wear and tear on engine parts. Tractors, like other agricultural machines, are operated in dusty or muddy and polluted environments of the farmland, which strongly affects the engine oil quality. To prevent damage to the tractor engine, assist in preventive maintenance planning, reduce unexpected stoppages and excessive repair costs, and increasing the availability and reliability of agricultural tractors, this study aimed to find the precise time of a 4WD tractor’s engine oil change using RSM-GRA optimization hybrid method to determine and optimize oil change intervals in infant mortality, useful, and wear-out lives of tractors. The effect of operating hours and tractor age on the quality features of engine oil showed that all wear debris increases when tractor age and operating hours increase. However, the impact of tractor age was greater than operating hours. Overall, operating hours of 93.6 h are suggested for oil change intervals of tractors in infant mortality life, which resulted in a Fe of 0.076, Cu of 0.96, Si of 0.52, B of 0.01, Zn and P of 0.014, Ca of 0.3, Mn of 0.0072, PQ of 0.002, and the rest of the metals are zero. Moreover, operating hours of 53.22 h and 35.5 h are the optimal conditions for oil change intervals of tractors in useful life and wear-out life, respectively. As the tractor’s age increases, the oil change intervals become shorter. As a result, it can be said that the tractor’s age and the oil change intervals are closely related, and as the tractor wears out, the oil change interval is sometimes reduced by half.

Introduction

Maintenance is one of the most important aspects of boosting the efficiency and productivity of agricultural mechanized production1. Timely agricultural activities in accordance with the plan should be guaranteed by an appropriate maintenance strategy. The tractor, which is essential to the planting, growing, and harvesting processes as well as the mechanization industry, is now regarded as one of the power sources in mechanized agriculture and a significant portion of the farm’s fixed costs. Agricultural mechanization progress hinges on how this power source is used. Since it is important to reach an ideal level of mechanization and manufacturing technology for agricultural machinery in order to accomplish sustainable agriculture2.

The optimal use of the tractor depends on the attention paid to the quality and durability of its consumable parts. If the quality of the parts is not optimal, their breakage and wear will cause unwanted stops in the field, which affects the technical performance and economic efficiency of the machine. Therefore, paying attention to the probability of failure, repair time, and using accurate predictive tools seems necessary1. If proper field maintenance of tractors is carried out over the long term, a large part of the work will be done before major repairs are needed. The extent of the tractor breakdown problem in developing countries is more serious compared to developed countries, and this is due to the lack of critical spare parts, preventive maintenance, and the lack of a new plan to integrate maintenance management and programs that try to detect early defects before they become critical3,4. Determining the capacity and optimal number of required machines and planning their work, as well as providing spare parts and determining the optimal time of replacement to maintain machine reliability, are some of the prerequisites for the requirement for forecasting5. The service and maintenance costs and the machine stoppage are reduced to an acceptable extent by establishing an effective maintenance strategy that causes a significant increase in added value in production activities6. Ownership costs and variable costs are the two main categories of expenses associated with tractors. Maintenance expenses typically account for 10–15% of the entire cost of the machine7, 35, it can be decreased up to 50% of maintenance costs by implementing an effective maintenance strategy1. Maintenance is a set of activities that are specifically and usually planned to prevent the sudden breakdown of machines, equipment, and facilities to increase the machine’s reliability and availability8. Maintenance strategies are divided into two types: preventive maintenance (PM) and corrective maintenance (CM)9. In the corrective maintenance strategy, maintenance is done after a breakdown or an apparent failure occurs. In a preventive maintenance strategy, maintenance is implemented to prevent the breakage of parts. Preventive maintenance strategy applies time-based maintenance (TBM) programs to determine replacement parts10,11), which includes a daily lubrication schedule, checks, and adjustments12. Engine oil absorbs metal particles, iron shavings, or any impurity inside engine components, and this has a direct effect on increasing its concentration. Engine oil that loses its standard condition is neither suitable for cooling nor can it lubricate parts well. This state works like the blocked arteries in the human body. Just as blocked arteries put a lot of pressure on the heart, improper engine oil also puts a lot of pressure on the car’s engine system; pressure can raise component temperatures and friction, which can ultimately lead to engine failure.

Oil analysis is a method of maintenance and repairs that can examine the machine thoroughly and identify any hidden or neglected unfavorable conditions13. Any machinery and mechanical equipment that uses oil as a lubricant or a power transmission can use the oil analysis method. It is feasible to gather thorough knowledge regarding wear debris suspended in machine oil by employing oil analysis14. In mechanical systems where components are in contact with oil, the oil monitoring method has a high capability in detecting mechanical defects. As an effective tool, it provides the possibility of optimizing systems and various monitoring, such as checking the depreciation process, the quality of parts and materials, and how to carry out repairs. Sejkorova and Hurtova15 exceeded the exchange intervals recommended by JohnDeere tractors’ manufacturer and compared the obtained results of the experimental evaluation. They did not provide any oil change interval optimizations for operators to use. Raposo et al.16 presented a mathematical model to follow and manage the oil condition for urban buses. They aimed to maximize availability by predicting the next intervention with the maximum time between them. In another research, they studied the evolution of degradation and developed a predictive maintenance strategy for oil replacement. Extending the oil replacement intervals following the oil condition analysis can result in increased availability17. Vališ et al.18 optimize combustion engine maintenance and predict the remaining useful life of the system by applying a fuzzy inference system for oil analysis. They considered both operating time and calendar time as affected factors on the concentration of wear particles, iron and lead, and additive particles in the oil. The oil analysis was examined by Kučera et al.19 in order to apply preventive maintenance. They reported some key parameters of hydraulic oils, turbine oils, gear oils, and compressors without providing numerical results. Engine oil characteristics and specifications were investigated by Rawashdeh et al.20 to look into how they affected engine performance and maintenance. To assess the effect of field activities on tractor engine oil changes, Gołębiowski et al. (2022) measured the trace element content of the oil, which included Cu, Cr, Fe, Ni, Zn, Ca, P, Pb, and Mo. Famakinwa and Shibutani21 proposed a method that gives the prospect of monitoring the cylinder liners’ wear without physically measuring marine engine cylinder liners using X-ray. Zaharia et al.22 evaluated engine oil wear and issued maintenance recommendations to diagnose the truck engine operation. They analyzed the viscosity, density, flash point, fuel contamination, and water content of the oil (oil properties). The optimization of engine oil change intervals was not conducted in this study.

So far, no study has been done to investigate and predict the effect of tractor wear and hours of oil use on wear debris and water content of the 4WD tractor, namely 399 ITM made in Iran Tractor Manufacturing Company (ITM Co); so farmers and tractor operators can understand the difference in the effect of use hours on the quality of engine oil in different life of the tractor. Therefore, this study tries for the first time to investigate the effect of tractor engine wear and hours of its use on the process of creating wear debris in engine oil with the help of the engine oil analysis method, and to predict it in the three initial, useful, and wear-out lives.

Materials and methods

Tractor selection and life stage classification

Due to a large number of ITM 399 4WD tractors (more than 60% of all tractors) and the greater use of this tractor model than other models by farmers in the region, three life of ITM 399 4WD tractors were considered in the initial, useful, and wear-out lives based on bathtub curve (Fig. 1). Three distinct phases are represented by the bathtub curve, a graphical depiction of the variation of component failure rates over their life cycle: early failures (infant mortality), a period of constant failure rate (useful life), and increasing failure rates as a result of wear-out mechanisms23. Table 1 lists the specifications of the ITM 399 4WD tractor.

First, the breakdown statistics of the tractors in Ahvaz city were gathered during the previous year to determine the wear condition of the tractors. Based on the failure rate and the bathtub curve, a decision was made regarding the tractor’s life. The failure rate (λ), which is a measure of the frequency of breakdowns, is the parameter that determines a machine’s reliability. Once the samples were collected, we sent them to the oil analysis laboratory to measure 19 wear debris in the oil, fuel dilution content, and water content. In this experiment, experimental treatments included the first factor of tractor life and the second factor of engine oil usage hours. Following the results, RSM-GRA methods were used to investigate the optimal oil condition based on wear debris, fuel dilution content, and water content with varying use hours to determine the appropriate hour of engine oil change for each tractor’s life.

Oil sampling procedure

To investigate and predict the effect of tractor life and use hours on engine oil quality, 19 types of particles including wear metals detected in oil (iron, aluminum, copper, chromium, lead, nickel, tin, molybdenum, silver, and titanium), common additive metals (phosphorus, zinc, barium, magnesium, and calcium), and contaminate metals (sodium, silicon, vanadium, and boron) in ppm, pollution including water content, fuel dilution content and particle quantifying (PQ) in engine oil, the factorial test was done based on a completely randomized design with three replications.

Testing of metallic and non-metallic elements in oil is identified through spectroscopic testing. Metal elements in used oil that result from wear, pollution, or additive deterioration are identified and measured with this test. In this method, the oil is placed in direct contact with an energy source so that each of the elements absorbs a certain amount of energy to determine the concentration of the elements in the oil. This result will indicate all insoluble metal elements (even additives) and pollution particles.

The water content was measured through the Karl Fischer test. The test result reports the exact content of water in ppm. This test, which is known as the Crackle Test, is done to roughly identify the content of water in the oil. In this method, a few drops of oil are placed on an electric hot plate temperature of around 150 °C, and we try to observe for bubbles, crackling, or popping. If the oil crackles, it indicates water. Water in the oil will bubble up and separate from the oil. The majority of manufacturers of mechanical equipment recommend against adding more than 0.1% to the oil because doing so can contaminate the oil and alter the wear condition of the equipment. Generally speaking, values under 0.1% are not problematic, unless the device’s manufacturer has set a lower allowable limit.

Experimental procedure

Atomic emission spectroscopy

In order to analyze metals using Atomic Emission Spectroscopy, initially, for each element, the desired lamp is placed in the instrument, and the instrument is adjusted according to the relevant wavelength. The sample is dissolved in acid. After preparation, the sample is introduced into an atomic plasma (like in ICP-OES), which excites the metallic particles, causing them to emit light. The intensity of this emitted light at specific wavelengths reveals the type and concentration of wear elements present. To produce the flame, the air, compressed air and nitrogen oxide gas are used, and the type of oxidizer gas depends on the element. Acetylene gas is also consumed as a fuel.

Particle Quantifier(PQ)

The particle Quantifier instrument is designed to show the iron particles present in the lubricants. The basic operation of the particle quantity measuring instrument, which is a magnetometer, is to measure the changes in the magnetic force of iron particles in the magnetic field due to proximity to the oil sample. In fact, the magnetic field lines of a coil inserted into the instrument by iron particles present in the oil sample are distorted, and the amount of these changes is represented by a digital system. The results shown by the instrument are called the PQ index.

Viscosity Index(VI)

The resistance that an oil reacts to the flow due to its internal friction is called viscosity. The viscosity of an oil is changed by the oil temperature. In the case of higher oil temperature, its viscosity increases. Hence, it is always necessary to express the viscosity along with the temperature at which the viscosity is present. To determine the kinematic viscosity, capillary tubes called viscometers are usually used.

Water content

Many of the engine failures are due to oil pollution with water or antifreeze. With conventional water pollution tests, the amount of water in the engine oil is not properly represented, as the engine oil is so hot that the water in it evaporates, so water analysis is mainly used in hydraulic systems, gearboxes, and so on. But by the use of spectrometry, it is possible to obtain anti - frozen chemical elements in the oil in terms of ppm of sodium (Na), boron (B), and potassium (K). When sodium, boron, or potassium was found in the Crankcase housing oil, it was found that antifreeze contamination was occurring.

Grey relational coefficient calculation

GRA is a component of grey system theory that uses quantitative analysis to demonstrate the similarities and differences between references and alternative series. Similar to the TOPSIS and VIKOR techniques, the gray relational analysis method begins with a decision matrix. However, in addition to distinguishing between negative and positive criteria, it also identifies the most desirable value. Furthermore, the TOPSIS method has a high dependence on the weight of each index to be evaluated, and the weight between the indicators in the TOPSIS method will affect the final evaluation result28, while the resulting weights of GRA are more closely aligned with real-world scenarios because the method considers the characteristics of the index system and the relationships between indicators.

Grey Theory overcomes the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) by enabling decision-making with limited or uncertain data, reducing reliance on expert experience and computational complexity, and providing a more robust model to handle “grey” systems where information is incomplete or vague. Therefore, the GRA method is suitable for oil monitoring analysis, as poor sampling techniques or delays in sending samples to the lab can result in outdated or inaccurate data.

Hybrid method of RSM and GRA

It is unlikely that GRA makes any inferences regarding the relationship between the circumstances of the alternative and the ideal or non-ideal alternative; it can only provide a broad understanding of the trend between an alternative, an ideal alternative, and a non-ideal alternative. All of the aforementioned shortcomings can be filled in with RSM. Additionally, by taking into account the significant variables when calculating each option’s score, a ranking of all potential solutions can be obtained. To find the best alternative, a new MADM method called RSM-GRA is proposed. This method combines the advantages of both the RSM and GRA methods. Figure 2 illustrates the phases in the RSM-GRA approach. First, we defined the factors that may influence the response. Process parameters, material characteristics, or other pertinent variables might be examples of these input variables. Then, we determined the range and levels at which each factor will be tested. This guarantees a thorough comprehension of the factor space. Next, we selected a suitable experiment design that yielded reliable data while making the most use of available resources. Box-Behnken Design (BBD), Central Composite Design (CCD), and factorial tests are examples of common designs. Once the ANOVA test was run, we found the solutions by numerical optimization. The optimized solutions were considered as the inputs of Grey Theory. After the GRA calculations were performed, the risky wear debris was determined.

Wear debris

As engine components operate, microscopic amounts of wear debris are added to the oil. Under normal engine conditions, wear occurs slowly, and the amount of wear debris in the oil increases steadily and slowly. Regular and sequential sampling and evaluation of the amount of wear debris in these samples will identify any imbalances and, in this way, possible causes can be identified. The response surface methodology’s findings regarding the impact of tractor age and operating hours on engine oil wear metals revealed that the tractor’s age had a greater impact on wear metal levels than the operating hours, although the changes of these metals were considerable in different operating hours.

As observed in Fig. 4, the operating hours and tractor age had a significant effect on wear metals, including Fe, Cr, Al, Pb, and Ni. Nevertheless, these changes for wear debris at the infant mortality life were less severe than the wear-out life. However, the rise in wear debris is significantly influenced by tractor age. For total Fe, Cr, Pb, Al, and Ni metals changes, wear metals increased when the oil engine was used for 80–100 h in the wear-out life of the tractor. The most often used structural component is Fe, which is also the most frequently occurring wear metal in used lubricants. On the other hand, an increased Fe content is a natural phenomenon for oil because Fe is the main structural metal in the engine29, that is in agreement with Gołębiowski, and Zając (2020)34. So, increased Fe suggests the wear of several parts include cylinder liners, piston rings, valve train, crankshaft, camshaft, spring gears, lock washers, nuts, pins, fittings, cylinder block, and oil pump.

The most common forms of elemental Fe are carbon steel, cast iron, and high alloy steels, which are used to make valves, cams, and shafts. Cast iron is used to make most machinery cases. The majority of roller-bearing races and rolling parts are made of carbonized steel. Carbonized steel is also used in the production of most gears. Stainless steel or high alloy steel is used to make most industrial cams, valves, and shafts. These high alloy steels contain trace amounts of Ni, Cr, and sometimes titanium. Besides that, the most common alloying metal used is Cr in high-alloy and stainless steels. Furthermore, Cr is used to make chrome-plated rings. Shaft wear is usually indicated by higher iron levels and residues of Cr. Increased Cr in engine oil indicates wear of the rings, liners, and exhaust valves of a diesel engine.

Pb is used as an alloying metal in Babbitt sleeve-bearing overlays. The bulk of Babbitt metal in electric motors and engines is lead-based; hence, lead will be the primary alloy component even if the majority of Babbitt metal is tin-based. Other sources of lead include sealing compounds, corrosion-resistant coatings used within cases (older equipment), and solder connections. Increased lead indicates wear in the main, fixed, and moving bearings, which could be part of the contamination from diesel fuel. Gołębiowski et al. (2022) discovered that significant wear-induced changes in the metal elements composition, including Cr, Cu, and Pb, were caused by agricultural operations, including a cultivator.

As observed for Al, Cu, and Mo metals, the trend of these showed that the highest wear debris was related to wear-out life with operating hours of 100 h. In the infant mortality life, increasing operating hours was effective on the wear of Mo and Cu metals, while both including operating hours and tractor age affected the wear of Mo, Cu, and Al metals in the wear-out life. Al is one of the most valuable metals in equipment because of its high strength. It also has a very high corrosion resistance. Al alloys with other metals increase heat resistance. Engine block is frequently constructed primarily of Al. Al can also be used to make aluminum bronze, another alloying metal. Al is layered over a lot of bushings and sleeve bearings. Some sealing chemicals also contain Al. Therefore, increased Al can predict wear of cylinders, pistons, air intakes, oil pump bushings, and some bearings. In addition to Al, Cu is almost always found as an alloy, most commonly as bronze (Cu and Sn) or brass (Cu and Zn). These alloys with high copper content are called “Yellow Metals.” Cu from these metals may be leached or corroded by certain AW and EP additives. Certain cooler exchangers also contain Cu, which can contaminate the lubricant. In other words, the presence of copper metal in the oil might signal cracks in the cooling system and leakage into the lubricant. A small number—mostly gear lubricants—include Cu as an anti-wear ingredient. In general, copper has good malleability and is a good conductor of heat and cold. This metal is widely used in friction-reducing systems (bearings) as well as in heat transfer. High levels of copper in engine oil indicate wear in the gudgeon pin bush, cam, thrust buttons, valve gear, governor, and bearings. Based on Fig. 3, and according to Hönig et al.29, the increased amount through operating hours and also tractor age can occur due to the increased abrasion.

Common additive metals

The results in Fig. 5 illustrate that the increase in tractor age had a significant effect on increasing the additive metals, especially Mg, Ca, and Cd. The Ca and Mn levels rise with tractor’s operating hours, reaching 3620 and 4 ppm at 100 h during the wear-out life, respectively. For the Mn metal, the changes for the infant mortality life and useful life were similar to Z and P, but with the increase of operating hours in the wear-out life, the amount of Mn alloying metal sharply increased in the engine oil.

According to Kumar et al.30,31, additives create different properties. An additive that prevents corrosion is Ca. High concentrations of it are only found in engine oils; R&O lubricants never contain it. It is the primary ingredient in the majority of engine oils for acid neutralization. The more calcium there is in modern engine lubricants, the greater the TBN, or level of protection against the buildup of corrosive acids. While the average concentration of calcium in modern lubricants is between 500 and 3000 ppm, certain high-base diesel engine oils contain as much as 4000 or 5000 ppm.

Contaminant elements

As found, the average contaminated metalloids (Si and B), and metal (Na) were increased by increasing the hours of working essential oils for all three lives, infant mortality, useful and wear-out lives (Fig. 6). Based on many types of research, the Si, Na, and B gradually increase during tractor life. Based on these metals’ results, one of the main sources of Na is dust and impurities, which are abundant in agricultural fields and during tractor operation, and can be increased by increasing dust. Increased dust penetration from the diesel engine’s air intake increases Si. Furthermore, Si is the most common metalloid on Earth. Numerous materials such as sand, airborne dirt and dust (poor condition of the air filter), coatings, sealants, cooling water, and grease are potential sources of Si contamination. In extremely rare circumstances, it may be present as an anti-foam additive; however, in most cases, its concentration is less than 20 ppm.

The most common way that B is detected is as a byproduct of cooling water intrusion. Most coolant corrosion inhibitor packages contain B as an additive, which is a detergent and anti-corrosion agent. The majority of it is waterborne. Contamination is almost always an indication of coolant intrusion. B gives some engine and gear oils their anti-wear and detergent properties. As an additive, its usual concentration is less than 300 ppm.

Physical properties

The results in Fig. 7 illustrate that the increase in tractor age had a significant effect on increasing PQ. Regardless of the size or shape of the debris, the PQ Index measures the relative amount of Fe metal in your oil by using a magnetic field to identify it. The overall concentration of ferrous material, which is typically linked to wear, increases with a sample’s PQ index reading. By increasing the operational hours of the tractor, the PQ of the engine oil is raised such that the PQ is estimated as 14.6 for infant mortality life, 18.2 for usable life, and 36.8 for wear-out life, respectively. This observation is in agreement with Álamo et al.32, who reported that the PQ was increased by more operating machines. Moreover, the Vis40 of the engine oil was decreased by increasing both the operating hours and tractor age. The results were in agreement with those of Hönig et al.29, who observed that the Vis40 concentration of all three CaseQuadTrac 600 tractors, the Case Magnum RowTrac 340 tractor, and the New Holland T7.250 tractor significantly decreased during operating hours.

The fuel dilution ranges from 0 to 0.1 ppm, and its limit of 0.1 ppm has not been exceeded, and this value was constant in all engine oil samples. When oil is diluted by mixing with fuel, the quality of lubrication decreases, causing wear on parts and an increase in engine operating temperature. Oil separates the metal surfaces of the engine, provides a seal between the combustion chamber and the crankcase, and is also responsible for heat transfer in the engine. If it is diluted, it does not perform these functions properly and causes other failures in the engine. This phenomenon occurs due to leaking or defective injectors, excessive idle, incomplete compression, worn rings or gaskets, improper timing (incorrect timing of engine valves), inexperienced operators or drivers, working environmental conditions, leakage from the fuel pump and its gasket, improper air-to-fuel ratio, poor fuel quality, and using the engine for purposes other than those for which the engine was designed. The water content was not observed in any oil sample, and the test result was negative for all engine oil samples. The presence of water can reduce oil life by 3 to 10 times and cause severe corrosion in some bearings.

Engine oil degrades over time and becomes thicker, loses important qualities, and impairs engine performance with time. It decreased engine failure as well as efficiency and performance. Also, Engine noise can increase due to a delay in the oil change. Therefore, the presence of elements in the oil, regardless of their type, harms the performance of the engine. In addition, engine oil degrades and loses its lubricating properties. As a result, friction between engine parts increases, which leads to reduced engine efficiency and increased fuel consumption. Moreover, if the oil is not changed on time, these deposits accumulate in various parts of the engine and may clog filters and oil passages, resulting in impaired oil flow in the engine.

Optimization

Numerical RSM and RSM-GRA optimization techniques were used to explore the tractor’s optimal conditions for operation. Indicators and optimization objectives for each of the variables are given in Table 4 according to the durability and oil quality. The results of this optimization are presented in Table 5 for all three lives of the tractor. By using the RSM method, 13 solutions were obtained with desirability from 0.76 to 0.947. The RSM method placed different treatments in the same ranking, but the RSM-GRA method assigned a rank to each treatment. The results of RSM-GRA suggested the following as the optimal condition: operating hours of 93.6 h for infant mortality life, which resulted in a Fe of 0.076, Cu of 0.96, Si of 0.52, B of 0.01, Zn and P of 0.014, Ca of 0.3, Mn of 0.0072, PQ of 0.002, and the rest of the metals are zero. Moreover, operating hours of 53.22 h and 35.5 h are the optimal conditions for oil change intervals of tractors in useful life and wear-out life, respectively. Karthikeyan et al.33 suggested that engine oil change intervals for two oil brands can be extended to 900 operating hours, but because the engine oil is subjected to high load and temperature, and some of its physical properties are lost, this distance must be significantly reduced. Therefore, it is necessary to change the oil after 300 operating hours of the tractor.

Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis was used to determine how tractor age and operating hours parameters affected GRG and to determining the effect of tractor age and operating hours on oil change time. Because of this sensitivity analysis were included for the factors. The results are displayed in Fig. 9.

An estimate of the studied parameters average (tractor age and operating hours) and GRG was made in the sensitivity analysis. The impact of various studied parameters, such as + 10% to + 40% (Fig. 9a) and − 10% to − 40% (Fig. 9b), on GRG ranges was examined. The tractor age has the greatest favorable impact on GRG when the proportion of variance is increased than the other parameter. Both studied parameters which increases the proportion of variance, have a greater negative impact on GRG. Consequently, given the significance of tractor age in raising the GRG, this parameter has a basic role in the oil change intervals of tractor’s diesel engine. As the tractor ages, these intervals become shorter as the tractor engine and its components wear out and wear debris increase in the engine oil. This conclusion also serves as a warning to farmers that, as the engine wears out, it is time to replace the tractor with a new one.

Conclusion

The measurement of oil wear debris is important not only as a criterion for determining the right time for the oil change but also as an indicator to detect the incorrect use of the machine and to track the mechanical erosion of different parts of the machine, which is a great help to the management decisions of tractors and agricultural machines. When tractor age and operating hours increase, all wear debris increases. It showed that the tractor age plays a much more important role than operating hours in determining oil change intervals. Overall, operating hours of 93.6 h are suggested for oil change intervals of tractors in infant mortality life, which resulted in a Fe of 0.076, a Cu of 0.96, a Si of 0.52, a B of 0.01, a Zn and P of 0.014, a Ca of 0.3, a Mn of 0.0072, a PQ of 0.002, and the rest of the metals are zero. Moreover, operating hours of 53.22 h and 35.5 h are the optimal conditions for oil change intervals of tractors in useful life and wear-out life, respectively. The studies are accountable to promote efficiency and accurate detection. Hence, there are some areas in which further studies might be conducted: (i) These intervals should be validate on a larger fleet of tractors of different brands and in diverse operational environments, (ii) Research could integrate real-time oil sensor data with the proposed RSM-GRA framework for continuous condition monitoring, (iii) As comparative research, several MCDM methods can be utilized to study the feasibility of soft computing approaches for monitoring the lubricant health state.

The results of this study, obtained from a single model (ITM 399) in one geographic region, severely limit the generalizability of the findings. A small sample may not represent the full spectrum of operators’ habits, environmental conditions, and geographic regions. Additionally, it presents a challenge to confidently apply the findings to a broader population of tractors and regions. To overcome its limitations, future researches could be plan on different tractor models in various geographic regions to be representative of the larger population.
