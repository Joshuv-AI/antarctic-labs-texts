TUBA4A Pathogenic Variant Manifesting With Adulthood‐Onset Genetic Myasthenic Syndrome, Myopathy, and Infertility
Authors: Margherita Milone, Stefan Nicolau, Zhiyv Niu, Teerin Liewluck, Eric J. Sorenson, Xin‐Ming Shen, C. Michael Harper, William J. Litchy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectives TUBA4A pathogenic variants are associated with ALS, frontotemporal dementia, spastic ataxia, spasticity, ataxia, Parkinson's disease, female infertility, macrothrombocytopenia, and myopathy. Four recently reported patients with TUBA4A neonatal/childhood onset myopathy had also a decrement on repetitive nerve stimulation (RNS), but such a finding was not further characterized. We describe a patient with a TUBA4A pathogenic variant with adulthood‐onset genetic myasthenic syndrome accompanied by myopathy and infertility to highlight the neuromuscular junction defect as the main feature of the patient's phenotype.MethodsWe reviewed the patient's clinical and laboratory findings and performed transcriptomic analysis on the patient's muscle.ResultsA 53‐year‐old woman with infertility of unknown etiology manifested fatigability and proximal upper limb muscle weakness in her mid‐30s, followed by lower limb involvement. Her examination showed proximal muscle weakness and fatigability but spared facial muscles. CK values were mildly elevated. Anti‐AChR, MuSK, P/Q‐type calcium channel, and LRP4 antibodies were absent. 2 Hz RNS showed decrement (−14% to −48%) in limb muscles that improved with 3,4‐dyaminopyridine (3,4‐DAP). Facilitation (231%) occurred in the trapezius. Muscle biopsy showed patchy loss of oxidative enzyme reactivity and no C5b9 or IgG at neuromuscular junctions. Whole genome sequencing identified a heterozygous known TUBA4A pathogenic variant (c.850G>A, p.Glu284Lys). Patient improved with 3,4‐DAP and albuterol.DiscussionTUBA4A p.Glu284Lys can lead to treatable myasthenic syndrome with postsynaptic and likely presynaptic involvement, as suggested by the patient's electrophysiological findings and response to therapy. This patient expands the TUBA4A‐disorder spectrum to include overlapping myasthenic syndrome‐myopathy and shows that neuromuscular disease and infertility can occur within the same patient.

Introduction

TUBA4A encodes the alpha‐tubulin 4A isoform which forms heterodimers with the beta tubulin to compose microtubules [1]. Microtubules have a spectrum of cellular functions, including a role in RNA, protein and organelle trafficking [2]. They are relevant for development of axons and neuronal synapses as well as muscle mechanical stability. Additionally, a specialized microtubules network is present at the neuromuscular junctions (NMJs) where it might restrict mobility of RNAs produced in postsynaptic nuclei [2, 3]. Focal insertion of acetylcholine receptor (AChR) in the postsynaptic membrane is regulated by stable microtubules [2, 3].

TUBA4A pathogenic variants were initially identified in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) where they have a dominant‐negative effect [4]. Later TUBA4A pathogenic variants were detected in frontotemporal dementia, Parkinson's disease, ataxia, spasticity, spastic ataxia, macrothrombocytopenia, female infertility, and myopathy [1]. Following the first report of TUBA4A‐myopathy in two pediatric patients [5], a recent paper described a cohort of 31 patients with myopathy [6]. Four of these patients with neonatal/childhood‐onset had a decrement on repetitive nerve stimulation (RNS). Such electrophysiological findings were not underscored as part of the phenotype, and these patients were diagnosed with “myo‐tubulinopathy” [6].

We describe a patient with adulthood‐onset congenital/genetic myasthenic syndrome, co‐existing myopathy and infertility, carrying a heterozygous TUBA4A pathogenic variant. We demonstrate that the patient has electrophysiologic evidence of postsynaptic and likely presynaptic defects of neuromuscular transmission responsive to pharmacological treatment.

Patient and Methods

Patient's clinical and laboratory data were reviewed. Genetic testing included a next generation sequencing (NGS) panel targeting 100 genes causative of myopathies and congenital/genetic myasthenic syndromes, deletion/duplication analysis via array comparative genomic hybridization (aCGH) of AGRN, ALG2, BIN1, CHAT, CHRNE, COLQ, DES, DNM2, DOK7, DPAGT1, GFPT1, GMPPB, LAMB2, MTM1, MUSK, PLEC, RAPSN, RYR1, SCN4A, and SLC5A7, and whole genome sequencing. RNA sequencing was performed on RNA extracted from muscle specimens from the patient and 3 disease‐controls with myopathy and no electrophysiological defect of neuromuscular transmission. To minimize spurious results in the absence of replicates, analysis focused on genes involved in neuromuscular transmission, including the human orthologs of a set of 472 transcripts found to be enriched in NMJ myonuclei in mice (Supplemental Methods).

Results

A 53‐year‐old woman manifested muscle fatigability and proximal upper limb weakness in her mid‐30s, followed a decade later by neck and proximal lower limb weakness. She described exacerbation of the weakness prior to her menses. Currently, she ambulates independently for about 100 m but can walk longer distances with a walker. She remains free from visual and bulbar symptoms. A trial of treatment with IVIG for presumed seronegative myasthenia gravis was not beneficial. The patient has beneficial effects from 3,4‐dyaminopiridine (3,4‐DAP) and albuterol in isolation and now in combination, resulting in improved strength and ability to walk for longer distances. The 3,4‐DAP was later replaced with amifampridine. She had no benefit from pyridostigmine. She had normal motor development and swam competitively until symptom onset. She had infertility of unknown etiology. Oocyte in vitro fertilization was reportedly successful, but the fertilized zygotes failed to progress. Her family history was unrevealing. Neurological examination in her late 30s showed weakness of trapezius, external rotators, and biceps brachii (MRC 4/5), deltoid, wrist and finger extensors (MRC 4+/5) as well as deltoid fatigability. Follow‐up examination at age 50 demonstrated persistent muscle fatigability, trace weakness of the frontalis, new lower limb weakness predominantly affecting quadriceps (MRC 4−/5) and weakness of neck flexors (MRC 4/5). The rest of the examination was normal. Despite the patient's complaint of intermittent difficulty maintaining the erect posture, no camptocormia was observed on examination and abdominal wall muscle strength was normal, as suggested by the patient's ability to pass from supine to sitting without help and lack of Beevor sign.

CK values were 1.8–2.9‐folds upper limit of normal. AChR binding, muscle specific kinase (MuSK), low‐density lipoprotein receptor‐related protein 4 (LRP4), and P/Q type calcium channel antibodies were negative.

EMG studies showed rapid recruitment of low amplitude, short duration motor unit potentials (MUPs) especially in proximal muscles where a few fibrillation potentials were recorded. Unstable MUPs were present in all muscles examined. 2 Hz RNS showed a baseline decrement in multiple limb muscles: trapezius (spinal accessory nerve) −37%, biceps brachii (musculocutaneus nerve) −24%; rectus femoris (femoral nerve) −23%; tibialis anterior (fibular nerve) −37%. There was no significant facilitation (< 200%). Decrement was only −6% in the abductor digiti minimi and absent in the nasalis. Improvement of decrement occurred after administration of 3,4‐DAP (Figure 1A–H). A 231% postexercise facilitation was recorded in the trapezius, years later, after the patient had been taking albuterol for a couple of years (Figure 1I–K).

Deltoid biopsy showed nonspecific myopathic changes (Figure 2). A few NMJs were present in the biopsy; most of them showed normal reactivity for AChR and acetylcholinesterase, as assessed by fluorescence, but some of them were small and occasionally extended around the muscle fiber (Figure 2G). No C5b9 or IgG deposits were present at any NMJ.

Hemidiaphragms ultrasound showed a thin left hemidiaphragm at functional residual capacity (0.12 cm; normal thickness > 0.15 cm), with normal thickening ratio. Electrocardiogram and echocardiogram were normal. Maximal respiratory pressures were 30%–60% of predicted; overnight oximetry was normal.

Whole genome sequencing identified a heterozygous known TUBA4A pathogenic variant (c.850G>A, p.Glu284Lys; NM_006000.3). The TUBA4A variant, absent in control populations, is predicted to be deleterious by multiple computational algorithms (REVEL score: 0.824) (PP3_Moderate). This variant was previously reported as pathogenic and causative of female infertility due to zygotic arrest, as supported by the conserved status of the affected amino acid across mammalian species, Alphafold2 structure prediction, and experimental studies in mouse oocytes [6]. Later, this same variant was reported in patients with myopathy inherited with autosomal dominant modality, further supporting its pathogenicity [7].

In the patient muscle sample, the TUBA4A variant was present in 46% of transcripts. Among 22 genes implicated in neuromuscular transmission, only VAMP1 expression fell more than 2‐fold outside the range seen in controls (Figure S1). Alterations in cytoskeletal gene expression in the patient sample were comparable to disease controls (Figure S2).

Discussion

We report a patient with a TUBA4A heterozygous pathogenic variant (c.850G>A, p.Glu284Lys) whose muscle fatigability, due to the severe failure of neuromuscular transmission, is the dominant phenotypic feature. The improvement with 3,4‐DAP and albuterol supports the defect of neuromuscular transmission as the main culprit of the patient's neuromuscular symptoms. The premenstrual exacerbation of symptoms is in keeping with what is observed in other congenital/genetic myasthenic syndromes [8]. The lack of AChR, MuSK, LRP4, and P/Q type calcium channel antibodies and absence of complement and IgG at NMJs point away from seronegative autoimmune myasthenia. The mild CK elevation and myopathological abnormalities indicate associated myopathy. Therefore, TUBA4A expands the spectrum of overlapping genetic/congenital myasthenic syndrome‐myopathy [9].

The degree and pattern of decrement indicate a postsynaptic defect of neuromuscular transmission. The 231% postexercise facilitation, although noted only while the patient was on albuterol, suggests a likely co‐existing presynaptic component [10]. Thus, TUBA4A broadens also the spectrum of genetic myasthenic syndromes with combined electrophysiological features of post‐synaptic and presynaptic failure of neuromuscular transmission [11]. However, without in vitro studies to assess parameters of quantal release, true presynaptic involvement cannot be established with confidence. The increased amplitude of the compound muscle action potential and reduced decrement, following 3,4‐DAP, could further support the presynaptic involvement but can also occur in postsynaptic defects [12]. The reduced synaptobrevin (VAMP1) expression, crucial for neurotransmitter release, may be involved in disease pathophysiology contributing to presynaptic dysfunction [11]. However, such a hypothesis, based on an observation at the RNA level in a single patient, would require validation in additional patient samples and at the protein level. The compromised neuromuscular transmission in the setting of defective TUBA4A is not surprising as TUBA4A has been shown to be involved in development of NMJs in drosophila [13]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that the stability of subjunctional microtubules is needed for proper AChR insertion in the postsynaptic membrane [2, 3, 14].

None of the previously described patients carrying the same TUBA4A variant present in our patient was reported to have a defect of neuromuscular transmission [6]. In these individuals, RNS were not listed among the performed tests, and therefore the myasthenic component of the disease may have gone undetected. Our patient highlights also the importance of performing RNS in subjects with poorly characterized genetic myopathies, especially in adults with limb‐girdle weakness, as most adulthood‐onset congenital/genetic myasthenic syndromes have no ocular findings [15]. Our patient improved with both 3,4‐DAP and albuterol but not with pyridostigmine. Among the four patients with TUBA4‐myotubulinopathy and decrement reported by Johari et al., pyridostigmine resulted in an initial possible effect in one (c.722C>T, p.Ser241Phe, homozygous), mild but transient improvement in another (c.1061G>A, p.Gly354Asp, homozygous), while a short treatment trial with salbutamol was not effective in two (c.679C>T, p.Leu227Phe, heterozygous, and c.1049G>T, p.Gly350Val, heterozygous) [6].

Patient's muscle pathological findings are in alignment with what was previously reported in TUBA4A myopathy [6]. Spheroid bodies and dystrophic changes (described in two patients carrying the same TUBA4A variant present in our patient), nemaline rods and increased glycogen content (observed in one of the three previously reported patients with TUBA4A variants and decrement) were absent in our patient's muscle biopsy.

TUBA4 c.850G>A, p.Glu284Lys was previously reported in subjects with autosomal dominant or indeterminate inheritance myopathy [6]. Considering patients' parents and siblings are asymptomatic (DNA not available), we speculate that the TUBA4A variant occurred de novo in our patient.

TUBA4 c.850G>A, p.Glu284Lys was initially identified in females with infertility due to zygotic arrest [7]. TUBA4A is highly expressed in spindle oocytes, crucial for embryo formation [7]. While infertility was not described in any of the previously reported patients with TUBA4A myopathy, including those carrying p.Glu284Lys, and neuromuscular disease has not been reported in patients with TUBA4A infertility, our patient experienced infertility for which no etiology had been previously identified. This suggests that neuromuscular involvement and infertility can occur in the same individual with a TUBA4A pathogenic variant.

The reason for which TUBA4A pathogenic variants or even the same TUBA4 pathogenic variant can lead to different phenotypes remains elusive, as for other genetic disorders associated with a spectrum of clinical presentations. Genetic modifiers, variable penetrance, variant‐specific effects, potential tissue‐specific impact, and other factors could contribute to the resulting phenotype. Certainly, TUBA4A plays crucial roles in cellular processes, undergoes posttranslational modifications, and interacts with a variety of proteins. Therefore, it is currently difficult to pinpoint the specific mechanisms leading to any specific clinical manifestation. Additionally, too few patients with TUBA4A‐disorder have been reported so far to confidently correlate genotype and phenotype.

In conclusion, our patient's findings would add TUBA4A to the genes linked to congenital/genetic myasthenic syndrome and suggest a mandatory search for neuromuscular transmission failure in all subjects with TUBA4A pathogenic variants manifesting with a myopathy phenotype or unexplained fatigue, as findings have therapeutic implications. However, additional data from large patient cohorts are necessary to better understand the full spectrum of TUBA4A disorder and the underlying mechanisms leading to specific tissue involvement and different clinical manifestations in different patients.
