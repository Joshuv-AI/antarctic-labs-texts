Computation of structured stability radii for Dissipative-Hamiltonian systems
Peter Benner, Volker Mehrmann, Anshul Prajapati, Punit Sharma

Abstract
We study linear time-invariant Dissipative Hamiltonian (DH) systems arising in energy-based modeling of dynamical systems. An advantage of DH systems is that they are always stable due to the structure of their coefficient matrices, and, under further weak conditions, even asymptotically stable. In this paper, we discuss the computation of the stability radii for a given asymptotically stable DH system; i.e., the smallest structured perturbation that puts a DH system on the boundary of the region of asymptotic stability, so that it has purely imaginary eigenvalues. We obtain explicit computable formulas for various structured stability radii. For this, the problem of computing stability radii is reformulated in terms of minimizing the Rayleigh quotient of a Hermitian matrix or the sum of two generalized Rayleigh quotients of Hermitian semidefinite matrices. This reformulation results in the problem of minimizing the largest eigenvalue of an eigenvector-dependent Hermitian matrix or minimizing the smallest eigenvalue of a Hermitian matrix which depends on the eigenvector. It is also demonstrated (via numerical experiments) that, under structure-preserving perturbations, the asymptotic stability of a DH system is much more robust than under general perturbations, since the distance to instability is typically much larger when structure-preserving perturbations are considered. Finally, similar results are obtained for optimally robust representations of stable systems.

Introduction

In this paper, we consider the (asymptotic) stability analysis and in particular the computation of stability radii for the class of
linear time-invariant port-Hamiltonian (pH) systems
of the form

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle=(J-R)Qx+(G-P)u,

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=(G+P)^{*}Qx+(S+N)u,

where
x,u
, and
y
are the state, input, and output of the system. The function
x\mapsto x^{*}Qx
, with
Q^{*}=Q

positive definite, describes the
stored energy
of the system and is called the
Hamiltonian
of the system. The coefficient matrices
J,R,Q\in\mathbb{C}^{n,n},G,P\in\mathbb{C}^{n,m},S,N\in\mathbb{C}^{m,m}
, where
\mathbb{C}^{n,n}
is the set of
n\times n
complex matrices, satisfy

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}J&G\\
-G^{*}&N\end{array}\right]^{*}=-\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}J&G\\
-G^{*}&N\end{array}\right],\qquad W=W^{*}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}R&P\\
P^{*}&S\end{array}\right]\geq 0,

where
W\geq 0
(
W>0
) denotes that the matrix
W
is positive semidefinite (definite).

PH systems possess numerous significant geometric and algebraic properties that are nicely encoded in their representation; see
[
13
,
22
,
36
,
34
]
. In this paper, we focus on the property that PH systems are stable, i.e., all eigenvalues of the system matrix
A=(J-R)Q
are in the closed left half of the complex plane, and all eigenvalues located on the imaginary axis are semisimple, see
[
30
,
32
]
. For the stability analysis, the port matrices can be ignored, resulting in a
Dissipative Hamiltonian (DH)
system represented by

\dot{x}=(J-R)Qx,

(1)

where
J
is skew-Hermitian,
R\geq 0
,
Q>0
.

If the product in (
1
) is multiplied out to create a matrix
A=(J-R)Q
and the DH structure is ignored, then the stability of the system is no longer evident from the structure of the coefficients. In this case, to see if the system is stable, one can either compute the eigenvalues or use the Lyapunov theorem
[
23
]
. If
A
has purely imaginary eigenvalues, then arbitrarily small perturbations (such as data or roundoff errors) can cause eigenvalues to move to the right half complex plane. This is particularly the case for linear systems that emerge from the linearization of nonlinear systems near stationary reference solutions
[
12
]
, from data-driven realizations (see, e.g.,
[
6
,
29
]
), or from classical finite element modeling
[
16
]
. Since in practice, the system model experiences perturbations, stability can only be assured when the system maintains a sufficient distance from instability; see
[
19
,
21
]
. Computing the distance to instability
[
11
,
14
,
17
,
39
]
is an optimization problem and usually again subject to perturbations.

The situation is different for DH systems which are automatically stable under perturbations, as long as the DH structure is preserved. However, DH systems are not necessarily asymptotically stable, i.e., they may have purely imaginary eigenvalues. Therefore, it is important to know whether a DH system is simply stable or even asymptotically stable. Knowing whether the system is robustly asymptotically stable is even more important, meaning that small (structured) perturbations maintain its asymptotic stability. The latter again requires that the system has a reasonable distance from a DH system with purely imaginary eigenvalues. To study this question is an important topic in many applications, in particular, in power systems and circuit simulation (see, for example,
[
28
,
26
,
27
,
37
]
) and multibody systems (see, for example,
[
16
,
38
,
40
]
).

Example 1.1
.

Large-scale second-order differential equations of the form

1

It has been observed already a long time ago that the perturbation bounds under structured perturbation differ significantly from those under unstructured perturbation; see e.g. the landmark paper
[
18
]
. Our work is following this direction of research.

In recent years, there has been an increasing interest in the investigation of the stability radii of dissipative and port-Hamiltonian systems under structure preserving perturbations; see
[

,

,

,

,

. In

In this paper, we revisit the stability radii of DH systems under various structure-preserving perturbations to both

In Section

) is actually a minimum and is attained for some
x=\hat{x}
. For a given
\omega
, set

\hat{\Delta}_{R}=\begin{cases}-\frac{(RQ\hat{x})(RQ\hat{x})^{*}}{\hat{x}^{*}QRQ\hat{x}}&\text{ if }RQ\hat{x}\neq 0,\\
\qquad 0&\text{ if }RQ\hat{x}=0,\end{cases}\quad\quad\hat{\Delta}_{J}=\hat{H}_{(Q\hat{x},(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)\hat{x})},

(22)

where
\hat{H}
is defined in (
14
). By
[
30
, Lemma 4.1]
, it then follows that for the optimal perturbation
\hat{\Delta}_{R}
we have that
R+\hat{\Delta}_{R}\geq 0
so that equality holds in (
21
), as then
(\hat{\Delta}_{J},\hat{\Delta}_{R})\in S_{d}(J,R)
.
∎

Note that the objective function in (
15
) depends on two generalized Rayleigh quotients, i.e., on four Hermitian positive semidefinite matrices. However, with the change of coordinates
y:=Qx
, the objective function can be represented in the variable
y
, which gives

\phi(y)=\left(\frac{y^{*}R^{2}y}{y^{*}Ry}\right)^{2}+\frac{y^{*}Q^{-1}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)^{*}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)Q^{-1}y}{y^{*}y}.

One advantage of writing the objective function in this form is that we can further reformulate this optimization into the minimization of a rational function over the joint numerical range of three Hermitian matrices; we discuss this in more detail in Section
4
. This reformulation is actually the formulation that is used in DH differential-algebraic equations, see
[
34
]
for a detailed survey.

We directly obtain the following bounds for
r^{S_{d}}(J,R)
:

r(J,R)\leq r^{S}(J,R)\leq r^{S_{i}}(J,R)\leq r^{S_{d}}(J,R),

which automatically gives lower bounds to
r^{S_{d}}(J,R)
. Further detailed bounds for
r^{S_{i}}(J,R)
and
r^{S}(J,R)
are discussed in Sections
3.2
and
3.3
, respectively.

Another lower bound for
r^{S_{d}}(J,R)
, which is a direct consequence of Theorem
3.1
is presented in the following corollary, which also gives an upper bound for
r^{S_{d}}(J,R)
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Consider an asymptotically stable DH system of the form (
1
). Let
P^{*}RP=D
be the spectral decomposition of
R
, where
P\in\mathbb{C}^{n,n}
is unitary and
D=\text{diag}(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{n})
is such that
d_{1}\geq d_{2}\geq\ldots\geq d_{n}\geq 0
. Then

d_{n}^{2}+\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda_{\min}(Q^{-1}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)^{*}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)Q^{-1})\leq r^{S_{d}}(J,R)^{2}\leq d_{n}^{2}+\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{\|(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)\hat{x}\|^{2}}{\|Q\hat{x}\|^{2}},

(23)

where
\hat{x}=Q^{-1}Pe_{n}
, with
e_{n}
being the nth column of the
n\times n
identity matrix.

Proof.

For the lower bound, by Theorem
3.1
and the change of coordinates
y=Qx
, we have

\displaystyle r^{S_{d}}(J,R)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\left\{\left(\frac{x^{*}QR^{2}Qx}{x^{*}QRQx}\right)^{2}+\frac{x^{*}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)^{*}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)x}{x^{*}Q^{2}x}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\left(\frac{x^{*}QR^{2}Qx}{x^{*}QRQx}\right)^{2}+\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{x^{*}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)^{*}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)x}{x^{*}Q^{2}x}

\displaystyle=\inf_{y\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\left(\frac{y^{*}R^{2}y}{y^{*}Ry}\right)^{2}+\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\inf_{y\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{y^{*}Q^{-1}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)^{*}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)Q^{-1}y}{y^{*}y},

(24)

\displaystyle=d_{n}^{2}+\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda_{\text{min}}(Q^{-1}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)^{*}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)Q^{-1}).

(25)

Then (
25
) holds as the infimum in the first part of (
24
) has the value
d_{n}^{2}
, where
d_{n}
is the smallest eigenvalue of
R
. Indeed, if
R>0
then the infimum is given by the minimal eigenvalue of the pair
(R^{2},R)
, i.e.
\lambda_{\text{min}}(R^{2},R)=\lambda_{\text{min}}(R)=d_{n}
. However, if
R\geq 0
is singular, then
\frac{y^{*}R^{2}y}{y^{*}Ry}\geq 0=d_{n}^{2}
, and the infimum is attained by the eigenvector
\hat{y}
corresponding to the smallest eigenvalue of
R
by letting the fraction
\frac{0}{0}
have the value 0.

From the proof of Theorem
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle r^{S_{d}}(J,R)^{2}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\Big(\inf_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}}\inf\{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}:(\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R})\in S_{d}(J,R),\penalty\ (R+\Delta_{R})Qx=0\text{ for some $x$ satisfying }

\displaystyle\hskip 256.0748pt(J+\Delta_{J})Qx=i\omega x\}\Big).

An upper bound can be obtained by first solving for
x
and
\Delta_{R}
such that
\|\Delta_{R}\|
is minimized under the constraint
\Delta_{R}\leq 0,R+\Delta_{R}\geq 0
and
(R+\Delta_{R})Qx=0
, and then using the optimal solution
\hat{x}
, to solve for
\Delta_{J}
such that
\|\Delta_{J}\|
is minimized under the constraint
(J+\Delta_{J})Q\hat{x}=i\omega\hat{x}
for a given scalar
\omega
.

It is easy to check that the optimal solution to
\inf\{\|\Delta_{R}\|:\Delta_{R}\leq 0,R+\Delta_{R}\geq 0\text{ and }(R+\Delta_{R})Qx=0\}
is
d_{n}^{2}
and is attained for
\hat{\Delta}_{R}=-d_{n}\hat{x}\hat{x}^{*}
, where
\hat{x}=Q^{-1}Pe_{n}
. Note that
R+\hat{\Delta}_{R}\geq 0
follows from
[
30
, Lemma 4.1]
. For this
\hat{x}
we determine
\Delta_{J}
such that
\|\Delta_{J}\|
is minimized under the constraint
(J+\Delta_{J})Q\hat{x}=i\omega\hat{x}
. Using Lemma
2.3
, there exists a skew-Hermitian matrix
\Delta_{J}
satisfying
\Delta_{J}Q\hat{x}=(i\lambda I_{n}-JQ)\hat{x}
if and only if
\Re(x^{*}Q(i\lambda I_{n}-JQ)x)=0
and the minimal norm is given by

\|\Delta_{J}\|^{2}=\frac{\|(i\lambda I_{n}-JQ)\hat{x}\|^{2}}{\|Q\hat{x}\|^{2}}.

(26)

Note that the existence condition for
\Delta_{J}
, i.e.,
\Re(x^{*}Q(i\lambda I_{n}-JQ)x)=0
, always holds; hence, using (
26
) we get

r^{S_{d}}(J,R)^{2}\leq d_{n}^{2}+\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{\|(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)\hat{x}\|^{2}}{\|Q\hat{x}\|^{2}}

(27)

which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Note that taking any particular vector

in (

) will give an upper bound to

. The upper bound given in Corollary

is one such bound with the vector

3.2
The structured stability radius
r^{S_{i}}(J,R)

In this subsection, we discuss the stability radius
r^{S_{i}}(J,R)
defined in (
9
) under perturbations to
J
and
R
from the set
S_{i}(J,R)
with Hermitian but possibly indefinite perturbations in
R
of the form
R+\Delta_{R}\geq 0
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Consider an asymptotically stable DH system of the form (
1
). For
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
, define
G(\omega):=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}RQ\\
(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)\end{array}\right]
.

1.

If
R>0
, then

r^{S_{i}}(J,R)=\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\sigma_{\min}\left(G(\omega)Q^{-1}\right).

(28)

2.

If
R\geq 0
is singular, then

r^{S_{i}}(J,R)\geq\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\sigma_{\min}\left(G(\omega)Q^{-1}\right),

(29)

where
\sigma_{\min}(A)
denotes the smallest singular value of the matrix
A
.

Proof.

By Definition
2.1
, we have

r^{S_{i}}(J,R)=\inf\left\{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\penalty\ :\penalty\ (\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R})\in S_{i}(J,R),\penalty\ \Lambda((J+\Delta_{J}-(R+\Delta_{R}))Q)\cap i\mathbb{R}\neq\emptyset\right\}.

(30)

Note that for
(\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R})\in S_{i}(J,R)
, the perturbed system
(J+\Delta_{J}-(R+\Delta_{R}))Q
remains dissipative Hamiltonian; and hence, using the spectral properties of DH systems
[
30
,
32
]
, we obtain

\displaystyle r^{S_{i}}(J,R)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}:(\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R})\in S_{i}(J,R),\penalty\ (R+\Delta_{R})Qx=0\text{ for some eigenvector $x$ of }(J+\Delta_{J})Q\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf\{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}:(\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R})\in S_{i}(J,R),\penalty\ \Delta_{R}Qx=-RQx\text{ for some $x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}$ satisfying }

\displaystyle\hskip 227.62204pt(J+\Delta_{J})Qx=i\omega x,\omega\in\mathbb{R}\}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\Big(\inf\Big\{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}:(\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R})\in S_{i}(J,R),\penalty\ \Delta_{R}Qx=-RQx,(J+\Delta_{J})Qx=i\omega x\Big\}\Big)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\vartheta^{S_{i}}_{\omega},

where, as in Theorem
3.1
, in the second-to-last equation the optimization problem is divided into sublevel optimization in the variables
\omega
,
x
and
(\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R})
, and for a given
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
,
\vartheta^{S_{i}}_{\omega}
is defined as

\displaystyle\vartheta^{S_{i}}_{\omega}

\displaystyle:=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\Big(\inf\Big\{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}:(\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R})\in S_{i}(J,R),\penalty\ \Delta_{R}Qx=-RQx,

\displaystyle\hskip 227.62204pt\Delta_{J}Qx=(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)x\Big\}\Big).

(31)

Considering the inner optimization in (
3.2
), for given
x
and
\omega
, using Lemma
2.3
, there exists
\Delta_{R}
and
\Delta_{J}
Hermitian and skew-Hermitian, respectively, such that
\Delta_{R}Qx=-RQx
and
\Delta_{J}Qx=(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)x
if and only if
\Im(x^{*}QRQx)=0
and
\Re(x^{*}Q(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)x)=0
, which holds because of the structure of the matrices
J,R
and
Q
, and the minimal norms of such
\Delta_{R}
and
\Delta_{J}
are given by

\|\Delta_{R}\|=\frac{\|RQx\|}{\|Qx\|},\quad\|\Delta_{J}\|=\frac{\|(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)x\|}{\|Qx\|}.

This implies that

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{*}QR^{2}Qx}{x^{*}Q^{2}x}+\frac{x^{*}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)^{*}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)x}{x^{*}Q^{2}x}

(32)

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{*}((RQ)^{*}RQ+(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)^{*}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ))x}{x^{*}Q^{2}x}.

Using (
32
) and
S_{i}(J,R)\subseteq\{(\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R}):\Delta_{J}^{*}=-\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R}^{*}=\Delta_{R}\}
in (
3.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle(\vartheta^{S_{i}}_{\omega})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\left\{\frac{x^{*}((RQ)^{*}RQ+(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)^{*}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ))x}{x^{*}Q^{2}x}\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf_{y\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\left\{\frac{y^{*}Q^{-1}G(\omega)^{*}G(\omega)Q^{-1}y}{y^{*}y}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\text{min}}\left(G(\omega)Q^{-1}\right),

(33)

where
y:=Qx
and
G(\omega):=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}RQ\\
(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)\end{array}\right]
. This proves (
29
) and the inequality in (
28
). If, for
R>0
, we can show the equality in (
3.2
), then the proof is complete. Let
R>0
and let
\hat{y}
be a right singular vector corresponding to the smallest singular value of
G(\omega)Q^{-1}
, and set
\hat{x}=Q^{-1}\hat{y}
.

Let
(\hat{\Delta}_{J},\hat{\Delta}_{R})
be such that
\hat{\Delta}_{J}^{*}=-\hat{\Delta}_{J}
,
\hat{\Delta}_{R}^{*}=\hat{\Delta}_{R}
, satisfying

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}_{J}Q\hat{x}

\displaystyle=(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)\hat{x},\quad

\displaystyle\|\hat{\Delta}_{J}\|=\frac{\|(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)\hat{x}\|}{\|Q\hat{x}\|},

(34)

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}_{R}Q\hat{x}

\displaystyle=-RQ\hat{x},\quad

\displaystyle\|\hat{\Delta}_{R}\|=\frac{\|RQ\hat{x}\|}{\|Q\hat{x}\|}.

(35)

It is now enough to show that
R+\hat{\Delta}_{R}\geq 0
, as this will imply
(\hat{\Delta}_{J},\hat{\Delta}_{R})\in S_{i}(J,R)
. Note that the optimal matrix
\hat{H}
attaining the minimal norm in Lemma
2.3
has at most one negative eigenvalue, since it is either a rank one or rank two matrix, and if it has rank two, then it is easy to check that
y\pm\frac{\|y\|}{\|x\|}x
are eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalues
\pm\frac{\|y\|}{\|x\|}
, respectively. This implies that
\hat{\Delta}_{R}
has at most one negative eigenvalue. By using (
35
), we obtain

(R+\hat{\Delta}_{R})Q\hat{x}=RQ\hat{x}+\hat{\Delta}_{R}Q\hat{x}=RQ\hat{x}-RQ\hat{x}=0.

Thus,
\hat{\Delta}_{R}
is a matrix with at most one negative eigenvalue and also satisfies that
R+\Delta_{R}
is singular, which implies, in view of the spectral properties of DH systems, see
[
30
,
32
]
, that
R+\Delta_{R}\geq 0
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

As we have already noted earlier, the structured stability radius
r^{S_{i}}(J,R)
gives the lower bound

r^{S_{i}}(J,R)\leq r^{S_{d}}(J,R).

(36)

A natural question that arises is how good this lower bound is as compared to the one obtained in Corollary
3.2
. Using the variational inequality for eigenvalues of Hermitian matrices, see
[
15
]
, we see that

\displaystyle r^{S_{i}}(J,R)

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda_{\min}(Q^{-1}G^{*}GQ^{-1})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda_{\min}(R^{2}+Q^{-1}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)^{*}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)Q^{-1})

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{\min}(R)^{2}+\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda_{\min}(Q^{-1}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)^{*}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)Q^{-1}).

This shows that the bound for
r^{S_{d}}(J,R)
in Corollary
3.2
is weaker than the bound
r^{S_{i}}(J,R)\leq r^{S_{d}}(J,R)
.

3.3
The structured stability radius
r^{S}(J,R)

In this subsection, we discuss the stability radius
r^{S}(J,R)
defined in (
12
), while considering perturbations from the set
S
in (
10
), allowing
R+\Delta_{R}
to become indefinite. Note that in this case, the DH structure may be destroyed. Thus, to obtain a characterization of

, we employ the eigenvalue backward error
\eta^{S}(J,R,i\omega)
defined in (

r^{S}(J,R)=\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\eta^{S}(J,R,i\omega),

(37)

where we first derive a computable formula for
\eta^{S}(J,R,i\omega)

Theorem 3.6
.

Consider a DH system of the form (
1
), and let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
be such that
M:=((J-R)Q-\lambda I_{n})^{-1}

H^{(\lambda)}:=[I_{n}\penalty\ -I_{n}]^{*}M^{*}Q^{2}M[I_{n}\penalty\ -I_{n}],

(38)

H_{0}^{(\lambda)}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}QM+M^{*}Q&-QM\\
-M^{*}Q&0\end{array}\right],\quad H_{1}^{(\lambda)}:=i\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-M^{*}Q\\
QM&-QM+M^{*}Q\end{array}\right].

(39)

\eta^{{S}}(J,R,\lambda)=\left(\min_{t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda_{\max}(H^{(\lambda)}+t_{0}H_{0}^{(\lambda)}+t_{1}H_{1}^{(\lambda)})\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Proof.

The dependence on
\lambda

for

H_{0}^{(\lambda)}
and
H_{1}
for
H_{1}^{(\lambda)}

2.1

\displaystyle\eta^{S}(J,R,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\inf\{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}:(\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R})\in S(J,R),\lambda\in\Lambda((J+\Delta_{J}-(R+\Delta_{R}))Q)\}

\displaystyle=\inf\{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}:(\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R})\in S(J,R),\text{det}((J+\Delta_{J}-(R+\Delta_{R}))Q-\lambda I_{n})=0\}.

Next we replace the determinant condition

((J+\Delta_{J}-(R+\Delta_{R}))Q-\lambda I_{n})x=0

\Delta_{J}QM(v_{J}-v_{R})=v_{J}

\Delta_{R}QM(v_{J}-v_{R})=v_{R}

\displaystyle\eta^{S}(J,R,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\inf_{v_{J},v_{R}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}}\inf\{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}:(\Delta_{J},\Delta_{R})\in S(J,R),\penalty\ v_{J}-v_{R}\neq 0,

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\Delta_{J}QM(v_{J}-v_{R})=v_{J},\penalty\ \Delta_{R}QM(v_{J}-v_{R})=v_{R}\}.

(42)

Applying Lemma
2.3

3.3

\displaystyle(\eta^{S}(J,R,\lambda))^{2}

\displaystyle=\inf_{v_{J},v_{R}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}}\bigg\{\frac{\|v_{J}\|^{2}}{\|QM(v_{J}-v_{R})\|^{2}}+\frac{\|v_{R}\|^{2}}{\|QM(v_{J}-v_{R})\|^{2}}:v_{J}-v_{R}\neq 0,

\displaystyle\hskip 92.47145pt\Re{(v_{J}^{*}QM(v_{J}-v_{R}))}=0,\penalty\ \Im{(v_{R}^{*}QM(v_{J}-v_{R}))}=0\bigg\}.

(43)

v_{J}^{*}QM(v_{J}-v_{R})+(v_{J}^{*}QM(v_{J}-v_{R}))^{*}=0

\displaystyle(\eta^{S}(J,R,\lambda))^{2}

, and therefore the condition
v^{*}Hv\neq 0
is superfluous. Next, we will apply
[
10
, Theorem 2.6 ]
in (
3.3
). For this we have to check that
t_{0}H_{0}+t_{1}H_{1}
is indefinite for every
(t_{0},t_{1})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
. Suppose, on the contrary that there exists
(\hat{t}_{0},\hat{t}_{1})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
\hat{t}_{0}H_{0}+\hat{t}_{1}H_{1}
is semidefinite. Then, with
N:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{n}&I_{n}\\
0&I_{n}\end{array}\right]
, we obtain

N^{*}(\hat{t}_{0}H_{0}+\hat{t}_{1}H_{1})N=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\hat{t}_{0}QM+t_{0}(QM)^{*}&((\hat{t}_{0}+i\hat{t}_{1})QM)^{*}\\
(\hat{t}_{0}+i\hat{t}_{1})QM&0\end{array}\right].

(46)

Since
\hat{t}_{0}H_{0}+\hat{t}_{1}H_{1}
is semidefinite, this implies that
(\hat{t}_{0}+i\hat{t}_{1})QM=0
, which further implies that
\hat{t}_{0}+i\hat{t}_{1}=0
, since
M
and
Q
are invertible. This gives
\hat{t}_{0}=0
and
\hat{t}_{1}=0
, and proves that
t_{0}H_{0}+t_{1}H_{1}
is indefinite for every
(t_{0},t_{1})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
and hence the result follows using
[
10
, Theorem 2.6]
.
∎

We then have the following corollary.

Corollary 3.7
.

Consider an asymptotically stable DH system of the form (
1
). Then

r^{S}(J,R)=\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\left(\min_{t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda_{\max}(H^{(i\omega)}+t_{0}H_{0}^{(i\omega)}+t_{1}H_{1}^{(i\omega)})\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(47)

where
H^{(i\omega)},H_{0}^{(i\omega)},\text{ and }H_{1}^{(i\omega)}
are defined in (
38
) and (
39
).

Remark 3.8
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n,n}
have all its eigenvalues in the open left half complex plane. Then for a positive definite solution
X=X^{H}>0
of the strict Lyapunov inequality

-A^{H}X-XA>0,

(48)

let
T
be the positive definite square root
X^{\frac{1}{2}}
or the Cholesky factor of
X
. Multiplying
\dot{x}=Ax
from the left by
T
and performing a basis change
y=Tx
, we obtain a DH system
\dot{y}=(J-R)y
with
Q=I
,
where the right-hand side has a positive Hermitian part
R>0
.

Since the solution of (
48
) is not unique, one may ask the question whether there is a solution
X
that leads, in some sense, to an ’optimal’ DH representation. It has been suggested in
[
2
]
to solve the Lyapunov-like equation

-A^{H}X-XA=-2\mu X,

(49)

where
\mu<0
is the spectral abscissa, i.e. the maximal real part of an eigenvalue of
A
. This choice of representation then leads to the smallest (maximal) eigenvalue of
R
equal to
\mu
, which means that the field of values of
J-R
lies completely to the left of the line
\mu+i\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore, it then follows that the decay of the spectral norm of the fundamental solution matrix
\|e^{(J-R)t}\|
is bounded by
1+\mu t+{\mathcal{O}}(t^{2})
, see also
[
3
,
4
]
.

4
Minimization of functions involving generalized Rayleigh quotients

In this section we consider the inner optimization problem in
r^{S_{d}}(J,R)
in Theorem
3.1
, i.e., the minimization problem involving the generalized Rayleigh quotient of the form

\min_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\|x\|=1}f(x):=\left(\frac{x^{*}R^{2}x}{x^{*}Rx}\right)^{2}+x^{*}Px,

(50)

where
R
is an Hermitian positive semidefinite matrix and
P=Q^{-1}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)^{*}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)Q^{-1}
, with
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
,
J
skew-Hermitian and
Q
Hermitian positive definite. Problem (
50
), can be solved by conventional Riemannian optimization techniques, such as the Riemannian trust region method; see, e.g.,
[
1
]
. However, these general-purpose Riemannian optimization methods often target local minimizers, and they do not fully exploit the special form of the objective function for analysis and computation. In
[
24
]
, a Joint Numerical Range (JNR)-based approach was suggested for the solution of a minimization problem similar to (
50
) with objective function

\frac{x^{*}Ax}{x^{*}(\alpha_{1}I_{n}+\beta_{1}C)x}+\frac{x^{*}Bx}{x^{*}(\alpha_{2}I_{n}+\beta_{2}C)x},

where
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for
i=1,2
and
A,B,C
are Hermitian positive semidefinite matrices. It was shown that via the JNR approach, the reduced problem has fewer local minimizers than the original one. Following the same approach, we reformulate the minimization problem (
50
) into a new optimization problem over a JNR of the matrices
R^{2},R
and
P
. The convexity in the JNR also allows for the development of a nonlinear eigenvector approach to efficiently solve the optimization problem.

Let
\mathcal{K}:=(R^{2},R,P)
and define the JNR associated with
\mathcal{K}
as

W(\mathcal{K}):=\left\{\rho_{\mathcal{K}}(x):x\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\|x\|=1\right\},

(51)

where
\rho_{\mathcal{K}}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
consists of quadratic forms of the Hermitian matrices in
\mathcal{K}

\rho_{\mathcal{K}}(x):=[x^{*}R^{2}x,x^{*}Rx,x^{*}Px]^{T}.

(52)

It is well-known that
W(\mathcal{K})
is a closed and connected region in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and it is a convex set if the size
n
of the matrices
R,P
satisfies
n\geq 3
, see
[
7
,
35
]
.

The objective function
f(x)
in (
50
) then can be written as

f(x)=h(\rho_{\mathcal{K}}(x)),

(53)

where
h:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

h(z):=\left(\frac{z_{1}}{z_{2}}\right)^{2}+z_{3}.

(54)

Therefore, by using the intermediate variable
z=\rho_{\mathcal{K}}(x)
, the minimization problem (
50
) can be reformulated as

\min_{z\in W(\mathcal{K})}h(z),

(55)

where
W(\mathcal{K})
is the JNR associated with
\mathcal{K}
defined in (
51
).

In the JNR minimization (
55
), the variable
z\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is real, in contrast to a complex and
n
-dimensional variable vector
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
of the original minimization problem (
50
). Typically
n\gg 3
in practice, so the new feasible region
W(\mathcal{K})
is a convex set. This facilitates analysis, computation, and visualization of the JNR minimization
[
24
]
.

The following lemma from
[
24
]
which holds for the minimization problem (
50
), indicates that the JNR minimization (
55
) is superior to the
original minimization problem (
50
).

Lemma 4.1
.

Consider the optimization problems (
50
) and (
55
).

1.

A vector
x_{\star}\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\|x\|=1
is a global minimizer of n (
50
) if and only if
z_{\star}=\rho_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{\star})
is a global minimizer of the JNR minimization problem (
55
).

2.

If
z_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a local minimizer of the JNR minimization problem (
55
), then any
x_{\star}
with
\rho_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{\star})=z_{\star}
is a local minimizer of (
50
).

We will now establish a variational characterization for the local minimizers of (
55
) by exploiting the convexity of the JNR. This characterization can be equivalently expressed as a nonlinear eigenvalue problem with eigenvector dependency (NEPv). The following theorem is an adaption of
[
24
, Theorem 3.2]
for our problem.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
M=(R^{2},R,P)
,
W(\mathcal{K})
be defined by (
51
), and
h
by (
54
). Suppose that
z_{\star}
is a local minimizer of
h
in
W(\mathcal{K})
, and that
h
is differentiable at
z_{\star}
. Then

\min_{z\in W(\mathcal{K})}\nabla h(z_{\star})^{T}z=\nabla h(z_{\star})^{T}z_{\star}.

(56)

Moreover, (
56
) holds if and only if
z_{\star}=\rho_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{\star})
for an
x_{\star}\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\|x_{\star}\|=1
satisfying the NEPv:

H(x)x=\mu x,

(57)

where
H(x)\in\mathbb{C}^{n,n}
is the Hermitian matrix given by

H(x):=2\frac{x^{*}R^{2}x}{(x^{*}Rx)^{2}}R^{2}-2\frac{(x^{*}R^{2}x)^{2}}{(x^{*}Rx)^{3}}R+P

(58)

and
\mu
is the smallest eigenvalue of
H(x)
.

Proof.

The proof follows by taking
h(z)=\left(\frac{z_{1}}{z_{2}}\right)^{2}+z_{3}
in
[
24
, Theorem 3.2]
.
∎

In literature, nonlinear eigenvalue problem characterizations have been explored in various optimization problems with orthogonality constraints; see
[
9
]
and the references therein. These characterizations allow for efficient solutions of the optimization problem by exploiting state-of-the-art eigensolvers. Particularly in
[
42
,
43
]
, the authors propose such characterizations that apply to the optimization of the sum of Rayleigh quotients

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\|x\|=1}\frac{x^{T}H_{1}x}{x^{T}H_{2}x}+x^{T}H_{3}x,

(59)

where
H_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,n}
for
i=1,2,3
are Hermitian positive definite matrices, and in
[
24
]
such characterizations are suggested for the optimization of sums of generalized Rayleigh quotients

\min_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\|x\|=1}\frac{x^{*}A_{1}x}{x^{*}(\alpha_{1}I_{n}+\beta_{1}A_{2})x}+\frac{x^{*}A_{3}x}{x^{*}(\alpha_{2}I_{n}+\beta_{2}A_{2})x},

(60)

where
A_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{n,n}
are Hermitian positive semidefinite. Notice that the minimization problem (
50
) cannot be generally reduced to the form (
59
) or (
60
), due to the square term in (
50
). Therefore, the previous analysis does not directly apply to the minimization (
50
).

Note that in Theorem
4.2
, the function
h(z)
needs to be differentiable at the local minimizer
z_{\star}
. Non-differentiability may occur if the fraction in
h(z_{\star})
becomes
\frac{0}{0}
. The following theorem considers this case and shows that such local minimizers can be obtained easily.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
y_{\star}
be a local minimizer of
h
over
W(\mathcal{K})
.

1.

If
R>0
, then
y_{\star}
must be a differentiable point for
h(y)
and hence it admits the NEPv characterization given by Theorem
4.2
.

2.

If
R\geq 0
is singular, then
z_{\star}
may be a non-differentiable point for
h(z)
and in that case it must be expressed by
z_{\star}=\rho_{\mathcal{K}}(Uv)
, where
U
is an orthonormal basis matrix of the nullspace of
R
, and
v
is the eigenvector for the smallest eigenvalue of the matrix
U^{*}PU
.

Proof.

The function
h
is differentiable at
z
if and only if
z_{2}=x^{*}Rx\neq 0
. If
R>0
, then by parameterizing
z_{\star}=\rho_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{\star})
, we must have
x_{\star}^{*}Rx_{\star}\neq 0
and hence
h
is differentiable at
z_{\star}
.

Suppose now that
h
is not differentiable at
z_{\star}=\rho_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{\star})
, i.e., the denominator term in (
54
) is
(z_{2})^{2}=(x_{\star}^{*}Rx_{\star})^{2}=0
, so we have
x_{\star}\in\text{null}(R)=\text{range}(U)
. As
R^{2}
and
R
have the same nullspaces, by the local optimality of
z_{\star}
and Lemma
4.1
,
x_{\star}
must be a local minimizer of
f(x)
restricted to the subspace range
(U)

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=Uv\\
\|v\|=1\end{subarray}}f(x)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=Uv\\
\|v\|=1\end{subarray}}x^{*}Px=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|v\|=1\end{subarray}}v^{*}(U^{*}PU)v,

(61)

where the first equality is due to
\frac{x^{*}R^{2}x}{x^{*}Rx}=\frac{0}{0}=0
for all
x\in\text{range}(U)
. Hence, the local minimizer of (
55
) is
z_{\star}=\rho_{\mathcal{K}}(Uv)
, where
v
is the eigenvector corresponding to the smallest eigenvalue of
U^{*}PU
.
∎

To find the minimal solution of (
50
), we can solve the NEPv (
57
), provided that it has a solution, along with the smallest eigenvalue and the corresponding eigenvector of the matrix
U^{*}PU
. We can then select
x_{\star}
with the minimal objective value
f(x_{\star})
as the solution.
Thus, in view of Theorems
4.3
and
3.1
, we have the following result that gives an explicit formula for the structured stability radius
r^{S_{d}}(J,R)
in terms of solution of an NEPv.

Theorem 4.4
.

Consider a DH system of the form (
1
) and consider the NEPv
H(x)x=\mu x
defined in (
57
) for the matrix
H(x)
defined in (
58
).

1.

If
R>0
, then

r^{S_{d}}(J,R)=\left(\frac{x_{\star}^{*}R^{2}x_{\star}}{x_{\star}^{*}Rx_{\star}}\right)^{2}+\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}x_{\star}^{*}Q^{-1}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)^{*}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)Q^{-1}x_{\star},

where
x_{\star}
is a solution to the NEPv
H(x)x=\mu x
.

2.

If
R\geq 0
is singular and if a solution
x_{\star}
to the NEPv
H(x)x=\mu x
exists,
then

\displaystyle r^{S_{d}}(J,R)

\displaystyle=\min\bigg\{\left(\frac{x_{\star}^{*}R^{2}x_{\star}}{x_{\star}^{*}Rx_{\star}}\right)^{2}+\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}x_{\star}^{*}Q^{-1}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)^{*}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)Q^{-1}x_{\star},

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda_{\min}(U^{*}Q^{-1}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)^{*}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)Q^{-1}U)\bigg\}.

3.

If
R\geq 0
is singular and the NEPv
H(x)x=\mu x
is not solvable,
then

\displaystyle r^{S_{d}}(J,R)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda_{\min}(U^{*}Q^{-1}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)^{*}(i\omega I_{n}-JQ)Q^{-1}U),

where

U
is the orthonormal basis matrix of the nullspace of matrix
R
.

Numerical experiments

In this section, we present some numerical experiments to illustrate our results on structured stability radii. These numerical experiments emphasize that the stability radii under structure-preserving perturbations can be much larger than those under general perturbations.

•

To compute the stability radius
r^{S_{d}}(J,R)
obtained in Theorem
3.1
, we use the NEPv characterization for the inner optimization discussed in Section
4
, and for the outer optimization, we use the function
fminsearch
in MATLAB. We use the level-shifted self-consistent field (SCF) iteration
[
9
,
41
,
43
]
to solve NEPv (
57
): starting from an initial guess
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we iteratively solve Hermitian eigenvalue problems

[H(x_{k})+\sigma_{k}x_{k}x_{k}^{*}]x_{k+1}=\lambda_{k+1}x_{k+1},

(62)

for
k=0,1,\dots
, where
\sigma_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
is a given level-shift, and
x_{k+1}
is the eigenvector corresponding to the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{k+1}
of the Hermitian matrix
H(x_{k})+\sigma_{k}x_{k}x_{k}^{*}
. The equation (
62
) reduces to the plain SCF iteration if
\sigma_{k}\equiv 0
. Using a nonzero level-shift
\sigma_{k}
often helps to keep the plain SCF stable and to speed up the convergence process. In particular, a plain SCF may not converge; however, for sufficiently large
\sigma_{k}
, a level-shifted SCF is always locally convergent under mild assumptions; see, e.g.,
[
9
]
. In our implementation, we adaptively select level-shifts
\sigma_{k}
by trying sequentially

\sigma_{k}=0,\,2\delta_{k},\,2^{2}\delta_{k},\,2^{3}\delta_{k},\dots,

(63)

until
h(x_{k+1})<h(x_{k})
is achieved, where
\delta_{k}=\lambda_{2}(H(x_{k}))-\lambda_{1}(H(x_{k}))
is the eigenvalue gap between the smallest and second smallest eigenvalue of
H(x_{k})
. The use of
\sigma_{k}=2\delta_{k}
is common practice; see, e.g.,
[
41
,
43
]
. Often, a plain SCF step with
\sigma_{k}=0
can produce a reduced
h(x_{k+1})
, and then there is no need to actually enter the selection loop (
63
) for
\sigma_{k}
. Finally, the level-shifted SCF (
63
) is considered to have converged if the relative residual norm satisfies

\frac{\|H(x_{k})x_{k}-s_{k}x_{k}\|}{\|H(x_{k})\|_{1}+1}\leq 10^{-10},

(64)

where
s_{k}=x_{k}^{*}H(x_{k})x_{k}
and
\|\cdot\|_{1}
denote the matrix 1-norm (i.e., maximal absolute column sum).

For the stability radius,
r^{S_{i}}(J,R)
obtained in Theorem
3.4
, we used the function
fminsearch
in MATLAB.

For the stability radius
r^{S}(J,R)
obtained in Theorem
3.6
, we used the convex programming package CVX in MATLAB for inner optimization and
fminsearch
for the outer optimization.

6
Conclusions

We have derived explicit computable formulas for various stability radii of DH systems with respect to skew-Hermitian perturbations in
J
and three different types of structured perturbations in
R
, first, Hermitian negative semidefinite perturbations, which keep the perturbed matrix
\tilde{R}
positive semidefinite; second, Hermitian perturbations such that the perturbed matrix
\tilde{R}
is Hermitian positive semidefinite; and third, Hermitian perturbations. The results demonstrate that limiting perturbations to those that preserve structure results in larger robustness, as it takes substantially larger perturbations to shift an eigenvalue to the imaginary axis. The case where perturbations in all three matrices
J
,
R
