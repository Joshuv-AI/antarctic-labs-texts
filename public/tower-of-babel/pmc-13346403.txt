A multicenter study identifies an immune inflammatory and nutritional prognostic signature in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma
Authors: Jie Tang, Benjie Xu, Yun Tao, Shengjun Ji, Jie Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmune dysfunction, systemic inflammation, and malnutrition are closely associated with tumor progression and prognosis in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC). This multicenter retrospective study aimed to evaluate the prognostic value of immune-inflammatory-nutritional biomarkers and to establish a novel cholangiocarcinoma immune-inflammatory-nutritional score (CIINS).MethodsA total of 284 ICC patients who underwent curative surgical resection at three medical centers were retrospectively analyzed and divided into a training cohort (n = 182) and a validation cohort (n = 102). Twelve immune, inflammatory, and nutritional biomarkers derived from routine preoperative blood tests were assessed. The CIINS was constructed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator Cox regression. Nomograms integrating CIINS with independent clinicopathological factors were developed to predict overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). Model performance was evaluated using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic curves, calibration plots, and decision curve analysis.ResultsThe CIINS consisted of four biomarkers: neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, neutrophil-to-albumin ratio, and prognostic nutritional index. Patients were stratified into high- and low-CIINS groups using the median value. High CIINS was significantly associated with poorer OS and PFS in both the training (both p < 0.001) and validation cohorts (OS: p = 0.006; PFS: p = 0.007). Elevated CIINS correlated with obstructive jaundice, increased CA19-9 levels, and advanced AJCC TNM stage. Multivariate analysis confirmed CIINS as an independent prognostic factor. CIINS-based nomograms demonstrated good discrimination and calibration for survival prediction.ConclusionCIINS is a simple and clinically applicable prognostic tool for patients with resected ICC, supporting individualized risk stratification and postoperative management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05227-2.

Introduction

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) ranks as the second most prevalent primary liver cancer following hepatocellular carcinoma, comprising about 10–20% of such cases [1]. Over the past few decades, the incidence of ICC has significantly increased worldwide, particularly in North America and Europe, potentially due to the rising prevalence of hepatitis virus infection, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, and cirrhosis [2–4]. Surgical resection continues to be the sole potentially curative treatment for ICC, despite advancements in systemic therapies like chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy [5]. Despite curative-intent resection, prognosis is poor, with a 5-year overall survival rate of 10% to 35%, primarily due to high postoperative local and distant recurrence rates [6, 7]. Although the widespread use of prognostic tools like the AJCC staging system and tumor biomarkers such as CEA and CA 19 − 9, their accuracy is often inadequate for comprehensive treatment guidance and risk stratification [8, 9]. Identifying novel and reliable prognostic biomarkers is essential for predicting outcomes, supporting personalized therapeutic strategies, and enhancing the clinical management of ICC patients.

Emerging evidence suggests that immune dysfunction, chronic inflammation, and malnutrition are pivotal in cancer initiation, progression, and metastasis [10–12]. ICC, which frequently arises in the context of chronic inflammation such as biliary disease or hepatitis B infection, exhibits strong associations with systemic inflammatory responses and nutritional status [13, 14]. Inflammatory biomarkers derived from blood, such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR), and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), have been extensively studied for their prognostic value in various cancers, including ICC [15, 16]. Nutritional indicators like the prognostic nutritional index (PNI) and albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) score have demonstrated significant predictive value for survival outcomes across various cancers [17–19]. However, previous studies often focused on isolated markers, which fail to comprehensively capture the interplay between immune dysregulation, inflammatory response, and nutritional depletion. Consequently, it is imperative to create a comprehensive prognostic model that incorporates immune, inflammatory, and nutritional factors.In this study, we aimed to establish a novel cholangiocarcinoma immune-inflammatory-nutritional score (CIINS) by integrating multiple biomarkers, with the goal of improving risk stratification and individualized prognostication for patients undergoing resection for ICC.

Materials and methods

Patients selection

This retrospective study screened ICC patients who underwent curative surgical resection between April 2014 and October 2021 at The Affiliated Hospital of Jiangnan University, The Affiliated Suzhou Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, and Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histologically confirmed diagnosis of ICC; (2) receipt of radical (curative-intent) resection; (3) absence of extrahepatic metastasis at the time of surgery; (4) availability of complete baseline laboratory test information. The exclusion criteria included: (1) prior treatments before surgery, such as transarterial chemoembolization, radiofrequency ablation, or systemic therapy; (2) a history of hematologic, autoimmune, or other malignancies; and (3) incomplete clinical, laboratory, or follow-up data.The final analysis included 284 patients, with 182 in the training cohort from The Affiliated Hospital of Jiangnan University and The Affiliated Suzhou Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, and 102 in the validation cohort from Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital (Figure S1). Patients were staged using the AJCC 8th edition and monitored until June 2024. The present study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangnan University (approval number LS2021054), the Affiliated Suzhou Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (approval number K20150001), and the Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital (approval number KY2017-19).

Adjuvant chemotherapy

Postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy was recommended for patients with high-risk pathological features, including lymph node metastasis, vascular or perineural invasion, poor tumor differentiation, or advanced TNM stage. Commonly used regimens were gemcitabine-based or fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy, including gemcitabine alone, gemcitabine combined with cisplatin or oxaliplatin, and oxaliplatin-based regimens such as FOLFOX or capecitabine plus oxaliplatin. Chemotherapy was administered intravenously and initiated within 6–8 weeks after surgery, provided adequate postoperative recovery. The planned treatment course generally consisted of 4–6 cycles, with dose adjustments or treatment discontinuation based on patient tolerance and adverse events.

Data collection and definition of variables

This study gathered demographic and tumor-related data, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), obstructive jaundice presence, HBV infection status, CEA and CA19-9 levels, tumor differentiation, perineural invasion, and AJCC 8th edition TNM classification staging. Preoperative hematological parameters assessed included counts of lymphocytes, neutrophils, monocytes, and platelets, as well as levels of fibrinogen (FIB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and serum albumin. This study examined 12 biomarkers associated with immunity, inflammation, and nutrition, including ALT, AST, FIB, ALRI, NLR, MLR, PLR, SII, SIRI, NAR, PNI, and GNRI. The specific calculation formulas for these biomarkers are provided in Table S1. Overall survival (OS) refers to the duration from curative surgery until death or the most recent follow-up. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from the initiation of treatment to the first documented evidence of disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first.

Optimal cutoff values for the 12 immune-inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers were determined using X-tile software and were applied exclusively for Kaplan–Meier survival analyses to stratify patients into high and low groups. For all regression-based analyses, including LASSO regression, CIINS construction, and Cox proportional hazards models, these biomarkers were treated as continuous variables.

Construction of CIINS and evaluation of prognostic value

To identify the most informative prognostic biomarkers while minimizing multicollinearity and overfitting, the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) Cox regression model was applied. LASSO is a penalized regression method that introduces a regularization parameter (λ), which shrinks the coefficients of less relevant variables toward zero and retains only those with the strongest prognostic contribution. All twelve candidate immune-inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers were simultaneously entered into the LASSO Cox regression model. Ten-fold cross-validation was performed to determine the optimal penalty parameter (λ) corresponding to the minimum partial likelihood deviance. Based on the optimal λ value, the biomarkers retained non-zero coefficients, indicating their independent and stable prognostic relevance. The remaining biomarkers were excluded because their coefficients were shrunk to zero by the LASSO penalty.

The CIINS was treated as a continuous variable during model construction and Cox regression analyses. For risk stratification and clinical interpretability, patients were dichotomized into high- and low-CIINS groups using the median CIINS value derived from the training cohort. This median-based cutoff was subsequently applied unchanged to the validation cohort to ensure robustness and avoid overfitting. The association between the CIINS and various clinical and pathological characteristics was evaluated using the Chi-square test. The predictive performance of the CIINS was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for both training and validation cohorts over follow-up periods of 12, 36, and 60 months.

Development and assessment of nomogram

Independent risk factors for OS and PFS in ICC were determined using univariate Cox regression analysis, followed by multivariate inverse stepwise Cox regression modeling. Nomogram models were developed using the identified predictors to estimate OS and PFS. The models’ predictive performance was assessed through ROC survival curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA) in both training and validation cohorts.

In addition to CIINS and serum tumor markers, conventional tumor-related pathological variables, including tumor size, tumor number, lymph node metastasis, and resection margin status, were initially evaluated in univariate Cox regression analyses and considered as candidate variables for multivariate modeling.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR) as appropriate. Group comparisons were performed using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Optimal cutoffs for the 12 biomarkers were determined with X-tile software [20]. Prognostic factors were identified by univariate Cox regression, followed by multivariate analysis using inverse stepwise Cox regression for variables with p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.1), and a two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

This multicenter study included 284 patients who underwent radical surgical resection for ICC. The median age of the patients was 62 years (range: 32–78 years), with 74 (26.06%) females and 210 (73.94%) males. The median follow-up time was 28 months (range, 1–84 months), and the last follow-up was conducted in June 2024. The OS rates at 12, 36, and 60 months were 72.2%, 35.6%, and 16.5%, respectively. Correlation analysis of clinical baseline characteristics and pathological features between the training cohort (n = 182) and validation cohort (n = 102) demonstrated a balanced distribution, with no significant differences between groups (p > 0.05, Table 1).

Survival analysis of twelve indicators and CIINS

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed significant correlations between all 12 biomarkers and both OS and PFS as illustrated in Figs. 1 and 2. Subsequently, LASSO regression analysis was conducted on the 12 indicators, identifying the NLR, PLR, NAR, and PNI as significant prognostic factors (Fig. 3A and B). The CIINS was formulated using coefficients from the LASSO analysis as follows: CIINS = 0.0407 × NLR + 0.0119 × PLR + 0.1669 × NAR – 0.0166 × PNI. Patients were then categorized into High CIINS (score ≥ 0.085) and Low CIINS (score < 0.085) groups based on the median value.

We analyzed the correlation between CIINS and clinical-pathological characteristics in the training and validation cohorts. Elevated CIINS levels were significantly linked to obstructive jaundice, increased CA19-9 levels, and advanced 8th AJCC TNM stages, underscoring the clinical importance of CIINS (Table 2). The comparative analysis demonstrated that CIINS exhibited a significantly higher AUC than each of the 12 individual biomarkers in both the training and validation datasets (Figure S2). The performance of CIINS with key combinations of its constituent components was conducted (Figure S3). The AUC of CIINS was consistently higher than that of any individual marker or their combinations, highlighting its superior incremental predictive value. In the training set, the ROC curves for 12-, 36-, and 60-month OS showed AUCs of 0.723, 0.662, and 0.655, respectively, while in the validation set, the AUCs were 0.697, 0.649, and 0.647 (Fig. 3C and D).

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that patients with Low CIINS had significantly improved OS and PFS compared to those with High CIINS (Fig. 4A-D). Univariate and multivariate reverse stepwise Cox regression analyses, including CIINS and other clinical and pathological variables, were conducted on the training set. The findings indicated that CIINS independently predicted both OS (adjusted HR 2.066; 95% CI 1.344–3.205; p = 0.001) and PFS (adjusted HR 2.053; 95% CI 1.366–3.086; p < 0.001) (Table 3). No statistically significant differences in OS or PFS were observed among the three centers (all P values > 0.05) (Table S2).

Although tumor-related pathological factors such as tumor size, tumor number, lymph node metastasis, and resection margin status demonstrated prognostic relevance in univariate analyses, they did not remain statistically significant after adjustment for CIINS and other covariates in the multivariate Cox regression model. This may be attributable to the strong prognostic contribution of CIINS, which captures host systemic immune-inflammatory and nutritional status beyond anatomical tumor characteristics.

Development and assessment of nomograms

Multivariate reverse stepwise Cox regression analysis identified CIINS, CEA, CA19-9, and perineural invasion as independent predictors for the construction of the OS (Fig. 5A) and PFS (Fig. 5B) prognostic models. The AUCs for predicting 12-, 36-, and 60-month OS were 0.705, 0.759, and 0.813 in the training set, and 0.686, 0.748, and 0.828 in the validation set, respectively (Fig. 5C and D). In the training set, the PFS AUCs at 12, 36, and 60 months were 0.719, 0.672, and 0.774, respectively, while in the validation set, they were 0.710, 0.713, and 0.826 (Fig. 5E and F). Calibration curves and DCA for 12-, 36-, and 60-month endpoints were plotted using both training and validation sets, showcasing the models’ excellent predictive performance for postoperative survival in ICC patients (Fig. 6 and Figure S3).

Discussion

This multicenter retrospective study developed and validated a novel CIINS for prognostic prediction in ICC patients undergoing radical resection. The CIINS effectively stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups by integrating four biomarkers: NLR, PLR, NAR, and PNI.Elevated CIINS levels were notably linked to poorer OS and PFS outcomes in both the training and validation cohorts. Moreover, multivariate Cox regression analysis confirmed the CIINS as an independent prognostic factor for both OS and PFS. Nomograms incorporating CIINS and other clinicopathological variables demonstrated robust discriminatory ability and good calibration in predicting 12-, 36-, and 60-month survival outcomes. The CIINS, as a composite indicator of systemic immunity, inflammation, and nutritional status, offers an effective and user-friendly tool for personalized risk assessment and outcome prediction in resected ICC patients.

Previous studies have highlighted the critical prognostic value of immune-inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers in patients with ICC. Multiple studies have shown that a high NLR is strongly linked to worse OS and a higher risk of recurrence following surgical resection for ICC, highlighting the impact of systemic inflammatory responses on tumor progression [15, 21–24]. Similarly, a higher PLR has been correlated with unfavorable survival outcomes in ICC patients, serving as a surrogate marker for tumor-induced inflammatory activation [25–27]. In contrast, a higher PNI, which integrates serum albumin and lymphocyte counts, has been consistently associated with better survival, underscoring the protective effect of preserved nutritional and immune status against cancer progression [18, 28–33]. Although research on the NAR in ICC remains limited, NAR has been increasingly recognized in various malignancies as an indicator combining inflammatory burden and nutritional impairment, and may offer complementary prognostic information [34]. Given these insights, our study systematically evaluated the prognostic impact of NLR, PLR, NAR, and PNI, and developed a novel integrated score termed the CIINS. Unlike previous investigations that focused on individual biomarkers, our approach combined multiple dimensions of immune function, systemic inflammation, and nutritional status into a unified index, thereby capturing the complex interactions between tumor biology and host response. Notably, the CIINS demonstrated superior predictive performance compared to any single marker when validated across independent cohorts. These findings suggest that composite biomarkers, which reflect the multifaceted nature of cancer progression, may offer more robust and clinically meaningful prognostic stratification in ICC. Moreover, the CIINS provides a readily accessible and cost-effective tool based on routine laboratory parameters, facilitating its potential application in clinical decision-making.

Recently, several studies have developed comprehensive prognostic indices that integrate immune, inflammatory, nutritional, and tumor-related parameters to improve risk stratification in ICC. A multicenter study proposed the tumor morphology immune inflammatory nutritional (TIIN) score, which incorporated tumor morphology characteristics alongside inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers to predict postoperative outcomes [35]. Unlike the TIIN model, which incorporates tumor morphological characteristics, the CIINS model is based entirely on systemic immune-inflammatory and nutritional markers that are readily obtained from standard laboratory tests. This not only simplifies the risk stratification process but also makes CIINS particularly valuable in settings where access to tumor morphological assessment or advanced imaging is limited, thereby broadening its clinical applicability. Another study established a prognostic score based purely on immune-inflammatory-nutritional markers derived from preoperative blood indices, without incorporating tumor morphological features [36]. A separate study demonstrated the prognostic significance of combining inflammatory markers, like the systemic immune-inflammation index, with liver function parameters, such as the albumin-bilirubin grade, to assess systemic inflammation and hepatic reserve [17]. In comparison, our study developed the CIINS score without including tumor morphological factors, aiming to provide a purely systemic biological assessment. Unlike the TIIN score, which integrates tumor burden parameters, the CIINS model is based exclusively on systemic inflammatory and nutritional status, making it potentially more accessible in diverse clinical settings without reliance on imaging or pathological evaluations. Furthermore, during the development of the nomogram, our study highlighted the prognostic significance of perineural invasion, which has been consistently recognized as a critical adverse factor in ICC. Recent multicenter studies demonstrated that the presence of perineural invasion is independently associated with inferior overall survival and relapse-free survival [37]. Molecular analyses have further revealed that perineural invasion is linked to an immunosuppressive microenvironment, with increased neutrophil infiltration and upregulated immune checkpoint molecules, promoting tumor progression and recurrence [38]. Importantly, perineural invasion may also predict which patients derive more benefit from postoperative chemotherapy, highlighting its dual prognostic and therapeutic stratification value [39]. Our study corroborates these findings, confirming that perineural invasion remains an independent predictor of worse outcomes and should be considered an essential factor in future prognostic modeling and treatment planning for ICC patients. Notably, recent advances in hepatocellular carcinoma have demonstrated that spatially heterogeneous treatment strategies, such as lattice stereotactic body radiotherapy, can modulate the tumor immune microenvironment and enhance therapeutic efficacy in biologically aggressive tumors, underscoring the importance of integrating biological risk stratification into treatment planning [40, 41]. In parallel, emerging artificial intelligence–based imaging models in hepatocellular carcinoma have shown strong capability in capturing intratumoral heterogeneity and predicting treatment response and prognosis, further supporting the concept that combining systemic biomarkers with advanced spatial or imaging-informed approaches may refine individualized risk assessment and therapeutic decision-making in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma.

A potential concern is that several well-established tumor-related pathological factors, such as tumor size, tumor number, lymph node metastasis, and resection margin status, were not incorporated into the final nomogram. These variables were initially evaluated during model development; however, they did not retain independent prognostic significance in multivariate analysis after adjustment for CIINS and serum tumor markers. This finding suggests that CIINS captures prognostic information that is not fully explained by conventional tumor burden indicators alone. Unlike traditional pathological factors that primarily reflect anatomical tumor extent, CIINS integrates systemic immune response, inflammatory status, and nutritional reserve, which are increasingly recognized as critical determinants of tumor progression, recurrence, and treatment response. Several studies have demonstrated that host-related biological factors can provide prognostic information complementary to tumor-centric models, particularly in heterogeneous diseases such as intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma. Therefore, the CIINS-based nomogram should be viewed not as a replacement for tumor factor–based models, but as a biologically informative supplement that enhances individualized risk stratification beyond conventional staging systems.

The study is subject to several limitations. The retrospective design could lead to selection bias and restrict the establishment of causal relationships. Although multivariate analyses were performed to adjust for potential confounders, unmeasured variables may still have influenced the results. Second, despite conducting external validation with an independent cohort, the study’s generalizability may be restricted due to all participating centers being situated in China. Third, the CIINS was calculated based on a single preoperative blood test, and dynamic changes in systemic inflammatory, immune, and nutritional status during follow-up were not assessed, which could provide additional prognostic information. Forth, we recognize that inter-institutional differences in laboratory techniques, equipment calibration, and reference ranges may introduce variability in the measurement of systemic biomarkers such as NLR, PLR, and PNI. To promote reproducibility, we recommend that future studies clearly define the calculation methods for each marker, standardize blood collection and processing procedures, and, where possible, utilize harmonized or internationally recognized laboratory protocols. Adherence to these guidelines will facilitate the broader clinical application and validation of the CIINS model. Additionally, we acknowledge that the cutoff strategy differed between individual biomarkers and CIINS. However, given that CIINS is a composite score derived from multiple outcome-associated variables, a median-based cutoff was intentionally selected to reduce optimism bias and improve generalizability. Future prospective studies may further explore outcome-driven or multi-level CIINS stratification strategies.

Conclusion

The CIINS score, constructed from systemic inflammatory, immune, and nutritional biomarkers, demonstrated strong prognostic value for postoperative outcomes in patients with ICC. Its simplicity and accessibility underscore its potential for clinical risk stratification, warranting further validation in prospective multicenter studies.
