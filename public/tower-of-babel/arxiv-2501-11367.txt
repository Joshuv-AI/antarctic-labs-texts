Abstract
. Take an interval [ t , t + 1 ] [t,t+1] on the x x -axis together with the same interval on the y y -axis and let ρ \rho be the normalized one-dimensional Lebesgue measure on this set of two segments. Continuing the work done by Lev [ Lev18 ] , Lai, Liu and Prince [ LLP21 ] as well as Ai, Lu and Zhou [ ALZ23 ] we examine the spectrality of this measure for all different values of t t (being spectral means that there is an orthonormal basis for L 2 ​ ( ρ ) L^{2}(\rho) consisting of exponentials e 2 ​ π ​ i ​ ( λ 1 ​ x + λ 2 ​ y ) e^{2\pi i(\lambda_{1}x+\lambda_{2}y)} ). We almost complete the study showing that for − 1 2 < t < 0 -\frac{1}{2}<t<0 and for all t ∉ ℚ t\notin{\mathbb{Q}} the measure ρ \rho is not spectral. The only remaining undecided case is the case t = − 1 2 t=-\frac{1}{2} (plus space). We also observe that in all known cases of spectral instances of this measure the spectrum is contained in a line and we give an easy necessary and sufficient condition for such measures to have a line spectrum.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Spectrality and the Fuglede Conjecture

Let
\mu
be a Borel probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with compact support
\Omega
. The measure
\mu
is called
spectral
if there exists a countable set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

E_{\Lambda}:=\{e^{2\pi i\lambda\cdot x}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}\ \text{ forms an orthogonal basis for}\ L^{2}(\mu),

i.e., there exists a countable set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

f(x)=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}c_{\lambda}(f)e_{\lambda}(x),\text{ with }e_{\lambda}(x)=e^{2\pi i\lambda\cdot x},

for any
f\in\ L^{2}(\mu)
, where

c_{\lambda}(f)={\langle f,e_{\lambda}\rangle}_{\mu}=\int f(x)e^{-2\pi i\lambda\cdot x}\,d\mu(x).

In this case, we call
\Lambda
a spectrum of
\mu
.
In particular, if
\mu
is the Lebesgue measure restricted on the set
\Omega
of Lebesgue measure 1, then we say
\Omega
is a spectral set. (The definition is trivially extended to finite nonnegative Borel measures, not necessarily probability measures, and sets of finite Lebesgue measure.)

Spectral sets were first introduced by Fuglede
[
Fug74
]
who proposed the
Fuglede Conjecture
(also known as
Spectral Set Conjecture
).

Fuglede Conjecture:

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a spectral set if and only if it tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by translation.
The set
\Omega
is said to tile
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by translations if there exists a discrete set
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\bigcup_{l\in L}(\Omega+l)=\mathbb{R}^{d}\quad\text{ and }\quad m((\Omega+l_{1})\cap(\Omega+l_{2}))=0\ \text{ for all }l_{1}\neq l_{2}\in L,

where
m(\cdot)
denotes the Lebesgue measure and
L
is called a tiling complement of
\Omega
.

The classical example in dimension 1 is the interval
\Omega=[0,1]
which is both a spectral set and a translational tile. More precisely, the set
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}
serves simultaneously as a spectrum and a tiling complement of
\Omega
, and
f(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{n}(f)e^{2\pi inx}
for any
f\in\ L^{2}([0,1])
. For more general examples, Fuglede
[
Fug74
,
Fug01
]
proved that the conjecture is true in the case of a triangle or a disk in the plane (both sets are neither spectral nor tiles), and he also proved that
\Omega
can tile with a lattice tiling complement
L
if and only if the dual lattice
L^{*}
is a spectrum for
\Omega
.

It has been proved
[
Tao04
,
KM06b
,
KM06a
,
FMM06
,
FR06
]
that both directions of the conjecture are not valid when the dimension is at least 3, but the conjecture is still open in both directions in dimensions 1 and 2. Although the conjecture is not correct in high dimensions, it has been an important topic of research and there are many positive and negative results about the relation of tiling to spectrality. For example, an important result was recently proved by Lev and Matolcsi
[
LM22
]
, who showed that the conjecture holds in any dimension for a convex body. For some cyclic groups, this conjecture is also true if some appropriate conditions are restricted, and more results can be found in
[
Łab02
,
Zha23
,
MK17
,
KMSV22
]
. See also the recent survey
[
Kol24
]
for a more thorough description of the problem and the existing results.

The goal of the paper is to study the spectrality of a class of so-called
symmetric additive measures
. They are described in the next section.

Theorem 1.1
.

If

All the spectra of the measure
\rho

[

Let

be a straight line through the origin and

the orthogonal subspace to

(a straight line also). We denote by

the orthogonal projection operator onto line

(but taking values in

\pi_{L}(v)=t
for any

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
then the projection of

onto

is the measure

on
{\mathbb{R}}
defined by

\pi_{L}\nu(E)=\nu(Eu+u^{\perp}),

where

.

.

Suppose
\rho
is a probability measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
whose support is a finite union of line segments. Suppose also that
L
is a straight line through the origin such that the orthogonal projection
\pi_{L}
onto

is one-to-one

-almost everywhere.

Then
\rho
has a spectrum
\Lambda u\subseteq L
if and only if the projection measure

has spectrum
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
.

We prove Theorem

in Section

and known results about one-dimensional spectral sets such as the characterization in

Remark 2.1
.

The fact that the multiplicity of
\Lambda
is 1 (all points of
\Lambda

[

Take the function

\rho

\displaystyle{\langle f,e_{\lambda}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\int f(x,y)e^{-2\pi i(\lambda_{1}x+\lambda_{2}y)}\,d\rho(x,y)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1/2&\text{ if }\lambda_{1}=0,\\

4.
Line spectra from projections

Here we prove Theorem
1.2
and see how it can be applied to produce line spectra for some collections of measures supported on line segments.

Let
u
be a unit vector in the straight line
L
that goes through the origin. By our assumption on the injectivity of
\pi_{L}

\rho
-almost everywhere, any function
f(x)
on
{\rm supp\,}\rho
can be written as

f(x)=\widetilde{f}(u\cdot x),

for
\rho
-almost all
x

\widetilde{f}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is supported on
u\cdot{\rm supp\,}\rho

\pi_{L}

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}{\left|{f}\right|}^{2}d\rho=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\left|{\widetilde{f}}\right|}^{2}d\pi_{L}\rho

is a Hilbert space isometry

and inner products are also preserved.

Next, observe that if
\lambda u\in L
for some
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
, then we have that
e_{\lambda(x)u}=e^{2\pi i\lambda u\cdot x}
,
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, is constant on lines perpendicular to

. In other words,
e_{\lambda u}(x)=\widetilde{e_{\lambda}}(\pi_{L}(x))=e_{\lambda}(\pi_{L}(x))
.

This implies that
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
is a spectrum for
\pi_{L}\rho
if and only if
\Lambda u\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is a spectrum for
\rho
, as we had to show.

-axis.

5.
The case of irrational
t

In this section we prove Theorem
1.3
. In what follows
\rho
is the probability measure
\mu\times\delta_{0}+\delta_{0}\times\mu
where
\mu
is one-half Lebesgue measure on the interval
[t,t+1]
, where
t\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus{\left\{{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\}}
is fixed.

Our first goal is to show that for all points
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\Lambda
the two coordinates are comparable.

Lemma 5.1
.

If
\Lambda
, with
0\in\Lambda
, is a spectrum of
\rho
then it is not possible to have an infinite sequence
\lambda^{n}=(\lambda^{n}_{1},\lambda^{n}_{2})\in\Lambda
tending to infinity such that
\lambda^{n}_{2}=o(\lambda^{n}_{1})
or
\lambda^{n}_{1}=o(\lambda^{n}_{2})
.

In other words there is a constant
K>1
such that for all
\lambda\in\Lambda\setminus{\left\{{(0,0)}\right\}}
we have

(7)

K^{-1}{\left|{\lambda_{1}}\right|}\leq{\left|{\lambda_{2}}\right|}\leq K{\left|{\lambda_{1}}\right|}.

Proof.

For any two different points
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}),\nu=(x,y)\in\Lambda
we have, from (
1
), that

(8)

{\left|{\frac{\sin\pi(x-\lambda_{1})}{\pi(x-\lambda_{1})}}\right|}={\left|{\frac{\sin\pi(y-\lambda_{2})}{\pi(y-\lambda_{2})}}\right|}.

The set
\Lambda
is infinite and there is a positive lower bound on the distance of any two of its points (this is true for all spectra), so it must be the case that its points tend to infinity.

Fix a point
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\Lambda\setminus{\left\{{(0,0)}\right\}}
and apply (
8
) while
\nu=(x,y)\to\infty
, with
y=o(x)
, is a point of
\Lambda
.
It is clear that the left hand side is
o(\cdot)
of the right hand side, unless
\sin\pi(y-\lambda_{2})\to 0
(for some subsequence of the points
\nu
, which we may consider to be the whole sequence), which is equivalent to
{\left\{{y-\lambda_{2}}\right\}}\to 0
, where
{\left\{{\cdot}\right\}}
denotes the fractional part. Using the same reasoning with the point
(0,0)\in\Lambda
in place of
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
we also obtain that
{\left\{{y}\right\}}\to 0
. Together these two imply that
\lambda_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Thus we showed that all
\lambda_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. From (
8
) with
(x,y)=(0,0)
it follows that if
\lambda_{2}=0
then we also have
\lambda_{1}=0
and if
\lambda_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus{\left\{{0}\right\}}
then we also have
\lambda_{1}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus{\left\{{0}\right\}}
. We have proved that
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
which is impossible by the results in
[
LLP21
, Theorem 4.2]
(but see also our Remark
2.1
). This concludes the proof that we cannot find a sequence
\nu=(x,y)\in\Lambda
tending to infinity with
y=o(x)
or, by symmetry,
x=o(y)
. Also by
[
LLP21
, Theorem 4.2]
it follows that for
\lambda\in\Lambda\setminus{\left\{{(0,0)}\right\}}
we have
\lambda_{1}\neq 0
and
\lambda_{2}\neq 0
(multiplicity one, in the language of
[
LLP21
]
). From this observation and the impossibility of
\lambda_{2}=o(\lambda_{1})
or
\lambda_{1}=o(\lambda_{2})
the bound (
7
) follows for some
K>1
.

∎

Next, we show that the spectrality assumption for
\rho
leads to a certain one-dimensional tiling, which will allow us to deduce properties of the projection of
\Lambda
to the coordinate axes.

Lemma 5.2
.

If
\Lambda
is a spectrum of
\rho
then we have the level-2 tiling of the real line

2=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{\left|{\widehat{{\mathbbm{1}}_{[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]}}}\right|}^{2}(x-\lambda_{1})\ \ \ (x\in{\mathbb{R}}).

Proof.

Consider the function
f\in L^{2}(\rho)
which is
0
on the
y
-axis and equal to
\phi
on
[t,t+1]\times{\left\{{0}\right\}}
for some
\phi\in L^{2}([t,t+1])
. Expanding
f
with respect to
\Lambda
we get

2{\left\|{\phi}\right\|}_{L^{2}([t,t+1])}^{2}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{\left|{\widehat{\phi}(\lambda_{1})}\right|}^{2}.

Picking
\phi(s)={\mathbbm{1}}_{[t,t+1]}(s)e^{2\pi ixs}
for some
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
we get

(9)

2=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{\left|{\widehat{{\mathbbm{1}}_{[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]}}}\right|}^{2}(x-\lambda_{1}).

∎

The next Lemma shows the very crucial property of the set
\Lambda_{1}
, that it is of finite complexity: in any fixed-length window on the line we may only see a finite set of different patterns of
\Lambda_{1}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

The tiling in Lemma
5.2
is of finite complexity. This means that there are finitely many different gaps among successive points in
\Lambda_{1}={\left\{{\lambda_{1}:\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\Lambda}\right\}}
.

Proof.

Let us write the set
\Lambda={\left\{{\lambda^{n}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\right\}}
in increasing order of the first coordinates as follows

\cdots\leq\lambda^{-1}_{1}\leq\lambda^{0}_{1}=0\leq\lambda^{1}_{1}\leq\lambda^{2}_{1}<\cdots.

Our first goal is to show that
\lambda^{1}_{1}
can take only finitely many values.

By the tiling property, there is an absolute constant
C
such that
\lambda^{1}_{1}\leq C
, so
\lambda^{1}
belongs to the vertical strip

V={\left\{{(x,y):0\leq x\leq C}\right\}}.

From (
7
) we also know that
\lambda^{1}
belongs to the union of two sectors

S={\left\{{(x,y):x\geq 0,\ K^{-1}x\leq{\left|{y}\right|}\leq Kx}\right\}}.

See Fig.
6
.

[formula]

Figure 6.
The shaded region is
V\cap S
. Only finitely many of the lines
y-x=k/(2t+1)
,
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, intersect this region and on each of these lines only an interval is contained in the region.

Finally we already know that we must have

\lambda^{1}_{2}-\lambda^{1}_{1}=\frac{k}{2t+1}

for some
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
(remember that we have assumed
t\neq-1/2
), and it follows that
{\left|{k}\right|}
must be bounded because only finitely many of the straight lines
y-x=k/(2t+1)
intersect
V\cap S
. For each of the finitely many eligible values of
k
there are finitely many points on the line
y-x=k/(2t+1)
which are zeros of
\widehat{\rho}
and belong to
V
, since on each such line the zeros of
\widehat{\rho}
are a discrete set as they correspond the values of
x
such that

\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}=\pm\frac{\sin\pi(x+\frac{k}{2t+1})}{\pi(x+\frac{k}{2t+1})},

and these are zeros of two entire functions of
x
that are not identically zero.
It follows that the possible locations for
\lambda^{1}_{1}
are finitely many. Let us call the set of these locations
L\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
.

By translating
\Lambda
by
-\lambda^{n}
we obtain that the only possible values for
\lambda^{n+1}_{1}-\lambda^{n}_{1}
are again the set
L
. We have proved that the tiling (
9
) is a tiling of finite complexity.

∎

In the next Lemma we use a result
[
IK13
]
which says that tilings of finite complexity, whose set of translates has a spectral gap, are necessarily periodic. This was first used
[
IK13
]
to prove that spectra of bounded subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}
are necessarily periodic.

Lemma 5.4
.

The projections of
\Lambda
onto the
x
- and
y
-axes,
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
, are periodic sets, with periods in
\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{Z}}
. In other words, there are positive
T_{1},T_{2}\in\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
\Lambda_{1}=\Lambda_{1}+T_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}=\Lambda_{2}+T_{2}
.

Proof.

From Lemma
5.3
we know that the tiling (
9
) is a tiling of finite complexity. It was proved in
[
IK13
]
, though not stated precisely in this form (see
[
KL16
, Theorem 5.1]
), that in any tiling of finite complexity with a spectral gap, the set of translates is periodic. To have a spectral gap, the distribution
\widehat{\delta_{\Lambda_{1}}}
needs to vanish on an interval. To see that this is the case here we need to apply
[
KL16
]
[Theorem 4.1] with

f={\left|{\widehat{{\mathbbm{1}}_{[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]}}}\right|}^{2}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}).

By the conclusion of that result we obtain that

{\rm supp\,}{\widehat{\delta_{\Lambda_{1}}}}\subseteq{\left\{{0}\right\}}\cup{\left\{{\widehat{f}=0}\right\}}.

But
\widehat{f}={\mathbbm{1}}_{[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]}*{\mathbbm{1}}_{[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]}
so that
{\left\{{\widehat{f}=0}\right\}}
is the complement of
(-1,1)
, and we see that
\widehat{\delta_{\Lambda_{1}}}
vanishes in any proper subinterval of
(0,1)
. We conclude that
\Lambda_{1}
is periodic. Let
T_{1}>0
be one of its periods. By symmetry there must also exist a positive real number
T_{2}
such that
\Lambda_{2}=\Lambda_{2}+T_{2}
.

It is easy to see from the tilings that
T_{1},T_{2}\in\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{Z}}
. Indeed, since the integral of
{\left|{\widehat{{\mathbbm{1}}_{[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]}}}\right|}^{2}
is 1 it follows that the integral of the right hand side of (
9
) over a period, which is
2T_{1}
by looking at the left hand side of (
9
), must be equal to the total number of copies in a period, call it
n_{1}
, which gives
2T_{1}=n_{1}
, i.e.
T_{1}\in\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{Z}}
, and similarly
T_{2}\in\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{Z}}
.

∎

In the next Lemma we exploit the fact that exponentials with frequencies on the line
y=x
cannot span
L^{2}(\rho)
as the projections of the two segments on that line overlap.

Lemma 5.5
.

If
\Lambda
is a spectrum for
\rho
then
\Lambda
has infinitely many points not on the line
y=x
.

Proof.

The exponentials of the form
e_{(\lambda,\lambda)}(x,y)=e^{2\pi i(\lambda x+\lambda y)}
are constant along any straight line that is perpendicular to the line
y=x
. Thus the exponentials with frequencies in
\Lambda\cap{\left\{{(x,y):y=x}\right\}}
can only generate part of the subspace of
L^{2}(\rho)
which consists of functions that are symmetric with respect to the mapping
(x,y)\to(y,x)
. Clearly this subspace has infinite co-dimension in
L^{2}(\rho)
(its orthogonal complement contains all functions which are negated under the transformation
(x,y)\to(y,x)
), so infinitely many exponentials are required outside
y=x
if the exponentials with frequencies in
\Lambda
are to generate
L^{2}(\rho)
.
∎

The irrationality of
t
is exploited next to show that there is at most one point of the spectrum on each line
y-x=k/(2t+1)
for

.

Lemma 5.6
.

If
0\in\Lambda
is a spectrum of
\rho
and

then on each line of the form

y-x=\frac{k}{2t+1},\ \ \ \text{ for some }k\in{\mathbb{Z}},

there is at most one point of
\Lambda
.

Proof.

It is easy to see from (
1
) that

(10)

{\left\{{\widehat{\rho}=0}\right\}}\cap{\left\{{(x,x):x\in{\mathbb{R}}}\right\}}={\left\{{(n,n):0\neq n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\right\}}.

Since
\Lambda-\Lambda\subseteq{\left\{{\widehat{\rho}=0}\right\}}\cup{\left\{{0}\right\}}
we deduce that any two different points of
\Lambda
on the same line

L_{k}={\left\{{(x,y):x-y=\frac{k}{2t+1}}\right\}},\ \ \ k\in{\mathbb{Z}},

must differ by a vector of the form
(n,n)
, with
0\neq n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Let us first assume
k\neq 0
.

We now show that if
t
is irrational then there is at most one point of
\Lambda
on each line
L_{k}
with
k\neq 0
. Indeed, suppose that

(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}),(\lambda_{1}+\nu,\lambda_{2}+\nu)\in L_{k}\cap\Lambda,\text{ for some }\nu\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

Then we have

(11)

\displaystyle e^{\pi i(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})(2t+1)}\frac{\sin\pi\lambda_{1}}{\pi\lambda_{1}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sin\pi\lambda_{2}}{\pi\lambda_{2}}

(12)

\displaystyle e^{\pi i(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})(2t+1)}\frac{\sin(\pi\lambda_{1}+\pi\nu)}{\pi\lambda_{1}+\pi\nu}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sin(\pi\lambda_{2}+\pi\nu)}{\pi\lambda_{2}+\pi\nu}.

Since
\sin(x+\pi\nu)=(-1)^{\nu}\sin x
for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
we can, assuming that both
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are not integers, divide (
12
) by (
11
) and get

1+\frac{\nu}{\lambda_{1}}=1+\frac{\nu}{\lambda_{2}}.

Since
k\neq 0
it follows that
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
so the last equation implies
\nu=0
, as we had to show. It remains to ensure that
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\notin{\mathbb{Z}}
. If one of them is an integer then so is the other, by (
11

\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}=\frac{k}{2t+1}
we obtain that

, contrary to our assumption.

Let us now show the result for
k=0
.

Assume that
\nu\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus{\left\{{0}\right\}}
and that
(-\nu,-\nu)\in\Lambda
. From Lemma
5.5
there exists at least one point of
\Lambda
outside the line

. Let us call this point
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
. We immediately have equations (
11
) and (
12
) and by the same argument as above we arrive at a contradiction.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Assuming
\Lambda
to be a spectrum of
\rho
, with
0\in\Lambda
, we obtain from Lemma
5.4
that

(13)

\Lambda_{1}=T_{1}{\mathbb{Z}}+A,\ \ \Lambda_{2}=T_{2}{\mathbb{Z}}+B,

for some positive
T_{1},T_{2}\in\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{Z}}
and some finite sets
A\subseteq[0,T_{1})
and
B\subseteq[0,T_{2})
.

If
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\Lambda
then, by Theorem
2.1
, there is
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that

\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}=\frac{k}{2t+1}.

From (
13
) we have that
\lambda_{1}=mT_{1}+a
and
\lambda_{2}=nT_{2}+b
, for some integers
m,n
and some

,

so, taking fractional parts,

{\left\{{2\lambda_{2}-2\lambda_{1}}\right\}}={\left\{{2b-2a}\right\}}={\left\{{\frac{2k}{2t+1}}\right\}}.

The quantity
{\left\{{2b-2a}\right\}}
takes only finitely many values as

are finite sets. For different
k

{\left\{{\frac{2k}{2t+1}}\right\}}
are different since

is irrational. This means that there are at most finitely many
k

\Lambda
which contradicts Lemma

.

∎
