YAP Promotes Microtubule Growth to Facilitate Sarcomere Disassembly in Adult Cardiomyocytes
Authors: Feng Zhang, Jenna K. Cusick, Jie Liang, Fangfei Wang, Andrew T. Fernandes, Jacob T. Gafranek, Joshua S. Waxman, Zhiyuan Chen, Katherine E. Yutzey, Shijie Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Mature mammalian cardiomyocytes (CMs) develop compact sarcomeric structures that inhibit proliferation. Consequently, CMs must dedifferentiate to a fetus-like state, which is accompanied by sarcomere disassembly, to enable successful cytokinesis. However, the regulation and coordination of CM dedifferentiation, cell cycle progression, and sarcomere reorganization remain unclear.METHODS:We generated adenovirus and adeno-associated virus (MyoAAV) vectors expressing YAP5SA and YAP5SA-S94A under Xon control for LMI070-inducible protein expression. We also developed MyoAAV-cTnT-Tuba1b-shRNA-miR30 for cardiomyocyte-specific knockdown of Tuba1b. These tools were used to investigate CM dedifferentiation and proliferation and sarcomere disassembly. We also performed Cleavage Under Targets and Release Using Nuclease to map the genome-wide binding sites of YAP5SA and YAP5SA-S94A in combination with RNA sequencing to identify YAP target genes. In addition, time-course live-imaging analysis was used to evaluate microtubule and sarcomere dynamics in adult CMs.RESULTS:We show that microtubule expression and network density decline with cardiac maturation. Overexpression of YAP5SA, a constitutively active YAP mutant, promotes microtubule growth by stabilizing microtubule dynamics, leading to CM dedifferentiation, cell cycle re-entry, and sarcomere disassembly. In contrast, colchicine blocks these processes and significantly attenuates YAP-induced cardiac regeneration. Live imaging reveals a distinct mode of sarcomere disassembly driven by enhanced microtubule polymerization, wherein microtubule plus-ends directly interact with α-actinin and displace α-actinin fragments, thereby facilitating sarcomere breakdown. Furthermore, the YAP5SA-S94A mutation, which disrupts the YAP and TEA domain interaction, significantly reduces YAP5SA-induced microtubule growth, sarcomere disassembly, and cell cycle activity. Mechanistically, cleavage under targets and release using nuclease combined with RNA sequencing identified direct YAP targets, including Ajuba and Tuba1b, which are critical for microtubule growth. CM-specific knockdown of Tuba1b attenuates YAP-driven sarcomere disassembly.CONCLUSIONS:These findings identify microtubule networks as an essential regulator modulating CM dedifferentiation and sarcomere reorganization, which is critical for CM cytokinesis and cardiac regenerative repair.

The heart cannot repair itself after injury because lost cardiomyocytes (CMs) cannot be replenished. Although new CMs can arise from preexisting ones, this occurs at a very low rate in adult mammals.1,2 After birth, CMs undergo a maturation process characterized by increased cell size, a denser and more complex sarcomere structure, and changes in electrophysiology and calcium handling. Metabolism also shifts from glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation to meet the heightened energy demands. These progressive maturation steps are accompanied by alterations in gene expression and alternative splicing.3 Fully matured CMs can generate the contractile force necessary to pump blood throughout the body. However, these developmental changes also create barriers to cell division, leading to CM cell cycle exit within the first postnatal week in mice.4 As a result, adult CMs exhibit limited self-renewal capacity, a central mechanism underlying the pathogenesis of ischemic heart failure.

Reactivating the intrinsic proliferative ability of CMs, similar to what occurs naturally in fish, amphibians, and neonatal mammals to repair cardiac damage, has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for myocardial regeneration. Various approaches to stimulate adult CM proliferation in adult mammals have shown potential for improving heart function after injury.5,6 It is believed that proliferating CMs must first undergo dedifferentiation, a reversal of the maturation process, including the breakdown of sarcomere structures to facilitate therapeutic cell division.7–9 Indeed, factors such as Neuregulin1/ErbB,10,11,12 the Hippo/YAP pathway,13–15 Yamanaka factors,16 microRNAs,17,18 and deletion of Meis1 and Hoxb13,19 all of which promote CM proliferation, have been shown to induce CM dedifferentiation and sarcomere disassembly. Similarly, metabolic reprogramming—shifting from fatty acid oxidation to glycolysis through inactivation of Cpt1b20 or succinate dehydrogenase21 or by reducing the production of reactive oxygen species22,23—has been found to promote adult CM proliferation, accompanied by dedifferentiation and sarcomere breakdown. This dedifferentiation process, including sarcomere disassembly and reorganization, is considered a prerequisite for CM cytokinesis.

The Hippo signaling pathway, an evolutionarily conserved kinase cascade involving multiple adaptors and effectors, plays a critical role in regulating CM proliferation and organ size. When the Hippo pathway is active, its downstream effectors, YAP and TAZ, which act as transcriptional coactivators, are phosphorylated, preventing their accumulation in the nucleus. In the absence of phosphorylation, YAP (and TAZ) translocates to the nucleus and interacts with TEA domain (TEAD) transcription factors to activate gene expression programs that promote cell proliferation, differentiation, and organ growth.24 Our recent studies demonstrated that a gene therapy strategy involving knockdown of Salvador (Sav), a core Hippo pathway component, promotes cardiac functional recovery and regeneration in a pig model of myocardial infarction without inducing arrhythmias, highlighting its strong translational potential.25,26 Furthermore, direct overexpression of YAP5SA (a constitutively active mutant form of YAP) in CMs induces robust CM proliferation and myocardial thickening, representing one of the most potent models of adult CM proliferation.13 Recent studies have also explored the therapeutic potential of adeno-associated virus (AAV)–mediated YAP5SA gene delivery in both mouse and pig models of ischemic heart disease.27,28 These findings suggest that tightly controlled YAP activity represents a promising therapeutic approach. YAP induces dedifferentiation and drives sarcomere disassembly to facilitate CM proliferation. Although YAP is known to transcriptionally regulate cytoskeleton-associated genes, it remains unclear how these changes of gene expression affect sarcomere organization.29 The mechanisms by which YAP coordinates the transition from a mature to a proliferative CM state—including sarcomere disassembly—are still poorly understood.

To gain insights into how dedifferentiation reverses cytoskeletal reorganization to enable CM division, we examined cytoskeletal protein expression during CM maturation and found that tubulin levels and the expression of microtubule-associated genes were significantly reduced in adult CMs compared with neonatal CMs, accompanied by a marked decrease in microtubule network density. In contrast, overexpression of YAP in adult mouse CMs increased tubulin expression and promoted microtubule network growth during CM dedifferentiation. This microtubule densification was essential for YAP-induced CM proliferation and dedifferentiation, sarcomere disassembly, and cardiac regeneration. Disruption of microtubules with colchicine significantly inhibited these YAP-driven processes. Mechanistically, live-cell imaging of microtubule dynamics revealed that YAP overexpression in CMs enhances microtubule polymerization and reduces depolymerization. The stabilized microtubule plus-ends physically interact with and displace the disrupted α-actinin fragments, facilitating sarcomere disassembly. Cleavage under targets and release using nuclease (CUT&RUN) and RNA-sequencing analyses identified direct YAP target genes, including Ajuba, and Tuba1b, as essential mediators of YAP-dependent microtubule dynamics and growth.

Methods

Study Design

The objective of this study was to determine the molecular mechanism by which YAP regulates microtubule growth during CM dedifferentiation and sarcomere disassembly in proliferating CMs, processes that are essential for adult CM cytokinesis and regenerative cardiac repair. We performed molecular analyses using mouse models and isolated neonatal mouse CMs, neonatal rat CMs, and adult CMs. Time-course live-imaging analyses were conducted to evaluate the dynamics of the microtubule network and sarcomere structure, as well as their interactions. In addition, we performed functional studies in mouse models of myocardial infarction to assess regenerative cardiac repair. Considering rigor, reproducibility, and statistics, the sample size of animal cohorts in the proposed studies was selected to provide ≥80% power (α=0.05) to detect the significant differences found in preliminary results. Mouse numbers used in each experiment are indicated in each figure legend. Mixed numbers of male and female mice were included in each study. Mice from different genotypes were grouped in a blinded and randomized fashion, and the investigators were blinded to the mouse genotypes and treatments that mice received during the data analysis.

Mice

All mouse experiments were conducted in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center (Cincinnati, OH) under protocol 2023-0007. Mice were housed under a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle (lights on from 7 am–7 pm) and provided standard food pellets and water ad libitum. Wild-type mice used in this study were of the ICR background. Yapflox/flox mice were of a mixed C57BL/6 and 129S1 background and carried 2 LoxP sites flanking exon 1 of the Yap1 gene.

Colchicine Treatment

For chronic in vivo colchicine treatment, 8- to 12-week-old male ICR wild-type mice were randomly assigned to either the saline or colchicine treatment group. Colchicine was administered intraperitoneally every other day following a modified protocol based on References 2 and 3. The dosing regimen involved gradual escalation (0.5, 0.75, and 1 mg/kg body weight) to improve tolerance, after which mice were maintained at 1 mg/kg. An equivalent volume of saline was administered as the vehicle control.

For in vitro assays, adult mouse CMs were treated with 1 µmol/L colchicine in culture medium for several days before collection for downstream analyses. An equivalent volume of PBS was added as the control.

LMI070 Treatment

The methods are adapted from Reference 30. For in vivo assays, LMI070 was administered at a dose of 5 mg/kg body weight (MedChemExpress, HY-19620) or vehicle control through intraperitoneal injection every other day. For in vitro assays, adult mouse CMs or neonatal rat CMs were treated with 1 µmol/L LMI070 in culture medium for 2 days before being harvested for downstream analyses.

Adenovirus Production

To generate recombinant adenoviruses expressing YAP5SA and YAP5SA-S94A, the coding sequences for Xon-YAP5SA and YAP5SA-S94A were subcloned into the pShuttle-CMV plasmid downstream of the CMV promoter. The resulting pShuttle-CMV-Xon-YAP5SA construct was linearized with PmeI and electroporated into AdEasier BJ5183 electrocompetent cells harboring the adenoviral backbone plasmid pAdEasy-1, enabling homologous recombination. Positive recombinant clones were identified by restriction enzyme digestion and subsequently amplified in Escherichia coli DH5α. The final recombinant plasmid was linearized with PacI and transfected into HEK-293 cells with Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher, L3000001). Adenoviruses were harvested from HEK-293 cells 14 to 20 days after transfection and amplified through multiple rounds of infection. Viral particles were released by repeated freeze-thaw cycles and stored at −80° C. The AdEasy system was used following the protocol described by Luo et al.31

AAV Production and Delivery

Full-length Xon-YAP5SA and Xon-YAP5SA-S94A were amplified by polymerase chain reaction and individually cloned into the SalI and HindIII restriction sites of the AAV–cardiac troponin T (cTnT) vector using the NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Cloning Kit (E5520S). GFP (green fluorescent protein) cDNA had been previously cloned into the same AAV-cTnT vector. AAV-Xon-YAP5SA, AAV-Xon-YAP5SA-S94A, and AAV-GFP were produced in house with the MyoAAV 1A capsid. Recombinant viruses were harvested from HEK293 cells and culture media and then purified by ultracentrifugation with an iodixanol gradient.32 AAV titers were quantified by TaqMan-based quantitative polymerase chain reaction using the C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories). For in vivo delivery, 1×1011 genomic copies of AAV-GFP, AAV-Xon-YAP5SA, or AAV-Xon-YAP5SA-S94A were injected directly into postnatal day 1 or day 2 ICR pups.

Live Imaging of Microtubule Dynamics by EB3

Isolated neonatal rat ventricular myocytes (NRVMs) and adult CMs were infected with an adenovirus encoding EB3-GFP. After 48 hours, live-cell imaging was performed with a Yokogawa SoRa W1 spinning disk confocal microscope equipped with a 60× oil immersion objective (NA 1.4). Cells expressing EB3-GFP with signal localized to microtubule plus-ends were imaged for 4 minutes at a frame rate of 1 frame per second. Image sequences were blinded, gaussian blurred, and Z projected with ImageJ (National Institutes of Health) to generate kymographs. Microtubule dynamics were quantified by manually tracing EB3-GFP comets to determine run time, distance, and velocity. Catastrophe and rescue events were counted per kymograph, and their frequencies were calculated per cell by dividing the number of events by the total duration of growth (for catastrophes) or shrinkage (for rescues). A minimum of 3 independent CM isolations were performed per experimental group.

Cleavage Under Targets and Release Using Nuclease

Neonatal rat CMs overexpressing Ad-GFP, Ad-Xon-YAP5SA, or Ad-Xon-YAP5SA-S94A for 2 days were harvested and washed 3 times with CUT&RUN wash buffer (20 mmol/L HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mmol/L NaCl, 0.5 mmol/L spermidine [Sigma-Aldrich], and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail [Sigma-Aldrich]). Cells were transferred to 1.5-mL tubes containing 50 μL wash buffer and incubated with BioMagPlus Concanavalin A magnetic beads (Polysciences). Samples were incubated overnight (16 hours) at 4° C in antibody incubation buffer (20 mmol/L HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mmol/L NaCl, 0.5 mmol/L spermidine, 2 mmol/L EDTA, 0.02% digitonin [Thermo Fisher], and 1× EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail [Roche]) with rabbit anti-YAP antibody (1:100; Novus, NB110-58358) or mouse anti-YAP antibody (1:100; Santa Cruz, sc-101199). After unbound antibody was washed away, 500 ng/mL protein AG-MNase (made in house) was added at a 1:200 ratio and incubated for 3 hours at 4° C. Enzyme activation was initiated by adding CaCl2 to a final concentration of 2 mmol/L and incubating for 20 minutes at 4° C. The reaction was quenched with 1/10 volume of 10× STOP buffer (1700 mmol/L NaCl, 100 mmol/L EDTA, 20 mmol/L EGTA, 250 μg/mL RNase A, 250 μg/mL glycogen [Thermo Fisher]). Protein-DNA complexes were released by 10-minute incubation at 37° C, followed by centrifugation at 20 000g for 5 minutes at 4° C. Supernatants were transferred to LoBind tubes. DNA was released by incubation with 1/100 volume of 10% SDS and 1/80 volume of 2.5 mg/mL Proteinase K (Thermo Fisher) for at least 1 hour at 55° C. DNA was then purified by phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol extraction followed by ethanol precipitation with glycogen and resuspended in nuclease-free water.

CUT&RUN Data Processing and Peak Analysis

CUT&RUN libraries were sequenced and processed by trimming adapters and aligning reads to the rat reference genome (rn7) using Bowtie2. Duplicate and low-quality reads (MAPQ < 30) were removed with SAMtools. Peaks were called with MACS2 using matched immunoglobulin G controls to define background. For quantification, consensus peak regions across replicates were generated with BEDTools. Peak scores (−log10 adjusted P values from MACS2) were assigned to genomic intervals and summed within each gene body ±2-kb flanking regions, yielding a total peak score per gene. Genes with cumulative peak scores >200 were defined as YAP5SA-positive binding targets. Peak scores were further compared between YAP5SA and YAP5SA-S94A to identify condition-specific enrichment. Signal intensity profiles and heat maps were generated with deepTools. Gene ontology analysis of YAP5SA-positive targets was performed to assess enriched biological pathways.

Using MACS2 peak calling, we summed all peak scores located within the genomic region of each gene to obtain a total peak score per gene. Genes with a cumulative score >200 were defined as YAP5SA-positive binding sites. Genes with a YAP5SA minus YAP5SA-S94A cumulative score >50 were defined as YAP5SA-specific binding sites.

Echocardiography

Left ventricular systolic function was assessed with 2-dimensional echocardiography on a VisualSonics Vevo 3100 system equipped with a 40-MHz transducer. Mice were anesthetized with 0.5% to 1.0% isoflurane, and thoracic hair was removed to optimize image acquisition. Mice were positioned supine on a heated platform to maintain body temperature. To evaluate cardiac function, M-mode images were obtained at the level of the papillary muscles in the short-axis view. Ejection fraction and fractional shortening were calculated from measurements of left ventricular internal diameter in diastole and systole. At least 8 mice were used per group. Group sizes for in vivo studies varied and are specified in the figure legends. Echocardiographic analyses were performed by investigators blinded to the treatment groups.

Quantification and Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean±SEM. Comparisons between 2 groups were performed with either the Mann-Whitney U test or a 2-tailed paired t test as appropriate. For comparisons involving multiple groups, 1-way or 2-way ANOVA was used, followed by Tukey or Bonferroni post hoc tests as specified in the figure legends. A value of P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Exact P values are indicated in the corresponding figures when statistical analyses were performed.

All data and supporting materials have been included with the published article. The sequencing data generated in this study have been deposited in the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus under accession number GSE327161.

Results

Microtubule Networks Decrease During Heart Maturation

To understand the cytoskeletal shift during heart maturation, we analyzed the expression of cytoskeletal proteins in heart samples from postnatal day 0, postnatal day 10, and 3-month-old mice. An interesting finding was that α- and β-tubulin expression progressively declined during postnatal maturation, reaching the lowest levels in 3-month-old hearts (Figure 1A–1C). In contrast, sarcomeric proteins, including α-actin and cTnT, showed a modest increase, while α-actinin levels remained largely unchanged (Figure 1A and Figure S1A–S1C). We also noticed a slight decrease in the levels of YAP and an increase in YAP S112/YAP ratio through postnatal day 0 to 3-month-old hearts (Figure 1A and Figure S1D–S1F), this is consistent with the previous study,33 implying that YAP may be involving in regulating cardiac maturation. Further analysis of the microtubule network by immunofluorescence staining revealed decreased microtubule network in adult CMs compared with neonatal CMs (Figure 1D–1F). RNA sequencing from the published database34 revealed that the most significantly downregulated genes in postnatal day 23 compared with postnatal day 1 mouse hearts were associated with microtubule binding (Figure 1G). Of note, this decreased expression of microtubule-associated genes is conserved across species, including human, monkey, rabbit, and zebrafish (Figure 1H and 1I and Figure S1G–S1I). Together, these results suggested that the microtubule network significantly decreased during the CM maturation, which correlates with decreased YAP expression.

YAP Enhances the Microtubule Network in Adult CMs

Given the role of YAP in cytoskeletal reorganization and CM dedifferentiation, we next investigated whether YAP also regulates the microtubule network. To better study YAP-regulated microtubules in CM proliferation, we used the Xon system, an inducible alternative splicing-based platform that enables precise, dose-dependent control of gene expression using the small-molecule LMI070 (Figure S2A–S2C).30 We adapted Xon to generate cardiac-specific Adeno and AAV vectors encoding a constitutively active YAP mutant, YAP5SA (Ad-Xon-YAP5SA and AAV-Xon-YAP5SA), under the control of the cTnT promoter and tagged with Flag for detection (Figure S2A). AAV-Xon-YAP5SA was delivered to neonatal mice (postnatal day 9) through subcutaneous injection. Three weeks later, a single dose of LMI070 was administered intraperitoneally, and heart tissue was collected at 2, 4, and 6 days after induction (Figure S2B). Western blotting confirmed robust YAP5SA expression at day 2, which declined by days 4 and 6, indicating an in vivo half-life of 2 to 4 days (Figure S2C). This system provides a powerful tool to temporally regulate YAP5SA expression in CMs for in vitro and in vivo mechanistic studies of YAP-driven CM proliferation.

We injected the AAV-Xon-YAP5SA virus into neonatal mice at postnatal days 4 through 7, and the AAV-GFP is used as the control; when the mice reached 8 weeks of age, we injected 3 doses of LMI070 to induce the YAP5SA overexpression and harvested the hearts 5 days after the first LMI070 injection (Figure 2A). Before LMI070 injection, there were no differences between the mice receiving AAV-Xon-YAP5SA and those receiving AAV-GFP. However, after YAP5SA induction, some mice receiving AAV-Xon-YAP5SA started displaying a hunched posture 4 days after the first LMI070 injection, reminiscent of phenotypes seen in mice with YAP5SA overexpression, which typically died 5 to 6 days after YAP5SA overexpression.13 We observed an increased heart/body ratio in mice that received AAV-Xon-YAP5SA compared with those that received AAV-GFP (Figure 2B), indicating increased CM proliferation. Western blotting of whole-heart lysates revealed ≈10 times increase in α-tubulin levels and 4 times increase in β-tubulin levels in YAP5SA-overexpressed hearts compared to controls. In contrast, cardiac α-actinin levels were decreased, and cTnT remained unchanged (Figure 2C–2E and Figure S2D and S2E). Immunofluorescence staining with α-tubulin antibodies showed a significant increase in the microtubule network in CMs with YAP5SA overexpression, as indicated by higher α-tubulin density and signal intensity (Figure 2F–2I). Quantification showed a significant increase in microtubule density (from 5% in controls to 26% in YAP5SA-overexpressing CMs). Similar results were also observed in NRVMs and CMs overexpressing wild-type YAP (Figure 2J and 2K and Figure S3A), indicating that YAP promotes microtubule growth.

To determine whether YAP regulates microtubules in physiological context, we treated NRVMs with the YAP inhibitor verteporfin.35 Verteporfin treatment significantly reduced α-tubulin expression in a dose-dependent manner, suggesting that YAP activity is required for maintaining microtubule levels (Figure S3B–S3D). To further investigate the role of YAP in regulating microtubules, we isolated neonatal mouse CMs from Yapflox/flox mice and infected them with adenovirus (Ad)-Cre to delete the Yap alleles. Compared with the Ad-GFP–infected controls, Yapflox/flox CMs infected with Ad-Cre had significantly decreased α-tubulin protein levels (Figure 2L and 2M). Together, these results suggest that YAP is essential for maintaining the expression of α-tubulin under physiological conditions.

YAP Regulates Microtubule Nucleation and Dynamics

Microtubules are highly dynamic. The microtubule network consists of α-tubulin and β-tubulin heterodimers that polymerize into long, rigid tube structures. The increased microtubule network in YAP5SA-overexpressed CMs could be attributed to the higher expression of the tubulins, more microtubule nucleation (de novo formation of microtubules), and stabilized microtubule network (more polymerization, less depolymerization). To test this, we performed a fractionation assay to separate free tubulin from polymerized tubulin in the dynamic microtubule pool.36 YAP5SA significantly increased both free and polymerized α-tubulin expression, as well as the ratio of polymerized α-tubulin in the microtubule pool (Figure 3A–3E). To further study whether YAP5SA regulates microtubule dynamics, we infected the adult mouse CMs with adenovirus containing GFP-labeled end-binding protein (EB3-GFP)37 to directly visualize the plus-end of growing microtubules by time-lapse imaging (Videos 1–6). EB proteins such as EB1 and EB3 are microtubule plus-end tracking proteins that selectively bind to the growing ends of microtubules. Therefore, EB1/EB3 labeling specifically marks actively polymerizing microtubule plus-ends and is commonly used to monitor microtubule dynamics.38 The dynamic properties of microtubules can be quantified as events that mark their transitions from growing (polymerization) to shrinking (depolymerization) states. These events consist of catastrophes (transition from growth to shrinkage) and rescues (transition from shrinkage to growth; Figure 3F). We treated the CMs with Ad-Xon-YAP5SA to control the YAP5SA expression. Before the induction of YAP5SA overexpression with LMI070, microtubule dynamics were similar between CMs treated with Ad-Xon-YAP5SA and control (Figure 3F and Videos 1 and 2). However, 72 hours after LMI070 treatment, the microtubules in YAP5SA-overexpressing CMs were more stable, showing an increased growth time and longer growth distances (Figure 3G and 3H and Videos 3 and 4). This increased stability was due to a significant decrease in microtubule catastrophe frequency, rescue frequency, and shortening rate; the microtubule growth speed was not significantly different in the control and YAP5SA-overexpressed CMs (Figure 3I–3L). In addition, we assessed microtubule nucleation using a microtubule regrowth assay. NRVMs overexpressing YAP5SA displayed markedly faster microtubule regrowth after the removal of the microtubule destabilizer nocodazole (Figure 3M and 3N). Together, these results suggest that YAP enhances microtubule nucleation and stabilizes microtubule polymerization.

The Microtubule Network Is Required for YAP-Induced Sarcomere Disassembly

We observed a dramatic morphology change in cultured adult CMs starting 48 hours after LMI070 treatment. The rod-shaped adult CMs became rounded and developed protrusions (Figure S4 and Videos 5 and 6). Seventy-two hours after YAP5SA overexpression, ≈60% of CMs exhibited this morphological shift, whereas no obvious changes were observed in the Ad-GFP-infected controls (Figure S5A–S5D). Immunofluorescence staining of α-actinin, a critical sarcomere protein localized at the Z disc, showed a transition from a striated pattern to a disrupted distribution spreading from the periphery toward the center in YAP5SA-overexpressing CMs 72 hours after LMI070 treatment (Figure 4A and 4B and Figure S5E). Sarcomere disassembly initiates at sites of protrusion, suggesting that microtubules contribute to a broader cytoskeletal remodeling program that coordinates both peripheral membrane remodeling and sarcomere disassembly during CM dedifferentiation. These findings further indicate that protrusions may be required to trigger this process (Figure S5F). To assess the cell cycle activity, EdU was administered beginning at YAP5SA activation, thereby labeling CMs that entered the cell cycle through the entire experimental period. Notably, most CMs with disorganized sarcomere were not in the cell cycle, with only ≈15% showing EdU incorporation (≈10% across all CMs; Figure 4C and 4D and Figure S5G), suggesting that sarcomere reorganization is largely independent of YAP5SA-induced cell cycle reentry. In contrast, all EdU-positive CMs exhibited varying degrees of sarcomere breakdown (Figure 4C and 4E and Figure S5F), suggesting that sarcomere disassembly occurs before YAP-induced cell cycle reentry in this context. A similar pattern was observed in Ccnd1-positive CMs (Figure 4F–4H and Figure S5H). Treating the CMs with colchicine or nocodazole, which disrupts microtubules, completely prevented both the protrusion and sarcomere disruption, as well as the YAP5SA-induced cell cycle reentry (Figure 4C–4G and Figure S5A–S5H). Moreover, colchicine inhibited sarcomere breakdown in a dose-dependent manner, suggesting that CM sarcomere disruption depends on microtubules growth (Figure S6A and S6B). Consistently, CMs with higher YAP activity exhibited more pronounced sarcomere disruption (Figure S6C and S6D). Together, these results suggested that the microtubule network is required for sarcomere disassembly in CMs.

We observed strong colocalization between α-actinin and microtubule networks, particularly in regions with intact or partially disrupted α-actinin structures, suggesting a physical interaction between the two (Figure 4A). This finding aligns with previous reports that showed that the microtubule networks interact with Z disc through desmin.39 To further investigate this interaction, we generated an adenoviral construct expressing ACTN2-mCherry, enabling real-time visualization of α-actinin through live imaging. Adult CMs were coinfected with EB3-GFP and ACTN2-mCherry to examine microtubule–α-actinin interactions in YAP5SA-overexpressing CMs. Although microtubules remained highly dynamic, α-actinin structures appeared more stable, exhibiting minimal movement (Video 7). Similar to the immunofluorescence staining shown in Figure 4A, we observed punctate patterns of α-actinin fragments that remained relatively stable in size, with no obvious reduction or degradation during the 30-minute to 2-hour time-course imaging. This suggests that the disrupted α-actinin structure is not primarily resolved through protein degradation (Video 7). At 72 hours after YAP5SA overexpression, we observed direct interactions between polymerizing microtubule plus-ends and disrupted α-actinin fragments, manifested as microtubule growth arrest or delayed on contact, directional changes, or squeezing through gaps in α-actinin fragments (Figure 4I and Video 7A and 7B). Of note, some α-actinin fragments moved on contact with microtubule plus-ends, with motion direction aligned to microtubule polymerization tracks (Video 7C). Quantification of microtubules movements revealed more directional changes but fewer polymerization arrests in YAP5SA-overexpressing CMs compared with control CMs, indicating that sarcomere organization influences microtubule–α-actinin interaction (Figure 4J). Taken together, these findings suggest that microtubule plus-ends physically interact with α-actinin fragments and that polymerization-generated mechanical forces may drive α-actinin displacement, potentially contributing to sarcomere disassembly in YAP5SA-overexpressing CMs, although the initial trigger of sarcomere breakdown remains unclear. Similar phenotypes were also observed in vivo. The sarcomere structures, including thin filaments (cTnT), thick filaments (α-actinin), and intermediate filaments (desmin), revealed a disrupted pattern 5 days after YAP5SA overexpression. Periodic colchicine treatment partially rescued these phenotypes (Figure 4K and 4L and Figure S7A–S7C).

YAP-TEAD Interaction Is Required for YAP-Induced Microtubule Growth

To investigate the molecular mechanism by which YAP regulates microtubule network growth, we generated vectors expressing YAP5SA-S94A, a mutant that disrupts YAP-TEAD interaction and thereby abolishes the transcriptional activity of YAP (Figure 5A). This allowed us to determine whether transcriptional regulation, rather than other mechanisms such as direct YAP-microtubule interaction reported in our recent study,40 is critical for YAP-induced microtubule growth. Our results showed that overexpression of YAP5SA increases α-tubulin expression and stabilizes the microtubule network in CMs. However, YAP5SA-S94A overexpression failed to induce α-tubulin expression in the heart (Figure 5B and 5C and Figure S8A). Furthermore, YAP5SA significantly enhanced microtubule density, promoted the sarcomere disassembly, and increased the number of EdU- or phosphorylated histone H3 (pHH3)–positive CMs, whereas YAP5SA-S94A had no such effects (Figure S8B–S8G). These findings indicate that YAP-TEAD transcriptional activity is essential for microtubule network expansion, sarcomere disassembly, and CM cell cycle reentry.

To identify direct YAP targets involved in microtubule regulation, we performed CUT&RUN and RNA sequencing on NRVMs overexpressing YAP5SA or the transcriptionally inactive mutant YAP5SA-S94A. At 36 hours after LMI070 treatment, cells were harvested for CUT&RUN to map YAP5SA binding sites and for RNA sequencing to profile gene expression. Two independent YAP antibodies were used for the CUT&RUN analysis (Figure S9A). Peak overlap between the 2 antibodies reached 82% in wild-type–YAP (endogenous YAP), 92% in YAP5SA-expressing NRVMs (YAP5SA and endogenous YAP), and 70% in YAP5SA-S94A–expressing NRVMs between mouse and rabbit antibodies (Figure S9B). To be stringent, only YAP peaks shared between 2 antibodies were used for downstream analysis. YAP peaks were enriched at promoter regions (Figure S9C) and were largely similar between control and YAP5SA-expressing NRVMs (Figure S10A). However, motif analysis revealed that endogenous YAP-binding sites were more strongly enriched for NRF and KLF transcription factor motifs, whereas YAP5SA-binding sites showed greater enrichment for TEAD motifs, suggesting a preferential association of YAP5SA with TEADs (Figure S10B and S10C). As a dominant negative control, YAP5SA-S94A exhibited significant loss of TEAD motif enrichment and diminished YAP occupancy at canonical target loci such as Lats2, CTGF (Ccn2), Ankrd1, and Amotl2 (Figures S10D and S11A). Genome-wide analysis identified 8803 YAP-binding peaks in YAP5SA-expressing NRVMs and 2564 peaks in YAP5SA-S94A–expressing NRVMs, with 2369 shared peaks (Figure 5D). Notably, the signal intensity of these peaks was substantially reduced in the YAP5SA-S94A group compared with the YAP5SA group (Figure 5E), suggesting that these loci represent transcriptionally active YAP targets. Integration of CUT&RUN and RNA-sequencing datasets identified 699 direct YAP target genes (Figure 5F and G), the majority of which are involved in cell cycle regulation, cell adhesion, and muscle development (Figure S11B). It is important to note that several genes with known interactions with microtubules—including Ift43, Knstrn, Kif20a, and Ajuba—were upregulated by at least 4-fold in YAP5SA-overexpressing cells compared with YAP5SA-S94A controls (Figure 5H and 5I). Together, these findings suggest that YAP promotes microtubule growth through the transcriptional activation of microtubule-associated genes.

To further determine which YAP targets are required for YAP-regulated microtubule growth, we knocked down Ift43, Knstrn, Mns1, Kif20a, and Ajuba in NRVMs. Knockdown of these genes reduced α-tubulin expression in NRVMs, with Ajuba knockdown causing the most pronounced decrease (Figure 6A and 6B and Figure S12A–S12I). We therefore focused on Ajuba for further analysis of microtubule dynamics. Specifically, knockdown of Ajuba in NRVMs led to a significantly reduction in both free and polymerized α-tubulin (Figure 6C–6F), indicating a critical role of Ajuba in regulating tubulin polymerization. Time-course live imaging using EB3-GFP to track microtubule plus-end dynamics revealed significantly increased catastrophe and rescue frequency in Ajuba-knockdown NRVMs (Figure 6G–6M), consistent with increased microtubule turnover and reduced network stability. Last, Ajuba knockdown in the context of YAP5SA overexpression significantly diminished microtubule intensity and network density, as assessed by immunofluorescence, indicating that YAP promotes microtubule growth at least in part through its direct target Ajuba (Figure 6N–6P).

CM-Specific Knockdown of Tuba1b Attenuates YAP-Induced Sarcomere Disassembly

There are approximately 8 α-tubulin and 9 β-tubulin isoforms in both mice and humans. RNA-sequencing analysis revealed that many of these isoforms are upregulated in YAP5SA-overexpressing NRVMs except for Tuba8, which is significantly downregulated (Figure 7A). CUT&RUN analysis further identified Tuba1b as the only tubulin gene with direct YAP binding (Figure 7B). To investigate the role of Tuba1b in YAP5SA-mediated microtubule growth and sarcomere disassembly, we generated AAV vectors expressing 2 Tuba1b shRNA-miR30 constructs under the control of the CM-specific cTnT promoter. Initial validation in NRVMs showed ≈50% knockdown of α-tubulin levels (Figure 7C and 7D). We then coinjected AAV-Tuba1b-shRNA-miR30 and AAV-Xon-YAP5SA into 8-week-old adult mice through retro-orbital delivery (AAV-Xon-YAP5SA plus AAV-GFP served as controls). Two weeks after viral delivery, mice received 3 doses of LMI070 to induce YAP5SA expression, following the same regimen as in Figure 2. Because AAV-Tuba1b-shRNA-miR30 coexpresses GFP, we were able to specifically assess microtubule networks in CMs expressing both YAP5SA and Tuba1b shRNA. Our results showed an ≈40% reduction in tubulin intensity in CMs with Tuba1b knockdown compared with controls (Figure 7E and 7F). Immunofluorescence analysis further revealed that Tuba1b knockdown attenuated YAP5SA-induced sarcomere disassembly (Figure 7G and 7H) and reduced the number of EdU- and pHH3-positive CMs (Figure 7I–7K). Together, these findings support a CM-specific role for microtubules in mediating YAP5SA-induced sarcomere disassembly and cell cycle progression.

The Microtubule Network Is Required for YAP-Induced CM Proliferation, Dedifferentiation, and Regenerative Cardiac Repair

YAP-induced adult CM proliferation involves dedifferentiating to a fetus-like state.13,29 We wondered whether the YAP-microtubule regulatory axis could regulate CM dedifferentiation. Immunofluorescence staining for ANP and MYH7, 2 markers for fetal CMs, revealed a significant increase in CMs of AAV-Xon-YAP5SA–injected hearts (Figure S13). However, the expression of ANP and MYH7 was reduced in YAP5SA-overexpressed CMs when treated with colchicine, suggesting that YAP-induced CM dedifferentiation requires the presence of the microtubule network (Figure S13).

Consistent with previous findings in cultured CMs and mouse model, YAP5SA induced a significant increase in EdU-positive CMs in the AAV-Xon-5SA–injected hearts but not in the control hearts 5 days after LMI070 treatment. However, concurrent treatment of colchicine, which disrupts the microtubule network, significantly decreased the number of EdU-labeled CMs in AAV-Xon-YAP5SA–injected hearts (Figure 8A–8C). In addition, we detected the G2/M cell cycle marker, pHH3-positive CMs in the AAV-Xon-5SA–injected hearts but not in the control hearts after LMI070 treatment. The number of pHH3-positive CMs also decreased after colchicine treatment (Figure 8D and 8E). YAP5SA has been reported to promote CM proliferation through both cell-intrinsic and cell-extrinsic mechanisms.13 Our quantification of EdU- and pHH3-positive CMs across the total CMs suggested that the microtubule networks are required for YAP-induced cell cycle reentry and progression through both mechanisms.

Cardiac activation of YAP can stimulate CM proliferation and repair the heart in mouse and pig models.25,41,42 We administered 2 doses of LMI070 instead of 3 to improve tolerability, following a similar approach reported in a recent study.28 Our results revealed that overexpression of YAP5SA by AAV-Xon-YAP5SA 2 weeks after myocardial infarction can promote regenerative cardiac repair, including cardiac function recovery and cardiac fibrosis (Figure 8F–8J). However, concurrently treating the mice with colchicine significantly inhibited YAP5SA-induced cardiac regenerative repair (Figure 8F–8J). Taken together, these results suggested that the microtubule network is required for YAP-induced CM dedifferentiation, cell cycle progression, and regenerative cardiac repair.

Discussion

In this study, we found that the microtubule expression and networks within CMs gradually decline after birth, correlating with cardiac maturation. Using a dedifferentiation model—overexpressing YAP5SA in adult CMs—we observed enhanced microtubule network density attributable to stabilized microtubule polymerization, which in turn facilitated CM dedifferentiation and proliferation and sarcomere disassembly. Live imaging revealed a direct interaction between polymerizing microtubule plus-ends and α-actinin, suggesting a mechanical role of microtubules in promoting sarcomere breakdown. This YAP-microtubule regulatory axis is essential for YAP-induced cytokinesis and regenerative cardiac repair; colchicine treatment significantly impaired the regenerative response. Further analysis identified a critical transcriptional role for YAP in regulating genes involved in microtubule dynamics, including direct targets such as Ajuba and Tuba1b, the knockdown of which resulted in decreased microtubules. Our findings suggest that the microtubule network functions as a higher-order regulatory structure driving sarcomere remodeling during CM dedifferentiation and proliferation.

The contractile nature of CMs poses unique challenges to cell replication, distinguishing them from other cell types. It has long been believed that the myofibrils within CMs obstruct nuclear division and cell cleavage, acting as barriers that hinder proliferation or leading to cell binucleation or multinucleation. Consequently, the terminally differentiated phenotype of CMs has historically been considered irreversible. However, growing evidence over the past 2 decades indicates that adult CMs retain a degree of plasticity, enabling cytoskeletal and sarcomeric remodeling both in vivo and in vitro. This plasticity allows CMs to overcome the structural constraints of contractility and undergo cell cycle activity. During the fetal and early neonatal CM mitosis, sarcomeres undergo complete disassembly and are subsequently reassembled in the daughter cells.7,43 The disassembly of the sarcomere during cytokinesis begins at the Z disk and thin filament–associated proteins followed by the thick filament–associated proteins.7,44 Similar sarcomeric disassembly occurs during adult heart regeneration in zebrafish8 and in neonatal mice after apical resection, which triggers regeneration rather than scarring.45 In humans, hibernating CMs after cardiac injury also display depletion of sarcomeres and a structural protein profile resembling fetal CMs.46 Recent studies have shown that factors such as Nrg/Erbb4,12 Hippo/YAP,14,15,25 Yamanaka factors,16 and MicroRNA-199a17 can stimulate adult CM proliferation, accompanied by dedifferentiation into a fetus-like state and sarcomere breakdown to overcome the structural block for cytokinesis. Similarly, deletion of Cpt1b,20 Meis1, or Hoxb13 promotes CM proliferation with sarcomeric remodeling.19,47 Despite these observations, the mechanisms driving sarcomere disassembly in adult CM mitosis remain unclear. In our study, we found that YAP-induced microtubule network growth is required for the initiation and progression of the sarcomere breakdown and that the mechanical tension from microtubule polymerization may contribute to this process. Constitutive ERBB2 activation also upregulates the tubulin expression,11 suggesting a potential shared mechanism by which ERBB2 and YAP coordinate CM dedifferentiation and sarcomere disassembly. Unlike mammals, adult zebrafish retain robust regenerative capacity.8 We also observed a reduction in the microtubule network during zebrafish heart maturation. It will be important to determine whether microtubule growth is reactivated in response to cardiac injury and if such remodeling facilitates CM proliferation.

Recently, Adducin was identified as a regulator of sarcomere disassembly during CM mitosis.48 Adducin is primarily expressed during the neonatal regenerative period but declines after birth. Overexpression of adducin promotes sarcomere disassembly, and it seems that the disrupting sarcomere alone is sufficient to stimulate the CM mitosis.48 In our study, we observed that most of the YAP-overexpressed CMs with disorganized sarcomeres are not in the cell cycle. However, all EdU-positive CMs displayed sarcomere disassembly, suggesting that the sarcomeric breakdown may be required for CM cell cycle reentry and progression. Direct expression of cell cycle genes such as CCNA249 or CCND250 or the combined expression of 4 factors (CDK1, CDK4, CCNB1, and CCND1)51 has been shown to drive adult CMs reentering the cell cycle without obvious sarcomere disorganization. These findings raise important questions about the relationships between cell cycle reentry and progression, dedifferentiation, and sarcomere disassembly and how these processes are coordinated during successful cytokinesis.

The microtubules, one type of the nonsarcomeric cytoskeleton, are essential for maintaining CM structure and function. In cardiac cells, microtubules serve at least 2 essential functions. First, microtubules form the mitotic spindle, which is essential for both chromosome alignment during metaphase and the separation of sister chromatids during anaphase. Second, microtubules serve as the core components of the cytoskeleton and play an essential role in cellular migration, cell polarity, intracellular transport, and even sensing and responding to mechanical stress. The persistence of the microtubular density is critical for CM structure and heart function. Furthermore, recent studies suggest that the structure of microtubules is associated with CM stiffness and directly affects the CM contraction.52 Stabilization of microtubule networks has been demonstrated in patient tissue and various animal models of cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure.53 It was reported that microtubule stabilization is associated with α-tubulin detyrosination, and a recent study has demonstrated an increase in detyrosinated α-tubulin shortly after myocardial infarction, which increases CM stiffness and impedes CM contraction.54 Unlike YAP-induced acute microtubule growth, the chronic microtubule growth through α-tubulin detyrosination seems not disrupt the sarcomere structure, potentially because the detyrosinated microtubules are less dynamic but have enhanced interaction with the Z disc through desmin,39 providing more structure support to the sarcomere. Our results showed that an increase of the microtubule networks could disrupt the sarcomere structure, while deletion of the microtubule network had little effect, suggesting that the mechanic tension generated by microtubule polymerization plays a crucial role during sarcomere disassembly.

Although colchicine treatment attenuated YAP5SA-induced sarcomere disassembly in vivo, it did not fully restore sarcomere organization. This partial rescue may reflect limited or transient colchicine activity in vivo compared with the more controlled conditions in cultured CMs. In addition, the in vivo microenvironment may contribute to this discrepancy. Previous studies have shown that YAP5SA activates C3ar1+ macrophages, which play an essential role in CM proliferation and sarcomere disassembly.14 An important remaining question is whether these C3ar1+ macrophages regulate sarcomere disassembly through microtubule-dependent mechanisms or alternative pathways. Of note, our CM-specific Tuba1b knockdown results demonstrate that reducing microtubules is sufficient to attenuate YAP-induced sarcomere disassembly. However, it remains unclear whether C3ar1+ macrophage activation is affected by Tuba1b knockdown.

We identified YAP as a transcription factor that regulates microtubule growth. To date, only a few transcriptional factors have been shown to influence microtubule dynamics. Most known regulators are direct interactors of microtubules such as MAPs, EBs, kinesins, and CLIPs, which bind to microtubule plus-ends and directly modulate their dynamics. Other regulators, including TATs/HDACs, VASHs/TTLs, and TTLLs/CCPs, control microtubule acetylation/deacetylation, tyrosination/detyrosination, and glutamylation/deglutamylation, respectively. These posttranslational modifications affect microtubule dynamics and growth through different mechanisms. Given that YAP activity responds to various pathophysiological stimuli such as mechanical stress55 and metabolic changes,40 how YAP coordinates with other mediators to regulate microtubule dynamics and growth warrants further investigation. Moreover, we identified several direct YAP targets, including Ajuba and Knstrn, that have been reported to interact with microtubules. How these factors influence microtubule dynamics in CMs remains unclear, and their roles in regulating dedifferentiation and sarcomere reorganization will be critical for future study.

Colchicine is considered an anti-inflammatory drug; the primary mechanism is tubulin disruption, which leads to subsequent downregulation of multiple inflammatory pathways and modulation of innate immunity.56 Several recent clinical trials evaluated the effects of colchicine on cardiovascular outcomes in patients with acute myocardial infarction and other vascular diseases, with mixed results across 5 trials. COLCOT (Colchicine Cardiovascular Outcomes Trial) and LODOCO2 (Low-Dose Colchicine 2) showed a reduction in cardiovascular outcomes.57,58 However, the 3 other recent trials, CLEAR (Colchicine and Spironolactone in Patients With MI), CHANCE-3 (Colchicine in High‐Risk Patients With Acute Minor‐to‐Moderate Ischemic Stroke or Transient Ischemic Attack), and CONVINCE (Colchicine for Prevention of Vascular Inflammation in Non‐Cardioembolic Stroke) showed no significant benefit of colchicine in cardiovascular diseases.59–61 Another clinical trial demonstrated that colchicine has no effect on infarct size and heart function after myocardial infarction.62 Collectively, these studies suggest colchicine has no adverse effect on cardiac function. Therefore, we infer that colchicine inhibits YAP-induced cardiac repair mainly through disruption of the microtubule networks in CMs, subsequently inhibiting CM dedifferentiation and proliferation and sarcomere reorganization. We expect that colchicine can suppress YAP-induced inflammatory response and could be used to limit excessive YAP-driven dedifferentiation and sarcomere disassembly once sufficient new CMs have been generated and to mitigate other adverse effects of YAP overexpression.13 Thus, colchicine may serve as a safe modulatory control in YAP-base gene therapies for patients with heart failure.
