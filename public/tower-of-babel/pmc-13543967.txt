Trans-osseous coaxial biopsy for pulmonary lesions under CT guidance: Technical efficacy for challenging location
Authors: Bing Zhang, Yuan Yan, Jie Chen, Rui Guo, Bing-Yu Huang, Zheng-Yu Lin, Jin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo retrospectively analyze CT-guided percutaneous cutting biopsy approach of lung lesions via the trans-osseous approach.MethodsThe data of 17 lesions in 16 patients who underwent consecutive CT-guided percutaneous trans-osseous coaxial cutting biopsy of lung lesions were retrospectively analyzed. The procedure involved creating an artificial bone channel using a bone puncture needle under CT guidance, through which the coaxial cutting biopsy was performed. We assessed technical success rate, complications and pathological diagnosis accuracy.Results17 lesions across 16 patients were biopsied using the CT-guided percutaneous trans-osseous coaxial cutting technique. The scapular approach was used for 13 lesions in the posterior upper lung lobe, while the sternal approach was employed for 4 lesions in the anterior upper lung lobe near the heart and major vessels. The technical success rate was 94%, yielding a positive pathological diagnosis rate of 100%. The incidence of pneumothorax was 12.5%, and all of them required intervention. No other complications were reported in the remaining patients.ConclusionCT-guided percutaneous trans-osseous coaxial cutting biopsy technique is a valuable and viable alternative technique, particularly for lung lesions in challenging locations, such as the posterior segment and anterior upper lobe adjacent to the heart and major vessel. This approach may reduce the risk of complications such as pneumothorax and hemoptysis, making it a valuable option for difficult-to-access lung lesions.Keywords: Lung neoplasms, Lung biopsy, CT-guided, Trans-osseous

Introduction

CT-guided percutaneous lung needle biopsy (PLB) is a widely employed technique in clinical practice for obtaining tissue samples from lung lesions [1,2]. However, this procedure is associated with complications such as pneumothorax and hemorrhage, which are among the most common adverse events reported. Factors that increase the risk of pneumothorax include the depth of the pulmonary nodule (pleural-lesion distance), the maximum axial diameter of the nodule, repeated pleural puncture, interlobar fissure puncture, and the presence of emphysema [3]. Similarly, the risk of hemorrhage during lung biopsy is heightened when the puncture path crosses vertical blood vessels or when the lesion itself has a rich blood supply [4]. To minimize these risks, it is generally recommended to avoid puncture through interlobar fissure, areas of emphysema, or pulmonary bullae, and to select a short, parallel-to-vessel path whenever possible [5,6].

Certain lung lesions, however, are situated in locations that complicate the use of conventional short-path or parallel vascular approaches during CT-guided PLB, especially, lung lesions located in the upper postero-lateral nodules or sheltered by pulmonary emphysema and bullae, or adjacent to major vessels and vital organs. Bony structures such as the scapula or sternum may obstruct the path to target lesion, making it difficult to reach and increasing the likelihood of complications such as pneumothorax and hemorrhage. In some cases, these obstacles may even prevent successful puncture [7]. Previously, Rebonato et al. proposed that the trans-scapular approach could be a safe pathway for biopsy of upper posterolateral lung lesions, with technical success rate of 91% and complication rate of 27%, respectively [8]. Rendon et al. reported the biopsy of intrathoracic lesions through the sternal approach, and the results showed that no complications directly related to the sternal approach occurred [9]. Although there have been limited reports on the use of CT-guided trans-scapular biopsy for lesions located in the posterior segment of the upper lobe of the lung [[8], [9], [10]], these approaches have not yet been widely promoted in clinical practice.

This study aims to retrospectively analyze cases in which CT-guided percutaneous trans-osseous coaxial cutting biopsy was used for challenging or high-risk lesions at our institution. We investigate the safety and efficacy of this technique as a viable alternative to conventional methods, particularly for lesions that are difficult to access due to their proximity to bony structures.

Material and methods

All procedures in this retrospective study were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study was approved by our institutional ethics committee, and the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Patients

Among 8908 consecutive patients who underwent CT-guided PLB over the period January 2015 to March 2025 in our institution, we collected data for all the cases in which a CT-guided coaxial lung biopsy was performed through a bone approach. A total of 17 lesions in 16 patients (12 males, 4 females) with a mean age of 63.6 ± 8.8 years (range: 41–81 years) underwent CT-guided trans-osseous puncture biopsy were included in this study.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: pulmonary lesions with high pneumothorax and bleeding risk via conventional transthoracic approach, lesions sheltered by emphysema and bullae, lesions adjacent to great vessels and vital organs, and patients with narrow intercostal space or poor respiratory cooperation. The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with severe osteoporosis, osseous lesions, uncorrected coagulopathy and uncontrolled severe cardiopulmonary dysfunction. The decision to perform a trans-osseous PLB was taken intra-procedurally, in some cases after the failure of conventional approaches.

Biopsy protocol

Preoperative preparation

All patient underwent a comprehensive preprocedural assessment with a routine chest CT and contrast-enhanced scan within 1 month prior to the procedure to assess pulmonary status, delineate the vascular supply to the lesion, and evaluate its anatomical relationship with adjacent critical structures. Additionally, a complete blood count, coagulation profile, and electrocardiogram(ECG) were performed to rule out coagulopathies and significant cardiac conditions. Special attention was given to the management of anticoagulant and antiplatelet medications, with appropriate discontinuation schedules followed in accordance with clinical guidelines [11].

Prior to the biopsy, all patients had an intravenous catheter placed for vascular access, and written informed consent was obtained. Emergency medications, oxygen delivery systems, and chest drainage equipment were prepared and kept on standby to address any potential complications.

The biopsy procedures were conducted by two interventional radiologists, each with over five years of experience in performing percutaneous lung biopsies. Their expertise ensured adherence to procedural protocols and optimized patient outcomes.

Biopsy procedure

Biopsy procedures were performed using a 64-row multi-slice spiral CT scanner (SOMATOM Definition AS, Siemens, Germany). Technical parameters were 120 kV, 80 mA, slice thickness of 5 mm and 1 mm. Based on the preoperative chest CT image, the optimal patient position, either supine or prone, was selected. Patients were instructed to remain still and trained to hold their breath at the end of exhalation to minimize lung movement during the procedure. A positioning grid was then affixed to the patient's skin at the intended needle entry site, followed by a full chest CT scan to guide the procedure. The puncture trajectory was meticulously planned using the acquired imaging, with efforts made to choose the shortest possible path parallel to the pulmonary vessels. This approach aimed to minimize the risk of complications by avoiding the interlobar fissure and pulmonary bullae. Once the puncture site was identified, surface marking was performed to determine the precise insertion angle, depth, and thickness of any bone along the intended path.

Following standard disinfection and draping procedures, local anesthesia was administered using 1% lidocaine, infiltrating layer by layer down to the periosteum. A small skin incision, approximately 3–5 mm in length, was made using a sharp scalpel to facilitate needle insertion. Under CT guidance, a 9 G with 150-mm-long or 11 G with 70-mm-long bone puncture needle (Gallini, Italy) was carefully advanced along the predetermined path, either through the scapula (Fig. 1, Fig. 2) or the lateral cortical bone of the sternum (Fig. 3). The needle core was removed, and adjustments to the depth and force of insertion were made based on the previously measured thickness of the cortical bone. Once the needle had successfully traversed the posterior edge of the target bone to create a channel, the bone puncture needle was withdrawn. Subsequently, a 17 G with 110-mm-long coaxial trocar (GCO, Gallini, Italy) was introduced through the newly established bone channel and advanced to the periphery of the lesion under CT guidance (Fig. 3c). After removing the core of the coaxial trocar, an 18 G semi-automatic cutting biopsy needle with 160-mm-long (TZ Evo, Gallini, Italy) was inserted into the lesion to obtain tissue samples. If the lesion was located near critical structures such as major blood vessels or the heart, an additional CT scan was performed after positioning the cutting groove of the biopsy needle to ensure a safe distance from these vital structures before activating the biopsy device (Fig. 3d). Typically, 2–3 pathological tissue specimens, each measuring 1–2 cm in length and approximately 1 mm in thickness, were collected and immediately fixed in formalin for pathological examination. Upon completion of the biopsy, the needle was withdrawn, and a sterile dressing was applied to the puncture site. A routine chest CT scan was performed immediately afterward to assess for potential complications such as pneumothorax and hemorrhage.

For subpleural lesions (Fig. 1), a relatively short 11 G bone puncture needle was also used as a coaxial needle. However, attention should be paid to the length compatibility with the semi-automatic cutting biopsy needle (Fig. 1j).

Postprocedural management

The patient was accompanied by the doctor back to the ward after the biopsy. The patient received ECG monitoring, oxygen therapy, and other symptomatic treatments and was closely observed for signs of chest tightness, shortness of breath, chest and back pain, or hemoptysis. A routine chest X-ray examination was performed 24 h after the operation to check for complications such as pneumothorax or hemorrhage.

Index of observation

Taking the successful completion of the operation as a technical success, we calculated the technical success rate. The incidence of massive pneumothorax (a gas width between the pleural line and the top of the chest wall ≥ 3 cm or persistent pneumothorax) were recorded. The specimens were required to meet defined pathological diagnostic criteria, and the positive rate of pathological diagnosis was evaluated.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM, Armonk, New York). Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Categorical data is presented as a percentage (number/total).

Results

Among these, 9 patients had a history of malignant tumors, including 1 case of recurrent lung tumor after total pneumonectomy for right lung cancer, 3 cases with a history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and 1 case with a right hilar lesion where endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) did not yield a definitive pathological diagnosis.

Patients and procedure characteristics.

13 lesions in 12 patients located in the posterior segment of the upper lobe of the lung were accessed via the scapular approach (Fig. 1, Fig. 2), while 4 lesions in 4 patients located in the anterior segment of the upper lobe were accessed via sternal approach (Fig. 3). One patient with severe emphysema and pulmonary bullae underwent two subpleural biopsies: one of the left lung and one of the posterior segment of the right upper lobe, both via the scapular approach (Fig. 1). The average diameter of the lesions was 22.8 ± 8.7 mm (median: 22 mm; range: 10.5–42.3 mm). The average procedure time was 33.3 ± 14.2 min (range: 17.4–64.2 min).

Technical success rate and complications

The operation was successfully completed for most patients, only one of them failed and changed the path due to pneumothorax during the procedure, and the technical success rate was 94%. Pneumothorax occurred in 2 cases after operation (2/16, 12.5%). One patient experienced a small pneumothorax on the left side during the procedure. A chest X-ray taken 24 h post-operation showed progression of the left pneumothorax, with approximately 30% lung compression. One patient developed pneumothorax and closed thoracic drainage was performed immediately after the puncture during the procedure. Both pneumothorax improved after catheter drainage. The remaining 14 patients experienced no complications such as pneumothorax, hemoptysis, or pain in the sternum or scapula area.

Pathological results

In one patient with double upper lung lesions, pathology revealed small-cell lung cancer in the left upper lobe and invasive lung adenocarcinoma in the right upper lobe. The remaining diagnosis included 5 cases of invasive lung adenocarcinoma, 4 cases of lung metastasis, and 6 cases of inflammatory lesions. The 6 patients diagnosed with inflammatory lesions improved after receiving anti-inflammatory treatment, and follow-up chest CT showed lesion absorption, further confirming diagnostic accuracy. The positive rate of pathological diagnosis was 100%.

Discussion

Electromagnetic navigation and virtual bronchoscopy have been developed to optimize the navigation of bronchoscopy to improve the diagnostic efficiency of peripheral pulmonary nodule sampling [12]. Early meta-analysis of Wang-Memoli et al. reported that the navigation success rate of this technique was 95%, the diagnostic rate was 70%, and the incidence of pneumothorax was 1.5% [13]. A subsequent systematic review from Kops et al. yielded a pooled diagnostic rate of 71% across all navigational bronchoscopy cohorts [12]. Conversely, conventional CT-guided transthoracic biopsy could achieve approximately 93% diagnostic accuracy for solitary pulmonary nodules but carries markedly higher pneumothorax risk (18.8%–25.9%) [[14], [15], [16]]. Collectively, navigational bronchoscopy confers superior pneumothorax-related safety but delivers inferior diagnostic performance relative to CT-guided percutaneous lung biopsy.

CT can clearly display lung lesions and anatomical structures, accurately locating the puncture needle’s position. CT-guided PLB is a crucial method for diagnosing lung lesions [17,18]. While generally safe, the procedure is associated with potential complications, including pneumothorax requiring intervention, hemothorax, needle tract seeding, air embolism, respiratory failure, and, in rare cases, cardiac arrest and death [19,20]. Additionally, the occurrence of complications is closely related to the location of the lesion. When selecting a percutaneous puncture path for lesions located in the posterior and anterior segments of the upper lobe of the lung, particularly those adjacent to the anterior part of the cardiac large vessels, the short or non-transpulmonary path—situated near the lung parenchyma is often obstructed by the scapula or sternum. Many practitioners opt to avoid the bony structure through the long path of the interlobar fissure [6], which can result in a puncture trajectory more perpendicular to the pulmonary vessels and cardiac large vessels. This increases the risk of pneumothorax, hemothorax, and cardiac injury, and may even lead to puncture failure [20,21]. Therefore, utilizing a short, non-pulmonary path that avoids interlobar fissure and remains parallel to the pulmonary bronchial vascular bundle during CT-guided PLB is an important measure to reduce the risk of pneumothorax and hemothorax [22].

For pulmonary lesions that are challenging or risky to approach using conventional methods, the key to successful biopsy and minimizing complications is the flexible selection of the puncture path using various techniques [23]. Umberto G performed biopsies on five cases of lesions successfully in the posterior segment of the upper lobe of the lung using the coaxial trans-scapular approach [10]. In this study, 13 lesions in 12 patients located in the posterior segment of the upper lobe of the lung were approached via the scapular route to avoid blood vessels, interlobar fissures, and pulmonary bullae. Before attempting trans-scapular puncture, we tried several reported methods to obtain a short path, including changing the patient's position, internally rotating the shoulder joint to move the scapula outward, and using non-axial tilt needle insertion; however, these methods were often insufficient to avoid scapular obstruction [24].

Some studies have found that patients with chronic lung disease have an increased risk of complications from lung biopsy [25,26]. Additionally, some researchers have reported that using a non-transpulmonary, short-path approach for CT-guided biopsies of subpleural lung lesions can reduce the pleural-lesion distance to zero, avoiding passage through the interlobar fissure, pulmonary bullae, and other structures, thereby lowering the incidence of pneumothorax [22,27]. In this study, 4 patients had 5 subpleural lesions in the posterior segment of the upper lobe of the lung (3 patients with severe emphysema, including 1 case with single lung). The conventional transpulmonary approach posed a high risk of damaging pulmonary bullae or the interlobar fissure, thereby increasing the risk of pneumothorax and hemorrhage. As a result, we performed CT-guided trans-scapular, non-transpulmonary puncture biopsy. However, in one case, due to the change of the patient 's position during the procedure, the puncture needle was offset and passed through the air-containing lung tissue, resulting in pneumothorax, and the original needle was difficult to reach the edge of the lesion for biopsy. Then change the approach into a long path.

Studies have reported that lung biopsy adjacent to the heart and large vessels can lead to complications such as pericardial tamponade, massive hemorrhage, and cardiac perforation, which can be life-threatening in severe cases [28]. Hsu L-S suggested that when the distance between a lung lesion and the mediastinal pleura is less than 2 cm, the physician should exercise caution when performing a lung biopsy. The operator must choose a puncture path that can safely reach the target lesion, avoid direct perpendicular puncture into the heart and large blood vessels, and visualize the position of the needle tip after penetrating the target [29]. In this study, the lesions in 4 patients who underwent transsternal lung biopsy were located in the anterior segment of the upper lobe of the lung, adjacent to the large blood vessels of the anterior chest wall. To ensure the safety of the biopsy while obtaining satisfactory pathological samples, a puncture path parallel to the heart and large blood vessels through the short path of the sternum was selected.

Coaxial cutting biopsy technology has been widely used in lung biopsies, offering advantages such as allowing multiple multi-point sampling, shortening operation time, and reducing complications and patient radiation dose [30]. In this study, all cases were treated using coaxial cutting lung biopsy via a bone path and all lesions had clear pathological diagnosis. Pneumothorax occurred in two patients during PLB through trans-scapular approach in this study. Retrospective analysis revealed that in one case with the lesion located in the posterior segment of the left upper lobe, adjacent to the oblique fissure, we initially selected the posterior approach for puncture, but the needle path passed through the oblique fissure and was perpendicular to adjacent vessels, causing pneumothorax and intrapulmonary hemorrhage, resulting in suboptimal biopsy sample. Therefore, we switched to the trans-scapular approach, which was parallel to the pulmonary vessels and interlobar fissure. The procedure proceeded smoothly, and a post-operative CT scan showed no progression of pneumothorax or intrapulmonary hemorrhage. However, a chest X-ray taken 24 h later revealed an increase in the left pneumothorax. No other complications were observed in this study.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, as a single-center, retrospective, non-control group study with small sample size, potential biases and errors are inevitable. Secondly, the CT-guided trans-osseous approach is more complex than conventional lung biopsy methods, which prolongs the operation time and increases the difficulty of the procedure to some extent. This approach still requires verification through multi-center studies with larger sample sizes in the future. Additionally, standardized pain assessment and long-term clinical follow-up were not implemented in the original data collection.

In summary, the study is not designed to compare the safety and efficacy of trans-osseous and nontrans-osseous routes. However, the results of the study, namely the high feasibility and lack of osseous complications, indicates that the trans-osseous approach is a valuable and viable alternative route to CT-guided biopsy of lung lesions when other methods fail. This method is expected to reduce the risk of pneumothorax and hemorrhage, making it a valuable option for difficult-to-access lung lesions.
