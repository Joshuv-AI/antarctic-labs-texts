Long-term survival in generalized peritonitis: the impact of early critical periods in major emergency abdominal surgery
Authors: Maria Olausson, Mette Astrup Tolver, Ismail Gögenur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGeneralized peritonitis is a severe intra-abdominal infection associated with high morbidity and mortality in patients undergoing major emergency abdominal surgery. While short-term outcomes are well documented, long-term survival and prognostic factors remain less explored. This study investigated four-year survival and conditional survival to assess the impact of generalized peritonitis on long-term outcomes.MethodsThis was a retrospective study based on prospectively collected data from 2017 to 2020, including patients aged ≥ 18 years who underwent major emergency abdominal surgery. All patients were treated within a high-quality, multidisciplinary standardized protocol. The primary outcome was four-year overall survival stratified according to the degree of peritonitis. Secondary outcomes were conditional survival, in which time at risk began on postoperative days 30 and 90 and included only patients alive beyond these time points. Associations between survival and risk factors were assessed using Cox regression analyses.ResultsA total of 966 patients were included, of whom 21% (203/966) were found perioperatively to have generalized peritonitis. The four-year overall survival rate was 66% (95% CI: 62.38–69.12%) in patients without generalized peritonitis and 53% (95% CI: 46.52–60.39%) in patients with generalized peritonitis, with the log-rank test showing a significant difference in survival (P = 0.00014). Among patients alive 30 days after surgery, generalized peritonitis was still associated with significantly lower survival (log-rank test, P = 0.028). However, this association was no longer observed among patients surviving beyond 90 days after surgery (log-rank test, P = 0.51). Furthermore, age > 70 years, ASA ≥ 3, intra-abdominal malignancy, and generalized peritonitis were significantly associated with poor survival, whereas among patients surviving beyond 90 days, generalized peritonitis was no longer associated with four-year survival.ConclusionGeneralized peritonitis was independently associated with significantly worse four-year overall survival. Although this survival disadvantage remained significant among 30-day survivors, it was no longer observed beyond 90 days postoperatively, suggesting that the prognostic impact of generalized peritonitis is primarily confined to the early postoperative period.Keywords: Secondary peritonitis, Intraabdominal infection, Long-term outcomes, Acute, Laparotomy, Risk factors

Introduction

It is estimated that postoperative death is the third leading cause of death worldwide [1]. With a global population increasing in size, age, and multimorbidity, it is reasonable to assume that high-risk procedures such as major emergency abdominal surgery place a substantial burden on both patient outcomes and society [2–4]. The introduction of multidisciplinary perioperative protocols in major emergency abdominal surgery has reduced mortality and postoperative complications in this high-risk surgical population [5–7]. Despite these improvements in the surgical pathway, there is still considerable variation in mortality and morbidity among patients undergoing major emergency abdominal surgery [8]. Many known factors contribute to this disparity, including patient-level factors, elements within the perioperative pathway, and the etiology of the acute disease requiring surgery [9–16].

It is well known that secondary peritonitis, most often caused by perforation of the gastrointestinal tract, has one of the highest mortality rates due to profound physiological stress driven by the severity of the infection and the development of organ dysfunction [17]. Furthermore, it has been established that generalized peritonitis, defined as diffuse contamination with purulent or fecal soiling, is a risk factor for poor short-term survival and that the mortality rate in patients with generalized peritonitis is markedly higher than in those without, despite optimized multidisciplinary care [18]. The long-term aspect has been less thoroughly investigated, as most studies report follow-up at 30 and 90 days [19–24], and only a few have investigated survival beyond one year after surgery and the associated risk factors [25–27]. However, patient-related factors appear to be more strongly associated with adverse long-term outcomes than factors related to the surgical pathway [27–31].

Knowledge about the long-term survival and prognosis of patients with generalized peritonitis remains limited. In particular, the dynamics of survival and how risk factors evolve over time in this patient group have yet to be established. By investigating long-term outcomes in combination with conditional survival, we can improve understanding of patients’ trajectories and gain insight into how prognosis changes over time among those who survive the initial critical postoperative period. This may allow for more accurate survival estimates, which could ultimately help clinicians adjust postoperative surveillance and follow-up strategies. Furthermore, examining whether risk patterns change over time may help identify key factors affecting survival and potentially support more personalized or subgroup-tailored treatment strategies. Lastly, long-term follow-up may help patients align expectations of recovery with realistic outcomes.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate four-year overall survival and conditional survival in patients with generalized peritonitis undergoing major emergency abdominal surgery, and to assess patient- and surgery-related risk factors associated with long-term outcomes, thereby adding valuable insight to the existing literature.

Methods

Study design and setting

The study was a retrospective observational cohort study using data from a prospectively collected local database. The database contains data from the implementation of a high-quality, multidisciplinary standardized protocol for patients undergoing major emergency abdominal surgery at the Department of Surgery, Zealand University Hospital, from March 2017 to July 2020 [32]. The protocol consisted of 15 perioperative items ranging from rapid diagnosis and initial treatment to optimization of postoperative care. Throughout the pathway, care was provided by attending or consultant physicians across all departments, incorporating surgical, emergency, anesthesiologic, radiological, physiotherapy, and nutritional support care bundles. A detailed list of all items has been published elsewhere [32].

The study was approved by Region Zealand regulatory authorities (approval ID: REG-080-2022). All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Reporting followed the STROBE guidelines for observational studies in epidemiology [33].

Study population

All patients included in the study were ≥ 18 years of age, had Danish citizenship, and underwent major emergency abdominal surgery for bowel obstruction, perforation, ischemia, non-traumatic intra-abdominal bleeding or abscess, anastomotic leakage, or another diagnosis requiring emergency laparoscopy or laparotomy. Furthermore, patients with malignant pathology requiring emergency surgery due to the above-mentioned conditions were also included. Exclusion criteria were trauma, primary laparoscopic appendectomy and cholecystectomy, hernia without bowel obstruction, and any elective surgery. However, patients could be included if they underwent reoperation due to a complication after non-emergency surgery and met the above criteria. Starting in March 2017, all patients meeting the specified criteria received standardized treatment as part of the quality assurance project. Patients were included only once in the study.

Supported by previous literature, the cohort was divided into a non-generalized peritonitis group and a generalized peritonitis group according to the intraoperative grade of secondary peritonitis caused by intra-abdominal contamination [11, 34–37]. The non-generalized group included no peritonitis findings, serous peritonitis, or local contamination, whereas the generalized peritonitis group included findings of blood, purulent, or fecal contamination in ≥ 2 intra-abdominal quadrants. The peritonitis grade was assessed by the primary investigator through review of the surgical procedure reports.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was four-year overall survival after major emergency abdominal surgery.

The primary outcome, four-year overall survival, was defined as the time from the end of surgery to death from any cause during a follow-up period of four years. Patients still alive at the end of follow-up were censored at their last known survival time. Overall survival was stratified according to (1) non-generalized peritonitis and (2) generalized peritonitis.

Secondary outcomes were conditional survival, in which time at risk began on postoperative days 30 and 90 and included only patients alive beyond these time points. These time points served as starting points for subsequent long-term estimation. Thirty- and 90-day mortality were chosen as outcomes because they are the most widely used measures of short-term mortality, thereby making the results comparable with those of other studies. Additional outcomes were length of admission, admission to the intensive care unit (ICU), length of ICU stay, postoperative complications, Clavien–Dindo classification ≥ 3b, reoperation, and mortality.

Data collection and variables

Data were collected prospectively from electronic patient records (EPR) and stored in an electronic case report form. Deaths of all patients are registered in the Danish Civil Registration System, which provides 100% follow-up and is automatically linked to the electronic patient record.

Demographic variables included sex, age, body mass index (BMI) in kg/m2, smoking status, alcohol consumption above the recommended limit (84 g/week for women and 168 g/week for men), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, WHO performance status, comorbidities and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), preoperative malignancy, use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and systemic glucocorticoids, and quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA). Perioperative variables included time to surgery (from indication for surgery), duration of surgery, type of procedure (laparoscopy, conversion to laparotomy, or laparotomy), operative findings (obstruction, perforation, intra-abdominal bleeding, leakage, bowel ischemia, other, or no pathology), intra-abdominal malignancy (defined as the presence of local disease, metastases, or carcinosis intra-abdominally from any type of malignancy), surgical procedure (no bowel resection, upper gastrointestinal, small bowel, small and large bowel, large bowel, or other), perioperative transfusion, and surgery after previous non-emergency surgery.

The patients included in the study had no missing data on peritonitis, mortality, or postoperative complication assessments.

Statistics

For descriptive statistics, categorical data were presented as numbers and percentages, and continuous data as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). For group comparisons, categorical data were analyzed using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, whereas continuous data were analyzed using either the unpaired t-test or the Mann–Whitney test, depending on data distribution.

The primary outcome, four-year overall survival, was defined as the time from the end of surgery to death from any cause during a follow-up period of four years. Patients still alive at the end of follow-up were censored at their last known survival time. Patients were excluded if data on time of surgery were missing. Survival probabilities were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Overall survival rates at 1, 2, 3, and 4 years were reported with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and the log-rank test was used to compare survival distributions between patients with generalized and non-generalized peritonitis. The same approach was used to analyze conditional survival among patients surviving beyond 30 and 90 days after surgery.

To assess the association between demographic and perioperative covariates and conditional 30- and 90-day survival, both univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were performed. Covariate selection was based primarily on clinically relevant preoperative factors and established operative prognostic factors in emergency surgery [17, 18, 24, 38], rather than solely on statistical significance. The variables were sex, age > 70 years, active smoking, alcohol consumption above recommendations, ASA ≥ 3, reoperation after non-emergency surgery, laparotomy, bowel resection, generalized peritonitis, intra-abdominal malignancy, and perioperative transfusion. Variables associated with the outcome at p < 0.2 in the univariable analyses, as well as peritonitis regardless of p-value, were included in the multivariable analysis. Univariable screening at p < 0.20 was used to avoid excluding potentially important confounders, consistent with established regression modelling strategies. To avoid model overfitting, the number of covariates was limited according to events-per-variable considerations. Only variables with significant associations are shown in the table. Furthermore, variables were tested for multicollinearity by assessing correlation and variance inflation factor (VIF), and the proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residual tests. A sensitivity analysis was performed excluding cases with hemoperitoneum to assess the robustness of the main findings.

Results are presented as adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using RStudio Version 2025.05.1 + 513 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

During the study period from March 2017 to July 2020, a total of 1076 patients underwent major emergency abdominal surgery within an optimized multidisciplinary protocol. Overall survival

The overall four-year survival rate was 63% (95% CI: 60–66%) in the total population. As shown in Fig. 2, patients with generalized peritonitis had a significantly lower overall four-year survival compared with those without generalized peritonitis (P = 0.00014). The survival rates at 1, 2, 3, and 4 years were 81%, 75%, 70%, and 66%, respectively, for patients with non-generalized peritonitis, and 67%, 61%, 57%, and 53%, respectively, for patients with generalized peritonitis (Table 3). The four-year mortality rate was 47% (95% CI: 40–53%) in the generalized peritonitis group and 34% (95% CI: 31–38%) in the non-generalized peritonitis group, and 37% in the total population.

Adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression showed that generalized peritonitis was associated with an increased risk of mortality (HR: 1.57, 95% CI: 1.22–2.01, P < 0.001) (Table 4). Other significant factors associated with mortality were age > 70 years (HR: 2.48, 95% CI: 1.95–3.15, P < 0.001), ASA ≥ 3 (HR: 3.08, 95% CI: 2.42–3.93, P < 0.001), and intra-abdominal malignancy (HR: 3.40, 95% CI: 2.61–4.42, P < 0.001). Moreover, surgery after non-emergency surgery showed the opposite association, with a reduced risk of mortality (HR: 0.58, 95% CI: 0.36–0.94, P = 0.03) (Table 4). The proportional hazards assumption was not violated for the overall survival model (global test P = 0.22).

Conditional survival

Conditional survival analyses were conducted with time at risk beginning at 30 and 90 days after surgery. When time at risk began 30 days after surgery, the four-year conditional survival rate was 71% (95% CI: 68–74%) for the total population (Table 3). The survival rates at 1, 2, 3, and 4 years were 90%, 84%, 78%, and 78%, respectively, for patients with non-generalized peritonitis, and 82%, 75%, 70%, and 65%, respectively, for patients with generalized peritonitis (Table 3). Figure 3 shows a Kaplan–Meier curve that still indicates significantly lower four-year conditional survival in patients with generalized peritonitis (P = 0.028), although the association was weaker. The cumulative four-year mortality rate was 35% (95% CI: 27–42%) for the generalized peritonitis group and 22% (95% CI: 24–30%) for the non-generalized peritonitis group.

Four-year conditional survival in patients undergoing major emergency abdominal surgery. Kaplan–Meier curves are shown for patients with generalized peritonitis and non-generalized peritonitis, with time at risk beginning 30 days after surgery. The p-value was calculated using the log-rank test

Adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression indicated that generalized peritonitis was associated with an increased risk of mortality (HR: 1.53, 95% CI: 1.12–2.09, P = 0.008). Furthermore, other factors associated with mortality were age > 70 years (HR: 2.53, 95% CI: 1.91–3.36, P < 0.001), ASA ≥ 3 (HR: 2.73, 95% CI: 2.06–3.61, P < 0.001), and intra-abdominal malignancy (HR: 3.97, 95% CI: 2.58–5.52, P < 0.001). Moreover, surgery after non-emergency surgery showed the same opposite association as in the overall survival analysis, with a reduced risk of mortality (HR: 0.43, 95% CI: 0.23–0.82, P = 0.010) (Table 4).

Figure 4 shows the Kaplan–Meier curve for conditional survival when time at risk began 90 days after surgery. When the groups were compared from this time point, no difference was observed in the four-year conditional survival rate (P = 0.51). The four-year conditional survival rate for the total population was 76% (95% CI: 73–79%). The survival rates at 1, 2, 3, and 4 years were 94%, 88%, 81%, and 76%, respectively, for patients without generalized peritonitis, and 92%, 85%, 79%, and 74%, respectively, for patients with generalized peritonitis (Table 3). The four-year mortality rate was 26% (95% CI: 19–33%) in the generalized peritonitis group and 24% (95% CI: 20–27%) in the non-generalized group.

Adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression with time at risk beginning 90 days after surgery showed that only age > 70 years (HR: 2.61, 95% CI: 1.90–3.57, P < 0.001), ASA ≥ 3 (HR: 2.66, 95% CI: 2.04–3.77, P < 0.001), and intra-abdominal malignancy (HR: 3.66, 95% CI: 2.47–5.42, P < 0.001) were associated with an increased risk of mortality (Table 4).

Other findings

The time elapsed between the decision to operate and the initiation of the procedure was significantly shorter for patients with generalized peritonitis, with a median of 175 min versus 223 min (P < 0.001). On the other hand, patients with generalized peritonitis had a significantly longer duration of surgery, 133.50 min versus 121 min (P = 0.022), and a higher conversion to laparotomy rate, 33.5% (68/203) versus 19.3% (147/763) (P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Regarding procedure-related findings, patients with generalized peritonitis were significantly more likely to undergo upper gastrointestinal tract procedures, 27.1% (55/203) versus 4.5% (34/763) (P < 0.001), and large bowel resections, 27.1% (55/203) versus 7.9% (60/763) (P < 0.001). Perforated bowel was significantly more often found in patients with generalized peritonitis, 73.4% (149/203) versus 12.3% (94/763) (P < 0.001), whereas patients with non-generalized peritonitis had a significantly higher occurrence of obstruction, 68.3% (521/763) versus 8.9% (18/203) (P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Postoperatively, patients with generalized peritonitis were significantly more likely to be admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU), 38.4% (78/203), compared with patients without generalized peritonitis, 14.8% (113/763) (P < 0.001) (Table 5). Furthermore, they experienced both a significantly longer ICU stay (3.56 days vs. 2.98 days, P = 0.018) and a longer length of hospitalization (9.75 days vs. 6.46 days, P < 0.001). Postoperative complications and reoperations were also significantly more frequent in patients with generalized peritonitis, 86.7% (176/203) versus 68.9% (526/763) (P < 0.001), and 34% (69/203) versus 21% (160/763) (P < 0.001), respectively. The rates of in-hospital, 30-day, and 90-day mortality were all significantly higher in patients with generalized peritonitis (Table 5).

Discussion

This study explores the long-term outcomes of generalized peritonitis in patients undergoing major emergency abdominal surgery, an area with limited existing research. The study reported four-year overall and conditional survival when the time at risk began 30 and 90 days after surgery. Patients with generalized peritonitis had significantly lower four-year overall survival compared to patients without generalized peritonitis, and this difference was also observed among those alive 30 days after surgery. However, among patients alive 90 days after surgery, this association was no longer present.

Due to the urgent and critical nature of major emergency abdominal surgery, the primary focus has been on survival during the early postoperative phase. Consequently, most reports on mortality and morbidity concern the 30- and 90-day periods following surgery. Long-term outcomes are most often reported as survival or mortality rates up to one year [20, 23, 33–35]. A few studies have reported two-year survival rates of 60–75% among patients undergoing emergency laparotomy [25, 39]. One study investigated the effect of intermediate care versus standard surgical care and found that only half of the patients were alive after six years [40]. Two studies of patients older than 80 years undergoing emergency laparotomy reported that more than half were still alive after three [26] and four years [41]. Building on the existing literature, this study demonstrated comparable survival rates for the overall population, with two-, three-, and four-year survival rates of 72%, 67%, and 63%, respectively.

Previous studies have provided valuable insights into survival after major emergency surgery and have identified areas requiring further exploration, with generalized peritonitis being a notable concern. It is well known that generalized peritonitis is a severe critical condition, and short-term mortality rates vary depending on patient-related factors, the perioperative course, and management. Generalized peritonitis has been shown to be an independent risk factor for mortality within 30 days [9, 10, 42, 43], and if intensive care is required, the 30-day mortality rate is as high as 42% [44]. In the long term, two-year survival rates in patients with generalized peritonitis have been reported to range from 60 to 75% [28, 31, 45, 46], and after 5 years, 52% [47], whereas among patients older than 80 years, only 23% were alive [48]. In our study, with four years of follow-up, 53% of patients with generalized peritonitis were alive compared with 66% of patients without generalized peritonitis, demonstrating a significant difference in overall survival between the two groups.

Most studies report long-term outcomes as cumulative survival or mortality rates. This includes the critical postoperative period, during which the most severely affected patients die or experience postoperative complications. In the present study, conditional survival analysis was performed. Analyzing updated survival rates among patients alive after the initial critical postoperative period may help clarify the effect of time on prognostic factors known to influence outcomes after major emergency abdominal surgery. In this way, the dynamic nature of survival probabilities may be explored in a more accurate manner, providing clinicians with better estimates to support decision-making and more accurate information about patients’ chances of recovery.

This study builds on previous research by assessing the long-term effects of generalized peritonitis in patients undergoing major emergency abdominal surgery, using both overall survival and conditional survival among patients alive at 30 and 90 days after surgery. The study showed that among patients alive 30 days after surgery, there was still a significantly lower four-year survival rate in those with generalized peritonitis than in those without. In contrast, among patients alive 90 days after surgery, the significant difference in survival between the two groups was no longer observed. These findings suggest that although generalized peritonitis is an important factor in the initial postoperative period, it may not play a major role beyond 90 days of survival.

In the current study, the risk factors associated with long-term mortality for both overall survival and 30-day conditional survival were age > 70 years, ASA ≥ 3, generalized peritonitis, and intra-abdominal malignancy. In line with the present study, Thorsen et al. investigated long-term mortality in PPU and found that age ≥ 60 years, active cancer, hypoalbuminemia, pulmonary and cardiovascular disease, and severe postoperative complications were associated with long-term mortality among patients alive 30 days after surgery [31]. That study did not consider the extent of contamination, which in the present study was found to be associated with long-term mortality despite survival beyond the first 30 days; however, the results were still comparable in showing that age and cancer played an important role.

While no change in the risk pattern was demonstrated between overall survival and 30-day conditional survival, surviving at least 90 days after surgery showed that only age > 70 years, ASA ≥ 3, and intra-abdominal malignancy remained independent risk factors. A few other studies have applied a similar conditional survival approach. One study investigated long-term survival in patients undergoing emergency laparotomy when time at risk began 90 days after surgery and reported ASA > 2, CCI > 5, duration of surgery > 60 min, and medical complications as prognostic factors [27]. Another study investigating the incidence of PPU over 40 years showed a marked decline in five-year survival with ASA > 2; however, among patients surviving 100 days after surgery, only those with severe comorbidities (ASA ≥ 4) had a very low survival rate [49].

The present study found intra-abdominal malignancy to be strongly associated with poorer long-term survival, with no apparent impact of elapsed time after surviving the first 30 and 90 days. Similarly, Schneider et al., in a study of severe secondary peritonitis in the intensive care unit, reported a one-year mortality rate of 77.4% after ICU admission; however, among patients who recovered from peritonitis and survived beyond four months, the mortality rate was only 17.8%, and the prognostic factors were older age and advanced tumour disease, with no time-varying effect [44]. This corresponds well with other studies on emergency abdominal surgery in cancer populations [24, 50, 51]. Although generalized peritonitis carries a significant short-term mortality risk, patients who survive the initial critical period appear to have a relatively good prognosis. In contrast, for patients with concomitant malignant disease, the prognosis remains considerably worse regardless of survival following peritonitis. Future research is needed to investigate the relative contribution of malignancy and peritonitis to mortality in major emergency abdominal surgery, as these patients represent a heterogeneous group in whom survival is also determined by oncological factors such as tumour type, stage, and disease burden. This was beyond the scope of the present study.

As the current study and a few others indicate, perioperative factors seem less influential on long-term mortality when patients survive at least 90 days after surgery, whereas severe comorbidities such as cancer and high ASA score may be the primary drivers of poor outcomes. Interestingly, even in a severe condition such as generalized peritonitis, patients who remain alive at least 90 days after surgery have similar long-term outcomes to patients without generalized peritonitis. Accordingly, among patients with generalized peritonitis who survive the initial postoperative phase, long-term survival appears comparable to that of other patients undergoing major emergency abdominal surgery. These findings underscore the importance of optimizing management during the first 90 postoperative days, as this early period seems pivotal for longer-term survival. Tailored in-hospital treatment combined with structured post-discharge follow-up may therefore provide meaningful benefits. Conversely, in patients with a very limited likelihood of long-term survival, a treatment strategy prioritizing symptom control and quality of life may represent a more appropriate and humane alternative to major emergency surgery.

Because the excess mortality associated with generalized peritonitis is concentrated in the first 90 postoperative days, this period represents a key window for intensified surveillance and supportive care. Structured post-discharge follow-up is not routinely incorporated into the care pathway for major emergency abdominal surgery. However, given the higher early mortality among patients with generalized peritonitis—particularly those of advanced age and with a high ASA score—post-discharge assessment may support recovery and enable earlier detection of complications. The surgical pathway should therefore include risk stratification based on these factors to facilitate timely multidisciplinary follow-up after discharge.

The study has several limitations. It is a retrospective single-centre study, which by nature limits the generalizability of the findings, especially in relation to hospital resources and protocols for managing emergency abdominal surgery. However, the study reflects real-world clinical conditions, which strengthens its external validity and increases the applicability of the findings. Selection bias was mitigated by including all patients requiring major emergency abdominal surgery. However, excluding patients who were assessed as ‘not fit for operation’ may have omitted important information. Furthermore, some patient records lacked detailed clinical information, and relevant data on organ dysfunction, sepsis-related biomarkers, and scoring systems such as SOFA score and lactate levels were not consistently available, limiting our ability to adjust for important markers of clinical severity. As a consequence, residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. Additionally, hemoperitoneum was included as part of the secondary peritonitis classification in the study, which may be debatable, as it leads to chemical rather than infectious peritonitis if not accompanied by purulent or faecal contamination. This may introduce misclassification bias; however, sensitivity analyses excluding hemoperitoneum yielded similar results. The study population was heterogeneous with respect to underlying malignancies, indications for surgery, and operative procedures, all of which may have influenced survival outcomes. Although multivariable adjustment was performed, these factors limit the ability to fully attribute survival differences to the variables included in the models. All included variables were tested for collinearity using correlation analysis and calculation of the variance inflation factor. CCI was correlated with age, since age is part of the CCI score, leading to the decision to exclude CCI from the Cox regression analyses. No other variables showed concerning values.

In conclusion, the main findings of this study show that patients with generalized peritonitis have significantly lower four-year overall survival than patients without generalized peritonitis. However, among patients who survived beyond 90 days following surgery, the previously observed differences in long-term survival were no longer evident. Multivariable analysis identified age greater than 70 years, ASA score of 3 or higher, intra-abdominal malignancy, and generalized peritonitis as independent risk factors associated with poor long-term survival. Notably, however, the prognostic significance of generalized peritonitis was lost when the analysis was restricted to patients surviving beyond the 90-day postoperative threshold.

This study introduces a novel temporal dimension to the understanding of major emergency abdominal surgery and generalized peritonitis, offering clinicians and researchers a more nuanced framework for interpreting long-term outcomes and refining prognostic expectations based on the time elapsed since surgery.
