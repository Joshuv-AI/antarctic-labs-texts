LDDHybridNet: an ROI-aware CNN–LSTM hybrid framework for accurate and early leaf disease detection in precision agriculture
Authors: Ch Santoshi, Suresh Kumar Mandala, L Raghavendar Raju
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly and accurate detection of plant leaf diseases is an essential requirement for precision agriculture, given their severe impact on global food security. While much has been done recently, many deep learning–based approaches will still fail in real-world tests because of challenges such as background clutter, differences in illumination, occlusion, or the fact that visual symptoms for these diseases can be very subtle early on. Traditional CNN- and Transformer-based architectures generally lack accurate lesion localisation and interpretability, hindering their practical deployment in agricultural decision-support tools. To address these issues, we present LDDHybridNet, a region-based, explanation-friendly deep learning framework that can identify leaf disease at an early, accurate stage. It then applies preprocessing steps guided by ROI, based on leaf segmentation from the U-Net, followed by a compact CNN-based spatial feature-extraction framework. We arrange spatial feature embeddings extracted from lesion regions into an ordered sequence and employ a Bi-LSTM with attention to model structured contextual dependencies, allowing progression-aware feature learning without requiring actual temporal image sequences. Lastly, Grad-CAM–based post-hoc explainability is employed to interpret model decisions, enabling transparent visualisation of disease-relevant regions. We conduct extensive experiments on the PlantVillage benchmark and the FieldPlant dataset and show that LDDHybridNet consistently outperforms representative CNN, transformer, and hybrid baselines across multiple evaluation metrics. Although the near-ceiling performance on PlantVillage reveals the dataset’s artificial nature, the proposed framework achieves 95.37% accuracy under real-world field conditions and 92.84% on weak-lesion early-stage samples, demonstrating the method’s robustness and early-stage detection potential. The performance boosts are statistically significant (P < 0.01). In general, LDDHybridNet is an interpretable and robust deep learning framework for leaf disease detection, which can support data-driven crop protection and precision agriculture applications.Keywords: Leaf Disease Detection, Precision Agriculture, CNN–LSTM Hybrid Model, Region of Interest Extraction, Explainable Deep LearningSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Plant leaf disease epidemics threaten regional and global crop yields, food security, and agroecosystem sustainability—introduction (max 15 Bullet points). Early and precise detection of crop disease is essential to avert crop losses and facilitate timely interventions. Traditional manual inspection methods are subjective, time-consuming, and non-scalable. Automatic plant disease diagnosis using deep learning technologies is a new research hot spot, with potential significance for ensuring the growing food demand while agricultural land is limited1,2. However, research indicates that these models do not generalise well in data-invariant field settings, which are characterised by variable ambient lighting, cluttered and occluded background scenes that change over time, and shifts between laboratory and field imagery domains3,4. Hybrid and transformer-based models have attempted to tackle multi-scale lesion patterns and global context5,6, but they remain primitive for early-symptom detection, lacking a specialised module to segregate disease-specific regions. Furthermore, current studies are challenging to interpret and offer little guidance on the biological logic underpinning model predictions7,8.

These limitations indicate the possibility of a novel deep learning framework with robust ROI extraction, improved learning of spatial–temporal features, and, most importantly, interpretable decisions. This paper aims to develop a unified architecture for precise early leaf disease identification (LDI) with explainability across controlled & field conditions. LDDHybridNet performs: (i) the two-stage ROI pipeline (U-Net + YOLO) that uses input ROI image patches to guide feature extraction while remaining fully connected using a low-dimensional global representation offered through vision transformer (ViT) architecture; (ii) multi-scale ASPP PE that combines local and global representations with ViT-based global representation that captures more global context while modeling many channels; and (iii) the attention mechanism through both the SE and CBAM attention mechanisms as feature selection approaches for advanced feature selection, and (iv) end-to-end tracking of the lesion through the temporal consistent Bi-LSTM having the global temporal modeling ability with the attention mechanism as a light-weight learner to model the query representation of the temporal context for the lesion progression modeling. This work provides the following novelties: (i) a precise ROI-isolating method operating in a two-stage fashion; (ii) a unified spatial–temporal hybrid architecture capable of differentiating and capturing high-resolution, fine-grained and evolving disease cues; (iii) improvement on the early-stage detection capability; and (iv) biologically meaningful interpretability represented in a new layer through validating Grad-CAM.

Approaches to recent precision agriculture trends have been centred more on deployment-oriented, near–real-time visual analytics rather than on aspects of early-phase plant monitoring, especially for early-stage plants in resource-constrained settings. Deep learning solutions compatible with Edge have already proven practical for plant-based analysis, achieving lower latency and reduced computational load for real-time seed detection and germination evaluation on embedded platforms9. More specifically, we may even find lightweight semantic segmentation networks for agricultural images10. Our results underscore the need for region-wise processing, a small architecture, and post-processing for feature extraction of real-life farm scenarios. Informed by these advancements, we thus take a deployment-aware design approach in this work, aiming for robustness, interpretability, and early-disease sensitivity, rather than necessarily validated real-time performance.

This paper has the following contributions. It exhibits substantial end-to-end benchmark superiority over state-of-the-art CNN, transformer and hybrid models on the PlantVillage and FieldPlant datasets;2) it shows a good generalisation under difficult field conditions;3) it discerns each element via ablation and statistical significance analyses;4) it offers explainability-driven visual reasoning, which can potentially guide practical agronomic decision-making.

Related work

It summarises CNN-based, Hybrid, ROI-aware, Explainable, and deployment-oriented techniques for reviewing recent developments in deep learning-based leaf disease detection and identifying their limitations, which motivate our proposed LDDHybridNet framework.

Deep learning paradigms for leaf disease detection

Deep learning has revolutionised automated leaf disease detection by moving away from handcrafted features and integrating feature learning into end-to-end training on raw images. CNN-based models were found to be superior in terms of accuracy, robustness, and scalability over traditional machine learning methods for plant disease classification in a few early comparative studies11,12. Later review papers systematically chronicled this evolution and identified CNN-based pipelines as the method of choice across a variety of crops and imaging conditions13–17. These studies highlighted the capacity of deep architectures to learn hierarchical representations that relate to the proper colour, texture, and morphology of lesions and are essential for distinguishing diseases. Nonetheless, they have also pointed out recurring difficulties with background clutter, variability in disease stage, and limited generalisation across crops, which have led to persistent motivations for architectural improvements18–20.

CNN architectures and transfer learning in crop-specific disease detection

A significant amount of research has been directed toward developing CNN networks customised for different crops and disease classes. VGG-based models21, EfficientNet variants5,22,23, and compound-scaled CNNs24 have shown strong performance with light-weight and scalable networks. Transfer learning13,25 is a common approach for small annotated datasets8 that allows the models to generalize faster and better for rice26,27, apple22, grape24,28, maize29, jasmine30, and citrus2,6 and tomato crops31. Although they can yield high classification performance under controlled conditions, such methods generally process whole-leaf images, which (I) often dilute disease information via spatial averaging and (II) also hamper early-stage symptom detection, especially in complex field environments32–34.

Hybrid deep learning models with sequential and contextual learning

To improve representational power over spatial features, hybrid deep learning models that combine CNNs with sequence learners such as LSTM, BiLSTM, and GRU have gained popularity. Such architectures harness contextual and temporal dependencies, which are vital for disease progression analysis and challenging background modelling. Prommakhot et al. Some common approaches include CNN feature extractors + BiLSTM and CNN feature extractors + transformer-based sequential learning to enhance discriminability1, and CNN–BiLSTM fusion, which improves robustness for real-time tomato disease detection3. Contextual hybrid models that measure spatio–temporal imagery and multispectral data have also been implemented for agricultural monitoring35,36. Overall, these studies show that CNN–RNN hybrids work better than CNNs alone. Still, most existing works do not provide explicit spatial attention mechanisms to extract disease-specific regions for sequential learning.

ROI-Aware, segmentation, and localised disease analysis

Over the last couple of years, activities have focused on ROI (Region of Interest) learning and localised disease-detection approaches to enhance the precision of early diagnosis. In this regard, patch-based and lesion-centric strategies were found to improve crop generalisation by minimising reliance on global leaf structure37,38. Then, this localisation of disease provides greater interpretability and reliability39. Particularly in the context of detecting tomato diseases, object detection-based models address this problem in two significant aspects: the recognition of small lesions when partially covered and under complex background conditions31. Real-time dataset development and field deployment analyses focused on ROI-guided computation to minimize the inference cost at the expense of accuracy preservation32. However, most ROI-aware methods are spatially static and do not directly model the temporal evolution of the disease.

Explainable, multimodal, and deployment-oriented frameworks

This indicates the increasing deployment of AI systems in precision agriculture and, hence, an increasing demand for interpretability, contextual awareness, and real-world feasibility. Grad-CAM and attention visualisation and other explainable AI (XAI) techniques have been used to confirm where the model is focused so that it can be trusted5,6, as evidenced by numerous reviews that highlight that XAI is an essential facilitator for adoption4,14,26. Multimodal disease prediction studies have also been conducted in parallel, in which multimodal environmental and Internet of Things (IoT) sensor streams, in addition to image data, were fused to provide disease risk and potentially an early warning system25,40–42. However, citizen science imagery and UAV-based sensing research also identified user-scaling advantages and data-quality challenges7. On the other hand, lightweight and edge-friendly models have been proposed to facilitate the real-time operation of agriculture NPUs optimised detection models8. While these methods improve functionality, they also introduce architectural overhead and are not well-integrated, lacking a standard design for understanding ROI, temporal modelling, and explainability.

A critical review of the literature has revealed remarkable advancements in CNN-based, hybrid, ROI-aware, and explainable leaf disease detection systems. Nevertheless, current approaches usually consider these aspects in a decoupled manner. Despite continuous advancements, there remains an evident need for a single framework that simultaneously incorporates ROI-aware spatial learning, temporal modelling of disease progression, and interpretability, whilst being computationally efficient enough for precision agriculture. First, this gap has been the primary motivation for the proposed LDDHybridNet, which locally blends and integrates (after extraction) ROI-guided CNN feature extraction with LSTM-based sequential learning to yield LDD that is accurate, early, and interpretable. Datasets and acquisition

To validate that the LDDHybridNet framework is robust and practical, we trained and evaluated it on a small-scale, but diverse, representative subset of publicly available datasets. The choice of these datasets helps train a model suitable for a home lab environment. Still, it generalises better at the field level across challenging environmental conditions such as background clutter, occlusion, and lighting.

The main dataset used for training is the PlantVillage dataset43, a comprehensive, high-quality collection of 54,738 images of healthy and diseased leaves from 14 crop species and 38 disease classes. This allows these images to be produced in a controlled lab environment, enabling pretraining and ablation studies on a base model. The images are of fixed size and taken under the same lighting conditions, making it a noiseless platform for learning spatial disease pattern recognition (Gupta).

To test generalisation in the field, it uses the FieldPlant dataset44. It includes approximately 5,170 real images for research, taken in a real farming field under normal ambient light, with shadows, background noise, and leaf occlusions. It does not struggle with slight realistic distortions during the testing phase, as they are what would be encountered in field-deployment scenarios, thereby aiding generalisation.

System overview

We propose LDDHybridNet, a region-based deep learning framework to detect leaf diseases in precision agriculture in an accurate and interpretable manner while tackling the challenges of (i) background clutter; (ii) subtle or invisible lesions; and (iii) early recognition of leaf diseases. It relies on spatial learning guided by ROI and contextual feature modelling to represent disease when recognised from static field images robustly.

LDDHybridNet follows an end-to-end pipeline having five interrelated stages: (i) image input through image acquisition, (ii) image preprocessing and ROI extraction, (iii) deep spatial feature representation via a CNN backbone, (iv) contextual dependency modeling with an LSTM module and ultimately, (v) supervised disease classification with post-hoc explainability. We collect raw images from state-of-the-art benchmark leaf disease datasets that contain different disease classes, lighting conditions, and occlusion situations. These images undergo normalisation and generic noise suppression to stabilise the following processing steps.

ROI extraction is an important preprocessing step, during which disease-relevant regions are separated. The background complexity is then suppressed using edge-aware masking techniques whilst preserving the structurally significant symptomatic regions of the leaf. The ROI-masked output image is then passed into a CNN encoding network that converts spatial patterns from small areas of the image into a low-dimensional feature vector that describes important textural, colour, or shape information of the lesion.

Figure 1 Each CNN embedding generates a vector for each local patch, which itself encodes spatial features. To account for contextual dependencies across those spatial features, the CNN embeddings are sequenced to form an ordered sequence of spatial feature vectors, indicating a row-wise and column-wise progression pattern across the corresponding ROI. This ordering isn’t a feature of the temporal observations, but rather encodes spatial dependencies/structure. This ordered representation is processed by an LSTM network, which propagates contextual information across spatial regions, allowing the model to learn progression-aware feature relationships characteristic of both disease severity and distribution.

The significance of Grad-CAM, however, is that it functions as a post-hoc visualisation method and is not involved in either feature learning or decision-making.

Design rationale and architectural justification

LDDHybridNet is motivated by a problem-driven architectural philosophy rather than a heuristic stack of popular deep learning modules. Three core challenges arise in early leaf disease detection for precision agriculture: (i) subtle leaf lesion patterns typically lie within complex visual backgrounds, (ii) there is an innate sequential nature of disease progression, yet temporal annotations are rarely explicit, and (iii) agronomic trust and adoption require transparent decision-making. We introduce components within the proposed framework, each of which targets one of these challenges without overlap and with a clear functional motivation.

Initially, we use ROI-aware preprocessing to spatially limit feature extraction to the leaf regions relevant to the disease. Previous works have also shown that symptoms can be blurred in early images due to background noise and non-informative leaf areas. By serving as a locality before isolating lesion-dominant regions, the model reduces computational overhead and can learn from fine-grained pathological cues, thereby improving sensitivity to early manifestations of the disease.

Secondly, since convolutional feature extraction enables the effective capture of localised texture, colour variation and edge formation regarding leaf diseases, it is used as the primary spatial encoding strategy. Instead of having different levels of attention or multi-scale enhancement modules stacked on top of each other, it has an intentionally lean backbone that retains discriminative power without introducing a computationally expensive architecture. This selection provides sufficiently robust spatial representations while still being efficient enough for precision agriculture workflows.

Third, an additional Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) layer is incorporated to capture the implicit dynamics of disease progression. While specific leaf images likely do not directly encode time-series data, the spatial patterns associated with disease time-series evolution (e.g., lesion spread, morphological change, and severity gradients) can be inferred from spatial images across regions of interest. The LSTM allows the network to learn relationships between the extracted spatial features, thereby obtaining progression-aware representations without requiring longitudinal image sequences. Such a design provides a computationally efficient approach to temporal modelling while maintaining the capacity for context reasoning based on the transformer.

Several of the issues addressed in the framework are not entirely new to research, but, importantly, they treat explainability as a separate design objective rather than a trade-off-affecting component. Only for the sake of visualising the rationale behind the trained model’s decisions, we incorporate Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM), ensuring that biologically meaningful lesion regions drive the predictions. Grad-CAM does not affect feature learning or classification predictions; instead, it provides a validation process to confirm that our model remains interpretable visually and agronomically.

As such, the derived architecture is a lightweight but discrete combination of ROI-driven visual-spatial learning and multi-level temporal contextual modelling, not during training explanation. Each module has its specific function, and we don’t use any components for redundancy or trend-based designs. In contrast, existing approaches stack modules without a clear rationale, whereas LDDHybridNet integrates modules in a principled manner through its design. In addition, following ablative observations corroborate that removing ROI guidance or substituting LSTM with a spatial pooling layer consistently results in performance deterioration, reiterating the indispensability of these components in our architecture.

All preprocessing and augmentation steps are applied after fold construction to avoid data leakage. Only the training folds are augmented, while validation and test folds remain unchanged. Baseline models and comparative framework

To rigorously evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed LDDHybridNet framework, it is compared with a variety of leading state-of-the-art models for leaf disease detection and image classification, including convolutional, transformer-based, hybrid, and ROI-aware models. We choose these baselines such that they cover all the cases where the contribution of (i) ROI-aware preprocessing, (ii) multi-scale and attention-based spatial encoding, and (iii) temporal modelling can be isolated.

The first is the standard CNN backbones, where each network is trained end-to-end on full images without performing ROI extraction, namely VGG16, ResNet50, DenseNet121, MobileNetV3-Small, and EfficientNet-B0. They provide a solid foundation for adopting deep feature-extraction-based approaches across a range of computational budgets and are well documented in the literature for plant disease classification. They provide examples of the distances these well-designed, lightweight CNNs can achieve without explicit modelling of either time or ROIs.

Secondly, to assess the advantages of global context modelling using transformers, we introduce a Vision Transformer (ViT-B/16) baseline trained on 224 × 224 leave images. Such a model learns long-range dependencies and patch-level relations, enabling comparisons between pure self-attention architectures and the CNN–LSTM hybrid design employed in the LDDHybridNet approach. A CNN–LSTM baseline, which utilises a standard ResNet-based encoder followed by a unidirectional LSTM, is also implemented to evaluate the advantages of leveraging bidirectional temporal modelling and attention-based fusion.

Finally, we compare with the most similar frameworks to our setting: ROI-aware and hybrid frameworks. These were the previously proposed LeafDiseaseNet (ROI-unaware CNN with SE blocks), as well as the HybridLeafDiseaseNet (ROI-based hybrid CNN–ASPP–CSAM–LSTM backbone). We also evaluate an ROI + CNN-only variant (U-Net + YOLO + CNN, without LSTM or temporal attention) to separate the role of temporal modelling. Collectively, these baselines provide an extensive comparison framework, establishing that the advantages in accuracy, robustness, and early-stage disease detection provided by the explicit incorporation of ROI-aware spatial encoding, multi-scale attention, and temporal sequence learning with LDDHybridNet are consistent within controlled laboratory and field environments.

Robustness under field conditions

To assess the robustness of LDDHybridNet, its ability to generalise to FieldPlant images collected in an uncontrolled agricultural setting with varying natural light conditions, complex background clutter, overlapping leaves, and natural occlusions was also tested. Even with these challenges, we achieved 95.37% accuracy, demonstrating that the model generalises well beyond the constrained PlantVillage environment. For most disease categories, performance degradation was minimal. However, there was some confusion among visually similar lesions, namely between early blight and late blight, under simulated low-light or high-shade conditions.

For a more in-depth understanding of misclassified samples, we inspected them individually. We saw that errors generally occurred when the leaf was only partly visible due to background clutter (e.g., soil textures, crop residues) or surface properties (e.g., water droplets), or when a high gradient of illumination obscured the lesion’s structures. Finally, overlapping leaves or differences in angle introduce temporal inconsistency between pairs of ROIs and, in some cases, cause prediction uncertainty. These inconsistencies were avoided mainly by Bi-LSTM and attention-based temporal context modelling, which helped stabilise the feature representation across frames.

We show the model’s predictions on a subset of the dataset and observe that disease-region localisation is challenging to perform correctly, as shown in the example fields and their predicted labels. One remarkable aspect we noticed from LDDHybridNet is that, despite the naturally complicated problem (i.e., LDD), the model can still extract the most relevant areas for a disease by utilizing ROI extraction and refining Grad-CAM attention, which suggests that the time-space integrated image recognition can provide substantial improvement in interpretability and validation of the decisions made. These results demonstrate that the framework maintains a high degree of performance under conditions closer to those encountered during caretaking tasks, as would be expected in typical precision agriculture scenarios.

Early-stage disease detection analysis

In precision agriculture, early-stage autonomous disease detection is essential for prompt intervention. LDDHybridNet is used for the detection of early-stage leaf diseases, which exhibit limited, localised, and mild visual symptoms (e.g., light discolouration, smooth-texture distortion, and low-intensity lesion patterns) and are naturally widespread before high-level infection. Due to its sensitivity to these faint symptomatic cues, our proposed model obtains an accuracy of 92.84% on a curated balanced subset of early-stage leaf images, significantly outperforming representative baseline models (over 5% for EfficientNet-B0 (86.31%), and over 7% for ViT-B/16 (84.92%), and CNN–LSTM hybrids (88.40%).

Much of this improvement was due to the proposed ROI-guided pipeline. Even for small or imperfect disease lesions, isolating lesion-susceptible areas and suppressing background content made them easier for the CNN-LSTM encoder to interpret. The multi-scale ASPP module then boosts detection by capturing local texture differences, subtle venation distortions, and early necrotic gradients that traditional CNNs overlook. This was augmented by the ViT head to provide global contextual cues to distinguish an early blight–like anomaly from natural leaf pigmentation.

Discussion

Despite the tremendous progress made by deep learning techniques for the most recent leaf disease detection works, the task of leaf disease detection in a real-world agricultural environment is still challenging due to a high level of intra-class variabilities, presence of subtle symptoms in initial stages, illumination variabilities, presence of cluttered backgrounds and domain shifts between laboratory and field images. Although CNN-based models tend to perform well on cleaned datasets, their generalisation ability drops significantly in unconstrained field——like conditions. Several Transformer-based and hybrid models partly mitigate scale variability but often do not incorporate explicit strategies to isolate biologically relevant regions in their interpretive outputs that implicate causative lesions, thus undermining their agronomic decision-support capacity.

In this regard, LDDHybridNet demonstrates the ability to learn spatial constraints from ROI without visually distracting syntactic components, thereby increasing robustness, especially for weak-lesion early samples. The framework focuses feature extraction on leaf regions obtained via segmentation, which reduces background interference and is more sensitive to faint discolouration and localised texture distortions. Structured feature sequencing with Bi-LSTM-based contextual modelling enables the network to learn dependencies across spatial features, which is particularly useful when symptoms may be sparse or not visually separable.

But the experimental results also show some significant failure modes, especially in harsh field conditions. This typically leads to performance degradation when early-stage lesions have a visual appearance indistinguishable from natural leaf texture variations, heavy occlusions obscure lesion boundaries, or when the chromatic cues are dominated by illumination artefacts. This may not isolate disease-relevant regions as fully as one would like, resulting in incomplete or noisy feature representations. The preprocessing step shows that there is still inherent sensitivity to image and segmentation quality in uncontrolled settings, despite the advantages of ROI-guided pre-processing.

By progressively ablating combinations of components, the analysis indicates that the performance increase is caused by the components’ interactions in a mutually exclusive manner and is not due to any specific module. Though attention mechanisms and global context modelling help reduce discrimination, errors driven by visually confounding patterns (e.g., nutrient deficiencies or symptoms of abiotic stress) cannot be eliminated. As expected, the Grad-CAM visualisations show that, in most cases, the model attends to regions relevant to the lesion; however, being a post-hoc method, Grad-CAM provides only coarse localisation, which may not accurately localise features contributing to the prediction, especially in failure cases.

In general, they seem to imply that LDDHybridNet has been solved in the field and is deployable, which is far from the truth and, in fact, the opposite; in fact, if we look at all the discussion of the results. Strengths are higher relative sensitivity over all early stages, interpretability, consistency across datasets; weaknesses highlight the need for more comprehensive datasets, more robust ROI, and complementary sensing modalities.”

Limitations of the Study

There are, however, some limitations to this study, despite the good overall performance. First, the evaluation is based on only two publicly available datasets (with limited geographic and crop diversity), which could potentially hinder generalisation to unseen fields. Second, the success of our approach relies heavily on accurate ROI extraction; strong occlusion, motion blur, or extreme lighting conditions may severely degrade segmentation quality and, in turn, classification results. Third, despite its improved interpretability, Grad-CAM offers post-hoc explanations rather than complete feature attribution or causal explanations. Lastly, no quantitative benchmarking for deployment on edge or resource-constrained platforms is provided, thereby limiting claims about real-time or field implementations.

To overcome these limitations, it shall be necessary to use more extensive multi-region datasets, stronger segmentation strategies, explainability contrasts, and systematic evaluation on deployment-efficient hardware. These guidelines lay the foundation for future generalisations towards scaling up and building trustworthy AI in agriculture.

Conclusion and future work

In this paper, we introduce an ROI-aware CNN–LSTM hybrid framework, LDDHybridNet, for plant leaf disease detection, focusing on effective spatial feature representation learning, contextual dependency modelling, and explainability. Where ROI-guided preprocessing and structured feature modelling guided the approach, the engineered method efficiently directs focus to lesion-specific regions while limiting contamination from background noise, illumination variation, and partial occlusions, which are typical in agricultural images. Extensive exhaustive experiments conducted on the PlantVillage and FieldPlant datasets demonstrate that LDDHybridNet consistently outperforms all representative CNN-, transformer-, and hybrid-based baselines across various evaluation metrics, including accuracy, F1-score, and AUC, and with respect to its sensitivity to weak-lesion early-stage samples. Additionally, Grad-CAM–based interpretability analyses demonstrate that the model’s predictions are guided by biologically relevant lesion locations, thereby providing transparency and confidence in the diagnostic outputs. The results show that the framework proposed here provides a reliable, interpretable method for automatically analysing leaf disease in precision agriculture pipelines. Although these results are promising, the present study is not without limitations. The framework has been evaluated on a small number of datasets with limited geographic and crop workflow diversity and is only as effective at ROI extraction in challenging imaging environments. In addition, this work did not investigate the generation of deployment benchmarks for edge or embedded platforms, although the architecture is intended for computationally constrained environments. Future work will extend this approach to broader validation using multi-regional field datasets covering different climate and crop species contexts. The generalisation to heterogeneous agricultural environments may also be improved by extending the framework through domain adaptation, self-supervised learning, and meta-learning. Another potential avenue to enhance early disease detection is to include additional sensing modalities, like hyperspectral imagery, Unmanned Aerial Vehicle-based data or environmental metadata. Last, it will be essential to investigate alternative explainability methods beyond post-hoc Grad-CAM and to evaluate the effectiveness of lightweight, deployment-oriented implementations so that agricultural intelligence systems can be scalable and practically implementable.
