Resting state electroencephalography alpha activity predicts cognitive scores in rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder
Authors: Raphael Angerbauer, Martijn Piet, Kang-Min Choi, Si On Park, Ki-Young Jung, Alan Jung Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIdiopathic rapid eye movement (REM) sleep behavior disorder (iRBD) is characterized by the loss of atonia during REM sleep, causing dream-enacting behavior. Although iRBD occurs without any clear signs of neurodegenerative disorders, most patients with iRBD eventually develop Parkinson’s disease or dementia with Lewy bodies, typically accompanied by cognitive decline. Hence, identifying a biomarker that reflects a neurophysiological state of iRBD has therapeutic potential. Here, we show that spatially clustered alpha (8–12 Hz) oscillatory activities in the scalp can predict cognitive performance in patients with iRBD. A cohort of 62 Korean patients with iRBD underwent resting-state electroencephalography (rsEEG) recordings and participated in the Montreal Cognitive Assessment tests, a common measure for cognitive function. Spectral analysis of the rsEEG data revealed that overall power and transient bursting parameters of alpha activity negatively correlated with Montreal Cognitive Assessment test scores. These results accounted for potential confounding factors such as the spatial distribution of the electrodes, age, sex, emotional states, and medication use. This finding was specific to the alpha activity because theta (4–8 Hz) and beta (12–30 Hz) oscillatory activities were not correlated with the cognitive test scores. Thus, these results suggest that clustered resting-state alpha activity is associated with cognitive impairments in iRBD. Our findings emphasize the importance of rsEEG dynamics in cognitive assessment and highlight the potential utility of rsEEG as an early biomarker for cognitive decline in iRBD patients.Keywords: iRBD, EEG, alpha power, alpha burst, MoCA

Introduction

EEG measured at a resting-state (rsEEG) captures spontaneous electrical activities of the brain when an individual is awake and at rest, providing valuable insights into cognitive function [1]. Both static and transient rsEEG metrics reflect neural dynamics that support cognition [2, 3]. Particularly, frontal area EEG activities in theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), and beta (12–30 Hz) bands are implicated in attention, cognitive control, and working memory [4, 5].

Patients with idiopathic rapid eye movement (REM) sleep behavior disorder (iRBD) lack the ability to remain completely still during REM sleep, a condition known as REM-atonia, causing violent enactment of dreams [6]. These patients often develop Parkinson’s disease and dementia with Lewy bodies [7, 8]. Indeed, deficits in attention, executive function, and verbal memory in iRBD are well known [9, 10]. Moreover, individuals with iRBD exhibit abnormal rsEEG activity, including increased low-frequency (0.5–7 Hz) power relative to high-frequency (7–30 Hz) power, a phenomenon known as electroencephalography (EEG) slowing [11]. While EEG slowing has been linked to mild cognitive impairment within iRBD populations [12], its reliability as a cognitive marker remains debated due to inconsistent findings [13]. The relationship between static rsEEG power in theta, alpha, and beta bands and cognitive performance remains unclear. While some studies found a decrease in alpha power with decreasing cognitive test scores [14, 15], others reported the opposite [12]. These inconsistencies are likely due to cohort heterogeneity, including proximity to phenoconversion, age and sex differences, or comorbidities such as depressive symptoms.

Here, incorporating potential confounding factors such as age, sex, emotional status, and medication treatment, we directly tested whether static and transient rsEEG metrics, particularly in the alpha, theta, and beta bands, can predict cognitive performance in individuals with iRBD. This work highlights rsEEG metrics as a potential biomarker for cognitive deficits in iRBD.

Materials and Methods

Subject selection and cognitive evaluation

This study was authorized by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Seoul National University Hospital (IRB Number 1406-100-589, 1708-169-883). More comprehensive information on inclusion criteria and EEG recording procedures is described elsewhere [16]. The participants diagnosed with iRBD were recruited from the sleep clinic at Seoul National University Hospital. The diagnosis was made through overnight video-polysomnography recordings, following the criteria outlined in the International Classification of Sleep Disorders, third edition. To ensure potential neurodegenerative conditions, each patient underwent a comprehensive neurological evaluation by two neurologists. This evaluation screened for signs of dementia, cerebellar ataxia, Parkinsonism, or other neurodegenerative diseases. Patients were excluded from the study if they had an existing neurodegenerative or neurological disorder, a severe medical illness, or severe obstructive sleep apnea (defined as an apnea–hypopnea index ≥30). Participation in the study was voluntary, and all participants provided written consent before enrollment.

Participants underwent the Korean version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) to assess their general cognitive capability [17, 18]. These cognitive evaluations and EEG scans were often acquired on different days, and participants who had an interval of more than a month were excluded from the study.

E‌EG recording and preprocessing

Scalp EEG was recorded using a 60-channel EEG cap arranged in the international 10–10 system (Wave-Guard EEG cap, Advanced NeuroTechnology, Enschede, Netherlands). A reference electrode was placed on the earlobe, and the AFz electrode was used as the ground electrode. Electrode impedance was kept under 10 KΩ. Two electrooculogram electrodes were placed on the lateral canthi to account for eye-movement artifacts. The sampling frequency was set as 400 Hz. Resting-state EEG was recorded for a total of 5 min, while subjects were opening and closing their eyes every 30 s, in order to maintain a vigilant state [16]. EEGLAB was used for subsequent pre-processing [19]. Recordings were referenced to the average over all electrodes and filtered using both a 0.5 Hz high-pass filter and a 60 Hz notch filter. Recordings were then decomposed using Independent Component Analysis (ICA), and eye artifacts were automatically removed using the ICLabel plugin, with the probability threshold set to 90 percent [20]. After the removal of artifacts and epochs with poor signal quality based on visual assessment, 100 s of concatenated eyes-closed segments were analyzed. All pre-processing and metric calculations were done using MATLAB version R2023b [21].

Feature extraction

Bandpower

The preprocessed EEG signals were normalized by the root-mean-square of the entire signals, meaning the square root of the squared signal average over the entire recording, to account for impedance differences between electrodes. Subsequently, time-frequency information of the signal was extracted using complex Morlet wavelet convolution with a time-bandwidth product of 60, symmetry parameter of 3, and 10 voices per octave. Power densities were obtained by averaging the power component of the transformed time series over all time points. These values were further aggregated into three frequency bands: theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), and beta (12–30 Hz).

Burst detection

To measure transient, periodic EEG activity, we utilized the extended Better OSCillation Detection (eBOSC) toolbox [22, 23]. EEG signals consist of both periodic (oscillatory) and non-periodic components. The non-periodic component, which follows a 1/f distribution [24], represents all non-oscillatory activity in the EEG, corresponding to periods when no bursting occurs. To isolate the non-periodic component, a 1/f distribution was fit to the power spectrum of each electrode. The frequency range of interest was excluded from this fitting process to avoid distortion caused by oscillatory activity [25]. A power threshold was established to identify periods of bursting activity. The threshold was set at the 95th percentile above the fitted 1/f distribution (Supplementary Figure S1, A). This threshold distinguished supra-threshold activity indicative of bursting from sub-threshold activity. To define bursting episodes, we applied a minimum duration criterion. Only episodes lasting at least three cycles were considered bursting events. The power threshold was then applied to the wavelet-transformed signal. To exclude smoothing artifacts created by the wavelet transformation, the first and last seconds of the signal were excluded. To accurately detect bursting episodes at the edges of the signal, additional padding of 0.5 s is applied which was removed after burst detection. After identifying supra-threshold activity, a binary detection matrix was generated. This matrix indicated the time points where the power of the signal exceeded the power threshold for each frequency (Supplementary Figure S1, B).

The eBOSC toolbox allows to further refine the detected oscillatory activity into specific bursting episodes of a specific duration and frequency [23]. This process involved sparsifying the binary detection matrix so that a bursting event that spans many frequencies is now reduced to one frequency. Bursting episodes of a certain frequency are then defined as stretches of uninterrupted bursting activity of this frequency, only including the episodes which exceed the aforementioned temporal threshold of three cycles (Supplementary Figure S1, C). Edge artifacts introduced by the wavelet transform are removed using “MaxBias” postprocessing which involves simulating the smoothing effect of wavelet convolution and tries to correct for it. For each bursting episode, the burst length was defined as the duration of the episode in seconds. Burst power was calculated as the average power during the episode. An episode was attributed to a specific frequency band if the episode frequency was within the range of this band.

Statistics

Statistical analysis was performed using MATLAB version R2023b [21]. MoCA scores were not normally distributed (Shapiro–Wilk test, p < .05), necessitating data transformation. We thus rank-transformed the scores and computed the partial Spearman correlation coefficient to quantify the relationship between EEG power or burst statistics and MoCA scores, considering covariates such as age, sex, depression status, and drug treatment.

We initially considered using a linear mixed-effects model (LMM), a statistical approach that accounts for both fixed effects (such as age or treatment) and random effects (such as individual variability or group clustering), to quantify the association between EEG features and MoCA scores. LMMs are particularly valuable when dealing with repeated measures or hierarchical data structures. While our dataset is hierarchically structured as signals from many electrodes stem from one participant, LMM is not an ideal measure in our case, as it does not take into account the spatial relationship between signals from each electrode. Instead, we chose to use a cluster correction procedure where clusters of electrodes showing spatially significant associations are identified. To determine the association between EEG features and MoCA scores at each electrode, we elected to calculate partial Spearman’s rho, a non-parametric measure of correlation. It quantifies the relationship between MoCA scores and EEG metrics while adjusting both variables for covariates. This represents an advantage over linear regression, where only the EEG metrics would be adjusted [26]. In addition, Spearman’s rho offers a clear interpretation as an effect size, capturing the strength and direction of the monotonic relationship between the variables on a standardized scale from –1 to +1. All calculations were performed using MATLAB’s partial correlation function. For categorical comparisons, we used the Mann–Whitney U test or the Kruskal–Wallis H-test, depending on the number of groups. Statistical significance was set at p < .05.

Cluster correction

To correct for multiple comparisons across all electrodes simultaneously, cluster correction (Supplementary Figure S2) was performed to identify a cluster of electrodes showing a significant correlation between cognitive test scores and EEG power or burst statistics, considering covariates including age, sex, depression status, and drug treatment. The cluster correction process assumes that only clusters of a certain size correspond to true effects, while smaller clusters may represent only spurious correlations. This method was chosen because any true effect should be observed in many adjacent EEG electrodes due to volume conduction. The Spearman correlation coefficient was calculated for each electrode, and adjacent electrodes showing a significant correlation of the same sign were identified. Adjacency was determined using Delaunay triangulation by employing the Delaunay triangulation function in MATLAB. The output was checked visually. To identify clusters of electrodes showing significant correlation, we performed permutation tests. Power values were shuffled between patients for each electrode, and the Spearman correlation coefficient was calculated for each electrode as described above. Covariates were not shuffled. Then, clusters of adjacent electrodes showing significant correlation were identified, and the size of the largest cluster was saved. This process was repeated 10 000 times to generate a distribution of the size of the largest clusters. If the size of the original cluster fell above the 95th percentile of this distribution, all of its electrodes were considered to show a true correlation between EEG power and cognitive test scores. This method resolves the issues of multiple comparisons across spatially distributed EEG electrodes. No other method of multiple comparison correction was used.

Results

iRBD patient demographics

This study included 42 male (mean age: 65.5 ± 0.9) and 19 female (mean age: 66.1 ± 1.2) patients with iRBD (Figure 1A). Sixty-one participants were evaluated using the Korean version of the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-K), and most of them (n = 61, 74.2 per cent) were normal (GDS-K < 16) (Figure 1B). Motor function was evaluated by the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) for 47 participants, and most of them (n = 30, 63.8 per cent) showed normal function (UPDRS = 0) (Figure 1C). To assess the impact of medication use, participants were stratified into four groups: those taking neither clonazepam nor melatonin (C−/M−; n = 15, 24.6 per cent), clonazepam only (C+/M−; n = 11, 18.0 per cent), melatonin only (C−/M+; n = 23, 37.7 per cent), and both medications (C+/M+; n = 12, 19.7 per cent) (Figure 1D).

We first explored associations between these covariates and cognitive performance measured by MoCA scores. MoCA scores showed negative correlations with age, GDS-K, and UPDRS scores (Figure 1E–G). No significant differences in MoCA scores were observed across the medication groups (Figure 1H). Although all patients were treated with 2 mg of melatonin, the dosage of clonazepam was either 0.25 or 0.5 mg, and this dosage difference did not affect MoCA scores (0.25 mg: 15 patients, 25.07 ± 1.42; 0.5 mg: 9 patients, 24.71 ± 0.85; p = .45; mean ± SEM).

A recent study demonstrated that subdividing the MoCA into anterior and posterior cognitive domains improves the prediction of phenoconversion to dementia in iRBD [27]. To explore this relationship in the context of predicting cognitive function, we grouped MoCA subscores into anterior (clock drawing, attention, verbal fluency, and executive function) and posterior domains (abstraction, delayed recall, visuospatial, orientation, naming, and sentence repetition). The anterior domain is functionally associated with the frontal lobe, while the posterior domain corresponds to the function of the temporal, parietal, and occipital lobes [27]. Anterior subscores negatively correlated with age (Supplementary Figure S3A) and GDS-K scores (Supplementary Figure S3B), but showed no relationship with UPDRS (Supplementary Figure S3C) or medication use (Supplementary Figure S3D). Posterior subscores negatively correlated with both age (Supplementary Figure S3E) and UPDRS (Supplementary Figure S3G) but not GDS-K scores (Supplementary Figure S3F). Similar to total and anterior MoCA scores, posterior subscores did not differ between the medication groups (Supplementary Figure S3H). Overall, these findings bolster the importance of accounting for covariates when assessing EEG features associated with cognitive performance.

Clustered EEG alpha activity predicts cognitive test scores

Frontal EEG electrodes have been used to study cognition [4, 28]. We first undertook conventional approaches to test whether rsEEG measured at frontal electrodes (Figure 2A) can predict MoCA test scores in iRBD patients, without considering individual demographic information. Two randomly selected frontal electrodes showed inconsistent results. Both FCz and Fpz electrodes displayed a dominant alpha-band peak, but the overall power spectral density was lower in the Fpz electrode (Figure 2B). Representative time-frequency plots from these electrodes revealed frequent alpha activity, while beta and especially theta activity appeared sparser (Figure 2C). Correlations between average EEG power in the theta, alpha, or beta bands and MoCA test scores showed a significant negative correlation between theta power and MoCA scores in the FCz electrode (Figure 2D), whereas no significant correlation was found for all frequency bands in the Fpz electrode (Figure 2E). These inconsistent findings highlight the importance of considering the spatial distribution of all electrodes and demographic information in EEG analysis.

Taking multiple comparisons across all 60 electrodes and covariates, such as age, sex, depression status, and drug treatment, can result in spurious findings. To avoid this, we applied a cluster-based correction, as illustrated in Supplementary Figure S2. This method is based on the graph theory in which adjacency between electrodes is determined using Delaunay triangulation [29]. The resulting adjacency structure is shown in Figure 3A. A key assumption of this method is that adjacent electrodes exhibit similar EEG signals due to volume conduction [30]. To validate this, we calculated Spearman correlation coefficients between signals from all electrode pairs and averaged the results based on the distance between electrodes. Distance was defined as the minimum number of paths between electrodes on the previously created adjacency graph (Figure 3A). This analysis revealed a clear decrease in correlation as a function of distance (Figure 3B), supporting the hypothesis that adjacent electrodes are influenced by volume conduction, thereby justifying the use of cluster-based correction.

We next assessed the relationship between rsEEG power in clusters of electrodes and MoCA test scores. Specifically, we calculated Spearman partial correlation coefficients to account for MoCA score distributions and covariates, including age, sex, depression status, and drug treatment. UPDRS scores were not included as a covariate because they were available for 75 per cent of the participants, and among them, 63.8 per cent had normal scores (UPDRS = 0) (Figure 1C). The resulting p-values were corrected using the cluster correction method (Supplementary Figure S2). As shown in Figure 3C, alpha band power in large areas spanning anterior-to-posterior regions negatively correlated with total MoCA scores. This relationship was not found in the theta and beta bands. A potential explanation for this could be that the activities in theta and beta bands were sparse (Figure 2C), leading to a dilution of the effect due to prolonged periods of low activity. To address this, we extracted bursting episodes in each frequency band. We calculated Spearman correlation coefficients between bursting activities and cognitive test scores, accounting for age, sex, depression status, and drug treatment. The resulting p-values were corrected using the cluster-based correction (Supplementary Figure S2). This analysis revealed that alpha burst power in a wide range of electrodes negatively correlated with MoCA scores. Burst power for theta and beta bands did not show any significance (Figure 3D). Additionally, we applied these analyses to anterior and posterior MoCA subscores [27]. While each MoCA subscore may involve a broader range of brain regions due to the complexity of the tasks, we observed that alpha band power and burst power were negatively correlated with the posterior and anterior subscores, respectively (Supplementary Figure S4). Together, these findings emphasize the importance of accounting for spatial dependencies and covariates in EEG analyses and demonstrate that clustered EEG alpha activity can predict cognitive performance in iRBD patients.

Discussion

We found a significant relationship between rsEEG activity and cognitive performance in patients with iRBD. Specifically, rsEEG alpha activity clustered across the brain negatively correlated with MoCA scores, suggesting that clustered alpha activity reflects cognitive performance in this population. This association remained robust even after controlling for confounding factors such as age, sex, depression levels, and medication use. Thus, our study identified a potential biomarker that could characterize the cognitive status of patients with iRBD.

We found that increased alpha activity correlates with lower cognitive performance in patients with iRBD. One possible explanation is that it reflects a compensatory mechanism in response to underlying neural dysfunction [31]. However, rather than being beneficial, this increased alpha power could interfere with task-relevant cortical dynamics, leading to impaired cognitive performance [32, 33]. Alternatively, elevated alpha activity may indicate cholinergic disruption, since symptoms of RBD are associated with a dysregulated cholinergic pathway [34]. Furthermore, cholinergic denervation is associated with reduced cognitive performance in Parkinson’s disease and Lewy body dementia [35]. However, it is unclear if acetylcholine-dependent signaling affects alpha rhythms [36]. While our results cannot distinguish between these hypotheses, future studies in animal models incorporating pharmacological or optogenetic manipulations could help clarify whether increased alpha power reflects compensatory adaptation, pathological disinhibition, or a combination of both mechanisms.

Recently, EEG features such as connectivity, network topology, and aperiodic (1/f) EEG activity have been shown to be informative in iRBD and Parkinson’s disease [31, 37–39]. Extending this line of research, the removal of the aperiodic signal allows us to examine transient burst activities, which may reflect more dynamic and transient brain processes that have not yet been investigated in iRBD [25]. To our knowledge, this is the first study demonstrating transient alpha-bursting events as a biomarker for cognitive decline in iRBD. Specifically, we found that the power of alpha-band bursting activity negatively correlates with cognitive performance, mirroring the relationship observed for overall static alpha power. Given that transient alpha activity has been implicated in cognitive processes such as attention, working memory, and cognitive control [40, 41], alterations in bursting dynamics may have important implications for cognitive decline in iRBD. Notably, the aperiodic 1/f distributed signal was removed to detect the periods of bursting events, but was included in all subsequent analyses (see Materials and Methods). Given that the aperiodic components themselves may reflect different features of cognition, future studies may therefore extend their role in cognitive decline in iRBD.

Methodologically, we applied cluster-based correction to account for volume conduction between neighboring electrodes. This allows us to identify functionally relevant brain areas rather than individual electrodes. While this approach reduces the risk of spurious correlations, it may also be overly conservative, as significant clusters often encompass multiple potential sources of activity. Future studies could mitigate this limitation by increasing the number of electrodes or employing spatial smoothing techniques such as conditional autoregression [42].

Our model provides a valuable foundation for future work. Notably, most participants exhibited minimal errors on MoCA tests, and we accounted for this factor in our analysis by using Spearman’s rho, which does not assume homoscedasticity of the data. Although the MoCA test is commonly used in clinics, developing more sensitive cognitive assessments that can parse out subtle differences in cognition will significantly improve diagnostic precision. Additionally, we have six patients (<10.3 per cent) who developed Parkinson’s disease, multiple system atrophy, or dementia with Lewy bodies within 1 to 6 years after undergoing EEG recordings and MoCA testing. Furthermore, the subjects included in this study are ethnically homogenous. Future research with a more diverse cohort, sensitive cognitive and psychiatric assessments, and longitudinal EEG recordings will help determine clustered alpha activity as a potential biomarker for early cognitive changes and disease onset.

In summary, our results highlight an association between clustered alpha activity and the level of cognitive function in patients with iRBD, providing further insight into the neurophysiological markers of cognitive decline in this high-risk population. Future studies should explore whether these findings extend to other prodromal neurodegenerative conditions and investigate the mechanistic role of alpha activity in cognitive impairment.
