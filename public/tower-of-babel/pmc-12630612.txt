Achieving upper-branch steady-state solutions for inerter-damped cable parametric vibration via targeted runge-kutta initial conditions
Authors: Zengwei Guo, Shuangqing Xu, Xingyu Tang, Guowen Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Increasing the span length of stay cables markedly increases their susceptibility to parametric resonance. This study investigates mitigating parametric resonance in stay cables using a parallel inerter damper and focuses on a methodology to obtain the upper-branch steady-state solution via Runge–Kutta integration. The approach computes both transient and steady-state responses under arbitrary support excitation. Furthermore, we examine the dependence of the Runge–Kutta steady-state solution on initial conditions by analyzing phase portraits of the cable–inerter system. Based on the method of multiple scales, we propose a general procedure to select initial conditions that ensure convergence to the upper-branch steady-state solution. The results show that different initial conditions drive the transient Amplitude along different phase trajectories and may converge to distinct steady-state solutions. The inerter damper modifies the critical phase trajectory of the cable–damper system, thereby shifting the feasible region of initial conditions. Thus, when initial conditions from the undamped case are used in the presence of an inerter damper, the Runge–Kutta solution may converge to the lower-branch stable solution, which may overestimate the damper’s control effectiveness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24456-z.

Introduction

Cables are slender structural elements widely used in civil, mechanical, aerospace, and marine engineering1. Owing to their high flexibility and large slenderness ratios, these one-dimensional structures are prone to large amplitude vibrations and complex dynamic behavior under various excitations2,3. Among cable applications, cable-stayed bridges are particularly critical, making vibration control essential. Their slender configuration and long-span capability have made cable-stayed bridges a dominant solution for long crossings, with main spans now exceeding 1,000 m4. As span length increases, stay cables become more flexible, with lower mass per unit length and reduced intrinsic damping. This condition increases susceptibility to vibrations, including parametric resonance induced by parametric excitation from support motion. In parametric resonance of a stay cable, the displacement response can be large even when the excitation amplitude is small5. Such sustained vibrations can induce fatigue damage, compromise the anticorrosion system, and potentially lead to cable failure, thereby jeopardizing bridge safety and durability6–8. Therefore, effective vibration-control strategies are crucial to ensure the structural integrity and serviceability of modern cable-stayed bridges7,9,10.

Mechanical dampers are widely installed in bridges to mitigate cable vibrations. Although viscous dampers (VD) are the most commonly used, their control performance for parametric resonance is limited11 because the additional damping provided by VD is insufficient for effective control. Inerter-based dampers (IBDs) offer an alternative; prior studies report improved vibration mitigation. Hwang12 introduced a rotational inertial damper into a single-degree-of-freedom system, and the study revealed that the equivalent damping ratio of the structure increased significantly from 1.14% to 17%. Ikago13 compared systems equipped with tuned vibration mass dampers (TVMD) and traditional VD and found that TVMD achieved greater vibration reduction. Lazar14 computed the dynamic responses of multi-degree-of-freedom structures equipped with inerter damper, tuned mass damper (TMD), and viscous damper (VD); both inerter damper and TMD outperformed VD. Lu15 demonstrated effective cable-vibration control using viscous inertial mass dampers (VIMD).

Although extensive research have examined the control performance of inerter-based dampers for wind-induced and rain-wind-induced cable vibrations, their effectiveness for parametric resonance requires further study because the governing equations contain time-varying parameters that induce strongly nonlinear behavior. Consequently, accurately computing the transient and steady-state responses of the cable–inerter system remains challenging. Typically, four approaches are used to determine the steady-state response of parametric resonance in stay cables: (Ⅰ) finite element (FE) methods, (Ⅱ) experimental methods, (Ⅲ) the method of multiple scales, and (Ⅳ) direct time-integration (time-marching) methods (e.g., Runge–Kutta, Newmark-β, central difference). FE methods offer high accuracy and flexibility and can capture various structural modes, but constructing an accurate FE model is nontrivial16–19. Experimental methods provide high reliability but are costly and time-consuming because they require scaled models and dynamic loading facilities20–22. The method of multiple scales provides analytical insight into system dynamics and enables rapid, intuitive results; however, it is best suited to simple systems or specific scenarios and may not capture all aspects of complex behavior23–25. Numerical difference methods, such as the Runge–Kutta method, are applicable to complex nonlinear vibration systems and can handle diverse boundary conditions and non-uniformities. However, the steady-state solution obtained by the Runge–Kutta method can depend strongly on the initial conditions—inputs that are required but often assumed during the solution process26–28.

Typically, the method of multiple scales and Runge–Kutta time integration are used jointly to determine the steady-state response of parametric resonance in cables and to cross-validate the results29–32. In most cases, when implemented correctly, the two approaches agree. However, during frequency-sweep analyses, the Runge–Kutta solution may not coincide with the multiple-scales solution within certain frequency ranges because of improperly chosen initial conditions33–35. This initialization sensitivity is a hallmark of multistable, internally resonant systems and has been explicitly documented in time-marching studies36,37. Prior studies have shown that Runge–Kutta integration exhibits strong dependence on initial conditions in multiple-solution systems. For example, Li20 and Su38reported that different initial displacements or velocities in cable–beam and TMD vibration problems can steer the numerical solution to the lower-branch or to unstable responses, thereby preventing accurate recovery of the system’s steady-state behavior. These studies highlighted that initial conditions determine the convergence path of the numerical solution; however, they focused primarily on numerical phenomena and did not elucidate the underlying dynamics that give rise to this dependence. Similarly, Demšić et al25. compared Runge–Kutta schemes with other numerical methods and emphasized that, despite their high accuracy, initial conditions still determine whether the solution converges to the upper- or lower-branch in parametric-resonance problems, and this sensitivity appears unavoidable.

In recent studies on capturing the large-amplitude upper branch of cable parametric resonance, Wang and Zhao26 used the shooting method combined with continuation to trace the complete frequency–response curve, including unstable segments, while Macdonald39 and others also relied on continuation-based approaches to compute periodic solutions under support excitation, overcoming the limitations of direct time integration. Kundu and Gupta40employed path continuation to compute the upper-branch solution and mitigate dependence on initial conditions. Subsequently, He et al41. investigated stay-cable parametric resonance using Galerkin discretization and direct integration, with numerical scanning to identify multivalued responses; other works adopted the harmonic balance method with path-following and Floquet analysis to obtain upper and lower branches and to assess stability.Although these approaches succeed in computing large-amplitude solutions, they remain primarily algorithmic: they typically do not account for critical trajectories in phase space, do not offer a mechanism-based explanation for initial-condition sensitivity, and do not provide a systematic strategy for selecting or optimizing initial conditions.

In contrast to prior studies, this work combines the method of multiple scales with phase-portrait analysis to reveal how initial conditions govern trajectory evolution and demonstrate that heightened sensitivity in the vicinity of the saddle point is the primary mechanism underlying initial-condition dependence. Building on this insight, we develop an initial-condition optimization strategy grounded in amplitude–phase relations and amplitude–displacement conversion, enabling numerical integration to converge reliably to the upper-branch solution. We further incorporate a parallel inerter damper into the parametric-resonance analysis of stay cables and show that adding an inerter shifts the critical phase trajectories, thereby redefining the feasible region of initial conditions. These results both clarify the mechanism underlying this dependence and provide practical tools for improving the accuracy of damper-control assessment. Finally, we detail the sensitivity of Runge–Kutta steady-state solutions for stay cables to initial conditions and present a systematic procedure for selecting appropriate initial conditions to avoid missing the true steady-state response in parametric resonance.

Nonlinear vibration equations of stay cable with parallel inerter damper

As the span length of stay cables increases, their flexibility grows, making them more susceptible to vibrations under stochastic excitation. These vibrations cause periodic variations in cable tension, which can, in turn, trigger large-amplitude parametric resonance in stay cables. Installing dampers at both ends of the stay cables is a feasible and essential measure to suppress such vibrations and enhance structural safety. To derive the governing equations of parametric resonance for a stay cable equipped with a parallel inerter damper, the following assumptions are made42:

1 The gravity-induced sag of the stay cable is approximated by a parabolic profile.

2 The cable’s flexural, torsional, and shear rigidities are negligible.

3 The constitutive relation of the stay cable follows Hooke’s law.

4 Damping in both the stay cable and the inerter damper is modeled as linear viscous damping.

Runge-Kutta solution of nonlinear vibration and its initial condition dependence

Dependence of Runge-Kutta solution on initial conditions

The Runge–Kutta method is a widely used, single-step, high-accuracy algorithm for engineering applications. Owing to its adaptability and efficiency, it is well suited to numerically solving initial-value problems of differential equations. In essence, Runge–Kutta schemes are explicit time-integration methods that require selecting an appropriate integration scheme and suitable initial conditions. The single-step update of the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method is given by:72\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${y_{n+1}}={y_n}+\frac{1}{6}({k_1}+{k_2}+{k_3}+{k_4})$$\end{document}

where73\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}{l}} {{k_1}=f({t_n},{y_n})\Delta t} \\ {{k_2}=f({t_n}+\frac{{\Delta t}}{2},{y_n}+\frac{{{k_1}}}{2})\Delta t} \\ {{k_3}=f({t_n}+\frac{{\Delta t}}{2},{y_n}+\frac{{{k_2}}}{2})\Delta t} \\ {{k_4}=f({t_n}+\Delta t,{y_n}+{k_3})\Delta t} \end{array}} \right.$$\end{document}.

Consequently, the next value yn+1​ is determined by the current value yn​ and the estimated increment over the time step Δt. This increment is a weighted average of four intermediate slopes, denoted k1, k2, k3, k4​. Given an initial response at t = 0, the Runge–Kutta method explicitly computes the solution at any time tn ​. The governing equation for parametric resonance is a second-order, nonhomogeneous differential equation with variable coefficients. When solving Eq. (13) using the Runge–Kutta method, the initial displacement and velocity of the cable must be prescribed.

Evidently, the Runge–Kutta solutions lie on the frequency–amplitude curves obtained from the multiple-scales solutions, confirming their correctness. Moreover, the frequency–amplitude curve becomes multivalued within certain frequency-ratio ranges. In these regions, three amplitude states exist: the upper-branch stable solution, the unstable solution, and the lower-branch stable solution23. For the parametric resonance of stay cables, the steady amplitude is governed by the match between the excitation frequency and the structure’s phase-lag–energy-input relation. Under the phase-resonance perspective47–49, the force–displacement phase difference is not fixed at 90°; as the frequency ratio ω/ω1 varies, an off-quadrature (non-90°) phase condition can arise, producing multivalued solutions at the same frequency ratio—besides the original large-amplitude upper branch, a small-amplitude lower branch appears, with an unstable solution between them.

Regarding Fig. 5: the second-order superharmonic resonance case (Fig. 5a) shows a single-peak then roll-off curve and, after ω/ω1 ≈ 0.50, never reaches the emergence threshold, so no multivalued solutions occur. In primary resonance (Fig. 5b), as ω/ω1​ increases from about 1.05 to 1.10, this threshold is crossed, generating a small-amplitude lower branch associated with an off-quadrature phase, together with an unstable solution. The 2:1 principal parametric resonance (Fig. 5c) exhibits the same generation of solutions near ω/ω1 ≈ 2.10. Comparing multiple-scales solutions with and without an inerter damper shows that the damper shifts the location of the multivalued regions and narrows their width in primary resonance and second-order superharmonic resonance, thereby reducing the peak amplitude.

Moreover, the inerter damper affects the initial-value dependence of the Runge–Kutta solution. As shown in Fig. 5, the Runge–Kutta steady-state solution of the cable–inerter system may not coincide with the upper-branch stable solution under the same initial conditions that yield the upper branch for the cable without an inerter damper. Figure 6 presents the transient displacement responses of the cable with and without an inerter damper at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{\omega}/\overline{\omega}_{1}$$\end{document}=1.1 and initial conditions q(0) = 0.03, q̇(0) = 0, which correspond to the upper-branch stable solution of the stay cable without inerter dampers. Comparing Fig. 6a and b, the steady-state amplitude with an inerter damper is significantly smaller than that without an inerter damper. When the initial conditions of the cable–inerter system are changed from (q(0), q̇(0)) = (0.03,0) to (q(0), q̇(0)) = (0.03, −0.01), the transient displacement response with the inerter damper is shown in Fig. 6c. As shown in Fig. 6c, the steady-state amplitude is considerably higher than in Fig. 6b, indicating that the reduction observed in Fig. 6b arises from unsuitable initial conditions rather than from the control effect of the inerter damper. Therefore, initial values that yield the upper-branch solution for the cable without inerter dampers may fail to produce the upper-branch solution when an inerter damper is present. Thus, the presence of an inerter damper modifies the initial-condition dependence of the Runge–Kutta solution. If the initial conditions from the undamped case are used, the Runge–Kutta steady-state solution of the stay cable’s parametric resonance with a damper may lie on the lower branch, potentially leading to misjudgment of the damper’s control effectiveness. Our conclusion is consistent with prior control studies indicating that nonlinear controllers can reshape frequency-response curves and stability characteristics under resonance conditions50.

The phase portraits of parametric vibration. Notes: 1. The arrow indicates the direction of the trajectory line along time t̅;2. indicates the starting point of the phase trajectory line.

In the phase portraits, points in the phase plane represent the system state, whereas trajectory lines depict its dynamics. The phase portraits of the cable’s parametric resonance are periodic in φ with a period close to 2π. Within one 2π interval, three singular points—K1, K2 and K3​—appear in both cases (Fig. 7a and b). K1 and K3​ are foci associated with stable steady-state solutions, whereas K2​ is a saddle associated with an unstable steady-state solution. The variation of phase trajectories is consistent with the frequency-response curves (Fig. 5). Most trajectories asymptotically approach the two stable singular points, K1 and K3​, reflecting the system’s long-term behavior. Trajectories passing near the saddle K2 are transient and do not linger. In particular, trajectories near K2​ are highly sensitive to initial conditions: those starting inside the region bounded by the critical line are attracted to the focus K3​ and converge to the upper-branch stable solution, whereas the others are attracte to K1 and converge to the lower-branch stable solution. Small perturbations to the initial conditions can redirect the solution toward K1​ or K3, producing marked changes in the system state. The initial conditions set the starting point of the trajectory, and the critical line delineates the bifurcation between the upper- and lower-branch solutions. If the starting point is not on the critical line, the trajectory will eventually approach one of the two stable solutions, which explains how initial conditions affect the steady-state solutions computed by the Runge–Kutta method.

By comparing the phase portraits with and without an inerter damper, it is evident that the damper not only changes the amplitude and phase of the parametric resonance, but also modifies the critical phase trajectory and the region of suitable initial values. With an inerter damper, the trajectories exhibit smaller amplitudes and phase shifts. This occurs because the damper absorbs and redistributes vibrational energy, thereby reducing the vibration amplitude and adjusting the vibration phase. In addition, the inerter damper alters the governing equations that describe the system’s evolution in the phase plane, leading to shifts in the convergence region of stable solutions and changes to the critical line. Consequently, the inerter damper can determine whether the final solution lies on the upper-branch or lower-branch stable solution.

The solution process of the Runge–Kutta method in the phase plane can be summarized as follows. Starting from an initial point, the Runge–Kutta steady-state iteration advances to the next point by computing an estimated slope k to set the forward direction and using a time step Δt to set the forward distance. The newly reached point then serves as the next starting point, and the same procedure is repeated. The resulting path of the Runge–Kutta steady-state iteration approximates a specific phase trajectory up to a terminal point. Consequently, different initial values drive the Runge–Kutta solution along different trajectories, yielding either the upper-branch or the lower-branch solution. This mechanism underlies the initial-value dependence of the Runge–Kutta solution. To attain the steady-state solution, suitable initial values must be prescribed so that the Runge–Kutta trajectory is attracted to the correct steady state.

The reasonable initial values of Runge-Kutta large-amplitude steady-state solution

In light of the origin of the initial-value dependence of the Runge–Kutta solution, the critical line passing through the saddle point K2​ in the phase plane delineates the boundary between the upper- and lower-branch solutions. Accordingly, initial values that yield the steady-state solution can be determined from this critical line. Conclusions and outlook

In this study, the vibration equations for a stay cable with a parallel inerter damper were solved by both multi-scale method and Runge-Kutta method. After discussing the dependence of Rugge-Kutta solution on initial conditions, the causes for this initial condition dependence were revealed by analyzing the phase portraits of cable-inerter system. Finally, an analytical solution of initial conditions was proposed to achieve the stable solution based on muti-scale method. The following conclusions were reached.

The Runge-Kutta solution for cables’ parametric vibration exhibit a strong dependency on the initial conditions, i.e., initial displacement, initial velocity and initial acceleration. Unsuitable initial conditions hinder the Runge-Kutta method from obtaining stable solutions for cable’s parameter vibrations. This phenomenon can be interpreted and comprehended by analyzing the phase portraits determined by multi-scale method. Specifically, phase trajectories near the saddle point K2 exhibit high sensitivity to initial conditions so as to converge to an unstable solution. Trajectories starting from the region enclosed by the critical line will be attracted to the focal point K3 and finally converge to an upper branch stable solution, while others will gravitate towards the other focal point K1 and finally converge to lower branch stable solution. Initial conditions determine the initial position of the phase trajectories, and the critical line determines the bifurcation of the upper and lower branch solutions in the system. Different initial displacements and velocities prompt the Runge-Kutta transient response to develop along different phase trajectories and converge towards different steady-state solutions. Therefore, the critical phase trajectory traversing the saddle point can be used to determine suitable initial values corresponding to upper branch solutions. This critical phase trajectory can be determined by solving the derived amplitude-phase correlation equations and amplitude-displacement converting equations. Moreover, the inerter damper alters the critical phase trajectory on the phase plane, consequently shifting the region of suitable initial values and impacting the initial value dependence of the Runge-Kutta solution. Using the initial values suitable for the upper branch solution of the cable without dampers, the Runge-Kutta steady-state solution for the cable-inerter system might manifest as the lower branch solution, potentially leading to misjudgments concerning the damper’s control efficacy.

Future work will systematically examine how the inerter damper’s parameters—particularly the damping coefficient, the inertance, and their coupling—affect vibration control performance and steady-state branch selection. In the present study, the cable sag was approximated by a parabolic profile rather than a catenary; while common for preliminary analyses, this simplification may introduce shape-related bias. Subsequent studies will revisit the formulation with catenary and alternative sag representations to quantify and reduce this error.
