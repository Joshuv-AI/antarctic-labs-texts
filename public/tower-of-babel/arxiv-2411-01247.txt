\spacedallcaps Probabilistic Effectivity in the Subspace Theorem
\spacedlowsmallcaps Faustin ADICEAM & \spacedlowsmallcaps Victor SHIRANDAMI

Abstract
The Subspace Theorem due to Schmidt (1972) is a broad generalisation of Roth’s Theorem in Diophantine Approximation (1955) which, in the same way as the latter, suffers a notorious lack of effectivity. This problem is tackled from a probabilistic standpoint by determining the proportion of algebraic linear forms of bounded heights and degrees for which there exists a solution to the Subspace Inequality lying in a subspace of large height. The estimates are established for a class of height functions emerging from an analytic parametrisation of the projective space. They are pertinent in the regime where the heights of the algebraic quantities are larger than those of the rational solutions to the inequality under consideration, and are valid for approximation functions more general than the power functions intervening in the original Subspace Theorem. These estimates are further refined in the case of Roth’s Theorem so as to yield a Khintchin–type density version of the so–called Waldschmidt conjecture (which is known to fail pointwise). This answers a question raised by Beresnevich, Bernik and Dodson (2009).

Introduction

1.1
Effectivity in the Subspace Theorem.

Given an integer
n\geq 2
and vectors
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, denote by
\bm{x\cdot y}
the scalar product between
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
. In its initial version, the celebrated Subspace Theorem due to Schmidt
[
18
]
states the following :

Theorem 1.1

(The Subspace Theorem — Schmidt, 1972)
.

Let
\bm{\alpha}_{1},\dots,\bm{\alpha}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be
n
linearly independant vectors with algebraic coordinates. Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Then, the set of solutions in
\bm{q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}
to the inequality

\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left|\bm{q}\bm{\cdot}\bm{\alpha}_{i}\right|\;\leq\;\left\|\bm{q}\right\|^{-\varepsilon}

(1.1)

lies in a finite union of proper rational subspaces of
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
.

Generalisations including non–Archimedean absolute values are known — see
[
5
]
for further details. An upper bound for the
number
of rational subspaces in this statement can be effectively determined — see, e.g.,
[
19
]
and the related references. One of the most fundamental open problems in Diophantine Analysis is to establish an effective version of this statement; namely, to determine the maximal height attained by the subspaces in the finite union.

The overarching goal undertaken here is to tackle this effectivity problem from a probabilistic point of view by estimating the proportion of algebraic vectors for which the Subspace Inequality admits a large solution; that is, a solution lying in a subspace with large height. Given an integers
Q\geq 1
, this probabilistic approach can be very broadly described as such :

(
Main Problem — General Formulation
)
What is the proportion of the set of
n
–tuples of algebraic vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of fixed degrees such that the Subspace Inequality (
1.1
) admits a nonzero integer solution
\bm{q}
lying in a rational subspace of height at least
Q
?

A number of terms needs to be specified in this question — this will be the purpose of the rest of the introduction culminating with the statement of the main Theorems
1.3
and
1.6
. For the time being, the following points should be noted :

•

the notion of ”proportion of algebraic vectors of fixed degrees” is interpreted in terms of a density function as defined in §§
1.2
&
1.5
. It relies on the definition of a suitable class of height and degree functions for the set of algebraic vectors, which is introduced in §
1.4
;

•

Estimates in terms of density are derived asymptotically from counting problems dealing with finite point sets. To formalise this approach, together with the integer
Q=Q_{1}
measuring the height of the rational subspace appearing in the statement of the Main Problem, another two parameters are introduced : one, denoted by
H\geq 1
, measures the maximal height of the algebraic vectors
\bm{\alpha}_{i}
of fixed degrees, and another one, denoted by
Q_{2}
, bounds the height of the rational solutions
\bm{q}
lying in the subspace.

•

the Main Problem is solved in a quantitative form so as to be able to answer a question such as the following :

How big should the real quantities
H,Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
be chosen under the assumption that
H\geq Q_{2}\geq Q_{1}
to guarantee that at most, say 5%, of the
n
–tuples of algebraic vectors
\bm{\alpha}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
of given degrees and of heights at most
H
yield the existence of a solution
\bm{q}
to the Subspace Inequality (
1.1
) meeting these two properties :
(i)

\bm{q}
has height at most
Q_{2}
and
(ii)

\bm{q}
lies in some rational subspace of height at least
Q_{1}
?

Of paramount importance is the interplay between the parameters
H
(measuring the heights of the algebraic vectors
\bm{\alpha}_{i}
) and
Q_{2}
(measuring the heights of the rational solutions
\bm{q}
). The main results provide a solution to this question when the parameter
H
is larger than
Q_{2}
: this situation, involving the study of the statistical distribution of algebraic vectors, is what is referred to here as the
probabilistic regime
. The other relevant alternative, viz. when the parameter
Q_{2}
is larger than
H
, can be referred to as the
pseudo-deterministic regime
and will not be broached here
3
.

†
†
footnotetext:
3
The pseudo-deterministic regime essentially comes down to taking into account all possible rational solutions
\bm{q}
to the Subspace Inequality lying in some subspace of height at least
Q_{1}
. Solving the Main Problem in this regime is, in fact, little different from the original problem of effectivity : it reduces to finding an effective bound for the heights of the rational solutions in the Subspace Theorem valid for all but a small and quantified set of exceptional
n
-tuples of algebraic vectors of bounded height.

The particular case where the Subspace Theorem is specialised to Roth’s Theorem in Diophantine Approximation is considered with a special interest in the following section. The above approach is then refined, and the obtained results are more complete and more apparent.

1.2
Probabilistic Estimates in Roth’s Theorem.

Roth’s Theorem amounts to considering the case
n=2
and to setting
\bm{\alpha}_{1}=(1,0)
and
\bm{\alpha}_{2}=(\alpha,1)
in Theorem
1.1
:

Theorem 1.2

(Roth, 1955)
.

Assume that
\alpha
is an irrational algebraic number. Then, given any
\varepsilon>0
, the number of solutions in integers
q\geq 1
and
p\in\mathbb{Z}
to the inequality

\left|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}\right|\;\leq\;\frac{\Psi(q)}{q}

(1.2)

is finite when
\Psi(q)=q^{-\varepsilon}
.

More generally, let
\Psi\penalty\ :\mathbb{R}_{+}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
be an approximation function; that is, a nonincreasing function tending to zero at infinity. The Main Problem is considered in the generality where Roth’s inequality (
1.2
) is satisfied for any approximation function satisfying a convergence or divergence condition (of which the original case
\Psi(x)=x^{-\varepsilon}
is a particular example). To this end, given a fixed degree
d\geq 2
and an integer
H\geq 1
, let
\mathbb{A}_{d}(H)
be the set of
real
algebraic numbers of degree
d
and of (naive) height (defined as the maximum of the absolute values of the coefficients in its minimal polynomial) bounded by
H
.

Given integers
Q_{2}>Q_{2}\geq 1
, in view of inequality (
1.2
), the goal is to enumerate the elements of the set
\mathbb{A}_{d}(H)
which, when reduced modulo one, fall inside the union of intervals

J_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\;=\;\bigcup_{Q_{1}\leq q<Q_{2}}\bigcup_{0\leq p\leq q}B^{*}\left(\frac{p}{q},\frac{\Psi(q)}{q}\right).

(1.3)

Here and throughout, for
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
r>0
, one sets

B^{*}(x,r)=\left[x-r,x+r\right]\cap[0,1].

(1.4)

By computing explicitly the implicit constants involved in the classical sieving argument outlined in
[
20
, p.25–28]
, it is not hard to see that, under the assumption that the approximation function
\Psi
is nonincreasing,

\Sigma_{\Psi}\left(Q_{1},Q_{2}\right)-25\cdot\left(\Sigma_{\Psi}\left(Q_{1},Q_{2}\right)\right)^{2}\;\leq\;\left|J_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\right|\;\leq\;\Sigma_{\Psi}\left(Q_{1},Q_{2}\right),

(1.5)

where

\Sigma_{\Psi}\left(Q_{1},Q_{2}\right)\;=\;\sum_{Q_{1}\leq q<Q_{2}}\psi(q)

and where
\left|E\right|
denotes the Lebesgue measure of a measurable subset
E\subset\mathbb{R}
. Of course, the first inequality in (
1.5
) is only of interest when
\Sigma_{\Psi}\left(Q_{1},Q_{2}\right)\leq 1/5
, which restricts the range of relevant parameters
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
. In the general case, the sieving process outlined in
[
20
, p.25–28]
must be pushed further to get a finer lower bound. An alternative and more efficient approach is outlined in §
3.2
below.

Considering as above the set
\mathbb{A}_{d}(H)
reduced modulo one to see if it intersects non-trivially
J_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})
accounts for the invariance modulo one in
\alpha
of the Diophantine inequality (
1.2
). This makes it equally natural to work instead with the set

\mathbb{A}^{\prime}_{d}(H)=\mathbb{A}_{d}(H)\cap[0,1]

(1.6)

(the point is that the height of an algebraic number is not invariant under translation by an integer). The methods developed here are indeed flexible enough to deal with these two cases simultaneously. To simplify the notations, let from now on
\mathbb{X}_{d}(H)
denote either of the sets
\mathbb{A}_{d}(H)
or
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}_{d}(H)
. Here and throughout, given any integer
d\geq 0
, set furthermore

l(d)\;=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{if }d=2,\\
0&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(1.7)

The following is a very particular, simplified case of the much more general statement established in Theorem
3.1
(see §
3.1
) :

Theorem 1.3
.

(Effective probabilistic estimates for the generalised Roth inequality)
Let
\Psi\penalty\ :\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
be an approximation function. Fix a degree
d\geq 2
and integers
Q_{2}>Q_{1}\geq 1
. Then, there exist constants
H(d),H^{\prime}(d),C(d),C^{\prime}(d),C_{1}(\mathbb{X}_{d}),C_{2}(\mathbb{X}_{d})>0
such that upon defining for each
H\geq 1

E_{\mathbb{X}_{d}}(H)\;=\;\begin{cases}C(d)\cdot\sqrt{\left(\log H\right)^{l(d)}/H}&\textrm{ when }\mathbb{X}_{d}=\mathbb{A}_{d}\textrm{ and }H\geq H(d);\\
C^{\prime}(d)\cdot\left(\log H\right)^{l(d)}/H&\textrm{ when }\mathbb{X}_{d}=\mathbb{A}^{\prime}_{d}\textrm{ and }H\geq H^{\prime}(d),\end{cases}

the following effective probabilistic estimates hold :

•

Upper bound for the probability :
let

and

Assume that

\displaystyle\frac{\#\left(J_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\cap\mathbb{X}_{d}(H)\right)}{\#\mathbb{X}_{d}(H)}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\;C_{2}(\mathbb{X}_{d})\cdot\left|J_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\right|\cdot\left(1+\Theta_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\right)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{1-\delta}+\frac{E_{\mathbb{X}_{d}}(H)}{\Theta_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})}\right).

•

let

be the closure of the complement in

of the set

. Let

denote the minimal length of the connected components of

–

, then for any
\delta\in(0,1)
such that
len^{*}\!\left(\widetilde{J}_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\right)<C_{1}(\mathbb{X}_{d})\cdot\delta,

\displaystyle 1-

\displaystyle\frac{\#\left(J_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\cap\mathbb{X}_{d}(H)\right)}{\#\mathbb{X}_{d}(H)}\;

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq C_{2}(\mathbb{X}_{d})\cdot\left|\widetilde{J}_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\right|\cdot\left(1+len^{*}\!\left(\widetilde{J}_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\left(\frac{1}{1-\delta}+\frac{E_{\mathbb{X}_{d}}(H)}{len^{*}\!\left(\widetilde{J}_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\right)}\right).

Furthermore, all the constants are explicit and the quantity
len^{*}\!\left(\widetilde{J}_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\right)
can be determined recursively in an effective way.

To be precise, all quantities appearing above are effectively given in §
3.2
(see Equation (
3.13
) onwards) although no attempt has been made to optimise them (the proofs make it clear that their values can be improved). This statement is therein seen as a particular case of a more general one where the set of algebraic numbers of fixed degree and bounded height is endowed with a large class of discrete probability measures.

Theorem
1.3
can also be interpreted in terms of the concept of upper density naturally emerging in the probabilistic regime. More precisely, given an approximation function
\Psi
, define the upper density of the set of algebraic numbers admitting a large solution to the Roth-type inequality (
1.2

\Psi

\overline{d}(\Psi)\;=\;\limsup_{Q_{1}\rightarrow\infty}\;\;\limsup_{Q_{2}\rightarrow\infty}\;\;\limsup_{H\rightarrow\infty}\;\;\left(\frac{\#\left(J_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\cap\mathbb{X}_{d}(H)\right)}{\#\mathbb{X}_{d}(H)}\right).

(1.9)

Similarly, define the analogous lower density

by replacing the upper limits above by lower limits. Finally, in the case that the upper and lower densities coincide, define the density

Corollary 1.4

(Khintchin–type result for the density of the set of algebraic numbers satisfying a Roth-type inequality)

Given any approximation function
\Psi

d(\Psi)\;=\;\begin{cases}0&\textrm{ if }\;\;\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\Psi(q)<\infty;\\
1&\textrm{ if }\;\;\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\Psi(q)=\infty\textrm{ and if }\;\Psi\textrm{ is monotonic}.\end{cases}

In particular, the density

is well–defined under the above assumptions.

•

1.3
A Reformulation of the Subspace Theorem.

The Main Problem stated in §
1.1
above is tackled in the full generality of the Subspace Theorem from a geometric standpoint for which a projective reformulation of Theorem
1.1
is more convenient. To this end, given a vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}
, denote by
\left[\bm{x}\right]
the line spanned by it seen as an element of the projective space
\mathbb{P}_{n-1}(\mathbb{R})
identified with the Grassmannian
G_{n,1}(\mathbb{R})
. More generally, for each integer
1\leq k\leq n-1
, let
G_{n,k}(\mathbb{R})
denote the set of
k
–dimensional linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
L\in\mathbb{P}_{n-1}(\mathbb{R})
is a projective point, let also
L^{\perp}
be the subspace orthogonal to it seen as an element of the Grassmannian
G_{n,n-1}(\mathbb{R})
. In the case that
L=\left[\bm{x}\right]
for some
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}
, set for the sake of simplicity
L^{\perp}=\bm{x}^{\perp}
.

Fix two lines
L,\Lambda\in G_{n,1}(\mathbb{R})
. The projective distance
\delta(L,\Lambda^{\perp})
between the subspaces
L
and
\Lambda^{\perp}
is by definition the sine of the acute angle between the line
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the hyperplane
\Lambda^{\perp}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. When
L=\left[\bm{x}\right]
and
\Lambda=\left[\bm{y}\right]
for some
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}
, set for simplicity
\delta(L,\Lambda^{\perp})=\delta(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{\perp})
in such a way that

\delta(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{\perp})\;=\;\frac{\left|\bm{x\cdotp y}\right|}{\left\|\bm{x}\right\|_{2}\cdotp\left\|\bm{y}\right\|_{2}}\cdotp

(1.10)

The height
H(L)
of a rational line
L\in\mathbb{P}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})
spanned by a nonzero integer point
\bm{q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is defined as the Euclidean norm of the vector
\bm{q}/\gcd(\bm{q})
(where
\gcd(\bm{q})
is the gcd of the coordinates of
\bm{q}
). When
1\leq k\leq n-1
, the height
H(\Xi)
of a
k
–dimensional rational subspace
\Xi
(that is, of an element
\Xi
in the Grassmannian
G_{n,k}(\mathbb{Q})
) is defined in the usual way as the height of the projective point determined by the Plücker coordinates of this subspace (that is, by the coordinates of the wedge product of any of its rational bases — see
[
17
]
for details).

It is known, see
[
7
, Corollary p.25]
and
[
13
, Lemma 2]
, that given a vector
\bm{q}\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\left\{\bm{0}\right\}
, one always has that
H(\bm{q}^{\perp})\leq\left\|\bm{q}\right\|_{2}
. Furthermore, equality holds whenever
\bm{q}
is primitive (in the sense that
\gcd(\bm{q})=1
). This observation and the above definitions are easily seen to yield the following equivalent projective reformulation of Theorem
1.1
. Here and throughout, the notation
\mathbb{A}
refers to the set of
real
algebraic numbers. Also, given algebraic vectors
\bm{\alpha}_{1},\dots,\bm{\alpha}_{n}\in\mathbb{A}^{n}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}
, each spanning a line denoted by
L_{i}
, recall that the
orthognality defect
of these lines is the real defined as

\xi\left(L_{1},\dots,L_{n}\right)\;=\;\frac{\left\|\bm{\alpha}_{1}\wedge\dots.\wedge\bm{\alpha}_{n}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|\bm{\alpha}_{1}\right\|_{2}\cdots\left\|\bm{\alpha}_{n}\right\|_{2}}\;=\;\frac{\left|\det\left(\bm{\alpha}_{1},\dots,\bm{\alpha}_{n}\right)\right|}{\left\|\bm{\alpha}_{1}\right\|_{2}\cdots\left\|\bm{\alpha}_{n}\right\|_{2}}\cdotp

(1.11)

This ratio is strictly positive precisely when the
L_{1},\dots,L_{n}
are linearly independent. It is bounded above by 1 from Hadamard’s Inequality, with the equality holding precisely when the lines are mutually orthogonal — see
[
22
, eq. (13) p.49]
for further detail.

Theorem 1.5

(Reformulation of the Subspace Theorem)
.

Let
L_{1},\dots,L_{n}\in G_{1,n}(\mathbb{A})
be
n
linearly independent algebraic lines in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
. Then, the set of rational solutions
\Lambda\in G_{1,n}(\mathbb{Q})
to the inequality

\prod_{i=1}^{n}\delta(L_{i},\Lambda^{\perp})\;\leq\;\frac{\xi\left(L_{1},\dots,L_{n}\right)}{H\left(\Lambda\right)^{n}}\cdot\Psi\left(H\left(\Lambda\right)\right)

(1.12)

lies in a finite union of proper rational subspaces of
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
when
\Psi(H(\Lambda))=H(\Lambda)^{-\varepsilon}
. Here,
\xi\left(L_{1},\dots,L_{n}\right)\in\left(0,1\right]
denotes the orthogonality defect of the system
L_{1},\dots,L_{n}
.

1.4
Heights and Degrees of Algebraic Vectors through Semialgebraic Parametrisations.

The analytic nature of the problem (relying on various volume calculations) makes it natural to define the heights and degrees of points in
\mathbb{P}_{n-1}(\mathbb{R})
from a real parametrisation of the projective space
\mathbb{P}_{n-1}(\mathbb{R})
. This also allows for the consideration of a large class of height and degree functions. To achieve this, let

\bm{e}_{0}\;=\textbullet\;\left[1:0:\dots:0\right],\quad\bm{e}_{1}\;=\textbullet\;\left[0:1:\dots:0\right],\quad\;\dots\;\quad,\quad\bm{e}_{n-1}\;=\;\left[0:0:\dots:1\right]

be the canonical basis vectors of
\mathbb{P}_{n-1}(\mathbb{R})
. Extending the definition (
1.10
), let

\delta(\bm{x},\bm{y})\;=\;\frac{\left\|\bm{x}\wedge\bm{y}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|\bm{x}\right\|_{2}\cdotp\left\|\bm{y}\right\|_{2}}

(1.13)

stand for the usual projective distance between the lines
L=\left[\bm{x}\right]
and
\Lambda=\left[\bm{y}\right]
in
\mathbb{P}_{n-1}(\mathbb{R})
. The height and degree functions under consideration are defined over the dense open subset

\mathbb{P}_{n-1}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R})\;=\;\left\{L\in\mathbb{P}_{n-1}(\mathbb{R})\;:\;\delta(L,\left[\bm{e}_{0}\right])\neq 1\right\}.

1.5
Probabilistic Estimates for the Generalised Subspace Inequality

Let
\Psi\penalty\ :\mathbb{R}_{+}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
be an approximation function. The Main Problem is considered in the generality where the Subspace Inequality (
1.12
) is satisfied for any approximation function meeting a convergence condition (of which the original case
\Psi(x)=x^{-\varepsilon}
is a particular example).

Let
1\leq k\leq n
. Say that a
k
-dimensional subspace
\Pi
of
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
(that is, an element of the Grassmannian
G_{k,n}(\mathbb{Q})
) is a
\Psi
-solution to the Subspace Inequality (
1.12
) given algebraic lines
(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})\in G_{1,n}(\mathbb{A})
if :

•

there exists infinitely many rational lines
\Lambda\in G_{1,n}(\mathbb{Q})
in
\Pi

1.12
);

•

there exists
k

\Lambda\in G_{1,n}(\mathbb{Q})
in
\Pi
satisfying the Subspace Inequality (
1.12
).

These stipulations allow for a greater generality upon working with a fixed value of
k
. Indeed, if the first were not satisfied, the finitely many solutions within
\Pi
should be regarded as a finite set of one-dimensional solution subspaces, and if the second one were not satisfied, then a particular proper subspace of
\Pi
should be regarded as the appropriate solution subspace. Note in particular that the first point rules out the particular case of Roth’s Theorem dealt with separately in §
1.2
above.

Let
1\leq k<n
and
Q_{1},Q_{2}\geq 1
be integers. The density estimates are concerned with the number of algebraic elements of fixed degree and bounded height in the set

J_{\Psi}\left(k;Q_{1},Q_{2}\right).

(1.24)

This is defined as the set of all those lines
\left(L_{1},\dots,L_{n}\right)\in\left(\mathbb{P}_{n-1}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R})\right)^{n}
for which there exists a
\Psi
-solution rational subspace
\Pi\in G_{k,n}(\mathbb{Q})
with (usual) height
H(\Pi)\geq Q_{1}
for which there exist
k

\Lambda\in\Pi\cap G_{1,n}(\mathbb{Q})
satisfying the Subspace Inequality (
1.12
) and obeying

.

The Main Problem is tackled in the next theorem by providing effective bounds for the proportion of algebraic lines with fixed degree and bounded height admitting a
\Psi

1.12

1.23

Theorem 1.6
.

(Effective Probabilistic Estimates for the Generalised Subspace Inequality)
Assume that
\bm{\varphi}
is an admissible parametrisation as in (
1.15
). Fix a system of degrees
\bm{d}\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}^{n-1}
and a dimension
1\leq k<n
and denote by
\omega_{k}
the volume of the
k

\Psi
be an approximation function such that
\Psi(Q)\geq Q^{-n}
satisfying the convergence condition

\sum_{Q=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Psi(Q)}{Q}\cdot\left(\log Q\right)^{n-1}\;<\;\infty.

(1.25)

Then, there exists an effective constant
C_{n}(\bm{d},\bm{\varphi})>0
such that, given integers
Q_{1}\geq 1
and
Q_{2}\geq 2(Q_{1}/(k!\cdot\omega_{k}))^{1/k}

\bm{H}=\left(H_{1},\dots,H_{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\#\left(P_{\bm{\varphi}}\!\left(\bm{d},\bm{H}\right)\cap J_{\Psi}\left(k;Q_{1},Q_{2}\right)\right)}{\#P_{\varphi}\left(\bm{d},\bm{H}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{n}(\bm{d},\bm{\varphi})\cdot\left(\sum_{2(Q_{1}/(k!\cdot\omega_{k}))^{1/k}\leq Q\leq Q_{2}}\frac{\Psi(Q)}{Q}\cdot\left(\log Q\right)^{n-1}+Q_{2}^{n}\cdot\max_{1\leq j<n}\frac{\left(\log H_{j}\right)^{l(d_{j})}}{H_{j}}\right).

(1.26)

1.25

\Psi
-solution to the Generalised Subspace Inequality (
1.12

\limsup_{Q_{1}\rightarrow\infty}\;\;\limsup_{Q_{2}\rightarrow\infty}\;\;\limsup_{\min(\bm{H})\rightarrow\infty}\;\;\left(\frac{\#\left(P_{\varphi}\!\left(\bm{d},\bm{H}\right)\cap J_{\Psi}\left(k;Q_{1},Q_{2}\right)\right)}{\#P_{\bm{\varphi}}\!\left(\bm{d},\bm{H}\right)}\right)\;=\;0.

2
Analytic Properties of Koleda’s Density Function

Let
d\geq 2
be an integer. A key result at the foundation of the effective results stated in the introduction is due to Koleda
[
12
]
. It implies in particular that algebraic numbers of degree
d

Theorem 2.1

(Koleda, 2017)
.

Let
d\geq 2
. Then, there exists a constant
K_{1}(d)>0
and a continuous, strictly positively valued probability density function
\rho_{d}\penalty\ :\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that for any subinterval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
and any integer
H\geq 1

\left|\frac{\#\left(\mathbb{A}_{d}(H)\cap I\right)}{H^{d+1}}-\frac{1}{2\cdot\zeta(d+1)}\int_{I}\rho_{d}\right|\;\leq\;K_{1}(d)\cdotp\frac{\left(\log H\right)^{l(d)}}{H},

where the quantity
l(d)
is defined in (
1.7
). In this relation, the probability density function
\rho_{d}
is explicitly given for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
by

\rho_{d}(x)\;=\;\int_{\Delta_{d}(x)}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d}kp_{k}x^{k-1}\right|\cdot dp_{1}\cdots dp_{d},

(2.1)

where
\zeta
is the Riemann function and where
\Delta_{d}(x)
is the semialgebraic domain of integration

\Delta_{d}(x)\;=\;\left\{\left(p_{1},\dots,p_{d}\right)\in\left[-1,1\right]^{d}\;:\;\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{k}x^{k}\right|\leq 1\right\}.

Moreover, the function
\rho_{d}
is even and satisfies for any
x\neq 0
the identity

x^{2}\cdot\rho_{d}(x)\;=\;\rho_{d}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right).

(2.2)

The function
\rho_{d}
defined in (
2.1
) is from now on referred to as
Koleda’s density function
.

Proposition 2.2

(Properties of Koleda’s probability density function)
.

Keep the above notations. Then,

1.

(
Explicit constant in the error term
.) An admissible value of the constant

in Theorem

K_{1}(d)\;=\;d\cdot\left(12d^{2}\cdot 2^{d(d-2)}\cdot(d+1)^{d/2}+2^{d+2}\cdot p(d)\cdot\left(2p(d)-1\right)^{s(d)}\right),

(2.3)

where

p(d)=4^{\left(4^{d+1}-1\right)/3}\cdot d^{4^{d+1}}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad s(d)=3\cdot\left(2d+3\right)^{2^{d+1}}\cdot d^{\left(16^{d+1}-1\right)\cdot\left\lceil\log d/\log 2\right\rceil}.

2.

(
Bounds on the density function
.) It holds that

\rho_{d}(x)\;\leq\;\frac{d(d+1)}{2}\qquad\textrm{for all }\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}

(2.4)

and that

\frac{1}{3d}\cdot\left(\frac{2}{3d}\right)^{d}\;\leq\;\rho_{d}(x)\qquad\textrm{for all }\qquad x\in\left[-1,1\right].

(2.5)

3.

(

.) Set

K_{2}(d)\;=\;\frac{d(d+1)}{2}\cdot\left(4^{d}+(2\sqrt{d})^{d+3}\cdot\omega_{d-1}\right),

\left|\rho_{d}(x)-\rho_{d}(y)\right|\;\leq\;K_{2}(d)\cdot\left|x-y\right|\quad\textrm{whenever}\quad\left|x-y\right|\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2^{d+1}d}

Proof.

1. This is a very particular case of the much more general statement given in Theorem
4.1
below. In the notations within, the above value of

follows when taking the semialgebraic familiy
Z
as the set of real intervals and then upon setting
n=1
,
s(Z)=2
and
p(Z)=1
.

2. Identity (
2.2
) imply that
\rho_{d}
attains its maximum over the interval
[-1,1]
. Thus,

\max_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{d}\;\leq\;\int_{[-1,1]^{d}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}k\left|p_{k}\right|\right)\cdot dp_{1}\cdots dp_{d}\;\leq\;\frac{d(d+1)}{2},

which proves the upper bound (
2.4
). As for the lower bound (
2.5
), the evenness of the map
\rho_{d}
allows for restricting the variable

to the interval

centered at the vector in

\rho_{d}(x)\;=\;\int_{\Delta_{d}(x)}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d}kp_{k}x^{k-1}\right|\cdot dp_{1}\cdots dp_{d}\;\geq\;\frac{Vol_{d}(B_{\infty}^{(d)})}{3d}\;=\;\frac{2^{d}}{(3d)^{d+1}},

where

3. Let

\displaystyle\left|\rho_{p}(x+\varepsilon)-\rho_{d}(x)\right|\;

\Delta_{d}(x+\varepsilon)\cap\Delta_{d}(x)\supset\left\{\left(p_{1},\dots,p_{d}\right)\in[-1,1]^{d}\;:\;\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{k}x^{k}\right|\;\leq\;1-2^{d}d\varepsilon\right\}

\varepsilon\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2^{d+1}d},

the set of all those
\bm{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
meeting this requirement defines the union of two slabs, each of thickness

\frac{2^{d+1}d\varepsilon}{\sqrt{x^{2}+\dots+x^{2d}}}\;\underset{\eqref{necConempt}}{\leq}\;\frac{2^{d+1}d\varepsilon}{x}\;\underset{\eqref{necConempt}}{\leq}\;\frac{2^{d+1}d^{2}\varepsilon}{1-2^{d}d\varepsilon}\;\underset{\eqref{boundvarepsik}}{\leq}\;\ 2^{d+2}d^{2}\varepsilon.

The volume of the set
\mathcal{C}_{d}(x,\varepsilon)\subset[-1,1]^{d}
is then bounded above by the measure of intersection of these two slabs with the Euclidean ball centered at the origin with radius
\sqrt{d}
, which is easily seen to be at most

2\left(\sqrt{d}\right)^{d-1}\cdot\omega_{d-1}\cdot 2^{d+2}d^{2}\varepsilon.

As a consequence,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{B}_{d}(x,\varepsilon)}\max\left\{\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d}kp_{k}(x+\varepsilon)^{k-1}\right|;\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d}kp_{k}x^{k-1}\right|\right\}\cdot dp_{1}\cdots dp_{d}

\displaystyle\underset{\eqref{inclusionBxeps}}{\leq}\;Vol_{d}\left(\mathcal{C}_{d}(x,\varepsilon)\right)\cdot\max_{\bm{p}\in[-1,1]^{d}}\max\left\{\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d}kp_{k}(x+\varepsilon)^{k-1}\right|;\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d}kp_{k}x^{k-1}\right|\right\}

\displaystyle\underset{\eqref{inegxepsi}\&\eqref{inclusionBxeps}}{\leq}\;d^{(d-1)/2}\cdot B_{d-1}\cdot 2^{d+3}d^{2}\varepsilon\cdot\frac{d(d+1)}{2}

\displaystyle=\;(2\sqrt{d})^{d+3}\cdot B_{d-1}\cdot\frac{d(d+1)}{2}\cdot\varepsilon.

Combining this upper bound with (
2.8
) completes the proof of the statement.
∎

3
General Counting Measures in Roth’s Theorem

3.1
Statement of the General Result

Let
\left(\mathbb{X}(H)\right)_{H\geq 1}
be a sequence of finite subsets of
\mathbb{R}
(it should be emphasized that each
\mathbb{X}(H)
need not coincide with the more specific subset
\mathbb{X}_{d}(H)\in\left\{\mathbb{A}_{d}(H),\mathbb{A}^{\prime}_{d}(H)\right\}
defined in the Introduction). Let then
\left(\mu_{H}\right)_{H\geq 1}
be the sequence of probability measures on the unit interval
\left[0,1\right]
defined as

\mu_{H}\;=\;\frac{1}{\#\mathbb{X}(H)}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{X}(H)\!\!\!\!\!\pmod{1}}\delta_{\alpha},

(3.1)

where
\delta_{\alpha}
denotes the Dirac mass at
\alpha
. Assume that
\left(\mu_{H}\right)_{H\geq 1}
converges weakly to a probability measure
\mu
admitting a strictly positive and continuous density function
\rho\penalty\ :[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Assume also that there exists a uniform error term sequence
E\penalty\ :\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
in the sense that for all
H\geq 1
,

\sup\;\left|\mu_{H}(I)-\int_{I}\rho\right|\;\leq\;E(H),

(3.2)

where the supremum is taken over all subintervals
I\subset[0,1]
.

Theorem
1.3
is derived in §
3.2
below from the following more general statement. It gives quantitative probabilistic estimates in terms of the uniform pointwise modulus of continuity function
\eta_{\rho}
defined in (
2.14
) and of the corresponding level set function
\nu_{\rho}
defined in (
2.15
).

Theorem 3.1
.

(Effective probabilistic estimates for general counting measures in Roth’s Theorem)
Let
\Psi\penalty\ :\mathbb{R}_{>0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be an approximation function and let
\left(\mu_{H}\right)_{H\geq 1}
be the sequence of probability measures defined in (
3.1
) assumed to satisfy property (
3.2
). Fix a degree
d\geq 2
, integers
Q_{2}>Q_{1}\geq 1
and recall the definition of the set

in (

).

•

Upper bound for the probability :
assume that

\Theta_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\;:=\;\min_{Q_{1}\leq q<Q_{2}}\frac{2\Psi(q)}{q}\;\;<\;\;\nu_{\rho}(\delta)\cdotp

Then,

\displaystyle\frac{\#\left(J_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\cap\mathbb{X}(H)\right)}{\#\mathbb{X}(H)}\;

\displaystyle\leq\;4\left(\int_{J_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})}\rho\right)\;+\;S_{\Psi,\rho}^{(\delta)}(H,Q_{1},Q_{2})

\displaystyle\quad+\;4\left|J_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\right|\cdot\eta_{\rho}\left(\Theta_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\right),

where

\displaystyle S_{\Psi,\rho}^{(\delta)}(H,Q_{1},Q_{2})\;=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{8\delta}{1-\delta}+\frac{E(H)}{\Theta_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\cdot\min\rho}\right)\cdot\left(\left|J_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\right|\cdot\eta_{\rho}\left(\Theta_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\right)+\int_{J_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})}\rho\right).

•

Lower bound for the probability :
assume that
\delta\in(0,1)
is such that

0<len^{*}\left(\widetilde{J}_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\right)<\nu_{\rho}(\delta),

where
\widetilde{J}_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})
denotes the closure of the complement in
[0,1]
of the set
J_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})
, and where
len^{*}\left(\widetilde{J}_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\right)
is the minimal length of its connected components. Then,

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\#\left(J_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\cap\mathbb{X}(H)\right)}{\#\mathbb{X}(H)}\;

\displaystyle\leq\;4\left(\int_{\widetilde{J}_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})}\rho\right)\;+\;T_{\Psi,\rho}^{(\delta)}(H,Q_{1},Q_{2})

\displaystyle\quad+\;4\left|\widetilde{J}_{\Psi/2}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\right|\cdot\eta_{\rho}\left(len^{*}\left(\widetilde{J}_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\right)\right)

with

\displaystyle T_{\Psi,\rho}^{(\delta)}(H,Q_{1},Q_{2})\;=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{8\delta}{1-\delta}+\frac{E(H)}{len^{*}\left(\widetilde{J}_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\right)\cdot\min\rho}\right)

\displaystyle\;\times\left(\left|\widetilde{J}_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\right|\cdot\eta_{\rho}\left(len^{*}\left(\widetilde{J}_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\right)\right)+\int_{\widetilde{J}_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})}\rho\right).

Furthermore, if
len^{*}\left(\widetilde{J}_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\right)=0
, then the set
J_{\Psi}(Q_{1},Q_{2})
coincides with the full interval
[0,1]
.

The main ingredient in the proof of Theorem
3.1
is the following Proposition where, there and throughout this section, all notations and assumptions introduced in the above statement are kept. It is also convenient to set
B(x,r)=[x-r,x+r]
when

and

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
H\geq 1
be an integer and let
\delta\in(0,1)
,
\gamma\in[0,1)
and
\varepsilon\in\left(0,\nu_{\rho}(\delta)/2\right)
be reals such that
(1+\gamma)\varepsilon<1/2
. Assume that
\Omega
is a measurable subset of
[0,1]
and that
g\penalty\ :[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
is a continuous map such that

\left|g(\alpha)-\alpha\right|\leq\gamma\varepsilon\qquad\textrm{for all}\qquad\alpha\in[0,1].

(3.3)

Then,

\displaystyle\left(1-\gamma\right)\cdot\left(\int_{\Omega}\rho\right)-\left(\frac{2\delta}{1-\delta}+\frac{E(H)}{\varepsilon\cdot\min\rho}\right)\cdot\left(2\left|\Omega\right|\cdot\eta_{\rho}\left((1+\gamma)\cdot\varepsilon\right)+\int_{\Omega}\rho\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\gamma\cdot\frac{2\delta}{1-\delta}\cdot\left(\int_{\Omega}\rho-\left|\Omega\right|\cdot\eta_{\rho}\left((1+\gamma)\cdot\varepsilon\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\;\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\left|B^{*}(g(\alpha),\varepsilon)\cap\Omega\right|}{\left|B^{*}(g(\alpha),\varepsilon)\right|}\cdot d\mu_{H}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\leq\;2\left(1+\gamma\right)\cdot\left(\int_{\Omega}\rho\right)+2\left(\frac{2\delta(1+\gamma)}{1-\delta}+\frac{E(H)}{\varepsilon\cdot\min\rho}\right)\cdot\left(\left|\Omega\right|\cdot\eta_{\rho}\left((1+\gamma)\cdot\varepsilon\right)+\int_{\Omega}\rho\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+2(1+\gamma)\cdot\left|\Omega\right|\cdot\eta_{\rho}\left((1+\gamma)\cdot\varepsilon\right).

Here, given
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
r>0
, the set

is defined in (
1.4
).

The proof of Proposition
3.2
relies on an auxiliary statement :

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
H\geq 1
be an integer and let
\delta\in(0,1)
be a real. Then, for any subinterval
I\subset[0,1]
such that

0<\left|I\right|<\nu_{\rho}(\delta),

(3.4)

it holds that

\left|\frac{1}{\left|I\right|}\cdot\int_{I}\frac{d\mu_{H}(x)}{\rho(x)}-1\right|\;\leq\;\frac{2\delta}{1-\delta}+\frac{E(H)}{\left|I\right|\cdot\min\rho}\cdotp

Proof.

Since

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{H}(I)}{\max_{I}\rho}\;\leq\;\int_{I}\frac{d\mu_{H}(x)}{\rho(x)}\;\leq\;\frac{\mu_{H}(I)}{\min_{I}\rho},

it follows from the definition of the uniform error term sequence
E
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\max_{I}\rho}\cdot\left(\int_{I}\rho-E(H)\right)\;\leq\;\int_{I}\frac{d\mu_{H}(x)}{\rho(x)}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\min_{I}\rho}\cdot\left(\int_{I}\rho+E(H)\right).

(3.5)

Let then
x^{+}\in I
and
x^{-}\in I
be points realizing the maximum and minimum, respectively, of
\rho
over the interval
I
. Under assumption (
3.4
), one has

\eta_{\rho}(|I|)\leq\delta\cdot\underset{[0,1]}{\min}\;\rho,

(3.6)

yielding

\displaystyle\frac{1-\delta}{1+\delta}\;\underset{\eqref{condidibs}}{\leq}\;\frac{\rho(x^{+})-\eta_{\rho}(|I|)}{\rho(x^{+})+\eta_{\rho}(|I|)}\;

\displaystyle\leq\;\frac{1}{\rho(x^{+})}\cdot\frac{1}{|I|}\cdot\int_{I}\rho

\displaystyle\leq\;\frac{1}{\rho(x^{-})}\cdot\frac{1}{|I|}\cdot\int_{I}\rho\;\leq\;\frac{\rho(x^{-})+\eta_{\rho}(|I|)}{\rho(x^{-})-\eta_{\rho}(|I|)}\;\underset{\eqref{condidibs}}{\leq}\;\frac{1+\delta}{1-\delta}\cdotp

Inequalities (
3.5
) then imply that

\displaystyle\frac{1-\delta}{1+\delta}-\frac{E(H)}{|I|\cdot\min\rho}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{|I|}\cdot\int_{I}\frac{d\mu_{H}(x)}{\rho(x)}\;\leq\;\frac{1+\delta}{1-\delta}+\frac{E(H)}{|I|\cdot\min\rho},

which concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.2
from Lemma

.

Given

such that assumption (

(3.7)

In other words, denoting by
\chi_{A}

\chi_{B(x,(1-\gamma)\varepsilon)}(\alpha)\;\leq\;\chi_{B(g(\alpha),\varepsilon)}(x)\;\leq\;\chi_{B(x,(1+\gamma)\varepsilon)}(\alpha).

Since
\rho
takes strictly positive values, one has

\displaystyle\int\frac{\left|B(g(\alpha),\varepsilon)\cap\Omega\right|}{\left|B^{*}(g(\alpha),\varepsilon)\right|}\cdot d\mu_{H}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\;\int\frac{1}{\rho(\alpha)\cdot\left|B^{*}(g(\alpha),\varepsilon)\right|}\cdot\left(\int_{\Omega\cap B(g(\alpha),\varepsilon)}\left(\rho(\alpha)-\rho(x)+\rho(x)\right)\cdot dx\right)\cdot d\mu_{H}(\alpha).

This implies that

\displaystyle\left|\int\frac{\left|B(g(\alpha),\varepsilon)\cap\Omega\right|}{\left|B^{*}(g(\alpha),\varepsilon)\right|}\cdot d\mu_{H}(\alpha)-\int\frac{1}{\rho(\alpha)}\cdot\frac{1}{\left|B^{*}(g(\alpha),\varepsilon)\right|}\cdot\left(\int_{\Omega\cap B(g(\alpha),\varepsilon)}\rho\right)\cdot d\mu_{H}(\alpha)\right|

\displaystyle\;\underset{\eqref{incluballs}}{\leq}\;\eta_{\rho}\left((1+\gamma)\varepsilon\right)\cdot\int\frac{\left|B(g(\alpha),\varepsilon)\cap\Omega\right|}{\left|B^{*}(g(\alpha),\varepsilon)\right|}\cdot\frac{1}{\rho(\alpha)}\cdot\mu_{H}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\leq\;\eta_{\rho}\left((1+\gamma)\varepsilon\right)\cdot\int_{\Omega}\left(\int\frac{\chi_{B(g(\alpha),\varepsilon)}(x)}{\varepsilon\cdot\rho(\alpha)}\cdot d\mu_{H}(\alpha)\right)\cdot dx

\displaystyle\underset{\eqref{incluballs}}{\leq}\;\;\eta_{\rho}\left((1+\gamma)\varepsilon\right)\cdot\int_{\Omega}\left(\int\frac{\chi_{B(x,(1+\gamma)\varepsilon))}(\alpha)}{\varepsilon\cdot\rho(\alpha)}\cdot d\mu_{H}(\alpha)\right)\cdot dx.

\displaystyle\left|\int\frac{\left|B(g(\alpha),\varepsilon)\cap\Omega\right|}{\left|B^{*}(g(\alpha),\varepsilon)\right|}\cdot d\mu_{H}(\alpha)-\int\frac{1}{\rho(\alpha)}\cdot\frac{1}{\left|B^{*}(g(\alpha),\varepsilon)\right|}\cdot\left(\int_{\Omega\cap B(g(\alpha),\varepsilon)}\rho\right)\cdot d\mu_{H}(\alpha)\right|

The proof in the case of the measure

Lemma 3.6

(Uniform error term estimate for the discrete probability measure
\lambda_{H}
)
.

Consider the discrete measure on

Fix an interval

\displaystyle\int_{I}\left(\sum_{|n|\leq N}\rho_{d}(x+n)\right)\cdot\frac{\textrm{d}x}{2\cdot\zeta(d+1)}\;-\;4(2N+1)\cdot z_{d}(H)

\displaystyle\gamma_{H}\left(\mathbb{R}\backslash[-N,N]\right)\;

Given an irreducible fraction

\displaystyle l^{+}(Q_{1},Q_{2},i)\;=\;

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\max_{i<j\leq N(Q_{2})}\left\{\frac{p_{j}}{q_{j}}-\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}+\tau\left(\frac{p_{j}}{q_{j}}\right)\;:\;\frac{p_{j}}{q_{j}}-\tau\left(\frac{p_{j}}{q_{j}}\right)>\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}+\tau\left(\frac{p_{j}}{q_{j}}\right)\quad\textrm{and}\quad q_{j}\geq Q_{1}\right\}

\displaystyle l^{-}(Q_{1},Q_{2},i)\;=\;

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\max_{0\leq j<i}\left\{\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}-\frac{p_{j}}{q_{j}}+\tau\left(\frac{p_{j}}{q_{j}}\right)\;:\;\frac{p_{j}}{q_{j}}+\tau\left(\frac{p_{j}}{q_{j}}\right)<\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}-\tau\left(\frac{p_{j}}{q_{j}}\right)\quad\textrm{and}\quad q_{j}\geq Q_{1}\right\}

Distribution of the Solutions to the Subspace Inequality

4.1
Algebraic Points in Semialgebraic Families

A
semialgebraic family
is a map
Z
from a semi-algebraic set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
to the set of all subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(with
m,n\geq 0
integers) such that the graph set
Z^{*}=\left\{(\bm{t},\bm{x})\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\;:\;\bm{x}\in Z(\bm{t})\right\}
is semialgebraic. Given
\bm{t}\in\Omega
, call
Z(\bm{t})
the
fibre
over
\bm{t}
. Throughout, the integers

s(Z)\qquad\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\qquad p(Z)

(4.1)

denote respectively the number of polynomial equalities and inequalities used in a given representation of the graph set
Z^{*}
, and the maximal degrees of the polynomials involved in such a representation.

Theorem
1.6
is derived from the theorem below established in the following section. To state it, given an
n
-tuple of positive integers
\bm{d}=\left(d_{1},\dots,d_{n}\right)\in\left(\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}\right)^{n}
, set for the sake of simplicity of notations
d\bm{x}=dx_{1}\dots dx_{n}
,

\zeta(\bm{d}+\bm{1})\;=\;\prod_{i=1}^{n}\zeta(d_{i}+1)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\bm{\rho}_{\bm{d}}(\bm{x})\;=\;\prod_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{d_{i}}(x_{i})\quad\textrm{for all}\quad\bm{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
\rho_{d_{i}}
is Koleda’s density function (
2.1
). When
\bm{H}=(H_{1},\dots,H_{n})
is an
n
–tuple of integers, it will also be convenient to set
\bm{H}^{\bm{d}}=\prod_{1\leq i\leq n}H_{i}^{d_{i}}
. Finally, recall the definition of the set
\mathbb{A}_{\bm{d}}(\bm{H})
in (
1.22
).

Theorem 4.1

(Counting algebraic points in semialgebraic families)
.

Let
m,n\geq 1
be integers and let
\bm{d}=\left(d_{1},\dots,d_{n}\right)\in\left(\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}\right)^{n}
be an
n
-tuple of positive integers. Assume that
Z\penalty\ :\bm{t}\in\Omega\mapsto Z(\bm{t})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a bounded semialgebraic family defined over
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Then, for any
\left(\bm{t},\bm{H}\right)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
, it holds that

\left|\frac{\#\left(\left(\mathbb{A}_{\bm{d}}(\bm{H})\right)^{n}\cap Z(\bm{t})\right)}{\bm{H}^{n\cdot(\bm{d}+\bm{1})}}-\int_{Z(\bm{t})}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\bm{\rho}_{\bm{d}}\left(\bm{x}^{(k)}\right)\cdot d\bm{x}^{(k)}}{2^{n}\zeta(\bm{d}+\bm{1})}\right|\;\leq\;M_{1}(Z,\bm{d},n)\cdot\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\frac{(\log H)^{l(d_{i})}}{H_{i}},

where the symbol
l(d_{i})
is defined in (
1.7
) and where

\displaystyle M_{1}(Z,\bm{d},n)\;=\;

\displaystyle\left(d_{1}\dots d_{n}\right)^{n}\cdot\left(2^{n^{2}}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}E_{1}(d_{i})\;\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\quad\left.+\;2^{n(2D+1)+1}\cdot p(Z,\bm{d})\cdot\left(2\cdot p(Z,\bm{d})-1\right)^{n+s(Z,\bm{d})-1}\right).

(4.2)

In this relation,

•

given an integer
d\geq 2
, one sets

E_{1}(d)\;=\;6\cdot d^{2}2^{d(d-2)}(d+1)^{d/2};

(4.3)

•

the integers
s(Z,\bm{d})
and
p(Z,\bm{d})
are defined as

s(Z,\bm{d})=3S^{2^{D+n}}\cdot P^{\left(16^{D+n}-1\right)\cdot\left\lceil\log P/\log 2\right\rceil}\;\;\;\textrm{and}\;\;\;p(Z,\bm{d})=4^{\left(4^{D+n}-1\right)/3}\cdot P^{4^{D+n}},

(4.4)

where

D=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(d_{i}+1\right)

(4.5)

and where

S\;=\;s(Z)+n\cdot(D+(d_{1}\dots d_{n})^{n}+1)\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad P\;=\;\max\left\{p(Z),\max_{1\leq j\leq n}d_{j}\right\}.

Theorem
1.6
stated in the introduction is first deduced from the above theorem. This deduction relies on a lattice point counting estimate valid for semialgebraic families which is essentially due to Davenport
[
9
,
10
]
. Versions of it extended to the larger class of
o
–minimal structures have been obtained by Baroero & Widmer
[
2

. The following lemma, however, presents the advantage of making all parameters completely effective.

Lemma 4.2

(Davenport’s Lemma for Semialgebraic Families)
.

Let
m,n\geq 0
be integers and let
Y^{*}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
be the graph of a semialgebraic family with bounded fibers
Y(\bm{t})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
for all
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Then, there exists a constant
C(Y)>0
depending only on the family
Y
such that for any
\bm{t}
,

\left|\#\left(Y(\bm{t})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}\right)-Vol_{n}(Y(\bm{t}))\right|\;\leq\;\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}C(Y)^{n-j}\cdot V_{j}(Y(\bm{t})),

(4.6)

where
V_{0}(\,\cdot\,)=1
and where for
1\leq j\leq n-1
, the quantity
V_{j}(\,\cdot\,)
denotes the sum of the
j
–dimensional Lebesgue measures of all orthogonal projections of its argument on every
j
-dimensional coordinate subspace. Furthermore, an admissible value for the constant
C(Y)
is

C(Y)\;=\;p(Y)\cdot(2p(Y)-1)^{n+s(Y)-1},

(4.7)

where
p(Y)
and
s(Y)
are defined as in (
4.1
).

Proof.

Let
\left(\bm{e}_{1},\dots,\bm{e}_{n}\right)
denote the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Davenport
[
9
,
10
]
shows that the estimate (
4.6
) holds when
C(Y)
is replaced with a constant depending on the parameter
\bm{t}
, say
C(Y,\bm{t})
. This constant is defined as the maximum over all nonempty subsets
I\subset\left\llbracket 1,n\right\rrbracket
, all indices
i_{0}\in I
and all lines
l(I,i_{0})
parallel to
\bm{e}_{i_{0}}
lying in the span of the family
\mathcal{F}(I)=\left\{\bm{e}_{i}:i\in I\right\}
, of the number of connected components of
\pi_{I}(Z(\bm{t}))\cap l(I,i_{0})
. Here,
\pi_{I}
denotes the orthogonal projection from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\mathcal{F}(I)
.

It is furthermore known, see
[
8
, Proposition 4.13]
, that
C(Y,\bm{t})
defined this way is bounded above by
p^{\prime}(2p^{\prime}-1)^{k^{\prime}+s^{\prime}-1}
when the fibre
Y(\bm{t})
is defined by a system of
s^{\prime}
polynomial equations and inequalities in
k^{\prime}
variables of degrees at most
d^{\prime}
. Since such a system can be obtained from a polynomial system defining the graph set
Y^{*}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
by freezing the vector variable
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, it is clear that, in the notations of the statement, one can assume that
s^{\prime}\leq s
,
k^{\prime}\leq n
and
p^{\prime}\leq p
. This completes the proof.
∎

Fix an admissible parametrisation
\bm{\varphi}
as in (

4.1
is applied to the family of sets parametrised by the map

(4.8)

which to

\left(\bm{t},\bm{H}\right)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\#\left(W_{\bm{d},\nu}(\bm{t},\bm{H})\cap\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Z}_{prim}^{n(d_{i}+1)}\right)}{\bm{H}^{n\cdot(\bm{d}+\bm{1})}}-\frac{Vol_{n\times D}\left(W_{\bm{d},\nu}(\bm{t},\bm{1})\right)}{\zeta\left(n\cdot(\bm{d}+\bm{1})\right)}\right|\;\leq\;\frac{2^{n(2D+1)+1}\cdot C(\bm{d},Z)}{\min_{1\leq i\leq n}H_{i}},

where
D
is the integer defined in (
4.5
) and where

C(\bm{d},Z)\;=\;p(Z,\bm{d})\cdot(2\cdot p(Z,\bm{d})-1)^{n+s(Z,\bm{d})-1}

(4.22)

with the integers
p(Z,\bm{d})
and
s(Z,\bm{d})
defined in (
4.4
).

Proof.

From Lemma
4.2
and with the notations therein,

\left|\#\!\left(W_{\bm{d},\nu}(\bm{t},\bm{H})\cap\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Z}^{n(d_{i}+1)}\right)-Vol_{n\times D}\!\left(W_{\bm{d},\nu}(\bm{t},\bm{H})\right)\right|\leq C(\bm{d},\nu,Z)\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{nD-1}\!V_{j}\!\left(W_{\bm{d},\nu}(\bm{t},\bm{H})\right).

Here, by homogeneity of the set
W_{\bm{d},\nu}(\bm{t},\bm{H})
in each component
H_{i}
,

Vol_{n\times D}\left(W_{\bm{d},\nu}(\bm{t},\bm{H})\right)\;=\;\bm{H}^{n\cdot(\bm{d}+\bm{1})}\cdot Vol_{n\times D}\left(W_{\bm{d},\nu}(\bm{t},\bm{1})\right).

Moreover, since
W_{\bm{d},\nu}(\bm{t},\bm{1})\subset\left[-1,1\right]^{n\times D},

it also holds that

\displaystyle V_{j}\left(W_{\bm{d},\nu}(\bm{t},\bm{H})\right)\;

\displaystyle\leq\;V_{j}\left(\bm{H}^{n\cdot(\bm{d}+\bm{1})}\cdot W_{\bm{d},\nu}(\bm{t},\bm{1})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\;V_{j}\left(\bm{H}^{n\cdot(\bm{d}+\bm{1})}\cdot \left[-1,1\right]^{n\times D}\right)\;\leq\;{nD\choose j}\cdot\frac{2^{j}\cdot\bm{H}^{\bm{d}+\bm{1}}}{\min_{1\leq i\leq n}H_{i}^{nD-j}}\cdotp

As a consequence,

\left|\frac{\#\!\left(W_{\bm{d},\nu}(\bm{t},\bm{H})\cap\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Z}^{n(d_{i}+1)}\right)}{\bm{H}^{n\cdot(\bm{d}+\bm{1})}}-Vol_{n\times D}\!\left(W_{\bm{d},\nu}(\bm{t},\bm{1})\right)\right|\leq\frac{2^{2nD}\cdot C(\bm{d},\nu,Z)}{\min_{1\leq i\leq n}H_{i}}\cdotp

(4.23)

Returning to the count of primitive points and denoting by
\mu
the Möbius function, it follows from the inclusion-exclusion principle that

\displaystyle\frac{\#\left(W_{\bm{d},\nu}(\bm{t},\bm{H})\cap\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Z}_{prim}^{n(d_{i}+1)}\right)}{\bm{H}^{n\cdot(\bm{d}+\bm{1})}}

\displaystyle=\;\sum_{\bm{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}^{n}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mu(m_{i})\right)\cdot\frac{\#\!\left(W_{\bm{d},\nu}(\bm{t},\bm{H}/\bm{m})\cap\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Z}^{n(d_{i}+1)}\right)}{\bm{H}^{n\cdot(\bm{d}+\bm{1})}},

where
\bm{H}/\bm{m}
denotes the vector with components
H_{i}/m_{i}
. From (
4.23
), one then infers the existence of reals
\theta,\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n}\in[-1,1]
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\#\left(W_{\bm{d},\nu}(\bm{t},\bm{H})\cap\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Z}_{prim}^{n(d_{i}+1)}\right)}{\bm{H}^{n\cdot(\bm{d}+\bm{1})}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\;Vol_{n\times D}\!\left(W_{\bm{d},\nu}(\bm{t},\bm{1})\right)\cdot\sum_{1\leq m_{1}\leq H_{1}}\cdots\sum_{1\leq m_{n}\leq H_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mu(m_{i})}{m_{i}^{n\cdot(d_{i}+1)}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\theta\cdot 2^{2nD}\cdot C(\bm{d},\nu,Z)\cdot\sum_{1\leq m_{1}\leq H_{1}}\cdots\sum_{1\leq m_{n}\leq H_{n}}\frac{1}{\min_{1\leq i\leq n}\frac{H_{i}}{m_{i}}}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{m_{i}^{n\cdot(d_{i}+1)}}\cdotp

From the inclusion
W_{\bm{d},\nu}(\bm{t},\bm{1})\subset\left[-1,1\right]^{n\times D}
and from standard identities on the Riemann zeta function, the main term can be expressed with the help of a parameter
\eta\in[-1,1]
as

Vol_{n\times D}\!\left(W_{\bm{d},\nu}(\bm{t},\bm{1})\right)\cdot\sum_{\underset{1\leq i\leq n}{1\leq m_{i}\leq H_{i}}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mu(m_{i})}{m_{i}^{n\cdot(d_{i}+1)}}\;=\;\frac{Vol_{n\times D}\!\left(W_{\bm{d},\nu}(\bm{t},\bm{1})\right)}{\zeta{\left(n\cdot\left(\bm{d}+\bm{1}\right)\right)}}+\eta\cdot\frac{2^{2nD}}{\bm{H}^{n\cdot(\bm{d}+\bm{1})}}\cdotp

As for the error term, it can be estimated from the inequalities

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq m_{1}\leq H_{1}}\cdots\sum_{1\leq m_{n}\leq H_{n}}\frac{1}{\min_{1\leq i\leq n}\frac{H_{i}}{m_{i}}}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{m_{i}^{n\cdot(d_{i}+1)}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\;\frac{1}{\min_{1\leq i\leq n}H_{i}}\cdot\sum_{1\leq m_{1}\leq H_{1}}\cdots\sum_{1\leq m_{n}\leq H_{n}}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}m_{i}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{m_{i}^{n\cdot(d_{i}+1)}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\;\frac{\zeta\!\left(n\cdot\left(\bm{d}+\bm{1}\right)-\bm{1}\right)}{\min_{1\leq i\leq n}H_{i}}\;\leq\;\frac{2^{n}}{\min_{1\leq i\leq n}H_{i}}\cdotp

The statement follows upon noticing that, from Lemma
4.5
, the integer defined in (
4.22
) consitutes a uniform upper bound for the quantities
C(\bm{d},\nu,Z)
when
1\leq\nu\leq(d_{1}\dots d_{n})^{n}
.
∎

Completion of the proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Combined with the conclusions of Lemma
4.4
and Corollary
4.6
, the definition of the map
W_{\bm{d},\nu}
in (
4.21
) and identity (
4.20
) yield that for all
\left(\bm{t},\bm{H}\right)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\#\left(\left(\mathbb{A}_{\bm{d}}(\bm{H})\right)^{n}\cap Z(\bm{t})\right)}{\bm{H}^{n\cdot(\bm{d}+\bm{1})}}-f_{\bm{d}}(\bm{t})\right|\;\leq\;M_{1}(Z,\bm{d},n)\cdot\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\frac{(\log H)^{l(d_{i})}}{H_{i}}.

Here, from Lemma
4.4
and Corollary
4.6
,

M_{1}(Z,\bm{d},n)\;=\;\left(d_{1}\dots d_{n}\right)^{n}\cdot\left(2^{n^{2}}\cdot\max_{1\leq i\leq n}E_{1}(d_{i})+2^{n(2D+1)+1}\cdot C(\bm{d},Z)\right)

and

f_{\bm{d}}(\bm{t})\;=\;\sum_{1\leq\nu\leq(d_{1}\dots d_{n})^{n}}\nu\cdot\frac{Vol_{n\times D}\left(W_{\bm{d},\nu}(\bm{t},\bm{1})\right)}{\zeta\left(n\cdot(\bm{d}+\bm{1})\right)}\cdotp

The above value of
M_{1}(Z,\bm{d},n)
coincides with the one given in Theorem
4.1
. Furthermore; the function
f_{\bm{d}}
can be explicitly determined. Indeed, Koleda’s Theorem
2.1
implies that for any nonempty set
U_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
defined as a product of one–dimensional intervals,

\frac{\#\left(\left(\mathbb{A}_{\bm{d}}(\bm{H})\right)^{n}\cap U_{n})\right)}{\bm{H}^{n\cdot(\bm{d}+\bm{1})}}\;=\;\int_{U_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\bm{\rho}_{\bm{d}}\left(\bm{x}^{(k)}\right)\cdot d\bm{x}^{(k)}}{2^{n}\zeta(\bm{d}+\bm{1})}+O_{\bm{d},n}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\frac{(\log H)^{l(d_{i})}}{H_{i}}\right),

where the notation for the error term means that the implicit constant depends only on the degree vector
\bm{d}
and on the integer
n
. Since each fiber of
Z
is a bounded semialgebraic set, it is Jordan measurable. It can thus be approximated arbitrarily well from above and below by finite disjoint unions of product of intervals. Standard arguments from measure theory then yield that any
\bm{t}\in\Omega
,

f_{\bm{d}}(\bm{t})\;=\;\lim_{\min\bm{H}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\#\left(\left(\mathbb{A}_{\bm{d}}(\bm{H})\right)^{n}\cap Z(\bm{t}))\right)}{\bm{H}^{n\cdot(\bm{d}+\bm{1})}}\;=\;\int_{Z(\bm{t})}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\bm{\rho}_{\bm{d}}\left(\bm{x}^{(k)}\right)\cdot d\bm{x}^{(k)}}{2^{n}\zeta(\bm{d}+\bm{1})},

which concludes the proof.
∎
