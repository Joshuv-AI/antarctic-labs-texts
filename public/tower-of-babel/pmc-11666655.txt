Removal of benzo[a]pyrene by a highly degradable microbial community immobilized by modified wheat straw biochar
Authors: Xiaoxiao Chen, Rong Meng, Meihui Geng, Jiahui Zhou, Yuewu Pu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBenzo[a]pyrene (BaP), a high-molecular-weight polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (HMW-PAHs), is a prevalent organic pollutant. Due to various environmental factors, the viability and degradation capacity of PAHs-degrading bacteria in contaminated soil are significantly reduced. Therefore, it is imperative to maintain microbial activity and enhance degradation efficiency. This study aims to optimize BaP removal by utilizing biochar-immobilized BaP for the enhancement of microbial communities’ degradative potential. The immobilization of modified wheat straw biochar (MWBC) significantly enhanced the removal efficiency of BaP by a highly efficient microbial community enriched from oil-contaminated soil, achieving a large removal efficiency of 75.18% for BaP (5–20 mg/L) in a mineral salts medium in 12 d. The study also involved a detection of the species richness and intermediate metabolites of microbial community, as well as an assessment of the challenges and difficulties associated with managing real contaminated sites.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11356-024-35717-1.Keywords: Degradation of benzo[a]pyrene, Biochar immobilized microorganisms, Microbial community

Introduction

Benzo[a]pyrene (BaP) is a kind of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAH) organic pollutant characterized by its five-benzene-ring structure, classifying it as a high molecular weight PAH (HMW-PAH). Featuring stable chemical properties (Nzila and Musa 2021) and hydrophobic nature (Guo and Wen 2021), BaP readily adheres to solid particles. It is commonly found in the atmosphere, water bodies, vegetation, and soil. Notably toxic and profoundly carcinogenic (Bukowska et al. 2022), BaP poses significant health risks to humans (Kim et al. 2013). The Council of Ministers of the Environment of Canada (CCME) ever proposed BaP as one of the indicators for the classification of soil pollution. Meanwhile, BaP is also the earliest and most important carcinogen found in PAHs (Wang et al. 2023). To this end, BaP was hereby selected as the representative pollutant of HMW-PAHs for corresponding remediation technology research. This endeavor holds immense importance in the realm of treating soil contaminated with HMW-PAHs.

Microbial remediation is one of the most important remediation methods for BaP-contaminated soil at present (Bezza and Chirwa 2017; Ostrem Loss and Yu 2018). While employing PAH-degrading bacteria directly might prove more efficient in laboratory settings, their viability and degradation efficacy in contaminated soil are often hampered by environmental factors and competition from indigenous microbial populations, making it imperative to address the challenge of maintaining microbial activity and enhancing degradation efficiency (Hou et al. 2019; Punetha et al. 2022).

Based on the above, immobilized microtechnology becomes an important method. This technology can maintain the high biomass content of microorganisms, enhance the tolerance to harmful environmental conditions of strains, and prevent their loss. At the same time, BaP migrates from pollutants to the carrier surface through hydrophobic interaction, adsorption between donors and receptors or specific interactions, which promotes the degradation of BaP by carrier immobilized microorganisms and improves the removal efficiency of BaP (Bianco et al. 2021). Among the numerous carrier materials, biochar made from agricultural and forestry wastes is a preferred choice. Biochar immobilized microorganisms are reported to have the shortest half-life and the highest biodegradation efficiency in the process of biodegradation of pollutants, and release enough nutrients for microbial metabolism (Zhang et al. 2019a, b).

The study was conducted to screen the efficient BaP-degrading microbial community and improve the BaP removal efficiency by immobilizing BaP-degrading microbial community on biochar. At the same time, the differences of microbial community during the removal of different concentrations of BaP under the condition of biochar immobilization or not were analyzed by amplification sequencing. In this study, the biochar carrier was prepared by combining high-efficiency BaP degrading microorganisms with low-cost agricultural and forestry waste, which laid a foundation for its application in the soil BaP remediation.

Materials and methods

Soil sampling and enrichment

According to reports, samples obtained from land heavily contaminated by petroleum hydrocarbons may yield microorganisms resistant to petroleum hydrocarbon toxicity or capable of utilizing and degrading these compounds (Bidja et al. 2020). In comparison with recently heavily contaminated land, microorganisms that utilize petroleum hydrocarbons may have sufficient time to reproduce and accumulate in abandoned oil fields or land contaminated by spills of oil and diesel (Maddela et al. 2015; Shlimon et al. 2021). Thus, soil samples (0–10 cm from the surface) were collected from the oil field in Karamay (Xinjiang, China) (land that has been contaminated for a long time and has not been exploited recently), the oil field in Dongying (Shandong, China), abandoned soil and oil shale in Maoming open-pit Mine Park (Guangdong, China). With BaP as carbon source, the leaching solution of soil samples was added to Mineral Salts Medium (MSM) to enrich BaP-degrading microbial community.

The enrichment process is as follows: 1 g soil samples were added to 100 mL aseptic water and cultured in shaker (DSX-18L-I, Shen'an Medical Device Factory, Shanghai, China) (200 rpm) at 30℃ for 2 d. BaP (Macklin, Shanghai, China) was dissolved in acetone (Macklin) to prepare BaP-acetone concentrate (2 g/L). The MSM medium was prepared by adding the BaP concentrate to the sterilized MSM-BaP medium (Supplementary Table S1) and waiting for acetone to volatilize. At the same time, the MSM-BaP medium with 1 g/L glucose (Damao, Tianjin, China) was added under the same conditions. The experiments of two kinds of media were carried out, respectively: the leaching solution of 2 mL soil sample was transferred into 20 mL medium with BaP concentration of 5 mg/L. After being cultured in 200 rpm at 30℃ for 7 d, the 2 mL culture material was transferred into 20 mL medium with BaP concentration of 10 mg/L for further culture. By taking 7 d as a round, the concentration of BaP carbon source was gradually increased to 15, 20 and 25 mg/L. The culture medium of each sample was taken and expanded. After the expansion, the degradation experiment of 10 mg/L concentration of BaP was carried out within 7 d, and the microbial community with the highest removal efficiency was selected on the 7th day.

Preparation and characterization of biochar

The biochar was prepared by limiting oxygen and controlling temperature (Chun et al. 2004). It has been reported that the structure of biochar is affected by the pyrolysis process of biomass. At the pyrolysis temperature of 300–700 °C, the biomass will increase more pores and increase the concentration of functional groups (Chen et al. 2017; Xiao et al. 2018). However, in this temperature range, the biochar yield decreases with the increase of temperature (Qilin et al. 2021; Zhai et al. 2022), and the amount of PAHs generated during the preparation also decreases with the increase of temperature (Luo et al. 2016). Considering all these factors, 500 ℃ was chosen as the pyrolysis temperature. Among the many modification methods used to optimize the pore structure, surface functional groups (Lee and Shin 2021), and adsorption sites of biochar (Lee and Shin 2021), alkali modification is a good choice (Ding et al. 2016; Qu et al. 2021).

First, the wheat straw was pre-treated. Cleaned the impurities such as soil and gravel on the surface of raw materials with tap water first, and then put them in a cool and ventilated place to dry naturally for 2 d. After that, put them in a 60 ℃ oven (DHG-9075A, Qixin Scientific Instrument Co. Ltd, Shanghai, China) to dry completely, remove them and cut them to 1–2 cm, and then go through a 40-mesh screen after being crushed by a grinder (LD-200, Yongkang SUN Electromechanical Co., LTD, China). The pretreated wheat straw was sealed in a porcelain crucible and put into a muffle furnace (SX2-4–10, Lichen Bangxi Instrument Technology Co. LTD, Shanghai, China). Subsequently, the wheat straw underwent pyrolysis and carbonization for a duration of 2 h, with a heating rate maintained at 10 ℃/min under N2 atmosphere. After the complete reaction, the wheat straw biochar (WBC) was obtained by being naturally cooled down to room temperature. WBC and sodium hydroxide solid (Guangzhou Chemical Reagent Factory, China) were added to blank conical bottle at 1:2 (m/m) and then add 150 mL sterile water. Transferred them to a shaker (180 rpm) at 30 ℃ for 24 h, which were dried in an oven at 105 ℃ after centrifugation, and then pyrolyzed again under the same conditions. After cooling, the solution was soaked in deionized water, and its pH was adjusted to neutral. After washing and drying, the modified wheat straw biochar (MWBC) was obtained through 40 mesh sieve and encapsulation.

The specific surface area and pore size of MWBC samples were measured using Automatic gas adsorption analyzer (Autosorb iQ, Anton Paar, Austria) and the BET method, and the structure and morphology were observed using High resolution field emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Merlin, Zeiss, Germany) scanning electron microscope.

Preparation of microbial community immobilized by biochar

Microbial community was immobilized on biochar by adsorption method (Bouabidi et al. 2019). Preparation of biochar-immobilized microbial community was conducted as follows: 0.02 g MWBC was added to 18 mL inorganic salt medium, and then placed in a conical bottle for 20 min of sterilization in a sterilizer at 121C. After cooling to room temperature, 2 mL of heavy suspension (OD600 = 1, made from selected enriched microbial community in the “Soil sampling and enrichment” section) was added. After mixing, the bacteria were placed in a shaker (30 ℃, 180 rpm) and fully oscillated to be loaded on the biochar as much as possible. After 24 h of adsorption and fixation, the sample was transferred to a high-speed centrifuge and centrifuged for 5 min under the condition of 4500 rpm. Then, the supernatant was removed and the immobilized sample was washed with sterile water. The MWBC immobilized bacterial community was obtained by repeated centrifugation for 2 times. The fixed time was 4, 8, 12, 16, 20 and 24 h, respectively. After each group was fixed, MWBC immobilization was transferred to 5 min in 20 mL aseptic water, then 10 min was placed statically, the supernatant was taken, and the supernatant was diluted 10,000 times. The number of microorganisms adsorbed was determined by dilution coating plate method to determine the best fixed time. On the basis of the optimal fixed time, the pH values of MSM were 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9, respectively. After each group was fixed, the best pH value was determined by the same method mentioned above. All measurements were carried out in 3 replicates. Under the conditions of the best fixed time and the best pH value, the MWBC-immobilized microbial community was prepared for later experiments.

Experiments on BaP removal of microbial community and MWBC-immobilized microbial community

Experiment on BaP removal of microbial community (Experiment 1): The microbial community screened in the “Soil sampling and enrichment” section was inoculated into Super Optimal Broth (SOB, Supplementary Table S2) and transferred to a shaker at 30 ℃ for 15 h. After 5 min of centrifugation under the condition of 8000 rpm, the culture was washed with inorganic salt medium (MSM) for 3 times, and then re-suspended in MSM. According to the “Soil environmental quality – Risk control standard for soil contamination of development land” (GB 36600–2018) published by Standardization Administration of China (SAC), the BaP control value index of the second type of construction land is 15 mg/kg. Thus, in the BaP removal experiment of microbial community, the initial BaP concentration was 5, 10 and 20 mg/L, respectively. The experiments were carried out at 15 ℃, 20 ℃, 25 ℃, 30 ℃ and 35 ℃. The grouping of specific experimental conditions is shown in Table 1, and it is named T1 ~ T15 according to different temperatures and different BaP concentrations. Besides, 2 mL resuspension inoculant (OD600 = 1) was inoculated in 20 mL MSM-BaP medium. The same concentration of MSM-BaP mediums without microbial community was prepared as blank control groups. All experiments were designed with 3 replicates, and destructive sampling was carried out after 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12 d to detect the removal of BaP. Extraction and detection of BaP

By taking dichloromethane (Macklin) with the same volume as the culture medium, the samples were extracted and repeated twice. The concentration of BaP was determined using high performance liquid chromatograph (LC-20AT, Shimadzu, Japan) (Bansal et al. 2017). The mobile phase was acetonitrile (Macklin) and water, with a ratio of 9:1 (v/v). A C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm) was adopted, the injection volume was 10 μL, and the detection wavelength was 290 nm. In order to calculate the concentration of BaP, the standard curve of the external standard detection of BaP was drawn, and the correlation coefficient was 0.99.

Detection of microbial community amplificon

In Experiment 1 and Experiment 2 in the “Experiments on BaP removal of microbial community and MWBC-immobilized microbial community” section, when T10, T11, T12, W10, W11 and W12 were cultured at 30 ℃ 12th day, the cultures were taken for amplification. The samples of T10, T11 and T12 were recorded as B groups, while those of W10, W11 and W12 were recorded as M groups. The groups were named according to the concentration of BaP: B5, B10, B20, M5, M10 and M20, respectively.

The samples of M5, M10 and M20 were pretreated first. The sample was transferred to a centrifuge tube and shaken with Vortex shaker (XH-C, Changzhou Runhua Electrical Appliances Co., Ltd, China) for 5 min to free the fixed microorganisms from the biochar. Then DNA from all samples was extracted by using Bacterial Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (components of the kit are in the Supplementary Table 3, using steps in Supplementary Appendix 1). After extraction, the samples were sent to Novogene (Beijing, China) for amplicon sequencing and analysis. The forward primer for 16S rRNA analysis is Bakt_341F (CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG), and the reverse primer is Bakt_806R (GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT). The process involved DNA and PCR extraction, DNA amplification, library construction, NovaSeq6000 analysis, sequencing and data processing. Data was reprocessed by DADA2 method (Callahan et al. 2016; Li et al. 2020) to generate ASVs (Amplicon Sequence Variants). QIIME2’s classify-sklearn algorithm was adopted to annotate the species of each ASV using the pre-trained NaiveBayes classifier (Bokulich et al. 2018; Bolyen et al. 2019). According to the results of ASVs annotation and the characteristic table of each sample, the species abundance table was obtained, and the Alpha diversity index was calculated.

Detection of metabolites

Adjust the pH of the samples to approximately 2.0 with hydrochloric acid (Damao, Tianjin, China). Extract the samples with the same volume of ethyl acetate (Macklin) as the culture medium, repeated three times. The combined organic phase was placed in a round-bottom flask and concentrated and dried by a rotary evaporator, then dissolved in n-hexane, and filtered to obtain the sample to be tested. Metabolites are detected using gas chromatography mass spectrometry (GC–MS, QP 2010 Ultra, Shimadzu, Japan), with a chromatographic column specification of Agilent DB-5 (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 µm), injection volume of 5 µL, programmed temperature increase, initial temperature of 100 ℃ retained for 2 min, heated to 300 ℃ at 10 ℃/min, then operated stably at 300 ℃ for 12 min, totaling 34 min, scan range, ion source EI, ionization energy 70 eV, interface temperature 260 ℃, and injector temperature 280 ℃ Fig. 1.

Results and discussion

Results of enrichment of BaP degrading microbial community

The experimental results of degradation of benzo [a] pyrene in 10 mg/L concentration of Benzo [a] pyrene in inorganic salt medium within 7 d by the enriched bacteria of each sample are shown in Fig. 2–3 (The microbial communities of soil samples from Karamay and Dongying were named KL and DY. The removal efficiency of 10 mg/L benzo [a] pyrene by microbial communities KL1, KL2, DY1, DY2, MT1, MT2, MY1 and MY2 on the 7th day reached 42.32%, 17.85%, 31.99%, 18.10%, 26.73%, 14.15%, 35.34% and 19.56%, respectively. The highest experimental results showed that the degradation rate of 10 mg/L benzo [a] pyrene was the highest in soil samples from Karamay oilfield by increasing the concentration of benzo [a] pyrene in the medium of glucose-Benzo [a] pyrene. Therefore, this microbial community was selected as the benzo [a] pyrene degrading bacteria for subsequent experiments.

BaP removal efficiency of microbial community

The removal efficiency of different concentrations of BaP by the microbial community at different temperatures is shown in Fig. 4. Since the study of BaP degrading microbial community is applied to the actual remediation of BaP contaminated environment (especially soil or water pollution), it is helpful to explore the removal efficiency of BaP at different temperatures and find the suitable use temperature, which is conducive to the selection of the corresponding location, season and time period of these temperatures after investigation in the actual application to achieve a good removal effect. Therefore, we mainly analyze the removal efficiency of different temperatures under different concentrations of BaP in the following.

Removal efficiency of 5 mg/L (a), 10 mg/L (b) and 20 mg/L (c) BaP by microbial community

For 5 mg/L BaP (Fig. 4a), the BaP removal efficiency on the 12th day reached 41.56% (T1), 48.20% (T4), 57.88% (T7), 56.50% (T10) and 53.69% (T13) at 15, 20, 25, 30 and 35 ℃, respectively. The highest removal efficiency was obtained at 25 ℃ (T7) and 30 ℃ (T10), and the change trend of removal efficiency was also similar. They reached 38.78% and 34.44% from the second day, more than half of the final removal efficiency. After the 2nd day, the increase rate of removal efficiency slowed down, and the final removal efficiency reached more than 56%. At the same time, they were higher than the other three temperatures throughout the experiment. At 35 ℃ (T13), the removal efficiency was only about 20% at 2–4 d, but rapidly increased to 41.83% at 6 d, and then slowed down until reaching 53.69% at 12 d. At 20 ℃ (T4), the removal efficiency was similar to that at 35℃ within 4 d, but it maintained the lifting rate at the 4th day and was not significantly increased until the 8th–12th d. The removal efficiency of 15 ℃ (T1) was the lowest, which increased at a higher than average rate in the first 8 d, but only changed insignificantly after 8 d, and finally reached about 40%. Therefore, based on the final removal efficiency and the changing trend of removal efficiency, 5 mg/L BaP was best removed by the degraded community in the range of 25–30 ℃, and the removal efficiency was also good at 35 ℃.

For 10 mg/L BaP (Fig. 4b), the BaP removal efficiency on the 12th day reached 39.79% (T2), 46.52% (T5), 44.83% (T8), 45.59% (T11) and 55.34% (T14) at 15, 20, 25, 30 and 35 ℃, respectively. The highest removal efficiency was at 35℃ (T14), which was the lowest within 4 d, only about 10%. However, after the 4th day, the growth rate of removal efficiency increased significantly, and exceeded other experimental groups after the 10th day, finally reaching 55.34%. At 15–30 ℃, the removal efficiency increased rapidly or slowly, but the overall trend was similar. The removal efficiency kept a stable increase within 8 d, and only changed insignificantly after 8 d. Their final removal efficiencies were also relatively close, reaching a range of 39–46%. Therefore, in terms of final removal efficiency, 10 mg/L BaP was best removed by the degraded community at 35 ℃.

For 20 mg/L BaP (Fig. 4c), the removal efficiency of BaP at 15, 20, 25, 30 and 35 ℃ on the 12th day reached 35.18% (T3), 34.36% (T6), 39.07% (T9), 35.59% (T12) and 46.88% (T15), respectively. Overall, it can be seen that the removal efficiency of each group is very similar at the 6th day, reaching the range of 26–29%. Then, the removal efficiency at 35℃ (T15) increased rapidly, which was much higher than that at other temperatures, reaching 46.70% on the 10th day, and the increase in removal efficiency basically stopped within the next 2 d. At 15–30 ℃, their removal efficiency increased within a range after 6 d, but the final removal efficiency was relatively close, reaching the range of 35–40%. Therefore, based on the final removal efficiency and the changing trend of removal efficiency, the best removal effect of 20 mg/L BaP by the degraded community was obtained at 35 ℃.

It can be seen that the selected microbial community has a good removal effect on middle and low concentration (5–20 mg/L) of BaP at 35℃, and the removal effect of 5 mg/L BaP is better at 25–30 ℃. This is close to the optimal growth temperature of microorganisms with good BaP removal capacity that we consulted (Punetha et al 2022). At the same time, it can be seen from the trend of the curve that the removal efficiency of most experimental groups is relatively low within the first 4 d, which may be related to the nature of BaP. It has a large octyl alcohol water partition coefficient (Zhou et al. 2023), which leads to its low bioavailability and is not preferentially selected as an energy source by microorganisms under normal circumstances. Although BaP was used as the sole carbon source in the experiment, the toxicity of BaP, its stress to environmental organisms and its complex thick ring structure, as mentioned before, also make the initial degradation effect less than ideal. At the appropriate temperature, the removal efficiency increased significantly after 4–6 d, which also reflected that the microorganism began to adapt to the environment and started normal growth and metabolism in the presence of BaP.

For 20 mg/L BaP (Fig. 5c), the BaP removal efficiency reached 43.71% (W3), 42.29% (W6), 48.36% (W9), 55.72% (W12) and 65.27% (W15) at 15, 20, 25, 30 and 35 ℃ on the 12th day, respectively. At 35℃ (W15), although the removal efficiency was low within 2 d, it maintained a high increasing trend throughout the experiment, and was significantly higher than that of the other groups after the 8th day, and finally reached the highest removal efficiency of 65.27% on the 12th day. Therefore, based on the final removal efficiency and the changing trend of removal efficiency, the best removal effect of 20 mg/L BaP by the degraded community was obtained at 35 ℃.

In addition, the final removal efficiency of experiment 1 (adding free microbial community) and experiment 2 (adding MWBC-immobilized microbial community) under the same BaP concentration was compared. The results of statistical analysis are shown in Fig. 6 (t test, ***p < 0.001). At the same BaP concentration and temperature, the removal efficiency of immobilized MWBC microbial community was significantly higher than that of free microbial community. The highest removal efficiency of 5–20 mg/L BaP was 65.27–72.85%. This indicated that immobilization of biochar could improve the removal effect of BaP and lay a foundation for improving the remediation effect of BaP pollution. The microbial community adhered to the biochar can enhance cell density, biological activity, and resistance to environmental interference through synergistic interactions with the biochar (Wu et al. 2021), thereby improving the remediation efficiency of BaP pollution. Specifically, biochar serves as a nutrient source for microorganisms. Apart from its high carbon content, it also provides macronutrients (N, P, K), secondary nutrients (Ca, Mg, S), and micronutrients (Zn, Cu, Fe, Mn B and Mo)(Mukherjee et al. 2022). The porous structure of biochar offers ample space for microbial growth and reproduction (Wang et al. 2021a, b). These two factors contribute to the proliferation of microbial communities. Additionally, the layered functionalization of biochar’s porous structure includes different pore sizes such as micropores, micropores mesoporous pores, and macropores. The abundance of micropores significantly enhances organic pollutant adsorption due to their large surface area; whereas mesoporous pores improve liquid–solid adsorption effects (Dai et al. 2021; Li et al. 2021), enabling efficient mass transfer of pollutants like BaP from contaminated environments to the microbial community, resulting in enrichment and degradation by microorganisms. Moreover, BaP absorbed within or near biochar exerts stress on non-BAP degrading dominant microorganisms or those unable to resist BaP toxicity. This stress effect aids in screening dominant strains while further enhancing enrichment of degrading microorganisms (Quero et al. 2015). In conclusion, the immobilization of biochar greatly enhances BaP degradation by microbial communities, reaching a removal efficiency rate up to 75.18% for 5 mg/L concentration. Result of final removal efficiency is similar to or even slightly higher than the remediation effect of some biochar immobilized microorganisms on BaP reported previously. For example, E. prolifera biochar and unmodified wheat straw application of biochar to BaP removal efficiency of 66.9% and 63.1% (Guo et al. 2021; Qiao et al. 2020).

Analysis of microbial community structure

Alpha diversity was used to analyze the diversity of microbial communities within samples (Supplementary Table S4). The coverage index was ≥ 0.999, indicating that the sequencing results could represent the real situation of the sample microorganisms. The largest chao1 index is B5 and the smallest is M20. It can be seen that group B (adding free microbial community) was larger than group M (adding MWBC immobilized microbial community), and it decreased with the increase of BaP concentration in the group. Combined with chao1, Shannon and Simpson indices in the table, the order of community diversity from high to low was B5 > B10 > B20 > M5 > M10 > M20. According to the data within the group, the community diversity decreased with the increase of BaP concentration in the samples with the same dosage condition. This is due to the strong stress effects such as toxicity of high concentrations of BaP on other microorganisms and species with low abundance, which cannot be tolerated by these microorganisms. The dominant microorganisms can grow and metabolize normally after adapting to the concentration conditions. This ultimately leads to reduced community diversity (Quero et al. 2015). Compared with adding free microbial community, adding biochar immobilized degraded microbial community reduced the diversity of microbial community and favored the growth of dominant microorganisms. This is due to the rich internal pore structure of biochar and the nutrients provided that promote microbial growth and enrichment (Mukherjee et al. 2022). Meanwhile, the adsorption of BaP by biochar expanded the growth and reproduction of dominant microorganisms that could degrade BaP (Anyika et al. 2015; Haider et al. 2022), and inhibited the reproductive tendency of other microorganisms. The combined effects of the two factors resulted in the reduction of community diversity. This is exactly the opposite of the trend of BaP removal efficiency between groups. This may reflect that the enrichment of dominant degrading microorganisms is the direct cause of the improved BaP removal efficiency. In addition, it has been reported that biochar can induce the shift of microbial communities (Zhang et al. 2018). It is speculated that when the biochar immobilized microbial community is put into the actual environment, other types of dominant microorganisms that may exist outside will also be attracted to biochar, which is more conducive to the cooperation between microorganisms. An efficient microbial consortium would be an important reason to improve the degradation efficiency of BaP (Zhang et al. 2021).

Figure 7a shows the relative abundance of six groups of samples at the phylum level (the richness was among the top 10). In B groups, Proteobacteria was the phylum with the highest relative abundance, with relative abundance of 96.95% (B5), 96.11% (B10) and 91.95% (B20), followed by Firmicutes and Bacteroidota. The relative abundance of the three phylums added together was more than 99%. In M groups, the phylum with the highest relative abundance was Firmicutes, which was 72.46% (M5), 69.72% (M10) and 68.56% (M20), followed by Proteobacteria, accounting for more than 25%. Proteobacteria is a common PAHs degrading bacteria (Akash et al. 2023). According to the study, in most mixed microorganisms, γ-Proteobacteria, α-Proteobacteria and β-Proteobacteria can all occupy a dominant position at the class level and the order level (Gosai et al. 2022; Sun et al. 2010). Therefore, the high abundance of Proteobacteria might explain its high BaP removal efficiency. Herein, under the condition that only this microbial community was added in B groups, Proteobacteria occupied an absolutely dominant position as the dominant phylum. On the other hand, Firmicutes is also a common phylum isolated from PAHs enrichment culture (Akash et al. 2023). Some studies have confirmed its better efficiency in adapting to higher temperature than Proteobacteria, and its degradation rate of total PAHs generally increases significantly with the increase of the temperature (Sun et al. 2022). This may be one of the reasons that the BaP removal efficiency of microbial community at 35 ℃ is not significantly lower than or even higher than that at 25–30 ℃. In M groups, the relative abundance of Firmicutes was much larger than that of Proteobacteria, indicating that the environment and nutrients provided by WMBC contributed to the accumulation of Firmicutes, became the dominant bacteria in the degradation of BaP, and significantly improved the removal efficiency of BaP. At the same time, Proteobacteria also accounted for more than 1/4, which still played its own role in the removal of BaP in M groups. In many reports, many PAHs-degrading microbial communities isolated from highly polluted aged PAHs contaminated soil or heavy crude oil contaminated soil contain high abundance of Proteobacteria and Firmicutes, and they could still maintain high growth and metabolic ability after different treatments of soil (Gou et al. 2020; Lee et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2018). This demonstrated that the favorable synergism between them could improve the activity of microbial community and the degradation effect of PAHs. In addition, Bacteroidota is good at degrading various polymers (Huang et al. 2023), and is also a PAHs-degrading bacteria (Akash et al. 2023). Therefore, although relatively abundant in the flora, it could also contribute to the removal of BaP and metabolic intermediates.

Relative Abundance of microbial community at phylum (a) and genus (b) level

Figure 7b shows the relative abundance of six groups of samples at the genus level (the richness ranked in the top 30). On the whole, the relative abundance of Pseudomonas, Stenotrophomonas and Bacillus was similar to that of the family. Pseudomonas is a common genus of PAHs-degrading bacteria (Premnath et al. 2021), which can degrade not only LMW-PAHs (low molecular weight polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons), but also HMW-PAHs. According to the report, Pseudomonas can effectively biodegrade polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and heterocyclic derivatives through the transverse hydrogen peroxide pathway (Liu et al. 2021). In addition, Pseudomonas aeruginosa can use biosurfactant (Zang et al. 2021) and has an anti-heavy metal effect (Safahieh et al. 2012), which may be the basis of BaP degradation ability. Stenotrophomonas, a genus within the Xanthomonadaceae family, is traditionally recognized as a type of plant pathogenic bacteria. However, it has also been discovered in studies of microbial communities inhabiting oil-contaminated soils. Various research findings indicate that certain Stenotrophomonas species possess the capability to utilize PAHs as their sole carbon source for growth. As part of a microbial alliance, Stenotrophomonas not only efficiently degrades pollutants such as Phe and Pyr (Gosai et al. 2022), but also improves the tolerance to L-PAHs such as BaP and its ability as a source of energy (Zafra et al. 2014). This may be one of the reasons explaining the high BaP degradation ability of the microbial community. Herein, in B groups, when the concentration of BaP was low, Stenotrophomonas demonstrated a higher capability to utilize BaP compared to other microorganisms, making it the dominant genus initially. However, when the BaP concentration rose, its utilization ability diminished. This decrease in BaP utilization ability might be attributed to the inhibitory effect of higher BaP concentrations on microorganisms within the community. However, at this time, Pseudomonas still possessed a high degradation ability to a higher concentration of BaP and occupied a dominant position. Bacillus is another common genus of PAHs-degrading bacteria, which plays a pioneering role in microbial recovery by consuming mobilized organic matter under adverse environmental conditions (Medina et al. 2020), as well as tolerance to heavy metals (Rabani et al. 2022). Bacillus exerts a good degradation effect on PAHs such as BaP, BaA, Pyr and heavy crude oil (Kong et al. 2022). In the case of microbial alliance, whether the combination of Bacillus licheniformis and Bacillus mojavensis of the same genus (Eskandary et al. 2017), or the interaction with other genus microorganisms (Jacques et al. 2008), PAHs removal becomes more efficient. Herein, in M groups, Bacillus was always the dominant genus, indicating that the environment and nutrition provided by biochar could be preferentially utilized by Bacillus, and then cooperate with Pseudomonas to effectively degrade BaP. In addition to these dominant microorganisms, the low-abundance species also belonged to Alcaligenaceae and Sphingobacteriaceae, which had been reported to degrade PAHs and had certain BaP degradation ability (Mangwani et al. 2017; Yang et al. 2015).

GC–MS analysis results of metabolites in the degradation process of Benzo[a]pyrene

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) constitutes an analytical technique that gauges the ion-to-charge ratios for the analysis of chemical substances. The sample under detection is separated based on the disparity in retention time resulting from the distribution coefficient between the fixed phase and the mobile phase within the gas chromatograph. Subsequently, the components of diverse groups are segregated at distinct times and flow out of the gas chromatograph column into the ion source of the mass spectrometer. Gas molecules undergo ionization from the ion source, thereby generating ion fragments (the precursor ion in a decomposition reaction is called the parent ion). The ions are separated in accordance with their mass-to-charge ratio due to their distinct movement behaviors in an electric or magnetic field, and the mass spectrum is obtained (Dymerski 2018). The electron impact ion source (EI) is a frequently utilized ion source that employs a high-speed electron beam to impact the sample, thereby generating electrons and molecular ions M+. M+ continues to be bombarded by electrons, inducing the rupture of chemical bonds or molecular rearrangement and generating a variety of ion fragments.

The mass spectrums of metabolic intermediates detected by GC–MS during the degradation of benzo[a]pyrene by the microbial community are illustrated in Fig. 8, while Supplementary Table S5 presents the mass-to-charge ratios of all ion fragments corresponding to each substance. During the analysis of the substances mentioned below, the mass spectrum of each substance was compared using a similarity search method against the standard mass spectral library to deduce their composition.

GS-MC mass spectrums of metabolites

Figure 8a shows the mass spectrum of substance I. The peak of substance I appeared at 6.445 min. After searching, it was presumed to be the derivative product of 1, 2-epoxy-3, 4-dihydroxycyclohexane [a] pyrene. The molecular weight of this derivative was determined to be 530. The ionic fragments with m/z = 57 and m/z = 473 (M+−57) were the fragment -C4H9 under ionic bombardment and the different remaining parts of the lost fragment. Since the substance was alkylated, it was inferred to be 1, 2-epoxy-3, 4-dihydroxycyclohexane[a]pyrene.

The mass spectrum of substance II is depicted in Fig. 8b. The peak corresponding to substance II was observed at a retention time of 10.075 min. The molecular ion (M+) exhibited an m/z value of 274, while the ion fragment with m/z = 55 was presumed to be -C4H7. Additionally, the ion fragment with m/z = 221 (M+−53) resulted from the loss of -C4H5 upon bombardment of M+. It was possible that the ion fragment with m/z = 167 (M+−107) originated from the addition of a hydrogen after losing two -C4H7 groups from M+. This differed by a value of 18 from the most abundant ion fragment with m/z = 149 (M+−125), which could be attributed to the combined molecular weights of one oxygen and two hydrogens. After conducting further research, it had been suggested that substance II may be di(butyl-3-ene-1-yl) phthalate ester, having a molecular formula C16H18O4 and a molecular weight matching that of its molecular ion (m/z = 274). It was also plausible to speculate that the most abundant ion fragment with m/z = 149 (M+−125), generated during bombardment by an ion source, corresponds to phthalic anhydride ion (C8H4O3H+), indicating that this substance could potentially be a derivative formed from phthalic acid during pretreatment.

The mass spectrums of substances III and IV are shown in Fig. 8c and d. Their peaks were observed at retention times of 12.873 min and 13.000 min, correspondingly. The molecular ions exhibited m/z values of 178 (M+), while the ion fragments contained ones with m/z = 152 (M+−26), m/ z = 126 (M+−52), and m/z = 98. M+−26 could be generated by the loss of two -CH fragments from M+, while M+−52 could be obtained by the loss of four -CH fragments from M+. Usually, -CH fragments are lost by ionization of aromatic compounds. The ion fragment with m/z = 98 might be derived from M+−52 as the parent ion through a loss of two -CH2 fragments. Based on m/z value of the molecular ions being equal to 178, it was inferred that the original substances were aromatic compounds with an approximate molecular weight of 178. Both phenanthrene (Phe) and anthracene (Ant) have molecular weights matching this criterion (both are equal to 178). Based on the retrieval contrast and the fact that Phe appeared slightly earlier than Ant in gas chromatography (Anyakora et al. 2005; Wang 2004), substance III was presumed to be Phe and substance IV to be Ant.

Figure 8e shows the mass spectrum of substance V. The peak of this substance appeared at 16.255 min. The m/z value of the molecular ion was 202 (M+), and there were ion fragments with m/z = 174 (M+−28), m/z = 150 (M+−52), and m/z = 122. The presence of M+−28 indicated the loss of two -CH2 fragments from M+, whereas M+−52 indicated the loss of four -CH fragments from M+. The fragment with m/z = 122 could obtain by using M+−52 as the parent ion and losing two -CH2 fragments. Based on the m/z value of the molecular ion of 202, it could be inferred that the origin substance is an aromatic compound with a molecular weight of about 202. The molecular weight of Pyrene (Pyr) was found to be 202, and it was speculated that several -CH2 and -CH fragments were continuously lost during ion bombardment, resulting in the production of these ion fragments. Therefore, the substance V was assumed to be Pyr.

Figure 8f illustrates the mass spectrum of substance VI. The peak of substance VI appeared at 22.248 min. According to the figure, the m/z value of the parent ion was 252 (M+), while fragments with m/z values of 224 (M+−28), 200 (M+−52), 174, and 126 were observed in the ion fragments. The presence of M+−28 and M+−52 suggested a loss of two -CH2 fragments and four -CH fragments from M+. Another loss of two -CH fragments from M+−52 might result in the fragment with an m/z value of 122. Additionally, the fragment with m/z value of 126 could be considered as the one retaining exactly two linked benzene ring structures and having half the m/z value compared to that of the parent ion. The molecular weight analysis revealed that the original substance was likely an aromatic compound weighing approximately 252, consistent with BaP. It could be inferred that substance VI was BaP and its carbon skeleton was disrupted by ion source bombardment, leading to losses in several -CH2 and -CH fragments and generating these specific ion fragments.

Based on the results of metabolite analysis, putative BaP degradation pathways in the microbial community are illustrated in Fig. 9. It is postulated that substance I, following derivatization, corresponds to the original compound 1,2-epoxy-3,4-dihydroxycyclohexane[a]pyrene. Some ion fragments (m/z = 149 and m/z = 221) of substance II were observed during the ion source bombardment process. Their structures were found to be similar as phthalic anhydride and diethyl phthalate. Considering the acidification in the pretreatment procedure, it was plausible that substance II was formed through esterification reaction between phthalic acid and hydrocarbon present in the sample. It is therefore speculated that substance II was phthalic acid before the derivation reaction. It is also a metabolite on the metabolic pathways of Phe and Ant. Given the detection of Ant, it is hypothesized that an intermediate substance between BaP and Ant exists in the metabolic pathway—Benzo(a)anthracene (BaA). Consequently, it is suggested that BaP undergoes degradation through two pathways: one from BaP to BaA, then to Ant and ultimately to phthalic acid for subsequent degradation; while the other proceeds from BaP to 1,2-epoxy-3,4-dihydroxycyclohexane[a]pyrene, via Pyr and Phe, and finally leading to phthalic acid for further degradation.

Conversely, it is crucial to evaluate the potential detrimental effects of intermediate metabolites such as tricyclic and tetracyclic PAHs, including Phe, Pyr, and Ant, resulting from the aforementioned analysis on both the physical and chemical properties of soil and the indigenous microbial community structure when introduced into actual organic-contaminated sites. Particularly in cases where contaminated sites are situated deep within the soil profile and come into contact with groundwater, there exists a risk that certain phenanthrene and pyrene compounds may be transported by groundwater prior to subsequent degradation reactions taking place. This could potentially give rise to secondary pollution hazards for water sources, agricultural land, and artificial construction areas (Zhang et al. 2019a, b). To effectively address this issue during bioremediation of organic-contaminated sites, an additional approach would involve incorporating specific degrading microorganisms targeting LMW-PAHs like phenanthrene, pyrene, and anthracene alongside those capable of degrading BaP into biochar immobilization systems. This strategy aims to expedite further degradation of these metabolic intermediates into inorganic compounds.

Conclusion

In this study, a highly efficient microbial community capable of removing benzo[a]pyrene (BaP) was enriched from oil-contaminated soil. Upon immobilization with modified wheat straw biochar, this microbial community exhibited a remarkable BaP removal efficiency of up to 75.18% within 12 d in an inorganic salt medium containing 5–20 mg/L BaP. Meanwhile, the microbial community was observed and studied by 16SrRNA sequencing analysis, and the results demonstrated Pseudomonas, Stenotrophomonas and Bacillus as the main genus in the microbial community. This lays the foundation for us to potentially apply MWBC immobilized microbial communities to soil or water contamination remediation in the future.

Nevertheless, successful application of this biochar-immobilized microbial consortium in practical environmental restoration efforts targeting BaP-contaminated areas necessitates overcoming several hurdles. (1) In addition to BaP, various organic pollutants, such as petroleum hydrocarbons and halogenated hydrocarbons, may exist in most contaminated sites (Liu et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2021a, b; Zeng et al. 2019). Thus, prior investigation into the toxicity of other pollutants towards the microbial community is required. After confirming their safety without causing significant harm to the microbial community, further microbial strains for degrading other pollutants must be incorporated. (2) The intermediate metabolites generated during degradation may lead to secondary environmental pollution. Hence, microorganisms capable of degrading known intermediate metabolites should be co-introduced, and continuous monitoring for other potentially harmful metabolites should be conducted. If any additional harmful metabolites emerge, corresponding degrading microorganisms should be promptly introduced. (3) The immobilized microbial community of biochar prepared through the adsorption method may exhibit instability in real soil or sewage sites due to uncontrollable factors such as gravity and impact forces. Therefore, it is crucial to enhance the immobilization technique in future studies, such as embedding method(Qiao et al. 2020), to improve both the effectiveness and stability of biochar immobilization. (4) Biochar may also influence the physical and chemical properties of contaminated sites, necessitating the collection of soil and sewage samples in advance for research purposes and subsequent testing during the treatment process. In conclusion, although the bioremediation method utilizing MWBC-immobilized microbial community exhibits promising results at the laboratory scale, further comprehensive investigations are imperative prior to its implementation on a larger environmental scale.
