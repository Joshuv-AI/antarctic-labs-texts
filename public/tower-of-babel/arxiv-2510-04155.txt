Mixing cycles on triods
Sourav Bhattacharya

Abstract
. We prove a necessary and sufficient condition for a cycle on a triod τ \tau to be mixing . We show that for continuous maps on a triod τ \tau , the rule governing the coexistence of periods of slow , fast , and ternary mixing cycles induces three different linear orderings of the natural numbers, and that these orderings persist even when the underlying map is slightly perturbed.

Primary 37E05, 37E15, 37E25, 37E40; Secondary 37E45 37B20

1.
Introduction

From the perspective of dynamical systems, one of the most fundamental manifestations of long-term behavior of points is
periodicity
. Cycles (or periodic orbits) therefore play a central role in understanding dynamics, and describing the possible sets of periods that can arise for a given class of maps is a natural and compelling problem. A landmark result in this direction is the celebrated theorem of A. N. Sharkovsky
[
11
]
, which gives a complete account of the possible sets of periods of cycles for a continuous self-map of the interval. To formulate the theorem, we need to first introduce the
Sharkovsky ordering
on the set of natural numbers,
\mathbb{N}
:

3\succ\mkern-14.0mu_{s}\;5\succ\mkern-14.0mu_{s}\;7\succ\mkern-14.0mu_{s}\;\dots\succ\mkern-14.0mu_{s}\;2\cdot 3\succ\mkern-14.0mu_{s}\;2\cdot 5\succ\mkern-14.0mu_{s}\;2\cdot 7\succ\mkern-14.0mu_{s}\;\dots

\succ\mkern-14.0mu_{s}\;\dots 2^{2}\cdot 3\succ\mkern-14.0mu_{s}\;2^{2}\cdot 5\succ\mkern-14.0mu_{s}\;2^{2}\cdot 7\succ\mkern-14.0mu_{s}\;\dots\succ\mkern-14.0mu_{s}\;8\succ\mkern-14.0mu_{s}\;4\succ\mkern-14.0mu_{s}\;2\succ\mkern-14.0mu_{s}\;1

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, define
Sh(k)=\{\,m\in\mathbb{N}:k\succ\mkern-14.0mu_{s}\;m\,\}\cup\{k\}

and set
Sh(2^{\infty})=\{\,1,2,4,8,\dots,2^{n},\dots\,\}
. Let
\mathrm{Per}(f)
denote the set of all periods of cycles of
f
. The
Sharkovsky Theorem
may then be stated as follows:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
11
]
)
.

Let
f:[0,1]\to[0,1]
be a continuous map. If
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
m\succ\mkern-14.0mu_{s}\;n
and
m\in\mathrm{Per}(f)
, then
n\in\mathrm{Per}(f)
. Consequently, there exists some
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{2^{\infty}\}
such that

\mathrm{Per}(f)=Sh(k)
. Conversely, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{2^{\infty}\}
, there exists a continuous map
f:[0,1]\to[0,1]
satisfying
\mathrm{Per}(f)=Sh(k)
.

The significance of Sharkovsky’s Theorem lies in its introduction of the notion of a
forcing relation
. Specifically, if
m\succ\mkern-14.0mu_{s}\;n
, then the existence of a cycle of period
m
for an interval map necessarily implies the existence of a cycle of period
n
. In this way, the theorem reveals how different “
types
” of cycles (where the “
type
” refers to the
period
) are interconnected through
forcing
. Building on this, several avenues of research naturally emerge. One direction is to replace the interval by more general spaces; another is to move beyond periodic orbits to more general orbits, such as
homoclinic trajectories
. A further challenge is to establish a more “
refined
” framework describing the coexistence of cycles, than that offered by Theorem
1.1
. For a detailed account of the theorem’s development, historical context, extensions, and its broader role in the theory of dynamical systems, the reader could refer to
[
9
]
. The line of extending Sharkovsky’s result from intervals to more complex spaces was pursued for maps of
triods
in
[
1
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
6
]
and
[
5
]
.

A
triod

\tau
is defined as
\tau=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:z^{3}\in[0,1]\}
. Geometrically, it may be viewed as a continuum formed by three copies of
[0,1]
, joined at a common endpoint, called the
branching point

``a"
. Each connected component of
\tau\setminus\{a\}
is called a
branch
of
\tau
. The set of periods of cycles for a continuous map
f:\tau\to\tau
, where the central
branching point

a
remains fixed, was studied in
[
1
,
4
]
. A more detailed account was provided in
[
3
]
, where it was demonstrated that the set of possible periods can be represented as union of “initial segments” of certain linear orderings, each associated with rational numbers in the interval
(0,1)
having denominators not exceeding
3
. These orderings were defined on specific subsets of the rationals. However, this phenomenon was only empirically observed and lacked a theoretical proof.

Finally in 2001, Blokh and Misiurewicz (see
[
6
]
), introduced
rotation theory
for
triods
and provided a coherent justification of the previously observed phenomenon. It is noteworthy that
rotation theory
originates with Henri Poincaré, who first introduced
rotation numbers
for
circle homeomorphisms
(see
[
10
]
).

Building up on the results obtained in
[
6
]
, the concept of
triod-twists
—the
simplest
cycles associated with a prescribed
rotation number

\rho
—was introduced in
[
5
]
. Specifically, a cycle
P
is called a
triod-twist
, if there exists a map
f:\tau\to\tau
for which
P
is the
unique
cycle having
rotation number

\rho
. In
[
5
]
, such cycles were systematically studied, leading to their complete characterization. Moreover, the dynamics of all possible
unimodal triod-twist
cycles corresponding to a given
rotation number
were also described.

Recall that a continuous map
f:X\to X
on a topological space
X
is called
mixing
if, for any two nonempty open sets
U,V\subset X
, there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{n}(U)\cap V\neq\varnothing
for all
n>m
. A natural question is how this notion manifests in the context of maps on a
triod
. A first step in this direction is to characterize cycles on
triods
whose dynamics emulate those of a
mixing map
. Additionally, one may ask whether a notion of
forcing
can be defined among the periods of such cycles, and, if so, whether this
forcing order
remains robust under small
perturbations
of the map.
In this paper, we will answer precisely these questions.

To formalize these ideas, we first introduce the notion of a
mixing cycle
. A map
f_{P}:\tau\to\tau
is called
P
-
linear
for a cycle
P
on
\tau
, if it
fixes

a
, is
affine
on every component of
[P]-(P\cup\{a\})
and also
constant
on every component of
\tau-[P]
where
[P]
is the convex hull of
P
. A cycle
P
on a
triod

\tau
is called a
mixing
cycle if the
P
-
linear
map,
f_{P}
is a
mixing
map. This provides a natural dichotomy for cycles on
triods
: they are either
mixing
or
non-mixing
.

This paper aims to explore the problem of co-existence among the periods of
mixing
cycles on a
triod

\tau
. This question can be addressed once we fully characterize
mixing
cycles on
triods
. To this end, we employ the notion of a
block structure
for cycles of maps on a
triod
, originally developed by Misiurewicz for circle maps (see
[
2
,
8
]
) and later adapted to maps of
triods
(see
[
6
]
). We prove that a cycle
P
on a
triod

\tau
is
mixing
if and only if it has
no block structure
. This constitutes our first main result (see Section
3
, Theorem
3.7
).

Next, leveraging this result together with the frameworks developed in
[
6
]
and
[
5
]
, we undertake a detailed study of the structural properties of
mixing
cycles on
triods
in Section
4
. As observed in the paper
[
5
]
, a qualitative
bifurcation
occurs in the family of
triod-twist
cycles at the
rotation number
,
\rho=\frac{1}{3}
(see Theorem
2.10
). This observation motivates a natural classification of cycles of maps on
triods
into three categories: those with
\rho<\frac{1}{3}
, referred to as
slow
cycles; those with
\rho>\frac{1}{3}
, referred to as
fast
cycles; and those with
\rho=\frac{1}{3}
, called
ternary
cycles. The dynamics of
mixing
cycles within these classes are analyzed in Section
4
(see Section 4, Theorems
4.1
,
4.2
,
4.4
,
4.5
, and
4.8
).

The framework developed in Section
4
enables us in Section
5
, to study the
forcing relations
among
slow
,
fast
, and
ternary

mixing
cycles separately. This yields three distinct orderings of the natural numbers that capture the
forcing structure
within these three classes. Moreover, these orderings are shown to persist under small
perturbations
of the map, thereby demonstrating the robustness of the forcing structure (see Section
5
, Theorems
5.2
,
5.4
and
5.7
).

The organization of the paper is as follows:

(1)

In Section 2, we state all essential definitions and theorems that will be used throughout the manuscript.

(2)

In Section 3, we prove that a cycle on a
triod
is
mixing
if and only if it has
no block structure
.

(3)

In Section 4, we investigate the structural properties of
mixing
cycles on
triods
, providing foundation for the subsequent analysis.

(4)

In Section 5, we apply the results obtained in Sections 3 and 4 to derive explicit orderings among the periods of
slow, fast
and
ternary

mixing
cycles on
triods
, which depict the
forcing relation
among these cycles. We also establish the
stability
of this ordering.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Symbols and conventions

Let
\mathcal{U}
represent the family of all continuous self-maps
f:\tau\to\tau
for which the central
branch point

a
of
\tau
serves as the unique
fixed point
. We denote by
\mathcal{R}\subset\mathcal{U}
the subclass consisting of all
regular
maps
f:\tau\to\tau
(refer to Section
2.5
for definition of
regular
maps). For any two points
x,y\in\tau
, we write
x>y
whenever both points belong to the same
branch
of
\tau
and
x
lies farther from
a
than
y
. The notation
x\geqslant y
is used to indicate that either
x>y
or
x=y
. Given any subset
A\subset\tau
, its
convex hull
will be denoted by
[A]
.

2.2.
Monotonicity

A continuous map
f:\tau\to\tau
is said to be
monotone
on a subset
U\subset\tau
if, for every
v\in f(U)
, the preimage
f^{-1}(v)
is a
connected subset
of
U
. A subset
U\subset\tau
is called a
lap
of
f
if it is a
maximal open subset
of
\tau
on which
f
is
monotone
, maximality being understood with respect to set inclusion. The number of
laps
of a map
f\in\mathcal{U}
is referred to as the
modality
of
f
. Similarly, the
modality
of a periodic orbit (or
cycle
)
P
is defined as the
modality
of the
P
-
linear
map
f

associated
with
P
.

2.3.
Patterns

We say that two cycles
P
and
Q
on the
triod

\tau
are
equivalent
if there exists a homeomorphism
h:[P]\to[Q]
that conjugates
P
and
Q
while
fixing
the individual
branches
of
\tau
. This relation partitions the set of all cycles on the
triod

\tau
into
equivalence classes
, called
patterns
. A cycle
P
of a map
f\in\mathcal{U}
is said to
exhibit
a
pattern

A
if
P
belongs to the
equivalence class

A
. Both a cycle and its corresponding
pattern
are said to be
primitive
when all points of the cycle lie on distinct
branches
of
\tau
. We say that a
pattern

A

forces
another
pattern

B
provided that every map
f\in\mathcal{U}
possessing a cycle of
pattern

A
necessarily has a cycle of
pattern

B
. It follows from
[
1
,
6
]
that if a
pattern

A

forces
a
pattern

B\neq A
, then
B
does not
force

A
. Furthermore, a cycle
P
is said to
force
another cycle
Q
when the
pattern exhibited
by
P

forces
the
pattern exhibited
by
Q
.

The following result provides a particularly elegant characterization of all
patterns
that are
forced
by a given
pattern

A
.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
1
,
6
]
)
.

Let
f
be a
P
-linear map, where
P
is a cycle of pattern
A
.
Then a pattern
B
is forced by
A
if and only if
f
possesses a cycle
Q
of pattern
B
.

Definition 2.2
.

Given a cycle
P
, a map
f\in\mathcal{U}
is said to be
P
-adjusted
if it has no other cycle, distinct from
P
, that exhibits the same
pattern
as
P
.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
1
]
)
.

For any cycle
P
of a map
f\in\mathcal{U}
, there exists a
P
-adjusted map
g
such that
g
coincides with
f
on
P
; that is,
f|_{P}=g|_{P}
.

2.4.
Rotation Theory for triods

We are now prepared to introduce the
rotation theory
for
triods
, as developed in
[
6
]
. In our model, we regard the
triod

\tau
as an embedded subset of the plane, with the central
branching point

a
located at the origin and each
branch
represented by a straight-line segment. The three
branches
of
\tau
are denoted by
B=\{b_{i}\mid i=0,1,2\}
, labeled in the clockwise direction, where indices are taken modulo
3
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{U}
and let
P\subset\tau\setminus\{a\}
be a finite set. The
oriented graph
associated with
P
, denoted by
G_{P}
, is defined as follows: the
vertex set
of
G_{P}
consists of the points of
P
, and for
x,y\in P
, we declare that there exists a
directed edge
(
arrow
)
x\to y
, whenever there exists a point
z\in\tau
such that
x\geqslant z
and
f(z)\geqslant y
. A
directed cycle
in
G_{P}
will be referred to as a
point loop
in
\tau
. A
point loop
is called
elementary
if it passes through each
vertex
of
G_{P}
at most once.

If
P
is a periodic orbit of period
n
, the
directed loop

\gamma:x\to f(x)\to f^{2}(x)\to\cdots\to f^{n-1}(x)\to x
where
x\in P
, is called the
fundamental point loop
associated with
P
. Assume that the
oriented graph

G_{P}
is
transitive
, that is, there exists a
directed path
between any two
vertices
. Note that if
P
is a periodic orbit, then
G_{P}
is automatically
transitive
.

Let
A
denote the set of
directed edges
(
arrows
) of
G_{P}
. We define the
displacement function

d:A\to\mathbb{R}
by
d(u\to v)=\frac{k}{3}
, whenever
u\in b_{i}
and
v\in b_{j}
with
j=i+k\pmod{3}
. For a
point loop

\Gamma
in
G_{P}
, we define
d(\Gamma)
as the sum of the
displacement values
along the
edges
of
\Gamma
. In this planar realization of
\tau
,
d(\Gamma)
represents the total number of complete clockwise turns about the origin, and hence
d(\Gamma)
is an integer.

We introduce the
rotation pair
and
rotation number
of a
point loop

\Gamma
as
rp(\Gamma)=(d(\Gamma),|\Gamma|),\quad\rho(\Gamma)=\frac{d(\Gamma)}{|\Gamma|}
, where
|\Gamma|
denotes the length (i.e., number of
edges
) of
\Gamma
. The closure of the set of
rotation numbers
of all
loops
in
G_{P}
is called the
rotation set
of
G_{P}
and is denoted by
L(G_{P})
. According to
[
12
]
, the set
L(G_{P})
coincides with the smallest interval containing the
rotation numbers
of all
elementary loops
in
G_{P}
.

Following the notations in
[
6
]
, a
rotation pair

rp(\Gamma)=(mp,mq)
, where
p,q,m\in\mathbb{N}
with
g.c.d(p,q)=1
, can be represented in the form
mrp(\Gamma)=(t,m),\quad\text{where }t=\frac{p}{q}
. The pair
(t,m)
is referred to as the
modified rotation pair (mrp)
of the point loop
\Gamma
.

The
rotation number
,
rotation pair
, and
modified rotation pair
of a cycle
P
are defined to be those of its
fundamental point loop

\Gamma_{P}
. Similarly, the corresponding quantities for a
pattern

A
are defined as those of any cycle
P
that
exhibits

A
. The
rotation interval forced
by a
pattern

A
is defined as the
rotation set

L(G_{P})
of the
oriented graph

G_{P}
associated with a cycle
P
which
exhibits

A
. Finally, we denote by
mrp(A)
, the set of all
modified rotation pairs
of
patterns
that are
forced
by the
pattern

A
.

Figure 1.
Schematic representation of
modified rotation pairs
(
mrp
) on the real line with attached
prongs

Modified rotation pairs
admit a convenient geometric interpretation (see Figure
1
). Consider the real line, and imagine that at each rational point a
prong
is attached, while irrational points are equipped with degenerate prongs. On the prong corresponding to each rational point, mark the set
\mathbb{N}\cup\{2^{\infty}\}
, ordered according to the
Sharkovsky ordering

\succ\mkern-14.0mu_{s}\;
, with
1
placed nearest to the real line and
3
placed farthest from it. All points lying directly on the real line are labeled by
0
. The union of the real line together with all its attached prongs will be denoted by
\mathbb{M}
.

A
modified rotation pair

(t,m)
is then represented by the element of
\mathbb{M}
corresponding to the number
m
on the prong attached at
t
.
No actual
rotation pair
, however, corresponds to
(t,2^{\infty})
or to
(t,0)
. For two elements
(t_{1},m_{1})
and
(t_{2},m_{2})
in
\mathbb{M}
, the
convex hull

[(t_{1},m_{1}),(t_{2},m_{2})]
is defined as the set of all
modified rotation pairs

(t,m)
such that either
t
lies strictly between
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
, or
t=t_{i}
and
m\in Sh(m_{i})
for
i=1,2
.

2.5.
Regular patterns

Definition 2.4
.

A
pattern

A
is said to be
regular
if it does not
force
any
primitive pattern
of period
2
. A cycle
P
is called
regular
when it
exhibits
a
regular pattern
.

By transitivity of the forcing relation, every
pattern
that is
forced
by a
regular pattern
is itself
regular
. A map
f\in\mathcal{U}
is termed
regular
if all of its cycles are
regular
. We denote by
\mathcal{R}
the collection of all such
regular
maps.

Theorem 2.5

(
[
7
]
)
.

Let
A
be a regular pattern corresponding to a map
f\in\mathcal{R}
.
Then there exist patterns
B
and
C
with modified rotation pairs
(t_{1},m_{1})
and
(t_{2},m_{2})
respectively such that
mrp(A)=[(t_{1},m_{1}),(t_{2},m_{2})]
.

Theorem
2.5
provides a complete characterization of the sets of
periods
of cycles of maps on
triods
, as originally established in
[
1
,
3
]
.
This result enables one to infer detailed information about the dynamics of a
regular map

f\in\mathcal{R}
using only limited structural data.

2.6.
Loops of points and intervals

2.6.1.
Point loops and their properties

In Section
2.4
, we introduced the concept of
oriented graph

G_{P}
and the corresponding
point loops
associated with a finite subset
P\subset\tau\setminus\{a\}
. The following theorem shows that to determine the
patterns
forced by a given
pattern

A
, it suffices to analyze the
point loops
within the oriented graph
G_{P}
, where the cycle
P

exhibits

A
.

Theorem 2.6

(
[
6
]
)
.

The following statements hold:

(1)

For any point loop
x_{0}\to x_{1}\to\dots\to x_{m-1}\to x_{0}
in
\tau
, there exists a point
y\in\tau\setminus\{a\}
such that
f^{m}(y)=y
, and for each
k=0,1,\dots,m-1
, the points
x_{k}
and
f^{k}(y)
lie on the same branch of
\tau
.

(2)

Let
f
be a
P
-linear map corresponding to a nontrivial cycle
P\neq\{a\}
.
If
y\neq a
is a periodic point of
f
with period
q
, then there exists a point loop
x_{0}\to x_{1}\to\dots\to x_{q-1}\to x_{0}
in
\tau
such that
x_{i}\geqslant f^{i}(y)
for all
i
.

2.6.2.
Loops of intervals and their properties

We now extend the concept of
loops
from
points
to
intervals
.
The following definitions adopt the standard formulation of
[
2
,
1
]
.
For any two points
x,y\in\tau
lying on the same
branch
, the
convex hull

[x,y]
is called the
interval
on
\tau
connecting
x
and
y
.
An interval
I\subset\tau
is said to
f
-cover
another interval
J\subset\tau
, denoted by
I\rightarrow J
, if
f(I)\supset J
. We say that a sequence of intervals
I_{0}\rightarrow I_{1}\rightarrow I_{2}\rightarrow\cdots
forms a
chain of intervals
on
\tau
if each interval
I_{i}

f
-covers the subsequent one
I_{i+1}
. When the
chain
closes upon itself, that is, there exists
p\in\mathbb{N}
such that
I_{p}\supset I_{0}
, then
I_{0}\longrightarrow I_{1}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow I_{p-1}\longrightarrow I_{0}

is called a
loop of intervals
. An interval
I
on
\tau
is said to be
admissible
if one of its endpoints coincides with the
branching point

a
. A
sequence
(or
loop
) of
admissible intervals
is then referred to as an
admissible chain
(respectively, an
admissible loop
) on
\tau
.

Given a periodic orbit
P
of period
n
, the
loop

\Gamma:[x,a]\to[f(x),a]\to[f^{2}(x),a]\to\cdots\to[f^{n-1}(x),a]\to[x,a],\quad x\in P
, is called the
fundamental admissible loop of intervals
associated with
P
. A result analogous to Theorem
2.6
can also be established for
loops of intervals
on
\tau
.

Theorem 2.7

(
[
1
,
12
]
)
.

Let
I_{0}\to I_{1}\to\cdots\to I_{q-1}\to I_{0}

be a loop of intervals of length
q
on
\tau
.
Then there exists a point
x_{0}\in I_{0}
such that
f^{i}(x_{0})\in I_{i}\quad\text{for all }i=0,1,\dots,q-1
, and
f^{q}(x_{0})=x_{0}
.

2.7.
Colors of points

In describing the
arrows
in the
oriented graph

G_{P}
corresponding to a finite set
P\subset\tau\setminus\{a\}
, we adopt the
color convention
introduced in
[
6
]
.
For any
directed edge (arrow)

u\to v
in
G_{P}
, where
u,v\in P
, we assign the following
colors
according to the
displacement
value
d(u\to v)
:
green
if
d(u\to v)=0
,
black
if
d(u\to v)=\tfrac{1}{3}
,
red
if
d(u\to v)=\tfrac{2}{3}
.
If
P
is a cycle of a map
f\in\mathcal{R}
, the
color
of a point
x\in P
is defined to be the
color
of the arrow
x\to f(x)
in its
fundamental point loop
.
A
loop
composed entirely of
black arrows
will be called a
black loop
.

Theorem 2.8

(
[
6
]
)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{R}
and let
P
be a cycle of
f
. Then the following statements hold:

(1)

For every point
x\in P
, there exists a black loop of length
3
passing through
x
.

(2)

If
x
is a green point, then
x>f(x)
.

(3)

The cycle
P
contains at least one point on each branch of
\tau
.

(4)

The cycle
P
always forces a primitive cycle of period
3
.

Moreover, there exists an ordering
\{b_{i}\mid i=0,1,2\}
(where indices are taken modulo
3
) of the branches of the triod
\tau
such that the points
p_{i}\in P
,
i=0,1,2
, which are closest to the branching point
a
on each branch

, are all black.
This ordering is called the canonical ordering of the branches of
\tau
.

From this point onward, we assume that the

of
\tau
are arranged according to their

.

2.8.
Triod-twist patterns

Definition 2.9
.

A

A

.

In

Theorem 2.10

(
[
5
]
)
.

Let

.

(1)

If
\rho<\tfrac{1}{3}
, the pattern

contains no red points.

We conclude this section by providing a necessary condition for a given

Definition 2.11

(

]
)
.

Theorem 2.12

3.
Characterization of regular mixing patterns

From this point onward, all references to a
pattern
or a
cycle
will, unless otherwise stated, refer to a
regular pattern
or a
regular cycle
, respectively.
We begin by introducing a suitable
metric
on the
triod

\tau
.

Let
b_{0},b_{1},
and
b_{2}
denote the three
branches
of
\tau
, with
a
representing the
central branching point
.
For each
i=0,1,2
, there exists a homeomorphism
\gamma_{i}:[0,1]\to b_{i}
satisfying
\gamma_{i}(0)=a
and
\gamma_{i}(1)
being the
endpoint
of
b_{i}
.
Define a function
\psi:\tau\to[0,1]
by setting
\psi(y)=t
whenever
\gamma_{i}(t)=y
for some
i\in\{0,1,2\}
.
In other words,
\psi(y)
measures the normalized
distance
of the point
y
from the
central point

a
along its
branch
.

We now define a metric
d_{\tau}
on
\tau
by

d_{\tau}(x,y)=\begin{cases}|\psi(x)-\psi(y)|,&\text{if $x$ and $y$ belong to the same $branch$,}\\[6.0pt]
\psi(x)+\psi(y),&\text{if $x$ and $y$ belong to different $branches$.}\end{cases}

For
f,g\in\mathcal{R}
, we define
D(f,g)=\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\tau}d_{\tau}\big(f(x),g(x)\big)
. A
neighborhood
of a map
f\in\mathcal{R}
will henceforth refer to one taken with respect to the metric
D
on
\mathcal{R}
. Furthermore, we shall call any open connected subset of the
triod

\tau
an
open interval
on
\tau
, and its
diameter
(with respect to
d_{\tau}
) its
length
. Let
P
be a cycle of a
P
-
linear
map
f\in\mathcal{R}
. We will call the components of the set
[P]\setminus(P\cup\{a\})
,
P
-
basic intervals
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
P
be a cycle of a
P
-linear map
f\in\mathcal{R}
of period
n
. Let
J
be a
P
-basic interval. Suppose there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
1<m<n
and an open interval
K\subset J
such that
f^{i}(K)\cap P=\varnothing
for
i=0,1,2,\dots m
and
f^{m}(K)\subset J
. Then, the length of
f^{m}(K)
is strictly greater than the length of
K
.

Proof.

Choose
x\in K
. Consider the maximal (in terms of set inclusion)
open interval

L\subset J
containing
x
such that
f^{i}(L)\cap P=\varnothing
for
i=0,1,2,\dots m
. Let
\ell
and
r
be the left and right
endpoints
of the
open interval

L
. We claim that
f^{m}(\ell)\in P
and
f^{m}(r)\in P
. Otherwise, we can find a slightly larger
open interval

L^{\prime}\supset L
which also satisfies
f^{i}(L^{\prime})\cap P=\varnothing
for
i=0,1,2,\dots m
, contradicting the maximality of
L
. Moreover, since
f
is
P
-
linear
, the restriction
f^{m}|_{L}
is
monotone
on
L
. Hence
f^{m}(L)=J
, and the result follows.

∎

In
[
6
]
, Blokh and Misiurewicz generalized to maps on
triods
the notion of
block structure
—a concept originally introduced for maps of the circle (see
[
2
,
8
]
).

Definition 3.2
.

Let
P
be a periodic orbit on a
triod

\tau
corresponding to a
P
-
linear
map
f\in\mathcal{R}
.
We say that
P
possesses a
block structure
over another cycle
Q
if
P
can be partitioned into disjoint subsets
P=P_{1}\cup P_{2}\cup\dots\cup P_{m}
,
called
blocks
, all of equal cardinality, where
m
is the period of
Q
.
These blocks satisfy the following conditions:

(1)

the
convex hulls

[P_{i}]
are pairwise disjoint and none contains the
branching point

a
of
\tau
;

(2)

each
block

P_{i}
contains exactly one point
x_{i}
from
Q
;

(3)

whenever
f(x_{i})=x_{j}
, we have
f(P_{i})=P_{j}
.

The same terminology is applied to
patterns
.
Specifically, we say that a
pattern

A
has a
block structure
over a
pattern

B
if there exists a cycle
P
which
exhibits

A
and admits a
block structure
over a cycle
Q
which
exhibits

B
. Two fundamental results concerning
block structures
, due to Blokh and Misiurewicz
[
6
]
, are stated below.

Theorem 3.3

(
[
6
]
)
.

Let
A
,
B
, and
C
be patterns.
If
A
has a block structure over
B
and
A
forces
C
, then either
C
also has a block structure over
B
, or
B
forces
C
.
Moreover, if
P
exhibits
A
for a
P
-linear map
f\in\mathcal{R}
, then for any pattern
C
with a block structure over
B
, there exists a representative
Q
of
C
contained in
\displaystyle\bigcup_{i}[P_{i}]
, where
P_{i}
are the blocks of
P
.

Theorem 3.4

(
[
6
]
)
.

Assume that a pattern
A
forces a pattern
B
of period
m
, where
A
has no block structure over
B
and
B
is not a doubling. Then, for every integer
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the pattern
A
forces another pattern
C_{k}
of period
km
which has a block structure over
B
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
P
be a cycle of a
P
-linear map
f\in\mathcal{R}
. Suppose
P
has no block structure. Then every
P
-basic interval
J
eventually covers the convex hull of
P
.

Proof.

Consider the set
\mathcal{A}=\displaystyle\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}f^{i}(J)
. We first claim that
\mathcal{A}
is connected. Suppose, on the contrary, that
\mathcal{A}
has
k>1
connected components
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{k}
. Since
f(A_{i})
is a connected subset of
\mathcal{A}
for each
i
, there exists a permutation
\theta:\{1,2,\dots,k\}\to\{1,2,\dots,k\}
such that
f(A_{i})=A_{\theta(i)}\quad\text{for all }i
. In particular, the sets
A_{i}\cap P
,
i=1,2,\dots,k
, form non-trivial
blocks
of
P
. This contradicts the assumption that
P
has no
block structure
. Hence,
\mathcal{A}
is connected.

Next, let the
branches
of
\tau
be
canonically ordered
, and let
p_{i}
(
i=0,1,2
) denote the point of
P
closest to the
branching point

a
in each
branch

b_{i}
. Define
B
as the convex hull of the points
p_{0},p_{1}
, and
p_{2}
. Since
\mathcal{A}
is connected, and the points of
P
are cyclically permuted by
f
, there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{m}(J)\supseteq B
. Moreover, it is easy to see that
f(B)\supseteq B
. Consequently, for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
,
j\geqslant m
we have
f^{j+1}(J)\supseteq f^{j}(J)
. By induction, it follows that there exists
p\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{p}(J)

covers
the entire convex hull of
P
.

∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
P
be a cycle of a
P
-linear map
f\in\mathcal{R}
. Suppose
P
has no block structure. Then, for any open interval in
\tau
contained in
[P]
, there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{m}(U)\cap P\neq\varnothing
.

Proof.

Let us assume to the contrary that
f^{i}(U)\cap P=\varnothing
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, there exists a
P
-
basic interval

J
such that
f^{i}(U)\subset J
for infinitely many
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
M=\{j\in\mathbb{N}

|f^{j}(U)\subset J\}
. Let
\Gamma=\displaystyle\bigcup_{i\in M}f^{i}(U)
. From Lemma
3.1
, if
i_{1},i_{2}\in M
,
i_{2}>i_{1}
, then the length of
f^{i_{2}}(U)
is larger than the length of
f^{i_{1}}(U)
. But the total length of the
P
-
basic interval

J
is finite. So,
\Gamma
can have at-most finitely many components. So, we can choose the largest component
C
of
\Gamma
. By assumption, there exists
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{\ell}(C)\subset J
. But this means by Lemma
3.1
that length of
f^{\ell}(C)
is larger than the length of
C
which is a contradiction, since,
C
is the largest component of
\Gamma
.
∎

We now prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
P
be a cycle of a
P
-linear map
f\in\mathcal{R}
. Then
f
is mixing if and only if
P
has no block structure.

Proof.

Assume first that
P
has a
block structure
. Then every
open interval

K
contained in the convex hull of a
block
has its forward images contained within convex hulls of
blocks
. Consequently,
f
cannot be
mixing
.

Now suppose
P
has no
block structure
, and let its period be
q
. Take any two
open intervals

U,V\subset[P]
. By Lemma
3.6
, there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{m}(U)
contains a point
x_{0}\in P
. Choose a subset
K\subset f^{m}(U)
lying entirely in a
P
-
basic interval

J
with
x_{0}
as an endpoint.

If either
f^{q}(K)
or
f^{2q}(K)
already
covers

J
, the claim follows immediately. Otherwise, depending on the orientation about
x_{0}
, one of these images lies strictly inside
J
. Without loss of generality, assume that
f^{2q}(K)\subsetneq J
; the other case is analogous. Consider the sequence of sets,
\{f^{j\cdot 2q}(K):j\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Each element of this sequence has
x_{0}
as an endpoint. By Lemma
3.1
,
f^{2q}(K)
is strictly longer than
K
, and
f^{4q}(K)
is strictly longer than
f^{2q}(K)
. If
f^{4q}(K)
covers
J
, we are done; otherwise,
f^{4q}(K)\subsetneq J
, and again Lemma
3.1
ensures that
f^{8q}(K)
has strictly greater length than
f^{4q}(K)
. Since, the length of
J
is finite, repeating this argument, we conclude that there exists
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{\ell\cdot 2q}(K)

covers

J
. Since,
K\subseteq U
, so
f^{\ell\cdot 2q}(U)
also
covers

J
.

Finally, by Lemma
3.5
, there exists
s\in\mathbb{N}
with
s>\ell\cdot 2q
such that
f^{s}(U)=[P]
. Therefore, for all
n\in\mathbb{N},n>s
,
f^{n}(U)\cap V\neq\varnothing
. This proves that the map
f
is a
mixing map
.
∎

4.
Properties of Regular mixing patterns

In this section we will study general properties of
regular mixing patterns
. We begin by formulating a criterion that allows us to identify
regular mixing patterns
based on its
rotation pair
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
P
be a cycle of a
P
-linear map
f\in\mathcal{R}
with rotation pair
(k,m)
. If
P
has a block structure with
q
points in each block, then
q
divides both
k
and
m
. In particular, if
k
and
m
are coprime, then
P
is a mixing cycle.

Proof.

Suppose
P
has
block structure
. Since
P
has period
m
and each
block
contains
q
points, it follows that
q
divides
m
. Collapsing each
block
to a single point yields a cycle
Q
of period
m^{\prime}=\tfrac{m}{q}
. Let the
rotation pair
of
Q
be
(k^{\prime},m^{\prime})
. Since
P
and
Q
have the same
rotation number
, we have
\frac{k}{m}=\frac{k^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}=\frac{k^{\prime}}{\frac{m}{q}}
. This implies
k=k^{\prime}q
, and hence
q
divides
k
. Therefore,
q
divides both
k
and
m
, completing the proof.
∎

Next, we show that a
point loop
in a
triod

\tau
persists under sufficiently small
perturbations
of the map.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{R}
. Let
\Gamma:x_{0}\to x_{1}\to x_{2}\to\dots x_{n-1}\to x_{0}
be a point loop for
f
in
\tau
,
x_{i}\in\tau
,
i\in\{0,1,2,\dots n-1\}
. Then, there exists a neighborhood
N
of
f
in
\mathcal{R}
, such that for each
g\in N
,
\Gamma
is a point loop for
g
in
\tau
.

Proof.

By definition of a
point loop
, for each
x_{i}
,
i\in\{0,1,2,\dots n-1\}
, there exists
z_{i}
with
x_{i}>z_{i}
and
f(z_{i})>x_{i+1}
. Since these inequalities are strict, they remain valid for all maps sufficiently close to
f
with respect to the metric
D
. Thus, there exists a neighborhood
N
of
f
in
\mathcal{R}
such that for every
g\in N
, we still have
x_{i}>z_{i}
and
g(z_{i})>x_{i+1}
. Hence
\Gamma
persists as a
point loop
for
g
.
∎

From Theorem
2.10
, the
color
associated with the points of a
triod-twist pattern

\pi
is completely determined by its
rotation number

\rho(\pi)
. Specifically, if
\rho(\pi)<\tfrac{1}{3}
, the
pattern

\pi
comprises only
green
and
black
points, whereas for
\rho(\pi)>\tfrac{1}{3}
, it consists solely of
red
and
black
points. In the special case
\rho(\pi)=\tfrac{1}{3}
, the pattern
\pi
corresponds to a
primitive cycle
of period three and hence consists entirely of
black
points.

Since the
rotation number
quantifies physically the effective “
speed
” of a
pattern
, this observation naturally motivates the following classification of all
regular patterns
according to their
rotation numbers
.

Definition 4.3
.

A
regular pattern

\pi
is:

(1)

slow
, if
\rho(\pi)<\tfrac{1}{3}
;

(2)

fast
, if
\rho(\pi)>\tfrac{1}{3}
; and

(3)

ternary
, if
\rho(\pi)=\tfrac{1}{3}
.

Let
P
be a cycle of a
P
-
linear
map
f\in\mathcal{R}
.
For each
branch

b_{i}

(i=0,1,2)
of
\tau
, let
p_{i}
denote the point of
P
lying closest to the
branching point

a
.
By Theorem
2.8
, there exists a
canonical ordering
of the
branches
of
\tau
such that
p_{0},p_{1},p_{2}
are all
black
.
Throughout the rest of the paper, we shall assume that the
branches
are
canonically ordered
and that the points
p_{0},p_{1},p_{2}
are chosen accordingly.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
P
be a triod-twist cycle of a
P
-linear map
f\in\mathcal{R}
whose period is strictly greater than
3
. Then:

(1)

If
P
is slow, at least one of the points
p_{0},p_{1},p_{2}
is the image of a green point.

(2)

If
P
is fast, at least one of the points
p_{0},p_{1},p_{2}
is the image of a red point.

Proof.

Assume that
P
is a
slow triod-twist cycle
of a
P
-linear map
f\in\mathcal{R}
. Suppose, for contradiction, that none of the points
p_{0},p_{1},p_{2}
is the image of a
green
point. Then each must be the image of a
black
point.

Define
f^{-1}(p_{i})=q_{i}
and
f(p_{i})=r_{i}
for
i=0,1,2
. Since
q_{i}
is a
black
point for
i=0,1,2
, it follows that
q_{1}\geqslant p_{0},\quad q_{0}\geqslant p_{2},\quad q_{2}\geqslant p_{1}
.

Since, the period of
P
is strictly greater than
3
, at least one of these inequalities is strict. Suppose, for instance, that
q_{1}>p_{0}
. Because
p_{0}
is a
black point
, we have
f(p_{0})=r_{0}>p_{1}
. But then
q_{1}>p_{0}
while
f(p_{0})=r_{0}>f(q_{1})=p_{1}
, which implies that
P
is not
order-preserving
. This contradicts the fact that
P
is a
triod-twist
cycle (see Theorem
2.12
).

The argument in the case where
P
is a
fast triod-twist cycle
is entirely analogous, with “
green
” replaced by “
red
.”
∎

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{R}
. Let
f
has a twist cycle
P
with rotation pair
(k,n)
and period strictly greater than
3
. Then,

(1)

if
P
is slow, then there exists a neighborhood
N
of
f
such that for each
g\in N
,
g
has a mixing slow cycle with rotation pair
(k+1,n+3)
.

(2)

if
P
is fast, then there exists a neighborhood
N
of
f
such that for each
g\in N
,
g
has a mixing fast cycle with rotation pair
(k+1,n+3)
.

Proof.

(i) Assume first that
P
is a
slow triod-twist cycle
of a
P
-linear map
f\in\mathcal{R}
. By Theorem
4.4
, one of
p_{0},p_{1}
and
p_{2}
is the image of a
green
point. Without loss of generality, let us assume,
p_{0}
be the image of a
green
point. Let
\alpha
be the
fundamental admissible loop
of intervals associated with
P
. We replace
p_{0}
in the
point loop

\alpha
by
p_{0}\to p_{1}\to p_{2}\to p_{0}
to form a new
point loop

\beta
under
f
. Clearly, the
rotation pair
of
\beta
is
(k+1,n+3)
. By Theorem
4.2
, there exists a neighborhood
N
of
f
such that for each
g\in N
,
g
has the
point loop

\beta
. By Theorem
2.6
, for each
g\in N
,
g
has a cycle
Q_{g}
with
rotation pair

(k+1,n+3)
,
associated
with the
point loop

\beta
.

We now show that
Q_{g}
has
no block structure
. Let
f^{-1}(p_{0})=q_{0}
. Then,
q_{0}>p_{0}
by assumption. Consider the segment
q_{0}\to p_{0}\to p_{1}\to p_{2}\to p_{0}
of the
point loop

\beta
. Again, by Theorem
2.6
, there exists
x\in Q_{g}
such that,
q_{0}>x
,
p_{0}>f(x)
,
p_{1}>f^{2}(x)
,
p_{2}>f^{3}(x)
and
p_{0}>f^{4}(x)
(See Figure
3
). Let
A
be the
convex hull
of the points
p_{0},p_{1}
and
p_{2}
. Clearly,
A=[p_{0},a]\cup[p_{1},a]\cup[p_{2},a]
. It is easy to see that
f(A)\supset A
, that is, the fixed point
a
is
repelling
. So,
f^{4}(x)>f(x)
. Thus,
x>f^{4}(x)>f(x)
in the
branch

b_{0}
.

Figure 3.
Formation of
point loop
of
rotation pair

(k+1,n+3)
from the
point loop
of
rotation pair

(k,n)
in the case,
\frac{k}{n}<\frac{1}{3}
(See Theorem
4.5
)

By the construction of the
loop

\beta
, it follows that each of the intervals
[p_{0},a]
,
[p_{1},a]
, and

Theorem 5.5
.

Let

be a regular mixing ternary cycle of a

-linear map

of period

. Then

is a multiple of

and if

and

be the number of red and green points of

, then,

Proof.

Since,

is

, clearly

must be a multiple of

and simple computation yields

. Now, if

and hence cannot be a

map by Theorem

.

∎
