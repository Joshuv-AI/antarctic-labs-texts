Experienced and anticipated HIV-related stigma and depression among pregnant and postpartum women living with HIV in the Greater Accra Region of Ghana: the role of internalized HIV stigma
Authors: Setor K Sorkpor, Ibrahim Yigit, Rachel G A Thompson, Yijiong Yang, Jerry John Ouner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostpartum women living with HIV (WLWH) in sub-Saharan Africa face intersecting structural, psychological, and social challenges. HIV-related stigma, including experienced, anticipated, and internalized dimensions, has been linked to poor mental health outcomes. However, little is known about how these distinct stigma mechanisms interact to influence depression in Ghanaian WLWH. This study aimed to examine whether internalized stigma mediates the relationship between experienced or anticipated HIV stigma and depressive symptoms among pregnant and postpartum WLWH in Ghana.MethodsThis study used cross-sectional data from 176 postpartum WLWH receiving care at 11 public health facilities in Greater Accra, Ghana, between March and May 2022. Participants completed validated measures of experienced, anticipated, and internalized HIV stigma, as well as depressive symptoms. Descriptive statistics were computed, and two mediation models were tested to assess whether internalized stigma mediated the associations between experienced or anticipated stigma and depressive symptoms.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 31.91 years (SD = 5.12), and the mean depression score was 17.41 (SD = 3.56). In the first mediation model, experienced stigma was not directly associated with depression (B = 0.20, p = .360) but showed a significant indirect effect via internalized stigma (B = 0.31, 95% CI [0.151, 0.520]). Similarly, anticipated stigma had no significant direct effect (B = 0.47, p = .103) but also demonstrated a significant indirect effect through internalized stigma (B = 0.32, 95% CI [0.122, 0.557]).ConclusionInternalized HIV stigma significantly mediates the effects of both experienced and anticipated stigma on depressive symptoms in postpartum WLWH in Ghana. These findings highlight internalized stigma as a key psychological mechanism linking social adversity to poor mental health in this population. Integrating stigma-reduction strategies and targeted mental health support into postnatal HIV care may be critical for improving psychological and physical health outcomes among postpartum WLWH in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Mental health, Indirect effects, Antenatal, Postnatal, Ghana

Background

Despite significant progress in HIV treatment and prevention, women living with HIV (WLWH) continue to face substantial social and psychological challenges, particularly during pregnancy and in postpartum period [1, 2]. WLWH experience higher rates of depression, anxiety, and trauma-related distress than their HIV-negative counterparts, with stigma playing a central role in exacerbating these mental health burdens [1–3]. While increased access to antiretroviral therapy (ART) has improved health outcomes, HIV-related stigma remains a persistent barrier to comprehensive maternal care, influencing both mental health and healthcare engagement [1, 4]. Beyond its impact on ART adherence, stigma may also shape maternal-infant interactions and overall postpartum psychological well-being, yet these dimensions remain underexplored [5].

HIV stigma is a complex and multidimensional psychosocial construct that includes multiple interrelated dimensions such as experienced (enacted) stigma, anticipated stigma, and internalized stigma [6, 7]. Experienced stigma refers to direct discriminatory actions, such as social exclusion, verbal abuse, and mistreatment in healthcare settings. Anticipated stigma involves the expectation or fear of being discriminated against, which can lead to self-isolation and avoidance of healthcare services. Internalized stigma, or self-stigma, occurs when individuals internalize negative societal beliefs about HIV, leading to self-blame, shame, and reduced self-worth [6, 7]. While each of these stigma dimensions has been linked to poor mental health outcomes among people living with HIV (PLWH)[8–10], the ways in which they interact to influence depression, particularly among pregnant and postpartum women in resource-limited settings, remain underexplored [7, 11, 12]. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for developing targeted interventions that address both stigma reduction and mental health support for this population.

HIV-related stigma is closely linked to structural inequalities, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, where systemic poverty and limited access to healthcare disproportionately burden WLWH [13]. In Ghana, postpartum WLWH experience unique vulnerabilities due to the intersection of stigma, economic hardship, and constrained health infrastructure [14]. These structural challenges not only increase the risk of postpartum depression but also intensify experiences of stigma, further restricting access to essential care and psychosocial support [15, 16] In the context of Ghana, pregnant and postpartum WLWH experience a unique intersection of biological (e.g., HIV-related immune suppression and risks of mother-to-child transmission), psychological, and social challenges, with HIV-related stigma remaining a major barrier to their health and well-being [13, 14, 17]. Despite ongoing efforts to reduce stigma, it remains deeply entrenched and continues to shape maternal health outcomes, particularly in resource-limited settings such as Ghana [13, 14, 17]. Stigma not only limits healthcare access and ART adherence but also deepens mental health burdens, particularly in the postpartum period when women are already vulnerable to psychological distress [11, 12, 17]. One of the most concerning consequences of HIV-related stigma is its association with postpartum depression, a leading cause of maternal morbidity and disability worldwide [16, 18]. Among postpartum WLWH, depression is often exacerbated by stigma, which affects both maternal and infant health, increasing the risk of poor developmental, nutritional, and emotional outcomes for infants [11, 12, 19] The interplay of biological, social, and psychological stressors further complicates the postpartum period[20], making it a particularly critical time for maternal mental health [11, 12]. When combined with HIV-related stigma, postpartum WLWH face an even greater risk of depression[11, 16, 19], which can have cascading effects on maternal-infant bonding[12], treatment adherence[12, 19], and long-term well-being [11, 12].

Given these compounding factors among pregnant or postpartum women living with HIV, understanding how different forms of HIV-related stigma contribute to mental health disparities is crucial. Internalized stigma may act as a psychological pathway through which experienced or anticipated stigma leads to depression [6, 12, 21]. Addressing internalized stigma has been shown to improve ART adherence and maternal health outcomes among HIV-positive women [12] and is associated with lower depression and increased self-efficacy [8]. This suggests that individuals experiencing high levels of enacted or anticipated stigma may internalize negative societal beliefs about HIV, exacerbating psychological distress [22]. However, limited empirical evidence explores this pathway among pregnant and/or postpartum women in sub-Saharan Africa, where stigma remains deeply embedded in cultural and structural systems [11].

In this study, we aimed to understand how HIV-related stigma impacts pregnant and postpartum WLWH in the Greater Accra Region of Ghana by examining two key pathways: experienced stigma and anticipated stigma and their effects on depression through internalized stigma. More specifically, we hypothesize that higher experienced or anticipated stigma is associated with higher internalized stigma, which in turn results in greater depression. The mediation models highlight the potential psychological mechanisms linking stigma to mental health, emphasizing internalized stigma as a key factor influencing depressive symptoms. By identifying these relationships, the study provides insights that may inform targeted interventions aimed at reducing internalized stigma and improving maternal mental health outcomes in Ghana and other similar settings.

Methods

Study design and setting

Data for this cross-sectional analysis were derived from a study conducted between March and May 2022 across 11 public hospitals and health centers in the Greater Accra Region of Ghana [23]. These facilities serve as primary sites for antenatal and postnatal care, including the prevention of mother-to-child transmission services for WLWH.

Participants and recruitment

Participants were recruited using convenience sampling during routine antenatal and postnatal visits. Eligibility criteria included (a) being at least 18 years of age, (b) receiving ART for a minimum of two months, and (c) the ability to provide written informed consent in either English or a local language. Women were excluded if they had a documented history of intellectual impairment or serious medical or psychiatric conditions likely to interfere with participation, as determined by clinical records. All participants completed structured, interviewer-administered surveys in private settings within the health facilities. Interviews were conducted in the participant’s preferred language by trained research assistants. These assistants were fluent in English and widely spoken local languages (e.g., Ewe, Ga, and Twi), and were trained to deliver the instruments consistently. Although the survey tools were not formally translated or validated in these languages, standardized verbal translation was used during administration. This practice aligns with established methodological guidance on cross-language research, which recommends using trained bilingual interviewers to ensure conceptual equivalence and participant comprehension [24]. This approach is also commonly employed in Ghana, where English is the official language of education and clinical documentation, and bilingual research personnel frequently conduct interviews in local dialects to ensure participant understanding.

Data collection procedures and ethical considerations

Data were collected using electronic tablets programmed with Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) [25]. The survey included built-in range and logic checks to minimize data entry errors and ensure internal consistency. Research assistants received standardized training and conducted all interviews in person, ensuring consistency in data collection protocols across sites. All participants provided written informed consent prior to data collection.

Ethical approval for the parent study was obtained from the University of California San Francisco Institutional Review Board with approval number 21–35733 and Ghana Health Service Ethics Review Committee number GHS-ERC: 003/12/21. The present analysis used de-identified data and complied with ethical standards for secondary data use.

Measures

Experienced HIV stigma

Experienced HIV stigma was measured using a 9-item scale adapted from the Enacted Stigma subscale of the HIV Stigma Mechanisms Scale (HIV-SMS) [26]. This scale assesses participants’ self-reported experiences of discriminatory or differential treatment due to their HIV status across three domains: family members, community/social workers, and healthcare providers. Sample items include: “Family members have avoided me,” “Community/social workers have discriminated against me,” and “Healthcare workers have treated me with less respect.” Participants rated how often they experienced each behavior in the past six months using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Never”) to 5 (“Very often”), with higher scores indicating more frequent experiences of experienced stigma. A composite score was calculated by taking the mean of the individual items. The scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency in the current sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.97). In the original validation sample, this subscale demonstrated strong reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.87)[26].

Anticipated HIV stigma

Anticipated HIV stigma was assessed using a 9-item scale adapted from the Anticipated Stigma subscale of the HIV Stigma Mechanisms Scale (HIV-SMS) [26]. The scale captures participants’ expectations of experiencing discrimination or negative treatment due to their HIV status across three domains: family, community/social workers, and healthcare providers. Sample items include “Family members will avoid me,” “Community/social workers will deny me services,” and “Healthcare workers will treat me with less respect.” Participants rated each item on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Very unlikely”) to 5 (“Very likely”), with higher scores indicating greater anticipated HIV stigma. A composite score was calculated by taking the mean of the individual items. The scale demonstrated strong internal consistency in the current sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.84). The original version of the scale also demonstrated excellent reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.87)[26].

Internalized HIV stigma

Internalized HIV stigma was assessed using Internalized Stigma of AIDS Tool (ISAT)[27] [28] The scale included statements such as “Having HIV infection is like being branded with shame” and “I feel ashamed about having HIV/AIDS.”. Participants rated their agreement with each statement on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Strongly disagree”) to 5 (“Strongly agree”), with higher scores indicating greater internalized stigma. A composite score was calculated by averaging the individual items. The ISAT demonstrated high internal consistency in its original validation (Cronbach’s α = 0.91)[27]. In the present study, the ISAT demonstrated strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.84).

Depression

Depressive symptoms were measured using an 11-item version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D), derived from the original 20-item scale developed by Radloff[29], and validated in abbreviated form by Kohout et al.[30] Participants were asked to indicate how often they experienced each symptom in the past week, with responses scored on a 3-point scale: 1 (“Some or a little of the time [1–2 days]”), 2 (“Occasionally or a moderate amount of time [3–4 days]”), and 3 (“Most or all of the time [5–7 days]”). This modified 3-point scale was used to reduce respondent burden and enhance interpretability, particularly in a community-based sample of PLWH, where simplified response options may improve data quality and reduce cognitive fatigue. A total score was calculated by summing all individual items. The original 11-item CES-D demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.81) in a validation study in adults [30]. The adapted scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in the current sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.70). Items covered affective, somatic, and cognitive domains, including “I felt depressed,” “My sleep was restless,” and “I had trouble keeping my mind on what I was doing.” Positively worded items such as “I was as good as others”, “I was happy”, and “I felt hopeful about the future”, were reverse-coded. Higher total scores reflect greater depressive symptom severity.

Sociodemographic measures

Sociodemographic data were collected using a structured survey and included participant age, marital status, number of children, partner’s HIV status, religion, education level (self and partner), employment status, monthly income, and place of residence. These variables were primarily categorical and analyzed as frequencies and percentages; age and psychosocial scales were treated as continuous variables.

Data analysis

We calculated descriptive statistics for all participants. We conducted mediation analyses to examine whether experienced or anticipated HIV stigma was associated with internalized HIV stigma, which in turn is associated with depression. We utilized SPSS Version 29.0 and PROCESS macro (Model 4) with 5,000 bootstrapped resamples using percentile confidence intervals (CIs) [31]. When the CI does not include zero, the indirect effect is considered significant. All meditation analyses were cross-sectional, with both unstandardized and standardized coefficients reported, adjusting for age.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics are presented in Table 1. Among 176 pregnant and postpartum WLWH, the mean age was 31.91 years (SD = 5.12, range = 18–44). Nearly 50% reported a monthly income of less than 500 Ghanaian Cedi (GHS). 34% (n = 60) had completed primary school. Nearly 55% (n = 96) were married, and 23.7% (n = 40) had a current partner living with HIV. Mean scores were 1.81 (SD = 1.25, range = 1–6) for experienced HIV stigma, 2.75 (SD = 0.96, range = 1–5) for anticipated HIV stigma, 2.61 (SD = 0.91, range = 1–5) for internalized stigma, and 17.41 (SD = 3.56, range = 11–25) for depression.

Indirect effects

We tested the first mediation model depicted in Fig. 1, examining whether experienced HIV stigma was associated with depression through internalized HIV stigma. The total effect (i.e., the effect when the mediator is not included in the model) of experienced HIV stigma on depression was not significant (B = 0.20, β = 0.07, SE = 0.22, p =.360), which is not a prerequisite for testing indirect effects [32]. The indirect effect was significant (B = 0.31, SE = 0.09, CI[0.151, 0.520]), indicating that experienced HIV stigma was significantly associated with internalized HIV stigma (B = 0.23, β = 0.31, SE = 0.05, p <.001), which in turn was significantly associated with depression (B = 1.39, β = 0.36, SE = 0.29, p <.001).

Discussion

This study provides important insights into the psychological pathways through which HIV-related stigma impacts mental health among pregnant and postpartum WLWH in Ghana. Our findings indicate that both experienced and anticipated stigma are associated with higher levels of internalized stigma, which in turn are associated with greater depressive symptoms. Notably, these indirect pathways were significant even in the absence of a direct relationship between external stigma (i.e., experienced and anticipated) and depression, positioning internalized stigma as a critical mechanism through which social adversity is psychologically internalized and transformed into emotional distress.

These findings are consistent with prior literature indicating that internalized stigma can act as a conduit linking external stigmatizing experiences to poor psychological outcomes. For instance, Ramlagan et al.[33] demonstrated that internalized stigma and depressive symptoms predicted suboptimal ART adherence in a longitudinal study among pregnant WLWH in South Africa. Our study builds upon this evidence by documenting this mechanism within a maternal health context in Ghana, where cultural expectations around motherhood and disclosure of HIV status may amplify internalized shame and self-blame. This aligns with broader stigma frameworks that emphasize how societal rejection can lead individuals to adopt stigmatizing attitudes about themselves [26].

Importantly, our findings highlight anticipated stigma as a significant psychological stressor. Although this dimension remains comparatively underexplored in sub-Saharan African research relative to internalized or enacted stigma[34], it has been consistently linked to healthcare avoidance and psychological distress among PLWH [35–37]. In the Ghanaian context, where HIV disclosure can jeopardize family support and continuity of care, anticipated stigma presents a profound psychological and behavioral barrier. Consistent with our findings, Nutor et al. reported that anticipated stigma frequently deters PLWH from disclosing their HIV status or accessing HIV care, primarily due to fears of gossip, rejection, and being identified at clinics [17]. Mohammed et al. similarly found that stigma in healthcare and community settings contributes to social isolation, disrupted support systems, and psychological distress, including suicidal ideation [38]. These Ghana-specific findings reinforce broader evidence suggesting that the internal burden of fearing discrimination, particularly in perinatal and caregiving contexts, can be as psychologically detrimental as directly experienced stigma [35]. Our results extend this literature by emphasizing the mediating role of internalized stigma in linking both experienced and anticipated stigma to depression among WLWH and by grounding these associations within the lived realities of Ghanaian maternal health.

Given the centrality of internalized stigma in these pathways, our findings suggest that integrating stigma-reduction strategies into maternal HIV services in Ghana could be beneficial. While our study did not test interventions directly, prior research underscores the importance of screening for HIV-related stigma and depressive symptoms during antenatal and postnatal care to help identify women at risk of poor mental health outcomes [34]. Culturally responsive approaches such as peer support, family engagement, and community-based counseling may enhance the acceptability and effectiveness of stigma-reduction efforts, particularly when aligned with local social dynamics and health beliefs. Studies in Ghana have documented how anticipated stigma, fear of community judgment, and service avoidance shape the lived experiences of WLWH, underscoring the need for community-informed and contextually sensitive care models [17, 38]. In addition, addressing stigma and discrimination within healthcare settings is essential for improving care quality and psychological outcomes for pregnant and postpartum WLWH in Ghana, as healthcare workers’ attitudes are a known contributor to internalized stigma [34]. Embedding these practices within maternal HIV services may contribute meaningfully to improving both mental health and treatment outcomes among pregnant and postpartum WLWH.

Moreover, these results have broader implications for national health policy. Stigma reduction and the integration of mental health services into HIV care are increasingly recognized as critical components of comprehensive HIV responses in Ghana and other sub-Saharan African settings [39]. Our findings support this emerging priority by providing evidence for actionable psychological targets and underscore the need for stigma screening and psychosocial support services to be embedded within maternal HIV care pathways. Such integration could contribute meaningfully to achieving Sustainable Development Goal 3, which includes ensuring mental health and well-being for all [40].

While this study has notable strengths, including the use of validated measures and a focus on an under-researched yet highly vulnerable population, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional nature of the data limits the ability to draw causal inferences. Because mediators and outcomes were assessed at the same point in time, we cannot confirm their temporal ordering, which constrains our ability to determine whether the observed associations truly represent pathways linking independent and dependent variables. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as identifying associations consistent with mediation, rather than definitive evidence of causal mechanisms. Longitudinal designs that assess mediators at intermediate time points are needed to establish temporal sequencing and clarify the pathways involved. Second, reliance on self-reported measures may introduce recall or social desirability bias, particularly in relation to sensitive constructs such as stigma and depression. Third, the use of convenience sampling may limit the generalizability of findings, as participants who are accessible during routine clinic visits may differ systematically from those who are not engaged in care. This approach may introduce selection bias and reduce the sample’s representativeness. Fourth, we did not collect information on how many participants were pregnant versus postpartum. Given that the prevalence and severity of depression can vary across different stages of the perinatal period, this lack of distinction limited our ability to examine stage-specific patterns or include perinatal status as a covariate in our analysis. Future studies should consider collecting and analyzing this information to provide a more nuanced understanding of perinatal mental health. Fifth, we were unable to control for some potentially confounding variables (e.g., education status, marital status, and socioeconomic status) in our mediation models due to imbalanced sample distributions, which may limit the precision and generalizability of our findings. Future studies with more diverse or adequately powered samples should more thoroughly examine the influence of these variables. A strength of this study is the use of bootstrapping to estimate confidence intervals for indirect effects. This method does not assume normality and provides more accurate, reliable estimates, especially with smaller samples or skewed data, enhancing the validity of our mediation results. However, this study employed traditional mediation models that do not account for potential exposure-mediator interactions. As a result, the estimated indirect effects assume a constant mediating effect across all levels of the exposure, which may limit the nuance of interpretation. Additionally, the models did not address possible unmeasured confounding, which could bias the mediation estimates. Future research should consider advanced causal mediation techniques that allow for interaction terms and better control for unmeasured confounders (e.g., coping, self-efficacy, social support) [8, 35, 41, 42]. A further limitation is that the mediation models used in this study did not account for potential relationships between experienced and anticipated stigma. It is possible that experienced stigma may influence anticipated stigma, or that the two forms of stigma interact in affecting depression. These scenarios would require the use of more advanced mediation frameworks, such as parallel or serial mediation models. Future research should explore these possibilities to more fully understand the pathways through which different types of stigma influence mental health outcomes. Finally, this study did not assess experiences of intimate partner violence (IPV), which is known to be both prevalent among WLWH in sub-Saharan Africa and strongly associated with both psychological distress and stigma [43]. Future research should consider IPV or other relevant factors as potential moderators or mediators in the relationship between stigma and mental health outcomes. Moreover, while this study examined stigma and depression primarily through the lens of HIV status, intersectional factors such as socioeconomic position, educational attainment, and partner HIV status are also likely to influence how stigma is experienced and internalized. Future research that incorporates intersectional and structural dimensions may offer a more nuanced understanding of how layered social identities shape mental health outcomes among WLWH in Ghana.

Conclusion

This study identifies internalized HIV stigma as a key psychological mechanism through which both experienced and anticipated stigma influence depressive symptoms in pregnant and postpartum WLWH in Ghana. These findings highlight the urgent need to address internalized stigma within both clinical and policy frameworks. Embedding culturally adapted stigma-reduction interventions within maternal HIV services and national policy agendas may serve as a strategic lever to improve maternal mental health, HIV treatment outcomes, and intergenerational health for women and their children.
