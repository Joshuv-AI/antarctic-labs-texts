Testing for correlation between network structure and high-dimensional node covariates
Alexander Fuchs-Kreiß *, Keith Levin *

Abstract
In many application domains, networks are observed with node-level features. In such settings, a common problem is to assess whether or not nodal covariates are correlated with the network structure itself. Here, we present four novel methods for addressing this problem. Two of these are based on a linear model relating node-level covariates to latent node-level variables that drive network structure. The other two are based on applying canonical correlation analysis to the node features and network structure, avoiding the linear modeling assumptions. We provide theoretical guarantees for all four methods when the observed network is generated according to a low-rank latent space model endowed with node-level covariates, which we allow to be high-dimensional. Our methods are computationally cheaper and require fewer modeling assumptions than previous approaches to network dependency testing. We demonstrate and compare the performance of our novel methods on both simulated and real-world data.

Introduction

In a vast range of scientific disciplines, data takes the form of collections of interacting entities that are naturally described by networks
(
Newman 2018
)
.
For example, social media data typically comprise users, pairs of whom engage in friendship or following relations
(
Golbeck 2013
;
McCulloh et al. 2013
)
.
Co-authorship and collaboration networks describe which pairs of researchers work together
(
Ji et al. 2022
)
.
In neuroscience, networks arise in the form of connectome data, in which nodes correspond to brain regions or individual neurons and edges encode strength of connections between brain regions or formation of synapses between neurons
(
Sporns 2012
;
Sotiropoulos and Zalesky 2019
;
Loomba et al. 2022
;
Winding et al. 2023
)
.
Networks arise in bioinformatics and omics in the form of interaction networks, in which the nodes correspond to genes or proteins, and edges encode co-expression or involvement in the same biological processes
(
Alon 2003
;
Hakes et al. 2008
;
Barabási et al. 2011
;
Su et al. 2023
)
.
Network data is common in economics as a way to describe trade among countries
(
Chaney 2014
;
Setayesh et al. 2022
;
Sosa et al. 2024
)
or correlation structure among asset prices
(
Elliott et al. 2014
;
Acemoglu 2015
;
Sosa and Betancourt 2023
)
.

In many settings such as those described above, networks are observed along with node-level covariates that may or may not be related to network structure.
In social media data, user-level features are typically available in the form of user post histories or basic demographic and background information.
Nodes in co-authorship networks are typically endowed with biographical information, current or previous institutional affiliations, and authors’ research interests.
In connectome data, brain regions may be accompanied by cortical thickness measurements
(
Han et al. 2020
, see, e.g.,)
or transcriptome data
(
Hawrylycz et al. 2012
)
.
Nodes in omics interaction networks may be accompanied by annotations or known auxiliary data about what traits or processes a gene or protein is implicated in
(
von Mering et al. 2002
)
.
Trade networks may have node covariates in the form of country-level data such as GDP, demographic makeup, geographic information or membership in treaty organizations.

When such node-level data is available, a natural question concerns whether or not node covariates are associated with network structure.
These covariates may drive network structure, as in social networks in which users who attend the same college or who share hobbies are more likely to form friendships.
In the other direction, there are settings where network structure may drive covariates, such as in the presence of contagion.
A fundamental task for network researchers is to identify which, if any, node-level features are predictive of network structure or vice versa.

Let us consider a specific example: In a social media setting, suppose that we observe which users are connected via an undirected friendship relation.
In addition to the network structure, suppose that we observe many features for each user (e.g., age, gender, interests, and so on).
When modeling this network and its relation to node features, if we care about interaction terms or we are unsure about how features relate to network structure (e.g., a linear or non-linear relationship), we might want to include higher-order interaction terms and series expansions of node-level attributes.
Such a feature vector can quickly grow to intractable size.
For social media analysis, it is clearly of interest to know to what extent the network constitutes new information: if node features explain the network structure well, we can potentially ignore the network and restrict our attention to node features when performing downstream tasks such as node classification.
If the covariates are predictive of network structure, this may also help us in link prediction tasks or to understand a new vertex for which we only observe the features.
On the other hand, if the network is not well explained by the features, the network structure constistutes additional information that must be taken into account in modeling and analysis.

Of course, beyond the prediction setting just sketched, the relation between network and node features may also be of scientific interest in and of itself.
In the same social media setting, for example, it might be of interest to social scientists to understand whether connections between social media accounts are related to, for example, the topics or pages that users engage with.
In protein-protein interaction data, scientists might be interested in whether or not interactions of certain proteins are related to their (known) functions or other annotation information.
In studying trade networks, it may be of interest to understand which country-level features are related to trade or other dyadic relations among nations.

Abstracting the above examples, our goal in this work is to assess whether or not observed node-level features explain network structure (or vice versa).
We approach this problem by assuming that the observed network structure is driven by low-dimensional latent variables associated to the nodes.
If these latent variables were known, we could directly assess whether or not they are associated with the observed node-level covariates.
Of course, in practice, latent network structure is not observed and must be estimated.
A second challenge arises from the possibility that node-level covariates may be high-dimensional, complicating the process of assessing which, if any, of them are predictive for the network structure.
Handling both of these complications is the main technical problem addressed in this paper.

We approach the network dependency testing problem by supposing that the network is generated via a random dot product graph
(
Athreya et al. 2018
, RDPG;)
, a latent space network model in which latent node-level vectors, termed
latent positions
, drive network structure.
The RDPG includes the widely-used stochastic blockmodel and its variants
(
Holland et al. 1983
;
Karrer and Newman 2011
;
Airoldi et al. 2008
;
Gao et al. 2018
;
Jin et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
as special cases, and is the restriction of the broader class of graphons to the case of finite spectra
(see, e.g.,
Lei 2021
;
Lovász 2012
, for details)
.
We then aim to ask how the observed features relate to the unobserved latent positions.
To this end, we suggest two approaches, one model-based and the other model-free.
In the model-based approach, we relate the features and the latent positions through a high-dimensional linear model (see Section
2
), using ridge and LASSO regularization to deal with high-dimensional node covariates.
In the model-free approach, we do not make any explicit modeling assumptions on the relation between latent positions and node covariates, and instead use canonical correlation analysis (CCA) between the node features and the latent positions to detect potential dependency.
While we focus in the present work on the RDPG, we anticipate that our results, particularly those related to our CCA-based methods, can be extended to a broader class of node-exchangeable network models, such as those in
Hoff et al. 2002
;
Lovász 2012
;
Lei 2021
.

Contribution

This paper contributes to the study of network dependency testing.
We introduce four basic methods for assessing whether network structure is correlated with node-level covariates, under the assumption that the network is generated from a low-rank latent space model.
Two of these methods are based on linear regression (see Section
2.1
).
The other two are based on CCA (see Section
2.2
).
Our methods are general enough to incorporate high-dimensional node-level features, either via regularization or via high-dimensional or sparse CCA methods, and since they rely only on matrix-vector products and leading singular values, they are computationally efficient compared to previous methods for this task.

1.1
Prior Work

Incorporating node-level covariates into network models has long been of interest to the statistical network analysis community.
A prominent line of work in this direction concerns network regression, in which the aim is to use network structure, be it latent or observed, to predict node-level respones.
For example,
Acharyya et al. 2023
posit a network model in which the latent positions of an RDPG lie on a manifold.
The estimated latent positions are used to predict node-level responses.
Similar methods aimed at the network regression setting have been pursued previously in the literature
(see, e.g.,
Li et al. 2019
;
Hayes and Levin 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Hayes et al. 2025
, and references therein)
.
Unlike the present setting, these models make the assumption that the node-level covariates are responses in a regression and are thus inherently low-dimensional, obviating the possibility of applying these methods to the high-dimensional setting without substantial modification.

Much recent work in network regression has focused on causal inference
(
McFowland III and Shalizi 2023
;
Chang and Paul 2024
;
Nath et al. 2025
;
Hayes et al. 2025
, see, e.g.,)
wherein we observe responses at each of the vertices and aim to assess causal effects of node- or network-level treatment.
We stress that while the present work is related to network regression, we do not aim to answer causal questions here, and our methods are largely agnostic as to the direction of causal influence between the node-level covariates and the network structure.
Similarly, while recent work has explored the identifiability issues that arise when both contagion and homophily are present in networks
(
Shalizi and Thomas 2011
)
, we are not concerned here with determining which of these two dynamics are at play.
Rather, our goal in the present work is only to test whether or not node-level covariates are associated with network structure, rather than determining which drives the other.

Many methods have been developed over the past fifteen years aiming to leverage node covariates to improve estimation of latent network structure such as community memberships, latent positions or graphon structure
(
Xu et al. 2012
;
Yang et al. 2013
;
Zhang et al. 2016
;
Binkiewicz et al. 2017
;
Su et al. 2020
;
Chandna et al. 2022
;
James et al. 2024
)
.
Since they are focused on estimation of latent network structure, these methods do not directly yield a way to assess whether node features are predictive of (or predicted by) network structure.
Indeed, most of these methods make the implicit assumption that some or all observed node covariates are related to latent network structure.
A related line of work relates latent variables to observed node covariates via a parametric model
(
Perry and Wolfe 2013
;
Yan et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024a
;
Zhao and Niu 2025
)
.
As a side effect of their strong modeling assumptions, it is possible that these methods can be modified to test for whether or not covariates are related to latent network structure, either by leveraging asymptotic distributional results
(
Perry and Wolfe 2013
;
Yan et al. 2018
;
Li et al. 2024a
)
or via computationally intensive Bayesian inference
(
Wang et al. 2023
;
Zhao and Niu 2025
, e.g.,)
.
To the best of our knowledge, however, none of the above-cited papers apply their methods to network dependency testing.

Zhang et al. 2022
proposed a model in which node-level latent variables drive both network structure and the observed node covariates via two different generalized linear models (GLMs).
They developed a method for estimating the latent variables and the GLM parameters and demonstrated the utility of their model in link prediction for missing entries of the adjacency matrix.
Their proposed model is similar to ours in its use of node-level latent variables, but the presence of two models, one for the network and one for the node covariates, renders their approach ill-suited to the task of directly testing dependence between node features and network structure.

Most similar to the present work,

Fosdick and Hoff 2015
considered network dependency testing under a Hoff-style latent space model
(
Hoff et al. 2002
;
Hoff 2005
)
.
They consider a setting in which node-level latent positions and observed covariates are jointly normally distributed.
Under the null hypothesis, the covariance between the latent positions and observed covariates is zero.
Our work follows a similar intuition, albeit under a different, much broader class of models (see Sections
2.1
and
2.2
below).
Our methods apply under much weaker assumptions, relying on permutation tests rather than distributional assumptions, and do not require computationally intensive sampling methods. Moreover, we allow for high-dimensional covariates and provide theoretical guarantees even for methods that use estimated latent positions rather than the true latent positions in a naïve plug-in approach.

The other previous work closest to the methods presented here is due to
Lee et al. 2019
, who proposed to assess network dependency by applying a diffusion maps embedding to the observed network and testing for dependency between these embeddings and the node covariates using distance correlation
(
Székely and Rizzo 2013
;
Székely and Rizzo 2014
)
.
Among the key assumptions in their work is that the network is generated according to a node-exchangeable model
(
Aldous 1981
;
Lovász 2012
;
Orbanz and Roy 2015
)
.
As such, our assumption in the present work that the network be generated from a random dot product graph is only a slight restriction of this assumption
(
Lei 2021
, i.e., the equivalent of imposing spectral decay conditions on the network-generating graphon; see)
.
Our CCA-based methods, introduced in Section
2.2
below, make similar modeling assumptions to those in
Lee et al. 2019
, but we avoid the computational and analytical complexity introduced by choosing the diffusion map parameter
t
and constructing the distance correlation statistic, which requires computing all pairwise distances among vertices’ embeddings and node covariates.

Elsewhere in the literature, there is also an interest in studying the relation between network structure and edge-level covariates, such as in
Chandna et al. 2024
.
In that work, the network latent positions are used to capture the structure that is
not
explained by the edge-level covariates.
In a related line of work,
Mu et al. 2022
considered a block structure as residual randomness after controlling for assortativity derived from a categorical vertex covariate.
That is, on top of the block structure, two vertices are more likely to form an edge when they share the same covariates.
Broadly similar ideas were pursued in
Latouche et al. 2018
under the graphon, motivated by goodness-of-fit testing.
While in principle these modelling strategies can be used to develop a test for the assortative impact of a single categorical covariate in a block model, their interest lies in community detection, and dependency testing is not pursued.

More broadly, the question of whether and when node-level attributes are helpful for inference has been explored in other areas.
For example,
Levin et al. 2020
characterized the conditions under which node-level covariates are or are not beneficial in the context of vertex nomination problems
(
Marchette et al. 2011
;
Coppersmith 2014
;
Fishkind et al. 2015
)
.
The importance of incorporating node covariates has also been highlighted in the related graph matching literature
(
Li et al. 2024b
, see, e.g.,)
.

2
Setup and Methodology

We assume that our data takes the form of a network on
n
vertices with adjacency matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, in which each vertex
i=1,2,\dots,n
has an associated feature vector
Z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
We will allow throughout that
p
depends on
n
(possibly even
p>n
).
Exact restrictions on their relation are provided in the individual theorems.
The network may be either binary, in which case all entries of
A
are in
\{0,1\}
, or weighted, in which case the entries of
A
are elements of
\mathbb{R}
, typically but not necessarily non-negative.
For the sake of simplicity, we assume that the observed network is undirected, so that
A=A^{\top}
, though most of our results presented below can be extended straightforwardly to the case of a directed network.
We make no assumption about the presence or absence of self-edges (i.e., the diagonal entries of
A
), as the diagonal elements of
A
have no influence on the asymptotic behavior of the estimators that we consider below.

We model the observed network as coming from a random dot product graph
(
Athreya et al. 2018
, RDPG;)
, a low-rank latent space network model in which each vertex
i\in[n]
has an associated
latent position

X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, drawn i.i.d. according to some distribution
F
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Conditional on these latent positions, the edges of the network are generated independently in such a way that

\left\{A_{ij}-X_{i}^{\top}X_{j}:1\leq i<j\leq n\right\}

(1)

is a collection of independent mean-zero random variables.
When this is the case, we write

(A,X)\sim\operatorname{RDPG}(F,n),

where we have collected the latent positions
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the rows of
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
for notational simplicity.
Most typically under the RDPG
(
Sussman et al. 2012
, see, e.g.,)
,
F
is such that the latent positions obey
0\leq X_{i}^{\top}X_{j}\leq 1
, and we take
A_{ij}\sim\operatorname{Bern}(X_{i}^{\top}X_{j})
.
We stress again that the setting considered here is more general: we require only that the elements of
A-XX^{\top}
be independent (up to symmetry) and mean zero conditional on
X
.
We do not necessarily make any particular distributional assumptions on the edges beyond standard moment decay conditions (see Section
3
for further discussion of this point).
We assume throughout that the dimension
d
of the latent space is constant with respect to
n
, though we expect that many of our results can be extended to the case where
d
is growing suitably slowly with
n

(
Levin et al. 2022
, see, e.g., the bounds in)
.
Finally, while we assume here that the observed network is distributed as an RDPG, we anticipate that our results can be extended to related models, namely any suitably-structured graphon such as the generalised RDPG
(
Rubin-Delanchy et al. 2022
)
or the graph root distribution
(
Lei 2021
)
, albeit at the expense of more extensive bookkeeping.

To model association (or lack thereof) between node features and network structure, we assume that the latent positions and the node-level features are drawn i.i.d. according to a joint distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
That is, we assume that
\{(X_{i},Z_{i}):i=1,2,\dots,n\}
is a collection of i.i.d. random variables.
If the covariance
\operatorname{Cov}(X_{i},Z_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}
is non-zero, this indicates that node-level features and (latent) network structure are related.
For ease of notation, we will always assume that
Z_{i}
contains an intercept term.
Just as we collect the latent positions into the rows of
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, we collect the node attributes into the rows of
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
.
We remind the reader that our goal in this work is simply to test association between network structure and node-level features.
As discussed in Section
1
, we do not wish to make any assumptions in the present work about the direction of causal influence between the features and the latent positions.
Rather, our only assumption is that the network (as encoded by
A
) and the node-level features
Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots,Z_{n}
are independent conditional on
X
.

Remark 2.1
.

In many settings, it may be appealing to allow the node covariates to depend directly on
A
, rather than on the latent network structure encoded by
X
.
For example, the feature vector
Z_{i}
may include the degree
d_{i}=\sum_{j}A_{ij}
of vertex
i\in[n]
.
Alternatively, we may wish to model node-level features as arising from a contagion process, whereby the features
Z_{i}
of vertex
i
depend in some way on the average features of its neighbors, e.g.,
Z_{i}=\sum_{j}A_{ij}Z_{j}/d_{i}
.
Under the RDPG model assumed above, we anticipate that many of these settings can be treated by the methods that we develop below.
For example, the tools developed and deployed in
Hayes and Levin 2024
and
Hayes et al. 2025
show that we can often treat
A
and
XX^{\top}
as (approximately) interchangeable, a property that we use frequently in the sequel.
Unfortunately, these settings where features are allowed to depend directly on
A
(rather than relating to
A
only via
X
) require far more bookkeeping and analysis.
Thus, for the sake of space and simplicity, we leave this interesting setting for future work.

Remark 2.2
.

It is well known
(
Athreya et al. 2018
, see, e.g.,)
, and can be seen directly from Equation (
1
), that for any orthogonal matrix
Q\in\mathbb{O}_{d}
, the matrix of latent positions
XQ
yields the same distribution over networks as does
X
.
Thus, the latent positions
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}
are identifiable only up to some unknown orthogonal transformation.
Interestingly, with minor exception in Section
2.1
below, this will be of no consequence in our results to follow, as this non-identifiability will typically “cancel out”.

2.1
Model-Based Approach

In the first of our two broad approaches, we assume that the latent positions
X
and the features
Z
are related according to

X=ZB+E,

(2)

where
B\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
is an unknown matrix of coefficients,
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is the matrix of features with independent rows,

E\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
is a noise matrix with independent mean-zero rows, generated independently of
Z
.
In other words, for
k\in[d]
, we assume that the
k
-th latent dimension
X_{\cdot k}
is related to the features
Z
via a linear model with coefficient vector
B_{\cdot k}
.
We stress that in this model, we assume independent latent positions (inherited from the independence structure of
Z
and
E
), but this does not preclude the fact that edges are independent only
conditionally
on
X
.
Moreover, the columns of
Z
,
E
, and
X
may be dependent.
Our goal is to estimate
B
, which will in turn tell us which features (i.e., columns of
Z
), if any, explain the network structure encoded by
X
.
In particular, non-zero rows of
B
correspond to columns of
Z
that are informative about network structure.
Said another way, for a vertex
i\in[n]
, if for dimension
k\in[p]
the row
B_{k,\cdot}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is equal to zero, then this indicates that the
k
-th feature
Z_{ik}
is irrelevant to explaining the latent positions.
That is, feature
k\in[p]
, is unrelated to the network structure.
Similarly, if a column
B_{\cdot k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
for
k\in[d]
is equal to zero, this indicates that the
k
-th coefficient of the latent position
X_{ik}
is not explained by any of the observed node covariates.
Thus, to understand the relation between the features and the network under this model, it suffices to estimate
B
.

As discussed in Remark
2.2
, for any orthogonal transformation
Q\in\mathbb{O}_{d}
, the matrices
X
and
XQ
give rise to the same distribution over networks, so that we cannot hope to distinguish
X
from
XQ
based only on the adjacency matrix
A
.
Analogously, right-multiplying Equation (
2
) by
Q
implies that we can only hope to estimate
BQ
for some unknown
Q
.
This non-identifiability is typically not an issue for downstream applications
(
Athreya et al. 2018
, see discussion in)
.
Unfortunately, the matrix
Q
may obfuscate sparsity patterns present in
B
unless an entire row of
B
equals zero.
Therefore, in the high-dimensional setting, we require sparsity in the sense that most rows of
B
are equal to zero, i.e., that the corresponding node feature is irrelevant for explaining the latent positions.
One way to encourage this sparsity is to split Equation (
2
) into
d
separate regressions, one for each latent dimension, and encourage sparsity in each of the
d
regressions.
That is,

X_{\cdot k}=Zb^{(k)}+E_{\cdot k}~\text{ for }~k=1,2,\dots,d,

(3)

where
b^{(1)},b^{(2)},\dots,b^{(d)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
correspond to the columns of
B
encoding the regression coefficients.
The coefficient vector
B_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
corresponding to the
j
-th node covariate is then given by
B_{j}=(b_{j}^{(1)},b_{j}^{(2)},\dots,b_{j}^{(d)})^{\top}
.

We perform the regression in Equation (
3
) for each of the
d
dimensions of the latent positions.
To deal with high-dimensional node features, we consider using either ridge regression or LASSO, which we discuss in detail below in Sections
3.1.1
and
3.1.2
, respectively.
Of course, since we do not have access to the latent positions
X
directly, we must plug in estimates
\hat{X}
for them.
This estimation can be achieved using, for example, the adjacency spectral embedding
(
Sussman et al. 2012
, ASE;)
or related methods
(
Xie and Xu 2020
;
Xie and Xu 2021
;
Wu and Xie 2022
;
Levin et al. 2022
)
.
This yields estimates

\hat{b}_{r}^{(k)}=\underset{b\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\frac{1}{n}\left\|\hat{X}_{\cdot k}-Zb\right\|^{2}+\alpha_{k}\|b\|_{r}^{r},\text{ for }k\in[d],

(4)

where
r=1
corresponds to the regular LASSO and
r=2
corresponds to ridge regression,
and
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{d}\in[0,\infty)
are regularization parameters.
We collect the estimates
\hat{b}_{r}^{(1)},\hat{b}_{r}^{(2)},\dots,\hat{b}_{r}^{(d)}
in the columns of a matrix
\hat{B}_{r}
, that is,
\hat{B}_{r,ik}=\hat{b}_{r,i}^{(k)}
.
Moreover, we write for easier recognition

\hat{B}^{\operatorname{ridge}}:=\hat{B}_{2}~\text{ and }~\hat{B}^{\operatorname{rLASSO}}:=\hat{B}_{1}.

If
B
is indeed row-sparse, then the
d
separate regressions in Equation (
3
) share the same sparsity structure, which can be promoted directly using group LASSO or multi-task learning
(
Bühlmann and van de Geer 2011
, see, e.g, Chapters 4 and 8 of)
.
As such, we might instead consider the estimator

\hat{B}^{\operatorname{gLASSO}}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{B}_{\operatorname{gLASSO},1}^{\top}\\
\hat{B}_{\operatorname{gLASSO},2}\\
\vdots\\
\hat{B}_{\operatorname{gLASSO},p}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}=\underset{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\frac{1}{nd}\left\|\hat{X}-Z\beta\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\|\beta_{j}\|,

(5)

where
\alpha\geq 0
is a regularization parameter.

For selection of the tuning parameters
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{d}
in Equation
4
or
\alpha
in Equation
5
, we use cross-validation
(
Hastie et al. 2015
)
.
Note that a ridge penalty of the type
\sum_{j=1}^{p}\|\beta_{j}\|^{2}
corresponds to the case
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\cdots=\alpha_{d}
in the separate regression and will therefore not be treated separately.
If regularization is not required by the user, it is of course possible to set
\alpha=0
in all estimators (in fact, all these estimators are identical in this case).
We study this case when we analyze ridge regression in Section
3.1.1
below.

Remark 2.3
.

2.2
Model-Free Approach

In many settings, we may wish to avoid assuming an explicit relation between
X
and
Z
as in Equation (
2
), but we may still be willing to believe that the network
A
is generated according to a low-rank model as in Equation (
1
).
If the latent positions
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
were observed, a classical model-free way of assessing the presence of a relation between the latent network structure and the observed features
Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots,Z_{n}
would be canonical correlation analysis
(
Hotelling 1936
, CCA;)
.
We measure the association between the latent positions and the node covariates according to how well we can correlate their projections onto a common one-dimensional space:

\displaystyle\rho_{X,Z}=\max_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\max_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u^{\top}X_{i}v^{\top}Z_{i}-\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u^{\top}X_{i}\right)\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v^{\top}Z_{i}\right)

(7)

\displaystyle\text{ subject to }~

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(u^{\top}\!X_{i}\right)^{2}\!-\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u^{\top}\!X_{i}\right)^{2}\!=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(v^{\top}\!Z_{i}\right)^{2}\!-\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v^{\top}\!Z_{i}\right)^{2}\!=1.

In other words, we aim to find directions
u
and
v
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, respectively, along which our two data sets are maximally correlated.
The optimal choices of
u
and
v
correspond to the
first canonical directions
for
X
and
Z
.
The resulting value of the optimization problem is the CCA alignment score or, as we will often call it in the sequel, the
CCA coefficient
.
If this alignment score is suitably far from zero, then this constitutes evidence that the latent positions and the node-level features are related.
We note that the CCA alignment in Equation (
7
) can be extended to allow for multiple canonical directions by choosing collections of orthonormal vectors
u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
We largely focus here on the case of one canonical direction for the sake of space and simplicity.
That said, our theoretical results established below, which draw from tools in the subspace perturbation literature
(
Cai and Zhang 2018
;
Cape et al. 2019
, see, e.g.,)
, apply also to this more general multi-dimensional setting.

As has been observed elsewhere
(
Muirhead 1982
;
Scharf and Mullis 2000
;
Kessler and Levina 2023
, see, e.g.,)
, the CCA problem in Equation (
7
) can be recast in terms of finding the singular values of a matrix:

\rho_{X,Z}=\max_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\|u\|=1}\max_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\|v\|=1}u^{\top}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{X}^{-1/2}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{X,Z}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z}^{-1/2}v,

(8)

where, writing

M_{n}=I-\frac{1}{n}J\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

(9)

for the “centering matrix”,

\widetilde{\Sigma}_{X}=\frac{1}{n}X^{\top}M_{n}X\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}~\text{ and }~\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z}=\frac{1}{n}Z^{\top}M_{n}Z\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}

(10)

are the the sample covariance matrices of
X
and
Z
, respectively,
and

\widetilde{\Sigma}_{X,Z}=\frac{1}{n}X^{\top}M_{n}Z\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}

(11)

is the sample covariance matrix between the latent positions
X
and the node features
Z
.
We note that we include tildes on all three of these matrices to stress that they are finite-sample estimates of respective population quantities
\Sigma_{X}
,
\Sigma_{X,Z}
and
\Sigma_{Z}
.
Thus, in light of Equation (
8
), we may equivalently examine the leading singular value(s) of

\operatorname{CCA}(X,Z)=\widetilde{\Sigma}_{X}^{-1/2}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{X,Z}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z}^{-1/2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}.

(12)

Of course, in practice, the latent positions
X
are unobserved and thus cannot be used in the optimization in Equation (
8
).
To account for this, as with our model-based approaches presented in Section
2.1
, we will replace
X
with an estimate
\hat{X}
and proceed with the CCA alignment of
\hat{X}
and
Z
instead, examining the singular values of

\operatorname{CCA}(\hat{X},Z)=\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\hat{X}}^{-1/2}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\hat{X},Z}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z}^{-1/2}.

(13)

The main challenge in obtaining a performance guarantee for the CCA matrix in Equation (
13
) arises from the fact that we have replaced the matrix of latent positions
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
with estimates
\hat{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
.
As discussed previously in Remark
2.2
, this comes at the cost of rotational nonidentifiability, in the sense that
\hat{X}
recovers
X
only up to right-multiplication by some unknown orthogonal transformation.
The key technical challenge in our proofs lies in accounting for this orthogonal non-identifiability and showing that the resulting error between
\hat{X}
and a suitably transformed
X
is not too large, so that the CCA alignment score of
\hat{X}
and
Z
is close to that of
X
and
Z
.
We will see that the orthogonal non-identifiability poses no particular challenge, as the CCA coefficient defined in Equation (
8
) is ultimately a property of subspace alignment, and is thus invariant to orthogonal transformations of the rows of either
X
or
Z
.

In building the matrix in Equation (
13
), as in our model-based approaches, a user must select an embedding dimension, and Remark
2.3
applies here as well.
In the event that the node covariates are high-dimensional, a range of CCA methods exist for handling such data, including those designed to account for structure (e.g., sparsity) in the features.
In our theoretical results below, we assume that the dimension
p
of the features
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is fixed with respect to
n
.
We anticipate, however, that standard methods for sparse or high-dimensional CCA could be deployed here
(
Witten and Tibshirani 2009
;
Song et al. 2016
;
Gao et al. 2017
, see, e.g.,)
as could regularized covariance estimation methods
(see, e.g.,
Fisher and Sun 2011
;
Ledoit and Wolf 2018
;
Le et al. 2020
, and citations therein)
.

The method just described has the disadvantage of needing to select the latent dimension
d
.
Since
X
is not observed, an alternative to working with
X
is to directly apply CCA between
A
and
Z
, and examine the behavior of
\operatorname{CCA}(A,Z)
.
In so doing, we avoid having to choose a latent dimension
d
, but this comes at the cost of having introduced another high-dimensional feature: the
n
-dimensional rows of the adjacency matrix
A
.
As with estimating
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z}
, there are a range of methods available to handle the resulting high-dimensional covariance matrix.
We use a simple shrinkage estimate, and consider the singular values of the matrix

\operatorname{CCA}_{\gamma}(A,Z)=\left(\widetilde{\Sigma}_{A}+\gamma I\right)^{-1/2}A^{\top}M_{n}Z\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z},

(14)

where
\gamma=\gamma_{n}\geq 0
is a regularization parameter to be specified below,
M_{n}
is the centering matrix as in Equation (
9
), and
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{A}
is defined analogously to
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{X}
and
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z}
above,

\widetilde{\Sigma}_{A}=\frac{1}{n}A^{\top}M_{n}A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}.

We will see in our results below that a wide range of values for
\gamma
result in satisfactory behavior of
\operatorname{CCA}_{\gamma}(A,Z)
, meaning that this is a far less fraught model selection problem than selecting the embedding dimension.

3
Main Results

As mentioned in Section
2
, we consider a more general edge noise model than the “classical” RDPG
(
Athreya et al. 2018
)
.
Rather than making explicit distributional assumptions on the edges in our network (e.g., Bernoulli edges), our theoretical results require only that the adjacency matrix and certain quantities derived from it concentrate about their population analogues at a suitably fast rate.
We state in Assumptions
1
,
2
and
3
below the rates that are relevant for our discussion, and describe afterwards two settings in which these rates can be explicitly given.

Assumption 1
.

Conditional on
X
, the random network
A
has expectation
XX^{\top}
and obeys

\left\|A-XX^{\top}\right\|=O_{P}\!\left(\eta_{n}\right)

(15)

for some sequence of non-negative reals
(\eta_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
.

Since we do not observe
X
, our results rely on estimating
X
(up to orthogonal non-identifiability, as discussed in Remark
2.2
).
A number of methods for estimating
X
are available, depending on the precise choice of model for how
A
varies about
X

(
Rohe et al. 2011
;
Sussman et al. 2012
;
Lei and Rinaldo 2015
;
Levin and Lyzinski 2017
;
Lyzinski et al. 2017
;
Levin et al. 2017
;
Athreya et al. 2018
;
Xie and Xu 2020
;
Xie and Xu 2021
;
Wu and Xie 2022
;
Levin et al. 2022
, see, for example,)
.
Rather than choosing one of these particular methods or models, we formulate our results under more general assumptions on how well the estimate
\hat{X}
recovers the true latent positions
X
.

Assumption 2
.

We can produce a sequence of estimates
\hat{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
of the latent positions
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
such that there exists a sequence of random orthogonal matrices
Q\in\mathbb{O}_{d}
satisfying

\left\|\hat{X}-XQ\right\|_{{2,\infty}}=O_{P}\!\left(\xi_{n}\right)

for some sequence of non-negative reals
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Our results for the model-free approaches presented in Section
2.2
depend on a slightly stronger assumption on the edge distribution than that in Assumption
1
.

Assumption 3
.

The entries of
A-XX^{\top}
are such that, for any sequence of
W,V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
conditionally independent of
A-XX^{\top}
given
X
and having columns of bounded norm,

\left\|W^{\top}\left(A-XX^{\top}\right)V\right\|=O_{P}\!\left(\zeta_{n}\right)

for some sequence of non-negative reals
(\zeta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

The above assumptions concern how quickly the observed network concentrates about its expectation and how quickly an associated estimate of the latent positions
X
recovers them (as measured in two-to-infinity norm) and how the error
A-XX^{\top}
interacts with fixed subspaces.
Before proceeding, we mention a pair of results from the literature under two different network models and indicate the choices of
\eta_{n}
,
\xi_{n}
and
\zeta_{n}
that they imply.

In the case of binary networks, consider the following simplification of Lemma 5 from
Lyzinski et al. 2017
.

Theorem 3.1

(
Lyzinski et al. 2017
, Theorem 5)
.

Let
(A,X)
follow an RDPG with binary edges and suppose that
\mathbb{E}X_{1}X_{1}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is full rank with
d
distinct eigenvalues.
There exists a sequence of orthogonal matrices
Q\in\mathbb{O}_{d}
such that

\left\|\hat{X}-XQ\right\|_{{2,\infty}}=O_{P}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\log^{2}n\right).

This result lets us choose
\xi_{n}=\sqrt{d/n}\log^{2}n
in Assumption
2
.
Using Theorem 5.2 from
Lei and Rinaldo 2015
and trivially upper bounding the row sums of
XX^{\top}
by
n
, we may choose
\eta_{n}=\sqrt{n}
in Assumption
1
.
Using standard concentration inequalities
(
Vershynin 2020
)
, we may choose
\zeta_{n}=d\log n
.

In order to give these rates for weighted networks, as well as for many of our results in the sequel, we will make use of standard concentration inequalities for sub-gamma random variables
(
Boucheron et al. 2013
;
Vershynin 2020
)
.

Definition 3.2
.

A centered random variable
Y
is called
(\nu,b)
-sub-gamma for
\nu,b>0
if for all
t\in\left(-\frac{1}{b},\frac{1}{b}\right)
,

\log\mathbb{E}e^{tY}\leq\frac{t^{2}\nu}{2(1-b|t|)}.

Now, in the case of weighted networks, consider the following result, adapted from
Levin et al. 2022
.

Theorem 3.3

(
Levin et al. 2022
, Lemma 5, Theorem 6)
.

Suppose that conditional on
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, the entries of
A-XX^{\top}
are independent
(\nu,b)
-subgamma random variables.
Then

\left\|A-XX^{\top}\right\|=O_{P}\!\left(\sqrt{(\nu+b^{2})n}\log n\right).

Suppose further that

(\nu+b^{2})n\log^{2}n=o\left(\lambda_{d}^{2}(XX^{\top})\right).

Then there exists a sequence of orthogonal matrices
Q\in\mathbb{O}_{d}
such that

\left\|\hat{X}-XQ\right\|_{{2,\infty}}\leq\frac{Cd\sqrt{\nu+b^{2}}\log n}{\lambda_{d}^{1/2}(X^{\top}X)}+\frac{Cdn(\nu+b^{2})\kappa(X^{\top}X)\log^{2}n}{\lambda_{d}^{3/2}(X^{\top}X)},

where
\kappa(M)
denotes the condition number of matrix
M
.

Thus, under the setting of Theorem
3.3
, we may choose

\eta_{n}=\sqrt{\nu+b^{2}}\log n

in Assumption
1
and

\xi_{n}=\frac{Cd\sqrt{\nu+b^{2}}\log n}{\lambda_{d}^{1/2}(X^{\top}X)}+\frac{Cdn(\nu+b^{2})\kappa(X^{\top}X)\log^{2}n}{\lambda_{d}^{3/2}(X^{\top}X)}

in Assumption
2
.
Using standard concentration inequalities
(
Levin et al. 2022
, see, e.g., Proposition 22 in)
, we may take

\zeta_{n}=d\sqrt{\nu+b^{2}}\log n

in Assumption
3
.

Some of our results below make use of the following additional assumption on the covariance structure of
X
and
Z
.

Assumption 4
.

The
(X_{i},Z_{i})
pairs are drawn i.i.d. according to a distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that
(X_{1},Z_{1})\in\mathbb{R}^{d+p}
has covariance

\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{X}&\Sigma_{X,Z}\\
\Sigma_{X,Z}^{\top}&\Sigma_{Z}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+p)\times(d+p)},

(16)

with both
\Sigma_{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\Sigma_{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
invertible.

3.1
Model-Based Approaches

We begin by establishing results for a pair of network association testing methods under parametric modeling assumptions.
In particular, we consider settings where the latent positions
X
are related to the observed nodal covariates
Z
via the linear model in Equation (
2
).

3.1.1
Ridge regression

Our model in Equation (
2
) assumes that the latent positions (i.e., the rows of
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
) are related to the node-level covariates (i.e., the rows of
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
) via a linear transformation encoded by
B\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
.
Ignoring for now the fact that the latent positions are accessible only via the observed adjacency matrix
A
, this suggests regressing the latent positions against the nodal covariates.
While we might consider an estimation procedure for all
d
columns of
B
at once, we leave this matter for future work and instead consider the admittedly more naïve approach of modeling the
d
columns of
X
via
d
separate regressions:

X_{\cdot,k}=Zb^{(k)}+E_{\cdot,k}~\text{ for }~k\in[d].

(17)

Recall the ridge regression estimators
b_{2}^{(k)}
from Equation (
4
) with
r=2
.
For simplicity of notation, we denote these estimates in this section by
\hat{b}^{(k)}
.
These can be expressed as

\hat{b}^{(k)}=\left(\frac{1}{n}Z^{\top}Z+\alpha_{k}I_{p}\right)^{-1}\frac{1}{n}Z^{\top}\hat{X}_{\cdot,k},

(18)

where for each
k\in[d]
,
\alpha_{k}\geq 0
is a regularization parameter, which we allow to change for each column of
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
.
An immediate challenge arises from the fact that we do not have access to
X
, except through
A
.
In addition, as discussed in Remark
2.2
,
X
is identified only up to an orthogonal transformation
Q
.
If the true
X
were available, we could obtain the following oracle estimators for any orthogonal matrix
Q

\tilde{b}^{(k)}_{Q}=\left(\frac{1}{n}Z^{\top}Z+\alpha_{k}I_{p}\right)^{-1}\frac{1}{n}Z^{\top}(XQ)_{\cdot,k}.

(19)

The following result shows that the estimates
\hat{b}^{(k)}
based on the estimated latent positions
\hat{X}
are close to the oracle estimates
\tilde{b}^{(k)}_{Q}
, once we choose
Q
so as to account for the orthogonal non-identifiability in the latent space.

Theorem 3.4
.

Suppose that
(A,X)\sim\operatorname{RDPG}(F,n)
with the distribution
F
corresponding to the model in Equation (
17
).
In addition to Assumptions
1
and
2
, suppose that the nodal covariates
Z
are such that for all
i\in[n]
and
j\in[p]
,
Z_{ij}
is a
(\nu_{Z},b_{Z})
-subgamma random variable with
(\nu_{Z}+b_{Z}^{2})\log(p\vee n)=O(n)
.
Then there is a sequence of random orthogonal matrices
Q=Q_{n}\in\mathbb{O}_{d}
such that

\max_{k\in[d]}\left\|\hat{b}^{(k)}-\tilde{b}^{(k)}_{Q}\right\|=O_{P}\!\left(\frac{\xi_{n}}{\min_{k\in[d]}\alpha_{k}}\right).

A proof can be found in Appendix
A
.

Corollary 3.5
.

Suppose that the ridge regularization parameters
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{d}
are chosen according to

\alpha_{k}=\frac{\|b^{(k)}\|^{2}}{\gamma\sigma_{k}^{2}},

where
\gamma=\lim_{n\to\infty}p/n\in(0,\infty)
and
\sigma_{k}^{2}=\operatorname{Var}E_{1k}
.
Then, with
Q
as in Theorem
3.4
,

\max_{k\in[d]}\left\|\tilde{b}^{(k)}_{Q}-\hat{b}^{(k)}\right\|=O_{P}\!\left(\gamma\xi_{n}\max_{k\in[d]}\frac{\sigma_{k}^{2}}{\|b^{(k)}\|^{2}}\right).

Remark 3.6
.

Recall that we collected the estimates produced by Equation (
4
) with
r=2
in the columns of the matrix

\hat{B}^{\operatorname{ridge}}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{b}_{2}^{(1)}&\hat{b}_{2}^{(2)}&\cdots&\hat{b}_{2}^{(d)}\end{bmatrix}.

Analogously, we may construct for any orthogonal matrix
Q\in\mathbb{O}_{d}
, the matrix

\tilde{B}_{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{b}^{(1)}_{Q}&\tilde{b}^{(2)}_{Q}&\cdots&\tilde{b}^{(d)}_{Q}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d},

where for
k=1,2,\dots,d
, the
\tilde{b}^{(k)}_{Q}
are the oracle estimates defined in Equation (
19
).
By definition, for
\ell=1,2,\dots,p
,

\tilde{B}_{Q,\ell\cdot}=\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}Z^{\top}Z+\alpha_{k}I_{p}\right)^{-1}\right]_{\ell\cdot}\frac{1}{n}Z^{\top}XQ.

Thus, while there is a non-identifiability associated with this regression, it applies only to the basis for the latent positions
X
, and not to the node-level covariates
Z
.
This means that there is no ambiguity in which node features do or do not contribute to variation in latent positions, even if those latent positions are never observed.
For example, since
Q
is orthogonal, the Euclidean norms of the rows of
\tilde{B}_{Q}
are invariant to
Q
.
Thus, one can compare the magnitudes of rows of
\tilde{B}_{Q}
to compare the influences of different node features on network structure, even though this structure is unobserved.

3.1.2
LASSO

Analogously to our ridge regression results above, we prove a convergence rate for our group LASSO-based estimator in Equation (
5
), following the arguments and general proof structure in Chapters 6.2.2 and 8.6 of
Bühlmann and van de Geer 2011
.
The main challenge is again that we must account for the fact that we work with
\hat{X}
rather than
X
and that
\hat{X}
recovers
XQ
for some unknown random orthogonal matrix
Q
, rather than recovering
X
directly.

In the LASSO literature, estimation rates often depend on the compatibility constant.
In typical settings, one considers the covariates (
Z
in our notation) fixed and proves results conditionally on these covariates.
In such a setting, the compatibility constant is, as the name suggests, a constant.
Here, we would like to allow for randomness in
Z
, and thus our compatibility “constant” is random, similar to the situation in
Kreiss et al. 2025
.
The convergence rate of our estimator thus depends on the behavior of the
random multitask compatibility constant
.

Definition 3.7
.

Given a set of indices
S\subseteq[p]
, define
\mathcal{B}_{p,d}(S)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
to be the set of all matrices
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
with rows
\beta_{j}
such that
\sum_{j\in S^{c}}\|\beta_{j}\|\leq 3\sum_{j\in S}\|\beta_{j}\|
.
We call the random variable

\phi_{S}=\min_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}_{p,d}(S)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{nd}}\left\|Z\beta\right\|_{F}\frac{\sqrt{d|S|}}{\sum_{j\in S}\|\beta_{j}\|},

(20)

the random multitask compatibility constant.

Since our compatibility constant is random, we require what we call a
Random Multitask Compatibility Condition
for the set
S
, namely
1/\phi_{S}=O_{P}(1)
.
For a given
S
and fixed non-random
Z
,
\phi_{S}>0
is a classical condition in high-dimensional statistics.
See Section 8.6.3 of
Bühlmann and van de Geer 2011
and references therein for a discussion and comparison to other conditions.

Similarly to the classical LASSO literature, our results require tail bounds on the correlations between the noise and the covariates.
Equation (
39
) gives a precise statement of the event that has to hold with high probability.
To guarantee the existence of such tail bounds, we require some regularity conditions on the noise in the form of subgamma tail bounds
(
Boucheron et al. 2013
;
Vershynin 2020
, see)
.
We use the sub-gamma property to prove a concentration result on sums with random coefficients, which appears as Lemma
B.3
in Appendix
B
.
Using this result, we can formulate the main result of this section.

Theorem 3.8
.

Under the model in Equation (
2
), assume that
Z
and
E
are independent of one another and have i.i.d. rows.
Under Assumption
2
, suppose that for each
k\in[d]
,
E_{1k}
is
(\nu,b)
-sub-gamma and that, for each
j\in[p]
,
Z_{1j}^{2}-\mathbb{E}(Z_{1j}^{2})
is
(\nu_{Z},b_{Z})
-sub-gamma.
Suppose furthermore that
\log pd=o(n)
and
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\max_{j\in[p]}\mathbb{E}(Z_{1j}^{2})<\infty
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, there are positive constants
K_{\epsilon}
and
N_{\epsilon}
such that for

\alpha_{0,n}=K_{\epsilon}\max\left\{\frac{\xi_{n}}{\sqrt{d}},\frac{\log\max(pd,n)}{\sqrt{n}}\right\},

(21)

\alpha\geq 2\alpha_{0,n}
, and
n\geq N_{\epsilon}
, it holds with probability at least
1-5\epsilon
that

\frac{1}{nd}\left\|Z\left(\hat{B}_{\operatorname{gLASSO}}-BQ\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left\|\hat{B}_{\operatorname{gLASSO},j}-Q^{\top}B_{j}\right\|\leq\frac{4\alpha^{2}|S|}{\phi_{S}^{2}}.

(22)

Let, in addition,
\omega_{n}\to\infty
be arbitrary and
\phi_{S}^{-1}=O_{P}\!\left(1\right)
.
Then, there exists a choice of regularization parameter
\alpha
with
\alpha=O_{P}\!\left(\max\left\{\frac{\omega_{n}}{\sqrt{d}}\xi_{n},\frac{\log\max(pd,n)}{\sqrt{n}}\right\}\right)
such that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left\|\hat{B}_{\operatorname{gLASSO},j}-Q^{\top}B_{j}\right\|=O_{P}\!\left(|S|\max\left\{\frac{\omega_{n}}{\sqrt{d}}\xi_{n},\frac{\log\max(pd,n)}{\sqrt{n}}\right\}\right).

(23)

A detailed proof can be found in Appendix
B
.

Remark 3.9
.

The requirement of Theorem
3.8
that all entries of
Z
have the same tail properties is only a mild restriction compared to allowing every variable to have its own sub-gamma parameters, in light of the fact that one typically rescales the features to be of comparable orders when performing regression. Similarly, a uniform tail bound on the columns of
E
seems, in consideration of the previous discussion, reasonable to assume.

Remark 3.10
.

In the proof of Theorem
3.8
, we give precise definitions for
N_{\epsilon}
and
K_{\epsilon}
. Hence, it is possible to remove the asymptotic view point from Equation (
23
) and prove a finite sample result by replacing these definitions in (
21
) and (
22
). In fact, by investigating the interplay of all involved constants, for any given
n
, it is possible to choose
N_{\epsilon}<n
(even
N_{\epsilon}=1
is possible) at the expense of an increase in
K_{\epsilon}
. In this way, one can produce a finite sample statement that holds for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

To illustrate our above results, let us consider a concrete situation.
Suppose that
p\to\infty
and that we are in the setting of Theorem
3.1
, in which case we have
\xi_{n}=\sqrt{d/n}\log^{2}n
.
Choose furthermore
\omega_{n}=\sqrt{\log p}\to\infty
. Then, the convergence rate of Equation (
23
) in Theorem
3.8
simplifies to

\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left\|\hat{B}_{\operatorname{gLASSO},j}-Q^{\top}B_{j}\right\|=O_{P}\!\left(|S|\frac{\max\left\{\sqrt{\log p}\log^{2}n,\log(pd\vee n)\right\}}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

In typical LASSO results
(
Bühlmann and van de Geer 2011
, see, e.g., Corollary 6.2 in)
, the convergence rate in
\ell_{1}
-norm is of the order
|S|\sqrt{n^{-1}\log p}
.
The rate implied by Theorem
3.8
is slightly worse by logarithmic factors.
This is for two reasons.
First, because we observe
\hat{X}
rather than
X
, we must control the error between these two terms by ensuring that certain events hold with high probability.
Using Theorem
3.1
for this purpose yields the additional
\log^{2}n
term.
Secondly, we allow for random covariates
Z
, which we handle by a slightly different exponential inequality in Lemma
B.3
.
The union bounds needed for this argument incur the additional
\log pd
term in our rate.

Theorem
3.8
states that there is a random orthogonal transformation
Q
such that each row of
\hat{B}_{\operatorname{gLASSO}}
lies close to the corresponding row of
BQ
.
Since
Q
is unknown in practice, it is only of limited use to directly interpret the values of
\hat{B}_{\operatorname{gLASSO}}
.
If one is interested in the actual effects of the covariates, one can instead interpret
\hat{B}_{\operatorname{gLASSO}}\hat{B}_{\operatorname{gLASSO}}^{\top}
which approximates
BQ(BQ)^{\top}=BB^{\top}
, since
Q
is orthogonal.
This allows us to interpret the size of the overall effect of the covariates and their relations to each other, as discussed in Remark
3.6
.

3.2
Results for CCA-Based Approaches

Motivated by relaxing the linear model assumptions of the regression-based methods above, we now present two methods based on CCA.
These methods aim to detect correlation between the node-level features and network structure as encoded in the latent positions without assuming anything about how
X
is generated from
Z
(or vice versa).
One way to assess whether network structure is correlated with node-level features
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is to consider the CCA alignment between
Z
and the latent positions of the network.
That is, we look at the ordered singular values of

\operatorname{CCA}(Z,X)=\widetilde{\Sigma}_{X}^{-1/2}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{X,Z}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z}^{-1/2},

(24)

where
M_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the centering matrix defined in Equation (
9
),
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{X,Z}
is as defined in Equation (
11
), and
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
are as defined in Equation (
10
).

In particular, we have the associated CCA coefficient

\rho_{X,Z}=\left\|\operatorname{CCA}(Z,X)\right\|.

(25)

Remark 3.11
.

We note that the definition of the CCA coefficient defined in Equation (
25
) relies on covariance estimates
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{X},\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z}
and
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{X,Z}
.
In the high-dimensional regime where
p\rightarrow\infty
, as considered in Sections
3.1.1
and
3.1.2
, the latter two estimates will be unstable in the absence of regularization of some form.
The simplest approach to this is to replace, for example,
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z}
with a regularized version

\frac{1}{n}Z^{\top}M_{n}Z+\gamma I

for some small
\gamma>0
.
Of course, high-dimensional covariance estimation is a well-studied problem, and far more sophisticated methods have been proposed
(see, e.g.,
Bickel and Levina 2008
;
Fisher and Sun 2011
;
Ledoit and Wolf 2012
;
Touloumis 2015
;
Yao et al. 2015
, and references therein)
.
In what follows, for the sake of space and simplicity, we restrict our attention to the case where both
d
and
p
are fixed with respect to
n
.
We anticipate that the results in the sequel can be extended to the regime where
p
grows with
n
by replacing
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z}
with a suitably-chosen regularized estimate of
\Sigma_{Z}
from this extensive literature.
In our high-dimensional experiments presented in Section
4
, we use the regularization method from
Fisher and Sun 2011
.

As discussed previously, in practice, we do not have access to the true latent positions
X
, but rather must estimate them (up to some rotational non-identifiability) as
\hat{X}
.
Substituting these estimates for the true latent positions in Equation (
24
), we consider the leading singular values of

\operatorname{CCA}(\hat{X},Z)=\left(\frac{1}{n}\hat{X}^{\top}M_{n}\hat{X}\right)^{-1/2}\frac{\hat{X}^{\top}M_{n}Z}{n}~\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z}^{-1/2}.

(26)

The following result establishes that CCA applied to the estimated latent positions and the node-level features correctly estimates the correlation between the node features and the latent network structure.
A detailed proof is given in Appendix
D
.

In what follows, we assume that the latent positions
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}
are drawn i.i.d. with a non-singular covariance matrix
\Sigma_{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
We note that this assumption is with minimal loss of generality: if
\Sigma_{X}
has rank
d^{\prime}<d
, we may make a suitable change of basis and restrict our attention to a
d^{\prime}
-dimensional subspace, yielding
d^{\prime}
-dimensional latent positions with an invertible covariance matrix.

Theorem 3.12
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
,
2

and
4
hold,
and suppose further that the quantity
\xi_{n}
in Assumption
2
obeys
\xi_{n}=o(1)
.
Then

\rho_{\hat{X},Z}=\left\|\operatorname{CCA}(\hat{X},Z)\right\|

satisfies

\left|\rho_{\hat{X},Z}-\rho_{X,Z}\right|=o_{P}\!\left(1\right).

Following the intuitive idea of performing CCA between the node-level features of the network with the latent positions, it is natural to consider CCA alignment of the node covariates and the adjacency matrix itself.
That is, we look at the ordered singular values of

\operatorname{CCA}(A,Z)=\widetilde{\Sigma}_{A}^{-1/2}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{A,Z}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z}^{-1/2},

where
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z}
is as defined in Equation (
10
) and
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{A}
is defined analogously as

\widetilde{\Sigma}_{A}=\frac{1}{n}AM_{n}A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},

(27)

and, analogously to Equation (
11
),

\widetilde{\Sigma}_{A,Z}=\frac{1}{n}AM_{n}Z.

(28)

Unfortunately, doing this naïvely will result in a trivial CCA alignment score close to
1
, owing to the fact that
A
is typically near full-rank.
To account for this problem, we regularize the eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{A}
, and consider the regularized full-network CCA matrix

\operatorname{CCA}_{\gamma}(A,Z)=\left(\widetilde{\Sigma}_{A}+\gamma I\right)^{-1/2}\frac{1}{n}AM_{n}Z\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z}^{-1/2},

(29)

where
\gamma>0
.
This yields a corresponding CCA coefficient

\rho_{A,Z}^{(\gamma)}=\left\|\operatorname{CCA}_{\gamma}(A,Z)\right\|.

(30)

As in Theorem
3.12
above, under suitable conditions, this (regularized) full-network CCA method also correctly detects the presence or absence of correlation between the node-level features and network structure.
A detailed proof is given in Appendix
E
.

Theorem 3.13
.

Under Assumption
4
,
suppose that Assumption
1
holds with

\eta_{n}=o(n)

(31)

and Assumption
3
holds with

\zeta_{n}=o(n).

(32)

Let
\rho_{A,Z}^{(\gamma)}
be as in Equation (
30
) and let
\rho_{X,Z}
as in Equation (
25
).
Then, provided that the regularization parameter
\gamma
is positive and obeys
\gamma=O(n)
,

\left|\rho_{A,Z}^{(\gamma)}-\rho_{X,Z}\right|=O_{P}\!\left(\frac{\eta_{n}}{\sqrt{\gamma n}}+\frac{|\gamma|}{n}+\frac{\zeta_{n}}{n^{3/2}}\right).

Corollary 3.14
.

Under the setting of Theorem
3.13
, suppose that

\eta_{n}^{2}/n\ll\gamma\ll n
.
Then

\left|\rho_{A,Z}^{(\gamma)}-\rho_{X,Z}\right|=o_{P}\!\left(1\right).

Remark 3.15
.

It is tempting to optimize the rate in Theorem
3.13
with respect to the regularization parameter
\gamma=\gamma_{n}\geq 0
by choosing
\gamma=C\eta_{n}^{2/3}n^{1/3}
.
Under the typical setting (see Theorems
3.1
and
3.3
above),
\zeta_{n}
and
\eta_{n}/\sqrt{n}
are both of at most polylogarithmic order and so a natural option is to ignore the logarithmic factors and choose the (approximately) optimal
\gamma=Cn^{2/3}
.
We note, however, that
\eta_{n}^{2}/n\ll\gamma\ll n
is sufficient to ensure consistency of the regularized full-network CCA method, in the sense that

\left|\rho_{A,Z}^{(\gamma)}-\rho_{X,Z}\right|=o_{P}\!\left(1\right).

In our experiments in Section
4
, we take
\gamma=\sqrt{n}
throughout.

4
Experiments

In this section, we evaluate our proposed methods on a range of data, both simulated and from real-world sources.
Since, in applying all of the methods presented above, our ultimate goal is to assess whether or not the latent positions
X
are associated to the node covariates
Z
, we describe in Section
4.1
a permutation test to formally test for such association.
In the following Section
4.2
, we begin by assessing our methods on simulated data.
Our focus lies on investigating the effects of model choice and of the strength and structure of correlation between the node covariates and latent positions on the permutation tests.
In Section
4.3
, we apply our methods to a pair of network data sets collected in the real world.

4.1
Permutation Tests

Our aim is to formally test the null-hypothesis
no association between
X
and
Z
. In our model-based methods described in Section
2.1
, this corresponds to testing the hypothesis

H_{0}:B=0.

In the case of our CCA-based methods described in Section
2.2
, this corresponds to testing

H_{0}:\mathbb{E}~\Sigma_{X}^{-1/2}(X_{1}\!-\!\mathbb{E}X_{1})^{\top}(Z_{1}\!-\!\mathbb{E}Z_{1})\Sigma^{-1/2}_{Z}=0,

where
\Sigma_{X}
and
\Sigma_{Z}
are the population covariances of
X_{1}
and
Z_{1}
, respectively.
In all cases, we use permutation testing.
That is, for a given choice of test statistic

T_{n}=T(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n};Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots,Z_{n}),

we repeatedly randomly permute the rows of
Z
to obtain replicates of the form

T\left(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n};Z_{\pi(1)},Z_{\pi(2)},\dots,Z_{\pi(n)}\right),

and compare these replicates to our original observed statistic.

LASSO-based methods

For both regular and group LASSO, we choose
T_{n}
to be the
covariance test
introduced by
Lockhart et al. 2014
.
See Appendix
F
for further details.
In the case of the regular LASSO, we separately test for association of each latent dimension
k\in[d]
with the covariates and apply a Bonferroni correction to account for multiple testing.

For the group LASSO,
Lockhart et al. 2014
does not provide a specific formula for
T_{n}
.
Therefore, we derive in Appendix
F
a formula for the covariance test statistic for the group LASSO in our case (or, more generally, in the case of multivariate regression; see Example 4.2 in
Hastie et al. 2015
for a brief introduction).
These results may be of independent interest.
Note that using group LASSO avoids the multiple testing issues encountered in the regular LASSO.

Ridge Regression

For ridge regression, the covariance test statistic used above cannot be directly generalized.
Instead, we take
T_{n}
simply to be the
L^{2}
-norm of the estimate obtained when the tuning parameter is chosen via cross-validation.
Since each repetition of the permutation step requires running cross-validation again, computations are relatively slow for this statistic.

CCA based methods

For the CCA-based methods, we use the CCA value as a test statistic.
That is, our test statistic is

T(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n};Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots,Z_{n})=\left\|\frac{1}{n}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{X}^{-1/2}X^{\top}M_{n}Z\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z}^{-1/2}\right\|.

In contrast to the penalized methods above, CCA has the advantage that we do not have to choose a tuning parameter. Therefore, the computations are comparatively fast and the permutation test can be computed efficiently.

4.2
Simulations

In this section, we evaluate our proposed methods empirically in six quite different scenarios that are designed to illustrate the advantages and shortcomings of our methods.
Four of these scenarios follow the model in Equation (
2
).
The remaining two scenarios follow a different structure.

4.2.1
Data Generating Processes

The data generating processes of the first four scenarios all have the following structure:
We specify a covariance matrix
\Sigma_{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and generate random node covariates according to

Z_{i}\overset{\text{ind.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{Z})~\text{ for }~i\in[n].

We generate a matrix
E\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
according to

E=[E_{ij}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}~\text{ where }~E_{ij}\overset{\text{ind.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,2)~\text{ for }~i\in[n]\text{ and }j\in[d].

We then specify a matrix of coefficients
B\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
and construct latent positions and an adjacency matrix according to

X=sZB+E,\qquad A=XX^{\top}+N,

where
s\geq 0
corresponds to a signal strength parameter and

N_{ij}\overset{\text{ind.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}\left(0,(s^{2}+1)^{2}\right)~\text{ for }~1\leq i\leq j\leq n.

Thus, our first four scenarios will all follow the model in Equation (
2
), differing only in the choice of coefficients
B\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
, node feature covariance matrix
\Sigma_{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and signal strength
s\geq 0
.
By varying
s\geq 0
, we change the distance from the base hypothesis

H_{0}:\text{No covariate impact on latent positions},

which is true when
s=0
.
In Remark
4.1
below, we discuss in more detail how
s
measures this deviation in terms of signal to noise ratio (SNR).

We describe now the first four data generation scenarios.
Note that the random steps below are only executed once in our experiments.
Afterwards, the chosen values remain fixed for all replicates.
Moreover, after generating the matrix
B
, we rescale its columns such that
\|B_{\cdot r}\|_{2}\in\{0,1\}
for all
r=1,2,\dots,d
.

(i)

No Sparsity.

\Sigma_{Z}:=\begin{bmatrix}1&q&\dots&q&q\\
q&1&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&1&q\\
q&q&\dots&q&1\end{bmatrix},\quad q=\frac{1}{p-1},\quad B_{kr}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{1}{k},\frac{1}{9k^{2}}\right).

The different entries of
B
are assigned independently.

(ii)

Entry-wise sparsity.

\Sigma_{Z}
as in (i) and
B_{kr}=0
except for
\lfloor(\log n)/2d\rfloor
randomly selected entries, which are independently distributed as
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

(iii)

Row-wise sparsity.

\Sigma_{Z}
as in (i) and
B_{kr}=0
except for
\lfloor(\log n)/2\rfloor
randomly selected rows. For each selected
k\in[p]
, we generate
B_{kr}\overset{\text{ind.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
independently for
r\in[d]
.

(iv)

High correlation.

B
is generated as in (iii) and

\Sigma_{Z}:=\sigma_{0}^{2}\begin{bmatrix}1&q&q^{2}&\dots&q^{p-1}\\
q&1&q&\dots&q^{p-2}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
q^{p-2}&\dots&q&1&q\\
q^{p-1}&q^{p-2}&\dots&q&1\end{bmatrix},\,q=0.95,

with
\sigma_{0}

2
.

Remark 4.1
.

Note that, by construction, the largest eigenvalue of
\Sigma_{Z}
equals
2
in all four cases described above.
For any column
B_{\cdot r}
of
B
, we hence have

\mathbb{E}\left(\left|Z_{i}^{\top}B_{\cdot r}s\right|^{2}\right)=s^{2}B_{\cdot r}^{\top}\Sigma_{Z}B_{\cdot r}\leq 2s^{2},

since the columns
B_{\cdot r}
are standardized.
Since
E_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,2)
, we have a signal-to-noise-ratio (SNR) of at most
s^{2}
in the regression of
X
on
Z
.
Thus,
s\geq 0
quantifies the level of the impact of the covariates on the latent positions as desired.
However, an undesired consequence of this is that the latent positions
X
are more dispersed on average. Thus, the edge-level noise
N
becomes less important. To counteract this effect, we increase the strength of the noise accordingly. To argue that the edge noise
N
has exactly the right strength, we first compute the strength of the signal
X
to be

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\|X\|_{F}^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\operatorname{trace}XX^{\top}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}X_{i}^{\top}X_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}(Z_{i}^{\top}Bs+E_{i\cdot})(Z_{i}^{\top}Bs+E_{i\cdot})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left(s^{2}Z_{i}^{\top}BB^{\top}Z_{i}+E_{i\cdot}E_{i\cdot}^{\top}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s^{2}\operatorname{trace}B^{\top}\left(\mathbb{E}Z_{i}Z_{i}^{\top}\right)B+2dn

\displaystyle=ns^{2}\underbrace{\operatorname{trace}B^{\top}\Sigma_{Z}B}_{\leq 2d}+2dn\leq 2dn(s^{2}+1).

On the other hand, the noise level is given by
\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\|N\|_{F}^{2}}=n(s^{2}+1)
, because the noise is applied to
XX^{\top}
.
Hence, for our choices, the SNR in the regression of
A
on
X
is bounded by
2d
, which is independent of
s
.
Thus, the variance of the edge noise
N
is always of the same order as the variation of the latent positions, regardless of our choice of
s
. In this sense,
s
affects the importance of the covariates on the latent positions, but does not effect the SNR in the regression of
A
on
X
.

.

(v)

Network

.

Similarly to Remark

, the SNR in the regression of

Finally, we consider a scenario that is completely different from the model in Equation (

(vi)

5
Discussion and Conclusion

In this paper, we have considered the problem of high-dimensional network association testing, in which we aim to understand whether observed network structure is associated with (potentially high-dimensional) vertex features.
To this end, we considered a model in which the network is generated via an RDPG, and we suggest two types of methods: model-based and model-free methods.
The model-based methods assume that the latent positions of the RDPG are linearly related to the vertex features.
We then estimate this relation using ridge, LASSO and group LASSO regression.
In the model-free approach, we are agnostic as to the relation between latent positions and features, and instead we aim to estimate potential relations between them using CCA between features and either latent positions or rows of the adjacency matrix.
All of our proposed methods operate via permutation tests, and we have applied them to both simulated and real world data in both low- and high-dimensional settings.
Our theoretical and empirical results suggest that when a linear model relating features to network structure is correct, our model-based methods perform quite well, while our CCA-based methods perform better when these linearity assumptions are violated.

The five methods that we have suggested come with different advantages and disadvantages. The main restriction for the model-based methods is that they rely on a linear model. However, in that case, we are able to prove performance guarantees in the form of an oracle inequality for the ridge regression and a convergence rate for the group LASSO, even in the high-dimensional case.
We relaxed the linearity assumption by using CCA to test for network association. These methods all require an initial estimate of the latent positions which comes with its own challenges, e.g., selecting the latent dimension. Therefore, we finally presented a network CCA method that performs CCA alignment between the adjacency matrix and network features. For these two methods, we have shown consistency in the low-dimensional regime.
Our empirical results show that their performance remains good in high-dimensional settings, provided one regularizes appropriately.

This research can be extended in several interesting directions.
Our focus here was correlation, that is, linear dependence.
A natural extension is to consider non-linear dependence, i.e., to test for dependence as opposed to correlation.
Distance correlation
(
Lee et al. 2019
)
actually tests for independence, but we saw in our empirical results that it is computationally expensive compared to our proposed methods.
One possible next step would be to study inference, e.g., using de-biasing or post-LASSO in our LASSO-based methods. Similarly, we have not discussed variable screening, i.e., selecting the most relevant features with high-probability. Likely, under a so-called beta-min condition
(see, e.g.,
Bühlmann and van de Geer 2011
, Section 7.7)
, the LASSO theory can be extended to such results, but it is unclear how the estimated latent positions affect its behavior in this regard.

In another direction, it would be interesting to extend these methods to multiple network observations. This could either consider repeated independent realisations of the same network, or the observation of several networks in which the exact relation to the covariates is different in each network, but, for example, the identity of relevant covariates is the same across all networks. Lastly, it is worthwhile to consider Remark
2.1
, i.e., settings in which the covariates derive from the network itself rather than latent positions.
This setting promises a more general set of tools, but will doubtless require more careful bookkeeping and analysis than that undertaken here.

\frac{2}{nd}\left\|Z\left(\hat{B}-BQ\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j\in S^{c}}\|\hat{B}_{j}\|\leq\frac{3\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j\in S}\|\hat{B}_{j}-Q^{\top}B_{j}\|,

(40)

where
Q\in\mathbb{O}_{d}
is the orthogonal matrix guaranteed by Assumption
2

Proof.

The proof follows that of Lemma 6.3 in
Bühlmann and van de Geer 2011
.
Recall our notation that
\beta_{j}
denotes the
j
-th row of a matrix
\beta
written as a column vector and recall
\Delta=\hat{X}-XQ
from Equation (
38

For any matrix
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
, by cyclicity of the trace, the triangle inequality, and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{2}{nd}\operatorname{trace}\left(Z\beta\left(EQ+\Delta\right)^{\top}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{2}{nd}\operatorname{trace}\left(\beta\left(EQ+\Delta\right)^{\top}Z\right)\right|\leq\frac{2}{nd}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left|\beta_{j}^{\top}\left(EQ+\Delta\right)^{\top}Z_{\cdot j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{nd}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\|\beta_{j}\|~\left\|\left(EQ+\Delta\right)^{\top}Z_{\cdot j}\right\|.

On the event
\mathcal{T}_{n}

\left|\frac{2}{nd}\operatorname{trace}\left(Z\beta\left(EQ+\Delta\right)^{\top}\right)\right|\leq\frac{\alpha_{0,n}}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\|\beta_{j}\|.

Taking
\beta=BQ-\hat{B}

\displaystyle\frac{2}{nd}\left\|Z\left(BQ-\hat{B}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{2\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\|\hat{B}_{j}\|

(41)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\|\hat{B}_{j}-Q^{\top}B_{j}\|+\frac{2\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\|B_{j}\|.

Since
B_{j}=0
for all

, we have

\sum_{j=1}^{p}\|\hat{B}_{j}-Q^{\top}B_{j}\|=\sum_{j\in S}\|\hat{B}_{j}-Q^{\top}B_{j}\|+\sum_{j\in S^{c}}\|\hat{B}_{j}\|.

Applying this to Equation (
41

B_{j}=0
for
j\in S^{c}

\displaystyle\frac{2}{nd}\left\|Z\left(BQ-\hat{B}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{2\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\|\hat{B}_{j}\|

(42)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j\in S}\|\hat{B}_{j}-Q^{\top}B_{j}\|+\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j\in S^{c}}\|\hat{B}_{j}\|+\frac{2\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j\in S}\|B_{j}\|.

Applying the triangle inequality and using the fact that
Q
is an orthogonal matrix,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}\|\hat{B}_{j}\|

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j\in S}\left(\|Q^{\top}B_{j}\|-\|\hat{B}_{j}-Q^{\top}B_{j}\|\right)+\sum_{j\in S^{c}}\|\hat{B}_{j}\|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in S}\left(\|B_{j}\|-\|\hat{B}_{j}-Q^{\top}B_{j}\|\right)+\sum_{j\in S^{c}}\|\hat{B}_{j}\|.

Using this to lower-bound the left-hand side of Equation (

\displaystyle\frac{2}{nd}

\displaystyle\left\|Z\left(BQ-\hat{B}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j\in S^{c}}\|\hat{B}_{j}\|\leq\frac{3\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j\in S}\|\hat{B}_{j}-Q^{\top}B_{j}\|,

as we set out to show.

The following is a basic concentration inequality
[
Boucheron et al. 2013
,
Vershynin 2020
, see, e.g.,]
.
We include a proof here for the sake of completeness.

Lemma B.3
.

Let
(Z_{i},\epsilon_{i})_{i\in[n]}
be a sequence of independent, identically distributed random variables with
Z_{i}
and
\epsilon_{i}
independent for each
i\in[n]
.
Suppose that the
\epsilon_{i}
are centered
(\nu_{\epsilon},b_{\epsilon})
-subgamma random variables and that
Z_{i}^{2}-\mathbb{E}(Z_{1}^{2})
is
(\nu_{Z},b_{Z})
-sub-gamma for all
i\in[n]
.
Then, for any
c_{1},c_{2},t>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left[\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\epsilon_{i}\right|>\!\sqrt{2\left(\sqrt{2\nu_{Z}c_{1}}+b_{Z}c_{1}+\mathbb{E}Z_{1}^{2}\right)\nu_{\epsilon}t}+b_{\epsilon}t\sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{2\nu_{Z}c_{2}n}+b_{Z}c_{2}n+\mathbb{E}Z_{1}^{2}}{n}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-t}+e^{-c_{1}n}+ne^{-c_{2}n}.

Proof.

For ease of notation, let
E_{0}
denote the event that

\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\epsilon_{i}\right|\leq\sqrt{2\left(\sqrt{2\nu_{Z}c_{1}}+b_{Z}c_{1}+\mathbb{E}Z_{1}^{2}\right)\nu_{\epsilon}t}+b_{\epsilon}t\sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{2\nu_{Z}c_{2}n}+b_{Z}c_{2}n+\mathbb{E}Z_{1}^{2}}{n}}

(43)

and write
Z:=(Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots,Z_{n})
.

We first note that conditionally on
Z_{i}
,
Z_{i}\epsilon_{i}
is a
(Z_{i}^{2}\nu_{\epsilon},|Z_{i}|b_{\epsilon})
-sub-gamma random variable.
Thus, our independence assumptions imply that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\epsilon_{i}
is,
conditional on
Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots,Z_{n}
, a sub-gamma random variable with parameters

\left(\nu_{\star},b_{\star}\right)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}^{2}\nu_{\epsilon},~b_{\epsilon}\max_{i\in[n]}|Z_{i}|\right).

By standard concentration inequalities
[see
Boucheron et al. 2013
, Section 2.4]
, for any
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\epsilon_{i}\right|>\sqrt{\frac{2\nu_{\epsilon}t}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}^{2}}+\frac{b_{\epsilon}t\max_{i\in[n]}|Z_{i}|}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigg|Z\right]\leq e^{-t}.

(44)

Define the events

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}^{2}\leq\sqrt{2\nu_{Z}c_{1}}+b_{Z}c_{1}+\mathbb{E}Z_{1}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\text{ and }~E_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{\max_{i\in[n]}|Z_{i}|^{2}\leq\sqrt{2\nu_{Z}c_{2}n}+b_{Z}c_{2}n+\mathbb{E}Z_{1}^{2}\right\},

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are positive constants.
We have

\mathbb{P}\left[E_{0}^{c}\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[E_{0}^{c},E_{1}\cap E_{2}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[E_{1}^{c}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[E_{1}^{c}\right].

(45)

Since since the
Z_{i}^{2}-\mathbb{E}Z_{1}^{2}
are
(\nu_{Z},b_{Z})
-sub-gamma by assumption,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(Z_{i}^{2}-\mathbb{E}Z_{1}^{2}\right)
is
(n\nu_{Z},b_{Z})
-sub-gamma, and thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[E_{1}^{c}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(Z_{i}^{2}-\mathbb{E}Z_{1}^{2}\right)>\sqrt{2n\nu_{Z}\cdot c_{1}n}+b_{Z}c_{1}n\right]\leq e^{-c_{1}n}.

Similarly, by the union bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[E_{2}^{c}\right]

\displaystyle\leq n\mathbb{P}\left[|Z_{i}|^{2}\!-\!\mathbb{E}Z_{1}^{2}>\sqrt{2\nu_{Z}c_{2}n}+b_{Z}c_{2}n\right]\leq ne^{-c_{2}n}.

(46)

Applying the above two displays to Equation (
45
),

\mathbb{P}\left[E_{0}^{c}\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[E_{0}^{c},E_{1}\cap E_{2}\right]+e^{-c_{1}n}+ne^{-c_{2}n}.

(47)

Note that on the event
E_{1}\cap E_{2}
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2\nu_{\epsilon}t}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}^{2}}+\frac{b_{\epsilon}t\max_{i\in[n]}|Z_{i}|}{\sqrt{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\nu_{\epsilon}t}\sqrt{\sqrt{2\nu_{Z}c_{1}}+b_{Z}c_{1}+\mathbb{E}Z_{1}^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{b_{\epsilon}t}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\sqrt{2\nu_{Z}c_{2}n}+b_{Z}c_{2}n+\mathbb{E}Z_{1}^{2}},

from which Equation (
44
) and the definition of event
E_{0}
in Equation (
43
) imply

\mathbb{P}\left[E_{0}^{c},E_{1}\cap E_{2}\right]\leq e^{-t}.

Applying this bound to Equation (
47
) completes the proof.
∎

The following lemma is an adaptation of Lemma A.1 in
Tsybakov 2009
, which holds under general settings for
L_{2}
functions.
To provide a self-contained exposition, we state and prove a simpler version for vectors.

Lemma B.4
.

Let
d,n\in\mathbb{N}
and and suppose that
a_{i,k}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
i\in[n]
and
k\in[d]
.
Then it holds that

\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,k}\right)^{2}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{i,k}^{2}}.

Proof.

The proof is a simplified version of Lemma A.1 of
Tsybakov 2009
.
For each
k\in[d]
, define

b_{k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,k},

and let
b=(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{d})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\|b\|^{2}=\langle b,b\rangle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{d}|b_{k}|\sum_{i=1}^{n}|a_{i,k}|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{d}|b_{k}||a_{i,k}|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|b\|\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{d}|a_{i,k}|^{2}}.

Dividing by
\|b\|
and recalling the definition of
b_{k}
yields

\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,k}\right)^{2}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{d}|a_{i,k}|^{2}},

as we set out to prove.
∎

Lemma C.1

∎

Lemma C.2
.

Under Assumption
4
, with
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{X}
as defined in Equation (

\left\|\widetilde{\Sigma}_{X}-\Sigma_{X}\right\|=o_{P}\!\left(1\right).

(56)

Further, suppose that

holds with

from Equation (

Lemma C.3

Appendix D
Proof of Theorem

Proof of Theorem

.

By definition,
\rho_{\hat{X},Z}
and

are the leading singular values of, respectively,

\operatorname{CCA}(\hat{X},Z)~\text{ and }~\operatorname{CCA}(X,Z).

Since singular values are invariant to orthogonal transformation, it will suffice for us to bound

\left\|Q^{\top}\operatorname{CCA}(\hat{X},Z)-\operatorname{CCA}(X,Z)\right\|

for some choice of
Q\in\mathbb{O}_{d}
to be specified below.

Applying the singular value decomposition,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}M_{n}X=WRV^{\top},

(62)

where
W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
has orthonormal columns,
R=\operatorname{diag}(r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{d})
for
r_{1}\geq r_{2}\geq\cdots r_{d}\geq 0
, and
V\in\mathbb{O}_{d}
.
Analogously, write

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}M_{n}\hat{X}=\hat{W}\hat{R}\hat{V}^{\top},

(63)

where
\hat{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
,
\hat{V}\in\mathbb{O}_{d}
and
\hat{R}=\operatorname{diag}(\hat{r}_{1},\hat{r}_{2},\dots,\hat{r}_{d})

\hat{r}_{1}\geq\hat{r}_{2}\geq\cdots\hat{r}_{d}\geq 0
.
We note that by Lemma
C.2
, along with the fact that
\|\tilde{\Sigma}_{XQ_{X}}\|=\|\tilde{\Sigma}_{X}\|
for
Q_{X}=Q
from Assumption
2
and our assumption that
\Sigma_{X}
is invertible, the diagonal entries of both
\hat{R}
and
R
converge to non-zero constants.
Thus, recalling the definitions from Equations (
24
) and (
26
) and recalling that

is idempotent, we have

\displaystyle Q^{\top}\operatorname{CCA}(\hat{X},Z)

\displaystyle-\operatorname{CCA}(X,Z)

\displaystyle=\left[Q^{\top}\left(\frac{\hat{X}^{\top}M_{n}\hat{X}}{n}\right)^{-1/2}\frac{\hat{X}^{\top}M_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}-\left(\frac{X^{\top}M_{n}X}{n}\right)^{-1/2}\frac{X^{\top}M_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right]\frac{M_{n}Z}{\sqrt{n}}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z}^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\left[Q^{\top}\hat{V}\hat{R}^{-1}\hat{V}^{\top}\hat{V}\hat{R}\hat{W}^{\top}-VR^{-1}V^{\top}VRW^{\top}\right]\frac{M_{n}Z}{\sqrt{n}}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z}^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\left(Q^{\top}\hat{V}\hat{W}^{\top}-VW^{\top}\right)\frac{M_{n}Z}{\sqrt{n}}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z}^{-1/2}.

By construction,

\left\|\frac{M_{n}Z}{\sqrt{n}}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z}^{-1/2}\right\|\leq 1,

and thus submultiplicativity of the norm implies

\left\|Q^{\top}\operatorname{CCA}(\hat{X},Z)-\operatorname{CCA}(X,Z)\right\|\leq\left\|Q^{\top}\hat{V}\hat{W}^{\top}-VW^{\top}\right\|.

(64)

Let

be the matrix satisfying

By Lemma 1 in

\left\|\hat{W}O_{W}-W\right\|\leq\sqrt{2}\left\|\sin\Theta(\hat{W},W)\right\|,

(65)

where

\hat{W}
and
W

We note that the diagonal entries of
\sin\Theta(\hat{W},W)
are precisely the non-zero singular values of
(I-\hat{W}\hat{W}^{\top})W

[
Bhatia 1997

.

Taking

in Equation (
64

Q_{W}=VO_{W}^{\top}\hat{V}^{\top},

we then have

\left\|Q_{W}^{\top}\operatorname{CCA}(\hat{X},Z)-\operatorname{CCA}(X,Z)\right\|\leq\left\|V\left(\hat{W}O_{W}\right)^{\top}-VW^{\top}\right\|\leq\sqrt{2}\left\|\sin\Theta(\hat{W},W)\right\|,

(66)

where the second inequality follows from unitary invariance of the norm and Equation (
65
)

Observe that since
M_{n}
is idempotent,
\hat{W}
and
W
also span the leading rank-
d
eigenspaces of, respectively,

\frac{1}{n}M_{n}\hat{X}\hat{X}^{\top}M_{n}=\hat{W}\hat{R}^{2}\hat{W}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}~\text{ and }~\frac{1}{n}M_{n}XX^{\top}M_{n}=WR^{2}W^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}.

Thus, Theorem 2 from
Yu et al. 2014
implies

\left\|\sin\Theta(\hat{W},W)\right\|\leq\frac{\sqrt{d}}{r_{d}^{2}}\left\|\frac{1}{n}M_{n}\hat{X}\hat{X}^{\top}M_{n}-\frac{1}{n}M_{n}XX^{\top}M_{n}\right\|\leq\frac{\sqrt{d}}{nr_{d}^{2}}\left\|\hat{X}\hat{X}^{\top}-XX^{\top}\right\|,

where the second inequality follows from submultiplicativity of the norm and the fact that
M_{n}
is idempotent.
Let
Q_{X}\in\mathbb{O}_{d}
denote the matrix guaranteed by Assumption
2

Adding and subtracting appropriate quantities and applying the triangle inequality,

\left\|\sin\Theta(\hat{W},W)\right\|\leq\frac{\sqrt{d}}{nr_{d}^{2}}\left\|\hat{X}Q_{X}Q_{X}^{\top}\hat{X}^{\top}\!-\!XX^{\top}\right\|\leq\frac{\sqrt{d}}{nr_{d}^{2}}\left[2\|\hat{X}Q_{X}\!-\!X\|\|X\|+\left\|\hat{X}Q_{X}\!-\!X\right\|^{2}\right].

Applying the trivial upper bound

\|\hat{X}Q_{X}-X\|\leq\sqrt{n}\left\|\hat{X}Q_{X}-X\right\|_{{2,\infty}}

,

\left\|\sin\Theta(\hat{W},W)\right\|\leq\frac{\sqrt{d}}{r_{d}^{2}}\left[2\left\|\hat{X}Q_{X}-X\right\|_{{2,\infty}}+\left\|\hat{X}Q_{X}-X\right\|^{2}_{{2,\infty}}\right].

Using Assumption

\left\|\sin\Theta(\hat{W},W)\right\|\leq\frac{\sqrt{d}}{r_{d}^{2}}\left(2\xi_{n}+\xi_{n}^{2}\right).

Plugging this back into Equation (

\left\|Q_{W}^{\top}\operatorname{CCA}(\hat{X},Z)-\operatorname{CCA}(X,Z)\right\|\leq\frac{C}{r_{d}^{2}}\left(\xi_{n}+\xi_{n}^{2}\right),

where we have used our assumption that

is constant.
The result now follows from the fact that, since
\Sigma_{X}
is invertible by assumption,

r_{d}^{2}=\lambda_{d}\left(\frac{1}{n}X^{\top}M_{n}X\right)\rightarrow\lambda_{d}\left(\Sigma_{X}\right)>0,

in combination with our assumption that
\xi_{n}=o(1)
.
∎

\left\|\hat{W}_{\perp}^{\top}\widetilde{W}_{\parallel}\right\|=\left\|\sin\Theta(\hat{W}_{\parallel},\widetilde{W}_{\parallel})\right\|=O_{P}\!\left(\frac{\eta_{n}}{n}\right).

(109)

Applying the triangle inequality to Equation (

\displaystyle\left\|\left[\left(\frac{AM_{n}A}{n}+\gamma I\right)^{-1/2}-\left(\frac{PM_{n}P}{n}\right)^{-1/2}\right]\frac{PM_{n}Z}{n}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{Z}^{-1/2}\right\|

Appendix F
Covariance Test for group LASSO

The general idea of the covariance test in the case of the regular LASSO is to compute, for each
k\in[d]
, the covariance of
\hat{X}_{\cdot k}
with the predicted values
Z\hat{B}_{\textrm{rLASSO},k}(\alpha)
, where
\hat{B}_{\textrm{rLASSO},k}(\alpha)
is the solution to the optimization problem in Equation (
4
) with tuning parameter
\alpha>0
chosen suitably.
If the covariates have no impact, this covariance is likely small.

Lockhart et al. 2014
argue that a good choice of
\alpha
is the lowest value for which only one covariate is selected. This value of
\alpha
can be computed and therefore no computationally intensive cross-validation is required.

In the following, we briefly discuss how to generalize the covariance test from
Lockhart et al. 2014
to
multivariate regression
, in the case where the goal is to test the hypothesis
H_{0}:\beta=0
.
More precisely, we consider

\hat{\beta}(\alpha)=\underset{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\frac{1}{nd}\left\|\hat{X}-Z\beta\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\|\beta_{j}\|,

(111)

where
\beta_{j}
denotes the
j
-th row of
\beta
. This problem is exactly as introduced in Section
2.1
, but we emphasize that the
multivariate regression
settting as above might also be of independent interest.

In this setting, the covariance test statistic is is given by

T_{\textrm{cov}}=\frac{1}{\hat{\sigma}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\hat{X}_{ik}\left[Z\hat{\beta}(\alpha_{2})\right]_{ik},

(112)

where
\alpha_{2}>0
is the smallest value of
\alpha
at which
\hat{\beta}_{j}(\alpha)\neq 0
for exactly one
j\in[p]
and
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
is an estimator for the error variance (see Remark
F.4
below).
We fix further notations, which are chosen for easy comparison with the results of
Lockhart et al. 2014
for the standard LASSO.
We denote by
\alpha_{1}
the smallest value of
\alpha
for which
\hat{\beta}_{j}(\alpha)=0
for all
j\in[p]
.
Let furthermore

U_{k}:=\frac{1}{n}\hat{X}^{\top}Z_{\cdot k},\quad R_{\ell,k}:=\frac{1}{n}[Z^{\top}Z]_{\ell,k}\text{ for }\ell,k\in[p].

To use the test statistic
T_{\textrm{cov}}
that was introduced in Equation (
112
), we have to provide a formula for
\alpha_{2}
. Such a formula is tedious to find, but it can be done in this case by characterizing
\hat{\beta}(\alpha)
as defined in Equation (
111
) using sub-differential calculus. The objective function on the right hand side of Equation (
111
) is not differentiable but convex. Therefore, we may compute its sub-differential, which is a set-valued function. We denote by
B_{1}(0):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x\|\leq 1\}
the unit ball around
0
. Then, the sub-differential with respect to
\beta_{k}
is given by

-\frac{2}{nd}\hat{X}^{\top}Z_{\cdot k}+\frac{2}{nd}\sum_{\ell=1}^{p}\left[Z^{\top}Z\right]_{\ell,k}\beta_{\ell}+\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\|\beta_{k}\|^{-1}\beta_{k}&\textrm{ if }\beta_{k}\neq 0\\
B_{1}(0)&\textrm{ if }\beta_{k}=0\end{array}\right..

Note that the above is a proper set if
\beta_{k}(\alpha)=0
(namely a shifted and scaled ball). Otherwise, the above is a single number which we interpret as a set with exactly one element. Sub-differential calculus implies that the
0
-vector must be contained in this set when we plug-in the optimizer
\hat{\beta}_{k}(\alpha)
. This has to be true for all
k\in[p]
. In other words, using the previously introduced notation,

0\in-\frac{2}{d}U_{k}+\frac{2}{d}\sum_{\ell=1}^{p}R_{\ell,k}\hat{\beta}_{\ell}(\alpha)+\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\|\hat{\beta}_{k}(\alpha)\|^{-1}\hat{\beta}_{k}(\alpha)&\textrm{ if }\hat{\beta}_{k}(\alpha)\neq 0\\
B_{1}(0)&\textrm{ if }\hat{\beta}_{k}(\alpha)=0\end{array}\right..

(113)

In the case that
\hat{\beta}_{k}(\alpha)\neq 0
, the right hand side above is just a set with a single element and the
\in
-relation can in fact be read as an equality.

We first establish the value of
\alpha_{1}
.

Lemma F.1
.

Suppose that

m:=\underset{k\in[p]}{\operatorname{argmax}}\frac{2}{\sqrt{d}}\|U_{k}\|

is unique. In the situation above, we have

\alpha_{1}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{d}}\|U_{m}\|.

Proof.

We must show that
\hat{\beta}_{k}(\alpha)=0
for all
k\in[p]
is a solution to (
111
) if and only if
\alpha\geq\alpha_{1}
. In view of (
113
),
\hat{\beta}_{k}(\alpha)=0
for all
k\in[p]
is equivalent to

0\in-\frac{2}{d}U_{k}+\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}B_{1}(0)\text{ for all }k\in[p],

which is true if and only if

\left\|\frac{2}{\sqrt{d}\alpha}U_{k}\right\|\leq 1\text{ for all }k\in[p],

which implies the statement.
∎

In Section 4.3.1 of
Hastie et al. 2015
, some computational details for the group LASSO are considered. A main challenge is that the solution of the group LASSO problem has no closed-form solution.
However, in the multivariate regression setting that we consider here, we can formulate the solution explicitly for the special case of the first variable entering the regression.
We will show in the following that
\hat{\beta}_{m}(\alpha)
is the first non-zero variable for
\alpha<\alpha_{1}
. In the first step, we assume that this is the case, and compute an explicit formula for
\hat{\beta}_{m}(\alpha)
.

Lemma F.2
.

Suppose
\alpha<\alpha_{1}
is such that
\hat{\beta}_{m}(\alpha)\neq 0
and
\hat{\beta}_{k}(\alpha)=0
for all
k\neq m
. Then

\hat{\beta}_{m}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{R_{m,m}}\left(1-\alpha\frac{\sqrt{d}}{2\|U_{m}\|}\right)U_{m}.

Proof.

By Equation (
113
), for
\hat{\beta}_{m}(\alpha)
, we have

0=-\frac{2}{d}U_{m}+\frac{2}{d}R_{m,k}\hat{\beta}_{m}(\alpha)+\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{d}\|\hat{\beta}_{m}(\alpha)\|}\hat{\beta}_{m}(\alpha).

Rearranging the above equation yields

\hat{\beta}_{m}(\alpha)=\frac{2}{d}\left(\frac{2}{d}R_{m,m}+\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{d}\|\hat{\beta}_{m}(\alpha)\|}\right)^{-1}U_{m}.

(114)

Taking the norm on both sides, noting that
R_{m,m}\geq 0
,

\|\hat{\beta}_{m}(\alpha)\|=\frac{2}{d}\left(\frac{2}{d}R_{m,m}+\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{d}\|\hat{\beta}_{m}(\alpha)\|}\right)^{-1}\|U_{m}\|.

Solving the above equation for
\|\hat{\beta}_{m}(\alpha)\|
yields

\|\hat{\beta}_{m}(\alpha)\|=\frac{2\|U_{m}\|-\alpha\sqrt{d}}{2R_{m,m}}.

Replacing the above in Equation (
114
) yields

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{m}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{d}\left(\frac{2}{d}R_{m,m}+\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{d}\frac{2\|U_{m}\|-\alpha\sqrt{d}}{2R_{m,m}}}\right)^{-1}U_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{R_{m,m}}\left(1+\frac{\alpha\sqrt{d}}{2\|U_{m}\|-\alpha\sqrt{d}}\right)^{-1}U_{m}=\frac{1}{R_{m,m}}\left(\frac{2\|U_{m}\|}{2\|U_{m}\|-\alpha\sqrt{d}}\right)^{-1}U_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{R_{m,m}}\left(1-\frac{\alpha\sqrt{d}}{2\|U_{m}\|}\right)U_{m},

which is the statement we wanted to prove.
∎

We can now show that there is indeed
\alpha_{2}
such that, for all
\alpha\in[\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1})
,
hat{\beta}_{m}(\alpha)\neq 0
and
\hat{\beta}_{k}(\alpha)=0
for all
k\neq m
.
We will also provide a formula for
\alpha_{2}
.
We will achieve this by showing that
\hat{\beta}_{m}(\alpha)
as in Lemma
F.2
and
\hat{\beta}_{k}(\alpha)=0
for
k\neq m
provides a set of solution for the system in Equation (
113
) when
\alpha
is chosen suitably.
Note firstly that, by construction, Equation (
113
) always holds true for
k=m
.
For
k\neq m
, Equation (
113
) reads (replacing the formula from Lemma
F.2
)

0\in-\frac{2}{d}U_{k}+\frac{2}{d}\frac{R_{m,k}}{R_{m,m}}\left(1-\frac{\alpha\sqrt{d}}{2\|U_{m}\|}\right)U_{m}+\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}B_{1}(0).

Noting that the right hand side above is a shifted and scaled ball, it is clear that the above relation holds if and only if

\left\|\frac{2}{\alpha\sqrt{d}}\left(U_{k}-\frac{R_{m,k}}{R_{m,m}}U_{m}\right)+\frac{R_{m,k}}{R_{m,m}}\cdot\frac{U_{m}}{\|U_{m}\|}\right\|^{2}\leq 1.

Expanding the norm and rearranging the equation, finally yields

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}\left(\frac{R_{m,k}^{2}}{R_{m,m}^{2}}-1\right)+\alpha\cdot\frac{R_{m,k}}{R_{m,m}}\cdot\frac{4\left(U_{k}^{\top}U_{m}-\frac{R_{m,k}}{R_{m,m}}\|U_{m}\|^{2}\right)}{\sqrt{d}\|U_{m}\|}+\frac{4\left\|U_{k}-\frac{R_{m,k}}{R_{m,m}}U_{m}\right\|^{2}}{d}\leq 0.

(115)

We are going to prove that the above statement, and thus (
113
), can always be made true by choosing
\alpha<\alpha_{1}
suitably. We define for ease of notation

A_{k}:=\frac{R_{m,k}}{R_{m,m}}\text{ and }B_{k}:=U_{k}^{\top}U_{m}-\frac{R_{m,k}}{R_{m,m}}\|U_{m}\|^{2}.

Now, viewing the left hand side of Inequality (
115
) as a second order polynomial in
\alpha
, the roots are given by

\frac{-A_{k}\cdot\frac{4B_{k}}{\sqrt{d}\|U_{m}\|}\pm\sqrt{A_{k}^{2}\cdot\frac{16B_{k}^{2}}{d\|U_{m}\|^{2}}-\frac{16\left(A_{k}^{2}-1\right)\left\|U_{k}-A_{k}U_{m}\right\|^{2}}{d}}}{2\left(A_{k}^{2}-1\right)}

(116)

Note at this point that, in the case where
R_{m,k}^{2}\leq R_{m,m}^{2}
, that is,
A_{k}\in[-1,1]
, the term inside the square root is clearly non-negative and hence there are two solutions. In order to see that there are two solutions also in the case where
R_{m,k}^{2}\geq R_{m,m}^{2}
, note that, by tedious but elementary algebra, the term inside of the root in Equation (
116
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{16}{d\|U_{m}\|^{2}R_{m,m}^{2}}\left(\left[U_{k}^{\top}U_{m}R_{m,k}-R_{m,m}\|U_{m}\|^{2}\right]^{2}+\left(R_{m,k}^{2}-R_{m,m}^{2}\right)\left(\|U_{m}\|^{4}-\|U_{k}\|^{2}\|U_{m}\|^{2}\right)\right).

In this notation, it is evident that for
R_{m,k}^{2}\geq R_{m,m}^{2}
the above term and thus the term inside the root of Equation (
116
) is also non-negative by choice of
m
.
Therefore, (
115
) holds always with equality for exactly two values of
\alpha
.
Hence, it it is always fulfilled for some range of
\alpha
.
Moreover, by plugging in
\alpha_{1}
from Lemma
F.1
into Inequality (
115
), we can see after some elementary algebra that
\alpha_{1}
fulfills Inequality (
115
) for all
k\neq m
.
Therefore, we conclude the existence of
\alpha_{2}<\alpha_{1}
such that for all
\alpha\in[\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1})
,
\hat{\beta}_{k}(\alpha)=0
for
k\neq m
and
\hat{\beta}_{m}(\alpha)
is given by the Formula from Lemma
F.2
.

The following lemma provides a specific formula for
\alpha_{2}
.

Lemma F.3
.

In the situation of Lemma
F.1
, it is true that

\alpha_{2}=\max_{k\neq m}\frac{-A_{k}\frac{4B_{k}}{\sqrt{d}\|U_{m}\|}-\sqrt{\frac{16A_{k}^{2}B_{k}^{2}}{d\|U_{m}\|^{2}}-\frac{16\left(A_{k}^{2}-1\right)\left\|U_{k}-A_{k}U_{m}\right\|^{2}}{d}}}{2\left(A_{k}^{2}-1\right)}.

Proof.

By Lemma
F.1
, we have that
\hat{\beta}_{k}=0
for all
k\in[p]
for
\alpha\geq\alpha_{1}
.
Hence, we have left to show that (
115
) holds true for all
k\neq m
and
\alpha\in[\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1})
but not for
\alpha<\alpha_{2}
.

Let
\alpha_{k,-}
denote the root of the left hand side of Inequality (
115
) understood as a polynomial in
\alpha
given by Equation (
116
) when choosing
-
in place of
\pm
.
Analogously,
\alpha_{k,+}
denotes the same for
+
in place of
\pm
.
With this notation, the statement of the Lemma can be restated as
\alpha_{2}=\max_{k\neq m}\alpha_{k,-}
.
We prove that this number has the right properties.
We distinguish two cases:

R_{m,k}^{2}<R_{m,m}^{2}
:

In this case, the parabola (in
\alpha
) to the left of Inequality (
115
) is downwoards facing.
Hence, every
\alpha
larger than the larger root of the parabola provides a valid solution.
Since
A_{k}^{2}-1<0
, we see that
\alpha_{k,-}
is the larger root.
Therefore, we have to choose
\alpha\geq\alpha_{k,-}
for all
k
for which
R_{m,k}^{2}<R_{m,m}^{2}
.
In particular, for
\alpha\in[\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1})
, Inequality (
115
) holds true.

R_{m,k}^{2}\geq R_{m,m}^{2}
:

In this case, the parabola on the left of (
115
) is upwards facing, and Inequality (
115
) holds true for
\alpha\in[\alpha_{k,-},\alpha_{k,+}]
(note that in this case
\alpha_{k,-}\leq\alpha_{k,+}
).
Hence, in order to prove that (
115
) holds true for all
\alpha\in[\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1})
, we have to show that
\alpha_{k,+}\geq\alpha_{1}
.
Denote the parabola (in
\alpha
) to the left of Inequality (
115
) by

, and recall that it is upwards facing.
By elementary computations, we see that
P(\alpha_{1})<0
if

is the unique maximizer of

, which we assume.
It follows that
\alpha_{1}\in[\alpha_{k,-},\alpha_{k,+}]
.

Hence, in summary, we have proven that Inequality (

) holds true for all
\alpha\in[\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1})
.
We have left to argue that, in the case

, Inequality (

) is violated for at least one

.
If

, then

for some

.
The discussion about the location of the roots of

and the orientation of the parabolas in either of the previous steps shows that Inequality (

) is violated for this choice

.
The result follows.
∎

We finally mention a simple form of
T_{\textrm{cov}}
that is analogue to what
Lockhart et al. 2014
show in their Lemma 1.
Using Lemma
F.2
and Lemma
F.1
in Equality (
112
), we get

\displaystyle T_{\textrm{cov}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hat{\sigma}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\hat{X}_{ij}\left[Z\hat{\beta}(\alpha_{2})\right]_{i,j}=\frac{1}{\hat{\sigma}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\hat{X}_{ij}Z_{im}\frac{1}{R_{m,m}}\left(1-\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{1}}\right)U_{m,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n}{\hat{\sigma}^{2}R_{m,m}}\left(1-\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{1}}\right)\|U_{m}\|^{2}=\frac{n}{\hat{\sigma}^{2}R_{m,m}}\left(1-\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{1}}\right)\frac{\alpha_{1}^{2}d}{4}=\frac{nd}{4\hat{\sigma}^{2}R_{m,m}}\alpha_{1}\left(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}\right).

Remark F.4
.

For a given dataset, we estimate
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
by computing the empirical variance of the residuals of a group LASSO fitted with a cross-validated
\alpha
. For a single data set this is computationally feasible. For the permutation test, however, we want to understand the behavior of the test statistic on the hypothesis. In this case, we therefore use an estimator that is valid under
H_{0}
, i.e.,
B=0
. On the hypothesis, no covariate is active, and therefore we choose simply the empirical variance of the observations as
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
.
