Diverse germline-targeting HIV Env immunogens select for distinct mutations in the same knock-in mice B cell receptors
Authors: Parul Agrawal, Junli Feng, Latha Kallur Siddaramaiah, Arineh Khechaduri, Kelsey R. Salladay, Karli Hinton, Madison Means, Julianna A. Rose, Luziana Cohen, Ming Tian, Sabyasachi Baboo, Jolene K. Diedrich, Todd Reese, Derin Kara, John R. Yates, James C. Paulson, Frederick W. Alt, Marie Pancera, Leonidas Stamatatos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
VRC01-class HIV broadly neutralizing antibodies have been isolated from people living with HIV. Their unmutated (germline) forms do not bind Env and do not neutralize HIV. They acquire their broadly neutralizing potentials through the accumulation of specific somatic mutations. Here, we identify key modifications that allow Env-derived proteins from diverse HIV clades to effectively engage germline VRC01-class B cell receptors (BCRs) expressed by naive B cells. Noticeably, these germline-targeting Env proteins interact with VRC01-class BCRs differently depending on the specific Env background. When used as immunogens in a knock-in mouse model, all germline-targeting Envs successfully activate VRC01-class B cells. However, the resulting BCRs accumulate distinct mutations at key positions. Thus, while the same BCRs are initially activated, they follow different maturation pathways depending on the immunogen used. These findings have important implications not only for HIV vaccine design efforts but also for other immunogens aiming to direct specific BCR maturation pathways.

Introduction

VRC01-class broadly HIV-neutralizing antibodies (bnAbs) have been isolated from several people living with HIV-1 and bind a conserved epitope within the receptor-binding site (CD4-binding site, CD4-BS) of the viral Env1–6. Two phase 3 clinical trials demonstrated that they prevent HIV acquisition7 and therefore could be an important component of the immune responses a successful HIV vaccine would elicit.

VRC01-class bnAbs are derived from the pairing of diverse antibody light chains (LCs) that only express 5 amino acid long CDRL3s, with antibody heavy chains (HCs) derived from the VH1-2*02 or *04 alleles6,8. Their primary mode of interaction with the Env is through their VH1-2 gene segment-encoded CDRH2 regions5,6,9,10, but their CDRH3s, CDRL1s, and CDRL3s, also participate in this interaction11–13. The broadly neutralizing forms of VRC01-class antibodies are highly mutated both in their HCs and LCs. In contrast, their unmutated (germline, gl) forms do not engage wild-type (WT) Envs, nor do they neutralize HIV-1 viruses14–18.

Glycans expressed on the conserved N-linked glycosylation site (NLGS) in Loop D of the gp120 subunit (position 276) are the major obstacle preventing the binding of glVRC01-class antibodies (or BCRs) to Env, but glycans expressed on NLGSs in the V5 region of gp120 also abate this interaction14,17–19. However, the somatic mutations that accumulate on the maturing VRC01-class BCRs allow them to accommodate the above-mentioned glycans1–6. Thus, WT Env-derived immunogens fail to elicit VRC01-class bnAbs because they fail to activate the B cells expressing the precursor forms of these antibodies20.

The elimination of the N276- and V5-associated glycans from the clade C Env 426c (426c triple mutant, 426c.TM), allows some, but not all known, glVRC01-class antibodies to bind that Env. Similar modifications on the clade A Env BG505, also led to its recognition by some of the known glVRC01-class Abs19. However, the elimination of N276- and V5-associated NLGSs from other Envs does not have a similar effect18. So, in the vast majority of Envs, additional obstacles prevent their recognition by glVRC01-class antibodies.

On the background of the 426c.TM Env, the elimination of the variable domains 1, 2, and 3 of gp120 (426c.TM4ΔV1-3, now called 426c modified core or 426c.Mod.Core) results in the binding of a greater number of glVRC01-class antibodies, as compared to the 426c.TM17. Thus, in addition to the above-mentioned carbohydrates, elements of the variable regions 1, 2, and 3 (V1, V2, and V3, respectively) of the gp120 subunit can also prevent the binding of certain glVRC01-class antibodies to Env.

Here, to determine whether the combined removal of the NLGSs near CD4-BS and of the V1, V2, and V3 domains from other Envs also leads to their recognition by glVRC01-class antibodies, we introduce similar modifications on five additional Envs (from clades A, B, and C). While all modified Envs are recognized by glVRC01-class antibodies (i.e., they became ‘germline-targeting’), differences in the relative binding of individual glVRC01-class antibodies to a given modified Env are evident. Differences in the binding of a given glVRC01-class antibody to different modified Env are also evident. When used as immunogens, the modified Envs activate naive B cells expressing glVRC01-class BCRs, which accumulate different amino acid substitutions at the exact same positions of the VHs, 4 weeks post immunization. These positions are considered key residues as they are linked with the broadly neutralizing forms of VRC01-class antibodies. Potentially, the rate at which these antibody responses mature towards their broadly neutralizing forms during prime-boost immunization regimens may differ depending on the germline-targeting Env employed to initiate this process.

Results

Reducing the glycan occupancy around the CD4-binding site of Env rarely results in glVRC01-class antibody binding to Env

The CD4-BS on Env is surrounded by NLGSs, including those located in Loop D (N276), C2 (N197 and N234), the CD4-binding loop (N362/363), and in V5, at varying numbers and positions depending on the Env background21. The associated glycan moieties limit the binding of anti-CD4-BS bnAbs and prevent the activation of the corresponding naive B cells by Env-derived proteins. The 426c Env naturally lacks the N234 NLGS, which is present in ~80% of HIV-1 strains, and the N362/N363 NLGS, which is present in ~42% of HIV-1 strains22–24. Thus, the artificial elimination of the N276 and V5 (N460 and N463) NLGSs (426c.TM), along with the natural absence of N234 and N362/N363 NLGSs, could be the reason why 426c.TM is recognized by glVRC01-class antibodies, while the elimination of the same N276 and V5 NLGSs from other Envs does not lead to such recognition18.

To investigate this possibility, we engineered full-length gp120 proteins derived from the 823c (clade C), 706c (clade C), Q168a2 (clade A), and Q461e2 (clade A) Envs25,26 lacking NLGSs at positions N234, N276, and from V5 (Envs termed ‘modified gp120s’, or Mod.gp120 for simplicity) (Table S1). We additionally removed glycans at another highly conserved (>92%) NLGS proximal to the CD4-BS (position N197)27,28 in these four Mod.gp120s and examined whether and how mVRC01- and glVRC01-class antibodies recognize them. These Mod.gp120s were produced as self-assembling nanoparticles (NPs; see Materials and Methods and Figs. S1, S2) based on the oligomerization motif of the C4b-binding protein, to increase avidity and allow for the detection of weak antibody-Env interactions17,29,30. We also produced the corresponding self-assembling NPs of the unmodified gp120 proteins (WT.gp120.C4bs; Figs. S1, S2) for comparison.

As expected, both WT.gp120 (Fig. S3A–E) and Mod.gp120 (Fig. S3F–J) NPs were recognized by all mVRC01-class antibodies. The corresponding glVRC01-class antibodies displayed no detectable binding to the WT.gp120 NPs, also as expected (Fig. 1A–E). Only two of five Mod.gp120 NPs were recognized by some glVRC01-class antibodies. 426c.Mod.gp120.C4b was recognized by glVRC01 and gl12A21 (Fig. 1F), while 706c.Mod.gp120.C4b was recognized by glBG24 (Fig. 1H). We conclude that in certain cases, the elimination of NLGSs surrounding the CD4-BS can lead to recognition by some but not all glVRC01-class antibodies. Removal of the variable 1, 2, and 3 gp120 domains rarely leads to glVRC01-class antibody binding to Env

V1, V2, and V3, limit the accessibility of the CD4-BS to anti-CD4-BS antibodies31–33. We therefore eliminated them from the above-mentioned WT.gp120 NPs and examined whether this modification led to their recognition by glVRC01-class antibodies. We refer to these proteins as ‘wild-type core NPs’ (WT.Core.C4bs; Figs. S1, S2) because they still express NLGSs at N197, N234, N276, and in V5-associated NLGSs.

As expected, all WT.Core NPs were recognized by the mVRC01-class antibodies (Fig. S3K–O), but only 426c.WT.Core.C4b was recognized by some glVRC01-class antibodies (glVRC01, gl12A21, and glN6) (Fig. 1K). We confirmed by mass spectrometry analysis that the NLGSs on the 426c.WT.Core.C4b were mostly occupied by glycans (Fig. S4). Notably, however, glycan occupancy at N276 was only 78%, reflecting a higher level of ‘unoccupancy’ than found on other WT.Core NPs (>90%). This small but significant unoccupancy at N276 on 426c.WT.Core.C4b (21.5%) could partially explain the observed binding by some glVRC01-class antibodies.

Thus, on 426c Env background NPs, the elimination of either the NLGSs that surround the CD4-BS (Fig. 1F) or the elimination of the V1, V2, and V3 loops (Fig. 1K), led to recognition by some glVRC01-class antibodies. We note, however, that while the elimination of the NLGSs around the CD4-BS of 706c (Mod.gp120.C4b) (Fig. 1H) led to a weak recognition by glBG24, the elimination of the variable loops (WT.Core.C4b) did not (Fig. 1M). Thus, it appears that the NLGSs play a more crucial role than the variable loops in limiting the access of glVRC01-class antibodies to this Env.

Broad recognition of Env by glVRC01-class antibodies requires the removal of variable 1, 2, and 3 domains and NLGSs surrounding the CD4-BS

Based on the above observations, we removed the variable regions 1, 2, and 3, from the different Mod.gp120 NPs. We refer to these NP constructs as ‘modified cores’ (Mod.Core.C4bs; Figs. S1, S2). By negative stain we confirmed that all constructs formed self-assembling nanoparticles (Fig. S5). We then modeled the expected location of key glycans on the different WT and Mod.Core Envs (Fig. S6). All Mod.Core NPs were recognized by mVRC01-class antibodies, as expected (Fig. S3P–T). Except for 823c.Mod.Core, all other Mod.Core NPs were efficiently recognized by a majority of the glVRC01-class antibodies (Fig. 1P–T). Interestingly, glBG24 did not bind 426c.Mod.Core.C4b (Figs. 1P, S7A), while it bound all other Mod.Core NPs (except 823c.Mod.Core.C4b). The 426c.Mod.Core protein used here was designed in 201617 and expresses the N197 NLGS, while that NLGS was eliminated from all other Mod.Core NPs designed in this study (Table S1). As previously mentioned, this NLGS is located proximally to the VRC01 epitope (Fig. S6). Consequently, we eliminated the N197 NLGS from the 426c.Mod.Core NPs (426c.Mod.Core.C4b.Δ197), and this single modification on the 426c.Mod.Core background resulted in glBG24 binding, in addition to a substantial increase in binding by other glVRC01-class antibodies (Fig. S7B). Thus, it appears that while some glVRC01-class antibodies can bind the VRC01 epitopes in the presence of glycans expressed on N197, the binding of other glVRC01-class antibodies is blocked.

The above modifications were introduced on the background of either clade A or C Envs. To examine whether these modifications on clade B-derived Env will also have a similar effect, we generated the HxB2.WT.Core and HxB2.Mod.Core NPs (Figs. S1, S2). Both were recognized by mVRC01-class antibodies (Fig. S8A, C). While the HxB2.WT.Core.C4b was not recognized by the glVRC01-class antibodies (Fig. S8B), the HxB2.Mod.Core.C4b was recognized by the majority of glVRC01-class antibodies tested, but with different potencies (Fig. S8D).

In sum, we identified a set of modifications, including deletions of the V1, V2, and V3 loops, and of NLGSs surrounding the CD4-BS, that result in the binding of glVRC01-class antibodies to different Envs. However, the relative binding of individual glVRC01-class antibodies to a given Mod.Core NP differs, and the binding of a given glVRC01-class antibody to different Mod.Core NPs also differ. These differences could, in turn, affect the ability of these modified NPs to activate and initiate the maturation of glVRC01-class BCRs during immunization.

Selection of distinct amino acid mutations in the VRC01-class antibodies elicited by the different germline-targeting immunogens

The relative abilities of the above-described six germline-targeting Mod.Core NP immunogens to activate and initiate the maturation of VRC01-class B cells in vivo were compared in transgenic mice that have incorporated the human hVH1-2*02 segment, hJH2 segment, and the pre-rearranged gl-Vκ3-20 LC with mature VRC01 CDRL3 in them34. In this mouse model, the hVH1-2 and hJH2 segments recombine with mouse D segments during B cell development, and the V(D)J recombination generates hVH1-2 HCs with diverse CDRH3s. The hVH1-2*02 HCs associate with the gl-Vκ3-20 LC to form VRC01 precursors. We were specifically interested in determining whether the different immunogens would select for the same or different amino acid mutations at key positions in VRC01-class BCRs and whether they would select for VRC01 HCs with different CDRH3s.

All six NPs were immunogenic, as evident from the immunogen-specific serum antibody responses at 2- and 4-weeks post-immunization at week 0 (Fig. 2). A single immunization with the NPs elicited robust autologous plasma antibody responses in all animals (Fig. 2B) that were highest for the 706c.Mod.Core.C4b group (Mean titer value at W2: 1.39) and lowest for 823c.Mod.Core.C4b (Mean titer value at W2: 0.65) at both time points, but these differences were not statistically significant. In five of six cases, the majority of the autologous serum binding antibodies targeted the CD4-BS, although variations within each group were evident (Fig. 2B, S9A). The exception was 823c.Mod.Core, where only ~40% of the autologous antibodies targeted the CD4-BS. Variations in the CDRH3 lengths of the elicited VRC01-class antibodies by all six immunogen NPs were observed (Fig. S18A). Amino acid sequence analysis of CDRH3s indicated that the immunogens not only activated B cells expressing VRC01-class BCRs with unique CDRH3 sequences (Fig. S18B; shades of gray slices), but also expansion of B cells expressing VRC01-class BCRs with identical CDRH3 sequences in all cases (Fig. S18B; solid color slices). Interestingly, VRC01-class B cells with identical CDRH3s became activated by multiple immunogens in different animals (Fig. S18B; dotted solid color slices).

In the LCs, the amino acid mutations were primarily accumulated in CDRL1, followed by CDRL2 (Figs. 4, S17). No major differences in the frequency of amino acid substitutions at the key positions or in the identity of the replacing amino acid were observed. Only S30 was ~21% mutated in the VRC01 antibodies elicited by 823c.Mod.Core NPs (Fig. S17, Table S2), which were higher compared to all other immunogens tested, possibly due to the lower BCR recovery in the 823c.Mod.Core NP immunized animals.

Discussion

The development of VRC01-class bnAbs requires the activation of naive B cells expressing the corresponding unmutated BCRs by specifically designed Env-derived proteins and the subsequent mutation of amino acids at key positions in the antibody HCs and LCs during the germinal center reaction. To date, Env-derived proteins capable of engaging glVRC01-class BCRs have been successfully designed from only three Envs: clade C (426c series)17,18,38, clade B (HxB2; eOD series)39,40, and clade A (BG505 series)19. The overall designs of these three germline-targeting proteins differ, and as a result, they recognize glVRC01-class antibodies with different affinities.

Here, we identify common mutations that allow Envs from diverse HIV clades to engage glVRC01-class antibodies. These proteins are stabilized core gp120 NPs, which include the inner and outer gp120 domains, in the CD4-bound conformation. Nevertheless, differences in the relative binding of known glVRC01-class antibodies to these proteins were evident. Similarly, the relative binding of known glVRC01-class antibodies to any given germline-targeting Env also differed. Such differential binding could be due to differences in the CDRH3 composition of the inferred germline VRC01-class antibodies12 and/or variations in the biophysical properties of the areas surrounding the VRC01 epitope on the different Env backbones due to differences in their amino acid sequences. Consequently, the different germline-targeting immunogens would differentially activate naive B cells expressing glVRC01-class BCRs in vivo. This, in turn, could lead to differences in the rate of somatic mutation accumulation in those BCRs, and/or to differences in the type of amino acid substitutions accumulating in those BCRs depending on the germline-targeting Env used as an immunogen.

Although the amino acid sequences of the fully matured, broadly neutralizing VRC01-class antibodies can be up to 50% divergent, the number and positions of amino acid mutations that are relevant to the broadly neutralizing forms of these antibodies are limited35–37. Indeed, we find that the rate and frequency of somatic mutations, and the type of amino acid substitutions at these key positions differ among VRC01-class antibodies elicited by the different germline-targeting immunogens. Thus, in response to the different germline-targeting Envs, glVRC01-class B cell clones accumulate distinct amino acid mutations at the same positions. We hypothesize that this differential amino acid mutation selection process arises due to differences in the biophysical properties of the different Env backbones around the VRC01 epitope. This could be one reason why the VRC01-class bnAbs isolated from different people living with HIV can be up to 50% divergent in their amino acid sequences, as they have been elicited in response to infection by different viruses4,5. It will be important to determine whether and how such amino acid differences affect the binding affinities of the elicited antibodies and whether it influences how rapidly these antibodies acquire their broadly neutralizing forms.

Germline-targeting immunogens to initiate the activation of glVRC01-class B cells have already entered phase 1 clinical testing (36,41,42, ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT05471076), and follow-up clinical studies are being planned to evaluate whether heterologous Env immunogens can further drive the maturation of the elicited VRC01-class B cell responses (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT06796686). Our study implies that certain germline-targeting immunogens may be more effective than others in facilitating the progression of VRC01-class antibodies toward their broadly neutralizing forms in a human setting.

Although our study was conducted with HIV-derived immunogens, our findings have broader relevance to other vaccine development efforts. Specifically, they suggest that the design of immunogens aimed at activating particular B cell clones should not only emphasize the efficient presentation of the desired epitope but also consider the structural backbone on which the epitope is displayed.

Methods

HIV-1 envelope constructs

Germline-targeting immunogen genes, including those of Avi-tagged Core monomers, Core.C4bs, Mod.gp120.C4bs, and WT.gp120.C4bs, were synthesized and cloned into PTwist-CMV-orip vectors (Twist Bioscience). Upon receiving plasmids and glycerol bacterial stocks, we sequenced whole lengths of the inserts using primers complementary to the PTwist-CMV-orip vector backbone (Genewiz) or sequenced the whole lengths of plasmids (Plasmidsaurus). All corresponding mutant genes with CD4-BS knockout (KO) were generated using Q5-Site Direct Mutagenesis Kit (New England Biolabs). Three amino acids: N279A or D279A, D368R, and E370A were mutated in the KO version. As previously done for 426c.Mod.Core, K65C, and S115C modifications were introduced in the germline targeting Envs to improve their stability upon expression. Primers for mutagenesis were designed according to NEBaseChanger. All plasmids were confirmed by sequencing before transfection into HEK293 cells for envelope protein expression. See Figs. S19–S24 for key sequences.

Recombinant HIV-1 envelope expression and purification

Nanoparticle and monomer encoding plasmids were transfected into HEK293E (Accession number CVCL_HF20; National Research Council of Canada) cells at a density of 1×106 cells/ml in Freestyle 293 media using the Polyethylenimine (PEI) transfectant reagent (Millipore Sigma)17. Clarification of cell harvest material was carried out by centrifugation at 3374 g for 30 min followed by 0.22 µM filtration. Clarified NP cell supernatants were purified by Agarose-bound Galanthus Nivalis Lectin (GNL) resin (Vector Laboratory)17. The eluted NP envelope was further purified using size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) on either a HiLoad 16/600 Superose 6 column or a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL column, depending on the sample load (Fig. S1). Both columns were pre-equilibrated with tris-buffer saline (TBS). Avi tagged monomeric envelopes were purified by GNL resin and Superdex 200 SEC. His-tagged monomeric envelopes were purified on His60 Ni Superflow resin (Takara Bio) equilibrated with 50 mM Tris, 300 mM NaCl, 15 mM imidazole, pH 8.0, and then eluted with 50 mM Tris, 500 mM NaCl, 200 mM imidazole, pH 8. The Ni column eluted his-tagged monomeric envelopes, which were then purified on Superdex 200 SEC. All SEC purified envelopes were aliquoted and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until use.

Thermal stability assay

Thermal stability of the purified immunogens (as monomers or NPs) was assessed using a SYPRO Orange–based thermal shift assay. This method provides a quick and effective way to assess protein stability in solution and compares the relative stability of different protein variants under identical conditions. Each protein was diluted to a final concentration of 3 μg/ml in 1x TBS and mixed with SYPRO Orange dye to a final concentration of 7.5x. Fluorescence measurements were recorded from 25 °C to 95 °C at 0.5 °C increments using a Bio-Rad CFX96 real-time PCR instrument. The fluorescence intensity was plotted as a function of temperature, and the first derivative of the fluorescence signal (–dF/dT) was used to determine the melting temperature (Tm), corresponding to the midpoint of the unfolding transition (Fig. S2). The SYPRO thermal shift assay revealed that all proteins exhibited well-defined unfolding transitions, indicating proper folding and stability under the tested conditions. Only HxB2.Mod.Core.C4b displayed high intrinsic fluorescence that interfered with the assay and did not result in a calculated melting temperature.

Mice and Immunizations

Orthologs of the human VH1-2*02 gene are not expressed in animal species such as mice, rats, rabbits, and non-human primates. Thus, the study was conducted in transgenic mice expressing the human VH1-2*02 and JH2 genes and a prearranged human germline κ3-20 LC expressing the mature CDRL3 of VRC0134. Knock-in mice, were bred and kept at the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center with the following housing conditions: dark/light cycle: 12 h/12 h; ambient temp: 72 ± 3o Fahrenheit; and humidity: monitored with HVAC system, between 30–70%. Mice were 6–12 weeks of age at the initiation of experiments. We do not provide disaggregated results for sex and gender, as we do not expect different outcomes in our experiments due to sex and/or gender differences of the knock-in mice used in the study. Overall, we ensured adequate representation of both males and females in all our study plans (see source data file, section Fig. 2A). Two independent immunizations were carried out at week 0 where recombinant HIV-1 Env protein NPs along with 3M-052-AF+Alum (AAHI) as adjuvant were administered intramuscularly (n = 3/4 per group each time; Fig. 2A). All Env antigens were administered at a concentration of 50 ug/mouse. Sub-mandibular bleeds were collected before (week 0) and during (week 2) the immunization. Terminal bleeds along with spleen and lymph node tissues were collected at the end of immunization (week 4). Organ processing was carried out as follows: spleens were mashed through 70-mm-pore-size nylon cell strainers (Falcon) to obtain a single cell suspension and then treated with Red Blood Cell Lysing Buffer Hybri-Max (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1.5–2 min followed by two washes with 1x PBS. Splenocytes were resuspended in FBS supplemented with 10% DMSO and frozen in Mr. Frosty Freezing Container (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at −80 °C overnight, then moved to liquid nitrogen for storage until further use.

Single B-cell sorting and VH/VL gene sequencing

Tetramers of Avi-tagged Env proteins were generated by combining biotinylated Env with Streptavidin conjugated to either a PE or APC fluorophore (Prozyme, PJFS25 and PJ27S) and added at 1uM concentration per sample29,30,38. Class-switched IgG B cells from immunized animal splenocytes were sorted based on the following markers: CD3-, CD4-, Gr-1-, F4/80-, B220+, CD19+, IgM-, IgD-, IgG1+, IgG2b+, IgG2c+, IgG3+, antigen+/antigen KO- (Fig. S11). See source data file, section Fig. S11, for details on reagent information. Individual B-cells were sorted using BD FACSymphony S6 into 96-well skirted plates (Eppendorf) containing lysis buffer (Supplementary Data 3). The sort data was collected using BD FACSDiva Software. RNA from each well was reverse transcribed to cDNA, and two rounds of PCR were employed to amplify both VH and VL genes. All primers and cycling conditions can be found in Supplementary Data 3. cDNA was then purified using the enzyme ExoSap-IT (Affymetrix, Cat#: 78201) and sent for Sanger sequencing (Genewiz, Seattle, WA)29,30,38. HC and LC sequences were analyzed using the Geneious software and the online IMGT/V-QUEST tool29,30,38. BCR sequencing was performed multiple times with at least 2-3 individual mice sorted for each group (Supplementary Data 1).

ELISA

Plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were coated with 0.1 µM his/avi-tagged Env protein (426c.Mod.Core, Q168a2.Mod.Core, Q461e2.Mod.Core, HxB2.Mod.Core, 706c.Mod.Core, and 823c.Mod.Core) diluted in 0.1 M sodium bicarbonate, at room temperature (RT) overnight. Plates were then washed (PBS plus 0.02% Tween20) and incubated with block buffer (10% milk, 0.03% Tween20 in PBS) for 1-2 h at 37 °C. Following the wash step, mouse plasma was added, serially diluted (1:3), and incubated at 37 °C / 1 h. Plates were washed, and horse radish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (BioLegend) was added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Post final wash, SureBlue Reserve TMB Microwell Peroxidase Substrate (KPL Inc.) was added for 5 min and the reaction was stopped with 1 N H2SO4. The optical density (OD) was read at 450 nm, and the average OD of blank wells was subtracted from all wells before analysis. Cross-reactive plasma antibody titers for pooled mice sera from each group were measured at a single dilution (1:90) of the plasma against the indicated Envs (Fig. S10). Pre-immunization blood samples (pre-bleed) from mice, Anti-His or Anti-Avi, and mVRC01 mAbs were used as internal controls in every ELISA (Figs. S9 and S10). The CD4-BS fraction (based on the endpoint titer values provided in the source data file, section Fig. S9) on a specific Env was calculated by subtracting the binding to the CD4-BS KO version of the corresponding protein and then plotting as percentages.

Biolayer interferometry

BLI assays were performed on the Octet Red instrument (ForteBio)29,43. Anti-human IgG Fc capture biosensors (ForteBio/Sartorius) were employed to immobilize mAbs at 20 μg/μL, and baseline interference was measured for 60 s in kinetics buffer (KB: PBS pH7.4, 0.1% bovine serum albumin, 0.02% Tween-20, 0.1% Kathon). Sensors were immersed in wells containing Envs at a concentration of 1 μM for 300 s during the association phase, followed by another 300 s in KB during the dissociation phase. All Env-Ab binding measurements were corrected by subtracting the signal obtained from concurrent tracing of the corresponding Env using an irrelevant IgG. Curve fitting was executed using ForteBio’s Data analysis software.

Negative stain electron microscopy

Samples were stained with 2% (w/v) uranyl formate on formvar/carbon 400 mesh copper grids with a concentration of ~0.005-0.01 mg/mL in 1x TBS. Grids were imaged on a Talos L120C Transmission Electron Microscope using Leginon data collection software44 with a pixel size of 1.58 A, 2 mm spherical aberration, and a 40 e/A˚ 2 total exposure dose. The micrographs collected for each nanoparticle were uploaded to CryoSPARC45 to be processed. Particles were selected using Blob Picker and Inspect Picks before being sorted into 2D classes. After filtering out noise from each nanoparticle ~8000–12,000 particles remained to generate the final 10 2D classes. From each set of ten 2D classes, a single class was then selected.

Env Core structures with glycan location prediction

PDB ID 6MFT22 was used to predict the glycans on the 426 c.Cores, and PDB ID 7RDW46 was used to predict the glycans on HxB2.Cores. Other germline targeting Core structures were predicted using Alphafold347. Glycans were modeled using the GlycoShape server48. All glycans modeled were high mannose with the following components, Man:5, GlcNAc:2. All models are shown using Pymol (https://www.pymol.org; Version 2.5.4).

Mass spectrometry analysis

Proteinase K treatment and deglycosylation

HIV Env Core glycoprotein media was exchanged to water (Fisher Scientific) using Microcon Ultracel PL-10 centrifugal filter (Millipore Sigma). Glycoprotein was reduced with 5 mM tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP-HCl) and alkylated with 10 mM 2-Chloroacetamide (Sigma-Aldrich) in 100 mM ammonium acetate (Sigma-Aldrich) for 20 min at RT, 24 °C. Initial protein-level deglycosylation was performed using 250 U of Endo H (New England Biolabs) for 5 µg Env, for 1 h at 37 °C. Glycoprotein was digested with 1:25 Proteinase K (PK; Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at 37 °C. PK was denatured by incubating at 90 °C for 15 min, then cooled to RT. Peptides were deglycosylated again with 250 U Endo H for 1 h at 37 °C, then frozen at −80 °C and lyophilized. 100 U PNGase F (New England Biolabs) was lyophilized, resuspended in 20 µl 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate (Sigma-Aldrich) prepared in H218O (Sigma-Aldrich), and added to the lyophilized peptides. Reactions were then incubated for 1 h at 37 °C, subsequently analyzed by LC-MS/MS.

LC-MS/MS

Samples were analyzed on an Orbitrap Eclipse Tribrid mass spectrometer. Samples were injected directly into a 25 cm, 100 μm ID column packed with BEH 1.7 μm C18 resin (Waters). Samples were separated at a flow rate of 300 nL/min on an EASY-nLC 1200 UHPLC (Thermo Scientific). Buffers A and B were 0.1% formic acid (J.T.Baker) in 5% and 80% acetonitrile (Honeywell), respectively. The following gradient was used: 1–25% B over 100 min, an increase to 40% B over 20 min, an increase to 90% B over another 10 min, and held for 10 min at 90% B for a total run time of 140 min. The column was re-equilibrated with buffer A prior to the injection of the sample. Peptides were eluted from the tip of the column and nanosprayed directly into the mass spectrometer by application of 2.8 kV at the back of the column. The mass spectrometer was operated in a data-dependent mode. Full MS1 scans were collected in the Orbitrap at 120,000 resolution. The cycle time was set to 3 s, and within this 3 s, the most abundant ions per scan were selected for HCD MS/MS at 35 NCE. Dynamic exclusion was enabled with an exclusion duration of 60 s and singly charged ions were excluded.

Data processing

Protein and peptide identification were done with the Integrated Proteomics Pipeline (IP2). Tandem mass spectra were extracted from raw files using RawConverter49 and searched with ProLuCID50 against a database comprising UniProt reviewed (Swiss-Prot) proteome for Homo sapiens (UP000005640), UniProt amino acid sequences for Endo H (P04067), PNGase F (Q9XBM8), and PK (P06873), amino acid sequences for the examined proteins, and a list of general protein contaminants. The search space included no cleavage-specificity. Carbamidomethylation (+57.02146 C) was considered a static modification. Deamidation in the presence of H218O (+2.988261 N), GlcNAc (+203.079373 N), oxidation (+15.994915 M), and N-terminal pyroglutamate formation (–17.026549 Q) were considered differential modifications. Data was searched with 50 ppm precursor ion tolerance and 50 ppm fragment ion tolerance. Identified proteins were filtered using DTASelect251 and utilizing a target-decoy database search strategy to limit the false discovery rate to 1%, at the spectrum level52. A minimum of 1 peptide per protein and no tryptic end per peptide were required, and precursor delta mass cut-off was fixed at 10 ppm. Statistical models for peptide mass modification (modstat) were applied. Census2 (http://fields.scripps.edu/yates/wp/?page_id=824) label-free analysis was performed based on the precursor peak area, with a 10 ppm precursor mass tolerance and 0.1 min retention time tolerance. “Match between runs” was used to find missing peptides between runs. Data analysis using GlycoMSQuant53 was implemented to automate the analysis. GlycoMSQuant summed precursor peak areas across replicates, discarded peptides without NGS, discarded misidentified peptides when N-glycan remnant-mass modifications were localized to non-NGS asparagines, and corrected/fixed N-glycan mislocalization where appropriate.

Glycan occupancy

DeGlyPHER53,54 is used to ascertain site-specific glycan occupancy and processivity on the examined glycoproteins.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments were performed at least two to three times with similar results. Details of statistical tests performed are listed in the respective figure legends. All immunization studies were also repeated to confirm our initial findings. Overall, all attempts at replication were successful in generating reproducible results. For mouse experiments, the sample size of n = 3 or 4 per independent experiment was determined by animal availability. The experiments were not randomized, and the investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
