Assessment of Rhodococcus sp. (MG1) induced biochemical alterations and genotoxicity in Spodoptera litura
Authors: Pallvi Mehra, Arushi Mahajan, Jaswinder Kaur, Satish Koundal, Shiv Kumar, Pooja Chadha, Harvinder Singh Saini, Sanehdeep Kaur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spodoptera litura (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae) is recognised as a major agricultural pest causing economic losses to a number of agricultural and vegetable crops. Chemical insecticides remain the primary control method. However, excessive and repeated applications have led to the evolution of resistance, environmental contamination and risk to nontarget organisms. To address these issues, sustainable and eco-friendly alternatives like microbial control have become key strategies in integrated pest management. The present study evaluates the effects of entomopathogenic bacterium, Rhodococcus sp. (MG1) on digestive, detoxifying, and antioxidant enzymes along with histological and genotoxic effects on S. litura larvae. Our results revealed a significant drop in the activity levels of major digestive enzymes—including α-amylase, glucosidases, galactosidases, lipases, and proteases due to MG1 infection. Feeding larvae with castor leaves treated with LC50 MG1 cell suspension significantly increased the level of detoxifying enzymes. Maximum induction of acid phosphatases (1.95-fold) and glutathione S-transferases (2.47-fold) were observed in larvae after 48 h and 96 h, respectively over control. Catalase (CAT) and Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activities were also significantly elevated in treated larvae. Significant effect of bacterial infection was also recorded on immune response of larvae with an increase in the activity of lysozyme and phenoloxidase. However, total hemocyte count was found to be reduced following infection. Additionally, Rhodococcus sp. infection induced genotoxicity as manifested by increased values of all the comet parameters viz. tail length (TL), tail intensity (TI), tail moment (TM) and olive tail moment (OTM) compared to control.

Introduction

Spodoptera litura (Fabricius), commonly known as tobacco caterpillar, is a highly destructive polyphagous pest belonging to family Noctuidae (Order: Lepidoptera). Although native to tropical and subtropical regions of Southeast Asia, it has expanded steadily beyond its original range and is now spreading across other warm regions of the world1,2. It is considered as one of the significant threats to present-day intensive agriculture and changing cropping patterns worldwide, next only to Helicoverpa armigera (Hubner). It is known to feed on more than 389 plant species across 109 families, including economically important crops such as cotton, soybean, tobacco, groundnut, corn, eggplant, and several vegetables3. In India, S. litura is among the most damaging pests of cotton and many vegetable crops, causing yield losses ranging from 20 to 60% annually4,5.

Chemical insecticides have been the primary tool for managing S. litura populations over the years. However, indiscriminate and excessive use of synthetic chemicals has led to development of insect populations resistant to multiple insecticide classes, including pyrethroids, organophosphates, and neonicotinoids6. Moreover, chemical control poses a risk to non-target organisms, pollinators, and the environment, while also promoting pest resurgence and secondary outbreaks7. In light of these challenges, there is an urgent need to explore sustainable and ecologically sound pest management alternatives.

Entomopathogenic bacteria such as Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) are widely used commercially under the name of various formulations viz. LARVECT 50 ®, Montery B.t., Delfin, Halt, Biospal, Dipel, and Biobit8,9. Besides Bt, Serratia marcescens, Photorhabdus luminescens, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Pseudomonas paralactis have also emerged as promising biocontrol agents10–12. There have also been studies suggesting the combined use of entomopathogenic agents with insecticides13. Our recent studies have documented the entomopathogenic potential of Rhodococcus sp. (MG1)14. The entomopathogenic bacteria have been reported to produce toxins, hydrolytic enzymes, and immune-suppressing compounds that compromise host physiology, leading to mortality via septicemia or toxemia15–17. These pathogens can disrupt the insect’s peritrophic membrane, which is a protective chitinous barrier lining the midgut of insect. The disruption in peritrophic membrane impairs nutrient absorption and exposes gut epithelial cells to bacterial toxins and enzymes. It can further lead to alterations in digestive enzyme activities, thereby compromising larval development and survival18. Despite their demonstrated efficacy in inducing mortality and growth inhibition, relatively little is known about the underlying biochemical effects of bacterial infection in S. litura.

Insects possess intricate defense mechanisms to counter microbial pathogens, including the activation of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione-S-transferase, etc. which help to mitigate oxidative stress induced by infection19–21. Additionally, detoxification enzymes such as esterases may be modulated in response to bacterial toxins22,23. Understanding these biochemical alterations is essential for improving the efficacy of microbial biocontrol agents.

Thus the present study aims to evaluate the impact of cell suspension of Rhodococcus sp. on key digestive, antioxidant and detoxification enzymes in S. litura larvae. Additionally, the study also investigates the histopathological and genotoxic effects of bacterial isolate on S. litura.

Materials and methods

Rearing of insects

The egg masses and larvae of S. litura were collected from cabbage and cauliflower fields around Amritsar (Punjab), India. These larvae were reared in the laboratory on fresh castor leaves using rearing jars (10 cm ×15 cm). The culture was maintained at a controlled temperature of 25 ± 2 °C and relative humidity of 60 ± 5%. The castor leaves were changed daily till pupation. The pupae were transferred to pupation jars, and freshly emerged adults were shifted to oviposition jars lined with filter paper to facilitate egg laying. Adults were fed on a honey-water mix (1:4) soaked on cotton swabs. The newly hatched larvae were transferred to fresh castor leaves for continued culture maintenance. These lab-reared larvae were subsequently used for all experimental work.

Bacterial culture

The bacterial isolate Rhodococcus sp. (MG1), with GenBank accession number MZ268625, was obtained from the Department of Microbiology, Guru Nanak Dev University, Amritsar (Punjab), India. Our recent studies documented the entomopathogenic potential of Rhodococcus sp. with the help of a concentration response assay with concentrations (1.1 × 103, 3.2 × 104, 4.8 × 105, 6.3 × 107, and 7.8 × 108 cfu/ml) against S. litura14. Based on mortality in concentration response assay, LC50 value of MG1 was calculated in our previous studies by probit analysis14. The obtained value of LC50 MG1 was serially diluted from 10− 1 to 10− 9 and 100 µl of each dilution was plated onto LB agar plate. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h and colonies were counted from the plates having 30–300 colonies. The following formula was applied:

Biochemical studies

Estimation of digestive enzymes

To study the effect of bacterial infection on digestive enzymes, third instar larvae (10 days old) of S. litura were fed on leaves treated with LC50 of MG1 for 48 and 96 h. The control group larvae were fed leaves treated only with phosphate buffer saline (PBS). There were three independent biological replicates, each consisting of 10 larvae for each treatment (n = 30). The larvae were dissected and their guts were collected and homogenized in 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). The gut homogenate (1%, w/v) was further centrifuged at 13,000 rpm at 4 °C for 20 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant (enzyme extract) was collected and stored at −20 °C for further analysis.

α-amylases

The α-amylase activity was measured according to the procedure outlined by Mehrabadi et al.24. For estimation, 20 µl of enzyme extract was combined with 100 µl of 0.02 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.1) and 1% soluble starch. The mixture was incubated at 35 °C for 30 min. To terminate the reaction, 100 µL of dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) reagent was added, and the mixture was heated to boiling in a water bath for 10 min. After cooling, the absorbance of the solution was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Eon, USA). A standard curve was generated by serially diluting 0.01 M maltose to create concentrations ranging from 100 to 1000 µM. The enzyme activity was calculated and expressed as µM of maltose produced per mg of fresh larval weight.

Glucosidases

Glucosidase activity was assessed following the method of Zibaee25. The enzyme extract (20 µl) was pre-incubated at 37 °C for 10 min with 40 µl of 5 mM p-nitrophenyl-α-D-glucopyranoside (pNαG) and 100 µl of 0.02 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.1). The reaction was stopped by adding 150 µl of 1 M sodium carbonate. The absorbance of the solution was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Eon, USA). A standard curve was constructed with p-nitrophenol (0.01 M) at concentrations ranging from 100 to 1000 µM. The glucosidase activity was calculated as µM of p-nitrophenol produced per mg of fresh larval weight. For β-glucosidase activity, the same procedure was followed, but with p-nitrophenyl-β-D-glucopyranoside (pNβG) as the substrate instead of pNαG.

Galactosidases

The α-galactosidase activity was assessed as per the methodology described by Zibaee25. The gut homogenate (20 µl) was incubated with 40 µl of p-nitrophenyl-α-D-galactopyranoside (5 mM) and 100 µl of phosphate buffer (0.02 M, pH 7.1) at 37 °C for 10 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 150 µl of sodium carbonate (1 M). The absorbance was recorded on a microplate reader (BioTek Eon, USA) at 450 nm. The standard curve was prepared using serial dilutions of 0.01 M p-nitrophenol (100–1000 µM). Enzyme activity was calculated as µM of p-nitrophenol produced per mg of fresh larval weight. For β-galactosidase activity, the procedure was the same, except that p-nitrophenyl-β-D-galactopyranoside (pNβG) (5 mM) was used as the substrate.

Lipases

Lipase activity was measured according to the protocol described by Tsujita et al.26. The enzyme extract (20 µl) was combined with 40 µl of 27 mM p-nitrophenyl butyrate and 100 µl of 0.02 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.1), and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C. After 1 min, 100 µl of 1 M NaOH was added to halt the reaction. The absorbance was then recorded at 405 nm using a BioTek Eon microplate reader. A standard curve was prepared with p-nitrophenol (0.01 M) concentrations ranging from 100 to 1000 µM. Lipase activity was expressed as µM of p-nitrophenol produced per mg of fresh larval weight.

Proteases

Protease activity was evaluated using hemoglobin (20 mg/ml) as the substrate, following the method described by Cohen27. Hemoglobin solution (40 µl) was mixed with 100 µl of 0.02 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.1). The reaction was initiated by adding 40 µl of enzyme extract and incubating the mixture at 30 °C for 120 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 100 µl of 30% trichloroacetic acid (TCA). Enzyme activity was determined based on absorbance at 410 nm, with bovine serum albumin (0.01 M) used as the standard to generate a standard curve for quantification. Activity was expressed in µM per mg.

Estimation of detoxification enzymes

Glutathione S-transferases (GST)

For the estimation of GST, the larvae were first homogenised in 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.6) containing 0.1 mM phenylthiourea (PTU), following the earlier standardised protocols28,29. The assay mixture included buffer, ethanolic CDNB (1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene) (30 µl), reduced glutathione solution (100 µl), and enzyme extract (50 µl), for a total volume of 0.2 ml. The absorbance was measured at 340 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Eon, USA) for 5 min at 25 °C, with one-min interval and the activity was calculated as µM/mg/min.

Esterases (EST)

The method of Katzenellenbogen and Kafatos30, was followed to measure the activity of esterases. A total of 6.25 µl of the pooled enzyme extract was mixed with 125 µl of a substrate solution (1 mM α-naphthyl acetate) in a sodium phosphate buffer (0.1 M) at a pH of 6.5 and incubated at 30 °C for 30 min. To halt the reaction, 62.5 µl of a post-coupling solution (which consists of 4% (w/v) sodium lauryl sulfate and 1% (w/v) fast red TR salt in 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer at pH 6.5) was added, and the absorbance of the resulting mixture was quantified at 540 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Eon, USA). Serial dilutions of 0.01 M α-naphthol ranging from 100 to 1000 µM were employed to construct the standard curve.

Phosphatases

The phosphatases were estimated using the protocol of MacIntyre31. To determine the activity of acid phosphatases (ACP), 2% (w/v) larval homogenate was prepared in cold acetate buffer (0.05 M, pH 5.0). The substrate solution (sodium α-naphthyl phosphate) (0.005 M) (100 µl) prepared in buffer was pre-incubated at 30 °C for 10 min. The enzyme extract (10 µl) was then added and the resultant solution was incubated at 30 °C for 30 min. To stop the reaction, a post-coupling solution containing 5:2 (v/v) sodium lauryl sulphate (4% w/v) and Fast Red TR salt (0.2% w/v) prepared in buffer was added, and absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Eon, USA) after 1 h. A standard curve was created by serially diluting 0.01 M α-naphthol to get concentrations ranging from 100 to 1000 µM and the activity was expressed in µM/mg. The extraction and estimation of alkaline phosphatases (AKP) was carried out following the same procedure as for ACP, except that 1% (w/v) larval homogenate was prepared in Tris buffer (0.05 M, pH 8.6).

Estimation of antioxidant enzymes

Superoxide dismutase (SOD)

Superoxide dismutase activity was estimated following the method described by Kono32, with slight modifications. Enzyme homogenate (10% (w/v) was prepared using 50 mM sodium carbonate buffer at pH 10.0. The reaction mixture consisted of 1.3 ml of the same buffer (pH 10.0), 0.5 ml of the enzyme extract, 0.1 ml of 0.6% Triton X-100, 0.5 ml of nitro-blue tetrazolium (NBT), and 0.1 ml of 20 mM hydroxylamine hydrochloride (pH 6.0). Absorbance was recorded at 540 nm for 5 min at 25 °C, with readings taken every minute. SOD activity was expressed in units/mg/min.

Catalase (CAT)

The activity of catalase was determined following the method described by Bergmeyer (1974)33. Larval homogenate (5% w/v) was prepared in 0.05 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and centrifuged at 2500 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant served as the enzyme extract. The assay reaction included the enzyme extract and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), and the decrease in absorbance was monitored at 240 nm for 3 min at 25 °C. Catalase activity was expressed in µM/mg/min.

Immune response

To assess the impact of bacteria on the immunological response, third instar larvae were fed castor leaves treated with the LC50 of MG1. The larvae were fed continuously for 96 h. The hemolymph was collected at 48 and 96 h intervals by puncturing the prothoracic legs with a sterile needle. The hemolymph was collected from 10 randomly selected larvae per treatment and control group. The collected hemolymph samples were pooled for each group and used to evaluate phenoloxidase (PO) and lysozyme activities. All experiments were conducted thrice.

Phenoloxidase assay

The activity of phenoloxidase was measured following the protocol described by Cotter and Wilson34. From the pooled hemolymph, 4 µl aliquot was diluted in 200 µl of phosphate-buffer saline (PBS, pH 7.4). From this mixture, 50 µl was combined with 50 µl of 20 mM L-DOPA (L-3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine). The reaction was monitored using amicroplate reader (BioTek Eon, USA) by measuring absorbance at 475 nm at 1 min interval for 30 min.

Lysozyme activity

The activity of lysozyme was measured according to de Azambuja et al.35. From the pooled hemolymph as described above, 2 µl was added to 50 µl of ice-cold PBS. Then 50 µl of hemolymph-PBS mixture was added to 150 µl of Micrococcus luteus cell suspension, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature. The absorbance change was measured on a microplate reader (BioTek Eon, USA) at 450 nm at 1 min intervals for 30 min.

Total hemocyte count (THC)

The THC in S.litura larvae was determined using the method described by Tauber and Yeager36. Hemolymph was drawn into a white blood cell pipette up to 0.5 mark and diluted to the 11 mark with Tauber-Yeager fluid (composition: (NaCl = 4.65 g, KCl = 0.15 g, CaCl2 = 0.11 g, Gentian violet = 0.005 g, Acetic acid = 0.125 ml, Distilled water = 100 ml) (Tauber and Yeager, 1934). After thoroughly shaking the pipette, the first three drops were discarded. A drop of the diluted hemolymph was then placed on a Neubauer hemocytometer, and hemocytes were counted under an Olympus microscope at 40X magnification. Counts were taken from four corner squares and one central 1 mm² square. Histological analysis

The second-instar larvae (approximately six days old) of S. litura were used to evaluate the histological effects of LC50 of MG1 on their midgut tissue. The castor leaves were surface sterilised with 5% (v/v) NaOCl solution and washed with distilled water. The surface-sterilised leaves (approximately 20 cm2) were treated by dipping in 10 ml of bacterial cell suspension for 10–15 s. After air drying, the castor leaf discs were placed in rearing vials and offered to the larvae. The control group received leaves dipped in PBS only. The leaves were changed every alternate day. Experimental conditions were same as in the section “Rearing of insects”. After 96 h feeding duration, larvae were carefully dissected under aseptic conditions, and the midgut was excised and immediately washed in distilled water. Gut specimens were then fixed in 10% formalin and stored until further processing. Three larvae were dissected each from control group and treatment group.

For histological preparation, fixed tissues were thoroughly washed in distilled water and subsequently dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%). The tissues were then embedded in paraffin wax. Once the wax blocks hardened, they were sectioned into thin ribbons using a microtome. These sections were mounted on glass slides pre-coated with a thin layer of Mayer’s egg albumin and gently warmed on a hot plate (40–45 °C) to ensure proper adhesion and spreading.

The slides were further dehydrated using ascending and descending alcohol concentrations (30–90%), and stained with hematoxylin and eosin following the protocol outlined by Dutta et al.38. Finally, each slide was examined under an Evos XL Core microscope at 20X magnification to assess midgut histopathological changes.

Comet assay

DNA damage was assessed using a modified alkaline comet assay based on Singh et al. (1988)39. Third instar larvae were fed castor leaves treated with the LC50 of cell suspension of MG1 for 96 h. Hemolymph (40 µl) was collected from punctured prolegs of ten larvae into PBS (60 µl) (pH 7.4).

For slide preparation, clean, degreased glass slides were first coated with 1% normal melting point agarose (NMPA) and left to dry at 30 °C overnight. The second layer consisted of 0.5% low melting point agarose (LMPA) mixed with 20 µl of the hemolymph sample, which was allowed to solidify in the refrigerator for 10–15 min. A final layer of 0.5% LMPA was applied, and coverslips were placed over the slides. Once the gel had solidified, the coverslips were removed, and the slides were submerged in ice-cold lysing solution containing 2.5 M NaCl, 0.25 M NaOH, 100 mM EDTA, 0.25 M Tris buffer, 1% Triton X-100, and 10% DMSO, double distilled water, pH 10.0 for a minimum of 2 h.

Following lysis, the slides were placed in an electrophoretic unit containing electrophoretic buffer (1 mM EDTA, 0.3 M NaOH, double distilled water, pH > 13.0) and subjected to electrophoresis at 25 V and 300 mA for 20 min. After electrophoresis, the slides were neutralized using Tris buffer and allowed to dry overnight in the dark. Ethidium bromide was used to stain the DNA.

The slides were then analyzed under a Nikon Eclipse E200 fluorescence microscope at 40X magnification, using a 515–560 nm excitation filter and a 590 nm barrier filter, with images captured using an Olympus camera. Three replicates were used for control and treatment groups. A total of 100 comets were randomly scored per slide using the CASP LAB image analysis software. DNA damage was evaluated based on tail length, percentage of tail DNA, tail moment, and olive tail moment.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis wascarried out by using SPSS softwarefor Windows version 16.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago) and Microsoft Office Excel 2007 (Microsoft Corp., USA). An independent Student’s t-test was used for enzymatic and comet analysis. All the biochemical parameters and comet parameters were replicated three times, and the values are represented as mean ± standard error (SE).

Results

Biochemical analysis

Digestive enzymes

The results presented in Fig. 1 showed a significant decrease in the activity of all digestive enzymes when S. litura larvae were treated with bacterial cell suspension.As the exposure time increased, the activity level further decreased. A significant effect was observed in all the enzyme activities except the β-glucosidase at 48 h.With respect to control, there was 23.05 and 56.02% drop in the levels of α-amylases when the larvae were exposed to MG1 for 48 (t = 6.57, p ≤ 0.05) and 96 h (t = 23.70, p ≤ 0.05) respectively. Similarly, α- and β-glucosidases activity significantly decreased by 69.80 and 41.93%, respectively after 96 h, against their respective controls(t = 15.63, p ≤ 0.05)(t = 23.11, p ≤ 0.05). The treated larvae showed a similar trend for α- and β-galactosidases which declined by 66.67 and 77.37% at 96 h compared to their respective controls (t = 7.09, p ≤ 0.05) (t = 19.41, p ≤ 0.05). Treatment with Rhodococcus sp. (MG1) also significantly suppressed lipase and protease activities (Fig. 1).

Detoxifying enzymes

Treatment with MG1 significantly increased the activity of detoxifying enzymes at both the time intervals, with respect to control. When larvae were fed on castor leaves treated with MG1 cell suspension for 48 and 96 h, the activity of AKP was increased by1.30 and 1.55 times compared to their respective controls(48 h: t = 19.30, p ≤ 0.05)(96 h: t = 12.56, p ≤ 0.05)(Fig. 2A). Similar trend was detected in the activity of ACP which showed 1.95 and 1.62 times rise after 48 (t = 26.75, p ≤ 0.05) and 96 h (t = 15.63, p ≤ 0.05) respectively when compared with their respective controls (Fig. 2B). Likewise, the larvae showed a slight increase of 1.10 and 1.14 times in EST activity at 48 and 96 h respectively incomparison to their respective controls(48 h: t = 3.07, p ≤ 0.05)(96 h: t = 8.75, p ≤ 0.05)(Fig. 2C). The GST levels were also found to be significantly higher in treated larvae at both the time intervals when compared to their respective controls (Fig. 2D).

Antioxidant enzymes

The activity of antioxidant enzymes of S. liturawas also assessed post MG1 treatment. The CAT levels increased after both the time intervals i.e. 48 and 96 h of MG1 treatment. At 48 h post-MG1 treatment, CAT activity was significantly elevated, showing a 1.92-fold increase (t = 14.34, p ≤ 0.05), and remained significantly higher after 96 h of treatment in comparison to their respective controls (t = 10.99, p ≤ 0.05)(Fig. 3A). A similar trend was recorded for SOD levels. There was 45.32 and 39.91% rise in SOD activity of treated larvae with respect to their control respectively after 48 (t = 17.83, p ≤ 0.05) and 96 h (t = 5.45, p ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 3B).

Immune response

The bacterial infection induced the immune response of larvae by increasing the lysozyme and phenoloxidase (PO) activity. As is evident from Fig. 4, with the increase in time duration from 48 to 96 h, the level of lysozyme increased from 7.37 to 22.98% over control (48 h: t = 2.17, p ≤ 0.05)(96 h: t = 4.84, p ≤ 0.05). A significant induction of 1.96-fold in phenoloxidase activity was also detected after 48 h of treatment (t = 10.57, p ≤ 0.05). However, after 96 h of MG1 treatment, phenoloxidase activity did not increase significantly (Fig. 4).

Histopathological analysis

Exposure of S litura larvae to MG1 resulted in pronounced histological alterations in the gut. Following MG1 infection, the midgut tissue exhibited significant morphological and cellular damage across the epithelial, peritrophic, and muscle layers. The affected cells appeared enlarged, disorganized, and showed prominent cytoplasmic cavities, with notable detachment of the epithelial layer from the basement membrane. The epithelium became irregular and showed clear signs of degradation, while the cytoplasm was highly vacuolized and the peritrophic membrane showed disruption (Fig. 5). In contrast, the well-conserved muscle and epithelial layers were observed in unexposed control larvae.

Comet assay

The larvae exposed to bacterial cell suspension for 96 h exhibited a marked increase in all the four comet parameters. An increase of 73.98% was observed in tail length compared to control. Discussion

Rhodococcus sp. (MG1) has been documented to cause larval mortality, growth inhibition as well as alteration in nutritional physiology of S. litura14. The present study revealed biochemical, histological and genotoxic effects of MG1 on S. litura larvae. There was a significant reduction in the activity of key digestive enzymes, including amylases, glucosidases, galactosidases, lipases, and proteases in S. litura larvae treated with bacterial cell suspension. Our findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that bacterial exposure can significantly impair the digestive physiology of insect pests by targeting midgut tissues and associated enzymatic functions29. Infections with Bt, Photorhabdus temperata, and S. marcescens in species such as H. armigera, Plutella xylostella (Linnaeus), and Anticarsia gemmatalis (Hubner) result in reduced digestive enzyme levels and damage to midgut epithelial cells. These effects further impair nutrient absorption and overall gut function40–42. In the current study, treatment of S. litura larvae with MG1 cell suspension reduced the activity levels of α- and β-glucosidases and galactosidases. Previously, Zibaee et al.41. and Carneiro et al.43. reported similar variations in the activity of α and β-glucosidases and galactosidases of Diatraea saccharalis (Fabricius) and Hyphantria cunea (Drury) larvae treated with P. temperate and Bt. On the other hand, there was upregulation of both detoxifying (GST, EST, ACP and AKP) and antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT) of S. litura larvae on exposure to cell suspension of Rhodococcus sp. The increased activity of acid and alkaline phosphatases aligns with findings of Kamel et al.44 that showed induction in phosphatase activity of Spodoptera littoralis (Boisduval) larvae treated with Bt formulations (Dipel 2X and Dipel 2 F). Similarly, enhanced levels of GST were detected in Galleria mellonella (Linnaeus) and S. litura infected with Bt and S. marcescens, as part of a generalised oxidative stress and detoxification response45,46. Specifically, in G. mellonella, Bt infection was shown to significantly augment GST and SOD activity in the midgut shortly after inoculation, supporting the idea of a rapid redox-mediated defense45,46. The elevated EST activity reflects the larvae’s increased hydrolysis of ester-based toxins. It is consistent with earlier suggestions that esterase induction enables detoxification of ester-containing xenobiotics47,48.

Our findings further reveal that MG1 infection triggers a marked increase in the key antioxidant enzymes SOD and CAT, indicative of heightened oxidative stress in treated larvae compared to controls. SOD and CAT serve as frontline defenses against reactive oxygen species, preserving cellular redox balance49–51. Induction of SOD level has earlier been documented in S. litura and G. mellonella following Bt or S. marcescens infection45,46. Elevated CAT activity has been widely reported as a downstream response to ROS overproduction52.

The present findings also demonstrated an immune response in S. litura larvae following infection with the MG1, as evidenced by elevated levels of phenoloxidase and lysozyme activity. These findings are consistent with previous reports documenting enhanced phenoloxidase and lysozyme levels following microbial infections in various insect species. For instance, G. mellonella, Trichoplusia ni (Hubner), and Colorado potato beetle larvae exhibited increased hemolymph phenoloxidase and lysozyme levels shortly after oral infection with Bt53–55. Other studies have reported similar immunological upregulation in G. mellonella, S. litura, and Antheraea assamensis (Helfer) upon exposure to pathogens such as K. pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterobacter cloacae, Staphylococcus aureus, and S.marcescens46,56–59. However, contrary to these observations, some studies have documented decreased phenoloxidase activity in response to specific bacterial infections60,61, suggesting pathogen-specific modulation of the host immune system. Histological study revealed pronounced disruption of the peritrophic matrix, degeneration of microvilli, and damage to midgut epithelial cells in S. lituralarvae following MG1 infection. These structural alterations suggest a compromised gut barrier that facilitated the systemic spread of the pathogen, causing septicemia as documented by Mehra et al.14. Similar observations have been reported following oral ingestion of toxins from bacterial pathogens such as Photorhabdus akhurstii, K. pneumoniae, Thauera sp., Shewanella sp., and Pseudomonas paralactis, which caused gut epithelial damage and systemic infection in Spodoptera frugiperda (J E Smith), S. litura, and H. armigera larvae61–65. The peritrophic matrix plays a critical protective role in insect digestion by shielding the gut epithelium from physical and microbial damage66. Breach of this barrier allows opportunistic pathogens to proliferate in the hemolymph, as demonstrated with Thauera sp. and K. pneumoniae infections in S. litura64,67, ultimately leading to host mortality due to systemic bacterial spread. Similar gut disruption and systemic infection have also been documented in many lepidopterans following Bt infection68. Such histopathological changes likely impair the enzyme–substrate interaction and reduce gut peristalsis, ultimately limiting digestive efficiency and nutrient absorption29. In addition, a reduction in total hemocyte count was recorded in S. litura larvae post-infection with MG1. This decline in THC corroborates earlier findings where oral or systemic infections led to reduced hemocyte numbers in insects such as Acheta domesticus (Linnaeus), G. mellonella, and S. litura, following exposure to Xenorhabdus nematophila, Actinobacillus pleuropneumoniae, E. cloacae, and Escherichia coli K169–71,59. Together, these findings suggest that while certain immune parameters such as phenoloxidase and lysozyme activity are elevated during microbial challenge, other aspects like THC may be suppressed, possibly due to immune evasion strategies of pathogen.

The comet assay results revealed that exposure to MG1 induced significant genotoxic effects in S. litura larvae, as evidenced by increased DNA damage in hemocytes. The formation of comet tails suggests DNA damage, activation of apoptosis and a compromised cellular integrity. Although genotoxicity of plant- and fungal-derived compounds on insects has been reported previously72,73, limited information is available regarding bacterial toxin-induced DNA damage. Oberholster et al.74. reported DNA damage in Periplaneta Americana (Linnaeus) and Tenebrio molitor (Linnaeus) induced by cyanobacterial metabolites such as microcystin-LR. Recent reports documented genotoxicity of Atlantibacter hermannii and Klebsiella sp. in Culex pipiens (Linnaeus)75. Cho and Kim76, reported DNA fragmentation in fifth instar larvae of Bombyx mori (Linnaeus) infected with X. nematophila and P. temperata. The bacterial toxins are likely to induce both single- and double-strand DNA breaks, leading to cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, or necrosis77. Nuclear fragmentation, observed as tail formation during the comet assay, further supports the progression of hemocyte apoptosis78. The genotoxic effects, as indicated by DNA damage in the comet assay, are likely associated with systemic oxidative stress generated during bacterial infection, where excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) can damage cellular components, including DNA77. In addition, certain bacterial metabolites and toxins may contribute directly or indirectly to DNA damage by disrupting cellular homeostasis and inducing inflammatory responses74. Hemocytes, being the primary immune cells, are highly susceptible to such oxidative and toxic stress. Therefore, the observed genotoxicity may result from a combination of oxidative stress and bacterial pathogenic factors rather than a single specific bacterial genotoxin.

Conclusion

Our study reveals that MG1 causes substantial histopathological, biochemical and genetic disruptions in S. litura. Weakening of immune system and oxidative stress might further impair various physiological processes and ultimately adversely affect its survival. Therefore, Rhodococcus sp. appears promising as a biocontrol agent against insect pests; however, further research is necessary to optimize bacterial mass production techniques and evaluate their effectiveness under field conditions.
