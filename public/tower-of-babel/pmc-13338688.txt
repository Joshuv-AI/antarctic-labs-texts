Survey on COVID-19 Vaccination among Pediatric Cancer Patients and Their Parents: A Single-Center Study from Korea
Authors: Areum Shin, Doo Ri Kim, Hee Young Ju, Ji Won Lee, Keon Hee Yoo, Ki Woong Sung, Yae-Jean Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmunocompromised children are a high-risk group for severe coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19). A nationwide study in Korea found that the average COVID-19 vaccination rate in high-risk children was higher than in the general pediatric population, whereas vaccination rates in pediatric cancer patients were lower.Materials and MethodsSurveys on COVID-19 vaccination among pediatric cancer patients and parents were conducted during the pandemic (July 1, 2022–July 31, 2022) and post-pandemic (December 26, 2023–January 5, 2024) periods. Vaccination rates of patients and their parents were compared with general population data. Factors associated with the vaccination status of patients were identified. Parental reasons for not vaccinating their children were also assessed.ResultsIn the first survey of 111 patients, the two-dose COVID-19 vaccination rate was 28.8%, lower than the rate in the general pediatric population, 40.7% (P=0.028). The two-dose vaccination rates for fathers (90.1%) and mothers (85.6%) were also lower than those of the general adult population (96.4%) (P<0.001). In the second survey of 26 patients, two patients received seasonal COVID-19 vaccination (7.7%). Among patients aged 12–19 years from the first survey, those in the younger age group, hematopoietic cell transplantation recipients, or those with parental under-vaccination status were more likely to be unvaccinated. Parental concerns about vaccine adverse reactions were a primary factor in their decision not to vaccinate their children.ConclusionConsidering the negative attitude toward the COVID-19 vaccine among parents of children with cancer, the government and healthcare professionals should endeavor to increase parental trust in this novel vaccine platform.Keywords: COVID-19, Vaccination, Child, Cancer, Parents

Introduction

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) has caused one of the most significant pandemics in recent human history, causing tremendous suffering and loss [1,2]. However, breakthrough scientific progress was also achieved during the pandemic, especially in vaccines. In 2023, the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was given to researchers who made key contributions to the development of effective mRNA vaccines against COVID-19 in such a short time after the virus sequence was released [3].

Although the end of the pandemic was declared in May 2023, COVID-19 remains a major threat to public health, and vaccination recommendations continue as a seasonal vaccine in many countries [4,5,6]. Although the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has recently lowered the recommendation level of COVID-19 immunization among children aged 6 months through 17 years, vaccination is still recommended for those who are moderately or severely immunocompromised [7]. The Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) recommends the COVID-19 vaccine to high-risk people aged 6 months and older [8].

Pediatric cancer patients represent high-risk, immunocompromised children with a considerable COVID-19 disease burden [9,10,11,12,13,14]. However, COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy has become widespread among parents, and its impact appears disproportionate. In Korea, based on the National Health Insurance Service database as of July 2022, the average two-dose vaccination rate of 12–17-year-olds with underlying comorbidities (76.5%) was higher than that of the general child population of the same age (66.1%), while pediatric cancer patients of the same age had the second lowest rates (52.1%) among high-risk adolescents [15].

Since immunocompromised patients were primarily excluded from clinical trials of COVID-19 vaccine development, questions about the safety and efficacy of the vaccine in this population were common during the early pandemic [16]. However, several pieces of data have been accumulated on the safety and effectiveness of the COVID-19 vaccine in cancer patients, indicating its benefit not only in terms of reducing the infection or severity of COVID-19 and its potential complication of multisystem inflammatory syndrome, but also in cancer patient-specific circumstances such as delays in chemotherapy or transplantation [17,18,19,20,21,22]. In addition, millions of children have been safely vaccinated globally [23,24].

We investigated COVID-19 vaccination among pediatric cancer patients and their parents at our center during and after the pandemic to find ways to improve vaccine acceptance in this vulnerable population.

Materials and methods

1. Study participants

This study investigated COVID-19 vaccination among pediatric cancer patients and their parents who visited Samsung Medical Center (SMC) in Seoul, Korea. The first survey was performed from July 1 to July 31, 2022, during the COVID-19 pandemic period. This was 10 months, eight months, and three months after age-specific COVID-19 vaccine implementation in Korea for all individuals 18–19 years (August 26, 2021), all children 12–17 years (16–17 years on October 18, 2021, 12–15 years on November 1, 2021), and high-risk children who were 5–11 years (March 31, 2022), respectively. Third-dose vaccination was recommended for high-risk children aged 12–17 years and for people over 18 years on March 14, 2022. Participants were divided into three age groups for the first survey: Group 1 (5–11 years), Group 2 (12–17 years), and Group 3 (18–19 years).

The second survey was conducted from December 26, 2023 to January 5, 2024, during the post-pandemic period. This was approximately two months after the first 2023–2024 seasonal COVID-19 vaccination was recommended for high-risk individuals aged 12 years and older on October 19, 2023, and subsequently extended to high-risk children aged 6 months and older on November 1, 2023. Notably, COVID-19 vaccination has been recommended for high-risk infants aged 6 months to 4 years since February 20, 2023. Therefore, participants were expanded to include patients aged 6 months to 19 years for the second survey, and a new group, Group 0 (6 months–4 years), was added.

2. Ethics statement

The study was approved by the SMC Institutional Review Board to ensure research ethics, including patient confidentiality (2022-07-095).

3. Data collection

The survey papers were freely available from the reception desk and were returned by interested patients and parents. The survey questions were designed to be concise, enabling participants to complete them during their outpatient clinic waiting periods. Questions were divided into three sections. First, patient information, including age, sex, and cancer-related characteristics such as cancer diagnosis (hematologic malignancy vs. solid cancer), current treatment status (ongoing chemotherapy vs. finished chemotherapy vs. palliative care), and history of hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT) (allogenic vs. autologous) was collected. Second, COVID-19 vaccination status was obtained for patients and their parents. Third, if the child was unvaccinated, the reasons of his or her parents were investigated via multiple-choice questions with eight possible responses adapted from a previous study [25]. Responders were allowed to choose one or more options. The eight options were as follows: a distrust in COVID-19 vaccine effectiveness, expecting COVID-19 to be a mild disease, a belief that social preventive measures alone are sufficient, recovered from COVID-19 in the past, concerns about COVID-19 vaccine adverse reactions, difficulties in post-vaccination care, inability to choose the vaccine desired, and distrust of vaccination in general [25]. Detailed information on the survey can be found in the Supplementary material. For the second survey, the same questionnaire form was used, and a question about seasonal COVID-19 vaccination history was added.

4. Data display of reasons for parents not vaccinating their children

Eight reasons for parents not vaccinating their children against COVID-19 were displayed in a radar chart, referenced in a clockwise descending order of response rates. Each rate was calculated by dividing the number of responders in each item by the total number of responders. The same data were also visualized in pie charts, displaying the proportions of each item among the total responses.

5. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables as numbers with corresponding proportions. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare differences between continuous variables, while the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables. The COVID-19 vaccination rate of each group was compared to age-matched general pediatric population data from the KDCA as of July 14, 2022, using a one-sample test of proportions. The overall parental two-dose vaccination rate was also analyzed, based on KDCA data for individuals aged 30–59 years. Univariate analysis with logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with COVID-19 vaccination status among pediatric cancer patients. Multivariable logistic regression was applied to calculate the adjusted odds ratio (aOR) with the 95% confidence interval (CI).

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). A P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

1. COVID-19 vaccination status of pediatric cancer patients and their parents

A total of 137 pairs of patients and their parents participated in the surveys, comprising 111 pairs in the first survey and 26 pairs in the second survey. During the first survey, the overall participation rate was 18.9% (106/561); 15.2% among parents of children ages 5–11, 21.8% among parents of children ages 12–17, and 23.0% among parents of children ages 18–19. During the second survey, the overall participation rate decreased to 7.5% (26/348); 11.2% among parents of children ages 6 months to 4, 8.6% among parents of children ages 5–11, 4.7% among parents of children ages 12–17, and 2.9% among parents of children ages 18–19.

Patient characteristics for each survey are summarized in Table 1. The median ages were 13.6 and 6.5 years, and patients with hematologic malignancies comprised 58.8% and 30.8% of the total, respectively. The male-to-female ratio and the proportion of HCT recipients were similar across the surveys. Among the 15 HCT recipients of the first survey (12–19 years only), the median post-transplantation period at the time of the survey was 1.8 years (IQR, 0.6–5.1 years). Thirteen patients (13/15, 86%) had been at least 3 months after HCT, a period recommended for COVID-19 vaccination by the Infectious Disease Society of America. Only three of them received the vaccination, all of whom were more than 24 months after HCT.

In the first survey, the two-dose COVID-19 vaccination rate among total pediatric cancer patients was 28.8%, significantly lower than the 40.7% rate in the general pediatric population with a similar age group proportion (P=0.028) (Fig. 1). Subgroup comparisons also showed a similar trend (Groups 1, 2, and 3). In addition, the two-dose vaccination rates for fathers and mothers were 90.1% and 85.6%, respectively, which were lower than the 96.4% rate of the general adult population aged 30-59 years (P<0.001) (Fig. 1). The rate of both parents completing the two doses was 80.2% (Table 1).

In the second survey, only two patients received the 2023–2024 seasonal COVID-19 vaccine, with a total vaccination rate of 7.7% (Table 1). Their parental vaccination rates were 34.6% for fathers and 26.9% for mothers. Further statistical analysis was not performed on data from the second survey because only a small number of responses were retrieved.

2. Factors associated with the COVID-19 vaccination status of pediatric cancer patients aged 12-19

The factors associated with the COVID-19 vaccination status of pediatric cancer patients were determined in patients aged 12 to 19 years from the first survey. Group 1 was excluded because no one had received the vaccination. Univariate logistic regression analysis showed that younger ages of 12 to 15 years, HCT recipients, or parental COVID-19 under-vaccinated status were significantly associated with unvaccinated status (Table 2). In contrast, cancer type or current treatment status were not related to patient vaccination status.

3. Reasons why parents did not vaccinate their children with cancer against COVID-19

In the first survey, 61 of 77 parents who did not vaccinate their children responded (79.2%), with an average of 2.2 reasons, as presented in Supplementary Figure 1. Concerns about COVID-19 vaccine adverse reactions (44 of 61 parents, 72.1%) were the most common, followed by distrust in COVID-19 vaccine effectiveness (20 of 61, 32.7%), a belief that social preventive measures alone are sufficient (20 out of 61, 32.8%) and recovery from COVID-19 in the past (17 of 61, 27.9%) (Fig. 2A). In the second survey, 19 of 24 parents who did not vaccinate their children responded (79.2%), with an average of 2.6 reasons, as presented in Supplementary Figure 1. Of these, 31.2% of the parents from the second survey (6 of 19) provided more than three reasons, compared to 11.5% of the parents from the first survey (7 of 61). For individual reasons, a similar pattern was observed, predominated by concerns about COVID-19 vaccine adverse reactions (Fig. 2A).

Compared to the first survey, there was a tendency toward decreased concern regarding vaccine adverse reactions (33.6% vs. 26.0%), while a tendency toward increased difficulty with post-vaccination care (5.3% vs. 10.0%) and distrust regarding vaccination in general (9.2% vs. 12.0%) was observed, but none of these were statistically significant (Fig. 2B).

Discussion

Our study highlighted that vaccination status among pediatric cancer patients was particularly low, even considering universal parental hesitancy in pediatric COVID-19 vaccination. We observed that the two-dose COVID-19 vaccination rate among 111 pediatric cancer patients was less than one-third, significantly lower than that of the age-matched general population as of July 2022. Their parents also exhibited a lower two-dose vaccination rate compared to the general adult population. Only two of 26 patients received seasonal COVID-19 vaccination as of mid-January 2024. Among patients aged 12–19 years from the first survey, younger age, HCT recipients, or under-vaccinated status of parents were significantly associated with unvaccinated status. Parental reasons for not vaccinating their children focused on concerns about vaccine adverse reactions. These findings, along with the previous nationwide study, suggest that the government’s COVID-19 vaccination recommendations targeting high-risk children have been less effective in persuading parents of cancer patients.

Only a few studies have focused on the COVID-19 vaccination status of pediatric cancer patients, in contrast to adult cancer patients [26,27,28,29,30]. In most cases, vaccination rates were not compared to the general pediatric population, which made it difficult to evaluate the impact of different pediatric COVID-19 vaccination policies across countries [26,27]. A more systematic approach seems necessary to assess global acceptance of the COVID-19 vaccine among pediatric cancer patients.

Many studies have examined sociodemographic factors influencing the willingness of parents to vaccinate their children with cancer against COVID-19 [26,27,31,32,33,34]. Overall, parents who were hesitant to vaccinate their children were often described as young parents with young children, parents with low levels of education, or parents with low household incomes, in addition to those with their own low COVID-19 vaccination rates. These findings were also consistent with previous Korean studies targeting parents in general [35,36]. In our research, it was also observed that parental under-vaccination and younger patient age were associated with the unvaccinated status of pediatric cancer patients. However, we did not examine other sociodemographic factors, such as household incomes or parents' education levels.

Our study identified that receiving HCT was an important factor in pediatric cancer patients not being vaccinated against COVID-19. This contrasts with previous studies on COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy among parents of pediatric HCT recipients: a national survey conducted in the U.S. reported low hesitancy scores, and a single-center study from Canada found no significant impact [27,34]. Moreover, we found an unexpected association between HCT recipients and their parents’ under-vaccination status. The parents of HCT recipients may face practical constraints in obtaining the COVID-19 vaccination since they are being tied to care for their sick children. However, this did not appear to be a significant factor, as the median post-transplantation period for the study patients had already exceeded a year, passing the acute intensive stage. In addition, domestic accessibility to COVID-19 immunization was high in Korea, ensured by vaccination rates of over 95% among adults.

These vaccination behaviors observed in Korean parents of pediatric cancer patients are challenging to understand. We propose that a negatively skewed attitude toward COVID-19 vaccines prevailed among these parents. Jeanine et al. reported that the parents of children with cancer were significantly more vulnerable to COVID-19 misinformation than the parents of children without cancer [37]. The authors reasoned that these parents likely experienced higher pandemic stress, which led to extensive online searches with impaired critical thinking. From this perspective, vulnerability to misinformation among these parents, especially those of HCT recipients, may have contributed to negative attitudes toward COVID-19 vaccines, affecting vaccination behavior for themselves and their children. Indeed, parents of HCT recipients reported a higher proportion of concerns about COVID-19 vaccine adverse reactions than parents of non-HCT recipients, which might reflect a more negative attitude, though the difference was not statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. 2B).

A recent national study found that providing additional authoritative information about the COVID-19 vaccine via an online platform was effective in reducing vaccine hesitancy among parents in general [38]. This approach may not apply to parents of cancer patients, however, who may already hold negative biases toward the vaccine due to an inherent vulnerability to misinformation.

We suggest that messages be delivered more directly to these parents through conversations with physicians, a method already supported by many studies [26,27,31,32,33,34]. A global survey among parents of children with cancer also emphasized the importance of building an alliance with parents before recommending COVID-19 vaccination [39,40]. To facilitate this shared decision-making process, the government should foster a healthcare environment that encourages open conversation, acknowledging that low uptake of COVID-19 vaccines among pediatric cancer patients is not solely the responsibility of parents.

As with the influenza vaccination, the COVID-19 vaccination during the cancer clinic visit would be the most effective way to persuade these parents, rather than through the separate primary care clinic visits, which lack authority over them. However, leading the conversation to overcome vaccine hesitancy among negatively skewed parents within a limited clinic time would be a burden for most oncology physicians who have less experience compared to influenza vaccines. We suggest a 2-stage system: first, oncology physicians should provide a brief but firm indication to eligible patients and their parents that COVID-19 vaccination is the default option. Second, transfer those parents who remain hesitant but wish to discuss further to the infectious disease clinic.

We also investigated the psychological aspects of parents that impacted their decision not to vaccinate their children. Although the demographics of patients differed between the first and second surveys, the priorities among parental reasons for not vaccinating their children were consistently focused on concerns regarding vaccine adverse reactions, as reflected in studies of parents in general [35].

However, the proportion of concerns about vaccine adverse reactions among total responses showed a decrease in the second survey. Several factors may have contributed, including differences in survey timing or patient demographics. In either case, this illustrates that understanding the dynamics of parental psychology requires continuous monitoring, which might provide key information facilitating effective communication.

This study has a few limitations. First, because this was a single-center study, it may not represent the whole picture of the national data. However, pediatric cancer patients were reported as having the second-lowest COVID-19 vaccination rate among various underlying groups of Korean children from national data [15]. Therefore, our observation may still be meaningful for consideration. Second, this was a voluntary outpatient survey, and selection bias could have occurred because parents with stronger views (either pro or cons) on COVID-19 vaccination may have been more likely to respond. This could have influenced the estimated vaccination rate. Third, during the second survey (when negative information had accumulated further), several parents with stronger negative views on COVID-19 vaccination doubted the researchers’ intention of the survey, reacted aggressively, and refused to participate. This eventually led to the premature discontinuation of the second survey. Strategies to improve the survey participation rate should be sought in future studies. The fourth limitation is that lower vaccination rates among HCT recipients should be interpreted with caution, because vaccination in this group is strongly influenced by clinical eligibility, timing after transplantation, and physician recommendations, rather than parental attitudes alone. In our study, the majority of patients passed the early post-transplantation period but did not receive the COVID-19 vaccine, though they were eligible for the vaccination. We did not investigate other clinical scenarios that may prevent these patients from receiving the COVID-19 vaccine, nor whether they received the COVID-19 vaccination recommendation from their physician. Fifth, we did not include ‘not eligible for vaccination’ as a response option on the survey sheet. Although there exist situations in which patients cannot receive vaccination (induction chemotherapy, early post-HCT period less than 3 months, post-rituximab treatment within 6 months, etc.), as with influenza vaccines, most cancer patients can receive the COVID-19 vaccine between chemotherapy cycles. Accordingly, to assess whether the decision of ineligibility is medically valid, the reasons their physicians gave for deferring COVID-19 vaccination should be collected simultaneously. Sixth, the COVID-19 vaccination status of the study participants was not compared with the immunization status of conventional vaccines, such as influenza. Of note, Korea maintained a high participation rate in the national immunization program even during the COVID-19 pandemic.

In conclusion, despite the recommendations of the COVID-19 vaccination policy for high-risk children, several parents of cancer patients in this small-scale single-center study did not fully vaccinate their child or themselves for COVID-19. Negative attitudes toward the COVID-19 vaccine among these parents should be considered, and we suggest shifting our perspective from viewing them as objects of education with simple message delivery to conversation partners. Continuous efforts from the government and healthcare professionals are required to increase parental confidence in the novel vaccine platform for this vulnerable group of children.
