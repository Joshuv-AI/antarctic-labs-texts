Rural variations in primary care prostate cancer diagnosis and survival: a cohort study using linked Australian primary care electronic medical record data
Authors: Olivia Wawryk, Ian M. Collins, Alex Lee, Mark Buzza, Simonne Neil, Jessica Freeman, Jon Emery, Meena Rafiq
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcross the state of Victoria, Australia around one-third of residents live in rural areas. There are persisting disparities and poorer prostate cancer outcomes in rural areas compared with metropolitan areas. Prostate Specific Antigen (PSA) blood tests are commonly used in primary care to investigate patients with suspected prostate cancer. Understanding variations in PSA test use and follow-up of abnormal results between rural and metropolitan areas could identify potential causes of disparities in prostate cancer detection and outcomes.MethodsWe conducted a longitudinal cohort study using Australian primary care data linked to cancer registry data. Male patients aged ≥ 18 years, diagnosed with prostate cancer between 30 June 2010 and 31 July 2022 were included if they visited primary care in the year pre-diagnosis and had a primary care PSA test. Logistic regression was used to compare demographic and tumour characteristics between patients in rural and metropolitan areas and the occurrence and timing of abnormal PSA results pre-diagnosis. Poisson regression models were used to estimated trends in PSA test use pre-diagnosis. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to quantify overall and cancer-specific survival rates.ResultsThe study population consisted of 2,470 patients, (52% rural, 48% metropolitan). Rural patients had higher Gleason scores and were almost twice as likely to have significantly elevated (> 20ng/mL) PSA levels at diagnosis. There were prolonged diagnostic intervals (the time between first abnormal PSA test and cancer diagnosis) of 7 months in metropolitan patients and 8 months in rural patients. In both rural and metropolitan areas, two-thirds of patients with a PSA > 3ng/ml did not have guideline concordant care (a repeat PSA test within 3 months).ConclusionsRural Victorians with prostate cancer have higher PSA levels and Gleason scores at diagnosis and experience longer diagnostic intervals compared to metropolitan patients. In both rural and metropolitan areas, abnormal PSA tests are frequently detected several months before prostate cancer diagnosis and there is low guideline concordance regarding repeat tests. Targeted interventions to identify and action abnormal PSA blood tests results in primary care could expedite prostate cancer diagnosis and reduce rural-metropolitan disparities in prostate cancer outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15044-5.

Background

Prostate cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in Australian men, with 24,217 incident diagnoses nationally in 2022 [1]. In the state of Victoria, prostate cancer accounts for approximately one third of all newly diagnosed cancers in men, and it is a leading cause of cancer death [2]. Across Victoria, an estimated one-third of residents reside in rural and remote areas [3]. While prostate cancer incidence is similar in metropolitan and rural areas [4], there are persisting disparities in the outcomes and treatment patterns of men in rural areas compared to their metropolitan counterparts [5]. Existing studies have reported mortality rates up to 35% higher in rural patients [5, 6] and lower rates of curative treatments compared to patients residing in metropolitan areas [7].

Rural variation in prostate cancer outcomes could be due to variations in access to treatments, differences in tumour biology or diagnostic delays. They could also be influenced by patient factors regarding health literacy and the costs of accessing care. Access to healthcare significantly varies between rural and metropolitan Australia, with rural communities often experiencing long travel distances, poorer access to primary and specialist health services and more financial barriers than people living in major cities [3, 8, 9]. Differences in the pre-diagnostic phase between metropolitan areas and rural areas have not previously been explored. Most prostate cancer patients first present to their General Practitioner (GP), with prostate-specific antigen (PSA) blood tests widely used to investigate for possible prostate cancer in primary care, either due to lower urinary tract symptoms or for asymptomatic early detection [10]. There is a need to examine primary care activity, particularly relating to PSA use and follow up of abnormal results, prior to prostate cancer diagnosis in rural and metropolitan Victoria to identify if any variation exists in the diagnostic process that could contribute to disparities in outcomes. We therefore conducted a study using linked Victorian cancer registry and primary care data to explore potential pre-diagnostic causes for variations in prostate cancer outcomes between men living in rural versus metropolitan areas of Victoria.

Methods

Aim

The aim of this study was to examine rural and metropolitan variations in the use of PSA tests among patients diagnosed with prostate cancer, including referrals at different thresholds, concordance with clinical guidelines for repeat testing, length of diagnostic intervals between abnormal test results and cancer diagnosis, and associations between PSA level and stage and survival.

Design and datasets

We conducted a longitudinal cohort study utilising Australian linked primary care data and cancer registry data. This project identified Victorian men newly diagnosed with prostate cancer between 30 June 2010 and 31 July 2022, as recorded in the Victorian Cancer Registry (VCR) using International Classification of Disease (ICD10) code C61. The VCR is a population-based cancer registry for the state of Victoria, Australia, which collects data on the date of diagnosis, tumour anatomical location, morphology, grade, behaviour and patient demographics (including rurality) [11]. The Centre for Victorian Data Linkage (CVDL) linked the VCR data to the National Death Index dataset, and the Patron (Primary Care Audit, Teaching and Research Open Network) primary care dataset via the application of iterative deterministic linkage. The Patron primary care dataset is managed by the Department of General Practice and Primary Care at the University of Melbourne [12]. It contains data from over 130 Victorian general practices on 1.8 million patients between 1996 and 2022. Data from the 2016 Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) Census was used to compare the study cohort with the broader Victorian population [13].

Defining the outcome and study population

Men aged ≥ 18 diagnosed with an incident prostate cancer in the VCR during the study period were included in the analyses if they had at least one primary care visit recorded in a linked Patron practice in the year prior to diagnosis, and at least one GP requested PSA test in the two years prior to diagnosis. Data were extracted from the VCR on date of diagnosis, tumour characteristics (Gleason grade (used to determine International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) group) and VCR-defined stage at diagnosis), socioeconomic status (using the Index of Relative Socio-economic disadvantage (IRSD) socio-economic indexes for areas (SEIFA) quintiles) and ASG (Australian Statistical Geography Standard) Statistical Area 2 (SA2) region codes. Registry defined stage was derived from pathology reports, hospital notifications and reported stage from hospital and radiotherapy services, and is based on TNM data (see Supplement A) [2]. Although PSA and Gleason scores may have been used to determine stage in some patients where available, in some cases stage would have been determined without consideration of PSA and Gleason scores at diagnosis. The Victorian Cancer Registry therefore recommend using ISUP to describe prostate cancer stage in their reports [2]. SA2 region codes were used to define whether patients resided in a rural or metropolitan area at the time of diagnosis. This was done using established methods to map SA2 region codes to their corresponding Remoteness Areas (Accessibility/Remoteness Index of Australia - ARIA), categorised as Major City, Inner Regional, Outer Regional, Remote and Very Remote [14]. For this study major city was classified as metropolitan and all other categories were classified as rural, as per previous studies [15]. Date of death was extracted from the linked National Death Index dataset which provided cause of death using ICDO-3 topography. Prostate cancer specific mortality was defined using ICDO-3 code C619.

Defining the explanatory variables

For eligible patients, data were extracted from the Patron primary care dataset on patient demographics (date of birth, sex) and all PSA blood tests (date and result) requested in primary care in the five years before prostate cancer diagnosis. Where more than one test result was reported for the same patient on the same day the mean of the values was used. Results were categorised as normal or abnormal using different cut off values (>3, >4, >10 and >20 ng/ml) to take into account variation in practice and changes in referral thresholds which occurred after publication of national consensus guidelines in 2016, which considered a PSA >3ng/ml to be abnormal [16].

Statistical analysis

Logistic regression analyses were used to describe the patient and tumour characteristics of the study cohort, comparing patients in rural versus metropolitan areas and adjusting for age and socioeconomic status (IRSD quintile). Census and VCR data were used to compare the distribution of age and IRSD quintile in the study population with the broader Victorian population and all patients diagnosed with prostate cancer in Victoria, respectively.

Prostate cancer test use and frequency of abnormal results were compared in patients residing in rural and metropolitan areas for sequential 6-month time periods pre-diagnosis (0–6 months, 6–12 months, 12–18 months and 18–24 months) using logistic regression, adjusting for age, socioeconomic status and diagnosis year. This was repeated using different thresholds for categorising results as abnormal. Median (alongside interquartile range (IQR)) PSA levels for each time period were calculated for rural and metropolitan patients, with linear regression used to examine trends in PSA level over time over 5 years pre-diagnosis in both rural and metropolitan areas stratified by age group.

National guidelines currently recommend repeat PSA testing within 3 months in patients with a PSA result ≥ 3ng/ml [16]. Therefore, the proportion of patients who had a repeat PSA test after an abnormal result was calculated, alongside the median number of days between repeat tests following an abnormal result comparing practice in rural and metropolitan areas. The median number of days between the first abnormal PSA test result (in the 2 years pre-diagnosis) and prostate cancer diagnosis was then calculated using different thresholds (PSA >3, >4, >10, >20 ng/ml) for metropolitan and rural patients. Prior to introduction of the national consensus guidelines for PSA testing in 2016, various other recommendations for PSA testing existed. These included recommending further investigation in men with an PSA >3.0ng/ml [17] and retesting PSA within 1–3 months [18]. Given the similarity between the 2016 guidelines and these pre-existing recommendations, guideline concordance was examined across the study period. A sensitivity analysis was also conducted restricting the study period to 2016 onwards to ensure no differences in results.

To examine trends over time in primary care diagnostic activity before prostate cancer diagnosis, Poisson regression models were used to calculate the monthly rate of PSA test requests for the 24 months pre-diagnosis. The ‘diagnostic window’, which represents the time point when primary care activity first starts to increase before diagnosis, was estimated using established methods [19–21]. Briefly, this involved running a series of Poisson regression models with sequential monthly inflection points and selecting the model with the best fit for estimating when PSA testing rates in primary care first start to increase from the background rate at 24 months pre-diagnosis. This was conducted separately for patients in rural and metropolitan areas. Univariable logistic regression was used to assess associations between PSA results, ISUP group and survival in both patient cohorts. Overall survival and cancer-specific survival were visualised by applying the Kaplan-Meier method using both univariable analysis and multivariable analysis adjusting for age.

Ethical approval for this project was provided by the Melbourne Health Human Research Ethics committee. Research governance approval was obtained from the independent Patron Data Governance Committee.

Results

A total of 2,470 patients with prostate cancer were included in the study (Supplementary Fig. 1). 1,193 (48%) resided in metropolitan Victoria and 1,277 (52%) in rural Victoria. Patients living in rural areas were more likely to be older (47% aged ≥ 60 versus 33%, respectively), with a mean age at diagnosis of 69 years in rural areas compared to 66 years in metropolitan. Rural patients were more likely to reside in an area of greater economic & social disadvantage than those living in metropolitan areas (31% in the most disadvantaged IRSD quintile vs. 9%, respectively) (Table 1). This compares with general trends in age and SEIFA distributions observed in the broader Victorian population when comparing rural and metropolitan areas, although metropolitan patients in our cohort were slightly more socio-economically advantaged and rural patients were more disadvantaged than the general Victorian population (Supplementary Fig. 2). The age at diagnosis for the study cohort shows a similar distribution to the age of diagnosis for prostate cancer patients in the broader Victorian population (Supplementary Fig. 3), with diagnoses mostly occurring between 60 and 80 years of age, and very few in the under 40 age group. In Victoria, prostate cancer patients in metropolitan areas are younger than those in rural areas.

Tumour characteristics

Tumour stage at diagnosis was comparable between patients living in rural and metropolitan areas, with 28% in rural areas diagnosed at a late stage (stage 3 or 4) and 31% of patients in metropolitan areas (stage data were missing for 34–36% of patients) (Table 1). In the VCR dataset Gleason scores were largely missing for patients after 2019, therefore a subgroup of patients with a Gleason score recorded between 2011 and 2019 (n = 1,537) were analysed. In this subgroup, rural patients were almost two-fold as likely to have high grade disease at diagnosis than their metropolitan counterparts (Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) 1.86, 95%CI (1.32–2.41) p < 0.001), with 24% having a Gleason ≥ 8 compared to 14% in metropolitan areas (Table 1) and were two-fold more likely to be diagnosed in the highest ISUP group (AOR 2.03, 95%CI (1.34–3.08) p < 0.001). Rural patients had 1.6-fold higher odds of having significantly elevated PSA levels before diagnosis (> 20) than metropolitan patients (AOR 1.62 95%CI (1.21–2.18) p = 0.001) with the last PSA level recorded pre-diagnosis being > 20 in 16% of rural patients versus 8% of metropolitan patients (Table 2). A higher PSA result pre-diagnosis was moderately correlated with an increased Gleason score at diagnosis in both metropolitan and rural patients (Spearman’s Rho: 0.39, p < 0.01).

PSA test use

In patients subsequently diagnosed with prostate cancer, GP requesting of PSA blood tests was comparable between metropolitan and rural areas (Table 2). Examination of the rate of PSA test use over time found that GP requests for PSA blood tests first start to increase 9 months before prostate cancer diagnosis in metropolitan areas and 10 months before diagnosis in rural areas (the diagnostic window) (Fig. 1).

Abnormal PSA results

Examination of abnormal PSA results found there were differences in the frequency and timing of abnormal tests between rural and metropolitan areas. After adjusting for age and socioeconomic status, rural patients were more likely to have abnormal PSA results for each of the PSA thresholds examined, and these abnormalities were more likely to occur earlier before prostate cancer diagnosis (Table 2; Fig. 2). These associations were more prominent when applying higher thresholds for abnormal PSA, with 17% of rural and 9% of metropolitan patients having a PSA > 20ng/ml pre-diagnosis compared to 91% and 88% of patients having a PSA > 3ng/ml. 39% of rural patients had a PSA of over 10ng/ml detected a year before they were diagnosed with prostate cancer, almost twice the proportion in metropolitan areas (24%) (AOR 1.46 95%CI (1.19–1.80) p < 0.001, Table 2).

Median PSA levels were consistently higher for rural patients compared to metropolitan across all time periods and progressively increased closer to diagnosis, with an average PSA level in the 6 months before cancer diagnosis of 8.1ng/ml in rural areas compared to 6.2ng/ml in metropolitan areas (Table 2). Taking the last PSA test result before diagnosis, PSA levels were significantly higher in the rural cohort compared to the metropolitan cohort (7.8 vs. 5.9ng/ml, p < 0.01) and 16% of rural patients recording a PSA over 20 ng/ml compared to 8% of metropolitan patients (p < 0.01) (Table 2). Guideline concordance for repeat PSA tests

Examination of repeat testing is important to understand the level of concordance with clinical recommendations for patients with abnormal PSA results. Repeat testing behaviours were comparable in rural and metropolitan areas, with two thirds of patients (65% in metropolitan and 70% in rural) with an abnormal PSA result > 3 ng/ml having a repeat PSA test and one third of patients (34% and 37%, respectively) having a repeat PSA test within 3 months. Results were similar when restricting the cohort to PSA tests from 2016 onwards (Table 3). The median interval for a repeat test following an abnormal test was 132–136 days (Table 3).

Time to diagnosis

Prolonged diagnostic intervals were noted between the first detected abnormal PSA result in primary care and prostate cancer diagnosis, with a median interval of 222 days in metropolitan patients and 254 days in rural patients (Table 3). Figure 2 shows the distribution of time between the first abnormal PSA test result and a diagnosis of cancer, for various PSA thresholds. The average time between abnormal PSA result and diagnosis varies from 4 months to almost 12 months with the time decreasing for higher PSA values.

Association between last PSA result and stage or survival

After excluding patients with missing stage (5.5% of metropolitan patients, 10.6% of rural patients), there was no significant difference in registry-derived stage at diagnosis between metropolitan and rural patients (Table 1). On univariable analysis, there was a significant difference in 5-year survival curves, with lower survival in rural areas compared to metropolitan, however these differences were not observed after adjusting for age (Fig. 4). Similar differences in survival were noted when examining survival by level of last PSA before diagnosis and ISUP group in patients from rural and metropolitan regions, with higher PSA results at last test before diagnosis and higher ISUP group at diagnosis associated with worse 5-year survival (p < 0.01), but no differences in survival after adjusting for age and at diagnosis (Fig. 4, supplementary Fig. 4).

Discussion

This is the first study to examine differences in primary care PSA test results before prostate cancer diagnosis, their follow-up and tumour characteristics in metropolitan versus rural patients in Australia. At diagnosis, patients living in rural Victoria had substantially higher Gleason scores, higher PSA levels and were more likely to have a PSA > 20ng/ml before diagnosis. Our findings show there is substantial variation in tumour characteristics and PSA levels pre-diagnosis between rural and metropolitan areas which could contribute to disparities in prostate cancer survival.

Rural patients were more likely to have a delay from first abnormal PSA result to cancer diagnosis, with almost double the proportion of patients having a PSA result > 10ng/ml detected in primary care over a year before diagnosis compared to metropolitan patients. These higher levels of PSA at diagnosis were associated with poorer 5-year survival. The average time from the first abnormal PSA test to prostate cancer diagnosis was 7 months in metropolitan patients and 8 months in rural patients. In both rural and metropolitan areas, only one third of patients with a PSA > 3ng/ml had strict guideline concordant care with a repeat PSA test conducted within 3 months, with the average time for a repeat test of 4 months. It should be noted that the recommendation for repeat testing within 1–3 months is a low evidence recommendation, which may account for the low adherence. Additionally, it may be reasonable in some instances (e.g. a significantly raised PSA > 20ng/ml or an abnormal digital rectal examination) to refer directly without repeating the PSA.

Delays in referral of patients with elevated PSA levels can contribute to diagnostic delay, potentially impacting the stage of cancer at eventual diagnosis and patient outcomes including reduced survival [22, 23]. Although we did not identify differences in registry-derived stage at diagnosis between metropolitan and rural patients, rural patients had higher Gleason scores, ISUP group and PSA levels at diagnosis, even after adjusting for age and socioeconomic status. Registry-derived stage does not necessarily take into account PSA and Gleason scores, which may explain why no difference was seen between stage at diagnosis in our study. Additionally, CVDL only provided Gleason scores until 2019. Future studies with access to more reliable stage data (e.g. using AJCC TNM 8th Edition or NCCN risk groups) and ISUP/Gleason scores post-2019 should be used to examine if regional variations in stage and grade at diagnosis exist.

Further exploration of why referrals are made at higher levels of PSA levels in rural patients and examination of the rate and timing of prostate biopsies pre-diagnosis may lead to the identification of opportunities to expedite cancer diagnosis for some of these patients. Possible explanations could relate to poorer access to urologists in rural Victoria, or greater competing demands on rural patients in terms of time and finances which may affect shared decisions on when to be referred [24, 25].

This study is unique in its ability to examine the activity and PSA results before cancer diagnosis to explore potential causes for established worsening of prostate cancer outcomes. While we have identified one potential cause, diagnostic delays are greater in rural areas, it is plausible that other factors contribute to worse outcomes too. The higher PSA at diagnosis, and associated higher grade and ISUP group, in rural regions may suggest a variation in cancer biology that this study is not able to explore. While the ethnicity of the Victorian population is diverse, there may be genetic differences between the cohort in rural and metropolitan areas affecting tumour behaviour and subsequent outcomes. The delay in repeat PSA tests or presenting for follow-up diagnostics may also be due to patient behaviours. Lower socioeconomic status is commonly associated with poorer health literacy, and thus lower rural health literacy could contribute both to the delays in presentations, and delays in follow-up care [26]. It could also reflect the growing out-of-pocket costs experienced by patients to access general practice care (as described in the 2024 General Practice Health of the Nation report), and poorer access to general practice in rural Victoria [27]. While the rural cohort are older at diagnosis, this probably reflects the demographic of the general rural population and differences persisted after adjusting for age. While this cohort all had a prostate cancer diagnosis, the specificity and sensitivity of the PSA test is poor at lower levels. Thus, repeat testing after a short duration may exclude non-malignant causes of an elevated PSA including infection.

Guidelines have changed over the last 20 years in terms of advice to GPs on screening male patients for prostate cancer in the absence of symptoms. The changes in the threshold for an abnormal PSA that occurred with the Australian PSA testing guidelines in 2016 were not effectively disseminated and were not necessarily reflected in the abnormal ranges provided in pathology results to GPs. Nonetheless, this does not explain the high proportion of rural men who were not referred at relatively high levels of PSA. Australian guidelines for PSA testing are currently under review. If further changes to recommendations are made in these new guidelines, they will require more effective strategies to implement them in general practice and a specific focus on how to reduce variations in follow-up experienced by rural Australians.

Strengths and limitations

This data linkage study utilised diagnostic data from the VCR, which captures all prostate cancer diagnoses across Victoria. Linkage to primary care data allows examination of previously unexplored primary care activity before prostate cancer diagnosis for the first time in the Australian context. A major strength of our primary care dataset is the availability of accurate PSA results which are not available through alternative approaches linking Medicare Benefit Schedule data on PSA testing [28].

A potential limitation is that the Patron data are not population level data. PATRON is broadly representative of the Victorian adult population in terms of age and sex [12, 29], but further studies are needed to confirm its representativeness across broader sociodemographic factors. Australian patients can receive care in multiple GP practices, including practices not captured in Patron, and we could potentially have missed some primary care activity including PSA tests occurring elsewhere. Additionally, our study was restricted to prostate cancer patients who had a primary care PSA test in the 2 years prior to diagnosis. It is possible that some patients with poorer access to primary care may not have had a PSA test in this period and would not be represented in our study. This may disproportionally affect patients in rural areas, where access to primary care can be more limited than in metropolitan areas. Our linked data could not determine if/where further delays occurred from the point of GP referral to specialist care prior to diagnosis.

Conclusions

Rural Victorians with prostate cancer have higher PSA levels and higher Gleason scores and ISUP group at diagnosis compared to metropolitan patients. Abnormal PSA tests are frequently detected several months before prostate cancer diagnosis in both rural and metropolitan areas. Patients with abnormal PSA results often do not receive guideline concordance repeat PSA tests within 3 months. The reasons for these findings are likely complex, but include patient and clinician behaviours and health system factors that prevent timely follow up of abnormal PSA blood test results, particularly in rural areas. Targeted interventions to identify and action abnormal PSA blood tests results in primary care could have potential for expediting prostate cancer diagnosis and reducing disparities in outcomes for rural Australians with prostate cancer.
