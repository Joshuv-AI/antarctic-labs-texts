On Wagstaff primes in the k k -Lucas number sequence
Herbert Batte

Abstract
A Wagstaff prime is a prime number of the form ( 2 𝔭 + 1 ) / 3 (2^{\mathfrak{p}}+1)/3 , where 𝔭 \mathfrak{p} is an odd prime. Let ( L n ( k ) ) n ≥ 2 − k (L_{n}^{(k)})_{n\geq 2-k} be the k k -Lucas number sequence defined by the recurrence relation L n ( k ) = L n − 1 ( k ) + ⋯ + L n − k ( k ) L_{n}^{(k)}=L_{n-1}^{(k)}+\cdots+L_{n-k}^{(k)} , for all n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 , with initial terms L 0 ( k ) = 2 L_{0}^{(k)}=2 and L 1 ( k ) = 1 L_{1}^{(k)}=1 for all k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 , and L 2 − k ( k ) = ⋯ = L − 1 ( k ) = 0 L_{2-k}^{(k)}=\cdots=L_{-1}^{(k)}=0 for k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 . In this paper, we show that the only solutions to the Diophantine equation L n ( k ) = ( 2 𝔭 + 1 ) / 3 L_{n}^{(k)}=(2^{\mathfrak{p}}+1)/3 are ( n , k , 𝔭 ) ∈ { ( 5 , 2 , 5 ) , ( 6 , 4 , 7 ) } ∪ { ( 2 , k , 3 ) : k ≥ 2 } (n,k,\mathfrak{p})\in\{(5,2,5),(6,4,7)\}\cup\{(2,k,3):k\geq 2\} . We use linear forms in logarithms and the LLL reduction method to prove our result.

Introduction

1.1
Background

For an integer
k\geq 2
, the sequence of
k
-generalized Lucas numbers
(L_{n}^{(k)})_{n\geq 2-k}
is defined by the recurrence relation

L_{n}^{(k)}=L_{n-1}^{(k)}+L_{n-2}^{(k)}+\cdots+L_{n-k}^{(k)},\quad\text{for all}\ n\geq 2,

with the initial conditions
L_{0}^{(k)}=2
,
L_{1}^{(k)}=1
, and
L_{j}^{(k)}=0
for
2-k\leq j\leq-1
. When
k=2
, we get the classical Lucas numbers
2,1,3,4,7,11,\dots
.

In Number theory, a Wagstaff prime is a prime of the form

W=\frac{2^{\mathfrak{p}}+1}{3},

where
\mathfrak{p}
is an odd prime. These primes are named after the mathematician Samuel S. Wagstaff Jr. and are related to Mersenne primes and perfect numbers. Some examples of Wagstaff primes include 3, 11, 43, and 683 (see sequence A000979 in
[
13
]
).

The search for special types of prime numbers within linear recurrence sequences is a popular topic. Recently, Rezaiguia et al.
[
12
]
studied the Diophantine equation
F_{n}^{(k)}=(2^{\mathfrak{p}}+1)/3
, where
F_{n}^{(k)}
is the
k
-generalized Fibonacci sequence. They proved that 3 is the only Wagstaff prime that appears in those sequences.
Motivated by their work, we ask the same question for the
k
-Lucas number sequence. Since
k
-Lucas numbers grow at a similar rate to
k
-Fibonacci numbers but start with different initial values, it is natural to check if they contain more Wagstaff primes. In this paper, we solve the Diophantine equation

L_{n}^{(k)}=\frac{2^{\mathfrak{p}}+1}{3},

(1.1)

in non-negative integers
n,k,\mathfrak{p}
where
\mathfrak{p}
is an odd prime and
k\geq 2
. Since
L_{0}^{(k)}=2
and
L_{1}^{(k)}=1
for all
k\geq 2
, and these are not Wagstaff primes, we may assume
n\geq 2
throughout the paper.

We prove the following result.

1.2
Main Result

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(L_{n}^{(k)})_{n\geq 2-k}
be the sequence of
k
-generalized Lucas numbers. Then, the only integer solutions
(n,k,\mathfrak{p})
to Eq. (
1.1
) with
n\geq 0
,
k\geq 2
and prime
\mathfrak{p}\geq 3
are

\displaystyle(n,k,\mathfrak{p})\in\{(5,2,5),(6,4,7)\}\cup\{(2,k,3):k\geq 2\}.

Specifically,
L_{2}^{(k)}=3=(2^{3}+1)/3
,
L_{5}^{(2)}=11=(2^{5}+1)/3
and
L_{6}^{(4)}=43=(2^{7}+1)/3
are the only solutions to (
1.1
).

2
Methods

2.1
Preliminaries

It is known that

\displaystyle L_{n}^{(k)}=3\cdot 2^{n-2},\qquad\text{for all}\qquad 2\leq n\leq k.

(2.1)

Additionally,
L_{k+1}^{(k)}=3\cdot 2^{k-1}-2
and by induction one proves that

L_{n}^{(k)}<3\cdot 2^{n-2}\qquad{\text{\rm holds for all}}\qquad n\geq k+1.

(2.2)

Next, we revisit some properties of the
k
–generalized Lucas numbers. They form a linearly recurrent sequence of characteristic polynomial

\Psi_{k}(x)=x^{k}-x^{k-1}-\cdots-x-1,

which is irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}[x]
. The polynomial
\Psi_{k}(x)
possesses a unique real root
\alpha(k)>1
and all the other roots are inside the unit circle, see
[
11
]
. The root
\alpha(k):=\alpha
is in the interval

\displaystyle 2(1-2^{-k})<\alpha<2

(2.3)

as noted in
[
15
]
. As in the classical case when
k=2
, it was shown in
[
3
]
that

\displaystyle\alpha^{n-1}\leq L_{n}^{(k)}\leq 2\alpha^{n},\quad\text{holds for all}\quad n\geq 1,~k\geq 2.

(2.4)

In particular, combining (
2.4
) and (
1.1
), we have

\displaystyle\alpha^{n-1}\leq L_{n}^{(k)}=\frac{2^{\mathfrak{p}}+1}{3}<2^{\mathfrak{p}}+2<2^{\mathfrak{p}+1},

for all odd primes
\mathfrak{p}
. Taking logarithms both sides, we get
(n-1)\log\alpha<(\mathfrak{p}+1)\log 2
, or equivalently
n<2\mathfrak{p}+3
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle 2\alpha^{n}\geq L_{n}^{(k)}=\frac{2^{\mathfrak{p}}+1}{3}>\frac{2^{\mathfrak{p}}}{3}>2^{\mathfrak{p}-2},

holds for all odd primes
\mathfrak{p}
. Taking logarithms both sides, we get
n\log\alpha>(\mathfrak{p}-3)\log 2
, or equivalently
n>\mathfrak{p}-3
, where we have used the fact that
\alpha<2
from (
2.3
). Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}-3<n<2\mathfrak{p}+3.

(2.5)

Next, let
k\geq 2
and define

f_{k}(x):=\dfrac{x-1}{2+(k+1)(x-2)}.

We have

\frac{df_{k}(x)}{dx}=-\frac{(k-1)}{(2+(k+1)(x-2))^{2}}<0,\qquad{\text{\rm for~all}}\qquad x>0.

In particular, inequality (
2.3
) implies that

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}=f_{k}(2)<f_{k}(\alpha)<f_{k}(2(1-2^{-k}))\leq\dfrac{3}{4},

(2.6)

for all
k\geq 3
. It is easy to check that the above inequality holds for
k=2
as well. Further, it is easy to verify that
|f_{k}(\alpha_{i})|<1
, for all
2\leq i\leq k
, where
\alpha_{i}
are the remaining
roots of
\Psi_{k}(x)
for
i=2,\ldots,k
.

Moreover, it was shown in
[
3
]
that

\displaystyle L_{n}^{(k)}=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}(2\alpha_{i}-1)f_{k}(\alpha_{i})\alpha_{i}^{n-1}~~\text{and}~~\left|L_{n}^{(k)}-f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}\right|<\dfrac{3}{2},

(2.7)

for all
k\geq 2
and
n\geq 2-k
. This means that

L_{n}^{(k)}=f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}+e_{k}(n),\qquad{\text{\rm where}}\qquad|e_{k}(n)|<1.5.

(2.8)

The left expression in (
2.7
) is known as the Binet formula for
L_{n}^{(k)}
. Furthermore, the right inequality expression in (
2.7
) shows that the contribution of the zeros that are inside the unit circle to
L_{n}^{(k)}
is small.

A better estimate than (
2.7
) appears in Section 3.3 page 14 of
[
1
]
, but with a more restricted range of
n
in terms of
k
. It states that

\displaystyle\left|f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}-3\cdot 2^{n-2}\right|<3\cdot 2^{n-2}\cdot\frac{36}{2^{k/2}},\qquad{\text{\rm provided}}\qquad n<2^{k/2}.

(2.9)

2.2
Linear forms in logarithms

We use Baker–type lower bounds for nonzero linear forms in logarithms of algebraic numbers. There are many such bounds mentioned in the literature but we use one of Matveev from
[
10
]
. Before we can formulate such inequalities, we need the notion of height of an algebraic number recalled below.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\gamma
be an algebraic number of degree
d
with minimal primitive polynomial over the integers

a_{0}x^{d}+a_{1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+a_{d}=a_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\gamma^{(i)}),

where the leading coefficient
a_{0}
is positive. Then, the logarithmic height of
\gamma
is given by

h(\gamma):=\dfrac{1}{d}\Big(\log a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\max\{|\gamma^{(i)}|,1\}\Big).

In particular, if
\gamma
is a rational number represented as
\gamma=p/q
with coprime integers
p
and
q\geq 1
, then
h(\gamma)=\log\max\{|p|,q\}
.
The following properties of the logarithmic height function
h(\cdot)
will be used in the rest of the paper without further reference:

\displaystyle h(\gamma_{1}\pm\gamma_{2})

\displaystyle\leq h(\gamma_{1})+h(\gamma_{2})+\log 2;

\displaystyle h(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{\pm 1})

\displaystyle\leq h(\gamma_{1})+h(\gamma_{2});

\displaystyle h(\gamma^{s})

\displaystyle=|s|h(\gamma)\quad{\text{\rm valid for}}\quad s\in\mathbb{Z}.

In Section 3, equation (12) of
[
2
]
these properties were used to show the following inequality:

\displaystyle h\left(f_{k}(\alpha)\right)<3\log k,~~\text{for all}~~k\geq 2.

(2.10)

A linear form in logarithms is an expression

\Lambda:=b_{1}\log\gamma_{1}+\cdots+b_{t}\log\gamma_{t},

where
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{t}
are positive real algebraic numbers and
b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}
are integers. We assume,
\Lambda\neq 0
. We need lower bounds
for
|\Lambda|
. We write
{\mathbb{K}}:={\mathbb{Q}}(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{t})
and
D
for the degree of
{\mathbb{K}}
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
.
We start with the general form due to Matveev, see Theorem 9.4 in
[
4
]
.

Theorem 2.1

(Matveev, see Theorem 9.4 in
[
4
]
)
.

Put
\Gamma:=\gamma_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots\gamma_{t}^{b_{t}}-1=e^{\Lambda}-1
. Assume
\Gamma\neq 0
. Then

\log|\Gamma|>-1.4\cdot 30^{t+3}\cdot t^{4.5}\cdot D^{2}(1+\log D)(1+\log B)A_{1}\cdots A_{t},

where
B\geq\max\{|b_{1}|,\ldots,|b_{t}|\}
and
A_{i}\geq\max\{Dh(\gamma_{i}),|\log\gamma_{i}|,0.16\}
for
i=1,\ldots,t
.

In our application of Theorem
2.1
, we need to ensure that the linear forms in logarithms are indeed nonzero. To ensure this, we shall need the following result given as Lemma 2.8 in
[
7
]
.

Lemma 2.1

(Lemma 2.8 in
[
7
]
)
.

Let
N:=N_{\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{Q}},
where
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
. Then

(i)

For
n,m\geq 1
and
k\geq 2
,
|N(\alpha)|=1
.

(ii)

N(2\alpha-1)=2^{k+1}-3
and
N(f_{k}(\alpha))=(k-1)^{2}/(2^{k+1}k^{k}-(k+1)^{k+1})
.

(iii)

For
k\geq 3
,
N((2\alpha-1)f_{k}(\alpha))<1
.

During the calculations, upper bounds on the variables are obtained which are too large, thus there is need to reduce them. To do so, we use some results from
approximation lattices and the so-called LLL–reduction method from
[
9
]
. We explain this in the following subsection.

2.3
Reduced Bases for Lattices and LLL–reduction methods

Let
k
be a positive integer. A subset
\mathcal{L}
of the
k
-dimensional real vector space
{\mathbb{R}^{k}}
is called a lattice if there exists a basis
\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{Z}b_{i}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}b_{i}\mid r_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

We say that
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
form a basis for
\mathcal{L}
, or that they span
\mathcal{L}
. We
call
k
the rank of
\mathcal{L}
. The determinant
\text{det}(\mathcal{L})
, of
\mathcal{L}
is defined by

\displaystyle\text{det}(\mathcal{L})=|\det(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k})|,

with the
b_{i}
’s being written as column vectors. This is a positive real number that does not depend on the choice of the basis (see
[
5
]
, Section 1.2).

Given linearly independent vectors
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we refer back to the Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization technique. This method allows us to inductively define vectors
b^{*}_{i}
(with
1\leq i\leq k
) and real coefficients
\mu_{i,j}
(for
1\leq j\leq i\leq k
). Specifically,

\displaystyle b^{*}_{i}

\displaystyle=b_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mu_{i,j}b^{*}_{j},~~~\mu_{i,j}=\dfrac{\langle b_{i},b^{*}_{j}\rangle}{\langle b^{*}_{j},b^{*}_{j}\rangle},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the ordinary inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Notice that
b^{*}_{i}
is the orthogonal projection of
b_{i}
on the orthogonal complement of the span of
b_{1},\ldots,b_{i-1}
, and that
\mathbb{R}b_{i}
is orthogonal to the span of
b^{*}_{1},\ldots,b^{*}_{i-1}
for
1\leq i\leq k
. It follows that
b^{*}_{1},b^{*}_{2},\ldots,b^{*}_{k}
is an orthogonal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Definition 2.2
.

The basis
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}
for the lattice
\mathcal{L}
is called reduced if

\displaystyle\|\mu_{i,j}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2},\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq j<i\leq n,~~\text{and}

\displaystyle\|b^{*}_{i}+\mu_{i,i-1}b^{*}_{i-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3}{4}\|b^{*}_{i-1}\|^{2},\quad\text{for}\quad 1<i\leq n,

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the ordinary Euclidean length. The constant
{3}/{4}
above is arbitrarily chosen, and may be replaced by any fixed real number
y
in the interval
{1}/{4}<y<1

(see
[
9
]
, Section 1)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a
k-
dimensional lattice with reduced basis
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
and denote by
B
the matrix with columns
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
.
We define

l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\min_{x\in\mathcal{L}}||x-y||\quad;~~y\not\in\mathcal{L}\\
\min_{0\neq x\in\mathcal{L}}||x||\quad;~~y\in\mathcal{L}\end{array}\right.,

where
||\cdot||
denotes the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. It is well known that, by applying the
LLL–algorithm, it is possible to give in polynomial time a lower bound for
l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)
, namely a positive constant
\delta
such that
l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)\geq\delta
holds (see
[
14
]
, Section V.4).

Lemma 2.2

(
[
14
]
, Section V.4)
.

Let
b_{1},\dots,b_{k}
be an LLL-reduced basis for a lattice
\mathcal{L}
and
b_{1}^{*},\dots,b_{k}^{*}
be the corresponding Gram-Schmidt orthogonal basis. Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
z=B^{-1}y
.

(i)

If
y\not\in\mathcal{L}
, let
i_{0}
be the largest index such that
z_{i_{0}}\neq 0
and put
\lambda:=\{z_{i_{0}}\}
.

(ii)

If
y\in\mathcal{L}
, put
\lambda:=1
.

Now, define

c_{1}:=\max_{1\leq j\leq k}\left\{\dfrac{\|b_{1}\|}{\|b_{j}^{*}\|}\right\}.

Then,
l(\mathcal{L},y)\geq\delta=\lambda\|b_{1}\|c_{1}^{-1}
.

In our application, we are given real numbers
\eta_{0},\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{k}
which are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
and two positive constants
c_{3}
and
c_{4}
such that

\displaystyle|\eta_{0}+x_{1}\eta_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}\eta_{k}|\leq c_{3}\exp(-c_{4}H),

(2.11)

where the integers
x_{i}
are bounded as
|x_{i}|\leq X_{i}
with
X_{i}
given upper bounds for
1\leq i\leq k
. We write
X_{0}:=\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq k}\{X_{i}\}
. The basic idea in such a situation, from
[
6
]
, is to approximate the linear form (
2.11
) by an approximation lattice. So, we consider the lattice
\mathcal{L}
generated by the columns of the matrix

\mathcal{A}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\ldots&0&0\\
0&1&\ldots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&1&0\\
\lfloor C\eta_{1}\rfloor&\lfloor C\eta_{2}\rfloor&\ldots&\lfloor C\eta_{k-1}\rfloor&\lfloor C\eta_{k}\rfloor\end{pmatrix},

where
C
is a large constant usually of the size of about
X_{0}^{k}
. Let us assume that we have an LLL–reduced basis
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
of
\mathcal{L}
and that we have a lower bound
l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)\geq\delta
with
y:=(0,0,\ldots,-\lfloor C\eta_{0}\rfloor)
. Note that
\delta
can be computed by using the results of Lemma
2.2
. Then, with these notations the following result is Lemma VI.1 in
[
14
]
.

Lemma 2.3

(Lemma VI.1 in
[
14
]
)
.

Let
S:=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}X_{i}^{2}
and
T:=\dfrac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}}{2}
. If
\delta^{2}\geq T^{2}+S
, then inequality (
2.11
) implies that we either have
x_{1}=x_{2}=\cdots=x_{k-1}=0
and
x_{k}=-\dfrac{\lfloor C\eta_{0}\rfloor}{\lfloor C\eta_{k}\rfloor}
, or

H\leq\dfrac{1}{c_{4}}\left(\log(Cc_{3})-\log\left(\sqrt{\delta^{2}-S}-T\right)\right).

Finally, we present an analytic argument which is Lemma 7 in
[

]
.

Lemma 2.4

(Lemma 7 in
[

]
)
.

If

.

In this section, we prove Theorem

. We do this in two separate cases depending on
n
versus

.

3.1
The case

In this case, we have
L_{n}^{(k)}=3\cdot 2^{n-2}
. Comparing (
2.1
) with (
1.1
), we have
3\cdot 2^{n-2}=(2^{\mathfrak{p}}+1)/3
, or simply

\displaystyle 9\cdot 2^{n-2}=2^{\mathfrak{p}}+1.

(3.1)

If
n=2
, then
9\cdot 2^{2-2}=2^{\mathfrak{p}}+1
, from which
\mathfrak{p}=3
for all

. This solution is stated in the main result. If
n\geq 3
, then the left-hand side of (
3.1
) is even while the right-hand side is odd. This means that (
3.1
) has no solutions in the interval
3\leq n\leq k
. From now on, we assume

.

3.2
The case

In this case, we assume
n>k
. Since

, then we can also assume that

for the remaining part of the proof.

3.2.1
Bounding
n
in terms of

We begin by proving the following result.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
n
,
k
,
\mathfrak{p}
be integer solutions to Eq. (
1.1
) with
n>k\geq 2
and
\mathfrak{p}\geq 3
be a prime number, then

\displaystyle n<2.0\cdot 10^{15}k^{4}(\log k)^{3}.

Proof.

We go back to (
1.1
) and rewrite it using the Binet formula in (
2.8
) as

\displaystyle L_{n}^{(k)}-f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}

\displaystyle=e_{k}(n),

\displaystyle\dfrac{2^{\mathfrak{p}}+1}{3}-f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}

\displaystyle=e_{k}(n),

\displaystyle\dfrac{2^{\mathfrak{p}}}{3}-f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}

\displaystyle=e_{k}(n)-\dfrac{1}{3}.

Taking absolute values, we get

\displaystyle\left|\dfrac{2^{\mathfrak{p}}}{3}-f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}\right|

\displaystyle<\dfrac{3}{2}+\dfrac{1}{3}=\dfrac{11}{6},

and dividing both sides by

, gives

\displaystyle\left|2^{\mathfrak{p}}(3(2\alpha-1)f_{k}(\alpha))^{-1}\alpha^{-(n-1)}-1\right|

\displaystyle<\dfrac{11}{6f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}}

\displaystyle<\dfrac{11}{3}\alpha^{-n}.

In the above simplification, we have used the fact that
f_{k}(\alpha)>1/2
from (
2.6
) and

for all
k\geq 2
from (

). Therefore,

\displaystyle|\Gamma_{1}|:=\left|2^{\mathfrak{p}}(3(2\alpha-1)f_{k}(\alpha))^{-1}\alpha^{-(n-1)}-1\right|

\displaystyle<\dfrac{11}{3}\alpha^{-n}.

(3.2)

Notice that
\Gamma_{1}\neq 0
, otherwise we would have

\displaystyle(2\alpha-1)f_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{n-1}=\dfrac{2^{\mathfrak{p}}}{3}.

Taking norms in
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)
and using

(ii)

, the above equation becomes

\displaystyle N\left(f_{k}(\alpha)\right)\cdot N(2\alpha-1)=\left(\dfrac{2^{\mathfrak{p}}}{3}\right)^{k}.

This implies that

\displaystyle 1\geq\dfrac{(k-1)^{2}}{2^{k+1}k^{k}-(k+1)^{k+1}}\cdot\left(2^{k+1}-3\right)=\left(\dfrac{2^{\mathfrak{p}}}{3}\right)^{k}\geq\left(\dfrac{2^{3}}{3}\right)^{2}=\dfrac{64}{9},

which is absurd.

The algebraic number field containing the following
\gamma_{i}
’s is
\mathbb{K}:=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
. We have
D=k
,
t:=3
,

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}

\displaystyle:=2,\qquad

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}

\displaystyle:=3(2\alpha-1)f_{k}(\alpha),\qquad

\displaystyle\gamma_{3}

\displaystyle:=\alpha,

\displaystyle b_{1}

\displaystyle:=\mathfrak{p},

\displaystyle\quad b_{2}

\displaystyle:=-1,

\displaystyle\quad b_{3}

\displaystyle:=-(n-1).

Notice that
h(\gamma_{1})=h(2)=\log 2
,

A_{1}:=k\log 2
and
A_{3}:=0.7
.
For
A_{2}
, we first compute

\displaystyle h(\gamma_{2})

\displaystyle:=h\left(3(2\alpha-1)f_{k}(\alpha)\right)

\displaystyle\leq h(3)+h\left(2\alpha-1\right)+h\left(f_{k}(\alpha)\right)

\displaystyle\leq h(3)+h(2)+h\left(\alpha\right)+2\log 2+h\left(f_{k}(\alpha)\right)

\displaystyle<9\log k,

for all

. In the above computations, we have used inequality (
2.10
). So, we can take
A_{2}:=9k\log k
. Next,
B\geq\max\{|b_{i}|:i=1,2,3\}
, and by relation (
2.5
), we can take
B:=n+3
.

Now, Theorem
2.1
gives,

\displaystyle\log|\Gamma_{1}|

\displaystyle>-1.4\cdot 30^{6}\cdot 3^{4.5}\cdot k^{2}(1+\log k)(1+\log(n+3))\cdot k\log 2\cdot 0.7\cdot 9k\log k

\displaystyle>-3.0\cdot 10^{12}k^{4}(\log k)^{2}\log(n+3).

(3.3)

Comparing (
3.2
) and (
3.2.1
), we get

\displaystyle n\log\alpha-\log(11/3)

\displaystyle<3.0\cdot 10^{12}k^{4}(\log k)^{2}\log(n+3)

\displaystyle<6.0\cdot 10^{12}k^{4}(\log k)^{2}\log n.

Therefore, we obtain
n<2.0\cdot 10^{13}k^{4}(\log k)^{2}\log n
.

We now apply Lemma
2.4
with
p:=n
,
r:=1
,
T:=2.0\cdot 10^{13}k^{4}(\log k)^{2}
and have

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle<2\cdot 2.0\cdot 10^{13}k^{4}(\log k)^{2}\log\left(2.0\cdot 10^{13}k^{4}(\log k)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=4.0\cdot 10^{13}k^{4}(\log k)^{2}\left(\log(2.0\cdot 10^{13})+4\log k+2\log\log k\right)

\displaystyle<4.0\cdot 10^{13}k^{4}(\log k)^{3}\left(\dfrac{31}{\log k}+4+\dfrac{2\log\log k}{\log k}\right)

\displaystyle<2.0\cdot 10^{15}k^{4}(\log k)^{3},

for all
k\geq 2
. Thus,
n<2.0\cdot 10^{15}k^{4}(\log k)^{3}
and the proof of Lemma
3.1
is complete.
∎

3.2.2
The case
k>190

Let us assume for a moment that
k>190
.
By Lemma
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle n<2.0\cdot 10^{15}k^{4}(\log k)^{3}<2^{k/2},

where the last inequality holds true for all
k>190
. Since
n<2^{k/2}
, this puts us in position to use inequality (
2.9
), which together with (
2.7
) gives

\displaystyle\left|\dfrac{2^{\mathfrak{p}}+1}{3}-3\cdot 2^{n-2}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|L_{n}^{(k)}-3\cdot 2^{n-2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|L_{n}^{(k)}-f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}\right|+\left|f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}-3\cdot 2^{n-2}\right|

\displaystyle<\dfrac{3}{2}+3\cdot 2^{n-2}\cdot\frac{36}{2^{k/2}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\left|\dfrac{2^{\mathfrak{p}}}{3}-3\cdot 2^{n-2}\right|

\displaystyle<\dfrac{3}{2}+3\cdot 2^{n-2}\cdot\frac{36}{2^{k/2}}+\dfrac{1}{3}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{11}{6}+3\cdot 2^{n-2}\cdot\frac{36}{2^{k/2}}

\displaystyle<2+2^{n}\cdot\frac{27}{2^{k/2}}

\displaystyle<2^{n+1}\cdot\frac{27}{2^{k/2}},

since we are in the case
n>k>k/2
. Multiplying through by 3 and dividing through by
2^{n-2}
, we get

\displaystyle\left|9-2^{\mathfrak{p}-n+2}\right|<\frac{648}{2^{k/2}}.

(3.4)

Notice that since
n>k
, we can rewrite (
1.1
) using (
2.2
) as

\displaystyle\frac{2^{\mathfrak{p}}}{3}<\frac{2^{\mathfrak{p}}+1}{3}=L_{n}^{(k)}<3\cdot 2^{n-2}.

This gives
2^{\mathfrak{p}}<9\cdot 2^{n-2}
and taking logarithms both sides simplifies it to
\mathfrak{p}<n+2
. Therefore,
\mathfrak{p}-n+2<4
. Note also that
\mathfrak{p}-n+2
is an integer, so
\mathfrak{p}-n+2\leq 3
and hence
1\leq\left|9-2^{\mathfrak{p}-n+2}\right|<9
. Comparing this inequality with (
3.4
), we get

\displaystyle 1\leq\left|9-2^{\mathfrak{p}-n+2}\right|<\frac{648}{2^{k/2}},

from which we deduce that
2^{k/2}<648
. This gives
k<19
, contradicting the assumption that
k>190
. Hence, Equation (
1.1
) has no solutions whenever
k>190
.

satisfies

\displaystyle|\log(1+x)|

\displaystyle<|x-x^{2}/2+-\dots|<|x|+\frac{|x|^{2}+|x|^{3}+\dots}{2}<|x|\left(1+\frac{|x|}{2(1-|x|)}\right)<\frac{3}{2}|x|.

(3.5)

We use this inequality in the next subsection.

3.2.3
The case
