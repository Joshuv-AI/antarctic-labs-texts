Teaching Vaginal Surgery Through Simulation
Authors: Payton Schmidt, Christine Vaccaro, Chi Chiung Grace Chen, Douglas Miyazaki, LaTeesa James, Veronica Lerner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Current vaginal surgery simulations are diverse with variation in validity evidence; future research should focus on assessment standards and real-world skill transfer.

Simulation has revolutionized surgical education with benefits that include patient safety, effective learning, and innovation. Gynecologic vaginal surgery has unique challenges, including the need for strong ambidexterity, effective use of assistants, ability to work in restricted spaces, understanding of complex anatomic concepts resulting from a limited field of view, and specific instrumentation and reliance on haptic feedback. Furthermore, declining vaginal hysterectomy1,2 and vaginal reconstructive prolapse surgery3 volumes pose even greater challenges. Thus, the demand for evidence-based, simulation-based educational interventions that develop and refine vaginal surgery skills is increasingly evident, with vaginal surgery perfectly positioned to benefit from simulation training. In addition to skill-specific simulations, there is a pressing need for comprehensive training curricula that help learners obtain training in essential gynecologic vaginal procedures.

Choosing effective surgical simulation tools depends on several key factors: defining targeted learning objectives and skills (whether basic, task specific, or procedure specific); considering the fidelity, or degree of realism, of the simulation (physical, conceptual, and psychologic dimensions tailored to the task and learner); selecting appropriate assessment tools for both formative and summative evaluation; evaluating evidence of validity with standards such as the Medical Education Research Study Quality Instrument score; and accounting for implementation factors such as cost, practicality, curriculum availability, and educational effectiveness. Diverse vaginal surgery simulation models exist but vary in quality, methodology, validity, cost, and outcomes. Given the growing number of publications, a comprehensive review is essential to inform curriculum development. This scoping review summarizes current literature on simulations for teaching and evaluating vaginal surgical skills, focusing on validity,4 assessment tools, cost, feasibility, and future research needs.

METHODS

After conducting a workshop at the Society of Gynecologic Surgeons' annual meeting in 2022, we performed a scoping review of the literature on vaginal surgical simulation. Because a scoping review is typically undertaken to understand the breadth and depth of the literature on a topic, particularly in a complex or underresearched area,5 we chose this approach given the heterogeneous nature of the literature on simulation for gynecologic vaginal surgery and the scarcity of other comprehensive reviews on vaginal surgical simulation.

An informationist developed search strategies for the following databases: PUBMED, Ovid MEDLINE, and Epub Ahead of Print, In-Process, In-Data-Review & Other Non-Indexed Citations, Daily and Versions; Elsevier Embase (including Embase Classic); Elsevier Scopus; Web of Science Core Collection (SCI-EXPANDED, SSCI, A&HCI, CPCI-S, CPCI-SSH, BKCI-S, BKCI-SSH, ESCI, CCR-EXPANDED); and Google Scholar. The search was performed from the inception of each database until June 6, 2025, to identify relevant articles or meeting abstracts describing evidence on surgical simulation models for vaginal surgery. Each search used key words and indexing language (for databases that have controlled vocabularies) describing surgical simulation models and vaginal surgeries. A set of sentinel articles was identified before the search process and used to generate search terms and to test the effectiveness of the strategies in each database. Original search strategies were created in Ovid MEDLINE and translated, as appropriate, to other databases with the Systematic Review Accelerator Polyglot tool.6 The search was not limited by language, publication date, or any other restrictions. The top 500 Google Scholar results were used for gray literature searching. Citations were deduplicated with a modified version of the Bramer Method in EndNote (version 20). The complete search strategies are available in Appendix 1 (available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/E633).

After the search was completed, all titles and their respective abstracts were reviewed by one author using Rayyan software.7 Any studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria were eliminated. The articles of the remaining titles were then extracted and reviewed by two authors. If discrepancies arose regarding article inclusion or data extraction, conflicts were resolved through discussion and consensus; if consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer adjudicated. All articles deemed relevant were included (Fig. 1).

Studies were included if they included medical students, residents, fellows, or practicing surgeons; involved surgical simulation, model creating, or skills assessment relevant for vaginal surgery; were task- or procedure-specific simulations relevant for vaginal surgeries; and evaluated the use of surgical simulation or skills assessment in a teaching setting (medical school training, residency training, fellowship training, practicing physician workshop). Relevant vaginal procedures included vaginal hysterectomy, anterior and posterior repair (or colporrhaphy), vaginal apical ligament suspension (sacrospinous or uterosacral ligament suspension), colpocleisis, and incontinence sling placement. Study types included randomized controlled trials; nonrandomized controlled trials; before-and-after and interrupted time-series studies, analytic observational studies (ie, prospective and retrospective cohort studies), case–control studies, analytical cross-sectional studies, and descriptive observational study designs (ie, case series and descriptive cross-sectional studies). Studies available only as an abstract were excluded; those with an associated full-text article or video available were included.

A data extraction form was developed to capture variables relevant for comparing studies and enabling meaningful synthesis across the literature. The variables included year of publication; study description; simulation component; procedure- or task-specific simulation; trainee level of participants; number of participants; Medical Education Research Study Quality Instrument score; skills and cognitive portions included in the study; evidence of validity; study outcomes; performance metrics; assessment scores; assessment of the simulator; and variables relevant to accessibility, implementation, and feasibility of the use of the simulator. The Medical Education Research Study Quality Instrument assesses six domains with scores ranging from 0 to 18; a higher score reflects a stronger research design, data collection, analysis, and outcome assessment.8,9 These variables were selected to systematically address study heterogeneity, to identify key simulation tasks and knowledge components, to assess methodologic quality and research rigor, and to highlight practical considerations important to educators who may seek to replicate or adapt these simulation methods. In addition, ease of use —the reviewing author's subjective rating considering cost, construction time, and material accessibility—was included to further inform educators about practical implementation. After each author completed the data extraction form, all data were compiled for synthesis.

RESULTS

Article Selection

The literature search resulted in 5,560 articles. After deduplication, 3,140 titles were selected for review; of these, 2,953 were excluded, and 187 were considered for inclusion. Any literature that was available only as an abstract was excluded, and any that had an associated full-text article or video available was kept for review. Two reviewers were assigned for each study, and after a full review, 41 articles were included. The 41 eligible studies included four randomized controlled trials,10–13 15 single-institution cross-sectional cohort studies,14–28 five multi-institutional cross-sectional studies,29–33 two prospective cohort studies,34,35 two retrospective cohort studies,36,37 one study with a nested design combining a multi-institutional cohort and a single-institution cross-sectional component,38 and 12 descriptive studies.39–50

The majority of studies were relevant to either vaginal hysterectomy training (20 articles) or urogynecology vaginal procedural training (ie, prolapse, incontinence, or fistula surgeries) (19 articles). In comparison, two articles were relevant to the assessment of vaginal surgical skills.

Three studies focused on task-specific skills: two used a synthetic, constructed model,15,26 and one used a manufactured, three-dimensional printed model.38 Thirteen studies described simulation models that were procedure specific; 12 used a synthetic constructed model,10,13,14,16,17,19,21,32,36,39,40,48 three used a manufactured three-dimensional printed model,20,28,30 one used a cadaver model,50 and one used an animal model.41 Of the 20 included studies, only five determined proficiency cutoff scores for simulation performance.15,19,30,51,53

Two studies investigated rating scales for assessing vaginal hysterectomy performance. In 2010, Chen et al29 performed a two-site, single-group cross-sectional study evaluating two rating scales for assessing intraoperative vaginal hysterectomy technique. They compared a Global Rating Scale with the authors' new rating scale, the Vaginal Surgical Skills Index, to evaluate the performance of 212 procedures by 27 trainees and five faculty evaluators. Jelovsek et al18 further analyzed the performance data from the Chen et al study and set Vaginal Surgical Skills Index scores of 32 and Global Rating Scale scores of 18 as competency cutoff scores. Participants took approximately 27 and 21 total vaginal hysterectomies to achieve this competency level, respectively. Both rating scales are readily available and easy to use.

Urinary Incontinence Surgeries

Six studies presented simulation models for midurethral incontinence sling procedures: four prospective cohort studies,22,34,35,45 one retrospective cohort study,37 and one descriptive study (Tables 2 and 4).46 Three studies were task specific and focused on retropubic midurethral sling trocar passage.22,34,35 Three studies investigated procedure-specific interventions teaching retropubic or transobturator midurethral sling placement.37,45,46 No studies calculated or reported a proficiency score. One study by Kent et al33 presented a simulation model for transvaginal repair of vesicovaginal fistulas.

DISCUSSION

As the volume of vaginal surgeries continues to decline, the use of surgical simulation in the instruction and advancement of vaginal surgical skills is becoming increasingly important. A diverse range of simulations exists for this purpose, from basic anatomic representations to advanced, procedure-specific simulations. In conducting this scoping review, we evaluated 20 studies on models for teaching vaginal hysterectomy and two that examined assessment rubrics for appraising the performance of this procedure. In addition, we analyzed 12 studies on obliterative and reconstructive prolapse surgeries, one study on vesicovaginal fistula surgery, and six concerning urinary incontinence sling procedures. Despite the growing arsenal of models for vaginal surgery education, challenges with heterogeneity in study design, difficulties in focusing on outcomes research (in both the simulation spaces and actual operating rooms), high-quality validity evidence, and implementation feasibility persist in the literature.

Although various simulators are available for teaching vaginal surgery, the components of an effective comprehensive vaginal surgery curriculum remain unexplored. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first scoping review to provide a detailed compilation of simulations and assessment tools for teaching vaginal surgery, including recommendations for using each model based on the learner's targeted skill level, cost considerations, and practical implementation.

Significant progress has been made in vaginal surgical simulation, but numerous challenges and prospects for future research stand out. First, there is wide variation in the evidence of validity for available current vaginal surgery simulation trainers, as indicated by the Medical Education Research Study Quality Instrument scores. In this review, scores ranged from unreported (11 studies) to 16.5 (reported in only one study), and none achieved the maximum score of 18, suggesting that although some studies demonstrate high research rigor, many fall short in key methodologic areas. Ideally, a higher Medical Education Research Study Quality Instrument score is desirable because it indicates more reliable and valid educational research, which is essential if simulation assessments are to be used for advancement or credentialing.55 Moreover, many existing studies were unable to evaluate skill acquisition or the effect of the simulation on actual surgical outcomes. Assessing how effectively skills learned through simulation transfer to real-life operations is crucial yet underrepresented in the literature, as reported by Orejuela et al.56 Future research should aim to demonstrate the influence of simulation training on patient outcomes and safety and how to support surgical educators in their work. In addition, our extraction of variables related to simulation components, trainee level, outcomes, validity, accessibility, implementation, and ease of use provides insight into the heterogeneity and practical challenges educators face. These aspects are critical for designing scalable and effective simulation curricula, underscoring the need for standardized assessment tools and transparent reporting to facilitate broader adoption. Furthermore, a deeper understanding of how best to incorporate simulations into gynecologic surgical programs is necessary. In addition, a “putting it all together” approach that takes trainees through all phases and aspects of learning vaginal surgery (from basic skills and anatomic concepts to partial-task trainers to the entire procedure, with the goal of skill transfer to the operating room) is needed. To accomplish these goals, significant resources, funding, grants, and cultural change are required at all levels, from the institutional to the national level. Advocacy for vaginal surgery education is required to save this essential skill, which defines our specialty. Given all the challenges and obstacles, along with the increasing pressure to prioritize clinical productivity over all else, it is important to note that the body of work generated by surgical educators “on the ground” with little or no support is impressive and inspiring. It helps solidify, validate, and unite surgical educators, moving the field forward.

The strengths of this review include a thorough search strategy that captured all relevant surgical simulations and assessments for vaginal surgery, ensuring a comprehensive review. The authors who reviewed the articles are experienced educators who contributed insights on factors crucial to educators developing a curriculum such as cost, ease of implementation, and appropriateness for various learner levels. Limitations of this review include heterogeneity in research methods and outcome measures across the included studies, which prevented the authors from establishing the effect of training on skill acquisition and its translation to the operating room.

With decreasing opportunities in vaginal surgery for trainees, incorporating surgical simulation into gynecologic surgical training is becoming necessary to develop and maintain competency in core gynecologic vaginal procedures. This scoping review presents an overview of the current literature, spotlighting various simulation methods, their educational value, and remaining challenges. Further investigation is essential to refine the integration of simulation into gynecologic surgical training and to enhance patient outcomes.
