The prevalence and influence of self-reported loneliness in the older with community-acquired pneumonia: a multi-center cohort study
Authors: Jin Jin, Yu Wang, Bingxuan Weng, Mengyuan Wang, Wenshu Jiao, Jingnan Li, Meng Ma, Xunliang Tong, Yanming Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesLoneliness is increasingly recognized as a determinant of poor prognosis in older adults with chronic diseases, yet its impact on community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) remains unclear. This study investigates the association between loneliness and long-term adverse outcomes in older CAP patients.MethodsThis is a multi-center, prospective cohort study. Patients aged ≥ 65 years with CAP were included from April 2021 to January 2023. At admission, self-reported loneliness, geriatric assessments, CAP severity, and laboratory parameters were evaluated. Participants were followed for 180 days post-discharge. The primary outcome was 180-day all-cause mortality; the secondary outcome was readmission for respiratory infection. Multi-variable Cox regression models assessed associations between loneliness and outcomes.ResultsA total of 619 older patients were included in the analysis, with a median age of 78 years, and 12.8% reporting loneliness. During the 180-days follow-up, all-cause mortality and respiratory infection-related readmission rates were 8.7% and 10.5%, respectively. After adjusting for confounders, lonely patients had a nearly twofold higher risk of 180-days post-discharge mortality compared to non-lonely group (adjusted HR:2.047; 95% CI:1.036–4.044) and no significant association was observed with hospital readmission.ConclusionsLoneliness is an independent risk factor for all-cause mortality in the older patients with CAP. The assessment of loneliness and the implementation of targeted interventions may improve the long-term outcomes.RegistryClinicalTrials.govRegistration date19 April 2021ClinicalTrials.gov IDChiCTR2100045574Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06741-8.

Introduction

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is a common infectious disease in older adults, with both incidence and mortality rates increasing with age. The annual incidence of CAP is 63.0 per 10,000 among individuals aged 65–79 years, rising to 164.3 per 10,000 among those aged 80 years and older [1]. CAP accounts for approximately 102,000 deaths annually, with mortality rates reaching 13.0%, 23.4%, and 30.6% at 1, 6, and 12 months post-diagnosis, respectively [2]. The prognosis of CAP is influenced by factors such as age, disease severity, and social determinants, which significantly affect hospitalization risk and mortality in older adults [3].

Loneliness, which is often defined as the perceived discrepancy between desired and actual social relationships [4], is highly prevalent among older adults and associated with adverse outcomes in chronic conditions [5]. A multinational study projects that the number of lonely adults aged ≥ 50 across 27 countries will rise from 104.9 million in 1990 to 333.5 million by 2050 [6]. Moreover, loneliness is a recognized risk factor for chronic disease progression and mortality in older adults [7, 8]. For instance, among individuals over 60, loneliness was linked to a 22.8% mortality rate over six years compared to 17.9% in non-lonely counterparts, with this association persisting after adjusting for demographics, socioeconomic status, depression, and health measure [8]. However, the role of loneliness—a growing public health concern—remains underexplored in this population.

Despite its health implications, the impact of loneliness on CAP prognosis in older adults remains unstudied. To address this gap, this study evaluates the effects of loneliness on long-term clinical outcomes in older CAP patients through a multi-center prospective cohort study and to identify the demographic and clinical characteristics associated with loneliness in this population.

Methods

Study design and participants

This multi-center, prospective cohort study was conducted across eight centers in China, enrolling hospitalized patients aged ≥ 65 years with CAP from April 15, 2021, to January 15, 2023. CAP diagnosis adhered to the American Thoracic Society and Infectious Diseases Society of America (ATS-IDSA) guidelines. Exclusion criteria were: total functional dependence before the onset of disease (Basic Activities of Daily Living [BADL] score ≤ 20), severe cognitive impairment (Mini-Mental State Examination [MMSE] score < 10), advanced malignancy (histologically confirmed stage IV cancer or life expectancy < 180 days as estimated by the treating physician). Written informed consent was obtained from participants or their proxies. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Beijing Hospital (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: ChiCTR2100045574).

Data collection

Before the start of the study, department nurses who served as assessors completed three structured training sessions. Upon admission, all participants were assessed using standardized methods by these trained evaluators.

At the time of admission, comprehensive baseline assessments were conducted, including demographic characteristics, behavioral factors, pneumonia severity, and geriatric assessment. Demographic variables included age, sex, marital status, and pension status. Behavioral status included smoking, alcohol consumption, and living alone. Geriatric assessment comprised body mass index (BMI), nutritional status, comorbidity, frailty, sleep quality, affective measurements, physical function, cognitive function and the number of medications use. The severity of CAP was evaluated using the CURB-65 score, physical examination findings (e.g., blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, oxygenation index). In addition, laboratory tests at admission were also recorded.

Self-reported loneliness

Loneliness was assessed via a single self-reported question—“Do you feel lonely?”, categorizing participants as lonely or non-lonely based on a yes/no answer. Although this direct measurement has been criticized because of the subjectivity leading to low reliability, it has been shown to have good validity and predictive utility in numerous studies, including for elderly populations [9, 10].

Geriatric assessments

Frailty

was evaluated using the Fried Frailty Phenotype, comprising five criteria: unintentional weight loss, self-reported exhaustion, weakness (grip strength), slow walking speed, and low physical activity. Participants were classified as frail (≥ 3 criteria), pre-frail (1–2 criteria), or robust (0 criteria) [11].

Cognitive function

was assessed with the MMSE, using education-specific cutoffs for impairment: ≤19 for illiterate individuals, ≤ 22 for elementary education, and ≤ 26 for middle school education or higher [12].

Affective status

was measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-2 (GAD-2), with a cutoff score of ≥ 3 indicating depressive or anxiety symptoms [13].

Sleep disturbance

was evaluated based on the use of sleep medications and the Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS). The AIS is a self-administered, 8-item questionnaire developed to assess the severity of insomnia in accordance with ICD-10 criteria. Each item measures a specific aspect of sleep disturbance, such as sleep induction, nighttime awakenings, and daytime dysfunction—on a 0 to 3 scale, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 24. A total score of ≥ 6 was used to define clinically significant insomnia [14].

Physical function

was evaluated with the Barthel Index (BI), a validated scale assessing ten BADL domains (e.g., feeding, bathing, mobility). Scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better function. Baseline BI scores reflected self-reported function two weeks prior to hospitalization [15, 16].

Malnutrition

was objectively defined as a body mass index (BMI) < 18.5 kg/m², in accordance with the guidelines of the European Society of Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) [17].

Comorbidity burden

was assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), based on an integrated analysis of self-reported information and clinically documented diagnoses.

Social factors

included marital status, living alone, and pension satisfaction.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality at 180 days post-discharge. The secondary outcome was readmission due to respiratory infection within 180 days. Data were sourced from electronic medical records, supplemented by telephone follow-up with patients or proxies when necessary.

Follow-up

Trained medical staff conducted telephone follow-ups at 30, 90, and 180 days post-discharge. Participants were considered lost to follow-up if they had no hospital records and could not be reached after three consecutive call attempts.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables, assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, were non-normally distributed and reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Participants were stratified by loneliness status, with between-group comparisons performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Patients were stratified into two groups based on loneliness status. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Survival analyses were conducted to examine differences in all-cause mortality and respiratory infection-related readmission between the loneliness and non-loneliness groups. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were plotted and compared using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with adjustment for potential confounders identified from prior literature and clinical judgment. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals in R, and no significant violations were observed. To ensure robust variable selection for the Cox regression model, we implemented a multi-step approach integrating causal inference. First, based on prior evidence from literature reviews and expert consultation, we assessed multicollinearity among potential variables using the variance inflation factor (VIF), confirming that no substantial multicollinearity existed (all VIF < 10). Second, we constructed a directed acyclic graph (DAG) to identify covariates requiring adjustment to minimize confounding bias, resulting in the inclusion of age, gender, loneliness, BI, frailty, emotional disorder, CURB-65, and CCI. For dimensionality reduction (Supplementary Figure S1), we applied LASSO Cox regression, with the regularization parameter (λ) determined via 10-fold cross-validation to optimize predictive performance. To further enhance the stability of variable selection, we performed 1,000 bootstrap resamples (sampling 70% of participants with replacement in each iteration) and calculated the selection frequency of each variable. Variables with a selection frequency ≥ 0.2 were retained for the final model. This relatively low threshold was deliberately chosen to avoid excluding potentially important predictors given the sample size constraints. The final model retained age, loneliness, BI, frailty, and emotional disorder (supplementary Figure S2).

To explore associations between loneliness and other clinical variables, Kendall’s tau-b correlation coefficients were calculated and visualized as a heatmap. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0 and R software version 4.2.2.

Results

Of 619 CAP patients included, the median age was 78 years (IQR: 70.0–85.0), and 65.9% were male. Loneliness was reported by 79 (12.8%) participants. Compared to non-lonely patients, lonely patients were older (median: 80.0 vs. 77.0 years, P = 0.004), less likely to be male (55.7% vs. 67.4%, P = 0.040), unmarried (58.2% vs. 80.2%, P < 0.001), and living alone (30.4% vs. 9.4%, P < 0.001). Lonely patients exhibited lower BI scores (75.0 vs. 90.0, P = 0.030), higher prevalence of insomnia (55.7% vs. 38.1%, P = 0.003), emotional disorder (16.5% vs. 4.1%, P < 0.001), and cognitive impairment (46.8% vs. 27.4%, P < 0.001). The etiological diagnosis was established in 47.0% patients, among whom 37.2% had bacterial infections and 10.3% had viral infections. Laboratory findings showed lower hemoglobin (P = 0.028) and sodium levels (P = 0.044) in lonely patients. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the study participants. While some variables, such as attitude toward retirement pay, did not show statistically significant differences between groups (P>0.05), they are included here for completeness and to provide a comprehensive overview of the study sample. Interestingly, a relatively high level of satisfaction with retirement pay was observed across all cohorts, with about 70.0% of patients reporting satisfaction in the total sample, the lonely group, and the non-lonely group alike.

Basic characteristics of the older patients with CAP

All-cause mortality rates were 2.3%, 4.0%, 5.5%, and 8.7% at discharge and at 30, 90, and 180 days post-discharge, respectively (Fig. 1). The cumulative readmission rates for respiratory infection were 4.4% at 30 days, 8.4% at 60 days, and 12.6% at 90 days post-discharge. The estimated 180-days survival and readmitted hazards by loneliness are presented with Kaplan-Meier curves in Fig. 2. Loneliness was associated with increased short- and long-term mortality risk. After adjusting for age, frailty and BI, lonely patients had a 2.047-fold higher risk of 180-day mortality (adjusted HR: 2.047; 95% CI: 1.036–4.044). However, no significant association was found between loneliness and 180-day readmission for respiratory infection (Table 2). During the 180-day follow-up period, 78 patients (12.60%) were readmitted due to respiratory infection. Among them, 27 (4.36%) were readmitted within 30 days after discharge, 25 (4.04%) between 30 and 90 days, and 26 (4.20%) between 90 and 180 days. Kendall’s tau-b analysis revealed moderate correlations between loneliness and marital status (τ = 0.18) and depressive symptoms (PHQ-2, τ = 0.17), with weaker associations for anxiety (GAD-2, τ = 0.13), insomnia (τ = 0.12), and comorbidity burden (CCI, τ = 0.11) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to examine the association between loneliness and clinical outcomes in older adults with CAP. Loneliness emerged as an independent risk factor for short- and long-term all-cause mortality, but not for respiratory infection-related readmission.

Loneliness has been recognized as a significant public health issue [18, 19], and has been described as an “epidemic of loneliness” [20]. Especially in the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic, loneliness among older adults has significantly worsened [21, 22]. In our study, the reported prevalence of loneliness among older adults with CAP was 12.8%. However, this prevalence may be underestimated, as cultural norms of emotional restraint among the older adults. Another study by researchers at the University of Michigan reported that 34% of older adults experienced feelings of loneliness [23].

Accumulating evidence indicates that loneliness is not merely a social issue but also constitutes a serious threat to both physical and mental health, as well as social behavioral functioning [24]. Previous studies have demonstrated that loneliness is associated with an increased risk of acute cardiovascular events and the development of various chronic conditions, including hypertension, obesity, immune dysfunction, anxiety, depression, cognitive decline, gastrointestinal disorders, cataracts or glaucoma, osteoarthritis, chronic respiratory diseases [7, 8, 25–27]. Our finding that loneliness remains significantly associated with all-cause mortality, even after adjusting for confounders, underscores the need to elucidate the mechanisms through which loneliness translates into a significantly increased risk of mortality. Indeed, several potential mechanisms have been proposed to explain the impacts of loneliness on death—primarily poor health behaviors, a heightened stress response, and inadequate physiological repair—which could be mapped onto the pathophysiology of aging and acute infection [28]. Poor health behaviors represent a widely accepted explanation. For instance, it has been reported that lonely individuals are at greater risk of physical inactivity and smoking, as well as engaging in multiple health-risk behaviors [29]. However, our analysis revealed the association between smoking and loneliness was not significant.

Notably, the heightened stress response pathway provides a direct biological link. Loneliness acts as a chronic psychological stressor, neurologically activating the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to insufficient expression of genes carrying anti-inflammatory glucocorticoid response elements and upregulation of pro-inflammatory gene transcripts. This process is accompanied by abnormal proportions of circulating leukocytes (e.g., neutrophils, lymphocytes, and monocytes) and an enhanced inflammatory response [30, 31]. For an older adult, this persistent inflammatory milieu is a key driver of frailty, sarcopenia, and malnutrition—geriatric syndromes that directly deplete the physiological reserves needed to mount an effective defense against an acute infection like pneumonia [32, 33]. A frail patient with diminished muscle mass and nutritional reserves is less able to cope with the hypermetabolic demand of infection, leading to respiratory failure, multi-organ dysfunction, and ultimately, death [34].

Furthermore, loneliness is also closely linked to impaired physiological repair, with sleep being the most fundamental restorative behavior. Previous studies have demonstrated an association between sleep disturbances and loneliness [35], which aligns with our findings. The possible explanation is that, sleep is essential for immune regulation, tissue repair, and cognitive function. And chronic sleep deprivation, therefore, can accelerate the progression of frailty and sarcopenia, further weakening the patient’s resilience [36]. While the causal direction between loneliness and poor sleep can be bidirectional, their co-occurrence creates a vicious cycle that critically impairs an older adult’s ability to recover from an acute physiological insult [37]. However, the specific molecular mechanism is still not clear and needs further exploration.

Additionally, contrary to most prior research linking loneliness to a higher risk and rate of readmission [38, 39], we observed no significant relationship between loneliness and readmission risk in our Cox regression analysis. While this finding was unexpected, there were some existing literature consistent with our found. For example, Gloria et al. surveyed 300 hospitalized older adults across three Italian hospitals and found that global loneliness were not associated with readmission risk in longitudinal analysis [40]. Here we discuss potential explanations for this null result. One plausible explanation is that loneliness may exert its influence on health through chronic pathways, such as sustained physiological stress responses, dysregulation of the immune system, or poorer long-term health behaviors [41]. These mechanisms are more likely to contribute to cumulative health deterioration and mortality over time, rather than directly precipitating an acute event like a respiratory infection that leads to readmission. Besides, readmission is often more directly influenced by biological factors (e.g., pathogen virulence, pulmonary function), the quality of initial care or the number of comorbidities, potentially diminishing the relative impact of a psychosocial factor like loneliness [42, 43]. Alternatively, the null finding for readmission could be due to a limited sample size or a lower-than-expected number of readmission events, which may have precluded us from detecting a modest association.

Other findings concern the risk factors associated with loneliness. Our study revealed that loneliness was closely associated with living alone, marital status, cognitive function, and mental health. Specifically, patients who were married, exhibited lower levels of anxiety and depression, and had fewer comorbidities were less prone to experience loneliness. These findings are consistent with previous studies that highlights loneliness as a substantial health issue affecting a significant segment of the population, increasing risks of distress (depression, anxiety, etc.), suicidal ideation, unhealthy behaviors, and higher healthcare utilization [44]. While these factors may mediate the relationship between loneliness and mortality, their precise causal mechanisms remain unclear and warrant further investigation. But more importantly, these findings indicate that loneliness is potentially modifiable. Interventions such as avoiding living alone, promoting marital relationships, improving psychological well-being and sleep quality, and reducing comorbidities may help prevent, treat, or even reverse loneliness [45]. In fact, lots of reviews and clinical trials have examined “Interventions” to improve loneliness in the older, which often typically aim to provide psychological therapy and social support by expanding social networks and improving social contact [46, 47]. In recent years, the rapid development of artificial intelligence and network has also made it studied by the majority of scholars as one of the media of loneliness intervention [48]. For example, Lim et al. conducted a non-randomized controlled trial, revealing that after six weeks of intervention with the social robot PIO, participants demonstrated enhanced cognitive performance along with reduced scores on depression and loneliness scales [49].

The key strengths of this study include being the first to examine the impact of loneliness on clinical outcomes in older patients with CAP, as well as the use of a multi-center design and extended follow-up period. However, this study has several limitations. First, although the clinical relevance of loneliness has been increasingly recognized, its assessment in our study relied on a single self-reported item, which may not fully capture the multidimensional nature of loneliness. Nevertheless, our findings suggest that even a simple self-reported measure of loneliness is clinically meaningful and predictive of adverse outcomes, highlighting the importance of incorporating loneliness into clinical evaluation. Second, loneliness was measured only once at admission and we were unable to track changes in loneliness status over time. Future studies should use validated multidimensional tools to assess loneliness and track its changes over time. Third, a pathogen diagnosis was not established for over half of the patients. However, pathogen identification remains a well-recognized challenge in CAP as previous studies have reported that causative organisms are only identified in 5–20% of CAP cases in the elderly [50].

Conclusion

This multi-center cohort study highlights loneliness as an independent risk factor for all-cause mortality in older adults with CAP. Loneliness was associated with marital status, emotional disorders, sleep disturbances, and comorbidities. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating loneliness into mortality risk assessments and support the use of comprehensive geriatric evaluations in hospitalized CAP patients.
