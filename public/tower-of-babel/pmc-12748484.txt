Current progress and future trends in nanoparticle applications for lymphoma: a bibliometric analysis from 2004 to 2024
Authors: Hongwei Lin, Xiaolin He, Luming Huang, Xiao Xiao, Qijun Hou, Huafang Wang, Yang Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLymphoma continues to pose a major global health challenge. While the application of nanoparticles (NPs) in lymphoma research has shown considerable promise, a comprehensive evaluation of research trends and the underlying knowledge structure remains lacking. This study aims to systematically evaluate the current progress and future trends in NP applications for lymphoma.MethodsWe performed a bibliometric analysis using CiteSpace, VOSviewer, and R software on 1040 publications retrieved from the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) between 2004 and 2024. Data on publications, countries, institutions, journals, authors, references, and keywords were analyzed.ResultsResearch output demonstrated a consistent upward trend, with China and the United States emerging as the leading contributors. The Chinese Academy of Sciences had the highest output, while the University of North Carolina led in citation impact. The International Journal of Nanomedicine published the most studies, whereas Nature Reviews Drug Discovery ranked first in citations. Partha Partim Manna was the most prolific author, and Jindrich Kopecek’s work had the strongest influence. Our analysis revealed crucial research areas, including enhanced drug delivery, nanovaccines, nanodiagnostics and monitoring, green synthesis, and the optimization of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy.ConclusionThis bibliometric analysis suggests that several directions in NP applications for lymphoma may hold promise for near-term progress. NP-mediated chemotherapy and targeted therapy could improve drug delivery and help overcome resistance, while NP-based imaging agents may enhance sensitivity and enable real-time monitoring. The integration of NPs with CAR-T therapy may also offer strategies to improve efficacy and safety. Additional avenues, such as nanovaccines and green synthesis, highlight opportunities for immune modulation and biocompatibility. Despite notable translational challenges, multifunctional NP platforms may gradually contribute to advances in lymphoma management.Keywords: Nanoparticles, Lymphoma, Drug delivery, Bibliometrics, Therapy

Introduction

Lymphoma, a heterogeneous malignancy originating from the lymphatic system, is primarily categorized into two types: Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) and non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) [1]. According to the Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN) 2022, lymphoma remains a significant public health burden. In particular, NHL accounted for 553,000 new cases and 250,000 deaths, placing it 10th in incidence and 11th in mortality rates among all cancers, while remaining the most prevalent among hematologic malignancies. Furthermore, an estimated 82,000 new HL cases occur annually worldwide [2]. The complex biological characteristics of lymphoma result in substantial variability in its clinical presentation [3]. Consequently, there is an urgent need in contemporary clinical practice for the development of more effective therapeutic strategies to address this significant medical challenge.

Over recent decades, lymphoma treatment has advanced significantly. Traditional approaches, including chemotherapy, monoclonal antibodies, and radiotherapy, have been widely used in clinical practice [4]. In addition, new therapies like targeted therapy and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell immunotherapy have significantly improved survival outcomes for patients with certain lymphoma subtypes [5, 6]. The rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (R-CHOP) regimen has become the standard first-line treatment for diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) [7]. Brentuximab vedotin and immune checkpoint inhibitors have shown remarkable efficacy in treating relapsed or refractory HL [8]. However, many patients still experience relapse or resistance, particularly in aggressive or indolent subtypes [9, 10]. High-intensity regimens cause severe toxicity, limiting use in elderly or immunocompromised patients [11]. Additionally, the molecular characteristics of lymphoma are highly heterogeneous, which complicates the development of standardized treatment protocols [12]. Given these limitations, developing novel therapeutic strategies that focus on enhancing drug delivery efficiency, minimizing systemic toxicity, and increasing the treatment precision has emerged as a critical challenge [13].

Nanoparticles (NPs) typically refer to materials with particle sizes ranging from 1 to 100 nanometers [14]. Due to their unique physicochemical properties, such as a large surface area, diverse surface functionalities, and the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, NPs have found widespread applications in the biomedical field [15, 16]. In cancer treatment, NPs can facilitate targeted drug delivery, controlled release, optimize pharmacokinetics, and reduce systemic toxicity [17]. These advantages are particularly valuable in hematologic malignancies like lymphoma, where conventional therapies face limitations in biodistribution and off-target effects [18]. Researchers have developed various types of NP carriers, such as liposomes (LPs), polymeric nanoparticles (PNPs), dendritic polymers (DPs), and gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), to deliver chemotherapeutic drugs, monoclonal antibodies, or nucleic acids. These NP systems have significantly improved drug targeting and bioavailability [19]. Additionally, functionalized NPs show remarkable potential for concurrent therapeutic and diagnostic applications [20]. Several investigations have also explored the immunomodulatory approaches mediated by NPs [21]. These developments highlight the role of NPs in enabling personalized, low-toxicity, and effective lymphoma therapies, providing technological support to overcome existing clinical barriers.

In recent years, research on the application of NPs in lymphoma diagnosis and treatment has shown steady growth. Although several bibliometric analyses and reviews have explored the use of nanomaterials in hematologic malignancies or general cancer therapy, none have specifically focused on the intersection between NPs and lymphoma [22–24]. Bibliometric analysis offers an objective approach to identifying leading research groups, influential academic contributions, and emerging interdisciplinary hotspots by quantitatively evaluating the scientific literature [25]. In this study, we performed a systematic bibliometric analysis of NP applications in lymphoma, using data from the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) and visualization tools such as VOSviewer and CiteSpace. Our goal is to provide insights that may inform and promote future progress in both basic and clinical research.

Methods

Data source and search protocol

The study employed a systematic search of English-language publications from the WoSCC database from January 1, 2004, to December 31, 2024. The search terms [TS=(“nano* material*” OR “nanoparticle*” OR “nanotechnology” OR “nanomedicine” OR “nano-based” OR “nano-carrier*”) AND TS=(“lymphoma” OR “lymphoma treatment” OR “lymphoma therapy” OR “lymphoma management” OR “Hodgkin lymphoma” OR “non-Hodgkin lymphoma”)] were used to identify relevant publications. To prevent citation count variations caused by real-time database updates, the literature search was conducted exclusively on March 25, 2025. From the 1083 studies initially identified, 1040 reviews and articles were selected for bibliometric analysis. The remaining 51 publications were excluded, comprising meeting abstracts, retracted publications, editorial materials, and other ineligible document types. The overall process is shown in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of the publications screening process. A total of 1086 publications related to the application of nanoparticles in lymphoma from 2004 to 2024 were retrieved from the Web of Science Core Collection. After excluding irrelevant and duplicate records, 1040 publications were included in the final analysis. The exported data were analyzed using VOSviewer, CiteSpace, and R software

Bibliometric analysis and visualization

We utilized complementary tools, including CiteSpace (version 6.3. R1), VOSviewer (version 1.6.20), and R software (version 4.4.3) to generate comprehensive visualizations of the bibliometric results. These tools enabled multidimensional analysis of publications, countries, institutions, journals, authors, references, and keywords. CiteSpace was used for cluster analysis, dual-map overlays of journals, identification of emerging trends, and detection of reference and keyword bursts. VOSviewer, a widely used visualization tool, was applied to construct country collaboration maps and keyword co-occurrence networks. Additionally, R software was used to produce bar charts, line graphs, tables, and treemaps to present key findings. All data were obtained from publicly available databases. Ethical approval was not required, as the study involved neither human participants nor animal subjects.

Results

Analysis of all publications and citations

We analyzed 1040 publications spanning 2004 to 2024. Although the annual number of publications fluctuated, it showed an overall upward trend, with an average of 52 publications per year. From 2004 to 2009, research on NP applications for lymphoma was still in its infancy, with only 46 publications. Since 2010, the field has experienced notable growth and reached a peak of 113 publications in 2024, reflecting the increasing global interest in this area. Citation trends have mirrored the rise in publication volume, further highlighting the growing influence of research in this domain (Fig. 2).

Analysis of countries and institutions

A total of 53 countries and regions contributed to the global research output. Among them, China emerged as the most productive with 306 publications, surpassing the United States, which produced 202 publications. In terms of research impact, however, the United States maintained clear leadership, amassing 15,763 total citations. This number is nearly twice the citation count achieved by China, which reached 8421. This contrast highlights the emphasis on research quantity in China, whereas the United States demonstrates a stronger influence and research quality. Notably, the United States, Japan, and Canada reported the highest average citation counts per publication, affirming their status as centers of high-impact research (Table 1). As illustrated in Fig. 3, extensive international collaboration is evident in the field of NP applications for lymphoma. The United States stands out as the most collaborative country, maintaining a broad and active global network. In particular, cooperation between China and the United States is the most frequent, further highlighting their leading roles in this area.

Analysis of journals

The analysis identified a total of 410 journals contributing to this research area. The International Journal of Nanomedicine was the most productive, publishing 42 papers and achieving an h-index of 21. Its content primarily focused on innovative studies related to nanodrug delivery systems, nanodiagnostics, and nanotoxicity assessments. In comparison, Nature Reviews Drug Discovery received the highest number of citations, totaling 2,993, largely attributable to the influential review titled “Strategies in the design of NPs for therapeutic applications” by Robby A. Petros et al., published in 2010. As a leading review journal in drug development, it publishes high-quality reviews and insightful perspectives that span drug discovery, development, and translational medicine (Table 3). The dual-map overlay analysis visually represents the distribution and relationships among journal topics. As illustrated in Fig. 4, four major citation pathways were identified. Publications originating from the fields of Physics/Materials/Chemistry and Molecular/Biology/Immunotherapy most frequently cited journals in the areas of Chemistry/Materials/Physics and Molecular/Biology/Genetics. This pattern highlights the highly interdisciplinary nature of research in this domain.

The third category, research objects and experimental materials, consists of “lymphoma”, the central focus of this research field, along with “murine beta-defensin 2” and “Alexa Fluor 488”, both of which are commonly used in immunological studies and imaging experiments. Discussion

Frontiers and hotspots

In bibliometric studies, reference and keyword analyses are instrumental in identifying core research themes and uncovering emerging trends. These methods facilitate a comprehensive understanding of the knowledge structure, developmental trajectories, and potential research gaps within a given field. In this study, such analyses revealed five major thematic clusters in the application of NPs for lymphoma: chemotherapy and targeted therapy, nanovaccines, diagnostic imaging, green synthesis and nanotoxicity, and integration with CAR-T cell therapy. Together, these clusters not only map the current research landscape but also shed light on promising future directions.

Chemotherapy and targeted therapy

Frequent keywords such as “liposomes,” “bortezomib,” “cytarabine,” and “ibrutinib” indicate sustained interest in nanocarrier-mediated drug delivery systems and targeted therapeutics. These bibliometric findings are supported by existing studies demonstrating that nanocarriers, including LPs, PNPs, and lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), improve drug accumulation at tumor sites, enhance therapeutic efficacy, and reduce systemic toxicity [26]. For instance, liposomal formulations of doxorubicin and vincristine have been widely adopted to mitigate adverse effects while maintaining antitumor activity [27–29]. Further optimization strategies, such as surface modification with antibodies targeting CD20, have improved the specificity and cellular uptake of these platforms [30]. Certain nanocarriers are also designed to be responsive to the tumor microenvironment, allowing controlled drug release under specific physiological conditions [31]. Beyond preclinical research, clinical evaluation of nanocarrier-based therapies has also been initiated. A phase I trial (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT03003546) is assessing nab-paclitaxel/rituximab-coated NPs (AR160) in patients with relapsed or refractory B-cell NHL, focusing on dose optimization and safety. This formulation integrates the cytotoxic activity of paclitaxel albumin-stabilized NPs with the immunotherapeutic effects of rituximab, exemplifying the translational potential of nanocarrier platforms. Collectively, these advancements may offer potential strategies to improve treatment outcomes in lymphoma, although further clinical validation is required.

Nanovaccines

The prominence of terms such as “vaccines,” “adjuvants,” and “antigen” reflects the growing focus on immunotherapeutic strategies. Conventional lymphoma vaccines are often limited by suboptimal immunogenicity and inefficient lymph node targeting [32]. NP-based vaccine platforms provide enhanced antigen stability, facilitate targeted delivery to lymphoid tissues, and promote efficient uptake by antigen-presenting cells [33]. Incorporating immunostimulatory molecules such as toll-like receptor (TLR) ligands into NP systems has been shown to boost dendritic cell activation and stimulate robust T cell responses [34]. These features suggest the potential of nanovaccines to elicit durable antitumor immunity, as shown in preclinical lymphoma models, though clinical efficacy remains to be established.

Diagnostic imaging and monitoring

Keywords such as “gold nanoparticles,” “MRI,” and “tracking” suggest increasing emphasis on diagnostic applications of nanotechnology. Traditional imaging modalities, including computed tomography (CT) and positron emission tomography (PET), have limitations in terms of sensitivity and resolution. To address these challenges, various functional NPs have been developed for enhanced imaging, including gold nanorods (AuNRs), iron oxide nanoparticles (IONPs), and PNPs. These nanomaterials can be conjugated with antibodies or aptamers that recognize lymphoma-specific markers, thereby improving target specificity [35–38]. In addition, nanozymes with enzyme-like activity have been engineered to generate detectable signals in response to tumor-associated substrates, facilitating real-time monitoring of disease progression and treatment efficacy [39]. Some platforms integrate both diagnostic and therapeutic capabilities, which may contribute to the development of theranostic approaches that are currently under preclinical investigation [40, 41]. Clinical translation is also beginning to emerge. An ongoing phase I study (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT07066436) is evaluating ImmunoMRI for DLBCL, in which an FDA-approved IONP formulation (ferumoxytol) is repurposed as an MRI contrast agent to visualize tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). This approach enables whole-body assessment of immune cell dynamics before and after CAR T-cell or bispecific antibody therapy, aiming to predict remission status, progression-free survival, and the risk of immune-related toxicities. Such investigations exemplify how nanotechnology-enabled imaging can bridge mechanistic insights with clinical decision-making, potentially guiding individualized treatment strategies.

Green synthesis and nanotoxicity

Emerging terms such as “green synthesis” and “silver nanoparticles” point to increasing awareness of environmental and biosafety concerns. Traditional synthesis methods often involve hazardous reagents, which may lead to cytotoxicity and contamination [42]. Green synthesis approaches, by contrast, utilize biologically derived reducing agents such as plant extracts and operate under mild reaction conditions. These methods minimize the environmental burden and enhance biocompatibility, making them more suitable for biomedical applications [43]. Continued progress in this area may improve the safety profile of NPs and expand their clinical applicability.

Integration with CAR-T cell therapy

The emergence of keywords such as “CD20,” “receptor,” “lipid nanoparticles,” and “gene therapy” in recent years indicates a growing research interest at the intersection of nanotechnology and cellular immunotherapy. These terms were primarily detected in recent publications, as revealed by keyword burst and temporal trend analyses, suggesting that this topic represents a newly developing frontier in the field. Although CAR-T cell therapy has demonstrated remarkable efficacy in treating certain subtypes of lymphoma, its broader application is currently restricted by high manufacturing costs, T cell exhaustion, and severe treatment-related toxicities [44]. NP-based technologies offer potential solutions to these limitations. For example, LPs and LNPs can be used to deliver mRNA encoding CARs, allowing transient expression in T cells without the need for viral vectors [45]. This approach reduces immunogenicity and eliminates the risk of insertional mutagenesis [46, 47]. Moreover, NP can facilitate the delivery of supportive agents such as interleukins or immune checkpoint inhibitors, which help to prolong CAR-T cell persistence and enhance therapeutic efficacy [48, 49]. The integration of nanotechnology into CAR-T therapy represents an emerging research direction, holding potential to enhance therapeutic precision, reduce toxicity, and possibly lower production barriers, while these advantages require further validation. The bibliometric evidence supports this shift, indicating a strong trend toward the convergence of material science and adoptive cell therapy in the pursuit of next-generation lymphoma treatments.

Taken together, these bibliometric findings, when interpreted in the context of current research, suggest that future studies will likely focus on the development of multifunctional NP systems that simultaneously address diagnosis, drug delivery, immune modulation, and cell therapy enhancement. Such efforts may play an important role in advancing personalized treatment approaches for lymphoma and addressing barriers such as resistance and relapse.

Cross-disease comparison and translational gap

When comparing bibliometric findings from NP research in lymphoma with those in other hematological malignancies, such as leukemia, a distinct disparity emerges. NP research in leukemia exhibits significantly higher publication output, stronger citation performance, greater researcher engagement, and a more advanced degree of clinical translation [24]. This discrepancy may stem from fundamental biological differences between leukemia and lymphoma, as well as varying degrees of clinical urgency [50–52].

Reviewing the progression of NP research in lymphoma, it is evident that, despite their considerable potential demonstrated in cellular and animal models, most findings remain confined to the preclinical stage, with limited advancement into clinical settings. This gap may be attributed in part to the substantial biological heterogeneity of lymphoma and the complexities of ensuring treatment safety [53–55]. Nevertheless, the advantages of NPs in improving diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic efficacy suggest broad application prospects. Future research is expected to facilitate the shift from experimental studies to clinical implementation.

Advances and limitations

Our research employs bibliometric methods to systematically analyze publications, research hotspots, and trends related to NP applications in lymphoma. This approach provides a structured overview of the field, helping to identify key frontiers and guiding future investigations. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Primarily, this study relies solely on the WoSCC database, which was selected for its standardized bibliometric indicators and reproducibility; nevertheless, this choice may have resulted in the omission of relevant publications indexed in other databases such as Scopus and PubMed. Secondly, we restricted our analysis to English-language publications in order to ensure consistency, accessibility, and comparability of bibliometric indicators across studies; however, this choice may have led to the exclusion of valuable research published in other languages, particularly from non-English-speaking countries with active contributions in nanomedicine and hematology. Thirdly, while tools like VOSviewer and CiteSpace yield useful insights, their outputs are heavily influenced by keyword selection and analytical settings, which may result in the omission of some non-explicit hotspots or emerging trends.

Conclusion

This bibliometric analysis outlines several research directions in NP applications for lymphoma that may hold promise for near-term progress. NP-mediated chemotherapy and targeted therapy could improve drug delivery, enhance solubility, and help overcome resistance. Diagnostic imaging and monitoring with NP-based agents, including IONPs and AuNPs, may offer opportunities to increase sensitivity and support real-time assessment of treatment response. The integration of NPs with CAR-T therapy may help to address certain limitations in efficacy and safety. Other directions, such as nanovaccines and green synthesis, present complementary possibilities through immune modulation and improved biocompatibility. Future investigations may increasingly focus on multifunctional NP platforms that combine therapeutic and diagnostic functions. Despite notable translational barriers, these developments suggest that NPs could gradually contribute to advances in the management of lymphoma.
