Effects of Jintiange on the healing of osteoporotic fractures in aged rats
Authors: Jie Liu, Ting-Ting Liu, Hai-Cheng Zhang, Chen Li, Wei Wei, Ai-Jun Chao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo examine the effects of Jintiange on enhancing the healing of osteoporotic fractures in aged rats.MethodsAn osteoporotic fracture model of femur was established using 70 SD rats (aged > 12 months), which were randomly numbered and divided into an experimental group and a control group, each with an equal sample size (n = 35). The experimental group received Jintiange capsule ingredients via intragastric administration, while the control group received an equal volume of saline via the same method. X-ray examinations were conducted at the 4th and 12th weeks to evaluate fracture healing in the rats. After 12 weeks, micro-CT scanning was employed to assess the microstructure of the healthy femurs, and the parameters of the experimental and control groups were compared.ResultsAfter 4 weeks, the mean fracture healing scores were 0.78 ± 0.43 in the experimental group and 0.56 ± 0.51 in the control group. After 12 weeks, the mean scores were 1.50 ± 0.71 in the experimental group and 0.96 ± 0.68 in the control group. The scores in the experimental group were higher than those in the control group (P < 0.05). Micro-CT examination of the femurs showed an improvement trend in bone microarchitecture in the experimental group, indicated by increased bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb. N), and trabecular thickness (Tb. Th). However, these improvements were not statistically significant. A negative correlation with statistical significance was observed between trabecular separation (Tb. Sp) and fracture healing scores.Conclusions Jintiange may promote osteoporotic fracture healing in aged rats by enhancing bone microstructure.

Background

Osteoporosis is a systemic bone disease marked by low bone mass, deterioration of bone microstructure, increased bone fragility, and a heightened risk of fractures [1]. As osteoporosis prevalence increases in the population, one of its severe complications, which is osteoporotic fractures, has garnered significant attention. According to the International Osteoporosis Foundation’s 2013 report, half of women and one-fifth of men over 50 worldwide experience osteoporotic fractures, with half of these individuals likely to suffer a subsequent fracture [2]. The high incidence of osteoporotic fractures leads to considerable mortality and disability rates, especially with hip fractures. A survey in Hong Kong revealed that female patients aged 65 to 69 with hip fractures have a one-year mortality rate of 6.91% and a five-year mortality rate of 23.80% [3].

Osteoporotic fractures are more prevalent in aged individuals, particularly postmenopausal women. While conventional treatments like surgery, traction, and brace fixation are common, using medications with proven efficacy and minimal side effects is crucial for enhancing fracture healing and reducing recovery time in aged patients. The primary ingredient of the Jintiange capsule is artificial tiger bone powder, which contains various trace elements, amino acids, and peptides. In traditional Chinese medicine, it is believed to benefit the liver and kidney meridians and is thought to dispel wind, unblock meridians, and strengthen muscles and bones. The Jintiange capsule is used to treat conditions like rheumatism, numbness, sore feet, and knee pain.

Clinical studies have shown that Jintiange can treat osteoporosis and alleviate related pain symptoms [4, 5]. There has been some research that has explored the healing effects of Jintiange and its mechanisms in patients with osteoporotic fractures, but few studies have evaluated its impact on fracture healing in aged patients. In this study, we created an osteoporotic fracture model using aged and estrogen-deficient rats to better simulate osteoporotic fractures in aged person. We investigated the effect of Jintiange on promoting fracture healing, aiming to provide clinical guidance for elderly patients with osteoporotic fractures, shorten fracture healing time, and reduce the risk of fracture related complications.

Materials and methods

Animals

Seventy female SD rats, over 12 months old and weighing between 180 and 200 g, were purchased from Beijing HFK Bioscience Co., Ltd. These rats were randomly assigned to experimental and control groups using a random number table and housed at the Institute of Radiation Medicine, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. They underwent a two-week acclimatization period with a standard rodent diet and free access to water in a controlled environment at (22 ± 3) ℃. Routine disinfection and ventilation were maintained as per guidelines. The study received approval from the Animal Ethics Committee of Tianjin Hospital, with the ethical number: Medical Ethics Review 049.

Drugs

Jintiange capsules were supplied by Xi’an Ginwa Pharmaceutical Factory, Ginwa Enterprise (Group) Inc., under National Drug Approval No. Z20030080. Each capsule contains 0.4 g of pure artificial tiger bone powder. The artificial tiger bone powder was extracted from the bones of various farmed animals (cervus elaphus, cervus nippon, etc.) and contained proteins and minerals.

Equipment

Digital radiography (DR) machine; micro-CT scanning system (Guangzhou Zhongke Kaisheng Medical Technology Co., Ltd. ZKKS Micro-CT 3.0).

Establishment of the osteoporotic fracture model with aged rats

Establishment of the aged postmenopausal osteoporosis model

To establish a postmenopausal osteoporosis model, aged rats were used. Five rats were randomly selected to scan bilateral femur and tibia with Dual-Energy X-Ray Absorptiometry (DEXA). The rats were anesthetized with 2.5% isoflurane for induction and maintained with 0.2–0.4% isoflurane. After shaving and sterilizing the abdomen with iodine tincture, an ovariectomy and partial salpingectomy were performed. A 1 to 2 cm incision was made in the lateral abdomen, and fascia and muscle were bluntly opened with scissors to expose the ovaries and part of the fallopian tubes, which were then excised. The remaining fallopian tubes were ligated, the adipose tissue was repositioned into the abdominal cavity, and the incision was sutured. Penicillin was administered postoperatively to prevent infection, continuing for 3 days. After 12 weeks of feeding, DEXA again detected the same site, and it was found that the BMD of rats after ovariectomy was (0.2722 ± 0.0337(mg/cm2), n = 5) significantly lower than that of rats before ovariectomy (0.3268 ± 0.0209(mg/cm2), n = 5), p < 0.05, indicating statistical significance. Osteoporosis model was established successfully. (Fig. 1)

Establishment of the aged osteoporotic femur fracture model

After verifying the aged postmenopausal osteoporosis model, the rats were anesthetized with 2.5% isoflurane induction and maintained with 0.2–0.4% isoflurane. The abdomen was shaved and sterilized with iodine tincture. A 2 cm skin incision was made along the medial side of the thigh, extending towards the femur. Subcutaneous tissues were incised or bluntly separated to expose and locate the midsection of the femur. Using a bone hammer, the femur was broken using a swift motion, creating neat opposite facets at each fractured end. The patellar ligaments were then incised and pulled laterally to dislocate the femoral condyle, exposing the intercondylar recess. A Kirschner pin was retrogradely inserted into the greater trochanter of the femur with appropriate length, then severed with bone forceps. The distal end of the pin was buried under the intercondylar cortex, and the skin was sutured. Penicillin injections were administered for three days to prevent infection.

Allocation and administration regimen

Once the aged osteoporotic femur fracture model was established, the rats were randomly assigned to either the experimental or control group using a random number table, with 35 rats in each group. Once the animal model was confirmed and allocated, medication was administered via intra-gastric administration from the second day onwards. Rats in the experimental group received Jintiange capsules (pure artificial tiger bone powder) at a dosage of 0.05 g per kilogram body weight, with a gavage volume of 1 ml/100 g. Rats in the control group received normal saline (50 ml/kg) via intra-gastric administration.

Measurements

General observations

The rats were monitored daily for their overall condition, activity levels, and the healing progress of their incisions. Additionally, their body weight was measured weekly to calculate and adjust the medication dosage accordingly.

X-ray evaluation

X-ray examinations were initially performed at four weeks after the administration of Jintiange. The rats were anesthetized, and anteroposterior X-ray images of the femur were taken to observe the progress of fracture healing. Following the X-ray examination, the rats were treated according to the prescribed regimen, with dosage adjustments made based on their body weight. A second X-ray examination was conducted at 12 weeks to assess fracture healing. The evaluation of fracture healing was conducted independently by two radiologists who were not involved in the study. Assessment criteria included the width of the fracture line, the formation of bony callus around the fracture, and the degree of mineralization, each scored on a scale of 0, 1, or 2 (0 indicating non-union, 1 indicating partial union, and 2 indicating union). Garrett’s evaluation standard was mainly used as the main reference, but in actual operation, it was found that the fracture ends showed too obvious differences, and it was difficult to score too fine, otherwise it might cause large errors. Finally, the evaluation standard within the scheme was determined based on Garrett’s score and the actual situation.

If the assessments of the two radiologists were in agreement, the fracture healing was categorized accordingly. In cases where there was disagreement in the assessment, a third independent expert was consulted for further evaluation.

Micro-CT scanning

After the 12-week healing period, animals from both the experimental and control groups were randomly selected for sampling according to a predetermined ratio. The rats were euthanized, and the contralateral femur was harvested. Regions of interest (ROI) were chosen at the cancellous and cortical bone tissues of the femoral head and neck. All specimens underwent independent scanning using a Micro-CT system with the following parameters: voltage set at 60 KV, power at 40 W, detector configuration B1, 4-frame superposition, turntable angle sampling interval of 0.72 degrees/rotation, and 500 projection views. Micro-CT can display detailed imaging changes of the fracture site, and usually has no specific threshold value. Its evaluation of fracture healing is mainly based on data such as bone volume fraction BV/TV, trabecular number Tb.N, trabecular thickness Tb.Th and trabecular separation Tb.Sp, etc. The shape, quantity and density of bone trabeculae in 3D reconstructed images were analyzed. The field of view for the translation stage was set to LargeField. A grayscale value of 81 was used as the threshold to distinguish between bone and non-bone tissues. The ROI was defined as the region within a 5 mm range above and below the fracture site, parallel to the contralateral side. Threshold segmentation was applied to isolate the bone tissue for analysis. Clicking “Display Information” allowed for the visualization of the parameters of the selected ROI. Parameters such as bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular separation (Tb. Sp), and trabecular thickness (Tb. Th) were analyzed and compared in this study.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was performed using R language (version 4.3.2). Quantitative data with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Inter-group comparisons were made using independent-sample t-tests, while correlation analysis was conducted using Pearson correlation analysis. For data that did not follow a normal distribution, statistical description was provided using the median.[P25,P75] Inter-group comparisons were performed using an independent sample rank sum test, and correlation analysis was conducted using the Spearman correlation coefficient. Frequency and constituent ratio were used to describe measurement data, with inter-group comparisons made using the chi-square test. In cases where the data were not suitable for the chi-square test, Fisher’s exact probability test was utilized. Tables and charts were generated using R language software. All tests were two-sided, and a significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General observations

No mortality occurred among the rats during the anesthesia procedure. However, following the establishment of the fracture model, seven rats died, leaving 34 surviving rats in the control group and 29 in the experimental group. All surviving rats did not demonstrate signs of incision infection, and their mobility recovered within 7 days post-operation. Throughout the housing period, additional rat mortalities were observed, resulting in a total of 51 out of 70 rats surviving. The deceased rats displayed symptoms of lethargy and decreased appetite prior to death. Weekly measurements of body weight did not show statistically significant differences between the groups.

X-ray evaluation

Eventually, 43 rats met the criteria and were included in the assessment of fracture healing, comprising 19 in the experimental group and 24 in the control group. Exclusion criteria involved instances such as dislodgment and loosening of the fracture fixation pins. X-ray images depicting fracture healing in rats from both the experimental and control groups at 4 and 12 weeks are illustrated in Fig. 2. At 4 weeks, the X-ray fracture healing scores in the experimental group (0.78 ± 0.43) did not exhibit statistical significance compared to those in the control group (0.56 ± 0.51, t = 1.523, P = -0.136). Conversely, at 12 weeks, the X-ray fracture healing scores for the experimental group (1.50 ± 0.71) were significantly different from those of the control group (0.96 ± 0.68), with a statistically significant difference (t = 2.517, P = -0.016), as depicted in Table 1. The box plots of X-ray fracture healing scores revealed no significant difference between the experimental and control groups at 4 weeks (P > 0.05). However, a statistically significant difference was evident at 12 weeks (P < 0.05), as illustrated in Fig. 3.

Micro-CT scanning

Eight femurs were randomly and proportionally selected from the control group, while seven were chosen from the experimental group for processing and evaluation of bone microstructure using Micro-CT, as illustrated in Fig. 4. Within the ROI, the BV/TV in the experimental animals (0.48 ± 0.25%) appeared higher than that of the control group (0.29 ± 0.06%, t = -1.908, P = 0.101). Similarly, the Tb. N in the experimental group (3.65 ± 0.76/mm) exceeded that of the control group (3.15 ± 0.30/mm, t = -1.610, P = 0.148). Moreover, the Tb. Th in the experimental group (0.12 ± 0.04 μm) was greater than that of the control group (0.09 ± 0.01 μm, t = -1.903, P = 0.102). Conversely, the Tb. Sp in the experimental group (0.16 ± 0.10 μm) was less than that of the control group (0.22 ± 0.03 μm, t = 1.643, P = 0.143). However, none of these differences between the groups reached statistical significance, as illustrated in Table 2. Positive correlations were observed between the fracture healing scores and BV/TV, Tb. N, and Tb. Th at both the 4th and 12th weeks, although these correlations did not achieve statistical significance (P > 0.05). Conversely, a negative correlation was noted between Tb. Sp and fracture healing scores at both the 4th and 12th weeks, respectively, with statistical significance (P < 0.01), as illustrated in Fig. 5.

Discussion

Primary osteoporosis is classified into postmenopausal osteoporosis (Type I) and senile osteoporosis (Type II), reflecting the influence of reduced estrogen levels and aging on bone metabolism, respectively [6]. Estrogen deficiency triggers significant activation of osteoclast function, leading to increased bone resorption and accelerated bone turnover. Senile osteoporosis is primarily characterized by decreased bone turnover, particularly a decline in osteoblast function. However, osteoporosis in aged females encompasses both these primary features [7]. Conventional osteoporosis models typically exhibit rapid bone turnover due to a sudden decline in estrogen levels in 2 to 3-month-old female rats following ovariectomy. In our study, we used female rats aged 12 months or older, undergoing ovariectomy to establish a model that incorporates both aging and estrogen deficiency. This model was designed to more accurately simulate the osteoporosis condition in aged women, aiming to provide experimental evidence and theoretical insights for interventions targeting aged females with osteoporotic fractures.

The most serious complication associated with osteoporosis is osteoporotic fractures, which exhibit distinct characteristics compared to fractures in normal bone. Current understanding suggests that osteoporosis arises from a normal or slightly reduced osteoclastic capacity for bone resorption, coupled with a diminished ability of osteoblasts to form new bone tissue. This imbalance between bone resorption and formation, where resorption outpaces formation, contributes to the development of osteoporosis. Osteoporotic fractures occur when bones predisposed to such changes are subjected to triggers like falls [8]. These fractures occur in bones that have already experienced a reduction in trabecular number and a decline in both the number and function of osteoblasts—key features of osteoporosis—prior to the fracture event. Consequently, the capacity for fracture healing and the healing time are compromised. Studies, such as that by Tianling et al., have observed impaired tibial healing in ovariectomized rats [9]. These rats, lacking estrogen due to ovariectomy, exhibited signs of osteoporosis, including low callus density and inadequate formation of the lamellar callus, compared to non-ovariectomized rats, potentially leading to impaired fracture healing.

Tiger bone, a prominent component in traditional Chinese medicine renowned for its ability to strengthen muscles and bones, serves as the primary ingredient in Jintiange capsules. Artificial tiger bone closely mimics the composition of natural tiger bone, containing various essential elements such as amino acids, collagen, osteopeptides, and a multitude of minerals like calcium, phosphorus, strontium, magnesium, zinc, iron, copper, and manganese. These elements are known to play vital roles in bone formation and regeneration. Studies, such as that by Na et al., have demonstrated the efficacy of artificial tiger bone powder in promoting cell proliferation and enhancing the vitality of osteoblasts [10]. Additionally, it has been shown to stimulate the synthesis and secretion of type 1 collagen by osteoblasts, both crucial factors in the bone healing process. Similarly, research by Weiran et al. involving a rat model of long bone shaft fractures revealed promising outcomes following the administration of Jintiange capsules [11].

Although no significant differences were initially observed between the experimental and control groups, by week 8, the experimental group exhibited a higher recovery rate of maximum load. Histological analysis further demonstrated improved trabecular orientation and thickness, along with increased osteogenesis activity in the experimental group. These findings suggest that Jintiange may enhance bone microstructure, alkaline phosphatase activity, and bone mineral density. In our study involving aged female rats with compromised fracture healing capacity, we observed significantly higher fracture healing scores in the experimental group at both week 4 and week 12 compared to the control group. These results underscore the potential of Jintiange in promoting osteoporotic fracture healing, particularly when administered early, highlighting its potential benefits for aged women with osteoporosis and fragility fractures.

In addition to traumatic incidents, bone strength plays a crucial role in determining the likelihood of fractures. Bone strength encompasses both bone density and bone quality. Bone density refers to the amount of bone constituents present within a given volume or area, which correlates with the microstructure of trabeculae, including their distribution, arrangement, and connectivity. Therefore, the microstructure of bone significantly influences both the occurrence and healing of fractures [12]. Micro-CT scanning, capable of detecting bones with a thickness of nearly 1 μm, allows for the measurement of parameters such as BV/TV, Tb. Sp, Tb. Th, and Tb. N, offering insights into trabecular quality [13]. BV/TV represents the ratio of bone volume to total volume, indicating the amount of effective bone composition. Meanwhile, Tb. Th denotes the average width of trabeculae in a two-dimensional scan, where a greater thickness signifies better bone quality. A higher number of trabeculae also suggests superior bone quality. Conversely, Tb. Sp indicates the average distance between trabeculae, with larger separations suggesting poor bone microstructure [14]. The findings of our study revealed that rats in the experimental group exhibited higher BV/TV, greater Tb. Th, and increased Tb. N in their contralateral femurs compared to those in the control group. Additionally, Tb. Sp was lower in the experimental group, although these differences did not reach statistical significance. Nevertheless, these results indicate that Jintiange may enhance bone microstructure, potentially exerting a beneficial effect on bones in osteoporotic aged rats.

The effectiveness of artificial tiger bone in promoting fracture healing can be attributed to two primary factors. Firstly, artificial tiger bone is rich in collagen, which facilitates matrix formation in osteogenesis. Secondly, its high calcium content can elevate blood calcium levels to a certain extent [15, 16]. In a clinical study conducted by Xiaolan et al., it was reported that Jintiange promotes the formation of fibrous connective tissue in the granulation tissue of the fracture site, thereby enhancing the connection of the fracture ends and positively influencing the formation of osteoid tissue [17]. The experimental subjects in our study were aged ovariectomized rats, characterized by increased bone resorption and diminished osteogenic capabilities. Artificial tiger bone was hypothesized to restore the balance between bone resorption and formation, thereby promoting fracture healing—a hypothesis supported by our findings. Our study revealed a correlation between fracture healing scores and bone microstructure, as assessed by micro-CT scanning, with Tb. Sp demonstrating the most significant association (P < 0.05). Poor fracture healing was characterized by a wide average cavity width between trabeculae, sparse trabeculae, and an inadequate microstructure. While positive correlations were observed between BV/TV, Tb. N, and Tb. Th and fracture healing scores, respectively, they did not reach statistical significance. This could potentially be elucidated more prominently with a prolonged treatment duration or an expanded sample size.

In conclusion, our study revealed that the administration of Jintiange led to improved fracture healing scores, decreased Tb. Sp, and enhanced bone microstructure. These findings suggest that Jintiange may effectively promote osteoporotic fracture healing in aged rats, indicating its potential utility for early treatment in aged patients with such fractures. However, several factors encountered during our study, including the high mortality rate among elderly animals, limited experimental conditions, small sample size, and variations in observation time points during the fracture healing process, warrant cautious interpretation of the results. Future research efforts should involve larger sample sizes to further assess Jintiange’s effects on osteoporotic fracture healing in elderly rats, thereby providing more robust and reliable evidence for clinical applications.
