Acute left ventricular unloading in chronic ovine biventricular failure with functional tricuspid regurgitation
Authors: Magda Piekarska, Manikantam Gaddam, Marcin Malinowski, Tomasz Jaźwiec, Austin Goodyke, Boguslaw Gaweda, Manuel Rausch, Tomasz Timek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesRight ventricular failure and functional tricuspid regurgitation are associated with left ventricular assist device implantation. We set out to investigate the effect of acute left ventricular unloading on right ventricular subvalvular geometry in the setting of ovine biventricular heart failure with functional tricuspid regurgitation.MethodsSeven adult Dorset male sheep underwent left thoracotomy and pacemaker implantation and were paced until the development of biventricular dysfunction and functional tricuspid regurgitation. Subsequently, animals underwent sternotomy and cardiopulmonary bypass for implantation of sonomicrometry crystals around the tricuspid annulus, on each papillary muscle tip, and on the right ventricular epicardium. After weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass, a continuous-flow left ventricular assist device was implanted. Simultaneous hemodynamic, sonomicrometry, and echocardiographic data were acquired before and after full left ventricular unloading.ResultsPacing resulted in a significant increase in left ventricular end-diastolic dimension, tricuspid annular size, and tricuspid and mitral regurgitation. There was a significant reduction in left ventricular ejection fraction (60% ± 6% vs 34% ± 11%, P = .002) and right ventricular fractional area contraction (56% ± 8% vs 38% ± 12%, P = .001). During left ventricular unloading, inter-papillary muscle distances and papillary muscle area increased without changes in the distance of each papillary muscle tip to tricuspid annular plane. The septal region of the tricuspid annulus tended to increase during unloading. Tricuspid regurgitation did not change during left ventricular assist device unloading.ConclusionsLeft ventricular unloading in an ovine model of biventricular failure with tricuspid regurgitation was associated with increased inter-papillary distances and papillary muscle area most likely due to septal shift. These geometric alterations in subvalvular geometry may contribute to valvular insufficiency and right ventricular failure over time.

Advanced heart failure has reached epidemic proportions in the United States, and improving effective treatment methods has become particularly important.1 Left ventricular assist device (LVAD) implantation is connected with reasonable results but is also associated with significant adverse events. Right ventricular failure (RVF) is observed in 20% to 50% of patients with LVAD implants,2 and development of right heart failure significantly worsens prognosis. RVF results in a reduction of flow to the LVAD and consequently leads to reduction of pump output and impairs end-organ perfusion,3 with hemodynamic changes, geometric alterations, and patient-related comorbidities and variability contributing to RVF after LVAD implantation.4 Long-term impact of left ventricle (LV) unloading is clinically relevant for the right ventricle (RV), but in clinical practice, we also observe acute RV insufficiency onset even on the operating table, shortly after LVAD implantation, which demands immediate treatment. Acute RVF occurs in approximately 10% to 40% of patients after LVAD implantation5,6 and is a major cause of postoperative morbidity and mortality. In that light, acute unloading is still a significant problem worth our attention. Experimental studies in healthy animals have yielded conflicting results on the impact of LV unloading and altered septal geometry on RV function.7,8 In an acute healthy ovine model, our group previously demonstrated that mechanical LV unloading significantly increased the distance between RV free wall and septum at end-diastole and was associated with dilation of the septal annuls.9 However, the effect on subvalvular geometry was not studied. After LVAD placement, the complexity of geometric and mechanical changes poses a formidable challenge to extrapolating experimental data from healthy animals to humans with end-stage biventricular heart failure. Therefore, we set out to expand on previous findings10 and elucidate tricuspid annular and subvalvular geometry after LV unloading in a chronic ovine model of biventricular heart failure with tricuspid regurgitation (TR).

Materials and Methods

All surgical and experimental procedures were performed in compliance with Principles of Laboratory Animal Care formulated by the National Society of Medical Research and the Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The study protocol has been approved by Calvin University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee Protocol Number 2018-439 approved March 4, 2019.

Pacemaker Implantation

During the initial operation, 7 healthy Dorset adult male sheep (58 ± 4 kg) underwent pacemaker implantation with an epicardial lead. The detailed surgical protocol was described previously9 and will be presented here in abbreviated form. A left lateral minithoracotomy was used as access and epicardial echocardiography was performed to assess biventricular function and valvular competence (baseline). A monopolar pacing lead was sutured onto the lateral LV wall and exteriorized through the thorax to a pacemaker (Consulta CRT-P, Medtronic) placed in the subcutaneous pocket near the spine. After a 4- to 5-day recovery, pacing of the LV was initiated with a pacing rate of 220 to 240 beats/min. All animals received echocardiography every 3 days during the pacing protocol, until the development of biventricular dysfunction and functional tricuspid regurgitation (FTR) due to tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy (TIC) as described previously.10 Mean time of rapid pacing was 22 ± 7 days. Figure E1 shows an echocardiographic view of the heart after the pacing protocol with TR. The Video 1 presents pacing on a heart in real time.

Terminal Procedure

The terminal procedure was performed as in our previous study and is described here in brief.10 The criteria for performing terminal operations included LV ejection fraction less than 30% assessed on surveillance transthoracic echocardiography. The echocardiographic examination was performed as a closed-chest study after the development of TIC and before the terminal procedure. The pacemaker was stopped 12 hours before the terminal procedure. A median sternotomy was performed, and the heart was dissected from adhesions. Repeat epicardial echocardiography was performed. After full heparinization and cannulation of the right common carotid artery and superior and inferior vena cava, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) was initiated. With the heart beating, the right atrium was opened and 6 sonomicrometry crystals were placed around the tricuspid annulus, 1 on each papillary muscle tip and another 14 on the RV epicardium (Figure 1, A). RV and LV pressure transducers were placed through the cardiac apex. Subsequently, the right atrium was closed, and the animal was weaned from CPB and stabilized hemodynamically. A 34F right angle venous cannula was inserted through the left atrial appendage into the left atrium and connected to a centrifugal pump (550 Bio-Console, Bio-Medicus, Medtronic). The arterial limb of the circuit was connected to the previously inserted aortic cannula. Animals were allowed to stabilize hemodynamically for 30 minutes before data collection.Figure 1The LVAD (A) and location of the sonomicrometry crystals implanted on the heart (B and C) and the distance between the septal and posterior papillary muscles and ventricle free wall (D). Echocardiography

All images were acquired with Vivid S6 (GE Healthcare) using the 1.5 to 3.5 Mhz probes. The all-echocardiographic examinations were performed by certified cardiac sonographers and analyzed by the research team. The American Society of Echocardiography criteria were followed. The detailed protocol has been published previously.10,11

Data Acquisition and Analysis

Animals were studied with an open chest and on the ventilator with respiration paused during data acquisitions. Simultaneous hemodynamic, sonomicrometry, and echocardiographic data were acquired before (TIC) and after full LV unloading (LVAD) for 10 minutes. Sonomicrometry data were acquired using a Sonometrics Ultrasonic Measurement System DS3 (Sonometrics Corporation) as described before.10 Sonomicrometry measurements were averaged over 10 cardiac cycles in normal heart sinus rhythm before and after LV unloading at maximal support (3.9 ± 0.2 L/min). Data were acquired at 128 Hz with simultaneous LV pressure, RV pressure, and electrocardiogram recordings.

Data were analyzed with CardioSOFT Software (Sonomicrometric Corporation) and MATLAB code (MathWorks). Distances between crystals were used to assess changes in RV dimensions and shape (Figure 1, A-C). The RV volume was estimated using the convex hull method based on 3-dimensional coordinates of crystals on the annulus and RV epicardium. RV epicardial strain was calculated based on epicardial RV crystal coordinates as described previously.9 Segmental ventricular contraction was defined as the fractional area change for each RV segment. To assess the changes in diameter of RV, we measured the distance between the line connecting the tips of the septal and posterior papillary muscles and RV free wall (Figure 1, D).

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. The measured variables were compared using a paired Student 2-tailed t test or for data that did not meet assumptions, a nonparametric equivalent Wilcoxon signed-ranked test was used. All statistical analyses were performed using MedCalc Statistical Software, version 20.114.

Results

Hemodynamics

Hemodynamic parameters at the time of the terminal study did not differ between TIC and LVAD conditions. Heart rate (116 ± 15 vs 111 ± 22 beats/min, P = .31), central venous pressure (14 ± 8 vs 17 ± 9 mm Hg, P = .34), and RV end-diastolic volume (181 ± 42 vs 178 ± 40 mL, P = .81) did not change between TIC and LVAD conditions. Systolic RV pressure also remained unchanged (18 ± 6 vs 21±7 mm Hg, P = .11).

Discussion

Our ovine model of TIC resulted in biventricular failure with clinically significant tricuspid and mitral regurgitation reflective of clinical findings in patients presenting for mechanical support. LV unloading in the acute setting affected RV subvalvuar geometry as demonstrated by alterations in papillary muscle geometry and area. Papillary muscle displacement is known to contribute to FTR,12 and these changes may influence the genesis and progression of TV insufficiency possibly potentiating RVF in patients with durable LV mechanical support devices.

RV volume and tricuspid annular area were unchanged in our study; thus, the most likely factor responsible for modification in inter-papillary muscle distances and area is intraventricular septal shift. Geometric changes in the RV that restrict or tether the tricuspid valve leaflets are sufficient to create TR in patients even without significant annular size dilatation.13 The position of the septum and movement secondary to LVAD have been demonstrated as a potential mechanism for RVF in pulsatile models of LVAD.14,15 LVAD implantation alters a biventricular insufficient heart by reversing septal shift into the RV. In our study, the distance between the interventricular septum and RV free wall during LVAD unloading increased. This observation corroborates the data of Moon and colleagues,16,17 who conducted similar studies in canines. To investigate ventricular interdependence during LVAD support, Sack and colleagues18 studied the mechanical impact of acute LV unloading on RV mechanics and septal wall motion. These studies demonstrated that LV assistance reduces myofiber stress in the LV and the RV free wall, while increasing leftward septal shift with increased operating speeds. LVAD support reduced septal wall myocardial stress, but the RV experienced a shift toward higher end-diastolic pressure and volume during LVAD condition, which kept RV stresses high. However, this model demonstrated that septal shifting alone is not sufficient to induce RVF.18

Our acute assessment of TR did not demonstrate a significant increase during LV unloading, although TR intensified more toward a severe grade. The septal region of the tricuspid annulus did increase, and although increased annular size is associated with TR severity clinically, this is only one of the mechanisms driving clinical TR. Patients with LVAD develop TR as geometric changes such as enlargement of the RV apically, lengthening of the ventricle, tricuspid valve dilatation, and papillary muscle displacement become apparent,19 but only tricuspid annular dilatation and area have been correlated with severity of TR.20 Our study demonstrated geometric changes with LVAD implantation commensurate to what is observed clinically, and perhaps longer observation would allow for worsening of RV function and possibly failure. Previous studies have identified tricuspid incompetence and altered RV geometry as predictors of RVF after LVAD implantation, and current data demonstrating altered subvalvular geometry may underlie these prognostic indicators.21,22 Our study revealed that interpapillary muscle distances and papillary muscle area increased significantly with LVAD support, suggesting splaying of the papillary muscles as a result of LV unloading. Enlargement of the area between papillary muscle tips has been shown to contribute to ovine tricuspid insufficiency in pulmonary artery banding23 and TIC models of FTR.24 These subvalvular alterations already present in animals with FTR are therefore further potentiated after LVAD implantation and can be expected to contribute to propagation of tricuspid valve and RV insufficiency. TR in the setting of an LVAD has substantial clinical implications. TR is a negative prognostic indicator for outcomes in patients after LVAD, and given the interdependencies of RV function and TR, this may be more than correlative. Duration of postoperative inotropic support, hospital stay, and temporary RV assist device requirement are all increased in patients with significant preimplant TR.25 Furthermore, post-LVAD survival in patients with persistent TR is reduced.22 Clinical studies have also demonstrated that preoperative tricuspid annular diameter was significantly associated with residual TR (no improvement of significant preoperative TR or TR worsening) and TV annulus dilatation was associated with survival after LVAD implantation in patients without severe TR.26

In our study, load-independent indexes of RV function as well as regional RV strains did not differ between TIC and LVAD conditions. These results challenge prior experimental studies, which showed that global RV contractility remained compromised during LV unloading.16,17 Additionally, RV contractility was found to be reduced during LV unloading, and the reduction was greater with a thinner septal wall or lower regional myocardial contractility.27 A recent clinical study revealed that early RV contractile adaptation to LVAD support is heterogenous.28 Patients with maladaptation (ie, reduction of RV ejection fraction, changes in longitudinal/antero-posterior components with compensatory increase in radial diameter) had more advanced heart failure status before surgery, elevated right-sided filling pressures, and impaired pulmonary vascular coupling. Sack and colleagues29 concluded that the LVAD is a good tool for normalizing LV stress, but not for the RV. The elevated RV stress during LV unloading is probably related to the mechanical and structural dependence of the RV on LV contractility, which is impaired despite LVAD operation.

The high incidence of RVF after LV assistance is a significant clinical problem, and although it is known that clinical parameters such as RV and tricuspid valve function can improve after LVAD implantation, a group of patients with significant TR experience worsening TR after LVAD implantation.22,30 The decision to perform concomitant tricuspid valve surgery is controversial, because it increases CPB time, cardiac arrest may be necessary, and it may increase operative risk.31 There are no clear guidelines for tricuspid valve repair (TVR) during LVAD implantation. A retrospective study with propensity score matching32 showed concomitant TVR with LVAD implantation was associated with reduced mortality and reduced grade of TR over an 8-year follow-up period. However, other studies have illustrated that TVR at the time of LVAD implantation does not improve survival33 or may lead to worse survival.34 Shah and colleagues32 suggested that the mechanism for the observed reduction in mortality with concomitant tricuspid surgery was through a protective effect of TVR. Contemporary surgical repair with ring annuloplasty is focused only on annular dilatation, and our data suggest that for hearts with altered right heart geometry undergoing LVAD implantation, a concurrent papillary muscle procedure may need to be considered.35,36 This study confirmed that geometric alterations in the subvalvular apparatus may contribute to valvular insufficiency and RVF, indicating a potential role for multilevel repair of the TV complex at the time of LVAD implantation.

Limitations

This study was performed in an open-chest experimental model with accompanied effect of general anesthesia. The chest was not closed because central cannulation has complications when closing it. RV geometry assessment was conducted shortly after implantation of LVAD. Our LVAD model used a drainage cannula in the left atrium that departs from apical drainage used in clinical practice, which may alter LV geometry and septal dynamics differentially. LV apical cannulation actively unloads the ventricle, shrinking LV volumes and shifting the interventricular septum to a neutral position. Proper decompression via apical cannulation straightens the interventricular septum, pulling it away from the RV and preventing RV strain. Conversely, left atrium cannulation leaves the ventricular muscle intact and geometries unaltered. However, excessive pumping can cause a “suction event” in LV apex cannulation. The decrease in LV volume can create a leftward septal shift, which impairs RV geometry and function. LA cannulation largely avoids this leftward mechanical encroachment. In LA cannulation, we observed a persistent right to left shift. This can push the septum rightward into the RV, causing RV dysfunction. The interventricular septum normally assists with RV and LV contraction. When an LVAD permanently unloads the heart, the septum's systolic thickening and workload decrease. This mechanical disuse can lead to localized atrophy of the septal wall over time. The main clinical scenario is connected with LV apex cannulation; however, in specific situations, restrictive cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy when the LV is too small, and stiff LA cannulation are used instead. Severe calcification of LV apex, cardiac amyloidosis, severe LV thrombus, and precapillary pulmonary hypertension are situations of choice in place of cannulation.

Conclusions

The TIC model does not completely mimic clinical cardiomyopathy because there is no associated hypertrophy and collagen deposition; however, the RV increases in myocardial mass.37,38 Animals have different reactions for pacing protocol, and in the work of Malinowski and colleagues,10 pacing of the LV took 14 ± 5 days and RV fractional area contraction changed from 52% ± 11% to 25% ± 9% (P = .005). In our study, pacing took longer. The mean time of rapid pacing was 22 ± 7 days, and RV fractional area contraction changed from 56% ± 8% to 38% ± 12% (P = .001). Of note, in comparison with clinical cardiomyopathy, we stopped pacing 12 hours before terminal surgery, although heart failure did not improve over this time period. The number of animals in the study is limited; however, we observed consistent changes in RV geometry in reaction to LV unloading.
