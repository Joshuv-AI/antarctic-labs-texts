The prevalence of depression among parents of individuals with disabilities in Saudi Arabia and the influencing factors
Authors: Mahmoud Mohamed Eltantawy, Mohamed Abdou Husseiny
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Mental health problems, including depression, are common among parents of individuals with disabilities. Therefore, the current study aimed to identify the prevalence of depression among parents of individuals with disabilities and explore the impact of certain factors on it, such as the type of disability, age of the individual with disabilities, the number of individuals with disabilities in the family, and the gender of the individuals with disabilities.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A descriptive research method was utilized in the study. The study participants were 403 parents, including 327 parents of individuals with disabilities (119 fathers and 208 mothers), and 76 parents of individuals without disabilities (34 fathers and 42 mothers) in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Data were collected using the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI-II).RESULTS:The results revealed that the prevalence of depression among parents of individuals with disabilities was 38.5% in general, with higher rates among mothers (45.2%) compared to fathers (26.9%). The study also found that parents of individuals with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) had higher rates of depression (58.5%). Furthermore, the results indicated that the type of disability had a significant impact on parental depression, while factors such as the number of individuals with disabilities within the family, the gender of the individual with a disability, and the age of the individuals with disabilities did not.CONCLUSION:The study recommends further research on parental depression using larger samples and focusing on specific disability categories to gain a clearer understanding of the situation for different groups of parents.

Introduction

Raising a child with a disability can significantly impact the mental health and well-being of family members. When parents feel isolated due to their child’s unpredictable behaviors or medical needs, they may experience feelings of loneliness and exclusion. This can lead to increased stress and depression, affecting not only the parents but also the entire family.[1]

Parents face numerous challenges in meeting daily life necessities and navigating the demanding situations associated with raising a disabled child. These situations often give rise to tension in their personal relationships, particularly in their marital and emotional bonds. Furthermore, parents may encounter inappropriate reactions to these complex circumstances, amplifying feelings of guilt. The responsibilities of caring for their child also impose significant demands on parents, including their time, financial resources, and energy, further exacerbating the stress and difficulties they experience.[2345] Research has shown that the chronic psychological distress faced by parents increases the risk of family disintegration, family dysfunction, and the development of certain health conditions, both physical and psychological.[6]

The evidence indicates a higher prevalence of mental health problems among parents of children with disabilities compared to those without disabled children.[7] Studies have shown that parents of children with disabilities commonly experience high levels of stress, including anxiety, depression, and caregiver stress. For instance, a study found that 72.7% of parents reported moderate stress levels, while 8.2% experienced severe stress, categorized as severe stress, significantly impacting their quality of life, and increasing family burden.[89] In a study by Wahab and Ramli,[10] it was revealed that 41.2% of parents of children with intellectual disability (ID) experienced psychological distress, with varying levels of severity: 36.6% severe distress, 21% moderate distress, and 21.9% mild distress. Furthermore, parents of children with disabilities commonly face higher levels of stress, anxiety, depression, and other mental health problems.[611] These parents have lower mental health rates compared to the general population and are more susceptible to psychological issues.[12] Mothers of children with disabilities also frequently experience burnout.[13] Kimura and Yamazaki[14] found that mothers of children with disabilities exhibited lower levels of hope and poorer mental health compared to mothers of children without disabilities. Additionally, despite their desire to have another child, 42.5% of these mothers have decided against it, suggesting the complex challenges they face.

Several factors contribute to the occurrence of psychological problems among parents of individuals with disabilities, extending beyond the disability itself. These factors include low socioeconomic status, limited knowledge about the child’s disability, parents’ lack of confidence in managing the behavior of the disabled child, perceived increase in caregiving burden, and inadequate support sources.[15] Work also plays a significant role in individuals’ mental health, as working women are more susceptible to depression compared to men, as women, unlike men, are expected to look after the family and perform household chores even if they are employed.[16] Therefore, the mental health of parents caring for their disabled child is influenced by a combination of factors related to the child and the family.[17] For example, research indicates that having a child with cerebral palsy reduces the probability of the mother’s employment by 6.2%, increases the probability of divorce by 2.3%, and elevates the risk of depression by 28%. These effects are particularly pronounced for parents living in challenging socioeconomic conditions.[18] Another significant factor impacting parental depression is the family’s financial situation, as low-income families tend to experience higher levels of depression compared to higher-income families.[19] Furthermore, while stress, anxiety, and depression among caregivers are prevalent worldwide, they are particularly exacerbated in low-income countries due to factors such as inadequate government services, the pressures associated with poverty, the stigma surrounding disability, and limited access to mental health services.[20]

Depression is a distressing psychological problem commonly experienced by parents of individuals with disabilities. It is recognized as a prevalent and severe medical condition that adversely impacts emotions, thinking patterns, and behavior. Individuals with depression often experience feelings of sadness and a loss of interest in activities they once enjoyed. This condition can give rise to a range of emotional and physical problems and impair functioning in both work and home environments.[21] Globally, depression is a widespread mental disorder, affecting approximately 5% of adults. Women are more susceptible to depression than men, and in severe cases, depression can even lead to suicide. Fortunately, effective treatments exist for mild, moderate, and severe forms of depression.[22]

Depression manifests through a range of significant symptoms experienced by an individual. The most important symptoms include a persistent feeling of sadness or a depressed mood, a loss of interest or pleasure in activities that were once enjoyed, changes in appetite resulting in unexplained weight loss or gain, disruptions in sleep patterns such as insomnia or excessive sleeping, a loss of energy or increased fatigue, noticeable changes in physical activity levels or slowed movements and speech, feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt, difficulties with concentration, decision-making, and thinking, and recurrent thoughts of death or suicide.[21] These symptoms collectively contribute to the burden of depression and require attention and appropriate treatment.

The prevalence of depression in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia has been found to vary. According to the results of the 2022 national screening on anxiety and depression, the study participants had a depression rate of 12.7%.[23] Additionally, other statistics indicate a depression prevalence of 32.4% among adolescents, with a higher prevalence among females compared to males.[24] Furthermore, a review of the literature highlighted several significant risk factors associated with depression in Saudi Arabia. These risk factors include gender, with a particular emphasis on females, being single, having a low educational level, experiencing financial problems, living in poor housing conditions, having medical issues, experiencing sleep disorders, having psychiatric/psychological disorders, experiencing life events, lacking social support, being exposed to psychological stressors, facing stress, encountering educational/personal problems, and struggling with smartphone addiction.[25]

Several studies have examined the prevalence of depression among parents of individuals with disabilities, and the results consistently indicate higher rates of depression in this population.[2627] For instance, research by Sharma et al.[28] found that 35.4% of fathers and 66.3% of mothers of children with disabilities exhibited clear depressive symptoms. Claudia and Sandu[29] also highlighted the high levels of depression experienced by parents in this context. Depression was prevalent among caregivers of children with developmental disabilities, with an overall prevalence of approximately 20%. Notably, 5–6% of caregivers experienced moderate depressive symptoms.[30] Furthermore, Scherer et al.[31] found that 31% of parents of children with intellectual and developmental disabilities (IDD) experienced moderate levels of depression, representing a 24% increase compared to parents of children without IDD.

Furthermore, the age of the child was identified as a factor influencing the degree of depression experienced by parents.[26] In particular, parents of children with disabilities aged between 12 and 17 years demonstrated a significantly higher need for mental health services compared to parents of children without disabilities.[7] In this regard, Bitsika and Sharpley[32] reported that higher age in autistic girls was associated with increased parental restlessness, a core symptom of generalized anxiety disorder.

Research on the impact of a child’s disability on mothers has revealed important findings. Specifically, mothers of individuals with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) tend to experience higher levels of depression compared to mothers of children with ID.[33] In the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, studies have shown that parents of children with ASD and caregivers have higher rates of anxiety and depression.[34] Similarly, mothers of children with cerebral palsy have been found to have higher rates of depression compared to mothers of children without disabilities.[35] In one study, it was reported that 73% of mothers of children with disabilities experienced varying degrees of depression, with 21% experiencing severe depression, highlighting the significant impact of a child’s disability on maternal depression.[36] Moreover, 22.7% of mothers caring for children with disabilities experienced depression.[37] Among mothers of children with learning disabilities, a substantial 89.6% experienced depression, with 27.1% experiencing mild-to-moderate depression, 26.4% experiencing moderate-to-severe depression, and 36.1% experiencing severe depression.[38] Mothers of children with ID, in general, reported higher levels of stress and depression compared to mothers of children without disabilities. Depression was found to have a negative impact on coping strategies among these mothers.[394041] The relationship between levels of depression and despair among mothers of children with disabilities has also been observed.[42] Mothers often face more difficulties compared to fathers, yet they demonstrate high levels of resilience and adaptability despite experiencing a loss of social support due to having a child with ID.[43]

Research on the impact of a child’s disability on parents has primarily focused on the mental health of mothers, with relatively less attention given to the mental health of fathers. Studies have found significant differences in mental health outcomes between fathers and mothers of children with ID, with fathers generally being less susceptible to mental health problems.[44] Conversely, mothers tend to experience higher levels of depression compared to fathers.[19] Furthermore, research has indicated that fathers and grandparents of children with disabilities are at a significant risk of experiencing depression.[45]

Research has examined the impact of the type of disability on parental depression and has produced important findings. Parents of children with ID have been found to experience higher levels of depression and anxiety compared to parents of children with physical disabilities.[46] In the case of specific diagnoses, parents of children with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) have reported higher levels of anxiety and depression compared to parents of children with ASD or ID.[47] Furthermore, parents of children with ASD and intellectual developmental delay have been found to experience higher levels of stress, depression, and anxiety.[1219]

Research has demonstrated the significant impact of intervention programs provided to parents of individuals with disabilities in reducing the severity of parental depression and promoting mental well-being. Training programs offered to parents of children with ID have shown positive effects in lowering parental depression.[48] Rational-emotive behavior therapy is effective in reducing depression severity among parents of children with Down syndrome.[49] Integrated parenting intervention programs targeting parents of children with ID and depression have played a significant role in reducing the severity of parental depression.[50] Social support programs provided to mothers of children with disabilities have demonstrated the ability to reduce their susceptibility to depression, with higher levels of perceived social support correlating with less severe depression, even in the presence of high parental stress.[51] Group counseling, employing a selective approach, has proven effective in addressing the challenges faced by parents of children with disabilities.[52] Collaborative efforts with various institutions and counseling centers are urgently needed to organize specialized training courses aimed at supporting parents. Increasing the number of specialists, particularly in the psychological field, who can address the mental health needs of both children and their parents, is crucial.[27] Seeking help from qualified and trained healthcare professionals can be life-saving, and further efforts should be made to improve knowledge of depression symptoms and provide appropriate sources of assistance for patients.[53]

From the aforementioned, it is evident that parents of individuals with disabilities experience depression due to the burdens imposed by the disability. Additionally, various factors may contribute to their depression alongside having a child with disabilities. The current study aims to identify the prevalence of depression among parents of individuals with disabilities in Saudi Arabia, as there is a scarcity of research addressing depression among parents of children with disabilities in the country. The study also seeks to investigate the impact of certain factors on parental depression, such as the type of disability, the number of individuals with disabilities in the family, the gender of the individuals with disabilities, and the age of the individuals with disabilities. It is important to note that the study examines depression among parents of individuals with disabilities in a different cultural and social environment, considering various variables, aspects, and available services. The problem statement of the study can be summarized in the following questions:

Q1: What is the prevalence of depression among parents of individuals with disabilities compared to parents of individuals without disabilities?

Q2: What is the prevalence of depression among parents of individuals with disabilities based on the type of disability?

Q3: Does the gender of the individual with disabilities (male/female) have an impact on parental depression?

Q4: Does the type of disability (ID, ASD, ADHD, hearing impairment, visual impairment, multiple disabilities, no disability) have an impact on parental depression?

Q5: Does the age of the individual with disabilities have an impact on parental depression?

Q6: Does the number of children with disabilities in the family (one child/more than one child) have an impact on parental depression?

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a descriptive research design to examine the prevalence of depression among parents of individuals with disabilities and to investigate the impact of various demographic and disability-related factors on parental depression. The study was conducted in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, targeting parents of individuals both with and without disabilities from various schools and disability centers.

Study participants and sampling

The study sample consisted of 403 parents, including 327 parents of individuals with disabilities (119 fathers and 208 mothers), and 76 parents of individuals without disabilities (34 fathers and 42 mothers) in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. The distribution of parents of individuals with disabilities based on the type of disability was as follows: 134 parents of individuals with ID, 41 parents of individuals with ASD, 16 parents of individuals with ADHD, 77 parents of individuals with hearing disabilities, 34 parents of individuals with visual disabilities, and 16 parents of individuals with multiple disabilities.

Data collection tool and technique

Data were collected using the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI-II), which had been previously translated into Arabic by a group of researchers. Ghareeb[54] conducted a study to identify the psychometric properties of the inventory, involving 145 participants, including 44 females and 101 males. Among the participants, 36 were psychiatric patients. The results demonstrated a high level of reliability, with test-retest reliability coefficients ranging from 0.74 to 0.83, as measured by Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. Concurrent validity was also high, indicated by a correlation coefficient of 0.76 between the modified BDI-IA and BDI-II. Factor analysis revealed that depression, as measured by the BDI-II, consists of two main factors: cognitive-affective and somatic. Additionally, Al-Mashouh[55] assessed the psychometric properties of the inventory in the Saudi Arabian context. The internal consistency reliability of the BDI-II was verified in a sample of 112 participants, with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.39 to 0.79. The reliability of the BDI-II was also assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, resulting in a value of 0.92.

The inventory utilized in this study consists of 21 items, with response options graded on a scale of 0 to 3. Participants are asked to choose only one item that best describes their feelings in the past two weeks, including today. The degrees of depression according to the inventory range from no depression to mild, moderate, severe, and profound depression.

The relevant data for the research sample was collected by creating an electronic version of the BDI-II using Google Forms. The inventory link was shared via email and WhatsApp with administrators at special education schools, centers for individuals with disabilities, and some regular schools in Riyadh for distribution to parents of both individuals with and without disabilities.

Ethical consideration

Ethical procedures were followed throughout the study. Informed consent was obtained from all participants included in the study. Parents of students with disabilities who participated in responding to the inventory were informed about the nature and purpose of the study. It was clearly communicated that their participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw or discontinue their responses at any time without any consequences.

Statistical analysis method

The data were analyzed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp. (2017). IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.) to address the research questions. Appropriate statistical methods, such as percentages, were used to determine the prevalence and levels of depression among the participants. Non-parametric statistical tests, including the Mann-Whitney U test and the Kruskal-Wallis test, were employed due to the non-normal distribution of the data as indicated by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests.

Discussion

The results of the statistical analysis for the first question indicated that the prevalence of depression among parents of individuals with disabilities was 38.5% in general compared to parents of individuals without disabilities. Among fathers, the prevalence was 26.9%, with 13.4% experiencing mild depression, 6.7% experiencing moderate depression, and 6.7% experiencing severe depression. Among mothers, the prevalence was 45.2%, with 18.8% experiencing mild depression, 14.4% experiencing moderate depression, 9.6% experiencing severe depression, and 2.4% experiencing profound depression. In this regard, Li et al.[56] indicated that mothers of children with severe ASD reported significantly higher levels of stress, anxiety, and depression compared to fathers. Notably, 13.8% of mothers experienced moderate-to-severe anxiety, and 13.1% experienced moderate-to-severe depression, compared to 9.9% and 8.0% for fathers, respectively.

It is also evident from the results that the prevalence of depression is higher among parents of individuals with disabilities. This aligns with the WHO’s[22] indication that the prevalence of depression among adults is 5%. These findings are consistent with the results of several studies that highlighted elevated levels of depression among parents of individuals with disabilities.[26272829]

The results indicated that 17.6% of fathers and 15.6% of mothers of individuals without disabilities reported symptoms of depression. The severity of depression in this group was generally mild to moderate. These findings align with previous research, such as that conducted by Scherer et al.[31] and Qi and To.[57] Scherer et al.[31] found that 31% of parents of children with IDD experienced moderate levels of depression, a 24% increase compared to parents of children without disabilities. Similarly, Qi and To[57] reported that 46.9% (54/115) of parents of autistic children experienced moderate to extremely severe stress, while 28.9% (13/45) of parents of children with other neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) and 12.5% (14/112) of parents of children without disabilities reported similar levels of stress.

Overall, the results suggest that parents of individuals with disabilities are more susceptible to experiencing depression compared to the general population. The higher prevalence of depression among parents in this study underscores the need for targeted support and interventions to address the mental health challenges faced by these parents. The findings also contribute to the existing body of literature that emphasizes the impact of caregiving for individuals with disabilities on parental mental health.

The study results are consistent with the notion that depression is more prevalent among mothers than fathers, and they are more susceptible to mental health problems, as indicated by several studies.[1944] Furthermore, higher levels of stress have been reported among Saudi mothers compared to fathers in Saudi Arabia.[58] The higher prevalence of depression among parents of individuals with disabilities can be attributed to the burden placed on them in caring for their children. The mental health of parents who care for a child with disabilities is influenced by factors related to the child and the family.[17] The challenges faced by parents of individuals with disabilities included meeting daily life necessities and coping with the difficult situation related to having a child with disabilities.[2] Additionally, the higher prevalence of depression among mothers can be explained by their primary role as caregivers, especially in a culturally specific context like Saudi Arabia, coupled with concerns about their children’s future, difficulties in accessing support groups, and limited employment opportunities for many, confining them primarily to childcare responsibilities. Additionally, the fact that many mothers are from different cities than their families can make it difficult to receive support from their families.

Regarding the results of the second question, the findings indicated that the prevalence of depression in its four categories (mild, moderate, severe, profound) was as follows: 34.3% among parents of individuals with ID, 58.5% among parents of individuals with ASD, 37.5% among parents of individuals with ADHD, 36.4% among parents of individuals with hearing impairments, 38.2% among parents of individuals with visual impairments, and 36% among parents of individuals with multiple disabilities. The highest prevalence of depression was observed among parents of individuals with ASD, which could be attributed to the nature of the disorder. ASD is a developmental disorder that affects various aspects of an individual’s development, such as cognitive, social, behavioral, and verbal development, making them constantly in need of care. Some individuals with ASD might also experience accompanying issues such as ADHD, sleep disorders, eating disorders, repetitive and aggressive behaviors, and communication problems. Moreover, these individuals require self-care, which necessitates continuous care and places a significant burden on parents. There may also be influences from other factors, such as the social stigma resulting from having a child with ASD, which may affect parents’ social interaction and limit their participation in various social activities. As the results of a study by Efe et al.[59] showed, stigma, anxiety, depression, and family performance among parents of children diagnosed with ASD led to various negative outcomes. Furthermore, access to services could also be a contributing factor to the higher depression rates among parents of individuals with ASD. The results of a study by Alnemary et al.[60] highlighted the need to develop policies and interventions that can alleviate the scarcity of services provided to children with ASD in Saudi Arabia.

The study results align with previous studies indicating that parents of children with ASD and ID have high levels of stress, depression, and anxiety.[121934] This aligns with Qi and To,[57] who found that parents of children with autism experienced slightly higher levels of stress compared to parents of children with other NDDs and typically developing children. Furthermore, in their systematic review, Lam et al.[61] analyzed data from 40 studies encompassing 13,853 caregivers of children with ASD. This review revealed a high prevalence of depression among caregivers, with approximately 45% experiencing depressive symptoms—a significant increase compared to findings from previous research.

However, the study results differ from the results of a study by Alrahili[47] conducted in Saudi Arabia, which indicated that parents of children with ADHD had higher levels of anxiety and depression compared to parents of children with ASD or ID. That study also indicated that the prevalence of depression among parents of individuals with multiple disabilities was not higher than that of other disability types, which might be due to the small sample size.

Regarding the third question concerning the influence of the individual with disabilities’ gender on parental depression, the results indicated no statistically significant differences in the levels of depression among parents of individuals with disabilities due to the individual’s gender (male, female). This result was unexpected, as it was anticipated that there would be an impact on gender, particularly among females, due to cultural factors associated with increased anxiety and fear for females. Females with disabilities often face greater difficulty in obtaining employment compared to males with disabilities, which hinders their independence. Moreover, issues related to family formation and caregiving responsibilities after the death of parents are pressing concerns for families with daughters. However, this unexpected result could be attributed to the quality of services provided to these families, considering the higher living standards and the presence of social care services and social security for individuals with disabilities. The availability of these supportive services may mitigate the burden on parents and contribute to an equal level of depression experienced by both male and female individuals with disabilities. It is important to further investigate this finding and evaluate the specific factors that may contribute to the absence of gender-based differences in parental depression.

Regarding the fourth research question, the results indicated that the type of disability had a significant impact on parental depression. Specifically, parents of individuals with ASD reported significantly higher levels of depression compared to parents of individuals with ID and parents of individuals without disabilities (P < 0.01). Furthermore, parents of individuals with ASD also reported significantly higher levels of depression compared to parents of individuals with hearing impairment or visual impairment (P < 0.05). Parents of individuals with multiple disabilities also reported significantly higher levels of depression compared to parents of individuals without disabilities (P < 0.05). These findings can be attributed to the severity and complexities associated with ASD and multiple disabilities, which can lead to various psychological challenges, including depression, for both the individual with the disabilities and their family. These findings align with other studies that found higher levels of depression among mothers of children with ASD compared to mothers of children with ID.[33]

Concerning the fifth question, the results showed no effect of the age of the children with disabilities on parental depression. This result differs from some studies that indicated that the age of the individual with disabilities was a factor influencing the level of depression.[26] Parents of children with disabilities between the ages of 12 and 17 had a much higher need for mental health services.[7] Additionally, for the sixth question, the results showed no significant effect of the number of children with disabilities (one child/more than one child) within the family on parental depression. This is also an unexpected result, as it was expected that the number of children with disabilities within the family would have an impact on parental depression.

The results of the fifth and sixth questions can be explained by the high quality of life in Saudi Arabia, where depression is associated with lower quality of life.[62] Poor quality of life is strongly associated with the severity of depression,[6364] as well as social status, housing, total family income, occupation, and support groups.[65] The mental health impairment and reduced quality of life among caregivers of children with disabilities are associated with financial instability, the severity of the child’s disability, and the lack of healthcare services.[20] The resilience of parents with children with ASD was found to be positively associated with family income and negatively associated with parental stress.[66] Therefore, the quality of services provided to families and the presence of others assisting in the care of the child with disabilities, alongside the family, are prevalent aspects in society. Many families rely on assistance, have high living standards, and have access to services to support their children with disabilities. The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia offers a wide range of medical, psychological, and social services for individuals with disabilities and their families, as well as employment services and free transportation services to schools. Furthermore, the Kingdom provides a range of services for individuals with disabilities, including disability assessment for access to government services, monthly financial assistance, financial coverage for enrollment in daycare centers, home healthcare services with parental support and guidance, rehabilitation services in comprehensive centers, and financial aid for medical devices and assistive tools. Additionally, parents benefit from informal services such as family counseling and educational programs. These services may mitigate the impact of factors such as disability type, age, and gender on parental depression. It is worth mentioning that the study sample was drawn from Riyadh, the capital city, and an urban area with specific characteristics.

It is important to note the cultural considerations that may have influenced the study’s findings. The availability of services in the capital, which is a high-income area, as well as the ability to hire caregivers to assist with childcare, can alleviate the burden on families. Furthermore, parental satisfaction with ASD services may vary based on their child’s gender, particularly for females during adolescence and beyond, due to cultural and societal influences. Family support services, such as counseling and educational programs, also play a crucial role. It is also important to note that Saudi society is characterized by extended families, where grandparents, uncles, aunts, and cousins play a role in caring for children with disabilities. Furthermore, gender roles in Saudi society have become increasingly similar, especially in urban areas.

The findings of this study can inform the development of services for parents of individuals with disabilities. For instance, the Ministry of Health could conduct comprehensive surveys to identify parents experiencing depression or other mental health issues and provide appropriate psychological interventions. Additionally, workshops and support groups could be organized for parents within schools or specialized centers. The Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development and the Ministry of Education could develop online educational and training platforms offering counseling and educational sessions for parents. Moreover, the establishment of online support groups facilitated by professionals could enable parents to share experiences, advice, and information.

Limitations and recommendations

It should be noted that there are limitations to the study that should be taken into consideration when interpreting and generalizing the results. The study was conducted in a specific cultural environment with its own characteristics and features. Additionally, it was conducted in an urban environment, specifically the capital city, where a wide range of services, including medical, educational, rehabilitative, and support services, are readily available. The sample also predominantly represented a relatively high socioeconomic status. Furthermore, the urban environment of the study, where gender disparities are significantly diminishing, allows women to undertake numerous roles and benefit from a wide range of services. These contextual factors may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions or populations within Saudi Arabia. Therefore, it is important to consider these factors when interpreting the findings.

Based on the study results, several recommendations can be made. Firstly, it is crucial to further investigate the mental health issues faced by parents of individuals with disabilities and conduct additional research in different Saudi cities with varying socioeconomic levels and cultural characteristics different from the urban environment where the study was conducted. These cities possess distinct cultural and social characteristics, which may affect the availability of services and support for parents and their children with disabilities. Additionally, future research should aim to include larger sample sizes of parents and examine each type of disability separately to obtain a more comprehensive understanding of parental depression.

Furthermore, highlighting the importance of intervention programs for parents is essential. These programs should focus on reducing the severity of depression and mitigating its negative effects on family functioning.

Further research is needed to investigate the influence of quality of life, support services, and cultural factors on parental depression among individuals with disabilities in different settings that vary in socioeconomic level and cultural considerations.

Conclusions

The study findings reveal a high prevalence of depression among parents of individuals with disabilities compared to parents of individuals without disabilities. Additionally, the study found no significant impact of the number of individuals with disabilities within the family, the gender of the individual with a disability, or the age of individuals with disabilities on levels of parental depression. However, the type of disability was found to be a significant predictor of parental depression. This study contributes to the existing literature by uncovering new factors that have not been previously explored in Saudi Arabia, thus providing valuable insights into the nature of depression among parents of individuals with disabilities.
