Integrating gender-based violence first-line response into family planning and antenatal care services: an assessment of feasibility, appropriateness and acceptability to healthcare providers in Sokoto and Ebonyi States, Nigeria
Authors: Chioma Oduenyi, Sylverius Obafemi, Myra Betron, Emily Bryce, Nneka Chijioke-Dikeocha, Emeka Ifemenam, Adamu Maiakwai, Samaila Yusuf, Bello Kabir, Emeka Sampson, Hadiza Yaro, Gertrude Odezugo, Oniyire Adetiloye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionGender-based violence (GBV), particularly intimate partner violence (IPV) and reproductive coercion (RC), is associated with poor reproductive and maternal health outcomes. Despite studies establishing the prevalence and severity of GBV across many settings, health settings largely lack systematic strategies to prevent and effectively respond to IPV and RC. Our study assessed the feasibility, appropriateness and acceptability of integrating GBV first-line response and counseling for IPV and RC in antenatal care (ANC) and family planning (FP) clinical settings in Nigeria.MethodsA mixed methods study, embedded within a matched-pair, cluster-controlled trial, was used to examine the feasibility, appropriateness and acceptability of GBV screening and first-line response intervention, which adapted and combined the World Health Organization’s LIVES model and the Addressing Reproductive Coercion in the Health Setting (ARHCES) model in antenatal care (ANC) and family planning (FP) clinical settings in twenty health facilities in Ebonyi and Sokoto States, Nigeria. Seventy-five health providers (16 nurses-midwives and 59 Community Health Extension Workers) were trained on GBV first-line response and empowerment counseling and were mentored and followed over sixteen months. Provider surveys were collected at months one, three and nine, and a health facility quality assurance tool was administered pre-intervention, three months after the commencement of the intervention and at nine months. Additionally, we identified barriers to and facilitators of implementation through lessons learned from workshops with health providers.ResultsQuality assurance standards increased over time (P ≤ 0.05), demonstrating improved readiness for service availability and appropriateness, facility readiness and infrastructure, the availability of Information, Education and Communication (IEC) and visibility materials, appropriate systems, commodities and infrastructure. The pulse survey showed no significant change over time for feasibility, appropriateness and acceptability; however, service providers generally viewed the integration of GBV first-line response into ANC and FP as appropriate and acceptable, based on average scores of approximately 3 out of a maximum of 4. The “lessons learned workshop” indicated that providers initially perceived the intervention as additional work, but over time, it became part of their routine workflow, which they acknowledged improved their ability to provide quality GBV services. However, a lack of funds and limited staff resources were found to be key barriers to integration.ConclusionOur results showed that integrating GBV first-line support into ANC and FP services is broadly feasible, appropriate and acceptable in both Sokoto and Ebonyi States, Nigeria. However, modest declines observed in month nine indicate ongoing challenges and a need for continued mentoring and support. Hence, GBV integration should be scaled up across health facilities whilst making available human and financial resources with specific investments in staff capacity, ongoing supervision, and community engagement for continuous strengthening of GBV service provision in both states and Nigeria at large.

Background/introduction

Gender-based violence (GBV), particularly intimate partner violence (IPV) and reproductive coercion (RC), is associated with poor reproductive and maternal health outcomes [1] and can also be the primary reason for women’s health deprivation during and after pregnancy, resulting in adverse birth outcomes [2, 3]. Estimates published by the World Health Organization (WHO) indicate that, globally, approximately one in three women worldwide have been subjected to either physical and/or sexual IPV or non-partner sexual violence in their lifetime. Worldwide, almost one-third (27%) of women aged 15–49 years who have been in a relationship report that they have been subjected to some form of physical and/or sexual violence by their intimate partner [4]. Nigeria’s Demographic Health Survey 2018 revealed that almost one in four ever-married women in Nigeria aged 15–49 years reported experiencing IPV (physical, sexual, or emotional). The percentages of ever-married women reporting experiences with IPV in Sokoto and Ebonyi states were 35.4% and 53.9%, respectively [5]. The evidence suggests that some women who experience IPV are more likely to experience it during pregnancy because of lower levels of social support, whereas higher levels of social support reduce antenatal anxiety and depression in women experiencing IPV during pregnancy [6]. A descriptive, cross-sectional study among 260 pregnant women attending ANC in a tertiary health facility in Sokoto metropolis reported that the lifetime prevalence of IPV during pregnancy was 30.4%, while 32.9% of the surveyed women experienced IPV in their first pregnancies, and 67.1% of the IPV occurred during their subsequent pregnancies [7]. RC is a form of GBV that adversely affects contraceptive use by women of childbearing age and interferes with pregnancy [8]. The RC has since been examined in parts of Nigeria, including in the states of Kano and Lagos, with RC rates of 5% and 2.5%, respectively, in representative women of reproductive age in need of contraception [9]. RC thrives on harmful social and gender norms, which celebrate early fertility for girls and low acceptance of contraceptive use for adolescent girls, with only 1.2% of married girls between ages15-19 meeting their contraception needs [10, 11].

Studies that establish the prevalence and severity of GBV abound across many settings, yet systematic strategies to prevent and effectively respond to IPV are largely lacking. To address GBV care in health settings, the WHO recommends screening and first-line support for IPV, which entails case identification, service provision and support to nonclinical referrals [12]. However, these recommendations are not readily operationalized across many low- and middle-income countries, including Nigeria. Nigeria’s national and state governments have continued to intensify efforts to address the increasing prevalence of GBV with the signing into law of the 2015 Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act, and notably, the Act has now been domesticated by all 36 states of Nigeria [13, 14]. In 2008, the Federal Ministry of Women’s Affairs and Social Development produced national guidelines and referral standards for GBV. In 2017, the Federal Ministry of Health produced standards and guidelines for the medical management of victims of violence in a bid to operationalize the VAPP law for health service delivery. This was followed in 2018 by the development of the National Gender in Health policy. Nigeria’s Federal Ministry of Health (FMOH) has made efforts to mount a health sector response to rising GBV challenges by quickly moving from policy to practice through the operationalizing of the WHO’s recommendation of routine GBV screening in clinical settings. Specifically, the FMOH adapted the WHO clinical guidelines on health care for women subjected to IPV for country-wide use in 2021. The Nigerian guidelines, titled “National guideline in healthcare for those who have experienced GBV: A clinical handbook and training curriculum for healthcare providers, are currently being used for health providers’ training across the country [15]. Despite these commendable efforts, an enormous gap still exists in identifying and managing GBV cases in health settings. A 2021 GBV service provider mapping in Ebonyi and Sokoto states revealed significant gaps in the existing knowledge base of service providers and health facility infrastructure available for providing quality post-GBV services [16, 17]. For example, most health care providers lack the capacity to provide basic first-line clinical support to GBV survivors (defined as counseling, safety planning, and referrals). Most healthcare workers have never received any form of training in GBV services; hence, they typically limit their care to treating physical injuries only. Essential services required to provide post-GBV care effectively at health facilities (e.g., private rooms for safe counseling) are nonexistent across most facilities. Only 12 (3% of the 284 mapped health facilities in Ebonyi state and only 1 (0.4%) of the 443mapped health facilities in Sokoto state met the minimum criteria for initiating post-GBV service provision [16, 17]. The results from service provider mapping in both states revealed a lack of facility readiness in terms of essential infrastructure, human resources for health and health provider capacity to provide quality survivor-centered post-GBV services. Specific facility-related issues around family planning, such as an inadequate number of skilled family planning providers, commodity supplies, misconceptions about family planning methods, and a lack of awareness of options for the use of contraceptive methods, also contribute to a lack of adoption of FP and fuel the culture of silence regarding reproductive coercion [18]. Hence, there is a need to establish the feasibility, appropriateness and acceptability of integrating IPV and RC screening first-line responses into routine ANC and FP services.

Several studies that have described the challenges and facilitators of IPV screening and counseling by healthcare workers have been conducted in high-income countries (HICs); however, studies evaluating the feasibility and acceptability of such integration in LMICs, including Nigeria, are nascent [19]. Barriers and facilitators to health service responses to IPV in HIC studies included a lack of provider training on IPV, few healthcare workers who receive specialized training in IPV, time constraints, inadequate privacy during clinical assessments and poor employer support from healthcare providers [20]. Owing to the complexity of risk factors and prevention strategies for IPV, systems-level interventions have been proposed, such as the creation of safe places, survival empowerment, the establishment of positive connections between service providers and survivors, and the management of the emotional/psychological impact of trauma [21]. However, a lack of time, a shortage of staff, and providers’ attitudes are frequently cited as barriers to GBV screening. However, those alone are not enough to explain screening practices. For example, according to Chamberlain and Perham-Hester (2002), only 6% of providers routinely screen and do not consider such a barrier [22]. Other influencing factors across studies were associated with clinic-level, provider-level, and client-level factors. Between February 2017 and March 2018, a process evaluation assessed the feasibility and acceptability of integrating screening and referrals for gender-based violence into HIV counseling and testing (HCT) for adolescent girls and young women in Tanzania and South Africa and revealed that GBV screening in HCT was feasible, with 31% of young women screening positive for GBV. Furthermore, the sensitive and empathetic approach of the health facility staff helped mitigate the distress of GBV disclosure at both sites [23]. We evaluated the feasibility, appropriateness and acceptance of integrating GBV (IPV and RC screening) and first-line response into routine family planning and ante-natal care clinics at intervention sites in a clinical trial in Sokoto and Ebonyi States, Nigeria.

Rationale for exploring service providers’ perspectives

Our rationale for focusing on providers’ perspective was drawn from the fact that healthcare providers’ attitudes, perceptions, and biases significantly impact the extent to which GBV screening is implemented effectively and the successful integration of GBV screening and response into routine services. Providers are often the first point of contact for survivors within the healthcare system, making their understanding and approach critical to creating an environment conducive to disclosure and support. An effectiveness, cost-effectiveness and barriers mixed systematic review revealed that integrating sexual reproductive health violence against women consultation and services require better tailoring to women’s needs and expectations and hence the need for service providers to adequately understand what women want and need and what is feasible [24]. Additional contextual challenges that affect GBV service delivery in health facilities across the two states include the following:

Provider bias and norms

Situations where providers unconsciously internalize societal stigmas, viewing GBV as a private or nonmedical issue leading to judgmental attitudes, reluctance to engage in conversations about GBV, or even outright dismissal of survivor concerns affect how providers respond to GBV survivors. Such biases undermine the trust and safety necessary for effective screening and response.

Work and competing priorities

Providers in the two study states operate in resource-constrained environments, where high patient loads and limited staffing worsen the pressures, hence making them perceive integrating GBV screening into routine services as an additional burden, as they are already overworked from playing multiple roles, for instance, in most cases; the officer in charge is the same person as the focal person for family planning and ANC and doubles as the same person attending to the outpatient department as well in charge of immunization owing to understaffing.

Lack of training and confidence

Many providers lack the training required to screen survivors for GBV sensitively and effectively. This includes understanding how to ask about GBV, recognizing nonverbal cues, and responding empathetically to disclosures. However, GBV significantly affects family planning and sexual and reproductive health outcomes. However, service providers are often ill supported and undertrained to recognize the outcome dynamics between GBV and family planning/SRH service uptake, build confidence and competence in discussing violence, and provide first-line responses to clients in need of help.

Institutional and policy-level gaps

Even when providers are willing to integrate GBV screening, systemic issues such as unclear protocols, a lack of referral pathways, and inadequate follow-up mechanisms hinder effective implementation. Providers do not receive the necessary support and are not well mobilized, leading to frustration and reduced motivation to prioritize GBV screening.

In Ebonyi and Sokoto States, these provider challenges are compounded by even more systemic barriers, such as cultural norms, where GBV is seen as the norm and people who report are stigmatized, providers are usually afraid of community and family backlash or legal repercussions for addressing sensitive issues. There is an overwhelming lack of trust in health systems by community members, where many survivors of the GBV approach healthcare settings with mistrust due to prior experiences of judgment, stigma, stereotyping or neglect, hence placing additional pressure on providers to rebuild confidence.

Methods

Study setting & design

Ebonyi and Sokoto States have a high burden of GBV, with 53.9% prevalence in Ebonyi and 35.4% prevalence in Sokoto (NDHS 2018). We selected local government areas for study based on areas with high clientele loads for family planning and ANC visits. The study forms part of a larger study involving a matched-pair, cluster-controlled trial with intervention in 20 public health facilities across the states of Ebonyi and Sokoto from December 2021 to April 2023 [25]. These facilities had functional antenatal care and family planning units and could provide at least three modern family planning methods and one long-acting reversible contraceptive (LARC). However, the study data were collected from intervention sites only in line with the study objectives.

We trained all 75 health care providers (16 nurses-midwives and 59 Community Health Extension Workers) in ANC and FP clinics at the 20 intervention sites on GBV first-line response and empowerment counseling, mentored and followed them up over 16-months, and they participated in the feasibility, appropriateness and acceptability studies. A health facility quality assurance tool was administered before the intervention, three months after the intervention commenced and at nine months. We conducted pulse surveys at months one, three and nine, and pulse surveys are brief questionnaires used to quickly gather stakeholder (provider) feedback and monitor the progress of an intervention. The questions were grouped by implementation outcomes (e.g., adoption, feasibility, acceptability) and were rated on a Likert scale from 1 to 4 (none = 1, a little = 2, moderate = 3, a lot = 4). The average scores were used to identify outliers, with scores above 3 or equal to 4 considered significant. In addition, a workshop to reflect on the lessons learned was conducted with health facility staff at the end of the study.

Intervention description

To integrate GBV first-line support in ANC and FP clinics, the MOMENTUM Country and Global Leadership (MCGL) Nigeria program trained 75 health providers via a combined training strategy of LIVES (Listening, Inquiring, Validating, Enhancing Safety, and Support) plus ARCHES (Addressing reproductive coercion in Health settings). The training modules comprised Nigeria’s National Guidelines in Healthcare for those who have experienced GBV: A clinical handbook and training curriculum for healthcare providers, Caring for Women Subject to Violence adapted from the WHO, which emphasizes LIVES, and adapted ARCHES into (LIVES + ARCHES) [26, 27]. The adapted WHO-recommended model of first-line support for gender-based violence provides practical care and response to women’s emotional, physical, safety and support needs, whereas the ARCHES strategy trains health workers on how to provide education on RC and IPV, contraceptive methods that can be used without risk of detection, screening for RC and IPV with supportive response not focused on disclosure, a warm referral to IPV support services for those who disclose IPV, and a palm-side educational booklet with information on RC, IPV, FP and referral resources [27]. MCGL conducted the training over a 5-day period covering topics on IPV, RC, provider bias and survivor-centered care. The training methodology included peer practice, group and individual exercises, simulated role play, demonstrations, return demonstrations, feedback, clinical hands-on practice and value clarification. The MCGL provided job aids for IPV and RC counseling, including adapted client education materials such as the ARCHES client mini-handbook, posters hung in waiting areas and counseling rooms at clinics to support client awareness of IPV and the RC. Trained health providers routinely screen clients for GBV, focusing on IPV and RC via a standardized screening form, and counsel survivors on IPV and RC, which lasts between 15 and 20 min, as well as safety planning and referrals to other nonclinical services via approved referral directories. To improve the quality of services, MCGL conducted monthly supportive supervision and mentoring visits to all 20 health facilities during the study period. Study procedures & measures

This study was embedded within a matched-pair, cluster-controlled trial published elsewhere; however, data collection was conducted only at intervention sites, in line with the study’s objective. We collected qualitative and quantitative data to assess the implementation outcomes of feasibility, appropriateness and health care provider acceptability of the screening and first-line response intervention, as put forth by Proctor et al. using a survey that includes items to be assessed on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (none) to 4 (a lot) [28]. The study assessed feasibility, or the extent to which first-line support, including screening and the LIVES + ARCHES intervention can be carried out in the public health facility through multiple methods. The first consisted of a provider pulse survey feasibility module used to obtain provider perspectives; the skills, time and resources required for implementation; and whether these were sufficient during the study period. The second was a health facility quality assurance assessment, which assessed the extent to which facilities were able to reach and maintain quality service delivery over the study period. The register reviews and monitoring data (supportive supervision checklists & knowledge assessments) at multiple time points were used to assess the extent to which the screening portion of the intervention was carried out over time, as well as an endline reflection workshop with the providers to identify barriers and facilitators to implementing the intervention (see Table 2). Using provider pulse surveys and an endline reflection workshop, we assessed the appropriateness or the perceived fit, effectiveness or relevance of the LIVES + ARCHES intervention for a setting, problem and/or audience. We used provider pulse surveys and endline workshops to assess acceptability, the extent to which the providers perceived the screening and first-line intervention to be agreeable, for example, if they enjoyed implementing the intervention, if the intervention and related materials were clear, and how implementing the intervention affected their routine workflow. Providers at the 20 intervention sites participated in this study to measure outcomes based on the training they received and the subsequent services they provided. Process documentation: primary data collection and iterative adaptations

This study employed an iterative learning and adaptation approach during implementation based on outputs from its three primary data collection methods—a pulse survey, a quality assurance assessment and lessons learned workshops. The pulse survey, a Likert-scale tool administered six times, was used to assess the feasibility, acceptability, and appropriateness of the intervention. The lessons learned from the sessions provided qualitative data to complement the quantitative findings, whereas the quality assurance assessment helped us understand how the facility improved with respect to the GBV service provision standard criteria over time.

Pulse surveys were conducted over time (six iterations) to enable continuous adaptation as the study progressed. During the first two months, the survey tool was administered biweekly (four rounds in total), allowing for the timely detection of low-scoring variables and the development of targeted recommendations. Early issues identified included two health facilities with low acceptability scores, complaints about inadequate staff welfare, and low scores related to alignment with male cultural norms. To address these challenges, we implemented several adaptations, such as the following:

These interventions led to noticeable improvements, as evidenced by the results from Pulse 5. However, between Pulsers 5 and 6, no further adaptations were implemented, leading to a slight drop in scores, as seen in the pulse survey table. The quality assurance tool, on the other hand, provided detailed insights into facilities’ compliance with GBV service provision standards [29, 30]. Following the completion of the first round of data collection during the pre-implementation period, we trained service providers and conducted post training supervision over a two-month pilot phase to ensure a thorough understanding of the intervention. This preparation facilitated a smooth transition to the intervention’s implementation phase.

The tool was readministered three months after data collection began, with the final administration occurring at the nine-month follow-up. Despite the absence of additional adaptations during this period, the facilities continued to show improvement in quality assurance standards, as evidenced by the endpoint results. Notably, these improvements may also be attributed to other projects that introduced GBV interventions within the same facilities during the study period. While these external interventions were not comprehensive (e.g., they did not include ARCHES or GBV screening), they increased the visibility and awareness of general GBV programming at health facilities, encouraging more provider interest in meeting the required standards. The third primary data collection method, the lessons learned workshop, was instrumental in triangulating findings from the pulse surveys, as reflected in the results presented.

Analysis

We conducted all the quantitative analyses with Stata version 14 and Microsoft Excel and the qualitative analyses with Dedoose software. For the pulse surveys, we conducted paired t tests, given that the samples were not independent (i.e., surveyed the same set of providers), to compare values at baseline and 9 months, with a significance value set at p < 0.05. For the quality assurance descriptive analyses, we used chi-square tests to compare baseline and 9-month data, also with a significance level of p < 0.05. Facility averages were estimated across all the providers for the pulse surveys, while the quality assessments and screening rates were measured by facilities. We uploaded the provider reflection workshop notes to Dedoose software and thematically coded them with a code book developed a priori and based on the CFIR domains and constructs, with codes for facilitators, barriers and recommendations [31]. We coded one set of notes first to allow for any new themes to emerge, after which the codebook was reviewed and revised by two analysts before the remaining notes were analyzed. The identified facilitators, barriers and recommendations for the implementation outcomes of feasibility, acceptability and appropriateness were summarized by the CFIR domains (intervention characteristics, inner setting, outer setting, individuals involved and process).

The CFIR constructs consist of five domains: intervention characteristics, the outer setting, the inner setting, individual characteristics, and the process. The intervention characteristics explore constructs such as relative advantage, which is the perceived advantage that the intervention has over others, adaptability and complexity of the intervention. The outer setting refers to the external factors influencing the intervention, which could include policies and local laws. In contrast, inner settings refer to internal factors influencing the intervention, including compatibility and relative priority. Individual characteristics explore the attributes of individuals in the intervention, such as provider knowledge and beliefs, whereas process domains refer to activities related to the intervention, such as planning and executing.

Results

Out of the 75 trained healthcare providers, 57 implemented the intervention and participated in the study. Out of the 75 trained providers, 7 were transferred from their health facilities during the study implementation, and 11 were not available during the data collection and were thus not included in this study. However, the lessons learned workshop included all 68 service providers who were available at the sites after the 9-month follow-up.

Feasibility

In the provider pulse survey, the overall feasibility score, which included questions related to provider skills, time and resources, increased from 2.71 in month one to 2.77 at month three then decreased from 2.71 to 2.53 by month nine (see Table 3). Reasons for the low scores include the lack of available resources (e.g., computers, airtime, etc.) and the organizational budget and remuneration, all of which scored low from baseline to endline. However, when we removed these variables, the feasibility score reflecting provider skills and time remained at 3.26 at baseline and endline. By endline, there was a decrease in provider confidence in being sufficiently skilled to implement the intervention, which increased from 3.42 in month one to 3.53 at month three then decreased from 3.53 to 2.78 by month nine. This may be due to reduced frequency of supervision and mentoring toward months six and nine hence, indicating a likely need for additional mentoring or refresher training. However, over time, providers maintained that they had time to implement the intervention, with slight increases in the time-related question scores over time. Across all time points, providers generally thought that their remuneration and organizational budgets were not sufficient to carry out the intervention.

Discussion

This study identified facility-level and provider-level factors that determine the feasibility, appropriateness and acceptability of integrating IPV and RC screening and first-line response (LIVES + ARCHES) into routine ANC and FP services in Sokoto and Ebonyi States, Nigeria. To measure feasibility, appropriateness and acceptability, our study identified specific facilitators, such as improved provider capacity to screen, identify and care for GBV survivors because of training and ongoing supportive supervision, which exposed providers to the importance of confidentiality and privacy settings in the provision of post-GBV care at health facilities. Study respondents opined that the LIVES + ARCHES intervention filled a significant knowledge gap and made GBV screening readily available at health facilities. The identified barriers include staff shortages, a lack of trained staff, heavy workloads, user fees, a lack of incentives, a lack of funds, resource constraints, stockouts of screening materials and poor facility infrastructure. Our findings corroborate previous findings showing that facilitators of IPV screening include privacy and confidentiality, establishing positive connections between service providers and survivors, and managing the emotional/psychological impact of trauma [32], whereas barriers include a lack of provider training on IPV, few healthcare workers who receive specialized training in IPV, time constraints, inadequate privacy during clinical assessments and poor employer support from healthcare providers.

Feasibility of LIVES + ARCHES intervention

The results of both pulse surveys showed a decrease in self-assessed skills, which may in some part be attributed to an increased awareness of skill gaps after training [33], while lessons learned from reflection workshops indicated that providers felt that they had sufficient skills and time to screen for IPV and RC and provide care for identified cases. However, they complained about shortages of human resources, which resulted in an increased workload for them. The increase in the number of quality assurance standards met by health facilities reflects facility willingness and readiness to screen, identify and provide services to survivors. Our findings strongly indicate that providers perceived the LIVES + ARCHES intervention as additional work that requires additional material, human and financial resources for the work to be seamless. Specifically, healthcare providers indicated that a lack of facility funds and client funds were barriers to linking women to additional services. During implementation, average screening rates across the 20 implementation sites were less than 60%, raising questions about feasibility and acceptability. However, the lessons learned from the reflection workshop revealed that providers reported time challenges at the beginning of the intervention due to the time-consuming nature of screening, but by the end of the study, the same providers maintained that they could implement it more seamlessly when they became accustomed to the screening procedures. Like our findings, other studies in Africa have found that resource constraints are a barrier to providing comprehensive GBV care. A pilot study in South Africa reported that GBV response could be provided only once a month at each PHC facility because one service provider had only ten days a month to dedicate to the intervention, which was carried out over a large geographical area [34]. Additionally, a study in rural Kenya reported that limited funds were available to support transport costs for clients and community volunteers to reach referral agencies in the nearest town to cover cell phone costs for health workers and volunteers so that they could communicate with each other and for referral agencies and biweekly meetings of volunteers [35]. Another Kenyan study in refugee camps reported that screening patients in ANC individually was time-consuming, whereas a South African study reported that primary care providers were reluctant due to the extra time it took [36]. These three studies corroborate our finding that time constraints are also an important factor for providers. In terms of providers’ knowledge and skills to effectively screen for GBV and provide services, we found that not all aspects of the LIVES + ARCHES intervention were adequately implemented consistently throughout the study period; however, with additional mentoring and ongoing supportive supervision, there was an optimal increase in the quality of screening and service provision. Thus, we posit that on-going mentoring and on-site supportive supervision will help ensure the fidelity and quality of the LIVES + ARCHES intervention and are important in building health workers’ capacity to ensure quality and comprehensive screening and GBV services. This finding is consistent with a study in Tanzania and South Africa, which revealed that experiential learning and mentoring are critical for sustaining screening in HIV counseling and testing interventions for adolescent girls and young women, as is a study in rural Kenya, where providers stressed the need for on-going refresher training and sensitization. Furthermore, a 2021 Cochrane review consisting of 19 trials in high-income settings revealed that training providers on IPV may have a large effect on their self-perceived readiness to respond to IPV survivors, although the evidence was uncertain (SMD2.44, 95% CI 1.51–3.37; 6 studies, 487 participants; very low-certainty evidence) and may improve the identification of IPV at six months post-training (RR 4.54, 95% CI 2.5–8.09), as in one study (with 54 participants), although three studies (with 48 participants) reported little to no effects of training on the identification or documentation of IPV, or both [35].

Appropriateness and acceptability of LIVES + ARCHES intervention

Findings from both pulse surveys and reflection workshops indicate that providers perceive the LIVES + ARCHES intervention to fit well within their work, as they reported that they liked the LIVES + ARCHES intervention because the knowledge and skills they gained made them better providers and that they perceived that it was effective in addressing their client’s needs. Specifically, providers reported that there was significant improvement in confidentiality and privacy during service provision. Our findings are consistent with findings of a comparative analysis of population-based data from 12 countries in Latin America and the Caribbean, which improved privacy, improved recognition of violence by providers, privacy, confidentiality and strengthened referrals for legal services [37, 38]. However, our findings differed in that providers did not accompany survivors to nonclinical referrals because most facilities do not have enough human resources to physically accompany survivors to access nonclinical referrals. However, we taught warm referrals and encouraged providers to first contact the service provider where referrals were made via telephone so that the provider would expect the survivor, but some survivors did not complete such referrals for other reasons, such as a lack of transportation and distance. Overall, providers perceived our LIVES + ARCHES intervention to be culturally appropriate for themselves but less so for the general community, as they perceived that cultural norms were a barrier for the LIVES + ARCHES intervention as well as in supporting survivors in accessing follow-up care.

Strengths and limitations

Some strengths of our study include the multiplicity of qualitative data collection methods, which allowed us to adequately unpack the responses and effectively triangulate the information gathered. We were also able to assess changes in provider perceptions over time through repeat applications of pulse surveys and the GBV quality assurance Tool. There are also some limitations to this study. First, pulse survey results are subjective, as they are self-reported by providers. The convenience sampling of trained providers at the 20 implementation sites was small and not representative of the healthcare provider population across both states. In addition, we were not able to survey all trained participants, as 24% were not available at the time of data collection (18 of 75). We assessed the verification criteria for the quality assurance tool via interviews, as we did not observe service provisions; hence, provider bias in the responses could have influenced the scores. Also, some of the study sites were receiving additional routine support from other GBV programs which may have also contributed to some facility improvements, hence observed changes may not be attributed solely to our intervention. Finally, the study did not assess the feasibility, acceptability, appropriateness or quality of services from the perspective of clients, hence, restricting the appropriateness and acceptability of the LIVES + ARCHES intervention recorded to providers perspectives alone.

Conclusion

Our results show that integrating LIVES + ARCHES into ANC and FP services is broadly feasible, appropriate and acceptable in both Sokoto and Ebonyi States, Nigeria however, the modest declines by month nine indicate ongoing challenges and a need for continued mentoring and support. Hence, our findings suggest that while integration is feasible and acceptable, this is under certain conditions, which include where there is supportive supervision, provider training, and minimal infrastructure in place. Scaling-up needs to be accompanied by investment in staff capacity, ongoing supervision, and community engagement. Hence, GBV integration should be scaled up across health facilities while making available human and financial resources for continuous strengthening of GBV service provision in both states and Nigeria at large. Similar to the findings of other studies in both LMICs and high-income settings and in synchronization with the WHO’s recommendation to screen for IPV in health services, where minimum requirements are met around protocols, training and privacy and confidentiality, first-line responses, including basic counseling and referrals, are provided as part of routine care. Hence, the integration of LIVES + ARCHES as part of the routine package of care in ANC and FP services should be scaled up across all health facilities. To enhance on-going mentoring and supportive supervision, which our study found instrumental in deepening providers’ knowledge and skills, GBV indicators should become an integral component of integrated supportive supervisory visits to health facilities. Additionally, governments at all levels should make budgetary allocations for LIVES + ARCHES intervention at health facilities, and primary health facilities in Nigeria can leverage the Basic Health Care Provision Fund (BHCPF) to ensure the availability of budgetary allocations and release for GBV services. Findings on staff shortages require that adequate resources be made available at health facilities to close the existing staff shortage gap, reduce work burden and allow health providers more time to attend to GBV survivors. Finally, providers opined that eliminating user fees for GBV services would increase access to and utilization of quality survivor-centered GBV services at health facilities. Future research should explore acceptability and appropriateness from the perspective of clients, examine in more detail the time and resources needed to screen and provide GBV services, and examine further integration of the intervention into primary health care services in Nigeria and globally.
