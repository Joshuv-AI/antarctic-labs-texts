Testing Monotonicity of Real-Valued Functions on DAGs
Yuichi Yoshida

Abstract
We study monotonicity testing of real-valued functions on directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) with n n vertices. For every constant δ > 0 \delta>0 , we prove a Ω ⁡ ( n 1 / 2 − δ / ε ) \Omega(n^{1/2-\delta}/\sqrt{\varepsilon}) lower bound against non-adaptive two-sided testers on DAGs, nearly matching the classical O ⁡ ( n / ε ) O(\sqrt{n/\varepsilon}) -query upper bound. A key technical ingredient in both lower bounds is positive-matching Ruzsa–Szemerédi families. 1.3
Technical overview

1.3.1
Non-adaptive two-sided lower bound

Lower bounds: from induced matchings to positive matchings.

The classical lower-bound approach for monotonicity testing on general posets, pioneered by
Fischer et al. [18], is built around
Ruzsa–Szemerédi (RS) graphs
: one packs many large

induced
matchings and uses them to generate hard YES/NO instances.
The relevance of matchings is that violated pairs in a matching are vertex-disjoint, so repairing all of them
requires changing at least one endpoint per pair, which directly yields a distance-from-monotonicity lower bound.
For query lower bounds, matchings also localize evidence: a tester typically needs to query both endpoints of one
matched pair to observe informative violations.
Inducedness then plays a dual role there.
It is what makes the violation pattern “clean” for
Boolean
-valued
functions (extra comparabilities among matching endpoints would immediately create additional
violations), and at the same time it becomes the bottleneck: extremal graph theory severely limits
how many linear-size induced matchings can coexist in a single graph, which is why RS-based
templates stall far below the
\tilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{n})
regime.

Our first conceptual step is to identify the right analogue of induced matchings for

real-valued
functions. On bipartite DAGs (all edges oriented left-to-right), the question
“can a matching be realized as
exactly
the set of violated edges?” has a precise answer:
this holds if and only if the matching is
positive
(
Section

3.1
).
Informally, “positive” means there is a vertex weight assignment
w
such that
w(\ell)+w(r)>0

exactly on matching edges and
w(\ell)+w(r)\leq 0
on all other edges.
Equivalently, positivity
excludes alternating cycles/closed walks on the matched vertices (
Theorem

2.3
).
This motivates our central combinatorial abstraction:
positive-matching
RS (PMRS) families
, obtained by relaxing RS from induced matchings to positive matchings
(
Section

3.2
). This relaxation is tailored to the real-valued setting and bypasses the inducedness
barrier that constrains the Fischer et al. framework.

From PMRS to a near-tight non-adaptive lower bound.

Once a PMRS decomposition is available, the lower bound becomes essentially information-theoretic.
Given
s
edge-disjoint positive matchings
M_{1},\dots,M_{s}
(each of linear size), we hide a random
index
i\in[s]
and construct two distributions over functions. The YES distribution is monotone,
built from a base monotone function with a constant margin on non-matching edges (so small random noise does not create new violations), while the NO distribution is
\varepsilon
-far because it violates
a constant fraction of edges in the hidden matching
M_{i}
(
Section

3.3
).
Crucially, the two distributions have identical
single-vertex marginals
; they differ only in a
local correlation between the two endpoints of a matching edge. Therefore, a non-adaptive algorithm
can obtain distinguishing information only if it queries
both
endpoints of some edge in
M_{i}
.
Since the matchings are edge-disjoint, a
q
-query set can fully cover at most
O(q^{2})
edges, and
the probability of hitting the hidden matching index is at most
O(q^{2}/s)
, yielding the

\Omega(\sqrt{s})
lower bound (
Theorem

3.8
).
A useful technical fact (as with induced matchings) is that positivity is closed under taking submatchings
(
Section

3.1
), which lets us refine each large matching
into many smaller positive matchings and cleanly extract the
1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}
dependence
(
Sections

3.5
and
3.5
).

Constructing PMRS: a polynomial-size packing beyond RS limitations.

The remaining challenge is to build explicit graphs with
many
large edge-disjoint positive matchings.
This is precisely where the departure from induced matchings matters: unlike RS graphs, PMRS packings
exhibit different extremal behavior and admit simple polynomial-size constructions.
Our construction (
Section

3.4
) uses vertex labels
(x,z)
with
x\in[N]^{k}
and an auxiliary “height”
coordinate
z\in[N^{2}]
. Each matching is indexed by a shift vector
a
and consists of translated edges

(x,z)\mapsto(x+a,\,z+\|a\|_{2}^{2})
. The nontrivial point is positivity: we design a weight function
w_{a}

so that for an edge belonging to shift
b
, the positivity-witness sum

w_{a}(\ell)+w_{a}(r)
(for edge endpoints
\ell,r
) collapses to a quantity of the form

1/2-\|b-a\|_{2}^{2}
, which is positive exactly when
b=a
(
Theorem

3.9
). This “quadratic lift” is the key
mechanism that separates different matchings by a single linear functional and enables

s=\Omega(n^{k/(k+2)})
edge-disjoint linear-size positive matchings. Combined with the PMRS
\Rightarrow

lower-bound template above, this yields our near-
\sqrt{n/\varepsilon}
non-adaptive two-sided lower bound.

1.3.2
Adaptive one-sided lower bound

Softly planted violations and the witness principle.

Moving from non-adaptive to adaptive testers, and from two-sided to one-sided error, changes the lower-bound game qualitatively. In the bipartite-DAG setting (all edges oriented left-to-right), one-sidedness comes with a very strong “witness principle” (
Section

4
): if the tester has not queried a violated edge, then the partial transcript can always be extended to a globally monotone function. In other words, a one-sided tester is not “estimating distance” indirectly; it is literally searching for a witnessed violation. The challenge is that adaptivity seems to help precisely with such search problems: after observing a few values, the tester can steer future queries toward edges that are more likely to be violated.

Section

4
’s strategy is to turn this apparent strength of adaptivity into a liability by embedding the search problem into an indexing problem. As in the PMRS-based non-adaptive lower bound, we start from a scaffold graph containing many edge-disjoint positive matchings
M_{1},\dots,M_{s}
, and we hide a random index
I\in[s]
. The goal is to generate a hard distribution over functions such that (i) with high probability the sampled function is
\varepsilon
-far from monotone because it violates almost all edges of the hidden matching
M_{I}
, (ii) edges outside
M_{I}
are “almost never” violated (so the tester cannot reject without learning something about
I
), and (iii) even an adaptive algorithm learns only a tiny amount of information about
I
per query. The last two requirements are exactly where naive “plant a matching of violations” constructions break: once we condition on an adaptive prefix, the remaining unqueried variables are no longer independent, and a tester might try to create (or expose) violations on non-matching edges by strategically revealing endpoints.

The key conceptual move is to replace a combinatorial YES/NO gadget by a soft, analytically controlled prior: a truncated, strongly log-concave Gibbs distribution
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i\in[s]}
over real-valued labelings
f
. The Hamiltonian has three roles. A quadratic “pinning + smoothing” backbone

\frac{\alpha}{2}\sum_{v}f(v)^{2}+\lambda\sum_{(\ell,r)\in E}(f(\ell)-f(r))^{2}

enforces global strong convexity, which later yields stability of conditional marginals under arbitrary transcript conditioning. On top of this, hinge-square potentials implement a “margin-separated” monotonicity pattern: for edges
e\notin M_{i}
we add a penalty
\beta(f(\ell)-f(r)+\gamma)_{+}^{2}
, which energetically prefers
f(\ell)-f(r)\leq-\gamma
(monotone with slack), while for edges
e\in M_{i}
we add
\beta(\gamma-(f(\ell)-f(r)))_{+}^{2}
, which prefers
f(\ell)-f(r)\geq\gamma
(a robust violation). Finally, we truncate to a box
[-B,B]^{V}
to keep all Lipschitz/concentration arguments uniform. The result is a “softly planted” violated matching: violations are not imposed as hard constraints, but they are so energetically favored that they occur with overwhelming probability, and crucially this preference survives conditioning on adaptively revealed coordinates.

Two technical bottlenecks then dominate the proof, and
Section

4
resolves them via two lines of analysis: spurious control and information leakage, explained below.

Spurious violations under adaptivity.

Even if a fresh non-matching edge is very unlikely to be violated unconditionally, an adaptive tester might try to bias it by first querying one endpoint and then deciding whether to query the other. The analysis therefore must be prefix-conditioned: after any transcript prefix, when the tester “closes” a new edge, the conditional probability of witnessing a violation on an edge outside
M_{I}
must still be tiny. This is handled by a careful one-dimensional reduction on the difference coordinate
d=f(\ell)-f(r)
for an edge
e=(\ell,r)
: after conditioning on everything except the two endpoints, the induced density of
d
is log-concave with explicit strong curvature coming from the quadratic backbone. The hinge term sets a target point (
+\gamma/2
for matching edges and
-\gamma/2
for non-matching edges), and strong convexity translates “the derivative at the target has the right sign” into an exponentially small tail probability of landing on the wrong side (violated vs. non-violated).

The subtlety is the external drift contributed by all other incident constraints: under adaptive conditioning, this drift is random and depends on the revealed neighborhood.

Section

4
controls this drift at the two critical points
d=\pm\gamma/2
with a high-probability argument: for
C_{4}
-free scaffolds, the relevant neighborhood variables become independent after conditioning on the endpoints, and strong log-concavity gives subgaussian tails for the drift (the “drift-tail” bound in
Section

4.2
). This is paired with a separate “mean-control” step to ensure the conditional mean of the drift is also small; in the final instantiation on our explicit dense scaffold (
Section

4.7
), this is the most delicate analytic point.

There is a second, purely one-sided obstacle: the box truncation introduces boundary effects, and an adaptive tester might try to push queried values near
\pm B
, where the feasible interval of
d
shrinks and the clean tail bound could fail.
The fix is an “inward drift” phenomenon (
Sections

4.1
and
4.1
): when
\beta>\lambda
, the hinge potentials create a deterministic force that pushes single-site conditionals away from the dangerous boundary strips. This yields a strategy-independent bound on the probability that any query ever lands near
\pm B
(
Section

4.3
), and hence bounds the probability that an adaptive strategy can exploit truncation to manufacture spurious witnesses.
Putting these together yields an adaptive spurious-control theorem (
Section

4.3
): with high probability, any
q
-query adaptive strategy witnesses no violated edge outside the hidden matching unless it hits an exponentially unlikely boundary event.

Information leakage from adaptive queries.

Even if spurious violations are suppressed, an adaptive tester could hope to learn the hidden index
I
from the queried values, and then target an edge of
M_{I}
.
Section

4
controls this via a mutual-information upper bound
I(I;T)
, where
T
is the full adaptive transcript. The core technical ingredient is a “Green-function comparison” inequality for uniformly convex Gibbs measures (
Section

4.4
): for any prefix conditioning
\tau
, the KL divergence between the one-site conditional distributions under
\mu_{i}(\cdot\mid\tau)
and
\mu_{j}(\cdot\mid\tau)
is bounded by a weighted sum of local discrepancies, with weights given by entries of
(\alpha I+2\lambda L_{\tau})^{-1}
, where
L_{\tau}
is the Laplacian on the still-unqueried vertices after conditioning on the prefix
\tau
. Unlike Dobrushin-type arguments, this comparison needs no global mixing/uniqueness regime; it leverages only the strong convexity provided by the quadratic backbone and survives arbitrary adaptive conditioning. A simple row-sum bound on this Green function then gives a clean per-query leakage scale
\kappa_{Q}
, leading to the budgeted bound
I(I;T)\leq q\kappa_{Q}
uniformly over all adaptive strategies with
q\leq Q
(
Section

4.4
).

With spurious-control and leakage in place, the lower bound becomes information-theoretic in a way that is tailor-made for one-sided testing. Because the matchings are edge-disjoint, any witnessed violated edge in
\bigcup_{i}M_{i}
uniquely identifies the hidden index
I
. By the one-sided witness principle, rejection implies the transcript contains a witnessed violated edge; by spurious-control, with high probability this violated edge lies in
M_{I}
. Thus any successful tester yields a decoder that recovers
I
from
T
with constant probability, and a standard Fano argument (
Section

4.5
) forces
I(I;T)\gtrsim\log s
. Comparing
\log s
to the leakage upper bound
q\kappa_{Q}
produces a fixed-budget barrier (
Theorem

4.22
): below a certain query budget, no adaptive one-sided tester can simultaneously (a) keep spurious witnesses rare and (b) accumulate enough information to find a true matching-edge violation reliably.

Dense-graph instantiation and final bound.

Section

4.7
instantiates this framework on an explicit dense scaffold: a
C_{4}
-free, almost-regular subgraph of the shift-matching construction (
Section

4.7
) with
s=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
disjoint positive matchings. In this regime the auxiliary probabilities (matching-edge non-violation, spurious violations, boundary failures, and mean-drift failures) can all be driven superpolynomially small by setting the margin
\gamma
to grow like
\sqrt{\log Q}
, while the per-query leakage remains on the order of
(\log Q)/\sqrt{n}
. Choosing
Q\asymp\sqrt{n}
then makes the information barrier bite and yields an
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
adaptive one-sided lower bound for a constant
\varepsilon
(
Theorem

4.28
).

1.3.3
Upper bounds

Bottleneck routing from a violation matching (
Section

5
).

Our algorithmic upper bounds are guided by a single structural fact: distance to monotonicity is witnessed by a large matching of violated comparable pairs in the transitive closure. Concretely, if
f
is
\varepsilon
-far, then the violation graph (on comparable pairs) contains a matching
M
of size
|M|\geq\varepsilon n
(via Konig’s theorem), so there are many disjoint source–sink violations
(u\!\rightsquigarrow\!v,\ f(u)>f(v))
. The difficulty is that these violations typically live in the transitive closure
\mathrm{TC}(G)
, while the input provides only the transitive reduction (Hasse diagram), whose edge set can be much smaller and may contain very few direct violations. The message of
Section

5
is that this apparent obstacle can be turned into a win: if direct violating edges are scarce, then they must act as bottlenecks through which many matched violations are routed, and this forces a large supply of violated pairs that can be detected by simple sampling.

The core combinatorial viewpoint is a bottleneck partition of the violation matching
M
. Fix, for each
(u,v)\in M
, an arbitrary directed path
P_{uv}
in the transitive reduction from
u
to
v
. Since
f(u)>f(v)
, along that path there must be a first edge
(x,y)
where the inequality flips, i.e., a violating edge of the reduction. Assign
(u,v)
to this first violating edge, and thereby partition

M=\biguplus_{e\in F_{f}}M_{e},\qquad F_{f}:=\{(x,y)\in E:\ f(x)>f(y)\}.

This routing/assignment does two crucial things simultaneously.

1.

It converts global violations in
\mathrm{TC}(G)
into local witness edges in
E
.

2.

Because
M
is a matching, the sets of left endpoints
\{A_{e}\}
and right endpoints
\{B_{e}\}
of the parts
M_{e}
are disjoint across different bottlenecks
e
. This disjointness is what makes the probabilistic analysis clean and is the main technical reason the approach works at scale.

From here,
Section

5
develops two testers (
Algorithms

1
and
2
), each exploiting a different way to capitalize on this dichotomy and proved in
Theorems

5.1
and
5.2
.

(i) A transitive-reduction/transitive-closure hybrid tester:
O\!\left(\sqrt{m\ell/(\varepsilon n)}\right)
queries.

The first tester (
Algorithm

1
, see also
Section

5.1
) samples a moderate number of edges from the transitive reduction
E
and, independently, a moderate number of comparable pairs from
E(\mathrm{TC}(G))
. The analysis is built around a sharp either–or principle:

•

If many reduction edges are violated (i.e.,
|F_{f}|
is large), then uniform sampling from
E
finds a witness quickly.

•

If few reduction edges are violated, then the bottleneck partition above implies a quadratic blow-up in the number of violated comparable pairs in the closure: each bottleneck edge
e
with a large bucket
M_{e}
certifies many violated pairs of the form
(a,b)
with
a\in A_{e}
,
b\in B_{e}
. The median trick used in the proof formalizes this amplification: by splitting endpoints around a median threshold
t_{e}
, one obtains large subsets
A^{+}_{e}\subseteq A_{e}
,
B^{-}_{e}\subseteq B_{e}
such that every
(a,b)\in A^{+}_{e}\times B^{-}_{e}
is a violated pair in
\mathrm{TC}(G)
. Thus each
e
contributes
\Omega(|M_{e}|^{2})
violated comparable pairs, and summing (plus Cauchy–Schwarz) yields a lower bound of order
|M|^{2}/|F_{f}|
on the number of violated closure edges.

Balancing these two regimes with an optimized threshold
\tau
leads to a rejection probability bounded away from
1/2
using

q\asymp\sqrt{\frac{m\ell}{\varepsilon n}}

samples in each loop (up to constants). Conceptually, this is a tradeoff between easy local witnesses and many global witnesses: if one cannot see violations locally, they must manifest globally in abundance, and vice versa. This bound can beat the classical
O(\sqrt{n/\varepsilon})
tester on sparse reachability structures, precisely when
m\ell=o(n^{3})
for constant
\varepsilon
.

(ii) A two-stage tester independent of
\ell
:
O(m^{1/3}/\varepsilon^{2/3})
queries.

The second tester (
Algorithm

2
, see also
Section

5.2
) removes dependence on
\ell
altogether by avoiding uniform sampling from
\mathrm{TC}(G)
. Instead, after a first stage that samples reduction edges (to catch the many-violating-edges case), it runs a second stage that samples two random multisets of vertices
Q_{L},Q_{R}
and then checks all comparable pairs between them (reachability tests and scanning
Q_{L}\times Q_{R}
are free in the model). This is where the bottleneck partition becomes even more powerful: the disjoint endpoint structure means that if there are few violating edges, then the matching
M
must be split across few buckets, so some bucket
M_{e}
must be large; and if
Q_{L}
hits
A^{+}_{e}
and
Q_{R}
hits
B^{-}_{e}
for the same
e
, the tester certifiably rejects.

The technical obstacle in making this quantitative is dependence: sampling vertices with replacement creates complicated correlations when analyzing the event “for some
e
, we hit both sides of
A^{+}_{e}\times B^{-}_{e}
.”
Section

5
resolves this with a classic but elegant tool, Poissonization/Poisson approximation. By coupling the multinomial sampling counts with independent Poisson variables, the analysis turns the success events for different bottlenecks
e
into (essentially) independent events, exactly because the
A^{+}_{e}
’s are disjoint and the
B^{-}_{e}
’s are disjoint. This produces a clean product-form bound and again leverages Cauchy–Schwarz to relate
\sum_{e}|M_{e}|^{2}
to
|M|^{2}/|F_{f}|
. Choosing the stage sizes to balance the many-violated-reduction-edges regime with the few-bottlenecks regime yields

q\asymp\frac{m^{1/3}}{\varepsilon^{2/3}}

queries overall.

;
connections to isoperimetric inequalities for the Boolean cube were developed in

. A substantial

[

.
In parallel, for general posets, testing was initiated by Fischer et al.

Beyond standard testing, distance estimation and tolerant testing were studied in

More recently, in product domains, real-valued monotonicity has been connected to isoperimetric inequalities for

Against this backdrop, for explicit-DAG monotonicity testing with real-valued ranges, prior lower bounds in both non-adaptive and adaptive models were far from the

scale.

Graph-theoretic background.

Graphs whose edges can be partitioned into induced matchings (Ruzsa–Szemerédi-type graphs)
have been extensively studied in extremal graph theory since
[
30
]
. In particular, when each induced
matching has linear size
r=cn
, the number of matchings in such a decomposition is known
to be severely limited (up to constants/logarithmic factors depending on
c
), highlighting
a barrier for RS-based lower-bound templates
[
3
,
20
]
.
Positive matchings have also been studied under the name of positive matching decompositions
[
18
]
.
The graph-theoretic motivation is that positivity gives a structured class of matchings, so one can ask whether
the whole edge set can be partitioned into positive matchings and how many parts are necessary.
Concretely, positive matchings are characterized by the absence of
M
-alternating closed walks (where
M
denotes the matching under consideration) on the

M
-saturated vertices
[
18
]
; in bipartite graphs this is equivalent
to being alternating-cycle-free (uniquely restricted matching)
[
24
]
.
Our PMRS framework can be viewed as a packing/RS-style relaxation tailored to real-valued
monotonicity testing on bipartite DAGs, where positivity is exactly the realizability
condition for “violations supported on a matching.”

2.1
Graph notation

Let
G=(V,E)
be a directed graph with
n:=|V|
.
For disjoint vertex sets
S,T\subseteq V
, let
e(S,T)
denote the number of edges
(u,v)\in E

with
u\in S
and
v\in T
.
We write
u\leadsto v
if there is a directed path from
u
to
v
in
G
.
The
transitive closure

\mathrm{TC}(G)
has edge set

E(\mathrm{TC}(G)):=\{(u,v)\in V\times V:u\leadsto v\},

and we write
\ell:=|E(\mathrm{TC}(G))|
.
For a DAG
G
, the
transitive reduction
(Hasse diagram) is the unique DAG

\mathrm{TR}(G)=(V,E_{R})
with
\mathrm{TC}(\mathrm{TR}(G))=\mathrm{TC}(G)
and minimal edge set.
Since monotonicity depends only on reachability, we may replace
G
by
\mathrm{TR}(G)
without loss of
generality and hence assume
G
is transitively reduced; we write
m:=|E|
.

2.2
Positive matchings

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be an undirected graph and let
M\subseteq E
be a matching.

Definition 2.1

(Positive matching
[
18
]
)
.

The matching
M
is
positive
(with respect to
\Gamma
) if there exists
a weight function
w:V\to\mathbb{R}
such that for every edge
e=\{u,v\}\in E
,

w(u)+w(v)>0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad e\in M,

equivalently:
w(u)+w(v)>0
for
e\in M
and
w(u)+w(v)\leq 0
for
e\notin M
.
1
1

1

Some references require
w(u)+w(v)<0
for
e\notin M
. For finite graphs, the two formulations are equivalent: a sufficiently small perturbation of a witness
w
makes all non-matching sums strictly negative without changing the signs on matching edges.

We recall alternating walks and the characterization of positive matchings via alternating closed walks,
and its bipartite specialization to alternating-cycle-free (uniquely restricted) matchings.
Let
V(M):=\{v\in V:v\text{ is incident to an edge of }M\}
.

Definition 2.2

(Alternating walks and cycles)
.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a graph and
M\subseteq E
a matching.
An
M
-alternating walk
is a walk whose edges alternate between
M
and

E\setminus M
.
An
M
-alternating closed walk
is an alternating walk that starts and ends at
the same vertex.
An
M
-alternating cycle
is an alternating walk that is a (simple) cycle.

Theorem 2.3

(Characterization of positive matchings
[
18
,
24
]
)
.

A matching
M
in
\Gamma
is positive if and only if the induced subgraph
\Gamma[V(M)]

contains no
M
-alternating closed walk. In bipartite graphs this is equivalent to the
absence of an
M
-alternating cycle, and such matchings are also known as

uniquely restricted
.

2.3
Monotonicity and violations

A function
f:V\to\mathbb{R}
is
monotone
(order-preserving) if

f(u)\leq f(v)
for every
(u,v)\in E(\mathrm{TC}(G))
.
Define the distance to monotonicity

d_{\mathrm{mon}}(f):=\min_{g:V\to\mathbb{R}\ \text{monotone}}|\{v\in V:\ f(v)\neq g(v)\}|.

For
\varepsilon>0
, we say that
f
is
\varepsilon
-far
from monotonicity if

d_{\mathrm{mon}}(f)>\varepsilon n
.

A
violating pair
for
f
is an edge
(u,v)\in E(\mathrm{TC}(G))
with
f(u)>f(v)
; when

(u,v)\in E
we call it a
violating edge
.
The
violation graph

G_{f}
is the bipartite graph with left part
V_{L}
(a copy of
V
),
right part
V_{R}
(another copy of
V
), and an edge
\{u_{L},v_{R}\}
whenever
u\leadsto v
in
G

and
f(u)>f(v)
. We write
\nu(H)
for the maximum matching size of a graph
H
.
By the standard vertex-cover characterization (Kőnig’s theorem),
d_{\mathrm{mon}}(f)
equals
the minimum vertex cover size of
G_{f}
and hence
\nu(G_{f})

[
19
]
.

2.4
Testers and query complexity

We consider randomized algorithms with oracle access to
f:V\to\mathbb{R}
.
A tester queries
f
at selected vertices and, based on the query answers (and its internal randomness),
outputs
accept
or
reject
.
We require
completeness
and
soundness
: for every monotone
f
, the tester accepts with probability at
least
2/3
(and with probability
1
for one-sided testers), and for every
f
that is
\varepsilon
-far
from monotone, it rejects with probability at least
2/3
.
A tester is
non-adaptive
if its query set depends only on its internal randomness,
and
one-sided
if it always accepts every monotone
f
.
We work in the standard massively parameterized model: the input DAG
G
is given explicitly and only oracle
queries to
f
are counted. Hence any computation on
G
(including computing
\mathrm{TR}(G)
,

\mathrm{TC}(G)
,
\ell
, reachability tests, and sampling uniformly from
E
or
E(\mathrm{TC}(G))
) is free.

Non-Adaptive Two-Sided Lower Bounds

This section is organized as follows.
We first relate positive matchings to violation patterns in bipartite DAGs and define PMRS families
(
Sections

3.1
and
3.2
).
We then prove non-adaptive two-sided lower bounds from PMRS (
Section

3.3
),
construct explicit PMRS graphs (
Section

3.4
), and combine these ingredients to obtain

Finally, we discuss how the argument changes for bounded ordered ranges (

3.1
Positive matchings and violations

We recall the basic definitions and known characterizations of positive matchings in

Section

2
. Here we relate them to violation patterns in bipartite DAGs.

In the bipartite setting, let
U=(L,R;E)
be an undirected bipartite graph, and let

G:=P(U)
be the bipartite DAG obtained by orienting each edge from
L
to
R
.

Lemma 3.1

(Violations exactly on a matching)
.

Let
U=(L,R;E)
be bipartite and let
M\subseteq E
be a matching.
The following are equivalent:

1.

M
is a positive matching in
U
.

2.

There exists a function
f:L\cup R\to\mathbb{R}
such that for every edge

(\ell,r)\in E
,

f(\ell)>f(r)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(\ell,r)\in M.

In other words, the
violated edges
of
f
on the bipartite DAG
G

are
exactly
the matching edges
M
.

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2): Let
w
certify positivity of
M
, i.e.,

w(\ell)+w(r)>0
iff
(\ell,r)\in M
for edges
(\ell,r)\in E
.
Define

f(\ell)=w(\ell)\quad(\ell\in L),\qquad f(r)=-w(r)\quad(r\in R).

Then for
(\ell,r)\in E
,

f(\ell)-f(r)=w(\ell)+w(r),

so
f(\ell)>f(r)
iff
(\ell,r)\in M
.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(1): Given such
f
, define
w(\ell)=f(\ell)
for
\ell\in L

and
w(r)=-f(r)
for
r\in R
. Then
w(\ell)+w(r)=f(\ell)-f(r)
, hence the sign
pattern on
E
matches
M
.
∎

Lemma 3.2

(Submatchings preserve positivity)
.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a graph and let
M\subseteq E
be a positive matching in
\Gamma
.
Then every submatching
M^{\prime}\subseteq M
is also a positive matching in
\Gamma
.

Proof.

Let
M^{\prime}\subseteq M
and set
V^{\prime}:=V(M^{\prime})\subseteq V(M)
.
Suppose for contradiction that
M^{\prime}
is not positive.
By
Theorem

2.3
, the induced subgraph
\Gamma[V^{\prime}]

contains an
M^{\prime}
-alternating closed walk
W
.
Since
M
is a matching and
M^{\prime}\subseteq M
, no edge of
M\setminus M^{\prime}
has an endpoint in
V^{\prime}
,
and thus
M\cap E(\Gamma[V^{\prime}])=M^{\prime}
.
Therefore
W
is also an
M
-alternating closed walk contained in
\Gamma[V(M)]
,
contradicting the positivity of
M
(again by
Theorem

2.3
).
∎

3.2
A positive-matching analogue of RS

We now formalize PMRS families, a relaxation of RS graphs obtained by replacing induced matchings
with positive matchings. This notion underlies the lower-bound constructions in the sequel.

Definition 3.3

(PMRS: Positive-Matching RS family)
.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
.
A bipartite graph
U=(L,R;E)
with
|L|=|R|=n_{0}
is
(s,\varepsilon)
-PMRS

if there exist matchings
M_{1},\dots,M_{s}\subseteq E
such that:

1.

(Edge-disjoint)
M_{i}\cap M_{j}=\emptyset
for all
i\neq j
.

2.

(Linear size)
|M_{i}|\geq\varepsilon n_{0}
for all
i
.

3.

(Positive) Each
M_{i}
is a positive matching in
U
.

By
Theorem

2.3
, in bipartite graphs positivity is equivalent to being
alternating-cycle-free (a.k.a. uniquely restricted), so PMRS can be viewed as a
packing of large edge-disjoint uniquely restricted matchings.

If
U=(L,R;E)
is
(s,\varepsilon)
-PMRS with
|L|=|R|=n_{0}
, then

|E|\geq\sum_{i}|M_{i}|\geq\varepsilon n_{0}s,

hence
s\leq|E|/(\varepsilon n_{0})\leq n_{0}/\varepsilon
.
Our explicit construction combined with refinement attains

s=n_{0}^{1-o(1)}/\varepsilon
, approaching this ceiling up to subpolynomial factors.

3.3
Non-adaptive two-sided lower bounds from PMRS

We begin by proving a non-adaptive two-sided lower bound from PMRS: for any
(s,4\varepsilon)
-PMRS graph, any
non-adaptive
two-sided
tester requires
\Omega(\sqrt{s})
queries.
The proof builds a YES/NO distribution using a margin-normalized witness and a correlation gadget,
which we set up below.

3.3.1
Farness from a large violated matching

Lemma 3.4

(A violated matching forces
\varepsilon
-farness)
.

Let
G=P(U)
for some bipartite graph
U
, and let
f:V\to\mathbb{R}
be a function on
G
.
If the set of violated edges contains a matching
M
of size
|M|\geq\varepsilon|V|
,
then
f
is
\varepsilon
-far from monotone.

Proof.

Each violated edge
(\ell,r)\in M
requires changing
f(\ell)
or
f(r)
in any
monotone correction, since the inequality
f(\ell)\leq f(r)
depends only on these
two endpoints. Because
M
is a matching, all endpoints are distinct, so at least

|M|
vertices must be modified.
Hence the normalized distance is at least
|M|/|V|\geq\varepsilon
.
∎

3.3.2
Margin and a slack monotone base

Fix an index
i\in[s]
and let
M_{i}
be the corresponding matching in an
(s,4\varepsilon)
-PMRS graph

U=(L,R;E)
.
Let
w_{i}:L\cup R\to\mathbb{R}
be any weight function certifying that
M_{i}
is positive, i.e.,

w_{i}(\ell)+w_{i}(r)>0
for
(\ell,r)\in M_{i}
and
w_{i}(\ell)+w_{i}(r)\leq 0
for
(\ell,r)\notin M_{i}
.
Define

\delta_{i}:=\min_{e=(\ell,r)\in M_{i}}\bigl(w_{i}(\ell)+w_{i}(r)\bigr).

Since
M_{i}
is finite and each term is positive, we have
\delta_{i}>0
.
Define the normalized witness

\widetilde{w}_{i}(v):=w_{i}(v)/\delta_{i}-\tfrac{1}{4}\qquad\text{for all }v\in L\cup R.

Then for every edge
(\ell,r)\in E
,

\widetilde{w}_{i}(\ell)+\widetilde{w}_{i}(r)\geq+\tfrac{1}{2}\quad\text{if }(\ell,r)\in M_{i},\qquad\widetilde{w}_{i}(\ell)+\widetilde{w}_{i}(r)\leq-\tfrac{1}{2}\quad\text{if }(\ell,r)\notin M_{i}.

(1)

Define
f_{i}:L\cup R\to\mathbb{R}
by
f_{i}(\ell)=\widetilde{w}_{i}(\ell)
for
\ell\in L
and

f_{i}(r)=-\widetilde{w}_{i}(r)
for
r\in R
. Then, for every
(\ell,r)\in E
,

f_{i}(\ell)-f_{i}(r)=\widetilde{w}_{i}(\ell)+\widetilde{w}_{i}(r),

so by (
1
) we have
f_{i}(\ell)-f_{i}(r)\geq+\tfrac{1}{2}
on
M_{i}
and
\leq-\tfrac{1}{2}
on

E\setminus M_{i}
.

Let
L_{i}\subseteq L
and
R_{i}\subseteq R
be the sets of endpoints of
M_{i}
.
For each matching edge
e=(\ell,r)\in M_{i}
, define
\Delta_{e}:=f_{i}(\ell)-f_{i}(r)
, so by (
1
)
we have
\Delta_{e}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
.
We convert
f_{i}
into a
monotone
function with slack by shifting matching endpoints:

\displaystyle g_{i}(\ell)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}f_{i}(\ell)-\Delta_{e}/2,&\ell\in L_{i},\\
f_{i}(\ell),&\ell\notin L_{i},\end{cases}

(2)

\displaystyle g_{i}(r)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}f_{i}(r)+\Delta_{e}/2,&r\in R_{i},\\
f_{i}(r),&r\notin R_{i}.\end{cases}

Here, for
\ell\in L_{i}
(resp.
r\in R_{i}
),
e
denotes the unique edge of
M_{i}
incident to

\ell
(resp.
r
); this is well-defined since
M_{i}
is a matching.
Then for every edge
(\ell,r)\in E
we have

(\ell,r)\in M_{i}\implies g_{i}(\ell)=g_{i}(r),\qquad(\ell,r)\notin M_{i}\implies g_{i}(\ell)\leq g_{i}(r)-\tfrac{1}{2}.

(3)

Indeed, for non-matching edges we have
f_{i}(\ell)-f_{i}(r)\leq-\tfrac{1}{2}
by (
1
), and the
shifts in (
2
) only decrease the left endpoint (if any) and increase the right endpoint
(if any), so the difference can only decrease.
In particular,
g_{i}
is monotone on the bipartite DAG
G
, and every non-matching edge has
slack at least
1/2
.

3.3.3
YES/NO distributions

We now define two distributions over functions.
Fix the noise alphabet size
K:=4
and step size
h:=1/8
.
For each matching edge
e=(\ell,r)\in M_{i}
, sample
Z_{e}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\{0,1,2,3\})

independently, and define the noise value
\eta_{e}:=hZ_{e}\in\{0,h,2h,3h\}
.

YES distribution
\mathcal{D}_{+}
.

Sample
i\sim\mathrm{Unif}([s])
, build
g_{i}
as in (
2
), sample
\{Z_{e}\}_{e\in M_{i}}
,
and define
F_{i}^{+}:L\cup R\to\mathbb{R}
by

F_{i}^{+}(v)=g_{i}(v)\quad\text{for }v\notin L_{i}\cup R_{i},\qquad F_{i}^{+}(\ell)=g_{i}(\ell)+\eta_{e},\quad F_{i}^{+}(r)=g_{i}(r)+\eta_{e}\quad\text{for }e=(\ell,r)\in M_{i}.

NO distribution
\mathcal{D}_{-}
.

Sample
i
and
\{Z_{e}\}_{e\in M_{i}}
as above, and define
F_{i}^{-}
by

F_{i}^{-}(v)=g_{i}(v)\quad\text{for }v\notin L_{i}\cup R_{i},\qquad F_{i}^{-}(\ell)=g_{i}(\ell)+\eta_{e},\quad F_{i}^{-}(r)=g_{i}(r)+\eta^{\prime}_{e}\quad\text{for }e=(\ell,r)\in M_{i},

where
\eta^{\prime}_{e}:=h\cdot((Z_{e}-1)\bmod 4)
(i.e., we cyclically shift the noise on the right endpoint).

Note that in both distributions, the marginal noise on a single endpoint is the same:
it is uniform over
\{0,h,2h,3h\}
.
The only difference is the
correlation
between the two endpoints of the same matching edge.

3.3.4
Completeness, soundness, and indistinguishability

Lemma 3.5

(Completeness)
.

Every function in the support of
\mathcal{D}_{+}
is monotone on
G
.

Proof.

Fix
i
and consider any edge
(\ell,r)\in E
.

If
(\ell,r)\in M_{i}
, then
g_{i}(\ell)=g_{i}(r)
by (
3
) and the same noise
\eta_{e}

is added to both endpoints, so
F_{i}^{+}(\ell)=F_{i}^{+}(r)
.

If
(\ell,r)\notin M_{i}
, then
g_{i}(\ell)\leq g_{i}(r)-1/2
by (
3
).
The noise values lie in
[0,3h]
, hence

F_{i}^{+}(\ell)-F_{i}^{+}(r)\leq(g_{i}(\ell)-g_{i}(r))+3h\leq-\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{3}{8}=-\tfrac{1}{8}<0.

Thus
F_{i}^{+}(\ell)\leq F_{i}^{+}(r)
for every edge, i.e.,
F_{i}^{+}
is monotone.
∎

Lemma 3.6

(Many violations in the NO distribution)
.

Fix
i
and draw
F_{i}^{-}\sim\mathcal{D}_{-}
.
Then an edge
e=(\ell,r)\in M_{i}
is violated by
F_{i}^{-}
iff
Z_{e}\neq 0
.
Consequently, the violated edges contain a matching of size

X_{i}:=|\{e\in M_{i}:Z_{e}\neq 0\}|\sim\mathrm{Bin}(|M_{i}|,3/4).

In particular, if
X_{i}\geq 2\varepsilon n_{0}
, then
F_{i}^{-}
is
\varepsilon
-far from monotone
(by
Section

3.3.1
).
Moreover, if
|M_{i}|\geq 4\varepsilon n_{0}
(e.g., when
U
is
(s,4\varepsilon)
-PMRS),
then for all sufficiently large
n_{0}
,

\Pr[X_{i}\geq 2\varepsilon n_{0}]\ \geq\ 1-\exp(-\Omega(\varepsilon n_{0}))\ \geq\ 9/10.

Proof.

For
e=(\ell,r)\in M_{i}
, we have
g_{i}(\ell)=g_{i}(r)
, hence

F_{i}^{-}(\ell)-F_{i}^{-}(r)=\eta_{e}-\eta^{\prime}_{e}.

If
Z_{e}\in\{1,2,3\}
, then
\eta_{e}-\eta^{\prime}_{e}=h>0
so
e
is violated.
If
Z_{e}=0
, then
\eta_{e}-\eta^{\prime}_{e}=0-3h<0
so
e
is not violated.
Thus the violated edges are exactly
\{e\in M_{i}:Z_{e}\neq 0\}
, which is a submatching of
M_{i}
of
size
X_{i}
.

If
X_{i}\geq 2\varepsilon n_{0}
, then

X_{i}\geq\varepsilon|V|
because
|V|=2n_{0}
, and
Section

3.3.1
implies

\varepsilon
-farness.

Finally, if
|M_{i}|\geq 4\varepsilon n_{0}
, then

\mu:=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=(3/4)|M_{i}|\geq 3\varepsilon n_{0}
.
A Chernoff bound gives

\Pr[X_{i}<2\varepsilon n_{0}]=\Pr\!\left[X_{i}<\left(1-\tfrac{1}{3}\right)\mu\right]\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{(1/3)^{2}\mu}{2}\right)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{\varepsilon n_{0}}{6}\right),

which is at most
1/10
for all sufficiently large
n_{0}
.
∎

Lemma 3.7

(No queried matching edge
\Rightarrow
identical transcripts)
.

Fix
i\in[s]
and a non-adaptive query set
Q\subseteq L\cup R
.
If
Q
does not contain both endpoints of any edge in
M_{i}
, then the joint distribution of the
oracle answers on
Q
is identical under
F_{i}^{+}
and
F_{i}^{-}
.

Proof.

Under the assumption, each matching edge contributes to the transcript through
at most one

queried endpoint.
For any queried endpoint of a matching edge
e
, the observed noise is a uniform element of

\{0,h,2h,3h\}
both in
F_{i}^{+}
and in
F_{i}^{-}
(because
Z_{e}
is uniform and the map

z\mapsto(z-1)\bmod 4
is a permutation).
Across distinct edges, the noises are independent in both distributions.
Hence the induced joint distribution of all answers on
Q
is identical.
∎

Theorem 3.8

(Non-adaptive two-sided
\Omega(\sqrt{s})
lower bound)
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1/8]
and let
U=(L,R;E)
be any
(s,4\varepsilon)
-PMRS graph with
|L|=|R|=n_{0}
.
Set
G:=P(U)
.
Then any non-adaptive two-sided
\varepsilon
-tester for monotonicity on the bipartite DAG
G

with range
\mathbb{R}
must make
q=\Omega(\sqrt{s})
queries.

Proof.

By Yao’s minimax principle, it suffices to consider deterministic non-adaptive testers.
Fix such an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, and let
Q
be its query set with
|Q|=q
.
Define

E(Q):=E\cap(Q\cap L)\times(Q\cap R),

the set of edges whose
both
endpoints are queried.

Condition on the choice of
i\in[s]
.
If
E(Q)\cap M_{i}=\emptyset
, then
Q
contains no full matching edge from
M_{i}
, so by

Section

3.3.4
the transcript distribution is identical under
F_{i}^{+}
and
F_{i}^{-}
.
Therefore, for this fixed
Q
,

\mathrm{TV}\big(\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{D}_{+},Q),\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{D}_{-},Q)\big)\ \leq\ \Pr_{i\sim[s]}\big[E(Q)\cap M_{i}\neq\emptyset\big].

Since the matchings
M_{1},\dots,M_{s}
are edge-disjoint, each edge in
E(Q)
belongs to at most one
matching. Hence the above event can occur for at most
|E(Q)|
indices
i
, implying

\mathrm{TV}\big(\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{D}_{+},Q),\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{D}_{-},Q)\big)\ \leq\ \frac{|E(Q)|}{s}\ \leq\ \frac{|Q\cap L|\cdot|Q\cap R|}{s}\ \leq\ \frac{q^{2}}{4s}.

(4)

Now,
\mathcal{D}_{+}
is supported on monotone functions (
Section

3.3.4
),
so completeness implies

\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ accepts }f\sim\mathcal{D}_{+}]\geq 2/3.

On the other hand, by
Section

3.3.4
, with probability at least
9/10

(over
f\sim\mathcal{D}_{-}
) the sampled function is
\varepsilon
-far; thus soundness implies

\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ rejects }f\sim\mathcal{D}_{-}]\ \geq\ (9/10)\cdot(2/3)\ =\ 3/5.

Equivalently,
\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ accepts }f\sim\mathcal{D}_{-}]\leq 2/5
.

Therefore,
\mathcal{A}
distinguishes
\mathcal{D}_{+}
from
\mathcal{D}_{-}
with constant bias:

\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ accepts }\mathcal{D}_{+}]-\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ accepts }\mathcal{D}_{-}]\ \geq\ \frac{2}{3}-\frac{2}{5}\ =\ \frac{4}{15}.

Any such bias is upper bounded by the total variation distance in (
4
), hence

q^{2}/(4s)\geq 4/15
, which yields
q=\Omega(\sqrt{s})
.
∎

3.4
An explicit PMRS construction

Fix an integer
k\geq 2
and a parameter
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
Write
[N]=\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}
and denote vectors in
[N]^{k}
by bold letters.
Let
\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{2}
and
\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}x_{i}
.

3.4.1
Vertex sets

Let
L
and
R
be two
disjoint copies
of
[N]^{k}\times[N^{2}]
:

L=\{(\mathbf{x},z)_{L}:\mathbf{x}\in[N]^{k},\ z\in[N^{2}]\},\qquad R=\{(\mathbf{y},t)_{R}:\mathbf{y}\in[N]^{k},\ t\in[N^{2}]\}.

Then

n_{0}:=|L|=|R|=N^{k}\cdot N^{2}=N^{k+2}.

3.4.2
A family of shift vectors

Fix a constant
\alpha\in(0,1)
(to be chosen as a function of
\varepsilon
and
k
)
and set

P:=\lfloor\alpha N\rfloor,\qquad A:=\{0,1,\dots,P\}^{k}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\},\qquad s:=|A|=(P+1)^{k}-1.

3.4.3
Matchings and the underlying bipartite graph

For each
\mathbf{a}\in A
, define a set of edges
M_{\mathbf{a}}\subseteq L\times R
by

M_{\mathbf{a}}:=\Bigl\{\bigl((\mathbf{x},z)_{L},\ (\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{a},\ z+\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2})_{R}\bigr)\;:\;\mathbf{x}\in[N]^{k},\ z\in[N^{2}],\ \mathbf{x}+\mathbf{a}\in[N]^{k},\ z+\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}\in[N^{2}]\Bigr\}.

Let

E:=\bigcup_{\mathbf{a}\in A}M_{\mathbf{a}}\quad\text{and}\quad U:=(L,R;E).

Theorem 3.9

(Polynomial-size PMRS)
.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
and an integer
k\geq 2
.
There exists a choice of
\alpha=\alpha(\varepsilon,k)>0
such that for all sufficiently
large
N
(so that
P\geq 1
), the bipartite graph
U
above is an
(s,\varepsilon)
-PMRS
with
n_{0}=N^{k+2}
and

s=\Omega\!\bigl(n_{0}^{k/(k+2)}\bigr).

In particular, taking
k=2
yields
s=\Omega(\sqrt{n_{0}})
.

Proof.

We verify the three PMRS conditions.

(1) Each
M_{\mathbf{a}}
is a matching.

Fix
\mathbf{a}\in A
.
Each left vertex
(\mathbf{x},z)_{L}
is incident to
at most one
edge of
M_{\mathbf{a}}
,
namely to
(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{a},z+\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2})_{R}
if that vertex lies in
R
.
Conversely, each right vertex has at most one preimage under this translation map.
Hence
M_{\mathbf{a}}
is a matching.

(2) Edge-disjointness.

Every edge
e\in E
belongs to a unique
M_{\mathbf{b}}
: indeed, if

e=\bigl((\mathbf{x},z)_{L},(\mathbf{y},t)_{R}\bigr)\in E,

then necessarily
\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
for some
\mathbf{b}\in A
, and this
\mathbf{b}

is uniquely determined by the endpoints. Thus the matchings
\{M_{\mathbf{a}}\}_{\mathbf{a}\in A}

are pairwise edge-disjoint.

(3) Linear size.

For
\mathbf{a}\in A
,

|M_{\mathbf{a}}|=\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{k}(N-a_{i})\Bigr)\,(N^{2}-\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}).

Since
0\leq a_{i}\leq P\leq\alpha N
and
\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}\leq kP^{2}\leq k\alpha^{2}N^{2}
, we have

|M_{\mathbf{a}}|\geq((1-\alpha)N)^{k}\cdot(1-k\alpha^{2})N^{2}=n_{0}\,(1-\alpha)^{k}(1-k\alpha^{2}).

Choose
\alpha=\alpha(\varepsilon,k)>0
small enough so that

(1-\alpha)^{k}(1-k\alpha^{2})\ \geq\ \varepsilon,

which is possible because the left-hand side tends to
1
as
\alpha\to 0
.
Then
|M_{\mathbf{a}}|\geq\varepsilon n_{0}
for all
\mathbf{a}\in A
.

(4) Positivity of each
M_{\mathbf{a}}
.

Fix
\mathbf{a}\in A
. Define a weight function
w_{\mathbf{a}}:L\cup R\to\mathbb{R}
by

w_{\mathbf{a}}\bigl((\mathbf{x},z)_{L}\bigr):=z-2\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x},\qquad w_{\mathbf{a}}\bigl((\mathbf{y},t)_{R}\bigr):=-t+2\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{y}-\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}.

Consider any edge
e\in E
. By construction,
e
lies in a unique
M_{\mathbf{b}}
for some

\mathbf{b}\in A
, and has the form

e=\bigl((\mathbf{x},z)_{L},\ (\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b},z+\|\mathbf{b}\|^{2})_{R}\bigr).

A direct calculation gives

\displaystyle w_{\mathbf{a}}\bigl((\mathbf{x},z)_{L}\bigr)+w_{\mathbf{a}}\bigl((\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b},z+\|\mathbf{b}\|^{2})_{R}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl(z-2\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x}\bigr)+\bigl(-(z+\|\mathbf{b}\|^{2})+2\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b})-\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}-\|\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}\|^{2}.

Since
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}
have integer coordinates,
\|\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}\|^{2}=0
iff
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{a}
,
and otherwise
\|\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}\|^{2}\geq 1
. Hence

w_{\mathbf{a}}(u)+w_{\mathbf{a}}(v)>0\iff\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{a}\iff e\in M_{\mathbf{a}},

and for every edge
e\notin M_{\mathbf{a}}
we have
w_{\mathbf{a}}(u)+w_{\mathbf{a}}(v)\leq-\tfrac{1}{2}\leq 0
.
This is exactly the definition of
M_{\mathbf{a}}
being a positive matching in
U
.

(5) Counting matchings.

Finally,

s=|A|=(P+1)^{k}-1=\Theta(N^{k}).

Since
n_{0}=N^{k+2}
, this yields

s=\Theta\!\bigl(n_{0}^{k/(k+2)}\bigr),

as claimed.
∎

3.5
Main lower bound

We now combine the PMRS lower-bound template with the explicit construction.
As a first step, we state a refinement lemma that converts larger positive matchings
into many smaller ones while preserving positivity.

Lemma 3.10

(Refining a PMRS family to smaller
\varepsilon
)
.

Let
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1/2)
and let
U=(L,R;E)
be an
(s,\varepsilon_{0})
-PMRS graph with
|L|=|R|=n_{0}
.
Then for every
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}]
, the same graph contains an
(s^{\prime},\varepsilon)
-PMRS family with

s^{\prime}\;\geq\;\sum_{i=1}^{s}\Big\lfloor\frac{|M_{i}|}{\varepsilon n_{0}}\Big\rfloor\;\geq\;s\Big\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\varepsilon}\Big\rfloor.

Proof.

Let
M_{1},\dots,M_{s}
be the edge-disjoint positive matchings from
Section

3.2
.
Fix
i
and define

t_{i}:=\big\lfloor|M_{i}|/(\varepsilon n_{0})\big\rfloor.

Partition
M_{i}
arbitrarily into
t_{i}
pairwise edge-disjoint submatchings
M_{i,1},\dots,M_{i,t_{i}}
,
each of size exactly
\varepsilon n_{0}
(discarding a remainder of size
<\varepsilon n_{0}
).
Each
M_{i,j}
is a matching, and it is positive by
Section

3.1
.
Since the original
M_{i}
’s are edge-disjoint across different
i
, all
M_{i,j}
are edge-disjoint as well.
Moreover,
|M_{i}|\geq\varepsilon_{0}n_{0}
implies
t_{i}\geq\lfloor\varepsilon_{0}/\varepsilon\rfloor
.
Thus the collection
\{M_{i,j}\}
forms an
(s^{\prime},\varepsilon)
-PMRS family with
s^{\prime}=\sum_{i}t_{i}

and the stated lower bound on
s^{\prime}
.
∎

Corollary 3.11

(
\varepsilon
-dependent two-sided PMRS lower bound)
.

Let
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1/2)
and let
U
be an
(s,\varepsilon_{0})
-PMRS graph with
|L|=|R|=n_{0}
.
Set
G:=P(U)
.
Then for every
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}/4]
, every non-adaptive two-sided
\varepsilon
-tester for
monotonicity on
G
requires

q\;=\;\Omega\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{s\varepsilon_{0}}{\varepsilon}}\right)

queries.

Proof.

By
Section

3.5
with parameter
4\varepsilon
,
U
contains an
(s^{\prime},4\varepsilon)
-PMRS
family with
s^{\prime}\geq s\lfloor\varepsilon_{0}/(4\varepsilon)\rfloor
.
Applying
Theorem

3.8
to this family yields
q=\Omega(\sqrt{s^{\prime}})
,
which gives the claimed bound (absorbing the floor into constants).
∎

Theorem 3.12

(Near-
\sqrt{n/\varepsilon}
two-sided lower bounds)
.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1/8]
. For every
\delta>0
, there exist infinitely many
n
and bipartite DAGs on

n
vertices for which any non-adaptive two-sided
\varepsilon
-tester needs

q\;=\;\Omega\!\left(\frac{n^{1/2-\delta}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)

queries.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon_{0}:=1/2
. Choose an integer
k\geq 2
so that
1/(k+2)\leq\delta
.
By
Theorem

3.9
, for all sufficiently large
N
there exists an

(s_{0},\varepsilon_{0})
-PMRS graph with

n_{0}=N^{k+2}
and
s_{0}=\Omega\!\bigl(n_{0}^{k/(k+2)}\bigr)
.
Applying
Section

3.5
yields

q=\Omega\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{s_{0}\varepsilon_{0}}{\varepsilon}}\right)\;=\;\Omega\!\left(\frac{n_{0}^{k/(2k+4)}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)\;=\;\Omega\!\left(\frac{n_{0}^{1/2-1/(k+2)}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right).

Now let
n:=|V|=2n_{0}
. Since
n
and
n_{0}
differ only by a factor of
2
,
this is

q=\Omega\!\left(\frac{n^{1/2-1/(k+2)}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)=\Omega\!\left(\frac{n^{1/2-\delta}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right).

Because this construction exists for all sufficiently large
N
, it gives infinitely many values of
n
.
∎

3.6
Bounded ordered ranges and polylogarithmic ranges

So far we have worked over an unbounded totally ordered range (e.g.
\mathbb{R}
) for
notational convenience, and noted that the constructions can be scaled to
\mathbb{Z}
(and any
unbounded totally ordered range). In this subsection we briefly discuss what happens if the range is a

finite
totally ordered set, say
[B]=\{0,1,\dots,B-1\}
, and in particular when

B=\mathrm{polylog}(n)
.

The PMRS lower-bound template does not inherently require unboundedness (two-sided).

The non-adaptive two-sided lower bound derived from an
(s,\varepsilon_{0})
-PMRS decomposition
(
Theorems

3.8
and
3.5
) is information-theoretic: it constructs YES/NO
distributions by adding a constant-alphabet noise to a slack monotone base and argues that a
non-adaptive query set cannot distinguish the two distributions unless it queries both endpoints
of some matching edge. The proof uses only the
relative order
of function values and a
constant slack/margin separation; it does
not
rely on the range being unbounded beyond
the ability to realize the prescribed pattern of inequalities. Consequently, the same template
extends to
[B]
provided that the construction can be implemented with values lying in
[B]

(after an order-preserving relabeling and an affine scaling/translation if needed).

Formally, it suffices that the set of values used by the YES/NO distributions admits an
order-preserving injection into
[B]
. Since the construction in
Section

3.3
uses a constant
slack (at least
1/2
) and a constant noise alphabet (of size
4
), one may first scale to integers
and then shift so that all used values are contained in
\{0,1,\dots,B-1\}
, as long as the

dynamic range
required by the monotone base is at most
B
.

Dynamic-range requirement of the explicit PMRS construction.

In the explicit PMRS graph from
Section

3.4
(
Theorem

3.9
), the vertex sets are

L,R\cong[N]^{k}\times[N^{2}]
and thus

n_{0}:=|L|=|R|=N^{k+2}
(so the total number of vertices is

n=2n_{0}
). The positivity witness for a fixed
matching index
\mathbf{a}
is

w_{\mathbf{a}}((\mathbf{x},z)_{L})=z-2\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x},\qquad w_{\mathbf{a}}((\mathbf{y},t)_{R})=-t+2\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{y}-\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}.

Because
z,t\in[N^{2}]
, the witness (and hence the derived slack monotone base in

Section

3.3.2
) necessarily varies on a scale
\Theta(N^{2})
(up to factors depending mildly on

k
and the choice of
\alpha
). The subsequent normalization and constant-alphabet noise
addition preserve the required comparisons while changing the value span only by constant
factors. Therefore, to realize the same hard instance over a bounded range
[B]
, it is natural
to require

B\;\gtrsim\;c\,N^{2}

(5)

for a suitable absolute constant
c>0
(after scaling to integer values and shifting).

Specialization to
B=(\log n)^{C}
and the resulting lower bound.

Assume
B=(\log n)^{C}
for a fixed constant
C>0
, where
n
denotes the total number of vertices.
Since
n_{0}=\Theta(n)
, enforcing (
5
) by
taking

N\asymp\sqrt{B}=(\log n)^{C/2}

forces the parameter
k
(the “dimension” in
Theorem

3.9
) to grow with
n
via

n_{0}=N^{k+2}
, namely

k=\log_{N}n_{0}-2=\frac{\log n_{0}}{\log N}-2=\frac{2}{C}\cdot\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}-2\;=\;\Theta\!\Big(\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\Big).

To keep each matching
M_{\mathbf{a}}
of linear size (Condition (2) in
Theorem

3.9
), the
parameter
\alpha
must shrink with
k
; a natural choice is
\alpha=\Theta(1/k)
so that

(1-\alpha)^{k}
remains a constant (and
1-k\alpha^{2}=1-o(1)
). With
\alpha=\Theta(1/k)
,
we have
P=\lfloor\alpha N\rfloor=\Theta(N/k)
and hence the number of matchings is

s=(P+1)^{k}-1\approx(N/k)^{k}.

Plugging
N=(\log n)^{C/2}
and
k=\Theta(\log n/\log\log n)
into
\log s\approx k\log(N/k)

gives the asymptotic exponent

\log s=\Big(1-\frac{2}{C}-o(1)\Big)\log n,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad s=n^{\,1-\frac{2}{C}-o(1)}\quad\text{(for $C>2$)}.

(When
C=2
, one obtains
s=n^{o(1)}
; when
C<2
,
N/k\to 0
and the above parameter choice
does not yield a nontrivial PMRS packing.)

Finally, applying the
\varepsilon
-dependent PMRS lower bound (
Section

3.5
, with constant

\varepsilon_{0}
) to this
s
yields, for every fixed
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}/4
,

q\;=\;\Omega\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{s}{\varepsilon}}\right)\;=\;\Omega\!\left(\frac{n^{\,\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{C}-o(1)}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)\qquad\text{for }B=(\log n)^{C}\text{ with }C>2.

Thus, under the explicit construction of
Section

3.4
, a polylogarithmic bounded range of size

(\log n)^{C}
yields a polynomial non-adaptive two-sided lower bound precisely once
C>2
, with
exponent
\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{C}
(up to
o(1)
terms).

on

on every edge.

I\sim\mathrm{Unif}([s])

\delta:=\min_{v\in L\cup R}\deg(v),\qquad\Delta:=\max_{v\in L\cup R}\deg(v),\qquad m:=|E|.

Fix an external query budget
Q\geq 1
.
Set parameters
(\alpha,\lambda,\beta,\gamma,B)
(possibly depending on
n,Q
), where:

\alpha=\Theta(1),\qquad\lambda>0,\qquad\beta\geq 10,

and
\gamma=\gamma(Q)
,
B=B(Q,n)
are chosen later.
Crucially,
\alpha
is not scaled with
\Delta
.

Let

\mathcal{K}_{B}:=[-B,B]^{L\cup R}.

For each
i\in[s]
, define

\mu_{i}\equiv\mu_{i}^{(Q)}(df):=\frac{1}{Z_{i}}\exp(-H_{i}(f))\,\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{B}}(f)\,df,

with energy

\displaystyle H_{i}(f):=\

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{2}\sum_{v\in L\cup R}f(v)^{2}+\lambda\sum_{(\ell,r)\in E}(f(\ell)-f(r))^{2}

\displaystyle+\beta\sum_{(\ell,r)\in E\setminus M_{i}}(f(\ell)-f(r)+\gamma)_{+}^{2}+\beta\sum_{(\ell,r)\in M_{i}}(\gamma-(f(\ell)-f(r)))_{+}^{2}.

All probabilities and expectations below are under these truncated measures.

For
v\in L\cup R
, define

m_{v}:=\alpha+2\lambda\deg(v),\qquad m_{\min}:=\alpha+2\lambda\delta.

Lemma 4.2

(Single-site conditional curvature)
.

For every
i
,
v
, and boundary condition on
L\cup R\setminus\{v\}
, the one-site conditional

\mu_{i}(f(v)\in\cdot\mid f_{-v})
on an interval contained in
[-B,B]
is
m_{v}
-strongly log-concave.

Proof.

In the one-dimensional conditional potential, the base term contributes curvature
\alpha
.
Each smoothing term
\lambda(X-c)^{2}
from an incident edge contributes curvature
2\lambda
.
Hinge-square terms are convex and add nonnegative curvature.
The hard truncation only restricts the support interval and does not reduce strong convexity.
∎

Uniform control of boundary-margin failures.

A key step in adaptive spurious control is the following: when closing a fresh
nonmatching edge with exactly one previously revealed endpoint, the support interval of

d=f(\ell)-f(r)
must contain the target point
-\gamma/2
.
This can fail only if a previously revealed endpoint value lies extremely close to the hard
truncation boundary
\pm B
.
We show that such boundary-strip hits are
uniformly
unlikely under
any
transcript
prefix, thanks to a deterministic inward drift created by the hinge terms when
\beta>\lambda
.
Throughout this boundary-strip discussion, we impose the support condition
B\geq\gamma/2
.

Lemma 4.3

(Uniform inward drift near the bad boundary strips)
.

Fix
i\in[s]
, a vertex
v\in L\cup R
, and any boundary condition on
(L\cup R)\setminus\{v\}
.
Let
X:=f(v)
under the one-site conditional
\mu_{i}(\,\cdot\,\mid f_{-v})
, supported on an interval

J\subseteq[-B,B]
, with one-dimensional potential
h(\cdot)
so that the conditional density is
proportional to
\exp(-h(x))\mathbf{1}_{J}(x)
.

Assume
B\geq\gamma/2
and
\beta>\lambda
.
Then the following deterministic slope bounds hold (uniformly over the boundary condition):

1.

If
v\in L
, then for every
x\in J\cap[B-\gamma/2,B]
,

h^{\prime}(x)\ \geq\ (\deg(v)-1)\cdot(\beta-\lambda)\gamma\ -\ (3\beta+\lambda)\gamma.

2.

If
v\in R
, then for every
x\in J\cap[-B,-B+\gamma/2]
,

h^{\prime}(x)\ \leq\ -(\deg(v)-1)\cdot(\beta-\lambda)\gamma\ +\ (3\beta+\lambda)\gamma.

Proof.

Fix a vertex
v
and condition on all other vertex-values (and on
I=i
), so that the one-site conditional law of
X_{v}
has density proportional to
\exp(-h(x))
on
[-B,B]
for some convex differentiable function
h
(differentiable except at finitely-many hinge kinks, which is enough for the pointwise bounds below).
Write
d:=\deg(v)
and let
y_{u}:=f(u)\in[-B,B]
denote the conditioned neighbor-values.

By definition,
h
is the sum of: the vertex term
\frac{\alpha}{2}x^{2}
; and, for each incident edge, a smoothing term
\lambda(x-y_{u})^{2}
plus a hinge-square term of coefficient
\beta
whose form depends on whether the edge lies in
M_{i}
.
Since
M_{i}
is a matching, at most one incident edge of
v
lies in
M_{i}
.

Case 1:
v\in L
and
x\in[B-\gamma/2,B]
.

For every nonmatching incident edge
(v,u)\notin M_{i}
(with
u\in R
),
the edge-contribution to
h
equals

\lambda(x-y_{u})^{2}+\beta(x-y_{u}+\gamma)_{+}^{2},

hence its derivative is

2\lambda(x-y_{u})+2\beta(x-y_{u}+\gamma)_{+}.

On the strip
x\in[B-\gamma/2,B]
we have

x-y_{u}+\gamma\geq(B-\gamma/2)-B+\gamma=\gamma/2
, so the hinge is active and

(x-y_{u}+\gamma)_{+}=x-y_{u}+\gamma\geq\gamma/2
.
Also
x-y_{u}\geq(B-\gamma/2)-B=-\gamma/2
.
Therefore each nonmatching incident edge contributes at least

2\lambda(x-y_{u})+2\beta(x-y_{u}+\gamma)\ \geq\ 2\lambda(-\gamma/2)+2\beta(\gamma/2)\ =\ (\beta-\lambda)\gamma

to
h^{\prime}(x)
.

If
v
has a matching incident edge
(v,u_{0})\in M_{i}
, its edge-contribution is

\lambda(x-y_{u_{0}})^{2}+\beta\big(\gamma-(x-y_{u_{0}})\big)_{+}^{2}=\lambda(x-y_{u_{0}})^{2}+\beta(y_{u_{0}}-x+\gamma)_{+}^{2},

whose derivative is

2\lambda(x-y_{u_{0}})-2\beta(y_{u_{0}}-x+\gamma)_{+}.

On the same strip,
x-y_{u_{0}}\geq-\gamma/2
so
2\lambda(x-y_{u_{0}})\geq-\lambda\gamma
, and

y_{u_{0}}-x+\gamma\leq B-(B-\gamma/2)+\gamma=3\gamma/2
so

-2\beta(y_{u_{0}}-x+\gamma)_{+}\geq-3\beta\gamma
.
Thus the matching edge contributes at least
-(3\beta+\lambda)\gamma
.

Finally, the vertex term contributes
\alpha x\geq 0
on this upper strip since
x\geq B-\gamma/2\geq 0
under
B\geq\gamma/2
.
Summing the contributions, we obtain

h^{\prime}(x)\ \geq\ (\deg(v)-1)\cdot(\beta-\lambda)\gamma\ -\ (3\beta+\lambda)\gamma,

which is the first claimed bound.

Case 2:
v\in R
and
x\in[-B,-B+\gamma/2]
.

Now each nonmatching incident edge has the form
(u,v)\notin M_{i}
with
u\in L
, and contributes

\lambda(y_{u}-x)^{2}+\beta(y_{u}-x+\gamma)_{+}^{2}

to
h
. Its derivative is

2\lambda(x-y_{u})-2\beta(y_{u}-x+\gamma)_{+}.

On the strip
x\in[-B,-B+\gamma/2]
we have

y_{u}-x+\gamma\geq(-B)-(-B+\gamma/2)+\gamma=\gamma/2
, so the hinge is active and

(y_{u}-x+\gamma)_{+}\geq\gamma/2
.
Also
x-y_{u}\leq(-B+\gamma/2)-(-B)=\gamma/2
, so
2\lambda(x-y_{u})\leq\lambda\gamma
.
Hence each nonmatching incident edge contributes at most

2\lambda(x-y_{u})-2\beta(y_{u}-x+\gamma)\ \leq\ \lambda\gamma-\beta\gamma\ =\ -(\beta-\lambda)\gamma

to
h^{\prime}(x)
.

If
v
has a matching incident edge
(u_{0},v)\in M_{i}
, its edge-contribution is

\lambda(y_{u_{0}}-x)^{2}+\beta\big(\gamma-(y_{u_{0}}-x)\big)_{+}^{2}=\lambda(y_{u_{0}}-x)^{2}+\beta(\gamma-y_{u_{0}}+x)_{+}^{2},

whose derivative is

2\lambda(x-y_{u_{0}})+2\beta(\gamma-y_{u_{0}}+x)_{+}.

On our strip,
x-y_{u_{0}}\leq\gamma/2
so
2\lambda(x-y_{u_{0}})\leq\lambda\gamma
, and

\gamma-y_{u_{0}}+x\leq\gamma-(-B)+(-B+\gamma/2)=3\gamma/2
so the hinge term is at most
3\beta\gamma
.
Thus the matching edge contributes at most
(3\beta+\lambda)\gamma
.

Finally, the vertex term contributes
\alpha x\leq 0
on this lower strip since
x\leq-B+\gamma/2\leq 0
under
B\geq\gamma/2
, so it only helps the upper bound.
Summing, we obtain

h^{\prime}(x)\ \leq\ -(\deg(v)-1)\cdot(\beta-\lambda)\gamma\ +\ (3\beta+\lambda)\gamma,

which is the second claimed bound.
∎

Lemma 4.4

(Uniform boundary-strip probability bound)
.

Assume
B\geq\gamma/2
,
\beta\geq 2\lambda
,
\delta\geq 10
, and moreover
\alpha\leq(\lambda+\beta)\delta
.
Then there exist absolute constants
c_{\mathrm{bdry}},C_{\mathrm{bdry}}>0
such that for every
i\in[s]
,
every vertex
v
with
\deg(v)\geq\delta
, and every boundary condition on
(L\cup R)\setminus\{v\}
,

\Pr_{\mu_{i}(\cdot\mid f_{-v})}\bigl[X\in[B-\gamma/2,B]\bigr]\ \leq\ C_{\mathrm{bdry}}\cdot\frac{\lambda+\beta}{\beta-\lambda}\cdot\frac{B+\gamma}{\gamma}\cdot\exp\!\left(-c_{\mathrm{bdry}}\cdot\delta\cdot\frac{(\beta-\lambda)^{2}}{\lambda+\beta}\cdot\gamma^{2}\right)\qquad(v\in L),

and

\Pr_{\mu_{i}(\cdot\mid f_{-v})}\bigl[X\in[-B,-B+\gamma/2]\bigr]\ \leq\ C_{\mathrm{bdry}}\cdot\frac{\lambda+\beta}{\beta-\lambda}\cdot\frac{B+\gamma}{\gamma}\cdot\exp\!\left(-c_{\mathrm{bdry}}\cdot\delta\cdot\frac{(\beta-\lambda)^{2}}{\lambda+\beta}\cdot\gamma^{2}\right)\qquad(v\in R).

Proof.

We prove the first claim (for
v\in L
); the second is analogous by left/right symmetry.

Fix
v\in L
and fix any conditioning on
\{f(u):u\neq v\}
and on
I=i
, so that
X:=f(v)
has density proportional to

\exp(-h(x))
on
[-B,B]
, for a convex function
h
.
By
Section

4.1
,
h
is
m_{v}
-strongly convex, i.e.
h^{\prime\prime}\geq m_{v}
(in the sense of distributions).
Moreover, since each quadratic term contributes curvature
2\lambda
and each hinge-square term contributes curvature at most
2\beta
when active,
we also have a uniform
upper
curvature bound

h^{\prime\prime}\ \leq\ M_{v}\ :=\ \alpha+2(\lambda+\beta)\deg(v)\qquad\text{on }(-B,B).

(6)

Let
x^{\star}
be a minimizer of
h
on
[-B,B]
(so
h^{\prime}(x^{\star})=0
if
x^{\star}\in(-B,B)
).
Let
a:=B-\gamma/2
.

Step 1: a uniform slope at the strip.

By
Section

4.1
, for all
x\in[a,B]
,

h^{\prime}(x)\ \geq\ (\deg(v)-1)(\beta-\lambda)\gamma-(3\beta+\lambda)\gamma\ =:\ A.

Under
\deg(v)\geq\delta\geq 10
and
\beta\geq 2\lambda
, this satisfies
A>0
and in fact

A\geq c_{1}\,\deg(v)(\beta-\lambda)\gamma
for a universal constant
c_{1}>0
.

Step 2: mode separation.

Step 1 shows that
h^{\prime}(x)\geq A>0
for all
x\in[a,B]
.
Therefore any minimizer
x^{\star}
of
h
on
[-B,B]
lies in
[-B,a]
(in particular
x^{\star}\neq B
).
If
x^{\star}\in(-B,a]
, then
h^{\prime}(x^{\star})=0
, and since
h^{\prime}
is
M_{v}
-Lipschitz by (
6
),

A\ \leq\ h^{\prime}(a)-h^{\prime}(x^{\star})\ \leq\ M_{v}\,(a-x^{\star}),\qquad\text{hence}\qquad a-x^{\star}\ \geq\ A/M_{v}.

If instead
x^{\star}=-B
, then
a-x^{\star}=a+B=2B-\gamma/2
.
In this case the same bound still holds because

\frac{A}{M_{v}}\ \leq\ \frac{\deg(v)(\beta-\lambda)\gamma}{2(\lambda+\beta)\deg(v)}\ \leq\ \frac{\gamma}{2}\ \leq\ a+B

using
A\leq\deg(v)(\beta-\lambda)\gamma
,
M_{v}\geq 2(\lambda+\beta)\deg(v)
, and
B\geq\gamma/2
.
Thus in all cases,

a-x^{\star}\ \geq\ A/M_{v}.

(7)

Step 3: a potential gap at the strip.

Strong convexity (
h^{\prime\prime}\geq m_{v}
) implies
h(x)\geq h(x^{\star})+\tfrac{m_{v}}{2}(x-x^{\star})^{2}
for all
x
.
In particular, by (
7
),

h(a)-h(x^{\star})\ \geq\ \frac{m_{v}}{2}(a-x^{\star})^{2}\ \geq\ \frac{m_{v}A^{2}}{2M_{v}^{2}}.

(8)

Step 4: compare numerator and denominator.

For
x\in[a,B]
, convexity gives

h(x)\geq h(a)+h^{\prime}(a)(x-a)\geq h(a)+A(x-a)
, so

\int_{a}^{B}e^{-h(x)}\,dx\ \leq\ e^{-h(a)}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-At}\,dt\ =\ \frac{e^{-h(a)}}{A}.

(9)

For the denominator
Z:=\int_{-B}^{B}e^{-h(x)}dx
, we lower bound the normalizing constant by integrating over a small neighborhood of the minimizer.
Since
X\in[-B,B]
and all conditioned neighbor values lie in
[-B,B]
, every term in
h^{\prime}(x)
is uniformly bounded on
[-B,B]
:
the vertex term contributes at most
\alpha B
in magnitude, each quadratic edge term contributes at most
4\lambda B
,
and each hinge-squared term contributes at most
2\beta(2B+\gamma)
.
Therefore

|h^{\prime}(x)|\ \leq\ L_{v}\ :=\ \alpha B+\deg(v)\bigl(4\lambda B+2\beta(2B+\gamma)\bigr)\qquad\text{for all }x\in(-B,B),

(10)

and hence
h
is
L_{v}
-Lipschitz on
[-B,B]
.
Let
r:=1/(2L_{v})
and
I:=([x^{\star}-r,\,x^{\star}+r]\cap[-B,B])
.
Then
|I|\geq r
and
h(x)\leq h(x^{\star})+1/2
for all
x\in I
, so

Z\ \geq\ \int_{I}e^{-h(x)}\,dx\ \geq\ |I|\,e^{-h(x^{\star})-1/2}\ \geq\ \frac{1}{2L_{v}}\,e^{-h(x^{\star})-1/2}.

(11)

Combining (
9
)–(
11
) and using (
8
) gives

\Pr[X\in[a,B]\mid\text{conditioning}]\ \leq\ \frac{2L_{v}}{A}\,e^{1/2}\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{m_{v}A^{2}}{2M_{v}^{2}}\Bigr).

Step 5: keep an explicit prefactor.

Using
m_{v}=\alpha+2\lambda\deg(v)\geq 2\lambda\deg(v)
and, since
\deg(v)\geq\delta
and we assume
\alpha\leq(\lambda+\beta)\delta
, we have
M_{v}\leq\alpha+2(\lambda+\beta)\deg(v)\leq 3(\lambda+\beta)\deg(v)
,
together with
A\geq c_{1}\deg(v)(\beta-\lambda)\gamma
, we obtain

\frac{m_{v}A^{2}}{2M_{v}^{2}}\ \geq\ c_{2}\cdot\deg(v)\cdot\frac{(\beta-\lambda)^{2}}{\lambda+\beta}\cdot\gamma^{2}\ \geq\ c_{2}\cdot\delta\cdot\frac{(\beta-\lambda)^{2}}{\lambda+\beta}\cdot\gamma^{2},

for a universal constant
c_{2}>0
.

For the prefactor, recall
L_{v}=\alpha B+\deg(v)\bigl(4\lambda B+2\beta(2B+\gamma)\bigr)
from (
10
)
and
A\geq c_{1}\deg(v)(\beta-\lambda)\gamma
.
Since
\deg(v)\geq 1
and
\beta\geq 2\lambda
, we have

\frac{2L_{v}}{A}\ \leq\ C_{3}\cdot\frac{\lambda+\beta}{\beta-\lambda}\cdot\frac{B+\gamma}{\gamma}

for a universal constant
C_{3}>0
.
Combining this with the bound at the end of Step 4 yields the lemma with

C_{\mathrm{bdry}}:=e^{1/2}C_{3}
and (after possibly decreasing
c_{2}
by an absolute factor)
c_{\mathrm{bdry}}:=c_{2}
.
∎

Lemma 4.5

(Score expectation equals boundary leakage (1D integration-by-parts))
.

Let
X
have density
p(x)=Z^{-1}e^{-h(x)}\mathbf{1}_{[a,b]}(x)
where
h
is absolutely continuous on
(a,b)
.
Then

\mathbb{E}[h^{\prime}(X)]\ =\ \frac{e^{-h(b)}-e^{-h(a)}}{\int_{a}^{b}e^{-h(x)}\,dx}.

Proof.

Differentiate
e^{-h(x)}
and integrate over
[a,b]
:

\int_{a}^{b}h^{\prime}(x)e^{-h(x)}\,dx\;=\;-\int_{a}^{b}\frac{d}{dx}\bigl(e^{-h(x)}\bigr)\,dx\;=\;e^{-h(b)}-e^{-h(a)}.

Dividing by
Z=\int_{a}^{b}e^{-h(x)}\,dx
gives the claim.
∎

4.2
Edge-tail bounds and admissible parameter set

This subsection derives edge-tail bounds under the truncated model and states the parameter conditions needed for these bounds.

Probabilistic control of the external drift.

Fix an edge
e=(\ell,r)
and the difference coordinate
d=f(\ell)-f(r)
.
Informally, all constraints incident to
\ell
or
r
other than the designated edge
e

create an external “force” on
d
; we call this contribution the (external) drift.
In formulas, this drift is the term
R_{e,t}(d)
, and
R^{\prime}_{e,t}(d):=\frac{d}{dd}R_{e,t}(d)

is its directional effect on the
d
-coordinate.
A precise definition is given by the decomposition (
21
) in the proof of

Section

4.2
.
In our analysis, we prove a high-probability bound at the
two
points
d_{0}\in\{\pm\gamma/2\}

that appear in the edge-tail argument.
Fix a drift tolerance
u>0
and define

p_{\mathrm{drift}}(u)\;:=\;2\exp\!\left(-\,c_{\mathrm{drift}}\cdot\frac{m_{\min}\,u^{2}}{(\lambda+\beta)^{2}\,\Delta}\right),

(12)

where
c_{\mathrm{drift}}>0
is an absolute constant.

Standing structural hypothesis.

The next lemma (and only this lemma) uses an additional structural property of the scaffold graph:
we assume that the underlying undirected graph of
U
is
C_{4}
-free (has no
4
-cycles).
This hypothesis is satisfied by the explicit family constructed in
Section

4.7
below.
All subsequent uses of
p_{\mathrm{drift}}(u)
and the edge-tail bounds depend on this hypothesis.

Lemma 4.6

(External drift tail bound for
C_{4}
-free graphs)
.

Assume that the underlying undirected graph of
U
contains no
4
-cycles (equivalently:
U
is
C_{4}
-free).
Fix any time
t
and any edge
e=(\ell,r)
.
Then for each
d_{0}\in\{\pm\gamma/2\}
and every conditioning on
(I=i,T_{<t})
,

\Pr\bigl[\,|R^{\prime}_{e,t}(d_{0})-\mathbb{E}[R^{\prime}_{e,t}(d_{0})\mid\mathcal{F},f(\ell),f(r)]|>u\,\bigm|\,I=i,\;T_{<t}\bigr]\ \leq\ p_{\mathrm{drift}}(u).

Proof.

Fix
t,e=(\ell,r)
and
d_{0}\in\{\pm\gamma/2\}
.
Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the
\sigma
-field generated by
(I=i,T_{<t})
together with the values of all vertices

outside
the one-neighborhood of
\{\ell,r\}
.
Condition on
\mathcal{F}
throughout this proof.

Write

\Gamma:=\bigl(N(\ell)\cup N(r)\bigr)\setminus\{\ell,r\}.

Because the graph is bipartite, there are no edges within
N(\ell)\setminus\{r\}
and no edges within
N(r)\setminus\{\ell\}
.
Because
U
is
C_{4}
-free, there are also
no
edges between
N(\ell)\setminus\{r\}
and
N(r)\setminus\{\ell\}

(otherwise
\ell
–
r
together with such a cross-edge would form a
4
-cycle).
Hence, after conditioning on
\mathcal{F}
and on the endpoint values
(f(\ell),f(r))
,
the remaining random variables
\{f(v)\}_{v\in\Gamma}
are
independent
.

Next, evaluate the external drift
R^{\prime}_{e,t}(d)
at
d=d_{0}
.
By definition,
R_{e,t}(d)
collects the contributions to the one-dimensional potential on the difference coordinate
d

from all edges incident to
\ell
or
r

except
the designated edge
e
itself.
Each such incident edge involves exactly one neighbor
v\in\Gamma
together with one of the endpoints.
For the fixed discrete data
(i,t,e,d_{0})
(and the edge-type of the incident constraint),
the contribution of that incident edge to
R^{\prime}_{e,t}(d_{0})
can be written as a function of the
neighbor value alone
.
Hence there exist measurable functions
G_{v}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

R^{\prime}_{e,t}(d_{0})\;=\;\sum_{v\in\Gamma}G_{v}\!\bigl(f(v)\bigr).

We stress that, once the discrete data
(i,t,e,d_{0})
are fixed and we condition on the endpoint values
(f(\ell),f(r))
,
each incident-edge contribution becomes a deterministic function of the
single
neighbor spin
f(v)
.
(In the open-edge case this one-variable function may depend parametrically on the already revealed endpoint value,
since the probe location of the moving endpoint at
d=d_{0}
depends on it, but this dependence is frozen under the conditioning on
T_{<t}
.)
Conditioning on
(f(\ell),f(r))
is therefore used only to guarantee that
\{f(v)\}_{v\in\Gamma}
are independent.
Moreover, each
G_{v}
is
(\lambda+\beta)
-Lipschitz:
for quadratic terms
\lambda(f(u)-f(v))^{2}
the derivative contribution is linear with slope
\lambda
,
and for hinge-square terms
\beta(\,\cdot\,)_{+}^{2}
the derivative contribution has slope at most
\beta
.
Thus, for every
v\in\Gamma
,

\mathrm{Lip}(G_{v})\ \leq\ \lambda+\beta.

(13)

Now fix
v\in\Gamma
and consider the (conditional) one-dimensional law of
X_{v}:=f(v)
.
By
Section

4.1
, this one-site conditional distribution is
m_{v}
-strongly log-concave on
[-B,B]

with
m_{v}\geq m_{\min}
.
A standard consequence (e.g. 1D Bakry–Émery/Herbst) is that for any
L
-Lipschitz function
g
,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{\theta(g(X_{v})-\mathbb{E}g(X_{v}))}\bigr]\ \leq\ \exp\!\left(\frac{\theta^{2}L^{2}}{2m_{\min}}\right)\qquad\text{for all }\theta\in\mathbb{R}.

(14)

Applying (
14
) to
g=G_{v}
with
L=\lambda+\beta
gives that

G_{v}(X_{v})-\mathbb{E}G_{v}(X_{v})
is subgaussian with proxy
(\lambda+\beta)^{2}/m_{\min}
.

Since
\{X_{v}\}_{v\in\Gamma}
are independent conditional on
\mathcal{F}
and
(f(\ell),f(r))
,
we can sum the mgf bounds and obtain

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\Bigl(\theta\bigl(R^{\prime}_{e,t}(d_{0})-\mathbb{E}[R^{\prime}_{e,t}(d_{0})]\bigr)\Bigr)\,\Bigm|\,\mathcal{F},f(\ell),f(r)\right]\ \leq\ \exp\!\left(\frac{\theta^{2}(\lambda+\beta)^{2}|\Gamma|}{2m_{\min}}\right).

Finally,
|\Gamma|\leq 2\Delta
and
\frac{1}{m_{\min}}\leq\frac{\Delta}{m_{\min}}\cdot\frac{1}{\Delta}
, so by Chernoff,

\Pr\bigl[\,|R^{\prime}_{e,t}(d_{0})-\mathbb{E}R^{\prime}_{e,t}(d_{0})|>u\,\bigm|\,\mathcal{F},f(\ell),f(r)\bigr]\ \leq\ 2\exp\!\left(-\,c\cdot\frac{m_{\min}\,u^{2}}{(\lambda+\beta)^{2}\,\Delta}\right).

Absorbing constants and removing the extra conditioning (tower property) yields (
12
).
∎

Remark (centering vs. absolute drift).

Section

4.2
gives concentration only around the conditional mean.
To derive the one-sided bounds on
R^{\prime}_{e,t}(\pm\gamma/2)
needed in the edge-tail argument, we combine
Section

4.2
with the mean-bad events (
17
); see
Section

4.2
.

Admissible parameter conditions.

We impose the following support condition (strong enough to fix the sign on the boundary strips used below):

B\ \geq\ \gamma/2.

(15)

Given the drift tolerance
u
, we use the following separation condition:

\beta\gamma\ \geq\ \alpha\!\left(B+\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)+\lambda\gamma+u.

(16)

Mean and drift-bad events.

controls fluctuations about the conditional mean.
For the edge-tail argument we only need

an upper tail at

(used for matching edges) and a lower tail at

For

\displaystyle\mathsf{MeanBad}^{+}_{e,t}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\mathbb{E}\bigl[R^{\prime}_{e,t}(\gamma/2)\mid\mathcal{F},f(\ell),f(r)\bigr]>\frac{u}{2}\right\},

(17)

\displaystyle\mathsf{MeanBad}^{-}_{e,t}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\mathbb{E}\bigl[R^{\prime}_{e,t}(-\gamma/2)\mid\mathcal{F},f(\ell),f(r)\bigr]<-\frac{u}{2}\right\}.

For an edge

and round
t

be the event that the transcript prefix

T_{<t}
has revealed
at most one
of the endpoint values
f(\ell),f(r)
(equivalently, the edge is not

In all applications of the drift bounds below, we only invoke them for such “open” edges

(18)

Lemma 4.7

(From drift-tail to one-sided drift bounds)
.

Fix
u>0
. For any
i,t,e=(\ell,r)
,

\Pr\bigl(R^{\prime}_{e,t}(\gamma/2)>u\mid I=i,T_{<t},\ \mathsf{Open}_{e,t}\bigr)\;\leq\;p_{\mathrm{drift}}(u/2)+p_{\mathrm{mean}}(u),

and

\Pr\bigl(R^{\prime}_{e,t}(-\gamma/2)<-u\mid I=i,T_{<t},\ \mathsf{Open}_{e,t}\bigr)\;\leq\;p_{\mathrm{drift}}(u/2)+p_{\mathrm{mean}}(u).

Proof.

We prove the first inequality; the second is identical with signs reversed.
Condition on
\mathcal{F},f(\ell),f(r)
.
On the complement of
\mathsf{MeanBad}_{e,t}^{+}
we have

\mathbb{E}[R^{\prime}_{e,t}(\gamma/2)\mid\mathcal{F},f(\ell),f(r)]\leq u/2
.
Hence the event
\{R^{\prime}_{e,t}(\gamma/2)>u\}
implies

R^{\prime}_{e,t}(\gamma/2)-\mathbb{E}[R^{\prime}_{e,t}(\gamma/2)\mid\mathcal{F},f(\ell),f(r)]>u/2.

Define

\Xi_{e,t}:=R^{\prime}_{e,t}(\gamma/2)-\mathbb{E}[R^{\prime}_{e,t}(\gamma/2)\mid\mathcal{F},f(\ell),f(r)],\qquad\mathcal{C}:=\{I=i,\ T_{<t},\ \mathsf{Open}_{e,t}\}.

Taking probabilities and union bounding gives

\displaystyle\Pr\bigl(R^{\prime}_{e,t}(\gamma/2)>u\mid\mathcal{C}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\bigl(\mathsf{MeanBad}_{e,t}^{+}\mid\mathcal{C}\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\Pr\bigl(|\Xi_{e,t}|>u/2\mid\mathcal{C}\bigr).

The first term is bounded by
p_{\mathrm{mean}}(u)
by definition (
18
).
The second term is bounded by
p_{\mathrm{drift}}(u/2)
by definition (
12
) and

Section

4.2
.
∎

We now return to the edge-tail argument.
For the one-dimensional edge calculations below, define

(19)

the uniform lower bound on the curvature coming from the quadratic
(pinning and smoothing) part on a single directed edge.

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{match}}

\displaystyle:=\exp(-m_{\mathrm{edge}}\gamma^{2}/8)+p_{\mathrm{drift}}(u/2)+p_{\mathrm{mean}}(u),

(20)

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{spur}}

\displaystyle:=\exp(-m_{\mathrm{edge}}\gamma^{2}/8)+p_{\mathrm{drift}}(u/2)+p_{\mathrm{mean}}(u).

Remark.
We keep separate notation for

Lemma 4.8

(Edge-tail bounds)
.

Assume (
15
) and (
16
).
Fix any

.

1.

If
e=(\ell,r)\in M_{i}
, then

\Pr_{f\sim\mu_{i}}\bigl[f(\ell)\leq f(r)\bigr]\ \leq\ p_{\mathrm{match}}.

2.

If
e=(\ell,r)\notin M_{i}
and at time

exactly one endpoint value is revealed so that

\Pr_{f\sim\mu_{i}}\bigl[f(\ell)>f(r)\,\bigm|\,T_{<t}\bigr]\ \leq\ p_{\mathrm{spur}}.

Proof.

We prove both parts by reducing to a one-dimensional log-concave distribution on the difference coordinate
and then applying strong convexity plus a sign-of-derivative argument.
We condition on the drift being well-controlled at the relevant point
d_{0}\in\{\pm\gamma/2\}
.
Define

\mathsf{DriftGood}^{+}_{e,t}:=\Bigl\{\bigl|R^{\prime}_{e,t}(\gamma/2)-\mathbb{E}[R^{\prime}_{e,t}(\gamma/2)\mid\mathcal{F},f(\ell),f(r)]\bigr|\leq u/2\Bigr\}\cap\bigl(\mathsf{MeanBad}^{+}_{e,t}\bigr)^{c},

and

\mathsf{DriftGood}^{-}_{e,t}:=\Bigl\{\bigl|R^{\prime}_{e,t}(-\gamma/2)-\mathbb{E}[R^{\prime}_{e,t}(-\gamma/2)\mid\mathcal{F},f(\ell),f(r)]\bigr|\leq u/2\Bigr\}\cap\bigl(\mathsf{MeanBad}^{-}_{e,t}\bigr)^{c}.

On
\mathsf{DriftGood}^{+}_{e,t}
we have

, and on
\mathsf{DriftGood}^{-}_{e,t}

Moreover, by

4.2
,

\displaystyle\Pr\bigl((\mathsf{DriftGood}^{+}_{e,t})^{c}\mid I=i,T_{<t},\ \mathsf{Open}_{e,t}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq p_{\mathrm{drift}}(u/2)+p_{\mathrm{mean}}(u),

\displaystyle\Pr\bigl((\mathsf{DriftGood}^{-}_{e,t})^{c}\mid I=i,T_{<t},\ \mathsf{Open}_{e,t}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq p_{\mathrm{drift}}(u/2)+p_{\mathrm{mean}}(u).

In part (1) we may take

so the prefix transcript

is empty, and hence
\mathsf{Open}_{e,t}
holds automatically.
In part (2),
\mathsf{Open}_{e,t}
holds by the assumption that exactly one endpoint is revealed.

Part (1):

.

Let

.
Condition on all vertex values except
(f(\ell),f(r))
and change variables to

(21)

Here

is the quadratic part (pinning and smoothing) involving the endpoints and the edge

Let

If

On the event

where we used (

) in the last step.

Strong convexity then yields the left-tail bound

Part (2):

and

At time

exactly one endpoint is revealed, say

Then

by assumption.
As in part (1), conditioning on all other vertices yields a one-dimensional density

supported on

,
with

again

-strongly convex.
Now
\Psi_{e,i}(d)=(d+\gamma)_{+}^{2}
since

.
Let
d_{\star}
be a minimizer of
W_{e,t}
.
If
W^{\prime}_{e,t}(-\gamma/2)\geq 0
, then by convexity
d_{\star}\leq-\gamma/2
.
On the event

we have

W^{\prime}_{e,t}(-\gamma/2)\;\geq\;\underbrace{U^{\prime}_{e,t}(-\gamma/2)}_{\geq\ -\alpha(B+\gamma/2)-\lambda\gamma}\;+\;\underbrace{\beta\,\Psi^{\prime}_{e,i}(-\gamma/2)}_{=+\beta\gamma}\;+\;\underbrace{R^{\prime}_{e,t}(-\gamma/2)}_{\geq\ -u}\ \geq\ 0,

again by (

Hence

on

, and strong convexity gives

\Pr[d>0\mid\mathsf{DriftGood}^{-}_{e,t}]\ \leq\ \exp\!\left(-\frac{m_{\mathrm{edge}}\gamma^{2}}{8}\right).

Finally, since
(\mathsf{DriftGood}^{-}_{e,t})^{c}=\mathsf{MeanBad}^{-}_{e,t}\cup\mathsf{DriftBad}^{-}_{e,t}(u/2)
, a union bound gives

\displaystyle\Pr[d>0\mid T_{<t}]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr[d>0\mid\mathsf{DriftGood}^{-}_{e,t}]+\Pr[(\mathsf{DriftGood}^{-}_{e,t})^{c}\mid T_{<t}]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{m_{\mathrm{edge}}\gamma^{2}}{8}\right)+p_{\mathrm{drift}}(u/2)+p_{\mathrm{mean}}(u)

\displaystyle=p_{\mathrm{spur}}.

as claimed.
∎

4.4
Prefix-conditioned leakage via strong convexity (Green-function comparison)

This subsection bounds adaptive information leakage by comparing one-site marginals under different hidden indices via strong convexity.

For each
i
and site
v
, denote the one-site conditional kernel by

K_{v}^{(i)}(\cdot\mid x_{-v}).

We will
not
use Dobrushin uniqueness. Instead, we compare one-site
marginals under transcript prefixes using only the strong convexity from the quadratic part

\frac{\alpha}{2}\sum_{v}f(v)^{2}+\lambda\sum_{(\ell,r)\in E}(f(\ell)-f(r))^{2}
.

Let
\Gamma=(L\cup R,E)
be the underlying undirected graph and let
\mathbf{L}
be its
(combinatorial) Laplacian matrix:

\mathbf{L}_{v,v}=\deg(v)
,
\mathbf{L}_{v,u}=-1
if
\{v,u\}\in E
, and
0
otherwise.
For a partial transcript condition
\tau
fixing a set
S(\tau)\subseteq L\cup R
of coordinates,
let
V_{\tau}:=(L\cup R)\setminus S(\tau)
be the set of unfixed sites and let
\mathbf{L}_{\tau}

be the principal submatrix of
\mathbf{L}
indexed by
V_{\tau}
.
Define

\mathbf{M}_{\tau}:=\alpha I_{V_{\tau}}+2\lambda\mathbf{L}_{\tau},\qquad G_{\tau}:=\mathbf{M}_{\tau}^{-1}.

Row-sum bound for
G_{\tau}
.

The matrix
\mathbf{M}_{\tau}
has positive diagonal and nonpositive off-diagonal entries, and it is
strictly diagonally dominant because
\alpha>0
; hence it is a nonsingular
M
-matrix and

G_{\tau}
is entrywise nonnegative.
Moreover, writing
\mathbf{1}
for the all-ones vector on
V_{\tau}
, we have

\mathbf{L}_{\tau}\mathbf{1}\geq 0
entrywise (edges to fixed vertices contribute only to the diagonal),
and therefore

\mathbf{M}_{\tau}\mathbf{1}=\alpha\mathbf{1}+2\lambda\mathbf{L}_{\tau}\mathbf{1}\geq\alpha\mathbf{1}
.
Multiplying by the entrywise nonnegative
G_{\tau}
yields the coordinatewise bound

G_{\tau}\mathbf{1}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbf{1},\qquad\text{i.e.,}\qquad\sum_{u\in V_{\tau}}(G_{\tau})_{v,u}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\ \ \ (\forall v\in V_{\tau}).

(22)

For
i,j\in[s]
, define local KL discrepancies

b_{u}^{(i,j)}:=\sup_{x_{-u}\in[-B,B]^{V\setminus\{u\}}}\mathrm{KL}\!\big(K_{u}^{(i)}(\cdot\mid x_{-u})\,\|\,K_{u}^{(j)}(\cdot\mid x_{-u})\big).

Lemma 4.13

(Local one-site discrepancy)
.

For every
u
and
i,j
,

b_{u}^{(i,j)}\leq C_{b}\frac{\beta^{2}(B+\gamma)^{2}}{m_{u}},

for an absolute constant
C_{b}>0
.

Proof.

Fix
u,i,j,x_{-u}
.
Write the one-site conditionals as densities on
[-B,B]
:

p_{i}(x)\propto e^{-h_{i}(x)},\qquad p_{j}(x)\propto e^{-h_{j}(x)}.

Then

\partial_{x}\log p_{i}(x)-\partial_{x}\log p_{j}(x)=-(h_{i}^{\prime}(x)-h_{j}^{\prime}(x)).

The two one-site log-densities differ only in terms corresponding to incident edges whose
matching type changes from
i
to
j
; this is at most two terms.
For one such changed edge with neighbor value
y\in[-B,B]
, the hinge derivative contribution is of the form

\pm 2\beta(x-y+\gamma)_{+}
or
\pm 2\beta(\gamma-(x-y))_{+}
, so for
x\in[-B,B]
its magnitude is at most

2\beta(2B+\gamma)\leq C\beta(B+\gamma)
.
Summing over at most two changed edges gives

\sup_{x\in[-B,B]}\big|h_{i}^{\prime}(x)-h_{j}^{\prime}(x)\big|\leq C_{0}\beta(B+\gamma),

hence

\sup_{x\in[-B,B]}\big|\partial_{x}\log p_{i}(x)-\partial_{x}\log p_{j}(x)\big|\leq C_{0}\beta(B+\gamma).

Each one-site conditional is
m_{u}
-strongly log-concave (
Section

4.1
), so the
one-dimensional entropy estimate gives

\mathrm{KL}(p_{i}\|p_{j})\leq\frac{1}{2m_{u}}\,\sup_{x}\big|\partial_{x}\log p_{i}(x)-\partial_{x}\log p_{j}(x)\big|^{2}\leq C_{b}\frac{\beta^{2}(B+\gamma)^{2}}{m_{u}}.

Taking the supremum over
x_{-u}
yields the claim.
∎

To apply the Green-function/interpolation step below, we first work with smooth (
C^{2}
) approximations:
the original model has hinge kinks and a hard box truncation, so direct differentiation-based identities are not
available in full regularity. We then pass to the original model by letting the smoothing parameter tend to
0
.

Lemma 4.14

(Uniform stability of one-site KL under smooth approximation)
.

Let
B>0
and let
\Theta
be a compact parameter set.
For
k\in\{1,2\}
let
h^{(k)}:[-B,B]\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous and let
h_{\varepsilon}^{(k)}:[-B,B]\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous such that

h_{\varepsilon}^{(k)}\to h^{(k)}
uniformly on
[-B,B]\times\Theta
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.
Define the associated normalized densities on
[-B,B]
by

K_{\varepsilon}^{(k)}(x\mid\theta):=\frac{\exp(-h_{\varepsilon}^{(k)}(x,\theta))}{\int_{-B}^{B}\exp(-h_{\varepsilon}^{(k)}(y,\theta))\,dy},\qquad K^{(k)}(x\mid\theta):=\frac{\exp(-h^{(k)}(x,\theta))}{\int_{-B}^{B}\exp(-h^{(k)}(y,\theta))\,dy}.

Then

\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\Big|\operatorname{KL}(K_{\varepsilon}^{(1)}(\cdot\mid\theta)\,\|\,K_{\varepsilon}^{(2)}(\cdot\mid\theta))-\operatorname{KL}(K^{(1)}(\cdot\mid\theta)\,\|\,K^{(2)}(\cdot\mid\theta))\Big|\to 0\qquad(\varepsilon\downarrow 0),

and hence

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\operatorname{KL}(K_{\varepsilon}^{(1)}(\cdot\mid\theta)\,\|\,K_{\varepsilon}^{(2)}(\cdot\mid\theta))=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\operatorname{KL}(K^{(1)}(\cdot\mid\theta)\,\|\,K^{(2)}(\cdot\mid\theta)).

Proof.

Uniform convergence of
h_{\varepsilon}^{(k)}
implies uniform convergence of the unnormalized densities
\exp(-h_{\varepsilon}^{(k)})
and of the partition functions

Z_{\varepsilon}^{(k)}(\theta)=\int_{-B}^{B}\exp(-h_{\varepsilon}^{(k)}(y,\theta))dy
on the compact set
\Theta
.
Therefore
\log K_{\varepsilon}^{(k)}(x\mid\theta)\to\log K^{(k)}(x\mid\theta)
uniformly on
[-B,B]\times\Theta
,
and in particular the log-likelihood ratios
\log\frac{K_{\varepsilon}^{(1)}}{K_{\varepsilon}^{(2)}}
converge uniformly and are uniformly bounded in absolute value.
Since
K_{\varepsilon}^{(1)}(\cdot\mid\theta)
are probability densities, the KL integrands

K_{\varepsilon}^{(1)}\log\frac{K_{\varepsilon}^{(1)}}{K_{\varepsilon}^{(2)}}

converge uniformly and are dominated by an integrable constant, uniformly over
\theta
.
Dominated convergence yields the stated uniform convergence of KL.
∎

Lemma 4.15

(Comparison under partial conditioning)
.

For every partial transcript condition
\tau
(fixing any subset of coordinates),
every unqueried site
v\in V_{\tau}
, and every indices
i,j
,

\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mu_{i}}(f(v)\mid\tau)\,\middle\|\,\mathcal{L}_{\mu_{j}}(f(v)\mid\tau)\right)\leq\sum_{u\in V_{\tau}}(G_{\tau})_{v,u}\,b_{u}^{(i,j)}.

Proof.

Fix
i,j\in[s]
, a transcript
\tau
, and a site
v\in V_{\tau}
. Let
\nu_{k}:=\mu_{k}(\cdot\mid\tau)
be the conditional Gibbs measure on the remaining coordinates
V_{\tau}
, for
k\in\{i,j\}
. Its density has the form

\nu_{k}(dx)\ =\ \frac{1}{Z_{k}}\,\exp\!\big(-H_{k}^{\tau}(x)\big)\,\mathbf{1}\{x\in[-B,B]^{V_{\tau}}\}\,dx,

where
H_{k}^{\tau}
is the conditional Hamiltonian obtained from
H_{k}
by plugging in the fixed values from
\tau
.
Because every edge-term (quadratic smoothing and hinge-square) is convex in the spins, both
H_{i}^{\tau}
and
H_{j}^{\tau}
are convex, and moreover their Hessians (in the sense of distributions) satisfy the same uniform lower bound

\nabla^{2}H_{k}^{\tau}(x)\ \succeq\ \mathbf{M}_{\tau}\ :=\ \alpha I+2\lambda L_{\tau},\qquad k\in\{i,j\},

for all
x\in(-B,B)^{V_{\tau}}
. In particular,
\mathbf{G}_{\tau}=\mathbf{M}_{\tau}^{-1}
is well-defined.

Let
K_{u}^{(k)}(\cdot\mid x_{-u})
be the one-site conditional kernel at
u
under
\nu_{k}
.
Define

b_{u}^{(i,j)}\ :=\ \sup_{x_{-u}}\operatorname{KL}\!\left(K_{u}^{(i)}(\cdot\mid x_{-u})\,\big\|\,K_{u}^{(j)}(\cdot\mid x_{-u})\right).

The desired inequality

\operatorname{KL}\!\left(\nu_{i}(X_{v})\,\big\|\,\nu_{j}(X_{v})\right)\ \leq\ \sum_{u\in V_{\tau}}(\mathbf{G}_{\tau})_{v,u}\,b_{u}^{(i,j)}

is proved in the literature as an
entropy influence
/
Green–function comparison
inequality for uniformly convex Gibbs measures. Since this lemma is used as an analytic input for the leakage bound, we briefly justify it here and also explain why the hinge kinks and hard box truncation do not invalidate the argument.

Step 1 (smooth approximation of kinks and truncation).

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Replace each hinge-square term
t\mapsto(t)_{+}^{2}
by a convex
C^{2}
mollification
t\mapsto\psi_{\varepsilon}(t)
which agrees with
(t)_{+}^{2}
outside an
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of the kink at
t=0
.
Also replace the hard indicator
\mathbf{1}\{x\in[-B,B]\}
by adding a convex
C^{2}
barrier potential
\Psi_{\varepsilon}
that is
0
on
[-B,B]
and diverges to
+\infty
as
|x|\uparrow B
.
Let
H_{k,\varepsilon}^{\tau}
be the resulting
C^{2}
Hamiltonian on
\mathbb{R}^{V_{\tau}}
and let
\nu_{k,\varepsilon}
be the corresponding smooth Gibbs measure (without an indicator).
Because we only replace terms by
more
convex ones, we preserve the same uniform curvature lower bound

\nabla^{2}H_{k,\varepsilon}^{\tau}(x)\ \succeq\ \mathbf{M}_{\tau}\qquad(\forall x,\ k\in\{i,j\}).

Moreover
\nu_{k,\varepsilon}\Rightarrow\nu_{k}
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
(the barrier localizes to the box and the mollified hinges converge pointwise).
In particular, for every fixed boundary condition
x_{-u}
, the one-site conditional densities

K_{u,\varepsilon}^{(k)}(\cdot\mid x_{-u})
converge in
L^{1}([-B,B])
to
K_{u}^{(k)}(\cdot\mid x_{-u})
.
In Step 3 we record a uniformity statement that allows us to pass the supremum over
x_{-u}
.

Step 2 (Green–function comparison in the smooth uniformly convex case).

For the smooth measures
\nu_{k,\varepsilon}
the inequality

\operatorname{KL}\!\left(\nu_{i,\varepsilon}(X_{v})\,\big\|\,\nu_{j,\varepsilon}(X_{v})\right)\ \leq\ \sum_{u\in V_{\tau}}(\mathbf{G}_{\tau})_{v,u}\,b_{u,\varepsilon}^{(i,j)}

follows from the standard interpolation argument: interpolate
H_{t,\varepsilon}^{\tau}:=(1-t)H_{i,\varepsilon}^{\tau}+tH_{j,\varepsilon}^{\tau}
and differentiate the
v
-marginal likelihood ratio along this path.
The derivative can be expressed as a covariance between a
v
-measurable quantity and a
local
perturbation affecting only one site
u
.
Applying the Brascamp–Lieb covariance inequality (equivalently, the Helffer–Sjöstrand representation) under the uniform bound
\nabla^{2}H_{t,\varepsilon}^{\tau}\succeq\mathbf{M}_{\tau}
shows that the sensitivity of the
v
-marginal to such a perturbation at
u
is controlled by the Green function entry
(\mathbf{G}_{\tau})_{v,u}
.
Integrating over
t\in[0,1]
and using that the entropy cost of swapping the
u
-one-site kernels is at most
b_{u,\varepsilon}^{(i,j)}
yields the displayed bound.

Step 3 (remove the smoothing).

Finally, let
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.
The barrier potential
\Psi_{\varepsilon}
forces
\nu_{k,\varepsilon}
to concentrate on
[-B,B]^{V_{\tau}}
, while the mollified hinges converge pointwise to the original hinge-square terms on this compact domain.
In particular,
\nu_{k,\varepsilon}\Rightarrow\nu_{k}
weakly for
k\in\{i,j\}
, and the corresponding one-site conditional densities

K_{u,\varepsilon}^{(k)}(\cdot\mid x_{-u})
converge pointwise on
[-B,B]
to
K_{u}^{(k)}(\cdot\mid x_{-u})
for every fixed
x_{-u}
.

Moreover, because
u
interacts with only finitely many neighbors, the boundary condition
x_{-u}
enters each one-site potential only through the neighbor vector

\theta:=x_{\mathrm{bdry}_{u}}\in\Theta:=[-B,B]^{\deg(u)}
.
Our mollification
\psi_{\varepsilon}
and barrier
\Psi_{\varepsilon}
can be chosen so that, for each
k\in\{i,j\}
, the one-site conditional log-densities

h_{u,\varepsilon}^{(k)}(x\mid\theta)
converge
uniformly
to
h_{u}^{(k)}(x\mid\theta)
on
[-B,B]\times\Theta
.
Therefore
Section

4.4
applies and yields uniform convergence of the one-site KL cost as a function of
\theta
, which in particular justifies exchanging
\lim
and
\sup
:

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}b_{u,\varepsilon}^{(i,j)}\;=\;b_{u}^{(i,j)}.

Finally, KL is lower semicontinuous under weak convergence of the corresponding marginals, so passing to the limit in Step 2 gives the desired inequality for
\nu_{i},\nu_{j}
.
∎

Definition 4.16

(Per-query leakage scale)
.

\kappa_{Q}:=\frac{C_{b}}{\alpha}\cdot\frac{\beta^{2}(B+\gamma)^{2}}{m_{\min}}.

Lemma 4.17

(Leakage bound up to budget
Q
)
.

For every deterministic adaptive strategy making
q\leq Q
queries,

I(I;T)\leq q\,\kappa_{Q}.

Proof.

Fix
t\leq q
and condition on
T_{<t}=\tau
.
Let
v_{t}(\tau)
be the queried vertex and
P_{i}^{\tau}:=\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{Ans}_{t}\mid I=i,T_{<t}=\tau)
.
Then

I(I;\mathrm{Ans}_{t}\mid T_{<t}=\tau)\leq\max_{i,j}\mathrm{KL}(P_{i}^{\tau}\|P_{j}^{\tau}).

By
Section

4.4
and
Section

4.4
,

\mathrm{KL}(P_{i}^{\tau}\|P_{j}^{\tau})\leq\sum_{u\in V_{\tau}}(G_{\tau})_{v_{t}(\tau),u}\,b_{u}^{(i,j)}\leq\left(\max_{u\in V_{\tau}}b_{u}^{(i,j)}\right)\sum_{u\in V_{\tau}}(G_{\tau})_{v_{t}(\tau),u}.

Using
Section

4.4
,
m_{u}\geq m_{\min}
, and the row-sum bound (
22
), we get

\mathrm{KL}(P_{i}^{\tau}\|P_{j}^{\tau})\leq C_{b}\frac{\beta^{2}(B+\gamma)^{2}}{m_{\min}}\cdot\frac{1}{\alpha}=\kappa_{Q}.

So each summand in

I(I;T)=\sum_{t=1}^{q}I(I;\mathrm{Ans}_{t}\mid T_{<t})

is at most
\kappa_{Q}
, hence
I(I;T)\leq q\kappa_{Q}
.
∎

4.5
Unique decoding and standard Fano

This subsection turns witnessed violated edges into an index decoder and then applies Fano’s inequality to lower-bound the required information.

Because the matchings are edge-disjoint, every

e\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{s}M_{i}
has a unique index
J(e)\in[s]
with
e\in M_{J(e)}
.
Define

\mathsf{SpurQry}:=\{\exists\text{ queried violated edge in }E\setminus M_{I}\}.

Let

\eta_{\partial}:=\Pr[\mathsf{BdryQry}].

Definition 4.18

(Transcript decoder)
.

Given transcript
T
, define
\widehat{I}(T)
:

1.

if
T
contains at least one witnessed violated queried edge, take the first such edge
e
under a fixed
rule; if
e\in\bigcup_{i}M_{i}
, output
J(e)
, otherwise output
1
;

2.

if no witnessed violated queried edge exists, output
1
.

Lemma 4.19

(Worst-case to distributional rejection)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a (possibly randomized) one-sided
\varepsilon
-tester with worst-case
soundness
2/3
on
\varepsilon
-far inputs.
If a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over inputs satisfies

\Pr_{f\sim\mathcal{D}}[f\text{ is }\varepsilon\text{-far}]\geq 1-\delta
, then

\Pr_{f\sim\mathcal{D},\rho}[\mathcal{A}^{\rho}\ \mathrm{rejects}\ f]\geq(1-\delta)\cdot\frac{2}{3},

where
\rho
is the internal randomness of
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

Condition on the event
\{f\text{ is }\varepsilon\text{-far}\}
.
On this event, worst-case soundness gives rejection probability at least
2/3
.
Multiply by its probability at least
1-\delta
.
∎

Lemma 4.20

(Soundness implies decoding success)
.

For every deterministic adaptive one-sided tester,

\Pr[\widehat{I}(T)=I]\geq\Pr[\mathrm{reject}]-\Pr[\mathsf{SpurQry}].

Hence, if soundness is
2/3
on
\varepsilon
-far inputs (
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{0}/200
), and if

Section

4.3
and
Section

4.3
apply, then

\Pr[\widehat{I}(T)=I]\geq 0.65-\eta_{\partial}.

Proof.

On
\{\mathrm{reject}\}\setminus\mathsf{SpurQry}
, every witnessed violated queried edge belongs to
M_{I}
.
By one-sidedness (
Section

4
), rejection implies existence of a witnessed violated queried edge,
so the decoder outputs
I
.
Hence

\{\mathrm{reject}\}\setminus\mathsf{SpurQry}\subseteq\{\widehat{I}(T)=I\},

which gives the first inequality.
By
Section

4.3
, under the hard prior we have
\Pr[\varepsilon\text{-far}]\geq 0.99
.

Section

4.5
gives
\Pr[\mathrm{reject}]\geq 0.99\cdot(2/3)>0.66
.

Section

4.3
gives

\Pr[\mathsf{SpurQry}]\leq\eta_{\partial}+0.01
.
∎

Lemma 4.21

(Standard Fano (uniform prior))
.

Let
I\sim\mathrm{Unif}([s])
, transcript
T
, and decoder
\widehat{I}=\widehat{I}(T)
.
If
P_{e}:=\Pr[\widehat{I}\neq I]
, then

I(I;T)\geq(1-P_{e})\log s-1.

Proof.

Apply Fano’s inequality to the decoder
\widehat{I}(T)
for the uniform prior
I\sim\mathrm{Unif}([s])
.
With error probability
P_{e}=\Pr[\widehat{I}\neq I]
, this gives

H(I\mid T)\leq h_{2}(P_{e})+P_{e}\log(s-1)\leq 1+P_{e}\log s,

where
h_{2}(\cdot)
is the binary entropy function (and
h_{2}(P_{e})\leq 1
).
Since
H(I)=\log s
,

I(I;T)=H(I)-H(I\mid T)\geq(1-P_{e})\log s-1.

∎

4.6
Budgeted one-sided lower bound

We now combine the spurious-control and leakage bounds from the previous subsections
to obtain a fixed-budget barrier for adaptive one-sided testers.

Theorem 4.22

(Fixed-budget information barrier)
.

Fix
Q\geq 1
and parameters
(\alpha,\lambda,\beta,\gamma,B)
as above.
Assume the hypotheses needed for
Section

4.2
and
Section

4.4

(in particular (
15
) and (
16
)), and assume

Let
\mathcal{A}

In particular, with
\varepsilon:=\varepsilon_{0}/200

\mathcal{A}
is an

\varepsilon
-tester with soundness

on
\varepsilon
-far inputs, then

4.5

4.4
gives

.
∎

To apply the fixed-budget barrier

C_{4}
-free PMRS scaffolds from difference-free shifts.

Recall the PMRS construction in
Section

3.4
(“shift matchings”) which produces a family of positive matchings
indexed by a set
A
of integer vectors.
For this shift-matching construction, the resulting bipartite graph is
C_{4}
-free provided
A
is

difference-free
.

Lemma 4.23

(Difference-free shift sets yield
C_{4}
-free PMRS graphs)
.

Consider the “shift-matching” scaffold of
Section

3.4
built from a set of shifts
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}

and let
U
be the union graph of the matchings
\{M_{\mathbf{a}}\}_{\mathbf{a}\in A}
.
If
A
is difference-free in the sense that

(A-A)\cap A\ \subseteq\ \{0\},

then the underlying undirected graph of
U
has no
4
-cycles.
Moreover, each
M_{\mathbf{a}}
is a positive matching in
U
(as in
Section

3.4
).

Proof.

A
4
-cycle in the underlying undirected graph has the form

\ell{\mathrel{\to}_{\;\mathbf{b}\;}}r{\mathrel{\to}_{\;\mathbf{a}\;}}\ell^{\prime}{\mathrel{\to}_{\;\mathbf{b}\;}}r^{\prime}{\mathrel{\to}_{\;\mathbf{a}\;}}\ell,

where the labels
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in A
indicate which shift-matching each traversed edge belongs to.
(Traversing an edge backwards simply swaps endpoints but keeps the same shift label.)
Write

\ell=(x,z)_{L},\qquad\ell^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})_{L}.

By the definition of shift matchings, an edge of label
t
maps
(u,w)_{L}
to

(u+t,\;w+\|t\|_{2}^{2})_{R}
. Hence

r=(x+\mathbf{b},\;z+\|\mathbf{b}\|_{2}^{2})_{R}=(x^{\prime}+\mathbf{a},\;z^{\prime}+\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2})_{R},

and

r^{\prime}=(x+\mathbf{a},\;z+\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2})_{R}=(x^{\prime}+\mathbf{b},\;z^{\prime}+\|\mathbf{b}\|_{2}^{2})_{R}.

Comparing first coordinates gives

x^{\prime}-x=\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a},\qquad x^{\prime}-x=\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}.

Therefore
\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{b}
.
But then both edges incident to
\ell
in the cycle, namely
\ell r
and
\ell r^{\prime}
, belong to the
same matching
M_{\mathbf{a}}
, impossible because
M_{\mathbf{a}}
is a matching.
So no
4
-cycle can exist.

Positivity of each
M_{\mathbf{a}}
follows from the same weight witness as in
Section

3.4
:
for an edge from matching
M_{\mathbf{b}}
one has

w_{\mathbf{a}}(u)+w_{\mathbf{a}}(v)=\tfrac{1}{2}-\|\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}
,
which is positive iff
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{a}
.
∎

The explicit scaffold and trimming.

Lemma 4.24

(Balanced dense core of the

.

Fix

with

, and let

.
Let

, and let

.

Then there exist subsets

,

and a subset

with

|A^{\circ}|\geq\frac{c_{\delta}}{2-c_{\delta}}s

for the induced subgraph and

is

-free.

For every

.

3.

Each

has size at least
\varepsilon_{0}n_{0}
for an absolute constant

.

Degrees are bounded as

for all vertices

(thus

and

.

In particular, under the dense choice

, hence

s=\Theta(\sqrt{n_{0}})
and

.

Proof.

For each
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{2})\in A
, the matching

consists of all edges

(x,z)\mapsto(x+\mathbf{a},\;z+\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2})

and

|M_{\mathbf{a}}|=(N-a_{1})(N-a_{2})\,(N^{2}-\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2})\ \geq\ (N-P)^{2}\,(N^{2}-2P^{2}).

Let

. For

we have

.
Since the matchings are edge-disjoint, the union has

|E|=\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in A}|M_{\mathbf{a}}|\ \geq\ c_{0}\,s\,N^{4}.

Because

, the average degree on each side is at least

.

Now perform the standard degree-pruning procedure: starting from
U
, iteratively delete any vertex (on either side) whose current degree is less than
(c_{0}/4)s
,
together with all incident edges, until no such vertex remains.
Let the resulting subgraph be

.
By construction, every vertex in
U_{1}
has degree at least
(c_{0}/4)s
, and trivially every degree is at most
s
.
Each deletion step removes fewer than

Since each left vertex has degree at most
s
, we also have
|E_{1}|\leq s\,\min\{|L_{1}|,|R_{1}|\}
, and therefore

Moreover, because

has minimum degree at least
(c_{0}/4)s
and maximum degree at most
s
, the ratio of side sizes is bounded by

\frac{\max\{|L_{1}|,|R_{1}|\}}{\min\{|L_{1}|,|R_{1}|\}}\ \leq\ \frac{s}{(c_{0}/4)s}=\frac{4}{c_{0}}.

To balance the sides, let
n_{0}:=n_{1}
and (wlog) assume
|L_{1}|\geq|R_{1}|=n_{0}
.
Choose a subset
L^{\circ}\subseteq L_{1}
of size
n_{0}
uniformly at random, and set

.
For any fixed

is hypergeometric with mean

\mathbb{E}[\deg_{U^{\circ}}(r)]\geq\deg_{U_{1}}(r)\cdot\frac{|L^{\circ}|}{|L_{1}|}\geq\frac{c_{0}}{4}s\cdot\frac{n_{0}}{|L_{1}|}\geq\frac{c_{0}}{4}s\cdot\frac{1}{4/c_{0}}=\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{16}s.

A standard hypergeometric Chernoff bound implies that

\Pr\!\left[\deg_{U^{\circ}}(r)<\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{32}s\right]\leq\exp(-\Omega(s)).

Taking a union bound over all
r\in R^{\circ}
shows that with positive probability every right vertex has degree at least
(c_{0}^{2}/32)s
in
U^{\circ}
.
Left degrees are unchanged when we subsample
L_{1}
(we did not delete any right vertices), so every left vertex in
L^{\circ}
still has degree at least
(c_{0}/4)s
.
Thus, in the balanced graph
U^{\circ}
we have

\deg_{U^{\circ}}(v)\ \geq\ c_{\delta}s\qquad\text{with}\qquad c_{\delta}:=\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{32},

and also
\deg_{U^{\circ}}(v)\leq s
, i.e.,
\Delta\leq s
.

Since
A
is difference-free, the original union graph is
C_{4}
-free by
Section

4.7
, and this property is preserved under taking subgraphs, giving (1).
Positivity of each
M_{\mathbf{a}}
in
U
comes from
Theorem

3.9
, and it is preserved under restricting to a subgraph, giving (2).

Finally, the restricted matchings
\{M_{\mathbf{a}}^{\circ}\}_{\mathbf{a}\in A}
remain edge-disjoint and satisfy

\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in A}|M_{\mathbf{a}}^{\circ}|=|E^{\circ}|\geq|L^{\circ}|\cdot\min_{v\in L^{\circ}}\deg_{U^{\circ}}(v)\geq c_{\delta}sn_{0}.

Hence the average of
|M_{\mathbf{a}}^{\circ}|
over
\mathbf{a}\in A
is at least
c_{\delta}n_{0}
.
Let
A^{\circ}\subseteq A
be the set of shifts with
|M_{\mathbf{a}}^{\circ}|\geq(c_{\delta}/2)n_{0}
.
Since always
|M_{\mathbf{a}}^{\circ}|\leq n_{0}
, the averaging bound implies a
constant
fraction of shifts are dense.
Indeed, writing
p:=|A^{\circ}|/s
and using
|M_{\mathbf{a}}^{\circ}|\leq n_{0}
for
\mathbf{a}\in A^{\circ}
and

|M_{\mathbf{a}}^{\circ}|<(c_{\delta}/2)n_{0}
for
\mathbf{a}\notin A^{\circ}
, we have

\frac{1}{s}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in A}|M_{\mathbf{a}}^{\circ}|\leq p\cdot n_{0}+(1-p)\cdot(c_{\delta}/2)n_{0}.

Since the left-hand side is at least
c_{\delta}n_{0}
, rearranging gives

p\geq\frac{c_{\delta}}{2-c_{\delta}}.

Thus
|A^{\circ}|\geq\frac{c_{\delta}}{2-c_{\delta}}\,s
, and each
M_{\mathbf{a}}^{\circ}
has size at least
\varepsilon_{0}n_{0}

with
\varepsilon_{0}:=c_{\delta}/2
.
This proves (3)–(4) and completes the proof.
∎

In what follows, we fix the dense choice
P=\lfloor N/10\rfloor
and work with a balanced core
U^{\circ}
as in
Section

4.7
.
We write
n_{0}:=|L^{\circ}|=|R^{\circ}|
,
n:=|V(U^{\circ})|=2n_{0}
,
s:=|A^{\circ}|
, and let
\delta,\Delta
denote the minimum/maximum degrees of
U^{\circ}
;
then
\delta=\Theta(s)
and
\Delta\leq s
.

Mean control in the dense regime.

The remaining quantitative hypothesis is the smallness of the mean-bad probability

p_{\mathrm{mean}}(u)
from (
18
).
For the dense core from
Section

4.7
we have
\delta=\Theta(s)
and
\Delta\leq s
, hence

m_{\min}=\alpha+2\lambda\delta=\Theta(\Delta)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\Delta}{m_{\min}}=\Theta(1).

Intuitively, the external-drift conditional mean is a smooth (indeed Lipschitz) function of the boundary data, and
in the dense regime it concentrates at a constant scale.
The next lemma makes this quantitative.

Lemma 4.25

(Mean functional concentration in the dense regime)
.

Fix
i,t,e=(\ell,r)
and
d_{0}\in\{\pm\gamma/2\}
, and work in the open-edge regime
\mathsf{Open}_{e,t}
.
Define

M_{e,t}(d_{0}):=\operatorname{E}\!\bigl[R^{\prime}_{e,t}(d_{0})\mid\mathcal{F},f(\ell),f(r)\bigr],\qquad M:=M_{e,t}(d_{0}).

Let
\mathcal{Y}_{e,t}
denote the collection of real-valued coordinates among
(\mathcal{F},f(\ell),f(r))
that remain random after conditioning on
I=i,T_{<t},\mathsf{Open}_{e,t}
; equivalently,

\mathcal{Y}_{e,t}=\mathcal{F}\cup\{f(u):u\in\{\ell,r\}\ \text{and $f(u)$ is \emph{not} revealed by }T_{<t}\}.

Recall
m_{\min}:=\alpha+2\lambda\delta
. Under the conditional law given
I=i,T_{<t},\mathsf{Open}_{e,t}
:

1.

M
is
L_{\mathrm{mean}}
–Lipschitz as a function of the random coordinates
\mathcal{Y}_{e,t}
, with

L_{\mathrm{mean}}\leq C_{0}(\lambda+\beta)^{2}\frac{\Delta}{m_{\min}}.

2.

Consequently, for all
u>0
,

\Pr\!\left(\left.|M-\operatorname{E}[M]|\geq u/4\right|\,I=i,T_{<t},\ \mathsf{Open}_{e,t}\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{\alpha u^{2}}{32L_{\mathrm{mean}}^{2}}\right).

In particular, if
u\geq 4|\operatorname{E}[M]|
then

\Pr\!\left(\left.|M|\geq u/2\right|\,I=i,T_{<t},\ \mathsf{Open}_{e,t}\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{\alpha u^{2}}{32L_{\mathrm{mean}}^{2}}\right).

Proof.

We keep the notation
\Gamma=N(\ell)\cup N(r)\setminus\{\ell,r\}
. As in
Section

4.2
,

R^{\prime}_{e,t}(d_{0})=\sum_{v\in\Gamma}G_{v}\bigl(f(v)\bigr),\qquad\operatorname{Lip}(G_{v})\leq C(\lambda+\beta),

where each
G_{v}
is a deterministic one-variable function of
f(v)
once the discrete data
(i,t,e,d_{0})
are fixed and we condition on
(f(\ell),f(r))
.
In the matching-edge case,
G_{v}
is in fact independent of
(f(\ell),f(r))
due to the midpoint integration in
Section

4.2
.
In the open-edge case,
G_{v}
may depend parametrically on the already revealed endpoint value, but it is independent of every boundary coordinate that remains random under the conditioning (in particular, it does not depend on the
unrevealed
endpoint variable).
Thus, conditioning on
(\mathcal{F},f(\ell),f(r))
, the random variables
\{f(v)\}_{v\in\Gamma}
are independent and only their one-site laws vary with the boundary.
For each
v\in\Gamma
, let
\nu_{v}
be the one-site conditional law of
f(v)
given
(\mathcal{F},f(\ell),f(r))
, with potential
h_{v}
; it is
m_{v}
-strongly log-concave for some
m_{v}\geq m_{\min}
.

Step 1 (one-site sensitivity).

Fix
v\in\Gamma
and a boundary coordinate
y
that belongs to
\mathcal{Y}_{e,t}
and influences the one-site law
\nu_{v}
; in particular,
y\in\mathrm{bdry}_{v}
or
y\in\{f(\ell),f(r)\}
is an
unrevealed
endpoint variable. Write

m_{v}(\mathrm{bdry}_{v}):=\operatorname{E}_{\nu_{v}}\bigl[G_{v}(X)\bigr],\qquad X\sim\nu_{v}.

Differentiating under the integral sign gives

\frac{\partial}{\partial y}m_{v}(\mathrm{bdry}_{v})=\operatorname{E}_{\nu_{v}}\!\bigl[\partial_{y}G_{v}(X)\bigr]-\operatorname{Cov}_{\nu_{v}}\!\bigl(G_{v}(X),\,\partial_{y}h_{v}(X)\bigr).

By the discussion above, for every boundary coordinate
y
that remains random under the conditioning (namely
y\in\mathrm{bdry}_{v}
or
y
is an
unrevealed
endpoint variable),
G_{v}
has no dependence on
y
. Hence
\partial_{y}G_{v}\equiv 0
and therefore

\frac{\partial}{\partial y}m_{v}(\mathrm{bdry}_{v})=-\operatorname{Cov}_{\nu_{v}}\!\bigl(G_{v}(X),\,\partial_{y}h_{v}(X)\bigr).

Moreover
\operatorname{Lip}(\partial_{y}h_{v})\leq C(\lambda+\beta)
. By the Brascamp–Lieb covariance bound for an
m_{v}
-strongly log-concave measure,

\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial y}m_{v}(\mathrm{bdry}_{v})\right|\leq\frac{C(\lambda+\beta)^{2}}{m_{v}}\leq\frac{C(\lambda+\beta)^{2}}{m_{\min}}.

Since
|\mathrm{bdry}_{v}|\leq\Delta
and there are at most two distinguished boundary coordinates (the endpoints), this implies

\|\nabla_{\mathrm{bdry}_{v}}m_{v}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\Delta}\,\frac{C(\lambda+\beta)^{2}}{m_{\min}},\qquad\text{and for each }y\in\{f(\ell),f(r)\}\cap\mathcal{Y}_{e,t},\ \left|\frac{\partial}{\partial y}m_{v}\right|\leq\frac{C(\lambda+\beta)^{2}}{m_{\min}}.

Step 2 (global Lipschitzness of
M
).

Summing the above over
v\in\Gamma
and using that the sets
\{\mathrm{bdry}_{v}\}_{v\in\Gamma}
are disjoint in a
C_{4}
-free graph, we obtain

\|\nabla_{\mathcal{F}}M\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{v\in\Gamma}\|\nabla_{\mathrm{bdry}_{v}}m_{v}\|_{2}^{2}}\leq\sqrt{|\Gamma|\Delta}\,\frac{C(\lambda+\beta)^{2}}{m_{\min}}\leq C(\lambda+\beta)^{2}\frac{\Delta}{m_{\min}}.

Likewise, if an endpoint coordinate
y\in\{f(\ell),f(r)\}
is
not
revealed by
T_{<t}
(equivalently,
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{e,t}
),
then Step 1 gives
|\partial_{y}m_{v}|\leq C(\lambda+\beta)^{2}/m_{\min}
and hence

\left|\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\right|\leq\sum_{v\in\Gamma}\left|\frac{\partial m_{v}}{\partial y}\right|\leq|\Gamma|\frac{C(\lambda+\beta)^{2}}{m_{\min}}\leq C(\lambda+\beta)^{2}\frac{\Delta}{m_{\min}}.

Since
|\{f(\ell),f(r)\}\cap\mathcal{Y}_{e,t}|\leq 2
, combining this with the bound on
\|\nabla_{\mathcal{F}}M\|_{2}
yields

\|\nabla_{\mathcal{Y}_{e,t}}M\|_{2}\leq C_{0}(\lambda+\beta)^{2}\frac{\Delta}{m_{\min}}.

Thus
M
is
L_{\mathrm{mean}}
-Lipschitz as a function of
\mathcal{Y}_{e,t}
with the stated bound, proving (i).

Step 3 (concentration).

Conditioning on
I=i,T_{<t},\mathsf{Open}_{e,t}
fixes some coordinates but preserves
\alpha
-strong log-concavity of the remaining density (the pinning term contributes
\alpha I
on the free coordinates).
Standard Gaussian concentration for
\alpha
-strongly log-concave measures (Bakry–Émery/Herbst) therefore implies that any
L
-Lipschitz function
F
of the remaining coordinates satisfies

\Pr\!\left(\left.|F-\operatorname{E}[F]|\geq t\right|I=i,T_{<t},\ \mathsf{Open}_{e,t}\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{\alpha t^{2}}{2L^{2}}\right).

Applying this with
F=M
(viewed as a function of
\mathcal{Y}_{e,t}
) and
t=u/4
gives (ii). The final display follows by the triangle inequality.
∎

Lemma 4.26

(Mean control closes automatically in the dense almost-regular regime)
.

Consider the parameter choice in
Theorem

4.27
(in particular
\alpha=\lambda=1
,
\beta=10
,
B=4\gamma
,
u=\beta\gamma/10
),
and assume the scaffold
G
is
C_{4}
-free with degrees satisfying
\delta\leq\deg(v)\leq\Delta
for all
v
, where
\Delta/\delta=O(1)
and
\Delta=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
.
Then, for all sufficiently large
n
(equivalently, all sufficiently large
Q=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
), the mean-control quantity
from (
18
) satisfies

p_{\mathrm{mean}}(u)\leq\frac{1}{400Q^{2}}

provided the constant
C_{\gamma}
in the definition of
\gamma
is chosen large enough.

Proof.

Fix any
i,t,e
and set

\mathcal{C}:=\{I=i,\ T_{<t},\ \mathsf{Open}_{e,t}\}.

By
Section

4.7
(ii), for each
d_{0}\in\{\pm\gamma/2\}
,

\Pr\!\left(\left.|M_{e,t}(d_{0})-\operatorname{E}[M_{e,t}(d_{0})]|\geq\frac{u}{4}\right|\mathcal{C}\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{\alpha u^{2}}{32L_{\mathrm{mean}}^{2}}\right).

In this dense almost-regular core,
\Delta/\delta=O(1)
and
m_{\min}=\alpha+2\lambda\delta=\Theta(\Delta)
, hence
\Delta/m_{\min}=\Theta(1)
. Therefore
Section

4.7
(i) gives
L_{\mathrm{mean}}=O(1)
.
Thus the right-hand side is
\exp(-\Omega(u^{2}))=\exp(-\Omega(\gamma^{2}))=(100Q^{4})^{-\Omega(C_{\gamma})}
, which is at most
1/(800Q^{2})
for
C_{\gamma}
large.

We now show that the centering term
|\operatorname{E}[M_{e,t}(d_{0})]|
is negligible compared to
u
in this dense regime,
via (
26
).

Fix
i,t,e
and
d_{0}\in\{\pm\gamma/2\}
and condition on a transcript prefix
T_{<t}
for which
\mathsf{Open}_{e,t}
holds.
Write
S:=\{\ell,r\}\cup\Gamma
for the one-neighborhood of
e
, and let
\mathcal{F}_{S}
denote the
\sigma
-field generated by all spins outside
S
.
Under
\mu_{i}(\cdot\mid I=i,T_{<t},\mathcal{F}_{S})
the spins in
S
have a log-concave density on the box
[-B,B]^{S}
with Hamiltonian
H_{S}
obtained by freezing the exterior configuration.
Let
H^{\mathrm{inc}}_{e,t}(x_{\ell},x_{r},x_{\Gamma})
be the sub-sum of
H_{S}
consisting of
terms from edges incident to
\ell
or
r
, excluding the designated edge
e
.
Define

V(x_{\ell},x_{r}):=-\log\!\int_{[-B,B]^{\Gamma}}\exp\!\bigl(-H^{\mathrm{inc}}_{e,t}(x_{\ell},x_{r},x_{\Gamma})\bigr)\,dx_{\Gamma}.

This is the effective (convex) endpoint potential generated by exactly the incident-edge
terms that form the drift part
R_{e,t}
in the one-dimensional decomposition of

W_{e,t}(d)
.
(The remaining local terms, i.e., endpoint pinning and the
e
-edge terms, belong to
U_{e,t}
and
\Psi_{e,i}
in
Section

4.2
and are therefore not part of
R_{e,t}
.)
Since we work throughout under
\mathsf{Open}_{e,t}
, at most one endpoint value
can already be revealed by the prefix
T_{<t}
.
If neither endpoint is revealed then the endpoint marginal is two-dimensional on

[-B,B]^{2}
; if exactly one endpoint is revealed then the remaining endpoint has a
one-dimensional conditional marginal supported on a slice of
[-B,B]^{2}
.
All differentiation and boundary-leakage arguments below are performed

after conditioning on
any revealed endpoint value, and therefore apply in
either the 2D or 1D situation.
The only place where the distinction matters is in the definition of the
active-strip event
\mathcal{B}_{e,t}
in (
25
),
where we omit any endpoint that is already fixed by
T_{<t}
.

A direct differentiation under the integral (Leibniz rule) identifies the
conditional-mean drift as the derivative of an
external
effective
endpoint potential in the same one-dimensional direction that defines the
difference coordinate
d
in
Section

4.2
.
Under
\mathsf{Open}_{e,t}
there are three cases, and in each case we parametrize the endpoints as a path
d\mapsto(x_{\ell}(d),x_{r}(d))
so that
R_{e,t}(d)
is obtained by evaluating the incident edge terms at
(x_{\ell}(d),x_{r}(d))
:

•

If neither endpoint is revealed in
T_{<t}
, we work at fixed midpoint
z:=\tfrac{1}{2}(f(\ell)+f(r))
and set

x_{\ell}(d)=z+d/2
and
x_{r}(d)=z-d/2
.

•

If
f(\ell)
is revealed (but
f(r)
is not), we set
a:=f(\ell)
and vary only the right endpoint:

x_{\ell}(d)=a
and
x_{r}(d)=a-d
.

•

If
f(r)
is revealed (but
f(\ell)
is not), we set
b:=f(r)
and vary only the left endpoint:

x_{\ell}(d)=b+d
and
x_{r}(d)=b
.

In all three cases, differentiating under the integral and applying the chain rule yields, for every
d_{0}\in\{\pm\gamma/2\}
,

M_{e,t}(d_{0})\;=\;\left.\frac{d}{dd}\,V\!\bigl(x_{\ell}(d),x_{r}(d)\bigr)\right|_{d=d_{0}}.

(23)

Equivalently,

M_{e,t}(d_{0})=\begin{cases}\left.\tfrac{1}{2}(\partial_{x_{\ell}}-\partial_{x_{r}})V(x_{\ell},x_{r})\right|_{(x_{\ell},x_{r})=(z+d_{0}/2,\,z-d_{0}/2)},&\text{if no endpoint is revealed},\\[2.84526pt]
\left.-(\partial_{x_{r}})V(x_{\ell},x_{r})\right|_{(x_{\ell},x_{r})=(a,\,a-d_{0})},&\text{if only }f(\ell)\text{ is revealed},\\[2.84526pt]
\left.(\partial_{x_{\ell}})V(x_{\ell},x_{r})\right|_{(x_{\ell},x_{r})=(b+d_{0},\,b)},&\text{if only }f(r)\text{ is revealed}.\end{cases}

In particular, the evaluation point depends on
d_{0}
(and on which endpoint, if any, is fixed), so (
23
) does
not
imply
M_{e,t}(+\gamma/2)=M_{e,t}(-\gamma/2)
.

To bound the mean-shift term
|\operatorname{E}[M_{e,t}(d_{0})]|
, we isolate the “box effect” of the hard truncation.
In the untruncated model (no indicator on
[-B,B]
) the same differentiation-under-the-integral calculation that gives (
23
) yields a perfectly centered score identity (an integration-by-parts/score-centering argument; see
Section

4.1
), and in particular the corresponding mean term vanishes.
Therefore
\operatorname{E}[M_{e,t}(d_{0})]
is entirely due to boundary contributions coming from the hard box.

Let

\eta_{\mathrm{strip}}:=C_{\mathrm{bdry}}\cdot\frac{\lambda+\beta}{\beta-\lambda}\cdot\frac{B+\gamma}{\gamma}\cdot\exp\!\left(-c_{\mathrm{bdry}}\cdot\delta\cdot\frac{(\beta-\lambda)^{2}}{\lambda+\beta}\cdot\gamma^{2}\right),

(24)

where
c_{\mathrm{bdry}},C_{\mathrm{bdry}}>0
are the absolute constants from
Section

4.1
.
For
d_{0}\in\{\pm\gamma/2\}
, varying the difference coordinate
d
moves the endpoint pair
(x_{\ell}(d),x_{r}(d))
along the corresponding path discussed above (a diagonal line in the 2D case, and a horizontal/vertical line in the 1D cases) within the box
[-B,B]^{2}
.
When differentiating the truncated integral defining
V(x_{\ell}(d),x_{r}(d))
along this path, boundary contributions can arise only when the path hits a face of the box.
Since
R^{\prime}_{e,t}(d)
is the derivative with respect to
d
in the
positive
direction, the only face that can contribute with the adverse sign for the relevant one-sided mean control is the face approached when
d
increases.
In all cases of
\mathsf{Open}_{e,t}
this active face is always one of the two oriented faces
x_{\ell}=+B
(whenever
x_{\ell}
increases with
d
) and
x_{r}=-B
(whenever
x_{r}
decreases with
d
), independently of the sign of
d_{0}
.

Because a transcript prefix may already reveal one endpoint value, we define the corresponding active-strip event by omitting any endpoint that is fixed by
T_{<t}
:

\mathcal{B}_{e,t}:=\begin{cases}\{f(\ell)\in[B-\gamma/2,B]\}\ \cup\ \{f(r)\in[-B,-B+\gamma/2]\},&\text{if no endpoint is revealed},\\[2.84526pt]
\{f(r)\in[-B,-B+\gamma/2]\},&\text{if only }f(\ell)\text{ is revealed},\\[2.84526pt]
\{f(\ell)\in[B-\gamma/2,B]\},&\text{if only }f(r)\text{ is revealed}.\end{cases}

(25)

On
\mathcal{B}_{e,t}^{c}
, the probe segment stays a distance
\geq\gamma/2
away from the active boundary faces, so the boundary terms vanish and the remaining interior term cancels by the (untruncated) score centering (cf.
Section

4.1
).
Thus, using the uniform bound
|M_{e,t}(d_{0})|\leq\mathrm{poly}(\Delta,\lambda,\beta,B,\gamma)
(a sum of
O(\Delta)
one-edge forces, each of size
O((\lambda+\beta)(B+\gamma))
), we obtain

|\operatorname{E}[M_{e,t}(d_{0})]|\ \leq\ \mathrm{poly}(\Delta,\lambda,\beta,B,\gamma)\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{B}_{e,t}].

Finally,
Section

4.1
applies uniformly under any boundary conditioning, so whichever endpoint remains random under
T_{<t}
satisfies the corresponding strip bound.
From the definition (
25
) of
\mathcal{B}_{e,t}
we therefore have, uniformly over all transcript prefixes for which
\mathsf{Open}_{e,t}
holds,

\Pr[\mathcal{B}_{e,t}]\leq 2\eta_{\mathrm{strip}}.

Combining gives

|\operatorname{E}[M_{e,t}(d_{0})]|\;\leq\;\mathrm{poly}(\Delta,\lambda,\beta,B,\gamma)\cdot\eta_{\mathrm{strip}},

(26)

as claimed.

In the present dense regime,
Section

4.1
gives

\eta_{\mathrm{strip}}\leq\exp(-\Omega(\delta\gamma^{2}))
with
\delta=\Theta(\Delta)=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
,
so the right-hand side of (

) is superpolynomially small in

.
In particular, for all large

it is at most

.

Combining this with the deviation bound above yields

\displaystyle\Pr\!\left(\left.|M_{e,t}(d_{0})|\geq\frac{u}{2}\right|I=i,T_{<t},\ \mathsf{Open}_{e,t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\!\left(\left.|M_{e,t}(d_{0})-\operatorname{E}[M_{e,t}(d_{0})]|\geq\frac{u}{4}\right|I=i,T_{<t},\ \mathsf{Open}_{e,t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{800Q^{2}}.

Taking a union bound over

and then the supremum over

(as well as transcript prefixes with

) gives
p_{\mathrm{mean}}(u)\leq 1/(400Q^{2})
.
∎

Theorem 4.27

(Deterministic adaptive one-sided lower bound from the truncated Gibbs model)
.

There exist absolute constants
c,\varepsilon>0
such that for infinitely many
n
there is a bipartite DAG
G

on
n
vertices with the following property.
Every deterministic adaptive
one-sided

\varepsilon
-tester for monotonicity on
G

requires at least
c\sqrt{n}
queries.

Proof.

Apply
Section

4.7
with the dense choice
P=\lfloor c_{P}N\rfloor
for a sufficiently small absolute constant
c_{P}\in(0,1/10)
.
Let
G:=G^{\circ}
be the resulting bipartite DAG together with the family of edge-disjoint positive matchings

\{M_{i}\}_{i\in[s]}
(indexed by
A^{\circ}
in
Section

4.7
).
Write
n_{0}:=|L^{\circ}|=|R^{\circ}|
and
n:=|V(G)|=2n_{0}
.
In particular,

s=\Theta(\sqrt{n_{0}})=\Theta(\sqrt{n}),\qquad|M_{i}|\geq\varepsilon_{0}n_{0}\ \ (\forall i\in[s]),\qquad\text{and}\qquad c_{\delta}s\leq\deg(v)\leq s\ \ (\forall v),

so
\delta:=\min_{v}\deg(v)=\Theta(\sqrt{n_{0}})=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
and

\Delta:=\max_{v}\deg(v)=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
with
\Delta/\delta=O(1)
.

Set

Q:=\left\lfloor c_{Q}\sqrt{n_{0}}\right\rfloor

for a sufficiently small absolute constant
c_{Q}>0
(chosen at the end), and instantiate the truncated Gibbs family

\{\mu_{i}\}_{i\in[s]}
on
G
with parameters

\alpha=1,\qquad\lambda=1,\qquad\beta=10,\qquad\gamma:=\sqrt{C_{\gamma}\log(100Q^{4})},\qquad B:=4\gamma,\qquad u:=\frac{\beta\gamma}{10},

where
C_{\gamma}
is a sufficiently large absolute constant.

We verify the conditions of
Theorem

4.22
.

(i) Support and separation.

The support condition (
15
) holds since
B=4\gamma
.
For the separation condition (
16
), the right-hand side equals

\alpha\!\left(B+\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)+\lambda\gamma+u\;=\;\left(4+\frac{1}{2}+1+\frac{\beta}{10}\right)\gamma\;=\;6.5\,\gamma,

while the left-hand side is
\beta\gamma=10\gamma
, so (
16
) holds.

(ii) Tail probabilities
p_{\mathrm{match}}
and
p_{\mathrm{spur}}
.

With
\alpha=\lambda=1
we have
m_{\mathrm{edge}}=\alpha/2+2\lambda=5/2
, so

\exp\!\left(-\frac{m_{\mathrm{edge}}\gamma^{2}}{8}\right)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{5}{16}\gamma^{2}\right)=(100Q^{4})^{-5C_{\gamma}/16}.

Choosing
C_{\gamma}
large makes this at most
\min\{10^{-4},\,1/(400Q^{2})\}
.

For the drift term, note that
m_{\min}=\alpha+2\lambda\delta=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
and
\Delta=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
, hence

m_{\min}/\Delta=\Theta(1)
.
With
u=\beta\gamma/10
the drift tail (
12
) becomes

p_{\mathrm{drift}}(u/2)\leq 2\exp(-\Omega(\gamma^{2}))
,
so for
C_{\gamma}
large we have
p_{\mathrm{drift}}(u/2)\leq 1/(400Q^{2})
.
Finally,
Section

4.7
supplies the required mean-control bound

p_{\mathrm{mean}}(u)\leq 1/(400Q^{2})
for this instantiation.
Combining these with (
20
) gives
p_{\mathrm{match}}\leq 10^{-4}
and

p_{\mathrm{spur}}\leq 1/(100Q^{2})
.

(iii) Boundary-margin failures.

Section

4.3
bounds the boundary failure probability by

\eta_{0}\;\leq\;Q\cdot C_{\mathrm{bdry}}\cdot\frac{\lambda+\beta}{\beta-\lambda}\cdot\frac{B+\gamma}{\gamma}\cdot\exp\!\left(-c_{\mathrm{bdry}}\cdot\frac{\delta(\beta-\lambda)^{2}}{\lambda+\beta}\cdot\gamma^{2}\right).

Here
\delta=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
and
\gamma^{2}=\Theta(\log Q)
. Moreover, under our instantiation

B=4\gamma
and
\lambda,\beta
are fixed absolute constants, so the prefactor

C_{\mathrm{bdry}}\cdot\frac{\lambda+\beta}{\beta-\lambda}\cdot\frac{B+\gamma}{\gamma}
is an absolute constant.
Thus the exponent dominates,
so the exponent is
-\Theta(\sqrt{n}\log Q)
and in particular
\eta_{0}\leq 0.01
for all large
n
.

(iv) Leakage.

Since
m_{\min}=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
and
B+\gamma=\Theta(\gamma)
,

Section

4.4
gives

\kappa_{Q}\;\leq\;\frac{C_{b}}{\alpha}\cdot\frac{\beta^{2}(B+\gamma)^{2}}{m_{\min}}\;=\;O\!\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\;=\;O\!\left(\frac{\log Q}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Thus
Q\kappa_{Q}=O(c_{Q}\log Q)
.

(v) Apply the fixed-budget barrier.

Theorem

4.22
states that any deterministic adaptive one-sided tester with query budget
q\leq Q

must satisfy

q\,\kappa_{Q}\ \geq\ (0.65-\eta_{0})\log s-1.

Since
\eta_{0}\leq 0.01
and
s=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
we have
\log s=\Theta(\log Q)
.
On the other hand,
Q\kappa_{Q}=O(c_{Q}\log Q)
.
Choosing the absolute constant
c_{Q}>0
small enough makes

Q\kappa_{Q}<(0.65-\eta_{0})\log s-1
for all large
n
, so no adaptive one-sided tester using at most
Q
queries can
succeed.
This yields the stated
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
lower bound.
∎

Theorem 4.28

Algorithm 1

Monotonicity Tester via Transitive Reduction

Proof of
Theorem

5.1
.

The tester is one-sided error because it rejects only after finding a violating pair.

Fix a function
f:V\to\mathbb{R}
that is
\varepsilon
-far from monotonicity.
By the standard matching characterization of the distance to monotonicity (via Kőnig’s theorem),
there exists a matching
M
of violating pairs in
\mathrm{TC}(G)
with

|M|\geq\varepsilon n.

Let

F_{f}:=\{(x,y)\in E:f(x)>f(y)\}

be the set of
violating edges
in
G
.

We use the following dichotomy, governed by a threshold parameter
\tau>0
to be chosen later.

Case 1:
|F_{f}|\geq\tau
.

In the first loop of
Algorithm

1
, each sampled edge of
E
is violating with
probability at least
\tau/m
.
Thus, by choosing the hidden constant in
q=\Theta(m/\tau)
large enough, the rejection
probability in the first loop is at least

1-\left(1-\frac{\tau}{m}\right)^{q}\geq\frac{2}{3}.

Case 2:
|F_{f}|<\tau
.

For each violating pair
(u,v)\in M
, choose an arbitrary directed path
P_{uv}
from
u
to
v

in
G
.
Along
P_{uv}
, since
f(u)>f(v)
, there must exist at least one edge

(x,y)\in E
with
f(x)>f(y)
.
Let
\psi(u,v)
be the
first
such violating edge on the path
P_{uv}
, and partition

M=\biguplus_{e\in F_{f}}M_{e},\qquad M_{e}:=\{(u,v)\in M:\psi(u,v)=e\}.

For each
e\in F_{f}
, let
A_{e}
(resp.,
B_{e}
) be the set of left (resp., right) endpoints of
pairs in
M_{e}
. Since
M
is a matching, the families
\{A_{e}\}_{e}
and
\{B_{e}\}_{e}
are pairwise disjoint,
and
|A_{e}|=|B_{e}|=|M_{e}|
.

Fix
e=(x,y)\in F_{f}
. Let
t_{e}
be a median of the multiset
\{f(a):(a,b)\in M_{e}\}
and define

A_{e}^{+}\ :=\ \{a\in A_{e}:\ f(a)\geq t_{e}\},\qquad B_{e}^{-}\ :=\ \{b\in B_{e}:\ f(b)<t_{e}\}.

Then
|A_{e}^{+}|,|B_{e}^{-}|\geq|M_{e}|/2
(again because each
(a,b)\in M_{e}
has
f(a)>f(b)
). For any
a\in A_{e}^{+}
and
b\in B_{e}^{-}
we claim that

(a,b)\in E(\mathrm{TC}(G))
and it is a violating pair.
Indeed, by construction of the partition,
a
reaches
x
in
G
along
P_{a,\cdot}
,
and
y
reaches
b
in
G
along
P_{\cdot,b}
. Concatenating these paths with the edge
(x,y)
yields
a directed path from
a
to
b
in
G
, hence
(a,b)\in E(\mathrm{TC}(G))
, and
f(a)>f(b)
.
Therefore, for each
e
there are at least
|A_{e}^{+}|\cdot|B_{e}^{-}|\geq|M_{e}|^{2}/4
violating pairs in
\mathrm{TC}(G)

witnessed through
e
. Summing over
e
and applying Cauchy–Schwarz gives that the total number of
violating pairs in
\mathrm{TC}(G)
is at least

\frac{1}{4}\sum_{e\in F_{f}}|M_{e}|^{2}\ \geq\ \frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{\left(\sum_{e\in F_{f}}|M_{e}|\right)^{2}}{|F_{f}|}\ =\ \frac{|M|^{2}}{4|F_{f}|}\ >\ \frac{\varepsilon^{2}n^{2}}{4\tau}.

Hence, in the second loop of
Algorithm

1
, a uniformly sampled edge of

E(\mathrm{TC}(G))
is violating with probability at least
\varepsilon^{2}n^{2}/(4\tau\ell)
.
By choosing the hidden constant in
q=\Theta(\tau\ell/(\varepsilon^{2}n^{2}))
large enough, the
rejection probability in the second loop is at least

1-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}n^{2}}{4\tau\ell}\right)^{q}\geq\frac{2}{3}.

It remains to balance the two cases.
Setting

\tau:=\varepsilon n\sqrt{\frac{m}{\ell}}

makes
m/\tau
and
\tau\ell/(\varepsilon^{2}n^{2})
equal up to constants, and thus

Algorithm

1
has query complexity

q=\Theta\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{m\,\ell}}{\varepsilon n}\right).

This completes the proof.
∎

Algorithm 2

Two-stage monotonicity tester

Theorem 5.2
.

Algorithm

2
is a one-sided error monotonicity tester with query complexity

O\!\left(m^{1/3}/\varepsilon^{2/3}\right).

Proof.

It is immediate that
Algorithm

2
has one-sided error: if
f
is monotone, then
there are no violating pairs in
\mathrm{TC}(G)
, and in particular there are no violating edges in
E
,
so the algorithm always accepts.

Fix a function
f:V\to\mathbb{R}
that is
\varepsilon
-far from monotonicity. By the standard matching characterization of the distance to monotonicity
(via Kőnig’s theorem), there exists a matching
M
of violating pairs in
\mathrm{TC}(G)
such that

|M|\geq\varepsilon n.

(27)

Let

F_{f}:=\{(x,y)\in E:f(x)>f(y)\}

be the set of
violating edges
in
G
.

We will use a dichotomy based on the size of
F_{f}
.
Set the threshold

\tau:=(\varepsilon m)^{2/3}.

We show that with
q=\Theta(m^{1/3}/\varepsilon^{2/3})
, the algorithm rejects with probability at
least
2/3
in either case.

Case 1:
|F_{f}|\geq\tau
.

In the first stage of
Algorithm

2
, each sampled edge is violating with probability
at least
|F_{f}|/m\geq\tau/m
. Hence,

\Pr[\text{fail to reject in stage 1}]\leq\left(1-\frac{\tau}{m}\right)^{q}\leq\exp\!\left(-q\frac{\tau}{m}\right).

Choosing the hidden constant in
q=\Theta(m/\tau)=\Theta(m^{1/3}/\varepsilon^{2/3})
sufficiently
large, the right-hand side becomes at most
1/3
, so stage 1 rejects with probability at least
2/3
.

Case 2:
|F_{f}|<\tau
.

We analyze the second stage using the standard “bottleneck partition” and the Poisson approximation
argument.

For each violating pair
(u,v)\in M
, fix an arbitrary directed path
P_{uv}
from
u
to
v
in
G
.
Since
f(u)>f(v)
, along
P_{uv}
there must exist at least one edge

(x,y)\in E
with
f(x)>f(y)
; define
\psi(u,v)
to be the
first
such edge
on
P_{uv}
. This yields a partition

M=\biguplus_{e\in F_{f}}M_{e},\qquad M_{e}:=\{(u,v)\in M:\ \psi(u,v)=e\}.

For each
e\in F_{f}
, let
A_{e}
be the set of left endpoints of pairs in
M_{e}
and let
B_{e}
be the set of
right endpoints. Since
M
is a matching, the families
\{A_{e}\}_{e\in F_{f}}
and
\{B_{e}\}_{e\in F_{f}}
are
pairwise disjoint, and
|A_{e}|=|B_{e}|=|M_{e}|
.

Fix
e=(x,y)\in F_{f}
. Let
t_{e}
be a median of the multiset
\{f(a):(a,b)\in M_{e}\}
and define

A_{e}^{+}\ :=\ \{a\in A_{e}:\ f(a)\geq t_{e}\},\qquad B_{e}^{-}\ :=\ \{b\in B_{e}:\ f(b)<t_{e}\}.

Then
|A_{e}^{+}|,|B_{e}^{-}|\geq|M_{e}|/2
(since each
(a,b)\in M_{e}
has
f(a)>f(b)
). For any
a\in A_{e}^{+}
and
b\in B_{e}^{-}
, we have
a\leadsto x
and
y\leadsto b

in
G
by construction of the “first violating edge” assignment, hence
a\leadsto b
in
G
via
the path
a\leadsto x\to y\leadsto b
. Therefore
(a,b)\in E(\mathrm{TC}(G))
and
(a,b)
is a violating
pair. Consequently, if the sampled multiset
Q_{L}
contains at least one vertex in
A_{e}^{+}
and
Q_{R}

contains at least one vertex in
B_{e}^{-}
for some
e\in F_{f}
, then stage 2 rejects.

Let
X_{v}^{L}
(resp.,
X_{v}^{R}
) be the number of times
v\in V
is sampled when constructing
Q_{L}
(resp.,
Q_{R}
).
We bound the failure event

\mathcal{E}:=\bigl[\ \forall e\in F_{f},\ \sum_{v\in A_{e}^{+}}X_{v}^{L}=0\ \ \vee\ \ \sum_{v\in B_{e}^{-}}X_{v}^{R}=0\ \bigr].

Introduce independent Poisson variables
Y_{v}^{L},Y_{v}^{R}\sim\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)
with mean
\lambda:=q/n
.
By the Poisson approximation theorem
[
4
]
,

\Pr[\mathcal{E}]\leq 2\Pr\Bigl[\ \forall e\in F_{f},\ \sum_{v\in A_{e}^{+}}Y_{v}^{L}=0\ \ \vee\ \ \sum_{v\in B_{e}^{-}}Y_{v}^{R}=0\ \Bigr].

(28)

Since the sets
\{A_{e}^{+}\}_{e}
are disjoint and the sets
\{B_{e}^{-}\}_{e}
are disjoint, and the left/right
samples are independent, the events inside the probability in
(
28
) are independent, and thus

\Pr\Bigl[\ \forall e\in F_{f},\ \sum_{v\in A_{e}^{+}}Y_{v}^{L}=0\ \ \vee\ \ \sum_{v\in B_{e}^{-}}Y_{v}^{R}=0\ \Bigr]=\prod_{e\in F_{f}}\left(1-\bigl(1-e^{-\lambda|A_{e}^{+}|}\bigr)\bigl(1-e^{-\lambda|B_{e}^{-}|}\bigr)\right).

Using
|A_{e}^{+}|,|B_{e}^{-}|\geq|M_{e}|/2
, we can lower bound the success probability for each
e
by

(1-e^{-\lambda|M_{e}|/2})^{2}
, which only changes constants in the analysis below.

If there exists
e
with
\lambda|M_{e}|\geq 6
, then

\left(1-e^{-\lambda|M_{e}|/2}\right)^{2}\geq(1-e^{-3})^{2}
and hence the rejection probability is at least

1-2(1-(1-e^{-3})^{2})>2/3
.

Otherwise,
\lambda|M_{e}|\leq 6
for all
e\in F_{f}
. For
x\in[0,3]
we have
1-e^{-x}\geq x/4
,
so

1-\left(1-e^{-\lambda|M_{e}|/2}\right)^{2}\leq 1-\frac{\lambda^{2}|M_{e}|^{2}}{64}.

Therefore,

\Pr[\mathcal{E}]\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{64}\sum_{e\in F_{f}}|M_{e}|^{2}\right).

By Cauchy–Schwarz and (
27
),

\sum_{e\in F_{f}}|M_{e}|^{2}\ \geq\ \frac{\left(\sum_{e\in F_{f}}|M_{e}|\right)^{2}}{|F_{f}|}\ =\ \frac{|M|^{2}}{|F_{f}|}\ \geq\ \frac{\varepsilon^{2}n^{2}}{|F_{f}|}\ >\ \frac{\varepsilon^{2}n^{2}}{\tau},

where we used the assumption
|F_{f}|<\tau
in the last inequality.
Plugging this in and using
\lambda=q/n
, we obtain

\Pr[\mathcal{E}]\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}q^{2}}{64\tau}\right).

Finally, with
\tau=(\varepsilon m)^{2/3}
we have
\sqrt{\tau}/\varepsilon=m^{1/3}/\varepsilon^{2/3}
.
Thus, by setting
q=\Theta(\sqrt{\tau}/\varepsilon)=\Theta(m^{1/3}/\varepsilon^{2/3})
with a sufficiently
large hidden constant, the above probability becomes at most
1/3
, and stage 2 rejects with probability
at least
2/3
.

In both cases,
Algorithm

2
rejects
\varepsilon
-far functions with probability at least
2/3
,
and it uses
O(q)=O(m^{1/3}/\varepsilon^{2/3})
queries.
∎
