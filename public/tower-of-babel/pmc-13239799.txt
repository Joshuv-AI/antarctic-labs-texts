Inflammation and Erythropoietin Resistance in Chronic Kidney Disease: A Cross‐Sectional Study of C‐Reactive Protein and Anemia in Hemodialysis Patients
Authors: Collince Odiwuor Ogolla, Lucy W Karani, Stanslaus Musyoki, Phidelis Maruti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAnemia represents a frequent complication in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) on hemodialysis. Inflammation results in disruption of erythropoiesis and establishment of less effective responses to erythropoiesis‐stimulating agents (ESAs).ObjectiveThis study investigated how systemic inflammation, which C‐reactive protein (CRP) measured, relates to anemia severity and erythropoietin (EPO) responsiveness among patients with chronic kidney disease who undergo maintenance hemodialysis.MethodsCross‐sectional study design involving 120 CKD patients. Participants were classified into high inflammation group for CRP levels at or above 10 mg/L and low inflammation group for CRP levels below 10 mg/L. Hematological parameters and iron status indices were analyzed, and weekly EPO doses were recorded. Pearson correlation and multivariable linear regression analyses were done while controlling for age, sex, comorbidities, and dialysis duration.ResultsHigh inflammation group displayed hematocrit level of 29.6% ± 4.8% while the control group showed a level of 34.2% ± 5.2% which produced a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001). CRP exhibited a negative correlation with hematocrit (r = −0.42, p < 0.001) and transferrin saturation (r = −0.36, p < 0.001) but showed a positive correlation with EPO dose (r = 0.48, p < 0.001). CRP established an independent relationship with hematocrit (β = −0.43, 95% CI: −0.58 to −0.28) and EPO dosage (β = 401, 95% CI: 210 to 592) during the multivariable analysis.LimitationsCross‐sectional design restricts causal relationships while the research team evaluated CRP as the only inflammatory marker.ConclusionsSystemic inflammation establishes a significant connection to anemia severity and EPO resistance, which affects CKD patients.Keywords: anemia, chronic kidney disease, C‐reactive protein

Introduction

Anemia develops as a common complication of chronic kidney disease which particularly impacts patients who require ongoing hemodialysis treatment. The condition develops because of three main factors which include decreased erythropoietin production and iron deficiency and problems with red blood cell production. Systemic inflammation functions as a major factor in the development of anemia which occurs in CKD patients because it creates conditions that make their bodies resist erythropoietin and it interferes with their ability to process iron [1, 2]. The progression of CKD leads to increasing inflammation which results in higher CRP levels that link to greater symptoms and this condition creates challenges for anemia because it disrupts iron metabolism and red blood cell production [2, 3].

Erythropoietin resistance stands as a primary obstacle which healthcare professionals need to address when treating anemia that occurs in patients with chronic kidney disease. Several patients fail to reach the target hematocrit levels despite receiving adequate dose of EPO, which in turn leads to a need for being high EPO doses, thereby increasing healthcare costs and adverse outcomes such as cardiovascular disease [4, 5]. It is believed that such resistance is mediated by several putative factors, one of which is inflammation that boosts pro‐inflammatory cytokines production like interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) affecting negatively EPO responsiveness [6, 7]. The body reacts to inflammation by producing more hepcidin because this protein serves as a key controller for iron processing which leads to reduced iron uptake and decreased blood cell production that worsens anemia treatment [8, 9, 10]. The latest KDIGO guidelines from 2023 to 2025 show how inflammation specifically through the interleukin‐6 IL‐6 pathway affects hepcidin production to create functional iron deficiency and reduced response to ESA treatment in CKD patients [11, 12, 13]. Emerging evidence also highlights CRP as a clinically accessible surrogate marker of inflammatory burden associated with adverse hematologic outcomes [14].

CRP used as a very well system failed marker in inflammation; however, it may be applied as a potential predictor of severity of anemia in a patient with CKD. Studies suggest that an increase in CRP would also relate to a decrease in hemoglobin and hematocrit values and could predict a poor response to EPO [15]. However, the exact mechanisms through which inflammation induces anemia and EPO resistance are not known explicitly in CKD patients on hemodialysis. The present study was done to elicit the association of inflammation, CRP levels with the development of anemia and with the production of EPO resistance in chronic kidney disease patients who are undergoing hemodialysis treatments [16]. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the relationship between systemic inflammation, as measured by CRP, and anemia severity, iron status, and erythropoietin responsiveness in CKD patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis. We hypothesized that elevated CRP levels are independently associated with reduced hematocrit, impaired iron utilization, and increased EPO requirements.

Methods

Study Design

This was a hospital‐based analytical cross‐sectional study conducted among adult CKD patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis. The research study aimed to determine how systemic inflammation relates to various anemia outcomes, which included erythropoietin responsiveness.

Study Population

The study included participants who were 18 years or older and had been receiving hemodialysis for at least 6 months while maintaining stable health for 3 months. The researchers divided participants into two groups based on their C‐reactive protein levels, which showed high inflammation in one group and low inflammation in the other group with CRP levels of 10 mg/L. Most patients received dialysis treatment through tunneled central venous catheters while a smaller group used arteriovenous fistulas for their treatment. The dialysis records provided information about vascular access type because it directly influences inflammation and infection probabilities, which affect hemodialysis patients. All study participants received CKD stage 5 classification according to KDIGO guidelines because their glomerular filtration rates fell below 15 mL/min/1.73 m2 and they needed continuous hemodialysis [12].

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The study established inclusion criteria which required participants to be adults who were at least 18 years old and had stage 5 CKD while receiving hemodialysis for a minimum of 6 months. Patients needed to sustain their stable clinical condition without experiencing any acute medical problems for a duration of 3 months. The research excluded participants who had active infections, recent hospitalization, malignant conditions, chronic inflammatory diseases, recent blood transfusions, or who were using immunosuppressive or corticosteroid drugs. The study excluded patients who had recent infections or hospitalizations or who had received antibiotic or corticosteroid treatment because these factors would create confounding effects through inflammatory processes.

Data Collection

Data was collected from patient records together with incidental clinical assessments which took place at hemodialysis sessions. The study obtained demographic and clinical details which included age and gender together with information about existing medical conditions such as diabetes mellitus. All patients received standard iron supplementation according to institutional protocols, predominantly through intravenous iron formulations which doctors administered during dialysis sessions. The multivariable regression models included potential confounders which consisted of age, sex, diabetes, hypertension, dialysis duration, iron status, and vascular access type.

Laboratory Investigations

Laboratory investigations were carried out using standard protocols. The blood sample was withdrawn aseptically before the mid‐week dialysis session, while CBC was recorded as a parameter for assessing the severity of anemia. Anemia was defined according to KDIGO guidelines as hemoglobin < 13.0 g/dL in males and < 12.0 g/dL in females [13]. The erythropoietin resistance index (ERI) was calculated as weekly ESA dose (IU/kg) divided by hemoglobin concentration (g/dL). The hematocrit reference range was considered 36%–46% for females and 40%–50% for males. Serum C‐reactive protein (CRP) was estimated by high‐sensitivity immunoturbidimetric assays on the Beckman Coulter AU480 analyzer. A CRP level of ≥ 10 mg/L was taken to define the high‐inflammation group and a CRP level of < 10 mg/L for the low‐inflammation group. The normal reference range of CRP was < 5 mg/L. The study could not measure dialysis adequacy parameters like Kt/V, which created a situation where unmeasured variables could affect the research results.

From the prescription records, the weekly EPO dose per patient was recorded. Epoetin alfa was administered to the patients subcutaneously during dialysis sessions. Documentation of the dose was undertaken. Erythropoietin dose was additionally normalized to body weight (IU/kg/week) to calculate the erythropoietin resistance index (ERI). Serum ferritin and transferrin saturation were analyzed to assess iron status. Ferritin levels were measured by ECLIA with interpretation ranges 30–400 ng/mL on a Cobas e411 analyzer (Roche). TSAT was calculated from serum iron and TIBC; values of 20%–50% were considered normal. Iron deficiency was defined with reference to KDIGO clinical guidelines as serum ferritin < 100 ng/mL and/or TSAT < 20%. The laboratory tests followed both internal and external quality control procedures to establish data accuracy and test reproducibility. All assays were performed using standardized automated analyzers following manufacturer protocols. Internal quality control procedures were conducted daily, and external quality assurance was maintained through participation in national proficiency testing programs. The coefficient of variation (CV) for CRP and hematological parameters was maintained below 5%.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R statistical software version 3.3.2. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Independent t‐tests and chi‐square tests were used for group comparisons. Pearson correlation analysis assessed relationships between CRP and hematological parameters. Multivariable linear regression models were constructed to identify independent predictors of hematocrit levels and EPO dose. The study presented adjusted beta coefficients together with their 95% confidence intervals and p‐values. The study included age and sex and diabetes and hypertension and dialysis duration and iron status as covariates. The researchers established statistical significance at p‐values which reached levels below 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

The study was conducted in strict observance of the declaration of Helsinki. All patient data were anonymized by removing identifying information, and access to the records was restricted to authorized study personnel to ensure confidentiality throughout the research process.

Results

This research investigated how inflammatory conditions lead to anemia among hemodialysis patients suffering from chronic kidney disease through their study of inflammatory biomarkers which control both erythropoiesis and erythropoietin (EPO) reaction. The study included 120 patients receiving hemodialysis treatment at a tertiary care center for their chronic kidney disease. Study subjects were divided into two groups according to the level of inflammatory biomarker: high inflammation (CRP ≥ 10 mg/L) and low inflammation (CRP < 10 mg/L).

Multivariable Linear Regression Analysis of Predictors of Hematocrit and EPO Dose

In multivariable analysis, CRP remained independently associated with hematocrit (β = −0.43, 95% CI: −0.58 to −0.28, p < 0.001) and EPO dose (β = 401, 95% CI: 210 to 592, p < 0.001). Multivariable regression analysis demonstrated that CRP remained an independent predictor of both reduced hematocrit and increased EPO dose after adjusting for potential confounders, including age, sex, comorbidities, and iron status.

Multivariable regression models (adjusted for age, gender, diabetes, hypertension, and dialysis duration) indicated that inflammation, as measured by CRP, remained an independent factor associated with lower hematocrit levels and higher EPO doses (Table 3). Discussion

The study shows that systemic inflammation which causes increased CRP levels results in two effects because it independently links to anemia severity and the need for higher erythropoietin levels in hemodialysis patients with chronic kidney disease. The results support the theory which states that inflammation causes both erythropoietin resistance and impairment of erythropoiesis. High CRP patients showed lower hematocrit and used less iron while requiring greater erythropoietin (EPO) doses when compared to patients with low CRP levels [17, 18]. The research shows that inflammation causes anemia in chronic kidney disease through its effect on disease progression.

Our research supports existing worldwide evidence which shows that inflammation disrupts erythropoiesis by creating multiple pathways that lead to EPO resistance. The pro‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐6 especially induce hepcidin production by hepatocytes. Hepcidin decreases intestinal iron absorption while it causes iron storage inside macrophages, which results in functional iron deficiency that occurs with normal ferritin levels [19, 20]. The high inflammation group showed significantly lower transferrin saturation and serum ferritin values, which demonstrated that they had less available bioiron even though they maintained proper iron storage [21]. Cytokines serve to diminish erythroid progenitor growth, which leads to decreased bone marrow responsiveness toward EPO [22, 23]. These elements, which include CRP levels and their relationship to low hematocrit values, and the increase in EPO dosage need created a positive relationship which we observed in our group study. The cohort showed high percentages of catheter‐based vascular access, which led to increased inflammatory burden as central venous catheters are recognized sources of chronic inflammation and infection compared with arteriovenous fistulas [24, 25].

Ferritin serves as a primary method for measuring iron body reserves yet functions as an acute‐phase protein which shows elevated levels during inflammatory conditions. In CKD patients, elevated inflammation may therefore mask functional iron deficiency which creates difficulties for managing anemia and leads to erythropoietin resistance through hepcidin‐mediated iron sequestration [26, 27]. Multivariable adjustment showed an independent association which increases the likelihood of proving that inflammation causes anemia through direct effects. Unlike purely descriptive findings, this suggests that CRP may serve not only as a marker but also as a potential therapeutic target [21, 24].

An important strength of our study is that it contributes original data from sub‐Saharan Africa, where literature is sparse regarding the interplay between inflammation, anemia, and EPO resistance in CKD patients. Most previous studies have been done in the Western hemisphere or Asia, where possible differences in genetic predisposition, nutritional status, burden of infections, or dialysis practices might affect outcomes [9, 28]. These results suggest that inflammation is one modifiable cause for hypo‐responsiveness to erythropoietin in a resource‐poor setting such as Kenya, where the cost of treatment with erythropoietin is the major impediment to the effective treatment of anemia. Since the CRP test is cheap and easy to obtain, it can serve as a biomarker to help manage anemia within this framework. The results certainly favor routine measurement of CRP on a clinical basis, so I see it serving as a nonspecific biomarker to help clinicians anticipate resistance to erythropoietin and to fine‐tune their treatment strategies. Targeted strategies to reduce inflammation, optimize iron utilization, and improve dialysis‐related factors may enhance erythropoietin responsiveness; however, interventional studies are required to confirm these approaches [29, 30]. Early detection of patients with inflammation‐related anemia may spare sufferers from the use of high erythropoietin doses that had been previously associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events and healthcare costs [31, 32]. The inclusion of CRP in the standard assessment of anemia may, therefore, improve outcomes and provide an economically viable protocol.

The present research results match previous research which demonstrates that IL‐6 controls hepcidin production to create a biological link between inflammation and functional iron shortage together with reduced ESA effectiveness [8, 9, 33, 34]. The existing results require future research through multicenter studies that study African populations because they need to establish their credibility through continuous research. The research on anti‐inflammatory methods and specific iron treatments should proceed through interventional studies to evaluate their impact on EPO response. Additional biomarkers should be included to identify the specific mechanisms through which inflammation causes anemia in CKD patients.

Limitations

The research design prevents researchers from determining causal relationships because the study included only participants from one center which restricts generalization of results. The study did not evaluate nutritional status and concomitant medications because these variables could potentially impact both inflammation and anemia assessment. The study used CRP as the sole measurement of inflammatory status. The research would have gained from including the cytokines IL‐6 and TNF‐α together with hepcidin as they provide important biological information. The research should be conducted through future studies which need to follow up with large study groups from different geographical areas to confirm these results and assess the sustained impacts of combined treatment methods. The study did not evaluate nutritional status indicators together with subclinical inflammation markers through serum albumin measurement and malnutrition–inflammation scores which could have affected the results for inflammatory and anemia‐related measurements.

Conclusions

The study shows that systemic inflammation in CKD patients who receive hemodialysis treatment leads to both worse anemia and higher erythropoietin needs. High CRP levels create a separate risk that leads to decreased hematocrit levels and creates difficulties for the body to use iron. The study design limits researchers from establishing causal links because it uses cross‐sectional research methods. The addition of inflammatory markers to standard clinical tests will enhance anemia treatment results, but researchers must conduct more longitudinal studies and interventional studies to confirm this finding.
