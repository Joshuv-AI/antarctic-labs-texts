Validation and psychometric properties of the Turkish version of the brief sense of community scale
Authors: Ali Huseyin Dogan, Huseyin Elbi, Fatih Ozcan, Omer Aydemir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to adapt the Brief Sense of Community Scale into Turkish and to evaluate its psychometric properties among an adult cohort.MethodThis study involved 313 volunteers aged 18 years or older. The scale’s linguistic and cultural adaptation was performed through forward and backward translation, an expert panel review, and a pilot test. Construct validity was assessed using confirmatory factor analysis; internal consistency was evaluated with Cronbach’s alpha, McDonald’s omega, item-total correlations, and the changes in reliability coefficients after item removal. For criterion and convergent validity, correlations were calculated with the psychological well-being, perceived social support, and quality-of-life scales.ResultsThe Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the Turkish version of the Brief Sense of Community Scale was 0.882, and the McDonald’s omega coefficient was 0.885. Item-total correlations ranged from 0.461 to 0.786, and removing any item did not significantly improve internal consistency. Confirmatory factor analysis showed that the four-dimensional structure of the scale (Needs Fulfillment, Group Membership, Influence, and Emotional Connection) fit the data well (CFI = 0.985; TLI = 0.970; RMSEA = 0.069; SRMR = 0.026). Additionally, the Composite Reliability (CR) was 0.936, and the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) was 0.648. The total BSCS score showed moderate positive correlations with psychological well-being (r = 0.420; p < 0.001) and quality of life (r = 0.419; p < 0.001), and a weaker positive correlation with perceived social support (r = 0.238; p < 0.001). In a further comparison by recruitment source, the BSCS total and sub-dimension scores did not differ significantly between participants from psychiatry outpatient clinics and family medicine clinics.ConclusionThese findings demonstrate that the Brief Sense of Community Scale is a valid and reliable measure of sense of community among Turkish adults. The scale’s concise, practical structure supports its use in community-based research and in mental health practices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04852-4.Keywords: Sense of Community, Validity, Reliability

Introduction

Community feeling is a psychosocial concept that encompasses individuals’ belonging to a group, their essential place within it, and the emotional bonds they form with group members. This concept contributes to the formation of sustainable community structures by strengthening individuals’ sense of belonging and increasing social solidarity [1]. According to the theory developed by McMillan and Chavis, community feeling consists of four basic dimensions: membership, needs fulfillment, influence, and shared emotional bonds [2]. Membership refers to individuals’ sense of belonging to the community; needs fulfillment refers to individuals’ access to resources stemming from their community membership. Influence encompasses the impact of individuals on the community and the impact of the community on individuals [3]. Finally, shared emotional bonds refer to bonds among community members based on a common history, experience, and emotion [2, 3, 4].

The sense of community is a fundamental social construct that supports individuals’ mental and psychological well-being; it is associated with lower depression, anxiety, and stress, and with stronger perceptions of social support, life satisfaction, civic participation, and overall health [5, 6, 7]. A strong sense of community has been shown to improve stress management skills, reduce loneliness, strengthen social relationships, and promote social cohesion [5, 8, 9]. Studies in Japan and Slovenia show that this concept remains valid across diverse socio-cultural contexts and functions as a universal element supporting public health [10, 11]. In this context, systematically measuring the sense of community is critically important for developing interventions to improve well-being at both the individual and societal levels [12, 13].

Various measurement tools have been developed in the literature to assess sense of community; one of the most widely used is the Sense of Community Index (SCI), based on McMillan and Chavis’ theoretical model. Although the SCI is designed to assess the core dimensions of sense of community, limitations have emerged due to inconsistent validation of its factor structure across samples and contexts, the specificity of some items to certain community types, and its lengthy administration [1, 14]. In light of these limitations, alternative tools for measuring sense of community have been developed; among these, the Sense of Community Coherence scale, which focuses on the internal consistency of community experience, has been proposed [15, 16]. However, these tools have been found to be unable to address the theoretical components of sense of community (membership, influence, fulfillment of needs, emotional connection) in a multidimensional way. In this context, the Brief Sense of Community Scale (BSCS) stands out as a tool designed to directly assess the dimensions defined in the McMillan and Chavis model, with a concise, theoretically structured set of items [14]. Given that the BSCS was initially developed in the North American context and psychometrically evaluated primarily in samples exhibiting individualistic sociocultural characteristics, and considering community feeling as a psychosocial construct sensitive to cultural and social context, a methodological reassessment of the scale in different languages and cultures is necessary, not only through translation but also within the framework of conceptual equivalence and cultural appropriateness [14, 17].

Studies across diverse cultural contexts demonstrate that the BSCS exhibits acceptable psychometric properties, including construct validity and internal consistency, and that the scale is applicable across various community settings [14, 17]. However, because the sense of community is sensitive to cultural and contextual factors, it is methodologically recommended that measurement tools be adapted to the relevant language and cultural context and that their psychometric properties be re-evaluated rather than used directly across different societies [10, 17]. Although adaptation studies of the BSCS have been conducted in various countries, evidence for a culturally adapted version with comprehensively evaluated psychometric properties in a Turkish-speaking adult population is limited. Accordingly, this study aims to examine the Turkish version of the BSCS within the framework of cultural adaptation principles and to evaluate the scale’s psychometric properties in a Turkish-speaking adult sample, focusing on its relationships with theoretically related psychosocial indicators.

Method

Study design and research sample

This study is a methodological research project involving scale adaptation and psychometric evaluation. The study sample consisted of individuals aged 18 years or older who applied to the Family Medicine and Psychiatry outpatient clinics at Manisa Celal Bayar University Hospital and the Family Health Centers, and who volunteered to participate. Participants were selected using convenience sampling, a commonly used nonprobability sampling method that provides practical access to the target population [18]. A total of 313 participants responded at the end of data collection. Because all participants completed all scale items during face-to-face data collection, no participants were excluded for incomplete data. Outlier analysis was performed to examine the distribution of total scores; no statistically significant outliers were identified. The total scores followed a normal distribution. The sample size for this methodological study was planned according to the principle of determining the minimum number of participants, with at least 10 times the number of scale items [19]. Because this study is a validity and reliability study, no additional statistical records were kept for individuals who refused to participate. 52 participants (16.6%) were included from the psychiatry outpatient clinic, and 261 (83.4%) were included from the family medicine outpatient clinic.

Data collection

The Demographic Information Form collects participants’ sociodemographic characteristics, including age, gender, marital status, education level, and place of residence.

Brief Sense of Community Scale (BSCS)

The long form of the Sense of Community Scale (SCI), developed to assess the dimensions of needs met, group membership, influence, and emotional connection as defined in McMillan and Chavis’ theoretical model, consists of 24 items. In this study, the Turkish adaptation and psychometric properties of the 8-item Short Sense of Community Scale (BSCS) were evaluated using the same theoretical framework. The BSCS items refer to the respondent’s living environment and use a 5-point Likert-type response format ranging from ‘strongly agree’ to ‘strongly disagree’. The BSCS demonstrated strong internal consistency in the original study. Cronbach’s alpha for the overall BSCS was 0.92. Alpha values for the subscales were 0.86 for needs fulfillment, 0.94 for group membership, 0.77 for influence, and 0.87 for emotional connection. Confirmatory factor analysis provided evidence for the structural validity of the measure by supporting the first-order and second-order constructs of the BSCS. In addition, to further support the measure’s structural validity, the general BSCS and its subscales were associated with community participation, psychological empowerment, mental health, and depression, as expected [14].

Psychological well-being scale

The eight-item Psychological Well-Being Scale captures essential elements of human functioning, ranging from positive relationships and feelings of competence to a meaningful and purposeful life. Diener et al. initially named the scale the Psychological Well-Being Scale. However, they later renamed it the “Flourishing Scale,” which they believed more accurately reflected the content of well-being [20]. In this study, because a precise Turkish equivalent for the word “Flourishing” could not be found, the scale was named the “Psychological Well-Being Scale.” The items of the Psychological Well-being Scale are answered on a scale of 1–7, ranging from strongly disagree [1] to strongly agree [7]. All items are expressed positively. Scores range from 8 to 56. A high score indicates that the person has many psychological resources and strengths. The Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient for the scale was 0.87 [21]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha was calculated as 0.861.

The revised form of the Multidimensional Perceived Social Support Scale (MSPSS)

The scale was developed in 1988 to assess the elements of social support perceived by individuals [22]. The revised version of the original Turkish scale was used in the validity and reliability study by Eker et al. The scale comprises three subscales, each with four items: family, friend, and significant other support. It consists of 12 items, with a minimum score of 12 and a maximum of 84. A higher score indicates greater perceived social support. For this research, the scale’s Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated as 0.92 [23]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.889.

EUROHIS-QOL (World Health Organization Quality of Life Questionnaire)

The shortest general-purpose index derived from the World Health Organization Quality of Life Scale (WHOQOL-Bref) is the Quality of Life in Health (QHL) scale. It consists of 8 questions selected from the WHOQOL using a specific methodology. Two of these questions are general health and general quality-of-life questions from WHOQOL, and the remaining 6 questions are from the physical, mental, social, and environmental dimensions. The response options are a 5-point Likert scale. The extreme response options are “none” and “completely”. As the score increases, quality of life also improves. In the study titled “Psychometric Properties of the Turkish Version”, the Cronbach Alpha coefficient was 0.85 [24, 25]. In this study, the Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient was 0.867.

Language and culture adaptation process

The adaptation of the scale into Turkish was carried out in accordance with international scale adaptation guidelines, encompassing not only linguistic but also cultural and conceptual equivalence [26]. Considering that the BSCS was initially developed in the North American context and predominantly in samples with individualistic socio-cultural characteristics, the adaptation process was planned from a comparative cultural perspective. Using a back-and-forth translation method, the scale was translated into Turkish by two independent translators; a single form was created through consensus; and this form was then retranslated into English by two independent translators whose native language is English and who are fluent in Turkish. The back-translation text was evaluated by comparing it with the original text not only for lexical similarity but also for conceptual content and contextual meaning. Furthermore, the back-translation form was sent to the original developer of the scale; based on the feedback received, necessary adjustments were made to strengthen conceptual equivalence, and the final version of the Turkish form was created. Linguistic and cultural suitability was reviewed by a panel of academics specializing in family medicine, psychiatry, and measurement and evaluation, who were familiar with the scale’s theoretical framework and both cultural contexts. Specifically, the dimensions of membership, influence, needs fulfillment, and emotional connection were discussed in relation to their counterparts within the Turkish social context. Following revisions based on expert opinions, the scale was piloted on 20 participants; the internal consistency coefficient was found to be high in the pilot study (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.862), the comprehensibility and cultural appropriateness of the items were re-evaluated, and since no significant wording problems were reported, the Turkish form was finalized for the main study.

Application and statistical analyses

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS and Jamovi. Participants’ sociodemographic characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage).

The scale’s internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega. Furthermore, the scale was assessed using the rule that removing any item should not significantly increase the alpha or omega coefficients, indicating that the existing item structure is suitable for internal consistency [27, 28]. Item-total scale correlations were also calculated.

The theoretical structure of BSCS was tested using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) based on McMillan and Chavis’ four-factor structure of community feeling. Analyses were conducted using a first-order factor model; model fit was evaluated using the comparative fit index (CFI), the Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), the standardized root mean square error (SRMR), and the root mean square error approximation (RMSEA). 90% confidence intervals were reported for RMSEA; in addition, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayes Information Criterion (BIC) values were presented to evaluate model fit in terms of parsimony. Because the first-order model showed a strong fit and it was theoretically more meaningful to treat the sub-dimensions as separate but related structures, a second-order model was not preferred [29]. In the literature, CFI and TLI values above 0.95 are considered “excellent fit,” and values above 0.90 are considered “acceptable fit.” In contrast, RMSEA values below 0.06 are interpreted as “perfect fit,” and values below 0.08 as “acceptable fit” [30]. In addition, Composite Reliability (CR) values above 0.70, and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values above 0.50 are considered sufficient evidence of internal consistency and convergent validity [31].

Within the scope of criterion and construct validity, the relationships between the BSCS total and subscale scores and the psychological well-being, perceived social support, and quality of life scales were examined using the Pearson correlation coefficient. For known-groups validity, differences in BSCS scores across sociodemographic variables were evaluated using parametric or nonparametric tests appropriate to the variables’ distributions. Furthermore, participants from the psychiatry and family medicine outpatient clinics were compared on BSCS total and subscale scores, as well as other scale total scores.

Results

A total of 313 participants were included in the study. By age, 58.8% were under 40 years, 29.7% were 40–60 years, and 11.5% were 60 years or older. 61.7% of participants were actively employed, while 38.3% were unemployed. Regarding income, 48.6% reported that their income matched their expenses, 28.4% reported that it was less than their expenses, and 23% reported that it exceeded their expenses. University graduates constituted the largest group (44.4%), followed by high school graduates (30.4%). 67.1% of participants reported not attending a cultural event weekly, while the remaining 32.9% did (Table 1). Participants from the psychiatry and family medicine outpatient clinics were also compared on the main study variables. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in the total BSCS score and sub-dimension scores. Similarly, psychological well-being and perceived social support scores did not differ significantly between the groups. However, the EUROHIS-QOL score was significantly lower in the psychiatry outpatient clinic group compared to the family medicine group (p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 1).

Scale distribution characteristics and reliability analysis

Internal consistency

Item mean scores and standard deviations for the Brief Sense of Community Scale are presented in Table 2. Cronbach’s alpha for the BSCS was 0.882, and McDonald’s omega was 0.885. Item-total correlations ranged from 0.461 to 0.786, and all were significant (p < 0.001). No significant increase in the alpha coefficient was observed when any item was removed, indicating that it is appropriate to retain all items in the scale. Reliability values (Cronbach alpha, McDonald omega) for the sub-dimensions of the BSCS scale were also calculated (0.794, 0.794 for Needs Fulfillment; 0.900, 0.901 for Group Membership; 0.714, 0.720 for Influence; and 0.727, 0.731 for Emotional Connection, respectively).

Construct validity: Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA)

Confirmatory factor analysis was used to test the theoretical structure of the BSCS. Before the analysis, the sample’s suitability for factor analysis was assessed using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) coefficient and Bartlett’s test of sphericity; the KMO value was 0.848, and the test was statistically significant (p < 0.001). These results indicate that the data are suitable for factor analysis. CFA was conducted using a four-dimensional model (Needs Fulfillment [BSCS1– BSCS2], Group Membership [BSCS3– BSCS4], Influence [BSCS5– BSCS6], and Emotional Connection [BSCS7– BSCS8]), defined within McMillan and Chavis’s community feeling theoretical framework. Model fit was evaluated using the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), and root mean square error (RMSEA) (Fig. 1).

BSCS Path Diagram Model

The analysis results showed that the four-factor model fit the data well (χ²/df = 35.0/14; CFI = 0.985; TLI = 0.970; RMSEA = 0.069; 90% confidence interval = 0.041–0.098; SRMR = 0.026). CFI and TLI values above 0.95 indicate a good fit, while RMSEA values below 0.08 and SRMR values below 0.03 indicate a near-perfect fit. The standardized loadings of all items on their respective factors were statistically significant and above acceptable thresholds. These findings support the four-dimensional theoretical structure of the BSCS in the Turkish sample and provide evidence supporting the scale’s structural validity. Additionally, the Composite Reliability (CR) was 0.936, and the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) was 0.648 (Table 3).

Concurrent validity analysis

To evaluate criterion validity and support construct validity of the BSCS, Pearson correlation coefficients were used to examine relationships between BSCS scores and psychological well-being (PWW), perceived social support (PSSS), and quality of life (EUROHIS-QOL). The total BSCS score showed moderate positive correlations with psychological well-being (r = 0.420; p < 0.001) and quality of life (r = 0.419; p < 0.001). The correlation with perceived social support was positive, but of smaller magnitude (r = 0.238; p < 0.001). At the sub-dimension level, the pattern was similar; all sub-dimensions were positively related to psychological well-being and quality of life. These findings indicate that sense of community showed statistically significant positive correlations with psychological well-being and quality of life, while its correlation with perceived social support was positive but weaker (Table 4).

BSCS score distributions according to sociodemographic variables

BSCS total and sub-dimension scores were compared across sociodemographic variables. In the gender-based analysis, only the Emotional Connection sub-dimension showed a higher mean score for men than for women (p = 0.033). No significant differences were found in the other sub-dimensions or the total score.

Analysis by income level revealed significant differences in the " Needs Fulfillment” sub-dimension and the total score (p = 0.001). In comparisons by education level, statistically significant differences were found between groups in all sub-dimensions except " Needs Fulfillment” and in the total score.

When educational status was evaluated across three groups (middle school and pre-middle school, high school, and university graduates), University graduates scored higher on the Group Membership, Influence, and Emotional Connection sub-dimensions and on the total scale score than individuals with a middle school or pre-middle school education (p = 0.012; p = 0.006; p < 0.001; p = 0.017, respectively) (Table 5).

Discussion

Community feeling is a fundamental psychosocial construct that significantly affects individuals’ mental health, social adjustment, and life satisfaction [1, 2]. A sense of belonging, mutual bonds among members, and shared goals are protective factors, especially in the face of challenges such as alienation and social isolation brought about by modern life [2, 32]. To accurately assess the critical role of community feeling in individual and social health in the Turkish context, psychometrically robust and culturally validated measurement tools specific to this construct are needed. This study has shown that the Brief Sense of Community Scale (BSCS) meets these requirements and is a potentially useful, valid, and reliable measure within the sample.

In this study, the internal consistency of the BSCS was evaluated using both Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega. The close values for the overall scale, Cronbach’s alpha (α = 0.882) and McDonald’s omega (ω = 0.885), indicate that item balance is maintained despite the scale’s short-form structure and that internal consistency is strongly supported without relying solely on alpha-based assumptions [27, 28]. In the original study by Peterson et al., with a sample of neighborhood residents in the USA, the overall Cronbach’s alpha was reported as α = 0.92 [14]. In a different sample, Yu et al. reported a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.91 for the overall BSCS scores in the Japanese adaptation of the scale [10]. Sočan and Kobal Grum reported a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.901 for the total score in their adaptation study in Slovenia [11]. Similar results have been reported across different age and ethnic groups; Lardier et al. reported a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89 for the overall scale in their study of Hispanic/Latin adolescents in the USA [33]. In addition, Buckley et al. found an overall internal consistency of α = 0.85 in their study of older adults in Puerto Rico [34]. This consistency suggests that the scale items function similarly across different age groups, cultural contexts, and living environments, and that the measured theoretical construct is relatively stable. Furthermore, the sufficient level of item-total correlations and the fact that removing any item does not significantly affect reliability support the appropriateness of maintaining the short-form structure.

The results showed that the Brief Sense of Community Scale retains its original theoretical structure in the Turkish sample, as confirmed by Confirmatory Factor Analysis assessing its construct validity. The McMillan and Chavis model, comprising four core dimensions (Needs Fulfillment, Group Membership, Influence, and Emotional Connection), fits the data from our study well [14]. In this context, the fit indices indicate that the BSCS provides a statistically adequate representation of the theoretical framework in the Turkish sample.

The validation of the scale’s four-factor structure is consistent with the literature. In the scale’s original development study, Peterson et al. reported that the second-level four-factor model fit the data very well [14]. Similarly, Buckley et al. reported excellent fit for the second-level four-factor model in their study of Spanish-speaking older adults in Puerto Rico [34]. This structure is also preserved in US-based studies across different subgroups; a study with Black and Hispanic university students yielded excellent fit indices [33], while a study with Black and Hispanic youth living in disadvantaged urban areas reported a perfect fit [35]. Furthermore, a study with LGB individuals found that the second-level model showed acceptable fit [36]. Similar results have been reported in studies conducted in an Asian context; in the Japanese adaptation by Yu et al., the fit indices of the second-level model were within acceptable limits [10]. Taken together, these findings indicate that the four-dimensional theoretical structure of BSCS is also valid for the Turkish sample and that the results strongly align with the international literature.

In contrast to this general trend, the Slovenian adaptation of this scale shows a notable discrepancy with the literature on construct validity [11]. In their study, the authors reported that the four-factor model did not show acceptable fit, particularly with respect to the RMSEA value [11]. Given the high correlations among the sub-dimensions, the authors reported that a single-factor model with autoregressive effects fit the data significantly better [11]. The authors interpreted this as evidence that a one-dimensional structure is more frequently observed in scale adaptations in cultures with collectivist values, citing China [37], Korea [38], and Vietnam [39] as examples of this trend. In contrast, they stated that the four-factor structure was more supported in individualistic cultural contexts, such as the United States [35] and Germany [40]. In this study, the four-factor model’s good fit indices suggest that the theoretical structure of the BSCS is statistically suitable for the Turkiye sample and that the components that form the sense of community can be experienced as distinct yet related dimensions rather than a single, holistic structure that interacts with the cultural context.

Findings on criterion and construct validity showed that the total BSCS score exhibited moderate positive correlations with the Psychological Well-being Scale and the EUROHIS-QOL Quality of Life Questionnaire. These findings align with the literature, which emphasizes that sense of community is a crucial resource that can contribute to psychosocial well-being through the relationships of belonging, trust, and reciprocity an individual establishes with their social environment [1, 34, 17]. Indeed, a study of older adults in Hong Kong reported that sense of community, as measured by the BSCS, was positively correlated with self-reported health status [17]. In contrast, another study of LGB individuals in the United States found that BSCS scores were negatively correlated with depression and loneliness [36]. This pattern demonstrates that the BSCS can establish meaningful relationships with theoretically related constructs such as quality of life and psychological well-being, supporting the scale’s criterion validity.

In contrast, the positive and significant correlation between the BSCS and the Multidimensional Perceived Social Support Scale is lower than correlations with other scales, indicating that sense of community and perceived social support are conceptually related yet distinct constructs. Perceived social support primarily reflects the tangible and emotional help an individual receives from close relationships (family, friends, or a significant other) [23], whereas sense of community, as defined by McMillan and Chavis [2], is a more holistic, contextual construct that encompasses belonging, perceived influence, belief in the fulfillment of needs, and shared emotional bonds with a broader collective (such as a neighborhood, city, or interest group). This difference in scope and reference level may explain the lower correlation between perceived social support and sense of community. Indeed, previous studies examining the relationship between perceived social support and sense of community have emphasized that these two constructs represent complementary but non-overlapping psychosocial dimensions [34]. In this context, the relatively lower relationship between perceived social support and sense of community may reflect not a weakness but rather the fact that social support is a construct focused on the individual’s network of close relationships. In contrast, sense of community, as McMillan and Chavis [2] also stated, is a different construct that reflects the individual’s perceptions of belonging and reciprocity toward a broader, contextual collective. While social support focuses on the individual’s network of close relationships, sense of community reflects a wider, collective context. This finding can be interpreted as BSCS clearly measuring this conceptual distinction.

Further comparison by recruitment source showed that BSCS total and subscale scores did not differ significantly between participants from the psychiatry and family medicine outpatient clinics. This finding suggests that community feeling scores are similarly distributed across the two referral sources in the current sample. In contrast, the quality-of-life score was lower in the psychiatry outpatient clinic group, consistent with studies reporting that mental symptom burden may be related to health-related quality of life [41, 42]. Because this comparison is descriptive, it should not be interpreted as evidence of measurement invariance or psychometric equivalence between clinical groups.

Differences were observed in the validity analyses of known groups across sociodemographic variables. This finding aligns with previous studies emphasizing that the sense of community should be considered not only a cultural feature but also a multidimensional structure shaped by individual and contextual variables [1, 34]. Indeed, the subdimensions of the sense of community can vary across demographic characteristics, age groups, and socioeconomic conditions [17]. In this context, the findings suggest that the sense of community is not a uniform or homogeneous feature; it is interpreted differently across individuals’ social positions, living conditions, and environmental contexts.

When comparing by gender, no significant differences were found in the total scale score or in the Needs Fulfillment, Group Membership, and Influence sub-dimensions, suggesting that the sense of community is experienced similarly across genders on these sub-dimensions. This finding aligns with studies in diverse samples reporting no significant gender differences in the BSCS factor structure [33, 36, 43]. In contrast, males scored higher than females on the Emotional Connection sub-dimension, suggesting that some aspects of community feeling may be experienced differently across gender roles. Similarly, Lardier et al. reported that Hispanic/Latin male adolescents exhibited higher factor loadings on items related to “belonging” and “connection,” which they attributed to leadership and protective roles assigned to males [33]. In this context, the current finding suggests that gender roles may differentially shape emotional bonds formed with the neighborhood and social environment.

Individuals aged 60 and older scored higher on the Emotional Connection dimension than younger age groups, suggesting that emotional bonds with the local environment may strengthen with age. This result aligns with the study by Peterson et al. [14], which found a positive relationship between age and sense of community, and with the similar trend reported by Yu et al. in their study of older adults in Hong Kong [17]. Factors such as withdrawal from working life, increased time spent in the neighborhood, and continuity of residence, along with age, strengthen the emotional components of the sense of community.

Participants with higher incomes scored higher, particularly on the Needs Fulfillment sub-dimension and the total scale score, suggesting that socioeconomic status may be associated with perceptions of effectiveness and need fulfillment within the community. Similarly, Yu et al. reported that individuals with higher incomes exhibited a greater sense of community [17]. It is believed that socioeconomic status should be evaluated alongside individuals’ opportunities for social participation, the characteristics of the living environment, and social network structures, and that perceptions of community should be assessed within this framework.

Regarding educational status, studies show that university graduates scored significantly lower on “Group Membership,” “Influence,” “Emotional Connection,” and the total scale score. In the study by Yu et al., a significant positive relationship was found between educational level and the “Needs Fulfillment” sub-dimension of community feeling [10]. When higher education is considered in the context of individuals’ access to non-local professional and interest-based networks and their career-related spatial mobility, the patterns of change and interaction stemming from this social phenomenon should be examined through a social network lens. This situation may necessitate a more cautious interpretation of identity and belonging among highly educated individuals. This suggests that it may be appropriate to examine such forms of mobility and interaction together with differing patterns of social participation at local, national, and global levels [44, 45]. A study in Argentina directly supports this finding, reporting that individuals with high education and economic status socialize less in neighborhood settings than those with low status [46]. Sociological research in Turkiye also confirms that the segment with the most pronounced individualization tendencies is that with high education and socioeconomic status [47]. The long educational and career trajectories of university graduates may make it difficult for them to establish deep ties to a particular living space or local community, thereby increasing individualism. These findings may indicate a situation that runs counter to the common belief that education always leads to greater social integration.

The study found that individuals who participated in cultural activities at least once a week scored higher on the Needs Fulfillment sub-dimension, underscoring the importance of social participation for the functional components of community feeling. This finding aligns with Kawase and Eguchi’s study in Japan, which found that activities involving active, physical participation, such as group dancing at local festivals, were associated with a stronger sense of community than passive involvement in the festival or activities that did not involve dancing [48]. Therefore, regular social and cultural activities in the living environment can support a sense of community by meeting individuals’ needs.

In the study, individuals who participated in cultural activities at least once a week scored higher on the Needs Meeting sub-dimension. This finding suggests that social participation may be related to the functional components of community feeling. The findings are consistent with Kawase and Eguchi’s observations in a Japanese sample, which reported that activities involving active participation, such as group dancing at local festivals, are associated with a stronger sense of community [48]. However, income may be a confounding and/or moderating variable in this relationship, as participation in cultural activities may vary with socioeconomic resources and access. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously in this context [49, 50].

On the other hand, the finding that individuals living alone scored higher on the Needs Fulfillment sub-dimension in the study may reflect this group’s more deliberate reliance on external environments and community resources to meet their individual and social needs. This finding also aligns with previous studies reporting that individuals living alone tend to interact more with neighborhoods, neighbors, and local social networks to meet the needs they encounter in daily life [51]. Therefore, living alone may be a contextual factor that strengthens bonds with the community, especially in the Needs Fulfillment dimension, rather than one that weakens them.

Implications

This study demonstrates that the Brief Sense of Community is a practical and psychometrically robust measure in a Turkish sample. The short-form structure of the scale offers significant practical advantages for community-based research, primary care and mental health services, monitoring social cohesion and the risk of loneliness in old age, and evaluating interventions at the neighborhood and settlement levels. Using the Brief Sense of Community Scale alongside measures of psychological well-being, perceived social support, and quality of life enables a multidimensional assessment of the relationship between community sense and individual well-being and social relationships. This approach supports treating community sense not merely as a cultural characteristic but as a construct shaped by interactions with individual psychosocial functioning and the environmental context. From an application perspective, programs focused on social participation and local cohesion, aimed at strengthening community sense, may include functional components that contribute to individuals’ overall well-being.

Limitations and strengths

This study has certain limitations. First, the sample consisted of individuals from a single province and a specific healthcare facility, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Additionally, participants were recruited from both family medicine and psychiatry outpatient clinics, and some demographic differences were noted between these groups, particularly in gender distribution and average age. Although participants from psychiatry outpatient clinics and family medicine practices were compared, the study was not designed to compare these two referral groups or to test for measurement invariance. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive characterizations of the sample, not as evidence of psychometric equivalence or measurement invariance between clinical groups. Furthermore, because this study used a cross-sectional design, the relationships among sense of community, psychological well-being, perceived social support, and quality of life should not be interpreted as causal. Test-retest reliability was also not assessed in this study. Because the sense of community is considered a contextually sensitive and dynamic psychosocial trait rather than a fixed characteristic over time, establishing short-term temporal stability was not a primary psychometric goal. However, future research is encouraged to examine the scale’s stability across different time periods using longitudinal methods. Although the cultural context has been discussed in relation to existing literature, the lack of variables directly measuring cultural orientations, such as individualism-collectivism or local-cosmopolitan tendencies, may have limited the ability to more clearly test cross-cultural differences. Therefore, the observed patterns should be interpreted with caution, without attributing them solely to cultural factors.

However, these limitations are partially offset by complementary psychosocial measures that assess both individual and contextual expressions of community feeling. The Psychological Well-being Scale, the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support, and the EUROHIS-QOL Quality of Life Questionnaire, used alongside the BSCS, have contributed to a multidimensional approach to the indirect effects of cultural context by revealing how community feeling relates to subjective well-being, social network, and quality of life. This approach has enabled assessment of community feeling through its functional expressions across different socio-psychological domains, rather than by directly measuring culture.

Future research

Future research is recommended to use tools that measure cultural orientations, such as individualism-collectivism and localism-cosmopolitanism, in conjunction with the BSCS to examine the cultural dimensions of community feeling more directly and to use multicenter samples representing diverse sociocultural contexts. Furthermore, testing the scale’s measurement invariance across gender, age, and socioeconomic levels using multigroup confirmatory factor analysis, and examining patterns of change in community feeling over time in relation to psychological well-being and quality of life through longitudinal designs, will strengthen the BSCS’s theoretical and applied contributions. Measurement invariance (e.g., across age, gender, and education level) was not tested in this study. Therefore, differences in scores between sociodemographic groups should not be interpreted as evidence that the scale measures the same construct in all groups. It is recommended that future studies examine measurement invariance using multigroup confirmatory factor analysis.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the Brief Sense of Community Scale is a valid and reliable measure in a Turkish adult sample. Confirmatory Factor Analysis supported the scale’s four-dimensional theoretical structure, and expected correlations with psychological well-being and quality of life were observed. The lower correlation with perceived social support suggests that community sense may reflect belonging to a broader collective, conceptually distinct from perceived support from close relationships. The cultural context was addressed cautiously because direct measurements were unavailable; however, complementary psychosocial scales enabled a multidimensional assessment of individual and contextual reflections of community sense. In this respect, the Brief Sense of Community Scale offers a valuable practical and theoretical tool for community-based mental health research, social cohesion, and quality-of-life-focused assessments in Turkey. Future studies using cultural orientation measures and multicenter designs will further strengthen cross-cultural comparisons and the application of community sense.
