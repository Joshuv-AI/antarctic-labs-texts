Abstract

We prove a theorem giving a condition under which the dimension of the cohomology group of invertible sheaves on X X coincides with a natural lower bound. 1.
Introduction

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m},k)
be an excellent two-dimensional normal local domain containing an algebraically closed field isomorphic to the residue field
k
.
In this paper, we simply call such a local ring a
normal surface singularity
.
Let
X\to\spec(A)
be a resolution of the singularity with exceptional set
E=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}E_{i}
, where
E_{i}
are irreducible components.
We call a divisor
C
on
X
a cycle if
C
is supported in
E
, and write
\chi(C)=\chi(\mathcal{O}_{C})
.
It is known that many important invariants and properties are obtained from the cohomology of invertible sheaves on
X
.
There are various types of vanishing theorems for invertible sheaves on
X
and they play essential roles in the study of singularities. Laufer’s vanishing theorem (
[
8
]
), which is a generalization of the Grauert-Riemenschneider vanishing theorem (
[
4
]
), is one of the useful theorems and states that for an invertible sheaf
\mathcal{L}
on
X
, we have
H^{1}(\mathcal{L})=0
if
\mathcal{L}E_{i}\geq K_{X}E_{i}
for every
E_{i}\subset E
, where
K_{X}
denotes the canonical divisor on
X
.
Note that
K_{X}C=-2\chi(C)-C^{2}
by the Riemann-Roch formula, and
-C^{2}>0
for any cycle
C>0
.
Let
\mathcal{B}
denote the set of all cycles that appear in a computation sequence for the fundamental cycle
Z_{f}
(see. Section
2.1
).
Röhr
[
19
]
proved the following.

Theorem 1.1

(Röhr)
.

H^{1}(\mathcal{L})=0
if
\mathcal{L}C>-2\chi(C)
for every cycle
C\in\mathcal{B}
.

We have seen that Röhr’s vanishing theorem is very useful in the study of elliptic singularities and cone singularities (e.g.,
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
14
]
).
In the context of normal surface singularities, it is often important to determine the dimension of cohomology groups for invertible sheaves on resolution spaces, regardless of whether they vanish.
Motivated by this, we extend Röhr’s vanishing theorem under specific conditions.
For a divisor
L
on
X
, let
L^{\bot}=\bigcup_{LE_{i}=0}E_{i}
, and let
p_{g}(L^{\bot})
denote the sum of the geometric genus
of the singularities obtained by contracting
L^{\bot}
(see Section
2
).
If
\mathcal{O}_{X}(L)
has no fixed components, then
h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(L))\geq p_{g}(L^{\bot})
(see Lemma
2.12
).

Let
\mathcal{B}(L)^{e}
denote the set of all cycles
C>0
with the property that
C
has a decomposition
C=C_{1}+C_{2}
such that
C_{1}\in\mathcal{B}
,
C_{2}\geq 0
,
LC_{1}>0
,
LC_{2}=0
, and
C_{1}
is anti-nef on
C_{2}
(see Section
2.2
).
Then, we have the following.

Theorem 1.2

(see Theorem
3.4
)
.

Assume that
\mathcal{O}_{X}(L)
has no fixed components and that

LC>-2\chi(C)
for every
C\in\mathcal{B}(L)^{e}
.
Then
h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(L))=p_{g}(L^{\bot})
.

Note that
p_{g}(L^{\bot})=0
if and only if
L^{\bot}
contracts to rational singularities.
By Artin’s results
[
1
]
, the condition
p_{g}(L^{\bot})=0
is equivalent to
\chi(C)>0
for every
C>0
such that
\supp(C)\subset\supp(L^{\bot})
.
Since the cycle
C=C_{1}+C_{2}
in Theorem
1.2
satisfies
\chi(C)=\chi(C_{1})+\chi(C_{2})-C_{1}C_{2}\geq\chi(C_{1})+\chi(C_{2})
, Theorem
1.2
deduces Röhr’s vanishing theorem for
\mathcal{O}_{X}(L)
without fixed components.

In
[
17
]
, the authors introduced the
normal reduction number

\br
and proved its basic properties.
For any integrally closed
\mathfrak{m}
-primary ideal
I\subset A
and
its minimal reduction
Q
, we put

\displaystyle\br(I)

\displaystyle=\min{\left\{r\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\,\left|\,\overline{I^{n+1}}=Q\overline{I^{n}}\;\text{for all $n\geq r$}\right.\right\}},

\displaystyle\br(A)

\displaystyle=\max{\left\{\br(I)\,\left|\,I\subset A:\text{ integrally closed $\mathfrak{m}$-primary ideal}\right.\right\}}.

These invariants can also be expressed in terms of cohomology (see Proposition
4.4
).
We will apply Theorem
1.2
to obtain upper bounds for the normal reduction number of surface singularities (see Theorem
4.7
).

By
[
17
, §2.1]
, we have
\br(A)\leq p_{g}(A)+1
, where
p_{g}(A)=\dim_{k}H^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X})
, the geometric genus of
A
.
We expect that the normal reduction number may play an important role as a fundamental invariant of normal surface singularities. For example, we see that
A
is a rational singularity if and only if
\br(A)=1
.
However, computing the normal reduction number has been difficult, and no good combinatorial bounds have been known; here, an invariant of
A
is said to be
combinatorial
(or,
topological
) if it is determined by the resolution graph of
A
.
Recall that the fundamental genus
p_{f}(A)=1+Z_{f}(Z_{f}+K_{X})/2
and the arithmetic genus
p_{a}(A)=\max{\left\{1-\chi(C)\,\left|\,\text{$C>0$ is a cycle on $X$}\right.\right\}}
are
very fundamental combinatorial invariants of
A
and satisfy
p_{f}(A)\leq p_{a}(A)\leq p_{g}(A)
.
In general, the differences

p_{g}(A)-p_{a}(A)
and
p_{a}(A)-p_{f}(A)
can take various positive integers; for example,
if
A=k[[x,y,z]]/(f)
,
f
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
, then
p_{f}(A)=(d-1)(d-2)/2
,
p_{a}(A)=1+du(d-u-2)/2
, where
u=\left\lfloor d/2\right\rfloor-1
, and
p_{g}(A)=d(d-1)(d-2)/6
.
Moreover,
for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, there exists a singularity
A
with
p_{a}(A)=1
and
p_{g}(A)=n
(cf. Example
2.2
).
Our arguments in the proof of Theorem
1.2
provides an alternative proof of the following.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
10
]
(see Theorem
4.9
))
.

\br(A)\leq p_{a}(A)+1
.

This theorem immediately implies that
\br(A)=2
if
A
is an elliptic singularity, namely, if
p_{a}(A)=1
(cf.
[
12
, Theorem 3.3]
).
We provide a counter-example to the converse of this result (Example
6.2
).
Consequently, we obtain partial answers to
[
13
, Problem 3.1-3.2]
.

We introduce almost cone singularities (Section
5
), which generalize cone singularities, and prove the following.

Theorem 1.4

(see Theorem
5.14
)
.

Let
A
be an almost cone singularity.
Then
\br(A)\leq p_{f}(A)+1
.
Note that
p_{f}(A)
coincides with the genus of the central curve.

This paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we summarize the terminology and basic notions concerning cycles on a resolution of singularities.
This also includes a brief overview of the chain-connected component decomposition of cycles introduced by Konno
[
5
]
, which serves as a preparation for Theorem
1.2
.
In Section
3
, we prove Theorem
1.2
.
To this end, we consider the contraction
f\colon X\to Y
of
L^{\bot}
and reduce the problem to the vanishing of the cohomology of
f_{*}L
on the normal surface
Y
.
We introduce a pull-back
f^{+}W
of an arbitrary cycle
W
on
Y
, and reformulate the condition for the vanishing in terms of
f^{+}
in Theorem
1.2
.
In Section
4
, we apply the results in Section
3
to establish upper bounds of the normal reduction number (Theorem
4.7
, Theorem
4.9
).
In Section
5
, we introduce the notion of
almost cone singularities
and prove Theorem
1.4
.
In Section
6
, we prove that
\br(A)=2
for a non-elliptic hypersurface singularity (Example
6.2
), and show that there exist cone singularities with the same resolution graph but different normal reduction number (Example
6.4
). Then it follows that
\br(A)
is not a combinatorial invariant in general.
From the results mentioned above, it is natural to ask if
\br(A)\leq p_{f}(A)+1
holds in general.
We also give an explicit example to show that this inequality does not hold in general (Example
6.6
).

2.
Preliminaries

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m},k)
be a
normal surface singularity
, namely, an excellent two-dimensional normal local ring containing an algebraically closed field isomorphic to the residue field
k
.
Let
\pi\colon X\to\spec(A)
be a resolution of singularities with exceptional
set
E:=\pi^{-1}(\mathfrak{m})
and let
E=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}E_{i}
be the decomposition into the irreducible components.
A divisor on a resolution space whose support is contained in the exceptional set is called a
cycle
.
Let
\mathcal{E}
denote the set of all cycles on
X
and
\mathcal{E}^{+}
the set of all positive cycles;

\mathcal{E}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Z}E_{i},\quad\mathcal{E}^{+}={\left\{C\in\mathcal{E}\,\left|\,C>0\right.\right\}}.

The support of a divisor
D
is denoted by
\supp(D)
.
We say that a divisor
L
on
X
is
nef
on a cycle
C>0
if
LE_{i}\geq 0
for all
E_{i}\subset\supp(C)
; we simply say that
L
is
nef
if it is nef on
E
.
The divisor
L
is said to be
anti-nef
(on
C
) if
-L
is nef (on
C
).
It is known that the set

{\left\{Z\in\mathcal{E}^{+}\,\left|\,\text{$\supp(Z)=\supp(C)$ and $Z$ is anti-nef on $C$}\right.\right\}}

has a minimum, which is called the
fundamental cycle on
C
; in case that
C=E
, we simply call it the
fundamental cycle
and denote it by
Z_{f}
.
A cycle
F>0
is called the
fixed part
of the divisor
L
or the invertible sheaf
\mathcal{O}_{X}(L)
on
X
if
F
is the maximal cycle such that
H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(L-F))=H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(L))
.
An irreducible component of the fixed part
F
is called a
fixed component
.
For example, if
C>0
is a cycle, then
-C
has no fixed components if and only if there exists an element
h\in H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(-C))
such that
\di_{X}(h)=C+H
, where
H
does not contain any component of
E
;
if this is the case, we have that for a cycle
W>0
with
CW=0
,

(2.1)

\mathcal{O}_{W}(-C)\cong\mathcal{O}_{W}(H)=\mathcal{O}_{W}.

For a cycle
C\in\mathcal{E}^{+}
, we regard it as a scheme with structure sheaf
\mathcal{O}_{C}:=\mathcal{O}_{X}/\mathcal{O}_{X}(-C)
, and write

\chi(C)=\chi(\mathcal{O}_{C}):=h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{C})-h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{C}),

where
h^{i}(\mathcal{F})
denotes
\dim_{k}H^{i}(\mathcal{F})
.
We define the arithmetic genus
p_{a}(C)
of
C
by

p_{a}(C)=1-\chi(C).

By the Riemann-Roch formula, we have
\chi(C)=-C(C+K_{X})/2
, where
K_{X}
is the canonical divisor on
X
, and thus we obtain

(2.2)

\chi(C+C^{\prime})=\chi(C)+\chi(C^{\prime})-CC^{\prime}.

We define the
fundamental genus

p_{f}(A)
of
A
by
p_{f}(A)=p_{a}(Z_{f})
,
the
arithmetic genus

p_{a}(A)
of
A
by
p_{a}(A)=\max{\left\{p_{a}(C)\,\left|\,C\in\mathcal{E}^{+}\right.\right\}}
,
and the
geometric genus

p_{g}(A)
of
A
by
p_{g}(A)=h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X})
.
These three genera are independent of the choice of resolution.
While
p_{f}(A)
and
p_{a}(A)
are combinatorial invariants,

p_{g}(A)
cannot be determined by the resolution graph in general.
By the formal function theorem,
p_{g}(A)=\max{\left\{h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{C})\,\left|\,C\in\mathcal{E}^{+}\right.\right\}}
.
It is known that

(2.3)

p_{f}(A)\leq p_{a}(A)\leq p_{g}(A).

Definition 2.1
.

The singularity

is called a

, and an

.

It is known that

is rational if and only if

(see
[

]
) and that

is elliptic if and only if

(see
[

]
,
[

]
).

2.1.
The CCC decomposition

We will provide a brief overview of the chain-connected component decomposition of cycles introduced by Konno
[
5
]
.

It is known that the fundamental cycle
Z_{f}
can be computed
via a sequence
\{C_{1},\dots,C_{m}\}
of cycles such that

C_{1}=E_{j_{1}},\quad C_{i}=C_{i-1}+E_{j_{i}}\ (1<i\leq m),\quad C_{m}=Z_{f},

where
E_{j_{1}}
is an arbitrary component of
E
and
C_{i-1}E_{j_{i}}>0

for
1<i\leq m
.
Such a sequence
\{C_{i}\}_{i}
is called a

computation sequence
for
Z_{f}
.
Let
\mathcal{B}
denote the set of all cycles appearing in a computation sequence for
Z_{f}
.
Then we have the following (see
[
9
, (2.6), (2.7)]
):

(2.4)

h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{C})=1,\quad p_{a}(C)\leq p_{f}(A)\quad\text{for $C\in\mathcal{B}$}.

Definition 2.3

(
[
5
, 1.3, 1.6]
)
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{E}^{+}
.
Then
D
is said to be
chain-connected
if there does not exist
D_{1}\in\mathcal{E}^{+}
such that
0<D_{1}<D
and
D_{1}
is anti-nef on
D-D_{1}
.
The maximal chain-connected cycle
C\leq D
is called the
chain-connected component
of
D
. Note that the support of a chain-connected cycle is connected.

Lemma 2.4

(
[
5
, 1.2]
)
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{E}^{+}
. Then
D\in\mathcal{B}
if and only if
D
is chain-connected.

For a given cycle
D\in\mathcal{E}^{+}
, there exists a chain-connected component
C_{1}
of
D
.
If
D-C_{1}>0
, then we can take a chain-connected component
C_{2}
of
D-C_{1}
.
Repeating this procedure we obtain a decomposition of
D
into the sum of chain-connected cycles.

Theorem 2.5

(Konno
[
5
, 1.7]
)
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{E}^{+}
.
Then there exist a sequence
D_{1},\dots,D_{r}
of distinct chain-connected cycles with
D_{i}\leq D
and a sequence
m_{1},\dots,m_{r}
of positive integers that satisfy
the following conditions
:

(1)

D=m_{1}D_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}D_{r}
.

(2)

For
i<j
, either
\supp(D_{i})\cap\supp(D_{j})=\emptyset
or
D_{i}\geq D_{j}
.

(3)

For
i<j
,
D_{i}
is anti-nef on
D_{j}
.

(4)

If
m_{i}\geq 2
, then
D_{i}
is anti-nef on
D_{i}
.

Moreover, the sequences above are unique up to a suitable permutation of the indices.

Definition 2.6
.

The ordered decomposition
D=m_{1}D_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}D_{r}
as in Theorem
2.5
is called a
chain-connected component decomposition
or a
CCC decomposition
of
D
.

Assumption 2.7
.

In the following, when we say that
D=m_{1}D_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}D_{r}
is a CCC decomposition, we assume that this expression satisfies the conditions in Theorem
2.5
.

2.2.
A modification of Röhr’s lemma

Let us fix a divisor
L
on
X
which is not numerically trivial,
that is,
LE_{i}\neq 0
for some
E_{i}\leq E
.
Let

L^{\bot}=\bigcup_{E_{i}\subset E,\,LE_{i}=0}E_{i},

and define subsets
\mathcal{E}^{+}(L)
and
\mathcal{B}(L)
by

\mathcal{E}^{+}(L)={\left\{C\in\mathcal{E}^{+}\,\left|\,LC\neq 0\right.\right\}},\ \ \mathcal{B}(L)=\mathcal{E}^{+}(L)\cap\mathcal{B}.

For a cycle
C\in\mathcal{E}^{+}
, we consider the following condition:

(\#)

There exist effective cycles
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that

C=C_{1}+C_{2}
,
C_{1}\in\mathcal{B}(L)
,
\supp(C_{2})\subset L^{\bot}
,
and
C_{1}
is anti-nef on
C_{2}
.

We define the set
\mathcal{B}(L)^{e}
by

\mathcal{B}(L)^{e}={\left\{C\in\mathcal{E}^{+}(L)\,\left|\,\text{ $C$ satisfies $(\#)$ }\right.\right\}}.

Then we have
\mathcal{B}(L)\subset\mathcal{B}(L)^{e}
by the definition.

Remark 2.8
.

Suppose we have a decomposition

C=C_{1}+C_{2}\quad\text{with }C_{1}\in\mathcal{B}(L),\ \supp(C_{2})\subset L^{\bot}.

By applying Theorem
2.5
to the cycle
C
,
or just taking a chain-connected component of
C
,
we may choose
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
so that, in addition,

C_{1}
is anti-nef on
C_{2}
.

Example 2.9
.

Let us consider the singularity in Example
2.2
with
m=3
.
Assume that
X
is the minimal resolution and let
L=-E=-(E_{1}+E_{2}+E_{3})
. Since
E
is the exceptional part of
\di_{X}(z)
,
L
has no fixed components.
We have

L^{\bot}=E_{1}\cup E_{2},\quad\mathcal{B}(L)={\left\{C\in\mathcal{B}\,\left|\,C\geq E_{3}\right.\right\}}=\{E_{3},E_{2}+E_{3},E\}.

An element of
\mathcal{B}(L)^{e}
is expressed as
E_{3}+a_{2}E_{2}+a_{1}E_{1}

(a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})
.

The following proposition is a modified version of Röhr’s lemma
[
19
, Lemma 1.3]
; by replacing
\mathcal{B}(L)^{e}
with
\mathcal{B}
, and
\mathcal{E}^{+}(L)
with
\mathcal{E}^{+}
, it becomes the original situation.

Proposition 2.10
.

Assume that
LD>-2\chi(D)
for all
D\in\mathcal{B}(L)^{e}
.
Then
LD>-2\chi(D)
holds for all
D\in\mathcal{E}^{+}(L)
.

Proof.

Let
\eta(D)=(-1/2)LD
for
D\in\mathcal{E}^{+}
.
Then the inequality in the claim becomes
\eta(D)<\chi(D)
.
Assume that
D\in\mathcal{E}^{+}(L)
and let
D=m_{1}D_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}D_{r}
be the CCC decomposition.
By the formula (
2.2
) for
\chi
, we may assume that
\supp(D)
is connected.
Then,
D_{1}\geq D_{i}
for
i>1
since
\supp(D_{1})=\supp(D)
, and
\supp(D_{1})\not\subset L^{\bot}
.
Moreover, changing the indices if necessary,
we may assume that
\supp(D_{i})\not\subset L^{\bot}
, namely,
D_{i}\in\mathcal{B}(L)
for
i\leq p
, and
\supp(D_{i})\subset L^{\bot}
for
i>p
.
Let
D^{\prime}=D_{1}+m_{p+1}D_{p+1}+\cdots+m_{r}D_{r}
.
Then this expression is also a CCC decomposition, namely, the conditions (1)–(4) of Theorem
2.5
are satisfied.
Hence
D^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(L)^{e}
.
The conditions of Theorem
2.5
implies that
-D_{i}D_{j}\geq 0
for
i\neq j
, and thus
-D^{\prime}D_{i}\geq 0
for
1\leq i\leq r
.
By the assumption, we have
\eta(D^{\prime})<\chi(D^{\prime})
and
\eta(D_{i})<\chi(D_{i})
for
i\leq p
, since
\mathcal{B}(L)\subset\mathcal{B}(L)^{e}
.
Using the formula (
2.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\chi(D)

\displaystyle=\chi(D^{\prime}+(m_{1}-1)D_{1}+m_{2}D_{2}+\cdots+m_{p}D_{p})

\displaystyle\geq\chi(D^{\prime})+\chi((m_{1}-1)D_{1})+\chi(m_{2}D_{2})+\cdots+\chi(m_{p}D_{p})

\displaystyle\geq\chi(D^{\prime})+(m_{1}-1)\chi(D_{1})+m_{2}\chi(D_{2})+\cdots+m_{p}\chi(D_{p})

\displaystyle>\eta(D^{\prime})+(m_{1}-1)\eta(D_{1})+m_{2}\eta(D_{2})+\cdots+m_{p}\eta(D_{p})

\displaystyle=\eta(D^{\prime}+(m_{1}-1)D_{1}+m_{2}D_{2}+\cdots+m_{p}D_{p})

\displaystyle=\eta(D).\qed

2.3.
The cohomology of invertible sheaves without fixed components

We briefly summarize some results of

Definition 2.11
.

For a cycle
D\in\mathcal{E}^{+}
, let

p_{g}(D)=\max{\left\{h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{C})\,\left|\,C\in\mathcal{E}^{+},\,\supp(C)\subset\supp(D)\right.\right\}}.

We set

Note that if

and

can be contracted to normal surface singularities, then

is exactly the sum of the geometric genus of those singularities.

The next lemma follows from the arguments in
[

and
[

Lemma 2.12
.

Assume that a divisor
L
on
X
has no fixed components.
Then we have the following.

(1)

h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(sL))\geq h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}((s+1)L))
for
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
Let

s_{0}=\min{\left\{s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\,\left|\,h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(sL))=h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(tL))\;\text{for }t\geq s\right.\right\}}.

(2)

If
\mathcal{O}_{X}(L)
is generated
(
by global sections
)
, then

s_{0}=\min{\left\{s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\,\left|\,h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(sL))=h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}((s+1)L))\right.\right\}}\leq p_{g}(A).

(3)

h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(sL))=p_{g}(L^{\bot})
for
s\geq s_{0}
.

(4)

\mathcal{O}_{X}(sL)
is generated for

.

3.
A vanishing theorem on a partial resolution and its applications

The aim of this section is to prove a theorem that implies Theorem
1.2
.
We use the notation introduced in the preceding section.

Let us fix a divisor
L
on
X
that is not numerically trivial,
and assume that
\mathcal{O}_{X}(L)
has no fixed components.
In particular,
L
is nef and a general element of
H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(L))
induces an isomorphism
\mathcal{O}_{X}\cong\mathcal{O}_{X}(L)
on a neighborhood of
L^{\bot}
(cf. (
2.1
)).
By Lemma
2.12
(4), there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that
\mathcal{O}_{X}(nL)
is generated for every
n>n_{0}
.
Let
Y=\proj\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}(nL)
,
and let
f\colon X\to Y
be the natural morphism.
Then
Y
is normal, and the exceptional set of
f
coincides with
L^{\bot}
.
Since
\mathcal{O}_{X}(L)\cong\mathcal{O}_{X}
on a neighborhood of
L^{\bot}
and the local equations of
L
near
L^{\bot}
are also those of
f_{*}L
near
f(L^{\bot})
, we see that
f_{*}L
is a Cartier divisor on
Y
and
\mathcal{O}_{Y}(f_{*}L)
has no base points in
f(L^{\bot})
.

We also call a divisor
D
on
Y
a
cycle
if
\supp(D)\subset f(E)
.
Similar to the treatment of cycles on
X
, we regard a cycle
D
on
Y
as a scheme with structure sheaf
\mathcal{O}_{D}:=\mathcal{O}_{Y}/\mathcal{O}_{Y}(-D)
. We write
\chi(D)=\chi(\mathcal{O}_{D})
.

In general, a cycle
D
on
Y
is not a Cartier divisor (nor even
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier), so we cannot define
f^{*}D
in the usual way.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
W>0
be a cycle on
Y
.
We denote by
f_{*}^{-1}W
the proper transform of
W
on
X
.
Then
f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}(-f_{*}^{-1}W)=\mathcal{O}_{Y}(-W)
by the normality of
Y
.
Let
F
denote the
f
-fixed part of
\mathcal{O}_{X}(-f_{*}^{-1}W)
, namely,
F
is the maximal cycle such that

f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}(-f_{*}^{-1}W-F)=f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}(-f_{*}^{-1}W).

Then we define a cycle
f^{+}W
on
X
by

f^{+}W=f_{*}^{-1}W+F.

Note that
f^{+}W
is anti-nef on
L^{\bot}
and that
\supp(F)
is a union of some connected components of
L^{\bot}
.

For any coherent sheaf
\mathcal{F}
on
X
, the Leray spectral sequence implies the equality

(3.1)

h^{1}(X,\mathcal{F})=h^{1}(Y,f_{*}\mathcal{F})+h^{0}(Y,R^{1}f_{*}\mathcal{F}).

Lemma 3.2
.

We have the following.

(1)

f^{*}f_{*}L=L
.

(2)

h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(L))=h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{Y}(f_{*}L))+p_{g}(L^{\bot})
.

(3)

For a cycle
W>0
on
Y
, we have

\chi(W)-\chi(f^{+}W)=p_{g}(L^{\bot})-h^{0}(R^{1}f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}(-f^{+}W))\geq 0.

In particular,
\chi(W)=\chi(f^{+}W)
if
p_{g}(L^{\bot})=0
.

Proof.

(1) Let
F=f^{*}f_{*}L-L
. Then
\supp(F)
is
f
-exceptional and
0\geq F^{2}=(f^{*}f_{*}L-L)F=-LF=0
. Hence
F=0
.

(2) It follows from (
3.1
) because we have
h^{0}(R^{1}f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}(L))=p_{g}(L^{\bot})
since
\mathcal{O}_{X}\cong\mathcal{O}_{X}(L)
on a neighborhood of the
f
-exceptional set
L^{\bot}
.

(3)
Let
W^{\prime}=f^{+}W
.
From the exact sequence

0\to\mathcal{O}_{X}(-W^{\prime})\to\mathcal{O}_{X}\to\mathcal{O}_{W^{\prime}}\to 0,

we have

\chi(W^{\prime})=\dim_{k}H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{X})/H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(-W^{\prime}))+h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(-W^{\prime}))-h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}).

We also have the similar expression of
\chi(W)
.
Since
f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}(-W^{\prime})=\mathcal{O}_{Y}(-W)
by the definition of
f^{+}
and
f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}=\mathcal{O}_{Y}
, it follows from the equality (
3.1
) that

\displaystyle h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(-W^{\prime}))=h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{Y}(-W))+h^{0}(R^{1}f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}(-W^{\prime})),

\displaystyle h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X})=h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{Y})+h^{0}(R^{1}f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}).

Since
h^{0}(R^{1}f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X})=p_{g}(L^{\bot})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\chi(W^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\dim_{k}H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{Y})/H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{Y}(-W))+h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{Y}(-W))-h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{Y})

\displaystyle\quad\quad+h^{0}(R^{1}f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}(-W^{\prime}))-h^{0}(R^{1}f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X})

\displaystyle=\chi(W)+h^{0}(R^{1}f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}(-W^{\prime}))-p_{g}(L^{\bot}).

Applying Lemma
2.12
(1) with
s=0
and
L=-W^{\prime}
to each singular point of
Y
, i.e., each point of
f(L^{\bot})
, we obtain that
p_{g}(L^{\bot})\geq h^{0}(R^{1}f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}(-W^{\prime}))
.
∎

We use the terminology and results from
[
3
]
.
Let
W
be a cycle on
Y
.
Then
\mathcal{O}_{W}
is torsion free since
\mathcal{O}_{Y}(-W)
is reflexive.
Let
\omega_{W}
denote the dualizing sheaf of
W
.
The
degree
of a torsion free sheaf
\mathcal{L}
on
W
is defined to be

\deg\mathcal{L}=\chi(\mathcal{L})-\chi(W)
.
For a Cartier divisor
D
on
Y
, let
\mathcal{O}_{W}(D)=\mathcal{O}_{Y}(D)\otimes\mathcal{O}_{W}
and

DW:=\deg\mathcal{O}_{W}(D)
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
D
be a Cartier divisor on
Y
and let
W>0
be a cycle on
Y
such that
H^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{W}(D))\neq 0
.
Assume that
W
is minimal among all cycles on
Y
with this property.
Then
DW\leq-2\chi(W)
.

Proof.

By duality, we have
\Hom(\mathcal{O}_{W}(D),\omega_{W})\neq 0
.
Let
0\neq\phi\in\Hom(\mathcal{O}_{W}(D),\omega_{W})
.
Since
\mathcal{O}_{W}(D)
is invertible,
it follows from Lemma 2.4 and Remark 2.5 of
[
3
]
that
if
W^{\prime}\subset W
is a subscheme defined by the ideal
\ann\phi\subset\mathcal{O}_{W}
, then
W^{\prime}
is a cycle and
\phi
factors as
\mathcal{O}_{W}(D)\to\mathcal{O}_{W^{\prime}}(D)\xrightarrow{\;\phi^{\prime}}\omega_{W^{\prime}}\hookrightarrow\omega_{W}
,
where
\phi^{\prime}
is generically surjective.
However, by duality, the existence of such
\phi^{\prime}
implies that
H^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{W^{\prime}}(D))\neq 0
. By the minimality of
W
, we obtain that
W=W^{\prime}
.
Therefore, the morphism
\phi\colon\mathcal{O}_{W}(D)\to\omega_{W}
is generically surjective and, since
\omega_{W}
is invertible at general points, also generically injective.
Since
\mathcal{O}_{W}(D)
is torsion free,
\phi
is in fact injective.
Hence, we have the exact sequence

0\to\mathcal{O}_{W}(D)\xrightarrow{\phi}\omega_{W}\to\Coker\phi\to 0,

and that
\supp(\Coker\phi)
is a finite set.
Thus we obtain that

\chi(\omega_{W})-\chi(\mathcal{O}_{W}(D))=h^{0}(\Coker\phi)\geq 0.

Since
DW=\chi(\mathcal{O}_{W}(D))-\chi(W)
and
\chi(\omega_{W})=-\chi(W)
by duality, we have

DW=\chi(\mathcal{O}_{W}(D))-\chi(W)\leq\chi(\omega_{W})-\chi(W)=-2\chi(W).\qed

Let
\mathcal{B}(L)^{e}
be as in Section
2.2
.
The main result in this section is the following.

Theorem 3.4
.

Assume that
\mathcal{O}_{X}(L)
has no fixed components and that

LC>-2\chi(C)
for all
C\in\mathcal{B}(L)^{e}
.
Then
H^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{Y}(f_{*}L))=0
and
h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(L))=p_{g}(L^{\bot})
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.2
(2), it suffices to prove that
H^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{Y}(f_{*}L))=0
.
Recall that
f_{*}L
is a Cartier divisor and
\mathcal{O}_{Y}(f_{*}L)
has no base points in
f(L^{\bot})
.
Let
W>0
be an arbitrary cycle on
Y
.
Since
f^{+}W\in\mathcal{E}^{+}(L)
by the definition of
f\colon X\to Y
, it follows from Proposition
2.10
that
Lf^{+}W>-2\chi(f^{+}W)
.
Note that if
0\neq\sigma\in H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{W}(f_{*}L))
is a general section,
then the morphism
\sigma:\mathcal{O}_{W}\to\mathcal{O}_{W}(f_{*}L)
is injective and
\supp(\Coker\sigma)
is a finite subset of
f(E)\setminus f(L^{\bot})
.
Hence we have
(f_{*}L)W=h^{0}(\Coker\sigma)=(f^{*}f_{*}L)f^{-1}_{*}W
.
Using Lemma
3.2
, we obtain

(f_{*}L)W=(f^{*}f_{*}L)f_{*}^{-1}W=(f^{*}f_{*}L)f^{+}W=Lf^{+}W>-2\chi(f^{+}W)\geq-2\chi(W).

Therefore, by Lemma
3.3
,
H^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{W}(f_{*}L))=0
for an arbitrary cycle
W>0
on
Y
.
Hence we have
H^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{Y}(f_{*}L))=0
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

The condition

(3.2)

LC>-2\chi(C)
for all
C\in\mathcal{B}(L)^{e}

in Theorem
3.4
may be replaced by conditions that use
\mathcal{B}(L)
instead of
\mathcal{B}(L)^{e}
, as follows.
Let

\chi_{L}=\min{\left\{\chi(D)\,\left|\,D\in\mathcal{E}^{+},\;\supp(D)\subset L^{\bot}\right.\right\}}

and suppose
C\in\mathcal{B}(L)^{e}
.
By the definition of
\mathcal{B}(L)^{e}
,
C
can be expressed as
C=C_{1}+C_{2}
, where
C_{1}\in\mathcal{B}(L)
,
\supp(C_{2})\subset L^{\bot}
, and
C_{1}C_{2}\leq 0
.
We have
LC=LC_{1}
, and by (
2.2
) and (
2.4
),

\chi(C)\geq\chi(C_{1})+\chi(C_{2})\geq\chi(C_{1})+\chi_{L}\geq\chi(Z_{f})+\chi_{L},

where
Z_{f}
is the fundamental cycle.
Therefore, the condition (
3.2
) can be replaced by

(1)

LC>-2(\chi(C)+\chi_{L})
for all
C\in\mathcal{B}(L)
, or

(2)

LC>-2(\chi(Z_{f})+\chi_{L})
for all
C\in\mathcal{B}(L)
.

If
L^{\bot}
contracts to rational or elliptic singularities,
then
\chi_{L}\geq 0
, and thus
\chi_{L}
can be omitted in the conditions (1) and (2) above.

The next example shows that the assumption in Theorem
3.4
requiring
\mathcal{O}_{X}(L)
to have no fixed components is indispensable.

Example 3.6
.

Let us recall an example in
[
11
, 6.3]
.
Let
(S,o)\subset(\mathbb{C}^{4},o)
be a normal complex surface singularity defined by the
2\times 2
minors of the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}x&y&z\\
y-3w^{2}&z+w^{3}&x^{2}+6wy-2w^{3}\end{pmatrix}.

This is an elliptic singularity with
p_{g}(S,o)=1
.
Assume that
X\to S
is the minimal resolution with exceptional set
E
.
Then
E
consists of a rational curve
E_{1}
with
(2,3)
-cusp and a nonsingular rational curve
E_{2}
such that
E_{1}^{2}=-1
,
E_{2}^{2}=-2
, and
E_{1}E_{2}=1
.
In fact, the resolution graph of
(S,o)
coincides with that of the hypersurface singularity
(\{x^{2}+y^{3}+z^{13}=0\},o)\subset(\mathbb{C}^{3},o)
.
We have
Z_{f}=E
, and the maximal ideal cycle (e.g.,
[
11
, §3]
) is
Z_{f}+E_{1}
, because
(S,o)
is of type II in
[
11
, 3.11]
with
\alpha=\beta=1
.
Hence, the fixed part of
\mathcal{O}_{X}(-Z_{f})
is just
E_{1}
.
Let
L=-nZ_{f}
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.
Clearly,
L^{\bot}=Z_{f}^{\bot}=E_{1}
, and
p_{g}(L^{\bot})=1
since
E_{1}
contracts to a minimally elliptic singularity (
[
9
]
).
Every
C\in\mathcal{B}(L)^{e}
can be expressed as
C=mE_{1}+E_{2}
(
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
).
Thus,
\chi(C)=\chi(mE_{1})+\chi(E_{2})-mE_{1}E_{2}=(m-1)(m-2)/2\geq 0
, and the condition (
3.2
) is satisfied.
We will show that
h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(L))=0
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
n\neq 0
; that is,
h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(L))\neq p_{g}(L^{\bot})
for
L=-nZ_{f}
.
We have
\mathcal{O}_{E_{1}}(-Z_{f})\not\cong\mathcal{O}_{E_{1}}
by
[
11
, 2.13, 2.15]
.
Since
\pic(E_{1})
has no torsion elements (see the paragraph preceding
[
11
, 3.7]
), it follows that
\mathcal{O}_{E_{1}}(-nZ_{f})\not\cong\mathcal{O}_{E_{1}}
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
n\neq 0
.
However, since
\chi(\mathcal{O}_{E_{1}}(-nZ_{f}))=\chi(\mathcal{O}_{E_{1}})=0
, we have
h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{E_{1}}(-nZ_{f}))=h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{E_{1}}(-nZ_{f}))
.
It is well-known that a numerically trivial line bundle
\mathcal{L}
on a reduced cycle is analytically trivial if
H^{0}(\mathcal{L})\neq 0
.
Hence
h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(-nZ_{f}))=0
for every

,
n\neq 0
.

4.
An upper bound of the normal reduction number

We apply the results from Section
3
to study the normal reduction number of integrally closed
\mathfrak{m}
-primary ideals of the surface singularity
A
.
We follow the notation introduced in the preceding sections.

Let
I
be an
\mathfrak{m}
-primary ideal of
A
.
The integral closure
\bar{I}\subset A
of
I
is the ideal consisting of all solutions
z
of an equation of the form

z^{n}+c_{1}z^{n-1}+c_{2}z^{n-2}+\dots+c_{n-1}z+c_{n}=0
with coefficients
c_{i}\in I^{i}
.
Then
I\subseteq\bar{I}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}
.
We say that
I
is
integrally closed
if
I=\bar{I}
.

Definition 4.1

(
[
17
]
)
.

Let
I\subset A
be an integrally closed
\mathfrak{m}
-primary ideal,
and let
Q
be a minimal reduction of
I
.
The
normal reduction number
\br(I)
of
I
is defined by

\br(I)=\min\{r\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\;|\;\overline{I^{n+1}}=Q\overline{I^{n}}\;\mbox{\rm for all }n\geq r\}.

This is independent of the choice of
Q
.
The normal reduction number
\br(A)
of the singularity
A
is defined by

\br(A)=\max{\left\{\br(I)\,\left|\,\text{ $I$ is an integrally closed $\mathfrak{m}$-primary ideal of $A$}\right.\right\}}.

Proposition 4.2

(
[
12
]
,
[
18
]
)
.

We have the following.

(1)

A
is rational if and only if
\br(A)=1
.

(2)

If
A
is elliptic, then
\br(A)=2
.

(3)

If
A=k[[x,y,z]]/(f)
, where
f
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
, then
\br(A)=\br(\mathfrak{m})=d-1
.

Let
I\subset A
be an integrally closed
\mathfrak{m}
-primary ideal.
Then there exist a resolution
f\colon X\to\Spec A
and an anti-nef cycle
Z>0
on
X
so that

I=H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(-Z))
and
I\mathcal{O}_{X}=\mathcal{O}_{X}(-Z)
;
in this case, we say that
I
is
represented
by
Z
on
X
and write
I=I_{Z}
.

In the following,
I
always denotes an integrally closed
\mathfrak{m}
-primary ideal of
A
represented by a cycle
Z>0
on
X
.

Then the sheaf
\mathcal{O}_{X}(-Z)
has all information about
I
.

Definition 4.3
.

For every integer
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we define
q_{I}(n):=h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(-nZ))
.
In particular,
q_{I}(0)=p_{g}(A)
.
Notice that
q_{I}(n)
is independent of the representation (
[
15
, Lemma 3.4]
).

By Lemma
2.12
, we have
q_{I}(n)\geq q_{I}(n+1)
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
and
q_{I}(n)=p_{g}(Z^{\bot})
for every
n\geq p_{g}(A)
.
If
q_{I}(1)=q_{I}(0)=p_{g}(A)
, we have
q_{I}(n)=p_{g}(A)
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
; such an ideal is called a
p_{g}
-ideal (
[
15
]
).

Proposition 4.4

(
[
17
, §2]
)
.

We have the following.

\displaystyle\br(I)

\displaystyle=\min\{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\,|\,q_{I}(n-1)=q_{I}(n)\}

\displaystyle=\min\{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\,|\,q_{I}(n-1)=p_{g}(Z^{\bot})\}.

Lemma
2.12
and Proposition
4.4
imply the following.

Corollary 4.5
.

We have the following.

(1)

\br(I)=1
if and only if
p_{g}(A)=q_{I}(1)
or
p_{g}(Z^{\bot})
.

(2)

\br(I)=2
if and only if
p_{g}(A)>q_{I}(1)=p_{g}(Z^{\bot})
.

(3)

\br(A)\leq p_{g}(A)+1
.

Next, we will give sharpened upper bounds for
\br(A)
compared with Corollary
4.5
(3).

Definition 4.6
.

Let
U\to\spec(A)
be a resolution and let
W>0
be a cycle on
U
representing an integrally closed
\mathfrak{m}
-primary ideal of
A
.
We define

\lambda(W,U)=\max{\left\{\left\lfloor\frac{2p_{a}(D)-2}{-WD}\right\rfloor\,\left|\,D\in\mathcal{B}(W)^{e}\vphantom{\dfrac{A}{B}}\right.\right\}},

where
\mathcal{B}(W)^{e}
is defined for cycles on
U
in the same way as for cycles on
X
.
We define invariants
\lambda(I)
and
\lambda(A)
as follows:

\displaystyle\lambda(I)=\min{\left\{\lambda(W,U)\,\left|\,\text{$U$ is a resolution and $W$ a cycle on $U$ representing $I$}\right.\right\}}

\displaystyle\lambda(A)=\max{\left\{\lambda(I)\,\left|\,\text{ $I$ is an integrally closed $\mathfrak{m}$-primary ideal of $A$}\right.\right\}}.

Theorem 4.7
.

We have the following inequalities:

(1)

\br(I)\leq\lambda(I)+2\leq\lambda(Z,X)+2
,

(2)

\br(A)\leq\lambda(A)+2
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda=\lambda(Z,X)
.
For any
D\in\mathcal{B}(Z)^{e}
, since
-ZD>0
, we have

(\lambda+1)\cdot(-ZD)\geq\left(\left\lfloor\frac{-2\chi(D)}{-ZD}\right\rfloor+1\right)\cdot(-ZD)>-2\chi(D).

Therefore, by Theorem
3.4
,
h^{1}\left(\mathcal{O}_{X}(-(\lambda+1)Z)\right)=p_{g}(Z^{\bot})
.
Hence,
\br(I)\leq\lambda+2
by Proposition
4.4
.
Since the same argument applies to every representation of
I
,
the inequality (1) follows.
The inequality (2) follows directly from the definitions of
\lambda(A)
and
\br(A)
.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

If
-ZD\geq 2
for any
D\in\mathcal{B}(Z)^{e}
, then

\lambda(Z,X)\leq\max{\left\{p_{a}(D)-1\,\left|\,D\in\mathcal{B}(Z)^{e}\right.\right\}}\leq p_{a}(A)-1.

Hence we easily obtain
\br(I)\leq p_{a}(A)+1
in this case.

The following theorem gives a combinatorial upper bound for
\br(A)
.

Theorem 4.9
.

\br(A)\leq p_{a}(A)+1
.

Proof.

We may assume that
p_{a}(A)>0
, i.e.,
A
is not a rational singularity.
Let
I
be any integrally closed
\mathfrak{m}
-primary ideal and assume that
I
is represented by a cycle
Z
on
X
.
We will show that the inequality
\br(I)\leq p_{a}(A)+1
holds.
By Proposition
4.4
, it suffices to show that

Note that

5.
almost cone singularities

We continue to use the notation of the preceding section.
We say that

is a

if the exceptional set of of the minimal resolution is a smooth curve.
In
[

, we proved that for a non-rational cone-like singularity with the exceptional curve

where

The aim of this section is to generalize and sharpen this result.
The following results on the minimal model of a cycle will be used for introducing the notion of almost cone singularities,
which generalize the cone-like singularities,
and for the proof of facts in Example

(Konno

For

Recall that the

Theorem 5.14
.

Let
A
be an almost cone singularity
and let

be an integrally closed
\mathfrak{m}
-primary ideal of
A
represented by a cycle
Z
on
X
.
Let

denote the central curve with

and let

be the degree of

.
Then we have the following.

(1)

If
ZC<0
, then
\br(I)\leq\left\lfloor(2g-2)/\gon(C)\right\rfloor+2
.

(2)

If
ZC=0
, then
\br(I)\leq\left\lfloor(2g-2)/\delta\right\rfloor+2
,
where
\delta=\max\{2,d\}
.

Hence we obtain

\br(A)\leq\left\lfloor\frac{2g-2}{\min\{\gon(C),\delta\}}\right\rfloor+2\leq g+1.

Proof.

For every
D\in\mathcal{B}
, by Lemma
5.7
,
we have either

(i)

C\not\leq D
and
\chi(D)=1\geq DC
, or

(ii)

C\leq D
and
\chi(D)=\chi(C)=1-g
.

(1) Assume that
ZC<0
.
Since
\mathcal{O}_{C}(-Z)
is also generated,
there exist sections
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{C}(-Z))
which determine a surjective morphism
\phi\colon C\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
of degree
-ZC
.
Hence
-ZC\geq\gon(C)
.
To find an upper bound for
\lambda(Z,X)
in Definition
4.6
, from (i) and (ii) above, it is enough to take only

such that
D\geq C
(cf. Remark
3.5
).
For such a cycle

, we have

\frac{2p_{a}(D)-2}{-ZD}\leq\frac{2g-2}{-ZC}\leq\frac{2g-2}{\gon{C}}.

Hence
\lambda(Z,X)\leq\left\lfloor(2g-2)/\gon{(C)}\right\rfloor
, and the assertion (1) follows from Theorem
4.7
.

(2) Assume that
ZC=0
.
Let
B
be the connected component of
Z^{\bot}
containing
C

and let

be as in Lemma

(2) (note that

).
Then
-WC\geq\delta
.
The same argument as in the proof of Claim

shows that

is anti-nef.

Let

Let

and

.
Hence we obtain that

.
From the exact sequence

0\to\mathcal{O}_{X}(-s(Z+W))\to\mathcal{O}_{X}(-sZ)\to\mathcal{O}_{sW}(-sZ)\to 0,

we have

by Lemma
2.12
(3).
On the other hand, by Lemma
2.12
(1), we have

Hence
h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(-sZ))=p_{g}(Z^{\bot})
.
This implies that
\br(I)\leq(2g-2)/\delta+2
by Proposition

.

For the very last inequality, note that
\gon(C)\geq 2
since

\delta\geq-WC\geq 2
.
∎
