From Expert to Novice: An Empirical Study on Software Architecture Explanations
Satrio Adi Rukmono 1 2 3 , Filip Zamfirov 1 , Lina Ochoa 1 , Michel R. V. Chaudron 1

Abstract
The sharing of knowledge about software architecture is crucial in software development, particularly during the onboarding of new developers. However, existing documentation often falls short due to issues like incompleteness and ambiguity. Consequently, oral explanations are used for knowledge transfer. This study investigates what constitutes a good explanation of software architecture through an empirical study. It aims to explore how software architecture explanations are conducted, identify the main challenges, and suggest improvements. It addresses five key areas: relevant architectural concerns, explanation plans, supporting artefacts, typical questions, and expectations. An exploratory field study was conducted using semi-structured interviews with 17 software professionals, including 9 architecture explainers and 8 explainees. The study discovers that an explanation must balance both problem and technical domains while considering the explainee’s role, experience, and the goal of the explanation. The concept of the explanation window, which adjusts the level of detail and scope, is introduced to address these variables. We also extend the Twin Peaks model to guide the interplay between problem and solution domains during architectural explanations by adding an emphasis to the context surrounding both domains. Future research should focus on developing better tools and processes to support architecture explanations.

I
Introduction

Software architectures are high-level representations of

software systems.
Typically software architectures are represented through a set of views and complemented by key design decisions and design principles.
Developers need to have a good understanding of the system’s software architecture to properly implement it, safeguard the system’s quality, and ensure the system’s fruitful evolution.

A notable scenario where the understanding of a system’s software architecture is crucial is during the on-boarding of developers to an ongoing software development project
[
1
]
.

Dagenais et al. 2010
already touch upon the diverse challenges faced by newcomers at IBM in these situations.
Particularly, newcomers need to comprehend the system’s software architecture to assess whether their tasks align with the overall project and comply with the predefined architectural guidelines.
To face such a challenge, newcomers typically rely on documentation that encodes the knowledge of the development team, mostly focusing on the system’s technical perspective (e.g., architectural documentation, configuration guidelines, interface documentation).
However, in practice, software documentation is subject to issues such as incompleteness, ambiguity, obsoletness, inconsistency, and incorrectness
[
3
]
.
Additional issues of documentation relate to lack of readability, fragmentation, usability, and usefulness
[
4
,
5
,
3
]
.
Also, relevant information about the application-context and its alignment with the technical-perspective is frequently left behind, hindering the comprehension and development processes
[
6
]
.

To cope with these issues, development teams designate system experts with the task of
explaining software architecture
to newcomers.
Such experts aim to explain the system to uninformed developers and
transfer architectural knowledge

[
2
]
.
In short, these personal (i.e., involving an active
person
) explanations constitute an alternative approach to transferring knowledge about a system—likely building on, but potentially also extending existing documentation.
Related research has focused on unveiling documentation issues
[

,

[

[

However, little attention has been drawn to the specific topic of architect’s explaining of software architecture.

To bridge this gap, this study revolves around the following overarching question:

How is software architecture being explained?

To address this inquiry, we consider five concrete perspectives that can help us better understand and explain how software architecture explanation is conducted in practice.
In particular, we are concerned with
(1) architectural concerns (e.g., architectural patterns, views, analyses) that are of relevance during an explanation,
(2) the shape of a plan followed by software professionals to explain software architecture,
(3) artefacts supporting the explanation,
(4) typical questions received and asked during the explanation, and
(5) expectations during and after explaining software architecture.
The aforementioned perspectives lead to the research questions below:

RQ1.

What are the participants’
concerns
in a software architecture explanation?
In this research question, by
participants
we mean parties involved in a software architecture explanation, i.e., both the explainer and the explainee.

RQ2.

What does a
plan
to explain software architecture entail?

Planning a software architecture explanation can be of interest to practitioners to efficiently and effectively transfer system knowledge, drifting away from ad-hoc and impromptu processes. We aim at answering
how
these explanations are typically executed, providing a common ground for developers and architects.

RQ3.

What
supporting artefacts
are used during software architecture explanation?

To properly explain a system’s architecture, practitioners might rely on existing or built-on-the-fly artefacts (e.g., diagrams, tools), easing the comprehension process. We aim to identify
what assets
are commonly used by practitioners.

RQ4.

What are
typical questions
asked during software architecture explanation?

This question sheds light on recurrent questions asked by explainees during software architecture explanation.
What questions
do explainess find important? With this visibility, explainers are not taken off-guard when preparing an explanation targeted towards comprehending a system.

RQ5.

What are
expectations
during and after a software architecture explanation?

To come up with a solid plan for a software architecture explanation (see
), both explainers and explainees need to have concrete objectives regarding what they want to achieve with the process. These objectives can be better formulated in the future by knowing what are the current expectations of practitioners when it comes to explaining software architecture.

II
Related Work

To the best of our knowledge, there is no existing open-ended study that explores architecture explanations in practice, i.e., involving professionals. The related work focuses on one or several ways of conveying software architecture.

A study by
Ernst and Robillard 2023
explores the impact of documentation format on understanding software architecture for newcomers to a project. Two formats were tested: narrative essays and structured documents. The study found no significant impact of documentation format on understanding software architecture. Instead, prior exposure to the system’s source code and the type of architectural information sought were identified as key factors. The structured document was slightly preferred, but not statistically significant.

RoleViz
[
15
]
is a software structure visualisation tool that highlights the role stereotype
[
16
]
of the software components. A user study comparing RoleViz and a commercial software architecture comprehension tool was conducted. The study found that incorporating role stereotype into the visualisation significantly improved participants’ perceived usability and understanding scores without increasing cognitive load. Six out of 16 participants explicitly stated that visualising roles helped them complete their tasks.

Schoonewille et al. 2011
investigated the use of software design documentation, specifically UML diagrams and text, in understanding software design. Participants’ use of these media was recorded and analysed as they answered design-related questions. The study found a positive correlation between developer certainty and correctness of answers, but not the reverse. Self-rated experience and skill were associated with higher certainty levels. Participants rated information based on perceived importance and relied heavily on “common sense”. An interesting finding is that over
60\%
of answers were based on consulting a single medium, i.e., diagrams only or text only. The findings suggest that further investigation is needed.

Shahin et al. 2014
studied the role of Architectural Design Decisions (ADDs) and their rationale in improving the understanding of architecture design.
Despite the belief that ADDs enhance the architecting process and design results, there is limited empirical evidence about their adoption in industry.
The paper presents two controlled experiments examining the impact of ADDs on architecture understanding.
The study found that while the use of ADDs in documentation does not affect task completion time, the presence of ADDs significantly improves architecture design understanding.
Additionally, more experienced participants gain more from ADDs compared to less experienced ones.

Unphon and Dittrich 2010
investigated the role of software architecture in product development and evolution. Despite its established presence in software engineering, the influence of architectural knowledge on development practices appears limited. Architectural knowledge often exists implicitly within development discussions.
The study, based on 15 interviews, reveals a spectrum of architectural practices in software product development organisations.
A key finding is the role of a ‘walking architecture’, typically a chief architect or central developer, who devises changes, discusses designs, and updates knowledge about problematic aspects. Architecture documentation may not be used, especially if it replaces feedback from ongoing developments.
The study suggests that explicating existing architecture should be complemented by social protocols to support the ‘walking architecture”s communication and knowledge sharing processes.

Jolak et al. 2020
conducted a series of experiments involving 240 software engineering students from four universities.
The results suggest that graphical software design descriptions (GSD) improve design-knowledge transfer and communication by promoting active discussion, reducing conversation management effort, and improving the recall of design details.
On the other hand, a well-organized and motivated textual design description (Altered-TSD) enhances recall and active discussions but reduces the perceived quality of explaining. The study also discusses the implications for agile practices, development efforts, and pedagogical mediums, suggesting that GSDs can be beneficial in various software engineering contexts.
It highlights the potential benefits of including design rationale in design documentation to further improve communication.
The results have implications for practitioners, suggesting that GSDs can increase productivity, satisfaction, and effectiveness in software design communication.

There is a handful of papers that study questions that programmers ask during software development tasks
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
.
The study by
Sillito et al. 2006
explores and categorises the questions programmers ask during software evolution tasks, and how they use programming tools to answer these questions.
It involves two qualitative studies with programmers working on change tasks for medium to large-sized programs.
The researchers observed patterns of questions about the same entity and a series of questions where each subsequent question is about a newly discovered entity.

Kubelka et al. 2019
aimed to understand how developers ask and answer questions about source code and use tools to gain corresponding knowledge in Live Programming environments. Their methodology involves a study of developer questions during software evolution tasks in Live Programming environments, specifically Pharo.
The researchers compared question occurrences, complexity, and information sources used by developers in a Live Programming environment versus traditional programming environments.

Ratanotayanon and Sim 2006
conducted a laboratory study simulating a situation in software maintenance where an existing developer has to transfer his knowledge to assist another programmer who is also assigned to make a modification to a software system.
The study involved both novice and expert software engineers collaborating on a change request for a web application. The researchers observed how software developers ask questions to increase their understanding of the application in order to complete an assigned task.
The study found that both novices and experts often do not perform the seemingly simple activity of asking questions well or as frequently as necessary for successful knowledge collaboration.

While these studies also research the questions asking of software developers, there are notable differences with our perspective on transferring architecture knowledge:

Technical Task Orientation vs. Conceptual Understanding

Information seeking in programming is often motivated by a technical task that a programmer has to perform. Programmers typically look for specific answers to solve a problem or debug code.
The focus is on immediate application—finding a code snippet, algorithm, or library to perform a task. This often leads to searching documentation, forums like Stack Overflow, or technical blogs to implement features or address specific bugs.
In contrast, information transfer for understanding architecture is more conceptual and holistic.
The aim is often to create a long lasting understanding of the overarching design principles and structures.
Even though an explainee may have technical tasks to perform following the explanation, an architectural understanding guides the ‘big picture’ conformance rather than solving a specific programming problem.
The focus is on grasping the rationale behind decisions and the relationships between high level components within a system.

Granularity of Information

The information sought in programming tasks is typically more granular and immediate.
A programmer might need help with syntax, a specific function, or understanding a particular library’s behaviour.
This often leads to discrete answers that solve a narrow issue.

Information about architecture involves a broader scope. Understanding an architecture requires knowledge of high-level concepts, such as system design patterns, scalability-tactics, and trade-offs between various approaches.
It’s less about solving immediate problems and more about building a mental framework for complex interactions.

Exploration vs. Transfer

Information seeking in programming is often exploratory and experimental.
Programmers may go through a trial-and-error process, testing different solutions, and iterating until the problem is resolved.

In contrast, architecture information transfer is often more about conceptual/abstract knowledge.
It involves gaining an understanding from prior expertise, possibly the historical evolution of a system.

Methodology

Fig. 1:
Interview study methodology.

We conducted an exploratory field study to address our research questions aimed at understanding how software architecture is explained.
According to Stol and Fitzgerald
[
24
]
, field studies are inherently exploratory, being both inconspicuous and real, as there is no intervention or control over any variable within the studied environment.
Thus, we aim to gain a deep understanding of software architecture explanation in a specific, concrete, and realistic setting
[
24
]
.
However, this approach has its costs: there is low precision in measuring the behaviours studied, and the resulting findings have limited generalisability.

For data collection, we initially proposed conducting
observations
, where a researcher would be present during a real software architecture explanation session, such as an on-boarding process. Such a session would involve a software architect (the explainer) and a team member or another architect (the explainee). The objective was to qualitatively analyse the conversations to address our research questions. Unfortunately, we only found one company willing to participate, as other companies declined due to confidentiality and practical concerns.

Faced with this challenge, we decided instead to conduct a series of
interviews
with software architects and other software professionals. Our goal was to understand how software architecture is explained from five different perspectives:
stakeholder’s concerns
(
),
explanation plan
(
),
supporting artefacts
(
),
typical questions
(
), and
expectations
(
).
The interviews followed a semi-structured approach, with questions focusing on the topic at hand, ranging from specific to open-ended.
Specific questions elicit in-depth perspectives about anticipated information, while open-ended questions provide more nuanced and unexpected viewpoints
[
25
,
26
]
.

The methodology comprises three main phases: (1) the

III-A
1
Study stakeholders

This section refers to the “identify stakeholders” task in
Figure

1
.
The workflow starts with the identification of involved stakeholders.
As argued by Strandberg
[
11
]
, this step is crucial to adhere to the principle of beneficence—that is, to minimise the risk of harm to any stakeholder related to the study.
The study participants are undoubtedly key stakeholders of our study, and their selection will determine the existence of other relevant stakeholders (e.g., managers, supervisors, companies).
We consider two different population types in our research: first, software professionals who have been involved in on-boarding processes that necessitated a software architecture explanation, and,
second, individuals with a vast knowledge of a given system, who find themselves in the position of explaining software architecture to colleagues with less experience or knowledge of the system at hand.
For the sake of conciseness, we refer to software professionals explaining software architecture as
explainers
and other software professionals receiving a software architecture explanation as
explainees
.

Given the impracticality of performing a proper random sampling on the targeted population, we utilise a combination of two non-random sampling methods to find potential interview candidates—that is, convenience
[
27
]
and purposive sampling
[
28
]
.
In convenience sampling, participants are selected given their suitability for the study and easy accessibility
[
27
]
, while in purposive sampling, after deciding on the information required from participants, based on their knowledge of the population, researchers define a set of qualities participants should have to be able to get the expected data
[
29
,
28
]
.
Thus, we conveniently look for participants through software professionals in our network and that of our colleagues.
On the one hand, we recruit explainers who play an
expert
role in a given software project and thus have been working for such endeavour for at least one year.
Typically, we look for software professionals with experience working for software-oriented companies.
On the other hand, explainees are scouted by either looking for colleagues who have recently enrolled in a new company or asking members of our research cluster to inform us of master students who are starting thesis projects or internships in industry, open source projects, or academic projects where understanding the architecture of a software system is crucial.

Given the selected sample, the study stakeholders include colleagues and software professionals in our network referring us to the selected participants, and the companies where participants are currently working and their colleagues.
In the particular case of master students, their university supervisors and current educators also play a role as stakeholders.

We purposefully come back to this point throughout this section.

We initially expected that the situations that require software architecture explanation are mainly on-boarding ones, where a new team member joins a team or project, and off-boarding ones, in which an architect is leaving the team, necessitating some handover of knowledge responsibility. In the interview study, four explainee participants were in fact involved in on-boarding situations. However, we also see three different kinds of situation which motivate architecture explanation:

The first one is a research situation, where an expert is brought in to the team to research either the software system and its development or its impact to the organisation. Some examples are researchers that help manage legacy code or data scientists that seek to mine knowledge about business processes implemented in the software system.

The other two kinds of situation happen between a team representative and an external entity, rather than intra-team. One is when a software solution provider explains their proposed solution to their (prospective) client, and the other is communication between a first- or second-party architect (i.e., either an organisation-internal architect or a consultant) and a third-party development team, whose responsibility is either to implement a design or to integrate their (external) software system with the organisation’s.
Also, architectures are explained for the purpose of architecture evaluations.

III-C

Data Analysis

This section refers to the “derive concepts” and “derive themes” tasks in
Figure

1
.

With the promise of bringing qualitative rigour to the study, we follow the guidelines proposed in the Gioia methodology
[
12
]
.
We thus share the assumption that participants in our study are knowledgeable about their work and capable of explaining their thoughts, intentions, and actions
[
12
]
.
The process starts by deriving
concepts
(first-order codes) via in-vivo coding
[
14
]
.
For that, we use the participants’ terms and expressions to create the first set of codes with little effort in extracting categories.

We, then, proceed with themes (second-order codes) derivation using open coding
[
13
]
to make sense of the observed phenomena.
Even though the methodology does not encourage the use of prior constructs or theories, we follow a hybrid and pragmatic approach that can help us better interpret the interview study data.
In particular, we iteratively structure knowledge around the 4+1 view model of software architecture
[
33
]
to build atop existing constructs.
This model addresses different concerns relevant to the system’s stakeholders by splitting the architecture into five different views, namely: (1) the
logical view
showcasing the functional requirements of the system; (2) the
process view
focusing on the behaviour of the system; (3) the
development view
describing the organisation of the actual software; (4) the
physical view
identifying where and how software components are deployed in cloud services and dedicated servers, and; (5)
scenarios
depicting the main use cases of the system.
Two researchers open-code all interviews independently, to later have recurrent alignment sessions of their interpretation, aiming to develop a cohesive and consistent code book.

This section refers to the “derive dimensions”, “perform member checking”, and “analyse and report” tasks in
Figure

1
.

Once all codes have been identified, the researchers derive dimensions that cluster related themes together.
Before reporting, we perform member checking
[
34
]

IV-A

Managing complexity
Managing complexity

Given the complexity of many software architectures, an important aspect of effective explanation involves simplifying the architecture by focusing on a subset of the system. This reduction in complexity helps manage cognitive load and facilitates comprehension. Our study identifies two primary strategies for reducing complexity: selecting appropriate levels of abstraction and emphasising relevance to the task at hand.

Levels of abstraction
Levels of abstraction

Selecting the right level of abstraction is vital for effectively explaining and understanding software architecture. Too much detail can overwhelm the audience (
E_{9}
,
e_{7}
), while too little can leave important aspects unclear (
E_{2}
). Different audiences need different levels of detail; for instance, some stakeholders might need a high-level overview to grasp the overall structure, while others might require in-depth details to understand specific design decisions or quality concerns (
E_{2}
,
E_{4}
). For complex systems like microservices, which are inherently challenging to describe, balancing abstraction with necessary detail helps to clarify how components interact without causing confusion (
E_{3}
). Therefore, a thoughtful selection of the level of detail is key to conveying architecture in a way that is both informative and comprehensible to a specific audience profile (
e_{2}
,
E_{9}
).

Relevance to task-at-hand
Relevance to task-at-hand

As architecture explanations do not stand on their own—i.e., an explainee needs to understand an architecture in order to accomplish their task, such as implementing a new feature or refactoring code – it is important to attune the explanation on what is relevant to the task at hand. Instead of presenting every detail, it is better to curate the information, showing only those elements that are necessary for the current situation (
e_{5}
). This approach prevents overwhelming the audience with unnecessary details and keeps the explanation focused and clear (
E_{7}
). Limiting the information to what is needed ensures that the audience can grasp the scope and specific problems without getting lost in irrelevant complexities (
E_{1}
). Sometimes, starting with the big picture can help, especially if the team does not have an overview of the architecture, before zooming into details that matter most to their work (
E_{8}
).

Answer:
The participants’ concerns in a software architecture explanation revolve around three primary dimensions: understanding the system’s context and architectural composition, managing the complexity of the architecture, and addressing the factors that influence architectural decisions. These concerns underline the need to addresses both technical and contextual elements to facilitate effective comprehension of software architecture.

Comprehension model
Comprehension model

The activities and their sequence in an explanation are influenced by the comprehension model employed, among other factors.
We define the comprehension model as a cognitive framework that describes the process an individual uses to form an abstract representation of the object being understood
[
35
]
.
We build upon existing cognitive models reported in the literature
[
36
,
35
]
, such as top-down, bottom-up, opportunistic, and hybrid models, shaping the reported themes to fit the current body of knowledge.

Each of these models is briefly explained below.

Top-down model
Top-down model

The system’s architecture is comprehended by linking constructs at a higher level of abstraction to those at a lower level, reaching even the source code if needed
[
37
]
(
E_{5}
,
E_{7}
,
E_{8}
).
Explainer
E_{8}
mentioned that
“it
[
is
]
easier to know first what the system is doing as a whole and then focus on different parts of functionality …then, indeed, zooming in into the different components or even functions that provide this functionality.”

A special case of the top-down model in architecture explanations occurs when stakeholders remain at the top level of the architecture.
They aim to understand the overall layout of the system’s design and the interactions among its components without delving into further details (
E_{1}
,
E_{4}
,
E_{6}
,
E_{7}
).
In this approach, the explanation does not go beyond presenting relevant interfaces exposed to clients in the form of libraries, frameworks, or services (

,
E_{4}
,
E_{5}
,

,

Bottom-up model
Bottom-up model

The comprehension process starts by inspecting low-level constructs (e.g., source code) and grouping them together to form higher-level models
[
37
]
.

Opportunistic model

,
35
]
(

Hybrid model

A subset of comprehension models are interwoven

]

Answer:
A plan to explain software architecture entails a structured approach that encompasses a variety of activities, comprehension models, and explanation styles. The activities help in structuring the explanation and ensuring all necessary concerns are covered. The comprehension models guide the cognitive process of understanding the architecture. Explanation styles focus on how the explanation is delivered and how communication is managed. A software architecture explanation process should be tailored to the needs and context of both explainers and explainees in order to ensure comprehension.

IV-D
3
Challenging topics
Challenging topics

In the course of software architecture explanations, certain topics and questions can present challenges for explainers.
These challenging topics often require a deeper level of understanding, cross-functional knowledge, or involve elements outside the immediate control of the explainer.
Effectively addressing these challenges is crucial for maintaining a smooth and productive explanation process.
In this subsection, we identify and discuss the three main themes that describe these challenging topics.

Unknown knowledge
Unknown knowledge

Explainers sometimes encounter questions about topics they are unfamiliar with.
This can occur when the knowledge resides with a different colleague or has been lost over time due to staff turnover (
E_{3}
).
These questions might cover a wide range of topics, leaving the explainer to navigate how to provide accurate answers or guidance.
For instance, if an explainee asks about a specific interface or component that the explainer is not well-versed in, the explainer must either seek out the missing information or direct the explainee to someone else who has the expertise.
This situation can be particularly challenging when the required knowledge is critical to understanding the system’s architecture.

Outsourced services
Outsourced services

Questions concerning outsourced services, such as those deployed in the cloud, can present an additional layer of complexity for explainers (
E_{6}
).
In these scenarios, the organisation acts as an intermediary, connecting outsourced services with the system’s requirements.
However, explainers might struggle to provide clear answers about the alignment between the internal system and these external services.
This challenge is compounded when the specifications or behaviours of the outsourced services are not fully known or controlled by the organisation.
For example, explainers might need to address inconsistencies between the requirements of cloud services and the needs of the system, often making educated guesses or decisions in the absence of complete information.

Work ownership
Work ownership

Explainees can ask management-oriented questions regarding work ownership, distribution, and responsibility (
E_{6}
).
Knowing who is in charge of implementing parts of the system or executing certain activities is needed to facilitate the explainee’s working trajectory (
e_{1}
).
However, sometimes ownership is not clearly defined, which triggers a challenge not only for the explainer but also for management.

Answer:
Typical questions during software architecture explanation revolve around several key dimensions. These include the types of questions, such as yes/no questions for basic clarification, WH- questions (what, when, where, who, why, how) for in-depth understanding, impact questions exploring hypothetical scenarios, and clarification questions to piece together information. Common topics addressed include the system’s architecture itself, design decisions, business alignment, implementation details, changes and evolution of the system, and the semantics of diagrams and concepts. Additionally, certain challenging topics may arise, such as unknown knowledge, outsourced services, and work ownership, which can be particularly difficult for explainers to address.

IV-F

Software Architecture Explanation: A Holistic Approach

In this section, we provide a holistic perspective on how the aspects analysed in previous sections relate to each other and could be managed when explaining a system’s architecture.
We base our approach on the Twin Peaks model introduced by
Nuseibeh 2001
, which in turn adapts from the seminal model by
Ward and Mellor 1885
.
The Twin Peaks model is a software development life-cycle method that intertwines a system’s problem and solution domains to achieve incremental and efficient development
[
6
]
.
It follows a spiral process where the system requirements and their implementation are developed concurrently.

Figure

2
presents an adapted version of the Twin Peaks model to support software architecture explanation.

Fig. 2:
Software architecture explanation atop the Twin Peaks model.

IV-F
1
Explanation goal

Our study suggests that an effective software architecture explanation must seamlessly interweave knowledge from both the problem and solution domains.
To achieve this, the explanation should be guided by a clear, overarching goal that meets the expectations of both the explainer and the explainee (cf.,
Section

IV-E
).
Examples of such goals include implementing a new feature, refactoring part of a system, or providing a general understanding to a newcomer so they can independently work on their development team’s code.
The explainer and the explainee move iteratively from the problem to the solution space, following a spiral movement.

IV-F
2
The problem and solution domains

Software architecture explanations cover both the problem domain and the solution domain.
The problem domain encompasses information related to the stakeholders, their needs and requirements and the business-processes that need to be supported. Already in the problem domain there can be a high level conceptual decomposition of the capabilities of the system needed – however this is then described in abstract terms that are independent from any particular implementation technology.

The solution domain, on the other hand, focuses on the universe of implementation/construction of the system – including: architecture, design, and implementation technologies.
This domain typically involves a conventional technical architecture description, which expresses the system’s behaviour and structure, its constituent elements and their interactions, as well as its governing design principles
[
42
]
.
The architecture description can, for instance, be structured following the 4+1 views model to cater to diverse stakeholder perspectives.
As illustrated in
Figure

2
, the relevant concerns identified in
Section

IV-A
inhabit these spaces and are illustrated via supporting artefacts such as tools, documents, and diagrams (cf.,
Section

IV-C
).
In our study, we find that explanations frequently employ information that is not part of the IT-system, but from its wider organisational (or societal) context. This would then include: generic domain knowledge (e.g. legal/regulatory requirements), business/product roadmaps, (IT)vendor policies.
Similarly, there is contextual knowledge for the technical/implementation domain. This could e.g. include the rationale design decisions, the (accidental) historical evolution of the system (e.g. explaining legacy aspects of the system).
Such aspects are rarely found in traditional software requirements- or design- documentation.

IV-F
3
Alignment between domains

Software architecture documentation typically addresses specific concerns within either the problem or solution domain.
However, this documentation can be incomplete, ambiguous, obsolete, inconsistent, incorrect, verbose, fragmented, or entangled
[
3
]
.
In particular, a lack of clear alignment with the domains hinders the system’s comprehension.

A well-structured software architecture explanation can bridge this gap by emphasising the spiral movement between the problem and solution domains, ensuring consistency and alignment across them.

To illustrate, a design decision or a feature implementation in the source code should align with the strategy and requirements defined in the problem space to provide a holistic understanding.
Nevertheless, it is not solely up to the explainer to bridge the gap between the problem and solution domains; explainees support this process by asking open questions that clarify the system design.
As shown in the analysis in
Section

IV-D
, common topics surrounding questions touch upon both domains (e.g., business and semantics in the problem domain, and design decisions and implementation in the solution domain).
It is, however, a daunting task to provide a complete explanation in just one session or source (i.e., the explainer).
This is evident from the existence of challenging questions where the explainer does not have the knowledge to provide a reliable answer.

IV-F
4
The Cognitive Perspective

The spiral movement between the domains can also be understood from a cognitive perspective.
Here, the explainer and explainee need to agree on how the system will be comprehended by defining an explanation plan (cf.
Section

IV-B
).
The plan should outline the relevant activities that suit the overarching goal of the explanation, as well as the comprehension model to grasp the underlying information and the explanation style.
As seen in
Figure

2
, it is up to the stakeholders involved in the explanation to define the direction of the spiral (e.g., top-down or bottom-up).
They should also decide on which relevant concerns and entities to focus on (cf.
Section

IV-A
), thus establishing the depth and breadth of the explanation.

As conclusive remarks, we argue that awareness of the elements that constitute a software architecture explanation could help practitioners plan better for these sessions, reducing the complexity of system comprehension.
Therefore, a good software architecture explanation should be tailored by considering: (1) a clear overarching goal, (2) the relevant aspects constituting the problem and solution domains of the system, (3) the alignment between these two domains through structured knowledge transfer and open-ended questions, and (4) establishing a cognitive approach for the explanation.

V
Discussion

In this section, we reflect on the key findings from our study. We compare the perspectives of explainers and explainees, discuss the variability in explanation approaches and propose the concept of “explanation window” to address the inherent complexity of software systems, and explore how these findings can inform the development of better practices and tools to support the comprehension of software architectures.

V-A

Explainers vs. Explainees

In the process of explaining software architecture, there are some differences in the concerns and preferences of explainers and explainees, due to their distinct roles and objectives.

Explainers are primarily focused on delivering explanations that are both clear and comprehensive. A key concern for explainers is finding the right balance between providing enough detail to be useful without overwhelming the explainee. This requires a careful selection of the appropriate level of abstraction to present the architecture, which often involves deciding which architectural views (such as logical, process, or deployment views) are most relevant to the audience.

Explainees, on the other hand, have a more significant concern on the need for contextual or domain-specific information to fully grasp the architecture. As they are often newcomers in the context of the system being explained, they value explanations that connect technical details to the overall business or project context. This helps them understand why certain design choices were made and how these choices affect their work. (One notable exception is for the situation where a solution provider is proposing a technological solution to a domain expert. In such situation, the explainee may have more domain knowledge than the explainer.)Additionally, explainees often desire opportunities for interaction, where they can ask questions and seek clarifications. This interactive aspect is crucial for them to resolve any doubts and to deepen their understanding of the architecture.

V-B

Explanation Window

A recurring theme from the interviews is the inherent complexity of software systems, which makes it impractical and counterproductive to attempt to explain every minor detail of the system. Instead, a key aspect of effectively conveying software architecture is to strategically select relevant pieces of information that ensure both comprehensibility and practical utility for the explainee’s specific tasks. To facilitate this targeted approach, we introduce the concept of the
explanation window
.

The
explanation window
is a metaphorical lens that provides the explainee with a focused view of the system’s components and their interactions. This window constrains the range of abstraction levels and functionalities that are included in a given explanation, as depicted in

. For instance, the abstraction dimension can range from more abstract elements like
packages
to more concrete elements such as

In contrast, a “programmer’s view” focuses on the detailed implementation of a specific feature or component. This is represented by a narrower window positioned lower on the

While the explanation window can be of any size and shape in any position in the software component-space, the two example views mentioned serve as extreme or contrasting windows that are roughly of the same area.

Note that, while development view is used for the illustration above, the abstraction dimension is not strictly tied to any specific architectural view. One can apply the metaphorical lens at different views—for example, in the behaviour view, one can look at a high-level business process (architect’s view) or a detailed sequence of one specific feature from user interaction down to database accesses (programmer’s view). In fact, by also considering the augmented Twin Peaks model we described in

, the explanation window applies to both problem and solution domain; for every particular slice of view in the solution domain, there ideally exists a corresponding concept and context in the problem domain.

The concept of the explanation window is not limited to spatial dimensions; it also extends to the temporal dimension—the progression of explanations over time. This temporal aspect is best illustrated through various
comprehension models
outlined in
Section

IV-B2
:
In a
top-down
comprehension model, the explanation starts with an architect’s view and progressively narrows down, moving towards more detailed aspects (programmer’s view). This approach helps the explainee first understand the general structure before focusing on specific components.
Conversely, a
bottom-up
model begins with a programmer’s view and builds up to an architect’s view, which is useful when the explainee needs to learn from a granular, detail-oriented perspective and then see how those details fit into the bigger picture.
An
opportunistic
model is more flexible, starting with a small window focused on a particular area of interest. It can expand in either direction along the axes of abstraction and functionality as needed, adapting dynamically to the explainee’s evolving understanding and immediate needs.

Research in psychology, particularly studies on human cognition’s
working memory
limitations
[
43
,
44
,
45
]
underscores the importance of not overloading the explainee with too much information at once. As the explanation window moves and morphs along the component space, the amount of information presented at any given time should remain within the working memory capacity of both parties. This ensures that the explanations remain manageable, facilitating effective learning and retention.

Furthermore, the explanation window can be applied across different architectural views or perspectives, such as logical, process, and deployment views. For example, in a
concern-based
comprehension model, an explainer might maintain a consistent explanation window across these different views to keep the focus on a particular aspect of the system. This ensures that the explanation remains coherent and aligned with the explainee’s specific concerns or responsibilities.

V-C

Explanation Variability

Explanation variability arises from factors that influence how architectural knowledge is conveyed by experts (explainers) to those who need to understand it (explainees).
Our study revealed that variability in explanations is often tailored to specific situations, roles, levels of experience, tasks, and familiarity with the project.
This section discusses how these variables impact the approaches used in architecture explanations.

V-C
1
Explanation situation

The context in which the explanation occurs influences the explanation style and content.
Various situations, such as on-boarding new team members, off-boarding departing experts, or system integration with third-party applications, require different explanatory approaches.
For example, during on-boarding, explainers might start with high-level overviews to provide newcomers with a broad understanding before moving to more detailed aspects.
Conversely, in system integration scenarios, the explanation might be more focused on specific components, interfaces, or integration points crucial for successful system operation.

V-C
2
Role in the project

The explainee’s role within the project also affects the explanation.
Different roles demand different levels of understanding and focus.
A programmer might need detailed knowledge about specific modules or APIs, while someone in a more business-oriented position might only need to understand the architecture at a high level to align it with organisational goals.
As the explainee’s role shifts, so does the focus of the explanation—from high-level strategic overviews for management roles to in-depth technical details for engineering roles.

V-C
3
Technical experience

The amount of technical experience possessed by the explainee is another critical factor.
Less experienced individuals often require more foundational explanations, while more experienced professionals might prefer discussions about the rationale behind decisions.
Experienced developers may already be familiar with standard architectural patterns and thus prefer to focus on unique aspects of the project’s architecture.
On the other hand, newcomers might need more context and background information to understand even the basics.

V-C
4
Task at hand

The specific task that the explainee needs to accomplish also guides the explanation.
If the explainee is responsible for implementing a new feature, the explanation might focus on the relevant modules, data flows, and dependencies within the architecture.
For tasks involving bug fixing or refactoring, the explanation could centre around specific components or the system’s historical design decisions.
This task-oriented variability ensures that explanations directly align with what the explainee needs to know to complete their immediate work.

V-C
5
Familiarity with the project context

Familiarity with the project or its domain also affects how architecture is explained.
Newcomers with little familiarity require comprehensive introductions that cover both the project domain and the specific architecture.
On the other hand, team members who are already familiar with the domain may require only updates or changes to the architecture rather than a full explanation.
For example, an internal transfer from another project within the same domain might already understand the broader context, allowing the explainer to focus more narrowly on differences or recent changes.

Understanding these factors highlights the importance of tailoring architecture explanations to the specific needs of the explainee.
Software architects and team leads should consider these variables—situations, roles, experience levels, tasks, and familiarity—to customise their explanations accordingly.

Moreover, the variability in explanation strategies suggests a need for a flexible toolkit of explanation methods, including documentation, diagrams, and interactive sessions.
Tools and processes that allow dynamic adjustment of the depth and breadth of explanations, via the previously discussed explanation window, can better support diverse comprehension needs and scenarios.

we identified certain explanation activities that tend to happen before, at the beginning, during, at the end, or after the architectural explanation.
On top of this, we propose a set of new activities derived from our analysis that can help explainers and explainees structure their explanation sessions.
These activities, together with the ones extracted from the study (cf.

At the cognitive level, we suggest the introduction of the following activities when planning a software architecture explanation:
(1)
establish explanation goal
, where stakeholders formulate a goal driving the whole explanation (e.g., help the newcomer implement a new feature independently).
The goal directly influences decisions taken in other parts of the process and the learning efficiency.
(2)
establish or adjust explanation activities
, where, given the previously formulated goal, a working roadmap is defined.
The explainer and explainee mutually decide on the activities that should be undertaken.
(3)
establish comprehension model
, where, depending on the learning style of the explainee, the stakeholders agree on a cognitive model to address the explanation.
Lastly, (4)
establish explanation style
, where the explainer adjusts their instruction approach to align with the comprehension model that best fits the explainee’s learning process.

Concerning content-related activities, we differentiate between orthogonal activities that can pop up at any time during the explanation process—and not necessarily within a session—and sequential ones that tended to be mentioned in a specific order by the interviewees.
Orthogonal activities include
consider relevant concerns
, where stakeholders determine which architectural concerns are relevant to the goal and context at hand.
The set also considers the
review documentation
,
explore source code
,
interact with the system
, and
visualise system’s architecture
activities.

Referring back to sequential activities, the
gain prior knowledge
and
prepare explanation meeting
tasks should naturally take place prior to an explanation session.
The
support Q&A
activity plays an essential role when explaining software architecture.
It can actually impact certain decisions taken at the cognitive level as the explainer needs to adjust to answer the explainee’s inquiries.
As concluding activities, stakeholders face the need to
provide/receive feedback
from the given explanation as a way to assess understanding.
This task can inherently lead to the
derive working plan
activity, where actionable steps should be defined to continue pursuing the chosen goal.
Finally, once an explanation session has ended, it is time to perform the
document meeting
activity.

VI
Threats to Validity

Construct validity
One threat is that the participants have different definitions of software architecture. To mitigate this risk, we asked the interviewees to describe their definitions.

Internal validity
A possible risk is that our participants might have misunderstood the questions. To address this threat we conducted a pilot interview and modified the interview guide for the following interviews. Furthermore, participants could be hesitant to share difficulties related to the explanations they gave or received. To address this issue, we guaranteed them full anonymity and explained data privacy rights. Finally, to reduce researcher bias, we performed member-checking by providing the interviewees with a list of the codes originating from their interviews with short descriptions for each code. Participants were encouraged to provide their to correct any misinterpretations. We did not receive any corrections to the codes from the participants who replied.

External validity

We use a combination of convenience and purposive sampling
[
27
,
28
]
for participant recruitment. We expect that the experience and the difficulties of our participants are representative due to expertise, and their working environment. However, considering our concentrated interview scope and the number of participants, we cannot generalise our findings.

VII
Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of software architecture explanations within professional environments. Through a combination of semi-structured interviews with both experienced software architects and newcomers, we identified the primary concerns, strategies, and tools that underpin effective architectural communication.

Our findings reveal that an effective software architecture explanation involves both the problem domain and the technical domain of the software system.
Moreover, explanations include information from a variety of sources surrounding the software system and its development, such as: business-context (business trends, procurement strategies, stakeholder preferences, legal/compliance regulation, …) and development context (IT-roadmaps, system history, and design decisions made about the system). As such, explanations also highlight the relations (’tracability’) between different pieces of information.
Also, the content of explanations clearly differs from any single piece of documentation that is traditionally made during the development of software.

Furthermore, when explaining an architecture, it is necessary to manage the complexity of the explanation. This is typically done by selecting the appropriate abstraction level(s) of abstraction and scoping the concerned functionalities depending on the role and experience of the explainee and the end-goal of the explanation.

Future research should focus on developing and evaluating tools that facilitate the creation and maintenance of dynamic, real-time architectural explanations, as well as methods to streamline the explanation process across different levels of expertise. Such tools and processes can leverage the adapted Twin Peaks model and the introduced Explanation Window to guide the selection of information and slicing of architecture visualisations (diagrams). Moreover, exploring the role of emerging technologies, such as on-demand AI-driven explanation assistants, could prove beneficial in enhancing the clarity and efficiency of software architecture explanations.
