EQUATOR: A Deterministic Framework for Evaluating LLM Reasoning with Open-Ended Questions. # v1.0.0-beta
Raymond Bernard, Shaina Raza PhD, Subhabrata Das, PhD, Rahul Murugan

Abstract
Despite the remarkable coherence of Large Language Models (LLMs), existing evaluation methods often suffer from fluency bias and rely heavily on multiple-choice formats, making it difficult to assess factual accuracy and complex reasoning effectively. LLMs thus frequently generate factually inaccurate responses, especially in complex reasoning tasks, highlighting two prominent challenges: (1) the inadequacy of existing methods to evaluate reasoning and factual accuracy effectively, and (2) the reliance on human evaluators for nuanced judgment, as illustrated by Williams and Huckle (2024) [ 1 ] , who found manual grading indispensable despite automated grading advancements. To address evaluation gaps in open-ended reasoning tasks, we introduce the EQUATOR Evaluator (Evaluation of Question Answering Thoroughness in Open-ended Reasoning). This framework combines deterministic scoring with a focus on factual accuracy and robust reasoning assessment. Using a vector database, EQUATOR pairs open-ended questions with human-evaluated answers, enabling more precise and scalable evaluations. In practice, EQUATOR significantly reduces reliance on human evaluators for scoring and improves scalability compared to Williams and Huckle’s (2004) [ 1 ] methods. Our results demonstrate that this framework significantly outperforms traditional multiple-choice evaluations while maintaining high accuracy standards. Additionally, we introduce an automated evaluation process leveraging smaller, locally hosted LLMs. We used LLaMA 3.2B, running on the Ollama binaries to streamline our assessments. This work establishes a new paradigm for evaluating LLM performance, emphasizing factual accuracy and reasoning ability, and provides a robust methodological foundation for future research.

Introduction

Can a deterministic scoring framework, specifically the EQUATOR Evaluator, provide a more objective, reliable, and scalable method for evaluating the factual accuracy and reasoning capabilities of LLMs in response to open-ended questions compared to traditional evaluation methods?
The paper challenges the reliance on traditional evaluation methods, such as multiple-choice formats, which are often susceptible to biases like fluency bias and lack a thorough assessment of reasoning abilities.
The paper explores the hypothesis that a deterministic scoring framework, utilizing a vector database of human-evaluated answers, can mitigate these limitations and offer a more precise evaluation of LLM performance, particularly in complex reasoning tasks where factual accuracy is paramount.

As Large Language Models (LLMs) become increasingly integrated into various applications, evaluating their responses accurately is critical. Traditional evaluation methods often fail to assess model performance, mainly because LLMs generate fluent and logically coherent responses that are stochastic and hallucinate, which means they are usually factually incorrect

The real-world consequences of hallucinations and reasoning failures in large language models (LLMs) are profound, particularly in high-stakes applications such as healthcare, finance, legal systems, and governance. LLM hallucinations—when models generate false or fabricated information—can lead to decisions based on incorrect or misleading data, with potentially catastrophic outcomes. In healthcare, for instance, an LLM providing inaccurate advice on medical treatments or diagnostics could jeopardize patient safety. Similarly, in the financial sector, reasoning failures in tasks such as risk assessment, portfolio management, or fraud detection can result in significant monetary losses, regulatory violations, or even systemic instability. The stakes are equally high in legal systems, where flawed reasoning could misguide legal professionals or decision-makers, undermining justice and fairness.

A deterministic framework like EQUATOR is important because of its ability to systematically evaluate LLM reasoning under open-ended and context-dependent scenarios, where the risk of errors is amplified. By identifying the limitations of LLMs in reasoning and hallucination-prone areas, EQUATOR helps mitigate the risks associated with deploying these models in real-world, high-stakes environments. For instance, the framework’s structured evaluation ensures that models meet rigorous benchmarks before applying in sensitive domains. This not only improves the reliability of AI systems but also fosters trust among stakeholders who rely on them for critical decision-making. EQUATOR’s deterministic and reproducible approach to evaluation is therefore a vital step in bridging the gap between LLM capabilities and the demands of real-world applications, ensuring that their deployment aligns with safety, accuracy, and accountability standards.

2.1
Limitations of Existing Benchmarks

Many traditional evaluation frameworks, such as SuperGLUE (Wang et al., 2019)
[
11
]
and BIG-bench (Srivastava et al., 2022)
[
12
]
, focus on tasks that LLMs are already proficient in, like linguistic fluency and simple reasoning. While these benchmarks have advanced the field, they rely heavily on multiple-choice scenarios, which present inherent limitations:

Simplistic Question Formats: Multiple-choice questions often reduce the complexity of open-ended reasoning to a set of predefined answers, limiting the scope of model evaluation.

Bias Toward Fluent Responses: LLMs frequently score well on fluency-focused benchmarks, even when their answers are factually incorrect.

Performance Saturation: Models rapidly approach human-level scores on multiple-choice benchmarks, making it challenging to distinguish between truly innovative architectures and incremental improvements.

2.2
Innovations in EQUATOR

EQUATOR sets itself apart by addressing these limitations through a deterministic scoring framework designed for open-ended questions. This framework introduces several innovations:

Open-Ended Evaluation: Unlike prior benchmarks, EQUATOR eliminates the reliance on multiple-choice formats. By evaluating free-form responses, it captures the nuanced reasoning capabilities and factual accuracy of LLMs.

Deterministic Scoring: The framework employs a vector database of human-evaluated answers, ensuring objective and reproducible assessments. This contrasts with traditional methods that rely on subjective human grading or automated metrics susceptible to fluency bias.

Integration of Smaller LLMs: EQUATOR leverages smaller, locally hosted models like LLaMA 3.2B as automated evaluators. This approach balances computational efficiency with advanced language understanding, reducing the overhead associated with large-scale models.

2.3
Addressing Hallucinations and Reasoning Failures

The prevalence of hallucinations—where LLMs generate fluent but factually incorrect responses—is a significant challenge in model evaluation. Prior studies, such as those by Zhang et al. (2023)
[
13
]
and Ohi et al. (2024)
[
3
]
, have proposed methods to mitigate evaluation bias, but these often fall short in open-ended scenarios. EQUATOR’s deterministic framework directly tackles this issue by:

Pairing questions with human-evaluated answers stored in a vector database.

Using cosine similarity to objectively evaluate semantic closeness between model-generated responses and the reference answers.

Adopting binary scoring criteria, which prioritize factual correctness over reasoning or fluency.

3.2
Vector Database Construction

We construct a vector database using embeddings of open-ended questions and their corresponding human-evaluated correct answers. This database serves as the source of truth for evaluating LLM responses.
For embedding both questions and human-evaluated answers, we utilized the
ll-minilm
model from the Ollama library
[
14
]
. This model generates 384-dimensional sentence embeddings, balancing efficiency and performance in capturing semantic similarity.
Text inputs were preprocessed to maintain consistency by serializing them into JSON format. Embeddings were generated using a local embedding service implemented with Ollama and stored in a ChromaDB vector database. Cosine similarity was employed to measure the semantic closeness between embeddings during retrieval and evaluation.

3.3
Components

The evaluation framework relies on the following components:

1.

Semantic Similarity Search:
Vector databases compare student responses to reference answers by embedding them as vectors and performing similarity searches to determine their proximity to high-quality responses. This is analogous to how systems like VectorRAG retrieve relevant information for LLMs.

2.

Flexibility and Scalability:
Vector databases are more straightforward to scale and manage than graph databases, making them suitable for handling large datasets and frequently updated evaluation criteria.

3.

Integration with LLMs:
Vector databases integrate with LLMs for tasks such as retrieval, ranking, and clustering, enabling nuanced analysis
[
15
]
and accurate evaluation of student responses.

4.

Response Evaluation:
LLM-generated responses are embedded using the same methodology and compared against the vector database using cosine similarity. A high similarity score indicates that the response closely matches the correct answer.

5.

Threshold Determination:
Instead of setting an empirical similarity threshold, we retrieve the most semantically similar answer from the vector database as the answer key. The evaluator LLM compares the student’s response directly with this key. According to predefined criteria, a score of 100% is assigned if the answers match exactly, and 0% otherwise. This binary evaluation eliminates the need for manual threshold tuning while leveraging the evaluator LLM’s capabilities.

3.4
Deterministic Scoring Framework

Given a question
Q_{s}
, a student’s answer
A_{s}
, and a set of answer keys
K=\{K_{1},K_{2},\dots,K_{n}\}
, the deterministic scoring framework is defined through the following steps:

Step 1: Embed Inputs
Each input is transformed into its corresponding embedding representation:

\displaystyle Q_{e}

\displaystyle=\text{Embed}(Q_{s})\quad\text{(Embedding of the Question)}

\displaystyle A_{e}

\displaystyle=\text{Embed}(A_{s})\quad\text{(Embedding of the Student's Answer)}

\displaystyle K_{e}

\displaystyle=\{\text{Embed}(K_{i})\ |\ K_{i}\in K\}\quad\text{(Embeddings of the Answer Keys)}

Step 2: Retrieve Closest Answer Key

The closest answer key
K_{\text{best}}
is identified by maximizing the cosine similarity between the embedding of the student’s answer
A_{e}
and the embeddings of the answer keys
K_{e}
:

K_{\text{best}}=\arg\max_{K_{i}\in K}\text{Sim}(A_{e},K_{i})

Where the cosine similarity function is defined as:

\text{Sim}(x,y)=\frac{x\cdot y}{\|x\|\|y\|}

Step 3: Evaluate Match Based on Instructions

The match between the student’s answer and the best-matching answer key is evaluated based on a predefined similarity threshold
\tau
:

\text{Match}(A_{e},K_{\text{best}})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\text{Sim}(A_{e},K_{\text{best}})\geq\tau\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Step 4: Assign Score
The final score
S
is computed as:

S=\text{Match}(A_{e},K_{\text{best}})\times 100\%

Output
The framework outputs the final score
S
, which represents the deterministic evaluation of the student’s response against the provided set of answer keys.

3.5
LLM in an Evaluator Role

To automate the evaluation process and eliminate human bias, we employed a smaller LLM, specifically LLaMA 3.2B, as the evaluator. The evaluator LLM is prompted to act as a strict high school teacher who grades based solely on the provided answer key, ignoring reasoning or persuasive elements in the student’s response.

The scoring criteria are binary:

•

100%
: Assigned when the student’s final answer exactly matches the answer key.

•

0%
: Assigned when the student’s final answer does not match the answer key. No partial credit is allowed.

The evaluator LLM returns the score and a brief explanation in a structured JSON format. This method ensures a consistent and objective evaluation process while leveraging the efficiency of smaller LLMs to achieve scalability.

Token Consumption and the Importance of Savings

10.1
Token Consumption Calculation

Token consumption is a critical metric in evaluating the efficiency of natural language processing (NLP) models. In our study, token consumption is calculated based on the principle that one token represents approximately
four English text characters
of text in English. This approach aligns with the tokenization mechanism used by major language models, where a token can include characters, subwords, or full words, depending on the context.

The token count for a given text is calculated as:

\text{Tokens}=\left\lceil\frac{\text{Character Count}}{4}\right\rceil

(1)

Where:

•

Character Count
refers to the total number of characters in the input or output text.

•

The calculation ensures a minimum token count of one for any non-empty text.

For this study, we compute token consumption across several key components:

1.

Questions
: The input text for the model.

2.

Responses
: The model-generated responses, either as open-ended text or multiple-choice evaluations.

3.

Scores
: Numerical assessment or feedback.

10.2
Measuring Token Consumption with EQUATOR Evaluator

The LLM Evaluator is designed to reduce token consumption by leveraging a
localized evaluation mechanism
. Instead of sending both the question and multiple-choice options to the language model, the LLM Evaluator only sends the
questions
. The evaluation process is then handled locally, significantly reducing the number of tokens the model processes.

For example:

•

Multiple-Choice Method
:

–

Sends both the question and multiple-choice options for external evaluation.

–

Requires additional tokens for the model’s response to multiple options.

•

EQUATOR Evaluator
:

–

Sends only the question, with responses and scoring computed locally.

–

Eliminates token usage associated with transmitting and processing multiple-choice options.

This streamlined approach minimizes the reliance on the model, optimizing token usage and computational resources.

10.3
Importance of Token Savings

Token savings correlate with computational efficiency and cost reduction in NLP workflows. Most language models, particularly those accessible via APIs, charge based on the number of tokens processed during input and output. By reducing token consumption:

•

Cost Efficiency
: Organizations save on operational expenses.

•

Environmental Impact
: Lower computational requirements lead to reduced energy consumption, contributing to more sustainable AI practices.

•

Scalability
: Token-efficient systems can handle larger datasets or higher workloads within fixed resource constraints.

In our analysis, the EQUATOR Evaluator demonstrated significant token savings, particularly in the total token consumption metric. As shown in Table 8, the EQUATOR Evaluator reduced total token consumption by more than 12% compared to the multiple-choice method. This reduction underscores its value as an efficient evaluation framework.

Discussion

11.1
Multiple Choice Questions strengths

•

Efficiency:
The evaluation of multiple choice questions is considerably faster than that of open-ended generation questions. This is because multiple-choice questions can be reduced to a softmax operation over output logits, whereas open-ended questions require more complex auto-regressive generation and additional post-processing. For example, running the Qwen1.5-32B model on the original GSM8K dataset took 7 hours, while evaluating a multiple-choice version with four options only took 13 minutes.
[
13
]

•

Robustness:
LLM performance on multiple-choice questions remains stable, even when the distractor options and their order are randomized. An experiment indicated that the variation in model performance due to distractor choices was minor compared to the differences among models.
[
13
]
.

•

Correlation with Open-Ended Evaluation:
There is a strong correlation between LLM performance on multiple choice questions and their performance on open-ended generation tasks, regardless of the number of options. This was demonstrated in multiple experiments with varying numbers of multiple-choice options
[
13
]
.

•

Improving Existing Benchmarks:
Multiple choice questions can convert short-answer benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH into a more efficient format for evaluating LLMs, although this approach is limited to tasks with short, unique answers and doesn’t apply to tasks like translation or summarization
[
13
]
.

11.2
Multiple Choice Questions Weakness

•

Reliance on Short, Unique Answers:
The conversion of generation benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH into multiple-choice format relies on short, unique ground truth answers. This method is not suitable for open-ended tasks such as machine translation or summarization, which may not have clear, definitive answers
[
13
]
.

•

Limited Scope for Assessing Complex Capabilities:
In converting code generation datasets to a multiple choice format, researchers focused on program output prediction, which differs from the original program synthesis tasks in Human Eval and MBPP. As a result, the multiple-choice benchmark PythonIO may not fully capture the complex capabilities of the original datasets
[
13
]
.

•

Potential Bias Towards Certain Options:
LLMs may show bias towards selecting specific answer options, even when incorrect. This could be due to factors like training data or model architecture, potentially skewing evaluation outcomes
[
13
]
.

•

Tokenization Issues with Options:
The tokenization of options can affect evaluation accuracy. For example, tokenizing options like "A" may not consistently yield the correct token ID, as some tokenizers differentiate between "A" and " A". This issue can lead to incorrect scoring if not correctly handled
[
13
]
.

Limitations

In developing this version of the EQUATOR Evaluator, our primary aim is to rigorously assess leading models by focusing on their reasoning and logic, particularly at critical failure points. By identifying these limitations—especially in areas where human reasoning excels and LLMs often struggle—we highlight key opportunities for improvement. This evaluation is vital for pinpointing where LLMs can enhance their reasoning capabilities and achieve higher factual accuracy.

Inspired by unit testing in software development, our evaluation strategy emphasizes systematic testing at failure points until LLMs can reliably provide correct answers. However, we recognize that our evaluator may miss certain nuances required to assess information beyond the database vector, particularly in domains like history, politics, social science, and philosophy.

Our primary focus is to investigate well-documented logical and reasoning pitfalls, as discussed by Williams and Huckle
[
1
]
, where LLMs typically underperform. A significant innovation in our approach is the capability of our evaluator to assess open-ended questions—an area often overlooked by major LLM benchmarks, which tend to favor multiple-choice formats. While multiple-choice evaluations simplify the assessment process, we argue that they introduce inherent flaws, which we address in detail in the section on
Weaknesses of Multiple-Choice Questions
.

Importantly, we do not aim to constrain the creative potential of LLMs or limit the reasoning processes essential for innovation or nuanced explorations in domains like history, politics, or social sciences. Instead, our goal is to observe when LLMs overcome common logical and reasoning challenges. To that end, our framework evaluates factual accuracy and reasoning through a human-centric lens. As LLMs progress toward human-level reasoning, we will continue to adapt and refine our evaluation methodologies to align with their advancements.

We envision the LLM Evaluator being deployed in high-stakes domains, such as medicine and law, where reasoning accuracy is critical. Additionally, the evaluator can address bias-related concerns, particularly regarding underrepresented groups and diverse artistic expressions.

While we acknowledge that LLMs may eventually surpass human capabilities, our current evaluation framework is designed to measure their performance against the benchmarks of human reasoning.

12.1
Addressing Evaluation Bias

EQUATOR deterministic scoring framework mitigates the bias introduced by human evaluators who may favor fluent and persuasive responses.
The core principle of EQUATOR is to priortize factual correctness and consistency over fluency, potential position bias or persuasiveness. However, we anticipate that the choice of prompts
[
7
]
, the interpretation of the choices and critera for evaluation can be shaped by the perspectives or experiences of the human evaluators. The topic of bias is inherently subjective and influenced by an individual’s persona, shaped by factors such as their background, experiences, beliefs, and roles, all of which affect their perspectives and judgments
[
17
]
.

Below, we identify potential sources of bias and propose strategies for their mitigation.

12.2
Sources of Biases

Selection of Human-Evaluated Answers in the Vector Database.

A core component of EQUATOR is the vector database that constructed from human-evaluated answers. We anticipate that if the set of human evaluators is not diverse, whether demographically, culturally, or experientially, the resulting “gold-standard” answers may reflect certain viewpoints more than others. In this case, the deterministic scoring framework might prefer certain persona over others that are not systematically in advantage (e.g., in certain races, demogrpahics)
We also recognize the potential for the current database to become outdated and susceptible to concept drift over time.

Smaller language models (SLMs) as an Evaluator.

Using a SLM for evaluation can itself be a source of bias. SLMs typically have reduced capacity and training data compared to larger models, making them more susceptible to inaccuracies, narrow domain coverage, or oversights in reasoning. Recent comparative research
[
18
]
highlights that smaller LLMs may over-rely on specific linguistic cues or patterns learned during training. This can lead to biased scoring, particularly favoring or penalizing certain writing styles or argumentation methods.

Embedding-based models

Embeddings are learned representations that can encode societal and cultural biases present in their training corpus. Any bias in the embedding model representation of language
[
19
]
, such as associating certain demographics with negative sentiment or reinforcing stereotypes, may distort similarity measurements between model responses and reference answers. Such distortions can lead to biased retrieval of “closest” matches or unreliable comparisons, especially in edge cases or nuanced topics.

12.2.1
Mitigation Strategies

Diverse datasets

To mitigate bias stemming from an insufficiently representative set of reference answers, the EQUATOR vector dataset should incorporate contributions from a diverse pool of human evaluators. While crowdsourcing can provide variety, expert intervention is highly valued for ensuring quality and depth. Additionally, conducting periodic audits can help identify and address emerging biases before they become systemic.

Mixture of Evaluators

Instead of relying on a SLM as a single evaluator, it is good to have an ensemble of SLMs to minimize individual idiosyncrasies or systematic biases. Scoring mechanisms like majority voting or weighted voting, as highlighted in recent works
[
20
]
, can be used to deliver a more balanced and robust assessment by reducing the influence of any single evaluator perspective.

Transparency

Ensuring transparency in the embedding and evaluation processes is crucial for better monitoring and debugging of biases. Key elements such as training procedures, hyperparameters, and the corpora used for the embedding model should be documented to enable stakeholders to trace and identify potential sources of bias. Tools like Weights & Biases
1
1

1

https://wandb.ai/site/
, MLflow
2
2

2

https://mlflow.org/
, and similar platforms can be employed for tracking experiments. Additionally, interpretability tools (e.g., embedding visualizations or attention mechanism analysis) play a significant role in debugging the training and testing process and improving the interpretability of results.

Human-Centredness

A human-centred approach places the needs, values, and lived experiences of diverse individuals at the core of the design and evaluation process
[
21
]
. We can involve a broad group of stakeholders, such as subject matter experts, end-users, and communities potentially those affected by biases, to gain meaningful insights into the wide range of human experiences into the LLM work. This participatory process can bring inclusivity, fairness and ethical considerations such as privacy, consent, and equitable representation.

Conclusion

We introduced a deterministic scoring framework that prioritizes factual precision in evaluating LLM responses to open-ended questions. By leveraging a vector database of human-evaluated answers, we provide an objective, replicable, and superior alternative to traditional multiple-choice evaluation techniques.

Our approach effectively tackles the challenges of assessing LLMs in reasoning and logic tasks, offering a scalable solution that minimizes computational overhead. Integrating smaller LLMs for automated evaluation further enhances the efficiency and accessibility of our method.

The LLM Evaluator represents a pivotal advancement, enabling systematic evaluation of state-of-the-art models while uncovering their failure points. This groundwork opens the door to incorporating advanced reasoning steps and symbolic logic, addressing limitations at inference time. Ultimately, our vision extends to developing innovative methodologies incorporating fine-tuning and pre-training, paving the way for robust, next-generation LLM performance.

14
Future Work

Future research will focus on expanding the dataset to encompass a wider variety of question types and difficulty levels. As of this writing, the dataset has been increased to 1,005 questions, laying the groundwork for more robust evaluations. We also plan to explore integrating advanced reasoning techniques such as Chain-of-Thought prompting, Mixture of Agent frameworks, and Neuro-Symbolic AI hybrid methods. These approaches hold significant promise in advancing the capabilities of LLMs and are worth testing to determine their effectiveness in improving reasoning performance.

1. Cohen, Jacob (1988). Statistical Power Analysis for the Behavioral Sciences (2nd Edition).

This seminal book is the cornerstone of statistical power analysis, offering a comprehensive guide to understanding and applying statistical methods in behavioral research. Cohen introduces the concept of
effect size
as a practical measure of the magnitude of differences between groups, which is independent of sample size.

Key Contributions:

•

Cohen’s d
: A specific measure of effect size used to quantify the difference between two group means in terms of standard deviation units. Cohen also proposed benchmarks for interpreting effect size:

–

Small
(d = 0.2): Minor difference.

–

Medium
(d = 0.5): Noticeable but not large.

–

Large
(d = 0.8): Substantial difference.

•

Emphasizes that statistical significance alone is insufficient for meaningful research, advocating for the combined use of effect size and power analysis.

•

Provides work examples and recommendations for designing experiments with adequate statistical power.

According to Cohen’s conventions:

•

Small effect:
d=0.2

•

Medium effect:
d=0.5

•

Large effect:
d=0.8

This earlier edition laid the foundation for statistical power analysis as a critical part of experimental design and evaluation. Introduced the idea of

Formula to Estimate Sample Size (Number of Questions):

You can use a
sample size formula
commonly used in statistics:

n=\frac{Z^{2}\cdot p\cdot(1-p)}{E^{2}}

Where:

•

n
= required sample size (number of questions)

•

Z
= Z-score, based on the desired confidence level (1.96 for 95% confidence)

•

p
= estimated proportion of correct responses (0.5 is a safe assumption if unsure)

•

E
= margin of error (e.g., 0.05 for ±5%)

Example:

Confidence Level: 95% (
Z=1.96
) Estimated proportion of correct responses: 50% (
p=0.5
)
Margin of Error: ±5% (
E=0.05
)

n=\frac{(1.96)^{2}\cdot 0.5\cdot(1-0.5)}{(0.05)^{2}}=\frac{3.8416\cdot 0.25}{0.0025}=384.16

So, approximately
385 questions
are needed to achieve a statistically significant result with a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error.
