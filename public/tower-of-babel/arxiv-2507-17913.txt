A complete t t -intersection theorem for families of spanning trees
Elizaveta Iarovikova, Andrey Kupavskii

Abstract
. Let 𝒯 n \mathcal{T}_{n} denote the set of all labelled spanning trees of K n K_{n} . A family ℱ ⊂ 𝒯 n \mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{T}_{n} is t t -intersecting if for all A , B ∈ ℱ A,B\in\mathcal{F} the trees A A and B B share at least t t edges. In this paper, we determine for n > n 0 n>n_{0} the size of the largest t t -intersecting family ℱ ⊂ T n \mathcal{F}\subset T_{n} for all meaningful values of t t ( t ⩽ n − 1 t\leqslant n-1 ). This result is a rare instance when a complete t t -intersection theorem for a given type of structures is known.



1.
Introduction

We use standard notation
[n]
for the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
2^{X}
for the power set of
X
and
{X\choose k}
for the set of all
k
-element subsets of
X
. A family of sets
\mathcal{F}
is
t
-intersecting
if for any
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
we have
|A\cap B|\geqslant t.
For shorthand, we say that a family is
intersecting
instead of
1
-intersecting. The study of
t
-intersecting families goes back to the seminal paper of Erdős, Ko and Rado
[
4
]
, who studied intersecting and
t
-intersecting families in
2^{[n]}
and
{[n]\choose k}
. Shortly after, Katona
[
13
]
determined the largest
t
-intersecting family of sets in
2^{[n]}
for all
n,t.
In particular, he showed that for even
n+t
, the largest such family consists of all sets of size at least
(n+t)/2
.

Determining the largest
t
-intersecting family for
{[n]\choose k}
proved to be a much harder task. After several partial results
[
6
,
21
,
8
]
by Frankl, Wilson, and Frankl and Füredi, Ahlswede and Khachatrian
[
1
]
confirmed the conjecture of Frankl
[
6
]
and showed that, for any
n,k,t
, the largest
t
-intersecting family in
{[n]\choose k}
must, up to isomorphism, have the form

(1)

\Big\{F\in{[n]\choose k}:|F\cap[t+2i]|\geqslant t+i\Big\}

for some integer
i\geqslant 0
. A similar result was shown by Filmus
[
5
]
for
2^{[n]}
with the
p
-biased measure.

Over the last 65 years, the Erdős–Ko–Rado type theorems were obtained for many different structures. However, the only other case where a complete
t
-intersection theorem is known that we are aware of is the result of Frankl and Wilson
[
10
]
that determines the largest family of
k
-dimensional subspaces of an
n
-dimensional vector space over a finite field
GF(q)
with
q
elements. There, we say that the family of subspaces is
t
-intersecting if the intersection of any two subspaces from the family has dimension at least
t
.

One of the most notable and well-studied structures in that respect is
t
-intersecting permutations. We say that two permutations
\pi,\sigma
intersect in at least
t
elements if there are
t
distinct elements
x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}
such that
\sigma(x_{i})=\pi(x_{i})
for all
t
. Frankl and Deza showed that any intersecting family of permutations
[n]\to[n]
has size at most
(n-1)!
. After a series of results by Ellis, Friedgut and Pilpel
[
3
]
, Kupavskii and Zakharov
[
18
]
, Keller, Lifshitz, Minzer and Sheinfield
[
14
]
, Kupavskii
[
16
]
determined the largest family of
t
-intersecting permutations for all
t\leqslant(1-\epsilon)n
and
n>n_{0}(\epsilon)
. For
t\geqslant n/2
, the correct answer is no longer
(n-t)!,
corresponding to the family of all permutations that fix the elements in
[t],
but rather the examples of the type (
1
).

Permutations
[n]\to[n]
can be thought of as perfect matching in the complete bipartite graph
K_{n,n}.
Two permutations
t
-intersect iff the corresponding matchings share at least
t
edges. In this paper, we study
t
-intersecting families of closely related objects: spanning trees. We show that the situation with
t
-intersection theorems for trees is somewhat simpler than that for permutations.
Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}
denote the set of labelled spanning trees in a complete graph on
n
vertices. Cayley’s tree formula states that
|\mathcal{T}_{n}|=n^{n-2}
. We say that a family
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{T}_{n}
is
t
-intersecting
if for all
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
the trees
A
and
B
share at least
t
edges.

A common example of
t
-intersecting families are
trivial

t
-intersecting families. Those are the families of sets that all contain a given
t
-element set. In this paper, we show that such trivial intersecting families are always extremal for
t
-intersecting families of trees.
The main result of this paper is the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

There exists an absolute constant
n_{0}
such that for all
n\geqslant n_{0}
and
2\leqslant t\leqslant n-2
the following holds. If
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{T}_{n}
is
t
-intersecting then
|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant c_{n,t}n^{n-2-t}
, where
c_{n,t}
is the largest possible product of
n-t
positive integers with sum
n
. Moreover, the equality holds if and only if
\mathcal{F}
is the family of all spanning trees containing a fixed forest
F
with
t
edges and connected components of almost equal sizes.

To prove these results, we use the spread approximation technique introduced in
[
18
]
and developed in subsequent papers of the second author and coauthors
[
15
,
16
,
9
,
17
]
. In particular, our proof builds on the proof of the main result in
[
16
]
by the second author.

Note that each tree has
n-1
edges, and thus an
(n-1)
-intersecting family of spanning trees can contain at most one spanning tree. Thus the bound
t\leqslant n-2.
Our result extends the result of Frankl, Hurlbert, Ihringer, Kupavskii, Lindzey, Meagher and Tej Pantangi, who showed the same result for
n\geqslant 2^{19}
and
1<t\leqslant\frac{n}{4032\log_{2}n}
this result was proved in
[
7
]
. In this paper, for clarity we only present the proof for large
t
, even though in
[
7
]
the authors also used spread approximations and so the proof could be unified. The authors made certain conjectures concerning the size of the largest
t
-intersecting families of spanning trees for large
t,
and our main result confirms these conjectures.

The case
t=1
is somewhat different, as the largest family is not exactly trivial. The following was proved in
[
7
]
.

Theorem 2
.

For
n>2^{19}
, let
\mathcal{F}
be
1
-intersecting family of spanning trees on
n
vertices. Then

|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant 2n^{n-3}+n-2.

Moreover, equality holds if and only if
\mathcal{F}
consists of all stars plus all trees containing a fixed edge.

We should note that our proof of Theorem
1
implies that for
t<(1-\epsilon)\frac{n}{2}
or
t\geqslant n/2
any family that has size
(1+o(1))
times the size of the extremal family must be very close to a trivial
t
-intersecting family. In the regime
(1-\epsilon)\frac{n}{2}\leqslant t<n/2,
however, there is another example of approximately the same size: the analogue of the family as in (
1
) with
i=1.
For permutations, such example overtakes the trivial family once
t
passes across
n/2.
Here, however, once
t
becomes larger than
n/2,
the size of the corresponding
i=1
example becomes constant times smaller than the size of the trivial example, and the ratio grows as
t
grows. This could serve as one explanation for the fact that we managed to prove the
t
-intersection theorem in the entire range.

In the next section, we discuss spread families. In Section
3
we compare the sizes of
t
-intersecting families of trees in the spirit of (
1
) and show that trivial
t
-intersecting families are always the largest. The following sections are dedicated to the proof of the main theorem.

2.
Preliminaries

We treat spanning trees as
(n-1)
-element subsets of the set of all edges, therefore
\mathcal{T}_{n}\subset{{n\choose 2}\choose n-1}
. We use the following notation that is standard when working with families of sets. Given families
\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{S}
and a set
X
, we define

\mathcal{F}(X):=\{A\setminus X:\ A\in\mathcal{F},\,X\subset A\}

\mathcal{F}[X]:=\{A:\ A\in\mathcal{F},\,X\subset A\}

\mathcal{F}[\mathcal{S}]:=\bigcup\limits_{X\in\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{F}[X].

Given
q,r>1
a family
\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{[n]}
is said to be
r
-spread
if

|\mathcal{F}(X)|<r^{-|X|}|\mathcal{F}|

for all
X\subset[n]
.

We will use several observations concerning spread families.

Observation 3
.

If for some
\alpha>1
we have a family
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose\leqslant k}
such that
|\mathcal{F}|>\alpha^{k}
, then there is a set
X
such that
|\mathcal{F}(X)|>1
and
\mathcal{F}(X)
is
\alpha
-spread.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{F}
is
\alpha
-spread then we are done. Otherwise, find an inclusion-maximal set
X
such that
|\mathcal{F}(X)|\geqslant\alpha^{-|X|}|\mathcal{F}|.
Note that in this case
|\mathcal{F}(X)|\geqslant\alpha^{-k}|\mathcal{F}|>1
. Assume
\mathcal{F}(X)
is not spread, then there is a nonempty set
Y
disjoint from
X
such that
|\mathcal{F}(X\cup Y)|\geqslant\alpha^{-|Y|}|\mathcal{F}(X)|\geqslant\alpha^{-|X|-|Y|}|\mathcal{F}|
. But then we should have chosen
Y\cup X
in place of
X
which was supposed to be inclusion-maximal.
∎

Arguing as in the proof of Observation
3
, we get the following observation.

Observation 4
.

Fix
\alpha>1
and consider a family
\mathcal{F}
. If
X
is an inclusion-maximal set with the property that
\mathcal{F}(X)\geqslant\alpha^{-|X|}|\mathcal{F}|
, then
\mathcal{F}(X)
is
\alpha
-spread.

The main theorem concerning spread families is the following statement from
[
2
]
called the ‘Spread lemma’. We will need its sharpening due to Hu
[
11
]
. Later, Stoeckl in
[
20
]
established the same theorem with
1+h_{2}(\delta)\leqslant 2
instead of constant
2
, where
h_{2}(\delta)=-\delta\log_{2}\delta-(1-\delta)\log_{2}(1-\delta)
is the binary entropy of
\delta
.

Theorem 5
.

If for some
n,k,r\geqslant 1
a family
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose\leqslant k}
is
r
-spread and
W
is an
(\beta\delta)
-random subset of
[n]
, then

\Pr[\exists F\in\mathcal{F}\ :\ F\subset W]\geqslant 1-\Big(\frac{2}{\log_{2}(r\delta)}\Big)^{\beta}k.

We will also use an application of this theorem to find disjoint sets in spread families.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2}\subset 2^{[m]}
be
R
-spread uniform families of uniformity at most
k
. Suppose that
R>2^{5}\log_{2}2k
. Then there exist disjoint sets
G_{1}\in\mathcal{G}_{1},G_{2}\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
.

Proof.

Let us put
\beta=\log_{2}(2k)
and
\delta=(2\log_{2}(2k))^{-1}
. Note that
\beta\delta=\frac{1}{2}
and
R\delta>2^{4}
by our choice of
R
. Theorem
5
implies that a
\frac{1}{2}
-random subset
W_{i}
of
[m]
contains a set from
\mathcal{G}_{i}
with probability strictly bigger than

1-\Big(\frac{2}{\log_{2}2^{4}}\Big)^{\log_{2}(2k)}k=1-2^{-\log_{2}(2k)}k=\frac{1}{2}.

Note that
[n]\setminus W
is also a
\frac{1}{2}
-random subset of
[m]
. A simple union bound implies that with positive probability there are
G_{1}\in\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
G_{2}\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
such that
G_{1}\subset W,G_{2}\subset[m]\setminus W
. Thus there are disjoint
G_{1}\in\mathcal{G}_{1},G_{2}\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
.
∎

3.
Reconstruction of spanning trees

In this section, we discuss families of spanning trees containing a fixed subforest. The main goal being to compare the size of different examples of
t
-intersecting families and ultimately show that trivial families are always the largest.

We will use a generalization of Cayley’s tree formula from
[
19
, Lemma 6]
that counts the number of labelled trees that contain a specific subforest. We will also compare the sizes of various
t
-intersecting families. We refer to the number of edges in the forest as to its
size
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
F
be a spanning forest in the
K_{n}
that has
m
connected components of sizes
q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{m}
. Then the number of spanning trees in
K_{n}
, that contain
F
is exactly

q_{1}q_{2}\cdots q_{m}\ n^{n-2-\sum_{i=1}^{m}(q_{i}-1)}.

Let
c_{n,t}
denote the maximal size of
|\mathcal{T}_{n}[F]|/n^{n-2-t}
over all spanning forests of complete
n
-vertex graph with
t
edges, that is

c_{n,t}=\max_{\sum_{i=1}^{m}(q_{i}-1)=t,\sum_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}=n}q_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot q_{m}.

First, note that
m=n-t
. It is easy to see that the maximum value of
c_{n,t}
is attained if all variables
q_{i}
are almost equal, that is, differ by no more than 1.

More precisely, if
r
is such non-negative integer that
n=(n-t)\lfloor\frac{n}{n-t}\rfloor+r,r<n-t
. Then we have

(2)

c_{n,t}=\Big\lceil\frac{n}{n-t}\Big\rceil^{r}\cdot\Big\lfloor\frac{n}{n-t}\Big\rfloor^{n-t-r}.

Recall that the elements of
\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
correspond to the sizes of connected components of a certain spanning forest
F
. Therefore a forest
F
maximizes the value of
\mathcal{T}_{n}[F]
among all forests with
t
edges if and only if it consists of
n-t
connected components of sizes
\lfloor\frac{n}{n-t}\rfloor
and
\lceil\frac{n}{n-t}\rceil
. We note that the structure inside the components plays no role here.

Later we will prove that the extremal example of a
t
-intersecting family is always a trivial family, i. e. a family that consists of all spanning trees containing a fixed spanning forest
F
. Note that in the extremal case
F
should be as described above. However we will start with a broader collection of
t
-intersecting families. For
r=0,1,\ldots,n-1-t
and a spanning forest
F
of size
t+2r
consider a
t
-intersecting family

\mathcal{U}_{n,t,r,F}=\{A\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\mid|A\cap F|\geqslant t+r\}.

Denote

\mathcal{A}_{n,t,r}=\max_{F-\text{forest of size }t+2r}|\mathcal{U}_{n,t,r,F}|.

(We remark that here, as an exception, we use a calligraphic letter to denote a quantity rather than a family.) Note that

\mathcal{A}_{n,t,0}=c_{n,t}n^{n-2-t}.

One of the main goals of this section is to show that with fixed
n,t
the value of
\mathcal{A}_{n,t,0}
is the largest among
\{\mathcal{A}_{n,t,r}\}_{r=0}^{n-1-t}
. This result will be proved in Lemma
10
and Lemma
11
.

We start with comparing
c_{n,t+r}
and
c_{n,t}
, that is we prove the following lemma, which we then use as a base
r=1
case of induction on
r
.

Lemma 8
.

Let
n,t
be nonnegative integers such that
t\leqslant n-2
. Then

(i)

c_{n,t+1}/c_{n,t}\leqslant 2
;

(ii)

if
t\geqslant 2n/3
then
c_{n,t+1}/c_{n,t}\leqslant e/3
.

(iii)

if
n/2\leqslant t<2n/3
then
c_{n,t+1}/c_{n,t}\leqslant 9/8
.

(iv)

c_{n,t+1}/c_{n,t}\geqslant 1/n

Proof.

Recall that
c_{n,t}
can be thought of as of the product of
n-t
integer factors of almost equal values with sum
n
, cf. (
2
). Consider a sequence of such factors
q_{1}\leqslant q_{2}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant q_{n-t}
. For
i=1,\ldots,n-t-1
put
p_{i}=q_{i+1}
. Then for
j=1,\ldots,q_{1}
increase
p_{j}
by one where the numeration of
p_{i}
’s is considered to be cyclic,
p_{n-t}=p_{1}
. It means that if
q_{1}>n-t-1
, then there will be some
p_{i}
’s that we increase by
1
more then once.

It is easy to verify that the
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n-t-1}
obtained during this procedure are
n-t-1
integers that differ by at most
1
and that have sum
n
. Thus
c_{n,t+1}=p_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot p_{n-t-1}
. Put
k=\lfloor\frac{n}{n-t}\rfloor=q_{1}
. Before this process

(3)

p_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot p_{n-t-1}/q_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot q_{n-t}=1/q_{1}=1/k.

Each change
p_{i}:=p_{i}+1
increases the numerator on the LHS of the equation (
3
) by the factor of at most
1+1/k
. There are exactly
k
such steps, therefore

\frac{c_{n,t+1}}{c_{n,t}}=\frac{p_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot p_{n-t-1}}{q_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot q_{n-t}}\leqslant\frac{\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{k}}{k}=f(k).

Estimate (i) in the statement of the lemma can be verified directly for
k=1,2
., i.e. we should check that
f(1)\leqslant 2
,
f(2)\leqslant 2
. For
k\geqslant 3
we have
f(k)<e/3
since

\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{k}<e<3\leqslant k,

thus
f(k)\leqslant e/3
. To see (ii) note that if
t\geqslant 2n/3
then
n-t<n/3
and
k=\lfloor\frac{n}{n-t}\rfloor\geqslant 3
. Lastly
n/2\leqslant t<2n/3
implies
k=2
and
f(2)=9/8
. Note that since
f(1)=2
and
f(2)=9/8
presented bounds for
t\leqslant 2n/3
are best possible.

Lastly, since at start of the procedure
p_{i}=q_{i+1}
and during the procedure
p_{i}
’s can only increase, we get

\frac{c_{n,t+1}}{c_{n,t}}=\frac{p_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot p_{n-t-1}}{q_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot q_{n-t}}\geqslant\frac{1}{q_{1}}\geqslant\frac{1}{n},

which implies (iv) and completes the proof.
∎

Having the base case
r=1
, we proceed by induction and prove the following statement.

Lemma 9
.

Let
n,t,r
be nonnegative integers such that
t+r\leqslant n-2
. Then

(i)

c_{n,t+r}/c_{n,t}\leqslant 2^{r}
;

(ii)

if
t\geqslant 2n/3
then
c_{n,t+r}/c_{n,t}\leqslant(e/3)^{r}
.

(iii)

if
n/2\leqslant t<2n/3
then
c_{n,t+r}/c_{n,t}\leqslant(9/8)^{r}
.

(iv)

c_{n,t+r}/c_{n,t}\geqslant 1/n^{r}

Proof.

Case
r=0
is obvious, case
r=1
was treated in Lemma
8
. Now we proceed by induction on
r
. Assume the statement is true for
r=1,\ldots,s
. Consider
r=s+1
.

To prove (i) we use (i) of Lemma
8
:

\frac{c_{n,t+s+1}}{c_{n,t}}=\frac{c_{n,t+s+1}}{c_{n,t+s}}\cdot\frac{c_{n,t+s}}{c_{n,t}}\leqslant 2\cdot 2^{s}=2^{s+1}.

The proof of (ii) and (iii) is similar. If
t\geqslant 2n/3
, then
t+s+1\geqslant 2n/3
and

\frac{c_{n,t+s+1}}{c_{n,t}}=\frac{c_{n,t+s+1}}{c_{n,t+s}}\cdot\frac{c_{n,t+s}}{c_{n,t}}\leqslant(e/3)\cdot(e/3)^{s}=(e/3)^{s+1}.

If
n/2\leqslant t<2n/3
then
t+s\geqslant n/2
. Due to (ii) and (iii) of Lemma
8
we have
c_{n,t+s+1}/c_{n,t+s}\leqslant 9/8
. Thus
c_{n,t+s+1}/c_{n,t}\leqslant(9/8)^{s+1}
.

Lastly, using the same argument and (iv) of Lemma
8
, we get (iv) of the statement.
∎

Using this lemma, we can compare
\mathcal{A}_{n,t,r}
and
\mathcal{A}_{n,t,0}
.

Lemma 10
.

There is
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and an absolute constant
\alpha<1
such that for any positive integers
n,t,r
such that
t\leqslant n-2
,
t+r\leqslant n-1
and
n\geqslant n_{0}
we have
\mathcal{A}_{n,t,r}\leqslant\alpha\mathcal{A}_{n,t,0}
whenever we have either
r\geqslant 2
, or
r=1
and
t\geqslant n/2
.

Proof.

For any
n,t,r
and forest
F
with
n
vertices and
t+2r
edges the size of
\mathcal{U}_{n,t,r,F}
is not bigger than
{t+2r\choose t+r}\mathcal{A}_{n,t+r,0}
by a simple union bound. Taking the maximum over all such
F
we get

\mathcal{A}_{n,t,r}\leqslant{t+2r\choose t+r}\mathcal{A}_{n,t+r,0}={t+2r\choose r}c_{n,t+r}n^{n-2-t-r}\leqslant 2^{r}c_{n,t}{t+2r\choose r}n^{n-2-t-r},

where the last inequality is due to (i) of Lemma
9
.

There is a positive integer
r_{0}
such that for all integer
r\geqslant r_{0}
we have
4^{r}/r!\leqslant 4^{r_{0}}/r_{0}!<1
. Put
\alpha_{1}=4^{r_{0}}/r_{0}!
. From the inequality above and the fact that for all
r\geqslant r_{0}
we have
t+2r\leqslant 2n
, we get that the following holds:

(4)

\mathcal{A}_{n,t,r}\leqslant 2^{r}c_{n,t}\cdot\frac{(2n)^{r}}{r!}\cdot n^{n-2-t-r}\leqslant\frac{4^{r}}{r!}\cdot c_{n,t}n^{n-2-t}\leqslant\alpha_{1}\mathcal{A}_{n,t,0}.

Now consider
r<r_{0}
. Here we treat cases
t<n/2,n/2\leqslant t<2n/3
and
t\geqslant 2n/3
separately.

Case I:
t<n/2
.
This is the only case where we exclude
r=1
. Take
\varepsilon>0
such that
\alpha_{2}=(1+\varepsilon)^{2}/2<1
. Then
(1+\varepsilon)^{r}/r!<1
holds for all
r\geqslant 2
. There is such
n_{1}
that for all
n>n_{1}
it is true that
t+2r\leqslant n/2+2r_{0}<(1+\varepsilon)n/2.
We write a bound similar to the inequality (
4
).

\mathcal{A}_{n,t,r}\leqslant 2^{r}c_{n,t}\cdot\frac{((1+\varepsilon)n/2)^{r}}{r!}\cdot n^{n-2-t-r}\leqslant\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{r}}{r!}\cdot c_{n,t}n^{n-2-t}\leqslant\alpha_{2}\mathcal{A}_{n,t,0}.

Case II:
n/2\leqslant t<2n/3
.
In this case we choose
\varepsilon>0
so that
\alpha_{3}=(1+\varepsilon)\cdot 3/4<1
. There is
n_{2}
such that for any
n>n_{2}
holds
t+2r<(1+\varepsilon)2n/3
. We also use (iii) of Lemma
