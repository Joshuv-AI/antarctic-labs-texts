Reducing health and cancer disparities: an environmental scan of three promising health care access interventions in Alberta, Canada
Authors: Staci Hastings, Anna Pujadas Botey, Anna Santos Salas, Paula Robson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCancer incidence and mortality in Canada have improved greatly over recent decades, but these improvements have not been equally distributed across all populations. This environmental scan synthesizes evidence from the real-world implementation of three interventions identified to improve access to cancer care for underserved populations—patient navigation, eHealth, and education and counselling—targeting healthcare access disparities in Alberta. It aims to inform local improvements in cancer care access and healthcare access more broadly, while offering insights for other regions in Canada and internationally with similar health equity challenges.MethodsA comprehensive electronic database search was conducted to identify academic and grey literature from January 2013 to March 2024. Data on initiatives (context, setting, target population, modality, and lessons learned) were extracted, coded thematically, and, where relevant, mapped to the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research to support synthesis.ResultsTwenty-one initiatives involving patient navigation, 17 utilizing eHealth, and 7 inclusive of education and counselling were identified. Multimodal approaches were commonly used across interventions, enhancing flexibility and accessibility. Most initiatives were urban based, suggesting a gap in rural access. Virtual programming has recently expanded, potentially enhancing reach, while education and counselling initiatives remain limited.ConclusionsThree key lessons applicable to initiatives which aim to address disparities in cancer care were identified. These lessons emphasize the importance of simplifying implementation, securing leadership and stakeholders’ support, and fostering partnerships and collaboration for program success.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13583-y.

Background

Cancer incidence and mortality in Canada have improved greatly over recent decades [1–3], but these improvements have not been equally distributed across all populations [1, 3–5]. Despite Canada’s universal healthcare, inequities in access to cancer care remain, influenced by factors such as socioeconomic status, ethnicity, age, gender and sexual identity, location of residence, culture, and language [1, 4–9]. These inequities reflect not only individual characteristics, but also broader structural and institutional factors, including but not limited to systemic racism, the colonial legacy affecting Indigenous communities, and the centralization of cancer services in urban centres [10, 11]. These inequities in access contribute to disparities in cancer care outcomes [6–9], a trend that is not unique to Canada and is also observed in other countries with and without universal healthcare systems such as the United States [12], Denmark [13], Sweden [13], and Australia [14]. Similar sociodemographic and geographic factors driving inequities in access in Canada are at play internationally [12–14], underscoring the global relevance of this issue.

Alberta, one of Canada’s most demographically and culturally diverse provinces [15], offers an ideal setting to explore interventions aimed at improving access to healthcare and reducing disparities in health outcomes [16]. Alberta’s vast rural and remote regions, coupled with significant Indigenous and racialized populations impacted by colonial legacies [10, 15], mirror many of the healthcare challenges faced by other diverse regions in Canada and internationally. Addressing inequities in access to cancer care is crucial for improving health outcomes and achieving health equity. While various interventions have shown promise in reducing these access inequities and disparities in cancer care [17–20], evidence of their effectiveness in real-world settings remains limited.

This environmental scan builds on a previous systematic review [16] by members of our team that identified seven types of interventions to improve access to cancer care for underserved populations in high-income countries with universal health coverage. This study focuses on common interventions identified by the systematic review [16], including patient navigation, eHealth, and education and counselling, chosen for their relevance to cancer care and their distinct focus. The others were excluded due to limited applicability within the Alberta healthcare context (e.g., financial support) or being subsumed within broader categories (e.g., service redesign). The study examines how these three interventions have been implemented in Alberta, both within cancer care and across the broader healthcare context, to identify lessons for improving equity in access to cancer care in the province and other regions with similar health equity challenges.

Methods

Environmental scanning involves gathering and interpreting information from both internal and external environments to inform strategic planning and directions for future organizational action [21, 22]. Thus, to inform future discussions and recommendations about interventions to address inequities in cancer care access in Alberta, environmental scanning methods were selected.

The references were exported and managed using Zotero 6.0 reference management software. After removing duplicates, the searches yielded 471 results. Titles and abstracts were reviewed by one of the co-authors for relevance and inclusion (see Fig. 1). Articles were retained if they described initiatives involving patient navigation, eHealth, or education and counselling; were published between January 2013 and March 2024; had been implemented in Alberta; served adult patients with any health condition; and addressed access to health care. For initiatives with limited descriptive information, a general Google search using the title of the initiative was also conducted. Data were extracted from eligible articles using a pre-designed Excel spreadsheet, including source (author, year of publications, title of source, and doi or URL), brief description of the initiative, context and setting, target population, modality, objectives and intended outcomes, findings, initiative enablers and barriers, and discussions regarding the intervention of focus (see Additional file 1 for data extraction tool). Evidence was synthesized narratively, organized by intervention type. Initiative enablers and barriers were first synthesized using an inductive thematic approach to capture emergent factors reported in the literature. These inductively derived themes were then mapped to constructs of the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [28], an implementation science framework with five domains and 39 constructs that identifies factors supporting or hindering the implementation of interventions. Mapping to CFIR guided the organization and interpretation of findings, enhancing transferability and comparability with other implementation studies. Coding and mapping were conducted by a single coder (SH), with regular consultation with APB and other co-authors as needed to ensure consistency and resolve uncertainties.Fig. 1Environmental scan inclusion flow diagram

Results

Forty initiatives in Alberta were identified from the literature across the three intervention categories. Five included elements of multiple interventions (for a list and description of the initiatives identified, see Additional file 2). Twenty-one initiatives utilized patient navigation, 17 used eHealth technology, and 7 employed education and counselling. Characteristics and lessons learned from those identified are presented, separated by intervention category. When possible, enablers and barriers were synthesized and reported according to CFIR, which organizes implementation factors across intervention characteristics, inner and outer setting, individual characteristics, and the implementation process. This framework allows us to move beyond purely descriptive reporting toward a theory-informed synthesis of factors influencing implementation

Patient navigation interventions

Initiative characteristics

Context and setting

Approximately half (11 of 21) of the initiatives were delivered in urban settings [29–41], eight were offered province-wide [42–52], one was in a rural setting [53]. The context of one initiative was unclear [54]. Seven initiatives were based in hospitals [29–32, 39, 40, 42], five in community health centres [35, 39, 40, 42, 53], five in primary care networks [33, 36–38, 41, 54, 55], two in cancer centres [45, 52, 56], and four were delivered virtually [34, 43, 44, 46, 47, 57].

Target population

Target populations ranged from general to very specific groups. The most common target group was patients suspected of or diagnosed with cancer [29, 31, 32, 45, 52, 56]. Other target populations included all Albertans [43, 46, 46, 54, 57], patients with chronic diseases [37, 38, 48–51], and Indigenous patients and their families [39, 41]. Some initiatives targeted niche populations [30, 33–36, 40, 44, 47, 48, 53, 58] (e.g., adults from Siksika Nation (Indigenous Community) with inflammatory arthritis or osteoarthritis and at least one other chronic condition) [53].

Modality

Patient navigation initiatives were delivered through various means. Four were telephone-based [31, 34, 44, 45], two were in-person only [29, 42], and nine used a combination of in-person and telephone communication [30, 32, 33, 35, 36, 39, 47–52, 54]. One in-person program offered mobile outreach services [40]. Five initiatives [37, 38, 40, 42, 46, 57] used virtual technology (e.g., text messaging [42], webpage [57]), with three combining virtual and in-person delivery [37, 38, 41, 42].

eHealth interventions

Initiative characteristics

Context and setting

Ten initiatives were available to patients across Alberta [46, 48, 63–75]: four in rural and remote areas [76–82] (e.g., Northern Alberta [79–81], rural area of Calgary Zone [77]), and two in urban contexts [83, 84]. Over half (59%) of the initiatives provided electronic health care services to patients at home [46, 47, 67, 73–76, 82, 83, 83–85]. Three connected patients to healthcare providers through community telehealth sites [66, 78–81], and four facilitated connections between community healthcare providers and specialists [64, 68–70, 72, 77].

Target population

eHealth initiatives targeted a range of population groups, including pregnant women [63], older adults [77], patients with glaucoma [79–81], people who use drugs [66, 71, 75], healthcare workers and their families [46, 47], Indigenous patients [85], patients with cancer [74, 76, 78, 82], individuals who are overweight or obese [83], spinal surgery patients [84], dermatological patients [64], patients living with chronic disease [68–70, 74, 76, 78, 82], neuroscience and psychiatry patients [67], and those needing rehabilitation care [73].

Modality

A variety of electronic mechanisms were used to deliver eHealth interventions, including mobile applications [46, 63, 75], videoconferencing [66, 78, 85], telephone-only services [65, 72], a combination of telephone and videoconferencing [67, 73, 74], in-person exams with videoconferencing [76, 77, 82], and personalized virtual consultations combined with web-based instructions [83]. Some initiatives used electronic technology to facilitate collaborative care between healthcare providers [64, 68–70, 79–81]. Electronic platforms used included Zoom, Skype, ConsultDerm, Physitrack, DORS App, Jane App, and MIRA chatbot.

Education and counselling interventions

Initiative characteristics

Context and setting

Six initiatives were provided in an urban context [31, 32, 41, 58, 83, 87–91] and one was offered province-wide [73]. Initiative settings included hospitals [31, 32, 87–89, 91], community-based health centers [90], public clinics [83], emergency departments, and PCN-affiliated family clinics [41, 58]. One Initiative offered services in any setting [75].

Target population

The targeted populations for these initiatives varied widely, including patients living with cancer [31, 32, 87–89, 91], women experiencing vulnerabilities [90], individuals who were overweight or obese and living with lumbar spinal stenosis [83], individuals using opioids and other substances [75], adults who self-identified as low income and regular users of drugs and/or alcohol [41, 58], and PCN and emergency department physicians [41, 58].

Modality

Education was delivered through various modalities, including virtually [83, 87–89, 91], through in-person group sessions [90], in-person or over telephone [31, 32], through a phone application [75], and using a combination of in-person workshops and virtual content [41, 58].

Lessons learned

Enablers

Discussion

This environmental scan identified 40 initiatives implemented in Alberta that utilized patient navigation, eHealth, and education and counselling interventions, aimed at reducing disparities in health care access. Together, these interventions targeted diverse populations and employed a variety of delivery modalities, including telephone-based, in-person, and virtual methods, which increased flexibility in accessing services. The findings suggest that using multiple modalities enhances access by accommodating diverse patient preferences. Existing literature supports this, highlighting how multimodal approaches can empower diverse patient groups to engage with services in ways that best suit their needs and circumstances [16, 81, 85, 87, 90, 92]. Despite challenges in engagement, such as technological barriers and recruitment issues, which remain significant obstacles for underserved populations, the results underline the value of multimodality in providing more tailored and accessible services, particularly for diverse, rural, and typically underserved populations.

Findings also reveal the potential benefits of virtual care in addressing access disparities. The initiatives identified in this scan were primarily concentrated in urban areas, particularly the largest metropolitan cities in Alberta, Edmonton and Calgary, which host 69% of Alberta’s population [93]. This urban focus was similarly noted in a 2021 environmental scan of patient navigation initiatives in Alberta by Tang et al. [11], further highlighting the implications for cancer care where geographic inequities exacerbate disparities in health outcomes [13]. To reduce these disparities, equitable access to care is required for both rural and urban residents and virtual programming offers a potential solution. Virtual care has expanded rapidly since the advent of COVID-19 [63–66] and shows promise in bridging geographic gaps in access to health care services. Virtual care can help patients in rural and remote areas overcome transportation challenges [66, 77], employment obligations [63, 65], childcare responsibilities [66], and long appointment wait times [46, 69]. As discussed by many, virtual care can improve access not only for rural and remote patients [64, 71, 76–78, 94, 95], but also other traditionally underserved populations [46, 70, 96], including Indigenous Communities [85], individuals with substance use disorders [66], and older adults [77]. While the expansion of virtual care is promising, its long-term effectiveness remains inadequately evaluated. Our study identified key enablers, such as efficiency, and ease of use, but also challenges like unreliable internet connectivity and privacy concerns, highlighting the need for further research to assess the sustainability and long-term impact of these initiatives.

The literature identified in this scan emphasizes the growing uptake of patient navigation strategies across Alberta [11], initially developed in cancer care but now expanding beyond this context [11]. Patient navigation is widely recognized as an effective mechanism for improving access to healthcare services, particularly for underserved populations [53, 61]. Our findings support this, with initiatives like the Comprehensive Breast Care Program in Edmonton using nurse navigators to expedite diagnostic testing and specialist appointments, significantly reducing the time to diagnosis for individuals suspected of having breast cancer [62]. However, our results also revealed variations in the design and delivery of patient navigation initiatives, as well differences in contexts, settings, and populations served, complicating efforts to replicate and scale successful programs [55, 56]. Some critics argue that well-functioning healthcare systems should not require navigators, suggesting that systems themselves should be structured to ensure universal access to care [97, 98]. Our findings suggest that navigation remains an important strategy to mitigate access barriers in the health system; however, its ongoing necessity underscores the need for broader structural reforms and policy changes to ensure cancer care access is universal as well as equitable. Furthermore, patient navigation remains essential for populations who continue to face systemic barriers, as seen in initiatives targeting groups like Indigenous populations and those with chronic conditions. Our findings align with this view, emphasizing that optimizing the healthcare system should proceed alongside strengthening navigation services to ensure that patients are equipped to navigate the system as broader systemic improvements take place.

In comparison to eHealth and patient navigation interventions, this scan revealed limited evidence on education and counselling initiatives in the context of improving healthcare access. This gap may reflect a lack of evaluation and reporting of such programs, rather than an absence of them. For instance, psychosocial support programs in cancer care, where education and counselling are integral to patient management, are well-established in the province [99], but few studies examine their impact on healthcare access. Most research focuses on addressing issues encountered during care rather than access barriers [16]. The relative scarcity of research on education and counselling interventions suggests a need for more robust evaluation and reporting in this area across healthcare, including cancer care, where these services could play a pivotal role in addressing access inequities.

Taken together, these findings highlight not only the promise of patient navigation, eHealth, and education and counselling interventions in expanding access, but also the importance of ensuring that such initiatives are designed to be culturally safe and accessible to populations experiencing structural marginalization. Our synthesis shows that enablers were most often rooted in organizational capacity and implementation processes, while barriers reflected system-level pressures and intervention-specific challenges. Navigation depended on strong local leadership, eHealth on effective technology design and integration, and education/counselling on alignment with policy priorities. These findings highlight that successful implementation requires both robust internal supports and responsiveness to external contexts.

Several additional gaps warranting further investigation were identified through this scan. First, challenges such as recruitment difficulties, technological limitations, and inconsistent leadership support suggest the need for studies examining how these factors influence the effectiveness of initiatives. Second, racialized and ethnically diverse populations, newcomers, and other marginalized groups were underrepresented in this scan despite being disproportionately impacted by healthcare access disparities. These findings reflect a limitation of the current literature, or gaps within Alberta’s health care system, and highlight the importance of ensuring initiatives designed to improve healthcare access are reaching the populations most affected by inequities. While barriers to engaging underserved populations are well-documented in the literature [100, 101], there remains limited understanding of their impact on the long-term outcomes and sustainability of these initiatives. Furthermore, more research is needed on co-development approaches in education and counselling interventions, particularly to ensure the early and meaningful involvement of stakeholders. These methodologies, emphasizing shared decision-making and program co-creation, are crucial in addressing complex patient needs in cancer care and other settings [102, 103]. Involving patients, providers, and policymakers from the onset can enhance relevance, acceptability, and long-term success of these interventions [104, 105].

Limitations

While this environmental scan has provided valuable insights, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, this study did not evaluate the implementation of the initiatives identified. As such, the enablers and barriers identified are limited to what was reported in the literature. The variability in the level of detail reported in the literature also restricted our ability to conduct an in-depth analysis of the enablers and barriers associated with each initiative, constraining our discussion of the full scope of factors influencing successful implementation. Future studies should assess how initiatives are designed, adapted, and sustained over time, as these factors are crucial for ensuring their long-term impact and scalability. Additionally, our reliance solely on published literature meant that we may have missed insights from frontline healthcare personnel and other stakeholders directly involved in designing and implementing these interventions, as well as Indigenous-led, community-based or informal initiatives that have not been published on. Therefore, the results may not reflect the most current practices or the full breadth of patient navigation, eHealth, and education and counseling initiatives implemented across the province. Furthermore, while interventions applied in different healthcare domains may show promise, their applicability to other contexts including cancer care requires careful consideration. The unique characteristics of cancer care, such as the complexity of treatment pathways, specific patient needs, and coordination between care teams, may present challenges when adapting what has been successful in other healthcare domains. Finally, this scan did not include stakeholder validation or patient co-interpretation of findings, which could have provided additional perspectives on how these initiatives address access and equity. Despite these limitations, Alberta’s healthcare system has demonstrated a strong commitment to addressing healthcare access inequities, as evidenced by the wide variety of initiatives identified. This environmental scan provides valuable insights to inform efforts to improve care delivery, both locally and beyond.

Conclusions

This environmental scan offers three key lessons for improving access to care in both cancer care and the broader health system:

Simplify implementation

Simplify implementation

Effective implementation of patient navigation, eHealth, and education and counselling interventions relied on streamlined processes. Given healthcare system constraints, such as limited capacity and competing priorities, initiatives that were easy to adopt, adaptable, and leveraged existing resources were more likely to succeed. For example, standardizing processes, offering accessible training, and integrating interventions into existing workflows helped reduce the burden on healthcare providers and improved uptake across settings.

2.Secure leadership, stakeholder and community support

Secure leadership, stakeholder and community support

Support from leadership, healthcare providers and community stakeholders was essential for long-term success of initiatives. Actively engaging patients and communities made initiatives more relevant, better accepted, and more likely to achieve positive outcomes. Conversely, limited stakeholder engagement often hindered success, emphasizing the importance of comprehensive buy-in from all involved parties.

Fostering partnerships and collaboration

Collaboration among healthcare providers, patients and community stakeholders was a key enabler of successful interventions. Co-designed initiatives, which strengthened relationships between stakeholders and facilitated interprofessional cooperation, resulted in more integrated and effective care. This collaboration also helped create synergies between services, ensuring that initiatives are better equipped to meet patients’ diverse needs.
