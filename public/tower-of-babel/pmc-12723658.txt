Information about immigrants' deservingness reduces misperceptions and opposition to immigration
Authors: Amine Sijilmassi, Hugo Mercier, Lou Safra, Coralie Chevallier, Brian Schaffner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnti-immigration attitudes remain widespread across Western societies, raising concerns for social cohesion. This study investigates whether correcting misperceptions about immigrants' perceived deservingness—using cues such as intent to contribute and efforts to overcome socioeconomic challenges—can counter anti-immigration prejudice. In study 1 (n = 480), a factorial survey experiment showed that low-status immigrants were viewed more favorably when they exhibited deservingness traits. Studies 2a (n = 1,506) and 2b (n = 1,255), conducted as 1-week longitudinal studies during the 2024 European and French parliamentary elections, revealed that an information treatment emphasizing deservingness cues strongly reduced misperceptions about immigrants, modestly reduced opposition to immigration, and had a smaller effect for anti-immigration voting. These findings suggest that while immigrants are often perceived negatively, emphasizing perceived deservingness can mitigate prejudice, presenting a promising strategy for reducing anti-immigration bias.Keywords: immigration, deservingness, misperceptions, voting, policy

Anti-immigration attitudes are widespread across Western democracies, driving support for far-right parties and challenging social cohesion. This research demonstrates that correcting misperceptions about immigrants' efforts and intentions—key cues of deservingness—can reduce prejudice. Across three studies, we found that emphasizing deservingness traits substantially reduced misperceptions and led to modest but consistent reductions in negative attitudes toward low-status immigrants, including in the politically salient, real-world setting, of the 2024 French and European parliamentary elections. While effects on political behavior are negligible and the findings should be interpreted in light of sample constraints, this work highlights the potential of interventions that target perceived cooperative intent. This approach offers practical insights for policymakers and advocates seeking to foster social cohesion and mitigate hostility toward immigrant populations.

Introduction

Hostility toward immigration is widespread across Western societies. In a survey conducted in 2015, 52% of Europeans expressed that immigration in their country should decrease, while only 8% believed it should increase (1). These negative attitudes are reflected in the political sphere, with the steady rise of anti-immigration parties (2). Anti-immigration attitudes pose various policy challenges, as they can fuel discrimination, weaken social cohesion, and diminish support for immigration policies that are also beneficial to natives (3–7).

Anti-immigration attitudes are often associated with misperceptions about immigrants. For instance, people tend to overestimate the number of immigrants and the unemployment rate among immigrant populations (8, 9). This has prompted researchers to explore ways to correct such misperceptions, typically through survey experiments where participants are provided with accurate information about immigrants. Most of these interventions emphasize the positive socioeconomic contribution of immigration or aim to alleviate concerns about its potential cost to native citizens. For instance, previous studies have corrected misperceptions regarding immigrants' education levels (10), crime rates (9), and their perceived negative impact on natives' employment and wages (7, 11). The goal of these interventions is to portray immigrants as having a net positive economic impact—or at least as being less costly than participants had assumed.

Yet, emerging evidence from psychology and political science suggests that social judgments are driven not solely by people's absolute levels of contribution, as targeted in the experiments above, but also by their perceived deservingness (12–16). People are willing to support individuals who need help, provided that they are perceived as deserving. One of the heuristics people use to make this judgment is reciprocity: individuals who are seen as actively striving to become productive members of society are perceived as good cooperators and are thus categorized as deserving of help (12, 15, 16). Perceived deservingness may thus favor prosocial tendencies toward economically vulnerable individuals. Accordingly, communication strategies centered around deservingness are effective in reducing negative attitudes toward economically deprived people. For instance, presenting deprived or unemployed individuals as willing but unlucky increases support for redistribution policies (16). Similarly, people are less likely to support healthcare policies when they are targeted at diseases that are perceived as avoidable (17, 18). The deservingness heuristic is also likely to play an important role in perceptions of immigrants. Several vignette experiments suggest that immigrants are rated as more deserving of welfare benefits in the host society when they are perceived as active and hardworking (19–21). However, it remains unclear whether these findings translate in real-life contexts and, most importantly, if they can be leveraged to reduce negative attitudes toward immigration.

Indeed, the deservingness heuristic suggests that effective communication about immigration could not only correct misperceptions about the socioeconomic consequences of immigration but also provide insights into immigrants' psychological dispositions, such as their motivations and efforts. This approach has promising policy implications, as emphasizing deservingness might frame immigrants with a low socioeconomic status as “poor but deserving,” potentially enhancing the social acceptability of immigrants beyond those with the most favorable characteristics—such as high education, wealth, or proficiency in the native language (22). This is especially relevant given that immigrants, on average, tend to occupy lower socioeconomic positions compared with natives in many societies (5). In addition, previous studies have shown that newcomers to a group are often distrusted because they are seen as benefiting without having contributed (23, 24). As a result, people tend to be less trusting of recently arrived immigrants (25). Highlighting the cooperative motivations of immigrants, therefore, has the potential to enhance positive perceptions, even of those who have only recently arrived.

In this article, we propose that the deservingness heuristic can be leveraged to reduce misperceptions and negative attitudes toward immigrants and their families in realistic, politically salient contexts. In line with this hypothesis, we present the results of three preregistered studies conducted in France to test the impact of deservingness cues to reduce anti-immigration attitudes. France is a compelling setting for our research because negative attitudes toward immigration are widespread in the French population. In a longitudinal attitude poll conducted on a representative sample of French participants between 2012 and 2024, the percentage of French people who agreed with the statement “There are too many immigrants in France” was consistently above 60% (26). These attitudes seem to influence electoral outcomes, with the consistent rise of far-right political parties advocating anti-immigration policies. For instance, in the 2022 presidential election, one-third of the voters voted for one of the two main far-right candidates (Marine Le Pen and Eric Zemmour). Immigration was a particularly salient issue in the first half of 2024, during which we conducted our studies, with France holding two major elections—the European election on June 9, and the anticipated parliamentary elections on June 30 (first ballot) and July 7 (second ballot). In both elections, electoral polls showed immigration as the second most important issue for voters, behind purchasing power (27, 28). In line with this, both elections saw the unprecedented rise of the far-right political party “Rassemblement National,” which increased its share of votes in the European elections from 23 to 31% since 2019, and its representation in the French National Assembly from 15 to 25% of the deputy seats since the 2022 parliamentary elections. Overall, the exceptional salience of immigration in the French political context made our studies particularly timely, as participants' beliefs and attitudes were shaped by real-world concerns. Additionally, the electoral context allowed us to move beyond standard self-reported attitudes and measure reported voting behaviors.

In study 1 (n = 480 French participants; preregistered), we tested whether deservingness cues increased the social acceptability of low-status immigrants in a factorial survey experiment. Each participant rated three vignettes of a fictional male immigrant who arrived in France with no job, no degree, and poor French language skills. The profile varied randomly across four dimensions: reasons for migration (finding a job vs. access to welfare), improvement in French (rapid progress or no progress), employment efforts (rapidly found a job or took years to find one), and children's social mobility (children attained a white-collar profession or became low-skill workers like their father; see SI Appendix, Section 1.2). We selected these four dimensions to elicit deservingness intuitions, with an emphasis on reciprocity cues—i.e. cues that highlight immigrants' motivation and efforts to be good cooperators. The reason for migration (seeking employment vs. accessing welfare) communicates whether immigrants are driven by prosocial intentions rather than opportunistic motives. The speed at which immigrants learn French or enter the labor market indicates their willingness to contribute and adapt to the host society. Finally, information about the upward mobility of immigrants' children signals that both parents and their children make substantial efforts to ensure their socioeconomic integration over the long run.

The immigrant's name and country of origin (Moroccan or Italian) also varied—as previous work has shown that non-European Union (EU) immigrants are rated as less deserving than immigrants from the EU (29). Participants then rated their support for granting a permanent residency permit on a 7-point Likert scale. We hypothesized greater support for immigrants seeking jobs (H1A), learning French quickly (H1B), finding employment rapidly (H1C), and whose children experienced upward mobility (H1D).

We also examined whether deservingness cues had a uniform effect on the judgment of immigrant profiles across political ideologies. In line with previous findings, we expected the psychological mechanisms leading to deservingness judgements to be universal, with the deservingness heuristic playing an important role in policy support across ideologies (12, 13, 16). To measure participants' political ideology, we first asked for their self-reported political ideology on a continuous Left–Right axis, and divided them into a “Left-wing” and “Right-wing” category based on a median split. We also asked participants to state which political party they felt closer to. They were then classified as either Left wing, Centrists, or Right wing. We predicted that hypotheses H1A–H1D would be verified in each of these subsamples (H2).

In study 2a (preregistered), we extended our investigation in two ways: first, by examining the impact of deservingness cues on broader attitudes toward immigration, rather than on specific individuals. This shift is not trivial, as previous research has shown that people tend to evaluate individual immigrants more positively than immigration in general, suggesting that interventions that are effective at reducing prejudice toward a specific person may not generalize to broader attitudes (30). Second, by testing this effect in a realistic context, using real information to influence real-stake attitudes and behaviors toward actual immigration. Exploiting the exceptional electoral context of June 2024 in France, we tested whether providing French participants with accurate information about the deservingness of immigrants in France reduced misperceptions, opposition to immigration, and voting intentions for political parties with anti-immigration platforms.

We conducted a preregistered longitudinal study with two waves (pre-election wave: n = 1,506; postelection wave: n = 1,271), spaced an average of 1 week, before and after the 2024 European election that took place on June 9 (Fig. S30). The pre-election wave ran for 5 days, from Monday, June 3 to Friday, June 7, allowing sufficient time to collect and process responses before election day. The postelection wave was fielded for 5 days starting the day after the election, from Monday, June 10 to Friday, June 14. Participants could begin either wave at any point during its respective time window. In the pre-election wave, participants completed a questionnaire assessing their agreement with four misperceptions about immigration in France: that immigrants choose to immigrate based to benefit from welfare aids, that their mastery of French does not improve over time, that they do not actively seek employment, and that their children achieve lower educational and professional outcomes compared with the children of natives. These misperceptions correspond to the deservingness cues manipulated in study 1. Participants also completed a similar questionnaire on cultural activities in France, either focusing on cinema attendance or participation in sports (with the theme randomly assigned). This questionnaire included items such as the percentage of people in France who attend the cinema at least once a year or which forms of exercise are most common in France, and was included to serve as the basis of the control group's correction task. Finally, participants were asked to report their opposition to immigration and their voting intention for the European election.

They were then randomly allocated into one of two short correction tasks. In the treatment group, participants read short texts with accurate information addressing each of the four measured misperceptions about immigration in France. Participants in the control group received a similar correction task addressing their answers to the questionnaire about cultural activities in France. In both tasks, the corrections were delivered through a structured feedback mechanism, where participants were shown their initial responses alongside the correct response and a short explanation, and relied on data from official state institutions (e.g. the French National Institute of Statistics).

After the correction task, participants completed the same questionnaires a second time. Finally, they were asked the percentage of their payment that they were willing to donate to a pro-immigration charity. The postelection wave was launched the day after the election, with participants answering the same questionnaires as in the pre-election wave, but this time reporting their actual voting behavior.

Following the European election, French President Emmanuel Macron called snap parliamentary elections, which provided us with the unique opportunity to replicate study 2a in the context of a national election a mere 3 weeks after the European election. As for the European election, opinion polls showed that immigration was a salient policy issue among French voters (28). We conducted study 2b (preregistered) during the time surrounding the first round of the parliamentary election which took place on June 30. The pre-election wave (n = 1,255) ran from Monday, June 24 to Friday, June 28, and the postelection wave (n = 904) from Monday, July 1 to Friday, July 5. As in study 2a, participants could enter either wave at any point during its respective fieldwork period. We used the exact same design as in study 2a—except that we removed the charity donation measure and adapted the voting questionnaire to the new electoral context.

We predicted that the treatment would decrease the likelihood of endorsing several beliefs about immigrants: that they come to France primarily for welfare benefits (H3); that they do not improve their French over time (H4); that they do not actively seek employment (H5); and that their children fail to achieve the same educational and professional outcomes as native-born children (H6). Similarly, we expected the treatment to lower opposition to immigration policy (H7), reduce the probability of voting for a far-right party (H8) or one with a stronger anti-immigration stance (H9), and—in study 2a only—encourage a higher percentage of donations to charity (H10). We also anticipated that these effects would persist in the postelection wave (H3-Wave2 to H10-Wave2). To test these hypotheses, we computed the meta-analytic effect of the treatment in each wave across both studies, following best-practice recommendations (31–33). Meta-analyses efficiently summarize results across studies, allowing for broader generalization. In most cases, the meta-analytic results aligned with those from studies 2a and 2b. In line with our preregistered analysis plan, we also provide detailed results for each study (SI Appendix, Section 2.2.2).

Results

Study 1: deservingness cues increase the social acceptability of low-status fictional immigrant profiles

In line with our hypotheses, immigrant profiles received greater support for permanent residency when they came for job opportunities instead of welfare (β = 0.27; SE = 0.05; t(1,078) = 5.08; 95% CI = 0.17–0.38, P < 0.001; H1A supported), when they learned French quickly (β = 0.36; SE = 0.05; t(1,091) = 6.72; 95% CI = 0.26–0.47, P < 0.001; H1B supported), and when their children experienced intergenerational mobility (β = 0.44; SE = 0.05; t(1,068.01) = 8.38; 95% CI = 0.34–0.54, P < 0.001; H1D supported)—after controlling for the other manipulated variables, age, gender, education level and subjective socioeconomic status (Fig. 1 and Table S2). In contrast, the effect of finding a job quickly on perceived social acceptability was more limited and only marginally significant (β = 0.10; SE = 0.05; t(1,071.92) = 1.83; 95% CI = −0.01, 0.20, P = 0.07; H1C nonsupported). When restricting the analysis to the subsample including only the first-rated vignette for each participant, we partially replicated our results, with the effect of Intention to work remaining positive but only marginally significant, and the effect of Progress in employment no longer significant (Table S5).

These results provide evidence that highlighting the intentions and efforts of low-status immigrants and their children substantially improves their social acceptability in a fictional context. Deservingness cues can thus compensate for an immigrant's lower socioeconomic situation in the eyes of native participants. However, immigrants with non-EU (Moroccan) origins were still rated significantly lower on social acceptability after controlling for the four deservingness cues and other control variables (β = −0.35; SE = 0.06; t(17.80) = −5.92; 95% CI = −0.47, −0.24, P < 0.001)—replicating similar findings from previous studies (29, 34). Thus, our four deservingness cues did not eradicate the effect of discrimination toward culturally more distant foreigners.

Similar results were observed across political ideologies and party affiliations. In most subsamples, the effects of intention to work, progress in learning French, and children's upward mobility were in the expected direction, although they failed to reach significance in some cases, in particular for political groups with the lowest sample sizes (see Tables S3 and S4 for detailed analyses—H2 partially supported). Overall, our findings suggest that deservingness cues consistently shape social judgments of immigrants across political ideologies.

Studies 2a and 2b: providing accurate information about the deservingness of immigrants in France strongly decreases misperceptions about immigrants, modestly reduces opposition to immigration, but does not impact voting for anti-immigration political parties

Sample

In study 2a, we recruited 1,506 participants, of whom 1,271 returned for the postelection wave and could be matched with their pre-election responses, resulting in an attrition rate of 15.6%. In study 2b, we recruited 1,255 participants, with 885 returning for the postelection wave and successfully matched to their pre-election data, resulting in an attrition rate of 29.4%. Our participants closely matched the French population in terms of gender and region of residence. Additionally, participants' baseline voting intentions in both studies were representative of the French electorate at the time they were conducted—as measured by electoral polls. However, the samples were skewed by an over-representation of younger, more educated individuals—although the sample of study 2b was more balanced on these variables (see Tables S6–S8 for a detailed breakdown of the samples). Finally, the political and demographic composition of our sample remained consistent across the pre- and postelection waves in both studies, indicating no evidence of differential attrition (see Tables S9 and S10).

The baseline questionnaire confirmed that the four targeted misperceptions were widespread in our sample. In study 2a (2b), 62.2% (71.2%) of participants agreed that immigrants primarily choose to settle in France because of welfare, 33.7% (45.0%) that they do not actively learn French when arriving in France, 36.8% (51.6%) that they do not actively look for jobs, and 57.3% (51.0%) that their children do not attain the same educational and professional status as the children of natives. Moreover, we found that holding these negative beliefs was associated with more opposition to immigration and a higher intention to vote for a far-right party before the correction task in both studies—except for the belief that the children of immigrants do not attain the same educational and professional status as the children of natives, which was not associated with these outcomes (see SI Appendix, Section 2.2.1).

The randomization of participants was effective, resulting in balanced numbers of treated and control individuals (study 2a: NTreatment = 753, NControl = 753; study 2b: NTreatment = 625, NControl = 630). Baseline characteristics between treated and control groups were generally comparable across most demographic and political variables. Moreover, they were highly similar across the preelection and postelection waves in both studies, with no evidence of differential attrition between groups (Table S10). However, we found some baseline imbalances: in study 2a, treated individuals reported significantly less opposition to immigration (β = −0.15; SE = 0.06; t(1,504) = −2.52; 95% CI = −0.26, −0.03; P = 0.012). In study 2b, treated participants were significantly less educated (β = −0.23; SE = 0.11; t(1,253) = −2.17; 95% CI = −0.44, −0.02; P = 0.031), less likely to believe immigrants do not actively seek jobs (β = −0.23; SE = 0.11; z = −2.00; 95% CI = −0.45, −0.01; P = 0.046), and less likely to report an intention to vote for a far-right party (β = −0.28; SE = 0.13; z = −2.10; 95% CI = 0.54, −0.02; P = 0.036; see Tables S8 and S9). To ensure the robustness of our findings, we conducted additional analyses by incorporating double-Lasso-selected control variables into our preregistered models (35–37). In the following section, we present our preregistered analyses without control variables, as the results were largely consistent with those that included controls, except when specified otherwise. Detailed results of the analyses with double-Lasso-selected controls are available in the SI Appendix, Section 2.2.3.

To provide a more intuitive sense of the effect sizes in logistic regressions, we report the average marginal effect (AME) of the treatment on the relevant outcome. This represents the average change in the predicted probability of the outcome for individuals in the treatment group compared with those in the control group (Tables S11–S14). For the linear regressions involving a continuous outcome, we express the treatment effect as the percentage reduction on the outcome scale.

Main analyses

In line with our hypotheses, the treatment significantly reduced all four misperceptions about immigration across all waves of both studies (Fig. 2). The intervention reduced participants' belief that immigrants primarily choose to immigrate in France to access welfare aid (meta-analytic effect: β = −1.49; SE = 0.08; z = −18.14; 95% CI = −1.65, −1.33; P < 0.0001; H3 supported); that they do not improve their mastery of French (meta-analytic effect: β = −1.16; SE = 0.09; z = −12.75; 95% CI = −1.34, −0.98; P < 0.0001; H4 supported); that they do not actively seek employment (meta-analytic effect: β = −1.06; SE = 0.09; z = −12.20; 95% CI = −1.23, −0.89; P < 0.0001; H5 supported); and that their children do not attain the same educational and professional status as the children of natives (meta-analytic effect: β = −1.86; SE = 0.24; z = −7.71; 95% CI = −2.33, −1.38; P < 0.0001; H6 supported). These effects persisted one week (on average) after the intervention, with comparable effect sizes. In the postelection wave, across the two studies, treated individuals were still significantly less likely to endorse the four deservingness beliefs about immigrants (see SI Appendix; H3-Wave2 to H6-Wave2 supported). These effects also have important practical implications (Fig. S7). In the pre-election wave, the treatment reduced the predicted probability of endorsing welfare misperceptions by ∼32 percentage points (study 2a: AME = −0.32, 95% CI = −0.36, −0.29; study 2b: −0.32, 95% CI = −0.36, −0.28), and misperceptions about immigrants' children by 38 and 31 points, respectively. Misperceptions about immigrants' progress in language skills and employment dropped by 20 to 23 points across studies. We obtained similar effect sizes in the postelection wave (Tables S11–S14).

The treatment had more nuanced results on opposition to immigration, which was measured on a 5-point Likert scale (Fig. 2). Immediately after the correction task, the intervention significantly reduced opposition to immigration in study 2a (β = −0.22; SE = 0.06; t = −3.77; 95% CI = −0.33, −0.10; P < 0.0002), but not in study 2b (β = −0.03; SE = 0.06; t = −0.48; 95% CI = −0.14, 0.08; P = 0.631), resulting in a nonsignificant negative meta-analytic effect (meta-analytic effect: β = −0.12; SE = 0.10; z = −1.29; 95% CI = −0.31, 0.06; P = 0.196; H7 not supported). We found more robust evidence of a reduction in opposition toward immigration in treated participants one week after the intervention. In the postelection wave, treated individuals were significantly less opposed to immigration in study 2a (β = −0.28; SE = 0.06; t = −4.42; 95% CI = −0.40, −0.15; P < 0.0001) and showed a marginally significant reduction in opposition to immigration in study 2b (β = −0.13; SE = 0.07; t = −1.79; 95% CI = −0.26, 0.01; P = 0.073), resulting in a significantly negative meta-analytic effect (meta-analytic effect: β = −0.20; SE = 0.08; z = −2.71; 95% CI = −0.35, −0.06; P = 0.0067; H7-Wave2 supported). However, the effect sizes were small in all waves, with meta-analytic estimates ranging between −0.20 and −0.12 on a 5-point Likert scale, corresponding to a 3 to 5% reduction relative to the scale range.

Finally, we found limited evidence that the treatment impacted political behavior (Fig. 2). To examine these effects, we calculated the meta-analytic impact of the treatment on participants' intention and decision to vote for a far-right party in the European election (study 2a) and the French parliamentary election (study 2b). Although these elections were distinct, a meta-analysis was appropriate since they featured largely the same political parties and occurred just three weeks apart. The treatment had no impact on the intention to vote for a far-right party (meta-analytic effect: β = −0.14; SE = 0.09; z = −1.55; 95% CI = −0.32, 0.04; P = 0.121; H8 not supported), but a significant negative effect on the propensity to vote for a far-right party in the postelection round (meta-analytic effect: β = −0.22, SE = 0.10, z = −2.15, 95% CI = −0.43, −0.02, P = 0.032; H8-Wave2 supported). These effects were small, with the treatment reducing the predicted probability of voting for a far-right party by ∼1.7 to 3.4 percentage points. However, they had substantial electoral implications in study 2b: 26.7% of participants in the treatment group who initially intended to vote for the far-right changed their minds and reported voting for another party in the postelection wave, compared with 15.3% in the control group (Table S21).

We also used a second measure to capture the propensity to vote for parties with anti-immigration platforms: an expert survey of political scientists that attributes an anti-immigration voting score (on a 0–10 Likert scale) to eight of the most important French political parties, from the radical left to the radical right (38). These parties represented the political preferences of the majority of our participants: 81.4% (study 2a) and 63.4% (study 2b) of participants who reported their preferred party indicated voting for one of them. Using this alternative operationalization, we found that the treatment had a marginally significant negative effect on the anti-immigration score of the party for whom participants intended to vote in the pre-election wave (meta-analytic effect: β = −0.27; SE = 0.15; z = −1.78; 95% CI = −0.58, 0.03; P = 0.074; H9 not supported), and a significant one in the postelection wave (meta-analytic effect: β = −0.31; SE = 0.12; z = −2.45; 95% CI = −0.55, −0.06; P = 0.014; H9-Wave2 supported). In both cases, the effect was very small, corresponding to a reduction of 2.7 and 3.1% of the total scale, respectively.

These effects were reduced and lost their statistical significance when we introduced double-lasso-selected covariates in the model to correct for baseline asymmetries between the treated and untreated group. With these controls, the treatment had a negligible and nonsignificant impact on individuals' intention to vote for far-right political parties in both studies (meta-analytic effect: β = −0.12; SE = 0.79; z = −0.15; 95% CI = −1.67, 1.43; P = 0.878) or on the anti-immigration score of the party for which they reported an intention to vote (Meta-analytic effect: β = −0.02; SE = 0.03; z = −0.70; 95% CI = −0.07, 0.03; P = 0.486). We obtained a similar result in the postelection wave: the treatment had no significant impact on people's probability to have voted for the far right (meta-analytic effect: β = −0.08; SE = 0.18; z = −0.44; 95% CI = −0.44, 0.28; P = 0.661) or on the anti-immigration score (β = −0.05; SE = 0.08; z = −0.65; 95% CI = −0.22, 0.11; P = 0.513).

Finally, we found no impact of the treatment on participants' donations to a pro-immigration charity in study 2a, either in the pre-election wave (without controls: β = −0.03; SE = 1.30; t = −0.02; 95% CI = −2.59, 2.53; P = 0.981; with controls: β = −0.35; SE = 1.25; t = −0.28; 95% CI = −2.80, 2.10; P = 0.779; H10 not supported) or in the postelection wave (without controls: β = 1.11; SE = 1.40; t = 0.79; 95% CI = −1.63, 3.85; P = 0.428; with controls: β = 0.38; SE = 1.35; z = 0.28; 95% CI = −2.27, 3.02; P = 0.7809; H10-Wave2 not supported). Moreover, the effects were extremely small, corresponding to changes of −0.35 to −0.03% in the proportion of the endowment donated to charity in the pre-election wave, and 0.38 to 1.11% in the postelection wave.

Heterogeneity analyses

To examine the heterogeneous effects of the treatment across various social groups, we added an interaction term between the treatment and key variables of interest (e.g. demographic characteristics, baseline misperceptions, attitudes, and behaviors toward immigration) to our primary models. Overall, the treatment did not have a heterogenous impact depending on specific subgroups (see Tables S1–S64 in Supplementary Material, Heterogeneity analyses [study 2a] and Tables S65–S120 in Supplementary Material, Heterogeneity analyses [study 2b]). Across both studies, only a few interaction terms were statistically significant, and most did not hold consistently. These findings align with the results obtained in study 1 as well as from previous research, suggesting that individuals from different social groups rely on similar cues to assess immigrants and determine the support they are willing to offer (20, 39).

Additionally, our design allowed participants to optionally click a “Learn more” button within each of the four information pieces provided in the treatment, offering them further details and sources. We conducted additional, nonpreregistered analyses to test whether participants who chose to access supplementary information were more likely to endorse the corresponding information after treatment. We found no consistent evidence that clicking on additional sections significantly influenced treated participants' attitudes, with one notable exception: opposition to immigration was significantly lower among those who clicked for more details at least once (Figs. S10–S29). However, this effect generally disappeared after controlling for pre-treatment opposition to immigration, suggesting that participants who were initially less opposed to immigration were also more likely to seek out further information—rather than the clicks causing the attitude change. There was one exception to this pattern. In the postelection wave of study 2a, treated participants who clicked to learn more about at least one information section showed significantly lower opposition to immigration one week after the treatment, and this effect persisted even when controlling for baseline opposition (Tables S15 and S16). While this result was not replicated in study 2b or observed immediately after the treatment, it suggests that offering detailed information and sources may help sustain the treatment effect over time.

Discussion

Across three studies, we found that exposure to deservingness cues reduced negative misperceptions and attitudes toward immigrants in France. Study 1 showed that deservingness cues increased the social acceptability of a low-status immigrant in an hypothetical context. In studies 2a and 2b, correcting misperceptions about the deservingness of immigrants reduced negative beliefs and attitudes about immigration in realistic, politically salient contexts. Exposure to a short text providing accurate information about immigrants strongly reduced the endorsement of negative misperceptions about immigration Additionally, our treatment had a small but significant negative effect on opposition to immigration. These findings suggest that interventions that improve the perception of individual immigrants can extend to broader policy attitudes, despite prior research showing that evaluations of individuals and general immigration attitudes do not necessarily overlap (30). In contrast, the evidence on voting behavior was mixed: while the treatment significantly reduced the likelihood of voting for a far-right party in the first round of the French parliamentary elections, this effect was not consistent across studies or measures of voting behavior and was not robust to the inclusion of control variables. This finding aligns with a meta-analysis showing a null average effect of campaign interventions on voting behavior in major elections (40).

The results of the present research are striking in four respects. First, the misperceptions corrected are highly salient in the French political context, and the effect of the correction might impact policy acceptability. For instance, the belief that immigrants choose their destination based on the availability of welfare benefits has been a central theme for right-wing politicians in France, leading to recent reforms that restrict welfare access for immigrants (41). However, in study 2a (2b), 53% (44%) of participants who initially held this belief corrected it after exposure to our treatment, endorsing the opposite, more positive view (Tables S17 and S20).

Second, the effect on attitudes is small but robust, with important theoretical and practical implications. Previous studies that provided corrective information about immigration have typically found an effect on beliefs, but not on attitudes (42–44). These studies often focused on correcting misperceptions about the number of immigrants in the population (45). For example, across seven experiments, Hopkins et al. (42) found that correcting numerical misperceptions had no significant effect on immigration attitudes among American participants. More recent work has attempted to design more complex interventions, emphasizing macro-level characteristics of immigrant populations, but has often yielded similar results (42–44). Based on these findings, many researchers argue that immigration attitudes are not primarily shaped by factual beliefs, but rather reflect stable predispositions formed early in life (46, 47). This aligns with the broader political science literature suggesting that political attitudes are generally difficult to change in adulthood, and likely helps explain why our intervention had only a modest effect on attitudes compared with beliefs.

However, the fact that we still find a significant effect on attitudes suggests that deservingness beliefs could be playing a special role in shaping public views on immigration. This finding points to the potential of framing strategies that move beyond static macroeconomic indicators and instead emphasize perceived moral attributes—such as intentions, effort, or character—as a promising pathway to reduce prejudice against immigrants. It aligns with prior research suggesting that attitudes can, in fact, shift on high-profile issues with much media coverage and interpersonal discussion. Presumably, this is because people are then exposed to information about the issue at hand, which leads them to change their attitudes (48). In the case of immigration, the media coverage tends to be negative (49). Moreover, immigration can be seen by political parties as the remit of the far-right, and they might shy away from broaching the issue at all. The present results suggest that communication aimed at dispelling misperceptions about immigration, and more specifically misperceptions about the deservingness of immigrants, if done at scale, could influence the attitudes of the population.

Third, our effects were robust across an important range of contexts and populations. In study 1, deservingness cues improved the perceptions of immigration across political ideologies (for similar results, see (50)). Similarly, studies 2a and 2b found that participants reporting an intention to vote for the far-right still tended to update their views about immigration after exposure to cues of deservingness. For instance, even among participants who intended to vote for a far-right party before the treatment, 29% (study 2a) and 25% (study 2b) accepted that, contrary to their prior beliefs, most immigrants to France have not chosen this country for its welfare benefits (Tables S19 and S22). More generally, our heterogeneity analyses found no consistent evidence that the treatment was not effective on participants with more negative views on immigration (SI Appendix, Heterogeneity analyses). Theoretically, these findings support the view that deservingness cues are processed by universal cognitive mechanisms derived from our broader moral psychology (14–16, 51, 52). Practically, this means that providing accurate information about the deservingness of immigrants is not only unlikely to backfire with anti-immigrant audiences, but is on the contrary likely to also reduce their misperceptions and potentially their negative attitudes toward immigration. These findings highlight the added value of incorporating psychological theory into interventions aimed at reducing anti-immigration attitudes. By addressing the underlying psychological causes of these attitudes, researchers can develop interventions that are effective across different cultures and political ideologies (16).

Fourth, we observe tentative effects on reported voting behavior, although they were less precisely estimated than the effects on attitudes. The original specification, relying only on randomization, revealed an effect of the treatment on voting behavior, with a shift away from far-right parties. Adding controls reduced the size of the effect, and rendered it nonsignificant. However, it is important to note that the share of people voting for far-right parties dropped by 10 percentage points more after the treatment than after the control intervention in study 2b (while it only dropped by 3 percentage points in study 2a; Tables S18 and S21).

There are several limitations to our findings. First, we used convenience samples in all three studies, which may have led to demographic imbalances. In particular, older and less educated individuals were underrepresented. For example, in study 2a, only 0.2% of the sample was aged 75 or older, whereas this age group represents ∼12% of the French population (Table S7). However, existing evidence suggests that members of underrepresented groups would not have responded differently to our treatment. Although baseline attitudes toward immigration differ by age and education, with older and less educated respondents generally expressing more negative views (53, 54), prior research shows that persuasive messages have similar effects across demographic groups (50). Moreover, experimental findings from nationally representative samples replicate closely in online convenience samples, suggesting that treatment effect heterogeneity is generally low in standard social science survey experiments (55). Our own analyses confirm this, revealing no significant heterogeneity of the treatment effect by age or education (see SI Appendix, Heterogeneity analyses). Despite these limitations, our sample becomes more representative of the French population among individuals under the age of 60 and those with at least a high school education; and is broadly reflective of the French population in terms of gender, region, and, crucially, political opinion. Indeed, participants' voting intentions closely matched electoral polls conducted at the time of our studies (Tables S7 and S8).

Second, the relative influence of each deservingness cues on beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors remains uncertain. In study 1, we found that three of the four cues significantly influenced perceptions of an immigrant's social acceptability. However, the design of studies 2a and 2b precludes an evaluation of the effect of each cue in isolation.

Finally, while our treatments were designed to elicit deservingness intuitions through cues of reciprocity, alternative interpretations of our results remain. First, the treatments may have also influenced another dimension of deservingness: perceptions of need. Immigrants portrayed as successfully integrating—or whose children achieve upward mobility—might be seen as less needy, and therefore, paradoxically, less deserving of support (12, 13). This interpretation is conceptually plausible; however, any such effect would likely attenuate rather than inflate the observed treatment effects. The fact that we still find significant effects on beliefs and attitudes suggests that reciprocity cues exert a meaningful influence, and that our estimates may, if anything, be conservative due to a potential reduction in perceived need. Second, it is possible that information about immigrants' socioeconomic integration over time was also interpreted as evidence of cultural assimilation—which is known to shape immigration attitudes (56). However, we note that cultural integration can also be interpreted as a cue of prosocial motivation. In this sense, both interpretations—socioeconomic and cultural—may align with the deservingness framework, as each implies a will to cooperate with the host society. Lastly, the treatments in studies 2a and 2b may have influenced not only perceptions of deservingness but also, indirectly, beliefs about the sociotropic benefits of immigration: well-integrated immigrants can be seen as both morally worthy and economically beneficial. However, the current design significantly limits this possibility. Unlike much of the existing literature (7–10), the treatment does not stress the economic benefits of immigration in general, emphasizing instead the trajectories of migrants, directing attention to individual effort rather than economic contribution. Moreover, the treatment explicitly notes that immigrants initially face lower socioeconomic integration (e.g. lower employment rates and weaker language test performance than natives), before showing their progress over time.

More research is needed to better understand the role of deservingness in reducing anti-immigration attitudes. Future interventions may further disentangle psychological pathways with a design that jointly manipulates deservingness cues, cultural assimilation, and sociotropic contributions. Relatedly, a more granular approach—testing multiple treatments with different corrective messages—might help isolate the most effective arguments among the four that we used. While our studies focused on French participants; replicating these findings in other countries is a critical avenue for future research, especially since the deservingness heuristic is thought to function as a cross-cultural psychological mechanism (14, 15). Future work should also explore the effects of more structured, repeated exposure to information about immigrants' deservingness. For instance, classroom-based training sessions centered on providing information about the deservingness of immigrants could be designed to assess longer-term impacts on attitudes (57).

Materials and methods

The hypotheses, sample size, experiment design, and analysis plan were preregistered for all three studies before data collection on Open Science Framework (OSF) (study 1: https://osf.io/rm7sz; study 2a: https://osf.io/muctb; study 2b: https://osf.io/ztv2c). Respondents provided their informed consent at the beginning of each study. Studies 1, 2a, and 2b received an ethical approval by the Institutional Review Board of the Paris School of Economics on 2024 March 28, which was extended to study 2b on 2024 June 25 (IRB application #2024-014).

The survey software Qualtrics was used to collect all survey responses. Data were analyzed with R (58).

Study 1

Participants

A convenience sample of 506 French citizens aged 18+ was recruited online through the recruitment platform Crowdpanel, and agreed with the terms and conditions of the study. Following our preregistered exclusion criteria, we excluded 26 participants who met one or more of the following conditions: failing the captcha verification, completing the survey in <1 min, or taking <6 s for the vignette rating task. This resulted in a final sample of 480 participants (Table S1 and Figs. S1–S6).

Data was collected on 2024 April 17 and 18. All participants were compensated at a rate of 13.36 euros/h in accordance with Crowdpanel's payment policy.

Materials and procedure

After consenting to the study, providing their platform ID, and going through a series of checks (attention checks and a captcha verification), participants read the study instructions and completed a vignette rating task.

Each participant rated three vignettes of a male immigrant who arrived in France with no job, no degree, and poor French language skills. Participants were explicitly told that the profiles were fictional. The profile varied randomly across four dimensions: reasons for migration (finding a job vs. access to welfare), improvement in French (rapid progress or no progress), employment efforts (rapidly found a job or took years to find one), and children's social mobility (children attained a white-collar profession or became low-skill workers like their father). The immigrant's name and country of origin (Moroccan or Italian) also varied—as previous work has shown that non-EU immigrants are rated as less deserving than immigrants from the EU (29) (see SI Appendix, Section 1.2 for the full text of the vignettes). The immigrant's name was matched with the country of origin, but each country of origin was randomly matched with one of nine names. The nine Moroccan names were: “Mohammed,” “Ahmed,” “Youssef,” “Said,” “Rachid,” “Mustapha,” “Hicham,” “Khalid,” and “Omar”; and the nine Italian names were: “Giuseppe,” “Giovanni,” “Antonio,” “Francesco,” “Luigi,” “Salvatore,” “Vincenzo,” “Roberto,” and “Paolo.” After reading each vignette, participants answered a measure of their support for the immigrant's permanent settlement in France, measured by their response to the statement “People like [Name] should be allowed to settle permanently in France” on a 7-point Likert scale—which we adapted from (16). With each participant evaluating three vignettes, we collected 1,440 vignette evaluations across 480 participants, for an average of 45 evaluations for each of the 32 possible vignettes. After completing the vignette evaluations, participants were asked to complete a questionnaire capturing their sociodemographic information and political attitudes.

In addition to the primary outcome, we gathered data on participants' demographics and political opinions through a posttask questionnaire. Participants indicated their political ideology on a scale from 1 (Far-left) to 9 (Far-right) and identified the political parties they felt closest to from a list of major French parties: Parti Communiste Français, La France Insoumise, Europe Ecologie Les Verts, Parti Socialiste, La République en Marche, Les Républicains, Rassemblement National, and Reconquête!. Additionally, we recorded birth year, gender (female, male, other), education level (on a 1–9 scale from less to more educated), and subjective socioeconomic status (self-assessed from 1 = lowest income rank in French society to 10 = highest income rank)—which we used as control variables. We also measured a range of additional variables for exploratory purposes, which were not included in our analyses. Survey materials, including both PDF and QSF formats, are available in the OSF repository associated with this paper.

To ensure data quality, we implemented a diverse set of preregistered checks and exclusion criteria throughout our survey. First, participants were informed at the beginning of the survey that attention checks would be included. Two instructed eesponse items (IRIs) served as attention checks and were administered prior to the vignette ratings, following best-practice recommendations (59–61). Each check was explicitly identified to participants by the phrase “This is an attention check.” Participants who failed an attention check were removed from the data. Second, a reCaptcha test was used to confirm that participants were human, excluding any participant who failed this test. Third, at the conclusion of the survey, participants self-reported their attention level during the study (60), though this was not used as an exclusion criterion. Finally, response times were recorded for the three vignette ratings and for the survey's total duration. Participants who completed the survey in <1 min or took <6 s to rate all three vignettes were excluded from the analysis.

Data analysis

We used linear mixed models to investigate the effect of the five manipulated dimensions of the vignettes on the acceptability of the immigrant, while allowing random intercepts by participant and by immigrant's name, and controlling for age, education level, subjective social status, and gender. We used the lmer() function in the lme4 package in R to perform the analyses (62).

We also ran the same models on different subsets of our sample. We divided participants based on their self-reported political ideology into a “Left-wing” and “Right-wing” category using a median split. We also classified them, on the basis of the party they felt closer to, as either Left-wing (“Parti Communiste Français”; “La France Insoumise”; “Europe Ecologie Les Verts”; “Parti Socialiste”), Centrists (“La République en Marche”), or Right-wing (“Les Républicains”; “Rassemblement National”; “Reconquête!”).

The code for all statistical analyses are available in the R script attached to this article.

Studies 2a and 2b

Participants

Study 2a

Our study design required precise timing for participant recruitment, as we needed to secure the target sample size within a 5-day period. Thus, we opted to use multiple platforms to ensure timely and sufficient participation. Participants were recruited from three platforms: Crowdpanel (474 pre-election, 402 postelection), MSI (631 pre-election, 463 postelection), and Prolific Academic (481 pre-election, 441 postelection). For MSI and Crowdpanel, we requested representative samples based on age and gender for individuals aged 18–74, given the challenge of recruiting those aged 75+. Since this sampling option was unavailable on Prolific Academic, we instead used a convenience sample on that platform.

The pre-election wave took place between 2024 June 3 and June 7, just before the European elections on June 9. The postelection wave occurred between 2024 June 10 and June 14. Participants could enter either wave at any point during these respective fieldwork windows. We matched participants across waves using platform ID and a validation code. After removing duplicates, the final sample sizes were 425 (Crowdpanel), 604 (MSI), and 477 (Prolific) in the pre-election wave. In the postelection wave, the final samples were 381 (Crowdpanel), 458 (MSI), and 441 (Prolific). The overall attrition rate was 10.35% for Crowdpanel, 24.17% for MSI, and 8.11% for Prolific. Across all platforms, 1,506 participants completed the pre-election wave, 1,280 completed the postelection wave, and 1,271 were matched across both waves. A detailed description of the sample is described in Tables S6 and S7.

Study 2b

We recruited 1,298 participants through MSI in the pre-election wave and 905 in the postelection wave. The pre-election wave took place between 2024 June 24 and June 28, just before the pre-election wave of the anticipated parliamentary elections on June 30. The postelection wave took place between 2024 July 1 and July 5. Like in study 2a, participants could enter either wave at any point during these respective time windows. After removing duplicates, the final sample sizes were 1,255 in the pre-election wave and 904 in the postelection wave, with an attrition rate of 30.35%. A total of 885 participants were matched across both waves.

Material and procedure

In the pre-election wave, participants completed a questionnaire assessing their agreement with four misperceptions about immigration in France: that immigrants choose to immigrate based on welfare benefits, that their mastery of French does not improve over time, that they do not actively seek employment, and that their children achieve lower educational and professional outcomes compared with the children of natives. These misperceptions correspond to the deservingness cues manipulated in study 1. Participants also completed a similar questionnaire on cultural activities in France, either focusing on cinema attendance or participation in sports (with the theme randomly assigned). This questionnaire included items such as the percentage of people in France who attend the cinema at least once a year or which forms of exercise are most common in France. It was included to serve as the basis of the control group's correction task. Finally, participants were asked to report their opposition to immigration and their voting intention for the European election (study 2a) or the first round of the anticipated parliamentary election (study 2b).

They were then randomly allocated into one of two short correction tasks. In the treatment group, participants read short texts with accurate information addressing each of the four measured misperceptions about immigration in France. To counter the belief that immigrants choose their host country based on welfare availability, we presented data showing the high rate of nontake-up of welfare benefits among immigrants and survey evidence highlighting their lack of awareness about available welfare programs. To correct the other three beliefs, we provided official statistics showing that, on average, immigrants in France significantly improve their language test scores within a few years, rapidly increase their employment rate, and that their children generally achieve similar educational and professional status as native children. On average, treated participants spent 2 min on the correction task in both studies (study 2a: M = 2.07, SD = 4.42, min = 0.14; max = 97.54; study 2b: M = 1.91, SD = 4.04, min = 0.21; max = 86.60).

Participants in the control group received a similar correction task addressing their four answers to the questionnaire about cultural activities in France. In both tasks, the corrections were delivered through a structured feedback mechanism, where participants were shown their initial responses alongside the correct response and a short explanation. Additionally, participants could access more detailed information and references by clicking a “Learn more” option. In study 2a (2b), 25.4% (20.6%) clicked to learn more about the welfare item, 15.3% (9.8%) about language acquisition, 11.7% (7.7%) about employment, and 14.2% (6.9%) about children's upward mobility (Figs. S8 and S9). Clicking behavior did not vary meaningfully by education, political ideology, age, or gender (SI Appendix, Section 2.2.4). Corrections were shown sequentially rather than all at once; however, the treatment group concluded with an optional summary recap of all corrections. Only 3.4% of participants in study 2a and 3.5% in study 2b clicked on the option to read the recap, again with no substantial differences in clicking behavior across education, political ideology, age and gender (SI Appendix, Section 2.2.4). Both tasks relied on data from official state institutions (e.g. the French National Institute of Statistics).

Overall, the correction tasks were similar, with the only key difference being the correction theme (related to immigration or not). Using an “active” control group helps isolate the treatment effect, ensuring it reflects the impact of the specific information provided, rather than the general exposure to information (63). The complete text of the correction tasks in both groups—provided in the original French and in English translation—is available in the SI Appendix, Section 4. After the correction task, participants completed the same questionnaires a second time. In addition, they were asked the percentage of their payment that they were willing to donate to a pro-immigration charity (only in study 2a).

The postelection wave was launched the day after the election. Participants answered the same questionnaires as in the pre-election wave, but this time reporting their actual voting behavior. On average, the two participations were spaced by 6.42 days (M = 6.66, SD = 1.14, min = 2.76, max = 12.61) in study 2a and by 5.49 days (median = 5.28, SD = 1.28, min = 3.01, max = 9.23) in study 2b. To match participants across waves, we used participants' platform ID and a unique validation code that was given to them at the end of the pre-election wave.

To test the effect of the treatment, we collected several measures of beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors related to immigration, which we list below. Except for charity donations and actual voting decisions, all measures were collected three times: once before the correction task, once right after during the pre-election wave, and once more in the postelection wave.

Measures of beliefs about immigration

Belief that the availability of welfare is a major driver of immigration's decision to settle in France

Participants were asked which of the two following statements they agreed with the most: “In general, access to welfare benefits is a major motivation for immigrants who choose to settle in France” (coded 1) or “In general, access to welfare benefits is not a major motivation for immigrants who choose to settle in France” (coded 0).

Belief that non-French-speaking immigrants do not learn French

Participants were asked which of the two following statements they agreed with the most: “In general, non-French-speaking immigrants who settle in France do not learn the French language.” (coded 1) or “In general, non-French-speaking immigrants who settle in France learn the French language.” (coded 0).

Belief that immigrants in France do not actively look for jobs

Participants were asked which of the two following statements they agreed with the most: “In general, immigrants who settle in France do not actively seek employment.” (coded 1) or “In general, immigrants who settle in France actively seek employment.” (coded 0).

Belief that the children of immigrants attain lower educational and professional positions than the children of natives

Participants were asked which of the three following statements they agreed with the most: “In general, children of immigrants have a lower level of education and occupy lower job categories than French people whose parents are not immigrants.” (coded 1) or “In general, the children of immigrants have similar levels of education and occupy similar job categories to French people whose parents are not immigrants” and “In general, children of immigrants have a higher level of education and occupy higher job categories than French people whose parents are not immigrants.” (both coded 0).

Measures of attitudes toward immigration

Opposition to immigration

To measure this outcome, we asked: “Would you like to see the number of foreign immigrants allowed to live in France each year…” (1: increase a lot; 2: increase a little; 3: neither increase nor decrease; 4: decrease a little; 5: decrease a lot).

Measures of behaviors related to immigration

Intention/decision to vote

In the pre-election wave, we asked: “The [study 2a: European/study 2b: parliamentary] elections take place next Sunday. Which of the following [lists or political parties/coalitions] are you most likely to vote for? Please note that all data collected for this study is anonymous and confidential.” In the postelection wave, we asked the same question but inquired about their actual voting behavior rather than intentions. Participants were then provided an exhaustive list of options, with the possibility to report a blank vote, not voting, not knowing for whom to vote (only in the pre-election wave), and a “refuse to answer option.”

Donation to a pro-immigration charity

“Cimade is a French association that helps migrants and refugees. To find out more about Cimade: https://www.lacimade.org/. As part of this study, you can make a donation to Cimade with part of your payment. Your donation will be added to those of other participants in the study, and the total amount donated will go to Cimade. What percentage of your payment for this study would you like to donate to Cimade?” (Slider from 0 to 100%). This measure was only collected after the correction task in the pre-election and postelection waves in study 2a.

To interpret participants' voting decisions, we constructed two indices.

Far-right voting intention/decision

The voting intention or decision of participants was classified as far-right when it went to one of the two most important far-right political parties in France (“Rassemblement National” and “Reconquête!”).

Anti-immigration voting score

We used an expert survey of political scientists to attribute an anti-immigration voting score (on a 0–10 Likert scale) to eight of the most important French political parties—from the radical left to the radical right (38). These scores were respectively: La France Insoumise: 1.8; Parti Communiste Français: 4.1; Parti Socialiste: 3.6; Europe Ecologie Les Verts: 1.7; Renaissance: 5.9; Les Républicains: 8.1; Reconquête!: 10; Rassemblement National: 9.6. In the French parliamentary elections of 2024 (study 2b), the four Left-wing parties formed an electoral coalition called the “New Popular Front.” To determine its score, we averaged the scores of each party (2.8).

Quality checks

Our studies also included checks to ensure the quality of our data. First, as in study 1, two attention checks, implemented as IRI, were administered before the vignette ratings—following best-practice recommendations (59–61). Participants were informed at the beginning of the survey that attention checks would be included and were explicitly notified during the survey when an attention check was presented (preceded by the phrase “This is an attention check”). Second, at the conclusion of the survey, participants were asked to self-report their level of attention during the study (60).

Other measures

Finally, we collected information about participants' demographics and political opinion. Specifically, we measured participants' gender, age, education level, region of residence, subjective socioeconomic status, political ideology and social trust (see SI Appendix, Section 2.1).

Data analysis

To assess the effects of our treatment on beliefs, attitudes, and voting intentions toward immigration over two rounds, we preregistered regression models with each outcome as the dependent variable and treatment as the predictor. For continuous outcomes (opposition to immigration, anti-immigration voting score, and charity donation), we used linear regression with R's lm() function. For binary outcomes (the four deservingness beliefs and far-right voting intention/decision), we applied logistic regression with R's glm() function (using family = “binomial”).

To correct for potential baseline imbalances between our treatment and control group, we added a nonpreregistered analysis. We applied covariate adjustment using the postdouble selection Lasso method (35–37). This method identifies covariates by applying the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (Lasso) twice—first to select variables predictive of the outcome and then to identify those predictive of treatment status. This dual application serves as a principled robustness check to account for baseline imbalances in randomized controlled trials. All Lasso regressions were run using the glmnet() function of the glmnet package (64) and included the following covariates: age, education, political ideology, trust, the four baseline deservingness beliefs about immigration, baseline opposition to immigration, baseline intention to vote for the far-right, baseline anti-immigration ideology score, region of residence, and gender. We used the set.seed() function to ensure the reproducibility of our results. Detailed procedures and access to the selected covariates in each model are provided in the MODELS.R script attached to this manuscript.

The measured outcomes had no missing values, except for the anti-immigration voting score, which was calculated only for participants who reported voting for one of the eight political parties rated (63% of participants in study 2a and 53% of participants in study 2b participants reported voting for these parties). The effect of the treatment on anti-immigration voting score was thus measured on a significantly lower sample than the original one (study 2a: n = 855; study 2b: n = 774).

To examine the heterogeneous effects of the treatment across various social groups, we added an interaction term between the treatment and key variables of interest (e.g. demographic characteristics, baseline misperceptions, attitudes, and behaviors toward immigration) to our primary models. The exact specification and results of the heterogeneity analyses are presented in the SI Appendix, Heterogeneity analyses.

To facilitate interpretation, we ran additional, nonpreregistered analyses, and report AMEs for all logistic regressions, which account for the majority of our analyses. AMEs represent the average change in the predicted probability of the outcome associated with being in the treatment group rather than the control group. These estimates were computed using the margins() function from the margins package in R (65), and are presented in full in Tables S11 and S12. For linear regressions with continuous outcomes, we report the treatment effect as a percentage change relative to the full scale of the outcome variable.
