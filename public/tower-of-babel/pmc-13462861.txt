Interstitial oxide-ion conduction with unconventional polyhedral units in Bi2−xTe1+xO5+x/2
Authors: Lu Liang, Jinxin Ge, Hankun Xu, Yue Zhu, Jie Chen, Zhijian Tan, Changjian Li, Xiaohui Li, Kun Lin, Xiaojun Kuang, Qiang Li, Xianran Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Interstitial oxide-ion conductors are promising candidates for intermediate-temperature solid oxide fuel cells because of low migration barriers. However, such conductors with cation-centered polyhedral frameworks are relatively rigid and afford insufficient lattice free volume for the migration of interstitial oxygen. Herein, we report an interstitial oxide-ion conductor in layered Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05, which exhibits a conductivity of 1.41 × 10−3 S cm−1 at 650 °C, comparable to those of advanced oxide-ion conductors. The average structure shows that stereochemically active lone-pair electrons of Bi3+ and Te4+ induce highly distorted, non-cation-centered coordination units, thereby generating sufficient lattice free volume. Neutron diffraction coupled with maximum entropy method analysis determines the location and concentration of interstitial oxygen atoms, which are stabilized through coordination with adjacent Te atoms. The incorporation of interstitial oxygen fundamentally alters the local Te environment, driving a transition from exclusively three-coordinated units to a diverse coexistence of three-, four-, and five-coordinated units. Concurrently, neighboring lattice oxygen atoms are displaced by interstitial oxygen, giving rise to pronounced local structural relaxation and enhanced lattice flexibility that are conducive to interstitial oxide-ion transport. This work provides new insights into the design of interstitial oxide-ion conductors beyond conventional polyhedral framework systems.

Introduction

Solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs), which directly convert chemical energy into electrical energy, have attracted considerable attention in areas such as energy and transportation.1,2 They offer a number of advantages, including low pollutant emissions, high energy conversion efficiency, and fuel flexibility.3–5 Conventional oxide-ion conductors predominantly rely on oxygen vacancies as the main charge carriers. Examples include fluorites,6 perovskites,7,8 and BIMEVOX.9–11 These vacancy-mediated oxide-ion conductors typically exhibit a relatively high average oxygen migration energy barrier, which restricts their high ionic conductivity to high operating temperatures above 800 °C.12

In contrast to conventional vacancy-type conductors, interstitial oxide-ion conductors generally possess significantly lower migration energy barriers, enabling substantially higher ionic conductivities in the intermediate temperature range.12 This characteristic makes them promising candidates for lowering the operating temperature of SOFCs, thereby mitigating thermal mismatch, material degradation, and long-term stability issues.3,13–15 However, the accommodation of additional interstitial oxygen imposes stringent structural requirements, such as open framework structures, flexible and variable coordination environments, and sufficient lattice free volume.16 To date, interstitial oxide-ion conductors are primarily reported in several distinct families, including apatites,17–20 hexagonal perovskites,21,22 and the Sillén phase.23,24 They are generally based on framework structures constructed from cation-centered polyhedra (Fig. 1a).16,25

Herein, we demonstrate an interstitial oxide-ion conductor in Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05, consisting of alternating pure Bi layers and mixed Bi–Te layers stacked along the a-axis. The stereochemically active lone-pair electrons of Bi3+ and Te4+ induce highly distorted and non-cation-centered coordination units (Fig. 1a). Such structural features enhance lattice flexibility and create accessible local free volume, as also observed in hexagonal manganites, Rb5BiMo4O16 and so on.25–29 The resulting open local environment facilitates interstitial oxygen migration. In the present case, the conductivity of Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05 reaches up to 1.41 × 10−3 S cm−1 at 650 °C, comparable to those of advanced oxide-ion conductors.

Results and discussion

Solid solution of Bi2−xTe1+xO5+x/2

Ceramic pellets of Bi2−xTe1+xO5+x/2 (0 ≤ x ≤ 0.2) were successfully prepared with a relative density of approximately 87.8–93.1% (Table S1). Fig. 1b presents the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of Bi2−xTe1+xO5+x/2 (0 ≤ x ≤ 0.2). All compositions can be indexed to a single-phase orthorhombic structure. For 0 ≤ x ≤ 0.15, the (400) diffraction peak located near 36° gradually shifts toward higher diffraction angles. For x = 0.2, the peak shift becomes much less pronounced, accompanied by an evident change in the peak profile, implying that the lattice contraction tends to saturate at high doping levels. The evolution of the lattice parameters with increasing x (Fig. S1) further supports this trend. Owing to the significantly smaller ionic radius of Te4+ (0.52 Å) compared with that of Bi3+ (0.96 Å), the unit cell volume V continuously decreases in the range of 0 ≤ x ≤ 0.15, whereas it remains nearly unchanged when x reaches 0.2, indicating that x ≈ 0.15 represents the solid solution limit in Bi2−xTe1+xO5+x/2.

Rietveld refinement was performed on synchrotron XRD (SXRD) data of the parent Bi2TeO5 (x = 0) and the representative Te-excess composition Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05 (x = 0.1) using the Abm2 space group (Fig. 1c and S2). The refined crystallographic parameters are summarized in Tables S2 and S3. The refinement results for Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05 reveal that Te4+ dopants preferentially occupy the Bi2 site with an occupancy of 0.166, which is close to the nominal value of 0.20.

To assess the phase stability of Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05, variable-temperature XRD measurements were performed in air (Fig. 1d and S3), together with simultaneous thermogravimetric and differential scanning calorimetry (TG-DSC) analysis (Fig. S4). No additional diffraction peaks were observed from 25 to 690 °C, confirming that Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05 remains structurally stable without detectable phase transitions. With increasing temperature, all lattice parameters gradually expand, consistent with normal thermal expansion behavior (Fig. S3b).

The elemental distributions of Bi2TeO5 and Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05 were examined by field-emission electron probe microanalysis (EPMA) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) coupled with elemental mapping (Fig. 1e, S5 and S6). The results show uniform distribution of Bi, Te, and O across the grains.

Electrical properties of Bi2−xTe1+xO5+x/2

Fig. 2a presents the alternating-current (AC) impedance spectrum of parent Bi2TeO5 measured at 450 °C, which is characterized by a large semicircle in the high-frequency region. Based on the relation 2πfmaxRC = 1, where fmax is the peak frequency of the semicircle, the bulk capacitance (Cb) was estimated to be 4.28 × 10−12 F cm−1, which is consistent with typical bulk components of oxide ceramics.30 The intermediate-frequency response is assigned to a non-bulk interfacial process. Given the relatively low density of the Bi2TeO5 pellet, contributions from grain boundaries and internal pore surfaces may overlap.31,32 Moreover, the low-frequency response exhibits a depressed semicircular shape rather than a linear Warburg tail. Impedance spectra measured using Au and Pt electrodes show nearly identical high- and intermediate-frequency responses. Minor differences are observed in the low-frequency region, indicating an electrode-sensitive non-bulk polarization response (Fig. S7). This suggests that Bi2TeO5 is predominantly an electronic conductor.33 In contrast, the impedance spectrum of Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05 at 450 °C (Fig. 2b) displays a distinct Warburg-type response in the low-frequency region, characterized by an approximately 45° inclined line. The associated capacitance was estimated to be 6.96 × 10−6 F cm−1, which is typical for ion diffusion processes.34,35

Fig. 2c shows the Arrhenius plots of the bulk conductivity of Bi2−xTe1+xO5+x/2 (0 ≤ x ≤ 0.2) measured in ambient air between 300 and 650 °C. For 0 ≤ x ≤ 0.1, the conductivity increases systematically with increasing x. Notably, Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05 exhibits a conductivity of 1.41 × 10−3 S cm−1 at 650 °C, approximately 4.2 times that of parent Bi2TeO5 (3.33 × 10−4 S cm−1). Correspondingly, the activation energy Ea decreases from 1.096 eV (x = 0) to 1.005 eV (x = 0.1), indicating facilitated oxide-ion migration upon excess Te in Bi2−xTe1+xO5+x/2. However, when x increases further to 0.2, the conductivity decreases and the activation energy increases. The conductivity of Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05 is comparable to that of advanced oxide-ion conductors (Fig. S8).

To elucidate the charge carriers, impedance spectra of Bi2TeO5 and Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05 were collected at 650 °C under different oxygen partial pressures (Fig. 2d), and the typical capacitance values of the low-frequency arcs are shown in Table S4. For Bi2TeO5, the electrode response remains a depressed semicircle regardless of the oxygen partial pressure, indicating dominant electronic conduction. In contrast, Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05 exhibits a clear 45° Warburg-type response under pure Ar, which gradually evolves into a depressed semicircle as the oxygen partial pressure increases. This behavior is consistent with that of typical oxide-ion conductors.33,34 Consistent with the impedance results, the DC resistances in Fig. S9 comprise both the sample resistances and polarization resistances. The lower current in Ar is mainly attributed to increased polarization resistance arising from slower oxygen exchange kinetics.

The oxygen transport number tion was investigated using oxygen concentration cell measurement. As shown in Fig. 2e, the resulting tion values were 0.42–0.61 from 400 to 650 °C, confirming mixed electronic and oxide-ion conduction.

Fig. 2f shows the bulk conductivities of Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05 as a function of oxygen partial pressures. In the range of 10−4 to 0.21 atm, the conductivity remains nearly constant, which is characteristic of predominant oxide-ion conduction. Under pure O2, the conductivity decreases. However, a single high-pO2 data point is insufficient to assign a specific electronic carrier type.

Taken together, the combined evidence from AC impedance spectroscopy, EMF measurement, and oxygen partial pressure-dependent conductivity measurements suggests that partial substitution of Bi3+ by Te4+ significantly enhances the oxide-ion conductivity of Bi2−xTe1+xO5+x/2.

Because interstitial oxygen is typically present in low concentration, spatially dispersed, and often accompanied by local structural relaxation, its direct experimental identification is challenging. To quantitatively probe the presence and location of interstitial oxygen, time-of-flight (TOF) neutron powder diffraction (NPD) measurements were performed on Bi2TeO5 and Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05 (Fig. 3a and b). During refinement, excess Te atoms were allowed to randomly occupy the Bi1–Bi3 sites to evaluate site preference. The results reveal that excess Te atoms preferentially occupy the Bi2 site with an occupancy of 0.200, in excellent agreement with the nominal value. The refined crystal structure of Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05 is shown in Fig. 3c–e. The structure is layered, consisting of pure Bi layers (Bi1–Bi3) and mixed Bi2–Te layers.

The anisotropic displacement parameters (ADPs) derived from NPD, together with the thermal ellipsoids (Fig. 3d and e), show a clear trend: O2 and O5, which are only coordinated to Bi1 and Bi3, exhibit relatively smaller thermal vibrations compared with those of lattice oxygens coordinated to Bi2(Te2) and Te, particularly O3, O6, and O1 bonded to Te. This anisotropic vibrational behavior suggests that oxide-ion migration is predominantly confined to the mixed Bi2–Te layers, likely facilitated by cooperative rotational distortions of Te–O coordination units. This leads to a two-dimensional (2D) layered migration pathway. Furthermore, except for Te and O2 whose thermal parameters remain nearly unchanged, other atoms in Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05 exhibit enhanced atomic displacement compared to Bi2TeO5. This indicates increased lattice dynamics and structural flexibility induced by interstitial oxygen incorporation.

To further elucidate the stabilization form of interstitial oxygen, the neutron scattering length density (NSLD) maps were reconstructed using the maximum-entropy method (MEM) based on the refined structure factors. The MEM provides a less model-biased reconstruction of real-space scattering density, thereby minimizing subjective bias.36,37 In Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05 (Fig. 3f and g), an additional weak nuclear density feature was observed at the 8d site (∼0.116, ∼0, ∼0.238) between two neighboring Te atoms, which is absent in Bi2TeO5 (Fig. 3h and S10). Rietveld refinement yields an occupancy of ∼0.044 for interstitial oxygen Oi, close to the theoretical value of 0.05 estimated from charge balance. This provides direct structural evidence for interstitial oxygen incorporation. Considering the low occupancy of the interstitial oxygen, only a marginal improvement in the Rietveld refinement is expected. Nevertheless, combined with the MEM nuclear-density maps, the structural model containing the interstitial oxygen provides the most physically reasonable description of Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05.

Comparison of bond lengths derived from NPD (Table S7) reveals that the introduction of interstitial oxygen results in a noticeable elongation of Te–O1 and Te–O3 bonds, while the Te–O6 bond slightly shortens. Overall, lattice oxygen atoms shift away from the interstitial oxygen site, causing the increase in Te–O bond length. Meanwhile, Bi atoms undergo charge-compensating displacements, leading to an overall shortening of Bi–O distances. The distance between neighboring Te atoms decreases from 3.902 Å in Bi2TeO5 to 3.861 Å in Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05, due to the attraction of the interstitial oxygen.

Local Structure of Bi2−xTe1+xO5+x/2

High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) was employed to investigate the atomic structure of Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05. Fig. 4a and S11a display representative atomic-resolution HAADF-STEM images along the [011] and [100] zone axes, respectively. The corresponding overview images are shown in Fig. S12. The electron diffraction patterns were obtained by applying fast Fourier transform (FFT). For the [011] zone axis, the diffraction spots can be indexed to the (211̄), (100), and (022̄) lattice planes, whereas the [100] zone axis exhibits reflections corresponding to the (062), (060), and (002) planes (Fig. 4b and S11b). The electron diffraction patterns are in good agreement with the simulated patterns shown in Fig. S13. Furthermore, the experimental HAADF-STEM images along both the [011] and [100] zone axes show excellent agreement with the corresponding simulated STEM images derived from the proposed structural models (Fig. 4c and S11c).

Along the [011] zone axis, the atomic image exhibits a characteristic arrangement consisting of alternating rows of strong and weak atomic contrast, corresponding to the pure Bi layers and the mixed Bi2–Te layers, respectively. To visually analyze the local cation distribution, atomic column intensities were extracted from the HAADF-STEM images (Fig. 4d and e). The atomic column intensity within the pure Bi layers remains nearly unchanged, whereas pronounced and spatially irregular intensity fluctuations are observed in the mixed Bi2–Te layers. Considering the strong Z-contrast characteristic of HAADF-STEM imaging, these variations indicate that the excess Te atoms occupy the Bi2 sites in a disordered arrangement. Along the [100] zone axis, the atomic structure consists of alternating rows of one strong atomic column and two relatively weak atomic columns, corresponding to the Bi2–Bi3 layers and the Bi1–Te layers, respectively. The atomic structure observed by HAADF-STEM is consistent with the NPD refinement results.

The geometric phase analysis (GPA) of the (100) lattice plane (Fig. S14) exhibits a highly homogeneous strain distribution, indicating an intact lattice structure in this region without detectable local lattice distortions or abrupt strain gradients associated with dislocations. This observation suggests that the incorporation of excess Te and interstitial oxygen appears to be spatially well dispersed within the lattice. Such a uniformly distributed strain may provide a structurally continuous and energetically smooth environment for oxide-ion migration, which is favorable for long-range ionic transport.38

SXRD and NPD primarily probe the average crystal structure, whereas pair distribution function (PDF) analysis provides insight into the local structure over short- to medium-range length scales.39,40 Such local structural heterogeneity plays a critical role in determining oxide-ion migration behavior. For instance, variations in bond lengths, coordination asymmetry, and local lattice distortions can directly influence activation barriers, the stability of oxygen defects and migration pathways.

To further investigate the local structural characteristics, reverse Monte Carlo (RMC) simulations were performed based on the PDF data (Fig. 4f and S15). 6 × 4 × 12 supercells (∼69.4 × 65.9 × 66.4 Å3) containing a total of 20 736 atoms or vacancies were constructed for large-box fitting (Fig. S16).

The atomic pair distance distributions obtained from the RMC simulations are shown in Fig. 4g and S17. The Bi⋯Bi pair distances remain nearly unchanged after excess Te incorporation, indicating that the fundamental framework of the host structure is largely preserved. In contrast, the incorporation of interstitial oxygen induces noticeable local rearrangements around Te sites. Specifically, the interstitial oxygen attracts itsneighboring Te cations, leading to a shortening of the Te⋯Te pair distances accompanied by a slight elongation of the Te–O bond lengths (Fig. 4h).

Further insight into the local structural evolution can be obtained from the coordination environments of Te (Fig. 4i). In Bi2TeO5, Te predominantly adopts a three-coordinated configuration (Fig. 4i and S18a). By contrast, additional four- and five-coordinated Te species emerge in Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05 (Fig. 4i and S18b). The four-coordinated environments can be attributed to either (i) Te occupying the Bi2 site and forming covalent bonds with four oxygen atoms and exhibiting a secondary bond interaction with an additional oxygen atom41 or (ii) Te coordinated with an extra interstitial oxygen atom. Meanwhile, the five-coordinated environments are associated with Te occupying the Bi2 site or coordinating with two interstitial oxygen atoms. Quantitative analysis indicates that the relative proportions of three-, four-, and five-coordinated Te species in Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05 are approximately 20.2 : 2.7 : 1. This evolution is also reflected in the Te–O bond length distribution shown in Fig. 4g. The nearest-neighbor Te–O peak centered at ∼1.83 Å mainly originates from three-coordinated Te species, and its reduced peak area indicates a decrease in the population of such environments. Correspondingly, the enhanced peak intensity within the range of 2.0 ≤ r ≤ 2.4 Å suggests the increasing presence of four- and five-coordinated Te environments.

In comparison with Te, the coordination environments of Bi remain broadly distributed in both Bi2TeO5 and Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05, with coordination numbers ranging from 3 to 8, reflecting the intrinsically flexible and highly distorted local environments associated with the stereochemically active lone-pair electrons of Bi3+ (Fig. S19).

This structural evolution is corroborated by Bi L3-edge X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS). The normalized X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra (Fig. S20a) indicate that both Bi2TeO5 and Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05 exhibit absorption edges close to that of Bi2O3, confirming the Bi3+ oxidation state. In the k3-weighted Fourier-transformed extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra, the Bi–O coordination shell distances in Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05 decrease, consistent with the neutron refinement results (Fig. S20b). Wavelet transform (WT) analysis (Fig. 4j and S20c) reveals a pronounced attenuation of intermediate-range Bi⋯Bi (Te) correlations. This reduction arises from two effects: partial substitution of Bi3+ by Te4+ decreases the Bi population, and the introduction of interstitial oxygen disrupts intermediate-range ordering within the Bi sublattice.

Taken together, NPD, HAADF-STEM, PDF and XAFS results demonstrate that excess Te substitution at the Bi2 site induces the formation of interstitial oxygen in Bi2TeO5. The interstitial oxygen is stabilized through cooperative local lattice rearrangements, generating structurally flexible pathways that facilitate oxide-ion migration.

Migration mechanism of Bi2−xTe1+xO5+x/2

To further elucidate the oxide-ion migration mechanism in Bi2−xTe1+xO5+x/2, the bond-valence-based energy landscape (BVEL) calculations were performed based on the bond valence sum formalism. This approach has been widely employed to visualize migration pathways and evaluate energy barrier distributions in solid electrolytes.31,42–44 The refined crystal structure of Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05 obtained from NPD data was used as the structural model for the BVEL calculations.

Fig. S21 shows the iso-surfaces of the BVEL calculated results at 0.76 eV viewed along the direction of the [001] and [010] planes, respectively, which give the possible migration pathways of oxide ions. The calculated energy landscapes clearly reveal a pronounced 2D migration characteristic. The estimated activation energies (E3D = 0.398 eV > E2D = 0.200 eV) indicate that long-range transport is energetically favored within the 2D layers. The calculated migration energy (0.398 eV) is lower than the experimental activation energy (1.005 eV) derived from conductivity measurements because the experimental value includes both the defect formation energy and the defect migration energy, whereas the BVEL calculation accounts only for the migration energy.

As suggested by both BVEL mapping and other experimental results, oxygen migration is facilitated by cooperative rotational distortions of Te–O coordination units. The incorporation of interstitial oxygen locally perturbs the lattice and induces bond rearrangements. This process corresponds to a typical interstitialcy mechanism, in which the interstitial oxygen does not migrate independently but instead induces concerted displacement of neighboring lattice oxygen atoms. Such cooperative motion lowers the effective migration barrier and enhances transport dimensionality within the mixed Bi2–Te layers.

Conclusions

In conclusion, excess Te incorporation effectively tailors the local structural environment of Bi2−xTe1+xO5+x/2, leading to the emergence of interstitial oxide-ion conduction in this layered structure. The optimized composition, Bi1.9Te1.1O5.05, achieves an oxide-ion conductivity of 1.41 × 10−3 S cm−1 at 650 °C, comparable to that of advanced oxide-ion conductors. The highly distorted coordination environments and local free volume in Bi2TeO5 provide a structurally flexible lattice capable of accommodating interstitial oxygen species. These findings highlight the potential of non-classical lone-pair-driven structures as a new platform for developing high-performance interstitial oxide-ion conductors.
