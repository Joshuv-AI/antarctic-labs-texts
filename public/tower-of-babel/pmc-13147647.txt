Preserving patient safety in Nordic emergency care: concerns from Nordic anaesthesiology societies regarding development goals of emergency medicine
Authors: Danish Society of Anaesthesiology and Intensive Care Medicine (DASAIM), Swedish Society of Anaesthesiology and Intensive Care Medicine (SFAI), Norwegian Society of Anaesthesiology (NAF)
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the article “Definition and Developmental Goals for Nordic Emergency Medicine” [1], in which a working group under the Nordic emergency medicine societies proposes a definition and developmental objectives for the specialty in a Nordic context. We further note that the proposed definitions are approved by the five Nordic emergency medicine societies. As national anaesthesiology societies of Denmark, Sweden and Norway, we feel compelled to respond, given that the proposal includes a shift in responsibility for stabilization of critically ill and injured patients from anaesthesiology to emergency medicine. We fear such a shift may jeopardize patient safety.

The authors highlight key challenges facing Nordic emergency medicine and, in response, propose a revised definition of the specialty. This includes, among other points, that an emergency physician:

“Should be able to perform the initial evaluation and treatment of all patients presenting to the emergency department or in the pre-hospital setting.”

“Should be able to perform the initial evaluation and treatment of all patients presenting to the emergency department or in the pre-hospital setting.”

“Should have training to perform any acute procedures that are technically feasible to perform in the emergency department or on scene and required to stabilize unstable patients.”

“Should have training to perform any acute procedures that are technically feasible to perform in the emergency department or on scene and required to stabilize unstable patients.”

Should these ambitions be realized, they would represent a substantial departure from the current standard of care in Scandinavia or the Nordic countries, where anaesthesiologists play a central role as critical care providers.

The healthcare systems of the Nordic countries differ significantly from those in which emergency medicine occupies the role envisioned by the authors. A key challenge in maintaining critical care competencies in the Nordic countries is the combination of low population density and a high level of societal safety [2, 3]. This limits the number of critically ill and injured patients and the subsequent need for critical procedures. In our healthcare systems, anaesthesiologists are the only specialists who consistently acquire and maintain broad experience in the care of critically ill or injured patients. This expertise is built in operating rooms and intensive care units, where perioperative care necessitates advanced airway management, respiratory support, and circulatory stabilization in response to anesthetic agents and surgical procedures. Intensive care settings further refine these skills through continuous exposure to critically ill patients. The experience gained in these environments is essential for both technical and non-technical skills required in treatment of critically ill or injured patients.

Anaesthesiologists in the Nordic countries apply their critical care expertise throughout the hospital, responding to medical emergencies, cardiac arrests, and trauma in both adults and children. They also extend this role to the pre-hospital setting through mobile critical units. Thus, in the Nordic healthcare systems, the foundation of critical care is built upon anaesthesiology, which provides a comprehensive approach to managing critically ill patients across specialties and age groups. This model ensures optimal collaboration with other specialties, including emergency medicine, within hospitals, and with paramedics, ambulance personnel, and rescue services in the pre-hospital setting. The emergency medicine working group presents no patient-related justification for changing this structure, and we can think of no such justification either.

A key goal of the upcoming Danish healthcare reform is to “create the framework for a high-quality healthcare system across the entire country” [4]. In systems with a low volume of critically ill and injured patients, as seen in the Nordic countries, ensuring consistently high-quality critical care relies on one specialty maintaining continuity throughout the course of care. This specialty should be the one with the most extensive training and experience in managing critical illness across all hospital departments and in pre-hospital settings. That is why critical care and pre-hospital emergency response remain integral to anaesthesiology—forming one of the four pillars of the specialty, a position further supported by a recent Scandinavian Delphi study defining core competencies of critical emergency medicine [5] There is neither a structural basis nor sufficient patient volume in the Nordic healthcare systems to justify changing this, if we are to prioritize optimal patient care.

We understand and support our emergency medicine colleagues in their efforts to improve conditions in emergency departments and enhance recruitment to the specialty. However, this must not come at the expense of patient care by shifting responsibility for critically ill and injured patients to a broader group of physicians who, within a Nordic context, do not have the possibility of achieving and maintaining the necessary expertise. We are concerned that the extent of the Nordic societies’ ambitions to develop emergency medicine as a specialty may inadvertently compromise the quality of care for our most critical patients.
