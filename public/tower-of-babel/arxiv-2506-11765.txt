State constrained stochastic optimal control of a PV system with battery storage via Fokker-Planck and Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equations Journal: arXiv
Alfredo Bermúdez, Iago Padín

Abstract
With the growing global emphasis on sustainability and the implementation of contemporary environmental policies, the penetration of renewable energy sources has significantly increased –primarily due to their inexhaustible nature. In this context, integrating photovoltaic (PV) modules with energy storage systems offers a means to mitigate the inherent variability of solar energy. This paper proposes an optimal methodology for power management and market bidding strategies tailored to PV power producers. To achieve this, the PV system, electricity price dynamics, and battery storage behavior are modeled using a system of stochastic differential equations (SDEs), resulting in a stochastic optimal control problem with state constraints. The corresponding optimality conditions are derived from the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) and Fokker-Planck (FP) equations. Additionally, a simplified formulation of the control problem is introduced, which substantially reduces computational complexity by decreasing the dimension of the problem.

Introduction

Solar photovoltaics (PV) are expected to be the leading renewable energy source in the future due to their scalability, declining costs, and minimal environmental impact.
[
1
,

]
. Despite the evident advantages, this energy source also poses some technical challenges. The most critical inconvenient is the intermittency of solar irradiance. This issue complicates grid integration, energy planning, and market participation, making it necessary to develop control and optimization strategies to mitigate its effects.

To overcome the challenges associated with renewable energy, advancements in grid interconnection and management, along with improvements in weather forecasting, are of paramount importance. In the case of PV systems, various strategies have been proposed for optimizing its integration
[

, including hybrid approaches combining PV panels with wind turbines

. More recently, the combination of PV plants with energy storage systems has arised as a promising alternative with effective results

. In this context, batteries allow to smooth out sudden fluctuations in production, enhancing grid stability, improving overall energy reliability, and guaranteeing security of supply.

Accurately predicting the evolution of PV generation or energy market prices requires precise mathematical modelling. Among the various available approaches, SDEs are a widely used tool to handle uncertainty
[
10
,
11
]
. Other methodologies based on Markov decision processes (MDP),
[
12
]
, or time series offer different alternatives on forecasting and decision-making.

From the perspective of problem formulation, Dynamic Programming Principle (DPP) emerges as an appropriate framework. Several studies have explored its application in energy systems from a discrete-time perspective demonstrating its effectiveness
[
8
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
. In this paper, we employ stochastic optimal control theory within a continuous-time framework, which naturally leads to the HJB equation
[
16
]
. However, the inclusion of state constraints –such as bounds on battery energy levels– introduces additional complexity, making the HJB equation alone insufficient for solving the problem. Fortunately, this difficulty can be addressed by reformulating the stochastic optimal control problem as a deterministic optimal control problem using the FP partial differential equation (PDE)
[
17
,
18
]
. This methodology has been successfully applied in previous work
[
19
]
, where a wind power plant with a battery storage system was analyzed.

An interesting novelty of this paper lies in the reduction of the dimension of the stochastic control problem. The system state can be decomposed into two components: one influenced by the control input and another evolving independently of it. Consequently, the FP equation
[
20
]
can be decomposed into two equivalent lower-dimensional FP equations. This idea makes the approach highly scalable for practical applications.

This paper proposes a strategy to maximize the revenue of a renewable energy plant by optimizing both the real-time control of the storage system and the bids in the preceding electricity market auctions. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section

2
Stochastic control problem

This section introduces a stochastic control problem designed to handle the challenges of a PV energy plant with energy storage systems. The problem is then generalized to a broader formulation and rewritten as a deterministic control problem.

2.1
Mathematical modelling

The state system consists of three stochastic processes which are the Clear-Sky-Index (CSI),
Z_{s}
, the price of electricity,
\Pi_{s}
, and the energy content of the battery,
\mathcal{E}_{s}

1

The CSI was introduced in
[
11

to characterize the renewable factor associated with solar irradiance. It is defined as the ratio between the Global Horizontal Irradiance (GHI),
I_{s}
, and the Clear Sky Irradiance,
I_{CS}
, which represents the maximum irradiance observable under cloud-free conditions:

Z_{s}=\frac{I_{s}}{I_{CS}(s)}.

(1)

Notice that
I_{s}
, and consequently
Z_{s}
, are stochastic processes, while the deterministic function
I_{CS}
can be computed using the empirical formula introduced in
[
11

, which is given for a specific day of the year
D

\theta_{z}(s)

I_{CS}(s)=\cos(\theta_{z}(s))^{1.2}\bigg(83.69\sin\left(\frac{2\pi(D+82.07)}{365.24}\right)+1130.44\bigg).

(2)

To model the state vector, a stochastic system is proposed. For the CSI, the mean reverting SDE introduced in
[
11
]
is used, while the electricity price follows a mean-reverting-like SDE as in
[
21

. Finally, a bucket model is adopted to represent the energy stored in the battery. Thus, the state equations are

(3b)

\displaystyle d\mathcal{E}_{s}=

\displaystyle-P_{\text{bat},s}ds+\sigma_{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}}\mathcal{E}_{s}dB_{\mathcal{E},s},

(3c)

\displaystyle Z_{0}=

\displaystyle Z^{0},\quad\Pi_{0}=\Pi^{0},\quad\mathcal{E}_{0}=\mathcal{E}^{0}.

(3d)

In the mathematical problem,
P_{\text{bat},s}
is taken as the control function with the sign convention:
P_{\text{bat},s}
is positive when the battery is discharging and negative when it is charging.

The objective of the stochastic control problem is to maximize the expected revenues of the system, namely,

J(P_{\text{bat},s})=\mathrm{E}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{s}P_{\text{grid},s}\,\mathrm{d}s+g(\mathcal{E}_{T})\Bigg].

(4)

Here,
g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a deterministic function representing the cost of the battery’s energy content at the end of the process. This term ensures that the battery is not completely depleted at the final time, preserving some energy for the start of the next operational period.

The power exchanged with the grid is the sum of the power delivered by the battery and the power produced by the PV system, i.e.,
P_{\text{grid},s}=P_{\text{solar},s}+P_{\text{bat},s}=\hat{P}(I_{s})+~P_{\text{bat},s},
where the power produced by the PV plant can be computed by the deterministic function

\hat{P}(I)=10^{-6}A_{PV}\eta I\frac{\sin(\gamma(s)+\beta)}{\sin\gamma(s)}.

(5)

This function depends on the area of the PV system
A_{\text{PV}}
(m
2
), the efficiency
\eta
(-), the tilt angle of the PV module from the horizontal
\beta
(rad) and the solar inclination angle
\gamma(s)
(rad).

Finally, some constraints must be considered. On the one hand, there are limitations on the power delivered by the battery:
P_{min}\leq P_{\text{bat},s}\leq P_{max}
. On the other hand, the state variable

\mathcal{E}_{s}
is subject to physical constraints, as it must remain within the minimum and maximum capacity limits of the battery, i.e,
\mathcal{E}_{min}\leq\mathcal{E}_{s}\leq\mathcal{E}_{max}
. This state constraint is reformulated as an expectation, that is,
\mathcal{E}_{min}\leq\mathrm{E}[\mathcal{E}_{s}]\leq\mathcal{E}_{max}
.

2.2
General framework

The above problem belongs to the following general stochastic control framework. First,
the state of the system is a vector stochastic process,
\mathbf{X}_{s}^{t,\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
s\in[t,T]
which satisfies the SDE:

\displaystyle d\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}}_{s}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{b}(s,\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}}_{s},\boldsymbol{u}_{s})ds+\mathbf{\Sigma}(s,\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}}_{s})d\mathbf{B}_{s},

(6a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}.

(6b)

Here,
\boldsymbol{u}_{s}
denotes the control of the problem and verifies that
\boldsymbol{u}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}
where

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}=\{\boldsymbol{u}:[t,T]\rightarrow\mathrm{\mathbf{U}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{r}|~\boldsymbol{u}~\text{measurable}\},

being
\mathrm{\mathbf{U}}
a compact set. In the above SDE,
\mathbf{b}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}
are given vector and tensor fields, respectively, while the
d
components of
\mathbf{B}_{s}
are independent standard Brownian motions.

The objective function, also known as cost-to-go, is

J(t,\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{u}_{s})=\mathrm{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}f(s,\mathbf{X}_{s}^{t,\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{u}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s+g(\mathbf{X}_{T}^{t,\mathbf{x}})\right].

(7)

Finally, let
\mathcal{A}
be a convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then the state constraints are formulated as
\mathrm{E}[\mathbf{X}_{s}^{t,\mathbf{x}}]\in\mathcal{A},\,s\in[t,T].

2.3
Stochastic control problem reformulation

It is well-known that, in the absence of state constraints, the preceding stochastic optimal control problem can be solved using the DPP which is equivalent to the HJB equation. However, to handle state constraints it is convenient to reformulate the original problem as a deterministic optimal control problem with the FP equation playing the role of (deterministic) state equation.

First, the FP equation (also known as the forward Kolmogorov equation) is a linear PDE whose solution is the joint probability density function (pdf) of the stochastic process
\mathbf{X}_{s}^{t,\mathbf{x}}
satisfying (
6a
), (
6b
). Denoting the pdf of
\mathbf{X}_{s}^{t,\mathbf{x}}
by
\varphi(s,.):\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow~\varphi(s,\mathbf{y})\in~\mathbb{R},
the function
\varphi
satisfies the following initial-value problem:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial s}(s,\mathbf{y})+\mbox{div}\Big(\mathbf{b}(s,\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{u})\varphi(s,\mathbf{y})\Big)-\frac{1}{2}\mbox{div}\mathbf{div}\Big(\mathbf{\Sigma}(s,\mathbf{y})\mathbf{\Sigma}(s,\mathbf{y})^{\mathtt{T}}\varphi(s,\mathbf{y})\Big)=0,

(8a)

\displaystyle\varphi(t,\mathbf{y})=\varphi_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{y}),\ \mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(8b)

Second, we recall that the HJB equation provides the optimal control in feedback form. In other words, by solving this equation, we obtain in advance a deterministic feedback function that prescribes the optimal control based on the current state of the system:
\boldsymbol{\check{u}}:(s,\mathbf{y})\in[t,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\check{u}}(s,\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{r},
such that the optimal control at time
s
is
\boldsymbol{u}_{s}=\boldsymbol{\check{u}}(s,\mathbf{X}_{s}^{t,\mathbf{x}}).
Notice that, as
\boldsymbol{\check{u}}
is a function of the state of the system, then
\boldsymbol{u}_{s}
is a stochastic process.

This approach is highly convenient for our objectives. Indeed, from the perspective of energy management, once the optimal feedback control
\boldsymbol{\check{u}}
is computed, real-time implementation requires only measuring the current state of the system. On the other hand, if the optimal control is to be used in advance –as in the case of bidding in the day-ahead market– a plausible solution is to use its expected value, i.e,

\boldsymbol{u}(s)=\mathrm{E}[\boldsymbol{u}_{s}]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\boldsymbol{\check{u}}(s,\mathbf{y})\varphi(s,\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}.

(9)

2.4
Deterministic control problem

Now we can reformulate the original stochastic problem with state constraints as a deterministic control problem. To do so, we consider the pdf
\varphi(s,\mathbf{y})
as the state of the system. Accordingly, the state equation becomes the FP equation given by (
8
). Furthermore, we take the deterministic feedback function
\boldsymbol{\check{u}}
as the control.

Regarding the objective function to be maximized, the expectation involved can be computed using
\varphi(s,\mathbf{y})
. Therefore, it is rewritten as

\displaystyle J(\boldsymbol{\check{u}})

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(s,\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\check{u}}(s,\mathbf{y}))\varphi(s,\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(\mathbf{y})\varphi(T,\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}.

(10)

Similarly, state constraints can also be rewritten using
\varphi(s,\mathbf{y})
:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{y}\varphi(s,\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{A}.

(11)

3
Optimality system

This section presents the optimality conditions for the previous deterministic optimal control problem. Some insights into their derivation are provided. For further details, the reader is referred to
[
19
]
where the formal derivation is carried out. It is worth noting that, while in
[
19
]
a standard Lagrangian function is used to handle the state constraints, the Augmented Lagrangian as described, for instance, in
[
22
]
is adopted here. However, the procedure is quite similar in both cases.

By treating the state equation as an additional constraint, the corresponding Lagrange multiplier will be denoted by
v(s,\mathbf{y})
. This multiplier turns to be the
adjoint state
in the
maximum principle
for deterministic optimal control problem. Regarding the state constraints, the notation
\boldsymbol{\mu}(s)
will be used for the Lagrange multiplier associated with (
11
). Thus, we are led to define the Augmented Lagrangian function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{aug}(\boldsymbol{\check{u}},\varphi,v,\boldsymbol{\mu})

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(s,\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\check{u}}(s,\mathbf{y}))\varphi(s,\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(\mathbf{y})\varphi(T,\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v(T,\mathbf{y})\varphi(T,\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v(t,\mathbf{y})\varphi(t,\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle+\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\partial v}{\partial s}(s,\mathbf{y})\varphi(s,\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(s,\mathbf{y})\mathbf{b}(s,\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\check{u}}(s,\mathbf{y}))\cdot\mathrm{grad}\,v(s,\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle-\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{div}\Big(\mathbf{\Sigma}(s,\mathbf{y})\mathbf{\Sigma}(s,\mathbf{y})^{\mathtt{T}}\varphi(s,\mathbf{y})\Big)\cdot\mathrm{grad}\,v(s,\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle-\int_{t}^{T}(\chi_{\mathcal{A}})_{\lambda}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{y}\varphi(s,\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}+\lambda\boldsymbol{\mu}(s)\right)\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_{t}^{T}||\boldsymbol{\mu}(s)||^{2}\mathrm{d}s.

(12)

The last two terms incorporate the state-constraint enforcement via an Augmented Lagrangian framework, using the indicator function of
\mathcal{A}
,
\chi_{\mathcal{A}}
, to ensure feasibility. However, because this indicator function is non-differentiable, we instead replace it with its Moreau-Yosida regularization,
(\chi_{\mathcal{A}})_{\lambda}
, where
\lambda
is a positive regularization parameter (see
[
24
]
for further details).

As shown in
[
19
]
, the first-order partial derivatives of (
12
) yield the optimality conditions of the deterministic optimal control problem, leading to the following optimality system:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial s}(s,\mathbf{y})+\mathrm{div}\Big(\mathbf{b}(s,\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\check{u}}(s,\mathbf{y}))\varphi(s,\mathbf{y})\Big)-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{div}\mathbf{div}\Big(\mathbf{\Sigma}(s,\mathbf{y})\mathbf{\Sigma}(s,\mathbf{y})^{\mathtt{T}}\varphi(s,\mathbf{y})\Big)=0,

(13a)

\displaystyle\varphi(t,\mathbf{y})=\varphi_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{y}),\ \mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(13b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial s}(s,\mathbf{y})+\max_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathrm{\mathbf{U}}}\left\{\mathbf{b}(s,\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{u})\cdot\mathrm{grad}\,v(s,\mathbf{y})+f(s,\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{u})\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 32.72049pt+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Sigma}(s,\mathbf{y})\mathbf{\Sigma}(s,\mathbf{y})^{\mathtt{T}}\cdot\mathbf{grad}\,\mathrm{grad}\,v(s,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\hskip 32.72049pt-(\partial\chi_{\mathcal{A}})_{\lambda}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{y}\varphi(s,\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}+\lambda\boldsymbol{\mu}(s)\right)\cdot\mathbf{y}=0,

(13c)

\displaystyle v(T,\mathbf{y})=g(\mathbf{y}),\ \mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(13d)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}(s)=(\partial\chi_{\mathcal{A}})_{\lambda}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{y}\varphi(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}+\lambda\boldsymbol{\mu}(s)\right),

(13e)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\check{u}}(s,\mathbf{y})=\underset{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathrm{\mathbf{U}}}{\text{arg max}}\big\{\mathbf{b}(s,\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{u})\cdot\mathrm{grad}\,v(s,\mathbf{y})+f(s,\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{u})\big\}.

(13f)

The first optimality conditions (
13a
), (
13b
) are the FP equation. The second optimality conditions correspond to the adjoint state equation, (
13c
), (
13d
), which is a backward nonlinear PDE. However, we emphasize that, for each given control
\mathbf{u}
, it is linear in
v
. Importantly, in the absence of state constraints, i.e., when
\boldsymbol{\mu}(s)=\mathbf{0}
, this equation reduces to the standard HJB equation for the unconstrained stochastic control problem and its solution,
v
, corresponds to the corresponding
value function
. Moreover, in this case, the HJB equation can be solved independently of the FP equation, which is no longer required, thus recovering the standard DPP.

Regarding the third optimality condition (
13e
), it characterizes the Lagrange multiplier
\boldsymbol{\mu}(s)
. Here,
\partial\chi_{\mathcal{A}}
denotes the subdifferential of the indicator function of a set
\mathcal{A}
,
[
25
]
. Its Moreau-Yosida regularization is
(\partial\chi_{\mathcal{A}})_{\lambda}=(\mathbf{y}-\mathrm{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathbf{y}))/\lambda,
being
\mathrm{P}_{\mathcal{A}}
the orthogonal projection on the set
\mathcal{A}
.
Finally, the feedback control
\boldsymbol{\check{u}}
is characterized by (
13f
).

3.1
Numerical solution: Algorithm of multipliers

At this point, a fully coupled optimality system has been obtained. Consequently, its numerical resolution requires, not only appropriate discretizations in time and space, but also an iterative algorithm. In
[
19
]
, a method of multipliers based on a standard Lagrangian function is introduced. Here, we present several improvements aimed at accelerating convergence.

The algorithm consists in two coupled iterations. First, an outer loop handles the state constraints by updating the Lagrange multiplier
\boldsymbol{\mu}(s)
. At iteration
k
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}^{k}(s)

\displaystyle=\rho(\partial\chi_{\mathcal{A}})_{\lambda}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{y}\varphi^{k}(s,\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}+\lambda\boldsymbol{\mu}^{k-1}(s)\right)+(1-\rho)\boldsymbol{\mu}^{k-1}(s),

(14)

where
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
is a relaxation parameter. Second, the inner loop solves an optimal control problem without state constraints.

A key innovation here, relative to
[
19
]
, is the insight that when
\boldsymbol{\mu}(s)=\mathbf{0}
, the inner loop collapses to a pure penalty scheme for the state constraints, with

\lambda
playing the role of the penalty parameter. Our algorithm therefore begins by taking a small number of penalty-only iterations, which drives the control into a favorable ascent direction. Once sufficient progress has been made, we then switch to the standard Augmented Lagrangian method, updating
\boldsymbol{\mu}(s)
and continuing with the combined penalty-multiplier strategy. A schematic summary of the resulting procedure is provided in Algorithm

4
Spatial dimension reduction

The development carried out so far requires solving both FP and HJB equations, each involving as many spatial variables as the dimension of the vector stochastic process defined by the original state equation (
6
). This leads to the well-known
curse of dimensionality
, a term coined by Richard Bellman, which refers to the exponential increase in computational complexity as the number of state variables grows.

Recently, novel approaches have been developed to overcome the curse of dimensionality. In particular, combining artificial neural networks with Monte Carlo methods to express semilinear PDE solutions as expectations of backward SDEs trajectories has shown great promise for solving HJB equations (see
[
26
]
). Although a full treatment of these cutting-edge techniques is outside the scope of this paper, in this section we introduce a simplified variant that still substantially mitigates the dimensionality challenge, while remaining fully consistent with the overarching algorithmic framework presented.

The central idea is to decompose the stochastic process
\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which represents the state of the system, into two separate stochastic processes:
\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{1}}_{1,s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
and
\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{2}}_{2,s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
, where
n_{1}+n_{2}=n
.
That is,
\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}}_{s}=\left(\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{1}}_{1,s},\,\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{2}}_{2,s}\right)^{\mathtt{T}}
. These two components are obtained by solving two independent SDEs, thereby enabling a dimensionality reduction of the original problem.

Assuming that
\mathbf{b}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}
have the structure

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}(s,\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}}_{s},\boldsymbol{u}_{s})

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{b}_{1}(s,\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{1}}_{1,s},\boldsymbol{u}_{s})\\
\mathbf{b}_{2}(s,\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{2}}_{2,s})\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}(s,\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}}_{s})

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}(s,\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{1}}_{1,s})&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2}(s,\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{2}}_{2,s})\end{array}\right),

and that
\mathbf{B}_{s}=\left(\mathbf{B}_{1,s},\,\mathbf{B}_{2,s}\right)^{\mathtt{T}}
, the stochastic processes
\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{1}}_{1,s}
and
\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{2}}_{2,s}
satisfy the two SDEs:

\displaystyle d\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{1}}_{1,s}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{b}_{1}(s,\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{1}}_{1,s},\boldsymbol{u}_{s})ds+\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}(s,\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{1}}_{1,s})d\mathbf{B}_{1,s},

(15a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{1}}_{1,t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}_{1},

(15b)

\displaystyle d\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{2}}_{2,s}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{b}_{2}(s,\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{2}}_{2,s})ds+\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2}(s,\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{2}}_{2,s})d\mathbf{B}_{2,s},

(15c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{2}}_{2,t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}_{2}.

(15d)

In turn, the objective function to be maximized now is:

\displaystyle J(t,\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\boldsymbol{u}_{s})=\mathrm{E}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}f(s,\mathbf{X}_{1,s}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{1}},\mathbf{X}_{2,s}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{2}},\boldsymbol{u}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s+g(\mathbf{X}_{1,T}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{1}},\mathbf{X}_{2,T}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{2}})\Bigg].

(16)

Accordingly, the following general formulation for the state constraints is proposed. Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, then

\mathrm{E}[\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{1}}_{1,s},\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{2}}_{2,s})]\in\mathcal{A},\,s\in[t,T].

(17)

We emphasize that the two SDEs above are enterely uncoupled. In fact, the only interaction between
\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{1}}_{1,s}
and
\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{2}}_{2,s}
arises through the state constraints. Moreover, the SDE governing
\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{2}}_{2,s}
, given by (
15c
), (
15d
), does not involve
\boldsymbol{u}_{s}
, meaning this process cannot be directly influenced by the control; in particular, constraints such as bounds cannot be imposed on
\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{2}}_{2,s}
. Nonetheless, this does not prevent the inclusion of
\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{2}}_{2,s}
in the overall state constraints (
17
), whose satisfaction will depend on how the control affects
\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{1}}_{1,s}
.

Under the preceding assumptions, the stochastic optimal control problem has been equivalently rewritten as a new stochastic optimal control problem for
\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{1}}_{1,s}
and
\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{2}}_{2,s}
. To reformulate it as a deterministic problem and apply the outlined methodology, some considerations must be made regarding the application of the DPP: as control
\boldsymbol{u}_{s}
only appears in the SDE (
15a
), (
15b
) for
\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{1}}_{1,s}
, it is assumed that the stochastic control theory provides an optimal control in feedback form which depends only on this stochastic process. That is, the optimal control at time
s
is
\boldsymbol{u}_{s}=\boldsymbol{\check{u}}(s,\mathbf{X}_{1,s}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{1}}).

Hence,
\mathbf{X}_{1,s}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{1}}
is completely independent of
\mathbf{X}_{2,s}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{2}}
and so the joint pdf of
\mathbf{X}_{s}^{t,\mathbf{x}}
can be factorized as
\varphi(s,\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})=\varphi_{1}(s,\mathbf{y}_{1})\varphi_{2}(s,\mathbf{y}_{2}),\quad\mathbf{y}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}},\mathbf{y}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}},
being
\varphi_{1}(s,\mathbf{y}_{1})
and
\varphi_{2}(s,\mathbf{y}_{2})
the pdf for
\mathbf{X}_{1,s}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{1}}
and
\mathbf{X}_{2,s}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{2}}
, respectively.

It is important to note that simplifying the feedback control impacts the objective function. Specifically, reducing the spatial dimension is not feasible if the optimal feedback control depends on
\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{1}}_{1,s}
and
\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{2}}_{2,s}
. In this scenario, the SDE governing
\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{1}}_{1,s}
would also involve
\mathbf{X}^{t,\mathbf{x}_{2}}_{2,s}
, leading to the pdf
\varphi_{1}
depending on both components. Consequently, the previous decomposition would no longer be valid.

The factorization of
\varphi
also enables the decomposition of the FP equation into two independent equations: one governing the evolution of
\varphi_{1}
and the other corresponding to
\varphi_{2}
, both analogous to (
8a
), (
8b
). In the case of
\varphi_{2}
, the corresponding FP equation can be solved independently and beforehand, as it does not involve the control
\boldsymbol{u}_{s}
and both
\mathbf{b}_{2}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2}
are given.

As a consequence, in order to reformulate the stochastic control problem as a deterministic control problem, we take only
\varphi_{1}(s,\mathbf{y}_{1})
as state variable. Therefore, the state equation is the associated FP equation and the control in the new problem is the feedback function
\boldsymbol{\check{u}}(s,\mathbf{y}_{1})
. The objective function can be rewritten as

\displaystyle J(\boldsymbol{\check{u}})

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{T}\Bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}}f(s,\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2},\boldsymbol{\check{u}}(s,\mathbf{y}_{1}))\varphi_{2}(s,\mathbf{y}_{2})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}_{2}\bigg)\varphi_{1}(s,\mathbf{y}_{1})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}_{1}\Bigg)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}}g(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})\varphi_{2}(T,\mathbf{y}_{2})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}_{2}\right)\varphi_{1}(T,\mathbf{y}_{1})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}_{1},

where the expectation to be maximized is determined using
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
, with the latter being computed in advance. Analogously, state constraints can be expressed as

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}}\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})\varphi_{2}(s,\mathbf{y}_{2})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}_{2}\right)\varphi_{1}(s,\mathbf{y}_{1})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}_{1}\in\mathcal{A}.

Finally, the new optimality conditions can be obtained as follows. Again, their deduction closely follows the approach in
[
19
]
, and we refer the reader to that work for more details.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varphi_{1}}{\partial s}(s,\mathbf{y}_{1})+\mathrm{div}\Big(\mathbf{b}_{1}(s,\mathbf{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{\check{u}}(s,\mathbf{y}_{1}))\varphi_{1}(s,\mathbf{y}_{1})\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{div}\mathbf{div}\Big(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}(s,\mathbf{y}_{1})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}(s,\mathbf{y}_{1})^{\mathtt{T}}\varphi_{1}(s,\mathbf{y}_{1})\Big)=0,

(18a)

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}(t,\mathbf{y}_{1})=\varphi_{1,\mathbf{x}_{1}}(\mathbf{y}_{1}),\ \mathbf{y}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}},

(18b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial s}(s,\mathbf{y}_{1})+\max_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathrm{\mathbf{U}}}\bigg\{\mathbf{b}_{1}(s,\mathbf{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{u})\cdot\mathrm{grad}\,v(s,\mathbf{y}_{1})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}}f(s,\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2},\boldsymbol{u})\varphi_{2}(s,\mathbf{y}_{2})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}_{2}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}(s,\mathbf{y}_{1})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}(s,\mathbf{y}_{1})\mathtt{T}\cdot\mathbf{grad}\mathrm{grad}\,v(s,\mathbf{y}_{1})

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-(\partial\chi_{\mathcal{A}})_{\lambda}\Bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}}\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})\varphi_{2}(s,\mathbf{y}_{2})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}_{2}\bigg)\varphi_{1}(s,\mathbf{y}_{1})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}_{1}+\lambda\boldsymbol{\mu}(s)\Bigg)

\displaystyle\hskip 17.07182pt\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}}\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})\varphi_{2}(s,\mathbf{y}_{2})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}_{2}=0,

(18c)

\displaystyle v(T,\mathbf{y}_{1})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}}g(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})\varphi_{2}(T,\mathbf{y}_{2})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}_{2},\ \mathbf{y}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}},

(18d)

(18e)

\displaystyle\hskip-0.85355pt\boldsymbol{\check{u}}(s,\mathbf{y}_{1})=\underset{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathrm{\mathbf{U}}}{\text{arg max}}\bigg\{\mathbf{b}_{1}(s,\mathbf{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{u})\cdot\mathrm{grad}\,v(s,\mathbf{y}_{1})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}}f(s,\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2},\boldsymbol{u})\varphi_{2}(s,\mathbf{y}_{2})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}_{2}\bigg\}.

(18f)

Summarizing, to solve the optimal control problem, the first step is to compute
\varphi_{2}

5.4
Results

To numerically solve the PV plant optimization problem, both the FP and HJB equations are discretized in time using the implicit Euler scheme, and in space using first-order finite element methods. Depending on the spatial dimension of the problem, interval, triangular, or tetrahedral meshes are employed. Additionally, the computational procedure follows Algorithm
1

The chosen computational domain for the state variables is
(-5,5)\times(-40,220)\times(-30,40)

(0,24)

6
Conclusion

This paper builds on the framework introduced in
[
19
]
for wind farms, extending it to the joint optimization of real-time energy management and day-ahead market participation for PV plants with storage. Key to our approach is the reformulation of the original stochastic optimal control problem as a deterministic one via the FP equation. The resulting optimality system is fully coupled, consisting of a forward FP equation and a backward HJB equation. State constraints are enforced through a Lagrange multiplier that appears naturally in the HJB equation. We then design and implement a specialized numerical algorithm to solve this coupled FP–HJB system efficiently.

A major drawback of this approach is the exponential increase in computational complexity as the state-space dimension grows, the so-called
curse of dimensionality
. To address this, we introduce a dimension-reduction strategy fully compatible with our original framework. We decompose the state vector into two parts, isolating a subset of uncontrollable variables whose marginal distribution can be computed independently. The remaining (controllable) variables then define a reduced-dimension stochastic optimal control problem. We validate this methodology on a realistic PV plant case study, demonstrating that it preserves accurate control performance while offering a tunable trade-off between computational effort and solution quality.

Future work could incorporate additional sources of uncertainty, such as forecasting errors in market prices or demand, and extend the framework to multi-agent or networked energy systems. Another promising avenue is the integration of Monte Carlo methods with machine learning techniques to further alleviate the computational burden in higher-dimensional problems.
