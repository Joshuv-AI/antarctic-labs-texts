High average-flux laser-driven neutron source
Authors: Simon Vallières, François Fillion-Gourdeau, Sylvain Fourmaux, Benjamin Poupart-Raîche, Nils Dietrich, Nicholas F Beier, Ronan Lelièvre, Elias Catrix, Joël Maltais, Amina E Hussein, Patrizio Antici, François Légaré, Steve MacLean
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLaser-driven neutron generation is an attractive alternative to more established methods for compact, short-pulse-duration neutron sources with applications in medical science, material science and imaging. Despite extensive investigation of various techniques, achieving performance comparable to nuclear reactors or conventional accelerators remains challenging. In this work, we generate a stable, high-repetition-rate laser-driven neutron source reaching a record average flux of 7.8 × 107 n/sr/s, improving on other existing laser-based sources by more than one order of magnitude. Our approach is based on a two-step process where electrons are accelerated to relativistic energies via laser wakefield acceleration (LWFA), and subsequently generate neutrons through Bremsstrahlung emission followed by photonuclear reactions in a tungsten converter. Experimental results, supported by Monte Carlo simulations, show a neutron flux of 3.0 × 107 n/cm2/s near the target, on par with some compact accelerator-based neutron sources. Additionally, a direct comparison with the target-normal sheath acceleration (TNSA) pitcher-catcher scheme, performed on the same laser system, reveals a significantly higher total neutron yield of 3.9 × 108 neutrons per shot, outperforming the TNSA scheme by several orders of magnitude. These findings represent a significant advancement towards the development of practical laser-driven neutron sources and highlight the advantages of LWFA-based neutron generation for future applications.Subject terms: Experimental nuclear physics, Laser-produced plasmas, High-field lasers, Plasma-based accelerators

Introduction

Due to their highly penetrating characteristics, fast neutrons play a vital role across a wide range of scientific and industrial applications1–3. They enable non-destructive imaging4–6, radiation stress-test of semiconductor devices7, and allow for the detection of explosives or fissile materials8. Moreover, neutron sources are also used in medicine, for example, in cancer treatment using Boron Neutron Capture Therapy9, and in the field of cultural heritage10.

Conventional neutron sources are primarily based on fission reactions in nuclear reactors11,12 or spallation reactions in particle accelerators13–15. While these sources can generate high neutron fluxes (~1014–1017 n/cm2/s14), they rely on large and costly infrastructures that require substantial resources16. Compact neutron sources based on deuterium-deuterium (d,d) and deuterium-tritium (d,t) fusion are also commercially available, generating monoenergetic beams with yields up to ~1010 n/s17,18. These sources have small footprints and relatively low cost, making them attractive for many applications. Recent advancements in high-intensity laser technologies and laser-driven particle acceleration present a promising alternative to conventional approaches, offering a more compact and lightweight solution19–22. In addition, those laser-driven neutron sources (LDNS) generally produce short neutron bunches with high peak brilliance suitable for time-resolved applications.

Many variants of LDNS have been developed, with varying levels of complexity and efficiency. Early attempts included laser-induced nuclear reactions in bulk materials by initiating the (d,d) fusion reaction or the deuteron-induced neutron emission (d,n), either by inertial confinement fusion12, by fast ignition23, or by focusing a high-intensity laser in deuterated targets24–29. Over the years, these techniques have been significantly improved through precise fine-tuning of the target and the laser source, to the point where neutron yields of approximately ~1010 neutrons per shot have been obtained with less than 1 kJ lasers30,31. Recently, fusion ignition was achieved using indirect drive inertial confinement implosion and provided up to 1.12 × 1018 neutrons per shot32. Despite their success, these techniques are constrained to large laser facilities working in single-shot mode with low repetition rate lasers ( ~1 shot per hour or more). For this reason, other production schemes have been proposed and investigated, notably using secondary targets.

One of the main competing strategies is the “pitcher-catcher” scheme, in which a high-intensity laser is focused onto a thin material foil (the pitcher). Depending on the properties of this first target and the laser parameters, high-energy ions are generated through mechanisms such as target-normal sheath acceleration (TNSA)33, radiation pressure acceleration34, or others35,36. These ions are then directed onto a secondary target (the catcher, typically made of lithium or beryllium), where they generate nuclear reactions such as proton-induced neutron emission (p,n). The pitcher-catcher scheme has demonstrated promising performance in terms of neutron yield per shot due to the high interaction cross-section of protons with light nuclei21,30,37–47. However, its typical principal limitations include a low repetition rate (below 0.01 Hz) and a limited number of shots per cycle (fewer than ~1000) due to the renewal of the solid-state target pitcher at each shot48. Although these could be circumvented by using liquid jet targets, which allow for high-repetition-rate kHz neutron sources in the single target bulk material method49, they have not been implemented yet for neutron production with ion acceleration.

Another possible avenue for LDNS is to use relativistic electrons rather than ions in the primary step. The electrons, accelerated to high kinetic energy by the laser fields and plasma interactions, collide with a high-Z target converter. Inside the target, electrons are slowed down and emit gamma-rays through Bremsstrahlung. These gamma-rays then interact with the atomic nuclei and undergo (γ, xn) photonuclear reactions, dominated by the Giant Dipole Resonance (at around 15 MeV of photon energy for typical converter materials like tungsten), thereby releasing fast neutrons with ~1 MeV of kinetic energy. For this technique to be efficient and to maximize the neutron yield, it is essential to produce high-energy electrons in the acceleration stage as the electron-to-neutron conversion rate scales linearly with electron energy, at least up to 1 GeV50. There exist several alternatives to achieve this, such as direct electron acceleration, which have shown encouraging results for LDNS51,52. However, one of the most effective acceleration mechanisms is laser wakefield acceleration (LWFA)53. The bubble regime that can produce high-quality beams was first demonstrated experimentally in 200454–56. LWFA now enables electrons in the GeV range57–59. In the rest of this article, we shall call this scheme LWFA-based neutron generation, whereby electrons are accelerated through LWFA and converted to neutrons in a high-Z secondary target. Theoretically, this approach is expected to deliver high conversion efficiency and flux, benefiting from a large cross-section and the capability to operate at higher repetition rates50,60. There have been some experimental investigations with LWFA-based in single-shot mode or at low repetition rates (around 0.1 Hz), demonstrating some capabilities of this technique61–64.

In this work, we demonstrate that under our experimental conditions and by leveraging the LWFA beamline at the Advanced Laser Light Source (ALLS), LWFA-based neutron generation can produce more than 108 neutrons per shot at a repetition rate of 2.5 Hz. Furthermore, we also compare our results to the pitcher-catcher TNSA-based neutron generation scheme, which was performed on the same system using identical laser energy and pulse duration47. Finally, we compare with other existing LDNS. This allows us to highlight the superior average flux and stability of the LWFA-based technique, making this method more appealing for practical applications.

Results

Experimental setup for LWFA-based neutron generation at ALLS

The experiment was conducted on the LWFA beamline of the Advanced Laser Light Source (ALLS), a national laser facility at the Institut national de la recherche scientifique (INRS) in Varennes, Québec, Canada. This experimental endstation was originally developed to investigate phase-contrast imaging using betatron X-rays emitted from LWFA electrons65. The experimental setup, displayed in Fig. 1, is composed of a 150 TW Ti:Sapphire laser system operating at 2.5 Hz (see section “ALLS 150 TW LWFA beamline” for full details). The laser beam is focused down to reach a peak intensity of 4.0 × 1019 W/cm2 in a 5 mm-long gas cell, where electrons are accelerated via LWFA. A 3.5 cm-thick tungsten block is placed in the electron beam path to act as an electron-to-neutron converter at a distance of 20 cm between the gas cell and the block’s back surface (referred to as the neutron source plane). Previous Monte Carlo calculations have demonstrated that a target thickness of 3.5 cm is nearly optimal for maximizing the neutron yield with a few hundred MeV electrons, as increasing the thickness only provides marginal gains42,50.

Neutron fluences are measured using bubble detectors (BD) from BTI. The BD-PND type is sensitive to the passage of neutrons in the energy range from 0.2 to 15 MeV (fast neutrons), while being much less sensitive to other particles such as gamma-rays and electrons66,67. BD were positioned on the vacuum chamber walls at distances ranging from 0.42 to 2 m from the neutron source plane, and different polar angles θ with respect to the optical axis and the neutron source plane, as depicted in Fig. 1. Additionally, a Victoreen 190N neutron dosimeter from Fluke Biomedical, calibrated in absolute terms at the primary radiation standards of Canada using an AmBe source68, was used as a neutron fluence cross-validation diagnostic. This 190N detector, insensitive to gamma-rays ( <0.1% of measured dose), was placed 1.23 m away from the neutron source plane at an angle of ~10° with respect to the optical axis. Finally, a neutron Time-of-Flight (nToF) detector was located 4.0 m from the neutron source plane, heavily shielded by 25 cm of lead to attenuate the gamma-ray burst hitting the detector before the neutrons. In an alternative experimental configuration (see dashed objects in Fig. 1), a pair of permanent magnets is moved in the electron beam path in order to deflect the electrons into a spectrometer, while the tungsten block is also moved out of the optical axis, using translation stages.

Electron spectrum

In the first part of the experiment, the spectrum of electrons accelerated through LWFA is measured using the spectrometer described in the previous section. Our two-magnet configuration allows us to scan a large range of energies, from 25 MeV up to 1 GeV (see “Methods”). A typical measured electron spectrum, averaged on 10 shots, is shown in Fig. 2a with 30% charge uncertainty (red error bars). The mean electron energy is 100 MeV (94 MeV at 1/e2 of the peak charge), with the electron signal going up to ~400 MeV. The electron spectrum is exponentially decreasing at large energies, likely because of continuous ionization injection of electrons inside the plasma wakefield and laser beam imperfections69. There is also an important population of low-energy electrons below 100 MeV, signaling the presence of sub-optimal electron injection. Even if the latter are at lower energies, they have a non-negligible contribution to neutron production. The total charge is 811 ± 240 pC integrated over the whole spectrum, corresponding to (5.1 ± 1.5) × 109 electrons/shot, while the charge is 145 pC for high-energy electrons with Ee>140 MeV. The resulting beam divergence is estimated to be of Δθe−≈10 mrad at Half-Width Half-Maximum (HWHM), determined by the width of the electron trace on the lanex phosphor.

Neutron angular and energy distributions

In the next part of this work, the neutron angular distribution is measured and compared to numerical simulations. The experimental spectrum and divergence of the electron beam determined in section “Electron spectrum” are used as inputs in the Monte Carlo code Geant470,71 (see “Methods”). The simulated and measured neutron angular distributions dNndΩ(θ) are presented in Fig. 2b, showing neutron yields on the order of 3 × 107 neutrons/sr. The simulated angular distribution (full blue curve) is filtered in the energy range of (0.2 − 15) MeV to better compare with the BD measurements. The uncertainty on the theoretical curve corresponds to the uncertainty on the total charge, used to normalize the distribution. The simulated total neutron yield in this particular energy range is ntot = 0.072 neutrons per primary electron, resulting in (3.6 ± 1.1) × 108 neutrons/shot using the mean electron number estimation of (5.1 ± 1.5) × 109 electrons/shot. BD measurements (red dots), with experimental uncertainties ranging from 14% at 0° to 47% at 180°, show an excellent agreement with the simulated distribution for both shape and total yield of (3.9 ± 1.0) × 108 neutrons/shot. The experimental and simulation results are consistent within their uncertainties, even if neutron scattering on the aluminum vacuum chamber and the optical components inside can distort the angular distribution. Interestingly, a strong backward neutron fluence at 180° is noticeable, due to the large thickness of the tungsten block. As tungsten is a material with significant (n,xn) scattering cross sections and the photonuclear production process occurs within the first half of the converter with a quasi-isotropic emission angular distribution, a non-negligible proportion of neutrons is emitted with backward momentum72. The 190N detector reports a neutron yield per unit solid angle of 2.0 × 107 neutrons/sr, in agreement with the other results.

Figure 2c shows a series of 100 shots retrieved on the nToF detector at a repetition rate of 2.5 Hz. The major part of the neutron voltage signal is located around 1.8 MeV, with a continuously decreasing signal tail towards higher neutron energies. The measurement of higher neutron energies is limited by the distance from the source and the shielding of the gamma-ray burst saturating the detector. The implementation of a time-gated acquisition, to blind the nToF detector from the gamma peak, will be the subject of future investigations. This series of measurements also shows the shot-to-shot stability of the source (inlet of Fig. 2c), with a mean fluctuation of 33% over the whole neutron spectrum. Most importantly, this highlights its long-term stability, at least up to several minutes of acquisition time, which enables applications requiring shot accumulation. This is in stark contrast with most LDNS, which use a single-shot mode.

The simulated neutron spectrum is displayed in Fig. 2d, along with the spectrum of TNSA-based neutrons—a detailed comparison of both processes will be discussed in the next section. The LWFA-based spectrum has an emission peak around 450 keV, a mean energy of 1.0 MeV, and a total neutron yield of (3.9 ± 1.2) × 108 neutrons/shot (0.077 neutrons per primary electron). Furthermore, its shape is qualitatively consistent with the measured spectrum from the experiment, displayed in 2c, even though units are different. We emphasize that although the neutron spectrum is calculated, we expect it accurately represents the real energy distribution in the experiment. This is because it includes experimental inputs from the electron source, and because the simulations have been validated through the spatial distribution, as shown in Fig. 2b. Thus, the LWFA-based neutron source emits mostly fast neutrons, in the MeV range, with a high-energy cutoff around 11 MeV. These features of the neutron spectrum will be important for assessing the relevance of this source to some given applications.

Finally, the total yield and flux of the LWFA-based neutron source are reported in Table 1. Our experimental observations, combined with Geant4 calculations (see “Methods”) are showing that the maximum available fluence obtained at the neutron source plane reaches 1.2 × 107 neutrons/cm2. This provides a maximum average flux of 3.0 × 107 neutrons/cm2/s and a peak flux of 1.0 × 1017 neutrons/cm2/s, suitable for neutron beam applications. This is also comparable to some compact accelerator-driven neutron sources (CANS), where fluxes range from 102 to 1011 n/cm2/s, with several facilities operating in the 105 to 107 n/cm2/s range14. Commercial compact neutron generators like the one from Adelphi Technology can reach a thermal neutron flux of (3–5) × 107 n/cm2/s18. However, CANS neutron beams are usually more easily energy-tunable and monoenergetic compared to LDNS. Furthermore, the quoted flux value often considers thermal neutrons, which have already been moderated, while the LWFA-based flux is for fast neutrons. Controlling the beam characteristics of LDNS remains a challenge to be investigated in future work.

Comparison of LWFA- and TNSA-based neutron generation on the same laser system

The LWFA-based neutron generation source is now compared to the TNSA-based pitcher-catcher scheme. The latter was investigated experimentally in a previous publication using the same laser and identical laser pulse parameters, albeit performed at a repetition rate of 0.5 Hz47. The repetition rate of the TNSA experiment is essentially limited by the primary target refreshment speed. Protons were accelerated by TNSA and sent on a 1 mm-thick lithium fluoride (LiF) secondary target to induce (p,n) nuclear reactions. As in the LWFA-based analysis, the proton spectrum (Maxwellian spectrum with high-energy cutoff at ~7 MeV) and beam divergence (20° conical divergence) were measured experimentally and used as inputs for a Geant4 simulation (see the work of Lelièvre et al.47 for full details). The numerical results were then validated by comparing with the measured neutron yield. The corresponding neutron spectrum is displayed in Fig. 2d (blue curve), with 20% experimental uncertainty on the proton fluence.

To test the numerical predictions in Scheuren et al.50, this TNSA-based neutron spectrum is compared with that of the LWFA-based neutron generation method. The simulated TNSA-based neutron spectrum yields a total of (5.3 ± 1.1) × 105 neutrons/shot (1.8 × 10−5 neutrons per primary proton). In terms of energy conversion efficiency, the laser-to-proton gives about 0.2% and 2 × 10−6% for laser-to-neutron in the TNSA case, whereas for LWFA, we obtain 2.6% for laser-to-electron and 2 × 10−3% for laser-to-neutron. The combination of our experimental results and validated numerical calculations shows that the total number of LWFA-based neutrons is approximately 730× larger than TNSA-based. When comparing the normalized neutron yield per primary particle, the LWFA-based method rises to 4300× higher, consistent with the calculations of Scheuren et al.50, which predict a nearly two orders-of-magnitude higher yield. The remaining discrepancy between Scheuren et al.’s results and this work comes from the substantially higher proton cutoff energy (75 MeV)50, which enhances the proton-driven neutron yield compared to the 7 MeV cutoff in Lelièvre et al.47, and therefore strongly reduces their LWFA-to-TNSA ratio.

As correctly mentioned in Scheuren et al.50, the significantly higher neutron yield of the LWFA-based method cannot be solely explained by the cross-section maximum values. For the 7Li(p,n)7Be reaction (the most important one in LiF), the cross-section reaches ~580 mb, whereas for photonuclear reactions 184W(γ,xn), the cross-section goes up to 510 mb at the Giant Dipole Resonance73. Therefore, there is no major difference between the two processes in this aspect. To shed light on the three orders-of-magnitude higher yield of the LWFA-based method, we estimate the neutron yield Nn using the thin target approximation (see Methods for full details). Evaluating this expression numerically for our experimental configurations gives a normalized neutron yield ratio of (NnLWFA/Ne)/(NnTNSA/Np) = 910× , deviating by only a factor of ~5 from the experimental value of 4300× . To explain this discrepancy, it should be emphasized that equation (1) significantly overestimates the neutron yield from protons, thereby lowering the ratio. This occurs because the proton spectrum changes drastically over the range Δz, and this is not taken into account by the thin target approximation, whereas the photon spectrum remains more stable over the photon mean free path. Furthermore, it is clear that equation (1) neglects most parts of the particle cascade, in contrast to full-fledged Monte Carlo simulations. In this sense, equation (1) slightly underestimates the neutron yield for the LWFA-based method. Despite these limitations, the thin target approximation gives a reasonably accurate order-of-magnitude estimate of the neutron yield.

Based on the consistency of this estimate with experimental observations, we conclude that the large ratio stems from two main factors. First, the overlap integral in Eq. (1) is more than twice as high for LWFA-based due to the large number of photons emitted per electron (14.7 γ/e− in the present case) and because the 184W(γ,xn) cross-section is much more broadband. The latter is explained by the contribution of multiple neutrons emitted per photon events. Second, the penetration depth of protons in LiF is much lower than that for photons in W, which strongly reduces the effective atomic areal density (i.e., the number of possible interactions) for proton beams in LiF. The second factor turns out to be the dominant one for explaining the large yield ratio.

The comparison between LWFA- and TNSA-based can be extended further by looking at other beam characteristics, as presented in Table 1. Because the repetition rate of the LWFA experiment is 2.5 Hz, the total neutron yield per second nearly reaches 109 neutrons/s emitted over 4π steradian, about 40.000× higher than the TNSA-scheme performed on the same laser at 0.5 Hz. Although there now exist stable laser-based proton beams driven up to 5 Hz74, their energy spectra are peaked at relatively lower energies. Consequently, it is expected that a significant proportion of protons will be produced below the threshold energy of 1.9 MeV for the 7Li(p,n)7Be reaction. Compounded with the more narrowband cross-section, this will hinder high average flux neutron generation using laser-driven proton beams. Another option is to generate protons with larger energies from higher-power lasers. However, this typically drastically decreases the repetition rate, below the Hz-level. A further limitation for pitcher-catcher experiments is the pitcher target refreshment, as it is more difficult to increase the repetition rate of an experiment using solid targets, in order to reach the nominal repetition rate of the laser (0.5 Hz for the TNSA case here, for instance, whereas the laser operates at 2.5 Hz), rather than gas targets. Even if the repetition rate of new high-power laser systems rises, it is worth noting that this aspect can severely limit the average neutron flux for pitcher-catcher configurations. It is also possible to compare the maximum usable average neutron brilliance between both sources, which is the relevant metric to evaluate for most applications. It is obtained from the average flux at the back surface of each converter target divided by the neutron beam solid angle divergence, giving (3.0 × 107)/4π = 2.4 × 106 n/cm2/s/sr for the LWFA-based source and (3.0×104)/2π1−cos70∘=7.3×103 n/cm2/s/sr for the TNSA-based source. Hence, we see that even though the TNSA converter is thinner and that the resulting neutron beam divergence is narrower, this does not compensate for the much higher flux provided by the LWFA-based neutron source.

The results in this section demonstrate that the LWFA-based method is a promising approach for generating neutron sources with higher average fluxes than those produced by TNSA-based methods. However, the neutron energy in LWFA-based sources cannot be easily tuned or scaled up using (γ,xn) reactions, as these rely on resonances with limited momentum transfer to the nucleus, unlike (p,n) reactions. In TNSA-based sources, neutron energy can be directly controlled by the incoming proton energy, whereas in LWFA-based sources, achieving energy control requires modifying the material converter, which may compromise the flux. Finally, we would like to emphasize that this work is the first experimental comparison of LWFA- and TNSA-based neutron generation using the same laser system, hence there are still many features to clarify in further detailed studies.

The neutron yield per shot and per unit solid angle in this work reaches a moderate value of 3.1 × 107 n/sr, three orders of magnitude below Yogo et al.31 at 2.4 × 1010 n/sr. Some experiments used more energy per pulse (280× in the case of Yogo et al.) and solid primary targets, which give high primary particle yields. A subset of these experiments was able to reach higher yields than the LWFA-based scheme, in the range 108–109 n/sr, thereby positioning the LWFA-based neutron source in the middle tier for the total yield per shot.

In terms of yield efficiency, the source in this work achieves 9.7 × 106 n/sr/J. While it is not the most efficient LDNS per Joule of laser energy, it is only one order of magnitude below the leading experiment from Günther et al.21, at 2.5 × 108 n/sr/J. In comparison to all LDNS, this work occupies a competitive but not leading position. Nevertheless, it demonstrates the highest yield efficiency reported for laser systems operating below 10 Joules, comparable to the results obtained by Zulick et al.40.

Regarding the average neutron flux, the present source reaches a value of 7.8 × 107 n/sr/s, currently the highest reported to date for LDNS with lasers below 1 kJ. This is 17× more than Yogo et al.31, for which the experiment was performed in single-shot mode, at 4.6 × 106 n/sr/s (extrapolated value from the laser system’s repetition rate shown in Table 2). Compared to other high-repetition-rate experiments (i.e., without asterix on the repetition rate in Table 2), the present source has an average flux 118× greater than the next highest source, Arikawa et al.75 at 6.8 × 105 n/sr/s. The high performance of the present neutron source emerges from the 2.5 Hz capability of our LWFA system and from the relatively efficient electron acceleration mechanism, whereby the electron beam gains high kinetic energies and high charge within the plasma wave driven by the laser pulse. Our LWFA setup yields almost a nanocoulomb of 100 MeV electrons which, altogether, provides a high laser-to-electron energy conversion efficiency Ce of approximately 2.6%. As shown theoretically in Scheuren et al.50, the neutron yield per primary electron follows a linear relationship with electron energy, and this is further validated and extended here to electron energies up to 10 GeV (see Fig. 3b in section “Monte Carlo simulations”). The average neutron flux can then be estimated as N¯°n=aP¯Ce, where a is the slope of the linear relationship between neutron yield and electron kinetic energy and P¯ is the laser average power (see section “Monte Carlo simulations” for the full derivation). Hence, we can conclude that both the significant laser average power delivered in the experiment and the high laser-to-electron energy conversion efficiency (high electron energy and high charge) were determining factors for maximizing the average neutron flux.

On one hand, using kHz systems with a higher repetition rate but lower laser energy per shot restrains neutron emission per primary particle, as the accelerated particles reach lower kinetic energies. On the other hand, the repetition rate of higher peak power lasers is limited, despite the efficient energy conversion provided by higher particle energies. In light of these factors, it appears that Joule-level 100 Hz systems will play a critical role for high average-flux LDNS and short-pulse neutron beam applications in the near future. They offer a balanced compromise between high electron energies through LWFA and a sufficiently high repetition rate for acquisitions.

Discussion

In this article, an LWFA-based neutron source was developed and characterized. The combination of experimental and numerical results presented in the last sections unequivocally demonstrates that a short duration, high average neutron flux can be reached with this scheme. It was shown that with fixed laser parameters, LWFA-based provides better neutron beam characteristics than TNSA-based neutron sources. With an average flux approaching a level of 109 n/s for the first time using an LDNS, numerous applications become foreseeable in the near future, such as neutron imaging for rapid prototyping, welding inspections, concrete structure assessments, explosive device testing, quality assurance of devices, radiation hardening evaluations, as well as nuclear fuel inspections. Given the maximum average flux at the neutron source plane of 3 × 107 n/cm2/s (see Fig. 3), fast neutron radiographic images are definitely conceivable using this source, as they are typically performed with fluences in the 107−8 n/cm2 range76–78. At 50 cm from this source, where a neutron camera could be positioned, the approximate average flux is still > 1 × 105 n/cm2/s on the detector plane. At this rate, an image can be acquired with only a few minutes of acquisition time. The broadband neutron spectrum can be used for hyperspectral neutron imaging with a time-gated camera in Time-of-Flight configuration, a technique that cannot be performed with current monoenergetic neutron generator technology. However, in order to perform imaging at lower energies with thermal neutrons, a moderator is required. The latter will strongly reduce the flux and hence will possibly hinder this kind of application at the present time, even though acquisitions over tens of minutes are possible. More importantly, time-resolved applications necessitating short neutron bunch durations (~100 ps at the source, ns-ranged at the imaging plane) are now enabled. The temporal resolution would be higher than CANS or commercially available neutron generators, which are operated in continuous or longer-bunch modes. In contrast, the LWFA-based sources can be used in a pump-probe configuration into which a perturbation, such as a laser beam, an electrical discharge, heating, etc., is applied to a sample, which is then imaged by the neutron probe at a particular delay with respect to the perturbation (the pump). The robustness of the neutron pump-probe technique depends on the existence of a repeatable neutron source with low shot-to-shot fluctuations, and, as demonstrated in this work, our LWFA-based source meets this criterion. Furthermore, as this method relies on strong Bremsstrahlung photon production, a hard X-ray beam can be obtained with minor modifications to the experimental setup. One can then potentially benefit from time-resolved hard X-ray and neutron concomitant imaging, where each beam provides high-Z and low-Z contrasts of the same sample, similar to the method proposed in Treffert et al.79. To the best of our knowledge, this cannot be achieved with most CANS or commercial neutron generators, as they are based on different physical processes. Thus, the LWFA-based technique could find application in homeland security and will be tested further in the future.

As imaging imposes stringent constraints on fluence at the detector plane to obtain acceptable image quality, it is clear that other applications of LDNS are more easily achievable, such as neutron resonance spectroscopy, neutron activation analysis, in particular for container scans in ports and luggage checks of fissile materials in airports8, as well as radioisotope production. More specifically, the production of Mo-99 (the most used radioisotope for healthcare) from the 100Mo(γ,n)99Mo reaction80, with 100Mo as converter target for the LWFA electrons, can be foreseen with few-Joules high-power lasers and increasing repetition rate. Such a system would provide efficient conversion to neutrons from hundreds of MeV electrons, while the high-repetition rate would result in high average flux.

A high average neutron flux is required to enable applications. It is shown in this work that a 3.2 J and 2.5 Hz laser system is an attractive option to obtain a high average neutron flux using LWFA electrons. Currently, 150 TW laser systems are commercially available at 10 Hz. Thus, a factor 10 increase in average flux could easily be obtained with an optimized gas cell. This unlocks several industrial applications as was discussed in Zimmer et al.2. In the near future, considering a high laser-to-electron energy conversion efficiency scenario of 30% using a 400 TW laser system as in the work of Rockafellow et al.81, we can estimate an average neutron flux increase 85 times higher, reaching 260 times higher (or 7.8 × 109 n/cm2/s based on the current average flux show in Table 1) when considering 10 Hz systems currently under development82. Increasing the repetition rate of Joule-level high-power laser systems up to 100 Hz is also the object of active investigation and would increase the average neutron flux even more in the 1011 n/s range. Reaching such a neutron flux will open applications that should push laser companies to develop turn-key systems, as well as reducing their size and cost, to reach more customers outside the academic market. The laser systems with the required specifications are available commercially, and several existing companies have the know-how to design the laser-matter interaction module of this LDNS to build integrated solutions. Moreover, machine learning developments in complex laser-plasma processes will help to reach operation automation. Finally, the current development of Tm:YLF lasers, which are expected to provide 100 J at the kHz rate, can indeed prove transformative for laser-driven particle sources. They would emerge as highly efficient, compact, and low-cost laser systems83, potentially providing four orders of magnitude more neutron flux in the 1013 n/s range, bringing LDNS to the higher end of all accelerator-based sources. This would open the avenue for new laser-based disruptive technologies.

Methods

ALLS 150 TW LWFA beamline

The experimental setup uses the ALLS 150 TW Ti:Sapphire laser system with a central wavelength of 800 nm, delivering 3.2 J on target in 22 fs at a repetition rate of 2.5 Hz. The laser beam is focused using a 1.5 m focal length off-axis parabola (f/15), yielding a spot size of 15 μm at Full-Width Half-Maximum (FWHM). In order to properly optimize the laser beam, the laser pulse wavefront is measured after focusing by an imaging system at the laser's full energy (3.2 J) using wedges to decrease the laser fluence on the optics and the wavefront sensor (Phasics SID4). The laser pulse wavefront aberrations are thus corrected at full laser power (including thermal effects at 2.5 Hz) using a bimorph piezoelectric deformable mirror (by AKAOptics). A peak intensity of 4.0±0.4×1019 W/cm2 is achieved after focusing in a 5 mm-long gas cell, where electrons are accelerated via LWFA. The gas mixture consists of 99.5% helium and 0.5% nitrogen, maintained at a pressure corresponding to an electron density of about ~6 × 1018 cm−3. A 3.5 cm-thick tungsten block is placed in the electron beam path to act as an electron-to-neutron converter at a distance of 20 cm between the gas cell and the block’s back surface (referred to as the neutron source plane).

The electron spectrometer is made up of two 1 T magnets positioned along the optical axis and a lanex Fine screen imaged by a CCD camera. The photons created by the phosphor and collected by the camera exit the vacuum interaction vessel through a glass plate. They are then reflected by a high reflectivity dielectric mirror at 546 nm, they go through an interferometric filter (10 nm bandwidth centered at 546 nm), and are collected by a 50 mm focal length camera objective. From the measured magnetic field map of the two magnets, the electron energy dispersion dEdSN along the surface of the phosphor screen can be calculated. nToF measurements

A neutron Time-of-Flight (nToF) detector was placed at a distance d = 4.0 m from the neutron source plane, heavily shielded by 25 cm of lead to attenuate the gamma-ray burst hitting the detector before the neutrons. Monte Carlo simulations

In this section, details on the Geant4 (version 11.1.2) numerical simulations are given. For all calculations performed in this article, the QGSP_BIC_AllHP physics list, which contains all considered physical interactions and cross sections, is adopted owing to its high accuracy for electromagnetic processes and neutron transport over a wide range of kinetic energies. The simulations include an electron source (implemented via the general particle source function in Geant4) characterized by the experimental electron spectrum in Fig. 2a, as well as the experimental cone beam divergence of Δθe−=10 mrad. In a single calculation, 109 electrons are sent on the 5.08 × 5.08 × 3.5 cm3 tungsten block. Neutron numbers are scored on a sphere centered at the back of the tungsten target with a radius of R = 65 cm, corresponding to the approximate position of bubble detectors on the vacuum chamber. In the experiment, the BD are actually positioned at varying distances from the back surface of the tungsten target, ranging from 45 cm to 200 cm, due to the target offset from the center of the vacuum chamber. Changing the detection sphere’s radius in the simulations confirmed that within this range, the angular distribution is modified by less than 5%.

The upper bound on the average flux is also estimated via Geant4 using the same simulation configuration.
