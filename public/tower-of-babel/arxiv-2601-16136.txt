Abstract
. We show the failure of the pointwise convergence of averages along the Omega function in a number field. As a consequence, we show, for instance, that the averages 1 N 2 ​ ∑ 1 ≤ m , n ≤ N f ⁡ ( T Ω ⁡ ( m 2 + n 2 ) ​ x ) \frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{1\leq m,n\leq N}f(T^{\Omega(m^{2}+n^{2})}x) do not converge pointwise in ergodic systems, addressing a question posed by Le, Moreira, Sun, and the second author. On the other hand, using number-theoretic methods, we establish the pointwise convergence of averages along the Ω \Omega function defined on the ideals of a number field in uniquely ergodic systems. Using this dynamical framework, we also derive several natural number-theoretic consequences of independent interest.

1.
Introduction

It is well-known that the prime number theorem can be rephrased in terms of averages of the Liouville function. More precisely, the prime number theorem is equivalent to

(1)

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda(k)=o(1)_{N\to\infty}.

Here, the Liouville function
\lambda(n)\colon\mathbb{N}\to\{-1,1\}
is the completely multiplicative function (that is,
\lambda(n\cdot m)=\lambda(n)\cdot\lambda(m)
for
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
) defined by
\lambda(p)=-1
for all primes
p
.
Rewriting
\lambda
as
\lambda(n)=(-1)^{\Omega(n)}
, where
\Omega(n)
counts the prime factors of
n
(counting repeated factors with multiplicity), (
1
) is equivalent to

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(-1)^{\Omega(k)}=o(1)_{N\to\infty},

which suggests an ergodic-theoretic approach to the problem. This is indeed what Bergelson and Richter did in their breakthrough work
[
2
]
, where they proposed and proved the following more general dynamical statement.

Theorem 1

(
[
2
]
)
.

Let
(X,T)
be a uniquely ergodic system and let
\mu
be its unique invariant measure. Then, for any
f\in C(X)
and any
x\in X

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(T^{\Omega(n)}x)=\int_{X}fd\mu.

An application of this result to a
2
-point rotation system allows us to recover the prime number theorem. Using uniquely ergodic systems such as irrational rotations, certain symbolic systems, and nilsystems, allows us to obtain far-reaching, number-theoretic generalizations of this theorem (see
[
2
]
for further discussions).

It is natural to ask about norm and/or pointwise convergence of averages as in
Theorem

1
under no unique ergodicity of the system or a non-continuous function
f
. Loyd answered these questions in
[
14
]
, where she proved that the convergence in norm still holds, but the pointwise convergence fails for an extensive class of dynamical systems.

Theorem 2

(
[
14
]
)
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{X},T,\mu)
be an ergodic invertible m.p.s. where the measure
\mu
is non-atomic (
\mu(\{x\})=0
for all
x\in X
). Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a measurable set
A
such that
\mu(A)<\varepsilon
and for
\mu
-almost every
x\in X
, we have

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}_{A}(T^{\Omega(n)}x)=1\quad\text{ and }\quad\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}_{A}(T^{\Omega(n)}x)=0.

This type of behavior is commonly referred to in the literature as the
strong sweeping-out property
.

1.1.
Extensions to other number fields

Recently, Donoso, Le, Moreira, and Sun proved in
[
8
]
an analogous result to that of Bergelson and Richter, in the context of Gaussian integers.
As a consequence of a stronger “dynamical theorem”
[
8
, Theorem E]
, they obtained the convergence along the
\Omega(\cdot)
function of the norm of Gaussian integers.

Theorem 3
.

[
8
, Theorem D]

Let
(X,T)
be a uniquely ergodic system with the unique invariant measure
\mu
. Then for any
x\in X
and any
f\in C(X)
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{1\leq m,n\leq N}f\left(T^{\Omega(m^{2}+n^{2})}x\right)=\int_{X}f\,d\mu.

In the same paper, they raised questions in the spirit of Loyd’s result about the convergence of the averages in
Theorem

3
for general ergodic systems (for further discussions see
[
8
, Section 5]
).

Building on Loyd’s proof, we obtain the following generalization of her result, which applies in particular to Gaussian integers; for precise definitions, see Section
2
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a number field,
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}
its ring of integers, and
G_{\mathbb{K}}
the set of non-zero ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}
. Let
(X,\mathcal{X},T,\mu)
be an ergodic invertible m.p.s. with non-atomic
\mu
. For either
c(\mathfrak{a})=\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})
or
c(\mathfrak{a})=\omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})
, for every
\varepsilon>0

there exists a set
A\in\mathcal{X}
such that
0<\mu(A)<\varepsilon
, and for
\mu
-almost every
x\in X
,

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}\\
\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{1}_{A}(T^{c(\mathfrak{a})}x)=1,

and

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}\\
\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{1}_{A}(T^{c(\mathfrak{a})}x)=0.

In particular, Loyd’s result (
Theorem

2
) corresponds to the case
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}
and
c=\Omega_{\mathbb{Q}}=\Omega
in
Theorem

4
.

We can utilize
Theorem

4
to demonstrate the failure of pointwise convergence of the averages presented in
Theorem

3
. This is accomplished by transferring the failure of pointwise convergence of the averages along
\Omega_{\mathbb{Q}[i]}(\mathfrak{a})
to the averages along
\Omega(m^{2}+n^{2})
. The latter step requires calculating the density of specific arithmetic sets. The failure of pointwise convergence discussed above is a consequence of the following theorem.

Theorem 5
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)
be a non-atomic ergodic invertible m.p.s. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a set
A\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\mu(A)<\varepsilon
, and for
\mu
-almost every
x\in X

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{1\leq m,n\leq N}\mathbbm{1}_{A}\big(T^{\Omega(m^{2}+n^{2})}x\big)

\displaystyle\geq d\quad\text{, and}

\displaystyle\quad\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{1\leq m,n\leq N}\mathbbm{1}_{A}\big(T^{\Omega(m^{2}+n^{2})}x\big)

\displaystyle\leq 1-d,

where
d=\prod_{p\in\mathbb{P},p\equiv 3\mod{4}}(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}})\approx{}0.856
.
In particular, for
f=\mathbbm{1}_{A}
the average considered in
Theorem

3
does not converge for
\mu
-almost every
x\in X
.

Currently, we do not know if we can take
d=1
in
Theorem

5
.
Regarding results where we do have convergence, we propose the following generalization of
Theorem

1
to an arbitrary number field.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a number field,
(X,T)
a uniquely ergodic system with invariant measure
\mu
,
f\in C(X)
, and
x\in X
. For either
c(\mathfrak{a})=\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})
or
c(\mathfrak{a})
, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}\\
\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\end{subarray}}f(T^{c(\mathfrak{a})}x)=\int_{X}fd\mu.

In particular, by choosing the number field
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}
and
c(\mathfrak{a})=\Omega_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathfrak{a})
, we recover Bergelson and Richter’s statement. Also, by applying this theorem to the
2
-point rotation, we obtain

(2)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}\\
\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})}=0,

which is equivalent to the prime number theorem for number fields, also known as Landau’s prime ideal theorem. It is worth noting that our proof of
Theorem

6
does not provide a new proof of Landau’s prime ideal theorem, since it relies on a strengthening of that theorem. To see a proof of Landau’s prime ideal theorem, which is based on the ideas of Bergelson and Richter, we refer to
[
6
]
.

We remark that our methods for proving
Theorem

6
are not enough to prove the convergence for uniquely ergodic systems of other types of ergodic averages. For instance, we are not able to show the convergence of

(3)

\frac{1}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}\\
\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\end{subarray}}f(T^{\Omega(\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a}))}x).

The methods of
[
8
]
are able to deal with these and also more types of averages, in the particular case of the Gaussian integers.
We expect that the convergence of the averages as in (
3
), and possibly more general results, should follow from a “dynamical statement” concerning multiplicative actions defined in terms of prime ideals (or prime elements) of a number field. It would be interesting to show such a statement, even in particular classes, such as class number one fields (which are P.I.D.s) or imaginary quadratic fields. This problem remains open, but it is currently under investigation. A positive result in number fields would imply
[
2
, Theorem A]
,
[
8
, Theorem E]
,
Theorem

6
of this work, and would have many more consequences.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation

We denote the set of positive integers by
\mathbb{N}
and the set of (rational) prime numbers by
\mathbb{P}
. For a finite set
A
, we denote its cardinality by
|A|
. Following standard conventions,
\log_{2}(x)
denotes
\log(\log(x))
. For functions

f,g\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
we denote
f=O(g)
if there exists a constant
C
and an
N_{0}
such that for all
N\geq N_{0}
,
|f(N)|\leq C|g(N)|
, and
f=o_{N\to\infty}(g)
to denote
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{f(N)}{g(N)}=0
.

We follow the notation of Wu (
[
24
]
).
\mathbb{K}
denotes a number field and
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}
denotes its ring of integers. We denote an ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}
, usually by the letter
\mathfrak{a}
, a prime ideal by the letter
\mathfrak{p}
, and
G_{\mathbb{K}}
denotes the multiplicative semigroup of non-zero ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}
. The (absolute) norm of an ideal
\mathfrak{a}
is denoted by
\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})
.
An ideal
\mathfrak{a}
can be decomposed in terms of finitely many prime ideals (see, e.g.,
[
15
]
), and the decomposition is unique up to the order of the factors. If we write,

\mathfrak{a}=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
,
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{N}\ \cup\{0\}
,
1
1

1

We adopt this notation of convenience. Note that the product contains only finitely many
i
such that
\alpha_{i}>0
.

we define:

•

\omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})
as the number of distinct prime ideals dividing
\mathfrak{a}
.

•

\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})
as the total number of prime ideal factors of
\mathfrak{a}
, counted with multiplicity, i.e.,
\sum\alpha_{i}
.

\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}
satisfies the property
\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b})=\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})+\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{b})
for
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}
. Any function that fulfills this property is called completely additive. Similarly,
\omega_{\mathbb{K}}
satisfies
\omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b})=\omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})+\omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{b})
, provided that
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{b}
do not share a prime factor in their factorizations. Any function satisfying this property is called additive.

We also denote the
Dedekind Zeta function
of a number field as the function

\zeta_{\mathbb{K}}(s)=\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}}\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})^{s}}.

This function is initially defined only for
D=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Re(z)>1\}
, but admits a meromorphic extension to the complex plane with a single pole at
z=1
, which we still denote
\zeta_{\mathbb{K}}(\cdot)
. The residue at this pole, denoted by
\rho_{\mathbb{K}}
, is related to the growth of ideals.

Theorem 7
.

[
24
]
Let
\mathbb{K}
be a number field. Then there exists a
\delta
,
0<\delta<1
such that

|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\\
N(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}\\
N(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\end{subarray}}1=\rho_{\mathbb{K}}N+O(N^{\delta}).

2.2.
Asymptotic behavior of
\omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\cdot)
and
\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\cdot)

To prove
Theorem

6
and
Theorem

4
, we require some number-theoretical input. In particular, we need generalizations of the classical Hardy-Ramanujan theorem and of the Sathé-Selberg theorem, both of which have extensions to arbitrary number fields. These two results describe statistical properties of
\omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\cdot)
and
\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\cdot)
.
First, we start by presenting a version of the Erdős-Kac theorem for number fields, as established by Liu in
[
13
]
. While this specific form of the theorem is not explicitly stated in Liu’s work, it can be easily derived by combining Lemma 3 and Theorem 1 from that paper.

Theorem 8

(Erdős-Kac theorem for number fields)
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a number field. For either
c(\mathfrak{a})=\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})
or
c(\mathfrak{a})=\omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})
, and for every
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}

\lim_{x\to\infty,x\in\mathbb{Q}}\frac{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq x,\,\frac{c(\mathfrak{a})-\log_{2}x}{\sqrt{\log_{2}x}}\leq\gamma\}|}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq x\}|}=G(\gamma),

where
G(\gamma):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\gamma}e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}}dt
is the standard Gaussian distribution function.

As a consequence of
Theorem

8
, we obtain the following version of the Hardy-Ramanujan theorem for number fields.

Theorem 9

(Hardy-Ramanujan for number fields)
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a number field. For either
c(\mathfrak{a})=\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})
or
c(\mathfrak{a})
, for any
\varepsilon>0
and every sufficiently large
C=C(\varepsilon)>0
, we have

\lim_{x\to\infty,x\in\mathbb{Q}}\frac{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq x:\Bigl|\frac{c(\mathfrak{a})-\log_{2}(x)}{\sqrt{\log_{2}(x)}}\Bigr|>C\}|}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq x\}|}\leq\varepsilon.

This means that, for any small given
\varepsilon>0
, if we choose a sufficiently large
C
, then at least a (
1-\varepsilon
) fraction of the ideals have a number of prime factors in their decomposition (with or without multiplicity) lying in the moving interval

(4)

I_{N,C}:=\Bigl[\log_{2}(N)-C\sqrt{\log_{2}(N)},\,\log_{2}(N)+C\sqrt{\log_{2}(N)}\Bigr].

For
k\in I_{N,C}
, the distribution of the functions
\omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\cdot)
and
\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\cdot)
can be described precisely. In the case of integers, this was first established by Erdős
[
11
]
, refined by Sathé
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
, and simplified by Selberg
[
22
]
. This result is now known as the Sathé-Selberg theorem. Wu
[
24
]
later extended the Sathé-Selberg theorem to arbitrary number fields and provided asymptotic formulas for the following counting functions:
N_{k}(x):=|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq x,\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})=k\}|
and
\pi_{k}(x):=|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq x,\omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})=k\}|
.

Using the explicit form of the error term, together with
Theorem

7
and Stirling’s approximation as in
[
14
, Lemma 3.4]
, these formulas can be expressed in terms of exponential functions, which is convenient for further applications.

Lemma 1
.

Let
C>0
, and let
I_{N,C}
be as above. Then, for
k\in I_{N,C}

\frac{N_{k}(N)}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}=\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{k-\log_{2}(N)}{\sqrt{\log_{2}(N)}})^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\log_{2}(N)}}(1+\varepsilon_{k}(N)),

where the error terms converge uniformly to zero, i.e.,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{k\in I_{N,C}}|\varepsilon_{k}(N)|=0.

The same asymptotic formula holds for
\pi_{k}(N)
.

2.3.
Measure-preserving systems

We recall some basic notions of dynamical systems. A
topological dynamical system
(t.d.s.) is a pair
(X,T)
with
X
a compact metric space and
T\colon X\to X
continuous.

A
measure-preserving dynamical system
(m.p.s.) is a quadruple
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)
, where
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu)
is a probability space,
T\colon X\to X
is measurable and
\mu
is
T
-invariant (that is,
\mu(T^{-1}(A))=\mu(A)
for all
A\in\mathcal{X}
). If
T\colon X\to X
is an invertible transformation and its inverse is measurable, we say the system is
invertible
. A m.p.s. is called
ergodic
if for a set
A\in\mathcal{X}
,
A=T^{-1}A
implies
\mu(A)\in\{0,1\}
.
In all that follows, we may (and will) assume that
X
is a compact metric space,
\mathcal{X}
is its Borel
\sigma
-algebra, and
\mu
is a Borel probability measure.
By the Krylov–Bogolyubov theorem
[
4
]
,
every t.d.s. has at least one invariant measure. If this measure is unique, the system is called
uniquely ergodic
.

We also recall the following well-known fact (see, for instance,
[
3
, Chapter 1]
).

Proposition 1
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)
be a m.p.s. Consider the relation on
\mathcal{X}
given by
A\sim B
if and only if
\mu(A\Delta B)=0
. We define a metric
d
on
\mathcal{X}/\sim
as
d([A],[B])=\mu(A\Delta B)
for
[A],[B]\in\mathcal{X}/\sim
. Then
(\mathcal{X}/\sim,d)
is a complete separable metric space.

3.
Failure of pointwise convergence

3.1.
Failure of pointwise convergence for number fields

In this section, we extend
Theorem

2
. Loyd (
[
14
]
) proved
Theorem

2
in two main steps. In the first step, the author shows that the averages can be approximated by another family of averages, denoted
T_{N,C}
, for which classical tools can be employed to show the failure of pointwise convergence (we refer to
[
17
]
for more details on these tools). Once this approximation is established, she proves that the sweeping-out behavior of
T_{N,C}
can be transferred to the average of interest.
Thus, for our purposes, it suffices to show that the average we study can also be approximated by the operators
T_{N,C}
. Given a measure preserving system
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)
, and
C>0
, we define the operators
T_{N,C}\colon\mathcal{X}\to L^{1}(\mu)
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
, as

T_{N,C}(A)(x):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\log_{2}N}}\sum_{k\in I_{N,C}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{k-\log_{2}(N)}{\sqrt{\log_{2}N}}\right)^{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{A}(T^{k}x).

We need the following result, which is essentially contained in the proof of
[
14
, Theorem 1.2]
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)
be an ergodic invertible measure-preserving system with a non-atomic measure.
Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
and all sufficiently large
C=C(\varepsilon)>0
, there exists

G_{\delta}
-dense subset
R\subseteq\mathcal{X}/\!\sim
such that for every
representative
A\in\mathcal{X}
of a class
[A]\in R
and for
\mu
-almost every

x\in X

\limsup_{N\to\infty}T_{N,C}(A)(x)\geq 1-\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\liminf_{N\to\infty}T_{N,C}(A)(x)=0.

We now show that the sweeping-out behavior can be transferred to the averages of interest, which directly implies
Theorem

4
. Its proof follows closely that of
[
14
, Theorem 1.2]
.

Theorem 10
.

For any ergodic invertible m.p.s.
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)
, with
\mu
a non-atomic measure. Define
G_{N}
as follows

\displaystyle G_{N}\colon\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle\to L^{1}(\mu)

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\mapsto G_{N}(A)(x)=\frac{1}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:N(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}\\
N(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{1}_{A}(T^{\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})}x).

Then there exists a
G_{\delta}
set
R\subseteq\mathcal{X}/\sim
such that for every
[A]\in R
, for
\mu
-almost every
x\in X
,

\limsup_{N\to\infty}G_{N}(A)(x)=1\text{, and }\liminf_{N\to\infty}G_{N}(A)(x)=0.

Moreover, for every
\varepsilon>0
,
A
, may be chosen so that
\mu(A)<\varepsilon
.
The same statement holds for
\omega_{\mathbb{K}}
, where in the definition of
G_{N}
, we replace
\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}
with
\omega_{\mathbb{K}}
.

Proof.

Since the proofs for
\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}
and
\omega_{\mathbb{K}}
are identical, we only provide the proof for
\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}
.

Let
0<\varepsilon<1
. We claim that for any
A\in\mathcal{X}
and any point
x\in X
, there is a
C>0
such that

(5)

T_{N,C}(A)(x)+o(1)_{N\to\infty}\leq G_{N}(A)(x)\leq T_{N,C}(A)(x)+\varepsilon+o(1)_{N\to\infty}.

For the left-hand side inequality of (
5
), by applying
Lemma

1
, we have that for
k\in I_{N,C}

\frac{N_{k}(N)}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:N(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}=\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{k-\log_{2}(N)}{\sqrt{\log_{2}(N)}})^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\log_{2}(N)}}(1+\varepsilon_{k}(N)),

with the error terms satisfying
\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{k\in I_{N,C}}|\varepsilon_{k}(N)|=0
. Then, we obtain

\sum_{k\in I_{N,C}}\mathbbm{1}_{A}(T^{k}x)\frac{N_{k}(N)}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:N(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}=\sum_{k\in I_{N,C}}\mathbbm{1}_{A}(T^{k}x)\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{k-\log_{2}(N)}{\sqrt{\log_{2}(N)}})^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\log_{2}(N)}}(1+\varepsilon_{k}(N)).

By analyzing the contribution of the error terms, we get

\sum_{k\in I_{N,C}}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{k-\log_{2}(N)}{\sqrt{\log_{2}(N)}})^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\log_{2}(N)}}\varepsilon_{k}(N)=o(1)_{N\to\infty}.

Indeed, since
|I_{N,C}|=O(\sqrt{\log_{2}(N)})
and using that
0\leq e^{-x}\leq 1
for
x\geq 0
, it follows that

\sum_{k\in I_{N,C}}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{k-\log_{2}(N)}{\sqrt{\log_{2}(N)}})^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\log_{2}(N)}}\varepsilon_{k}(N)=O(\sup_{k\in I_{N,C}}|\varepsilon_{k}(N)|)=o(1)_{N\to\infty}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in I_{N,C}}\mathbbm{1}_{A}(T^{k}x)\frac{N_{k}(N)}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:N(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in I_{N,C}}\mathbbm{1}_{A}(T^{k}x)\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\big(\frac{k-\log_{2}(N)}{\sqrt{\log_{2}(N)}}\big)^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\log_{2}(N)}}

\displaystyle\quad+o(1)_{N\to\infty}.

Thus, we have proved the lower bound of (

T_{N,C}(A)(x)+o(1)_{N\to\infty}\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}\mathbbm{1}_{A}(T^{k}x)\frac{N_{k}(N)}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:N(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}=G_{N}(A)(x).

To establish the upper bound of (
5

Theorem

9
that, for every sufficiently large constant
C>0
, we have

(6)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{k\not\in I_{N,C}}\frac{N_{k}(N)}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:N(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}\leq\varepsilon.

Using
Lemma

1
again and (

\displaystyle G_{N}(A)(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in I_{N,C}}\mathbbm{1}_{A}(T^{k}x)\frac{N_{k}(N)}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:N(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k\notin I_{N,C}}\mathbbm{1}_{A}(T^{k}x)\frac{N_{k}(N)}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:N(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}

\displaystyle\leq T_{N,C}(A)(x)+o(1)_{N\to\infty}+\varepsilon.

This completes the proof of (
5
).

We now choose a sufficiently large constant
C
, for which both
Theorem

9
and
Lemma

2
hold for
\varepsilon>0
, and we obtain a dense
G_{\delta}
set
R_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\mathcal{X}/\sim
. For any set
[A]\in R_{\varepsilon}
, for
\mu
-a.e
x\in X
,

(7)

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}T_{N,C}(A)(x)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\varepsilon\text{, and}

(8)

\displaystyle\liminf_{N\to\infty}T_{N,C}(A)(x)

\displaystyle=0.

By taking
\limsup
and
\liminf
in (
5
),
we conclude by (
7
) and
(
8
)
, that for each
[A]\in R_{\varepsilon}
, for
\mu-

x\in X

(9)

\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon\leq\limsup_{N\to\infty}G_{N}(A)(x)

\displaystyle\leq 1\text{, and}

(10)

\displaystyle 0\leq\liminf_{N\to\infty}G_{N}(A)(x)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon.

By

1
and Baire’s category theorem, the set
R=\bigcap_{m\in\mathbb{N}}R_{\frac{1}{m}}\subseteq\mathcal{X}/\sim

Hence, for any

The existence of a set

3.2.
Failure of pointwise convergence along
\Omega(m^{2}+n^{2})
.

There are several obstacles if we want to follow Loyd’s approach for proving the failure of pointwise convergence of the following averages

(12)

\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{1\leq m,n\leq N}f(T^{\Omega(m^{2}+n^{2})}x),

In particular, such an approach would require at least an Erdős-Kac type theorem for the values of
\Omega(m^{2}+n^{2})
together with a uniform asymptotic approximation of the weights

\frac{|\{1\leq m,n\leq N:\Omega(m^{2}+n^{2})=k\}|}{N^{2}},

in terms of some explicit functions. To our knowledge, no such results are currently available.
Instead, our strategy consists of relating the averages in (
12
) to averages taken over the Gaussian integers in dilates of a quarter-disk.
Define
D^{+}:=\{u+vi\in\mathbb{C}:u^{2}+v^{2}\leq 1,\;u>0,v\geq 0\}
,
and for
N\geq 1
, let
S_{N}:=ND^{+}\cap\mathbb{Z}[i]^{*}
. That is,
S_{N}
consists of the Gaussian integers in the first quadrant of the disk of radius
N
.

The reason for considering the dilates of
D^{+}
is that every non-zero ideal of
\mathbb{Z}[i]
can be uniquely represented by a lattice point in some dilate of
D^{+}
. Therefore, averaging over the sets
(S_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
corresponds naturally to averaging over the non-zero ideals in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
.

For clarity, we slightly modify the notation by using
\mathcal{N}(\cdot)
to represent both the ideal norm and the standard (squared) Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{Z}[i]
. This is consistent, since for any
a+bi\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
, we have
\mathcal{N}((a+bi))=a^{2}+b^{2}
.

Note that the box
\{m+ni:1\leq m,n\leq N\}
is contained in the dilated set
S_{2N}
. Our goal is to relate (
12
) and

(13)

\frac{1}{|S_{2N}|}\sum_{m+ni\in S_{2N}}f\!\big(T^{\Omega_{\mathbb{Q}[i]}((m+ni))}x\big).

This will enable us to deduce the failure of the averages in (
12
) by leveraging the result of
Theorem

4
in the particular case of
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}[i]
and
c=\Omega_{\mathbb{Q}[i]}
.

To relate (
13
) and (
12
), we need to describe the values of
m+ni\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
such that
\Omega(m^{2}+n^{2})=\Omega_{\mathbb{Q}[i]}((m+ni))
. In
\mathbb{Z}[i]
, an element
p\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
is prime if
(p)
is a prime ideal in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
. A prime element
p
in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
can be put into two categories:

•

When
p=uq
, where
u
is a unit of
(\mathbb{Z}[i],\cdot)
,
q\in\mathbb{P}
, and
q\equiv 3\mod 4
, in this case
\mathcal{N}(p)
is the square of a (rational) prime.

•

When
\mathcal{N}(p)
is a (rational) prime, it holds that either
\mathcal{N}(p)\equiv 1\mod{4}
or
\mathcal{N}(p)=2
.

We will denote the primes in the first class by
\mathbb{P}_{I}
and the ones in the second class by
\mathbb{P}_{II}
.
Using this classification, we can precisely characterize the elements
m+ni\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
, for which
\Omega(m^{2}+n^{2})=\Omega_{\mathbb{Q}[i]}((m+ni))
.

Indeed, to see this, consider
the prime factorization of
m+ni
, where
u
is a unit

m+ni=u\prod_{p_{i}\in\mathbb{P}_{I}}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\prod_{p_{i}\in\mathbb{P}_{II}}p_{i}^{\beta_{i}}.

It follows that the ideal
(m+ni)
factors as

(m+ni)=\prod_{p_{i}\in\mathbb{P}_{I}}(p_{i})^{\alpha_{i}}\prod_{p_{i}\in\mathbb{P}_{II}}(p_{i})^{\beta_{i}}.

Then, on the one hand

\Omega(m^{2}+n^{2})=2\sum_{p_{i}\in\mathbb{P}_{I}}\alpha_{i}+\sum_{p_{i}\in\mathbb{P}_{II}}\beta_{i},

on the other hand

\Omega_{\mathbb{Q}[i]}((m+ni))=\sum_{p_{i}\in\mathbb{P}_{I}}\alpha_{i}+\sum_{p_{i}\in\mathbb{P}_{II}}\beta_{i}.

Then,
\Omega(m^{2}+n^{2})=\Omega_{\mathbb{Q}[i]}((m+ni))
if and only if all the
\alpha_{i}
are zero, which means that
m+ni
does not have a divisor in
\mathbb{P}_{I}
. The following result will be used to compute the density of Gaussian integers that do not possess a divisor in
\mathbb{P}_{I}
.

Theorem 11
.

[
8
, Theorem B]

If
f:\mathbb{Z}[i]^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
is a real-valued bounded completely multiplicative function satisfying
f(i)=1
and
(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is a dilated Følner sequence (see
[
8
]
for the definition), then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}f(n)\text{ exists and equals }\prod_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{1}}\frac{\mathcal{N}(p)-1}{\mathcal{N}(p)-f(p)}.

Here,
\mathbb{P}_{1}
denotes the Gaussian primes in the first quadrant
Q_{1}:=\{m+ni\in\mathbb{Z}[i]:m>0,n\geq 0\}
.

We now apply this theorem to compute the density of the exceptional set introduced above.

Proposition 2
.

Let
E=\{p\in\mathbb{Z}[i]:p\equiv 3\mod 4,p\in\mathbb{P}\}
, i.e., the elements of
\mathbb{P}_{I}
in the first quadrant. Define
D
as the set of Gaussian integers that do not have divisors in
E
. Then, the density of
D
taken along squares is given by

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{|\{a+bi\in\mathbb{Z}[i],1\leq a,b\leq N,a+bi\in D\}|}{|\{{a+bi\in\mathbb{Z}[i],1\leq a,b\leq N\}}|}=d,

where

d:=\prod_{p\in\mathbb{P},p\equiv 3\mod 4}(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}})\approx 0.856\ldots

Proof.

Let
f(m+ni)=\mathbbm{1}_{D}(m+ni)
. It is a real-valued bounded completely multiplicative function with
f(i)=1
. Consider the dilated Følner sequence given by
\Phi_{N}=(NU)\cap\mathbb{Z}[i]^{*}
, where
U=\{a+bi\in\mathbb{C}:0<a\leq 1,0<b\leq 1\}
and note that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{|\{a+bi\in\mathbb{Z}[i],1\leq a,b\leq N,a+bi\in D\}|}{|\{{a+bi\in\mathbb{Z}[i],1\leq a,b\leq N\}}|}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{k\in\Phi_{N}}\mathbbm{1}_{D}(k).

By
Theorem

11
, the limit above exists and equals

\prod_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{1}}\frac{\mathcal{N}(p)-1}{\mathcal{N}(p)-f(p)}=\prod_{p\in\mathbb{P},p\equiv 3\mod 4}\frac{p^{2}-1}{p^{2}}=\prod_{p\in\mathbb{P},p\equiv 3\mod 4}\bigl(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\bigr)=d.

Note that
d=\frac{1}{2K^{2}}
, where
K\approx 0.764223\ldots
is the Landau-Ramanujan constant (see
[
5
]
). It follows that
d\approx 0.856\ldots
.
∎

We now have all the ingredients to prove the failure of pointwise convergence of the averages in (
12
).

Proof of
Theorem

5
.

By the remark following
Theorem

10
(
Remark

1
), we may apply
Theorem

10
in the case
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}[i]
,
c=\Omega_{\mathbb{Q}[i]}
, for the sequence
a(N)=(2N)^{2}
. Hence, for every
\varepsilon>0
, we obtain the existence of a Borel set
A\in\mathcal{X}
, with
0<\mu(A)<\varepsilon
such that for the averages

P_{N}(x)=\frac{1}{|S_{N}|}\sum_{m+ni\in S_{N}}\mathbbm{1}_{A}(T^{\Omega_{\mathbb{Q}[i]}((m+ni))}x),

there exist, for
\mu
-almost every
x\in X
, subsequences
(r_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
, and

(g_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
of

\bigl(2N\bigr)_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
, such that

(14)

\lim_{N\to\infty}P_{r_{N}}(x)=1,

and

(15)

\lim_{N\to\infty}P_{g_{N}}(x)=0.

Now we fix
x
in this set of full measure. We first analyze the

m+ni\in D

\Omega(m^{2}+n^{2})=\Omega_{\mathbb{Q}[i]}((m+ni))

(16)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{1\leq m,n\leq N}\mathbbm{1}_{A}(T^{\Omega(m^{2}+n^{2})}x)

and by nonnegativity of the terms it suffices to obtain a lower bound for the first term of the right-hand side of (

4.
Convergence in uniquely ergodic systems and applications

4.1.
Convergence in the topological setting

To address pointwise convergence in uniquely ergodic systems as mentioned in
Theorem

6
, we adopt an approach outlined in
[
2
, Remark 1.3]
, which was introduced to Bergelson and Richter by Kanigowski and Radzwiłł for the particular case where
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}
and
c=\Omega_{\mathbb{Q}}
. It consists of proving the asymptotic shift-invariance of an average using the statistical properties of
\omega_{\mathbb{K}}
and
\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}
described in
Section

2
, from which the pointwise result follows immediately.

Theorem 12
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a number field. If
a:\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\to\mathbb{C}
is a bounded sequence, then for either
c(\mathfrak{a})=\omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})
or
c(\mathfrak{a})=\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}\\
\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\end{subarray}}a\big(c(\mathfrak{a})+1\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}\\
\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\end{subarray}}a\big(c(\mathfrak{a})\big)+o(1)_{N\to\infty}.

Remark 2
.

In the case of
\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}
, this result was proven without appealing to any form of the prime ideal theorem by Burgin in
[
6
]
.

Proof.

We provide the proof only for
c=\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}
, as the other case is identical.
For
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, recall that
N_{k}(N)=|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N,\;\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})=k\}|
,
and define
w_{N}(k):=\frac{N_{k}(N)}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}
.
We may write

\frac{1}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:N(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}\\
\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\end{subarray}}a(\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a}))=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}w_{N}(k+1)a(k+1)+o(1)_{N\to\infty},

and

\frac{1}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}\\
\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\end{subarray}}a(\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})+1)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}w_{N}(k)a(k+1)+o(1)_{N\to\infty}.

So, it suffices to show that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\|a(k)\|\cdot|w_{N}(k+1)-w_{N}(k)|=0.

Since
a
is bounded, it suffices to prove

(19)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}|w_{N}(k+1)-w_{N}(k)|=0.

To this end, let
\varepsilon>0
. By the Hardy-Ramanujan theorem (
Theorem

9
), there exists a sufficiently large
C>0
such that most of the weight of the
w_{N}(k)
functions is supported on an interval of the form
k\in I_{N,C}
(see (
4
)), that is

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{k\not\in I_{N,C}}w_{N}(k)<\varepsilon.

Then

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}|w_{N}(k+1)-w_{N}(k)|=\sum_{k\in I_{N,C}}|w_{N}(k+1)-w_{N}(k)|+o(1)_{N\to\infty}+O(\varepsilon).

Now, using
Lemma

1
, we have

w_{N}(k)=\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{k-\log_{2}(N)}{\sqrt{\log_{2}(N)}})^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\log_{2}(N)}}(1+\varepsilon_{k}(N)),

where the error terms satisfy
\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{k\in I_{N,C}}|\varepsilon_{k}(N)|=0
. Proceeding in the same manner as in the proof of
Theorem

4
, we address the summability of the error terms and obtain that (
19
) equals

\sum_{k\in I_{N,C}}\Bigl|\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{k+1-\log_{2}(N)}{\sqrt{\log_{2}(N)}})^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\log_{2}(N)}}-\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{k-\log_{2}(N)}{\sqrt{\log_{2}(N)}})^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\log_{2}(N)}}\Bigr|+o(1)_{N\to\infty}+O(\varepsilon).

Using the basic equality
(k+1-\log_{2}(N))^{2}=(k-\log_{2}(N))^{2}+2(k-\log_{2}(N))+1,

we can rewrite (
19
) as

\sum_{k\in I_{N,C}}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{k-\log_{2}(N)}{\sqrt{\log_{2}(N)}})^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\log_{2}(N)}}|e^{\frac{\log_{2}(N)-k}{\log_{2}(N)}-\frac{1}{2\log_{2}(N)}}-1|+O(\varepsilon)+o(1)_{N\to\infty}

Since for
k\in I_{N,C}
we have
-C\log_{2}(N)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{\log_{2}(N)-k}{\log_{2}(N)}\leq C\log_{2}(N)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

we obtain

\sum_{k\in I_{N,C}}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{k-\log_{2}(N)}{\sqrt{\log_{2}(N)}})^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\log_{2}(N)}}|e^{\frac{\log_{2}(N)-k}{\log_{2}(N)}-\frac{1}{2\log_{2}(N)}}-1|=o(1)_{N\to\infty}\cdot\sum_{k\in I_{N,C}}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{k-\log_{2}(N)}{\sqrt{\log_{2}(N)}})^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\log_{2}(N)}}.

Given that

\sum_{k\in I_{N,C}}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{k-\log_{2}(N)}{\sqrt{\log_{2}(N)}})^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\log_{2}(N)}}=O(1),

we conclude that

\sum_{k\in I_{N,C}}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{k-\log_{2}(N)}{\sqrt{\log_{2}(N)}})^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\log_{2}(N)}}|e^{\frac{\log_{2}(N)-k}{\log_{2}(N)}-\frac{1}{2\log_{2}(N)}}-1|=o(1)_{N\to\infty}.

Collecting all terms gives

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}|w_{N}(k+1)-w_{N}(k)|=O(\varepsilon)+o(1)_{N\to\infty}.

Thus, we obtain

(20)

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}|w_{N}(k+1)-w_{N}(k)|=O(\varepsilon).

Since this holds for any
\varepsilon>0
, we get (
19
), finishing the proof.
∎

The proof of
Theorem

6
follows from
Theorem

12
by nowadays standard arguments. For completeness, we provide them below.

Proof of
Theorem

.

For a fixed
x\in X
, consider the empirical measures

\mu_{N}=\frac{1}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}\\
\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\end{subarray}}\delta_{T^{c(\mathfrak{a})}x},\quad N\in\mathbb{N}

where

or

. Our goal is to show that

converges to

is

,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{\bigl|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}\bigr|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}\\
\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\end{subarray}}\bigl(f(T^{c(\mathfrak{a})}x)-f(T^{c(\mathfrak{a})+1}x)\bigr)=0.

But this follows directly from

4.2.
Norm convergence

To deduce norm convergence, we use a folklore transference principle that allows us to pass from results for rotations on the circle to general measure-preserving systems. More precisely, we will employ a generalization of the following well-known statement.

Proposition 3
.

Let
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of nonnegative integers such that, for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\setminus\{1\}

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\lambda^{a_{n}}=0.

Then, for every m.p.s.
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)
and every
f\in L^{2}(X,\mu)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\Bigg\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(T^{a_{n}}x)-P(f)\Bigg\rVert_{L^{2}(X,\mu)}=0,

where
P(f)
denotes the orthogonal projection onto

I(T):=\{g\in L^{2}(X,\mu):g\circ T=g\}.

Recall that if
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)
is ergodic, then
P(f)=\int f\,d\mu
.

To generalize this result to averages in number fields, it is convenient to introduce the following notion.

Definition 1
.

Let
(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of nonempty finite sets, and let
\tau:\bigcup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\Phi_{N}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
be a function. Then, the pair
((\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}},\tau)
is referred to as an averaging scheme.

Given an averaging scheme, we can consider the corresponding ergodic averages

\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{\tau(n)}x),\quad N\in\mathbb{N}.

By taking
\Phi_{N}=\{1,\ldots,N\}
and
\tau(n)=n
, we recover the classical ergodic averages.
Moreover, by setting

\Phi_{N}=\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}

and letting
\tau(\mathfrak{a})=\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})
or

\tau(\mathfrak{a})=\omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})
,
we recover the averages studied in previous sections.
This definition will be helpful for generalizing classical results from ergodic theory to specific averages that appear unnatural when indexed by the natural numbers, but arise naturally from a different underlying structure, such as a number field.

In this framework, we can extend
Proposition

3
as follows.

Lemma 3
.

Let
((\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}},\tau)
be an averaging scheme. If for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\setminus\{1\}

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\lambda^{\tau(n)}=0.

Then, for every m.p.s.
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)
and every
f\in L^{2}(X,\mu)
, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left\|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}f\bigl(T^{\tau(n)}x\bigr)-P(f)\right\|_{L^{2}(X,\mu)}=0.

This follows by applying the Bochner-Herglotz spectral theorem (see, for example,
[
9
, Theorem C.9]
).

Now, we have the tools to generalize
[
14
, Theorem 2.5]
, which corresponds to the special case
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}
and
c=\Omega_{\mathbb{Q}}

Theorem 13

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a number field and
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)
a m.p.s.
For any

Using the fact that irrational rotations and rotations on

4.3.
Applications to number theory

Since we have ergodic theorems for uniquely ergodic systems, we can derive equidistribution results for specific sequences as corollaries. Here, we illustrate some applications.

First, we present a generalization of the Pillai-Selberg theorem
[
16
,
23
]
to number fields. This result has previously been established for
\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}
by Burgin in
[
6
]
. The idea of the proof is standard and proceeds by analyzing exponential sums in
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
. The idea is to interpret these sums as ergodic averages in rotations on
m
-points. The desired result then follows directly from our understanding of these averages along
\omega_{\mathbb{K}}
and
\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}
, as established in
Theorem

6
.

Corollary 2
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a number field. For every integer
m\geq 2
and every
l\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\bigl|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N,\ \Omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})\equiv\ell\pmod{m}\}\bigr|}{\bigl|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}\bigr|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m},

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\bigl|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N,\ \omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})\equiv\ell\pmod{m}\}\bigr|}{\bigl|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}\bigr|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}.

Using the same ideas, we present a generalization of a result of Erdős-Delange
[
7
,
10
]
, which was also previously obtained by Burgin in
[
6
]
in the case of
\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}
. The proof follows from applying
Theorem

6
to irrational rotations and utilizing Weyl’s equidistribution criterion. Recall that
\{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor
denotes the fractional part of a real number.

Corollary 3
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a number field. For any irrational number
\alpha
and any interval
I\subseteq[0,1)
, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}\\
\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{1}_{I}(\{\alpha\Omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})\})=|I|,

and

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\{\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a}\in G_{\mathbb{K}}\\
\mathcal{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq N\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{1}_{I}(\{\alpha\omega_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{a})\})=|I|.

More generally, the same conclusion of
Corollary

3
holds, if we replace the polynomial sequence
p(n)=n\alpha
(where
\alpha
is irrational) by any generalized polynomial
g(n)
(see
[
1
]
for the definitions) such that
(g(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

1
.
Recall that a sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is said to be

uniformly distributed modulo
1
if for every interval

I\subset[0,1)
,
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\bigl|\{1\leq n\leq N:\{x_{n}\}\in I\}\bigr|=|I|
. To establish this extension, we require the following additional input.

Theorem 14
.

[
1
, Theorem A]

Let

be a generalized polynomial. Then there exists a nilmanifold

\{g(n)\}=f(T^{n}x),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}.

14

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\mathbbm{1}_{I}(\{Q(\tau(n)\})=|I|.

As a corollary, we derive the following result.

Corollary 4

Let
