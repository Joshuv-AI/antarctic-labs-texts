Homologous recombination deficiency testing implementation in the routine clinical setting: real-life experience and lessons learned
Authors: P. Romero-Lozano, M. Gómez-Rey, M. Vila-Casadesús, A. Martin, G. Buono, V. Bach, A. Onieva, M. Ricart, N. Deltiempo, E. Castillo, L. Fariñas, S. Aguilar, E. Garralda, R. Dienstmann, P. Nuciforo, J. Tabernero, C. Garcia-Duran, A Vivancos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHomologous recombination deficiency (HRD) is a critical biomarker for predicting response to poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors in high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC). Hence, genomic scar-based HRD testing must be implemented in routine clinical labs. This study aims to obtain a head-to-head comparison of some of the applications available for HRD status determination in a real-world clinical cohort to guide HRD testing standardization.Materials and methodsHRD scores were obtained from HGSOC formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded samples from the Molecular Prescreening Program at Vall d’Hebron Institute of Oncology (VHIO) using two approaches: VHIO-HRD (n = 229) and shallow sequencing-based HRD score (LSTsh-HRD; n = 123). Large-scale transitions (LST), telomeric allelic imbalance (TAI), and genomic loss of heterozygosity (LOH) were calculated using data from a custom hybrid-capture panel (VHIO-HRD score), and LST was determined from shallow whole-genome sequencing of a genomic library (LSTsh-HRD score). Validation was obtained by benchmarking against established and commercially available platforms. Tumor fraction (TF) was estimated using several computational tools.ResultsOptimized HRD status cutoff for VHIO-HRD (LSTVHIO-HRD + TAIVHIO-HRD + LOHVHIO-HRD) was set to ≥47 based on comparison with commercially available tools, whereas HRD status cutoff for LSTsh-HRD was predefined at ≥20. Despite differences in chemistry and scoring metrics, strong concordance in HRD classification was observed across assays. TF strongly influenced HRD performance, with optimal sensitivity at TF ≥0.4 for VHIO-HRD and ≥0.2 for LSTsh-HRD.ConclusionsWe validated that HRD scar quantification can be implemented across testing laboratories using assays with different library preparation chemistries and biomarker calculation algorithms. TF is a critical determinant of accurate HRD scoring; LSTsh-HRD quantification offers improved sensitivity in low-cellularity contexts, and VHIO-HRD enables status classification across the continuum of score values in samples with TF ≥0.4. A multiplatform, standardized HRD testing strategy may enhance biomarker-driven patient selection for PARP inhibitor therapies in HGSOC and other HRD-associated tumors.

Introduction

Homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) is a molecular condition characterized by the inability of the cell to effectively and accurately repair double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) breaks using the homologous recombination repair (HRR) pathway.1,2 This deficiency leads to genomic instability and chromosomal alterations that contribute to cancer development and progression.1,3 Importantly, HRD has been widely associated with increased sensitivity to DNA-damaging agents such as platinum-based chemotherapy and poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors,4,5 particularly in tumors harboring defects in the HRR pathway, including those with BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations.6, 7, 8

PARP inhibitors disrupt the DNA repair pathway by targeting the PARP enzyme, which is critical for repairing single-stranded DNA breaks. In cancer cells with HRD, where the cell’s ability to repair dsDNA breaks is already compromised, this disruption leads to the accumulation of DNA damage and ultimately cell death. As a result, PARP inhibitors induce synthetic lethality in BRCA-mutated and HRD-positive tumors, making this a targeted approach for treating cancers such as ovarian cancer characterized by an inherited mutation of the BRCA1/29,10 genes or genomic instability signatures associated with loss of function of HRR genes.11,12 Initially approved for ovarian cancer13 harboring BRCA1/2 mutations or genomic instability associated with HRD, PARP inhibitors have since demonstrated efficacy in other cancers, such as breast cancer with germline mutations in BRCA1/2 genes,14,15 pancreatic cancer with germline mutations in BRCA1/2 genes,16,17 and prostate cancer with germline or somatic mutations in HRR genes.18,19

The introduction of PARP inhibitors into clinical practice has significantly transformed treatment strategies in both first-line and relapse settings.4,5,20,21 In this context, accurate assessment of HRD has become clinically relevant for guiding treatment selection by identifying patients most likely to benefit from PARP inhibitor therapy. Recognizing the clinical relevance of HRD status as a predictive biomarker, multiple assays have been developed for its evaluation.2,22, 23, 24, 25

These assays mainly rely on two strategies: detecting mutations in HRR-related genes and/or the presence of HRD-related genomic instability, manifested as the accumulation of specific genomic scars,26 namely, loss of heterozygosity (LOH),27 telomeric allelic imbalance (TAI),28 and large-scale transitions (LSTs).29 Among available HRD tests, Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved and Conformité Européenne-marked Myriad MyChoice® CDx remains the gold standard for identifying HRD-positive patients.30 In addition, other commercially and publicly available HRD tools, such as the SOPHiA DDM™ Dx HRD test and ShallowHRD, are also employed in clinical laboratories to evaluate HRD status.31, 32, 33

However, commercial assays frequently rely on centralized testing models or, when accessible for local testing, require a minimum set of samples to be batched before processing, resulting in longer turnaround times and operational complexity for lower-volume laboratories. In contrast, decentralized in-house strategies implemented within molecular pathology laboratories allow independent testing, with sample preparation, sequencing, and data analysis performed within the same institution. Such approaches can increase flexibility, facilitate integration within existing genomic profiling workflows, and improve responsiveness in clinical decision making. Consequently, there is a growing need for robust in-house HRD testing strategies that maintain analytical reliability while enabling scalable and cost-effective implementation in routine diagnostic settings. In this context, accurate tumor fraction (TF) estimation is critical. Pathologist-estimated neoplastic cellularity, although widely used as an initial inclusion requirement for downstream genomic analysis, is a qualitative measure that may overestimate the effective tumor DNA fraction, as it does not account for stromal and inflammatory components, tumor ploidy, or subclonality.34 In contrast, genomics-based TF estimation provides a more accurate and relevant measure for copy number-based and HRD-based analyses.35

Multiple studies have shown that tumor admixture with normal cells and tumor aneuploidy can substantially compromise the accuracy of HRD scoring.36, 37, 38 Despite their clinical utility, HRD assays lack standardized methods for TF assessment and do not account for the quantitative impact on HRD scoring. Ongoing challenges in assay accessibility and sensitivity continue to limit their broader clinical adoption, highlighting the need for optimized HRD evaluation strategies.2,22,25,39

Accurate assessment of HRD status is critically dependent on precise TF measurements and copy number alteration (CNA) analysis. Publicly available and widely used bioinformatic tools for CNA analysis include Allele-Specific Copy Number Analysis of Tumors (ASCAT)40 and ichorCNA.41 ASCAT estimates allele-specific copy numbers, tumor ploidy, and the proportion of cells with aberrant DNA from single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) arrays or sequencing data, whereas ichorCNA is optimized for shallow whole-genome sequencing (shWGS) data. However, computational tools such as ASCAT can fail under certain conditions, such as low tumor content or technical artifacts, as they are unable to reliably estimate tumor purity and return Aberrant Fraction = 1 (100% tumor purity),38 representing a computational failure mode.

Published studies consistently recommend excluding low tumor cellularity samples to ensure reliable HRD scoring and avoid the risk of false-negative results; however, no universal cellularity cutoff has been established, with proposed thresholds varying widely (0.2-0.4 tumor content).42,43

In this study, we aimed to address key technical challenges related to HRD status assessment and implementation by comparing correlation and concordance between two approaches (VHIO-HRD and LSTsh-HRD), as well as evaluating the impact of tumor cellularity on scoring performance. Both approaches were applied to a cohort of patients with high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) from the Molecular Prescreening Program at Vall d’Hebron Institute of Oncology (VHIO MPP).44, 45, 46 This real-world comparison enabled evaluation of performance, strengths, and limitations across computational tools and library types.

Materials and methods

Samples

Established in 2010, VHIO MPP has driven precision oncology through advanced molecular diagnostics. The program has profiled more than 9000 patients with cancer to match targeted therapies and enrollment in clinical trials. A multidisciplinary team of experts in cancer biology, genomics, bioinformatics, pathology, genetic counseling, and oncology supports molecular profiling and biomarker-driven approaches for early-phase clinical trials, treatment selection, clinical decision making, and translational research.

Between June 2021 and May 2024, a total of 2999 formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor samples from 2884 patients enrolled in the VHIO MPP were screened. TF, estimated from molecular data, was used to select HGSOC samples with TF ≥0.2 for HRD analysis. Two HRD assays were evaluated: VHIO-HRD (212 samples analyzed) and LSTsh-HRD (114 samples). For the analysis focused on validation, cut-off determination and concordance between HRD approaches, only HGSOC samples with a tumor fraction (TF) ≥ 0.4 were included to ensure optimal conditions for HRD score quantification. Samples with TF between 0.20 and 0.39 were incorporated exclusively in the evaluation of TF limits for VHIO-HRD and LSTsh-HRD quantification. A consort diagram summarizing sample inclusion throughout the study is provided in Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108242.

Hematoxylin and eosin

After obtaining informed consent, tumor profiling was performed at the Molecular Oncology Laboratory. In accordance with standard operating procedures, hematoxylin and eosin-stained sections were reviewed by a pathologist to determine neoplastic cellularity. Samples were required to meet a minimum pathologist-estimated TF ≥0.2 for downstream genomic analysis.

Isolation of FFPE-derived tumor DNA

DNA extraction from FFPE samples (6 sections of 10-μm FFPE tissue) was performed with the automated system Maxwell FFPE Plus LEV DNA Purification Kit (Promega; Walldorf, Germany). dsDNA concentration was measured with Qubit (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Vilnius, Lithuania) and DNA quality was assessed with Tape Station (Agilent Technologies; Waldbronn, Germany).

VHIO300 test

VHIO300, implemented within the VHIO MPP, is a custom hybrid-capture next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel that targets over 430 cancer-related genes [including DNA damage repair (DDR) genes such as BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, ATM, ATRX, and CHEK2] and 13 intronic regions, enabling detection of single-nucleotide variants, indels, and gene fusions. The panel also contains a backbone of probes that cover 105 SNPs for genome-wide CNA and LOH profiling by determining the B-allele frequency (BAF) of each individual SNP. To add CNA profiling capacity, the genomic pre-enrichment library was sequenced in parallel at low depth (with shWGS).

CNA and LOH profiles were used for HRD assessment by calculating the VHIO-HRD score. In addition, a second HRD score was calculated (LSTsh-HRD) by analyzing the shWGS CNA profile (see details in HRD score calculation below).

Libraries were prepared according to the Agilent standard protocol (SureSelect XT Human, Agilent). Briefly, 500 ng of dsDNA was fragmented (S2, Covaris), end-repaired, A-tailed, and linked to adapters. After 10 cycles of PCR, 5 μL of the genomic library (10-80 ng genomic DNA) was taken to perform shWGS and 750 ng was used as input for capture, followed by an additional postcapture 12-cycle PCR step. Capture-based and genomic-constructed libraries were pooled and sequenced in an Illumina instrument, 2 × 151 paired-end reads.

Sequencing reads were aligned (BWA v0.7.17, Samtools v1.9) against the hg19 reference genome, base recalibrated, realigned (ABRA2 v2-2.23), indel realigned [Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) v3.7.0], and variant called by two different tools (VarScan2 v2.4.3; GATK Mutect2 v4.1.0.0). A minimum of seven reads supporting the variant allele were required to call a mutation. Frequent SNPs in the population were filtered based on the gnomAD database (allele frequency ≤0.0001) and CNAs were calculated (CNVkit v0.9.6.dev0). Clinical significance classification of the variants was performed using the following databases: COSMIC (https://cancer.sanger.ac.uk/cosmic), cBioPortal (http://www.cbioportal.org/), ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/), OncoKB (https://www.oncokb.org/), and VarSome (https://varsome.com/). Finally, manual data curation was performed among all the knowledge databases to harmonize their criteria.

For shWGS, aligned reads were analyzed using the publicly available computational tool ichorCNA to detect a genome-wide CNA profile. The ichorCNA workflow consisted of three main steps: computing read coverage, data normalization, and CNA prediction, along with TF estimation.

TF estimation

For the hybrid-capture module, TF was assessed using ASCAT (ASCAT TF). Cases in which ASCAT failed to reliably estimate tumor purity (due to the lack of significant copy number changes) were excluded (ASCAT without one TF); an additional analysis was performed to evaluate the impact of this limitation on HRD assessment.22,23 For shWGS, TF was determined by ichorCNA (ichor TF). A curated TF determination was implemented by reviewing TF while considering the variant frequency of the highest somatic variant, LOH loci, and CNA. This approach was applied to determine TF in the present study.

A comparison of TF estimation approaches is shown in Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108242. TF estimation impacts HRD score reliability, classification, and variability across ranges. Curated TF values were consistently lower than pathologist estimates and showed only moderate correlation (r = 0.406), indicating that histological assessment should be used with caution as a surrogate, as it may overestimate the effective tumor DNA fraction relevant for HRD analysis.

HRD score calculation

Two different HRD scores were obtained from the VHIO300 data. First, the VHIO-HRD score, adapted from Marquard et al.,26 was derived from regions captured by the VHIO300 panel, which includes cancer-related genes and a genome-wide SNP backbone. This backbone of SNPs distributed across the genome, with an average allele frequency of ∼0.5, enabled reconstruction of genome-wide copy number and allelic imbalance profiles from targeted NGS data. Copy number alterations were inferred from read depth using CNVkit,47 while BAF from heterozygous SNPs enabled detection of allelic imbalance and LOH. Together, these signals generated a genome-wide copy number landscape from which the three genomic scar components—LOH, TAI, and LST—were quantified and integrated to produce the final VHIO-HRD score.

In addition, a second HRD score was calculated by the genomic scar-based algorithm ShallowHRD (v1.13),32 which analyzed LST derived from the shWGS. Samples with scores ≥20 were classified as HRD, from 15 to 19 were considered borderline (BDL), and scores <15 were classified as homologous recombination proficiency (HRP) or HRD-negative.

For terminological consistency and to facilitate direct comparison, ShallowHRD is referred to as LSTsh-HRD.

Comparison against gold-standard HRD tests

Myriad MyChoice® CDx is the first FDA-approved commercial in vitro diagnostic test used as a companion diagnostic for PARP inhibitor therapy. Based on NGS of DNA from FFPE samples, the assay evaluates HRD by combining genomic instability score (GIS)—the unweighted sum of LOH, LST, and TAI—with BRCA1/2 mutation status.14 A GIS ≥42 and/or the presence of a pathogenic or likely pathogenic BRCA1/2 variant classifies a tumor as HRD-positive, while a GIS <42 is considered HRD-negative or HRP.2,43 To assess our VHIO-HRD score correlation with Myriad, 24 paired FFPE HGSOC tumors were used for parallel HRD testing. BRCA1 and BRCA2 status were also evaluated alongside HRD status determination.

The SOPHiA DDM™ Dx HRD is a decentralized, commercially available assay for local HRD assessment using shWGS. It combines a targeted capture panel of 28 HRR genes with shWGS at 1× depth.43 HRD status is determined by integrating BRCA1/2 mutation analysis with the Genomic Integrity Index (GII), derived from shWGS data. A GII ≥0 indicates an HRD-positive result.48 Notably, this assay shows high concordance (94.5%) with the MyChoice® CDx test, supporting its reliability as an alternative HRD assessment tool.49

LSTsh-HRD assessment, along with BRCA1 and BRCA2 evaluation, was performed on 44 FFPE HGSOC samples, using the SOPHiA DDM™ Dx HRD test.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graph generation were performed using the R programming language (version 4.1.3). Statistical testing was conducted with the rstatix package, and visualizations were created using ggplot2, ggpubr, ggalluvial, and GGally.

Differences in HRD score estimated across TF ranges and between methods and chemistries were evaluated using the Wilcoxon test. Associations between HRD scores calculated by different methods and libraries were assessed using Pearson correlation and/or parametric metrics performance.

Results

Workflow overview and HRD module development in the VHIO300

Within the framework of the VHIO MPP, we developed an HRD module into the VHIO300 test consisting of two independent HRD-induced scar quantification approaches: VHIO-HRD and LSTsh-HRD. HRD-related genomic scars were quantified from different sequencing data using distinct calculation approaches. LST, TAI, and LOH derived from targeted captured regions were integrated to generate the VHIO-HRD score, whereas LST counts from shWGS data were used to compute the LSTsh-HRD score.

VHIO-HRD cutoff determination and HRD detection strategies cross-validation

The analysis was performed on HGSOC samples from patients enrolled in the VHIO MPP (n = 119), generating a VHIO-HRD score distribution across the cohort. The cutoff for HRD status in the VHIO-HRD score was defined using the subset of BRCA1/2-deficient tumors within the cohort (n = 18), which were expected to be HRD-positive in 95% of the cases.14 The cutoff for HR deficiency was set at 47, the midpoint between 43 and 51, the two lowest scores in the BRCA1/2 mutant subgroup, representing 5% of this population (Figure 2A). Cutoff validity was confirmed in an independent HGSOC cohort (n = 43) and is shown in Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108242. After setting up the HRD scoring approaches, we benchmarked them against the established gold-standard tests: Myriad MyChoice® and SOPHiA DDM™ Dx HRD. The Pearson correlation between the hybrid-capture VHIO-HRD score (LSTVHIO-HRD + TAIVHIO-HRD + LOHVHIO-HRD) and the FDA-approved Myriad MyChoice® CDx was strong across 24 FFPE HGSOC tumor samples (r = 0.83; P = 5.6 × 10-7) (Figure 2B), with an overall agreement (OA) of 0.89 with respect to the gold standard (Table 1). To cross-validate the LSTsh-HRD score, 44 FFPE HGSOC samples were concurrently assessed with the SOPHiA DDM™ Dx HRD assay. Samples classified as HRD-positive or HRD-negative by LSTsh-HRD were concordantly classified with SOPHiA (Figure 2C), with an OA of 1.0 between the two assays (Table 2). Notably, during the quality-performance analysis, 10 of 44 (22.7%) cases were excluded, primarily due to BDL classification by LSTsh-HRD and not-evaluable outcomes on SOPHiA DDM™ Dx HRD resulting from suboptimal sample quality or process failures. Since the critical clinical outcome of HRD testing is HRD status classification, we next compared the agreement between categorical outcomes for both tests. As shown in Figure 3B, concordance between VHIO-HRD and LSTsh-HRD was 83.3% (50 of 60) for HRD-positive calls and 83.9% (26 of 31) for HRD-negative calls, yielding an OA of 83.5% (76 of 91) across the cohort. Notably, 12.1% (11 of 91) of the samples were assigned as BDL according to the LSTsh-HRD test.

In cases of discordance between the two strategies, HRD status could be determined by HRR gene alterations or RAD51 assays. In this study, 4.4% of cases (4 of 91) were discordant, including two harboring germline BRCA mutations. The remaining discordant cases lacking BRCA mutations (2.2%; 2 of 91) were considered inconclusive.

Overall, VHIO-HRD classified a higher proportion of samples as HRD-positive—65.9% (60 of 91) versus 57.1% (52 of 91) by LSTsh-HRD. Unlike LSTsh-HRD, VHIO-HRD has no BDL zone, providing definitive classification for all samples (91 of 91). Among the 11 BDL cases assigned by LSTsh-HRD, 72.7% (8 of 11) were classified as HRD-positive by VHIO-HRD.

TF limits HRD quantification

Accurate HRD quantification is critical for therapeutic decision making, but its reliability can be affected by sample TF.31,36, 37, 38 To examine the impact of TF on both VHIO-HRD (n = 212) and LSTsh-HRD (n = 114), samples with TF 0.2-0.39 were included in this analysis. HRD scores and classification outcomes stratified by sample TF ranges (0.2-0.39, 0.4-0.59, 0.6-0.79, and 0.8-1.0) are shown in Figure 4A-D. A statistically significant bias underestimating HRD scores was detected for VHIO-HRD in the 0.20-0.39 TF stratum (P < 0.0001). The same bias was observed for the individual components contributing to VHIO-HRD—LST, TAI, and LOH (Supplementary Figure S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108242). As TF increased, the percentage of HRD-positive samples observed by VHIO-HRD stabilized at ∼67% for TF ≥0.4 (Figure 4A and B). Hence, these findings support a minimum TF threshold of 0.4 for VHIO-HRD, below which reliable HRD scoring could not be achieved in 23.6% (50 of 212) of this cohort. In contrast, LSTsh-HRD showed no detectable bias across TF ranges (Figure 4C). Discussion

This study evaluated the implementation and clinical validation of two complementary HRD quantification methods, VHIO-HRD and LSTsh-HRD, embedded within the VHIO300 test in a real-world clinical diagnostic setting. Both approaches demonstrated suitability for HRD assessment, supporting their integration into clinical workflows, particularly in HGSOC and other HRD-associated tumors.

This study presents a comparative analysis of HRD detection approaches and, consistent with prior literature, highlights TF as a critical determinant of HRD assessment.31,36, 37, 38 VHIO-HRD delivered a robust cutoff and classified a larger proportion of HRD-positive tumors at TF levels ≥0.4. These data suggest a TF ≥0.4 threshold as the minimum tumor content required for reliable VHIO-HRD assessment. However, 23.6% of specimens fell below this threshold and were therefore not candidates for this method. By contrast, LSTsh-HRD exhibited greater sensitivity and broader applicability to low-cellularity specimens with lower TF (≥0.2), making it particularly suitable for challenging specimens with limited tumor content. Conversely, it yielded BDL outputs in 16.3% (8 of 49) of cases, introducing interpretive uncertainty associated with intermediate results. Although this has improved in version 2 through noise reduction and reduced BDL classifications, the method still lacks a publicly accessible code repository, which remains available only under license.

Collectively, our results underscore a trade-off between stringent TF requirements with definitive calls by VHIO-HRD and expanded eligibility with a higher frequency of indeterminate results by LSTsh-HRD. These findings highlight the strengths and constraints of currently well-established HRD detection options. Implementation of both methodologies in conjunction would improve success ratios in reporting HRD status for patients and eligibility for PARP inhibitors in HGSOC and other HRD-related tumors, a present requirement in real-life health care practice.

Limitations of the study

This study faces some inherent limitations. Among them, the absence of a universally accepted ground truth to unequivocally define HRD status remains a challenge across the field, as well as the lack of a standardized methodology for TF determination, which has been identified as a critical factor influencing HRD assessment. Additionally, while this work supports the relevance of proposed biomarkers and methodologies, prospective clinical validation would be necessary to robustly confirm their predictive value. These limitations, while outside the scope of this analysis, highlight important directions for future work and underscore the complexity of translating genomic signatures into routine clinical tools.

Conclusion

In conclusion, refining and improving our understanding of HRD detection methods—particularly through accurate and standardized TF estimation—has the potential to facilitate the implementation of the HRD biomarker in the routine clinical setting, which would significantly enhance patient selection for PARP inhibitor therapies, leading to improved outcomes in HGSOC and other HRD-associated tumors. Furthermore, the development of more sensitive assays, especially for samples with low tumor content or limited availability, is essential to reduce false negatives and support more informed treatment decisions in standard clinical practice.
