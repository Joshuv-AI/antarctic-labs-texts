Case reports of pregnancies in women with Shone syndrome: role for multidisciplinary planning
Authors: Joel Tiller, Rochelle Judd, Katherine H Hatter, Eliza McElwee, David Gregg, Stephanie S Gaydos, Luis Antonio Moreno-Ruiz, Melonie Johns, Sheila Vania Sánchez López, Edgar Garcia, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundShone syndrome is a complex congenital cardiac condition describing multiple obstructive left-sided heart lesions. We present four cases of pregnancies in three women with Shone syndrome and the role for preconception counseling and multidisciplinary delivery planning.Case summarySingle-centre chart review of pregnant women with Shone syndrome over 5 years, including maternal and foetal outcomes and delivery plans. There were four deliveries amongst three nulliparous women ages 25–31 years with prior surgical repairs for Shone syndrome. Multidisciplinary delivery planning occurred in the third trimester of each pregnancy. There were four term deliveries (vaginal deliveries for three, caesarean for one) with cardiac monitoring and adjustments to typical peripartum protocols. One patient required acute intensive care unit care for postpartum tachycardia and pulmonary oedema, and another required medical management of gestational hypertension. One infant required neonatal intensive care for respiratory distress. All patients and infants were discharged by postpartum Day 3 without early readmission. One patient developed acute decompensated heart failure at 7 months postpartum and died following cardiac arrest.DiscussionWomen with Shone syndrome possess multiple obstructive cardiac lesions often considered in isolation as high risk for cardiac events in pregnancy, though not completely captured in risk prediction algorithms and with heterogeneity requiring case-by-case management. Multidisciplinary delivery planning including cardiologists, obstetricians, and obstetric anaesthesiologists can safely navigate immediate maternal and neonatal care needs. Pre-conception counselling and close follow-up are imperative.Keywords: Cardio-obstetrics, Shone syndrome, Delivery-planning, Case series

Introduction

Shone syndrome, or Shone’s complex (SC), is a rare condition making up 0.6% of all congenital heart disease, first described by Shone in 1963.1 Shone’s complex is classically described as multiple obstructive left-sided heart lesions: supravalvular mitral membrane or ring, parachute mitral valve (MV), subvalvular aortic stenosis, bicuspid aortic valve (BAV), and coarctation of the aorta (CoA). There is frequently an anatomic spectrum with variability in severity of individual lesions. Early surgical intervention is common, and most patients require reoperation(s) throughout childhood. Lifelong congenital cardiac follow-up is recommended.

With surgical advancements, patients with SC typically survive into adulthood with over 90% living into the third decade.2 Many women pursue pregnancy; however, there are heightened risks for maternal cardiac events with certain SC lesions noted in pregnancy risk stratification tools.3–5 No existing risk algorithm captures SC specifically, nor the cumulative maternal or foetal risk of sequential left-sided obstructive cardiac lesions, which are particularly vulnerable to maladaptation to the physiologic changes of pregnancy. There is an underrepresentation of management approaches and outcomes for pregnant women with SC. We present a case review of four pregnancies of SC patients, emphasizing multidisciplinary delivery planning, maternal, and foetal outcomes.

Each pregnancy in this series was co-managed between Maternal Fetal Medicine and Adult Congenital Cardiology teams. Patient cardiac complexity was categorized by ‘ACHD AP Classification’, incorporating both anatomic and physiologic factors.6 Risks of cardiac events were estimated using modified World Health Organization (mWHO),7 ‘CARdiac disease in PREGnancy’ (CARPREG II),5 and +/−ZAHARA4 indices. A multidisciplinary delivery care plan conference occurred for each patient, including Adult Congenital Cardiology, Maternal Fetal Medicine, and Obstetric Anesthesia team members, with chart documentation.

Case presentations:

Patient 1, Pregnancy A

Patient 1 was a 26-year-old female with SC: subaortic membrane, BAV, and CoA, as well as partial atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD) with parachute left atrioventricular valve (LAVV). She had a prior subclavian flap repair of CoA, LAVV repair with primum atrial septal defect closure, subaortic membrane resection, and a modified Konno procedure for redo subaortic membrane re-resection (four total surgeries, last 3 years prior to pregnancy). She had pre-conception counselling, with additional clinical details in Table 1. Pre-pregnancy transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) showed mild LAVV stenosis with mild leaflet restriction, residual subaortic membrane with mildly stenotic gradient, and mild coarctation gradient (Table 2). She had normal aortic arch dimensions by fairly recent computed tomography angiography (CTA). Cardiology evaluation occurred during each trimester of her achieved pregnancy, and she remained asymptomatic. Foetal echocardiogram was normal. The only cardiac medication in pregnancy was metoprolol tartrate, initiated for palpitations and to prolong diastolic filling through the stenotic LAVV. The final TTE findings prior to delivery are presented in Table 2 and imaging in Figure 1. She had a soft systolic ejection murmur, quiet diastole, clear lungs, and no peripheral oedema. She presented in spontaneous labour at 38 weeks gestational age (WGA) and had a dural puncture epidural for analgesia with slow titration. She underwent an unassisted vaginal delivery with non-invasive cardiac monitoring (right upper extremity blood pressure cuff cycled every 1–3 min). There were no maternal cardiovascular or obstetric complications in the peripartum setting. The infant was healthy without cardiovascular issues though small for gestational age. Patient was well at 1-week and 3-month postpartum cardiac assessments. She declined the offered contraception.

Patient 1, Pregnancy B

The above patient achieved a repeat pregnancy (G4P1021 with interval spontaneous abortion) at age 28 years. Pre-conception cardiac evaluation included repeat CTA chest showing normal aortic arch dimensions and CoA repair site and treadmill stress echocardiogram. This showed increased mean LAVV gradient during exercise from 5 to 18 mmHg (resting to peak heart rate 71–162 b.p.m.), increased right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) from 23 to 45 mmHg, and mildly increased BAV velocity with exercise. Specific cardiac risks in pregnancy were reviewed with increased concern about LAVV dysfunction in a repeat pregnancy given significant pressure increases on stress echocardiogram. She elected to proceed and was New York Heart Association (NYHA) Class II at conception and throughout pregnancy with Cardiology reassessments each trimester and continuation of p.o. metoprolol tartrate. Pre-pregnancy and intrapartum echocardiographic findings are summarized in Table 2. She missed her recommended foetal echocardiogram. She developed gestational hypertension at 35 WGA, for which p.o. labetalol was initiated. Cardiac medications at delivery included both p.o. metoprolol tartrate and p.o. labetalol. She had a soft systolic ejection murmur, quiet diastole, clear lungs, and mild bilateral lower extremity oedema. Induction of labour occurred at 37 WGA, with arterial line placement and subsequent dural puncture epidural for analgesia with slow bolus titration and continuous maintenance infusion. She underwent an unassisted vaginal delivery with arterial blood pressure and telemetry monitoring. She experienced worsened postpartum hypertension and peripheral oedema requiring i.v. diuretics and p.o. labetalol up-titration before discharge. There were no obstetric complications. The infant was small for gestational age, had a healthy cardiovascular status, and required initial neonatal intensive care unit (ICU) care for positive pressure ventilation treating respiratory distress.

The patient denied symptoms at Cardiology outpatient visits at 1 week and 2 weeks postpartum, though hypertension medication adjustments were required. She has had a subsequent gap in Cardiology care. She completed her 6-week obstetric postpartum visit and declined early contraception; she later received contraception at 1 year postpartum. She had stable echocardiographic findings and was asymptomatic at 15 months postpartum.

Patient 2

Patient 2 was a 31-year-old with SC: subaortic membrane, BAV, mild unrepaired CoA, and partial AVSD with parachute LAVV with cleft. Surgical history included atrial septal defect closure, LAVV valvuloplasty with subaortic membrane resection, redo LAVV repair, and bioprosthetic aortic valve replacement (three total surgeries, most recently 11 years prior to pregnancy—see Table 1). Last echocardiogram was 2.5 years prior to pregnancy showing mild prosthetic aortic valve stenosis (Table 2). Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) of the chest at this time reported aortic root dilation to 4.3 cm, no coarctation complications, and normal left ventricular volume and systolic function. She had no preconception counselling due to a gap in Cardiology follow-up and was first seen by Adult Congenital Cardiology at 24 WGA with late referral, at which time she denied symptoms. Echocardiogram showed thickened LAVV leaflets to single papillary muscle with moderate stenosis, mild-to-moderate LAVV regurgitation, and moderate-to-severe prosthetic aortic valve stenosis (peak velocity 4.0 m/s, mean gradient 39 mmHg, dimensionless velocity index 0.44) without aortic insufficiency (Table 2; Figure 2). Multidisciplinary care meeting recommended repeat non-contrast MRA of the chest to measure aortic dimensions for delivery mode planning; this showed aortic root measurements of 3.9 × 3.3 cm (with intra-reader consistency comparing both MRAs). Pre-delivery echocardiogram showed similar valvar findings, though increased RVSP of 52 mmHg and unchanged left ventricle ejection fraction (LVEF%) (Table 2; images in Figure 3). She continued to deny cardiac limitations, even though she was admitted for threatened preterm labour (34 WGA) and found with significant lower extremity oedema, harsh systolic ejection murmur with soft diastolic rumble, and clear lungs. Labs showed elevated B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) (207 pg/mL). Low-dose diuretics were initiated. She appeared compensated in follow-up and denied symptoms, so delivery planning continued.

Teams planned for induction of labour and vaginal delivery at 38 WGA on the obstetric floor with early arterial blood pressure and telemetry monitoring, an assisted second stage, and postpartum recovery in cardiac ICU. The epidural initiation bolus was slowly titrated, followed by maintenance analgesia of a continuous infusion. She delivered with vacuum-assisted pushing. One hour postpartum, she developed tachycardia and hypotension treated with esmolol and phenylephrine. She was hypoxic with chest X-ray showing pulmonary oedema. She was transferred to the cardiac ICU for heart failure management with i.v. diuretics and low-dose metoprolol; she also received i.v. antibiotics for suspected endometritis. She returned to the postpartum floor within 24 h and was discharged home with a healthy newborn on postpartum Day 3 (infant born small for gestational age, normal foetal echocardiogram, and no postnatal cardiovascular concerns). The patient accepted the recommended contraception at 6 weeks postpartum.

She maintained close Cardiology follow-up with three ambulatory visits in the first 4 months postpartum and continued to deny cardiac symptoms. Surgical re-intervention options and timing were being discussed given persistently severe prosthetic aortic valve stenosis (mean gradient 51 mmHg and LAVV dysfunction on 4-month postpartum TTE). Surgery would be an extensive fourth-time median sternotomy with decisions regarding mechanical valve options, which would impact possible future pregnancies. Patient was contemplating her family planning with this decision in mind. However, prior to reintervention, she presented at 7 months postpartum in acute decompensated heart failure, was hospitalized, and died of cardiac arrest in the hospital with unsuccessful resuscitation effort.

Patient 3

Patient 3 was a 25-year-old with SC: supra-mitral ring, subaortic membrane, BAV, membranous ventricular septal defect (VSD), and CoA. Surgical history included neonatal left subclavian artery turndown coarctation repair and end-to-end anastomosis with subsequent VSD closure, subaortic membrane resection, supra-mitral ring resection (two total surgeries, most recently 8 years prior to pregnancy); see Table 1. Pre-pregnancy TTE showed moderate mitral stenosis, mild mitral regurgitation, mild aortic valve stenosis, unobstructed CoA site; see Table 2. There was no recent advanced thoracic imaging. Pre-conception and throughout pregnancy (achieved via intrauterine insemination), she remained asymptomatic without cardiac or obstetric hospitalizations. Pre-delivery echocardiogram showed an increased mean MV gradient of 15 mmHg and a stable mild aortic stenosis (Table 2). Multidisciplinary meeting recommended scheduled caesarean section delivery, based on concern of MV dysfunction and patient input when reviewing risks, with cardiac monitoring and postpartum ICU recovery. At the time of delivery, only cardiac medication was metoprolol succinate. Exam was pertinent only for a soft systolic ejection murmur. She underwent caesarean section at 39 WGA with pre-neuraxial radial arterial line placement and spinal anaesthesia. She remained haemodynamically stable on standard phenylephrine infusion throughout surgery and recovered in cardiac ICU for monitoring. There were no maternal cardiovascular, obstetric, or foetal complications in the peripartum setting. Foetal echocardiogram was normal. Mother and healthy infant were discharged home on postpartum Day 3. She had regular Cardiology follow-up at 1 week, 3 months, and 6 months postpartum and at regular intervals thereafter without postpartum cardiac complications.

Discussion

Cardiac complications during pregnancy with isolated ‘left-sided’ cardiac conditions are fairly well described, however far less so for women with multiple left-sided lesions and anatomic/physiologic variability such as those with SC. Pre-conception cardiac evaluation and counselling for women with SC is vital to review risks in pregnancy and consider pre-conception interventions to lower these risks. Once pregnancy occurs, multidisciplinary delivery planning can collaboratively review case-by-case variability and strategies to minimize maternal or foetal complications. The team should review patient clinical status and cardiac history, obstetric issues, disease-specific risks, and neonatal care needs (particularly with increased CHD recurrence risks in offspring for women with left-sided obstructive lesions such as SC).8 Delivery plans should be accessible in patient’s chart to all providers.

The normal cardiac physiologic changes of pregnancy—including increased stroke volume, heart rate, and cardiac output, accompanied by reduced systemic vascular resistance—can be poorly tolerated or met with inability to adapt in left-heart obstructive lesions and aortopathies. Note that ‘Pregnancy 2’, the only postpartum mortality, was the only case without augmented LVEF% on intrapartum TTE. Pre-conception exercise testing can non-invasively approximate haemodynamic adaptations to this higher-output state and contribute to risk assessment. More dramatic haemodynamic increases in heart rate, blood pressure, and cardiac output occur during labour and delivery. Women with aortopathies have heightened risks of aneurysm or dissection with untreated hypertension. Conversely, abrupt reductions in preload as can occur with postpartum haemorrhage or anaesthetic agents are poorly tolerated in significant subaortic or aortic stenosis. Arterial blood pressure monitoring can be advantageous in women with SC to assess either risk, typically in the right radial artery given frequent surgical history of coarctation repair rendering the left upper extremity blood pressure less accurate. Additionally, immediate postpartum preload increases related to uterine decompression and placental autotransfusion can elicit pulmonary oedema in significant mitral stenosis. Beta-blockers are often utilized in pregnancy to avoid tachycardia, which may be poorly tolerated with mitral stenosis, which retrospectively may have been beneficial for ‘Pregnancy 2’.

Team planning should review optimal delivery mode. Existing case reports of women with SC describe outcomes of predominantly caesarean sections, in contrast with our series with predominantly vaginal deliveries.9,10 Generally, vaginal deliveries are recommended for compensated women with cardiac disease.11 Assisted vaginal birth can be considered to minimize haemodynamic changes associated with the second stage of labour, though there are inconsistent indications for such. Planned induction of labour can ideally facilitate that a higher-risk delivery occur during hours with specialized team member availability. Caesarean sections are typically reserved for significant aortopathy (such as patients with BAV and rapid aortic enlargement or dimension of >4.5 cm), symptomatic severe valvar obstruction, decompensated heart failure, severe pulmonary hypertension, or use of oral anticoagulation and presentation in labour. Regardless, variability exists in these decisions. ‘Pregnancy 3’ did not meet caesarean indications and may have well-tolerated vaginal delivery. ‘Pregnancy 2’ seemed asymptomatic and compensated prior to delivery, though perhaps immediate outcome may have differed with a caesarean. With either delivery mode, modification of i.v. fluid protocols is often warranted to avoid iatrogenic volume overload, pulmonary oedema, or congestive heart failure.

Anaesthesia choices during labour and delivery are of equal importance. Medications that further exacerbate maternal tachycardia, including tocolytics, should be avoided in women with SC and significant mitral obstruction. Neuraxial anaesthesia can be successfully used with caution through utilizing low doses, slow titrations, and frequent assessments of the block to avoid precipitating hypotension and bradycardia, both suboptimal in left ventricular outflow tract obstruction.12 Teams should have vasopressors available, often phenylephrine, which avoids tachycardia.10 Combined spinal epidurals may be optimal in some cases. Careful selection of inhalation agents is relevant if general anaesthesia is indicated.

Lastly, contraception should be highlighted to women with SC by all care team members, to facilitate a planned pregnancy in low-risk patients or prevent pregnancy if risks seem prohibitive. This is a critical part of counselling. Options include barrier methods; combined hormonal or progesterone-only, long-acting reversible contraception; and permanent methods.
