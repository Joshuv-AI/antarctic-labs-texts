Small Loss Bounds for Online Learning Separated Function Classes: A Gaussian Process Perspective
Adam Block, Abhishek Shetty

Abstract
In order to develop practical and efficient algorithms while circumventing overly pessimistic computational lower bounds, recent work has been interested in developing oracle-efficient algorithms in a variety of learning settings. Two such settings of particular interest are online and differentially private learning. While seemingly different, these two fields are fundamentally connected by the requirement that successful algorithms in each case satisfy stability guarantees; in particular, recent work has demonstrated that algorithms for online learning whose performance adapts to beneficial problem instances, attaining the so-called small-loss bounds , require a form of stability similar to that of differential privacy. In this work, we identify the crucial role that separation plays in allowing oracle-efficient algorithms to achieve this strong stability. Our notion, which we term ρ \rho -separation, generalizes and unifies several previous approaches to enforcing this strong stability, including the existence of small-separator sets and the recent notion of γ \gamma -approximability. We present an oracle-efficient algorithm that is capable of achieving small-loss bounds with improved rates in greater generality than previous work, as well as a variant for differentially private learning that attains optimal rates, again under our separation condition. In so doing, we prove a new stability result for minimizers of a Gaussian process that strengthens and generalizes previous work.



1
Introduction

In order to satisfy modern resource constraints it is important to develop computationally efficient algorithms for learning. Unfortunately, many function classes of interest are computationally hard to learn, which prompts the search for algorithms that make efficient use of computational primitives, or oracle, that have heuristically motivated and practical implementation in settings of interest. To wit, a common such primitive in learning is the notion of an Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) Oracle, which grants one the ability to efficiently find a function that minimizes the empirical loss of some loss function on a given data set. This oracle is motivated both by it sufficiency for classical learning
(
Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David, 2014
, Chapter 4)
and its practical instantiation in a variety of settings of interest, such as deep neural networks
(
LeCun et al., 2015
)
.
While a single call to an ERM oracle on independent data suffices for classical learning, there are many alternative settings where the extent to which this oracle can aid learning is less well understood; two examples of such are online and differentially private learning.

In online learning, a learner receives adversarially generated data points one in a series of
T
rounds and, at each time step
t
, makes a prediction; the learner attempts to minimize his regret to the best function in a given class
\mathcal{F}
viewed in hindsight
(
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi, 2006
)
.
In addition to being an important learning setting in its own right, online learning algorithms are an important primitive in a number of other applications including sequential decision-making
(
Lattimore and Szepesvári, 2020
)
, algorithm design
(
Arora et al., 2012
)
, learning in games
(
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi, 2006
)
, and fairness
(
Haghtalab et al., 2023
)
.
While online learning is separated from classical learning by removing assumptions on the data, differentially private learning
(
Dwork et al., 2006
)
allows independent data but forces the algorithm to satisfy strong stability constraints that ensure that release of a trained model does not leak private information about the data on which it is trained. Due to the increasing deployment of machine learning models, often trained on sensitive customer data, privacy has seen wide application in many fields
(
Dwork et al., 2014
)
and has even been adopted by the US Census
(
Abowd, 2018
)
. While a priori these two settings may seem quite different, in fact they are intimately related because successful algorithms in both settings require some degree of
stability
. Indeed, a formal example of this connection is the fact that the combinatorial notion of complexity known as the
Littlestone dimension

(
Littlestone, 1988
;
Ben-David et al., 2009
)
characterizes learnability in both settings
(
Alon et al., 2019
;
Bun et al., 2020
)
.

Another way in which online and differentially private learning are connected is in the study of small loss (or
L^{\star}
) bounds in online learning
(
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi, 2006
, Section 2.4)
.
Typically, one expects regret for online learning to scale like
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T})
in the worst case. Often, however, one may expect
\mathcal{F}
to be chosen so that the
f\in\mathcal{F}
performing best in hindsight has total loss
L^{\star}=o(T)
; in such cases, one may hope for algorithms whose regret scales with
L^{\star}
instead of with
T
, automatically adapting to the beneficial problem instance. Prior work
(
Hutter and Poland, 2004
;
Abernethy et al., 2019
;
Wang et al., 2022
)
has identified differential privacy as an essential ingredient to proving
L^{\star}
bounds for online learning for a family of algorithms conducive to the application of an ERM oracle, but the generality in which one can efficiently achieve such guarantees are unknown.

In this work, we identify a new condition on function classes,
\rho
-separation
(
Definition

5
), that suffices to ensure oracle-efficient online and differentially private learning. Our condition unifies and generalizes several earlier approaches including small separator sets
Dudík et al. (2020)
;
Syrgkanis et al. (2016)
and
\gamma
-approximability
(
Wang et al., 2022
)
. The notion of
\rho
-separation requires that functions in
\mathcal{F}
are sufficiently distinguishable in the sense that distinct functions are not too close to each other in
L^{2}(\mu)
for some measure
\mu
; intuitively, this separation helps ‘boost’ stability in an
L^{2}
sense to differential privacy. Using
\rho
-separation, we demonstrate the following:

•

We present an oracle-efficient algorithm for online learning (
Theorem

1
) that attains a small-loss (
L^{\star}
) bounds whenever the function class is
\rho
-separated. We compare our regret to that of alternative oracle-efficient algorithms and find that our approach generalizes and, in many settings, improves upon existing methods.

•

We show that a variant of the algorithm used for online learning is capable of differentially private PAC-learning with optimal rates whenever
\mathcal{F}
is
\rho
-separated, improving on existing results in many cases.

In the course of our analysis, we prove a new stability bound for minimizers of Gaussian Processes (
Lemma

1
) that stengthens prior such bounds both in the precise notion of stability controlled and the generality of the result itself. In
Section

2
we state a number of prerequisite notions from learning theory as well as provide a formal definition of
\rho
-separation. In
Section

3
, we provide rigorous statements of our main results as well as the oracle-efficient algorithms achieving the stated bounds; we conclude in
Section

4
by providing the main ideas behind our proofs. We defer an extended discussion of related work to
Appendix

A
.

Prequisite Notions and
\rho
-Separation

We now recall prerequisite notions, define the main problems of study, and introduce the fundamental assumption underlying our work:
\rho
-separation.

2.1
Notions from Learning Theory

Recall that the fundamental premise of learning theory is to assume the learner has access to some function class
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
mapping contexts
\mathcal{X}
to labels
\mathcal{Y}
, where we always consider
\mathcal{Y}\subset[-1,1]
in this work. Given a loss function
\ell:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, the goal of the learner is to produce labels with minimal loss relative to the benchmark function class
\mathcal{F}
. A standard formal instantiation of this idea is the PAC-learning framework
Vapnik and Chervonenkis (1964)
;
Valiant (1984)
.

Definition 1

(PAC Learning)
.

Given n independent and identically distributed data points
(X_{i},Y_{i})
and a loss function
\ell
, an algorithm
\widehat{f}
depending on these data is said to
(\alpha,\beta)
-learn if for independent
(X,Y)
it holds that
\mathbb{P}\left(\ell(\widehat{f}(X),Y)\geq\alpha+\inf_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\ell(f(X),Y)\right)\leq\beta
.
The number of samples required to
(\alpha,\beta)
-learn is referred to as the sample complexity.

There are a wide variety of complexity measures of the function class, that dictate the sample considered.
In this work, we will work with the Gaussian complexity.

Definition 2

(Gaussian Complexity)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a class of functions from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathbb{R}
. For a fixed sample
S=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, the
empirical Gaussian complexity
of
\mathcal{F}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{S}(\mathcal{F})=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\,f(x_{i})\right],

(2)

where
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
are independent standard Gaussians.

The Gaussian complexity of a function class is related to a number of natural notions of function class size.
For example, if
\mathcal{F}
is finite, then
\mathcal{G}_{S}(\mathcal{F})\lesssim\sqrt{\log(\left|\mathcal{F}\right|)}

(
Boucheron et al., 2013
, Theorem 2.5)
.
Furthermore, the Gaussian complexity is bounded by the square root of the
VC dimension

(
Bartlett and Mendelson, 2001
, Theorem 6)
, which is the canonical measure of complexity in binary classification and is defined as follows
(
Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David, 2014
)
.

Definition 3

(VC Dimension)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a class of functions from a domain
\mathcal{X}
to
\{0,1\}
.
We say that
\mathcal{F}
shatters a set
X\subseteq\mathcal{X}
if for all
Y\subseteq X
, there exists a function
f\in\mathcal{F}
such that
f(x)=1
if
x\in Y
and
f(x)=0
otherwise.
The VC dimension of
\mathcal{F}
is the size of the largest set
X\subseteq\mathcal{X}
such that there exists a function
f\in\mathcal{F}
that shatters
X
.

Online Learning.

In contradistinction to PAC learning where data are iid and come all at once, in the online learning framework, a learner interacts with an adversarial environment over a sequence of rounds. At round
t
, the adversary selects a context
x_{t}
and a label
y_{t}
, while the leaner selects a hypothesis
f_{t}
from some
\mathcal{F}
; the context and label are then revealed to the learner, who suffers loss
\ell_{t}(f_{t})=\ell(f(x_{t}),y_{t})
, with the goal of minimizing
regret
over
T
rounds, defined to be

\displaystyle\reg_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(f_{t})-\inf_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(f).

(3)

Rates in online learning have been classically established in a number of settings
(
Ben-David et al., 2009
;
Rakhlin et al., 2011
;
Rakhlin et al., 2015
)
and the minimax rate for simple function classes typically scales like
O(\sqrt{T})
along with some complexity parameter of the class
\mathcal{F}
. Unfortunately, fully adversarial online learning is known to be strictly harder than PAC learning and its difficulty is characterized by the
Littlestone dimension

(
Littlestone, 1988
;
Ben-David et al., 2009
)
as opposed to the Gaussian complexity or VC dimension above. On the other hand, in some settings the minimax
\sqrt{T}
regret scaling may be overly pessimistic. For example, if the best hypothesis suffers a small loss
L^{\star}=\inf_{f}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(f)
, e.g. in the realizable setting where
L^{\star}=0
, we may hope to get algorithms whose regret scales only
logarithmically
in
T
and polynomially in
L^{\star}
, which is a vast improvement over the minimax framework. One of the main contributions of this work is to introduce an
efficient
algorithm capable of achieving this rate whenever
\mathcal{F}
satisfies a separation condition discussed in the sequel. We now discuss the other primary learning setting considered here.

Differential Privacy.

While online learning makes success more difficult by removing assumptions on the data, in differential privacy, we place additional requirements on the algorithm.
Motivated by data-privacy concerns, we consider the following notion of privacy
(
Dwork et al., 2006
)
.

Definition 4

(Differential Privacy)
.

An algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is said to satisfy
(\varepsilon,\delta)
(approximate) differential privacy if for all datasets
S_{1},S_{2}
differing in a single element, and all measurable sets
A
in the output space, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}(S_{1})\in A\right)\leq e^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}(S_{2})\in A\right)+\delta.

(4)

If
\delta=0
, we say that
\mathcal{A}
satisfies
\varepsilon
(pure) differential privacy.

The goal of private learning is to design an algorithm that is both an
(\alpha,\beta)
-learner and is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private with the number of samples scaling as
\mathrm{poly}\left(1/\alpha,1/\varepsilon,\log(1/\beta),\log(1/\delta)\right)
.
Recent work has demonstrated that differentially private learning is intimately connected with online learning in that the ability to succeed in either framework is characterized not by the Littlestone dimension mentioned above
(
Alon et al., 2019
)
and
L^{\star}
bounds in online learning arise from private online algorithms
(
Hutter and Poland, 2004
;
Abernethy et al., 2019
;
Wang et al., 2022
)
.

Oracle Efficiency.

In both the online and private learning frameworks, due to practical resource constraints, we are interested in computationally efficient algorithms; unfortunately, for most cases of interest, learning is computationally intractable even for simple concept class such as halfspaces (see
(
Tiegel, 2022
)
and references therein).
To circumvent pessimistic fact and develop theory that incorporate the success of practical heuristics, we consider the notion of oracle efficiency
(
Kalai and Vempala, 2005
)
. In aprticular, we suppose that the learner has access to an
Empirical Risk Minimization Oracle
for the class
\mathcal{F}
such that, given loss functions
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k}
and data points
(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{k},y_{k})
can efficiently return the minimizer of the cumulative loss:

\displaystyle\widehat{f}\in\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\ell_{i}(f(x_{i}),y_{i}).

(5)

The time complexity of a given algorithm is the number of oracle calls the algorithm makes multiplied by the number of inputs to each oracle call. Our algorithms consider the special case where
k=n+m
, the
\ell_{1}=\cdots=\ell_{n}=\ell
and for
n<i\leq n+m
,
\ell_{i}(f(x_{i}),y_{i})=\xi_{i}f(x_{i})
for
\xi_{i}
a gaussian.

Additional Notation.

We denote by
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{m}
the empirical
L^{2}
norm on
m
points, assumed to be given and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1}
,
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{2}
the (unnomralized)
\ell^{1}
and Euclidean norms on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We use
f=\mathcal{O}(g)
to say that
f/g
is bounded and
f=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(g)
to mean that
f/g
is bounded by polylogarithmic factors of the inputs to
f,g
; we use
f\lesssim g
if
f=\mathcal{O}(g)
.

2.2
Key Assumption:
\rho
-Separation

In this section, we will introduce the key assumption underlying our work:
\rho
-separation.

Definition 5
.

Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
be a function class. We call a measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}
a
\rho
-separating measure
for
\mathcal{F}
if for all
f,g\in\mathcal{F}
such that
f\neq g
, we have
\left\|f-g\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\geq\rho
. We abuse notation and say a set of points
\left\{z_{1},\dots,z_{m}\right\}\subset\mathcal{X}
is
\rho
-separating if the empirical measure is
\rho
-separating.

In essence,
\rho
-separation ensures that no two functions in
\mathcal{F}
are too close together, which allows boosting stability in
L_{2}
into differential privacy guarantees. To gain intuition, note that
\rho
-separation is implied for finite function classes by a natural spectral property of the function class.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a finite function class, let
z_{1},\dots,z_{m}\in\mathcal{X}
be points, and consider the matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times\left|\mathcal{F}\right|}
whose columns are
(f(z_{i}))
. Letting
\sigma
be the minimal singular value of
A
, we have that
\mathcal{F}
is
\rho
-separated by the empirical measure on
\mathcal{X}
with
\rho=\sigma\sqrt{2/m}
.

We defer a proof of this result to
Section

D.3
, which is a simple consequence of the definition of a singular value.
Such spectral properties of function classes are appear naturally in signals processing
(
Vetterli et al., 2014
)
, wavelet theory
(
Stephane, 1999
)
, frame theory, and harmonic analysis
(
Benedetto, 2020
)
.
Learning with respect such function classes corresponds to the natural problem of recovering the large coefficient in a signal decomposition.
As a concrete example, if the function class corresponds to the Fourier basis (which it is easy to see satisfies our separation assumption), the learning problem corresponds to recovering the large Fourier coefficients of a signal.

Furthermore,
\rho
-separation can be seen as a generalization of the notion of a separator set
Goldman et al. (1993)
, which has previously been used in the context of oracle efficient learning
(
Dudík et al., 2020
;
Neel et al., 2019
;
Block et al., 2024a
)
.

Definition 6

(Separator Set)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{X}\to\left\{\pm 1\right\}
be a function class.
We call a set
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}
a separator set for
\mathcal{F}
if for all
f,g\in\mathcal{F}
such that
f\neq g
, there exists
x\in S
such that
f(x)\neq g(x)
.

In the special case of binary classification, if
S
is a separator set then it yields a
\rho
-separating measure with
\rho=1/\sqrt{\left|S\right|}
. Furthermore, in this special case, while the existence of a separating measure itself does not directly bound the complexity of a function class, under the additional assumption that the function class is learnable,
we have that
\rho
-separation implies finiteness as well as .
Further, we can show that the existence of
\rho
-separating measure implies the existence of a separator set.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a learnable, binary-valued function class and suppose there exists some measure
\mu
that
\rho
-separates
\mathcal{F}
. Then
\mathcal{F}
is finite and has a separating set of size polynomial in
1/\rho
.

We defer a proof of this result to
Section

D.3
, and note that it shows that, at least in the binary-valued case,
\rho
-separation and the existence of a small separator set are qualitatively equivalent, although we will see that quantitatively they can lead to different rates.
We emphasize that
Proposition

2
implies that if
\mathcal{F}
is
\rho
-separated and PAC-learnable, then it is both online- and privately-learnable by finiteness in an information theoretic sense, without regard to computation time.
The primary focus of our paper is demonstrating that this learning can be achieved (oracle-)efficiently.

3
Main Results

In this section, we present our main results.
First, we will look at the small loss regret bounds for oracle efficient algorithms.
We present nearly optimal regret bounds under the assumption that the function class is well-separated, unifying and improving on prior work. Second, we will provide an oracle-efficient algorithm capable of achieving differentially private learning under separation that matches the sample complexity of a non-private learning algorithm, up to the standard
\varepsilon^{-1}
blow up due to privacy, improving upon the results of
Block et al. (2024a)
.

3.1
An Oracle-Efficient Algorithm with a Small-Loss Bound

Input:

Function class
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
,
horizon
T
,
loss function
\ell
,

\rho
-separating set
\mathcal{Z}=\left\{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m}\right\}
,
noise level
\eta>0
,
initial loss
L_{0}(f)=0

for

1\leq t\leq T

do

//
Step 1: Sample Gaussian Perturbation

Draw
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)

Compute perturbation:
\omega_{t}(f)=m^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}f(Z_{i})

//
Step 2: Select Predictor

Set
f_{t}\leftarrow\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}}L_{t-1}(f)+\eta\cdot\omega_{t}(f)

//
Step 3: Play and Update Loss

Observe
x_{t}
, play
\hat{y}_{t}=f_{t}(x_{t})
, and observe
y_{t}

Incur loss
\ell(\hat{y}_{t},y_{t})

Update loss:
L_{t}(f)=L_{t-1}(f)+\ell(f(x_{t}),y_{t})
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}

end for

Algorithm 1

Follow the Perturbed Leader with Gaussian Perturbation

We now present our first main result, a new, oracle-efficient algorithm for online learning capable of achieving a adaptive regret for bounded, separated function classes
\mathcal{F}
. Our algorithm is an instantiation of Follow the Perturbed Leader
(
Kalai and Vempala, 2005
)
and appears (with differently tuned parameters) in
Block et al. (2022)
;
Block et al. (2024a)
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{X}\to[-1,1]
be a function class and let
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\mathcal{F}
is
\rho
-separated by the empirical measure on the
Z_{i}
. Let
\ell
be a loss function bounded in
[-B,B]
and suppose the learner plays according to
Algorithm

1
, i.e. at each time
t
, plays

\displaystyle f_{t}\in\argmin L_{t-1}(f)+\eta\cdot\omega_{t}(f),\qquad\text{where}\qquad\omega_{t}(f)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}f(Z_{i}),

(6)

with
\xi_{i}
standard Gaussian random variables,
L_{t}
the cumulative loss until time
t
, and
\eta>0
a regularization parameter. For some choice of
\eta
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\reg_{T}\right]\leq 1+\frac{16\sqrt{B\log\left(2T\left|\mathcal{F}\right|\right)L^{\star}}}{\rho}+\frac{64B\log(2T\left|\mathcal{F}\right|)}{\rho^{2}}.

(7)

Note that in the regime where the separation
\rho
is constant, an example of which we furnish in the sequel, we attain regret
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\log(\left|\mathcal{F}\right|)\cdot L^{\star}}+\log(\left|\mathcal{F}\right|)\right)
, which is optimal up to logarithmic factors of
T

(
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi, 2006
)
. Furthermore, we require only a single oracle call per round with a dataset of size
\mathcal{O}(T+m)
. In the worst case, when no function in
\mathcal{F}
performs well in cumulative loss,
L^{\star}\approx T
and we recover a regret scaling as
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{T\log(\left|\mathcal{F}\right|)}/\rho\right)
, which is optimal for constant
\rho
; thus, if we can choose
m
to be small, we achieve a new oracle-efficient regret bound.

As above, the downside of
Theorem

1
is that we require
\mathcal{F}
to be
\rho
-separated, which limits the generality of the bound, although we now highlight several key ways in which this separation can be achieved. The first way is to construct new points
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m}
that explicitly separate the function class
\mathcal{F}
. As we are assuming that
\mathcal{F}
is finite, we can simply introduce points
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{\left|\mathcal{F}\right|}
such that
f^{\prime}(Z_{f})=\mathbb{I}\left[f=f^{\prime}\right]
for all
f,f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
. The downside of this approach is twofold: first, this requires modifying the assumption on the oracle, but, more importantly, it requres feeding in
\mathcal{O}(\left|\mathcal{F}\right|)
points to the oracle per round, precluding any hope of efficiency in the typical regime where we expect
\mathcal{F}
to be exponentially large. This polynomial in
\left|\mathcal{F}\right|
dependence is expected due to the computational lower bound of
Hazan and Koren (2016)
. By significantly strengthening the oracle, we can avoid this issue, as we can back
\left|\mathcal{F}\right|
points into a sphere of dimension
\log(\left|\mathcal{F}\right|)
with constant separation; while this solves the oracle-efficiency issues, it is not at all clear how to instantiate the oracle in practice, even heuristically.
A less general, but more optimistic, way to construct points
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m}
separating
\mathcal{F}
is to assume sample access to some measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}
that
\rho
-separates
\mathcal{F}
. In this case, standard uniform concentration bounds imply that we can take
m=\mathcal{O}\left(\log(\left|\mathcal{F}\right|)/\rho^{2}\right)
(cf.
Lemma

7
), which implies that achieving average regret
\varepsilon
is possible with
\mathrm{poly}\left(\log(\left|\mathcal{F}\right|),\rho^{-1},\varepsilon^{-1}\right)
time.

A third instance in which
\rho
-separation holds is in the presence of a
small separator set
(cf.
Definition

6
)
(
Goldman et al., 1993
;
Syrgkanis et al., 2016
)
. Because the existence of a separator set of size
m
implies that
\mathcal{F}
is
\rho
-separated with
\rho=m^{-1/2}
, and
L^{\star}\leq T
, we see that (
6
) achieves expected regret
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(m\log(\left|\mathcal{F}\right|)+\sqrt{mT\log(\left|\mathcal{F}\right|)}\right)
, which shaves a
m^{1/4}
factor from the dominant term in the bound of
Syrgkanis et al. (2016, Theorem 2)
. A related condition, termed
\delta
-admissability, is introduced in
Dudík et al. (2020)
, where they use this condition to achieve an oracle-efficient regret bound using a different variant of FTPL; we remark that their condition implies that
\mathcal{F}
is
\rho
-separated for a set of size
m
with
\rho=\delta/\sqrt{m}
and thus, with the same number of calls to the oracle with the same size set of inputs, our regret becomes (using the
\delta
from
Dudík et al. (2020)
)
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(m\log\left(\left|\mathcal{F}\right|\right)/\delta^{2}+\sqrt{mT}/\delta\right)
, shaving a factor of
\sqrt{m}
from that of
Dudík et al. (2020, Theorem 2.5)
. We emphasize that neither the result of
Syrgkanis et al. (2016)
nor that of
Dudík et al. (2020)
can adapt to the small loss setting where
L^{\star}=o(T)
, but we see that even outside of this regime,

Analysis Techniques

In this section we sketch the proofs of our main results above. We begin with the technical result underlying our main theorems, which establishes a stability property for Gaussian processes. Then, in

, we prove

4.1
An Improved Gaussian Process Stability Bound

We now describe the key Gaussian process stability bound on which our results rely. This bound is a stronger form of the bounds found in
Block et al. (2022)
;
Block et al. (2024a)
, which conclude that approximate minimizers of a Gaussian process are, in some sense, close to the true minimizer with reasonable probability. We now formally define a Gaussian process.

Definition 8

(Gaussian Process)
.

Let
T
be an index set.
A collection of random variables
\{\omega(t)\}_{t\in T}
is a Gaussian process if for all finite subsets
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
, the random vector
\{\omega(t)\}_{t\in T^{\prime}}
has a multivariate Gaussian distribution.
The function
m:T\to\mathbb{R}
given by
m(t)=\mathbb{E}[\omega(t)]
is called the mean function of the Gaussian process and
the function
K:T\times T\to\mathbb{R}
given by
K(t,t^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}[(\omega(t)-m(t))(\omega(t^{\prime})-m(t^{\prime}))]
is called the covariance kernel of the Gaussian process.

We recall that a Gaussian process on an index set
T
can be fully characterized by its mean function
m:T\to\mathbb{R}
and covariance kernel
K:T\times T\to\mathbb{R}
, and define a Gaussian process now. The covariance kernel in particular can be interpreted as a measure of distance between points in
T
, with two points
s,t\in T
being considered close if
K(s,t)\approx K(t,t)
.
In particular, define the distance between two points
s,t\in T
as
\left\|s-t\right\|=\sqrt{K(s,s)+K(t,t)-2K(s,t)}
.
We use the norm notation though
T
need not be a vector space; this is standard notation and can be justified using standard representation theorems for Gaussian processes.

The results of
Block et al. (2022)
;
Block et al. (2024a)
involve
approximate minimizers
of a Gaussian process
\Omega:T\to\mathbb{R}
with mean function
m
and covariance kernel
\eta^{2}\cdot K
for some variance parameter
\eta>0
, where
t\in T
is an approximate minimizer if
\Omega(t)\leq\Omega(t^{\star})+\tau
for some tolerance
\tau>0
for
t^{\star}=\argmin\Omega(s)
. In particular, those results say that assuming the covariance kernel is well conditioned in the sense that
\sup_{t}K(t,t)/\inf_{t}K(t,t)
is not too large, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists t\in T\text{ s.t. }\Omega(t)\leq\Omega(t^{\star})+\tau\text{ and }\nicefrac{{K(s,t^{\star})}}{{K(t^{\star},t^{\star})}}\leq 1-\rho^{2}\right)\lesssim\frac{\tau\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in T}\Omega(t)-m(t)\right]}{\rho^{2}\eta^{2}}.

(11)

The upper bound on the probability that there exist approximate minimizers of
\Omega
far from the true minimizer scales naturally with the tolerance
\tau
and distance
\rho
as well as the size of
T
, as measured by the Gaussian complexity, suggesting that the larger an index set is, the more likely it is that far away approximate minimizers exist. Moreover, as the scale of the noise, parameterized by
\eta
, increases, the probability of far away approximate minimizers decreases, reflecting the fact that the true minimizer becomes less dependent on the mean function
m
.

For learning theory applications, we can instantiate (
11
) in the case where
T=\mathcal{F}
is a function class,
\omega
is a centred Gaussian process with covariance kernel
K(f,f^{\prime})=\left\langle f,f^{\prime}\right\rangle_{m}
, the empirical inner product on a dataset
\left\{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m}\right\}
, and
\Omega(f)=m(f)+\eta\cdot\omega(f)
for some mean function
m
. Given an alternative mean function
m^{\prime}
such that
\sup_{f}\left|m(f)-m^{\prime}(f)\right|\leq\tau
, we can define the offset process
\Omega^{\prime}(f)=m^{\prime}(f)+\eta\cdot\omega(f)
and let
f^{\star^{\prime}}=\argmin\Omega^{\prime}(f)
. Applying (
11
) and some rearranging then implies that with reasonable probability,

\displaystyle\|f^{\star}-f^{\star^{\prime}}\|_{m}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\tau\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f}\omega(f)\right]}{\eta},

(12)

i.e., minimizers of Gaussian processes with similar mean functions are close in
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{m}
. While powerful, unfortunately (
11
) and its corollary (
12
) do not yield sufficiently strong guarantees to establish small loss bounds for online learning, nor does it immediately yield a differentially private algorithm. Instead, as explained in the sequel, a stronger guarantee is necessary to establish these results.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function class and
\mu
a measure on
\mathcal{F}
with
\omega:\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}
the canonical Gaussian process associated to this space; further assume that
\mu
is a
\rho
-separating measure on
\mathcal{F}
in the sense that
\left\|f-f^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\geq\rho
for all
f\neq f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
.
Let
m,m^{\prime}:\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}
be two mean functions satisfying
\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|m(f)-m^{\prime}(f)\right|\leq\tau
. If
f^{\star}=\argmin_{f}\Omega(f)
, where
\Omega(f)=m(f)+\eta\cdot\omega(f)
and
f^{\star^{\prime}},\Omega^{\prime}
are defined similarly then as long as

\displaystyle\eta\geq\frac{32\tau}{\rho^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\omega(f)\right]+\sqrt{2\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}\right),

(13)

it holds for any
f
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(f^{\star}=f\right)\leq\left(1+\frac{32\tau}{\eta\rho^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\omega(f)\right]+\sqrt{2\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}\right)\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(f^{\star^{\prime}}=f\right)+\delta.

(14)

In contradistinction to (
11
),
Lemma

1
requires an additional assumption of
separation
on the function class
\mathcal{F}
, which cannot be dropped in general because its application to private learning, which is only possible with finite Littlestone dimension
(
Alon et al., 2019
)
. What is gained from this reduction in generality, however, is the fact that the guarantee on stability is significantly stronger than that of (
12
) in that it provides fine-grained control on the
distributional
stability of the approximate minimizers, which is essential for our applications. Furthermore, we eliminate the assumption of well-conditionedness on the covariance kernel, which substantially increases the generality of application of this result, in particular by allowing functions with arbitrarily small norm.

While we defer a full proof of
Lemma

1
to
Appendix

B
, we sketch the argument here. The proof begins in the same way as that of
Block et al. (2022)
;
Block et al. (2024a)
, by introducing the ‘bad’ event
\mathcal{E}(\rho,\tau)
corresponding to the existence of a
\tau
-approximate minimizer existing at lesat distance
\rho
from the true minimizer. In this first step we additionally assume that the covariance kernel
K
is well-conditioned in the sense that
1\geq K(f,f)\geq\kappa^{2}>0
for some
\kappa
, a requrement we later drop. As in those earlier bounds, we then fix an arbitrary
y\in\mathbb{R}
and condition on the event that
f^{\star}=t
and
\Omega(f^{\star})=y
; unlike those earlier works, however, we also require conditioning on the event
\Phi_{\delta}
, occuring with probability at least
1-\delta
, that the supremum of
\omega
is not too much larger than its expectation, the reason for which we explain below. As
\Phi_{\delta}
occurs with high probability, we may condition on this event without losing more than a constant in the final bound; similarly, we will ignore the ‘good’ event
\mathcal{E}^{c}(\rho,\tau)
, as the resulting probability of this event will appear on the right hand side of (
14
). The proof then uses a fundamental fact of Gaussian Processes: the covariance kernel of a pinned Gaussian Process does not depend on the value at which this process is pinned. After careful analysis using several more properties specific to Gaussian Processes, we conclude

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(f^{\star}=f\text{ and }\mathcal{E}(\rho,\tau)\text{ and }\Phi_{\delta}\right)\lesssim\frac{\tau}{\eta\kappa^{2}\rho^{2}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\omega(f^{\star})\mathbb{I}[\Phi_{\delta}]|t^{\star}=t\right]\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(t^{\star}=t\right).

(15)

It is here that relying on
\Phi_{\delta}
is essential, as under
\Phi_{\delta}
, it holds that
\omega(f^{\star})
is not so large, even conditioned on the possibly low probability event that
f^{\star}=f
. A stronger version of (
11
) follows after rearranging terms. In order to remove the dependence on
\kappa
, we introduce an auxiliary process
\widetilde{\Omega}
by adding additional points
\widetilde{Z}_{i}
such that
f(\widetilde{Z}_{i})=1
for all
f
. Adding enough of these points ensures that the auxiliary process is well-conditioned and careful comparisons of
\widetilde{\Omega}
to
\Omega
as well as a sharp understanding of the relationship between the induced covariance kernel and the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|
on
\mathcal{F}
allows us to conclude the desired result. Note that the introduction of this auxiliary process is a subtle point as one
cannot use this approach to remove the
\kappa
parameter from
(
11
) because the resulting separation between points as measured by correlation is hurt by the indroduction of these auxiliary points; in order for this approach to work, we carefully relate the norm induced on
\mathcal{F}
by
\widetilde{\Omega}
to that by
\Omega
, which is significantly better behaved than the correlation. With the main technical step thus proved, we now apply this result to online learning.

4.2
Online Learning Proofs

While a full proof of
Theorem

1
can be found in
Appendix

C
, we sketch the main argument here and present the full algorithm.
The proof proceeds by applying
Lemma

1
to the well-known ‘Be-the-Leader’ lemma from
Kalai and Vempala (2005)
;
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi (2006)
, which states that ‘Follow-the-Leader’ style algorithms have regret that can be bounded by the stability of the predictions. In particular, the lemma concludes that if
f_{t}
is chosen as in (
6
), then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\reg_{T}\right]\leq 2\eta\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\omega(f)\right]+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\ell_{t}(f_{t})-\ell_{t}(f_{t+1})\right].

(16)

Here, the first term measures the ‘bias,’ i.e., how large the perturbation is, while the second term measures the stability of the predictions. For Lipschitz
f
, it suffices to control the stability of predictions measured by the
L^{2}
norm of the perturbation, as was done for the case of smoothed data in
Block et al. (2022)
. In order to realize
L^{\star}
bounds, however,
Hutter and Poland (2004)
used a stronger notion of stability akin to differential privacy, which was then further explored in
Wang et al. (2022)
. This stronger notion of stability is precisely what is controlled by
Lemma

1
; thus, we apply that result
with
\delta=\nicefrac{{1}}{{T\cdot\left|\mathcal{F}\right|}}
and, bounding the Gaussian complexity by
\sqrt{\log(\left|\mathcal{F}\right|)}

(
Boucheron et al., 2013
)
, we sum over
f\in\mathcal{F}
and see that for sufficiently large
\eta
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\ell_{t}(f_{t})-\ell_{t}(f_{t+1})\right]\lesssim\frac{1}{T}+\frac{B\sqrt{\log(T\left|\mathcal{F}\right|)}}{\eta\rho^{2}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\ell_{t}(f_{t+1})\right].

(17)

Plugging the preceding display into (
16
), we observe that the regret is bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\reg_{T}\right]\lesssim 1+\eta\cdot\sqrt{\log(\left|\mathcal{F}\right|)}+\frac{B\sqrt{\log(T\left|\mathcal{F}\right|)}}{\eta\rho^{2}}\cdot\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\ell_{t}(f_{t+1})\right].

(18)

The result concludes by controlling the summation in the last term by
L^{\star}
, which is another consequence of the Be-the-Leader Lemma, and balancing
\eta
subject to (
13
).

.

(22)

Simplifying the above expression using the identity
1+x\leq e^{x}
, we get for any measurable set

that

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\hat{f}(S)\in O\Bigr)\;\leq\;e^{\varepsilon}\,\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\hat{f}(S^{\prime})\in O\Bigr)+\delta,

(23)

as required.
∎

It remains to be shown that the output of the algorithm

2
is still accurate on the test distribution.
In order to reason about this, the key quantity to control is the size of the perturbation term.
Fortunately, since we are using Gaussian perturbations, this is naturally bounded in terms of the noise level
\eta
and the Gaussian complexity of the function class which in turn bounds the generalization error of the ERM procedure even if the Gaussian perturbation were not present.

Lemma 3

(Accuracy of Perturbed ERM)

Let
\beta,\eta>0

The output of the

2
satisfies

accuracy as long as the number of samples

satisfies

\displaystyle n\geq\max\left\{\frac{\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{F})^{2}+\log(1/\beta)}{\alpha^{2}},\eta\frac{\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{F})+\sqrt{\log(1/\beta)}}{\alpha}\right\}.

(24)

Proof.

Note that from standard uniform convergence bound in terms of Gaussian complexity, we have that with probability at least
1-\beta
, that for all
f\in\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f(x_{i}),y_{i})-\mathbb{E}\ell(f(x),y)\right|\leq\alpha

as long as

.
Thus, for the optimizer
\hat{f}
from
Algorithm

2
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\ell(\hat{f}(x),y)\leq\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}\ell(\hat{f}(x),y)+\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{\eta}{n\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}f(z_{i})+\alpha

From Gaussian concentration for Lipschitz functions

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{\eta}{n\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}f(z_{i})+\frac{\eta}{n}\sqrt{\log(1/\beta)}.

(27)

Note that this is the same as the Gaussian complexity of the function class
\mathcal{F}
.
∎

Plugging this in (and simplifying) with the choice of
\eta
from the privacy guarantee from
Lemma

2
, we get the following result we get that

\displaystyle n\geq\max\left\{\frac{\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{F})^{2}+\log(1/\beta)}{\alpha^{2}},\frac{\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{F})^{2}+\sqrt{\log(1/\beta)\log(1/\delta)}}{\alpha\varepsilon\rho^{2}}\right\}

(28)

as required.

Appendix A
Related Work

Oracle-Efficiency in online learning.

In order to better develop algorithms for online learning, and motivated by the success of optimization in more classical paradigms,
Kalai and Vempala [2005]
introduced the notion of oracle-efficient online learning with the
Follow the Perturbed Leader
family of algorithms. This approach to online learning serves to stabilize the most naïve approach to prediction, i.e. finding the function that performs best in hindsight and playing this, in order to ensure good performance against adversarial data; subsequent work within this framework
[
Dudík et al., 2020
,
Syrgkanis et al., 2016
,
Wang et al., 2022
,
Agarwal et al., 2019
,
Suggala and Netrapalli, 2020
]
has significantly generalized the application of these techniques. One particularly relevant work is that of
Hutter and Poland [2004]
, which made a connection between small loss bounds and a stability condition closely resembling differential privacy; this connection was then made explicit in
Abernethy et al. [2019]
. While oracle-efficiency allows for the devlopment of a powerful suite of algorithms,
unfortunately,
Hazan and Koren [2016]
showed that in the worst-case, oracle efficiency cannot be achieved for general function classes and adversarial data.
In order to circumvent this, a recent line of work has focused on structured instances such as smoothed online learning
[
Haghtalab et al., 2020
,
Haghtalab et al., 2022a
,
Haghtalab et al., 2022b
,
Block et al., 2022
,
Bhatt et al., 2023
,
Block and Polyanskiy, 2023
,
Block et al., 2023
,
Block et al., 2024b
,
Block et al., 2024c
]
and has constructed oracle-efficient algorithms for a variety of settings.
Our work has a conceptually similar goal of circumventing the worst-case hardness of oracle efficiency by focusing on a structured class of functions,
\rho
-separated functions, and constructing oracle-efficient algorithms for online learning. Unlike these other works, we primarily focus on
L^{\star}
bounds as opposed to minimax regret. Recently,
Wang et al. [2022]
also studied
L^{\star}
bounds in the oracle-efficient setting, and we compare our results to theirs in
Section

3
.

Oracle-Efficiency in differentially private learning.

As in online learning, the using the framework of oracle efficiency to develop practical algorithms for differentially private learning has been an active area of research
[
Neel et al., 2019
,
Neel et al., 2020
,
Vietri et al., 2020
,
Gaboardi et al., 2014
,
Nikolov et al., 2013
,
Block et al., 2024a
]
and the connections between online learning and differential privacy have been extensively explored
[
Alon et al., 2019
,
Bun et al., 2020
,
Abernethy et al., 2019
]
. Of particular relevance to our work are the works of
Neel et al. [2020]
,
Block et al. [2024a]
; the former develops oracle-efficient, differentially private learners for function classes with a small separator set (which our results generalize), while the latter uses a similar algorithmic technique as ours to develop oracle-efficient algorithms that makes use of some public data, building upon work in semi-private learning
Beimel et al. [2015]
,
Bassily et al. [2019]

Lemma 4
.

Let
T
be a set,
K
a covariance kernel, and suppose that

is separable with respect to the metric induced by
K
. Suppose that there is some
0<\kappa\leq K(t,t)\leq 1
for all

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\rho,\tau)=\left\{\text{there exists }s\in T,\text{ s.t. }\frac{K(s,t^{\star})}{K(t^{\star},t^{\star})}\leq 1-\rho^{2}\text{ and }\Omega(s)\leq\Omega(t^{\star})+\tau\right\}.

(29)

Then, for any

it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(t^{\star}=t\right)\leq\left(1+\frac{2\tau}{\eta\kappa^{2}\rho^{2}}\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\omega(f)\right]+\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}\right)\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(t^{\star}=t\text{ and }\mathcal{E}(\rho,\tau)^{c}\right)+\delta,

(30)

whenever

\displaystyle\eta\geq\frac{2\tau}{\kappa^{2}\rho^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in T}\omega(t)\right]+\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}\right).

(31)

Proof.

We modify the proof of
Block et al. [2024a, Theorem 5]
which in turn is an improvement on
Block et al. [2022]
. First, for
\delta>0
, let

\displaystyle\Phi_{\delta}=\left\{\sup_{t\in T}\omega(t)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in T}\omega(t)\right]+\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}\right\}.

(32)

By standard Gaussian tailbounds (see, e.g.,
Vershynin [2018, Theorem 8.5.5]
), it holds that
\mathbb{P}(\Phi_{\delta})\geq 1-\delta
. Now, a union bound implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(t^{\star}=t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(t^{\star}=t\text{ and }\mathcal{E}(\rho,\tau)^{c}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(t^{\star}=t\text{ and }\mathcal{E}(\rho,\tau)\text{ and }\Phi_{\delta}\right)+\delta.

(33)

We focus on the middle term and show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(t^{\star}=t\text{ and }\mathcal{E}(\rho,\tau)\text{ and }\Phi_{\delta}\right)\leq\frac{2\tau}{\eta\kappa^{2}\rho^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in T}\omega(t)\right]+\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(t^{\star}=t\right).

(34)

Given (
34
), rearranging (
33
) and observing that
(1-x)^{-1}\leq 1+2x
for
0\leq x\leq 1
concludes the proof of the theorem.

To establish (
34
), we follow
Block et al. [2024a]
and introduce for
y\in\mathbb{R}
and

:

\displaystyle m_{t,y}(s)=m(s)+\frac{K(s,t)}{K(t,t)}(y-m(t)),\qquad a(s)=\frac{\tau}{\rho^{2}}\cdot\frac{K(s,t)}{K(t,t)},\qquad\text{and}\qquad b(s)=\frac{\tau}{\rho^{2}}-a(s).

(35)

It is immediate that if
K(s,t)\leq(1-\rho^{2})K(t,t)
, then
b(s)\geq\tau
; moreover,
b(s)\geq 0
for all
s
by Cauchy-Schwarz and
m_{t,y+\tau/\rho^{2}}(s)=m_{t,y}(s)+a(s)
pointwise. The utility of introducing
m_{t,y}
is that the distribution of
\Omega(s)
conditioned on
\Omega(t)=y
is also a Gaussian process with mean
m_{t,y}
and covariance
K_{t}
, independent of
y
. Thus, we have for all fixed
t\in T
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left(\Phi_{\delta},\,t^{\star}=t\text{, and }\inf_{K(s,t)\leq(1-\rho^{2})K(t,t)}\Omega(s)\geq y+\tau|\Omega(t)=y\right)

(36)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\Phi_{\delta},\,t^{\star}=t\text{, and }\inf_{K(s,t)\leq(1-\rho^{2})K(t,t)}\Omega(s)-b(s)\geq y|\Omega(t)=y\right)

(37)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\Phi_{\delta},\,t^{\star}=t\text{, and }\inf_{K(s,t)\leq(1-\rho^{2})K(t,t)}\Omega(s)-b(s)-a(s)+a(s)\geq y|\Omega(t)=y\right)

(38)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\Phi_{\delta},\,t^{\star}=t\text{, and }\inf_{K(s,t)\leq(1-\rho^{2})K(t,t)}\Omega(s)+a(s)\geq y+\frac{\tau}{\rho^{2}}|\Omega(t)=y\right)

(39)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\Phi_{\delta},\,t^{\star}=t\text{, and }\inf_{K(s,t)\leq(1-\rho^{2})K(t,t)}\Omega(s)\geq y+\frac{\tau}{\rho^{2}}|\Omega(t)=y+\frac{\tau}{\rho^{2}}\right).

(40)

Letting

\displaystyle q_{t}(y)=(2\pi K(t,t,)^{-1/2})\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{(y-m(t))^{2}}{2\eta^{2}K(t,t)}\right)

(41)

denote the density of
\Omega(t)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left(t^{\star}=t\text{ and }\mathcal{E}(\rho,\tau)\text{ and }\Phi_{\delta}\right)

(42)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(t^{\star}=t\text{ and }\Phi_{\delta}\right)-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\Phi_{\delta},\,t^{\star}=t\text{, and }\inf_{K(s,t)\leq(1-\rho^{2})K(t,t)}\Omega(s)\geq y+\tau|\Omega(t)=y\right)q_{t}(y)dy

(43)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(t^{\star}=t\text{ and }\Phi_{\delta}\right)-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\Phi_{\delta},\,t^{\star}=t\text{, and }\inf_{K(s,t)\leq(1-\rho^{2})K(t,t)}\Omega(s)\geq y+\frac{\tau}{\rho^{2}}|\Omega(t)=y+\frac{\tau}{\rho^{2}}\right)q_{t}(y)dy

(44)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(q_{t}(y)-q_{t}\left(y-\frac{\tau}{\rho^{2}}\right)\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\Phi_{\delta},\,t^{\star}=t\text{, and }\inf_{K(s,t)\leq(1-\rho^{2})K(t,t)}\Omega(s)\geq y|\Omega(t)=y\right)dy

(45)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(q_{t}(y)-q_{t}\left(y-\frac{\tau}{\rho^{2}}\right)\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\Phi_{\delta}\text{ and }t^{\star}=t|\Omega(t)=y\right)dy.

(46)

Noting now that

\displaystyle q_{t}(y)-q_{t}\left(y-\frac{\tau}{\rho^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{\tau q_{t}(y)}{\eta^{2}\kappa^{2}\rho^{2}}(y-m(t))

by the proof of

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(t^{\star}=t\text{ and }\mathcal{E}(\rho,\tau)\text{ and }\Phi_{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tau}{\eta^{2}\kappa^{2}\rho^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(y-m(t))\mathbb{P}\left(\Phi_{\delta}\text{ and }t^{\star}=t|\Omega(t)=y\right)q_{t}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau}{\eta^{2}\kappa^{2}\rho^{2}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\eta\cdot\omega(t)\cdot\mathbb{I}\left[\Phi_{\delta}\text{ and }t^{\star}=t\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau}{\eta\kappa^{2}\rho^{2}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\omega(t^{\star})\mathbb{I}[\Phi_{\delta}]|t^{\star}=t\right]\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(t^{\star}=t\right).

(50)

Now, noting that, by definition,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\omega(t^{\star})\mathbb{I}[\Phi_{\delta}]|t^{\star}=t\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in T}\omega(t)\right]+\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)},

∎

Note that unlike

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\widetilde{t}^{\star}=t\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\widetilde{t}^{\star}=t|\xi\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(t^{\star}=t\right)\right]=\mathbb{P}\left(t^{\star}=t\right).

Thus

have the same distribution. Let

and observe the above holds for

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\reg_{T}\right]\leq 2\eta\cdot\sqrt{\log\left|\mathcal{F}\right|}+1+\frac{64B}{\eta\rho^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\log\left(2T|\mathcal{F}|\right)}\cdot\left(L^{\star}+2\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\omega_{1}(f)\right]\right)

In order to compare our result to that of

Definition 9

(Hadamard Class)
.

Let the domain be
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
i.e. the set of all
n
-bit strings with addition modulo 2.
The Hadamard class
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Had}}
is the set of all linear functions
f_{y}:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
given by
f_{y}(x)=\left\langle x,y\right\rangle
for
y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.

By standard abuse of notation, we will associate the output
\mathbb{F}_{2}
with
\left\{0,1\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}
.
We first note that this class is
\rho
-separated by the uniform distribution on
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
with
\rho=1/\sqrt{2}
.
This follows from the fact that for any
y\neq 0
, there exists
\mathbb{P}_{x\sim\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(\left\langle x,y\right\rangle=0)=1/2
.
While one can show with a similar argument
\gamma

\gamma=O(1)
, note that lead term in the regret bound
Equation

8
survives even if we set
\gamma=0
.
Thus, evaluating the regret bound of
Wang et al. [2022]
for this class, we find that using their algorithm and plugging in (
8
), regret is bounded as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\reg_{T}\right]\lesssim\left(n+\sqrt{n2^{n}}\right)\cdot\left(1+\sqrt{L^{\star}}\right).

(76)

On the other hand, if we use
Algorithm

1
, we see by
Theorem

1
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\reg_{T}\right]\lesssim 1+n\log(T)+\sqrt{n\log(T)L^{\star}},

(77)

which is an exponential improvement in
n
in regret. While this separation is extreme, the size of the separating set is
2^{n}
, where
n
can be considered the natural parametrization of the size of the problem, and thus the oracle complexity of both algorithms is exponential.

We can modify the construction, however, to preserve a separation while keeping the oracle complexity polynomial. Indeed, note that if we consider the set
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}=\left\{e_{i}\right\}
where

are the standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Had}}
is
\rho
-separated with
\rho=1/\sqrt{2}

In this case, the regret bound of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\reg_{T}\right]\lesssim\left(1+n\right)\cdot\left(1+\sqrt{L^{\star}}\right),

(78)

while the bound from

1
remains as in (

D.3
Proof of
Proposition

2

In this section we state and prove a more general version of
Proposition

2
, which applies to arbitrary real-valued function classes. We first recall two more notions of function class complexity: fat-shattering dimension
[
Bartlett et al., 1994
]
and covering numbers.

Definition 10
.

Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function class. We say that
\mathcal{F}
is shattered at scale
\alpha>0
by points
z_{1},\dots,z_{m}
if there exists some witness
s\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that for all
\varepsilon\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}^{m}
there is an
f_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{F}
satisfying
\varepsilon_{i}(f_{\varepsilon}(z_{i})-s_{i})\geq\alpha/2
. The fat-shattering dimension at scale
\alpha
,
\mathrm{fat}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{F})
is the maximal number of points that can be shattered at scale
\alpha
.

Definition 11
.

Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function class. The
\varepsilon
-covering number of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to a measure
\mu
, denoted by
N(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F},\mu)
is the smallest cardinality of
\mathcal{F^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
such that for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
, there exists a
f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
such that
\left\|f-f^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}<\varepsilon
.

The following fundamental result controls covering numbers by fat-shattering dimension.

Theorem 4

(
Mendelson and Vershynin [2003]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function class. There exist universal constants
c,C>0
such that for all
\varepsilon
and all
\mu
, it holds that

\displaystyle N(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F},\mu)\leq\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\right)^{C\cdot\mathrm{fat}_{c\varepsilon}(\mathcal{F})}.

(80)

In the special case where
\mathcal{F}
is binary-valued,
\mathrm{fat}_{0}(\mathcal{F})
becomes the VC dimension and the classical Dudley extraction result
[
Dudley, 1969
]
follows. We have the following result.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function class and suppose that
\mu
is a
\rho
-separating measure. Then there exist constants
c,C>0
such that

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{F}\right|\leq\left(\frac{2}{\rho}\right)^{C\cdot\mathrm{fat}_{c\rho}(\mathcal{F})}.

(81)

Moreover, there exists a
\rho
-separating set of size
m=\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{fat}_{c\rho}(\mathcal{F})/\rho^{2}\right)
. In particular, if
\mathcal{F}
is a binary-valued function class of VC dimension
d
, then
\left|\mathcal{F}\right|\leq\left(\frac{10}{\rho}\right)^{d}
and there exists a separator set of size
m=\mathcal{O}\left(d/\rho^{2}\right)
.

Proof.

We first note that if
\mathcal{F}
has a
\rho
-separating measure
\mu
, then
\left|\mathcal{F}\right|\leq N\left(\rho,\mathcal{F},\mu\right)
. This is because if
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is a
\rho
covering of
\mathcal{F}
, we must have
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
.
To see this note that, from
\rho
separation, the set
\left\{f\in\mathcal{F}:\left\|f-f^{\prime}\right\|_{\mu}<\rho\right\}=\{f^{\prime}\}
.
Since, we have
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is a
\rho
-covering, by singletons, we must have
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
. The first statement follows by applying
Theorem

4
.

On the other hand, by
Lemma

7
and the probabilistic method, it holds that there exists some set of size
m=2\log(N(\rho,\mathcal{F},\mu))/\rho^{2}
that
\rho
-separates
\mathcal{F}
. Again applying
Theorem

4
gives the second statement. The binary-valued
\mathcal{F}
case is proved similarly.
∎

Lemma 7
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a function class such that
\mu
is a
\rho
-separating measure for
\mathcal{F}
.
Then, with probability
1-\delta
, a sample
S=\left\{x_{i}\right\}
of size
m=(\log(N(\rho,\mathcal{F},\mu))+\log(1/\delta))\rho^{-2}

\displaystyle\left\|f-g\right\|_{m}^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i}(f(x_{i})-g(x_{i}))^{2}\geq\frac{\rho}{2}.

(82)

Further, this set is a separator set for
\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

The proof follows from standard uniform convergence arguments for finite classes
[
Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David, 2014
]
.
∎

We note here that in the presence of
\rho
-separation for
\mu
, we get uniform convergence in terms of the covering number for
\mu
as opposed to the standard result in terms of the uniform convering numbers
\sup_{\nu}N(\rho,\mathcal{F},\nu)
.
