Development and validation of a 10-year predictive model for cardiovascular and metabolic disease risk: insights from a large-scale health examination cohort
Authors: Dejie Wang, Jiang-Shan Tan, Ruihan Liu, Yingjuan Ma, Yugang Han, Wei Gan, Yanmin Yang, Jian Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop and validate a 10-year predictive model for cardiovascular and metabolic disease (CVMD) risk using comprehensive health examination data from nearly 37 701 individuals.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used health examination data, including demographic information, clinical measurements, laboratory tests and lifestyle factors. Potential predictors were selected based on a literature review and exploratory analysis. Machine learning techniques (including random forest and gradient boosting) were employed to develop the predictive model. The model’s performance was evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Model validation was conducted on separate test and validation sets.ResultsA total of 37 701 electric power employees were included in this study after applying rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria. The dataset was divided into training, validation and testing sets in a 70:15:15 ratio, with no significant differences observed in baseline characteristics, ensuring robust analysis. Feature selection using the random forest classifier identified the top predictors of CVMD. Machine learning models, particularly random forest and gradient boosting, demonstrated superior predictive performance compared with traditional Cox regression methods. These results significantly outperformed traditional Cox models, which yielded an AUC of approximately 0.60. Correlation analysis revealed strong associations between key variables, such as systolic and diastolic blood pressure, low-density lipoprotein and total cholesterol, and creatinine and blood urea nitrogen, highlighting the complex interactions among CVMD risk factors.ConclusionThe developed 10-year predictive model for CVMD risk, based on health examination data, shows promising potential for early identification and targeted intervention in individuals at high risk for CVMDs. This approach could contribute to the reduction of CVMD incidence and related morbidity and mortality.Keywords: Risk Factors, Biomarkers, Electronic Health Records

Introduction

Cardiovascular and metabolic diseases (CVMDs) collectively represent a significant threat to global health.1,3 Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), such as coronary heart disease (CHD), cerebrovascular disease and peripheral arterial disease (PAD),4 and metabolic diseases, including diabetes mellitus (DM), obesity and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD),5 are major contributors to morbidity and mortality worldwide.1 2 These conditions are interconnected through shared risk factors and complex pathophysiological mechanisms,6 making the study of CVMDs as a combined entity crucial for advancing public health and clinical outcomes.

Most CVDs are primarily driven by dysregulated metabolic processes, and their global prevalence is alarming, with CVDs accounting for an estimated 17.9 million deaths annually, representing 32% of all global deaths.7 CVDs also pose a significant health burden in China, accounting for 46.74% and 44.26% of all deaths in rural and urban areas, respectively, in 2019.8 This indicates that nearly two out of every five deaths in China are attributable to CVD, affecting approximately 330 million individuals nationwide.8 This high prevalence underscores the critical need for effective preventive strategies and interventions.8

Metabolic diseases, such as DM, dyslipidaemia and obesity, further exacerbate the risk of developing CVDs.7 9 In China, millions of individuals are affected by these metabolic conditions, which often progress insidiously, leading to severe vascular diseases and critical events like myocardial infarction and stroke by the time they are detected.8 In a large Chinese cohort study involving 30 378 participants aged 35–64 years, the presence of metabolic syndrome components was associated with an increased risk of CVD incidence.10 Patients with NAFLD (one condition of metabolic disease) also have a significantly higher risk of developing CVDs, even among lean individuals.11 These metabolic abnormalities contribute to a pro-inflammatory state, oxidative stress and vascular dysfunction, which are underlying mechanisms for CVD development.12

Metabolic diseases and CVDs share numerous common risk factors, including dyslipidaemia, diabetes, obesity, smoking, physical inactivity and unhealthy diet.9 13 These modifiable risk factors contribute significantly to the development and progression of both types of diseases (also called CVMDs).8 Therefore, exploring these shared risk factors is essential for constructing effective prediction models and developing comprehensive prevention strategies. By identifying and analysing common risk factors, we can create predictive models that estimate the risk of developing CVMDs. This approach allows for the early identification of high-risk individuals, enabling timely and targeted interventions. Consequently, predicting the risk of these diseases will facilitate more effective prevention and control measures, ultimately reducing the overall burden of CVDs in China8.

Several risk prediction models, such as the Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation (SCORE2) algorithm, have been developed to estimate the 10-year risk of CVD, incorporating factors such as age, cholesterol levels, blood pressure, smoking and diabetes.14 The SCORE2 model, which has been validated across large European cohorts, also includes specific adaptations for subpopulations, such as individuals with type 2 diabetes15 and chronic kidney disease.16 Besides, other models have also been developed to predict CVD risk, including the Framingham Risk Score17 and the Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease (ASCVD) Risk Calculator.18 These models have been instrumental in identifying individuals at high risk of developing CVD by incorporating various risk factors such as age, sex, blood pressure, cholesterol levels, smoking status and diabetes.17,20 The Framingham Risk Score, for instance, has been widely used due to its simplicity and ability to provide a general estimate of CVD risk over 10 years.21 The SCORE model, which focuses on fatal cardiovascular events, has been adapted for use in various European populations,22 while the ASCVD Risk Calculator is tailored for use in the USA and emphasises a broader range of risk factors, including race.23

Despite their strengths, these classical models have notable limitations. They might not fully capture the complexities of interactions between different risk factors. These models are primarily designed to predict CVD risk alone and do not consider the interconnectedness with metabolic diseases such as diabetes and obesity, which are significant contributors to cardiovascular risk.17,20 Currently, no comprehensive model exists to predict the combined risk of CVMD. Given the intertwined nature of these conditions and their shared risk factors, developing a predictive model for CVMD is crucial. Such a model would enable a more holistic approach to risk assessment and intervention, addressing both cardiovascular and metabolic health simultaneously.

Traditional risk assessment tools often struggle to manage the complexity and volume of contemporary health data. In contrast, machine learning algorithms can efficiently process large datasets, uncovering complex patterns that might be missed by conventional statistical approaches, which have found significant applications in various medical fields, demonstrating their potential for cardiovascular event prediction,24 ASCVD risk prediction,25 coronary artery disease prediction26 and so on.

Therefore, this study aims to build the first predictive model for CVMDs, using extensive health examination data and advanced machine learning techniques to provide a more accurate and integrated risk prediction tool. This pioneering effort will enhance our ability to predict, prevent and manage CVMDs, ultimately improving public health outcomes.

Methods

Study population and data collection

This study is based on the data collected in the science and technology project of Guozhong Healthcare and includes all electric power employees who underwent physical examinations at Shandong Electric Power Central Hospital from January to December 2013.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria: (1) adults aged 18 years or older who completed a physical examination at the centre from January to December 2013; (2) participants and their family members agree to participate in the study and sign the informed consent form.

Exclusion criteria: (1) diagnosis of CVMD at the time of the physical examination from January to December 2013; (2) failure to complete at least one follow-up due to reasons such as resignation.

The health examination data collected encompassed a wide range of information. Demographic details such as age and sex were recorded. Clinical measurements included vital signs (eg, blood pressure, heart rate), anthropometric data (eg, height, weight, body mass index ()) and ECG results. Laboratory tests covered a comprehensive panel of biomarkers, including lipid profiles, glucose levels, liver and kidney function tests and inflammatory markers. Additionally, lifestyle factors such as smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity and dietary habits were assessed through structured questionnaires. This dataset provided a robust foundation for the development of the predictive model for CVMD risk.

Definition and diagnostic criteria of CVMD

CVMD encompasses a range of interrelated conditions that affect both the cardiovascular system and metabolic processes. This study defines CVMD as the occurrence of CVDs or metabolic diseases, recognising the significant interplay and shared risk factors between these two groups of conditions.

CVDs refer to a group of disorders involving the heart and blood vessels. The primary conditions included under this category are CHD, myocardial infarction, hypertension, PAD and heart failure (HF).

Metabolic diseases involve disorders related to abnormal metabolic processes. The primary conditions included under this category are DM, obesity, NAFLD and metabolic syndrome. These conditions are characterised by metabolic dysregulation, such as impaired glucose metabolism, excessive body fat accumulation, abnormal liver fat deposition, elevated blood pressure and abnormal lipid levels.

Follow-up and the primary outcome

The participants were followed up annually, and their physical examination data were collected to ensure comprehensive follow-up information. The final follow-up data used in this study were recorded in December 2023. The primary outcome of the follow-up in this study is the incidence of CVMD, which includes the first occurrence of any CVDs and the diagnosis of any metabolic diseases defined in the above part. The endpoint is defined by the earliest event recorded during the follow-up period. Each event is validated through clinical records, diagnostic tests and adherence to established diagnostic criteria as outlined in the online supplemental material. The follow-up period aims to capture comprehensive data on the progression and development of these conditions to accurately assess the risk factors and build a predictive model for CVMD.

Data collection and preprocessing

The dataset used in this study comprised comprehensive health records collected from 2013 to 2023. The dataset included both categorical and numerical variables, which were preprocessed to ensure data integrity and suitability for analysis.

Handling missing data

To address missing values, numerical columns were imputed using the mean of each respective column. This method is advantageous for maintaining the distribution of numerical features. For non-numerical columns, forward fill and backward fill methods were applied sequentially to impute missing values. This approach ensures continuity in categorical data, leveraging adjacent values for imputation.

Dataset splitting

To ensure robust model validation, the dataset was split into training, validation and testing sets in a 70:15:15 ratio. This was achieved by first splitting the data into training (70%) and a temporary set (30%) and subsequently splitting the temporary set equally into validation (15%) and test (15%) sets. This stratification was conducted to maintain the distribution of the target variable across all subsets.

Feature selection

Initial feature selection was performed using a random forest (RF) classifier to rank features by their importance. The top 10 features with the highest importance scores were selected for subsequent model training. This process was essential to reduce dimensionality and enhance model performance by focusing on the most informative features.

Handling imbalanced data

Given the potential imbalance in the target variable, synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE) was employed on the training set. SMOTE generates synthetic samples for the minority class, thereby balancing the class distribution and mitigating the risk of model bias towards the majority class.

Model training and hyperparameter tuning

Two machine learning algorithms were employed: RF and gradient boosting (GB). Hyperparameter tuning was performed using grid search with fivefold cross-validation to identify the optimal settings for each model. The parameters tuned for the RF included the number of estimators, maximum depth and minimum sample split. For the GB, parameters such as the number of estimators, maximum depth and learning rate were tuned.

Model evaluation

Model performance was evaluated using several metrics: accuracy, area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) and confusion matrix. The ROC AUC score was calculated to assess the model’s ability to discriminate between classes. The concordance index was used as a measure of the model’s predictive accuracy, particularly in the context of survival analysis.

Comparison with traditional methods

In addition to machine learning models, several traditional Cox regression models were used for comparison, including forward selection (AIC-FW-Cox), backward selection (AIC-BW-Cox), LASSO-Cox and the standard Cox proportional hazards model (Cox-PHM). These models were implemented using the lifelines library, and feature selection was conducted manually through iterative steps.

Statistical analysis

Locally weighted scatterplot smoothing (Lowess) curves were plotted to illustrate the relationship between each of the top 10 selected features and the target variable. This non-parametric regression method provided a smoothed visualisation of the underlying trends and patterns in the data. To evaluate global feature importance, we employed XGBoost and Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP). SHAP values were computed for each fold using the TreeExplainer, and the mean absolute SHAP values were aggregated across folds to determine feature importance. All analyses were conducted using Python (V.3.8). Key libraries included Pandas for data manipulation, Scikit-learn for machine learning, imbalanced-learn for handling imbalanced datasets and Statsmodels for statistical analysis. Visualisation was performed using Matplotlib and Seaborn. The lifelines library was used for survival analysis and Cox regression modelling.

Results

Study population

A total of 70 981 electric power employees who underwent physical examinations were initially included in the study. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 37 701 participants remained eligible for the study (figure 1). As is shown in table 1 and online supplemental table S1, the mean age of participants was 39 years, with 55% being male. Baseline data were similar among the training, test and validation sets, with no significant differences observed between the groups, ensuring the robustness of the dataset partitioning (table 1 and online supplemental table S1). During the 10-year follow-up period, a total of 15 231 incident CVMD events were recorded among the 37 701 participants, representing an overall event rate of 40.3%. These outcome events included newly diagnosed cases of CVMDs, as defined in the study protocol.

Feature selection

Initial feature selection using a RF classifier identified the top 10 most important features (figure 2), which included systolic blood pressure, age, diastolic blood pressure, BMI, triglyceride (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), total cholesterol, fasting glucose, creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN). These features were used for subsequent model training, as they were deemed to provide the most significant contributions to the prediction of CVMD risk. They also account for non-linear relationships, and the Lowess curves were plotted to visualise the relationship between the top 10 selected features and the target variable (figure 3). These curves provided a smoothed data representation, highlighting key trends and patterns. The analysis revealed that higher systolic blood pressure, age, diastolic blood pressure, BMI and LDL levels were strongly associated with an increased risk of CVMD.

Discussion

This study represents the first attempt to develop and validate a predictive model for CVMD risk as a combined entity, rather than as separate conditions. The results demonstrate that machine learning methods are exceptionally well-suited for meaningful risk prediction in extensively phenotyped large-scale epidemiological studies. Specifically, the RF-based method of risk prediction provided superior event prediction over traditional standard risk scores, marking a significant advancement in the field. The findings also underscore the significance of deep phenotyping using subclinical markers, as evidenced by their prominent presence among the top 10 predictors for CVMD risk prediction. The top 10 features identified as most important in predicting CVMD risk were systolic blood pressure, age, diastolic blood pressure, BMI, LDL, total cholesterol, fasting glucose, creatinine and BUN.

Our study confirms and extends previous research highlighting the interrelationship between CVMDs. The identification of systolic blood pressure, age, diastolic blood pressure, BMI, LDL, total cholesterol and fasting glucose as top predictors aligns with established knowledge regarding the role of advanced age, hypertension, diabetes and hyperlipidaemia in CVMD risk.27,29 Identifying these variables in our study further underscores their pivotal role in the pathogenesis of CVMDs.

Although BMI ranked above LDL in the model’s global feature-importance list, this metric summarises each variable’s overall contribution to fit across trees and interactions and should not be interpreted as a direct, per-patient effect. Consistent with this, our SHAP analyses indicate that LDL exerts a larger marginal impact on predicted CVMD risk than BMI, as reflected by greater absolute SHAP values and steeper dependence profiles. The apparent discrepancy is expected: permutation/split-based importance redistributes credit among correlated predictors (eg, LDL, TGs, total cholesterol) and interaction terms, whereas SHAP quantifies each feature’s case-level contribution to the prediction.

This study did not perform formal tests of independent prediction in the regression sense. Besides, this machine-learning framework prioritises predictive performance and captures non-linearities and interactions without assuming additivity. Interpretability is therefore provided by SHAP rather than partial regression coefficients. Taken together, these findings support BMI as a broad proxy for metabolic milieu (obesity, insulin resistance, hypertension)30 and LDL as a lipid-specific driver of CVMD risk.31 The LDL’s stronger SHAP influence underscores its clinical salience even when its global importance rank is lower due to correlation structure within the lipid panel.

Furthermore, the inclusion of creatinine and BUN emphasises the impact of metabolic health32 33 and renal function34 35 on cardiovascular outcomes, which is consistent with findings from other large cohort studies. Renal function plays a critical role in the development and progression of CVMDs. Multiple biomarkers of renal function, including creatinine36 37 and BUN,38,40 have been reported to be closely associated with cardiovascular outcomes. Creatinine, a by-product of muscle metabolism, is a widely used marker for assessing renal function.41 Elevated creatinine levels indicate impaired kidney function, which is linked to an increased risk of CVDs such as HF, myocardial infarction and hypertension.42 Similarly, BUN, another key marker of renal function, reflects the balance between nitrogen intake and excretion and is used to evaluate kidney function.43 Elevated BUN levels are associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events and mortality.44 45 The relationship between renal dysfunction and CVMDs is complex and multifactorial, involving haemodynamic changes, neurohormonal activation and metabolic disturbances.46 Impaired renal function exacerbates the burden on the cardiovascular system, leading to a vicious cycle that accelerates the progression of both renal and CVDs.47 Therefore, monitoring and managing renal function biomarkers such as creatinine and BUN are crucial in the comprehensive assessment and prevention of CVMDs.

Although smoking is widely recognised as a significant risk factor for both CVMDs,48 it was not included in our final predictive model. In our feature selection process, smoking was ranked 37th in terms of its importance, indicating that other variables, such as blood pressure, BMI and cholesterol levels, were more predictive of CVMD risk in our cohort. This finding may reflect the specific characteristics of our population, which consists predominantly of electric power employees with relatively lower smoking rates and better access to healthcare compared with the general population. Additionally, smoking may be collinear with other lifestyle and socioeconomic factors, which could explain its reduced importance in the final model. While our model does not include smoking, we acknowledge that smoking remains a critical public health risk factor. Future models that aim to predict CVMD risk in more diverse populations may need to incorporate smoking as a key predictor, particularly in groups with higher smoking prevalence.

The integration of machine learning in our predictive modelling offers distinct advantages over traditional statistical methods. Unlike conventional models, machine learning algorithms can handle complex, high-dimensional data and uncover non-linear relationships between variables,49 enhancing our models’ predictive accuracy and reliability, making them more robust tools for clinical and public health applications.

The development of a predictive model for CVMD risk has significant implications for both clinical practice and public health. Predictive models, such as clinical prediction models, leverage a variety of patient characteristics to estimate the probability of adverse outcomes or specific diagnoses. This personalisation allows healthcare providers to tailor interventions based on individual risk profiles, shifting the focus from a one-size-fits-all approach to more targeted strategies that consider unique patient circumstances.50 51 Besides, accurate risk assessment is fundamental for preventing CVDs.51 Predictive models use conventional risk factors and can incorporate novel biomarkers, enhancing their discriminative ability. By identifying individuals at high risk, healthcare systems can implement preventive measures, such as lifestyle modifications or pharmacotherapy, to mitigate the onset of CVMD.51 52 Moreover, the risk prediction models provide a quick and systematic way to evaluate a patient’s risk, which can inform treatment decisions.53 For instance, variations in risk scores can significantly influence decisions regarding interventions like statin prescriptions, potentially affecting millions of patients based on the model used,53 which underscores the need for robust models that can reliably inform clinical choices. However, there is substantial variability in risk predictions depending on the modelling decisions made, such as the selection of covariates and the handling of missing data. Understanding these uncertainties is essential, as it can lead to misclassification of risk and inappropriate treatment decisions. Therefore, continuous refinement and validation of predictive models are necessary to ensure their reliability in clinical settings.

One of the primary strengths of this study is the large and well-characterised cohort, which enhances the generalisability of our findings. The comprehensive data collection, including demographic, clinical and lifestyle variables, provides a robust foundation for model development. Additionally, the application of advanced machine learning techniques ensures high predictive performance and reliability. Besides, there are also many advantages in machine learning methods over traditional approaches. First, machine learning algorithms can process and analyse vast amounts of data rapidly and accurately, identifying complex patterns and relationships that may be overlooked by human analysts. This capability is particularly advantageous in medicine, where datasets are often large and multifaceted. Second, machine learning models possess the ability to continuously improve their performance by learning from new data, thereby enhancing their predictive accuracy over time. Third, machine learning techniques can integrate diverse types of data, including clinical, imaging, genetic and lifestyle information, providing a more comprehensive understanding of disease processes and risk factors.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study population, consisting of electric power employees from Shandong Province, may not be representative of the general population. Future research should aim to validate the model in more diverse cohorts. Additionally, while the machine learning models performed well, the complexity of these algorithms may pose challenges for implementation in routine clinical practice. Efforts should be made to develop user-friendly tools that can facilitate the integration of these models into healthcare systems.

Future research should focus on external validation of the predictive model in different populations and settings to confirm its generalisability and applicability. Additionally, incorporating more granular data, such as genetic information and detailed dietary assessments, could further enhance the model’s predictive power. The continuous development and refinement of machine learning algorithms will also play a critical role in advancing predictive modelling in medicine. Furthermore, exploring the integration of these predictive models into electronic health record systems can streamline risk assessment and clinical decision-making. By providing real-time risk predictions, these tools can support proactive healthcare management and improve patient outcomes.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed and validated a predictive model for CVMD risk using machine learning techniques. The model demonstrated superior performance compared with traditional methods, highlighting the value of integrating cardiovascular and metabolic parameters in disease risk assessment. The findings underscore the potential of machine learning to enhance predictive modelling in medicine, offering a powerful tool for improving public health and clinical practice. Future efforts should focus on validating and refining the model in diverse populations and integrating these tools into routine healthcare practice to maximise their impact.
