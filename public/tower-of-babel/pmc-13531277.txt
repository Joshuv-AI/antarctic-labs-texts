ABSTRACT
Shareable abstractThis article provides mechanistic insights into lung injury and repair, and describes how omics technologies and advanced in vivo and ex vivo systems presented at the #ERSCongress 2025 are advancing the landscape of translational respiratory medicine https://bit.ly/3QJ4oqy

The 2025 European Respiratory Society (ERS) Congress, held in Amsterdam, the Netherlands, from 27 September to 1 October, brought together respiratory experts from around the world to present advances across basic and translational sciences [1, 2]. Several sessions explored how multi-omics integration and innovative experimental models are reshaping our understanding of lung injury, repair and regeneration from early development to chronic lung disease. As in previous highlights articles [1], this article features only selected contributions presented at the Congress, illustrating how animal models and ex vivo tools bridge fundamental discoveries with emerging therapeutic strategies in chronic pulmonary diseases.

Scientific “year in review” session

The scientific “year in review” session highlighted recent advances in basic and translational sciences, with a particular focus on how multi-omics and spatial technologies are reshaping the understanding of structural cells and their interactions within the lung microenvironment.

The session opened with a presentation highlighting how spatial transcriptomics and proteomics are applied to advance our understanding of lung disease. Despite limitations related to cost, technical complexity, and challenges in data interpretation, these approaches preserve spatial context within tissues, thereby enabling precise selection of experimental models for therapeutic development and evaluation. Several studies illustrated the transformative impact of spatially resolved technologies on disease modelling and treatment strategies. For example, it was reported that enrichment of lymphoid follicles in emphysema is characterised by gene expression signatures associated with follicle organisation, B-cell activation, proliferation and class switching [3], highlighting the dynamic activation of B-cells in ectopic lung lymphoid tissues. In idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), two major studies demonstrated that aberrant epithelial cells within fibrotic foci not only interact with fibroblasts and myofibroblasts but, upon shedding, also recruit macrophages, thereby perpetuating disease progression [4, 5]. Spatial transcriptomics has also advanced the understanding of airway remodelling in asthma. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors modulate mast cell and epithelial niches, resulting in reduced airway hyperresponsiveness, decreased mast cell numbers, and lower tryptase levels [6]. Additionally, the presence of highly remodelled mucus plugs in bronchioles has emerged a potential biomarker of disease, exhibiting altered immune cell composition with predominant neutrophilia [7, 8].

The focus then shifted to stem cell dynamics, highlighting insights into cellular origins and differentiation in repair. Whole-mount analyses of entire murine lung lobes revealed that keratin (KRT)5+ cells in squamous cancer lesions are clonally related to KRT4+/KRT13+ cells in the trachea, a finding confirmed in human lungs [9]. In another study, transitional fibroblasts and transitional epithelial cell populations were detected in the butylated hydroxytoluene model of fibrosis. Ablation of these populations resulted in divergent outcomes, including alveolar simplification, increased air spaces and obstructive lung function [10]. Using confetti mice, a mouse strain used for lineage tracing, two alveolar type 2 (AT2) epithelial cell populations were distinguished in the KRASG12D model, slow and fast cycling cells, with the latter capable of tumour formation through interleukin (IL)-1 signalling and nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B-cells (NF-κB) activation, bypassing normal regenerative control [11]. Furthermore, two alveolar fibroblast (AF) subsets were identified during influenza infection: platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR)α+ AF cells that proliferate upon injury and differentiate into PDGFRβ+ AF cells which interact with basal cells via Notch signalling to promote fibrosis [12].

The role of mechanical forces and epithelial integrity in shaping disease outcomes was also reviewed. Asthmatic bronchoconstriction was shown to induce excessive epithelial extrusion, while targeting epithelial crowding with gadolinium hexahydrate chloride, an inhibitor of mechanosensitive channels, protected the barrier, reduced inflammation and accelerated repair [13]. Moreover, airway stem cells engaged in competitive interactions mediated by Myc/Yap signalling, leading to the formation of basal cell pods, whereas Taz-driven destabilisation of β-catenin promoted alveolar epithelial differentiation [14]. In addition, transmembrane 63 (TMEM63) has been identified as a mechanosensitive ion channel essential for maintaining alveolar integrity and regulating surfactant release [15].

Finally, advances in cell-based therapies were presented, reflecting the growing integration of mechanistic insights into therapeutic development. Using single-cell transcriptomics, researchers can now characterise the identity and in vivo behaviour of therapeutic cell products. Adoptive T-cell therapy is an emerging cancer treatment strategy whereby engineered T-cells are transferred to enhance antitumor immunity. In this regard, OT-I-caIL12 cells, CD8+ T-cells expressing a constitutively active IL-12 construct, prevented tumour growth and enhanced endogenous T-cell cytotoxicity, activation, and proliferation [16]. Mesenchymal stromal cell therapy is being explored as a regenerative approach for bronchopulmonary dysplasia. Administration of human mesenchymal stromal cells in rats improved lung function when cells exhibited a stem-like phenotype with low human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-A/B/C expression [17]. In a clinical setting, transplantation of autologous p63+ lung progenitors in IPF donors improved gas exchange and exercise tolerance, alongside partial resolution of honeycombing [18]. Collectively, these findings underscore how the convergence of multi-omics and spatial technologies is transforming the understanding of lung disease and enabling the identification of novel targets and treatments for precision medicine.

Oral presentation “From omics to targets: emerging insights into chronic pulmonary disease mechanisms”

The oral presentation session “From omics to targets: emerging insights into chronic pulmonary disease mechanisms” highlighted significant advances in the molecular understanding of chronic pulmonary diseases. Together with related omics-focused abstracts, the session emphasised how multi-layered omics approaches, including genomics, transcriptomics (both single-cell and spatial), proteomics, and metabolomics, are uncovering novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets, thereby accelerating the implementation of precision medicine in respiratory research (figure 1). Since the completion of the Human Genome Project in 2003, omics technologies have expanded considerably to capture multiple layers of biological complexity. This methodological spectrum continues to evolve through the integration of established platforms with emerging technologies, extending into fields such as medical imaging via approaches like radiomics, which extracts quantitative imaging features beyond visual assessment, and was also discussed at the 2025 ERS Congress.

DNA methylation, the most extensively studied epigenetic mechanism, regulates gene expression and downstream cellular function without altering the DNA sequence. Due to its sensitivity to environmental influences, DNA methylation has been widely investigated using epigenome-wide association studies, revealing its important role in both childhood and adult asthma, as well as in transgenerational epigenetic inheritance [19]. In this session, methylation changes associated with lung function were discussed, demonstrating that DNA methylation signatures linked to lung function are established early in life. Some genes and differentially methylated positions are shared across age groups, suggesting partially overlapping biological mechanisms linking lung function and COPD severity from early life to adulthood, alongside age-specific regulatory differences [20].

Single-cell RNA sequencing has substantially advanced respiratory research by enabling the identification of previously unrecognised lung cell types and refining the appreciation of cellular composition. For example, the discovery of ionocytes as major producers of cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) transcripts has provided new insights into airway biology [21]. Large-scale profiling of more than 300 000 lung cells from patients with interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) has further revealed a previously uncharacterised population of aberrant basaloid epithelial cells, exhibiting basal, mesenchymal, senescence, and developmental markers, localised to myofibroblast foci [22, 23]. Building on these findings, both single-cell and spatial transcriptomics technologies were prominently featured in this session, with applications in asthma, pulmonary fibrosis, and the small airways of adolescents exposed to e-cigarettes [24–26]. In addition, the therapeutic potential of seralutinib in IPF was discussed, with single-cell RNA sequencing analyses demonstrating modulation of expression of genes related to fibrotic pathways, which was also confirmed at protein level [27]. These findings highlighted multicellular effects and strong intercellular interactions between disease-relevant cell populations, supporting its development as a novel inhaled therapy for fibrotic lung disease.

Childhood ILD (chILD) represents a heterogeneous group of rare respiratory disorders frequently associated with poor prognosis. Recent European collaborative follow-up data extending into adulthood identified category B1 (sarcoidosis, haemosiderosis, connective tissue disorders, vasculitis) and surfactant-related disorders as the most prevalent forms, whereas unclassified ILD cases exhibited the most severe impairment in lung function. More than half of affected individuals remained on corticosteroid therapy [28]. Within this session, bi-allelic lysosome-associated membrane protein 3 (LAMP3) variants were highlighted as a molecular cause of chILD, linking LAMP3 dysfunction to impaired surfactant metabolism. Functional studies demonstrated that altered LAMP3 expression and instability disrupted interactions with surfactant protein (SP)-B and SP-C, providing mechanistic insight into surfactant-related disease pathophysiology [29, 30].

Despite rapid advances in high-throughput technologies, the full potential of omics for precision medicine in lung disease has yet to be fully realised. Integrating large-scale omics datasets with machine learning approaches offers opportunities for improving the diagnosis of rare and complex respiratory disorders. However, heterogeneity across studies continues to limit reproducibility and the robustness of predictive models. Greater international collaboration and secure data sharing infrastructures are needed to translate omics discoveries into clinically applicable tools.

Symposium “Repairing the lung: from single cells and tissue organisation to regenerative therapy”

The symposium “Repairing the lung: from single cells and tissue organisation to regenerative therapy” presented key contributions originally featured at the Lung Science Conference in Estoril [31], highlighting advances in lung repair, epithelial-immune interactions, regenerative cellular models, and translational strategies relevant to patient care (figure 2).

The session opened with work identifying Notch signalling as a potential therapeutic target to prevent or reverse fibrotic processes in the lung. Basal stem cell-derived Notch3 signalling was essential for maintaining intraepithelial airway macrophage (IAM) identity. Disruption of delta-like canonical Notch ligand 1 in basal cells or Notch2/3, ligands that act as activators of the Notch signalling pathway, in IAMs induced macrophage differentiation and loss of major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II) expression, indicating a role for epithelial Notch signalling in maintaining IAM phenotype. A comparable IAM signature was detected in human airways, suggesting potential relevance to asthma [32]. Building on earlier evidence that Notch 3 activation promotes fibroblast survival and myofibroblast differentiation, AT2-specific Jag1 and Jag2 (Notch ligands) knockout mice were examined following bleomycin injury, a well-established model of fibrotic lung remodelling. In both knockout models, attenuation of fibrosis was observed 14 days after bleomycin injury, consistent with a profibrotic role for Notch/Jag signalling. Extending these findings to human systems, precision-cut lung slices (PCLS) derived from IPF patients were treated with neutralising antibodies, providing a human tissue system that preserves lung architecture and cell-to-cell interactions, to target Notch/Jag signalling to assess pathways in human fibrotic lung tissue, this study is ongoing, and these findings remain under investigation.

A novel human primary alveolar model was then introduced, based on secretory AT2-like progenitor cells (secAT2) located in the respiratory bronchioles, a population absent in mice [33–35]. In progenitor organoid cultures derived from primary adult cells, both SP-C+ AT2 cells and carcinoembryonic antigen-related cell adhesion molecule 6 (CEACAM6)+ or secretoglobin family 3A member 2 (SCGB3A2)+ secAT2 populations were present. An AT1/2M differentiation condition (cells with an alveolar type 1 (AT1) phenotype without a complete loss of SP-C) was established through large tumour suppressor inhibition. Single-cell transcriptomics of these cultures demonstrated strong alignment with human lung atlas references, confirming representation of AT1, AT2, cycling AT2, and secAT2 identities. When exposed to interferon γ (IFN-γ) for 4 h, progenitor organoids upregulated the anti-apoptotic marker baculoviral IAP repeat containing 3 (BIRC3), indicating an adaptative survival response under inflammatory stress. These findings suggest IFN-γ relevance to inflammatory context during infection-mediated exacerbations in COPD. As IFN-γ signalling is increased during viral infections and COPD exacerbations, these findings support the feasibility of the human organoid alveolar model to study inflammatory-associated epithelial injury.

The translational potential of stem cell technologies was further highlighted through engineered tissue and organoid-based approaches applied to congenital and infectious lung disease, which serve as experimental platforms to model lung development and pathology. A notable example included transplantation of a decellularised tracheal scaffold seeded with patient-derived cells in a child with congenital tracheal stenosis [36]. Similar decellularisation and re-cellularisation strategies were subsequently used to create a mouse model for studying respiratory syncytial virus and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection in a bioengineered whole-organ system [37]. Current work in this field focuses on amniotic fluid-derived stem cells, which offer a minimally invasive source for modelling congenital diaphragmatic hernia [38, 39]. These cells can produce extracellular vesicles and differentiate into proximal and distal lung organoids. Organoids derived from fetuses with congenital diaphragmatic hernia are being investigated to inform future treatment strategies [40]. This body of work underscores the expanding role of stem cell engineering as a complement to conventional transplant and surgical interventions.

The session concluded with research examining the long-term impact of antenatal second-hand cigarette smoke extract (CSE) exposure on lung development in a model of early-life environmental effects in lung structure and function [41]. In C57BL/6 mice exposed to cigarette smoke before mating and during gestation, intrauterine growth restriction and reduced neonatal body size were observed. At postnatal day 10 (P10), decreased alveolarisation and cord length were evident compared with ambient air controls, although catch-up growth occurred by postnatal day 21 (P21). At P21, reduced SP-C+ expression and increased AT2 proliferation (SPC+/Ki67+) were detected. Early time points showed decreased fibroblast abundance and increased angiogenesis, whereas at P21 both fibroblasts and lipofibroblasts were reduced without changes in angiogenesis. Previous studies in the same model reported later-life vascular dysfunction, reduced vimentin expression, and elevated oxidative stress [42]. Collectively, these findings indicate that antenatal CSE disrupts early lung maturation and may predispose offspring to impaired responses to secondary injury in adulthood.

Overall, the studies presented in this symposium describe innovative experimental and regenerative models that enable advanced understanding of lung development, epithelial-immune interactions, tissue repair and disease mechanisms. Together, these approaches highlight the importance of human-relevant and bioengineered systems to investigate fibrosis, infection, and early-life environmental exposure, to be able to gain mechanistic insights into lung diseases.

Oral presentation “Best of lung injury and repair: from development to disease”

The oral presentation session “Best of lung injury and repair: from development to disease” focused on emerging experimental models that elucidate mechanisms of injury and repair underlying chronic lung diseases. The respiratory system is continuously exposed to harmful agents, including pathogens and environmental pollutants. Multiple endogenous mechanisms support tissue repair and regeneration; however, defects in these processes, occurring either early or later in life, contribute to the development and progression of chronic lung diseases.

The respiratory tract begins forming early during embryonic and fetal development into a highly complex network of interacting tissues. Recent advances combining mass cytometry with spatial mapping of the human fetal lung have revealed an intricate and dynamic cellular composition that evolved over time into organised and specialised structures. Disruption of this tightly regulated process by preterm birth is associated with abnormal lung development and risk of chronic lung diseases later in life, including COPD. To investigate these mechanisms, an ovine model was developed involving intra-amniotic lipopolysaccharide injection and neonatal ventilation of preterm lambs. These animals exhibited increased airway hyperreactivity, reduced bronchiole numbers, small airway remodelling, enhanced mucus production and inflammation that persisted into adulthood [43]. Complementary studies in neonatal mouse lungs have shown that oxidative stress disrupts communication between AT2 cells and alveolar macrophages through IL-6 and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signalling, thereby reducing the regenerative capacity of AT2 cells [44].

In parallel, bioinformatic analyses of novel in vitro and ex vivo models, including PCLS, lung organoids, and airway-lung injury models, have provided additional insight into mechanisms driving respiratory disease. PCLS, lung organoids, and airway-lung injury models have proven particularly informative [45]. These platforms have uncovered defects in intercellular communication that contribute to chronic lung disease pathophysiology. For example, the development of a perfused muscle-on-a-chip model demonstrated that monocytes co-cultured with myoblasts previously exposed to tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) and hypoxia undergo phenotypic changes. A subset of monocytes adheres to myoblasts and expresses high levels of CD68 and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), while non-adherent monocytes acquire M1 macrophage characteristics. Myoblasts subsequently exposed to conditioned medium from these M1 macrophages express pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines associated with muscle wasting [46]. Immune cell crosstalk has also been demonstrated in a mouse model of acute lung injury, where C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 1 (CXCR1) mediates communication between type 2 dendritic cells and T-cells. CXCR1 deficiency in dendritic cells promotes regulatory T-cell differentiation while impairing T-helper type 17 (Th17) cell differentiation [47].

Cellular interactions within the alveolar niche were further explored in the context of CSE. Cigarette smoke was shown to disrupt iron homeostasis and induce ferroptosis in AT2 cells, leading to altered mitochondrial morphology and increased oxidative stress, features closely associated with COPD [48]. These alterations may also contribute to vascular remodelling through increased iNOS expression in Acta2+ cells. In PCLS exposed to cigarette smoke, deletion of iNOS promoted cell proliferation, particularly within Acta2+ populations, and enhanced tissue repair [49]. Finally, organoid systems were presented as powerful tools to model the alveolar niche and investigate how cell–cell interactions drive fibrotic processes. Co-culture of AT2 cells with fibroblasts in Matrigel generates “budding organoids” that can be used as screening platforms for drug discovery, offering new avenues for therapeutic development in chronic lung diseases [50].

Conclusions

The 2025 ERS Congress highlighted how the integration of multi-omics technologies, spatial profiling and advanced experimental models may transform understanding of lung structure, cellular interactions and repair mechanisms from early development to chronic lung diseases. Across IPF, asthma, COPD and ILD studies, as well as early-life environmental exposures, researchers demonstrated how the use of animal models and in vitro approaches can reveal previously unrecognised pathways linking injury to defective regeneration and accelerate the identification of therapeutic targets and precision strategies for pulmonary diseases.
