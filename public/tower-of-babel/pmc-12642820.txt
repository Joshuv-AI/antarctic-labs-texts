Prognostic Implications of Heart Rate Score and Its Temporal Change in Left Ventricular Systolic Dysfunction: Insights From the HINODE Study
Authors: Masato Okada, Koichi Inoue, Nobuaki Tanaka, Koji Tanaka, Yuko Hirao, Torri Schwartz, Torsten Kayser, Akihiko Nogami, Takeshi Mitsuhashi, Toyoaki Murohara, Wataru Shimizu, Takanori Ikeda, Nobuhiro Nishii, Kenji Ando, Yasushi Sakata, Kazutaka Aonuma, the HINODE Investigators
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHeart rate score (HrSc) ≥ 70% reflects chronotropic incompetence and predicts prognosis in patients with cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs) and left ventricular (LV) systolic dysfunction. However, its prognostic value in Japanese patients remains underexplored, and longitudinal changes in HrSc and their contributing factors are poorly characterized.MethodsThis post hoc analysis of the Heart Failure Indication and Sudden Cardiac Death Prevention Trial in Japan (HINODE) included 172 patients with CIEDs and LV ejection fraction ≤ 50%. HrSc, defined as the percentage of all atrial‐paced and sensed beats in a single tallest 10 beat/min device histogram bin, was assessed using remote monitoring.ResultsAt baseline, the median HrSc was 53% (interquartile range, 41%–83%), with 68 (39.5%) patients having HrSc ≥ 70%. During the 2‐year follow‐up, the incidence of all‐cause death or heart failure (HF) events was similar between patients with HrSc ≥ 70% and < 70% (31.1% vs. 29.4%, log‐rank p = 0.862). However, among 142 patients whose follow‐up data were available, HrSc increased from < 70% to ≥ 70% in 13 patients (13%) and decreased from ≥ 70% to < 70% in 19 (9.1%). An increase in HrSc was associated with lower LV ejection fraction, antiarrhythmic drug initiation, increased lower rate limit, and HF events.ConclusionIn the HINODE study, baseline HrSc ≥ 70% was not predictive of 2‐year cardiovascular outcomes. However, HrSc changed in approximately one‐quarter of patients and was associated with clinical and device‐related factors, as well as HF events. HrSc is not a static measure but a dynamic marker that reflects evolving patient conditions and CIED programming.

Introduction

Chronotropic incompetence, the inability of the heart to appropriately increase its rate in response to physiological demands, is associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes [1, 2]. In patients with cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs), the heart rate score (HrSc) serves as a device‐based surrogate for chronotropic incompetence, readily accessible via remote monitoring. The HrSc is defined as the percentage of all atrial‐paced and sensed beats in the single tallest 10 beat/min device histogram bin during a non‐atrial fibrillation (AF) status [3, 4, 5, 6, 7].

Previous studies have revealed that an HrSc ≥ 70%, indicating less variation in heart rate, correlates with treadmill measures of chronotropic incompetence [7] and is associated with increased mortality in patients with implantable cardioverter defibrillators (ICDs) or cardiac resynchronization therapy defibrillators (CRT‐Ds) [3, 4, 5, 6]. However, these studies were based on the baseline HrSc alone, treating it as a static variable.

In clinical practice, however, the HrSc may change over time and is influenced by evolving clinical conditions, including the progression of sinus node dysfunction, initiation or adjustment of medications such as beta‐blockers or amiodarone, and changes in the CIED settings such as the lower rate limit (LRL) [8] and rate‐response programming [4] (Figure 1). Despite these potentially dynamic characteristics, the longitudinal changes in the HrSc have not been well described.

In this observational post hoc analysis of a multicenter registry, we aimed to evaluate the prognostic significance of the baseline HrSc in Japanese patients with CIEDs and left ventricular (LV) systolic dysfunction. In addition, we examined the 2‐year temporal changes in the HrSc and aimed to identify the clinical and device‐related factors associated with these changes.

Methods

The HINODE Study

The Heart Failure Indication and Sudden Cardiac Death Prevention Trial Japan (HINODE) study (NCT03185832) is a prospective multicenter registry evaluating ventricular arrhythmias, HF events, and mortality in Japanese patients with systolic HF. The study design, inclusion and exclusion criteria, device programming, and event adjudication have been previously described [9, 10]. The protocol was approved by the ethics committees of 34 participating hospitals, and all patients provided written informed consent. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Based on the Japanese guidelines [11, 12], patients indicated for primary prevention were prospectively enrolled in one of four treatment cohorts: (i) ICD (n = 102), (ii) CRT‐D (n = 69), (iii) non‐device (n = 115), and (iv) pacing cohort (including patients with a cardiac resynchronization therapy pacemaker [CRT‐P]) (n = 68). Patients in cohorts (i–iii) had a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of ≤ 35% and a history of HF. Patients in cohort (iv) had a mildly reduced LVEF of ≤ 50% and required bradycardia device implantation. Medical therapy was administered at the investigators' discretion. ICD device programming followed a high cutoff rate or delayed therapy strategy in line with the MADIT‐RIT trial [13]. Patients were followed for a minimum of 12 months. All patients using the device were connected to a remote monitoring system.

Study Patients

The HINODE study enrolled 354 patients between June 2017 and June 2019. After excluding the non‐device cohort, CIEDs were implanted in 239 patients. The pacing mode and LRL were determined at the discretion of the attending physicians. Patients with persistent AF, VVI mode, or missing telemetry data were excluded. A subset of patients with HrSc data available at both baseline and the 24‐month final follow‐up comprised the paired cohort for temporal analysis (Figure 2).

Data Collection and Follow‐Up

At approximately 1‐month post‐implantation, patients were checked using the remote monitoring system (Latitude; Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA), and the HrSc was measured. Subsequently, patient follow‐ups were performed every 6 months until the final visit at 24 months. The echocardiographic parameters and laboratory data were collected during the study period. Device diagnostics, including HrSc histograms, were collected via remote monitoring at approximately 1‐month intervals and reviewed during scheduled 6‐month follow‐up visits. If a patient was connected to the remote monitoring system but unable to attend a standard in‐clinic visit, a telephone follow‐up could replace a standard in‐clinic visit. During each visit, device assessments were conducted following the standard of care, and both serious and non‐serious adverse events were recorded. An independent committee adjudicated serious adverse events, including lethal ventricular arrhythmias, death, and HF events [9, 10]. Lethal ventricular arrhythmias were defined as those requiring device therapy, including anti‐tachycardia pacing or defibrillation shocks, or those resulting in hemodynamic instability. HF events were defined as those primarily caused by cardiac dysfunction with signs and symptoms of congestive HF. To meet the criteria, the patient must have had either of the following conditions: (1) been hospitalized and received a new or intensified decongestive HF regimen with oral or parenteral medications, or (2) not been hospitalized but received intravenous diuretic therapy in an outpatient setting.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables with normal distribution are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while non‐normal variables are reported as median (interquartile range [IQR]). Categorical data are presented as numbers (percentages). Continuous variables were compared between two groups using Student's t‐test or the Mann–Whitney U test when non‐normally distributed. Categorical variables were compared between two or more groups using Pearson's χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test when any category contained less than five measures. Based on previous literature [3, 4, 5, 6, 7], an HrSc of ≥ 70% was used as the threshold indicative of chronotropic incompetence.

Two analyses were conducted. First, a prognostic analysis was conducted on the baseline HrSc cohort, which included patients with available HrSc data at 1‐month post‐device implantation. The primary endpoint was a composite of all‐cause death and HF events during the HINODE study period. The secondary clinical endpoint was the individual components of the primary endpoint: all‐cause death and HF events. These were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier analysis and compared using the log‐rank test. Cox proportional hazard regression models were used to calculate the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for each endpoint. In addition, subgroup analyses were conducted to assess whether the prognostic value of baseline HrSc differed according to patient characteristics.

Second, an exploratory analysis was conducted on the paired HrSc cohort, which included patients with available HrSc data at both 1‐month post‐implantation (baseline) and at the final follow‐up. Temporal changes in the HrSc were described, and their associations with selected clinical and device‐related factors were examined. Associations between the temporal changes and clinical events during the study period were also explored descriptively. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using data available at the time of the final data lock. As the dataset was transferred to the Japanese Heart Rhythm Society in March 2024, no additional analyses could be performed thereafter.

Results

Study Population

Of the 354 patients enrolled in the HINODE registry, 239 received CIEDs. After excluding patients with persistent AF (n = 10), VVI mode (n = 1), or missing telemetry data (n = 56), 172 patients were included in the baseline HrSc cohort for prognostic analysis (Figure 2). Among them, 142 patients had available HrSc data at both baseline and the 24‐month follow‐up, comprising the paired HrSc cohort for a temporal analysis. The reasons for exclusion from the paired cohort included death (n = 19), device replacement or upgrade (n = 6), change in VVI mode (n = 1), and missing follow‐up telemetry (n = 4).

Baseline Characteristics

The baseline patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median HrSc was 53% (IQR, 41%–83%) and 68 (39.5%) patients had an HrSc ≥ 70%. Compared to those with an HrSc < 70%, patients with an HrSc ≥ 70% had a significantly lower resting heart rate (62 ± 17 vs. 71 ± 15 bpm; p < 0.001) and were more frequently treated with AADs (37% vs. 23%, p = 0.052). Device type differed between groups: ICD and CRT‐D devices were more common in the HrSc < 70% group, whereas CRT‐P and pacemakers were more frequently implanted in the HrSc ≥ 70% group (p = 0.024). The HrSc < 70% group also had a higher prevalence of DDI mode and lower LRL settings, presumably due to the preserved intrinsic rate. The use of the rate‐response function did not differ between groups.

Clinical Outcomes

During a median follow‐up of 21 (IQR, 16–25) months, 49 (28.4%) patients reached the primary endpoint. All‐cause death occurred in 14 (8.1%) patients. HF events occurred in 44 (25.6%) patients. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed no significant difference in event‐free survival between the HrSc ≥ 70% and < 70% groups for the primary composite endpoint (68.9% vs. 70.6%, log‐rank p = 0.862), all‐cause death (91.2% vs. 88.1%, log‐rank p = 0.703), and HF events (72.5% vs. 73.5%, log‐rank p = 0.852), as shown in Figure 3A–C. Similarly, Cox proportional hazard regression analysis showed no significant difference in risk between the HrSc ≥ 70% and < 70% groups. The HRs (95% CIs) for HrSc ≥ 70% versus < 70% were 1.05 (0.60–1.86) for the primary composite endpoint, 0.81 (0.27–2.41) for all‐cause death, and 1.06 (0.58–1.93) for HF events. Subgroup analyses revealed that the effect of HrSc ≥ 70% for the primary endpoint may differ according to the LRL of < 50 bpm or ≥ 50 bpm (p for interaction = 0.028) (Figure 4). Among patients with LRL < 50 bpm, the incidence of the primary composite endpoint was higher in patients with HrSc ≥ 70% than in those with HrSc < 70% (HR 7.11, 95% CI 1.18–42.7). Temporal Changes in the HrSc

During the 2‐year study period, several device settings were modified at the attending physician's discretion (Table 2). In the paired HrSc cohort, the proportion of patients with an LRL ≥ 70 bpm increased from 16% to 24% (p = 0.001). The introduction rate of the rate‐response function increased from 4.2% to 12% (p = 0.030). With these changes, the atrial pacing rate increased from 9% (0%–55%) to 15% (0%–69%) (p = 0.062).

Table 3B presents the association between HrSc changes and potential modifying factors. The use of beta‐blockers and DDD pacing mode was similar across groups. However, patients whose HrSc increased from < 70% to ≥ 70% were more likely to have initiated AADs, had an increase in the LRL ≥ 70 bpm, and introduced the rate‐response function, compared with those whose HrSc remained < 70%. Conversely, among patients with a baseline HrSc ≥ 70%, those whose HrSc declined to < 70% were less likely to have an LRL ≥ 70 bpm at follow‐up compared with those who maintained an HrSc ≥ 70%.

Table 3C shows the association between HrSc change and clinical events during the 2‐year study period. A total of 7 patients experienced VA events, and patients with a baseline HrSc ≥ 70% and those with a baseline HrSc < 70% had similar event rates (7.1% [4/56] vs. 3.5% [3/86], p = 0.43). However, VA event rates tended to be higher in patients whose last HrSc was ≥ 70% than in those whose final HrSc was < 70% (9.8% [5/51] vs. 2.2% [2/91], p = 0.098). In contrast, 27 (19.0%) patients experienced HF events. The incidence of HF events was similar between patients with an initial HrSc ≥ 70% and those with an initial HrSc < 70% (17.9% [10/56] vs. 19.8% [17/86], p = 0.78). However, patients whose HrSc increased from < 70% to ≥ 70% had significantly higher rates of HF events than those whose HrSc remained < 70% (53.8% vs. 13.7%, p = 0.003).

Discussion

Main Findings

The HrSc is a simple surrogate for chronotropic incompetence that can be easily assessed in patients with CIEDs. While previous large‐scale studies have demonstrated its prognostic value [3, 4, 5, 6], its application in clinical practice remains limited, partly due to the influence of CIED programming and rate‐lowering medications on the HrSc. In this post hoc analysis of the HINODE study, we examined the prognostic value of the baseline HrSc and the clinical relevance of its temporal changes. A baseline HrSc ≥ 70% was not associated with cardiovascular outcomes over a 2‐year study period. However, the HrSc changed over time in approximately one‐quarter of patients. An increase in the HrSc to ≥ 70% was associated with lower baseline LVEF, initiation of AADs, increases in the LRL, and a higher incidence of HF events. These findings support the interpretation of the HrSc as a dynamic marker reflecting evolving clinical and device‐related conditions.

Prognostic Significance and Temporal Change in the HrSc

In the HINODE registry, 39.5% (68/172) of patients had an HrSc ≥ 70%, comparable to the LIFE study (42.7%, 214/501) [7]. In contrast, large‐scale data from the LATITUDE registry reported a lower prevalence: 22.4% (12 993/57 893) in ICD and 25.8% (17 505/67 929) in CRT‐D recipients [3]. The higher prevalence in the HINODE registry may reflect its inclusion of patients without defibrillators and frequent beta‐blocker use as part of heart failure therapy. Resting bradycardia and a higher proportion of pacemaker recipients likely contributed to an elevated HrSc, indicating inherent chronotropic impairment.

While prior studies have demonstrated the prognostic value of baseline HrSc values [3, 4, 5, 6], our data showed no association between a baseline HrSc ≥ 70% and cardiovascular outcomes. Although we adopted the HrSc threshold of ≥ 70% based on the prior studies, the optimal cutoff may differ between Japanese and Western cohorts. The lack of association observed in our cohort may also be attributable to limited statistical power, relatively short follow‐up, or inter‐patient variability in device programming, particularly in LRL settings. In fact, the subgroup analysis revealed a significant interaction between the HrSc (≥ 70% vs. < 70%) and LRL (< 50 bpm vs. ≥ 50 bpm) for the primary endpoint. Consistent with a previous study [8], high LRL settings artificially elevate HrSc, potentially masking its risk stratification value in patients with CIEDs.

More importantly, our findings highlight the limitations of a static HrSc assessment. The HrSc changed over time in 22% (31/142) of patients in the paired cohort: it increased from < 70% to ≥ 70% in 13 (9.2%) patients and decreased from ≥ 70% to < 70% in 18 (13%). HF events were more frequent in patients with an increased HrSc and less frequent in those with a decreased HrSc, compared to patients whose HrSc remained unchanged. These results suggest that serial HrSc monitoring may better reflect evolving clinical status and therapeutic responses than a single baseline value.

Factors Associated With Temporal Change in the HrSc

In the present study, lower baseline LVEF, AAD initiation, and an increase in LRL were associated with an increase in the HrSc from < 70% to ≥ 70%. The association between lower LVEF and an increase in the HrSc may reflect the natural progression of sinus node dysfunction [14] and autonomic nervous system degeneration [15] in advanced systolic HF. These patients are more susceptible to chronotropic incompetence due to sympathetic overactivation, parasympathetic withdrawal, and sinoatrial remodeling [1, 2]. However, it should be acknowledged that medications with negative chronotropic effects and pacemaker settings can influence the HrSc.

Medications With Negative Chronotropic Effects

Recent studies have suggested that the prescription of beta‐blockers slows the heart rate and can worsen chronotropic incompetence in patients with HF with preserved LVEF [16, 17]. However, in the present study, we did not identify a significant relationship between beta‐blocker use and changes in the HrSc, probably because of the high prescription rate at baseline and lack of statistical power. Although beta‐blockers can contribute to elevated HrSc through their negative chronotropic effects, they also provide well‐established cardioprotective benefits in patients with systolic HF. Therefore, net clinical impact may depend on the balance between these competing actions. In our cohort, beta‐blocker use was comparably high across the HrSc groups (approximately 80%), and beta‐blocker initiation did not appear to drive changes in the HrSc.

In contrast, the rate of newly prescribed AADs was significantly higher in patients whose HrSc increased from < 70% to ≥ 70%. Although the reasons for AAD prescription were not available, the results are important given the non‐negligible incidence of ventricular arrhythmias [10, 18] and new‐onset AF in patients with systolic dysfunction [19] as well as in patients with CIED [20].

Pacemaker Programming

Pacemaker programming, especially the LRL, directly influences the HrSc [8]. A higher LRL increases atrial pacing and consequently increases the HrSc. Notably, our data partly explain how the LRL setting affected temporal changes in the HrSc. Of 12 patients whose LRL increased from < 70 bpm to ≥ 70 bpm, the HrSc increased from < 70% to ≥ 70% in 5 (42%) patients. As the HrSc remained unchanged in the remaining seven patients, the LRL setting cannot exclusively explain the temporal change in the HrSc; however, it is an important contributing factor.

At last, introducing the rate‐response function may decrease the HrSc [21, 22, 23]. In the current study, the rate‐response function was introduced in 6 patients at the initial follow‐up and in 17 at the last follow‐up. Contrary to our expectations, of the 11 patients with a newly introduced rate‐response function, the HrSc decreased from ≥ 70% to < 70% in only one patient. Considering this was a retrospective analysis, it is reasonable to assume that the rate‐response function was introduced because of a decrease in the HrSc. The existing rate‐response function does not sufficiently induce physiological changes and may not improve an already low HrSc.

Association Between Change in the HrSc and HF Events

In the present study, the HrSc increased from < 70% to ≥ 70% in 13 patients. Among them, 7 (53.8%) experienced HF events, representing the highest incidence compared with all other patients. While causality cannot be established, an increase in the LRL after HF events is a presumed scenario that also increases the HrSc. Additionally, HF exacerbations can trigger atrial or ventricular arrhythmias, and the subsequent use of AADs may increase the HrSc. In contrast, the HrSc increased without HF events in 6 patients. In these patients, the attending physician may preventively increase the LRL before the manifestation of HF. Alternatively, the spontaneous progression of sinus node dysfunction may have reduced the HrSc.

Notably, the HrSc decreased from ≥ 70% to < 70% in 18 patients, and the incidence of HF events was the lowest in this group compared with all other patients. Certainly, the retrospective analysis was inconclusive regarding the causal relationship of this trend; however, the association between a decrease in the HrSc and LRL settings indicated that the high incidence of HF events may be attributable to the suboptimally high LRL. Although a higher LRL can increase the cardiac output, it may reduce the diastolic filling time and increase myocardial oxygen consumption [24], potentially worsening HF. Irrespective of its causality, the HrSc and its temporal changes may serve as an integrated marker reflecting both therapeutic interventions and underlying disease progression.

Clinical Implications

Our findings emphasized that HrSc should be interpreted as a dynamic marker influenced by device programming and pharmacological therapy, rather than as a static prognostic tool. In patients with lower LRL (< 50 bpm in our small study and ≤ 75 bpm in a previous large CRT‐D study [8]), HrSc ≥ 70% was associated with higher event rates, indicating possible chronotropic incompetence or suboptimal pacing. In such cases, closer follow‐up, adjustment of the LRL, or activation of the rate‐response function may be beneficial. In contrast, among patients with higher LRL settings, HrSc ≥ 70% was not prognostic, likely reflecting intentional pacing for hemodynamic support.

Although causality cannot be established, a decrease in the HrSc was associated with a lower incidence of HF events. When the HrSc increases due to arrhythmia‐related AAD initiation, catheter ablation may be a preferred alternative to reduce long‐term AAD dependency. If the HrSc increases owing to an increased LRL setting to support cardiac output during decompensated acute HF, reassessing and reducing the LRL may be appropriate once the patient has been stabilized with optimized HF medications. Serial HrSc monitoring through remote surveillance may facilitate earlier intervention, optimize management, and improve outcomes in patients with CIEDs and LV systolic dysfunction.

Limitations

The present study had certain limitations. First, patient selection bias was possible. The data in the HINODE study were derived from institutions specializing in cardiology and the management of cardiac arrhythmias. Thus, patients with HF with reduced LVEF who were relatively well managed may have been included in this study. Additionally, the HINODE registry exclusively enrolled Japanese patients, and racial or regional differences compared with Western cohorts could not be fully assessed. Although equivalent favorable outcomes have been reported in Japanese patients receiving ICD or CRT‐D therapy [25, 26], the findings may not be directly generalizable to other populations. Second, the exploratory analysis investigated changes in the HrSc during the 2‐year study period; some observed changes in the HrSc may have been influenced by regression to the mean, particularly among patients with extreme baseline values. Third, the HINODE study did not specifically examine the association between the HrSc and cardiovascular prognosis. Owing to the lack of statistical power and the short‐term follow‐up period, it was not possible to fully examine the factors that caused changes in the HrSc. Additionally, insufficient data on AADs and their indications limited our understanding of which medications influenced the temporal changes in the HrSc. Finally, this study could not establish a causal relationship between HrSc changes and HF events. Given the potential for reverse causality, these findings should be interpreted descriptively. To better understand the causal relationship, a landmark analysis using HrSc data from earlier time points (e.g., 6 or 12 months) would be essential to evaluate the impact of HrSc changes on subsequent clinical outcomes. However, due to the closure of the data center, such analyses could not be performed. This remains an important area for future research, as the longitudinal behavior of HrSc may provide clinical value in patient monitoring and risk stratification. Clarifying this relationship would ideally require prospective studies that monitor events following temporal changes in the HrSc with fixed device settings and consistent AAD use, which is challenging in real‐world clinical practice.

Conclusion

In this post hoc analysis of the HINODE study, baseline HrSc values did not predict 2‐year cardiovascular prognosis in patients with CIEDs and LVEF ≤ 50%. However, during this period, HrSc changed in approximately one‐quarter of patients, and an increase in the HrSc from < 70% to ≥ 70% was associated with lower baseline LVEF, AAD initiation, and an increase in LRL settings. These patients experienced more frequent HF events, highlighting the need to interpret the HrSc as a dynamic marker influenced by evolving clinical conditions and device programming.
