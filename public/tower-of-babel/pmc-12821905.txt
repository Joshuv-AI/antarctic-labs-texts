Evaluation of bioactive glass primer on dentin bonding durability and interface properties
Authors: Macarena Aburto, Jael Escobar, Miguel Angel Muñoz, Issis V Luque-Martinez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of a novel phosphosilicate bioactive glass (BG)-containing primer on dentin bond strength, nanoinfiltration, and the biomechanical and ultrastructural properties of the dentin-primer interface.MethodsA primer was formulated with 7.5% (w/w) phosphosilicate BG particles (SiO₂·P₂O₅·Na₂O·CaO; particle size 0.2–10 μm) suspended in distilled water.Four groups were tested (n = 5): (1) control groups using an etch-and-rinse (ER) or self-etch (SE) adhesive strategy per manufacturer’s instructions, and (2) experimental groups where the BG-primer was applied before the ER or SE adhesive (designated BG-ER and BG-SE, respectively).Specimens were subjected to micro-tensile bond strength (µTBS) testing immediately and after 12 months of aging in distilled water (37 °C). Nanoleakage was assessed by SEM (n = 3 per group). The chemical profile and ion-release potential of the BG-primer were analyzed using ATR-FTIR spectroscopy under dry conditions, immediately after hydration, and after six hours. Data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA and Tukey’s test (α = 0.05).ResultsThe incorporation of the BG-primer did not significantly affect immediate µTBS compared to control strategies. However, after 12 months, both BG-ER and BG-SE exhibited significantly higher µTBS values than their respective controls. The BG-ER group maintained its bond strength over time, while the BG-SE showed a significant increase. Nanoleakage analysis revealed comparable silver nitrate in filtration among all groups. SEM identified BG particles localized near the hybrid layer and partial dentinal tubule occlusion in BG-treated specimens. ATR-FTIR confirmed sustained ionic release and time-dependent chemical shifts, evidenced by decreased crystallinity upon aqueous exposure and spectral shifts indicating persistent ionic reactivity.ConclusionIncorporation of a BG-containing primer improved the long-term stability of resin–dentin bonds without compromising immediate adhesion, supporting its potential to enhance the durability of adhesive interfaces.

Introduction

Achieving durable adhesion to dentin remains one of the greatest challenges in restorative dentistry due to the complex structure of this tissue. Unlike enamel, dentin is a hydrated, collagen-rich, and structurally heterogeneous substrate with fluid-filled tubules that hinder optimal adhesive infiltration and polymerization [1–3]. These limitations are particularly critical at the base of the hybrid layer, where incomplete resin impregnation leaves exposed collagen fibrils susceptible to hydrolytic degradation. In this vulnerable region, nanoleakage pathways and the enzymatic activity of endogenous matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) contribute to progressive collagen breakdown and compromise the long-term stability of the resin–dentin interface [4–8].

To overcome these limitations, numerous strategies have been investigated, including the modification of etching protocols, application of protease inhibitors, and chemical alterations of adhesive systems [1, 3, 8]. More recently, the incorporation of bioactive particles into primers, adhesives, and resin composites has gained attention as a promising approach to enhance the stability of the hybrid layer and promote intrafibrillar remineralization. Materials such as phosphorus pentoxide–niobium, calcium orthophosphate, and bioactive glass have been evaluated for their ability to induce mineral deposition, reinforce the collagen matrix, and mitigate hydrolytic and enzymatic degradation at the adhesive interface [4, 6, 9, 10].

Among these, phosphosilicate-based bioactive glass (BG) has emerged as a particularly promising candidate. Owing to its high bioactivity, BG can release calcium, phosphate, and silicate ions, leading to the precipitation of hydroxycarbonate apatite and strengthening of the bonding interface [11–16]. Its chemical composition and ion-release profile resemble those of dentin, suggesting potential advantages for hybrid layer stabilization. Furthermore, recent studies have demonstrated that incorporating silanized calcium-containing 45S5 BG into adhesive systems may improve polymerization, promote hydroxyapatite deposition, and reduce collagen degradation [10]. However, performance is highly dependent on BG concentration, composition, and surface functionalization. While concentrations above 10 wt% may interfere with resin infiltration or network polymerization, lower concentrations or alternative delivery methods—such as water/solvent-based primers—could better position BG particles within the moist dentin environment and potentiate their bioactive effects [4, 7–10].

Based on this rationale, the present study aimed to evaluate the immediate and 12-month effects of incorporating a 45S5–based bioactive glass primer (BG-primer) applied prior to a universal adhesive system under both etch-and-rinse and self-etch strategies. Dentin bond strength, nanoleakage, and interfacial morphology were assessed to characterize the resin–dentin interface and to determine the influence of the primer on the long-term stability of adhesion. The null hypothesis was that BG-primer incorporation would not significantly affect either immediate or aged adhesive performance.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval

The study was approved by the scientific and ethical committee of the Valparaiso University and has the Ethics approval and consent to participate [ID: CB029-2014]. All experiments involved in this study were conducted in accordance with relevant ethical guidelines and regulations, including those established by the Declaration of Helsinki, and national regulatory framework.

Tooth selection and preparation

Twenty extracted molars were selected for therapeutic reasons, free of caries, and donated under informed consent. The teeth were stored in physiological saline solution. A commercial biosilicate (Biosilicate®, Vitrovita, Sao Carlos, SP, Brazil; particle size distribution of 0.2–10 μm) was used for BG-primer formulation. A 7.5% (w/w) suspension of biosilicate in distilled water was prepared, based on evidence indicating that higher concentrations (> 10%) may adversely affect adhesive infiltration, while lower concentrations (< 5%) may not achieve significant bioactive effects [16, 17]. This intermediate concentration was selected to optimize long-term stability without compromising immediate bond strength. The suspension was homogenized using an ultrasonic bath, and immediately before application, it was agitated for 30 s to ensure uniform dispersion of the particles.

The third molars were debrided using a curette to remove residual organic tissue and subsequently cleaned in an ultrasonic bath for ten cycles. Enamel was removed by wet grinding to expose the occlusal dentin. Complete dentin exposure (absence of exposed pulp horns and enamel staining on the occlusal surface) was confirmed using a stereoscopic loupe at 40x magnification. The smear layer was standardized using 600-grit SiC sandpaper.

Experimental design

The teeth were divided into four groups (n = 5) based on the use of BG-primer and the adhesive strategy for dentin hybridization: self-etch (SE) or etch-and-rinse (ER), both using Scotchbond Universal Adhesive (3 M ESPE, Minnesota, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Table 1).

Availability of data and materials

All data generated or analyzed during this study are included in this published article and its tables and figures.

Restorative procedure and specimen preparation

The adhesive protocols were applied as detailed in Table 1 and performed by a single operator. Composite resin restorations were built incrementally (2 mm layers) using Filtek Z250 (3 M ESPE, Minnesota, USA) and light-cured for 15 s at 1200 mW/cm² (Bluephase, Ivoclar, Liechtenstein). The restored teeth were stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h prior to further testing.

Microtensile bond strength test (µTBS)

Restored teeth were sectioned along the “X,” “Y,” and “Z” axes to produce resin-dentin samples with a cross-sectional area of 0.8 mm² (250 rpm/IsoMet 1000, Buhler, Lake Bluff, IL, USA). Half of the samples were preserved in distilled water at 37 °C for 12 months for long-term evaluation. Specimens were subjected to tensile testing at a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm/min until fracture to determine the µTBS at the dentin-resin interface (100 N load cell; Universal Testing Machine, Instron, Sao Paulo, Brasil). The µTBS was calculated by dividing the failure load by the cross-sectional bond area.

Failure mode analysis

After microtensile testing, fractured specimens were examined under a stereomicroscope (×40 magnification; Microscope, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Failure modes were classified as follows: adhesive failure—fracture located entirely at the resin–dentin interface; cohesive in resin—fracture within the adhesive or composite material; cohesive in dentin—fracture within the dentin substrate; and mixed failure—combination of adhesive and cohesive characteristics. Premature failures (PF) were defined as specimens that fractured during specimen preparation or handling before mechanical testing and were recorded separately from the other failure modes. All data was recorded in a table.

Nanoleakage (NL) and BG-Primer localization (L) in the adhesive interface

Three specimens per tooth were selected for immediate testing to evaluate nanoleakage (NL) and BG localization (L) within the adhesive interface using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), following a protocol adapted from Muñoz et al. [2]. Specimens were immersed in ammoniacal silver nitrate solution for 24 h in the dark, rinsed with distilled water, and immersed in a photo-developing solution under fluorescent light for 8 h. Polishing was performed using a sequence of wet silicon carbide papers (600–2500 grit) followed by 1 μm and 0.25 μm diamond pastes on a polishing cloth. Specimens were ultrasonically cleaned, air-dried, mounted on aluminum stubs, and coated with a thin gold layer (~ 10–20 nm) using a QUORUM Q150R ES Plus sputter coater (Quorum Technologies, East Sussex, UK) to prevent charging without obscuring fine details.

The resin–dentin interfaces were analyzed using two SEM platforms: (1) a field-emission SEM (HITACHI SU 3500, Tokyo, Japan) operated in backscattered electron mode, and (2) an environmental SEM (ESEM; Thermo Scientific Quattro S, Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with a concentric backscatter (CBS) detector. ESEM imaging was performed at 10 kV accelerating voltage with a working distance of 16 mm, using the outermost ring (segment D) of the CBS detector to capture contrast changes in the prismatic architecture. SEM images were used to assess nanoleakage expression and BG particle localization within the adhesive and hybrid layers. Quantitative nanoleakage (% area) and qualitative BG presence (yes/no) were analyzed using UTHSCSA ImageTool 3.0 software (The University of Texas Health Science Center, San Antonio, TX, USA) by a blinded examiner. Additionally, images of the target surface (n = 2) were captured to observe the immediate interaction of the BG-primer with the wet dentin substrate under identical conditions to those applied prior to adhesion.

ATR-FTIR chemical analysis

ATR-FTIR spectroscopy was performed on BG powder under three conditions: dry, immediately hydrated, and after 6 h of hydration in distilled water. FTIR spectra were collected using a spectrometer (Nicolet iS5; Thermo-Nicolet Instruments, Madison, WI, USA) with a diamond crystal (Smart Orbit, Thermo Scientific, USA). Spectra were recorded in the range of 4000 to 400 cm− 1 at a spectral resolution of 4 cm⁻¹, using the co-addition of 32 scans. Measurements were repeated three times to ensure reproducibility.

Characteristic absorption bands of mineral components were analyzed as described in Table 2. Background signals were subtracted for semi-quantitative comparison among groups. The crystallinity index (CI) was calculated using peak heights at 602 cm⁻¹ (B), 564 cm⁻¹ (A), and 595 cm⁻¹ (C) with the formula CI = ∑AB/C. Spectral normalization and analysis were conducted using OMINIC software (Thermo Scientific, USA).

Statistical analysis

In this in vitro study, the tooth was considered the experimental unit, with n = 5 teeth per group. This design is commonly reported and statistically acceptable for evaluating micro-tensile bond strength (µTBS) in adhesive interfaces in dentin. Previous studies have demonstrated that this approach reduces biological variability while maximizing data output using multiple specimens per tooth [19, 20].

For each tooth, approximately 15–20 beam-shaped sticks (specimens) were obtained and individually tested. To prevent pseudo replication and ensure valid statistical inference, the mean bond strength per tooth was used as the unit of analysis, following recommended practices in the literature [21, 22]. Although each group comprised only five teeth, the actual number of measurements per group was significantly higher, enhancing the precision of intra-tooth averaging.

This design enables reliable parametric tests if assumptions are met, and variability is controlled. In our study, we maintained a coefficient of variation (CV) below 20%, which is considered acceptable in mechanical testing of dental biomaterials [23, 24].

Regarding the statistical analysis of nanoleakage (NL) and bioactive glass (BG) localization within the adhesive interface, three specimens per tooth were selected for immediate testing using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The NL of all sticks from the same tooth were averaged for statistical purposes, and the NL (%) means for each testing group were expressed as the average of the five teeth used per group.

To assess the normality of the data, the Shapiro-Wilk test was applied. Subsequently, the NL (%) data were subjected to a two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) (adhesive strategy vs. BG primer), followed by a post hoc test (Tukey’s test at α = 0.05) for pairwise comparisons. For µTBS tests, data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA and Tukey’s test (α = 0.05). Qualitative analyses were conducted to evaluate BG localization within the adhesive interface and on the dentin surface.

This combination of high internal replication, controlled variability, and adherence to established statistical procedures ensures the robustness and reliability of the reported outcomes.

For the immediate evaluation period, ER strategies exhibited higher bond strength values than SE strategies, regardless of whether the BG-primer was used (p < 0.05; Table 3). After 12 months of water storage, both ER and SE strategies demonstrated significantly higher bond strength values when the BG-primer was applied compared to their respective controls (p < 0.05; Table 3).

At 12 months, no statistical differences were observed between SE + BG and ER + BG or between the control groups. However, the ER control group exhibited significantly lower bond strength values at 12 months compared to its immediate results. In contrast, the ER + BG group maintained consistent bond strength between immediate and long-term evaluations. For the SE + BG group, there was a significant improvement in bond strength after 12 months, indicating better long-term performance.

ATR-FTIR chemical analysis

Qualitative analysis of BG powder by ATR-FTIR revealed characteristic peaks associated with carbonates, phosphates, and silicon in asymmetric and stretching modes. After 6 h of hydration, spectral modifications were observed, including a reduction in the intensity of the supplementary peak at 1027 cm⁻¹ (Fig. 3).

Quantitative analysis showed a significant decrease in the crystallinity index when BG powder was exposed to an aqueous solution. This index decreased significantly after 6 h of exposure (p = 0.0018).

Discussion

The null hypothesis—stating that the incorporation of BG-primer would not affect the immediate or long-term properties of the adhesive interface—was partially rejected. While no significant differences were observed among groups in the immediate evaluation, BG-primer significantly enhanced long-term bond strength, particularly in the ER strategy after 12 months of aging. These results demonstrate that the primer does not interfere with polymerization or compromise initial performance but rather contributes to the preservation and stability of the adhesive interface over time. The stabilization observed in both ER + BG and SE + BG groups underscores the primer’s ability to enhance the longevity of resin–dentin adhesion [25–27].

Differences between the ER and SE strategies further illustrate the interaction of BG with dentin substrates. Phosphoric acid etching in the ER strategy creates deeper demineralization and greater collagen exposure, whereas the SE approach preserves more residual calcium, facilitating direct interaction of BG with hydroxyapatite [7, 9, 28]. Importantly, nanoleakage analysis revealed comparable infiltration levels across groups, indicating that the BG-primer did not compromise the quality of adhesive penetration. This compatibility suggests that the bioactive effect of BG was achieved without weakening interface integrity.

The improved long-term bond stability is likely explained by the multiple mechanisms of BG bioactivity. Sustained release of calcium, phosphate, sodium, and silicate ions fosters the nucleation of hydroxyapatite crystals and the formation of a mineral-rich interfacial zone [29]. A silica-rich layer acts as a template for apatite deposition, reinforcing the hybrid layer [27, 30–32]. Concurrently, the alkalinizing effect of BG dissolution elevates local pH, which may suppress the activity of proteolytic enzymes such as MMPs and cathepsins, thereby protecting exposed collagen fibrils within the hybrid layer [8, 9, 16, 17]. The chosen 7.5% (w/w) concentration was based on literature evidence showing that low to moderate BG loadings (≤ 10%) enhance durability and reduce microleakage while maintaining bond strength [33, 34], whereas very low concentrations (≤ 5 wt%) often lack bioactive effects [4, 7–10] and high concentrations (>20%) can impair infiltration or polymerization [17]. The present results confirm that this intermediate concentration did not impair immediate bond strength while significantly improving long-term performance. This concentration-dependent response highlights the need for further studies testing multiple concentrations to establish an optimal therapeutic window for BG incorporation into adhesive systems.

Spectroscopic analyses further corroborated these findings. ATR-FTIR revealed shifts in the ~ 1050 cm⁻¹ (Si–O–Si stretching) and ~ 3400 cm⁻¹ (OH groups) bands after immersion, consistent with BG hydrolysis and the formation of a bioactive phase [24–26, 35, 36]. Detection of phosphate-related bands in the 900–1200 cm⁻¹ region supports ion exchange and apatite nucleation [25–27, 35, 37]. The observed reduction in BG crystallinity after immersion reflects breakdown of Si–O–Si bridges, leading to amorphous domains that facilitate ion diffusion and enhance remineralization [4, 6, 28–30, 35, 36, 38]. Together, these results indicate that BG-primer promotes both physicochemical stabilization and biomineralization at the adhesive interface.

On the other hand, SEM analysis confirmed the presence of bioactive glass particles within the adhesive interface, frequently arranged as micro-clusters adjacent to the base of the hybrid layer. These clusters were consistently observed in the BG-primer groups and did not compromise the continuity of the adhesive film. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) further corroborated their chemical identity, showing characteristic peaks of calcium, phosphorus, sodium, and silicon associated with BG composition [24, 27, 35]. The localization of these particles, together with ATR-FTIR evidence of chemical transformation and ion release, supports the hypothesis that BG exerts its effect directly at the interface by serving as nucleation sites for mineral deposition and contributing to long-term stabilization of the hybrid layer. However, the direct remineralization of dentin was not assessed in this study. Future research should specifically address the remineralizing capacity of BG-primers at the resin–dentin interface to better establish their translational impact in adhesive dentistry.

A noteworthy feature of this study is the novel delivery of BG via a water/solvent-based primer rather than embedding it in the adhesive matrix. This approach maintains adhesive integrity while positioning BG particles near the hybrid layer, where their bioactive potential can be fully expressed [33–38]. Although this method introduces an additional clinical step, it remains compatible with conventional adhesive protocols, functioning as a three-step ER or two-step SE system depending on the etching approach [39].

This study has limitations inherent to its in vitro design. The aging protocol, based on water storage, may not replicate the full complexity of oral challenges, and clinical performance must be confirmed in vivo. Furthermore, only one BG concentration was evaluated; testing multiple loadings would provide valuable insights into dose-dependent effects. Nevertheless, the present results demonstrate that BG-primer effectively enhances long-term bond stability without compromising immediate adhesion, underscoring its potential clinical relevance. By preserving interface integrity and mitigating degradation pathways, bioactive primers may represent a promising strategy to extend the functional longevity of adhesive restorations.

Conclusion

The application of a calcium sodium phosphosilicate-based bioactive glass primer (BG-primer) prior to adhesive procedures significantly improved the long-term performance of resin–dentin bonds without compromising immediate strength or increasing nanoleakage.
