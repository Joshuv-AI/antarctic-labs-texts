Multi-Pattern Generalization in CO2‑EOR: Physically Consistent Surrogate for Saturation-Field Evolution
Authors: Junwei Zhao, Yukun Dong, Jiyuan Zhang, Kuankuan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate and efficient forecasting of CO2 saturation-field evolution is essential for screening injection–production strategies in CO2-EOR/CCUS, yet conventional simulators are too expensive for rapid multiscenario evaluation and many data-driven models generalize poorly across well-pattern topologies. To bridge these gaps, we propose a multimodal, physics-constrained, generative spatiotemporal network (MPG-STNet). The model employs an enhanced Fourier neural operator to perform efficient, physics-aware inference on static geological fields, a Graph Attention Network to explicitly encode well-pattern topology, and a temporal Transformer encoder to extract features from dynamic three-phase production sequences. These signals jointly condition a conditional generative adversarial network that produces high-fidelity CO2 saturation fields, thereby coupling spatial, temporal, and physical information within a single generative pathway. We further embed physics constraints on the discriminator sidemost notably material balancetreating mass conservation as a hard criterion to enhance the physical acceptability of the generated fields. Evaluated on a high-fidelity data set of 4,500 simulated cases covering nine heterogeneous well patterns over a 150-month horizon, MPG-STNet achieves a mean squared error of 0.0062 and structural similarity index measure of 0.9788 on the test set and maintains strong performance on unseen well-pattern configurations. The proposed surrogate provides fast, physically plausible saturation-field predictions for well-pattern generalization and operational decision support.

Introduction

Managing and utilizing CO2 has become a pivotal issue in the global energy system’s transition toward sustainability. On the one hand, as the dominant greenhouse gas, the cumulative effect of CO2 intensifies mitigation pressures worldwide, prompting many countries to accelerate CCUS deployment as a concrete decarbonization pathway; on the other, in oil and gas development, CO2-EOR carries both technical and policy relevanceenhancing oil recovery while durably storing industrial CO2 in the subsurface, thereby aligning economic returns with carbon-constraint objectives. Injected CO2 lowers oil viscosity and induces oil swelling, improving displacement efficiency and mobilizing residual oil. Accurate and efficient saturation-field prediction is essential for optimizing injection–production strategies and supporting the scalable rollout of CCUS.

CO2-EOR modeling largely relies on physics-based numerical simulations, with commercial platforms like Eclipse and CMG solving complex PDE systems to model CO2 migration in reservoirs. Yet this strategy faces three structural constraints. First, there is an inherent trade-off between computational demand and simulation accuracy: field-scale problems often require very fine grids and small time steps to achieve precise solutions, pushing single runs toward large-scale parallel computation and long wall-timescosts that are ill-suited to batch evaluation in workflows like history matching and parameter inversion. Second, uncertainties in geological descriptorssuch as permeability fields and relative-permeability curvescan profoundly affect the credibility of the results. Ensemble Kalman filtering (EnKF) and related assimilation methods help quantify and reduce these uncertainties, but the overall workflow typically has a high expertise barrier and nontrivial implementation cost in practice. Finally, conventional simulators have limited capability for decision-making that continuously ingests real-time bottom-hole pressure and rate data; the lack of an efficient data-to-model feedback loop hinders progress toward true real-time optimization and intelligent management of CO2-EOR operations. These gaps, repeatedly observed in engineering practice, motivate the MPG-STNet framework proposed in this study.

Recent advances in machine learning and deep learning have broadened the toolset for forecasting complex subsurface dynamics, especially for time-dependent signals such as production rate and pressure. In classical pipelines, tree-based ensembles (e.g., XGBoost with Bayesian optimization and random forests) have demonstrated strong robustness to noisy, nonstationary data and correlated predictors, offering reliable performance in a range of spatiotemporal prediction tasks. , Deep sequence/vision models further strengthen long-horizon representation learning: long short-term memory (LSTM)-based architectures capture long-range temporal dependence, while CNN-based feature extractors provide effective spatial characterization that supports spatiotemporal fusion in engineering settings. , In petroleum applications, these ideas have been validated across multiple assets, including production forecasting studies on Norway’s Volve field (e.g., KAN/TFT) and the Norne field (e.g., N-BEATS/LSTM with transfer learning), as well as recent China-based works reporting adversarial transfer learning, Transformer-based forecasting, and physics-guided deep models for operational optimization. − Despite this progress, two deployment challenges remain critical for oil-and-gas workflows: maintaining generalization under domain shift/distribution drift and ensuring physical consistency and interpretability when extrapolating beyond the training regimemotivating physics-constrained, multimodal designs tailored to subsurface flow prediction.

To address these challenges, we developed MPG-STNet, a multimodal, physics-constrained spatiotemporal generative surrogate for long-horizon forecasting of CO2 saturation-field sequences. MPG-STNet provides capabilities that existing standalone or partially hybrid approaches typically lack. Compared with standalone Fourier neural operators (FNOs), which mainly learn field-to-field mappings under fixed forcing and are not designed to explicitly generalize across changing injector–producer layouts, MPG-STNet conditions the generator on well-pattern topology encoded as a graph, enabling transfer to unseen well configurations. Compared with Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN)-style methods, which enforce PDE residuals via direct backpropagation and can be difficult to scale to high-dimensional, long-horizon saturation sequences with heterogeneous operational data, MPG-STNet enforces physical plausibility through a discriminator-side material-balance residual, providing a practical physics constraint that works naturally with adversarial learning. Compared with graph networks that often focus on well-level scalar predictions, MPG-STNet targets dense, grid-level saturation fields by coupling (i) an enhanced FNO backbone (FP-FNO) to generate physically coherent coarse fields from static geology, (ii) a Graph Attention Network (GAT) to encode injection–production coupling and spatial topology, and (iii) a Transformer to represent long-range dependencies in production histories; these representations jointly condition a cGAN that refines displacement-front details while maintaining global consistency. This design explicitly links topology, operations, and physics constraints to saturation-field generation, forming the basis for multipattern generalization evaluated in the subsequent sections.

Data Set Design

To rigorously evaluate the topological generalization and physical fidelity of our model, we constructed a specialized synthetic data set that systematically varies two key factors: well-pattern configuration and geological heterogeneity. This design ensures that the model is tested on its ability to extrapolate to unseen well layoutsthe core of our generalization claim. Using MRST for geological generation and CMG for compositional flow simulation, we generated 4,500 high-fidelity scenarios spanning nine distinct well patterns over 150 months, ensuring that each sample provides the necessary multimodal inputs (static fields, dynamic series, and well graphs) and high-resolution saturation-field outputs for our proposed framework.

Geological Structure

High-quality geological modeling with quantifiable uncertainty is a prerequisite for generating credible saturation fields and for subsequent flow simulation. , We adopt a hybrid strategy that combines multiple-point geostatistics (MPS) with sequential Gaussian simulation: training images encode characteristic architectural styles and connectivity, while the target variogram captures scale-dependent spatial continuity; together, these yield permeability fields exhibiting complex spatial structures. The governing relation is given in eq . K(x)=exp(G(x)+ϵMPS(x)) 1 G(x) denotes a Gaussian random field that honors the prescribed variogram, and ϵMPS(x) is the residual introduced by the MPS procedure, consistent with the structural patterns of the training image.

The porosity field and permeability field are cogenerated via an empirical rock-physics relation so that the two properties remain physically compatible. The mapping is shown in eq . ϕ(x)=a+b·ln(K(x)−KminKmax−K(x)) 2 Where ϕ­(x) represents porosity, a and b are formation-specific empirical constants, and K min and K max denote cutoff bounds for permeability.

The pressure field is generated from a reference pressure and the hydrostatic gradient, and it is given in eq . Pi(x)=Pref+ρg(D(x)−Dref) 3 where P ref is the pressure at the reference depth D ref, ρ is the mean fluid density, g is gravitational acceleration, and D(x) is the grid-cell depth.

Temperature and reservoir thickness are also included as part of the static inputs; both follow geological priors with reasonable spatial trends and stochastic perturbations. Regarding the reservoir thickness, the prior information incorporates a relatively broad probability distribution, which is designed to enhance the robustness of the model. The majority of simulation outcomes, however, are concentrated within geologically common ranges. For example, while the overall range considered spans from 1 to 20 m, it is rare to encounter reservoirs as thin as 1 m in actual field blocks. Consequently, the thickness of the vast majority of samples falls within the 3–10 m interval. Extreme parameter values are not excluded; instead, they are treated as highly uncommon cases, which helps improve the learning and extrapolation capabilities of the deep learning model. The aforementioned five geological parameters and the permeability field are all configured as 100 × 100 grid matrices, with a default side length of 5 m assigned to each square cell. The application of a cosimulation methodology for these parameters significantly enhances the realism of the geological model. This approach not only reproduces complex geological structures but also preserves the consistency of petrophysical relationships among different property fields, thereby providing a reliable geological foundation for subsequent flow simulations.

Well Pattern Structure

The topology and producer–injector ratios of a well pattern are key design variables that govern the advance of the CO2 displacement front and ultimately determine sweep volume. Classic layouts include linear (line-drive) floods as well as five-, seven-, and nine-spot patterns. , Although these designs can reproduce many field cases of injection–production coupling, a fixed producer layout and a single injector count often lead to discrepancies between numerical forecasts and actual performance. To move beyond such limitations, we systematically consider nine representative regimes spanning one to three injectors paired with four, six, and eight producers. In the modeling, injectors and producers are encoded as −1 and +1, respectively, and each sample is assigned a distinct spatial arrangement of injection–production wells, thereby covering a spectrum of drive mechanisms from regular patterns to more complex mixed configurations.

Figure illustrates the design paradigm that couples a well-pattern topology with the corresponding permeability field. Injectors are preferentially placed along high-connectivity injection corridors to ensure stable injectivity and pressure support, while producers are positioned to track relatively stable displacement fronts and to enlarge the swept volumefacilitating dynamic surveillance and control, and avoiding severe misalignment between the advancing front and the principal permeability direction, which would otherwise degrade sweep efficiency and amplify forecast errors. To emulate field practice during sample generation, we impose a minimum interwell spacing constraint; any injector–injector, producer–producer, or injector–producer conflict (overlap or overly short spacing) is removed via resampling or small positional perturbations. The constraint is written in eq . ∥wm−wn∥2≥Lβπ,∀m≠n,β∈[0.6,0.8] 4 where w m and w n denote the coordinates of the two well locations, and L is a coordinate scaling factor determined by practical design considerations and the assumed statistical distribution. β ∈ [0.6,0.8] is a dimensionless well-spacing coefficient used to scale the strength of the minimum interwell distance constraint. Specifically, it multiplies the baseline minimum spacing, thereby controlling the sparsity–density of the well layout and preventing unrealistically tight placements that could lead to well-pattern overlap or numerical instability. The interval [0.6,0.8] is chosen to reflect typical engineering variability in practical well-spacing constraints: smaller β corresponds to denser development scenarios, whereas larger β represents more conservative layouts with wider spacing. This range increases layout diversity while maintaining geometrically feasible and physically reasonable well configurations.

Working Schedule

Dynamic injection–production schedules are central to the evolution of CO2 saturation fields in both CO2-EOR and CCUS. Compared with fixed injection plans, cyclic, harmonized control of rates can more robustly raise recovery under geological uncertainty while lowering early gas breakthrough and conformance risks. In particular, WAG has repeatedly been shown to enhance both microscopic and macroscopic sweep by moderating CO2 mobility and delaying channeling, with field applications (e.g., Australia’s Cornea field) and numerous studies underscoring its effectiveness. Accordingly, we link the injection-rate design with the WAG strategy, so that the injection signal reflects operational flexibility while still embodying the underlying displacement physics. Under baseline-rate control with interwell interference/operational adjustments, the gas-injection rate is written as eq . Qg(t)=Qg,base·(1+α·sin(2πtTcycle+ϕrand))·f(Pavg(t)) 5 Where Q g,base is the base rate set from the well-pattern and pore-volume considerations, α is the oscillation amplitude, T cycle is the cycle period, ϕrand is a random phase, and f(P avg(t)) is a modulation term tied to the pattern-average pressure to emulate interwell interference and operational tuning.

For selected patterns, we also apply nonuniform WAG: gas–water slug durations are drawn within a prescribed range and then adaptively adjusted each cycle based on the preceding cycle’s displacement efficiency. This time-varying, nonstationary rate design better mirrors field practice and avoids overfitting to a fixed schedule, enabling the deep model to learn how injection signals dynamically influence saturation, rather than merely reflecting a static mapping.

Deep Learning Models

After verifying that the data set adheres to the governing physics, we design a physics-constrained, multimodal deep-learning framework tailored to its multimodal makeup and spatiotemporal structure, and we employ a bespoke hyperparameter-optimization scheme. This section details each module’s design and advantages and then explains our strategy for module fusion and hyperparameter tuning.

FP-FNO Algorithm

FNO learns the functional mapping for solving coefficients directly through its operator-learning characteristic. It parametrizes the integral kernel in the Fourier domain, possessing properties of discretization invariance and a global receptive field, making it suitable for mapping static geological attributes to dynamic physical fields. , Therefore, the FNO module we designed aims to learn efficient mapping from static geological attributes to dynamic physical fields. Its input includes heterogeneous spatial fields such as permeability and attribute fields obtained by interpolating geological parameters, both defined as discrete functions on a regular grid. The core operation of FNO is performed in Fourier space. Given an input function a(x), it is transformed back to the physical space via the inverse Fourier transform F−1 , combined with a local linear transformation W, and finally outputs the target function u(x). The operation of a single FNO layer can be expressed as eq . u(x)=σ(Wa(x)+F−1[R·F[a]](x)) 8 Where σ is a nonlinear activation function, R is the processing matrix used to adjust the frequency-domain signal after the Fourier transform.

Additionally, we attach a differentiable mass conservation residual operator to the FNO output. Instead of solving a constrained projection, we compute the discrete mass-balance residual from the saturation equation and use it as a physics regularizer to penalize physically inconsistent predictions. The specific form is given by eq . Rt+1=S^t+1−(St−Δt∇h·(ut⊙f(S*))),Lmass=1T∑t=0T−1∥Rt+1∥22 9 Where S t and Ŝ t+1 represent the saturation fields at the current and predicted next time steps, respectively, Rt+1 denotes the discrete mass conservation residual field, Δt is the time-step size, ∇ h ·(·) is the discrete divergence operator on the Cartesian grid, u t is the Darcy velocity field at time step t used for advective transport, ⊙ denotes elementwise multiplication, and f(S*) is the CO2 fractional-flow function evaluated at the saturation state S*, with S* typically chosen as S t for flux calculation. T is the number of time steps in the prediction horizon, and Lmass is the time-averaged squared residual used as a physics regularizer to penalize violations of discrete mass conservation.

At each time step, the FP-FNO outputs Ŝ t+1. Using S* (set to S t or a smoothly bounded Ŝ t+1), we compute the flux u t ⊙ f(S*), apply ∇ h · to form Rt+1 , and add Lmass to the loss. All operations are differentiable, enabling back-propagation via automatic differentiation without a separate projection solver during training.

By stacking multiple such layers, FNOs with physical constraint (FP-FNO) can capture multiscale spatial dependencies. The output of this module is a preliminary, low-resolution sequence of CO2 saturation fields capturing macroscopic flow patterns dominated by static geological backgrounds. GAT and Temporal Transformer Encoders

Within each node’s neighborhood, the GAT encoder adaptively allocates neighbor weights by encoding self-attention, which naturally accommodates irregular topology and variable edge strength for well-pattern graphs. For this study, we selected the GAT to model well-pattern topology, as its attention mechanism can adaptively quantify interwell influence weights. Compared to the fixed filtering of Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN), GAT is more suitable for capturing dynamic injection-production correlations and nonuniform spatial dependencies, thereby enhancing the flexibility of topological representation. The encoder handles nine well-pattern regimes and their interwell spacing/adjacency relations by representing each pattern as a graph G = (V,E), where V are wells and E are weighted connections determined from Euclidean distances.

GAT introduces multihead self-attention (MHSA) so that, when aggregating neighborhood information, each node assigns different importances to different neighbors and then fuses them. For a center node and a neighbor, the attention score is given in eq . eij=LeakyReLU(a→T[Wh→i∥Wh→j]) 10 Where e ij is the attention score between nodes, h→i and h→j are the initial features, W is a learnable linear transform, a→T is the attention vector, and ∥ denotes concatenation. The scores are normalized over i’s neighborhood by a softmax as eqs and . αij=exp(eij)∑k∈Niexp(eik) 11 h→i’=σ(∑j∈NiαijWh→j) 12 Where α ij represents the normalized attention weight, and h→i′ represents the updated feature of the node.

For temporal modeling, a Transformer encoder is employed. Its MHSA mechanism explicitly captures cross-step dependencies within long sequences, offering superior advantages over LSTM networks in terms of long-range dependency modeling and computational efficiency, thereby effectively parsing the dynamic patterns embedded in production history. Therefore, the Transformer encoder that we designed aims to capture the long-term dependencies across samples and the monthly production rates of oil, gas, and water phases. The input sequence, after undergoing positional encoding, is processed through its embedded MHSA mechanism, which calculates the relevance between each time step and all of the other time steps. The formula is as eq . Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(QKTdk)V 13 Where Q, K, and V represent the query, key, and value matrices, respectively. Finally, the temporal feature vectors of all wells are concatenated and compressed to obtain a feature vector that encapsulates the dynamic production history.

Physics-Constrained cGAN

In the generator, the preliminary field produced by the FNO module serves as a draft input, while feature vectors from the GAT and Transformer encoders are fused via fully connected layers to perform spatial transformations and channel-wise sampling, yielding a refined saturation field. The discriminator then distinguishes real from generated fields. Module Integration and Hyperparameter Optimization

This study employs a hierarchical processing strategy to integrate three heterogeneous data modalitiesgeological, dynamic, and topological. The permeability field and derived geological attributes are passed directly into the FP-FNO module, which learns field-scale physical patterns and produces an initial saturation trend. Dynamic production sequences are encoded by a temporal Transformer to extract long-range dependencies into a compact feature vector. Well-network topology is processed by a GAT, where interwell influence is adaptively weighted to form a structured topological representation.

The fusion of the aforementioned three core modules is not a simple superposition but an organic coupling and spatial reconstruction achieved through feature concatenation and spatial transformation. The preliminary physical field generated by FP-FNO serves as the input “sketch” for the cGAN generator, establishing the fundamental physical structure of the prediction results. The feature vector representing the dynamic production history output by the Transformer and the feature vector representing the spatial structure generated by the GAT are concatenated along the channel dimension with the initial field map after necessary dimensionality adjustments, serving as key conditional variables to jointly guide the cGAN generator in performing detailed rendering. This enables the generator to effectively incorporate macroscopic physical trends, dynamic production context, and spatiotopological constraints when reconstructing the final high-resolution saturation field. During training, physical conservation constraints are integrated into the discriminator of the cGAN. Consequently, this discriminator not only effectively distinguishes between real and generated images but also assesses their physical plausibility based on embedded physical conservation laws. This dual constraint significantly enhances the physical credibility of the generated results. Subsequent experiments demonstrate that the integrated MPG-STNet exhibits excellent generalization capability, providing stable and reasonable predictions even for well network configurations and production schemes not encountered in the training data. More importantly, it can clearly delineate the sweep area and complex displacement fronts, which often appear blurred in traditional numerical simulations. This offers a promising technical pathway for real-time adjustment of injection-production strategies and optimization of CO2 sweep efficiency in oilfield operations. Deep learning model performance depends critically on hyperparameter selection, including the learning rate, network depth, and loss weights. To efficiently identify optimal settings, we employed the Optuna framework using the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator algorithm. Unlike grid or random search, Optuna adaptively samples promising hyperparameter combinations by constructing a probabilistic model from prior validation results, thereby converging to near-optimal configurations with fewer trials. , In practice, we defined a search space encompassing key hyperparameters such as learning rate, FNO modes, Transformer heads, and loss weights. During optimization, Optuna proposed configurations, trained the model, and updated its probabilistic model based on validation performance. Poor trials were automatically pruned to focus resources on promising candidates. This systematic approach ensures that MPG-STNet operates at its best performance, strengthening the reliability of our findings.

Results and Discussion

The central methodological challenge lies in fusing heterogeneous, high-dimensional data modalities into a cohesive, physics-constrained generative model. To this end, we introduce the MPG-STNet architecture, which is explicitly designed to (1) map static geology to dynamic fields via a physics-augmented neural operator, (2) encode discrete well topology for pattern-agnostic reasoning, and (3) enforce physical consistency within an adversarial learning paradigm. The following subsections detail each component and its role in addressing this fusion challenge.

Evaluating Metrics

This study employs four widely recognized quantitative evaluation metrics: mean squared error (MSE), mean absolute error (MAE), structural similarity index measure (SSIM), and coefficient of determination (R 2). These metrics collectively offer a rigorous assessment of model performance from multiple perspectives, including pixel-wise error, structural similarity, and explanatory power.

MSE quantifies the average of the squared differences between predicted and true values, imposing a greater penalty on larger errors. In this study, MSE is used to evaluate the overall prediction accuracy of the CO2 saturation field, with particular attention to regions exhibiting exceptionally high errors, such as displacement fronts. It is calculated as eq . MSE=1N∑i=1N(yi−y^i)2 16 Where N denotes the total number of pixels in the saturation field, y i and ŷ i represent the true saturation value and the model-predicted saturation value at the i-th pixel, respectively. An MSE value closer to zero indicates a higher overall prediction accuracy of the model.

MAE computes the average of the absolute differences between the predicted and true values. In contrast to the MSE, the MAE is less sensitive to outliers and provides a more intuitive reflection of the average prediction error. A smaller MAE value indicates that the model’s predictions are numerically closer to the true saturation field. It is defined as eq . MAE=1N∑i=1N|yi−y^i| 17 SSIM is a perceptual metric used to assess the similarity between two images in terms of luminance, contrast, and structure. For the CO2 saturation field prediction, the spatial distribution patternssuch as gas displacement fronts and fluid morphologyare equally important as numerical accuracy. SSIM effectively evaluates whether the model accurately reconstructs the spatial structural characteristics of the saturation field. Its calculation is based on a local sliding window, as shown in eq SSIM(x,y)=(2μxμy+C1)(2σxy+C2)(μx2+μy2+C1)(σx2+σy2+C2) 18 Where x and y represent the true and predicted saturation field image patches, respectively; μ x and μ y denote their mean values; σ x 2 and σ y 2 represent their variances; σ xy is their covariance; C 1 and C 2 are small constants added to ensure numerical stability. The SSIM value ranges from −1 to 1, with values closer to 1 indicating a higher structural similarity between the two images.

R 2 quantifies the model’s ability to explain the variance in the target variable. Model Training and Experimental Results

In our model, we define the total loss function as a combination of adversarial loss, physics loss, and MSE. The adversarial loss (eq ) encourages the generator to produce realistic saturation fields that can deceive the discriminator, ensuring that the model learns to generate plausible data. The physics loss, derived from the constraint (eq ), enforces physical consistency by penalizing any deviation from the mass balance in the generated fields. The MSE loss term is used to minimize the difference between the generated saturation fields and the reference saturation fields from the simulation, ensuring accurate data fitting. The final loss function is a weighted sum of these components.

When working with limited samples, employing stratified cross-validation enables full utilization of the data and yields more robust estimates of the generalization performance. This strategy helps reduce the relatively optimistic bias inherent in multifold training and evaluation while mitigating overfitting. Consequently, we adopted a stratified cross-validation strategy, randomly partitioning the complete data set containing 4500 samples into subsets representing nine distinct well pattern configurations and operational regimes. The data set was split into training, validation, and test sets in a 3:1:1 ratio. In each cross-validation round, one subset was sequentially selected as the test set, while the remaining eight subsets were used for training and validation. During the partitioning process, we ensured that the proportion of samples from each well pattern configuration remained consistent across the training and test sets. This precaution prevented the introduction of evaluation bias due to uneven distribution of well pattern structures. All experimental results are presented as the mean values along with their standard deviations derived from the cross-validation rounds, providing a comprehensive reflection of the model’s generalization capability and stability. Simultaneously, Optuna was utilized to systematically search and optimize key hyperparameters based on the loss performance observed on the validation set during training.

Ablation Experiment

In model design, ablation studies serve as critical steps for verifying the actual contribution of each component and quantifying its marginal benefit. This process effectively tests whether each modification genuinely leads to performance gains, thereby helping to mitigate the risk of overengineering and ensuring the necessity of the model’s complexity. Noteworthy cases from biomedical informatics have demonstrated that simplified models can sometimes outperform complex ones, highlighting the objective reality that complexity does not equate to effectiveness. The experimental results indicate that when physical constraints are removed, the model exhibits significant performance degradation in predictions for heterogeneous regions. Conversely, the loss of the GAT’s ability to encode interwell topological relationships leads to a substantial increase in prediction error near production wells. These findings demonstrate that each component within the MPG-STNet plays a crucial role in the overall performance, and the deep fusion of multimodal information is the core factor enabling high-precision predictions. Spatial topology, dynamic temporal sequences, and static geological parameters form a complete information chain for characterizing reservoir dynamics. The absence of any single element compromises the model’s overall reasoning capability. The modules do not simply combine additively, but rather create a profound synergistic enhancement effect. This insight provides a clear technical pathway for constructing the next generation of intelligent reservoir prediction models.

Conclusion

This study addresses the challenge of long-term sequence prediction for saturation fields in CO2-EOR and CCUS applications by developing a multimodal physics-constrained generative framework. The framework innovatively integrates the efficient field-to-field mapping capability of FP-FNO, the topological relationship representation strength of GAT, and the long-term dependency capture of the Transformer. By incorporation of physical conservation constraints into the cGAN, it ensures the physical plausibility of the generated results. The effectiveness of this method was validated on a high-fidelity data set encompassing 9 well patterns and 4500 samples. On the test set, the method achieved excellent metrics, with MSE and SSIM reaching 0.0062 and 0.9788, respectively, significantly outperforming a series of baseline models. It is particularly noteworthy that MPG-STNet not only provides a highly detailed characterization of complex displacement fronts and CO2 sweep volumes but also demonstrates exceptional generalization capability for well pattern configurations not encountered during training.

Our research confirms the effectiveness of neural operators in field-scale reservoir displacement prediction, achieving orders-of-magnitude efficiency improvements while maintaining physical resolution. Furthermore, we proposed a strategy of injecting well network topology and temporal operational conditions as conditional information into the generator and having conservation residuals participate in the adversarial training. This strategy effectively mitigates the issues of physical drift and error accumulation common to generative models. From an engineering application perspective, this study provides a new technical pathway for rapid injection-production scheme screening, sweep efficiency evaluation, and storage capacity prediction, offering a powerful tool for optimizing decision-making at the field level. Future research will focus on extending the application of this method to complex geological conditions involving strong heterogeneity and multiformation coupling, thereby further enhancing its capability to address practical oilfield challenges.
