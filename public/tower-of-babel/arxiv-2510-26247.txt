Curious QNEIs from QNEC: New Bounds on Null Energy in Quantum Field Theory
Jackson R. Fliss, and Andrew Rolph

Abstract
We derive new families of quantum null energy inequalities (QNEIs), i.e. bounds on integrated null energy, in quantum field theories in two and higher dimensions. These are universal, state-independent lower bounds on semi-local integrals of ⟨ T v ​ v ⟩ \langle T_{vv}\rangle , the energy-momentum flux in a null direction, and the first of this kind for interacting theories in higher dimensions. Our ingredients include the quantum null energy condition (QNEC), strong subadditivity of von Neumann entropies, defect operator expansions, and the vacuum modular Hamiltonians of null intervals and strips. These results are fundamental constraints on null energy in quantum field theories.

Introduction

Energy plays a ubiquitous role in physics;
it underlies dynamics in all systems, connects matter to geometry in gravity, and is central to thermodynamics and statistical physics.
Many physical classical field theories obey pointwise energy conditions, and these have played an important role in, for example, singularity theorems
penrose1965gravitational
.
However, for local, relativistic quantum field theories (QFTs), the study of energy is both richer and more precarious.
Due to the short-distance entanglement of the vacuum, the energy-momentum density at any point can be made arbitrarily negative
epstein1965nonpositivity
.

To get a lower bound on the stress tensor operator in QFTs, it must be ‘smeared’ – integrated against a test function – over a spacetime domain.
Such constraints are called
quantum energy inequalities
(QEIs)
Fewster:2012yh
.
In general, a QEI can express the lower bound on the energy density in terms of the expectation values of other operators; however, particularly powerful are
state-independent
QEIs which express smeared energy densities in terms of a state-independent functional of the smearing, schematically:

\int_{D}\text{d}^{p}x\,g(x)\langle\xi_{1}^{\mu}\xi_{2}^{\nu}T_{\mu\nu}\rangle_{\psi}\geq-\mathcal{F}_{\xi_{1,2}}[g]~.

(1)

g
is a smooth and positive smearing function with support on a spacetime region
D
. The dimension
p
of
D
varies between QEIs; anything from a one-dimensional curve to a codim-0 subregion.

\mathcal{F}_{\xi_{1,2}}
is a functional of
g
that depends on the components of the stress-energy tensor being considered,
as well as other details of the quantum field theory (e.g. correlation lengths).

From here onwards, whenever we use the term ‘QEI’, we will mean a state-independent bound of the form (
1
).
Perhaps the best known QEI is the
averaged null energy condition
(ANEC),
which states that the null stress tensor integrated uniformly over an entire (achronal) null geodesic is positive in all states:

\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{d}v\,\langle T_{vv}\rangle\geq 0~,\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{(ANEC)}

(2)

where
v^{\mu}
is the null vector generating the geodesic.
Among other applications, achronal ANEC is necessary for causality in AdS/CFT
Gao:2000ga
, and forbids long traversable wormholes
morris1988wormholes
;
Hochberg:1998ii
.
However, of additional interest are QEIs of the more semi-local variety, where, unlike in ANEC,
D
is a compact domain.
Due to their state-independence and semi-locality, such QEIs have been a key tool in extending the classical singularity theorems of general relativity (such as those by Hawking and Penrose
penrose1965gravitational
;
Hawking:1966sx
;
Hawking:1966jv
;
Hawking:1967ju
) to semi-classical gravity coupled to QFT
Brown:2018hym
;
Fewster:2019bjg
;
Fewster:2021mmz
.

While the ANEC has been shown to hold for all QFTs in Minkowski spacetime
Faulkner:2016mzt
;
Hartman:2016lgu
, and for free theories in certain classes of curved spacetimes
Wald:1991xn
;
Kelly:2014mra
;
Kontou:2015yha
, deriving and proving QEIs of the semi-local variety is a difficult endeavour. Perhaps the best known example is Fewster and Hollands’ proof that all two-dimensional conformal field theories (CFTs) admit the following semi-local QEI on the stress tensor
Fewster:2004nj

\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\int\text{d}v\,g(v)\langle T_{vv}\rangle\geq-\frac{c}{48\pi}\int\text{d}v\frac{g^{\prime}(v)^{2}}{g(v)}~,\qquad\text{(FH bound)}

(3)

where
c
is the central charge of the CFT. See also
Blanco:2017akw
for a stronger but state-dependent version of (
3
), and
Blanco:2013lea
, which derives bounds on how far negative energy can be isolated away from positive energy. Both of those papers use monotonicity of relative entropy, which we will use too.
The first result in this paper will be a new, elementary proof and extension of (
3
).

A different extension of the FH bound to states of
d>2
QFTs consistently coupled to semi-classical gravity, coined the
smeared null energy condition
(SNEC), was conjectured by Freivogel and Krommydas
Freivogel:2018gxj

\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\int\text{d}v\,g(v)\langle T_{vv}\rangle\geq-\frac{1}{32\pi G_{N}}\int\text{d}v\frac{g^{\prime}(v)^{2}}{g(v)}~,\qquad\text{(SNEC)}

(4)

in which the central charge is replaced by the inverse Newton’s constant. The key differences with respect to the FH bound are the coupling to gravity and that
d>2
. The SNEC has been proven for holographic CFTs coupled to induced gravity in a braneworld model
Leichenauer:2018tnq
, however, broader evidence for the SNEC has so far remained elusive. This is partially because, as a gravitational bound, gravitational physics must be part of its derivation.

Amongst the QEIs that can be established purely in QFTs in greater than two dimensions, almost all results have been established for free QFTs (with ANEC the notable counterexample).
Furthermore, it is known that certain free theories do
not
admit state-independent lower bounds:
an example is the non-minimally coupled scalar field for which one can construct states of arbitrarily negative expectation value of the timelike or null-contracted stress tensor, regardless of the domain over which it is smeared
Fewster:2007ec
;
Fliss:2023rzi
. This
non-existence
of a QEI is pertinent to the free scalar CFT even in flat space, where the non-minimal coupling manifests as an improvement term needed to make the stress tensor traceless.

It is natural to wonder whether this QEI non-existence is particular to free field theories, or whether QEIs are generically too much to hope for.
Before this paper, this question remained largely unanswered, though investigations into large-
N
CFTs with a ‘generalized free field’ structure have indicated that interactions may prevent the ‘pile-up’ of negative energy density allowed in free theories
Fliss:2024dxe
.

We will derive new QEIs on null energy, and so answer this question.
To do so, we will make use of a completely complementary approach from those used so far in deriving QEIs, one that makes use of the connection between energy and entropy,
through the

quantum null energy condition
(QNEC)
Bousso:2015mna
, which bounds the local expectation value of the null stress tensor by a second-order shape derivative
of the entanglement entropy at that point:

\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\langle T_{vv}(v)\rangle\geq\frac{1}{2\pi}\partial_{v}^{2}S~.\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{(QNEC)}

(5)

The above formula is schematic; in the body of this paper, we will provide more precise details.
The QNEC lends a universality to our results, because it has been proven for free and super-renormalisable
Bousso:2015wca
, holographic
Koeller:2015qmn
, and generic interacting QFTs
Balakrishnan:2017bjg
;
Ceyhan:2018zfg
;
Hollands:2025glm
.
Moreover, for
d>2
interacting theories, there is strong evidence that this local form of the QNEC is saturated
Leichenauer:2018obf
;
Balakrishnan:2019gxl
, implying that it is the tightest possible bound on local energy densities. Note that ANEC follows from integrating QNEC.
The entanglement entropy that appears in (
5
) obeys its own inequalities – weak monotonicity, strong subadditivity (SSA), and others – and we will leverage these in our derivations to go from entropy-dependent to state-independent bounds.

In the gravitational context, QNEC, and the
quantum focussing conjecture
(QFC) from which it descends
Bousso:2015mna
, connect energy and entropy, which has been insightful for holography and semi-classical gravity
Faulkner:2022mlp
.
Related inequalities, such as the generalised second law
Bekenstein:1974ax
and the Bousso bound
Bousso:1999xy
;
Rolph:2022csa
, are also effective starting points for constraining semi-classical gravity in a manner complementary to QEI-based approaches
Wall:2010jtc
;
Bousso:2022cun
;
Bousso:2022tdb
;
Bousso:2025xyc
.

However, from a purely field-theoretic, non-gravitational point of view, it is not clear that the QNEC, even when it is saturated, is an effective bound on the energy density.
For one, there are states for which the right-hand side of (
5
)
is arbitrarily negative
(consistent with the pointwise stress tensor being an unbounded operator).
Secondly, at a practical level, the evaluation of entanglement entropy is extremely difficult, except for certain symmetric regions and for holographic theories
Ryu:2006bv
.
Lastly, there is an undesirable mismatch between the linear (on the left-hand side) and non-linear (on the right-hand side) state dependences of (
5
).

In this paper, we will derive new state-independent bounds on smeared null energy in generic interacting QFTs. We call these
quantum null energy inequalities
(QNEIs).
Our method will be to start by integrating QNEC against a smearing function, and then, to make the bound state-independent, use entanglement entropy inequalities, vacuum modular Hamiltonians, and defect operator product expansions (OPEs).

Firstly, in Section
2.1
, we show that the ‘conformally improved’ QNEC
Wall:2011kb
directly implies the FH bound (
3
) and extends it to all super-renormalizable QFTs. Our derivation is extremely simple, yet, as far as we are aware, has not yet been presented in the literature.

Subsequently, in Section
2.2
, we show how the QNEC, along with monotonicity of relative entropy, imply an infinite family of QNEIs in generic 2d QFTs parametrised by a function
\zeta
. To be specific, given a positive and localised smearing function
g
, our bounds take the following form

\boxed{\int\text{d}v\,m_{[g,\zeta]}(v)\langle T_{vv}\rangle\geq-\frac{c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{UV}}}{12\pi}\int\text{d}v\frac{g^{\prime}(v)}{v-\zeta(v)}~,}

(6)

where
c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{UV}}
is the central charge of the UV-fixed point of the theory and
m_{[g,\zeta]}(v)
is a convolution of
g
with a kernel associated to the modular Hamiltonian of a null strip whose precise form is given in (
59
). Much like
g
itself,
m
is positive and localised (e.g. has compact support when
g
has compact support).
The function
\zeta
essentially parametrizes the choice of null strips as entangling regions in our monotonicity argument and appears in the definition of
m
.

A simple grounding example is given by choosing
\zeta(v)=v+\sgn(v)\delta
, with
\delta>0
. Then the right hand side of (
6
) simply becomes
-c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{UV}}g(0)/(6\pi\delta)
. The relevant
m
to which it applies is also straightforwardly computable depending on the initial smearing function. We can, e.g., let
g
be a Gaussian. Then the correction to the original smearing function,
h=m-g
, is given by (
141
) and plotted in Fig.
4
.

In Section
3
, we adapt our methods to interacting CFTs in
d>2
dimensions. By considering the monotonicity argument applied to ‘nearly null’ strips, we derive an infinite family of QNEIs smeared over a causal diamond uniform in
d-2
transverse directions. Again, to be specific, given a positive and localized function
g(u,v)
of the lightcone coordinates, we derive

\boxed{\begin{split}\int\text{d}^{d-2}y_{\perp}&\int\text{d}u\text{d}v\mathcal{M}_{[g,\zeta]}(u,v)\langle T_{vv}\rangle\\
&\geq-\frac{\beta\,c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}}}{2\pi}\int\text{d}^{d-2}y_{\perp}\int\text{d}u\text{d}v\left(\frac{\partial_{v}g(u,v)}{\epsilon_{u}(u,v)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(v-\zeta(u,v))^{\frac{d}{2}}}+O(\epsilon_{u})\right)~,\end{split}}

(7)

where
c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}}
is the stress tensor two-point coefficient, and
\beta
is a theory-dependent constant that we will define later.
\mathcal{M}
is defined completely analogously as
m
in our
d=2
bound: it is a particular convolution of
g
with a kernel associated to the modular Hamiltonian of a null strip and depends on the choice of
\zeta
. Its precise definition is given in (
3.2
). The new ingredient in (
7
) in comparison to (
6
) is the additional function
\epsilon_{u}
, and that (
7
) applies when
\epsilon_{u}\ll 1
. The origin of
\epsilon_{u}
is a parameterization of how far away the ‘nearly null’ strip regions in our derivation are from being truly null. We discuss relations of this new family of higher-dimensional QNEIs to the recently proposed
double smeared energy condition
(DSNEC)
Fliss:2021gdz
;
Fliss:2021phs
. While the uniform transverse smearing imposes some limitations on the utility of our results (essentially introducing a divergent IR volume to the bound),
to our knowledge, our results present both the first instances of QEIs derived taking the QNEC as an input, and the first QEIs in a large class of interacting theories in greater than two dimensions. We see this as a major step in addressing the existence of state-independent bounds on locally smeared energy density in generic interacting theories.

Notational conventions

We will denote the vacuum state of the QFT by
|\Omega\rangle
and a generic state by
|\psi\rangle
. Expectation values will be denoted by a subscript, i.e.

\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{\psi}=\langle\psi|\mathcal{O}|\psi\rangle~.

(8)

When an expectation value is for a generic state, we will often omit the subscript. We will always denote vacuum quantities as such, however.

Entropies depend both on a region and a state. We will denote state dependence with a subscript and the region dependence with parentheses. The complement of a region,
A
, will be denoted as
A^{c}
. Much like expectation values, when the context is clear for a generic state, we will often omit this subscript. Vacuum entropies will always be denoted as such with a subscript. To give an example of our notation, let us recall the definition of the vacuum entanglement entropy here:

\rho_{\Omega}(A)=\textrm{Tr}_{A^{c}}\ket{\Omega}\bra{\Omega}~,\qquad S_{\Omega}(A)=-\textrm{Tr}_{A}\left(\rho_{\Omega}(A)\log\rho_{\Omega}(A)\right)~.

(9)

We will work in
d
-dimensional Minkowski spacetime in ‘mostly plus’ signature:

\text{d}s^{2}=\eta_{\mu\nu}\text{d}x^{\mu}\text{d}x^{\nu}=-\text{d}t^{2}+\delta_{ij}\text{d}x^{i}\text{d}x^{j}~.

(10)

We will use lightcone coordinates
v=t+x^{1}
and
u=t-x^{1}
, denoting the remaining coordinates on
\mathbb{R}^{d-2}
as
\vec{y}_{\perp}
:

\text{d}s^{2}=-\text{d}u\text{d}v+\sum_{i=2}^{d}\text{d}y_{\perp}^{i}\text{d}y_{\perp}^{i}~.

(11)

Two-dimensional quantum null energy inequalities

2.1
Fewster-Hollands bound from 2d QNEC

Consider a QFT on 2d Minkowski spacetime and let
T_{vv}(v)
be the
vv
component of its stress tensor at the point
p=(0,v)
. The choice of
u=0
is without loss of generality.
The QNEC for 2d QFTs is
Wall:2011kb
;
Bousso:2015mna
;
Koeller:2015qmn

2\pi\langle T_{vv}(v)\rangle\geq\partial_{v}^{2}S(v)+\frac{6}{c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{UV}}}(\partial_{v}S(v))^{2}~,

(12)

where
c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{UV}}
is the central charge of the CFT at the QFT’s UV fixed point.
S(v)
is the entanglement entropy of the state
\psi
in question reduced on any achronal curve with one endpoint at
p
, such as a semi-infinite curve ending at the
(0,v)
and extending to infinity
1
1

1

If the global state is pure, then
S(v)
is the same whether the half-line extends to the left or right. If the state is mixed, then this is no longer true, and there are separate QNEC inequalities for the left and right half-lines.
.

The 2d QNEC (
12
) has been proven for holographic 2d CFTs, and relevant deformations thereof, in
Koeller:2015qmn
, but not in complete generality.
Compared to QNEC in higher dimensions, the 2d QNEC is special because it has the
(\partial_{v}S)^{2}
term, which is absent from the higher-dimensional QNEC. In CFTs, the 2d QNEC is saturated by Bañados states (Virasoro coadjoint orbit states)
Khandker:2018xls
;
Ecker:2019ocp
. The
(\partial_{v}S)^{2}
term makes the right-hand side of (
12
) transform as a conformal primary, which means that both sides of the inequality have the same conformal anomaly, making it independent of the conformal frame
Wall:2011kb
.

Let
g(v)
be a smooth, non-negative, smearing function which is either a bump function or a Schwartz function.
2
2

2

Bump functions, also known as test functions, are smooth and have compact support. An example would be
g(v)=\exp((v^{2}-\sigma^{2})^{-1})
for
|v|\leq\sigma
and
0
for
|v|>0
. Schwartz functions, such as the Gaussian function, decay faster than any power at infinity.

We integrate both sides of (
12
) against
g(v)
, and then integrate the

\partial_{v}^{2}S
term
by parts, giving

2\pi\int\text{d}vg(v)\langle T_{vv}\rangle\geq\int\text{d}v\left(\frac{6}{c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{UV}}}g(v)(\partial_{v}S)^{2}-g^{\prime}(v)\partial_{v}S\right)+\left[g(v)\partial_{v}S\right]^{\infty}_{-\infty}~.

(13)

We assume that the boundary term vanishes, which is true if
g(v)
is a bump function, and a mild assumption on
\partial_{v}S
at infinity if
g(v)
is a Schwartz function.
Next, we take the rest of the right-hand side of (
13
), complete the square for the
\partial_{v}S
terms, and drop the resulting non-negative, squared term from the inequality, and this gives

\boxed{\int\text{d}vg(v)\langle T_{vv}\rangle\geq-\frac{c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{UV}}}{48\pi}\int\text{d}v\frac{g^{\prime}(v)^{2}}{g(v)}~.}

(14)

This is precisely the Fewster-Hollands (FH) bound for 2d CFTs
Fewster:2004nj
introduced in Section
1
.

To give an example and gain some intuition for the
FH bound, let us take
g(v)
to be a normalised Gaussian of width
\sigma
, for which (
14
) becomes

\int\text{d}vg(v)\langle T_{vv}\rangle\geq-\frac{c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{UV}}}{48\pi\sigma^{2}}~.

(15)

If we take
\sigma\to\infty
, then this bound reduces to ANEC, and the bound becomes trivial in the
\sigma\to 0
limit. The latter limit is in accordance with the general expectation that pointwise energy densities are unbounded in QFT.

Some other comments on the
FH bound (
14
):

•

The right-hand side is state-independent, and also non-positive definite, as it must be for a state-independent energy bound given that the LHS is zero for the vacuum state.

•

Unlike the 2d QNEC, (
12
), the FH
bound is not saturated for the vacuum state for the simple reason that
\langle T_{vv}\rangle_{\Omega}=0
. However at the UV fixed point, the bound is tight and the states saturating it can easily be constructed
jrf_modave
as

|\psi\rangle=U[g]|\Omega\rangle~,

(16)

where
U[g]
is the unitary operator implementing the conformal transformation

U[g]T_{vv}(v)U[g]^{\dagger}=(\tilde{v}^{\prime}(v))^{2}T_{\tilde{v}\tilde{v}}(\tilde{v}(v))-\frac{c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{UV}}}{24\pi}\{\tilde{v},v\}~,

(17)

with
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
and Schwarzian derivative and
\tilde{v}(v)
chosen such that
\tilde{v}^{\prime}(v)=1/g(v)
.

•

The FH
bound (
14
) only holds in two dimensions; there are explicit counterexamples in higher dimensions. Namely, it was shown by Fewster and Roman that the left-hand side of (
14
) is unbounded in the class of “0+2 states” (superpositions of zero and two-particle states) of free scalar field theory in
d=4
dimensions
Fewster:2002ne
. More generally, one can construct a series of squeezed states in free scalar field theories of arbitrary dimension
d>2
where the left-hand side of (
14
) can be made arbitrarily negative
Fliss:2021gdz
. However, when the free theory comes equipped with a UV cutoff on the transverse momentum, then the properties of free theories quantized on lightsheets
Burkardt:1995ct
;
Wall:2011hj
allow one to “bootstrap” the FH
bound to higher dimensions:

\int\text{d}v\,g(v)\langle T_{vv}(v)\rangle\geq-\frac{c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{UV}}}{48\pi\,a^{d-2}}\int\text{d}v\frac{(g^{\prime})^{2}}{g}~.

(18)

Here
a
is the ‘pencil width’ of the lightsheet discretized in the transverse directions, which provides a natural (inverse) UV cutoff on the transverse momenta, and
c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{UV}}
is simply the number of independent scalar fields.

•

Our derivation of (
14
) is novel because it is both elementary and extends the proof of this
FH bound to all 2d QFTs for which (
12
) is proven, which includes some non-conformal theories, namely relevant deformations of holographic 2d CFTs
Koeller:2015qmn
.

•

Lastly, as mentioned above, the coefficient
c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{UV}}
appearing in (
14
) is the central charge of the QFT’s UV fixed point. However, at the other end of the RG flow, the QFT is described by an IR CFT with central charge
c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{IR}}
. The stress tensor of this theory will satisfy the FH
bound with coefficient
c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{IR}}
, which can be proven directly in the CFT. This is consistent with our claim due to the monotonicity of the central charge under RG flows
Zamolodchikov:1986gt
,

c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{IR}}\leq c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{UV}}~.

(19)

2.2
An family of 2d QNEIs

2.2.1
Integrating QNEC

Integrating (

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{d}vg(v)\langle T_{vv}(v)\rangle\geq-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{d}v~g^{\prime}(v)~\partial_{v}S(v)~.

A canonical example of which to keep in mind would be the normalized Gaussian,

We have already hinted at this in our discussion at the end of that section, namely that within the context of free field theories, it is possible to construct a series of squeezed states with no lower bound on their null-energy density integrated along a single null geodesic. Also, as pointed out in

In addition to the concrete counterexamples in free theory, there exists a general argument against a lower bound on null energy density integrated over a finite segment of a null geodesic which goes as follows: (1) the operator

8

8

, which establishes a lower bound on the null variation of

\displaystyle\partial_{v}S(A)\geq\int\text{d}^{d-2}y_{\perp}

\displaystyle\left[-2\pi\int_{v}^{v_{C}}\text{d}v^{\prime}\mathcal{G}\left(\frac{v_{C}-v^{\prime}}{v_{C}-v}\right)\langle T_{vv}(u,v^{\prime},y_{\perp})\rangle-\frac{\beta_{d}\,c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}}}{\epsilon_{C}(u)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(v_{C}-v)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+O(\epsilon_{C})~,

(104)

where, using the properties of

), we’ve employed
\mathcal{G}(1-s)=\mathcal{G}(s)+G^{\prime}(s)~.

We can now follow similar steps to those in Section
2.2
. That is, we return to the integral (
64
)
and we will suppose
g(u,v)
to be such that
-\text{sgn}(v)\partial_{v}g\geq 0
and we will define functions

\zeta(u,v)=\begin{cases}v_{B}(u,v),&v<0\\
v_{C}(u,v),&v\geq 0\\
\end{cases}~,\qquad\epsilon_{u}(u,v)=\begin{cases}\epsilon_{B}(u,v),&v<0\\
\epsilon_{C}(u,v),&v\geq 0\\
\end{cases}~.

(105)

More generally, we can take
\zeta(u,v)
and

to be any functions such that

\text{sgn}(v)\text{sgn}(\zeta(u,v)-v)\geq 0~,\qquad-\text{sgn}(v)\text{sgn}(\epsilon_{u}(u,v))\geq 0~.

(106)

Next we invert the integration domain to arrive at

\displaystyle\int\text{d}^{d-2}y_{\perp}\int\text{d}u\text{d}v

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(u,v)\,\langle T_{vv}\rangle\geq

\displaystyle-\frac{\beta_{d}\,c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}}\,V_{\perp}}{2\pi}\int\text{d}u\text{d}v\,\left(\frac{\partial_{v}g(u,v)}{\epsilon_{u}(u,v)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(v-\zeta(u,v))^{\frac{d}{2}}}+O(\epsilon_{u})\right)~,

(107)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(u,v):=

\displaystyle g(u,v)-\int_{\zeta^{-1}(u,v)}^{v}\,\text{d}v^{\prime}(\partial_{v^{\prime}}g(u,v^{\prime}))\mathcal{G}\left(\frac{v-\zeta(u,v^{\prime})}{v^{\prime}-\zeta(u,v^{\prime})}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\zeta^{-1}(u,v)}^{v}\text{d}v^{\prime}\,g(u,v)\,G^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{v-\zeta(u,v^{\prime})}{v^{\prime}-\zeta(u,v^{\prime})}\right)\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}v^{\prime}}\left(\frac{v-\zeta(u,v^{\prime})}{v^{\prime}-\zeta(u,v^{\prime})}\right)^{2}~.

(108)

We see that the function

is positive, and has compact support when

Similar manipulations to those at the end of Section

\displaystyle\int\text{d}^{d-2}y_{\perp}

\displaystyle\int\text{d}u\,\text{d}v\,\mathcal{M}(u,v)\langle T_{vv}(u,v,\vec{y}_{\perp})\rangle\geq

\displaystyle-\frac{\beta_{d}c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}}V_{\perp}}{2\pi}\int\text{d}u\,\text{d}v\left(\frac{\partial_{v}\mathcal{M}(u,\zeta)\Theta(\partial_{v}\zeta-1)+\partial_{v}\mathcal{M}(u,v)\Theta(1-\partial_{v}\zeta)}{\epsilon_{u}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(v-\zeta)^{\frac{d}{2}}}+O(\epsilon_{u})\right)

where for brevity of notation we have left the

Again, much like our simplified 2d QEI, (

) is now expressed in terms of a single smearing function,
\mathcal{M}

and

parameterizing choices of nearly null intervals. For a given

can be realized by a choice of

appearing in (

4
Discussion

In this paper, we have derived new quantum null energy inequalities, i.e. lower bounds on
\langle T_{vv}\rangle_{\psi}
, the null-null component of the stress tensor, integrated over a spacetime region in interacting quantum field theories. While our study of QNEIs is partially motivated by their potential applications to semi-classical gravity (e.g. as input in a semi-classical singularity theorme) such lower bounds on null energy are, of course, of intrinsic interest in the study of interacting QFTs. In particular the foundation of our QNEIs are deep connections between energy and entropy: in our derivation we utilize QNEC, strong subadditivity, and the vacuum modular Hamiltonians of null strips. More specifically, given a positive smearing function,
g
, we bound the stress tensor integrated against a smooth kernel, determined by the properties of the modular Hamiltonian of a null strip, in terms of a state-independent functional of
g
. In the case of
d>2
dimensions, our bounds are the first such
state-independent QEIs integrated over a finite null segment in a broad class of interacting QFTs, namely CFTs.

For
d=2
, these QNEIs are

\int\text{d}v\,m(v)\langle T_{vv}\rangle\geq-\frac{c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{UV}}}{12\pi}\int\text{d}v\frac{g^{\prime}(v)}{v-\zeta(v)}~.

(111)

where a precise form of
m
can be found in (
59
). In contrast to QNEC, these lower bounds are state-independent, because the right hand side does not depend on the state
\psi
. And, in contrast to ANEC, these bound are localised, in the sense that we can choose
g
such that we are integrating
\langle T_{vv}\rangle
against a bump function on the left hand side.

We have an infinite family of QNEIs, one for each choice of
\zeta
, which is both powerful but also increases the complexity of the inequality. The choice of
\zeta
affects both the definition of
m
, the correction to the smearing function, and the value of the lower bound and arises from a choice of null intervals appearing in intermediate stages of our derivation. However, ultimately
\zeta
is an invertible function obeying (
48
), but otherwise arbitrary.
For a given
\langle T_{vv}\rangle
, there does exist a
\zeta
that makes this intermediate stage inequality as tight as possible, but, for general
\langle T_{vv}\rangle
, there is not an analytic solution for this
\zeta
(see Sec.
2.2.4
).
We explicitly determined the QNEI for a few example choices of
\zeta
. It would be interesting to see whether the general form of our QNEIs can be simplified.

In higher dimensions, the QNEIs are of the form

\begin{split}\int\text{d}^{d-2}y_{\perp}&\int\text{d}u\text{d}v\mathcal{M}(u,v)\langle T_{vv}\rangle\\
&\geq-\frac{\beta\,c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}}}{2\pi}\int\text{d}^{d-2}y_{\perp}\int\text{d}u\text{d}v\left(\frac{\partial_{v}g(u,v)}{\epsilon_{u}(u,v)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(v-\zeta(u,v))^{\frac{d}{2}}}+O(\epsilon_{u})\right)~,\end{split}

(112)

where
\mathcal{M}
is defined in (
3.2
). As for
d=2
, this inequality is state-independent, and the integral over
\langle T_{vv}\rangle
is localised (in the
(u,v)
plane). Unlike for
d=2
, we have to integrate
\langle T_{vv}\rangle
over the second null direction
u
to get a non-trivial bound and over the transverse directions
y_{\perp}
to get a universal result.
Additionally this bound depends on a second function,
\epsilon_{u}
, which parametrises how close we take our strip regions to being null in an intermediate step of the derivation (see Sec. (
3.2
)), and we have to work perturbatively in this function, to make use of the universal form of
\Delta K
for null strips. To our knowledge, these are the first QEIs, integrated over a finite null region, proven for higher-dimensional interacting QFTs.

While we view this work as an important first step in the study of QNEIs in interacting theories there is still much more to explore:

•

Our QNEIs in higher dimensions are localised in the
(u,v)
plane, but not in the transverse space, so they are not truly localised bounds on the stress tensor. Additionally this integration strictly trivialises the right-hand side of our bounds (albeit in a physically uniteresting way). It would obviously be interesting and useful to try to leverage the techniques we’ve established here to find bounds that are also localized in this transverse direction. At a technical level, it was necessary to have a flat entanglement cut in the transverse directions to make use of the results for
\Delta K
for a null strip given in
Bousso:2014uxa
. Having the expression for
\Delta K
for null strips with “wiggly edges” would allow us to utilize local variations and the QNEC in its local form, (
69
). However this expression for the modular Hamiltonian is not known outside of free theories. The modular Hamiltonians of infinite null half-sheets with wiggly edges
do
admit closed form expressions
Faulkner:2016mzt
;
Casini:2017roe
however we have so far not been able to leverage this expression to derive any new interesting bounds. Regardless, it is worth revisiting and reviewing this approach more carefully in the future.

It is also useful to look for alternative approaches for deriving a truly local QNEI. One potential approach is trying to realize the stress tensor in the OPE of two operators. This is essentially a more sophisticated form of the free field theory statement that
T_{vv}
is a square of two operators. One obvious hang-up to this approach is that operator spectra in CFTs are not universal and so there is potential ambiguity in which OPE we try to realize stress tensor. To address this we can once again utilize entanglement based techniques. Namely
defect CFTs
living along twist defects defining replica path-integrals admit a universal set of displacement operators with the stress tensor appearing in their OPE channel
Balakrishnan:2019gxl
. One might speculatively hope that a reflection symmetric OPE of such displacement operators could provide a universal and local QEI in interacting CFTs.

•

We have worked in Minkowski spacetime, both for simplicity and because our QNEIs are only proven for theories and background geometries for which our inputs are proven, QNEC in particular. The proof of QNEC given in
Balakrishnan:2017bjg
is valid for all relativistic QFTs in Minkowski spacetime. However, there are also proofs of QNEC on curved backgrounds,
for a smaller set of QFTs,
such as in
Bousso:2014sda
;
Fu:2017evt
. It would advantageous, particularly with the application to semi-classical gravity in mind, to generalise our work to curved backgrounds. One obstacle to overcome is the transverse integration of the previous bullet point. Because the integration region of our bound in
d>2
dimensions could detect global structures of a background, we might incur large curvature corrections or even topological obstructions to our bound. A more local expression of the form mentioned in the previous bullet would allow us to work perturbatively when smearing over regions smaller than the typical curvature scale.

•

Our derivations take QNEC as one of their inputs. In the same way that, while ANEC can be proven by integrating QNEC along a complete null line, it can also be proven directly using causality
Hartman:2016lgu
and monotonicity of relative entropy
Faulkner:2016mzt
, so we expect that our QNEIs can be proven without taking QNEC as input, perhaps using the causality and monotonicity of relative entropy.

•

It would be interesting to extend our work to QFTs coupled to gravity. One intriguing possibility is that gravity itself provides a natural regulator of negative energy densities. This is essentially the core idea of the SNEC (
4
) mentioned in the introduction of this paper. However, as mentioned there, collective evidence for or even proving such QEIs is a hard task because they require coupling to gravity as input. Existing proofs of QNEC, such as
Balakrishnan:2017bjg
, assume a fixed background geometry, but the original statement of QNEC came as a corrollorary of the QFC
Bousso:2015mna
, and the motivation of that paper was to study QFTs coupled to gravity.
It would be interesting to see whether QEIs for QFTs coupled to gravity can be proven directly from the QFC. We are actively working in this direction and will report results in a future publication
gravRolphBounds
.

Appendix A
Basics of functional differentiation.

Suppose we have a functional
F[f]
. The functional derivatives are defined through

\delta F[f]:=F[f+\delta f]-F[f]=\int\text{d}x\,\frac{\delta F}{\delta f(x)}\delta f(x)+\frac{1}{2}\int\int\text{d}x\,\text{d}y\,\frac{\delta^{2}F}{\delta f(x)\delta f(y)}\delta f(x)\delta f(y)+\dots

(113)

Simple example 1:

F[f]=\int\text{d}x\,f(x)^{2}
. Then

\frac{\delta F}{\delta f(x)}=2f(x),\quad\frac{\delta^{2}F}{\delta f(x)\delta f(y)}=2\delta(x-y)~,

(114)

and the higher variational derivatives vanish. Note that the 2nd variational derivative has a delta function, like in (
68
).

Simple example 2:

F[f]=\int\text{d}x\,f(x)^{3}
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F}{\delta f(x)}=3f(x)^{2}~,\quad\frac{\delta^{2}F}{\delta f(x)\delta f(y)}=6f(x)\delta(x-y)~,

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{3}F}{\delta f(x)\delta f(y)\delta f(z)}=6\delta(x-z)\delta(y-z)=6\delta(y-z)\delta(x-y)~.

(115)

Simple example 3 (non-local):

F[f]=\int\text{d}x\,\text{d}y\,f(x+y)f(x-y)
. We get

\frac{\delta F}{\delta f(x)}=\int\text{d}y\,(f(x-2y)+f(x+2y)),\quad\frac{\delta^{2}F}{\delta f(x)\delta f(y)}=1~.

(116)

Note that the 2nd variational derivative has no delta-function, and is non-zero for
x\neq y
. In (
68
), it is the non-locality of the entropy functional that allows it to have off-diagonal terms.

Delta-function variations:
Suppose that
\delta f(x)=\lambda\delta(x-x_{0})
. Plugging this into (
113
) gives

\delta F=\lambda\frac{\delta F}{\delta f(x_{0})}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\frac{\delta^{2}F}{\delta f(x_{0})^{2}}+\dots

(117)

and so

\frac{\text{d}F}{\text{d}\lambda}\bigg|_{\lambda=0}=\frac{\delta F}{\delta f(x_{0})},\quad\frac{\text{d}^{2}F}{\text{d}\lambda^{2}}\bigg|_{\lambda=0}=\frac{\delta^{2}F}{\delta f(x_{0})^{2}},\quad\dots

(118)

This is sometimes how the variational derivatives are defined. Note that to define the ordinary derivative
\frac{\text{d}^{n}F}{\text{d}\lambda^{n}}
we had to specify the variation
\delta f
. The variational derivatives do not depend on what
\delta f
is.

Box variations:
Suppose that the variation is box shaped: it equals
\lambda
in some small neighbourhood of a point:
\delta f(x)=\lambda\Theta(\frac{\epsilon}{2}-|x-x_{0}|)
. Then we get

\delta F\sim\lambda\epsilon\frac{\delta F}{\delta f(x_{0})}\qquad\epsilon\to 0~.

(119)

To correctly go from this to the ordinary derivative, note we have to take
\epsilon\to 0
first before taking
\lambda\to 0
, i.e. if we define

\frac{1}{\epsilon}\frac{\text{d}F}{\text{d}\lambda}\bigg|_{\lambda=0}:=\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{F[f+\lambda\Theta(\frac{\epsilon}{2}-|x-x_{0}|)]-F[f]}{\epsilon\lambda}~,

(120)

then we have

\frac{1}{\epsilon}\frac{\text{d}F}{\text{d}\lambda}\bigg|_{\lambda=0}=\frac{\delta F}{\delta f(x_{0})}~.

(121)

\epsilon
is a coordinate width, and so becomes
\mathcal{A}/\sqrt{h}
for higher dimensional curved manifolds, where
\mathcal{A}
is the proper area of the top of the infinitesimal box shape variation.
This then connects to the notation used in
Bousso:2015mna
.
