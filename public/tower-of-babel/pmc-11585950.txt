Forearm bone mineral density as a predictor of adjacent vertebral refracture after percutaneous kyphoplasty in patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Jinzhou Wang, Xiansong Xie, Yuwei Gou, Yucheng Wu, Hongyu Pu, Qian Chen, Jiangtao He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe incidence of adjacent vertebral body re-fracture after percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP) is associated with a number of variables, of which decreased bone mineral density is one of the major risk factors. Forearm bone mineral density (BMD) measurements are gaining attention because of their convenience and validity, but there is a lack of systematic research on the specific relationship between forearm BMD and the risk of adjacent vertebral re-fracture after PKP.PurposeTo investigate the correlation between forearm BMD and the risk of adjacent vertebral re-fracture after PKP in osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCF) patients.MethodsRetrospective evaluation of 198 OVCF patients receiving PKP was conducted in this study. The patients were divided into two groups: the no-fracture group and the re-fracture group, according to whether or not they had undergone vertebral re-fracture. Obtain basic information about the patient's age, sex, body mass index, bone cement leakage, smoking history, diabetes history, and surgical segmentation. Using computed tomography, the mean Hounsfield unit (HU) values for the BMD of the L1 lumbar spine were determined. For the dual-energy X-ray BMD test, the distal one-third lengths of the patient's nondominant forearm's radius and ulna were chosen. Receiver operating characteristic curves were utilized to evaluate the predictive value of forearm BMD versus lumbar CT values for vertebral re-fracture, and univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were employed to identify characteristics related with vertebral re-fracture following PKP.ResultsRe-fracture rate after PKP was 17.2% at a minimum 12-month follow-up. Significant differences were seen between the refracture and non-fracture groups in terms of hypertension, Cobb angle correction, vertebral height recovery rate, intradiscal cement leakage, forearm bone density, and vertebral HU values. In multifactorial logistic regression analysis, forearm bone density (OR 0.821; 95% CI 0.728–0.937, p = 0.008) and HU values (OR 0.815; 95% CI 0.733–0.906, p = 0.005) were independent risk factors for vertebral re-fracture. The area under the curve (AUC) for forearm BMD values and HU values predicting adjacent vertebral re-fracture were 0.956 and 0.967, respectively.ConclusionsForearm BMD is an independent risk factor for re-fracture of adjacent vertebrae after PKP. In addition, forearm BMD, as a valid indicator of postoperative re-fracture after PKP in patients with OVCF, and the HU value of lumbar spine CT were both powerful tools for predicting re-fracture.

Introduce

Percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP) has become a classic minimally invasive procedure for the treatment of OVCF due to its rapid pain relief, improvement of vertebral loss of height, and restoration of localized kyphosis correction angle [1, 2]. However, re-fracture of adjacent vertebrae has become a common complication after PKP surgery [3, 4]. According to certain research, there is a 12–38% chance of refracture following vertebroplasty [5, 6].adjacent vertebral body re-fractures can lead to new or worsened back pain and increased kyphosis, which can lead to recurrent hospitalizations and increased costs, limiting the patient's quality of life. Since low bone mineral density (BMD) is an independent risk factor for vertebral refracture after PKP [7, 8], preoperative measurement of BMD is critical to predict this complication. Dual energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) is currently the gold standard recommended by WHO for measuring BMD [9]. Among these, the most often utilized location for measuring bone density is the lumbar spine (L1-4). OVCF is more prevalent in the thoracolumbar vertebrae due of the particular structure of the spine [10], and it has been observed that spinal vertebral fractures may hide the real quantity of cancellous bone [11, 12], Because distortion of the bone structure in the location of the fracture might produce bias in DXA readings, around 50% of persons have DXA findings that show just decreased or normal bone mass [13–15]. In addition when combined with degenerative changes, such as calcification of the abdominal aorta, degenerative bone changes, or bone matrix formation may lead to increased bone density, resulting in a false negative result [16].The accuracy of the lumbar Hounsfield unit (HU) value in determining the amount of vertebral bone mineral density has been extensively confirmed in recent years [17, 18]. However, the criteria of vertebral CT-HU value in the diagnosis of osteoporosis are not clear, and the measurement methods are different in different studies [19, 20], Recent studies have shown that forearm bone mineral density has adequate accuracy and sensitivity in evaluating bone BMD in osteoporosis screening, particularly in postmenopausal women and patients with spinal degeneration. Forearm DXA is more accurate than lumbar DXA and may be an effective alternative method [21–23]. Additionally, it has been shown that forearm BMD can evaluate the risk of cage subsidence and pedicle screw loosening following lumbar fusion surgery [24, 25]. However, the relationship between forearm BMD and refracture after kyphoplasty remains unclear. In this study, we reviewed kyphoplasty in patients with osteoporotic single-level vertebral fractures to explore the possible association between forearm BMD and postoperative refractures of adjacent vertebral bodies. Given that forearm DXA measurements are not affected by adverse spinal factors, we hypothesized that forearm BMD would be as effective as lumbar HU values in predicting postoperative adjacent vertebral refractures.

Methods

Patient population

Approved by our hospital's Medical Ethics Committee, this study retrospectively examined clinical data from 198 patients treated with PKP for unisegmental OVCF in the Department of Orthopaedics from June 1, 2021, to June 1, 2023. The following were the inclusion criteria:(1) Individuals who have had three weeks or less of treatment for osteoporotic spinal compression fractures; (2) Patients who have undergone single-segment kyphoplasty at our institution, using the thoracolumbar segment (T11-L2) as the operative location; (3) Over 70 years of age; (4) Complete imaging and clinical data, including preoperative lumbar spine CT and x-rays, the final follow-up (at least 12 months), the postoperative 1-week follow-up, and the forearm and lumbar DXA measures at that follow-up (at least 12 months). Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of spinal surgery; (2) history of spinal disorders such as spinal TB, spinal tumors, ankylosing spondylitis, etc.; (3) insufficient data and loss of visits (4) Non-dominant forearm and wrist fractures; (5) High-energy impact fractures; (6) Multiple segmental vertebral fractures. After 12 months of follow-up, all patients were divided into two groups: 34 in the refracture group and 164 in the non-fracture group, based on the presence or absence of vertebral fracture of contiguous segments during the cycle.

Dual‑energy X‑ray absorptiometry

Prior to surgery, bone mineral density measurements should be conducted on the non-dominant forearm of the patient, specifically at the distal one-third of the radius and ulna (measured in grams per square centimeter). The bone density assessments are performed by trained personnel using the EXA-3000 Osteosys absorption measurement system. The parameters for the measurement include a scanning current of 0.25 mA and tube voltages of 80 kV/55 kV, with relevant settings referenced from the literature [25]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification criteria, osteoporosis is diagnosed by a T-score ≤ −2.5; osteopenia is diagnosed by a T-score of −2.5 < T-score < −1.0; and normal BMD corresponds to a T-score ≥ −1.0.

Measurements of HU value

All patients underwent lumbar spine CT (Definition, Siemens) examination before surgery. The voltage of the CT scanning tube is set to 120 kV. The HU value in ROI was automatically read using the picture archiving and communication system (PACS) software commonly used in film reading. The axial plane below the upper endplate, the middle of the vertebral body, and only the axial plane above the lower endplate were selected (Fig. 1). The average of the HU values calculated by the three ROIs is taken as the average HU value of the L1 vertebra [17]. The mean HU values for T12 and L2 were utilized as a stand-in for L1-HU if the L1 vertebrae were fractured [26]. Follow‑up assessment

Thoracolumbar spine radiographs, which are routine postoperative follow-up images at our institution. Postoperative radiographs were evaluated at a minimum of 12 months of follow-up, and postoperative re-fracture of the adjacent vertebrae was diagnosed if the anterior–posterior height of the vertebral body in the non-operated segment was reduced by more than 20% or if there was a wedge-shaped change or a “biconcave” change with trabecular thinning (e.g. Fig. 2). The rate of vertebral body height recovery and the angle of Cobb angle correction were calculated during follow-up based on the orthopedic and lateral imaging of thoracolumbar spine radiographs (e.g. Fig. 3), and the rate of vertebral body anterior margin height recovery = (HB − HA)/ (h1 + h2)/2*100%).Fig. 2First postoperative review(A) and postoperative follow-up radiographs of adjacent vertebral fractures(B)Fig. 3A: preoperative lateral spinal X-ray; B: postoperative follow-up lateral spinal X-ray (θ: preoperative vertebral Cobb angle; vertebral anterior margin height recovery rate = (HB-HA)/(H1 + H2)/2*100%)

Statistical analysis

The statistical software SPSS (US version 26) was used for the analysis. The standard deviation ± mean was used to express continuous variables. Percentages were used to express quantitative measures. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to check the normalcy of continuous data. separate samples For variables that had a normal distribution, the student t-test was employed; for variables that did not, the Mann–Whitney U-test was utilized. Categorical variables were subjected to the chi-square test. The study employed logistic regression analysis to identify independent variables associated with vertebral refracture. The predictive value of vertebral re-fracture was evaluated using the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC), and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. If a difference was p < 0.05, it was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

For the first time, we demonstrated that forearm DXA-based bone mineral density evaluation was useful in predicting adjacent vertebral fractures following PKP, and that forearm BMD levels below 0.265 g/cm2 significantly increased the chance of adjacent vertebral fracture incidence. In predicting adjacent vertebral fractures, BMD of the forearm was similarly predictive to mean HU values of the lumbar spine.

Common complications after PKP adjacent to vertebral fractures are attributed to osteoporosis, increased intervertebral bone density discrepancies [27], unstable bone cement distribution, and biomechanical changes [8]. In addition to the low BMD detected before surgery, other factors were closely related to surgical technique and intraoperative selection. Therefore, accurate bone density values should be obtained as much as possible prior to vertebral kyphoplasty. The most widely used method of BMD measurement is DXA, and the measurement site is usually chosen to be the lumbar spine (L1–L4). However, DXA is a planar projection technique that measures area BMD, including the density of all mineral components encountered in the x-ray projection path of the cancellous bone, cortical bone, and anterior and posterior vertebral structures of the vertebral body [11]. Therefore, in patients with severe lumbar degeneration, degenerative structures including vertebral fractures, osteophyte formation, calcification of the abdominal aorta, and endplate sclerosis may falsely increase BMD measurements, masking the true changes in cancellous bone mass [12, 28], so in recent years, people have raised many questions about its measurement accuracy. Although the vertebral HU value is widely recognized as an effective means of assessing bone mineral density and detecting osteoporosis, its measurement results may be influenced by CT scanning parameters, surveyor error, contrast media use, choice of measurement ROI, and diagnosis of the vertebral body. A uniform optimal HU value has not been determined to distinguish normal bone mineral density from low bone mineral density, and often needs to be used in conjunction with other assays and clinical assessments. QCT, which has also been recommended in recent years for BMD measurement and osteoporosis diagnosis, provides detailed information on three-dimensional bone structure [29, 30], allowing for a more accurate assessment of bone density and its distribution compared to traditional two-dimensional imaging. As an advanced imaging technique, it can provide a new perspective for bone density assessment and bone health research. Previous studies by Jones [31] and Rey [32] et al. have demonstrated that forearm BMD is highly correlated with lumbar and femoral neck BMD in adult women, with similarly high rates of recognition in osteoporotic patients. Bina Eftekhar-Sadat [23]discovered that wrist BMD is more accurate than lumbar BMD in detecting osteoporosis in postmenopausal women. According to a research by Pouillès [33], forearm BMD correctly determines bone health in the axial skeleton and is a viable tool for OP screening. Furthermore, HU values of lumbar CT vertebrae and forearm bone density showed a strong positive association, according to the researchers [34, 35]. Appellate studies have shown that forearm bone density can be used as an indicator of bone quality changes in the lumbar spine, thus providing an important reference value in the evaluation of postoperative bone density-related complications in the spine.

The incidence of recurrent vertebral fractures ranges from approximately 7.8% to 21.7% within 1 year after undergoing PKP [36]. Of these recurrent fractures, approximately 86.67% occurred in the adjacent vertebral region [37, 38]. In our follow-up of at least 12 months, the overall incidence of adjacent vertebral fractures was 17.2%, which is similar to the 17.5% incidence of adjacent vertebral fractures reported after PKP by Lee et al. [39]. However, in a retrospective study by Ren et al. [40]. it was found that postoperative re-fractures accounted for 26.5% of all re-fractures and more than half (57.1%) of the patients with re-fractures developed a new fracture within six months of the time period. This might be related to the fact that our study only included individuals with single-segment vertebral fractures, whereas their study included patients with fractures of two or more segments. The presence of more than two initial fractures has also been shown to be an independent risk factor for vertebral refractures [7], with the increase in the number of initial fractures increasing the risk of vertebral refractures by 2.52 times [40]. In addition, hypertension, cobb angle correction, vertebral height recovery rate, average forearm BMD, average forearm T-score and mean overall HU values differed significantly between patients in the re-fracture group and non-fracture group.

Therefore, we further screened the risk factors by logistic regression. In univariate logistic regression analysis, a significant correlation was found between postoperative vertebral refracture and hypertension. It's possible that changes in calcium metabolism, such as elevated calcium loss, parathyroid gland activation in response, and calcium translocation from the skeleton, are significantly linked to hypertension. These changes can result in an imbalance in calcium homeostasis, which is linked to senile osteoporosis [41]. In a retrospective study that included 586 postmenopausal women, hypertension was found to be associated with reduced spinal BMD in postmenopausal women [42]. However, in multivariate logistic regression, the effect of hypertension became insignificant after accounting for other factors such as forearm bone mineral density and vertebral HU value, which implies that hypertension may be correlated with forearm bone density or vertebral HU values to a certain extent, being influenced by their variations. In univariate and multifactor logistics regression analysis, cobb angle correction and Vertebral height recovery rate always remain significant, indicating that excessive pursuit of recovery of vertebral height and excessive correction of cobb Angle will increase the risk of vertebral fracture. Some scholars [43]believe that every 1% increase in the recovery rate of the height of the affected vertebra will increase the risk of re-fracture by 7 times. Every 1 degree correction of the Cobb Angle increases the risk of refracture by 9%, so overcorrection should be avoided depending on the patient. As a common complication of vertebroplasty, cement leakage is considered by most scholars [7, 44]to be related to postoperative vertebral re-fracture, and our study also found that the risk of re-fracture in patients with cement leakage was 2.314 times higher than that in patients without leakage. This could be because bone cement seeps into the intervertebral disc's interior, mechanically compressing the nucleus pulposus and causing it to shift, deform, and fracture. On the other hand, the cytotoxicity of the bone cement results in metabolic problems for the intervertebral disc cells and malfunction of the biochemical components, which speeds up the intervertebral disc's degeneration [45].In our study, forearm T-scores and BMD as well as mean overall HU values were significantly lower in 34 patients in the re-fracture group than in 164 patients in the non-fracture group. Univariate as well as multivariate logistic regression analyses confirmed that low BMD as well as HU values were independent risk factors for adjacent vertebral fractures, further supporting the central role of BMD and vertebral HU values in evaluating the risk of vertebral fracture, as in previous studies [46, 47]. In Model 2 after adjusting for all confounders, the OR for forearm BMD was 0.821 (OR 0.821; 95% CI 0.728, 0.937, p = 0.008), and thus the incidence of vertebral re-fracture increased by approximately 17.9% for each unit of decrease in forearm BMD, however, in a retrospective study of postoperative new fractures of the vertebrae in 390 postmenopausal patients study [48], the risk of vertebral re-fracture was reduced by 66% for each unit increase in lumbar BMD, which may be related to patient selection and the biomechanical properties of the lumbar spine in terms of weight bearing and support. In addition, both forearm BMD and mean HU values predicted vertebral refracture. Mean HU values (AUC, 0.967; 95% CI, 0.939, 0.995) were more predictive than forearm BMD (AUC, 0.956; 95% CI, 0.925, 0.986). In our study, a forearm BMD threshold of 0.265 was used, with a sensitivity of 89.0% and a specificity of 91.2%; the mean overall HU value threshold was 100.5, with a sensitivity of 93.9% and a specificity of 94.1%. Thus, patients with a forearm BMD value of less than 0.265 g/cm2 are at greater risk of recurrent fracture after PKP. To avoid postoperative refracture, spine surgeons must enhance the clinical management of patients with forearm BMD values less than 0.265 g/cm2, as well as evaluate patients for risk factors for refracture of surrounding vertebrae. Patients with severe osteoporosis and low BMD should be carefully considered for this surgery.When surgical treatment is necessary, a brace should be worn for an extended length of time following surgery to give additional support and protection while also improving the effectiveness of anti-osteoporotic therapy.

Study limitations

This study has a number of drawbacks. Initially, As the study was analyzed retrospectively, patient selection may have been influenced by prior medical records and inclusion criteria. There were instances of patients being lost to follow-up, which may have resulted in missing data and thus introduced selection bias.Therefore, in order to improve the external validity and dependability of the results, large-scale, long-term prospective studies are required to validate our findings. Second, patients with multisegment compression fractures require additional research because the study group only included patients with single-segment thoracolumbar vertebral body fractures. Thirdly, The PKP procedures in this study were not performed by the same surgeon. Differences in the precision of puncture and balloon dilatation, as well as differences in the experience of different practitioners in controlling the amount of cement injected and how it is distributed, may affect the stability of the vertebral body as well as the rate of fracture recurrence in the postoperative period.

Conclusions

The incidence of postoperative refracture was substantially correlated with lower lumbar HU values and lower forearm BMD. Forearm BMD is an independent risk factor for vertebral refracture after PKP. Both forearm BMD and lumbar HU values were effective in predicting the risk of adjacent vertebral fracture, with lumbar HU values having slightly higher predictive ability. Given the limitations of lumbar DXA measurements as a result of lumbar fractures, forearm BMD provides a reliable alternative for preoperative risk assessment. Longer follow-up prospective studies are required in the future to confirm the prognostic efficacy of forearm BMD for fracture risk and to strengthen the clinical applicability of the results.
