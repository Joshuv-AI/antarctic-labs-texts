Cost-Effectiveness of Community-Based Rehabilitation for People With Schizophrenia
Authors: Ruoxi Ding, Miaomiao Zhao, Yanshang Wang, Yiqi Xia, Xiaolong Guan, Zhenyu Shi, Ping He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionIs a group-based, community-based rehabilitation (CBR) intervention cost-effective compared with facility-based care alone for people with schizophrenia in China?FindingsIn this secondary analysis of a cluster randomized trial involving 334 participants across 18 subdistricts in China, CBR significantly improved symptoms, functioning, caregiver burden, and quality of life, with cost savings of $24 636 to $27 847 per quality-adjusted life-year gained at 12 months.MeaningThe group-based CBR model is a clinically effective and cost-saving approach for schizophrenia management in resource-constrained settings, supporting scaled implementation of CBR.

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a debilitating mental illness that confers a high global disease burden, profoundly affecting individuals’ health and societal functioning while requiring long-term treatment and support.1,2 Epidemiologically, the disorder has a global incidence of 1.2 million cases per year and is responsible for 14.80 million disability-adjusted life-years.3,4 The management of schizophrenia is especially challenging in low- and middle-income countries, where systemic issues like scarce psychiatric resources and social exclusion exacerbate patient outcomes. Focusing on China, a country bearing a disproportionate share of this burden, reveals the scale of the challenge: In 2021, China had approximately 5.30 million prevalent cases of schizophrenia, accounting for more than one-third of the global total (13.60 million), with an annual incidence of 236 175 cases and 3.40 million disability-adjusted life-years.4 Therefore, addressing the profound public health challenge of schizophrenia, particularly within high-burden, low-resource settings like China, is of critical importance.

Globally, the World Health Organization (WHO) Mental Health Gap Action Programme guidelines advocate for community-based rehabilitation (CBR), positioning it as a key complementary approach to medication for managing schizophrenia.5,6,7 This model is designed for implementation by primary health workers with specialist support, underscoring its viability and strategic value in the context of low- and middle-income countries. A growing body of evidence demonstrates CBR’s efficacy in enhancing functional outcomes and bridging the service gap for this population. Success has been documented in high-income nations like the United Kingdom through specialized community mental health teams.7 Parallel findings from low- and middle-income countries, including initiatives in India8,9 and Ethiopia,10,11 reveal that supplementing standard facility-based care (FBC) with collaborative or layperson-led CBR yields significant improvements, particularly in reducing symptom severity and disability.12 Consequently, the deployment of CBR is a pivotal strategy for advancing schizophrenia care in areas where resources are constrained.

While the beneficial effects of CBR for people with schizophrenia are demonstrated,13 robust economic evidence to support its widespread adoption is still lacking. Preliminary findings suggest potential cost-effectiveness,8,14,15 but comprehensive and detailed analyses, especially those conducted in low- and middle-income countries, are scarce. Given the severe constraints on mental health resources in these settings, conducting rigorous economic evaluations is crucial for providing decision-makers with the evidence needed to implement and scale up community-based mental health care effectively.

Building on our previous cluster randomized trial,16 which demonstrated that a group-based CBR model in China can alleviate symptoms and enhance social functioning and quality of life for individuals with schizophrenia, a critical policy question emerges. Against the backdrop of China’s limited primary mental health infrastructure and its national goal for 80% of counties to provide rehabilitation services by 2025, we must ask: Is this CBR model cost-effective? To answer this critical question and inform evidence-based scale-up, the present study conducts a comprehensive, multiperspective evaluation of the program’s 12-month cost-effectiveness as part of the original trial.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This secondary analysis evaluated a cluster randomized trial assessing the effectiveness of CBR for schizophrenia management across 18 urban and rural subdistricts in Weifang City, Shandong Province, China, from March 2023 to October 2024. The trial protocol was previously published16 and is also presented in Supplement 1. The trial followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guideline extension for cluster randomized trials and the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) reporting guideline. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Peking University (No. IRB00001052-21170). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants (or their legal guardians for those without decision-making capacity) prior to enrollment. Overall, 18 subdistricts from 3 districts were included in the trial; 9 were randomly allocated to the intervention arm and 9 to the control arm (eFigure 1 in Supplement 2).

This cluster randomized trial enrolled community-dwelling, clinically stable adults aged 18 to 59 years from communities in Weifang, China, with an International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) diagnosis of schizophrenia, an illness duration of at least 12 months, and a primary caregiver willing to participate. Key exclusion criteria included severe physical illnesses, intellectual disabilities, substance dependence, organic brain disease, learning disability, hearing impairment, and comorbid bipolar or severe depressive disorders. Potential participants were identified via the National Information System for Psychosis. Recruitment occurred between March 2023 and July 2023.

Randomization and Masking

Eighteen subdistricts were pairwise matched by sociodemographic and clinical characteristics (population size, number of registered patients with schizophrenia, average annual income, and rural/urban composition), then randomized 1:1 to FBC plus CBR or FBC alone by an independent statistician (M.Z.).17 Matching produced broadly comparable groups across all characteristics (eTable 3 in Supplement 2). Outcome assessors, data collectors, and the analysis team were blinded to treatment allocation.

Intervention

All participants received standard FBC (psychiatrist-led antipsychotic medication management and monthly consultations on illness education, medication adherence, and clinical monitoring). The intervention group additionally received a structured, group-based CBR program, developed based on WHO guidelines and local stakeholder input8,9,10,11,13 and delivered by trained mental health professionals over 12 months in 2 phases: phase 1 (months 1-6) comprised 12 biweekly sessions focused on therapeutic alliance, illness knowledge, medication adherence, physical health (including tai chi), and life and social skills. Phase 2 (months 7-12) comprised 6 monthly sessions reinforcing these competencies and developing individualized rehabilitation and relapse prevention plans. Sessions lasted between 90 and 120 minutes and were conducted in community health centers for groups of 10 to 20 participants (eTable 1 in Supplement 2).

Economic Evaluation

In this analysis, we evaluated the health effects, cost, and cost-effectiveness over the 12-month intervention period. All costs are expressed in 2023 US dollars (US $1 = ¥7.0467). Health effects were assessed using both generic (QALYs) and disease-specific measures, including symptom severity, social functioning, and caregiver burden. Costs were comprehensively captured across intervention delivery, health care utilization, and indirect components from 3 analytical perspectives.

Health Effects

Primary outcomes were quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs), symptom severity (Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale [PANSS]; range 30-210, higher scores indicating more severe symptoms), social functioning (Personal and Social Performance Scale [PSP]; range 1-100, higher scores reflecting better functioning), and caregiver burden (Burden Assessment Scale [BAS]; range 19-76, higher scores representing greater burden). The BAS was formally translated into Chinese for this study, and its validity and reliability were established.18 Secondary outcomes, including medication adherence and illness knowledge, will be reported separately.

QALYs were calculated as the area under the curve of utility scores at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months. Health utilities were derived from the Schizophrenia Quality of Life Scale (SQLS) and converted to utility values on the EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level health-related quality of life questionnaire (EQ-5d-5L) using a validated mapping algorithm.19

The EQ-5D was also administered as specified in the trial protocol but exhibited a pronounced ceiling effect, with a substantial proportion of participants reporting full health at baseline, a known limitation of generic preference-based instruments in schizophrenia that limits their ability to detect improvements over time. Health utilities were therefore derived from the SQLS, a 30-item disease-specific instrument assessing psychosocial functioning, motivation/energy, and symptoms/adverse events (domain scores range 0-100, higher scores indicating poorer quality of life). The mapping algorithm incorporates the 3 SQLS domains (including quadratic terms), age, and sex, and was developed and validated in an Asian population with schizophrenia. Full details of the mapping equation are provided in the eAppendix in Supplement 2.

Cost Components

Direct Costs: Intervention Setup and Delivery

Setup costs included module development (expert consultation and site visits for cultural adaptation) and staff training (on-site and online lectures, curriculum, and materials). Delivery costs included personnel (intervention staff and primary health care workers for outreach and coordination), printed educational materials, and participant gifts (daily necessities to encourage attendance, consistent with real-world CBR practice in China). A 10% administrative overhead covered fidelity monitoring and supervision. Setup costs were prorated across the intervention group (n = 172), yielding $42.10 per participant, a conservative estimate given economies of scale under larger implementation.

Direct Costs: Health Care Utilization

Costs were categorized into outpatient care, inpatient care, and self-treatment. Outpatient and inpatient expenditures, including FBC costs (psychiatrist consultations, antipsychotic medications, and clinical monitoring), were extracted from medical insurance claims, which comprehensively capture service fees, prescription medications, laboratory tests, imaging, surgical procedures, and nursing care. Self-treatment costs were collected via semiannual surveys.

Indirect Costs: Time, Lost Productivity, Food, and Transportation Costs

Food, transportation, and related expenses for participants and escorts were derived from survey data. Productivity losses were calculated by multiplying missed workdays (outpatient visits: 0.5 days; inpatient stays: full duration) by daily wages. No discounting was applied given the 1-year time horizon. Detailed cost categories are provided in the eAppendix in Supplement 2.

Statistical Analysis

We conducted a within-trial cost-effectiveness analysis from 3 perspectives: the health care system, the multipayer, and the societal. The multipayer perspective included direct health sector costs, intervention implementation costs, and indirect costs (food, transportation, and related expenses). The societal perspective, a modified one, additionally incorporated participants’ time costs and productivity losses, capturing the broader societal impact across all payers and beneficiaries. Following established guidelines, we adopted the societal perspective as our primary analytical framework (see the eAppendix in Supplement 2 for detailed justification).

Given baseline cost imbalances, we adjusted postintervention costs using a generalized linear model with baseline costs as a covariate, following Van Asselt et al.20 This approach is recommended for handling baseline cost differences and provides unbiased estimates of intervention effects on costs. Health outcomes were adjusted using linear regression models with baseline outcome values, sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, education, marital status, household income), and clinical variables (baseline PANSS, illness duration) as covariates. Cost-effectiveness was quantified using incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs), calculated as the incremental cost divided by the incremental health effect.

For QALYs, cost-effectiveness was evaluated against willingness-to-pay thresholds of 1 to 3 times China’s 2023 per capita gross domestic product (GDP) ($13 064-$39 192), per WHO guidelines. For clinical outcomes (PANSS, PSP, BAS), where established thresholds are lacking, we derived threshold values required to achieve 95% probability of cost-effectiveness. Sampling uncertainty was addressed using nonparametric bootstrap (5000 replications) with cluster-level resampling to preserve within-community correlation; standard errors were clustered at the community level in all regression models. Results were presented as cost-effectiveness planes and cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (CEACs).

Data were collected via face-to-face surveys at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months by trained assessors blinded to group assignment. Survey data were supplemented with medical insurance claims and work logs to capture health care utilization and additional cost parameters. Sample size was based on previous trials indicating an approximately 20% reduction in PANSS total scores after 12 months of CBR, with minimal change expected in the control arm. Assuming an intraclass correlation of 0.05 and α of .05, 110 participants per arm provided 90% power to detect this difference. The target was increased by 20% to 264 participants to accommodate potential attrition.

We conducted 4 sensitivity analyses to assess robustness: (1) reanalysis without baseline cost adjustment; (2) multiple imputation (30 datasets) to address missing data from loss to follow-up and deaths, as an alternative to the primary complete-case analysis; (3) Tobit model to account for the right-skewed distribution of cost data and zero-cost observations21; and (4) additional adjustment of both cost and outcome models for individual-level sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, education, marital status, household income) and clinical variables (baseline PANSS, illness duration).

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata statistical software, version 16.0 for Mac (StataCorp). Statistical significance was assessed using 2-sided tests with a significance threshold of P < .05. Analyses were conducted between June 2025 and May 2026.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 334 participants were enrolled and randomized by subdistrict: 172 to CBR plus FBC (9 subdistricts) and 162 to FBC alone (9 subdistricts). Mean (SD) baseline age was 46.7 (9.3) years; 180 participants (53.9%) were male; 204 (61.1%) resided in rural areas. Retention was high at both 6 months (161 of 172 participants [93.6%] in CBR plus FBC; 154 of 162 [95.0%] in FBC alone) and 12 months (161 of 172 [93.6%] in CBR plus FBC; 154 of 162 [95.0%] in FBC alone). A total of 24 participants were lost to follow-up (19 at midline, 5 at endline), with 1 death in each group (cardiovascular disease and pneumonia, respectively) and 11 withdrawals for personal reasons. Medical insurance claims data were complete for all retained participants; survey-based cost data had occasional item-level missingness.

At baseline, mean (SD) PANSS scores were 68.8 (25.7) in the intervention group and 65.8 (26.3) in the control group; QALY scores were 0.903 (0.040) and 0.907 (0.039), respectively. Costs differed between groups at baseline across all perspectives (eTable 2 in Supplement 2), necessitating baseline cost adjustment in subsequent analyses.

Table 1 presents the 12-month costs and health effects for both groups. Mean (SD) total costs were lower in the intervention group from all perspectives: health system ($1795.00 [$2729.70] vs $2011.30 [$3743.80]), multipayer ($1824.30 [$2810.50] vs $2064.90 [$2774.40]), and societal ($2191.90 [$3390.00] vs $2430.30 [$4199.00]). Health care utilization was the largest cost component in both groups ($1569.40 of $2191.00 [71.60%] in the intervention group; $2011.30 of $2430.30 [82.76%] in the control group), while CBR program costs accounted for $226.10 of $2191.00 (10.32%) of the intervention total (eFigure 2 in Supplement 2).

Formal Health Sector: Intervention and Health Care Utilization

The mean CBR intervention cost, adjusted for baseline costs, was $226.10 per participant, with labor as the largest component ($98.30 [43.5%]), followed by gifts ($60.00 [26.5%]) and setup costs ($42.10 [18.6%], primarily driven by module development at $29.70) (Table 1). Mean (SD) health care utilization costs were lower in the intervention group ($1569.40 [$2730.10]) than in the control group ($2011.30 [$3743.70]), with the intervention group incurring higher self-treatment costs but lower outpatient and inpatient expenditures.

Indirect Costs

Indirect costs also differed between groups (Table 1). Mean (SD) food, transportation, and other expenses were lower in the intervention group ($30.40 [$255.70] vs $64.30 [$223.30]), while time and productivity losses were slightly higher ($365.90 [$851.70] vs $354.70 [$778.10]). The intervention group had higher productivity losses from outpatient and inpatient care, but lower losses from self-treatment and lower escort time costs.

Cost-Effectiveness

Table 3 shows that the CBR intervention was associated with cost savings across all perspectives. ICERs indicated savings of $24 636 (health system), $26 713 (multipayer), and $27 847 (societal) per QALY gained. Savings per unit of clinical improvement ranged from $20.70 to $46.90 across outcomes and perspectives. The probability of cost-effectiveness was consistently high: 91.0% to 98.7% for QALYs at WTP thresholds of 1 to 3 times per-capita GDP, and exceeding 95% for all clinical outcomes at derived threshold values of $24.30 to $52.00 per unit (Table 3).

Sensitivity Analysis

Bootstrapped simulations confirmed that the CBR intervention was associated with net cost savings alongside improved outcomes, with greater uncertainty in cost estimates than clinical outcomes (Figure 1; eFigure 3 in Supplement 2). The probability of cost-effectiveness for QALYs ranged from 88.70% to 98.42% across perspectives at WTP thresholds of 1 to 3 times GDP ($13 064-$39 192). For clinical outcomes, derived threshold values at which cost-effectiveness probability reached 95% ranged from $24.30 to $52.00 per unit across perspectives, all declining to 0 at 50% confidence. Robustness Checks

Findings were robust across all sensitivity analyses. First, without baseline cost adjustment, savings ranged from $9750 to $15 607 per QALY gained (probability of cost-effectiveness, 67.6%-91.5%) (eTables 4-6 in Supplement 2). Second, after multiple imputation for missing data, savings were $23 012 to $27 099 per QALY gained (eTables 7-8 in Supplement 2). Third, Tobit model re-estimation yielded a 90.3% to 98.7% probability of cost-effectiveness for QALYs, with savings of $21.50 to $55.90 per unit across clinical outcomes (eTables 9-10 in Supplement 2). Fourth, with additional adjustment for patient-level sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, savings were $23 542 to $28 817 per QALY gained (eTables 11-12 in Supplement 2). Across all scenarios, cost-effectiveness probabilities exceeded 95% for clinical outcomes.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive health economic evaluation of a culturally adapted CBR model for schizophrenia based on a randomized trial in China, and one of the largest such trials in a low- or middle-income country. It is also the first economic evaluation of a group-based CBR approach to incorporate cost-effectiveness analyses of social functioning and caregiver burden. Our within-trial analysis, conducted across 18 socioeconomically diverse communities, integrated administrative records with survey data to enhance cost-estimation accuracy. The results indicate that this 12-month, group-based CBR intervention is clinically effective and was associated with net cost savings alongside improved outcomes, consistent with a dominant strategy compared with FBC alone. These findings were robust across sensitivity analyses, supporting the scaling up of this model in China and similar settings.

Our findings contribute to the evolving evidence on CBR models for schizophrenia. Existing economic evaluations in low- and middle-income countries present a mixed picture. The community-based intervention for people with schizophrenia and their caregivers in India (COPSI) trial demonstrated symptom improvements at moderate cost, with ICERs of 131 international dollars per PANSS point reduction and 519 international dollars per point on the Indian disability evaluation and assessment scale (IDEAS).8 A meta-analysis in Iran reported variable but generally acceptable ICERs of $613 to $8400 per QALY for community mental health services.14 Conversely, a Czech study found community care cost more than €250 000 ($295 000) per QALY, exceeding conventional thresholds.22 Economic advantages of community-based models have also been reported in the US, where Intensive Psychiatric Community Care was associated with improved outcomes and reduced societal costs,23 and in India, where CBR lowered out-of-pocket spending on medications and outpatient consultations.24 In this international context, our point estimates suggest that group-based CBR in China is not merely cost-effective but likely cost-saving, achieving improved outcomes while reducing economic burden, supporting the potential of sustainable community care in resource-constrained settings.

The cost savings observed in our trial can be attributed to several structural, therapeutic, and contextual factors. Notably, the clinical improvements were both statistically significant and clinically meaningful: 63.2% of intervention participants achieved at least a 20% reduction in PANSS total score, compared with 30.5% in the control group. First, the group-based modality offered a fundamental efficiency advantage over the 1-to-1 approaches often used in previous studies, enabling lower per-participant costs without compromising delivery quality. Second, the multicomponent design, integrating psychoeducation to improve illness awareness, behavioral rehearsal to strengthen daily living skills, medication adherence strategies, physical health promotion, and social skills training, collectively reduced dependence on acute medical services, thereby curbing both direct and indirect health care costs. Third, the inclusion of ongoing caregiver support addressed a critical need in resource-limited settings, where family members shoulder the majority of care responsibilities25,26,27; regular contact and structured aftercare alleviated caregiver burden and enhanced their capacity to support recovery, potentially averting costs related to burnout and familial crisis. Finally, systematic fidelity monitoring ensured consistent delivery, reinforcing the cumulative effect on outcomes and cost reductions.

This cost-effectiveness analysis extends the previously established clinical benefits of CBR by providing comprehensive economic evidence to inform policy.28 The consistency of results across sensitivity analyses supports the conclusion that CBR offers a high probability of cost savings. Our findings directly inform the Jingkang Ronghe Action initiative (2023), which emphasizes building a standardized community rehabilitation system for mental disorders.29,30 The results suggest that shifting investment from long-term institutional care toward robust, group-based CBR represents a more efficient allocation of limited resources. This evaluation, which used both generic and disease-specific outcomes, aligns with implementation science principles in which financial sustainability is critical for real-world adoption. A key consideration for optimizing future CBR models is ensuring high intervention quality, as our prior analysis indicated that quality moderates both clinical outcomes and cost reductions. Furthermore, CBR was associated with a shift in health care utilization patterns, with reduced reliance on outpatient and inpatient services. While focused on schizophrenia, the CBR model offers a scalable framework for managing a range of severe mental disorders in resource-limited settings. Future research should elucidate the specific cost components most affected by CBR.

We acknowledge that these findings were derived from communities in a single province, implemented under a structured research environment with trained personnel and organized infrastructure, conditions that may not be uniformly present across China. The substantial heterogeneity in mental health workforce capacity, service availability, community infrastructure, caregiving norms, and reimbursement structures means that local trial evidence should be distinguished from broader national or global applicability. While the pragmatic design across urban and rural settings supports generalizability within similar contexts, further validation in regions with weaker community mental health systems would be valuable. Future longitudinal studies across broader geographic regions and more diverse populations should track long-term service use, clinical outcomes, and indicators such as postdischarge suicide, mortality, and patient satisfaction, to ensure that mental health reforms are clinically beneficial, economically sustainable, and aligned with national efforts to establish integrated, community-based recovery systems.31

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several key methodological strengths. First, health care utilization and costs were measured using health administrative records rather than relying solely on self-report, thereby reducing recall bias and strengthening the validity of the economic findings. Second, the detailed cost data collection allowed for a comprehensive economic assessment from multiple analytical perspectives (health system, multipayer, and societal) and across a broad set of outcome measures, moving beyond a narrow focus on QALYs, a level of economic detail that is often a limiting factor in trials conducted in resource-constrained settings. Third, the trial’s scale, being one of the largest of its kind, and its implementation across diverse urban and rural communities enhance the external validity of the results and support the potential for scaling this group-based CBR model.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the 12-month trial duration restricts the economic evaluation to the active intervention phase; the long-term cost-effectiveness and sustainability of CBR during the postintervention maintenance phase remain unknown. Second, although we used cluster-level bootstrap resampling and cluster-robust standard errors, small-sample corrections and formal cluster-level sensitivity analyses were not performed. These methods could have provided additional reassurance regarding the precision of our estimates given the modest number of clusters (n = 18). Third, while the diverse community settings enhance generalizability within China, the model’s applicability to other health systems and cultural contexts requires further investigation. Key contextual factors likely to influence successful implementation include mental health workforce capacity, psychiatric service availability, community health infrastructure, family caregiving norms, and health care financing mechanisms, all of which vary considerably across and within countries. Fourth, although the point estimates indicated net cost savings, the 95% CIs for incremental costs crossed 0 across perspectives, reflecting statistical uncertainty around the magnitude of savings. The economic case for CBR therefore rests on the combination of significantly improved outcomes with a high probability of cost-effectiveness rather than on statistically significant cost reductions alone. Fifth, the sensitivity of QALY estimates to the SQLS-to-EQ-5D mapping algorithm was not formally assessed. Given the modest incremental QALY gain (0.012), uncertainty from utility mapping could influence the economic conclusions. Finally, the societal perspective applied in this study was a modified one. Broader societal cost domains, including caregiver opportunity costs, presenteeism, longer-term productivity impacts, lost household production, and accommodation expenses, were not captured, potentially leading to an underestimation of the full economic burden.

Conclusions

In this secondary analysis of a cluster randomized trial, CBR represents a promising and economically advantageous strategy for managing schizophrenia, with potential applicability to other severe mental illnesses in resource-constrained settings. This trial demonstrates that a culturally adapted, group-based CBR model in China is clinically effective and potentially cost savings from health system, multipayer, and societal perspectives. Its efficiency stems from the group-based delivery format, focus on functional recovery, and incorporation of caregiver support. These findings provide a practical framework for mental health service reform in China and a reference for similar settings in other low- and middle-income countries. Future work should include long-term evaluations to verify sustainability and implementation research to support the effective scaling of this person-centered care model.
