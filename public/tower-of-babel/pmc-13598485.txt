Phylogenetic Placement and Morphological Description of Three Species of Heterotrophic Euglenids (Euglenozoa) From Arizona: Scytomonas flumensis n. sp., Ploeotia protuberantia n. sp., and Hokulea pusilla comb. nov
Authors: Maia V. Palka, Alfredo Rodriguez‐Ruiz, Gordon Lax, Yesenia Garza, Brian S. Leander, Jeremy G. Wideman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEuglenids are microbial flagellates best known for their highly flexible photosynthetic species (e.g., Euglena gracilis ), though many heterotrophic groups remain poorly characterized at both molecular and ultrastructural levels. In particular, rigid heterotrophs such as ploeotids and petalomonads are highly diverse, yet most described species were documented only with light microscopy, largely because they are difficult to cultivate. Here, we establish cultures for three new isolates of ploeotids and petalomonads (Hokulea pusilla comb. nov., Ploeotia protuberantia sp. nov., Scytomonas flumensis sp. nov.) and examine them using light microscopy, scanning electron microscopy, and 18S rDNA phylogenetic analyses. These data support the recognition of two new species, Ploeotia protuberantia sp. nov. and Scytomonas flumensis sp. nov., and justify the transfer of Ploeotia pusilla to Hokulea pusilla comb. nov. based on its morphology and its affiliation with Hokulea waialensis in 18S rDNA phylogenies.

Introduction

Euglenids (Euglenozoa) are a group of eukaryotic flagellates that occupy a wide range of environments and employ diverse nutritional modes, including phagotrophy, osmotrophy and phototrophy (Leander et al. 2017). A major synapomorphy of euglenids is the pellicle, which consists of proteinaceous strips that run beneath the plasma membrane and are subtended by microtubules (Lax et al. 2019, 2021, 2023; Lax and Simpson 2020; Leander and Farmer 2000, 2001; Leander, Triemer, and Farmer 2001; Leander, Witek, and Farmer 2001; Leander et al. 2017; Sommer and Blum 1965). The number of pellicle strips varies drastically among euglenid groups, ranging from as few as 4 strips to more than 120 strips per cell (Esson and Leander 2008; Leander 2004; Leander et al. 2007, 2017). The pellicle determines how rigid or flexible a euglenid cell is, with cell rigidity broadly correlated to the number and arrangement of strips (Leander et al. 2017). In taxa with fewer than 20 strips (petalomonads and ploeotids), which are often—though not always—longitudinally arranged, cells are rigid (Lax et al. 2019, 2023; Leander, Triemer, and Farmer 2001; Leander, Witek, and Farmer 2001; Leander et al. 2017). Comparatively, in taxa with more than 20 strips (i.e., the spirocutes), the pellicle strips slide past one another, allowing the cells to contract in a characteristic form of motility called metaboly (Leander, Triemer, and Farmer 2001; Leander, Witek, and Farmer 2001; Leander et al. 2017). Euglenids also possess a feeding apparatus that enables phagocytosis of both bacterial and eukaryotic prey, consisting of microtubular arrays associated with the vestibulum and opening into the flagellar pocket (Leander et al. 2017). The structure and organization of this apparatus differs markedly among euglenid groups. It is not a perfect correlation, but broadly speaking, euglenids with more pellicle strips (particularly those capable of metaboly) and a robust feeding apparatus are generally able to consume larger prey, particularly eukaryotes (Leander et al. 2017; Palka et al. 2025).

Euglenids comprise three major groups: the two well‐resolved clades Spirocuta and Petalomonadida, and the paraphyletic ‘ploeotids’ (Lax et al. 2023). The Spirocuta includes the phototrophic euglenids (Euglenophytes), the osmotrophic euglenids (Aphagea), and a diverse assemblage of phagotrophic euglenids (Lax et al. 2019, 2021, 2023; Lax and Simpson 2020; Lee and Simpson 2014a). The spirocutes are defined by an ancestral pellicle‐strip duplication event that produced a helical, highly flexible pellicle with more than 20 strips that glide alongside one another to facilitate metaboly (Cavalier‐Smith 2016; Lax et al. 2019; Leander 2004; Leander, Witek, and Farmer 2001; Leander et al. 2007; Lee and Simpson 2014a, 2014b; Paerschke et al. 2017). Petalomonads and ploeotids, on the other hand, are strictly rigid and have 12 or fewer pellicle strips.

The robustly supported Petalomonadida, the sister branch to all other euglenids in multigene phylogenies, comprises phagotrophic flagellates that frequently prey on smaller organisms and glide using an anterior flagellum (Leander et al. 2017; Lee and Simpson 2014b; Lee et al. 2026). Petalomonads have a relatively simple cell morphology consisting of a rigid pellicle with 4–10 strips, one or two emergent flagella, and a simple feeding apparatus supported by a microtubule‐reinforced pocket that lacks feeding rods. Almost all petalomonads are motile, with the exception of Dolium sedentarium, a sessile petalomonad that lacks a flagellum for at least part of its life cycle (Larsen 1990; Lax and Keeling 2023), and Scytomonas saepesedens, which has an attached sedentary stage for feeding (Cavalier‐Smith et al. 2016).

Petalomonads are arguably the least‐studied group of euglenids in terms of molecular data despite inhabiting both freshwater and marine environments, and historically, they were distinguished primarily by morphological characteristics (Lee and Simpson 2014b; Lee et al. 2026). Despite their key phylogenetic position as the deepest branch of euglenids (Lax and Keeling 2023; Lax and Simpson 2020; Lax et al. 2021; Leander 2004; Leander et al. 2007), they have remained relatively poorly characterized at both molecular and microscopic levels until recently, and multigene phylogenetic data are still available for only four genera (Lax and Keeling 2023). Consequently, early phylogenies inferred from 18S rDNA sequences are notoriously plagued by paraphyletic genera, which have only recently begun to be disentangled through 18S rDNA sequencing and ultrastructural characterization of 13 newly isolated freshwater and marine strains thereby resolving petalomonads into six distinct clades and several new genera (Lee et al. 2026). Despite this substantial advancement in our understanding of petalomonad diversity, the group remains under‐characterized relative to other euglenid lineages, with environmental sequencing surveys demonstrating profound hidden diversity within this group (Lee et al. 2026).

Lastly, the ‘ploeotids’ are a paraphyletic assemblage of rigid heterotrophic euglenids that share several morphological traits: a conspicuous feeding apparatus with robust rods and vanes, 10–12 pellicle strips, and two flagella where one anterior flagellum consistently sweeps in front of the cell while the other (often longer and thicker) posterior flagellum facilitates gliding motility (Lax and Simpson 2013; Lax et al. 2019, 2023; Leander et al. 2007, 2017). The pellicle of these taxa contains considerable diversity in the arrangement, width and length of pellicle strips. For example, in some ploeotids, these strips can be heteromorphic (e.g., narrow, recessed strips alternating with wider strips in Serpenomonas costata; Lax et al. 2019; Triemer and Fritz 1986) or projecting outwards like ‘keels’ (e.g., Ploeotia vitrea, P. oblonga , Olkasia polycarbonata; Palka et al. 2025). More recently, the rigid ploeotid Hokulea waialensis has been shown to possess pellicle strips that appear helically arranged (Ruiz et al. 2026), a trait often associated with spirocutes (Leander, Triemer, and Farmer 2001; Leander, Witek, and Farmer 2001; Leander et al. 2017). The distinctive feeding apparatus of ploeotids consists of two rods and four vanes that together can appear either hook‐shaped, as observed mostly in alistosan ploeotids, or chisel‐shaped, like in Olkasia polycarbonata and Ploeotia plumosa (Lax et al. 2019, 2023; Lax and Simpson 2020; Lee and Patterson 2000; Palka et al. 2025). Ploeotids represent a significant portion of euglenid diversity, including several distinct clades (e.g., Alistosa and Karavia) as well as several taxa whose placement among euglenids remains uncertain with the limited molecular data available (Lax et al. 2019, 2023).

The eponymous genus Ploeotia contains 18 described species, most of which were originally characterized only by light microscopy. Consequently, the taxonomy of Ploeotia has been repeatedly revised as molecular phylogenetic data have revealed paraphyly within the group. For example, Ploeotia costata, which had been transferred from Serpenomonas costata in 1988 to Ploeotia costata based on ultrastructural data (Farmer and Triemer 1988), was formally transferred back to Serpenomonas costata after forming an independent clade within the ploeotids in molecular phylogenetic analyses, compared to the phylogenetic placement of the type taxon, Ploeotia vitrea (Cavalier‐Smith 2016; Lax et al. 2019). To date, only two species examined with molecular data have been demonstrated as true members of the genus Ploeotia ( P. vitrea and Ploeotia oblonga), although many of the originally described members of this genus still lack further study beyond light microscopy (Lax et al. 2019). Because several described ploeotids still lack molecular and ultrastructural data, continued study of these groups is necessary to further resolve their phylogenetic relationships and elucidate patterns of character evolution among ploeotids.

Most well‐studied petalomonads and ploeotids originate from marine habitats, with comparatively few from freshwater systems examined using molecular data, underscoring freshwater environments as priority targets for discovering and characterizing euglenids, and for reconstructing transitions between marine and freshwater lineages within these groups (Lax and Simpson 2020; Lee et al. 2026). This is particularly true for petalomonads, where most of the described diversity is found in freshwater environments (e.g., Huber‐Pestalozzi 1955). We aimed to identify novel heterotrophic euglenids in freshwater samples from Arizona using cultivation techniques and successfully established three new cultures of heterotrophic euglenids from these environments. These include the ploeotids Hokulea pusilla comb. nov. and Ploeotia protuberantia sp. nov., as well as the novel petalomonad Scytomonas flumensis sp. nov Here, we formally describe S. flumensis sp. nov. and P. protuberantia sp. nov. using light microscopy, scanning electron microscopy, and phylogenetic analyses of 18S rDNA sequences. We also transfer Ploeotia pusilla to H. pusilla comb. nov. based on its morphology and its phylogenetic affiliation with Hokulea waialensis in the phylogeny inferred from 18S rDNA sequences (Kubín et al. 2024; Schroeckh et al. 2003).

Materials and Methods

Sampling, Isolation, and Cultivation

S. flumensis and P. protuberantia were isolated in 2022 from sediment and water samples from a small creek near Camp Ponderosa (34°16′58.8″ N 111°08′34.6″ W) in Arizona. H. pusilla was collected in 2025 from Salt River (33°25′15.4″ N 112°03′53.7″ W) in Phoenix, Arizona. In both cases, samples were left in the dark for ~2 weeks to limit growth of photosynthetic organisms. Euglenids were initially identified under bright field microscopy at 400× magnification. The S. flumensis and P. protuberantia cultures were established by single‐cell isolation, in which individually identified cells were transferred into media using a glass‐drawn pipette. H. pusilla was isolated using enrichment and constant subcultivation. All cells are kept in ventilated cell culture flasks containing freshwater DY‐V media (Andersen and Phycological Society of America 2009) kept at 23°C. Cultures were maintained by weekly sub‐cultivation in a 1:1 ratio of fresh DY‐V medium.

Differential Interference Contrast Microscopy (DIC)

Light microscopy of all three isolates was performed using a Nikon (Eclipse Ti fluorescence microscope) inverted microscope with differential interference contrast (DIC) microscopy at 1000× magnification. Multiple video recordings of all isolates were captured, and images were extracted from them using VLC v3.20. Twenty‐five cells were measured using FIJI (Table S1).

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

We prepared each cell for SEM using the methods described in Ruiz et al. (2026) with minor modifications. Approximately 10 mL of each culture was transferred into a large Petri dish and vapor‐fixed with osmium tetroxide for 10 min at room temperature, followed by post‐fixation in 1% osmium tetroxide for 10 min at room temperature. Fixed cells were transferred onto a Millipore membrane filter and washed once in distilled water. Fixed cells were dehydrated using a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 85%, 90%, 95%) followed by three washes in 100% ethanol. Membranes containing fixed cells were critical point dried using a Tousimis Autosamdri 815B critical point dryer. Cells were sputter‐coated with 2 nm Pt/Pd using a Leica EM ACE600 sputter coater. Sputter‐coated cell‐containing stubs were viewed using a Zeiss XB350 Crossbeam scanning electron microscope at the UBC Bioimaging Facility (University of British Columbia).

Single‐Cell Amplification, Sequencing, and Assembly

Individual cells of H. pusilla , P. protuberantia, and S. flumensis were manually isolated from cultures and washed 2–4 times in filter‐sterilized fresh DY‐V media. Individual cells for each species were placed in 0.2 mL PCR strip tubes. The REPLI‐g Advanced Single Cell Kit (Qiagen) was used to obtain single‐cell amplified genomes (SAG) following the manufacturer's protocol. The resulting SAGs were then quantified using a Qubit BR DNA assay (Invitrogen). For S. flumensis, SAGs were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X series instrument with paired end 2 × 150 bp reads (performed by Psomagen, psomagen.com). Raw reads were quality trimmed using fastp v.0.23.2 (Chen et al. 2018). Trimmed paired reads were used as input for SPAdes version 4.0.0 (Prjibelski et al. 2020) and assembled under single‐cell mode with default parameters, which take multiple displacement amplification bias into account (Prjibelski et al. 2020; Spits et al. 2006; Záhonová et al. 2021). Contamination was assessed using Blobtoolkit v4.3.5 (Challis et al. 2020). We used minimap2 v2.1 (Li 2021) under default parameters to determine assembly coverage. Taxonomic affinity was determined by searching each contig against the NCBI non‐redundant database using BLASTn v2.16.0+ (Camacho et al. 2009) and against the Uniref90 database using Diamond v2.1.10.164 (Buchfink et al. 2021). Bacterial and other obvious human contaminants we identified were filtered out using Seqkit (Shen et al. 2016). The SAG will be made publicly available in a future manuscript in which more details than 18S will be reported.

Phylogenetic Reconstruction

For S. flumensis, 18S rDNA sequences were extracted from SAGs using barnapp (https://github.com/tseemann/barrnap). For P. protuberantia and H. pusilla , gDNA from the SAGs for each species were subjected to PCR amplification of the 18S rDNA using OneTaq DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs). For P. protuberantia, euglenid specific primers were used: EzoaF 5′ CGCAAATTGCCCAATR 3′ and reverse EzoaR 5′ GGRGCATCACAGACCTGC 3′ (Lax and Simpson 2013) under the following parameters: initial denaturing 94°C for 2 min; then 35× cycles of denaturing at 94°C for 1 min, followed by annealing at 55°C for 1 min, extension at 72°C for 2 min; and a final extension for 5 min. For H. pusilla, universal eukaryotic primers were used: EukA 5′ AACCTGGTTGATCCTGCCAGT 3′ and EuKB 5′ TGATCCTTCTGCAGGTTCACCTAC 3′ (Medlin et al. 1988) under the following parameters: initial denaturing at 94°C for 2 min followed by 35 cycles of denaturing at 94°C for 30 s, annealing at 48°C for 35 s, extension at 72°C for 1 min, and the final extension at 72°C for 5 min. The PCR product was run on an agarose gel, gel‐purified (Monarch DNA Gel Extraction Kit, New England BioLabs) and then sent to Eurofin Genomics for Nanopore amplicon sequencing (https://eurofinsgenomics.com). Reads for H. pusilla were cleaned by Eurofin Genomics. Reads for P. protuberantia were cleaned by manually stitching the forward and reverse reads. 18S rDNA sequences were deposited on NCBI under the accession numbers PX375662 (Hokulea pusilla comb. nov), PX375663 (Ploeotia protuberantia sp. nov.) and PX375664 (Scytomonas flumensis sp. nov.). Raw reads generated from SAGs of S. flumensis will be deposited in NCBI upon the publication of a subsequent paper that is focused on resolving the backbone of euglenids.

18S rDNA sequences from each species were then aligned with a previously published dataset (Lax and Simpson 2020) using MAFFT E‐INS‐I version 7.481 (Katoh and Standley 2013). Novel petalomonad and environmental 18S sequences from Lee et al. (2026) were added to the alignment using the—add function implemented on the MAFFT web server (https://mafft.cbrc.jp/alignment/server/add_sequences.html). The resulting alignment was manually corrected and trimmed using the Geneious Prime 2024.0.4 alignment masking algorithm. A Maximum‐likelihood (ML) tree of the final alignment of 267 taxa with 1143 positions was estimated with IQTree2 under the GTR + I + F + G4 model with 1,000 non‐parametric bootstrap replicates. Untrimmed and trimmed alignments are included in the Supporting Information of this article.

Results and Discussion

Hokulea pusilla comb. nov. Is Conspecific With Ploeotia pusilla (formally Ploeotia obliqua ) and Closely Resembles the Sister Species, Hokulea waialensis

In our molecular phylogenetic analysis of euglenids inferred from 18S rDNA sequences (Figure 1), H. pusilla branched sister to H. waialensis (83% bootstrap support) within a larger clade containing both Decastava and Lentomonas, consistent with the tree topology recovered in our previous study (Ruiz et al. 2026). Both Hokulea species form a clade with two environmental sequences, BF‐A1‐1‐3a and BF‐A1‐1‐5a (Ruiz et al. 2026). The pairwise identity between H. pusilla and H. waialensis is 95.5%.

H. pusilla comb. nov. strongly resembles the previously described ploeotid Ploeotia pusilla (syn., Ploeotia obliqua) in cell size, cell shape and cytoskeletal organization as observed with light microscopy; both isolates are relatively small compared to other ploeotids (Figure 2A–D) (Duangjan et al. 2017; Kubín et al. 2024; Lee et al. 2005; Ruiz et al. 2026; Schroeckh et al. 2003). Like P. pusilla , H. pusilla cells are colorless, rigid and have a slight asymmetrical ovoid shape with a slightly pointed posterior end, reminiscent of a lemon. H. pusilla cells have a length of 11.35 ± 1.21 μm and a width of 6.49 ± 1.07 μm (Figure 2A–D). The two flagella of H. pusilla originate from the conspicuous flagellar pocket and differ in thickness and length, with the posterior flagellum slightly thicker and considerably longer (26.33 ± 4.36 μm long; 2.32× cell length) than the anterior flagellum (6.57 ± 1.00 μm long; 0.58× cell length) (AF, PF and FP, Figure 2A–D,F, Figure 3C). Like other ploeotids, the anterior flagellum of H. pusilla exhibits a near‐constant whipping or sweeping motion in front of the cell, whereas the posterior flagellum extends far beyond the cell and is used for gliding. A feeding apparatus was not observed with light microscopy in H. pusilla , unlike in previous descriptions of P. pusilla (Kubín et al. 2024; Lee et al. 2005; Schroeckh et al. 2003); however, this is likely due to the small size of these cells and the resolution of the microscope available.

H. pusilla exhibits differences in cell shape from the type species, H. waialensis, when observed with both light microscopy and SEM (Ruiz et al. 2026). While H. waialensis has an oblong cell shape, H. pusilla is more lemon‐shaped, with a distinct tapered end that seems to be characteristic for this species (Figure 2A–D; Figure 3A–C,E). H. pusilla cells are slightly dorsoventrally flattened, with some but not all cells appearing concave on the ventral side of the cell (Figure 3B), unlike H. waialensis, which, while being slightly dorsoventrally flattened, does not contain a clear depression on the ventral side in any cell observed in our previous study. The cell surface and pellicle organization of H. pusilla is nearly identical to that of H. waialensis, where the surface of H. pusilla is covered in 10 proteinaceous pellicle strips (arrowheads; Figure 3A,D) that are helically arranged, flattened and contain strip junctions that appear even shallower than those of H. waialensis. A glycocalyx can be observed covering the cell surface, particularly at the anterior end near the feeding apparatus and flagella (G, Figure 3B,D). Glycocalyx has been observed in other lineages of euglenids, including the type species Hokulea waialensis, the flexible phagotroph Neometanema parovale, and several osmotrophic species (Leander et al. 2017; Lee and Simpson 2014a; Ruiz et al. 2026).

The surface of both flagella is covered in a dense fibrous matrix likely representing a combination of ordered mastigonemes beneath a glycocalyx, which is present across the entire cell surface (Figure 3C,D). This appears similar to the mastigonemes, tomentum and glycocalyx associated with the flagella near the vestibulum described for H. waialensis in Ruiz et al. (2026), Figure 2D. The feeding apparatus of H. pusilla also shares several similarities with H. waialensis (FA, Figure 3D). It contains hair‐like filaments on the exterior surface of the feeding apparatus at the anterior tip of the cell and is continuous with a ventral pellicle strip bordering the vestibular opening (FA, Figure 3B,D). The feeding apparatus of H. pusilla appears flush with the anterior end of the cell without extending outwards as dramatically as in H. waialensis. The clear similarities and differences in ultrastructure as observed with SEM are consistent with H. pusilla and H. waialensis forming sister taxa in our phylogenetic analyses of 18S rDNA sequences.

Because there are no transmission electron micrographs (TEM) of any Hokulea species, characterizing the ultrastructure of either H. pusilla or H. waialensis using TEM would help clarify the organization and placement of the feeding apparatus within the cell as well as the structure and arrangement of the pellicle.

Remarks

P. pusilla and H. pusilla are indistinguishable from one another and H. pusilla forms the sister lineage to H. waialensis, which is well supported with the morphological evidence presented in this study. As such, we regard P. pusilla (previously P. obliqua ) as a junior synonym (basionym) of H. pusilla and transfer it to Hokulea based on its placement in phylogenies inferred from 18S rDNA sequences.

Ploeotia protuberantia sp. nov. Is Closely Related to P. vitrea and P. oblonga

In our molecular phylogenetic analysis of euglenids inferred from the 18S rDNA sequences, P. protuberantia was recovered as a member of Ploeotia, branching sister to P. vitrea with moderate to low bootstrap support (48%) (Figure 1). P. protuberantia was recovered as the sister to P. oblonga in some analyses, but in all cases, its placement was recovered within Ploeotia. The pairwise identity between P. protuberantia and P. vitrea (MK213407) and between P. protuberantia and P. oblonga (MK239306) was 68% and 67.7%.

P. protuberantia cells are rigid, oblong and appear colorless with a rounded posterior end, 21.27 ± 1.78 μm long and 12.42 ± 1.82 μm wide (Figure 2E–H) when observed using DIC light microscopy. Typical of ploeotids, P. protuberantia has two flagella originating from the flagellar pocket that differ in thickness and length, with the posterior flagellum being slightly thicker and significantly longer (37.58 ± 6.60 μm long; 1.77× cell length) than the anterior flagellum (8.86 ± 1.88 μm long; 0.42× cell length) (AF, PF; Figure 2F). The motility of P. protuberantia is consistent with that of other ploeotids, where the posterior flagellum is used for gliding while the anterior flagellum whips in front of the cell. P. protuberantia contains a hook‐shaped feeding apparatus extending nearly the entire length of the cell that closely resembles the feeding apparatus of other ploeotids, namely Serpenomonas costata, Ploeotia vitrea and P. oblonga (FA; Figure 2E). This feeding apparatus contains two rods with a cap at the very distal end near the cell surface, lying adjacent to a ventral pellicle strip (FA; Figure 2E; Figure 4A,B). The pellicle of P. protuberantia cells consists of 10 distinct, longitudinally arranged pellicle strips that are equal in width and form double‐edged keels that project outwards, visible with both light microscopy and SEM (PS, Figure 2G–H; arrowheads, Figure 4A,C). Similar to P. vitrea , the central ventral pellicle strip is slightly narrower than the remaining strips, although this difference in width is less pronounced in P. protuberantia.

There are several key morphological differences between P. protuberantia and its close relatives, P. vitrea and P. oblonga , particularly in the organization of the pellicle. Although both P. protuberantia and P. vitrea have 10 equally spaced pellicle strips that form double‐edged keels at their junctions (Lax et al. 2019), the strip length and how they converge at the posterior end of the cell differs. Unlike in P. vitrea and P. oblonga , where alternating pellicle strips are slightly shorter than the adjacent strips so the longer ones meet to form a five‐pointed star at the posterior end of the cell, in P. protuberantia, most of the pellicle strips are the same length, forming an 8–10 pointed star at the posterior end (arrowheads; Figure 4C). P. oblonga further differs in having heterogeneously spaced pellicle strips, with every second strip markedly narrower than its neighbor, in contrast to the uniformly spaced strips in P. protuberantia and P. vitrea (Lax et al. 2019).

A particularly interesting feature of P. protuberantia is a consistent and distinct layer of small granules that cover the cell surface (Figure 4). These granules, also present in P. vitrea and P. oblonga when observed with SEM in Lax et al. (2019), are less than 100 nm in size and appear to be contained within thin web‐like fibers (M; Figure 4D). The structure and composition of these granules are unknown for any of these three species in the absence of TEM and warrant further investigation. Regardless, these granules may be a clade‐defining trait for Ploeotia as they have been observed in all confirmed members of this genus and are not present on the surface of any other euglenids.

Remarks

Considering the close yet distinct morphological traits among P. protuberantia, P. vitrea , and P. oblonga , along with the strong support for P. protuberantia's placement within Ploeotia in our phylogenies inferred from 18S rDNA sequences, we propose that P. protuberantia sp. nov. should be recognized as a novel species of Ploeotia.

Scytomonas flumensis sp. nov. Is a Novel Freshwater Petalomonad From Arizona That Is Sister to Scytomonas saepesedens

In our molecular phylogenetic analysis of 18S rDNA sequences, Scytomonas flumensis sp. nov. was recovered as the sister to Sytomonas saepesedens within the Petalomonas I, Scytomonas clade (Lee et al. 2026) with moderate to high bootstrap support (92%) (Figure 1). The pairwise identity between the 18S rDNA sequences of S. flumensis and S. saepesedens is notably low for sister species (79%). Although these species are currently sister species in 18S rDNA phylogenies, the low pairwise identity suggests that they may not be true immediate sister species, and future sampling of freshwater petalomonads may uncover hidden diversity within this group.

Cells of S. flumensis are small (9.72 ± 1.82 μm long and 5.82 ± 1.34 μm wide), colorless and have a characteristic oval shape with a slight taper at the anterior end of the cell (Figure 2I–K, Figure 5A,B). These cells possess one long anterior flagellum on which they glide forwards, measuring 11.27 ± 3.26 μm long (1.16× cell length). (AF; Figure 2I, Figure 5A,B). This flagellum is thick in diameter when viewed with SEM and appears to be covered in fine flagellar hairs (Figure 5A–C). S. flumensis cells have 5 pellicle strips that are equal in width and are evenly distributed on either side of the cell (arrowheads; Figure 5). These pellicle strips form a distinct depression on the dorsal side of the cell, between two raised pellicle strip junctions, resulting from invagination of the central pellicle strip from the adjacent ridge (arrowheads; Figure 5). The pellicle strip junctions on either side of this depression are prominent and visible under light microscopy (PS; Figure 2J,K). The pellicle strips converge at the posterior end of the cell, at a junction that extends to the dorsal side (arrowheads; Figure 5D). A ventral protrusion (VP; Figure 5C) is present at the anterior end of the pellicle strip that runs adjacent to the flagellar canal, consistent with other ventral protrusions observed in many other petalomonads, including Tonotosolenus spp., Petalomonas kkongi, Petalomonas poosilla, Petalomonas larseni, Scytomonas saepesedens, among others (Cavalier‐Smith et al. 2016, Figure 1B,D; Lee et al. 2026).

Although S. flumensis closely resembles S. saepesedens in the general organization of the pellicle and its appearance under light microscopy, there are several key morphological differences between these two species. Namely, S. flumensis possesses a clear dorsal depression that is not observed in S. saepesedens, which is more spherical when viewed with SEM (Cavalier‐Smith et al. 2016). It is unlikely that this depression is a result of a fixation artifact, as this almost crumpled appearance is consistent with other petalomonads observed in Lee et al. (2026) and was observed in all cells across multiple fixation attempts. S. flumensis appears more elongated in the cell body and does not possess a pronounced collar, unlike S. saepesedens. S. flumensis and S. saepesedens were also isolated from drastically different environments; S. flumensis was recovered from a freshwater creek in Arizona, USA, whereas S. saepesedens was isolated from horse manure in Scotland (Cavalier‐Smith et al. 2016).

Remarks

Given the differences in locality, the morphological differences observed under SEM, and the clearly distinct phylogenetic relationship of S. flumensis and S. saepesedens as sister taxa in phylogenies inferred from 18S rDNA sequences, we conclude that our isolate of S. flumensis sp. nov. represents a novel member of Scytomonas.
