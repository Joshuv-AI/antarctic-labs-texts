Robust Welfare under Imperfect Competition
Konstantin von Beringe, Mark Whitmeyer

Abstract
We study welfare analysis for policy changes when supply behavior is only partially known. We augment the robust-demand approach of Kang and Vasserman 2025 with two supply primitives–intervals of feasible pass-through and conduct (market-power) parameters–applied to two equilibrium snapshots. A simple accounting identity distills the supply-side contribution to welfare to a simple integral expression. From there, we deduce that the bounds are produced by a single-threshold “bang-bang” inverse pass-through function. This, plus a modification of Kang and Vasserman 2025 ’s demand-side characterization, delivers simple bounds for consumer surplus, producer surplus, tax revenue, total surplus, and deadweight loss. We also study an ad valorem extension.



Keywords:
Welfare analysis, Welfare bounds, Robustness, Functional form assumptions; Pass-through; Conduct parameters; Market power

C14; C51; D04; D61; F14; H21; Q48

Introduction

Empirical evaluations of tax and regulatory policies often rest on functional-form assumptions that impute unobserved objects–most commonly, the demand curve connecting two observed price-quantity points. Recent work by
Kang and Vasserman 2025
(
KV
) formalizes how to assess the robustness of welfare conclusions to such demand-side assumptions by asking how different the true curve must be from a maintained specification to overturn a qualitative conclusion (e.g., that a policy is net beneficial). Their framework shows that, holding fixed the observed endpoints and shape class, welfare is maximized or minimized by simple, canonical demand curves.

This paper builds off of KV’s work: we develop a
supply-side
analog of robust welfare analysis for discrete (non-marginal) policy changes. Our starting point is the same minimal evidence set–two snapshots around a tax:
\left(p_{0},Q_{0},\tau_{0}\right)
and
\left(p_{1},Q_{1},\tau_{1}\right)
–but we add two primitives that encode supply behavior without imposing a parametric cost function: (i)
an interval of feasible pass-through rates
through which the unobserved policy path
p(\tau)
can trace, and (ii)
an interval of feasible conduct (market-power) parameters
that link price–cost markups to the familiar marginal-revenue correction via the conduct identity introduced in
Bresnahan 1982
(see also
Genesove and Mullin 1998
and
Weyl and Fabinger 2013
):

p-\tau-\mathrm{MC}(Q)=\kappa(Q)\left[-P^{\prime}(Q)Q\right]\text{,}

where
\kappa(Q)
is a conduct parameter nesting, e.g., perfect competition, monopoly, and (symmetric) Cournot competition.

These ingredients let us bound consumer surplus (
\mathrm{CS}
), producer surplus (
\mathrm{PS}
), tax revenue (
\mathrm{TR}
), total surplus (
\mathrm{TS}
), and deadweight loss (
\mathrm{DWL}
) while remaining agnostic about functional forms beyond the demand-shape classes developed by
Kang and Vasserman 2025
and only imposing very weak regularity on the policy path.

The key technical observation is an accounting identity that isolates all path dependence in a single linear integral term:

\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}Q(p)u(p)dp,\quad\text{where}\quad u(p)\in[\alpha,\beta]\quad\text{and}\quad\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}u(p)dp=\Delta\tau\text{,}

where
u
is the inverse pass-through (the change-of-variables weight induced by the unobserved price schedule via
d\tau=u(p)dp
) and
\alpha
and
\beta
are the inverses of the exogenously-given pass-through bounds.

Because welfare objects are linear combinations of
\intop\nolimits Qdp
(the demand primitive), the path term
\intop\nolimits Qudp
, and a linear conduct term, the overall problem is bilinear in
(Q,u)
. We show that the extremal
u
is always bang–bang with a single cutoff (“low-to-high” or “high-to-low” pass-through over the price support) via a simple (Hardy–Littlewood) rearrangement argument. On the demand side, we modify the
Kang and Vasserman 2025
extremal curves for the selected demand family. Combining these delivers a four-corner characterization: sharp global bounds occur at one of the four possible combinations of the two extreme paths and two extreme demand curves. The result is a recipe that produces tight bounds for consumer surplus, producer surplus, total surplus, and deadweight loss (and any convex combination thereof), once one picks a demand-shape class and pass-through/conduct intervals.

Our treatment also clarifies the role of market power. The conduct identity can be rewritten as

p-\tau-MC(Q)=\kappa(Q)\left[-P^{\prime}(Q)Q\right]=L(p)p,\qquad\text{with}\quad L(p)=\frac{\kappa(Q)}{\left|\eta(p)\right|}\text{,}

for the price-elasticity of demand
\eta
, so conduct
\kappa
and inverse elasticity jointly govern the Lerner index
L(p)
along the path. This yields transparent upper/lower producer surplus and total surplus bounds: worst-case welfare arises at the upper conduct bound and the path that maximizes the
Q
-weighted exposure to high
u
, whereas best-case welfare swaps the endpoints. When one has external moments on elasticities or markups, they can be plugged directly into our formulas. We also explore an
ad valorem
variant that works in log price (so the weight integrates to the change in the tax rate and the primitives become
\intop\nolimits Q(p)/pdp
and its path-weighted analog). The bounds still depend only on (i) two endpoints, (ii) a demand-shape choice, and (iii) feasible intervals for pass-through and conduct.

1.1
Related Work

Our paper contributes to a growing literature that studies welfare analysis under minimal functional-form assumptions. The closest antecedent is
Kang and Vasserman 2025
, who develop
robust
bounds for finite policy changes using shape restrictions on demand and show that familiar functional forms (linear, CES, exponential etc.) are extremal within various canonical families. We build on this program by bringing the supply side into the bounding exercise: we integrate conduct information (via Lerner and markup relationships) and path restrictions on pass-through into welfare formulas. In doing so, we deliver sharp, closed-form “four–corner” bounds that combine (i) demand-shape extremals with (ii) extremal price paths consistent with our pre-specified pass-through intervals. A producer-surplus identity makes the roles of conduct and path-dependence transparent.

A second strand is the tax-incidence and pass-through literature under imperfect competition.
Weyl and Fabinger 2013
synthesize incidence principles beyond perfect competition and link global incidence to the integral of local pass-through (their various “principles of incidence 5”). Our approach operationalizes this integral for policy changes by treating the (unobserved) price path as a weighted integral over demand, with weights pinned down by bounds on pass-through. This makes explicit how pass-through heterogeneity along the path maps into welfare ranges.

We also connect to work comparing specific and
ad valorem
interventions under market power. Classic analyses show that the two instruments can yield different pass-through and incidence patterns under imperfect competition
(
Delipalla and Keen 1992
;
Anderson et al. 2001
)
. Our
ad valorem
variant rewrites the problem in log prices, leading to simple bang–bang path weights and the same four–corner structure as in our main setting.

On the supply side, our conduct intervals relate to the new empirical IO tradition that infers market power and tests conduct. Foundational contributions include the Lerner index as a measure of monopoly power
(
Lerner 1934
)
and the identification and testing of oligopoly conduct in both homogeneous and differentiated-product settings
(
Bresnahan 1982
;
Nevo 1998
;
Genesove and Mullin 1998
)
. We exploit the standard connection
L=\kappa/|\eta|
to translate bounds on conduct (or Lerner indices) and on elasticities into transparent wedges in our welfare accounting.

More broadly, our robustness perspective complements two adjacent literatures. First, the
sufficient-statistics
approach emphasizes using a few elasticities or reduced-form objects to conduct welfare analysis under maintained structure
(
Chetty 2009
)
. Our results can be read as delivering ranges when only inequality-type information about these objects is credible. Second, the
partial identification
program (e.g.,
Manski 2003
) advocates reporting identified sets rather than point estimates under weak assumptions. Our welfare bounds are a structural incarnation of this principle tailored to price–quantity data and policy shocks. Relatedly, robust pricing and mechanism-design work studies optimal behavior under model uncertainty, e.g., robust monopoly pricing under worst-case payoffs or regret
(
Bergemann and Schlag 2011
;
Condorelli 2022
;
Le 2024
)
. We shift the focus from the seller’s objective to social welfare under constrained knowledge of demand and supply. There are also other papers bounding objects of interest under monopoly and imperfect competition
(
Condorelli and Szentes 2022
;
Condorelli 2022
;
Le 2024
)
.

On a technical level, our work relates to other recent papers that study linear or convex programs over spaces of measures.
Kleiner et al. 2021
characterize the extreme points of functions that majorize or are majorized by a given function. Their companion paper
(
Kleiner et al. 2022
)
studies the multidimensional variant of this problem, and
Nikzad 2022
studies
constrained
majorization, with a particular emphasis on its use in mechanism design.
Yang and Zentefis 2024
characterize the extreme points of “monotone function intervals,” using them to reduce persuasion/security-design problems to tractable LPs over measures; and
Augias and Uhe 2025
provide the extreme points of “convex function intervals,” with applications to persuasion, delegation, and screening.
Yang and Yang 2025
look at the extreme points of multidimensional monotone functions, and put their characterization to good use in mechanism design.

2
The Model

We observe two
Snapshots
around a
specific
(per-unit) tax:

\left(p_{0},Q_{0},\tau_{0}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\left(p_{1},Q_{1},\tau_{1}\right)\text{,}

with

\Delta p\coloneqq p_{1}-p_{0}>0,\quad\Delta Q\coloneqq Q_{1}-Q_{0}<0,\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta\tau\coloneqq\tau_{1}-\tau_{0}>0\text{.}

We seek
Bounds
on

\Delta\mathrm{CS},\quad\Delta\mathrm{PS},\quad\Delta\mathrm{TR},\quad\Delta\mathrm{TS}\coloneqq\Delta\mathrm{CS}+\Delta\mathrm{PS}+\Delta\mathrm{TR},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{DWL}\coloneqq-\Delta\mathrm{TS},

for

(i)

a particular
Demand-shape class
between
\left(p_{0},Q_{0}\right)
and
\left(p_{1},Q_{1}\right)
,

(ii)

an interval of feasible
Pass-through
parameters, and

(iii)

an interval of feasible
Conduct
(market-power) parameters.

Demand.

We assume that inverse demand
p=P(Q)
is strictly decreasing on the relevant range, with demand denoted
Q(\cdot)=P^{-1}(\cdot)
. The change in (Marshallian) consumer surplus is equal to the area below the demand curve between
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
:

\Delta\mathrm{CS}=-\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}Q(p)dp\text{.}

Supply.

Let
p\colon\left[\tau_{0},\tau_{1}\right]\to\left[p_{0},p_{1}\right]
be the (unobserved) equilibrium price as a function of the policy level (the specific tax). Basically, for every tax level between the two we observed, imagine the equilibrium price that would prevail holding everything else fixed, then collect those prices into a (regular) function
p(\tau)
. We don’t observe it, so we will consider all such functions consistent with the endpoints and some mild other conditions and take the best and worst cases over that set when computing welfare bounds.

Assumption 1
.

p
is absolutely continuous: the pass-through
\rho(\tau)\coloneqq dp/d\tau
exists a.e., and, moreover, satisfies

0<\rho_{L}\leq\rho(\tau)\leq\rho_{U}<\infty\quad\text{a.e. on }\left[\tau_{0},\tau_{1}\right]\text{.}

Absolute continuity guarantees
p(\tau)=p_{0}+\intop\nolimits_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}p^{\prime}(s)ds
. The lower bound ensures
p
is a.e. increasing and (a.e.) invertible. We assume, furthermore, that supply-side behavior can be succinctly captured by the following equation, with assumed bounds on the feasible conduct parameters, i.e., the market structure.

Assumption 2
.

For all
Q\in\left[Q_{1},Q_{0}\right]
,

p-\tau-\mathrm{MC}(Q)=\kappa(Q)\left[-P^{\prime}(Q)Q\right],\qquad\kappa(Q)\in\left[\kappa_{L},\kappa_{U}\right]\subseteq[0,1]\text{.}

This formula originates in
Bresnahan 1982
and is also used and discussed in, e.g.,
Genesove and Mullin 1998
,
Corts 1999
,
Weyl and Fabinger 2013
, and
Matsumura and Otani 2023
.
It nests perfect competition (
\kappa=0
), symmetric
n
-firm Cournot competition (
\kappa\approx 1/n
), and monopoly/collusion (
\kappa=1
). Moreover, under this conduct identity, the Lerner index satisfies

L(p)=\frac{p-\tau-\mathrm{MC}(Q)}{p}=\frac{\kappa(Q)}{\left|\eta(p)\right|}\text{,}

where
\eta(p)
is the price-elasticity of demand at price
p
. To see this, observe that

\frac{dQ}{dp}=\frac{1}{P^{\prime}(Q)}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\left|\eta(p)\right|=-\frac{1}{P^{\prime}(Q)}\frac{p}{Q}\quad\Rightarrow\quad-P^{\prime}(Q)Q=\frac{p}{\left|\eta(p)\right|}\text{.}

Then, simply plug this into (
2
) and divide by
p
.

In more detail, the term
-P^{\prime}(Q)Q
is the familiar “marginal-revenue correction” from market power: with downward-sloping demand, marginal revenue equals
p+P^{\prime}(Q)Q
.
Rewriting
-P^{\prime}(Q)Q
as
p/\left|\eta\right|
puts this correction on the elasticity scale at the observed
(p,Q)
.
The conduct identity says that the per-unit net-of-tax markup
p-\tau-\mathrm{MC}(Q)
equals a conduct factor
\kappa(Q)
times that
\mathrm{MR}
correction.
Normalizing by
p
produces the Lerner index:

\underbrace{\frac{p-\tau-\mathrm{MC}(Q)}{p}}_{\text{markup as a share of price}}=\underbrace{\kappa(Q)}_{\text{market power / conduct}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{\left|\eta(p)\right|}}_{\text{inverse elasticity}}\text{.}

Thus, higher conduct (more market power) or lower elasticity (more inelastic demand) both raise the Lerner index.

We highlight the following special cases:

•

Perfect competition
(
\kappa\equiv 0
):
L\equiv 0
so that
p=\tau+\mathrm{MC}(Q)
.

•

Monopoly
(
\kappa\equiv 1
):
L=1/\left|\eta\right|
, which is the textbook Lerner rule (here for net-of-tax price).

•

Symmetric Cournot with
n
firms
:
\kappa\equiv 1/N
so that
L=(1/N)/\left|\eta\right|
.
More generally, with heterogeneous shares the conduct proxy equals the Herfindahl
H
, yielding
L=H/\left|\eta\right|
.

2.1
Preliminary Results

We begin by stating and reformulating the central objects of our exercise. In particular, we single out the crucial decomposition of producer surplus that engenders our bounds. For a finite specific-tax change,

\Delta\mathrm{TR}=\tau_{1}Q_{1}-\tau_{0}Q_{0}\text{.}

Next, define

u(p)\coloneqq\frac{d\tau}{dp}=\frac{1}{\rho(\tau(p))}\text{.}

By Assumption
1
(and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus)

u(p)\in[\alpha,\beta]\coloneqq\left[\frac{1}{\rho_{U}},\frac{1}{\rho_{L}}\right]\quad\text{a.e. on }\left[p_{0},p_{1}\right],\quad\text{and}\quad\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}u(p)dp=\Delta\tau\text{.}

As we will shortly derive, one important component of the change in producer surplus is the cumulative, direct hit to producers from a tax increase,
\intop\nolimits_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau_{1}}Q(p(\tau))d\tau
. It can conveniently be written as an integral over prices instead, using the inverse pass-through to change scale:

Remark 2.1
.

\intop\nolimits_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau_{1}}Q(p(\tau))d\tau=\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}Q(p)u(p)dp\text{.}

Toward deriving our formula for the change in producer surplus, let profits at policy
\tau
be

\Pi(\tau)=(p(\tau)-\tau)Q(\tau)-\intop\nolimits_{0}^{Q(\tau)}\mathrm{MC}(Q)dq\text{.}

The following lemma states our central observation noted in the introduction. Namely, pass-through affects producer surplus only through a single integral term, the “path term” in (
2.2
).

Lemma 2.2
.

Under Assumptions
1
-
2
,

\Delta\mathrm{PS}=\underbrace{\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}Q(p)dp}_{-\Delta\text{CS}}-\underbrace{\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}Q(p)u(p)dp}_{\text{path term}}-\underbrace{\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}\kappa(Q)Q(p)dp}_{\text{conduct/markup}}\text{.}

Think of a per-unit tax change as tracing a path in
(p,\tau)
-space. Producer surplus moves for three distinct reasons, and Lemma
2.2
simply tallies them. First, when price moves by
dp
, holding the instant’s quantity
Q
fixed, producers gain the consumer-to-producer transfer
Qdp
accumulated along the path,
\intop\nolimits Qdp=-\Delta\mathrm{CS}
. Second, the tax itself takes a bite: each instant you levy
d\tau
on
Q
units, so producers lose
\intop\nolimits Qd\tau
(which we rewrite using Remark
2.1
). Third, as
p
changes, firms re-optimize quantity along demand: the value of the marginal units that disappear equals the markup on the margin times
dQ
. Using the conduct identity (
2
), this contribution is
-\intop\nolimits\kappa(Q)Qdp
. Intuitively, stronger conduct
\kappa
absorbs more of the potential transfer from higher prices because output contracts more.

We finish this section with some accounting:

\Delta\mathrm{TS}_{\mathrm{priv}}\coloneqq\Delta\mathrm{CS}+\Delta\mathrm{PS}=-\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}Qudp-\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}\kappa(Q)Qdp\text{,}

\Delta\mathrm{TS}\coloneqq\Delta\mathrm{CS}+\Delta\mathrm{PS}+\Delta\mathrm{TR}=\Delta\mathrm{TR}-\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}Qudp-\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}\kappa(Q)Qdp\text{,}

and

\mathrm{DWL}\coloneqq-\Delta\mathrm{TS}=\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}Qudp+\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}\kappa(Q)Qdp-\Delta\mathrm{TR}\text{.}

Note that
\Delta\mathrm{TR}
is given exactly by (
2.1
),
\Delta\mathrm{CS}
depends only on the curve
Q(\cdot)
, and the only path dependence is the term
\intop\nolimits Qudp
.

It is straightforward to rewrite the change in producer surplus as

\Delta\mathrm{PS}=\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}Q(p)dp-\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}Q(p)u(p)dp-\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}L(p)\left|\eta(p)\right|Q(p)dp\text{,}

since
L(p)\left|\eta(p)\right|=\kappa(Q)
pointwise.
This interchangeability is useful when one has external information on Lerner indices or demand elasticities instead of direct bounds on
\kappa(\cdot)
(say).

3
The Supply-Side

Fix any (weakly) decreasing and integrable demand curve
Q\colon\left[p_{0},p_{1}\right]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
. From the decomposition in (
2.2
), we see that the only contribution of
u
–the path
d\tau/dp
takes–to any of the welfare objects of interest is the term
\intop\nolimits Qudp
. Accordingly, it suffices to compute bounds only by manipulating this term.

We define

\mathcal{U}\coloneqq\left\{u\in L^{\infty}\left(\left[p_{0},p_{1}\right]\right)\colon\ \alpha\leq u\leq\beta\ \text{a.e.},\ \intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}udp=\Delta\tau\right\}\text{,}

and assume that
\Delta\tau\in\left[\alpha\Delta p,\beta\Delta p\right]
(or else
\mathcal{U}=\varnothing
). Define

F(u)\coloneqq\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}Q(p)u(p)dp\text{.}

Our first result identifies the simple form extremal, and, thus, bound-producing, paths must take:

Proposition 3.1
.

F
is maximized (minimized) over
\mathcal{U}
by
u
that takes only the endpoint values of
\left[\alpha,\beta\right]
with a single cutoff
p^{*}\in\left[p_{0},p_{1}\right]
:

u_{\max}(p)=\begin{cases}\beta,&p\in\left[p_{0},p^{*}\right),\\
\alpha,&p\in\left[p^{*},p_{1}\right],\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad u_{\min}(p)=\begin{cases}\alpha,&p\in\left[p_{0},p^{*}\right),\\
\beta,&p\in\left[p^{*},p_{1}\right]\text{,}\end{cases}

where
p^{*}
is uniquely pinned down (up to flat portions of
Q
of positive measure) by the “budget:” for
u_{\max}
,

\beta\left(p^{*}-p_{0}\right)+\alpha\left(p_{1}-p^{*}\right)=\Delta\tau\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad p^{*}=p^{*}_{\max}\coloneqq p_{0}+\frac{\Delta\tau-\alpha\Delta p}{\beta-\alpha}\text{,}

and for
u_{\min}
,

\alpha\left(p^{*}-p_{0}\right)+\beta\left(p_{1}-p^{*}\right)=\Delta\tau\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad p^{*}=p^{*}_{\min}\coloneqq p_{0}+\frac{\Delta\tau-\beta\Delta p}{\alpha-\beta}\text{.}

Hence, writing

I^{\omega}_{1}\coloneqq\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{\omega}^{*}}Q(p)dp\quad\text{and}\quad I^{\omega}_{2}\coloneqq\intop\nolimits_{p_{\omega}^{*}}^{p_{1}}Q(p)dp\quad\left(\omega\in\left\{\min,\max\right\}\right)\text{,}

we have

\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}Qu_{\max}dp=\beta I^{\max}_{1}+\alpha I^{\max}_{2},\quad\text{and}\quad\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}Qu_{\min}dp=\alpha I^{\min}_{1}+\beta I^{\min}_{2}\text{.}

The proof is simple: we are optimizing a linear functional over a compact and convex set, and can, therefore, find optima at extreme points. These take a simple form:
u(p)
travels between a high level
\beta
and a low level
\alpha
along the price axis
p\in\left[p_{0},p_{1}\right]
. The budget constraint
\intop\nolimits u=\Delta\tau
constrains how much “high time” we are allowed, but not where to place it. But this is easy, when we are maximizing the objective
F
, its form rewards allocating the high level where the demand
Q
is large. Since demand is (weakly) decreasing, the best use of the scarce “high time” is to allocate it to the left (low
p
), where
Q
is highest. This is the bathtub principle: if we pour a fixed volume of water into a tub whose bottom slopes downward from left to right (here
Q
is larger on the left), we fill the deep end first. The result is a single waterline (a cutoff
p^{*}
): to the left of
p^{*}
we are at
\beta
, to the right we are at
\alpha
, and the budget pins down
p^{*}
. The mirrored logic applies to the minimization task.

Moreover, if an optimizer is not of this threshold form, we can improve it by a tiny swap. Suppose there is a left location with higher weight
Q_{\mathrm{left}}
where
u
currently equals
\alpha
, and a right location with lower weight
Q_{\mathrm{right}}
where
u
equals
\beta
. Let’s move an
\varepsilon
-length slice of
\beta
-mass from the right to the left and–as we need to keep
\intop\nolimits u
fixed–move an
\varepsilon
-slice of
\alpha
-mass from the left to the right. The objective changes by

\Delta F=\varepsilon(\beta-\alpha)\left(Q_{\mathrm{left}}-Q_{\mathrm{right}}\right)\geq 0\text{,}

strictly so whenever
Q_{\mathrm{left}}>Q_{\mathrm{right}}
. Iterating these swaps pushes all
\beta
-mass leftward and all
\alpha
-mass rightward until a single cutoff remains. If
Q
is flat at the threshold, swaps within that plateau neither help nor hurt, explaining the possible non-uniqueness there (if
Q
is strictly decreasing a.e., the cutoff is unique).

Plugging
u_{\min}
into the producer-surplus formula produces

\Delta\mathrm{PS}=I^{\min}_{1}(1-\alpha)+I^{\min}_{2}(1-\beta)-\intop\nolimits\kappa(Q)Qdp\text{.}

Furthermore,
\kappa(Q)
lies in the interval
\left[\kappa_{L},\kappa_{U}\right]
and
Q(p)\geq 0
pointwise, hence, the optimizing
\kappa
is constant, so the upper
\mathrm{PS}
bound is

\Delta\mathrm{PS}=(1-\kappa_{L}-\alpha)I^{\min}_{1}+(1-\kappa_{L}-\beta)I^{\min}_{2}\text{.}

In turn, the lower bound exchanges
\alpha
and
\beta
and
\kappa_{L}
with
\kappa_{U}
:

\Delta\mathrm{PS}=(1-\kappa_{U}-\beta)I^{\max}_{1}+(1-\kappa_{U}-\alpha)I^{\max}_{2}\text{.}

Thus, we have

-(\beta I^{\max}_{1}+\alpha I^{\max}_{2})-\kappa_{U}\left(I^{\max}_{1}+I^{\max}_{2}\right)\leq\Delta\mathrm{TS}_{\mathrm{priv}}\leq-(\alpha I^{\min}_{1}+\beta I^{\min}_{2})-\kappa_{L}\left(I^{\min}_{1}+I^{\min}_{2}\right)\text{,}

and

(\alpha I^{\min}_{1}+\beta I^{\min}_{2})+\kappa_{L}\left(I^{\min}_{1}+I^{\min}_{2}\right)-\Delta\mathrm{TR}\leq\mathrm{DWL}\leq(\beta I^{\max}_{1}+\alpha I^{\max}_{2})+\kappa_{U}\left(I^{\max}_{1}+I^{\max}_{2}\right)-\Delta\mathrm{TR}\text{.}

4
The Demand-Side Result

In their demand-side analysis, KV do not make parametric assumptions about the true demand curve, but instead study the collection of demand curves that satisfy various shape restrictions. Namely, KV posit two increasing functions
A(q)
and
B(p)
and define a function
G\coloneqq A\circ Q
as

G\left(s\right)\coloneqq A\left(Q\left(p\left(s\right)\right)\right),\quad\text{for}\quad s\in\left[s_{0},s_{1}\right]=\left[B\left(p_{0}\right),B\left(p_{1}\right)\right].

Economically,
B
picks the “units” along the price axis (levels versus logs, etc.), while
A
picks the “units” along the quantity axis (levels, logs, etc.). KV then impose shape restrictions on
G
either by assuming that the derivative of
G
lies in a specified interval or by assuming that the second derivative lies in a specified interval. Importantly,
A
and
B
are simply ways of reparametrizing demand so that shape information is stated in the most natural units.

The researcher, thus, picks
A
and
B
to align the constraint with the empirical object she trusts: if she wants to make assumptions on the slope of demand, she posits
A\left(q\right)=q
and
B\left(p\right)=p
, so that
G^{\prime}\left(s\right)=dQ/dp
and
G^{\prime\prime}\left(s\right)=d^{2}Q/dp^{2}
. If she wants to assume an interval of elasticities,

\mathcal{W}\coloneqq\left\{w\in L^{\infty}(\left[p_{0},p_{1}\right])\colon\ 0\leq w\leq 1\ \text{a.e.},\ \intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{1}}wdp=m\right\}\text{.}

Moreover, its extreme points are the indicators of measurable sets of measure
m
:

\operatorname{ext}(\mathcal{W})=\left\{\mathbf{1}_{A}\colon\ A\subset\left[p_{0},p_{1}\right],\ \lambda(A)=m\right\}\text{.}

Since
\Phi
is linear, it attains its maximum and minimum at extreme points, i.e.,
w=\mathbf{1}_{A}
with
\lambda(A)=m
. For any such
A
,

\Phi(\mathbf{1}_{A})=\intop\nolimits_{A}Q(p)dp.

Because
Q
is (weakly) decreasing, among all
A
with
\lambda(A)=m
,

\intop\nolimits_{A}Q\leq\intop\nolimits_{[p_{0},p_{0}+m)}Q,\quad\text{and}\quad\intop\nolimits_{A}Q\geq\intop\nolimits_{(p_{1}-m,p_{1}]}Q\text{,}

i.e., the integral is maximized by the leftmost initial interval and minimized by the rightmost terminal interval. This is the Hardy-Littlewood rearrangement inequality (Lemma
A.1
): if
Q^{*}
,
w^{*}
are the decreasing rearrangements, then
\intop\nolimits Qw\leq\intop\nolimits Q^{*}w^{*}
; here
Q^{*}=Q
(up to null sets), and the maximizing
w^{*}
is
\mathbf{1}_{[p_{0},p_{0}+m)}
.

Finally, we undo the normalization. Returning to
u=\alpha+(\beta-\alpha)w
,

u_{\max}(p)=\alpha+(\beta-\alpha)\mathbf{1}_{[p_{0},p_{0}+m)}(p)=\begin{cases}\beta,&p\in\left[p_{0},p_{\max}^{*}\right),\\
\alpha,&p\in\left[p_{\max}^{*},p_{1}\right],\end{cases}\quad p_{\max}^{*}\coloneqq p_{0}+m,

and similarly for
u_{\min}
with the high segment placed on
(p_{1}-m,p_{1}]
.
Using
m=(\Delta\tau-\alpha\Delta p)/(\beta-\alpha)
gives

p_{\max}^{*}=p_{0}+\frac{\Delta\tau-\alpha\Delta p}{\beta-\alpha},\quad\text{and}\quad p_{\min}^{*}=p_{0}+\frac{\beta\Delta p-\Delta\tau}{\beta-\alpha}.

For
\omega\in\left\{\max,\min\right\}
, writing
I^{\omega}_{1}=\intop\nolimits_{p_{0}}^{p_{\omega}^{*}}Q
, and
I^{\omega}_{2}=\intop\nolimits_{p_{\omega}^{*}}^{p_{1}}Q
, the objective values are

\intop\nolimits Qu_{\max}dp=\beta I^{\max}_{1}+\alpha I^{\max}_{2},\qquad\intop\nolimits Qu_{\min}dp=\alpha I^{\min}_{1}+\beta I^{\min}_{2}.

We note that if
Q
has a flat (zero-slope) plateau at the threshold level, any mixture on that plateau achieving measure
m
is optimal and yields the same value. If
Q
is strictly decreasing a.e., the cutoff
p^{*}
is unique.
∎

Proof of Propositions

and
4.5
.

Though we assume that
\varphi
changes sign once, we will
characterize the case when it is always positive first.

The problems are

\max/\min\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s_{1}}\varphi(s)A^{-1}\left(G\left(s\right)\right)ds\text{,}

subject to either

\left\{\begin{aligned} &f(s)=G^{\prime}(s)\in\left[\underline{f},\overline{f}\right]\ \text{a.e. on }[s_{0},s_{1}],\quad\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s_{1}}f(s)ds=\Delta A,\\
&\text{and}\quad G(s)=A(q_{0})+\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s}f(t)dt,\end{aligned}\right\}

or

\left\{\begin{aligned} &h(s)=G^{\prime\prime}(s)\in[\underline{h},\overline{h}]\ \text{a.e. on }[s_{0},s_{1}],\quad\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s_{1}}h(s)ds=\Delta f,\\
&\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s_{1}}(s_{1}-s)h(s)ds=\Delta A-f(s_{0})(s_{1}-s_{0}),\\
&f(s)=f(s_{0})+\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s}h(t)dt,\\
&\text{and}\quad G(s)=A(q_{0})+\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s}f(t)dt.\end{aligned}\right\}

Next, we recall some standard facts. Fix
s_{0}<s_{1}
and let
\lambda
denote the Lebesgue measure on
\left[s_{0},s_{1}\right]
.
For an integrable function
f\colon\left[s_{0},s_{1}\right]\to\mathbb{R}
, its (equimeasurable) decreasing rearrangement

f^{\downarrow}\colon\left[s_{0},s_{1}\right]\to\mathbb{R}
is the unique nonincreasing function such that

\lambda\left(\left\{f>t\right\}\right)=\left(\left\{f^{\downarrow}>t\right\}\right)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Given
f\in L^{1}\left(\left[s_{0},s_{1}\right]\right)
, define

G_{f}(s)\coloneqq A(q_{0})+\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s}f(t)dt,\quad\text{and}\quad G_{f^{\downarrow}}(s)\coloneqq A(q_{0})+\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s}f^{\downarrow}(t)dt\text{,}\quad s\in\left[s_{0},s_{1}\right].

Then,

Lemma A.2
.

For any
f\in L^{1}\left(\left[s_{0},s_{1}\right]\right)
and every
s\in\left[s_{0},s_{1}\right]
,

\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s}f(t)dt\leq\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s}f^{\downarrow}(t)dt,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s_{1}}f=\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s_{1}}f^{\downarrow}.

Equivalently,
G_{f^{\downarrow}}(s)\geq G_{f}(s)
for all
s
.

Proof.

Fix
s\in\left[s_{0},s_{1}\right]
and set
\ell=s-s_{0}
.
Among all measurable sets
E\subseteq\left[s_{0},s_{1}\right]
with
\lambda(E)=\ell
, Lemma
A.1
gives

\sup_{\lambda(E)=\ell}\intop\nolimits_{E}f=\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s_{0}+\ell}f^{\downarrow}(u)du=\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s}f^{\downarrow}(u)du.

Since
\left[s_{0},s\right]
has measure
\ell
, we obtain

\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s}f\leq\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s}f^{\downarrow}
.

\intop\nolimits f=\intop\nolimits f^{\downarrow}
is by the definition of an equimeasurable rearrangement.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

Suppose
\underline{f},\overline{f}\in\mathbb{R}
with
\underline{f}\leq f\leq\overline{f}
a.e. on
\left[s_{0},s_{1}\right]
.
Define the normalized control

\iota(s)\coloneqq\frac{f(s)-\underline{f}}{\overline{f}-\underline{f}}\in\left[0,1\right]\quad\text{a.e.,}

and its total mass

m\ \coloneqq\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s_{1}}\iota(t)dt=\frac{\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s_{1}}f-\underline{f}(s_{1}-s_{0})}{\overline{f}-\underline{f}}=\frac{\Delta A-\underline{f}(s_{1}-s_{0})}{\overline{f}-\underline{f}}.

Then for every
s\in\left[s_{0},s_{1}\right]
,

\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s}f(t)dt=\underline{f}(s-s_{0})+\left(\overline{f}-\underline{f}\right)\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s}\iota(t)dt\leq\underline{f}(s-s_{0})+\left(\overline{f}-\underline{f}\right)\min\{s-s_{0},m\}.

Equality holds simultaneously for all
s
if and only if

\iota=\mathbf{1}_{\left[s_{0},s_{0}+m\right]}
a.e., i.e.,
f=\overline{f}
on
\left[s_{0},s_{0}+m\right]

and
f=\underline{f}
on
\left(s_{0}+m,s_{1}\right]
.

Proof.

Since
0\leq\iota\leq 1
a.e., for any
s
we have

\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s}\iota\leq\min\left\{\lambda\left(\left[s_{0},s\right]\right),\ \intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s_{1}}\iota\right\}=\min\left\{s-s_{0},m\right\}
.
This bound is attained by the bang-bang control
\iota^{*}=\mathbf{1}_{\left[s_{0},s_{0}+m\right]}
,
for which
\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s}\iota^{*}=\min\left\{s-s_{0},m\right\}
.
We insert into the identity for
\intop\nolimits_{s_{0}}^{s}f
to conclude.
∎
