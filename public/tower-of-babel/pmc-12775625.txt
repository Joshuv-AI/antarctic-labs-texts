Gigantic lumbar aneurysmal bone cyst with abdominal extension in a pediatric patient: case report and its multidisciplinary management
Authors: Anouar Bourghli, Faisal Konbaz, Firas Alsebayel, Monerah Annaim, Ziad Alyousif, Jehan Howsawi, Zakaria Habib, Khalid AlMusrea
Published: 2025

Key findings

• A 12-year-old female presented with gigantic expansile lytic lesion with extension to visceral organs found to have largest aneurysmal bone cyst (ABC) of the spine in the pediatric literature.

What is known and what is new?

• Known treatment options include surgical excision, embolization, sclerotherapy, cryotherapy, and denosumab.

• Since the lesion is big and expansile in a young female, we elected to excise it as a treatment to avoid further calcification inside the abdomen which may affected her future pregnancies given its mass effect.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Multidisciplinary surgical management enabled satisfactory clinical and radiological outcomes with no recurrence at the last follow-up.

• When there is an ABC in the abdomen, the decision to treat such cases surgically might be dictated by the mass size, as sclerotherapy and embolization will calcify the lesion and the mass effect might harm the adjacent organs specially in a young female given future pregnancy.

Introduction

Aneurysmal bone cyst (ABC) is a rare, benign, and highly vascular osteolytic bone lesion. It is characterized by expansile cystic spaces filled with blood and surrounded by thinned cortical bone (1). ABCs were first described in 1942 by Dr. Jaffe and Lichenstein as a “peculiar blood containing cyst of large size”. ABCs comprise 1.4% of all primary bone tumors. The vertebral column is involved in 3–30% of cases and most commonly found in the posterior elements of the lumbar spine. ABC occurs in the first two decades of life with slight female predominance (2).

The evaluation of ABC typically includes radiological studies like computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which characteristically exhibit well defined, thin walled, multiseptated expansile osteolytic cavities with fluid-fluid levels. However, histological studies are the gold-standard for diagnosis of ABC as some radiological findings can co-exist with other spinal pathologies, such as giant cell tumor or osteosarcoma (3).

Management of spinal ABCs poses a challenge to the treating physician given its proximity to the neural structures, with treatment options variating from observation to surgical resection. Radiation therapy, cryotherapy, and embolization are also other effective treatment methods. However, no treatment protocol has been established in the literature, particularly when the tumor is uncommonly large (1,4). Herein, we present the case of 12-year-old female who presented with painless flank swelling and found to have massive ABC of the lumbar spine occupying her abdominal cavity. We present this case in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-25-84/rc).

Case presentation

A 12-year-old female, previously healthy, presented to our spine surgery clinic with a 4-month history of left flank swelling. The swelling was painless, not preceded by trauma. Patient and parent denied symptoms of abdominal pain, change in bowel habits, weight loss, night sweats, or loss of appetite. On examination, the patient had a mass felt with superficial abdominal palpation, otherwise normal examination with intact neurological exam of upper and lower limbs. Diagnostic imaging was performed to study the nature of the lesion. Standard X-ray imaging showed mild sclerotic deformity of the lumbar spine. A CT scan revealed a 10.8 cm × 10.2 cm × 10.8 cm expansile lytic lesion in the paraspinal region originating from the left pedicle and vertebral body of L4 with extension to the abdominal viscera. MRI further characterized the lesion as a large fluid-fluid mass on the left side of the L4 vertebral body. This mass caused significant displacement of surrounding tissues and muscles, with relatively minimal neural compression. These imaging findings led to the suspicion of an ABC while considering other differential diagnoses such as telangiectatic osteosarcoma and giant cell tumor. Biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of ABC. The patient underwent ultrasound (US)-guided embolization and sclerotherapy performed by the interventional radiology. Post-procedure MRI showed progression of the expansile lesion in the L4 vertebral body with mass effect on the surrounding tissues. Embolization and sclerotherapy showed low effectiveness. Due to persistent compression on internal organs, and the eventual possibility that with time progressive calcification of the tumor inside the abdomen might compromise internal organs such as uterus expansion during future pregnancies, surgical intervention was deemed necessary. The parents were counseled and it was opted for tumor excision with spinal instrumentation. Case was discussed in a multidisciplinary team meeting, and surgical excision was done with the aid of urology and pediatric surgery teams.

Surgical technique

The patient was placed supine on a regular table. A left ureteric stent was placed by pediatric urology team. Later, pediatric surgery team joined, and an access through a midline laparotomy incision with trans-peritoneal approach was performed, and the tumor was exposed. The tumor was released from all the surrounding tissues including the overlying iliopsoas muscle. A complete extended intralesional resection was performed. Various samples were sent for histopathology. The disc space of L3–4 and L4–5 was removed from the left side and cage preparation followed. A Mesh cage was placed with bone graft. The cage had a satisfying primary stability. By then the first stage of surgery was completed. Figures 5-7 show intraoperative gross images of the tumor.

Afterwards, On the same setting, the patient was positioned prone on a Jackson table for the second stage of procedure. With a traditional posterior approach, a subperiosteal dissection was performed. A short segment instrumentation from L3 to L5 was performed, skipping left L4 given that the tumor occupied the left pedicle and part of the lamina. The remaining posterior part of the tumor was resected protecting L4 nerve root, that included L4 left hemilaminectomy and left sided pedicle removal. Patient tolerated the surgery well. Surgical time was 5.5 hours, with an estimated blood loss of 1,000 mL. Patient postoperatively was shifted to pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) for observation. Patient had an uneventful hospital stay with satisfactory recovery; she was discharged day 6 postoperatively. Histopathological samples came back to be consistent with ABC. Follow-up

Patient was followed at 2 weeks, 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year and 2 years postoperatively. Patient had good recovery and stable implant fixation. Patient postoperative CT confirmed full excision of the tumor and regular MRIs done during the follow-up visits confirmed no recurrence of the ABC. Figure 8 shows postoperative X-rays, CT and MRI.

Discussion

Management of giant ABC poses a formidable challenge due to its complexity and potential neurological complications. Due to the paucity of such cases, guidelines dictating the management and expected complications are scarce. This case report offers crucial aspects of management of such cases and demonstrates the importance of multidisciplinary approach.

US-guided embolization and sclerotherapy has been used previously in the literature. Marushima et al. reports a case of ABC managed exclusively with selective arterial embolization. Similarly, Guibaud et al. reported 16 patients who underwent embolization with acceptable outcome (5,6). Sclerotherapy was also found to show promising results, with success rate reaching 91% in meta-analysis reported by Cruz et al. however, further research and high level studies are needed to display its effectiveness (7). The combination of sclerotherapy and US-guided embolization have been described previously by Masthoff et al. for nonresectable and refractory masses, showing significant mineralization of the mass (8). In this case, the use of sclerotherapy and selective arterial embolization adequately mineralized the mass for subsequent resection.

Prior to the emergence of non-surgical therapies, surgery has been the cornerstone of treatment of ABCs, however, due to the complexity of tumor features and high complication rates, the use of adjunct treatments is needed. The aim of surgical intervention is complete eradication of the tumor, as well as achievement of spinal stability. Conversely, complete eradication is challenging as reported several studies exhibiting a 14% to 50% recurrence rate (1,9,10).

The utilization of denosumab has emanated as a potential treatment for ABC in recent years. While its use for giant cell tumor (GCT) has been approved and standardized, its use for ABCs is still understudied and further research is needed, as shown by Sandhu et al. recent review showing 67 cases reported its use, with 24 patients developing calcium abnormalities, all among pediatric group (11). Although results are encouraging, the authors believe that further studies regarding its safety among pediatric population is needed prior to standardize its use.

Spinal ABC management remains controversial, including radiation, sclerotherapy, embolization, curettage and surgical excision. It all depends on size, location, neurological compromise and spinal stability. Total surgical excision is considered the optimal approach in preventing recurrence. In the current case, the patient initially underwent US-guided embolization and sclerotherapy, aiming to reduce the vascularity of the lesion and its size. The tumor size was the biggest concern, since the embolization and sclerotherapy failed to regress the mass. Surgical intervention with tumor excision was decided considering the factors of size and its relation to the surrounding abdominal organs. In literature, this is the first reported case of such size and presentation. In addition, having it done through a double anterior and posterior approach in a single setting without complications and with a good outcome. The reported case had a combined anterior and posterior approach based on the need for complete excision of the lesion while ensuring spinal stability. The anterior approach allowed for thorough resection of the mass, while the posterior approach facilitated a complementary posterior resection and stabilization of the spine. This patient underwent a hemi-resection of the vertebral body, allowing more stability anteriorly with half of the body, in addition to the mesh cage. Given patient age, and presence of L4 remaining pedicle, a short-instrumented fusion was performed, one pedicle screw could be inserted through the latter allowing short instrumented fusion from L3 to L5 and therefore better motion preservation.

Conclusions

ABCs are rare benign primary bone tumors that can present in the spine without any back pain or neurological deficit, as it was presented in the current case. This case described the largest spinal ABC in the pediatric literature and highlighted the importance of thorough investigation and importance of multidisciplinary management in such presentation. Surgical resection prevents recurrence and alleviates the future implications that long term mass effect might have on surrounding structures.
