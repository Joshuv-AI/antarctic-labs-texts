Self-duality of massless scalar three-point amplitudes
Oliver Schnetz

Abstract
. We prove that massless scalar three-point amplitudes are self-dual under Fourier transformation. This implies that the momentum space amplitude can be expressed as the position space amplitude of the same graph with transformed edge-weights (not the dual graph) if external vertices are labeled accordingly. In particular, a massless scalar three-point integral can be expressed as a graphical function. The result follows immediately from a theorem by M. Golz, E. Panzer and the author on parametric representations of position space integrals (2015), but it was only observed by X. Jiang in 2025 in the context of four-dimensional Super-Yang-Mills theory. We generalize Jiang’s result and discuss the consequences of the self-duality in the context of graphical functions. In particular, we derive a new identity for graphical functions and a new twist relation for scalar integrals (Feynman periods) in ϕ 4 \phi^{4} theory.

Concretely, we consider a connected graph

D=2\lambda+2.

The graph

|\mathcal{V}_{G}^{\mathrm{int}}|

The edges of

A standard edge

we can write a double edge (a bubble) as a single edge of weight
\nu_{e}=2

The signature of the norm

The three-point integral

(3)

A_{G}(z_{0},z_{1},z_{2})=\Big(\prod_{x_{i}\in\mathcal{V}_{G}^{\mathrm{int}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{D}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{D}x_{v}}{\pi^{D/2}}\Big)\prod_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{G}}p_{e}(x,z),

In position space, the scaling weight of the graph
G

(4)

N_{G}=\Big(\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{G}}\nu_{e}\Big)-\frac{D}{2\lambda}|\mathcal{V}_{G}^{\mathrm{int}}|.

A key feature of position space three-point integrals is that they can be expressed as a single-valued function on the punctured complex plane

\frac{||z_{20}||^{2}}{||z_{10}||^{2}}=z{\overline{z}},\qquad\frac{||z_{21}||^{2}}{||z_{10}||^{2}}=(z-1)({\overline{z}}-1).

In the context of Super-Yang-Mills (SYM) theory, one encounters convergent four-point functions that are conformal in the sense that every internal vertex has weighted degree

In Section

2.
Integer dimensions

In this Section we prove (
19
) in integer dimensions
D\geq 1
. To do this, we only use that
the Feynman integral
A_{G}
can be expressed in terms of the invariants
||z_{20}||^{2}/||z_{10}||^{2}
and
||z_{21}||^{2}/||z_{10}||^{2}
; see (
5
) and (
6
).
Note that in a scalar theory the Fourier transform is an involution,
\widehat{\widehat{A_{G}}}=A_{G}
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
1\leq D\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and let
G
be a connected weighted graph with three external vertices
0,z_{1},z_{2}
and
|\mathcal{V}_{G}^{\mathrm{int}}|
internal vertices
such that the position space Feynman integral
A_{G}(0,z_{1},z_{2})
in (
3
) exists.
If
N_{G}=D/2
(see (
4
)), then
A_{G}(0,z_{1},z_{2})
is invariant under the Fourier transform (
15
),

(23)

A_{G}(0,z_{1},z_{2})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2D}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}}{(2\pi)^{D}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x_{1}\cdot z_{2}-\mathrm{i}x_{2}\cdot z_{1}}A_{G}(0,x_{1},x_{2}).

Proof.

Up to a scale, we can express
A_{G}(0,x_{1},x_{2})
in terms of the invariants
x_{2}^{2}/x_{1}^{2}
and
(x_{2}-x_{1})^{2}/x_{1}^{2}

(see (
5
) and (
6
) for
x_{0}=0
),

A_{G}(0,x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{1}{||x_{1}||^{D}}f\Big(\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{x_{1}^{2}},\frac{(x_{2}-x_{1})^{2}}{x_{1}^{2}}\Big),

for some function
f
. We first consider the integral over
x_{2}
,

I_{1}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{D}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{D}x_{2}}{||x_{1}||^{D}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}x_{2}\cdot z_{1}}f\Big(\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{x_{1}^{2}},\frac{x_{2}^{2}-2x_{2}\cdot x_{1}+x_{1}^{2}}{x_{1}^{2}}\Big).

We use polar decompositions for
x_{1}
and
z_{1}
,
x_{1}=||x_{1}||\hat{x}_{1}
,
z_{1}=||z_{1}||\hat{z}_{1}
, where
\hat{x}_{1}
and
\hat{z}_{1}
are unit vectors.
By rotational symmetry, the integral
I_{1}
only depends on
||x_{1}||
,
||z_{1}||
and the scalar product
\hat{x}_{1}\cdot\hat{z}_{1}
.
In particular,
I_{1}
is invariant under exchange of
\hat{x}_{1}
and
\hat{z}_{1}
. Changing the integration variable
x_{2}
to
||x_{1}||x_{2}
yields

I_{1}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{D}}\mathrm{d}^{D}x_{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}||x_{1}||x_{2}\cdot\hat{x}_{1}||z_{1}||}f(x_{2}^{2},x_{2}^{2}-2x_{2}\cdot\hat{z}_{1}+1).

Note that the function
f
does not depend on
x_{1}
and that the exponent simplifies to
-\mathrm{i}||z_{1}||x_{2}\cdot x_{1}
.
By definition of the Fourier transform, the integrand has an infinitesimal Gaussian suppression factor
\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma(x_{1}^{1}+x_{2}^{2})}
in the limit
\sigma\to 0
.
With this convention, the integral is absolutely convergent and we may swap the order of the integrations. The integration over
x_{1}
gives a
\delta
function,

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{D}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{D}x_{1}}{(2\pi)^{D}}I_{1}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{D}}\mathrm{d}^{D}x_{2}\delta^{D}(z_{2}-||z_{1}||x_{2})f(x_{2}^{2},x_{2}^{2}-2x_{2}\cdot\hat{z}_{1}+1).

The integral over
x_{2}
gives
x_{2}=z_{2}/||z_{1}||
, yielding

\frac{1}{||z_{1}||^{D}}f\Big(\frac{z_{2}^{2}}{z_{1}^{2}},\frac{z_{2}^{2}-2z_{2}\cdot z_{1}+z_{1}^{2}}{z_{1}^{2}}\Big)=A_{G}(0,z_{1},z_{2}).

∎

The first identity in (
19
) is proved in the Lemma. The second identity follows because, by (
18
), the momentum space integral
A_{G}^{p}(p_{1},p_{2})
has the same scaling
weight as
A_{G}(0,z_{1},z_{2})
. Note that the factor of
\lambda
in
\lambda N_{G}
comes from the convention to extract
\lambda
from the exponent in propagators (so that weight
one in position space corresponds to weight one in momentum space).

Alternatively, one can derive the second identity in (
19
) from the first identity by Fourier transforming (
14
).

Self-duality (
19
) is special to massless three-point functions. It does not generalize to higher
n
-point functions or to theories with particle masses.

3.
Parametric representation

It is a classic result in QFT that Feynman integrals can be expressed in parametric space (Schwinger or Feynman parameters) where the integration is over positive real variables

\alpha_{e}
that correspond to the edges
e\in\mathcal{E}_{G}
of a connected graph
G
; see e.g.
[
15
]
. We define the graph (Kirchhoff, first Symanzik) polynomial
[
18
]

(24)

\Psi_{G}(\alpha)=\sum_{\mathrm{span}\,T}\prod_{e\notin T}\alpha_{e},

where the sum is over spanning trees in
G
. The second Symanzik polynomial carries the kinematic structure (we only consider the massless case). It is a sum over spanning two-forests,

(25)

F_{G}(\alpha)=\sum_{\mathrm{span}\,T_{1}\cup T_{2}}p(T_{1})^{2}\prod_{e\notin T_{1}\cup T_{2}}\alpha_{e},

where

(26)

p(T_{1})=\sum_{v\in T_{1}^{\mathrm{ext}}}p(v)=-p(T_{2}).

Here, the sum is over the vertices of
T_{1}
that are external in
G
and
p(v)
is the ingoing momentum at
v
. By momentum conservation, the total ingoing momentum is zero,
hence
p(T_{1})=-p(T_{2})
.

With these polynomials, we can write the Feynman integral
A_{G}^{p}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
as projective integral over the positive coordinate simplex
\Delta=\{\alpha_{e}>0\}
,

(27)

A_{G}^{p}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})=\frac{\Gamma(N_{G}^{p})}{\prod_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{G}}\Gamma(\nu_{e}^{p})}\int_{\Delta}\Omega\frac{\prod_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{G}}\alpha_{e}^{\nu_{e}^{p}-1}}{\Psi_{G}^{D/2-N_{G}^{p}}F_{G}^{N_{G}^{p}}}.

The integration measure
\Omega
is the projective volume form

(28)

\Omega=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{G}}(-1)^{e}\alpha_{e}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\mathrm{d}\alpha_{e-1}\wedge\mathrm{d}\alpha_{e+1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\mathrm{d}\alpha_{|\mathcal{E}_{G}|},

where we assume that the edges are labeled from
1
to
|\mathcal{E}_{G}|
.

Note that in parametric representation, the dimension
D
is a parameter in the exponents of the denominator. This makes it possible to generalize Feynman integrals
to any noninteger
D
for which the integral (
27
) exists. By analytic continuation,
A_{G}^{p}
can be extended to a meromorphic function in
D
on the complex plane.

In practice, one transfers to affine space by setting one
\alpha_{e}=1
(or, less efficiently,
\sum_{e}\alpha_{e}=1
) yielding the integration

\int_{0}^{\infty}\cdots\int_{0}^{\infty}\cdots\mathrm{d}\alpha_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\mathrm{d}\alpha_{e-1}\wedge\mathrm{d}\alpha_{e+1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\mathrm{d}\alpha_{|\mathcal{E}_{G}|}
.
The sign
(-1)^{e}
is absorbed by the orientation of the hyperplane
\{\alpha_{e}=1\}
.
For small to medium loop orders one can use the parametric representation to explicitly perform one integral after the other
[
1
,
6
,
17
,
19
]
.
For higher loop orders, parametric integration becomes very slow, producing a large number of terms in intermediate steps. In the setup of massless three-point functions
(or conformal four-point functions), the method of graphical functions is much more efficient.

In
[
13
]
, the analogous result in position space was derived. We need the following set of polynomials

(29)

\Psi_{G}^{1,\ldots,|\mathcal{V}_{G}^{\mathrm{ext}}|}(\alpha)=\sum_{\mathrm{span}\,F}\prod_{e\notin F}\alpha_{e},

where the sum is over spanning
|\mathcal{V}_{G}^{\mathrm{ext}}|
-forests in which every tree has exactly one external vertex, and

(30)

\Psi_{G}^{ij,\{k\neq ij\}}(\alpha)=\sum_{\mathrm{span}\,F}\prod_{e\notin F}\alpha_{e},

where the sum is over spanning
|\mathcal{V}_{G}^{\mathrm{ext}}|-1
-forests in which one tree has the external vertices
i,j
and every other tree has exactly one of the other external vertices.
From the second set of polynomials we construct the polynomial
\Phi
that contains the position space invariants
||z_{ij}||^{2}
,

(31)

\Phi(\alpha)=\sum_{i>j}||z_{ij}||^{2}\Psi_{G}^{ij,\{k\neq ij\}}(\alpha).

For the three-star on the left hand side of (
7
), for example, we get

(32)

\Psi_{G}^{0,1,z}(\alpha)=\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{1}\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3},\quad\Phi(\alpha)=||z_{21}||^{2}\alpha_{1}+||z_{20}||^{2}\alpha_{2}+||z_{10}||^{2}\alpha_{3},

where we labeled the edges attached to
0,1,z
by
1,2,3
.

If all edge weights
\nu_{e}>0
, we obtain for the Feynman integral
A_{G}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{|\mathcal{V}_{G}^{\mathrm{ext}}|-1})
(see Corollary 1.8 in
[
13
]
)

(33)

A_{G}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{|\mathcal{V}_{G}^{\mathrm{ext}}|-1})=\frac{\Gamma(\lambda N_{G})}{\prod_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{G}}\Gamma(\lambda\nu_{e})}\int_{\Delta}\Omega\frac{\prod_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{G}}\alpha_{e}^{\lambda(1-\nu_{e})}}{(\Psi_{G}^{1,\ldots,|\mathcal{V}_{G}^{\mathrm{ext}}|})^{D/2-\lambda N_{G}}\Phi_{G}^{\lambda N_{G}}}.

If we specialize to three external vertices
z_{0},z_{1},z_{2}
, the polynomials
\Psi_{G}^{10,2}
,
\Psi_{G}^{20,1}
,
\Psi_{G}^{21,0}
are given by sums over two-forests.
With the identification of external vertices with momenta in Figure
2
, we obtain

(34)

\Phi_{G}=F_{G}.

Moreover, with (
13
) and (
18
) we obtain

(35)

A_{G}(0,p_{1},p_{2})=\frac{\Gamma(D-N_{G}^{p})}{\prod_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{G}}\Gamma(\lambda\nu_{e})}\int_{\Delta}\Omega\frac{\prod_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{G}}\alpha_{e}^{\nu_{e}^{p}-1}}{(\Psi_{G}^{0,p_{1},p_{2}})^{N_{G}^{p}-D/2}F_{G}^{D-N_{G}^{p}}}.

If
N_{G}^{p}=D/2
, the polynomials
\Psi_{G}
in (
27
) and
\Psi_{G}^{0,p_{1},p_{2}}
in (
35
) drop and we obtain

(36)

\frac{\prod_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{G}}\Gamma(\lambda\nu_{e})}{\Gamma(D/2)}A_{G}(0,p_{1},p_{2})=\frac{\prod_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{G}}\Gamma(\nu_{e}^{p})}{\Gamma(D/2)}A_{G}^{p}(p_{1},p_{2}).

Equation (
20
) follows.

We close this section by expressing the polynomials
\Psi_{G}^{z_{0},z_{1},z_{2}}
and
\Phi
(see (
29
) and (
31
)) in terms of graph polynomials (
24
).
The advantage of such a representation is that graph polynomials are well studied in literature; see e.g.
[
7
,
28
]
.

To do this, we reduce the graph
G
to the graph
G_{012}=G(z_{0}=z_{1}=z_{2})
by identifying all external vertices.
If we identify the edges in
G_{012}
with the edges in
G
we observe that

(37)

\Psi_{G}^{z_{0},z_{1},z_{2}}=\Psi_{G_{012}}

because the spanning three-forests of
G
are in one to one correspondence with the spanning trees of
G_{012}
.
This construction generalizes to any number of external vertices
[
13
]
.

Likewise, for
i,j\in\{0,1,2\}
we define
G_{ij}=G(z_{i}=z_{j})
as
G
with vertices
z_{i}
and
z_{j}
identified.
So,
G_{ij}
has the two external vertices
z_{i}=z_{j}
and
z_{k}
, where
\{i,j,k\}=\{0,1,2\}
. Again, we identify the edges of
G_{ij}
with the edges of
G
.
By correspondence of spanning two-forests with spanning trees, we obtain

(38)

\Psi_{G}^{ik,j}+\Psi_{G}^{jk,i}=\Psi_{G_{ij}}.

The above set of equations inverts to (this is only possible for three external vertices)

(39)

\Psi_{G}^{ij,k}=\frac{\Psi_{G_{ik}}+\Psi_{G_{jk}}-\Psi_{G_{ij}}}{2}.

Substitution into (
31
) yields

(40)

\displaystyle\Phi(\alpha)

\displaystyle=(z_{1}-z_{0})^{2}\frac{\Psi_{G_{20}}+\Psi_{G_{21}}-\Psi_{G_{10}}}{2}+\text{two cyclic terms}

\displaystyle=\frac{(z_{2}-z_{0})^{2}+(z_{2}-z_{1})^{2}-(z_{1}-z_{0})^{2}}{2}\Psi_{G_{10}}+\text{two cyclic terms}

\displaystyle=(z_{2}-z_{1})\cdot(z_{2}-z_{0})\Psi_{G_{10}}+(z_{1}-z_{2})\cdot(z_{1}-z_{0})\Psi_{G_{20}}+(z_{0}-z_{2})\cdot(z_{0}-z_{1})\Psi_{G_{21}}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{z({\overline{z}}-1)+{\overline{z}}(z-1)}{2}\Psi_{G_{10}}+\frac{2-z-{\overline{z}}}{2}\Psi_{G_{20}}+\frac{z+{\overline{z}}}{2}\Psi_{G_{21}}\Big)||z_{10}||^{2}.

In the last equation, we used the identification (
6
) of invariants with expressions in
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

5.
A new identity for scalar integrals in
\phi^{4}
theory

In this section, we consider (convergent) scalar integrals in massless
\phi^{4}
theory, where all edges have weight
\nu_{e}=1
and
D=4
,
\lambda=1
.
These scalar integrals (in a mathematical context called
\phi^{4}
periods) are renormalization scheme independent contributions to the beta function arising
from vertex integrals with no sub-divergence. The corresponding graphs are called primitive (see e.g.
[
24
]
).
Alternatively, one can consider scalar integrals as massless
p
-integrals at unit momentum.

In parametric space, the scalar integral of a primitive
\phi^{4}
graph
G
can be calculated with the graph polynomial, see (
24
),

(44)

P_{G}=\int_{\Delta}\frac{\Omega}{\Psi_{G}(\alpha)^{2}}.

In
\phi^{4}
theory, there exists one primitive graph at one loop, no primitive graph at two loops, and
one primitive graph at three loops; see Figure
3
.
The scalar integrals of the bubble and the tetrahedron are
1
and
6\zeta(3)=6\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{-3}
, respectively.

bubble
tetrahedron

Figure 3.
The bubble and the tetrahedron are the smallest primitive graphs in
\phi^{4}
theory.

First systematic calculations of scalar integrals in
\phi^{4}
theory were done with exact numerical methods by D. Broadhurst and D. Kreimer
[
5
]
.
The method was extended by the author in
[
24
]
.

With the theory of graphical functions
[
3
,
26
,
33
]
, it was possible to extend the data in
[
5
,
24
]
to hundreds of graphs up to loop order
eleven (and beyond
[
11
]
). In six-dimensional
\phi^{3}
theory, results exist up to loop order nine. With this data, a connection to motivic Galois theory became visible
[
22
]

which led to further investigations of the motivic structure of QFTs
[
8
,
9
]
(the ‘cosmic’ Galois group) and of the geometries that underlie the number content
of
\phi^{4}
periods
[
10
,
25
,
28
]
.

It is convenient to use conformal symmetry and complete the primitive vertex graph
G
by joining the four external legs to an extra vertex ‘
\infty
’.
The completion
\overline{G}
of
G
is a four-regular graph (every vertex has four edges) that serves as an equivalence class of decompleted graphs which all have the same
scalar integral
[
24
]
. Completion is the conformal analogon to the (projective) cut and glue identities for
p
-integrals that allows one to close an edge and open a
different edge.

We define

(45)

P_{\overline{G}}\equiv P_{\overline{G}\backslash\{v\}}

for any vertex
v
in
\overline{G}
.
Completion significantly reduces the number of graphs. A list of all completed primitive graph up to eight (decompleted) loops is in
[
24
]
.
Graphs up to eleven loops together with all known results are contained in the Maple package
[
33
]
.

Different completed graphs can have equal integrals. Some identities of such type are known: the twist
[
3
,
26
]
, the Fourier identity
[
5
,
26
]
,
the Fourier-split
[
14
]
, and the five-twist
[
32
]
.
However, even after many years, not all
\phi^{4}
identities are known.

A practical solution comes from the combinatorial Hepp bound that is conjectured to be a graph invariant which detects equal scalar integrals
[
20
]
.
More recently, the Martin sequence emerged as a tool that can unify all combinatorial invariants and that can also be used to determine the scalar integral
[
21
,
23
]
.

These recent developments support the picture that there exist many more identities for
\phi^{4}
periods than those that can be explained by known transformations.
The first examples are at loop order eight, where it is conjectured that

(46)

P_{8,30}=P_{8,36}\qquad\text{and}\qquad P_{8,31}=P_{8,35}

in the notation of
[
24
]
.
Note that the graphs of the periods
P_{8,35}
and
P_{8,36}
do not transform under known identities.

Like every identity for graphical functions, Equations (
43
) give identities for the scalar integral
P_{G}
in the case that
G
has a three-vertex split.
This follows from the property of the scalar integral that one can choose three vertices
0,1,z
in
G
and calculate
P_{G}
from the graphical function of
G
with external vertices

0,1,z
by integrating
z
over the complex plane
[
26
]
.

The smallest example of (
43
) in
\phi^{4}

While the identity
P_{7,1}=P^{\mathrm{non\,}\phi^{4}}_{7,31}
can also be obtained with the twist at differenct vertices, the identity
P_{7,1}=P^{\mathrm{non\,}\phi^{4}}_{7,18}

is new. Note that, like every other identity on scalar integrals, the new identity does not change the loop order and hence generates a finite group action on all graphs with
convergent integral at a given loop order.

In general, the new identity is somewhat similar to the twist. Up to loop order eight, however, it is even a bit more powerful: the new identity generates 18 relations
that are not twists while only four twists are not also obtained with the new identity.

Further restricting to transformations that map
\phi^{4}
graphs to
\phi^{4}
graphs, we (e.g.) get the following identities at eleven loops that are not twists

(50)

P_{11,2857}=P_{11,2868},\quad P_{11,2860}=P_{11,2863},\quad P_{11,3058}=P_{11,3061}.

All three identities, however, follow from twists via the intermediate graphs
P_{11,2863},P_{11,2857},P_{11,3057}
, respectively. This means that
twists of
P_{11,2863}
(e.g.) give
P_{11,2863}=P_{11,2857}
and
P_{11,2863}=P_{11,2868}
implying the left identity in (
50
). We also conclude that
P_{11,2857}=P_{11,2860}
.

Altogether, the application of the new identity inside
\phi^{4}
theory gives no new identities.
However, the new identity is more prone to provide identities between
\phi^{4}
graphs and graphs outside
\phi^{4}
theory, so that it should be significantly
more powerful in chains of transformations on all (
\phi^{4}
and non
\phi^{4}
) scalar integrals (see
[
32
]
for a more detail discussion in a similar situation).

Regretfully, even with such chains, the new identity does not explain the conjectured identities (
46
).

The new identity can also be used (instead of the twist) in the setup of the five-twist
[
32
]
, potentially leading to another new identity.
Experiments, however, have not produced any new results in
\phi^{4}
theory.
