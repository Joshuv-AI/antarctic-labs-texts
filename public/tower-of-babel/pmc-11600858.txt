Effect of sodium hypochlorite and EDTA pretreatment on the resin infiltration efficacy and acid resistance of enamel white spot lesions: an in vitro study
Authors: Ke Ni, Lijuan Chen, Jingyu He, Yu Ding, Jian Meng, Qingfei Meng, Karl Lyons, May Lei Mei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis in vitro study aimed to evaluate the effect of sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) and ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) as pretreatments on the resin infiltration efficacy and acid resistance of enamel white spot lesions (WSLs).MethodsEnamel blocks prepared from the buccal surfaces of sound human premolars were placed in a demineralisation solution for 3 days to establish artificial enamel WSLs. All the blocks with WSLs were randomly divided into four groups (n = 40 per group): RI (Control): resin infiltration only. N/RI: 5.25% NaOCl pretreatment + resin infiltration; E/RI: 17% EDTA pretreatment + resin infiltration; NE/RI: combined pretreatment of 5.25% NaOCl with 17% EDTA + resin infiltration; Enamel pore exposure was measured by using SEM observation and Image J software in each group after pretreatment prior to resin infiltration (n = 8 per group). Resin penetration depth and enamel surface microhardness were assessed after resin infiltration (n = 8 per group for each test) by a confocal laser scanning microscope and a Vickers’ microhardness test respectively. The remaining specimens were subjected 7 days of pH cycling after which surface enamel roughness and microhardness were assessed by an atomic force microscope and a microhardness test respectively.ResultsCompared to the control group, the enamel surface pore exposure (p = 0.007), resin penetration depth (p = 0.011) and enamel surface microhardness (p < 0.001) were all significantly increased after the pretreatment of 5.25% NaOCl alone or combined with 17% EDTA by one-way ANOVA. After pH cycling, no significant differences were found in enamel surface roughness or microhardness among the four groups (p = 0.55) by two-way ANOVA.ConclusionsEnamel surface pretreatment of 5.25% NaOCl or combined with 17% EDTA improved the efficacy of the resin infiltration of WSLs, but might be useless in enhancing the acid resistance of resin-infiltrated WSLs.Keywords: White spot lesions, Resin infiltration, Pretreatment, NaOCl, EDTA

Introduction

Enamel white spot lesions (WSLs) are the initial stages of caries, caused by plaque accumulation on the local enamel surfaces, resulting in a non-cavitated lesion characterized by demineralisation on the enamel surface or subsurface [1]. It is seen in dental fluorosis and enamel hypoplasia commonly as well as in post-orthodontic white spots that can occur because of compromised oral hygiene maintenance due to the use of fixed appliances, especially in labial surfaces of the enamel [2]. The choice of treatment options depends on the extent of tooth staining or the size of the defect as well as to suit the patient’s aesthetic needs. These options include enamel remineralisation, microabrasion, resin infiltration, veneers, or full crowns [3]. The demineralised tooth tissue can be managed by remineralising agents like fluoride products, or calcium phosphate based bioactive products such as casein phosphopeptide-amorphous calcium phosphate (CPP-ACP) or bioactive glass [4]. However, the remineralisation process is lengthy and often requires long-term cooperation of the patients and may not lead to an aesthetic improvement of the appearance of the teeth [4]. Microabrasion is commonly used as a pretreatment in combination with remineralisation or resin infiltration therapy [5].

The resin infiltration technique is a micro-invasive treatment for WSLs, that involves introducing flowable resin into the demineralised enamel pores. Recently, resin infiltration has been suggested as the preferred treatment method for WSLs because of its low viscosity, high permeability (enhanced by the inclusion of bisphenol A-glycidyl methacrylate (BisGMA) and triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (TEGDMA) in the methacrylate matrix of the infiltrated resin), and aesthetic appearance (the refractive index of the infiltrant resin is 1.475, which is close to 1.62 of enamel) [6–9]. However, some studies have shown that the efficacy of the resin infiltration in WSLs might be affected by organic residue in the demineralised enamel pores, that originated from the cariogenic plaque or the smear layer and could not be removed by conventional cleaning or acid-etching methods [10, 11]. Moreover, that resin infiltrate contains TEGDMA that has a property of pronounced hydrophilicity and could lead to further demineralisation in un-infiltrated deep lesions of the enamel or cause microleakage at the interface between enamel and resin when exposed to acidic or basic conditions [12].

Recently, as a protein denaturant, 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) solution has been shown to be capable of cleaning protein residues from the enamel surface, facilitating the exposure of enamel crystals to acid etchant [13, 14]. Ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) is a potent chelating agent featuring four carboxyl groups, that can destabilize and break down the protein complex or other organic contents via chelation; furthermore, it selectively dissolves hydroxyapatite [15–17]. Because of its capability of dissolving the organic and inorganic contents on the smear layer, it has been suggested that an irrigation solution of 5.25% NaOCl or 17% EDTA could bean effective solution for root canal irrigation and/or dentine surface rinsing, which would enhance the penetration of the resin tags into the dentin tubules [18, 19]. However, there are few reported studies on the effect of NaOCl or EDTA as pretreatment on WSLs for resin infiltration [10].

Therefore, the aim of this in vitro study was to evaluate the effect of 5.25% NaOCl and 17% EDTA pretreatment on the resin infiltration efficacy and acid resistance of WSLs. The first null hypothesis was that the pretreatment of 5.25% NaOCl or 17% EDTA solution had no significant effect on resin infiltration efficacy of WSLs. The second null hypothesis was that the pretreatment of 5.25% NaOCl or 17% EDTA solution also had no significant effect on the acid resistance of resin infiltrated WSLs.

Materials and methods

Preparation of specimens with enamel white spot lesions

One hundred and sixty sound human premolars extracted for orthodontic purposes from patients aged 18–30 years residing in a consistent geographic area were selected and used in this study. Written informed consent was secured in accordance with the protocol approved by Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Central Hospital (No. XZXY-LK-20220805-064). After examination with a 10× binocular microscope to ensure the teeth were caries-free and had no cracks, the teeth were preserved in artificial saliva (0.400 g/L NaCl, 0.400 g/L KCl, 0.795 g CaCl2, 0.780 g/L NaH2PO4·2H2O, 0.005 g/L Na2S·2H2O, 1 g/L urea, pH = 7.0) for subsequent use [20].

The flowchart of the experiment is illustrated in Fig. 1. Enamel blocks, sized 4 mm×4 mm×2 mm, were cut from the central buccal surfaces of the premolars with a low-speed diamond saw (Accutom-50, Struers, Denmark). The buccal surface of the block was polished with water proof silicon carbide paper in a sequence from 600 to 2500 grits to make a flat enamel surface. All the surfaces of the enamel blocks received a coating of two layers of acid-resistant nail polish (Maybelline, USA) except the buccal surfaces. Subsequently, the enamel blocks were immersed in a demineralisation solution (13.415 g/L Ca(NO3)2, 16.166 g/L KH2PO4, 832.639 g/L CH3COOH, pH = 4.5) at 37 °C with the solution being refreshed every 24 h [9]. All the enamel blocks were removed from the demineralisation solution after 72 h and thoroughly rinsed with deionized water. After drying with oil-free air, distinct chalky white spots appeared on the buccal surfaces of the enamel blocks, indicating the establishment of enamel WSLs in vitro [9].

Experimental flowchart of the study: RI, the group with resin infiltration only; N/RI, the group with 5.25% NaOCl pretreatment and resin infiltration; E/RI, the group with 17% EDTA pretreatment and resin infiltration; NE/RI, the group with both the pretreatments of 5.25% NaOCl and 17% EDTA and resin infiltration; T0: the time point before resin infiltration; T1: the time point after resin infiltration; T2: the time point after resin infiltration and pH cycling test

The sample size was calculated using the G*Power software (latest ver. 3.1.9.7; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany; http://www.gpower.hhu.de) based on previous studies [9]. The formula was as follows: ANOVA: Fixed effects, omnibus, one-way was selected from F-tests family, with 5% significance and 80% power. A sample size of 8 teeth for each group was calculated as the minimum in the study.

Experimental pretreatments on enamel surfaces

All the enamel blocks with WSLs were randomly assigned into four equal groups (n = 40 each) using a table of random numbers. As control, no enamel surface pretreatment was used in group RI. The enamel surfaces of the blocks were pretreated with a 5.25% NaOCl solution (pH = 9.2, Wuhan Langli Biopharmaceutical Co., China) or a 17% EDTA solution (pH = 6.5, Fuzhou Hongcang Biotechnology Co., China) for 1 min in groups N/RI and E/RI respectively; the combined use of the pretreatment with5.25% NaOCl and 17% EDTA solutions were applied in group NE/RI. After pretreatments, all the enamel surfaces were rinsed with deionized water, dried with oil-free air and then etched with 15% hydrochloric acid (Icon-Etch, DMG, Germany) for 2 min, rinsed thoroughly with deionized water, and dried with anhydrous ethanol (Icon-Dry, DMG, Germany) for 30 s according to the manufacture’s instruction.

Resin infiltration efficacy evaluation

Enamel surface exposed pore measurement

Eight specimens were randomly chosen from each group to undergo gradient dehydration with different concentrations of ethanol solutions, drying, and gold sputtering (108 Auto, Cressington, UK) on the enamel surfaces. The morphological changes of the enamel surfaces after pretreatment among the four groups were observed by scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Maia3, Tescan, Czech Republic). Three SEM images were obtained of each specimen. SEM images from each group were imported into Image J software (Fiji, NIH, USA). After the colour gamut and threshold of the image adjusted, the area of the shadow region (enamel pore exposure area) was automatically identified and calculated by Image J software. The enamel surface pore exposure area (S1, in µm2) was calculated with the SEM scanning area (S2, in µm2) serving as a reference (Fig. 2). The enamel surface pore exposure ratio (ER) was determined by the formula: ER = S1/S2 × 100% [21, 22].

The calculation of the enamel surface pore exposure area in SEM images: S1, the area inside the red box; S2, the area inside the yellow box

Resin penetration depth

Eight specimens from each group were immersed in a 0.1% Rhodamine B ethanol solution (Sigma, USA) for 12 h to ensure that the demineralised lesions of the enamel were completely penetrated and stained with the Rhodamine B dye. Specimens were then rinsed thoroughly with deionized water and dried with anhydrous ethanol (Icon-Dry, DMG, Germany) for 30 s. Following this, the enamel surfaces of all specimens had flowable resin (Icon-Infiltrant, DMG, Germany) applied for 3 min and light-cured for 40 s at 600 mW/cm2 (Variable Intensity Polymerizer Junior, BISCO, USA) with 2-mm distance from the surface according to the manufacture’s instruction. Each specimen was then cut perpendicularly to the centre of the enamel surface with a low-speed diamond saw to prepare an enamel slice with 1 mm thickness. Specimens were then polished with 1200 to 4000 grit sandpaper until translucency was achieved, and then all enamel slices were immersed in a 30% hydrogen peroxide solution for 12 h to bleach the Rhodamine B dye that not covered by the flowable resin. Finally, the processed enamel slices were stained in a 50% ethanol solution (Sigma, USA) containing 100 µmol/L sodium fluorescein (NaFl) for 3 min to mark the demineralised lesions that had not been infiltrated by the flowable resin. After rinsing with deionized water for 10 s and drying with oil-free air, all enamel slices were observed by the confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) (TCS-SP8, Leica, Germany) in dual-fluorescence mode to assess the resin infiltration efficacy [23]. The Rhodamine B dye was excited at the wavelength of 561 nm and detected at 572–682 nm; the NaFl dye was excited at the length of 488 nm and detected at 498–551 nm [24].

As shown in Fig. 3, resin-infiltrated lesions were depicted in red in the CLSM images, whereas un-infiltrated areas are shown in green. The demineralised lesion depths (LD) and the penetration depths (PD) were measured at three random points in each slice of the CLSM image using Image J software and the average values of LD and PD for each specimen were calculated and recorded for statistical analysis. The total demineralised lesion area (LA) and penetration area (PA) for each specimen were also measured, and the percentage of penetration area (PA%) was calculated as PA/LA×100% [9].

The measurement of the diagram in CLSM images: LD, lesion depth; PD, penetration depth; LA, lesion area (Red&Green area); PA, penetration area (Red area)

Surface microhardness (SMH)

Eight specimens from each group were randomly selected for microhardness testing. Half of the buccal surface of each specimen was coated with two layers of acid-resistant nail polish as the internal control side, while the exposed half had flowable resin (Icon-Infiltrant, DMG, Germany) applied for 3 min and light-cured for 40 s as the experimental side. After resin infiltration treatment, the nail polish on the internal control surface of each specimen was removed using acetone and all the specimens were subjected to a Vickers microhardness test (KB-30s, KB, Germany) where a 200 g force was applied to the enamel surface for 15 s to measure the surface microhardness (SMH) values. The results observed from the control side of the specimen were identified as T0 (before resin infiltration) and those from the other exposed side were identified as T1 (after resin infiltration), as shown in Fig. 1. Five indentations, separated by at least 100 μm, were made on each side of the specimen and the mean of the five values was recorded as the SMH value for the side of the specimen.

Acid resistance evaluation for WSLs

pH cycling

The remaining 16 specimens in each group underwent pH cycling. Firstly, the buccal enamel surface of the specimen had the resin infiltration treatment applied. Then half of the buccal surface was coated with two layers of acid-resistant nail polish as control, leaving the exposed half as the experimental side. All specimens were then subjected to a pH cycling test for 7 days.The specimens were immersed in a demineralisation solution (2.0 mM Ca(NO3)2∙4H2O, 2.0 mM NaH2PO4∙2H2O, 0.075 mM acetate buffer, 0.02 ppm F, pH = 4.7) for 6 h and then a remineralisation solution (1.5 mM Ca(NO3)2∙4H2O, 0.9 mM NaH2PO4∙2H2O, 150 mM KCl, 0.1 mol/L Tris buffer, 0.03 ppm F, pH = 7.0) for 18 h in the first 5 days; the specimens were stored only in the remineralisation solution in the last 2 days [25]. After the pH cycling test, the nail polish was removed by acetone from the buccal surface of the control side of the specimen for subsequent analyses. The observed results from the control side were identified as T1 (after resin infiltration without pH cycling test), and those from the exposed sides were identified as T2 (after pH cycling test).

Enamel surface roughness (Ra) measurement

Eight specimens from each group were randomly selected for enamel surface roughness measurement by an atomic force microscope (AFM, Bruker, Germany). Three points in each side of the enamel surface (T1 and T2 respectively) were randomly chosen for morphological observation, with a scanning range of 2 μm×2 μm. The surface morphology images were analysed and the roughness values (Ra) were calculated by a measurement software (NanoScope Analysis, v1.40, Bruker, Germany). The average of the three values was taken to yield one roughness value for each side of the specimen.

Surface microhardness (SMH) measurement

The remaining eight specimens from each group had their enamel surface microhardness measured before and after the pH cycling test (T1 and T2 respectively) by a Vickers hardness tester (KB-30s, KB, Germany). The measurement method was the same as that in Sect. 2.3.3.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 25.0 software for Windows was used for statistical analysis. The data was presented as a mean ± standard deviation for each group. Normality was analyzed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, where p > 0.05 indicated a normal distribution. Differences in ER, PD and PA were analyzed using one-way ANOVA and post-hoc Tukey Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test, and two-way ANOVA and Tukey HSD test were used to detect the interactions of Ra and SMH values between groups. The significant level for statistical analyses was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Resin infiltration efficacy analysis

As shown in Fig. 4, the enamel surface was mostly covered with irregular minerals and granular substances, exposing only a few enamel pores in group RI; in contrast, the enamel pores were exposed more clearly after the pretreatment with NaOCl or EDTA solution on the enamel surfaces in the other three groups. The exposure areas (EA) and exposure ratios (ER) for each group are shown in Fig. 5 and analyzed in a normal distribution using normality test respectively (p > 0.05). One-way ANOVA indicated that the pretreatment could significantly affect the pore exposures on the enamel surfaces (p = 0.007). HSD-test analysis revealed that pretreatment with 5.25% NaOCl alone, and in combination with 17% EDTA, significantly increased the EA and ER of the enamel surfaces compared to group RI with no pretreatment (p < 0.05). But no significant difference was found between groups E/RI and RI (p > 0.05).

SEM observation of the enamel surfaces after pretreatment in four groups (×2000)

Analyses of pore exposure area (A) and pore exposure ratio (B) for four groups. The asterisk (*) indicates a significant difference between groups (*, p < 0.05)

The CLSM images of the specimens, along with LD, PD, PD/LD, and PA/LA values, were presented in Figs. 6 and 7. The penetration area is shown in red, with the un-penetrated area in green under the dual fluorescence modes of CLSM observation. The data of LD, PD, PD/LD, and PA/LA values were analyzed in a normal distribution respectively (p > 0.05), and one-way ANOVA analysis revealed no significant differences in LD values (p = 0.73), but significant differences in PD (p = 0.02), PD/LD (p = 0.01), and PA/LA values (p = 0.03) among the groups. HSD-test results demonstrated that the enamel surfaces pretreated with 5.25% NaOCl or in combination with 17% EDTA in groups N/RI and NE/RI respectively exhibited significantly higher values of PD, PD/LD, and PA/LA compared to that in group RI (p < 0.05). No statistical difference was found, however in resin infiltration efficacy between groups RI and E/RI (p > 0.05).

Analysis of resin infiltration efficacy: (A), LD and PD values for each group; (B), PD/LD values for each group; (C), PA/LA values for each group. The asterisk (*) indicates a significant difference between groups (*, p < 0.05)

The SMH values before and after resin infiltration for the four groups are shown in Fig. 8, the data of which were analyzed in a normal distribution (p > 0.05). Two-way ANOVA indicated that both pretreatment (p = 0.01) and resin infiltration (p = 0.00) could significantly affect the SMH of the specimens, with a significant interaction between them (p = 0.00). HSD-test analysis showed that: (1) Compared to groups RI and N/RI, the pretreatment of 17% EDTA or the combination of 5.25% NaOCl and 17% EDTA on the enamel surface in groups E/RI or NE/RI, significantly reduced the SMH values of the specimens before resin infiltration treatment (T0, p < 0.05); (2) All the SMH values of the specimens had a significant increase after resin infiltration (T1, p < 0.001), especially in groups N/RI and NE/RI; (3) The lowest increase in SMH values of the specimens was in the control group RI, followed by groups N/RI and E/RI, with the highest increase being in group NE/RI (T1-T0).

Acid resistance analysis after pH cycling test

The morphology of the enamel surface and the Ra values of the specimens are shown in Figs. 9 and 10. All the Ra values were analyzed in a normal distribution (p > 0.05), and the two-way ANOVA showed that the Ra values of the specimens were significantly affected by the factors of both the pretreatment (p = 0.02) and pH cycling (p = 0.00). HSD-test analysis indicated that: (1) There was no difference in the Ra values of the enamel surfaces among groups before pH cycling (T1, p > 0.05), but there was a significant increase in all four groups after pH cycling (T2, p < 0.001); (2) The smallest increase in Ra values was in group N/RI, which was significantly lower than that in control group RI (T2-T1, p < 0.01).

Discussion

According to the results of this study, the first null hypothesis that the pretreatment of 5.25% NaOCl or 17% EDTA solution had no significant effect on resin infiltration efficacy of WSLs, was rejected; the second null hypothesis that the pretreatment had no significant effect on the acid resistance of WSLs, could not be rejected.

During the enamel surface pretreatment of WSLs for resin infiltration, 15% hydrochloric acid (HCL) demonstrated a better effect than that of 37% phosphoric acid (H3PO4) in etching away the overly mineralised layer in the enamel surface. However, the pretreatment of micro-abrasion with diamond abrasive strips and 37% H3PO4 on the enamel surface could achieve the same efficacy of resin infiltration as that of 15% HCL [26]. Furthermore, the pretreatment of both 37% H3PO4 and 15% HCL combined with ultrasonic-assisted infiltration on the enamel surface could significantly increase resin penetration depth in WSLs [27]. However, it was noted that 15% HCL and 37% H3PO4 can only etch away the inorganic substances and minerals, but only had a limited effect on removing the organic impurities found in the enamel surface [10].

The dentinal irrigants of 5.25% NaOCl and 17% EDTA had been extensively used for removing the smear layer and organic impurities in the dentin tubules by denaturing proteins or chelating to dissolve organics in the rinsing process of root canal cavities [13, 14, 17, 28]. In this study, SEM observation showed that only 15.49% of the enamel pores were exposed in the control group, but the exposing area and ratios of the enamel pores were both significantly increased after the pretreatment of 5.25% NaOCl or in combination with 17% EDTA (Figs. 4 and 5). Although no significant difference was found between groups RI and E/RI, pretreatment with 17% EDTA still increased the exposing ratio of the enamel surface pores. The reason for this could be that the four carboxyl groups of EDTA facilitated metal ion chelation in the tooth matrix, selectively dissolving hydroxyapatite and removing the smear layer, achieving effects akin to that of NaOCl [18].

Increased exposure of enamel surface pores would be conducive to the following process of resin infiltration. The indirect fluorescence technique employed in this study using CLSM observation in the dual fluorescence modes could visually reflect the area of demineralised lesions and the effect of resin penetration. The results revealed that pretreatment with either 5.25% NaOCl alone or the combination with 17% EDTA led to a significant increase in both penetration depth and penetration area values in groups N/RI and NE/RI. However, 17% EDTA pretreatment did not show a significant improvement in resin penetration efficacy compared to the control group, potentially due to its distinct mechanism in removing organic matter and its neutral pH value (pH = 6.5) [29, 30]. Nonetheless, the combination of NaOCl and EDTA enhanced resin infiltration efficacy slightly more than NaOCl alone. It is plausible that NaOCl removed organic substances, while EDTA dissolved a small amount of inorganic substance, creating a synergistic effect. Furthermore, penetration depth is also correlated with the duration of the resin infiltration; longer duration of exposure results in deeper penetration. However, as a 3-minute infiltration in natural lesions could acquire the effectiveness of penetration depth similar to that of 5-minute infiltration [31], a 3-minute infiltration time was selected in this study.

In the current research, it has shown that resin infiltration treatment resulted in a significant increase in the surface microhardness of the enamel in all groups. An explanation for this may be that the flowable resin infiltrated into the enamel pores of the lesions, leading to the formation of an enamel prism-resin complex, which markedly enhanced the surface microhardness of the demineralised enamel [7, 32, 33]. Furthermore, the surface microhardness values were significantly higher by pretreatment of 5.25% NaOCl or 17% EDTA enhanced resin infiltration more than the control group, which further confirmed the role of enamel surface pretreatment in improving the efficacy of resin infiltration of WSLs. However, it was revealed that the SMH values of the demineralised enamel decreased when 17% EDTA alone was used or combined with 5.25% NaOCl compared to the other two groups. This may be related to the mechanism of EDTA on the tooth tissue surface. As a potent chelating agent, EDTA facilitates the chelation of metal ions to the tooth matrix, causing selective hydroxyapatite dissolution, which might have resulted in a decrease in the microhardness of the tooth surface to some extent [34–36].

In this investigation, the significant increase in enamel surface roughness was found not only in the control group RI, but also in the test group with the pretreatment of 5.25% NaOCl or 17% EDTA after pH cycling test. This increase could be due to the polymerization shrinkage from the triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (TEGDMA) monomer in the resin matrix [37], and its hydrophilic degradation in aqueous environments [38]. In order to reduce the shrinkage, an allylic monomer of bisphenol allylic derivate (BPhADAC) was added to the resin matrix and had been shown to be a potential alternative to TEGDMA in the formulation of low-shrinking resin [39], but its efficacy in enhancing acid resistance of the tooth tissue remains inconclusive. Our previous study revealed that the remineralisation treatment of casein phosphopeptide-amorphous calcium phosphate with fluoride (CPP-ACPF) or bioactive glass could provide uniform mineral deposits in the resin-infiltrated enamel surface and reduce the microleakage in the interface between the enamel rods and the resin, which partially resisted the oral acid-base environment, and enhanced its acid-resistant ability [9]. Interestingly, the surface roughness in group N/RI was the lowest among all groups after pH cycling (T2) and significantly lower than that in group RI, which indicated that the pretreatment of 5.25% NaOCl in the enamel surface might be useful in improving the acid resistance of the specimens in some extent. In addition, this research found no significant decrease in enamel surface microhardness in all groups following pH cycling. This outcome may stem from the distinct pH cycling model applied or the limited duration of simulation days [40–42].

Inevitably, several limitations are included in this study. Firstly, the variability among ex vivo teeth, the creation of demineralised teeth models, and the simulation of the oral micro-environment in this study highlight the differences between the experiment and the real clinical settings. Secondly, in contrast to the dynamic and complex in vivo ecological environment, this study focused solely on the changes in surface roughness and microhardness of the enamel in evaluating the acid resistance, neglecting other critical factors, such as temperature, abrasion, and dental plaque [43, 44]. Therefore, caution should be exercised in interpreting the results. In addition, It has been found that the free radicals generated by NaOCl during dentin rinsing may inhibit the polymerisation of the resin composite, which in turn leads to a reduction in the bonding strength between the dentin and the resin composite [45, 46], and that the pretreatment of NaOCl or NaOCl and Icon on the surface of the enamel affected with molar incisor hypomineralisation also did not enhance the enamel bonding strength [47], but few studies were focused on the effect of NaOCl or EDTA pretreatment on the bonding strength of the WSLs and the resin composite. More researches are needed to assess the impact of various pretreatment agents on the efficacy and stability of resin-infiltrated WSLs, also included with its binding ability to resin composite.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, it is suggested that the pretreatment of enamel surfaces with WSLs with 5.25% NaOCl or combined with 17% EDTA before resin infiltration could significantly enhance the enamel pore exposure, resin penetration depth, and surface microhardness, thus improving the resin infiltration efficacy of WSLs. However, the pretreatment procedure in this study showed no effect on enhancing the acid resistance of resin-infiltrated WSLs.
