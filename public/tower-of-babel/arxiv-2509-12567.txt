A causal random synthetic turbulent velocity field: I. The underlying Gaussian structure
Matthieu Chatelain 1, Júlia Domingues Lemos 1, Wandrille Ruffenach 1, Mickaël Bourgoin 1, Charles-Edouard Bréhier 2, Laurent Chevillard 1,3, Ilias Sibgatullin 1, Romain Volk 1

Abstract
We develop, simulate and extend an initial proposition by Chaves et al. 2003 concerning a random incompressible vector field able to reproduce key ingredients of three-dimensional turbulence in both space and time. In this first article, we focus on the important underlying Gaussian structure that will be generalized in a second article to account for higher-order statistics. Presently, the statistical spatial structure of this velocity field is consistent with a divergence-free fractional Gaussian vector field that encodes all known properties of homogeneous and isotropic fluid turbulence at a given finite Reynolds number, up to second-order statistics. This includes the velocity power spectral density (PSD), with a given regularization at low wave-vectors allowing to introduce the integral length scale in the picture, and an exponential-like dissipative behavior taking place around a viscous-dependent appropriate length scale. The temporal structure of the velocity field is introduced through a stochastic evolution of the respective Fourier modes. In the simplest picture, Fourier modes evolve according to an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, where the characteristic time scale depends on the wave-vector amplitude. For consistency with direct numerical simulations (DNSs) of the Navier-Stokes equations, this time scale is inversely proportional to the wave vector amplitude. As a consequence, the characteristic velocity that governs the eddies is independent of their size and is related to the velocity standard deviation, which is consistent with some features of the so-called sweeping effect . To ensure differentiability in time while respecting the causal nature of the evolution, we use the methodology developed by Viggiano et al. 2020 to propose a fully consistent stochastic picture, predicting in particular proper temporal covariance of the Fourier modes. We finally derive analytically all statistical quantities in a continuous setup and develop precise and efficient numerical schemes of the corresponding periodic framework. Both exact predictions and numerical estimations of the model are compared to DNSs provided by the Johns Hopkins database.



keywords

Homogeneous turbulence, Isotropic turbulence, stochastic models for turbulence.

Introduction

Fully developed fluid turbulence is emblematic of fluid mechanics and out-of-equilibrium statistical physics. It is usually observed in various natural flows and studied, in simpler situations than those of geophysical flows, in laboratory experiments and numerical simulations of the forced Navier-Stokes equations. An ancient tradition of measurements and observations has allowed the development of a very precise and self-consistent phenomenology capable of describing its statistical structure with few free parameters
(
Monin & Yaglom 1971
;
Tennekes & Lumley 1972
;
Frisch 1995
;
Pope 2000
)
. From the theoretical side, the velocity of a turbulent fluid can be seen as the vanishing viscosity limit of the statistically stationary, isotropic, and homogeneous solution of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations sustained by a random
(
Eswaran & Pope 1988
)
, possibly deterministic
(
Vincent & Meneguzzi 1991
)
, force. Because of the interplay between non-linear and non-local aspects of this dynamics, with non-trivial influence of viscosity, the rigorous study of these solutions remains a fantastic mathematical challenge. The main purpose of this article is the presentation, extension, and simulation of a spatio-temporal turbulent velocity field that evolves according to a Markovian dynamics in the simplest situation, to a causal one in a more sophisticated version, able to reproduce several key statistical signatures. This stochastic model encompasses the spatial multi-scale nature of the observed turbulent velocity field, and a random evolution in time, which will be shown to obey a causal dynamics. In the infinite Reynolds number limit and when the analysis is restricted to second-order statistical laws, as captured in particular by a Gaussian framework, and assuming the model to evolve according to a Markovian dynamics, the present approach becomes equivalent to that proposed by
Chaves et al. 2003
. As far as we know, such models are unrelated to Navier-Stokes solutions, and we will therefore consider them as being synthetic, although they reflect important aspects of fluid turbulence.

Before presenting the proposition of
Chaves et al. 2003
, let us mention that synthetic turbulence has a long tradition in the literature, probably starting with fractional Brownian motions
(
Mandelbrot & Van Ness 1968
)
, known to have been initially proposed by
Kolmogorov 1940
to describe the expected irregular nature of the velocity field, and later formulated in terms of spatial fields by
Kraichnan 1968
. Such fields, called fractional Gaussian fields, are at the core of turbulent phenomenology and give a precise random picture of the spatial structure of a turbulent velocity field, including in particular the famous power-law behavior of the power spectral density “
k^{-5/3}
”, which is reminiscent of the asymptotic fractional regularity of the velocity field. Soon after proposing such random fields,
Kraichnan 1970
proposed a time-evolving version in which the time is included in the phase of the respective (spatial) Fourier modes. Such fields, referred to as
Kinematic Simulations
, have then been popularized in a series of articles
(
Fung et al. 1992
;
Fung & Vassilicos 1998
;
Malik & Vassilicos 1999
;
Castro & Paz 2013
)
mostly devoted to the study of the dispersion of particle pairs. At the cost of introducing several free parameters that have yet to be physically interpreted,
Favier et al. 2010
were able to reproduce in a realistic fashion several important aspects of the time evolution observed in direct numerical simulations (DNSs) of the Navier-Stokes equations while introducing a fluctuating nature in the dispersion relation relating wave numbers and frequencies. However, kinematic simulations have been acknowledged to be limited for certain aspects of particle dispersion
(
Thomson & Devenish 2005
;
Eyink & Benveniste 2013
)
, and, moreover, a precise dynamical formulation of this approach is still elusive.

The purpose of the present article is to propose a random incompressible field that evolves according to a causal dynamics consistent with the temporal structure of turbulence, which has been repeatedly observed, mostly in DNSs
(
Kaneda et al. 1999
;
Chevillard et al. 2005
;
Favier et al. 2010
;
Canet et al. 2017
;
Gorbunova et al. 2021
)
. It is more difficult to observe this phenomenon experimental measurements because it requires the absence of a mean flow, which is present in wind tunnels and jets. However,
Gorce & Falcon 2022
have conducted measurements of the temporal evolution of a flow stirred by magnetic particles able to generate turbulence without a mean flow, and temporal statistics were found to be similar to those observed in aforementioned numerical simulations. The temporal spectrum, defined as the variance of the temporal Fourier modes at a given frequency
\omega
, behaves in a universal fashion as a power-law “
\omega^{-5/3}
”, with thus a similar exponent as observed on the spatial spectrum. Although understood and predicted by
Tennekes 1975
, this power-law decay can be seen as counterintuitive and differs drastically from similarity arguments which would predict a much faster decrease, i.e. with a
-2
power-law exponent, as has been observed in the Lagrangian framework, i.e. following fluid particles along their trajectory
(
Yeung 2002
;
Toschi & Bodenschatz 2009
;
Pinton & Sawford 2012
)
. Temporal regularity of the velocity field is imposed by this power-law, which is thus the same as the spatial one, and turns out to have important consequences. In fact, the characteristic velocity of eddies of a given size is independent of that scale and turns out to be the one at large scale: this is the so-called
sweeping effect
. From a statistical viewpoint, both this power-law decay of the temporal PSD and the scale-independent characteristic velocity of eddies can be included in the model by setting a decorrelation time scale
T_{\mathbf{k}}
of Fourier modes proportional to the inverse wave-vector amplitude and whose multiplicative constant is inversely proportional to the velocity standard deviation allowing a consistent physical picture and proper dimension. This time scale dependent on
k
is at the core of the random picture of
Chaves et al. 2003
.

The proposed random velocity field of
Chaves et al. 2003
, see also earlier propositions of
Komorowski & Papanicolaou 1997
and
Fannjiang & Komorowski 2000
, encodes all the aforementioned spatial and temporal statistical structures. The associated temporal kernel has a pure exponential shape, and its characteristic correlation time scale is
T_{\mathbf{k}}
. This is reminiscent of a dynamics for Fourier modes similar to the one of a Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, as early recognized by
Komorowski & Peszat 2004
and
Eyink & Benveniste 2013
. We intend to model the turbulent velocity field, up to second-order statistics, in the asymptotic regime of infinite Reynolds numbers. In a Gaussian framework, a given spatio-temporal covariance function of velocity components can be achieved by a stochastic process stirred by Gaussian space-time white noise. The very purpose of the present article is the formulation and simulation of such an incompressible random velocity field, where viscous corrections will be furthermore included in order to be compared to experimental measurements and DNSs of the Navier-Stokes equations. To do so, we not only need to impose viscous corrections to the spatial formulation, but also come up with a proper temporal dynamics that would ensure a consistent evolution of Fourier modes, giving rise to a smooth behavior at any wave vector, while respecting the causal nature of the underlying time marching. This will be achieved using the multi-layered evolution proposed by
Viggiano et al. 2020
. Also, each step of the formulation will be compared to DNSs data, in both space and time. The numerical data provided by the Johns Hopkins Turbulence Database
(
Li et al. 2008
)
allow such a comparison. Preliminary analysis of this DNS database has been performed by
Reneuve 2019
, and we will reproduce them here for convenience and completeness.

As already mentioned, the purpose of the present article is the formulation and simulation of a Gaussian stochastic representation of the spatio-temporal structure of turbulence, which corresponds to the reproduction of the second-order statistical signatures that already encompass several key properties. Nonetheless, it is well known that the turbulent velocity field is non-Gaussian, as can be seen by the behavior through scales of higher-order statistical quantities, such as third-order moment of velocity increments that quantifies the asymmetry of Probability density Functions (PDFs) and energy transfers, and the fourth-order one which focuses on the heavy-tail nature of these PDFs
(
Frisch 1995
)
. These non-Gaussian features of the velocity field are referred to as the
intermittency phenomenon
which has a strong connection to multifractality. Several spatial random fields have been already proposed in the literature that are able to reproduce many aspects of this phenomenon, in particular the distributional nature of the dissipation field
(
Yaglom 1962
;
Yaglom 1966
;
Mandelbrot 1972
;
Mandelbrot 1974
;
Kahane & Peyrière 1976
;
Kahane 1985
;
Meneveau & Sreenivasan 1987
;
Meneveau & Sreenivasan 1991
;
Rhodes & Vargas 2014
)
, and their extension to the velocity field
(
Juneja et al. 1994
;
Biferale et al. 1998
;
Arneodo et al. 1998
;
Bacry et al. 2001
;
Robert & Vargas 2008
;
Chevillard et al. 2010
;
Bacry et al. 2012
;
Pereira et al. 2016
;
Granero-Belinchón et al. 2018
;
Chevillard et al. 2019
;
Muzy 2019
;
Chevillard et al. 2020
)
, with generalizations towards structures and turbulent magnetic fields
(
Rosales & Meneveau 2006
;
Rosales & Meneveau 2008
;
Durrive et al. 2020
;
Durrive et al. 2022
;
Lübke et al. 2024
)
. Let us mention that these aforementioned models are usually time-independent and they are meant to give a stochastic representation of statistically stationary solution of the Navier-Stokes equations. Recent works provide some generalization of these random fields with a given time evolution
(
Reneuve & Chevillard 2020
;
Peixoto Considera & Thalabard 2023
)
. We keep the generalization of the present spatio-temporal random field for future works, which will be presented in a second article (in preparation).

This article is organized as follows. In Chapter
2
, we recall several important ingredients of the phenomenology of fluid turbulence based on second-order statistical quantities. Focusing on the spatial structure, we present in Section
2.1
basic statistical properties such as the viscous independence of velocity variance and dissipation per unit of mass, the wave-vector dependence of the power spectral density and the respective scale dependence of the spatial structure functions. This allows us to define with precision several key parameters of the formulation, such as the mean Hölder exponent
H=1/3
, the Kolmogorov universal constant
C_{2}
and the dissipative length scale
\eta_{K}
. These predictions are reformulated for a Gaussian model and compared with a DNS in Section
2.2
. We then discuss in Section
2.3
the behavior of characteristic time scales. In particular, we explore the implication of the sweeping effect, which has for statistical consequence that the characteristic turnover time scale of
eddies
of a given size is independent of the scale and coincides with the large integral time scale. Following these observations, we deduce the implied statistical structure of the velocity field based on the second-order temporal structure function and the associated temporal PSD in Section
2.4
. We then present our spatio-temporal model in Chapter
3
. In Section
3.1
, we begin by reformulating former considerations with periodic boundary conditions, allowing to unambiguously consider Fourier modes since they are well defined in this context (unlike the former continuous formulation in full space). We formulate the model in full generality, for any Hölder exponent
0<H<1
and for abitrary dependence of the characteristic time
T_{\mathbf{k}}
on the wave-vector amplitude
|\mathbf{k}|
. In order to achieve the statistical temporal structure for the description of turbulence, we propose in Section
3.2
a causal stochastic evolution capable of generating such a field starting from any initial conditions. To do so, we first need to define the Fourier transform of a white noise, a formulation that we provide in subsection
3.2.1
. This eventually allows us to define in subsection
3.2.2
a Gaussian random field that mimics the spatial structure depicted in Sections
2.1
and
3.1
, which can be seen as a fractional Gaussian vector field. In subsection
3.2.3
, we recall the associated stochastic dynamics of
Chaves et al. 2003
, and generalize it to a smooth and causal context, even toward an infinitely differentiable setup, in subsection
3.2.4
. An effective numerical schemes is proposed in Chapter
4
. We gather conclusions and perspectives in Chapter
5
. Throughout the presentation of the model, we provide a precise comparison between the theoretical predictions of the model with both its numerical representations and the DNS data extracted from the Johns Hopkins turbulence database
(
Li et al. 2008
)
. These comparisons concern both the spatial and temporal structure of velocity fields. We also provide key technical developments in the appendices.

2
The spatio-temporal statistical structure of a turbulent velocity field, and its continuous formulation on the whole space

2.1
Basic ingredients of the phenomenology of fluid turbulence and the implied statistical spatial structure

Let us consider the velocity vector field
\mathbf{u}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
t\geq 0
, which reads component-wise
\mathbf{u}^{\nu}=(u_{i}^{\nu})_{1\leq i\leq 3}
, a solution of the Navier-Stokes equations, for incompressible fluids of unit density,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}^{\nu}}{\partial t}+(\mathbf{u}^{\nu}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}^{\nu}=-\nabla p^{\nu}+\nu\Delta\mathbf{u}^{\nu}+\mathbf{f},

(1)

where
\nu
is the kinematic viscosity,
p^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{x})
the pressure field obtained by the Poisson equation using the incompressibility constraint
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\nu}=0
, and
\mathbf{f}(t,\mathbf{x})
a forcing vector assumed to be divergence-free, smooth in space, correlated on a characteristic length scale
L
of the order of the integral length scale and independent of viscosity. We will denote the velocity gradient
\partial_{j}u_{i}^{\nu}
of the component
u_{i}^{\nu}
along
x_{j}
.

For quite a general form of the forcing field
\bf{f}
, observations suggest that the velocity field
\mathbf{u}^{\nu}
reaches a statistically homogeneous and stationary regime, in which the velocity variance and average dissipation depend very weakly on viscosity, such that

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}=\lim_{\nu\to 0}\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathbf{u}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{x})|^{2}\right],

(2)

and

\displaystyle\varepsilon=\lim_{\nu\to 0}\lim_{t\to\infty}\nu\sum_{ij}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\partial_{j}u^{\nu}_{i}\right)^{2}\right],

(3)

are positive numbers. Starting from the randomly forced Navier-Stokes equations (
1
), the expectations in (
2
) and (
3
) are taken over the the forcing realisations. From a practical point of view, we will assume that expectations can be accurately replaced by empirical averages over space and/or time, which is in particular guaranteed by the statistical homogeneity and stationarity observed in DNSs and experiments. In the asymptotic limit of infinite Reynolds numbers, i.e. in the vanishing viscosity limit
\nu\to 0
, the finite quantities
\sigma
(
2
) and
\varepsilon
(
3
) are expected to be related according to the dimensional prediction

\displaystyle\varepsilon\propto\frac{\sigma^{3}}{L}.

(4)

The independence of the velocity variance on viscosity (
2
) is far from being obvious and is at the heart of the phenomenology of fluid turbulence. Recall that energy is injected into the fluid in a statistically stationary way through the forcing term
\bf{f}
(
1
). In order to maintain a velocity variance that becomes independent of viscosity (
2
) as
\nu\to 0
, the fluid develops
small scales
by populating Fourier modes located at higher wave numbers than those excited by the forcing.

To characterize this behavior, usually referred to as the
cascade
phenomenon, the power spectral density (PSD)
E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(\mathbf{k})
has been measured and estimated in many studies. This quantity is defined to be the Fourier transform of the trace of the covariance tensor of the velocity vector field, that is - using Einstein’s summation convention over repeated indices -

\displaystyle E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(\mathbf{k})=\int_{\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{-2i\pi\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\mathbb{E}\left[u^{\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{x})u^{\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{r})\right]{\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{r}.

(5)

Note that the correlation
\mathbb{E}\left[u_{i}(t,\mathbf{x})u_{i}(t,\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{r})\right]
is independent of both position
\mathbf{x}
and of time
t
due to statistical homogeneity and stationarity respectively. Furthermore, by virtue of statistical isotropy,
E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(\mathbf{k})=E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(|\mathbf{k}|)
, i.e. the PSD is a function of the norm
|\mathbf{k}|
only, and is independent of the direction of
\mathbf{k}
. Note that the upper letter
{}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}
indicates that
E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(\mathbf{k})
is a genuinely spatial, i.e.
Eulerian
, quantity. As shown by
Pope 2000
, the PSD
E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(\mathbf{k})
defined in (
5
) fully determines the covariance function of the velocity field owing to the identity:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[u^{\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{x})u^{\nu}_{j}(t,\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{r})\right]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{2i\pi\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(|\mathbf{k}|)\widehat{P}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})\ {\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{k},

(6)

where Leray’s projector on divergence-free vector fields
\widehat{P}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})
has been used and is defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{P}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})=\delta_{ij}-\frac{k_{i}k_{j}}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}},

(7)

with
\delta_{ij}
the Kronecker delta symbol.

Although being accessible in Direct Numerical Simulations (DNSs) of the Navier-Stokes equations, the 3
d
power spectral density
E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(\mathbf{k})
(
5
) is difficult to obtain from experimental measurements. Instead, one-dimensional
longitudinal
power spectral density has traditionally been estimated. By virtue of statistical isotropy, this quantity is defined as the one-dimensional Fourier transform of the correlation function of one of the component of velocity, say
u_{1}
, along the direction
\hat{e}_{1}
, that is:

\displaystyle E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E,long}}}(k)=\int_{\ell\in\mathbb{R}}e^{-2i\pi k\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[u^{\nu}_{1}(t,\mathbf{x})u^{\nu}_{1}(t,\mathbf{x}+\ell\hat{e}_{1})\right]{\mathrm{d}}\ell.

(8)

Ìt can be checked that
E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E,long}}}(k)
is a positive and even function of
k
. The three-dimensional PSD
E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(\mathbf{k})
(
5
), which is a function of the norm
k=|\mathbf{k}|
of the wave vector
\mathbf{k}
, and the one-dimensional longitudinal PSD
E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E,long}}}(k)
(
8
) are related as follows:

\displaystyle E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(k)=\frac{k}{2\pi}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}k}\left(\frac{1}{k}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E,long}}}(k)}{{\mathrm{d}}k}\right).

(9)

The above identity is a consequence of incompressibility and statistical isotropy. A complete proof of the relation (
9
) is provided by
Pope 2000
.

Going back to the aforementioned phenomenological aspects of fluid turbulence, a manifestation of the underlying cascading process of energy can be read on the PSD, which will follow, in the limit of infinite Reynolds numbers, a power-law behavior at large wave numbers. This is known as the Kolmogorov “
k^{-5/3}
” spectrum
(
Frisch 1995
)
. At a given finite Reynolds number, a realistic parameterization of the longitudinal PSD
E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E,long}}}(k)
(
8
) would require a regularization at small wave numbers of order
1/L
, i.e. around the inverse integral length scale stemming from the forcing term, and a dissipative cut-off at large wave numbers
1/\eta_{K}
, where
\eta_{K}=O\left((\nu^{3}/\varepsilon)^{1/4}\right)
is known as Kolmogorov’s dissipative length scale. Sophisticated models that do so have been proposed in the literature
(
Pope 2000
;
Meyers & Meneveau 2008
)
. For the sake of simplicity, we will be working with the following simple but realistic parameterization:

\displaystyle E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E,long}}}(k)=D_{2}|k|_{1/L}^{-5/3}e^{-\eta_{d}k},

(10)

where we have introduced a regularized norm
|k|_{1/L}=\sqrt{k^{2}+1/L^{2}}
, a multiplicative constant
D_{2}>0
which is related to velocity variance (
2
) according to the identity

\displaystyle D_{2}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{3L^{2/3}}\frac{\Gamma(5/6)}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(1/3)},

(11)

and

\displaystyle\eta_{d}=2\pi\left(\frac{10C_{2}\Gamma(4/3)}{\Gamma(1/3)}\right)^{3/4}\left(\frac{\nu^{3}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/4},

(12)

which is of order of the Kolmogorov dissipative length scale
\eta_{K}
.
\Gamma\left(z\right)
denotes here the gamma function. The expression of the pre-factor in the definition of
\eta_{d}
(
12
) is derived below. The so-called Kolmogorov constant
C_{2}
has been estimated on experimental data and is close to
C_{2}\approx 2
, see
Pope 2000
. From the proposed expressionl for
E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E,long}}}(k)
(
10
), we deduce from the identity (
9
) the expression of the three-dimensional PSD
E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(k)
(
5
), which has a particularly simple expression in the vanishing viscosity limit, i.e.

\displaystyle E^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(k)=\lim_{\nu\to 0}E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(k)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{55}{9}D_{2}k^{2}|\mathbf{k}|_{1/L}^{-17/3}.

(13)

Note that the proportionality constant appearing in the expression of
D_{2}
(
11
) ensures that the velocity variance
\sigma^{2}
(
2
) is given by the integral of the PSD (
10
). We therefore have the identity

\displaystyle\sigma_{\nu}^{2}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathbf{u}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{x})|^{2}\right]=\int_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(\mathbf{k}){\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{k}=3\int_{k\in\mathbb{R}}E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E,long}}}(k)\ {\mathrm{d}}k.

(14)

Consequently, using the limiting value of
\sigma_{\nu}^{2}
as
\nu\to 0
as expressed in (
14
) and the parameterization of the longitudinal PSD provided in (
10
) and recalling that
\eta_{d}
vanishes as
\nu\to 0
(
12
), one obtains:

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}=\lim_{\nu\to 0}\sigma_{\nu}^{2}=6D_{2}\int_{k=0}^{\infty}|\mathbf{k}|_{1/L}^{-5/3}\ {\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{k},

(15)

where
D_{2}
is given by (
11
). Also, as claimed above, the regularization parameter
L
appearing in the expression of the longitudinal PSD (
10
) can be interpreted as the correlation length scale of the longitudinal component. More precisely,
L
can be related to the so-called integral length scale
L_{\text{\tiny{int}}}
, i.e. the integral of the correlation function, as follows:

\displaystyle L_{\text{\tiny{int}}}\equiv\int_{\ell>0}\frac{3}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[u^{\nu}_{1}(t,\mathbf{x})u^{\nu}_{1}(t,\mathbf{x}+\ell\hat{e}_{1})\right]d\ell=\frac{3}{2\sigma^{2}}E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E,long}}}(0)=\frac{\Gamma(5/6)}{2\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(1/3)}L.

(16)

Concerning the average dissipation
\varepsilon_{\nu}
, by making use of the isotropy condition, we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\nu}=15\nu\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\partial_{1}u_{1}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=15\nu\int_{k\in\mathbb{R}}(2\pi)^{2}k^{2}E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E,long}}}(k){\mathrm{d}}k

\displaystyle=30(2\pi)^{2}D_{2}\nu\eta_{d}^{-4/3}\int_{k=0}^{\infty}k^{2}|k|_{\eta_{d}/L}^{-5/3}e^{-k}{\mathrm{d}}k

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{\nu\to 0}}30(2\pi)^{2}D_{2}\nu\eta_{d}^{-4/3}\Gamma(4/3).

(17)

Taking into account the expressions of
D_{2}
(
11
) and
\eta_{d}
(
12
), we then obtain

\displaystyle\varepsilon=\lim_{\nu\to 0}\varepsilon_{\nu}=2\pi\left(\frac{\Gamma(5/6)}{\sqrt{\pi}C_{2}}\right)^{3/2}\frac{\sigma^{3}}{L}.

(18)

In the vanishing viscosity limit, a Gaussian random field which possesses a PSD that decays as a power-law of the type proposed in (
10
) is known in the literature as a fractional Gaussian field of Hölder (or Hurst) parameter
H=1/3
. In particular, such a field is continuous and its variance is finite (
15
), but it is nowhere differentiable, and must be regarded as being Hölder-continuous. To characterize the Hölder-continuity, instead of using gradients that are expected to be infinite, one needs to consider velocity increments that are well-posed. Precisely, the longitudinal velocity increment
\delta_{\mathbf{\ell}}^{\text{\tiny{long}}}\mathbf{u}^{\nu}
is defined by

\displaystyle\delta_{\ell}^{\text{\tiny{long}}}\mathbf{u}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{x})=\left(\mathbf{u}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{x}+\ell)-\mathbf{u}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{x})\right)\cdot\frac{\ell}{|\ell|}\ ,

(19)

for all
\mathbf{\ell}\neq 0
. The variance of the longitudinal increment (
19
) is known as the longitudinal second-order structure function, and is given by

\displaystyle S_{2,\nu}^{\text{\tiny{long}}}(\ell)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\delta_{\ell}^{\text{\tiny{long}}}\mathbf{u}^{\nu}\right)^{2}\right].

(20)

In the vanishing viscosity limit, one obtains

\displaystyle S_{2}^{\text{\tiny{long}}}(\ell)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\nu\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\delta_{\ell}^{\text{\tiny{long}}}\mathbf{u}^{\nu}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=2\int_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\left(1-e^{2i\pi k|\ell|}\right)E^{\text{\tiny{E,long}}}(k){\mathrm{d}}k=4D_{2}\int_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(1-\cos(2\pi k|\ell|)\right)|k|_{1/L}^{-5/3}{\mathrm{d}}k

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{|\ell|\to 0}}4D_{2}|\ell|^{2/3}\int_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(1-\cos(2\pi k)\right)k^{-5/3}{\mathrm{d}}k=3D_{2}(2\pi)^{2/3}\Gamma(1/3)|\ell|^{2/3}.

(21)

As can be seen from the behavior of
S_{2}^{\text{\tiny{long}}}(\ell)
(
2.1
) on small scales
|\mathbf{\ell}|\to 0
, the second-order structure function exhibits a power-law behavior with respect to the scale
\ell
with the exponent
2/3
. Using then the expression of
D_{2}
(
11
), and expressing the remaining multiplicative constant in units of the average dissipation
\varepsilon
(
18
), we finally get

\displaystyle S_{2}^{\text{\tiny{long}}}(\ell)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{|\ell|\to 0}}C_{2}|\varepsilon\ell|^{2/3},

(22)

that justifies the introduction of the Kolmogorov constant
C_{2}
in the definition of the dissipative cutoff
\eta_{d}
(
12
).

More generally, let us go back to the full vector field. Its covariance is provided in (
6
). Let us introduce the increment of a given velocity component
u_{i}^{\nu}

\displaystyle\delta_{\ell}u_{i}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{x})=u_{i}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{x}+\ell)-u_{i}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{x}),

(23)

and the respective second-order structure function

\displaystyle S_{2,ij}(\ell)=\lim_{\nu\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{\ell}u_{i}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{x})\delta_{\ell}u_{j}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{x})\right].

(24)

Then, under the assumption of statistical isotropy and incompressibility, from
(
Pope 2000
)
, one obtains

\displaystyle S_{2,ij}(\ell)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{|\ell|\to 0}}C_{2}|\varepsilon\ell|^{2/3}\left[-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\ell_{i}\ell_{j}}{|\ell|^{2}}+\frac{4}{3}\delta_{ij}\right].

(25)

As a consequence, one has

\displaystyle S_{2}(\ell)\equiv S_{2,ii}(\ell)=\lim_{\nu\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\delta_{\ell}\mathbf{u}^{\nu}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=2\int_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left(1-e^{2i\pi\mathbf{k}\cdot\ell}\right)E^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(|\mathbf{k}|){\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{k}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{|\ell|\to 0}}\frac{11}{3}C_{2}|\varepsilon\ell|^{2/3}.

(26)

Firstly taking the limit
\ell\to 0
, and secondly the limit
\eta_{d}\to 0
(or equivalently
\nu\to 0
), we obtain then the power-law behavior

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\delta_{\ell}^{\text{\tiny{long}}}\mathbf{u}^{\nu}\right)^{2}\right]\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{|\ell|\to 0}}2|\ell|^{2}\int_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\left(2\pi k|\ell|\right)^{2}E^{\text{\tiny{E,long}}}_{\nu}(k){\mathrm{d}}k\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{\nu\to 0}}\frac{\varepsilon_{\nu}}{15\nu}|\ell|^{2},

(27)

where the expression of
\varepsilon_{\nu}
is given in (
2.1
). The power-law (
27
) is associated with differentiable velocity fields instead of being Hölder continuous with fractional exponent
H=1/3
.

2.3
The statistical temporal structure of the turbulent velocity field and the sweeping effect

Fluctuations in time of turbulent velocity can be considered counter-intuitive from a dimensional point of view
(
Tennekes & Lumley 1972
;
Tennekes 1975
)
. In particular, the sweeping effect that can be seen, loosely speaking, as the advection of small scales by large scales, has been investigated and defined in very different ways by several groups
(
Kraichnan 1964
;
Belinicher & L’vov 1987
;
Gotoh et al. 1993
;
Kaneda et al. 1999
;
Chaves et al. 2003
;
Chevillard et al. 2005
;
Favier et al. 2010
;
Biferale et al. 2011
;
Drivas et al. 2017
;
Canet et al. 2017
;
Gorbunova et al. 2021
;
Matsumoto et al. 2021
)
. We should bear in mind that such a mechanism should not be confused with Taylor’s hypothesis related to the presence of a mean flow
(
Wilczek & Narita 2012
;
He et al. 2017
)
. As far as we are concerned, let us discuss this phenomenon in the spirit of
Tennekes 1975
which focuses on the dimensional aspects of this phenomenon and its statistical signature.

From the statistical spatial structure of the velocity field formerly presented, in large part given by the scaling relation provided in (
22
) and (
2.1
), let us infer the statistical temporal structure of the velocity field
\mathbf{u}^{\nu}
in the asymptotic limit
\nu\to 0
. The Hölder regularity pinpointed in (
22
) and (
2.1
) suggests that eddies of characteristic length scale
|\ell|
have a characteristic turnover time scale
\tau_{\text{e}}(|\ell|)
given by

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{e}}(|\ell|)\equiv\frac{|\ell|}{\sqrt{S_{2}(\ell)}}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\propto}\limits_{|\ell|\to 0}}\frac{|\ell|^{2/3}}{\sqrt{C_{2}}\varepsilon^{1/3}}.

(28)

The square-root of the structure function
\sqrt{S_{2}(\ell)}
can be interpreted as the typical velocity of eddies of size
|\ell|
. The characteristic turnover time
\tau_{\text{e}}(|\ell|)
(
28
) of eddies of typical size
\ell
would then follow a power-law with exponent
2/3
as a function of the length scale
|\ell|
. One may wonder whether this prediction is correct. The proposed definition of the time scale
\tau_{\text{e}}(|\ell|)
(
28
) clearly depends solely on the spatial structure of the velocity field and is independent of the temporal structure of the velocity field. In order to take into account the temporal structure, let us thus define a characteristic time scale
\tau_{c}(|\ell|)
which is based on the correlation in time of the spatial increment given by

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{c}}(|\ell|)\equiv\frac{1}{S_{2}(\ell)}\lim_{\nu\to 0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{\ell}\mathbf{u}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{x})\cdot\delta_{\ell}\mathbf{u}^{\nu}(t+\tau,\mathbf{x})\right]{\mathrm{d}}\tau.

(29)

As it will be argued below, we could also define a similar time scale as
\tau_{\text{c}}(|\ell|)
(
29
) to characterize the turnover time scale of an eddy of size
\ell
using instead the temporal structure of the velocity Fourier modes at a given wave vector
k
. This indicates that one assimilates in a loose sense the length scale
|\ell|
to
|\mathbf{k}|^{-1}
. This must be treated with great care since we have assumed in this Chapter that the spatial domain is infinite, i.e.
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and thus Fourier modes must be considered as distributions. Nevertheless, we would define in a formal way the following time scale

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{f}}(k)\equiv``\lim_{\nu\to 0}\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{k})\right|^{2}\right]}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{k})\cdot\overline{\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\nu}(t+\tau,\mathbf{k})}\right]{\mathrm{d}}\tau",

(30)

where
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{k})
is the Fourier transform of the velocity field
\mathbf{u}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{x})
, and
\overline{\cdot}
standing for the complex conjugation. The definition of the time scale
\tau_{\text{f}}(k)
(
30
) is for the time being put in quotes because, as we will see, the Fourier transform over all space
has a distributional nature, and necessitates the formulation of the model in a periodic setting.

Note that the time scale
\tau_{\text{f}}(k)
is expected to depend solely on the norm
k
of the wave vector
\mathbf{k}
by statistical isotropy and can be interpreted as the typical correlation duration at a given wave number. The two time scales
\tau_{\text{c}}(|\ell|)
(
29
) and
\tau_{\text{f}}(k)
(
30
) are related according to

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{c}}(|\ell|)=``\frac{\int_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left[1-\cos\left(2\pi\mathbf{k}\cdot\ell\right)\right]\tau_{\text{f}}(k)\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\widehat{\mathbf{u}}(t,\mathbf{k})\right|^{2}\right]{\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{k}}{\int_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left[1-\cos\left(2\pi\mathbf{k}\cdot\ell\right)\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\widehat{\mathbf{u}}(t,\mathbf{k})\right|^{2}\right]{\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{k}}",

(31)

where we have denoted
\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\widehat{\mathbf{u}}(t,\mathbf{k})\right|^{2}\right]
the limit as
\nu\to 0
of the PSD
\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{k})\right|^{2}\right]
. Once again, we recall that expressions such as (
31
) should be taken in a formal way. Only the consideration of periodic boundary conditions gives a proper meaning to such quantities.

With its definition (
30
), the characteristic time scale
\tau_{\text{f}}(k)
has been estimated in DNS by several groups
(
Kaneda et al. 1999
;
Favier et al. 2010
;
Gorbunova et al. 2021
)
while assuming periodic boundary conditions instead of the full space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, leading to well defined Fourier modes. The numerical investigations from the articles quoted above show, in a consistent manner with the dimensional analysis of
Tennekes 1975
, that

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{f}}(k)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\propto}\limits_{k\to\infty}}\frac{1}{\sigma k}.

(32)

The behavior of the time scale
\tau_{\text{f}}(k)
(
32
) at large wave numbers as
k^{-1}
suggests, based on the dimensional ground, that the typical velocity entering in the motion of eddies of size
k^{-1}
is the standard deviation of the velocity
\sigma
, i.e. the characteristic velocity of large scales. To see this more clearly, let us see the implications of this behavior on the time scale
\tau_{\text{c}}(|\ell|)
(
29
). To do so, consider the identity displayed in (
31
) and rescale the dummy integration variable
\mathbf{k}
by
|\ell|
, then one obtains

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{c}}(|\ell|)

\displaystyle=``\frac{\int_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left[1-\cos\left(2\pi\mathbf{k}\cdot\frac{\ell}{|\ell|}\right)\right]\tau_{\text{f}}(|\mathbf{k}|/|\ell|)\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\widehat{\mathbf{u}}(t,\mathbf{k}/|\ell|)\right|^{2}\right]{\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{k}}{\int_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left[1-\cos\left(2\pi\mathbf{k}\cdot\frac{\ell}{|\ell|}\right)\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\widehat{\mathbf{u}}(t,\mathbf{k}/|\ell|)\right|^{2}\right]{\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{k}}"

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\propto}\limits_{|\ell|\to 0}}\frac{|\ell|}{\sigma}\frac{\int_{\rho\geq 0,\theta\in[0,\pi]}\left[1-\cos\left(2\pi\rho\cos\theta\right)\right]\rho^{-8/3}{\mathrm{d}}\rho{\mathrm{d}}\theta}{\int_{\rho\geq 0,\theta\in[0,\pi]}\left[1-\cos\left(2\pi\rho\cos\theta\right)\right]\rho^{-5/3}{\mathrm{d}}\rho{\mathrm{d}}\theta},

(33)

where we have used the asymptotic behaviors of
\tau_{\text{f}}(k)
(
32
) and of the PSD
\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\widehat{\mathbf{u}}(t,\mathbf{k})\right|^{2}\right]\sim|\mathbf{k}|^{-11/3}
(
13
) at large argument. Notice that the remaining ratio of double integrals entering in (
2.3
) can be shown to be finite (an exact expression can even be obtained using a symbolic calculation software). The relevant result given in (
2.3
) is the fact that, as a consequence of the observation that
\tau_{\text{f}}(k)
becomes inversely proportional to
k
at large
k
(
32
), the genuine characteristic time scale
\tau_{\text{c}}(|\ell|)
of eddies of typical size
|\ell|
(
29
) becomes proportional to
|\ell|
(
2.3
). Consequently, the relevant characteristic velocity appearing in (
2.3
) is
\sigma
, i.e., the characteristic velocity of the largest eddies, while it may have been more natural to obtain their typical velocity
\sqrt{S_{2}(\ell)}
as in the expression (
28
) of
\tau_{\text{e}}(|\ell|)
: this is the phenomenon of advection of the small scales by the large eddies, referred to here and in the turbulence literature as the sweeping effect.

2.4
Implications for of the spatio-temporal covariance matrix of the velocity field

Let us now present the general form of the covariance structure in time and space of the velocity field that encompasses both the spatial structure detailed in Paragraph
2.1
and the very peculiar temporal structure which is governed by the sweeping effect, as is in the former discussion
2.3
.
To do so, let us begin by defining the relevant spatio-temporal covariance of the velocity field
\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{\nu}(\tau,\ell)
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{\nu}(\tau,\ell)=\mathbb{E}\left[u_{i}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{x})u_{j}^{\nu}(t+\tau,\mathbf{x}+\ell)\right].

(34)

Again,
\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{\nu}(\tau,\ell)
is independent of position
\mathbf{x}
and time
t
by statistical homogeneity and stationarity, respectively. We mention that, moreover, the spatiotemporal covariance function (
34
) is an even function of the temporal argument
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{\nu}(-\tau,\ell)=\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{\nu}(\tau,\ell)
. In the following, we will also assume statistical isotropy and explore the consequences of this assumption on the structure of the covariance function. The natural extension of the spatial covariance matrix (
6
) to a spatio-temporal framework
\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{\nu}(\tau,\ell)
(
34
) can be conveniently written in Fourier space as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{\nu}(\tau,\ell)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{2i\pi\mathbf{k}\cdot\ell}F(\tau/T_{\mathbf{k}})E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(\mathbf{k})\widehat{P}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})\ {\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{k},

(35)

where
F
encodes the temporal dependence, assumed to be an integrable function normalized such that
F(0)=1
,
T_{\mathbf{k}}
a time scale that depends on the wave vector
\mathbf{k}
,
E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(\mathbf{k})
is the PSD of the velocity field (
5
) studied above, and
\widehat{P}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})
the Leray projector on divergence-free vector fields (
7
). The covariance structure (
35
) was initially proposed by
Chaves et al. 2003
when
F
is chosen such that
F(\tau)=\exp(-|\tau|)
. As it will be explained below, this choice leads to a Markovian evolution for the Fourier modes, as early noticed by
Komorowski & Peszat 2004
.

As we have already mentioned, it is clear from (
35
) that the PSD
E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(k)
corresponds to the Fourier transform of
\mathcal{C}_{ii}^{\nu}(0,\ell)
. As a consequence, we recover from (
35
) the scaling behaviors of the second-order structure functions (
22
) and (
2.1
). To make a connection with the discussion developed in Section
2.3
, let us establish the link between the characteristic time scale
\tau_{\text{c}}(|\ell|)
(
29
) and the
k
-dependent time scale
T_{k}
entering in
\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{\nu}(\tau,\ell)
(
35
). We have

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{c}}(|\ell|)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\tau=0}^{\infty}\left[\mathcal{C}_{ii}^{\nu}(\tau,0)-\mathcal{C}_{ii}^{\nu}(\tau,\ell)\right]{\mathrm{d}}\tau}{\mathcal{C}_{ii}^{\nu}(0,0)-\mathcal{C}_{ii}^{\nu}(0,\ell)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left(1-e^{2i\pi\mathbf{k}\cdot\ell}\right)T_{\mathbf{k}}E^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(|\mathbf{k}|){\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{k}}{\int_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left(1-e^{2i\pi\mathbf{k}\cdot\ell}\right)E^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(|\mathbf{k}|){\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{k}}\int_{0}^{\infty}F(s){\mathrm{d}}s.

(36)

As suggested, in a formal manner, by the dependence on the wave number
k
of the characteristic time scale
\tau_{\text{f}}(k)
(
30
), let us similarly assume, as has been done by
Chaves et al. 2003
, that

\displaystyle T_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{k\to\infty}}\frac{1}{D_{3}k},

(37)

where
D_{3}
is a free parameter of the description that has the dimension of a velocity. Similarly to the derivation of the relationship between
\tau_{\text{c}}(|\ell|)
and
\tau_{\text{f}}(k)
obtained in (
2.3
), using the scaling behavior obtained in (
37
) and the behavior of the PSD in the same limit (
13
), we get from (
2.4
), while performing the remaining ratio of integrals expressed in spherical variables,

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{c}}(|\ell|)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{|\ell|\to 0}}\frac{|\ell|}{D_{3}}\frac{2^{1/3}\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma^{2}(1/6)}{15\Gamma(11/6)}\int_{0}^{\infty}F(s){\mathrm{d}}s.

(38)

The asymptotic behavior in (
38
), assuming the asymptotic behavior of
T_{k}
(
37
) is consistent with the expected behavior given in (
2.3
). This suggests that the free parameter
D_{3}
can be expressed in units of the velocity standard-deviation
\sigma
(
2
).

Although physically insightful, the behavior of the characteristic time scale
\tau_{\text{c}}(|\ell|)
with the eddy’s size
|\ell|
(
38
) is not traditionally estimated in experiments and numerical simulations. Instead, the temporal second-order structure function and the time spectrum are usually studied.

Concerning the temporal second-order structure function
S_{2}^{\text{\tiny{T}}}(\tau)
, let us define the velocity time increment, i.e. the variation of velocity in time at a fixed position
\mathbf{x}
,

\displaystyle\delta_{\tau}\mathbf{u}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{u}^{\nu}(t+\tau,\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{u}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{x}).

(39)

Using the covariance structure of the velocity field (
35
), we obtain

\displaystyle S_{2}^{\text{\tiny{T}}}(\tau)\equiv\lim_{\nu\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\delta_{\tau}\mathbf{u}^{\nu}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=2\left[\mathcal{C}_{ii}(0,0)-\mathcal{C}_{ii}(\tau,0)\right]

\displaystyle=2\int_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left[1-F(\tau/T_{\mathbf{k}})\right]E^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(|\mathbf{k}|){\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{k}

\displaystyle=2\int_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left[1-F(\tau/T_{\mathbf{k}/\tau})\right]E^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(|\mathbf{k}|/\tau){\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{k}/\tau^{3},

(40)

such that, in the limit of vanishing time scales
\tau\to 0^{+}
, using the asymptotic behaviors of
T_{\mathbf{k}}
(
37
) and
E^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(|\mathbf{k}|)
(
13
) at large wave vector amplitude, one obtains

\displaystyle S_{2}^{\text{\tiny{T}}}(\tau)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{\tau\to 0^{+}}}4\frac{55}{9}D_{2}D_{3}^{2/3}\tau^{2/3}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[1-F(\rho)\right]\rho^{-5/3}{\mathrm{d}}\rho.

(41)

The integral in the right-hand side of (
41
) is finite as long as
1-F(\rho)
goes to 0 as
\rho^{a}
with
a>2/3
, which will be the case in all future situations. Expressing
D_{2}
in units of the average dissipation
\varepsilon
(compare, for instance, the expressions provided in (
2.1
) and (
22
)) and
D_{3}
in units of velocity standard-deviation

, the scaling behavior obtained for the temporal second-order structure function (

\displaystyle S_{2}^{\text{\tiny{T}}}(\tau)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\propto}\limits_{\tau\to 0^{+}}}\sigma^{2/3}(\varepsilon\tau)^{2/3},

(42)

which coincides with the dimensional prediction of
Tennekes 1975
.

Similarly, let us consider the corresponding temporal (or frequency) spectrum
E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{T}}}(\omega)
defined by

\displaystyle E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{T}}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}e^{-2i\pi\omega\tau}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{u}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\nu}(t+\tau,\mathbf{x})\right]{\mathrm{d}}\tau=\int_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}e^{-2i\pi\omega\tau}\mathcal{C}_{ii}^{\nu}(\tau,0){\mathrm{d}}\tau

(43)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}e^{-2i\pi\omega\tau}F(\tau/T_{\mathbf{k}})E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(\mathbf{k}){\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{k}\ {\mathrm{d}}\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}T_{\mathbf{k}}\widehat{F}(\omega T_{\mathbf{k}})E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(\mathbf{k}){\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}T_{\omega\mathbf{k}}\widehat{F}(\omega T_{\omega\mathbf{k}})E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(\omega|\mathbf{k}|)\omega^{3}\ {\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{k},

(44)

where
\widehat{F}
is the Fourier transform of the function
F
. Once again, using the expressions of
T_{\mathbf{k}}
(
37
) and
E^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(|\mathbf{k}|)
(
13
) at large wave vector amplitude, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{\nu\to 0}E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{T}}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\omega T_{\omega\mathbf{k}}\widehat{F}(\omega T_{\omega\mathbf{k}})E^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(\omega|\mathbf{k}|)\omega^{2}{\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{k}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{\omega\to\infty}}2\frac{55}{9}D_{2}D_{3}^{2/3}\omega^{-5/3}\int_{0}^{\infty}\widehat{F}\left(\rho^{-1}\right)\rho^{-8/3}{\mathrm{d}}\rho.

(45)

The integral in the right-hand side of (
2.4
) will be shown to be finite for the classes of functions
F
considered below. After being reformulated in units of
\varepsilon
and
\sigma
, similarly to what has been done in (
42
), the frequency dependence of the time spectrum (
2.4
) is given by

\displaystyle\lim_{\nu\to 0}E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{T}}}(\omega)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\propto}\limits_{\omega\to\infty}}\sigma^{2/3}\varepsilon^{2/3}\omega^{-5/3},

(46)

which is consistent with the dimensional predictions of
Tennekes 1975
.

3
Gaussian random velocity vector, and its causal evolution

3.1
Formulation of the model with periodic boundary conditions: statistical structure of Fourier modes

For practical and numerical reasons, we formulate a version of the Gaussian model described in the former section on the three dimensional torus of size
L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}
: the set
[-L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2\ ;\ L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2]^{3}
with periodic boundary conditions as has been done in DNS. The model is Gaussian, it is thus fully determined in the statistically homogeneous and stationary regime by its covariance function (
34
).
The respective velocity field will thus thus
L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}
-periodic
u^{\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{x})=u^{\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{x}+L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}\hat{e}_{p})
in any Cartesian direction
p=1
,
2
or
3
. Let us mention that future simulations will be performed using
L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}=2\pi
in order to be consistent with the DNS. A fundamental consequence of considering a periodic setting is the possibility to expand the velocity field using a Fourier series, i.e.

\displaystyle u^{\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{L^{3}_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}e^{2i\pi\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\widehat{u}^{\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}),

(47)

with Fourier modes
\widehat{u}^{\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})
, depending on
\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}=\mathbf{n}/L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}
for
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
. The Fourier modes are defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{u}^{\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})=\int_{[-L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2\ ;\ L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2]^{3}}e^{-2i\pi\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\mathbf{x}}u^{\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{x}){\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{x}.

(48)

It can be checked that the variance of the Fourier modes is finite. This solves the issue exhibited in the former Section, where Fourier modes in the whole space need to be interpreted in a distributional sense. Since the velocity field
\mathbf{u}^{\nu}
is real-valued, Fourier modes are hermitian symmetric, i.e.

\displaystyle\overline{\widehat{u}^{\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})}=\widehat{u}^{\nu}_{i}(t,-\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})=\widehat{u}^{\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k}_{-\mathbf{n}}).

(49)

This implies that the Fourier mode for
\mathbf{n}=(0,0,0)
is real-valued.

The purpose of this Section is the formulation in a periodic setting of the statistical quantities defined in the whole space in Section
2.4
. To do so, let us begin by defining the spatio-temporal covariance of the velocity field
\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{\nu}(\tau,\ell)
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{\nu}(\tau,\ell)=\mathbb{E}\left[u_{i}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{x})u_{j}^{\nu}(t+\tau,\mathbf{x}+\ell)\right].

(50)

The definition is identical to which is very similar to (
34
), however
\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{\nu}(\tau,\ell)
given in (
50
) is periodic. If it is furthermore required that
\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{\nu}
(
50
) characterizes a statistically homogeneous and stationary incompressible velocity field, the right-hand side of (
50

\mathbf{x}
and time
t
. The covariance structure of the Fourier modes is then written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{u}_{i}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})\widehat{u}_{j}^{\nu}(t+\tau,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{m}})\right]=\frac{L^{3}_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}}{2}F(\tau/T_{k_{\mathbf{n}}})E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(k_{\mathbf{n}})\widehat{P}_{ij}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})\delta_{\mathbf{n},-\mathbf{m}}^{(3)},

(51)

where
\widehat{P}_{ij}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})
is the Leray projector (
7
) at the discrete wave vector
\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}
(whose norm is
k_{\mathbf{n}}
and also corresponds to
k_{n}
in virtue of isotropy),
\delta_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}^{(3)}=\prod_{i}\delta_{n_{i},m_{i}}
the three-dimensional Kronecker delta function over the indices
\mathbf{n}
and
\mathbf{m}
, scalar functions
F
and
T_{k_{\mathbf{n}}}
encoding the temporal structure similarly to what we have seen in Section
2.4
, and the respective PSD defined as the expectation of the norm square of the Fourier mode, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\nu}(t,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})\right|^{2}\right]=L^{3}_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(k_{\mathbf{n}}).

(52)

This allows to write the covariance of the velocity field (
50

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{\nu}(\tau,\ell)=\frac{1}{2L^{3}_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}e^{2i\pi\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\ell}F(\tau/T_{k_{\mathbf{n}}})E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(k_{\mathbf{n}})\widehat{P}_{ij}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}),

(53)

which is obtained from (
50

For the sake of generality, let us consider an expression of the uni-dimensional PSD
E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E,long}}}(k)
(

\displaystyle E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E,long}}}(k)=D_{2}|k|_{1/L}^{-(2H+1)}e^{-\eta_{d}k},

where the dissipative cut-off

only when

(

\eta_{d}
as a free parameter. As we have seen in Section
2.1
, imposing a given PSD in the longitudinal case

(

In the limit of an infinitely large periodic box

\displaystyle\lim_{L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}\to\infty}\frac{1}{L^{3}_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}f(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})=\int_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(\mathbf{k})\ {\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{k},

Let us also explore the statistical structure of the spatial structure functions at small scales implied by the power-law behavior of the PSD given in (

3.2
Causal evolution of the Fourier modes

Let us now discuss the time evolution of the Fourier modes
\widehat{u}^{\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})
(
48
), such that the correlation structure is given by (
51
), leading to the expected covariance structure
\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{\nu}(\tau,\ell)
given in (
53
). This cannot be done in full generality for any function
F
since we are asking for a causal evolution. Actually, very few choices for
F
can be achieved for a given causal evolution, and we will only explore a very limited set of them. As far as we are concerned, we will present choices of
F
which give a causal evolution.

3.2.1
Fourier modes of a white noise

Before proceeding, we define a Gaussian white noise which is the building block of Gaussian stochastic processes and its Fourier series. A Gaussian white noise, and more precisely a periodic Gaussian white vector over
\mathbf{x}\in[-L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2\ ;\ L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2]^{3}
, will be denoted “
{\mathrm{d}}W(\mathbf{x})
”, which is put in quotes because it has no meaning locally at a given position
\mathbf{x}
, and needs to be properly defined, to be integrated against a deterministic test function as defined in (
3.2.1
). Component-wise, a Gaussian white vector
{\mathrm{d}}W_{i}(\mathbf{x})
for
i=1,2
or
3
is a Gaussian random distribution which is characterized by, for arbitrary and appropriate deterministic functions
f
and
g
, the average

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbf{x}\in[-L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2\ ;\ L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2]^{3}}f(\mathbf{x}){\mathrm{d}}W_{i}(\mathbf{x})\right]=0

(64)

and its covariance

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbf{x}\in[-L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2\ ;\ L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2]^{3}}f(\mathbf{x}){\mathrm{d}}W_{i}(\mathbf{x})\right.

\displaystyle\left.\int_{\mathbf{y}\in[-L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2\ ;\ L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2]^{3}}g(\mathbf{y}){\mathrm{d}}W_{j}(\mathbf{y})\right]

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}\int_{\mathbf{x}\in[-L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2\ ;\ L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2]^{3}}f(\mathbf{x})g(\mathbf{x}){\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{x}.

(65)

Fourier modes (
48
) of the vector white noise will be denoted by
\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})
and are defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{i}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})=\int_{[-L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2\ ;\ L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2]^{3}}e^{-2i\pi\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\mathbf{x}}{\mathrm{d}}W_{i}(\mathbf{x}).

(66)

Interestingly, whereas the white noise in the physical space has a distributional nature, its Fourier modes can be understood in a classical sense. They are complex Gaussian random variables, Hermitian symmetric, fully characterized by their average, which turns out to be zero, and their covariance structure, which reads

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{i}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{j}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{m}})\right]=L^{3}_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}\delta_{i,j}\delta_{\mathbf{n},-\mathbf{m}}^{(3)},

(67)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{i}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})\overline{\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{j}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{m}})}\right]=L^{3}_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}\delta_{i,j}\delta_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}^{(3)}.

(68)

As far as we are concerned, we furthermore have to add a temporal dependence to this white noise and define the space-time vector white noise “
{\mathrm{d}}W_{i}(t,\mathbf{x})
”, which furthermore includes independent in time instances, which again have to be considered in a distributional manner, i.e., it has to be integrated against test functions, in a similar fashion as has been done in (
64
) and (
3.2.1
). The respective Fourier mode (
48
) of the vector white noise will be denoted “
\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})
” as

\displaystyle``\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})"=\int_{[-L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2\ ;\ L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2]^{3}}e^{-2i\pi\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\mathbf{x}}{\mathrm{d}}W_{i}(t,\mathbf{x}).

(69)

Once again, the quotes refer to its distributional nature in time. Because the Fourier mode is a linear operation, “
\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})
” remains a zero-average Gaussian distribution, but it is complex-valued and Hermitian symmetric. For the sake of simplicity, we write in a formal manner

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{j}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{m}})\right]=L^{3}_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}\delta_{i,j}\delta_{\mathbf{n},-\mathbf{m}}^{(3)}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\ {\mathrm{d}}t\ {\mathrm{d}}t^{\prime},

(70)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})\overline{\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{j}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{m}})}\right]=L^{3}_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}\delta_{i,j}\delta_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}^{(3)}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\ {\mathrm{d}}t\ {\mathrm{d}}t^{\prime},

(71)

where
\delta(t)
stands for the Dirac-delta distribution.

3.2.2
The statistical structure of the stationary solution: spatial Fractional Gaussian vector Field

The stochastic evolutions that are proposed below are designed such that their statistically stationary solutions are well defined, of finite variance and all coincide with a so-called fractional Gaussian vector (fGv) field, that we will denote
\mathbf{u}^{\text{\tiny{fGv}},\nu}(\mathbf{x})
. The corresponding Fourier modes are denoted by
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{\tiny{fGv}},\nu}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})
.

Considering the spatial white noise “
{\mathrm{d}}W(\mathbf{x})
”, with statistical structure provided in (
64
) and (
3.2.1
), and its Fourier modes
\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})
with covariance (
67
) and (
68
), the real random field
\mathbf{u}^{\text{\tiny{fGv}},\nu}(\mathbf{x})
is fully defined by its Fourier mode, and is given, component-wise, by

\displaystyle\widehat{u}^{\text{\tiny{fGv}},\nu}_{i}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})=\sqrt{\frac{E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(k_{\mathbf{n}})}{2}}\widehat{P}_{ip}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{p}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}).

(72)

The covariance coincides with (
51
) when
\tau=0
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{u}^{\text{\tiny{fGv}},\nu}_{i}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})\widehat{u}^{\text{\tiny{fGv}},\nu}_{j}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{m}})\right]=\frac{L^{3}_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}}{2}E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(k_{\mathbf{n}})\widehat{P}_{ij}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})\delta_{\mathbf{n},-\mathbf{m}}^{(3)}.

(73)

3.2.3
The Markovian evolution of
Chaves et al. 2003

The first natural evolution for Fourier modes, which coincides with the proposition of
Chaves et al. 2003
, as early recognized by
Komorowski & Peszat 2004
, is of Ornstein-Uhlenbeck type, genuinely Markovian, and reads as the stochastic differential equation

d\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k})=-\frac{1}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k}){\mathrm{d}}t+\sqrt{\frac{E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(\mathbf{k})}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}}\widehat{P}_{ip}(\mathbf{k})\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{p}(t,\mathbf{k}),

(74)

for a given discrete wave vector
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}
.
T_{\mathbf{k}}
is the characteristic time of the Fourier mode
\mathbf{k}
defined in (
56
),
\widehat{P}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})
Leray projector ensuring the incompressibility defined in (
7
) and
E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(k)
is the previously defined 3D-PSD obtained from (
54
) using the isotropic relation (
9
). The superscript
(1)
appearing in the notation of the resulting velocity field
\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k})
(
74
) illustrates that it is obtained using a single evolution with respect to the white noise. The justification for this notation will become clear in the next paragraph. The random contribution to the evolution (
74
) is provided by the stochastic vector term
\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{p}(t,\mathbf{k})
characterized by the covariances (
70
) and (
71
).

The unique solution of the stochastic dynamics proposed in (
74
) reads

\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k})=\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i}(0,\mathbf{k})e^{-\frac{t}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}}+\sqrt{\frac{E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(\mathbf{k})}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}}\widehat{P}_{ip}(\mathbf{k})\int_{s=0}^{t}e^{-\frac{t-s}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}}\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{p}(s,\mathbf{k}),

(75)

for a given initial condition
\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i}(0,\mathbf{k})
. It is well known that the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck dynamics proposed in (
74
) eventually reaches a statistically stationary regime at large time. Moreover, the solution
\widehat{u}^{\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k})
will get independent of the initial condition at large time, as it can be seen from its expression provided in (
75
) where the initial condition
\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i}(0,\mathbf{k})
is weighted by an exponentially decreasing factor.

It turns out that, by choosing the particular complex Gaussian initial condition
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(1),\nu}(0,\mathbf{k})
to coincide with a fractional Gaussian vector
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{\tiny{fGv}},\nu}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})
as depicted in subsection
3.2.2
, then, it the initial condition is fully characterized by the following covariances

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i}(0,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{j}(0,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{m}})\right]=\frac{L^{3}_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}}{2}E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(k_{\mathbf{n}})\widehat{P}_{ij}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})\delta_{\mathbf{n},-\mathbf{m}}^{(3)},

(76)

and

\displaystyle F^{(1)}(\tau)=e^{-|\tau|},

3.2.4
Generalization to a causal and differentiable-in-time framework

In the Section above, we have described the statistical properties in time of the Markovian model of
Chaves et al. 2003
which can be seen as an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck type of evolution for Fourier modes (
74
). Fourier modes of the velocity field
\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k})
at a given wave number
\mathbf{k}
are not differentiable in time because they solve a first-order evolution equation driven by a noise which is white-in-time. As a consequence, the temporal paths of
\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k})
share the same regularity as the Brownian motion and are only Hölder continuous. This non-differentiable nature of Fourier modes has a counterpart in the physical space: the corresponding velocity field
u^{(1),\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{x})
is not differentiable in time. This is consistent with the behavior of the temporal second-order structure functions
S_{2,\nu}^{(1),\text{\tiny{T}}}(\tau)
, which behaves proportionally to the time scale
\tau
(
85
), even for a finite viscosity
\nu>0
. If the velocity field were differentiable, we would expect instead a quadratic behavior, i.e.
S_{2,\nu}^{(1),\text{\tiny{T}}}(\tau)=O_{\nu}(\tau^{2})
where
O_{\nu}(\tau^{2})
stands for a term of order
\tau^{2}
with a viscous dependent multiplicative factor. The necessity of proposing a dynamic capable of achieving this goal in a causal way is of tremendous importance. To do so, we will exploit an idea first proposed by
Sawford 1991
and then generalized to an infinitely differentiable framework by
Viggiano et al. 2020
that consists of replacing the white noise entering in the dynamics (
74
) by a continuous random force, which is itself of Ornstein-Uhlenbeck type and/or itself governed by a dynamics stirred by a continuous random force, which would ensure the finiteness of the first and/or subsequent time derivatives.

We are now in position to introduce the evolution for the Fourier mode
\widehat{u}^{(N),\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k})
.Given an integer
N\geq 2
, we consider the following set of
N
embedded evolution equations:

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\widehat{u}^{(N),\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k})}{{\mathrm{d}}t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{4N}}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}\widehat{u}^{(N),\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k})+\widehat{f}^{(N-1)}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k})\;

(90)

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\widehat{f}^{(N-1)}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k})}{{\mathrm{d}}t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{4N}}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}\widehat{f}^{(N-1)}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k})+\widehat{f}^{(N-2)}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k})\;

(91)

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\widehat{f}^{(2)}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k})}{{\mathrm{d}}t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{4N}}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}\widehat{f}^{(2)}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k})+\widehat{f}^{(1)}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k})\;

(92)

\displaystyle{\mathrm{d}}\widehat{f}^{(1)}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{4N}}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}\widehat{f}^{(1)}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k}){\mathrm{d}}t+\sqrt{q^{(N)}E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(\mathbf{k})}\widehat{P}_{ip}(\mathbf{k})\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{p}(t,\mathbf{k})\;,

(93)

where we introduce in the right-hand side of (
93
) the parameter
q^{(N)}
defined by

\displaystyle q^{(N)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{\left[1+\pi^{2}\frac{T_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}{N}\omega^{2}\right]^{N}}d\omega}\left(\frac{4N}{T_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}\right)^{N}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{T_{\mathbf{k}}\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(N)}{\sqrt{N}\Gamma(N-1/2)}\left(\frac{4N}{T_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}\right)^{N}.

(94)

We show in Appendix
A
that, in the statistically stationary regime, one has

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{u}^{(N),\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})\widehat{u}^{(N),\nu}_{j}(t+\tau,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{m}})\right]=\frac{L^{3}_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}}{2}F^{(N)}(\tau/T_{\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}})E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(k_{\mathbf{n}})\widehat{P}_{ij}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})\delta^{(3)}_{\mathbf{n},-\mathbf{m}},

(95)

where the temporal correlation function

is defined by

\displaystyle F^{(N)}(\tau)=\frac{2|\sqrt{N}\tau|^{N-1/2}K_{N-1/2}\left(2|\sqrt{N}\tau|\right)}{\Gamma(N-1/2)}.

In (

is the modified Bessel function of the second kind. We recall that the expression of

(
96
) should be considered for

. In particular, considering

(
96

given in (
81

is differentiable
(N-1)

\displaystyle F^{(\infty)}(\tau)=\lim_{N\to\infty}F^{(N)}(\tau)=e^{-\tau^{2}}.

(97)

As a consequence, one obtains

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{u}^{(N),\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})\widehat{u}^{(N),\nu}_{j}(t+\tau,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{m}})\right]=\frac{L^{3}_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}}{2}e^{-(\tau/T_{\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}})^{2}}E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(k_{\mathbf{n}})\widehat{P}_{ij}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})\delta^{(3)}_{\mathbf{n},-\mathbf{m}}.

\displaystyle\times\int_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\mathbf{k}|^{2}|\mathbf{k}|_{1/L}^{2(2N-1)\beta-(2H+5)}\ {\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{k}.

4.1
Remarks concerning the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)

In all simulations, we consider a finite number of Fourier modes, and thus, we need to truncate in an appropriate manner the Fourier series (
47
) and perform an associated Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT). For full benefit of the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm, we will be considering an even number of modes
N_{x}
in each direction, which will furthermore be taken as a power of 2, i.e.
N_{x}=2^{p}
, with
p
being a given integer. In this spirit, over the grid
\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{n}}=\mathbf{n}L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/N_{x}
with
\llbracket\ -N_{x}/2\ ;\ N_{x}/2-1\ \rrbracket^{3}
, here and further, we define the DFT as, for
\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{m}}=\mathbf{m}/L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}
with
\mathbf{m}\in\llbracket\ -N_{x}/2\ ;\ N_{x}/2-1\ \rrbracket^{3}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{u}^{\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{m}})=\left(\frac{L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}}{N_{x}}\right)^{3}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\llbracket\ -N_{x}/2\ ;\ N_{x}/2-1\ \rrbracket^{3}}e^{-2i\pi\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{m}}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{n}}u^{\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{x}_{n}),

(115)

with corresponding inverse DFT

\displaystyle u^{\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{n}})=\frac{1}{L^{3}_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\llbracket\ -N_{x}/2\ ;\ N_{x}/2-1\ \rrbracket^{3}}e^{2i\pi\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{m}}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{n}}}\widehat{u}^{\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{m}}).

(116)

4.2

4.2.1
Numerical scheme for the Markovian evolution (
N=1
)

Consider first the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process given by (
74
), and recall that in the Space Sci. Rev. its solution is given by (
79
). The derivation of a numerical scheme is based on a generalization of the expression (
75
) of the solution
\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k})
at time
t
, depending on its initial value
\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i}(0,\mathbf{k})
at time
0
: for any nonnegative integer
m
, the solution at time
t_{m+1}=t_{m}+\delta t
is expressed depending on the solution at time
t_{m}
as

\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i}(t_{m+1},\mathbf{k})=e^{-\frac{\delta t}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}}\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i}(t_{m},\mathbf{k})+\sqrt{\frac{E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(k)}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}}\widehat{P}_{ip}(\mathbf{k})\int_{s=t_{m}}^{t_{m+1}}e^{-\frac{t_{m+1}-s}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}}\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{p}(s,\mathbf{k}).

(117)

The two terms on the right-hand side of (
117
) are independent Gaussian random variables, therefore the values of
\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i}(t_{m},\mathbf{k})
can be computed recursively if one is able to sample the sequence of independent Gaussian random variables
\left(\int_{s=t_{m}}^{t_{m+1}}e^{-\frac{t_{m+1}-s}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}}\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{p}(s,\mathbf{k})\right)_{m\geq 0}
. It suffices to compute the expected value

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{s=t_{m}}^{t_{m+1}}e^{-\frac{t_{m+1}-s}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}}\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{p}(s,\mathbf{k})\right]=0

and, using (
71
), the second-order moments

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{s=t_{m}}^{t_{m+1}}e^{-\frac{t_{m+1}-s}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}}\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{p}(s,\mathbf{k})\right|^{2}\right]=L^{3}_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}\int_{s=t_{m}}^{t_{m+1}}e^{-\frac{2(t_{m+1}-s)}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}}ds=\frac{L^{3}_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}T_{\mathbf{k}}\left(1-e^{-\frac{2\delta t}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}}\right)}{2}.

For any
m
, consider a Gaussian white noise vector
\Gamma_{p,m}(\mathbf{x})
and define the associated Fourier modes
\widehat{\Gamma}_{p,m}(\mathbf{k})
following (
66
), i.e. set

\widehat{\Gamma}_{p,m}(\mathbf{k})=\int_{[-L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2\ ;\ L_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2]^{3}}e^{-2i\pi\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\Gamma_{p,m}(\mathbf{x}){\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{x}\ .

As a consequence of (
67
) and (
68
), one has the equality in distribution

\int_{s=t_{m}}^{t_{m+1}}e^{-\frac{t_{m+1}-s}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}}\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}W}_{p}(s,\mathbf{k})=\sqrt{\frac{T_{\mathbf{k}}\left(1-e^{-\frac{2\delta t}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}}\right)}{2}}\widehat{\Gamma}_{p,m+1}(\mathbf{k}),

(118)

meaning that the random variables on the left and the on the right-hand sides of the identity above are Gaussian, centered, and have the same covariance structure.

This suggests to introduce the numerical scheme defined by

\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i,m+1}(\mathbf{k})=e^{-\frac{\delta t}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}}\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i,m}(\mathbf{k})+\sqrt{\frac{E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(\mathbf{k})\left(1-e^{-\frac{2\delta t}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}}\right)}{2}}\widehat{P}_{ip}(\mathbf{k})\widehat{\Gamma}_{p,m+1}(\mathbf{k}),

(119)

which can be simulated in practice by sampling a new Gaussian white noise vector and its Fourier modes at each iteration. This means that the numerical approximation defines a discrete time Markov process.

If the initial value
\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i,0}(\mathbf{k})
is equal in distribution to the initial value
\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i}(0,\mathbf{k})
of (
117
), then for all
m\geq 0
one has
\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i,m}(\mathbf{k})=\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i}(t_{m},\mathbf{k})
in distribution. As a result, the proposed numerical scheme solves exactly in distribution the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck dynamics (
74
) (at grid times
t_{m}=m\delta t
).

A stationary version of the numerical approximation is obtained by choosing the initial value
\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i,0}(\mathbf{k})
distributed as the fractional Gaussian vector field studied in subsection
3.2.2
, which can be sampled following (
72
) as

\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i,0}(\mathbf{k})=\sqrt{\frac{E_{\nu}^{\text{\tiny{E}}}(\mathbf{k})}{2}}\widehat{P}_{ip}(\mathbf{k})\widehat{\Gamma}_{p,0}(\mathbf{k}).

(120)

It is then straightforward to check that by construction the distribution of
\widehat{u}^{(1),\nu}_{i,m}(\mathbf{k})
is independent of
m
.

Applying a numerical scheme which is exact in distribution for the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck dynamics naturally preserves its stationary Gaussian distribution, this is fundamental to obtain numerical simulations which exhibit the correct spatial regularity and statistical properties.

0&1&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&1&0\\
0&0&\ldots&0&1\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&1&\ldots&0&0\\
0&0&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&0&1\\
0&0&\ldots&0&0\end{pmatrix}

(122)

and the row vector

\mathbf{e}^{(N)}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\vdots\\
0\\
1\end{pmatrix},

(123)

the vector-valued unknown
\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i}^{(N),\nu}(t,\mathbf{k})
can be interpreted as the solution to the multi-dimensional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck dynamics

{\mathrm{d}}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i}^{(N),\nu}(t,\mathbf{k})=\mathbf{A}^{(N)}(\mathbf{k})\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i}^{(N),\nu}(t,\mathbf{k}){\mathrm{d}}t+\sqrt{q^{(N)}E_{\nu}(k)}\widehat{P}_{ip}(\mathbf{k})\mathbf{e}^{(N)}{\mathrm{d}}\widehat{W}_{p}(t,\mathbf{k}).

(124)

Considering the matrix exponential
e^{t\mathbf{A}^{(N)}(k)}
for all
t\geq 0
, the variation of constants formula provides an expression for the solution at any time
t\geq 0

\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i}^{(N),\nu}(t,\mathbf{k})=e^{t\mathbf{A}^{(N)}(k)}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i}^{(N),\nu}(0,\mathbf{k})+\sqrt{q^{(N)}E_{\nu}(k)}\widehat{P}_{ip}(\mathbf{k})\!\int\limits_{s=0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\mathbf{A}^{(N)}(k)}\mathbf{e}^{(N)}{\mathrm{d}}\widehat{W}_{p}(s,\mathbf{k}).

(125)

Using (
122
), it is straightforward to check that for all
t\geq 0
one has

e^{t\mathbf{A}^{(N)}(k)}=e^{-\frac{\sqrt{4N}t}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}}\begin{pmatrix}1&t&\ldots&\frac{t^{N-2}}{(N-2)!}&\frac{t^{N-1}}{(N-1)!}\\
0&1&\ddots&\ddots&\frac{t^{N-2}}{(N-2)!}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&1&t\\
0&0&\ldots&0&1\end{pmatrix}

(126)

and as a result one has

e^{t\mathbf{A}^{(N)}(k)}\mathbf{e}^{(N)}=e^{-\frac{\sqrt{4N}t}{T_{k}}}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{t^{N-1}}{(N-1)!}\\
\frac{t^{N-2}}{(N-2)!}\\
\vdots\\
t\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

(127)

Like in the derivation of the numerical scheme when
N=1
, it is convenient to express the solution at time
t_{m+1}=t_{m}+\delta t
depending on the solution at time
t_{m}
, as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i}^{(N),\nu}(t_{m+1},\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=e^{\delta t\mathbf{A}^{(N)}(k)}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i}^{(N),\nu}(t_{m},\mathbf{k})

(128)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{q^{(N)}E_{\nu}(|\mathbf{k}|)}\widehat{P}_{ip}(\mathbf{k})\int_{s=t_{m}}^{t_{m+1}}e^{(t_{m+1}-s)\mathbf{A}^{(N)}(k)}\mathbf{e}^{(N)}{\mathrm{d}}\widehat{W}_{p}(s,\mathbf{k}).

It is required to sample at each iteration the Gaussian random vector

\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}_{p,m}^{(N)}(\mathbf{k})=\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{Z}_{p,m}^{(N)}(\mathbf{k})\\
\widehat{Z}_{p,m}^{(N-1)}(\mathbf{k})\\
\vdots\\
\widehat{Z}_{p,m}^{(2)}(\mathbf{k})\\
\widehat{Z}_{p,m}^{(1)}(\mathbf{k})\end{pmatrix}=\int_{s=t_{m}}^{t_{m+1}}e^{(t_{m+1}-s)\mathbf{A}^{(N)}(k)}\mathbf{e}^{(N)}{\mathrm{d}}\widehat{W}_{p}(s,\mathbf{k})

(129)

which has components given by

\widehat{Z}_{p,m}^{(n)}(\mathbf{k})=\int_{s=t_{m}}^{t_{m+1}}e^{-\frac{\sqrt{4N}(t_{m+1}-s)}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}}\frac{(t_{m+1}-s)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}{\mathrm{d}}\widehat{W}_{p}(s,\mathbf{k}).

(130)

The components of
\widehat{Z}_{p,m}^{(n)}(\mathbf{k})
are not independent since they depend on the same white noise. In order to sample the centered Gaussian random vector
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}_{p,m}^{(N)}(\mathbf{k})
, one needs to compute its covariance matrix
\mathbf{Q}^{(N)}(\mathbf{k})
with entries given by

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{Q}^{(N)}(\mathbf{k})\right)_{n_{1},n_{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\widehat{Z}_{p,m}^{(n_{1})}(\mathbf{k})\overline{\widehat{Z}_{p,m}^{(n_{2})}(\mathbf{k})}]

\displaystyle=L^{3}_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}\int_{s=t_{m}}^{t_{m+1}}e^{-2\frac{\sqrt{4N}(t_{m+1}-s)}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}}\frac{(t_{m+1}-s)^{n_{1}-1}}{(n_{1}-1)!}\frac{(t_{m+1}-s)^{n_{2}-1}}{(n_{2}-1)!}ds

\displaystyle=L^{3}_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}\int_{s=0}^{\delta t}e^{-2\frac{\sqrt{4N}s}{T_{\mathbf{k}}}}\frac{s^{n_{1}-1}}{(n_{1}-1)!}\frac{s^{n_{2}-1}}{(n_{2}-1)!}ds.

In order to sample a centered
N
-dimensional Gaussian random vector with a given covariance matrix
\mathbf{Q}
, it is sufficient to identify any matrix
\mathbf{R}
such that
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{T}
. Indeed, if
\Gamma
is a Gaussian random vector with independent components distributed as standard centered normal random variables, then
\mathbf{R}\Gamma
is a centered Gaussian random vector with covariance matrix
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{T}=\mathbf{Q}
. The identification of such matrices
\mathbf{R}
can be performed in several ways. Recalling that the covariance matrix
\mathbf{Q}
is nonnegative symmetric, one may compute its square root (which requires to compute first its eigenvalues and eigenvectors). In practice, when
\mathbf{Q}
is positive definite, a more convenient approach is to compute the Cholesky decomposition of
\mathbf{Q}
, which amounts to finding a matrix
\mathbf{R}
that is lower triangular. The matrix
\mathbf{R}
is unique if the diagonal entries of
\mathbf{Q}
are positive. Moreover, Cholesky decomposition can be computed using a straightforward algorithm.

Let
\mathbf{R}^{(N)}(\mathbf{k})
denote the matrix obtained by the Cholesky decomposition of the covariance matrix
\mathbf{Q}^{(N)}(\mathbf{k})
whose entries are given above. The Gaussian random vector
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}_{p,m}^{(N)}(\mathbf{k})
can be sampled as
\mathbf{R}^{(N)}(\mathbf{k})\widehat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{p,m}^{(N)}
,
where
\widehat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{p,m}^{(N)}
is a Gaussian random vector given by

\widehat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{p,m}^{(N)}=\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\Gamma}_{p,m}^{(N)}(\mathbf{k})\\
\widehat{\Gamma}_{p,m}^{(N-1)}(\mathbf{k})\\
\vdots\\
\widehat{\Gamma}_{p,m}^{(2)}(\mathbf{k})\\
\widehat{\Gamma}_{p,m}^{(1)}(\mathbf{k})\end{pmatrix}

with entries
\widehat{\Gamma}_{p,m}^{(N)}(\mathbf{k}),\ldots,\widehat{\Gamma}_{p,m}^{(1)}(\mathbf{k})
given as Fourier modes of independent Gaussian white noise vectors
\Gamma_{p,m}^{(N)}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,\Gamma_{p,m}^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})
.

Finally, one obtains the numerical scheme defined by

\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i,m+1}^{(N),\nu}(\mathbf{k})=e^{\delta t\mathbf{A}^{(N)}(k)}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i,m}^{(N),\nu}(\mathbf{k})+\sqrt{q^{(N)}E_{\nu}(|\mathbf{k}|)}\widehat{P}_{ip}(\mathbf{k})\mathbf{R}^{(N)}(\mathbf{k})\widehat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{p,m}^{(N)}.

(131)

The numerical approximation defines a discrete time Markov process, where at each iteration
N
new Gaussian white noise vectors and their Fourier modes need to be computed.

If the initial value
\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i,0}^{(N),\nu}(\mathbf{k})
is equal in distribution to the initial value
\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i}^{(N),\nu}(0,\mathbf{k})
of the continuous time dynamics, then for all
m\geq 0
one has
\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i,m}^{(N),\nu}(\mathbf{k})=\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i}^{(N),\nu}(t_{m},\mathbf{k})
in distribution. As a result, the proposed numerical scheme solves exactly in distribution the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck dynamics (at grid times
t_{m}=m\delta t
).

A stationary version of the numerical approximation is obtained by choosing the initial value
\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i,0}^{(N),\nu}(\mathbf{k})
distributed according to the Gaussian stationary distribution described in subsection
3.2.4
. If this holds, then the distribution of
\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i,m}^{(N),\nu}(\mathbf{k})
is independent of
m
.

5
Conclusions and perspectives

We have proposed a time evolving stochastic model of a Gaussian velocity field that reproduces several important aspects of fluid turbulence, up to second-order statistical quantities. The present version of the model is formulated in a Gaussian framework and is fully characterized by its spectral properties, or equivalently by its second-order structure function, in both space and time. To the best of our knowledge, this is the unique stochastic representation of fluid turbulence with a realistic statistical temporal structure, associated to a causal formulation, which encompasses the dimensional predictions of
Tennekes 1975
. The statistical signatures of this random field compare in a satisfactory manner with the observations made on DNS velocity fields, provided by the Johns Hopkins database
(
Li et al. 2008
)
. We provide efficient numerical schemes, exact in probability distributions, that are straightforward to implement.

As mentioned throughout the article, the present random model is a Gaussian field and therefore misses the possibility of reproducing several other fluid turbulence properties of crucial importance. Among them, the model misses the asymmetrical nature of the probability density functions of the velocity increments, known in turbulence as the skewness phenomenon
(
Frisch 1995
)
. The skewness phenomenon is clearly understood in the context of energy transfers. Its statistical formulation is fully taken into account by the Kármán and Howarth equation
(
Frisch 1995
)
and even locally in the context of the budget of weak solutions of the Navier-Stokes equations
(
Duchon & Robert 2000
;
Eyink 2024
)
. It is usually associated with the existence of a cascading phenomenon of energy towards small scales. Thus, in this sense, the present Gaussian model is not able to reproduce some crucial ingredients of the nonlinear dynamics, that are at the heart of the equations of fluid motion. In the same spirit, other non-Gaussian behaviors, as can be observed in higher-order statistical quantities, such as the flatness of velocity increments
(
Chevillard et al. 2012
)
, are not reproduced either. These anomalous behaviors are usually associated to the intermittency phenomenon, also known as multifractality
(
Frisch 1995
)
. In a future communication (part II), we aim at generalizing the present Gaussian approach to a multifractal framework, while including some ingredients of the vorticity stretching phenomenon, in the spirit of previous works
(
Chevillard et al. 2010
;
Pereira et al. 2016
)
.

Also, as we mentioned in Section
3.3
when we compared the temporal structure of the present modeled velocity field with DNS, the present approach misses an important ingredient of the sweeping effect, but presents excellent agreement from a statistical point of view. A recent proposition by
Armstrong & Vicol 2025
seems to allow this property by introducing in their iterative construction of a velocity field the inverse of the Lagrangian flow map. It is thus tempting to apply such a procedure in the context of fractional Gaussian fields and explore whether it gives a realistic picture in time and if this remains affordable from a numerical point of view. Once again, we keep this aspect in mind for future investigations.

Computation and limit of the covariance structure of

Following Proposition A.1 of Ref.

(132)

and for any

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\widehat{f}_{i}^{(n),\nu},\widehat{f}_{j}^{(n),\nu}}(\tau,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{f}^{(n),\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k})\widehat{f}^{(n),\nu}_{j}(t+\tau,\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\right].

(133)

In the statistically stationary regime, we can write, similarly to (

\displaystyle\widehat{u}^{(N),\nu}_{i}(t,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\;e^{-\sqrt{4N}(t-s)/T_{\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}}}\widehat{f}^{(N-1),\nu}_{i}(s,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}){\mathrm{d}}s,

(134)

and as a result, one obtains

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\widehat{u}_{i}^{(N),\nu},\widehat{u}_{j}^{(N),\nu}}

\displaystyle(\tau,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}},\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{m}})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\int_{-\infty}^{t+\tau}\;e^{-\sqrt{4N}(2t+\tau-s_{1}-s_{2})/T_{\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}}}\mathcal{C}_{\widehat{f}_{i}^{(N-1),\nu},\widehat{f}_{j}^{(N-1),\nu}}(s_{1}-s_{2},\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}},\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{m}}){\mathrm{d}}s_{1}{\mathrm{d}}s_{2}

(135)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\widehat{u}_{i}^{(N),\nu},\widehat{u}_{j}^{(N),\nu}}(\tau,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}},\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{m}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}g_{T_{\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}}}(\tau+s_{2})g_{T_{\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}}}(s_{1})\mathcal{C}_{\widehat{f}_{i}^{(N-1),\nu},\widehat{f}_{j}^{(N-1),\nu}}(s_{1}-s_{2},\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}},\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{m}}){\mathrm{d}}s_{1}{\mathrm{d}}s_{2}

We will eventually need the behavior of the function

(144)

To do so, we have to perform a development of the modified Bessel function of the second kind. We found the approach developed by

At small argument, we get the following

\displaystyle F^{(N)}(\tau)=(-1)^{N+1}\pi\frac{1}{\Gamma(N-1/2)}\left(\frac{1}{\Gamma(-N+3/2)}+\frac{N\tau^{2}}{\Gamma(-N+5/2)}+o(\tau^{2})\right),

simplifies to,

\displaystyle F^{(N)}(\tau)=1-\frac{N}{N-3/2}\tau^{2}+o(\tau^{2}).

(146)
