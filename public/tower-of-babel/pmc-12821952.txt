Cooking fuel choices and household respiratory health in Nigeria
Authors: Noah Olasehinde, Patricia Ajayi, Oluwaseun Oloyede
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNigeria is a leading oil-producing nation with a significant portion of the population depending on polluting solid fuels for cooking. This reliance contributes to substantial indoor air pollution, posing a major public health threat, particularly in the form of acute respiratory infections (ARIs). This study investigates the factors driving household cooking fuel choices and examines their association with respiratory health outcomes.MethodsThis study utilized data from the 2018–19 Nigeria General Household Survey (Wave 4), a nationally representative sample of 26,367 households. To account for the non-sequential nature of fuel-stacking behavior, a multinomial logit model was employed to identify the determinants of primary cooking fuel choice (firewood, charcoal, kerosene, gas, electricity). The relationship between the chosen cooking fuel and the prevalence of ARI was then analyzed using a standard logit model.ResultsKey determinants of fuel choice included residential location, cooking place, household size, income, and education level. Rural and low-income households demonstrated a significantly higher likelihood of using polluting fuels like firewood, charcoal, and kerosene. In contrast, households with higher education and income levels were more likely to use cleaner fuels, such as gas and electricity. A key finding was the lower prevalence of ARIs among households using charcoal or kerosene compared to those using firewood, highlighting a health gradient even among unclean fuels.ConclusionThe persistent use of polluting cooking fuels in Nigeria, driven by socioeconomic factors, poses a severe risk to respiratory health. Policy interventions must be multifaceted: promoting cleaner transitional fuels (charcoal, and kerosene) for households unable to immediately adopt electricity and LPG alongside making clean fuels more accessible and affordable, especially in rural areas, and implementing public health campaigns on the dangers of indoor air pollution.Keywords: Acute respiratory infections, Cooking fuel, Multinomial logit model, Nigeria

Background

Respiratory health is defined as the absence of lung-damaging diseases, often caused by respiratory infections induced by smoke, air pollution, occupational chemicals, and dust [4]. Globally, air pollution from household fuels caused about 3.2 million deaths in 2019 [33]. A leading contributor to this burden is acute respiratory infections (ARIs), a major reasons for hospitalization and mortality, especially among children [37]. Pneumonia, a key ARI, kills over 140,000 children every year [31]. In Nigeria, it was estimated that 20 percent of under-five deaths between 2008 and 2013 were due to pneumonia [1].

Household air pollution from cooking with solid fuels is a primary driver of this crisis. Approximately 70 percent of the Nigerian population relies on solid fuel as the principal resource for household cooking (NBS 2024). These fuels, including firewood, charcoal, and crop residues, release harmful emissions. Domestic usage of biomass fuels results in ARI, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), lung cancer, and other eye disorders [23]. Furthermore, solid fuel use also contributes to outdoor air pollution, increasing particulate concentrations in urban areas [35].

This problem is particularly acute in developing countries like Nigeria, where women and children, who are often present during cooking, face disproportionate risks [6, 17]. Adults exposed to this pollution have a higher chance of developing respiratory issues, high blood pressure, and lung cancer [9, 16]. Exposure to firewood is a primary risk factor for over half of COPD cases in Nigeria [18], and women who cook with biomass have a three times higher risk of developing COPD than those using clean fuels [30]. This is especially alarming given that Nigeria, a leading oil and gas exporter, faces a severe energy crisis where over 90% of the population relies on biomass for cooking [15].

A substantial body of literature has investigated the determinants of household fuel choice, identifying key factors such as income, education, location, and household size [3, 7], [14]. Concurrently, studies have established a clear relationship between the use of polluting fuels and adverse respiratory health outcomes [19, 30]. However, a critical gap remaining is that many studies apply the energy ladder hypothesis, which implies a sequential transition through fuel types. This often leads to the use of ordered regression models, which may not accurately capture the complex fuel-stacking behaviour, the concurrent use of multiple fuels, that characterizes household energy use in Nigeria.

This study addresses this by employing a multinomial logit model, which is better suited to analyse the non-sequential choice among discrete cooking fuel options. Using a large, nationally representative dataset, this research provides contemporary evidence on the socioeconomic determinants of fuel choice and quantifies the distinct association between primary fuel type, which include transitional fuels like charcoal and kerosene, and the prevalence of ARIs. The findings offer nuanced insights essential for designing effective public health and clean energy policies in Nigeria.

Literature review

The theoretical discourse on household energy choices is predominantly based on the Energy Ladder Model [39], [41]. This framework posits a linear transition where households progressively move from traditional biomass fuels like firewood and dung to modern, efficient fuels, such as LPG, electricity, as their socioeconomic status, particularly income, improves [38], [2]. The model describes a three-stage progression: from universal biomass, to transitional fuels like kerosene, and finally to modern fuels.

However, empirical evidence, especially from developing economies like Nigeria, reveals a major drawback of this model. Its rigid, hierarchical structure often fails to capture the reality of fuel stacking where households simultaneously use multiple fuels for different purposes to manage costs, ensure energy security, and accommodate varying cooking needs [42]. The energy ladder model overlooks critical factors such as fuel availability, the cost of energy appliances, and cultural preferences, leading to an oversimplification of household decision-making. Consequently, this study is situated within a conceptual framework that recognizes fuel stacking as the prevailing reality, which justifies the use of a multinomial logit model, which is adept at analysing discrete, non-sequential choices among multiple alternatives, providing a more accurate empirical representation of household behavior than models that assume a natural ordering.

Empirical studies consistently affirm the role of socioeconomic factors in fuel choice, aligning with the broad tenets of the energy ladder while illustrating the complexity of stacking. In Nigeria, Orji et al. [24] identified household size and income as pivotal determinants, while Oyeniran and Isola [25] emphasized the significant influence of education and place of residence. This pattern is confirmed by recent multi-country studies,for instance, Choudhury et al. [5], analysing data from India, found that wealth, education, and access to clean fuel sources were the most powerful predictors of clean fuel adoption. Similarly, Mangeni et al. [12] reported that in rural and peri-urban Kenya, household income, education level, and proximity to trading centres were critical factors enabling transitions to cleaner energy, reinforcing the universal importance of socioeconomic empowerment. Also, Ma et al. [11] and Wang et al. [32] demonstrated that education and income are key drivers of fuel transitions in China.

Globally, the reliance on solid fuels remains a pressing issue, with over 2.4 billion people still using them for cooking (IEA 2023), leading to severe respiratory health consequences, particularly for women and children [33]. The dire health implications of solid fuel use are well-documented. Recent studies have robustly linked these fuels to adverse respiratory outcomes. Xu et al. [36], in a robust analysis of two national prospective cohorts, provided compelling longitudinal evidence that household air pollution from solid fuel use is significantly associated with an increased incidence of respiratory symptoms and diseases. This aligns with the findings of Olagunju et al. [20], who also found that solid fuels elevate the risk of acute respiratory infections (ARI) and cardiovascular diseases, a relationship previously identified in the Nigerian context by Ozoh et al. [26], who established associations between kerosene/LPG use and respiratory symptoms in Lagos. Beyond health, factors like culture and affordability perpetuate the use of biomass, as shown by Mannir et al. [13] in Katsina State, Nigeria.

Building on the solid foundation the existing literature provides, previous studies in Nigeria have effectively established a link between fuel type and respiratory symptoms. However, the studies often treat fuel choices in a binary or limited categorical manner like clean vs. unclean or focus on specific regions. Therefore, this study moves the discourse forward by explicitly modelling fuel-stacking behaviour using a multinomial logit framework to analyse the determinants of five distinct primary fuel choices (firewood, charcoal, kerosene, gas, electricity) without imposing a hierarchical order. It also provides a contemporary, nationally representative analysis of the fuel-ARI nexus, capturing nuances that may be lost in regional studies; and finally, it investigates the health gradient across all fuel types, specifically testing whether transitional fuels like charcoal and kerosene offer a measurable respiratory health benefit compared to the most polluting option, firewood, a nuance with significant policy implications that is underexplored in the Nigerian context.

By integrating a theoretical model of fuel choice with a robust analysis of health outcomes, this research provides a better understanding required to craft targeted public health and energy policies in Nigeria.

Methods

The empirical analysis was conducted in two stages to systematically address the study's objectives. First, the socioeconomic and demographic determinants of households' primary cooking fuel choices were investigated. Second, the effect of these fuel choices on the prevalence of ARIs within households was examined.

Estimation technique and endogeneity considerations

The nature of the dependent variables in this study necessitates the use of discrete choice models estimated via Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE). For the first-stage model (Eq. 1), where the dependent variable consists of five unordered, mutually exclusive cooking fuel choices, the multinomial logit model (MNL) is the appropriate estimator. While the Energy Ladder Hypothesis suggests a natural ordering, the empirical reality in Nigeria is characterized by fuel-stacking behaviour, where household choices are dictated by a complex mix of affordability, availability, and habit rather than a simple progression. This results in a lack of a meaningful sequential order, making the MNL a more theoretically consistent and flexible choice than an ordered logit model (Wooldridge 2015)

For the second-stage model (Eq. 2), where the outcome is a binary indicator for whether a household member experienced an ARI, a binary logit model is applied. However, a potential endogeneity concern arises from reverse causality: a household's cooking fuel choice may be influenced by pre-existing respiratory health conditions. If present, this would render the fuel choice variable endogenous, as the error terms of Eqs. (1) and (2) would be correlated, leading to biased estimates.

To test for this endogeneity, we followed an instrumental variable (IV) approach for discrete endogenous regressors, as outlined in Wooldridge (2015) [34] and applied in similar contexts [10, 22]. We selected two instruments believed to influence cooking fuel choice but not to directly affect ARI prevalence after controlling for other model covariates:

The intuition is that these variables strongly predict fuel choice (e.g. cooking outdoors permits smokier fuels; low income necessitates cheaper options) but are unlikely to directly cause an ARI outside of their pathway through fuel selection. The test was implemented using a two-stage residual inclusion (2SRI) method. First, a multinomial logit model predicted cooking fuel choice, including the instruments and all other exogenous variables. The generalized residuals from this first-stage regression were then included as additional regressors in the second-stage logistic regression for ARI. The statistical insignificance of these residual terms constitutes a test for exogeneity [43].

The preliminary analysis showed that the coefficients on the included residuals were jointly statistically insignificant (p > 0.10). This failure to reject the null hypothesis indicates that the cooking fuel variable can be treated as exogenous in our model. This suggests that, for this national sample, unobserved factors influencing ARI prevalence are not systematically correlated with unobserved factors driving fuel choice after controlling for our observed covariates. Therefore, a standard logistic regression model is used to estimate Eq. (2), providing consistent and efficient estimates. Results are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All estimations were performed using STATA version 17.

Data and sources

The analysis for this study is based on the 2018/19 General Household Survey (GHS)—Wave 4. This dataset is a part of the Living Standards Measurement Study (LSMS)—Integrated Survey on Agriculture, a flagship project implemented by the Nigerian National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) in collaboration with the World Bank.

The GHS employs a stratified, multi-stage random sampling design to select a nationally representative sample of approximately 5,000 households, ensuring coverage across all six geopolitical zones of Nigeria. The survey provides comprehensive data on household demographics, socio-economic status, housing characteristics, health, nutrition, and detailed energy use, including the primary source of cooking fuel.

This study utilizes the post-harvest wave of the survey. The 2018/19 wave was selected for this analysis as it represents the most recent, publicly available, and comprehensive dataset that concurrently contains harmonized information on both household cooking fuel choices and specific health outcomes, which is essential for investigating the research objectives.

Discussion

The results from the multinomial logit model robustly demonstrate that location, income, and education are primary drivers of fuel selection in Nigeria. This finding is not isolated; it resonates with recent international evidence of Choudhury et al. [5] in India and Mangeni et al. [12] in Kenya, who identified the same triad of socioeconomic factors like wealth, education, and rurality as the fundamental barriers to clean energy adoption. This cross-continental consistency underscores that the energy poverty faced by Nigerian households is part of a broader global challenge, rooted in universal economic principles of accessibility, cost minimization, and human capital [25].

In rural areas, modern fuels like LPG and electricity often face significant distributional inefficiencies and higher relative costs, making biomass a rational, albeit harmful, choice due to its availability and low monetary cost, though it carries high health externalities. Conversely, the strong, positive gradient between educational attainment and the use of clean fuels (gas and electricity) supports Ma et al. [11]. This relationship can be interpreted through the lens of information asymmetry and human capital theory,higher education likely enhances awareness of the health damages associated with pollution (reducing information asymmetry) and increases the value placed on long-term health (human capital), thereby changing preferences and willingness-to-pay for cleaner, more efficient energy sources.

A critical finding of this study pertains to the health outcomes associated with different fuel types. While firewood use was consistently associated with the highest prevalence of ARIs, users of charcoal and kerosene exhibited a statistically significant reduction in ARI risk. This finding challenges a purely binary (clean vs. unclean) classification of fuels and introduces a crucial health gradient. It suggests that charcoal and kerosene, while still polluting, may be vital transitional fuels within the Nigerian context. This finding partially aligns with Olagunju et al. [20], who documented the harms of solid fuels, but uniquely showed that all solid fuels are not equally detrimental. From an economic perspective, this gradient reflects a marginal reduction in health damage for households making an initial, albeit incomplete, upgrade on the energy ladder. For low-income households for whom an immediate switch to LPG is financially prohibitive, transitioning from firewood to charcoal may represent a cost-effective strategy for achieving a public health gain, a point often overlooked in policy discussions focused solely on a full transition to modern fuels.

The analysis of controlled covariates further enriches this narrative. The inverse relationship between household size and the use of cleaner fuels, consistent with Kwakwa et al. [7], underscores a budget constraint effect,larger households face greater total energy demands, making the recurring cost of clean fuels disproportionately burdensome and reinforcing reliance on cheaper, polluting alternatives.

Finally, the econometric finding that cooking fuel choice could be treated as exogenous in our model is itself a noteworthy result. It implies that, at a national level, unobserved household preferences or characteristics driving fuel selection are not the primary determinants of ARI risk. Instead, the direct physiological impact of emissions exposure appears to be the dominant mechanism, strengthening the causal argument for interventions that target fuel quality and combustion efficiency directly.

Limitations of the study

While this study provides valuable, nationally representative insights, few limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional nature of the data allows for the identification of robust associations but cannot definitively establish long-term causal relationships. The dynamics of fuel switching and the cumulative health impacts of exposure to cooking smoke are inherently longitudinal processes that could be better captured by panel data.

Second, the measures of acute respiratory infections (ARIs) were based on self-reported symptoms (cough, pneumonia, tuberculosis) in the 12 weeks preceding the survey, rather than on clinical diagnosis. This could lead to under-reporting or misclassification of cases, a known issue in health-seeking behavior research in Nigeria [21]. However, this is a common constraint in large-scale household surveys, and the measure provides a consistent, population-level indicator of respiratory illnesses.

Lastly, while the study employed a rigorous two-stage modelling approach and tested for endogeneity, the possibility of omitted variable bias cannot be entirely ruled out. Unobserved factors, such as actual local fuel availability or genetic predispositions to respiratory illness, could potentially influence both fuel choice and health outcomes.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that socioeconomic status, particularly location, income, and education, is the primary determinant of cooking fuel choice in Nigeria, with profound implications for respiratory health. A key finding is the health gradient among fuels; while all biomass is harmful, charcoal and kerosene users show a lower risk of ARIs than firewood users, highlighting their role as transitional fuels. Despite this, over 90% of Nigerians rely on these polluting fuels, trapped by the high cost and poor availability of clean alternatives like LPG and electricity. A multi-faceted approach essential to address this crisis include: launch public health campaigns on the dangers of firewood and promote cleaner transitional fuels; subsidise LPG and expand its distribution to rural areas; and invest in education and income-generation programmes to enable a permanent shift to clean energy.

These actions are vital for achieving Sustainable Development Goals 3 (Health) and 7 (Clean Energy). Future research should explore cleaning biomass technologies and utilize longitudinal data to confirm long-term health impacts.
