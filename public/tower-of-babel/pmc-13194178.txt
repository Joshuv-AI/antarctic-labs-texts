Early atherogenesis: In search of etiology
Authors: Paul A. Brown
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Atherosclerosis is a complex chronic inflammatory disease that affects the larger arteries. Early atherogenesis involves a group of pathological processes including endothelial activation and dysfunction, lipoprotein transcytosis and modification, monocyte recruitment and transmigration, and foam cell formation, culminating in fatty streak formation. These processes are mediated by numerous molecules, which could be considered potential diagnostic and therapeutic targets. Atherogenic processes are also driven by several risk factors, including the demographic factors age and sex; family history; dyslipidemia and related chronic lifestyle diseases; unhealthy behaviors related to exercise, diet, alcohol intake, smoking, and sleep; microbial activity; air pollution; and disturbed hemodynamics including low or oscillatory wall shear stress. However, despite our growing knowledge of atherogenesis, the etiology of atherosclerosis remains unsettled. This review first describes early atherogenic processes and their key mediators. The complexity of atherogenesis is then briefly explored to illustrate the uncertainties surrounding the initiation of atherogenesis, including the complex relationships between the evidence-based risk factors identified and atherogenic processes. Finally, the etiology of this important pathology is explored by applying established criteria for causation, including temporality, strength of association, plausibility, and experimental evidence, to the identified risk factors. The review concludes that of all the evidence-based risk factors identified, disturbed wall shear stress is the only one that approximates the criteria for causation, and ends with an overview of the implications.

Background

Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory condition that has been documented in mummified human remains dated centuries ago [1]. Despite our prolonged exposure, this pathology remains a dominant contributor to cardiovascular and overall mortality [2], implying that an improved understanding of the pathogenic mechanisms involved remains a healthcare priority. However, atherogenesis is highly complex, characterized by the development of several pathogenic processes involving a myriad of molecules and associated with several risk factors [[3], [4], [5]]. In addition, there is heterogeneity in the expression of key genes for atherosclerosis and cellular enrichment between arterial beds, which implies inter-arterial pathogenic diversity [6].

Considering the pathogenic complexity and lack of a cure, prevention assumes great importance. Secondary prevention of atherosclerotic disease involves aggressive individually-tailored interventions following diagnostic evaluation to document established lesions [7,8]. This contrasts with primary prevention, which lacks emphasis on identification of early atherogenesis [8,9]. This lack of emphasis on objective evidence of early atherogenic processes from endothelial activation to foam cell formation, and their molecular drivers, arguably represents a lethal gap that allows this highly fatal pathology [2], to go unnoticed. Further, given the promiscuity of molecular functions, targeting down-stream mediators risks inadequate, unfavorable, and unpredictable outcomes [10,11]. Together, these observations highlight a pressing need to understand and target the earliest stages of atherogenesis, in search of etiology.

Atherosclerosis has been extensively reviewed. These reviews often present broad overviews [3,5,12,13] or describe specific pathogenic processes e.g. endothelial dysfunction [14], lipoprotein transcytosis [15,16], immunity [17], or inflammation [[18], [19], [20]]. However, a focused but comprehensive overview of early atherogenesis is less often undertaken, despite the importance of understanding the initiation of atherosclerosis as the basis for exploring potential etiologic factors. The objectives of this review were therefore to summarize recent reviews on the pathogenesis of early atherosclerosis and to identify evidence-based risk factors associated with early atherogenic processes that would then be explored for their etiologic potential. To achieve these objectives, this review presents an initial overview of the major pathologic processes and associated molecular drivers, from endothelial activation to foam cell formation. This is followed by briefly exploring the lack of clarity regarding the etiology of atherosclerosis, before discussing the likely etiology based on established criteria of causation, including temporality, strength of association, plausibility, and experimental evidence [21]. The review concludes that of all the evidence-based risk factors identified, disturbed wall shear stress is the only one that approximates the criteria for causation, and ends with a discussion of the implications. It is hoped that this approach will serve as a useful resource for researchers dedicated to exploring targeted options for the primary prevention of atherosclerotic disease.

Methodology

This review was based on two searches of PubMed. The first search was aimed at identifying recent reviews on the pathogenesis of early atherosclerosis. The search term used was atherosclerosis [Title] AND pathophysiology [Title] and the results were limited by the following filters: Publication Date = January 2021 – December 2025, Article type = Review, Article Language = English; and Species = Humans. The identified article abstracts were assessed and filtered based on relevance to the review objectives. A total of 13 unique articles were identified. Of these, 4 articles were excluded because of lack of relevance to the review objectives, or scope focused on limited aspect of atherogenesis in a specific patient group. The remaining 9 articles, and a review of their references, were used to summarize our current knowledge of early atherogenic processes and supplement the results of the second search as outlined below.

The second search was aimed at identifying evidence-based risk factors of early atherogenesis. The search terms included risk factor [Title/Abstract], risk factors [Title/Abstract], predictor [Title/Abstract], predictors [Title/Abstract], hemodynamics [Title/Abstract], and haemodynamics [Title/Abstract] each in combination with atherogenesis [Title/Abstract] and early atherogenesis [Title/Abstract]. Each search was limited by the following filters: Article type = Books and Documents, Meta-Analysis, Randomized Controlled Trial, and Systematic Review; Article Language = English; and Species = Humans. The identified article abstracts were assessed and filtered based on relevance to the objectives of the review. A total of 56 unique articles were identified, spanning from 1990 to 2026. Of these, 2 articles were reviews, and a further 34 articles were excluded because of lack of relevance to the review objectives, scope broader than initiation of atherogenesis or limited to a specific patient group, or negative results. The remaining 20 articles were used to identify evidence-based risk factors associated with the initiation of atherogenesis that could be explored for their etiologic potential.

Pathogenic processes

Atherosclerosis is now understood as a complex chronic disease of larger arteries, with reasonable consensus on the major pathogenic processes involved in its initiation and early development. These include endothelial activation and dysfunction, lipoprotein endothelial cell (EC) transcytosis and modification, monocyte recruitment and transmigration, impaired lipoprotein efflux/reverse cholesterol transport (RCT), and foam cell formation [[3], [4], [5]]. Atherogenic risk factors include the demographic factors age and sex [22], family history [[23], [24], [25], [26]], dyslipidemia [[27], [28], [29], [30]] including elevated lipoprotein (a) [23], hypertension [28,31], diabetes [28,29,32], obesity [28,29], inadequate physical activity [33], sub-optimal nutrition [[34], [35], [36], [37], [38], [39], [40]], microbial infection [[41], [42], [43]], dysbiosis [44,45] metabolites including trimethylamine N-oxide [45], excessive alcohol intake [46], smoking [47], air pollution [48,49], and poor sleep hygiene [50]. Disturbed hemodynamics, which involves low or oscillatory wall shear stress (WSS), also appears to play a pivotal role [5,51,52], though dissenting voices exist, as reviewed by Peiffer et al. [53].

Endothelial activation and dysfunction

The normal endothelium is lined by ECs, which under conditions of homeostasis promotes vasodilation, while moderating lipid infiltration, leukocyte adhesion and migration, oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, and thrombosis [5,52]. Endothelial activation is characterized by EC expression of adhesion molecules e.g. vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1), intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), P-selectin, and E-selectin, as well as pro-inflammatory mediators [54,55]. Persistent activation can evolve into endothelial dysfunction, which involves structural endothelial damage including EC apoptosis [55]. Disruption of endothelial structure and hence function results in loss of homeostasis leading to decreased synthesis, release, and/or activity of endothelium-derived nitric oxide (NO) and therefore impaired vasodilatation, as well as enhanced endothelial permeability, lipoprotein infiltration, leukocyte adhesion, oxidative stress, inflammation, and platelet activation [5,14,[54], [55], [56], [57], [58], [59], [60]].

Atherogenic risk factors can impact endothelial function. For example, a recent correlation study among coronary heart disease patients reported a significant negative association between total cholesterol and endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) [61]. However, unlike the likely systemic impact of plasma lipid or other non-hemodynamic factors, shear stress has the potential to alter vascular biology in a location-specific manner, with laminar shear stress associated with expression of antioxidative and anti-inflammatory genes [62]. Accordingly, disturbed flow induces EC gene expression that favors endothelial activation and dysfunction [5,54]. This includes induction/sustained activation of proinflammatory molecules, adhesion molecules e.g. VCAM-1, VSMC chemo-attractants, the endothelium permeability enhancer vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and many other potentially atherogenic factors [63,64]. Disturbed flow also suppresses the oxidative stress-response molecule heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1.) [63] Such gene expression is mediated by several transcription factors [63,65]. Lipoprotein EC transcytosis and modification

Atherogenesis is driven by EC uptake of lipoproteins including LDL and remnants of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (TLRs) [88]. TLRs have a triglyceride and cholesterol ester core, as well as phospholipids, free cholesterol, and Apos within a surface monolayer [89]. LDL particles comprise a core consisting of cholesteryl ester, triglyceride, and unesterified cholesterol molecules, and a surface layer with a single copy of apolipoprotein B-100 (ApoB-100), unesterified cholesterol and phospholipid molecules [90]. ApoB-100 remains an integrated component of the LDL structure as it traverses the vascular system and tissues [91]. As illustrated in Fig. 1, EC transcytosis of LDL, the major transporter of circulating cholesterol, is either direct or indirect [15]. The process is mediated by EC-associated molecules including activin receptor-like kinase 1 (ALK1), scavenger receptor B1 (SR-BI), and caveolin-1 throughout the systemic circulation, as well as the LDL receptor (LDLR) in brain capillary ECs which facilitate LDL transcytosis across the blood-brain barrier [15,16]. ALK1, SR-BI, and LDLR mediate receptor-specific indirect transcytosis, which involves binding of LDL molecules [15,66]. ALK1 appears to bind the ApoB-100 LDL component, facilitating colocalization of the ALK1-LDL complex during an endocytic process, that by avoiding lysosomal degradation, promotes LDL transcytosis [92]. Indeed, LDL uptake and transcytosis were blocked by a selective anti-ALK1 monoclonal antibody in a murine model [93]. With respect to SR-BI, its binding of LDL leads to recruitment of dedicator of cytokinesis 4 (DOCK4) and Ras-Related C3 botulinum toxin substrate 1 (Rac1) activation that enhances SR-BI endocytic internalization and subsequent LDL transcytosis [66,67]. Both SR-B1 and DOCK4 expression are increased in the atheroprone murine aortic arch lesser curvature prior to lesion formation [66]. Unlike the classic clathrin-coated pit-mediated LDLR pathway [94], LDLR-mediated LDL transcytosis occurs across brain capillary ECs, is blocked by the C7 anti-LDLR monoclonal antibody [95], and is likely not significant in atheroprone systemic arteries [96]. Finally, caveolin-associated caveolae-mediated transcytosis is direct, though associated with both ALK1 and SR-BI [15,16,97].

Hypertension has been shown to promote LDL uptake more so in higher-pressure proximal artery regions, which may explain the predilection of atherosclerosis to larger arteries [98], However, hemodynamics, which is a local risk factor [5], appears to influence location-specific lipoprotein transcytosis. Disturbed flow was associated with increased caveolin-1 expression, significantly less caveolae within the apical plasma membrane, and significantly more intracellular and basolateral caveolae, in the atheroprone murine aortic lesser curvature [94]. Not surprisingly, disturbed WSS regions tend to overlap with regions of increased endothelial permeability [52,88,99] and lipoprotein uptake [69,88,98], which could partly explain the predilection of atherosclerosis to specific locations within larger arteries.

LDL molecules trapped within the subendothelial space can be modified, including oxidization by free radicals, a process enhanced by relative absence of protective antioxidants [5]. However, LDL oxidation is also facilitated by the membrane-associated LDL receptor-related protein (LRP) that binds LDL and mediates translocation of 12/15-lipoxygenase to the macrophage membrane, where it oxidizes the LDL cholesterol esters [68].

LDL transcytosis is countered by cholesterol efflux from lipid-laden foam cells, the first stage of RCT [15,82,83,100,101], that delivers cholesterol to the liver and subsequently the intestine, from where it is either reabsorbed or excreted [91,102]. This process involves efflux of cholesterol from peripheral cells including macrophages, by HDL and its primary protein apolipoprotein A-1 (ApoA-1), both of which must undergo transcytosis across ECs to gain access to the subendothelial space [15]. The mature HDL particle comprises a hydrophobic lipid core with cholesteryl esters and triglyceride, and a surface layer with apoproteins, primarily ApoA-1, and phospholipid molecules [103,104]. Unlike ApoB-100, ApoA-1 can dissociate from the HDL, allowing it to interact independently with peripheral cells including ECs [91]. As shown in Fig. 1, HDL transcytosis across ECs is mediated by the cholesterol transporters SR-B1 and ATP-binding cassette transporter G1 (ABCG1), while ApoA-1 transcytosis is mediated by the cholesterol transporter ABCA1 [15,16]. HDL transcytosis across ECs occurs via the endosomal pathway [105]. With respect to ApoA-1 EC transcytosis, ABCA1 has been shown to mediate ApoA-1 binding to bovine aortic endothelial cells as well as colocalize with ApoA-1 in intracellular vesicles [106]. Monocyte recruitment and transmigration

Monocyte recruitment (Fig. 2) involves rolling along the endothelium, activation, adhesion to ECs, and transmigration into the subendothelial space [5] and involve several molecular drivers (Table 1). Rolling is mediated primarily by the interaction between EC surface-bound P-Selectin and the leukocyte ligand P-selectin glycoprotein ligand-1 (PSGL-1) [70], during which monocytes become activated by EC membrane-bound chemokines, resulting in monocyte adhesion to the endothelium via interaction between monocyte integrins (e.g. α4β1) and EC membrane-bound VCAM-I and ICAM-I [5,70]. The interaction between the integrin α4β1 and VCAM-1 leads to activation of myosin light-chain kinase (MLCK) and protein kinase C α (PKCα), with subsequent phosphorylation and dissociation of CDH5 from the actin cytoskeleton within adherens junctions (AJs) [[74], [75], [76]]. The resulting EC contraction and weakened AJs, enhances paracellular transmigration [74,75]. This contrasts with transcellular transmigration, the less frequent route, which involves passage through individual ECs via transcellular pores associated with caveolin-1 and ICAM-1 clustering [77,107]. However, disturbed flow induces cytokines and adhesion molecules (e.g. ICAM-1, VCAM-1 and Selectin) leading to monocyte adhesion to ECs as well as disruption of adherens junctions, which facilitates paracellular transmigration. Within the context of elevated blood pressure, connexin43 (Cx43) has been shown to induce PI3K/AKT/NF-κB-dependent VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 expression, which enhanced monocyte-EC adhesion [71]. On the contrary, monocyte Cx37 activity has been associated with reduced adhesion [72] and ox-LDL reduces expression of monocyte Cx37 but enhances expression of Cx43, thereby promoting monocyte adhesion to ECs [73]. Low and oscillatory WSS also appear crucial for these processes. An early leporine study reported substantial localized monocyte attachment to, and migration across, the common carotid endothelium particularly at cell-cell junctions, in response to ligation-induced low shear stress [108]. Mechanistically, this could be explained by a subsequent murine model that demonstrated increased VCAM-I and ICAM-I expression in low WSS regions of the aorta [109]. More recent reviews outline several potential mechanisms of disturbed flow, including the reduction of vascular EC coverage, induction of pro-inflammatory gene expression including cytokines and adhesion molecules, and protracted contact between circulating leukocytes and ECs, thereby enhancing mononuclear leukocyte adhesion and transmigration (Fig. 2) [52,110].

Foam cell formation and pro-inflammatory signaling

Once within the subendothelial space, monocytes differentiate into macrophages, with either the M1 (pro-inflammatory) or M2 (anti-inflammatory) phenotype [5]. For example, Angiotensin II induces M1 macrophage polarization via the Cx43/NF-κB pathway [79]. Although macrophages can accumulate cholesterol from the uptake of unmodified native LDL [111], scavenger receptors, including CD36, CD-14, SR-A and lectin-like oxLDL receptor-1 (LOX-1), mediate uptake of modified LDL [80,81]. Potential toxicity is prevented by intracellular processing of both exogenous, including lipoprotein-derived, and endogenous cholesterol [82]. Lysosomal acid lipase hydrolyses oxLDL-derived cholesterol esters within the endolysosomal compartment, yielding free cholesterol that is then re-esterified by acyl CoA: cholesterol acyltransferase 1 (ACAT1) and stored as lipid droplets including within the endoplasmic reticulum [80]. Cholesterol esters undergo secondary hydrolysis by neutral cholesterol ester hydrolases (e.g., NCEH1 and nCEH) yielding free cholesterol that can be trafficked to the plasma membrane to be effluxed, or re-esterified [80,82]. Cholesterol efflux from lipid-laden foam cells (Fig. 1, Fig. 3), represents the first stage of RCT [15,82,83,100], with cholesterol accepted from ABCA1 by ApoA-1 and from SR-BI and ABCG1 by HDL [80]. These processes are quite complex, whereby cholesterol transporters shuttle free cholesterol between cytoplasmic organelles including the endolysosomal compartment (ABCA1 [83]) and endoplasmic reticulum (ABCG1 [83]), and the plasma membrane [112,113]. Once received by the cholesterol acceptors, free cholesterol is again re-esterified to prevent reuptake [82,83], and transported across ECs for delivery to the liver [91]. Disruption of this cycle (Fig. 3), results in cholesterol accumulation within macrophages, which facilitates foam cell formation [5,80]. In addition to monocytes, intimal vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) also contribute substantially to the foam cell population following uptake of modified LDL molecules [114]. There is evidence that cholesterol-loaded VSMCs undergo transcriptomic changes associated with loss of expression of VSMC markers while simultaneously developing into macrophage-like cells [86]. This phenotypic switch, from contractile to proliferative/synthetic phenotype, also facilitates migration from media to intima and production of extracellular matrix components [59]. The potential of macrophage-like cells of VSMC lineage to develop into foam cells might be explained by several mechanisms which limit cholesterol efflux [82,87]. Atherosclerosis is also an important example of a sterile inflammatory disease, characterized by full activation of the nod-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome that requires 2 signals: priming and activating assembly of the inflammasome complex of NLRs [81]. Activation of the inflammasome complex can be triggered by interaction between CD36 and oxLDL, while the cooperation of CD36 with the TLR heterodimer TLR4-TLR6, which recognize oxLDL, facilitates priming [81,84]. Priming consists of transcriptional induction of NLRP3 and pro-IL-1β, while NLRP3 inflammasome activation involves uptake of oxLDL, incorporation within the endolysosomal compartment, and subsequent cholesterol crystallization that induces lysosomal rupture and inflammasome activation [81,84]. Free cholesterol is readily crystallized [81], hence cholesterol overload within the endolysosomal compartment can result in crystal formation, inducing lysosomal destabilization and rupture and subsequent NLRP3 activation [80,81,84]. NF-κB- and reactive oxygen species-induced NLRP3 inflammasome priming leads to caspase-1 dependent induction of IL-1β and IL-18 [13,60,81].

Disturbed flow appears to aid inflammation (Fig. 3). For example, a murine model demonstrated increased VCAM-I and ICAM-I expression in low WSS regions of the aorta [109], which by enhancing monocyte recruitment and transmigration, could facilitate vascular inflammation. Low WSS has also been shown to enhance caveolin-1-dependent Notch1 expression and NF-κB-dependent inflammatory responses, including secretion of IL-1β and IL-6 [85].

Other immune cells are involved in inflammatory atherogenic responses. For example, as recently reviewed by Ajoolabady et al., [102] dendritic cell cytokine-mediated induction of T-helper cells drives the macrophage response and stimulates recruitment of other leukocytes, thereby aggravating atherogenesis. Similarly, neutrophils when present, may facilitate monocyte/macrophage recruitment, and neutrophil extracellular traps may facilitate macrophage pro-inflammatory signaling including inflammasome formation [60,115]. In addition, Abdolmaleki et al. outlined the role of various immune cells including macrophages, T, B, and mast cells, as well as eosinophils, in atherosclerotic development and progression, mediated by cytokines and antibodies, e.g. IgE [17]. Immune cells also interact with fibroblasts, which are known to express high levels of inflammatory genes [101]. For example, as reviewed by Faa et al., [116] myofibroblasts, activated adventitial fibroblasts, migrate into the intima where they recruit and activate resident immune cells via production of proinflammatory cytokines, e.g. IL-1. The fatty streak

Substantial intracellular (macrophage- and VSMC-derived foam cells) and extracellular accumulation of lipids within the intima produces the earliest gross atherosclerotic lesion, the fatty streak [5,13,58]. This is the nidus from which more advanced lesions develop [5,13].

A complex start to a complex pathology

For a minority of patients, atherogenic risk is straightforward, including being driven by genetic (e.g. familial hypercholesterolemia) or immunologic (e.g. post-allotransplantation) factors [117]. However, for most patients, atherogenesis is much more complex. Therefore, although the preceding section presented a series of atherogenic processes in a familiar order, atherosclerosis is rightly described as a complex pathology, and this description is no more apt than at the earliest stages of pathogenesis.

Several risk factors for atherogenesis have been identified. A few are non-modifiable, e.g. the demographic factors age and sex [22], and family history [[23], [24], [25], [26]]. Others, including dyslipidemia [23,[27], [28], [29], [30]], hypertension [28,31], diabetes [28,29,32], obesity [28,29], inadequate physical activity [33], sub-optimal nutrition [[34], [35], [36], [37], [38], [39], [40]], microbial infection, [[41], [42], [43]], dysbiosis [44,45], metabolites including trimethylamine N-oxide [45], excessive alcohol intake [46], smoking [47], air pollution [48,49], and poor sleep hygiene [50], are considered modifiable and therefore potential targets for management. In addition, disturbed flow may be a pivotal player [5,51,52], and being largely a function of vascular anatomy [52,110,[118], [119], [120]], can be considered a substantially non-modifiable risk factor.

The current model of atherogenesis considers systemic dyslipidemia to be a key early trigger, with the atherogenic process being driven largely by elevated circulating lipoproteins including LDL [[3], [4], [5]]. However, the primary mechanism responsible for the initiation of atherogenesis is not settled. Multiple hypotheses have been proffered over the years, including the early “humoral disease theory” of Carl Rokitansky and “cellular inflammatory theory” of Rudolf Virchow, and later the “response to injury,” “altered lipoprotein,” “retention of modified LDL,” “inflammation/autoimmunity,” [117] and “low shear,” [121,122] hypotheses. This plethora of theories and hypotheses underscores the complexities that have been tackled throughout the history of atherosclerosis research, and reflect a degree of obscurity regarding early atherogenesis and the ultimate etiology of atherosclerosis.

The complex relationships between putative risk factors and atherogenic processes

Atherogenic risk factors drive multiple processes associated with early atherogenesis. Hypertension, a major risk factor, may enhance atherogenesis by several mechanisms including oxidative stress [123], and inducing mechanical EC injury, intimal LDL permeability and EC monocyte adhesion [124]. Similarly, diabetes-driven atherogenesis involves shear stress-induced EC reprogramming potentially promoting oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammation, hyperglycemia-, advanced glycation end products (AGE)-, and renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system-mediated oxidative stress, as well as trained immunity-induced inflammation promoted by epigenetic modifications [125]. Smoking also promotes oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction associated with reduced NO bioavailability but increased production of superoxide anions and endothelin, as well as vascular inflammation via multiple mechanisms including NLRP3 inflammasome activation induced by damage- and pathogen-associated molecular patterns, and Toll-like receptor activation [126]. Similarly, obesity, which is often associated with lack of physical activity and an unhealthy diet, is associated with diverse atherogenic processes. Adipocyte hypertrophy, a key feature of obesity has been associated with immune and inflammatory pathways e.g. NF-kB signaling and pro-inflammatory gene expression including TNF-α and IL-6, but reduced respiratory chain gene expression with the potential to exacerbate ROS production and oxidative stress [127].

Together, these findings demonstrate that atherogenic risk factors can drive similar pathogenic processes. For example, they all appear capable of promoting oxidative stress. Similarly, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, visceral fat, and smoking, can potentially enhance production of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-18 and IL-1β via stimulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome by various danger signals, not just cholesterol crystals [18]. The shared mediation of atherogenic processes by various risk factors adds a layer of complexity with respect to the etiologic potential of any single factor. However, there is evidence that implies a pre-existing driver, that sits upstream of other factors.

In search of etiology

Functional promiscuity within biological systems implies that targeting downstream disease mediators in complex pathogenic processes may result in inadequate, unfavorable, and unpredictable outcomes, and therefore less effective interventions [10,11]. This principle underscores the utility implicit in identifying the earliest driver: the etiological factor. Etiology can be defined as the science of disease causation, with a given cause referring to the entity that leads to the disease: A cause is considered “necessary” if its absence precludes disease occurrence, or “sufficient” if its presence inevitably produces disease [128]. When assessing disease causation, crucial criteria include temporality (the presumed cause must precede the disease process), strength of association (between the cause and disease occurrence), plausibility (a causal association must be reasonable considering other options), and experimental evidence [21].

Temporality

A confident case for temporality can only be made if the risk factor is expected to precede evidence of the initiation of atherogenesis. Early atherosclerotic lesions, include fatty streaks as well as intimal thickening (IT) and associated increased intima-media thickness (IMT) [[129], [130], [131], [132]]. Fatty streaks, which may precede or contribute to IIMT [130], have been documented in the second to third trimesters [133]. Similarly, coronary artery IT has been demonstrated in the early second [132] and third [134] trimesters. However, IMT is generally not demonstrable in the first trimester [135], leaving open the possibility of atherogenesis in very young fetuses. With respect to postnatal atherogenesis, there is evidence that fatty streaks may regress during the latter stages of gestation and/or during early postnatal life (children <3 years old) [136]. However, fatty streaks rebound postnatally in diverse populations [137]; and were found in the vast majority (87%) of aortas in the 5-14 age group in a large multinational sample representing 5 WHO regions [138]. Temporality can therefore be assessed with respect to both fetal and postnatal atherogenesis.

Non-modifiable risk factors, e.g. increasing age, sex [22], and family history [[23], [24], [25], [26]], are clearly preexisting characteristics of the post-fertilization zygote and neonate, and therefore a case for temporality appears reasonable with respect to subsequent fetal and postnatal atherogenesis. Regarding hemodynamics, although blood flow pattern is not present at the earliest stages of development, it is largely a function of vascular anatomy [52,110,[118], [119], [120]], and disturbed WSS would therefore closely follow vasculogenesis. Analysis of human embryos suggest that cardiac-mediated circulation begins possibly as early as the 4th week post-fertilization (6th week of gestation [139]), and atherogenesis by definition is not possible before this occurs. Similarly, blood flow patterns would be present at birth, before the subsequent resurgence of atherogenesis (cf. [137], [138]) As a result, disturbed WSS appears to meet the criterion of temporality.

The early fetus may also be exposed to modifiable atherogenic risk factors, which can be transferred from the maternal circulation. For example, the corresponding maternal exposure or disorder can result in elevated blood pressure in fetal arteries [140], as well as fetal hyperglycemia [141], hypercholesterolemia [133,142], or pollution risk [143]. Fetoplacental [144] and uteroplacental [145] circulation is established towards the latter first trimester, at which time effective exchange across the placenta begins [144,146], thereby facilitating the impact of these risk factors. Similarly, neonatal hyperglycemia [147], obesity [148], hypercholesterolemia [149], and low birth weight [150], as well as childhood physical inactivity, compromised sleep, tobacco exposure [150], alcohol abuse [151], infections like Chlamydia pneumoniae [152], and dysbiosis [88], may occur in early postnatal life before the subsequent resurgence of atherogenesis (cf. [137], [138]) Therefore, the identified modifiable atherogenic risk factors may also meet the criterion of temporality.

Strength of association

Of course, temporality does not prove causation. However, the weight of temporality is enhanced by strong association between potential risk factors and atherogenesis. Earlier studies suggest the association between most risk factors and early atherosclerosis is far from perfect. The Bogalusa Heart Study reported significant unadjusted correlations between aortic fatty streak involvement and total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, and HDL/LDL + VLDL ratio, as well as between coronary fatty streak involvement and triglycerides, LDL cholesterol, VLDL cholesterol, and blood pressure [153]. Only the correlations between aortic fatty streak burden and dyslipidemia-related variables demonstrated large effect size (r ≥ 0.5) [154], but moderate correlation (r < 0.65) [155]. The Fate of Early Lesions in Children (FELIC) study, identified age, birthweight, total cholesterol, hypertension, and maternal history of coronary heart disease, as significantly correlated with fatty streak size [136]. However, as with the Bogalusa Heart Study, all correlations failed to reach large effect size (r ≥ 0.5) [154], with the exception of a moderate correlation for age (r = 0.65) [155]. There were also moderate (r = 0.52 and r = 0.61) correlations [155], between insomnia and left and right carotid artery intima-media thickness, respectively. However, child sex and type 1 diabetes, as well as maternal type 1 diabetes, hypertension, and smoking were not significantly correlated with fatty streak size [136]. The above observations imply these atherogenic risk factors may not be sufficient to initiate atherogenesis. In addition, some pregnancies are not complicated by risk factors, with early atherosclerotic fetal lesions found in the absence of dyslipidemia [131,133], smoking [131], and advanced maternal age [[131], [132], [133],136]. Lesions have also been documented in both male and female fetuses [132], as well as in the absence of family history [24], physical inactivity [156], poor diet [157], alcohol use [46], and Chlamydia pneumoniae infection [41] as well as in low air pollution regions [158]. Further, although antibiotic-mediated suppression of murine intestinal microflora blocked choline- [159] and carnitine-mediated [160] atherosclerosis enhancement, it did not regress aortic lesions. Together, the observations imply that these risk factors may not be necessary for atherogenesis. This may seem counter-intuitive especially with respect to dyslipidemia, a risk factor integral to lipoprotein EC transcytosis [88]. However, the fact that atherogenesis occurs in the presence of normocholesterolemia implies that LDL transcytosis can occur at normal cholesterol levels [133]. Similarly, although triglyceride-rich lipoprotein-related genetic variants are strongly associated with CVD risk [89], there is evidence of normal triglyceride levels in some patients with established atherosclerotic CVD [161].

With respect to hemodynamic risk, unlike other atherogenic risk factors, WSS is not a systemically uniform variable. Therefore, the relevant association is that between disturbed WSS at sites of perturbed flow and early atherogenesis, and in this regard, early evidence suggests strong associations. A 1983 study reported maximal intimal thickness (0.9 ± 0.1 mm) along the outer wall of the carotid bifurcation where WSS ranged from 0 to −6 dyn/cm2 at the carotid sinus midpoint, in contrast with minimal thickness (0.05 ± 0.02 mm) at and within 15° of the flow divider where WSS ranged from 31 to 160 dyn/cm2 [162]. In 1990, Asakura and Karino demonstrated that along the human coronary arterial tree, atherosclerotic wall thickening and plaques were found almost exclusively along the outer wall at major bifurcations and T-junctions, as well as along the inner wall of curved segments, both characterized by low WSS. Equally important was the observation that atherosclerotic lesions were never found at the high WSS flow-divider of branching vessels [118], suggesting low WSS is “necessary.” Early quantitative work by Ku et al., in 1985, reported significant and very strong correlations [155] between intimal thickness and the inverse of maximum shear stress (r = 0.90) and mean shear stress (r = 0.82) as well as oscillatory shear index (r = 0.82), and near perfect correlation [155], if analysis was limited to inner and outer (lowest minimum WSS) carotid sinus wall measurements (r = 0.94, 0.95, and 0.93 respectively) [163]. Subsequently in 1997, an analysis of abdominal aortas of young adults aged 18-40 years, demonstrated significant and very strong correlations [155], between intimal thickness and mean (rest and exercise) minimum WSS (r = −0.93) and oscillatory shear index (r = 0.92) [164].

The apparent strength of association suggested by these earlier studies has been questioned. In their systemic review of 27 studies, Peiffer et al. argued that some authors restricted their study to descriptive analysis or to a subset of the data, while others utilized different WSS thresholds and measured different outcomes (lesion occurrence vs. severity) [53]. Although these are all valid concerns that complicate interpretation, they do not disprove an association. Further, Peiffer et al. limited studies to those that utilized computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to simulate blood flow [53]. On the contrary, the four early studies used other non-invasive methods of measuring flow metrics. These included laser doppler anemometry [[162], [163], [164]] and flow visualization and cinematography [118], which are capable of generating full-field velocity profiles [165], and are used to validate CFD simulations [165,166]. In addition, although the study by Peiffer et al. reported five studies that did not find evidence of any association between flow metrics and early disease, all five utilized metrics derived from diseased geometries [53]. The systemic review also included studies at various stages of atherogenesis from wall thickness to plaques and included non-human studies [53], while the early studies previously cited [118,[162], [163], [164]], involved human data and three [118,162,164] involved intimal thickness, which is more relevant when assessing associations of flow metrics with the initiation of atherogenesis.

Finally, a recent study in 2018, involving patients with nonobstructive coronary artery disease (median age = 52) demonstrated a strong association (OR: 9.14; p < 0.001) between low WSS in the coronary artery and the very early atherosclerotic change of severe endothelial dysfunction, using multivariable logistic regression to control for appropriate risk factors including age, sex, dyslipidemia, diabetes, hypertension, and smoking status. This strong association surpassed that of the other two identified independent predictors: diabetes (OR: 5.01) and female sex (OR: 2.44) [167]. The latter study also reported that no segment with low WSS was characterized by normal endothelial function [167], suggesting that low WSS may be “sufficient” to initiate atherogenesis.

Plausibility and experimental evidence: mechanistic analysis of risk factors

If disturbed WSS meets the criterion of temporality and is strongly associated with atherogenesis, the case for a casual impact also requires plausible pathogenic mechanisms. Given the plethora of risk factors, attributing causation to any, is strengthened by establishing antecedence. In this regard, although fetal circulation is estimated to be established by the 6th week of gestation [139], fetoplacental [144] and uteroplacental [145] circulation is not established until weeks later towards the end of the first trimester [144,146]. This implies that disturbed flow precedes exposure to modifiable risk factors in utero. Similarly, since in-utero hemodynamic patterns presumably persist as a function of vascular anatomy [52,110,[118], [119], [120]] into early postnatal life, disturbed flow also precedes other risk factors postnatally.

Mechanistically, straight arterial segments are characterized by ordered laminar flow, which promotes normal EC biology consistent with an atheroprotective phenotype [52]. On the contrary, vascular curvatures, branches, or bifurcations are characterized by disturbed flow, and are prone to atherogenesis [5,52,63]. Atherosclerosis-prone regions are associated with arterial shear stress <4 dyn/cm2, much lower than normal arterial shear stress of 10 – 70 dyn/cm2 [122]. These sites are characterized by changes in endothelial function [52], with EC gene expression that mediates endothelial activation and dysfunction [5,54], including loss of laminar flow-induced upregulation of eNOS [63]. Low WSS also coincides with regions of increased endothelial permeability [99], high LDL uptake [98], and suppression of the oxidative stress-response molecule HO-1 and laminar flow-induced downregulation of VCAM-1 [63]. The latter are consistent with evidence that oscillatory shear promotes oxidative stress, and subsequently vascular damage and inflammation [168]. Similarly, low WSS enhances caveolin-1-dependent Notch1 expression and NF-κB-dependent secretion of IL-1β and IL-6, also promoting vascular inflammation [85]. Low and oscillatory shear stress promotes location-specific vascular inflammation by several mechanisms, including reduced coverage of vascular ECs, pro-inflammatory gene expression, and enhanced mononuclear leukocyte adhesion and transmigration [110].

Flow-mediated atherogenic mechanisms are facilitated by the fact that vascular ECs contain mechanosensors [52,169]. Shear-sensitive structures include ion channels, focal adhesions, cell-matrix and cell-cell junctional components, receptor–tyrosine kinases e.g. VEGF receptor, G-protein-coupled receptors, as well as the glycocalyx, caveolae, and primary cilia [52,169,170]. Disturbed flow is detected by these mechanosensitive EC proteins and structures, stimulating mechanotransduction pathways that appear to reprogram ECs towards an atherogenic phenotype [51]. Integrated single-cell RNA sequencing as well as single-cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing demonstrate that disturbed flow induced new EC subclusters, increased VSMCs, monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, and T cells, as well as led to transcriptomic changes associated with endothelial dysfunction, endothelial inflammation, and endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition [51]. Similarly, the annexin A2/integrin α5 interaction promotes oscillatory WSS -induced integrin α5β1 translocation to lipid rafts, with subsequent EC activation and monocyte adhesion [170].

Taken together, the above evidence suggests that the concept of disturbed WSS as etiologic is not only plausible, but well-grounded in experimental mechanistic evidence. An etiologic role of disturbed WSS would also help to explain the known anatomic predisposition of atherosclerosis [5,52,63].

Etiologic potential and limitations of the “low WSS-induction hypothesis”

The evidence implies that disturbed WSS is the only evidence-based atherogenic risk factor identified that meets or approaches the definition of “sufficient” and “necessary,” and has a reasonable potential to fulfill the criteria for causation. Being largely a function of vascular anatomy [52,110,[118], [119], [120]], implies that disturbed WSS precedes other risk factors, and is capable of plausibly initiating atherogenesis. However, this does not mean that low WSS initiates a linear pathogenic process. Indeed, research has shown that other risk factors may influence flow metrics. For example, carotid artery WSS has been shown to be altered in persons with other atherosclerosis risk factors, with a significant positive correlation between mean WSS and HDL cholesterol, but significant negative correlations between mean WSS and age, fasting blood glucose, systolic blood pressure, smoking, and number of risk factors, with the latter associated with mean WSS independent of carotid IMT [171]. Therefore, low WSS may induce atherogenesis, but may itself be modulated by other risk factors in the progression of disease. However, this hypothesis has some potential limitations. Firstly, investigation of early atherosclerosis lesions in early fetuses is technically challenging [135], presenting some uncertainty about atherogenesis in very young fetuses regarding the issue of temporality. However, as WSS is influenced by vascular anatomy [52,110,[118], [119], [120]], it seems unlikely that early atherosclerosis could precede disturbed WSS. The precise relationship between low WSS and early atherosclerosis lesions is also not clearly defined, because methodologies used to determine this association vary across some studies [53]. The pattern appears fairly consistent, but greater confidence in this conclusion would be enhanced by methodological consistency. Further, there may be other flow metrics involved in the initiation of atherogenesis that have not been well studied [53,172], which suggest avenues for future research.

Implications: atherosclerosis as the cancer of the vascular system

In 1963, Reiner et al. identified coronary and abdominal aortic arteries as more atheroprone compared with the mesenteric artery [173]. Similarly, in 2006, Sawabe et al. reported differences in atherosclerosis severity between several anatomically distant arteries including intracranial, carotid, coronary, aortic, and femoral arteries [174]. More recently, Yamaguchi and Morino classified arteries into atheroprotective (e.g. the internal thoracic and most upper limb arteries) or atheroprone (e.g. the carotid, vertebral, as well as most thoraco-abdominal and lower limb arteries) [175]. These findings suggest that atherosclerosis differs across the vascular system, such that cerebral atherosclerosis for example, may represent a distinct variant compared with lesions found in the carotid, coronary, aortic, mesenteric, femoral, or other arteries. This location-specific pathologic expression resembles another group of pathologies: cancer. However, unlike cancer, which has multiple etiologies that with the aid of many risk factors drives various pathogenic process (e.g., genome instability, DNA mutations, and tumor-promoting inflammation) [176,177], this review proposes that atherosclerosis may have a primary etiology that with the aid of several risk factors drives multiple pathogenic processes.

A primary etiologic role for hemodynamics in atherogenesis would have several important implications. Being partly a function of vascular anatomy [52,110,[118], [119], [120]], WSS patterns vary between vascular territories [118,162,164]. It is therefore not surprising that atherosclerosis is characterized by heterogeneity between arterial beds, with differential expression of key genes as well as divergent cellular enrichment [6,178], consistent with documented differences in pathologic expression across arteries. For example, primarily foam cell and intermediate lesions have been reported in carotid arteries, contrasting with lipid-cored plaques within coronary arteries, and thickened or normal femoral artery intimae [179]. Similarly, more frequent fibrous cap atheromata and fibrocalcific plaques were reported in carotid and femoral arteries respectively, suggesting higher femoral plaque-associated calcium and lower cholesterol [180].

Inter-artery pathologic heterogeneity is compatible with the artery-specific proclivities demonstrated by several atherogenic risk factors, with hypertension, diabetes, and smoking preferentially impacting the carotid, lower limb, and abdominal aortic arterial territories, respectively [6]. With respect to hemodynamics, being reliant on vascular anatomy [52,110,[118], [119], [120]] also means heterogenous WSS distribution within a particular artery, with the potential to influence intra-artery location-specific atherogenesis. Accordingly, disturbed flow can induce proinflammatory and adhesion molecules e.g. ICAM-1, VCAM-1, and E-Selectin [63]. Similarly, increased endothelial permeability [99] and high LDL uptake [98], coincide with low WSS. Together these findings could partly explain the predilection of atherosclerosis to specific locations within any artery [118] that may be preferentially targeted by other risk factors.

Against this background of diverse pathogenic and subsequently clinical heterogeneity, it is conceivable that clinicians and researchers will come to formally view atherosclerosis, as with cancer, as a group of diseases. Accordingly, they may one day routinely refer to terms like cerebral, carotid, coronary, aortic, mesenteric, femoral, and tibial atherosclerotic disease, as opposed to the generic term “atherosclerosis.” A dominant hemodynamic etiology that underpins pathogenic heterogeneity would also likely explain why the targeting of traditional risk factors has not proven more effective in reducing atherosclerosis-related mortality [2]. Targeted atherosclerosis management, that takes into consideration local flow-driven atherogenic processes and tailored to specific vascular beds, may therefore become the goal of future research. Accordingly, in the vein of “Oncology,” it is tempting to consider whether a new discipline aimed at the management of atherosclerotic disease throughout the body may eventually emerge: perhaps “Atherology,” derived from the combining form “athero” that portrays atheroma/atheromatous in the resulting compound word atherosclerosis [181]. By increasingly treating atherosclerosis as a diverse group of related but location-specific diseases driven by local flow dynamics, we may make our steps more certain and our progress more rapid.

Conclusion

Disturbed WSS is often described as an important driver of atherogenesis [3,62,110]. However, authors often stop short of characterizing hemodynamics as causal, with notable exceptions [5,122,169,182]. This review however, presents evidence that of all the evidence-based risk factors identified in associated with atherogenesis, disturbed WSS is the only one that approximates the criteria for causation.
