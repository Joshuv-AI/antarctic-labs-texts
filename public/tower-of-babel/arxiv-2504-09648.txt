RANSAC Revisited: An Improved Algorithm for Robust Subspace Recovery under Adversarial and Noisy Corruptions
Guixian Chen, Jianhao Ma, Salar Fattahi

Abstract
In this paper, we study the problem of robust subspace recovery (RSR) in the presence of both strong adversarial corruptions and Gaussian noise. Specifically, given a limited number of noisy samples—some of which are tampered by an adaptive and strong adversary—we aim to recover a low-dimensional subspace that approximately contains a significant fraction of the uncorrupted samples, up to an error that scales with the Gaussian noise. Existing approaches to this problem often suffer from high computational costs or rely on restrictive distributional assumptions, limiting their applicability in truly adversarial settings. To address these challenges, we revisit the classical random sample consensus (RANSAC) algorithm, which offers strong robustness to adversarial outliers, but sacrifices efficiency and robustness against Gaussian noise and model misspecification in the process. We propose a two-stage algorithm, RANSAC+, that precisely pinpoints and remedies the failure modes of standard RANSAC. Our method is provably robust to both Gaussian and adversarial corruptions, achieves near-optimal sample complexity without requiring prior knowledge of the subspace dimension, and is more efficient than existing RANSAC-type methods.

Introduction

Modern datasets are generated in increasingly high dimensions and vast quantities. A fundamental approach to analyzing such large-scale, high-dimensional datasets is to represent them using a potentially low-dimensional subspace—a process known as
subspace recovery
. By projecting data onto this subspace, we can significantly reduce its dimensionality while preserving its essential structure and information.
Motivated by this, we focus on the fundamental problem of
robust subspace recovery
(RSR):

Given a collection of
n

\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathcal{S}^{\star}
of dimension
\dim(\mathcal{S}^{\star})=r^{\star}\ll d
that nearly contains a significant portion of the uncorrupted samples?

In this context, we consider one of the most challenging corruption model, namely the

1.2
Summary of contributions

In this work, we revisit the classical RANSAC algorithm, precisely remedying the scenarios where it breaks down. In particular, our goal is to leverage the robustness of RANSAC against adversarial corruptions while mitigating its sensitivity to Gaussian noise, rank misspecification, and its prohibitive computational cost.

Our proposed approach, which we call
two-stage RANSAC
(RANSAC+), consists of two key stages.
In the first stage, we perform an efficient coarse-grained estimation of the underlying subspace, yielding an initial estimate of
\mathcal{S}^{\star}
, denoted as
\mathcal{V}
. While
\mathcal{V}
may not exactly coincide with
\mathcal{S}^{\star}
, it satisfies two important properties: (1) it nearly contains
\mathcal{S}^{\star}
with an error scaling with the noise level, and (2) the dimension
\hat{r}
of
\mathcal{V}
matches the true dimension
r^{\star}
up to a constant factor. Despite its promising performance, our proposed algorithm does not require any prior knowledge of
r^{\star}
and remains highly efficient. Specifically, it requires a sample size that scales near-linearly with
r^{\star}
, while its runtime scales linearly with the sample size
n
and the ambient dimension
d
, and near-linearly with
r^{\star}
.
An immediate consequence of this result is that the entire sample set can be safely projected onto the subspace
\mathcal{V}
, effectively reducing the ambient dimension from
d
to
\hat{r}
.

In the second stage, we refine the subspace
\mathcal{V}
by applying a robustified variant of the classical RANSAC algorithm for finer-grained estimation. A key advantage of this refinement is that, by operating on the projected sample set within the low-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{V}
identified in the first stage, it enables a provably more accurate and efficient characterization of
\mathcal{S}^{\star}
compared to a direct application of RANSAC.

Figure

1
illustrates the performance of RANSAC+ compared to the previously discussed RSR methods. As shown, RANSAC+ demonstrates superior robustness to adversarial corruption relative to the other methods, with the exception of classical RANSAC. In contrast,
Figure

2
highlights how RANSAC+ outperforms RANSAC under dimension misspecification, increased noise levels, and in terms of runtime.

1.3
Related works

In this section, we review relevant works on RSR, with a particular focus on adversarial robustness. A more comprehensive literature review is provided in

[
LM18b
]
.

RANSAC-type methods

The RANSAC algorithm, first introduced in
[
FB81
]
, operates as follows: it begins by randomly sampling points until they span a
r^{\star}
-dimensional subspace. It then determines the number of points whose distances to this subspace fall below a specified threshold, classifying them as clean samples. If the number of clean samples exceeds a predefined consensus threshold, the algorithm outputs the corresponding subspace. Otherwise, after a fixed number of iterations, it returns the subspace with the highest consensus count.

As empirically demonstrated above, RANSAC suffers from dependence on the true dimension
r^{\star}
, sensitivity to noise, and high computational cost.
To address computational challenges of RANSAC,
[
HM13
]
proposed the
Randomized-Find
(RF) algorithm, a faster variant of RANSAC. The RF algorithm relies on the key assumption that outliers are in general position—that is, they do not lie within a low-dimensional subspace. For a dataset
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with size
n>r^{\star}
, RF repeatedly selects random subsets
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
of size
d
from
\mathcal{X}
. It then identifies subsets where
\text{rank}(\widetilde{\mathcal{X}})<d
, as these must contain at least
r^{\star}+1
clean samples. The indices of the clean samples are then determined from the nonzero elements of a vector in the kernel of
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
.
However, RF is not robust against truly adversarial corruptions, as these corruptions typically violate the general location assumption (see Section
1.1
).
Moreover, extending RF to noisy settings requires unrealistic assumptions, such as the determinant separation condition
[
HM13
, Condition 11]
, which does not align with the adversarial contamination model.

Inspired by RF,
[
ACW17
]
introduced an even more efficient variant of RANSAC that only samples subsets of
r^{\star}+1
points until a linearly dependent set is identified. However, this method also fails under truly adversarial settings. For instance, if the outliers lie on a one-dimensional subspace, subsets containing two outliers can lead to arbitrarily poor subspace estimates.

Other RSR methods

Byond RANSAC-type algorithms, another approach that has shown great promise is Tyler’s M-estimator (TME). Originally designed for robust covariance estimation, TME was later shown in
[
Zha16
]
to be applicable to RSR. However, this approach also assumes that the outliers are in general positions, thereby making it ineffective against adversarial contamination. TME has notable advantages, such as scale invariance and simplicity of implementation. However, the extension of TME to the noisy RSR setting, as presented in Theorem 3.1 of
[
Zha16
]
, is limited.

Another line of research involves direct optimization of the robust least absolute deviations function over the non-convex Grassmannian manifold
G(d,r^{\star})
. The work
[
LM18a
]
proposed a
fast median subspace
(FMS) algorithm, which employs iteratively re-weighted least squares (IRLS) to minimize an energy function. In a follow-up study,
[
MZL19
]
developed a geodesic gradient descent (GGD) method to optimize the same energy function on
G(d,r^{\star})
, leveraging ideas from
[
EAS98
]
. Additionally,
[
MZL19
]
analyzed the landscape of this energy function over
G(d,r^{\star})
. However, as noted in
[
ML19
]
, this energy function has limitations—it is not robust against adversarial outliers with large magnitudes.
Despite stronger theoretical guarantees for GGD compared to FMS, neither method effectively handles real adversarial corruption, as previously mentioned.

Finally, another line of research focuses on
robust covariance estimation
by directly minimizing an
\ell_{1}
-loss
[
MF23b
,
DJC+21
,
MF21
,
MF23a
]
. While accurately estimating the covariance matrix is sufficient to recover the desired subspace, the assumptions required for outliers and inliers in these approaches are significantly more restrictive than those considered in this work.

3
Preliminaries

Next, we present the necessary tools for our theoretical analysis.
First, we introduce a classic concentration result on the singular values of a Gaussian ensemble.

Lemma 1

(Theorem 6.1 in
[
Wai19
]
)
.

Let
X=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}&x_{2}&\cdots&x_{n}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
be drawn from a
\Sigma
-Gaussian ensemble, meaning that each column
x_{i}
is independently drawn from a Gaussian distribution with a zero mean and covariance matrix
\Sigma
. Then, for any
t>0
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{\max}(X)}{\sqrt{n}}\geq\gamma_{\max}(\Sigma^{1/2})(1+t)+\sqrt{\frac{{\operatorname{tr}}(\Sigma)}{n}}\right)\leq e^{-nt^{2}/2}.

Moreover, when
n\geq d
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{\min}(X)}{\sqrt{n}}\leq\gamma_{\min}(\Sigma^{1/2})(1-t)-\sqrt{\frac{{\operatorname{tr}}(\Sigma)}{n}}\right)\leq e^{-nt^{2}/2}.

Next, we present tail bounds on binomial and hypergeometric distributions, both of which are direct consequences of Hoeffding’s inequality.

Lemma 2

(Proposition 2.5 in
[
Wai19
]
)
.

Let
z_{1},\ldots z_{n}
be independently drawn from a Bernoulli distribution with a success probability
p>a
, where
a\in(0,1)
is a constant. Then, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}\leq a\right)\leq e^{-2n\left(p-a\right)^{2}}.

Lemma 3

(
[
Chv79
]
)
.

Suppose that
z
follows a hypergeometric distribution with parameters
(n,k,m)
, where
n
is the population size,
k
is the number of success states in the population and
m
is the number of draws. Then, for any
0\leq t\leq\frac{k}{n}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(z\leq\left(\frac{k}{n}-t\right)m\right)\leq e^{-2mt^{2}}.

Our next assumption entails that a clean sample
x\sim\mathcal{P}
is not concentrated too much around zero, when projected along any direction
v\in\col(U^{\star})
. This assumption, famously known as
small-ball condition
, is extensively used in the literature and is crucial in understanding the concentration and distribution properties of random variables, especially in high-dimensional settings
[
LM17
,
Men15
,
Men17
,
NV13
]
. For a random vector

with a covariance
\Sigma^{\star}=U^{\star}D^{\star}{U^{\star}}^{\top}
, define its normalized variant as
w={D^{\star}}^{-1/2}{U^{\star}}^{\top}x
. Indeed, it is easy to see that
w
has a zero mean and an identity covariance
I_{r^{\star}}
.

Assumption 3

(Small-ball condition)
.

Given any fixed vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{r^{\star}}
with
\left\lVert v\right\rVert=1
, random variable
x\sim\mathcal{P}
, and
w={D^{\star}}^{-1/2}{U^{\star}}^{\top}x
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|v^{\top}w\right|\geq t_{0}\right)\geq\frac{3}{4},

where
0<t_{0}\leq 1
is a universal constant.

The small-ball condition is known to hold for a broad range of distributions (including heavy-tailed ones) and under mild assumptions
[
Men14
,
Men15
,
LM17
]
.
For instance, when
w
has a standard Gaussian distribution, this condition holds with
t_{0}=\frac{1}{4}
.

Given
n
i.i.d. copies of
w
, denoted by
\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\}
, our next lemma shows that the minimum eigenvalue of the empirical covariance of
\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\}
is bounded away from zero with a high probability under
Assumption

3
.

Lemma 4

(Corollary 2.5 in
[
LM17
]
)
.

Given
Assumption

3
and i.i.d. samples
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\sim\mathcal{P}
, define
w_{i}={D^{\star}}^{-1/2}{U^{\star}}^{\top}x_{i}
for
i\in[n]
. There exist universal constants
1\!<\!c_{1}
and
0\!<\!c_{2}\!\leq\!1/2
such that if the sample size satisfies

, then with probability at least
1-e^{-c_{2}n}
, we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{\min}\left(\frac{1}{n}W_{n}W_{n}^{\top}\right)\geq\frac{3t_{0}^{2}}{8},

where
W_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}&w_{2}&\cdots&w_{n}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{r^{\star}\times n}
.

Next, we prove a useful property of the normalized random variables
\{w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\}
, which will be used in our analysis. Specifically, we establish that, with high probability, most samples in
\{w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\}
satisfy the lower bound
|v^{\top}w_{i}|\geq\frac{t_{0}}{2}
, uniformly over all unit vectors
v\in\mathbb{S}^{r^{\star}-1}
.

Lemma 5
.

Given
Assumption

3
and i.i.d. samples
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\sim\mathcal{P}
, define
w_{i}={D^{\star}}^{-1/2}{U^{\star}}^{\top}x_{i}
for
i\in[n]
. There exist universal constants
c_{3},c_{4}>0
such that if the sample size satisfies
n\geq c_{3}r^{\star}\log\left(\frac{r^{\star}}{t_{0}}\right)
, with probability at least
1-e^{-c_{4}n}
, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{v\in\mathbb{S}^{r^{\star}-1}}\left|\left\{i\in[n]:|v^{\top}w_{i}|\geq\frac{t_{0}}{2}\right\}\right|\geq\frac{5}{8}n.

Proof.

By Chebyshev’s inequality, for each
i\in[n]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\left\lVert w_{i}\right\rVert\geq 4\sqrt{r^{\star}})\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert w_{i}\right\rVert^{2}]}{16r^{\star}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[{\operatorname{tr}}\left(w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}\right)\right]}{16r^{\star}}=\frac{1}{16}.

By
Assumption

3
, for any unit vector
v\in\mathbb{S}^{r^{\star}-1}
and
i\in[n]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|v^{\top}w_{i}|\geq t_{0},\,\left\lVert w_{i}\right\rVert\leq 4\sqrt{r^{\star}}\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(|v^{\top}w_{i}|\geq t_{0}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert w_{i}\right\rVert\leq 4\sqrt{r^{\star}}\right)-1\geq\frac{11}{16}.

Since
\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are independent, for any fixed
v\in\mathbb{S}^{r^{\star}-1}
, the cardinality of
\{i\in[n]:|v^{\top}w_{i}|\geq t_{0},\,\left\lVert w_{i}\right\rVert\leq 4\sqrt{r^{\star}}\}
follows a binomial distribution. Thus, given any fixed
v\in\mathbb{S}^{r^{\star}-1}
, by
Lemma

2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\left\{i\in[n]:|v^{\top}w_{i}|\geq t_{0},\,\left\lVert w_{i}\right\rVert\leq 4\sqrt{r^{\star}}\right\}\right|\geq\frac{5}{8}n,

with probability at least
1-e^{-\frac{n}{128}}
. Given any
\epsilon_{0}>0
, define an
\epsilon_{0}
-covering net
\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon_{0}}
of the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{r^{\star}-1}
as follows
[
Wai19
, Definition 5.1]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon_{0}}=\{v_{0}:\text{for any $v\in\mathbb{S}^{r^{\star}-1}$, there exists $v_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{r^{\star}-1}$ such that $\left\lVert v-v_{0}\right\rVert\leq\epsilon_{0}$}\}.

It is well known that there exists an
\epsilon_{0}
-covering net of the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{r^{\star}-1}
with cardinality upper bounded by
|\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon_{0}}|\leq\left(1+\frac{2}{\epsilon_{0}}\right)^{r^{\star}}

[
Wai19
, Example 5.8]
. A simple union bound on the elements of this
\epsilon_{0}
-covering net leads to

\displaystyle\inf_{v_{0}\in\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon_{0}}}\left|\left\{i\in[n]:|v_{0}^{\top}w_{i}|\geq t_{0},\,\left\lVert w_{i}\right\rVert\leq 4\sqrt{r^{\star}}\right\}\right|\geq\frac{5}{8}n,

with probability at least
1-(1+\frac{2}{\epsilon_{0}})^{r^{\star}}e^{-\frac{n}{128}}
. On the other hand, for any unit vector
v\in\mathbb{S}^{r^{\star}-1}
, there exists
v_{0}\in\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon_{0}}
such that
\left\lVert v_{0}-v\right\rVert\leq\epsilon_{0}
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle|v^{\top}w_{i}|\geq|v_{0}^{\top}w_{i}|-|(v_{0}-v)^{\top}w_{i}|\geq|v_{0}^{\top}w_{i}|-\epsilon_{0}\left\lVert w_{i}\right\rVert.

Hence, for any
i\in[n]
such that
|v_{0}^{\top}w_{i}|\geq t_{0}
and
\left\lVert w_{i}\right\rVert\leq 4\sqrt{r^{\star}}
, we obtain
|v^{\top}w_{i}|\geq t_{0}-4\epsilon_{0}\sqrt{r^{\star}}
.
Upon choosing
\epsilon_{0}=\frac{t_{0}}{8\sqrt{r^{\star}}}
, we can conclude that
|v^{\top}w_{i}|\geq\frac{t_{0}}{2}
, which implies

\displaystyle\inf_{v\in\mathbb{S}^{r^{\star}-1}}\left|\left\{i\in[n]:|v^{\top}w_{i}|\geq\frac{t_{0}}{2},\,\left\lVert w_{i}\right\rVert\leq 4\sqrt{r^{\star}}\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{v_{0}\in\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon_{0}}}\left|\left\{i\in[n]:|v_{0}^{\top}w_{i}|\geq t_{0},\,\left\lVert w_{i}\right\rVert\leq 4\sqrt{r^{\star}}\right\}\right|\geq\frac{5}{8}n,

with probability at least
1-\left(1+\frac{16\sqrt{r^{\star}}}{t_{0}}\right)^{r^{\star}}e^{-\frac{n}{128}}=1-e^{r^{\star}\log\left(1+\frac{16\sqrt{r^{\star}}}{t_{0}}\right)-\frac{n}{128}}.

Therefore, when
n\geq 256r^{\star}\log\left(1+\frac{16\sqrt{r^{\star}}}{t_{0}}\right)
, with probability at least
1-e^{-\frac{n}{256}}
, we have

\inf_{v\in\mathbb{S}^{r^{\star}-1}}\left|\left\{i\in[n]:|v^{\top}w_{i}|\geq\frac{t_{0}}{2}\right\}\right|\!\geq\!\inf_{v\in\mathbb{S}^{r^{\star}-1}}\left|\left\{i\in[n]:|v^{\top}w_{i}|\!\geq\!\frac{t_{0}}{2},\,\left\lVert w_{i}\right\rVert\leq 4\sqrt{r^{\star}}\right\}\right|\geq\frac{5}{8}n.

∎

4
Proposed Algorithm

In this section, we formally introduce our two-stage algorithm (RANSAC+) and provide the intuition behind it.

As previously noted, the computational cost of classic RANSAC, given by
\mathcal{O}\left(ndr^{\star}e^{r^{\star}}\right)
, becomes prohibitive in high-dimensional settings when
d
or
n
is large, even for moderate values of
r^{\star}
. While the exponential dependence on
r^{\star}
appears unavoidable, we demonstrate that the dependency on
d
and
n
can be reduced from multiplicative to additive. The reduction in
d
-dependence is straightforward: by first reducing the ambient dimension from
d
to
\mathcal{O}(r^{\star})
, we defer the exponential cost
e^{r^{\star}}
to a lower-dimensional space. The reduction in
n
-dependence arises from a fundamental difference in how our method and classic RANSAC recover the target subspace. Classic RANSAC evaluates each candidate subspace by checking all samples to count how many lie within a specified angular threshold—an operation linear in
n
. In contrast, our method processes candidate batches by computing their spectrum and then recovers the final subspace by identifying a consistent eigengap across batches. Thanks to the projection step, computing the spectrum of each batch depends only on
r^{\star}
, not on
d
or
n
.

More specifically, the first-stage algorithm (
Algorithm

2
) proceeds as follows. It takes as input an
(\epsilon,\Sigma_{\xi})
-corrupted sample set
\mathcal{X}
, along with an estimate of
{\operatorname{tr}}(\Sigma_{\xi})
and
\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert
. The algorithm begins by initializing the search batch size to
B=2
. In each iteration, the algorithm randomly samples a subset
\mathcal{X}_{B}
of size
B
from
\mathcal{X}
and computes the basis
V
of the subspace
\col(X_{B})
. It then calculates the median residual, defined as the median of the distances
\left\{\left\lVert x-VV^{\top}x\right\rVert:x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{X}_{B}\right\}
. If this median residual falls below the threshold
\eta_{\mathrm{thresh}}
, the algorithm terminates and outputs the corresponding subspace. Otherwise, it doubles the batch size
B
and continues to the next iteration.

4.1
Analysis of the First-stage Algorithm

In this section, we present the theoretical analysis of the first-stage algorithm (
Algorithm

2
). In particular, our goal is to prove the following theorem, which is the formal version of
Theorem

1
.

Theorem 3
.

Fix
r^{\star}\!\geq\!2
,
\epsilon\!\leq\!0.1
,
C\!\geq\!2.2
,
n\!\geq\!\max\left\{2c_{3}\log\left(\frac{r^{\star}}{t_{0}}\right),\!50c_{1},\frac{100}{c_{2}},\!800\right\}r^{\star}
, and
n\geq\max\left\{\frac{1}{c_{4}},250\right\}\log(\frac{3}{\delta})
, where
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4}
are the same universal constants in
Lemma

4
and
Lemma

5
. Moreover, suppose that
5\sqrt{r^{\star}\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert}+\sqrt{{\operatorname{tr}}(\Sigma_{\xi})}\leq\frac{t_{0}^{2}}{4t_{0}+4C}\sqrt{\gamma^{\star}_{\min}}
. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,
Algorithm

2
terminates within
\mathcal{O}(dn{r^{\star}}\log(r^{\star}))
time, and satisfies:

\displaystyle\hat{r}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{2c_{1},\frac{4}{c_{2}},32\right\}\cdot r^{\star},

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left(I_{d}-VV^{\top}\right)x\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4C}{t_{0}^{2}}\left(5\sqrt{\frac{r^{\star}\left\lVert{\Sigma_{\xi}}\right\rVert}{\gamma^{\star}_{\min}}}+\sqrt{\frac{{\operatorname{tr}}(\Sigma_{\xi})}{\gamma^{\star}_{\min}}}\right)\left\lVert x\right\rVert,\quad\text{for}\quad x\sim\mathcal{P}.

Our strategy to prove

proceeds as follows.
First, we prove that when
B<r^{\star}
, the stopping criterion in Line

is not triggered with high probability, thereby preventing the algorithm from terminating prematurely. Second, we prove that when the stopping criterion in Line

is triggered, then the recovered subspace

4.1.1
Step 1: The condition

When

Proposition 1
.

Fix

and

Assuming

Next, we proceed with the proof of
Proposition

1
. To this goal, we denote the orthogonal complement of

as
V_{\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(d-B)}

Lemma 6
.

When
B<r^{\star}
, we have
\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}\right\rVert=1

Proof.

One can write

\displaystyle\dim(\textbf{col}(V_{\perp})\cap\textbf{col}(U^{\star}))\!\geq\!\dim(\textbf{col}(V_{\perp}))\!+\!\dim(\textbf{col}(U^{\star}))\!-\!d=(d-B)+r^{\star}-d=r^{\star}-B\geq 1,

Thus, there exists a vector
x\in\textbf{col}(V_{\perp})\cap\textbf{col}(U^{\star})
with
\left\lVert x\right\rVert=1
such that
x=V_{\perp}y_{1}=U^{\star}y_{2},

for some
y_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-B}
and

1=\left\lVert x\right\rVert=\left\lVert V_{\perp}y_{1}\right\rVert=\left\lVert y_{1}\right\rVert
.
Similarly, we have
1=\left\lVert y_{2}\right\rVert
. On the other hand,
V_{\perp}y_{1}=U^{\star}y_{2}
leads to
y_{1}=V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}y_{2}
, implying

\displaystyle 1=\left\lVert y_{1}\right\rVert=\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}y_{2}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}\right\rVert\left\lVert y_{2}\right\rVert=\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}\right\rVert.

On the other hand,
\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}\right\rVert\left\lVert U^{\star}\right\rVert=1
.
Therefore, we conclude that
\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}\right\rVert=1
.

Proof of

For any

\displaystyle\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}x\right\rVert=\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}(U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}w+\xi)\right\rVert\geq\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}w\right\rVert-\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}\xi\right\rVert\geq\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}w\right\rVert-\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert.

(1)

Define the compact SVD of

\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{r^{\star}}\geq 0

\displaystyle\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}w\right\rVert=\left\lVert P\Lambda Q^{\top}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}w\right\rVert=\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{r^{\star}}\lambda_{i}\left(q_{i}^{\top}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}w\right)p_{i}\right\rVert=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{r^{\star}}\left|\lambda_{i}\left(q_{i}^{\top}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}w\right)\right|^{2}},

where
p_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{r^{\star}}
and

denote the

column of
P
and
Q
, respectively. The last equality is due to the fact that the columns of
P
are pairwise orthogonal. On the other hand, since
\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}\right\rVert=1
according to

\lambda_{1}=1
. This yields:

\displaystyle\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}w\right\rVert\geq\left|\lambda_{1}\left(q_{1}^{\top}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}w\right)\right|=\left|q_{1}^{\top}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}w\right|.

(2)

Upon defining

Combined with

\displaystyle\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}x\right\rVert\geq\sqrt{\gamma^{\star}_{\min}}\left|v^{\top}w\right|-\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert.

(3)

Let
\mathcal{N}
collect the index set of the samples within the set
\mathcal{X}\backslash\mathcal{X}_{B}
. Moreover, let

denote the index set of the inliers within the set

.
By

5
, when

, with probability at least
1-e^{-c_{4}n}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\left\{i\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{in}}:\left|v^{\top}w_{i}\right|\geq\frac{t_{0}}{2}\right\}\right|\geq\frac{5}{8}\left|\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{in}}\right|.

Since

\displaystyle\left|\left\{i\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{in}}:\left|v^{\top}w_{i}\right|\geq\frac{t_{0}}{2}\right\}\right|\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{20}\right)(n-B).

(4)

Next, for every
i\in\mathcal{N}
, define the Bernoulli random variable

\displaystyle z_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\left\lVert\xi_{i}\right\rVert\leq\sqrt{{\operatorname{tr}}(\Sigma_{\xi})}+5\sqrt{\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Invoking

1
with

. On the other hand, since

with

(since

\displaystyle\left|\left\{i\in\mathcal{N}:\left\lVert\xi_{i}\right\rVert\leq\sqrt{{\operatorname{tr}}(\Sigma_{\xi})}+5\sqrt{\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert}\right\}\right|\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{20}\right)(n-B).

By combining

and

\displaystyle\left|\left\{i\in\mathcal{N}:\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}x_{i}\right\rVert\geq\frac{t_{0}}{2}\sqrt{\gamma^{\star}_{\min}}-\left(\sqrt{{\operatorname{tr}}(\Sigma_{\xi})}+5\sqrt{\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert}\right)\right\}\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}(n-B)=\frac{1}{2}|\mathcal{N}|,

with probability at least

\displaystyle\mathbf{MedRes}(V)=\mathrm{median}\left(\left\{\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}x_{i}\right\rVert:i\in\mathcal{N}\right\}\right)\geq\frac{t_{0}}{2}\sqrt{\gamma^{\star}_{\min}}-\left(\sqrt{{\operatorname{tr}}(\Sigma_{\xi})}+5\sqrt{\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert}\right).

∎

. Therefore, we conclude that

(6)

4.1.3
Step 3: The condition

Proposition 3

Fix

Before presenting the proof of

3
, we note that, due to the definition of the threshold

in

0<t_{0}\leq 1
,

Therefore, if

Without loss of generality, let

be the set of inliers within the batch
\mathcal{X}_{B}
in Line

. To provide the proof of

3
, we first provide a lower bound on the number of inliers

within the batch

Lemma 7
.

For any subset
\mathcal{X}_{B}
sampled uniformly at random without replacement from
\mathcal{X}

denote the number of inliers in
\mathcal{X}_{B}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(B_{\rm in}\leq(1-\epsilon-t)B)\leq e^{-2t^{2}B}.

Proof.

The proof follows immediately by noting that

follows a hypergeometric distribution with parameters
(n,(1-\epsilon)n,B)
and invoking
Lemma

3
.
∎

As a direct consequence of

7

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(B_{\rm in}\geq\frac{1}{2}B\right)\geq 1-e^{2\left(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon\right)^{2}B}.

Therefore, we conclude that at least half of the samples within a single batch are inliers with high probability. Next, we prove the following key lemma, which controls the estimation error of the subspace
\mathcal{V}
recovered by

.

Lemma 8
.

Suppose that
B_{\rm in}\geq c_{1}r^{\star}
. With probability at least
1-e^{-c_{2}B_{\rm in}}-e^{-\frac{B_{\rm in}}{8}}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}\right\rVert\leq\frac{3\sqrt{B_{\rm in}\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert}+2\sqrt{{\operatorname{tr}}(\Sigma_{\xi})}}{t_{0}\sqrt{B_{\rm in}}}.

Proof.

Note that
V_{\perp}^{\top}x_{i}=0
for
i\in[B_{\rm in}]
. This implies that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}\right\rVert&=\sup_{\left\lVert v\right\rVert=1}\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}v\right\rVert\\
&=\sup_{\left\lVert v\right\rVert=1}\inf_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{B_{\rm in}}}\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}\left(U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}v-\sum_{i=1}^{B_{\rm in}}\lambda_{i}x_{i}\right)\right\rVert\\
&\leq\sup_{\left\lVert v\right\rVert=1}\inf_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{B_{\rm in}}}\left\lVert U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}v-\sum_{i=1}^{B_{\rm in}}\lambda_{i}\left(U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}w_{i}+\xi_{i}\right)\right\rVert\\
&=\sup_{\left\lVert v\right\rVert=1}\inf_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{B_{\rm in}}}\left\lVert U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}\left(v-\sum_{i=1}^{B_{\rm in}}\lambda_{i}w_{i}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{B_{\rm in}}\lambda_{i}\xi_{i}\right\rVert.\end{aligned}

Due to the absolute continuity of the probability distribution
\mathcal{P}
(
Assumption

2
), the normalized i.i.d. samples
\{w_{1},\dots,w_{B_{\rm in}}\}
with
B_{\rm in}\geq r^{\star}
span the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{r^{\star}}
almost surely. This implies that the linear system
\sum_{i=1}^{B_{\rm in}}\lambda_{i}w_{i}=v
has a solution
\lambda^{\star}
. In particular, upon defining
W=\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}&w_{2}&\cdots&w_{B_{\rm in}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{r^{\star}\times B_{\rm in}}
, one can take
\lambda^{\star}=W^{\dagger}v
, where
W^{\dagger}
denotes the pseudo-inverse of
W
. Letting
\Xi=\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{1}&\xi_{2}&\cdots&\xi_{B_{\rm in}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times B_{\rm in}}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}\right\rVert&\leq\sup_{\left\lVert v\right\rVert=1}\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{B_{\rm in}}\lambda^{\star}_{i}\xi_{i}\right\rVert=\sup_{\left\lVert v\right\rVert=1}\left\lVert\Xi W^{\dagger}v\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\Xi\right\rVert\left\lVert W^{\dagger}\right\rVert=\frac{\left\lVert\Xi\right\rVert}{\sigma_{\min}(W)}.\end{aligned}

By
Lemma

4
, when
B_{\rm in}\geq c_{1}r^{\star}
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{\min}(W)\geq\sqrt{\frac{3t_{0}^{2}B_{\rm in}}{8}}\geq\frac{t_{0}}{2}\sqrt{B_{\rm in}},

with probability at least
1-e^{-c_{2}B_{\rm in}}
.
Moreover, by
Lemma

1
with
t=1/2
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\Xi\right\rVert=\sigma_{\max}(\Xi)\leq\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{B_{\rm in}\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert}+\sqrt{{\operatorname{tr}}(\Sigma_{\xi})},

with probability at least
1-e^{-\frac{B_{\rm in}}{8}}
. By combining the two bounds mentioned above, we obtain:

\displaystyle\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}\right\rVert\leq\frac{3\sqrt{B_{\rm in}\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert}+2\sqrt{{\operatorname{tr}}(\Sigma_{\xi})}}{t_{0}\sqrt{B_{\rm in}}},

with probability at least
1-e^{-c_{2}B_{\rm in}}-e^{-\frac{B_{\rm in}}{8}}
when
B_{\rm in}\geq c_{1}r^{\star}
. ∎

Based on this lemma, we are ready to complete the proof of
Proposition

3
.

Proof of
Proposition

3
.

Given any inlier
x=U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}w+\xi

\displaystyle\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}x\right\rVert=\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}(U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}w+\xi)\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}\right\rVert\left\lVert w\right\rVert+\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert.

(7)

By Chebyshev’s inequality, we have for any

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\left\lVert w\right\rVert\geq t)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert w\right\rVert^{2}]}{t^{2}}=\frac{{\operatorname{tr}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[ww^{\top}\right]\right)}{t^{2}}=\frac{{\operatorname{tr}}\left(I_{r^{\star}}\right)}{t^{2}}=\frac{r^{\star}}{t^{2}}.

Therefore,

holds with probability at least
\frac{3}{4}
. On the other hand, invoking
Lemma

1
with

yields:

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\left(\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert\geq\sqrt{{\operatorname{tr}}(\Sigma_{\xi})}+5\sqrt{\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert}\right)\leq e^{-8}.

(8)

Therefore, by combining
Lemma

8
and
Equation

8
with
Equation

7
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}x\right\rVert\leq\frac{2\sqrt{r^{\star}}\left(3\sqrt{B_{\rm in}\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert}+2\sqrt{{\operatorname{tr}}(\Sigma_{\xi})}\right)}{t_{0}\sqrt{B_{\rm in}}}+\left(\sqrt{{\operatorname{tr}}(\Sigma_{\xi})}+5\sqrt{\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert}\right),

with probability at least
\frac{3}{4}-e^{-c_{2}B_{\rm in}}-e^{-\frac{B_{\rm in}}{8}}-e^{-8}
. In particular, upon choosing

with probability at least
\frac{3}{4}-2e^{-2r^{\star}}-e^{-8}-e^{-64(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon)^{2}r^{\star}}
. Recall that
\mathcal{N}
collects the index set of the samples within the set

define the Bernoulli random variable:

\displaystyle z_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}x_{i}\right\rVert\leq\left(\frac{6\sqrt{r^{\star}}}{t_{0}}+5\right)\sqrt{\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert}+\left(\frac{1}{t_{0}}+1\right)\sqrt{{\operatorname{tr}}(\Sigma_{\xi})},\\

Indeed, given

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3}{4}-2e^{-2r^{\star}}-e^{-8}-e^{-64\left(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon\right)^{2}r^{\star}}\geq\frac{3}{4}-2e^{-4}-2e^{-8}\geq\frac{7}{10}.

with probability at least

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{6\sqrt{r^{\star}}}{t_{0}}+5\right)\sqrt{\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert}+\left(\frac{1}{t_{0}}+1\right)\sqrt{{\operatorname{tr}}(\Sigma_{\xi})}.

∎

4.2
Analysis of the Second-stage Algorithm

In this section, we present the theoretical analysis of the second-stage algorithm (

Theorem 4
.

Suppose that the conditions of
Theorem

3
hold. Additionally,
fix
\epsilon\leq\min\left\{0.1,\,0.9\cdot\left(1-e^{-\frac{c_{2}}{4}}\right)\right\}
,
C^{\prime}\geq\max\{2c_{1},8/c_{2}\}
with
c_{1},c_{2}
appearing in

n\geq 11C^{\prime}\max\left\{\hat{r},\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)\right\}

3
terminates in
\mathcal{O}({r^{\star}}^{3}e^{r^{\star}})
time and, with probability at least

, satisfies:

\displaystyle\tilde{r}=r^{\star},\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lVert(V\widehat{U}_{k})(V\widehat{U}_{k})^{\top}-U^{\star}{U^{\star}}^{\top}\right\rVert\leq\frac{4(t_{0}+C)C}{t_{0}^{3}}\left(6\sqrt{\frac{r^{\star}\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert}{\gamma^{\star}_{\min}}}+\sqrt{\frac{{\operatorname{tr}}(\Sigma_{\xi})}{\gamma^{\star}_{\min}}}\right).

Similar to our analysis of the first-stage algorithm, we begin by outlining the high-level idea of our proof strategy. Consider the following two events:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\{\text{For some $j\in[T]$, $\widehat{X}_{j}$ only contains inliers.}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{\text{For all $j\in[T]$, at least half of the samples in $\widehat{X}_{j}$ are inliers.}\right\}.

Conditioned on
\mathcal{E}_{1}
, let
j^{\star}
be the index for which the batch
\widehat{X}_{j^{\star}}
only contains inliers. Then, we will show that

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{r^{\star}+1}:=\min_{j\in[T]}\left(\widehat{\Sigma}_{r^{\star}+1}^{(j)}\right)^{2}\leq\left(\widehat{\Sigma}_{r^{\star}+1}^{(j^{\star})}\right)^{2}=\mathcal{O}(\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert).

Moreover, conditioned on
\mathcal{E}_{2}
, we will establish that

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{r^{\star}}:=\min_{j\in[T]}\left(\widehat{\Sigma}_{r^{\star}}^{(j)}\right)^{2}=\Omega(\gamma^{\star}_{\min})\gg\mathcal{O}(\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert).

These two relations together establish a significant gap between
\hat{\gamma}_{r^{\star}+1}
and
\hat{\gamma}_{r^{\star}}
.
In particular, the first relation implies that the computed index
\tilde{r}
in Line

satisfies
\tilde{r}\leq r^{\star}
, while the second ensures that
\tilde{r}\geq r^{\star}
. Together, they establish that
\tilde{r}=r^{\star}
, thereby guaranteeing the exact recovery of the true subspace dimension.
Finally, we show that by selecting the batch size
B
and the number of iterations
T
as specified in Lines
2
and
3
of
Algorithm

, the probability of the desired events occurring satisfies

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2})\geq 1-2\delta
.

To formalize the above argument, we begin with the following lemma, which establishes a connection between the true covariance matrix
\Sigma^{\star}
and its projected counterpart
V^{\top}\Sigma^{\star}V
.

Lemma 9
.

Given
\Sigma^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\widehat{\Sigma}^{\star}=V^{\top}\Sigma^{\star}V\in\mathbb{R}^{\hat{r}\times\hat{r}}
, we have
\gamma_{k}(\widehat{\Sigma}^{\star})\leq\gamma_{k}(\Sigma^{\star})
and
\sqrt{\gamma_{k}(\widehat{\Sigma}^{\star})}\geq\sqrt{\gamma_{k}(\Sigma^{\star})}-\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}\right\rVert
, for every
k\in[\hat{r}]
.

Proof.

By the min-max theorem for eigenvalues
[
HJ94
, Theorem 3.1.2]
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \gamma_{k}(\widehat{\Sigma}^{\star})&=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\hat{r}}\\
\dim(\mathcal{M})=k\end{subarray}}\inf_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{x^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}^{\star}x}{x^{\top}x}\\
&=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\mathbb{R}^{\hat{r}\times k}\\
\text{rank}(M)=k\end{subarray}}\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\frac{(My)^{\top}V^{\top}\Sigma^{\star}V(My)}{(My)^{\top}(My)}\\
&=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\mathbb{R}^{\hat{r}\times k}\\
\text{rank}(M)=k\end{subarray}}\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\frac{(VMy)^{\top}\Sigma^{\star}(VMy)}{(VMy)^{\top}(VMy)}\\
&=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\mathbb{R}^{\hat{r}\times k}\\
\text{rank}(M)=k\end{subarray}}\inf_{z\in\textbf{col}(VM)}\frac{z^{\top}\Sigma^{\star}z}{z^{\top}z}\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}\\
\text{rank}(V^{\top}W)=k\end{subarray}}\inf_{z\in\textbf{col}(W)}\frac{z^{\top}\Sigma^{\star}z}{z^{\top}z}\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}\\
\text{rank}(W)=k\end{subarray}}\inf_{z\in\textbf{col}(W)}\frac{z^{\top}\Sigma^{\star}z}{z^{\top}z}=\gamma_{k}(\Sigma^{\star}),\end{aligned}

for every
k\in[\hat{r}]
, where
(a)
follows from a change of variable
W=VM
, and
(b)
holds since
k=\text{rank}(V^{\top}W)\leq\text{rank}(W)\leq k
, which leads to
\text{rank}(W)=k
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\gamma_{k}(\widehat{\Sigma}^{\star})}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\gamma_{k}\left(V^{\top}U^{\star}D^{\star}{U^{\star}}^{\top}V\right)}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{k}(V^{\top}U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{k}(VV^{\top}U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{k}((I_{d}-V_{\perp}V_{\perp}^{\top})U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2})

\displaystyle\geq\sigma_{k}(U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2})-\sigma_{\max}(V_{\perp}V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\gamma_{k}(\Sigma^{\star})}-\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}\right\rVert,

for every
k\in[\hat{r}]
. This completes the proof.
∎

Returning to
Algorithm

3
, recall that each batch
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{j}
is selected independently and uniformly at random from
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
and is stored in the matrix
\widehat{X}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{\hat{r}\times B}
. Let the empirical covariance matrix of the
j^{\text{th}}
batch be denoted by

\widehat{\Sigma}_{j}=\frac{1}{B}\widehat{X}_{j}\widehat{X}_{j}^{\top}.

Furthermore, let
B_{j}^{\rm in}
and
B_{j}^{\rm out}
represent the number of inliers and outliers in
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{j}
, respectively. Denote the ensemble of the inliers and outliers in
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{j}
by
\widehat{X}_{j}^{\rm in}\in\mathbb{R}^{\hat{r}\times B_{j}^{\rm in}}
and
\widehat{X}_{j}^{\rm out}\in\mathbb{R}^{\hat{r}\times B_{j}^{\rm out}}
, respectively. Finally, define the empirical covariance matrices of the inliers and outliers in
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{j}
as
\widehat{\Sigma}^{\rm in}_{j}=\frac{1}{B_{j}^{\rm in}}\widehat{X}_{j}^{\rm in}(\widehat{X}_{j}^{\rm in})^{\top}
and
\widehat{\Sigma}^{\rm out}_{j}=\frac{1}{B_{j}^{\rm out}}\widehat{X}_{j}^{\rm out}(\widehat{X}_{j}^{\rm out})^{\top}

It follows that
\widehat{\Sigma}_{j}=(1-\mu_{j})\widehat{\Sigma}^{\rm in}_{j}+\mu_{j}\widehat{\Sigma}^{\rm out}_{j}
, where
\mu_{j}:={B_{j}^{\rm out}}/{B}
is the fraction of outliers in
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{j}
.

Let the eigen-decomposition of the projected covariance matrix
\widehat{\Sigma}^{\star}=V^{\top}\Sigma^{\star}V
be given by
\widehat{\Sigma}^{\star}=\widehat{U}^{\star}\widehat{D}^{\star}(\widehat{U}^{\star})^{\top}
,
where
\widehat{U}^{\star}\in O(\hat{r},r^{\star})
and
\widehat{D}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{r^{\star}\times r^{\star}}
is a diagonal matrix satisfying
\widehat{D}^{\star}\preceq D^{\star}
. Moreover, let
\widehat{U}^{\star}_{\perp}\in O(\hat{r},\hat{r}-r^{\star})
denote the orthogonal complement of
\widehat{U}^{\star}
. Our next lemma establishes concentration bounds on
\widehat{\Sigma}^{\rm in}_{j}
in terms of
\widehat{U}^{\star}
and
\widehat{U}^{\star}_{\perp}
.

Lemma 10
.

For any fixed
j\in[T]
, with probability at least
1-e^{-B_{j}^{\rm in}/2}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert(\widehat{U}^{\star}_{\perp})^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}^{{\rm in}}_{j}\widehat{U}^{\star}_{\perp}\right\rVert\leq\left(2+\sqrt{\frac{\hat{r}}{B_{j}^{\rm in}}}\right)^{2}\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert.

Moreover, assuming
B_{j}^{\rm in}\geq c_{1}r^{\star}
, with probability at least
1-e^{-c_{2}B_{j}^{\rm in}}-e^{-B_{j}^{\rm in}/2}
, we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{\min}\left((\widehat{U}^{\star})^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}^{{\rm in}}_{j}\widehat{U}^{\star}\right)\geq\left(\frac{t_{0}}{2}\left(\sqrt{\gamma^{\star}_{\min}}-\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}\right\rVert\right)-\left(2+\sqrt{\frac{\hat{r}}{B_{j}^{\rm in}}}\right)\sqrt{\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

Given the ensemble of inliers
\widehat{X}_{j}^{\rm in}
, we decompose it as
\widehat{X}_{j}^{\rm in}=\widetilde{X}_{j}^{\rm in}+\widehat{\Xi}_{j}^{\rm in}
,
where
\widetilde{X}_{j}^{\rm in}
denotes the ensemble of projected clean components, and
\widehat{\Xi}_{j}^{\rm in}
corresponds to the ensemble of projected Gaussian noise associated with each inlier in
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{j}
. Each column of
\widetilde{X}_{j}^{\rm in}
is drawn from a distribution with a zero mean and covariance
\widehat{\Sigma}^{\star}=V^{\top}\Sigma^{\star}V
. Moreover, each column of
\widehat{\Xi}_{j}^{\rm in}
is drawn from a Gaussian distribution with a zero mean and covariance
V^{\top}\Sigma_{\xi}V
.
Since
(\widehat{U}^{\star}_{\perp})^{\top}\widetilde{X}_{j}^{\rm in}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left\lVert(\widehat{U}^{\star}_{\perp})^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}^{\rm in}_{j}\widehat{U}^{\star}_{\perp}\right\rVert&=\frac{\left\lVert\left((\widehat{U}^{\star}_{\perp})^{\top}\widehat{X}_{j}^{\rm in}\right)\left((\widehat{U}^{\star}_{\perp})^{\top}\widehat{X}_{j}^{\rm in}\right)^{\top}\right\rVert}{B_{j}^{\rm in}}\\
&=\frac{\left\lVert\left((\widehat{U}^{\star}_{\perp})^{\top}\widehat{\Xi}_{j}^{\rm in}\right)\left((\widehat{U}^{\star}_{\perp})^{\top}\widehat{\Xi}_{j}^{\rm in}\right)^{\top}\right\rVert}{B_{j}^{\rm in}}\\
&\leq\frac{\left\lVert(\widehat{\Xi}_{j}^{\rm in})(\widehat{\Xi}_{j}^{\rm in})^{\top}\right\rVert}{B_{j}^{\rm in}}=\left(\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\widehat{\Xi}_{j}^{\rm in})}{\sqrt{B_{j}^{\rm in}}}\right)^{2}.\end{aligned}

By
Lemma

1
, with probability at least
1-e^{-B_{j}^{\rm in}/2}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\widehat{\Xi}_{j}^{\rm in})}{\sqrt{B_{j}^{\rm in}}}\leq 2\sqrt{\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert}+\sqrt{\frac{{\operatorname{tr}}(V^{\top}\Sigma_{\xi}V)}{B_{j}^{\rm in}}}\leq\left(2+\sqrt{\frac{\hat{r}}{B_{j}^{\rm in}}}\right)\sqrt{\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert}.

(11)

This completes the proof of the first statement. To prove the second statement, recall that, given any random vector
x\sim\mathcal{P}
, its projection
\widehat{x}=V^{\top}x
has zero mean and covariance
\widehat{\Sigma}^{\star}=\widehat{U}^{\star}\widehat{D}^{\star}(\widehat{U}^{\star})^{\top}
. Therefore,
\widehat{w}=(\widehat{D}^{\star})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\widehat{U}^{\star})^{\top}\widehat{x}
is normalized, and can be easily verified to satisfy
Assumption

3
. This implies that each column of
(\widehat{D}^{\star})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\widehat{U}^{\star})^{\top}\widetilde{X}_{j}^{\rm in}
independently satisfies
Assumption

3
, and hence, by
Lemma

4
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{\min}\left((\widehat{D}^{\star})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\widehat{U}^{\star})^{\top}\widetilde{X}_{j}^{\rm in}\right)}{\sqrt{B_{j}^{\rm in}}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{3t_{0}^{2}}{8}}\geq\frac{t_{0}}{2},

(12)

with probability at least
1-e^{-c_{2}B_{j}^{\rm in}}
, provided that
B_{j}^{\rm in}\geq c_{1}r^{\star}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\gamma_{\min}\left((\widehat{U}^{\star})^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}^{\rm in}_{j}\widehat{U}^{\star}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{\min}\left(((\widehat{U}^{\star})^{\top}\widehat{X}_{j}^{\rm in})((\widehat{U}^{\star})^{\top}\widehat{X}_{j}^{\rm in})^{\top}\right)}{B_{j}^{\rm in}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\sigma_{\min}\left((\widehat{U}^{\star})^{\top}\widehat{X}_{j}^{\rm in}\right)}{\sqrt{B_{j}^{\rm in}}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{\sigma_{\min}\left((\widehat{U}^{\star})^{\top}\tilde{X}_{j}^{\rm in}\right)}{\sqrt{B_{j}^{\rm in}}}-\frac{\sigma_{\max}\left((\widehat{U}^{\star})^{\top}V^{\top}\widehat{\Xi}_{j}^{\rm in}\right)}{\sqrt{B_{j}^{\rm in}}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{\gamma_{\min}\left(({\widehat{D}^{\star}})^{1/2}\right)\sigma_{\min}\left((\widehat{D}^{\star})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\widehat{U}^{\star})^{\top}\tilde{X}_{j}^{\rm in}\right)}{\sqrt{B_{j}^{\rm in}}}-\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\widehat{\Xi}_{j}^{\rm in})}{\sqrt{B_{j}^{\rm in}}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{t_{0}}{2}\left(\sqrt{\gamma^{\star}_{\min}}-\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}\right\rVert\right)-\left(2+\sqrt{\frac{\hat{r}}{B_{j}^{\rm in}}}\right)\sqrt{\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert}\right)^{2},

with probability at least
1-e^{-c_{2}B_{j}^{\rm in}}-e^{-B_{j}^{\rm in}/2}
. In the last inequality, we used
Equation

11
,
Equation

12
, and
Lemma

9
, which leads to
\gamma_{\min}\left(({\widehat{D}^{\star}})^{1/2}\right)\geq\sqrt{\gamma^{\star}_{\min}}-\left\lVert V_{\perp}^{\top}U^{\star}{D^{\star}}^{1/2}\right\rVert
.
∎

Lemma 11

, we have

Proof.

Define the following events:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\left\{\text{At least half of the samples in $\widehat{X}_{j}$ are inliers.}\right\},\text{ for }j\in[T],

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{3}^{(j)}

\mathcal{E}_{3}=\bigcap_{j=1}^{T}\mathcal{E}_{3}^{(j)}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{1})

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{2}\cap\mathcal{E}_{3})

It follows that the probability of the event

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}\cap\mathcal{E}_{3})

and
\hat{\gamma}_{r^{\star}}

, we have, for every

,

\displaystyle\gamma_{r^{\star}+1}(\widehat{\Sigma}^{\rm in}_{j})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{B}\\
\dim(\mathcal{M})=B-r^{\star}\end{subarray}}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{x^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}^{\rm in}_{j}x}{x^{\top}x}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times(B-r^{\star})}\\

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\sup_{x=\widehat{U}^{\star}_{\perp}y}\frac{x^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}^{\rm in}_{j}x}{x^{\top}x}=\left\lVert(\widehat{U}^{\star}_{\perp})^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}^{\rm in}_{j}(\widehat{U}^{\star}_{\perp})\right\rVert

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}\left(2+\sqrt{\frac{\hat{r}}{B_{j}^{\rm in}}}\right)^{2}\left\lVert\Sigma_{\xi}\right\rVert

(b)

As a final technical lemma before presenting the proof of

.

Lemma 12
.

The following statements hold:

•

If
n\geq 11B
and
T\geq\left(\frac{1}{1-1.1\epsilon}\right)^{B}\log(\frac{1}{\delta})
, then

\displaystyle 1-\left(1-\frac{\binom{(1-\epsilon)n}{B}}{\binom{n}{B}}\right)^{T}\geq 1-\delta.

(15)

•

If
\epsilon\leq 0.1
and
T\leq\frac{1}{3}e^{c_{2}B/2}\delta
, then

\displaystyle 1-T\left(e^{-2(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon)^{2}B}+e^{-\frac{1}{4}B}+e^{-\frac{c_{2}}{2}B}\right)\geq 1-\delta.

(16)

•

If
\epsilon\leq\min\left\{0.1,\,0.9\cdot\left(1-e^{-\frac{c_{2}}{4}}\right)\right\}
,
n\geq 11B
,
B\geq\frac{4}{c_{2}}\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)
, and
T=\left(\frac{1}{1-1.1\epsilon}\right)^{B}\log(\frac{1}{\delta})
, then

\displaystyle\left(1-\left(1-\frac{\binom{(1-\epsilon)n}{B}}{\binom{n}{B}}\right)^{T}\right)\left(1-T\left(e^{-2(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon)^{2}B}+e^{-\frac{1}{4}B}+e^{-\frac{c_{2}}{2}B}\right)\right)\geq 1-2\delta.

Proof.

To prove the first statement, let
\alpha:=\frac{\binom{(1-\epsilon)n}{B}}{\binom{n}{B}}
. When
n\geq 11B
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{\binom{(1-\epsilon)n}{B}}{\binom{n}{B}}=\frac{((1-\epsilon)n)\cdots((1-\epsilon)n-B+1)}{n(n-1)\cdots(n-B+1)}\geq\left(\frac{(1-\epsilon)n-B+1}{n-B+1}\right)^{B}\geq(1-1.1\epsilon)^{B}.

Therefore, the condition
T\geq\left(\frac{1}{1-1.1\epsilon}\right)^{B}\log(\frac{1}{\delta})
implies that
T\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}\log(\frac{1}{\delta})
. On the other hand,
1-\frac{1}{x}\leq\log(x)
for any
x>0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle T\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\geq\frac{\log(\frac{1}{\delta})}{\log(\frac{1}{1-\alpha})}\implies 1-(1-\alpha)^{T}\geq 1-\delta.

To prove the second statement, let
\beta:=e^{-2(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon)^{2}B}+e^{-\frac{B}{4}}+e^{-\frac{c_{2}B}{2}}
. When
\epsilon\leq 0.1
, we have
\beta\leq 2e^{-\frac{B}{4}}+e^{-\frac{c_{2}B}{2}}\leq 3e^{-\frac{c_{2}B}{2}}
,
where we used the fact that
c_{2}\leq 1/2
due to
Lemma

4
. Therefore, the condition
T\leq\frac{1}{3}e^{c_{2}B/2}\delta
implies
T\leq\frac{\delta}{\beta}
, which in turn yields
1-T\cdot\beta\geq 1-\delta
.

Finally, to establish the third statement, it suffices to show that, under the given conditions, the first and second statements hold simultaneously.
In particular, we show that if
\epsilon\leq 0.9\cdot\left(1-e^{-\frac{c_{2}}{4}}\right)
and
B\geq\frac{4}{c_{2}}\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)
, then the required upper bound on
T
in the second statement exceeds its required lower bound in the first statement. This implies that
T=\left(\frac{1}{1-1.1\epsilon}\right)^{B}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
is a feasible choice for both statements. From
\epsilon\leq 0.9\cdot\left(1-e^{-\frac{c_{2}}{4}}\right)
, we obtain
\frac{c_{2}}{4}\geq\log\left(\frac{1}{1-1.1\epsilon}\right)
. Moreover, from
B\geq\frac{4}{c_{2}}\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)
, we obtain
\frac{c_{2}}{4}B\geq\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)
. Combining these two inequalities leads to

\displaystyle\left(\frac{c_{2}}{2}-\log\left(\frac{1}{1-1.1\epsilon}\right)\right)B\geq\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)\implies\left(\frac{1}{1-1.1\epsilon}\right)^{B}\log(\frac{1}{\delta})\leq\frac{1}{3}e^{c_{2}B/2}\delta.

∎

5
Conclusions

We address robust subspace recovery (RSR) under both Gaussian noise and strong adversarial corruptions. Given a limited set of noisy samples—some altered by an adaptive adversary—our goal is to recover a low-dimensional subspace that approximately captures most of the clean data. We propose RANSAC+, a two-stage refinement of the classic RANSAC algorithm that addresses its key limitations. Unlike RANSAC, RANSAC+ is provably robust to both noise and adversaries, achieves near-optimal sample complexity without prior knowledge of the subspace dimension, and is significantly more efficient.
