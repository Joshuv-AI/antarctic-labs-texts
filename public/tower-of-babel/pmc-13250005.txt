Dry Eye Disease: Perspectives of a Patient and Both US and UK Physicians
Authors: Irene S Zhou, Dhiresh Bandaru, Jacklyn Vainshtein, Emily Clark, Radhika Rampat, Dagny Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDry eye disease (DED) is a multifactorial disorder of the ocular surface characterized by tear film instability and inflammation. Evaporative dry eye driven by meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) accounts for most DED cases and can cause a substantial burden on a patient’s quality of life. Current treatment algorithms include lipid-containing drops and in-clinic procedures, but the reliance on trial-and-error management can result in delayed symptom relief for patients. To better illustrate the patient’s lived experience of DED and the challenges of treatment options, a female patient (E.C.) in her mid-30s living with MGD-related DED collaborated with physician authors to develop this patient-led manuscript. We integrate E.C.’s narrative with a literature review and discussion of current guidelines in both the UK and the USA to examine how trial-and-error treatment approaches and limited access to advanced testing and procedures can affect patient outcomes. E.C.’s experience within the UK healthcare system illustrates how time constraints and financial barriers can delay effective treatment and contribute to patient frustration. E.C. reported fluctuating DED symptoms and poor correlation between clinical signs and symptom severity. Coupled with the feeling of being dismissed and mismanaged, E.C. felt that she needed to rely on online communities for novel therapy ideas and support to gain a better understanding of her disease. These perspectives will allow patients and clinicians to better understand the challenges of DED management and guide the development of more effective, patient-focused care pathways.Keywords: Dry eye disease, Patient perspectives, Patient experience

Key Summary Points

Dry eye disease (DED) is a common ocular surface disorder that can cause fluctuating symptoms of eye irritation and redness, and its trial-and-error treatment can lead to prolonged symptoms and reduced patient quality of life.This article incorporates a patient’s first-hand account of living with DED and two physicians (one in the UK and one in the USA) commenting on management options to explore the treatment burden for patients and discuss new therapies.Our patient experienced inconsistent diagnosis and trial-and-error therapy that prolonged symptoms and contributed to a sense of being dismissed by healthcare providers.Differences in system-level barriers between the UK and USA, such as financial limitations and fragmented healthcare services, can significantly impact outcomes for patients with chronic DED.

Introduction

Dry eye disease (DED) is a common disorder of the eye’s surface caused by a tear film imbalance that prevents the eyes from lubricating efficiently. Patients typically present with symptoms of excessive tearing, burning, or itching of the eyes, or feeling like something is stuck in their eyes. These symptoms are often worsened by allergies or environmental changes such as a rapid change from warm to cold temperatures or vice versa. However, since symptoms of DED can be heterogeneous, there are many instances of patients being misdiagnosed or not receiving adequate treatment of symptoms, leaving many patients feeling ignored and uncomfortable.

DED is driven by the hyperosmolality of tears, surface inflammation, and sensory abnormalities, and is typically classified into three categories: aqueous-deficient dry eye, evaporative dry eye, or mixed [1–3]. Meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD), characterized by duct obstruction or altered secretions, is the leading cause of evaporative DED, resulting in tear film instability, excessive tear evaporation, ocular surface inflammation, and the hallmark symptoms of DED [4–6]. MGD is highly prevalent, affecting an estimated 70% of Americans over 60 years old with risk factors including increasing age, female sex, Sjögren’s syndrome, contact lens use, and more [7–9].

The patient (E.C.) discusses her experience bouncing around between different healthcare providers and not finding symptom relief even after multiple therapies. Despite its high prevalence, DED is often written off as a minor condition, even though it is one of the most common complaints in ophthalmology clinics. Symptoms range from sensations of grittiness, burning, blurred vision, and tearing to cosmetic symptoms such as eye redness or eyelid inflammation. As highlighted in E.C.’s perspective, symptoms are unpredictable and can fluctuate from hour to hour, further adding to the difficulty of daily management. In addition to physical discomfort, DED can impact a patient’s quality of life. E.C. discusses her struggle with the social stigma of DED symptoms, describing how the constant tearing and eye redness frequently lead to embarrassment and unwanted attention.

Patient Perspective

Two years ago, on a particularly windy day, my left eye began watering and has not stopped since. At first, I believed this was an allergy symptom, and I attempted to treat myself with over-the-counter eye drops, and later through prescribed ones via my general practitioner (GP). After a few months of no results, my GP referred me to the optician, who diagnosed me with MGD and dry eye. I was sold expensive eye drops and a microwave heat mask, was told that this would be a lifelong condition that I would need to learn to manage, and asked if I had any questions before I was led out the door.

From there I spent months trying to make the eye drops work and using the eye mask twice a day, every day. I struggled with the eye mask as the heat necessary to make it work would leave large, obvious red patches around my eyes, and I would struggle with blurry eyesight for about 15 min after each use, making it hard to find a convenient time to use it. After about 8 months, I was seeing no tangible results. My eye was either watering incessantly or it wasn’t, and no consistency or variation of my treatment changed that. I called the optician to book an appointment to discuss next steps, only to be told that the eye drops prescribed were the only treatment option, and if my symptoms remained it would be due to user error on my end. When I pressed and asked to be referred to an ophthalmologist or specialist, I was met with dismissal.

Disheartened, I then moved to another optician who assessed me and gave me eye drops from the same brand but targeting dry eye and allergies, as well as a cleaning gel for my eyelids. They were sympathetic to my previous experience, and I was told to try the new drops for a while, and if I saw no changes to get in touch and they would refer me to a local dry eye clinic. For the first time since receiving my diagnosis, I felt seen and heard. While I did notice mild results with these drops, I still felt that, no matter what I did, there was no real relief from my symptoms. I got back in touch with the opticians, and I was told they could try me on another eye drop instead, and that they didn’t really intend to refer me on to a specialist as the eye drops “should work.” The optician suggested I get tested for Sjögren’s and that if that came back positive, they’d be able to help further. As I also have intermittent joint pain, dryness, rhinitis, and gastrointestinal symptoms, I have had a few healthcare providers (HCP), including physio, ear nose throat, and optician, refer me to my local rheumatology department; however, to date my blood work is not elevated to an amount they’ll consider. As I seem unable to access further optical care beyond a high street optician, who seem to be the gatekeepers to accessing further dry eye care in the UK, I have found myself at a loss on where to go from here.

My symptoms fluctuate frequently, from consistent tearing, to dried tears and green gunk around my eye, a gritty feeling when I first wake up, loss of visibility in times of low-light, light sensitivity, blurred vision, red eyes, and burning sensations. I can have a week where I have the majority of these symptoms, which makes it extremely difficult to go about my everyday life, and the next week I might have only one or two symptoms. While both of my opticians have sworn by the prescribed drops, I’ve never felt that the eye drops or the heating have done more than just provide temporary relief for a few minutes at most. My partner has been incredibly supportive throughout in dealing with the emotional effects of this condition, as I have gone through stages of depression and anxiety, as well as grief over being diagnosed with something that won’t go away and doesn’t seem to respond to treatment.

I’ve always wondered why this happened, if it was because I spent too many years wearing eye liner, or staring at my laptop/phone, or if it has to do with moving to a new country; I previously lived in a consistently moderate climate in California, and in the UK, I now experience colder winters, wetter springs, and more humid summers. My mother was recently diagnosed with dry eye; does that have a correlation? Could climate change be playing a role in it? If so, am I looking at my symptoms getting worse down the road? I have so many unanswered questions, and no one to answer them.

Approximately 1/3 of the population of the UK have dry eye [10]. However, with the looks I regularly get, I feel isolated in my symptoms and routinely have to respond to questions and jokes around the continual tears, both from colleagues, friends, and strangers who I can tell don’t understand my symptoms. I have heard so many variations on the joke “Don’t cry, it’s not that serious” more times than I care to remember. I consider myself a fairly confident person, but I still get embarrassed when I’m in a meeting and have tears rolling down my face that I’m trying to discreetly wipe away. When I run errands, I often leave my sunglasses on inside of the shops to try to hide my symptoms. I’d rather people think I look stuck-up than to be able to see how much discomfort I’m in. Even worse are the times when my symptoms are flared and I have visibly red eyes, and redness and inflammation around my eyelid. During these times, I’m embarrassed to leave the house. I hate to think other people are stuck in the same boat that I am, trying to manage their symptoms at home, and hitting wall after wall, unable to access support or find next steps for treatment, feeling embarrassed over something they aren’t able to control.

The most success I have had in learning about dry eye and MGD and treatment options has been through communities on Reddit. It was there that I learned about a handheld heating wand that was designed for dry eye, which is more discreet and easier to use than the microwaveable mask. It also came with a balm you can apply around your eye, which I use before heating and I put around my eye before sleeping to help with dryness during particularly bad periods. This was not a cheap option, however the cost was worth it to me to be able to take something with me on the go for the days my symptoms feel unmanageable. I’ve seen people recommend humidifiers, vitamins, and supplements, and have shared the research they’ve done around chemicals in facial products such as moisturizer and make-up that could affect dry eye. The amount of individual research people have done has been so helpful to me, and while I’m aware that this isn’t coming from medical professionals, it does come from other people’s lived experiences. It is more information than I’ve received from my HCPs, and it at least gives me hope that, by changing something in my routine, I might alleviate my symptoms.

It’s been helpful also to hear that I’m not alone in my feelings of depression and grief around my diagnosis. Hearing first-hand about the treatments that have worked for others, and how they handle the days where they just feel completely overwhelmed by it. So many posts are made about the emotional toll dry eye has had on their lives, how it has affected their personal life and relationships, as well as their general appearance. There are hundreds of questions on Reddit around makeup and dry eye, and as dry eye tends to affect women more than men, it is often an undiscussed element of the disease that many women have to come to terms with [11]. I went to Reddit to see what products people were recommending, but also to hear other women’s experiences of learning to live make-up free, which is the decision I eventually made, both due to necessity from my symptoms and frustration at my make-up being ruined before getting to my destination most days.

The overarching takeaway that I’ve found being part of this community is that so many people are diagnosed and then told to take whatever eye drops their optician recommends, and that that specific brand will work. So many people are then frustrated when the drops don’t alleviate symptoms, and are then left without support from their HCP. Most people have compiled together, through their own research, a treatment option that works for them and then share their successes or failures to the wider community. What I’ve learned through reading all of these stories is that there is no one-size-fits-all treatment for dry eye. Anyone who has managed to control their symptoms outside of seeing a specialist has done so through a unique treatment plan that they’ve had to figure out themselves, over time and usually with significant financial investment. I understand that it would be a lot for an optician to work with one patient to figure out a treatment plan like this, but why aren’t we given a list of options to try? Why is it that opticians are pushing a one-size-fits-all treatment for dry eye? How can people in my situation, who aren’t responding to treatment, access further care and advocate for ourselves? And why have I felt that I’ve gotten better support from a community of anonymous people worldwide on Reddit than I have from physicians in my own community?

I do feel there is a severe lack of patient care for those of us with dry eye. I have felt abandoned by the two opticians I’ve seen and am still struggling to figure out how to manage my symptoms or how to access seeing an ophthalmologist. I wish the HCPs involved in my case knew that when diagnosing me with dry eye that there was a weight to the diagnosis, that was going to affect me physically, mentally and emotionally. I had no idea the breadth of symptoms I would experience and how much it would affect my day-to-day life when I was diagnosed.

At present, I am stuck in my treatment journey. My husband is supportive of me going private at this point; however cost is a consideration, and I would like to unearth every available option through the National Health Service (NHS) to help lessen the amount we’ll have to pay out of pocket. Until then I will be doing my best to manage my symptoms and will continue to try out different options I’ve seen people discuss on Reddit, hoping something will stick.

Physician Perspective

UK Healthcare Perspective

E.C.’s narrative presented here will resonate with many clinicians working within the UK’s overstretched and often fragmented eye-care system. DED affects nearly one in three adults in the UK, yet the patient experience still diverges markedly from the framework laid out in TFOS DEWS II and expanded in TFOS DEWS III, both of which emphasize early subtype identification, objective diagnostic confirmation, and an individualized, staged management strategy. [10, 12]

The reality for many UK patients—particularly those with recalcitrant MGD—is a pathway that is inconsistent, limited in scope, and heavily reliant on high-street optometry as the primary and often only point of care.

TFOS DEWS II establishes a clear diagnostic and treatment algorithm that begins with confirming evaporative versus aqueous-deficient disease, assessing risk factors, measuring tear film stability, and evaluating gland structure and function [12]. TFOS DEWS III builds on this by integrating new diagnostic technologies and refining the concept of personalized care across disease phenotypes [13].

In the UK, however, the gap between this evidence-based model and what patients actually receive, is substantial. Several systemic challenges contribute to this discrepancy. First, NHS ophthalmology services remain under immense pressure, with referral thresholds focused on sight-threatening conditions. DED—even when severe or debilitating—rarely qualifies for an NHS referral, leaving high-street optometrists as the de facto managers. Many of these clinicians provide excellent care, but the time-limited, commercially driven structure of high-street practice does not always allow for the depth of assessment or follow-up consistency recommended in TFOS guidelines. As reflected in E.C.’s story, this often results in repeated cycles of lubricants and warm compresses rather than a TFOS-aligned, multifactorial treatment plan.

Second, the UK lacks structured, tiered dry eye services comparable to the approach advocated in TFOS DEWS II/III [12, 13]. While TFOS recommends tools such as meibography, non-invasive breakup time, tear osmolarity, and staged in-office procedures, these are largely confined to the private sector. NHS access to advanced diagnostics or procedural therapies remains inconsistent and geographically variable. Without clear referral pathways or widespread availability of dry eye clinics, patients who fail first-line therapy often stagnate in care, leading to patient frustration, worsening symptoms, and a sense of clinical abandonment.

Third, financial inequity plays a significant role in shaping outcomes. TFOS DEWS II and III both acknowledge that successful DED management frequently requires layered, combination therapy using both home-based and in-office interventions [12, 13]. Yet many UK patients cannot afford repeated purchases of lid hygiene products, heated masks, supplements, or private treatments such as intense pulsed light (IPL) or low level light therapy (LLLT)—leaving the burden of disease disproportionately higher among those with fewer financial resources.

Another critical dimension highlighted by TFOS DEWS III is the psychosocial impact of DED [13]. E.C.’s narrative vividly illustrates how often this burden is overlooked. DED is frequently minimized as a minor irritation, but its fluctuating symptoms can significantly impair self-esteem, social engagement, work performance, and emotional wellbeing. TFOS DEWS III stresses that quality-of-life impairment should be a core factor guiding escalation of care, yet UK consultations do not routinely integrate this into clinical decision-making. [13]

Women, who are more frequently affected by MGD, face additional challenges related to makeup intolerance, visible redness, and social stigma—issues rarely addressed explicitly in standard clinical interactions [11]. One of the most striking aspects of E.C.’s account is her reliance on Reddit and other online communities to understand her disease and identify treatment options. This growing trend underscores two unmet needs within UK care: first, patients receive limited education on chronicity, prognosis, and lifestyle modifications during routine appointments; and second, publicly accessible, clinician-led NHS resources on DED are scarce. TFOS guidelines emphasize patient engagement and structured self-management, but without adequate information, patients are left to curate their own treatment plans from anecdotal online advice—often with mixed results [12, 13].

Moving forward, several TFOS-aligned improvements could strengthen UK DED care. A national dry eye referral pathway, like those for glaucoma or age-related macular degeneration, would enable prompt escalation for patients with ongoing symptoms or notable signs and symptoms. Upskilling optometrists through DEWS-aligned continuing professional development, especially regarding treatment options and use of validated symptom questionnaires, would improve consistency. Selective commissioning of advanced therapies, such as IPL, could reduce long-term burden and improve outcomes. Finally, routine recognition of mental health impacts, as recommended by TFOS DEWS III, should become standard [13].

Ultimately, TFOS DEWS II and III remind us that DED is not a single disease with a single treatment, but a complex, multifactorial condition requiring personalized, adaptive care [12, 13]. For UK patients like the one described, success means more than improved scores; it means comfort, confidence, and the ability to engage fully in daily life. Aligning UK practice more closely with TFOS principles offers a path toward more equitable, compassionate, and effective dry eye management.

US Healthcare Perspective

As patients navigate the unpredictable nature of DED, they often describe feelings of frustration and helplessness. Clinical management of DED is frequently a trial-and-error process with artificial tears, warm compresses, and prescription drops. They are then told that any persistent symptoms after this treatment regimen are due to user error, despite TFOS DEWS II guidelines recommending escalation of care. This can leave patients feeling dismissed and unsupported as described by many patients on virtual platforms, especially when multiple interventions fail to provide relief, as in E.C.’s experience. Patients struggling with chronic DED will anecdotally turn to online support groups for validation of their emotions as well as guidance on how to manage the condition. Platforms such as Reddit and Facebook communities can provide patients with the opportunity to share treatment experiences and coping strategies. While the health advice shared in these forums may not always be evidence-based, it offers practical suggestions or anecdotal experiences that may provide relief for some patients. E.C. emphasizes that people who have successfully managed their DED symptoms typically do so after much trial and error and personal research, compared to the one-size-fits-all treatment suggested by providers.

Most treatments follow the TFOS DEWS II “stepwise” plan, which can be adapted based on the specific type and severity of dry eye to restore a healthy tear film [10]. The first steps are simple: learning triggers, adjusting the environment (humidifiers, screen breaks), and using lubricating eye drops regularly, similar to what E.C. was recommended by her optician and through her research. Lipid-containing drops are beneficial when blocked oil glands drive evaporation [12]. Lid hygiene with warm compresses (about 40–45 °C) for 4–5 min remains core, as recommended by the optician to E.C.; coupling it with gentle massage or expression improves oil flows; however, technique and consistency are crucial [14]. If there is anterior blepharitis or Demodex, tea tree oil-based care or prescription-strength 0.25% lotilaner ophthalmic solution can be added, which is likely the lid scrub that E.C. referred to [12, 15]. Perfluorohexyloctane ophthalmic solution, a unique water- and preservative-free formulation that specifically targets evaporative DED, is also available over-the-counter in some countries and was recently FDA-approved in the USA [16]. This semi-fluorinated alkane mimics the natural function of meibum and forms a protective barrier on the eye’s surface to decrease tear evaporation. For more chronic DED, anti-inflammatory drops such as cyclosporine and LFA-1 antagonist lifitegrast can be layered on as well [17, 18]. Short courses of topical steroids can treat flares but are not recommended for long-term use due to risks like glaucoma and cataract formation [12, 14]. To conserve tears, tiny plugs (punctal occlusion) may be placed after inflammation is controlled, especially in those with low tear production [12].

In-clinic procedures can also be explored to target the glands directly. In the USA, these treatment options exist in both optometry and ophthalmology offices based on practitioner preference. Intense pulsed light with manual gland expression has been shown to improve gland function [19]. Vectored thermal pulsation, a brief heat and massage treatment, can also improve gland secretions and comfort after a 12-min session [20].

Medicines that target gland function and surface inflammation are another option. Topical azithromycin can reduce lid redness and crustiness, improving comfort while helping to normalize the quality of the oils produced by the glands [21]. Oral doxycycline can also improve symptoms and tear stability over an eight-week course and works well in combination with azithromycin, so clinicians may choose it when lid inflammation or rosacea is prominent [22]. For stubborn blockage, intraductal probing to open the gland ducts has been shown to provide rapid relief for patients [23]. In advanced cases, TFOS DEWS II supports specialized options such as autologous serum tears, scleral lenses, and even amniotic membranes or surgical approaches to preserve tears [12]. Finally, several clinical trials are being conducted, including OCS-2 (a topical anti-TNF candidate), reproxalap, and AZR-MD-001, which aim to clear ductal obstruction to improve lipid secretion and reduce symptoms [24–26]. Together, these approaches reflect a shift toward personalized combination care that treats both inflammation and gland obstruction.

In the USA, DED management is provider specific, with both optometrists and ophthalmologists providing care for these patients. While most general practitioners are willing to provide initial treatment with eye drops and compresses and prescribe medications, only some providers have the instruments required to provide massage or light treatments. These treatment options are typically found on their websites or through referrals from other local providers. However, escalation of care from optician to optometrist to ophthalmologist is not required as all patients are eligible to set up an appointment and pay with insurance or out of pocket.

We anticipate that, based on E.C.’s perspective, she was deemed to fall within the initial stages of the TFOS DEWS II stepwise plan, although as she stated, no escalation of care was seen. Ultimately, a lack of standardization is evident within the treatment management of DED worldwide, which upon improvement can improve patient outcomes. Additionally, if E.C. was instead living in the USA, it would be difficult for us to predict what type of escalation of care she would receive without doing our own exam.

Conclusions

DED, although incredibly common with the number of cases steadily increasing, does not always have a straightforward management system, especially when addressed in the setting of two different countries, as an increasing number of treatment options are hitting the market. Owing to the uniqueness of every patient and their symptoms, practitioners must pay special attention to patients who are not getting relief from their symptoms, especially after trialing several of the first-line management options. Ultimately, the management goal of DED that should prevail is not a measurable value or change that the practitioner can observe, but rather the happiness and comfort of each patient.
