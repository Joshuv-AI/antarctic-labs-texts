Arthroscopic Targeted Screw Placement: A Minimally Invasive Approach for Hindfoot Fractures
Authors: Srihan Anand, Kevin D Martin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractArthroscopic techniques have emerged as a promising alternative to traditional open reduction internal fixation in the treatment of foot and ankle fractures, offering minimally invasive methods to address intra-articular fractures. These techniques provide direct visualization of the fracture site while preserving the soft tissue envelope and osseous blood supply, mitigating risks associated with open surgical approaches such as infection, malunion, and soft tissue complications. This Technical Note highlights the surgical technique and applications of arthroscopic targeted screw placement (ATSP) for talus fractures, trimalleolar ankle fractures, and distal tibia fractures. Utilizing preoperative imaging for surgical planning, the procedure involves arthroscopic reduction, debridement, and minimally invasive fixation under fluoroscopic guidance. Advantages of ATSP include reduced operative morbidity, earlier mobilization, and detection of subtle intra-articular injuries often missed by conventional methods. However, barriers to widespread adoption include the learning curve, costs of arthroscopic equipment, and the need for specialized training. Despite these challenges, arthroscopic techniques have shown improved functional outcomes, reduced postoperative pain, and lower complication rates compared to open reduction internal fixation. This Technical Note describes ATSP in detail, outlining its potential applications, procedural steps, pearls, pitfalls, and considerations for cost and training.

Traditionally, displaced intra-articular foot and ankle fractures have been treated with open techniques involving fragment reduction and internal fixation.1,2 However, these approaches may compromise osseous blood supply and are associated with wound and soft tissue complications, infections, delayed union, and nonunion, all of which may hinder fracture healing.

Arthroscopy is increasingly used for hindfoot fractures, including osteochondral defects, synovitis, arthrofibrosis, impingement, and arthrodesis.3, 4, 5 More recently, arthroscopy in the foot and ankle has been used in acute trauma to aid in intra-articular fracture reduction and reduce cytokines and metalloproteinases that lead to post-traumatic osteoarthritis.6, 7, 8, 9 By preserving the soft tissue envelope and osseous blood supply, achieving direct visualization of the articular surface, and reducing the likelihood of complications, arthroscopy presents a significant advancement in the treatment of foot and ankle fractures.7, 8, 9

This Technical Note shows the versatility of arthroscopic targeted screw placement for the management of talus fractures, trimalleolar ankle fractures, and distal tibia/pilon fractures. The article outlines step-by-step procedural methods along with pearls, pitfalls, and technical considerations, as well as potential advantages and disadvantages compared to traditional open approaches.

Surgical Technique

Preoperative planning is essential to determine patient positioning and implant selection. Plain radiographs are required, and if intra-articular fractures are suspected, a computed tomography (CT) scan is highly recommended. CT is paramount for planning screw placement, identifying loose bodies, and assessing the most biomechanically advantageous screw trajectory. Examples of patient radiographs are shown in Figure 1.

(A) Sagittal right side computed tomography scan of a low-energy pilon fracture showing a small loose body and impaction. (B) Corresponding axial image showing the rotational component of the die-punch lesion that can be rotated and secured.

The 1.9-mm NanoScope (Arthrex) is placed on the bone to visualize the fracture. The camera is removed, and a guidewire is passed through the sheath under fluoroscopy. Arthroscopic targeted screw placement can be performed through any portal, as the sheath protects the surrounding soft tissue.

Once the guidewire is in place and confirmed with fluoroscopy, we measure with a cannulated depth gauge. We then place a slotted drill guide over the guidewire and drill/countersink. We then slide a fully threaded headless compression screw (Arthrex 3.5-mm compression FT screw) down the drill guide and tighten under direct visualization, ensuring its countersunk (Fig 2). A mini C-arm fluoroscopy is used throughout to confirm placement.

(A) Patient is in the prone position, with a right talus fracture, viewed from the posteromedial (PM) portal, using a depth gauge. (B) A drill guide is placed over the guidewire to allow safe passage of the drill and screw. (C) A fully threaded 3.5-mm headless compression screw is placed down the guidewire through the drill guide. (D) An arthroscopic image from the PM portal showing the screw advancing down the guide.

Intraoperative C-arm fluoroscopy is necessary. In our cases, we use the mini C-arm, which reduces radiation exposure while giving us optimal x-ray positioning. We obtain lateral, mortise, and anteroposterior views to confirm the optimal screw placement, as shown in Figure 3.

Weightbearing radiographs of a left ankle following anterior arthroscopic targeted screw placement for an intra-articular distal tibia fracture. (A) Lateral, (B) mortise, and (C) anteroposterior views.

Anterior Distal Tibia Arthroscopic Reduction

The patient is positioned supine with the ankle resting in a neutral position off the end of the operative table or on a radiolucent foam bump.

A low-flow 1.9-mm NanoScope (Arthrex) is inserted through the anteromedial portal using the nick-and-spread technique. The anterolateral portal is then established under direct visualization, without traction, as shown in Figure 4.

Operating room image of a left ankle placed in the supine position with the ankle in traction. The 1.9-mm arthroscope is inserted into the anteromedial portal with a reduction device to facilitate elevation of the fracture.

Once inside the joint, we remove the fracture hematoma, metalloproteinases, and loose bodies with a standard small joint arthroscopic shaver and graspers. The fracture site is evaluated, and the fracture hematoma is removed utilizing the shaver and arthroscopic probe.

When fracture lines are clearly visualized, the surgeon can start to reduce the fracture(s). We typically use small elevators, probes, and picks to mobilize the fractures while preserving the cortical rim. This may require the establishment of accessory portals to insert reduction instruments and clamps. Noninvasive skeletal traction can also be utilized to provide ligamentotaxis reduction. Additionally, if the patient presents with an external fixator, it can be left in place to help maintain distraction, as shown in Figure 5. During fracture reduction, the arthroscope maintains visualization of the intra-articular reduction, while fluoroscopy is used to verify the global reduction. Temporary guidewire fixation can be percutaneously placed and later utilized for screw placement as needed, as shown in Figure 5. Additionally, arthroscopic targeted screws are placed in the same manner as described previously, keeping the screws low and tight to the articular surface, ensuring no joint penetration occurs.

(A) Right ankle in the supine position, following arthroscopic targeting. The camera is removed, and a guidewire is placed down the camera sheath. (B, C) Targeting for a patient with an external fixator in place. (C) The external fixator is maintained to provide joint distraction as visualized, allowing the intra-articular surface to be restored arthroscopically.

Talus Arthroscopic Reduction

Most patients are positioned prone, although anterior loose bodies or unique fracture patterns may require modifications. The patient’s feet should extend off the edge of the operating table in a neutral alignment, allowing for full plantarflexion and dorsiflexion. Fluoroscopy is performed using a mini C-arm, with the monitor and arthroscopy equipment placed opposite the surgeon.7

The procedure begins with the creation of posteromedial and posterolateral portals using the nick-and-spread technique adjacent to the Achilles’ tendon at the level of the medial malleolus, as shown in Figure 6. For talar body fractures, the portals are positioned slightly more distally at the distal fibula level. Optimal screw placement is perpendicular to the fracture site, which is also the optimal biomechanical location. A sinus tarsi portal may be added to address varus deformity. Throughout the procedure, the flexor hallucis longus tendon serves as a critical landmark for orientation.

Operating room image of a right ankle in the prone position. In this position, the talus fracture is reduced and visualized through the posteromedial (PM) and posterolateral portals. A guidewire is being introduced through the PM portal.

A 1.9-mm NanoScope enhances visualization secondary to 0° straightforward viewing, allowing inspection of the talar dome and subtalar joint. A shaver is employed to remove the deep joint capsule and clear out the fracture hematoma.

Once an initial reduction is performed, the fracture can be stabilized with temporary K-wires. Final reduction is achieved by manipulating the ankle and using the accessory sinus tarsi portal as needed, with an elevator or small reduction instrument introduced through one of the posterior portals. At this point, guidewires are placed according to the arthroscopic targeted screw placement (ATSP) technique described previously. ATSP is paramount as these screws must be countersunk and ideally just off the cartilage margin. Also, the sheath and drill guide protect the neurovascular bundle, which is dangerously close to the medial screws.

Posterior Malleolus Arthroscopic Reduction

Posterior arthroscopy is initially performed in each case to facilitate early and precise removal of loose bodies, minimizing fluid extravasation from subsequent incisions. Anterior arthroscopy is conducted if preoperative CT scans indicate loose bodies in the anterior quadrants.

The feet are left off the bed in a neutral position for ligamentous reduction and optimal fluoroscopic imaging. An external positioning arm, Trimano (Arthrex), and an ankle distraction strap are used to gently assist with distraction and positioning (as needed, not frequently used).

Standard posterior medial and lateral arthroscopic portals are created adjacent to the Achilles’ tendon at the level of the fibula tip, with the flexor hallucis longus tendon marking the medial safe zone. The fracture hematoma is cleared using an arthroscopic shaver, and loose bodies are removed. An accessory posterolateral portal is created proximally if needed to reduce interposed die-punch or posterior malleolus fragments arthroscopically. Next, arthroscopic targeted screw placement (ATSP) is performed via the standard portals, with no additional approaches needed. The drill guide allows for protected drilling and screw delivery while maintaining direct visualization.

If syndesmosis instability is identified after posterior malleolus arthroscopic reduction8 using a dynamic stress examination, or a 3-mm probe advances into the syndesmosis, we proceed to suspensory fixation. A medium-sized reduction clamp is used, as the clamp is tightened under direct arthroscopic visualization to ensure anatomic reduction is achieved. After fluoroscopic confirmation, we place a guidewire lateral to medial across the syndesmosis. We then drill and pass a suspensory fixation device (TightRope; Arthrex).

Rehabilitation

A splint or removable boot is applied postoperatively to protect soft tissue and prevent contractures. The patient is maintained nonweightbearing for the first 2 weeks in a postoperative boot. At 2 weeks, sutures are removed, and therapy begins, including stationary biking and blood flow restriction therapy. Patients are given oral 81 mg of aspirin twice daily during their nonweightbearing period or deep vein thrombosis prevention. During the first 6 weeks, patients are highly encouraged to utilize a hands-free crutch to maintain muscle reactivity. At 6 weeks, the patient initiates progressive protected weightbearing in the postoperative boot. The boot is discontinued at 10 weeks as tolerated.

Discussion

While open reduction internal fixation (ORIF) has been the standard for treating foot and ankle fractures, complications and poor outcomes may occur, often due to undetected intra-articular injuries that go unaddressed during surgery.1, 2, 3

Arthroscopy is increasingly used as an adjunct to ORIF due to the ability to identify subtle displacements or intra-articular lesions that may be missed in preoperative radiography and during ORIF intraoperative fluoroscopy.3,10,11 In select cases, arthroscopy has been described as a standalone method of fracture reduction and fixation, although its role continues to be defined.

In a comparative study between open and arthroscopic reduction in the treatment of supination-external rotation ankle fractures, Chiang et al.12 reported less postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, and a lower overall incidence of complications in the arthroscopy group. This included lower rates of reoperation for infection, a major complication following ORIF.12 Macera et al.13 found a 31.5% complication rate following ORIF, with 21.7% requiring further surgery. While these studies suggest potential benefits, further research is needed to clarify long-term functional outcomes and patient selection criteria.

The potential advantages of ATSP include precise screw placement under direct visualization, avoidance of an open approach, and reduced fluoroscopy time. These benefits can translate into preservation of the soft tissue envelope and potentially decreased wound complications. However, there are notable limitations to the arthroscopic approach. The most frequent events are transient neural injuries related to portal placement; anterior approaches most often involve the superficial peroneal nerve, while posterior work risks the sural and medial plantar branches. Contemporary series of posterior ankle/hindfoot arthroscopy report an overall complication rate of 6% to 7%, with neurologic complications predominating and a higher risk when an accessory posterolateral portal is used.14,15 These data emphasize meticulous portal placement within known safe zones and avoidance of accessory portals unless clearly indicated. Additional risks include iatrogenic chondral scuffing, tendon irritation, instrument breakage, hemarthrosis, infection, deep vein thrombosis, and complex regional pain syndrome. These events are uncommon but documented in systematic reviews and large series.16

This technique requires a significant level of arthroscopic confidence and experience. A learning curve exists; early cases may take longer, and surgeon experience is central to safety. Meta-analyses comparing arthroscopy-assisted fixation with ORIF suggest similar overall operation times, even when functional scores favor arthroscopy-assisted techniques, underscoring that the approach does not eliminate risk.17 Furthermore, the use of arthroscopic equipment introduces additional costs, which may pose financial challenges for some health care systems or patients.

In conclusion, ATSP represents a feasible technique for select foot and ankle fractures. Further research is warranted to define indications, compare outcomes with traditional approaches, and evaluate cost-effectiveness.

Video 1

This video shows arthroscopic targeted screw fixation of an intra-articular talar fracture. The patient is positioned supine, and a 1.9-mm NanoScope (Arthrex) is inserted through the anterolateral viewing portal under low-flow irrigation (30-40 mm Hg) to inspect the joint surface and fracture site. After diagnostic assessment, the fracture hematoma and loose bodies are debrided with a blunt probe and small joint shaver (0:26).

Reduction is obtained under combined arthroscopic and fluoroscopic guidance using a periarticular reduction clamp and temporary percutaneous guidewires (1:30). The NanoScope sheath is then aligned perpendicular to the fracture, and a guidewire is advanced to the optimal screw trajectory, confirmed with fluoroscopy (2:07). Screw length is measured with a depth gauge (3:07), and a slotted drill guide is placed to allow safe passage of fully threaded, headless compression screws (Arthrex COMPFx) (3:29).

The screw is advanced under direct arthroscopic visualization, with final countersinking completed by hand to avoid articular impingement (4:10). Postoperative fluoroscopic imaging shows perpendicular screw placement and stable fixation of the talar fracture (4:29).
