Hydrogen peroxide concentration as an indicator of cyanobacterial response to diurnal variation in light intensity
Authors: Takashi Asaeda, Mizanur Rahman, Junichi Akimoto, Akio Nohara, Fumiko Imamura
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe measured diurnal variations in oxidative stress conditions of cyanobacteria utilizing field observations and laboratory experiments in order to evaluate photoinhibition effects. On clear summer days, transparent bottles filled with surface water were set up at several depths and were collected every three hours together with the measurement of the photosynthetically active radiation (PAR). In the laboratory experiment, two cyanobacterial species were exposed to gradually increasing and then decreasing light intensities. The samples were analyzed with the PAR-induced (H2O2), along with the total hydrogen peroxide concentrations (total H2O2), the catalase activities (CAT), OD730, protein (Protein), and chlorophyll a (Chl a) contents, and so on. Protein was significantly proportionate with OD730 and Chl a, and was used as an indicator of cell biomass. Increasing PAR, H2O2 concentration increased proportionately with the PAR intensity. Then, an oxidative stress indicator in a cell, H2O2/Protein is given by the PAR divided by cell volume, evaluated by Protein. CAT activity in a cell, far largest among antioxidant activities, solely followed total H2O2/Protein. The prediction model for H2O2/Protein was developed with the sufficient agreement with the experimental and field observation results. The model elucidated that the maximum H2O2/Protein in a day was larger with lower cell density even at the water surface, indicating that the higher photoinhibition was imposed at low density, in addition to the lower attenuation of PAR. These results indicate that H2O2/Protein is an effective biomarker to indicate the stress level of cyanobacteria; the observed levels of H2O2 to freshwater may prove useful in designing the criteria for cyanobacteria management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-80269-6.Keywords: Cyanobacteria, Hydrogen peroxide, Photoinhibition, Diurnal behaviors, Antioxidant activity, Oxidative stressSubject terms: Ecosystem ecology, Freshwater ecology

Introduction

Cyanobacteria are photosynthetic prokaryotes found in all habitats, and they play a significant role in maintaining ecological balance. However, cyanobacteria blooms often produce toxic metabolites that are harmful to other organisms as well as to humans. Therefore, a suitable system to control their blooming is essential in the management of natural water.

Similar to other organisms, cyanobacteria face an array of abiotic and biotic stresses from the environment, including metal ion toxicity, salinization, temperature, light conditions, eutrophication, allelopathy, and pathogens1. As an adaptive mechanism, they exhibit various responses in morphology, biochemistry, physiology, and anatomy to counter these stresses. Among these responses, biochemical responses have widely been used as biomarkers to detect the stress on organisms2–4. The concentrations of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in aquatic plants, algae, and cyanobacteria, antioxidant enzyme activity, amino acid profiles, and gene expression are some of the biomarkers used to detect stress in these organisms.

As with other organisms, ROS accumulation causes harmful impacts on cyanobacteria as oxidative stress, such as protein denaturation, impaired photosynthesis, growth inhibition, and anatomical damage5,6. ROS are important for growth regulation and signaling mechanisms at the same time7,8. When biological systems produce ROS in their metabolism7, biological systems can control excess ROS production with the inherent scavenging enzymes and nonenzymatic components2,7,9–11. Under a stressed environment, however, ROS production exceeds its scavenging capacity, and consequently, ROS accumulate inside cells and induces oxidative stress.

Light intensity is an important factor affecting the growth of cyanobacteria12,13. At the same time, however, even moderate solar radiation may cause stress4,14. Cyanobacteria are capable of conducting photosynthesis and respiration via an elaborate electron transport pathway15. Collecting solar energy at photosystem II (PSII) in the thylakoid membrane oxidizes water molecules and produces electrons, which then reduce plastoquinone, a molecule involved in the electron transport chain, then are transported to PSI, where they are consumed in the synthesis of carbohydrates. However, surplus numbers of electrons, generated by an overabundantly absorbed solar energy at the PSII antennae complex in the PSII reaction center, produce super oxide radicals16,17. They damage cellular components, such as D1 protein, which otherwise recovers the damaged photosynthesis apparatus2,18,19. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) catalyzes superoxide radicals into H2O2, which is then detoxified into water by antioxidant activities5,7,10,20. This mechanism, photoinhibition, has been intensively studied in the last two decades for different types of plants14–18.

Photoinhibition is an important process in the ecology of cyanobacteria in water, and it contributes greatly to the reduction of cyanobacteria biomass. Measurements indicated that the peak in the vertical distribution of biomass is away from the water surface, although the intensity of the solar radiation is highest at the surface21,22. However, although the mechanism is essentially based on oxidative stress, so far only few approaches have been conducted from the standpoint of oxidative stress, at least, as intended for field management use23,24.

One of the most prominent ROS is hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). The oxidative stress of cyanobacteria is highly dependent on the cellular H2O2 content25. H2O2 concentration is yielded from scavenging part of the ROS via antioxidant activities in the cyanobacterial cells. Therefore, antioxidant activities may have a possibility to evaluate the effect of the oxidative stress2,25. However, in natural water, H2O2 is also produced by the photolysis of dissolved organic carbon (DOC) by UV26–30. It may affect the oxidative stress of the cyanobacteria, too. Therefore, H2O2 content is a more direct and suitable indicator of the stress level to reduce cyanobacterial biomass rather than antioxidant activities.

Interestingly, cyanobacteria are more vulnerable to H2O2 toxicity than other phytoplankton31–33. Recently, artificial endorsement of H2O2 has been explored as a method to suppress cyanobacteria blooms in natural water33–37. In addition to the endorsement of H2O2, the biological generation of H2O2 in cyanobacterial body has a possibility to reduce the cyanobacterial biomass at the surface layer.

The intensity of solar radiation changes diurnally. Over the daily cycle, different amounts of H2O2 are generated38–40. As a consequence, cyanobacteria may exhibit the different kinds and degrees of damage over the diurnal variations. All these effects must be elucidated to develop a system to prevent cyanobacterial blooms efficiently.

The production rate is influenced by the nutrient content of the water body41. However, it is extremely difficult to adjust all components in the field. On the other hand, in the laboratory studies, where the environmental condition is controllable, researchers can easily evaluate these effects by comprehensive monitoring the production rate of H2O2. However, there has been no suitable method to evaluate the diurnal variation of the cyanobacterial condition in the field under changing intensities of various stresses.

Thus, this research was designed to study (1) the effect of light regime changes on cyanobacteria, (2) the relationship between solar radiation and H2O2 concentrations, and the antioxidant enzyme activities of cyanobacteria, then (3) to develop a prediction model to estimate H2O2 per protein value subjected to the solar radiation.

Discussion

The H2O2 content in a cell and the absorbed energy

Protein was highly proportionate with OD730, and Chl a concentration. It is available as a suitable indicator of cell biomass.

The H2O2 concentration increases with exposure to PAR in both laboratory and field experiments. A similar increasing trend of H2O2 concentration of both the field and laboratory experimental results indicates that the absorbed solar energy increases the oxidative stress of cyanobacteria. The obtained trend was relatively unique, being shared by the investigated species and conditions with only slight difference.

The condensation process in the analysis indicates that the obtained H2O2 corresponds to the amount in algal biomass. Compared with the H2O2 concentration before exposed to PAR, the H2O2/Protein increased by 50 nmol/mg at OP (surface), 70 nmol/mg at BP (surface), and less than 10 nmol/mg at experiments. These amounts are biologically generated in a cell by the absorbed PAR.

The pond water contained some amount of dissolved organic matter (DOM) and can react with H2O2 in water. However, the amount of H2O2 obtained here was contained in the cyanobacterial cells, and the concentration in water was less than 10% of the amount in cells. Therefore, the effect of DOM on the H2O2 seemed to be negligible.

H2O2 concentration was measured in other types of plant species, where approximately 1–50 µmol H2O2/gFW was reported, and the growth was inhibited over 10 µmol H2O2/gFW35,42–45. Considering that cyanobacteria are more vulnerable to H2O2 toxicity than other plants, the H2O2 concentration in the present study is consistent with other plant results.

The H2O2 concentration in a day

In OP samples, 18.3 nmol/mg of H2O2 per protein was measured before exposure to PAR in the morning. It was about 181 nmol/mg of H2O2/Protein in BP samples (Fig. 7). In the laboratory, the amount of H2O2/Protein for M.aeruginosa (30 °C, 20 °C, and 10 °C) were 4.0, 1.8, and 2.0 nmol/mg, meanwhile 18.9, 11.3, and 20.3 nmol/mg for P. ambiguum (30 °C, 20 °C and 10 °C) (Fig. 7). These early morning values were likely induced by other stresses rather than PAR, and they are species-specific. Therefore, H2O2/Protein has more variety than cell density or protein only39,44–48. Compared with purely incubated laboratory samples, field samples contained various algal species. However, the composition of M.aeruginosa was significantly higher in the OP sample; thus, the impact of other algal species could be small.

After exposure to PAR, H2O2 concentration was further enhanced. The maximum H2O2 induced in a cell by PAR was 49.3 nmol/mg of H2O2/Protein for OP samples, 70.7 nmol/mg of H2O2/Protein for BP samples, 3.2, 6.1 and 5.5 nmol/mg of H2O2/Protein for M.aeruginosa experiment at 30 °C, 20 °C and 10 °C, and 4.4, 4.5 and 6.1 nmol/mg of H2O2/Protein for P. ambiguum experiment at 30 °C, 20 °C and 10 °C, respectively. Therefore, the same order of H2O2 amount, compared with other stressors, generated, exposed to the solar radiation. The cyanobacteria were, therefore, subjected to highly stressful conditions under high solar radiation.

Formulation to derive diurnal H2O2 concentration patterns in a cell

PAR-induced H2O2 concentration follows the intensity of PAR for a fixed protein content. At the same time, a part of generated H2O2 is catalyzed by the antioxidant activities. Although the light intensity sharply changed from the increasing phase to a decreasing phase at 12:00, the H2O2 concentration continued to rise even later, until 15:00 sampling, and then turned into the decreasing phase. In contrast, the increasing rate of the antioxidant activity gradually decreased with increasing H2O2 concentration (Fig. 6). H2O2 is catalyzed majorly by CAT activity, which is associated with the amount of H2O2 post signal transmission24,49. The high correlation between the H2O2 concentration and the CAT activity implies that the signal transmission of high H2O2 concentration is rapid8.

Under low PAR, the generation rate of H2O2 is balanced with the catalyzing rate by antioxidant activity. The CAT activity/protein is empirically given by the logarithmic function of the total H2O2/Protein (Fig. 6). With the increasing H2O2 concentration in a cell, the CAT activity become off the proportionate relationship with total H2O2/Protein. The effect of PAR-induced of H2O2 on the lethal concentration for cyanobacteria

Many studies have been conducted on the lethal H2O2 concentration of cyanobacteria, mainly via incubation in laboratories, with the endorsement of different concentrations of H2O2. Specifically, the growth of cyanobacteria was suppressed with H2O2 concentrations of 9000–60,000 µmol/L36, 30 µmol/L51,52 100 µmol/L26, 118 µmol/L12, 325 µmol/L43,53, and 275–500 µmol/L44,54. Although the H2O2 concentration in a cell does not necessarily reach the same as that of the endorsed concentration in the environmental water, these values are at least suitable reference for the lethal condition for the cyanobacteria.

Although biomass was not measured in this study, one third to one half of the cyanobacteria biomass is composed of protein43,55 indicating that the cell biomass was two or three times the protein content. Thus, a half or one third of the H2O2/Protein was biologically generated in mg of cell biomass exposed to the PAR.

The 50 nmol H2O2/protein is equivalent to about 100–200 nmol of H2O2 per biomass (mg). Considering the nearly neutral buoyancy of the cyanobacteria cells in water, the H2O2 concentration in a cell, which is equivalent to approximately 300–600 mg H2O2/L was contained within the cell volume for at least several hours. These estimates indicate that the PAR-induced H2O2 is sufficiently high enough to satisfy the lethal condition of cyanobacteria. In the present observation, many dead cells were observed after the period of high solar radiation. Therefore, it is possible to develop a method of controlling cyanobacteria by this natural system, which has a high solar radiation level.

Distribution of H2O2 per protein indicator and the effect on photoinhibition

H2O2 is generated with the absorbed energy, provided by PAR. Thus, H2O2 contained in a cell, H2O2/Protein, follows PAR/Protein. H2O2/Protein is higher with lower Protein content, or cyanobacterial biomass, even with the same amount of PAR. In the homogeneously distributed cyanobacterial biomass, the light intensity attenuates inversely proportional to the k value, which follows the protein concentration (Eq. 7). PAR attenuates exponentially with the depth. The PAR reduction is more rapid with higher Protein mass, which is normally high in eutrophic water and low in oligotrophic water.

In oligotrophic water, where cyanobacterial biomass is low, typically shown by the case with 1 mg/L protein, PAR is high with low absorption of light in the whole layer and protein is low. Accordingly, H2O2/Protein is homogeneously high in the whole layer. In addition, H2O2/Protein changes largely in the whole layer associated with the change of PAR. A part of H2O2 is catalyzed by the antioxidant activity after it is generated. The antioxidant activity is weakened with high H2O2/Protein (Eq. 2), and the high H2O2/Protein continues long in the evening. Therefore, the intensive photoinhibition is imposed on cyanobacteria, and their growth highly restricted.

In eutrophic water, on the other hand, as shown by the case of 100 mg/L protein, the H2O2/Protein is generally low even at the water surface, as well as at deeper layer, with high biomass and low PAR, which is associated with the high light extinction. As the light intensity is low in the deep layer, the H2O2/Protein keeps consistently low throughout the day. In eutrophic water, therefore, photoinhibition is less even at the water surface, which promotes the growth of cyanobacteria. Cyanobacteria seem to reduce the mortality rate by growing intensively and increasing their biomass.

Vertical migration of cyanobacteria

Lake cyanobacteria exhibit vertical migration behavior as an ecological strategy to minimize predation pressure, nutrient limitations, competition, and so forth24,55–58. In most cases, cyanobacteria move to the deepest layers of the water body from 12:00 to 18:00, later than the highest solar radiation period, and gradually rise thereafter59. The present results indicate that the highest H2O2/Protein occurs later than the time of highest illumination. Then, the H2O2 concentration gradually lowers due to antioxidant activities. The cyanobacteria stay in the deeper zone in the early afternoon, probably to avoid the high solar radiation and the oxidative stress before the recovery of homeostasis by the increasing antioxidant activities56.

Efficiency of destratification system on species bloom

In the destratification system, to reduce algal biomass, the passive migration of algae into the deep layer by mechanical enforcement, such as artificial mixing, wind mixing, and night-time convection, is expected to prevent photosynthesis60–62. The present study indicates a strong photoinhibition of cyanobacteria under solar radiation near the surface and under slightly lower light conditions as best for cyanobacteria to grow. Thus, there is a possibility to increase their growth after migrating into the deeper zone. It is suggested that further studies are required to estimate such aspects in order to potentially help to manage cyanobacterial blooms.

Cyanobacteria management criteria can be designed with the help of H2O2 per protein biomarker. With high cyanobacterial concentration, the photoinhibition lowers even with the same amount of PAR. As a result, photoinhibition in oligotrophic water is much stronger than in eutrophic water under the same PAR intensity. H2O2 per protein can help to identify specific cyanobacterial bloom threshold level in different temperatures and light intensities. This information is useful for more specific treatment of lakes or ponds in study subject.

Limitations

It is recommended to collect and analyze samples from different ponds and seasons from time to time in order to verify the present findings. For extended research, other cyanobacterial species should be used besides the selected species. Light intensities in laboratories may exceed or be similar to those in the field in terms of photosynthetically active radiation. It is possible to develop an intensive model to provide a more accurate explanation of the molecular mechanism of each species, although these models give a clearer conclusion of the present study.

Conclusions

Regardless of differences in cyanobacterial species, similar diurnal trends were obtained. Protein content, an indicator of cyanobacteria biomass, did not change substantially in one-day experiments. Biologically generated H2O2 by PAR increased following PAR intensity with approximately 3–4 h delay of the instantaneous PAR intensity. An oxidative stress is evaluated by the amount of H2O2 in a cell, which is given by the H2O2/Protein. In the increasing phase of PAR, such stress was proportional to the instantaneous PAR intensity, divided by the cell volume, the protein concentration. CAT activity in a cell, which is by far the largest compared with other antioxidant activities, solely followed the total H2O2 concentration in a cell rather than PAR-induced H2O2. Using these relationships, the prediction model to estimate H2O2 per protein value was developed with a sufficient agreement with experimental and the field observation results. The application of the model elucidated that the H2O2 per protein was higher with lower protein concentration even at the same PAR intensity, indicating stronger photoinhibition is imposed on the low cyanobacterial culture. With high cyanobacterial concentration, the photoinhibition lowers even with same amount of PAR. These results indicate that (1) H2O2 per protein is an effective biomarker to detect the stress level of cyanobacteria, in designing the criteria for cyanobacteria management, and (2) photoinhibition is far stronger in oligotrophic water than in eutrophic water under the same PAR intensity.

Methodology

Field observation

Field observations were conducted on a clear windless day, at two ponds near Tokyo, Ojagaike pond (OP) (35° 33′ 29′′ N, 140° 19′ 30′′ E) on September 6th, 2022, and at Besshonuma pond (BP) (35° 51′ 25′′ N, 139° 38′ 29′′ E) on September 24th, 2023. The average depth was about 2.0 m at OP, and 1.0 m at BP. Figure 9 shows a general diagram of the collection of pond samples at different depths.

The set-up of the observation. Three bottles were collected for analysis from each sampling time.

Because all the water is subject to convection at night, the water was homogeneous in early morning. Before 6:00, six semi-cubic transparent bottles filled with surface water and tightly capped were set up at different depths: surface water, 0.1 m, 0.3 m, and 0.5 m (BP only). At 9:00, 12:00, 13:30 (OP only), 15:00, 18:00 and 19:00, one bottle was collected from each depth and stored in a cool box filled with dry ice (~ − 20 °C) for transporting to the laboratory. Therefore, from each sampling depths 100 mL liquid were collected from the initial bottles in each collection time. Those 100 mL At each sampling time, light intensity, i.e., photosynthetically active radiation (PAR), was measured at each depth, along with temperature, pH, conductivity, and dissolved oxygen content (DO), by using a light intensity meter (Apogee, MQ-200, United States) and a water quality meter (U-51, Horiba, Kyoto, Japan). The peak of solar radiation occurred at 11:40 there.

The temperature was in the range of 27.5–32.6 °C in OP, and 30.5–32.9 °C in BP, while the pH fluctuated from 8.42 to 8.92 in OP, and from 8.85 to 9.45 in BP. The turbidity was about 24 NTU, and ranged from 27.5 to 30.2 NTU, in OP and in BP, respectively. The conductivity was 23 ms/m in OP, and from 28.6 to 30.2 ms/m in BP.

Nutrient concentrations of these ponds were TN 1.5 mg/L, TP 0.15 mg/L in OP, and TN 1.2 mg/L, TP 0.3 mg/L, in BP, respectively.

To identify the dominance species, Hallegraeff et al.63 process was utilized. The collected OP and BP samples were placed in a sedimentation chamber to allow them for settle. The samples were concentrated by gentle filtration. Samples wer adapted to room temperature to reduce the risk of air-bubble formation in counting chamber. After that the species were identified and counted under a microscope. In OP, the dominant species was Microcystis aeruginosa, accounting for 70% of the cell density (7500 cells/mL), while 28% was attributed to the diatom, Aulacoseira granulata (Aulacoseiraceae, 3050 cells/mL). Other species occupied less than 1% of cell density. About 55% of the biomass and 2/3 of the light absorption are attributed to M. aeruginosa64. In BP, more than 99% was occupied by Lingbya spp, (Lyngbya limnetica 410,000 cells/mL, Lyngya contorta 150,000 cells/mL).

In photosynthetic organisms, H2O2 induced oxidative stress, negatively affecting Fv/Fm measurements. This oxidative stress can impair the function of Photosystem II (PSII), which is directly involved in the measurement of Fv/Fm. When analyzing Fv/Fm, it’s important to control or account for the presence of ROS like H₂O₂, especially if the objective is to assess the health of the photosynthetic system in an aquatic ecosystem. In the meantime, although high levels of H2O2 can affect Chl a measurement indirectly by affecting phytoplankton and other photosynthetic organisms, they do not directly affect the Chl a pigment or measurement methods (such as spectrophotometry or fluorometry).

Laboratory experiments

The laboratory experiments were conducted with more accurate conditions to confirm the trend observed in the field experiment. Microcystis aeruginosa (N111) and Phormidium ambiguum (N2119) were obtained from the National Institute of Environmental Studies (NIES), Japan. All experiments were conducted by using incubators (MIR-254, Sanyo, Tokyo, Japan). These two strains were cultured and acclimatized in an autoclaved BG 11 medium65 for 14 days at 30 °C with a 12 h:12 h light-and-dark (25 µmol/s m 2) cycle, using a VBP-L24-C2 light source (Valore, Kyoto, Japan) and subcultured until sufficient amount gained. Each sufficient culture was divided into 18 flasks (100 mL), for 10 °C, 20 °C, or 30 °C and the maximum light intensities (600 µmol/s m 2), for triplicate, (altogether 36 flasks), which were used as experimental units. Within the experimental period, the variation of the cell density was relatively small compared to other factors. In order to elucidate the effect of light intensity and temperature, as was the small cell density in the field, the cell densities of experiments were adjusted to relatively smaller values. Consistent relationships generally existed between the experimental and field results, in spite of the difference in cell densities between field and experiments. During the experiment, the cultures were exposed to light intensity, shifting from 0 µmol/s/m2 at 6:00 to 600 µmol/s/m2 at 12:00 by changing the intensity with an interval of 50 µmol s/m2 at every 30 min. Then, the light intensity was decreased at the same rates until 18:00. The temperature was kept constant (10 °C, 20 °C, or 30 °C) throughout the experiment. Every 3 h, (6:00, 9:00, 12:00, 15:00, 18:00, and 21:00), three flasks were taken for a stress response analysis, while the remaining experimental units were manually shaken and continued for the light exposure. The collected samples were subjected to bioassays, which are described below. The experimental units per each light condition were triplicated to confirm reproducibility.

The light intensities were measured using a quantum sensor (Apogee, MQ-200, United States) and adjusted uniformly in the media. Cyanobacteria culture flasks were gently shaken three times daily to ensure homogeneous exposure to the light environment.

Laboratory samples used 2 mL tubes, 1 mL of M. aeruginosa or P. ambiguum, while for pond samples of 4 mL were taken. The cells were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed and kept at − 80 °C until biochemical analysis. Cyanobacterial cell walls are prevented from breaking down by using the buffer (phosphate buffer (pH7; 0.1 M), NaOH solution for measuring H2O2 and protein, respectively). Therefore, five to eight 3 mm beads (BMS Inc., Japan) were added with buffer and vigorously shaken. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was used as an extract for further analysis. Each culture group was replicated three times to conduct the experiment.

Chlorophyll concentration analysis

Using ISO 10260:1992; Gregor and Marsšalek’s method61,62, Chl a concentrations were measured in cyanobacteria samples. First, 1.5 mL of 90% ethanol was added to the unfrozen cyanobacteria cells. The mixture was homogenized with 3 mm beads via vertexing and kept at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) for 24 h overnight in darkness. Identification of cells and cell density measurement

A small aliquot of 1 mL of each treated cell suspension was fixed at room temperature for 2 h using glutaraldehyde solution and stored at 4 °C for counting. Cell identification and their densities were obtained by counting the fixed cyanobacterial cells using a light microscope supported with a digital camera (Nikon DXM 1200 C) at its highest resolution (4116 × 3072 pixels), equipped with a bacteria-counting chamber with a depth of 0.02 mm (SLGC, Japan).

To estimate the growth of cyanobacteria, the OD730 was measured by taking 1 mL of sample from each flask. The OD730 was measured with a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (uVmini-1240, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) at an optical absorption wavelength of 730 nm using a previously proposed methodology66.

Total protein content analysis

The cyanobacterial cells were homogenized using 0.5 M NaOH solution43. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 4 °C for 15 min at 10,000 rpm. Then, 51 µL of supernatant extract was added to 969 µL of Coomassie Bradford protein assay for each sample. The mixture was kept at room temperature for 10 min, and the absorbance was recorded at 595 nm using a UVmini − 1240. The protein content was determined using a known concentration series of Albumin67.

Stress assay

H2O2 assay

The cellular H2O2 contents were estimated according to the e-FOX method68,69, and the results were confirmed by TiClO4 method70. In the e-FOX method, 1mL of the sample was taken from each flask, and the supernatants were removed by centrifugation at 10,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. To extract the cellular H2O2, the cell pellets were homogenized in 1 mL of 0.1 M pH7.0 phosphate buffer and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant (100 µL) was added to 1 mL of the assay solution containing 0.250 mM ferrous ammonium sulphate, 0.1 mM sorbitol, 0.1 mM xylenol orange, 1% ethanol, and 25 mM H2SO4, which had been deoxygenated with gaseous nitrogen to prevent artifact production in hydrogen peroxide during the reaction. The mixture was kept for 15 min at room temperature. After that the optical absorption, mainly the xylenol orange complex that forms with oxidized metals, such as ferric iron, was measured at 560 nm using a spectrophotometer (UVmini-1240).

Using the TiClO4 method, 1 mL was collected from each flask, and the supernatants were removed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The cell pellets were washed once with ultrapure water (Milli-Q direct 5, Merck kGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). To extract the cellular H2O2, the cell pellets were homogenized in 1 mL of 0.1 M pH6.5 phosphate buffer and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. A total of 750 µ L of 1% Titanium chloride in 20% H2SO4 (v/v) was then added to initiate the reaction. The optical absorption was measured at 410 nm using a spectrophotometer (UVmini-1240), following centrifugation (10,000 g for 5 min) at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C). The H2O2 concentration was determined using a standard curve of the optical absorption, prepared using a series of samples with known H2O2 concentration. However, the absorption at 560 nm–410 nm may have a possibility to include the effect of other soluble compounds67,69,70. Thus, the H2O2 concentration was calculated from the slope of the standard curve obtained from the known H2O2 concentration, which was offset, derived by the intercept absorption rate with zero H2O2 concentration samples66. The results were compared with the results of both analyses, and suitable agreement was obtained (r = 0.85)68,71.

CAT, APX, and POD activity assays

In the CAT activity assay, a total of 1 mL of each culture was centrifuged at 10,000 g at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was removed, and the cell pellets were homogenized in 1 mL potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.0), containing 0.1 mM EDTA. After centrifuging again (10,000 g at 4 °C for 10 min), the supernatant was collected as the enzyme extract. The CAT activity was measured by reacting 15 µL of 750 mM H2O2, 920 µL of potassium phosphate buffer, and 65 µL of extract supernatant. Optical absorption was measured at 240 nm using UV mini-1240. The measurements were recorded every 10 s for 3 min, and the CAT activity was calculated using an extinction coefficient of 39.4 cm/mM72. For the APX assay, the reaction mixture contained 100 µL of enzyme extract, 200 µL of 0.5 mM ascorbic acid in 50 mM of potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), and 2 mL of 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), mixed with 60 µL of 1 mM H2O2. The decrease in absorbance at 290 nm was recorded every 10 s for 3 min. The APX activity was calculated using an extinction coefficient of 2.8 mM− 1 cm− 173. The POD activity was assayed following74, with slight modifications. The cyanobacteria cells were harvested by centrifuging 1-mL samples at 10,000 g at 4 °C for 10 min and removing the supernatant and cell pellets, which were homogenized in 1 mL of potassium phosphate buffer (100 mM, pH 7.0). A total of 65 µL of enzyme extract were then mixed with 920 µL of potassium phosphate buffer (100 mM, pH 7) containing 20 mM of guaiacol. With the addition of 15 µL of 0.6% H2O2, the absorbance change was then recorded at 470 nm every 10 s for 3 min using UV mini-1240. POD activity was calculated with an extinction coefficient of 26.6 mM/cm.

As the effect of APX and POD were smaller than that of CAT, analyses of APX and POD were conducted only for limited number of samples, and CAT analysis was conducted only the experimental and the surface water of OP samples.

Statistical analyses

The collected data were tested for normality with the Shapiro–Wilk’s test before the statistical analyses. All results were presented as the mean ± SD (n = 3). The data were subjected to a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and the bivariate analysis was used and followed by Pearson’s correlation method to evaluate the relationship among the parameters. Independent sample t test was performed to find correlation between each time period for example 9:00 to 12:00. One way ANOVA is performed to find relationship different times in a day. The statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS V25.
