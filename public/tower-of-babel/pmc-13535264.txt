Fertility Preservation in Female Patients with Cancer. Part III: Royan Human Ovarian Tissue Bank Guideline for Prepubertal Girls and Women, A Review
Authors: Naeimeh Sadat Abtahi, Zeinab Ghezelayagh, Rouhollah Fathi, Mojtaba Rezazadeh Valojerdi, Bita Ebrahimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Advances in early cancer diagnosis and treatment have significantly improved survival rates. However, as awareness grows about the adverse effects of cytotoxic therapies on ovarian function and fertility, an increasing number of patients are seeking fertility preservation before undergoing anti-cancer treatments. Embryo cryopreservation remains the gold standard for fertility preservation when there is sufficient time for ovarian stimulation and oocyte retrieval. For single women, oocyte freezing offers a viable alternative, allowing them to preserve their eggs for future use. For prepubertal girls and women who cannot delay treatment, ovarian tissue cryopreservation is currently the only feasible method of fertility preservation. Between 2000 to 2010, researchers at Royan Institute conducted a wide range of investigations on ovarian tissue cryopreservation, providing a structured and evidence-based fertility preservation protocol for cancer patients candidates for this service. In 2010, the Royan Institute established the Royan Human Ovarian Tissue Bank (OTB) as a subgroup of the Embryology Department. After obtaining ISO 9001:2015 certification, the OTB was officially transferred to the Royan Infertility Clinic. This article presents the Royan OTB guidelines for cryopreserving ovarian tissue in prepubertal girls and women with cancer, described step by step.

1. Introduction

Data from the Iranian Cancer Association (ICA, Tehran, Iran) indicates a concerning increase in cancer cases nationwide in recent years (1). Thanks to advances in modern medicine, many cancers are now treatable when detected early, allowing patients to resume normal life after chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery. As a result, many cancer types are no longer considered fatal (2). Although the mentioned methods are the only way of treatment in many cases and have increased the population of survivors of malignant diseases, they can cause secondary premature ovarian failure (POF) and decrease ovarian follicular reserve. This has caused the treated people to suffer lifelong infertility with little hope of regaining fertility potential after cancer treatment. Consequently, the demand for fertility preservation among cancer patients is greater than ever before (3).

In this regard, various assisted reproductive techniques (ART), such as egg freezing, embryo freezing, and ovarian tissue freezing, have played a pivotal role in maintaining fertility. These methods are different for single, married, adult, and immature people, and the most appropriate method will be chosen for each patient’s circumstances. For married women who have enough time for stimulation, embryo freezing is the most effective and widely accepted method worldwide. Otherwise, when the patient is single, egg freezing is considered the best option. If egg and embryo freezing are not possible due to time or clinical limitations, ovarian tissue freezing becomes the only available method of fertility preservation (2, 4). Ovarian tissue freezing preserves a large number of follicles at various developmental stages without delaying cancer treatment. This method is the only option for preserving fertility in prepubertal girls. The primary goal of ovarian banks worldwide is to maintain fertility for these patients (5). Reports on the birth of more than 200 live and healthy babies after orthotopic transplantation of frozen-thawed ovarian tissue until 2022 underscore the effectiveness of this method (6). Therefore, this article aims to provide the current recommendations of the Royan Human Ovarian Tissue Bank (OTB) about ovarian tissue cryopreservation in women and prepubertal girls with cancer and compare them with international guidelines. It should be noted that the Royan Human OTB Guideline is the first of its kind in Iran. All the recommendations in this text are based on consensus, clinical cases, or original research.

2. International guidelines of fertility preservation in cancer patients

Fertility clinics worldwide provide personalised fertility preservation options tailored to each patient’s health status and in accordance with the center’s established guidelines. The most well-known guidelines are the Edinburgh Research Explorer guidelines (7, 8), the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) guidelines (9), and the Gardner guidelines (10). Although each country and center have its own guidelines, several nations have established structured fertility-preservation networks at regional, national, and international levels (11). The fertiPROTEKT (12, 13), Oncofertility Consortium, and Danish Fertility-Preservation networks are three different-sized networks that are referenced in this article (11). Here, we compare the Royan OTB guidelines with these international standards.

3. Royan Institute clinical practice guideline for ovarian tissue cryopreservation in girls and women with cancer

The Royan OTB, as a part of the Royan Oncofertility team, provides consultation to cancer patients without requiring a scheduled appointment. The patient should be referred to the OTB by the oncologist as soon as the disease is diagnosed. It should be noted that in cases where a patient presents without a referral letter from the oncologist, Royan OTB will still accept her. In both scenarios (with or without a referral letter), the Royan OTB gynecologist communicates directly with the patient’s oncologist to confirm and coordinate the procedures required for ovarian tissue cryopreservation. The consultation should be conducted at least one day before the laparoscopy surgery. In this initial consultation, individual factors such as age, marital status, physical and mental condition, type and stage of cancer, degree of disease progression, prior treatments, and any previous fertility treatments are evaluated (14). It should be noted that ovarian tissue cryopreservation is age-dependent, and case-by -case assessment is required for prepubertal girls. After the consultation, a contract is signed between the OTB and the applicant, approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Royan Institute (IR.ACECR.ROYAN.REC.1396.212). An informed consent form is obtained from adult patients, and for those under 18 years old, permission is obtained from their parents. This consent form specifies that if the participant passes away or fails to respond within six months of the last recorded contact, the biological sample will either be (1) destroyed or (2) utilized for approved scientific research - with the choice determined by the participant's documented preference.

In the following, the Royan OTB guidelines for ovarian tissue cryopreservation are described step by step and noted wherever any difference or contradiction with international guidelines exists.

3-1- The guidelines for ovarian tissue removal

3-1-1- Patient acceptance criteria

The criteria considered for accepting patients for ovarian tissue freezing in the Royan OTB are as follows:

• Age limitation

- The patient must be younger than 37 years, but in some cases, with written consent from the patient or her first-degree relatives, the age range may be extended up to 40 years.

- The patient must not have received any chemotherapy or radiotherapy before ovarian tissue cryopreservation if aged 15 years or older at diagnosis. Mild, non-gonado -toxic chemotherapy is acceptable if the patient is younger than 15 years (15, 16).

- The patient should not be pregnant.

• Ovarian reserve assessment

- Antral follicle count is conducted by ultrasonography to suggest sufficient ovarian reserve.

- Hormonal assessment for AMH (>1 ng/mL preferred, but not absolute) and FSH (preferably <10-12 IU/L) is also conducted (17).

• Considering the risk of gonadotoxic treatment

- A high risk (>50%) of premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) exists after gonadotoxic treatments (18, 19). For these cases, the ovarian tissue is cryopreserved according to the situation of the patient.

- A realistic chance of surviving for five years survival should be considered before cryopreservation (8).

• Evaluations

- Serology tests for HIV, hepatitis B (HBs), and hepatitis C (HCV) must be conducted before ovarian tissue cryopreservation.

- Cryosection (intraoperative pathology test) results must be negative for malignancy.

- If ovarian tissue is involved in malignancy, the patient’s sample is refused. In other cases, admission is not prohibited.

• Exclusion criteria

- Individuals with advanced age and/or very low ovarian reserve.

- Patients medical conditions elevating surgical risks.

- Patients with primary ovarian cancer or metastatic dis ease involving the ovaries.

The Edinburgh, Gardner, and Danish guidelines use nearly identical selection criteria for ovarian tissue dissection. The main difference is that these guidelines also exclude patients who have already had children and require samples to be negative for HIV, syphilis, and HBs.

3-1-2- How to remove the ovarian tissue?

The gold standard for ovarian tissue removal is laparoscopy. Laparotomy should only be performed, if necessary, within the scope of the underlying oncological disease. The ovarian tissue should be detached using scissors, and sources of bleeding should only be coagulated in a targeted manner (punctate coagulation). An intraoperative pathology test (cryosection) must be conducted by the center where the ovarian tissue is removed to rule out the possibility of the presence of malignant cells. For this purpose, the surgeon must send an ovarian tissue sample to the laboratory during surgery, and its result must accompany the sample when sent to the bank.

3-1-3- How much ovarian tissue should be removed?

At least 1/2 to 2/3 of a complete ovary (preferably without ovarian pathologies) must be removed.

- The cortex and medulla should be removed together; peeling the cortex is not advised.

- In exceptional cases, depending on the surgeon’s judgment, the medical team’s decision, and the patient’s underlying disease, both ovaries may be removed (bilateral ovariectomy).

- Before surgery, the patient should be informed about the possibility of unilateral ovariectomy, especially if there is a high risk of POI due to primary or secondary reasons.

3-2- Ovarian tissue transport guidelines

The OTB places no restrictions on which hospital may perform ovarian tissue biopsy surgery for its patients, and the patient herself is free to choose the hospital (with the OTB's guidance according to each patient’s conditions).

Special terms and conditions for transferring the ovarian sample from the hospital are provided in a letter sent to the hospital along with the transfer medium and cold transfer box.

The liaison person between the OTB and the hospital must be a first-degree relative of the patient. He/she must inform the OTB about the sample size and bring the sample with the cryosection and serology results (HBs-Ag, HBs-Ab, Anti-HCV-Ab, Anti-HIV-Ab).

• Transport temperature

The temperature of the ovarian tissue transfer medium in the special cold transfer container should be kept at 4-8°C. At this temperature, cellular metabolism is decreased, and oxygen consumption is reduced. The low temperature is provided by dry ice and accompanying ice pads inside the cold transfer box.

• Transport time

The sample should be placed in the transfer medium and transferred to the OTB as soon as possible, within 2 hours after the ovarian tissue is separated from the patient's body.

As all guidelines focus on centralised cryobanking, the transfer of the sample from distant cities takes more time. Therefore, the transport time can be increased up to 24 hours if suitable conditions are maintained. The tissue must be transferred to the mentioned cold transfer boxes that maintain a stable temperature between 4-8°C.

The Edinburgh, Gardner and Danish guidelines also have the same criteria for ovarian tissue transfer and mention a limitation of 18-24 hours for ovarian tissue transfer.

It should be noted that, although 18 to 24 hours may be feasible under optimal conditions, more than 24 hours significantly increases ischemic damage & follicular loss. Each additional hour decreases the likelihood of success, making less than 24 hours the gold standard for reliable results.

• Transport medium

There are no adequate investigations that show which transport medium is most suitable, but according to Royan OTB guidelines, medium199 plus HEPES+20% human serum albumin (HSA) or isotonic saline solution is used. Before transferring the sample to the transfer tube, the sample must be washed several times with sterile normal saline. The transfer tube should be tightened properly and placed in the cold transfer box.

3-3- The guidelines for cryopreservation of ovarian tissue

3-3-1- Dissection

• Dissection site and instruments

According to international standards (Edinburgh guide line, Danish), the ovarian tissue must be processed in a suitable in vitro fertilization (IVF)/ART laboratory with a sterile Class II laminar airflow unit. Dissection is per formed using straight, wide, threaded forceps, Metzen baum scissors, and occasionally scalpels. Instruments with sharp or hooked tips that may damage the ovarian cortex are not recommended.

• Dissection medium

The suitable and necessary medium for ovarian tissue dissection is medium199 plus HEPES plus 20% HSA, prepared at least 24 hours before the freezing process. It should be noted that according to the Edinburgh, Danish guidelines, in emergencies, isotonic saline solution may be used if preparation of the dissection medium is not possible.

• Dissection process

The size of the ovarian tissue should be written before dissection. All procedures are performed under a Class II sterile hood on a cooled surface. Tissue is washed with Medium 199 plus HEPES plus 20% HSA. The medulla is removed, and thin cortex strips of 1–1.5 mm thickness, sized 5 × 8–10 mm, are prepared. International guidelines (Edinburgh, Danish, FertiPROTEKT) suggest strip sizes ranging from 1×2×3 mm to 2×5×15 mm, which slightly differ from Royan’s practice.

For histological evaluation before freezing, one tissue piece is randomly fixed and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E).

3-3-2- Vitality test of the ovarian tissue

Histological evaluation before cryopreservation is conducted by the human ovarian bank expert via H&E staining. Markers assessed include total tissue integrity, follicular population, and morphology, oocyte evaluation (in terms of pycnotic, vacuolation, or granulation of the oocyte nucleus, and ooplasm, oolema, and zona pellucida situation), granulosa conditions (in terms of attached/distributed, and pycnotic). Also, ovarian tissue functionality is examined, considering the presence or absence of the corpus luteum or corpus albicans in the tissue. All this information is recorded and stored in the Royan human OTB histology description form (Fig .1), which is specific to each individual.

3-3-3- Cryopreservation methods

In general, two major freezing techniques are used in reproductive medicine: slow freezing and vitrification. Both methods have reported live birth (2). Royan OTB currently uses the slow freezing method (20).

The Royan human Ovarian Tissue Bank (OTB) histology description form. This form is specific to each individual and describes the histological condition of the tissue before cryopreservation. Total tissue integrity, follicular population, and morphology, oocyte evaluation (in terms of pycnotic, vacuolation, or granulation of the oocyte nucleus, and ooplasm, oolema, and zona pellucida situation), and granulosa conditions (in terms of attached/ distributed, and pycnotic) are written in this form.

• Freezing medium

Selecting the proper cryoprotectant is an important point, as it is largely responsible for the cryopreservation success. The most common and best-established cryoprotectant internationally used is dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). At Royan OTB, the freezing medium is Medium 199 plus HEPES, supplemented with 2% HSA and 10% DMSO.

• Freezing process

Fresh freezing medium is prepared on the day of cryopreservation, stored at 4°C for ≥1 hour, and used to fill cryovials (1.5 mL/vial). Ovarian tissue strips are rinsed in the medium, loaded into vials, and promptly transferred to a programmable freezer. The cryopreservation is done slowly in a programmable freezer (Cryologic, Australia) with the following program: cooling starts from 0°C to -8°C at 2°C/minutes followed by manual seeding, and then cooling continues to -40°C at 0.3°C/minutes and ramps to -70°C at 5°C/minutes. The program includes holding time for 6 minutes at -70°C. Samples are then plunged into liquid nitrogen at -196°C for long-term storage.

3-3-4- Sample labeling and filling

All the frozen samples are initially stored in a temporary tank before being transferred to the main tank and completing the documents related to the serology and pathology results. According to the Royan OTB guidelines, the ID no for each patient is given after the storage of the samples in the main tank, which is a code consisting of 4 digits. For example, 87/1/2/5 (34-51) in order from left to right is Reception No./ Tank No./ Rack No./ Floor No. (tube number). Each patient also receives an individualised cryopreservation card documenting frozen strips, oocytes (if present), freeze/recharge dates, and expiry dates (Fig .2).

Liquid nitrogen tanks are checked daily and recharged according to the prepared checklist every 2-3 days in Royan OTB.

International guidelines (Edinburgh, Danish) recommend storing samples in two separate tanks at different locations as a safety measure.

The Royan human ovarian tissue cryopreservation and banking card. This card is individualized for each patient. The ID number for each patient, number of freeze ovarian strips, number of freeze oocytes (if graafian follicles were observed during cryopreservation procedure), and the freeze, recharge, and expire date are written in this card.

3-4- Transplantation of ovarian tissue guidelines

• When and which age is appropriate for transplantation?

According to Royan OTB, transplantation is considered for women under 45 years of age with a disease-free interval of at least 3-5 years (21-23). Before transplantation, a basic evaluation (sonography monitoring and FSH, LH, and AMH hormonal assay) and consultation for the causes of infertility must be performed for each patient.

Depending on the follicular density and quality of the patient's stored tissue (recorded in the histology description form), at least half of the frozen strips are thawed and used for transplantation. The available reports from the centers with successful transplantations that have led to live births (2, 6, 22) indicate that in many cases, there is a need to re-transplant the frozen tissue pieces for the patient.

• Thawing medium preparation

It should be noted that for cases requesting tissue transplantation, one cryopreserved vial is randomly selected and thawed for histological evaluation to assess tissue integrity before the transplantation procedure. Samples should be thawed a few hours prior to surgery in a clean room next to the operating room. The cryovials are placed at room temperature for 2 min before being immersed in a water bath at 37°C for 2 minutes. The tissue strips are then transferred to the thawing medium (medium 199 plus HEPES supplemented with 10% HSA) and incubated at 37°C for 30 minutes, repeated twice.

• Surgical technique and site of transplantation

Transplantation of ovarian tissue is generally performed via laparoscopy, after obtaining informed consent from the patient. In routine clinical practice, orthotopic trans plantation is most frequently employed, with implanta tion sites including the pelvic peritoneum, parietal peri toneum, or directly on or within the ovary. The detailed surgical approach is fully described in another section of the Royan Institute guidelines. The tissue pieces must be positioned with the medullary surface in contact with the transplantation site, aligned side by side rather than stacked. According to international recommendations, the transplantation site should be marked (e.g., with ti tanium clips) and thoroughly documented for follow-up purposes.

4- Conclusion

Current challenges and future possibilities

Since its establishment in 2010 as a subgroup of the Royan Institute’s Embryology Department, the Royan Human OTB has provided consultation to over 1,000 patients between the ages of 7 and 47 years. Ovarian samples have been cryopreserved from 117 patients aged 7-35 years, most commonly with diagnoses including cervical adenocarcinoma, breast cancer, Ewing's sarcoma, contralateral ovarian tumor, endometrial adenocarcinoma, colon cancer, Hodgkin's lymphoma, acute lymphoblastic leukemia, and major thalassemia. To date, three patients have undergone ovarian tissue transplantation after completing their cancer treatment. Despite these promising outcomes, progress in optimising the long-term function of transplanted ovarian tissue has been limited, and this remains a critical challenge for future research. Enhancing graft survival, prolonging endocrine function, and improving pregnancy rates will be key priorities in the years ahead.

In alignment with international best practices, which emphasise standardised sample storage, documentation and filing, and patient follow-up, many countries are shifting toward centralized ovarian tissue banking systems. Centralization improves sample quality by standardizing sample transfer methods, enhances patient follow-up through unified databases, and optimizes the allocation of government financial resources by minimising the number of centers. Given these advantages, our country should actively adopt this model, embedding it within national healthcare policy and institutional culture. Such a shift would not only harmonise practices with global standards but also enhance reproducibility in research and strengthen clinical applications of ovarian tissue banking.
