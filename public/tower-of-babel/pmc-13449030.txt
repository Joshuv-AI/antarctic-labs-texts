Pediatric Eosinophilic Cystitis: A Case Series From a Single Institution
Authors: Leslie Hoyt, Alexandra Bain, Stacy Ardoin, Miriam Conces, Christina Ching, Kalyani Sen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEosinophilic cystitis (EC) is an inflammatory disease characterized by eosinophilic bladder infiltrate. It is primarily seen in adults with presenting symptoms of dysuria and gross hematuria, and diagnosis is confirmed with bladder biopsy. Possible etiologies include allergies, prior bladder trauma, or parasitic infections and management involves nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs, antihistamines, corticosteroids, and occasionally immunologic agents. EC is rare and especially uncommon in the pediatric population. We present a case series of five pediatric patients diagnosed with EC over a 20‐year review at a single institution, including how they presented and their management. This case series highlights the first pediatric case of EC requiring an immunologic agent for symptom control as well as several cases uniquely associated with schistosomiasis.

1. Introduction

Eosinophilic cystitis (EC) is a rare inflammatory disorder of the bladder characterized by irritative voiding symptoms and transmural eosinophilic infiltration [1]. EC has been associated with allergies, bladder trauma, parasitic infections, and chemotherapeutic exposures [2, 3]. It is theorized to be the result of an antigen–antibody reaction [3]. There have been approximately 200 cases reported of EC [2]. A case series of 135 patients demonstrated a median age of presentation of 41.6 years, with an age range of 5 days to 87 years; there was a male predominance only seen in the pediatric population [4]. Presenting symptoms in this case series included frequency, dysuria, gross hematuria, suprapubic pain, and/or urinary retention, with approximately 25% having a positive urine culture and 43% having peripheral eosinophilia [4]. Diagnosis was confirmed histologically with a bladder biopsy demonstrating prominent eosinophilic infiltrate [4].

There is no consensus on the management of EC with various suggestions often including a combination of oral medications including nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs, antihistamines, and corticosteroids [2, 5]. Additional considerations can include surgical intervention or immunologic agents for severe refractory cases [3]. Immunologic agents often target the eosinophilic pathway. Mepolizumab is a monoclonal antibody that acts by antagonistically binding to the cytokine interleukin‐5 (IL‐5) to prevent binding to its receptor [1, 6]. IL‐5 stimulates the allergic inflammatory response by binding to IL‐5 receptor positive cells like eosinophils. By blocking IL‐5 from binding to the surface receptor on eosinophils, mepolizumab prevents the eosinophilic‐driven inflammatory response [1, 7].

We reviewed our experience with EC at a single pediatric tertiary care hospital. This case series utilized the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist for reporting. Inclusion criteria included all patients who received a diagnosis code for EC from 2005–2025 within the institution. This study involves historical patients and a retrospective chart review for which we have IRB approval (NCH IRB#16‐00249). Unfortunately, due to the historical nature, we were unable to request consent in patients for whom we are unable to contact. We were able to ask one of the contemporary patients for consent of being included for which we obtained verbal consent. We identified five cases of pediatric patients diagnosed with EC who required multiple different methods of management for symptom control.

2.1. Case 1

T.M. is a 15‐year‐old previously healthy female who initially presented to her pediatrician with symptoms of dysuria and terminal gross hematuria. Urine culture was obtained demonstrating 50–100,000 colony forming units (cfu)/mL Escherichia coli and she was treated with a 7‐day course of antibiotics. Her symptoms initially resolved but returned after several days despite a repeat sterile urine culture. She was referred to a urology clinic at this time. She reported no smoking history, history of allergies, or history of previous UTIs. Physical exam was only notable for moderate left costovertebral angle tenderness. She continued to have significant gross hematuria and a complete blood count demonstrated eosinophilia at 12.9% (normal 0.3%–9.3%). Renal and bladder ultrasound (RBUS) demonstrated asymmetric posterior bladder wall thickening and mild bilateral hydronephrosis (Figure 1). CT abdomen and pelvis also demonstrated hyperattenuating, irregular bladder wall thickening.

Renal ultrasound demonstrating mild bilateral hydronephrosis and irregular posterior bladder wall thickening measuring up to 1.57 cm.

The patient underwent cystoscopy with findings of grossly abnormal urothelium from the trigone extending to lateral and posterior walls of the bladder, with sparing of the anterior wall and dome. The bladder had sessile appearing lesions carpeting the involved areas (Figure 2). Urine cytology was negative for high‐grade urothelial cell carcinoma. Bladder histopathology of biopsies taken demonstrated a diffuse eosinophilic infiltrate in both superficial and deep (muscle containing) specimen biopsies. Bladder histopathology of biopsies taken demonstrated a diffuse eosinophilic infiltrate with eosinophils present in the urothelium and throughout the lamina propria with extension into the deeper muscle layer (Figure 3).

Intraoperative images captured during cystoscopy. Diffuse inflammation noted along the trigone, lateral, and posterior walls.

Histologic examination demonstrates sheets of eosinophils within the lamina propria.

The patient was treated with loratadine, prophylactic antibiotics, and prednisone 60 mg daily for 1 week followed by a steady 6 week taper—after which her symptoms resolved. A RBUS obtained 1 month post operatively demonstrated resolution of previous bladder wall thickening (Figure 4). However, shortly after her prednisone taper was complete, her dysuria and gross hematuria recurred. In reviewing her prior treatment, she had developed bothersome side effects from taking steroids including weight gain and mood disturbances and thus preferred to avoid restarting them. She was referred to rheumatology who discussed mepolizumab for treatment. In 2 month follow‐up after having started treatment, TM had complete resolution of symptoms and was tolerating the immunotherapy well. She is planned to undergo monthly mepolizumab injections for 2 years. Her prophylactic antibiotic and her loratadine are being stopped while she is maintained on mepolizumab.

Ultrasound of the bladder demonstrating resolution of posterior bladder wall thickening, now measuring 0.45 cm.

2.2. Case 2

M.D. is a 10‐year‐old male with a history of asthma who initially presented to the emergency department (ED) with a 1‐month history of severe suprapubic pain, dysuria, gross hematuria, and pyuria. Urinalysis demonstrated 2464 WBC (normal < 6) and 4 RBC (normal < 2). Despite multiple sterile urine cultures, he was empirically treated with three courses of antibiotics for presumed UTIs without symptom improvement. He underwent RBUS, which demonstrated right uroepithelial thickening and a hyperemic, irregularly thickened, and trabeculated bladder wall. He was thus referred to the urology clinic, where video urodynamics demonstrated left Grade 2 vesicoureteral reflux, detrusor overactivity, but otherwise a normal capacity bladder with normal compliance. He was trialed on anticholinergics for 2 months with no change to his symptoms, including his intermittent gross hematuria. He underwent cystoscopy, which showed multiple areas of erythema and inflammation. Bladder biopsies were taken, and pathology showed mixed inflammation including eosinophils, lymphocytes, few plasma cells, and neutrophils. Eosinophils and lymphoplasmacytic inflammation extended to the lamina propria. Findings were consistent with EC. He was treated with a course of oral antihistamines but never returned for scheduled follow‐up evaluation in the clinic. Of note, there have been no further ED visits for hematuria or other genitourinary symptoms. Blood work was never checked for eosinophilia.

2.3. Case 3

Y.A. is a 4‐year‐old male who presented to the ED with abdominal pain and gross hematuria. He had no previous history of UTIs or urinary symptoms. Urinalysis and urine culture showed 952 RBCs (normal < 2) but no bacterial growth. He did have a RBUS, which demonstrated asymmetric bladder wall thickening with submucosal hypoechoic lesions. Due to a remote history of travel to Egypt as an infant, urine and stool were tested for Schistosoma sp. ova, which were negative. Both Urology and Infectious Disease were consulted inpatient for ongoing evaluation and management. The patient was taken for cystoscopy with bladder biopsy. There were multiple flat, erythematous lesions along the posterior bladder wall, which were biopsied. Pathology showed subepithelial eosinophilic inflammation concerning for mild EC versus parasitic infection. He had peripheral eosinophilia with an absolute eosinophilic count of 1000/μL. With the patient′s remote travel history to Egypt, even though there were no Schistosoma sp. ova or parasites identified, it was presumed he had developed acute EC secondary to chronic genitourinary schistosomiasis and was treated with two doses of 300 mg praziquantel. He had complete symptom resolution and repeat RBUS 1 month posttreatment was normal. He is 2 years from initial treatment and remains symptom free.

2.4. Case 4

A.B. is a 15‐year‐old male who presented to the ED with a 1‐day history of left upper quadrant abdominal pain. The patient reported similar pain several years ago when he was treated for “worms” after completing a “rock eating competition”. Pertinent social history includes immigrating from Somalia 3 months prior. He underwent RBUS demonstrating left hydroureteronephrosis to the ureterovesical junction with two calculi identified, the largest measuring 7 mm. The patient was arranged to undergo left ureteroscopy with laser lithotripsy. At the time of the procedure, inspection of the bladder revealed two areas of erythematous, edematous, and somewhat cobblestone‐appearing mucosa lesions that were concerning for infectious etiology. Several biopsies were taken of the lesions. Urine culture obtained at the start of the procedure was negative for infection and negative for ova and parasites. Pathology demonstrated extensive inflammatory infiltrate, composed mostly of eosinophils, within the transitional epithelium. Admixed within the infiltrate were a few oval‐shaped eggs and calcified parasites. Findings were consistent with acute EC and schistosomiasis. The patient was treated with a one‐time dose of 1200 mg of praziquantel. The patient underwent ureteral stent removal 4 weeks after surgery and inspection of the bladder demonstrated persistent cobblestone appearance. The patient was scheduled for repeat RBUS in 4 weeks′ time for re‐evaluation but he never returned for his appointment and was lost to follow up. Blood work was never checked for eosinophilia.

2.5. Case 5

Y.Y. is an 11‐year‐old female who presented to the ED due to a two‐week history of dysuria, gross hematuria, and lower back pain. Pertinent social history includes immigration from Somalia as a child. On exam, she was noted to have left costovertebral angle tenderness. Urinalysis demonstrated moderate leukocyte esterase, negative nitrite, and large blood. Urine culture was sterile. RBUS demonstrated mild left hydronephrosis with debris in the bladder but normal bladder wall thickness. Due to the hematuria in the absence of infection, the patient underwent cystoscopy, which demonstrated several areas of erythematous, edematous lesions along the superior and posterior bladder wall. Several biopsies were taken of the lesions. Pathology demonstrated inflammatory infiltrates consisting of eosinophils and histiocytes. Numerous parasitic eggs consistent with Schistosoma sp. were present in the submucosa. The patient was then lost to follow up and underwent unknown treatment. The patient did have a point of care urinalysis performed at her well child visit with her pediatrician 2 months after cystoscopy and this demonstrated trace leukocyte esterase, negative nitrite, and negative blood. Blood work was never checked for eosinophilia.

3. Discussion

This case series features five pediatric patients with EC, ages ranging from 4 to 15 years old. Theorized triggers for EC were schistosomiasis in three of the patients, E. coli UTI in one patient, and unknown cause in the last patient (although they had asthma). Schistosomiasis is a parasitic disease that affects at least 200 million people worldwide. Schistosoma sp. are most commonly found in sub‐Saharan Africa, the Nile valley, the Arabian Peninsula, and less commonly found in China, the Philippines, and northeast Brazil [8]. This case series highlights the importance of obtaining an appropriate patient history and that recent travel to any of the above‐mentioned regions should prompt consideration of urinary schistosomiasis.

All patients presented with gross hematuria and various degrees of other symptoms including dysuria, suprapubic pain, or flank pain. Bladder ultrasound demonstrated abnormal wall thickening in three of the cases. Cystoscopy demonstrated there is a lack of uniform presentation of EC with variable lesions including flat, cobblestone, and edematous, but all appeared hypervascular. There have been several reports in the literature documenting cases of EC mimicking a malignant bladder lesion, which is why transurethral resection of bladder lesion may be the initial form of management in select cases [9, 10].

Three of the patients were lost to follow‐up before assessment of their response to treatment. Of the three patients with schistosomiasis, one had complete resolution of symptoms with praziquantel alone, one had cystoscopic evidence of persistent disease after praziquantel but was then lost to follow‐up, and the final patient was immediately lost to follow‐up. The patient without an identifiable cause was treated with antihistamines and lost to follow‐up. The patient with an initial E. coli UTI was treated with antihistamines, prophylactic antibiotics, and initially with corticosteroids.

Treatment for EC should be personalized to the patient. First line management can include removing or treating the stimulus that caused the allergic reaction, antihistamines to mediate the IgE response, and/or corticosteroids to decrease the inflammatory state [2]. Surgical intervention is typically reserved as second line management if symptoms are unable to be controlled with medication alone; surgical intervention can include bladder augmentation or cystectomy. There is one documented case of a patient with EC who developed a contracted bladder and required augmentation ileocystoplasty for management [11]. Additional cases have been documented demonstrating the necessity of a simple cystectomy for control of EC symptoms [12].

Corticosteroids can alleviate systemic symptoms for numerous disease states. In this setting, they can improve allergic reactions through anti‐inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects. However, long term dosing can result in serious adverse effects, some of which include cushingoid features, osteoporosis, myopathy, increased risk of infections, as well as hypomanic or depressive moods [13]. These side effects can be especially detrimental to a pediatric patient, as we experienced with one of our patients.

Alternative medical management targeting the eosinophilic pathway can offer more specific treatment for EC with decreased systemic side effects and avoiding surgical intervention. Mepolizumab is a targeted immunologic agent that blocks the IL‐5 protein from binding to its receptor to reduce the production and proliferation of eosinophils [1, 7]. It has been shown to be successful in the treatment of other eosinophilic conditions such as eosinophilic asthma and there are several case reports documenting successful management of symptoms with EC [7, 14]. Common side effects include injection site reactions and headaches [14]. We report the first pediatric case of EC requiring a targeted immunologic agent for symptom control, which has been well tolerated and successfully managed her symptoms thus far.

4. Conclusion

EC is a rare condition consisting of eosinophilic infiltration of the bladder wall. Cystoscopy and bladder biopsy are the gold standard for diagnosis. Management can include treating the allergy, nonspecific medications including nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs, antihistamines, and corticosteroids, and/or surgical intervention. Immunologic agents are an additional form of management and can specifically target the eosinophilic pathway, thereby decreasing some of the systemic side effects associated with nonspecific medications such as corticosteroids and avoiding surgery. We report a case of its successful use. Overall, there are multiple forms of management for EC, which can be tailored to the patient and their specific needs.
