Adenosine‐Sensitive Focal Atrial Tachycardia Late After Atriopulmonary Connection Fontan Procedure: A Case Report
Authors: Takahiko Kinjo, Daiji Takeuchi, Keiko Toyohara, Kei Inai, Morio Shoda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We describe a case of adenosine‐sensitive focal atrial tachycardia (AT) in a patient late after atriopulmonary connection (APC) Fontan procedure. Radiofrequency application successfully abolished the AT; meanwhile, conversion to total cavopulmonary connection (TCPC) and/or Maze procedure may fail to suppress the AT because the origin was crista terminalis near the sinus node. This case highlights the importance of catheter ablation for AT in patients undergoing the APC Fontan procedure, even before TCPC conversion. Clinicians should know this infrequently reported phenotype.

Atrial tachycardia (AT) is common in patients late after an atriopulmonary connection (APC) Fontan procedure. Several retrospective studies [1, 2] reported that most atrial tachyarrhythmias are intra‐atrial reentrant AT; however, details of AT with other mechanisms remain unknown. Here, we describe a case of adenosine‐sensitive focal AT that occurred late after the APC Fontan procedure.

A 36‐year‐old man with refractory narrow QRS complex tachycardia was referred to our hospital (Figure 1). The patient was diagnosed with (S, D, D) double outlet right ventricle, ventricular septal defect, straddling and overriding mitral valve, pulmonary stenosis, and right aortic arch 1 month after birth. At 7 years of age, the patient underwent APC Fontan procedure. Pulmonary artery to right atrial appendage anastomosis with an autologous pericardium was performed. No additional atrial incision was made apart from the right atrial appendage–pulmonary artery anastomosis. He was followed up at another clinic and did not receive any medication until he developed tachycardia. An intravenous 20 mg bolus of adenosine 5′‐triphosphate terminated the tachycardia after atrioventricular block (Figure 1C). Cardiac catheterization revealed high central venous pressure (CVP) with 16 mmHg.

Catheter ablation was performed after bisoprolol failed to suppress the tachycardia. Three‐dimensional computed tomography revealed a dilated right atrium (Figure 2). Ventriculoatrial dissociation was observed during ventricular overdrive pacing during tachycardia and sinus rhythm. The activation map of tachycardia showed a centrifugal pattern from the crista terminalis (Figure 3A), and the tachycardia was diagnosed as focal AT originating from the crista terminalis. The 3.5‐mm irrigation catheter (ThermoCool SmartTouch Surround Flow, Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA, USA) recorded the local electrogram of the earliest activation site, in which 125 ms proceeded to the onset of the P wave (Figure 3B,C), and subsequent radiofrequency application (30 W) terminated the AT (Figure 3D). Additional ablation was required for the induced macroreentrant AT around the atrial septal, which was patch used for oblique partitioning. Thereafter, the AT was no longer induced. The patient remained free from AT for 7 months and underwent conversion to total cavopulmonary connection (TCPC).

This case highlights the importance of catheter ablation for AT in patients after the APC Fontan procedure, even before TCPC conversion. Several studies have shown that TCPC conversion [1] or combined TCPC conversion and modified Maze procedure [3] inhibits atrial tachyarrhythmia. However, AT originating near the sinus node, as in this case, may not be suppressed even after TCPC conversion with the Maze procedure, because the lesion sets of Cox‐Maze IV [4] or modified procedures [3] were designed to avoid the sinus node region. This case provides a detailed characterization of the atrial substrate in a patient after the Fontan procedure, highlighting the importance of recognizing key anatomical structures—such as atriotomy scars, the crista terminalis, sinus venosus, and right atrial appendage—when evaluating the atrial substrate in this population. Chronic high CVP may have contributed to marked dilation and electrical scarring of the sinus venosus.

Focal AT in this case might have resulted from triggered activity, although micro‐reentry cannot be completely excluded. A low‐voltage area was observed at sinus venosus, which may be prone to dilation because of the lack of pectinate muscles. The crista terminalis may have stretched as the sinus venosus was dilated. Adenosine‐sensitive focal AT, typically caused by triggered activity, is common in patients without congenital heart disease [5]. Meanwhile, focal AT in patients late after the APC Fontan procedure has not been well documented. The high incidence of macroreentrant AT [1, 2] may reduce the frequency of focal AT in this population.

Overall, we described a rarely reported case of adenosine‐sensitive focal AT in a patient late after the APC Fontan procedure. This case highlights the importance of catheter ablation for AT in patients undergoing the APC Fontan procedure, even before the TCPC conversion.
