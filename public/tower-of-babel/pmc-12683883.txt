Overexpression of NtGSTU10 gene enhances nicotine content in hybrids by promoting the synthesis and transport metabolism of nicotine
Authors: Zejun Mo, Ying Huang, Kai Pi, Lili Duan, Min Xie, Benshan Long, Renxiang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNicotine exhibits positive heterosis, and through its utilization, new tobacco varieties with ideal nicotine content have been bred. However, the potential mechanism underlying the increased transport and nicotine accumulation capacity in hybrids remain poorly understood.ResultsThe purpose of this study was to reveal the regulatory role of the key gene NtGSTU10, which was identified during the early stages of nicotine heterosis. NtGSTU10-over expression lines were created through genetic transformation, and the nicotinic content, transport coefficient, and heterosis of NtGSTU10-over expression lines and wild-type tobacco plants were compared. The results suggested that the nicotine content in the leaves of V×OB7 (The over expression hybrids were produced by crossing Va116 as the female parent with OB7 as the male parent.) and V×OB12 (The over expression hybrids were produced by crossing Va116 as the female parent with OB12 as the male parent.) was significantly higher than that of the wild-type hybrid (V×B). Compared to the V×B, the nicotine transport coefficients of V×OB7 and V×OB12 were significantly increased by 39.50% and 40.67%, respectively; and nicotine heterosis was significantly increased by 120.07% and 127.59%, respectively. Further analysis showed that overexpression of the NtGSTU10 gene could result in up-regulated expression of genes related to nicotine synthesis (AOC, QS, ODC, and PAO) and transport (ABCB1, ABCC1, and ABCC2), as well as down-regulation of negative regulatory factors (ERF1, ETR, and JAZ).ConclusionsThe results indicated that overexpression of the NtGSTU10 gene promotes the synthesis and transport metabolism of nicotine in tobacco, positively contributing to increased nicotine content in hybrids. The research findings provide a molecular basis for regulating the nicotine transport and accumulation in hybrids, and also provide genetic resources for the targeted cultivation of new tobacco varieties with suitable nicotine content.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12896-025-01063-4.Keywords: Nicotine, Heterosis, NtGSTU10 gene, Synthesis, Transport, Nicotiana tabacum L.

Background

Hybrids play a crucial role in modern agriculture and are of great significance in ensuring global food security [1]. Compared to genetically different parents, hybrid varieties often exhibit outstanding performance in various aspects, including vigorous growth, higher reproductive success rate, strong stress resistance, and significant improvement in yield and quality [2, 3]. Researchers are dedicated to uncovering the genetic basis of exceptional traits in hybrids by developing predictive models and exploring strategies for their stable performance. Several theories have been proposed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms, including the dominance hypothesis [4, 5], overdominance hypothesis [6], and epistasis remain the most prominent and widely supported [7, 8]. In this process, advancements in quantitative genetics, particularly in the in-depth study of phenomena such as “allele-specific expression“ [9] and “single-parent expression complementation“ [10], are providing new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the outstanding performance of hybrids.

Nicotine, a characteristic alkaloid predominantly present in tobacco, holds great economic significance [11]. The ideal nicotine content in tobacco leaves is generally around 2.5%, as deviations from this level may significantly impact the sensory characteristics, including fragrance, flavor intensity, and sensorial attributes of smoke [12]. A natural nicotine-derived essence greatly enhances the sensory profile of cigarettes by enhancing both fragrance and smoking satisfaction. This nicotine-enriched formulation provides additional economic benefits by reducing the required quantity of premium tobacco leaves, thereby lowering overall manufacturing expenses [13]. Nicotine and its derivatives have significant potential in multiple industries, including pharmaceutical development, agricultural pest management, and cosmetic formulations [14, 15]. Therefore, regulation of nicotine content plays a crucial role in the tobacco industry ‘s progress.

Nicotine is a long-distance transport metabolite that is initially synthesized in the roots, transported through the xylem, and finally accumulated in the vacuoles of leaf cells. This process requires transport across multiple tissue membranes [16, 17]. NUP1/2, JAT2, and NtMATE1/2 play crucial roles in the transport of nicotine in the aboveground part and the deposition of nicotine in the vacuoles [18, 19]. These transporter proteins prevent nicotine sequestration into vacuoles and protect cells from alkaloid-induced cytotoxicity. Research has demonstrated that nicotine exhibits pronounced heterosis, with overdominance effects being particularly influential in shaping its heterotic expression in tobacco [20]. At the same time, hybrid roots exhibited marked upregulation of nicotine biosynthesis and transport-related genes [21]. Therefore, we speculated that the efficiency of nicotine transport and accumulation in hybrids may have been improved.

In the early stages, our research group studied the formation mechanism of nicotine hybrid vigor based on transcriptome and proteome, and found that the glutathione S-transferase gene (Accession number: A0A1S3YUL3; Gene number: Nitab4.5:0000008g0150, Originating from Sylvestris.) showed significant up-regulation expression in hybrids with strong nicotine heterosis [22]. According to sequence alignment and gene identification, it was found that the gene belongs to the 10th gene in the Tau subfamily (U), and therefore named NtGSTU10 [23]. Glutathione S-transferases (GSTs) are a diverse superfamily of dimeric enzymes that play a critical role in regulating plant secondary metabolism and responding to multiple environmental stresses [24, 25]. Glutathione S-transferases (GSTs) help detoxify plants by catalyzing the conjugation of GSH with electrophilic compounds. The conjugates are separated into vacuoles or exported via extracellular vesicles, allowing for degradation of toxic metabolites and xenobiotics [26, 27]. In numerous plants, GSTs can also catalyze the binding of GSH to anthocyanins, selectively transferring anthocyanins to vacuoles through ABC transporters [28, 29]. Furthermore, GSTs can also act as a ligand protein, bind to substrates, and transport them to the vacuole membrane [30]. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that this gene plays a role in nicotine transport metabolism, potentially contributing to increasing the nicotine content in hybrids.

In this work, we analyzed the bioinformatics and spatiotemporal expression patterns of the NtGSTU10 and created over expression lines by constructing overexpression vectors and genetic transformation. Subsequently, the nicotine content, nicotine transport coefficient, and heterosis of over expression lines and wild-type were analyzed. Based on transcriptome analysis, the biological function of NtGSTU10 gene in tobacco and its molecular mechanism in enhancing nicotine content in hybrids were preliminarily revealed. The research results are of great significance in advancing theoretical research on nicotine heterosis and breeding tobacco varieties with suitable nicotine content. Furthermore, by regulating the expression of NtGSTU10 to improve the efficiency of nicotine biosynthesis, it is expected to provide a more efficient plant source for the production of raw materials for products such as E-cigarettes and nicotine patches, potentially enhancing nicotine recovery and production sustainability.

Results

Bioinformatics and functional prediction analysis of NtGSTU10 gene

Bioinformatics analysis is crucial in understanding the structure of genes and making functional predictions. We amplified the full-length NtGSTU10 gene from tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum cv. Basma) using polymerase chain reaction (PCR). NtGSTU10 has 663 bp and an open reading frame (ORF) that encodes a protein with 220 amino acids. Afterwards, we also analyzed the basic physical and chemical properties of NtGSTU10 protein (Table S3). Our analysis of the conserved NtGSTU10 domains showed the presence of typical GST_C_Tau and GST_N_Tau domains, which are characteristic of the GST family (Fig. 1A). Analysis of the NtGSTU10 promoter identified multiple stress-responsive cis-acting elements (drought, low temperature, damage induction, etc.) (Fig. 1B), light responsiveness (Fig. 1C), growth and development (Fig. 1D), and phytohormone responsiveness (abscisic acid, gibberellin, salicylic acid, etc.) (Fig. 1E). Overall, the cis-acting elements of the NtGSTU10 exhibit diversity and may influence plant growth and metabolic processes.

Analysis of the tobacco NtGSTU10 gene for bioinformatics and functional prediction. (A) Domain structure of NtGSTU10 gene. (B-E) The cis-acting element analysis of the NtGSTU10 gene. The order was stress responsiveness, light responsiveness, growth and development, and phytohormone responsiveness

To study the expression characteristics of the NtGSTU10 gene in tobacco plants, the expression levels of the gene were analyzed by RT-qPCR in multiple tissues and growth stages. The results showed that the NtGSTU10 expression was detected in three tissues: roots (Fig. 2A), stalks (Fig. 2B), and leaves (Fig. 2C). Within the same tissue, the expression of NtGSTU10 varied significantly across stages. The expression levels reach their peak at two weeks after topping and then begin to decline. One week after topping, the expression level of NtGSTU10 began to increase and reached its peak two weeks after topping, followed by a decrease in expression. The expression level of NtGSTU10 in the root was significantly positively correlated with nicotine content (r = 0.62) and transport coefficient (r = 0.57) (Fig. 2D). There was a positive correlation between the expression levels in stalks and leaves and the nicotine content and the transport coefficient. These results suggest that the NtGSTU10 gene may play a role in nicotine transport and metabolism.

Generation and identification of NtGSTU10 over expression lines

To determine if NtGSTU10 regulates the formation of nicotine heterosis, the cultivar “Basma” was selected for genetic transformation. The vector carrying the target gene was introduced into tobacco Basma cells through Agrobacterium infection, and four positive over expression lines (OE-NtGSTU10) were analyzed. PCR results confirmed over expression lines showed Hyg marker amplification (Fig. 3A). These four over expression plants were used in subsequent studies. An expression level analysis was performed on the over expression lines, and the gene expression levels in these lines were found to be significantly higher than those in wild-type plants, particularly OB7 and OB12 (Fig. 3B). Therefore, we used OB7 and OB12 as male parent materials and hybridized them with the female parent Va116 to create over expression hybrid hybrids V×OB7 and V×OB12 for further research on T1 generation.

The influence of NtGSTU10 gene on nicotine related traits

To identify the biological function of the NtGSTU10 gene, we determined the nicotine content in wild-type and over-expressed lines and evaluated the nicotine transport coefficient and nicotine heterosis (Table S6). Compared to the wild-type (Basma), the nicotine content in the roots and leaves of OB7 and OB12 was extremely significantly increased, while the increase in nicotine content in the stalks was relatively small (Fig. 4A). For hybrid combinations, the nicotine content in the roots of V×OB7 and V×OB12 was significantly lower than that of the wild-type hybrid (V×B), but the nicotine content in the leaves was extremely significantly increased (Fig. 4B). The results showed that compared to the wild-type (Basma), the nicotine transport coefficients of OB7 and OB12 increased by 32.21% and 36.47%, respectively, with a significant difference (Fig. 4C). In comparison to wild-type hybrid (V×B), the nicotine transport coefficients of V×OB7 and V×OB12 increased by 39.50% (P ≤ 0.05) and 40.67% (P ≤ 0.01), respectively (Fig. 4D). Meanwhile, the heterosis value of nicotine content in tobacco leaves increased by 120.07% (P ≤ 0.05) and 127.59% (P ≤ 0.05), respectively (Fig. 4E). The results showed that the NtGSTU10 gene could positively regulate the formation of heterosis in nicotine transport and content in tobacco leaves.

Transcriptome differences between wild-type and over expressed lines caused by NtGSTU10 gene

To gain a comprehensive understanding of how the NtGSTU10 gene improves nicotine transport efficiency and heterosis in tobacco, we compared transcriptome information between the over expression parent (OB12) and wild-type (Basma), as well as the V×B and over expression hybrid (V×OB12). The sequencing data quality control analysis is summarized in Table S4, while Table S5 displays the alignment results between the processed data and the reference genome. These results confirm the reliability of the sequencing data for subsequent differential gene expression analysis that relates to the target traits. The correlation analysis showed that the experimental design of the study was reasonable and the correlation between biological replicates was strong (Fig. 5A). The results showed that there were 1546 and 5726 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in OB12_vs_Basma and V×OB12_vs_V×B, respectively (Fig. 5B), assuming that the NtGSTU10 gene has a greater impact on heterozygous materials. The interactive analysis results showed that a total of 728 DEGs were co-expressed between the two comparison groups (Fig. 5C). Therefore, we speculat that these 728 DEGs are more likely to be involved in regulating the transport of nicotine.

To gain a deeper understanding of the biological functions of these DEGs, we conducted GO functional annotation analysis on 728 DEGs (Appendix 1). The results showed that these DEGs were annotated to biological processes such as substance transmembrane transporter activity, secondary metabolic process, response to external biotic stimuli, intracellular protein transmembrane transport and export across plasma membrane (Fig. 5D). The pathways related to nicotine synthesis, such as tropane, piperidine and pyridine alkaloid biosynthesis, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, and arginine and proline metabolism, were significantly enriched (Fig. 5E). The results showed that overexpression of NtGSTU10 gene could result in the positive expression of genes related to nicotine synthesis and transport, and promote the synthesis and transport metabolism of nicotine.

NtGSTU10 enhances nicotine content in hybrids by regulating downstream genes

Further analysis of the genes within these pathways revealed that ABC transporter family, GST family genes, and nicotine synthesis related genes were significantly expressed in the over expression plants (V×OB12) compared to V×B (Fig. 6A-E). Subsequently, we simulated a molecular docking between NtGSTU10 protein and nicotine, and the results showed that the lowest binding energy was − 6.64 kcal/mol, indicating a strong binding ability between nicotine and NtGSTU10 protein (Figure S1). This result serves as a theoretical reference for future experiments on the binding of NtGSTU10 protein to nicotine and in-depth research on the regulatory mechanism of NtGSTU10 gene on nicotine transport.

Therefore, we speculated the mechanism by the NtGSTU10 gene that regulates the formation of nicotine transport heterosis in tobacco hybrids (Fig. 7). The overexpression of NtGSTU10 gene led to the up- regulation of the ABC family members (ABCB1, ABCC1, and ABCC2), promoting the transmembrane transport efficiency of nicotine. At the same time, as nicotine transport efficiency increased, nicotine content in the root system decreased, stimulating up-regulation of nicotine synthesis related genes (AOC, QS, ODC, and PAO), and down-regulation of negative regulatory factors (ERF1, ETR, and JAZ), promoting nicotine synthesis and transport metabolism. Overall, the improvement in nicotine transport and synthesis efficiency in hybrids led to an increase in nicotine content in tobacco leaves.

Discussion

Before conducting functional research on a specific gene, using bioinformatics analysis software and databases to predict its structure and function can prevent research blindness and save experimental costs [31]. The cis-acting elements exist in the side sequence of a gene and can affect gene expression [32]. The analysis of cis-acting elements is typically focuses on studying the promoter sequences upstream of the target gene at around 2000–3000 bp [33]. The results of gene function research showed that members of the GST gene family can participate in various biotic and abiotic stress responses, such as mechanical wounds, heavy metals, drought, temperature, and plant hormones [34, 35]. This study conducted a cis-acting element analysis on the NtGSTU10 gene promoter and speculated that the NtGSTU10 gene can respond to various environmental stresses, such as plant hormones, stress, circadian rhythm, and wound induction. This result was in line with the promoter analysis results of SlGST and AtGST, indicating that the presence of these responsive elements directly affects the transcriptional expression level and stress response ability of the NtGSTU10 gene under stress [25]. Nicotine’s synthesis, transport, and accumulation in tobacco are regulated by salicylic acid and methyl jasmonate [36]. Therefore, we speculate that the NtGSTU10 gene may play a role in regulating the nicotine synthesis and transport in tobacco through these salicylic acid and methyl jasmonate responsive elements. These results provided a reference for studying the biological function of the NtGSTU10 gene. In the following research, the multiple functions of this gene can be further explored by constructing over expression lines and systematically analyzing their resistance to drought/salt stress.

Topping is a mechanical wound that refers to the removal of the top growth point of tobacco plants. It is a common agricultural measure during tobacco growth that significantly affects nicotine content [37, 38]. Our study found that the cis-acting element of the NtGSTU10 gene promoter contains the wound-inducing element WUN motif, suggesting that the expression of NtGSTU10 gene in tobacco plants may be induced by topping this wound operation. Research has shown that topping has an up-regulation effect on the expression of the NtGSTU17 gene. The expression level slightly increased in the early stages of topping, but it was not significant and then rapidly increased. The up-regulation of the NtGSTU17 gene caused by topping continued until the mature stage of the middle leaves [39], which aligns with our research results. The correlation analysis showed that the expression of NtGSTU10 gene was positively correlated with nicotine content and nicotine transport coefficient, reaching extremely significant levels in both roots and leaves. Nicotine synthesis and accumulation are regulated by topping, and the nicotine content rapidly increases after topping [40]. Therefore, we speculated that the up-regulated expression of NtGSTU10 gene may be related to the topping wound and the increase in nicotine content in tobacco plants.

Previous studies have shown that when the plant GST genes are used as transport vectors, they transport the substrates to the vacuole membrane, which are then transported by specific transport proteins on the membrane and accumulated in the vacuole or extracellular space [41]. ABCC type proteins have long been considered classic GSH-S coupling pumps [42], and multiple studies have demonstrated that both ABC- and MATE-type transporters require glutathione S-transferase (GST) for flavonoid transport and accumulation [43]. The preliminary transcriptomic analysis showed that GST, MATE, and ABC were significantly up-regulated in hybrids at the same time. The transcriptomic results of our study using wild-type and over expressed lines showed that plant hormone signal transduction, glutathione metabolism, and ABC family transporters related to nicotine transport were significantly enriched, and DEGs were up-regulated significantly in the over expression lines. Therefore, we speculate that these nicotine transport genes are regulated by NtGSTU10 to facilitate nicotine transport from the membrane space to the extracellular or vacuole. The molecular docking results of nicotine and NtGSTU10 protein indicated that they can bind to NtGSTU10 protein through van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonding forces, and π bonds. This research result opens up the possibility for the NtGSTU10 protein to function as a transporter for nicotine. This mechanism may be consistent with the transport and accumulation of metabolites such as grape anthocyanins [44], red grape anthocyanins 3-O-glucoside [45], and cyclamen anthocyanins [46]. In this study, the molecular docking results of nicotine and NtGSTU10 protein were based on preliminary analysis of the model. It is necessary to conduct future experimental verification. This provides a crucial foundation for understanding the mechanism of action of NtGSTU10 in nicotine transport heterosis in hybrids.

The synthesis, transportation, and accumulation of substances are integral continuous flow processes that can be explained by the source-sink-flow theory of crops. In the source-sink-flow system, the assimilation and synthesis products are transported and distributed through “flow”, and the flow rate and direction also affect the size of the source and sink [47]. After nicotine is synthesized in tobacco root tip cells, it is transported through the xylem to the mesophyll cells of tobacco leaves and stored in vacuoles. It is speculated that several proteins play a role in the nicotine transport process [48]. After overexpression of the potential nicotine transport gene NtGSTU10, we found that many genes related to nicotine synthesis were also induced, including up-regulation of positive regulatory factors and down-regulation of negative regulatory factors, indicating that these regulatory genes can act as downstream effectors of NtGSTU10. As a result, we speculated that the overexpression of NtGSTU10 caused an increase in the nicotine transport rate and stimulated the high expression of nicotine synthesis genes in the roots. This cyclic and efficient operating system enhances the nicotine transport capacity in hybrids, increasing the nicotine content in tobacco leaves. Of course, when an overexpressed line is used as a parent, the genetic background it provides is the basis for producing heterosis, but heterosis itself is the result of multiple gene interactions and is not solely driven by a single gene, NtGSTU10. Therefore, more mechanisms underlying the formation of nicotine hybrid vigor still need to be uncovered.

Conclusions

Genetic transformation has confirmed that NtGSTU10 enhances nicotine heterosis in leaves. The transcriptome analysis revealed that DEGs were annotated to biological processes such as substance transmembrane transporter activity, response to external biotic stimuli, and intracellular protein transmembrane transport. The pathways related to nicotine synthesis, such as tropane, piperidine and pyridine alkaloid biosynthesis, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, and arginine and proline metabolism, were significantly enriched. Further analysis revealed that ABC family members and genes related to nicotine synthesis were significantly up-regulated, while negative regulatory factors for nicotine synthesis were significantly down-regulated. As a result,, we speculate that the overexpression of NtGSTU10 caused an increase in the nicotine transport rate and stimulated the synthesis of nicotine in the roots. This cyclic and efficient operating system improved the nicotine transport capacity in hybrids, increasing the nicotine content in tobacco leaves. The research results provided a molecular basis for regulating the nicotine transport and accumulation in hybrids, and also offer more possibilities for targeted cultivation of new tobacco varieties with suitable nicotine content.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis of NtGSTU10

The nucleotide sequence of the NtGSTU10 gene was obtained from the NCBI database. The Coding DNA Sequence (CDS) of NtGSTU10 was used as the template for primer design. ExPASy was used to analyze the physicochemical properties and hydrophilicity of NtGSTU10 protein (http://www.expasy.org/tools/). Phosphorylation sites of NtGSTU10 were predicted using NetPhos 3.1 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/). The Motif Scan website was used to perform motif prediction analysis and assess conserved structural motifs in the NtGSTU10 protein. To analyze the regulatory elements of NtGSTU10, we obtained its 2.2-kb upstream sequence from the Solanaceae database (https://solgenomics.net/organism/nicotiana_tabacum/genome). The NtGSTU10 promoter was analyzed using PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent) to identify regulatory motifs and cis-acting elements.

Vector construction, creation of over expression lines

The NtGSTU10 coding sequence was PCR-amplified and cloned into the pBWA(V)HS vector downstream of the CaMV35S promoter using BsaI/Eco31I restriction sites (Table S1). The pBWA(V)HS-35 S-NtGSTU10 construct was introduced into A. tumefaciens GV3101, resulting in the creation of the OE-NtGSTU10 strain. The Basma tobacco variety was genetically transformed using Agrobacterium-mediated leaf disc transformation. The specific operation method is described by Zhao et al. (2020). Primers specific to the hygromycin resistance gene (Hyg; Table S1) were designed and synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology (Beijing, China) to identify positive transgenic lines. Genomic DNA was extracted from transformed plant leaves and used as a template for PCR amplification using these primers. The amplicons were analyzed using gel electrophoresis to confirm the presence of the Hyg sequence. Subsequently, RT-qPCR was performed to detect for high levels of transgene expression in the lines.

Experimental materials: planting and sampling

In the experiment of expression pattern analysis, the tobacco variety K326 was used to analyze the expression pattern of the NtGSTU10 gene in various tissues and stages. These fourteen materials (Table S2) were used to determine nicotine content, calculate transport coefficients, and correlation analysis with the expression levels of the NtGSTU10 gene. The cultivation method for tobacco seedlings was described by Mo et al. [22]. When they reached the sixth real leaf, they were transplanted into an experimental field. All the plants were topped on the same day, when over 50% of the plants flowering. On the day of topping, one week after topping, two weeks after topping, and three weeks after topping, three consistently growing plants in each replicate were selected for sampling, including three tissues (roots, stalks, and leaves), and three biological replicates were set. Refer to Mo et al. [21] for the collection and processing method of experimental samples.

In gene function validation experiments, Hgy resistance screening was performed on each T1 plant, followed by individual PCR genotype identification to obtain T1 plants that were confirmed to be transgenic positive. The wild-type Va116 was used as the female parent and hybridized with the over expressing male parent Basma to produce seeds for the F1 hybrid combination V×OB. Based on the expression level of the NtGSTU10 gene, two over expression parents and combination-type hybrids were selected for functional identification experiments. Therefore, this study used Va116, Basma, OB7, and OB12 as parents, and V×B, V×OB12, and V×OB7 as hybrids, with a total of seven experimental materials. These plant materials were cultivated in a greenhouse at the Tobacco Science Research and Experimental Base of Guizhou University, following the same cultivation scheme and sample collection procedures as previously described. The samples were used to determining nicotine content and transcriptome sequencing analysis.

RNA-seq profiling of over expression lines

Total RNA was extracted from each of the twelve samples using the RNAprep Pure Plant Total RNA Extraction Kit. The concentration and purity of the isolated RNA were measured with a Nanodrop2000 spectrophotometer. RNA integrity was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis, and RNA integrity number (RIN) values were determined on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. The sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeqXT0n platform by Shanghai Meiji Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The clean reads were aligned to the tobacco reference genome using HISAT2 (version 2.1.0) [50]. Cufflinks was used to measure gene expression levels in FPKM (fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads) using version 2.2.1, and reads were obtained for each gene using the HTSeq-count tool [51, 52]. Differential gene expression was analyzed using DESeq2 (version 1.10.1) [53]. P ≤ 0.05 and foldchange ≥ 1.5 or foldchange ≤ 0.67 were used as thresholds to screen for differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The functional annotation of DEGs was conducted via the GO and KEGG databases. Enrichment analysis was then performed using an R-based tool to associate these genes with specific GO categories and KEGG pathways, elucidating their biological functions [54, 55].

Determination of gene expression levels (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated using the RNAprep Pure Plant RNA Mini Kit (TIANGEN Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), and reverse transcription into cDNA was performed using the FastKing gDNA Dispelling RT SuperMix from the same supplier. All procedures were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s specifications. Fluorescent primers targeting the NtGSTU10 gene were designed using NCBI’s online primer design tools and synthesized by Beijing Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd. (Table S1). The tobacco L25 gene served as a reference for normalization and RT-qPCR was performed to measure NtGSTU10 expression. All qPCR reactions were performed using the SYBR PRIME qPCR Kit (Fast HS; Baoguang Biological, Cat. No. BG0014) as directed by the manufacturer. Amplification was performed on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Life Technologies, Beverly, MA, USA). Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2 −ΔΔCt method [56].

Data statistics and analysis

Duncan’s new multiple range test was performed in SPSS 16.0 to determine the significance of the results (P ≤ 0.05 and P ≤ 0.01). Figures were prepared using Origin 2018 (95_64), GraphPad Prism 9, and Adobe Illustrator CS6.
