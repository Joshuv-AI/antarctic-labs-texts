Protective effect of green tea extract on chlorpyrifos induced toxicity in liver of male mice
Authors: Hafiza Hira Rubab, Naila Riaz, Faiza Zubair, Muddussara Saleem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPesticides are vital for boosting agricultural yields and ensuring global food security, yet they have far-reaching ecological consequences that impact diverse species across trophic levels. Chlorpyrifos (CPF) is a prevalent but controversial organophosphate pesticide that acts as an endocrine disruptor, oxidative stress inducer, and hepatotoxic agent. This research was designed to examine the potential of green tea extract to reverse the functional and structural alterations induced by CPF in male albino mice and to evaluate any related hepatotoxicity associated with green tea extract. The experimental protocol lasted for 28 days, with adult male mice (N = 40) assigned to one of four experimental cohorts (n = 10 per group): (i) Control (untreated): standard diet and water; (ii) Green Tea: 20 mg/kg/day sole GT extract; (iii) CPF: 25 mg/kg/day CPF (dissolved in corn oil); (iv) CPF + GT: 25 mg/kg/day CPF in corn oil and 20 mg/kg/day GT extract, with 2-hour gap. Results demonstrated that CPF administration induced hepatomegaly (p < 0.01) and reduced the body weight significantly (p < 0.05). Histological analysis revealed an increase in binucleated hepatocytes (p < 0.05), tissue loosening, portal vein necrosis and enlarged mononucleated hepatocytes and nuclear diameter (p < 0.001). Serum Alanine Aminotransferase (ALT) and Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) were elevated (p < 0.01), while a significant decrease in albumin levels was observed (p < 0.05). Conversely, co-administration of green tea mitigated these effects, restoring liver weight and hepatocyte count, promoting hepatic tissue regeneration, and improving serum liver enzyme and protein levels.Keywords: Chlorpyrifos, Hepatotoxicity, Liver function test, Herbal treatment, Green teaSubject terms: Biochemistry, Environmental sciences, Physiology, Zoology

Introduction

Pesticides have become integral to contemporary agricultural practices for managing harmful pests with an estimated 3.69–3.73 million metric tons used globally each year (Statista,1. However, it is concerning that only a small proportion of pesticides (less than 1%) actually hit the target pests, while more than 90% end up contaminating our land, water, and air. Furthermore, numerous pesticides have molecular targets that overlap with those of nontarget organisms in terrestrial and aquatic environments2. Recent studies indicate that pesticides acute and intentional exposures (self-poisoning) cause ~ 150,000 deaths annually3. Organophosphorus (OP) pesticides are a key group of pesticides that were initially developed as chemical weapons for military purposes during World War II. Gradually, they have made their way into the commercial market and are now widely utilized as pesticides worldwide. Despite OP exposure causing significant morbidity and mortality in developing countries, these chemicals hold about 45% of the international pesticide market in agriculture4.

Chlorpyrifos (CPF) is a key representative of OP insecticides. Since its introduction to the global market in 1965 as a substitute for the DDT, this pesticide has become an indispensable tool in the global fight against agricultural and domestic pests5. It is a broad-spectrum, non-systemic pesticide that is effective when pests come into contact with, breathe, or consume it. It exhibits moderate persistence in the environment, with a half-life of 10–120 days depending on the conditions6. WHO classified the CPF as a moderately hazardous Class II insecticide, capable of inducing hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity, reproductive and developmental toxicity7. Among vulnerable populations, such as pregnant women, children and newborns to CPF may result in chronic toxic effects, reduced cognitive ability, delayed development, and autism8,9.

The liver serves as the principal organ that processes and detoxifies foreign substances, such as chemicals and pesticides10. Chlorpyrifos (CPF)-induced hepatotoxicity involves a complex interplay between oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, and apoptosis. Hepatic bioactivation by cytochrome P450 enzymes converts CPF to chlorpyrifos-oxon, a highly toxic metabolite that increases reactive oxygen species (ROS). This causes lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, DNA damage, depletion of antioxidants (glutathione, SOD, catalase), and reduced activity of key marker hepatic enzymes11,12. Mitochondrial damage induces permeability transition pore opening and membrane potential collapse, promoting the release of pro-apoptotic factors such as cytochrome c. These events activate caspase-9 and caspase-3, triggering apoptosis and necrosis. Concurrently, NF-κB activation elevates pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), exacerbating the injury. Disruption of the Nrf2–Keap1 pathway and an imbalance in Bax/Bcl-2 further accelerate hepatocellular damage13. These findings highlight the liver’s vulnerability to CPF and the need for protective measures against CPF-induced hepatotoxicity.

For millennia, plant-based treatments have played a crucial role in the medicine of various cultures. Historical records suggest that the application of herbal medicine can be traces its lineage to the Paleolithic era, roughly 60 millennia ago, with the Sumerians documenting the application of medicinal plants over 5,000 years ago14. Green tea, sourced from the Camellia sinensis plant, is highly valued for its rich polyphenol content and has been integral to Asian folk medicine practices since ancient ages. It ranks among the most consumed nonalcoholic beverages globally, cherished not only for its taste but also for its impressive health advantages, including its antioxidant, anticancer, hypoglycemic, and neuroprotective effects. Green tea crude extract mitigates chlorpyrifos-induced hepatotoxicity primarily through its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory actions which is attributed to its rich polyphenolic content. Catechins from green tea show promising ability to reduce chlorpyrifos-induced oxidative stress and apoptosis15. Green tea’s longstanding reputation as a hepatoprotective agent is backed by research and demonstrate its ability to reduce lipid peroxidation, restore the level of hepatic enzymes and reverse alterations at the tissue level16,17. Green tea polyphenols enhanced hepatic glutathione content and activities of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, GST) and suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in liver tissue (Wang et al., 2022).

Although the origins of herbal supplements are deeply rooted in antiquity, there is increasing awareness of the potential hazards linked to their consumption. Green tea is often viewed as having a positive safety profile; however, excessive consumption at high doses (> 6–8 cups/day) may result in adverse effects, including gastric irritation, indigestion, nausea, vomiting, and loss of appetite. Hepatotoxicity from green tea is considered idiosyncratic, rare, unpredictable, and, in some cases, dose-dependent18. One possible explanation for this could be the presence of contaminants in the extract, potential herb-drug interactions, or the consumer’s health status19. Overall, moderate green tea consumption is generally considered safe for most individuals. Despite this, further scrutiny is needed to determine the precise dosage levels that ensure liver protection in humans. Thus, in the current research, we sought to explore the ameliorative potential of the crude aqueous extract of GT in mitigating hepatotoxicity induced by repeated doses of CPF in male mice. Additionally, we evaluated the potential hepatotoxic effects of GT extract within a moderate dosage range, as supported by several studies. Materials and methodology

Chemicals and reagents

CPF (99% purity) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Corn oil was used as the vehicle for CPF administration. Green tea leaves were sourced locally.

Preparation of chlorpyrifos dose

To create the CPF stock solution, the compound was solubilized in corn oil. The desired dose of 25 mg/kg was achieved by adjusting the CPF concentration in the stock solution. To ensure weight-dependent administration, the CPF concentration was recalibrated for each animal through precise dilution while maintaining a fixed dosing volume of 0.1 mL20.

Preparation of green tea extract

Green tea extract was prepared as a crude aqueous infusion to simulate typical dietary consumption. To prepare a fresh aqueous extract, 20 g of green tea leaves were pulverized using a manual grinder. The homogenized powder was then infused with 1 L of distilled water and maintained at a sub-boiling temperature (< 90 °C). The mixture was processed for 5 min, consistent with reported protocols21 to facilitate polyphenolic recovery and subsequently passed through Whatman No. 1 paper to ensure the removal of particulate matter. The aqueous extract was transferred to an airtight vial for storage22. Although chromatographic standardization (e.g., HPLC) was not performed, aqueous extraction is widely recognized to efficiently recover the principal bioactive constituents of Camellia sinensis. HPLC-based studies consistently demonstrate that aqueous green tea extracts are rich in polyphenolic catechins, predominantly epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), along with epigallocatechin (EGC), epicatechin (EC), and epicatechin gallate (ECG), accompanied by smaller amounts of other phenolics and alkaloids. Recent reports further confirm that hot-water extraction effectively yields these compounds, with catechins representing the major bioactive fraction in aqueous green tea extracts23,24.

Animal housing and experimental conditions

Male albino mice (Mus musculus) were procured from the breeding stock available at animal husbandry wing maintained by Department of Zoology, UOS (Sargodha, Pakistan). The subjects were kept in plastic cages equipped with corrosion-resistant steel wire gauze covers and wood chip bedding, which was replaced daily. Environmental conditions were meticulously regulated, maintaining a temperature of 25 °C (± 3 °C) and 45% relative humidity. A standardized diurnal cycle (12-hour light/dark) was implemented, and the mice were nourished with standard chick feed and water ad libitum25. This arrangement ensured optimal animal welfare and experimental consistency in the study. Mice were selected as model organisms in this study because of their behavioral, genetic, and biological similarities to humans. Before the commencement of the experiment, the mice were acclimatized to their surroundings for at least two weeks. All procedures were conducted in strict compliance with the ethical framework sanctioned by the Research Ethical Review Board for Science and Technology, Computing, Informatics, Engineering Committee. The study adhered to ethical standards, as authorized by the decision numbered ERB/FS/170,821 dated 17 March 2023. The research was performed in alignment with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Experimental Protocol: A cohort of 40 mature male mice of reproductive age, with a weight range of 25–33 g, underwent a two-week acclimatization period25. Experimental animals were then randomly allocated to one of four treatment groups, with each group comprising ten individuals (n = 10) as follows: Group 1 (G-1) Normal Control (Untreated): Mice received a standard diet and water. Group 2 (G-2) Green Tea (GT) Treatment: The subjects received 0.1 mL of GT aqueous solution (20 mg/kg BW) via intragastric gavage on a daily basis for 28-day duration17. Group 3 (G-3) CPF Treatment: Animals were treated with 0.1 mL CPF (equivalent to 25 mg/kg BW) in a corn oil vehicle once every 24 h for four weeks. Cometa,20. Group 4 (G-4): Combined CPF and GT Treatment: Animals received a combined treatment of 0.1 mL CPF (25 mg/kg BW using corn oil as vehicle) and 20 mg/kg BW aqueous GT extract in distilled water (0.1 mL)15. The treatments were administered via gavage at 2-hour interval between doses daily for 28 days. GT treatment was administered after 2 h of CPF treatment.

Animal dissection and organ recovery

On the last day of the 28-day experiment, final body mass was recorded for all animals using a calibrated digital scale with a precision of 0.1 before conducting the autopsy. Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (95 mg/kg) in combination with xylazine (7 mg/kg) to achieve deep unconsciousness prior to euthanasia through cervical dislocation by trained personnel26. This anesthetic protocol is well established for providing reliable sedation and analgesia, thereby minimizing animal distress and ensuring humane euthanasia in accordance with ethical guidelines. The harvested livers were weighed using a digital weighing scale (Sartorius BSA224S-CW) and preserved in 10% formalin solution until histological examination.

Blood sample analysis

Blood samples (1 mL each) were drawn from the inferior vena cava using a fine-needle syringe and placed in vials coated with a clot activator. Serum was extracted by centrifuging the collected samples at 3500 rpm for 20 min. Serum samples were subsequently analyzed for liver function, with assessments of albumin, total protein, globulin, AST (aspartate aminotransferase), ALT (alanine aminotransferase) and ALP (alkaline phosphatase) levels conducted with the aid of a commercial spectrophotometer-enzymatic kit.

Histological examination: Liver samples were immersed in Carnoy’s fixative (ethyl alcohol, formaldehyde and glacial acetic acid in a 6:3:1 ratio) for 4–5 h at ambient temperature. The liver underwent dehydration in ascending ethanol solutions (50%, 70%, and 90%) for 3–5 h each. Following fixation and dehydration, the organs were bathed in xylene for 5–6 h for clearance. Tissues were embedded in wax blocks for 3–5 h at 56–58 °C to facilitate serial sectioning at 5 μm using an ERMA TOKYO 42 rotary microtome. These sections were then stretched in a 45 °C water bath and positioned on glass slides coated with adhesive albumin layer27.

Staining

Rehydration was achieved by sequential immersion of the tissues in a series of descending ethanol concentrations (100% to 50%) and subsequently deparaffinized them using xylene. To achieve vibrant coloration, the slides were stained with hematoxylin and eosin after rinsing with water. Subsequently, the stained slides were mounted by carefully adding a few drops of a resinous mounting medium (Canada balsam), and a coverslip was placed on top of each slide. Finally, the prepared slides were subjected to microscopic evaluation to identify and characterize histological anomalies.

Digital photography and image processing

Photomicrographs of liver sections were captured by coupling an Infinix Hot 10 camera sensor (Model X682B) to the trinocular port of a Labomed FN18 microscope. The images were then refined using Corel DRAW 2019 software for color adjustment, contrast optimization, cropping, scaling and trimming. To facilitate clarity, key histological features were highlighted by applying digital tags, and the processed micrographs were compiled in Microsoft Word for comprehensive labeling and annotation.

Micrometry

Digital images of liver sections from each group were analyzed at 40X magnification. Five non-overlapping, randomly selected portions from each image were measured using Corel DRAW-11 software with a pre-calibrated digital scale. The microscopic field choice was randomized by Overlaying a Digital lattice on the captured histological sections. A digital randomization tool (ImageJ software) was employed to delineate specific areas of interest for quantification, thereby ensuring the exclusion of redundant or overlapping target zones. The mean cross-sectional areas of hepatocytes, hepatic nuclei, and central veins were measured. Additionally, within a quadrant measuring 10/10 cm², the mean number of oval and Kupffer cells, as well as binucleated and mononucleated hepatocytes per unit area, was counted. Statistical analysis

Quantitative data processing was performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics suite (v. 25). A one-way ANOVA was used to evaluate the variance across the treatment cohorts. Subsequently, Tukey’s test was used as a post-hoc measure to identify any specific inter-group disparities. Outcomes are reported as the mean ± standard error of the mean with a threshold for statistical relevance established at a p-value of less than 0.05.

Histological observations

Histological examination of the control group slides (Fig. 3A) demonstrated a typical liver structure, characterized by adjacent and dense sheets of hepatocytes constructing intact hepatic cords around a clearly defined, compact central vein. The central vein was encircled by hepatocytes with centrally located round nuclei that exhibited prominently stained nucleoli and chromatin. Hepatocytes exhibited centrally positioned, round nuclei with well-stained nucleoli and chromatin. The granular cytoplasm was uniformly distributed around the nucleus. Hepatocytes were arranged in a radial pattern extending from the central vein toward the lobular margins, bordered by sinusoids. Well-organized hepatic portal triads were embedded within the connective tissue. Hepatocytes displayed pentagonal or hexagonal morphology and were observed in two variants: mononucleated and binucleated, with the former being more prevalent than the latter. Elongated Kupffer cells were aligned along the margins of the hepatocytes. Sinusoidal spaces of typical size containing oval cell deposits were observed between the hepatic cord.

Additionally, the sinusoidal spaces appeared dilated. Microscopic analysis of the GT group slides (Fig. 3B) revealed features comparable to those observed in the control group, including preserved hepatic architecture with a normal lobular structure. However, certain distinctions were noted, including moderate dilation of the central vein, slight enlargement of hepatocytes accompanied by an increase in their number, and mild congestion in the sinusoidal spaces.

Histological examination of liver sections from the CPF-treated group (Fig. 3C) revealed significant alterations in hepatic architecture. Extensive necrosis and degeneration were observed in the hepatic tissues, affecting both the hepatic cords and portal veins. The total hepatocyte count exhibited marked changes, with a notable increase in the number of binucleated cells. These cells exhibited enlarged nuclei with minor irregularities in their form. Localized vacuolated spaces, likely representing sinusoid remnants, were observed throughout the parenchyma. Furthermore, a pronounced elevation in the mean count of oval cells was observed, concurrent with an increase in the population of Kupffer cells. These findings indicate substantial hepatocellular damage and potential regenerative responses in CPF-exposed liver tissues.

Histological investigation of the slides from the combined treatment group (CPF + GT) showed promising results. A marginal reduction in the overall number of binucleated hepatocytes was observed, accompanied by a notable amelioration of hepatocellular and central vein damage. The lobular trabecular arrangement exhibited significant regeneration, indicating the considerable restoration of the intricate architecture of the liver tissue. Hepatocyte contraction led to the presence of few hepatocytes within the sinusoidal lumina (Fig. 3D).

Mean binucleated cell count (Area = 10 cm2)

Statistical analysis of variance revealed a marked elevation (p < 0.05) in the mean cell count of binucleated hepatocytes after CPF administration compared to that in the baseline group. Conversely, the GT-treated group exhibited a highly profound reduction (p < 0.001) in the binucleated cell count relative to the control group. The toxic effects of CPF were evident, as both CPF and CPF + GT-treated arms displayed a highly significant surge (p < 0.001) in the average binucleated cell count compared to the green tea-only group. Notably, green tea demonstrated protective effects in the co-administered group (CPF + GT), causing a pronounced decline (p < 0.001) in the mean cell count compared with the CPF-treated arm (Table 2).

Mean oval cells count (Area = 10 cm2)

The analysis of variance (ANOVA) exhibited a highly significant elevation in the mean count of oval cells in the chlorpyrifos (CPF) group (p < 0.001) which was partially attenuated after GT based treatment in the combined (CPF + GT) group but still significantly higher (p < 0.01) in comparison to the baseline group. Among the treated cohorts, a marked increase in oval cell count was noticed in the CPF (p < 0.001) and CPF + GT (p < 0.01) cohorts compared to that in the GT-exposed group. Notably, the combined treatment group exhibited a highly significant decrease in oval cell count (p < 0.001) compared to the CPF-treated arm. These findings demonstrate the detrimental effects of chlorpyrifos and suggest a potential ameliorative role for green tea when administered in combination with CPF (Table 2).

Mean kupffer cells count (Area = 10 cm2)

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed a statistically negligible increase in the mean count of Kupffer cells in the green tea group. However, Chlorpyrifos administration induced a highly significant increase (p < 0.001) in both the chlorpyrifos (CPF) and (CPF + GT) administered groups in contrast to the baseline group. Among the treatment arms, a pronounced increase in Kupffer cells was noticed in the CPF (p < 0.01) and CPF + GT (p < 0.05) cohorts compared to that in the GT-only group. The toxic effects of chlorpyrifos were evident, whereas green tea demonstrated attenuating effects in the co-exposed group (Table 2).

Discussion

Liver and dietary antigens have an ongoing relationship. It is considered the organ of choice in toxicological studies because it is a highly sensitive indicator of toxicity. As the first organ to encounter external toxins, such as pesticides, the liver serves as the body’s primary line of defense by neutralizing these substances28. However, this detoxification process can occasionally lead to hepatotoxicity29. Chlorpyrifos is a prominent environmental toxin that targets the liver as it is the territory of its metabolism30. Humans are routinely exposed to chlorpyrifos (CPF) through ingestion, inhalation, and dermal contact owing to its extensive use and lipophilic properties that enhance systemic absorption. Prolonged exposure has been associated with cardiovascular, endocrine, and neurological disorders31,28.

Interestingly, Green Tea has shown competent skills in rescuing against hepatotoxic agents such as CPF through its antioxidant and cytoprotective properties15. Therefore, present research was designed to investigate the hepatic dysfunction triggered by CPF and the ameliorating effects of GT. Furthermore, the effects of GT monotherapy on liver morphology were also considered in this study to establish a baseline for its safety profile.

Body mass and proportional organ weights are essential indicators in toxicological investigations for evaluating toxicity32. Our findings demonstrated a statistically pronounced reduction (p < 0.05) in the average body mass of mice in the CPF-treated cohort (25 mg/kg BW) compared to the control. This observation aligns with the research carried out by Pei et al.,30 on male mice, which documented a marked decline in body mass following CPF treatment (10 mg/kg). Furthermore, a profound decline in overall weight (p < 0.001) was observed within the GT group (20 mg/kg BW) relative to the control group. Iwaniec,33 experimentally confirmed a similar effect using a 2% GT dosage in mice. It is worth mentioning that attenuation of body mass following GT administration in this study does not suggest any toxicity. The NOAEL for green tea extract (GTE) in rats and mice is set at 500 mg/kg BW34. Therefore, this weight loss is consistent with the well-documented benefits of green tea on metabolism, such as boosting lipid metabolism, which helps reduce fat tissue and increase energy use. It may even influence the gut microbiota, helping to manage obesity and related metabolic issues35.

This study observed a remarkable elevation regarding mean absolute liver mass (p < 0.01) in CPF-treated mice (25 mg/kg BW) compared to that in the control group, indicating hepatomegaly. This observation aligns with those of previous studies that reported similar effects of CPF administration in experimental animals36,30,37. Increased liver weight may be attributed to toxicological effects or decreased body weight gain38. Conversely, GT (20 mg/kg BW) slightly reduced the relative liver weight compared to that in the control group. These results mirror the findings of Heikal et al.,16, who reported that CPF treatment (25 mg/kg BW) in rats for 28 days significantly reduced body weight gain and increased relative liver weights. GT administration (20 mg/kg BW) to insecticide-exposed groups demonstrated an ameliorative effect on either the loss of body weight or the increase in relative liver weights. These findings collectively support the potential of green tea for weight management and hepatoprotection.

Histopathological examination is a vital biomarker for evaluating pesticide toxicity32. The liver histology in the GT-supplemented cohort closely resembled that of the control group, showing mild hepatocyte degeneration and Kupffer cell proliferation. These findings align with those of Heikal et al.,16, with a GT dose of 30 mg orally administered for 28 days. In comparison to the baseline group, there was not any noticeable alteration occurred in hepatocyte size or central vein diameter; furthermore, the sinusoidal spaces were clearly visible in most areas. Similar findings were reported by Uzun and Kalender17 after administering an oral dose of catechin (20 mg/kg BW) for 28 days. Notably, there was no significant karyomegaly compared to the control as evidenced by Chan et al.,34 that only mild karyomegaly occurred at even high doses of GT (1000 mg). These findings underscore the minimal impact of GT on liver histology, reinforcing its safety profile at the standard doses.

When CPF exposure occurs, it leads to some noticeable changes in the liver, such as pyknotic nuclei, sinusoidal dilation, and a substantial surge in Kupffer cells relative to the GT group. These findings align with those of Uzun and Kalender17, who used 5.4 mg CPF. Kupffer cells, which are hepatic macrophages, facilitate the clearance of CPF-induced necrotic tissues, as noted by Khatun et al.,39. Additionally, significant elevation (p < 0.05) in binucleated hepatocytes following CPF administration compared to the untreated group was observed, which was consistent with the results of Ezzi et al.,40 and El-Bendary et al.,41 at 6.2 mg and 15 mg CPF doses.

Nuclear enlargement and cellular hypertrophy were evidenced by a marked increase (p < 0.001) in the diameter of mononucleated hepatocytes in contrast to the control and GT cohorts, mirroring the findings of Tripathi and Srivastav42 and Khalifa et al.,43 at CPF doses of 5, 10 and 6.75 mg/kg. Our findings demonstrated profound dilation (p < 0.001) in the centrilobular vein diameter, contrasting sharply with the observations of the untreated control and GT groups, which corresponds with previous studies with an oral dose of CPF (6.75 mg/kg) for 28 days16. These findings indicate that CPF exposure has a notable impact on liver cell structure and function.

In the co-administered (CPF + GT) group, GT administration showed a protective effect against liver damage by restoring the number of Kupffer cells, which aligns with the previous findings regarding the administration 1.5% w/v green tea extract in rats16; Luo et al.,44. This combined treatment significantly reduced the central vein diameter compared to that in the CPF group. This finding is similar to that observed in a study using 10 mg/kg of the insecticide fenitrothion45. Although green tea did not completely reverse the damage caused by CPF, it played a key role in mitigating its harmful effects, supporting the results of a study using 30 mg CPF46. The partial reduction of CPF’s harmful effects on liver histology by green tea is further supported by studies using different doses of GT extract (200 mg and 400 mg) against 40 mg of malathion, another OP insecticide47.

Hepatotoxicity induced by chlorpyrifos (CPF), as evidenced by alterations in the profile of liver marker enzymes, is consistent with the findings of previous studies on organophosphate pesticides48. Significant increases (p < 0.01) in serum ALP and non-significant surge in ALT and bilirubin levels in the CPF group corroborate findings from various studies using similar dosages (ranging from 5.6 to 25 mg/kg) and durations (ranging from 1 week to 4 weeks) conducted on rats and mice49–51,16,52,17. The non-significant reduction in AST levels also corresponds with the findings of Mehri et al.,53, who used 150 mg/kg malathion, another organophosphate pesticide, in their study. In this study, the CPF-induced ALP boost was reduced in the CPF + GT group compared to the group treated exclusively with CPF. Comparable results were reported by Mehana et al.,54, who demonstrated the ability of 1.5% (w/v) green tea extract for 8 weeks to attenuate lead-induced increases in ALP levels in rat. These results collectively support the hepatotoxic effects of CPF on liver biomarkers and contribute to a broader understanding of organophosphate-induced liver damage.

Albumin, a highly abundant blood plasma protein primarily synthesized in the liver, has been identified as a potential biomarker of CPF toxicity. In the current investigation, the pronounced decrease (p < 0.05) in serum albumin levels and statistically insignificant decline in the mean level of total protein and globulin following CPF exposure aligns with previous studies55–57,17. Co-administration of GT and CPF demonstrated a protective effect, as evidenced by increased albumin and globulin levels compared with those observed with CPF alone. This finding corroborates previous research showing the ability of GT (1.5% w/v) to improve albumin levels in rats58 . Similarly, Uzun and Kalender,17 reported that catechin (20 mg/kg BW), a component of GT, mitigated the CPF-induced decrease in albumin levels by 13%. Collectively, these results support the potential of GT as an ameliorative agent against CPF-induced hepatotoxicity.

Our results indicate that GT holds promise as a safe, long-term therapeutic option for alleviating pesticide-induced liver damage without causing adverse side effects which is consistent with Mehri et al.‘s (2016) observation. These findings highlight GT’s protective role against hepatotoxicity caused by environmental toxins.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that chlorpyrifos exposure induces significant hepatotoxicity, as evidenced by reduced body weight, hepatomegaly, histological alterations, and disrupted liver enzyme activity. Green tea (GT) extract administration effectively attenuated these changes, indicating its protective potential against pesticide-induced liver injury. No significant adverse effects were observed in the GT-treated group at the administered dose, suggesting good tolerability under the present experimental conditions. These findings highlight the hepatoprotective potential of GT, likely mediated through its antioxidant properties. However, further studies are required to characterize its phytochemical composition and elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, as well as to assess the long-term effects and sustainability of its protective efficacy following treatment withdrawal.
