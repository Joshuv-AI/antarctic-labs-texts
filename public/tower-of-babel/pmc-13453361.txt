Associated Factors of Pre-Anesthesia Blood Pressure Elevation in Elective Non-Cardiac Surgical Patients: A Case-Control Study Comparing Two Diagnostic Thresholds
Authors: Xiao-Min Li, Yan Yang, Hao Pan, Wang Li, Ge-Yi Ai, Xi-Jiang Liu, Guang-Fen Zhang, Jie Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPre-anesthesia blood pressure (BP) elevation frequently occurs in normotensive elective surgical patients and may disrupt perioperative safety. This study adopted two mainstream diagnostic criteria (absolute and relative thresholds) to define BP elevation, and integrated physical, laboratory and psychological indicators to systematically explore relevant associated factors.MethodsThis case-control study enrolling a total of 528 non-cardiac surgical patients. Patients were divided into case (elevated pre-anesthesia BP) and control (normal pre-anesthesia BP) groups. Pre-anesthesia BP elevation was defined as absolute threshold (systolic BP [SBP] ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic BP [DBP] ≥90 mmHg), and relative threshold (≥20% increase from baseline). Multivariable logistic regression was used to screen associated factors.ResultsThe incidence of pre-anesthesia BP elevation was 35.6% (n=188) under the absolute threshold and 28.2% (n=149) under the relative threshold. Multivariable analysis revealed that increasing age (absolute threshold: odds ratio [OR] 1.08, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.05–1.10, relative threshold: OR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.04), hyperlipemia (absolute threshold: OR 2.66, 95% CI 1.84–3.86, relative threshold: OR 1.49, 95% CI 1.18–1.88) and anxiety (absolute threshold: OR 1.17, 95% CI 1.10–1.25, relative threshold: OR 1.07, 95% CI 1.02–1.13) were correlated with BP elevation under both definitions (all p < 0.01). Under the absolute threshold, body mass index (BMI) (OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.03–1.21) and alcohol drinking (OR 2.74, 95% CI 1.55–4.84) were positively correlated (both p < 0.01), whereas prolonged sleep duration (2–4 hours: OR 0.37, 95% CI 0.15–0.95, 4–6 hours: OR 0.14, 95% CI 0.06–0.35, 6–8 hours: OR 0.12, 95% CI 0.04–0.34) was negatively correlated (all p < 0.05).ConclusionIncreasing age, hyperlipemia and preoperative anxiety are consistently associated with transient pre-anesthesia BP elevation under both criteria. Further studies are required to confirm causal links and verify whether intervening on these correlates can reduce perioperative BP fluctuations.Keywords: pre-anesthesia, blood pressure, absolute threshold, relative threshold

Introduction

In clinical practice, anesthesiologists often observe transient perioperative blood pressure (BP) elevation in the operating room before anesthesia induction, a phenomenon distinct from chronic hypertension diagnosed by long-term out-of-hospital monitoring. Even patients with stable normal home BP measurements may develop this temporary BP surge, which affects 10–53% of surgical candidates according to previous clinical data.1–3 Severe pre-anesthesia BP elevation may delay or cancel scheduled operations and raises the risk of perioperative cardiovascular and cerebrovascular complications.3–6 Therefore, implementing appropriate preventive measures holds significant benefits. However, the factors associated with elevated BP before anesthesia have not been well defined, which limits the effectiveness of preventing at-risk individuals.

Although several previous studies have assessed associated factors for pre-anesthesia blood pressure elevation, they have several limitations.2,7,8 Different studies define pre-anesthesia BP elevation inconsistently: some use an absolute threshold of 140/90 mmHg,2,8 while others use a relative threshold of a ≥20% increase from baseline.7 Divergent definitions yield inconsistent incidence and correlative factor findings, and it remains unclear how their screening performances differ for perioperative cardiovascular risk. Comparing their characteristics helps standardize perioperative BP evaluation. Most researches have primarily focused on the clinical characteristics of patients experiencing pre-anesthesia BP elevation, with limited consideration psychological factors such as anxiety and sleep quality.2,8 Anxiety triggers sympathetic overactivation and catecholamine secretion, while poor sleep impairs vascular regulation. Both pathways induce transient BP elevation, justifying the inclusion of these psychological variables.9–11 Therefore, this study aims to define pre-anesthesia BP elevation based on both absolute and relative thresholds, and to comprehensively analyze the factors influencing BP increases, including a wide range of demographic, clinical, and psychosocial factors.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This case-control study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zibo Central Hospital, Zibo, China (approval number: 202307080). This trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2300078789, www.chictr.org.cn/showprojEN.html?proj=203832). This case–control study was conducted in strict adherence to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

After obtaining informed consent, patients undergoing elective surgery at the Zibo Central Hospital between December 2023 and March 2024 were invited to participate in the study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients scheduled for elective non-cardiac surgery and non-obstetric surgery under appropriate anesthesia (general anesthesia, epidural, or nerve block anesthesia); (2) age ≥18 years; (3) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification of I–III; (4) no history of hypertension, and the basic systolic (SBP) <140 mmHg and diastolic BP (DBP) <90 mmHg while resting in the hospital room 1 d before the operation, and not receiving any vasoactive agent. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) severe arrhythmias; (2) psychiatric disorders or cognitive impairment that limited their ability to communicate.

Participants were separately divided into case (pre-anesthesia BP elevation) and control (normal pre-anesthesia BP without elevation) groups based on absolute and relative pre-anesthesia BP elevation cutoffs.

Data Collection

Patients were admitted to the hospital 1–3 days before their scheduled surgery. Detailed demographic and clinical information were collected from the patients 1 day before surgery, including but not limited to age, sex, BMI, ASA class, comorbidities, smoking status (defined as one or more cigarettes per day for at least 6 months),12 alcohol drinking status (defined as consuming one or more alcoholic beverages per week on average for at least 6 months),12 type of surgical procedure, and preoperative laboratory data, including triglyceride (TG) and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels.

Sleep duration on the night before surgery was recorded, and patients completed the Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS) and Perioperative Anxiety Scale-7 (PAS-7) questionnaires in the surgical waiting room after being separated from their companions. The AIS13 is a validated self-assessment tool used to measure sleep difficulty and insomnia severity over the preceding month (Supplementary Figure 1). It comprises 8 items, each rated by patients on a 4-point scale (0 = not at all or never; 3 = greatly or often). The total score is the sum of the 8 items, with an AIS score > 6 indicating insomnia. Patients also reported their use of hypnotics the night before surgery. The PAS-714 questionnaire is a brief self-reported scale specifically designed for Chinese surgical patients to assess their level of preoperative anxiety (Supplementary Figure 2). It includes seven items rated by the patients on a five-point scale ranging from 0 (not anxious at all) to 4 (extremely anxious). The anxiety score was calculated as the sum of the seven items, with a PAS-7 score of 8 or higher indicating significant preoperative anxiety. Details regarding the items constituting the subscales of the AIS and PAS - 7 questionnaire are provided in the supplemental material.

Blood Pressure Monitoring

The patients’ basic BP was defined as the measurement taken in the hospital room one day before the surgery. All patients stayed in the supine bed position, and trained nurses recorded BP and heart rate (HR) with a standard upper-arm cuff sphygmomanometer (HBP-9030, Omron, Tokyo, Japan). Two readings were obtained separately in the morning (7:00–8:00 AM) and afternoon (13:00–14:00 PM). We adopted the measurement matching the patient’s actual operation period (morning or afternoon) as the final baseline value. For pre-anesthesia BP measurement, patients were transferred to the operating table and rested for a minimum of 5 minutes after ECG electrodes and BP cuffs were attached. Pre-induction BP was detected via an anesthesia monitor (IntelliVue MP50; Philips, Netherlands).

All ward BP assessments were performed on the patient’s single fixed upper arm, which was consistently used for subsequent intraoperative pre-anesthetic BP testing. Two consecutive measurements were taken at three-minute intervals and averaged for both ward baseline and intraoperative pre-anesthetic assessments. If the difference between the two measurements was greater than 10 mmHg, a third measurement was performed, after which the two closest values were averaged.

Defining Pre-Anesthesia BP Elevation

The definition of pre-anesthesia BP elevation was based on two thresholds commonly employed in clinical practice: one absolute and one relative to baseline.

1) An absolute threshold: SBP ≥140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥90 mmHg in the operating room prior to anesthesia induction.2

2) A relative threshold: pre-anesthesia SBP and/or DBP increase of more than 20% from baseline values.7

Sample Size Calculation

Preliminary data showed that, when defined by the absolute threshold, the incidence rate of pre-anesthesia BP elevation was about 35% in individuals with no history of hypertension, with approximately eight factors associated with the increase. Considering a potential 10% loss to follow-up, we adopted the events per variable (EPV) method for sample size estimation in logistic regression analyses.15,16 Using this approach, we determined that a total sample size of 508 was required (calculation: 8×20 ÷ 0.35 ÷ 0.9 = 508).

Statistical Analysis

The distribution of all continuous data was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± SD and compared by independent t-tests; non-normal data were reported as median (IQR) and analyzed with Mann–Whitney U-tests. All categorical data were summarized as counts (percentages). Unordered categorical variables were compared with chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests where applicable, and ordinal categorical variables were assessed using Mann–Whitney U-tests. Univariate logistic regression was conducted for all candidate variables. Variables with univariate P < 0.05 were tested for multicollinearity (VIF < 5, tolerance > 0.1). Collinearity existed among the three lipid indicators (TG, HDL and TG/HDL ratio); and only TG/HDL ratio was retained, and all other non-collinear variables were included in multivariable logistic regression. Odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to assess the strength of the association between each observed indicator and pre-anesthesia BP elevation. The ability of the model to discriminate between patients with and without pre-anesthesia BP increase was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated to quantify its predictive accuracy. A calibration curve was generated to evaluate the agreement between the predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. Furthermore, we performed bootstrap internal validation with 1000 resamples for both threshold models to calculate optimism-corrected AUC and mitigate overfitting bias in this single-center study. Two sensitivity analyses were conducted to test result robustness: we separately added eight-category surgical type and operation timing (morning/afternoon) into the adjusted regression models.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 25.0; IBM, USA) and R (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant Selection and Clinical Characteristics

This case-control study enrolled 540 adult patients undergoing elective non-cardiac and non-obstetric surgeries. Patients with severe arrhythmias (n = 2), psychiatric conditions or inability to communicate (n = 4), and incomplete questionnaires (n = 6) were excluded. The final analysis included 528 patients, of whom 188 (35.6%) experienced BP increase according to the absolute threshold, and 149 (28.2%) experienced BP increase based on the relative threshold (Figure 1).

Associated Factors for Pre-Anesthesia BP Elevation Defined by Absolute Threshold

Univariate logistic regression analysis was initially performed to assess associated factors for pre-anesthesia BP elevation defined by absolute threshold. This analysis identified 8 variables with a p-value < 0.05 (age, BMI, DM, alcohol drinking, TG/HDL ratio, PAS-7 scores, AIS scores, and preoperative sleep duration), as detailed in Table 3. Subsequently, all 8 variables were included in a multivariable logistic regression analysis following the collinearity analysis. The final multivariable model revealed a positive correlation between pre-anesthesia BP elevation occurrence and the following variables: increasing age (OR, 1.08; 95% CI, 1.05–1.10, P < 0.001), high BMI (OR, 1.12; 95% CI, 1.03–1.21, P = 0.006), alcohol drinking (OR, 2.74; 95% CI, 1.55–4.84, P < 0.001), elevated TG/HDL ratio (OR, 2.66; 95% CI, 1.84–3.86, P < 0.001), and high PAS-7 scores (OR, 1.17; 95% CI, 1.10–1.25, P < 0.001). In contrast, a long preoperative sleep duration was associated with a reduction of pre-anesthesia BP elevation incidence (2–4 hours: OR 0.37, 95% CI 0.15–0.95, P = 0.039, 4–6 hours: OR 0.14, 95% CI 0.06–0.35, P < 0.001, 6–8 hours: OR 0.12, 95% CI 0.04–0.34, P < 0.001). Associated Factors for Pre-Anesthesia BP Elevation Defined by Relative Threshold

To further explore the factors contributing to pre-anesthesia BP elevation, we conducted an additional analysis using a relative threshold to define pre-anesthesia BP elevation. Five variables with P-values <0.05 (age, TG/HDL ratio, PAS-7 scores, AIS scores and preoperative sleep duration) were identified and included in the multivariable logistic regression model. The results revealed that increasing age (OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.01–1.04, P < 0.001), elevated TG/HDL ratio (OR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.18–1.88, P < 0.001) and high PAS-7 score (OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.02–1.13, P = 0.007) were positively correlated with a greater change in pre-anesthesia BP. Discussion

In this case-control study, both absolute and relative thresholds were employed to define pre-anesthesia BP elevation. Among the 528 patients included in this study, 188 (35.6%) experienced BP increase by the absolute threshold and 149 (28.2%) experienced BP increase by the relative threshold. Analyses revealed that when the absolute threshold was applied to define pre-anesthesia BP elevation, increasing age, high BMI, hyperlipemia, alcohol drinking, and anxiety were positively correlated with transient BP elevation, whereas longer preoperative sleep showed an inverse correlation. Under the relative threshold, increasing age, hyperlipemia and anxiety remained significantly correlated with BP elevation. Despite the discrepancy in detected correlates between the two classification standards, the shared relevant indicators identified by both approaches reflect the core modifiable and non-modifiable correlates closely linked to perioperative transient BP surges in normotensive surgical populations.

Our study revealed an interesting phenomenon: when patients were categorized based on different BP elevation criteria (absolute threshold vs relative threshold), the resulting groups exhibited distinctly opposite trends in baseline SBP levels. Specifically, patients with pre-anesthesia BP elevation as defined by the absolute threshold had significantly higher basic SBP levels (128.23 ± 8.48 mmHg) compared to controls (117.97 ± 10.15 mmHg). This finding suggests that individuals prone to transient BP elevation before anesthesia already have a relatively elevated baseline BP due to underlying health issues or associated factors. Similar to white-coat hypertension, these patients with pre-anesthesia BP elevation may also face an increased risk of developing sustained hypertension.17 Notably, the opposite pattern emerged for patients whose BP increase met the relative threshold: their baseline SBP levels (118.83 ± 12.41 mmHg) were significantly lower than those of the control group (122.72 ± 9.86 mmHg). This finding echoes a previous cohort study, where lower baseline MAP was linked to larger relative BP surges due to regression to the mean.18 The AUC of the logistic model based on the absolute cutoff was higher than that of the relative threshold model. Absolute BP readings directly reflect cardiovascular load, whereas relative percentage change is affected by individual baseline BP levels, which may interfere with its predictive ability.

Consistent with existing studies,2,7 our multivariable analysis confirmed increasing age as associated factors. In detail, each additional year increased the odds of pre-anesthesia BP elevation by 8% (OR 1.08, 95% CI 1.05–1.10, P<0.001) under the absolute threshold and by 3% (OR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.04, P<0.001) under the relative threshold. Of note, among patients aged > 60 years, 61.4% developed pre-anesthesia BP elevation as defined by the absolute threshold, which is markedly higher than the overall incidence rate of 35.6%. This finding aligns with established epidemiological data showing hypertension (≥140/90 mmHg) prevalence escalates from 7.3% (18–39 years) to 64.9% (≥60 years).19 The observed age-related susceptibility may reflect progressive arterial stiffening, which amplifies SBP variability and predisposes to transient hypertensive episodes.20 These findings highlight the importance of BP monitoring and management in elderly patients undergoing surgery.

A significant correlation was observed between preoperative anxiety scores and pre-anesthesia BP elevation according to both definitions. Remarkably, the reported prevalence of preoperative anxiety is 25.6% among Chinese adults undergoing elective surgery.21 Anxiety may trigger sympathetic activation and increased catecholamine secretion, which could raise heart rate and blood pressure and potentially correlate with perioperative adverse events.9,10 Individuals who experience heightened anxiety during a clinic or hospital visit are more prone to developing white-coat hypertension.22,23 Prior studies have noted that anti-anxiety interventions can lower blood pressure in patients with chronic hypertension,24,25 and preoperative anxiolytic agents such as bromazepam may attenuate pre-anesthetic BP readings in some cohorts.26 However, as the present study only identifies an associative relationship rather than proving causal effects or intervention efficacy, we cannot confirm that routine preoperative anti-anxiety medication reliably alleviates transient pre-anesthesia BP elevation. Further prospective interventional research is needed to verify whether anxiety-targeted measures can mitigate such perioperative hemodynamic fluctuations.

The present study identified elevated TG/HDL ratio as a correlate of pre-anesthesia BP elevation under both diagnostic criteria. The TG/HDL ratio serves as a marker reflecting chronic lipid metabolic disturbance, and prior evidence links this index to subclinical endothelial dysfunction, vascular inflammation and enhanced oxidative stress. These metabolic abnormalities may impair vascular compliance, accelerate arterial stiffening and disturb physiological blood pressure regulatory pathways, which collectively correlate with sustained high blood pressure in general populations.27,28 Apart from lipid profiles, higher BMI was also significantly associated with perioperative BP rise defined by the absolute threshold. Consistent with our results, previous research has reported obesity as a clinical correlate of pre-anesthetic hypertension.2 The Italian Lookup 7+ study (n = 7,907) showed that a gradient of increasing BP with higher levels of BMI.29 Although our observational data cannot confirm causal effects or verify the efficacy of preoperative lipid and weight intervention, these correlative associations still suggest that lipid metabolism and body weight deserve close preoperative evaluation as modifiable clinical indicators related to perioperative hemodynamic instability.

To explore sleep-related correlates of transient pre-anesthesia BP elevation, we evaluated patients’ preoperative overnight sleep duration and sleep quality via the AIS scale. Multivariable analyses showed longer preoperative sleep was inversely correlated with BP elevation under the absolute threshold. Relative to patients sleeping 0–2 hours, each incremental 2-hour sleep interval exhibited progressively lower correlation magnitudes (2–4 h: OR 0.37, 95% CI 0.15–0.95; 4–6 h: OR 0.14, 95% CI 0.06–0.35; 6–8 h: OR 0.12, 95% CI 0.04–0.34; all p < 0.05). Although univariate regression identified an association between AIS scores and BP elevation, this link disappeared after adjusting for confounders in multivariable modeling. Sleep may influence BP through multiple mechanisms, such as the nervous system, hormones, circadian rhythm, and vascular function.30–32 Insufficient sleep, poor sleep quality, or circadian rhythm disruption can all result in increased BP and abnormal circadian rhythms, which in turn raises the risk of hypertension and cardiovascular diseases.33,34 Taken together, these findings provide evidence to support targeted interventions focused on improving sleep duration the night before surgery to help maintain stable pre-anesthesia BP.

Alcohol consumption was identified as an associated factor for pre-anesthesia BP elevation when defined using the absolute threshold. Previous research has confirmed that alcohol intake, particularly heavy drinking patterns, increases the risk of primary hypertension.35–37 Alcohol consumption can cause oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction, which are early warning signs of blood vessel damage and atherosclerosis.38 Additionally, alcohol can increase intracellular calcium levels, enhance vascular reactivity, and contribute to hypertension.39 Although multivariable logistic regression analysis failed to identify diabetes as associated factors for pre-anesthesia BP elevation, we observed that diabetic patients had a markedly higher incidence (51.51%) of pre-anesthesia BP elevation according to the absolute threshold than non-diabetics (33.33%). Diabetes and hypertension share several common associated factors, including endothelial dysfunction, vascular inflammation, arterial remodeling, atherosclerosis, hyperlipemia, and obesity.40,41 These conditions frequently exacerbate each other, resulting in more severe cardiovascular diseases.

This study has several limitations. First, this single-center case-control study with a limited sample size may restrict the generalizability of our findings. Second, baseline blood pressure was measured on ward one day before surgery, which might not accurately reflect patients’ habitual home blood pressure status. Third, preoperative sleep duration was collected via self-report rather than objective sleep monitoring equipment. Fourth, patients with chronic hypertension and those receiving RAAS-regulating medications were excluded from enrollment; these clinically important populations require dedicated exploration in subsequent research. Fifth, sex-stratified subgroup analyses were not performed due to insufficient sample capacity, and potential gender-specific differences in correlates of pre-anesthesia BP elevation remain unclear. Sixth, only pre-induction blood pressure was recorded, without continuous monitoring of intraoperative and postoperative hemodynamic changes or related adverse complications.

Conclusion

Approximately one-third of normotensive surgical patients experience transient pre-anesthesia BP elevation, and the absolute threshold identifies more affected patients. Older age, hyperlipemia and preoperative anxiety showed associations with this temporary hemodynamic fluctuation regardless of the two BP definition criteria. Analyses based on the absolute threshold further detected correlations with high BMI and alcohol consumption, while longer sleep duration was correlated with a lower likelihood of such BP elevation. Future multicenter large-sample studies are needed to validate these correlations and extend relevant exploration to patients with chronic hypertension. Prospective trials should further investigate whether adjusting modifiable factors mitigates transient pre-anesthesia BP elevation and identify its associated postoperative adverse outcomes, to build solid evidence for perioperative anesthetic care.
