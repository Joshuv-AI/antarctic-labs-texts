Overall survival of immunotherapy versus standard of care in chemorefractory microsatellite stable metastatic colorectal cancer: a propensity score matched analysis of 708 patients
Authors: Guglielmo Vetere, Emerik Osterlund, Mahmoud M G Yousef, Songwit Payapwattanawong, Giulia Maddalena, Jumanah Alshenaifi, Kristin D Alfaro, Mark Knafl, Van K Morris, Christine M Parseghian, Kanwal P S Raghav, Robert A Wolff, Arvind Dasari, Michael J Overman, Scott Kopetz, John Paul Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have limited efficacy in proficient mismatch repair/microsatellite stable (pMMR/MSS) metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC). However, selected patients with specific metastatic patterns may derive benefit.MethodsPatients with chemorefractory pMMR/MSS mCRC treated with ICI-based regimens were retrospectively identified. A comparison cohort treated with trifluridine/tipiracil±bevacizumab, regorafenib, or fruquintinib as standard of care (SOC) was generated through 1:1 propensity score matching by age, sex, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS), liver metastases (present/absent), and RAS/BRAF status. Overall survival (OS) was compared using Cox regression.ResultsA total of 354 patients treated with ICIs and 354 treated with SOC were matched. Median age was 55 years, 52% male, 32% ECOG PS 0, 30% right-sided, and 69% RAS mutated in both groups, while 61% and 60% had liver metastases, respectively. Median OS (mOS) was 10.8 months with ICIs and 9.0 months with SOC (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.64 to 0.92, p=0.004). In patients without liver metastases, mOS was longer with ICIs than SOC (19.1 vs 13.2 months, HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.43 to 0.80, p<0.001), whereas outcomes were similar in patients with liver metastases (6.4 vs 6.5 months, p=0.303). In univariable analyses, age, sex, primary tumor site, and RAS/BRAF status were not associated with OS. Treatment with ICIs, absence of liver metastases, one prior line of therapy, less than three metastatic sites, and ECOG PS 0 were associated with the most favorable outcomes in univariable and multivariable models.ConclusionsIn chemorefractory pMMR/MSS mCRC without liver metastases, ICI-based regimens yielded longer OS than SOC. Further investigation of ICIs in this patient population is warranted.

Background

Metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) remains a significant clinical challenge, particularly for patients who progress beyond standard first-line and second-line therapies. Despite considerable advances in systemic treatment over the past two decades,1–3 clinical benefit in the refractory setting remains limited.4–7

Current treatment guidelines recommend several third-line and later-line options for patients with refractory mCRC following failure of fluoropyrimidine-based, oxaliplatin-based, and irinotecan-based regimens.2 3 These include trifluridine/tipiracil (FTD/TPI), either as monotherapy4 or in combination with bevacizumab,5 and the oral multitargeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors (mTKIs) regorafenib6 and fruquintinib.7 The phase III SUNLIGHT trial established FTD/TPI plus bevacizumab as the preferred third-line regimen, demonstrating superior outcomes compared with FTD/TPI monotherapy, with a median overall survival (mOS) approaching 11 months for the combination,5 leaving regorafenib and fruquintinib as subsequent options.2 However, objective response rates (ORR) remain modest across all standard-of-care (SOC) regimens, typically ranging from 1–5%, and median progression-free survival (mPFS) rarely exceeds 4–5 months.4–7

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionized the outcome of the minority of patients with mismatch repair deficient/microsatellite instability-high (dMMR/MSI-H) mCRC.8–10 However, the proficient mismatch repair/microsatellite stable (pMMR/MSS) subtype, which accounts for approximately 93.5–96.5% of all mCRC cases,2 11 is largely resistant to ICIs12 due to its characteristically “cold” tumor microenvironment, marked by low tumor mutational burden, reduced neoantigen generation, and limited T-cell infiltration.13–16 This unmet need in pMMR/MSS mCRC has driven intensive investigation into combination immunotherapy strategies designed to convert immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors responsive to ICIs.

The combination of ICIs targeting both cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) and programmed death-1 (PD-1)/programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) has emerged as a potential approach in pMMR/MSS mCRC. CTLA-4 blockade enhances T-cell priming and activation in lymphoid tissues, while PD-1/PD-L1 inhibition restores effector T-cell function within the tumor microenvironment.17 Botensilimab, a novel Fc-enhanced anti-CTLA-4 antibody designed to augment antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity and enhance innate immune activation, combined with balstilimab (anti-PD-1) achieved an ORR of 17% and a disease control rate of 61% in heavily pretreated patients with MSS mCRC.18

Combination strategies incorporating anti-angiogenic agents with immunotherapy have also gained substantial attention. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibition can normalize tumor vasculature, enhance T-cell infiltration, reduce immunosuppressive cell populations, and upregulate PD-L1 expression, effects that may synergize with immune checkpoint blockade.19–22 Several phase I/II trials combining antiangiogenic mTKIs (regorafenib, fruquintinib, lenvatinib) with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors have reported inconsistent ORRs (6–33%) and mPFS (1.8–3.8 months) in MSS mCRC,23–26 whereas the RIN regimen (regorafenib, ipilimumab, nivolumab) achieved an ORR of 28%, mPFS of 5 months and mOS of 20 months.27 Most recently, the randomized phase III STELLAR-303 trial investigated the combination of zanzalintinib (mTKI) and atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1) compared with regorafenib and was the first to show a significant improvement in overall survival (OS) with an ICI-based regimen in chemorefractory pMMR/MSS mCRC.28

The presence of liver metastases has increasingly been proposed as a determinant of resistance to immunotherapy in MSS mCRC. Several subgroup analyses of clinical trials investigating ICI-based strategies have shown the negative impact of liver metastases on treatment efficacy.18 23–25 29 30 These findings are aligned with the uniquely immunotolerant microenvironment of the liver, characterized by an enrichment of regulatory T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and tumor-associated macrophages.31

This large retrospective analysis uses propensity score matching (PSM) to assess the outcomes of ICI-based and SOC regimens for patients with chemorefractory pMMR/MSS mCRC and aims to provide robust comparative evidence regarding OS of these treatment approaches with a focus on the presence or absence of liver metastases.

Methods

Study design

This is a retrospective cohort study conducted at The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center, evaluating OS in patients with chemorefractory mCRC treated with ICI-based or SOC regimens.

Population and eligibility

Patients with histopathologically confirmed colorectal cancer treated with either an ICI-based regimen from 2014 to 2025 (experimental cohort) or an approved later-line SOC regimen from 2012 to 2025 (comparator cohort) for metastatic disease were screened. Patients in the comparator cohort were included from the time of regorafenib approval to maximize the pool available for PSM. “Chemo-refractory” was defined as progression, intolerance or contraindication to standard cytotoxic chemotherapy (fluoropyrimidine, oxaliplatin, irinotecan) with or without VEGF and/or epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitors based on mutational status and clinical decision; prior oxaliplatin received in the adjuvant setting was also considered. Exclusion criteria were dMMR/MSI-H status or Lynch syndrome, treatment being received for minimal residual disease or resectable stage IV disease, and presence of loss-of-function POLE mutations.32 Patients with incomplete data or other concurrent active malignancies were removed a priori and not screened. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cohort studies.

Treatment exposure and regimen classification

SOC regimens included FTD/TPI±bevacizumab, regorafenib, and fruquintinib, reflecting contemporary later-line SOC options.

ICI-based regimens contained any ICI single or double with or without other agents and were further divided into three classes according to mechanisms of action and clinical relevance: (1) Single-ICI±other (anti-PD-1/PD-L1 with or without vaccines, targeted therapies, or other immunomodulatory agents); (2) Double-ICI±other (anti-PD-1/PD-L1 and anti-CTLA-4 with or without vaccines, targeted therapies, or other immunomodulatory agents); (3) mTKI+ICI (mTKI combined with single-ICI or double-ICI). Patients treated with combinations of ICIs with cellular therapy or BRAF and EGFR inhibitors were excluded.

Treatment was administered until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, death, or patient/treating physician’s decision. Patients were considered eligible if at least one cycle of therapy was completed. Prior treatment with FTD/TPI±bevacizumab, regorafenib, or fruquintinib was not allowed in the SOC group.

Definitions of metastatic sites

Metastatic sites were determined radiographically at the start of index treatment from Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) reads (where available) or radiology reports and classified either as presence or absence of liver metastases. Metastatic patterns were further subdivided in liver±others, peritoneum±others not including liver (denoted “peritoneum±others”), lung±others not including liver or peritoneum (denoted “lung±others”), and others-only not including liver, peritoneum, or lung (denoted “others-only”).

Propensity score matching

We used PSM to mitigate baseline imbalances between groups. Propensity scores were estimated with a logistic regression model in which the experimental cohort was regressed on prespecified covariates selected a priori for clinical and prognostic relevance: age <55/≥55 years old (median age in the experimental group), sex, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS), presence versus absence of liver metastases at treatment start, and RAS/BRAF mutation status. We performed nearest-neighbor matching without replacement with a 1:1 pairing. Covariate balance was assessed using absolute standardized mean differences (SMDs); values <0.10 were considered excellent and <0.25 acceptable. Balance diagnostics and matched datasets were generated with the MatchIt and cobalt packages.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was OS of ICI-based versus SOC regimens in the overall population and in subgroups by presence or absence of liver metastases. OS was defined as the time from index treatment initiation to death from any cause; patients alive at last contact were censored on that date (data cut-off: August 6, 2025). Secondary prespecified endpoints were OS by ICI-based regimen class and metastatic pattern.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of the matched population were reported using summary statistics. Median follow-up was estimated using the reverse Kaplan-Meier method. OS was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. HRs and 95% CIs were obtained using Cox proportional hazards models. Proportional hazard assumption was assessed visually from the Kaplan-Meier curves; no substantial violations were seen. Univariable and multivariable models were fitted including treatment group (all ICI-based or ICI-based classes vs SOC), presence/absence of liver metastases or metastatic pattern, ECOG PS, sex, age at treatment start, primary tumor location, RAS/BRAF mutational status, number of metastatic sites, and number of prior lines of therapy. Only variables that reached significance (p<0.10) in univariable analyses were included in the multivariable analyses. Two-sided p<0.05 and HRs not crossing 1 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted in R V.4.5.1 (“Great Square Root” released on June 13, 2025).

Results

Description of experimental and comparator cohorts

A total of 475 patients with pMMR/MSS chemorefractory mCRC treated with ICI-based regimens from 2014 to 2025 were identified. After exclusion of 121 patients, the final experimental cohort consisted of 354 patients. Similarly, 907 patients with chemorefractory pMMR/MSS mCRC treated with SOC regimens from 2012 to 2025 were identified. After exclusion of 12 patients, the final comparator cohort available for PSM matching consisted of 895 patients. Details are reported in figure 1.

Covariate balance and final propensity score matched cohort

Before matching all covariates showed varying degrees of imbalance (absolute SMD of distance=0.71) due to intrinsic differences between the cohorts. After matching, all covariates achieved absolute SMDs <0.10, indicating excellent postmatch balance across groups (online supplemental figure S1). The final matched cohorts consisted of 354 patients in the experimental group each paired with 354 patients in the comparator group, yielding 708 patients suitable for comparative analyses.

Baseline demographics of experimental and comparator cohorts

Median age was 55 years, 52% were male, 32% had ECOG PS 0, 30% had right-sided primary tumors, and 69% were RAS mutated in both the ICI and SOC groups, while liver metastases were present in 61% and 60%, respectively (table 1). Non-liver metastatic patterns were peritoneum±others in 14% vs 16%, lung±others in 21% vs 19%, and others-only in 4% vs 5% of the ICI and SOC groups, respectively. White/Caucasian was the most prevalent race/ethnicity in both groups. All patients had received previous 5-FU/capecitabine in both groups, and nearly all patients had received oxaliplatin and/or irinotecan (97–100%). Bevacizumab was used in 92% in both groups, EGFR-inhibitors in 86% and 97% of RAS and BRAF wild-type, and encorafenib+cetuximab in 55% and 70% of BRAF V600E mutants in the ICI and SOC groups, respectively.

Treatments in the experimental and comparator cohorts

ICI-based regimens were single-ICI±other in 72%, double-ICI±other in 14% and mTKI+ICI in 14% of the patients (table 1). Detailed descriptions of mechanisms of action are presented in online supplemental table S1. SOC regimens were FTD/TPI in 31%, FTD/TPI+bevacizumab in 34%, regorafenib in 33%, and fruquintinib in 3%. The median number and IQR of subsequent lines of therapy was 1 (0–2) in the ICI group and 1 (0–1) in the SOC group (Mann-Whitney U test p=0.282).

Overall survival for ICI-based and SOC regimens by presence or absence of liver metastases

Median follow-up was 25.4 months (95% CI 22.2 to 28.5). Among all patients, mOS was 10.8 months in the ICI group and 9.0 months in the SOC group (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.64 to 0.92, p=0.004, figure 2A). mOS was similar between the ICI and SOC groups in patients with liver metastases (6.4 vs 6.5 months, p=0.303, figure 2B), whereas mOS was significantly longer with ICI compared with SOC for patients without liver metastases (19.1 vs 13.2 months, HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.43 to 0.80, p<0.001, figure 2C).

Overall survival for ICI-based regimen subgroups and SOC regimens by presence or absence of liver metastases

When classifying ICI-based regimens into subgroups, mOS was 9.0 months for SOC, 10.1 months for single-ICI±other, 10.7 months for double-ICI±other, and 15.4 months for mTKI+ICI, with single-ICI±other and mTKI+ICI having significantly longer OS than SOC, p=0.023 and 0.006, respectively (figure 3A). OS estimates for each SOC regimen are presented in online supplemental figure S2. In patients with liver metastases, mOS was 6.5 months for SOC, 6.4 months for single-ICI±other, 5.7 months for double-ICI±other, and 10.9 months for mTKI+ICI, with no significant differences compared with SOC (figure 3B). Among patients without liver metastases, mOS was 13.2 months for SOC, 18.4 months for single-ICI±other, 20.5 months for double-ICI±other, and 20.4 months for mTKI+ICI; all ICI subgroups had a significantly longer OS than SOC with the best outcomes observed for double-ICI±other and mTKI+ICI, p=0.010–0.025 (figure 3C).

Overall survival by the addition of VEGF inhibition to ICI-based regimens

The role of adding any VEGF inhibition (mTKI, n=48, or bevacizumab, n=7) to immunotherapy was further explored. In all patients, ICI±other and ICI+VEGF inhibition±other showed an improved OS compared with SOC (mOS 10.1 vs 15.5 vs 9.0 months, p=0.031 and 0.002, online supplemental figure S3A). This was also seen in the subgroup without liver metastases (mOS 18.4 vs 20.4 vs 13.2 months, p=0.003 and 0.012, online supplemental figure S3C), whereas no significant difference was seen in patients with liver metastases (online supplemental figure S3B).

Overall survival for metastatic patterns by treatment type

Outcomes of non-liver metastatic patterns were also analyzed. Among all patients, mOS was 6.4 months for liver±others, 12.5 months for peritoneum±others, 17.9 months for lung±others, and 19.9 months for others-only (p<0.001, online supplemental figure S4A). In the SOC group, mOS was 6.5 months for liver±others, 13.3 months for peritoneum±others, 13.2 months for lung±others, and 13.4 months for others-only (p<0.001, online supplemental figure S4B). In the ICI group, mOS was 6.4 months for liver±others, 11.8 months for peritoneum±others, 19.9 months for lung±others, and 67.1 months for others-only (p<0.001, online supplemental figure S4C).

Overall survival for ICI-based and SOC regimens by metastatic patterns

When ICI-based regimens were compared with SOC regimens in each non-liver metastatic subgroup, ICI-based regimens achieved a significantly better OS in the lung±others (19.9 vs 13.2 months, HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.33 to 0.78, p=0.002), whereas no statistically significant differences were observed in peritoneum±others and others-only (online supplemental figure S5A–C).

Univariable and multivariable Cox regression models for overall survival

In univariable analysis age, sex, primary tumor location, and RAS/BRAF status did not significantly affect OS. ECOG PS 0, one prior line of therapy, absence of liver metastases, and treatment with ICI-based regimens were associated with a longer OS, whereas the presence of three or more metastatic sites was associated with a shorter OS. In a multivariable model including only covariates significant in univariable analyses, ECOG PS 0, one prior line of therapy, absence of liver metastases, and treatment with ICI-based regimens remained statistically significant predictors of improved OS, while the presence of three or more metastatic sites confirmed its correlation with a worse outcome (figure 4).

As ICI-based regimen classes and non-liver metastatic subgroups were significantly associated with OS in the univariable analyses, these were further explored alongside more stratified number of metastatic sites and prior lines of therapy. ECOG PS, number of metastatic sites, number of prior lines of therapy, metastatic pattern, and treatment regimen were included in the multivariable models; the presence of three or more metastatic sites remained a significant predictor of shorter OS. One prior line of therapy confirmed to be correlated with a better outcome, while no difference was observed between receiving two versus three or more. Peritoneum±others, lung±others, and others-only predicted an increasingly better OS compared with liver±others, whereas single-ICI±other and mTKI+ICI were correlated with improved OS compared with SOC regimens (online supplemental figure S6).

Discussion

In this large propensity score matched cohort of patients with chemorefractory pMMR/MSS mCRC, treatment with ICI-based regimens was correlated with significantly longer OS compared with SOC therapies. Notably, this comparison was made against a contemporary SOC cohort that included standard later-line therapies following the adoption of FTD/TPI plus bevacizumab, rendering it a clinically representative comparator group. Our findings confirm at scale that immunotherapy can provide meaningful benefit in a subset of patients with pMMR/MSS mCRC, a population historically characterized by limited sensitivity to ICIs.

Several ICI-based combinations have shown early signals of activity in pMMR/MSS mCRC, but results have varied widely across studies. Dual checkpoint blockade with botensilimab and balstilimab demonstrated encouraging early-phase activity, primarily in patients without active liver metastases,18 but ORR did not meet expectations when evaluated prospectively in a non-liver cohort while survival data are awaited.29 Similarly, studies combining PD-1 blockade with mTKIs have shown heterogeneous but encouraging activity, with outcomes often influenced by metastatic site distribution. In a recent pooled analysis of the phase II REGONIVO (regorafenib plus nivolumab) and phase I RIN (regorafenib plus ipilimumab and nivolumab) trials, objective responses were observed almost exclusively in patients without liver metastases, where lung-only or lung-predominant patterns achieved the best outcomes, underscoring a strong site-specific dependence of efficacy.33 Furthermore, in the phase III LEAP-017 trial evaluating lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab versus SOC, a trend for improved OS was observed in the non-liver subgroup, whereas patients with liver metastases derived no benefit.25 However, these observations are drawn from subgroup analyses of a statistically negative trial precluding definitive conclusions. Likewise, in the phase Ib/II study of fruquintinib plus tislelizumab in heavily pretreated MSS mCRC, efficacy outcomes were numerically more favorable in patients without baseline liver metastases, achieving notably longer median PFS, median OS, and higher disease control rate despite comparable response rates.26 Collectively, these results support the therapeutic potential of mTKI+ICI combinations while highlighting the strong influence of metastatic sites on treatment benefit and the limitations of current evidence pending confirmation in biomarker-stratified randomized trials.

More recently, the phase III STELLAR-303 trial demonstrated improved OS and progression-free survival (PFS) for zanzalintinib (mTKI) plus atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1) compared with regorafenib, providing the first randomized evidence supporting an ICI-based regimen in this population.28 However, the interpretation of the results is limited by the use of regorafenib as the comparator, and by non-mature subgroup data in patients without liver metastases. Thus, while STELLAR-303 prospectively demonstrates that immunotherapy-based regimens can improve outcomes in pMMR/MSS mCRC, the patient populations most likely to benefit remain insufficiently characterized.

Our study clarifies this by showing that the OS benefit of ICI-based therapy was restricted to patients without liver metastases, whereas outcomes were similar between ICI-based and SOC regimens among those with hepatic involvement. This finding persisted in the multivariable analysis, in which treatment with any ICI-based regimen and the absence of liver metastases were independent predictors of improved OS. These results reinforce the concept that metastatic site distribution is a major determinant of immunotherapy benefit in pMMR/MSS mCRC.

The differential outcomes we observed across metastatic patterns align with prior observations and the immunologic characteristics of different sites of metastasis. The liver maintains a constitutively tolerogenic immune environment, and metastatic involvement amplifies systemic immune suppression through increased regulatory T-cell activity, myeloid-derived suppressor cell recruitment, impaired dendritic cell activation, and sequestration of activated cytotoxic T cells.34–37 In preclinical models, hepatic tumor antigen recruits CCR2+ monocyte-derived macrophages that delete activated Fas+CD8+ T cells via FasL-dependent apoptosis while retaining them in the liver, yielding a systemic loss of tumor-specific CD8+ T cells rather than a purely intrahepatic effect.34 Through a complementary, largely PD-1-independent mechanism, hepatic antigen also drives regulatory T cells and CD11b+ myeloid-derived suppressor cells to deprive CD8+ T cells of CD80/86 costimulation, imposing functional anergy, which may explain the limited activity of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibition in these patients.37 In contrast, patients with the lung±others pattern achieved the most favorable outcomes with ICI-based therapy in our cohort, consistent with a more immune-permissive microenvironment characterized by richer T-cell and B-cell infiltration within the tumor core, more frequent tertiary lymphoid-like structures, and a higher abundance of antigen-presenting cells (APC) with more advantageous APC-CD8+ T-cell spatial relationships.38 39 Peritoneal involvement, on the other hand, was associated with intermediate outcomes, an observation that may reflect its distinct stromal and microenvironmental organization including macrophage-mediated immune regulation, reduced densities of APCs and CD8+ T cells, increased fibroblast infiltration and elevated PD-1/PD-L1 expression.38 40 41 Outcomes associated with other metastatic sites were limited by low numerosity and remained largely anecdotal.

It is important to acknowledge, however, that the poor outcomes associated with hepatic involvement in our cohort may not be entirely attributable to immunotherapy-specific resistance. In the SOC group, treated exclusively with non-immunotherapy regimens, patients with liver metastases achieved a mOS of only 6.5 months, compared with 13.2–13.4 months across all non-liver metastatic patterns, suggesting that hepatic involvement confers a degree of general treatment resistance and/or is associated with intrinsically more aggressive tumor biology independent of any immunological mechanism. The limited ICI benefit observed in liver-involved disease in our cohort therefore likely reflects the convergence of intrinsic tumor biology and general treatment resistance with immune-specific mechanisms.

Notably, all non-liver metastatic patterns were independently prognostic of longer OS compared with those involving the liver, but the lung±others subgroup was the only metastatic pattern in which ICI-based regimens were associated with significantly longer OS than SOC. This supports the concept that uniquely immune-permissive phenotypes associated with specific metastatic patterns influence immunotherapy responsiveness in pMMR/MSS mCRC, where lung-predominant may represent the most clinically sensitive in the absence of liver or peritoneal involvement.

Our analysis of ICI-based regimen classes provides further insight into how immunotherapy may be most effectively deployed in this setting. Among patients without liver metastases, all ICI-based strategies were associated with improved OS compared with SOC in univariable analysis, with the longest OS observed for dual checkpoint blockade and mTKI+ICI combinations. However, in multivariable models adjusting for liver involvement or metastatic pattern, number of metastatic sites, ECOG PS, and number of prior lines, statistical significance was retained only for single-ICI±other and mTKI+ICI regimens. The absence of significance for double-ICI regimens likely reflects limited sample size and variability rather than a lack of biological activity, as the survival estimates were comparable to single-ICI-based approaches. Taken together, these results suggest that multiple immunotherapy strategies may offer benefit in selected patients, but efficacy of different combinations varies, and the best strategy is yet to be defined.

Our three classes differ in their core immunobiology. Single-agent PD-1/PD-L1 blockade restores exhausted effector T-cell function, while the added CTLA-4 blockade in double-ICI regimens also enhances T-cell priming, at the cost of greater toxicity; the mTKI+ICI class adds antiangiogenic vascular normalization to promote T-cell infiltration. The remaining partner agents (online supplemental table S1) span a broad, largely investigational landscape, none represented by sufficient numbers for dedicated analysis. Class-level effects should therefore be read as reflecting the dominant mechanistic backbone rather than any specific combination.

Additionally, we observed an association between VEGF pathway inhibition (via multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitors or bevacizumab) and improved outcome in non-liver disease which aligns with the hypothesis that angiogenic modulation can enhance immune responsiveness by restoring vascular normalization, improving T-cell infiltration, and reducing immunosuppressive myeloid populations.19–22 On the other hand, the addition of these agents did not significantly improve outcomes in patients with liver metastases, suggesting that angiogenic modulation alone is insufficient to overcome the profound immune dysfunction characteristic of hepatic involvement. Findings from the AtezoTRIBE study showed that liver metastases, while associated with poor prognosis, did not preclude benefit from the addition of atezolizumab to first-line FOLFOXIRI+bevacizumab in pMMR/MSS mCRC.42 Notably, patients with an Immunoscore-Immune-Checkpoint (IS-IC)-high tumor microenvironment derived greater benefit from atezolizumab even in the presence of hepatic involvement, suggesting that intensive chemotherapy and immune-related biomarkers may partially overcome the immune-refractory milieu of liver metastases.43 While definitive conclusions cannot be drawn given the exploratory nature of our analysis and the distinct treatment setting of the AtezoTRIBE study, these observations further support the need for biologically and anatomically tailored therapeutic strategies, rather than a uniform application of immunotherapy across all patients with pMMR/MSS mCRC.

The strengths of this study include its large sample size, minimal missing data, and the excellent covariate balance with the control cohort which enabled solid comparisons between the groups. Importantly, the control cohort is representative of contemporary later-line therapies with a third of the patients receiving FTD/TPI plus bevacizumab. Limitations include its retrospective nature, single high-volume academic tertiary center setting, the absence of randomization and translational correlatives, the possibility of residual confounding from unmeasured variables despite PSM, the heterogeneity of the SOC comparator, and the potential effects of subsequent therapies. Because ICIs are not standard therapy in this setting, the experimental cohort was largely trial-enrolled and thus subject to eligibility-based selection for fitness and possibly more indolent biology that PSM cannot capture; consistent with this, ICI-treated patients were more heavily pretreated (48% vs 35% with ≥3 prior lines). Although the OS benefit persisted after adjustment for prior lines, residual selection bias may limit generalizability to real-world populations. Additionally, PFS and response rates were not assessed, which limits broader applicability of our results, though OS is widely recognized as the most robust endpoint in the later-line setting. The heterogeneity of the other agents combined with ICIs precluded more stratified analysis by mechanism of action, but this landscape reflects more than a decade of attempts to find the right partner for immunotherapy in pMMR/MSS mCRC.

In conclusion, ICI-based regimens yielded longer OS in chemorefractory pMMR/MSS mCRC, but this benefit was restricted to patients without liver metastases. Our findings clarify previously inconclusive results, contextualize emerging randomized data, and support metastatic site distribution as a key determinant of immunotherapy benefit. Even in the refractory setting, a selected subset of patients with lower, non-hepatic disease burden may benefit, supporting continued investigation of ICIs in later lines with appropriate selection; similar principles may also inform evaluation in earlier, lower-burden or minimal residual disease settings. Future clinical trials should be stratified by metastatic patterns and incorporate translational endpoints to further characterize the underlying biological determinants of immunotherapy sensitivity in pMMR/MSS mCRC.
