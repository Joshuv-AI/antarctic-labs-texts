Transforming mental health care in Kenya: The critical role of routine outcomes monitoring in specialized services
Authors: Clara Paz, Anne A Obondo, Ian Kanyanya, Caleb Othieno, Manasi Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis short communication advocates for the strategic implementation of Routine Outcome Monitoring (ROM) systems within Kenya’s specialized mental health institutions, especially teaching and referral hospitals. The proposed shift aims to transform these institutions into learning health systems, dynamic structures that systematically capture and utilize data from everyday clinical practice to drive continuous improvement in care quality, accountability, and population mental health outcomes. Kenya’s mental health system, while advancing in policy and infrastructure, still faces a critical gap in routine, data-driven practices that support outcome-based care. ROM offers a practical and scalable solution to bridge this divide.We emphasize that this transition requires more than technological adoption. It demands a systemic reorientation: establishing digital standards for outcome tracking, allocating protected time for data reflection, strengthening referral pathways, and aligning local practices with national health indicators. These changes can help institutions become national exemplars, where evidence from clinical encounters informs policy, enhances training, and fosters research capacity.ROM is framed not as a technical add-on but as a transformative tool capable of reshaping how care is delivered, evaluated, and improved over time. It can anchor Kenya’s mental health care in real-time learning, ensuring that services adapt responsively to the needs of patients and communities. With political will, stakeholder collaboration, and thoughtful implementation, Kenya has the opportunity to lead among LMICs in establishing a mental health system that is evidence-informed, equitable, and sustainable.Keywords: Practice-Based Evidence, Mental health systems, Learning health systems, Kenya, Quality improvement, Practice-Oriented Research, Routine Outcome Monitoring

Introduction

Mental health services in Kenya operate within a context of considerable resource scarcity, receiving only 0.05 % of the national health budget despite a mounting burden of mental illness (Kwobah et al., 2023). This fundamental constraint hinders the scaling of services to meet national needs (Kumar et al., 2021). A critical, yet often overlooked, dimension of this challenge is the absence of robust data systems. The current lack of routine, clinically meaningful data creates a cycle of inefficiency, impeding progress. It prevents accurate planning, weakens advocacy for increased resources, and obscures opportunities to improve the quality and patient-centeredness of existing services. Without data to demonstrate need and effect, even the most dedicated services struggle to justify investment and evolve beyond a crisis-driven model. While the Kenya Health Information System (KHIS) tracks basic facility data, it lacks the granularity to support clinical learning or drive quality improvement in mental health (Division of Health Sector Monitoring and EvaluationMinistry of HealthKenya, 2020). This systemic failure manifests in three key areas: the inability to effectively allocate scarce resources, the difficulty in demonstrating the impact of services to funders and policymakers, and the challenge of ensuring that the care provided is evidence-based and effective. Here, Routine Outcome Monitoring (ROM) presents a transformative opportunity. ROM is not merely a technical tool for data collection; it is a foundational strategy to address these systemic failures directly. By enabling the continuous, real-time assessment of patient outcomes, ROM generates the actionable data needed to target resources more effectively, advocate for funding with concrete evidence, and drive continuous quality improvement.

The implementation of ROM represents a paradigm shift towards a learning health system for mental health in Kenya. Such a system uses data from everyday clinical encounters to inform decision-making at all levels, empowers clinicians, administrators, and policymakers to make data-informed decisions, promotes transparency and accountability, and ultimately improves the quality of care and patient outcomes at scale.

This communication highlights the pressing need to develop and implement ROM systems within Kenya’s specialized mental health institutions, with a particular focus on teaching and referral hospitals. We argue that ROM is a catalyst for innovation, equity, and sustainability, bridging a key gap in the system by enabling the continuous, real-time assessment of patient outcomes to guide care and resource allocation.

The status quo: missed opportunities for learning health systems

Kenya’s existing health information infrastructure, though adequate for basic service tracking, fails to support the iterative learning processes required for quality mental healthcare. For example, at Mathari National Teaching and Referral Hospital, ROM is either absent or inconsistently implemented—reflecting broader systemic challenges such as inadequate health information systems, lack of training in data utilization, and insufficient resources to enforce monitoring protocols (Munyaka et al., 2024). This gap is particularly detrimental in educational settings, where the absence of ROM limits opportunities to train future healthcare professionals in data-driven decision-making.

Currently, training often occurs in a data-poor vacuum, failing to equip clinicians, students in psychiatry and mental health, and even professionals from disciplines like nursing servicing psychiatric patients, with the skills to critically evaluate care processes and patient progress. We have not found many exemplary studies other than what we are currently doing in Kenya that focus on this theme. A notable example is the ‘Programme for Improving Mental health carE’ (PRIME), funded by the UK government’s Department for International Development (DFID) more than a decade ago, with its focus on two South Asian and three Sub-Saharan African countries. The PRIME consortium’s framework (Bantjes et al., 2018; Jordans et al., 2016) emphasized that effective monitoring must evaluate not only access but also acceptability, quality, and effectiveness—dimensions systematically overlooked in current practice. In this commentary, we are argue that by integrating a ROM system built as an extension of such a framework, teaching hospitals could transform clinical training. Students, researchers, and practitioners of mental health would learn to use real-world data to answer critical questions: Is a treatment not only accessible but also acceptable to the patient and therefore likely to be continued? Is it delivered with sufficient quality to be effective? This hands-on experience with multi-dimensional outcome data is fundamental for cultivating a new generation of practitioners who instinctively use data to guide, evaluate, and personalize care, moving beyond a sole reliance on clinical intuition. This framework also allows for concrete data and practice driven evidence that can help advance policies and investment in mental health systems, which are currently dismal in LMICs in Africa (World Health Organization, 2025).

The current deficit in the availability and functioning of ROM creates three critical system failures: 1) compromised care quality; 2) resource inefficiency, and 3) policy stagnation due to absent outcome data. The continued reliance on paper records exacerbates these problems, rendering therapy progress and comorbidities invisible to decision-makers and ultimately preventing the health system from learning and evolving based on its own operations (Paton and Muinga, 2018).

Advancing routine outcomes monitoring for shaping a learning health system in Kenya

ROM is the systematic and repeated measurement of patient-reported outcomes and clinical data throughout the care process. Unlike episodic data collection, ROM integrates outcomes measurement directly into clinical workflows, creating a continuous loop of assessment, interpretation, and treatment adjustment. In practice, the ROM system follows a continuous cycle. It begins with measurement, where patients regularly complete standardized measures. The scores are entered into a registry or electronic system, which automatically generates visual progress graphs and clinical alerts. The clinician reviews this data for decision-making. Our vision for ROM is radical, we see it not merely as a performance dashboard but as a policy-facing and system-strengthening tool of high practical value.

This systemic approach represents a paradigm shift toward data-driven, patient-centered service delivery. In mental health, ROM offers a dual advantage: it improves clinical outcomes and facilitates system-wide quality improvement. Practice-based evidence has consistently shown that ROM enhances symptom tracking, reduces hospitalizations, and fosters collaborative decision-making (Boswell et al., 2015). Experiences from middle-income countries in Latin America indicate that therapists value outcome data to complement their clinical judgement (Paz et al., 2025), and once this is internalized across mental health systems in the public sphere, a learning health system can be accelerated. Some progress has been reported in Latin American countries regarding the uptake of ROM in mental heatlh services (Fernández-Alvarez et al., 2024; Paz et al., 2025) We are inspired by this momentum and want to advance this through South-South learning in Africa.

At the operational level, ROM systems uncover service delivery bottlenecks, guide resource allocation, and inform strategic planning (Roe et al., 2022); functions that are particularly vital in low-resource contexts. A hospital administrator, for example, could use aggregated ROM data to identify that patients receiving a specific therapy show faster improvement, justifying targeted investments in training more staff in that modality. Importantly, ROM is highly adaptable. It can be implemented using culturally and linguistically appropriate symptom scales or patient-reported outcome measures, making it especially suitable for low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) (Fernández-Alvarez et al., 2024). For Kenya, this adaptability can help tailor care models to local community needs and better justify resource allocations through locally grounded evidence. Ultimately, the integration of robust data and monitoring systems is not just a technical upgrade; it’s a foundational requirement for equitable, accountable, and effective mental healthcare systems (Mutiso et al., 2020). Once a ROM system is in place, clinicians can access timely and responsive insights to inform patient care, enhance practice facilitation, and support practice-led improvements, ensuring that they remain at the center of the care process (Kumar et al., 2025).

Strategic role of teaching and referral hospitals in system transformation

Kenya’s specialized teaching and referral hospitals are uniquely positioned to pioneer the adoption of ROM and drive system-wide innovation. Their potential extends beyond their quadripartite mission of service delivery, training, research, and policy influence to include distinct structural strengths. As central hubs for complex cases, they naturally aggregate rich clinical data. Their role as primary training sites for the next generation of health professionals creates an inherent multiplier effect for new skills and practices. Furthermore, their established connections to national policymaking bodies provide a direct pathway to scale up successful innovations.

However, these inherent strengths are currently undermined by significant operational barriers that also hinder the implementation of systems like ROM.

Implementing ROM is a strategic step to unlocking these hospitals’ potential. By addressing the specific barrier of inadequate infrastructure, ROM directly generates the data needed to tackle broader systemic challenges. For instance, ROM data can provide objective evidence to advocate for increased funding, optimize patient flow to manage excessive loads, and demonstrate the need for specific staff positions. Ultimately, embedding ROM into these central institutions will not only modernize their own operations but also create a replicable model for a data-driven, learning mental health system across Kenya.

To be successful, ROM systems must reflect the realities of LMICs, particularly in terms of workforce capacity, infrastructure limitations, and existing workflows. Effective implementation of this whole-system approach requires three interdependent foundations: culturally adapted measurement tools that reflect Kenya’s specific mental health needs and linguistic diversity; policy integration that embeds ROM indicators within national performance frameworks and budget cycles to ensure institutionalization; and meaningful multi-stakeholder engagement that brings together patients, providers, and policymakers in the co-design process to guarantee both clinical relevance and practical utility. In our work, we are trying to address these levels and recommend that researchers focusing on this theme consider each of these tiers.

To bridge the implementation gap, we propose four strategic priorities that must work in concert: First, co-designing ROM tools with frontline clinicians and patients ensures clinical relevance and minimizes workflow disruption. Second, system integration requires embedding ROM within existing national health information systems, such as the Kenya Health Information System (KHIS), to avoid parallel reporting burdens. Third, sustainable capacity development depends on establishing regional learning collaboratives that enable peer-to-peer skill transfer among providers. Finally, long-term transformation requires incorporating ROM competencies across all mental health training curricula to develop a new cadre of practitioners who are fluent in data-driven care. Together, these interdependent strategies address the technical, human, and systemic dimensions of successful ROM implementation.

Conclusion: moving beyond technical fixes toward system-wide transformation

The future of mental health care in Kenya depends on the strategic transformation of teaching and referral hospitals into learning health systems. This transition requires the establishment of minimum digital standards for outcome tracking, protected time for data reflection, strengthened referral pathways, and the alignment of institutional practices with national indicators and health goals.

The benefits are substantial: greater accountability, real-time learning, improved patient outcomes, and robust research capacity. These institutions can become national exemplars, modeling how evidence from everyday clinical encounters can shape policy, enhance training, and ultimately improve population-level well-being.

Implementing ROM is more than a technical fix; it is a catalyst for health system transformation. In Kenya, ROM can bridge the long-standing divide between policy intent and clinical reality, enabling a shift toward a mental health system that learns and improves with every patient it serves. ROM equips providers with actionable insights, reinforces clinical accountability, and builds a foundation for integrated service delivery and policy innovation.

Specialized mental health institutions, particularly teaching and referral hospitals, are not merely points of service delivery; they are vital hubs of knowledge generation, workforce training, and policy influence. As such, they are uniquely positioned to lead the way in embedding ROM practices into the fabric of mental health care. With strong political will, sustained stakeholder engagement, and a commitment to context-sensitive, inclusive system design, Kenya can set a precedent for other low- and middle-income countries. The path forward is clear. ROM provides the tools. The opportunity is now. The moment demands action.
