The interplay between HPV, vaginal microbiota and host immunity in cervical carcinogenesis
Authors: Nima Afshar Moghaddam, Aria Mohabbat, Maryam Pourdehghan Jigheh, Ametis Mahboubi, Zahra Zenderuh Ravanlo, Dariush Shanehbandi, Somayeh Shokri, Hossein Bannazadeh Baghi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCervical cancer (CC) remains a major global health challenge, largely driven by persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) genotypes. Emerging evidence suggests that the interplay between HPV, the vaginal microbiota (VM), and host immune responses critically influences viral persistence and disease progression. In particular, dysbiotic VM marked by reduced Lactobacillus dominance and increased prevalence of anaerobic bacteria such as Gardnerella, Sneathia, Prevotella, and Atopobium—can impair HPV clearance, promote chronic inflammation, and facilitate progression from cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) to invasive carcinoma. Understanding these tripartite interactions offers opportunities for novel preventive and therapeutic strategies, including microbiome modulation, immunotherapy, and gene editing. This review highlights the potential of microbiome-targeted interventions to enhance immune responses against HPV and reduce the burden of CC, although the underlying causal mechanisms require further investigation in prospective studies.Keywords: Vaginal microbiota, HPV, Immune responses, Cervical cancer, Inflammation

Background

The Human Papillomavirus (HPV) is a leading factor in the development of genital warts and cervical cancer (CC), making it the most common sexually transmitted infection [1]. It is essential to differentiate between low-risk (LR-HPV) genotypes (e.g., HPV-6 and − 11), which typically cause benign genital warts, and high-risk (HR-HPV) genotypes (e.g., HPV-16 and − 18) [2]. HPV is linked to 5% of all cancers worldwide, which includes cervical, anal, penile, vaginal, vulvar, and oropharyngeal cancers. Among these, CC has the most significant impact, resulting in approximately 260,000 deaths each year. Even with the HPV vaccine accessible, the projected effects of CC remains significant over the next 3–5 decades because of inadequate vaccination levels in low to middle-income countries and the absence of proper CC screening on a global scale [3, 4]. Despite initiatives aimed at promoting preventive vaccination of HPV among young females, CC continues to be the fourth most prevalent cancer affecting sexually active women worldwide [5]. Approximately 90% of women can eliminate HPV infections on their own within a timeframe of 6 to 18 months, and there are very few instances of progression from precancerous lesions to invasive CC [6]. Several studies have suggested that the mechanisms of host defense, the composition of the genital microbiome, and various other elements within the female genital tract contribute to the clearance and persistence of HPV, as well as the associated chance of developing CC [7–9].

The innate and acquired immune responses form the essential barrier of host protection at the mucosal surface against infections such as HPV [10]. A variety of research has shown a correlation between genital mucosal cytokine levels and the management or eradication of HPV infection in the CC [8, 11, 12]. It is proposed that the cytokine response initiates within days following the onset of an HPV infection and is later diminished once effective clearance of HPV is accomplished by the appropriate effector cells [6]. The primary reaction to HPV infection involves mature antigen-presenting cells (APCs) that release cytokines and play a role in the activation and recruitment of additional immune cells to the infection site [13]. Following engagement with mature APCs, naïve CD4 + and CD8 + T cells undergo differentiation into distinct T helper effector lineages and cytotoxic T lymphocytes, respectively, which are essential for the efficient elimination of HPV [6]. Nonetheless, these cells do not completely halt the advancement of the disease.

HPV utilizes multiple immune evasion strategies to diminish the antiviral effects of the immune response, promoting immune tolerance to infection. The presence of HPV infection may influence the maturation process of monocytes into fully developed dendritic cells (DCs), while also having a unique impact on the functionality of CD4+/CD8 + T cells and regulatory T cells [14]. The heightened production of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 within the Th2 response has been linked to weakened innate and adaptive immune defenses, as well as the advancement of cervical lesions in the context of high-risk HPV (HR-HPV) infection [6]. While research has shown the antiviral functions of immune cells, the role of cell-mediated mucosal immunity in the incidence, persistence, and clearance of HPV is not well understood.

Recent findings indicate that women exhibiting a low relative abundance of vaginal Lactobacillus species, alongside elevated levels of the Gardnerella, Sneathia, and Atopobium genera, may have a reduced likelihood of clearing HPV infection [9, 15, 16]. Recent meta-analyses have synthesized data from various microscopy and molecular studies, indicating that women possessing Lactobacillus-dominant microbiomes exhibit a lower likelihood of acquiring HPV infections when contrasted with those experiencing an overabundance of bacteria linked to Bacterial vaginosis (BV), such as Gardnerella, Atopobium, and Prevotella [15]. An enriched microbiome featuring Lactobacillus gasseri was associated with accelerated rates of HPV clearance [6]. A dominant microbiome of L.iners has frequently been observed in females diagnosed with cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN). Numerous investigations have indicated a correlation between microbial communities associated with BV, such as Dialister, Prevotella, Streptococcus, Gardnerella, Ureaplasma, Megasphaera, and Mycoplasma, and the persistence of HR-HPV infections, which could lead to the advancement of CIN [15, 17]. Additionally, particular vaginal microbiota (VM) may influence the immune responses of the host, encompassing essential components of anti-tumor and antiviral immunity within the women’s genital tract [18, 19]. Varied microbial populations have been intricately linked to modified innate immune responses and the host’s vulnerability to infections [19–21], and the development of cervical diseases; however, the established link between cause and effect is still ambiguous.

Although there exists proof suggesting a connection between the vaginal microbiome, the immune responses of the host, and HPV infection, the relationship between the vaginal microbiome, host responses, and the advancement to HPV-related cervical cancer is still not well defined. Thus, comprehending the dynamics of the vaginal microbiome and the corresponding immune responses of the host during the various stages of HPV infection may illuminate potential mechanisms linked to the progression of CC. This review outlines the impact of variations in the vaginal microbiome, the involvement of cytokines, and the cellular immune response in the context of infection with, persistence, and clearance (Fig. 1).

Immune response and evasion mechanisms

Females exhibit a heightened susceptibility to HPV, with the majority of infections occurring via heterosexual contact [22]. HPV infections are confined to keratinocytes. The mucosal epithelium serves as the primary barrier against pathogen invasion and orchestrates the initial immune response to HPV [13].

Innate immune response

The innate immune system comprises various cells, including monocytes, mast cells, neutrophils, eosinophils, macrophages, and DCs, which play essential roles in detecting and responding to pathogens. These cells utilize pattern recognition receptors, such as toll-like receptors (TLRs), nucleotide oligomerization domain (NOD)-like receptors (NLRs), and retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I)-like receptors (RLRs), to initiate protective mechanisms against invaders [23]. APCs, including Langerhans cells, macrophages, and DCs, are crucial for bridging the innate and adaptive immune systems [24, 25]. Langerhans cells are the sole APCs capable of interacting with HPV proteins in the outermost epithelial layer [26]. The transfection of DCs by HPV results in alterations to their migratory behavior and stimulates the production of cytokines, potentially dampening the immune response to viral antigens [27]. Macrophages are essential in eliminating HPV infections by utilizing mechanisms that involve the production of tumor necrosis factor-alpha and nitric oxide [28, 29]. NK cells are crucial for eliminating viral infections, the removal of cells infected with HPV, and the prevention of cancer in the context of HPV-related carcinogenesis [30]. While the relationship between neutrophils and CC remains less understood, recent findings indicate that elevated levels of neutrophils are significantly linked to unfavorable outcomes and advancement of the disease in women diagnosed with CC [31] (Fig. 2).

Adaptive immune response

The adaptive immune response serves as an additional layer of defense for the host against invading pathogens. The immune response is categorized into two distinct pathways: the T-helper 1 (Th1) cell responses, which enhance cell-mediated immunity, and the Th2 cell responses, which facilitate humoral immunity [13]. In the course of HPV infection, the antigen is internalized by DCs through phagocytosis and subsequently migrates to lymphoid tissues, where it activates adaptive immunity through the expression of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-12 [10]. T lymphocytes (CD4+) identify HPV antigens presented by mature antigen-presenting cells and subsequently undergo processes of proliferation and differentiation. They also process the antigen into shorter peptide fragments or differentiate into effector T cells, which engage [6] with naive B cells and secrete cytokines that facilitate the maturation of B cell responses [32]. An effective antiviral immune response requires the activation of Th1 cells, which produce cytokines such as IL-12, IL-2, and interferon Gamma (IFN-γ). The role of these cytokines is essential in stimulating various immune cells, including naïve CD8 + T-lymphocytes. (Subsequently, these cells differentiate into CTL and evolve into effector T-cells capable of targeting and eliminating cells infected with CIN or HPV [10]. CD8 + CTL, CD4 + T cells, and other Th1 responses have been linked to the successful elimination of HPV16 and HPV18 [33], the absence of these T lymphocyte lineages has been linked to ongoing HPV infection and the progression to high-grade disease [6]. Additionally, Th2 cells secrete cytokines including IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10, which facilitate the differentiation of B cells into plasma cells that generate HPV-specific antibodies for circulation [24]. Memory B and T cells specific to HPV undergo transformation, migrating to the bone marrow where they persist as long-lived memory cells. Upon reinfection, these cells can differentiate into plasma cells or activated T cells (Fig. 3) [34]. Conversely, earlier research suggested that women with high-grade HPV-positive cervical lesions exhibited an elevated IL-17-associated (Th17) response, which is recognized as pro-tumorigenic in the context of HPV-related cancers [6]. The activation and recruitment of Th17 cells are facilitated by stromal tumor-associated fibroblasts and tumor-derived chemokines, specifically CXCR3 [35]. When considered collectively, these observations indicate that not all components of the innate and adaptive immune systems possess the ability to migrate to infection sites for the purpose of eliminating HPV-infected cells; in fact, some may inadvertently facilitate the advancement of cervical lesions into invasive cervical cancer.

The strategies employed by HPV to avoid host immune responses

HPV life cycle and immune recognition

The HPV life cycle is intrinsically linked to epithelial differentiation [36]. It establishes persistent infection by initially infecting basal epithelial cells exposed through micro wounds [37]. Viral entry into the nucleus occurs during cell division when the nuclear envelope breaks down. In these undifferentiated cells, the viral genome is amplified and maintained as a stable episome. Epithelial differentiation then triggers the productive phase of the cycle, prompting massive amplification of the viral genome [38–40]. This phase includes the expression of late genes, such as the L1 and L2 capsid proteins, for virion assembly. Mature viral particles are eventually released from the uppermost layers of the epithelium [41, 42]. Due to its limited coding capacity, HPV relies on cellular factors and must reactivate the cell cycle in differentiating cells. It achieves this primarily through the E6 and E7 oncoproteins, which degrade p53 and pRb, thereby creating a replication-conducive environment [43, 44].

Modulation of innate immunity

The IFN response acts as the body’s initial defense mechanism against viral infections. This family of proteins is divided into three types: Type I (e.g., IFN-α, β, κ), Type II (e.g., IFN-γ), and Type III (IFN-λ1–4) [45]. Primarily, Type I and III IFNs are activated when pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) detect viral nucleic acids known as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs). These PRRs include TLRs, RLRs, cGAS, and IFI16 [46, 47]. TLRs recognize distinct forms of viral RNA or DNA in endosomes, while RIG-I and MDA5 (Melanoma Differentiation-Associated protein 5) identifies long double-stranded viral RNA in the cytoplasm, signaling through MAVS (Mitochondrial Antiviral-Signaling protein). cGAS (cyclic GMP-AMP synthase) is a cytosolic sensor that detects viral DNA and produces signaling molecules to activate the STING pathway, leading to immune activation [48, 49]. Both the cGAS-STING and RLR-MAVS pathways activate TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1), which phosphorylates transcription factors IRF-3 and IRF-7, triggering the creation of Type I and III IFNs [50]. Once secreted, IFNs bind to specific receptors, activating the JAK/STAT signaling pathway and inducing the expression of hundreds of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) that help clear the virus [51]. Keratinocytes, the cells targeted by HPV, also express these PRRs and can initiate an IFN-mediated immune response, playing a crucial role in frontline antiviral defense [52].

Subversion of adaptive immunity

The body’s adaptive immune system, particularly CTLs that target HPV16 proteins, is a key defense for clearing the virus. However, HPV16 employs several strategies to evade this immune response. It avoids triggering inflammation and immune recognition by not killing its host cells during replication. Furthermore, its E6 and E7 proteins suppress IFN signaling [53].

The E6 and E7 proteins produced by HPV16 suppress the host cells’ production of type-1 IFNs. As a result of the suppression, during an immune response, the necessary co-stimulatory signals from cytokines like IFNs are absent, which can lead to immune tolerance instead of an effective attack [54]. In addition, the HPV16 E5 protein decreases the expression of HLA-class I molecules, helping infected cells avoid detection and destruction by CTLs [54]. Together, these evasion strategies allow the virus to establish a persistent infection, which can ultimately result in the development of CC [55]. This combination of stealth tactics can lead to immune tolerance, allowing the infection to persist. Ultimately, this persistent infection is what can lead to the development of CC [56].

Role of viral oncoproteins in immune evasion (E6, E7, E5)

Ongoing infection with HR-HPV results in the incorporation of viral DNA into the host cell’s genome, accompanied by the increased expression of the viral oncogenes E5, E6, and E7 [57]. The E5 product encourages cell fusion, leading to aneuploidy, and enhances the transformative effects of E6 and E7, as well as the virus’s ability to evade the immune system [58]. Furthermore, E5 reduces the expression of class I major histocompatibility complex and triggers inflammation by elevating cyclooxygenase 2 (COX2) expression, thereby increasing prostaglandin secretion and its receptors [59]. The E6 and E7 gene products disrupt the functions of p53 and intracellular pRb, leading to a breakdown in cell cycle regulation and DNA repair mechanisms. This disruption promotes the accumulation of mutations and heightens the risk of neoplastic transformation [60–62]. E6 stimulates telomerase activity, thereby inhibiting senescence and cell death and immortalizing the cell [63]. E7 promotes IL1β expression, which subsequently activates NF-κB, an essential regulator of the progression from chronic inflammation to cancer [63–65]. E6 and E7 continue to show a relationship with the production of the matrix metalloproteinase enzyme, which plays a role in remodeling the vascular system, aiding in diapedesis and angiogenesis [66]. Patients with ICC exhibited elevated levels of TGF-β and IL-6, −10, −17, and − 23, alongside reduced levels of IFN-γ, compared with healthy females, indicating a suppression of the immune response in those with ICC [67–69].

Cervix and vaginal microbiome

A balanced vaginal microbiome

The Human Microbiome Project has dramatically enhanced our understanding of the complex relationship between the human microbiome and overall health; nonetheless, the vaginal microbiome has long been recognized as crucial to maintaining vaginal health. BV is a notable change in the vaginal microbial ecosystem that has been linked to adverse clinical consequences, such as premature delivery and pelvic inflammatory conditions [70–72].BV is defined by the absence of significant populations of large gram-positive rods, specifically Lactobacillus, alongside a predominance of gram-variable cocci and curved rod-shaped bacteria [73]. Lactobacillus has been recognized for its significant contribution to maintaining the health of vagina. One of its essential functions is to sustain a somewhat acidic milieu and synthesize protective proteins that serve as the initial barrier against pathogens [3]. Lactobacillus employs two distinct mechanisms to safeguard against pathogenic organisms. Lactobacillus forms biofilms and protective barriers by adhering to mucus, thereby competing with and inhibiting pathogen colonization. Additionally, it produces protective antimicrobial agents such as bacteriocin-like substances, biosurfactants, hydrogen peroxide, and lactic acid. Therefore, a healthy vaginal microbiome is characterized by a predominance of Lactobacillus species [3].

Ravel et al. [74] identified five distinct community state types (CSTs), characterized by the dominant bacterial species, four of which are Lactobacilli: L. crispatus, L. iners, L. gasseri, and L. jensenii. The fifth was centered on the reduction of Lactobacilli species and the prevalence of a diverse array of anaerobic microorganisms, commonly described as a non-Lactobacillus dominated condition and/or scenarios characterized by high diversity. This condition corresponds to clinical BV. It is noteworthy that a highly diverse gut microbiome is linked to overall health, whereas a lower diversity in the vaginal microbiome is considered indicative of better health. Nonetheless, the narrative surrounding Lactobacillus species is intricate, as only one in four women appears to possess a beneficial environment characterized by L. crispatus-dominated microbiomes, while as much as a quarter exhibit a microbiome that is not dominated by Lactobacillus. Another complicating aspect of these studies is the significant variability in microbiome composition observed throughout the menstrual cycle [74, 75]. In certain women, the dominance of Lactobacillus may diminish during menstruation, while in others, it remains relatively stable throughout this period. Additionally, some individuals may experience fluctuations on a daily basis [76]. Various environmental influences, including the use of antibiotics, nicotine consumption, pregnancy, breastfeeding, and vaginal douching, contraceptive methods, co-infections, and recent sexual activity, are linked to conditions that result in a temporary reduction of Lactobacillus levels [3]. The clinical ramifications of this temporary loss remain ambiguous.

The vaginal microbiome and its association with inflammatory processes

Research conducted both in living organisms and in controlled laboratory settings has demonstrated links between clinical BV and the bacteria associated with it, highlighting their roles in both immune activation and immune suppression [3]. Anahtar et al. [19] demonstrated that bacteria associated with BV exhibited a strong correlation with pro-inflammatory cytokines derived from cervical samples, on the other hand Novak et al. [77] demonstrated that the levels of Secretory Leukocyte Protease Inhibitor (SLPI), a crucial protective innate protein, were reduced in females diagnosed with BV. Certain studies indicate a correlation between L. iners and Gardnerella vaginalis with pro-inflammatory conditions, while L. gasseri, L. jensenii, and L. crispatus tend to be linked with relatively non-inflammatory states [78]. An investigation revealed that elevated levels of cytokines in cervical fluids correlate with indicators of compromised epithelial barriers, potentially due to the release of proteases that may lead to tissue damage and remodeling [79]. A different research team noted that communities dominated by G. vaginalis, which is frequently associated with BV, exhibited proteomic profiles associated with immune activation, apoptosis, compromised epithelial integrity, and processes related to scar healing [78]. This tissue may indeed be susceptible to HPV infection, as the virus necessitates entry into basal cells, typically facilitated by inflammation or injury [3].

VM-HPV interaction in HSIL/SCC development

Similar to findings at various mucosal locations, the vaginal microbiome possesses the ability to produce bacterial genotoxins, which may result in long-term inflammation. This inflammatory environment may promote cancer development by causing cellular and DNA damage [80]. A multitude of research efforts have revealed connections between HPV or squamous intra-epithelial lesions (SIL) and specific bacterial species; however, the findings frequently exhibit variability and inconsistency [9, 81, 82]. Generally, women exhibiting persistent HPV and high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSIL) tend to have a microbiome that is not dominated by Lactobacillus and shows increased bacterial diversity. Among bacterial species, the most reliably linked to HPV and HSIL are G. vaginalis, Sneathia, Megasphaera, and Prevotella [15]. Norenhag et al. [15] demonstrated that L. iners, in contrast to L. crispatus, exhibited a heightened risk associated with the occurrence of HPV (OR 2.11; 95% CI 1.28, 3.46) and SIL (OR 1.95; 95% CI 1.07, 3.56). In comparison, L. gasseri was solely linked to HPV (OR 3.3; 95% CI 1.35, 8.04). Various species, including Atopium vaginae, Clostridium, L. reteri, Fusobacteria, mycoplasma, and Ureaplasma pavum, have been identified in association with HPV and SIL in cross-sectional studies, though such associations are infrequently consistent.

Research involving young women diagnosed with CIN 2 indicated that the natural regression of untreated CIN 2 occurred more slowly in those with non-Lactobacillus-dominated microbiomes [83]. This research indicates that women who did not experience regression by the 12-month mark possessed microbiomes characterized by high diversity, with notable enrichment of Prevotella, Megasphaera, BV-associated bacteria (BVAB) 1, Sneathia, and Atopobium species. Through the application of Sankey diagrams, the analysis revealed no notable difference in VM between the sample taken just before and the one taken just after regression (n = 63). This indicates that CIN 2 is not a driving factor for VM; instead, VM may be a risk factor for CIN progression.

A further investigation that bolsters this assertion involved analyzing women prior to and following excisional treatment for CIN [84]. This investigation involving 103 women revealed that those with CIN exhibited a higher prevalence of a vaginal microbiome characterized by a high diversity of Lactobacillus species, alongside elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and antimicrobial peptides (AMPs). The localized surgical removal of CIN resulted in decreased concentrations of AMPs, yet it did not affect the composition of the VM or the levels of cytokines. This suggests that women who experience CIN possess an intrinsic susceptibility to a diverse pro-inflammatory milieu that remains unaddressed even after surgical removal of the condition and clearance of HPV. The inability to restore a Lactobacillus-dominant community state type might clarify the persistently increased risk of both pre-invasive and invasive disease recurrence in women. Although the overall structure of the microbiome community remained stable in women before and after treatment, notable alterations in specific bacterial taxa were detected when comparing the samples from pre- and post-treatment to those of untreated, healthy controls. Sneathia has been repeatedly identified as a potential microbiological indicator of HPV infection and advanced disease conditions. Within this group, Sneathia amnii, which was previously excessively present prior to removal, showed no significant overexpression post-treatment when compared to controls. This observation reinforces the hypothesis that the proliferation of Sneathia amnii in women with HPV and CIN is a result of the underlying disease process [3]. Prevotella bivia levels were consistently higher both prior to and following treatment when compared to control groups, accompanied by a notable deficiency of Lactobacillus spp. Prevotella bivia has been proposed as an initial colonizer in BV and in high-diversity models associated with biofilm development. Prevotella bivia is believed to facilitate the colonization of secondary species, including Atopobium and Sneathia [85]. This outlines a credible mechanism that accounts for the heightened chance of subsequent HPV infection, recurrence of CIN, and the progression to CC in females who have undergone local treatment for CIN. While the function of the VM in the progression of cancer is not completely comprehended, its involvement is certainly conceivable. Chronic inflammation is recognized for its role in the production of nitrosamines, which are established carcinogens [86–88]. The generation of microbial toxins may result in either the inhibition or enhancement of cellular signaling pathways. Ilhan et al. [89] showed that metabolomic profiles associated with cancers were also linked to HPV infections, cervical inflammation, and the local microbiome, highlighting the intricate interactions among these elements.

While temporary inflammation is probably crucial for eliminating viruses, a persistent pro-inflammatory condition leads to tissue injury and the accumulation of immune cells that subsequently produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) and other possible carcinogens. This process likely increases the oncogenic potential of HPV by hastening DNA and cellular damage. Oxidative stress (OS) happens when ROS are produced and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) surpass the body’s ability to effectively neutralize pathogens or mitigate toxic environments, primarily due to the availability of antioxidants. ROS are typically produced in aerobic cells as a result of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, as well as through microsomal and peroxisomal oxidation processes. ROS and RNS are generated as a consequence of inflammation, infections, and immune responses pertinent to this topic [90]. The vaginal microbiome, characterized by its aerobic nature, suggests a regular creation of ROS and RNS. Due to their established toxic effects [91], they pose a potential carcinogenic factors if not regulated. OS is linked to more than 100 DNA damage events and a variety of protein structural alterations. The harmful activities encompass alterations in the functionality of cell membrane lipids, modifications in gene expression responses, and disruptions in the cellular redox balance. Their toxic properties necessitate oversight by antioxidant and scavenging mechanisms. It is proposed that OS works in conjunction with the transforming abilities of HR-HPV, leading to an increased efficacy in cancer progression, or it may engage with HPV to either enhance or inhibit its intrinsic molecular processes [92, 93]. As mentioned above by Moscicki et al. [94], the cytokine surge noted right after the clearance of HPV 16 was linked to a rise in states dominated by non-Lactobacillus species, along with a rise in the variety of bacteria. The research investigated the microbiome and inflammatory markers across multiple visits, encompassing pre-acquisition, persistence, immediate post-clearance, and several months thereafter. This approach allowed for an analysis of both predisposing factors and their subsequent effects. The states dominated by Lactobacillus and exhibiting reduced diversity reverted to a baseline condition (similar to the pre-acquisition state) during the follow-up visit multiple months after clearance, which corresponded with a decline in cytokine levels as well. Upon investigation of bacterial species L. crispatus and L. gasseri, a notable increase was observed during periods of persistence, followed by significant declines immediately after clearance. In contrast, G. vaginalis exhibited a rise right after clearance, eventually returning to baseline levels after several months. The research additionally identified a significant association between G. vaginalis and elevated cytokine levels, indicating that G. vaginalis may be partially responsible for this inflammatory response. This phenomenon may have arisen from a transition from a balanced anti-microbial environment to one that favors anti-viral responses, leading to a diminished control over bacterial populations and allowing non-Lactobacillus species, such as G. vaginalis, to prevail. While G. vaginalis and increased diversity within the vaginal microbiome have been regarded as more pathogenic, they may have significantly contributed to the inflammatory response and subsequent clearance processes. Another study identified a correlation between G. vaginalis and the process of clearance [95]. The role of G. vaginalis in driving inflammation remains a subject of debate.

Relationship between the vaginal microbiome and HPV infection

The vaginal and ectocervical environments are primarily inhabited by bacteria that produce lactic acid, alongside cervicovaginal fluids that serve as a lubricant, effectively capturing and neutralizing invading pathogens [96]. The presence of Lactobacillus spp. in the genital microbiome is linked to favorable pregnancy results, absence of unusual vaginal symptoms and urogenital disorders, as well as a decreased likelihood of various sexually transmitted infections, such as HPV and HIV [97]. Conversely, a different scenario is evident in the genital tract, which is characterized by a predominance of suboptimal VM. Several Lactobacillus species have been identified in the non-BV condition, with L. crispatus, L. gasseri, and L. jensenii being the most prevalent and abundant [98, 99]. The microbiome enriched with L. gasseri was associated with accelerated HPV clearance [100]. A recent investigation revealed that women possessing a microbiome enriched with L. crispatus exhibited a lower likelihood of having prevalent HR-HPV infections compared to those with an overabundance of pathogenic microorganisms associated with BV, including Gardnerella, Atopobium, and Prevotella. Additionally, the presence of L. crispatus in the genital tract was linked to the clearance of HPV, indicating a potential relationship between the two factors [6]. BV is a condition marked by a transition in the vaginal microbiome, where there is a reduction in Lactobacillus dominance and an increase in a variety of other bacteria. This includes both strict and facultative anaerobes like Sneathia, Prevotella, and Gardnerella, frequently leading to symptoms such as vaginitis and abnormal discharge [6]. BV has been linked to heightened stages of various cytokines, including IL-1a, IL-1b, IL-7, IL-18, MIP-1a, MIP-1b, IL-8, MIF, TNF-a, and TNF-b, as well as cellular biomarkers such as CCR5 + CD4+ T cells. These factors are indicative of inflammation and are linked to a higher likelihood of acquiring HIV [101, 102]. Additionally, various research efforts have indicated a correlation between BV and other sexually transmitted infections, such as Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Chlamydia trachomatis, and cervical HPV [103–105]. The inflammatory and ulcerative consequences resulting from sexually transmitted infections and/or BV present biologically credible pathways that may enhance the vulnerability to human papillomavirus in individuals with co-infections. A significant association between Gardnerella and Atopobium vaginae and CIN has been reported [106, 107]. The elevated presence of Sneathia, Atopobium, and Gardnerella correlates with the occurrence of HR-HPV infections (Fig. 2) [108, 109]. A research project carried out by Lee et al. [110] showed that women infected with HPV displayed reduced levels of Lactobacillus species, alongside an increase in Fusobacteria and Sneathia when compared to their HPV-uninfected counterparts. Consistent with these observations, a longitudinal study involving 32 sexually active females indicated that a diminished Lactobacillus community, characterized by elevated levels of Atopobium species, correlated with a reduced rate of HPV clearance [111]. Although current research indicates a link between genital dysbiosis and HPV infection, there remain valid concerns that these positive associations may be a result of unaccounted confounding factors related to sexual risk behaviors, including engaging in unprotected sex and sexual activity with uncircumcised partners [112]. Therefore, additional studies exploring this interaction are necessary. A comprehensive analysis of the genital microbial composition and architecture in females affected by HPV infection could elucidate the causal relationships linking microbiota, HPV infection, and the development of cervical cancer.

The link between genital inflammation and HPV

Sources of genital inflammation

Chronic inflammation over an extended period, along with a continuous HPV infection, may contribute to the onset of ICC. This process involves reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, various cytokines and chemokines, factors that promote cell growth and survival, enzymes such as COX and metalloproteinases, prostaglandins, and certain microRNA [113].

HPV-associated cancers, particularly CC, are primarily driven by the direct actions of HPV oncogenes. These oncogenes can also stimulate cancer indirectly by inducing chronic inflammation, which itself is a consequence of their activity. This persistent inflammation causes repeated tissue injury and mutations in critical tumor suppressor genes. Key viral proteins E5, E6, and E7 promote this environment by upregulating the COX2 and prostaglandin (PG) E2 pathway, a significant source of inflammation, and by increasing pro-inflammatory cytokines. Furthermore, factors like microRNAs and aging can amplify this inflammatory response, accelerating the progression from infection to cancer. This review evaluates the precise mechanisms by which HPV oncogenes and other factors cause inflammation to facilitate CC development [114].

HPV-induced inflammation and cervical carcinogenesis

Chronic inflammation helps tumors evade immune attack by altering immune cell function. Within tumors, prevalent myeloid cells trigger a response that promotes immunosuppressive tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), characterized by high ARG1 expression [115]. Chronic inflammation severely depletes the amino acid L-arginine. This deficiency during cancer leads to a dysfunctional immune response, inhibiting T-cells and promoting immunosuppressive cells. Consequently, high expression of a dysfunctional ARG1 enzyme is linked to worse patient survival across cancers. It fosters an immunosuppressive environment by driving the development of suppressive immune cells [116, 117].

Clinical implications: inflammation as a biomarker and therapeutic target

Furthermore, a clinical focus on the concentration of the inflammatory factors in the cervical tissue can also point to a possible infection with HR-HPV genotypes [118]. For instance, expression patterns of five genes (CXCL8, CXCL10, CX3CR1, FCGR3B, and SELL) along with other immune-related cells such as, macrophages, CD4 + T cells, and mast cells appear to differ significantly between normal and cancerous tissue [119]. Another possible diagnostic options could be the expression levels of ARG2, HSP90AA1, EZH2, ICAM1, macrophage M1, and activated CD4 memory T cells [120]. Four predictors (HSP90AA1, EZH2, ICAM1, and macrophage M1) tend to increase significantly. In contrast, ARG2 levels steadily declined, suggesting it may have a protective role [120].

Conclusion

In summary, the complex interactions among HPV, the vaginal microbiome, and the immune responses of the host play a pivotal role in shaping the progression of CC. HR-HPV genotypes circumvent both innate and adaptive immune responses via oncoproteins such as E5, E6, and E7. These proteins inhibit IFN signaling, promote immune tolerance, and contribute to chronic inflammation, which is further intensified by dysbiotic microbiomes characterized by diminished Lactobacillus prevalence and an increase in taxa such as Gardnerella, Sneathia, and Prevotella. Alterations in microbial composition hinder HPV elimination, promote its persistence, and facilitate the transition from CIN to invasive carcinoma through mechanisms including dysregulation of cytokines, OS, and disruption of the epithelial barrier. Prophylactic vaccination, molecular screening, and therapies that modulate the microbiome offer promising avenues for prevention and intervention. However, it is crucial to conduct additional longitudinal studies to clarify causal relationships and create targeted strategies, thereby progressing toward the goal of eliminating CC.
