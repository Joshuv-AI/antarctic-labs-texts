Donation After Circulatory Death in Pediatric Heart Transplantation
Authors: Sarah Dudley Grorud, Michael D. Porter, Taufiek Konrad Rajab, R. Jerome Dixon, Michael McCulloch, Firezer Haregu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeDonation after circulatory death (DCD) is an emerging heart transplantation (HT) strategy with improved waitlist and comparable post‐transplant outcomes to donation after brain death (DBD) in adults. Pediatric DCD‐HT is underutilized but gaining broader adoption. We assessed the impact of DCD listing on waitlist outcomes and graft survival in pediatric HT.MethodsWe queried the OPTN (Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network)/UNOS (United Network for Organ Sharing) database for all pediatric primary isolated HT candidates between January 2021 and June 2024. Waitlist outcomes were compared by final listing type to account for crossovers. Offer dynamics were assessed by average interval between offers stratified by OPTN/UNOS status. Multivariable regression modeled offer frequency as a function of DCD listing. We compared 30‐day survival between recipients of DCD and DBD organs.ResultsAmong 2486 candidates, 86 were initially listed eligible for DCD and 2400 for DBD. DCD candidates had higher clinical acuity than DBD candidates. DCD listing was associated with significantly shorter intervals between offers across all statuses and increased offer rate by 134% (95% CI: 92%–186%). Waitlist outcomes did not differ significantly by final listing type. There was no difference in 30‐day survival between DCD and DBD recipients.ConclusionsDCD listing in pediatric HT is associated with a shorter interval between offers and more frequent offers. Waitlist survival was similar between groups despite DCD candidates being sicker at listing. There was no difference in 30‐day survival between DCD and DBD recipients. These findings suggest that broader adoption of pediatric DCD‐HT can expand access to donor hearts without compromising early post‐transplant outcomes.

Introduction

Waitlist mortality remains a major challenge for children with end‐stage heart failure awaiting heart transplantation (HT). Twenty years ago, approximately one in five children died on the waitlist or were removed due to clinical deterioration [1]. Although outcomes have improved, current analyses estimate 10%–13% of children on the heart transplant waitlist in the United States die or are delisted for clinical decline [1, 2]. The 2016 revision to the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) heart allocation policy intended to prioritize the sickest candidates [3, 4], yet it has not achieved the desired effect of reducing waitlist mortality, and multifactorial contributors to this issue persist [1, 2].

Children with congenital heart disease now account for 61% of waitlist candidates and are at higher risk for waitlist morality compared to those with cardiomyopathy [2, 5]. Infants under 1 year of age comprise 21% of candidates and experience the highest mortality rate among all age groups. Beyond patient factors, institutional variability in organ offer acceptance practices contributes to prolonged wait times and increased mortality [2, 6, 7, 8]. However, the persistent imbalance between available donors and listed candidates remains the primary barrier influencing waitlist outcomes. In 2023, 1248 pediatric candidates were on the waitlist while only 507 transplants were performed [5]. Expanding the donor pool through donation after circulatory death (DCD), which involves donors whose declaration of death is based on irreversible cessation of circulatory function rather than brain death, has the potential to address this disparity by reducing waitlist time and improving access to transplantation [9, 10].

In adult HT, DCD has demonstrated substantial clinical impact. A recent analysis of the OPTN (Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network) database demonstrates 33% of adults listed for HT are DCD candidates [11]. In 2023, 14% of adult heart transplants utilized DCD donors, a substantial increase from only 0.2% in 2019 [5]. Moreover, multiple adult studies have shown DCD listing and transplantation increases the likelihood of transplantation, and decreases waitlist duration and mortality while maintaining similar post‐transplant outcomes [12, 13, 14]. Despite increasing utilization in adults, DCD‐HT remains rare in the pediatric population [15].

Widespread implementation of DCD‐HT in pediatric patients is limited by unique technological, logistical, and ethical challenges [16, 17, 18]. The TransMedics Organ Care System, used for ex vivo heart perfusion and functional assessment (direct procurement and perfusion), is not suitable for donors weighing less than 40 kg, limiting its use in smaller donors [19]. Normothermic regional perfusion (NRP) offers a potential alternative by restoring in vivo perfusion of thoracoabdominal organs following circulatory death via cardiopulmonary bypass. However, NRP requires significant institutional resources and raises ethical concerns regarding potential violation of the dead donor rule, limiting its broad adoption [17]. Despite these challenges, there has been increased utilization of DCD‐HT in pediatric patients over the last several years [20, 21, 22]. Given this rising adoption, we analyzed the OPTN database to assess the impact of DCD listing on pediatric waitlist and post‐transplant outcomes.

Methods

Data Source

This study utilized data from the OPTN. The OPTN data system includes data on all donors, waitlisted candidates, and transplant recipients in the U.S., submitted by the members of the OPTN. The Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA), U.S. Department of Health and Human Services provides oversight to the activities of the OPTN contractor. The data reported here have been supplied by the UNOS as the contractor for OPTN. The interpretation and reporting of these data are the responsibility of the authors and in no way should be seen as an official policy of or interpretation by the OPTN or the U.S. Government. This study was approved by the institutional review board of the University of Virginia (IRB #21814).

Study Population & Data Collection

We identified patients listed for primary isolated HT between January 1, 2021 and June 30, 2024, who were younger than 18 years at listing. Demographic and clinical characteristics at listing were compared between candidates initially listed eligible for DCD versus donation after brain death (DBD). Waitlist outcomes were compared by final listing type to account for crossovers, including the proportion of candidates who underwent transplantation, were delisted for improvement, died on the waitlist, were delisted for clinical deterioration, or remained active. Candidates removed for transfer to another center, who refused transplant, were unable to be contacted, or were removed in error were classified as other.

The average interval between organ offers was compared between groups by OPTN/UNOS listing status (1A, 1B, 2). Sample sizes were weighted to reflect the proportion of total waitlist time each candidate spent in each status and listing type (DCD vs. DBD). Five patients were only listed as temporarily inactive (Status 7) so were excluded from this analysis. To evaluate the association between DCD listing and organ offer frequency, we performed multivariable negative binomial regression adjusted for status, blood type, weight, and listing center.

Post‐transplant outcomes were assessed among patients who underwent transplantation by June 30, 2024. Those without 30‐day post‐transplant follow‐up were excluded. Post‐transplant survival was compared between DCD candidates who received DCD versus DBD donor hearts to account for potential selection effects related to listing strategy.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were compared using chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests, and continuous variables were analyzed with the Mann–Whitney U test. Waitlist outcomes were compared by chi‐square tests. Survival analysis was performed using Kaplan–Meier estimates, and group comparisons were made using the log‐rank test.

Results

DCD Transplant Centers

Only 12 of 69 centers listed at least one DCD candidate during the study period. During the study period, the median transplant volume of centers that listed DCD candidates (38.5, IQR 31.8–45.5) was significantly greater than the median transplant volume of centers that did not list DCD candidates (13.0, IQR 3.0–31.0, p = 0.001). Only 5 centers performed at least one DCD heart transplant during the study period.

Discussion

This study offers important insights on the impact of DCD in pediatric HT. Using the largest national transplant registry, we found that DCD eligible candidates experienced a 134% increase in expected offer frequency and significantly shorter intervals between offers across all statuses compared to candidates listed only for DBD. Despite greater illness severity for DCD eligible candidates at the time of listing, waitlist survival was similar to that of DBD candidates. This pattern suggests that increased offer frequency may help offset the higher baseline clinical risk in DCD eligible candidates by effectively expanding the donor pool. These findings are consistent with adult literature demonstrating improved waitlist outcomes for DCD eligible heart transplant candidates [11, 12, 13, 14, 18]. Importantly, we did not observe a difference in 30‐day post‐transplant survival between recipients of DCD and DBD hearts, indicating that potential waitlist benefits are not offset by early graft dysfunction related to DCD organ procurement. Notably, this was observed even though recipients of DCD hearts were younger and smaller, both known risk factors for early post‐transplant mortality [18, 23, 24].

A recently published analysis using the same dataset reported no waitlist survival benefit from DCD listing among pediatric Status 1A candidates, with benefits limited to Status 1B and 2 candidates [25]. However, measuring waitlist survival alone can obscure the impact of DCD listing by not accounting for important factors such as changes in status, crossover between listing strategies, and center‐level organ acceptance practices. In contrast, we evaluated access to organs by analyzing offer dynamics and found the interval between organ offers declined substantially across all listing statuses, including a more than 50% decrease for candidates listed Status 1A. This indicates that a DCD eligible listing strategy meaningfully increases access to organs, even for the highest‐acuity patients.

A recent single center, retrospective case–control study of nine DCD‐HT recipients found significantly shorter waitlist times for DCD recipients compared to matched DBD recipients [22]. Post‐transplant outcomes were similar between groups, including rates of primary graft dysfunction, treated rejection, 1 year graft function, and 1 year survival. Notably, 89% of patients in each cohort were Status 1A, supporting the potential of DCD eligible listing to improve access without compromising early post‐transplant outcomes in the sickest children.

During our study period, only 3.5% of candidates were initially listed eligible for DCD‐HT. As this listing practice gains wider adoption, offer frequency per candidate may diminish to some degree. However, as demonstrated in the adult population, DCD has the potential to expand the donor pool considerably [9, 11, 16]. Currently, DCD listing and transplantation in pediatric patients is concentrated in a small number of high‐volume centers, underscoring the resource and operational constraints that must be addressed to broaden access nationally.

Remaining barriers to wider adoption of pediatric DCD heart transplant are being met with promising solutions. Early reports have demonstrated the feasibility of NRP in pediatric donors [26, 27]. Practical hurdles remain, particularly for smaller donors, including the need for two perfusion teams (for donor and recipient), with associated personnel, financial, and equipment demands. Ongoing advances in perfusion platforms, pediatric‐specific circuits, and streamlined team models are likely to reduce these constraints over time. Moreover, a promising recent surgical adaptation for organ procurement in children describes on‐table reanimation, which has the potential to ameliorate ethical concerns surrounding in vivo reanimation through NRP [28].

Limitations

The retrospective registry design introduces the potential for missing or inaccurately coded variables, and for residual unmeasured confounding variables that may affect outcomes. The registry also lacks granular donor and procurement data relevant to DCD, such as functional warm ischemia time and perfusion specifications, which can influence results. Additionally, DCD listing and transplantation were concentrated in a small number of high‐volume centers, limiting generalizability to programs with less DCD experience and fewer resources. Finally, the small cohort reflects the early stage of this practice in pediatrics. Similarly, the short follow‐up period, reflective of recent growth in DCD adoption, limits assessment of intermediate to long‐term outcomes.

Conclusions

Listing pediatric candidates for DCD hearts was associated with significantly more offers and shorter intervals between offers across all statuses, while waitlist survival remained comparable to DBD listed candidates despite greater baseline clinical acuity in the DCD group. Early post‐transplant outcomes were similar between DCD and DBD recipients, even though DCD recipients were younger and smaller. These findings support the broader adoption of DCD‐HT in pediatrics to expand access and improve waitlist outcomes without compromising early post‐transplant outcomes. Future work should explore expanded procurement options specifically designed for small children, assess donor and offer level data that can impact access and outcomes, and evaluate long‐term outcomes to inform policy and widespread programmatic development.
