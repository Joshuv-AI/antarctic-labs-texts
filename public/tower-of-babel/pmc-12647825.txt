LCRMP-1 induces tumor angiogenesis by transcriptionally upregulating SERPINE1 in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Yuan-Ling Hsu, Pei-Fang Hung, Chi-Chung Wang, Yih-Leong Chang, Chen-Tu Wu, Pei-Shan Wu, Xuan-Ren Chen, Tse-Ming Hong, Pan-Chyr Yang, Szu-Hua Pan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Angiogenesis is a crucial process in cancer progression, particularly in malignant tumors, as it facilitates tumor growth and metastasis by generating new blood vessels that supply vital nutrients and oxygen. However, the regulatory mechanism governing the development of these tumor neo-vessels by endothelial cells is still not fully understood. This study investigates the role of long-form collapsin response mediator protein-1 (LCRMP-1) in angiogenesis within non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Through in vitro tube formation, in vivo plaque angiogenesis assays, and immunohistochemical staining, we identify SERPINE1 as a crucial factor regulated by LCRMP-1. Mechanistically, LCRMP-1 functions as a co-transcriptional factor, enhancing the expression of SERPINE1 through its interactions with TP53. This enhancement promotes the secretion of SERPINE1, alters the tumor microenvironment, and assists endothelial cells in the formation of new blood vessels, thereby further advancing the progression of NSCLC.

Introduction

Angiogenesis plays a crucial role in cancer progression by facilitating the delivery of oxygen and nutrients, removing waste, supporting tumor cell survival, and enabling metastasis1–3. The process involves the breakdown of the basement membrane, migration and proliferation of endothelial cells, and the formation of capillary tubes2,4. The angiogenic switch process includes pro- and anti-angiogenic factors, leading to an expanding vascular network5,6.

Several pro-angiogenic factors have been identified and leveraged in developing diverse anti-tumor therapies, including receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) signaling proteins, hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), thrombospondin-1 (TSP-1), plasminogen-plasmin system, and reactive oxygen species (ROS). These factors are associated with angiogenesis and can be triggered by microenvironmental stress or genetic alterations5–10. Inhibiting tumor angiogenesis has emerged as an effective strategy to slow down tumor growth11,12. For example, VEGFR2 is essential for initiating tumor angiogenesis, which has promoted the use of anti-VEGF antibodies as a standard treatment for cancer5,13–16. Platelet-derived growth factors (PDGFs) bind to platelet-derived growth factor receptors (PDGFRs), activating a pathway like VEGF. Overactive PDGF signaling is linked to tumor vascularization in cancers such as non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and ovarian cancer. A recent study suggests that using PDGFR inhibitors may improve the effectiveness of VEGFR blockers in cancer treatment17. The 45 kDa glycoprotein SERPINE1, also known as plasminogen activator inhibitor (PAI-1), plays a crucial role in the plasminogen-plasmin system. As a serine proteinase inhibitor, its expression significantly influences tumor vascularization and cancer progression18–23. More than fifty inhibitors have been developed to target angiogenesis by SERPINE122,24–26. Compounds such as SK-216 and SK-116 have demonstrated anti-angiogenic properties by reducing serum SERPINE1 levels22,27,28. Additionally, tiplaxtinin has shown potential therapeutic effects on the NSCLC cell line29,30.

Despite the development of numerous angiogenic inhibitors, achieving the necessary concentration in both blood and tumor tissue for extended periods remains a significant challenge31,32. Furthermore, the emergence of drug resistance to anti-angiogenic therapies raised a serious concern, as it may lead to a decrease in sustained clinical response5. Therefore, there is a need to improve the efficiency of SERPINE1 inhibitors and to explore novel regulators of pro-angiogenic factors that could attenuate the expression of SERPINE1’s angiogenic targets. Nowadays, various signals, including transcription factors, cytokines, hormones, and environmental stresses, have been shown to modulate the expression of SERPINE122. Several transcription factors regulate SERPINE1 at the transcriptional level, including SMADs, TP53, and AP-133,34. In HepG2 hepatoma cells, the TP53 and SMADs complex may interact with the promoter of SERPINE133. Similarly, in breast cancer, components of SMAD2/3 and AP-1 have been observed interacting with SERPINE134. However, the comprehensive mechanisms that govern SERPINE1 remain unclear.

The collapsin response mediator protein (CRMP) family consists of five cytosolic phosphoproteins, initially identified as essential components in SEMA3A-mediated growth cone collapse in neuronal cells35–38. SEMA3A is a secreted protein that plays a role in repulsion by forming a complex with PLXNA1 and NRP-139. NRP-1 binds KDR/folk-1 (also known as VEGFR2 or folk-1), creating an active receptor important for angiogenesis. This process is crucial for developing vascular branching, endothelial chemotaxis, and mitogenesis40. Extensive research has identified the CRMP-1 protein as a pivotal factor in inhibiting the invasion and metastasis of lung cancer41. Furthermore, CRMP-1 has been shown to reduce VEGF-A-dependent angiogenesis in human lung adenocarcinoma by mediating the inhibition induced by CTGF42,43. Recent studies have also discovered a new isoform of the CRMP-1 protein, termed long-form CRMP-1 (LCRMP-1), which acts oppositely to the effects of CRMP-1 in relation to cell migration, invasion, and metastasis44,45. LCRMP-1 is responsible for forming filopodia and invading cells; its expression levels are positively correlated with cell invasiveness. Overexpressing LCRMP-1 enhances filopodia formation, as well as the migration and invasion of cancer cells, through the WAVE-1 pathway. This effect can override the influence of dominant negative CDC42 on filopodia formation44. These findings suggest that small GTPases, like CDC42, play a crucial role in LCRMP-1-mediated signaling, essential for the mechanosensitive transcriptional mechanism of tumor angiogenesis46–48. Although LCRMP-1 expression does not affect cell proliferation in vitro44, cells that overexpress LCRMP-1 have been shown to enhance tumor growth in vivo (Fig. 1a). Given the vital role of angiogenesis in tumor progression, we explored the potential involvement of LCRMP-1 in tumor angiogenesis due to these contrasting outcomes. Our study suggests that LCRMP-1 may promote angiogenesis in lung cancer by modulating the angiogenic factor SERPINE1. Results

The expression of LCRMP-1 protein is positively correlated with tumor angiogenesis

Our previous research identified a positive correlation between LCRMP-1 expression and metastasis in NSCLC patients44,45. To expand on these findings, we sought to determine whether ectopic expression of LCRMP-1 could enhance cell proliferation and tumor growth. To achieve this, we established stable cell lines expressing Flag-tagged LCRMP-1 in CL1-0 cells and knocked down endogenous LCRMP-1 expression in CL1-5 or A549 cells using small-interference RNA (siRNA). Subsequently, we evaluated the growth capabilities of these stable cells using an independent colony formation assay in vitro. Additionally, we assessed the expression of LCRMP-1 to investigate its impact on xenograft tumor growth in vivo. The results showed that altering LCRMP-1 expression in these cells (Fig. 1a, left) did not affect their growth ability (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, in the xenograft tumor growth assay, it was found that CL1-0/LCRMP-1 overexpressing cells formed larger tumors compared to the vector control (Fig. 1a, right top). The tumor volume was measured at 163 ± 51 mm3 for CL1-0/LCRMP-1 and 55 ± 26 mm3 for CL1-0/vector, with a P value of 0.029. Conversely, A549 cells with LCRMP-1 silencing formed smaller tumors than the scramble control (Fig. 1a, right bottom). The tumor volume was 212 ± 38 mm3 for A549/scramble and 50 ± 14 mm3 for A549/ siLCRMP-1, with a P value of less than 0.001. Considering that LCRMP-1 expression levels did not impede cell proliferation in vitro but instead promoted tumor growth in vivo, we aimed to investigate whether the tumorigenic effects of LCRMP-1 are linked to its ability to enhance tumor angiogenesis. First, we established the correlation between LCRMP-1 protein expression and angiogenic activity in vitro. The conditioned medium from various lung cancer cell lines was used for a tube formation assay with endothelial cells. The results indicated a positive correlation between the expression of LCRMP-1 in lung cancer cell lines and their abilities to induce tubular sprouting in HUVECs (Fig. 1b; R2 = 0.7780 for LCRMP-1 protein expression in cancer cells vs tube formation in HUVECs). Then, we evaluated microvessel density in vivo using our xenograft tumor specimens. The findings showed that tumors overexpressing CL1-0/LCRMP-1 had a significantly higher microvessel density (Fig. 1c, left; P = 0.0212). In contrast, xenograft tumors where A549/LCRMP-1 was silenced exhibited a decreased microvessel density (Fig. 1c, right; P = 0.0014). Expanding on our findings, we conducted an analysis of immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining results for LCRMP-1 and CD31 expressions in 151 NSCLC specimens collected in 2010, with approval from the Institutional Review Board at the National Taiwan University Hospital (IRB number: 200812097R). The aim of this investigation was to explore the correlation between LCRMP-1 expression and microvessel density (Fig. 1d). The clinical parameters and pathological characteristics of LCRMP-1 and CD31 are summarized in Table 1. Our analysis demonstrated a significant correlation between blood vessel density in patients’ tumor tissues and the expression level of LCRMP-1 (Table 2, P < 0.0001). LCRMP-1 facilitates the process of angiogenesis

The expression of LCRMP-1 is positively correlated with angiogenesis. Therefore, our next step was to demonstrate that controlling the expression of LCRMP-1 can regulate angiogenesis. We collected conditioned media from stable cancer cells with varying levels of LCRMP-1 and used them to perform in vitro endothelial cell-tube formation assays and in vivo plaque assays (Fig. 2a, b). The results revealed that the conditioned medium from CL1-0/LCRMP-1 overexpressing cells significantly enhances tubular sprouting (Fig. 2a, left; P = 0.0438), and a similar phenomenon was also observed in EKVX and A549 cells (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). In contrast, conditioned media from CL1-5/LCRMP-1 silencing cells showed a decrease in sprouting in HUVECs (Fig. 2a, right; P = 0.0006). Our observations in the plug assay system demonstrated that a Matrigel plug, presoaked with conditional medium from CL1-0/LCRMP-1 overexpressing cells, effectively attracted endothelial cells, leading to an increase in hemoglobin concentration. This increase reflects an upregulation of angiogenetic activity (Fig. 2b, left; P = 0.0040). The effect of LCRMP-1 in inducing this response was diminished when LCRMP-1 expression was reduced in CL1-5 cells (Fig. 2b, right; P = 0.0243). To evaluate the migration capabilities of HUVECs, a 3-well wound healing assay was conducted (Ibid). HUVECs were placed in the central well, surrounded by LCRMP-1-expressing cancer cells and control cells. To investigate whether LCRMP-1 protein can be secreted into the conditioned medium and influence angiogenesis, we collected the conditioned medium from CL1-0/LCRMP-1 overexpressing cells and various lung cancer cells expressing high levels of LCRMP-1. We then employed an immunoblotting assay to detect the protein expression of LCRMP-1 (Fig. 2d, e). The results showed a significant distribution of LCRMP-1 expression in cellular lysates, suggesting that LCRMP-1 is unlikely to be a secretory protein. Following a closer examination, we hypothesized that the conditioned media might contain diverse secretory proteins, as indicated by our Coomassie blue staining (Supplementary Fig. 3). After clarifying the indirect regulatory role of LCRMP-1 in angiogenesis, we initiated the process of identifying potential angiogenic factors that may modulate LCRMP-1.

LCRMP-1 controls angiogenesis via the regulation of SERPINE1

To identify angiogenic factors regulated by LCRMP-1 and their involvement in angiogenesis, we utilized the Human Angiogenesis Antibody Array to analyze the conditioned media (Supplementary Fig. 4a) and evaluated the cell lysates using a cDNA microarray (Supplementary Fig. 4b). The results indicated that specific angiogenic enhancers significantly elevated in CL1-0/LCRMP-1 overexpressing cells, whereas their levels were reduced in CL1-0/LCRMP-1 silencing cells (Fig. 3a). The top11 angiogenic factors that exhibited significant changes in both the protein array and cDNA microarray are detailed in Fig. 3b. The figure highlights notable differences in the expression levels of SERPINE1, CXCL16, Coagulation Factor III, and PENTRAXIN3 between cells that either overexpress or silence LCRMP-1 and the control group. Consequently, these candidates were selected for further investigation in the conditioned medium (Fig. 3c). The data indicated a significant alteration in the expression of SERPINE1, reinforcing the notion that SERPINE1 may serve as a critical regulator in cells expressing LCRMP-1, potentially influencing angiogenesis. To evaluate this hypothesis, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of SERPINE1 expression in the conditioned medium from various lung cancer cell lines, specifically CL1-0, CL1-5, Hop62, H1650, and H23. Our results revealed a direct correlation between the levels of SERPINE1 expression and LCRMP-1 expression in the corresponding cell lysates (Fig. 3d). Moreover, we employed an anti-SERPINE1 antibody to neutralize the SERPINE1 protein in the conditioned medium from CL1-0/LCRMP-1 overexpressing cells. When HUVECs were exposed to this neutralized medium, we observed a significant reduction in their ability to form tubular structures (Fig. 3e; P = 0.0157). In addition, we generated a stable CL1-0 cell line overexpressing LCRMP-1 while silencing SERPINE1. We confirmed the expression of SERPINE1 protein by immunoblotting (Fig. 3f, bottom). Upon collecting conditioned medium from these cells, we noted a substantial decrease in the tube formation capability of HUVECs (Fig. 3f; P = 0.0021). Discussion

LCRMP-1, a novel isoform of CRMP-1, has been recognized as an enhancer of invasion44. It promotes filopodia formation and cancer cell migration/invasion via the WAVE-1 pathway, effectively counteracting the inhibitory effects of dominant negative CDC42 on filopodia. This activity appears regulated by the N-terminal residues 22–72 of LCRMP-1. Notably, elevated levels of LCRMP-1 or diminished levels of CRMP-1 correlate with lymph node metastasis and poor survival in NSCLC patients45. Here, we demonstrated that LCRMP-1 can drive tumor angiogenesis in vitro and in vivo while enhancing cancer cells’ migratory and invasive capabilities. The formation of a vascular network is essential for cancer progression, as tumors without an adequate blood vessel supply lack the necessary nutrients and oxygen to sustain the survival of cancer cells, thereby limiting their metastatic potential. Our findings demonstrate that LCRMP-1 expression in cancer cells stimulates endothelial cells to form spouting microvessels. Through analysis of an angio-factors protein array and microarray, we pinpointed SERPINE1, a recognized angiogenesis factor, as a potential key regulator influenced by LCRMP-1. It may promote angiogenesis by modulating its expression and facilitating its secretion into the bloodstream. Further investigation revealed that LCRMP-1 can significantly enhance the transcriptional activity of SERPINE1 in a dose-dependent manner via interacting with TP53 (Fig. 5f).

The CRMP family proteins are known for their roles in axon guidance and cancer metastasis through cytoskeletal rearrangement49. Our recent findings have heightened our interest in exploring the role and regulation of LCRMP-1 within the cell nucleus. In Fig. 4c, we detected LCRMP-1 in the nucleus of CL1-0 cells that were transfected with either WT or a phosphorylation mutant (T628D) of LCRMP-1. This observation aligns with recent studies on CRMP-2 and CRMP-5, which also identified these proteins in the nucleus50,51. Oliemuller et al. reported that phosphorylated (Thr 514) CRMP-2 is predominantly found in the nucleus of NSCLC tumor tissues51. Previously, we established that phosphorylated LCRMP-1 (Thr 628) can enhance filopodia formation, migration, and invasion. Notably, the phosphorylation site (Thr 628) of LCRMP-1 corresponds with the phosphorylated site (Thr 514) of CRMP-2, leading us to hypothesize that LCRMP-1 similarly localizes to the nucleus. Our results from transfections of CL1-0 cells with GFP-tagged WT LCRMP-1, a constitutively phosphorylated LCRMP-1 mutant, or a dominant negative LCRMP-1 mutant revealed LCRMP-1 signaling in both the cytosol and the nucleus in cells expressing phosphorylated LCRMP-1. This underscores the existence of LCRMP-1 in the nucleus. Additionally, Brot et al. noted that CRMP-5 exhibits both cytosolic and nuclear localization in GBM tumors50, further supporting our observation of LCRMP-1 in the nucleus.

The top 11 angiogenic factors that exhibited significant changes in both the protein array and cDNA microarray are shown in Fig. 3b. Our analysis revealed an upregulation of SERPINE1, CXCL16, Coagulation Factor III, and PENTRAXIN3 in LCRMP-1 overexpressing cells compared to vector cells, these factors were downregulated in LCRMP-1 silencing cells compared to scramble cells. These findings suggest that SERPINE1 is the primary angiogenesis factor regulated by LCRMP-1, while CXCL16, Coagulation Factor III, and PENTRAXIN3 may also contribute to angiogenesis. Further experiments with HUVECs treated with SERPINE1 neutralizing-conditioned medium (Fig. 3e) or conditioned medium from CL1-0/LCRMP-1 overexpressing stable cells, which silenced SERPINE1 (Fig. 3f), indicated that some tube formation ability persisted in HUVECs. This implied that other angiogenic factors regulated by LCRMP-1 are also involved in sprouting angiogenesis.

Our study has demonstrated that LCRMP-1 not only indirectly interacts with TP53 but also directly binds to the promoter region of SERPINE1 (Fig. 5e). We found that the transcriptional activity of SERPINE1 was significantly higher in the cells co-transfected with WT TP53 and LCRMP-1 plasmids compared to those only transfected with LCRMP-1 or co-transfected with mutant-type TP53 (R248W) and LCRMP-1 (Fig. 5f). These results indicated the presence of both TP53-dependent and independent pathways for regulating the SERPINE1 promoter. The TP53-dependent pathway involves the formation of the LCRMP-1–TP53 complex, which then binds to the SERPINE1 promoter, enhancing its transcriptional activity. To further clarify the TP53-independent mechanisms by which LCRMP-1 regulates SERPINE1 expression, we conducted functional assays using p53-deficient cells that overexpress LCRMP-1. Our results indicate that LCRMP-1 can effectively enhance angiogenic activity in HUVECs and increase SERPINE1 expression at both the transcriptional and secretory levels, even in the absence of TP53 (Supplementary Fig. 7e–g). However, the effects observed in the p53-deficient cells were less pronounced compared to those seen in TP53-proficient cells.

We propose two potential mechanisms of action for LCRMP-1 that are independent of the TP53 pathway. One possibility is that LCRMP-1 may contain an uncharacterized DNA-binding motif, suggesting its role as a novel transcriptional regulator. To assess this hypothesis, we performed preliminary in silico analyses using motif prediction tools such as MEME, Tomtom, and JASPAR. While we did not identify any classical DNA-binding domains52,53, our ChIP assay data (Fig. 4f) reveal that the region encompassing amino acids 72–105 is critical for binding to the SERPINE1 promoter. This observation indicates the existence of a non-canonical DNA-interacting domain or a platform for the recruitment of co-factors. Further investigation is required to confirm this hypothesis; however, our findings highlight a broader regulatory role for LCRMP-1 in angiogenesis and gene transcription beyond its association with TP53.

Several transcriptional factors may collaborate with LCRMP-1 to enhance the activity of the SERPINE1 promoter in the absence of TP53. While WT TP53 is known to promote the transcription of SERPINE1, mutant-type TP53 (R248W) does not produce the same effect (Fig. 5f). However, cells that have been transfected with mutant-type TP53 (R248W) in conjunction with LCRMP-1 still exhibited increased transcription of SERPINE1. These findings support the notion that LCRMP-1 not only interacts with TP53 but also engages with other transcription factors to boost the activity of the SERPINE1 promoter in cells with mutant-type TP53. To further substantiate this hypothesis, we investigated other potential cofactors identified in Fig. 5b, including AP-1, NFKB1, ARNT, E2F1, and USF. Among these, AP-1 was found to interact with LCRMP-1, indicating that it may act as a co-regulator with LCRMP-1 in the regulatory pathway of SERPINE1.

In conclusion, this study investigates the role of LCRMP-1 in tumor angiogenesis. The findings indicate that LCRMP-1 may enhance angiogenesis in lung cancer by regulating SERPINE1 (Fig. 6). This research provides significant evidence for the potential clinical application in treating lung adenocarcinoma in the foreseeable future.

Methods

Cell lines and culture conditions

The CL1-0 human lung adenocarcinoma cell line was isolated from a 64-year-old male patient with poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma. In Dr. Yang’s lab, the subline (CL1-5) with increasing invasive ability and a similar genetic background was obtained using in vitro chamber invasion as previously described54. HUVECs (human umbilical vein endothelial cells) were obtained from the Bioresource Collection and Research Center (BCRC). Additionally, several cell lines, including A549 (a human lung adenocarcinoma epithelial cell line), H1299 (a human lung non-small lung cancer cell line), HEK293 (a human embryonic kidney cell line), and HEK293T (a highly transfectable derivative of HEK 293 cells, containing the SV40 T-antigen) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Furthermore, numerous NCI-60 cell lines (60 human cancer cell lines used by the National Cancer Institute) were purchased from the National Cancer Institute’s Developmental Therapeutics Program (NCI), including Hop62 (a human lung adenocarcinoma epithelial cell line), Hop92 (a human lung non-small lung cancer cell line), H1650 (a human lung adenocarcinoma epithelial cell line), H23 (a human lung adenocarcinoma epithelial cell line), and H460 (a human lung non-small lung cancer cell line). These lung cancer cell lines were validated through the identification of specific biomarkers.

A549, H1299, HEK293, and HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, #12800017), while CL1-0, CL1-5, Hop62, Hop92, H1650, H23, and H460 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium (RPMI 1640, Gibco, #31800022). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 100 U/ml penicillin (Sigma, #SI-P3032), and 100 μg/ml streptomycin (Sigma, #SI-S9137). The HUVECs were cultured with EGMTM-2 Endothelial Cell Growth Medium containing 2% FBS and growth factor supplements (Lonza, #CC-3162). All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. All tested cell lines have been confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination.

Patients and tumor specimens

Lung tumor tissue specimens were obtained from 151 patients diagnosed with NSCLC who underwent surgical treatment at the National Taiwan University Hospital (Taipei, Taiwan) from December 28, 1995, to December 26, 2005. The project received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB number: 200812097R) of the National Taiwan University Hospital. All enrolled patients had not received any drug treatment or irradiation therapy prior to sample collection. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and all relevant ethical regulations concerning human research subjects were strictly followed. All tissue samples were formalin-fixed, sectioned, stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and examined under microscopy. Pathological staging was conducted by Dr. Yih-Leong Chang from the Department of Pathology and Graduate Institute of Pathology, National Taiwan University, following the international staging system for lung cancer55.

Plasmids for transfection

The amino acid sequence alignment of LCRMP-1 and TP53 is derived from their Gene Bank accession numbers NP_001014809 and NP_000537.3, respectively. Furthermore, the alignment of the promoter sequences of SERPINE1 is based on a publication from 199756. The cDNA encoding full-length human LCRMP-1 and TP53, and the genomic DNA for the complete human SERPINE1 promoter and deletion fragments (−305 and −101) were extracted from a human lung cancer cell line using polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The LCRMP-1 expression plasmids (pCMV-Tag2A-LCRMP-1, pEGFP-LCRMP-1, pcDNA3-LCRMP-1), along with amino acid substitution mutants (pEGFP-LCRMP-1-T628A and pEGFP-LCRMP-1-T628D), and LCRMP-1 silencing plasmid (p-Silencer-LCRMP-1) were previously described in refs. 44,57. The TP53 plasmid was sub-cloned into the pcDNA3.1-Myc-His (−) B (Invitrogen, #V855-20) plasmid, resulting in pcDNA3.1-Myc-His-TP53. Constructs of the SERPINE1 promoter were established within the pGL4 (Promega, #TM259) plasmid: specifically, pGL4-SERPINE1 promoter [FL], pGL4-SERPINE1 promoter [−305], and pGL4-SERPINE1 promoter [−101].

The establishment of stable cell lines expressing LCRMP-1

Plasmid pCMV-Tag2A-LCRMP-1 (3.5 μg) was transfected into CL1-0 cells at 70% confluency using 20 U of Lipofectamine reagent (Invitrogen, #18324012) in a total volume of 1 ml of Opto-MEM (Gibco, #31985070). Additionally, plasmid p-Silencer-LCRMP-1 (3.5 μg) was transfected into CL1-5, A549, or Hop62 cells at 70% confluency using 10 μl Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, #11668019). Gentamycin (G418, Goldbio, #108321-42-2) was added to select stable cell lines, as previously described in ref. 57. The transfection efficiency of stable cell lines was confirmed through immunoblotting analysis.

Antibodies

The specific primary antibodies for LCRMP-1 were produced in our laboratory44,58 (anti-C2, WB: 1:5000; anti-Lon-Cys, WB: 1:10,000). The commercial primary antibodies used for immunoblotting were as follows: anti-Flag (M2, Sigma, #SI-F3165, WB: 1:5000), anti-Myc (Millipore, 9E10 clone, WB: 1:1000), anti-HA (BioLegend, #901515, WB: 1:1000), anti-SERPINE1 (Cell Signaling, #11907S, WB: 1:1000), anti-TP53 (Santa Cruz, #SC-126, WB: 1:1000), anti-CD31 (Proteintech, #66065-2-Ig, WB: 1:1000), anti-CXCL16 (GeneTex, #GTX632502, WB: 1:1000), anti-Coagulation Factor III (GeneTex, #GTX100808, WB: 1:1000), anti-PENTRAXIN3 (GeneTex, # GTX45054, WB: 1:1000), anti-β-actin (Sigma, #SI-A5441, WB: 1:20,000), anti-Hsp90 (Enzo, #ADI-SPA-835, WB: 1:1000), anti-Lamin B (Santa Cruz, #SC-365214, WB: 1:10,000), and anti-GST(BD BaculoGold™, #554805, WB: 1:1000) antibodies. The HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-mouse, anti-rabbit, and anti-rat) were purchased from Croyez.

Lentivirus production and transduction

The pLKO.1 SERPINE1 shRNA was obtained from the RNAi Core at Academia Sinica, Taiwan. The pLKO.1 vector plasmid was used as a negative control. HEK293T cells were co-transfected with the shRNA plasmids and two helper plasmids, pCMV△R8.91, and pMD.G, using the Lipofectamine 2000 reagent. Virus-containing medium was collected 24, 48, and 72 h after the transfection, centrifuged, and filtered through 0.45 μm-pore-size filters. The percentage of pLKO.1 SERPINE1 shRNA-infected cells were examined to determine relative lentivirus titers. CL1-0/LCRMP-1 overexpressing cells were infected with the indicated lentivirus in media containing 8 μg/ml polybrene (Sigma, #107689). After 24 h post-infection, cells were treated with fresh medium for 24 h, and 2 μg/ml puromycin (Sigma, #SI-P7255) was added to select pooled stable cell lines. The CL1-0-LCRMP-1 shSERPINE1 stable cells were used for experiments.

Colony formation assay

CL1-0/LCRMP-1 overexpressing and CL1-5/LCRMP-1 silencing stable cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 102 cells per well in 6-well culture dishes. They were suspended in 0.35% agar (Vivantis, Chino, California) containing medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and placed on top of a layer of 0.7% agar containing the same medium. The plates were then incubated for 4–8 weeks, followed by washing with 1× PBS and fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma, #SI-P6148) at room temperature for 10 min. After another wash with 1× PBS, the cells were stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Sigma, #SI-C0775), and the colonies were subsequently counted visually. The average number of colonies per well was calculated for the control and experimental groups. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Xenograft tumor growth assay

For the in vivo tumor growth assay, CL1-0 LCRMP-1 overexpressing cells, A549 LCRMP-1 silencing cells, and their respective control cells (CL1-0/Vector and A549/Scramble) were subcutaneously injected into 8-week-old CAnN.Cg-Foxn1nu/CrlNarl mice. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups, with ten mice per group to account for potential loss due to unexpected death or absence of tumor formation. Tumor growth was monitored weekly using digital calipers. Tumor volume was calculated using the standard formula: 0.5 × a × b2, where a is the longest diameter and b is the shortest diameter (both in mm).

Mice were euthanized and tumors harvested on day 35 for the CL1-0/LCRMP-1 overexpressing group (N = 7) and on day 39 for the A549/LCRMP-1 silencing group (N = 8). All tumor sizes complied with the guidelines of the Laboratory Animal Center at the National Taiwan University College of Medicine (Taiwan), under the approved IACUC protocol (No. 20150378), and remained below the maximum volume limit of 1500 mm3. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis of LCRMP-1 and microvessel density in tumor samples from experimental tumor growth in vivo for patients with NSCLC

To prepare for IHC staining of LCRMP-1 and CD31 protein expression, the sections were first autoclaved in Antigen Retrieval Citra Solution (BioGenex) at 121 °C for 10 min. Then, the samples were treated with 3% H2O2 and sequentially incubated with 0.1% BSA for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation with a monoclonal anti-C2 antibody (1:300) and a monoclonal anti-CD31 (1:100) at 4 °C. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the immunostaining was detected using the Super Sensitive Non-Biotin Polymer HRP Detection System (BioGenex). The expression levels of LCRMP-1 and CD31 were analyzed by quantifying the percentage of positively stained cells present in the tissue slides. Samples with more than 50% expression of LCRMP-1 were designed as LCRMP-1 positive, whereas those exhibiting more than 15% expression of CD31 were classified as CD31 positive.

Endothelial cell tube formation assay

The conditioned medium from various cancer cells was collected and concentrated using Amicon® Ultra Centrifugal Filter Devices (Millipore) to an 80-fold concentration. Next, 10 μl of Matrigel (Corning, #356231) was added to the wells of μ-slide (Ibidi, #81506) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Then, 2 × 104 HUVECs suspended in 50 μl conditioned medium were placed in each well and incubated for 6 h at 37 °C. Finally, the 100× bright field cell images were acquired using an inverted microscope equipped with a CCD system in conjunction with OCULAR software, and the number of the tubular segments, branches, and junctions was calculated to assess tube formation ability using Angiogenesis Analyzer for ImageJ, integrated with Java 859.

Endothelial cell migration assay

The HUVECs migration assay used the Culture-Insert 3 Well (Ibidi, #80369). The 7 × 104 HUVECs were plated in the middle well of the culture insert, and different cancer cells with varying LCRMP-1 expressing levels were seeded on both sides of the insert, filling it to 95% capacity. After 12 h, the inserts were removed, washed with PBS, and incubated at 37 °C for 8 h. Bright-field pictures at 100x magnification were taken using an inverted microscope equipped with a CCD system in conjunction with OCULAR software at 0 and 8 h, and the migrated HUVECs were counted to assess endothelial cell migration ability.

Plaque angiogenesis assay in vivo

For the in vivo plaque angiogenesis assay, the conditioned media from cancer cells were mixed with Matrigel and then subcutaneously injected into 8-week-old NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid/JNarl mice were provided by the National Laboratory Animal Center in Taiwan. After 2–4 weeks, the relevant mice were sacrificed using carbon dioxide anesthesia, the plaques were removed, and the hemoglobin was dissolved. The concentrations of hemoglobin in the plaques were measured to evaluate angiogenesis ability. All mouse experiments followed animal guidelines and were approved by the Laboratory Animal Center at the National Taiwan University College of Medicine (Taiwan, IACUC No. 20150378).

RNA extraction for microarray and quantitative real-time RT-PCR (Q-PCR)

The RNAs from different cell lines were isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, #15596018), and then the cDNAs were reverse transcribed using the Super Script III Reverse Transcriptase system (Invitrogen, #18080051). Microarrays were used to analyze the RNAs from cells overexpressing or silencing LCRMP-1, and these microarrays were described in a commercial, personal report from Phalanx Biotech Group. The analysis focused on the expression of genes known as angiogenic factors. To validate the microarray results, the expression of mRNA genes was detected using quantitative real-time RT-PCR analysis. The mRNA levels were measured after 30–40 amplification cycles with an annealing temperature of 60–64 °C by QuantStudio Design & Analysis Software. The primer sequences used were as follows: LCRMP-1, forward: 5-CTCGGCCGGGACAATG-3, reverse: 5-AAGGGATTGGTCATCGTTGATG-3; SERPINE1, forward: 5-CAACGTGGTTTTCTCACCCT-3, reverse: 5-GGGCGTGGTGAACTCAGTAT-3; TBP, forward: 5-CACGAACCACGGCACTGATT-3, reverse: 5-TTTTCTTGCCAGTCTGGAC- 3. TBP was used as a control gene.

Human angiogenic antibody array

The human angiogenic antibody array was conducted using a commercial kit from R&D Systems (#ARY007). LCRMP-1 expressing cells derived from CL1-0 or CL1-5 (1 × 106) were cultured in 4 ml of serum-free medium for 24 h. The conditioned media were then collected and concentrated using Amicon® Ultra Centrifugal Filter Devices (Millipore) to an 80-fold. Equal volumes of supernatants were incubated with the membrane overnight at 4 °C with rotation. The protein expression images were captured using a Luminescence Imaging System and quantified using ImageJ software.

Subcellular protein isolation

The proteins from the cell plasma and nucleus were isolated using a nuclear extraction kit (QIAGEN, #37582) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were harvested at 5 × 106 by scraping in cold PBS. The cells were then resuspended in 0.5 ml of cytosolic buffer, NP solution was added, and the mixtures were vortexed on ice for 15 s. The supernatant (cytosol fraction) was collected by centrifuging at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The nucleus lysate was resuspended in 200 μl RIPA buffer and sonicated on ice 15 times. The supernatant (nuclear fraction) was isolated by centrifuging at 12,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C.

Immunofluorescence staining

Plasmids, including pEGFP-LCRMP-1, pEGFP-LCRMP-1-T628A, and pEGFP-LCRMP-1-T628D, were transfected into CL1-0 cells for immunofluorescence staining. The transfected cells were fixed with 3.7% cold paraformaldehyde (Sigma, #SI-P6148), washed with PBS, and then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma, #SI-T8787). Subsequently, the cells were stained with rhodamine-conjugated phalloidin (Molecular Probes, Invitrogen, #A12381). Finally, the cells were mounted onto microscope slides with ProLongTM Gold antifade reagent with DAPI (Molecular Probes, Invitrogen, # P36931). The cell images were observed and photographed using an LSM 700 laser scanning confocal microscope from Carl Zeiss.

Immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting

Immunoprecipitation was performed as previously described in ref. 45. Briefly, transfected HEK293T, CL1-0, and H1299 cells were lysed on ice for 5–10 min in a solution containing 20 mM Tris (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 100 μM Na3VO4, 50 mM NaF, 30 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 0.5% NP-40 (Sigma-Aldrich), and a 25-fold dilution of a stock solution was treated with one Mini Protease Inhibitor Cocktail Tablet (Roche, #10061200-4) dissolved in 2 ml of PBS. The cell lysates were passed several times through a 21-gauge needle and clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were collected as the total cell lysates. LCRMP-1 was immunoprecipitated using anti-Flag (1:1000) or anti-Myc (1:1000) antibodies and protein G-Sepharose beads (Invitrogen, #10004D). The immunoprecipitated proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene membranes (Millipore) for immunoblotting with anti-Flag (1:5000), anti-Myc (1:1000), anti-LCRMP-1 (Long-Cys; 1:10,000), or anti-β-actin (1:20,000) primary antibodies. Immunoblotting was performed according to the standard procedure57. The chemiluminescent signals generated from immunoblotting were recorded utilizing TOPBIO Software. ImageJ, integrated with Java 8, was employed to analyze the results obtained from immunoblotting.

Luciferase assay

The luciferase assays were performed using a commercial kit from Promega (#E1960). CL1-0, A549, and H1299 cells were co-transfected with pGL4-SERPINE1 promoter, pCMV-Tag2A-LCRMP-1, and pcDNA3.1-Myc-His-TP53, and then incubated at 37 °C. After 48 h, the cell lysates were treated with the reagents from the kit, and the OD value was measured using an ELISA Reader (BioRed).

ChIP assay

The ChIP assay was carried out using a commercial kit from Millipore (#MAGNA0014). In brief, 90% of confluent cells plated in a 10 cm culture dish were treated with 1% formaldehyde to cross-link chromatin-associated proteins to DNA. The cell lysates were sonicated to fragment the DNA into lengths between 200 and 1000 base pairs. Equal amounts of cell lysates were then incubated with 1 μg of anti-LCRMP-1 antibody or an anti-IgG antibody (Bioss, #bs-0295P), which was used as a negative control. All fractions were incubated with 20 μl magnetic Protein A/G beads overnight at 4 °C with rotation. The LCRMP-1 protein–DNA complex was then purified to examine DNA fragments of the human SERPINE1 promoter. Specific primers were designed to detect the SERPINE1 promoter by RT-PCR (Primer sequence: forward 5’-CACACACACACATGCCTCAG-3’, reverse 5’-TCTTCTTGACAGCGCTCTTG-3’).

GST pull-down assay

To examine the direct interaction between LCRMP-1 and TP53, a GST pull-down assay was performed. The N-terminal region of human LCRMP-1 (amino acids 1–127) was cloned into a pGEX-5x-1 vector to generate a GST-tagged fusion protein. This construct was transformed into E. coli, wherein the GST–LCRMP-1 fusion protein was expressed and subsequently purified using glutathione-Sepharose 4B beads (GE Healthcare, #17-0756-01) under native conditions, following the manufacturer’s protocols. Following purification, either the GST–LCRMP-1 (1–127 aa) or GST control proteins were incubated with the cell-free TP53 protein, which was synthesized from the plasmids (pcDNA3.1-Myc-His-TP53) utilizing the TNT T7 Quick Coupled Transcription/Translation System (Promega, #L4610). The incubation was conducted in binding buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5; 150 mM NaCl; 1% NP-40; 1 mM EDTA; and protease inhibitors) at 4 °C for 2 h with gentle rotation. Subsequent to this incubation, the beads were extensively washed to eliminate unbound proteins, and the bound proteins were eluted by boiling in SDS sample buffer. The eluted proteins were then analyzed using SDS-PAGE, followed by immunoblotting with anti-TP53 and anti-GST antibodies.

EMSA

The EMSA was performed using a commercial kit from Thermo Fisher (#PIE20148). The cell-free proteins were generated from the plasmids (pcDNA3-LCRMP-1 and pcDNA3.1-Myc-His-TP53) using the TNT T7 Quick Coupled Transcription/Translation System (Promega). Biotin-labeled and unlabeled probes from the relevant SERPINE1 promoter region were designed as follows: Biotin-labeled probe: GACGGACTCCCAGAGCCAGTGAGTGGGTGGGGCTGGAACATGAGTTCATCTATTTCCTGCCCACA, and unlabeled probe: GTGGGTGGGGCTGGAACATGAGTTCA. In brief, the protein–DNA complex was separated using a 6% polyacrylamide gel following the provided protocol. The complexes were then transferred to a nylon membrane (Millipore). Finally, the results were detected using a luminescence imaging system to visualize the luminescent signals.

Statistics and reproducibility

Most molecular biology experiments were conventionally performed with two to three replicates, each comprising one or two biologically independent samples, and typically involve at least two researchers. The majority of data are presented as mean ± SEM, based on a minimum of three biologically independent samples. However, under certain conditions, specific experiments were conducted using only a single replicate. Animal studies were conducted in accordance with the principles of the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement). To ensure statistical reliability, each experimental group consisted of at least seven mice. For the IHC staining of clinical specimens, only a single replicate was performed due to the unique and irreplaceable nature of the samples. Nonetheless, the conclusions were drawn from a total of 151 tissue specimens. Due to budgetary limitations, only one replicate was performed for the protein array and microarray experiments. However, each protein spot on the array membrane and each microarray sample was analyzed in duplicate.

Statistical analyses were conducted using the Student’s t-test, one-way ANOVA, or Pearson’s chi-squared test, as appropriate. High levels of LCRMP-1 immunoreactivity occurred in more than 50% and 70% of tumor specimens, depending on the cohort. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism.
