NeuMC - a package for neural sampling for lattice field theories
Piotr Białas, Piotr Korcyl, Tomasz Stebel, Dawid Zapolski

Abstract
We present the NeuMC software package, based on PyTorch , aimed at facilitating the research on neural samplers in lattice field theories. Neural samplers based on normalizing flows are becoming increasingly popular in the context of Monte-Carlo simulations as they can effectively approximate target probability distributions, possibly alleviating some shortcomings of the Markov chain Monte-Carlo methods. Our package provides tools to create such samplers for two-dimensional field theories.

1
Introduction

Monte-Carlo methods rely on random samples generated from some
target
distribution
p(\bm{\phi})
. Often,
e.g.
in lattice field theories, that distribution is complicated and depends on all degrees of freedom of the system, hence there are no known methods for sampling from this distribution directly and independently. Instead, the approach of choice is to construct the associated Markov chain of samples, giving rise to the so-called Markov Chain Monte-Carlo (MCMC) approach. To be more precise, each step of the algorithm has two stages: in the first stage for the given configuration
\bm{\phi}_{i}
, a new trial configuration
\bm{\phi}_{trial}
is proposed from the distribution
q(\bm{\phi}_{trial}|\bm{\phi}_{i})
. In the second stage, the trial configuration is accepted with a probability
p_{a}(\bm{\phi}_{trial}|\bm{\phi}_{i})
usually given by the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance probability
[
1
,
2
]

p_{a}(\bm{\phi}_{trial}|\bm{\phi}_{i})=\min\left\{1,\frac{p(\bm{\phi}_{trial})}{q(\bm{\phi}_{trial}|\bm{\phi}_{i})}\frac{q(\bm{\phi}_{i}|\bm{\phi}_{trial})}{p(\bm{\phi}_{i})}\right\}

(1)

In order to keep the acceptance rate high, typically the configuration
\bm{\phi}_{trial}
differs from
\bm{\phi}_{i}
only on a small subset of degrees of freedom,
e.g.
a single lattice site. This makes the consecutive samples highly correlated. The length of this autocorrelation, as measured in the number of Monte-Carlo samples, usually increases as we approach the critical point of the theory. This phenomenon is known as
critical slowing down
and is the major obstacle in obtaining reliable estimates from lattice simulations.
Neural samplers try to alleviate this problem. The idea is to use a neural network-based model to sample data from some other distribution

q(\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta})
. The parameters
\bm{\theta}
are then tuned as to approximate the target distribution as closely as possible.
This distribution can subsequently be used to enhance the Monte-Carlo simulations in different ways.

Neural samplers are a relatively new approach but have progressed quickly from the 2D Ising model
[
3
]
and scalar
\phi^{4}

[
4
]
theory, through gauge theories
[
5
,
6
]
including fermions
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
, to the 4D lattice QCD
[
10
,
11
]
. Some specific applications were already proposed,
e.g.
the estimation of thermodynamic quantities
[
12
,
13
]
and entanglement entropy
[
14
,
15
]
. Also, progress has been made implementing various neural architectures; this includes diffusion models
[
16
,
17
]
, transformers
[
18
]
, stochastic normalizing flows
[
19
,
20
]
and continuous normalizing flows
[
21
]
.

Despite rapid progress, neural samplers are still a long way from being on the level of the modern Monte-Carlo codes which have been developed over four decades, so further improvements are needed.
Our software aims to facilitate the research in this area by providing a framework, based on a popular
PyTorch
package, for building flow-based neural samplers for 2D lattice field theories. The first version of the software was successfully used while developing the REINFORCE gradient estimator and was tested on
\phi^{4}
and the
U(1)
gauge model with fermions (Schwinger model)
[
22
,
23
]
.

In this paper, we present the code which allows users to build their own samplers. The software’s structure allows for easy modifications and adding new functionalities. All physical models which are currently implemented in our package were discussed in the context of neural samplers already in the literature; however, up to now to our best knowledge, there was no general framework for implementing flow-based samplers. There exists an excellent introduction to neural samplers in form of the notebook
[
24
]
which actually was an inspiration and the starting point for this work (some snippets of this code, taken under the CC 4.0 license, still remain in our package). A more general framework
NeuLat
was announced but it is not yet made public
[

In the next sections, we describe the general idea behind the concept of neural samples. In particular, we discuss the equivariant flows and gradient estimators and provide a description of our implementations. At the end, we provide an example using one physical model. The
NeuMC

2
Neural samplers

Training the distribution
q(\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta})
is done by minimizing a suitable loss function that provides some measure of the difference between
q(\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta})
and target distribution
p(\bm{\phi})
. The most common choice is the (reverse) Kullback-Leibler divergence
[
27
]

D_{KL}(q|p)=\int{\,\mskip 0.0mu{}{\mathrm{d}\bm{\phi}}\mskip 0.0mu}\,q(\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta})\left(\log q(\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta})-\log p(\bm{\phi})\right).

(2)

Actually, often we know the target distribution
p(\bm{\phi})
only up to a multiplicative constant
Z
that we will call
partition function

P(\bm{\phi})=Z\cdot p(\bm{\phi}).

(3)

If we insert
P(\bm{\phi})
instead of
p(\bm{\phi})
in the formula (
2
) we obtain

F(q|P)\equiv\int{\,\mskip 0.0mu{}{\mathrm{d}\bm{\phi}}\mskip 0.0mu}\,q(\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta})\left(\log q(\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta})-\log p(\bm{\phi})-\log Z\right)=F+D_{KL}(q|p),

(4)

where
F=-\log Z
is a quantity which, with some abuse of notation, we will call
free energy
. As
F
does not depend on
\bm{\theta}
, minimizing
F(q|P)
is the same as minimizing
D_{KL}
.

Knowledge of
Z
is not required for training the model, but once trained the model can be used to estimate
F
, something that is very difficult to do with a traditional Monte-Carlo. Firstly, the
F(q|P)
provides a variational approximation of
F
that approaches true
F
from above because
D_{KL}
is always non-negative. Additionally, we can also construct an unbiased estimator of
Z

[
12
,
13
]
. This can be achieved using the unnormalized importance weights

w(\bm{\phi})\equiv\frac{P(\bm{\phi})}{q(\bm{\phi})}=Z\frac{p(\bm{\phi})}{q(\bm{\phi})}.

(5)

Obviously

\int{\,\mskip 0.0mu{}{\mathrm{d}\bm{\phi}}\mskip 0.0mu}\,q(\bm{\phi})w(\bm{\phi})=Z\int{\,\mskip 0.0mu{}{\mathrm{d}\bm{\phi}}\mskip 0.0mu}\,p(\bm{\phi})=Z,

(6)

so
Z
can be approximated using

Z\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}w(\bm{\phi}_{i}),\quad\bm{\phi}_{i}\sim q(\bm{\phi}_{i}).

(7)

The symbol
\sim
is used to denote the fact that
\bm{\phi}_{i}
is drawn with probability
q(\bm{\phi}_{i}|\bm{\theta})
.

2.1
Neural Markov Chain Monte-Carlo

Once trained, the model can be used within the Metropolized Independent Sampling (MIS)
[
28
]
approach where the whole configuration
\bm{\phi}_{trial}
is proposed by the model and then accepted or rejected using the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance probability

p_{a}(\bm{\phi}_{trial}|\bm{\phi}_{i})=\min\left\{1,\frac{P(\bm{\phi}_{trial})}{q(\bm{\phi}_{trial}|\bm{\theta})}\frac{q(\bm{\phi}_{i}|\bm{\theta})}{P(\bm{\phi}_{i})}\right\}.

(8)

Such an algorithm is called Neural Markov Chain Monte-Carlo (NMCMC)
[
12
]
.
With a properly trained model, the autocorrelation can be substantially smaller than in the case of standard MCMC,
e.g.
Metropolis–Hastings algorithm, cluster algorithms or hybrid Monte-Carlo (see Ref.
[
29
]
for a discussion).

2.2
Neural importance sampling

Another approach, dubbed the Neural Importance Sampling (NIS)
[
12
]
, uses the importance weights (
5
)
to reweight the expectation values to the correct distribution

\left\langle{O}\right\rangle_{p}=\int{\,\mskip 0.0mu{}{\mathrm{d}\bm{\phi}}\mskip 0.0mu}p(\bm{\phi})O(\bm{\phi})=\frac{\int{\,\mskip 0.0mu{}{\mathrm{d}\bm{\phi}}\mskip 0.0mu}\,q(\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta})w(\bm{\phi})O(\bm{\phi})}{\int{\,\mskip 0.0mu{}{\mathrm{d}\bm{\phi}}\mskip 0.0mu}\,q(\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta})w(\bm{\phi})}.

(9)

This expression can be approximated by sampling from distribution
q(\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta})
:

\left\langle{O}\right\rangle_{p}\approx\frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}w(\bm{\phi}_{i})O(\bm{\phi}_{i})}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1}^{N}w(\bm{\phi}_{i})},\quad\bm{\phi}_{i}\sim q(\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta}).

(10)

In this case all the configurations are statistically independent but the variance of weights
w
affects the variance of the result. This can be expressed using the effective sample size (ESS):

ESS=\frac{\left\langle{w}\right\rangle^{2}}{\left\langle{w^{2}}\right\rangle}=\frac{1}{\frac{\operatorname{var}\left[w\right]}{\left\langle{w}\right\rangle^{2}}+1},

(11)

which is a measure of the quality of the sampler with regard to importance sampling
[
28
,
30
]
and provides a handy, commonly used, way of measuring the progress of the training. For the perfect training the weights have zero variance and ESS is equal to one.

3
Physics models

We start with a description of the lattice models we have included in our package. Formally the models are specified by providing a definition of the action
S(\bm{\phi})
that defines the unnormalized target distribution
P(\bm{\phi})

P(\bm{\phi})=e^{-S(\bm{\phi})}.

(12)

Please note that this does not restrict in any way the class of target probability distributions, as every distribution can be described in this way.

Actions are defined as classes that can be instantiated with different parameters, e.g.

Unfortunately, defining the action is not enough. Different theories can have different kinds of fields: scalar, vector, U(1), gauge fields, etc. requiring different treatments. This will be covered in sections

3.1
\phi^{4}
theory - scalar and vector fields

One of the simplest field theories is the
\phi^{4}
theory. We provide the implementation for both the scalar and the vector theory. A
D
dimensional vector field
\bm{\phi}
is represented by a tensor of size

(N_{b},D,L_{x},L_{y})
. The first dimension is the batch dimension, the second is the vector dimension, and the remaining ones are the spatial dimensions. Putting the vector component dimension before spatial dimensions may look strange, but this is done to conform to the
PyTorch
convention for convolutional neural networks where channel dimension is before spatial dimensions.

The
\phi^{4}
action for a vector field is defined as

\begin{split}S(\bm{\phi}|m^{2},\lambda)&=\frac{1}{2}\kappa\sum_{i,j=0}^{L-1}\sum_{k=0}^{D-1}\left((\phi_{k,i+1,j}-\phi_{k,i,j})^{2}+(\phi_{k,i,j+1}-\phi_{k,i,j})^{2}\right)\\
&\phantom{=}+\sum_{i,j=0}^{L-1}\left(\frac{m^{2}}{2}|\phi_{i,j}|^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{4!}\left(|\phi_{i,j}|^{2}\right)^{2}\right),\end{split}

(13)

where

|\phi_{i,j}|^{2}=\sum_{k}\phi_{k,i,j}^{2}.

(14)

This can be rewritten as

\begin{split}S(\bm{\phi}|m^{2},\lambda)&=-\kappa\sum_{i,j=0}^{L}\sum_{k}\left(\phi_{k,i+1,j}\phi_{k,i,j}+\phi_{k,i,j+1}\phi_{k,i,j}\right)\\
&\phantom{=}+\sum_{i,j=0}^{L}\left(\frac{m^{2}+4\kappa}{2}|\phi_{i,j}|^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{4!}\left(|\phi_{i,j}|^{2}\right)^{2}\right)\end{split}

(15)

and this is the form we use in our implementation which is provided by the
VectorPhi4Action
class.

This action has a global
O(D)
symmetry, where
O(D)
is a group of orthogonal matrices of size
D
. One can easily check that transforming every element

\phi^{\prime}_{k^{\prime},i,j}=\sum_{k=0}^{D-1}o_{k^{\prime},k}\phi_{k,i,j},\quad\bm{o}\in O(D)

(16)

does not change the action. In the scalar case this reduces to the
Z_{2}
symmetry

\phi^{\prime}_{i,j}=-\phi_{i,j}.

(17)

The scalar field could be defined as a tensor of shape
(N_{n},1,L_{x},L_{y})
but for convenience it is "squeezed" to the shape
(N_{b},L_{x},L_{y})
and we provide a matching
ScalarPhi4Action
class.

Of course, the user may provide her own implementation of the action, as long as it expects scalar or vector fields as the input. For example, one could add a regularization term.

3.2
XY model - periodic scalar fields

The
XY
model can be considered as an extension of the Ising model. The spins are generalized to two-dimensional vectors of unit length

|\bm{s}_{i,j}|=1.

(18)

The action is

-\beta\sum_{i,j=0}^{L-1}\left(\bm{s}_{i,j}\cdot\bm{s}_{i+1,j}+\bm{s}_{i,j}\cdot\bm{s}_{i,j+1}\right).

(19)

Each spin can be uniquely represented by an angle

\bm{s}_{i,j}=(\cos\phi_{i,j},\sin\phi_{i,j})

(20)

and the action then becomes

-\sum_{i,j=0}^{L-1}\left(\cos(\phi_{i+1,j}-\phi_{i,j})+\cos(\phi_{i,j+1}-\phi_{i,j})\right).

(21)

This is the formulation we use in our implementation in
XYAction
class from module
neumc.physics.xy
.

This model has a global
O(2)
symmetry: rotating every spin by the same angle
\varphi

\phi^{\prime}_{i,j}=\phi_{i,j}+\varphi

(22)

does not change the action.

3.3
U(1) gauge theory

The most complicated model we provide is the
U(1)
gauge theory with fermions.
The gauge fields
U
are defined on the
links
of the lattice instead of sites and are members of some gauge group
\mathcal{U}

[
31
]
. By definition, we require gauge fields to respect a local gauge symmetry. The gauge symmetry is defined by a set of gauge fields
g(\bm{x})
on the sites of the lattice. If we denote by
U_{\mu}(\bm{x})
a gauge field on a link emerging from site
x
in the direction
\mu
, then this link variables transform accordingly to

U_{\mu}(\bm{x})\rightarrow g(\bm{x})U_{\mu}(\bm{x})g^{\dagger}(\bm{x}+\hat{\mu})

(23)

where
\bm{x}+\hat{\mu}
denotes a lattice site on the next site in the direction
\mu
from
\bm{x}
.
The simplest action that respects this symmetry is
[
31
]

S(U)=-\beta\sum_{\bm{x}}\operatorname{Re}P(\bm{x})

(24)

where
P(\bm{x})
is a
plaquette
(see Figure
1
)

P(\bm{x})=U_{1}(\bm{x})U_{0}(\bm{x}+\hat{1})U_{1}^{\dagger}(\bm{x}+\hat{0})U_{0}^{\dagger}(\bm{x}).

(25)

The versor
\hat{\mu}
is the displacement vector of one lattice site in the direction
\mu
.

Figure 1:
Scheme of plaquette.

The gauge symmetry introduces many unphysical degrees of freedom and is usually an obstacle for training neural samplers, and we will require that the probability
q(\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta})
is also invariant under its action. How to achieve this is described in Section
4.6
.

The elements of the
U(1)
group are just complex numbers
z
with unit norm
|z|=1
and can be represented by a phase angle
\phi

z=e^{i\phi}.

(26)

The action then becomes

-\beta\sum_{\bm{x}}\cos\left(\phi_{1}(\bm{x})+\phi_{0}(\bm{x}+\hat{1})-\phi_{1}(\bm{x}+\hat{0})-\phi_{0}(\bm{x})\right).

(27)

and is implemented as
U1GaugeAction
class in module
neumc.physics.u1
. This module provides also many additional helper functions.

Fermionic degrees of freedom are introduced into the theory as anticommuting Grassmann variables living on the sites of the lattice
[
31
]
. They cannot be represented on a computer but can formally be integrated out. After integration, we are left with the determinant of the Dirac operator. The action with two massless fermion fields thus becomes

S(U)=-\beta\sum_{\bm{x}}\operatorname{Re}P(\bm{x})-\log\det D[U]^{\dagger}D[U],

(28)

where the Wilson-Dirac operator
D[U]
is defined as

\begin{split}D[U](\bm{y},\bm{x})^{\alpha\beta}&=\delta(\bm{y}-\bm{x})\delta^{\alpha\beta}\\
-\kappa&\sum_{\mu=0,1}\Big\{[1-\sigma^{\mu}]^{\beta\alpha}U_{\mu}(\bm{y}-\bm{x}+\hat{\mu})\delta(\bm{y}-\bm{x}+\hat{\mu})\\
&\phantom{-\kappa\sum_{\mu=0,1}}+[1+\sigma^{\mu}]^{\beta\alpha}U^{\dagger}_{\mu}(\bm{y}-\hat{\mu})\delta(\bm{y}-\bm{x}-\hat{\mu})\Big\},\end{split}

(29)

where
\sigma^{\mu}
are the Pauli matrices. The size of this matrix is
2L^{2}\times 2L^{2}=4L^{4}
so it grows very quickly with lattice size. At the moment we are constructing the complete matrix and are calculating the determinant directly. This unfortunately allows for only rather small lattice sizes up to
24\times 24

[
22
]
. This is implemented in
QEDAction
class in module
neumc.physics.schwinger
.

4
Normalizing flow

Normalizing flow may be defined as the tuple of functions
[
4
,
32
,
33
]

\mathbb{R}^{D}\ni\bm{z}\longrightarrow(q_{pr}(\bm{z}),\bm{\varphi}(\bm{z}|\bm{\theta}))\in(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{D}),

(30)

where the function
q_{pr}(\bm{z})
is the probability density defining a
prior
distribution of a random variable
\bm{z}
. If
\bm{\varphi}(\bm{z}|\bm{\theta})
is a
bijection
and input
\bm{z}
is drawn from
q_{pr}(\bm{z})
then the output
\bm{\phi}
is distributed according to

q(\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta})=q_{z}(\bm{z}|\bm{\theta})\equiv q_{pr}(\bm{z})\left|J(\bm{z}|\bm{\theta})^{-1}\right|,\quad\bm{\phi}=\bm{\varphi}(\bm{z}|\bm{\theta}),

(31)

where

J(\bm{z}|\bm{\theta})=\det\left({\frac{\partial\mskip 0.0mu\bm{\varphi}(\bm{z}|\bm{\theta})}{\partial\bm{z}}}\right)

(32)

is the determinant of the Jacobian of
\bm{\varphi}(\bm{z}|\bm{\theta})
. The requirement for
\bm{\varphi}(\bm{z}|\bm{\theta})
to be a bijection is needed to ascertain that
\bm{z}
is the only solution of the equation
\bm{\phi}=\bm{\varphi}(\bm{z}|\bm{\theta})
. For practical reasons, normalizing flows are constructed in such a way that the Jacobian determinant is relatively easy to compute.

The
neumc.nf
package contains utilities to build general normalizing flows as well as some specific implementation.
In our package, the transformations
\bm{\varphi}
are implemented as classes that derive from
Transformation
abstract base class and must implement a
forward
function that takes a configuration
\bm{z}
and returns the transformed configuration
\bm{\phi}(\bm{z})
together with the logarithm of the Jacobian determinant of this transformation. Coupled with a prior, this enables to sample from the distribution
q(\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta})
defined by the flow as illustrated below

Very often flows are built by chaining together many transformations. To this end, we provide the

A feature of the normalizing flows is that we start from a prior configuration (noise)
\bm{z}
and then obtain both
\bm{\phi}

q(\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta})

\bm{\phi}

and use the relation

\left|\det\frac{\partial\bm{\varphi}(\bm{z})}{\partial\bm{z}}\right|^{-1}=\left|\det\frac{\partial\bm{\varphi}^{-1}(\bm{\phi})}{\partial\bm{\phi}}\right|

(33)

leading to

\log q(\bm{\phi})=\log q_{pr}(\bm{z})+\log\left|\det\frac{\partial\bm{\varphi}^{-1}(\bm{\phi})}{\partial\bm{\phi}}\right|,\quad\bm{z}=\bm{\varphi}^{-1}(\bm{\phi}).

4.1
Coupling layers

Currently, all flows in our package are based on coupling layers (see. e.g.
[
34
,
35
]
). The coupling layers rely on the subdivision of the lattice into active, frozen, and passive components. Only the active components are transformed while the frozen components are used to compute the parameters of the transformation, the passive components do not affect anything (the need for them will be explained in Section
4.6
)

\begin{split}\bm{\phi}^{\prime}_{act.}&=\bm{h}\left(\bm{\phi}_{act.}|\bm{\Theta}(\bm{\phi}_{frz.})\right)\\
\bm{\phi}^{\prime}_{frz.}&=\bm{\phi}_{frz.}\\
\bm{\phi}^{\prime}_{pas.}&=\bm{\phi}_{pas.}.\end{split}

(35)

If
\bm{h}
, called the
coupling function
, is a bijection, the whole transformation is also a bijection. The function
\bm{\Theta}(\bm{\phi}_{frz.})
is called a
conditioner
(we follow the notation from
[
34
]
) and is used to calculate the parameters of the transformation based on the frozen part of the configuration. This is actually the part where machine learning takes place, and it is usually implemented using neural networks. This function depends on the learnable parameters
\bm{\theta}
, usually the weights of the neural network, but we omit this dependence for clarity.

The resulting Jacobian matrix is block diagonal

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\immediate\immediate\immediate{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mskip 0.0mu\bm{h}\left(\bm{\phi}_{act.}|\bm{\Theta}(\bm{\phi}_{frz.})\right)}{\mathrm{d}\bm{\phi}_{act.}}}&\immediate\immediate\immediate{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mskip 0.0mu\bm{h}\left(\bm{\phi}_{act.}|\bm{\Theta}(\bm{\phi}_{frz.})\right)}{\mathrm{d}\bm{\phi}_{frz.}}}&0\\
0&\mathbb{I}&0\\
0&0&\mathbb{I}\end{array}\right).

(36)

So the Jacobian determinant is determined only by the derivatives with respect to
\bm{\phi}_{act.}
,

J=\left|\det{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mskip 0.0mu\bm{h}\left(\bm{\phi}_{act.}|\bm{\Theta}(\bm{\phi}_{frz.})\right)}{\mathrm{d}\bm{\phi}_{act.}}}\right|.

(37)

If the coupling function
\bm{h}
acts on
\bm{\phi}
pointwise

\bm{h}(\{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\ldots\}|\bm{\Theta})=\{h_{1}(\phi_{1}|\Theta_{1}),h_{2}(\phi_{2}|\Theta_{2}),\dots\},

(38)

where
\Theta_{i}
are components of the conditioner, then the Jacobian determinant is just the product of derivatives

J=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left|{\frac{\partial\mskip 0.0muh_{i}(\phi_{i}|\Theta_{i})}{\partial\phi_{i}}}\right|.

(39)

Very often the functions
h_{i}
are identical
h_{i}=h
and the only difference is in the parameters
\Theta_{i}
of the transformation.

Actually, in order to avoid numerical issues in our package, instead of working with probabilities, we use their logarithms. The formula (
39
) becomes

\log J=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\left|{\frac{\partial\mskip 0.0muh_{i}(\phi_{i}|\Theta_{i})}{\partial\phi_{i}}}\right|.

(40)

To achieve the required expressivity, many such layers are combined. The active, frozen, and passive parts are changed in each layer in some predefined fashion. In the simplest example, there is no passive part and active and frozen parts are interchanged in each consecutive layer.

The coupling flows are implemented in our package as
CouplingLayer
class. Instances of this class are initialized with a coupling function, a conditioner, and a mask. The masks, described in more detail in the next section, are used to subdivide the configurations into active, frozen, and passive parts. The method
_call
of this class is presented in the Listing
1
. It handles both forward and reverse transformations.

This function can actually receive as an input a sequence of tensors. The first element of this sequence is considered the configuration to be transformed. The remaining elements are used only by the conditioner to calculate the parameters of the transformation. The need for this will become apparent in Section
4.6
where we describe the flows for the gauge fields.

4.5
Equivariant flows

Often, if not always, the physical models possess some symmetry. Those symmetries can be represented as a group
\mathcal{G}
with elements acting on the configuration
\phi

\phi^{\prime}=g(\phi),\qquad g\in\mathcal{G}.

(58)

The group
\mathcal{G}
is a symmetry of the model if the target probability distribution is invariant under the action of its elements

p(g(\bm{\phi}))=p(\bm{\phi})\qquad g\in\mathcal{G}.

(59)

We will also require that

\left|\det{\frac{\partial\mskip 0.0mu\bm{g}(\bm{\phi})}{\partial\bm{\phi}}}\right|=1

(60)

which is automatically satisfied if
\bm{g}
is a unitary transformation.

It is advantageous for the distribution
q(\bm{\phi})
to also respect this symmetry. In principle, the model could learn the symmetry by itself during the training. In practice, however, it may be unable to sample all the symmetry sectors leading to broken symmetries and mode collapse. Also when the symmetry group is very big, this puts an additional burden on the model.

One way to construct the flows that respect the symmetry is by the use of equivariant flows. An equivariant transformation
\bm{h}
commutes with the symmetry group

\bm{h}(\bm{g}(\bm{\phi}_{act.})|\bm{\Theta}(\bm{g}(\bm{\phi}_{frz.})))=\bm{g}(\bm{h}(\bm{\phi}_{act.}|\bm{\Theta}(\bm{\phi}_{frz.}))),\qquad\bm{g}\in\mathcal{G}.

(61)

If we pair this transformation with an invariant prior

q_{pr}(\bm{g}(\bm{z}))=q_{pr}(\bm{z})

(62)

we obtain an invariant distribution
q(\bm{\phi})
.

The simplest example of such equivariance is the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry:
\bm{g}(\bm{\phi})=-\bm{\phi}
. In this case, the equivariance requires the
\bm{h}
to be an odd function:
\bm{h}(-\bm{\phi})=-\bm{\phi}
.

4.6
Gauge equivariant layers

A very important class of symmetries are the gauge symmetries (
23
) described in Section
3.3
.

We provide an equivariant gauge layer as defined in
[
5
]
. The idea is that when we want to transform a link
U_{\mu}(\bm{x})
we first form an open (untraced) loop

L(\bm{x})=U_{\mu}(\bm{x})S(\bm{x}+\hat{\mu},\bm{x})

(63)

where
S(\bm{x},\bm{y})
is some path that starts at
\bm{x}
and ends in
\bm{y}
. Except for the case of abelian gauge groups like
U(1)
, this is not an invariant and under gauge symmetry this loop transforms
as

L(\bm{x})\rightarrow g(\bm{x})L(\bm{x})g^{\dagger}(\bm{x})

(64)

so we need to find a transformation, called a kernel in
[
5
]
, that commutes with this symmetry

h(g(\bm{x})L(\bm{x})g^{\dagger}(\bm{x}))=g(\bm{x})h(L(\bm{x}))g^{\dagger}(\bm{x}).

(65)

At the moment, we provide only the implementation for the gauge group
U(1)
. As this is an abelian group, the relation above is trivially satisfied. For the general case of
SU(N)
, see
[
39
]
.

After transforming the loop, the link
U_{\mu}(\bm{x})
is modified in such a way that the loop value changes to
h(L(\bm{x}))
. This can be done by setting

U^{\prime}_{\mu}(\bm{x})=h(L(\bm{x}))S^{\dagger}(\bm{x}+\hat{\mu},\bm{x}).

(66)

Because

S(\bm{x}+\hat{\mu},\bm{x})=U_{\mu}^{\dagger}(\bm{x})L(\bm{x})

(67)

we can rewrite (
66
) as

U^{\prime}_{\mu}(\bm{x})=h(L(\bm{x}))L^{\dagger}(\bm{x})U_{\mu}(\bm{x}).

(68)

The general loop transformation
\bm{h}(\bm{L}|\bm{\Theta})
is implemented as a coupling layer. As in other coupling layers, the kernel
\bm{h}(\bm{L}|\bm{\Theta})
will transform only a subset of loops and use the rest to calculate the parameters of the transformation
\bm{\Theta}
. We will demand that
\bm{\Theta}
depend only on the quantities that are invariant under the gauge transform (
23
)

\bm{\Theta}(\bm{\phi}_{frz.})=\bm{\Theta}(\bm{I}(\bm{\phi}_{frz.}))

(69)

Here
\bm{I}
stands for any set of gauge invariant quantities constructed from fields
\bm{\phi}_{frz.}
. In general, they will be traces of some loops.

The subdivision of the configuration into active and frozen parts is slightly more involved than simple masks described previously.
First of all, we will have a set of active links that are to be transformed by the layer. Corresponding to that, we will have a subset of loops that will be used to change those links according to (
68
). We will call them
active
loops.
Those loops cannot contain other active links. Then there will be a set of loops that do contain the active links but were not used to transform them. We will call those loops
passive
, as they will not play any role in the transformation. And finally, there will be loops that do not contain any of the active links. Those loops will be called
frozen
and will be used to form gauge invariants
\bm{I}(\bm{\phi}_{frz.})
used to calculate the parameters
\bm{\Theta}
of the kernel
\bm{h}
.

The exact way of subdividing the lattice will depend crucially on the type of loops we use. The simplest loop is a plaquette (

5.5
Forward training

So far we have been training the models in the
self-supervised
way, that is the model was used to generate the data that was later used in training. To train the model a reverse Kullback-Leibler divergence (
2
) was used, as we were generating data from distribution
q(\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta})
.
Sometimes it may be advantageous to train the model on the data sampled from the target distribution
p(\bm{\phi})

[
42
]
. To this end the
forward
KL divergence can be used. The forward KL divergence is defined as

D_{KL}(p|q)=\int\text{d}\bm{\phi}\,p(\bm{\phi})\left(\log p(\bm{\phi})-\log q(\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta})\right).

(78)

and differs from (
2
) by the order of the arguments. Because distribution
p(\bm{\phi})
does not depend on parameters
\bm{\theta}
, minimizing
D_{KL}(p|q)
amounts to minimizing

-\int\text{d}\bm{\phi}\,p(\bm{\phi})\log q(\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta})\approx-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log q(\bm{\phi}^{i}|\bm{\theta}),\quad\bm{\phi}^{i}\sim p(\bm{\phi}^{i}).

(79)

This requires a reversible flow so that
q(\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta})
can be calculated according to (
34
).

Some rudimentary functionality to facilitate this kind of training is provided in the

ForwardGradientEstimator
class in module
neumc.training.forward
. As in the case of other gradient estimators, this class provides the
forward_pass
method. The input to this method is a batch of configurations generated from the distribution
p(\bm{\phi})
. The user is responsible for feeding the batched configurations to the estimator, which we do here using a dataloader provided in
PyTorch
. This is illustrated in the Listing

We would like to stress that the implementation contains several components that can be varied independently within reasonable constraints. First, we may vary the action. For example, we can obtain a pure gauge model by using the
U1GaugeAction
instead of
QEDAction
, but we may also use some form of improved action. However, including a larger set of loops may require changing the masking patterns.

Second, we may vary masking patterns and the loops used to calculate the invariants for the conditioner. However, we must be careful to match the masks with different loops.

Third, we may change the transformation used in the flow. In this example, we are limited to non-compact projection and circular splines, but the users may implement their own transformations.

Fourth, we may use different gradient estimators, however, the choice depends on the transformation. If the transformation does not have the
reverse

And of course, we can change the conditioner, which would usually amount to changing the neural network architecture inside it.

7
Summary and outlook

In this paper, we presented
neumc
a software package that allows users to implement 2D flow-based samplers for field theories. It is written using
PyTorch
library. The package is constructed using separate modules for flow transformation, prior sampling, and gradient estimator, such that each of them can be modified separately without interfering with the others. At this point, the available physical models are:
\phi^{4}
scalar theory and its vector version, XY model, and
U(1)
gauge theory. We implemented affine coupling layers, non-compact projection layers, and circular splines. In the case of gauge theory, we constructed layers such that they are gauge equivariant, which is crucial for training efficiency. Finally, we provide the implementation for three gradient estimators that are used in the literature in the context of field theory samplers.

There are several possible directions in which code can be developed further. First, sampling in more than two dimensions is required to cover more physically motivated models. Second, more complicated (in particular non-abelian) gauge groups should be implemented. Finally, a more efficient way for the treatment of fermions needs to be provided.
This is a subject of ongoing work.

To conclude, we believe that the
neumc
may be a useful tool for anyone interested in flow-based samplers.

Appendix C
Rational splines

First, it is important to note that we have a different spline for every different configuration
\bm{\varphi}
and each lattice site in those configurations. So for each batch, the splines are defined by a set of three tensors
\bm{x}
,
\bm{y}
and
\bm{s}
of dimension
(N_{b},L_{x},L_{y},N_{k})
each, where
N_{k}=K+1
is the number of knots.

Evaluating the
h(\varphi)
for arbitrary
\varphi\in[0,2\pi)
requires finding such
k
that
{x}^{(k)}\leq\varphi<{x}^{(k+1)}
. This is performed by a function
torch.searchsorted
. This function will take tensor
\bm{x}
of size
(N_{b},L_{x},L_{y},N_{k})

and the batch of configurations
\bm{\varphi}
"unsqueezed" to dimension
(N_{b},L_{x},L_{y},1)
. It would return a tensor
\bm{k}^{\prime}
that we "squeeze" back to the dimension
(N_{b},L_{x},L_{y})
such that

x_{n,i,j,(k^{\prime}_{n,i,j}-1)}\leq\varphi_{n,i,j}<x_{n,i,j,k^{\prime}_{n,i,j}}

(80)

Given the tensor
\bm{k}=\bm{k}^{\prime}-1
we have to use it to index the tensor
\bm{x}
. This can be done using the so-called
smart indexing
. This works as follows: given a tensor
\bm{x}
of dimension
(N_{b},L_{x},L_{y},N_{k})
and four integer tensors
(\bm{i}_{0},\bm{i}_{1},\bm{i}_{2},\bm{i}_{3})
of dimension
(N_{b},L_{x},L_{y})
we can create a new tensor

\bm{x}[\bm{i}_{0},\bm{i}_{1},\bm{i}_{2},\bm{i}_{3}]
, of dimension
(N_{b},L_{x},L_{y})
, such that

\begin{split}\bm{x}&[\bm{i}_{0},\bm{i}_{1},\bm{i}_{2},\bm{i}_{3}][i,j,l]\\
&=\bm{x}[\bm{i}_{0}[i,j,l],\bm{i}_{1}[i,j,l],\bm{i}_{2}[i,j,l],\bm{i}_{3}[i,j,l]]\end{split}

(81)

Comparing with (
80
) we see that we require
\bm{i}_{3}=\bm{k}
and

\bm{i}_{0}[i,j,l]=i,\;\bm{i}_{1}[i,j,l]=j,\;\bm{i}_{2}[i,j,l]=l

(82)
