Group B streptococcus-associated invasive infections in the adult, non-pregnant population; incidence, risk factors, and clinical presentations beyond borders
Authors: Almurtada Razok, Ali Alzarka, Maisa Ali, Hamad Abdel Hadi
Published: 2024

Introduction

Streptococcus agalactiae or group B streptococcus (GBS) is a gram-positive, beta-hemolytic bacterium. Based on its capsule, it was categorised into 10 serotypes. GBSs association with fatal peripartum infections was identified in 1938 and its clinical relevance derived from its association with neonatal sepsis, morbidity and mortality. In 1960s, more invasive infections in adults were being reported, indicating that GBS is an important pathogen in this population as well.1, 2 GBS is associated with multiple life-threatening infections such as blood stream infections, necrotising fasciitis and toxic shock syndrome. Clinical manifestations include skin and soft tissue infections, endocarditis, pneumonia, meningitis and osteomyelitis.3, 4, 5 Invasive GBS infections are higher in the elderly population according to studies conducted in the USA, UK and Europe.6 The epidemiology, prevalence and burden of GBS vary between countries, with scarce data available from the middle east. The literature is reviewed for research investigating GBS infections, with comparison of available data between USA, UK, Europe and the middle east.

Methods and materials

Databases searched for relevant articles included PubMed, Google Scholar and Scopus. Articles are from multiple regions including North America, Europe, UK, Asia and the middle east. Studies limited to the pregnant population were excluded. Keywords used for searching included Streptococcus agalactiae, prevalence, incidence, risk factors, invasive infections, the adult population, bacteremia, skin and soft tissue infections, meningitis, peritonitis, infective endocarditis, urinary tract infections, respiratory infections, and bone and joint infections.

Results and discussion

Although the incidence of GBS varies by location, the majority of studies showed increasing rate of invasive GBS infections in the adult non-pregnant population in the last two decades. In the US, the incidence of invasive GBS infections in 2008 was 8.1/100,000 population, which increased to 10.9 by 2016, with higher incidence in males and patients older than 80 years.7 In England, the annual incidence was 3.48/100,000 in the year 2016. The incidence was higher in the elderly as well. In Qatar, the incidence of blood stream infections rose from 1.48 to 2.09/100,000 from 2015 to 2019 with most cases occurring in females. The incidence was higher in young adults than the elderly.8 In Brussels, an upward trend in the annual incidence of invasive infections in non-pregnant adults was demonstrated, increasing from 3.7 to 8.2/100,000 population from 2009 to 2018. The majority of cases were in males and 47.2% occurred in patients older than 65 years. 88.7% of cases had one comorbid condition such as diabetes, renal disease, peripheral vascular disease, or malignancy.9

Conclusion

GBS is an emerging pathogen and has been associated with multiple invasive infections in the adult, non-pregnant population. Although complications have been declining, the incidence of invasive infections has been increasing in both developing and developed countries. Our review indicates that further research is needed in multiple locations, to determine the burden of invasive GBS infections and understand its significance in local and systemic infections.
