Prime graphical parking functions and strongly recurrent configurations of the Abelian sandpile model
Thomas Selig, Haoyue Zhu

Abstract
. This work investigates the duality between two discrete dynamical processes: parking functions, and the Abelian sandpile model (ASM). Specifically, we are interested in the extension of classical parking functions, called G G -parking functions, introduced by Postnikov and Shapiro in 2004. G G -parking functions are in bijection with recurrent configurations of the ASM on G G . In this work, we define a notion of prime G G -parking functions. These are parking functions that are in a sense “indecomposable”. Our notion extends the concept of primeness for classical parking functions, as well as the notion of prime ( p , q ) (p,q) -parking functions introduced by Armon et al. in recent work. We show that from the ASM perspective, prime G G -parking functions correspond to certain configurations of the ASM, which we call strongly recurrent . We study this new connection on a number of graph families, including wheel graphs, complete graphs, complete multi-partite graphs, and complete split graphs.



1.
Introduction and definitions

1.1.
Introduction

This paper investigates the connection between parking functions and sandpile models, two dynamical processes in Discrete Mathematics. We begin with an informal introduction to these two processes, with more formal definitions being given in Sections
1.2
and
1.3
(these also contain detailed surveys of the literature).

Parking functions were originally introduced by Konheim and Weiss
[
40
]
in their study of a hashing technique known as
linear probing
. Informally, consider the following problems. We have
n
cars which enter a street sequentially, and each car has a preferred parking spot in the street. What happens if a car’s preferred spot is already occupied when it enters, i.e. if a previous car has parked there? In hashing terminology, this is known as a
collision
. Konheim and Weiss’s classical parking model dictates that in such a case, the car simply drives forward to the next available parking spot. A (classical)
parking function
is an assignment of preferences which allows all cars to park through this process.

Since their introduction, parking functions have become a rich source of research topics in Mathematics and Theoretical Computer Science, with connections to a variety of different fields. In particular, there have been a number of parking functions considered and studied in the literature. Some of these are based on a modification of the collision rule. Others modify or generalise various characterisations of classical parking functions. A more detailed review of the existing literature around parking functions and their variations is given in Section
1.2
. In this paper, we are interested in so-called
graphical parking functions
, or
G
-parking functions. These extend the parking process to a given graph
G
.
G
-parking functions were introduced by Postnikov and Shapiro
[
42
]
, who showed a rich connection to another discrete dynamical process called the
Abelian sandpile model
(ASM) on
G
.

In the ASM, each vertex of
G
is assigned a number of grains of sand. At each step of the process, a vertex
v
of
G
is selected at random, and an extra grain added to it. If this causes the number of grains at
v
to exceed a certain threshold (usually the degree of
v
), we say that
v
is
unstable
. Unstable vertices
topple
, sending grains to their neighbours in the graph. This may cause some neighbours to become unstable, and we topple these in turn. A special vertex, the
sink
, allows excess grains to exit the system, and so this process eventually stabilises. Of central importance in ASM research are the so-called
recurrent
configurations of the model, which appear infinitely often in its long-time running. Section
1.3
gives more details on results relating to these recurrent configurations.

Notably, Postnikov and Shapiro
[
42
]
exhibited a straightforward bijection between recurrent configurations for the ASM on
G
and
G
-parking functions (see Theorem
1.9
). It is this duality between the ASM and parking functions that we explore further in this paper. More precisely, we will be interested in a notion of
primeness
for parking functions. In this context, prime parking functions are parking functions which cannot be “decomposed” into smaller parking functions. We extend the notion of primeness to
G
-parking functions, and connect prime
G
-parking functions to a subset of recurrent configurations for the ASM, which we refer to as
strongly recurrent
.

Our paper is organised as follows. In the remainder of this section, we formally introduce parking functions and their variations, and the Abelian sandpile model. We survey some of the relevant literature relating to these two processes. In Section
2
, we introduce the concepts of
prime
G
-parking functions
and
strongly recurrent configurations
, and show that these are in bijection with each other (Theorem
2.11
). In Section
3
, we study these concepts in more depth on specific families of graphs, obtaining various enumerative and bijective results. In particular, we will see that our notion of primeness extends the classical prime parking functions (see
[
55
, Exercise 5.49]
) and a more recent concept of prime
(p,q)
-parking functions introduced by Armon
et al.

[
3
]
. Finally, Section
4
summarises our main result and discusses possible future research directions.

Let us now fix some basic notation. We denote by
\mathbb{Z}
the set of integers, and
\mathbb{N}
the set of (strictly) positive integers. Throughout this paper,

denotes a positive integer, and we let

. We are interested in two processes related to graphs. Here, we consider graphs
G=(V,E)

, called the

. We sometimes write

for clarity (

a

. The set of non-sink vertices will be denoted

For two vertices

For simplicity, we usually just write

. Finally, for

for the

.

1.2.
Parking functions

We begin by formally introducing the notion of parking functions. Consider a one-way street with
n
parking spots labelled
1
to
n
, and
n
cars, also labelled
1
to
n
, which want to park in the street. For each
i\in[n]
, car
i
has a preferred parking spot
p_{i}\in[n]
, and we say that
p=(p_{1},\dots p_{n})
is a parking preference. Cars enter the street sequentially in order
1,\dots,n
, and first drive to find their preferred spots. If a car
i
’s preferred spot
p_{i}
is unoccupied when it enters the street, then car
i
parks there and is said to
occupy
spot
i
. Otherwise, if a car
i
’s preferred spot
p_{i}
has been occupied by a previous car
j<i
, then we say that a
collision
occurs.

In classical parking functions, collisions are resolved as follows. If a car
i
’s preferred spot
p_{i}
is already occupied when it enters the street, it drives on, and parks in the first unoccupied spot
k>p_{i}
(because the street is one-way cars can only drive forwards). If no such spot exists, car
i
directly exits the street, having failed to park. We say that
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
is a (classical)
parking function
if all
n
cars are able to park through this process, and denote
\mathrm{PF}_{n}
the set of parking functions with
n
cars and
n
spots.

Parking functions were originally introduced by Konheim and Weiss
[
40
]
to study hashing functions.
Since their introduction, they have been a rich research topic in Mathematics and Computer Science, with connections to various fields such as graph theory, hyperplane arrangements, discrete geometry, and the Abelian sandpile model
[
13
,
20
,
21
,
54
,
56
]
. The interested readers are referred to the excellent survey by Yan
[
57
]
. For our purposes, we recall the following well-known characterisations of parking functions.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\in[n]^{n}
be a parking preference. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

We have
p\in\mathrm{PF}_{n}
.

(2)

For any
i\in[n]
, we have
\mathop{\mathrm{inc}}\left(p\right)_{i}\leq i
, where
\mathop{\mathrm{inc}}\left(p\right):=\left(\mathop{\mathrm{inc}}\left(p\right)_{1},\dots,\mathop{\mathrm{inc}}\left(p\right)_{n}\right)
denotes the non-decreasing re-arrangement of
p
.

(3)

For any
i\in[n]
, we have
\left|\{j\in[n];\,p_{j}\leq i\}\right|\geq i
.

(4)

For any subset
S\subseteq[n]
, there exists
i\in S
such that
p_{i}\leq n+1-|S|
.

Proof.

Conditions (2) and (3) can be found for example in
[
57
, Section 1.1]
. Condition (4) is perhaps less commonly seen in the literature, but it is convenient to state it here to draw a direct comparison with the Abelian sandpile model, and particularly Dhar’s burning algorithm (see Section
1.3
) and
G
-parking functions (Definition
1.2
). Its equivalence to Condition (3) is straightforward. Indeed, if
S
is such that
p_{i}>n+1-|S|
for all
i\in S
, then

\left|\{j\in[n];\,p_{j}\leq n+1-|S|\}\right|\leq\left|[n]\setminus S\right|<n+1-|S|
, breaking Condition (3). Conversely, if
i
is such that
\left|\{j\in[n];\,p_{j}\leq i\}\right|<i
, then by construction
S:=\{j\in[n];\,p_{j}>i\}
breaks Condition (4), since we have
|S|>n-i
, i.e.
i\geq n+1-|S|
, so that for all
j\in S
, we have
p_{j}>i\geq n+1-|S|
.
∎

In recent years, a fruitful research direction has focused on defining and studying variations on the classical parking functions introduced above. These variations fall into a few different categories.

•

There are parking models which consider the classical process but with
m
cars and
n
spots. These are called
\mathbf{(m,n)}
-parking functions
when
m\leq n
(see
[
39
]
for a lovely bijection to spanning forests), or
defective parking functions
when
m<n
, since in this case at least
n-m
cars will always be unable to park (see
[
12
]
for elegant enumerative results).

•

There are parking models with cars of different sizes, called
parking assortments
or
parking sequences
depending on the precise collision rule
[
32
,
34
,
14
]
.

•

There are models which modify the collision rule, such as
Naples parking functions

[
16
,
18
]
or
pullback parking functions

[
33
]
which allow some backwards movement,
MVP parking functions

[
37
,
48
]
where priority is given to later cars in the sequence,
friendship parking functions

[
38
]
which impose additional conditions on where cars are allowed to park, and so on.

•

There are models which allow cars to have a preferred subset of spots to park in, rather than a single preference. These are called
subset parking functions

[
53
]
. See also
[
17
]
for an in-depth treatment of
interval parking functions
, a special case where each preference subset is a sub-interval of
[n]
, and
[
1
]
for generalisations of these.

•

Finally, there are models that are defined by modifying some of the characterisations of parking functions from Theorem
1.1
. For example,
vector parking functions
(or
\mathbf{u}
-parking functions)
[
52
,
58
]
modify Condition (2) by replacing “
i
” in the right-hand side of the inequality with a vector coordinate
u_{i}
.
Rational parking functions

[
4
,
35
,
41
]
correspond to the special case where
\mathbf{u}
is affine. The multidimensional
\mathbf{U}
-parking functions
[
51
,
52
]
, where
\mathbf{U}
is a collection of multidimensional vectors, further generalise these to higher dimensions.

Another variant in a similar spirit to vector parking functions are the
graphical parking functions
, or
G
-parking functions, introduced by Postnikov and Shapiro
[
42
]
. These generalise Condition (4) of Theorem
1.1
as follows.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
G=(\Gamma,s)
be a graph, with vertex set (resp. non-sink vertex set)
V
(resp.
\tilde{V}
). For
S\subseteq V
(here,
V=\tilde{V}\cup\{s\}
includes the sink), we denote
S^{c}:=V\setminus S
its complement in
V
. We say that a funciton
p:\tilde{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
is a
G
-parking function
if, for every non-empty subset
S\subseteq\tilde{V}
, there exists
v\in S
such that
p(v)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{S^{c}}}\left(v\right)
. We denote by
\mathop{\mathrm{PF}}\left(G\right)
the set of
G
-parking functions.

Remark 1.3
.

Usually,
G
-parking functions are defined as functions which take non-negative values (rather than strictly positive), with a strict inequality
p(v)<\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{S^{c}}}\left(v\right)
used. The definitions are obviously equivalent. Our preference for the above one is driven by the classical definition for parking functions taking values in
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
instead of
\{0,\dots,n-1\}
(see Proposition
1.6
), and to avoid carrying a “-1” decrement in the bijection from
G
-parking functions to recurrent configurations of the ASM (see Theorem
1.9
).

Remark 1.4
.

The set
\mathop{\mathrm{PF}}\left(G\right)
is always non-empty, since the constant function
\mathbf{1}:\tilde{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{N},v\mapsto 1
is always a
G
-parking functions. Indeed, first note that for any
S\subseteq\tilde{V}
, the complement
S^{c}
is non-empty (it contains the sink
s
). Now, since
G
is connected, for any subset
S\subseteq\tilde{V}
, there must exist an edge from
S
to
S^{c}
, say with end-points
v\in S
and
w\in S^{c}
. In particular, we have
\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{S^{c}}}\left(v\right)\geq 1
, and so
\mathbf{1}
is a
G
-parking function, as desired.

Remark 1.5
.

Another straightforward observation is that, if
p
is a
G
-parking function, then for all
v\in\tilde{V}
, we must have
p(v)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}}\left(v\right)
. We obtain this by simply taking
S=\{v\}
in this case.

We end this introductory section by explaining how this notion generalises that of classical parking functions.

Proposition 1.6
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
K_{n}^{0}
denote the complete graph with vertex set
[n]\cup\{0\}
, rooted at
0
. Then we have
\mathrm{PF}_{n}=\mathop{\mathrm{PF}}\left(K_{n}^{0}\right)
.

Proof.

In the graph
K_{n}^{0}
, for any subset
S\subseteq[n]
and vertex
v\in S
, we have
\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{S^{c}}}\left(v\right)=\left|S^{c}\right|=\left|[n]\cup\{0\}\right|-|S|=n+1-|S|
, and the result follows immediately from Definition
1.2
and Theorem
1.1
, Condition (4).
∎

1.3.
The Abelian sandpile model

In this section, we formally define the Abelian sandpile model, which is the second process we are interested in in this paper. The sandpile model was initially introduced by Bak, Tang and Wiesenfeld
[
6
,
7
]
as the model to study a phenomenon called
self-organised criticality
. Shortly after, Dhar
[
25
]
formalised and generalised it, naming it the
Abelian sandpile model
(ASM).

Let
G=(\Gamma,s)
be a graph with vertex set (resp. non-sink vertex set)
V
(resp.
\tilde{V}
). A (sandpile)
configuration
is a function
c:\tilde{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
, where we think of
c(v)
as representing the number of “grains of sand” at the vertex
v
. We define
\mathop{\mathrm{Config}}\left(G\right)
the set of all sandpile configurations on
G
. For
v\in\tilde{V}
and
c\in\mathop{\mathrm{Config}}\left(G\right)
, a vertex
v
is said to be
stable
in the configuration
c
if
c(v)<\mathop{\mathrm{deg}}\left(v\right)
. The configuration
c
is
stable
if all its vertices are stable, and we denote
\mathop{\mathrm{Stable}}\left(G\right)
the set of stable configurations on
G
. Finally, for
c\in\mathop{\mathrm{Config}}\left(G\right)
, we define
|c|:=\sum\limits_{v\in\tilde{V}}c(v)
to be the total number of grains in the configuration
c
.

Unstable vertices
topple
by sending one grain along each of their incident edges. Formally, if
v
is an unstable vertex in a configuration
c
, we define the
toppling operator at
v
, denoted
\mathrm{Topp}_{v}
, by:

(1)

\mathrm{Topp}_{v}(c):=c-\mathop{\mathrm{deg}}\left(v\right)\!\cdot\!\mathds{1}_{v}+\sum\limits_{w\in\tilde{V}}\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(vw\right)\!\cdot\!\mathds{1}_{w},

where
\mathds{1}_{w}
denotes the indicator function at
w
(i.e.
\mathds{1}_{w}(u)=1
if
u=w
and
0
otherwise). In words,
\mathrm{Topp}_{v}(c)
is obtained from the configuration
c
by removing
\mathop{\mathrm{deg}}\left(v\right)
grains at
v
, and re-distributing them to the neighbours of
v
according to the multiplicities of the edges
vw
.

Toppling an unstable vertex
v
may then cause some of its neighbours to become unstable, and we topple these in turn. Now note that by applying Equation (
1
), we have
|\mathrm{Topp}_{v}(c)|=|c|-\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(vs\right)
. As such, the total number of grains in a configuration strictly decreases when we topple a neighbour of the sink, and otherwise remains the same. We think of the sink as “absorbing” grains when we topple one of its neighbours, with these grains exiting the system.

From this, it is relatively straightforward to see that, starting from an unstable configuration
c
and toppling unstable vertices successively, we will eventually reach a stable configuration
c^{\prime}
. Furthermore, Dhar
[
25
]
showed that the stable configuration
c^{\prime}
reached through this process does not depend on the order in which vertices are toppled.
We write
c^{\prime}=\mathrm{Stab}(c)
and call it the
stabilisation
of
c
. Figure

. In this figure,

We then define a Markov chain on the set
\mathop{\mathrm{Stable}}\left(G\right)
as follows. Let
\mu:\tilde{V}\rightarrow[0,1]
be a probability distribution on the set of non-sink vertices
\tilde{V}
such that
\mu(v)>0
for all
v\in\tilde{V}
.
At each step of the Markov chain we add a grain at the vertex
v
with probability
\mu(v)
and stabilise the resulting configuration.
Formally the transition matrix
Q
is given by:

(2)

\forall\,c,c^{\prime}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Stable}}\left(G\right)\!,\,Q(c,c^{\prime})=\sum\limits_{v\in\tilde{V}}\mu(v)\!\cdot\!\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{Stab}\left(c+\mathds{1}_{v}\right)\,=\,c^{\prime}}.

A configuration is called
recurrent
if it appears infinitely often in the long-time running of this Markov chain. We denote by
\mathop{\mathrm{Rec}}\left(G\right)
the set of recurrent configurations on
G
. These recurrent configurations are of central importance in ASM research. A seminal result shows that the number of recurrent configurations for the ASM on a graph
G
is equal to the number of spanning trees of
G
. This can be proved algebraically using the Kirkhoff’s matrix-tree theorem (see e.g.
[

), or bijectively (see e.g.
[
9
]
or
[
19
]
). Dhar
[
25
]
showed that the set of recurrent configurations forms an Abelian group when equipped with the addition operation “vertex-pointwise addition and stablisation”. He named this the sandpile group of the graph.
This group, also known as the critical group, graph Jacobian, or Picard group, has been widely studied in algebraic graph and matroid theory. Its most celebrated contribution is perhaps its role in developing discrete Riemann-Roch and Abel-Jacobi theory on graphs
[
8
]
.
The structure of the sandpile group has also been explicitly computed on a variety of graph families, including complete graphs
[
21
]
, nearly-complete graphs (complete graphs with a cycle removed)
[

]
, wheel graphs
[

]
, Cayley graphs of the dihedral group

[

]
, and many more (see e.g.
[

]
and the references therein for a more complete list).

In enumerative and bijective combinatorics, a fruitful direction of recent ASM research has also focused on graph families, but instead of looking at the sandpile group, the focus has been on calculating the set of recurrent configurations via bijections to other more easily enumerated or generated combinatorial objects. The graph families that have been studied include complete graphs
[

]
(see also
[
46

), complete bipartite or multi-partite graphs with a dominating sink

, complete bipartite graphs where the sink is in one of the two components

, permutation graphs
[

, and so on. We will make use of some of these studies in Section

We now give three useful characterisations of recurrent configurations of the ASM. The first (Condition (2)) is in terms of so-called forbidden subconfigurations. For a configuration
c
on a graph
G
, we say that
F\subset\tilde{V}
is a
forbidden subconfiguration
for
c
if
F\neq\emptyset
and, for all
v\in F
, we have
c(v)<\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{F}}\left(v\right)
. In words, this means that the configuration
c
restricted to the induced subgraph
G[F]
is stable. The second and third characterisations (Conditions (3) and (4)) are popular equivalent framings of Dhar’s
burning criterion
. The three characterisations are part of the “folklore” of ASM research, see e.g.
[
26
, Section 6]
or
[
43
, Section 3]
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
G=(\Gamma,s)
be a graph with vertex set (resp. non-sink vertex set)
V
(resp.
\tilde{V}
), and
c\in\mathop{\mathrm{Stable}}\left(G\right)
a
stable
configuration on
G
. The following are equivalent.

(1)

We have
c\in\mathop{\mathrm{Rec}}\left(G\right)
.

(2)

There exists no forbidden subconfiguration for
c
.

(3)

We have
\mathrm{Stab}\Big(c+\sum\limits_{w\in\tilde{V}}\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(ws\right)\!\cdot\!\mathds{1}_{w}\Big)=c
.

(4)

There exists a permutation
v_{0}=s,v_{1},\dots,v_{|\tilde{V}|}
of all vertices in
V
such that, for all
i\geq 1
, we have
c(v_{i})\geq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V\setminus\{v_{0},\dots,v_{i-1}\}}}\left(v_{i}\right)
.

If
c
is recurrent, a sequence such as in Condition (4) is called a

for

.
It is sometimes convenient to think of the configuration
c+\sum\limits_{w\in\tilde{V}}\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(ws\right)\!\cdot\!\mathds{1}_{w}
in Condition (3) as the configuration

in which we have “toppled the sink”. In fact, if
c
is recurrent, the stabilisation of this configuration topples every vertex exactly once, following the order of any burning sequence. With this interpretation, configuration

is recurrent when, starting from
c
and toppling the sink, every vertex topples exactly once in the resulting stabilisation process, finally yielding the original configuration
c
.

Remark 1.8
.

It is straightforward to see that recurrent configurations are
non-negative
, i.e.
c(v)\geq 0
for all
v\in\tilde{V}
(otherwise if
c(v)<0
, then
\{v\}
is a forbidden subconfiguration for
c
). It is most common to define all configurations as being non-negative: this gives a more realistic meaning to the physical interpretation of
c(v)
representing the number of grains of sand at
v
. However, in this paper we will be interested in removing grains of sand from configurations (see Equation (
3
)), which may cause the configuration to take negative values at some vertices, hence our approach.

We end this section by recalling the following result, which establishes the key duality between
G
-parking functions and recurrent configurations for the ASM on
G

Theorem 1.9

(
[

Let

be a graph. Then the map

is a bijection from
\mathop{\mathrm{Rec}}\left(G\right)
to
\mathop{\mathrm{PF}}\left(G\right)
.

2.1.
Prime classical parking functions

We begin by recalling the notion of primeness for classical parking functions. We say that a (classical) parking function

If

A

2.3.
Strong recurrence for the ASM

It is well known, and straightforward to check, that recurrent configurations of the ASM are closed under grain addition. That is, if
c\in\mathop{\mathrm{Rec}}\left(G\right)
, then for any
v\in\tilde{V}(G)
such that
c(v)<\mathop{\mathrm{deg}}\left(v\right)-1
, we have
c+\mathds{1}_{v}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Rec}}\left(G\right)
, where
\mathds{1}_{v}
is the indicator function at
v
(i.e.,
\mathds{1}_{v}(w)=1
if
w=v
and
0
otherwise). In this section, we are interested in the reverse property: which recurrent configurations are closed under grain removal?

Our motivation for this stems from the so-called
minimal recurrent
configurations. A configuration
c\in\mathop{\mathrm{Config}}\left(G\right)
is called minimal recurrent if it is recurrent, and for all
v\in\tilde{V}(G)
, the configuration
c-\mathds{1}_{v}
(obtained from the configuration
c
by removing one grain at the vertex
v
) is
not
recurrent. Minimal recurrent configurations have been quite widely studied in the literature, and are in bijection with certain acyclic orientations of the underlying graph
G
(see e.g.
[
44
]
). They have proved to be a key tool in various combinatorial studies of the recurrent configurations on graph families, see for example
[
31
,
47
,
45
]
.

The notion of
strong recurrence
that we introduce in this paper is in some sense orthogonal to that of minimal recurrence. Strongly recurrent configurations will be configurations that remain recurrent after we remove many grains from them. We begin by formally defining the notion.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
G=(\Gamma,s)
be a graph with vertex set
V=\tilde{V}\cup\{s\}
. For a recurrent configuration
c\in\mathop{\mathrm{Rec}}\left(G\right)
, we set
V_{M}(c):=\{v\in N^{V}(s);\,c(v)\geq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}}\left(v\right)-\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(vs\right)\}
. For
v\in V_{M}(c)
, we define the configuration

(3)

c^{v-}:=c-\sum\limits_{w\in\tilde{V}\setminus\{v\}}\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(ws\right)\!\cdot\!\mathds{1}_{w}.

We say that
c
is
strongly recurrent
(SR) if for all
v\in V_{M}(c)
, we have
c^{v-}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Rec}}\left(G\right)
. We denote by
\mathop{\mathrm{SR}}\left(G\right)
the set of SR configurations on
G
.

In words, the transformation
c\leadsto c^{v-}
removes grains from all neighbours of the sink except
v
according to their multiplicity. For many graph families, such as complete graphs, wheel graphs, fan graphs, complete multi-partite graphs with a dominating sink, and so on, this just means removing exactly one grain from all vertices except
v
. Leaving
c
unchanged at
v
is dictated by Dhar’s burning criterion (Theorem
1.7
, Conditions (3) and (4)):
V_{M}(c)
is simply the set of vertices which are unstable in the configuration
c+\sum\limits_{w\in\tilde{V}}\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(ws\right)\!\cdot\!\mathds{1}_{w}
(equivalently, the set of possible values for
v_{1}
in a burning sequence). In particular, if a configuration
c^{\prime}
is obtained from
c
by removing grains,
c^{\prime}
can only be recurrent if at least one of these vertices remains unstable in
c^{\prime}+\sum\limits_{w\in\tilde{V}}\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(ws\right)\!\cdot\!\mathds{1}_{w}
, hence the need to leave
c
unchanged at some
v\in V_{M}(c)
. We summarise this discussion through the following useful lemma.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
c\in\mathop{\mathrm{SR}}\left(G\right)
be a strongly recurrent configuration, and
v\in V_{M}(c)
. Then we have
V_{M}\left(c^{v-}\right)=\{v\}
.

Proof.

Since
c^{v-}(v)=c(v)
, and
v\in V_{M}(c)
, we have
v\in V_{M}\left(c^{v-}\right)
. Now if
w\neq v
, we have
c^{v-}(w)=c(v)-\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(vs\right)
, so that
c^{v-}(w)+\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(vs\right)=c(v)<\mathop{\mathrm{deg}}\left(v\right)
(since
c
is stable), i.e.
w\notin V_{M}\left(c^{v-}\right)
. This completes the proof.
∎

.

It is natural to ask what happens if we change the “for all” quantifier in Definition

with “there exists”, i.e. if we consider a recurrent configuration

to be strongly recurrent when there

\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
where
all

is also a rooted acyclic orientation of

for all

. We wish to show that
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
is compatible with
c_{*}^{v-}
. First, note that since
v,w\in V_{M}\left(c_{*}\right)
, the vertices

and
w
are both neighbours of the sink

in
G
, i.e.
\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(vs\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(ws\right)=1
. Therefore we have
c_{*}^{v-}=c_{*}^{w-}+\mathds{1}_{v}-\mathds{1}_{w}
.

By preceding remarks, it is sufficient to check the compatibility condition (
4
) for
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
at
v
and
w
. For
v
, we note that since

, we have
c_{*}^{v-}\geq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}}\left(v\right)-\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(vs\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}}\left(v\right)-1
.
Moreover, since

, we have
\mathrm{in}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}(v)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}}\left(v\right)-1
, and the compatibility condition is ensured at

. Now note that in the transformation from
\mathcal{O}
to
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
, at least one incoming edge at

\mathrm{in}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}(w)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{in}^{\mathcal{O}}}\left(w\right)-1

i.e. the compatibility condition holds at

is compatible with

, so that

is indeed recurrent. This concludes the proof.

2.4.
The main result

We now state and prove the main result of this paper.

Theorem 2.11
.

For any graph
G
, the map
c\mapsto\mathrm{\mathbf{deg}}-c
is a bijection from
\mathop{\mathrm{SR}}\left(G\right)
to
\mathop{\mathrm{PPF}}\left(G\right)
.

Proof.

The map
c\mapsto\mathrm{\mathbf{deg}}-c
is clearly bijective (it is an involution). It therefore suffices to show that
c
is strongly recurrent if and only if
p(c):=\mathrm{\mathbf{deg}}-c
is a prime
G
-parking function.

First, suppose that
c\in\mathop{\mathrm{Rec}}\left(G\right)
is
not
strongly recurrent. Then by definition there exists
v\in\tilde{V}
such that
c(v)+\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(vs\right)\geq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V}}\left(v\right)
(i.e.,
v\in V_{M}(c)
), but
c^{v-}\notin\mathop{\mathrm{Rec}}\left(G\right)
. Let
F\subseteq\tilde{V}
be a maximal forbidden sub-configuration for
c^{v-}
, i.e.,
c^{v-}(w)<\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{F}}\left(w\right)
for all
w\in F
. We claim that we have
v\notin F
, so
A:=\tilde{V}\setminus F\neq\emptyset
and is therefore a proper subset of
\tilde{V}
. Indeed, we have:

\displaystyle c^{v-}(v)=c(v)

\displaystyle\geq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V}}\left(v\right)-\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(vs\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{\tilde{V}}}\left(v\right)\geq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{F}}\left(v\right),

so
v\notin F
, as desired. We now show that
p:=\mathrm{\mathbf{deg}}-c
is decomposable with respect to the partition
(A,F)
.

By maximality of

, for
c^{v-}
we can “burn down to
F
” in Dhar’s burning algorithm. That is, starting from
c^{v-}+\sum\limits_{w\in\tilde{V}}\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(ws\right)\!\cdot\!\mathds{1}_{w}
, we can topple all vertices in
A
in some order. Since
c
is obtained from
c^{v-}
through adding grains, this remains a legal toppling sequence for
c+\sum\limits_{w\in\tilde{V}}\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(ws\right)\!\cdot\!\mathds{1}_{w}
(albeit one that does not result in stabilisation yet). In other words, there exists a sequence
v_{0}=s,v_{1}=v,\ldots,v_{|A|}
such that
A=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{|A|}\}
, and
c(v_{i})\geq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V\setminus\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{i-1}\}}}\left(v_{i}\right)
for all
1\leq i\leq|A|
.

Now consider
c^{A}:=\mathrm{\mathbf{deg}}^{A\cup\{s\}}-p^{A}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Config}}\left(G^{A}\right)
, and fix
w\in A
. Since
c
is stable, we have
c(w)<\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V}}\left(w\right)
. Recall that
c(w)=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V}}\left(w\right)-p(w)
, so that:

\displaystyle c^{A}(w)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{A\cup\{s\}}}\left(w\right)-p^{A}(w)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{A\cup\{s\}}}\left(w\right)-p(w)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V}}\left(w\right)-p(w)-\left(\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V}}\left(w\right)-\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{A\cup\{s\}}}\left(w\right)\right)

\displaystyle=c(w)-\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{F}}\left(w\right)<\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V}}\left(w\right)-\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{F}}\left(w\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{A\cup\{s\}}}\left(w\right),

which shows that
c^{A}
is stable on
G^{A}
. Now by construction, for
i=1,\dots,|A|
, we have:

c^{A}(v_{i})=c(v_{i})-\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{F}}\left(v_{i}\right)\geq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V\setminus\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{i-1}\}}}\left(v_{i}\right)-\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{F}}\left(v_{i}\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{A\cup\{s\}\backslash\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{i-1}\}}}\left(v_{i}\right),

so that
c^{A}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Rec}}\left(G^{A}\right)
by Dhar’s burning criterion (Theorem
1.7
, Condition (4)). Theorem
1.9
then implies that
p^{A}\in\mathop{\mathrm{PF}}\left(G^{A}\right)
.

We now consider
p^{F}:=p-\mathrm{\mathbf{deg}}^{A}
. Fix
w\in F
. Recall that
F
is a forbidden subconfiguration for
c^{v-}
, and that
v\notin F
, so that
c^{v-}(w)=c(w)-\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(ws\right)<\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{F}}\left(w\right)
, i.e.
c(w)<\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{F\cup\{s\}}}\left(w\right)
. Since
p(w)=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V}}\left(w\right)-c(w)
, this yields:

\displaystyle p^{F}(w)

\displaystyle=p(w)-\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{A}}\left(w\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V}}\left(w\right)-c(w)-\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{A}}\left(w\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{F\cup\{s\}}}\left(w\right)-c(w)>0.

Finally, for the parking function
p\in\mathop{\mathrm{PF}}\left(G\right)
, we have constructed an ordered set partition
(A,F)
of
\tilde{V}
such that
p^{A}
is a
G^{A}
-parking function and
p^{F}(w)>0
for all
w\in F
. From Proposition
2.4
, we deduce that
p
is decomposable with respect to
(A,F)
, i.e. that

is not prime, as desired.

We now show the converse. Suppose
p\in\mathop{\mathrm{PF}}\left(G\right)
is
not
prime, let
(A,B)
be a partition of
\tilde{V}
with respect to which
p
is decomposable, i.e.
p^{A}\in\mathop{\mathrm{PF}}\left(G^{A}\right)
and
p^{B}\in\mathop{\mathrm{PF}}\left(G^{B}\right)
. Let
c:=\mathrm{\mathbf{deg}}-p\in\mathop{\mathrm{Rec}}\left(G\right)
be the corresponding sandpile configuration. We wish to show that
c
is not strongly recurrent, i.e. that there exists
v\in V_{M}(c)
such that
c^{v-}
is not recurrent.

We first claim that
V_{M}(c)\cap A\neq\emptyset
. Define
c^{A}:=\mathrm{\mathbf{deg}}^{A\cup\{s\}}-p^{A}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Config}}\left(G^{A}\right)
. Since
p^{A}
is a
G^{A}
-parking function,
c^{A}
is recurrent. In particular, there exists

(for this, choose

to be any vertex

in a burning sequence for

1.7
, Condition (4)), so that:

\displaystyle c(v)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V}}\left(v\right)-p(v)=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V}}\left(v\right)-p^{A}(v)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{A\cup\{s\}}}\left(v\right)-p^{A}(v)+\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{B}}\left(v\right)

\displaystyle=c^{A}(v)+\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{B}}\left(v\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{A\cup\{s\}}}\left(v\right)-\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(vs\right)+\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{B}}\left(v\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V}}\left(v\right)-\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(vs\right),

which implies

, as desired.

Finally, we want to show that

. For all

, we have

, and by using

and

, we obtain:

\displaystyle c^{v-}(w)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V}}\left(w\right)-\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(ws\right)-p(w)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V}}\left(w\right)-\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(ws\right)-\left(p^{B}(w)+\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{A}}\left(w\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{B\cup\{s\}}}\left(w\right)-\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(ws\right)-p^{B}(w)

\displaystyle<\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{B\cup\{s\}}}\left(w\right)-\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(ws\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{B}}\left(w\right),

Since this holds for all

, this means exactly that

By transferring the definition of strong recurrence to parking functions via this bijection, we get the following straightforward consequence, which gives an alternate characterisation of prime
G
-parking functions.

Corollary 2.12
.

Let
p\in\mathop{\mathrm{PF}}\left(G\right)
, and define
V_{M}(p):=\{v\in\tilde{V};\,p(v)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(vs\right)\}
. Then
p
is prime if and only if, for all
v\in V_{M}(p)
, we have
p^{v+}:=p+\sum\limits_{w\in\tilde{V}\setminus\{v\}}\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(ws\right)\!\cdot\!\mathds{1}_{w}\in\mathop{\mathrm{PF}}\left(G\right)
.

3.
Applications to graph families

In this section, we study parking functions and strongly recurrent configurations on graph families with high degrees of symmetry. We begin with a useful technical lemma. For a graph
G
and vertex
v\in\tilde{V}(G)
, the map
\mathrm{\mathbf{Del}}_{v}
maps a function
p:\tilde{V}(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
to the function
p^{\prime}:\tilde{V}(G\setminus\{v\})\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
, where
p^{\prime}
is simply the restriction of
p
to vertices of
G\setminus\{v\}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G=(\Gamma,s)
be a graph, and
v\in\tilde{V}(G)
be a non-sink vertex such that for all
w\neq s,v
, we have
\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(ws\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(wv\right)
. Let
p\in\mathop{\mathrm{PPF}}\left(G\right)
be a prime
G
-parking function such that
v\in V_{M}(p)
. Then we have
\mathrm{\mathbf{Del}}_{v}(p)\in\mathop{\mathrm{PF}}\left(G\setminus\{v\}\right)
.

Proof.

To simplify notation, we write
V=V(G)
and
\tilde{V}=\tilde{V}(G)
. We then use
′
to indicate removal of the vertex
v
, i.e.
G^{\prime}=G\setminus\{v\}
,
V^{\prime}=V(G^{\prime})=V\setminus\{v\}
,
\tilde{V}^{\prime}=V^{\prime}\setminus\{s\}
, and
p^{\prime}=\mathrm{\mathbf{Del}}_{v}(p)
. We wish to show that
p^{\prime}
is a
G^{\prime}
-parking function. Let
S\subseteq\tilde{V}^{\prime}
. We want to find
w\in S
such that
p^{\prime}(w)=p(w)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V^{\prime}\setminus S}}\left(w\right)
. But
p
is a prime
G
-parking function, so by Corollary
2.12
we have
p^{v+}\in\mathop{\mathrm{PF}}\left(G\right)
. Since
S\subseteq\tilde{V}
, there exists
w\in S
such that
p^{v+}(w)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V\setminus S}}\left(w\right)
. We re-write this as
p(w)+\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(ws\right)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V^{\prime}\cup\{v\}\setminus S}}\left(w\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V^{\prime}\setminus S}}\left(w\right)+\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(wv\right)
, and the result immediately follows from the assumption of the lemma.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

The condition on
v
in Lemma
3.1
may seem a little non-intuitive, but it holds for example when the transposition
(vs)
is an isomorphism of

Theorem 3.12
.

There is a bijection from
\mathop{\mathrm{PPF}^{\mathrm{inc}}}\left(K_{p*,q}\right)
to
\mathop{\mathrm{PF}^{\mathrm{inc}}}\left(K_{(p-1)*,q}\right)
. In particular, we have
\left|\mathop{\mathrm{PPF}^{\mathrm{inc}}}\left(K_{p*,q}\right)\right|=\left|\mathop{\mathrm{PF}^{\mathrm{inc}}}\left(K_{(p-1)*,q}\right)\right|=\frac{1}{p+q-1}\binom{p+q-1}{p}\binom{p+q-1}{p-1}
.

Proof.

We first claim that if
p\in\mathop{\mathrm{PPF}}\left(K_{p*,q}\right)
is a prime parking function, then there exists
v\in P
such that
p(v)=1
. For this, first choose some
u\in Q
such that
p(u)=1
(such a vertex always exists by taking
S=\tilde{V}=V\setminus\{v_{0}^{p}\}
in Definition
1.2
). Now by definition, we have
u\in V_{M}(p)
so that
p^{u+}\in\mathop{\mathrm{PF}}\left(K_{p*,q}\right)
by Corollary
2.12
. By definition, we have
p^{u+}=p+\sum\limits_{w\in Q\setminus\{u\}}\mathds{1}_{w}
, and in particular
p^{u+}(w)>1
for all
w\in Q\setminus\{u\}
. We then take
S=V\setminus\{v_{0}^{p},u\}
in Definition
1.2
: there exists
v\in S
such that
p^{u+}(v)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{\{v_{0}^{p},u\}}}\left(v\right)
. However, by preceding remarks we cannot have
v\in Q
, since otherwise we would have
1<p^{u+}(v)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{\{v_{0}^{p},u\}}}\left(v\right)=1
, a contradiction. Therefore there exists
v\in P
such that
1\leq p(v)=p^{u+}(v)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{\{v_{0}^{p},u\}}}\left(v\right)=1
, and the claim is proved.

In particular, if
p\in\mathop{\mathrm{PPF}^{\mathrm{inc}}}\left(K_{p*,q}\right)
is an increasing prime parking function, we have
p(v_{1}^{p})=1
. To simplify notation and avoid relabelling vertices, we consider the graph
K_{(p-1)*,q}
to simply be the graph
K_{p*,q}
with
v_{1}^{p}
deleted. As in the proof of Lemma
3.1
we will write
V
, resp.
V^{\prime}
, for the set of vertices of
K_{p*,q}
, resp.
K_{(p-1)*,q}
, using the
\tilde{\square}
notation to denote non-sink vertices. We then claim that the deletion map
\mathrm{\mathbf{Del}}_{v_{1}^{p}}
is a bijection from
\mathop{\mathrm{PPF}^{\mathrm{inc}}}\left(K_{p*,q}\right)
to
\mathop{\mathrm{PF}^{\mathrm{inc}}}\left(K_{(p-1)*,q}\right)
.

First, let us show that
\mathrm{\mathbf{Del}}_{v_{1}^{p}}\left(\mathop{\mathrm{PPF}^{\mathrm{inc}}}\left(K_{p*,q}\right)\right)\subseteq\mathop{\mathrm{PF}^{\mathrm{inc}}}\left(K_{(p-1)*,q}\right)
. Note that we cannot apply Lemma
3.1
in this case since
v_{1}^{p}\notin V_{M}(p)
(we have
\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(v_{1}^{p}v_{0}^{p}\right)=0
).
Define
p^{\prime}:=\mathrm{\mathbf{Del}}_{v_{1}^{p}}(p)
, and let
S\subseteq\tilde{V}^{\prime}
be non-empty. We write
S=S_{P}\cup S_{Q}
with

and
S_{Q}\subseteq\{v_{1}^{q},\dots,v_{q}^{q}\}
.
We wish to show that there exists
v\in S
such that
p^{\prime}(v)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V^{\prime}\setminus S}}\left(v\right)
. As previously, since
p
is a parking function, we must have
p(v_{1}^{q})=p^{\prime}(v_{1}^{q})=1
(using the fact that
p
is increasing). In particular, since the sink vertex is always in
V^{\prime}\setminus S
, if
S_{Q}
contains
v_{1}^{q}
, we may simply choose
v=v_{1}^{q}
.

We therefore assume that
v_{1}^{q}\notin S_{Q}
. Now since
p
is prime, we have
p^{v_{1}^{q}+}\in\mathop{\mathrm{PF}^{\mathrm{inc}}}\left(K_{p*,q}\right)
by Corollary
2.12
.
Note that we have
p^{v_{1}^{q}+}=p+\sum\limits_{j=2}^{q}\mathds{1}_{v_{j}^{q}}
. Since

, by Definition
1.2
, there exists
v\in S
such that

.
Now if

, we have

.
Similarly, if

, then we have
p^{v+}(v)=p(v)=1=\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(vv_{0}^{c}\right)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V\setminus S}}\left(v\right)
, and there is nothing more to show. We therefore assume that
v\notin S
, i.e. that
S\subseteq\tilde{V}^{\prime}
is a subset of non-sink vertices in
S_{(m-1)*,n}
. Then since
p^{\prime}
is a
S_{(m-1)*,n}
-parking function, there exists
w\in S
such that
p^{\prime}(w)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V^{\prime}\setminus S}}\left(w\right)
, yielding
p^{v+}(w)=p(w)+1=p^{\prime}(w)+1\leq\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V^{\prime}\setminus S}}\left(w\right)+1=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V^{\prime}\setminus S}}\left(w\right)+\mathop{\mathrm{mult}}\left(wv_{1}^{c}\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{deg}^{V\setminus S}}\left(w\right)
, as desired. This completes the proof that
\mathrm{\mathbf{Del}}_{v_{1}^{c}}
is the desired bijection.

The enumeration result then follows from
[
28
, Corollary 3.4]
and the bijection between (increasing)
S_{(m-1)*,n}
-parking functions and (decreasing) recurrent configurations of the ASM on
S_{(m-1)*,n}
.
∎

4.
Conclusion and future work

The main goal of our paper was to introduce a notion of
primeness
of
G
-parking functions for general graphs
G
. We define prime
G
-parking functions as parking functions that cannot be decomposed in a certain sense (Definition
2.2
). Our notion addresses an open problem from
[
3
]
by generalising the concepts of primeness for classical parking functions (Proposition
3.4
) and for
(p,q)
-parking functions (Theorem
3.11
).

We then studied this new concept of primeness under the well-known duality between
G
-parking functions and recurrent configurations for the Abelian sandpile model (ASM) on
G
(see Theorem
1.9
in this paper). We showed that prime
G
-parking functions correspond to so-caled
strongly recurrent
configurations for the ASM on
G
(Theorem
2.11
). These are recurrent configurations which remain recurrent after removing grains of sand from certain vertices (Definition
2.7
).

Finally, we studied the sets of prime
G
-parking functions (equivalently, of strongly recurrent configurations for the ASM on
G
), in cases where
G
belongs to graph families with high degrees of symmetry. The cases considered include wheel graphs (Proposition
3.3
), complete bipartite graphs (Theorem
3.12
) and complete split graphs (Theorem
3.13
). Our results include both characterisations and enumerations of prime parking functions on these graph families.

One potentially interesting open problem is that of uniqueness of prime decompositions. In Remark
2.6
we noted that prime decompositions of
G
-parking functions are not necessarily unique. However, they are known to be unique (up to symmetry) for classical parking functions (equivalently, when
G=K_{n}^{0}
) and for
(p,q)
-parking functions (equivalently, when
G=K_{p,q}^{0}
, see
[
3
, Section 4.1]
).

Problem 4.1
.

Under what conditions on the graph
G
do all
G
-parking functions have a unique prime decomposition (up to the symmetries of
G
)?

In terms of future research, Armon
et al.

[
3
]
noted of generalised prime parking functions that “Much remains to be investigated in this area”. We echo their thoughts, in particular in terms of defining and studying concepts of primeness for some of the variations of parking functions which have been widely studied in the literature (see the survey in Section
1.2
of this paper). It would also be interesting to further the combinatorial study of prime
G
-parking functions on graph families that was instigated in this paper.
