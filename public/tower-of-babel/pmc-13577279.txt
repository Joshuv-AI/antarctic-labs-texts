Occupational heatstroke-induced acute kidney injury: a narrative review of mechanisms, biomarkers, and the timing of blood purification initiation
Authors: Sisi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOccupational heatstroke frequently causes acute kidney injury (AKI). Myoglobin, released by exertional rhabdomyolysis, is the central nephrotoxic amplifier and may be physically removable by extracorporeal techniques. Yet the trials governing purification timing enrolled general intensive care populations whose injury biology differs. We aimed to integrate heatstroke-specific renal pathophysiology, novel AKI biomarker performance, and evidence on extracorporeal modalities. We then mapped the temporal cascade onto a biomarker-augmented framework for initiating blood purification during a narrative State-of-the-Art Review. Ten search blocks were run in PubMed/MEDLINE to 16 August 2026, yielding 16,443 unique records. Eligible sources reported human or animal data, consensus statements, or mechanistic evidence bearing on three questions: (i) how does heat injure kidneys? (ii) which biomarkers detect injury? and (iii) when blood purification should begin? Records without an English-language abstract or without retrievable full text were excluded and, ultimately, 95 discreate sources were retained. Four pathways operate on distinct timescales: direct thermal cytotoxicity, myoglobin-driven ferroptosis, inflammatory-coagulation amplification, and gut-derived endotoxemia. L-FABP has the only prospective heat-specific human data; [TIMP-2]·[IGFBP7] has the strongest critical care record but none in heatstroke. No biomarker is validated for predicting later chronic kidney disease in this population. No modality has been shown superior in a heatstroke-specific population, and only one clinical study reports outcomes in exertional heatstroke. We propose a provisional three-tier initiation framework anchored to myoglobin concentration and tubular injury biomarkers rather than creatinine alone. It is a structured hypothesis rather than validated guidance, and prospective biomarker-stratified trials in occupational settings are needed.Keywords: Heatstroke, acute kidney injury, blood purification, myoglobin, biomarkers, occupational heat exposure

Introduction

Heatwaves are growing more frequent, more intense, and longer-lasting as the planet warms, with direct consequences for human health [1]. Dehydration, systemic inflammation, and circulatory stress are hallmarks of severe heat illness, and all three converge in the kidney. That convergence makes the kidney a particularly vulnerable target of heat-related injury [2,3]. Exertional heatstroke (EHS), defined by a core body temperature exceeding 40 °C with concurrent central nervous system dysfunction, is the most severe form. It disproportionately strikes occupational cohorts: agricultural workers, construction laborers, military personnel, and firefighters, who perform sustained physical work in hot, non-compensable environments [4].

Heatstroke precipitates multi-organ dysfunction, and acute kidney injury (AKI) ranks among the most common and prognostically significant organ failures. AKI complicates an estimated 25–50% of exertional heatstroke cases in hospital-based series. Estimates vary several-fold with the intensity of case ascertainment, as Section 3 sets out. AKI drives short-term mortality and can also open a pathway to chronic kidney disease in survivors [5,6]. The pathophysiology is multifactorial. Exertional rhabdomyolysis releases myoglobin into the circulation. This, together with systemic inflammation, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and direct thermal cytotoxicity, produces tubular injury through oxidative damage, intratubular cast formation, and renal vasoconstriction [7,8]. Importantly, these processes begin hours before serum creatinine rises. The result is a narrow therapeutic window.

Advances in AKI biomarker science and blood purification technology have expanded the options available to clinicians. Novel urinary biomarkers can detect tubular stress before functional criteria are met. The cell-cycle arrest markers TIMP-2 and IGFBP7, commercialized as NephroCheck, identify tubular stress 12–24 h ahead of a creatinine rise. This offers a potential trigger for biomarker-informed intervention [9,10]. In parallel, extracorporeal modalities for myoglobin and cytokine removal have evolved beyond conventional continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) to encompass high-cutoff membranes, hemoadsorption (e.g. CytoSorb), and therapeutic plasma exchange [11]. A small clinical series demonstrated that early continuous venovenous hemofiltration (CVVH) in heatstroke patients achieved rapid core cooling and effective myoglobin clearance, with universal survival in the treated cohort [12]. Yet the central clinical question, when to initiate blood purification in heatstroke-related AKI, remains unresolved. Landmark randomized trials of RRT initiation timing in general ICU populations have yielded contradictory results. The single-center ELAIN trial reported a mortality benefit with early initiation. The multicenter AKIKI and STARRT-AKI trials found no advantage of an accelerated strategy over a conservative, watchful-waiting approach [13–15]. These trials, however, enrolled heterogeneous AKI populations and included few, if any, patients with heatstroke. In heatstroke, the dominant injury mechanism is myoglobinuric tubular toxicity, which differs fundamentally from the hemodynamic and septic etiologies that predominated in those cohorts. An international Delphi consensus recently proposed myoglobin- and creatine kinase–based thresholds for initiating hemoadsorption in severe rhabdomyolysis. These recommendations remain expert opinion and are not specifically tailored to heatstroke [16].

We address this evidence gap by integrating three dimensions that have been discussed in isolation. The first is the advancing understanding of heatstroke-induced renal pathophysiology. The second is the emerging role of AKI biomarkers for risk stratification. The third is the unresolved controversy over the timing and modality of blood purification initiation. Unlike prior reviews that have surveyed heatstroke-associated multi-organ injury broadly or examined RRT timing in undifferentiated AKI, we focus specifically on occupational heatstroke-related AKI. We map the pathophysiological timeline onto a practical, biomarker-augmented decision framework for blood purification initiation. The occupational context adds complexity that existing ICU-based evidence does not capture: recurrent heat exposure, subclinical dehydration, and limited on-site monitoring. By keeping that context in view, we aim to provide emergency physicians, intensivists, and nephrologists with a synthesis that is both mechanistically grounded and clinically actionable. Two features of this literature shaped the choice of review type. The evidence is small, heterogeneous in design, and dominated by case series and retrospective cohorts, so pooling would misrepresent its strength. The questions asked here are also partly conceptual: how mechanistic timing, biomarker kinetics, and treatment thresholds should be aligned. We therefore conducted a narrative State-of-the-Art Review, structured around a documented literature search, rather than a systematic review or meta-analysis.

Methods: Literature search and selection

This article is a narrative State-of-the-Art Review. It does not follow a systematic review protocol, was not prospectively registered, and includes no formal risk-of-bias assessment or quantitative synthesis. The search is reported here so that readers can judge how the evidence base was assembled and can reproduce it.

Searches were first run in PubMed/MEDLINE in July 2026 and updated on 16 August 2026 in response to peer review. Ten search blocks were used. They covered heat illness and kidney injury, occupational and exertional heat exposure, and rhabdomyolysis with extracorporeal myoglobin removal. Further blocks covered kidney injury biomarkers, the timing of renal replacement therapy, and heatstroke cell-death pathways. Three more addressed biomarker validation in general critical care, chronic kidney disease of nontraditional origin, and climate-related kidney risk. A final block covered the diagnosis and management of rhabdomyolysis. No lower date limit was applied, and records dated after 16 August 2026 were excluded. The complete strategies appear in Supplementary Table S1. The blocks returned 17,464 records, or 16,443 unique records after deduplication.

Titles and abstracts were screened by a single author, and full texts were retrieved when relevance could not be settled from the abstract. Sources were eligible if they reported human or animal data, consensus statements, or mechanistic evidence bearing on one of three questions: How does heat exposure injure the kidney? Which biomarkers detect that injury? When should extracorporeal blood purification begin? Studies of heat-related illness without renal outcomes were retained only when they informed mechanism. Records were excluded if no English-language abstract was available, or if the full text could not be obtained, because claim-level verification was then impossible. One included source is published in French and was assessed from its English abstract only.

Reference lists of retrieved articles and of the relevant consensus statements were hand-searched. That route identified several sources, including the propensity-matched hemoadsorption analysis discussed in Section 6. Eighty-seven of the 95 cited sources are retrievable from the ten search blocks. The remaining eight came from hand-searching, citation tracing, or targeted searches run in response to peer review. Epidemiology of occupational heatstroke-related AKI

Occupational heatstroke-related AKI occupies a clinical intersection that remains under-recognized in both nephrology and occupational medicine. Heatstroke is classified into classic (passive) and exertional (EHS) forms. EHS is defined by a core body temperature exceeding 40 °C accompanied by central nervous system dysfunction during or immediately following strenuous physical activity [5]. Classic heatstroke predominantly affects the elderly and those with chronic comorbidities during heatwaves. EHS, in contrast, occurs in young, ostensibly healthy individuals whose heat production overwhelms the body’s dissipative capacity under conditions of high ambient temperature, humidity, and sustained muscular work [4]. Occupational EHS is further distinguished from athletic or military EHS by its chronicity. Workers in agriculture, construction, and outdoor industries face repeated daily exposure across entire seasons. They often lack structured heat-acclimatization protocols, on-site medical monitoring, and the discretion to cease exertion when prodromal symptoms appear [41]. The diagnosis of AKI in this context follows KDIGO criteria: a rise in serum creatinine by ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 h, or ≥1.5 times baseline within seven days. However, baseline creatinine is rarely documented in occupational health settings, whereas the oliguric phase may be masked by aggressive on-site hydration. The result is systematic under-ascertainment.

The reported incidence of AKI complicating EHS varies widely across populations (Table 1). This variation reflects differences in surveillance intensity, diagnostic thresholds, and cohort demographics rather than genuine epidemiological heterogeneity alone. Among 48 United States military personnel hospitalized with EHS between 2007 and 2014, a retrospective chart review documented one death and no case requiring dialysis. AKI was nonetheless diagnosed in 40% of them [18]. Earlier military data spanning 1980–2002 reported heat-illness hospitalizations at 14.5 per 100,000 person-years among active-duty soldiers. Exertional heatstroke accounted for approximately 10% of cases. Renal complications, including AKI, were documented in 13% of hospitalized heat casualties [17]. A more recent analysis of 24,121 U.S. service members with exertional heat illness (2012–2022) grouped cases by pattern of complication. Clusters defined by AKI, by rhabdomyolysis, or by both together accounted for roughly one case in six. These clusters carried a lower risk of medical separation than the smaller cluster defined by neurological symptoms [19]. Among civilian occupational cohorts, the epidemiological picture is far less complete. A pilot field study of Florida construction workers reported that 33% had a post-shift urine specific gravity consistent with significant dehydration. Creatinine-based AKI incidence was not formally assessed [21]. An analysis of OSHA severe-injury reports (2010–2020) identified confirmed, probable, or possible heat-related AKI across construction, agriculture, and manufacturing. The passive surveillance design almost certainly underestimated the true burden [20]. A systematic review encompassing 16 studies published between 2009 and 2020 reported that AKI ranked among the most frequent organ-specific complications of heatstroke, alongside hepatic and neurological injury. High-quality incidence estimates for occupational cohorts were absent [6].

The risk profile for occupational heatstroke-related AKI can be conceptualized along a three-tier continuum spanning environmental exposure, individual susceptibility, and occupational determinants. At the environmental tier, risk is defined by ambient heat load, conventionally quantified as wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT), humidity, and the duration and diurnal timing of exposure. Heatwaves amplify risk not merely through higher peak temperatures but through the cumulative strain of consecutive exposure days, which impairs thermoregulatory and renal reserve [42]. At the individual tier, age extremes represent a well-established vulnerability. Older workers show attenuated sweat rates, impaired cutaneous vasodilation, and altered RAAS responsiveness. Younger, unacclimatized workers are over-represented in EHS events due to inadequate heat adaptation and over-exertion [4]. Preexisting chronic conditions, including diabetes and obesity, compound vulnerability to heat-related kidney injury [40]. Medications that impair thermoregulation or fluid balance, including non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, diuretics and anticholinergics, further narrow the renal safety margin [43]. This evidence derives from older adults, and its applicability to younger workers has not been established. Sickle cell trait, present in approximately 8% of African American individuals, has been associated with exertional collapse and sudden death during strenuous exercise in hot environments. This is likely mediated by microvascular sickling, endothelial damage, and rhabdomyolysis [44]. The occupational tier acts as a catalyst. Work intensity (quantified in METs) interacts with protective equipment that impedes evaporative cooling, with prolonged shift duration, and with inadequate access to water, shade, and rest breaks. The first day of a heatwave and the first week of an unacclimatized worker’s assignment carry disproportionately elevated risk. This pattern is consistently observed in military training cohorts [45,46] and in agricultural cohorts [22].

The clinical trajectory following occupational heatstroke-related AKI spans a spectrum from full renal recovery to chronic kidney disease. Long-term outcomes remain poorly characterized in longitudinal studies. Short-term mortality in EHS with AKI has declined substantially where rapid cooling and intensive care are available. The U.S. military cohort cited above reported a single death among 48 hospitalized EHS patients. Survival is now the norm rather than the exception in well-resourced systems [18]. Renal recovery is the expected outcome in most cases, although convalescence may require days to weeks of supportive care. The risk of recurrent AKI with re-exposure has not been systematically quantified. The more concerning trajectory is the potential progression from recurrent subclinical heat-induced kidney injury to chronic kidney disease of nontraditional etiology (CKDnt). Epidemiological data from Central America, Sri Lanka, and India have documented clusters of CKDnt among young, male agricultural workers without hypertension or diabetes. Their defining occupational exposure is prolonged, repetitive physical labor under high ambient heat with recurrent dehydration [3,47]. In Mesoamerican nephropathy, recurrent heat stress and dehydration drive tubulointerstitial fibrosis independent of diabetes and hypertension. Its pathophysiological underpinnings have been increasingly characterized in the nephrology literature [48,49]. Reports of CKDnt among U.S. agricultural workers suggest this entity is not confined to tropical developing regions. It is emerging wherever prolonged occupational heat exposure occurs [50]. A 2026 perspective articulated the emerging scientific consensus. Heat stress-induced AKI, even when subclinical and self-limited after each episode, may cumulatively drive tubulointerstitial fibrosis. This is the hallmark histological lesion of Mesoamerican nephropathy and allied conditions [40]. Prospective follow-up gives this sequence a quantitative anchor. Among 326 Nicaraguan sugarcane workers with normal preharvest creatinine, 34 developed AKI during the harvest season [51]. Nearly half of them had established CKD 12 months later. Ten developed a de novo eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, and 11 had an eGFR decline exceeding 30% [51]. Experimental work links this trajectory to the pathways set out in Section 4. Rats exposed to recurrent heat stress with repeated rhabdomyolysis developed intrarenal oxidative stress and inflammasome activation, and the injury was largely prevented by allopurinol or bicarbonate [52]. Those findings come from a rodent model and have not been tested in heat-exposed workers. Climate change acts as a threat multiplier. Increasing heatwave frequency and severity expand the geographic range, seasonal duration, and population-attributable fraction of occupational heat exposure. This projects a growing burden of both acute and chronic heat-related kidney disease [47].

These epidemiological observations establish the public-health significance of occupational heatstroke-related AKI while exposing critical data deficiencies. What is missing: prospective, biomarker-augmented cohort studies in civilian occupational populations; standardized, occupation-specific surveillance case definitions; and full characterization of the AKI-to-CKD continuum in heat-exposed workers. These gaps are not merely academic. They obscure the denominator against which preventive interventions and therapeutic strategies must be evaluated. The bridge from descriptive epidemiology to rationally targeted intervention lies in the pathophysiological cascade that converts heat exposure into renal injury: the subject of the next section.

Pathophysiology: from heat stress to renal injury

The progression from occupational heat exposure to acute kidney injury is not mediated by a single mechanism. It follows a temporally organized, multi-pathway cascade in which each stage amplifies the preceding one and narrows the window for intervention. Sepsis-associated AKI is driven predominantly by hemodynamic and inflammatory mechanisms, with the microbial insult as the primary trigger. Exertional heatstroke-related AKI is different: the central event is rhabdomyolysis-driven myoglobin release, which converts a systemic hyperthermic insult into kidney-targeted oxidative and obstructive damage [53]. We trace this cascade along its temporal axis, from the initial direct cytotoxicity of heat through the amplifying loops of myoglobin, inflammation, coagulopathy, and gut-derived endotoxemia. The goal is to identify the nodes at which biomarker-guided or extracorporeal intervention may be most effective.

Direct thermal cytotoxicity and cell death pathways

The kidney is intrinsically vulnerable to direct thermal injury. At core temperatures exceeding 40 °C, the tubular epithelium undergoes a sequence of structural and functional disruptions. The proximal tubular cells of the S3 segment are particularly vulnerable. They sit in the outer stripe of the outer medulla, receive post-capillary venous blood, and depend on active transport [54]. Protein denaturation and aggregation trigger the heat shock response. Heat shock factor 1 (HSF1) dissociates from cytosolic heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) and translocates to the nucleus, driving the transcription of HSP70, HSP90, and other chaperones [55]. This endogenous protective system has finite capacity. When the thermal load exceeds the chaperone reserve, HSF1-mediated transcription becomes insufficient to prevent proteotoxic stress. This occurs in exertional heatstroke, where heat production continues for the duration of physical exertion [34]. Concurrently, mitochondrial electron transport chain uncoupling generates a burst of reactive oxygen species that opens the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, releasing cytochrome c and depleting ATP [56]. The mode of cell death that ensues depends on the severity of the insult and the intracellular ATP concentration: moderate heat stress favors caspase-3- and caspase-9-mediated apoptosis. Severe ATP depletion commits the cell to death rather than recovery [57]. Two additional death pathways, ferroptosis and pyroptosis, are of particular relevance to heatstroke AKI because they are directly engaged by downstream events. Ferroptosis is an iron- and lipid-peroxidation-dependent form of regulated necrosis. It is triggered when the GPX4 antioxidant system is overwhelmed by free iron released from myoglobin degradation in the proximal tubule [34]. Pyroptosis, a gasdermin-D-mediated lytic death, releases interleukin-1 beta and interleukin-18. It is activated by the NLRP3 inflammasome in response to damage-associated molecular patterns, connecting direct cytotoxicity to the systemic inflammatory cascade [56]. The implication is direct: in heatstroke, multiple cell death programs are activated simultaneously rather than sequentially. Single-pathway pharmacological blockade (ferroptosis inhibition, for instance) may therefore be insufficient to confer full renoprotection.

Rhabdomyolysis and the myoglobin-kidney axis

In exertional heatstroke, the combination of hyperthermia and sustained muscular work drives skeletal muscle beyond its compensatory limits, producing rhabdomyolysis. This is the principal amplifier that converts systemic heat illness into kidney-specific injury [7]. The initiating event is myocellular calcium overload. Heat stress impairs SERCA function and increases ryanodine receptor leak. Cytosolic calcium rises to levels that activate calpains and phospholipases, degrade the sarcolemma, and deplete ATP through uncontrolled contractile activity [58]. The resulting loss of myocyte membrane integrity releases myoglobin, creatine kinase, potassium, and phosphate into the systemic circulation [59]. Myoglobin, a 17.8-kDa heme protein, is freely filtered at the glomerulus and reabsorbed in the proximal tubule via the megalin-cubilin endocytic receptor complex. Within the acidic environment of the tubular epithelial lysosome, the heme moiety dissociates from the globin chain, and heme oxygenase-1 releases free ferrous iron. This ferrous iron participates in Fenton chemistry, generating hydroxyl radicals that initiate lipid peroxidation of cell membranes; the pathway was characterized in skeletal muscle [60]. When the local concentration of lipid peroxides exceeds the reducing capacity of GPX4, ferroptosis ensues. This death mode is especially destructive because it can propagate across neighboring cells [60]. At the same time, myoglobin co-precipitates with Tamm-Horsfall protein in the acidic distal nephron. The resulting granular casts obstruct tubular flow, raise intratubular pressure, and reduce GFR. Stasis and further cast formation then reinforce each other [7]. This dual mechanism, oxidative ferroptosis from within the tubular cell coupled with obstructive cast formation from within the lumen, makes the myoglobin axis uniquely nephrotoxic. It distinguishes exertional heatstroke AKI from classic heatstroke AKI, in which rhabdomyolysis is typically absent or modest and renal injury is driven primarily by dehydration and hemodynamic factors [5].

Systemic inflammation and the coagulation-inflammation amplification loop

Cellular injury from thermal cytotoxicity and rhabdomyolysis releases diverse damage-associated molecular patterns into the circulation. These include HMGB1, mitochondrial DNA, heat shock proteins, and histones. These engage Toll-like receptors (TLR2 and TLR4) and the receptor for advanced glycation end-products on monocytes, macrophages, and endothelial cells [61]. Downstream, nuclear factor kappa-B drives the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines: interleukin-1 beta, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, producing the hypercytokinemic profile that characterizes severe heatstroke [62]. This systemic inflammatory response converges with the coagulation system in a bidirectional amplification loop that is particularly injurious to the renal microvasculature. Heat-damaged endothelial cells shed their glycocalyx, expose von Willebrand factor and tissue factor, and upregulate adhesion molecules that capture circulating leukocytes and platelets [63]. The resulting thromboinflammation produces fibrin-platelet microthrombi that obstruct glomerular and peritubular capillaries. This compounds the ischemia already imposed by systemic hypotension and renal vasoconstriction [64]. Heatstroke coagulopathy has a distinctive phenotype: fibrinolytic-shutdown DIC. Elevated PAI-1 suppresses tPA-mediated clot lysis, favoring thrombus persistence over hemorrhagic conversion in the microvasculature [65]. This pattern differs from the consumptive coagulopathy of sepsis in important respects. Coagulation markers can be abnormal within hours of heatstroke presentation, and they resolve with effective cooling. This suggests that hyperthermia itself, rather than a persistent infectious nidus, is the proximal driver [53].

The gut-kidney axis and late-stage amplification

A fourth pathway, recognized only in the past decade as a significant contributor to heatstroke pathophysiology, involves the intestinal barrier. The same hemodynamic redistribution that delivers blood to the skin for thermoregulatory heat dissipation diverts flow from the splanchnic circulation, subjecting the intestinal epithelium to functional ischemia [35]. Hypoxic stress disrupts tight junction proteins (occludin, claudins, zonula occludens-1), increasing paracellular permeability. Lipopolysaccharide and bacterial fragments translocate from the gut lumen into the portal circulation. When hepatic Kupffer cell clearance is exceeded, these microbial products reach the systemic circulation. They engage pattern-recognition receptors on renal tubular epithelial cells, macrophages, and endothelial cells throughout the body [35]. This gut-derived endotoxemia provides a sustained, non-heat-dependent fuel source for the inflammatory-coagulation loop. It may explain why some patients with exertional heatstroke continue to deteriorate after core temperature has been normalized [66]. The gut-kidney axis also interacts synergistically with the myoglobin axis. Endotoxin exposure sensitizes the renal endothelium to the vasoconstrictive and pro-oxidant effects of myoglobin. This lowers the threshold at which tubular injury becomes irreversible [53].

Temporal integration and the therapeutic window

When these pathways are mapped onto a unified temporal framework, a structure of interventional opportunity emerges (Figure 2). In the first two hours after hyperthermia onset, direct thermal cytotoxicity dominates, and aggressive cooling, which removes the initiating stimulus before downstream amplifiers are fully engaged, is maximally effective. Between two and six hours, rhabdomyolysis-driven myoglobin release peaks. This opens a clearance window during which extracorporeal removal of myoglobin, by high-cutoff hemofiltration, hemoadsorption, or therapeutic plasma exchange, may prevent proximal tubular ferroptosis and cast formation. From six to approximately 24 h, the inflammatory-coagulation loop activates. This represents a window for anti-inflammatory or anticoagulant strategies, although the narrow therapeutic index of these interventions in the context of heat-induced coagulopathy has limited their clinical translation. Beyond 12 to 48 h, gut barrier disruption and sustained endotoxin translocation provide late-stage fuel to the inflammatory response. After roughly 48 h of sustained tubular injury, the histological picture shifts from potentially reversible injury to established tubular necrosis with cast nephropathy. At that point, renal recovery depends on regenerative capacity, not on removal of the initiating insult [10]. This sequence should be read as a working hypothesis rather than an established physiological chronology. It is assembled from animal experiments and indirect human observations, and no study has tracked all four pathways concurrently in a single heatstroke cohort. The temporal model carries a direct implication for clinical practice. The utility of any biomarker or blood purification approach depends on where the patient sits in this cascade when the intervention is deployed. Biomarkers, discussed in the following section, offer a potential solution to this timing problem.

Biomarkers for early recognition and risk stratification

The multi-pathway temporal cascade that converts occupational heat exposure into acute kidney injury creates distinct diagnostic requirements at each stage of its evolution. A biomarker capturing proximal tubular ferroptosis during the myoglobin surge may provide little information once microvascular thromboinflammation dominates at 12 h. A marker of established tubular necrosis cannot guide intervention when the therapeutic window is defined by reversibility. We therefore need three temporally nested categories of information. First, early warning before glomerular filtration declines. Second, early diagnosis while tubular injury is still reversible. Third, risk stratification to identify patients most likely to benefit from extracorporeal intervention. What follows evaluates candidate biomarkers against these temporal requirements. We proceed from traditional muscle-derived markers through proximal tubular injury markers to cell-cycle arrest markers and multi-marker approaches. Throughout, we distinguish evidence from heatstroke-specific contexts and evidence extrapolated from adjacent fields.

Serum myoglobin occupies a dual position in heatstroke-related AKI that is analytically useful and clinically challenging in equal measure. As the principal nephrotoxic mediator, myoglobin drives proximal tubular ferroptosis through Fenton-chemistry-derived hydroxyl radicals and co-precipitates with Tamm-Horsfall protein to form obstructive casts. Simultaneously, it serves as an exposure biomarker whose circulating concentration reflects the magnitude of rhabdomyolysis [67]. A rising myoglobin level identifies patients in whom the myoglobin-kidney axis is active and who therefore stand to benefit from myoglobin-clearing extracorporeal strategies. Yet myoglobin is rapidly cleared from the circulation, more rapidly than CK, such that a single normal value may miss a preceding surge. Moreover, its skeletal-muscle origin means that elevated concentrations do not localize injury to the kidney [68]. Creatine kinase alone is an imperfect predictor of AKI severity. It is confounded by inter-individual variation in muscle mass, hydration status, and sampling timing relative to injury onset. Its peak, at 24 to 72 h after muscle injury, arrives too late to serve as an early warning signal [69]. These limitations motivate the search for biomarkers that originate from the kidney itself, rise before GFR declines, and discriminate between reversible tubular stress and established necrosis.

Neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) is the most extensively studied novel AKI biomarker in contexts that share physiological features with exertional heatstroke. Among 16 runners completing a 60-km ultramarathon, serum NGAL rose about 1.6-fold and urinary NGAL nearly eightfold. Only the serum marker tracked the concurrent rise in creatinine [70]. In a controlled laboratory study, 12 of 20 men performed high-intensity exercise at 40 °C. Short-term heat acclimation did not attenuate the post-exercise fall in eGFR or the rise in serum creatinine [71]. This implies that thermal protection of the kidney may require adaptation protocols longer than the six-day regimen tested. A multicenter prospective study in five French emergency departments tested serum NGAL against 48-h AKI in 189 patients with rhabdomyolysis. Its discrimination was poor, with an area under the curve of 0.60 and a sensitivity of 0.65 at the optimal cutoff [72]. The authors concluded that NGAL has limited ability to predict AKI in this setting. That result argues against relying on any single tubular marker as a trigger. A naturally occurring canine heatstroke model further demonstrated that NGAL concentration correlates with AKI severity and clinical outcome [73]. The principal constraint on NGAL’s diagnostic performance in heatstroke is specificity. NGAL is released by activated neutrophils during systemic inflammation, a process intrinsic to severe heatstroke. Circulating concentrations may therefore rise independently of renal tubular injury, reducing positive predictive value precisely in the patients at highest risk.

Liver-type fatty acid binding protein (L-FABP) offers a complementary profile that addresses some of NGAL’s limitations. Expressed predominantly in the proximal tubular epithelium, L-FABP participates in the intracellular trafficking of free fatty acids. Its urinary excretion increases in real time when tubular cells experience hypoxia or oxidative stress, before structural damage becomes histologically evident. L-FABP is, critically, the only novel AKI biomarker for which prospective human data have been generated specifically in heat-related illness. A prospective observational study at the Japanese Self-Defense Force Fuji Hospital documented early urinary L-FABP elevation in patients with exertional heat-related illness [36]. A subsequent pilot study in Self-Defense Force personnel validated a point-of-care testing (POCT) format for urinary L-FABP [37]. This demonstrated that field-deployable measurement is operationally feasible in the settings where exertional heatstroke most commonly occurs. The POCT format bypasses the laboratory infrastructure typically unavailable at construction sites, agricultural fields, and military training grounds. This positions L-FABP as a plausible candidate for field use in occupational heat-exposure settings. That potential should not be overstated. However, both studies originate from a single research group, sample sizes are modest, and neither has been independently replicated. The POCT format has not been tested within a real-world occupational surveillance program.

The combination of urinary tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases-2 (TIMP-2) and insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 7 (IGFBP7), commercialized as NephroCheck, represents the most rigorously validated AKI biomarker platform in critical care. In the Sapphire multicenter study of 728 heterogeneous critically ill patients, the product [TIMP-2]·[IGFBP7] predicted moderate-to-severe AKI (KDIGO stage 2–3) within 12 h with an AUC of 0.80 [9]. It outperformed NGAL, KIM-1, interleukin-18, and cystatin C when each was assessed individually. Validation in patients undergoing major non-cardiac surgery confirmed an AUC of 0.85 for any AKI [74]. A meta-analysis of five studies encompassing 1,619 patients reported pooled sensitivity of 0.89 and specificity of 0.48 at the 0.3 (ng/mL)2/1000 threshold [75]. Raising the cutoff to 2.0 reversed the profile, yielding sensitivity of 0.45 and specificity of 0.93. A focused clinical review emphasized that the test’s greatest utility lies not in replacing KDIGO criteria but in identifying high-risk patients before functional decline [76]. This enables earlier implementation of the KDIGO prevention bundle. The biological rationale is mechanistically distinctive. TIMP-2 and IGFBP7 are mediators of G1 cell-cycle arrest, an adaptive response in which tubular epithelial cells halt proliferation to avoid replicating damaged DNA. Their urinary appearance signals cellular stress before the commitment to death programs, including apoptosis, necrosis, or ferroptosis, has occurred [77]. This temporal positioning, upstream of irreversible injury, aligns conceptually with the early-intervention window in the heatstroke pathophysiological cascade. The decisive gap is evidential rather than conceptual. No study has evaluated [TIMP-2]·[IGFBP7] in heatstroke-related AKI. Its performance characteristics in a population with concomitant rhabdomyolysis, massive volume resuscitation, and rapid intravascular compartment shifts remain entirely unknown.

Whether any single biomarker can capture all the pathways enumerated in the heatstroke-AKI cascade is doubtful. This recognition has motivated interest in multi-marker and multimodal strategies. A simple, universally available predictor with specific validation in heatstroke is thrombocytopenia. In a single-center retrospective analysis of 176 patients with heatstroke, early thrombocytopenia independently predicted the development of severe AKI. The decline in platelet count preceded the rise in serum creatinine by several days in many cases [78]. Integrating a tubular injury marker (NGAL or L-FABP) with a systemic-severity indicator such as platelet count exemplifies the multimodal approach advocated for AKI risk stratification. Combining these with a clinical risk score further strengthens the assessment [79]. Applied to heatstroke, where the renal insult involves simultaneous thermal, myoglobinuric, inflammatory, coagulopathic, and microvascular components, such a composite strategy is conceptually attractive even though prospective validation is lacking.

A separate question is whether the markers that flag acute tubular injury also forecast the chronic trajectory. The available evidence suggests they do not transfer automatically. A systematic review of 25 studies in chronic kidney disease of uncertain etiology found urinary KIM-1 and urinary NGAL to be the two most frequently measured biomarkers [80]. It also reported substantial variability in study design, assay method and statistical approach, which prevented meta-analysis [80]. A comparative test-accuracy study in Mesoamerican sugarcane cutters is more pointed. The best predictor of cross-harvest eGFR decline was not a tubular injury protein. Post-shift dipstick leukocyturia performed best, and falling blood hemoglobin was also predictive. Workers with leukocyturia of ++ or greater lost 13 mL/min/1.73 m2 more eGFR across the harvest than those without, with a 95% confidence interval of 10 to 16 [81]. Both markers are measurable at the point of care. The implication is that acute-phase and chronic-phase surveillance may require different marker sets. Whether L-FABP or NGAL measured during an acute heatstroke episode predicts subsequent CKDnt has not been tested.

The biomarker landscape for heatstroke-related AKI, mapped against the temporal framework of its pathophysiology, reveals a gradient of proximity to clinical application. Myoglobin measurement is widely available and directly quantifies the nephrotoxic burden, but its short detection window limits its utility as a standalone trigger for intervention. NGAL has the most extensive evidence base across exercise, rhabdomyolysis, and heatstroke-adjacent contexts. A systematic review identified NGAL and cystatin C as the two most studied serum markers after prolonged exercise, with NGAL elevation associated with subclinical renal injury [82]. Its specificity, however, is constrained in the inflamed host. L-FABP is supported by the only prospective heat-related-illness-specific human data and has been measured in a POCT format suited to occupational settings. It may be the marker closest to field deployment. That judgment rests on two unreplicated studies from a single group, and the POCT format has not been tested in routine occupational surveillance. NephroCheck has the strongest ICU validation record but no heatstroke-specific data. Four translational priorities emerge. First, embed prospective biomarker collection in civilian occupational heat-exposure cohorts. Second, validate NephroCheck in exertional heatstroke. Third, define optimal sampling time points relative to hyperthermia onset rather than hospital arrival. Fourth, establish biomarker thresholds at which blood purification initiation should be formally assessed. That is the question to which the discussion of extracorporeal modalities and their timing now turns.

Blood purification in heatstroke-related AKI: Modalities and evidence

The rationale for deploying extracorporeal blood purification in heatstroke-related AKI rests on a dual objective that distinguishes this scenario from the typical indications for renal replacement therapy. In conventional AKI, RRT is initiated to substitute excretory renal function: correction of hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, volume overload, and uremia. In exertional heatstroke, an additional and temporally more urgent objective is renal protection. The aim is extracorporeal removal of myoglobin, the principal nephrotoxic mediator released by rhabdomyolysis. Some modalities also concurrently adsorb pro-inflammatory cytokines and damage-associated molecular patterns that fuel the inflammatory-coagulation amplification loop [59]. Whether a given modality achieves both objectives determines its suitability for the heatstroke-AKI population. The cost must also be weighed: albumin loss, anticoagulant exposure, and resource utilization all matter.

The evolution of RRT modalities for myoglobin removal can be traced along a gradient of membrane permeability (Table 2). Standard intermittent hemodialysis relies on diffusion through low-flux membranes with a molecular-weight cutoff of approximately 10–15 kDa. It clears urea and small solutes efficiently but removes negligible quantities of myoglobin, a 17.8-kDa protein whose molecular radius further restricts its passage [11]. Continuous modalities using convection (CVVH and CVVHDF) improved myoglobin clearance by harnessing solvent drag, and high-flux and super-high-flux membranes extended the effective cutoff further. A super-high-flux membrane with a 100-kDa cutoff achieved roughly a fourfold to fivefold increase in myoglobin clearance over a standard polysulfone membrane [24]. High-permeability polysulfone dialyzers likewise reduced serum myoglobin in a prospective series of six patients, although albumin loss required monitoring [23]. The next step in permeability, the high-cutoff (HCO) dialyzer with a nominal 45–60 kDa cutoff, was tested in a randomized trial. In 70 patients randomized 1:1, CVVHD with an HCO membrane cleared myoglobin better than CVVHDF with a high-flux membrane throughout the study period. No albumin clearance was measurable in either arm, and dialyzer lifespan was longer with the HCO membrane [25]. One result challenges the assumption that continuous therapy is inherently superior: intermittent HCO hemodialysis achieved substantially greater myoglobin clearance rates than continuous HCO methods [26]. If replicated, this could simplify the logistical demands of myoglobin-targeted blood purification in resource-limited settings.

A parallel line of development has focused on direct hemoadsorption. Adsorbent cartridges containing porous polymer beads (CytoSorb being the most extensively studied) remove myoglobin and inflammatory mediators through size-exclusion pore capture and hydrophobic surface interactions [59]. These mechanisms are independent of diffusion or convection. The Hemoadsorption in Rhabdomyolysis Task Force, an international Delphi panel across six European countries, issued a consensus statement in 2024. It reached strong consensus that patients with serum myoglobin above 10,000 ng/mL should be considered for extracorporeal myoglobin removal. Hemoadsorption should ideally begin within 24 h of admission. The panel did not agree on a creatine kinase threshold. A proposed value of 5,000 U/L for defining severe rhabdomyolysis split the panel evenly, and myoglobin was to take priority when both were available. The statement also specified criteria for monitoring and termination [16]. A randomized trial in eight patients compared high-cutoff CVVHD alone with the same circuit plus an adsorber. The primary outcome was the area under the curve of relative myoglobin concentration, and it was lower in the adsorber arm at both 24 and 48 h [28]. A larger retrospective analysis of 328 critically ill patients with rhabdomyolysis reached a more sobering conclusion. Myoglobin reduction rates did not differ between kidney replacement therapy and conservative management, and no advantage could be confirmed for any single modality [30]. Evidence for a renal benefit comes instead from a propensity-score-matched analysis restricted to patients with myoglobin above 10,000 ng/mL. Among 35 matched pairs, independence from kidney replacement therapy at 30 days was reached by 31.4% of those treated with hemoadsorption versus 11.4% of those who were not [31]. A subsequent retrospective study of 111 patients found that CRRT combined with hemoperfusion was associated with faster CK decline and with lower 90-day mortality on multivariable analysis [32]. The unadjusted between-group difference did not reach significance. An earlier critical review had concluded that CK and myoglobin thresholds alone should not dictate RRT initiation in rhabdomyolysis [83]. The ReplaceRhabdo pilot trial randomized 15 patients in a three-arm design. Myoglobin clearance did not differ between modes over 24 h, although the CytoSorb arm was superior during the first hours before its performance decayed. Simulation favored an HCO dialyzer combined with adsorption and 12-hourly cartridge exchange [39]. The principal limitation of hemoadsorption is nonselective binding. Albumin, antibiotics, and other therapeutic proteins may be simultaneously removed, and the net clinical impact remains incompletely characterized [29]. A 2026 review advanced the concept of biomarker-guided, individualized blood purification for cast nephropathy in the ICU. It proposed matching the timing of extracorporeal myoglobin clearance to the trajectory of tubular injury biomarkers rather than to fixed molecular thresholds [84]. This framework is directly applicable to heatstroke.

Evidence specific to heatstroke, as distinct from rhabdomyolysis in general, is limited to a single clinical study. Zhou and colleagues retrospectively analyzed 16 patients with exertional heatstroke who received CVVH initiated with low-temperature replacement fluid. The goal was rapid core cooling from 41.3 °C to 38.7 °C within two to two-and-a-half hours while simultaneously clearing myoglobin, CK, and total bilirubin. All patients survived, and serum urea and creatinine improved significantly within 96 h [12]. This dual effect, therapeutic hypothermia combined with toxin removal, is unique to the heatstroke setting and has not been reproduced prospectively. It also creates an interpretive problem. Because the replacement fluid was cooled, the contribution of core temperature reduction cannot be separated from the contribution of myoglobin clearance. Either mechanism, or both, could account for the observed survival. Any prospective test of myoglobin-targeted purification in heatstroke will need a cooling-matched comparator to resolve this. The crush-syndrome literature offers a complementary, though indirect, evidence base. Following the Hanshin earthquake, continuous hemodiafiltration achieved superior myoglobin clearance and clinical outcomes compared with intermittent hemodialysis and plasma exchange [33]. Two features make this literature relevant. The nephrotoxic mediator is the same, and the injured muscle mass is large enough to generate comparable myoglobin loads. Two features limit its transfer. In crush syndrome the release is largely a reperfusion event. Myoglobin and potassium enter the circulation once compression is relieved, and the metabolic consequences depend on the time to extrication [85]. In exertional heatstroke, muscular contraction and hyperthermia continue until cooling succeeds, so release is better described as sustained production than as a single bolus. Crush victims also present with trauma, hypovolemia and compartment syndrome, none of which is intrinsic to heatstroke. A teaching case commentary on rhabdomyolysis-associated AKI restated the core principles that remain central to management. Aggressive volume expansion is the cornerstone of prevention. Nephrotoxins should be avoided. Extracorporeal clearance is reserved for patients with established AKI or extreme myoglobin burden [86]. The ADQI 30 consensus endorsed a key principle: extracorporeal blood purification should be matched to the physicochemical properties of the target solute [29]. For the 17.8-kDa, protein-bound myoglobin, combining convective clearance with adsorptive augmentation is a rational strategy.

The current evidence base for blood purification in heatstroke-related AKI can be summarized as adequate to justify clinical use but insufficient to mandate a specific protocol. Retrospective data, including the only heatstroke-specific study, consistently suggest benefit. Randomized evidence supporting one modality over another is confined to general rhabdomyolysis populations and limited by small sample sizes. The pragmatic clinician can reasonably deploy an integrated approach: high-cutoff or super-high-flux continuous hemofiltration combined with adjunctive hemoadsorption when myoglobin burden is extreme, while monitoring albumin and antibiotic levels. Two limitations of hemoadsorption deserve emphasis before routine adoption. Binding is nonselective. A systematic review of 26 studies examined drug removal by the CytoSorb adsorber. Vancomycin and linezolid are cleared by the device, and data for most other antibiotics are insufficient to judge clinical relevance. The authors advise therapeutic drug monitoring whenever possible during treatment [27]. Cost is raised as a further objection, but the evidence does not settle it. The only real-world economic evaluation covers 246 patients with septic shock at a single Swiss center, where hemoadsorption was not associated with higher total hospital costs [87]. That population is not a heatstroke population, and no economic data exist for this indication. It also bears repeating that no modality has been shown superior in a heatstroke-specific population. Every comparative dataset in Table 2 derives from general rhabdomyolysis or from crush syndrome. The more fundamental question is when to initiate any form of blood purification in the first place. That question turns on the distinction between preemptive clearance guided by biomarkers and reactive initiation driven by conventional RRT criteria. It is the central controversy to which the following section turns.

Timing of blood purification initiation: the central controversy

When should extracorporeal blood purification begin in a patient with heatstroke-related AKI? The question lies at the intersection of two streams of evidence that point in opposite directions. The first stream, comprising the large multicenter trials that define contemporary intensive care nephrology, has converged on a consistent finding. In the absence of life-threatening complications, an accelerated RRT strategy does not improve survival over a more conservative approach. It may increase catheter-related adverse events and the risk of RRT dependence at 90 days. The second stream, drawn from the rhabdomyolysis and heatstroke literature, is smaller and predominantly retrospective. This line of evidence suggests that when a known nephrotoxin (myoglobin) is continuously released from injured muscle, its early extracorporeal removal may prevent irreversible tubular damage and improve renal recovery. Reconciling these two streams requires understanding why the general-AKI trials may not capture the biology of exertional heatstroke.

The landmark trials that anchor contemporary practice were all designed to answer a single pragmatic question. In a heterogeneous population of critically ill patients with severe AKI, does initiating RRT at an earlier stage of biochemical severity confer a survival advantage over waiting for conventional indications? STARRT-AKI randomized 3,019 patients across 168 centers in 15 countries to an accelerated strategy or a standard strategy. The trial reported no significant difference in 90-day mortality: 43.9% versus 43.7% [15]. Notably, 38% of patients in the standard-strategy arm never received RRT at all. This suggests that a substantial proportion of severe AKI resolves with conservative management. A cost-utility analysis confirmed that the accelerated strategy was not cost-effective [88]. The AKIKI trial enrolled 620 patients in France and similarly found no mortality difference between an early strategy at KDIGO stage 3 and a delayed strategy triggered by conventional indications. The delayed arm had fewer RRT sessions and fewer catheter-related bloodstream infections [38]. AKIKI 2 randomized 278 patients to either a delayed strategy or a more-delayed strategy. The more-delayed strategy failed to increase RRT-free days and was associated with a higher 60-day mortality (hazard ratio 1.65) [14]. IDEAL-ICU, restricted to septic shock, likewise found no benefit, and a meta-analysis of 4,753 patients across ten trials confirmed the absence of an overall mortality difference [89]. Its subgroup analysis is easily overlooked and directly relevant here. Accelerated initiation was associated with lower all-cause mortality in surgical ICU patients and in those treated with continuous modalities. Both subgroups were also more likely to end up free of dialysis. A Bayesian reanalysis of STARRT-AKI placed the posterior probability of any mortality benefit from the accelerated strategy below 5% [90]. The collective weight of this evidence has shifted practice toward a more conservative posture. Recent observational data document a secular decline in RRT utilization among ICU patients with AKI [91].

Why these trials cannot be automatically transposed to exertional heatstroke, turns on the biology they were designed to address. The study populations were characterized predominantly by sepsis, post-cardiac-surgery AKI, and hypoperfusion states. In these conditions, AKI is driven by hemodynamic alterations, inflammation, and microcirculatory dysfunction. The rationale for early RRT rests on the speculative premise that removing inflammatory mediators might attenuate distant organ injury. This premise has not been substantiated in randomized trials. In exertional heatstroke, the rationale is fundamentally different and mechanistically specific. Myoglobin, the principal nephrotoxic effector, is physically removable by extracorporeal techniques [59]. The decision to initiate blood purification therefore involves a calculus unique to this setting: removing a quantifiable nephrotoxin before irreversible tubular necrosis occurs. This consideration has no counterpart in the populations enrolled in the landmark timing trials. In addition, the temporal coordinate systems of these two evidence bases are misaligned. The general-AKI trials defined “early” by the KDIGO creatinine stage. In exertional heatstroke, the myoglobin surge peaks between two and six hours after injury [10]. By the time serum creatinine meets KDIGO stage 2 thresholds, the surge may have largely subsided. An initiation strategy keyed to creatinine is thus “early” relative to conventional RRT indications but may already be “late” relative to the myoglobin clearance window.

The evidence stream specific to rhabdomyolysis and heatstroke is consistent in the direction of its signal. A retrospective analysis of 328 patients with rhabdomyolysis compared conservative management against several RRT modalities. It found no confirmable difference between them [30]. A propensity-score-matched analysis of 35 pairs did report a renal benefit. All included patients had myoglobin above 10,000 ng/mL, and freedom from kidney replacement therapy at 30 days was more common with hemoadsorption [31]. The only study specifically reporting blood purification outcomes in exertional heatstroke analyzed 16 patients treated with CVVH initiated early. It documented 100% survival and significant renal function improvement within 96 h [12]. This case series does not constitute high-grade evidence: there was no control arm, the sample was small, and publication bias cannot be excluded. It remains, nevertheless, the sole clinical report directly addressing the question this review poses. On the guideline front, the Hemoadsorption in Rhabdomyolysis Task Force reached strong consensus that myoglobin above 10,000 ng/mL should prompt consideration of extracorporeal removal [16]. It reached no agreement on a creatine kinase threshold. This recommendation, while consensus-based rather than GRADE evidence-based, represents the only structured guidance on extracorporeal thresholds specific to rhabdomyolysis. In contrast, the Danish DASAIM/DSIT guideline recommended following standard AKI indications for RRT initiation in rhabdomyolysis: hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, volume overload, uremia. It cited the absence of randomized evidence that myoglobin-targeted initiation improves patient-centered outcomes [92]. These two documents, published within five years of each other, capture the controversy. The same evidence base is read by one panel as supporting preemptive clearance and by another as insufficient to justify deviation from standard practice.

The asymmetry between these two evidence streams should be stated plainly. The trials that found no overall benefit from accelerated initiation randomized more than 4,000 patients across dozens of centers [15,38,89]. The evidence that argues for preemptive myoglobin clearance rests on a propensity-matched analysis of 35 pairs and a 16-patient uncontrolled series [12,31]. The largest retrospective series, covering 328 patients, could confirm no advantage for any modality [30]. Judged on design quality and sample size alone, the comparison is not close. But that is not the comparison that matters. The question is not which body of evidence is methodologically stronger; it is which one describes the patient in front of the clinicians. A randomized trial conducted in a population whose injury mechanism differs from the one being treated does not become applicable by virtue of its size. Nor does a small retrospective series become sufficient by virtue of its relevance. What follows is an argument about the boundaries of external validity, not a claim that the smaller literature overturns the larger one.

The path beyond this impasse is unlikely to come from larger versions of the “early versus late” trial designs of the general-AKI literature. Herewith the heterogeneity of the heatstroke-AKI population is the core problem. Myoglobin burden, the tempo of renal injury, the presence or absence of coagulopathy, and time since hyperthermia onset all vary widely between patients. Any single dichotomous threshold will therefore be simultaneously too aggressive for some and too conservative for others. A more productive approach may integrate biomarkers into an individualized initiation decision. The decision to initiate blood purification would be guided by a composite assessment rather than a fixed threshold. Consider a patient with a rising L-FABP or NGAL concentration and a myoglobin level in the region of 5,000 to 10,000 ng/mL. Such a patient would be a candidate for early, preemptive extracorporeal clearance. This would apply even without conventional RRT indications. A patient with elevated myoglobin but normal tubular injury biomarkers and preserved urine output, by contrast, might be managed conservatively with aggressive volume expansion and cooling. This approach has been proposed for AKI in general [79] and has conceptual appeal for heatstroke specifically. It has not been tested prospectively in any population, let alone in exertional heatstroke.

A pragmatic, three-tier initiation framework may serve as a provisional clinical reference point (Figure 3). We offer it with the explicit caveat that it reflects the authors’ synthesis rather than a validated guideline. Tier 1, emergency initiation, applies when life-threatening complications are present: severe hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis refractory to medical management, or diuretic-resistant pulmonary edema. This is uncontroversial. Tier 2, preemptive initiation, applies when conventional RRT indications are absent but myoglobin exceeds 10,000 ng/mL. Additional triggers include rising tubular injury biomarkers, oliguria despite adequate volume resuscitation, or evidence of concomitant consumptive coagulopathy, provided the patient is within approximately 12 h of hyperthermia onset. This corresponds to the window before which established tubular necrosis becomes dominant. Tier 3, conservative monitoring, applies when myoglobin is below this threshold, tubular injury biomarkers are not elevated, and urine output is preserved. Patients in tier 3 should undergo serial monitoring at intervals not exceeding six hours, because myoglobin trajectory can change rapidly in the first 24 h. Three operational points make the Tier 2 trigger usable at the bedside. First, myoglobin should be measured as soon as possible after presentation. Renal clearance is rapid, and circulating concentrations may already be falling within the first 24 h after symptom onset [16]. Second, once extracorporeal removal has begun, the consensus ties re-measurement to the 8- to 12-h cartridge exchange interval [16]. It also advises continued measurement after treatment stops, with reinstatement if concentrations rebound [16]. Treatment is maintained until myoglobin is consistently below 5,000 ng/mL [16]. Third, the threshold cannot be applied identically to early and late presenters. A worker arriving many hours after collapse may fall below 10,000 ng/mL on a single sample despite a large cumulative myoglobin load. In that setting a low value should be read alongside creatine kinase and tubular injury markers, not as grounds for withholding therapy. Three caveats attach to the Tier 2 threshold. First, the 10,000 ng/mL value comes from a Delphi consensus on rhabdomyolysis in general, not from heatstroke-specific data [16]. There is a specific reason to doubt equivalence. In crush syndrome, which supplies much of the disaster-nephrology experience cited above, release follows decompression and behaves as a reperfusion bolus whose size tracks the time to extrication [85]. Exertional heatstroke involves continuing contraction and hyperthermia until cooling succeeds, so release is better described as sustained production. A single concentration can therefore mean different things in the two settings. The same 10,000 ng/mL may mark a falling bolus in one patient and a rising plateau in another. We retain the threshold because no heatstroke-specific alternative has been derived, not because biological equivalence has been shown. Second, that consensus reached no agreement on a creatine kinase threshold, which is why none appears here. Third, the approximately 12-h limit has partial external support. The same Delphi panel reached majority agreement that therapy should ideally start within 12 h of the onset of severe rhabdomyolysis [16]. Our framework anchors that interval to hyperthermia onset rather than to the onset or detection of rhabdomyolysis. That re-anchoring is our own inference from the temporal model in Section 4.5. No threshold in this tier has been tested against a renal outcome in a heatstroke population. A fourth caveat applies to the biomarker trigger. No validated threshold defines a significant rise in L-FABP or NGAL in this population. For operational purposes we treat a doubling from the admission value on a repeat sample drawn within six hours as a working definition. That figure is a proposal, not a validated cutoff. These numbers should be read as the best available anchors, not as validated cutoffs. This framework is provisional. Its validation in prospective cohorts is among the highest-priority research needs identified in the concluding section. The urgency is amplified by the occupational context: delayed or absent access to intensive care and CRRT compresses the decision-making interval and raises the stakes of getting the timing right.

Biomarker-augmented, three-tier framework for blood purification initiation in heatstroke-related acute kidney injury. Tier 1 applies when a life-threatening complication is present and is uncontested. Tier 2 applies when conventional indications are absent but myoglobin exceeds 10,000 ng/mL. Additional triggers are rising tubular injury biomarkers, oliguria despite adequate resuscitation, or consumptive coagulopathy, within approximately 12 h of the hyperthermic event. Tier 3 applies below this threshold and requires reassessment at intervals of 6 h or less. The myoglobin value derives from a Delphi consensus on rhabdomyolysis in general [16], not from heatstroke-specific data. That consensus reached no agreement on a creatine kinase threshold. A majority of that panel also supported initiation within 12 h of the onset of severe rhabdomyolysis. Re-anchoring that interval to the hyperthermic event is our inference from the temporal model in Figure 2. The rising-biomarker criterion is defined operationally in Section 7 and is proposed rather than validated. This is a provisional framework, not an evidence-validated guideline.

Special considerations in occupational settings

The translational frameworks developed in the preceding sections presume a clinical infrastructure that exists in well-resourced ICUs. The occupational heatstroke patient, by contrast, typically becomes symptomatic far from an ICU, whether in a sugarcane field, a construction site, an agricultural plot, or a military training ground. The nearest hospital with renal replacement capability may be hours away. The first responder is often a fellow worker with no medical training. This contextual mismatch is not merely a logistical inconvenience. It defines which strategies are feasible, which evidence gaps are most consequential, and which research designs are capable of generating implementable answers.

The pre-hospital interval in occupational heatstroke is the most consequential period for renal outcomes and the least studied. Core temperature continues to rise for as long as exertion persists and cooling is delayed. Each additional minute of hyperthermia extends the duration of direct thermal cytotoxicity against the tubular epithelium. In practice, the 2–6 h myoglobin clearance window is consumed substantially or entirely during the pre-hospital phase. It takes time for a worker to be discovered, for emergency services to arrive at a remote location, and for transport to a facility with extracorporeal capability. In the military setting, where on-site medics and rapid aeromedical evacuation compress this interval, the experience reported by Zhou and colleagues may be replicable [12]. In civilian settings, the interval from collapse to hospital arrival routinely exceeds four hours and may extend beyond 12. The therapeutic window for preemptive myoglobin clearance may close before the patient reaches the emergency department. The absence of laboratory infrastructure at the point of injury compounds this disadvantage. Serum creatinine, myoglobin, CK, and the biomarkers discussed in Section 5 are unavailable at the point of injury. Diversion to a tertiary center is guided by clinical signs (core temperature, mental status, urine color). These lack the granularity to distinguish reversible tubular stress from established necrosis.

The infrastructure gradient between where heatstroke occurs and where blood purification is delivered is not uniform geographically. In high-income countries, most occupational heatstroke patients can reach a hospital with CRRT within a timeframe that leaves the therapeutic window at least partially open. The situation is starkly different in the low- and middle-income countries where a disproportionate share of occupational heat exposure occurs. In Central America, South Asia, and sub-Saharan Africa, CRRT may be unavailable not only at the nearest hospital but at any hospital in the region [3]. Even intermittent hemodialysis may be the only available modality. High-cutoff membranes and hemoadsorption cartridges are generally not stocked. The experience from disaster nephrology reinforces that early aggressive fluid resuscitation is the single most effective preventive measure when RRT is unavailable [93]. For myoglobin removal, CRRT with convective clearance should be prioritized over intermittent hemodialysis. The finding that intermittent HCO hemodialysis can exceed continuous methods in myoglobin clearance acquires practical significance here [26]. The ADQI 30 consensus similarly emphasized matching blood purification strategies to locally available technology [29]. The referral decision itself, whether to transfer a patient from a facility with only intermittent hemodialysis to one offering CRRT and hemoadsorption, lacks an evidence base. The potential benefit must be weighed against the risk of deterioration during transport. This tradeoff has never been formally studied.

Prevention and surveillance acquire a kidney-specific dimension when viewed through the AKI-to-CKDnt lens. The rest-shade-hydration-hygiene (RSHH) intervention, prospectively evaluated in Nicaraguan sugarcane workers, has demonstrated efficacy in reducing incident kidney injury [22]. Low-cost measures include workplace heat-acclimatization protocols, environmental WBGT monitoring with mandatory work-rest cycling, and worker education on recognizing dark urine and reduced urine output as early indicators of rhabdomyolysis. The emerging availability of POCT biomarker testing raises the prospect of periodic surveillance in high-risk occupational cohorts. L-FABP has been validated in a field-deployable format [37], analogous to the periodic creatinine screening proposed for CKDnt surveillance [94]. Whether such a program can reduce severe AKI incidence or interrupt CKD progression has not been tested. Return-to-work assessment after occupational heatstroke-related AKI is another domain without guidance. The duration of convalescence needed, the risk of recurrent AKI upon re-exposure, and the role of biomarker normalization in clearance decisions remain unaddressed.

Climate change transforms these considerations from the occupational-health periphery to the public-health foreground. The increasing frequency, intensity, and geographic spread of heatwaves expands the exposed workforce beyond the tropical and subtropical bands historically studied [2]. Occupational heat exposure is expanding into regions not historically considered at risk [40]. The workforce itself is changing. In high-income countries, agricultural and construction laborers are aging, with higher rates of hypertension and diabetes. In low- and middle-income countries, internal and international migrant workers constitute a growing share of the heat-exposed workforce. This population is young but medically underserved and often excluded from surveillance systems [95]. Both trajectories increase population-level vulnerability and underscore the urgency of closing the evidence gaps listed below.

Evidence gaps and future research directions

The evidence reviewed in the preceding sections converges on a translational paradox. The pathophysiological rationale for early, biomarker-guided blood purification in heatstroke-related AKI is mechanistically strong. Each amplifier in the cascade is identifiable. Each node is potentially targetable. And myoglobin, the principal nephrotoxic mediator, is physically removable by extracorporeal techniques. Yet the clinical evidence required to implement this strategy in the workers who sustain the injury is largely absent. The gaps are systemic, spanning the biological frontier, the translational chasm, and the implementation vacuum. We set them out by feasibility of resolution (Table 3).

At the biological level, four uncertainties define the frontier. First, the relative contribution of ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and apoptosis at successive time points in the heatstroke cascade has not been quantified [34]. Simultaneous activation of multiple death programs suggests that single-pathway blockade may confer incomplete renoprotection. The magnitude of this redundancy, and whether it varies with hyperthermia severity and duration, remains unknown. Second, the quantitative contribution of the gut-kidney axis has not been characterized across the spectrum of heatstroke severity [35]. Endotoxin and myoglobin interact synergistically in mechanistic models, but whether this functions as a primary driver of late deterioration or a minor amplifier is undefined. Third, the temporal model has not been prospectively validated with serial biomarker sampling anchored to hyperthermia onset [10]. The transition point from reversible stress to established necrosis remains a biological construct, not a measurable clinical threshold. Fourth, the biomarker validation gap is deep and narrow. L-FABP data originate from a single research group [36,37]. NephroCheck has never been evaluated in heatstroke [9]. A systematic review emphasized that novel AKI biomarker performance in exertional settings varies with exercise intensity, which is uncontrolled in most field studies [82].

The translational deficits are more consequential because they determine whether mechanistic insight can reach the patient. The most fundamental gap is the absence of a prospective, biomarker-augmented cohort of civilian occupational heatstroke. Without it, the incidence, biomarker kinetics, and AKI-to-CKDnt transition rate have no reliable denominator. Every downstream research question is asked in an epidemiological vacuum. A second deficit concerns trial design. The landmark RRT-timing trials were built for sepsis-associated and post-surgical AKI. Their “early-versus-late” design cannot accommodate an injury where the therapeutic window is measured in hours from hyperthermia onset. A biomarker-stratified randomization design, allocating patients by a composite of myoglobin concentration and tubular-injury biomarker elevation, is conceptually appropriate but has never been tested in rhabdomyolysis or heatstroke. Third, the sole clinical report of blood purification in exertional heatstroke is a 16-patient series now 15 years old [12]. The ReplaceRhabdo pilot enrolled general rhabdomyolysis patients, not heatstroke-specific cases [39]. Fourth, diametrically opposed guideline interpretations expose a field where expert opinion substitutes for evidence [16,92]. Fifth, the referral decision has no published evidence base. Sixth, return-to-work guidance after heatstroke-related AKI is absent from the literature.

Closing these gaps requires research designed for the populations and settings where occupational heatstroke actually occurs. The feasibility gradient suggests a logical sequence. The most feasible starting point is a prospective occupational cohort with embedded serial biomarker collection. L-FABP POCT is already field-validated [37]. Combined with standardized clinical endpoints, it would generate the first reliable estimates of AKI incidence, biomarker performance, and AKI-to-CKDnt transition in civilian heat-exposed workers. The next step is a biomarker-stratified feasibility trial randomizing heatstroke patients to preemptive myoglobin clearance versus conservative management. The ReplaceRhabdo design is an available template, though enrollment criteria must capture exertional heatstroke specifically. Beyond the conventional RCT, designs adapted to the occupational setting offer methodological alternatives: cluster-randomized trials with the worksite as the unit of randomization, registry-embedded pragmatic trials, and stepped-wedge implementation designs. These align with the dispersed geography and resource heterogeneity of the target population. Combining the RSHH intervention [22] with periodic POCT biomarker surveillance represents a testable implementation bundle that would simultaneously address prevention and early-detection gaps. Heterogeneity of this magnitude also invites a different analytic approach. Rather than searching for a single initiation threshold, machine-learning estimation of individualized treatment effects could ask which combinations of biomarker trajectory, myoglobin burden, and elapsed time predict benefit. The obstacle is sample size: such methods need pooled multicenter data that no single center will generate. Data sharing across the cohorts proposed above is therefore a precondition, not an afterthought.

Climate change is not background context for this agenda; it is a design variable. The populations at greatest risk, agricultural and construction workers in low- and middle-income countries, migrant laborers excluded from surveillance, are precisely those least accessible to conventional RCTs. If the research methods deployed cannot accommodate this reality, the evidence base will continue to serve populations that least need it. The workers who sustain occupational heatstroke-related kidney injury will still lack the evidence required to guide their care. Bridging this methodological gap is the central message of this review.

Limitations of this review

This review has limitations that follow from both its design and its evidence base. The design is narrative rather than systematic. Searches were run in PubMed/MEDLINE only, because Embase and Web of Science were not accessible to the author, so records indexed exclusively elsewhere may have been missed. Screening and selection were performed by a single author and were not conducted against a pre-registered protocol. The number of records excluded at each intermediate step was therefore not recorded and cannot be reported in Figure 1. No formal risk-of-bias instrument was applied to the included sources. Eligibility was also restricted to sources with an English-language abstract. That constraint matters for a condition concentrated in Central America, South Asia and other regions where primary reports may appear only in other languages. Selection was purposive: sources were chosen for their contribution to the mechanistic, biomarker and timing arguments rather than by exhaustive enumeration. A systematic or scoping review with duplicate screening would give a more reproducible account of the same literature, and remains a reasonable next step.

The evidence base is the more substantial constraint. Only one clinical study reports blood purification outcomes in exertional heatstroke, and it is a 16-patient uncontrolled series [12]. No randomized trial has been conducted in this population, and every comparative modality dataset derives from general rhabdomyolysis or crush syndrome. Biomarker evidence specific to heat illness rests on two studies from a single research group [36,37]. The temporal model in Section 4.5 and the three-tier framework in Section 7 are therefore syntheses of indirect evidence. They are offered as structured hypotheses for testing, not as validated guidance.

Conclusions

Occupational heatstroke-related AKI is driven by a temporally organized, multi-pathway cascade. Myoglobin, a 17.8-kDa heme protein released by rhabdomyolysis and physically removable by extracorporeal techniques, serves as the central amplifier that converts systemic hyperthermia into kidney-targeted damage. The temporal model developed in this review organizes four interacting pathways (direct thermal cytotoxicity, rhabdomyolysis-driven myoglobin release, inflammatory-coagulation amplification, and gut-derived endotoxemia) into a sequence of therapeutically distinct windows. These span the 0–2 h cooling opportunity, the 2–6 h myoglobin clearance interval, and the 6–24 h inflammatory window. This framework provides a biological rationale for transitioning from creatinine-triggered, reactive RRT to biomarker-guided, preemptive blood purification. The three-tier initiation framework offers a provisional reference point, calibrated against the best available evidence while acknowledging that prospective validation has not been conducted.

The translational value of this framework extends from bench to field. At the mechanistic level, it identifies specific intervention nodes: ferroptosis in the proximal tubule, myoglobin cast formation in the distal nephron, and endotoxin sensitization of the renal endothelium. At the clinical level, it defines the biomarker panel and sampling schedule needed for prospective cohort studies and feasibility trials. At the implementation level, it specifies two immediately testable components: L-FABP POCT and the RSHH prevention bundle. Their combination could be evaluated in cluster-randomized designs adapted to the dispersed geography of the at-risk workforce (Box 1).

Four layers of challenge stand between this framework and its clinical validation. Experimentally, the relative contribution of ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and apoptosis remains unquantified, and death-pathway redundancy implies incomplete single-pathway renoprotection. Epidemiologically, the absence of a prospective cohort means incidence, biomarker kinetics, and AKI-to-CKDnt transition rates lack reliable estimates. Methodologically, the landmark RRT-timing trials were designed for a different biology and cannot be transposed. Operationally, the populations at greatest risk are least accessible to conventional RCTs, and machine-learning methods for estimating individualized treatment effects have not been applied.

Resolution and validation are reciprocally dependent. Prospective cohort data would calibrate the temporal model against human physiology. Biomarker-stratified feasibility trials would generate the evidence to convert the three-tier framework from provisional synthesis to evidence-supported algorithm. Implementation studies across the infrastructure gradient would test real-world adaptability. Each validation cycle would strengthen biological foundations and clinical utility. We would advance from expert consensus toward evidence-informed consensus. The urgency of initiating this cycle is amplified by climate change, which expands the geographic range, seasonal duration, and population-attributable fraction of occupational heat exposure. It lends immediacy to a research agenda that might otherwise be deferred. For practice, three implications follow directly from this synthesis. Myoglobin should be measured early and repeatedly in workers presenting with exertional heatstroke, because the value that matters is a trajectory rather than a single number. Blood purification decisions should be timed against the interval since hyperthermia onset, not against creatinine stage alone. And the three-tier framework should be used as a structured way to record why a decision was made, not as an authority for making it.
