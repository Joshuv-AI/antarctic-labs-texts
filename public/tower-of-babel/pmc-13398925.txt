A Prediction Model for One‐Year Disability Risk Among Community‐Dwelling Older Adults in China: Integrating Physical, Metabolic, and Psychosocial Factors
Authors: Yanjun Ma, Chaofan Geng, Zhibin Wang, Yiwei Zhao, Lixin Ma, Pengpeng Ye, Leilei Duan, Guoping Peng, Yi Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDisability in older adults, characterized by progressive limitations in performing activities of daily living, poses a significant public health challenge in aging societies. Early identification of individuals at risk is critical for implementing targeted interventions to mitigate functional decline. However, existing prediction models often prioritize disease‐related physiological indicators and overlook psychosocial dimensions, limiting their practicality and scalability in community‐based settings. This study aimed to develop and validate a practicable prediction model for 1‐year disability risk by integrating multidimensional indicators.MethodsA prospective community‐based cohort in Beijing, functionally independent at baseline, was followed for 1 year. Disability was defined as a decline in the Barthel Index score. Potential predictors included demographic, lifestyle, clinical, physical, and psychosocial measures. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used for variable selection, followed by logistic regression to construct the final model. Model performance was evaluated through fivefold cross‐validation repeated 100 times, with assessment of discrimination and calibration.ResultsAmong 1003 community‐dwelling older adults (mean age 68.4 ± 5.2 years; 58.3% female), 163 (16.3%) developed disability at follow‐up. The LASSO regression identified seven predictors: age, dyslipidemia, gait speed, waist circumference, difficulty in lifting weights, appetite, and emotion regulation ability. The model demonstrated moderate discrimination, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.716 (95% CI: 0.712–0.720). Calibration curves indicated good overall agreement between predicted and observed risks. A nomogram was developed to facilitate individualized risk prediction in clinical practice.ConclusionsThis study presents a practical disability risk prediction model incorporating physical, metabolic, and psychosocial factors. The model exhibits acceptable discrimination and calibration, supporting its potential for early screening and stratified management of community‐dwelling older adults. Future multicenter validations are warranted to enhance generalizability and explore dynamic interventions targeting modifiable factors like emotion regulation and gait speed.Keywords: community‐based cohort, disability risk prediction, emotion regulation, gait speed, LASSO regression, nomogram, older adults

Background

Disability in older adults generally refers to a persistent or progressive limitation in performing activities of daily living due to chronic diseases or frailty, requiring assistance from others [1, 2]. It represents a major global public health and social security challenge, characterized by high prevalence and substantial disability burden [3, 4]. With the rapid acceleration of population aging in China, individuals aged 60 years and older numbered approximately 297 million in 2023, accounting for 21.1% of the total population [5, 6, 7]. The increasing dependence and functional limitations among older adults are strongly associated with adverse outcomes such as falls, depression, malnutrition, and cognitive impairment, further increasing the demand for family caregiving and long‐term care services [8, 9, 10].

Previous community‐based practices have predominantly focused on disease‐related physiological indicators, such as blood pressure, lipid levels, and comorbidity burden, while paying insufficient attention to psychological and social participation dimensions [11, 12, 13]. This narrow focus reduces sensitivity to early functional decline and constrains the external validity and contextual applicability of risk assessment models. In particular, given the substantial regional disparities in medical resources and diagnostic accessibility across China [14, 15], there remains a lack of a disability‐risk prediction tool that effectively balances feasibility and predictive performance and can be sustainably implemented in routine primary‐care follow‐up.

To address the gap in psychosocial assessment and the lack of a feasible predictive tool, this study adopted the framework of the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) to comprehensively integrate key dimensions of body structure and function, activity and participation, as well as environmental and personal factors [16, 17, 18]. We conducted a prospective study among community‐dwelling older adults in China. The study hypothesized that, beyond routinely accessible physiological indicators, psychosocial factors such as psychological resilience and emotion regulation have independent predictive value for early disability risk. Accordingly, a 1‐year follow‐up was undertaken to construct and validate a nomogram‐based model for predicting disability risk.

Taken together, this study aims to establish a simple, interpretable, and scalable screening tool for early identification of disability risk among community‐dwelling older adults, thereby providing an evidence‐based reference for primary health management and policy formulation in promoting healthy aging in China.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This study was designed as a prospective community‐based cohort study. Participants were recruited from the Beijing Disability Risk and Ageing Monitoring Study (BEAM, ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06394817). The baseline assessments of the BEAM were conducted between May and September 2023, followed by a 1‐year longitudinal follow‐up in 2024. The BEAM study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University (Approval [2022]234). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The detailed description of the protocol of the BEAM as well as the characteristics of participants at the baseline survey of the BEAM had been reported elsewhere [19]. Participants in the BEAM were eligible for inclusion in this analysis if they met all of the following criteria: (1) aged 60 years or older at baseline; (2) having a Barthel Index score of 100 at baseline; and (3) attending the 1‐year follow‐up survey.

Measurements of Potential Predictors

Multi‐dimensional information was collected as potential predictors at the baseline of the BEAM study, encompassing demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, history of chronic diseases, physical measurements, psychological status, and functional and nutritional status. Demographic information included gender, age, marital status, and educational attainment. Lifestyle variables encompassed smoking and drinking habits, tea and coffee consumption, engagement in household chores, gardening, television viewing, internet use, and hobby participation. History of chronic conditions—such as hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, stroke, coronary heart disease, osteoarthritis, Parkinson's disease, dementia, malignant tumors, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease—was documented based on self‐reporting combined with medical verification. Physical function was assessed using the Strength, Assistance with walking, Rise from a chair, Climb stairs, and Falls (SARC‐F) questionnaire [20], and depressive symptoms were evaluated with the 15‐item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS‐15) [21, 22, 23]. Anthropometric parameters included height, body weight, waist circumference, and blood pressure. Lower extremity function was measured by the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) [24, 25], and nutritional status was determined using the Mini Nutritional Assessment–Short Form (MNA‐SF) [26, 27]. To enhance model parsimony, individual items from the SARC‐F, GDS‐15, SPPB, and MNA‐SF—rather than overall scale scores—were treated as separate predictor variables. Additionally, emotion regulation ability and psychological resilience were assessed to capture the capacity to cope with negative emotional experiences. A total of 86 variables at baseline survey were utilized as candidate predictors. All binary variables were assigned a code of 1 (present/yes) or 0 (absent/no).

All data collection was carried out through in‐person interviews administered by uniformly trained investigators, utilizing a suite of standardized questionnaires and structured physical assessments. To ensure immediate data quality, all completed forms underwent on‐site review for logical consistency and completeness. Any missing entries or internal inconsistencies were addressed in real‐time by reconfirming with the participants. Furthermore, each questionnaire was subjected to an independent, double‐blind verification by two dedicated quality‐control reviewers to maximize accuracy and minimize potential biases.

Definition of Disability

Disability was defined according to functional status measured by the Barthel Index of Activities of Daily Living (ADL) [28, 29]. The Barthel Index comprises 10 items: feeding, bathing, grooming, dressing, toileting, bowel control, bladder control, chair/bed transfer, walking, and stair climbing, with a score of 100 reflecting full function, 60–99 indicating mild limitation, 40–59 moderate limitation, 20–39 severe limitation, and scores below 20 representing complete dependency [30]. In this study, disability was operationally defined as any reduction in the total Barthel Index of ADL, representing early functional deterioration [30]. This approach identifies subclinical declines in daily functioning and serves as an early indicator for recognizing community‐dwelling older adults at risk of progressing to disability.

Statistical Analysis

The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was employed to perform feature selection, mitigating potential multicollinearity and overfitting issues arising from the high‐dimensional dataset. The optimal penalty parameter (λ) was determined via fivefold cross‐validation. To promote model sparsity and enhance interpretability, the final model was selected using the lambda.1se criterion. Predictors retained by the LASSO procedure were subsequently entered into a logistic regression to construct the final prediction model.

Model performance was comprehensively evaluated in terms of discrimination and calibration. Internal validation was conducted using a fivefold cross‐validation approach. The dataset was randomly partitioned into five roughly equal subsets. In each iteration, four subsets were used for training and the remaining subset for testing; this process was repeated until all subsets had served as the test set once. To improve the robustness and stability of the model, the entire fivefold cross‐validation procedure was repeated 100 times, resulting in a total of 500 training‐testing iterations. The model's discriminative ability was assessed by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), along with its mean and 95% confidence interval (CI). The calibration performance of the model was evaluated using a calibration curve with 1000 bootstrap resamples for visual assessment, along with the Hosmer‐Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test for statistical evaluation of the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. Finally, a nomogram was constructed based on the final logistic regression model to enable individualized risk prediction. To enhance the interpretability of the model and provide both global and individual‐level insights into feature contributions, we employed SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis.

All analyses, including predictor selection, model validation, and visualization, were performed using R software (version 4.4.2) (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the glmnet (v4.1.10), rms (v8.1.0), caret (v7.0.1), and pROC (v1.18.5) packages. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The issue of missing data was addressed by mean/mode imputation, a method justified by the fact that no variable had more than four missing observations in the study cohort.

Assessment of calibration revealed that the model tended to provide slightly conservative risk estimates in the low‐risk range (predicted probability < 5%), while showing minor overestimation in the high‐risk range (predicted probability > 40%). Nonetheless, the calibration curve indicated satisfactory overall agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test further supported adequate calibration, yielding a non‐significant p of 0.722, which suggests no substantial deviation from perfect calibration.

Additionally, a user‐friendly nomogram was developed from the final model to enable straightforward estimation of individual disability risk, as visualized in Figure 4. For practical implementation, we propose a 15% probability threshold. This value offers the best practical balance between sensitivity (68.1%) and specificity (64.3%) to ensure cost‐effectiveness in screening. Nomogram for predicting 1‐year disability risk in community‐dwelling older adults. To use the nomogram, first locate the individual's value for each predictor and draw a vertical line to the “Points” axis to determine the corresponding score. Sum the points for all predictors, then locate the total on the “Total Points” axis to determine the corresponding disability risk probability. Predictor assessments were standardized as follows: age (in years); dyslipidemia (1 = present and 0 = absent, based on medical verification or self‐report); gait speed (time in seconds to complete a 4‐meter walk); waist circumference (in cm); difficulty in lifting weights (Ability to lift and carry 4.5 kg, scored as 0 for “No difficulty”, 1 for “Some difficulty”, or 2 for “A lot of difficulty”); appetite and emotion regulation (rated on a 5‐point Likert scale, where 1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, and 5 = strongly agree). At thresholds of 10%, 15% (recommended), and 20%, the model demonstrated sensitivities of 85.3%, 68.1%, and 54.0%, and specificities of 43.1%, 64.3%, and 78.9%, respectively.

Discussion

Based on a prospective cohort of community‐dwelling older adults in Beijing, this study developed and validated a prediction model for disability risk among older adults who were functionally independent at baseline. Through LASSO regression, seven key predictors—age, dyslipidemia, gait speed, waist circumference, difficulty in lifting weights, appetite, and emotion regulation ability—were identified and incorporated into the final model. The model demonstrated moderate discriminative ability with an AUC of 0.716 (95% CI: 0.712–0.720) and satisfactory calibration upon internal cross‐validation. These findings suggest that an assessment framework combining easily measurable physiological indicators, such as gait speed and waist circumference, with psychosocial factors reflecting emotional resilience, provides a practical and scalable tool for the early identification of disability risk in community‐dwelling older adults.

Notably, dyslipidemia was identified as a potential protective factor against disability—a counterintuitive finding consistent with the “lipid paradox” reported in other aging populations [31, 32]. This underscores the importance of cautiously applying health standards derived from general adult populations when assessing older adults, especially the very old. Although the underlying mechanism of the lipid paradox remains incompletely elucidated, nutritional status has been proposed as one contributing factor [32].

Gait speed, a well‐established objective indicator of mobility, neuromuscular coordination, and overall health in older adults, is widely regarded as the “sixth vital sign” for predicting functional decline and disability [33, 34]. In this study, slower gait speed was significantly associated with increased disability risk, reinforcing its reliability as a functional status predictor. Supporting this, a Japanese cohort study showed that slower gait speed was linked to higher disability incidence, with physical activity mediating its association with mortality, suggesting that gait speed reflects both physical capacity and activity sustainability [34]. Similarly, the French Three‐City study reported that the rate of gait speed decline independently predicted subsequent disability, indicating that dynamic changes capture functional deterioration more effectively than single measurements [35, 36]. Collectively, these findings highlight gait speed as a simple yet powerful marker for early detection of disability risk.

Waist circumference, a key indicator of abdominal fat distribution, provides a more accurate measure of metabolic abnormalities and functional decline risk than traditional measures such as body weight or body mass index (BMI) [37, 38]. Central obesity showed a strong association with disability risk in older adults [38]. Previous longitudinal studies indicated that individuals in the highest waist circumference quintile had significantly elevated risks of mobility limitation and self‐care decline—even after adjusting for BMI—suggesting that abdominal fat accumulation exerts effects independent of overall obesity [39, 40]. Moreover, dynamic changes in waist circumference have been shown to reflect alterations in body composition and functional capacity over time [39, 40, 41]. These results consistently link central obesity to functional decline across populations, underscoring the importance of maintaining optimal waist circumference levels to preserve physical independence and reduce disability risk.

Emotion regulation—defined as the ability to manage and adapt emotional responses to stress—serves as a critical psychological resource for maintaining mental health and social functioning [42, 43]. Impaired regulation or persistent depressive states may accelerate physical decline and increase disability risk through neuroendocrine, inflammatory, and behavioral pathways [44]. Studies have shown that emotional, cognitive, and physical factors collectively influence daily functioning, with poorer emotion regulation associated with lower ADL performance [45]. Conversely, positive regulation strategies such as cognitive reappraisal and emotional reframing are linked to fewer functional limitations and better quality of life [46]. Thus, emotion regulation represents a modifiable determinant of disability risk, and integrating emotional management and psychosocial support into community health programs may enhance resilience, delay functional decline, and promote healthy aging.

The strength of this study lies in its integration of multidimensional information, particularly the inclusion of psychological factors, to develop a simple yet practical prediction model. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the generalizability of our findings requires careful consideration. The participant cohort was recruited solely from Beijing, which may limit applicability to other regions with different demographic or socioeconomic profiles. Furthermore, the model performance was evaluated through internal cross‐validation within the original cohort; it has not yet been externally validated in an independent population. Future studies are needed to test the model's robustness and transportability across diverse, external settings. Second, disability assessment relied solely on the Barthel Index of ADL, without incorporating higher‐level functional measures. Future studies would benefit from incorporating complementary measures such as Instrumental Activities of Daily Living scales and objective cognitive assessments to more comprehensively identify subclinical impairment and enhance the model's predictive accuracy for community independence. This may lead to an underestimation of subclinical functional impairment. Third, some chronic disease data were based on self‐reports, which could introduce recall bias. Nonetheless, the relatively straightforward data collection approach employed in this study may facilitate the model's scalability and potential for broader implementation in community settings. Fourth, while the predictive model in this study was developed for a 1‐year timeframe, functional decline among community‐dwelling older adults often manifests over a longer period. However, this 1‐year prediction window also enables the identification of individuals who require more immediate intervention.

Conclusions

This study identified seven independent predictors of 1‐year disability risk in initially independent older adults: age, dyslipidemia, gait speed, difficulty in lifting weights, waist circumference, appetite, and emotion regulation ability. The resulting model demonstrated good discrimination and calibration, supporting its use for early screening and stratified management in primary care. These findings highlight the role of physical, metabolic, and psychological factors in late‐life disability risk. Further multicenter, longitudinal studies are needed for external validation and to assess the model's practical utility. Future work should also integrate objective biomarkers and social‐support variables to improve predictive accuracy, and explore machine learning methods, including formal comparisons among a broader set of algorithms, to develop dynamic, ICF‐informed prediction frameworks.
