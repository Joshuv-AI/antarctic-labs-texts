Is there a relationship between smartphone addiction and cyber dating abuse among nursing students?
Authors: Rabiye Erenoğlu, Esma Elif KILIÇ
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDigital technologies, which are widely used today, are increasingly associated with the facilitation of violence-related behaviors. The aim of this study was to determine the relationship between smartphone addiction and cyberdating abuse, and some of the factors influencing this relationship.MethodsDescriptive and cross-sectional study. The sample of the study consisted of all students studying in the nursing department of a state university in a province located in the Mediterranean region of southern Turkey. The total sample size required for the research was determined as 224. “Personal Information Form”, “Smartphone Addiction Scale Short Form” and “Cyber Dating Abuse Scale” were used as data collection tools. The data was analyzed using descriptive statistics, Pearson Correlation test. Two Mann-Whitney U, Independent-t tests, Kruskal Wallis and One-Way Anova test. The study followed the STROBE reporting guideline.ResultsOf the participants, 50.9% were aged 18–20 years, 72.3% were female, 25.9% were third-year students, and 94.9% were single. The mean Smartphone Addiction Scale score was 28.30 ± 9.97, and the mean Cyber Dating Abuse Scale score was 56.30 ± 26.39. A weak but statistically significant positive relationship was found between smartphone addiction and all sub-dimensions of cyber dating abuse, including exposure to monitoring/control (r = 0.287, p = 0.003), exposure to direct aggression (r = 0.291, p < 0.001), exposure to violence (r = 0.278, p < 0.001), monitoring/control behaviors (r = 0.199, p = 0.003), direct aggression behaviors (r = 0.231, p < 0.001), and violence behaviors (r = 0.287, p < 0.001).ConclusionA weak but statistically significant positive relationship was found between smartphone addiction and cyber dating abuse.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Smartphone addiction, Cyber dating abuse, Nursing students, Digital technologies, Addiction

Introduction

The widespread adoption of information and communication technology, especially among younger generations, has fundamentally changed society’s consumption of information and consequently transformed communication and interaction patterns. However, alongside the positive effects of innovative technologies, increasing evidence suggests emerging problems related to their misuse [1]. Among these technologies, the internet plays a significant role in identity formation, personal autonomy, and establishing close relationships during young adults. However, it may also be misused for controlling, harassing, and exploiting romantic partners [2]. Furthermore, by increasing accessibility and exposure in digital environments, it may contribute to various forms of victimization, including cyber harassment, cyberbullying, and cyber dating abuse (CDA).

Cyber dating abuse is a form of intimate partner violence that occurs within romantic relationships through digital communication technologies [3]. Previous studies indicate that CDA is a widespread issue among adolescents and young adults [1–4]. A systematic review reported that CDA rates range between 8.1% in the past year and 93.7% over a lifetime [4].

Cyber dating abuse can be explained through Social Learning Theory, which suggests that individuals learn behaviors by observing and imitating others. Exposure to aggressive or controlling behaviors in both online and offline environments may increase the likelihood of reproducing such behaviors in romantic relationships [5].

Recent evidence has associated dating violence with substance use, emotional distress (anger, anxiety, sadness), antisocial behaviors, risky sexual behaviors, exposure to violence, and adherence to traditional gender roles [6, 7].

Considering that cyber dating abuse is mediated through digital communication technologies, smartphone addiction may also be an important contributing factor. Smartphone addiction is a behavioral addiction characterized by loss of control over smartphone use, continued use despite negative consequences, and impaired emotional regulation [8]. Excessive smartphone use may negatively affect users’ personal and social functioning and produce addiction-like symptoms [9]. It is defined as excessive and uncontrolled smartphone use that interferes with daily life [10].

From the perspective of problematic smartphone use literature, excessive smartphone use is conceptualized as a behavioral addiction characterized by loss of control, compulsive engagement, and impairment in daily functioning [5, 8]. Such problematic use has been associated with negative interpersonal outcomes, including reduced relationship satisfaction, increased jealousy, and controlling behaviors in romantic relationships. In this context, excessive smartphone use may contribute to vulnerability to cyber dating abuse. Worldwide, smartphone addiction is increasing among adolescents and young adults and negatively affects biopsychosocial well-being. Prevalence rates exceed 25% in several regions, including South Asia, the Middle East, East Asia, and Western countries, and are projected to reach 84% by 2025 [11, 12]. Smartphone addiction has been associated with anxiety, depression, ADHD, stress, low self-esteem, social anxiety, and poor psychological health [12–14]. Evidence also suggests associations between digital addiction and online aggression, including cyberbullying behaviors [15].

Previous studies have examined cyber dating abuse in relation to various psychosocial and behavioral factors, including substance use, emotional distress, and risky online behaviors. However, these factors have generally been investigated separately. Similarly, research on smartphone addiction has primarily focused on its psychological consequences, such as anxiety, depression, and impaired social functioning, whereas its interpersonal and relational effects have received comparatively less attention [1–12].

Although some studies have indirectly suggested a possible association between problematic smartphone use and online aggressive behaviors, its specific relationship with cyber dating abuse remains insufficiently explored. Although indirect evidence exists suggesting a potential association [16–18], no study has directly examined the relationship between smartphone addiction and cyber dating abuse among nursing students. Given the increasing use of digital communication technologies among young people in Türkiye and the limited research addressing cyber dating abuse within this cultural context, further investigation is needed to provide context-specific evidence and enhance understanding of this phenomenon.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the relationship between smartphone addiction and cyber dating abuse among nursing students and to identify related influencing factors. Nursing students were selected as the study population because they represent future healthcare professionals who will play an important role in health promotion, violence prevention, and the early identification of individuals at risk. As young adults who actively use digital technologies, they constitute a relevant group for examining the potential interaction between smartphone addiction and cyber dating abuse. Furthermore, their dual role as both healthcare professionals in training and members of a high-risk young adult population makes them an appropriate sample for understanding technology-related behavioral risks.

The hypotheses of the study are as follows:

H1

Nursing students have a measurable level of attitudes toward cyber dating abuse.

H2

Nursing students have a measurable level of smartphone addiction.

H3

Nursing students’ attitudes toward cyber dating abuse and smartphone addiction differ significantly according to sociodemographic characteristics.

H4

There is a significant relationship between nursing students’ smartphone addiction and their attitudes toward cyber dating abuse.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was conducted as a descriptive cross-sectional research. The study was reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guideline.

Target population and sample

The population of the study consisted of all students (N = 454) enrolled in the nursing department of a state university located in a province in the Mediterranean region of Turkey. The sample size was calculated using the Raosoft sample size calculator, based on a total population of 454 nursing students, with a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error [20]. The minimum required sample size was determined to be 209. The sample was selected using a convenience sampling method. Taking possible losses into account during data collection, surveys were distributed to 224 students. The research was completed with 224 nursing students who had past or current dating relationships and used smartphones. A post hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power version 3.1.9.7 [18] to support the selected sample size. In the post hoc power analysis conducted based on the correlation coefficient (rho: 0.278, p < 0.05) between the Total Score Average of the “Smartphone Addiction Scale Short Form” and the Total Score Average of the “Cyber dating abuse Scale” using the study data with a total sample size of 224, it was determined that the research sample size (n:224) was sufficient with 99% power, α = 0.05 significance level.

Preparation of the data collection tools

In the research, the “Personal Information Form”, which was developed by the researchers by scanning the relevant literature [1–12], and the standardized instruments “Smartphone Addiction Scale Short Form” and “Cyber Dating Abuse Scale”, which were previously developed and validated in the literature, were used as data collection tools. In this study, permission to use the scales was obtained via e-mail from the researchers who carried out the validity and reliability studies of the scales in the Turkish context.

Personal information form

The form includes questions about demographic data such as age, gender, class, place of residence for the longest period, income level, and parents’ educational status, as well as questions about dating violence and smartphone use.

Smartphone addiction scale short form

Developed by Kwon et al. (2013) and adapted into Turkish by Noyan et al. (2015). The scale items are scored from 1 to 6. Scale scores range from 10 to 60. As the scores obtained from the test increase, the risk for addiction is considered to increase. The scale is unifactorial and does not have subscales. In the study of Noyan et al., Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was found to be 0.867. In this study, the reliability coefficient of the scale was calculated as 0.873.

Cyber dating abuse scale

Developed by Borrajo, Gamez-Guadix, Pereda, and Calvete (2015) to examine experiences of cyber dating abuse in individuals aged 18–30. Adapted into Turkish by Bakır and Kalkan (2019). The scale is divided into two dimensions: perpetration of cyber violence and victimization of cyber violence. The scale has two sub-dimensions: direct aggression and monitoring/control. In the direct aggression dimension, there are 11 items (questions 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, 15, 16, and 18), while the monitoring/control dimension consists of 9 items (questions 1, 5, 7, 11, 13, 14, 17, 19, and 20). The scale is responded to with a 6-point Likert scale (1 never, 2 rarely, 3, 4, 5, 6 always). As scores increase, individuals’ tendencies to experience and perpetrate cyber dating abuse increase. Cronbach’s Alpha internal consistency values for exposure to monitoring/control were 0.85, for perpetration of monitoring/control were 0.84, for exposure to direct aggression were 0.78, and for perpetration of direct aggression were 0.79. The test-retest correlation for measuring the stability of the scale was found to be 0.80 for exposure to violence and 0.77 for perpetration of violence. In the validity and reliability study of the scale, Cronbach’s Alpha value was found to be 0.85 for Exposure to Violence Scale and 0.85 for Perpetration of Violence Scale. In this research, the calculated Cronbach’s Alpha internal consistency coefficient is 0.912 for exposure to monitoring/control, 0.862 for perpetration of monitoring/control, 0.936 for exposure to direct aggression, and 0.922 for perpetration of direct aggression.

Data collection

Students were informed about the aim and scope of the study, and both verbal and written informed consent were obtained from those who agreed to participate. Data were collected between October and December 2023 through self-administered questionnaires in classroom settings. The completion of the questionnaires took approximately 25–30 min.

Data analysis

The data obtained from the research were analyzed using IBM SPSS 23.0 (IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences) software package. The normality distributions of the data were evaluated using skewness and kurtosis values, Shapiro-Wilk test, histograms, and Q-Q plots. Descriptive statistics such as frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation were used for descriptive variables, while the relationships between scales were evaluated using the Pearson correlation test. Normality of the data distribution was assessed, and parametric tests were applied for variables showing a normal distribution. Accordingly, for variables meeting the assumption of normality, an independent samples t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, and One-Way ANOVA was used for comparisons among more than two groups. For variables that did not meet the assumption of normality, non-parametric tests were employed; the Mann-Whitney U test was used for two-group comparisons, and the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for comparisons involving more than two groups. The statistical significance of the obtained data was evaluated at the p < 0.05 level. The reliability of the scale items was tested using Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

All procedures performed in studies involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Prior to data collection, ethics approval was obtained from the Social and Human Sciences Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee of Hatay Mustafa Kemal University (Decision Date: December 7, 2022; Decision No: 20), institutional permission was secured. Both written (signed) and verbal informed consent were obtained from all participants.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of participants

More than half of the students (50.9%) were aged 18–20 years, 72.3% were female, 25.9% were third-year students, and 94.9% were single. Most participants had a nuclear family (84.4%), 33.5% had two siblings, 48.2% reported their mothers’ education level as primary school, and 31.7% reported their fathers’ education level as primary school. Additionally, 77.2% reported a moderate income level, and 76.3% lived in the city center.

The distribution of students’ views on dating abuse and smartphone addiction

In the research, it was found that 60.3% of the students had a history of past dating relationships, and currently they did not have any dating relationships. Additionally, it was determined that 50.9% of the students spent 3–6 h daily using smartphones, 50.4% spent 3–6 h daily on the internet using smartphones, and 61.6% mainly used the internet for entertainment and games. More than half of the students (64.3%) stated that they would feel lost without their smartphones, and 33% indicated that they would experience feelings of depression, etc., if they did not use smartphones (Table 1).

Discussion

Digital technologies, widely used today, can increasingly serve as tools for perpetrating violence. Although there are limited studies investigating technology-facilitated violence behaviors [16–24], evidence directly examining the relationship between smartphone addiction and cyber dating abuse remains scarce. Therefore, this study aimed to contribute to the literature by examining this relationship among nursing students.

In the present study, more than half of the students (64.3%) reported feeling lost without their smartphones, and 33% reported depressive feelings when not using them. The mean Smartphone Addiction Scale score indicated a low level of addiction (28.30 ± 9.97) In addition, more than half of the participants (50.9%) reported daily smartphone use of 3–6 h. However, considering that smartphone use exceeding 5 h per day is often regarded as indicative of problematic use, this finding alone does not necessarily reflect a high level of addiction. In line with this, the mean smartphone addiction score in the present study indicates a low level of addiction among participants. Previous studies have reported that a considerable proportion of university students may be at risk for smartphone addiction [25], and that excessive smartphone use is associated with anxiety and depression among young adults [26, 27]. Furthermore, some studies have identified moderate to high levels of smartphone addiction in student populations [28–30]. Nevertheless, the findings of the current study suggest that, although smartphone use is common, the overall level of addiction in this sample remains relatively low.

In this study, the levels of cyber dating abuse exposure and perpetration were found to be relatively low (56.30 ± 26.39). A systematic review reported that the prevalence of cyber dating abuse perpetration among adolescents was 24.0%, while victimization was 36.9% [31, 32], indicating variability across populations and measurement approaches.

A weak but statistically significant positive relationship was found between smartphone addiction and cyber dating abuse. Significant correlations were observed between smartphone addiction scores and Cyber Dating Abuse sub-dimensions, including Exposure to Monitoring/Control (r=0.287), Exposure to Direct Aggression (r=0.199), Exposure to Violence (r=0.278), Monitoring/Control Application (r=0.291), Direct Aggression Application (r=0.231), and Violence Application (r=0.287) (p < 0.01). These findings are consistent with previous studies suggesting that increased use of digital technologies may be associated with controlling and aggressive behaviors in romantic relationships [32–38].

The literature indicates that technology-facilitated abuse is associated with increased jealousy, insecurity, and controlling behaviors in intimate relationships. Therefore, digital platforms may simultaneously serve both positive communication functions and negative relational dynamics in youth relationships.

However, an important limitation of this study is that a considerable proportion of participants were not currently in a romantic relationship. This may have influenced responses related to cyber dating abuse, potentially limiting the generalizability of findings regarding dating-specific behaviors. Overall, although cyber dating abuse levels were low, the observed positive relationship with smartphone addiction highlights the potential role of digital dependency in shaping unhealthy relational patterns among young individuals.

Strengths and limitations of the study

One of the strengths of this study is that it adds to the existing literature by examining the relationship between smartphone addiction and cyber dating abuse among nursing students. The findings provide empirical evidence on a relatively underexplored topic in this population. However, the results should be interpreted with caution due to the weak strength of the observed correlations.

The study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design does not allow for the determination of causal relationships or changes over time. Second, the use of self-report measures may introduce response bias. Finally, the sample was limited to nursing students; therefore, the findings cannot be generalized to all student populations or other settings.

Conclusion

Dating violence is an important public health issue due to its prevalence and negative effects on individuals’ physical and mental health. Therefore, increasing awareness among young people and strengthening their ability to recognize related risk factors is important. Nurses, as educators and counselors, may play a key role in supporting individuals and contributing to the identification and prevention of dating violence.

The findings of this study showed that 64.3% of nursing students reported feeling lost without their smartphones, and 33% reported experiencing negative emotions such as depressive feelings when not using them. The mean smartphone addiction score was 28.30 ± 9.97, and 50.9% of participants reported daily smartphone use of 3–6 h. A weak, statistically significant positive association was found between smartphone addiction and cyber dating abuse. This finding should be interpreted with caution due to the cross-sectional design of the study.

Overall, the results suggest a possible association between problematic smartphone use and cyber dating abuse among nursing students. Further longitudinal studies are needed to confirm this relationship and to explore potential preventive interventions targeting technology-related risk behaviors.

Implications for nursing

It is important to raise awareness among young people regarding cyber dating violence and to strengthen their ability to recognize related behavioral and psychosocial risk factors. The findings of this study suggest that smartphone addiction may be associated with attitudes toward cyber dating abuse, indicating its potential relevance in preventive mental health and violence screening approaches.

Nurses play a key role in early identification, prevention, and intervention in issues affecting psychosocial health, including violence-related behaviors. As frontline healthcare professionals, nurses are well-positioned to assess risk behaviors, provide counseling, and guide individuals toward appropriate support services.

In this context, integrating awareness of problematic smartphone use into nursing education curricula may strengthen students’ competence in identifying digital risk behaviors linked to interpersonal violence. Additionally, incorporating brief screening questions on smartphone use and digital behavior into routine nursing assessments, particularly in adolescent and young adult populations, may support early detection of at-risk individuals.

Furthermore, school-based and community health programs led by nurses can include psychoeducation sessions focusing on healthy digital behavior, relationship safety, and violence prevention. The findings of this study may contribute to the development of structured educational, preventive, and screening-based nursing interventions targeting cyber dating abuse.
