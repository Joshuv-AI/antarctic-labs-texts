Prevalence and associated factors of intestinal parasitic infections in the Badi indigenous communities of Western Nepal
Authors: Jitendra Gautam, Rajendra Prasad Parajuli, Kishor Pandey
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite years of a national deworming campaign, the incidence of intestinal parasitic infections (IPIs) remains relatively high among certain ethnic groups, with lifestyle factors distinct from those of the general population. Despite existing studies on the prevalence of IPIs, there is limited research exploring the contextual associated factors within the Badi Indigenous community. Our objective in this study was to assess the prevalence of IPIs in the indigenous Badi community of Gurbhakot Municipality, Surkhet, Nepal, considering socioeconomic, lifestyle, and behavioral factors. A total of 205 stool samples were obtained from individuals aged 18–70 years through purposive sampling and preserved in a 2.5% potassium dichromate solution. A structured questionnaire was administered to the selected participants. Three diagnostic methods were used: direct wet mount, saturated salt flotation, and formal ether sedimentation. The study revealed an overall parasite prevalence of 27% (n = 55) in the indigenous Badi communities. Protozoan infections were found in 19% (n = 39) of the studied community, whereas helminthic infections were present in only 7.8% (n = 16). The highest prevalence was observed for Giardia lamblia at 16.1% (n = 33), followed by Ascaris lumbricoides at 5.9% (n = 12), Entamoeba histolytica at 2.9% (n = 6), and both Enterobius vermicularis and Hymenolepis nana at 1% (n = 2). The female population had a higher exposure to E. histolytica (5.8%, n = 6) than did the male population (0%, n = 0). The multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed increased odds of IPIs among participants involved in agriculture [aOR: 5.37, 95% CI: (2.31 to 12.47)], those who kept free-ranging pigs and poultry [aOR: 5.22, 95% CI: (1.87 to 14.53)], those who did not use soap before eating [aOR: 9.96, 95% CI: (2.11 to 46.99)], and those who consumed raw or undercooked meat [aOR: 2.76, 95% CI: (1.08 to 7.05)] compared with others. Introduction

Parasitic infections are a serious global health burden, as approximately 3.5 billion people are infected with intestinal parasites [1]. Each year, intestinal parasites are responsible for approximately 200,000 deaths worldwide [2]. Overall, the most prevalent intestinal parasitic infections (IPIs) worldwide include Ascaris lumbricoides, Trichuris trichiura, hookworms, Strongyloides species, and harmful protozoa such as Giardia lamblia and Entamoeba histolytica. In developing countries, while a variety of diseases are common, IPIs caused by helminths and protozoa stand out as some of the most prevalent health concerns [3]. These infections notably increase morbidity and illness in regions where they are endemic [4]. In South Asia, particularly Nepal, IPIs are prevalent across various ethnic groups, negatively impacting their growth and overall well-being [5]. Indigenous populations in Asia have historically been self-reliant and autonomous, guided by their unique beliefs and rituals that influence their way of life [6]. Indigenous people in Nepal constitute 35.81% of the country's total population [7].

Numerous studies have been carried out in various indigenous communities across Nepal. For example, studies carried out from 2001 to 2009 on the Chepang, Mushar, and Tharu communities revealed significant variations in the prevalence of IPIs [8–12]. Studies from 2012 to 2020 among various underprivileged and indigenous groups, such as Tharu, Kumal, Meche, Chepang, Satar, and Chaudhary, reported a diverse range of IPI prevalence but indicated an overall decrease compared with the previous decade [13–20]. This pattern suggests a reduction in the overall infection rates of IPIs from 2012 to 2020. A similar trend is observed in the general population, where the prevalence of IPIs has been declining since the initiation of the national deworming campaign in Nepal in 2004 [21]. However, among indigenous populations, recent studies conducted after 2021 revealed inconsistent trends in IPI incidence, with rates varying from 97 to 24%. For example, the prevalence was 97% in the Chepang community [22], 68% in the Deula community [23], 33% in the Musahar community, 28.04% in the Darai community, 24.29% in the Kumal community and 31.3% in the Sarki community [24–27].

Numerous earlier studies across various populations and communities have indicated that a broad range of socioeconomic, demographic, behavioral, and lifestyle factors are associated with different types of IPIs in various geographical areas. For example, several factors have been linked to different types of IPIs across various studies. These include poor personal hygiene and sanitation practices [16, 25, 28–30], being a farmer [31], a source of drinking water [25], agricultural occupation [32], being female [33], being associated with animals, and barefoot walking [13, 29, 34]; no deworming periodically [30]; and practices such as eating raw meat [35], which have been reported to be associated with IPIs. These findings collectively underscore the multifactorial nature of IPIs and the importance of addressing various risk factors for effective prevention and control.

Owing to the relatively high prevalence of IPIs compared with the general population, the present study focuses on the indigenous and underprivileged Badi community, with a total population of 11,470 (0.04% of Nepal's population) [36]. The “Badi” are a Hill Dalit community in Nepal whose name comes from the Sanskrit word "Vadyabadak," meaning a musician or one who plays instruments. Traditionally, they made their living through entertainment, performing music, songs, and stories [37, 38]. Although the caste system was legally abolished in 1963 and Nepal transitioned to a secular republic in 2008 [39], Badi still faces significant social discrimination, largely due to its historical association with prostitution [37, 38], keeping it at the bottom of the caste hierarchy. Currently, many Badi families live in poverty, relying on low-wage jobs such as fishing, woodcutting, and crafting musical instruments for survival. Despite legal protection, caste-based discrimination against Badi has continued [40]. The Badi community in Gurbhakot Municipality, Surkhet, is highly vulnerable to parasites because of its lifestyle, occupation, poor living conditions, reliance on nature, and limited access to healthcare. Various socioeconomic, demographic, and behavioral factors have been identified as potential risks for IPIs. While previous studies have reported the IPI prevalence in indigenous populations, few have comprehensively examined these risk factors. Therefore, this study aims to a) determine the prevalence of IPIs in the indigenous communities of the Surkhet district and b) investigate the relationships between contextual risk factors and IPIs in Gurbhakot Municipality, Surkhet, Nepal. This study sheds light on lifestyle or local contextual factors that may partially explain the existing parasitic burden among underprivileged Badi indigenous communities, where the IPI prevalence remained elevated compared with that of other general populations after decades of deworming campaigns.

Materials and methods

Study area

The research was conducted in Gurbhakot Municipality, which is positioned at 28.439042°N and 81.852828°E within Surkhet District, Karnali Province (Fig. 1). This area has an elevation ranging from 198 to 2,367 m above sea level (masl). Surkhet, located in midwestern Nepal approximately 600 km west of the capital city (Kathmandu), is one of the ten districts in Karnali Province. The region experiences a subtropical climate, with winter temperatures averaging a low of 12 °C and summer temperatures reaching 27 °C. The annual average rainfall in Surkhet is reported to be 1,609 mm [41]. The district includes four rural municipalities and five urban municipalities, with Gurbhakot being one of the urban municipalities with a population of 43,765 [42]. The municipality is bordered by Salyan to the south and to the east, west, and north by Simta, Chingad, and Salyan, respectively. It is organized into 14 wards. Nepali is the primary language for 95% of the population, with Magar spoken by 4.3% and Maithili and other languages spoken by 0.1%. The demographic composition includes 29.2% Chettri, 26.9% Nepali, 26.2% Magar, 5.0% Hill Brahmin, 3.4% Dami, 2.8% Thakuri, 2.2% Gurung, and 0.7% Badi, among other ethnic and caste groups [42].

Sample collection

The study was carried out from August 2023 to September 2023. We used purposive sampling to recruit participants from households within the study area. To ensure gender balance, we aimed to include at least 100 consenting individuals from each gender. Households were approached on a first-come, first-served basis until we exceeded the target of 200 participants. Post hoc power analysis confirmed that this sample size achieves over 80% power to detect moderate effect sizes, providing statistical robustness while aligning with recruitment constraints. Since the national deworming campaign (initiated in 2004) focused predominantly on younger populations [43], individuals under 18 years of age were excluded from our study. Eligible participants were those who (1) were at least 18 years old, (2) had lived in the study area for a minimum of one year, and (3) were willing to provide informed consent. To recruit participants, each eligible individual was approached, and we provided a clear explanation of the study's objectives and procedures. Those who expressed interest were given an opportunity to ask questions, and if they agreed to participate, they signed an informed consent form before proceeding. This approach allowed us to build a diverse sample while maintaining specific criteria for eligibility and consent. The study achieved a nearly 100% response rate among eligible participants who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. After consent was obtained, the participants were instructed to use a clean, sterile stick to collect a fecal sample—about the size of a thumb—from the beginning, middle, and end of their stool in the early morning. They were advised to place the sample in a sterile vial, ensuring that it was not contaminated with urine or soil. The research team then visited each participant's home the following morning to collect the samples. All samples were stored in sterile zip-lock bags with appropriate labeling. The fecal samples were preserved in a 2.5% potassium dichromate solution and kept at 4 °C until they were transported to the parasitology laboratory at the Central Department of Zoology for further analysis. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the institutional review board (IRB) of the Institute of Science and Technology (IOST), Tribhuvan University (approval no. 23–0066). Participants who tested positive for IP were referred to health facilities for treatment.

Laboratory examination

Wet mount technique

Approximately 2 g of each fecal sample was thoroughly mixed with a few drops of normal saline or Gram’s iodine. A small amount of this mixture was then placed on a glass slide and examined under a light microscope at high magnification (400x), following the procedures outlined in previous studies [22].

Saturated salt flotation technique

Approximately one milliliter of the filtrate was combined with 13 ml of sodium chloride (NaCl) in a 15-ml centrifuge tube. The mixture was then centrifuged for 5 min. After centrifugation, the tubes were placed in test tube stands. NaCl solution was added to fill the tube to the top, creating a meniscus. A coverslip was carefully placed over the solution, ensuring that it contacted the liquid. After allowing it to settle for 15–20 min, the coverslip was removed and placed on glass slides for examination of diagnostic parasite stages, following established protocols [44].

Formal ether sedimentation technique

One milliliter of filtrate was combined with ten milliliters of 10% formal saline in a 15-ml centrifuge tube. Three milliliters of ether was added, and the mixture was shaken well. The solution was then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was removed, and approximately two drops of the remaining solution were transferred onto a glass slide with Lugol’s iodine. The slide was then examined under a microscope for parasitic stages [44].

Questionnaire

A set of concise questionnaires was developed in the Nepali language to collect information on various factors, including socioeconomic status (SES), sociodemographic details (such as age, gender, and family size), and lifestyle and behavioral practices. These practices included risk factors such as drinking water sources, walking barefoot, hand-washing habits, nail trimming, and other relevant behaviors. The questionnaires were crafted to capture the essential data needed to meet the study’s objectives thoroughly.

Data analysis

Microsoft Excel was used for data input, and clean and stored MS words were used to create tables. Descriptive or inferential statistical analysis was conducted via the “IBM SPSS Statistics 25” software package. A parametric independent t-test was used for continuous data (i.e., for normally distributed data), and a nonparametric chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical data for group differences as inferential statistics wherever appropriate. Bivariate and multivariate logistic regression were conducted as inferential statistics to obtain odd ratios (ORs) and adjusted odd ratios (aORs) of IPI prevalence. In all cases, p < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

A total of 205 fecal samples were analyzed via various microscopic methods described previously. More than a quarter of the samples (55, or 27%) contained one or more species of IPIs (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1). Figure 3 presents the prevalence of IPIs among the study participants. Among the five-parasite species identified, two (E. histolytica and G. lamblia) were protozoa, whereas the remaining three (A. lumbricoides, E. vermicularis, and H. nana) were helminths. G. lamblia had the highest prevalence at 16.1%, followed by A. lumbricoides at 5.9%, with E. histolytica, E. vermicularis, and H. nana each ranging from 1% to 2.9%. Protozoan infections were more common (19.0%) than helminth infections were (7.8%). Compared with male participants, female participants presented a greater prevalence of E. histolytica, but no other significant differences in parasite prevalence were observed between the sexes.

Table 3 outlines the relationships between parasitic infections and related factors. The bivariate analysis revealed a greater prevalence of IPIs among participants who were illiterate, worked in agriculture, kept free-ranging pigs and poultry, did not use soap when washing their hands before meals, failed to wash raw fruits and vegetables before eating, consumed food that had fallen on the ground, and ate raw or undercooked meat than did their reference groups or counterparts. However, some associations identified in the bivariate analysis did not hold when adjusted for other significant variables in the multivariate model. For example, the multivariate logistic regression, which controlled for variables with significant associations (i.e., removing those variables with correlation coefficients (r) above 0.6 to avoid multicollinearity), revealed significantly higher odds of IPIs among participants engaged in agriculture [aOR: 5.37, 95% CI: (2.31 to 12.47)], those who kept free-ranging pigs and poultry [aOR: 5.22, 95% CI: (1.87 to 14.53)], those who did not use soap before eating [aOR: 9.96, 95% CI: (2.11 to 46.99)], and those who ate raw or undercooked meat [aOR: 2.76, 95% CI: (1.08 to 7.05)] than did their counterparts. No significant associations between IPIs and other demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, or behavioral characteristics were observed.

Discussion

This research examined the prevalence of intestinal parasitic infections (IPIs) among participants from the Badi indigenous group, an underprivileged community often considered the lowest caste in western Nepal [37]. Our findings, indicating a 27% prevalence of IPIs, are consistent with other studies. For example, a prevalence of 28.63% was reported among the indigenous Satar and Chaudhary communities in Birtamode Municipality, Jhapa, Nepal [45], whereas 23.3% was reported among Tibeto-Burman, Indo-Aryan, and Dalit schoolchildren in the Chitwan district [46]. Additionally, the prevalence was 31.32% among the Sarki ethnic group in Pala Rural Municipality, Baglung [27], and 33.3% among the Musahar community in the Makwanpur and Nawalsparasi districts [25]. However, significantly higher rates of IPIs have been reported in other studies, including 81% in the Musahar community in Balan Bihuli, Saptari, Nepal [20]. Similarly, a study in central Nepal reported an extremely high prevalence of 97% among the indigenous Chepang community [22]. The differences in IPI prevalence across these studies could be attributed to varying climatic conditions and levels of awareness [22]. Despite ongoing deworming programs, the relatively high (i.e., 27%, > a quarter of participants) prevalence of IPIs in our study participants may be partially explained by the poor sanitation conditions observed by researchers during fieldwork, along with unhealthy hygiene practices. Many participants (i.e., > 80%; > 3 quarters of participants) reported not using soap when washing their hands, neglecting regular nail trimming, and frequently biting their nails. Our multivariate logistic regression also revealed that a lack of soap use before meals was a significant risk factor. Further investigations are essential to examine and validate these associations.

The overall prevalence of IPIs and specific parasites was generally similar between males and females, with the exception of an elevated prevalence of E. histolytica among females. A few previous studies have reported comparable results, with a higher prevalence in females than in males [47]. This trend might be linked to cultural practices where women are often tasked with soil-related household chores and agricultural work [47]. However, several studies have reported comparable risk levels between males and females, such as in the Darai community [48] and among the Kumal indigenous group in Nepal [49]. However, research conducted among indigenous communities in rural Malaysia (Peninsular Malaysia) reported a lower prevalence of individual parasites and IPI infections in females than in males [29], as did a study in rural Guinea, Africa [50]. In this study, females exhibited more unfavorable behaviors and lifestyle factors than males did, such as consuming dropped food and eating raw meat, which may partially account for the sex differences in infection rates (Table 2). Nonetheless, females also demonstrated better hand hygiene than males did (Table 2). Additional studies are needed to investigate and confirm these relationships.

This study also revealed a greater prevalence of IPIs among participants engaged in agriculture than among those with other occupations, as reflected in both univariate and multivariate analyses. Our findings agreed with those of “previous study of ours” among representative participants of Nepal [32], where farmers were more likely to be infected with IPIs than other occupations were. Similarly, the prevalence of IPIs is high among the school children of farmers [51–53]. This is probably because agricultural workers often come into direct contact with soil, which can harbor parasitic eggs and larvae, increasing their risk of infection. Furthermore, many agricultural workers use untreated water sources for irrigation and personal use, which may be contaminated with parasites.

Among the various behavioral and lifestyle factors analyzed for their associations with IPIs, the use of soap to wash hands before meals had the strongest correlation, with the highest odds ratio (OR) in both the univariate and multivariate models [aOR: 9.96, 95% CI (2.11 to 44.99)]. Nearly half of the participants in this study were farmers (Table 1), and over three-quarters (79.5%) reported not using soap to wash their hands before eating, relying on water only, even after working in the fields. Similar behaviors have been documented in earlier studies [11], and various studies have identified "using soap to wash hands before eating" as a significant risk factor for IPIs [16], including studies focused on children from squatter communities in Dharan municipality. Comparable findings have been reported in other ethnic groups, such as the Tharu community [16] and the Satar and Chaudhary communities [45], as well as among the Chepang community in Saktikhor, Chitwan [18, 24].

The participants who kept free-ranging pigs and poultry presented a higher prevalence of IPIs. In rural and economically disadvantaged communities, such as those in this study, livestock such as pigs and poultry are crucial for subsistence, providing food and potential income. Badi families with limited resources may resort to free-ranging practices because of the high cost of building enclosures and purchasing feed. These free-roaming animals may defecate near water sources, food supplies, vegetables, and even open kitchens, leading to contamination and a greater risk of parasitic infections [54].

The participants who consumed raw or undercooked meat had a greater prevalence of IPIs. The consumption of raw or undercooked meat is a well-documented risk factor for IPIs [55–58]. Meat itself can harbor intestinal parasite cysts [59], and improper handling and storage practices increase contamination risk. Exposed meat may become contaminated by mechanical vectors such as houseflies, cockroaches, and rats that transfer parasite eggs and cysts [60]. Additionally, strong winds can carry parasite eggs onto uncovered meat [61], and meat from butchers with poor sanitation practices or contaminated water sources poses a greater risk of parasitic infections [62].

One key strength of this study is its thorough examination of IPIs, marking the first in-depth investigation within the Badi Indigenous community. By utilizing multivariate logistic regression, our study advances beyond simple prevalence data by analyzing an array of contextual factors linked to IPIs, filling gaps left by prior studies that often lack detailed inferential analysis. A major methodological limitation of this study is its cross-sectional design, which captures data at only one time point. This restricts our ability to track temporal changes or establish causal links. This design is especially limiting for examining parasite shedding, as shedding levels can fluctuate daily and even between stool samples from the same individual, potentially introducing variability and affecting the accuracy of our findings on infection prevalence and intensity. Another constraint is the relatively small sample size, particularly for subgroup analyses, which heightens the risk of Type II error and reduces the study's statistical power, potentially making it difficult to detect smaller associations. This limitation is further affected by our use of purposive sampling. While regression adjustments for socioeconomic and behavioral characteristics were applied to reduce selection bias, the limitations of purposive sampling may still affect the generalizability of our findings to the broader population. The reliance on self-reported data for personal hygiene practices presents an additional challenge, as participants may have responded in a socially desirable manner, especially on sensitive topics, potentially leading to an over- or underestimation of associations and impacting the validity of our findings. Finally, owing to resource limitations, we could not conduct a sensitivity analysis, which limits our capacity to assess the robustness of our results thoroughly in light of potential biases, including unmeasured confounding factors. Sensitivity analysis would have offered further insight into these limitations, and we recognize this as an area for enhancement in future studies. Further studies of sanitation in this population, incorporating a larger sample size and including longitudinal studies, will help to confirm the findings of this study and provide a fuller picture of the sanitation and health needs of this population. Future research is warranted to validate the associations between hygiene behaviors and IPIs in understudied and marginalized Badi populations in Surkhet and expand on other areas within Nepal. The findings of such research could direct public health interventions and government policy to maintain a healthy population.

Conclusions

Despite ongoing deworming efforts, 27% of the participants still presented signs of intestinal parasitic infections. The campaign appeared to be ineffective in raising awareness among disadvantaged community members, as many participants were unaware of intestinal parasites and had not used anthelmintics in the past six months. Notably, participants exhibited laxity in using soap to wash their hands before eating, often neglected regular nail trimming, frequently bit their nails, and failed to wash raw fruits and vegetables before consumption. Multivariate logistic regression identified behaviors such as handwashing practices, the consumption of raw or undercooked meat, and the presence of free-roaming pigs and poultry as significant contributors to IPIs. Basic health education on hygiene and lifestyle could significantly reduce parasitic infections in Badi communities.
