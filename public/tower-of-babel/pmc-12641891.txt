Efficacy of an instant messaging-based life-skills training program to prevent behavioral risk factors among adolescents in Europe: study protocol of a cluster-randomized controlled trial
Authors: Severin Haug, Andreas Wenger, Eunate Arana-Arri, Maitane Barasoain, Ainara Vélez-Del-Burgo, Kleio Koutra, Ana Molina-Barceló, Teresa De Pablo-Pardo, Nikolai Kiselev, Nikolaos Boumparis, Olivia Studhalter, Michael P Schaub, Liselot Hudders, Eline De Keyzer, Frans Folkvord, Katrina Champion, Andreas Triantafyllidis, SUNRISE consortium

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOne of the most significant risk factors for cancer and several other adverse health outcomes is nicotine and tobacco use. Life-skills training programs conducted within the school curriculum are effective in preventing substance use, including nicotine and tobacco use, however, large-scale implementation is hindered by time, organizational and financial constraints. Providing life-skills training programs via smartphones may be a more economical and scalable approach. SUNRISE SmartCoach is the first life skills training program to use instant messaging and storytelling to present life-skills training in an emotional and engaging way. This study protocol outlines a cluster-randomized controlled trial testing the efficacy of SUNRISE SmartCoach among adolescents in eight European countries.MethodsA two-arm, parallel-group, cluster-randomized, controlled trial will be conducted to test the efficacy of SUNRISE SmartCoach in comparison to an assessment only control group. The study participants will be assessed at baseline and at follow-ups after 6 and 18 months. The fully automated program is based on social cognitive theory and aims to improve self-management skills, social skills, and resistance to addictive behavior. Participants in the intervention group will receive online feedback on their life skills via Smartphone, as well as individually tailored coaching dialogues via WhatsApp or Viber over four months, to improve life skills. Active program engagement will be stimulated by interactive features such as quiz questions, message- and picture-contests, and integration of a friendly competition in which program users collect credits with each interaction. Study participants will be 3,500 secondary and upper secondary school students between the ages of 14 and 17 years from eight European countries: Switzerland, Greece, Slovenia, Spain, Cyprus, Italy, Belgium, and Romania. If this intervention approach proves to be effective, it could be easily implemented in various settings and could reach large numbers of young people in a cost-effective way.Trial registrationNCT06922201 (registration date: 2025-04-09).Keywords: Life-skills, Nicotine, Tobacco, Behavioral risk factors, Prevention, Adolescents, Smartphone, Instant messaging

Background

Behavioral risk factors among adolescents, such as tobacco smoking, poor diet, and physical inactivity are linked to an increased risk of both cancer and cardiovascular disease [1, 2]. These risk factors often develop during adolescence and can have long-lasting impacts on health [3]. This is of particular concern due to the developmental vulnerability of adolescents, who are at a critical stage of brain maturation, particularly in areas related to impulse control, decision making, and emotional regulation, making them more susceptible to substance use [4, 5]. Social and environmental influences also play an important role. Adolescents are strongly influenced by peer norms and social acceptance, with peer pressure and group dynamics often encouraging substance use [6, 7].

One of the most significant risk factors for cancer and several other adverse health outcomes is nicotine and tobacco use [8, 9], which remains widespread among mid-to late adolescents around the world, including Europe [10, 11]. Adolescents should therefore be at the center of preventive and early interventions for substance use in general and nicotine and tobacco use in particular.

Health promotion programs implemented at the school- or classroom levels play an important role, as they allow all members of a year group to be proactively addressed and invited for preventive offers [12]. Numerous meta-analyses and systematic reviews have evaluated the efficacy of school-based programs in preventing alcohol, tobacco, and other substance use. Despite differences in methodological rigor and theoretical foundations, these reviews indicate that school-based prevention can be effective [13–15]. Programs grounded in psychosocial theory, that focus on building drug resistance and general life-skills, and are delivered interactively by teachers or trained facilitators, tend to be particularly effective [14]. Life-skills intervention programs aimed at preventing substance use typically integrate training in self-management skills (such as stress adaptation, emotional self-regulation, and goal setting), social skills (including assertiveness and communication), and skills that help resist substance use (like opposing peer pressure to drink alcohol as well as recognizing and resisting media influences) [16–18].

However, implementing and disseminating these programs in schools poses significant challenges [19, 20]. Teachers and other professionals need sufficient time, strong motivation, knowledge, and skills to effectively deliver the program. Additionally, substantial resources, including personnel, funding, and time dedicated to substance use prevention, are necessary to prepare and administer these programs.

Digital interventions offer significant potential to overcome the challenges that often hinder effective implementation and large-scale dissemination of life-skills training programs in schools. They enable the transfer of psycho-educative information in an interactive, standardized, flexible, accessible, and convenient way. A hybrid life skills training program combining e-learning modules and face-to-face sessions among US middle school students led to significant reductions in substance use and led to greater gains in health-related knowledge, skills, and life skills compared to a control condition [19, 20].

SmartCoach presented the first mobile phone-based life-skills training program to prevent substance use. Its long-term effectiveness has been tested in a controlled study among adolescents in Switzerland [21, 22]. It included online feedback and individually tailored SMS text messages over six months. The content was based on social cognitive theory and addressed self-management skills, social skills, and substance use resistance skills. Efficacy results at the 18-month follow-up showed that, compared with controls, those in the intervention group had significantly lower prevalence of tobacco smoking and cannabis use, while no effects were observed on alcohol use, well-being, or social skills [21].

Within the EU-funded project “Sustainable interventions and healthy behaviors for adolescent primary prevention of cancer with digital tools” (SUNRISE), the SmartCoach program will be optimized, implemented in 8 European countries - Switzerland, Greece, Slovenia, Spain, Cyprus, Italy, Belgium, and Romania -, and evaluated for its longer-term efficacy within a cluster-randomized controlled trial. Major intervention program modifications include (1) the provision of the program via the instant messaging platforms WhatsApp and Viber instead of short messaging service (SMS) text messaging and (2) the use of storytelling to convey the coaching content.

While sending SMS text messages was the dominant form of private digital communication among young people for many years, this has largely shifted to instant messaging services such as WhatsApp or Viber in recent years. These services have characteristics that make them promising platforms for delivering individually tailored interventions. In many European countries, WhatsApp is the most widely used instant messenger and the most used smartphone application among adolescents and young adults [23]. Similar to SMS text messaging, WhatsApp and Viber do not require familiarization with the functionality of a new platform. In addition, these instant messaging services offer significant advantages over SMS text messaging, including the ability to directly incorporate media objects and the option to send messages free of charge with an existing internet connection. A recently published pilot study showed that an automated WhatsApp-based coaching program for smoking cessation among young people in Switzerland was feasible, well accepted by the users and promising for reducing the consumption of tobacco cigarettes and electronic nicotine products [24]. Based on these considerations and initial positive results, messenger-based coaching is also implemented with the SUNRISE SmartCoach intervention program.

In health communication, using scientific evidence and data is important, but facts alone might not always engage people to change their behavior or attitude. This is also reflected by the Communication for Health (C4H) approach of the World Health Organization [25] and will be incorporated by the SUNRISE SmartCoach. Stories provide meaning to facts and allow individuals to connect with the information both intellectually and emotionally [26, 27]. This emotional engagement can lead to better retention and understanding of health information. Initial reviews suggest that using storytelling to promote health behaviors is promising as stories help people relate to others, imagine themselves making different choices, and ultimately reduce resistance while inspiring new health behaviors [25, 28].

Within this study protocol, we describe a cluster-randomized controlled trial testing the efficacy of SUNRISE SmartCoach, a messenger- and storytelling-based life-skills training program to prevent nicotine use and behavioral risk factors among adolescents in Europe.

Methods/design

Design and hypotheses

A two-arm, parallel-group, single-blind, cluster-randomized, controlled trial will be conducted to test the efficacy of the intervention program in comparison to an assessment only control group. The study participants will be assessed at baseline and at 6- and 18-months follow-up. The study design is also displayed in Figure 1.

Study design

Our main hypothesis is that the messenger-based life-skills training program with a duration of four months will be more effective than assessment only, to prevent the onset and escalation of nicotine and tobacco use at 18-months follow-up.

Participants, setting, and procedure

The intervention program will be tested among secondary and upper secondary school students aged between 14 and 17 years. In this age group, the prevalence of experimental and regular use of nicotine and other risk behaviors is increasing noticeably [10], however, only a minority has established problematic or disordered behaviors. Furthermore, almost all adolescents of this age are familiar with using smartphones and instant messaging services and usually use them every day [29].

Each of the eight participating countries (Belgium, Cyprus, Greece, Italy, Romania, Slovenia, Spain, and Switzerland) will appoint a pilot administrator responsible for the recruitment and implementation of the study in their respective secondary and upper secondary school classes. They will contact and invite teachers to participate with their classes in the study, inform them about its objectives, implementation, and procedure. Teachers taking part will set aside 45–90 minutes for study introduction during school lessons in both study groups. During this time, the students will be introduced to the topic of stress through a short workshop; informed about the program and the accompanying study; and invited to participate in the study. The workshop and information session will be led by research assistants who have received training on the study and program, and who have expertise in working with young people and providing preventive interventions. The 30–60 minute workshop aims to stimulate young people's interest in stress management and social skills, providing them with basic information and interactive, playful exercises that emphasize the importance of these life skills in everyday situations.

Students willing to participate in the study will be required to sign an informed consent form online. When parents/guardians need to provide consent for their child's participation, they will be informed at least one week in advance and will be asked to sign the informed consent. Obtaining informed consent will follow all ethical and regulatory processes outlined by European guidelines and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) [30]. The consent process will provide a comprehensive explanation of the study's purpose, duration, procedures, potential risks and benefits, confidentiality measures, and the participant's right to withdraw at any time without any consequence. This information will be provided in a clear and comprehensible format, ensuring that participants understand what their participation entails.

Students who provide consent (and their parents/guardians if necessary) will use electronic devices like Smartphones, computers, or tablets to complete the baseline assessment at school.

Students (1) with a minimum age of 14, (2) who have online access, and (3) obtained parental informed consent, if necessary due to age and national legislation, will be invited to participate in the study and to fill in the baseline assessment.

For participants of the intervention group, the SUNRISE SmartCoach intervention program and its association with a friendly competition will be described in detail by the research assistants. Subsequently, participants of the intervention group will receive an individually tailored life-skills feedback directly on their Smartphone (see also section “life skills feedback”). During the subsequent 4 months, participants in the intervention group will receive the SUNRISE SmartCoach coaching messages during their free time, outside of the classroom setting (see also section “coaching dialogues”).

Participants of the assessment only control group, will be thanked for their study participation, they will be informed about their group assignment, and their compensation for participation in the follow-up assessment. This compensation is country-specific and can take the form of a fixed monetary incentive (5–10 Euro for each follow-up assessment), a voucher, or participation in a prize draw.

Follow-up assessments 6 and 18 months after baseline will be conducted remotely and follow a step-by-step procedure: initially, all study participants receive an invitation to take part in the online follow-up survey via e-mail. If they do not take part, reminders will be sent. As an alternative, it is also planned to carry out follow-up assessments in some countries during lessons in the classroom.

Ethical Review

The study protocol was approved by the respective Ethics Committee of all participating countries. The trial will be executed in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration [30].

Randomization and allocation concealment

To avoid spill-over effects within school classes, we will conduct a cluster-randomized controlled trial using school class as a randomization unit. Due to the heterogeneity of students in the different secondary schools, we will use a separate randomization list for each school (stratified randomization). Furthermore, we will use block randomization with computer generated randomly permuted blocks of 4 cases and an allocation ratio of 1:3, i.e., one control class for every three intervention classes [31].

Research assistants supervising the baseline assessment will be blinded to the group allocation of school classes. In addition, group allocation will not be revealed to participants until they had provided informed consent and baseline data including their mobile phone number. Furthermore, the research assistants who contact non-responding participants for the follow-up assessments for primary and secondary outcomes will be blinded to the group allocation.

Sample size calculation

The estimation of the expected effect sizes is based on the results of a Cochrane Review on school-based programs for preventing smoking [15] and the longer-term efficacy of the digital SmartCoach program tested among adolescents in Switzerland [21]. The Cochrane Review revealed a statistically significant effect in preventing the onset of smoking for combined social competence and social influences curricula (six RCTs) with an Odds Ratio (OR) of 0.49 (95% CI 0.28–0.87.28.87). The SmartCoach program demonstrated an intervention effect on cigarette smoking at 18-month follow-up with an OR of 0.67 (95% CI 0.47–0.96.47.96). Based on these studies, and after a slight downward correction, we expected an OR of 0.70 for the primary outcome (30-day prevalence rate of tobacco or nicotine use) at the 18-month follow-up for the present study.

Given this estimated OR of 0.70, an expected 30-day prevalence rate of tobacco or nicotine use in 16-year-old adolescents of 25% in the control group at the 18-month follow-up, and an allocation ratio of 1:3 (one control class for every three intervention classes), a sample size of n=1,896 will be required to achieve 80% power for a χ2-test (α=5%, 2-sided) to detect this difference.

Since students are nested within school classes, we had to consider a potential design effect for the sample size calculation. Based on the previous SmartCoach efficacy study [21], we anticipated an average cluster size of 16 participants per school class and an intra-cluster correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.05. This would result in a design effect of 1.75. Multiplying this design effect by the required size for an unnested sample (n=1,896) resulted in a required sample size of n=3,318 study participants. Based on these calculations and estimates, a sample size of approximately n=3,500 is targeted for the current study.

Intervention program

Theoretical background and intervention content

The intervention elements of the SUNRISE SmartCoach program are based on the Social Cognitive Theory [32, 33]. This theory is rooted in Social Learning Theory, as it is founded on principles of learning within the human social context [34], though it has also integrated several concepts from cognitive psychology. The key concepts of this theory, that will be addressed in the SUNRISE SmartCoach intervention program are: (1) outcome expectations, i.e., beliefs about the likelihood and impact of the consequences of behavioral choices; (2) self-efficacy, i.e., beliefs about one’s personal ability to perform a desired behavior which can be influenced by factors such as mastery, experience, or persuasion; (3) observational learning, i.e., learning new behaviors through exposure to interpersonal or media displays such as peer modelling; (4) facilitation, i.e., providing strategies, tools, and resources that make new behaviors easier to perform; and (5) self-regulation, i.e., controlling oneself through monitoring, goal-setting, feedback, and self-instruction.

The contents of the intervention program draw on proven, widely-disseminated life-skills programs, like IPSY [18], ALERT [17] and Life Skills Training [16] and cover the following three areas: (1) self-management skills, (2) social skills, and (3) resistance to addictive behavior. The program will be structured according to these major content areas. The coaching messages in weeks 1–7 will focus on self-management skills. Weeks 8–11 will focus on social skills, while weeks 12–16 will focus on resistance to addictive behaviors.

Life skills feedback

Program participants will receive individual, tailored web-feedback containing textual and graphical information on their life skills immediately after completing the online baseline assessment. This feedback includes an individual's stress level compared to a reference group of the same age, gender and country. This comparison is based on individual PSS-4 scores [35] and normative data from the Health Behaviour in School-Aged Children (HBSC) study [36]. In addition, feedback is provided on the currently most relevant individual stressors and social skills, based on the ICQ-10 [37]. Coaching dialogs

For a period of four months, program participants receive one to three coaching dialogues per week. The SUNRISE SmartCoach first contacts the participants via a push notification on instant messenger, after which they work on a specific topic for approximately one to five minutes per dialogue. These message dialogues will be generated and sent by the fully-automated system. As described in the Introduction, storytelling is used to convey the information in a playful and contextualized way.

Within the first seven weeks, the focus of the program is on self-management skills. During this time, the program participants will accompany a Stone Age man or woman (Stoney) on a journey to discover the origins of stress and learn how to cope with stressful situations. These situations will be based on the stressful situations that participants provided information on at the baseline assessment. In addition, the individual stress level is assessed at the beginning (week 1) and after the end (week 7) of the topic and the participants receive feedback on their stress progression.

During weeks 8–11, the coaching dialogs focus on developing social skills; e.g., making requests, refusing unreasonable requests or meeting new people. In this phase, participants accompany a cyborg named Eliza, as she discovers human social interactions and how they work. They support Eliza to cope with difficult social situations, which, based on the baseline survey, also represent a challenge for the participants.

In weeks 12–16, the coaching dialogs focus on resistance to addictive behaviors; e.g., recognizing and resisting media influences, social norms of substance use and the associations of self-management skills and social skills with addictive behaviors. During this time, the participants accompany the virtual Dr. Brain in his search for the origins and reasons for addictive behaviors and how these can restrict everyday life and quality of life. For the examples of excessive Smartphone use and nicotine consumption, specific strategies for dealing with cravings or social pressure are shown.

All message dialogs will be tailored according to the individual data from the baseline assessment; i.e., gender, age, stressful situations, level of stress [35], and social skills [37]. To exploit the full potential of Smartphones, several interactive features, like quiz questions, tasks to create individually-tailored if-then behavior plans based on implementation intentions [38], and picture contests, will be implemented within the program. The coaching contents will also include links to thematically appropriate video clips, pictures and related websites. Prize draw

To encourage active engagement with the program, participation will be associated with a friendly competition. This will allow users to collect credits for completing the weekly coaching dialogues. The more credits participants collect, the greater their chances of winning one of several attractive prizes in a country-specific prize draw held after the program’s completion. Participants will be able to retrieve their number of credits at any time from an individual profile page.

Data analyses

Descriptive statistics will be used to characterize each variable, employing frequency distributions for categorical variables and central tendency and variability statistics for metric variables. Baseline differences between the intervention and control groups will be examined using chi-square tests for categorical variables and t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for metric variables, depending on data distribution. The same tests will assess differences between participants lost to follow-up and those who responded, stratified by study group. Data analysis will follow the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, using multiple imputation procedures for missing data, conducted separately for each group to preserve homogeneity and potential intervention effects [44]. Potential predictors of missing data will be considered as covariates in the multivariate analyses. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) will be calculated for primary and secondary outcomes to determine outcome variance across classrooms. If the ICC exceeds 5%, linear mixed models (LMMs) and generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) will be used; otherwise, logistic or linear regressions will be applied. LMMs and GLMMs [45, 46] will include a random intercept for school class, with identical predictors and covariates as in regressions. Binary outcome analyses will focus on follow-up values, with baseline values for binary variables of interest, group as a predictor, and baseline difference variables as covariates. Continuous outcome analyses will examine changes from baseline to follow-up, with group as a predictor and baseline difference variables as covariates. Results with a type I error rate of P<.05 on two-sided tests will be considered statistically significant. Analyses will be performed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS, IBM Corp) and R (The R Foundation). Multiple imputation will be conducted using the mice (Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations) package in R, and LMM and GLMM will be conducted using the lme4 (Linear Mixed-Effects) package in R [47].

For cost-effectiveness analyses we will use the “Monitoring and Assessment Framework for the European Innovation Partnership on Active and Healthy Ageing” (MAFEIP) tool, that supports evidence-based decision-making processes for all institutions and users in the EU health and care sector. The MAFEIP-tool is a web-based tool whose purpose is to estimate the health and economic outcomes of a large variety of ICT-enabled social and health innovations. The Incremental Effectiveness Ratio will be the main outcome of the analyses that will show the cost-effectiveness.

Discussion

Substance use and other behavioral risk factors among adolescents pose a significant public health concern [48]. Although face-to-face life-skills training in school settings has been shown to be effective in preventing the onset and escalation of substance use [18], implementing and disseminating it poses significant challenges due to the substantial financial and time resources required [20]. In contrast to comprehensive school-based curricula, training and counselling via Smartphone is more economical and matches with the lifestyle and communication habits of the target group.

The SUNRISE SmartCoach, which is described in this protocol and is to be tested as part of the planned study, is based on positive experiences and results from previous digital life skills-training programs [20, 21, 49]. At the same time, it takes into account changes in communication habits among adolescents [29] and promising findings on the provision of health promotion through narrative interventions [25, 28].

Against this background, the planned study presents the first one to test the efficacy of a messenger- and storytelling-based life-skills training program to prevent nicotine use and behavioral risk factors among adolescents. This large-scale efficacy study conducted in eight European countries will have sufficient statistical power to demonstrate the long-term effects of reducing nicotine and tobacco consumption, one of the most important risk factors for cancer and cardiovascular disease. If the SUNRISE SmartCoach program proves to be effective, it could be disseminated to adolescents in various settings, regions and countries.
