A rare combination of a meningioma and an ethmoidal meningoencephalocele with osteo-dural defect: Diagnostic and therapeutic challenges
Authors: Dzeutouo Fobasso Ornella, Ababacar Sall Basse, Thierno Boubacar Diallo, Souleymane Diallo, Sagar Diop, Ibrahima Tine, Abdou Azize Diop
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Meningiomas are common, benign intracranial tumors, while meningoencephaloceles are rare congenital or acquired anomalies commonly associated with a dural defect. Their association is exceptional and poses a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge.Case Description: We present a case of a 35-year-old female patient who presented with chronic headaches, blindness, and decreased hearing for 2 years. An Ear-Nose-Throat (ENT) examination revealed a right endonasal mass on nasofibroscopy. Brain magnetic resonance imaging showed a large right temporo-parietal lesion with features suggestive of a meningioma, and a right endonasal lesion extending into the frontal lobe through a bone defect in the ethmoid bone, suggesting a meningoencephalocele. The meningioma was surgically excised (Simpson II), and histo-pathology confirmed a World Health Organization grade I meningothelial meningioma. Three months after surgery, the patient presented with right rhino-liquorrhea, and a decision to endoscopically treat the meningo-encephalocele was made, as well as repair the dural defect in ENT. The postoperative period was marked by failure of the dural closure, evidenced by the appearance of a compressive pneumocephalus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa meningitis. A transcranial approach allowed for effective repair with immediate cessation of the rhinoliquorrhea. Resorption of the pneumocephalus was observed 1 month after surgery by a follow-up computed tomography scan.Conclusion: The association of a meningioma with a meningoencephalocele is extremely rare. The persistence of a dural defect exposes the patient to serious infectious complications. This case illustrates the complexity of managing such a combination and highlights the importance of early diagnosis, rigorous follow-up, and effective repair of any dural defect.

INTRODUCTION

Meningiomas are one of the most common primary intracranial tumors in adults and have been well described over the past century, with the term coined by Harvey Cushing in 1922.[3,17] The classic symptoms include: headache due to raised intracranial pressure (ICP), focal neurological deficits (including those of the cranial nerves), or generalized or focal seizures caused by focal mass effects. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the gold standard for diagnosing as well as following up on meningiomas. The treatment is mainly surgical, and resection is graded according to Simpson’s classification.[3,14,17] Ethmoidal meningoencephaloceles are rare, and the most common symptoms include: nasal obstruction, spontaneous cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) rhinorrhea, recurrent meningitis, and headaches.[7,10,11,19] They may be congenital and associated with a bony defect leading to an osteomeningeal breach. They may also be a result of a skull base surgery complication or even idiopathic. Repair of these defects is essential to prevent complications such as meningitis, brain abscess, and pneumocephalus.[2,6,12] The association of a meningioma and a meningo-encephalocele is extremely rare.[11] Diagnosis and treatment of such a combination can be challenging for the surgeon.

CASE REPORT

We report a case of a 35-year-old woman, who presented with a 2 years history of chronic headaches, blindness, bilateral diminished hearing, and a stuffy nose. She was seen in the Ear-Nose-Throat (ENT) department for the stuffy nose, where a nasal fibroscopy was done, and a head computed tomography (CT) [Figure 1] and MRI were requested. The MRI showed a large right temporo-parietal extra-axial lesion with peri-lesional edema and a mass effect on neighboring structures, isointense on T1, enhancing intensely after injecting the contrast medium, suggesting a meningioma of the right temporo-parietal convexity [Figure 2]. The MRI also showed a frontal base lesion seeming continuous with the frontal lobe and extending into the paranasal sinuses on one side. Its characteristic of being iso-intense and nonenhancing was in favor of an ethmoidal endonasal meningo-encephalocele [Figure 3]. Fibroscopy showed an endonasal mass occupying almost the entire upper part of the nasal cavity [Figure 4]. The patient was then referred to neurosurgery for further treatment. On admission, she was stable, with a Glasgow coma score (GCS) of 15/15 with no motor deficits. She benefited from a Simpson grade II resection of the meningioma. The entire post-operative period was unremarkable. A day 2 post-operative head CT scan confirmed a complete excision of the meningioma. Clinically, there was amelioration of the headaches, the blindness, and decreased hearing; however, the condition remained unchanged. She was discharged and transferred back to ENT. At 1 month follow-up, the headaches had disappeared completely, she had a GCS of 15/15, with no sensory-motor deficits. She still suffered from blindness and hypoacusis.

Samples were sent for histo-pathological analysis. In total, seven tumoral fragments were sent, weighing about 200 g, measuring between 7 × 4 × 3.5 cm and 4 × 2 × 1 cm. The tissue was firm, whitish to yellowish, with an irregular external surface. Upon cutting, it had a solid consistency with some cystic and hemorrhagic areas. Histological examination showed a proliferation of cells with abundant cytoplasm, indistinct borders, round to oval nuclei, regular contours, and clear chromatin. These cells formed small clusters and irregular trabeculae, arranged in a more or less hyalinized collagenous matrix. Numerous psammomatous bodies were also observed. The morphological appearance was consistent with grade I meningothelial meningioma according to the World Health Organization (WHO) [Figure 5].

At 3 months postoperatively, she developed a right CSF rhinorrhea, which motivated the ENT team to attempt an excision of the meningo-encephalocele and repair the osteo-meningeal breach through an endonasal endoscopic approach. The post-operative period was marked by a failure of the repair. A second endonasal endoscopic repair was attempted, which also failed, evidenced by continuous CSF rhinorrhea and development of a microbiologically documented Pseudomonas aeruginosa meningitis. A head CT scan was done, which showed an osteo-meningeal breach and a compressive pneumocephalus [Figure 6]. Antibiotic therapy based on the sensitivity results was initiated to treat the meningitis, and open cranial surgery was planned to repair the defect. A right pterional approach permitted visualization of the right frontal base osteodural defect. With the aid of bipolar forceps, the brain tissue was coagulated at its entrance into the defect and then excised. This was followed by meticulous closure of the dural defect. The bony defect was filled with bone ex, and then a layer of periosteal tissue was harvested and spread over the entire area of the defect lying between the bony defect and the repaired dura mater. The immediate post-operative period saw a cessation of the CSF rhinorrhea, and the patient was discharged on post-operative day 5. The patient was seen 2 weeks later, and there was still cessation of the CSF rhinorrhea. A cerebral CT scan was done 1 month post-operatively, which showed an absence of the osteodural defect as well as a significant regression of the pneumocephalus [Figure 7].

DISCUSSION

Anterior skull base encephaloceles are rare congenital malformations, and they represent 1–10% of all encephaloceles. According to the location of the bony defect, they can be classified as follows: transethmoidal, sphenoethmoidal, transsphenoidal, and sphenomaxillaire.[13] In adults, a number of hypotheses have been proposed with regard to the origin of the defect. They occur most commonly post-traumatic or post-skull base surgery, but can also be the result of certain tumors at the base of the skull, inflammation, certain tumors of the nasal cavity, or, more generally, of the ENT area that can invade the base of the skull. However, it would seem that an increase in ICP was associated with osteodural defects leading to spontaneous CSF leaks and meningoencephaloceles. Certain areas of meningeal weakness, like areas with bony prominences such as the crista galli, the clinoid process, the posterior border of the lesser wing of the sphenoid, the superior part of the petrous part of the temporal bone, were identified. The pathophysiology can be explained by the fact that the arachnoid membrane in these areas of weakness has micro-tears and micro-hernias. Under the effects of variations in CSF pressure and its pulsations, these areas of meningeal weakness can increase, leading to the appearance of breaches, hernias, and the formation of arachnoid diverticula, which, when they come into contact with the dura mater, result in its fenestration and the formation of meningeal breaches. Later, bone erosion occurs due to the mechanical action of the meninges striking the bone during CSF pulsations. Through this osteomeningeal breach, the subarachnoid spaces can communicate with one or more air cavities at the base of the skull.[5] However, rhinorrhea may not be immediately clinically apparent, as the cerebral parenchyma present at this level, under the effect of intracranial hypertension, closes the breach. After resection of the tumor, ICP decreases, and CSF leakage occurs because the breach is no longer completely sealed. Rhinoliquorrhea manifested 3 months after meningioma resection, allowing time for post-surgical inflammatory remodeling and intracranial hypertension to decrease. This is therefore the most likely hypothesis in our case. As much as intracranial hypertension can be secondary to a brain tumor, there seems to be an overlap in the risk factors of idiopathic intracranial hypertension and those of spontaneous CSF leaks, such as the female sex and obesity. Ethmoidal meningoencephalocele, however, remains a rare entity. Nasal obstruction, spontaneous CSF rhinorrhea, recurrent meningitis, and headaches are the most common symptoms of skull base encephaloceles. The patient’s blindness was due to chronic intracranial hypertension and the mass effect of the large meningioma, which compressed midline structures. In contrast, bilateral diminished hearing was not related to the neurosurgical condition and was identified and evaluated by ENT specialists. This distinction highlights the importance of considering comorbidities and clarifying the origin of symptoms when assessing patients.

A beta-2-transferrin test, CT, and MRI cisternographies are the principal means of investigation of méningoencephalocèle.[7,10,11,19] The diagnosis is based on the clinical, biological, and radiological arguments in favor of it. It is important to detect the defect due to its potential complications, such as meningitis, subdural empyema, cerebral abscess, and rarely septic thrombophlebitis. Pneumocephalus is intimately connected to an osteodural defect and it is severe once it is compressive.[2,6,12] Hsu et al. reported a case of a patient with a meningoencephalocele associated with a meningioma. The patient initially benefitted from a craniotomy and resection of the meningioma. Five months later, the meningoencephalocele was excised via an endonasal endoscopic approach, and the defect was repaired using a tensor fascia lata flap.[11] The endonasal approach is the most commonly used technique to treat ethmoidal meningoencephaloceles. However, it is not devoid of certain complications.[8] A transcranial approach proved to be more effective in our case to repair the defect. Kubo et al. published a large case series, including 19 cases, 17 of whom benefitted from an intracranial approach to repair their malformations.[13,19] Mercea et al. demonstrated in their case presentation that a combination of both open surgery and endoscopy is safe with favorable post-operative and follow-up outcomes.[15]

The size of the defect influences treatment outcomes, especially the endonasal approach. Larger defects pose an extra difficulty due to larger resection of the duramater, larger dissection of the arachnoid mater, and exposure to increased flow of CSF in the subarachnoid cisterns. In general, the repair of large defects with free grafts may lead to a high rate of CSF leakage compared to small defects, and surgery of large defects may lead to unacceptable CSF leak rates.[8,9] We repaired the breach via an intracranial approach using a periosteal flap reinforced with Surgicel. This led to an immediate cessation of the CSF leak and a resorption of the pneumocephalus, which was seen on the control CT scan about a month later.

CONCLUSION

The association of a meningioma and a meningoencephalocele is extremely rare. The etiology of meningoencephaloceles is usually malformative or traumatic. This case illustrates the possibility of developing a meningoencephalocele as a consequence of chronic intracranial hypertension secondary to slow-growing tumors such as meningiomas. Adequate treatment of the malformation is necessary to prevent its complications, which are mostly infectious. This case also illustrates the complexity of the treatment of ethmoidal encephaloceles, and it underlines the importance of early diagnosis, close monitoring, and appropriate treatment of all osteomeningeal breaches.
