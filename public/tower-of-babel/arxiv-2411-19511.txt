Scalable Order-Preserving Pattern Mining
Ling Li ∗, Wiktor Zuba ∗, Grigorios Loukides, Solon P. Pissis, Maria Matsangidou

Abstract
Time series are ubiquitous in domains ranging from medicine to marketing and finance. Frequent Pattern Mining (FPM) from a time series has thus received much attention. This general problem has been studied under different matching relations determining whether two time series match or not. Recently, it has been studied under the order-preserving (OP) matching relation stating that a match occurs when two time series have the same relative order (i.e., ranks) on their elements. Thus, a frequent OP pattern captures a trend shared by sufficiently many parts of the input time series. Here, we propose exact, highly scalable algorithms for FPM in the OP setting. Our algorithms employ an OP suffix tree (OPST) as an index to store and query time series efficiently. Unfortunately, there are no practical algorithms for OPST construction. Thus, we first propose a novel and practical 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ σ ​ log ⁡ σ ) \mathcal{O}(n\sigma\log\sigma) -time and 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ) \mathcal{O}(n) -space algorithm for constructing the OPST of a length- n n time series over an alphabet of size σ \sigma . We also propose an alternative faster OPST construction algorithm running in 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ σ ) \mathcal{O}(n\log\sigma) time using 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ) \mathcal{O}(n) space; this algorithm is mainly of theoretical interest. Then, we propose an exact 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ) \mathcal{O}(n) -time and 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ) \mathcal{O}(n) -space algorithm for mining all maximal frequent OP patterns, given an OPST. This significantly improves on the state of the art, which takes Ω ⁡ ( n 3 ) \Omega(n^{3}) time in the worst case. We also formalize the notion of closed frequent OP patterns and propose an exact 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ) \mathcal{O}(n) -time and 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ) \mathcal{O}(n) -space algorithm for mining all closed frequent OP patterns, given an OPST. We conducted experiments using real-world, multi-million letter time series showing that our 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ σ ​ log ⁡ σ ) \mathcal{O}(n\sigma\log\sigma) -time OPST construction algorithm runs in 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ) \mathcal{O}(n) time on these datasets despite the 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ σ ​ log ⁡ σ ) \mathcal{O}(n\sigma\log\sigma) bound; that our frequent pattern mining algorithms are up to orders of magnitude faster than the state of the art and natural Apriori-like baselines; and that OP pattern-based clustering is effective.

Introduction

A
time series
is a sequence of data points indexed by time,
which are often recorded at successive, equally-spaced points in time. As a data type, a time series is thus simply a
string
over the alphabet of real numbers
[
11
]
, integers
[
7
]
, or even characters (e.g., of “strong”, “medium”, or “weak” type
[
28
]
).
Many application domains feature time series, including medicine, where they model e.g., electrocardiogram (ECG) recordings or electroencephalography (EEG) data
[
25
]
;
sensor networks, where they model e.g., sensor readings
[
8
]
; and finance, where they model e.g., quarterly revenue, monthly sales, or stock prices
[
31
]
.

In all these application domains, mining a time series is useful, as it can discover actionable patterns, such as
heart beats in ECG data
[
25
]
, sleep spindles in EEG sleep data
[
25
]
; electricity consumption profiles of devices, or correlations between traffic jams in sensor data
[
14
]
; and interesting stock co-movements in financial data
[
20
]
. Towards this goal, the problem of pattern mining (a.k.a motif discovery) from a time series has been studied for over twenty years (see
[
11
]
for a survey). This general problem asks for
patterns
(e.g., substrings, subsequences, or trends), which occur frequently in an input time series, based on a
matching relation
which dictates
whether two time series match or not.

There are numerous algorithms for dealing with this problem
most of which fall into two categories based on the matching relation they adopt. The algorithms in the first category (e.g.,
[
28
,
24
,
16
]
) are either applied to symbolic sequences
[
28
]
(i.e., strings of characters) directly, or first
transform the (numerical) input time series into symbolic sequences
[
24
,
16
]
. They all
adopt
string matching
relations which state that all occurrences of a pattern need to have exactly the same characters in the same order to match,
except possibly wildcard characters.
To discover the patterns, these algorithms resort to frequent string
[
3
]
or frequent
sequence
[
22
]
mining.
Thus, they typically find substrings or subsequences occurring sufficiently frequently. The algorithms in the second category (e.g.,
[
21
,
25
,
2
,
15
]
) adopt a
distance-based matching
relation stating that all occurrences of a pattern need to be “similar”, according to a distance function, to match. These algorithms typically find the largest set of patterns that match according to the matching relation. The drawback of both categories of algorithms is that they may not capture
trends
effectively. This is because such trends are often not preserved well after the transformation of the input time series into a symbolic sequence
[
31
,
27
]
; or because such trends may not be captured by patterns which are at small distance but nonetheless correspond to very different trends.

In response, a very recent class of pattern mining methods
[
27
,
31
,
30
,
29
]
utilizes the well-established
order-preserving
(OP) matching relation
[
18
,
10
]
.
This property states that two time series are
order-preserving
if and only if they have the same relative order (i.e., ranks) on their elements.
For example, the time series

X=(4,2,5,5,1)
and
Y=(5,2,7,7,0)

are OP, as they have the same sequence of ranks

(3,2,4,4,1)

on their elements
(e.g.,
4
and
5
is
the third smallest
element in
X
and
Y
, respectively).
This sequence of ranks is called an
OP pattern
. Thus, an OP pattern represents the collection of
all
time series with the same trend, captured by the sequence of their ranks. This is useful to derive the “shape” of these time series, even when there are small fluctuations in the data, as is common in practice
[
11
]
. The classic
frequent pattern mining
(FPM) problem extends naturally to the OP setting
[
27
,
31
]
: Given a string
w
of length
n
over a totally ordered alphabet of size
\sigma
and a frequency (minimum support) threshold
\tau
, return all OP patterns with frequency at least
\tau
in
w
.

Example 1
.

The time series
S
below models the average monthly price of a stock from the electric power sector over a 20-month period. The time series

S_{1}=(56,57,62,59,58)
and
S_{2}=(63,64,68,67,66)
are OP because they have the same sequence of ranks on their elements, i.e., OP pattern,
p=(1,2,5,4,3)
and hence the same trend (i.e., the price increased as winter approached and then decreased as spring approached). Pattern
p
is frequent for
\tau=2
, as it corresponds to two time series,
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, which are part of
S
.

A prime example among the class of OP pattern mining methods is the
MOPP-Miner

[
27
]
algorithm (see Section
6
), which mines
maximal
frequent OP patterns (i.e., those that cannot be extended to either side and still remain frequent).

Example 2

(cont’d from Example
1
)
.

The OP pattern
p
in Example
1
is maximal because it cannot be extended to either side and still have frequency at least
\tau=2
. Specifically,
if we prepend to
S_{1}
the element (letter) on its left and do the same for
S_{2}
we obtain
S^{\prime}_{1}=(57,56,57,62,59,58)
and
S^{\prime}_{2}=(58,63,64,68,67,66)
whose OP patterns are

p^{\prime}_{1}=(2,1,2,5,4,3)
and
p^{\prime}_{2}=(1,2,3,6,5,4)
, respectively, and have frequency
1<\tau
. Similarly, if we
append to
S_{1}
the letter on its right and do the same for
S_{2}
, we obtain
S^{\prime\prime}_{1}=(56,57,62,59,58,60)
and
S^{\prime\prime}_{2}=(63,64,68,67,66,65)
whose OP patterns are
p^{\prime\prime}_{1}=(1,2,6,4,3,5)
and
p^{\prime\prime}_{2}=(1,2,6,5,4,3)
, respectively, and have frequency
1<\tau
.

Although
MOPP-Miner
utilizes efficient methods for scanning time series, its time complexity is in
\Omega(n^{3})
in the worst case, and it
does not scale to large time series
in practice.

Our goal here is to propose exact, highly scalable algorithms for FPM in the OP setting. We would like to have mining algorithms with
both
theoretical guarantees (near-linear time and linear space) and practically efficient implementations. These characteristics are of utmost importance, as time series are ever-increasing in size. The two key ideas of our approach are to: (I)
develop a time- and space-efficient data structure (
index
) for storing and querying time series; and (II)
exploit compact representations of OP patterns that take
\mathcal{O}(n)
space.

Contributions.

Our work makes four main contributions.

1.

We observe that the
order-preserving suffix tree
(
OPST
), introduced in
[
10
]
, is essential to designing efficient algorithms for frequent OP pattern mining. Although other OP indexes exist
[
12
,
13
,
17
]
, they either focus only on
pattern matching
queries and do not have the full suffix tree functionality that is essential for
frequent pattern mining
; or they do not admit an
efficient
construction. We thus design a theoretically and practically efficient algorithm for constructing the
OPST
of an input string (time series)
w
of length
n
over a totally ordered alphabet of size
\sigma
,
which takes
\mathcal{O}(n\sigma\log\sigma)
time using
\mathcal{O}(n)
space.
We were motivated to design this algorithm because there are no practical algorithms for
OPST
construction; the state-of-the-art algorithm of
[
10
]
is of theoretical interest and to the best of our knowledge has no available implementation. Notably, the
\mathcal{O}(n\sigma\log\sigma)
time bound is never attained in our extensive experiments, where our implementation always runs in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time irrespective of the alphabet size
\sigma
.
Thus, our
\mathcal{O}(n\sigma\log\sigma)
-time algorithm is the first practical algorithm for constructing an
OPST
. Note that beyond pattern mining, it can be used directly for many other OP analysis tasks; see
[
10
]
.

As a bonus, we provide an
\mathcal{O}(n\log\sigma)
-time and
\mathcal{O}(n)
-space algorithm, which is mainly of theoretical interest. Interestingly, this construction algorithm is also theoretically faster than the state of the art
[
10
]
for small alphabets. Specifically, the algorithm in
[
10
]
has time complexity
\mathcal{O}(n\sqrt{\log n})
, which is larger than that of our algorithm when
\log\sigma=o(\sqrt{\log n})
.

2.

We design an exact algorithm for mining all
maximal
frequent OP patterns of
w
. By definition, every frequent OP pattern is included in a maximal frequent OP pattern; and the total number of all maximal frequent OP patterns is
\mathcal{O}(n)
. Our algorithm works in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time and space provided that the
OPST
of
w
has been constructed. The main idea is to perform a careful bottom-up traversal of
OPST
exploiting the fact that the maximality property
propagates upward in the tree. By utilizing
OPST
, we avoid the time series scans of the state-of-the-art
MOPP-Miner
algorithm
[
27
]
, which takes
\Omega(n^{3})
time in the worst case. Also, as the complexities of our algorithm do
not
depend on the frequency threshold, it can mine patterns of very low frequency efficiently, unlike
MOPP-Miner
.

3.

We formalize the notion of
closed
frequent OP patterns. Informally, these are all frequent OP patterns that
cannot
be extended to either side and still have the
same
frequency.
Importantly, every maximal OP pattern is also closed and the total number of all closed frequent OP patterns is
\mathcal{O}(n)
. For instance,
p
in Example
1
is a closed frequent OP pattern because it cannot be extended to the left or right and still have frequency
\tau=2
, as explained in Example
2
. We design an exact algorithm to compute all closed frequent OP patterns of an input string
w
.
Here, unfortunately, the closedness property does not propagate upward in the
OPST
of
w
. Thus, our main idea is to perform a careful bottom-up traversal enhanced by a novel application of lowest common ancestor queries
[
6
]
. Our algorithm works in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time using
\mathcal{O}(n)
space provided that
OPST
has already been constructed.

4.

We present experiments using
6
real-world, multi-million letter time series showing that our
\mathcal{O}(n\sigma\log\sigma)
-time
OPST
construction algorithm runs in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time on these datasets, despite the
\mathcal{O}(n\sigma\log\sigma)
bound, and that our frequent pattern mining algorithms are up to orders of magnitude faster than
MOPP-Miner
and natural Apriori-like baselines. Finally, we also present a case study on
2
publicly available datasets and on a proprietary dataset demonstrating the effectiveness of clustering based on maximal frequent OP patterns.

Preliminaries

We view a time series as a string
w=w[0\mathinner{.\,.}n-1]
of length
n

\Sigma

|\Sigma|=\sigma

can be mapped onto the range
[0,\sigma)

\mathcal{O}(n\log\sigma)

By

to the rightmost occurrence
j

w[0\mathinner{.\,.}n-2]
that is at most equal to

(i.e., the
predecessor
of
w[n-1]

j
(because
w[n-1]
is the smallest element), then
p(w)=\perp
. More formally,
p(w)
is the largest

j\leq n-2

such that
w[j]=\max\{w[k]:k\leq n-2,w[k]\leq w[n-1]\}
; if there is no such
j
, then
p(w)=\perp
. By
s(w)
we define the function that maps
w
to the rightmost occurrence
j
of the smallest element of
w[0\mathinner{.\,.}n-2]
that is at least equal to
w[n-1]
(i.e., the
successor
of
w[n-1]
). If there is no such
j
, then
s(w)=\perp
. Similarly,
s(w)
is the largest

j\leq n-2

such that
w[j]=\min\{w[k]:k\leq n-2,w[k]\geq w[n-1]\}
; if there is no such
j
, then
s(w)=\perp
. For implementation purposes, we map the special symbol
\perp
to integer
-1
.

4
The OPST Construction Algorithm

We present an
\mathcal{O}(n\sigma\log\sigma)
-time and
\mathcal{O}(n)
-space algorithm to construct
\textsf{\small OPST}(w)
for string
w
. Before that we show: the preprocessing of
w
into a compact data structure for computing the
LastCode
of any given fragment of
w
; and two crucial combinatorial lemmas: the first one bounds the outdegree of any branching node of
\textsf{\small OPST}(w)
; and the second the number of explicit non-branching nodes in any root-to-leaf path.
In the end, we provide a faster alternative algorithm to construct
\textsf{\small OPST}(w)
in
\mathcal{O}(n\log\sigma)
time using
\mathcal{O}(n)
space.

Preprocessing.

We preprocess

to efficiently compute the

of

for
range predecessor/successor search

Lemma 4

(Letter oracle)
.

Any string
w
of length
n
over an alphabet

\sigma\leq n

\mathcal{O}(n\log\sigma/\log n)

Combinatorial Results.

While, by Lemma
3
,
\textsf{\small OPST}(w)
contains
\Theta(n)
explicit nodes and edges, the maximal outdegree of any branching node of the tree is not known. For our construction algorithm, this is crucial. We next show a lemma bounding the outdegree of any branching node of
\textsf{\small OPST}(w)
by
\mathcal{O}(\sigma)
. This allows us to navigate through
\textsf{\small OPST}(w)
efficiently.

Lemma 5
.

Any branching node in
\textsf{\small OPST}(w)
, for any string
w
, has maximal outdegree
2\sigma+1=\mathcal{O}(\sigma)
.

Proof.

We first show that,
for any string
w
over a totally ordered alphabet of size
\sigma
, there exist only up to
2\sigma+1
possibilities for
\textsf{\small LastCode}(w\cdot a)
over all letters
a
.

Let us start with the trivial case, where
w
is the empty string and so
\textsf{\small LastCode}(w\cdot a)=\textsf{\small LastCode}(a)=(\perp,\perp)
, for any
a
.
In this case, the above upper bound clearly holds.

For the rest of the proof, we assume that
w
is nonempty. We have the following two cases:

1.

If letter
a
belongs to the alphabet of
w
, then
\textsf{\small LastCode}(w\cdot a)
is equal to
(x_{a},x_{a})
, where
x_{a}
is the
single position
of the rightmost occurrence of
a
in
w
. These are
\sigma
possibilities: one for every
a
.

2.

Otherwise let the letters of
w
in the sorted order be equal to
a_{j}
for
j\in[0,\sigma)
, and let
x_{j}
be the position of the rightmost occurrence of
a_{j}
in
w
.
There exists a single value
j\in[-1,\sigma)
such that
a_{j}<a<a_{j+1}
, where
a_{-1}=-\infty
and
a_{\sigma}=\infty
.
Then
\textsf{\small LastCode}(w\cdot a)
=
(x_{j},x_{j+1})
, where
x_{-1}=x_{\sigma}=\perp
.
Note that, in particular, only elements
x_{j}
can occur in
LastCode
.
These are
\sigma+1
possibilities:
(\perp,x_{0}),(x_{0},x_{1}),(x_{1},x_{2}),\ldots,(x_{\sigma-1},\perp)
.

The upper bound of
2\sigma+1
follows by combining cases 1 and 2 above.
By the above upper bound, any branching node of
OPST
cannot have more than

2\sigma+1
outgoing edges.
∎

In the standard suffix tree, every internal explicit node is branching. In
\textsf{\small OPST}(w)
, however, we may have an internal explicit node (other than the root) which is non-branching (e.g.,
v_{3}
in
Fig.

Lemma

6
bounds the number of explicit non-branching nodes on any root-to-leaf path; this is crucial to prove the time complexity of our construction algorithm.

Lemma 6
.

There can be at most
\sigma
explicit non-branching nodes on any root-to-leaf path in
\textsf{\small OPST}(w)
, and in particular between any two branching nodes.

Proof.

We first prove that an explicit non-branching node in
\textsf{\small OPST}(w)
can appear on a root-to-leaf path with witness suffix
w^{\prime}
only right before the first occurrence of some letter.

Let
xb
be a witness substring of a node directly below an explicit non-branching node
u
, where
x
is a string and
b
is a letter.
For the explicit non-branching node to be constructed there must exist a branching node
v
such that
\textsf{\small SufLink}(v)=u
. Let the witnesses of any two children of
v
be equal to
a^{\prime}x^{\prime}b^{\prime}
and
a^{\prime\prime}x^{\prime\prime}b^{\prime\prime}
, respectively, where
x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}
are strings and
a^{\prime},a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime},b^{\prime\prime}
are letters.
We have:

•

a^{\prime}x^{\prime}\approx a^{\prime\prime}x^{\prime\prime}
, since both those witnesses are represented by the same node
v
.

•

a^{\prime}x^{\prime}b^{\prime}\not\approx a^{\prime\prime}x^{\prime\prime}b^{\prime\prime}
, since the two children of
v
are distinct nodes – recall that node
v
is branching.

•

x^{\prime}b^{\prime}\approx xb\approx x^{\prime\prime}b^{\prime\prime}
, since
Locus
of both
x^{\prime}b^{\prime}
and
x^{\prime\prime}b^{\prime\prime}
(witnesses of children of
v
with their first letter deleted) must be equal to the single child of
u
– recall that node
u
is explicit but non-branching so it has only one child.

Notice that for two equal-length strings
w_{1}\not\approx w_{2}
, there must exist two positions
i,j
such that the relationship (
<
,
=
,
>
) between
w_{1}[i]
and
w_{1}[j]
is different than the relationship between
w_{2}[i]
and
w_{2}[j]
. Hence the relationship between
a^{\prime}
and
b^{\prime}
has to be different than the relationship between
a^{\prime\prime}
and
b^{\prime\prime}
as the relationship of all the other pairs of the letters of
a^{\prime}x^{\prime}b^{\prime}
and
a^{\prime\prime}x^{\prime\prime}b^{\prime\prime}
is fixed by the two listed equivalences.
This proves that
b^{\prime}
cannot appear in
x^{\prime}
as if that was the case the relationship between
a^{\prime}
and
b^{\prime}
(as a letter of
x^{\prime}
) would be fixed and the same as the relationship between
a^{\prime\prime}
and
b^{\prime\prime}
– a contradiction.
Thus
b
does not appear in
x
since
xb\approx x^{\prime}b^{\prime}
.

Now, since the explicit non-branching node on a root-to-leaf path can only appear just above the first occurrence of a letter, there are only at most
\sigma
such nodes. Clearly, this also implies that there can only be at most
\sigma
explicit non-branching nodes
between any two branching nodes.
∎

5.2
Mining Closed Frequent OP Patterns

Recall that
w
is a string of length
n
. An OP pattern
p=\textsf{\small PrefCode}(x)
for a string
x
is:

•

right-closed
in
w
, if there exists a substring
w[i\mathinner{.\,.}j]\approx x

whose
\textsf{\small PrefCode}(w[i\mathinner{.\,.}j])
is the
PrefCode
of strictly more fragments of
w
than
\textsf{\small PrefCode}(w[i\mathinner{.\,.}j+1])
;

left-closedness
is defined analogously.

•

closed
, if it is left-closed and right-closed.

We may characterize nodes of
OPST
based on the properties of the patterns they represent, e.g., right-closed, or closed.

We show an
\mathcal{O}(n)
-time and
\mathcal{O}(n)
-space algorithm for mining
all closed
\tau
-frequent OP patterns in
w
given
\textsf{\small OPST}(w)
.

Preprocessing.

We preprocess
\textsf{\small OPST}(w)
to answer lowest common ancestor (LCA) queries
[
6
]
using Lemma
8
. These queries are key to the efficiency of our algorithm.

Lemma 8

(
[
6
]
)
.

Any rooted tree
T
on
N
nodes can be preprocessed in
\mathcal{O}(N)
time to support the following
\mathcal{O}(1)
-time queries: given any two nodes
u
and
v
of
T
return node
\textsf{LCA}(u,v)
, that is, the LCA of
u
and
v
in
T
.

Phase I.

It marks each branching node of
\textsf{\small OPST}(w)
with at least
\tau
leaf descendants as a
candidate
using a DFS traversal. Since OP patterns ending at branching nodes are by construction right-closed, the candidate nodes represent all OP patterns that are
right-closed
and

\tau
-frequent.

Phase II.

It identifies each candidate that is
also
left-closed, to obtain all closed
\tau
-frequent OP patterns.

A candidate node
v
is left-closed if it has at least two witnesses with different codes when extended to the left by a single letter; i.e.,
v
has at least two leaf descendants
i
and
j
such that

w[i\mathinner{.\,.}i+\textsf{\small Depth}(v)-1]\approx w[j\mathinner{.\,.}j+\textsf{\small Depth}(v)-1]
but

w[i-1\mathinner{.\,.}i+\textsf{\small Depth}(v)-1]\not\approx w[j-1\mathinner{.\,.}j+\textsf{\small Depth}(v)-1]
(recall that
\textsf{\small Depth}(v)
is the string depth of
v
). An OP pattern is closed
\tau
-frequent if and only if it labels a branching node
v
that is left-closed
and
has at least
\tau
leaf descendants.

We next show how left-closed candidates are identified.
For each candidate node
v
, we need to extend each of its leaf descendants
w[\ell\mathinner{.\,.}n-1]
to
w[\ell-1\mathinner{.\,.}n-1]
(by a single letter to the left). Let
L_{v}
be the set of the leaf descendants of
v
, each represented by its label. The crucial idea is to check if
v
is left-closed by computing the string depth of the LCA of leaf nodes
\{\ell-1:\ell\in L_{v}\}
, denoted by
val(v)
.
Clearly, if
val(v)<\textsf{\small Depth}(v)+1
, then it is because at least two leaf descendants have different codes on their left, and so
v
is left-closed. This approach avoids the pairwise comparison of the
n

PrefCode
’s of the leaf nodes, which would result in
\Omega(n^{2})
time, instead of the
\mathcal{O}(n)
time of our approach.

Example 9
.

Node
v_{4}
in Fig.
1
has
\textsf{\small Depth}(v_{4})=3
and
L_{v_{4}}=\{5,2\}
.
Since
\{\ell-1:\ell\in L_{v_{4}}\}=\{4,1\}
, and

\textsf{LCA}(\{4,1\})
(i.e.,
v_{2}
) has string depth
3
, we have
val(v_{4})=3<\textsf{\small Depth}(v_{4})+1=3+1=4
.
Thus,
v_{4}
is left-closed.
Node
v_{3}
has
\textsf{\small Depth}(v_{3})=2
,
L_{v_{3}}=\{5,2\}
,
\{\ell-1:\ell\in L_{v_{3}}\}=\{4,1\}
, and

\textsf{LCA}(\{4,1\})
has string depth
3
. Since
val(v_{3})=3\geq\textsf{\small Depth}(v_{3})+1=3
,
v_{3}
is
not
left-closed.

In the standard suffix tree,
val(v)
is either
\textsf{\small Depth}(v)+1
or
0
:
val(v)=0
means that
v
and all its ancestors are left-closed and thus finding closed patterns can be done easily using a DFS. In
OPST
, however,
val(v)\in[0,\textsf{\small Depth}(v)+1]
, and it implies left-closedness for
v

only
if
val(v)<\textsf{\small Depth}(v)+1
(and this propagates only to ancestors that are deep enough).
Observation

1
highlights this essential difference.

Observation 1
.

If
w[i+1\mathinner{.\,.}j]=w[i^{\prime}+1\mathinner{.\,.}j^{\prime}]
and
w[i\mathinner{.\,.}j-1]=w[i^{\prime}\mathinner{.\,.}j^{\prime}-1]
then
w[i\mathinner{.\,.}j]=w[i^{\prime}\mathinner{.\,.}j^{\prime}]
; but
w[i+1\mathinner{.\,.}j]\approx w[i^{\prime}+1\mathinner{.\,.}j^{\prime}]
and
w[i\mathinner{.\,.}j-1]\approx w[i^{\prime}\mathinner{.\,.}j^{\prime}-1]
do not imply
w[i\mathinner{.\,.}j]\approx w[i^{\prime}\mathinner{.\,.}j^{\prime}]
in the OP setting.

To compute
val(v)
for all nodes
v
, instead of explicitly considering
L_{v}
for each
v
,
we use a DFS traversal on
\textsf{\small OPST}(w)
coupled with several LCA queries. For any leaf
v
,
val(v)
is set to
\textsf{\small Depth}(v)+1
. Since
val(v)<\textsf{\small Depth}(v)+1
does not currently hold, every leaf
v
is not left-closed. For any other node
v
,
val(v)
is computed as the minimum between the values
val
of its children and of the string depths of the LCA’s of
\textsf{\small Witness}(v)-1
and
\textsf{\small Witness}(u)-1
for every child
u
of
v
. We compute these LCA’s efficiently by employing
Lemma

8
: for any set of
k
nodes
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
, we can compute their LCA, denoted by
u_{k-1}
, in
\mathcal{O}(k)
time, by computing
u_{1}=\textsf{LCA}(v_{1},v_{2}),u_{2}=\textsf{LCA}(u_{1},v_{3}),\ldots,u_{k-1}=\textsf{LCA}(u_{k-2},v_{k})
. Then node
v
is left-closed if and only if
val(v)<\textsf{\small Depth}(v)+1
.
Since any DFS traversal takes linear time and we have
\mathcal{O}(n)
explicit nodes and edges, the total time is
\mathcal{O}(n)
provided that
\textsf{\small OPST}(w)
is given. We have thus proved the following result:

Theorem 3
.

For any string
w
of length
n
and any integer
\tau>1
,
we can list all closed
\tau
-frequent OP patterns of
w
in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time using
\mathcal{O}(n)
space if
\textsf{\small OPST}(w)
is given.

Example 10
.

Let
w=1\penalty\ 2\penalty\ 4\penalty\ 4\penalty\ 2\penalty\ 5\penalty\ 5\penalty\ 1
(see Fig.
1
) and
\tau=2
.
In Phase I, all branching nodes are
marked as candidates. In Phase II, we obtain
4
closed
2
-frequent OP patterns:
(\perp,\perp)
;

(\perp,\perp)\penalty\ (0,\perp)
;

(\perp,\perp)\penalty\ (0,\perp)\penalty\ (1,1)
; and

(\perp,\perp)\penalty\ (0,0)\penalty\ (\perp,1)
.
For example, node
v_{4}
is left-closed; it is also branching with
2
leaf descendants. Thus, it represents a closed
2
-frequent OP pattern, namely
(\perp,\perp)\penalty\ (0,0)\penalty\ (\perp,1)
. This pattern corresponds to the fragments
4\penalty\ 4\penalty\ 2
and
5\penalty\ 5\penalty\ 1
of
w
. On the other hand, node
v_{1}
is not left-closed, as
val(v_{1})=3=\textsf{\small Depth}(v_{1})+1
.

Related Work

Order-preserving indexing
[
18
]

and pattern matching
[
10
]
have been studied heavily by the theory community. However, only very recently practical mining algorithms were developed. Such algorithms mine various types of OP patterns (frequent
[
27
,
31
]
, maximal frequent
[
27
]
, co-occurrence
[
30
]
, or
top-
k
contrast
[
29
]
),
or OP rules
[
31
]
. The most relevant algorithm to our work is
MOPP-Miner

[
27
]
. This algorithm utilizes an efficient pattern matching algorithm, an Apriori-like enumeration strategy, and pruning strategies. Its time and space complexity is
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot\mu\cdot L)
and
\mathcal{O}(\mu\cdot(L+n))
, respectively, where
n
is the length of the input time series,
\mu
is the maximum length of a candidate frequent OP pattern, and
L
is the number of fixed-length candidate frequent OP patterns. Since in the
worst case
,
\mu=\Theta(n)
and
L=\Theta(n)
,
MOPP-Miner
needs
\Omega(n^{3})
time and
\Omega(n^{2})
space.
Thus, our algorithm for maximal frequent OP patterns is
faster theoretically and practically; see
Theorem

2
and
Section

7
, respectively.
