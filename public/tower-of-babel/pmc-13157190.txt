High-strength, programmable energy-dissipating liquid crystal elastomers via hydrogen-bond-locked 4D printing
Authors: Yue Wu, Ran Bi, Yuan Liu, Yanzhao Yang, Wei Feng, Ling Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySoft elasticity in monodomain liquid crystal elastomers (LCEs) is promising for impact-absorbing applications. However, its application has been limited by its low strength and restricted structural tunability. Here, we report a 4D-printing strategy for programmable damping in which the 2-ureido-4[1H]-pyrimidinone (UPy) motif is grafted into the LCE network. The resulting quadruple hydrogen bonds (HBs) rapidly lock the alignment during direct ink writing (DIW), which is subsequently fixed by UV curing. The UPy-LCEs achieved impact energy-dissipation ratios of up to 89.24% and showed anisotropic hysteresis under cyclic compression, with R ≈ 53.8% parallel to the director and R ≈ 28.8% perpendicular to it; the energy-dissipation behavior can be tuned by printing parameters. The materials exhibit tensile strengths up to 9.4 MPa. By coupling HB alignment locking with 4D-printable architectures, this work establishes an integrated route to high-performance and geometry-programmable energy-absorbing LCEs for soft protection, damping, and robotic interfaces.

Introduction

Additive manufacturing (AM) technologies offer unparalleled flexibility in achieving controlled composition, geometric shape, function, and complexity over traditional manufacturing methods—allowing us to readily optimize material systems’ performance for specific applications.1,2,3,4,5 One key application area is the development of bespoke lattice structures from lightweight materials for the dissipation of mechanical energy, providing vital functions including shock absorption and frequency attenuation for protective equipment, transportation, and packaging, as well as soundproofing.6,7,8 Recent studies have demonstrated that tailoring lattice architectures of lightweight materials through 3D printing enables precise control over their mechanical properties and energy dissipation characteristics. For example, lightweight cellular composites with the controlled alignment of silicon carbide whiskers and carbon fibers were fabricated using epoxy-based inks via 3D printing9; anisotropic reinforcement architectures with Bouligand-type aligned polymer resin A/multiwalled carbon nanotubes were created through electrically assisted nanocomposite 3D printing (polymer resin A contains high tensile epoxy diacrylate (≈ 9000 psi)).10 Also, gradient Bouligand structures with enhanced impact resistance were fabricated in ceramic-polymer composites using 3D printing (polycarbonate, thermoplastic urethane, and polymethyl methacrylate were used).11 Moreover, some conventional lightweight polymeric materials, such as polylactic acid,8 shear stiffening gel,12,13 polyetheretherketone,14 and polydimethylsiloxane,15,16 have been investigated for 3D printing energy dissipation applications. The selection of these traditional materials is primarily based on two considerations: ease of printing/forming and the viscoelastic behavior of the materials themselves. However, under high strain rate conditions, their mechanical performance and energy absorption efficiency are suboptimal, with the energy absorption density remaining nearly unchanged. Thus, novel materials that exhibit high damping capacity, high mechanical strength, and high modulus under high strain rate conditions are expected for the development of energy dissipation materials.

Liquid crystal elastomers (LCEs), composed of rigid, rod-like shaped mesogens in a polymeric network, combine the rubber elasticity and free rotation of liquid crystalline molecules in the elastomer matrix.17,18,19,20,21 Particularly, nematic LCEs exhibit a polydomain or a monodomain phase state where both phase states are composed of macro/micrometer-sized domains with local nematic ordering, resulting in a unique plateau-like mechanical response, so-called soft elasticity that differs markedly from those of conventional soft materials.22,23,24,25 This almost zero-stiffness plateau of constant stress upon strain caused by the internal rotation of the local director, which makes LCEs promising materials for energy-dissipative applications, with wide-ranging uses in biomedical fields, vibration-damping structures, and impact protection.26,27,28,29 Moreover, the “rate-dependent behavior” of LCEs demonstrates superior mechanical properties and enhanced energy absorption capabilities under high strain rate conditions, which enables the development of novel high-impact-resistant devices. Moreover, LCEs are particularly attractive for 3D printing because this technique enables control over both structure and mesogen organization in printed constructs. It not only allows the fabrication of complex geometries and lightweight lattices, but also provides a direct route to integrate shape design with tailored mechanical and functional anisotropy. For instance, polydomain LCEs had superior energy dissipation and rate dependency behavior compared to conventional isotropic elastomeric materials using digital light processing 3D printing.30 Importantly, monodomain LCEs that exhibit macroscopically aligned liquid crystal mesogens display pronounced anisotropic behavior. The angle between the director and the loading direction strongly influences the elastic modulus, loss factor, and energy absorption density.31,32 Despite this promise, the practical deployment of LCEs in load-bearing damping and protection applications is hindered by their limited mechanical robustness. Conventional LCEs are typically very soft with elastic moduli ranging from the kilopascal to tensile strengths on the order of a few megapascals, which exhibit pronounced soft elasticity, but it also leads to premature failure under high-impact or high-stress conditions.33,34,35,36,37,38,39 More broadly, the 3D printing of stimuli-responsive LCEs can also be viewed as 4D printing, because the printed structures are capable of time-dependent changes in shape or properties under external stimuli. In contrast, increasing the density of covalent crosslinks can enhance stiffness and strength but simultaneously restricts mesogen mobility, thereby suppressing the large actuation strains and soft elasticity that underlie efficient energy dissipation. Therefore, low strength arises primarily from the relatively low density of permanent covalent crosslinks that are required to preserve chain mobility for mesogen reorientation. It is desired to develop a new design of LCEs that synergistically combine mechanical strength with preserved mesogens mobility, thereby retaining soft elasticity and efficient dissipation performance.

Supramolecular interactions stand out in the design of such dynamic functional materials due to their unique advantages, including selective binding, tunable strength, and environmental responsiveness.17,40,41,42,43 Representative examples include halogen bonding, metal-ligand coordination, ion-dipole interaction, and so on.42,43,44,45,46 Among the diverse supramolecular interactions, hydrogen bonding (HB) is particularly appealing because it enables dynamic dissociation/reassociation behavior under thermal and mechanical stimuli, making it an effective tool for constructing adaptive polymer networks capable of mechanical reinforcement and energy dissipation. For example, the behavior of intrinsically reversible hydrogen-bonded supramolecular networks and their energy dissipation under mild conditions has been demonstrated. An impact-resistant supramolecular polymer leverages multiple HB motifs to enable dynamic aggregation of nanoscale hard segments and transient soft networks, thereby achieving reversible impact-hardening and stiffness-switchable mechanics47; HB was generated as a by-product in the amine-acrylate aza-Michael addition, enhancing energy dissipation in monodomain LCEs during partial crosslinking.48 These advantages lead to our hypothesis that incorporating HBs as dynamic cross-links into LCEs could provide a novel approach for the simple and efficient design of energy-dissipating materials. In particular, 2-ureido-4[1H]-pyrimidinone (UPy) serves as an ideal building block due to its ability to form robust self-complementary dimers via a donor-donor-acceptor-acceptor (DDAA) quadruple hydrogen-bonding array.49,50,51 When incorporated into an LCE network, UPy dimers behave as physical crosslinks that reinforce the network at rest, increasing the modulus and strength. Crucially, because these crosslinks are physical rather than chemical, they can dissociate during the LC phase transition or under extreme deformation, allowing the necessary chain mobility for actuation and director rotation. Beyond molecular design, the energy-dissipation performance of LCEs can also be regulated through structural design. By controlling mesogen alignment and geometric architecture, it becomes possible to tune stress-transfer pathways and deformation modes, thereby expanding the dissipative functionality of hydrogen-bonded LCEs. However, realizing such synergistic control over molecular interactions and structural organization remains challenging, as the fabrication method must simultaneously support mesogen alignment, complex 3D shaping, and the dynamic nature of supramolecular crosslinks. Introducing 4D printing as a fabrication strategy for LCEs offers additional opportunities to improve their performance through simultaneous control over mesogen orientation and structural geometry. Such integration is particularly attractive for energy-dissipating systems, because printing-enabled anisotropy and architectural design can provide extra freedom to regulate deformation behavior and mechanical response. However, implementing this concept is not straightforward, since the processing method must not only ensure sufficient printability and structural fidelity, but also preserve the liquid-crystalline ordering and dynamic supramolecular interactions that are essential to the material’s function.

Direct ink writing (DIW) has emerged as an attractive AM approach to program highly oriented mesogens and complex architectures in LCEs.52,53 By tailoring the printing path, it is possible to spatially program the director orientation and construct architected lattices with locally controlled anisotropy.54,55,56,57,58 In this work, by introducing the rapid immobilization of liquid crystal molecules via strong HBs combined with DIW technology, we have fabricated LCEs with a high degree of orientation and complex 3D structures for applications in the field of energy dissipation. We synthesize an UPy-functionalized mesogenic monomer capable of forming 4-fold HBs and formulate photocurable LCE inks suitable for DIW. The resulting UPy-LCE networks are systematically characterized to elucidate the influence of UPy content on their phase behavior, liquid crystal order, and mechanical strength. An investigation was conducted into their impact and cyclic compression damping performance, including the dependence of energy dissipation on strain rate, loading direction, and printed lattice architecture. We demonstrate that locking the nozzle-induced alignment via UPy quadruple HBs during DIW, followed by UV curing, enables UPy-LCEs that combine high damping ratios (impact energy-dissipation ratios up to 89.24%) with enhanced tensile strength (up to 9.4 MPa). These results highlight a general strategy for designing high-performance, highly oriented, and geometry-programmable LCEs for next-generation soft protection, damping, and robotic interfaces.

Results

The synthesis of UPy-LCE

In the precursor formulation of the LCE, n-pentylamine and 5-amino-1-pentanol were introduced as co-amines and jointly participated in the aza-Michael chain-extension reaction of the acrylate-functional liquid-crystal precursor. Among them, 5-amino-1-pentanol not only acted as a chain extender but also introduced pendant hydroxyl groups for the subsequent incorporation of the isocyanate-terminated UPy motif (denoted as UPy-NCO), whereas n-pentylamine served as a non-hydroxylated chain extender to regulate the precursor structure without participating in the later UPy incorporation step (Figure S1). In the synthetic procedure, RM82 was first reacted with n-pentylamine and 5-amino-1-pentanol to afford a hydroxyl-functionalized precursor, which was then dissolved in chloroform and further reacted with UPy-NCO in the presence of DBTDL as a catalyst for the hydroxyl-isocyanate reaction to form urethane linkages, followed by the addition of photoinitiator to obtain the final UPy-LCE ink (Figures 1A and S2). This molecule was developed not only to introduce reversible supramolecular interactions into the final network, but also to endow the ink with a process-active function during DIW. Specifically, when the UPy liquid crystal ink is maintained in the printing barrel and nozzle at elevated temperature, the quadruple hydrogen-bonding associations are largely dissociated, which preserves sufficient chain mobility and printability. Upon extrusion, the temperature drop promotes rapid re-formation of HBs between paired UPy units, thereby providing a transient supramolecular locking effect that helps suppress the relaxation of the nozzle-induced mesogen alignment before the subsequent UV curing step permanently fixes the anisotropic network. Subsequent UV irradiation triggers an addition reaction of the acrylate double bonds at both ends of the mesogens to form a crosslinked network that further stabilizes the orientation, ultimately yielding an LCE with a high degree of alignment (Figure 1B). The foundation of the high-performance UPy-LCE lies in the successful synthesis of the UPy monomer and its seamless integration into the polymer network. The 1H NMR spectrum of the synthesized UPy revealed three distinct, sharp peaks in the downfield region (10.0–14.0 ppm) (Figure S3). These peaks correspond to the N-H protons involved in the quadruple HB array (specifically, the intramolecular H-bond that planarizes the UPy unit and the intermolecular H-bonds that facilitate dimerization). The sharpness of these peaks confirms the high purity of the monomer and the formation of the desired supramolecular motif. The synthesis of the UPy monomer was confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy. Three sharp peaks are observed at 13.2, 11.9, and 10.2 ppm, which are assigned to the −NH protons of UPy. These signals are consistent with the 1H NMR data reported in the literature, confirming that UPy was successfully synthesized.Figure 1Synthesis and printing-induced alignment mechanism of UPy-LCE(A) Synthetic route and representative chemical structure of the UPy-LCE ink.(B) Schematic of UPy improves the orientation of liquid crystal molecules during the printing process.

Orientation behavior and mechanical properties of UPy-LCEs

The orientation of the printed LCE was characterized by polarized optical microscopy and two-dimensional wide-angle X-ray diffraction (2D-WAXD). The polarized optical micrograph of the printed UPy-LCE exhibits a pronounced change between bright and dark states upon rotating the sample by 45°, indicating good alignment of the liquid crystal mesogens. In addition, the 2D-WAXD pattern of the printed UPy-LCE recorded at room temperature (Figure 2A) displays two intense diffraction arcs, further demonstrating a high degree of orientational order of the mesogens. Both results confirm that the printed UPy-LCE possesses excellent orientation, providing a solid basis for subsequent investigation of its energy-dissipation performance. The higher the degree of orientation of the liquid crystal mesogens, the larger the contraction strain generated during the phase transition. The influence of printing temperature and UPy content on molecular orientation is shown in Figure 2B. For UPy-LCEs with different UPy contents, the actuation strain of the printed filaments decreases as the printing temperature increases. This behavior can be attributed to the competition between flow-induced mesogen alignment and thermally activated orientational relaxation: Although the liquid crystal mesogens in the UPy-LCE ink become oriented under the combined shear and extensional fields inside the nozzle, elevated printing temperatures enhance chain mobility and accelerate the tendency of the mesogens to relax back toward a disordered state before fixation (Figure S4). As a result, the printing-induced alignment is less effectively retained, leading to weaker anisotropic actuation and a reduced shrinkage strain of the printed filaments. This orientation-dependent behavior is further supported by the DMA results. Along the direction parallel to the LCE alignment, the storage modulus remains consistently higher than that measured in the perpendicular direction over the whole temperature range, indicating more effective load transfer through the aligned mesogenic network. Meanwhile, the tan δ curves reveal clear anisotropic relaxation behavior. The perpendicular direction exhibits a slightly higher and sharper tan δ peak, reaching a maximum value of 0.792 at 20 °C, whereas the parallel direction shows a somewhat lower peak, with a maximum value of 0.658 at 25 °C, but maintains a higher tan δ over a broader temperature window. These results indicate that the perpendicular direction undergoes a more concentrated segmental relaxation near the transition region, while the parallel direction combines higher stiffness with more sustained energy dissipation at elevated temperatures. Therefore, when both mechanical support and damping capability are considered together, the direction parallel to the mesogen alignment is more advantageous. In this sense, a lower printing temperature is beneficial because it better preserves the printing-induced mesogen orientation, thereby promoting anisotropic actuation. At the same time, increasing UPy content imposes an additional processing constraint. As the UPy fraction increases, the ink viscosity rises markedly, and a higher printing temperature is required to ensure smooth extrusion. Specifically, when the UPy content is 10% or 20%, the UPy-LCE ink can no longer be extruded smoothly at 50 °C. When the UPy content is increased to 30%, extrusion becomes difficult even at 60 °C. At 40% UPy content, printing is only possible at temperatures above 80 °C, under which the shrinkage strain of the printed filaments falls below 30%. Therefore, considering both printability and actuation performance, compositions containing 10%, 20%, and 30% UPy were selected for subsequent studies, and the printing temperature was fixed at 65 °C (Figure S5). Taken together, these results further support that the UPy motif plays a dual role throughout the printing process: Before curing, its reversible reassociation helps retain the flow-induced mesogen alignment generated inside the nozzle; after curing, the dynamic HBs serve as sacrificial supramolecular interactions that can dissociate and reform under deformation for stress relaxation and energy dissipation.

Effect of printing speed on the orientation. During printing, the orientation of the liquid crystal mesogens is primarily governed by two forces: One is the shear force experienced during extrusion through the conical nozzle, and the other is the extensional force arising from the fact that the printing speed is higher than the extrusion speed. The orientation induced by extrusion is mainly controlled by temperature and has been discussed above, whereas the orientation induced by stretching is mainly determined by the printing speed. To ensure smooth printing under all investigated conditions, an ink with 30% UPy content was selected, with the printing pressure set to 0.6 MPa and the printing temperature to 65 °C. The actuation strain of the UPy-LCE filaments first increases and then levels off as the printing speed increases. The plateau behavior may be attributed to the fact that a printing speed of 7 mm s−1 already achieves the optimal orientation attainable for the mesogens in the ink (Figure S6).

To identify an appropriate base formulation for the subsequent LCE-OH and UPy-LCE systems, we first examined the effect of the amine:acrylate stoichiometric ratio on the mechanical behavior of the precursor-derived network while keeping the mesogenic content constant (Figure 2C). This analysis was used to establish a suitable base composition for the subsequent incorporation of hydroxyl-functional and UPy-containing segments. As the amine:acrylate ratio decreases from 1.07 to 0.67, the stress-strain curves show a monotonic increase in slope and ultimate stress accompanied by a pronounced loss in extensibility. The amine-deficient sample (amine:acrylate = 0.67) fractures at a strain below 100% but sustains the highest stress, indicating a relatively dense network structure. In contrast, the amine-rich sample (amine:acrylate = 1.07) can be stretched to more than 600% strain but exhibits a much lower stress level, consistent with a more loosely connected network containing a larger fraction of dangling chains. Formulations with intermediate ratios (0.77–0.97) strike a balance between strength and stretchability, with the sample at amine:acrylate = 0.87 displaying a fracture strain around 200–250% while still maintaining a stress at break above 2 MPa. Considering both mechanical robustness and deformability, the composition with amine:acrylate = 0.87 was selected as the base formulation for the subsequent LCE-OH and UPy-LCE materials.

On the optimized covalent network (amine:acrylate = 0.87), we then evaluated the influence of hydroxyl functionalization and UPy incorporation on the tensile behavior (Figure S7). The pristine LCE exhibits a relatively low modulus and strength but very large elongation, reflecting its loosely crosslinked nature. Introducing hydroxyl groups along the mesogenic backbone (LCE-OH) markedly stiffens the material and increases the stress at a given strain while still retaining high extensibility (>600%), which can be attributed to additional intermolecular HB and a slightly increased effective crosslink density. After grafting UPy units onto the same backbone to form LCE-UPy, the stress-strain curve becomes substantially steeper, and the fracture stress further increases to nearly 9 MPa, albeit at a reduced fracture strain of about 250–300%. The pronounced strain hardening of LCE-UPy suggests that the quadruple HBs act as sacrificial physical crosslinks: They reinforce the network at small strains and progressively dissociate under large deformation, dissipating energy while preserving overall integrity. These results confirm that UPy-based supramolecular interactions provide an efficient route to simultaneously enhance the strength and potential energy-dissipation capability of the printed LCEs.

The effect of introducing UPy on the mechanical properties of the LCE was investigated by conducting tensile stress-strain tests on four UPy-LCE films with different UPy contents (0, 10, 20, and 30%). As shown in Figure 2D, the tensile strain at break gradually decreases with increasing UPy content, while the fracture stress and Young’s modulus increase. The fracture stresses of the UPy-LCE films with 10, 20, and 30% UPy are 7.8 MPa, 8.5 MPa, and 9.4 MPa, respectively, which are 2.00, 2.18, and 2.41 times higher than that of the pure LCE (3.9 MPa). The enhancement in mechanical properties induced by UPy is similar in origin to the increase in TNI: The incorporation of UPy introduces strong HBs into the LCE network, effectively increasing the crosslinking density and thereby improving the mechanical strength.

Energy dissipation performance of UPy-LCEs

Drop tests were carried out to investigate the effect of UPy content on the impact-mitigation capability of UPy-LCEs. A glass sphere was released from the same initial height onto either a wooden tabletop (blank group) or UPy-LCE samples with identical geometry but different UPy contents (0, 10, 20, and 30%) (Figure S8). The rebound height of the glass sphere was used as a preliminary indicator of the influence of UPy content on the impact energy dissipation of UPy-LCEs. The rebound height of the glass sphere in the blank group was 36.81 cm, corresponding to an energy-dissipation ratio of 26.38% (defined as (initial height − rebound height)/initial height). For the LCE, UPy0.1-LCE, UPy0.2-LCE, and UPy0.3-LCE groups, the rebound heights were 8.94 cm, 7.86 cm, 6.07 cm, and 5.38 cm, yielding energy-dissipation ratios of 82.12%, 84.28%, 87.86%, and 89.24% (Table S1), respectively. The energy-dissipation ratio of UPy0.3-LCE is about 63% higher than that of the blank group and about 7% higher than that of pure LCE. These results indicate that single-domain LCEs exhibit excellent impact energy-dissipation capability, and that the incorporation of UPy further enhances the dissipation performance; the energy-dissipation ability of UPy-LCEs increases progressively with increasing UPy content.

The hysteresis between the loading and unloading curves is taken as a measure of mechanical energy dissipation. By comparing Figures 3A and 3B, it can be seen that compression parallel to the alignment direction exhibits a much more pronounced hysteresis effect than compression perpendicular to the alignment direction: During unloading, the stress in the parallel-aligned sample drops to zero at a residual strain of about 12.6%, whereas in the perpendicular case the stress does not fall to zero until the residual strain is about 7.7%. This may be because compression parallel to the alignment direction facilitates more extensive rotation of the liquid crystal mesogens, allowing more energy to be absorbed, while the polymer chains aligned parallel to the stress direction can also undergo easier slippage, which further contributes to energy absorption. For convenient comparison and discussion, the energy represented by the hysteresis loop of each curve was normalized by the energy absorbed during loading and defined as the energy dissipation ratio. Specifically, the hysteresis area between the loading and unloading curves is denoted as E1, and the area under the loading curve as E0, so that the energy dissipation ratio R is given by R = E1/E0. The energy dissipation ratios for compression parallel and perpendicular to the alignment direction are approximately 53.8% (Figure 3C) and 28.8% (Figure 3D), respectively. Thus, compression along the alignment direction enables single-domain UPy-LCE to exhibit superior compressive energy-dissipation performance. Effect of pore size and printing architecture on compressive energy dissipation

We further investigated how structural design parameters introduced during printing, including pore size, filament arrangement, and lattice topology, can be used to program the compressive energy-dissipation behavior of UPy-LCE architectures. As shown in Figure 4, the energy dissipation ratios of 90° (Figures 4A and 4C) orthogonal and 45° (Figures 4B and 4D) staggered lattices with varying pore sizes (0.5, 0.7, and 0.9 mm) were systematically compared. For the 90° orthogonal lattice, the energy dissipation ratios were 44.2%, 55.1%, and 52.3% for pore sizes of 0.5, 0.7, and 0.9 mm, respectively. In contrast, the 45° staggered lattice exhibited higher values of 47.9%, 60.0%, and 56.2% under the same pore sizes. Notably, the 0.7 mm pore size achieved the optimal energy dissipation efficiency for both architectures, suggesting a critical balance between structural deformation capacity and material volume fraction. The observed trend can be attributed to two competing factors. Smaller pores (0.5 mm) restrict the reorientation of liquid crystal mesogens and the global deformation of the lattice struts, thereby limiting energy dissipation. Conversely, excessively large pores (0.9 mm) reduce the volumetric proportion of the LCE phase, diminishing the material’s intrinsic damping capacity despite enhanced local strut flexibility. The intermediate pore size (0.7 mm) effectively reconciles these constraints, enabling sufficient mesogen mobility while maintaining a high LCE content for robust energy absorption. In addition, the printed UPy-LCE lattices exhibited clear direction-dependent compressive responses (Figure S9). Under cyclic compression along the three orthogonal directions, both the 90° orthogonal and 45° staggered lattices showed distinct stress-strain hysteresis behaviors. This anisotropic response originates from the printing-defined filament arrangement and layer-stacking sequence, which determine the deformation pathway and stress distribution during compression, demonstrating that the damping behavior of the printed UPy-LCE architectures can be programmed not only by pore size and lattice topology, but also by the relative orientation between the applied load and the printed structure.Figure 4Effect of pore size and printing architecture on compressive energy dissipation(A) Photographs of the 90° orthogonal staggered lattices with different pore sizes (0.5, 0.7, and 0.9 mm). Furthermore, the lattice architecture significantly modulates the dissipation behavior. The 45° staggered configuration consistently outperformed the 90° orthogonal design, with an average improvement of ∼5% across all pore sizes (Figure 4E). This enhancement arises from the staggered architecture’s ability to distribute stress more uniformly and activate multi-directional deformation mechanisms during compression. The inclined strut alignment in the 45° staggered lattice promotes shear-dominated deformation modes, which amplify viscoelastic losses and interfacial friction between the LCE domains. The cyclic compression results further clarify the durability of this architected damping behavior. As shown in Figure S10, during the first 10 loading-unloading cycles, the hysteresis loops gradually become narrower, indicating a certain degree of structural accommodation and partial stress relaxation in the early stage of repeated deformation. Nevertheless, distinct loop areas are maintained throughout these initial cycles, confirming that the lattice retains substantial energy-dissipation capacity. To evaluate long-term cycling stability, representative hysteresis curves collected over 50 consecutive compression cycles are presented in Figure S11. The first few cycles show the largest loop area, followed by a gradual reduction and then convergence of the curves in later cycles. Importantly, even after 50 cycles, the loops remain clearly open, demonstrating persistent compressive energy dissipation under repeated loading.

In addition to pore size, lattice topology and loading rate also play decisive roles in the compressive energy-dissipation behavior of the 4D-printed structures. Figure 5 shows representative photographs and compressive stress-strain curves of regular hexagonal (Figure 5A), square (Figure 5B), and equilateral triangular (Figure 5C) UPy-LCE lattices compressed at strain rates of 0.1, 0.5, and 1 s−1. For all three architectures, increasing the strain rate leads to higher peak stresses and enlarged hysteresis loops, reflecting the intrinsic viscoelasticity of the UPy-LCE matrix and the rate-dependent activation of supramolecular HBs. Nevertheless, the magnitude of this rate dependence is strongly topology-dependent. The regular hexagonal lattice exhibits the lowest stiffness and the smallest hysteresis area, consistent with a deformation mode dominated by bending of thin cell ribs. The square lattice shows an intermediate response, with a pronounced plateau regime originating from progressive buckling and plastic bending of vertical and horizontal struts. In contrast, the equilateral triangular lattice is the stiffest of the three and delivers the largest hysteresis loops at all strain rates, indicating superior energy-dissipation efficiency. In this stretching-dominated architecture, most filaments are inclined with respect to the loading axis and are engaged simultaneously in axial stretching and bending, which promotes mesogen rotation and the reversible breaking of UPy HBs. Discussion

In this work, a 4D-printing strategy was developed for fabricating energy-dissipative LCEs based on a mesogen-functionalized network reinforced with UPy quadruple HBs. At an UPy content of 30 mol %, the printed LCEs reached a tensile strength of 9.4 MPa, corresponding to a 2.4-fold increase over the pristine matrix, which evidences the efficient supramolecular reinforcement. DIW enabled reliable mesogen alignment, while the rapid reformation of UPy HBs provided a transient locking effect during printing, resulting in monodomain architectures with good shape fidelity. The materials exhibited an impact energy-dissipation ratio of 89.24%, arising from the cooperative action of soft elastic director rotation and the dynamic breaking and re-forming of the hydrogen-bonding network. The damping response of the printed lattices was found to be highly anisotropic and rate-dependent and could be tuned by adjusting the printing path (director orientation) and structural geometry (grid density and lattice topology). These results identify UPy-LCEs as a promising platform for vibration and impact mitigation in advanced polymer systems, with potential use in shock-absorbing structural components and soft robotic devices. Future studies will address the long-term fatigue behavior of the supramolecular network and explore multi-material 4D printing for integrating these damping elements into rigid supporting frameworks.

Limitations of the study

The proposed contribution of UPy-based HB to orientation locking and energy dissipation is supported primarily by indirect structure-property evidence, and more direct in situ characterization would be valuable to further verify the mechanism. In addition, only a limited range of formulation, printing, and architectural parameters was examined. The mechanical tests were also performed under selected loading conditions and over a limited testing duration; therefore, long-term fatigue resistance, environmental stability, and behavior under broader strain-rate or repeated-impact conditions still require further study. Moreover, the present demonstrations are restricted to laboratory-scale samples and simplified lattice designs, and future work should assess scalability, reproducibility, and performance in more complex application-relevant structures.

Method details

Materials

1,4-bis-[4-(6-acryloyloxyhexyloxy)benzoyloxy]-2-methylbenzene (RM82), whose rod-like structure is responsible for the nematic phase behavior, was purchased from Jiangsu Synthetic New Materials Co., Ltd, 2-amino-4-hydroxy-6-methylpyrimidine (MIC) and hexamethylene diisocyanate (HDI), used to synthesize the UPy monomer, was acquired from Meryer (Shanghai) Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. 5-amino-1-pentanol was employed to functionalize the LC backbone, providing attachment points for the UPy units. acquired from Pusenruite (Tianjin) Biopharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. Catalysts and Initiators: Dibutyltin dilaurate (DBTDL) catalyzed the hydroxyl–isocyanate reaction, and Irgacure 651 served as the photoinitiator for the final UV curing step, was purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. n-pentylamine was acquired from Shanghai Shaen Chemical Co., Ltd. All chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Synthesis of UPy-NCO

MIC (5 g) was placed in a three-neck round-bottom flask, followed by the addition of 40 mL of HDI. The mixture was stirred at 100 °C for 16 h under a nitrogen atmosphere. The resulting precipitate was washed three times with pentane and isolated via suction filtration. Finally, the obtained white powder was dried in a vacuum oven at 50 °C for 6 h to yield UPy-NCO.

Preparation of UPy-LCE ink

A mixture of 2 g of RM82, 0.130 g of n-pentylamine, and 0.153 g of 5-aminopentanol was weighed into a 20 mL clear glass vial, where the molar ratio of the liquid crystal to amines was 1:1 and the molar ratio of n-pentylamine to 5-aminopentanol was 1:1. The mixture was heated to 80°C until completely dissolved and homogenized, and then kept in an oven at 60 °C for 12 h to obtain the ink precursor. Next, 8 mL of chloroform was added to the precursor to fully dissolve it, followed by the addition of 0.135 g of UPy and 10 μL of the catalyst DBTDL. The mixture was stirred at 65 °C for 12 h. Finally, 0.006 g of the photoinitiator Irgacure 651 was added, and the mixture was stirred thoroughly and then transferred into a high-temperature printing syringe. The syringe was left at room temperature for 12 h to partially evaporate the solvent, and subsequently placed in an oven at 60 °C for another 12 h to remove the remaining solvent (in order to prevent bubble formation during solvent evaporation), affording the UPy-LCE ink.

4D printing process

Install the high-temperature printing cartridge containing printable UPy-LCE ink on the 4D printing equipment, adjust parameters such as printing temperature, printing speed, and printing air pressure, select the diameter of the printing nozzle and the planned printing path, and print the UPy-LCE ink on the glass slide; while printing, place the printed UPy-LCE ink under UV light of 200 mW cm−2 for photopolymerization to prepare UPy-LCE with high orientation. Parameters: Temperature: The barrel temperature was set between 65 °C and 90°C, depending on the UPy content. Higher UPy content increased viscosity, requiring higher temperatures to dissociate the H-bonds for extrusion. Speed: Printing speeds ranged from 4 mm/s to 8 mm/s. Nozzle: A 0.5 mm diameter nozzle was standard. Pressure: Pneumatic pressure (approx. 0.6 MPa) drove the extrusion.

Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC)

UPy-LCE films containing different molar fractions of UPy (0, 10, 20, and 30 mol %) were used for DSC measurements. The thermograms were obtained using an S4800 differential scanning calorimeter (Hitachi, Japan). Film samples (2–10 mg) were sealed in aluminum crucibles and placed on the sample stage of a differential scanning calorimeter under nitrogen protection. The temperature range was set from −20 °C to 120°C with a heating/cooling rate of 10 °C min−1. The samples were first heated to 120 °C and held for 3 min to erase their thermal history. Subsequent cooling and heating cycles were then recorded to investigate the effect of UPy content on the isotropic–nematic transition temperature (TNI) of the UPy-LCE films.

Mechanical properties

UPy-LCE films with different UPy molar fractions (0, 10, 20, and 30 mol %) were mechanically characterized using an electronic universal testing machine in tensile mode. Before testing, the length, thickness, and width of each specimen were measured and input into the instrument, and the tensile speed was fixed at 10 mm s−1. The samples were stretched to failure, and three replicates were performed for each composition to investigate how UPy content influences the mechanical behavior of the printed UPy-LCE films.

Energy-dissipation performance of single-layer UPy-LCE

A transparent acrylic tube with an inner diameter of 5 cm and a length of 50 cm was placed vertically to define the dropping and rebounding trajectory of a glass sphere (diameter 2.5 cm). The glass sphere was released from a height of 50 cm onto a wooden tabletop, as well as onto UPy0.1-LCE, UPy0.2-LCE, UPy0.3-LCE, and UPy0.4-LCE samples, respectively, and the rebound height in each case was recorded. The thickness of a single-layer UPy-LCE film was approximately 0.6 mm. In the notation UPy0.1-LCE, the value 0.1 indicates an UPy molar fraction of 10%.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All quantitative experiments were performed using independently prepared and tested specimens. Data processing, graphing, and quantitative analysis were carried out using OriginPro. The study mainly relies on descriptive statistical analysis to evaluate reproducibility and variation across independently prepared samples, and no formal hypothesis-testing framework was applied unless explicitly stated in the corresponding figure legend. The experiments were conducted on samples grouped according to predefined formulations, printing parameters, and structural architectures. Sample sizes were chosen based on common practice in the field and on the reproducibility of independently fabricated specimens.
