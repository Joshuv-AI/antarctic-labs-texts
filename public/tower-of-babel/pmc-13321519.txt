Meloidogyne vitis infecting mulberry (Morus alba L.) in Yunnan, China: morphology, molecular identification, and pathogenicity
Authors: Yanmei Yang, Xinghua Yu, Dehong Tang, Wendong Xu, Zhengyu Zhang, Pengjun Wang, Shaojie Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMulberry is an economically important sericulture crop; however, infection by root-knot nematodes (RKNs) poses a serious threat to its production. We observed a high density of RKNs parasitizing mulberry in Yunnan, China. To identify the pathogenic species, we characterized the nematode using morphometric analysis, rDNA/mtDNA-based phylogenetics, and SCAR-PCR. Subsequently, we artificially inoculated healthy mulberry seedlings with second-stage juveniles (J2s) to assess their pathogenicity.ResultsThe perineal pattern of females is round to ovoid, featuring a moderately high dorsal arch and two large, prominent phasmids, similar to that of Meloidogyne vitis. The morphological and morphometric traits of females, J2s, and males were consistent with those of M. vitis. Genetic analyses further confirmed this, as the rDNA (ITS1-5.8 S-ITS2) and mtDNA (coxI and coxII) sequences showed > 99% similarity to M. vitis and clustered within the same clade with high support (99%-100%). Moreover, species identity was further confirmed using M. vitis-specific primers Mv-F/R, and a single specific fragment of 174 bp was obtained. Artificial inoculation demonstrated that the RKN isolated from mulberry could complete its life cycle in the roots of healthy seedlings of mulberry, producing typical root-knots and egg masses.ConclusionsThe RKN parasitizing in mulberry was confirmed as M. vitis based on the morphological features and molecular results. This is the first report of M. vitis attacking mulberry. M. vitis is capable of infecting and damaging mulberry, posing a potential threat to mulberry production. The results of this study providing a theoretical basis for accurately identifying and implementing future effective and integrated nematodes management strategy to safeguard mulberry cultivation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08819-w.

Background

Mulberry (Morus spp.), belonging to the genus Morus in the family Moraceae, is an economically important sericulture crop and has prime importance in the sericulture industry as it is the only food for silkworms [1, 2]. The Chinese Ministry of Health classifies it as one of the “food and medicine” agricultural products [3]. Mulberry leaf and fruit contain numerous medicinally active chemicals, which confer various biological functions such as regulating sugar and lipid metabolism, and exerting anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer and anti-hyperlipidemia effects [4, 5]. Mulberry is widely cultivated across Asia, North America, Africa, and Europe, in countries including India, Japan, China, and Korea [6]. In China alone, the mulberry cultivation area spans over 100,000 hectares [7]. Yunnan is one of China’s major provinces for mulberry cultivation, where the industry plays a crucial role in the agricultural sector. However, various pathogens, including root-knot nematodes (RKNs), pose a serious threat to the production of mulberry worldwide.

At present, mulberry RKN disease has been discovered in planting areas at home and abroad. In China, Tian reported that five RKNs species (M. incognita, M. arenaria, M. javanica, M. hapla, M. mali) were found to damage mulberry [8]. Among them, the RKNs have been documented in several Chinese provinces, including Shandong province, Shanxi province, Zhejiang province and Yunnan provinces, mainly were M. incognita and M. arenaria [9–12]. Notably, M. enterolobii has been identified as a pathogen in South China. Specifically, it has been reported in mulberry from Guangzhou City [13] and across multiple provinces such as Guangdong, Guangxi, Hainan, and Yunnan [14]. Globally, the mulberry RKNs disease has also been reported in Japan [15, 16], India [17], Spain [18], Brazil [19], Saudi Arabia [20], and other countries. The associated pathogenic species include M. suginamiensis, M. incognita, M. arenaria, M. hapla, M. mali, M. enterolobii and Meloidodera sp. Plant-parasitic nematodes can diminish global food production by approximately 12.3%, leading to annual economic losses of nearly $173 billion [21]. RKNs rank first among the top ten plant-parasitic nematodes [22] and are one of the greatest threats to agriculture production [23], Mulberry plants infected by RKNs exhibit stunted growth, leaf yellowing, and severe root galling. Both the taproot and lateral roots are damaged, which impairs water and nutrient uptake and leads to significant reductions in productivity. Lei et al.. reported that RKNs infection could reduce mulberry yield by approximately 20–30% and decrease the protein content in leaves, thereby compromising overall leaf quality [24]. Kumari et al.. reported that root knot disease caused by Meloidogyne incognita is severe, leading to a leaf yield loss of 10–12% [25]. In addition to causing direct damage, nematode infection can also predispose plants to fungal, bacterial, and viral diseases. Plant histopathological techniques constitute an essential tool for researching host-pathogen interactions, specifically to elucidate the microscopic manifestations of pathogen colonization and subsequent tissue alterations [26]. Observing the histopathological characteristics of mulberry root tissues damaged by RKN can further clarify its pathogenicity to mulberry plants, providing a reference for further exploration of its pathogenic mechanisms.

Given the severe damage caused by RKNs to mulberry, accurate identification of Meloidogyne species populations and characterization of their pathogenic races are imperative for designing effective control measures within sustainable and integrated pest management frameworks [27, 28]. In Yunnan, we observed severe mulberry RKNs disease, root samples was collected for Meloidogyne species identification, morphological methods (morphological observation and morphometric measurement of females, males, second-stage juveniles and perineal pattern) and molecular biological methods (rDNA-ITS1-5.8 S-ITS2 PCR, mtDNA-coxI PCR, mtDNA-coxII PCR, BLAST alignment and phylogenetic tree construction) were used to identify this RKNs species. Next, the pathogenicity of this nematode was further determined by artificial inoculation. Furthermore, histopathological characteristics of mulberry root tissues damaged by RKN were observed using the paraffin sectioning method. The results of this research will provide a theoretical guidance basis for the prevention and control of mulberry RKNs disease.

Results

Disease symptoms induced by root-knot nematodes

The aboveground part of the nematode-infected mulberry showed poor growth, leaf yellowing and shedding (Fig. 1A-C), while the underground part showed obvious symptoms, with severe swollen root knots. The roots—both taproots and branch roots—were atrophied and distorted. The root knots were uneven in size and long were beaded, measuring approximately 27.57 ± 42.69 mm3 (1.02 mm3 ∼ 193.54 mm3). The surface of the root knots presented milky white or yellowish-brown egg mass, the seriously nematode-infected roots were rotten and had become necrotic (Fig. 1D-F).

Nematodes morphological characters

Morphological characteristics of females, males, and J2s are presented in Table 1, while corresponding morphometric measurements are detailed in Table 2 (females: n = 25; males: n = 15; J2s: n = 26). These results are consistent with the original description of M. vitis [29]. The key identification features: the perineal pattern of female has two large and distinct phasmids (Fig. 2-H, I), with smooth and fine striae that are extremely dense and faint. The posterior part of the body is round, and the anal region has no protuberances. The excretory opening is located behind of the stylet knobs. Male has a distinct head region and the tail is mostly straight with a humped end. J2s possess a distinctly constricted hyaline tail.

PCR product electrophoresis

PCR amplification of the rDNA-ITS1-5.8S-ITS2, mtDNA coxI, and mtDNA coxII region from females isolated from mulberry each yielded only one single, clear amplicon. These amplicons were subsequently purified via gel extraction, cloned, and sequenced, revealing fragment sizes of 877 bp (Fig. 3-A), 413 bp (Fig. 3-B), and 545 bp (Fig. 3-C), respectively, all the sequence lengths are included with primer length.

PCR product sequencing and phylogenetic tree construction

The sequences of rDNA-ITS1-5.8 S-ITS2 of the females isolated from mulberry samples were uploaded to the NCBI database and obtained the GenBank accession numbers PQ452305 and PQ452306. The sequence sizes were 877 bp. BLAST search revealed that the most similar sequence were those of M. vitis (accession numbers MN816222.1, MN816223.1; Query cover 100%; E Value 0; Accession length 877 bp; Percent identity 100%) and M. suginamiensis (MN101822.1, MN101826.1, MN101823.1; Query cover 79%; E Value 0; Accession length 662 bp; Percent identity 100%) (Table S1). Phylogenetic trees (50 sequences in total; Rotylenchus buxophilus, GenBank accession number JX015432.1, was used as the out-group taxon) showed that the sequences from RKNs of mulberry most closely matched M. vitis (MN816222.1, MN816223.1) and M. suginamiensis (MN101822.1, MN101826.1, MN101823.1). These sequences formed a single group with high bootstrap support (100%) and were clearly separated from other species (Fig. 4).

The sequences of mtDNA-coxI were upload to the NCBI database and obtained the GenBank accession numbers PQ451474, PQ451475 and PQ451476. The sequence sizes were 413 bp, a BLAST search revealed that the most similar sequence was that of M. vitis (MN814829.1, MN814830.1; Query cover 100%; E Value 0; Accession length 413 bp; Percent identity 99.76% and 99.52%) and M. ichinohei (KY433448.1; Query cover 99%; E Value 7e-110; Accession length 432 bp; Percent identity 84.88%) (Table S1). These sequences formed a single group with high bootstrap support (99%) and were clearly separated from other species (Fig. 5).

SCAR-PCR analysis

Based on morphological identification and phylogenetic analysis, the RKN isolated from mulberry was preliminarily identified as M. vitis. To further confirm this identity, we used the M. vitis-specific primers Mv-F/R, a single specific fragment of 174 bp was amplified (Lanes 1–6), matching the expected PCR product size for M. vitis (Lane 7) [29]. Pathogenicity detection

After 90 days of inoculation with M. vitis, the mulberry plants exhibited severe damage. The aboveground parts showed yellowing leaves, while the roots were heavily galled. Both the axial roots and branch roots were infected, with the surface of the infected roots presenting numerous milky white or yellowish-brown eggs (Fig. 8A-C). The control mulberries had better growth; their leaves are lush and dark green. The fresh root weight of the control group CK (11.30 ± 1.45 g) was greater than that of the nematode-inoculated group (9.01 ± 1.53 g). The parameters of the inoculation treatment were as follows: Infection rate, Galls/root system, Egg masses/system, Eggs/root system, Root knot index, Egg index, Disease index (a fixed value), Reproduction factor, which were 74% ± 11%, 713.40 ± 154.29, 137.60 ± 35.36, 46412.48 ± 11928.41, 81.54 ± 22.18, 5323.17 ± 1704.41, 82, 18.56 ± 4.77, respectively (Table 3). In contrast, all corresponding values in the control group (CK) were 0. Following acidic fuchsin-staining, nematodes of all ages, including J2s, third stage juveniles, fourth stage juveniles, females and eggs, were observed within the roots (Fig. 8D-H). Dissection of the root knot tissue revealed the presence of milky-white, pear-shaped females (Fig. 8I). Observations of paraffin section showed that there were loosely arranged and significantly damaged cells in the roots of infected mulberry. Giant cells of unequal size were observed, some of which appeared hollowed out and had undergone lysis, resulting in their detachment from the surrounding root tissue. (Fig. 8J, K).

Discussion

Mulberry is an important economic forest species in China, traditionally revered as the “Oriental divine wood”. With the implementation of the national strategy of “moving mulberry from east to west”, the planting area of the mulberry in Yunnan Province has gradually increased, establishing the region as a key sericulture development base in western China [24]. Luliang County is the largest mulberry planting area in Yunnan Province and the occurrence of mulberry RKN disease in this county is more harmful, which significantly constrains the development of its sericulture industry. To date, seven RKNs have been reported to infect mulberry, including M. incognita, M. arenaria, M. javanica, M. hapla, M. enterolobii, M. mali, and M. suginamiensis. Among them, M. incognita is the most common species. In this study, we observed a high‑density RKN parasitizing mulberry, and systematic identification confirmed the pathogen as M. vitis.

M. vitis is a new species of RKN parasitic on grapes, which was identified and reported by our research group in 2021 [29]. Since its initial description, no additional host plants for M. vitis have been reported. In this study, we found that the morphological characteristics (females, perineal pattern, J2s, males) of RKNs parasitizing mulberry were basically consistent with the original reported M. vitis populations, except for minor variations in the morphometric data of males. Its longer gubermaculum length (10.60-19.91 vs. 8.15–14.88 μm), smaller head region height (2.17–4.31 vs. 4.70–5.76 μm) and head region width (5.43–8.25 vs. 8.33–12.32 μm). Ghaderi et al. reported that due to the substantial body size variation in adultRKNs, considerable morphometric differences exist among different populations [30]. Morphology and morphometric of M. javanica from different countries also exist variations [31]. The observed variations in male gubernaculum length and head region height and width compared to the original M. vitis description are likely attributed to individual variability and measurement error. The lengths of the rDNA-ITS1-5.8 S-ITS2, mtDNA coxI, mtDNA coxII, and SCAR region sequences obtained from mulberry-derived RKNs were 877 bp, 413 bp, 545 bp, and 174 bp, respectively, consistent with the original description of M. vitis.

In particular, the phylogenetic trees based on the rDNA-ITS1-5.8 S-ITS2 and mtDNA-coxII regions showed that the RKNs isolated from mulberry, together with M. vitis and M. suginamiensis (sequences from Gu et al. [32]), formed a distinct clade with high bootstrap support (100%), indicating a closer genetic relationship between M. vitis and M. suginamiensis. Therefore, morphological characteristics of M. vitis [29] were compared with the original description of M. suginamiensis [15]. The results are presented in Table S2. The female of M. vitis differs from that of M. suginamiensis in the following aspects: the body is pear-shaped and milky white rather than pearly white, and is sometimes yellow brow; the excretory pore is located on the ventral region between the 23rd and 25th annule behind lips, as opposed to being on the 22-28th annule from anterior end; no obvious lateral lips rather than lateral lips fused to the upper surface of head; the perineal pattern is round to ovoid, with smooth and fine striae, contrasting with slightly squarish outline, wavy striae of M. suginamiensis; the distance between two phasmids of the perineal pattern is wider than or equal to the length of the vulval slit, while it is only slightly wider in M. suginamiensis. The male of M. vitis differs from that of M. suginamiensis in that: the head region lacks an annulus, compared to having one or two annuli; the body slightly bends towards the ventral side after heat-killing, rather than being almost straight and twisted in posterior portion; the labial disc is horizontally ovoid-squared and lateral lips are not obvious, in contrast to the large, lozenge ­ shaped, and clear lateral lips; the lateral field has four incisures forming three bands completely covered with reticular striae, whereas in M. suginamiensis, the outer two bands have reticular patterns and the middle band has discontinuous striae; the tail is mostly straight and short with a humped tip, as opposed to being shorter with a broadly rounded tip. The J2s of M. vitis differs from those of M. suginamiensis in that: the lateral field has four incisures forming three bands fully covered with reticular striae, while in M. suginamiensis, the outer two incisures are slightly crenate and not areolated; the tail tip is conical and constricted, rather than conoid, short, deeply constricted, and rounded-blunt tip; and the hyaline tail is longer (10.31–17.45 μm vs. 3–5 μm). Furthermore, their host ranges differ, peppers are a host for M. suginamiensis but not for M. vitis [33].

Furthermore, M. vitis can be distinguished from M. incognita [34], which is the most common species parasitic on mulberry in that the female: the greater body length 984.72–1322.10 μm (vs. 510–690 μm in M. incognita) and body width 598.95–830.09 μm (vs. 300–430 μm); excretory pore is located in the posterior portion of the stylet knobs, 23rd and 25th annule behind lips (vs. excretory pore at level of, or posterior to, stylet knobs, 10–20 annuli posterior to labial region); perineal pattern with smooth and fine striae (vs. striae closely spaced, very wavy to zigzag); perineal pattern bearing two large, prominent and round phasmids the distance between two phasmids is wider than or equal to the length of the vulval slit (vs. phasmids not prominent and about same distance apart as width of vulva). In males, the lateral field has four incisures forming three bands, all covered with reticular striae (vs. lateral field with four incisures, outer bands areolated, inner band rarely cross-striated except at posterior end); the tail of male mostly straight and short with a humped tip (vs. tail blundly rounded, terminus unstriated). In J2s, the lateral field has four incisures forming 3 lateral bands, all filled with reticular striae (vs. lateral field with four incisures, outer bands cross striated). M. vitis can be distinguished from M. mali [35] by the following characteristics: the female possesses a perineal pattern with a moderately high dorsal arch, as opposed to a low and flat arch, and lacks lateral lines in the lateral fields, which are typically distinct and either single or double in M. mali; both the male and J2s have a shorter dorsal esophageal gland opening (DGO), measuring 2.35–3.91 μm and 1.02–2.01 μm respectively, compared to 6.00–13.00 μm and 4–6 μm in M. mali; the J2s also exhibits a longer tail (47.01–63.77 μm vs. 30–34 μm) and a smaller c value (6.15–8.77 vs. 12–15). M. vitis can be distinguished from several other Meloidogyne species that infect mulberry—such as M. javanica, M. arenaria, M. hapla, and M. enterolobii—by the presence of two large and prominent phasmids in the perineal pattern of females.

RKNs pose a global menace to agricultural crop production [36]. Furthermore, identifying their species is both complex and crucial for understanding host-parasite relationships and implementing appropriate management strategies [37, 38]. Reliable detection and identification technology is necessary for the protection of agricultural production systems and formulating effective prevention and control measures [39]. At the moment, more than 100 genus Meloidogyne have been described [40–42]; however, some nematodes have similar morphology and is difficult to distinguish due to interspecies similarity [43–45]. PCR analysis is a practical method for the routine and rapid diagnosis of RKN species, and has increasingly become the favored approach for identifying unknown samples [46–47]. Molecular markers for RKN identification commonly include DNA-based methods, such as sequences of ribosomal DNA (rDNA-ITS, rDNA-IGS, 28 S rDNA-D2/D3), mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA-coxI, mtDNA-coxII), and sequence-characterized amplified region (SCAR) markers [48–50]. However, some studies have suggested that the sequences of rDNA-ITS, 28 S rDNA-D2/D3, rDNA-18 S of some RKNs are extremely conserved and remains difficult to distinguish [51–54]. rDNA-IGS markers are more widely used in the identification of pathogenic fungi, but they have not yet been validated on a certain number of representative nematode populations and cannot be used in multiplex diagnostics [55]. Moreover, the reference sequence coverage of the IGS region in GenBank is much lower than that of rDNA ITS or mitochondrial DNA coxI and coxII genes, which, to some extent, limits its application in the identification of RKNs. This study also found that the M. vitis has a relatively close genetic relationship with the M. suginamiensis, but there are significant differences in morphology. Therefore, to accurately identify the RKNs species, it is necessary to combine morphological characteristics with molecular biological identification [56–58]. In this research, a combination of field symptom observation, morphological identification, multi-gene molecular techniques (rDNA-ITS, mtDNA-coxI, mtDNA-coxII, and SCAR PCR), phylogenetic analysis, and pathogenicity tests was employed to identify the RKNs parasitizing mulberry. The pathogen was confirmed to be M. vitis, demonstrating the accuracy and reliability of the identification. New hosts of this nematode have been identified again, which is of great significance.

M. vitis was previously found parasitizing grape in 2021, causing serious damage to the crop [29]. To our knowledge, this is the first report of M. vitis attacking mulberry, this finding provides important information for the diagnosis and control of mulberry diseases. Pathogenicity varies among different RKN species, and accurate identification of RKNs is fundamental to research on their biology and pathogenesis [59]. This precision is critical for predicting epidemics and formulating targeted control measures, including the breeding of resistant cultivars with effective resistance genes and the selection of specific biocontrol microorganisms, whose antagonistic effects vary by nematode species. Controlling RKNs in mulberry crops is challenging due to the plant’s perennial nature and the need to prevent chemical residues on leaves, which are used as silkworm feed [60]. To achieve safe, green, and pollution-free mulberry cultivation, RKN management should employ an integrated approach combining biological, manual, agricultural, and physical controls. Chemical control remains an effective approach for managing RKNs in field production. Low-toxicity, high-efficacy nematicides are available, including fluopyram, abamectin, and a fluopyram nanofiber formulation [61]. Biological nematicides, such as Paecilomyces lilacinus [62], Pochonia chlamydosporia and Bacillus firmus [60], can also be used to control RKN disease in mulberry. M. vitis, recently reported as a new species of RKN, currently lacks effective control measures, highlighting an urgent need for further research. Key research priorities include screening for mulberry varieties resistant to this nematode, along with potential chemical pesticides and biocontrol agents. Special attention should be given to developing synergistic combinations of chemical and microbial agents, as well as screening for novel control agents based on genetic targets unique to M. vitis.

Conclusion

RKNs attack a wide array of plant species and cause significant damage, resulting in economic losses. In this study, we found a high density of RKNs parasitizing mulberry in Yunnan, China. To accomplish this, we assayed the morphometric traits and measurements, rDNA and mtDNA-based phylogenetic relationships of this nematode and SCAR-PCR analysis to identify RKNs, the pathogen nematodes were identified as M. vitis. This is the first report of M. vitis attacking mulberry. Its pathogenicity was confirmed by the induction of typical root-knot symptoms and egg mass formation. Since the discovery of M. vitis, new hosts of this nematode have subsequently been identified. The findings of this study are valuable, as they not only expand the known host range of M. vitis—informing its potential distribution—but also provide a theoretical basis for accurately identifying this nematode and formulating effective, integrated management strategies to protect mulberry crops.

Methods

Field sampling and plant material

Mulberry root samples infected with RKNs: Collected from the planting field of mulberry (nü sang) in Xiaojie Street, Zhongshu Subdistrict, Luliang County, eastern Yunnan Province, southwestern China (24˚96’ N, 103˚66’ E), the soil type is red-loam soil. The root samples from RKN-infected plants were collected from six mulberry cultivation fields, in each field, roots from multiple infected mulberry were mixed to form one composite sample, a total of 6 samples were collected, named SS-1, SS-2, SS-3, SS-4, SS-5, SS-6.

Mulberry seedlings (nü sang): cutting seedling, branches of nü sang were collected from RKN-infected fields for cutting propagation. The branches were taken to the laboratory to be cut and rooted before being used for nematode inoculation in pathogenicity tests.

Observation the disease symptom

During field collection of root samples, symptoms on both the aboveground and underground parts of mulberry that were infected with RKNs were carefully observed and photographed. The root systems collected from the six samples were cleaned, gently dried. Those exhibiting typical symptoms were then selected, observed, and photographed under the stereomicroscope (Discovery.V12, Carl Zeiss, Germany). Twenty root segments, each approximately 10 cm in length, were randomly selected, providing a total of 454 root knots. From this population, we randomly sampled 100 knots for sizing using a root phenotype analysis system (GXY-B, Zhejiang Top Cloud-Agri Technology). The measurement data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation, with the range in parentheses (minimum - maximum).

Nematode population isolation and morphological identification

Female and eggs samples were extracted from the root tissues of mulberry, parts of the females were stored in a refrigerator at -80℃ for DNA extraction. Others were photographed under an inverted microscope (Axio Vert. A1, Carl Zeiss, Germany), and the morphologic index were measured following the method described by Yang et al. [29].

Approximately 30 individual egg masses isolated from mulberry were directly extracted and inoculated on the root of cucumber (Jinyan No. 4) for purification and propagation, each cucumber seedling was inoculated with one egg mass. After the successfully inoculated nematodes were identified as M. vitis, they were used for pathogenicity identification. Others were cultured in a constant temperature incubator at 28℃ to collect the J2s. At the same time, the males that attached to the egg masses swam out into the water and were then collected using a micropipette. J2s and males were heat-killed and fixed using 4% formaldehyde solution for morphological observation and measurement.

Perineal patterns of female adults were made following the method of Zhang et al. [63]. The perineal pattern was cleaned with a 45% lactic acid solution, placed on a glass slide in pure glycerin and covered with a coverslip, and then examined under a microscope for observation and photography.

DNA extraction, PCR amplification and sequencing

DNA extraction was conducted in an Eppendorf tube, DNA was extracted from a single female adult following the method described by Yang et al. [64]. Worm lysis buffer (WLB, 100 µL) was prepared: 10×PCR Buffer (Mg2+, free) 20 µL, MgCL2 16 µL, protease K 1 µL, ddH2O 63 µL. The females were pierced with sterilized skewer, added with 5 µL WLB, frozen in liquid nitrogen for 20 min, added with 10 µL paraffin oil, and kept at 56℃ for 80 min and 95℃ for 15 min in a PCR apparatus (T30, Hangzhou Langji).

PCR amplification, rDNA ITS1-5.8 S-ITS2 fragments and two mtDNA fragments (partial coxI and 16 S rRNA coxII) were amplified using the primer pairs 18 S/26S [65], cox1F/cox1R [66] and C2F3/1108 [67], respectively. SCAR-PCR was performed using the primer pairs Mv-F/Mv-R [63]. The primer sequences are listed in (Table 4). All of the polymerase chain reactions (PCRs) were performed in 25.00 µL of mixed solution containing 10× PCR buffer (Mg2་, plus) 2.50 µL, dNTPs 2.00 µL, forward and reverse primers (10 µmol/L) 1.00 µL each, template DNA (DNA from M. vitis was used as the positive control) 2.50 µL, Taq DNA polymerase (5 U/µL) 0.25 µL, and ddH2O 15.75 µL. The PCR amplification procedure is shown in (Table 4). PCR products were then electrophoresed.

Phylogenetic analyses

The sequences obtained from sequencing were submitted to the GenBank database and compared with other nematode sequences using the BLAST program. To analyze the phylogenetic relationships among Meloidogyne species, we used sequences from the rDNA ITS1-5.8S-ITS2 region and the mtDNA genes coxI and coxII for phylogenetic reconstruction, employing the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method in MEGA 5.1. During phylogenetic tree construction, we trimmed the sequence alignments to eliminate poorly informative sites, primarily by removing alignment columns containing extensive missing characters (e.g., ‘N’ or ‘-‘) and trimming gaps at the sequence termini.

Pathogenicity identification

The mulberry materials tested were cutting seedlings, and the cuttings were planted in plastic flower pots (18 cm diameter ×15 cm high) containing a sterilized mixed soil (humus soil/laterite soil/perlite = 3:1:1). After 6 months, M. vitis was artificially inoculated into mulberry root following the method of Velloso et al. [68]. Ten mulberry seedlings were inoculated, three noninoculated mulberries were used as the negative control. Nematode suspension liquid (2500 J2s/mulberry seedling for M. vitis) was pipetted into four holes (4 to 5-cm-deep) around the seedling stem base, the blank control CK was inoculated with the same amount of sterile water. After inoculation, the holes were gently covered with soil, and routine watering procedures were followed thereafter. All plants were cultured in a greenhouse at approximately 14 ℃ to 26 ℃. After 90 days, all plants were observed. After root sample cleaning, the infection rate, galls/root system, egg masses/root system, and root fresh weight/root system were statistically analyzed. Ten egg masses were randomly selected, and the number of eggs in each was counted; the average number of eggs per egg mass was then calculated. Disease grading and calculation of the Gall index (GI), Egg index (EI), reproduction factor (RF), and Disease index (DI) were performed according to the method by Wang et al. [69]. Grade 0: no galls per root system, Grade 1: 1%–20% of the root system exhibits root galls; Grade 2: 21%-40% of the root system exhibits root galls; Grade 3: 41%-60% of the root system exhibits root galls; Grade 4: 61%-80% of the root system exhibits root galls; Grade 5: 81%-100% of the root system exhibits root galls. The indices were calculated as follows: GI= number of galls per plant / root fresh weight per plant; EI = number of eggs per plant / root fresh weight per plant; RF= final egg count / initial inoculum level, DI= ∑(number of infected plants per grade×corresponding grade) /(total number of plants investigated×highest grade)×100. The morphology of nematodes at different growth stages within mulberry roots were observed using the acid fusion staining method followed by Tian et al. [70]. Galled roots of mulberry were collected for histopathological studies using the paraffin section staining method followed by Arun et al. [71].
