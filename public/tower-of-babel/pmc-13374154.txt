Placental mitochondrial DNA mutation-related oxidative stress underlies subsequent risk of adverse prognosis in sFGR twins
Authors: Nana Huang, Yun Zhi, Hai Jiang, Xiaoli Gong, Jing Yang, Xueju Wang, Luyao Li, Pengbo Yuan, Yuan Wei, Yangyu Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelective fetal growth restriction (sFGR), a serious complication of monochorionic diamniotic (MCDA) twin pregnancies, typically results from unequal placental sharing, especially in type II sFGR. This condition is associated with an increased risk of intrauterine death and adverse neurological outcomes in one of the twin fetuses. Mitochondria play an important role in fetal development and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) mutations have been implicated in the pathogenesis of singleton FGR. Mutations in the mtDNA can lead tomitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and apoptosis. However, the role of increased placental mtDNA mutations in the pathogenesis of sFGR and their potential association with fetal growth and nervous system development remain unclear.MethodsIn this study, we characterized and compared the mtDNA mutation rate (MMR) in placentas from eight pairs of type II sFGR twins and eight pairs of uncomplicated MCDA twin controls (MCDA-C). Furthermore, peripheral blood mtDNA from the mothers of both the sFGR and MCDA-C twins was sequenced to determine the differences in mtDNA heteroplasmy between the mothers and twins. The overall MMR and mutation frequencies of individual genes were analyzed separately. Mitochondrial damage, oxidative stress, and apoptosis in the placenta were investigated by immunohistochemistry. In addition, correlations between MMR, birth weight, gestational age, and brain injury were evaluated to investigate the relationship between mitochondrial defects and clinical outcomes.ResultsWhole mitochondrial genome sequencing identified a significantly higher MMR in sFGR placentas than in MCDA-C placentas, especially in respiratory chain genes (MT-ND3, MT-CYB, complex III, and complex V). Maternal peripheral blood MMR did not differ significantly between sFGR and MCDA-C groups. However, analysis of mtDNA heteroplasmy revealed a significantly greater mother-twin heteroplasmy difference in sFGR pairs than in MCDA-C pairs. Immunohistochemical staining showed increased PARKIN-mitochondria colocalization, 8-OHdG and cleaved caspase 3 signals in sFGR placentas, particularly in placentas from growth-restricted smaller twins (sFGR-S), suggesting increased mitochondrial damage, cellular oxidative stress, and apoptosis. In the correlation analysis, the placental MMR was negatively correlated with birth weight and gestational age at delivery. Notably, the incidence of fetal brain injury, especially periventricular leukomalacia (PVL), was higher in sFGR twins and was positively associated with the MMR.ConclusionsPlacental mtDNA mutations and related oxidative stress are associated with adverse intrauterine environments in sFGR twins and may be associated with a higher frequency of fetal brain injury.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09278-9.Keywords: Selective fetal growth restriction, mtDNA mutation rate, Oxidative stress, Placenta, Birth weight, Fetal brain injury

Background

Monochorionic (MC) twin pregnancies are associated with an increased risk of neonatal morbidity and mortality owing to the shared placenta with vascular connections, which can result in various complications [1–3]. Selective fetal growth restriction (sFGR) complicates up to 10–15% of monochorionic diamniotic (MCDA) twin pregnancies and is driven by unequal placental sharing, carrying a significant risk of intrauterine fetal death or adverse neurological outcomes [1, 4, 5]. Several diagnostic criteria have been used to define sFGR, including estimated fetal weight (EFW) below the 10th percentile, fetal weight discordance, and fetal abdominal circumference discordance (≥ 20% or 25% difference) [6, 7]. Clinically, sFGR can be divided into three types based on end-diastolic flow (EDF) [6]. Type II sFGR, characterized by persistently absent or reversed EDF, is associated with a high risk of imminent fetal demise [8]. Due to the unstable nature of umbilical blood flow in sFGR, abnormal hemodynamic changes can occur unpredictably, which may lead to preterm delivery, affecting not only perinatal outcomes but also long-term prognosis.

Mitochondria are highly dynamic intracellular organelles that play crucial roles in energy production and metabolism to support normal fetal development [9, 10]. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), located within the mitochondria, contains 37 genes, of which 13 encode proteins, two encode ribosomal RNA (rRNA), and 22 encode transfer RNA (tRNA). mtDNA is maternally inherited in mammals [11], and it is more vulnerable to damage than nuclear DNA due to the lack of protective histones, the generation of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS), and limited DNA repair mechanisms. The mtDNA mutation rate (MMR) is estimated to be 10–20 times higher than that of nuclear DNA, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired energy production, and excessive ROS generation. Under physiological conditions, mitochondrial ROS act as critical signaling molecules that maintain cellular homeostasis. However, damaged mitochondria tend to produce excessive ROS, which can induce oxidative stress, DNA damage, and ultimately cell death [12–14]. Previous studies have linked primitive mutations in placental mtDNA to compromised fetal development and postnatal health [15, 16]. Mitochondrial dysfunction associated with mtDNA mutations is involved in the pathogenesis of singleton FGR and has been associated with the development of cardiovascular and neurological disorders in adulthood [16, 17]. The pathogenesis of sFGR in MCDA twins shares some common mechanisms with that of singleton FGR, involving multiple pathways. Uneven mitochondrial distribution at the time of embryo splitting may result in variability in mtDNA heteroplasmy between MC twins [18, 19]. However, other studies have shown that adult MC twins have highly similar mtDNA sequences derived from blood [20]. Currently, the role of placental mtDNA mutations in fetal development and in the poor prognosis of sFGR remains unclear.

To address this knowledge gap, the present study employed whole mitochondrial genome sequencing to characterize and compare the MMR in placentas from sFGR affected MCDA twin and uncomplicated MCDA twin controls (MCDA-C). Additionally, we sequenced the peripheral blood mtDNA from mothers of both sFGR and MCDA-C twins to determine the differences in mtDNA heteroplasmy between mothers and their twins. Finally, we assessed the association between MMR, birth weight, gestational age, and brain injury to explore the relationship between mitochondrial defects and clinical outcomes.

Methods

Study participants

This prospective observational pilot study enrolled women with MCDA twin pregnancies who underwent cesarean delivery at our institution between September 2020 and December 2021. A total of 16 MCDA twin pairs were enrolled according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria described below. In this study, the sFGR group refers to the eight pairs of type II sFGR twins (including both the growth-restricted smaller fetus [sFGR-S] and its larger co-twin [sFGR-L]), whereas the MCDA-C group refers to the eight pairs of uncomplicated MCDA twins.This study was approved by the Medical Science Research Ethics Committee of Peking University Third Hospital (IRB00006761-2016145). All patients were fully informed of the purpose and procedures of the study and provided written informed consent. The study complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Type II sFGR was defined as an estimated fetal weight (EFW) of one twin below the 10th percentile and an intertwin growth difference of 25% or more, with the additional criterion of persistently absent or reversed end-diastolic flow in the umbilical artery of the growth-restricted fetus. Participants with chronic conditions such as hypertension and diabetes, severe fetal anatomical malformations, chromosomal abnormalities, or other complicated twin pregnancy conditions, including twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome (TTTS) [21], twin anemia polycythemia sequence (TAPS) [22, 23], or twin reversed arterial perfusion sequence (TRAPS) were excluded from this study [24, 25].

Umbilical artery Doppler abnormalities were diagnosed between 24 and 32 weeks of gestation, with surveillance performed every 7–14 days depending on clinical stability. According to Chinese clinical guidelines [26], delivery is recommended by 32 weeks of gestation for type II sFGR. For uncomplicated MCDA twins, delivery is typically planned at around 37 weeks. An expedited delivery is indicated if fetal heart monitoring reveals signs of fetal distress.

Maternal and fetal clinical characteristics, including maternal age, body mass index (BMI), gestational age at delivery, pregnancy complications, fetal birth weight, gender, 1-minute and 5-minute Apgar scores, and umbilical blood pH, were measured and recorded within 24 h after delivery.

All neonates underwent an initial cranial ultrasound (CUS) assessment within 72 h after birth, followed by a repeat examination at one week of age (± 1 day). The presence of brain injury was defined as abnormal findings on both assessments. In this study, CUS enabled the evaluation of the following findings: intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), periventricular leukomalacia (PVL), abnormal choroid plexus morphology, abnormal corpus callosum, formation of cerebral parenchymal malacia. The specific diagnostic criteria for these ultrasonographic assessments were referenced from the American College of Radiology (ACR), American Institute of Ultrasound in Medicine (AIUM), Society for Pediatric Radiology (SPR), and Society of Radiologists in Ultrasound (SRU) [27]. In accordance with standard clinical protocols, all neonates in the cohort underwent CUS examinations during their hospitalization. All CUS reports were jointly evaluated by a pediatric ultrasonographer and a pediatrician.

Sample collection

Placental tissue under the attachment point of the umbilical cord on the maternal side was collected and washed with 4 ℃ precooled PBS to maximally eliminate blood cells. The maternal decidual tissue was excluded and the chorionic villus layer was retained. As shown in the panoramic view of the MCDA-C placenta (Fig. 1), we collected only the chorionic villus layer because it is a critical placental site for nutrient and oxygen transfer to the fetus. For each twin pair (both the larger and smaller fetus), two pieces of placental tissue were collected: one was snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 ℃, and the other was placed in 10% neutral formalin and fixed for subsequent immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence analyses. Maternal peripheral blood was centrifuged at 1650 rcf (g) for 10 min at 4 ℃ to collect blood cells and stored at -80 ℃. All samples underwent only one freeze-thaw cycle before analysis.

mtDNA copy number estimation

Total DNA was extracted from the collected tissues using a TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (TIANGEN, China, DP304-03) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative RT-PCR was used to measure the mitochondrial DNA copy number relative to the genomic DNA copy number. The following primers were used: mtDNA sequence, mtMinArc-F: CTAAATAGCCCACACGTTCCC; mtMinArc-R: AGAGCTCCCGTGAGTGGTTA; genomic DNA sequence, b2M-F: GCTGGGTAGCTCTAAACAATGTATTCA; b2M-R: CCATGTACTAACAAATGTCTAAAATGGT.

Whole mitochondrial genome sequencing

Placental villus tissue (30 mg) was flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, ground, and filtered to form a cell suspension, and then the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (TIANGEN, China, DP304-03) was used to extract genomic DNA from this cell suspension. The genomic DNA was randomly broken into 150–200 bp pieces using a Bioruptor Pico sonicator (Diagenode, Belgium), and the pieces were end-repaired, A-tails, then ligated by adapters (Invitrogen, USA). The adapter-ligated templates were purified using Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, USA, A63881). The samples were labeled and enriched by PCR amplification. A library with a specific index was hybridized with an RNA probe labeled with streptavidin, and the exons of the target gene were obtained using magnetic beads labeled with streptavidin. Using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen, USA, Q32854) for quantification, a library concentration of > 25 ng/µL was considered a qualified library. For detection using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Agilent DNA 1000 Kit, Agilent, 5067 − 1504), the main peak of the library was approximately 220–320 bp, with no spurious peaks before or after the main peak. An Illumina platform sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, CA) was used to determine the library file length. Raw reads were filtered using Trimmomatic 0.38 to remove low-quality reads. After removing primer sequences, clean reads were mapped to the reference genome GRCh38 using BWA 0.7.12. Single-nucleotide variants (SNV) and insertions/deletions were called and annotated using VarScan v2.4.3.

The obtained data included the frequency of mutations in A/T/G/C bases in mtDNA and mitochondrial DNA-encoded proteins, including mitochondrially encoded NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase core subunits (MT-ND), mitochondrially encoded cytochrome c oxidase (MT-CO), mitochondrially encoded cytochrome B (MT-CYB), and mitochondrially encoded ATP synthase membrane subunit (MT-ATP). Complex I included MT-ND1, MT-ND2, MT-ND3, MT-ND4, MT-ND4L, MT-ND5, and MT-ND6; complex III included MT-CYB; complex IV included MT-CO1, MT-CO2, and MT-CO3; complex V (ATP Synthase) included MT-ATP6 and MT-ATP8; mitochondrially encoded 12 S rRNA (MT-RNR1); mitochondrially encoded 16 S rRNA (MT-RNR2); rRNA, ribosomal RNA; and tRNA, transfer RNA. All tRNA included MT-TA, MT-TR, MT-TD, MT-TC, MT-TE, MT-TQ, MT-TH, MT-TF, MT-TK, MT-TT, MT-TL2, and MT-TS1 (the corresponding amino acids are listed in Supplemental Table S1). In addition, the mitochondrial genome of each fetus was compared with that of the mother. The mtDNA mutations that occurred in all individuals were included to calculate the mtDNA heteroplasmy. The difference in heteroplasmy refers to the difference in the frequency of mtDNA mutations between the fetus and mother.

qRT-PCR

RNA was extracted from the placental villi using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen™, USA, 15596026). qRT-PCR was performed to investigate the expression of mitochondrial complexes III (MT-CYB) and V (MT-ATP6 and MT-ATP8). The following primers were used: MT-CYB-F, ATCACTCGAGACGTAAATTATGGCT; MT-CYB-R, TGAACTAGGTCTGTCCCAATGTATG; MT-ATP6-F, TAGCCATACACAACACTAAAGGACGA; MT-ATP6-R, GGGCATTTTTAATCTTAGAGCGAAA; MT-ATP8-F, TGCCCCAACTAAATACTACCGT; and MT-ATP8-R, AGGATTGTGGGGGCAATGAA.

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue samples embedded in paraffin blocks were cut into 5-µm thick sections and mounted onto slides for immunohistochemistry. Paraffin sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to determine the placental histomorphology. Briefly, slices were placed in boiling AR9 repair solution (EDTA 9.0) for antigen retrieval. Samples were incubated with primary antibodies against 8-hydroxy-2’-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) (1:200 dilution, ab48508, Abcam) and cleaved caspase 3 (1:2000 dilution, 9664 S, Cell Signaling Technology), and then incubated with PV-6000 immunohistochemistry kit (PV-6000, Beijing Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology) sequentially. Freshly prepared color developer DAB working solution was added dropwise, and the nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin.

Opal multiplexed immunofluorescence

To assess mitochondrial damage, antibodies against PARKIN (1:100 dilution, YT3593, Immunoway) and translocase of the outer mitochondrial membrane 20 (TOMM20) (1:300 dilution, ab56783, Abcam) were added to the slides after antigen retrieval. After the removal of the primary antibodies, the opal polymer HRP Ms + Rb (1:100 dilution, NEL811001KT, Akoya Biosciences) was added, and the nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Subsequently, the slides were sealed and images were captured under a fluorescence microscope. The captured images were evaluated using the Image J software with the immunohistochemistry image analysis toolbox plugin version 2 to confirm the staining intensity in all tissue specimens.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 23.0; IBM Co., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Group comparisons: For comparisons between independent groups (sFGR group vs. MCDA-C control group), independent-sample t-tests were used for parametric data, and Mann–Whitney U tests were used for non-parametric data. For comparisons between paired fetuses within the same twin pair (paired-sample t-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used as appropriate. Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables.

Correlation analyses: Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to assess the relationship between MMR and continuous variables with normal distributions (birth weight, gestational age). Spearman’s correlation analysis was used for non-normally distributed variables or ordinal clinical characteristics.

Linear mixed models: To account for the inherent correlation between twins from the same mother, linear mixed models were employed with a random intercept for each twin pair. These models were also used to adjust for potential confounding by gestational age at delivery. This approach was applied to all primary outcome analyses involving within-pair comparisons.

Multiple comparison correction: Given the number of comparisons performed across multiple genes and outcomes, Bonferroni correction was applied to control for type I error. A bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of P < 0.05/10 = 0.005 was considered statistically significant. Corrected significance thresholds are reported in the resultssection where applicable. Findings with uncorrected P < 0.05 but corrected P ≥ 0.05 should be interpreted as exploratory.

Results

Clinical characteristics

Detailed comparisons of the clinical characteristics of the sFGR and MCDA-C groups are presented in Table 1. Unless otherwise specified in this study, the comparisons of continuous variables between the sFGR and MCDA-C groups were performed using the mean values of the two fetuses within each pair to avoid unit-of-analysis issues. There were no significant differences in maternal age (P = 0.089), mode of conception (P = 0.248), gender (P = 0.590), or cord blood pH (P = 0.798) between the sFGR and MCDA-C groups. Pre-delivery BMI (P = 0.036), gestational age at delivery (P = 0.001), fetal birth weight (P < 0.001), 1 min Apgar score (P = 0.003), and 5 min Apgar score (P = 0.008) were significantly lower in the sFGR group than in the controls, whereas the birth weight discordance ratio (P < 0.001) appeared to be significantly higher in the sFGR group.

Accumulation of mtDNA mutations in sFGR placentas

Whole mitochondrial genome sequencing of the collected placentas was performed to assess the MMR in the sFGR group (both sFGR-S and sFGR-L) and MCDA-C groups. We analyzed the frequency of mutations in mtDNA (Supplemental Table S2) and focused specifically on the protein-coding genes of mtDNA (Table 2). Among the 13 protein-coding genes, the greatest increases in variant frequency in the sFGR group were observed in MT-ND2 (P = 0.050), MT-ND3 (P = 0.016), MT-ND6 (P = 0.026), and MT-CYB (P = 0.006). After further differentiation of variation types, the frequency of non-synonymous substitutions increased in the sFGR group for MT-ND3 (P = 0.004) and MT-CYB (P = 0.022) (Table 2).

Mitochondria are the power stations of eukaryotic cells that produce ATP through oxidative phosphorylation. In analyzing the frequency of variants in genes encoding respiratory chain complexes I through IV, we found that there was a significantly higher frequency of variants in complex III (P = 0.006) and complex V (P = 0.009) in the sFGR group compared with the MCDA-C group. Additionally, the frequency of non-synonymous substitutions was increased in the sFGR group for complexes III (P = 0.022) and V (P = 0.014) (Table 2). Furthermore, we used qRT-PCR to investigate the expression levels of mitochondrial complexes III (MT-CYB) and V (MT-ATP6 and MT-ATP8) and found no significant differences between the sFGR and MCDA-C groups (Supplemental Fig. S1).

When comparing the frequencies of variants in the rRNA- and tRNA-coding genes of mtDNA between the groups, we found that the variant frequency in 16 S rRNA (MT-RNR2) was significantly higher in the sFGR group than in the controls (P = 0.014). Twelve of the 22 tRNAs, including MT-TA, MT-TR, MT-TD, MT-TC, MT-TE, MT-TQ, MT-TH, MT-TF, MT-TK, MT-TT, MT-TL2, and MT-TS1, were detected (Supplemental Table S1). However, there was no statistically significant difference in the frequency of variants between the sFGR and MCDA-C groups (Table 2).

Sequencing of mtDNA revealed no significant differences in the frequency of variants between sFGR-S and sFGR-L (Table 3 and Supplemental Table S2). To clarify whether a difference in the mitochondrial mutation levels of mothers during pregnancy, peripheral blood was collected from the mothers. Sequencing of mtDNA revealed no significant differences in mitochondrial mutation levels between the two groups of pregnant women, except for an increase in MT-ND2 non-synonymous mutations in the sFGR group (Supplemental Tables S3 and S4).

Accumulation of mitochondrial damage and oxidative stress in sFGR placentas

Increased MMR may contributes to mitochondrial damage and oxidative stress [28], which can subsequently disrupt cellular homeostasis and even cause cell death. Given the increased MMR in sFGR placentas, we evaluated and compared mitochondrial damage and oxidative stress between sFGR and MCDA-C placentas. In the pathological sections of the placental chorionic villus layer, the placentas from sFGR twins showed a large number of fibrinoid deposits and syncytial trophoblastic nodules, which are signs of placental insufficiency [29] (Fig. 2A). Mitochondrial damage can be assessed by the accumulation of the PARKIN protein on damaged mitochondrial membranes. We labeled sFGR and MCDA-C placentas with PARKIN and TOMM20 and observed higher colocalization of the PARKIN signal with TOMM20, an indicator of mitochondrial depolarization and damage, in the placenta from both sFGR-S and sFGR-L compared with MCDA-C controls (Fig. 3A and B). 8-OHdG, a commonly used biomarker of oxidative DNA damage, was used to investigate oxidative stress. The 8-OHdG signal was significantly higher in the placentas from sFGR-S in comparison to the MCDA-C group, whereas no significant difference was observed between sFGR-L and the MCDA-C group (Fig. 2A and B). Compared with the MCDA-C group, a significant increase in cleaved caspase 3 levels was observed in the placentas from sFGR-S (Fig. 2A and C). A previous study reported significantly higher mtDNA copy numbers in FGR placental tissues, which were negatively correlated with birth weight [30]. We therefore estimated the mtDNA copy number in sFGR and MCDA-C placentas using quantitative PCR. sFGR-S placentas tended to possess higher mtDNA copies. However, no significant differences were observed among the three groups (sFGR-S, sFGR-L, and MCDA-C) (Fig. 3C).

MMR was negatively correlated with fetal birth weight and gestational age

To further explore the association between clinical phenotypes and the total and non-synonymous variants that differed significantly between the sFGR and MCDA-C groups (Table 2 and Supplemental Table S5), we conducted Pearson correlation analyses for ten different genes with birth weight and gestational age at delivery. Table 5 shows that the total variants in MT-CYB (r= -0.491, P = 0.004), complex III (r= -0.519, P = 0.002), complex V (r= -0.463, P = 0.008), and non-synonymous SNV in complex V (r= -0.454, P = 0.001) were negatively correlated with fetal birth weight (Table 5). Furthermore, we analyzed the correlation between gestational age at delivery and MMR. Total mutations in MT-CYB (r= -0.437, P = 0.012), complex III (r= -0.496, P = 0.004), complex V (r= -0.445, P = 0.011), non-synonymous SNV in complex V (r= -0.371, P = 0.036) were negatively correlated with gestational age (Table 5). To adjust for multiple comparisons (10 comparisons), we applied a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of P < 0.005 (P < 0.05/10 = 0.005). After correction, the correlation between birth weight and total variants in MT-CYB and complex III, and the correlation between gestational age and total variants in complex III, remained statistically significant.

MMR was correlated with fetal brain injury

Brain cells, particularly neurons, rely on healthy mitochondria to supply energy and maintain homeostasis. Postnatal brain injury was assessed in newborns with sFGR and MCDA-C using CUS (Supplemental Table S5). The incidences of IVH (P = 0.020), PVL (P < 0.001), and abnormal choroid plexus morphology (P = 0.002) were significantly higher in newborns from the sFGR group than in newborns from the MCDA-C group. Abnormal corpus callosum and formation of cerebral parenchymal malacia were observed only in sFGR-S, but these differences were not statistically significant compared with either the MCDA-C group or sFGR-L. Newborns with sFGR displayed significantly more severe brain injury than newborns with MCDA-C. Whether this association is explained by mtDNA mutations and subsequent energy deficiency warrants further investigation.

In exploring the association between fetal brain injury and MMR, we found that total variants in MT-ND6 (r = 0.477, P = 0.045), MT-CYB (r = 0.481, P = 0.005), complex V (r = 0.538, P = 0.001), and non-synonymous SNV in MT-CYB (r = 0.511, P = 0.003), MT-ND3 (r = 0.614, P = 0.001), and complex III (r = 0.511, P = 0.003) showed moderate to strong positive association (r > 0.4) with the occurrence of PVL (Table 6), which is a critical predictor of long-term adverse neurological outcomes. Additionally, total variants in MT-ND6 (r = 0.534, P = 0.022) were significantly associated with IVH, and total variants in complex V (r = 0.531, P = 0.002) and non-synonymous SNV of MT-ND3 (r = 0.446, P = 0.020) were significantly correlated with abnormal choroid plexus morphology. After Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (accounting for the number of tests performed), the following associations remained statistically significant: total variants in MT-CYB, complex V, and non-synonymous SNV of MT-CYB, MT-ND3, and complex III in PVL, and complex V in abnormal choroid plexus morphology.

Discussion

In this prospective observational study of MCDA twin pregnancies, we found that placentas from type II sFGR twins exhibited significantly higher mtDNA mutation rates (MMR) and mtDNA heteroplasmy compared with the MCDA-C group, and these alterations were associated with increased mitochondrial damage and oxidative stress. Specifically, elevated mutation frequencies were observed in MT-ND3, MT-CYB, complex III, and complex V. These mutational burdens were negatively associated with birth weight and gestational age at delivery, and positively associated with neonatal brain injury, particularly periventricular leukomalacia (PVL). To our knowledge, this is the first study to characterize placental mtDNA mutations in type II sFGR MCDA twins with matched maternal blood samples.

Mitochondrial respiratory chain mutations in sFGR

The oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) system comprises five complexes (I–V) of the electron transport chain and ATP synthase, which are the major processes in ATP generation in respiring cells and contribute significantly to cellular ROS production [31, 32]. Several individual mtDNA mutations observed in sFGR placentas merit closer examination from a mechanistic perspective. After controlling for gestational week variables, using linear mixed models to exclude the interaction effect of two fetuses sharing a placenta, the observed increase in MT-ND3 mutations in sFGR placentas is noteworthy. MT-ND3 encodes a core subunit of complex I. Previous studies have linked MT-ND3 mutations to neurological diseases, such as infant mitochondrial encephalopathy [33] and epilepsy [34]. Whether similar mechanisms operate in the context of sFGR warrants further investigation.

We also observed a higher frequency of MT-CYB mutations in sFGR placentas. Complex III, which contains MT-CYB as a major component, is a primary site of ROS production, and mutations in this complex have been associated with elevated DNA damage [35]. The increased oxidative stress observed in sFGR placentas (as indicated by 8-OHdG levels) is consistent with this finding. According to the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) theory, adverse intrauterine conditions may influence long-term metabolic health [36–38]. Thus, MT-CYB mutations may serve as a potential indicator associated with long-term prognosis in sFGR offspring, pending further validation.

Maternal inheritance and heteroplasmy

Consistent with maternal inheritance of mitochondria, we sequenced mtDNA from maternal blood. Only non-synonymous mutations in MT-ND2 differed significantly between mothers of sFGR twins and mothers of MCDA-C twins. However, sFGR twins showed a higher burden of MT-ND2 non-synonymous mutations than their mothers, suggesting that not all observed mutations were maternally inherited. This finding is preliminary given the small sample size and requires validation in larger cohorts.

Mitochondrial damage, oxidative stress, and mtDNA copy number

The high replication rate of mtDNA, together with the oxidative mitochondrial microenvironment, contributes to elevated mutation frequencies [39–41]. In sFGR placentas, particularly in sFGR-S, the association between increased MMR and markers of mitochondrial damage (PARKIN accumulation) and oxidative stress (8-OHdG) supports the hypothesis that insufficient oxygen and nutrient supply may alter mitochondrial function and homeostasis in the growth-restricted fetus [42]. This oxidative stress state may also upregulate cleaved caspase 3 expression, promoting apoptosis in sFGR-S placentas. In addition, OXPHOS dysfunction, which often results in impaired ROS scavenging, induces an increase in mtDNA levels [43]. Previously, singleton FGR was associated with an increased mtDNA copy number [30], we did not observe a significant increase in mtDNA copy number in sFGR placentas. This discrepancy may be explained by the unique physiology of MCDA twins: through placental vascular anastomoses, the larger placental territory may provide compensatory perfusion to the smaller territory, potentially alleviating hypoxic stress and initiating correction of mtDNA content [43–45].

Clinical correlates of placental MMR: birth weight, gestational age, and brain injury

Placental dysfunction is an important cause of fetal brain injury [46]. In sFGR, the two fetuses share the same genomic composition but display divergent growth rates [44]. After adjustment for multiple comparisons, placental MMR in complex III, MT-CYB were negatively correlated with birth weight and gestational age at delivery. Consistent with the results from the pregnancy cohort, the placental mtDNA mutational burden was related to birth weight and gestation length [47]. Notably, our results suggest that sFGR twins, particularly sFGR-S, show a high proportion of PVL, a condition linked to white matter damage [48]. Previous studies have suggested that white matter disorders are manifestations of mitochondrial diseases [49]. In our study, mutations in complex V, MT-CYB, MT-ND6, as well as non-synonymous SNVs of complex III, MT-CYB, and MT-ND3 were positively associated with fetal PVL. These same genes also showed elevated mutation frequencies in sFGR placentas. Other brain injuries, including IVH and abnormal choroid plexus morphology, were also more frequent in sFGR twins, possibly reflecting ischemia and hypoxia resulting from persistently absent or reversed end-diastolic flow in type II sFGR. Given the concept of the “placenta-brain axis” [38], these associations may offer a mechanistic perspective worth further investigation.

Lack of differences between sFGR-S and sFGR-L

We did not observe a statistically significant difference in MMR between sFGR-S and sFGR-L. As both twins inherit their mtDNA from the same mother [50–52] and develop under identical maternal conditions, mtDNA mutations alone may not explain their discordant growth rates. Environmental factors may also contribute to the onset of growth restriction. Elevated mtDNA mutations in sFGR may be associated with an overall energy metabolic crisis. We hypothesize that the larger placental territory better supports the larger fetus’s growth, whereas the smaller territory restricts the smaller fetus, leading to more severe mitochondrial dysfunction. This interpretation is consistent with studies of mtDNA heteroplasmy in discordant monozygotic twins in other human diseases, such as schizophrenia [52–54], indicating that environmental factors may also play an important role in shaping phenotypic discordance [44, 55]. However, we can not exclude the possibility that the combined effects of multiplex mtDNA heteroplasmy may have deleterious consequences in sFGR [56].

Pathophysiological insights and clinical implications

Accumulated histopathological and MMR changes in the sFGR placenta may provide valuable insights into the oxidative stress to which the placenta is exposed during pregnancy, as well as potential damage that may be of concern in the development of the fetal nervous system. A better understanding of how the placenta responds to intrauterine stressors may provide further insights into DOHaD-based low birth weight, abnormal neurobehavior, and potential avenues for diagnosing and treating sFGR.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size (eight pairs per group) limits statistical power and increases the risk of type I error, particularly given the number of comparisons performed. Although we applied Bonferroni correction (corrected threshold P < 0.005), findings with P values between 0.05 and this threshold should be interpreted as exploratory. Second, the difference in gestational age at delivery between groups may introduce confounding by prematurity; while we adjusted for this using linear mixed models, residual confounding may persist. Third, as an observational study, causal inference is not possible. Statements in this discussion suggesting potential mechanisms are intended to generate hypotheses for future research. Fourth, the single-center design and specific inclusion criteria may limit generalizability. Larger, multi-center, longitudinal studies with greater statistical power are needed to confirm our findings and further disentangle the effects of growth restriction from those of prematurity.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our data support the view that, in the context of an adverse intrauterine environment, mtDNA mutations in sFGR placentas are accompanied by elevated oxidative stress, and these alterations are associated with low birth weight, preterm birth, and an increased risk of fetal brain injury. These findings provide novel insights into the etiology of sFGR, may contribute to a better understanding of the potential underlying mechanisms of fetal brain injury, and could serve as potential indicators for evaluating the prognosis of sFGR twins.
