Eyes on the Road : State-of-the-Art Video Question Answering Models Assessment for Traffic Monitoring Tasks
Joseph Raj Vishal, Divesh Basina, Aarya Choudhary, Bharatesh Chakravarthi

Abstract
Recent advances in the video question answering domain offer promising applications across various fields, particularly in traffic monitoring, where the accurate and efficient interpretation of video data is vital. Within intelligent transportation systems, the ability to answer complex, real-time queries such as “How many red cars passed in the last 10 minutes?” or “Was there an incident between 3:00 PM and 3:05 PM?” can significantly enhance situational awareness and decision-making. Despite notable progress in vision-language models and large language models, video question answering remains a challenging task, especially in complex, dynamic environments involving multiple objects and intricate spatiotemporal relationships. This study presents a comprehensive evaluation of state-of-the-art video question answering models using non-benchmark video sequences comprising both synthetic and real-world traffic sequences. The evaluation framework leverages GPT- 4 ​ o 4o as an objective evaluator to assess model performance based on accuracy, relevance, and consistency across three categories: basic detection, temporal reasoning, and decomposition queries. Among the models evaluated, VideoLLaMA- 2 2 stood out, achieving an average accuracy of 57%, with particular strengths in compositional reasoning and answer consistency across related queries. However, all models, including VideoLLaMA- 2 2 , exhibited notable limitations in multi-object tracking, temporal coherence, and complex scene interpretation, underscoring critical gaps in current video question answering architectures. These findings highlight the potential of video question answering for traffic monitoring while emphasizing the need for further advancements. Future improvements in multi-object tracking, refined temporal reasoning, and enhanced compositional capabilities are essential for developing robust models capable of addressing the demands of real-world traffic management. By bridging these gaps, video question answering models could become indispensable tools in intelligent traffic systems, enabling faster incident detection, improved traffic flow management, and more responsive urban planning. The code and evaluation framework from this study are open-sourced for further research and exploration: https://github.com/joe-rabbit/VideoQA_Pilot_Study

3.3
Questions Selection Criteria

We initially had a pool of GPT-
4o
-generated questions that aligned with human reasoning patterns. However, to ensure consistency, relevance, and alignment with the study’s objectives, the questions were carefully filtered and selected based on a set of defined criteria
[
6
]
. These criteria were designed to extract meaningful insights from the VideoQA models while testing various aspects of their reasoning and observational capabilities. The selected criteria are as follows:

1.

Appropriate question types
:
The question types were deliberately chosen to include only those that could be answered using observable data, such as yes/no, how, where, and what questions. “Why” questions were excluded, as they typically require inferential reasoning or external knowledge beyond the scope of direct video analysis. This ensured that all questions could be answered based solely on the visual and temporal information available in the video. For instance, questions like “Where is the pedestrian?” or “What color is the car?” were included, while “Why is the person crossing the street?” were excluded.

2.

Relevance to video content
:
Each question was crafted to directly relate to the events, objects, and interactions shown in the video. This approach ensured that the questions were relevant and contextually anchored in the visual content, maintaining focus on the specific elements depicted. Irrelevant questions were avoided to ensure clarity and prevent confusion for the model. For example, in a traffic video, questions would address vehicles, pedestrians, or actions within the scene, rather than unrelated topics.

3.

Quantitative questions
:
These were included to assess the model’s ability to count objects or actions within the video. These questions required the model to track and enumerate visible elements, testing its spatial awareness and attention to detail. For example, questions like “How many cars are visible?” or “How many pedestrians cross the intersection?” evaluate the model’s capacity to monitor multiple entities and provide accurate counts, a crucial skill for interpreting complex scenes.

4.

Implied temporal data
:
Instead of explicitly requesting timestamps, questions were designed to imply temporal understanding. This approach evaluated the model’s ability to interpret events or actions in a sequential context without referring to specific timeframes. For example, questions like “Does the cyclist pass the pedestrian before the car arrives?” test the model’s ability to infer temporal relationships, focusing on relative timing rather than precise timestamps.

5.

Single-part focus
:
To ensure clarity and simplicity, questions were designed to focus on a single aspect of the video. This approach avoided multi-part questions that could create ambiguity or complicate interpretation. By isolating one element at a time, the questions enabled more accurate assessments of the model’s ability to focus on specific observations. For example, instead of asking, “What vehicles and pedestrians are present, and what are they doing?” the questions were separated into “What vehicles are present?” or “What are the pedestrians doing?”

To ensure the effectiveness of these criteria, each question was carefully reviewed for clarity, relevance, and alignment with the study’s objectives. By adhering to these guidelines, we aimed to create a well-defined set of questions that would provide meaningful insights into the capabilities of the VideoQA models, testing their ability to understand and reason about complex, real-world traffic scenarios. The selected questions were then used to evaluate the models across various visual perception, temporal reasoning, and contextual understanding aspects.

Despite its advantages,

However, given its strengths in multimodal integration and extended context comprehension, LLaVA-Next-Video was selected as a benchmark model for this study, suited to VideoQA applications that demand a thorough understanding of video event sequences and interaction patterns.

5.1
Language Models as Evaluators, Compositional Reasoning, and Consistency Metrics

Marcu et al.
[
5
]
, introduces the use of LLMs for evaluating VideoQA, and Liao et al.
[
6
]
, focuses on compositional reasoning through consistency metrics. These approaches combine to form a structured assessment framework.
Marcu et al.
[
5
]
proposed using language models like Lingo-Judge as evaluators to score responses based on criteria such as relevance, accuracy, and consistency. The method aligned model-generated answers with ground truth, showing that language models can objectively evaluate VideoQA performance without direct access to visual content. In this framework, GPT-
4o
serves as the primary evaluator, assessing responses based on these criteria.
Liao et al.

[
6
]
introduced the Video Alignment and Answer Aggregation (VA
3
) framework, which evaluates compositional reasoning using consistency metrics. These metrics measure how well models handle related questions within the same context. This framework integrates the cP metric into GPT-
4o
’s evaluation process to assess the consistency of model responses across similar queries.
By combining quality evaluation and consistency analysis, this framework provides a comprehensive assessment of model performance across various question types and levels of complexity.

5.2
GPT-
4o
as a Baseline Evaluator for Traffic VideoQA

GPT-
4o
was prompted to act as an evaluator by assessing the accuracy of model-generated responses against predefined ground truth answers. To ensure impartiality, GPT-
4o
was not provided with the actual video footage but only with the ground truth and the responses of the VideoQA models. This approach focused on semantic accuracy, capturing whether the model responses conveyed the same meaning as the ground truth.
A score of
1
was assigned if the response was semantically consistent with the ground truth, and
0
otherwise. This scoring method ensured that the evaluation remained objective and focused solely on aligning the ground truth and the model’s output (refer Figure
6
). By leveraging GPT-
4o
’s strong natural language understanding, the evaluation effectively measured the semantic correctness of responses, which is crucial for the reliability of VideoQA models in traffic analysis scenarios.

5.2.1
Prompt Design for Evaluation

An example prompt provided for GPT is as follows:

Create a table to compare the performance of VideoQA models on three different question sets related to traffic monitoring conditions. For each question set:

1.

Provide the
ground truth
for the question.

2.

List the
model responses
.

3.

Indicate whether the responses were
correct
(score as 1 for semantically correct and 0 for completely wrong; no partial grading).

4.

Calculate the
accuracy percentage
for the models as:

\text{Accuracy (\%)}=\frac{\text{Number of correct responses}}{\text{Total number of responses}}\times 100

5.

Calculate the
consistency precision
(
c_{P}
) for the models as:

c_{P}=\frac{\text{Total number of questions answered correctly with all questions in the category also correct}}{\text{Total number of questions with all questions answered correctly}}

The prompt outlines the task of comparing the performance of VideoQA models on three sets of questions related to traffic monitoring scenarios. For each question set, the evaluation involves providing the ground truth and the responses from the models. The responses were scored with no partial grading. The evaluation requires calculating two metrics: the
accuracy percentage
, which measures the proportion of correct responses out of the total responses, and the
cP
, which assesses how consistently a model answers all questions correctly within a category. The goal is to analyze the semantic alignment between the model responses and the ground truth, enabling a standardized and objective performance comparison of the VideoQA models in the context of traffic analysis.

Conclusion

This study presents a detailed evaluation of the SOTA VideoQA models, including GPT-
4o
, LLaVA-NeXT-Video-7B-hf, Gemini Pro
1.5
, Intern-VL, and VideoLLaMA-
2
, focusing on their ability to handle varying levels of question complexity across diverse traffic video scenarios. VideoQA, as a multimodal task, demands the integration of spatial and temporal analysis, object tracking, and compositional reasoning, posing significant challenges for AI models. To address these, this study implemented an evaluation framework, using GPT-
4o
as an impartial evaluator.

The results demonstrate that VideoLLaMA-
2
stands out among the evaluated models, achieving an average accuracy of 57%. It excelled in compositional reasoning and consistency across question types, especially in handling complex questions that required in-depth understanding of spatial-temporal relationships. GPT-
4o
and Gemini Pro
1.5
, both scored 53% and 51% respectively following closely, showing strengths in handling simpler and moderately challenging questions but facing limitations in complex reasoning tasks. Intern-VL, with a score of 46%, showed moderate performance but struggled with multi-object detection. LLaVA-NeXT-Video-7B-hf, with the lowest score of 40%, struggled particularly with quantitative tasks and complex interaction analysis, indicating room for substantial improvement. The key takeaways out of the experiments conducted in this study are:

•

Strengths of the top models
: VideoLLaMA-
2
’s performance underscores the importance of architectures capable of compositional and counterfactual reasoning, which are critical for complex VideoQA tasks. The robustness of the model in handling various types of questions indicates its adaptability to real-world VideoQA applications, where the complexity of queries can vary significantly.

•

Areas for improvement
: Despite their strengths, even the top models showed limitations in tasks requiring high accuracy in multi-object detection and complex temporal reasoning. Improvements in object-tracking mechanisms, temporal alignment, and consistency in multi-object detection would enhance performance in complex VideoQA scenarios.

•

Implications of using GPT-
4o
as an evaluator
: Leveraging GPT-
4o
as a judge provided a consistent and objective framework for scoring responses across varied question types, particularly useful in a domain as complex as traffic monitoring. However, future studies could refine this evaluation by incorporating specialized checks for nuanced traffic-related queries, such as identifying incidents or tracking vehicle trajectories under varying weather and lighting conditions.

The insights from this study suggest that VideoQA models, with further development, could become powerful tools in intelligent transportation systems. The ability to automatically answer questions like
“How many vehicles ran a red light?”
or
“Was there a collision between 3:00 PM and 3:05 PM?”
would enhance traffic management, improve response times to incidents, and enable more efficient urban planning. However, current model limitations in multi-object tracking and temporal reasoning must be addressed to fully realize these benefits.
In conclusion, this study contributes to the VideoQA field by combining objective evaluation with compositional metrics, identifying VideoLLaMA-2 as the most effective model while highlighting broader challenges. Future research should prioritize refining multi-object tracking and temporal reasoning to advance the applicability of VideoQA in real-world traffic monitoring scenarios, paving the way for smarter, more responsive transportation systems.
