The genetic landscape of northeastern Iberian communities from the early to late Iron Age
Authors: Daniel R Cuesta-Aguirre, Xavier Roca-Rada, Beatrice Di Biase, Diana C Vinueza-Espinosa, Carolina Sandoval-Ávila, Shyamsundar Ravishankar, Nuria Molist Capella, Montserrat Duran i Caixal, Imma Mestres Santacreu, Emili Junyent, Natalia Alonso, Jordi Martinez Majoral, Anna Kjær Knudsen, Liam T Lanigan, Hannes Schroeder, Bastien Llamas, Assumpció Malgosa, Cristina Santos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDuring the Iron Age (800–100 BCE), northeastern Iberia was shaped by interactions among local communities and Mediterranean societies. Iberian cultural traditions developed along the coast from the sixth century BCE and persisted through major social transformations leading into the Roman period. We analyzed ancient genomic data from 54 newborn burials recovered beneath domestic structures at three sites spanning the early Iron Age to the early Roman period (775 BCE–50 CE). Genome-wide data were obtained for 22 individuals, with additional mitochondrial haplogroups for nine more. The results indicate genetic continuity from Bronze Age-derived local populations through the Iron Age, accompanied by increasing Mediterranean-related ancestry. This population structure remained largely stable until Roman expansion, after which additional genetic inputs contributed to a more heterogeneous population. These findings link long-term cultural continuity with gradual genetic change and illuminate population dynamics during a key period of social and political transition in northeastern Iberia.Subject areas: Human geography, Genetics, Human genetics, Paleogenetics, Archeology

Introduction

The Iberian Peninsula, like many other territories worldwide, is characterized by its continual integration of diverse populations. Whether through historical conflicts and conquests or peaceful economic transitions, a complex cultural mosaic has been woven into this southwestern part of the Eurasian continent.1,2 By exploring the genetic landscape of this region, recent studies have elucidated the ancient populations that inhabited different parts of the peninsula.

The Iberian Peninsula served as a refugium during the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM, 26,000–19,000 years ago), with evidence of pre-LGM genetic ancestry persisting in post-LGM populations.3,4 In the Mesolithic, northern hunter-gatherers from the Iberian Peninsula exhibited stronger genetic influence from Central European Western Hunter-Gatherers (WHG) compared to those in the south of the Peninsula, reflecting dynamic genetic shifts.2 In the Early Neolithic, Anatolian farmers expanded into Europe, leading to a period of coexistence with local hunter-gatherers. This interaction resulted in higher Anatolian Neolithic ancestry in Early Neolithic populations, which gradually diluted in favor of WHG ancestry by the end of the Neolithic and the beginning of the Chalcolithic.3,5,6,7,8,9 This effect also took place in the Iberian Peninsula.2,10,11 The Chalcolithic in the Iberian Peninsula was characterized by continuity from the Neolithic.1,2,12

During the Bronze Age, different groups carrying Pontic-Caspian Steppe ancestry spread westward throughout Europe. In the Iberian Peninsula, the Early Bronze Age (2200–1500 BCE) was marked by the introduction of Steppe-related ancestry, although its influence varied regionally. Steppe-related ancestry was more prominent in the northern and northeastern regions of the Iberian Peninsula, while southern regions exhibited less impact, suggesting a gradual and uneven genetic transition during this period. This period also saw changes in Y chromosome lineages, with R1b replacing I2, G2, and H as the dominant lineage, although these earlier lineages persisted at lower frequencies.1,2,13,14

Throughout the Iron Age (Figure 1), diverse cultures coexisted in the Iberian Peninsula (800–100 BCE). Indigenous groups included different Iberian tribes (described in more detail afterward), Tartessians, and Celtic-influenced groups like the Celtiberians. There were also Celtic and Indo-European (pre-Celtic) groups such as the Lusitanians, along with other regional groups including the Gallaeci, Astures, Cantabri, Vascones, Vaccaei, Vettones, Turduli, and Turdetanians. It is important to note that the boundaries of these groups in terms of time and location were fluid, and cultural blending was common. Additionally, according to the archaeological record, the establishment of Mediterranean colonies in the Iberian Peninsula led to the southeast becoming a center of Phoenician culture, while the northeast saw the formation of Greek colonies.15

Main population movements around the Mediterranean sea during the Iron Age (750–250 BCE) that affected the Iberian Peninsula

During the 3rd century BCE, the Carthaginian empire (also known as Punics) and the Roman Republic emerged as dominant powers in the western Mediterranean. The Carthaginians, who descended from the Phoenicians, established a vast maritime empire, while Rome became the leader and protector of the Italian Peninsula. Their expansionist ambitions led to the Punic Wars (264–146 BCE). The Second Punic War (218–202 BCE) was particularly significant in the Iberian Peninsula, culminating in the Carthaginians’ expulsion from the territory in 206 BCE. The Romans established control over the region and incorporated it into their Empire to prevent potential resurgence. The local population became increasingly Romanized in the process, gradually adopting Roman customs and culture.16

During the Iron Age, the term “Iberians” was used by Greeks and Romans to refer to the communities inhabiting the eastern and southern Mediterranean coasts of the Iberian Peninsula. This designation includes a variety of independent tribes, such as the Layetani, Indiketes, Cessetani, Lacetani, Ilercavones, Ilergetae, and Ausetani, among others, each with its governance structures and customs that, while sharing similarities, were not identical.17 The Iberian culture, which flourished during the Iron Age around the 6th century BCE, reflects the rich history of the Iberian Peninsula, with significant influences from other Mediterranean civilizations such as Phoenicians, Greeks, and Carthaginians.18,19

The Iberians demonstrated significant advancement, with an economy based on metallurgy, agriculture, livestock, and, in coastal regions, fishing. They developed their own alphabet and currency system. In terms of religious practices, the Iberians adored a pantheon of deities, sharing several elements with other Mediterranean regions. Cremation was their main funerary rite.20 However, intramural newborn burials in houses and productive areas have been documented,21,22,23 providing an important source of information for anthropological and paleogenomic studies. While this practice was not exclusive to Iberians, as they have been documented globally from prehistoric to medieval times,24,25,26,27,28,29,30,31 a debate over the ritual significance of infant burials has emerged.32,33,34 However, recent evidence suggests that the age profile of newborns aligns well with natural mortality causes.35

The first paleogenetic research on Iberian culture groups focused on studying mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), specifically the hypervariable region I. These early studies aimed to identify the prevalent mitochondrial haplogroups among Iberians in comparison to ancient individuals from different periods (such as the Chalcolithic, Bronze Age, and Roman era)36 and cultural groups, including the Basques and Etruscans.37 These studies, which analyzed the remains of 27 adults from nine archaeological sites, often involved bone fragments found in regions historically occupied by Iberians, but sometimes with questionable Iberian origin. The results revealed that the haplogroup composition of ancient Iberians was remarkably similar to that of present-day Iberian Peninsula populations (with less variability), suggesting long-term genetic continuity in the region since pre-Roman times.37 Moreover, they did not find a close genetic relationship between the Iberians and the Etruscans, indicating considerable genetic heterogeneity in pre-Roman western Europe.37 The main haplogroup in those populations was H, with lineages of J, T, K, U4, U5, and W also present.36,37 Additionally, they found an unusually high frequency of haplogroup V, considered a marker of Paleolithic populations in Europe.36 Advancements in genetic research methods, particularly the adoption of next generation sequencing for paleogenomic studies, have provided more detailed insights into both mitochondrial and nuclear DNA obtained from the remains of 15 individuals (three newborns from Valencia and 12 adults) from seven Iberian archaeological sites,2 finding that Iron Age individuals from the Iberian Peninsula had increased ancestry related to northern and Central European populations compared to Bronze Age ancestry.2

Currently, no systematic paleogenomic study has focused on Iberians that span from the early Iron Age to the Roman conquest period, mostly due to widespread cremation practices during the Iron Age,38 resulting in an absence of skeletal remains suitable for ancient DNA analysis. To explore the genetic diversity of Iberian populations, we analyzed newborn burials from three sites located in the northeast of the Iberian Peninsula associated with Iberian culture (Figure 2A): the fortress of Vilars (VILA), Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola (OLE), and El Camp de les Lloses (CL) (detailed information in Document S1, Methods S1–S3). The fortress of Vilars, located in the territory of the Ilergetae tribe and occupied from the 8th to the 3rd century BCE, represents a long-lived settlement that allows the study of the transition from the Early to the Middle Iron Age (Figures S1–S4), in other words, from pre-Iberian to Iberian culture.39 Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola, located in the territory of the Cessetani tribe and inhabited by Iberians between the 4th and 2nd centuries BCE (Figures S5–S8), offers insights into an established Iberian community, with evidence of craft specialization and trade networks.21 Finally, El Camp de les Lloses, situated in the territory of the Ausetani tribe and dated from the late Iron Age (2nd century BCE) into the 1st century CE (Figure S9), reflects the increasing influence of Rome in the region, working as a specialized logistical and industrial center linked to Roman-republican military infrastructure.40 Together, these three sites provide a diachronic framework for investigating how genetic profiles evolved in northeastern Iberia across key phases of the Iron Age transition, from independent local communities to occupation shaped by growing Mediterranean and Roman interactions. Our study aims to provide a paleogenomic characterization of the three Iberian sites to evaluate the genetic evolution of the local population over time, from the Early Iron Age to the Roman Conquest period, providing insights into genetic ancestry, admixture, and demographic dynamics in the northeastern region of the Iberian Peninsula.

Results

Genome-wide data across the iron age in Iberian culture

Thirty-six newborns out of fifty-four reached the necessary amount of extracted DNA for generating libraries (Figure 2B). Eighty libraries were prepared using different methodologies (Table S2). Twelve libraries were excluded because they presented less than 10% of damage in the first 5′ position (Table S2A). Moreover, libraries that presented fewer than 100 reads for the mtDNA were also removed, except for the libraries from VILA01, VILA02, VILA03, and VILA08, because merging those libraries (from a single individual) could facilitate mtDNA haplogroup assignment. A total of eleven libraries were removed due to the low number of mtDNA recovered reads. So, fifty-seven successful libraries were obtained for thirty-two out of the thirty-six sequenced individuals (Table S2A). Thirteen individuals (OLE03, OLE07, VILA01, VILA02, VILA03, VILA04, VILA07, VILA08, VILA10, VILA11, VILA16, VILA19 and VILA21) exhibited fewer than 20,000 autosomal single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from the 1240k panel9 and were removed from population analyses, except for OLE03 (19,084 autosomal SNPs), VILA04 (19,381 autosomal SNPs) and VILA07 (18,610 autosomal SNPs), which presented almost 20,000 autosomal SNPs from the 1240k panel9 and was included in some analyses. Eight individuals (VILA12, OLE01, OLE04, OLE06, CL1, CL2, CL3, and CL6) presented between 20,000 and 50,000 autosomal SNPs from the 1240k panel.9 Seven individuals (VILA13, VILA14, CL12, CL7, CL8, CL9, and CL4) presented between 50,000 and 100,000 autosomal SNPs from the 1240k panel.9 Finally, four individuals (OLE08, VILA05, VILA15, and VILA18) had more than 100,000 autosomal SNPs from the 1240k panel.9 For the principal-component analysis (PCA), ADMIXTURE, and individual qpAdm analyses, the twenty-two individuals that presented more than around 20,000 autosomal SNPs from the 1240k panel9 were used. However, only the eleven individuals with over 50,000 autosomal SNPs from the 1240k panel9 were included for the f-statistic and qpAdm based on population analyses.

Genetic sex was estimated for 32 individuals (Table S1). Around 56% of the individuals (18 out of 32) were female. The highest female frequency was observed in Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola (0.83; five out of six individuals) and El Camp de les Lloses (0.67; six out of nine individuals), compared to the fortress of Vilars, which had a frequency of 0.41 (seven out of 17 individuals).

Mitochondrial haplogroups were called for 31 individuals (Table S4). Haplogroup H was the most common, making up 32.26% of the individuals (H1, H2, H3, and H4), followed by haplogroup K at 22.58% (K1a and K1b) and haplogroup U at 16.13% (U4, U5a, and U5b). The most frequent sub-haplogroup was K1 (K1a3a at the fortress of Vilars, K1a + 195 at both the fortress of Vilars and Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola, and K1b1a at El Camp de les Lloses). Significant diversity was observed across all sites, with various sublineages (H1, H3, H4, HV0, T2, K1, U4, U5a, U5b, N1, and M1) present in the Iberian culture. At the fortress of Vilars, individuals VILA01 and VILA07 shared the same haplotype. Additionally, individuals VILA16, VILA18, and VILA21, which were buried in the same burial, also shared the same haplotype. At the El Camp de les Lloses, CL6 and CL7 (H1at1), as well as CL9 and CL12 (H3 + 152), shared the same haplotypes, respectively. In contrast, no shared haplotypes were detected at Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola.

The Y chromosome haplogroups were successfully determined in ten out of 16 males (Table S5). None of the Y chromosome haplogroups from individuals at Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola could be determined. While most individuals belonged to the R1b haplogroup, VILA14 was classified under the H2a1 haplogroup. Given the sequencing resolution, it was not assess whether the R1b lineages were identical across individuals.

The analyses conducted at the fortress of Vilars and Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola did not reveal any first- or second-degree relationships for the analyzed individuals (Table S6). However, at El Camp de les Lloses, a first-degree relationship was identified between CL6 and CL7, while second-degree relationships were observed in pairs CL1-CL6, CL2-CL6, and CL2-CL9.

The multidimensional scaling plot of 1-f3(Ind1, Ind2; Mbuti) indicated no clustering based on chronology or sex when all Iberian populations from the northeast of the Iberian Peninsula were analyzed together (Figure S10). In each archaeological site (Figures S11–S13), some clustering along dimensions 1 and 2 could be observed, but again it was not associated with sex, chronology, or disposition in the site.

Genetic continuity between the Bronze Age and the early/middle iron age in Iberians (775–200 BCE)

To investigate the genetic profile of the Iberians, we merged our dataset with a collection of ancient Mediterranean individuals (see Table S3 for labels). These individuals were projected onto the first principal components (PC1 and PC2) of a PCA based on present-day populations from Europe, North Africa, the Caucasus, and southwestern Asia. After filtering, 616,427 autosomal SNPs from the 1240k panel9 remained for analysis.

Most individuals from the fortress of Vilars and Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola cluster closely with other Iron Age individuals. However, a few individuals exhibit a slight genetic shift toward present-day populations from the Caucasus, southwestern Asia, and North Africa (Figures 3 and S14). Almost all Iberians analyzed here fall within the genetic variation established by prehistoric Iberian Peninsula individuals from the Neolithic to the Iron Age. Among the analyzed individuals from those sites, VILA15 and VILA13 are the only individuals with more than 50,000 autosomal SNPs clustering closely within the cluster of local Iron Age individuals from the Iberian Peninsula, despite VILA18 being close to them (Figure 3). Other individuals that fall within the broader Iron Age genetic cluster, but have between 20,000 and 50,000 autosomal SNPs, are VILA07, VILA13, and OLE03 (Figure 3). The replicas from the supervised ADMIXTURE analysis presented an average pairwise similarity of 0.997, according to Pong v1.5.41 The Iberian newborns with more than 50,000 autosomal SNPs from the fortress of Vilars and Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola presented a profile composed of WHG, Anatolian Neolithic, and Yamnaya Bronze Age ancestries (Figure 4), very similar to other Iron Age or late Bronze Age samples, with an increased Steppe ancestry compared to Bronze Age population from the Iberian Peninsula (Figure S15).

To further assess these PCA- and ADMIXTURE-based observations, we performed outgroup f3 statistics of the form f3(Iberian Site, test; Mbuti) using individuals with more than 50,000 autosomal SNPs (Figure S16 and Table S7). This analysis was used to identify the closest genetic matches for the Iberians from the present study. Our results indicated a higher shared genetic drift with Bronze Age and Iron Age populations from the Iberian Peninsula, including the Celts, and the Iron Age individuals, Greeks-Hellenistics, and local Romans from the northeast of the Iberian Peninsula (Figure S16 and Table S7). In contrast, lower genetic affinity was observed with Roman populations from the territory of present-day Portugal, late Punics and Romans from the south of the Iberian Peninsula, and admixed Romans from the northeast of the Iberian Peninsula (Figure S16 and Table S7). These results support genetic continuity from the Bronze Age to the Iron Age in the northeast of the Iberian Peninsula.

To explore genetic relationships and potential admixture events, we applied f4 statistics of the form f4(Mbuti; Iberian Site; test, reference). Positive significant values indicate that the Iberian Site population shares more genetic similarity with the reference population than with the test population. Mbuti was selected as the outgroup. The individuals from the fortress of Vilars and Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola were analyzed together, and it was observed that they were more similar to other Bronze and Iron Age populations from the Iberian Peninsula than to other Mediterranean populations (Figures S17 and S18, and Table S8).

To test whether Iberians from the northeast of the Iberian Peninsula showed genetic continuity with earlier regional populations, we employed qpAdm to model their ancestry both as a single population and as a mixture of earlier populations. Individuals from the fortress of Vilars and Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola were merged for these analyses. Proximal models using Bronze Age populations from the northeast and southeast of the Iberian Peninsula yielded p values below 0.05, indicating poor model fit (Table S9). We then combined these Bronze Age populations with various Mediterranean populations, including Paleolithic Moroccans (Iberomaurusians), Neolithic Moroccans, Phoenicians (Israel), and Punics (Sardinia and Sicily). Any model reached significance (Table S9). In contrast, when northeast and southeast Iberian Bronze Age populations were combined with Bronze Age Yamnaya individuals from the Pontic-Caspian Steppe, the models fitted: northeast Iberian Bronze Age (0.793) + Yamnaya (0.207) had a p value of 0.143, while southeast Iberian Bronze Age (0.778) + Yamnaya (0.222) had a p value of 0.07 (Figure 5 and Table S9).

Notably, qpAdm models for individuals from the fortress of Vilars indicated that Early Iron Age newborns (775–675 BCE), such as VILA04, VILA05, VILA07, VILA12, and VILA18, could be adequately modeled using only local Bronze Age Iberian ancestries (Table S10). In contrast, later individuals, such as VILA15 (550-450 BCE) and VILA14 (450–325 BCE), could not be explained solely by Bronze Age Iberian ancestry and required additional contributions from Yamnaya or other Mediterranean sources (Table S10).

Genetic interaction between Iberians and other Mediterranean cultures during the Iron Age in the northeast of the Iberian Peninsula before Roman arrival (218 BCE)

Most Iberian individuals from the fortress of Vilars and Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola clustered within or near the general diversity of the Iron Age population from the Iberian Peninsula in the PCA; two individuals (VILA04 and OLE06) deviated from this Iron Age cluster, shifting toward the Caucasus and southwestern Asia (Figures 3 and S14). Additionally, VILA12, OLE01, and OLE08 exhibited a distinct pattern, being displaced toward the North African cluster (Figures 3 and S14). ADMIXTURE analysis revealed that VILA04 carried a notable proportion of Iranian Neolithic ancestry, while VILA12 and OLE08 carried smaller proportions of Paleolithic Moroccan (Iberomaurusian) ancestry (Figure 4). OLE01 and OLE06 exhibited both Iranian Neolithic and Paleolithic Moroccan (Iberomaurusian) ancestries, with the Iberomaurusian ancestry predominating in OLE01 and the Iranian Neolithic ancestry in OLE06 (Figure 4). It should be noted that, except for OLE08, the samples mentioned had between 20,000 and 50,000 autosomal SNPs, so these results should be interpreted cautiously. Notably, other local individuals from Greek-Hellenistic contexts and Iron Age individuals from the northeast of the Iberian Peninsula also carried Iranian Neolithic ancestry, as seen in the ADMIXTURE analysis (Figure S15). This suggests that some interactions between Iberians and other Mediterranean populations could have occurred during the Iron Age.

To explore these genetic connections in greater detail, we tested several proximal qpAdm models for each northeast Iberian newborn. Most individuals could be adequately explained by one-way models using Bronze Age Iberian populations (Table S10), indicating that additional ancestry sources may not be required to account for their genetic profiles. An exception is OLE06, which was also the only individual from the fortress of Vilars and Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola to exhibit different behavior in PCA and ADMIXTURE. For this individual, one-way qpAdm model using Bronze Age Iberian populations did not provide an adequate fit (p value <0.05), and several two-way models yielded acceptable fits (p value >0.05), combining Bronze Age Iberian ancestry with other Mediterranean ancestries, including Paleolithic Moroccan (Iberomaurusian), late Neolithic Moroccan, and Phoenicians from Israel (Table S10). However, these models are not unique and should be interpreted with caution, as multiple alternative combinations yield statistically comparable fits. In addition, this individual has relatively low coverage (∼23k SNPs), which may limit model resolution. However, other samples from the same site, with similar low coverage, were able to be explained by one-way models of the northeast or southeast Bronze Age (SE BA) population of the Iberian Peninsula, such as OLE01, OLE03, and OLE04 (Table S10).

Other individuals, all with more than 50,000 autosomal SNPs (VILA05, VILA13, and VILA14), also had well-fitting two-way models (p value >0.05) that combined Bronze Age Iberian ancestry with Mediterranean components (Table S10). For example, VILA05 could be modeled as either northeast Iberian Early Bronze Age (0.56) + late Neolithic Moroccan (0.44, p = 0.889) or southeast Iberian Bronze Age (0.517) + late Neolithic Moroccan (0.483, p = 0.973). In VILA13, the best-fitting model included northeast Iberian Early Bronze Age (0.524) + late Neolithic Moroccan (0.476, p = 0.618). For VILA14, the model combining northeast Iberian Early Bronze Age (0.856) with Paleolithic Moroccan ancestry (0.144, p = 0.152) provided the best fit (Table S10).

Overall, while qpAdm identifies multiple plausible admixture scenarios in several samples, the non-uniqueness of the solutions suggests that these results should be interpreted with caution and do not constitute definitive evidence for North African ancestry in the studied individuals.

The genetic influence of Roman gene flow on late Iberian populations (125 BCE–50 CE)

Five out of the nine individuals from El Camp de les Lloses, one of them with less than 50,000 autosomal SNPs, showed a clear deviation toward the North African cluster in the PCA, with only CL2 and CL7 clustering close to the Iron Age perimeter (Figure 3). ADMIXTURE analysis revealed heterogeneous ancestries among individuals from El Camp de les Lloses (Figure 4). While some displayed the typical WHG-Anatolia Neolithic-Yamnaya Bronze Age pattern seen in other Iron Age samples, others carried Paleolithic Moroccan (Iberomaurusian) and/or Iranian Neolithic ancestries (Figures 4 and S11). This pattern suggests a distinct genetic composition at this site, which dates between 125 BCE and 50 CE, a period when Roman presence in the northeast of the Iberian Peninsula was already well established.

To investigate genetic affinities, we calculated the outgroup f3-statistic of the form f3(El Camp de les Lloses, test; Mbuti), where test included Bronze Age, Iron Age, and Roman groups from the Iberian Peninsula (Figure S16 and Table S7). As observed for the fortress of Vilars and Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola, the closest genetic matches to El Camp de les Lloses were Bronze and Iron Ages populations, as well as the local Roman population from the northeast of the Iberian Peninsula, rather than admixed or late Roman groups from other regions of the Iberian Peninsula or the Punics from the south (Figure S16 and Table S7).

To further explore genetic relationships and potential admixture signals, we performed f4 statistics in the form of f4(Mbuti, El Camp de les Lloses; test, reference), using four reference populations: Bronze Age, Iron Age, local Roman, and admixed Roman, all of them from the northeast of the Iberian Peninsula. We observed a coherent temporal pattern when progressing through the Bronze Age, Iron Age, and Roman populations as reference (Figures S19–S22, and Table S8). Many comparisons showed significant positive results with the northeast Bronze Age population as reference (|Z| > 3), meaning that El Camp de les Lloses shared more genetic drift with the Bronze Age population than with the test populations (Figure S19 and Table S8). When the northeast Iron Age population is used as reference, the number of strong positive signals decreases, indicating that El Camp individuals were genetically more similar to other Iron Age populations than to the earlier Bronze Age ones (Figure S20 and Table S8). This trend continues when the Roman population is taken as reference: fewer significant positive f4 values appear, suggesting that El Camp de les Lloses became even less distinguishable from the broader genetic landscape of the Roman period (Figure S21 and Table S8). Altogether, advancing through the timeline shows a gradual decrease in strongly significant positive results, consistent with genetic continuity with contemporary populations. When the admixed Roman population is used as reference, the pattern shifts clearly (Figure S22 and Table S8). Instead of producing strong positive results, many comparisons yield negative significant f4 values (|Z| < 3), indicating that El Camp de les Loses shared more genetic drift with the test populations than with the admixed Roman population itself (Figure S22 and Table S8). This outcome likely reflects that El Camp de les Lloses retained a strong Iberian genetic signature. In other words, the negative values in these comparisons reinforce that the genetic profile of El Camp fits better within the local, earlier Iberian continuum than within the genetically diverse admixed Roman groups.

Although f statistics did not reveal strong differentiation between El Camp de les Lloses and earlier Iberian populations, the PCA and ADMIXTURE analyses suggested a distinct genetic composition. To clarify this, we evaluated different qpAdm models. First, we repeated the model applied to the fortress of Vilars and Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola, testing whether El Camp de les Lloses could be explained solely by the northeast early Bronze Age (NE EBA) or the SE BA populations from the Iberian Peninsula. These models presented p values below 0.05 (Table S11). Among the two-way models tested, two were accepted: NE EBA (0.764) + Yamnaya Steppe (0.236) with a p value of 0.531, and SE BA (0.761) + Yamnaya Steppe (0.239), with a p value of 0.096 (Figure 5 and Table S11). We then tested proximal models based on Iberian ancestry, using all unrelated individuals found in Iberian sites (from this and published studies) with over 50,000 autosomal SNPs. In this case, the simplest model, that El Camp de les Lloses ancestry could be explained by Iberian ancestry, was significant with a p value of 0.06 (Figure 5 and Table S11). Among two-way models, only one fitted well (Figure 5 and Table S11): combining Iberian ancestry (0.718) with late Neolithic Moroccan ancestry (0.282; p = 0.160). These results suggest that while the El Camp de les Lloses population largely derives from the local Iberian population, some contribution from other Mediterranean sources was likely necessary to fully explain its genetic composition.

All individuals from El Camp de les Lloses could be modeled with a proximal one-way qpAdm model based on the Iberians’ ancestry (Table S12). However, out of the tested two-way models, they only were significant for two individuals: CL1 (with less than 50,000 autosomal SNPs) was modeled with the Iberians’ ancestry (0.499) and late Punic ancestry from Sardinia (0.501), with a p value = 0.829); and CL8 was also modeled with the Iberians’ ancestry (0.581) and late Punic ancestry from Sardinia (0.419) with a p value of 0.482 (Table S12). However, for the semiproximal models, those based on Bronze Age ancestry from the Iberian Peninsula, individuals such as CL1, CL2, CL9, and CL4, presented some two-way models that included different Mediterranean ancestries (Table S11).

Discussion

Although some individuals from the fortress of Vilars and El Camp de les Lloses could not be evaluated, male and female individuals were identified at all three sites, albeit in varying proportions. This variability is likely influenced by the limited sample size, which restricts the ability to draw definitive conclusions about sex distribution. Nevertheless, the presence of both sexes at each site, combined with the natural mortality pattern observed by Martirosyan et al.,35 suggests that we have a representative sample of the population. This was also seen when genetic relatedness was analyzed, with few first- and second-degree relationships found. No first- or second-degree relationships were found in the fortress of Vilars. However, some shared mtDNA haplotypes were found among individuals, some buried together. The low number of common autosomal SNPs recovered for some pairs of individuals made it not possible to determine the genetic relatedness among them. Interestingly, two individuals from Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola (OLE04 and OLE06) have previously been hypothesized to be twins due to their discovery near a wall, positioned in opposite directions with their legs intermingled and no sediment separating the two skeletons.22 However, genome-wide analysis (Table S6) confidentially revealed that they were unrelated. Furthermore, they presented different mitochondrial haplogroups: OLE04 had haplogroup N1a1a1a3, and OLE06 had haplogroup M1. These findings conclusively rule out the possibility that they were twins. At El Camp de les Lloses, slightly more genetic relatedness patterns were observed, with a pair of sisters and two second-degree relationships identified.

Across the three analyzed Iberian sites, a high diversity of mitochondrial lineages is evident. Lineages such as H, J, T, K, N, and U are typically associated with Western Europe42 and are related to the expansion of Neolithic farmers.43,44,45 Most of them had already been detected in Iberian territory during the Iron Age.2,36,37 However, the presence of the N1 sub-haplogroup, which has also been linked to southwest Asia and northeast Africa,42 and sublineages of M1, characteristic of northwest Africa,46,47 may suggest a broader genetic influence. Remarkably, this mitochondrial diversity is high despite the low number of individuals analyzed. This pattern is consistent with other Iron Age communities from the Iberian Peninsula, such as the northern Celts48 or the northeastern Greek-Hellenistic populations,2 but it contrasts with the British Celts, who exhibited less diversity.49 Notably, haplogroup U4d2, typically associated with Eastern Europe50,51 was found in the most recent individual (CL4, 50 BCE–50 CE). Haplogroup V was not detected in any individual, contrasting with previous genomic studies made in the Iberian territory.36 However, Gamba et al. used 16298C as a defining position of haplogroup V, when nowadays it is used to define haplogroup HV0,52 which was frequent in our individuals.

Regarding Y chromosome haplogroups, the expected predominance of R1b lineages, associated with the arrival of Steppe-related individuals during the Bronze Age, which largely replaced earlier lineages in Europe and the Iberian Peninsula, is observed. However, the persistence of a Neolithic Y chromosome haplogroup, such as H2a1 (detected in VILA14), highlights continuity with earlier populations. H2 sublineage has been detected during the late Neolithic and Chalcolithic in the Iberian Peninsula2,9,11,13 and other European populations.53 Moreover, H2a1 has been previously found in two Bronze Age individuals from the Czech Republic54 and two Neolithic individuals from France.54

Our results indicate that the transition from the Bronze Age to the Iron Age in the northeast of the Iberian Peninsula occurred without significant external influence, aligning with previous studies.2 This transition highlights the continuity of local communities, which evolved into the Iberians as proposed by Junyent, who emphasized the transformation of late Bronze Age societies into hierarchical chiefdoms and eventually into archaic aristocratic states without significant external influence.55,56,57 Notably, the increase of Steppe-related ancestry in Iron Age northeastern Iberia compared to Bronze Age Iberia had previously been suggested to have resulted from further gene flow from Central Europe.2 Nonetheless, the eleven unrelated Bronze Age individuals that formed the Bronze Age population were from five different archeological sites, and presented a great variability among them.2,13,58 More Bronze Age individuals from the northeast of the Iberian Peninsula should be analyzed to validate the increase of Steppe-related ancestry during the Iron Age in that territory.

The fortress of Vilars aligns closely with Bronze Age and Iron Age populations, as well as with Iron Age individuals from various cultures across the northeast of the Iberian Peninsula. However, a Mediterranean influence was genetically detected. This observation is consistent with the material culture recovered from the site.59,60 During the earlier phases (775–450 BCE), most of the pottery corresponded to pre-Iberian or Iberian tradition.59,60 However, as early as 700–550 BCE, evidence of Phoenician influence appeared on the site, including jewelry and pottery.59,60 Punic- and Greek-influenced Iberian pottery became prominent by 550–450 BCE, alongside Mediterranean or Mediterranean-influenced ivory artifacts and additional Phoenician jewelry.59,60 The results of this site highlight individual variability within the broader population. Similar smooth Bronze Age/Iron Age transitions have been documented in the Iberian Peninsula48 and the territory of present-day France.61

A genetic continuity from the Bronze Age can also be observed at Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola. However, a higher proportion of Mediterranean ancestry was detected at this site (using PCA, ADMIXTURE, and mtDNA) than at the fortress of Vilars. This could be related to the fact that Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola is closer to the coast, connected to the Mediterranean through the Vilafranca stream, which leads to two potential ports at the Iberian sites of Darró and Sitges, located approximately 15–17 km away from Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola. Most individuals (OLE01, OLE06, and OLE08) are suggested to have Mediterranean ancestry to some extent, as evidenced in PCA and ADMIXTURE. However, OLE06 is the only individual from this site that cannot be explained with a sole Bronze Age ancestry and requires an additional Mediterranean or Yamnaya ancestry. This individual presented a mitochondrial haplogroup belonging to M1 (North African ancestry). This mix could make us think of this individual as an outlier, a non-local individual. However, as a newborn who died at birth,35 the most probable conclusion would be that this individual was the offspring of a local individual and a female with close Carthaginian/Punic ancestry. This theory could be supported by the fact that many Punic (Punic-Ebusitans and central Mediterranean) amphorae and ceramics have been found through the Iberian stratigraphy of this site.62 Italian and Greek ceramics were also found in this chronolgy,62 which could also be correlated with the Mediterranean ancestry found in other samples. However, it should be noted that the presence of amphorae, ointment jars, and semi-luxury tableware at Iberian sites was associated with commercial exchanges.62

During the Iron Age, Iberians exhibited a higher proportion of Yamnaya ancestry. This pattern has also been identified by Olalde et al. in decontextualized Iron Age individuals from Iberian sites2 and by Papac et al. in Celtic individuals from the north of the Iberian Peninsula.54 However, neither of these studies analyzed Mediterranean ancestry in their individuals. The analyses of our individuals confirm that Iberians interacted with other Mediterranean cultures. These interactions, corroborated by archaeological evidence, left a genetic imprint on Iberian populations, but evidence points to sustained genetic continuity in Iberian populations, as occurred in many other European Iron Age populations.61,63

The arrival of the Romans marked a gradual but transformative shift in Iberian communities, both culturally and genetically. This change is evident in the results from El Camp de les Lloses. Although f statistics did not reveal strong differentiation between El Camp de les Lloses and earlier Iberian populations, the PCA and ADMIXTURE suggested a distinct genetic composition for some of the individuals, implying a continuity of the population with some changes. The two-way proximal qpAdm analyses for the El Camp de les Lloses population revealed the presence of Mediterranean ancestry. These findings are not surprising, given that El Camp de les Lloses is associated with metalworking and logistical activities tied to the Roman military.64 In fact, a fusion of Iberian and Roman-Italian cultures is evident in the site’s architecture, which incorporates Roman-Italian styles, such as rectangular houses with central courtyards, while utilizing indigenous building materials like stone bases and earthen walls.64 Burial practices at the site align with Iberian traditions (e.g., cremation), and pottery assemblages include a mix of Iberian and Roman-Italian artifacts.64 Additionally, most of the coins recovered (90%) are of Iberian origin, although Roman and Greek coins from Massaliot (present-day Marseille) have also been found.64

Individuals CL12, CL6, and CL7 were the closest to the previous local populations, without traces of Mediterranean ancestry in the ADMIXTURE and qpAdm models without Mediterranean ancestry. As previously noted, the Roman influence at the site is evident and could have contributed to the introduction of Mediterranean or North African ancestries. However, the presence of pottery from Ebusus (a Phoenician city in present-day Ibiza, Balearic Islands), the Tripoli region (North Africa), and the southern Iberian Peninsula (around the Strait of Gibraltar)64 suggests that these ancestries could also have been introduced through Phoenician-Punic or Carthaginian contacts. CL2, who carried the mitochondrial haplogroup M1a3a, which is most commonly found in North and East Africa, was a different case. For this individual, no North or East African influences were detected, likely due to low nuclear DNA coverage, and their ancestry was consistent with a one-way qpAdm model using northeast Iberian Early Bronze Age as a unique contributing source. While M1a lineages have been previously identified in the Iberian Peninsula, such as M1a1b1 in a Bell Beaker individual from the central region,2 the specific M1a3a sublineage had not been documented in ancient individuals from the Iberian Peninsula. This may suggest that M1a3a may have been introduced through an earlier migration or contact but remained at very low frequencies. Over time, any associated North African nuclear ancestry may have been diluted through admixture with the local population, leaving the mitochondrial haplogroup as the strongest genetic trace of that ancestry in this individual. It must be noted that CL2 presented between 20,000 and 50,000 autosomal SNPs, so the genomic results should be taken cautiously. CL1, CL8, and CL9 showed signs of Mediterranean ancestry in both the ADMIXTURE and the semiproximal qpAdm two-way models, but they presented mitochondrial lineages commonly found in ancient Iberia (K1b1a for CL1, H3 for CL8, and H3 + 152 for CL9) and western Europe.65,66

This study provides the first paleogenomic insights into the origin and evolution of Iron Age Iberian communities belonging to Iberian culture, focusing on newborns interred under the pavements of houses and productive areas. Our findings confirm the archaeological theory that Iberian culture emerged from local Bronze Age populations, evolving gradually through the Iron Age. While interactions with other Mediterranean cultures contributed to the development of the Iberians, their genetic identity remained largely intact. However, with the arrival of the Romans, new genetic influences were introduced, leading to a more diverse Iberian-Roman population alongside significant social and political transformations. This study highlights the complex and enduring genetic legacy of Iberian communities.

Limitations of the study

Due to systematic cremation practices in Iberian culture, newborns constitute the only available inhumed anthropological sample. As a result, genetic inferences derive from individuals integrated into domestic burial contexts and reflect early-life mortality and household-level practices rather than the full age structure of Iron Age communities.

This study faces limitations primarily related to variable ancient DNA preservation and data resolution. Genome-wide data were recovered at different coverage levels, with approximately 20,000–50,000 autosomal SNPs from the 1240k panel obtained for 11 individuals and more than 50,000 SNPs for another 11 individuals. Both groups were included in principle component analyses and ADMIXTURE modeling, enabling assessment of broad-scale population structure and temporal ancestry trends across the Iron Age. Formal allele-frequency-based analyses, including f statistics and qpAdm modeling for populations, were restricted to individuals exceeding 50,000 SNPs, for whom the data provide sufficient power and reliability. Moreover, low endogenous DNA content also affected uniparental marker resolution, particularly Y chromosome haplogroup determination.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study analyzes ancient human remains from 54 newborn individuals recovered from three Iron Age archaeological sites in the northeastern Iberian Peninsula: the fortress of Vilars (n = 34), Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola (n = 8), and El Camp de les Lloses (n = 12). Archaeological context, chronology, and sample details are provided in Figure 1 and Table S1. More information about each archaeological site is provided in Document S1 (Methods S1–S3 and Figures S1–S9).

No living human participants were involved in this study. All samples derive from archaeological contexts and were handled in accordance with relevant institutional and national guidelines.

Method details

Sample preparation, DNA extraction, quantification, library preparation, and sequencing

Fifteen samples from 8 newborns from El Camp de les Lloses were processed in the dedicated clean laboratory facilities at the Globe Institute, University of Copenhagen (indicated as Protocol I in Table S2a). Around 100–200 mg of bone was used for DNA extraction. Powder bone was predigested for 10 min with 1 mL of digestion buffer DB1 (0.45 M EDTA, 0.25 mg/mL Proteinase K). After discarding the supernatant, 4 mL of digestion buffer DB1 were added to the sample, which was incubated at 37°C overnight. Extracted DNA was purified using a silica-in-solution method. First, 40 mL of binding buffer (5 M Gu-HCl, 100 mM NaOAc, 20 mM NaCl, 30% isopropanol) and 50 μL of silica pellets were mixed with the digestion supernatant. Then, the samples were rotated at room temperature for 1 h. Following incubation, the samples were spun at 2000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. Next, two cleaning steps were performed using 1 mL of ice-cold 80% ethanol, resuspending the pellet, after that the supernatant was removed, and the samples were left to air-dry briefly to allow any remaining ethanol to evaporate. Finally, the pellets were resuspended with 62 μL of EB buffer and incubated on a shaker at 37°C for 10 min. After centrifugation 60 μL purified extracted DNA were stored at −20°C. Blank controls were processed with each extraction.

For the library preparation, 20 μL of each DNA extract were built into single-stranded libraries using the SCR protocol.67 For each sample, 50 μL of the library was recovered and purified using SPRI beads. The optimal number of PCR cycles was determined by qPCR (MxPro 3000, Agilent Technologies). The qPCR reactions consisted of 1 μL library template with 19 μL Master Mix containing (12.3 μL H2O, 2 μL Taq Gold buffer, 2 μL MgCl2, 0.8 μL BSA, 0.8 μL SYBR green/ROXY, 0.4 μL Forward primer IS4, 0.4 μL Reverse primer P7 index primer #50 (10 μM), 0.16 μL dNTPs (25 mM), 0.16 μL Taq Gold polymerase). Library indexing was performed using the KAPA HiFi HotStart Uracil+ kit (Roche). Finally, the amplified libraries were purified using SPRI beads and quantified on a Fragment Analyser (Agilent). The amplified and indexed libraries were then pooled in equimolar amounts and sequenced with Illumina technology (Table S2A).

The remaining samples (Table S2A) were processed in the dedicated clean laboratory facilities at the Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona (UAB). Around 100–150 mg of bone powder was used for DNA extraction in a 48-h incubation with a solution of Proteinase K and EDTA, as in Vinueza-Espinosa et al. (2020).94 Extracted DNA was purified as described by Vinueza-Espinosa et al. (2020),94 using the High Pure Viral Nucleic Acid Large Volume Kit (Roche), a Silica in HE-membrane column-based method, and eluted in 60 μL of EBT. Blank controls were processed with each extraction. A quantitative PCR (qPCR) of a small mitochondrial DNA fragment was performed to confirm the absence of contamination. Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, 2015) was used to quantify each sample, and Bioanalyzer Agilent High Sensitivity DNA Kit (Agilent Technologies, 2017) was employed to obtain fragmentation profiles.

Only extractions with at least around 0.5 pmol of DNA in the input volume were used to make whole genome libraries using protocols II to IV (described afterward). Protocol V was used to generate a library and sequence the non-coding region (NCR) of mtDNA in some samples with poor mitochondrial DNA recovery. The type of library preparation protocol for each sample is described in Table S2A.

Protocol II - Blunt-end-single-tube (BEST)

32 μL of each DNA extract were used in the BEST protocol.69 For each sample, 60 μL of the non-indexed library was recovered and purified by MinElute Reaction Cleanup Kit (Qiagen). The optimal number of PCR cycles was determined by qPCR (Rotor-Gene SYBR Green, Qiagen). The qPCR reaction consisted of 1 μL of 1:100 diluted sample with 24 μL of Master Mix (12.5 μL of SYBR Green PCR Master Mix, 2.5 μL of each P5 (IS7) and P7 (IS8) primers at 1 μM, and 6.5 μL of RNase-free water. Library indexing was performed using the KAPA HIFI HotStart Uracil+ kit (Roche). The indexed libraries were purified with the MinElute PCR kit (Qiagen). 3 μL of the purified library were diluted 1:2. From here, 3 μL were quantified with Qubit 1× dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen), and the other 3 μL were used to study the fragmentation profile with a High Sensitivity DNA Assay in a 2100 Bioanalyzer Instrument (Agilent Technologies) at the Servei de Genòmica i Espectroscòpia de Biomolècules (SGiEB) from the UAB, Barcelona. The amplified and indexed libraries were then pooled in equimolar amounts and sequenced with Illumina technology (Table S2A).

Protocol III - Blunt-end-single-tube (BEST) with half-UDG

25 μL of each DNA extract were partially treated with Uracil-DNA-Glycosylase (UDG) with 7 μL of Master Mix (0.96 μL of Thermolabile USER II (New England BioLabs), 3.2 μL of CutSmart Buffer (New England BioLabs), and 2.84 μL of EBT (Qiagen) following the incubation times from Rohland et al. (2015).95 The resulting 32 μL were used in the BEST protocol as explained in “Protocol II”.

Protocol IV

“Protocol II” was followed, but with half of the volume for each step.

Protocol V

For VILA16, VILA21, OLE07, and CL3, NCR of mitochondrial DNA was sequenced using the PowerSeq CRM Nested System (Promega), considering the protocol for archaeological samples described by Vinueza-Espinosa et al. (2023).96 Samples were sequenced on an Illumina instrument (Table S2B).

aDNA data processing and authentication of whole genome sequencing

After demultiplexing based on a dual-unique index combination developed by the sequence provider, raw sequencing data was processed with the nf-core/eager pipeline v2.4.6.70,71 Adapters were removed with AdapterRemoval v2.3.272 and sequences shorter than 30 bp were removed. Then, sequences were mapped against the Human Reference Genome GRCh37 (hg19, GenBank accession GCA_000001405.1) with BWA v0.7.17-r118873 aln and samse/sampe commands. Parameters “-l 1024 -n 0.01 -o 2” were used, following recommendations from Oliva and colleagues.97,98 SAMtools v1.1274 was used to generate sorted BAM files with all the reads with a mapping quality of over 20. Duplicates were removed with the MarkDuplicates tool from Picard v2.26.0 software.99 Damage levels were assessed with PMDtools v0.50.75 Finally, libraries were clipped with BamUtils v1.1.276 based on the pre-library treatment. In libraries with half-UDG treatment, 2 bases were clipped at the start and end of each read. However, 7 bases were removed at the start/end of the reads for non-UDG treated libraries. The quality of FASTQ files was evaluated with FastQC v0.11.977 while the quality of the BAM file was analyzed with QualiMap v2.2.2-dev.78

Ancient DNA authenticity was evaluated in libraries using several criteria. An upper-bound rate for the confidence interval for the rate to the consensus mitochondrial sequence that exceeds 95%, as computed using contamMix-1.0.10,79 was used to retain libraries. Moreover, libraries with a C-to-T misincorporation pattern under 10% were excluded. The ratio of Y to the combined X + Y chromosome was evaluated, removing those individuals with values between 0.03 and 0.35, since they are indicative of the presence of DNA from at least two individuals from different sexes. For male individuals with sufficient coverage, X chromosome contamination, with both Methods S1 and S2 in ANGSD,80 was used with a threshold of 3%. In addition, mtDNA variants and putative heteroplasmic sites were visually checked in all the samples.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Sex determination and genotyping

Genetic sex was determined with Sex.DetERRmine.py v1.1.2,81 a tool implemented in the nf-core/eager pipeline, based on SNPs. Individuals were classified as male if the frequency of reads assigned to the Y- and X chromosomes was around 0.5, while they were classified as female if the Y chromosome frequency was 0 and the X chromosome was around 1.

Trimmed BAM files were used to call genotypes with pileupCaller, which generates pseudo-haploid genotypes by randomly choosing one allele at every SNP position. The panel with 1240k nuclear SNPs used in ancient population genomics was used.9

Mitochondrial and Y chromosome haplogroups assignment

Mitochondrial reads that had already been mapped with the mitochondrial DNA for the hg19 genome were realigned against the revised Cambridge Reference Sequence (rCRS, GenBank accession NC_012920.1)100 with nf-core/eager pipeline v2.4.6.70,71 CircularMapper v1.082 was used to avoid the problems of mtDNA molecule circularity. Genotypes were called with freebayes v1.3.683 and vcflib v1.0.3.84 Haplogrep 3 v3.2.185 was used to call mitochondrial DNA haplogroups. SNPs were manually double-checked by visualizing alignments with the Integrative Genome Viewer (IGV) v2.11.2.86 Mitochondrial DNA data from samples sequenced using “protocol V” were analyzed using the pipeline developed by Cuesta-Aguirre et al. (2024),87 which already provides the haplotype. Manually double-checking of the genotypes was done with IGV, as recommended. Data from NCR and whole-genome sequencing were combined. Results from the NCR analysis were considered reliable at a coverage of 10×, while the shotgun sequencing results were considered at 3× after visual confirmation. In the low-coverage regions, the damage, mutational, and heteroplasmic profiles101,102,103 were examined visually to improve confidence.

Based on the merged and trimmed BAM files, the Y chromosome genotype was determined for each individual using UnifiedGenotyper from Picard v2.26.0 (Broad Institute, 2019). For each individual, a BED file including all SNPs with a base quality greater than 30 and belonging to reads with a mapping quality exceeding 20 was created. The python2 script hFrpr2.py from HaploGrouper,88 alongside the default tree and the SNP file from the Ghr37 reference genome (https://gitlab.com/bio_anth_decode/haploGrouper), was used to obtain the Y chromosome haplogroups.

Genetic relatedness

Only individuals with more than 20,000 autosomal SNPs from the 1240k panel9 were used to study genetic relatedness. BREADR v1.0.289 was used based on autosomal SNPs to define first- and second-degree relatedness among pairs of individuals. The pairs of individuals were categorized between “Same/Twins”, “First-degree”, “Second-degree”, or “Unrelated” (in the first or second degree), according to their pair mismatch rate (PMR). Only pairs with at least 500 shared SNPs were trusted.

Population genetic analysis

Dataset

Newly reported individuals were selected based on the following criteria: 1) individuals with more than around 20,000 autosomal SNPs from the 1240k panel9 were included in PCA and ADMIXTURE, and individuals with more than 50,000 autosomal SNPs from that panel were included for the f-statistics and qpAdm analyses; 2) Among individuals from the same archaeological site sharing the same mitochondrial haplotype, the individual with the higher number of SNPs was selected; 3) For related individuals with a posterior probability of first- and second-degree relatedness higher than 0.6, the individual with the higher number of SNPs was chosen. These selected individuals were merged with previously published datasets of ancient and modern individuals from the Allen Ancient DNA Resource (AADR).53 Only the individual with the highest SNP coverage was retained when first- or second-degree of genetic relatedness was detected. Individuals from Human Origins104 were also included for principal component analysis (PCA). Individuals were labeled for each analysis as indicated in Table S3.

PCA

A principal components analysis (PCA) was performed using the smartpca software from EIGENSOFT v8.0.090,91 with the “lsqproject” and “shrinkmode” options activated (YES) and a list of modern populations from Europe, North Africa, Caucasus, and Southwestern Asia (Table S3). All ancient individuals were projected onto PC1 and PC2.

F-statistics

For f-statistics with ADMIXTOOLS v5.1 (https://github.com/DReichLab/AdmixTools). All f-statistics were plotted with R v4.4.292 using the ggplot2 v3.5.1 package.93

For each Iberian site (the fortress of Vilars, Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola, and El Camp de les Lloses), the genetic similarity between all pairs of individuals was quantified using the outgroup f3-statistic of the form f3(Ind1, Ind2; Mbuti), calculated with qp3Pop from ADMIXTOOLS v5.1 (https://github.com/DReichLab/AdmixTools). The f3 results were converted into a pairwise distance matrix where each cell represented 1-f3(Ind1, Ind2; Mbuti), such that smaller values corresponded to higher shared genetic drift. The resulting matrix was symmetric, and diagonal values were set to zero to preserve matrix completeness. Multidimensional scaling (MDS) was then performed using the classical metric scaling function “cdmscale” in R v4.4.292 (base stats package), with k = 2 dimensions and otherwise default parameters. The eigenvalues returned by “cdmscale” were used to calculate the proportion of variance explained by each of the first two MDS dimensions. The MDS coordinates were visualized using ggplot2 v3.5.1.93

Outgroup f3-statistics, computed using qp3Pop with the “inbreed” parameter activated, measured the shared genetic drift between two populations (A and B) relative to an outgroup (C), providing a similarity measure: higher values indicate greater genetic similarity between A and B. In this study, we calculated outgroup f3-statistics in the form f3(IberianPopulation, Test; Mbuti), where IberianPopulation refers to the individuals of the fortress of Vilars and Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola, altogether, or the population of El Camp de les Lloses. The Test populations were Iron Age and Roman populations from the Iberian Peninsula, with Mbuti, a present-day Sub-Saharan hunter-gatherer population from the Democratic Republic of Congo, serving as the outgroup.

For f4-statistics, qpDstats was used with “f4mode” and “printsd” parameters activated to examine genetic relationships between ancient populations from the Northeast Iberian Peninsula and ancestral influences. The f4-statistic in the form f4(Mbuti, Target; Test, Reference) evaluates whether the Target population shares more genetic drift with the Reference or Test population relative to an outgroup. Negative values indicate greater genetic affinity between the Target and Test populations, while positive values suggest a closer relationship between the Target and Reference populations. The Test population included a wide range of populations representing different cultural and temporal contexts across the Mediterranean and Western Europe.

Admixture modeling

The ADMIXTURE and the individual qpAdm analyses were performed for individuals with more than 20,000 autosomal SNPs. However, for the qpAdm analyses based on populations, only those individuals with more than 50,000 autosomal SNPs were employed.

ADMIXTURE was used to define the main genetic cluster profiles using five fixed populations as source (k = 5): Western Hunter-Gatherers (WHG), Paleolithic individuals from Morocco (Morocco_Iberomaurusian), Neolithic individuals from Turkey (Turkey_N), Neolithic individuals from Iran (Iran_N), and Bronze Age individuals from the Pontic-Caspian Steppe belonging to the Yamnaya culture (Steppe_Yamnaya_BA). Twenty replicates were performed, and their similarity was evaluated with Pong v1.5.41

Two one-way qpAdm analyses were conducted using ADMIXTOOLS v5.1 (https://github.com/DReichLab/AdmixTools) with the “allsnps” parameter set to “YES” to test if the northeastern Iberians (population composed of Iron Age Iberian newborns from the fortress of Vilars and Sant Miquel d’Olèrdola with more than 50,000 autosomal SNPs) and the El Camp de les Lloses populations could be explained by the Bronze Age populations from the northeast and southeast of the Iberian Peninsula. Several two-way qpAdm models were applied to determine the Mediterranean influence evidenced in the PCA and the ADMIXTURE analyses. For El Camp de les Lloses, a more proximal analysis was also performed to see if it can be explained by the Iberian population, composed of all the individuals found in Iberian sites with more than 50,000 autosomal SNPs from this and previous studies. Several two-way qpAdm models were also tested to study the Mediterranean influence in those populations. The populations used as outgroups (or right populations) in these analyses included.
