Fine-Tuning Language Models to Know What They Know
Sangjun Park, Elliot Meyerson, Xin Qiu, Risto Miikkulainen

Abstract
Metacognition is a critical component of intelligence, specifically regarding the awareness of one’s own knowledge. While humans rely on shared internal memory for both answering questions and reporting their knowledge state, this dependency in LLMs remains underexplored. This study proposes a framework to measure metacognitive ability d type2 ′ d_{\rm{type2}}^{\prime} using a dual-prompt method, followed by the introduction of Evolution Strategy for Metacognitive Alignment (ESMA) to bind a model’s internal knowledge to its explicit behaviors. ESMA demonstrates robust generalization across diverse untrained settings, indicating a enhancement in the model’s ability to reference its own knowledge. Furthermore, parameter analysis attributes these improvements to a sparse set of significant modifications.

Introduction

Figure 1
:
Overview of Evolution Strategy for Metacognitive Alignment (ESMA). The process begins with an initial parent LLM, whose weights are perturbed with Gaussian noise to create a population of model variants. Each variant is evaluated on a dual-axis task: a Direct Question (to test factual knowledge) and a Meta Question (to test self-knowledge). A Joint Reward is calculated based on the alignment between the correctness and meta responses, measuring whether the model knows what it knows. Finally, the next-generation LLM is produced by a weighted average of the variants, prioritizing higher rewards, and the cycle repeats.

Human intelligence is characterized by a sophisticated capacity for metacognition to monitor and regulate one’s own cognitive processes
(
6
;
7
;
29
)
.
Within the expansive domain of metacognition, this study concentrates specifically on self-knowledge, often defined as knowing what one knows.

Humans are typically capable of reporting their own knowledge
(
11
)
because answers are formed by referencing semantic memory
(
5
)
. Whether providing a direct answer or assessing their awareness of specific facts, individuals retrieve information from the same internal source. Consequently, a strong association exists between internal knowledge and explicit behavior across various contexts.

While the metacognitive capabilities of LLMs have received significant attention
(
8
;
40
)
, efforts have largely concentrated on controlling utility issues like hallucination and refusal
(
41
;
20
)
. However, there is a lack of research investigating the dependency between intrinsic knowledge and its explicit deployment in varied contexts.
When we ask an LLM, “Do you know X?”, does the LLM recall and use its knowledge about X to answer, or does it respond by simply parroting memorized answer, even for such meta-level questions? This remains an open question.
This study explores this specific aspect.

This paper first presents a systematic framework for measuring the metacognitive abilities of LLMs. In human research, metacognition is primarily measured by having subjects solve specific problems and report their awareness of the correctness to calculate
d_{\rm{type2}}^{\prime}
. To adapt this for models, a dual-prompt method is proposed. This method consists of Direct Questions that ask for the answer and Meta Questions that inquire if the model possesses the specific knowledge. This approach enables the estimation of
d_{\rm{type2}}^{\prime}
and the subsequent comparison of metacognitive performance across various existing models.

Second, Evolution Strategy for Metacognitive Alignment (ESMA) was designed to enhance LLM metacognition. To ensure this improvement represented a genuine capacity to reference internal knowledge rather than superficial alignment, trained models were evaluated across diverse conditions. These tests included datasets from varied sources, multiple languages, distinct questioning styles, and newly acquired knowledge. The consistent performance gains across these untrained settings suggest that ESMA effectively reinforces the binding between internal knowledge and explicit behavior.

Finally, an analysis of Evolution Strategies (ES) demonstrates that the majority of parameter modifications yield no performance effect. Instead, performance shifts are driven by parameters undergoing significant modification. These findings suggest that modifying every parameter is unnecessary, presenting new avenues for future optimization.

The contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

•

Inspired by psychological measurement methodologies, this study proposes a framework to evaluate LLM metacognition by calculating
d_{\rm{type2}}^{\prime}
via Direct Questions and Meta Questions, providing a comparative analysis of various existing models.

•

Evolution Strategy for Metacognitive Alignment (ESMA) is designed to reinforce the binding between model output and internal knowledge, demonstrating robust generalizations across diverse untrained settings, from different languages to newly acquired knowledge.

•

An analysis of parameter dynamics reveals that performance improvements are driven by a sparse set of significantly modified parameters, highlighting the potential for further optimization of ES.

Evolution Strategies

Evolution Strategies
(
33
;
35
, ES;)
are gradient-free optimization techniques that iteratively refine model parameters by evaluating a population of perturbed candidates.
Unlike backpropagation, which computes exact derivatives, ES requires only forward passes.
While traditionally limited by the high dimensionality of neural networks, recent advancements have successfully extended this approach to the full-parameter fine-tuning of LLMs
(
31
;
34
)
.

ES remains effective even for non-differentiable objectives where gradient-based approaches typically fail.
Since ES generates individuals with updated weights prior to reward calculation, it can model the entire behavioral patterns across diverse situations as a single reward, rather than focusing on isolated actions.
Because the alignment between Direct and Meta Questions is such a holistic reward function, ES was employed as the optimization method.

Measurement Framework

Measuring human metacognition is frequently operationalized via confidence-accuracy paradigms
(
23
)
, where subjects solve a cognitive task and judge their accuracy. Drawing on these psychological paradigms, this work establishes a framework to measure
d_{\rm{type2}}^{\prime}
in LLMs.
For details regarding the measurement of human metacognition and the proposed extension, refer to
Section

A.1
.

Task Setup

Adapted from human metacognitive assessment frameworks, a dual-questioning protocol is implemented to evaluate LLMs. For each data point, the model addresses two distinct tasks: (1) Direct Questions to elicit factual answers, and (2) Meta Questions to self-evaluate knowledge. Isolating these inquiries within independent contexts ensures that meta-responses avoid conditioning on previously generated text.

Metric

d_{\rm{type2}}^{\prime}
is a standardized metric that quantifies metacognitive ability. It measures a capacity to discriminate between accurate and inaccurate judgments by calculating the distance between the internal confidence distributions for each decision type.

d^{\prime}_{\rm type2}=\Phi^{-1}(\text{Hit Rate})-\Phi^{-1}(\text{False Alarm Rate}),

(1)

This formula quantifies metacognitive performance as the statistical separation between mental states. A higher value indicates that internal confidence reliably predicts accuracy.

In the framework of signal detection theory,
d^{\prime}
value is typically interpreted as follows
(
15
)
:

•

d^{\prime}_{\rm{type2}}\approx 0
(Chance-level): Inability to discriminate, where the observer cannot distinguish between correct and incorrect responses at all.

•

d^{\prime}_{\rm{type2}}\approx 0.5
(Low Sensitivity): The lower bound of typical experimental performance.

•

d^{\prime}_{\rm{type2}}\approx 1.0
(Moderate Sensitivity): A standard level of sensitivity often observed in discrimination tasks.

•

d^{\prime}_{\rm{type2}}\geq 2.5
(Effective Ceiling): A level of performance rarely encountered in standard tasks where discrimination is virtually perfect.

While the
d^{\prime}_{\rm{type2}}
metric is rigorous, it is helpful to also track other metrics that are more intuitive:

•

Raw Alignment: The averaged alignment between the meta-response and the correctness of the direct outcome. While this measure is intuitive, it’s susceptible to bias and statistical coincidences. For instance, a model that indiscriminately answers “No” would achieve a higher score in a difficult setup.

•

Accuracy: The ratio of correct answers provided for Direct Questions.

•

Yes Ratio: The frequency with which the model provides a positive response to Meta Questions, indicating a belief that it knows the answer.

•

Yes Failure Ratio (YFR): The proportion of instances where the model claims to know the answer but subsequently provides an incorrect response for the Direct Question.

•

No Failure Ratio (NFR): The proportion of instances where the model claims it does not know the answer but is actually able to provide the correct response.

Reward Design

The reward function is designed along two primary axes: the direct correctness of the model’s answer and its metacognitive alignment. Optimizing solely for direct correctness risks leaving the model metacognitively blind, whereas optimizing only for meta-alignment invites reward hacking. In the latter scenario, a model may maximize rewards by intentionally providing incorrect answers while consistently claiming ignorance. (Refer to
Section

B.1
for experimental validation).

To prevent such degenerate behaviors, a differential reward system is applied based on the joint outcome of the direct and meta question. Let
C=1
if the direct answer is correct and
C=0
otherwise. Let
A=1
if the meta answer is aligned with the actual knowledge state (e.g., saying “Yes” when
C=1
, or “No” when
C=0
) and
A=0
otherwise. The joint reward is used as follows:

R(C,A)=\begin{cases}2&\text{if }C=1,A=1\text{ (Correct \& Yes)}\\
1&\text{if }C=1,A=0\text{ (Correct \& No)}\\
1&\text{if }C=0,A=1\text{ (Incorrect \& No)}\\
0&\text{if }C=0,A=0\text{ (Incorrect \& Yes)}\end{cases}

(2)

This formulation effectively sums two independent reward signals: accuracy and metacognitive alignment. This dual-pressure mechanism promotes both preserving the model’s knowledge and faithful epistemic reporting.

5.2
Confidence-based Metacognition

To validate the model’s reliance on fundamental internal knowledge sources, this analysis contrasts continuous confidence scores with the discrete binary outputs (Yes/No) utilized in prior settings. Examining whether metacognitive alignment persists across these differing modalities determines if the model operates on robust internal representations rather than input-specific dependencies.
For this purpose, confidence
D
was calculated as the normalized probability of a positive self-assessment, derived from the logits of the “Yes” and “No” tokens.

D=\frac{P(\text{Yes})}{P(\text{Yes})+P(\text{No})}.

(3)

Psychology research frequently utilizes Type 2 AUCROC for continuous or granular metacognitive ratings. In alignment with this standard, the analysis applied a Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) method. ROC curves illustrate metacognitive ability by detailing the change in the True Positive Rate (TPR) as the False Positive Rate (FPR) increases. Consequently, the Area Under the Curve (AUC) provides a threshold-invariant metric for evaluating metacognitive sensitivity within continuous confidence data.

•

\rm{AUC}=0.5
: A random baseline where confidence provides no diagnostic information about accuracy.

•

0.5<\rm{AUC}<0.7
: Low to marginal metacognitive ability.

•

\rm{AUC}>0.7
: Moderate to high metacognitive ability, where the model’s internal state reliably distinguishes between known and unknown information.

Sweeping the confidence threshold from 0 to 1 produced the ROC trajectories for each model. As illustrated in
Figure

2
, the ROC analysis reveals distinct patterns of metacognitive ability using continuous confidence.
The original models already exhibited AUC values above the 0.5 random baseline. This observation confirms that LLMs possess a rudimentary metacognitive ability even without explicit tuning.
This 3B model exhibited a particularly notable plateau in the initial FPR stage (until 0.2), where it achieved a lower TPR compared to its smaller and larger counterparts. This result suggests that the original 3B model is more prone to misplaced certainty, assigning high confidence to incorrect answers before a higher threshold is reached.

After applying ESMA, the models showed a consistent and significant improvement in metacognitive ability across all scales. The fine-tuned curves (blue) significantly bow toward the upper-left corner compared to the Base curves (orange).
Critically, after fine-tuning, all models achieved an AUC
\approx
0.75, successfully reaching the high metacognition regime.
These results demonstrate that the improvements in metacognition achieved by ESMA remain robust even on a continuous scale, despite the model being trained solely on binary meta answers. Such findings imply that ESMA fundamentally shifts the underlying mechanism through which the model references its internal knowledge base.

5.4
Metacognitive Alignment with Unified Prompt

An experiment in a novel setting assessed the training method’s generalizability in strengthening the intrinsic association between the model’s knowledge state and its self-reports.

Experimental Design

The dual-question format is replaced with a single-prompt format by adding the instruction “If you don’t know the answer, just return ‘I don’t know”’ to the Direct Question prompt.

In this IDK format, there are no separate Meta Questions. Instead, the model’s output is considered as a merged response: a return of “I don’t know” is categorized as a Meta “No,” while any other response is categorized as a Meta “Yes.”
If indeed the ability is based on a genuine metacognitive link rather than a simple pattern-matching response to specific meta-questions, it should hold the ability even when the model was questioned with this setup.

Critically, this prompt was never used during the fine-tuning phase, and the models were never explicitly optimized to respond “I don’t know”. Consequently, the performance in this setting acts as a zero-shot probe into whether ESMA established a robust, format-independent association between internal knowledge and metacognitive response, rather than a narrow proficiency in answering isolated meta-questions.

Metrics

Three primary metrics were defined to assess performance in this setting:

•

IDK Accuracy
: The accuracy of the model on the direct question within this integrated prompt setting.

•

IDK Alignment
: The metacognitive alignment in this setting, calculated by comparing correctness with the occurrence of an “I don’t know” response.

•

All Alignment
: A stringent consistency metric. An instance is considered fully aligned only if the correctness and the meta answer in a dual-question setting, and the current setting’s meta-answer are all mutually consistent. This measures the stability of the model’s internal epistemic signal across prompt formats.

Results and Discussion

The results of this experiment are summarized in
Table

2
.
As anticipated, the IDK accuracy was consistently lower than the Dual-Question accuracy across all models, as they are encouraged to refuse answering uncertain questions, which is necessarily considered incorrect.
The findings revealed a significant improvement in the fine-tuned models. Even though they were not explicitly fine-tuned for the IDK prompt, the IDK Alignment improved significantly across all scales. Most notably, on the 3B scale, alignment increased from 59.88% to 78.07%.

Furthermore, the All Alignment metric improved substantially and consistently, particularly in the 3B and 7B variants. This provides another supporting evidence that ESMA effectively strengthens the underlying metacognitive association between knowledge and meta answers, allowing the model to rely on internal representations and processes regardless of whether they were elicited through an independent meta-assessment (i.e., dual-questioning) or integrated directly into its task-solving behavior (i.e., IDK prompting).

5.6
Sparse Contribution of Evolutionary Weight Updates

Figure 4
:
Effect of weight patching ratio on metacognitive abilities. The plot illustrates how
d_{\rm{type2}}^{\prime}
(blue bars, right axis) and raw alignment (%, orange bars, left axis) change as the top
p\%
of weight updates (by L1 magnitude) are applied to the Qwen2.5 1.5B model. Significant gains were observed with only the top 10% of updates, after which performance plateaued. This observation suggests that metacognitive improvements are driven by a sparse subset of parameters.

ES modifies all model parameters simultaneously at each iteration. However, even when a specific modification improves fitness, it does not necessarily follow that every individual parameter change contributes positively to the fitness. To investigate the efficiency and effectiveness of these learned updates, this study examined whether the improvements were driven by the collective shift of all parameters or by a subset of highly influential weight changes.

Experimental Procedure

The total parameter change,
\Delta\mathbf{W}
, is defined as the difference between the weights of the final fine-tuned model,
\mathbf{W}_{\rm tuned}
, and the base model,
\mathbf{W}_{\rm base}
, as

\Delta\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{W}_{\rm tuned}-\mathbf{W}_{\rm base}.

(4)

To evaluate the impact of these changes, every individual scalar value within
\Delta\mathbf{W}
was ranked by its magnitude (L1 norm). A patching ratio
p\in[0,100]\%
was then defined, representing the top
p\%
of weight changes with the highest absolute values. Finally, a partially patched model,
\mathbf{W}_{p}
, was constructed by adding only these selected updates to the base model:

\mathbf{W}_{p}=\mathbf{W}_{\rm base}+\text{Top}(\Delta\mathbf{W},p),

(5)

where
\text{Top}(\Delta\mathbf{W},p)
is a sparse tensor containing only the
p\%
largest magnitude updates from
\Delta\mathbf{W}
and zeros elsewhere. The parameter
p
was systematically increased from 0% (the base model) to 100% (the fully fine-tuned model), and the resulting
d^{\prime}_{\rm{type2}}
and raw alignment were measured.

Results

As illustrated in
Figure

4
, the experiment revealed a highly non-linear relationship between the volume of weight patching and the improvement in metacognitive performance. The most striking observation was the efficiency of the highest-magnitude updates. Applying only the top 10% of weight changes yielded a dramatic leap in performance: the
d^{\prime}_{\rm{type2}}
value rose sharply from 0.20 to 0.63, capturing roughly 80% of the total improvement through the entire training process.
Similarly, raw alignment increased from 53.30% to 63% within this initial 10% window.

Following this rapid ascent, the metrics showed diminishing returns. Increasing the patching ratio from 10% to 50% led to a more gradual improvement, with
d^{\prime}_{\rm{type2}}
peaking at 0.76 and raw alignment stabilizing around 65%. Notably, the remaining 50% of weight updates, consisting of the smallest scalar changes, contributed almost no additional metacognitive ability.
The fact that the vast majority of improvement was concentrated in the top 10% of updates indicates that metacognitive alignment is driven by specific, high-impact weight adjustments rather than a uniform shift across the entire parameter space.
Please refer to
Section

B.5
for a more detailed analysis of the top weight change and the results of bottom-up patching.

6
Discussion and Future Work

This study investigates whether LLMs possess introspection regarding their own knowledge and whether this capability can be enhanced. Functionally, we quantified metacognition by measuring the discriminative power of the model’s meta answers in predicting actual correctness using
d_{type2}^{\prime}
.

The results suggest organized internal knowledge of LLMs and their potential to conditionally reference this knowledge in diverse contexts. Furthermore, the success of ESMA demonstrates that this ability can be enhanced through training. Notably, the consistent results across unseen prompts and different languages strongly support the premise that this is not merely superficial data adaptation, but rather a fundamental shift in how the model accesses knowledge.

Despite ESMA’s success, the absolute metacognition level remains far below that of humans, especially given the very low metacognitive demands of this QA task. Since metacognition is a core requirement for human-level AI and a key capability that enables agents to autonomously seek necessary information, correct errors, and learn in a targeted and precise manner, it is imperative to continue finding innovative ways to enhance the metacognition of LLMs.

Methodologically, this work highlights the unique advantages of evolution strategies for optimizing holistic behavioral patterns across independent contexts where gradient-based methods face limitations.
This finding highlights the potential of ES as a powerful framework for modeling high-level cognitive functions that manifest only within the collective behavioral repertoire in diverse environments.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of machine learning by improving the reliability and self-awareness of Large Language Models. The primary potential societal consequence of our work is the reduction of misinformation and increase of consistency, which promotes the safe deployment of AI in high-stakes environments. We do not feel there are other specific negative societal consequences that must be highlighted here beyond the standard risks associated with training generative models.

Appendix A
Background

A.1
Measuring Human Metacognition

In psychology, the standard approach to measuring metacognitive ability involves assessing the correspondence between an individual’s objective performance and their subjective awareness of that performance
(
14
)
.
In a standard setup, participants are presented with a series of general knowledge questions and asked to provide an answer. For each question, they also provide a metacognitive judgment, such as a feeling-of-knowing or a confidence rating, which reflects their internal estimate of the likelihood that their answer is correct
(
36
;
23
)
. By comparing these subjective ratings against objective correctness, researchers can determine how accurately an individual monitors their own knowledge state.

This measuring process is formally analyzed through the lens of Type 1 and Type 2 cognitive processes. Type 1 processing refers to the primary cognitive action, such as answering a general knowledge question, while Type 2 processing involves the secondary monitoring of that action, characterized by the subject’s ability to evaluate the correctness of their own responses. Within the framework of Signal Detection Theory (SDT), Type 1 performance is quantified by
d^{\prime}
(d-prime), representing the subject’s ability to discriminate between correct and incorrect information. To evaluate the second-order monitoring process, researchers use Type 2
d^{\prime}
, which measures a subject’s ability to discriminate between their own correct and incorrect responses based on their confidence levels
(
28
;
10
)
.

To evaluate the second-order monitoring process, researchers use
d^{\prime}_{\rm type2}
, a standardized metric that quantifies metacognitive ability. It measures a subject’s capacity to discriminate between accurate and inaccurate judgments by calculating the distance between the internal confidence distributions for each decision type:

d^{\prime}_{\rm type2}=\Phi^{-1}(\text{Hit Rate})-\Phi^{-1}(\text{False Alarm Rate}),

(6)

where
\Phi^{-1}
denotes the inverse cumulative normal distribution. Under this binary setup, hit rate is defined as the probability of the model responding “Yes” when its Type 1 answer was correct:
P(\text{Meta-Yes}|\text{Correct})
. Conversely, false alarm rate represents the probability of the model responding “Yes” when its Type 1 answer was actually incorrect:
P(\text{Meta-Yes}|\text{Incorrect})
.

Inspired by these human metacognition metrics, this study employed this framework to measure the metacognitive capacities of LLMs.
Specifically, the experimental design consists of a Direct Question and a Meta Question judgment.
For Meta Questions, prompting the model with “Do you know the answer to the following question?” utilizes its “Yes” or “No” response as a form of self-report. This approach allows us to quantify the model’s subjective certainty in a manner analogous to human metacognitive monitoring.
This dual-prompting structure allows us to expand traditional behavioral metrics for LLMs, enabling a direct comparison between an agent’s internal confidence in that knowledge.

A.2
Evolution Strategies

Evolution Strategies
(
33
;
35
, ES;)
form a class of black-box optimization techniques that iteratively refine model parameters by evaluating a population of perturbed candidates, rather than relying on the calculus of gradients. Unlike backpropagation, which computes exact derivatives through the chain rule, ES operates as a derivative-free mechanism that requires only forward passes. While traditionally limited by the high dimensionality of neural networks, recent advancements have successfully extended this approach to the full-parameter fine-tuning of multi-billion parameter large language models
(
31
)
.

The primary obstacle in applying standard gradient-based learning to metacognition fine-tuning is the requirement to assess the relationship between two distinct behavioral outputs: the direct response and the meta-evaluation. In standard deep learning, training is performed using gradient descent on individual examples. Because a direct question and a meta-question are processed as independent inputs, standard backpropagation cannot calculate a gradient across these separate contexts. The model is updated based on the error of a single forward pass, making it impossible to optimize for joint coherence directly.

ES shares similarities with reinforcement learning (RL) in that both methods aim to maximize rewards. However, RL is inherently constrained to searching for improvements in the action space by modifying action probabilities.
In contrast, ES searches directly in the parameter space.
This approach allows ES to modify the entire behavior of the model at once, making it possible to optimize complex reward functions that integrate outcomes across different contexts where gradients cannot be calculated.
A distinct advantage of ES is the order of operations: variation occurs first, and application (reward-based selection) follows. This property allows for the execution of multiple inferences across various scenarios to assign a joint reward based on aggregated behavioral outcomes.

This distinction is analogous to the difference between biological conditioning and natural selection. While biological RL reinforces a single action via a dopamine-like feedback loop, evolution evaluates diverse “patterns of life” rather than isolated movements. Consider a scenario where a predator interrupts a foraging agent. While both a high-intelligence and a low-intelligence agent execute the same immediate action of fleeing, the superior agent might encode the food’s location to return later, whereas the inferior agent might simply escape and lose the resource. ES mimics this evolutionary advantage by optimizing for the aggregate trajectory of a lifespan rather than isolated steps, effectively selecting for superior behavioral patterns that maximize long-term survival and utility.

The optimization process is summarized as follows. To begin, let
\theta_{t}
represent the model parameters at generation
t
. A population of
N
perturbations is generated by adding Gaussian noise to the parent parameters:

\theta_{i}=\theta_{t}+\sigma\epsilon_{i},\quad\epsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),

(7)

where
\sigma
denotes the mutation strength. Each perturbed instance
\theta_{i}
is evaluated to determine its fitness score
F(\theta_{i})
. To ensure training stability and scale-invariance, these fitness results are typically
z
-standardized across the population to have a mean of zero and unit variance, resulting in
\hat{F}(\theta_{i})
.

The parameters for the next generation are then updated via a weighted average of the perturbations based on these standardized fitness scores with a learning rate
\alpha
:

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}+\alpha\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{F}(\theta_{i})\epsilon_{i}.

(8)

This iterative refinement allows the process to progressively explore the parameter space by shifting the distribution toward regions of higher reward. This cycle repeats for several generations, satisfying complex behavioral requirements without the need for a differentiable objective function.
