Complete sequence, analysis and organization of the Graphiphora augur granulovirus genome
Authors: Wenyi Jin, Uranbileg Ganbold, Byambasuren Mijidsuren, Byambadelger Batbaatar, Hongtuo Wang, Qian Meng, Jihong Zhang, Hongbin Liang, Qilian Qin, Huan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGraphiphora augur (Lepidoptera; Noctuidae) is an important forest pest, but its associated viruses remain largely uncharacterized. Baculoviruses, particularly granuloviruses, are widely used in biological control.ResultsHere, we report a novel granulovirus isolated from larvae in Mongolia. Morphological analysis showed typical Betabaculovirus. Morphological classification and BLAST analysis of the mitochondrial COI barcode sequence confirmed that the host of the virus was G. augur, thus the viral isolate was designated as Graphiphora augur granulovirus (GrauGV). The GrauGV genome is a 147,179 bp circular dsDNA molecule with 44.05% G + C content, encoding 167 predicted ORFs, including 38 core genes. Phylogenetic analysis based on core genes placed GrauGV within Betabaculovirus as a sister lineage to Agrotis segetum granulovirus. Pairwise distance analysis confirmed that it represents a distinct species.ConclusionsGrauGV represents a novel Betabaculovirus species with distinct genomic characteristics. This study expands current knowledge of granulovirus diversity and provides a foundation for future studies on baculovirus evolution and biological control applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-13000-6.Keywords: Graphiphora augur, Granulovirus, Betabaculovirus, Genome analysis

Introduction

Graphiphora augur (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae) is a widely distributed lepidopteran pest inhabiting moist hardwood and coniferous forests across Eurasia and North America. Due to its broad geographic distribution and polyphagous larval feeding behavior, G. augur poses significant ecological and economic challenges. Periodic population outbreaks can lead to severe defoliation, resulting in long-term damage to forest vegetation and disruption of ecosystem stability. To date, no baculovirus has been formally reported from this species, leaving a notable gap in our understanding of its viral pathogens.

Baculoviridae is a family of insect specific, rod shaped double stranded DNA viruses. Based on host range and virion morphology, this family is classified into four genera: Alphabaculovirus (lepidopteran specific nucleopolyhedroviruses, NPVs), Betabaculovirus (lepidopteran specific granuloviruses, GVs), Gammabaculovirus (hymenopteran specific NPVs), and Deltabaculovirus (dipteran specific NPVs). Baculovirus genomes range from approximately 80 to 180 kbp and consist of a circular, covalently closed, supercoiled double stranded DNA molecule encoding roughly 100 to 200 open reading frames (ORFs) [1]. Members of the genus Betabaculovirus are further distinguished by their unique occlusion bodies (OBs), which are typically ellipsoidal in shape with diameters ranging from 0.2 to 0.4 μm [2].

A defining feature of baculoviruses is their biphasic infection cycle, which produces two distinct progeny phenotypes with specialized functions. Budded viruses (BVs) are generated during the early phase of infection and are responsible for systemic dissemination within the insect host, and occlusion derived viruses (ODVs) are produced during the late phase of infection and mediate primary infection of midgut epithelial cells following oral ingestion by the host [3]. Mature ODVs are embedded within a proteinaceous matrix to form OBs, which protect virions from environmental degradation and facilitate horizontal transmission in nature. This dual-phenotype strategy underpins the ecological success of baculoviruses as highly adapted pathogens of insects [4].

To date, approximately 110 baculovirus species have been identified and characterized [5], with substantial research efforts focused on their application as biological control agents for agricultural and forestry pests. Baculoviruses are regarded as environmentally benign alternatives to chemical insecticides due to their host specificity and safety to non-target organisms. However, this same host specificity also represents a major limitation, as most baculoviruses exhibit a narrow host range, restricting their practical deployment. Consequently, there is a continuing need to discover and characterize novel baculovirus isolates with potential utility in pest management.

Beyond their applied value, newly identified baculoviruses provide important opportunities to deepen our understanding of viral evolution, host adaptation, and virus-host interactions. Comparative genomic and functional analyses of emerging baculovirus isolates can reveal mechanisms underlying host specificity, tissue tropism, and infection efficiency. Such insights not only inform the rational development of improved biological control strategies but also contribute to a broader understanding of the coevolutionary dynamics between insect viruses and their hosts. In this study, we report the isolation, pathological characterization, and complete genome sequence of a novel granulovirus infecting G. augur, and examine its evolutionary relationship within the genus Betabaculovirus.

Method

Baculovirus isolation and host identification

The virus isolate and its host larvae were collected in Undurkhangai soum, Mongolia (49°16’4.32"N, 94°50’58.54"E, altitude 1829 m) in 2025. OBs of the virus were harvested from infected larval cadavers and purified through sucrose gradient centrifugation. Total genomic DNA was extracted from insect tissue using the MightyPrep Reagent for DNA (Takara, Japan). To identify the host species, the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) barcode region was amplified using universal lepidopteran primers [6]. The resulting amplicons were sequenced via Sanger sequencing, and the sequences were analyzed using the BLAST algorithm against the GenBank database. Detailed primer information is provided in Table S1.

Electron microscopy

Morphological characteristics of the viral infection were initially examined using light microscopy (Leica DM2000, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). For ultrastructural analysis, purified OBs were observed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Hitachi SU8010, Tokyo, Japan) at an acceleration voltage of 5 kV. The average dimensions of the GrauGV OBs were determined by measuring 80 individual OBs from representative SEM micrographs.

Viral DNA isolation, sequencing, and assembly

Viral genomic DNA was extracted from purified OBs following alkaline lysis (pH 10.8). Whole genome sequencing was performed on the DNB seq platform, yielding high quality data with Q20 and Q30 scores of 97.84% and 93.19%, respectively. Raw reads were evaluated using SeqKit (v2.13.0) and mapped against the G. augur genome using Bowtie2 (v2.5.1) to remove host-derived contamination. Raw sequencing reads were evaluated using SeqKit. To reduce excessive sequencing depth, approximately 200× coverage reads were randomly subsampled. The high quality paired end reads were assembled de novo into contigs using Unicycler (v0.5.0) [7]. To ensure assembly accuracy, three regions with SNP were verified through PCR amplification (primers listed in Table S1) followed by Sanger sequencing to resolve any potential assembly errors [8].

Genome annotation and sequence analysis

ORFs encoding at least 50 amino acids were predicted using FGENESV (Softberry Inc.) [9] and the NCBI ORF Finder. Functional annotation of these ORFs was performed based on homology searches using the BLASTP implemented in the NCBI BLAST+ package against a custom baculovirus reference protein database using an E-value cutoff of 1e-5, minimum query coverage of 50%, and a maximum of five target hits retained per query. Genome annotations were manually curated based on BLAST results and conserved baculovirus gene organization. Detailed BLAST outputs are provided in Table S2. Gene parity plots were constructed to compare the genomic organization of GrauGV with representative baculoviruses [10]. To identify potential regulatory elements, the 180 nucleotide regions upstream of the initiation codon of each ORF were screened for early and late promoter motifs. Early promoter elements were identified based on the presence of a TATA-box motif (TATAW) associated with a CAKT mRNA start site located 25–35 bp downstream [11, 12], or LEF-associated TATA-like elements (TAATWAA) [13]. Late promoter motifs were defined by the presence of the conserved DTAAG sequence, typically located approximately 60 bp upstream of the start codon [14]. Protein physicochemical properties were analyzed using EMBOSS (v4.0.0) with the pepstats program.

Phylogeny and Kimura 2 parameter analysis

Phylogenetic analysis was conducted using the concatenated amino acid sequences of the 38 baculovirus core genes from 108 representative genomes (Table S4). Core genes were identified based on BLASTP annotation results. Multiple sequence alignments were generated using MAFFT [15] with the --auto --quiet options [16]. Maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic analysis was performed using IQ-TREE under the LG + G substitution model with 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates (-bb 1000) and automatic CPU detection (-nt AUTO) [17].

Kimura 2 parameter (K2P) pairwise distances [18] were calculated for the nucleotide sequences of polyhedrin/granulin, lef-8, and lef-9 fragments [19] using PHYLIP (v3.6). Substitution rates among sites were assumed to be uniform, and gaps were treated according to the pairwise deletion method.

SNP analysis

Filtered reads were mapped to the final GrauGV genome using BWA (v0.7.17) and processed using SAMtools (v1.19.2) for BAM conversion and coordinate sorting. Variants were identified using FreeBayes (v1.3.1) with parameters --min-coverage 10, --min-base-quality 20, and --min-mapping-quality 30. SNPs were extracted and filtered using BCFtools with thresholds of QUAL > 30 and DP > 10. High-confidence variants were further filtered using INFO/AB > 0.15, QUAL > 1000, and INFO/DP > 1000.

Result

Host identification and characterization of GrauGV

Field surveys conducted in Mongolia led to the collection of diseased lepidopteran larvae exhibiting clinical symptoms consistent with baculovirus infection (Fig. 1A, B). SEM was employed to examine the ultrastructural morphology of GrauGV OBs. The OBs exhibited an irregularly oval shape (Fig. 1C), a characteristic feature of members of the genus Betabaculovirus. Morphometric analysis of the OBs revealed an average length of 0.48 ± 0.03 μm, consistent with values reported for granuloviruses [2].

Host identification and ultrastructural morphology of GrauGV. A Graphiphora augur larva collected under natural field conditions. B Deceased G. augur larva exhibiting pathological characteristics typical of baculovirus infection. C Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image showing the irregularly oval shaped occlusion bodies (OBs) of GrauGV. Scale bar = 1 μm

Based on morphological characteristics, the specimens were identified as G. augur (Lepidoptera; Noctuidae). Upon death, the larvae displayed typical pathological features associated with baculovirus infection, such as body softening and systemic liquefaction (Fig. 1B). To confirm the taxonomic identity of the host insect, a mitochondrial cytochrome COI barcode fragment (658 bp) was amplified and sequenced. BLASTn analysis showed that the sequence shared the highest similarity with G. augur mitochondrial genome sequence (GenBank accession: OZ280803.1), with 99.85% nucleotide identity, 100% query coverage, and an E-value of 0.0. These results, together with morphological characteristics, supported the identification of the host species as G. augur. Voucher specimens were preserved at the Institute of Zoology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Beijing, China. Consequently, the viral isolate was designated as Graphiphora augur granulovirus (GrauGV).

Genome assembly and structural characteristics of GrauGV

The GrauGV genome was sequenced using the DNB seq platform. To reduce host-derived contamination, raw reads were mapped against the G. augur genome using Bowtie2, and unmapped reads were retained for downstream analyses. Approximately 0.16% of reads were identified as host-derived and removed, resulting in 4,128,136 clean read pairs. Because the sequencing depth was extremely high, approximately 1% of the filtered reads were randomly subsampled using BBTools prior to de novo assembly to reduce assembly graph complexity. The subsampled reads were assembled using Unicycle. Assembly graphs were revealed a circular genome structure with three ambiguous branching regions associated with SNP-rich loci. These regions were further validated by PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing to obtain the final consensus genome sequence. To evaluate assembly quality, all clean reads were remapped to the finalized GrauGV genome assembly using Bowtie2. The final assembly showed an average sequencing depth of 8,344×, with 99.18% of reads successfully remapped to the genome, supporting the completeness and accuracy of the assembled circular genome.

The complete GrauGV genome is a circular double stranded DNA molecule of 147,179 bp with an overall G + C content of 44.05%, which is consistent with other members of the genus Betabaculovirus [19]. Following baculovirus nomenclature conventions, the granulin gene was designated as ORF1, and its translation start codon was defined as the zero coordinate of the physical map (Fig. 2).

Circular genome map of GrauGV. The genome is represented as a circular double stranded DNA molecule. Open Reading Frames (ORFs) are color coded based on their conservation and classification: baculovirus core genes (red), lepidopteran infecting baculovirus conserved genes (blue), other common baculoviral genes (yellow), and putative novel ORFs unique to GrauGV (gray)

A total of 167 putative ORFs encoding proteins of at least 50 amino acids were predicted, covering approximately 91.20% of the genome. Among these, 81 ORFs (48.5%) are transcribed in the forward orientation, while 86 (51.5%) are in the reverse orientation (Table S2). Comparative genomics revealed that GrauGV contains 38 baculovirus core genes. Additionally, 35 genes conserved among baculoviruses [20] and 77 genes shared across the family Baculoviridae were identified (Fig. 2). 17 ORFs lacked detectable similarity to known viral proteins in BLASTp searches against the NCBI nr database under the current search criteria and were therefore classified as ORFs without identifiable homologs. None of the 17 putative novel ORFs overlapped with annotated baculovirus genes, supporting their potential authenticity as independently encoded proteins.

SNP analysis identified 4 nucleotide substitutions within the GrauGV genome. A total of 58 high-confidence sequence variants were identified in the GrauGV genome following stringent quality filtering. The average sequencing depth across variant sites was approximately 8,358×, with all retained variants supported by depths exceeding 500×. Among these variants, the majority were SNPs, while a small number of multi-nucleotide polymorphisms (MNPs) were also detected. Furthermore, a non-synonymous substitution was identified at position 142,251 within GrauGV-161 (encoding ecdysteroid UDP-glucosyltransferase, egt), resulting in an asparagine (Asn) to lysine (Lys) amino acid change. EGT inactivates host molting hormones by catalyzing the transfer of glucose to ecdysteroids [21]. By disrupting ecdysteroid signaling, EGT blocks larval molting and pupation [22], thereby extending the feeding period and maximizing viral progeny yield [23]. Because the viral DNA used for sequencing was obtained directly from field-collected infected larvae rather than plaque-purified virus populations, these SNPs likely represent naturally occurring sequence polymorphisms within the sampled viral population. However, additional experimental validation would be required to assess their potential functional significance.

To investigate the transcriptional regulation of the 17 putative novel ORFs, the 180 bp regions upstream of their start codons were analyzed (Table 1). Early promoter motifs (TATA box related elements such as TATAW/TAATWAA and the CAKT motif) were detected upstream of GrauGV-22. Conversely, late promoter elements (DTAAG motif) were identified for GrauGV-23, -25, -30, -51, -65, -74, -83, -119, -120, and − 164. Notably, GrauGV-21, -24, -44, -45, and − 166 possessed both early and late motifs, suggesting temporal regulation across different infection phases. No canonical motifs were found for GrauGV-19, indicating potential regulation by atypical elements.

Phylogenetic analysis of GrauGV

To elucidate the evolutionary placement of GrauGV within the family Baculoviridae, a phylogenetic analysis was performed using the concatenated amino acid sequences of 38 conserved core genes from 108 representative baculovirus genomes (Table S3). The resulting phylogenetic tree revealed that GrauGV clusters within the genus Betabaculovirus, consistent with the presence of the granulin gene in its genome (Fig. 3). Within the Betabaculovirus clade, GrauGV formed a well supported lineage that is most closely related to Agrotis segetum granulovirus (AgseGV), indicating a sister taxon relationship. Pairwise genome comparison showed that GrauGV shares a nucleotide identity of 52.7% with AgseGV, suggesting substantial genetic divergence despite their close evolutionary affinity.

Unrooted phylogenetic tree inferred from the concatenated amino acid sequences of the 38 core genes. The position of GrauGV is highlighted in red. Bootstrap support values obtained from 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates are indicated at the nodes. Major clades, including Alphabaculovirus, Betabaculovirus, and Gammabaculovirus, are indicated, with some branches partially collapsed for visual clarity

According to the established species demarcation criteria for baculoviruses, pairwise nucleotide distances were calculated using the Kimura two parameter (K2P) model based on partial sequences of polyhedrin/granulin, lef-8, and lef-9 [19]. Ambiguously aligned regions and nucleotide positions containing missing data or gaps were removed prior to analysis, resulting in a final trimmed alignment length of 1573 bp. Among all analyzed baculoviruses, GrauGV showed the closest relationship to AgseGV, with a K2P distance of 0.212, which is substantially higher than the accepted species demarcation threshold of 0.05 substitutions per site for baculoviruses. The K2P distances between GrauGV and all other analyzed baculoviruses exceeded the 0.05 substitutions per site threshold (Table S4). These results collectively support the classification of GrauGV as a novel species within the genus Betabaculovirus.

Gene parity plot analysis was employed to assess gene order conservation between GrauGV and representative baculoviruses (Fig. 4) [10]. The analysis included Culex nigripalpus nucleopolyhedrovirus (CuniNPV; Deltabaculovirus), Neodiprion sertifer nucleopolyhedrovirus (NeseNPV; Gammabaculovirus), Autographa californica multiple nucleopolyhedrovirus (AcMNPV; Alphabaculovirus group I), Lymantria dispar multiple nucleopolyhedrovirus (LdMNPV; Alphabaculovirus group II), and Adoxophyes orana granulovirus (AdorGV; Betabaculovirus). Additionally, closely related granuloviruses, such as Plutella xylostella GV (PlxyGV), Plodia interpunctella GV (PlinGV), and AgseGV, were compared. The gene order of GrauGV exhibited high collinearity with AgseGV, indicating strong conservation of genome architecture. Partial collinearity was observed with PlxyGV and PlinGV, further supporting its assignment to the Betabaculovirus genus. In contrast, GrauGV showed minimal gene order conservation with NeseNPV, CuniNPV, AcMNPV, and LdMNPV, consistent with the greater evolutionary divergence among baculovirus genera (Fig. 4).

Analysis of multi copy genes in the GrauGV genome

The majority of sequenced baculovirus genomes contain between 1 and 16 copies of the baculovirus repeat ORF (bro), although their precise biological functions remain to be fully elucidated [24]. Bro proteins possess DNA binding affinity and have been implicated in the modulation of host DNA replication, as demonstrated in Bombyx mori nucleopolyhedrovirus (BmNPV) [25]. In the GrauGV genome, eight bro genes were identified, several of which are organized in tandem clusters (e.g., GrauGV39-41, GrauGV 116–118, and GrauGV 150–151). The copy number and clustered organization of bro genes were greater than those observed in the closely related AgseGV [26], reflecting variation in bro gene expansion among related betabaculoviruses.

Discussion

In the present study, we identified and characterized a novel granulovirus, designated GrauGV, isolated from diseased G. augur larvae collected in Mongolia. Ultrastructural analysis revealed that GrauGV forms irregularly oval shaped OBs, a morphological hallmark consistent with the genus Betabaculovirus. Subsequent whole genome sequencing and annotation confirmed that GrauGV possesses the quintessential genomic architecture of granuloviruses, including the presence of the granulin gene, a genome size, and a G + C content comparable to those of other known Betabaculoviruses [2, 19]. Although baculovirus-like pathological symptoms were observed in infected larvae, controlled infection assays were not performed in this study. Therefore, future experimental infection studies will be necessary to confirm the pathogenicity and host range of GrauGV.

Phylogenetic inference based on the concatenated amino acid sequences of 38 conserved core genes robustly positioned GrauGV within the genus Betabaculovirus. Within this clade, GrauGV was identified as a sister taxon to AgseGV, a relationship further corroborated by gene parity plot analysis which showed high collinearity between the two genomes. Despite their close evolutionary proximity, the substantial nucleotide divergence and K2P distances provide compelling evidence for the classification of GrauGV as a distinct, novel species within the Baculoviridae family.

Several variant loci exhibited comparable proportions of reference and alternative alleles, suggesting the presence of sequence heterogeneity within the sampled viral population. This pattern is consistent with the genetic heterogeneity commonly observed in baculovirus populations, which frequently consist of mixed genotypic variants maintained during natural transmission and laboratory propagation [27]. Similar patterns of intra-isolate genetic diversity have been reported in several baculoviruses, where mixed-genotype populations were associated with altered virulence, transmission efficiency, host adaptation, and environmental persistence [28, 29]. Previous studies demonstrated that naturally occurring baculovirus populations frequently consist of multiple coexisting genotypes, and interactions among these variants may enhance viral fitness through functional complementation during infection [28, 29]. In some nucleopolyhedroviruses, mixed-genotype infections were shown to influence pathogenicity, speed of kill, tissue tropism, and progeny production compared with individual clonal variants [30, 31].

Because the viral DNA analyzed in this study was obtained directly from field-collected infected larvae rather than plaque-purified virus populations, the detected variants likely represent naturally occurring polymorphisms within the sampled viral population. However, additional studies, including controlled infection experiments and long-read sequencing approaches, will be necessary to further evaluate the biological significance and population structure associated with these polymorphic loci.

Collectively, these findings expand the currently available genomic resources for betabaculoviruses and provide a basis for future studies on baculovirus evolution, virus-host interactions, and intra-isolate genomic variation.

Conclusion

The characterization of the GrauGV genome significantly expands the understanding of Betabaculovirus diversity and highlights the remarkable evolutionary plasticity of the Baculoviridae. These findings not only enrich the genetic resources available for comparative virology but also provide a fundamental framework for future investigations into virus-host interactions and the development of GrauGV based biological control strategies against G. augur and related agricultural pests.
