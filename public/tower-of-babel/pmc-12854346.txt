Effectiveness of emotion-focused therapy on anger, guilt, and self-destructive behaviors in people with prolonged grief disorder: A randomized clinical trial
Authors: Zeynab Akbari, Abbas Masjedi-Arani, Maryam Bakhtiari, Amir S. Kianimoghadam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Grief, following the loss of loved ones, involves emotions from deep sorrow to intense anger. Various psychological interventions exist for bereavement, with emotion-focused therapy (EFT) being a notable choice. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of EFT in addressing the psychological factors associated with prolonged grief disorder (PGD), specifically targeting anger, guilt, and self-destructive behaviors in individuals with PGD.MATERIALS AND METHODS:The current study was a clinical trial design with an experimental and control group with a 3-month follow-up. Forty patients with prolonged bereavement disorder were selected based on their availability and randomly categorized into two experimental and control groups (20 people in each group) in Tehran, Iran, in 2022–2023. For the intervention (experimental) group, EFT was performed in the form of 10 1-h sessions (once a week individually and online), and the control group was not treated. Assessment tools included the Grief Experience Questionnaire (GEQ), State-Trait Anger Expression Inventory-2 (STAXI-2), Guilt Inventory, and Self-Harm Inventory (SHI). Analysis of variance with repeated measurements was used to analyze the data.RESULTS:Multivariate analysis of variance of anger, guilt, and self-destructive behaviors showed no significant difference found between the two groups in the pre-test (P > 0.05). However, the difference between the mean scores of the two groups was significant on these variables in the post-test and follow-up stages (P < 0.05).CONCLUSIONS:The findings of this study demonstrate that EFT is an effective intervention for addressing anger, guilt, and self-destructive behaviors among individuals diagnosed with PGD.

Introduction

The death of a loved one is among the most prevalent stressors.[1] Grief emerges following the loss of loved ones, encompassing a spectrum of emotions ranging from profound sorrow to intense anger.[2] The grieving experience encompasses various emotions, such as denial, sadness, depression, anger, anxiety, guilt, anguish, feelings of abandonment and isolation, as well as physiological reactions such as fatigue.[3]

Grief is a common human experience, and mourning is a natural response to the loss of a loved one. Although the majority of individuals who experience loss typically adapt within a period of 6 to 12 months and eventually establish a sense of normalcy in their lives, for some, this process becomes prolonged and problematic.[4] Although most people adjust to loss gradually and without conscious effort, deviations from this natural trajectory can occur.[5]

In the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5(, severe and enduring grief reactions that significantly impair daily functioning are classified as Persistent Complex Bereavement Disorder (PCBD).[6] Additionally, the DSM-5, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR) introduced Prolonged Grief Disorder (PGD) as follows: an abnormal and prolonged sense of sadness following the death of a loved one, lasting for at least 12 months (or 6 months for children and adolescents), and is distinct from normal grief reactions. This disorder is typically associated with the death of someone with whom the bereaved individual had a close relationship.[7] PGD is a prevalent mental health condition among adults, with estimates suggesting that between 9.8% and 21.5% of individuals who experience loss develop PGD.[8]

The phenomenon of grief holds significant importance due to the intricate psychological responses it elicits following the loss of loved ones.[9] There is a direct correlation between grief symptoms and various domains, including emotional, behavioral, cognitive, physical, and interpersonal functioning.[10] PGD is linked to adverse outcomes, heightening the risk of mortality from any cause.[11] Fear, anger, guilt, sadness, emptiness, low self-worth, and despair are among the seven initial distressing emotions experienced during grief, which may persist into prolonged grief beyond the first year.[12]

During the grieving process, individuals may grapple with feelings of anger, a complex and intense emotion that can manifest in various situations.[13] Brown et al.[14] identified anger as the initial emotion triggered by bereavement. This emotion often arises from feelings of disappointment, loss, threats to one’s dignity and well-being, and a sense of losing control over a situation, all of which are common experiences during grief. As such, anger is recognized as an integral component of the grieving journey.[13]

The passing of a loved one frequently evokes distressing emotions and thoughts. Alongside feelings of regret and sorrow, individuals commonly experience anxiety, guilt, or anger. Those who are bereaved often resist accepting the reality of death and naturally engage in imagining alternative scenarios where their loved one is still alive. Furthermore, many survivors grapple with feelings of guilt.[15]

Another psychological repercussion of grief is self-harm or self-destructive behavior. This pattern of behavior has been observed in individuals over extended periods and can culminate in isolation, reliance on substances, such as drugs or alcohol, or even suicidal ideation.[16]

Fortunately, there are numerous psychological interventions available for PGD, encompassing both individual and group-based approaches. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have demonstrated the effectiveness of psychological interventions in alleviating bereavement symptoms among grieving adults.[17]

Various psychological interventions can be employed for individuals experiencing grief, drawing from a range of therapeutic modalities in the field. These include cognitive–behavioral therapy, mindfulness-based approaches, pharmacotherapy, existential therapy, schema therapy, and acceptance and commitment therapy.[18192021222324] Given that grief profoundly impacts the emotions of bereaved individuals,[25] and considering that emotional distress and challenges in emotion regulation significantly contribute to the manifestation of grief disorder symptoms,[26] it is noteworthy that difficulties in emotion regulation and diminished perceived self-efficacy are associated with heightened severity of grief disorder symptoms.[27] In light of its humanistic orientation, EFT has been deemed suitable for addressing symptoms of grief disorder.[28]

This approach constitutes a brief, systematic model that integrates intrapersonal and interpersonal dimensions. Rooted in humanistic and ontological psychotherapy, it views emotions as pivotal to psychological functioning, dysfunction, and transformation.[29] Alongside employing effective therapeutic techniques, such as empathy, the empty chair technique, and two-chair conversations to address unresolved issues related to the deceased individual, the approach is underpinned by humanistic principles, including positive regard for clients and the provision of empathy. These elements render it particularly effective in working with the bereaved population.[28]

In the context of grief and loss, EFT aims to facilitate the processing of complex emotions and address impediments that disrupt the grieving process.[30] Central to this treatment are techniques, such as empathy and validation, which serve as forms of support for clients and foster their empowerment.[31] Thus, the provision of emotional support through empathy and validation, inherent in EFT, contributes to its heightened effectiveness.[32]

The effectiveness and therapeutic utility of EFT have been validated across various psychological disorders and conditions, encompassing childhood problems, emotional injuries, anxiety, and trauma.[333435363738] Furthermore, research outcomes have demonstrated that EFT is efficacious in mitigating depression, and addressing issues, such as divorce, infidelity, and marital boredom, among others.[39404142]

In a study titled “Effectiveness of Adolescent-Centered Mindfulness (MBCT) and EFT on Self-Esteem and Sleep Disorder of Bereaved Adolescents,” Taheri et al.[43] found that EFT enhanced self-esteem and alleviated sleep problems among bereaved adolescents, indicating significant effectiveness. Similarly, Davis et al.[28] researched the use of EFT techniques for enhancing identity integration and resolving grief among children, concluding that such techniques play a constructive role in these individuals’ lives.

In a comparative study examining the effectiveness of EFT and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy on bereavement symptoms in women affected by grief disorder stemming from COVID-19, Javidnia et al.[32] revealed that EFT significantly outperformed mindfulness-based therapy across various components of bereavement symptoms, including disappointment, fearful behavior, blame and anger, withdrawal, and confusion.

In another study, Watson[30] investigated the efficacy of employing the empty chair technique to facilitate emotional processing within the realm of bereavement. His findings revealed the effectiveness of this technique. Similarly, Elliott concluded that the utilization of reprocessing tasks proved effective once again in addressing grief-related issues.[30]

The effectiveness of EFT on grief is an area that has been understudied. Given that the loss of loved ones is a pervasive issue affecting all of humanity, further research in this domain is necessary. Additionally, among the existing grief-focused studies, very few have examined the symptoms of long-term grief (defined as grief lasting over 1 year). Importantly, none of the previous studies have specifically examined the impact of EFT on the variables of anger, guilt, and self-destructive behaviors, which are often associated with PGD.

The present study aimed to investigate the effectiveness of EFT in reducing symptoms of anger, guilt, and self-destructive behaviors in individuals suffering from long-term grief.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

The research design was a clinical trial with a pre-test, post-test, and 3-month follow-up, utilizing a single-blind, parallel-group approach from November 2022 to November 2023. The target population consisted of individuals who had experienced the loss of a loved one at least 1 year ago. To recruit the statistical sample, individuals in Tehran, Iran who had lost a close person at least 1 year ago were invited to participate in the study through virtual platforms (e.g., Instagram, Telegram, WhatsApp).

Study participants and sampling

The age of eligible participants were between 18 and 60 years (20 people in the control group and 20 people in the experimental group). A diagnostic interview (5-RV: DSM-5-SCID) was conducted online by a PhD candidate in clinical psychology, using a single-blind method. The statistical analyst and the interviewer who conducted the pre-test, post-test, and follow-up assessments were blinded, and only one person had access to the confidential participant information, which was coded numerically.

The sample size was calculated using the G*Power software, considering a medium effect size (f = 0.50), α = 0.05, power = 0.85, and two control and intervention groups. This resulted in a total sample size of 40 participants, with 20 individuals randomly assigned to the intervention group and 20 to the control group [Figure 1]. Randomization was performed using a simple randomization method via Microsoft Excel’s RAND function.

Data collection tool and technique

The following assessment tools were used in this study:

Grief Experience Questionnaire (GEQ): The GEQ was designed by Barrett and Scott in 1989 to measure various components of grief.[44] The original scale had 55 items and assessed 11 grief-related factors, including physical reactions, general grief reactions, search for an explanation, loss of support, self-labeling, guilt, responsibility, shame, rejection, self-destructive behavior, and unique reactions. The internal consistency of the GEQ, measured by Cronbach’s alpha, was 0.97 for the entire instrument. The Cronbach alpha coefficients for the individual factors ranged from 0.68 to 0.89.[44] The GEQ has been standardized for use in the Iranian context by Mehdipour et al.’s[45] study. Their research reported a Cronbach alpha of 0.88 for the overall questionnaire and values ranging from 0.40 to 0.86 for the individual components.

SCID-5-RV: SCID-5-RV is a guide for semi-structured interviews for the major DSM-5 diagnoses. The task is carried out by a skilled clinician or healthcare professional who is knowledgeable about the diagnostic criteria and classification of disorders in DSM-5.[46] The SCID-5-RV is generally administered in a single 45–90-min session.[47] The Persian version of the SCID-5-RV indicated an acceptable range for internal consistency (0.95–0.99), test–retest reliability (0.60–0.79), and kappa reliability (0.57–0.72).[48]

State-Trait Anger Expression Inventory-2 (STAXI-2):

The STAXI-2 is a self-report questionnaire developed by Spielberger in 1997 and revised in 1999 to assess the experience, expression, and control of anger.[49] The instrument consists of 57 items, including six main scales, five subscales, and an anger expression index. The six main scales are State Anger, Trait Anger, Anger Expression-Out, Anger Expression-In, Anger Control-Out, and Anger Control-In. The State Anger scale has three subscales that measure general feelings of anger, verbal expressions of anger, and physical expressions of anger. The trait anger scale has two subscales that assess angry mood and angry reactions. The anger expression and anger control scales measure how individuals tend to express and control their anger.[50]

The STAXI-2 has demonstrated strong psychometric properties. The alpha coefficients for the state anger and trait anger scales are higher than 0.84 (mean: 0.88). For the anger expression (anger externalization, anger internalization) and anger control (anger externalization control, anger internalization control) scales, as well as the overall anger index, the alpha coefficients are higher than 0.73 (mean = 0.82).[51] The questionnaire is scored on a 4-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 69 to 345. The scoring categories include low (16–80), moderate (11–55), high (5-25), and very high (5–25) levels of anger. The STAXI-2 is a well-validated and widely used measure that provides a comprehensive assessment of various dimensions of anger, making it a suitable tool for evaluating anger-related constructs in the current research context. Cronbach’s alpha of this questionnaire was calculated as 0.76 in the present study.

Guilt Inventory:

The Guilt Inventory is a self-report questionnaire developed to measure various aspects of guilt. The instrument was created, completed, and modified by Kugler and Jones between 1988 and 1992.[52] The Guilt Inventory consists of 45 items and assesses three subscales: Trait Guilt, State Guilt, and Moral Standards. The responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale. The validity of the Guilt Inventory’s factor structure has been confirmed by its creators. The reliability of the subscales, assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, yielded the following results: Trait Guilt (α = 0.78), State Guilt (α = 0.79), Moral Standards (α = 0.76), and Overall Guilt Feeling (α = 0.86).[52]

The convergent validity of the Guilt Inventory was also supported by Naziri (1995).[53] The test-retest reliability of the subscales was found to be: Trait Guilt (r = 0.71), State Guilt (r = 0.68), Moral Standards (r = 0.74), and Overall Guilt Feeling (r = 0.79).[53] The Guilt Inventory is a well-established and psychometrically sound measure that provides a comprehensive assessment of different facets of guilt. Its inclusion in the current research protocol allows for a detailed evaluation of guilt-related constructs among the study participants. Cronbach’s alpha of this questionnaire was calculated as 0.62 in the present study.

Self-Harm Inventory (SHI):

The Self-Harm Inventory (SHI) is a 22-item self-report questionnaire developed by Sansone, Wiederman, and Sansone in 1998.[54] The instrument is designed to assess an individual’s history of self-harm behaviors, including attempted suicide, overdose, intentional self-cutting, self-immolation, and engaging in relationships to be rejected. The response format for the SHI is a binary yes/no answer. Respondents indicate whether they have intentionally or purposefully engaged in each of the listed self-harm behaviors. A “no” response is scored as 0, whereas a “yes” response is scored as 1. The validity of the SHI has been established through research. A study found that using a cut-off score of five or more on the SHI accurately classified 84% of individuals who were diagnosed with borderline personality disorder based on a clinical interview.[54] Furthermore, a study by Hosseinzadeh et al.[55] (2013) reported Cronbach’s alpha of 0.74 for the SHI, indicating good internal consistency reliability. The SHI is a concise and efficient self-report measure that provides a comprehensive assessment of an individual’s past and present self-destructive behaviors. Its inclusion in the current research protocol allows for the identification of self-destructive history among the study participants, which is an important factor to consider in the context of the research objectives. Cronbach’s alpha of this questionnaire was calculated as 0.64 in the present study.

Ethical consideration

This research study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences with Ethics no. IR.SBMU.MSP.REC.1400.556. The study was also registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT) with the code IRCT20231103059943N1.

Measures

After the completion of the EFT, both the experimental and control groups (who did not receive any treatment) were assessed using three questionnaires to measure the effectiveness of the intervention. Furthermore, a 3-month follow-up assessment was conducted, where the same three questionnaires were administered to both groups to evaluate the stability and long-term effects of the EFT treatment. This follow-up assessment allowed the researchers to ensure that the observed improvements in the experimental group were maintained and not reversible over time. The inclusion of the 3-month follow-up assessment is an important aspect of the research design, as it allows for a more comprehensive evaluation of the efficacy and sustainability of the EFT intervention compared to the control group.

Data analysis

Descriptive and inferential statistics indices were used in data analysis. After collecting the data, the mean and standard deviation of the studied variables were first calculated in the descriptive statistics section, and then in the inferential statistics section, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was used to compare the two groups with pre-test control. Repeated measure analysis was used to investigate the effect of time and the changes in the measured outcomes over time. A significance level of less than 0.05 was used. SPSS version 24 was also used for data analysis.

Discussion

The loss of a loved one is often considered a significant stressful life event that can have a significant impact on the health and well-being of the bereaved.[57] The present study aimed to examine the effectiveness of EFT in reducing anger, guilt, and self-destructive behaviors in individuals experiencing PGD.

The results of this study showed statistically significant changes in the treatment group after the intervention and during the follow-up phase regarding anger in individuals with PGD. One explanation for the reduction in anger is that a common issue in psychotherapy is unresolved anger from the past, often toward a significant person in the individual’s life. This unfinished emotional business can be a major source of psychological distress.[56] In EFT, the therapist helps the client adaptively experience and express emotions such as anger and sadness, thereby allowing them to serve their intended function in addressing distressing situations.[58] This finding is consistent with the research conducted by Nameni, Mohammadpoor, and Noori, which confirmed the effectiveness of EFT in reducing anger.[59]

This research also found that there were statistically significant changes in the treatment group after the intervention and during the follow-up phase regarding the variable of guilt in individuals with PGD. The EFT includes methods of self-compassion and self-kindness, which can help explain the reduction in guilt among the bereaved. Additionally, self-compassion helps individuals feel a greater sense of belonging and security, leading to a better quality of life.[60] This finding is consistent with the research of Kurian,[61] Khosravi Asl, Nasirian, and Bakhshayesh,[62] which confirmed the effectiveness of EFT in reducing guilt.

Finally, this study showed a significant reduction in self-destructive behavior scores compared to the control group. According to research by Amongin et al. (2012), impulsivity is higher in individuals with grief disorders than in the general population.[63] This finding aligns with the research of Bentley et al.,[64] and Jabari and Aghili,[65] which concluded that EFT is effective in reducing self-destructive behaviors.

Overall, the results in patients with PGD showed statistically significant changes in the outcome variables after treatment, including reductions in anger, guilt, and self-destructive behaviors compared to the control groups. These findings align with the results from studies by Paivio et al.,[58] Javidnia et al.,[32] Davis et al.,[28] Sharbanee and Greenberg,[30] and Greenberg.[56] They indicated the effectiveness of EFT in improving the psychological symptoms of grief disorders. It is worth noting, however, that the mentioned studies examined different sample populations compared to the present study. The current research focused on the effectiveness of EFT on PGD (lasting more than one year), whereas the aforementioned studies examined individuals experiencing grief in general.

The effectiveness of the treatment can be explained by the processes underlying this therapy. The EFT aims to access the core of the pain, identify the associated needs, and repair the damaged emotion to reduce emotional pain and improve emotion regulation. In the intervention, efforts were made to create a secure relationship reinforced by the therapist’s empathy and validation of emotional messages to assess the emotional pain caused by the loss. Subsequently, using experiential techniques, such as the two-chair dialogue, the emotions and needs associated with the pain were re-experienced and adapted.

Given that these techniques often involve re-enacting past traumas related to the deceased and leading to adaptive responses such as forgiveness and self-compassion, this intervention has likely helped reduce grief symptoms, such as anger and others. Additionally, it is worth noting that part of the reason for grief symptoms is related to dependency issues on the deceased and the emergence of avoidance behaviors, such as avoiding acceptance of the deceased’s death.[66] In this context, EFT strives to access the attachment-based relationship with the deceased and facilitate the expression of emotions related to this relationship, leading to adaptive experiences like compassion, forgiveness, and validation toward oneself and the deceased.[67]

Limitations and recommendation

The main limitation of this research was the gender of the sample. Most participants were women, which limited the generalization and interpretation of the results to the opposite gender. Additionally, the sample size was relatively small. Another limitation of this study was the use of self-reporting tools.

Future research should attempt to replicate and extend these findings and their generalizability to the opposite gender. Additionally, future studies could be conducted with a larger sample size. Moreover, further research is needed to examine the impact of providing emotion-focused treatment in a group format. The advantage of group therapy is that a large number of individuals can be treated at the same time. This research can be used in counseling centers and psychotherapy services as part of the treatment for individuals experiencing grief.

Conclusion

Therefore, EFT sounds to be a hopeful intervention for individuals suffering from PGD, as it has shown effectiveness in improving anger, guilt, and self-destructive behaviors. Considering the promising findings for EFT, counselors, and psychotherapists may consider this therapy as a treatment option when working with grieving patients.

Based on this study, EFT is recommended as a practical and effective psychotherapy for patients with PGD. This therapy can be combined with other medical treatments that are beneficial for this population. This approach provides a promising foundation for further development of treatment.
