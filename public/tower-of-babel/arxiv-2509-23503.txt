Unifying local and nonlocal corrosion frameworks: A convergent nonlocal extension of the KKS phase-field model
Christian J. Cyron 1,2 , Marvin Fritz 3 , Alexander Hermann 1,4,∗ , Tobias Köppl 5 , Arman Shojaei 1 , Stewart Silling 6

Abstract
We introduce a nonlocal extension of the Kim-Kim-Suzuki (KKS) phase-field corrosion model aimed at bridging local and nonlocal corrosion modeling approaches, such as phase-field and peridynamic frameworks. In this formulation, classical gradient operators are replaced with integral operators defined over a finite interaction horizon, naturally embedding an intrinsic length scale that aligns with nonlocal theories like peridynamics. Under precise assumptions on function spaces and kernel functions, we define a nonlocal free energy that integrates a standard bulk free energy density with a nonlocal interaction term. Through differentiation in an appropriate Hilbert space, we derive evolution equations, yielding a nonlocal Allen–Cahn equation for the phase-field and a nonlocal Cahn–Hilliard-type equation for the concentration. The latter is expressed as a gradient flow in a metric induced by the inverse of the nonlocal operator, mirroring the classical H − 1 H^{-1} metric for conserved dynamics. We establish the well-posedness of these equations using Galerkin approximations, uniform energy estimates, and compactness arguments. Furthermore, we prove the convergence of the nonlocal model to its local KKS counterpart as the interaction horizon approaches zero, effectively unifying local and nonlocal perspectives. Numerical experiments, implemented via finite difference spatial discretization and explicit time-stepping, demonstrate the effects of nonlocality and confirm the theoretical convergence, reinforcing the connection between the two modeling paradigms.

Introduction

Nonlocal phase-field models have attracted significant attention in scientific computing because they naturally incorporate long-range interactions and introduce an intrinsic length scale into the governing equations
[
39
]
. Unlike classical local formulations that employ differential operators—which capture only infinitesimal interactions—nonlocal formulations replace these operators with integral operators that account for interactions over a finite horizon. This replacement not only provides a natural regularization mechanism for evolving interfaces but also establishes a direct connection with peridynamic theories, where the interaction horizon is a fundamental parameter
[
39
]
.

In many applications, including phase transitions
[
11
]
and corrosion phenomena (e.g., in biodegradable magnesium implants
[
25
,
29
]
), classical local models fail to capture important multiscale effects due to the absence of an intrinsic length scale. For instance, in the two-phase metal–electrolyte setting of corrosion, the classical KKS (Kim–Kim–Suzuki) phase-field model
[
27
]
—with a free energy comprising a bulk term and a gradient term—does not explicitly incorporate a finite spatial interaction range. Recent studies have demonstrated that introducing nonlocal interactions via integral operators can significantly alter interface dynamics, sometimes even producing phenomena that are not observed in local theory
[
10
,
36
,
20
]
.

Peridynamic models, initially proposed by Silling
[
39
]
, are reformulations of
classical local continuum mechanics in the form of nonlocal integral differential
equations. With the spatial derivatives of the stress tensor replaced by integrals of
force density functions, a nonlocal model allows the natural treatment of balance
laws on and off material discontinuities. We refer to the overview articles
[
40
,
41
]
and the books
[
4
,
28
]
on perdiynamic modeling.

Motivated by these developments and rigorous convergence results in nonlocal-to-local settings of Cahn–Hilliard and Allen–Cahn models
[
1
,
12
,
17
,
31
]
, we present in this work a rigorous nonlocal formulation of the KKS phase-field corrosion model. Our approach is based on defining a free energy functional that couples a standard bulk free energy density with a nonlocal interaction term characterized by a finite cut-off radius, akin to those employed in peridynamic models
[
39
]
and in tumor growth models with long-range interaction radius
[
19
,
20
]
. We rigorously derive the associated evolution equations via differentiation in a suitable Hilbert space framework. Our derivation yields a nonlocal Allen–Cahn equation for the phase-field and a nonlocal Cahn–Hilliard-type equation for the concentration, the latter formulated as a gradient flow in a metric induced by the inverse of the nonlocal operator, analogous to the classical
H^{-1}
-metric that naturally enforces mass conservation.

While our analysis, based on Galerkin approximations, uniform energy estimates, and compactness arguments, establishes the well-posedness of both equations and demonstrates that, under standard smoothness assumptions and proper scaling, the nonlocal model converges to its classical local counterpart, our primary focus is on clarifying the impact of the finite interaction horizon on the solution’s behavior. Furthermore, we highlight a structural equivalence between the nonlocal KKS model and peridynamic corrosion models under specific parameter regimes, thereby bridging phase-field and peridynamic approaches to corrosion modeling.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
we introduce the mathematical framework and state our assumptions. Section
3
reviews the classical local KKS phase-field corrosion model. Furthermore, we introduce the nonlocal free energy functional and derive the corresponding evolution equations via differentiation. In Section
4
, we prove the existence and uniqueness of the decoupled and fully coupled nonlocal models. Section
5
discusses the nonlocal-to-local convergence of the derived operators, while Section
6
presents our finite difference spatial discretization and explicit time-stepping scheme and presents numerical experiments on a two-dimensional pitting corrosion benchmark, and Section
7
concludes with a discussion and outlook for future work.

3
Local and nonlocal evolution equations

In this section, the classical (local) KKS phase-field corrosion model as derived in
[
27
]
is reviewed. Next, its nonlocal extension by computing Gâteaux derivatives of the corresponding nonlocal free energy and formulating nonlocal gradient flows are derived.

3.1
Local KKS phase-field corrosion model

In the classical formulation of the KKS phase-field corrosion model, one defines a Ginzburg–Landau type local free energy functional by

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{local}}[\phi,c]=\int_{\Omega}\Big\{f(\phi,c)+\frac{\alpha_{\phi}}{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2}+\frac{\alpha_{c}}{2}|\nabla c|^{2}\Big\}\,\textup{d}x,

where the free energy density
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R})
is as specified in Assumption
3
. In this context, the parameters
\alpha_{\phi},\alpha_{c}>0
quantify the energetic penalty associated with spatial variations in
\phi
and
c
, respectively, and thus control the thickness of the interfacial layer between the phases. The evolution of the phase-field
\phi
is derived as the
L^{2}
- gradient flow of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{local}}
, and therefore the resulting equation is of Allen–Cahn type. However, the evolution of the concentration
c
is obtained as an
H^{-1}
-gradient flow (which guarantees mass conservation), leading to a Cahn–Hilliard type equation. Specifically, one obtains the coupled Allen–Cahn/Cahn–Hilliard system

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi

\displaystyle=-L\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{local}}}{\delta\phi},\qquad\partial_{t}c=\nabla\cdot\left\{M\nabla\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{local}}}{\delta c}\right\},

(6)

where
L>0
is the kinetic coefficient for the phase-field evolution and
M>0
is the mobility constant for the concentration evolution. A direct computation shows that

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{local}}}{\delta\phi}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\phi}f(\phi,c)-\alpha_{\phi}\Delta\phi,\qquad\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{local}}}{\delta c}=\partial_{c}f(\phi,c)-\alpha_{c}\Delta c.

Thus, the local KKS phase phield corrosion model takes the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi

\displaystyle=-L\Bigl\{\partial_{\phi}f(\phi,c)-\alpha_{\phi}\Delta\phi\Bigr\},

(7)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}c

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\Bigl\{M\nabla\Bigl(\partial_{c}f(\phi,c)-\alpha_{c}\Delta c\Bigr)\Bigr\}.

(8)

These equations have to be interpreted in a weak sense (with appropriate initial and boundary conditions) and, under standard assumptions, one can establish well-posedness (see, e.g.,
[
29
]
). Note that the classical model does not incorporate an additional length scale beyond that determined by
\alpha_{\phi}
, which motivates the nonlocal formulation.

3.2
Nonlocal KKS phase-field corrosion model

To incorporate a finite interaction horizon and introduce an intrinsic length scale, we replace local gradient terms by nonlocal integral operators. The nonlocal Helmholtz-type free energy, see e.g.
[
21
,
22
]
, is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[\phi,c]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}f\bigl(\phi(x),c(x)\bigr)\,\textup{d}x+\frac{\alpha_{\phi}}{4}\iint_{\Omega\times\Omega}J_{\delta}(x-y)[\phi(x)-\phi(y)]^{2}\,\textup{d}(x,y)

\displaystyle+\frac{\alpha_{c}}{4}\iint_{\Omega\times\Omega}J_{\delta}(x-y)[c(x)-c(y)]^{2}\,\textup{d}(x,y),

where
J_{\delta}
is the scaled kernel from Assumption
1
, and
\alpha_{\phi},a_{c}\geq 0
are again penalty-like coefficients. Using the definition of the seminorm in
V_{\delta}
, see (
J4
), we may shortly write

\mathcal{F}[\phi,c]=\int_{\Omega}f\bigl(\phi,c\bigr)\,\textup{d}x+\frac{\alpha_{\phi}}{4}\lvert\phi\rvert_{V_{\delta}}^{2}+\frac{\alpha_{c}}{4}\lvert c\rvert_{V_{\delta}}^{2}.

The Gateaux derivatives (or: first variations) of
\mathcal{F}
at
(\phi,c)
in directions
\eta,\zeta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
are defined by

D_{\phi}\mathcal{F}[\phi,c]\{\eta\}:=\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\mathcal{F}[\phi+\varepsilon\eta,c]\right|_{\varepsilon=0},\quad D_{c}\mathcal{F}[\phi,c]\{\zeta\}:=\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\mathcal{F}[\phi,c+\varepsilon\zeta]\right|_{\varepsilon=0}.

Moreover, the Gateaux derivatives relate to the functional derivatives
\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\phi}
and
\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta c}
of
\mathcal{F}
with repsect to
\phi
and
c
, respectively, as follows

D_{\phi}\mathcal{F}[\phi,c]\{\eta\}=:\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\phi}\eta\,\textup{d}x,\qquad D_{c}\mathcal{F}[\phi,c]\{\zeta\}=:\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta c}\zeta\,\textup{d}x.

First, the Gateaux derivative w.r.t.
\phi
reads

\displaystyle D_{\phi}\mathcal{F}[\phi,c]\{\eta\}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}f(\phi+\varepsilon\eta,c)\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}\,\textup{d}x

\displaystyle+\frac{\alpha_{\phi}}{4}\iint_{\Omega\times\Omega}J_{\delta}(x-y)\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\Bigl[(\phi+\varepsilon\eta)(x)-(\phi+\varepsilon\eta)(y)\Bigr]^{2}\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}\,\textup{d}x\,\textup{d}y,

and a short computation gives

\displaystyle D_{\phi}\mathcal{F}[\phi,c]\{\eta\}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{\phi}f(\phi,c)\eta\,\textup{d}x+\frac{\alpha_{\phi}}{2}\iint_{\Omega\times\Omega}J_{\delta}(x-y)[\phi(x)-\phi(y)][\eta(x)-\eta(y)]\,\textup{d}x\,\textup{d}y

\displaystyle=(\partial_{\phi}f(\phi,c),\eta)_{L^{2}}+\alpha_{\phi}(\phi,\eta)_{V_{\delta}},

which we may rewrite using the nonlocal operator
\mathcal{L}_{\delta}
, see (
2
), as

D_{\phi}\mathcal{F}[\phi,c]\{\eta\}=\int_{\Omega}\bigl\{\partial_{\phi}f(\phi,c)+\alpha_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{\delta}\phi\bigr\}\eta\,\textup{d}x\quad\forall\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

The fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations yields the functional derivative of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
\phi
, that is,

\dfrac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\phi}(x)=\partial_{\phi}f(\phi(x),c(x))+\alpha_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{\delta}\phi.

(9)

Using the same techniques, the Gateaux derivative w.r.t.
c
reads

D_{c}\mathcal{F}[\phi,c]\{\zeta\}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}f(\phi,c+\varepsilon\zeta)\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}\,\textup{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\bigl\{\partial_{c}f(\phi,c)+\alpha_{c}\mathcal{L}_{\delta}c\bigr\}\zeta\,\textup{d}x.

Invoking the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations, it yields the following functional derivative of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
c
:

\dfrac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta c}=\partial_{c}f(\phi,c)+\alpha_{c}\mathcal{L}_{\delta}c.

(10)

We now formulate the nonlocal evolution equations as gradient flows of
\mathcal{F}[\phi,c]
. First, we consider the standard
L^{2}
-gradient flow for
\phi
as in the local KKS model (
6
) but we replace the functional derivative of the local energy
\mathcal{F}_{\text{local}}
by its nonlocal counterpart, that is (
9
), which yields

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi

\displaystyle=-L\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\phi}=-L\bigl\{\partial_{\phi}f(\phi,c)+\alpha_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{\delta}\phi\bigr\}.

(11)

For
\phi
, which is not mass conserved, the natural inner product is the
L^{2}
-scalar product. For
c
, mass conservation requires an
H^{-1}
-type structure. Thus, instead of assuming an
H^{-1}
-gradient flow governing
c
as we have done in the local setting, we consider a nonlocal gradient flow based on the inner product in
V_{\delta}^{*}
. The dual space
V_{\delta}^{*}
(that later becomes the nonlocal
H^{-1}
-type space) has the inner product:

\langle u,v\rangle_{V_{\delta}^{*}}=\langle u,\mathcal{L}_{\delta}^{-1}v\rangle_{L^{2}}=\int_{\Omega}u(x)(\mathcal{L}_{\delta}^{-1}v)(x)\,\textup{d}x.

(12)

Thus, the
V_{\delta}^{*}
gradient flow reads

\partial_{t}c=-M\mathcal{L}_{\delta}\Big(\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta c}\Big)=-M\mathcal{L}_{\delta}\big(\partial_{c}f(\phi,c)+\alpha_{c}\mathcal{L}_{\delta}c\big).

Integration over
\Omega
and using
\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}_{\delta}u\,\textup{d}x=0
shows that the concentration equation conserves mass, that is,
\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\int_{\Omega}c\,\textup{d}x=0
.
To prove energy dissipation, one computes

\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\mathcal{F}[\phi,c]=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\phi}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\,\textup{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta c}\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}\,\textup{d}x.

Substituting
\partial_{t}\phi=-L\delta\mathcal{F}/\delta\phi
and
\partial_{t}c=M\mathcal{L}_{\delta}(-\delta\mathcal{F}/\delta c)
leads to

\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\mathcal{F}[\phi,c]=-L\int_{\Omega}\Bigl|\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\phi}\Bigr|^{2}\,\textup{d}x-M\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta c}\mathcal{L}_{\delta}\Bigl(\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta c}\Bigr)\,\textup{d}x,

from which we conclude dissipativity
\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\mathcal{F}\leq 0
as
J_{\delta}
is symmetric, see Assumption
3
, and hence

\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta c}\mathcal{L}_{\delta}\Bigl(\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta c}\Bigr)\,\textup{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\Omega\times\Omega}J_{\delta}(x-y)\Bigl[\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta c}(x)-\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta c}(y)\Bigr]^{2}\,\textup{d}x\,\textup{d}y\geq 0.

In total, we consider in this work the nonlocal KKS phase-field corrosion model

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi

\displaystyle=-L\bigl[\partial_{\phi}f(\phi,c)+\alpha_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{\delta}\phi\bigr],

(13)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}c

\displaystyle=-M\mathcal{L}_{\delta}[\partial_{c}f(\phi,c)+\alpha_{c}\mathcal{L}_{\delta}c].

(14)

Intuitively, one could say that we simply replaced all Laplace operators
-\Delta
in the local KKS model (
7
)–(
8
) by the nonlocal operator
\mathcal{L}_{\delta}
. And indeed,
in the limit
\delta\to 0
with the properly normalized kernel (see Section
5
), the nonlocal operators converge to the Laplacian and local diffusion, recovering the classical model given by (
7
)-(
8
).

4
Well-posedness analysis

In this section, we establish well-posedness for the nonlocal KKS phase-field corrosion model (
13
)–(
14
). Since in the fully coupled system the nonlocal Allen–Cahn equation (
13
) depends on
c
and the Cahn–Hilliard equation (
14
) depends on
\phi
, we ultimately obtain the well-posedness of a nonlocal coupled Allen–Cahn/Cahn–Hilliard system. We prove the existence, uniqueness, and continuous dependence of weak solutions by constructing Galerkin approximations, deriving uniform energy estimates, and applying compactness arguments to pass to the limits.

Assumption 5
.

We assume:

(A1)

\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{1,2,3\}
, bounded Lipschitz domain,
T>0
is a fixed time;

(A2)

c_{0},\phi_{0}\in L^{2}
;

(A3)

\alpha_{\phi},L,M>0
;

(A4)

f
fulfills Assumption
3
;

(A5)

J_{\delta}
,
\delta>0
, fulfills Assumption
1
;

(A6)

either
\alpha_{c}=0
or
\alpha_{c}\geq 0

\wedge
(
J_{\delta}\in L^{1}

\vee

c_{0}\in V_{\delta}
).

As we will see in the proof, the value of
\alpha_{c}
is delicate. One option is assuming
\alpha_{c}=0
which means that the nonlocal effects solely appear due to the phase-field and then influence the concentration through the coupling mechanism. In the case of
\alpha_{c}>0
, we either require
J_{\delta}\in L^{1}
(which is true for the top-hat but not for the truncated fractional kernel) or
c_{0}\in V_{\delta}
. We begin by stating the definition of a weak solution to (
13
)–(
14
).

Definition 4.1
.

A tuple
(\phi,c)
with the regularity

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;V_{\delta}),\qquad\partial_{t}\phi\in L^{2}(0,T;V_{\delta}^{*}),

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;V_{\delta}),\qquad\partial_{t}c\in L^{2}(0,T;V_{\delta}^{*}),

is a
weak solution
to (
13
)–(
14
) if for a.e.
t\in(0,T)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}\phi(t),v\rangle

\displaystyle=-L\bigl(\partial_{\phi}f(\phi(t),c(t)),v\bigr)_{L^{2}}-\alpha_{\phi}L\bigl(\phi(t),v\bigr)_{V_{\delta}}

\displaystyle\forall v\in V_{\delta},\qquad\phi(0)=\phi_{0},

(15)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}c(t),v\rangle

\displaystyle=-M\bigl(\partial_{c}f(\phi(t),c(t)),v\bigr)_{V_{\delta}}-\alpha_{c}M\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{\delta}c(t),\mathcal{L}_{\delta}v\bigr)_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\forall v\in V_{\delta},\qquad c(0)=c_{0}.

We note that the inner product of the term with
\alpha_{c}
appears using the self-adjointness of
\mathcal{L}_{\delta}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let Assumption
5
hold. Then
there exists a weak solution

(\phi,c)
of (
13
)–(
14
) in the sense of Definition
4.1
which further satisfies
the energy inequality

\|\phi(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|c(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V_{\delta})}^{2}+\|c\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V_{\delta})}^{2}\leq C(T)\big(\|\phi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|c_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\big)\quad\forall t>0.

(16)

In addition, if
J_{\delta}
fulfills Assumption
4
and
(\phi_{1},c_{1})
is a strong solution in the sense that
\phi_{1},c_{1}\in L^{1}(0,T;L^{\infty})
and
(\phi_{2},c_{2})
is a weak solution, then the solutions depend continuously on the data, that is,

\|\phi_{1}(t)-\phi_{2}(t)\|_{L^{2}}+\|c_{1}(t)-c_{2}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|\phi_{1}(0)-\phi_{2}(0)\|_{L^{2}}+\|c_{1}(0)-c_{2}(0)\|_{L^{2}}.

Especially, if the initial data is the same for both solutions, then it holds
(\phi_{1},c_{1})=(\phi_{2},c_{2})
, which implies weak-strong uniqueness.

Proof.

Step 1 (Galerkin approximation).

Since
\mathcal{L}_{\delta}^{-1}:\mathring{L}^{2}\to\mathring{L}^{2}
is self-adjoint and compact (see (
J4
) in Assumption
1
), we invoke the spectral theorem to obtain the existence of an orthonormal basis
(v_{k})_{k\geq 1}
in
\mathring{L}^{2}
, which are further orthogonal in
V_{\delta}
. For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we set
V_{N}:=\operatorname{span}\{v_{1},\dots,v_{N}\}
and

\phi_{N}(t):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}^{(N)}(t)v_{i},\quad c_{N}(t):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}^{(N)}(t)v_{i}

with time-dependent coefficient functions

a^{(N)}_{i},b^{(N)}_{i}\in C^{1}
that shall satisfy the Galerkin system (for any
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
and a.e.
t\in(0,T)
)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}\phi_{N}(t),v)_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=-L\bigl(\partial_{\phi}f(\phi_{N}(t),c_{N}(t)),v\bigr)_{L^{2}}-\alpha_{\phi}L\bigl(\phi_{N}(t),v\bigr)_{V_{\delta}}

\displaystyle\forall v\in V_{N},\quad\phi_{N}(0)=\Pi_{V_{N}}\phi_{0},

(17)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}c_{N}(t),v)_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=-M\bigl(\partial_{c}f(\phi_{N}(t),c_{N}(t)),v\bigr)_{V_{\delta}}-\alpha_{c}M\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{\delta}c_{N}(t),\mathcal{L}_{\delta}v\bigr)_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\forall v\in V_{N},\quad c_{N}(0)=\Pi_{V_{N}}c_{0}.

We may rewrite the Galerkin system via the coefficient functions, using the orthonormality of the basis functions, which gives

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}a^{(N)}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=-L\bigl(\partial_{\phi}f(\phi_{N},c_{N}),v_{j}\bigr)_{L^{2}}-\alpha_{\phi}L\bigl(\phi_{N},v_{j}\bigr)_{V_{\delta}},

\displaystyle a^{(N)}_{j}(0)=(\phi_{0},v_{j})_{L^{2}},

(18)

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}b^{(N)}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=-M\bigl(\partial_{c}f(\phi_{N},c_{N}),v_{j}\bigr)_{V_{\delta}}-\alpha_{c}M\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{\delta}c_{N},\mathcal{L}_{\delta}v_{j}\bigr)_{L^{2}},

\displaystyle b^{(N)}_{j}(0)=(c_{0},v_{j})_{L^{2}}.

As the involved nonlinearities are continuous in the arguments, we invoke the Cauchy–Peano theorem that gives a continuous solution up to some maximal horizon
T_{N}\leq T
, that is,
(\phi_{N},c_{N})\in C([0,T_{N}];V_{N}\times V_{N})
.

Step 2 (Energy estimates).

Testing (
17
)
1
with
v=\phi_{N}(t)
yields

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\lVert\phi_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\alpha_{\phi}L\lvert\phi_{N}\rvert_{V_{\delta}}^{2}+L(\partial_{\phi}f(\phi_{N},c_{N}),\phi_{N})_{L^{2}}=0.

(19)

Using the splitting
f=f_{1}+f_{2}
, see (
F1
), and the growth conditions (
F3
) and semicoercivity (
F4
) of
f
, we have with Young’s and Hölder’s inequality that

\displaystyle\bigl|\bigl(\partial_{\phi}f_{1}(\phi_{N},c_{N}),\phi_{N}\bigr)_{L^{2}}\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}(1+\lvert c_{N}\rvert+\lvert\phi_{N}\rvert,\lvert\phi_{N}\rvert)_{L^{2}}\lesssim 1+\lVert c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert\phi_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},

\displaystyle\bigl(\partial_{\phi}f_{2}(\phi_{N}),\phi_{N}\bigr)_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq C_{6}\lVert\phi_{N}\rVert_{L^{4}}^{4}-C_{7}\lVert\phi_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

We insert this bound back into (
19
) to obtain the following:

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\lVert\phi_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\alpha_{\phi}L\lvert\phi_{N}\rvert_{V_{\delta}}^{2}+LC_{6}\lVert\phi_{N}\rVert_{L^{4}}^{4}\lesssim 1+\lVert\phi_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(20)

On the right-hand side of the inequality is the
L^{2}
-norm of
c_{N}
that we still have to bound. Indeed, testing (
17
)
2
with
v=Kc_{N}(t)
, where
K>0
will be determined later, it yields the estimate

\frac{K}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\lVert c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\alpha_{c}KM\lVert\mathcal{L}_{\delta}c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+MK(\partial_{c}f(\phi_{N},c_{N}),c_{N})_{V_{\delta}}=0.

We make use of the semicoercivity (
F4
) as assumed in Assumption
3
, to conclude

\bigl(\partial_{c}f(\phi_{N},c_{N}),c_{N}\bigr)_{V_{\delta}}\geq C_{4}\lvert c_{N}\rvert_{{V_{\delta}}}^{2}-C_{5}\lvert\phi_{N}\rvert_{{V_{\delta}}}^{2},

and we insert this back into the estimate to deduce

\frac{K}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\lVert c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\alpha_{c}KM\lVert\mathcal{L}_{\delta}c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+C_{4}MK\lvert c_{N}\rvert_{V_{\delta}}^{2}\leq C_{5}MK\lvert\phi_{N}\rvert_{{V_{\delta}}}^{2}.

We add this estimate to (
20
), which gives in total

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\lVert\phi_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\alpha_{\phi}L\lvert\phi_{N}\rvert_{V_{\delta}}^{2}+LC_{6}\lVert\phi_{N}\rVert_{L^{4}}^{4}+\frac{K}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\lVert c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\alpha_{c}KM\lVert\mathcal{L}_{\delta}c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+C_{4}MK\lvert c_{N}\rvert_{V_{\delta}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C+C\lVert\phi_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\lVert c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+MKC_{5}\lvert\phi_{N}\rvert_{{V_{\delta}}}^{2},

where the generic constant
C
is independent of
K
.
At this point, we select
K>0
sufficiently small to ensure the positivity of
\alpha_{\phi}L-MKC_{5}>0
.
Defining
y_{N}(t)=1+\lVert\phi_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+K\lVert c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
yields

\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}y_{N}(t)\leq Cy_{N}(t),

and Gronwall’s inequality
[
5
, Lemma II.4.10]
finally yields

\lVert\phi_{N}(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+K\lVert c_{N}(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\bigl(1+\|\phi_{N}(0)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+K\|c_{N}(0)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\bigr)e^{Ct},\qquad 0\leq t<t_{N}^{\max}.

(21)

At this point, we use that the initial data is chosen as the orthogonal projection onto
V_{N}
, which gives

\|\phi_{N}(0)\|_{L^{2}}=\|\Pi_{V_{N}}\phi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\lVert\phi_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}},

and in the same manner for
c_{N}(0)
.
Hence, bound (
21
) becomes independent of
N
, thus we may argue by the finite time blow-up theorem for ordinary differential
equations to conclude
t_{N}^{\max}\geq T
and thus the
Galerkin solution is global on
[0,T]
. Next, we integrate (
20
) over
(0,T)

and use the estimate (
21
), which yields the
N
-uniform bound:

\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\phi_{N}(t)\rVert_{L^{4}}^{4}+\lvert\phi_{N}(t)\rvert_{V_{\delta}}^{2}+\lvert c_{N}(t)\rvert_{V_{\delta}}^{2}\,\textup{d}t\lesssim 1+\lVert\phi_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert c_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(22)

Step 3 (Optional bound).
We require an additional bound in the case of
\alpha_{c}>0
and
J_{\delta}\notin L^{1}
(e.g. the truncated fractional kernel) as we will see in the later steps. In this case, we further have
\mathcal{L}_{\delta}c_{0}\in L^{2}
according to Assumption
5
. We note that
\mathcal{L}_{\delta}^{2}c_{N}
is a valid test function in (
17
)
2
due to the eigenbasis choice, and thus, we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\lVert\mathcal{L}_{\delta}c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\alpha_{c}M\lVert\mathcal{L}_{\delta}^{2}c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+M(\partial_{c}f(\phi_{N},c_{N}),\mathcal{L}_{\delta}^{2}c_{N})_{L^{2}}=0.

We apply the growth estimate (
F3
) on
\partial_{c}f(\phi_{N},c_{N})
and integrate the inequality in time to obtain with Young’s inequality

\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathcal{L}_{\delta}c_{N}(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\alpha_{c}M\lVert\mathcal{L}_{\delta}^{2}c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}(0,t;L^{2})}^{2}\lesssim 1+\lVert\mathcal{L}_{\delta}c_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}(0,t;L^{2})}^{2}+\lVert\phi_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}(0,t;L^{2})}^{2}.

The right-hand side is uniformly bounded to the previous energy estimate, which yields

\lVert\mathcal{L}_{\delta}c_{N}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}^{2}+\lVert\mathcal{L}_{\delta}^{2}c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}\leq C.

(23)

Step 4 (Time derivative bounds).
Lastly, we derive a bound of the time derivative of
\phi_{N}
and we proceed as in
[
5
, Lemma V.1.6]
. We consider an arbitrary function
\xi\in L^{2}(0,T;V_{\delta})
and its orthogonal projection is given by
\Pi_{V_{N}}\xi:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\xi,w_{i})_{V_{\delta}}w_{i}\in V_{N}
for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
. We test (
18
)
1
with
\Pi_{V_{N}}\xi
, take the sum over
j=1,\dots,N
, take the absolute value and integrate in time to deduce by the Hölder inequality that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\lvert\langle\partial_{t}\phi_{N},\Pi_{V_{N}}\xi\rangle\rvert\,\textup{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\Big|-\alpha_{\phi}L(\phi_{N},\Pi_{V_{N}}\xi)_{V_{\delta}}-L(\partial_{\phi}f(\phi_{N},c),\Pi_{V_{N}}\xi)_{L^{2}}\Big|\,\textup{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert\phi_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}(V_{\delta})}\|\Pi_{V_{N}}\xi\|_{L^{2}(V_{\delta})}+(1+\lvert c_{N}\rvert+\lvert\phi_{N}\rvert^{3},|\Pi_{V_{N}}\xi|)_{L^{2}(L^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert\phi_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}(V_{\delta})}\|\Pi_{V_{N}}\xi\|_{L^{2}(V_{\delta})}+\bigl(1+\lVert c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}(L^{2})}\bigr)\|\Pi_{V_{N}}\xi\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}+\lVert\phi_{N}\rVert_{L^{4}(L^{4})}^{3}\|\Pi_{V_{N}}\xi\|_{L^{4}(L^{4})}

At this point, we use on the left-hand side that the orthogonal projection is self-adjoint to deduce

\langle\partial_{t}\phi_{N},\Pi_{V_{N}}\xi\rangle=\langle\partial_{t}\Pi_{V_{N}}^{*}\phi_{N},\xi\rangle=\langle\partial_{t}\phi_{N},\xi\rangle\quad\forall\xi\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}),

and on the right-hand side that
\|\Pi_{V_{N}}\xi\|_{V_{\delta}}\leq\|\xi\|_{V_{\delta}}
, apply the previous uniform bounds (
21
)–(
22
) and take the supremum over all
\xi\in L^{4}(0,T;V_{\delta})
with
\|\xi\|_{L^{4}(V_{\delta})}\leq 1
to obtain the uniform bound

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}\phi_{N}\|_{L^{4/3}(V_{\delta}^{*})}

\displaystyle\leq C.

(24)

We note that attaining a similar estimate for
\partial_{t}c_{N}
is not as straight-forward due to the term with
\alpha_{c}
which is not bounded by the
V_{\delta}
-norm in the case of the truncated fractional kernel. In the case of
J_{\delta}\in L^{1}
(as for the top-hat kernel), we conclude

\lvert\mathcal{L}_{\delta}v(x)\rvert\leq\int_{\Omega}J_{\delta}(x-y)\lvert v(x)-v(y)\rvert dy\leq\left(\int_{\Omega}J_{\delta}(x-y)dy\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\Omega}J_{\delta}(x-y)\lvert v(x)-v(y)\rvert^{2}dy\right)^{1/2},

and it follows
\|\mathcal{L}_{\delta}\xi\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|\xi\|_{V_{\delta}}
for any
\xi\in V_{\delta}
.
Using this inequality, we proceed as before to deduce

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\lvert\langle\partial_{t}c_{N},\Pi_{V_{N}}\xi\rangle\rvert dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\Big|-\alpha_{c}M(\mathcal{L}_{\delta}c_{N},\mathcal{L}_{\delta}\Pi_{V_{N}}\xi)_{L^{2}}-M(\partial_{c}f(\phi_{N},c_{N}),\Pi_{V_{N}}\xi)_{V_{\delta}}\Big|\,\textup{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert\mathcal{L}_{\delta}c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}(L^{2})}\lVert\mathcal{L}_{\delta}\Pi_{V_{N}}\xi\rVert_{L^{2}(L^{2})}+(1+\lvert\phi_{N}\rvert+\lvert c_{N}\rvert,\lvert\mathcal{L}_{\delta}\Pi_{V_{N}}\xi\rvert)_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(1+\lVert\mathcal{L}_{\delta}c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}(L^{2})}+\lVert\phi_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}(L^{2})}+\lVert c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}(L^{2})}\big)\|\xi\|_{L^{2}(V_{\delta})}.

When
J_{\delta}\notin L^{1}
, then we can use the optional bound (
23
) to conclude

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\lvert\langle\partial_{t}c_{N},\Pi_{V_{N}}\xi\rangle\rvert dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert\mathcal{L}_{\delta}^{2}c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}(L^{2})}\lVert\Pi_{V_{N}}\xi\rVert_{L^{2}(L^{2})}+(1+\lvert\phi_{N}\rvert+\lvert c_{N}\rvert,\lvert\mathcal{L}_{\delta}\Pi_{V_{N}}\xi\rvert)_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(1+\lVert\mathcal{L}_{\delta}^{2}c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}(L^{2})}+\lVert\phi_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}(L^{2})}+\lVert c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}(L^{2})}\big)\|\xi\|_{L^{2}(W_{\delta})},

where
W_{\delta}\subset V_{\delta}
is the space of
L^{2}
-functions
w
with
\|\mathcal{L}_{\delta}w\|_{L^{2}}<\infty
. In the case of
J_{\delta}\in L^{1}
it holds
W_{\delta}=V_{\delta}
. In summary, we obtain

\lVert\partial_{t}c_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}(W_{\delta}^{*})}\leq C.

(25)

Step 5 (Passing to the limits).
By the uniform bounds (
21
)–(
24
), the Eberlein–Šmulian theorem
[
7
, Theorem 3.19]
guarantees subsequences (not relabelled) and limit functions
\phi
and
c
with the weak/weak-
*
convergences

\displaystyle\phi_{N}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup^{*}\phi\text{ in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}),\quad

\displaystyle\phi_{N}\rightharpoonup\phi\text{ in }L^{2}(0,T;V_{\delta})\cap L^{4}(0,T;L^{4}),\quad

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{N}\rightharpoonup\partial_{t}\phi\text{ in }L^{4/3}(0,T;V_{\delta}^{*}),

5.1
Convergence of the nonlocal operator for the phase-field

Let

(0,T)

Especially, the kernel is second-order normalized, see (
J5

\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}J_{\delta}(z)z_{i}z_{j}\,\textup{d}z=\frac{1}{d}\delta_{ij}\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}J_{\delta}(z)\lvert z\rvert^{2}\,\textup{d}z=2\delta_{ij}.

For each fixed
x\in\Omega
and
y\in B_{\delta}(x)
, we define
z=y-x
. A second-order Taylor expansion gives

\phi(x+z)=\phi(x)+\nabla\phi(x)\cdot z+\frac{1}{2}z^{T}D^{2}\phi(x)z+\frac{1}{6}\sum_{i,j,k}\partial_{ijk}\phi\bigl(x+\theta z\bigr)z_{i}z_{j}z_{k},

for some
\theta=\theta(x,z)\in(0,1)
. Hence, we obtain

\phi(x)-\phi(x+z)=-\nabla\phi(x)\cdot z-\frac{1}{2}z^{T}D^{2}\phi(x)z-\frac{1}{6}\sum_{i,j,k}\partial_{ijk}\phi\bigl(x+\theta z\bigr)z_{i}z_{j}z_{k}.

Furthermore, we define the restricted operator

L_{\delta}\phi(x)=\alpha_{\phi}\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}J_{\delta}(z)\bigl[\phi(x)-\phi(x+z)\bigr]\,\textup{d}z.

Lemma 5.1

(Dual-norm convergence of the nonlocal phase operator)
.

If
\phi\in H^{3}(\Omega)
and
\phi
is extended by zero outside
\Omega
, then there is a constant
C>0
, independent of
\delta
, such that

\bigl\|L_{\delta}\phi+\alpha_{\phi}\Delta\phi\bigr\|_{H^{-1}}\leq C\delta\lVert\phi\rVert_{H^{3}}.

Proof.

We fix an arbitrary
v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
with
\lVert v\rVert_{H^{1}}=1
. We have to bound

\langle L_{\delta}\phi+\alpha_{\phi}\Delta\phi,v\rangle=\alpha_{\phi}\int_{\Omega}\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}J_{\delta}(z)\bigl[\phi(x)-\phi(x+z)\bigr]v(x)\,\textup{d}z\,\textup{d}x+\alpha_{\phi}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla v\,\textup{d}x.

We change variables in the double integral and add-subtract the Taylor expansion of
\phi(x+z)
around
x
to deduce

\phi(x+z)=\phi(x)+\nabla\phi(x)\cdot z+\frac{1}{2}z^{T}D^{2}\phi(x)z+\frac{1}{6}\sum_{|\beta|=3}\partial^{\beta}\phi(x+\theta z)z^{\beta},\quad\theta=\theta(x,z)\in(0,1),

where
\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
is a multindex and we use the standard notations of
z^{\beta}=z^{\beta_{1}}\cdot\cdot\cdot z^{\beta_{n}}
and
\partial^{\beta}=\partial^{\beta_{1}}\dots\partial^{\beta_{n}}
.
We substitute this formulation into
L_{\delta}\phi
to obtain

L_{\delta}\phi(x)=-\alpha_{\phi}\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}J_{\delta}(z)\Bigl[\nabla\phi(x)\cdot z+\frac{1}{2}z^{T}D^{2}\phi(x)z+\mathcal{R}(x,z)\Bigr]\,\textup{d}z,

where we defined the remainder

\mathcal{R}(x,z)=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{|\beta|=3}\partial^{\beta}\phi(x+\theta z)z^{\beta}\quad\theta\in(0,1).

By symmetry, we deduce that

\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}J_{\delta}(z)\nabla\phi(x)\cdot z\,\textup{d}z=0,\qquad\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}J_{\delta}(z)z_{i}z_{j}\,\textup{d}z=\frac{2}{d}\delta_{ij},

from which we conclude

L_{\delta}\phi(x)=-\alpha_{\phi}\Delta\phi(x)-\alpha_{\phi}\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}J_{\delta}(z)\mathcal{R}(x,z)\,\textup{d}z.

Therefore, we obtain

\langle L_{\delta}\phi+\alpha_{\phi}\Delta\phi,v\rangle=-\alpha_{\phi}\int_{\Omega}\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}J_{\delta}(z)\mathcal{R}(x,z)v(x)\,\textup{d}z\,\textup{d}x.

We apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in
(x,z)
together with the estimate

\lVert\mathcal{R}(\cdot,z)\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\lVert\phi\rVert_{H^{3}(\Omega)}\lvert z\rvert^{3},

and the second-order normalization property (
J5
) to get

\displaystyle\lvert\langle L_{\delta}\phi+\alpha_{\phi}\Delta\phi,v\rangle\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{\phi}C\Bigl(\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}\lVert\mathcal{R}(\cdot,z)\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\lvert z\rvert^{-2}J_{\delta}(z)\,\textup{d}z\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\int_{\Omega}\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}J_{\delta}(z)\lvert z\rvert^{2}|v(x)|^{2}\,\textup{d}z\,\textup{d}x\Bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert\phi\rVert_{H^{3}}\lvert z\rvert_{\infty}\Bigl(\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}\lvert z\rvert^{2}J_{\delta}(z)\,\textup{d}z\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\int_{\Omega}\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}J_{\delta}(z)\lvert z\rvert^{2}|v(x)|^{2}\,\textup{d}z\,\textup{d}x\Bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta\lVert\phi\rVert_{H^{3}}\lVert v\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

Finally, the supremum over
\lVert v\rVert_{H^{1}}=1
yields the claimed
H^{-1}
-bound.
∎

Proposition 5.1

(Weak solution convergence)
.

Let
(\phi_{\delta},c_{\delta})
be the solutions of the nonlocal KKS model (
13
)–(
14
) with spatial regularity

\phi_{\delta}\in C^{3}(\overline{\Omega}),\quad g_{\delta}:=\partial_{c}f(\phi_{\delta},c_{\delta})\in C^{3}(\overline{\Omega}),\qquad\sup_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}\max_{|\alpha|\leq 3}\bigl|\partial^{\alpha}\phi_{\delta}(x)\bigr|+\bigl|\partial^{\alpha}g_{\delta}(x)\bigr|<\infty.

Then as
\delta\to 0
, it yields

L_{\delta}\phi_{\delta}\to-\alpha_{\phi}\Delta\phi\quad\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}),\quad\mathcal{L}_{\delta}\partial_{c}f(\phi_{\delta},c_{\delta})\to\Delta\partial_{c}f(\phi,c)\quad\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}).

Consequently, any weak-limit point
(\phi,c)
satisfies the classical local KKS system in the
H^{-1}
-weak sense.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.1
, for each
t
,

\|L_{\delta}\phi_{\delta}(t)+\alpha_{\phi}\Delta\phi_{\delta}(t)\|_{H^{-1}}\leq C\delta\|\phi_{\delta}(t)\|_{H^{3}}.

The uniform
H^{3}
-bounds and integration in time yield:

L_{\delta}\phi_{\delta}+\alpha_{\phi}\Delta\phi_{\delta}\to 0\quad\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}).

Since
\phi_{\delta}\rightharpoonup\phi
weakly in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{3})
, we have
\Delta\phi_{\delta}\rightharpoonup\Delta\phi
weakly in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1})
. Hence, for any test function
\psi\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0})
:

\langle L_{\delta}\phi_{\delta},\psi\rangle=-\alpha_{\phi}\langle\Delta\phi_{\delta},\psi\rangle+\langle L_{\delta}\phi_{\delta}+\alpha_{\phi}\Delta\phi_{\delta},\psi\rangle.

The second term tends to 0 by the convergence above, and the first term converges to
-\alpha_{\phi}\langle\Delta\phi,\psi\rangle
by weak convergence. Thus,

L_{\delta}\phi_{\delta}\to-\alpha_{\phi}\Delta\phi\quad\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}).

The identical argument applies to
\mathcal{L}_{\delta}\partial_{c}f(\phi_{\delta},c_{\delta})
. Passing to limits in the nonlocal weak form yields the classical local weak formulation.
∎

Consequently, the nonlocal Allen–Cahn equation

\partial_{t}\phi_{\delta}=-L\Bigl\{\partial_{\phi}f(\phi,c)+\alpha_{\phi}\int_{B_{\delta}(x)}J_{\delta}(x-y)[\phi(x)-\phi(y)]\,\textup{d}y\Bigr\}

converges, as
\delta\to 0
, to the classical local Allen–Cahn equation

\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}=-L\Bigl\{\partial_{\phi}f(\phi,c)-\alpha_{\phi}\Delta\phi\Bigr\},

with error
\mathcal{O}(\delta)
in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

5.2
Convergence of the nonlocal operator for the concentration evolution

Next, we consider the nonlocal concentration evolution,

\partial_{t}c=M\int_{B_{\delta}(x)}J_{\delta}(x-y)\Bigl[g(y)-g(x)\Bigr]\,\textup{d}y,\quad g(x):=\partial_{c}f\bigl(\phi(x),c(x)\bigr).

We assume
g\in C^{3}(\overline{\Omega})
with
|g|_{W^{3,\infty}}<\infty
and
for each fixed
x
and
y\in B_{\delta}(x)
, we define
z=y-x
. Again, using Taylor’s extension gives

g(x+z)=g(x)+\nabla g(x)\cdot z+\frac{1}{2}z^{T}D^{2}g(x)z+\frac{1}{6}\sum_{i,j,k}\partial_{ijk}g\bigl(x+\theta z\bigr)z_{i}z_{j}z_{k},\quad\theta=\theta(x,z)\in(0,1).

Then

g(x+z)-g(x)=\nabla g(x)\cdot z+\frac{1}{2}z^{T}D^{2}g(x)z+\frac{1}{6}\sum_{i,j,k}\partial_{ijk}g\bigl(x+\theta z\bigr)z_{i}z_{j}z_{k}.

Since
J_{\delta}
is radial, the linear term integrates to zero and the cubic-order term also vanishes by symmetry. Therefore

\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}J_{\delta}(z)[g(x+z)-g(x)]\,\textup{d}z=\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}J_{\delta}(z)z^{T}D^{2}g(x)z\,\textup{d}z+R_{\delta}^{g}(x),

where the exact error is

R_{\delta}^{g}(x)=\frac{1}{6}\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}J_{\delta}(z)\sum_{i,j,k}\partial_{ijk}g\bigl(x+\theta z\bigr)z_{i}z_{j}z_{k}\,\textup{d}z,

and

\bigl|R_{\delta}^{g}(x)\bigr|\leq\frac{1}{6}\Bigl(\sup_{|\alpha|=3}\|\partial^{\alpha}g\|_{L^{\infty}}\Bigr)\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}J_{\delta}(z)\lvert z\rvert^{3}\,\textup{d}z=\mathcal{O}(\delta).

By the same isotropy argument, we deduce

\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}J_{\delta}(z)z^{T}D^{2}g(x)z\,\textup{d}z=\sum_{i,j}\partial_{ij}g(x)\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}J_{\delta}(z)z_{i}z_{j}\,\textup{d}z=2\Delta g(x).

Hence, we conclude

\int_{B_{\delta}(x)}J_{\delta}(x-y)\bigl[g(y)-g(x)\bigr]\,\textup{d}y=\Delta g(x)+\widetilde{R}_{\delta}(x),\quad\|\widetilde{R}_{\delta}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}=\mathcal{O}(\delta).

It follows that

\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}(x,t)=M\Delta g(x)+\mathcal{O}(\delta)=M\Delta\Bigl[\partial_{c}f(\phi,c)\Bigr]+\mathcal{O}(\delta),

in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
Equivalently, as
\delta\to 0
,

\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}(x,t)\longrightarrow\nabla\bigl(M\nabla[\partial_{c}f(\phi,c)]\bigr)(x,t),

with error
\mathcal{O}(\delta)
in
L^{\infty}
. In particular, no extra
\delta^{-2}
rescaling is needed once the kernel is normalized as above.

Remark 5.1

(Weak solution convergence)
.

Although we assumed
C^{3}
-regularity for pointwise
\mathcal{O}(\delta)
error, one can show for general weak solutions that

L_{\delta}\phi\to-\alpha_{\phi}\Delta\phi\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{L}_{\delta}\mu\to\Delta\mu\quad\text{in }H^{-1}(\Omega)

as
\delta\to 0
. Consequently, the nonlocal gradient-flow system converges in the weak
H^{-1}
-sense to the classical KKS equations without requiring
C^{3}
hypotheses.

Remark 5.2

(Sharp-interface limit and peridynamic diffusion)
.

We want to comment on the scenario when one fixes the macroscopic interaction horizon

\delta>0
and lets the diffuse-interface width

\varepsilon\in(0,\delta/2)
tend to zero. A rigorous justification of this formal limit for related nonlocal phase-field models can be found in
[
10
,
38
]
and for local phase-field model in
[
33
]
. The
\varepsilon
-dependence is introduced only through the
double-well part of the KKS free energy density as discussed in Example
2
,

f_{\varepsilon}(\phi,c):=A\bigl[c-h(\phi)(1-c_{L})-c_{L}\bigr]^{2}+\frac{\omega}{\varepsilon}g(\phi).

All limits below are taken with
\varepsilon\to 0
at
fixed

\delta
. The
\Gamma
-convergence and PDE limits are therefore taken as

\varepsilon\to 0
with the macroscopic horizon
\delta>0
kept
fixed;
\delta
enters only through the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\delta}
that
appears in the peridynamic law. We also assume well-prepared initial data: there is a smooth open set

\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}^{0}\Subset\Omega
such that

\|\phi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,0)-\chi_{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}^{0}}\|_{C^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C\varepsilon,\qquad\|c_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,0)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C,

for a constant
C
independent of
\varepsilon
.
Finally, we impose an interface-boundary separation

\operatorname{dist}(\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(t),\partial\Omega)\geq d_{0}>0
(uniform in
t
) to avoid horizon truncation; see
Remark
5.3
below.
Under these hypotheses, the nonlocal Allen–Cahn solution

\phi_{\varepsilon}
converges in
L^{1}(\Omega)
, up to a subsequence, to
the characteristic function
\chi_{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}(t)}
of an
evolving metal domain. Then one proceeds to show
\phi_{\varepsilon}\to\chi_{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}(t)}
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
,
while the
\varepsilon
-independent peridynamic term is
retained in the limit. Formally let

\mu_{\varepsilon}:=\partial_{c}f\bigl(\phi_{\varepsilon},c_{\varepsilon}\bigr)=2A\Bigl[c_{\varepsilon}-c_{L}-(1-c_{L})h(\phi_{\varepsilon})\Bigr]=:2A\bigl[c_{\varepsilon}-c_{L}\bigr]+\vartheta_{\varepsilon},\qquad\vartheta_{\varepsilon}:=-2A(1-c_{L})h(\phi_{\varepsilon}).

Because
h(\phi_{\varepsilon})\to h(\phi_{0})\in\{0,1\}
a.e., the limit

\vartheta_{0}(x,t)\in\{-2A(1-c_{L}),0\}

is piecewise constant. The horizon operator annihilates constants, so

\mathcal{L}_{\delta}u(x):=\int_{\Omega}J_{\delta}(x-y)[u(x)-u(y)]\,\textup{d}y,\quad\mathcal{L}_{\delta}[\vartheta_{0}]=0,\quad\text{inside each pure phase}.

Indeed, for any constant
k
,

\mathcal{L}_{\delta}k(x)=\int_{B_{\delta}(x)}J_{\delta}(x-y)[k-k]\,\textup{d}y=0
.
Hence
\mathcal{L}_{\delta}[\vartheta_{0}]
is supported only in the

\delta
-strip

\mathcal{N}_{\delta}(t):=\bigl\{x\in\Omega:\operatorname{dist}\bigl(x,\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}(t)\bigr)<\delta\bigr\}.

Since it holds
\mathcal{L}_{\delta}[\vartheta_{0}](x)=O(\delta^{-1})
on

\mathcal{N}_{\delta}(t)
and
|\mathcal{N}_{\delta}(t)|=O(\delta)
, one can show
\|\mathcal{L}_{\delta}[\vartheta_{0}]\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=O(\delta^{-1/2}).

If we formulate the concentration equation on the

bulk domain

\displaystyle\Omega_{\delta}(t):=\Omega\setminus\mathcal{N}_{\delta}(t)
,
in fact

\mathcal{L}_{\delta}[\vartheta_{0}](x,t)\equiv 0
on

\Omega_{\delta}(t)
, so we obtain the peridynamic diffusion law

\partial_{t}c=2AM\mathcal{L}_{\delta}[c]\qquad\text{in }\Omega_{\delta}(t),

(31)

supplemented with interface conditions that follow from the sharp-interface
limit of the phase-field. Testing the nonlocal Cahn–Hilliard equation with a smooth
\psi=\psi(x,t)
, passing to the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, and using the
time-derivative bound established in Section
4
, we arrive formally at the peridynamic diffusion law (
31
) displayed above. The formal chain of arguments in this section can be summarised as

\text{nonlocal KKS }(\varepsilon,\delta)\xRightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{\text{sharp interface}}\text{peridynamic diffusion }(\delta).

In peridynamic diffusion, including corrosion and heat-conduction models

[
3
,
43
]
, one writes

\partial_{t}C=\int_{B_{\delta}(x)}\lambda(x,y){\cal J}(x-y)[C(y)-C(x)]\,\textup{d}y,

with a unit-mass influence function
{\cal J}
and fitted diffusivities

\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}
(metal) and
\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}
(electrolyte).
Choosing
{\cal J}=J_{\delta}
makes (
31
) identical to that
format provided one sets

\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}=2A(1-c_{L}),\qquad\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}=2Ac_{L}.

Thus all material parameters are inherited from the bulk free energy of
the phase-field model; the horizon
\delta
is the only new
peridynamic-specific length.

Remark 5.3

(Boundary layer)
.

If
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
hits
\partial\Omega
so that

\operatorname{dist}(\Gamma_{\varepsilon},\partial\Omega)=0
, the part of
the horizon
B_{\delta}(x)
lying outside
\Omega
produces an extra

H^{-1}
term bounded by
C\delta
. Because
\delta
is fixed in
this section, that contribution is uniformly bounded and can be absorbed
into the
O(\varepsilon)
error of the sharp-interface limit.

6
Numerical solution

There are several candidates for discretization schemes used with truncated nonlocal operators
[
16
]
. While meshfree methods (such as PeriPy) currently dominate practical applications of nonlocal models in mechanics
[
6
,
34
]
, both finite element and finite difference methods are widely applied.
As in the local case, finite difference schemes generally require a higher regularity of the solution
[
42
]
, while finite element methods offer greater flexibility and are well suited for complex geometries. Beyond these familiar differences, the nonlocal framework introduces additional nuances such as the computation of double integrals, which increases their computational cost. Nonetheless, the nonlocal models often exhibit stabilizing properties in contrast to their local counterpart, as it was also observed in the discretization of a nonlocal Cahn–Hilliard model in
[
9
]
. Furthermore, we mention the works
[
30
,
44
]
and the references therein for numerical methods for nonlocal PDEs.

In this work, we consider the explicit Euler time discretization of the KKS phase-field corrosion model and its meshfree approximation of nonlocal integrals via a one-point Gaussian quadrature (peridynamic-style) on a uniform grid. We apply this scheme to a two-dimensional pitting corrosion benchmark in a square domain and numerically demonstrate that, as the nonlocal horizon
\delta\to 0
, the nonlocal solution converges in the
L^{2}
-norm to the corresponding local (classical) solution.

6.1
Numerical setup

We discretize the (nondimensional) domain
\Omega=[0,50]\times[0,50]

\phi

At each grid point
(x_{i},y_{j})
with

and

\displaystyle\phi_{i,j}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\phi_{i,j}^{n}-\Delta tL\Bigl\{\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi}(\phi_{i,j}^{n},c_{i,j}^{n})-\alpha_{\phi}\Delta_{\text{FD}}\phi\bigl|_{i,j}^{n}\Bigr\},

\displaystyle c_{i,j}^{n+1}

7
Conclusion and Perspectives

In this work, we have developed a unified nonlocal framework for phase-field corrosion modeling that naturally bridges to peridynamic approaches. By introducing a finite interaction horizon
\delta
, our model captures long-range chemical interactions and diffusion, yielding a rigorous nonlocal evolution. We have rigorously shown via formal Gateaux differentiation and careful Taylor expansions that, as
\delta\to 0
, the nonlocal evolution equations converge to the classical Allen–Cahn and Cahn–Hilliard limits at an explicit
O(\delta)
rate. Numerically, using explicit finite-difference discretizations, we have confirmed convergence of the nonlocal solutions to their local counterparts and demonstrated how the horizon
\delta

and grid spacing
h
jointly govern pit growth and concentration profiles, establishing a solid theoretical and computational bridge between nonlocal phase-field models and peridynamic corrosion frameworks.

This work lays a rigorous and versatile foundation for building a comprehensive nonlocal toolset. A key goal is to extend this unified theory to encompass coupled electrochemical transport, mechanical deformation, and multicomponent chemistry. In particular, integrating our recent development of a nonlocal Nernst–Planck–Poisson (NNPP) system for multion transport with this KKS-style phase-field model—while also incorporating elastic and plastic mechanics in a nonlocal setting—would enable the simulation of moving boundaries, pH-dependent reactions, and mechanical stresses within a single consistent theory. Such an extended model could capture not only peridynamic-style pit growth but also coupled electromigration, stress-affected dissolution, and the evolution of a diffuse corrosion layer.

Future work will therefore focus on this integration, alongside pursuing rigorous error estimates for fully discrete schemes, extension to three-dimensional geometries, and validation against experimental data for multi-ion, pH-coupled corrosion (e.g., Mg-based biomaterials). We also plan to explore alternative nonlocal kernels—anisotropic or weighted by material microstructure—to enhance modeling flexibility. Ultimately, by unifying phase-field, peridynamic, and NNPP approaches, we aim to provide a comprehensive framework for simulating complex, multiscale corrosion processes across engineering and biomedical applications.
