Long‐Term Follow‐Up Incidence of Type 1 Diabetes and Associated Autoimmune Diseases in First‐Degree Relatives
Authors: Julie H Christensen, Gitte Lindved Petersen, Simranjeet Kaur, Alexander Lind, Samia Hamdan, Maria Naredi Scherman, Jesper Johannesen, Daniel Agardh, Julie C Antvorskov, Flemming Pociot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFirst‐degree relatives (FDRs) of individuals with type 1 diabetes (T1D) carry a significantly elevated risk of developing autoimmune diseases, including celiac disease (CD) and autoimmune thyroid disease (AITD). The DiaUnion 1.0 study aimed to characterize one‐time autoantibodies, genetic risk profiles and long‐term progression to T1D, CD or AITD in siblings of children newly diagnosed with T1D.MethodsBetween 1997 and 2010, 1427 Danish siblings provided plasma and DNA samples to the DanDiabKids biobank. Autoantibodies associated with T1D (GADA, IAA, IA2A, ZnT8A), CD (tTGA) and AITD (TPOA) were measured using ADAP technology and confirmed by radiobinding assays. Genotyping was performed to compute a T1D Genetic Risk Score (GRS). National registry data were used to track disease incidence up to year 2024.ResultsOut of 1417 screened samples, 7.8% were positive for islet autoantibodies (IAab), and 4.6% had multiple IAab. Disease incidence rate increased substantially with IAab positivity: 0.11 per 100 person‐years in IAab‐negative siblings, 2.46 in single‐IAab positive and 8.10 in those with multiple IAab. Similarly, 1.97 per 100 person‐years of tTGA‐positive siblings developed CD, and 0.69 per 100 person‐years of TPOA‐positive individuals developed AITD. Higher GRS and HLA DR3/DR4 haplotypes were associated with autoantibody positivity (p < 0.0001).ConclusionsA single screening for T1D, CD and AITD autoantibodies combined with genetic risk scoring stratified long‐term risk of clinically diagnosed autoimmune disease. These findings support further evaluation of targeted screening and follow‐up strategies in FDRs to enable surveillance and potential early intervention before clinical onset.Keywords: autoimmunity, cohort study, database research, first‐degree relatives, type 1 diabetes

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is a heterogeneous disease that develops along a continuum spanning genetic risk, the emergence of islet autoimmunity and eventual metabolic dysfunction [1]. Advances in understanding the natural history of T1D have enabled the identification of individuals in preclinical stages. The disease progression is now defined in three stages: Stage 1, characterized by multiple islet autoantibodies (IAab) and normoglycemia; Stage 2, defined by persistent multiple IAab with dysglycemia; and Stage 3, the onset of clinical diabetes [2]. Individuals with multiple IAab have an almost 100% lifetime risk of developing T1D, with more than 80% progressing to insulin dependence within 15 years. Pooled cohort analyses show that 50% progress within 5 years and 75% within 10 years [3]. In contrast, individuals with a single IAab who have not yet entered the presymptomatic stages have a substantially lower 10‐year progression risk of 12%–15% [3, 4].

Progression rates vary according to age at seroconversion, genetics, and the number and specificity of IAab [5]. The TrialNet Pathway to Prevention study of over 30 000 first‐ and second‐degree relatives of individuals with T1D demonstrated that seroconversion from single to multiple IAab is strongly age‐dependent, with cumulative incidence of 2% in children ≤ 10 years and 0.7% in older individuals [6]. Similar findings from the TEDDY study showed that the risk of developing single or multiple IAab declines exponentially with age [7]. IAab specificity further influences risk, for example, IA2A titre and epitope reactivity are associated with increased pathogenicity and faster progression in both children and adults [8, 9]. In paediatric cohorts such as BABYDIAB, IA2A positivity was followed by additional autoantibody development in 98% of children [10]. In contrast, IAA and GADA positivity are generally associated with slower progression, and reversion to autoantibody negativity is more common for these autoantibodies than for IA2A or ZnT8A [11, 12].

First‐degree relatives (FDRs) of individuals with T1D carry a 15–20‐fold higher risk of developing the disease compared to the general population [13, 14]. Although more than 85% of new T1D cases arise in individuals without family history, targeted screening of high‐risk groups such as FDRs remains a cost‐effective initial strategy for early‐stage T1D detection [13]. Importantly, individuals with T1D and their FDRs are also at increased risk for other autoimmune diseases, including celiac disease (CD) and autoimmune thyroid disease (AITD). Shared immunogenetic factors likely contribute to the clustering of these [15, 16, 17]. A population‐based screening study assessing autoantibodies associated with T1D, CD and AITD found that more than half of autoantibody‐positive children with an affected FDR screened positive for a different autoimmune condition than that of their relative [18]. These findings support the rationale for combined screening for T1D, CD and AITD.

Within the DiaUnion initiative, we have established a screening programme for these three autoimmune diseases to evaluate the feasibility of future large‐scale implementation. The aim of the present study was to describe the autoantibody profiles identified through a one‐time screening of siblings of newly diagnosed children with T1D and to characterize their long‐term progression to T1D, CD or AITD over a follow‐up period of up to 27 years.

Methods

Study Population

Between 1997 and 2010, a total of 1427 Danish siblings of newly diagnosed T1D probands provided a blood sample to the national biobank DanDiabKids (reference P‐2020‐1120). This prospective clinical study included siblings recruited in Denmark and linked through Danish civil registration. Individual‐level ethnicity/ancestry was not available. The mean age was 12.3 years at the time of sampling. The samples were originally collected with informed consent for future immunological and genetic analyses; therefore, no re‐contact of participants was required. No exclusion criteria were applied. The DiaUnion 1.0 study was approved by the Danish Research Ethics Committee (reference H‐21018597).

Autoantibody Analysis

All plasma samples with sufficient volume (≥ 250 μL) were analysed for autoantibodies (Aab) associated with T1D, CD and AITD. Of the 1427 biobank samples obtained from siblings of T1D probands, 1417 had sufficient volume for analysis. Autoantibody screening followed a two‐step protocol. Agglutination‐PCR ADAP was used as a high‐throughput first‐line assay to maximize sensitivity [19, 20, 21]. All ADAP‐positive samples were then tested by disease‐specific RBA [22], and only RBA‐confirmed positives were used for risk analyses. The lower number of RBA‐confirmed positives among register‐linked participants reflects both confirmatory specificity and exclusion of samples without register linkage.

The ADAP analyses were run in multiplex on an automated Hamilton MicroLab STAR platform [20, 23]. In this method, antibody‐mediated agglutination brings DNA‐barcoded antigens into proximity, enabling ligation of complementary DNA strands into full amplicons. Specific primers were added in separate wells to amplify target‐specific signals. Real‐time quantitative PCR was conducted for 40 cycles on a Roche LightCycler 480 System II (Roche Diagnostics International AG, Rotkreutz, Switzerland). Autoantibody levels were expressed as ΔCt values, calculated by subtracting each sample's cycle termination value from the average value obtained from eight assay buffer controls (phosphate‐buffered saline with Triton‐X).

Single‐plex RBA was used to verify ADAP results and to quantify autoantibodies associated with T1D (GADA, IAA, IA‐2A, ZnT8A), CD (tissue transglutaminase, tTGA) and AITD (thyroid peroxidase, TPOA), using established protocols [18, 20, 24]. Of the 388 ADAP‐positive samples, 196 were confirmed positive by the gold‐standard RBA. The positivity cut‐offs for each RBA were: tTGA‐IgA: < 4.0 U/mL; tTGA‐IgG: < 4.0 U7mL; IAA: < 3.7 U/mL; GADA: < 35 U/mL; IA‐2A: < 6 U/mL; ZnT8A: < 40 U/mL. TPOA positivity was not confirmed with RBA.

Genetic Analysis

Genotyping was performed on 1228 DNA samples, determining T1D associated polymorphisms, which was used to evaluate genetic risk variants and calculate a genomic risk score (GRS). Participants were genotyped on the Illumina Infinium Global Screening Array (GSA version 24.v2.0). Before quality control (QC), the GSA array had 722 659 SNPs. After QC and frequency pruning (−geno 0.02 –maf 0.05 –hwe 0.001 –mind 0.02) using PLINK v1.90, the genotyping rate was 0.99 and included 298 994 SNPs.

The T1D Genetic Risk Score (GRS1) was calculated using 29 out of 30 T1D‐associated SNPs, as identified in Oram et al. [25]. For the missing SNP, rs11594656, a proxy SNP analysis was performed using linkage disequilibrium patterns in the European (EUR) population through LDlink [26]. However, no suitable proxies with r 2 > 0.8 were identified, leading to the exclusion of this SNP from the analysis. The genetic risk score for the remaining 27 non‐HLA SNPs was calculated using the score function in PLINK. For the two HLA SNPs, rs2187668 and rs7454108, which capture the DR3/DR4‐DQ8 haplotypes, genotype weights for the highest‐risk heterozygous combinations were assigned based on evidence from Barker et al. [27]. These weights were incorporated into the final aggregate genetic risk score, GRS1.

Registry Follow‐Up

The study population was linked to national register data at Statistics Denmark by means of the personal identification number given to all Danish citizens at birth or upon residency [28, 29]. The inability to identify 103 participants (7% of the eligible cohort) was likely caused by incorrect entry of their personal identification numbers during enrollment. Baseline autoimmunity was defined by RBA‐confirmed autoantibody positivity. Incident cases were defined by Danish National Patient Registry ICD codes. For each study participant, we obtained information on T1D (ICD: DE10*), CD (ICD: DK900*, DL130*) and AITD (ICD: DE05*, DE06*) diagnoses and dates of diagnoses [30]. Each study participant was followed prospectively from baseline to T1D, CD and/or AITD diagnoses, emigration, death or end of follow‐up (2022‐12‐31), whichever came first. Emigrations and deaths were obtained from Statistics Denmark's database.

In accordance with Danish data‐protection regulations and GDPR requirements, results based on fewer than three individuals cannot be reported, as such small cell sizes may risk indirect identification.

Statistical Analysis

Autoantibody levels were treated as non‐normally distributed. Comparisons between independent groups were performed using Mann–Whitney U test, with the level of significance set at alpha 0.05. All autoantibody analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 (La Jolla, California, United States).

Differences in GRS between positive versus negative groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Associations between Aab status and HLA‐DR groupings were evaluated using Pearsons' chi‐square test. For comparisons involving more than two groups, the Kruskal‐Wallis test was applied to assess overall group differences, followed by pairwise Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests with p value adjustment to identify specific group differences.

Our main results include baseline characteristics (genetic risk, detected Aab and age at enrollment), mean age at diagnosis, incident disease events (T1D, CD and AITD), disease incidence per 100 person years (number of incident events/total number of included person years multiplied by 100) for T1D, CD and AITD, respectively, and incidence rate ratios (IRR = incidence rate (IR) in Aab positive/IR in Aab negative) for the relative comparison of Aab positive compared to negative siblings. All results are presented according to detected T1D, CD and AITD‐related Aab. In supplementary sensitivity analyses we first included T1D IR according to baseline IAab (0 and 1+) in strata of baseline age (0–9, 10–14 and 15+ years) to assess potential age‐dependent T1D IR. Second, we included T1D IR according to baseline IAab (0, 1 and 2+) in strata of follow‐up windows (0–4, 5–9 and 10+ years) to evaluate long‐term stability across time.

All statistical analyses were conducted in the RStudio (vers. 2023.12.1) for Windows.

Celiac Disease and Autoimmune Thyroid Disease Autoantibodies in Type 1 Diabetes Siblings

Among the 1324 register‐identified siblings, 45 (3.4%) tested positive for tTGA and 50 (3.8%) tested positive for TPOA (Table 1). Most siblings tested positive for only one category of Aab (IAab, tTGA or TPOA) and none of the included tested positive for all (Figure 2B). Analysis of antibody levels of tTGA and TPOA showed no significant differences between IAab positive and IAab negative groups (p > 0.2).

Progression to Type 1 Diabetes, Celiac Disease and/or Autoimmune Thyroid Disease

Type 1 Diabetes

Among the 1324 register‐identified study participants, 35 had developed T1D before study entry, leaving 1289 siblings at risk with a mean follow‐up of 17.1 years. Of these, 23 IAab negative individuals, 14 single IAab‐positive and 28 multiple IAab‐positive individuals were diagnosed with T1D during follow‐up, corresponding to T1D IRs of 0.1, 2.5 and 8.1 cases per 100 person‐years, respectively. Consequently, single IAab‐positive siblings had an IR of T1D that was more than 22 times that of IAab‐negative siblings during 17 years of follow‐up while those with multiple IAab positivity had an IR more than 74 times that of IAab‐negative siblings, also during 17.1 years of follow‐up (Table 2, section A). We also estimated age‐specific incidence of T1D according to baseline islet autoantibody status (Table S1), suggesting that younger age was associated with faster progression in both IAab positive and negative individuals. T1D IRs were relatively stable across follow‐up windows of 0–4, 5–9 and 10+ years from baseline (Table S2).

By the end of follow‐up, 60 tTGA‐negative and 5 tTGA‐positive siblings were diagnosed with T1D, corresponding to IRs of 0.3 and 0.8 cases per 100 person‐years. This translates to tTGA‐positive siblings having a T1D IR almost three times as high as tTGA‐negative siblings during 17.6 years of follow‐up. Corresponding results for TPOA were similar, with 60 negative and 5 positive siblings diagnosed with T1D during 17.8 years of follow‐up, corresponding to T1D IRs of 0.3 and 0.6 cases per 100 person‐years. This reflects an IR more than twice as high among TPOA‐positive compared with negative siblings (Table 2, section A).

Celiac Disease

Among the 1324 register‐identified study participants, 6 had developed CD before study entry, leaving 1318 siblings at risk with a mean follow‐up of 17–18 years. Of these, 6 tTGA‐negative and 13 tTGA‐positive individuals were diagnosed with CD during follow‐up, corresponding to CD IRs of < 0.1 and 2.0 cases per 100 person‐years, respectively (Table 2, section B). Consequently, tTGA‐positive siblings had an IR of CD more than 72 times as high as that of tTGA‐negative siblings during 17–18 years of follow‐up.

The number of siblings who progressed to CD during follow‐up within the IAab and the AITD strata was below the disclosure threshold and therefore cannot be reported. The IR differences (IRD) were < 0.1 case per 100 person‐years when comparing IAab‐positive and tTGA‐positive with all siblings, respectively (0.082–0.056 T1D cases per 100 person‐years and 0.082–0.085 AITD cases per 100 person‐years, Table 2, section B).

Autoimmune Thyroid Disease

Among the 1324 register‐identified study participants, 3 had developed AITD before study entry, leaving 1321 siblings at risk with a mean follow‐up of 18 years. Of these, 16 thyroid peroxidase antibodies (TPOA)‐negative and 6 TPOA‐positive individuals were diagnosed with AITD during follow‐up, corresponding to IRs of 0.1 and 0.7 cases per 100 person‐years, respectively. Consequently, TPOA‐positive siblings had an IR of AITD more than 9 times as high as that of TPOA‐negative siblings during 17–18 years of follow‐up (Table 2, section C).

The number of siblings who progressed to AITD during follow‐up within the IAab strata was below the disclosure threshold and therefore cannot be reported. The IRD was < 0.1 case per 100 person‐years when comparing IAab‐positive to all siblings (0.095–0.080 AITD cases per 100 person‐years). Although no tTGA‐positive siblings developed AITD during follow‐up and 22 of the tTGA‐negative siblings did, the IRD was < 0.1 fewer cases per 100 person‐years when comparing to all siblings (0.098–0.095, Table 2, section C).

Type 1 Diabetes Genetic Risk Score 1 and HLA Haplotypes Are Strongly Associated With Autoantibody Positivity in Early Autoimmunity

A statistically significant association was observed between GRS and Aab status. Individuals who were positive for autoantibodies associated with T1D, CD or AITD exhibited significantly higher GRS values compared with Aab‐negative individuals (p < 0.0001; Figure 3A). This association remained robust within the T1D‐specific subgroup (p < 0.0001; Figure 3B).

Consistently, a higher frequency of high‐risk HLA haplotypes, specifically DR3/DR4, was observed among Aab‐positive individuals, with marked enrichment in the subgroup positive for IAab (Figure 3C,D). Among participants with multiple IAab, both GRS levels and the prevalence of high‐risk HLA haplotypes were significantly elevated relative to Aab‐negative individuals (p < 0.0001).

Although not statistically significant, there was a trend towards higher GRS values and a greater proportion of high‐risk HLA haplotypes in individuals with multiple IAab compared with those with a single IAab (Figure S1). Furthermore, GRS values were significantly elevated in individuals positive for the IAab combination IA‐2A + ZnT8A (≥ 2 IAab) or for IA‐2A or ZnT8A alone (1 IAab), compared with other IAab profiles (p < 0.0001). IAab negative individuals, who developed T1D, had significantly higher GRS compared to IAab negative individuals with no T1D during follow‐up (p = 0.02).

Taken together, these findings highlight a strong additive relationship between genetic susceptibility and early immunological markers of autoimmunity, supporting the potential value of combined genetic and serological screening for improved risk stratification in at‐risk populations. However, the study was not designed or sufficiently powered to establish incremental clinical prediction beyond autoantibody status alone.

Discussion

This study supports the clinical relevance of a single childhood Aab screening for T1D, CD and AITD in siblings of children with newly diagnosed T1D. By integrating Aab profiling, genetic risk stratification and long‐term national registry follow‐up, we describe the occurrence of early autoimmune markers in a high‐risk paediatric population and document how subsequent T1D, CD and AITD diagnoses are distributed 17–18 years later across these markers. Siblings with multiple IAab had a markedly higher incidence of T1D compared to those with single or no IAab during follow up with an IR of 8.1 diagnoses per 100 person years compared with 2.5 among single IAab positive siblings, and 0.1 among IAab negative siblings. This corresponded to IRRs of more than 74 for multiple IAab positive siblings and more than 22 for single IAab positive siblings, compared with IAab negative siblings. Notably, T1D IR remained relatively stable across the follow‐up window, which suggest that baseline autoantibody status retained prognostic value over prolonged follow‐up despite the absence of repeated antibody measurements. Siblings with CD‐related or AITD‐related Aab similarly had markedly higher IRs of CD and AITD, and interestingly, they also had T1D IRs twice as those of CD/AITD Aab‐negative individuals. In contrast, the incidences of CD and AITD in siblings with T1D‐related Aab were below the disclosure threshold and thus appeared below public‐health‐relevance.

Our findings confirm that multiple IAab confer a markedly increased IR of clinical T1D. The number and specificity of IAab are biologically informative. IA‐2A and ZnT8A generally mark a more advanced and beta‐cell‐specific autoimmune response and have been linked to faster progression, whereas isolated GADA or IAA may be more heterogeneous and may revert. In our data, IA‐2A/ZnT8A‐containing profiles were associated with higher GRS, supporting their role as higher‐risk signatures. However, event counts for some combinations were small, and the primary grouped analyses were retained to avoid unstable estimates and disclosure risks. Data from cohorts from Finland, Germany, Sweden and US report 15‐year cumulative T1D incidences of 82%, 30% and 12%, respectively, for young children with multiple, single or no IAab, respectively [4]. Because cumulative incidence estimation requires a closed cohort with complete follow‐up, criteria our study does not meet, the estimates are not directly comparable. However, the authors do report a comparable IR of 5.6 T1D diagnoses per 100 person‐years over a 10‐year follow‐up among children aged 7.4–18.1 years at the development of multiple IAab [4]. The observed T1D IRs per 100 person‐years in our study (IAab‐negative: 0.1, single‐IAab: 2.5 and multiple‐IAab: 8.1), mirror progression trends reported in prior studies [3, 7], further supporting the IAab as early biomarkers. The progression rate of 34.1% of single IAab‐positive individuals to clinical disease is higher than in most studies and may reflect a longer observation period and the focus on a selected high‐risk population. Because data on glucose metabolism were not available, we could not differentiate between stage one and stage two T1D in the present study, but the findings suggest that follow‐up programmes for stage 1 and stage 2 T1D with complementary systematic monitoring of single IAab‐positive individuals identified within high‐risk families may be relevant. T1D among baseline IAab‐negative siblings likely reflects unobserved post‐baseline seroconversion, antibody levels below assay thresholds, transiently negative status or progression pathways not captured by the measured panel. The familial‐risk setting also means that IAab‐negative siblings retain higher background risk than unrelated children.

Importantly, our screening also identified individuals at risk of CD and AITD with substantial IRRs in disease‐specific Aab‐positive individuals. Importantly, many individuals positive for CD‐ or AITD‐associated autoantibodies were IAab‐negative, indicating that disease‐specific screening alone would fail to identify a considerable proportion of at‐risk individuals. These findings underscore the value of combined autoimmune screening to capture the broader spectrum of immune‐mediated disease risk.

The findings align closely with our previous study [18], which advocated for simultaneous screening of T1D, CD and AITD in paediatric populations. Like the study by Naredi Scherman et al. [18], we observed a high degree of cross‐reactivity, particularly for incident T1D, and co‐occurrence of autoimmune markers, emphasizing shared genetic and immunopathogenic mechanisms. The present study extends these findings by linking early serological markers to long‐term clinical outcomes and by integrating detailed genetic risk analysis.

Genetic analysis revealed a strong correlation between higher genetic risk score and positivity for T1D, CD and AITD autoantibodies. The enrichment of DR3/DR4‐DQ8 and higher GRS among IAab‐positive siblings is consistent with HLA‐mediated susceptibility to islet autoimmunity. Notably, positivity for IA2A and/or ZnT8A correlated with high genetic risk; these autoantibodies have previously been associated with accelerated T1D progression [9]. DR3 is also implicated in CD risk, whereas AITD shares partly overlapping but distinct immunogenetic architecture. Thus, the observed association between GRS/HLA and autoantibody positivity reinforces earlier work by Oram et al. and Jacobsen et al. [25, 31] and supports shared immune susceptibility, but should not be interpreted as a validated multi‐disease prediction model. We did not model unobserved seroconversion because no repeated samples were available and external transition rates would introduce assumptions not estimable from this cohort.

The study's strengths include its relatively large sample size, extensive follow‐up period of up to 18 years, and the use of national registries. The integration of multiplex autoantibody screening with high‐throughput genotyping adds further scientific rigour. The combination of serological, genetic and clinical registry data provides a multifaceted perspective on disease progression.

Nevertheless, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, a key limitation is that autoantibody status was measured only once. Therefore, our analyses classify participants according to baseline serostatus and cannot distinguish persistent autoimmunity from transient positivity, identify later seroconversion among baseline‐negative siblings, or evaluate titre changes and epitope spreading. At group level, the results, however, suggest T1D IR to remain relatively similar across 0–4, 5–9 and 10+ years from baseline. Second, registry‐based diagnosis, while robust, may not fully capture subclinical or undiagnosed cases. This may underestimate true disease burden, particularly for CD and AITD, and may bias incidence estimates downward in both antibody‐positive and antibody‐negative groups. Third, because the cohort consisted of Danish siblings of children with T1D, findings may not generalize to unrelated general‐population children, adults or populations with different ancestry distributions, HLA haplotype frequencies, health‐care access and diagnostic practices. External validation and recalibration of GRS thresholds are required before use in diverse cohorts. Fourth, another limitation is the lack of information on environmental exposures that may influence the development of autoimmunity and progression to clinical disease, including viral infections, diet, microbiome composition, vitamin D status and other lifestyle‐related factors. Consequently, we could not evaluate interactions between genetic susceptibility, environmental exposures and baseline autoantibody status. Finally, the study did not assess cost‐effectiveness, acceptability, optimal screening intervals, downstream clinical pathways or psychosocial outcomes; these factors are essential before broad implementation. Risk disclosure in asymptomatic children raises ethical issues, including anxiety, uncertain individual prognosis, insurance/social implications and the need for equitable follow‐up. Any implementation should include pre‐test information, confirmatory testing, genetic counselling where appropriate, psychological support and clear clinical pathways.

In this cohort of siblings of children with T1D, a single baseline screen for islet, CD‐associated and thyroid autoantibodies stratified long‐term risk of clinically diagnosed autoimmune disease over approximately 17–18 years of registry follow‐up. Multiple IAab identified the highest‐risk group for subsequent T1D, while tTGA and TPOA positivity identified siblings at increased risk of CD and AITD, respectively. Higher GRS and enrichment of high‐risk HLA haplotypes among autoantibody‐positive participants support a shared genetic contribution to early autoimmunity. However, because autoantibodies were measured only once, registry outcomes capture clinically recognized disease and incremental prediction by GRS beyond autoantibody status was not established, these findings should be interpreted as evidence of estimated frequencies rather than proof of implementation‐ready clinical utility. Ongoing repeated‐screening studies in Europe and North America are providing important evidence regarding implementation, psychosocial outcomes, screening intervals and cost‐effectiveness. Additional long‐term studies in diverse populations are nevertheless needed to optimize screening algorithms and follow‐up strategies.
