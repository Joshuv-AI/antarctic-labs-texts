Efficacy and safety of neoadjuvant FLOT treatment for resectable gastric/gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma in Turkish and German patients; a real-world data
Authors: İmdat Eroğlu, Christian Möhring, Farsaneh Sadeghlar, Adrianna Mańczak, Ozan Yazıcı, Ahmet Özet, Nuriye Özdemir, Maria A Gonzalez-Carmona, Fatih Gürler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerioperative FLOT is the current standard of treatment in resectable gastric adenocarcinoma(GC) and adenocarcinoma of gastroesophageal junction(GEJC). However, many ethnicities remain underrepresented in clinical trials or are not differentiated in subgroup analyses, making it unclear whether outcomes, especially major pathological response (mPR), vary across different populations.MethodsTurkish(Tp) and German(Gp) patients with resectable GC/GEJC who received perioperative FLOT were evaluated retrospectively. The primary endpoint was mPR which was defined as pathological complete response or near-complete response. The secondary endpoints were disease-free survival(DFS), overall survival(OS), and safety.ResultsForty-seven Tp and 55 Gp were included. While Tp had more GC(63.8% vs. 36.2%), GEJC was higher in Gp(41.8% vs. 58.2%)(p = 0.027). Tp had more node-positive diseases(91.5% vs. 63.6%, p = 0.005), and received more cycles of neoadjuvant FLOT(p < 0.001). The most commonly seen side effects were anemia in Tp, and neutropenia and neuropathy in Gp. R0 resection ratio was 87.2% in Tp and 92.7% in Gp. The ratio of mPR was higher in Gp (36% vs. 18.5%, p = 0.04) While German ethnicity and clinical node negativity was associated with better mPR in the univariate analysis, only clinical node negativity was associated with better mPR in the multivariate analysis [OR:3.951, 95% CI (1.168–13.365), p = 0.027]. There was no relation between mPR and other clinical factors including tumor location, histology and intensified neoadjuvant treatment (> 4 cycles of FLOT). Gp had longer DFS(53.22 vs. 21.42 months, p = 0.03) and OS(57.99 vs. 29.37 months, p = 0.05). However, mPR did not show any association with OS and DFS. Turkish ethnicity was related with worse OS and DFS in univariate analysis but not multivariate analysis. Multivariate analysis showed that intensified neoadjuvant treatment(> 4 cycles), signet-cell carcinoma, and higher pathological N-stage were independent risk factors for decreased OS. After adjusting for clinical node positivity and tumor location; intensified neoadjuvant treatment(> 4 cycles), and higher pathological N stage remained associated with worse OS.DiscussionThis bicentric study reveals that although ethnicity had limited prognostic impact, lymph node status remained the strongest determinant of outcome in perioperatively treated patients with GC/GEJC. Intensified treatment did not improve mPR and was associated with worse OS, while mPR itself was not independently linked to OS. Pathological nodal positivity and signet-ring cell carcinoma were the key predictors of poor survival. These findings highlight the importance of nodal risk stratification and tailored follow-up over therapy intensification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15043-6.Keywords: FLOT, Perioperative, Gastric cancer, Gastroesophageal junction, Ethnicity

Introduction

Although surgery is the primary curable treatment in the early stage of the disease, the introduction of perioperative chemotherapy has changed the standard treatment of locally advanced/resectable gastric adenocarcinoma (GC) and adenocarcinoma of gastroesophageal junction cancers (GEJC) [1]. In the MAGIC trial, conducted in England, perioperative (3 cycles before surgery and 3 cycles after surgery) ECF (epirubicin, cisplatin, fluorouracil) improved five-year overall survival (OS) (36% vs. 23%) compared to surgery alone in patients with resectable locally advanced GC/GEJC [2]. Similarly, doublet chemotherapy with cisplatin and fluorouracil showed significantly better five-year disease free survival (DFS) (34% vs. 19%), and OS (38% vs. 24%) compared to surgery alone in the phase 3 FNCLCC trial, conducted in France [3], and increased R0 resection rate (81.9% vs. 66.7%) in EORTC trial [4].

In the phase 2/3 FLOT4 trial, conducted in Germany, perioperative (4 cycles before surgery and 4 cycles after surgery) FLOT (Fluorouracil/leucovorin, oxaliplatin, docetaxel) regimen resulted in better median OS (50 months vs. 35 months) [5], and higher pathological complete response rates (16% vs. 6%) compared to perioperative ECF [6]. Currently, perioperative FLOT treatment is considered the standard- of-care in patients with locally advanced GC/GEJC [1].

According to the interim analysis of the MATTERHORN study presented in ESMO 2023, German patients with resectable GC/GEJC showed higher pathological complete response (pCR, 30% vs. 13% in the FLOT only group) than the non-German group (18–29% vs. 3–13%) when treated with durvalumab + FLOT regimen [7]. These results raised the question of whether the effectiveness of FLOT treatment, especially major pathological response (mPR), may be variable in different ethnic groups and emphasizes the need for a more detailed investigation of FLOT, being the future chemotherapy backbone in the upcoming neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy era for GC and GEJC [8]. Several real-world data on perioperative FLOT have been published [9–13] and approve the effectiveness of FLOT outside of clinical trials. Though, real-world experience of FLOT treatment with focus on regional or ethnic differences are missing. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of perioperative FLOT regimen in patients with locally advanced GC/GEJC from cohorts of two different ethnicities (Turkish vs. German patients).

Methods

Patients, study design and data collection

This study is designed as a retrospective observational study to evaluate the efficacy and safety of perioperative FLOT treatment between Turkish and German patients with GC/GEJC. The study included patients of two tertiary university hospitals (Gazi University, School of Medicine, Department of Medical Oncology, Ankara, Turkey, and Department of Internal Medicine I, University Hospital of Bonn, Germany). Medical records of patients diagnosed with locally advanced GC/GEJC between 2010 and 2023 were retrospectively evaluated, and among them, the data of those who received neoadjuvant/perioperative FLOT were reviewed. Demographic characteristics of the patients, tumor localization and histological subtype, neoadjuvant and adjuvant treatment schemes, pathological response to treatment, adverse event profiles, surgical and histological features, and survival data of the patients were recorded. Patients who did not receive neoadjuvant FLOT and had non-adenocarcinoma histology, patients of non-German origin treated at the University of Bonn, and patients of non-Turkish origin treated at Gazi University, if any, were excluded.

Treatment protocol

Patients who received at least one dose of preoperative FLOT were included. The FLOT protocol was administered as 2-week cycles of 50 mg/m2 docetaxel, 85 mg/m2 oxaliplatin, 200 mg/m2 leucovorin and 2600 mg/m2 5 fluorouracil as a 24-h infusion on day 1, according to the FLOT4 trial. The perioperative FLOT protocol consists of four neoadjuvant cycles and four adjuvant cycles. However, in case of unacceptable toxicity, disease recurrence or death, the protocol stopped earlier. Additionally, as most patients, particularly in the Turkish cohort, received treatment during the COVID-19 pandemic period, more than four cycles of neoadjuvant FLOT therapy were given as bridging treatment in cases where surgery was anticipated to be postponed. Local differences in treatment practice also contributed to this approach. The primary granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (G-CSF) administration depended on the primary physician’s choice. Patients were scheduled for surgery after 3 to 6 weeks of the last neoadjuvant FLOT cycle. Adjuvant treatments were started 4 to 6 weeks after surgery.

Study endpoints

The study’s primary endpoint was the major pathological response rate (mPR) defined as pathological complete or near-complete response to neoadjuvant FLOT treatment. Pathological responses were defined according to Becker’s tumor regression grade and evaluated by each institute’s own pathologists. Complete remission was defined as no residual tumor, near-complete/subtotal remission was defined as < 10% residual tumor, partial remission was defined as residual tumor between 10% and 50%, and minor/no response was defined as more than 50% residual tumor [14].

The secondary endpoints were safety, disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS). Overall survival was defined as the duration between the date of first diagnosis and death or the end of the observation period at the time of data cut-off. Disease-free survival was defined as the duration between the date of initial diagnosis of the patient and the date of disease recurrence, death, or end of the observation period. In case of loss of follow-up, the date of the patient’s last visit was used for censorship. Adverse events were assessed according to Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS software version 25.0 (S.P.S.S. Inc., Chicago, IL, U.S.A.). Categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages. Mean and standard deviation for normally distributed variables and the median and interquartile range (25th percentile-75th percentile) for non-normally distributed variables were given. Normal distribution was evaluated with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Pearson’s Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables. The estimated survivals were calculated using the log-rank test and presented as Kaplan-Meier curves. Survival data were expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Cox regression enter models were implemented to conduct univariate and multivariate analyses of DFS and OS. Binary logistic regression analysis were used to define predictors of mPR. The univariate analyses to identify variables associated with patient outcome (major pathological response vs. no major pathological response) was investigated using Chi-squire, Fischer exact, Student’s t and Mann-Whitney U test where appropriate. For multivariate analysis, the possible factors identified with univariate analysis were further entered into the logistic regression analysis to determine independent predictors of the outcome. Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness of fit statistics were used to assess model fit. While the Turkish and German groups were evaluated separately in pairwise comparisons and survival analyses, all patients were included in the Cox regression and binary logistic regression analysis. When performing multivariate analysis, statistically significant variables according to univariate analysis, and variables that were considered as clinically significant were selected. Statistical significance was determined by two-tailored P-values ≤ 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the patients

A total of 47 Turkish (Tp) and 55 German (Gp) patients were included. Mean age was 59.6 ± 9.9 years in Tp and 61.9 ± 11.6 years in Gp, with most patients being male (78.7% vs. 80%). ECOG status, BMI, Charlson Comorbidity Index, tumor histology, differentiation, and baseline CEA/CA19-9 levels were comparable. However, tumor location differed: GEJC was more common in Gp (58.2% vs. 36.2%), while GC predominated in Tp (63.8% vs. 41.8%; p = 0.027). Clinical node-positive disease was also significantly higher in Tp (91.5% vs. 63.6%; p = 0.005) (Table 1).

Treatment exposures and surgical outcome

Almost all patients completed planned neoadjuvant cycles (Tp: 93.6%, Gp: 94.5%; p = 1.000). However, the median number of cycles was higher in Tp (5, range 2–8) compared to Gp (4, range 1–6; p < 0.001). Primary G-CSF prophylaxis was given to 91.5% of Tp but to none of Gp (p < 0.001). Surgical procedures also differed between groups: total gastrectomy (43.5% vs. 56.4%), subtotal gastrectomy (30.4% vs. 0%), transthoracic esophagectomy (2.2% vs. 40%), and gastrectomy with transhiatal esophagectomy (23.9% vs. 3.6%; p < 0.001).

Adjuvant chemotherapy was administered more frequently in Tp (78.7% vs. 56.4%). Among Tp, 46.8% received FLOT and 31.9% other regimens, whereas all Gp patients received FLOT. Additionally, 21.7% of Tp but none of Gp underwent adjuvant radiotherapy (p < 0.001), mainly due to insufficient lymph node dissection (n = 5) or R1 resection (n = 5). R0 resection was achieved in 87.2% of the Tp, and 92.7% of the Gp (p = 0.623). Median number of metastatic lymph nodes (2 (range: 0–39) vs. 0 (range: 0–16), p = 0.021) and the perineural invasion rate (52.2% vs. 24.4%, p = 0.008) were significantly higher in Tp.

Determinants of major pathological response

The mPR rate was significantly higher in the Gp group. (36% vs. 18.5%, p = 0.04). Of the Tp 6 (13%) had complete, 3 (6.5%) had near-complete, 16 (34.8%) had partial, and 21 (45.7%) had no pathological response, while these numbers were 9 (18%), 9 (18%), 11 (22%), and 21 (42%) respectively in Gp. Although Turkish ethnicity was associated with worse mPR in univariate binary logistic regression (OR: 0.324, 95% CI: 0.144–0.974, p = 0.044), it did not showed any significance association in multivariate analysis. The patients with clinical nodal negative disease had a higher likelihood of mPR according to univariate (OR 5.537, 95%CI :1.762–16.292, p = 0.003) and multivariate (OR: 3.951, 95% CI: 1.168–13.365, p = 0.027) binary logistic regression analysis. Other clinical factors including tumor differentiation, tumor location, tumor type, carcinoembriyogenic antigen, gender, age and the number of neoadjuvant FLOT cycles were not associated with pathological response (Table 3).

Survival analysis

The median follow-up time was longer in Gp (47.9 months, 95% CI: 37.9–58.3), compared to Tp (26.2 months, 95% CI: 21.9–31.2) (p < 0.001). The median OS was significantly higher in Gp (57.9 months vs. 29.3 months, p = 0.005) (Fig. 1A). Gp also had better DFS than Tp (53.2 months, 95% CI: 26.4–79.9 vs. 21.42 months, 95% CI: 14.3–28.4, p = 0.03) (Fig. 1B).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of neoadjuvant/perioperative FLOT chemotherapy in Turkish (Tp) and German (Gp) patients with locally advanced gastric or gastroesophageal junction cancer. The primary endpoint, mPR, was lower in Tp (18.5%) than in Gp (36.0%). Clinical lymph node status was the single most important determinant of mPR rather than ethnicity and preoperative treatment intensity. Although Gp had significantly longer DFS and OS, mPR was not associated with OS and PFS. Independent predictors of OS were higher pathological N-stage, signet-ring cell carcinoma subtype, and receiving more intense neoadjuvant FLOT (> 4 cycles). Safety profiles differed between groups. Overall, these results indicate that baseline clinical characteristics, particularly nodal status, are more critical determinants of treatment response and prognosis rather than ethnicity.

FLOT4 and FLOT-AIO trials showed survival benefit and better pathological response rates of perioperative FLOT compared to perioperative ECF/ECX treatment in patients with resectable GC/GEJC [5, 6]. A meta-analysis revealed that pathological complete response after neoadjuvant chemotherapy improved DFS and OS in patients with resectable GC/GEJC in an Asian cohort [15], supporting mPR as clinically meaningful primary endpoints in neoadjuvant trials. However, it remains unclear whether ethnicity influences pathological response to neoadjuvant FLOT.

Interim analysis of the MATTERHORN study showed that German patients with resectable GC/GEJC had higher pCR rates to durvalumab + FLOT (30%) compared to placebo + FLOT (13%) [7]. These results raise the question of whether ethnicity plays a role in the pathological response to neoadjuvant FLOT in patients with resectable GC/GEJC. According to FLOT4-AIO trial which was conducted in 28 German oncology centers, the ratio of mPR was 37% [6]. Several other retrospective German studies also showed a similar ratio of mPR (31.9% and 39.1%) to neoadjuvant FLOT [11, 16]. In contrast, studies from non-German populations reported lower rates, including 22% in Japan and 23.8% in Turkey [10, 12]. Although they did not report near complete pathological response rates, the complete response rates were 9.8%, 3% and 4.6% in Dutch, Portuguese and Indian studies, respectively [17–19]. Our cohort showed mPR rates of 36% in German patients (Gp) and 19.5% in Turkish patients (Tp), consistent with previous literature in these ethnicities. Although these results could suggest a potential effect of ethnicity on pathological response, our analysis indicated that the primary determinant of reduced mPR was clinical node-positive disease, representing higher baseline tumor burden rather than ethnicity per se. This distinction is important because pathological response reflects tumor regression after treatment, whereas nodal positivity indicates initial disease extent, which independently predicts both pathological response and survival.

Predictors of pCR in the literature are heterogeneous, including non-signet cell histology, neoadjuvant chemoradiation, tumor grade, carcinoembryonic antigen levels, lymphocyte ratio, monocyte count, and tumor differentiation [20, 21]. In our cohort, interestingly, despite differences in baseline tumor characteristics and number of neoadjuvant cycles, most clinical parameters were not significant predictors of mPR in univariate analysis, while ethnicity was. However, baseline node positivity remained the strongest predictor, retaining significance in both univariate and multivariate analyses.

Variability in tumor regression grading (TRG) systems—Dworak, Mandard, Ryan, Becker, and JGCA-TRG—and differences in pathological evaluation may further contribute to inconsistencies across studies [22]. Pathological response assessment can vary between pathologists, leading to potential over- or underestimation. Smyth et al. reported tumor regression-like features even in patients without neoadjuvant therapy, highlighting inherent variability [23]. Interobserver agreement also differs across TRG systems, with Becker’s classification being more reproducible and prognostically relevant [24]. Although both Turkish and German pathologists in our study used Becker’s system, residual variability may have influenced the results. More standardized and robust TRG systems are needed to identify precise predictors of pCR.

The primary endpoint of this study, mPR, is not only a key measure of neoadjuvant FLOT efficacy but also informs adjuvant therapy decisions. Recent findings from the SPACE-FLOT study indicate that patients with partial pathological response derive the most benefit from adjuvant therapy, whereas those with complete or no response show limited benefit [25]. In line with this, a recent real-world study also reported that adjuvant chemotherapy might be unnecessary, particularly for patients achieving a mPR [26]. In our cohort, particularly among Turkish patients, receiving intensified neoadjuvant FLOT cycles did not improve mPR or R0 resection rates, suggesting that some patients may have undergone unnecessarily prolonged treatment. Additionally, the proportion of patients receiving any form of adjuvant therapy was higher in the Turkish group, often with alternative regimens such as FOLFOX to improve postoperative tolerability. Taken together with the SPACE-FLOT findings, it is possible that some patients may have been overtreated due to the lack of response-guided adaptation. Conversely, several retrospective series from the different countries have shown that perioperative treatment completion and appropriate sequencing are associated with improved survival [27, 28]. We believe that our findings complement the emerging evidence from SPACE-FLOT and underscore the need for further prospective research to clarify the optimal duration and sequence of perioperative chemotherapy in real-world practice, especially as chemoimmunotherapy is likely to become a new standard in this setting with the results of the MATTERHORN trial [8].

Although mPR is commonly used as an endpoint to assess early response to neoadjuvant therapy, its validity as a surrogate for long-term survival remains uncertain. While the FLOT-AIO trial demonstrated that pCR was associated with improved survival [5], other studies reported no clear survival benefit despite higher pathological response rates. For example, in the KEYNOTE-585 trial, adding pembrolizumab to platinum–fluoropyrimidine doublet therapy significantly improved pCR rates by 10.9%, but this did not translate into better EFS or OS [29]. Similarly, in our cohort, the primary endpoint of mPR was not associated with DFS or OS, underscoring that pathological response alone may not fully capture the survival benefit of perioperative treatment. It also remains unclear whether the therapeutic effect emerges predominantly during the neoadjuvant or adjuvant phase. A pooled analysis of two large prospective randomized trials (UK MRC OE05 and ST03) demonstrated that pathological lymph node status, rather than pathological tumor response, was the strongest determinant of overall survival [30]. Post-hoc analysis of the MAGIC trial found that favorable tumor regression (TRG 1–2) correlated with improved OS in chemotherapy-treated patients, but this effect was not independent of lymph node status, which remained the strongest predictor of survival [23]. Consistent with these findings, our study also showed that lymph node status was the most significant factor influencing both pathological response and survival, further supporting that mPR should not be adopted as an only primary endpoint in clinical trials.

As a secondary endpoint, OS and DFS were evaluated, both being shorter in the Tp. Despite differences in baseline tumor characteristics, multivariate analysis showed that pathological N stage, signet-cell carcinoma, and receiving more intense neoadjuvant FLOT were independently associated with worse OS. However, the primary endpoint, mPR, was not associated with OS or DFS. Median OS of Tp was relatively shorter compared to the European literature. For example, previous and current German studies found a median OS of nearly 50 mo [5, 11]. Geerts et al. reported a median OS of 48.1 mo in their Dutch study [17]. High number of clinical node-positive diseases and lower ypN0 diseases in Tp might explain the poorer survival ratios in Tp. Although ethnicity may also impact these different results, in our analysis, ethnicity was only significant predictor for OS in univariate analysis but not in multivariate analysis. Additionally, a recent real-world analysis from Germany also showed a surprisingly low overall survival (19.4 months), further indicating that tumor characteristics are the primary determinant rather than ethnicity.

Particularly interesting were the effects of the number of FLOT therapy cycles on survival. Contrary to expectations, patients receiving more intensified neoadjuvant cycles (>4 cycles) had poorer median OS, even after adjustment. The standard perioperative FLOT regimen consists of four neoadjuvant cycles followed by surgery and four adjuvant cycles [5]. Some trials evaluated intensified (more than four) or total neoadjuvant (eight cycles) FLOT. However, several trials evaluated intensified neoadjuvant (more than four cycles) or total neoadjuvant (eight cycles of neoadjuvant treatment) FLOT. A German study comparing four vs. six neoadjuvant cycles reported higher R0 resection rates and lower tumor stage with intensified FLOT, though some complications were more common, and OS was not reported [31]. A Chilean study analyzing total neoadjuvant FLOT found similar pathological responses but higher adverse effects and shorter OS (21.3 vs. 50 mo), with more node-positive disease, similar to our Tp [32]. In our cohort, approximately half of Tp received more than four cycles of neoadjuvant FLOT. This was largely due to for the desire to keep the patients’ chemotherapy-free period before surgery shorter against possible surgery delays, as most of the patients received this treatment during the COVID-19 pandemic period, when surgeries had to be postponed. However, local differences in treatment approach also affected this pattern. In this study, we found that receiving more than four neoadjuvant FLOT cycles was associated with poor OS, and this is a research topic that should be evaluated in larger cohorts. Giving long courses of preoperative chemotheraphy could result in delayed surgery in Tp. So that the carcinoma and its lymph nodes metastasis could have chance to grow and thereby worsening the outcome of patients, especially in those who did not adequately respond to chemotherapy. As discussed previously, and in line with the results of the SPACE-FLOT trial [25], some patients in our cohort received unnecessarily prolonged chemotherapy in either the neoadjuvant or adjuvant setting, which may partially explain the lower OS observed in the Tp.

The most common adverse events of FLOT are cytopenias, diarrhea, fatigue, and neuropathy [33]. In our cohort, adverse events differed between Tp and Gp: Tp had more anemia, possibly due to tumor location differences, while Gp had more neutropenia and neuropathy. The higher neutropenia in Gp may be partly explained by more frequent G-CSF use in Tp. Grade 3/4 neuropathy was similar, but any-grade neuropathy was higher in Gp (72.7% vs. 46.8%). This aligns with previous studies: the Japanese FLOT cohort had 47% any-grade neuropathy (similar to Tp), whereas the original German FLOT4 trial reported 71% (similar to Gp), suggesting potential population differences or reporting tendencies [5, 10].

This study has some limitations and strengths. First of all, the sample size was relatively small. Most importantly, the groups were heterogeneous regarding baseline tumor characteristics, surgical procedure types, and adjuvant treatment modalities. To address the heterogeneity between groups, we performed multivariate analyses with adjustment for baseline differences. However, despite these statistical adjustments, some residual confounding due to the retrospective design and population differences may remain. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, some data were missing. In addition, a standard evaluation may not have been provided since staging, treatment protocols, and follow-up protocols may differ between the two groups according to country standards. Additionally, we did not evaluated the immunohistochemistry and molecular basis of the tumors such as HER-2 status, PD-L1 expression and microsatellite instability which all can affect treatment responses and tumor behavior. Despite all this, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate ethnicity as a basis for perioperative FLOT treatment in resectable GC/GEJC patients. Additionally, since this study includes data from two different countries, it may provide more accurate information about real-life data regarding perioperative FLOT treatment.

Conclusion

In conclusion, although ethnicity and differences in clinical practice may partly contribute to variations in outcomes, our study demonstrates that lymph node status is the dominant prognostic factor in perioperatively treated gastroesophageal cancer. Intensification of therapy did not improve mPR and was even associated with worse survival. Clinical nodal positivity was the strongest predictor of mPR, yet mPR itself did not independently correlate with OS. Instead, pathological nodal positivity and signet-ring cell carcinoma histology emerged as the key determinants of survival. Optimizing perioperative therapy should therefore focus less on treatment intensification and more on patient selection and nodal risk stratification, with close surveillance and individualized management particularly warranted in patients with nodal involvement or signet-ring cell carcinoma.
