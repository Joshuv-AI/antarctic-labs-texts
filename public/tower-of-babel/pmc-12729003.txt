Arterial collagen IV and laminin levels associate with type 2 diabetes, glycemic control, major adverse cardiac events, and mortality in coronary bypass patients
Authors: Anne-Sofie Allermann Faarvang, Martin Overgaard, Pia Søndergaard Jensen, Lars Peter Schødt Riber, Mette Wod, Lasse Bach Steffensen, Lars Melholt Rasmussen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is associated with arterial diseases; particularly, the incidence of atherosclerotic conditions is increased among patients with diabetes. Basement membrane (BM) protein levels are increased in the artery wall of T2DM patients, but the underlying mechanisms and potential consequences remain elusive. This study aimed to identify putative connections between the level of arterial BM components, clinical characteristics, all-cause mortality and major adverse events (MACE) in groups of patients with and without T2DM.MethodsInternal thoracic artery samples from 668 patients undergoing coronary artery bypass graft surgery at Odense University Hospital, Denmark, from 2008 to 2018 were included, of which 27% were diagnosed with T2DM. Seventeen vascular proteins were quantified using liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry.ResultsLevels of BM components—collagen IV, laminin, and perlecan—were significantly elevated in T2DM patients compared to non-diabetic controls. In the diabetic group, we found strong correlations between collagen IV and laminin levels and hemoglobin A1c, whereas correlations with plasma lipids, blood pressure, and body mass index (BMI) were weak or absent. Moreover, collagen IV and laminin were associated with both all-cause mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) up to 14 years after surgery after adjustment for age, sex, and comorbidities.ConclusionsWe demonstrate that arterial basement membrane protein accumulation is related to glycemic status in T2DM. Furthermore, the observed associations between collagen IV and laminin levels and clinical outcomes support the notion that generalized alterations in arterial basement membranes may contribute to the development of cardiovascular disease.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-025-03026-7.

Research insights

What is currently known about this topic?

Data on molecular alterations in human arterial tissue from individuals with diabetes are scarce. Previous studies, however, have shown that arterial basement membrane components accumulate in the diabetic vasculature.

What is the key research question?

Is the arterial content of basement membrane proteins associated with glycemia and other cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors, and are these protein levels linked to the incidence of CVD events?

What is new?

Arterial levels of specific basement membrane proteins show a relatively strong positive association with glycemia (HbA1c), but weaker associations with other CVD risk factors, including lipid levels, blood pressure, and BMI. Higher protein levels are further associated with an increased incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE).

How might this study influence clinical practice?

Improved understanding of diabetes-related molecular alterations in the arterial wall—particularly in basement membrane composition—may uncover novel therapeutic targets to prevent vascular complications in diabetes.

Background

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) affects an estimated 537 million adults worldwide and is considered one of the most serious chronic illnesses of today [1, 2]. With prevalence on the rise globally, T2DM is a leading cause of cardiovascular complications and death [3] as well as a major economic burden [4]. It is well established that T2DM is associated with increased risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) [5], whereas the pathophysiological explanation remain, at least partly unknown. Effects of diabetes on the arterial wall can also be observed in other ways, such as increased arterial stiffness [6], disturbed vascular remodeling capacities [7], endothelial dysfunction [8] and accumulation of advanced glycation end products [9]. Collectively, these arterial conditions in diabetes support the idea, that there are specific links between the diabetic condition and widespread molecular and cellular changes in the arterial wall, similar to the situation in smaller vessels [10, 11].

To unravel molecular characteristics of arterial tissue in diabetes, we have previously performed unbiased proteome studies on human internal thoracic arteries (ITAs) and have identified basement membrane proteins as significantly upregulated in patients with T2DM [12]. The study artery is similar to coronary arteries in size, but is free from atherosclerotic plaques. In addition, coronary arteries are muscular type, while the ITA has both elastic properties proximally and muscular properties distally. Thus, the ITA can be used to determine putative effects of chronic metabolic changes on vascular cells, without inflammatory cells [11]. In a follow-up study, we could show that this accumulation of basement membrane proteins seemed to be part of more general arterial alteration, since it was found in other arterial beds as well and the accumulation was limited to tunica media of arteries rich in vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) [13]. These findings are in line with the knowledge that basement membrane accumulation is part of vascular alterations in diabetes, where particularly electron microscopically defined basement membrane accumulation has been described in kidney glomeruli vessels and in capillaries from many different locations [14, 15]. The most abundant part of basement membranes in the arterial wall is located around the individual vascular smooth muscle cells. The function of the arterial basement membranes are only partly known, but is believed to relate to both mechanical support of vascular cells, cellular communications and permeability of the tissue [16]. The most prominent proteins in arterial basement membranes are collagen IV, alpha 1 and alpha 2 chains. Interestingly, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the gene coding for arterial collagen IV (the COL4A1 and COL4A2 locus on chromosome 13) have in numerous genome wide association studies (GWAS) been identified as associated with several arterial diseases such as atherosclerotic conditions (myocardial infarction and peripheral arterial disease), abdominal aortic aneurysms, coronary artery dissections and arterial stiffness [17–22]. The molecular composition of basement membranes extends beyond collagens, as both glycoproteins and proteoglycans are included [23]. The glycoprotein laminin, which consists of three peptide chains (alpha (1–5), beta (1–2) and gamma (1–3)), can bind integrins and is involved in a number of cellular functions [24]. The proteoglycan perlecan is also a prominent part of basement membranes and has been shown to be important for the phenotype of vascular cells, among other things [25].

Overall, biochemical analyses point towards dysregulation of the arterial basement membrane in diabetes and genetic studies suggest that the most abundant protein in these structures, collagen IV, is involved in the development of arterial diseases. However, the putative clinical associations and pathophysiological roles of dysregulated arterial basement membranes in diabetes are unknown. In this study, we consequently investigate relations between several clinical characteristics and the arterial amounts of basement membrane proteins in groups of patients with and without T2DM. Moreover, we have examined putative prospective associations between the arterial amounts of basement membrane proteins and mortality and major cardiovascular events (MACE) in a follow-up period after coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery. To this end, we have applied our previously developed targeted proteomics methods based on liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) on small amounts of the internal thoracic artery [26]. Since our focus is on putative clinical associations with the amounts of arterial basement membrane proteins—particularly collagen IV (α1 and α2 chains), laminin (γ chain), and perlecan—these proteins were naturally included in the targeted LC–MS panel. The panel also comprises other abundant vascular proteins, selected for their high abundance and compatibility with LC–MS analysis, enabling reliable quantification in very small tissue samples and serving as suitable control proteins in this study [26]. We believe that potential positive findings demonstrating associations between arterial basement membrane levels and clinical parameters in humans may stimulate further mechanistic studies in model systems pointing towards new treatment targets against arterial complications in diabetes.

Methods

All patients, who were scheduled for a CABG operation at Odense University Hospital between 2008 and 2018, were potentially eligible for participation in the study (n = 3577) (see Fig. 1). Once admitted to hospital for CABG surgery, each patient was given oral and written study information and written consent was obtained. Patients were excluded if they were very nervous about the CABG surgery (not able to comprehend study information). Some patients were not included due to practical reasons (i.e. no time to obtain consent before surgery or not qualified staff at hand) and other patients declined participation after being informed (n = 2789). Furthermore, some patients’ tissue samples were not available for proteome analysis (arterial sample had been used for other purposes or sample was poor quality) (n = 63) and some samples failed to generate proteome data when analyzed (n = 57) (see Fig. 1). In total, we included 668 patients in this study: 181 with type 2 diabetes mellitus and 487 controls.Fig. 1Overview of patient selection: included and excluded cases

Overview of patient selection: included and excluded cases

During the surgery, a small section of the repair artery (internal thoracic artery) was secured and immediately transferred to a formalin suspension (4% buffered paraformaldehyde). After the sample had been fixated for 24–48 h at room temperature, each sample was embedded in paraffin before long term storage at Odense Artery Biobank (Odense, Denmark).

Baseline data regarding diabetes status, body mass index, smoking habits, laboratory results and blood pressure measurements were collected at the time of operation through the electronic patient registry system. When collecting baseline data, some patients had not filled out the questionnaire, or the laboratory results were not analyzed, leading to some missing data.

Follow-up data regarding medicine consumption, comorbidities, and death were retrieved from the Danish national registries at Statistics Denmark. An individual was assigned to the diabetic group if one or more of the following were true: diagnosis of T2DM according to the patient, HbA1c at or before surgery above 48 mmol/mol, T2DM diagnosis in national registries (ICD-10 code DE11) or the use of diabetes medication based on national registries.

Targeted proteomics

Proteome analysis of 17 selected endogenous tissue proteins was performed using a novel optimized protocol for extraction and quantitation of proteins [26]. In short, five 10-µm paraffin-embedded tissue Sects. (50 µm in total) were collected and stored at − 80 °C. Samples were then deparaffinized at 56 °C, washed, and the tissue was dissolved in extraction buffer with added control protein before heat denaturation. Proteins were precipitated with trichloroacetic acid, washed in acetone, dried, and then reduced, alkylated, and digested with the enzyme trypsin overnight. After acidification and centrifugation, samples were stored at − 20 °C. A prepared mixture of 34 synthetic internal standard peptides was added in approximately a one-to-one ratio to the endogenous peptides. The resulting peptide mixtures were loaded onto Evotip columns and analysed by liquid chromatography coupled to multiple-reaction-monitoring mass spectrometry using an Evosep One system coupled to a TSQ Altis mass spectrometer [26].

Data sources and statistics

This study was based on baseline data from patient records, generated biochemical data as described above and data from Danish nationwide health registries. Data were included from (1) The Central Person Register (CPR) [27] which contains basic data on all citizens of Denmark, (2) the Danish National Patient Registry (NPR) [28, 29] which includes details on hospitalizations for all Danish citizens since 1977, (3) the Danish National Prescription Registry (DNPR) which contains data on all redeemed prescriptions in Denmark since 1995 [30]. All data were analyzed using Stata Statistical Software (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas, USA) [31].

In this study, some baseline data were missing due to two main reasons: (1) certain data were not collected because they were not deemed necessary for clinical treatment (ie. no HbA1c if the patient has no diabetes), and (2) other data, such as BMI, may have been measured but were not recorded in the patient records. To ensure the reliability of our analyses, no imputation methods were applied, as such procedures could introduce bias. Instead, analyses were performed using only complete cases. Protein data were calculated as a ratio between endogenous (light) peptide and synthetic (heavy) peptide. Furthermore, protein data were normalized to the amount of endogenous smooth muscle actin in each sample to minimize variation as previously described [26]. Protein data were not normally distributed. However, as the data displayed a near-normal distribution and the sample size was large enough in all subgroups, we applied the Pearson correlation test to establish correlations between proteins and clinical characteristics. In addition, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied to analyze differences between groups [32].

To evaluate follow-up events, the Cox proportional hazards model was applied with adjustment for age, sex and Charlson comorbidity score. Proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals and assumptions were met for all proteins. Hazard ratios (HRs) were reported with 95% confidence intervals [33]. MACE was defined as the occurrence of either stroke, acute myocardial infarction or death based on registered ICD (International Classification of Diseases) codes (see Supplemental Table 7). The Charlson Comorbidity Index was calculated based on comorbidities documented prior to surgery [34]. The proteomic data were log-transformed to ensure comparability of hazard ratios across proteins with differing expression scales and to stabilise variance. This transformation also facilitated interpretation of effect sizes in the Cox models. Model diagnostics confirmed improved distributional properties and indicated that the transformation did not adversely affect model fit. For survival analysis, protein data were divided into four groups: high and low protein levels (divided at median value) in combination with diabetes vs. control group.

Results

The study population consisted of 668 individuals with 86% males (see Table 1)). This sex distribution was comparable in both the diabetic and non-diabetic group and is aligned with the known characteristics of patients undergoing CABG [35]. The participants had a median age of approx. 67 years. In the diabetic group, median body mass index (BMI) and median HbA1c were significantly higher than in the control group. Low density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol was lower among patients with diabetes, which may relate to a more intensive lipid lowering treatment among these patients. High density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol was lower and triglycerides higher in the diabetic group, probably related to obesity and insulin resistance. HbA1c was significantly correlated to collagen IV in the diabetic group, but not in the control group (see Fig. 2). Cox regression analysis was performed to assess putative relations between protein amounts and all-cause mortality and the development of MACE after the CABG operation (see Supplemental Table 6). For collagen IV, alpha 1, the crude HR for mortality was 2.43 (1.42–4.16) in the total population and 1.88 (1.04–3.37), when adjusted for age, sex and Charlson Comorbidity Index (Table 4). For collagen IV, alpha 2 and laminin, similar results were found, as can be seen in Table 4. Cox regression analysis performed only in the diabetic group showed similar trends, i.e. correlations between the protein amounts and mortality but with less statistical prominence, most likely due to lower number of patients and events. In the adjusted analysis, the remaining 13 proteins were not significantly associated to mortality (see Supplemental Material). In interaction analyses, the diabetes × protein term was not statistically significant (p-value > 0.05), indicating no formal evidence of effect modification by diabetes status. Stratified analyses showed that higher BM protein levels were associated with adverse outcomes in both diabetic and non-diabetic patients, with a numerically stronger association in the diabetic group. This pattern was consistent with the Kaplan–Meier curves, which demonstrated a stepwise increase in risk across the four groups (see Fig. 3 and Supplemental Figs. 1 and 2). Discussion

In this study, we investigate changes in the composition of human arterial tissue in individuals with and without T2DM and presumed relations between such changes and clinical features. Advancements in proteomic technologies, particularly targeted proteomics, have given us the opportunity to investigate the arterial molecular composition of small amounts of human arteries in a rather large population [26]. By enabling the simultaneous quantification of specific proteins associated with vascular basement membranes together with other extracellular and intracellular abundant arterial proteins, we have been able to study associations between the amount of these proteins in FFPE artery specimens and clinical features of patients undergoing CABG surgery. Since our previously published studies consistently have observed increased amounts of collagen IV (alpha 1 and 2) and laminin(γ) in human arteries from individuals with diabetes, this studies aim is to test these proteins for putative associations to diabetes, risk factors and outcomes—results from other arterial proteins in the targeted proteome dataset is viewed as controls. Overall, our data show that the increased levels of arterial basement membrane proteins, i.e. collagen IV, alpha 1 and alpha 2, together with laminin, in individuals with T2DM are associated with glycemic status, as determined by HbA1c, but only weakly correlated to other clinical cardiovascular risk factors. Moreover, our prospective results suggest that the arterial amounts of basement membrane proteins are associated with clinical outcomes after surgery (MACE and mortality). However, BM protein levels predicted adverse outcomes in both diabetic and non-diabetic patients. Despite a non-significant interaction term, the effect size was larger among patients with diabetes, in line with the graded risk pattern seen in the Kaplan–Meier analyses. These findings indicate that BM accumulation reflects a general vascular risk mechanism, which appears accentuated in diabetes.

To our knowledge, this study is the first of its kind with a study population of 668 patients to analyze the composition of human arterial tissue using targeted proteome analysis. The perspectives of the findings of this study include a deeper understanding of diabetic arterial pathology and uncovering of the significance of arterial molecular changes. We have chosen the internal thoracic artery as study object, which causes both advantages and limitations. First, the artery is easily available from CABG operations and from the standpoint of performing robust biochemical analysis, the artery is relatively homogenous compared to i.e. atherosclerotic plaque material, which is extremely heterogenous. The material consists mainly of vascular smooth muscle cells in tunica media and their matrix surroundings [12] and our results therefore reflects the state of this cell type in normal appearing arteries. Clearly our findings, especially concerning relations to outcomes, cannot be attributed to the thoracic artery itself, but should be seen as a marker for generalized arterial changes in diabetes, i.e. alterations which may appear also in non-atherosclerotic parts of the arterial tree. Such generalized changes have for many years been recognized in smaller vessels in diabetes and accumulation of basement membranes is, indeed, considered a critical component of diabetic microangiopathy, manifesting across various tissues affected by diabetes, i.e. the kidney and retina [36, 37]. As our previous discovery studies has shown, basement membrane alterations are also seen in several arteries [12, 13]. The limitations in using the thoracic artery of course also include the fact that observed alterations here may not reflect all arteries, for example in coronary and carotid arteries, which are much more prone to ASCVD. Clearly, it would also be interesting to obtain results concerning supposed basement membranes in atherosclerotic plaques from coronary or carotid arteries, but proteomic studies would demand microdissection prior to analysis to reduce variance due to the heterogeneous nature of such materials.

For our targeted proteomic method, we chose 17 proteins based on previous experiments from discovery proteomics [12, 13, 26]. The basement membrane proteins were selected because they previously showed the most prominent alterations in diabetes, whereas other abundant matrix components (for example collagen I and collagen III) did not show differences. Moreover, we have included several highly abundant proteins, of which some may reflect the phenotype of smooth muscle cells.

Our results indicate that generalized accumulation of arterial basement membrane proteins, i.e. collagen IV, alpha 1 and alpha 2, and laminin, is closely related to the glycemic status, which is consistent with findings from microvasculature, where BM accumulation is related to glycemia [38, 39]. This may be due to non-enzymatic glycation on some of the molecular components, which has been shown to occur [40, 41]. The mechanisms underlying the accumulation of arterial basement membrane components remain nevertheless poorly understood. However, low-grade inflammation may contribute to this process in the context of both diabetes and ASCVD. It is important to note, however, that the non-atherosclerotic vessel studied (ITA) does not itself contain inflammatory cells [12]. Moreover, our observations are compatible with the idea that BM accumulation is a part of generalized alterations in diabetes and may be involved in the development of adverse clinical outcome. Unfortunately, not much information is available concerning arterial BM involvement in arterial diseases. It may be speculated that collagen IV is associated with altered structural integrity of the arterial wall and that the protein could be involved in both remodeling, stiffness, and perhaps also susceptibility to ASCVD. In a previous experimental study of mice, genetically engineered to express only the half amounts of collagen IV, we could observe that all basement membrane proteins consequently were reduced and that the phenotypic state of vascular smooth cells were altered in these animals. In addition, the susceptibility to experimentally induced aneurysms was changed [42]. Clearly, the amount of collagen IV plays relevant roles, but knowledge about the development of ASCVD and its complications is still lacking. It is however worth mentioning that the genetic locus for collagen IV (COL4A1/A2 locus on chromosome 13) has been identified in many GWAS studies to be involved in several arterial conditions, underscoring the hypothesis that this protein has profound effects on arterial pathophysiology [43–45]. Laminin, a large adhesion molecule attached to the cell membrane and to basement membrane components, could perhaps play an independent role in relation to the functional state of vascular cells [46]. Furthermore, alterations in another key basement membrane component, perlecan—a large proteoglycan—may influence both lipid binding and smooth muscle cell function, particularly through its role in maintaining cells in a quiescent, highly differentiated state, an aspect considered important in atherogenesis [44, 45 ,47, 48]. In this context, the increased levels observed in diabetes and in particular the relations between high amounts of arterial perlecan and mortality and MACE are somewhat unexpected. However, it is important to note that we measured only the perlecan core protein, not the heparan sulfate side chains, where many of this proteoglycan’s functions are believed to reside. The situation might therefore differ if the side chains were analyzed.

This study also had some limitations. The study population is a selected group with known cardiovascular disease, as all patients had CABG surgery due to occlusion of cardiac arteries. As expected, this study population also had comorbidities (see Supplemental Table 2. Some literature suggests a link between autoimmune conditions and vascular remodeling [49]. However, only few of the included individuals had known autoimmune disease at the time of surgery according to the Danish National Patient Registry. Therefore, a subgroup analysis could not be performed due to the limited sample size. Other comorbidities mainly included cardiovascular conditions such as hypertension.

In addition, routine hospital discharge data could include coding errors. However, studies have shown that the discharge diagnoses used in this study have a high positive predictive value [28]. Also, information obtained from the patient may be affected by recall-bias. As with all observational studies, there may have been residual confounding due to imperfectly measured factors, as well as confounding from unmeasured factors such as socioeconomic factors and lifestyle factor. We did, however, include smoking status and BMI as a proxy for lifestyle factors.

Conclusions

In this study, we present novel insights into the molecular composition of arterial tissue in T2DM using a targeted proteome approach. We see significantly increased levels of the proteins laminin (γ), actin cytoplasmic 1, alpha actinin 4, perlecan and collagen IV, alpha 1 and alpha 2, in patients with T2DM. Furthermore, collagen IV, alpha 1 and alpha 2, and laminin (γ) was significantly correlated to HbA1c.

In analyses of arterial proteins and clinical outcomes, laminin and collagen IV showed higher hazard ratios for all-cause mortality and MACE, although these findings should be interpreted with caution. Our findings are consistent with the concept that arterial basement membrane components accumulate as a consequence of hyperglycemia and suggest that altered basement membranes may have previously unrecognized pathophysiological roles. We propose that future research focusing on the turnover and functional properties of arterial basement membranes may identify novel therapeutic targets for the prevention of macrovascular complications in diabetes.
