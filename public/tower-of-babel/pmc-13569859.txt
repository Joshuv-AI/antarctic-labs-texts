The Suture-Action-Gaze Efficiency (SAGE) guidance framework reveals expert gaze-action coupling based on a case study of simple interrupted suturing on an artificial skin pad
Authors: Nutcha Yodrabum, Taravichet Titijaroonroj, Sorachat Sungkhapan, Narisara Rinkaew, Kanlaya Boonlha, Nattanit Poungjantaradej, Sirin Apichonbancha, Pornsuree Jamsri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Simple interrupted suturing is a foundational surgical skill, yet the expert micro-behaviors that support efficient performance remain undercharacterized, particularly in open surgery at the level of individual stitches. This study examined expert gaze–action coupling during simple interrupted suturing and developed an empirically derived behavioral framework to translate these patterns into structured training guidance. Eight board-certified surgeons performed 335 standardized simple interrupted sutures on artificial skin while wearing Tobii Pro Glasses 3. Gaze and video streams were synchronized and segmented into stitch-level motor events. Analyses were conducted at both the stitch and subject levels using mean comparison tests and mixed-effects models. Four reproducible expert micro-behaviors were identified. First, surgeons typically directed initial fixation toward functional instrument tips rather than handles. Second, the right-to-left insertion pattern was more frequently used and was associated with shorter insertion times than the right-middle-left pattern, whereas wound-edge squeezing showed a descriptive stitch-level association that did not remain significant in mixed-effects analysis. Third, complete three-wrap knots were associated with efficient knot tying, while additional wraps and incomplete knots prolonged execution. Fourth, re-grasping efficiency was associated more strongly with technique than with needle placement location, with Gather-in-Hand and Designated Needle Placement supporting faster transitions than Slide-and-Secure. These findings were synthesized into the Suture–Action–Gaze Efficiency (SAGE) framework. SAGE should be interpreted as an observational, expert-derived behavioral guide rather than a validated causal training model.

Introduction

Mastering a simple interrupted suture is one of the earliest and most fundamental technical milestones in surgical training. Despite its apparent simplicity, novice error rates remain high, largely because traditional apprenticeship models–often summarized as “see one, do one, teach one”–rely heavily on tacit knowledge transmission and individualized coaching that is difficult to standardize1–4. While trainees may observe expert hand movements, they rarely gain access to the visual priorities and anticipatory cues that guide those movements. The perceptual strategies enabling efficient needle placement, knot formation, and stitch transition therefore remain largely implicit rather than explicitly taught.

In response to these limitations, surgical education has progressively adopted simulation-based training modalities and technology-enhanced learning5–7, including bench/cadaveric simulation and immersive platforms such as VR/AR, as well as AI-driven video analytics for objective assessment8–20. These approaches improve safety, accessibility, and scalability, but most evaluate performance retrospectively–focusing on outcomes or kinematics rather than the cognitive–visual processes that precede motor execution11,12. Consequently, although modern tools can measure how a stitch is performed, they often fail to reveal how experts allocate attention during the critical moments that structure each stitch cycle.

Eye tracking (ET) is a technology that measures where and how a person directs visual attention. It records eye movements such as fixations (when the eyes stay on one point), saccades (quick movements between points), and gaze patterns over time. By analyzing these movements, researchers can objectively understand how people process information, prioritize visual cues, and make decisions during a task. Unlike self-reports, which depend on what a person remembers or explains, eye tracking provides direct and unbiased data about attention. Because eye movements are closely linked to cognitive processes, ET serves as a bridge between observable behavior and internal mental activity. It helps reveal not only what someone does, but also how they think while performing a task.

In medical training, eye tracking captures where a surgeon looks just before making a movement. Studies show that fixations often occur about 200 ms before a motor action21. This makes it possible to identify the subtle visual–motor decisions that shape surgical performance. In this way, ET provides insight into how experts allocate attention during a procedure, not only the final outcome. These insights can be translated into guidance for novices to improve their cognitive strategy during training. In contrast, VR-, AR-, and AI-based systems typically evaluate performance after an action is completed and do not directly reflect the practitioner’s understanding during task execution. From a computer vision perspective, eye tracking uses cameras and algorithms to detect and follow eye movements. High-resolution images of the eyes are analyzed to track pupil position and corneal reflections, allowing systems to determine where a person is looking.

Recent systematic reviews consistently confirm that gaze-fixation patterns reliably differentiate experts from novices across procedures, including laparoscopy, arthroscopy, and basic suturing22. A 2024 scoping review of 32 dual-eye-tracking studies further underscored ET’s robustness by quantifying instructor–learner visual synchrony as a marker of “visual leadership”23. Complementary neuro-ergonomic work that combined ET with electroencephalography achieved multimodal prediction models explaining up to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^{2}=0.68$$\end{document} of performance variance and learning rate on da Vinci simulator tasks24. By revealing these hidden perceptual-motor strategies, ET diagnostics offer surgical educators detailed, significantly surpassing traditional simulation and video analytics25.

Tobii eye-tracking technology serves as a bridge between computer vision and behavioral science, enabling the objective measurement of visual attention and cognitive processes. Utilizing Pupil Center Corneal Reflection (PCCR) technology, Tobii hardware–such as the Tobii Pro Spectrum or Glasses 3–employs infrared illuminators and high-resolution sensors to track eye movements like fixations and saccades. As highlighted by26, these systems leverage advanced computer vision algorithms to ensure data accuracy even under varying lighting conditions or head movements, providing a reliable window into a user’s subconscious reactions.

In practical application, Tobii technology uses specialized hardware, such as Tobii Pro Glasses 3, and analytical software, such as Tobii Pro Lab, to study how people interact with their environment. This system tracks eye movements to create data such as Heat Maps, which show what captures a person’s attention, and Time to First Fixation (TTFF), which measures how fast they notice a specific object. According to27, these tools are essential because they reveal “hidden thinking.” By mapping gaze patterns to specific Areas of Interest (AOI), researchers can understand a person’s decision-making and mental workload without requiring them to speak.

At the same time, Tobii technology serves as a vital communication interface for individuals with physical disabilities. For people living with Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS), who lose the ability to move or speak, eye-tracking allows them to type, talk, and control a computer using only their eye movements (28). These diverse uses demonstrate that eye-tracking is a powerful tool for both scientific research and inclusive human-computer interaction. Together, they provide strong empirical evidence for using Tobii technology to optimize how humans and machines work together.

However, a critical challenge in eye-tracking has shifted from how we capture data to how we translate it. Converting dense streams of gaze data into simple, clear guidelines for beginners remains difficult. Consequently, while eye-tracking is established in laparoscopy and robotics21,29, there is no systematic approach that translates these complex insights into practical advice for open suturing. This leaves beginners without clear, evidence-based tips to help them understand where to look during each step of a stitch.

In practice, simply capturing diagnostic data is insufficient for training; the primary challenge for intervention lies in translating these complex gaze datasets into intuitive, real-time pedagogical guidance. Beyond diagnostics, eye-tracking data have become essential for educational interventions designed to enhance skill acquisition. Recent advances integrate real-time ET metrics with augmented reality, enabling gaze-contingent holographic cues tailored to trainees’ immediate visual focus30. Additionally, live gaze-sharing systems project expert fixation points directly onto trainee fields of view, supporting immediate guidance and accelerating procedural learning31. Randomized controlled trials show that trainees receiving gaze-guided instruction perform faster, commit fewer errors, and experience reduced cognitive load compared with conventional training32. Large-scale studies now corroborate these benefits: an RCT in robotic-prostatectomy training (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n{=}137$$\end{document}) demonstrated that eye-movement-modelling examples improved landmark-identification accuracy by 21 %33, while a real-time AI coaching pipeline that fuses live ET with kinematics reduced critical errors by 26 % and maintained non-inferior OSATS scores relative to in-person instruction34.

By translating gaze metrics into structured guidance for beginners, ET enables training to focus on attentional patterns rather than outcomes alone. Instead of evaluating what was achieved after the action, it highlights where and when visual attention should be directed during task execution. This attention-based guidance provides learners with performing the procedure effectively.

In surgical education, eye-tracking aligns effectively with four key theoretical frameworks underpinning surgical skill acquisition, including Deliberate Practice, Cognitive Apprenticeship, Cognitive Load Theory, and Mastery Learning. This theoretical integration provides robust empirical validation and pedagogical guidance for ET application, thereby directly informing the SAGE framework. Deliberate practice theory relies heavily on immediate feedback and repetitive task engagement; eye-tracking operationalizes this by delivering instantaneous, gaze-contingent metrics that facilitate rapid skill refinement and mastery21,35,36. Similarly, the Cognitive Apprenticeship framework promotes effective learning through explicit modeling, real-time coaching, and gradually reduced instructional support. Live gaze sharing systems directly reflect this by visually translating expert perceptual strategies into actionable trainee guidance, significantly improving performance accuracy and cognitive efficiency31,37,38. Cognitive Load Theory suggests leveraging oculometric indicators, such as pupil dilation and fixation duration, for real-time detection and management of cognitive overload, ensuring optimal instructional conditions32,39. Finally, Mastery Learning emphasizes clear, criterion-based benchmarks, and eye-tracking provides precise gaze-derived metrics critical for structured advancement, ensuring robust and sustained skill retention22,40.

Although eye-tracking has been extensively applied to minimally invasive surgical tasks such as laparoscopic knot-tying, arthroscopy, and endoscopic camera navigation21,32, the simple interrupted suture, the first manoeuvre taught in every surgical curriculum, remains significantly under-examined at a truly stitch-level, micro-behavioral scale. Prior attempts to segment gaze by procedural phases typically aggregate fixation data across entire stitches25, thereby obscuring critical expert gaze-action strategies. This absence of detailed stitch-specific insights severely limits trainees’ ability to explicitly identify and replicate the precise micro-behaviors foundational to surgical training curricula.

Recognizing this critical gap, the present study examines expert surgeons’ gaze–action behavior at the level of individual stitches, based on 335 expert stitches performed by eight board-certified surgeons (median experience = 17 years) and recorded using Tobii Pro Glasses. This study is based on behavioral and statistical analysis rather than predictive modeling. Material and methods

This study followed a structured five-stage methodological pipeline. First, expert surgeons performed standardized simple interrupted sutures on artificial skin models under controlled laboratory conditions. Second, head-mounted eye-tracking data were synchronously recorded using Tobii Pro Glasses 3 (Tobii Pro Lab, Stockholm, Sweden), capturing gaze streams and high-definition video at 50 Hz. Third, motor events within each stitch cycle were temporally segmented using predefined event markers in Tobii Pro Lab, enabling precise alignment between gaze behavior and procedural steps. Fourth, gaze metrics–including fixation allocation, functional-to-handle (F/H) ratios, insertion times, knot-tying durations, and re-grasping transitions–were extracted and statistically analyzed using non-parametric tests. Finally, reproducible gaze–action couplings associated with procedural efficiency were distilled into structured behavioral benchmarks forming the Suture–Action–Gaze Efficiency (SAGE) framework (Fig. 1). In addition, we clarify that our study does not involve the use of human tissue, as all experiments were conducted using an artificial skin pad. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University (COA no. Si 026/2023). All experimental protocols were approved by this committee. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects prior to participation.Fig. 1Flowchart of Methodology.

Simple suture procedure

The simple interrupted suture is foundational in surgical wound closure, facilitating precise approximation of wound edges for optimal healing. Addressing ethical and reproducibility demands, this study employed artificial skin pads, eliminating patient risk and enabling trainees to confidently refine their skills through repeated, standardized practice. The standardized tactile feedback from artificial skin ensures reproducibility, ideal for precise procedural proficiency evaluation.

Participants performed suturing tasks at clearly marked, standardized workstations. Instrumentation was strictly standardized, including needle holders, forceps (toothed/non-toothed), scissors, and standardized suture threads (silk/nylon), as illustrated in Fig. 3 and Table 2. This consistent equipment setup ensured controlled, reproducible conditions.

The procedure began with precise needle insertion approximately 5 mm from the wound edge, guided by correct needle-holder placement one-third along the needle’s curvature (Fig. 4(a)). Participants employed either a Right-to-Middle-Left (RML) multi-step method for wider or higher-tension wounds (Fig. 4(c2)) or a Right-to-Left (RL) single-pass method for narrower wounds (≤ 5 mm; Fig. 4(c1)). Knot type selection was standardized based on wound tension, using surgeon’s knots for high-tension wounds and square knots for lower tension (Fig. 4(e)-(g)). Each knot was secured with 3–5 throws, ensuring stability. Suture ends were consistently trimmed to approximately 5 mm, minimizing irritation and entanglement risks. Procedural steps were closely monitored and recorded to ensure timing consistency and minimize variability.

Participants repeated the standardized suturing process at intervals of approximately 5–8 mm along the artificial wound, enhancing procedural fluency. Particular attention was directed toward standardized needle re-grasping and needle placement techniques (clearly depicted in Fig. 4(h)-(j)), ensuring consistent execution during repetitive tasks. Eye-tracking and video analysis

This study employed Tobii Pro Lab (TPL) software (version 1.217) to systematically analyze participants’ gaze behaviors during the simple interrupted suture procedure. TPL was selected specifically for its comprehensive analytical capabilities, enabling precise synchronization between gaze data and corresponding video segments, enhancing analytical precision.

The analytical process consisted of five structured steps: (1) importing recorded video data into a TPL project; (2) selecting specific analysis functions (Event, Time of Interest (TOI), Area of Interest (AOI), Visualization); (3) configuring analytical procedures based on selected functions; (4) exporting metrics for detailed statistical analysis; and (5) interpreting results using graphs, tables, and images.

The Event function involved predefined marking and time-stamping of specific behaviors (e.g., needle insertion, knot tying, needle re-grasping). Each event was uniquely labeled and visually distinguished by color coding, allowing efficient mapping onto corresponding video segments. The Time of Interest function provided targeted analysis by defining precise intervals within video clips. Both automated and manually-defined TOIs were utilized; automated TOIs effectively identified clear behaviors, whereas manually-defined TOIs accurately captured subtle behaviors within specific video segments requiring focused analysis.

The Area of Interest function identified critical regions capturing visual attention through two methods: Snapshot AOIs, created by overlaying predefined regions onto static snapshots, and Drawing AOIs, manually annotated directly onto video clips. Analyzing gaze patterns within these AOIs revealed insights into visual attention allocation.

Finally, the Visualization function generated heatmaps and gaze plots. Heatmaps aggregated fixation durations using color gradients (red: high, yellow: medium, green: low), while gaze plots represented fixation sequences, durations, and scan paths.

Analytical framework for expert surgeon behaviors

This analytical framework systematically examines expert micro-behaviors and gaze-action couplings during the simple interrupted suturing procedure. The analysis is structured into four procedural categories: Equipment Usage, Needle Insertion, Knot Tying, and Needle Re-grasping, each analyzed in detail and comprehensively reported in the result section.

Tobii Pro Lab (TPL) provided essential analytical precision, identifying visual attention patterns critical to procedural expertise. Through Area of Interest (AOI), Time of Interest (TOI), Event markers, and visualizations (heatmaps, gaze plots), a methodological foundation was established for exploring visual-motor strategies and procedural efficiency.

The Equipment Usage analysis examines visual attention during the selection and utilization of surgical tools like needle holders, forceps, and scissors. Employing Tobii Pro Lab, the study observes gaze patterns, scan paths, and Areas of Interest on both functional and holding parts. This data will reveal how focused attention enhances precision and efficiency in surgical performance, while misdirected attention can lead to delays and errors. The Time of Interest function further isolates key intervals to analyze decision-making time.

The Needle Insertion analysis assessed visual-motor strategies for precise needle passage through artificial tissue, examining expert adaptation of visual attention relative to wound segments and insertion techniques. These insights enabled precise definition of performance benchmarks for instructional use.

The Knot-Tying analysis identified optimal strategies ensuring knot security and efficiency. The analysis focused on thread wrapping and effectiveness relative to wound characteristics, generating clear guidelines for structured knot-tying skill development.

The Needle Re-grasping analysis examined visual attention and procedural fluency during needle repositioning between stitches. Identifying efficient gaze strategies and positioning decisions facilitated clear recommendations for enhancing procedural efficiency.

Collectively, this integrated framework provided robust empirical data essential for defining performance benchmarks and structured, evidence-based training guidelines. Insights derived from this analysis underpin subsequent results and discussions, guiding effective translation into clinical and educational practices.

Statistical analysis

In this paper, the analyses were conducted at two levels: (1) the stitch level and (2) the subject level, to examine the data from different analytical perspectives.

At the stitch level, initial analyses were conducted using individual stitches as the unit of observation. Continuous variables were first tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables that deviated from a normal distribution at a significance level of 0.05 were analyzed using non-parametric statistical methods. For comparisons between two groups (e.g., different insertion patterns or the presence versus absence of squeezing), the Mann–Whitney U test was applied, and the effect size was reported as the rank-biserial correlation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{\textrm{rb}}$$\end{document}). When more than two groups were compared–such as anatomical wound segments, needle re-grasp techniques, or different wrap counts–the Kruskal–Wallis test (H test) was used. If the overall test was significant, Dunn’s pairwise comparisons with Holm–Bonferroni adjustment were performed. Effect sizes for these comparisons were reported as epsilon-squared (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon ^2$$\end{document}). Associations between categorical variables (e.g., insertion pattern with squeezing behavior or wound location) were evaluated using Pearson’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2$$\end{document} test, and the strength of association was interpreted using Cramer’s V. Statistical significance for all tests was set at a two-tailed α = 0.05.

At the subject level, mixed-effects models were used, with the subject treated as the unit of analysis. Subject was included as a random effect, while surgical factors were treated as fixed effects. Degrees of freedom for the F-tests were estimated using the Satterthwaite approximation. Effect size was quantified using Cohen’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f^2$$\end{document}, where values of 0.02, 0.15, and 0.35 indicate small, medium, and large effects, respectively41. In addition, the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was reported to estimate the proportion of variance attributable to clustering by surgeon. All statistical analyses were performed using JASP statistical software (version 0.95.0)42.

Results

This section presents the main findings from the analysis of expert suturing behavior. We analyzed 335 simple interrupted sutures performed by eight senior surgeons to examine how experienced surgeons coordinate visual attention and manual actions during suturing. First, we examine how surgeons direct their gaze when selecting and manipulating surgical instruments. Next, we analyze insertion strategies and how these strategies adapt to different wound conditions. We then investigate factors influencing knot-tying efficiency, including thread wrap configuration and knot completeness. Finally, we examine needle re-grasping behavior and how different techniques affect procedural flow.

Across these analyses, several consistent patterns of expert performance emerged. Surgeons tended to focus their visual attention on the functional parts of instruments, selected insertion techniques that adapted to wound characteristics, and used knot-tying strategies that balanced efficiency with procedural reliability. Differences in performance were occasionally associated with individual surgeons, but many behavioral patterns were shared across experts. Overall, these results provide a detailed picture of the gaze–action coordination that supports efficient execution of simple interrupted suturing.

Forceps usage

When analyzed per stitch, forceps followed a similar pattern: functional-tip fixations occurred in 191 stitches (1.395 ± 0.612 s) compared with 91 handle fixations (1.090 ± 0.431 s; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U = 6{,}372.5$$\end{document}, p <.001; Table 3). Tooth forceps demonstrated the highest F/H ratio (2.09), underscoring their importance for tactile control.

In the subject-level analysis, no significant difference in viewing time was observed between tooth and non-tooth types (U = 6,114, p =.696; Table 3). The effect of forceps in the linear mixed-effects model was not statistically significant, F(1, 106.63) = 0.004, p =.952, with a negligible effect size (<.001). Scissors usage

Considering individual stitches, scissors received more distributed attention. Out of 207 observations, surgeons focused on the handle in 113 cases (0.354 ± 0.264 s), the blades in 94 (0.309 ± 0.186 s), and skipped fixation altogether in 75. This non-significant difference (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U = 5{,}614.5$$\end{document}, p = 0.480) resulted in the lowest F/H ratio (0.83), suggesting scissors were cognitively deprioritized except during trimming as reported in Table 3.

At the subject level, scissors did not show a significant effect on viewing time (F(1, 204.72) = 0.78, p = 0.377), with a negligible effect size (0.004). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC = 0.255) suggests that about 25.5% of the variance was attributable to differences between surgeons (Table 4).

Summary of instrument-related visual attention patterns

Visual attention patterns varied across instrument types. For needle holders and forceps, expert surgeons directed greater visual attention toward the functional components of the instruments, whereas scissors showed a more distributed gaze pattern between the functional and holding parts.

When variability between surgeons was accounted for using linear mixed-effects models, none of the instrument categories demonstrated statistically significant fixed effects. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC = 0.230–0.379) indicated that a substantial proportion of the variance in viewing time was attributable to differences between surgeons.

Despite these individual differences, a consistent visual strategy was observed during instrument selection. Expert surgeons typically directed their initial fixation toward the operative tips rather than the handles. This anticipatory behavior, quantified by the functional-to-handle fixation ratio, reflects a pre-motor prioritization of action-relevant regions, as illustrated in Fig. 5.

Insertion strategies: wound-specific tactics

To better understand how surgeons adapt their suturing behavior, this section investigates insertion strategies across different wound conditions. We evaluate needle insertion techniques, the wound-edge squeezing maneuver, and the roles of wound width and wound part on insertion behavior and performance time using both stitch-level statistics and subject-level mixed-effects models.

Needle insertion techniques

Degrees of freedom were estimated using the Satterthwaite approximation. Needle insertion pattern showed a statistically significant effect, F(1, 6.02) = 9.116, p = 0.023, with a large effect size (0.60). Wound edge squeezing technique

At the stitch level, the wound approximation maneuver (Fig. 7)–temporary squeezing of the wound edges–significantly reduced insertion time (mean = 5.92 s with squeezing vs. 9.80 s without squeezing; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U = 4{,}299$$\end{document}, p < 0.001, effect size= -0.457; Table 5).

In the subject-level mixed-effects analysis (Table 6), degrees of freedom were estimated using the Satterthwaite approximation. Although the squeeze technique showed a large effect size (0.43), the fixed effect did not reach statistical significance, F(1, 5.53) = 4.123, p = 0.093. With an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.39, approximately 39% of the variance was explained by differences between surgeons.

Furthermore, squeezing was more frequently used in narrow wounds (125 of 139; 89.9%) than in wide wounds (153 of 196; 78.1%; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2 = 7.292$$\end{document}, p = 0.007; Table 7). It was also strongly associated with RL insertions (136 of 288; 97.2%) compared with RML insertions (3 of 47; 6.4%; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2 = 26.103$$\end{document}, p < 0.001; Table 8).

Wound width

Although wound width alone did not significantly affect insertion time (mean = 6.78 s for wide vs. 6.31 s for narrow; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U = 14{,}177$$\end{document}, p = 0.526) as reported in Table 5, needle insertion patterns varied with wound width. Specifically, the RL pattern was more frequently used in narrow wounds, whereas the RML pattern was predominantly observed in wide wounds (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2 = 26.103$$\end{document}, p < 0.001; Table 8). Consistent with the non-parametric analysis, wound width did not show a statistically significant fixed effect in the mixed-effects model, F(1, 330.91) = 0.132, p =.717, with a negligible effect size (<.001). The intraclass correlation coefficient was comparatively lower (ICC = 0.153), indicating that approximately 15.3% of the total variance was attributable to differences between surgeons (Table 6).

Wound part

At the stitch level, needle insertion time varied significantly across wound parts (KW = 17.623, p < 0.001; Table 9). The upper part required the longest time (mean = 7.77 s), followed by the middle (6.46 s) and lower parts (5.52 s), with all pairwise comparisons showing significant differences.

In the subject-level mixed-effects analysis, wound part showed a statistically significant effect on performance time, F(2, 323.85) = 5.67, p = 0.004, with a small effect size = 0.017 (Table 10). With an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.159, approximately 15.9% of the variation in performance time was associated with differences between subjects.

Summary of factors affecting needle insertion performance

Overall, needle insertion performance was significantly associated with insertion pattern, with the RL technique demonstrating greater temporal efficiency than RML. This relationship remained consistent in the mixed-effects model after controlling for subject-level clustering. Although the wound approximation maneuver was descriptively associated with reduced insertion time and showed a large effect size, its fixed effect did not reach statistical significance once hierarchical variance was considered.

Wound width alone did not significantly affect insertion time; however, it was significantly associated with technique selection. This indicates that wound width mainly influenced how surgeons selected their insertion technique rather than how quickly the stitch was executed. In addition, wound width was correlated with segment location: wider wounds were more frequently observed in the middle segment, whereas narrower wounds clustered in the lower segment (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2 = 77.102$$\end{document}, p < 0.001; Table 7).

These results indicate that surgeons adapt their insertion technique responsively to local wound conditions. The RL technique with wound approximation was more commonly used in narrow, lower wounds, whereas RML without squeezing appeared more frequently in wider wounds, particularly in the upper and middle segments. This pattern reflects a real-time gaze-informed adjustment strategy that is not explicitly described in traditional surgical training curricula. Knot-tying precision and procedural efficiency

This section examines factors affecting knot-tying precision and efficiency during simple suturing. Specifically, we analyze how thread wrap count, knot completeness, wound position, and first-throw variation influence knot-tying time. Both stitch-level statistical tests and subject-level mixed-effects models were used to evaluate these factors while accounting for variability between surgeons.

Number of thread wraps

We further investigated the effect of thread wrap count on knot-tying performance. At the stitch level, knot-tying time increased as the number of thread wraps increased. Three-wrap knots (n = 246) required an average of 9.93 s, whereas two-wrap knots (n = 5) were faster at 6.76 s. Knots with excessive wraps (4–6) required substantially longer times, with six-wrap knots averaging more than 31 s. The overall difference across wrap counts was statistically significant (KW = 54.087, p < 0.001, effect size = 0.168; Table 11). These results suggest that three wraps represent an effective balance between tying speed and knot security.

At the subject level, the relationship between thread wrap count and knot-tying time was examined using a linear mixed-effects model that accounted for clustering of observations within surgeons. In this model, surgeon was specified as a random intercept to address the non-independence of repeated measurements. The results showed a statistically significant main effect of wrap count on knot-tying time, F(4, 293.18) = 28.90, p < .001, with an effect size of 0.071, corresponding to a moderate magnitude (Table 12). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC = 0.243) further indicated that approximately 24.3% of the total variance in knot-tying time was attributable to differences between surgeons.

Thread completeness and re-tying

When analyzed at the stitch level, incomplete knots (n = 47) required longer tying times, averaging 14.49 s, compared with 9.87 s for complete knots (n = 257). This difference was statistically significant (U = 9572, p < 0.001; Table 13, effect size = 0.585). Re-tying caused by looseness added approximately 4.6 s per stitch, highlighting the additional time required when knots must be re-tied. Knot-tying time also varied across wound parts. Knots placed in the upper part required the longest time (11.20 s), followed by the middle part (10.59 s) and the lower part (9.90 s). These differences were statistically significant (KW = 10.487, p = 0.005; Table 11), although the effect size was minimal (0.023), suggesting only a limited practical difference.

To determine whether the stitch-level pattern remained consistent across surgeons, an additional subject-level analysis was conducted. Subject-level analysis was performed using a linear mixed-effects model, with surgeon included as a random intercept to account for clustering of stitches within individuals (Table 14). The Satterthwaite method was used to approximate the degrees of freedom. The model revealed a highly significant association between thread wrap configuration and knot-tying time (F(1, 6.76) = 37.46, p < 0.001), with a large effect size (0.847). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC = 0.322) indicates that approximately 32.2% of the total variance in knot-tying time was attributable to differences between surgeons. The effect of wound position on knot-tying time was further examined using a linear mixed-effects model that accounted for clustering within surgeons by including surgeon as a random intercept. As shown in Table 12, wound position did not demonstrate a statistically significant effect on knot-tying time, F(2, 294.24) = 2.77, p =.064, with a small effect size (0.009). First-throw variation

Based on the stitch-level analysis, no significant difference in knot-tying time was observed between single- and double-throw starts (U = 1841, p = 0.424; Table 13, effect size = 0.142). Thus, unlike wrap count or knot completeness, throw type appears to have minimal relevance for training feedback.

To examine the pattern at the subject level, a linear mixed-effects model with surgeon specified as a random intercept was fitted (Table 14). Degrees of freedom were estimated using the Satterthwaite approximation. Consistent with the non-parametric findings, first-throw variation did not significantly affect knot-tying time, F(1, 301.96) = 1.881, p = 0.171, with a negligible effect size (0.006). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC = 0.315) indicates that approximately 31.5% of the total variance in knot-tying time was attributable to differences between surgeons.

Summary of factors affecting knot-tying efficiency

Overall, at the stitch level, knot-tying time was strongly related to the number and configuration of thread wraps. Knots with more wraps required longer tying times, and incomplete knots also took substantially longer to complete. In contrast, complete three-wrap knots were tied more quickly while still achieving knot completion.

At the subject level, mixed-effects analyses showed that these patterns remained consistent after accounting for clustering within surgeons, although a meaningful proportion of variance was attributable to differences between surgeons. Variation in the first throw did not show a significant association with tying time across analytical approaches.

These findings indicate that factors related to wrap execution and knot completion play a greater role in simple suture efficiency than the configuration of the initial throw. This analysis focused on thread wrapping behavior and its effectiveness relative to wound characteristics (see fixation clusters in Fig. 8). Four behavioral factors were examined: thread wrap count, knot completeness, wound segment, and first-throw variation. Additional wraps and incomplete knots introduced measurable delays, whereas wound position and throw style showed only minor differences in timing. Visual–motor coordination in needle re-grasping and procedural flow

This section analyzes two aspects of needle re-grasping behavior: (i) the technique used between thread cutting and needle re-grasping, and (ii) the location where the needle is placed after each stitch.

Needle re-grasping techniques

Five distinct needle re-grasping techniques were observed (Figs. 9,10,11,12,13,14 and Table 15), each varying in efficiency and ergonomics:Lift-and-Grasp (67 stitches, 4.33 s): The non-dominant hand lifts the loose thread upward, exposing the needle tip for direct clamping by the needle holder. (See Lift-and-Grasp Technique Demonstration Video)Slide-and-Secure (30 stitches, 5.51 s): The thread is slid toward the non-dominant hand until the needle becomes accessible. This method often involves fingertip contact, increasing both the time required and the risk of accidental needle stick. (See Slide-and-Secure Technique Demonstration Video)Gather-in-Hand (46 stitches, 3.80 s): The thread is cut while being held in the non-dominant hand, positioning the needle near the dominant hand for immediate re-grasping and minimizing search time.(See Gather-in-Hand Technique Demonstration Video)Drop-and-Search (16 stitches, 3.77 s): The needle is allowed to fall without control and is later visually located for retrieval.(See Drop-and-Search Technique Demonstration Video)Designated Needle Placement (132 stitches, 3.88 s): The needle is intentionally placed in a fixed location after cutting, enabling consistent and rapid retrieval for the next stitch.(See Designated Needle Placement Technique Demonstration Video)When examined at the stitch level, the overall average re-grasping time across all techniques was 4.13 seconds. A Kruskal–Wallis test revealed a significant difference in re-grasping time between techniques (KW = 42.605, p < 0.001; Table 15), with a small-to-moderate effect size (0.135). Post-hoc comparisons using Dunn’s test showed that the Slide-and-Secure technique required significantly more time than Lift-and-Grasp, Gather-in-Hand, and Designated Needle Placement.

Needle placement location

We first examined the data at the stitch level. Surgeons typically placed the needle in one of three locations after cutting the thread (Fig. 15 and Table 15): bottom-center (n = 23), top-left (n = 139), and bottom-left (n = 129). The corresponding average re-grasping times were 3.48, 3.94, and 4.46 seconds, respectively. Although the bottom-center position showed the shortest average time, the differences in re-grasping time among the three placement zones were not statistically significant (KW = 3.679, p = 0.159), with a small effect size (0.006).

Subsequently, a complementary subject-level analysis was performed using a linear mixed-effects model with surgeon specified as a random intercept (Table 16). Consistent with the non-parametric results, needle placement location did not significantly affect re-grasping time (F(2, 102.26) = 1.988, p = 0.142), with a small effect size (0.019). Summary of factors affecting re-grasping performance

Overall, re-grasping time varied significantly across techniques but not across needle placement locations. Both the non-parametric and mixed-effects analyses consistently showed that the re-grasping technique played a meaningful role in determining execution time, whereas the spatial location where the needle was placed after cutting did not independently affect performance. Surgeon-level clustering explained only a small proportion of the variance across models (ICC range: 0.025–0.060.025.060), indicating relatively consistent re-grasping behavior across individuals.

Although needle placement location showed only a minor influence on timing, the efficiency of re-grasping was largely determined by the technique used. The Slide-and-Secure method required the longest time and may also introduce greater ergonomic and safety challenges. In contrast, techniques such as Gather-in-Hand and Designated Placement enabled faster and more predictable transitions. Even though placement zones did not produce statistically significant timing differences, consistently placing the needle in visually accessible locations may still support procedural fluency by reducing visual search and cognitive load.

Discussion

Key findings

This study identified four reproducible micro-behaviors that were associated with variation in performance during expert-performed simple interrupted suturing. Across 335 stitches, synchronized eye-tracking and motor-event analysis showed that expert performance was characterized by: (1) preferential fixation on the functional parts of instruments before use, (2) selection of wound-specific insertion strategies, particularly the more time-efficient right-to-left (RL) pattern, (3) knot-tying behavior centered around complete three-wrap configurations, and (4) faster transitions between stitches through efficient needle re-grasping techniques. Collectively, these factors formed the empirical basis of the Suture–Action–Gaze Efficiency (SAGE) framework (Table 17).

Importantly, the findings do not indicate that all observed behaviors independently improved performance in every model. Rather, the data suggest that specific visual–motor patterns were consistently associated with shorter execution times or more fluent transitions under the conditions studied. For example, the RL insertion pattern remained significantly associated with shorter insertion time at both stitch and subject levels, whereas the squeeze maneuver showed a strong descriptive association at the stitch level but did not retain statistical significance in the mixed-effects model after surgeon-level clustering was considered. Similarly, knot efficiency was more closely associated with wrap count and knot completeness than with first-throw variation, and re-grasping time was associated with technique rather than with the spatial location of needle placement alone

Taken together, as illustrated in Fig. 16, they form the empirical foundation of the SAGE model (Suture–Action–Gaze Efficiency). These results suggest that expert suturing is not defined solely by overall speed or outcome, but by a structured pattern of gaze-guided procedural organization across the stitch cycle. Interpretation

The present findings suggest that expert simple interrupted suturing is associated with a repeatable coupling between visual attention and procedural sub-steps. Rather than distributing gaze uniformly across the operative field, expert surgeons appeared to allocate attention selectively to task-relevant targets at moments that preceded or accompanied specific motor actions. This pattern was particularly evident during instrument selection, where the functional parts of needle holders and forceps attracted more attention than holding regions, consistent with a strategy of prioritizing action-relevant information before movement execution. However, because these findings were derived from observational analysis, they should be interpreted as reflecting characteristic expert behavior rather than demonstrating that such fixation patterns directly improve performance.

The insertion findings also support a context-sensitive interpretation. The RL technique was associated with shorter insertion times than the RML technique, while wound width itself was not independently associated with insertion time after mixed-effects modeling. At the same time, wound width was associated with technique selection, and narrower wounds were more often managed with RL insertions and squeezing. These results suggest that expert performance may involve flexible matching of insertion strategy to local wound conditions, rather than reliance on a single universally optimal technique. The squeeze maneuver may therefore be interpreted as part of a broader adaptive pattern, but the current analysis does not establish it as an independent causal determinant of faster insertion.

For knot tying, the data suggest that procedural efficiency was more closely associated with complete three-wrap knots than with the specific form of the initial throw. Additional wraps and incomplete configurations were associated with longer tying times and re-tying penalties, whereas three-wrap knots appeared to represent a practical balance between consistency and efficiency within this expert sample. Again, this should not be interpreted as evidence that three wraps are universally superior in all contexts, but rather that, under the standardized conditions of this study, three-wrap completion was the pattern most consistently associated with efficient expert performance.

A similar interpretation applies to re-grasping. Faster transitions were associated with techniques such as Gather-in-Hand and Designated Needle Placement, whereas Slide-and-Secure was associated with longer re-grasping times. In contrast, the exact placement zone of the needle was not independently associated with timing. This suggests that transition efficiency may depend more on the organization of the re-grasping maneuver itself than on location alone. More broadly, these findings support the interpretation that procedural fluency in expert suturing may emerge from a sequence of small, coordinated visual–motor decisions rather than from any single isolated action.

From an educational standpoint, these observations are relevant because they convert previously implicit expert behaviors into explicit, measurable benchmarks. The contribution of the SAGE framework, therefore, lies less in introducing a new sensing technology and more in providing a structured way to describe how expert gaze–action coupling is associated with procedural fluency at the stitch level. Any future educational or real-time feedback application should be tested prospectively in novice cohorts rather than assumed from the present expert-only, observational dataset

Comparison with literature

The present findings are broadly consistent with prior eye-tracking literature in surgery, which has repeatedly shown that gaze metrics can distinguish levels of expertise and provide insight into perceptual aspects of technical performance. Earlier systematic reviews concluded that experts generally demonstrate more targeted visual attention, fewer unnecessary fixations, and more efficient gaze strategies than novices, while also noting substantial heterogeneity in devices, metrics, and analytic methods across studies. Importantly, those reviews emphasized that much of the field has remained descriptive and has not yet converged on standardized43, instruction-ready benchmarks. In this context, the current study aligns with the broader literature in identifying expert-specific gaze patterns, but differs in focusing on stitch-level gaze–action coupling in open suturing rather than using aggregate gaze metrics alone21,25.

This distinction is important because several prior studies have mainly used eye tracking to classify performance level or estimate proficiency, especially in minimally invasive tasks. For example, Kulkarni and colleagues showed that scene-dependent and feedforward gaze metrics were highly informative for differentiating laparoscopic proficiency levels, supporting the view that anticipatory visual behavior is a meaningful feature of skilled performance. However, that work was conducted in a laparoscopic peg-transfer model and was oriented toward proficiency estimation rather than toward identifying concrete micro-behaviors that could be translated into step-specific instructional guidance. By comparison, the present study examines a foundational open surgical task and links gaze behavior to discrete subtasks such as instrument engagement, insertion strategy, knot execution, and re-grasping transitions. The contribution of SAGE therefore lies less in demonstrating that expert gaze differs from novice gaze, which is already well established, and more in organizing these expert patterns into a behavior-level framework that may be more directly interpretable for training21,44.

The current findings are also compatible with prior interventional work suggesting that gaze-informed teaching can support surgical learning, while remaining conceptually distinct from it. Randomized studies by Causer and colleagues found that quiet-eye–based training was associated with better knot-tying performance, more efficient gaze behavior, and improved retention or transfer compared with traditional technical instruction. More recently, Storck et al. reported that eye-movement modeling examples in robotic surgery increased learner engagement, although they did not improve anatomical landmark interpretation, highlighting that visual guidance does not automatically translate into superior performance outcomes. Taken together, these studies suggest that expert gaze information may have educational value, but they do not by themselves specify which micro-behaviors should be taught in a simple interrupted suture. The present study contributes at an earlier, descriptive-translational stage by identifying which expert behaviors are associated with more fluent stitch execution under standardized conditions. Accordingly, SAGE should be interpreted as a candidate instructional framework derived from expert observation, not as evidence that these behaviors have already been validated as causal training targets in novices32.

Overall, relative to existing research, the novelty of the present work lies in three areas: the focus on simple interrupted suturing as a core open surgical skill, the temporal synchronization of gaze with stitch-level motor events, and the translation of observed expert patterns into explicit behavioral benchmarks. This positions the study between two existing streams of literature: descriptive eye-tracking research that documents expertise-related gaze differences, and intervention studies that test gaze-guided instruction. The present work helps bridge that gap by proposing a structured intermediate layer–an empirically derived framework that may support future prospective validation in training settings21,25.

Theoretical & practical implications

As previously discussed, our study explicitly draws from key theoretical frameworks, including Deliberate Practice35 and Mastery Learning40, which emphasize precise goal-setting, immediate feedback, and structured repetition for skill mastery.

The SAGE framework operationalizes these foundational theories by translating detailed gaze-based micro-behaviours into clear, specific, and identifiable surgical performance. For example, micro-behaviours such as tip-first instrument fixation and adaptive wound-squeeze needle insertion directly embody targeted practice goals, providing explicit, actionable guidelines consistent with deliberate practice and mastery learning principles. These principles are widely established within simulation-based medical education and are familiar to surgical educators as core components of structured skill acquisition.

In future practical work, the actionable insights from the SAGE framework can be systematically implemented across three distinct tiers. Firstly, simulator overlays can provide immediate visual guidance during practice sessions, allowing trainees to instantly align their performance with expert gaze patterns. Secondly, residency dashboards can leverage detailed micro-behaviour data to offer continuous, objective tracking of trainees’ skill progression, thus informing personalized instructional strategies. Finally, the model holds potential for clinical translation in the operating room, where real-time supportive cues based on expert gaze behaviors could enhance surgical performance and patient safety.

Implementing the SAGE framework into surgical training curricula promises substantial educational and clinical benefits, including accelerated skill acquisition, improved procedural efficiency, enhanced trainee confidence, and ultimately better patient outcomes and safety.

Limitations & future work

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, the small sample size (eight senior surgeons) and use of an artificial skin model restrict generalisability. Despite aiming initially for a broader multi-sub-specialty sample, final recruitment was lower than anticipated. Future multi-centre studies conducted in real clinical environments are needed to validate and extend these findings.

Secondly, the eye-tracking device used (Tobii Pro Glasses 3, 50 Hz) may have missed rapid micro-saccades or subtle visual behaviors critical for refined surgical performance. Employing higher-frequency eye-trackers (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge 120\,\textrm{Hz}$$\end{document}) in future research could provide more detailed and accurate insights into surgeons’ visual strategies.

Thirdly, we did not include longitudinal data to assess skill retention or the consistency of identified micro-behaviours over time. Future longitudinal studies should investigate the long-term stability and retention of these expert strategies.

Fourth, patient-related outcomes, such as wound healing rates or complication frequencies, were not measured. Incorporating these clinical outcomes in future studies would significantly enhance the practical and clinical relevance of our findings.

Fifth, we did not include a novice comparison group, which limits our ability to directly evaluate the training effectiveness of the proposed framework. As such, the training implications are currently inferred from expert-derived behavioral patterns. Future work should include studies comparing novice and expert surgeons to further examine how the SAGE framework supports skill development in practice.

Finally, the SAGE framework is designed as a structured training guideline for medical education rather than a fully automated computer vision algorithm. Accordingly, this study prioritizes human-centered metrics, such as suture completion time, rather than algorithmic performance metrics.

Conclusions

This study identified reproducible expert gaze–action patterns during simple interrupted suturing at the level of individual stitches. Across 335 stitches, expert performance was characterized by tip-focused instrument fixation, context-adaptive insertion strategy selection, knot efficiency associated with complete three-wrap configurations, and faster stitch-to-stitch transitions associated with specific re-grasping techniques. These patterns were primarily derived from stitch-level analysis and were partially supported at the subject level, where key associations–particularly insertion strategy–remained consistent, while others showed reduced significance after accounting for surgeon-level variation. These observations were synthesized into the Suture–Action–Gaze Efficiency (SAGE) framework as an observational, expert-derived behavioral guide rather than a validated causal training model. Future studies should evaluate this framework prospectively in novice learners and real clinical settings.
