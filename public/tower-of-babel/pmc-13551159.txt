Beyond the Law: A Qualitative Socio‐Ecological Study of Substance Use Treatment Barriers and Adaptations in Ghana
Authors: Evans F Kyei, Dorothy Wilson, Grace K Kyei, Rockson Ansong, Mercy N Mumba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundGhana's response to substance use has long been dominated by criminalisation, yet limited research has examined how this policy environment shapes addiction treatment delivery. This study explored the perspectives of mental health nurses providing care to people who use drugs (PWUDs) using a socio‐ecological framework.MethodsAn exploratory qualitative study was conducted with 20 mental health nurses in the Kumasi Metropolitan Area between April and July 2024. Participants were purposively recruited from diverse healthcare facilities. Semi‐structured interviews explored factors influencing treatment provision at individual, interpersonal, organisational, community and policy levels. Interviews were audio‐recorded, transcribed verbatim and analysed using Braun and Clarke's six‐stage thematic analysis. A deductive approach informed by the socio‐ecological framework was combined with inductive theme development. Reporting followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ).ResultsFour themes emerged: (1) structural and systemic barriers, including fragmented treatment pathways, limited rehabilitation facilities and resource inequities; (2) economic and access barriers shaped by financial constraints and family systems; (3) societal attitudes and criminalisation, where stigma and punitive policies discouraged treatment engagement while corruption enabled drug access and (4) adaptation and resilience, reflecting how providers and patients navigated system limitations through creative strategies.ConclusionsMental health nurses deliver addiction care within significant structural constraints. A socio‐ecological framework revealed how barriers operate across multiple levels, underscoring the need for public health‐oriented drug policies, expanded treatment infrastructure and targeted workforce development to improve addiction care.Keywords: criminalisation, mental health nursing, substance use disorders

Introduction

This study explores the experiences of mental health nurses caring for people who use drugs (PWUDs) in Ghana, a perspective rarely examined in the literature. Using a qualitative exploratory design guided by the socio‐ecological framework and Braun and Clarke's thematic analysis and reported in line with COREQ, the study provides a rigorous account of systemic, cultural and institutional barriers that shape care within a criminalised drug policy environment. By amplifying the voices of frontline nurses, the findings offer critical insights to guide the development of addiction services in Ghana and other low‐resource contexts where punitive approaches to drug use prevail.

Substance use disorders represent a significant public health challenge globally, with an estimated 284 million people aged 15–64 using drugs worldwide (United Nations publication 2022). Low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) face particular challenges in addressing substance use disorders due to limited resources, inadequate treatment infrastructure and punitive policy frameworks (Sarkar et al. 2021). In Ghana, the prevalence of substance use has been increasing, with cannabis, alcohol, tobacco and prescription medications being the most used substances (Kabore et al. 2019). Cannabis in Ghana is primarily consumed in smoked forms rather than prescribed medical preparations, while alcohol and tobacco remain widely used legal substances that contribute to the broader substance use landscape. Injection drug use is reported to be relatively uncommon compared with other regions, although precise national surveillance data remain limited. Ghana's approach has historically been dominated by criminalisation, with the Narcotic Drugs (Control, Enforcement and Sanctions) Law of 1990 (PNDCL 236) imposing severe penalties for possession and use (Narcotics Control Commission 2020). More recently, the Narcotics Control Commission Act, 2020 (Act 1019), replaced incarceration for personal drug possession with monetary fines. While this reform acknowledges the need for proportionality, it maintains the underlying criminalisation framework, which remains in tension with the recognition of addiction as a health condition requiring treatment rather than punishment (Alhassan 2022).

Recent epidemiological data indicate that substance use disorders affect approximately 2%–3% of Ghana's adult population, though this likely underestimates the true prevalence due to underreporting and limited surveillance infrastructure (Mutiso et al. 2022; Wallace et al. 2021). Cannabis remains the most used illicit substance, but there has been a concerning rise in misuse of prescription medications, particularly tramadol and other opioids (Boun et al. 2024; Fuseini et al. 2019; Saapiire et al. 2021; Lasong et al. 2024). Studies in Ghana and across the region have documented increasing patterns of non‐medical tramadol use, often in combination with other substances, reflecting a broader shift towards prescription opioid misuse in West Africa (Saapiire et al. 2021; Lasong et al. 2024). Additionally, new psychoactive substances and local combinations are emerging, complicating detection and treatment (Danso and Anto 2021; Molobe et al. 2023). Amphetamine‐type stimulants have also been reported in parts of West Africa, although evidence regarding their prevalence in Ghana remains limited and fragmented. Regional evidence suggests substantial variation in substance use patterns across West Africa, with alcohol, cannabis, tramadol and codeine‐containing medications commonly reported across multiple countries. Ghana's mental health system has undergone reform since the Mental Health Act of 2012, which emphasised community‐based care (Weobong et al. 2023). Yet with less than 1% of the health budget allocated to mental health, facilities, medications and personnel remain insufficient to meet the growing need (Daliri et al. 2024). Addiction services are particularly under‐resourced, often ranking lower in priority than other psychiatric conditions (Tampah and Iddrisu 2024).

Addiction treatment infrastructure in Ghana consists primarily of psychiatric hospitals, small, specialised units in general hospitals and privately operated rehabilitation centres (Janson et al. 2024). Government‐operated rehabilitation facilities are scarce, while private centres charge fees unaffordable for most Ghanaians (Cadri et al. 2021). Detoxification services are commonly provided within psychiatric hospitals or general hospitals psychiatric units, while structured outpatient or community‐based rehabilitation programmes remain limited. Peer‐support programmes such as Alcoholics Anonymous or Narcotics Anonymous exist in some urban areas but are not widely integrated into the formal treatment system. Faith‐based and community organisations often play informal roles in supporting individuals with substance use disorders. Treatment approaches vary widely, with inconsistent implementation of evidence‐based practices (Iemmi 2020). Mental health professionals treating PWUDs face further challenges including stigma towards patients and providers, safety concerns in managing agitation and the need to navigate between health and justice systems (Afulani et al. 2021; Jaguga et al. 2022). Despite these barriers, they represent the frontline of addiction treatment, making their experiences valuable for understanding systemic gaps and opportunities (Botchwey 2021).

The socio‐ecological model provides a strong theoretical basis for examining these dynamics. It recognises that health behaviours and outcomes are shaped by interrelated factors at individual, interpersonal, organisational, community and policy levels (Bronfenbrenner 1977). This framework is, particularly, well suited to the Ghanaian context, where criminalisation, stigma, resource scarcity and family structures interact in complex ways to influence treatment access and outcomes (Kabore et al. 2019; Ratshisusu et al. 2024). By applying this model, the study situates provider experiences within broader structural and policy forces rather than viewing them as isolated clinical challenges.

Despite the growing prevalence of substance use, limited research has examined the perspectives of mental health nurses who deliver addiction treatment in Ghana. Most prior studies have focused on prevalence or intervention‐specific outcomes (Lasong et al. 2024; Saapiire et al. 2021; Ae‐Ngibise et al. 2025), with little attention to systemic and multi‐level barriers. To our knowledge, few studies have systematically examined these experiences from the perspective of mental health nurses within Ghana's criminalised policy environment. This study addresses that gap by exploring the challenges and adaptations of nurses treating PWUDs through a socio‐ecological lens. By highlighting how barriers and opportunities operate across individual, interpersonal, organisational, community and policy levels, the study contributes evidence to guide clinical practice and policy reforms that better support addiction treatment in Ghana and similar low‐resource, criminalised settings.

Methods

Study Design

This qualitative exploratory study examined the perspectives and challenges of mental health professionals treating PWUDs in Ghana's criminalised context. The socio‐ecological framework guided the design and implementation, enabling the examination of factors at multiple levels that influence treatment provision and outcomes. The study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines to ensure transparent and comprehensive reporting. A qualitative exploratory approach was selected because limited prior research has examined the experiences of mental health nurses treating PWUDs in Ghana, making in‐depth qualitative inquiry appropriate for understanding complex contextual and systemic influences.

Setting

The study was conducted in the Kumasi Metropolitan Area in the Ashanti Region of Ghana between April and July 2024. Kumasi is Ghana's second‐largest city, with an estimated population of 3.3 million (Peprah et al. 2020). The Kumasi Metropolitan Area contains multiple healthcare facilities providing mental health and addiction services, including the Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital's psychiatric unit, several district hospitals with mental health departments and private rehabilitation centres. This urban setting was selected because it offers access to mental health professionals working across diverse treatment contexts. These facilities represent major referral points for mental health and addiction services within the Ashanti Region and provide care to patients from both urban and surrounding rural communities.

Participant Recruitment and Sampling

Participants were mental health nurses working with PWUDs in healthcare settings across the Kumasi Metropolitan Area. We employed purposive sampling to recruit participants with direct experience treating PWUDs (Saunders et al. 2018). Recruitment began through psychiatric departments in local healthcare facilities and was followed by snowball sampling, where participants recommended colleagues with relevant experience Sedgwick 2013. Facility administrators were first contacted to introduce the study and facilitate access to eligible nurses. Interested nurses contacted the research team directly to avoid potential coercion from supervisors. Eligibility criteria included: (1) being a mental health nurse, (2) having at least 1 year of experience working with PWUDs, (3) currently practicing in the Kumasi Metropolitan Area and (4) ability to communicate in English. Three individuals declined participation, but no participants withdrew once enrolled. We continued recruitment until theoretical saturation was reached, when no new substantive insights emerged (Saunders et al. 2018; Starks and Brown Trinidad 2007). Saturation was assessed through ongoing analytic discussions among the research team during data collection and coding, with the final interviews confirming previously identified themes rather than generating new ones.

Data Collection

Semi‐structured interviews were conducted with 20 mental health nurses, with 16 conducted in person and 4 via Zoom. Evans Kyei (PhD, MSN, RN, Assistant Professor, male) and Dorothy Wilson (PhD student, BSN, RN, female) conducted all interviews. The interview guide was developed based on the socio‐ecological framework, piloted with two professionals not included in the final sample and revised accordingly. No repeat interviews were carried out. No one other than the participant and interviewer was present during interviews. Each session lasted 60–90 min, was audio‐recorded with consent and transcribed verbatim. Field notes capturing non‐verbal cues and contextual observations were also recorded. Participants received $10 as compensation for their time. Interviews explored participants' experiences providing care to PWUDs, perceived barriers to treatment, interactions with patients and families, institutional constraints and the influence of broader policy environments.

Data Analysis

Thematic analysis, following Braun and Clarke's six‐stage approach, guided the analytic process (Braun and Clarke 2006, 2012, 2019). MAXQDA software (version 2024) was used to organise the data. The analysis followed a primarily deductive approach informed by the socio‐ecological framework, while allowing for inductive emergence of themes. Evans Kyei and Dorothy Wilson independently coded three transcripts and jointly developed a preliminary codebook. This codebook, which grouped codes under individual, interpersonal, organisational, community and policy levels, served as the coding tree and was refined as coding progressed. Themes were derived by combining codes into broader patterns of shared meaning and deviant cases were considered to capture variation. After the initial codebook was finalised, the remaining transcripts were coded iteratively, with regular meetings held to discuss coding consistency and resolve discrepancies. To ensure trustworthiness, we employed multiple strategies (Halfpenny 1979; Thorne 2000): (1) prolonged engagement with the data, (2) member checking by sharing preliminary findings with four participants, (3) peer debriefing with colleagues familiar with the Ghanaian context, (4) thick description with participant quotations and (5) reflexivity through research team discussions about positionality and bias.

Reflexivity

We recognise that our interpretations were shaped by our professional backgrounds, cultural perspectives and lived experiences. The research team included both Ghanaian and American researchers with expertise in public health, nursing and addiction medicine, allowing for diverse interpretive lenses. Evans Kyei (PhD, MSN, RN, Assistant Professor, male) and Dorothy Wilson (PhD student, BSN, RN, female) conducted all interviews. Both had extensive qualitative training and experience, including published qualitative studies. Other team members included Grace Kyei (PhD student), Rockson Ansong (Assistant Professor) and Mercy Mumba (Professor), who contributed to study design, analysis and interpretation. No prior relationships existed between researchers and participants. Study goals and purposes were explained during recruitment, informed consent and at the beginning of each interview. Participants were also given the opportunity to review transcripts to confirm that they accurately reflected their views. Participation was voluntary and confidentiality was emphasised to encourage open discussion of potentially sensitive experiences related to substance use treatment and institutional practices. We remained aware of our positionality as nurses and health professionals, which could shape interpretations and engaged in regular reflexive discussions to minimise bias and ensure analytic rigour (Byrne 2022).

Four major themes spanning multiple levels of the socio‐ecological framework were identified. Each theme encompasses several sub‐themes that collectively shape the experiences of mental health professionals treating PWUDs in Ghana. Theme 1: Structural and Systemic Barriers to Effective Care

This theme captures the systemic challenges within Ghana's addiction treatment environment, operating primarily at organisational and policy levels of the socio‐ecological framework.

Fragmented Care and Treatment Discontinuity

Treatment discontinuity emerged as a significant challenge, with limited coordination between acute care and long‐term recovery support. Many patients begin treatment but do not complete the full course. Participants described this pattern as creating a recurring cycle of relapse and disengagement from care that undermines recovery efforts.

For the first time, users come in very aggressive … after that, we ask them to come for review. It has become another problem. They don't show up for review because their first episode is wiped off. They don't come back for review. (P5)

The fragmentation extends beyond hospital settings into post‐rehabilitation care, where Ghana lacks structured aftercare programmes and relies instead on family members who may not have the necessary expertise:

Sponsors are people who have already been part of the addiction program in the terms of the addict, but they have recovered, and then they monitor, or they help persons who are having substance challenges, you know, to go through the challenge so that they can also gain their independency or control over the use of substance. For Ghana, we do not have it. That's the challenge. And so, we use the family, you know, to do that kind of work. And but unfortunately, you know, the relapse rate is very high because of the caregiver's inexperience and then also the patient's own choice. (P11)

Treatment Infrastructure Deficits

The shortage of appropriate treatment facilities was consistently identified as a barrier. Participants highlighted the absence of specialised addiction units, forcing them to adapt general psychiatric spaces that may not be suitable for treating substance use disorders:

My facility has only six bed capacity for males and six for females, so in all 12. And if you look at the middle belt, this is the only facility that can admit patients who really want to do detox, then it's an issue. (P15)

The only place we do admissions is … that place we don't even have a proper sectioned place for dealing with people with substance use. We mix them with the normal psychotic patients who are also under discipline. (P14)

The problem is particularly acute for rehabilitation services, where participants noted an almost complete absence of government‐operated facilities. This privatisation creates significant barriers to access, as another participant explained:

We do not even have a place that we know where to send a person who is using drugs where they are solely protected. The ones we have are privately owned, they are not government. (P16)

Even when facilities exist, their geographical distribution presents logistical challenges:

For now, I barely know of three [facilities], that's what we normally use. And it's very far from our office to Asante Mampong. (P5)

Compounding these infrastructural deficits is a shortage of specialists trained in addiction treatment:

And currently we have about … advanced nurse practitioners who are addict specialists, certified addict specialists. They are not more than 10 in Ghana, and you can find all of them at Accra Mental. The other two are the other three are in Korle Bu [a tertiary hospital in Ghana], I mean, at an advanced level practice, yeah, and then just two doctors or psychiatrists who have majored in addiction, that is it. (P11)

Systemic Healthcare Inequities for Mental Health and Addiction Services

Resource disparities between mental and general healthcare create an additional layer of challenges. Participants described consistent patterns of discrimination in resource allocation that disadvantage mental health services:

… There is a much difference with mental health, that's how I see it. Because anytime we go there and need something, it's like, we don't have it. But when it comes to the general side, they always get more resources … (P5)

The inequities reflect institutional priorities that favour general healthcare over mental health services, as one participant articulated:

The facility sees that the general site generates more money than the mental health unit. So, at times, when you place your request, they consider the general people first … the general aspect, is being considered more, let me say, 99% more than the mental health aspect. (P7)

At the policy level, participants noted that these disparities are systematically embedded in budget allocations:

The budget that government read last year shows that just about 1% of resources has been allocated to mental health, and it is very bad. (P7)

Additionally, participants reported experiences of dismissal when requesting resources for mental health services:

At first you could even ask for something, and I remember an administrator asked me what mental health needs I'm using the supplies for. Can you imagine? (P16)

The consequences of these inequities were described as directly compromising patient care and treatment environments:

Some of our patients in certain institutions weren't able to be provided proper accommodation. When they come for admission, they make them lie on old beds where they could be lying in a pool [of water]. (P16)

Theme 2: Economic and Access Barriers

This theme addresses how economic factors and family dynamics influence treatment access and outcomes, operating primarily at individual, interpersonal and policy levels.

Financial Barriers Throughout the Treatment Continuum

The high costs of addiction treatment limit access and lead to treatment discontinuity.

… most of them are very poor. And you know, our drugs sometimes are expensive … when they don't have the funds to help with the treatment, maybe they come in for the first week … then the next two weeks, or when they come for review, they will tell you, 'I wasn't having money to come and continue. (P5)

While initial treatment services may be possible, rehabilitation remains unaffordable for many:

Rehabilitation is expensive. Very expensive. We can't just get up and say, 'I need rehab' without having any financial backup. (P6)

Participants provided specific figures that illustrate how unaffordable rehabilitation services are for average Ghanaians:

… the cheapest you can ever pay is about GHS 2500 ($150) monthly. But there are some other facilities where I know they pay from GHS 4000 to GHS 5000 ($250–$320), even other rehabs where you pay GHS 6000 ($400) per month. (P6)

In the face of these financial barriers, healthcare professionals sometimes use their personal resources, highlighting their dedication and the severity of systemic gaps:

Most of them don't have money, most of them. Sometimes we, the health practitioners, purchase their medication for them. (P8)

At the policy level, lack of insurance coverage compounds these financial challenges:

Health insurance doesn't cover any of their medications, so it means their treatment is more expensive compared to even physical conditions. So, most of them end up breaking treatment in between. (P15)

These conditions create a two‐tier treatment system where care depends on socioeconomic status:

If the person is coming from a rich family, it doesn't affect their treatment. Even if everything goes wrong, the time of staying on the ward is achieved. But when you get someone from a poor background, at the end of the day, we have to discharge them early. (P9)

Family Systems as Critical to Treatment Success

Within this economic context, family support becomes essential for treatment access and continuity. Healthcare providers try to engage families as partners in the treatment process:

We try to involve social welfare to get closer to the family so that they will be educated more about what they need to do to take care of the patient. (P8)

Beyond providing financial support, some families take extraordinary measures to create a recovery‐supportive environment:

I have some families who have really done a very good job by relocating their family members to a different facility, to a different society or community, which is free from drugs. (P12)

Conversely, the absence of family support creates vulnerability to substance use and challenges in recovery. As some participants observed:

… They don't have a wife or husband, or they don't have any children … Maybe that is another factor why some of them go into drugs, because maybe they think they are alone, they don't have anyone … what I'm trying to say is that I think if they belong to their partners, like maybe their husband or their wife, they will advise them to quit … That's another protective factor, which I think they don't get. (P10)

Most of the people who misuse those drugs lack parental care, and when you interview them, most of them are maybe broken homes…. (P16)

Another aspect of family involvement includes education about the medical nature of addiction, helping to shift understanding away from supernatural explanations:

We decide on family therapy, whereby we help the family members to understand that it wasn't because of evil spirits or curses, but it was due to recovery [withdrawal] that the patient was experiencing those symptoms. (P16)

Theme 3: Societal Attitudes and Criminalisation

This theme explores how social stigma, criminalisation and systemic corruption create a hostile environment for treatment engagement, operating primarily at policy, community and interpersonal levels of the socio‐ecological framework. Participants described a complex social landscape where contradictory forces condemn substance use while enabling its continuation.

Stigma and Social Exclusion of PWUDs

Stigma at multiple levels creates barriers to recovery and reintegration. This stigmatisation begins in communities and extends into families, creating isolation for PWUDs. One participant described the extent of discrimination faced by PWUDs in their daily lives:

… normally they face a lot of stigma and discrimination in the community … even in their own homes, they don't allow them to come close to the house. (P5)

Family rejection emerged as a particularly damaging form of stigma that perpetuates cycles of substance use. When family support systems break down, patients often return to substances as a coping mechanism, as illustrated by this participant's account:

It was because of rejection from the family that kept him going back [to drugs] again and again. He was coming from a respectable family, but the family actually rejected him, and so he kept returning to the drug. (P10)

At the community level, categorical labelling and exclusion immediately follow the discovery of substance use, creating barriers to seeking help. One participant captured this immediate categorisation:

… the moment you use drugs; they tag you as a bad person. ‘I don't even want anything to do with you’. (P5)

These labels have lasting consequences, as another participant explained:

They face a whole lot of stigma … The consequence of the stigma is that people neglect them, and the respect that they deserve from within the community, what they have been seeking for, they will not be getting it. (P8)

Particularly concerning was how stigmatising attitudes permeate healthcare settings, creating barriers even when PWUDs attempt to seek care:

Within the health center, when they come to the hospital, because of the way they dress, the way they are, their unkempt personal hygiene at times, the nurses or other healthcare workers become terrified to attend to them. (P7)

The stigma extends to mental health professionals themselves, creating a multi‐layered stigma affecting both providers and patients:

If you are at the general site here, your colleagues see you as not being okay. They think you think like your clients. (P6)

Stigma also impacts employment opportunities, as one participant recalled a community education session where people shared that they abused drugs because of unemployment. These individuals find social belonging and material support among peers who use drugs, which reinforces drug use:

… we went to a health education in a certain community and we asked them why they are using this. And they were like, there are no jobs for them … And then you find these people that they are into it, they are making money, whether legal or illegal. And you associate yourself with them, they will buy food for you … (P20)

Criminalisation's Impact on Treatment Engagement

The criminalisation of substance use creates additional barriers to treatment, as PWUDs avoid seeking help or withhold important information from health professionals due to fear of legal consequences:

Most of them will tell you that they don't even take drugs, thinking that if they tell us, maybe they will get arrested … So we are not able to get the vivid details of what is causing their condition … They retreat back into their shell because of that [fear of being reported]. (P6)

The combined effect of stigma and criminalisation creates a deterrent to seeking help:

The stigma and the fact that people will go and report you [prevents treatment seeking]. (P6)

Healthcare providers often respond by navigating carefully between legal requirements and therapeutic responsibilities, many prioritising treatment over legal reporting:

In this case, I don't really report them, because it's a challenge. It's a condition. So, in dealing with such cases, sometimes you spare the law, because addiction is a chronic illness, and it's classified as a mental health condition according to DSM‐5. We group them into conditions. (P12)

Participants described prisons as environments that expand rather than reduce substance use, contradicting the supposed deterrent effect of incarceration:

… I have one patient who learned about three or four different types of drugs while he was in jail for marijuana … Prison is where you get access to more drugs, different types of drugs. (P12)

Systemic Corruption Enabling Drug Distribution

The criminalisation approach faces an additional contradiction through systemic corruption that facilitates continued drug availability. Participants described sophisticated networks involving respected community members who maintain a deceptive public image:

Would you imagine that these agents that sell these drugs in the system are prominent members of society? In public, they behave as if they know nothing about it. (P11)

Participants mentioned law enforcement agents' involvement in drug distribution, revealing a contradiction between policy and implementation:

I realized that there are networks that transport these drugs from places to places within Ghana, from town to town, and then these drugs are guided by policemen. (P11)

Questions were raised about how controlled substances enter the country despite legal prohibitions:

How would it be possible for mescaline, barbiturates, and other drugs to be in Ghana now? They weren't in Ghana before. So, who brought them? Is it not those people who have diplomatic privileges to go to any country they want without being checked? (P11)

Economic factors were identified as driving participation in these networks:

If I'm working as an officer who is responsible for preventing the distribution of these drug networks, I might take money because I'm not paid well. (P11)

Beyond direct corruption, participants noted how the involvement of public figures in substance abuse undermines prevention efforts:

Sometimes those celebrities use drugs in public … the youth sometimes take them as role models … Someone will say, ‘That person who has money, that politician who has money, that king who has money, uses those drugs. So why? If the drug is not good, why are they using it?’ (P12)

Theme 4: Adaptation and Resilience in a Challenging Environment

This theme captures the adaptive responses of various actors within the treatment ecosystem, operating primarily at individual, interpersonal and community levels.

Evolving Substance Use Patterns and Concealment Methods

Users continuously develop sophisticated approaches to substance use that evade detection, adapting to criminalisation and societal stigma. As treatment providers attempt to identify substance use, patients employ increasingly complex concealment strategies:

… they do not reveal what substances they are involved in … They have certain techniques. They eat certain things. They rinse their mouth or body after taking such things, so you don't even smell it on them … It's not produced in a factory. From information I got from a user, they even sprinkle it on food, and you cannot detect any strange substance in the food. (P16)

Substance preferences themselves are evolving, with users shifting towards novel combinations of legal substances to avoid criminalisation:

Initially, they were using marijuana. But now the trend is buying energy drinks…When they buy these energy drinks, they put in Tramadol, and not even a lower dose of it. (P7)

These adaptations are enabled by failures in pharmaceutical regulation that create accessibility despite formal prescription requirements:

These medications are illegal to sell without prescription, but they're available, and patients know where to get them. Some licensed chemical shops even sell these medications to them. (P15)

Staff Safety Concerns and Personal Commitment

Mental health professionals face safety risks when treating PWUDs, often working without adequate institutional protection. The absence of compensation for risks deters professionals from engaging with PWUDs:

If they harm you, that's all. You go home and treat yourself. So, these are some of the things that push us away from getting closer to them … We don't have any risk allowances. (P8)

Despite these challenges, participants demonstrated remarkable commitment to their work. For many, seeing patients recover provides powerful motivation:

My motivation is to see my patients recover. If you achieve that, God will bless you in many ways. (P10)

Professional identity serves as a foundational motivating force:

Being a mental health professional drives me. That alone drives me. When I go out in the morning, I just feel happy. (P9)

Many providers bridge systemic gaps by contributing their personal resources, exemplifying their dedication:

For the home visits, we use our own money. There is no available vehicle … (P5)

Their motivation is reinforced by witnessing positive outcomes, creating a cycle of commitment despite the challenges:

What really motivated me was seeing several positive outcomes. I'm very glad some patients are responding to treatment … Through that work, I have seen some of them go back to school, and some continue their work. (P5)

Emergent Themes Unique to the Ghanaian Context

Beyond the four main themes organised within the socio‐ecological framework, our analysis revealed several unique phenomena specific to the Ghanaian context that merit particular attention.

Religious and Traditional Healing Interfaces

The Ghanaian treatment landscape is characterised by complex interactions between medical approaches and traditional healing systems. These interactions create distinctive treatment pathways for many patients, with few people seeking help from the hospital:

Mostly in the community, whenever they have this kind of psychotic episode, they don't bring the person straight to the hospital. They first go through faith‐based or traditional healers before eventually calling us … When you consider all cases, only about 40% come straight to the hospital… (P5)

Economic incentives create competition between traditional healers and medical providers:

They charge a lot of money from clients, so they don't want clients to know that it was our medication that actually healed them…. (P5)

These findings suggest that religious and traditional healing systems function as influential community‐level actors within the treatment pathway, shaping when and how individuals eventually enter formal care.

Discussion

This study employed the socio‐ecological framework to explore the perspectives and challenges of mental health professionals treating PWUDs in Ghana. The findings reveal a complex interplay of individual, interpersonal, organisational, community and policy‐level factors that collectively shape treatment provision and outcomes. The themes identified highlight systemic challenges while demonstrating the dedication of providers working within these constraints.

Our results show that treatment for PWUDs in Ghana occurs within a system characterised by profound infrastructural deficits, inequitable resource allocation and financial barriers that limit access to care. Previous research has documented similar constraints in mental health services across sub‐Saharan Africa (Janson et al. 2024; Weobong et al. 2023), but our study illustrates how these systemic weaknesses manifest specifically in addiction treatment within Ghana's criminalised policy environment. The lack of government‐operated rehabilitation centres and the reliance on expensive private facilities create a two‐tiered treatment system accessible only to the wealthy. The shortage of addiction specialists further exacerbates these challenges, leaving general mental health professionals to adapt their practice with limited training or resources. Participants' accounts also suggest that hospital‐based care is often reserved for severe presentations, including acute intoxication, withdrawal‐related complications, agitation, psychosis and co‐occurring mental health crises, while continuity of care after stabilisation remains weak.

The socio‐ecological framework provided a strong theoretical lens to interpret these findings. At the individual and interpersonal levels, stigma, family rejection and concealment strategies shaped treatment engagement. At the organisational level, inadequate facilities and workforce shortages created structural barriers. At the community level, traditional healing systems, corruption and pervasive stigma influenced treatment pathways. Finally, at the policy level, criminalisation perpetuated fear and avoidance of care. By situating these findings across interconnected levels, the socio‐ecological framework allowed us to capture how structural forces interact with personal and family‐level dynamics, offering a more comprehensive understanding than individual‐level explanations alone.

The findings also show that stigma operates at multiple levels, creating barriers not only for PWUDs but also for mental health professionals themselves, who face discrimination from colleagues for working in addiction care. This double burden affects workforce morale and capacity development. Our findings extend global evidence that punitive drug policies discourage treatment‐seeking and contribute to unintended harms (Alhassan 2022; Kimmel et al. 2021). The descriptions of prisons as environments where drug use expands further challenge the deterrent assumptions of criminalisation. Participants' narratives further suggest that criminalisation not only discourages help‐seeking, but may also delay presentation until symptoms become severe enough to require hospital‐based intervention.

Participants also highlighted systemic corruption that enables drug distribution, exposing contradictions between official policy and actual practice. Reports of law enforcement officers and community leaders facilitating distribution mirror findings in other contexts (Alare and Muili 2024; Onohuean and Oosthuizen 2025), but in Ghana this intersects with widespread poverty and unemployment, creating strong incentives for participation in illegal networks. Family systems emerged as critical determinants of treatment outcomes, echoing prior research on the importance of family support (Kabore et al. 2019). In Ghana's collectivist cultural context, family acceptance or rejection strongly influenced whether individuals could access and sustain treatment. Providers also demonstrated extraordinary personal commitment, often using their own resources to compensate for systemic deficits. While this reflects deep professional dedication (Afulani et al. 2021; Botchwey 2021), it is not sustainable and underscores the urgent need for institutional investment.

Several findings suggest the socio‐ecological framework requires adaptation for the Ghanaian context. The influential role of religious and traditional healers, the dual role of law enforcement as both enforcers and facilitators and the extent of personal resource utilisation by staff are context‐specific dynamics that may not be fully captured in existing models. Rather than sitting outside the socio‐ecological framework, religious organisations and traditional healing systems may be more appropriately understood as powerful community‐level actors that shape help‐seeking, referral pathways and treatment timing. These adaptations highlight the originality of this study in extending theoretical application beyond its typical Western use to a low‐resource, criminalised setting.

Policy Implications

Our findings have several important policy implications for addressing substance use in Ghana. First, they strongly suggest the need to reconsider the criminalisation approach to drug policy. The current punitive framework creates significant barriers to treatment engagement while failing to control substance availability. A shift towards a public health‐oriented approach that emphasises treatment access over punishment would address several barriers identified in this study (Kimmel et al. 2021). This should include stronger use of diversion‐oriented responses, such as referral to treatment or rehabilitation in place of incarceration for eligible individuals with substance use disorders.

Second, the treatment infrastructure deficit requires substantial government investment in affordable and accessible rehabilitation facilities. The current reliance on expensive private facilities creates a system where treatment is available only to the wealthy, perpetuating inequities. Developing a national network of government‐supported rehabilitation centres with standardised protocols would address this gap. Such investment should include not only inpatient rehabilitation but also lower‐cost outpatient, day‐treatment and step‐down recovery options that can support ongoing care after stabilisation. Third, the shortage of trained addiction professionals requires targeted workforce development initiatives. Formal addiction specialisation tracks should be established within psychiatric training programmes. In addition, integrating substance use disorder management into continuing professional development curricula would enhance provider capacity.

The documented healthcare inequities call for policy reforms to ensure parity between mental health/addiction services and general healthcare. Expanding health insurance coverage to fully include psychiatric medications and addiction treatment would remove a significant financial barrier (Daliri et al. 2024). Additionally, budgetary allocations should reflect the actual burden of mental health and substance use disorders, moving well beyond the reported 1% currently allocated.

Fourth, addressing pharmaceutical control failures requires strengthened regulatory oversight of licensed chemical shops and pharmacies. The reported easy accessibility of prescription medications without appropriate prescriptions undermines both treatment efforts and prevention initiatives. Implementing more effective monitoring systems while providing education to pharmacy professionals would help address this regulatory gap (Boun et al. 2024). Where feasible, prescription monitoring approaches, including more timely documentation and surveillance of controlled medication dispensing, may help reduce diversion of medications such as tramadol while supporting safer clinical practice. Finally, developing formal transitional housing and vocational support programmes would address the post‐treatment reintegration failures identified by participants. Without structured ‘step‐down’ care, many patients return directly to environments where drugs are readily available, contributing to high relapse rates. Policy frameworks should include support for community reintegration as an essential component of the treatment continuum (Ae‐Ngibise et al. 2025; Alhassan 2022).

Implications for Clinical Practice

For mental health professionals working with PWUDs in Ghana, several practice implications emerge from our findings. First, the importance of addressing treatment readiness and motivation suggests the need for stronger integration of motivational enhancement techniques in clinical protocols (Fuseini et al. 2019; Peprah et al. 2020). Given the identified challenges with treatment continuity, targeting motivation as a specific focus rather than assuming it exists could improve engagement. Second, the critical role of family systems in treatment outcomes suggests the need for formalised family involvement protocols. Practitioners should systematically assess family dynamics, provide structured family education about addiction as a disease and develop specific strategies for engaging relatives as partners in the recovery process (Kabore et al. 2019). This may include addressing family misconceptions that attribute addiction to spiritual causes.

Third, the evolving substance use patterns and concealment methods identified require clinicians to continually update their knowledge and assessment practices. Regular training on emerging substance combinations and detection strategies would help providers identify substance use that might otherwise go undetected (Molobe et al. 2023; Rutherford et al. 2023). Fourth, the significant safety concerns reported by participants highlight the need for formal safety protocols and training. Clinical settings should implement structured approaches to manage aggressive behaviour, ensure adequate staffing during high‐risk interactions and provide support for staff who experience violence or threats (Afulani et al. 2021; Botchwey 2021). Fifth, the healthcare professional vulnerability to addiction revealed by participants suggests the need for specialised support programmes for healthcare workers with substance use disorders. The unique challenges faced by professionals seeking treatment, including licensure concerns and heightened stigma, require tailored approaches that protect both confidentiality and career opportunities (Jaguga et al. 2022; Ratshisusu et al. 2024). Finally, the personal resource utilisation by staff, while admirable, indicates the need for more sustainable approaches to service delivery. Clinical programmes should advocate for adequate resources while developing creative solutions that do not rely on staff's personal finances. This might include forming partnerships with NGOs or developing income‐generating activities to support outreach efforts (Ae‐Ngibise et al. 2025; Weobong et al. 2023). Clinical practice should also strengthen referral pathways to available community and faith‐based supports where appropriate, while ensuring that such referrals do not replace evidence‐based treatment.

Future Research Directions

Our findings suggest several important directions for future research. First, studies examining the effectiveness of specific treatment approaches within the Ghanaian context are needed. Participants described various interventions but limited evidence exists regarding their comparative effectiveness for this population. Culturally adapted treatment models that incorporate Ghanaian social structures and values should be developed and evaluated. Second, research exploring the experiences of PWUDs themselves would complement the provider perspectives captured in this study. Third, implementation research examining how evidence‐based practices can be effectively adapted for resource‐constrained settings would address the challenges identified by participants. This could include studying simplified protocols, task‐shifting approaches or technology‐enabled interventions that extend limited professional resources (Weobong et al. 2023). Fourth, economic analyses documenting the cost‐effectiveness of treatment versus criminalisation would provide valuable evidence for policy advocacy. Participants described the high costs of the current approach, both financial and human, but systematic data on comparative costs would strengthen arguments for policy reform (Kimmel et al. 2021). Fifth, research examining how family‐cantered interventions can be optimised for the Ghanaian context would address the role of family systems identified in this study. Developing and evaluating culturally appropriate family involvement protocols could significantly improve treatment outcomes (Wallace et al. 2021). Finally, longitudinal studies tracking treatment outcomes over time would provide much‐needed data on recovery trajectories in this context. Participants described high relapse rates and challenges with sustained recovery, but systematic data on long‐term outcomes is lacking (Lasong et al. 2024; Saapiire et al. 2021). Future research should also examine the availability and effectiveness of outpatient, day‐treatment, peer‐support and faith‐based recovery programmes in Ghana, given their likely importance within the broader treatment ecosystem.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. The sample may not fully represent the broader range of providers treating PWUDs in Ghana, particularly those in rural settings. Additionally, the perspectives of PWUDs themselves were not included, which may have revealed additional barriers and facilitators not captured here. Despite these limitations, the study followed COREQ reporting guidelines, employed rigorous analytic procedures and incorporated strategies to enhance trustworthiness, including member checking and peer debriefing. These strengths support the credibility of the findings.

Conclusion

Mental health professionals in Ghana face significant challenges in treating PWUDs, navigating a system constrained by inadequate resources, financial barriers, stigma and criminalisation. The socio‐ecological framework revealed how these barriers operate simultaneously across multiple levels, while also highlighting the resilience and commitment of providers working in constrained environments. To our knowledge, this study is among the first to capture the experiences of Ghanaian mental health nurses within a criminalised drug policy environment, offering insights into how structural and cultural factors intersect with frontline practice. Addressing these challenges requires a policy shift towards public health‐oriented responses, sustained investment in specialised addiction services and strategies that support both patients and providers. By documenting these perspectives, the study contributes to international evidence on addiction care in low‐resource settings and underscores the importance of moving beyond punitive frameworks to treatment‐cantered approaches.

Relevance to Mental Health Nursing

This study directly addresses the experiences of mental health nurses working on the frontlines of addiction care in a challenging environment. The findings provide evidence‐based insights into systemic barriers that impact nursing practice, highlight the need for specialised training and support systems for nurses treating substance use disorders and demonstrate how nurses adapt creatively to deliver compassionate care despite profound resource constraints. The research contributes to understanding how policy environments influence nursing practice and provides practical recommendations for enhancing both patient outcomes and nurse well‐being in addiction care settings.
