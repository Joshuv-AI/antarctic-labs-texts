Influence of atomic configuration on electronic and magnetic properties of complex Ruddlesden-Popper oxides La2−xSrxCo1/2Fe1/2O4
Authors: Dina I. Fazlizhanova, Sergey V. Levchenko
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Ruddlesden-Popper (RP) transition-metal oxides belong to a versatile class of functional materials whose electronic properties can be tuned by varying chemical composition in a wide range. However, the relation between chemical composition and electronic properties of multi-metal RP oxides, in particular taking into account different distributions of metal ions in the lattice, has not been investigated systematically so far. In this work, we use density-functional theory (DFT) to explore the compositional dependence of electronic, magnetic, and structural properties of La\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2-x}$$\end{document}2-xSr\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_x$$\end{document}xCo\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{1/2}$$\end{document}1/2Fe\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{1/2}$$\end{document}1/2O\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_4$$\end{document}4 oxide series for x = 0, 1, 2. To account for localized nature of transition-metal d-orbitals, self-consistent DFT+U approach Agapito-Curtarolo-Buongiorno-Nardelli (ACBN0) is employed. We show that widely used electronic-structure-based descriptors of oxygen-defect formation energies, oxygen transport, and catalytic activity strongly depend on the distribution of transition metal ions. Nevertheless, the distribution-averaged and ground-state descriptor values for La\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2-x}$$\end{document}2-xSr\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_x$$\end{document}xCo\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{1/2}$$\end{document}1/2Fe\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{1/2}$$\end{document}1/2O\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_4$$\end{document}4 are found to depend monotonically on the composition x. Introduction

Ruddlesden-Popper (RP) oxides, with the general formula A\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{1+n}$$\end{document}1+nB\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_n$$\end{document}nO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{3n+1}$$\end{document}3n+1, where A is an alkali or alkali earth element, B is transition metal (TM), and n is the number of the perovskite layers, were demonstrated to be promising in a wide range of applications such as electrocatalysis1, solid oxide fuel cells2, oxygen membranes3, nonvolatile memory, giant magnetoresistance sensors, and magnetic read heads4. Tunability of properties due to variability in A and B site substitutions makes RP oxides an attractive class of materials for finding materials with the best target property.

Direct prediction of desired properties is usually computationally expensive. Therefore, there are numerous attempts in the literature to find more easily obtainable properties, called descriptors, that correlate with the target property of TM oxides. For example, O 2p-band center5,6 correlates with the oxygen vacancy and interstitial formation energies6, oxygen surface exchange coefficients, activation barrier for bulk oxygen transport7, catalytic activity for oxygen evolution reaction5. The descriptor concept is especially popular in predicting catalytic activity. For instance, transition metal’s band overlap1, hybridization of transition metal 3d and oxygen 2p electrons8, transition-metal unoccupied 3d band center, unoccupied 3d band center relative to O 2p-band center9, M-O-M bond angle10, are considered as descriptor candidates. However, these properties could be sensitive to the distributions of elements in mixed-metal oxides. The commonly accepted distributions are homogeneous1 or random6, although magnetic structure, charge ordering11, and various spin states12 make prediction of the most energetically favorable distribution complicated. Moreover, metastable distributions can be realized in experiments depending on the synthesis method, which could have very different electronic structure. Thus, knowledge of the relation between atomic and electronic structure is valuable for both understanding and predicting RP oxide functional materials.

Computational methods

We used Quantum Espresso program package with norm-conserving pseudopotentials24–26. The kinetic energy cutoff 90 Ry and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$6\times 6\times 6$$\end{document}6×6×6 k-point mesh were chosen. For our calculations, we used \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2 \times 2 \times 2$$\end{document}2×2×2 supercells constructed from the primitive cell. However, for clarity, we present the supercell structure in its conventional form in Fig. 1 a) and use this representation for structural analysis. The modeling of La/Sr doping in LSCFO and its effects on properties is discussed in detail in27. In that study, we examined three types of La/Sr and Fe/Co distributions, similar to the approach in this paper: uniform (Fig. 1b), stripes of La (Co) and Sr (Fe) within each layer (Fig. 1c), and alternating layers of La (Co) and Sr (Fe) (Fig. 1d), resulting in nine total structures of LSCFO. In this study, we utilize some of the results previously obtained in27.

To take into account the localized nature of transition-metal 3d-orbitals, we used ACBN0 approximation of exchange-correlation functional28 implemented in AFLOW\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi$$\end{document}π package29. As ACBN0 approach is not widely discussed in the literature, we represent here the key features of this method. Within the ACBN0 method the U parameter is calculated using unscreened Coulomb and exchange interactions between system-specific Hubbard orbitals, derived from unrestricted Hartree-Fock orbitals. Key steps of the ACBN0 method include:Selecting Hubbard orbitals as pseudoatomic orbitals (PAOs) from pseudopotential files.Representing PAOs as a combination of three Gaussian atomic orbitals for computational efficiency.Projecting Kohn-Sham orbitals onto the PAO basis.Renormalizing orbital occupations based on Mulliken population through projection onto the Hubbard basis.Explicitly calculating Hartree (U) and exchange (J) terms using Hartree-Fock kernels, normalized by the occupation of Hubbard orbitals.Thus, ACBN0 method is parameter free and one does not need experimental results to fit the U value, and for each crystallographically non-equivalent site the U value can be determined separately.

In principle, the ACBN0 approach suggests applying the Hubbard U correction to every orbital in the system. However, since the total energy remains unaffected by the U values of fully occupied or empty orbitals, it is sufficient to apply the correction only to the O 2p and Fe/Co 3d orbitals. The U correction applied to O 2p orbitals ensures accurately capturing the hybridized states between O 2p and the metal 3d orbitals. The U values was converged within 0.1 eV accuracy. ACBN0 was applied on top of the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional30. Atomic and lattice relaxations were performed with ACBN0.

For some structures, the energetically favorable magnetic structures obtained within PBE and ACBN0 are found to be different, even when using the same geometries and initial values of magnetic moments. For example, ACBN0 magnetic moments of iron always correspond to a HS state, while PBE sometimes gives LS states as the most favourable spin state. It is well-known that local density functionals suffer from self-interaction errors, leading to unphysical delocalization of electrons. This issue not only results in metallic conductivity in systems that are experimentally known to be insulators or semiconductors but also fails to reproduce correct magnetic structures. The reason is that delocalization suppresses superexchange interactions by causing partial occupation of d orbitals. While advanced methods based on the meta-generalized gradient approximation can sometimes reproduce the gapped nature of electronic structures and capture structural symmetry breaking31,32, they require for some cases the addition of a U correction to accurately capture the localized nature of d orbitals33. This is because no local functional can fully eliminate self-interaction errors. Therefore, it is essential to use methods beyond local functionals for a more accurate description.

For each structure, we attempted to stabilize various magnetic configurations. However, in most cases, we were unable to obtain different spin states for Co, despite adjusting the initial magnetic moments and mixer parameters. This indicates that the spin state of Co is largely determined by the structural configuration.

The verification of methods as well as convergence tests are described in our previous work27.

Results and discussions

As mentioned above, ACBN0 is a parameter-free functional, and the Hubbard U value is an internal technical parameter. To compare energies, we used the U value that has been self-consistently calculated for each specific structure. In principle, the U values may vary between different structures and this variation is a consequence of differences in electronic structure.

The converged U values are shown in Fig. 2. It is accepted in the literature that for a transition-metal atom in perovskite and RP oxide phases it is possible to use the same U value as for the binary oxide of the same transition metal, if the oxidation state of the transition metal and its local environment are the same in the more complex phases. However, we found that even for the same charge state the values of U can be different since the electronic structure depends on the atom environment. When Co\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{3+}$$\end{document}3+ and Fe\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{3+}$$\end{document}3+ are in the high spin state, each d orbital is occupied by at least one electron. According to the Pauli exclusion principle, the additional electron must have a spin direction opposite to the majority of electrons in the d orbital, leading to an underestimation of the magnetic moment assigned to the transition metal. Figure 3 shows that the magnetic moments of HS Co and Fe are correlated with their Löwdin oxidation states, which are defined as the difference between the number of valence electrons and the Löwdin charge. Specifically, the fewer electrons are assigned to the transition metal, the greater magnetic moment is. When Co is in the LS or IS state, there are unoccupied d orbitals, and the additional electron can, in principle, have any spin direction. However, in our study, for all configurations with Co in the IS state, the additional electron had the same spin direction as the majority of d electrons on Co, leading to an overestimation of the magnetic moment assigned to Co.

Additionally, there is a noticeable variation in Löwdin magnetic moments in LSCFO compared to LCFO. Both the variation in magnetic moments and the close values of Löwdin magnetic moments of Co in IS and HS states arise from the delocalization of electronic density between oxygen and the transition metal, as the partitioning method cannot clearly attribute the electronic density to either the transition metal or the nearest oxygen.

In SCFO, the Löwdin magnetic moments of Fe range from 3.2 to 3.7, while those of Co vary between 1.7 and 3.2, without forming distinct groups. For comparison, in LSCFO, the magnetic moments of Co in high- and intermediate-spin states range from 2.15 to 3, and Fe from 3.5 to 4. This indicates that the ranges of magnetic moment values in LSCFO and SCFO overlap. The Löwdin oxidation state of Co varies between 0.82 and 1.12, depending on configuration, while for Fe it ranges from 1.04 to 1.11. Oxidation states of some Co in HS and IS states, and Fe, also lie in this range in LSCFO (see Fig. 3). We explored alternative partitioning methods, including Bader partitioning and the partitioning implemented in Quantum Espresso (which involves integration over a sphere of a given radius). All these analyses yielded consistent results: the variation ranges of Co and Fe oxidation states in SCFO and LSCFO strongly overlap. In both LSCFO and SCFO, the holes introduced by substituting Sr for La are accommodated at the hybridized states of oxygen and transition metals. In the spin-majority channel, the main contribution comes from oxygen, while in the spin-minority channel, it is primarily determined by the transition metals. Most structures in SCFO exhibit metallic behavior in the spin minority channel, raising doubts about the localized spin model for SCFO and instead suggesting a picture of itinerant magnetism (see also Fig. 5). Unoccupied states in the spin majority channel in SCFO are more pronounced than in LSCFO: they are either closer to the Fermi level or their number has increased. In general, magnetic interaction between different TM layers in RP oxides is commonly accepted to be weak11,18,34 due to cation interleaving between the perovskite layers. However, in some cases the layers cannot be considered entirely independent. For example, in Ref.18 a gradual transition from 2D to 3D ferromagnetic ordering was observed in La\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2-x}$$\end{document}2-xSr\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_x$$\end{document}xCoO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_4$$\end{document}4 with increasing Sr concentration exceeding 1.3. To investigate this aspect further, we explored various magnetic orderings in Sr\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}2Co\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{1/2}$$\end{document}1/2Fe\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{1/2}$$\end{document}1/2O\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_4$$\end{document}4 with Co/Fe layers interchanged as an example (Fig. 1d). Structures were fully relaxed for each magnetic configuration. Our results indicate that layers are not independent. First, the energy difference per primitive cell between FM and AFM order within the Co layer depends on the magnetic order in the Fe layer: it is 0.2 eV for AFM order in the Fe layer, and 0.4 eV for the FM order in the Fe layer. Second, The Löwdin magnetic moments of Co (Fe) depend on the magnetic ordering in the Fe (Co) layer, even if the magnetic ordering in the Co (Fe) layer remains the same. (see Table 1). Additionally, there is a variation of Co Löwdin charge (charge disproportionation), except for the structure where both layers are FM-ordered.

As expected from Löwdin partitioning, the DOS projected on Co 3d orbitals depends not only on the magnetic configuration in the Co layer, but also on the magnetic configuration in Fe layer. When the Co layer is ordered AFM and the Fe layer FM, the occupied states of Co differ from what is typically expected for an AFM-ordered structure: DOS projected on the Co 3d orbitals for the different spin channels are different. However, when the Fe layer is AFM-ordered, Co 3d DOS are the same for both spin channels, as expected. In addition, the DOS projected on Co 3d states are non-zero at the Fermi level when Fe ordered AFM, indicating metallic behavior. However, when Fe ordered FM, DOS projected on Co 3d states is close to zero at the Fermi level, resulting in semi-metal behavior.

In the case of FM-ordered Co layer, Co unoccupied states are delocalized, resulting in strongly metallic behavior. Also, when Co is FM-ordered, more localized unoccupied states appear when Fe is AFM-ordered in comparison to the case when Fe layer is FM ordered.

The difference in DOS projected on Fe 3d orbitals for a fixed magnetic configuration of Fe but different magnetic configurations of Co, is also present but less pronounced.

The most energetically favorable doping distribution (considering also magnetic ordering) in SCFO and LCFO is the one where the number of Fe and Co atoms is the same in each layer but the two types of atoms are ordered in stripes (panel c in Fig. 1c). The dependence of lattice parameters on Sr concentration is shown in Fig. 7. Several factors contribute to the changes in lattice parameters. The smaller ionic radius of La\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{3+}$$\end{document}3+ compared to Sr\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{2+}$$\end{document}2+ results in a smaller distance between oxygen polyhedra, resulting in a reduced c parameter and an increased a parameter. However, replacing La with Sr also alters the oxidation state of the transition metals. More oxidized transition metals have a shorter distance to the apical oxygen. This effect has a greater influence on the lattice vectors than the size of A cation, leading to a decrease in lattice parameters with increasing Sr concentration in the sample. It is worth noting that in real samples oxygen deficiency and the resulting distortions play a significant role. Moreover, according to our calculations, the relaxed lattice parameters depend on doping distribution, as well as on electronic and magnetic state. In fact, the standard deviation from the values of lattice parameters averaged over all different metal distributions and spin states is of the same order as the difference between the averaged values for different Sr concentrations.

Figure 7b shows the dependence of the ACBN0 transition-metal-oxygen bond lengths on the Sr concentration x. The Fe/Co-in-plane oxygen O1 distance exhibits minimal dependence on x, while the Fe/Co-out-of-plane oxygen O2 distance decreases by approximately 10% as we move from LCFO to LSCFO and from LSCFO to SCFO. Notably, the bond length between the transition metal and out-of-plane oxygen is sometimes used as an indicator of the transition metalâ€™s oxidation state40. However, our calculations show that the electronic states associated with Fe change minimally between LSCFO and SCFO. Thus, a more accurate interpretation of bond length would consider the total electronic states assigned to both oxygen and the transition metal, rather than solely focusing on the transition metal’s oxidation state. Conclusion

We explored the impact of transition-metal distribution on the structural, electronic, and magnetic properties within the La\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2-x}$$\end{document}2-xSr\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_x$$\end{document}xCo\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{1/2}$$\end{document}1/2Fe\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{1/2}$$\end{document}1/2O\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_4$$\end{document}4 series, and assessed common electronic-structure descriptors that are discussed in literature for various target properties. The standard deviation of lattice constants resulting from distinct distributions of Fe/Co is substantial, comparable to the differences in averaged values observed for varying Sr concentrations. Conversely, the distances between Co/Fe and O exhibit a more pronounced variation with Sr concentration than with different distributions of Co and Fe.

We find a trend in the dependence of localized states’ energies with varying doping distribution and Sr concentration, while delocalized states undergo significant changes without a clear correlation to doping distribution and Sr concentration. The most energetically favorable magnetic configuration is determined by both Sr concentration and doping distribution.

The variation in the O 2p-band center, 3d unoccupied band center, charge transfer gap, and hybridization parameter based on Kohn-Sham orbitals among different distributions of transition metals for a fixed Sr concentration is significant. This variation is greater than the deviation of the averaged values from a least-squares fit of the Sr-concentration dependence of configuration-averaged descriptor values.
