Structure Elucidation of the Daptomycin Products Generated upon Heterologous Expression of the Daptomycin Resistance Gene Cluster drcAB
Authors: Lukas Kirchner, Tessa Marciniak, Christine Erk, Wilma Ziebuhr, Oliver Scherf-Clavel, Ulrike Holzgrabe
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Recently, a high-level daptomycin (DAP)-resistant Mammaliicoccus sciuri strain (TS92) was identified, which mediates a 33% decline of DAP when incubated in Mueller-Hinton (MH) medium. The genetic background of the DAP resistance in TS92 is a newly discovered two-gene operon, named drcAB, whose expression was reported to impair the structural integrity of DAP, eventually leading to its inactivation. Here, we set out to elucidate the chemical nature of drcAB-mediated DAP modification by applying a general unknown comparative screening (GUCS) approach in high-resolution mass spectrometry. DAP in MH medium was incubated with Staphylococcus aureus strain RN4220_Pxyl/tet-drcAB, which carries the drcAB operon under control of an inducible promoter on a plasmid, and GUCS test and reference samples were obtained upon and without drcAB expression. A two-step process catalyzed by DrcAB was discovered, comprising a structural alteration of DAP. The mass spectrometric data indicate an N-substitution at the aniline moiety of kynurenine with dehydroalanine and, subsequently, a cleavage of the ester bond of the DAP core between kynurenine and threonine by means of water. The structures postulated were confirmed by comparison of in silico versus measured fragmentation patterns.

Antibiotic-resistant bacteria are of great concern, especially when they are resistant to multiple or last resort antibiotics. The 2016 published O’Neill report warns that antimicrobial resistance (AMR)-related deaths will exceed those caused by cancer until 2050, unless this trend is slowed decisively.1,2 A crucial step in overcoming AMR is the restricted use of antibiotics. For evaluation and monitoring the use of antibiotics, the World Health Organization (WHO) therefore published the access, watch, reserve (AWaRe) classification for antibiotics whose latest version from 2023 includes 261 antibiotics. The reserve group contains 29 different drugs or drug combinations, such as the cyclic lipopeptide daptomycin (DAP), whose application is considered as last resort.3 Moreover, the detailed elucidation of existing resistance mechanisms is of great relevance, as it is the basis for both appropriate surveillance measures and targeted structural modification of the antibiotic to overcome resistance.

In 2017, the high-level DAP-resistant Staphylococcus sciuri strain TS92, reclassified as Mammaliicoccus sciuri (M. sciuri) in 2020, was discovered in an AMR study in pig farming.4,5 Initial investigations of the underlying resistance mechanism using liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) revealed a significant decline of the DAP concentration after a 24 h incubation of the antibiotic with M. sciuri TS92 in Mueller-Hinton (MH) medium when applying subinhibitory DAP concentrations.6 The resistance was recently found to be mediated by the two-gene operon drcAB, which was identified by transcriptomic profiling upon DAP pulse-exposure. The subsequent subcloning of drcAB on a plasmid vector and its heterologous expression in a Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) genetic background resulted in high-level DAP resistance in the recombinant S. aureus strain RN4220_Pxyl/tet-drcAB. Upon drcAB expression, a decrease of DAP concentration was observed, which was presumably due to DAP inactivation.7 However, the molecular fate of DAP upon incubation with S. aureus RN4220_Pxyl/tet-drcAB remains to be elucidated.

High-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS), e.g., by means of a qTOF instrument, has gained great importance for the qualification of unknown molecules in complex matrices. By providing high mass resolution and accuracy (<5 ppm), HRMS acquisition schemes such as the information-dependent acquisition (IDA) offer the possibility of structural elucidation.8−10

In this study, we aimed at elucidating the molecular fate of DAP upon incubation with S. aureus RN4220_Pxyl/tet-drcAB, which is carrying the drcAB gene cluster being responsible for resistance. We first deployed a LC-MS/HRMS-based nontargeted, general unknown comparative screening (GUCS) to mask out matrix-related molecules.8,11 Then, we focused on molecules that have emerged after induction of the gene expression and aimed to elucidate their structure. This may facilitate a hypothesis for the resistance mechanism.

Results and Discussion

In order to study the impact of the DAP-inactivating proteins DrcA and DrcB, DAP was incubated in MH medium with S. aureus RN4220_Pxyl/tet-drcAB, which carries the DAP resistance genes (drcA and drcB) from M. sciuri TS92. These genes were heterologously expressed under the control of an anhydrotetracycline (ATC)-inducible promoter. The so-obtained mixture of compounds was separated by means of a previously developed RP18 gradient LC-MS/MS method, which served for the measurement of the DAP concentration.6 The method had to be slightly optimized for separation and elution of all components and adopted for high-resolution qTOF mass detection. Considering that DAP (exact mass of 1619.71 Da for the neutral compound) mainly occurs as [M+2H]2+ adduct (m/z = 810.86) under the selected MS conditions, the focus was placed on products with a maximum m/z of 5000.

For the sake of comparison of the drcAB-expressing strains, the concentration loss of DAP upon incubation with S. aureus RN4220_Pxyl/tet-drcAB was investigated and compared to the previously obtained results with M. sciuri TS92. After 24 h of incubation, both strains showed a relative decline of DAP of 42% (Table 1) and 33%, respectively.6 It is therefore likely that both the recombinant S. aureus strain and the original M. sciuri strain are equivalent in this respect, so as they are in terms of the minimum DAP inhibitory concentration of 64 μg/mL.7

Untargeted Analysis GUCS

Incubation of S. aureus RN4220_Pxyl/tet-drcAB with DAP in MH medium was performed with (test sample) and without (reference sample) induction of the drcAB gene expression. For the GUCS approach, chromatographic-, MS-, and MS/HRMS-information on every substance detected in the test and reference samples were merged into so-called features. Only the two features with m/z of 845.4 (feature 1) and 854.4 (feature 2) showed area ratios of the test to reference sample of higher than ten, being 57.1 × 104 and 23.2 × 104, respectively (Table 1). Based on the isotope patterns of features 1 and 2, these were recognized as [M+2H]2+-adducts, like DAP. An overlaid chromatogram of the accordingly extracted m/z traces can be found in Figure S1.

Derivation of Sum- and Structural Formulas for the GUCS Features

Sum formulas for the precursor ions (non-fragmented) of features 1 and 2 were developed. The sum formula of C75H106N18O272+ (error: 1.18 ppm) was obtained for the adduct of feature 1 with a m/z of 845.3722, assuming C75H104N18O27 for the neutral molecule. In comparison to the sum formula of DAP (C72H101N17O26), there is a difference of C3H3NO. For feature 2 with a m/z of 854.3776, the sum formula of C75H108N18O282+ (error: 1.05 ppm) was obtained for its adduct and hence C75H106N18O28 for the neutral molecule. Comparing the sum formulas of features 1 and 2, feature 2 differs by a H2O molecule.

Subsequently, sum formulas were derived for all fragments of the features 1, 2, and DAP, with a relative intensity of ≥10% in the respective fragment spectrum (Tables S1–S3). The possible sum formula of each fragment was restricted to that of its precursor. The sum formula of the DAP fragments, and for the features 1 and 2, were then assigned to their corresponding part of the molecule to derive their structural formulas (Figures S2–S4). Since the fragments with m/z of 159.1, 187.1, 313.2, and 341.2 were found for DAP as well as for the features 1 and 2 (Figure 1), features 1 and 2 have parts of the DAP structure in common. They are related to the DAP chain (see the DAP chain and core sections in Figure 2) and the adjacent tryptophane moiety. Hence, the DrcAB-induced alteration of the structure must have taken place in other parts of DAP.

Identification of Kynurenine as the Site of the Enzymatic Alteration

In feature 1, the DAP fragments with m/z values of 640.8 and 1280.5 (Figure 1a) shifted to m/z values of 675.3 and 1349.5, respectively (Figure 1b). Under consideration of their charges, each of the two shifts can be assigned to a sum formula of C3H3NO, indicating a DrcAB-induced alteration in the DAP core. By considering the feature 1 fragments with m/z values of 217.1 and 261.1, a more precise localization is possible, as they can be attributed to the kynurenine (KYN) moiety of the DAP core (cf. Figure 2, highlighted in blue), which carries a C3H3NO moiety (Figures 1b and 2). Besides these two KYN fragments, an additional fragment with a m/z of 278.1, which shows a KYN-associated structure alteration, appeared in the fragmentation of feature 2 (Figures 1c and 2). Thus, this structure variation by DrcAB must have occurred for features 1 and 2. Moreover, these fragments confirm the maintained integrity of the carbonyl- and the carboxyl function but indicate a C3H4NO substitution at the anilino nitrogen of KYN (Figure 2).

When comparing the sum formulas of features 1 and 2, it becomes clear that feature 2 formally contains an additional water molecule. Since none of the fragments in the pattern of feature 2 exhibit a corresponding shift, this may be explained by a hydrolysis of the lactone moiety of the core. The fragment pathway of feature 1 (Figure S3) implies that the lactone bond may also be cleaved during the fragmentation process. Nevertheless, ring-closed fragments were found for feature 1. In contrast, no ring-closed fragment was found for feature 2 (Figure S4), hence indicating that the lactone moiety experienced a DrcAB-induced hydrolysis. A corresponding instability was not found in our previous work showing a constant concentration of DAP over 24 h at 37 °C in MH medium.6 Since there was also no hydrolysis in the absence of ATC (reference sample), this reaction cannot be explained by instability or another enzymatical activity.

The DrcAB-mediated reaction postulated requires the presence of the KYN anilino function, which appears to act as a nucleophile. In addition, another reaction partner is necessary to generate the product that was found. DAP′s mode of action can be regarded as a kind of oligomerization, and the site of action is located in phosphatidylglycerol (PG) and bactoprenyl-coupled lipid-intermediate-rich regions in the bacterial membrane.12 Hence, the search for the second reaction partner is restricted to molecules that can be found in the bacterial membrane and contain functional groups that have the potential to act as electrophiles, such as fatty acids or α-amino acids. According to the fragment shifts found, it must have a sum formula of C3H5NO2. The database-supported compound search (PubChem) resulted in nine hits (Figure S5).13 Due to their close relationship to amino acids, dehydroalanine (DHA) and its tautomer (2-iminopropanoic acid) represent the most promising hit. Hence, the DrcAB-mediated amide formation between DHA and DAP is postulated to inactivate DAP (Figure 3). The HRMS data do not distinguish between both tautomers.

(b) This reaction is followed by a hydrolytic cleavage of the lactone bond between KYN and threonine (Thr). The α,β-unsaturated carbonyl group of DHA is a potent Michael-acceptor and, thus, prone to react with nucleophiles (Michael-donors), such as thiols, amines, or alcohols.14 Although DAP contains some functional groups that may potentially serve as a Michael-donor, the sum formulas found indicate the amide formation rather than a corresponding Michael adduct. This observation suggests an enzyme-driven active transfer of DHA to DAP. The absence of ring-opened DAP in the reference sample further supports the idea of a two-step DAP alteration process in which the DHA transfer is followed by ring hydrolysis. Based on these findings, the existence of transferase and hydrolase activity can be assumed for DrcAB.

Discussion on the Plausibility of the Postulated Structures

With growing molecular weights, the number of possible sum formulas and their corresponding constitutional isomers increases. Hence, with regard to structural elucidation using LC-MS/HRMS approaches, the probable results are becoming increasingly blurred, particularly for multiple charged ions. Thus, structures proposed in this process must undergo (several) confirmation stages.

The most reliable confirmation is a targeted approach, in which the fragment pattern of an authentic reference substance is used for verification.8,15 However, due to the lack of commercial availability or synthesis strategies, this is not always applicable, as is the case here. This issue was addressed according to the workflow of Wohlfart et al. by comparing experimentally and theoretically (in silico fragmentation) gained MS/HRMS spectra.16 As the lowest selectable quadrupole 1 (Q1) mass resolution is 1 unit, isotopes within this range were able to pass through Q1, resulting in the occurrence of isotope fragments. This had to be taken into account when evaluating the fragment spectra. In order to ensure a high reliability for this type of scoring, fragments with a relative intensity of ≥10% in each spectrum were considered only. The accordance of these data was 100% for feature 1 and DAP and 92% for feature 2 (Tables S1–S3). A unique molecular structure was assigned to all but the fragment with the m/z of 1447.7 of feature 2 (Figures S2–S4). Moreover, the measured isotopic distribution ratios of features 1 and 2 did not differ more than 3% from their respective theoretical value (Figure S6). Hence, the assumptions made previously and thus the structures proposed were confirmed.

The occurrence of the DHA moiety as an artifact, which emerges from amino acids, such as alanine, cysteine, or serine during the MS-ionization process, is theoretically possible. However, this scenario is unlikely to occur since ESI is a soft ionization technique.17 Moreover, no similar phenomena were observed for DAP, and the respective features occur only in the samples in which the drcAB gene cluster was expressed. Nevertheless, it is plausible that DHA originates from cysteine or serine, as these are typical precursors in the biosynthesis of DHA.18

Recent structure–activity relationship models of DAP attribute KYN a crucial role in the interaction with the lipid bilayer, in the binding of calcium, and therefore in the antibacterial activity. In particular, Taylor and Palmer demonstrated a 47-fold loss of activity after replacing KYN with tyrosine, whereas Chow et al. demonstrated an increase in activity after KYN N-methylation.19−22 KYN N-methylated DAP (kynomycin) has been shown to possess a 2–4-fold higher antibacterial activity than DAP and to eradicate certain DAP-resistant strains.22 The improved antibacterial profile of kynomycin could now likely be explained by a reduced accessibility of transferases to N-methylated KYN. Hence, KYN N-substitution appears to be a promising bacterial mechanism to confer increased DAP insusceptibility in general. In the second stage of the drcAB-induced DAP inactivation, a hydrolysis of the lactone bond along with linearization of DAP-DHA was observed. This is remarkable considering that this linearization would be sufficient for DAP inactivation, as demonstrated by D’Costa et al.23 However, from the bacterial perspective, the combination of these two mechanisms will certainly ensure efficacy to combat DAP.

Apart from that, the question of the origin of DHA or its derivatives arises. Grein et al. postulated the interruption and delocalization of the cell wall biosynthesis machinery (transpeptidase and transglycosylase) as the main mode of action of DAP. They were able to demonstrate the formation of a tripartite complex of Ca2+-DAP with PG and bactoprenyl-coupled lipid intermediates (lipids I and II) within the membrane.12 The transpeptidase releases d-alanine, a DHA derivative, upon the cross-linking of the peptidoglycan units.24,25 Along with that, DrcAB is known to be a membrane-associated enzyme complex which is supposed to span the entire membrane.7 Thus, all of the enzymes and putative substrates involved are located close to each other. Also, the general possibility of modifications by transfer of amino acids, such as alanine in Gram-positive bacteria, has already been described, making the mechanism a plausible scenario.26

Conclusions

The generation of S. aureus RN4220_Pxyl/tet-drcAB can be considered a decisive tool as it enabled GUCS by providing test and reference samples. IDA data gained from LC-MS/HRMS measurements revealed the presence of two molecules, features 1 and 2, that emerged upon the heterologous expression of drcAB. Their structural elucidation was performed under consideration of the corresponding mass spectrometric data. With the knowledge of the DAP fragmentation pattern, features 1 and 2 were found to be DAP-derivatives that were generated by a DrcAB-mediated two-step process. This mechanism comprises N-substitution of the arylamine moiety of KYN with C3H4NO and a subsequent hydrolysis of the lactone moiety. Hence, DrcAB was shown to possess both transferase and hydrolase activity. The combined data led us to the hypothesis that DHA is transferred to DAP, resulting in DAP-DHA and along with this to a DAP inactivation. Bacteria have evolved a broad variety of transferases to add molecules or groups to potentially hazardous compounds, including antibiotics. A well-known and prime example is the modification (and inactivation) of aminoglycosides by acetylation, phosphorylation, or nucleotidylation. For lipopeptides, however, resistance through modifying enzymes has not been observed so far. By attributing transferase and hydrolase activity to DrcAB, we expand the antibiotic modifying resistance mechanisms to DAP and describe (to the best of our knowledge) for the first time the transfer of DHA to a KYN moiety in nature.

Experiments and Methods

Chemicals, Materials, and Bacteria

DAP (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor/Michigan, USA), daptomycin-d5 (DAP-d5) as trifluoroacetic salt (Alsachim, Illkirch-Graffenstaden, France), ATC (Sigma-Aldrich Chemie GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany), anhydrous MH medium, 70% v/v ethanol, and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (all Carl Roth GmbH + Co. KG, Karlsruhe, Germany), calcium chloride (CaCl2), and the LC-MS grade liquids formic acid (FA), acetonitrile (ACN), methanol (MeOH), and water (Sigma-Aldrich GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany) were employed. HPLC-Vials and glass inserts were purchased from Agilent Technologies (Waldbronn, Germany). PALL Acrodisc 32 mm syringe filters with a 0.2 μm Supor membrane and Eppendorf Protein LoBind tubes were purchased from VWR International GmbH (Darmstadt, Germany). The artificial S. aureus strain RN4220_Pxyl/tet-drcAB, carrying the plasmid encoded drcAB genes under control of an ATC-inducible promoter, was used.7,27

Instrumentation and Software

The incubation of the bacteria was carried out in an SM-30 shaker, covered by a TH-30 incubator hood (both Edmund Brühler, Bodelshausen, Germany). The optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was determined with an Ultrospec 3000 pro photometer (Biochrom Ltd., Cambridge, UK). For sample processing, a Vortex-Genie 2 (Scientific 95 Industries SI, Bohemia/New York, USA), a Heraeus multifuge X1R centrifuge, and a Heraeus Fresco 17 centrifuge (both Thermo Scientific, Waltham/Massachusetts, USA) were used. The chromatographic part of the LC-MS/MS measurements was performed using an Agilent Infinity II system (Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany) equipped with a binary pump G7120A, a thermostated multisampler G7167B and a thermostated column compartment G7116B. As stationary phase, a Waters SunFire C18 column (2.1 × 50 mm, 2.5 μm, 100 Å, Waters Corporation, Milford/Massachusetts, USA) was used. This system was coupled to a Sciex X500R qTOF mass spectrometer with a Turbo V Ion Source for electrospray ionization. Automatic calibration of the mass spectrometer was performed using a Sciex ESI Positive Calibration Solution 5600. The acquisition and subsequent evaluation of the LC-MS/HRMS data, including the GUCS approach, were performed using Sciex OS 2.1 software (all Sciex, Concord, Ontario, Canada).

Preparation of Standards, Collection, and Processing of Samples

MH medium was obtained from 21 g of anhydrous MH medium, which was dissolved in 1 L of deionized water and sterilized at 118–121 °C and 0.1 MPa overpressure for 15 min. Stock solutions of DAP in water (5 mg/mL), CaCl2 in MH medium (50 μg/mL), and ATC in PBS (100 μg/mL) were prepared separately. An overnight culture of S. aureus RN4220_Pxyl/tet-drcAB in MH medium (37 °C, 200 rpm, 16–18 h) was equally aliquoted in two parts and subsequently diluted with CaCl2 in MH medium to an OD600 of 0.1. Each aliquot was spiked with a DAP stock solution to a concentration of 16 μg/mL, while one aliquot was further spiked with ATC in PBS to a concentration of 100 ng/mL (GUCS test) and one with the same volume of ATC-free PBS (GUCS reference). After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C and 200 rpm, samples were collected, sterile filtered, and stored at −80 °C. For sample processing, 50 μL of both test and reference samples was separately added into 200 μL of MeOH to precipitate proteins. These suspensions were immediately vortexed for 10 s and centrifuged at 4 °C and 14,000 g for 10 min. 200 μL of the supernatant was transferred into a HPLC-Vial with glass insert to be measured.

Chromatographic and Mass Spectrometric Conditions

The chromatographic system was operated under gradient conditions, employing the mobile phase solvent A consisting of water, ACN, and FA (100:2:0.1, v/v/v) and mobile phase solvent B consisting of ACN, water, and FA (100:2:0.1, v/v/v). The gradient started with 10% B which was held for 2 min, raised to 60% B within 8 min, held for 0.5 min, and in the following 29.5 min, raised to 90% B. Eventually, a reconditioning step was carried out by decreasing the gradient to the initial conditions within 1 min, which was held for 4 min. The injection volume was 10 μL, the flow rate was 0.35 mL/min, and the temperatures of the autosampler and the column compartment were 8 and 35 °C, respectively.

IDA runs were performed in ESI-positive polarity ionization mode, applying ion source parameters as follows: temperature of 300 °C, gas 1 (N2) of 50 psi, gas 2 (N2) of 50 psi, spray voltage of 5,500 V, curtain gas (N2) of 20 psi. The IDA survey scan parameters were set to a Q1 mass resolution of 1 unit, a collision gas (CAD gas; N2) of 7 psi, a declustering potential (DP) spread of 70 ± 10 V, a collision energy (CE) spread of 10 ± 0 V, scanning a mass range from 800 to 5000 m/z during an accumulation time of 0.25 s. IDA criteria were set according to the small-molecule workflow, using a maximum of ten candidate ions and an intensity threshold of 30 counts per second. The background was dynamically subtracted. For the IDA-dependent scan, Q1 resolution, CAD and DP settings were kept, while CE was set to 45 ± 0 V, scanning a mass range from 150 to 4000 m/z during an accumulation time of 0.1 s.

Targeted HRMS scans were then conducted based on the results of the data processing described next, using the m/z of all unique features selected as precursors. The parameters from the IDA-dependent scans were adopted, but the mass scan range was reduced to 100–2000 m/z. The resulting spectra were subsequently used in structural analysis.

Data Processing and Structural Elucidation

For the GUCS approach employing a non-targeted workflow, data obtained from measurements of the test and the reference sample were used. An automated process, comprising a peak finding algorithm with subsequent comparison of the test and reference samples, revealed all unique features that were found in both samples. To avoid including peaks within the noise level, only those features with a minimum area ratio of ten (test/reference) were selected for further analysis of their structural composition.

Based on the targeted HRMS scans, sum and structural formulas were developed by means of the software integrated formula finder and in silico fragmentation tool, the exact mass (mass error <5 ppm), the isotopic distribution ratio, chemical restrictions, and the known fragments of DAP. When all aforementioned conditions were not in conflict for a proposed molecular structure, it was considered probable.
