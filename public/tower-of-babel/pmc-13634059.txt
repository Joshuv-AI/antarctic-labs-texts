ABSTRACT
Background and purposeTotal skin electron beam therapy (TSEBT) is an established radiation treatment modality for the management of cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (CTCL). In recent years, there has been an increasing interest in dose de-escalation strategies aimed at lowering treatment-induced side effects whilst maintaining efficacy. This evolution in clinical practice has highlighted the need for consistent technical standards to ensure treatment quality and comparability across medical institutions. A unified set of recommendations for TSEBT in the modern era was sought by the European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology (ESTRO) Guidelines Sub-Group on Physics, including physical requirements, with the aim of harmonizing clinical practice, promoting homogeneity for clinical trials, and facilitating wider adoption of the technique.Material and methodsThe ESTRO Guidelines Committee developed these recommendations using a Delphi consensus methodology. An expert panel consisting of radiation oncologists and medical physicists with specific expertise in the radiation treatment of primary cutaneous lymphoma was convened. Panel members completed a 14-question survey focusing on key technical aspects of TSEBT.ResultsConsensus was achieved on the principal physical and technical components of TSEBT delivery, including prescribed dose levels, field dimensions, treatment techniques, and QA requirements. Areas of agreement reflect current best practices and address sources of variability that may impact treatment reproducibility.ConclusionsESTRO recommendations provide a standardised framework for the delivery of TSEBT in the management of CTCL, supporting safe implementation and consistency across medical institutions in the context of reduced-dose treatment approaches.

Introduction

Total skin electron beam therapy (TSEBT) is an established radiation therapy modality used in the management of cutaneous T-cell lymphomas (CTCL) and is primarily used in the skin-directed treatment of mycosis fungoides (MF). TSEBT achieves high overall response rates, approaching 100%, with an acceptable, dose-dependent side-effect profile [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]. To minimize the treatment-related side effects of conventional TSEBT doses, low-dose TSEBT has gained increased interest as a skin-directed therapy for patients with MF [8], [9], [10], and has been widely adopted by CTCL specialists in clinical practice. More recently, the value of adjunctive therapies to extend the durability of treatment response from low-dose TSEBT, has become a topical research question [11]. Despite its proven efficacy, the availability and accessibility of TSEBT remain limited [12], resulting in lower-than-expected utilization rates [13]. Furthermore, the lack of international consensus on the optimal therapeutic treatment pathways creates uncertainty for the multidisciplinary teams responsible for treatment decisions and contributes to heterogeneity in clinical practices globally [9], [14], [15], [16]. These challenges to equitable and consistent care are further compounded by the technical complexity of TSEBT and the diversity of delivery techniques that have evolved across institutions.

Published in 2002, consensus guidelines of the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) addressed the clinical indications and technical recommendations for conventional-dose TSEBT [17]. However, as TSEBT techniques have evolved over time, substantial heterogeneity in treatment delivery has emerged, leading to variations in clinical care and complicating the interpretation and application of published clinical data. More recent EORTC radiotherapy guidelines now recommend 8–12 Gy for low-dose TSEBT, although higher doses may still have utility and are favoured in the setting of allogeneic stem cell transplantation for maximal debulking of skin prior to transplant [8]. Given these recent changes in practice, the ESTRO Guidelines Committee sought to establish clear and practical recommendations for the delivery of low-dose TSEBT in patients with MF. The aims of these recommendations are to improve consistency in clinical practice, promote homogeneity in clinical research, and facilitate wider and safer implementation of TSEBT across radiation oncology centres. Particular emphasis was placed on providing clear explanations of the underlying physical principles and quality assurance (QA) requirements, which are essential for the safe implementation and reproducible delivery of TSEBT. These consensus recommendations cover key aspects of TSEBT delivery, including prescription dose levels, treatment techniques, and the management and shielding of organs at risk. The recommendations are based on the collective experience and established practices of ESTRO members with expertise in the delivery of TSEBT.

Materials and methods

Between October and November 2024, a scientific panel consisting of seven experts in radiation oncology (five physicians and two physicists) with specific expertise in primary cutaneous lymphoma developed a dedicated questionnaire. From December 2024 to May 2025, the questionnaire was distributed in three rounds to 16 radiation oncology experts affiliated with ESTRO (Table 1), following the Delphi methodology (Fig. 1). This carefully designed tool comprised 14 multiple-choice questions addressing key technical and quality-related issues in the field (Table 2). The survey was administered electronically. The expert panel included nine male and seven female participants, all working in academic institutions. Participants reported a median of 20 patients treated per year with TSEBT (range: 10–75). Consensus was defined as agreement by more than 80% of respondents, and statements meeting this threshold were removed from subsequent Delphi rounds. A response rate between 66% and 80% was considered indicative of a strong level of agreement. Statements that lacked consensus were revised and re-presented in the next round. As no patient data were involved, ethical committee approval was not required.

Results

During the first Delphi round, consensus was reached for 7 out of 14 statements (50%). In the second round, we reached consensus on six more statements. In the third round, we agreed on the final statement.

The consensus statements are listed as follows.3.1.Electrons should be used for total skin irradiation, and the electron energies must be specified based on the depth-dose characteristics.

No consensus was reached in the first round mainly due to disagreements regarding explicit specifications of electron beam energy ranges. In the first round, 94% of the panellists agreed on the need to use either a scatterer-degrader plate or a specialised flattening filter during TSEBT. The plate is introduced to improve dose uniformity by scattering the electrons and to increase the skin dose by degrading the electron beam energy. All panellists agreed on the importance of comprehensive quality-assurance procedures to ensure accurate and reliable radiation dose delivery (100% agreement). Occasional patient-specific dosimetric verifications can serve as helpful spot checks, and an end-to-end test can be useful in checking all the steps involved in the TSEBT workflow. Previously published guidelines from the American Association of Physicists in Medicine Task Group #30 and Medical Physics Practice Guidelines 8 provide commissioning and quality assurance recommendations [35], [36].

Discussion

Skin infiltration by MF is typically highly morbid and can be challenging to treat. For these patients, TSEBT has proven efficacy and positive effects on quality of life [17], [35], [37]. To improve consistency in clinical practice and research, we provide updated guidance on the physical principles and quality-assurance requirements for the safe and reproducible delivery of TSEBT.

The daily radiation dose for each treated area should be tailored to the specific clinical situation of the patient. All treatment fields can be delivered in a single scheduled session; however, depending on the patient's condition and available resources at the institution, it may be necessary to spread the radiation fraction over two days. In a literature review, twelve studies indicated that all radiation fields were treated on the same day, while ten studies employed the Stanford technique and split the treatment into two days [7].

If the initial low-dose TSEBT is insufficient, higher doses of 20 Gy or 30 Gy may be administered. This decision will depend on the patient's cumulative radiation exposure and the availability of other systemic or skin-directed treatments. The EORTC Cutaneous Lymphoma Project Group [17] recommends a cumulative TSEBT dose of 44 Gy. Based on panellists' experience, doses up to 60 Gy can be safely administered, using an equivalent dose in 2 Gy fractions (EQ.D2), with an α/β ratio of 10 Gy. Radiation oncologists need to determine the dosage and timing for re-irradiation based on each patient's individual factors. These factors include the patient's response to the initial dose, the duration of clinical benefit, and the risks associated with cumulative adverse events.

In addition to these technical considerations, the panel also addressed key physics and quality assurance aspects that are essential for the safe and reproducible delivery of TSEBT. In the first round of discussions, panellists reached strong consensus on four physics-related statements. First, a scatter and attenuation plate or a special flattening filter should be used to achieve the desired dose uniformity and skin dose. Second, the optimal treatment distance between the gantry and the patient should be evaluated through precise measurements. Third, in vivo dosimetry should be performed for a selected group of patients when initiating a TSEBT program; once delivered doses are confirmed to be within the required range, the frequency of measurements may be reduced. Fourth, comprehensive quality assurance is recommended, including equipment calibration, patient-specific dosimetric verification, and end-to-end testing or auditor certification. In the second round, consensus was achieved on a fifth statement regarding surface dose, with 94% agreement that the acceptable surface dose for the lower back and umbilical region should range from 80% to 120% of the prescribed dose, although no specific dose rate was defined, allowing institutional flexibility.

The current implementation of the European Medical Device Regulation (MDR) has implications for the development and use of in-house radiotherapy treatment accessories. In the context of TSEBT, locally developed solutions, such as scattering foil/flattening filter, patient rotation systems, and customised shielding devices, may be used to improve dose homogeneity, organ protection, and overall treatment quality. However, the associated regulatory burden of certification may limit innovation and availability, particularly for low-volume and highly specialised applications. In this context, the role of the Medical Physics expert is pivotal in ensuring patient safety and technical appropriateness. Proportionate regulatory pathways, including collaborative development between clinical centres and manufacturers, could strike a balance between innovation and regulatory compliance while maintaining high safety standards.

These ESTRO recommendations provide a standard reference for TSEBT delivery in radiation oncology. While consensus was reached on all technical and physical aspects, individualised solutions may still be necessary depending on local resources and patient-specific circumstances.
