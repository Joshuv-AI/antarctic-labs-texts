From Theory to Application: A Practical Introduction to Neural Operators in Scientific Computing
Prashant K. Jha 1 Published in Mathematics ; DOI: https://doi.org/10.3390/math14132421

Abstract
This review examines neural operator architectures for learning solution operators of parametric partial differential equations (PDEs), with an emphasis on conceptual clarity and practical implementation. The work analyzes key models, including DeepONet, PCANet, and the Fourier Neural Operator, highlighting their underlying representations, computational structures, and comparative performance. These architectures are demonstrated on three canonical PDE problems: the Poisson equation, a linear elasticity problem, and a hyperelasticity problem. To make the presentation self-contained, key foundational topics are introduced, including finite-dimensional representations of function spaces, singular-value decomposition, and sampling from infinite-dimensional function spaces. Beyond forward modeling, the review discusses the use of neural operators as surrogate models within a Bayesian inverse-problem framework, including prior specification, forward-map approximation, and posterior computation. The performance of the three neural-operator architectures is evaluated on in-distribution samples, out-of-distribution samples, and Bayesian inference tasks. The review also discusses challenges related to prediction accuracy and generalization, outlining emerging strategies such as residual-based error correction and multi-level training. The review concludes by positioning neural operators within broader scientific-computing workflows and by identifying directions for reliable, scalable operator learning.

Introduction

Neural operators have emerged as powerful tools for approximating solution operators of parametric partial differential equations (PDEs). Their key advantage lies in learning mappings between infinite-dimensional function spaces, enabling efficient surrogate modeling for complex systems. In addition, their ability to provide rapid evaluations makes them particularly attractive for applications such as optimization, control, and inverse problems.

This article provides a practical and hands-on introduction to several neural operator architectures that have become central to scientific computing. The following neural operators are considered:

1.

Deep Operator Network (DeepONet)
Wang et al. (2021)
;
Lu et al. (2021)
;

2.

Principal Component Analysis/Proper Orthogonal Decomposition-based Neural Operator (PCANet/PODNet)
Bhattacharya et al. (2021)
;
Fresca and Manzoni (2022)
;

3.

Fourier Neural Operator (FNO)
Li et al. (2020)
;
Kovachki et al. (2023)
.

The presentation is designed to be self-contained and implementation-focused. Neural operator architectures, variants, and applications have been surveyed from broader taxonomic and benchmarking perspectives in several recent review articles
Kovachki et al. (2023)
;
Azizzadenesheli et al. (2024)
;
De Hoop et al. (2022)
. In contrast, the present article focuses on conceptual understanding, practical implementation of representative neural operator architectures, and the application of neural operator surrogates to Bayesian inverse problems, where these surrogates are used to accelerate repeated forward evaluations in MCMC simulations. To support this objective, several key topics are addressed:

•

Sampling random functions using Gaussian measures on function spaces;

•

Construction of data representations tailored to different neural operator architectures;

•

Algorithms and Python implementations for essential components, including random function sampling and Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods for Bayesian inverse problems;

•

Application of neural operators as surrogate models in Bayesian inverse problems.

To illustrate these ideas, two classical linear parametric PDEs and one nonlinear parametric PDE are used as model problems. The first is the Poisson equation for temperature distribution on a rectangular domain, where the input parameter field is the diffusivity. The second is a two-dimensional linear elasticity problem, where the input parameter field is Young’s modulus. The third is a hyperelasticity problem that serves as a nonlinear counterpart of the second problem. For these problems, neural operators are constructed to approximate the solution operator, and their accuracy is assessed on random samples of parameter fields.

In addition, neural operators are employed as surrogate models in Bayesian inverse problems, where the underlying parameter fields are inferred from observational data. The resulting posterior estimates are compared against those obtained using the true forward model, demonstrating that neural operators can provide accurate and computationally efficient approximations in this setting. The model problems considered here exhibit rapid decay of singular values in both input and output representations, indicating a low-dimensional structure that is favorable for operator learning. This is reflected in the high predictive accuracy of the neural operators when test samples are drawn from the same distribution as the training data and when the prior for Bayesian inference is well aligned with the training distribution. However, when applying the trained neural operators to out-of-distribution samples, such as those with higher frequencies or larger magnitudes than the training data, significant errors can arise. This is shown through performance assessments on out-of-distribution samples, where the neural operators exhibit large prediction errors.

For complex settings, such as highly nonlinear PDEs or challenging inference and optimization problems, neural operators may exhibit significant errors. This limitation motivates the need for improved strategies for controlling prediction accuracy. These issues are discussed in the concluding section, where existing approaches for error control are surveyed. The discussion also highlights emerging directions, including foundation models for PDEs that aim to construct surrogate operators capable of generalizing across families of PDEs, as well as related frameworks such as multi-task and universal neural operators.

2.2
Series representation of functions and finite-dimensional approximation

One of the central ideas that many neural operators leverage is the finite-dimensional representation of functions in terms of coefficients and basis functions in their respective spaces. Following the notations in the previous section and
Table

1
, suppose that
M
and
U
are Hilbert spaces and, therefore, have orthonormal bases
\phi^{M}=\{\phi^{M}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subset M
and
\phi^{U}=\{\phi^{U}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subset U
. Then a function
m\in M
can be represented as

m(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{m}_{i}\phi^{M}_{i}(x)\,,\qquad\forall x\in D_{M}\,,

(1)

where
\mathrm{m}_{i}=\left({m},{\phi^{M}_{i}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}
(inner product between
m
and
\phi^{M}_{i}
) are the coefficients or degrees of freedom associated with the
{i}^{\text{th}}
mode. Note that for each
i
,
\phi^{M}_{i}(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{M}}
for
x\in D_{M}
, and
\phi^{U}_{i}(y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{U}}
for
y\in D_{U}
.

It is useful to consider the example where the domain is the unit interval
D_{M}=(0,1)
and
m\in L^{2}(D_{M};\mathbb{R})
. In this case, one can write
m(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{m}_{i}\phi^{M}_{i}(x)
, where the basis functions take the form

\begin{split}&\phi^{M}_{1}=1\,,\quad\phi^{M}_{2}=\sqrt{2}\cos\left(2\pi x\right)\,,\quad\phi^{M}_{3}=\sqrt{2}\sin\left(2\pi x\right)\,,\cdots,\\
&\qquad\phi^{M}_{2j}=\sqrt{2}\cos\left(2j\pi x\right)\,,\quad\phi^{M}_{2j+1}=\sqrt{2}\sin\left(2j\pi x\right)\,,\cdots\end{split}

(2)

and the coefficients are given by

\mathrm{m}_{i}=\left({m},{\phi^{M}_{i}}\right)=\int_{0}^{1}m(x)\phi^{M}_{i}(x)\,\textup{d}x\,,\qquad\forall i\,.

(3)

Focusing on the abstract setting, let
\phi^{M}=\{\phi^{M}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p_{M}}
, where
p_{M}
is a finite integer and
\phi^{M}
is a finite collection of basis functions, and let
\{\mathrm{m}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p_{M}}
be the corresponding coefficients. Then, the finite-dimensional approximation is
\sum_{i=1}^{p_{M}}\mathrm{m}_{i}\phi^{M}_{i}(x)\,\,\approx m(x)
with the error given by
\left\lVert m-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p_{M}}\mathrm{m}_{i}\phi^{M}_{i}\right)\right\rVert
. Similarly, the function
u\in U
can be approximated as
u(y)\approx\sum_{i=1}^{p_{U}}\mathrm{u}_{i}\phi^{U}_{i}(y)
,
y\in D_{U}
. Neural operators often leverage this finite-dimensional representation viewpoint, where the goal is to determine, or learn, the basis functions
\phi^{U}=\{\phi^{U}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p_{U}}
(or their pointwise values
\{\phi^{U}_{i}(y)\}_{i=1}^{p_{U}}
for
y\in D_{U}
[e.g., in DeepONet]) and the coefficients
\mathrm{u}=\{\mathrm{u}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p_{U}}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{p_{U}}\mathrm{u}_{i}\phi^{U}_{i}
provides the “best” approximation of
u=F(m)
, where
m\in M
is the input function to the operator.

Remark 1
.

PCANet explicitly constructs reduced-dimensional representations of the input and output functions via singular value decomposition and learns the map between them (see
Section

2.3
). FNO follows a different construction, utilizing Fourier transforms to represent functions in the frequency domain and learning mappings through spectral convolutions applied to the Fourier modes.

2.2.1
Finite element approximation

For a general class of function spaces
M\subseteq\{m:D_{M}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{q_{M}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{M}}\}
and spatial domain
D_{M}
, the preceding construction of finite-dimensional approximations can be restrictive. A more straightforward numerical way to obtain finite-dimensional approximations is to use numerical techniques such as finite difference and finite element methods. In this work, the finite element method is used (e.g., to generate samples of input functions using Gaussian priors and solve PDE-based problems).

To be more precise, consider a finite element discretization
D_{M_{h}}
of the domain
D_{M}
consisting of simplex elements
\{T_{e}\}_{e=1}^{N_{e}}
, so that
D_{M_{h}}=\cup_{e}\bar{T}_{e}\approx D_{M}
. Suppose
\phi^{M}_{i}:D_{M_{h}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{M}}
denotes the basis function associated with the
i^{\text{th}}
scalar degree of freedom and
\mathrm{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}
the vector of coefficients associated with the basis functions
\{\phi^{M}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p_{M}}
. Then, the finite-dimensional approximation of
m
is given by
m_{h}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{p_{M}}\mathrm{m}_{i}\phi^{M}_{i}(x)
for
x\in D_{M_{h}}
. The same procedure can be used to construct the finite-dimensional approximation of
u
using the basis functions
\{\phi^{U}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p_{U}}
and coefficients
\mathrm{u}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{U}}
.

Remark 2
.

When
m
is vector-valued, that is,
m(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{M}}
with
d_{M}>1
, the finite element approximation must represent each component of
m
independently while using a common spatial discretization. This leads naturally to vector-valued basis functions.

Let
\{\psi_{j}:D_{M_{h}}\to\mathbb{R}\}_{j=1}^{n}
denote the standard scalar Lagrange basis functions associated with the
n
mesh vertices. Since each vertex carries
d_{M}
degrees of freedom, the total number of degrees of freedom is
p_{M}=n\,d_{M}
.

The vector-valued basis functions are constructed by combining the scalar spatial basis with the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d_{M}}
. Specifically, for each degree of freedom index
i
with
1\leq i\leq p_{M}
, there exist unique indices
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
k\in\{1,\dots,d_{M}\}
such that

i=(j-1)d_{M}+k,

where
j
identifies the vertex in the mesh and
k
identifies the component. The corresponding basis function associated with this degree of freedom is defined as

\phi^{M}_{i}(x)=\psi_{j}(x)\,\mathbf{e}_{k},\quad x\in D_{M_{h}},

where
\mathbf{e}_{k}
is the
k^{\text{th}}
canonical basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{M}}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{e}_{k}=(0,\ldots,1,\ldots,0)^{\top}
with the
1
in the
{k}^{\text{th}}
position.

Let
V_{M_{h}}=\mathrm{span}\{\phi^{M}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p_{M}}
, where
p_{M}
is the number of degrees of freedom. Then, the function
m\in M
can be approximated by a function
m_{h}\in V_{M_{h}}
given by

m(x)\qquad\approx\qquad m_{h}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{p_{M}}\mathrm{m}_{i}\phi^{M}_{i}(x)\,,\qquad\forall x\in D_{M_{h}}\,,

(4)

provided the coefficients
\mathrm{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}
are chosen appropriately. For example,
\mathrm{m}
is selected such that it minimizes the
L^{2}
-error
\left\lVert m-m_{h}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}
.

Given finite-dimensional approximations
m_{h}
and
u_{h}
of
m
and
u
, respectively, the map
F(m)=u
is also approximated by
F_{h}(m_{h})=u_{h}
in the sense that
F_{h}
takes
m_{h}
and returns the output
u_{h}
such that the operator error

\left\lVert F(m)-F_{h}(m_{h})\right\rVert

is small for the relevant class of inputs
m
.

Finally, note that, for a fixed mesh and the basis functions
\phi^{M}_{i}
, it is easy to see that if
\mathrm{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}
is fixed, then the function
m_{h}
is completely characterized. If
m_{h}
is fixed, using the unique representation of
m_{h}
, the coefficients
\mathrm{m}
are completely characterized. Thus,
V_{M_{h}}
can be identified using
{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}
(and vice versa). This makes it possible to represent the finite-dimensional function space
V_{M_{h}}
by the Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}
of the coefficients. Throughout the article, functions
m
and
u
will be represented by the finite-dimensional approximations
\mathrm{m}
and
\mathrm{u}
, where the corresponding functional representations,
m_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{p_{M}}\mathrm{m}_{i}\phi^{M}_{i}
and
u_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{p_{U}}\mathrm{u}_{i}\phi^{U}_{i}
, are assumed implicitly. In the same spirit, using
u_{h}=F_{h}(m_{h})
, the operator
F_{h}
can be identified with a map
{\mathrm{F}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{U}}
:

{\mathrm{F}}(\mathrm{m})=\mathrm{u}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad F_{h}(m_{h})=u_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{p_{U}}\mathrm{u}_{i}\phi^{U}_{i}\quad\text{with}\quad m_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{p_{M}}\mathrm{m}_{i}\phi^{M}_{i}\,.

(5)

Here,
{\mathrm{F}}
maps the coefficient vector
\mathrm{m}
to
\mathrm{u}
and is induced by the map
F_{h}
.

2.3
Dimensional reduction and singular-value decomposition (SVD)

While the theory is based on functions defined on a continuum domain, computer implementations introduce discretizations of the domain and, consequently, discrete approximations of functions. For example, the training data for neural operators is typically a collection of pairs
(\mathrm{m}^{I},\mathrm{u}^{I})
, where
\mathrm{m}^{I}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}
and
\mathrm{u}^{I}={\mathrm{F}}(\mathrm{m}^{I})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{U}}
are discrete approximations of functions in
M
and
U
, where
{\mathrm{F}}
is the finite-dimensional mapping between
{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{U}}
that approximates the operator of interest
F
. Generally speaking, the dimensions of the discretized input and target functions,
p_{M}
and
p_{U}
, are large, and the problem of approximating the map
{\mathrm{F}}
between high-dimensional spaces becomes challenging and potentially ill-posed.

The second key idea used in various neural operators, after the linear basis representation discussed earlier, is to reduce the dimensions of discretized input and output functions; see
Bhattacharya et al. (2021)
. If
\mathrm{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}
and
\mathrm{u}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{U}}
, and the goal is to determine a map
\mathrm{m}\mapsto\mathrm{u}={\mathrm{F}}(\mathrm{m})
from the data
\{(\mathrm{m}^{I},\mathrm{u}^{I})\}_{I=1}^{N}
, then, as an alternative to learning or approximating the map
{\mathrm{F}}
, one could attempt to characterize the map
\tilde{{\mathrm{F}}}
, where

\mathrm{m}\qquad\mapsto\qquad\mathrm{u}=\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{U}^{T}\left(\tilde{{\mathrm{F}}}\left(\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{M}(\mathrm{m})\right)\right)\,.

(6)

Here,
\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{M}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{M}\times p_{M}}
is the projection operator that projects
\mathrm{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}
into a low-dimensional subspace,
\tilde{\mathrm{m}}:=\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{M}(\mathrm{m})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{M}}
(with
r_{M}\ll p_{M}
).
\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{U}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{U}\times p_{U}}
has the same role as
\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{M}
but for target functions
\mathrm{u}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{U}}
. The transpose of
\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{U}
,
\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{U}^{T}
, projects an element in
{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{U}}
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{U}}
. Note that
\tilde{{\mathrm{F}}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{M}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{U}}
, which needs to be learned, is the mapping between two low-dimensional spaces, and hence identifying
\tilde{{\mathrm{F}}}
is less daunting than identifying
{\mathrm{F}}
. In summary, using
\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{M}
and
\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{U}
, the dimensions of the operator inference problem are significantly reduced, and, by controlling
r_{M}
and
r_{U}
, one can balance the trade-off between accuracy and computational cost.

2.3.1
Projectors via SVD

The projectors
\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{M}
and
\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{U}
used for dimensional reduction can be obtained via singular-value decomposition (SVD). Focusing on the input space
{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}
, let
{\mathrm{R}}
denote a
p_{M}\times N
matrix such that, for
1\leq I\leq N
,
\mathrm{m}^{I}
makes up the
{I}^{\text{th}}
column of the matrix
{\mathrm{R}}
.
Here,
N
may be smaller or larger than
p_{M}
, and since the samples may be linearly dependent, the rank of
{\mathrm{R}}
need not be equal to
\min\{p_{M},N\}
. Let

r=\mathrm{rank}({\mathrm{R}})\leq\min\{p_{M},N\}\,.

(7)

Consider the compact SVD

{\mathrm{R}}={\mathrm{U}}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathrm{V}}^{T}\,,

(8)

where
{\mathrm{U}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}\times r}
and
{\mathrm{V}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times r}
have orthonormal columns, and
{\mathrm{D}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times r}
is diagonal with positive singular values. Denote the columns of
{\mathrm{U}}
by
\{\mathrm{w}^{I}\}_{I=1}^{r}
, i.e.,

{\mathrm{U}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mid&\mid&&\mid\\
\mathrm{w}^{1}&\mathrm{w}^{2}&\cdots&\mathrm{w}^{r}\\
\mid&\mid&&\mid\end{bmatrix}.

(9)

The vectors
\{\mathrm{w}^{I}\}_{I=1}^{r}
form an orthonormal basis for the column space of
{\mathrm{R}}
, which is an
r
-dimensional subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}
.

Let
r_{M}>0
be the prescribed reduced dimension, with
r_{M}\leq r
. Given
r_{M}
, define
{\mathrm{U}}_{r_{M}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}\times r_{M}}
by retaining the first
r_{M}
columns of
{\mathrm{U}}
:

{\mathrm{U}}_{r_{M}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mid&\mid&&\mid\\
\mathrm{w}^{1}&\mathrm{w}^{2}&\cdots&\mathrm{w}^{r_{M}}\\
\mid&\mid&&\mid\end{bmatrix}.

(10)

Noting the properties of
{\mathrm{U}}_{r_{M}}
(e.g., see [
Jha, 2024
](Section 3.2.1)),
{\mathrm{U}}^{T}_{r_{M}}
is taken as the projector, i.e.,

\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{M}:={\mathrm{U}}^{T}_{r_{M}}.

(11)

Remark 3
.

The rank of
{\mathrm{R}}
depends on the linear independence of the sampled coefficient vectors
\{\mathrm{m}^{I}\}_{I=1}^{N}
. If these vectors are linearly dependent, then
\mathrm{rank}({\mathrm{R}})<\min\{p_{M},N\}
. In contrast, if the samples are drawn independently from a distribution on
{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}
that is not supported on a proper linear subspace, then
\mathrm{rank}({\mathrm{R}})=\min\{p_{M},N\}
. Numerically, the effective rank is determined by the decay of the singular values and the tolerance used to distinguish nonzero singular values.

For
\mathrm{u}
, the projector
\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{U}
is obtained following the same procedure as above using a matrix
{\mathrm{R}}
of size
p_{U}\times N
with
\mathrm{u}^{I}
making its
I^{\text{th}}
column. The left singular-vector matrix from the SVD of
{\mathrm{R}}
, denoted by
{\mathrm{U}}
, is truncated by retaining the first
r_{U}
columns of
{\mathrm{U}}
. If the truncated matrix is
{\mathrm{U}}_{r_{U}}
, then
\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{U}:={\mathrm{U}}^{T}_{r_{U}}
.

2.4
Sampling functions as infinite-dimensional random variables

The final topic in this section is the sampling of random parameters, which are functions. Consider a probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbb{P}})
, where
\Omega
is a sample space and
{\mathcal{F}}
is a
\sigma
-algebra on which the probability measure
{\mathbb{P}}
is defined with
{\mathbb{P}}(\Omega)=1
. The goal is to draw
W
-valued random fields, where
W
is assumed to be a separable Hilbert space. Equivalently, the goal is to define a random field
{\mathscr{Z}}:\Omega\to W
such that, for a given
z\in\Omega
,
w={\mathscr{Z}}(z)\in W
. Suppose such a
{\mathscr{Z}}
is available; then, the probability that
{\mathscr{Z}}
takes values in a subset
A\subseteq W
defines the pushforward measure
\mu_{{\mathscr{Z}}}
, given by

\mu_{{\mathscr{Z}}}(A)=\text{probability of }{\mathscr{Z}}\in A={\mathbb{P}}\left(\{z\in\Omega:{\mathscr{Z}}(z)\in A\}\right)={\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathscr{Z}}^{-1}(A)\right)\,,

(12)

where
{\mathscr{Z}}^{-1}(A)\in{\mathcal{F}}
is assumed to be measurable in the probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbb{P}})
. Thus,
\mu_{{\mathscr{Z}}}
is a measure on
W
induced by the random field
{\mathscr{Z}}
. Now, suppose that a random field
{\mathscr{Z}}
is such that the measure
\mu_{\mathscr{Z}}
is Gaussian in the sense of
Dashti and Stuart (2017)
;
Mandel (2023)
; specifically, see
(
Dashti and Stuart, 2017
, Definition 6 and Lemma 23)
. In this case,
\mu_{\mathscr{Z}}
is written as
\mu_{\mathscr{Z}}=N(\bar{w},C)
. Here,
\bar{w}\in W
is the mean function, and
C:W\to W
is called the covariance operator. At the outset,
C
is assumed to be a trace-class operator ([
Dashti and Stuart, 2017
, Lemma 23
] and [
Mandel, 2023
, Theorem 7
]).

Our next goal is to consider specific examples of
{\mathscr{Z}}
such that
\mu_{\mathscr{Z}}
is Gaussian, as mentioned above, and understand how random function samples are generated. In this direction, it is useful to highlight the role of
C
in generating samples
w={\mathscr{Z}}(z)\in W
; this helps explain why commonly used forms of
C
are effective. Consider another random field
{\mathscr{S}}:\Omega\to W
such that
\mu_{\mathscr{S}}=N(0,1)
, where
0\in W
is the mean function and
1:W\to W
is the identity covariance operator. Given a function
\bar{w}\in W
and a covariance operator
C:W\to W
, let
{\mathscr{Z}}
be such that
\mu_{\mathscr{Z}}=N(\bar{w},C)
. Then,
a sample
w={\mathscr{Z}}(z)
can be obtained by transforming the sample
s={\mathscr{S}}(z)
as follows:

{\mathscr{Z}}(z)=w:=\bar{w}+C^{1/2}s\,.

(13)

Thus,
C^{1/2}
, the square root of the covariance operator, plays a key role in generating random functions. It is therefore useful to choose
C
so that
C^{1/2}
is computationally tractable while ensuring that the
W
-valued samples are well defined, have the desired regularity, and exhibit controlled correlation between pointwise values.

2.4.1
Gaussian measures based on Laplacian-like operators

Following
Bui-Thanh et al. (2013)
, let
C=L_{\Delta}^{-2}
, where
L_{\Delta}:W_{L_{\Delta}}\subseteq W\to W
is a Laplacian-like operator given by

L_{\Delta}:=\begin{cases}-\mathsf{a}_{c}\nabla\cdot\mathsf{b}_{c}\nabla+\mathsf{c}_{c}\,,\qquad\qquad&\text{in }D_{W}\,,\\
\mathsf{a}_{c}n\cdot\mathsf{b}_{c}\nabla\,,\qquad\qquad&\text{on }\partial D_{W}\,.\end{cases}

(14)

Here,
D_{W}
is the domain of functions
w
, and
\mathsf{a}_{c},\mathsf{b}_{c},\mathsf{c}_{c}
are parameters in the operator (they could vary over the domain or be taken as constants). In this work,
\mathsf{a}_{c}
and
\mathsf{c}_{c}
will be constant, and in some situations,
\mathsf{b}_{c}
will be considered to be a spatially varying scalar-valued
L^{2}(D_{W})
function. In the literature, it is also common to take
\mathsf{b}_{c}
as
{\mathbb{R}}^{q_{W}\times d_{W}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{q_{W}\times d_{W}}
-valued function allowing one to encode anisotropy and inhomogeneous behavior in the prior. In the above,
W_{L_{\Delta}}\subseteq W
is the domain of the operator
L_{\Delta}
such that
L_{\Delta}(w)
is well-defined for all
w\in W_{L_{\Delta}}
. The natural choice is
W_{L_{\Delta}}=\{w\in W:\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{H^{2}}<\infty\}
. If
L_{\Delta}
is defined through the bilinear form

\left({v},{L_{\Delta}w}\right)=\int_{D_{W}}\left[\mathsf{a}_{c}\mathsf{b}_{c}\nabla w\cdot\nabla v+\mathsf{c}_{c}wv\right]\,\textup{d}x\,,

(15)

then, the operator domain can be taken as

\quad W_{L_{\Delta}}=W\cap H^{1}(D_{W};{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{W}})\,,

(16)

and take
v,w\in W_{L_{\Delta}}
.
In the bilinear form above, note that the boundary term in (
14
) cancels out the boundary term after integration by parts.

With this specific form of
C
, the sampling of functions
w\in W
can be summarized as follows:

Sampling a Gaussian random field with covariance operator
C=L_{\Delta}^{-2}

1.

Fix
\bar{w}\in W
,
C=L_{\Delta}^{-2}
, and let
{\mathscr{S}}:\Omega\to W
be a random field such that
\mu_{\mathscr{S}}=N(0,1)
.

2.

Draw a sample
s={\mathscr{S}}(z)
,
z\in\Omega
.

3.

Generate a new sample
w\sim N(\bar{w},C)
as follows:

\displaystyle w=\bar{w}+C^{1/2}(s)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;

\displaystyle w=\bar{w}+L_{\Delta}^{-1}(s)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;

\displaystyle w=\bar{w}+v\,,\text{ where }v\text{ satisfies }s=L_{\Delta}(v)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;

\displaystyle w=\bar{w}+v\,,\text{ where }v\text{ satisfies }\left({g},{s}\right)=\left({g},{L_{\Delta}(v)}\right)\,,\quad\forall g\in W_{L_{\Delta}}\,,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;

\displaystyle w=\bar{w}+v\,,\text{ where }v\text{ satisfies }

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\int_{D_{W}}sg\,\textup{d}x=\int_{D_{W}}\left[\mathsf{a}_{c}\mathsf{b}_{c}\nabla v\cdot\nabla g+\mathsf{c}_{c}vg\right]\,\textup{d}x\,,\quad\forall g\in W_{L_{\Delta}}\,,

(17)

where the last two equations express the weak-form definition of
L_{\Delta}
that can be solved using the finite element method.

In terms of the numerical implementation, consider a finite element mesh
D_{W_{h}}
and a finite element function space
V_{h}\subset W
with basis functions
\phi^{W}=\{\phi^{W}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p_{W}}
. Let the mean function
\bar{w}\in W
be projected onto
V_{h}
, with projection given by
\bar{w}_{h}=\sum_{i}\bar{\mathrm{w}}_{h_{i}}\phi^{W}_{i}
. The procedure to sample
w_{h}\sim N(\bar{w}_{h},C)
is as follows:

Sampling a Gaussian random field with
C=L_{\Delta}^{-2}
in a finite element setting

1.

To draw a sample
s={\mathscr{S}}(z)\in V_{h}
, consider the following steps:

(a)

For each
i
,
1\leq i\leq p_{W}
, draw a number
\mathrm{s}_{i}\sim{\mathrm{N}}(0,1)
, where
{\mathrm{N}}(0,1)
is the standard normal distribution on
\mathbb{R}
; and

(b)

Take
s=\sum_{i}\mathrm{s}_{i}\phi^{W}_{i}
, i.e.,
s
is a finite element function with coefficients
\mathrm{s}
.

2.

Solve for
v_{h}\in V_{h}
such that

\int_{D_{W_{h}}}sg\,\,\textup{d}x=\int_{D_{W_{h}}}\left[\mathsf{a}_{c}\mathsf{b}_{c}\nabla v_{h}\cdot\nabla g+\mathsf{c}_{c}v_{h}g\right]\,\,\textup{d}x,\qquad\forall g\in V_{h}.

(18)

This yields coefficients
\mathrm{v}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{W}}
and the finite element function
v_{h}=\sum_{i}\mathrm{v}_{h_{i}}\phi_{i}^{W}
.

3.

A new sample
w_{h}\sim N(\bar{w}_{h},C)
is given by

w_{h}=\sum_{i}\mathrm{w}_{h_{i}}\phi^{W}_{i}=\bar{w}_{h}+v_{h},\qquad\text{where}\qquad\mathrm{w}_{h_{i}}=\bar{\mathrm{w}}_{h_{i}}+\mathrm{v}_{h_{i}}.

(19)

Remark 4
.

Spectral methods, including Karhunen–Loève expansions, can also be used to sample Gaussian random fields with covariance operators defined through Laplacian-like operators. These methods are often efficient on simple domains with compatible boundary conditions, especially when the eigenpairs of the covariance operator or the associated differential operator are available. The finite element construction is used here because it is straightforward and directly compatible with the PDE discretizations used later for data generation and Bayesian inference. It also extends naturally to general geometries and boundary conditions, unstructured and higher-order meshes, spatially varying coefficients such as
\mathsf{b}_{c}
, and vector-valued finite element spaces.

\displaystyle u(x)

3.2
Linear elasticity problem

The second problem concerns deformation of a two-dimensional rectangular domain
D_{U}=(0,L_{1})\times(0,L_{2})\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. The deformation is governed by the linear elasticity equations. Suppose
m(x)
denotes the Young’s modulus field at a point
x\in D_{U}
,
u=(u_{1},u_{2}):D_{U}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
denotes the displacement field,
e(x)=(\nabla u+\nabla u^{T})/2
denotes the linearized strain,
\sigma(x)
denotes the Cauchy stress, and
b(x)
denotes the body force per unit volume. The equation for
u
is based on the balance of linear momentum and reads:

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\sigma(x)

\displaystyle=b(x)\,,\qquad

\displaystyle\forall x\in D_{U}\,,

(26)

\displaystyle\sigma(x)

\displaystyle=\lambda(x)\mathrm{tr}(e){\bm{\mathrm{I}}}+2\mu(x)e\,,\qquad

\displaystyle\forall x\in D_{U}\,,

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle=0\,,\qquad

\displaystyle\forall x\in\Gamma_{u_{d}}\,,

\displaystyle\sigma(x)n(x)

\displaystyle=t(x)\,,\qquad

\displaystyle\forall x\in\Gamma_{u_{q}}=\partial D_{U}-\Gamma_{u_{d}}\,,

where
\mathrm{tr}(e)=e_{11}+e_{22}
is the trace of the strain tensor,
{\bm{\mathrm{I}}}
is the identity second order tensor in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, and
\lambda
and
\mu
are Lamé parameters related to the Young’s modulus
m
and the Poisson ratio
\nu
as follows:

\lambda(x)=\frac{m(x)\nu}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu(x)=\frac{m(x)}{2(1+\nu)}\,.

(27)

In (
26
),
\Gamma_{u_{d}}:=\left\{x\in\partial D_{U}:x_{1}=0\right\}
and
\Gamma_{u_{q}}:=\partial D_{U}-\Gamma_{u_{d}}
,
n
is the unit outward normal, and
t
is the specified traction. The scalar field
m\in M
is considered to be uncertain, and the forward map
F:M\to U
is defined such that, given
m\in M
, the function
u=F(m)\in U
solves the BVP (
26
). To be consistent with the notations, here
D_{W}=D_{M}=D_{U}
,
q_{W}=q_{M}=q_{U}=2
,
d_{W}=d_{M}=1
, and
d_{U}=2
. Working in the variational setting, appropriate function spaces for the parameter field and solution are as follows:

M:=\left\{m:D_{M}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}:\left\lVert m\right\rVert_{L^{2}}<\infty\right\}\,,\;U:=\left\{u\in H^{1}(D_{U};{\mathbb{R}}^{2}):u=0\;\text{ on }\Gamma_{u_{d}}\right\}\,.

(28)

The weak form of the problem is: given
m\in M
, find
u\in U
such that

\int_{D_{U}}\sigma:\frac{(\nabla v+\nabla v^{T})}{2}\,\textup{d}x=\int_{D_{U}}b\cdot v\,\textup{d}x+\int_{\Gamma_{u_{q}}}t\cdot v\,\textup{d}s\,,\qquad\forall v\in U\,,

(29)

where
{\mathrm{A}}:{\mathrm{B}}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}{\mathrm{A}}_{ij}{\mathrm{B}}_{ij}
denotes the tensor inner product between second-order tensors
{\mathrm{A}}
and
{\mathrm{B}}
. To ensure positivity, a log-normal distribution is used for
m
:

{\mathscr{Q}}(z)=m=\alpha_{m}\exp(w)+\beta_{m}\,,\qquad\text{where }\quad z\in\Omega\,,w={\mathscr{Z}}(z)\,,\text{ and }\mu_{\mathscr{Z}}=N(0,C)\,.

(30)

3.2.1
Setup details and data generation

The domain is the unit square,
L_{1}=L_{2}=1
. The body force is zero, and a constant traction is applied on
\Gamma_{u_{q}}
:

b(x)=0\hat{e}_{1}+0\hat{e}_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad t(x)=20\hat{e}_{1}+50\hat{e}_{2}\,.

(31)

The Poisson ratio is fixed at
\nu=0.45
. The covariance operator is taken as
C=L^{-2}_{\Delta}
, with the parameters

\mathsf{a}_{c}=0.005\,,\quad\mathsf{b}_{c}=1\,,\quad\mathsf{c}_{c}=0.2\,,\quad\alpha_{m}=100\,,\quad\beta_{m}=1000\,.

(32)

As in the Poisson problem, a finite element approximation with triangle elements and linear interpolation is used for both the input and output functions. The number of elements is 5000, the number of nodes is
N_{nodes}=2601
,
p_{U}=2N_{nodes}=5202
, and

3.3
Hyperelasticity problem

The third problem concerns hyperelastic deformation on the same rectangular domain considered in
Section

3.2
. The uncertain parameter remains the Young’s modulus field
m(x)
, and the displacement field is
u:D_{U}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. The deformation gradient, right Cauchy–Green tensor, first invariant, and Jacobian are

{\bm{\mathrm{F}}}={\bm{\mathrm{I}}}+\nabla u\,,\qquad{\bm{\mathrm{C}}}={\bm{\mathrm{F}}}^{\top}{\bm{\mathrm{F}}}\,,\qquad I_{1}=\mathrm{tr}({\bm{\mathrm{C}}})\,,\qquad J=\mathrm{det}({\bm{\mathrm{F}}})\,.

(33)

A compressible Neo-Hookean strain-energy density
Korobeynikov et al. (2025)
is used:

W({\bm{\mathrm{F}}})=\frac{\mu}{2}\left(I_{1}-3-2\ln J\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}(\ln J)^{2}\,,

(34)

where
\lambda
and
\mu
are the Lamé parameters related to the Young’s modulus
m
and Poisson ratio
\nu
as in (
27
).
Using the chain rule and the identities (Eqs. (1.226), (1.228), and (1.232) in [
Jog, 2015
])

\frac{\partial I_{1}}{\partial{\bm{\mathrm{F}}}}=2{\bm{\mathrm{F}}}\,,\qquad\frac{\partial J}{\partial{\bm{\mathrm{F}}}}=J{\bm{\mathrm{F}}}^{-T}\,,\qquad\frac{\partial\ln J}{\partial{\bm{\mathrm{F}}}}={\bm{\mathrm{F}}}^{-T}\,,

(35)

the first Piola–Kirchhoff stress for the strain-energy density in (
34
) is

{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}=\frac{\partial W}{\partial{\bm{\mathrm{F}}}}=\mu{\bm{\mathrm{F}}}+\left(\lambda\ln J-\mu\right){\bm{\mathrm{F}}}^{-T}\,.

(36)

The strong form of the hyperelasticity problem on the displacement field
u
is written in the reference configuration as

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}(x)

\displaystyle=b(x)\,,\qquad

\displaystyle\forall x\in D_{U}\,,

(37)

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle=0\,,\qquad

\displaystyle\forall x\in\Gamma_{u_{d}}\,,

\displaystyle{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}(x)n(x)

\displaystyle=t(x)\,,\qquad

\displaystyle\forall x\in\Gamma_{u_{q}}=\partial D_{U}-\Gamma_{u_{d}}\,,

As in the case of linear elasticity, consider the function spaces

M:=\left\{m:D_{M}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}:\left\lVert m\right\rVert_{L^{2}}<\infty\right\}\,,\;U:=\left\{u\in H^{1}(D_{U};{\mathbb{R}}^{2}):u=0\;\text{ on }\Gamma_{u_{d}}\right\}\,.

(38)

Then, the weak form of the problem is: given
m\in M
, find
u\in U
such that

\int_{D_{U}}{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}:\nabla v\,\textup{d}x=\int_{D_{U}}b\cdot v\,\textup{d}x+\int_{\Gamma_{u_{q}}}t\cdot v\,\textup{d}s\,,\qquad\forall v\in U\,.

(39)

As before, the forward map
F:M\to U
is defined by
u=F(m)
, where
m\in M
is the uncertain Young’s modulus field.

The transformation from
w
to
m
and the prior model are the same as in
Section

3.2
.

Neural operators as surrogates for the forward problem

Over the years, several neural operator architectures have emerged that leverage neural networks to construct efficient approximations of
F
. This section presents representative approaches, emphasizing their underlying principles and implementation details to develop a practical understanding of their methods.

The following subsections introduce DeepONet, PCANet, and FNO, along with their implementation details.

4.1
Deep Operator Network (DeepONet)

DeepONet was first introduced in
Lu et al. (2019)
, and over the years, various extensions of the general framework and applications have been realized. Consider some
m
, and suppose
\{\phi^{U}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p_{U}}\subset U
is a finite collection of basis functions, then, at
x\in D_{U}
,

F(m)(x)=u(x)\approx\sum\;\underbrace{\left({F(m)},{\phi^{U}_{i}}\right)}_{=:\alpha_{i}(m)}\;\phi^{U}_{i}(x)\,,

(40)

where, coefficients
\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{i}(m)
depend on
m
. DeepONet’s underlying idea is to learn the above finite-dimensional representation of the output of operator
F
. That is, identify the linear bases or more precisely learn the values of basis functions
\phi^{U}_{i}(x)
at coordinates
x\in D_{U}
, and the coefficients associated with the bases dependent on the input
m
,
\{\alpha_{i}(m)\}
so that
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}(m)\phi^{U}_{i}(x)
is approximately equal to
F(m)(x)=u(x)
. Towards this, DeepONet considers two neural networks, so-called branch and trunk networks; see
Figure

4
. The branch network takes discretization of the input function, denoted by
\mathrm{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}
, and the neural network produces
N_{br}
number of coefficients, which are used as
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{br}}
. On the other hand, the trunk network takes as input the spatial coordinate,
x
, and outputs
d_{U}N_{tr}
numbers, which play the role of
\{\phi^{U}_{i}(x)\}_{i=1}^{N_{tr}}
in (
40
). If
m
and
u
are scalar fields then
N_{tr}=N_{br}
. Finally, the loss function is defined in terms of the norm of the difference between the ground truth and approximation by the joint output from the branch and trunk networks,
\sum_{i=1}^{N_{br}}\alpha_{i}(m)\phi^{U}_{i}(x)
.

Figure 4
:
Schematics of three neural operators, DeepONet, PCANet, and FNO. Grey and light green circles represent the input and output of neural operators. The blue box includes a parameterized neural network-based map. In this work, the blue boxes for DeepONet and PCANet employ fully connected multi-layer perceptron neural networks. In the case of FNO, trainable parameters (namely,
R_{l},W_{l},b_{l}
) appear within each Fourier layer.

In summary, the operator approximation in DeepONet takes the form, for
m\in M
(input actually is the finite-dimensional discretization
\mathrm{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}
of
m\in M
) and
x\in D_{U}
,

F_{NOp}(m)(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{br}}\alpha_{i}(m;\theta_{br})\phi^{U}_{i}(x;\theta_{tr})+b\,,

(41)

where

(i)

\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{i}(m;\theta_{br})\in\mathbb{R}
,
1\leq i\leq N_{br}
, are outputs of the branch network with
\theta_{br}
neural network trainable parameters,

(ii)

\phi^{U}_{i}(x;\theta_{tr})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{U}}
,
1\leq i\leq N_{br}
, are outputs of the trunk network (total
N_{tr}=d_{U}N_{br}
numbers) with
\theta_{tr}
neural network trainable parameters, and

(iii)

b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{U}}
is a bias.

A key feature of DeepONet is the separation between learning input-dependent coefficients and spatial representations via the branch and trunk networks. Another crucial property of DeepONet is that an approximation of
u=F(m)
at any arbitrary point

can be computed.

4.1.1
Implementation of DeepONet

To simplify the presentation, the input and output functions,
m\in M
and
u\in U
, respectively, are assumed to be scalar-valued, and they are appropriately discretized, e.g., using a finite element approximation. Extending the cases when one or both of these functions are vector-valued is trivial; see
Remark

5
.

Let
\mathrm{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}
be the input discretized function, and let
\mathrm{u}={\mathrm{F}}(\mathrm{m})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{U}}
be the corresponding output discretized function, where
{\mathrm{F}}
is the discretization of the operator of interest

. The following constitutes data for DeepONet:

1.

Consider the
N_{M}\times p_{M}
-matrix,
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}_{br}
, where
N_{M}
is the number of input function samples and
{I}^{\text{th}}
row of
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}_{br}
is the sample
\mathrm{m}^{I}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}
. Each row of
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}_{br}
will be the input to the branch network.

2.

Select
N_{x}
number of locations,

, where
x^{I}\in D_{U}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{q_{U}}
and
q_{U}
is the dimension of the domain. Each location
x^{I}
will serve as input to the trunk neural network, so the DeepONet output is the prediction of the target function’s value at
x^{I}
. In the present implementation, the typical input coordinate

corresponds to the
{I}^{\text{th}}
discretization grid or node of a mesh so that the value of the output function at

corresponding to that grid/node (this is true only for scalar-valued functions). The matrix
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}_{tr}
of size
N_{x}\times q_{U}
is the data for the trunk network, and each row of
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}_{tr}
is the input to the trunk network.

3.

N_{M}\times N_{x}
-matrix
{\bm{\mathrm{Y}}}
, such that an element
{\bm{\mathrm{Y}}}_{IJ}
is the pointwise value of the output data corresponding to
\mathrm{m}^{I}
at location
x^{J}
, i.e.,

{\bm{\mathrm{Y}}}_{IJ}=(\mathrm{u}^{I})_{J}\approx F(m^{I})(x^{J})\,.

(42)

I.e.,
{\bm{\mathrm{Y}}}_{IJ}
is the pointwise value of the output data corresponding to the branch network input data function

(

{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}_{br}

{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}_{tr}

Next, the combined training parameters

\displaystyle\theta^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\theta=\{\theta_{br},\theta_{tr},b\}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{\Theta}}}\;\frac{1}{N_{M}N_{x}}\sum_{I=1}^{N_{M}}\sum_{J=1}^{N_{x}}\left|{\bm{\mathrm{Y}}}_{IJ}-\underbrace{\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N_{br}}\alpha_{k}(\mathrm{m}^{I};\theta_{br})\phi^{U}_{k}(x^{J};\theta_{tr})+b\right)}_{\text{DeepONet output}}\right|^{2}\,.

Remark 5
.

The DeepONet framework described so far can be easily extended to the case where the operator’s target function is vector-valued. Suppose
u(x)=(u_{1}(x),u_{2}(x),...,u_{d_{U}}(x))\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{U}}
,
u_{j}
being the
{j}^{\text{th}}
component function. One way to extend the DeepONet framework discussed above to vector-valued target functions is by considering
N_{br}
branch outputs
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N_{br}}
and
N_{tr}=d_{U}N_{br}
trunk outputs
\{\phi^{U}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N_{br}}
and using (
41
). Here,
\phi^{U}_{k}
are
{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{U}}
-valued functions, so the number of scalar outputs from the trunk network is
N_{tr}=d_{U}N_{br}
.

The alternative construction considered in this work uses a trunk network that produces
N_{tr}
outputs
\{\psi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N_{tr}}
, where
\psi_{k}(x)\in\mathbb{R}
. The branch network produces
N_{br}=d_{U}N_{tr}
outputs
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N_{br}}
. Then, for the
x^{J}
row of
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}_{tr}
and the
\mathrm{m}^{I}
row of
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}_{br}
, the prediction
u_{pred}=(u_{pred,1},u_{pred,2},\ldots,u_{pred,d_{U}})
of

is given by

\displaystyle u_{pred,1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{tr}}\alpha_{k}(\mathrm{m}^{I};\theta_{br})\psi_{k}(x^{J};\theta_{tr})+b_{1}\,

(44)

\displaystyle u_{pred,2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{tr}}\alpha_{k+N_{tr}}(\mathrm{m}^{I};\theta_{br})\psi_{k}(x^{J};\theta_{tr})+b_{2}\,

\displaystyle u_{pred,d_{U}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{tr}}\alpha_{k+(d_{U}-1)N_{tr}}(\mathrm{m}^{I};\theta_{br})\psi_{k}(x^{J};\theta_{tr})+b_{d_{U}}\,,

i.e., the first

4.2
Principal Component Analysis-based Neural Operator (PCANet)

The second neural operator of interest is the PCANet introduced in
Bhattacharya et al. (2021)
, which utilizes Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to reduce the dimensions of input and output discretized functions and poses an operator learning problem in the reduced-dimensional spaces, thereby making learning more efficient. Consider
\{(\mathrm{m}^{I},\mathrm{u}^{I}={\mathrm{F}}(\mathrm{m}^{I}))\}_{I=1}^{N}
set of paired data, where
\mathrm{m}^{I}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}
and
\mathrm{u}^{I}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{U}}
. In general, learning the approximation of the target map
{\mathrm{F}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{U}}
becomes challenging if
p_{M}
and
p_{U}
are large. PCANet alleviates this challenge by introducing a low-dimensional approximation of
{\mathrm{F}}
. Specifically, in PCANet, the dimensions of input and output functions are reduced using SVD, and the neural network between reduced-dimensional inputs and outputs is introduced to approximate the mapping. The effectiveness of this approach depends on whether the input and output data admit accurate low-dimensional representations, which is typically reflected in the decay of the singular values of the corresponding data matrices. When the singular values decay slowly, a much larger reduced dimension may be required, and the efficiency and accuracy advantages of PCANet can be reduced.

To make this precise, let
r_{M}
and
r_{U}
denote the reduced dimensions for input and output functions, respectively, and
\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{M}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{M}\times p_{M}}
and
\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{U}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{U}\times p_{U}}
are the projectors based on SVD for dimension reduction. Now consider a parameterized neural network map
\tilde{{\mathrm{F}}}_{\theta}:{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{M}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{U}}
to construct the approximation
{\mathrm{F}}_{NOp}
of
{\mathrm{F}}
as follows, for a given
\mathrm{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}
,

{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{U}}\ni\;\mathrm{u}={\mathrm{F}}(\mathrm{m})\qquad\approx\qquad{\mathrm{F}}_{NOp}(\mathrm{m})=\underbrace{\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{U}^{T}\left(\underbrace{\tilde{{\mathrm{F}}}_{\theta}\left(\underbrace{\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{M}(\mathrm{m})}_{=:\tilde{\mathrm{m}}\text{ (project $\mathrm{m}$)}})\right)}_{=:\tilde{\mathrm{u}}\text{ (low-dim map)}}\right)}_{\text{lift $\tilde{\mathrm{u}}$}}\;\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{U}}\,.

(45)

Conceptually, the parameters
\theta
can be determined via the optimization problem:

\theta^{\ast}=\argmin_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{\Theta}}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{I=1}^{N}\left\lVert{\mathrm{F}}(\mathrm{m}^{I})-\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{U}^{T}\left(\tilde{{\mathrm{F}}}_{\theta}\left(\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{M}(\mathrm{m}^{I})\right)\right)\right\rVert^{2}\,,

(46)

where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
denotes the
\ell^{2}
-norm and
p_{\Theta}
is the number of trainable parameters. Here, the projection operators are fixed, and the only learnable component is the reduced map
\tilde{{\mathrm{F}}}_{\theta}
.

Thus, unlike DeepONet, which learns basis representations explicitly, PCANet relies on fixed data-driven bases obtained via SVD and focuses on learning the mapping in the reduced space. In practice, the optimization problem (
46
) is reformulated in the reduced space, i.e., the parameters
\theta
in
\tilde{{\mathrm{F}}}_{\theta}
are trained using projected data.
Figure

4
presents the schematic of PCANet and the associated projection steps.

4.2.1
Implementation of PCANet

Let
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}
and
{\bm{\mathrm{Y}}}
are two
N\times p_{M}
and
N\times p_{U}
matrices, respectively, such that rows of
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}
and
{\bm{\mathrm{Y}}}
are input and output function samples,
\mathrm{m}^{I}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}
and
\mathrm{u}^{I}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{U}}
, where
1\leq I\leq N
. The data for the neural network-based map
\tilde{{\mathrm{F}}}_{\theta}
in (
45
) is constructed using the following steps, which include the preprocessing to construct SVD-based projectors:

1.

Center and scale
the data matrices
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}
and
{\bm{\mathrm{Y}}}
by subtracting the sample mean and dividing elementwise by the sample standard deviation. For example, if
\bar{{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}}
and
\sigma_{X}
are
1\times p_{M}
row vectors containing the mean and standard deviation of the columns of
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}
, then the normalized input data
\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}}
is obtained by

\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}}=\frac{{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}-\bar{{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}}}{\sigma_{X}+\mathrm{tol}}\,,

where each row of
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}
is subtracted by the row vector
\bar{{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}}
, and the division by
\sigma_{X}+\mathrm{tol}
is performed elementwise. The small number
\mathrm{tol}
is introduced to ensure numerical stability. The normalized output data
\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{Y}}}}
is obtained in the same way.

2.

SVD projectors
for input and output data are determined following the procedure in
Section

2.3.1
. For example, take
{\mathrm{R}}=\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}}^{T}
and compute the projector
\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{M}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{M}\times p_{M}}
, where
r_{M}
is the specified reduced dimension. Similarly, the projector
\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{U}
is obtained using
{\mathrm{R}}=\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{Y}}}}^{T}
.

3.

Projected data for the neural network
are computed by projecting the rows of
\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}}
into the reduced space. To be specific, let the
I^{\text{th}}
row of
\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}}
be
(\hat{\mathrm{m}}^{I})^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1\times p_{M}}
, where
\hat{\mathrm{m}}^{I}
is the centered and scaled
I^{\text{th}}
input sample. Its projection is
\tilde{\mathrm{m}}^{I}=\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{M}\left(\hat{\mathrm{m}}^{I}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{M}}
. Using this projection, a new matrix
\tilde{{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}}
of size
N\times r_{M}
is formed, where
(\tilde{\mathrm{m}}^{I})^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1\times r_{M}}
is the
I^{\text{th}}
row. Similarly,
\tilde{{\bm{\mathrm{Y}}}}
of size
N\times r_{U}
is obtained by applying
\tilde{{\mathrm{P}}}_{U}
to each centered and scaled output sample.

4.

For the reduced-dimensional map
\tilde{{\mathrm{F}}}_{\theta}:{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{M}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{U}}
, defined by
\tilde{\mathrm{u}}=\tilde{{\mathrm{F}}}_{\theta}(\tilde{\mathrm{m}})
(see (
45
)), the matrices
\tilde{{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}}
and
\tilde{{\bm{\mathrm{Y}}}}
constitute the input and output data, respectively.

Given the projected data matrices
\tilde{{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}}
and
\tilde{{\bm{\mathrm{Y}}}}
and a neural network-based map

4.3
Fourier Neural Operator (FNO)

Fourier neural operator considers the composition of layers, with a typical layer involving an affine transformation and an integral kernel operator followed by nonlinear activation. These affine and integral kernel operations are parameterized. While there are multiple choices of integral kernel operator
Kovachki et al. (2023)
, this work uses the Fourier transform. Consider the case of
D_{M}=D_{U}=D\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{q}
(therefore,
q_{M}=q_{U}=q
) and the neural operator approximation
F_{NOp}
such that

u(x)=F(m)(x)\quad\approx\quad F_{NOp}(m)(x):=Q\left(\underbrace{G_{L}\left(G_{L-1}\cdots G_{1}\left(\underbrace{P\left(m(x)\right)}_{=z_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{h}}}\right)\right)}_{=z_{L}(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{h}}}\right)\,,

(48)

or, concisely,

F_{\mathrm{NOp}}=Q\circ G_{L}\circ G_{L-1}\circ\cdots\circ G_{1}\circ P\,,

(49)

where
\circ
denotes the composition of maps. The map
F_{NOp}
involves the following operations:

•

Lifting
m(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{M}}
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{h}}
, where
d_{h}
is the dimension of outputs from hidden layers via the trainable matrix
P
of size
d_{h}\times d_{M}
.

•

Projecting the final hidden-layer output
z_{L}(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{h}}
onto
{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{U}}
(space of
u(x)
) via the
d_{U}\times d_{h}
trainable matrix
Q
. Thus,
z_{L}
is a
d_{h}
-dimensional feature field defined over the domain
D
.

•

Application of operators
G_{l}
,
1\leq l\leq L
, where
G_{l}
is defined via:

{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{h}}\ni z_{l}=G_{l}(z_{l-1})=\sigma_{l}\left(\underbrace{W_{l}z_{l-1}+b_{l}}_{\text{linear/local operation}}+\underbrace{K_{l}(z_{l-1})}_{\text{nonlocal operation}}\right)\,.

(50)

Here,
z_{l}=z_{l}(x)
denotes a
d_{h}
-dimensional feature field defined over the domain
D
. Note that
z_{l-1}
is the output of the preceding layer,
\sigma_{l}
activation function,
W_{l}
weight matrix,
b_{l}
a bias vector, and
K_{l}(\cdot)
an integral kernel operator.

Up until now, the map
F_{NOp}
in
Equation

48
with the above definitions of
P
,
Q
,
G_{l}
,
l=1,...,L
, is abstract due to the generality of
K_{l}
. The linear operation in (
50
) captures the local effects on the reconstructed function, while the
K_{l}
is designed to capture the non-local effects (interaction with other degrees of freedom) via the integral kernel operation. There are various choices for
K_{l}
, as discussed in
Kovachki et al. (2023)
. For example:

(1)

Low-rank Neural Operator (LNO) in which
K_{l}
takes the form

K_{l}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left({\psi^{(i)}},{z}\right)\varphi^{(i)}(x),

where
\varphi^{(i)}
and
\psi^{(i)}
are some parameterized functions;

(2)

Graph Neural Operator (GNO) in which

K_{l}=\frac{1}{|B(x,r)|}\sum_{y_{i}\in B(x,r)}k(x,y_{i})z(y_{i}),

where
k(\cdot,\cdot)
is a kernel function; and

(3)

Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) maps
z
to Fourier space, followed by mapping the weighted Fourier modes back to the real space. Since FNO is the main focus of this article, it is discussed in more detail next. Readers are referred to
Li et al. (2020)
;
Kovachki et al. (2023)
for further discussion of FNO and associated ideas.

In FNO,
K_{l}(z)
, for
z:D\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{h}}
, takes the form:

K_{l}(z)={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\left(R_{l}{\mathcal{F}}(z)\right)\,,

(51)

where

•

{\mathcal{F}}(z)
is the Fourier transform applied to each component of the feature field
z(x)
over the spatial domain. Only the first
k_{max}
modes are retained, so the output of
{\mathcal{F}}(z)
is in
{\mathbb{C}}^{d_{h}\times k_{max}\times k_{max}}
.

•

R_{l}
applies the weighting to different Fourier modes.
R_{l}
is a complex-valued
d_{h}\times d_{h}\times k_{max}\times k_{max}
trainable tensor and
R_{l}{\mathcal{F}}(z)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d_{h}\times k_{max}\times k_{max}}
.

•

{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}(\cdot)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{h}}
is the inverse Fourier transform applied component-wise to reconstruct the feature field in physical space.

Figure

4
displays the FNO framework based on Fourier transforms. In practice, in the discrete setting, one evaluates the FNO output at all grid points to have

{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{U}}\ni\mathrm{u}={\mathrm{F}}(\mathrm{m})\quad\approx\quad{\mathrm{F}}_{NOp}(\mathrm{m}):=Q\left(G_{L}\left(G_{L-1}\cdots G_{1}\left(P(\mathrm{m})\right)\right)\right)\,,\quad\text{for }\mathrm{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{M}}\,.

(52)

The optimization problem to determine
\theta
is given by

\theta^{\ast}=\argmin_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{\Theta}}}\frac{1}{N_{m}}\sum_{I=1}^{N_{m}}\left\|\mathrm{u}^{I}-{\mathrm{F}}_{NOp}(\mathrm{m}^{I})\right\|^{2}\,,

(53)

where
p_{\Theta}
is the number of trainable parameters in FNO.

4.3.1
Implementation of FNO

The implementation of FNO requires function values at grid locations, and thus, preprocessing is required to obtain these values at grid points. Suppose
D_{M}=D_{U}=D=(0,1)^{2}
and consider the grid division of
\mathrm{closure}(D)
consisting of
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
number of points in

and

directions, respectively. Linear interpolation is used to map finite element solutions onto a structured grid required by FNO. The following describes the data:

1.

Let
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}
be an
N\times n_{1}\times n_{2}\times 3
matrix, where the outer dimension corresponds to the number of samples. The element of
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}
at
I^{\text{th}}
outer index is a
n_{1}\times n_{2}\times 3
matrix containing the interpolated values of a function
m^{I}
(or discretization of this function given by
\mathrm{m}^{I}
on a mesh) at all grid
(x^{1},x^{2})
points and the coordinates
x^{1}
and
x^{2}
of all grid points; thus, the inner dimension is three. The spatial coordinates are included to allow the network to learn non-translation-invariant features. Next, the function values are centered and scaled using the mean and standard deviation computed from the samples
1\leq I\leq N
.

2.

Let
{\bm{\mathrm{Y}}}
be
N\times n_{1}\times n_{2}\times d_{U}
matrix such that the element of
{\bm{\mathrm{Y}}}
at
I^{\text{th}}
outer index is a

matrix containing the interpolated values of
d_{U}
-valued function
u^{I}
(or discretization of this function given by
\mathrm{u}^{I}

{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}
and
{\bm{\mathrm{Y}}}
at the
I^{\text{th}}
outer index correspond to
\mathrm{m}^{I}
and
\mathrm{u}^{I}={\mathrm{F}}(\mathrm{m}^{I})
on a grid, thereby establishing the correspondence between input and output. Function values at grid points are also centered and scaled using the sample mean and standard deviation.

Given a sample from
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}
, denoted
\mathrm{x}=(m^{I}(x^{1},x^{2}),x^{1},x^{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, it is first lifted into
{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{h}}
, where
d_{h}>3
is a dimension of the input and output spaces of FNO layers, to get
z_{0}=P(\mathrm{x})=W_{P}\,\mathrm{x}+b_{P}
, where
W_{P}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{h}\times 3}
and
b_{P}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{h}}
. This representation is applied pointwise at each grid location, and the full input consists of all such values arranged over the grid. Next,
z_{0}
goes through
L
layers such that given
z_{l-1}
an output of
(l-1)^{\text{th}}
layer,
z_{l}=G_{l}(z_{l-1})
. The

Operator

z_{l}^{1}=W_{l}z_{l-1}+b_{l}
and Fourier-based transformation

z_{l}^{2}={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\big(R_{l}\,{\mathcal{F}}(z_{l-1})\big),

where
R_{l}
is a complex matrix of size
d_{h}\times d_{h}\times k_{max}\times k_{max}
,
k_{max}

and

are the weight and bias parameters, respectively. The output of the final layer

(54)

\displaystyle\left.\left\{W_{l}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{h}\times d_{h}}\,,b_{l}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{h}}\,,R_{l}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d_{h}\times d_{h}\times k_{max}\times k_{max}}\,,\quad 1\leq l\leq L\right\}\right\}\,.

It should be noted that extending the above to a vector-valued function (for a linear elasticity problem) as input and output is relatively straightforward.

5
Neural operators for Bayesian inference

Bayesian inference is a powerful framework for solving inverse problems in which the goal is to infer unknown parameters or fields from observed data. In PDE-constrained inverse problems, posterior sampling procedures such as Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) require repeated evaluations of the forward model, and the computational cost is often dominated by the associated likelihood evaluations. Neural operators can therefore serve as efficient surrogate forward models, accelerating these evaluations while maintaining acceptable approximation accuracy. The goal of this section is to demonstrate the integration of neural operator surrogates into the Bayesian inference framework and to evaluate the resulting inference performance.

We first formulate the Bayesian inverse problem in an abstract infinite-dimensional setting, following
Dashti and Stuart (2017)
;
Bui-Thanh et al. (2013)
;
Stuart (2010)
. We then present the inference results for the Poisson problem in the main text, while the corresponding results for the linear elasticity and hyperelasticity models are provided in
B
. The neural operator architectures and training procedures are described in the previous section, and the trained models are used here as surrogate forward models within the Bayesian inference problems.

5.1
Abstract Bayesian inference problem in infinite dimensions

Consider a parameter field
m\in M
to be inferred from observational data
\mathrm{g}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\mathrm{g}}}
and the corresponding solution of the PDE,
u=F(m)\in U
. Let
\bar{{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}}:U\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\mathrm{g}}}
denote the state-to-observable map such that
\bar{{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}}(u)
gives the predicted observations corresponding to the state
u
. The Bayesian inverse problem is formulated through the relation

\mathrm{g}=\bar{{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}}(u)+\eta=\bar{{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}}(F(m))+\eta\,,

(56)

where
\eta\sim{\mathrm{N}}({\rm{0}},\Gamma_{\mathrm{g}})
represents observational and modeling noise, and
\Gamma_{\mathrm{g}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\mathrm{g}}\times d_{\mathrm{g}}}
is the covariance matrix. For simplicity, the covariance matrix is taken to be
\Gamma_{\mathrm{g}}=\sigma_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}{\mathrm{I}}
, where
\sigma_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}
is the noise variance and
{\mathrm{I}}
is the identity matrix.

In many applications, the admissible parameter space
M_{ad}
is a subset of
M
incorporating physical constraints. For example, diffusivity and Young’s modulus must remain strictly positive. A transformation
m=m(w)
with
w\in W
is used to enforce the positivity constraint:

m(x)=\alpha_{m}\exp(w(x))+\beta_{m}\,,\quad x\in D_{W}\,,

(57)

where
\alpha_{m}
and
\beta_{m}
are constants. The inference problem is thus posed in terms of
w\in W
, where
W
is a Hilbert space.

Define the parameter-to-observable map
{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}:W\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\mathrm{g}}}
by

{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}(w)=\bar{{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}}(F(m(w)))\,.

(58)

Using
{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}
, the goal is to infer the probability distribution of
w
from

\mathrm{g}={\bm{\mathrm{B}}}(w)+\eta\,.

(59)

Together with the noise model, the above equation defines the conditional probability distribution of the data
\mathrm{g}
given
w
. Since the noise is assumed to be Gaussian,
\eta\sim{\mathrm{N}}({\rm{0}},\Gamma_{\mathrm{g}})
, the likelihood, i.e., the distribution of
\mathrm{g}
conditioned on
w
, is also Gaussian:
\mathrm{g}\mid w\sim{\mathrm{N}}({\bm{\mathrm{B}}}(w),\Gamma_{\mathrm{g}})
. Explicitly,

\pi_{like}(\mathrm{g}\mid w)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(\Gamma_{\mathrm{g}})}\left(2\pi\right)^{\frac{d_{\mathrm{g}}}{2}}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{g}-{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}(w))^{T}\Gamma_{\mathrm{g}}^{-1}(\mathrm{g}-{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}(w))\right]\,.

(60)

It is useful to define the likelihood potential function

\Phi(w):=-\log\pi_{like}(\mathrm{g}\mid w)\,,

(61)

where
\mathrm{g}
is fixed, so that
\Phi
is a function of
w
.

To solve (
59
), that is, to identify the distribution of
w
conditioned on the data
\mathrm{g}
and the noise model, one must specify a prior measure
\mu^{\mathrm{o}}
on
W
that encodes prior knowledge. For example, this prior knowledge may include assumptions on the mean and covariance. Next, suppose the prior is Gaussian,
\mu^{\mathrm{o}}=N(0,C)
, where the mean is zero and
C
is the covariance operator. With this Gaussian prior on
w
, the transformation (
57
) induces a log-normal prior on the parameter field
m\in M_{ad}
. The choice of
C
,
\alpha_{m}
, and
\beta_{m}
reflects prior knowledge of
m
.

Bayes’ rule relates the prior measure
\mu^{\mathrm{o}}
, the likelihood
\pi_{like}
, and the posterior measure
\mu^{\mathrm{g}}
, which is the distribution of
w
conditioned on the data
\mathrm{g}
, as follows:

\frac{\,\textup{d}\mu^{\mathrm{g}}}{\,\textup{d}\mu^{\mathrm{o}}}(w)=\frac{1}{Z}\pi_{like}(\mathrm{g}\mid w)=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\left(-\Phi(w)\right)\,,

(62)

where
Z
is the normalizing constant given by

Z=\int_{W}\exp\left(-\Phi(w)\right)\,\textup{d}\mu^{\mathrm{o}}(w)\,.

(63)

Bayes’ rule (
62
) indicates how the posterior and prior measures are related. Given (
62
), it is straightforward to see that

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\mathrm{g}}}\left[f\right]&=\int_{W}f(w)\,\,\textup{d}\mu^{\mathrm{g}}(w)\\
&=\int_{W}f(w)\frac{\exp\left(-\Phi(w)\right)}{Z}\,\textup{d}\mu^{\mathrm{o}}(w)\\
&={\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\mathrm{o}}}\left[f(\cdot)\,\frac{\exp\left(-\Phi(\cdot)\right)}{Z}\right]\,.\end{split}

(64)

The above identity shows that posterior expectations can be expressed as prior expectations weighted by the likelihood factor. In practice, direct evaluation of such expectations is generally not feasible in high or infinite dimensions, which motivates the use of sampling methods such as Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC). This is discussed next.

5.1.1
Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method to sample from the posterior measure

To sample from the posterior measure
\mu^{\mathrm{g}}
(i.e., to generate samples
w\in W
distributed according to
\mu^{\mathrm{g}}
), several algorithms are available (see [
Cotter et al., 2013
, Section 4
] and [
Dashti and Stuart, 2017
]). This work uses the preconditioned Crank–Nicolson (pCN) method due to its simplicity. The pCN algorithm, based on
Cotter et al. (2013)
, is as follows:

Preconditioned Crank–Nicolson (pCN) MCMC algorithm

1.

Compute an initial sample
w^{(0)}\sim\mu^{\mathrm{o}}=N(0,C)
.

2.

For
k=0,1,\dots,k_{mcmc}-1
:

(a)

Propose
v^{(k)}=w^{(k)}\sqrt{1-\beta_{pCN}^{2}}+\beta_{pCN}\xi
, where
\xi\sim\mu^{\mathrm{o}}
.

(b)

Compute
a=\Phi(w^{(k)})-\Phi(v^{(k)})
, and draw
b\sim\mathrm{Uniform}[0,1]
.

(c)

If
\exp(a)>b
(equivalently
a>\log(b)
), accept
v^{(k)}
and set
w^{(k+1)}=v^{(k)}
. Otherwise, set
w^{(k+1)}=w^{(k)}
.

3.

Discard the initial
k_{burn}
samples as burn-in, and use
\{w^{(k)}\}_{k=k_{burn}+1}^{k_{mcmc}}
as samples from the posterior. The posterior mean is approximated by

\bar{w}=\frac{1}{k_{mcmc}-k_{burn}}\sum_{k=k_{burn}+1}^{k_{mcmc}}w^{(k)}\,.

(65)

In the above algorithm,
\beta_{pCN}
is the proposal hyperparameter. To verify that the acceptance rule behaves as intended, consider two cases. First, if the proposed sample
v^{(k)}
has higher cost than the current sample
w^{(k)}
(i.e.,
\Phi(v^{(k)})>\Phi(w^{(k)})
), then
a<0
and
\exp(a)<1
, so the proposed sample may be accepted or rejected depending on the draw
b
. Second, if the proposed sample has lower cost than the current sample, then
a>0
and
\exp(a)>1
, so the proposed sample is accepted regardless of
b
.

shows the MCMC implementation based on the pCN method. Functions
proposal
and
sample
contain the core pCN implementation. The listing also shows how surrogate models are used to compute the forward solution in the
solveFwd
function.

7
Perspectives and conclusion

While the focus of this article has been on the foundational aspects of operator learning and their practical implementation, the subsections below briefly review recent developments in neural operators, with particular emphasis on the growing family of architectures, emerging foundation models for PDEs, and the critical issue of controlling prediction accuracy.

7.1
The growing field of neural operators

The field of neural operators continues to evolve through a range of frameworks that adapt the basic operator-learning idea to different discretizations, physical constraints, geometries, and application settings. One direction is to incorporate additional physical or differential information during training. Physics-Informed Neural Operators (PINO) include the residual of the governing PDE in the loss function, in addition to losses associated with model outputs and data mismatch
Li et al. (2024b)
;
Goswami et al. (2023)
;
Wang et al. (2021)
. Derivative-Informed Neural Operators (DINO)
O’Leary-Roseberry et al. (2024)
incorporate derivative information into the training process to improve learning efficiency and accuracy, particularly for problems where gradients play a dominant role.

Another line of work concerns the representation of the nonlocal operator and the underlying domain. Graph Neural Operators (GNO)
Kovachki et al. (2023)
are based on graph discretizations of the spatial domain and are therefore well suited for problems defined on irregular geometries. Their structure is similar to that of the FNO, with a different form of the nonlocal kernel operator. The Wavelet Neural Operator (WNO)
Tripura and Chakraborty (2022)
follows a related idea, in which the nonlocal operation is defined using wavelet transforms. The Laplace Neural Operator
Cao et al. (2024b)
extends operator learning to transient problems and non-periodic domains. Other works use attention-based or transformer-type parameterizations of the operator map, including GNOT
Hao et al. (2023)
, the operator transformer of
Li et al. (2022)
, and factorized transformer models such as FactFormer
Li et al. (2023)
. These approaches broaden the class of admissible input representations beyond the regular-grid setting for which classical FNO is most natural.

Multi-resolution and multi-scale architectures form another important direction. U-NO
Rahman et al. (2022)
introduces a U-shaped encoder-decoder structure for deeper operator networks, while Factorized FNO
Tran et al. (2021)
aims to improve parameter efficiency and scalability for high-resolution PDEs. Multiwavelet constructions
Gupta et al. (2021)
provide a principled way to separate coarse and fine scales and have been effective for multi-scale and coupled systems.

Geometry and domain complexity have also motivated several extensions. Local neural operators
Li et al. (2024a)
and diffeomorphism neural operators
Zhao et al. (2025)
aim to preserve discretization flexibility while improving transfer across domains and boundary configurations. In
Vemparala et al. (2024)
, a deep learning-driven domain decomposition (DLD
3
) method is proposed, where an FNO is trained to learn the solution map on smaller subdomains of a larger problem, thereby allowing the framework to be applied to different geometries and boundary conditions. Recently, the Kolmogorov–Arnold Neural Network (KAN) has motivated alternative parameterizations of nonlinear maps
Liu et al. (2024b)
.

There has also been considerable effort to extend neural operator frameworks to Bayesian settings, where uncertainty is modeled through probabilistic formulations of the learned operators; see
Psaros et al. (2023)
;
Jospin et al. (2022)
;
Garg and Chakraborty (2022)
;
Singh et al. (2024)
. These approaches aim to provide probabilistic predictions that reflect uncertainty in both the data and model parameters, alongside point estimates.

Digital twins are another application area where neural operators may be useful, especially when repeated state predictions are needed under varying model or control parameters. Digital twin frameworks are expanding due to advances in computational capacity, multi-physics modeling, sensing, and control technologies; see
Juarez et al. (2021)
;
Wagg et al. (2020)
. Neural operators can serve as fast surrogate models for predicting system states within such frameworks
Kobayashi and Alam (2024)
.

Neural operators have also demonstrated potential in Bayesian inverse problems, optimization, and control. In Bayesian inverse problems, they can accelerate posterior sampling by replacing expensive PDE solves with surrogate evaluations
Cao et al. (2023)
;
Cao et al. (2024a)
;
Gao et al. (2024)
. In optimization and control problems, the primary computational bottleneck is often the repeated evaluation of the forward solution for varying parameter fields
Shukla et al. (2024)
. A major challenge in these settings is the lack of reliable mechanisms for predicting surrogate accuracy. Training data are typically generated from a prior distribution, whereas optimization and control procedures may require evaluations far outside this regime.
Jha (2024)
demonstrates that neural operators trained on a prior distribution can perform poorly when used within optimization loops. This issue is discussed in more detail in the next subsection.

7.2
Controlling neural operator prediction accuracy

Neural operators can provide rapid surrogate evaluations, but their practical use depends on whether their prediction error can be estimated, controlled, or corrected in regimes relevant to the downstream task. The ability to predict and control accuracy is a central issue in uncertainty quantification, Bayesian inference, optimization, and control problems, where small forward-model errors may propagate through repeated evaluations. Several key works in this direction are reviewed below.

The multi-level neural network framework
Aldirany et al. (2024)
iteratively refines solutions by training successive networks to minimize residual errors from previous levels. This hierarchical approach progressively reduces approximation error. By capturing high-frequency components in subsequent networks, this framework addresses limitations of standard neural network approximations, which can have low sensitivity to higher-frequency modes. The multi-stage neural network framework
Wang and Lai (2024)
addresses convergence plateauing in deep learning by dividing training into sequential stages, with each stage fitting the residual from the previous one. This approach progressively refines the approximation. The methods in
Aldirany et al. (2024)
;
Wang and Lai (2024)
are reported to achieve errors approaching machine precision in controlled settings.

The Galerkin neural network framework
Ainsworth and Dong (2021)
integrates the classical Galerkin method with neural networks, using the neurons in a neural network layer as basis functions to approximate variational equations. It adaptively adds new neurons to a single-layer neural network, with each neuron corresponding to a basis function. By adding more neurons, the dimension of the approximation increases in a way that produces less error than lower-dimensional subspaces.

The residual-based error correction method
Cao et al. (2023)
;
Jha (2024)
, illustrated in
Figure

11
, builds on the idea of using lower-fidelity solutions to estimate modeling errors
Jha and Oden (2022)
. It treats the neural operator prediction as an initial guess and solves a variational problem to correct the residual error. This approach improves accuracy in applications like Bayesian inverse problems and topology optimization, where small errors can propagate and amplify. The trade-off in the residual-based error correction method is the introduction of a linear variational problem on the modeling error field, which must be solved and added to the neural operator prediction to obtain the improved prediction. For challenging nonlinear problems, the approach leads to significant speedups. In Bayesian settings, it enhances posterior estimates without increasing computational cost
Cao et al. (2023)
. For the example optimization problem of seeking a diffusivity field in the Poisson equation that minimizes the compliance, neural operators, when used as surrogates for the forward problem, produced minimizers with significant errors (about
80\%
). Here, the error is defined as the norm of the difference between the minimizer obtained using the true forward model and that obtained using the surrogate. Neural operator surrogates with the residual-based error correction produced minimizers with errors below
6\%
; see
Figure

12
, where the optimized diffusivities obtained using the
true
model and the surrogates are compared. A complementary direction is PhysicsCorrect
Huang and Perdikaris (2026)
, a training-free correction framework that stabilizes neural PDE simulations by enforcing physics-based consistency at inference time. It is conceptually aligned with residual-based correction strategies such as
Jha (2024)
, but avoids retraining and introduces only modest additional overhead.

Recent work also makes it clear that controlling prediction accuracy is not only a matter of training larger models.
A posteriori
and residual-based indicators must be made reliable in norms that are meaningful for the PDE under consideration. In this direction, rigorous error analyses for DeepONet and FNO-type models
Lanthaler et al. (2022)
;
Kovachki et al. (2021)
provide an important theoretical baseline, while newer work on neural functional a posteriori error estimates
Fanaskov et al. (2024)
aims to construct computable error majorants that can be used during or after training. These developments are significant because small empirical losses or averaged test errors do not, by themselves, provide reliable guarantees about the error of a specific prediction relevant to an optimization, inference, or control query.

Adaptive strategies are also playing an increasingly important role. The framework in
Gao et al. (2024)
locally refines a pretrained surrogate in regions most relevant to posterior exploration. This is conceptually appealing because high accuracy is rarely required uniformly over the entire input space. Instead, one often seeks high fidelity only on a dynamically discovered subset of the parameter regime. Multi-fidelity and multi-level formulations provide another route to balancing cost and accuracy. Multi-fidelity deep neural operators
Lu et al. (2022)
, multi-fidelity DeepONets
Howard et al. (2023)
, and application-oriented multi-fidelity operator fusion strategies
Xu et al. (2024)
exploit the fact that coarse simulations, reduced models, or monitoring data may be much cheaper to obtain than uniformly high-fidelity labels.

Out-of-distribution behavior remains especially challenging. PDEBench- and PDEArena-style evaluations have shown that generalization across coefficients, resolutions, time horizons, and physical regimes is uneven across architectures
Takamoto et al. (2022)
;
Gupta and Brandstetter (2022)
. Theoretical work on out-of-distribution risk bounds
Benitez et al. (2024)
, operator complexity
Lanthaler (2023)
;
Lanthaler and Stuart (2026)
, and data complexity
Kovachki et al. (2024)
reinforces the same message from another direction: one should not expect uniformly reliable accuracy over broad classes of operators without either exploiting stronger problem structure or incurring additional cost in data, model complexity, or online correction. Related results on DeepONet size lower bounds and neural scaling laws
Mukherjee and Roy (2023)
;
Liu et al. (2024a)
further indicate that practical accuracy limits are tied not only to optimization but also to intrinsic approximation and sample-complexity barriers.

Therefore, the current picture is that improvements in accuracy are possible through residual correction, adaptive refinement, multi-level training, multi-fidelity learning, and stronger theory, but each mechanism introduces a cost-accuracy trade-off. Extra linear solves, extra training stages, local fine-tuning, larger pretrained backbones, and more structured loss functions can all improve fidelity, yet they reduce some of the computational speed advantage that originally motivated neural operators. For many downstream applications, this could still be a favorable trade-off, but it highlights why predictability of accuracy, rather than average-case speed alone, remains the central challenge.

The works reviewed above highlight several important limitations. First, methods that substantially improve the accuracy of neural operator predictions generally introduce additional computation, for example, by adding extra training stages, adaptive updates, or the solution of correction problems. Second, the relevant notion of accuracy is usually application-dependent; an error level acceptable for forward prediction may be unusable for optimization, posterior inference, or control. Third, distributional shifts remain unavoidable in many realistic workflows, and current error indicators are still far from being fully calibrated under such shifts. Addressing these issues will likely require tighter integration between operator learning, adaptive numerical analysis, and application-specific notions of goal-oriented error.

7.3
Foundation models for PDEs

A prominent emerging trend in scientific machine learning is the construction of pretrained models that are not tied to a single PDE family, geometry, or discretization, but instead aim to learn transferable representations across broad classes of operator-learning tasks. In the context of PDEs, this direction includes multi-physics pretraining
McCabe et al. (2023)
, unified PDE solvers
Shen et al. (2024)
, Universal Physics Transformers
Alkin et al. (2024)
, large-scale pretrained operator transformers such as DPOT
Hao et al. (2024)
, and PDEformer-style models that condition directly on a representation of the equation itself
Ye et al. (2024)
;
Ye et al. (2025)
. Related work such as OmniArch
Chen et al. (2024)
further suggests that large-scale operator pretraining can improve sample efficiency, few-shot transfer, and robustness across related tasks. Compared to task-specific neural operators, these approaches seek more transferable latent representations of PDE dynamics, boundary interactions, and geometric variation.

At present, most PDE foundation models are predominantly data-driven. Their training typically relies on large collections of simulation trajectories drawn from multiple PDE families, resolutions, and parameter ranges
McCabe et al. (2023)
;
Hao et al. (2024)
;
Ohana et al. (2024)
. In contrast, physics-informed variants often enter through residual regularization, alignment objectives, or fine-tuning rather than replacing large-scale simulation data altogether
Chen et al. (2024)
;
Zhong and Meidani (2025)
. This distinction is important. Data-driven pretraining is currently the main mechanism for obtaining broad coverage across multiple equations and regimes, while physics-informed components are more often used to improve data efficiency, enforce consistency, or adapt a pretrained model to a smaller downstream dataset. In that sense, the present landscape is less about a single “foundation architecture” and more about a family of pretrained operator-learning systems that differ in how they encode PDE structure, geometry, and physical constraints.

The potential applications of such models are substantial. For rapid surrogate modeling across parametric families, a pretrained operator can amortize the cost of repeated PDE solves over many-query settings. This is directly relevant to inverse problems, design optimization, control, uncertainty propagation, and real-time digital twins, where the cost bottleneck is rarely a single forward solve but rather a large sequence of related solves
Wagg et al. (2020)
;
Juarez et al. (2021)
;
Shukla et al. (2024)
. A sufficiently transferable pretrained operator could also enable cross-domain transfer, for example, by moving from one geometry family to another, from one boundary-condition regime to another, or from one PDE class to a related coupled system with comparatively little task-specific retraining
Shen et al. (2024)
;
Alkin et al. (2024)
. Recent scaling efforts such as AB-UPT
Alkin et al. (2025)
and emerging multi-physics pretraining frameworks
Masliaev et al. (2025)
indicate that this direction is especially attractive for industrial simulation settings where geometry diversity and repeated evaluations dominate runtime.

At the same time, foundation models for PDEs raise difficult technical questions. The first is the lack of reliable error bounds. Even when zero-shot or few-shot performance is strong on held-out benchmarks, there is rarely a computable guarantee on the error for a new geometry, a new boundary condition, or a new coefficient regime. The second challenge is generalization outside the training regime. Broader pretraining improves average transfer performance, but it does not remove the need to reason carefully about out-of-distribution inputs, unseen physical couplings, or extrapolative parameter settings
Cao et al. (2023)
;
Jha (2024)
. The third challenge is scale: higher-dimensional PDEs, strongly coupled multi-physics systems, and industrial geometries still place severe demands on both model capacity and training data. Benchmark ecosystems such as PDEBench, PDEArena, and The Well
Takamoto et al. (2022)
;
Gupta and Brandstetter (2022)
;
Ohana et al. (2024)
help standardize evaluation, but they also make clear that data generation remains expensive and potentially biased toward the numerical solvers, regimes, and discretizations used to create the datasets.

There are also challenges of interpretability and physics consistency. A pretrained model that performs well across many systems may still obscure which physical mechanisms it has learned. It may also be unclear which conservation laws are embedded in the architecture and when a prediction should trigger solver-based verification. For this reason, foundation models for PDEs are unlikely to replace classical numerical methods outright. A more realistic direction is tight coupling with numerical solvers through warm starts, residual correction, adaptive verification, and solver-in-the-loop refinement. In that workflow, pretrained operator models provide fast approximate maps over broad classes of problems, while numerical analysis provides the local certification and correction required for high-consequence decisions. From the perspective of scientific computing, this hybrid view appears more plausible than a purely black-box replacement of established PDE solvers.

7.4
Final thoughts

Neural operators have established themselves as powerful surrogates for parametric PDEs, offering a different computational paradigm in which mappings between function spaces are learned once and evaluated rapidly. This shift has clear advantages in many-query settings such as uncertainty quantification, Bayesian inference, optimization, and control, where the dominant cost lies in repeated forward solves rather than a single high-fidelity simulation.

At the same time, the core limitation is now well understood: accuracy is not inherently predictable in the same way as in classical numerical methods. Neural operators do not provide built-in guarantees tied to discretization, mesh refinement, or variational structure. This gap becomes critical in downstream tasks where small errors can lead to qualitatively incorrect decisions, particularly in optimization, inverse problems, and control.

Recent advances suggest that this limitation is not insurmountable, but it requires a shift in perspective. Accuracy must be actively controlled rather than passively expected. Residual-based correction, adaptive refinement, multi-level training, and multi-fidelity formulations all point toward a paradigm in which neural operators provide fast approximate predictions, while additional mechanisms ensure reliability in task-relevant regimes. The emerging theme is that neural operators are most effective when embedded within a broader computational workflow rather than used as standalone replacements for PDE solvers.

Looking forward, three directions appear particularly important. First, developing reliable, computable, and meaningful error indicators for specific applications remains a central challenge. Second, scalable strategies for handling distributional shifts, especially in high-dimensional, multi-physics settings, are essential for real-world deployment. Third, integrating pretrained operator models with physics-based correction and solver-in-the-loop verification offers a promising path to combining generality with reliability. These observations suggest that progress in neural operators will depend less on isolated architectural advances and more on principled integration with numerical analysis and application-driven error control.

In this sense, the future of neural operators is likely to involve data-driven operator learning augmented by physics-based correction, adaptive numerical analysis, problem-specific validation, and goal-oriented error certification. Advancing this integration will be key to realizing the full potential of neural operators in scientific computing.
