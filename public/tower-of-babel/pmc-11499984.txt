Depletion of ATP13A2 in adult brain induces a Parkinsonian phenotype in mice and non-human primates
Authors: Veerle Baekelandt
Published: 2024

Parkinson’s disease (PD) has a complex etiology involving a combination of environmental and genetic factors. Many of the genes associated with PD point to endolysosomal pathways1,2. ATP13A2 is a P-type ATPase recently identified as a lysosomal exporter of polyamines3. Loss-of-function mutations in ATP13A2 (PARK9) have been linked to Kufor-Rakeb syndrome, a form of early-onset PD and a number of related neurodegenerative disorders, but the exact pathogenetic mechanism remains unclear.

Several efforts have been taken to model ATP13A2 loss-of-function in animals, but until now they were unable to reproduce the severe phenotype observed in human patients. In this issue 2 new similar approaches based on viral vector technology are published4,5.

In the study by Erb et al. recombinant adeno-associated viral vectors (AAV2/5) encoding Cre recombinase were stereotactically injected unilaterally in the substantia nigra of conditional (loxP-flanked) ATP13A2 knockout (KO) mice. This induces adult-onset deletion of the ATP13A2 gene in the transduced nigral cells. As a result, the authors observed a progressive loss of up to 35% dopaminergic (DA) neurons and 48% DA terminals between 3 and 10 months after injection. Also, transient increases in GFAP and Iba-1 were noticed indicative of neuroinflammatory processes, which were largely attenuated by 10 months after injection. In addition, accumulation of LAMP2-positive structures and P62 indicative of lysosomal dysfunction was present, as well as nuclear translocation of the lysosomal stress marker Transcription factor E3 (TFE3) in a subset of transduced cells. No changes in alpha-synuclein (αSyn) phosphorylation or aggregation were evident.

The second paper by Sikora et al. describes a pilot study in non-human primates where lentiviral vectors encoding a short-hairpin RNA directed against ATP13A2 were injected bilaterally in the substantia nigra of adult macaques. The resulting knockdown of ATP13A2 induced a loss of about 30% of DA neurons in the substantia nigra and a concomitant similar loss of DA nerve terminals in the striatum after 5 months. In addition, an increase in phosphorylated αSyn but not total or aggregated αSyn was noted. Also accumulation of LAMP-2 and LC-3-positive vesicles and lipofuscin deposits were present indicative of lysosomal dysfunction. Finally, increased expression of the iron-storage protein ferritin was observed in DA and microglial cells suggestive of altered metal homeostasis.

Interestingly, despite some differences between the two studies, they both independently demonstrate that loss of ATP13A2 induces significant DA neurodegeneration in two distinct animal species. These findings are somewhat at odds with two previously generated full-body ATP13A2 KO mice6,7. These models both display a mild phenotype with modest late-onset motor symptoms, astrogliosis and lipofuscinosis, but in the absence of DA neurodegeneration, even in mice older than 2 years.

The main difference between the studies published in this issue and the germline KO mice can probably be attributed to the viral vector-mediated approach to induce loss-of-function. Indeed, local stereotactic injection of viral vectors can provide an elegant alternative way to overexpress or downregulate genes of interest in the adult brain with several advantages: it represents a fast, flexible and relatively cheap way to modulate gene expression in different brain regions, at different ages and in different animal species or mouse strains. Additionally, this system offers significant flexibility by changing vector systems, doses, promoters, transgenes, and controls. The value of this approach has already been proven in the context of αSyn where most transgenic overexpression mice display no or only mild and late DA neurodegeneration, while several AAV-αSyn-based mouse and rat models have been reported with robust time-dependent DA neurodegeneration, motor deficits and αSyn aggregation8.

A second example more similar to the study by Erb et al. is the adult knock-out of parkin by a lentiviral vector encoding Cre recombinase in the substantia nigra of conditional parkin KO mice, which resulted in a 45% loss of DA neurons after 10 months, whereas the germline parkin KO mice fail to display DA cell loss even with advanced age9,10.

There are a few plausible explanations for the more robust phenotypes observed with a viral vector approach. Acute knock-out or knockdown in adult brain reduces the possibility of compensatory mechanisms during development. Indeed, in parkin KO mice an upregulation of the mitochondrial prosurvival factor, Mcl-1, has been suggested as a possible protective compensatory response11. Second, the viral vector transduction might elicit a mild inflammatory response as a secondary trigger reinforcing the effect of overexpression or downregulation of the specific transgene. Indeed, transient inflammation has been found in the study by Erb et al. as well as in other viral vector-based animal models8. A more detailed characterization by for example single cell RNA sequencing might help to resolve the discrepancies between the different models.

Nevertheless the viral vector-based approach also comes with some disadvantages and challenges : viral vector titers and purity are crucial in order to avoid non-specific effects, toxicity and inflammatory responses. Therefore, careful determination of optimal working titers with proper controls is crucial. In addition, stereotactic injections are labor-intensive and prone to interindividual variability. Correct targeting of the selected brain region, especially a tiny area like the substantia nigra in mice, requires experienced and skilled researchers. Erb et al. have included an appropriate control group of mice injected with AAV-GFP vectors with a group size of 7–12 mice/condition. However, in the study by Sikora at al, the number of animals is limited to 2 macaques (each injected bilaterally) at 1 time point and the characterization of the viral vector transduction in terms of cell type, efficiency and specificity is more limited, presumably because of ethical and cost considerations. As controls, non-injected historical control animals were used, which is suboptimal from an experimental point of view. On the other hand, the study in non-human primate models should be regarded as unique and extremely valuable because it reinforces the translational character of the findings in rodents.

The PD community is undoubtedly looking forward to follow-up manuscripts in the near future including behavioral characterization, more mechanistic studies or therapeutic manipulations.
