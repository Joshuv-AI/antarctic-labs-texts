Non-Hermitian electron-positron annihilation under thermal effects
D. S. Cabral, A. F. Santos, R. Bufalo, N. B. Xavier

Abstract
In this paper we examine the thermal effects into the e + ​ e − → ℓ + ​ ℓ − \displaystyle e^{+}e^{-}\to\ell^{+}\ell^{-} scattering in a non-hermitian extension of QED. We compute the thermal contributions to this scattering cross-section within the Thermo Field Dynamics approach. In order to highlight the non-hermitian effects we have considered some limits of interest: i) zero-temperature limit and high-energy limit and ii) high-temperature regime. Since this type of scattering possesses accurate experimental data for the cross-section (for muon and tau at the final state) it can be used to set stringent bounds upon the non-hermitian parameters.

Introduction

In standard quantum theory, hermiticity is a fundamental property that ensures observables, such as energy, have real eigenvalues, corresponding to physically measurable quantities. This property not only guarantees real energy levels, preventing unphysical complex values, but also preserves the unitarity of the theory, which is essential for probability conservation. The original and tilde spaces are linked through a mapping governed by the tilde conjugation rules. The Bogoliubov transformation acts as a rotation between these two spaces, embedding temperature effects. This approach results in a doubling of degrees of freedom, allowing Green’s functions to be represented in a two-dimensional matrix structure. Notably, the real-time propagators are composed of two parts: one representing the zero-temperature propagator and the other, a temperature-dependent component.

II
Non-hermitian QED

To evaluate scattering amplitudes in the TFD formalism, it is essential to obtain the solutions for the free fields as well as their completeness relations. Therefore, we begin by reviewing the main aspects of non-Hermitian QED
[
15
,
17
]
.

Our starting point is a non-hermitian extension of the free fermionic Lagrangian, given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\overline{\psi}\left[\frac{1}{2}i\gamma^{\alpha}\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial}_{\alpha}-m-\mu\gamma^{5}\right]\psi,

(1)

where the symmetric derivative is defined as
\displaystyle A\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial}_{\alpha}B=A\partial_{\alpha}B-(\partial_{\alpha}A)B
and
\displaystyle\gamma^{5}
denotes the usual axial matrix.
Actually, the presence of an axial mass term
\displaystyle\mu
breaks the Lagrangian hermiticity and induces new and interesting features.

Furthermore, the modified Dirac equation is obtained from the Lagrangian (
1
) and is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(i\gamma^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}-m-\mu\gamma^{5}\right)\psi(x)=0.

(2)

Hence, the dispersion relation for the fermionic field yields an effective mass

\displaystyle\displaystyle M=\sqrt{m^{2}-\mu^{2}},\quad\mu\in\mathbb{R}.

(3)

Besides modifying the dispersion relation in terms of the mass
\displaystyle M
(
3
), the non-hermitian parameter
\displaystyle\mu
controls the effective contribution from the left- and right-handed chiralities (see details in the section
II.1
), so that the model (
1
) has implications to neutrino physics
[
15
,
17
]
.
Moreover, the axial mass in (
1
) is responsible to stem phenomenological couplings, such as the electric dipole and toroidal moments, in the non-relativistic QED
[
31
]
.

As usual, the solutions for the Dirac equation (
2
) can be expanded in plane waves, that is

\displaystyle\displaystyle\psi\left(x\right)=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\,\sum_{s}\int\frac{\text{d}^{3}p}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}}\sqrt{\frac{M}{E_{p}}}\left[a_{p}^{s}u^{s}\left(p\right)e^{-ip\cdot x}+(b_{p}^{s})^{\dagger}\upsilon^{s}\left(p\right)e^{ip\cdot x}\right]

(4)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\,\psi^{+}(x)+\psi^{-}(x),

(5)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{\psi}\left(x\right)=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\,\sum_{s}\int\frac{\text{d}^{3}p}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}}\sqrt{\frac{M}{E_{p}}}\left[b_{p}^{s}\overline{\upsilon}^{s}\left(p\right)e^{-ip\cdot x}+(a_{p}^{s})^{\dagger}\overline{u}^{s}\left(p\right)e^{ip\cdot x}\right]

(6)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\,\overline{\psi}^{+}(x)+\overline{\psi}^{-}(x),

(7)

in which the upper index
\displaystyle(\pm)
corresponds to the positive and negative energy modes, respectively.
Moreover, within the second quantization, we have that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\{a_{p}^{r},(a_{q}^{s})^{\dagger}\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left\{b_{p}^{r},(b_{q}^{s})^{\dagger}\right\}=(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{3}(\vec{p}-\vec{q})\delta_{rs},

(8)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\{a_{p}^{s},(b_{q}^{s})^{\dagger}\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left\{b_{p}^{s},(a_{q}^{s})^{\dagger}\right\}=0

(9)

as the set of operators
\displaystyle(b^{\dagger},b)
and
\displaystyle(a^{\dagger},a)
are related with the fermion and anti-fermion, respectively.

From the solutions (
5
) and (
7
) we find the modified fermionic completeness relations

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{u}(p)=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\,\sum_{s}u^{s}(p)\overline{u}^{s}(p)=\frac{(m-\mu\gamma_{5})\not{p}+M^{2}}{2M^{2}},

(10)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{v}(p)=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\,\sum_{s}\upsilon^{s}(p)\overline{\upsilon}^{s}(p)=\frac{(m-\mu\gamma_{5})\not{p}-M^{2}}{2M^{2}}.

(11)

So that the free fermionic propagator is readily obtained

iS\left(p\right)=i\frac{\not{p}+m-\mu\gamma^{5}}{p^{2}-M^{2}}.

(12)

The easiest way to incorporate the electromagnetic coupling into the Lagrangian (
1
) is to examine its behavior under the local gauge transformations

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}\psi\to\exp\left[i\left(g_{v}+g_{a}\gamma^{5}\right)\theta\right]\psi\\
\overline{\psi}\to\overline{\psi}\exp\left[i\left(-g_{v}+g_{a}\gamma^{5}\right)\theta\right]\ \end{cases}

(13)

implying into

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\to\overline{\psi}\left[\frac{1}{2}i\gamma^{\alpha}\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial}_{\alpha}-\left(m+\mu\gamma^{5}\right)e^{i2g_{a}\gamma^{5}\theta}\right]\psi-\overline{\psi}\gamma^{\alpha}(\partial_{\alpha}\theta)\left(g_{v}+g_{a}\gamma^{5}\right)\psi

(14)

in which the last term clearly breaks the gauge invariance.
Hence, we can define the non-hermitian QED in terms of

\mathcal{L}=\overline{\psi}\left[\frac{1}{2}i\gamma^{\alpha}\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial}_{\alpha}-m-\mu\gamma^{5}\right]\psi+\overline{\psi}\left[\gamma^{\alpha}A_{\alpha}\left(g_{v}+g_{a}\gamma^{5}\right)\right]\psi

(15)

which incorporates two electromagnetic couplings: a vector-axial (V-A) interaction with a coupling
\displaystyle g_{a}
in addition to the electric one
\displaystyle g_{v}
.
Now, the full model (
15
) is invariant, in the massless limit
\displaystyle m=\mu=0
, under the transformations (
13
) and
\displaystyle A_{\mu}\to A_{\mu}-\partial_{\mu}\theta
.

An interesting aspect of the interaction Lagrangian

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}=\overline{\psi}\left[\not{A}\left(g_{v}+g_{a}\gamma^{5}\right)\right]\psi

(16)

is that it is hermitian, satisfying
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}
.
Thus, the non-hermiticity appears only in the free part of the non-hermitian QED, as shown in (
15
).

Finally, the modified interaction vertex
\displaystyle\left\langle\overline{\psi}A\psi\right\rangle
obtained from (
15

\left\langle\overline{\psi}A_{\alpha}\psi\right\rangle=-i\gamma_{\alpha}\left(g_{v}+g_{a}\gamma^{5}\right),

(17)

where the first term represents the usual vector coupling while the second one corresponds to the novel V-A coupling.

In a general context, an alternative term to the V-A coupling emerge from Lorentz violating (LV) QED models
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
in terms of the coupling

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{d}\sim d_{\alpha\beta}\overline{\psi}\gamma^{\alpha}A^{\beta}\gamma^{5}\psi.

(18)

This reproduces the V-A coupling in our case when the LV-tensor is the metric tensor,
\displaystyle d_{\alpha\beta}=\eta_{\alpha\beta}
. However, under this consideration, the similarity no longer signifies a violation of Lorentz symmetry. Moreover, the generation of electric dipole moment in QED can be achieved for the case of
\displaystyle d_{\alpha\beta}=\sigma_{\alpha\beta}
, in which
\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{i}{2}\left[\gamma_{\alpha},\gamma_{\beta}\right]

[
35
]
.

Furthermore, as discussed above, the gauge invariance of the model (
15
) is only manifest for the massless case (
\displaystyle m=\mu=0

Actually, this phenomenon can be understood in terms of the spontaneous symmetry breaking
[

]

We will examine the subject in the next section.

II.1
The problem of the leptonic mass

In order to establish the framework for our discussion about the spontaneous symmetry breaking (SSB) in the non-hermitian model, let us consider the massless Dirac theory (
1
) and define the left- and right-handed spinors

\displaystyle\displaystyle\psi_{L}=\frac{1-\gamma^{5}}{2}\psi,\quad\quad\psi_{R}=\frac{1+\gamma^{5}}{2}\psi,

(19)

where for brevity, we consider a single flavour fermion.
In this case, the model (
1
) takes the form

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{massless}}=\frac{1}{2}i\overline{\psi}_{R}\gamma^{\alpha}\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial}_{\alpha}\psi_{R}+\frac{1}{2}i\overline{\psi}_{L}\gamma^{\alpha}\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial}_{\alpha}\psi_{L}.

(20)

Furthermore, introducing a modified Yukawa interaction
[
17
]
, with a charged bosonic particle
\displaystyle\phi
, and its non-hermitian contribution, as

\mathcal{L}_{\text{Y}}=-G_{v}\left(\overline{\psi}_{L}\phi\psi_{R}+\overline{\psi}_{R}\phi^{\dagger}\psi_{L}\right)-G_{a}\left(\overline{\psi}_{L}\gamma^{5}\phi\psi_{R}+\overline{\psi}_{R}\gamma^{5}\phi^{\dagger}\psi_{L}\right),

(21)

in which
\displaystyle G_{v}
and
\displaystyle G_{a}
are the vector and axial coupling constants, respectively. The chiral term introduced into the model (

[

In addition, we consider that the dynamics of the boson (Higgs) field
\displaystyle\phi
is given by the
\displaystyle\phi^{4}

\mathcal{L}_{\phi}=\left({\partial}_{\alpha}\phi\right)^{\dagger}\left(\partial^{\alpha}\phi\right)-\frac{m_{\phi}^{2}}{2}\phi^{\dagger}\phi-\frac{f}{4}\left(\phi^{\dagger}\phi\right)^{2}.

(22)

Hence, the full model under consideration is written as

\mathcal{L}_{\text{full}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{massless}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{Y}}+\mathcal{L}_{\phi}.

(23)

On the other hand, by construction, when we consider the
\displaystyle U(1)\otimes SU(2)
symmetry, the transformation Eq.(
13

U(1):\exp[-i\frac{(Y_{v}+Y_{a}\gamma^{5})}{2}\theta_{1}];\quad\quad SU(2):\exp[-i\frac{(I_{v}+I_{a}\gamma^{5})}{2}\vec{\sigma}\cdot\vec{\theta}_{2}],

(24)

for the transformation elements under these groups, respectively.

Moreover, on the relations (
24

\displaystyle Y_{v}
and
\displaystyle Y_{a}
are the hypercharges, while
\displaystyle I_{v}
and
\displaystyle I_{a}

\displaystyle z-

It is well-known that these quantities in the QED take the values:
\displaystyle(I_{v},Y_{v})=(-1/2,-1)
to the left-handed and
\displaystyle(I_{v},Y_{v})=(0,-2)
to the right-handed fermion, and also
\displaystyle(I_{v},Y_{v})=(-1/2,-1)

Actually, these quantities are related in terms of the Gell-Mann–Nishijima relation

\displaystyle\displaystyle Q_{v}=I_{v}+\frac{Y_{v}}{2}

(25)

where
\displaystyle Q_{v}
is the electric charge.

It is important to note that an analogous relation can be established for the axial components of the transformations (

\displaystyle Q_{a}
as the chiral charge in such a way that the fields
\displaystyle\psi_{L}
,
\displaystyle\psi_{R}

Therefore, in other words, we can write the following values:
\displaystyle(I_{a},Y_{a})=(0,-2)

\displaystyle(I_{a},Y_{a})=(0,2)

\displaystyle(I_{a},Y_{a})=(0,0)

Taking these results into consideration, we obtain that the fundamental fields transform explicitly as

\psi_{L}\to\exp[i\frac{(1+2\gamma^{5})}{2}\theta_{1}]\psi_{L};\quad\quad\psi_{R}\to\exp[i(1-\gamma^{5})\theta_{1}]\psi_{R};\quad\quad\phi\to\exp[\frac{i}{2}\theta_{1}]\phi

\displaystyle U(1)

\psi_{L}\to\exp[-\frac{i}{4}\vec{\sigma}\cdot\vec{\theta}_{2}]\psi_{L};\quad\quad\psi_{R}\to\psi_{R};\quad\quad\phi\to\exp[-\frac{i}{4}\vec{\sigma}\cdot\vec{\theta}_{2}]\phi

\displaystyle SU(2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\nabla_{\alpha}=\partial_{\alpha}-i(a+b\gamma^{5})X_{\alpha}-i\frac{c}{2}\vec{\sigma}\cdot\vec{Z}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\phi\to\phi+v

\displaystyle v

(29)

At last, by comparing the full SSB model (

\displaystyle\displaystyle m=G_{v}v,\quad\text{and}\quad\mu=G_{a}v.

\displaystyle\phi

We introduce temperature using the TFD formalism, which allows for the analysis and calculation of thermal amplitudes in a manner similar to zero-temperature field theory.

III
Thermo Field dynamics

The main idea behind the TFD formalism is that we can interpret thermal vacuum expectation values of an arbitrary operator as its statistical average
[
29
,
30
]
.
Formally, we can write

\displaystyle\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\cal A}{0(\beta)}=Z^{-1}(\beta)\sum_{n}e^{-\beta E_{n}}\expectationvalue{\cal A}{n}.

(31)

This analysis can be carried out through a thermal ground state
\displaystyle\ket{0(\beta)}
.
The definition of this state is only possible due to the construction of a thermal Hilbert space
\displaystyle{\cal H}_{T}
in terms of the direct product of the original Hilbert space
\displaystyle{\cal H}
and the doubled (tilde) Hilbert space
\displaystyle\tilde{\cal H}
, that is,
\displaystyle{\cal H}_{T}={\cal H}\otimes\tilde{\cal H}

[
29
,
30
]
.

Moreover, this formalism has two essential elements: (i) doubling of variables, essentially due to the introduction of
\displaystyle\tilde{\cal H}
(physically speaking, the second Fock space is interpreted as a heat bath that ensures the system to stay in equilibrium), and (ii) the use of the Bogoliubov transformation, necessary to introduce thermal effects.

A direct consequence of the first observation is that we have the tilde conjugation rules which, for an arbitrary operator
\displaystyle{\cal A}
, are written as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widetilde{\left({\cal A}_{i}{\cal A}_{j}\right)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\tilde{\cal A}_{i}\tilde{\cal A}_{j},\quad\widetilde{\left(c{\cal A}_{i}+{\cal A}_{j}\right)}=c^{*}\widetilde{\cal A}_{i}+\widetilde{\cal A}_{j},

(32)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widetilde{({\cal A}_{i}^{\dagger})}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\tilde{\cal A}_{i}^{\dagger},\quad\widetilde{(\widetilde{\cal A}_{i})}=-\xi{\cal A}_{i},\quad\left[{\cal A}_{i},\widetilde{\cal A}_{j}\right]=0,

(33)

in which
\displaystyle\xi=\pm 1
for fermions and bosons, respectively.
The thermal ground state is formally defined as follows

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ket{0(\beta)}=\mathcal{U}(\beta)\ket{0,\widetilde{0}},

(34)

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\beta)
is the Bogoliubov operator of the thermal transformation, responsible to introduce the temperature, and defined as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\beta)=e^{-iG(\beta)},

(35)

in which
\displaystyle G(\beta)=i\theta(\beta)\left(\widetilde{a}a-a^{\dagger}\widetilde{a}^{\dagger}\right)
.
In this way, considering fermionic fields, the thermal annihilation and creation operators (analogous relations hold for
\displaystyle b(p,\beta)
) are cast as

\displaystyle\displaystyle a\left(p,\beta\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=e^{-iG(\beta)}a(p)e^{iG(\beta)},\quad a^{\dagger}\left(p,\beta\right)=e^{-iG(\beta)}a^{\dagger}(p)e^{iG(\beta)},

(36)

with similar expressions for the tilde operators.
Alternatively, we can expand the operator
\displaystyle U(\beta)
, so that we can rewrite (
36
) as the following

\displaystyle\displaystyle a(p,\beta)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=U(\beta)a(p)-V(\beta)\tilde{a}^{\dagger}(p),\quad a^{\dagger}(p,\beta)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=U(\beta)a^{\dagger}(p)-V(\beta)\tilde{a}(p),

(37)

where we have introduced the functions
\displaystyle(U,V)
in terms of the Fermi-Dirac distribution
\displaystyle n_{F}(E)
, which explicitly read

U(\beta)=\left(1+e^{-\beta E}\right)^{-1/2}=\sqrt{e^{\beta E}n_{F}(E)},\quad V(\beta)=\left(1+e^{\beta E}\right)^{-1/2}=\sqrt{n_{F}(E)}.

(38)

It is worth mentioning that, to obtain these relations for the tilde operators, it is sufficient to apply the tilde conjugation rules (
33
) to the expressions (
37
).

Moreover, the anti-commutation relations for the thermal operators are written as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\{a_{p}^{r}(\beta),(a_{q}^{s}(\beta))^{\dagger}\}=(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{3}(\vec{p}-\vec{q})\delta_{rs},

(39)

have the same form as those for
\displaystyle T=0
given in Eqs. (
8
) and (
9
).

Since we want to compute finite temperature effects into a fermionic scattering, we can consider the above development and formally define the thermal amplitude as

\mathcal{M}\left(\beta\right)=\left\langle f,\beta\left|\hat{S}\right|i,\beta\right\rangle

(40)

where the
\displaystyle\hat{S}
-matrix is given by

\hat{S}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(-i\right)^{n}}{n!}\int d^{4}x_{1}d^{4}x_{2}\ldots d^{4}x_{n}\,\mathcal{T}\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\textrm{int}}\left(x_{1}\right)\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\textrm{int}}\left(x_{2}\right)\ldots\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\textrm{int}}\left(x_{n}\right)\right]

(41)

in which
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}
is the time ordering operator and

\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\textrm{int}}\left(x_{n}\right)=\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{int}}\left(x_{n}\right)-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\textrm{int}}\left(x_{n}\right)

(42)

describes the Lagrangian interaction part in the doubled notation of the TFD formalism.
Finally, we can define the differential cross section, at the centre of mass frame, as usual

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=\frac{|\vec{p}_{i}||\vec{p}_{f}|}{(4\pi s)^{2}}\langle|\mathcal{M}|^{2}\rangle,

(43)

where
\displaystyle\langle|\mathcal{M}|^{2}\rangle
represents the squared modulus of the transition amplitude (
40
), and the Mandelstam variable
\displaystyle s
denotes the square of the center-of-mass energy (invariant mass). In addition,
\displaystyle\vec{p}_{i}
and
\displaystyle\vec{p}_{f}
are the 3-momenta of the initial and final particles, respectively.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ket{f(\beta)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sqrt{2E_{p_{f}}}\sqrt{2E_{k_{f}}}(a^{s_{3}}_{p_{f}}(\beta))^{\dagger}(b^{s_{4}}_{k_{f}}(\beta))^{\dagger}\ket{0(\beta)},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{S}^{(2)}=\frac{(-i)^{2}}{2!}\int d^{4}xd^{4}y\mathcal{T}\left\{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{I}(x)\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{I}(y)\right\}

(46)

which allows us to compute the amplitude (
40
) explicitly

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int d^{4}xd^{4}y\bra{f(\beta)}\mathcal{T}\left\{\overline{\psi}_{x}\left[\not{A}_{x}\left(g_{v}+g_{a}\gamma^{5}\right)\right]\psi_{x}\overline{\psi}_{y}\left[\not{A}_{y}\left(g_{v}+g_{a}\gamma^{5}\right)\right]\psi_{y}\right\}\ket{i(\beta)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-\int d^{4}xd^{4}y\bra{f(\beta)}:\overline{\psi}_{x}^{+}\left[\gamma_{\mu}\left(g_{v}+g_{a}\gamma^{5}\right)\right]\psi_{x}^{+}\overline{\psi}_{y}^{-}\left[\gamma_{\nu}\left(g_{v}+g_{a}\gamma^{5}\right)\right]\psi_{y}^{-}:\ket{i(\beta)}\Delta_{F}^{\mu\nu}(x,y;\beta)

(47)

where
\displaystyle:\ldots:
stands for the usual Wick’s normal ordering, while
\displaystyle\psi^{(\pm)}
and
\displaystyle\overline{\psi}^{(\pm)}
are the fermionic positive and negative energy solutions given in eqs (
5
) and (
7
). Moreover, the gauge field propagator is defined as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta_{F}^{\mu\nu}(x,y;\beta)=-i\int\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}e^{-iq(x-y)}D^{\mu\nu}(q;\beta)

(48)

in which the finite temperature propagator
\displaystyle D^{\mu\nu}(q;\beta)=\eta^{\mu\nu}\Delta_{\beta}(q)
have the following expression
[
30
]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta_{\beta}(q)=\frac{1}{q^{2}}-\frac{2\pi i}{e^{\beta|q_{0}|}-1}\delta(q^{2})

(49)

one can observe that the thermal effects are encoded in the second term.

Hence, evaluating the expectation value in the amplitude (
47
), also considering the above remarks, we are able to obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=-4M_{e}M_{\ell}F(\beta)\overline{v}^{s_{2}}(k_{1})\Gamma_{\mu}u^{s_{1}}(p_{i})\,\Delta_{\beta}(s)\,\overline{u}^{s_{3}}(p_{f})\Gamma^{\mu}v^{s_{4}}(k_{f})

(50)

in which we have defined
\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mu}=\left[\gamma_{\mu}(g_{v}+g_{a}\gamma^{5})\right]
, so that
\displaystyle\left(\overline{u}_{1}\Gamma_{\mu}u_{2}\right)^{\dagger}=\overline{u}_{2}\Gamma_{\mu}u_{1}
.
In addition, all thermal distributions are contained in the factor

\displaystyle\displaystyle F(\beta)=U_{p_{i}}U_{p_{f}}U_{k_{i}}U_{k_{f}}.

(51)

Finally, taking these results into account, we are able to compute the squared modulus of the
transition amplitude

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle|\mathcal{M}|^{2}\rangle=4M_{e}^{2}M_{\ell}^{2}F^{2}(\beta)|\Delta_{\beta}(s)|^{2}\Tr{\mathcal{P}_u(p_i)\Gamma^\mu\mathcal{P}_v(k_i)\Gamma^\nu}\Tr{\mathcal{P}_v(k_f)\Gamma_\mu\mathcal{P}_u(p_f)\Gamma_\nu},

(52)

where the definitions (
10
) and (
11
) have been used.

43

\displaystyle M_{e}
and
\displaystyle M_{\ell}

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell}

\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell}\ll 1

\displaystyle\displaystyle M_{\ell}^{2}=m_{\ell}^{2}(1-\lambda_{\ell}^{2}).

(53)

Thermal cross section

Since we have established a proper procedure to select the leading mass contributions on the (squared) amplitude (
52
), we are now able to compute the differential cross section (
43
).
Moreover, in order to evaluate the integration of the cross-section we will consider the center-of-mass frame, in which the Mandelstam variables are cast

\displaystyle\displaystyle s=E_{\rm cm}^{2}\qquad\text{and}\quad\quad t=m_{\ell}^{2}-\frac{\sqrt{s}}{2}\left(\sqrt{s}-2p_{f}\cos\theta\right).

(58)

Hence, under these considerations, we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\right)_{\beta}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle{F^{2}(\beta)}\left[\frac{1}{s^{2}}+\frac{(2\pi s)^{2}\delta^{2}(s)}{(e^{\beta\sqrt{s}}-1)^{2}}\right]\left(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\right).

(59)

This expression showcases the strength of the TFD formalism, because up to the moment we have used standard canonical methods to compute the transition amplitude, and naturally the thermal contributions were separated as a factor.
The thermal factor in this frame of reference is

\displaystyle\displaystyle F(\beta)=\frac{1}{4}\biggl[1+\tanh{\biggl(\frac{\beta\sqrt{s}}{2}\biggr)}\biggr]^{2}.

(60)

Furthermore, the non-thermal part of (
59
) is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\right)=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\,\frac{g_{a}^{4}\varepsilon}{128\pi^{2}s}\left[s\left(2\lambda_{\ell}^{2}+1\right)\Xi_{11}(2\theta)-2m_{\ell}^{2}\left(2\Xi_{11}(2\theta)+\lambda_{\ell}^{2}\Xi_{12}(2\theta)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\frac{(g_{a}^{3}g_{v}+g_{a}g_{v}^{3})\varepsilon}{32\pi^{2}s}\lambda_{\ell}\left[4\Xi_{00}(\theta)\left(2m_{\ell}^{2}\Xi_{00}(\theta)-\varepsilon\right)-s\Xi_{11}(2\theta)\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\frac{g_{a}^{2}g_{v}^{2}}{64\pi^{2}\varepsilon}\Big[16m_{\ell}^{4}\left(\lambda_{\ell}^{2}+2\right)\Xi_{00}^{2}(\theta)+(2\lambda_{\ell}^{2}+1)s\left(8\varepsilon\Xi_{00}(\theta)+s\Xi_{11}(2\theta)\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-2m_{\ell}^{2}\lambda_{\ell}^{2}\left(16\varepsilon\Xi_{00}(\theta)+5s\Xi_{11}(2\theta)-4s\right)+16\varepsilon\Xi_{00}(\theta)+4s\left(\Xi_{11}(2\theta)-1\right)\Big]

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\frac{g_{v}^{4}}{128\pi^{2}\varepsilon}\Big[8m_{\ell}^{4}\left(\lambda_{\ell}\Xi_{13}(2\theta)+4\left(\Xi_{00}^{2}(\theta)-1\right)\right)-2sm_{\ell}^{2}\left(8\Xi_{00}^{2}(\theta)+5\lambda_{\ell}^{2}\Xi_{11}(2\theta)\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+s^{2}(\lambda_{\ell}^{2}+1)\Xi_{13}(2\theta)\Big]

(61)

in which we can observe that the modified electron mass
\displaystyle m_{e}
is absent due to our previous discussion (i.e.
\displaystyle m_{e}\ll m_{\ell}
and
\displaystyle\lambda_{e}\ll\lambda_{\ell}
). Here, we have defined, by simplicity of notation, the angular dependence in terms of the quantity

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Xi_{ab}(\theta)=\cos(\theta)+a\left(2b+1\right),

(62)

and the squared energy quantity

\displaystyle\displaystyle\varepsilon=\sqrt{s\left(s-4m_{\ell}^{2}\right)}.

(63)

Finally, the remaining angular integration over the solid angle
\displaystyle d\Omega
in the expresion (
59
) can be readily performed, so that the cross-section for this scattering reads

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sigma_{\beta}(s)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle{F^{2}(\beta)}\frac{\chi(s)}{s^{2}}

(64)

in which the factor
\displaystyle\chi(s)
has the following expression

\displaystyle\displaystyle\chi(s)=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\,\frac{g_{a}^{4}\varepsilon}{12\pi s}\left[4m_{\ell}^{2}\left(\lambda_{\ell}^{2}-1\right)+s\left(\lambda_{\ell}^{2}+1\right)\right]-\frac{g_{a}^{3}g_{v}+g_{v}^{3}g_{a}}{3\pi s}\lambda_{\ell}\left[\varepsilon(s-m_{\ell}^{2})\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\frac{g_{a}^{2}g_{v}^{2}}{6\pi\varepsilon}\left[2m_{\ell}^{4}(\lambda_{\ell}^{2}+2)-sm_{\ell}^{2}(7\lambda_{\ell}^{2}+5)+s^{2}(2\lambda_{\ell}^{2}+1)\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\frac{g_{v}^{4}}{12\pi\varepsilon}\left[4m_{\ell}^{4}\left(5\lambda_{\ell}^{2}-2\right)-2sm_{\ell}^{2}\left(5\lambda_{\ell}^{2}+1\right)+s^{2}\left(2\lambda_{\ell}^{2}+1\right)\right].

(65)

It is important to note that the result obtained here from Eq.(
65
), is general and incorporates QED corrections by introducing temperature effects and non-Hermiticity through the axial-vector coupling constant
\displaystyle g_{a}
and the chiral parameter correction
\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell}
(up to second order). To validate this framework, it is essential to analyze all relevant limits, including those associated with temperature and non-Hermitian modifications.

Some remarks about the cross section (
64
) are in order: In one hand, in the zero-temperature limit (
\displaystyle\beta\to\infty
) we have that
\displaystyle F(\beta)=1
and the expression (
64
) becomes

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sigma_{\beta\to\infty}(s)=\frac{\chi(s)}{s^{2}}.

(66)

In addition, within the high energy limit (
\displaystyle\sqrt{s}\gg m_{\ell}
, which effectively yields
\displaystyle m_{\ell}\to 0
), the non-thermal vertex correction will given exclusively by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sigma_{\rm HE}=\lim_{m_{\ell}\to 0}\frac{\chi(s)}{s^{2}}=\frac{1}{s}\Delta(\alpha,\alpha_{a},\lambda_{\ell})

(67)

in which we have defined

\Delta(\alpha,\alpha_{a},\lambda_{\ell})=\frac{4\pi}{3}\left(\alpha+\alpha_{a}\right)\left[(\alpha+\alpha_{a})-4\sqrt{\alpha\alpha_{a}}\lambda_{\ell}+2(\alpha+\alpha_{a})\lambda_{\ell}^{2}\right],

(68)

where
\displaystyle\alpha=g_{v}^{2}/4\pi
and
\displaystyle\alpha_{a}=g_{a}^{2}/4\pi
represents the usual QED and axial fine structure constants, respectively.
One can also observe that the first term corresponds to the usual QED contribution
[
36
,
40
]
, while the remaining are corrections due to the additional axial mass and V-A coupling.

On the other hand, at the high-temperature regime (in which
\displaystyle\beta\sqrt{s}\ll 1
) we have the following leading thermal behavior

\sigma_{\rm high-T}\simeq\frac{1}{8}\Delta(\alpha,\alpha_{a},\lambda_{\ell})\left[\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{s}}+\frac{3\beta^{2}}{4}+\cdots\right],

(69)

where temperature independent terms were discarded.
We see a significant departure (in terms of its dependence on the squared energy
\displaystyle s
) of the high-temperature expression (
69
) when compared with the
\displaystyle T=0
result (
67
).

Notice that we have establish bounds on the thermal expressions of the cross-section, allowing us to estimate the chiral parameters at a given temperature.

To accurately determine these limits, a comparison with experimental data is required. Moreover, since the cross-section for the scattering process
\displaystyle e^{+}e^{-}\to\ell^{+}\ell^{-}
is known with high precision for final states involving muon and tau particles-but without accounting for temperature effects-we use the zero-temperature expression (
67
), the experimental cross-section data, and the values of
\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell}
from Table
1
to place stringent bounds on the V-A coupling
\displaystyle g_{a}
.

VI
Conclusions

In this work we have considered thermal effects into the
\displaystyle e^{+}e^{-}\to\ell^{+}\ell^{-}
scattering described by a non-hermitian extension of the QED within the Thermo Field Dynamics approach.
We started by presenting a modified Dirac equation in terms of a non-hermitian axial mass, in which we have determined their plane waves solutions as well as the modified fermionic completeness relations.
As usual, the couplings were introduced in terms of the gauge invariance, which is only manifest in the massless limit.
Actually, since this phenomenon can be understood in terms of the spontaneous symmetry breaking, we revised the Higgs mechanism applied to this non-hermitian model.
It is worth to mention that in this non-hermitian QED, the free fermionic propagator and also the vertex function were both modified by an axial mass and a V-A coupling, respectively.

Since the TFD formalism allows to compute transition amplitudes exactly as in the
\displaystyle T=0
field theory, by doubling the set of operators and its respective Fock space (where the
second set acts like a heat bath), we have explicitly evaluated the scattering matrix element related
with the process
\displaystyle e^{+}e^{-}\to\ell^{+}\ell^{-}
, showing in details how the temperature effects are incorporated.
Nonetheless, the cross section for this scattering gives an extensive expression, hence it was necessary to consider approximations on the expression (
52
) in order to select the leading contributions.
We implemented this idea by treating the non-expansion-based parameter
\displaystyle\mu
as a small perturbation, which allows us to obtain bounds on the non-Hermiticity through the introduced parameter
\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell}
.
So that we can use the parametrization
\displaystyle\mu_{\ell}=\lambda_{\ell}m_{\ell}
, with
\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell}
being a small parameter
\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell}\ll 1
; which allowed us to obtain bounds upon the non-hermitian parameter
\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell}
and also led to a (flavor) hierarchy
\displaystyle\lambda_{e}\ll\lambda_{\mu}\ll\lambda_{\tau}
on the leading contributions, allowing us to consider
\displaystyle M_{e}\to 0
on the calculations.

After computing explicitly the cross-section (for an arbitrary flavor final state) we have considered some limits of interest in order to highlight its physical behavior: i) since the experimental data for the
\displaystyle e^{+}e^{-}\to\ell^{+}\ell^{-}
