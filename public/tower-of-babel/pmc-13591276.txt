When nurses are held accountable for AI-driven clinical decisions, they must be engaged early and meaningfully
Authors: Meghan Reading Turchioe, Ann Wieben, Carolyn Sun, Christina Baker, Benjamin J Galatzan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHealth systems are adopting AI-powered tools faster than policy can address the implications for clinician liability. Nurses, as the largest healthcare profession and the most proximal clinicians to patients, must decide whether to accept, modify, or override AI-derived recommendations, often while managing competing cognitive demands. Yet the question of who bears responsibility when AI contributes to patient harm remains unresolved in policy and case law. At the 2025 American Medical Informatics Association Annual Symposium, a structured debate examined whether nurses should be held liable for AI-assisted clinical decisions. Proponents argued that professional ethics, existing tort precedent, and the nurse's role as human-in-the-loop require accountability. Opponents countered that AI opacity, unfair liability distribution, and limited nurse agency in AI procurement preclude fair responsibility assignment. Two reforms are essential: meaningful nurse engagement throughout the AI lifecycle and nursing-specific considerations in AI legislation and regulatory frameworks.

Introduction

Health systems are adopting AI-powered tools to support care delivery, including generative (eg, drafting notes, patient education) and predictive (eg, risk stratification) AI, faster than policy can regulate the implications.1 This creates holes in the patchwork of safeguards put in place to prevent AI-related harms (Figure 1). For frontline clinicians, a critical question remains unresolved: when AI-driven tools contribute to patient harm, who bears legal and professional responsibility?

This question is particularly urgent for nurses, who comprise the largest healthcare profession and spend the most time at the bedside. Nurses provide continuous monitoring, execute care plans, administer medications, and respond to changes in patient status, positioning them as the most proximal clinician to the patient and the last line of defense. In modern US healthcare, nurses are positioned as the final “layer of Swiss cheese” in the accident causation model2 before errors reach patients. When AI is integrated into these workflows, nurses must decide whether to accept, modify, or override AI-derived recommendations, often while managing multiple competing, cognitively demanding tasks. Questions of liability in nursing practice are closely linked to professional and ethical obligations that help define standards of care and expectations for safer practice. This commentary considers ethical issues insofar as they shape professional accountability for AI-assisted clinical decisions.

The question of clinician liability has yet to be directly addressed in policy or case law. Legal scholars expect courts will ask not only whether harm occurred but also whether the healthcare professional met the applicable standard of care, acknowledging that this standard is ambiguous when AI becomes embedded in clinical workflows.3 Does following AI-derived recommendations constitute the standard, or does contesting it? This is complicated by several thorny questions: Is the reasoning behind the AI recommendation interpretable? Do nurses have the AI literacy to apply it appropriately but contest it when necessary?

In November 2025, at the American Medical Informatics Association's Annual Symposium, an audience of informaticists, scientists, and clinicians gathered to wrestle with this question in a debate titled “To Err Is AI: Who Bears the Blame for AI-Driven Nursing Decisions?” The debate focused on the statement: “Nurses should be held liable for AI-assisted clinical decisions.” Below, we outline arguments from both sides and the implications for policy and AI development.

The affirmative case: nurses must be held accountable as part of their professional responsibility

Proponents advanced several arguments grounded in professional ethics, existing tort law, and patient safety principles. First, AI systems function as decision support tools, not decision-makers; the prevailing model is AI to recommendation, clinician to final decision. Nurses cannot defer blindly to algorithmic output; they must integrate it with patient assessment and professional standards.4 The ANA Code of Ethics reinforces this: Provision 4.2 states that nurses remain responsible for decisions made when assisted by technology, while Provision 7.5 requires nurses to critically evaluate innovations to ensure alignment with nursing values.5

Second, liability reinforces accountability and maintains professional standards. Courts assess whether clinicians meet customary standards of care when using technology. This is already well-established in nursing malpractise law for medical equipment—nurses can be held liable for failing to properly use IV pumps, respond to monitor alarms, or ensure devices are functioning correctly. Proponents argued AI follows the same precedent.6

Third, nurses serve as the “human-in-the-loop,” a role that carries both authority and responsibility. If a nurse administers medication based on a flawed AI dosing recommendation without verifying it against clinical assessment, the nurse cannot escape responsibility simply because the AI suggested the action. As one debater noted: “Liability is not punishment—it's a recognition of the nurse's vital role in patient care and decision-making.” Product-liability gaps and system opacity do not erase nursing accountability; if anything, they increase the likelihood that plaintiffs pursue clinicians and hospitals.

The opposing case: structural inequities preclude fair liability assignment

Opponents presented arguments rooted in AI opacity, unfair liability distribution, and undermined nurse agency. Current AI systems often function as “black boxes” that provide recommendations without explaining how outputs were derived. Nurses struggle to detect AI errors when they lack insight into training data, algorithmic logic, or system limitations. One debater noted that when proprietary training data remains unavailable, nurses cannot assess whether a tool will work appropriately for specific patient populations, and tool limitations are sometimes not fully disclosed until after implementation.

The second pillar addressed liability distribution. Responsibility should rest primarily with AI developers who create these tools and health systems that select and deploy them. Developers may attempt to shield themselves through licensing terms that shift liability to clinicians—an unjust arrangement when system limitations remain undisclosed.7 Health systems bear a duty to ensure adequate staff training but often fall short. Debaters cited Epic's Sepsis Model, implemented across hundreds of hospitals, which not only missed actual sepsis cases but also generated numerous false alarms, contributing to nursing burnout.8 As one debater summarized: “You cannot ask a nurse to be responsible for the data they are given if the thing generating the data is unreliable in the first place.”

The third pillar addressed nurse agency: assigning responsibility without corresponding authority is fundamentally unjust. Nurses typically lack input into AI selection and procurement decisions. California nurses held protests against AI tools they felt negatively impacted their work and endangered patients in an attempt to reclaim moral authority over their practice. ANA Code of Ethics Provision 7.5 not only establishes nurse accountabilities related to AI but also states that the nursing voice must be present when healthcare system decisions are made—yet clinician representation in AI governance remains inadequate, with nursing involvement significantly rarer than physician involvement.5

Implications

The discussion illuminated fundamental challenges in AI governance: regulatory gaps that leave clinicians exposed, power imbalances in procurement decisions, and the absence of the nursing voice in AI development. During audience discussion after the debate, a practicing nurse shared a powerful near-miss medication error on a neonate caused by a pump failure, which was identified through her vigilance, that encapsulated the core tension: nurses understand professional duty yet operate in systems with imperfect tools and inadequate safeguards. Perhaps most striking was the audience opinion shift: pre-debate polling showed a split audience, but post-debate polling revealed 75% of attendees agreed nurses should be held accountable for AI-assisted decisions. This exposes how nurses risk becoming “liability sinks”—absorbing responsibility for system failures while lacking control over AI design, selection, or implementation.9

If nurses are to be held liable, 2 critical reforms must occur. First, nurses must be engaged early, meaningfully, and often throughout the AI lifecycle, not merely consulted as a box to check. This requires representation on vendor selection committees with genuine decision-making authority, participation in AI governance, leadership in developing nursing-specific competencies, and authority to escalate performance concerns. Second, stronger advocacy is needed for nursing-specific considerations in AI legislation. As we previously reported, the current landscape comprises a patchwork of state laws, most failing to address nursing specifically.10 Regulatory frameworks should promote transparency, require disclosure of known limitations, support post-market surveillance, align liability with actual control, require competency-based training, and protect clinicians who report AI-related problems. Expectations for nurses’ legal and professional accountability in AI-assisted care must be matched by investments in AI literacy, competency-based education, and ongoing professional development.

If we expect nurses to critically evaluate AI recommendations, we must provide transparent systems, adequate training, and meaningful input into technology decisions. The path forward requires a multi-stakeholder partnership with nurses for patient safety and professional fairness, and successful AI integration into healthcare delivery.
