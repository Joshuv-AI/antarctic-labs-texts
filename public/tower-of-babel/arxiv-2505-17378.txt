Some of the most exciting properties of solids arise from strong collective interactions among electrons, spins, and the crystal lattice.
The emergent effects born out of such strong interactions are abundant and drive the formation of varied electronic and magnetic phases.
At the vanguard of current interest and debate is the question whether the strong interaction of the quantum fluctuations of electromagnetic modes in photonic cavities or meta-structures with elementary excitations in solids
can also prompt phase transitions and give rise to new quantum states of matter.
A grand aspiration of cavity quantum materials research is to uncover fundamentally new routes for controlling properties of matter by judiciously tailoring its quantum electromagnetic environment

In this work, we examine how superconductivity is altered at the hBN/

Meissner force probe of resonant and non-resonant interfaces

Magnetic force microscopy (MFM) probes the local superfluid density via the repulsive Meissner force experienced by a magnetized tip at the end of a cantilever oscillating above the surface of a superconductor
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
]

The force results from the supercurrent screening the stray magnetic field of the tip.
Notably, the Meissner force is not affected by inserting nonmagnetic materials between the tip and the superconductor, and
the data in Fig.
1
(a,b) attest that MFM probes superconductors encapsulated by insulating layers.
We measure the shift in the resonant frequency of the cantilever, which is proportional to the force gradient
\partial_{z}F_{z}

[
40
]
.
Analysis of
\partial_{z}F_{z}
allows extraction of the superfluid density
\rho_{0}

[
37
]
(Methods and Supplementary Information Section 4).

Next, we investigate resonant mode coupling across the hBN/
\kappa
-ET interface.
The hBN crystal hosts hyperbolic phonon polaritons (HPhPs) that exhibit light-like dispersion near the transverse optical (TO) frequency and phonon-like dispersion near the longitudinal optical (LO) frequency
[
15
]
. Here, TO and LO refer specifically to the in-plane modes.
We examine the HPhP dispersion upon interfacing with
\kappa
-ET through the calculated imaginary part of the p-polarized Fresnel reflection
\operatorname{Im}r_{p}
, as shown in Fig.
2
a (Supplementary Information Section 6). For free-standing hBN, the HPhP dispersion has a series of branches between the TO and LO phonon frequencies, each with a continuous dispersion
[
22
]
.
By contrast, for an hBN/
\kappa
-ET heterostructure, the HPhP branches are interrupted near the C=C stretching frequency.
Avoided crossings are visible, yet the gaps in the HPhP dispersion are not fully opened due to broadening caused by dielectric loss associated with the in-plane metallic response of
\kappa
-ET
[
45
,
46
]
.

To experimentally examine the mode coupling at the hBN/
\kappa
-ET interface, we use scattering-type scanning near-field optical microscopy (s-SNOM)
[
47
]
. Specifically, we evaluate the hBN HPhP dispersion as these modes couple to the C=C stretching mode of
\kappa
-ET. We illuminated the metallized tip of an atomic force microscope with a frequency-tunable IR laser. The strong electric field with high momentum achieved near the tip apex launches polariton waves. We performed nano-IR measurements at
T=50
K on a region of a hBN/
\kappa
-ET heterostructure with 32 nm hBN on 100 nm
\kappa
-ET.
Fringes arising from the interference of propagating HPhPs were observed in the nano-IR images: representative results are shown in Fig.
2
b for
\omega=1477.6
cm
-1
.
s-SNOM line profiles collected with varying incident light frequency show that the HPhP wavelength changes with photon energy (Fig.
2
c).
In free-standing hBN, the real-space fringes arising from HPhP interference evolve smoothly with frequency
[
48
]
. However, in our data on
\kappa
-ET-supported hBN, the fringe evolution exhibits a kink near the C=C frequency, indicated by the solid line tracking the fifth peak in Fig
2
c.
These nano-IR data attest to mode coupling across the hBN-
\kappa
-ET interface.

To gain insight into the experimentally observed mode coupling, we take a closer look at the interplay between the zero-point fluctuations of the hBN HPhP and the C=C stretching mode. First-principles molecular Langevin dynamics simulations indicate that the coupling arises from the interaction of the transverse component of the HPhP’s electric field with the C=C stretching mode.
The C=C stretching mode exhibits a dipole moment that is directed primarily out-of-plane (Fig
2
d and Supplementary Information Section 2).
Importantly, the HPhP field has an out-of-plane component that can couple to the C=C mode (Fig.
2
d). The combined contributions of many HPhP branches outspreading to high momenta make this field extend far beyond the hBN surface.
We calculated the C=C amplitude in the presence of an oscillating out-of-plane electric field with a frequency spectrum spanning the C=C frequency (fundamental frequency
\omega_{0}=1440
cm
-1
). Langevin molecular dynamics analysis carried out at
T=2
K (Supplementary Information Section 7) shows that the out-of-plane electric field induced by the zero-point HPhP fluctuations reduces the C=C amplitude and splits the peak in Fig.
2
e.
We therefore conclude that vacuum fluctuations—specifically, zero-point phonon motion—mediate the coupling between hyperbolic phonon polaritons and molecular vibrations without the need for external photons, thus enabling interaction in the dark.

Hyperbolic cavity electrodynamics

The data in Figs. 1-4 demonstrate that cavity electrodynamics plays a pivotal role in suppressing the superfluid density - a hallmark equilibrium property of superconductivity.
These experiments establish a precedent for successfully altering a material’s ground-state thermodynamic property through cavity engineering. Our specific implementation of the resonant structure reveals two notable features of hyperbolic cavity control.
First, hyperbolic modes entail both in-plane and out-of-plane components of the fluctuating electromagnetic fields. The out-of-plane component is crucial for coupling to the out-of-plane dipole moment of the C=C stretching mode in the
\kappa
-ET superconductor under investigation. Second, the multiple branches of hyperbolic modes span a broad momentum range, enabling continuous interaction with the weakly dispersing C=C resonance across the entire
(\omega,q)
phase space, as shown in Fig
2
a. Future experiments need to evaluate the spatial extent of the mode coupling away from the hyperbolic interface; our earlier data for a similar resonant hBN/MoO
3
heterostructure suggest that the relevant length scale may exceed
\sim 10^{2}
nm
[
49
]
.

Our data and modeling suggest a plausible pathway for cavity enhancement of superconducting properties. Indeed, our simulations indicate that the hBN cavity subdues the C=C stretching mode fluctuations prompting the stark suppression of superfluid density. By the same token, boosting fluctuations through mode softening in the cavity
[
1
]
, may increase the superfluid density and possibly
T_{\textrm{c}}
in
\kappa
-ET.
Furthermore, the concept of employing readily reconfigurable vdW hyperbolic cavities for resonant manipulations of materials properties is rather general.
We remind our reader that vdW crystals host hyperbolic modes of phononic, plasmonic and exitonic origin
[
50
]
spanning the range from THz to visible thus offering limitless options for hyper-cavity engineering.

Methods

Device fabrication

Heterostructures were fabricated by placing vdW materials, on the order of 10 nm to 120 nm thick, on large flat surfaces of
\kappa
-ET single crystals (ac crystallographic plane, Supplementary Information Section 2).
Measurements were performed on five devices (Supplementary Information Section 3). Data in Fig
1
is from device 1,
Fig
2
from device 2,
Fig
3
(b-d) from device 1, and
Fig
3
a from device 5.
Fig
4
is a compilation of results from devices 1, 3, and 4.

The
\kappa
-ET crystal surfaces were either as-grown (devices 1 and 3), exposed by cleaving bulk crystals (devices 4 and 5), or exposed by exfoliating single crystals (device 2). For bulk samples, the
\kappa
-ET single crystal was fixed onto a silicon substrate using either epoxy or silver paint. For devices 4 and 5, the crystal was then cleaved to expose new surfaces. Large atomically flat surfaces were then selected by optical and atomic force microscopy. As-grown surfaces were regularly found to be atomically flat. Cleaved surfaces showed larger variations in surface roughness.

Both hBN and RuCl
3
flakes were exfoliated using a standard scotch-tape technique onto a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) stamp and identified via optical and atomic force microscopy. The stacking process was performed using a commercial transfer stage with temperature control. The PDMS stamp was gently pressed onto the
\kappa
-ET surface and then slowly retracted.
The hBN flakes were released at approximately 60°C, while the RuCl
3
flakes were released at around 110°C.
Isotopically pure hBN (
10
BN) was used to achieve a narrow linewidth for the TO phonon. The narrow linewidth makes HPhPs visible in s-SNOM.

hBN/BSCCO samples were prepared by exfoliating and assembling BSCCO and hBN ctystals in a golvebox filled with inert gas (
\mathrm{O_{2}}<0.1
ppm;
\mathrm{H_{2}O<0.1}
ppm). BSCCO was first exfoliated on SiO
2
substrates. hBN flakes were then exfoliated on a PDMS stamp and transferred onto BSCCO at room temperature.

s-SNOM

The nano-infrared scattering experiments were performed using a home-built cryogenic scattering-type scanning near-field optical microscope (s-SNOM) housed in an ultra-high vacuum chamber with a base pressure of
\sim 7\times 10^{-11}
torr. The s-SNOM is based on a tapping-mode atomic force microscope (AFM) with the tapping frequency and amplitude of the AFM set to approximately 285 kHz and 70 nm, respectively. The s-SNOM operates by scattering tightly focused laser light from a sharp AFM tip, enabling near-field interactions to be probed with nanometer-scale resolution. The laser source is a tunable quantum cascade laser from Daylight Solutions. The laser beam was focused onto the metallized AFM tip using a parabolic mirror with a 12-mm focal length. The back-scattered light was registered by a mercury cadmium telluride detector and demodulated following a pseudoheterodyne scheme. The signal was demodulated at the
n
th harmonic of the tip tapping frequency, yielding background-free near-field signal. In this work, we selected
n
=3 and 4 to eliminate the far-field background.

MFM

MFM measurements were performed in an Attocube cantilever-based cryogenic AFM (attoAFM I with attoLIQUID 2000 cryostat), where the microscope sits in a helium exchange gas
at low temperature. Nanosensors PPP-MFMR probes with hard magnetic coating and force constants near
\mathrm{k}=2.8
N/m were used for all measurements.
Measurements were taken on different samples using different tips, all with resonance frequencies ranging from 75.5 kHz to 79.4 kHz.
Measurements recorded the resonant frequency shift of the probe cantilever (
\mathrm{\Delta f}
) and were converted to
\partial_{z}F_{z}
via
\partial_{z}F_{z}=-2\mathrm{k}/\mathrm{f_{0}}\times\mathrm{\Delta f}

[
40
]
, using the force constants
k
provided by the manufacturer for each cantilever and the measured resonant frequency far from the sample surface,
f_{0}
.
The tip oscillation amplitude was typically 50 nm - 70 nm. hBN is non-magnetic and therefore does not impact the detection of the Meissner effect over hBN/
\kappa
-ET. RuCl
3
is antiferromagnetic at low temperature, but has a low susceptibility.

To minimize noise and instrument drift,
\Delta f(z)
was typically averaged over multiple measurements repeated at each point and taken with high
z
resolution (typically 1-2 nm to define the surface accurately). During the measurements, the tip was electrically biased to compensate for the local contact potential difference and thus minimize electrostatic forces. The average curves were then smoothed with a uniform filter with width 30 to 50 pixels to reduce the appearance of noise. For the
\kappa
-ET heterostructures, a linear background was subtracted by fitting the data far from the sample surface where the Meissner force is negligible. For the BSCCO sample, a background measured on the silicon substrate was subtracted (Supplementary Information Section 5).
For proper comparison in Fig
1
, we have compensated for the thickness of the hBN and RuCl
3
such that the measurements for hBN/
\kappa
-ET, bare
\kappa
-ET, and RuCl
3
/
\kappa
-ET all have the
\kappa
-ET surface at zero.

Constant height MFM images were taken with the tip at a fixed height above the plane of the
\kappa
-ET surface. The images shown are raw data, except for the linear conversion from
\Delta f
to
\partial_{z}F_{z}
and an overall constant offset to calculate
\Delta\partial_{z}F_{z}
. During the measurements, the tip was held at a fixed electric bias to compensate for the contact potential difference at one point in the field of view.

The data points in Fig.
4
were obtained either by fitting
\partial_{z}F_{z}(z)
curves or by converting constant height MFM images to maps of the local
\lambda_{\mathrm{in}}
, the decay length for currents running parallel to the surface.
\lambda_{\mathrm{in}}
was converted to superfluid density via the proportionality to
1/\lambda_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}
, as described in Supplementary Information Section 4.

HPhP dispersion simulation

The HPhP dispersion was calculated using the transfer matrix method. The dispersion is represented by peaks in the imaginary part of the Fresnel reflectivity of the device for p-polarized light,
r_{p}
. In the dispersion calculation, we consider the real-valued frequencies and momentum. For coupling analysis, the eigenmodes are found by determining the poles of
r_{p}
, where the complex frequencies and real momentum are considered.
