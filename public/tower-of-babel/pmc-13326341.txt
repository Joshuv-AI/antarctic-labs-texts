Comparison of 3D extraoral scanning of artex articulator scan vs scan fixator in a fully edentulous arch – in-vitro study
Authors: Turki S Alkhallagi, Badr K Alshammari, Walaa A Babeer, Abdulrahman J Alhaddad, Khadijah M Baik, Thamer Y Marghalani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis in vitro study compared the precision of articulator-based scanning and scan fixator-based scanning in completely edentulous arches with implant scan bodies.Materials and methodsUpper and lower master casts were fabricated from a typodont, and six implant replicas were placed in each arch. Each configuration was scanned ten times using an extraoral desktop scanner, and all scans were exported as STL files. Clinical relevance

Scan fixator is an accessory used with extraoral desktop scanners to digitize mounted conventional models with acceptable clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Digital workflows have become integral to contemporary implant prosthodontics, offering improved consistency, accuracy, and efficiency in clinical and laboratory procedures [1, 2]. A critical step in this workflow is the acquisition of accurate digital impressions [3]. This step enables precise recording of implant positions and their spatial relationships within the arch [3, 4]. This is particularly important in completely edentulous cases, where accurate transfer of implant positions is essential for the fabrication of definitive prostheses [2, 4].

Beyond data acquisition, the accurate transfer of maxillomandibular relationships remains a key requirement for successful prosthesis fabrication. Mounting models on an articulator allows for the evaluation and simulation of occlusal relationships and mandibular movements, which are critical for achieving functional and harmonious prosthetic outcomes [5–7]. In digital workflows, this step is replicated using virtual articulators, which simulate conventional articulator functions and are increasingly incorporated into digital prosthetic design [1, 8].

Despite these advancements, the method used to transfer mounted models into the digital environment may influence the accuracy of the virtual articulation. Extraoral desktop scanners are commonly used to digitize conventional models, either while mounted on an articulator or using alternative mounting devices such as scan fixators [3, 5]. However, differences in laboratory procedures and equipment between these techniques may affect the accuracy of the resulting digital models, particularly in completely edentulous arches with implant scan bodies.

To date, there is a lack of published studies evaluating the differences between scanning conventional models mounted on an articulator and those using a scan fixator. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the precision of extraoral 3D scanning using an articulator versus a scan fixator in completely edentulous arches with implant scan bodies. This investigation seeks to provide evidence that may guide the selection of scanning techniques and laboratory devices in digital implant workflows.

The null hypothesis was that there is no statistically significant difference in the precision of 3D extraoral scanning between the articulator and the scan fixator in edentulous arches with implant scan bodies.

The alternative hypothesis states that there is a statistically significant difference in the precision of 3D extraoral scanning of the articulator versus the scan fixator in edentulous arches with implant scan bodies.

Materials and methods

Maxillary and mandibular edentulous Kavo typodontic models (KaVo, Biberach, Germany) were selected for replication using silicone duplication (BEGO GmbH & Co. KG, Bremen, Germany). The silicone mold was poured with dental stone type 4 (Kulzer GmbH, Hanau, Germany) to produce maxillary and mandibular models, which were allowed to set. Both models were drilled using a straight handpiece with a fissure bur (Pearson Dental, Sylmar, CA, USA) at six areas: the first molars, first premolars, and central incisors in the maxilla and first molars, first premolars, and canines in the mandible.

Implant replicas (Straumann, Basel, Switzerland) were placed 1 mm below the model surface and stabilized using dental stone type 4 (Kulzer GmbH, Hanau, Germany). Impression posts (Straumann, Basel, Switzerland) were connected to the implant replicas. Final impressions were made using the open-tray technique with a large stock tray and monophase impression material (Kulzer GmbH, Hanau, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Implant replicas (Straumann, Basel, Switzerland) were connected to the impression posts. A 2-mm gingival mask (Zhermack SpA, Badia Polesine, Italy) was applied, and the impressions were poured with dental stone type 4 (Kulzer GmbH, Hanau, Germany) to obtain the master models.

The master models were mounted on an Artex articulator CPR (Amann Girrbach GmbH, Pforzheim, Germany) using plaster of Paris (Kulzer GmbH, Hanau, Germany) with a hand articulation technique. A Polyvinyl siloxanes (PVS) putty index (Kulzer GmbH, Hanau, Germany) was used to standardize positioning and stabilization. Implant scanbodies (Straumann, Basel, Switzerland) were then attached to the implant replicas prior to digital scanning.

Post hoc calculation of the Power of the study using the G*Power software (Dusseldorf University v 3.1.9.5) was 1.00, calculated based on the generated Effect size Cohen’s d = 4.33, and α = 0.05, and an equal size sample of 10 in each group.

Each master model was scanned using an extraoral desktop scanner (i3DScan Color, iMes iCore GmbH, Eiterfeld, Germany). The mounted master models were scanned ten times on the Artex articulator to record articulation. Subsequently, the same models were mounted on a scan fixator (Fig. 1) and scanned ten times under identical conditions. All scans were exported as Standard Tessellation Language (STL) files.

Scan fixator set for artex articulator

All STL files were imported into CloudCompare software (Telecom ParisTech, Paris, France) for analysis. Precision was evaluated by performing pairwise superimpositions within each group (articulator and scan fixator). For each group (n = 10 scans), all possible pairwise combinations were generated (n(n − 1)/2 = 45 superimpositions per group). The maxillary model was used as the reference for alignment, while deviations in the mandibular model were measured, as shown in Fig. 2.

Superimposition of 3D model on cloudcompare software

The root-mean-square discrepancy (RMS, µm) was used as the primary outcome variable (Fig. 3). Each scan pair was measured using both devices. Therefore, the data were analyzed as a paired design, with each scan pair considered the unit of analysis.

Superimposition of a 3D model on cloudcompare software showing RMS

Descriptive statistics were calculated for each device, including mean, standard deviation, median, and interquartile range. Distributional characteristics were assessed visually with histograms, boxplots, and normal Q-Q plots. Because RMS values were right-skewed, a log10 transformation of RMS values was also generated for complementary analysis.

The primary comparison between the scan fixator and articulator groups on the original scale was performed with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. A complementary paired t-test was performed on the log-transformed RMS values to assess whether the findings remained consistent after reducing the influence of skewness and extreme values.

For sensitivity analysis, paired differences in RMS values were calculated as scan fixator RMS minus articulator RMS. Potential outlying pairs were identified with the 1.5 × interquartile range rule applied to the paired differences. The primary paired analyses were then repeated after excluding the identified outliers. This was done to evaluate the robustness of the results.

All statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics, version 31 (IBM Corp). The level of significance was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Each pairwise comparison from the articulator group was matched with the corresponding pair from the scan fixator group based on identical scan indices. A total of 45 paired observations were available for analysis, with one RMS measurement obtained from the scan fixator and one from the articulator for each pair.

The RMS values showed substantial dispersion and right-skewness in both groups. For the scan fixator, the mean RMS was 106.6 ± 206.8 μm, with a median of 17.9 μm and an interquartile range of 13.3 to 112.9 μm. For the articulator, the mean RMS was 178.9 ± 276.5 μm, with a median of 22.2 μm and an interquartile range of 14.4 to 176.2 μm as shown in Table 1.

In the primary paired comparison on the original scale, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test showed no significant difference between the scan fixator and articulator measurements (P=.446). Likewise, analysis of the log-transformed RMS values with a paired t test showed no significant difference between devices (mean paired difference, -0.140 log10 units; 95% confidence interval, -0.428 to 0.149; P=.334).

Inspection of the paired differences demonstrated several extreme values. Using the 1.5 × interquartile range criterion, 20 paired observations were flagged as outliers. After exclusion of these pairs, the findings remained unchanged. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test again showed no significant difference between devices (P=.491), and the paired t test on log-transformed RMS values also remained nonsignificant (mean paired difference, -0.055 log10 units; 95% confidence interval, -0.195 to 0.085; P=.428).

Overall, within the limitations of the present sample, the scan fixator and articulator produced comparable RMS discrepancy values, and the absence of a statistically significant difference remained consistent in both the primary and sensitivity analyses.

Discussion

This study compared the precision of extraoral scanning of edentulous arches with implant scan bodies using two different mounting systems: an Artex articulator CPR (Amann Girrbach GmbH, Pforzheim, Germany) and a scan fixator. The clinical relevance of this comparison is related to the practical limitations of extraoral desktop scanners, as not all systems can accommodate semi- or fully adjustable articulators, which may require larger and more costly scanning equipment. The scan fixator offers a simplified and cost-effective alternative; however, its effect on scanning precision in this context has not been previously investigated.

No previous studies have evaluated the precision of scanning mounted models using an articulator versus a scan fixator in completely edentulous arches with implant scan bodies. The absence of previous studies prevents direct comparison with the literature but emphasizes the novelty and clinical relevance of this investigation.

The results demonstrated that both systems produced highly variable RMS values; however, no statistically significant difference was found between the articulator and scan fixator groups. Although the articulator group showed numerically higher mean RMS values than the scan fixator group, this difference was not statistically significant in either the primary analysis or sensitivity analysis. This indicates that both mounting approaches provide comparable precision under the tested conditions.

From a clinical and laboratory perspective, these findings suggest that the use of a scan fixator may represent a viable alternative to articulator-based scanning in digital implant workflows, particularly in situations where cost and equipment size are limiting factors. However, given the wide variability observed in RMS values, careful consideration of workflow standardization and scanning conditions remains important.

This study has some limitations. First, it was conducted in vitro using standardized typodont models, which do not fully replicate clinical conditions such as soft tissue behavior and intraoral variability. Second, scanner type, mounting systems, and digital processing protocols may influence the results. Although all procedures were standardized, residual variability cannot be excluded. Future studies should evaluate these systems under clinical conditions and across different scanning platforms to further validate these findings.

Conclusion

There was no statistically significant difference in precision between articulator-based scanning and scan fixator-based scanning in edentulous arches with implant scan bodies. The scan fixator may represent a cost-effective and clinically acceptable alternative in digital implant workflows.
