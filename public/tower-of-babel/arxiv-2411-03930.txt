Gibbs partitions and lattice paths
Niccolò Bosio, Markus Kuba, Benedikt Stufler

Abstract
This work is devoted to the analysis of a Gibbs partition model, also known as a composition scheme. We consider a natural new condition on the component weights. It leads to a new behavior for the total number of components. We discover a condensation phenomenon, producing a unique giant component comprising almost the entire mass. Additionally, we prove a point process limit describing the asymptotic size of the non-maximal components exhibiting a sublinear power-law growth. A particular motivation for our article stems from applications, ranging from simple random walks in the cube, over lattice paths models in the plane, pairs of directed random walks, over to urn models and card guessing games.



keywords
Gibbs partitions; Composition schemes; Lattice paths

Introduction and main results

A great many combinatorial structures consist of more basic building
blocks or components. This situation is omnipresent in various fields, such as combinatorics, probability theory, and statistical mechanics. It manifests itself in a vast variety of topics, amongst others, permutations, random walks, trees, graphs, mappings, parking functions and urn models.

An important direction of research is the development of a general theory for studying the asymptotic component distribution, which can be applied to all the special cases. Successful lines of research are partition models satisfying a conditioning relation

,

[

]

[

We note that in the combinatorial literature
[

,

In this work we define and study a novel regime for the Gibbs partition model, assuming that the underlying weight sequences satisfy certain growth assumptions, see (
1.1
)
and (

We present concrete applications to combinatorial models.

1.1
The Gibbs partition model

Let
(w_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(v_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be two sequences of nonnegative real numbers, referred to as

component weights
and
component-count weights
, respectively.
We denote their ordinary generating functions by

W(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}w_{n}z^{n},\qquad V(z)=\sum_{k\geq 1}v_{k}z^{k}.

The associated
composition scheme
is given by the formal composition

U(z)=V\big(W(z)\big)=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}z^{n},\qquad u_{n}=\sum_{k\geq 1}v_{k}w_{n}^{(k)},\qquad w_{n}^{(k)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}+\dots+n_{k}=n\\
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}w_{n_{i}}.

For a given integer
n\geq 1
and
k\geq 1
with
w_{n}^{(k)}>0
, the
Gibbs distribution

\mathbb{P}_{n}^{(k)}
on ordered
k
-tuples
(n_{1},\dots,n_{k})

of non-negative integers summing to
n
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{n}^{(k)}(n_{1},\dots,n_{k})=\frac{1}{w_{n}^{(k)}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}w_{n_{i}}.

Mixing over
k
with the weights
(v_{k})_{k\geq 0}
yields the
Gibbs partition model
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{n}(n_{1},\dots,n_{k})=\frac{v_{k}\prod_{i=1}^{k}w_{n_{i}}}{u_{n}}.

We only consider values of
n
for which
0<u_{n}<\infty
, ensuring that the Gibbs partition model
\mathbb{P}_{n}
is a well-defined probability measure. We let
(K_{1},\ldots,K_{N_{n}})
denote a sequence of random non-negative integers that follows the
\mathbb{P}_{n}
-distribution. The dependence of the
K_{i}
on
n
is implicit. We refer to the coordinates of this sequences as components. Hence
N_{n}
denotes the number of components. We let
K_{(1)}\geq K_{(2)}\geq\ldots
denote the components ordered according to their size.

Standing assumptions.

In this work, we focus on the case where the weight sequences satisfy the following conditions.

1.

Condition on component weights:
there exists a slowly varying function
L_{w}
and a constant
\rho_{w}>0
such that

\displaystyle w_{n}\sim L_{w}(n)n^{-1}\rho_{w}^{-n},\qquad W(\rho_{w})\in]0,\infty[.

(1.1)

2.

Condition on component-count weights:
for some
\alpha>-1
and a slowly varying function
L_{v}
,

\displaystyle v_{n}\sim L_{v}(n)n^{\alpha-1}W(\rho_{w})^{-n}.

(1.2)

Throughout the following we assume that (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) hold. Let
X\geq 0
denote a random non-negative integer with probability generating function

\mathbb{E}\left[z^{X}\right]=W(\rho_{w}z)/W(\rho_{w}).

(1.3)

By Karamata’s theorem
[
37
, Rem. 1.2.7]
,
\mathbb{P}\left(X>x\right)
is slowly varying, and satisfies

\displaystyle L_{w}(n)=o(\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right)).

(1.4)

1.2
Main results

Our first main theorem describes the asymptotic distribution of the number of components.

Theorem 1.1
.

Uniformly for all integers
k=x/\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right)
with
x
restricted to a compact subset of
]0,\infty[
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(N_{n}=k\right)\sim\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right)x^{\alpha}\exp(-x)/\Gamma(\alpha+1).

Furthermore,

N_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right)\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}}\,Z

for a
\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha+1,1)
-distributed random variable
Z
. For each
q\geq 1

\mathbb{E}\left[N_{n}^{q}\right]\sim\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right)^{q}\frac{\Gamma(q+\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}.

In our proof we make use of a framework for random walk developed in
[
39
,
38
,
1
]
.
With the number of components under control, we turn to the extremal component sizes
K_{(1)}\geq K_{(2)}\geq\ldots
. We discover a condensation phenomenon, producing a unique giant component of size
n
up to second order asymptotics of smaller order. The non-maximal components behave like independent copies of
(X\mid X\leq n)
. In order to state this formally, we define the ’delete first maximum’ operator

\tau:\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}\to\bigcup_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k},\quad(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\bigr)\mapsto(x_{1},\dots,x_{J-1},\,x_{J+1},\dots x_{k}),

where
J=\min\{\,j:x_{j}=\max_{1\leq i\leq k}x_{i}\}
. Let
\tilde{X}_{1},\tilde{X}_{2},\ldots
denote independent copies of
(X\mid X\leq n)
that are independent of
N_{n}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

We have

d_{\mathrm{TV}}\Big(\mathcal{L}\big(\tau(K_{1},\dots,K_{N_{n}})\big),\mathcal{L}\big(\tilde{X}_{1},\dots,\tilde{X}_{N_{n}-1}\big)\Bigr)\;\longrightarrow\;0.

Consequently,

K_{(1)}/n\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{p}}}\,1,\qquad K_{(2)}=o_{p}(n).

A consequence of Theorem
1.2
is that for all
\epsilon>0
and all subsets
A_{n}
with
\max(A_{n})<(1-\epsilon)n
the number

F_{A}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{n}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{K_{i}\in A_{n}}

of components with size in
A_{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}\Big(\mathcal{L}\big(F_{A_{n}}),\mathrm{Binomial}(N_{n},\mathbb{P}\left(X\in A_{n}\mid X\leq n\right))\Bigr)\;\longrightarrow\;0.

(1.5)

The expectation of the binomial is asymptotically equivalent to
\frac{\mathbb{P}(X\in A_{n})}{\mathbb{P}(X>n)}
. For example, if
A_{n}=A
is constant,

\displaystyle F_{A}\mathbb{P}(X>n)\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}}\,\mathbb{P}(X\in A)Z.

(1.6)

In order to determine the higher order asymptotics of the largest component
K_{(1)}
and at the same time the precise limiting behavior of the
i
th largest component
K_{(i)}
for
i\geq 2
, we require an additional regularity assumption:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(X>n^{t}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right)}\quad\text{exists for all $0<t<1$.}

(1.7)

This regularity condition may be characterized:

Proposition 1.3
.

1.

(Equivalence)
Assumption (
1.7
) holds if and only if there exists
\gamma\geq 0
with

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(X>n^{t}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right)}=t^{-\gamma}

(1.8)

for all
0<t<1
.

2.

(Sufficient condition) If there is
\gamma>0
and some slowly varying function
L
with

\displaystyle L_{w}(n)=\frac{L(\log n)}{\log^{1+\gamma}{n}},

(1.9)

then (
1.8
) holds with the same
\gamma
. In this case

\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right)\sim W(\rho_{w})^{-1}\frac{L(\log n)}{\gamma\log^{\gamma}{n}}.

3.

(Partially necessary condition) If
L_{w}
is eventually monotone and (
1.8
) holds with
\gamma>0
, then (
1.9
) holds for some slowly varying function
L
.

Condition (
1.7
) allows us to define a locally finite Borel measure
\Lambda
on
]0,1]
such that

\Lambda([a,b])=a^{-\gamma}-b^{-\gamma}

for all
0<a<b\leq 1
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose that (
1.7
) holds.
The point process

\sum_{i=2}^{N_{n}}\delta_{\frac{\log(K_{(i)})}{\log(n)}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{K_{(i)}>1}

converges towards a Cox process directed by the random measure
Z\Lambda
for a
\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha+1,1)
distributed random variable
Z
.

Suppose that (
1.7
) holds with
\gamma>0
. Then the limiting point process in Theorem
1.4
is simple, and we let
Y_{(1)}>Y_{(2)}>\ldots
denote its ranked points. Since
N_{n}
grows at a slowly varying scale by Theorem
1.1
, it follows that

0\leq n-\sum_{i=1}^{m}K_{(i)}\leq(N_{n}-m)K_{(m+1)}=o_{p}(K_{(m)}),

yielding

K_{(1)}=n-\sum_{i=2}^{m}K_{(i)}+o_{p}(K_{(m)}).

Using Skorokhod’s representation theorem, we may find a probability space where the joint limits of the extremal component sizes and asymptotic bounds for the number of components hold almost surely. The remainder
n-\sum_{i=1}^{m}K_{(i)}\leq(N_{n}-m)K_{(m+1)}
is bounded from below by
K_{(m+1)}=n^{Y_{(m+1)}+o(1)}
and from above by
(N_{n}-m)K_{(m+1)}=n^{o(1)}K_{(m+1)}=n^{Y_{(m+1)}+o(1)}
. Hence, on this space we have for each
m\geq 2

K_{(1)}=n-\sum_{i=2}^{m}n^{Y_{(i)}+o(1)},\qquad K_{(m)}=n^{Y_{(m)}+o(1)}.

Proofs of our main results

2.1
The number of components.

We let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
denote independent copies of
X
and set
S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
. The following was shown in
[
1
, Thm. 1.1]
:

Proposition 2.1
.

We have uniformly for all
k
with
kL_{w}(n)\to 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}=n\right)\sim k\mathbb{P}(X=n)(1-\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right))^{k}.

(2.1)

There exists
c>0
independent of
n
such that for all large enough
n
and
k\geq 1

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}=n\right)\leq ck\mathbb{P}(X=n)(1-\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right))^{k}.

(2.2)

To be precise,
[
1
, Thm. 1.1]
considers step-distributions that are almost surely positive and hence states (
2.2
) for
k\leq n
. However, we may write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}=n\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\min(k,n)}\binom{k}{j}\mathbb{P}(X=0)^{k-j}\mathbb{P}(X>0)^{j}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j}X_{i}=n\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{j}>0\right).

Applying
[
1
, Thm. 1.1]
to the sum of independent copies of
(X\mid X>0)
and a quick calculation then yields (
2.2
).

See also
[
39
]
for tail-probabilities of these sums, and
[
38
]
for a strong renewal theorem.

Proposition 2.2
.

We have

u_{n}\sim\rho_{w}^{-n}\mathbb{P}(X=n)L_{v}(1/p_{n})p_{n}^{-\alpha-1}\Gamma(\alpha+1).

Proof.

By definition of
X
, we have

\displaystyle u_{n}\rho_{w}^{n}=\sum_{k\geq 0}v_{k}W(\rho_{w})^{k}\mathbb{P}(S_{k}=n).

(2.3)

We write
p_{n}:=\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right)
and set
k=x/p_{n}
for integers
k
. Let
0<a<b
. By (
1.4
) and (
2.1
) we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\frac{a}{p_{n}}<k<\frac{b}{p_{n}}}v_{k}W(\rho_{w})^{k}\mathbb{P}(S_{k}=n)

\displaystyle\sim\mathbb{P}(X=n)\sum_{\frac{a}{p_{n}}<k<\frac{b}{p_{n}}}L_{v}(k)k^{\alpha}(1-p_{n})^{k}

(2.4)

\displaystyle\sim\mathbb{P}(X=n)p_{n}^{-\alpha}\sum_{\frac{a}{p_{n}}<k<\frac{b}{p_{n}}}L_{v}(x/p_{n})x^{\alpha}\exp(-x)

\displaystyle\sim\mathbb{P}(X=n)L_{v}(1/p_{n})p_{n}^{-\alpha-1}\int_{a}^{b}x^{\alpha}\exp(-x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Fix
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small so that
\alpha-\epsilon>1
. By (
2.2
) and the Potter bounds
[
37
]
there exist
C>0
with

\displaystyle\sum_{k\notin[\frac{a}{p_{n}},\frac{b}{p_{n}}]}v_{k}W(\rho_{w})^{k}\mathbb{P}(S_{k}=n)

(2.5)

\displaystyle\leq c\mathbb{P}(X=n)\sum_{k\notin[\frac{a}{p_{n}},\frac{b}{p_{n}}]}L_{v}(k)k^{\alpha}(1-p_{n})^{k}

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{P}(X=n)p_{n}^{-\alpha}\sum_{k\notin[\frac{a}{p_{n}},\frac{b}{p_{n}}]}x^{\alpha}\max(x^{\epsilon},x^{-\epsilon})\exp(-x)

\displaystyle\sim C\mathbb{P}(X=n)L_{v}(1/p_{n})p_{n}^{-\alpha-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}\setminus]a,b[}x^{\alpha}\max(x^{\epsilon},x^{-\epsilon})\exp(-x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

The integral converges. As
0<a<b
were arbitrary, it follows by (
2.3
) that

\displaystyle u_{n}\rho_{w}^{n}\sim\mathbb{P}(X=n)L_{v}(1/p_{n})p_{n}^{-\alpha-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\alpha}\exp(-x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

This completes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof.

We have

\mathbb{P}\left(N_{n}=k\right)=\frac{v_{k}W(\rho_{w})^{k}\mathbb{P}(S_{k}=n)}{u_{n}\rho_{w}^{n}}.

Writing
k=x/\mathbb{P}(X>n)
with
x
restricted to a compact subset of
]0,\infty[
, it follows by Proposition
2.2
and identical arguments as in (
2.4
)

\mathbb{P}\left(N_{n}=k\right)\sim\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right)x^{\alpha}\exp(-x)/\Gamma(\alpha+1).

This local limit theorem implies

N_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right)\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}}\,\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha+1,1).

In order to verify convergence of all moments note that for all
q>1
we have by Proposition
2.2
and identical arguments as in (
2.5
) that for some constants
C>0
and
\epsilon>0
with
\alpha-\epsilon>-1

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(N_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right)\right)^{q}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\alpha+q}\max(x^{\epsilon},x^{-\epsilon})\exp(-x)\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

Consequently, all moments of
N_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right)
converge. This completes the proof.
∎

2.2
Condensation in the balls in boxes model

We show that under conditioning
\{S_{k}=n\}
, the
k-1
“small” summands remaining after deleting the unique maximum become asymptotically independent copies of
(X\mid X\leq n)
. To this end we require that
k=k(n)
doesn’t grow too fast. Recall that
\tau
denotes the ’delete first max’ operator.

Theorem 2.3
.

For each
n\geq 1
, let
k=k(n)
be an integer such that

kL_{w}(n)\to 0.

Denote by

\mathcal{L}(\tau(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})\,\bigm|\,S_{k}=n\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{L}((X_{1},\dots,X_{k-1})\,\bigm|\,X_{1}\leq n,\dots,X_{k-1}\leq n\bigr)

the corresponding conditional laws on
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\,k-1}
. Then, as
n\to\infty
,

d_{\mathrm{TV}}\Big(\mathcal{L}\big(\tau(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})\,\bigm|\,S_{k}=n\bigr)\;,\;\mathcal{L}\big((X_{1},\dots,X_{k-1})\,\bigm|\,X_{i}\leq n\ \forall\,i\bigr)\Bigr)\;\longrightarrow\;0.

Consequently,

\left(\max(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})\mid S_{k}=n\right)/n\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{p}}}\,1.

Proof.

By Karamata’s theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}X_{i}\,\,\Big|\,\,X_{1},\ldots,X_{k-1}\leq n\right]

\displaystyle\sim k\mathbb{E}\left[X\mid X\leq n\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{\mathbb{P}\left(X\leq n\right)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(X=i\right)i

\displaystyle\sim knL_{w}(n)/W(\rho_{w})

\displaystyle=o(n).

By Markov’s inequality and (
1.4
), it follows that there exists a sequence of integers
r_{n}=o(n)
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}X_{i}\geq r_{n}\,\,\Big|\,\,X_{1},\ldots,X_{k-1}\leq n\right)\;\longrightarrow\;0.

(2.6)

Let

\bm{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{k-1})\in\mathbb{N}^{\,k-1}\quad\text{with}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{\,k-1}s_{i}\;=:\;s\;<\;r_{n}.

Define

p_{\tau}(\bm{s})\;:=\;\mathbb{P}\left(\tau(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})=s\;\mid\;S_{k}=n\right)

and

p_{\leq n}(\bm{s})\;:=\;\mathbb{P}\left((X_{1},\dots,X_{k-1})=s\;\mid\;X_{1},\ldots,X_{k-1}\leq n\right).

By (
2.6
) it suffices to prove

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{s}\,:\,s<r_{n}}\Bigl|1-\frac{p_{\tau}(\bm{s})}{p_{\leq n}(\bm{s})}\Bigr|\;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(2.7)

Note that, if
\tau(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})=s
and
S_{k}=n
, then the deleted coordinate must equal
n-s
, and that the maximal value can occur at any of the
k
positions. Hence, for all sufficiently large
n
and each
\bm{s}
with
\sum s_{i}=s<r_{n}
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\tau(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})=\bm{s},\;S_{k}=n\right)\;=\;k\;\Bigl[\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{P}\left(X=s_{i}\right)\Bigr]\;\mathbb{P}\left(X=n-s\right).

Since
\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}=n\right)
is the normalising constant under the conditioning
\{S_{k}=n\}
,

p_{\tau}(\bm{s})=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\tau(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})=\bm{s},\;S_{k}=n\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}=n\right)}=\frac{k\,\bigl[\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{P}\left(X=s_{i}\right)\bigr]\;\mathbb{P}\left(X=n-s\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}=n\right)}.

On the other hand,

p_{\leq n}(\bm{s})=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{P}\left(X=s_{i}\right)}{\bigl[\mathbb{P}\left(X\leq n\right)\bigr]^{\,k-1}}.

Hence, using (
2.1
) and
s\leq r_{n}=o(n)

\displaystyle\frac{p_{\tau}(\bm{s})}{\,p_{\leq n}(\bm{s})\,}

\displaystyle=\;k\;\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(X=n-s\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}=n\right)}\;\bigl[\mathbb{P}\left(X\leq n\right)\bigr]^{\,k-1}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(X=n-s\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(X=n\right)}

\displaystyle\sim 1.

This verifies (
2.7
) and hence completes the proof.
∎

2.3
Characterising further regularity assumptions

In this section we characterise the regularity assumption (
1.7
) by proving Proposition
1.3
.

Proof of Proposition
1.3
.

If (
1.7
) holds, then the function
g:]0,1]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},t\mapsto\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(X>n^{t}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right)}
decreases monotonically,
g(1)=1
, and
g(st)=g(s)g(t)
for all
0<t,s\leq 1
. We may extend

to a multiplicative function
g:]0,\infty[\to]0,\infty[
by setting
g(t)=g(1/t)
for

. This way,
[
12
, Thm. 1.1.9]
applies, yielding
g(t)\equiv t^{-\gamma}
for some
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
g
decreases monotonically, we have
\gamma\geq 0
.

Is is easy to see that (
1.7
) implies the same limit when
n
grows along all real numbers (as opposed to the integers). This implies that
\mathbb{P}(X>\exp(y))
varies regularly at infinity with index
-\gamma
. If
L_{w}
is eventually monotone, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X>\exp(y)\right)

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{k>\exp(y)}L_{w}(k)k^{-1}/W(\rho_{w})

\displaystyle\sim\int_{\exp(y)}^{\infty}L_{w}(x)x^{-1}/W(\rho_{w})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{y}^{\infty}L_{w}(\exp(z))/W(\rho_{w})\,\mathrm{d}z

as
y\to\infty
.
By the monotone density theorem,

\displaystyle L_{w}(\exp(y))

\displaystyle\sim W(\rho_{w})\mathbb{P}\left(X>\exp(y)\right)\frac{\gamma}{y}

as
y\to\infty
. Hence
L_{w}(\exp(y))
varies regularly at infinity with index
-\gamma-1
. In other words, there exists a slowly varying function

with

L_{w}(y)\sim\frac{L(\log y)}{\log^{1+\gamma}(y)}

as
y\to\infty
.

Conversely, if
L_{w}(y)\sim\frac{L(\log y)}{\log^{1+\gamma}(y)}
as
y\to\infty
for some
\gamma>0
and some slowly varying function
L
, then by an elementary calculation and Karamata’s theorem

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right)

\displaystyle\sim W(\rho_{w})^{-1}\int_{n}^{\infty}\frac{L(\log y)}{y\log^{1+\gamma}(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=W(\rho_{w})^{-1}\int_{\log n}^{\infty}\frac{L(z)}{z^{1+\gamma}}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\sim W(\rho_{w})^{-1}\frac{L(\log n)}{\gamma\log^{\gamma}{n}}.

This implies

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(X>n^{t}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right)}=t^{-\gamma}.

Hence the proof is complete.

2.4

In the previous section we showed that the balls in boxes model exhibits a unique giant component with size

Let

Sampling without replacement urn

The sampling without replacement urn is a basic and fundamental urn model. One draws balls at random one after the other, observes the color of the drawn ball
and removes it. One may study different questions regarding this urn, we refer the interested reader to
[
19
,
29
]
The ball transition matrix of the sampling without replacement urn with three colors is given by

\left(\begin{matrix}-1&0&0\\
0&-1&0\\
0&0&-1\end{matrix}\right)

We note in passing that, in the general case, the replacement matrix is the negative identity matrix. The sample paths of the urn model take place in
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
. We provide the following connection between lattice paths, the diagonal, and the sampling urn.

Proposition 3.12
.

The random variable
N_{n}
, counting the number of returns to the diagonal
x=y=z
in the cube counts the number of times there are equally many balls in the sampling urn process, starting with
n=n_{1}=n_{2}=n_{3}
balls of each type.

Thus, our previous results provide a limit law for the number of equalities for the sampling without replacement urn.

Proof.

We look at the sample paths of the Sampling without replacement urn, starting with
n_{k}
balls of each type,
1\leq k\leq 3
, and the return to the diagonal
d\colon x=y=z
. By definition of the urn model, the transfer probabilities are given by

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left((n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})\to(n_{1}-1,n_{2},n_{3})\right)&=\frac{n_{1}}{n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}},\\
\mathbb{P}\left((n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})\to(n_{1},n_{2}-1,n_{3})\right)&=\frac{n_{2}}{n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}},\\
\mathbb{P}\left((n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})\to(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}-1)\right)&=\frac{n_{3}}{n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}},\end{split}

(3.11)

for
n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}\geq 0
with at least one nonzero value.
Thus, we easily observe that for every sample path
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
equation
3.11
implies that

\binom{n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}}{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\{\sigma\}=1,

where
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
denotes the probability measure induced by the process. Thus, the draw after draw evolution of the urn can be modeled by the uniform distribution in the cube.

3.7
Card Guessing games

Card guessing games are of great interest and have been considered in the literature in many articles, starting with the works of Diaconis
[
17
]

and Diaconis and Graham
[
18
]
; we refer the reader to
[
33
]
for more pointers to the literature, in particular for
applications to real world problems. The card guessing game of our main focus can be described as follows. A deck of a total of
N
cards is shuffled, and then the guesser in provided with the total number cards
N
, as well as the individual numbers of say hearts, diamonds, clubs and spades. After each guess, the person guessing the cards is shown the drawn card, which is then removed from the deck. This process is continued until no more cards are left. Assuming the guesser tries to maximize the number of correct guesses, one is interested in the total number of correct guesses. Of course, this card guessing procedure can be considered with an arbitrary number
m\geq 2
of different types of cards. The simplest case of
m=2
different types of cards, say, colors red (hearts and diamonds) and black (clubs or spades), is now very well understood, leading to connections to urn models, Dyck paths, hitting times in Brownian bridges
[
18
]
, as well as new phenomena in Analytic Combinatorics; see
[
33
]
for details.

In the general setting of
m
different types, a main interest has been in deriving asymptotics for the expectation of the total number of correct guesses
[
18
,
27
,
40
]
. Here, two different settings have been treated in the literature so far. In the first setting, one starts with a fixed number of cards
m
of different types and equally many cards
n=n_{k}
of each type
k
,
1\leq k\leq m
, as
n
tends to infinity. Second, one studies the case of
m\to\infty
and the individual numbers of cards
n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
all equal and fixed, or bounded. It highly desirable to shed more light on the fundamental result of Diaconis and Graham for the expected value in the first setting, mentioned before.

Theorem 3.13

(Diaconis-Graham
[
18
]
)
.

The expected value
\mathbb{E}\left[C_{n,\dots,n}\right]
of the number of correct guesses satisfies for fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}
, with
m\geq 2
fixed, and
n\to\infty
the expansion

\mathbb{E}\left[C_{n,\dots,n}\right]=n+\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot M\cdot\sqrt{n}+o(\sqrt{n}),

where
M=\mathbb{E}\left[\max\{N_{1},\dots,N_{m}\}\right]
and the random variables
N_{k}
are iid standard normal distributed.

Except for the case
m=2
, it is an open problem to derive the limit law of
C_{n,\dots,n}
, or even raw moments beyond the expectation. We look into the case of
m=3
and use a decomposition of
C_{n,n,n}
into different random variables. Such decompositions have turned out to be very fruitful in the two-color case. We use a link between or previous results for the returns to the diagonal in the cube and the card guessing game to obtain more insight on
C_{n,n,n}
.
The idea is to decompose the correct guesses according to two aspects: the number of types of cards being maximal in the deck when a correct guess occurs and the number of different types still present in the deck.

C_{n,n,n}=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}C_{n,n,n}^{(k,\ell)},

where
C_{n,n,n}^{(k,\ell)}
denotes the number of correct guesses with
\ell
cards being maximal in the deck, while
k
different types are still present.
We refer the reader to
[
34
]
for more details of this decomposition, as well as to
[
33
]
for a similar decomposition for two types of cards. Several simplifications occur
[
34
]
, and the main interest is into the random variable

C_{n,n,n}^{(3,3)}
, as well as
C_{n,n,n}^{(3,2)}
and
C_{n,n,n}^{(2,2)}
. Our results will allow us to give a complete description of
C_{n,n,n}^{(3,3)}
.

Proposition 3.14

(
[
34
]
)
.

The number of pure luck guesses
C_{n,n,n}^{(3,3)}
in a card guessing game with
n
cards of each of the three types is related to the number
N_{n}
of returns to the diagonal in a simple random walk
in the cube:

C_{n,n,n}^{(3,3)}=\mathrm{Binomial}\big(N_{n},\textstyle{\frac{1}{3}}\big).

Corollary 3.15
.

The random variable
C_{n,n,n}^{(3,3)}
has a Gamma limit law:
to an Erlang distribution with density function
f(x)=xe^{-x}
,
x\geq 0
:

\sqrt{3}C_{n,n,n}^{(3,3)}/(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(n))\to\text{Gamma}(2,1).

Proof of Corollary
3.15
.

We use a well-known classical property of the binomial distribution
R=\mathrm{Binomial}(n,p)
:

\mathbb{E}\left[R^{\underline{s}}\right]=n^{\underline{s}}p^{s},\quad s\geq 0.

Thus, we obtain for the conditional expectation

\mathbb{E}\left[\big(C_{n,n,n}^{(3,3)}\big)^{\underline{s}}\mid N_{n}\right]=N_{n}^{\underline{s}}\cdot\frac{1}{3^{s}}.

Consequently, We obtain the factorial moments of
C_{n,n,n}^{(3,3)}
by the tower rule of total expectation.
We get the desired expansion for the raw moments

\mathbb{E}\left[(N_{n})^{s}\right]=\sum_{k=0}^{s}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{s}{k}\mathbb{E}\left[(N_{n})^{\underline{k}}\right]\sim\frac{1}{3^{s}}\cdot(s+1)!\Big(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi}\Big)^{s}\log^{s}(n).

Carleman’s criterion
[
16
, pp. 189–220]
for the Stieltjes moment problem, support
[0,\infty)
, states
that if

\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\mu_{s}^{-1/(2s)}=+\infty,

(3.12)

then the moment sequence
(\mu_{s})_{s\geq 1}
determines a unique distribution. Furthermore, this implies that if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\mu_{s}\leq C^{s}(2s)!\quad\text{for }s\in\mathbb{N},

then Carleman’s criterion is satisfied. This is obviously true for the moments of the Erlang distribution,
such that the divergence in Carleman’s criterion is satisfied. Thus, by the Fréchet–Shohat theorem
[
24
]
,
we obtain the weak convergence of the scaled random variable to a Gamma-distributed random variable.
∎

3.8
Outlook

At the end of this work we outline how to apply our main results to the analysis of space contacts for other families of lattice paths in the cube, starting at the upper corner
(n,n,n)
and ending at the origin. In order to automate the derivation of the overall asymptotics of the lattice paths
from
(n,n,n)
to the origin with steps in
\mathcal{S}

F(t;x,y,z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\big(P(x,y,z)\big)^{n}t^{n}=\frac{1}{1-tP(x,y,z)},

where
P(x,y,z)=P_{-\mathcal{S}}(x,y,z)
denotes the step polynomial associated to the step set
-\mathcal{S}
.
The number of walks from the upper corner
(n,n,n)
to the origin is then encoded by the diagonal:
[x^{n}y^{n}z^{n}]F(1;x,y,z)
. The derivation of such diagonals and their asymptotics can be done using Analytic Combinatorics in several variables
[
36
,
41
]
, see also
[

]
.
Then, assuming that the walks are directed, we can simply use the arch decomposition,
leading to a combinatorial sequence construction and
V(z)=1/(1-z)
. Then, the asymptotics of the diagonal of
F(1;x,y,z)
are translated to the asymptotics of
W(z)
, which allows to check our standing assumptions and invoke our main results.
