Natural bioplastics enable sustainable triboelectric nanogenerators for biomechanical energy harvesting and rehabilitation monitoring
Authors: Anamika Barua, Ferdousi Mayoa, Kamaruzzaman, Md. Monjarul Alam, S M Sohel Rana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The growing demand for sustainable and autonomous power solutions highlights triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs) as promising candidates for energy harvesting and wearable sensing applications. Conventional TENGs, despite their effectiveness, use synthetic materials that generate e-waste and hinder skin-contact integration. Herein, we develop a sustainable strategy by fabricating a bioplastic-based TENG using radish (Raphanus sativus)- and turnip (Brassica rapa subsp. rapa)-derived bioplastics as the negative triboelectric layer in each TENG, while human skin serves as the positive triboelectric counterpart. These bioplastics feature bioactive constituents, such as d-glucose and polysaccharides, which provide excellent triboelectric performance, combined with biodegradability and flexibility, making them suitable for wearable positioning applications. Compared with the turnip-based TENG (T-TENG), the experimental findings reveal that the radish-based TENG (R-TENG) exhibited slightly enhanced performance, obtaining a maximum peak-to-peak voltage of ∼344 V along with a sensitivity of ∼15.28 V kPa−1 and a power density of ∼26.01 W m−2 under hand-tapping operation, capable of lighting up 62 LEDs instantly. To validate practicality, an insole prototype effectively captured biomechanical energy during walking and running, generating distinct electrical signals corresponding to gait phases and pressure distribution, while a stress-relief ball-type R-TENG effectively sensed finger and grip variations, highlighting its tactile rehabilitation utility as a proof of concept. This study advances eco-friendly material development for efficient next-generation sustainable wearable TENGs.

Introduction

As the global interest in sustainable development and eco-conscious technologies increases significantly, green energy harvesting systems have become a crucial need in recent times.1 In addition, many conventional materials and energy harvesting resources used in electronic applications are often rebuked for their adverse environmental consequences, resulting from their biological incompatibility, non-biodegradability, and toxicity.2 Moreover, the use of conventional energy sources such as chemical batteries in devices like motion and tactile sensors necessitates frequent replacement and proper disposal due to their limited lifespan, leading to increased e-waste and elevated operating expenses. The aforementioned limitations pose significant obstacles to the progress of long-term wearable devices, which demand attributes such as non-toxicity, environmental compatibility, and flexibility.3,4 Some existing conventional energy harvesting approaches, such as electromagnetic, photovoltaic, and thermoelectric technologies, are constrained by factors, including high manufacturing expenses, rigid configuration, broad spatial allocations, and environmental concerns during fabrication and disposal. These limitations emphasize the critical demand for alternative energy harvesting approaches that are lightweight, inexpensive, and environmentally sustainable as well.4,5 A groundbreaking study was carried out in 2012 by Professor Wang, who introduced TENG technology, offering a novel approach to convert low-frequency mechanical energy into electrical energy.4,6–8 This energy conversion is obtained by combining triboelectrification and electrostatic induction.9–11 When two materials with different electron affinities come into contact, charge transfer occurs between the surfaces and subsequently produces current through electrostatic induction upon separation.12,13 Therefore, it is important to choose perfect materials as positive triboelectric and negative triboelectric layers. TENGs can show better performance in energy conversion, especially for low-frequency and low-amplitude mechanical motions, highlighting enhanced energy conversion efficiency compared to conventional approaches.14 The triboelectric materials for fabricating TENGs can be collected from abundant sources such as metals, synthetic polymers, organic polymers, natural biomaterials, and plant polymers, enabling the production of large-scale power generation devices.15–26 The remarkable advancements of TENGs over the past decades have shown their significant potential for sustainable energy harvesting and self-powered sensing applications. Khanapurarm et al. reviewed recent state-of-the-art advancements in triboelectric materials, device optimization, and multifunctional applications of TENGs.27 Traditional TENGs widely use synthetic polymers like PDMS, PVC, PVDF, PTFE, silicone, and polyamide as triboelectric layers.26,28–31 Ahmed et al. introduced a fabric-based TENG (FWSF-TENG) with enhanced performance, with a power density of 2408.70 mW m−2 and stable operation over 60 000 cycles, and the developed TENG was made of synthetic materials such as nylon and PDMS.32 Rana et al. designed a porous Ecoflex/SEBS-based TENG, which showed high performance with a peak-to-peak output voltage of 2280 V through enhanced surface area and porosity. Despite having superior electrical characteristics, this TENG depended on conventional synthetic polymers.17 Ahmed et al. reported a diamond-structured fabric-based TENG (DSF-TENG) that achieved a high output voltage (763 V) and enhanced power density (2862.78 mW m−2) and showed excellent stability, gait-monitoring capabilities, and excellent mechanical performance. Despite its enhanced performance, the device also relies on traditional synthetic materials.33 These conventional TENGs offer a more cost-effective fabrication process and superior adaptability across various application domains, making them suitable for harvesting mechanical energy from different mechanical sources such as marine activity, human motion, airflow, raindrops, and light wind.34–40 However, due to their non-biodegradable nature, these materials result in environmental threats, and they lack compatibility with wearable electronic healthcare applications involving prolonged human contact. The non-renewable and non-biodegradable nature of these materials hinders the progress of eco-friendly and commercially viable TENGs.41 Therefore, investigating and developing environment-friendly triboelectric materials is a key challenge to fabricating eco-friendly TENGs. Recently, numerous research teams have been working on fully biodegradable and environmentally friendly TENGs using biomaterials, and these TENGs can be used in both energy harvesting applications and wearable devices.3,24,25,42–46 Ccorahua et al. proposed a starch polymer (derived from potato starch and glycerol) electrolyte-based bio-TENG, which acted as a positive triboelectric material.47 Recent studies have focused on improving stability and real-time integration of wearable TENGs. Gao et al. designed a fabric-reinforced triboelectric insole with excellent durability along with biomechanical harvesting capability, and the developed TENG can power up small electronic devices. Machine learning algorithms were integrated with this device for real-time activity recognition.48 A cellulose acetate-based porous nanocomposite, demonstrated by Bai et al., served as a positive triboelectric layer in applications involving energy harvesting and motion sensing.49 Xu et al. reported that although the bamboo leaf-based TENG demonstrates a sustainable approach, its limitations include the natural aging of green bamboo leaves, which deteriorates performance over time, as well as mechanical fatigue, which may result in structural damage of senescent leaves after a certain time, both of which adversely affect output reliability.50 Babu et al. utilized a biodegradable plant leaf powder, more specifically, Rumex vesicarius leaf powder, to design a TENG for sustainable biomechanical energy harvesting applications. This plant leaf-based material exhibited promising TENG performance and offers eco-friendly characteristics.51 Sarkar et al. presented a promising approach to TENG fabrication using thermoplastic starch, which offers a sustainable and eco-friendly positive triboelectric material for powering electronics and serving as a self-powered gait sensor.52 Vikram et al. developed a biowaste seashell-based TENG to harvest mechanical energy and highlighted sustainable energy applications such as powering a series-connected 240 LEDs.53 A biofilm-based TENG was developed by Wang et al. that leverages hydrogen bonding between hydroxyl-rich starch molecules and water to boost performance in high-humidity environments, although the sustained functional reliability and structural robustness of the biofilm material under continuous operation were not extensively addressed.54 Rani et al. introduced a biocompatible TENG-based edible electronic skin with enhanced flexibility and demonstrated its applications in sleep monitoring and Morse code transmission through self-powered sensing.45 Huang et al. reported a cellulose-derived TENG using a multi-fluid electrospinning technique, exhibiting superior filtration efficiency for respiratory protection and autonomous sensing applications.55 A study conducted by Alluri et al. highlighted the potential of Aloe vera in energy harvesting by combining triboelectric and piezoelectric approaches. However, due to degradation of Aloe vera gel after electrode insertion and insufficient power levels, practical application is constrained, which restricts its feasibility for extended use.56 Rana et al. introduced a biodegradable beetroot-agar-based TENG for real-time football player monitoring, delivering environmentally conscious energy solutions, though its long-term mechanical stability and durability under prolonged physical exertion necessitate further investigation.57 According to Chi et al., a rice paper-based TENG shows potential for eco-friendly energy harvesting and self-powered sensing applications; however, its performance is hindered by low electrical performance and a decline in structural reliability with sustained mechanical stress.58 These materials are safe and non-harmful to the human body, supporting their integration into wearable electronic devices. Previous research has investigated a range of both synthetic and bio-based triboelectric materials; however, achieving suboptimal output performance remains a key challenge for real-world applications. This has stimulated our interest in fabricating an eco-friendly triboelectric bioplastic film with biodegradability and biocompatibility.

Encouraged by the above literature review, we present an innovative approach to fabricate eco-friendly, flexible, biodegradable bioplastics from turnip–agar composite and radish–agar composite for self-powered motion and tactile sensing applications. Root vegetables such as radish and turnip possess substantial levels of d-glucose and cellulose, with hemicellulose present in smaller amounts, all of which play a role in enhancing their triboelectric properties. Mechanical strength and triboelectric properties of the bioplastic made from radish and turnip are enhanced by the inclusion of agar powder, a polysaccharide composed of agar and agaropectin, which has robust film-forming capabilities and contributes to increased charge density and structural integrity. These biodegradable films, unlike synthetic polymers, are environmentally friendly, flexible, and economically viable for production. This research focuses on the fabrication of radish- and turnip-based bioplastic layers as negative triboelectric materials individually integrated with human skin, serving as the positive counterpart in a vertical contact–separation mode TENG. To evaluate environmental compatibility, a water-induced degradation experiment was conducted, revealing significant material breakdown over time and confirming the biodegradability of the bioplastic matrix under ambient aqueous conditions. These newly designed bioplastic material-based R-TENGs show enhanced power density, flexibility, and superior mechanical stability after long-term use. By integrating R-TENGs into a smart insole and a stress relief ball, we demonstrate their capability to harvest biomechanical energy and detect tactile stimuli without external power sources. These prototypes exemplify the potential of R-TENGs as scalable, sustainable platforms for motion tracking, rehabilitation monitoring, and interactive wearable systems as a proof of concept. These results underscore the potential of TENGs as scalable, sustainable, and multifunctional components for next-generation self-powered sensing systems. Although several TENGs have been developed for biomechanical energy harvesting and rehabilitation monitoring, most reported devices rely on synthetic polymers, limiting environmental sustainability. This study advances this field by introducing an eco-friendly bioplastic-based TENG platform that integrates sustainable material development with energy harvesting and self-powered sensing capabilities.

Experimental section

Materials

The fresh radish and turnip for making bioplastic films were collected from the native land. A flexible fabric electrode with a thickness of 70 µm and polyamide (PI) tape were obtained from an online shop in Dhaka, Bangladesh.

Preparation of bioplastic films

Fresh and high-quality radishes and turnips were thoroughly washed with distilled water carefully to remove the dust particles and dried well under the sunlight to eliminate water content in the ambient environment. After that, they needed to be peeled and diced before blending them separately with distilled water to create a homogeneous mixture. The mixture was filtered through a cheesecloth to separate the juice from the pulp. To create the bioplastics made of radish and turnip separately, 90 g of the juice was combined with 2.5 g agar powder, 3.5 g vinegar, and 2.5 g glycerin. In addition, radish-based films were prepared at varying juice concentrations of 0%, 30%, 55%, 70%, and 90%. Usually, agar powder is used for forming a gel-like matrix as it ensures the desired structure, flexibility, and stability of the film. The brittleness of the film was reduced by adding glycerin here due to its plasticizer properties and enhancing its flexibility. After that, this mixture was heated at 65 °C for around 10–12 minutes with continuous stirring to dissolve the agar with juice completely and obtain the required viscosity. After obtaining uniform consistency, the mixture was spread onto a flexible silicone tray to form an even and thin layer. The layer was subsequently dried under a controlled heat source, such as an oven or an infrared lamp, until it became fully dry and was kept for 72 hours in a vacuum dryer at 40 °C to eliminate the moisture completely. The film was gently pulled off the tray after drying, revealing a flexible, biodegradable film that was ready to be used as a negative triboelectric layer for TENGs. The thickness of the film was 1 mm.

Fabrication of TENGs

To fabricate the radish-based TENG, a bioplastic film derived from radish was prepared first, which was then dried. The size of the radish bioplastic film was 2.5 cm × 2.5 cm. Similarly, the turnip-based TENG was constructed using turnip-derived bioplastic film, which was shaped into dimensions of 2.5 cm × 2.5 cm. Each film was attached to a flexible fabric electrode, pre-cut to align with its corresponding film dimension, and the thickness of this electrode is 70 µm. Finally, polyimide (PI) tape was applied to encapsulate both TENGs individually as a packing layer to completely pack the device and protect the electrode from damage and the connecting leads from disconnecting, enhancing durability and environmental protection to improve their mechanical stability for wearable energy-harvesting and motion-sensing technologies. When these devices were integrated separately with human skin, the radish film and turnip film served as the negative triboelectric layer, and human skin worked as the positive triboelectric layer of the TENG.

Measurement system

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) were used for the material characterization of the bioplastic films. Surface morphology and elemental composition of bioplastic films were analyzed using a ZEISS SmartEDX SEM system. A spectrum FTIR spectroscopy instrument (PerkinElmer) was used to perform functional group analysis for identifying the chemical bonds present in the bioplastic films. The electrical performance was evaluated and recorded using a GwInstek Digital Oscilloscope GDS-1102B. A flexible force-sensitive resistor (FlexiForce A101) was also utilized to record pressure variations during testing. To analyze the charge distribution and optimize the device performance, the potential distribution of the TENGs was simulated using COMSOL Multiphysics software.

Numerical simulation of potential distribution

The electrostatic behavior of the triboelectric nanogenerator (TENG) is investigated using COMSOL Multiphysics 5.4, employing the Finite Element Method (FEM) within a 2D axisymmetric framework. A stationary study is conducted using the electrostatics module, with air defined as the ambient medium. The simulation geometry comprises two triboelectric friction layers and corresponding electrodes, each measuring 1 mm in height and 30 mm in width. The interlayer separation distance is parametrically varied from 0 to 10 mm to emulate the contact–separation cycle intrinsic to TENG operation. Material properties are assigned based on experimental data and COMSOL's material library. Mechanical boundary conditions fix the bottom substrate while applying a time-dependent displacement to the upper triboelectric layer to simulate dynamic separation. Electrical boundary conditions ground one electrode and apply a time-varying potential to the other, while insulating boundaries are treated with floating potential or zero surface charge constraints. Surface charge densities of +1.50 × 10−6 C m−2 and −1.50 × 10−6 C m−2 are imposed on the triboelectric layers to represent the electrostatic field generated during contact electrification. The relative permittivity values are set to 4 for the positively charged material and 2.1 for the negatively charged counterpart, ensuring accurate representation of triboelectric asymmetry. A physics-controlled mesh with standard element sizing is employed, incorporating boundary layer refinement near dielectric interfaces and electrode surfaces to resolve steep electric field gradients. The simulation proceeds by solving the electrostatic potential distribution across the defined geometry, providing insight into charge localization and field intensity under varying separation conditions.

Result and discussion

Material characterization

Fig. 1a depicts the molecular structure of d-glucose (C6H12O6), the most prominent component of radish juice and turnip juice after water, playing a significant role in determining the triboelectric properties of bioplastic films. The main constituents of the pulps collected from radish and turnip are insoluble dietary fibers such as cellulose and hemicellulose (∼30–50%), with lesser amounts of lignin (∼10–25%), pectin, protein, etc. The extracted juice primarily consists of (∼90–93%) water and soluble carbohydrates (∼4–6%), with d-glucose identified as the most prominent component.59,60d-Glucose, a polysaccharide monomer, consists of abundant hydroxyl (–OH) groups that increase its capability to transfer charges, making it a promising triboelectric material. Fig. 1b shows the chemical representation of agar (C14H24O9). This agar powder is incorporated into the radish juice and turnip juice to enhance the mechanical stability and triboelectric properties of radish and turnip juice. Agar, a polysaccharide composed of agarose and agaropectin, is a gelling agent that provides structure to bioplastics, ensuring the materials maintain their form during deformation. The hydroxyl (–OH) groups in agar react with the d-glucose molecules of the juice, leading to an enhancement of the density of triboelectric sites. This interaction amplifies the charge transfer ability of bioplastic films. Fig. 1c and S1 represent the schematic depiction of the procedure of radish- and turnip-based bioplastic film making, respectively. Additionally, glycerin and vinegar are other important components of bioplastic formulation, acting as plasticizers, further increasing the flexibility of the bioplastic film. The combination of d-glucose from radish or turnip juice, agar, glycerin, and vinegar results in a biodegradable, flexible, and highly effective triboelectric material, which is ideal for use in wearable self-powered sensors. Fig. 1d and S2 represent the procedure of fabricating radish and turnip bioplastics, respectively. The microstructure and chemical properties of these two bioplastic films were examined using SEM and FTIR analysis. The SEM provided the surface morphology images (SEM images) of radish-based bioplastic (Fig. 2a and b) and turnip-based bioplastic (Fig. 2c and d). These images reveal a porous and fibrous agar matrix incorporated throughout turnip and radish particles individually, which increases surface roughness and facilitates superior triboelectric charge generation, thereby enhancing triboelectric performance. To investigate the origin of the enhanced triboelectric performance, comparative SEM analysis of pure agar film with radish bioplastic film and turnip bioplastic film was conducted (Fig. S3). SEM images revealed that the radish bioplastic film and turnip bioplastic film exhibited higher surface roughness than the pure agar film. The pure agar film (Fig. S3a) has a relatively smooth and homogeneous surface with only a few dispersed particles. Unlike the smooth surface of the pure agar film, both turnip (Fig. S3b) and radish (Fig. S3c) bioplastics represent noticeably rougher surfaces and more irregular surface morphologies with abundant microstructural features distributed across the surface. Such morphological characteristics can increase the effective contact area during contact–separation cycles with the counter material of the developed TENG device, thereby promoting greater charge generation and improving triboelectric performance. Working principle and simulation result of TENG

The working principle of the TENG is based on the combined effects of the triboelectrification effect and the electrostatic induction effect. The TENGs operate in a single-electrode mode employing vertical contact separation mechanisms, as depicted in Fig. 2g. In this configuration, the bioplastic films function as negative triboelectric layers, while human skin plays the role of a positive triboelectric layer. The behavior of the fabricated bioplastic was confirmed experimentally through its triboelectric interaction with human skin as the counter material, which is known as a positive triboelectric material. Upon repeated contact and separation between these two materials, the produced output signals indicated that electrons were transferred from skin to the bioplastic film's surface, demonstrating the electron-accepting (tribo-negative) nature of the developed bioplastic. Additionally, FTIR analysis indicates the presence of hydroxyl (–OH) groups along with cellulose and d-glucose-derived constituents, and these functional groups influence electron affinity and promote charge trapping in the surface. Together with the observed electrical performance, these findings ensure the tribo-negative nature of the developed bioplastic with respect to human skin. A highly conductive fabric electrode is attached to the bioplastic film to collect the induced triboelectric charge efficiently. When there is no physical contact between the skin and the bioplastic surface, there is no charge transfer between these two layers, and consequently, no potential difference is generated. However, when an external force from finger tapping or walking is applied, the two layers come into contact directly, initiating charge transfer through contact electrification. Due to the different electron affinities of these two layers, electrons move from skin to bioplastic film, thereby leading to a positively charged skin and a negatively charged bioplastic surface. When the applied force has been withdrawn, i.e., during the separation of these two triboelectric materials, the negatively charged bioplastic film generates a potential difference between the electrode and the ground, resulting in electron flow through the external circuit. When the skin presses against the bioplastic film again, the decreasing separation distance reduces the potential difference, causing electrons to return to the electrode and thereby closing the cycle. This continuous contact–separation mechanism of these two triboelectric layers generates a sustained electrical output. The sizes of the radish-based film and turnip-based film are 2.5 cm × 2.5 cm and 2.5 cm × 2.5 cm, respectively, and the thickness of the fabric electrode is 70 µm. Due to the higher conductivity, charge transfer efficiency, and flexibility, the fabric electrode was selected as an electrode. A strong adhesive was used to attach bioplastic films to the fabric electrodes to ensure a stable structure. The radish-based and turnip-based TENGS were attached so that their bioplastic-electrode surfaces directly interfaced with human skin. Fig. 2h shows the photograph of a TENG, and Fig. 2i illustrates the schematic depiction of single-electrode bioplastic material (radish or turnip) based TENGs. To investigate the potential distribution of TENGs operating in vertical contact separation mode through the application of the Finite Element Method (FEM), COMSOL Multiphysics 5.4 software was used. In the simulation procedure, to simulate the real-world human skin–TENG interactions, a 6 mm separation distance of two layers was maintained, while the dielectric permittivity of the bioplastics and human skin was employed based on existing literature data, considering factors such as surface polarity, moisture effects, and charge retention properties on electron transfer. The electrical potential distribution at different gap distances (1 mm, 3 mm, and 6 mm) further indicated the bioplastics' ability to retain charge efficiently, as shown in Fig. 2j.

Electrical characterization of TENGs

The electrical performance of a TENG depends mainly on the electron affinity between the two triboelectric materials. In this research, two distinct TENGs have been designed. In the first configuration, a turnip-based bioplastic film serves as the negative triboelectric layer, while human skin functions as the positive triboelectric layer. Conversely, in the second design, a radish-based bioplastic film employs a negative triboelectric layer, while human skin functions as the positive triboelectric layer. The interaction of these triboelectric pairs plays a crucial role in determining the electrical performance, particularly voltage, current, output power, and power density of both TENGs. We conducted numerous electrical measurements to investigate the triboelectric properties of TENGs, and all the experiments were conducted under ambient conditions with a temperature of approximately 25 °C ± 5 °C. This investigation aims to understand the charge transfer mechanisms between bioplastic film and skin and provides a fundamental insight for enhancing performance and exploring different applications in wearable self-powered sensing technology. A systematic experiment was conducted to determine the most effective bioplastic composition using R-TENG devices fabricated from the bioplastic films with different concentrations of radish–agar composites, and Fig. 3a shows that the 70% radish–agar composite bioplastic film generated the superior output voltage compared to the other compositions. To further reveal the effect of our developed bioplastics on triboelectric performance, a comparative analysis of the generated peak-to-peak voltage of pure agar-based TENG, T-TENG, and R-TENG was demonstrated in Fig. 3b. Both T-TENG and R-TENG exhibited higher output voltages than that of pure agar-based TENG, and this result showed enhanced triboelectric and electrical performances as well. Collectively, based on the comparative SEM, FTIR, and electrical characterization results, the radish-based bioplastic and turnip-based bioplastic demonstrate enhanced triboelectric performance compared with the pure agar film. Furthermore, due to having a rougher surface area with irregular microstructure on the radish bioplastic's surface, with the presence of abundant oxygen-containing functional groups, it exhibits a superior electrical output voltage than the turnip-based TENG. Fig. 3c is the representation of the electrical output voltage during hand-tapping on the T-TENG. T-TENG delivered the best output performance with a peak-to-peak voltage of 336 during hand-tapping. To analyze the optimum output performance, such as current, optimum power, and power density of the T-TENG, we connected the T-TENG to external resistances varying from 10 kΩ to 10 MΩ. The electrical current values shown in Fig. 3d were calculated from the experimentally measured output voltage using Ohm's law (V = I × R) rather than measuring directly, and the output voltages were obtained by gentle hand tapping. Fig. 3d represents an inverse relationship between current and resistance, while the output voltage of T-TENG increased with the increase in the external load and therefore achieved a maximum peak-to-peak voltage of 336 V at 10 MΩ. Under the same conditions, as the resistance increased, the current showed a reduction, reaching a minimum value of 33.6 µA at 10 MΩ. At an external resistance of 1 MΩ, the T-TENG achieved its maximum power output of 13.93 mW following the maximum power transfer theorem, i.e., P = V2/4R. However, output power reduced beyond this resistance due to the impedance mismatch (Fig. S4). As the dimension of T-TENG is 2.5 cm × 2.5 cm, the maximum output power density is 19.34 W m−2, which was obtained with an external resistance of 1 MΩ (Fig. 3e). The dependency of voltage on hand-tapping frequency of T-TENG is illustrated in Fig. 3f. At lower frequencies, such as 1 Hz, T-TENG employed a lower output peak-to-peak voltage of 188 V due to having longer time intervals between contact–separation cycles. When the frequency was increased gradually and reached 4 Hz, the cycle occurred more rapidly, resulting in more frequent charge generation and redistribution per cycle. This exhibits a higher output voltage (peak-to-peak) of 336 V, highlighting the correlation between excitation frequency and output voltage. Output voltage was recorded at different levels of applied forces and pressures to examine the sensitivity of the T-TENG, which is shown in Fig. 3g and h. T-TENG's output voltage increases proportionally with the applied force. It suggests that stronger contact enhances charge density and thereby elevates efficient charge separation and increases voltage generation. To showcase the practical functionality of the T-TENG in pressure-sensing applications such as self-powered motion monitoring or tactile sensing applications, its performance was investigated under various externally applied pressures varying from 10 kPa to 80 kPa. This self-powered sensor output exhibited a positive correlation with external pressure, obtaining a maximum value of 336 V (peak-to-peak) at 50 kPa. Beyond this point, the output voltage reached the saturation level, indicating that triboelectric materials attained their maximum contact interface. According to the result, two distinct linear regions were identified in the pressure range (8–20 kPa) with a high linear correlation coefficient (R2 = 0.977) and sensitivity of 15.09 V kPa−1, and in the pressure range of 20–50 kPa, the sensitivity dropped by 3.12 V kPa−1, although the correlation between the generated voltage and pressure remained almost linear (R2 = 0.989). To examine the durability and stability of T-TENG, a durability assessment of the device was carried out. The obtained results are illustrated in Fig. S5, clearly elucidating that the electrical performance remained almost unchanged, confirming excellent durability and stability even after 16 000 cycles at 4 Hz during hand tapping. The output signals exhibited negligible variations during the entire period, confirming its stable electrical performance with operational stability and durability. Fig. 4a presents the output voltage generated by finger tapping on the R-TENG. R-TENG reached the highest output performance, with a peak-to-peak voltage of 344 during finger tapping. R-TENG was connected to an external load, and the output performance was measured to further examine the output performance of the TENG as a power source. In Fig. 4b–d, the electrical performances, including current, power, and power density of R-TENG, are demonstrated. With an increase in resistance, the current exhibited a reduction, dropping to 34.4 µA at 10 MΩ. Under the same conditions, it exhibited an increase in output voltage at all resistance levels, reaching a maximum of 344 V peak-to-peak at 10 MΩ. The R-TENG obtained a peak output power of 16.38 mW at a resistance of 1 MΩ, after which the output power began to decline. The maximum power density of 26.01 W m−2 was observed for the R-TENG, whose dimensions were 2.50 cm × 2.50 cm. The dependency of output voltage on frequency was also investigated, and it was shown in Fig. 4e that at a frequency of 1 Hz, R-TENG produced 196 V peak-to-peak, which reached up to 344 V peak-to-peak when the frequency increased to 4 Hz, showcasing effective charge transfer across varying excitation frequencies. The observed rise in voltage can be explained by the acceleration- and impact-speed-induced improvements in charge transfer rate. It is postulated that a higher hand-tapping frequency accelerates the movement of external electrons, thereby avoiding the complete neutralization of the friction layer's surface charge. These findings provide clear evidence of a positive correlation between hand-tapping frequency and electrical output voltage. As depicted in Fig. 4f, the output voltage is enhanced with an increase in the applied force to the R-TENG. The electrical output voltage rose from peak-to-peak 97 V to 344 V with the incremental application of external force ranging from 5 N to almost 33 N. The primary reason behind this relationship is that stronger mechanical forces lead to improved interfacial contact in R-TENG, which boosts surface charge density and subsequently increases the output performance. Fig. 4g highlights the correlation between the generated voltage and applied pressure to analyze the real-world applicability of the R-TENG as a self-powered sensor. Its electrical performance was examined under different pressure levels ranging from 10 kPa to 80 kPa. The output increased proportionally with the applied pressure, with the voltage reaching up to 344 V (peak-to-peak) at 48.5 kPa. Subsequently, the voltage levels off, suggesting that the radish-based bioplastic and human skin have maximized their effective contact area at 48.5 kPa. The R-TENG revealed dual-linear behavior, comprising R1 (low-pressure zone, below 17 kPa) and R2 (high-pressure zone, 17–48.5 kPa), showing sensitivities of 15.28 V kPa−1 (correlation coefficient = 0.982) and 3.22 V kPa−1 (correlation coefficient = 0.997, which was almost linear), respectively. Hence, this self-powered sensor can be employed in personalized healthcare applications such as motion monitoring and tactile sensing. To analyze its mechanical stability and durability under continuous operation for real-time development, an assessment was conducted on the R-TENG. At room temperature, a water-induced degradation experiment was performed to assess the biodegradability of radish-based bioplastic (Fig. S6(a–f)). During the early stage of immersion, the bioplastic exhibited no visible deterioration. However, after around 8 minutes, discernible cracks emerged on a particular section of the bioplastic. The fissures expanded to multiple areas across the surface within 12 minutes. During exposure to light and manual shaking of water, the bioplastic disintegrated into smaller fragments after a few minutes. Finally, the entire bioplastic was broken down and underwent aqueous dissolution completely within 25–27 minutes, indicating its biodegradable nature and ecological sustainability. To investigate the performance of the R-TENG under different humidity conditions, a systematic experiment was conducted using a customized environmental chamber that allowed precise control of relative humidity. The output performance of the R-TENGs was recorded under varying humidity levels, and the results are demonstrated in Fig. 5a, with an inset showing the experimental setup. The data revealed a progressive decline in peak-to-peak output voltage with increasing humidity, which can be attributed to the adsorption of water molecules onto the hydrophilic surfaces of the bioplastic films. This adsorption facilitated charge dissipation and suppressed effective charge transfer, thereby reducing triboelectric performance. To assess the possible degradation effects, the output performance of the R-TENG was systematically investigated and recorded at different time intervals over 15 days. The recorded signals shown in Fig. 5b exhibited minimal variations, demonstrating consistent electrical signals with no significant changes in signal intensity, confirming the excellent long-term stability of the developed device. In a recent study, Liu et al. have demonstrated that ferroelectric polymers can achieve significantly improved piezoelectric performance through morphotropic phase boundary design along with structural engineering approaches, offering efficient electromechanical energy conversion.73 Furthermore, Yuan et al. developed a scalable solution-processing method to optimize polymer architectures and enhance piezoelectric responses, confirming their potential for flexible and self-powered sensing applications.74 Unlike ferroelectric polymer-based piezoelectric nanogenerators, where the enhanced performance relies on polarization and piezoelectric coefficient, the output performance of the developed T-TENG and R-TENG is governed by surface chemical composition, surface morphology, and functional groups. The comparative analysis of the power density of recently published natural materials-based TENGs and the developed TENGs of this research work is illustrated in Fig. 5f. These enhanced performances support the R-TENG's effectiveness in sustainable power generation and wearable technologies.

Application and demonstration

The practical feasibility of the R-TENG in real-world scenarios was assessed by integrating the fabricated device into two distinct wearable configurations—within a smart shoe insole and on a stress relief ball for biomechanical energy harvesting, biomotion monitoring, and tactile sensing. These topologies were evaluated to utilize the potential of the biodegradable, eco-friendly TENG as a self-powered sensor for real-time motion and tactile sensing. For motion monitoring applications, the R-TENG was embedded at the zenith of the shoe sole to ensure interaction with the foot during walking, as demonstrated in Fig. S8a. Fig. 6a and b reveals the output waveforms captured under different walking conditions (fast walking, slow walking). During fast walking conditions (Fig. 6a), the TENG exhibited a highly dynamic voltage response characterized by densely clustered voltage spikes of greater amplitude, indicating stronger foot–TENG interaction and rapid charge transfer due to stronger mechanical contact. Compared to fast walking, slow walking generated signals with a lower peak and a more consistent pattern (Fig. 6b), reflecting the reduced contact force and frequency. This variation confirms the R-TENG's capability for sensing different motion patterns and enabling real-time gait monitoring and step analysis. In the tactile sensing setup, R-TENG was incorporated onto the surface of a stress relief ball (Fig. S8b) to evaluate its tactile sensing capability. The output waveforms during different pressing speeds are illustrated in Fig. 6c and S9. Under rapid pressing and releasing of the ball (Fig. S9), the R-TENG generated rapid and distinct signal peaks with high amplitude, highlighting swift contact–separation cycles and effective triboelectric response. In contrast, the slower actuation (Fig. 6c) resulted in reduced signal intensity, more widely spaced signal peaks with reduced amplitude, and lower triboelectric output due to lower deformation speed and lighter contact interactions. These findings confirm the R-TENG's versatility in dual sensing applications—gait sensing and tactile sensing applications as a proof of concept. The generated output signals by R-TENG were recorded in Fig. 6d from three different individuals, demonstrating the user-dependent variation of R-TENG. To validate the performance of the developed R-TENG, a real-world performance test was conducted using this device as a power source to illuminate a series of 62 commercial LEDs under a periodic impact force of around 15 N at a tapping frequency of 4 Hz. The intense illumination, as captured in Fig. 6e and Movie S1 in SI, highlights the device's remarkable energy-harvesting efficiency. Fig. 6f demonstrates the charging behavior of the R-TENG for capacitances of 2.2 µF and 4.7 µF utilizing a bridge rectifier (MB10F bridge rectifier) and thereby storing power from mechanical energy. The newly fabricated R-TENG obtained output voltages of peak-to-peak 5.48 V and peak-to-peak 3.52 V across 2.2 µF and 4.7 µF capacitors, respectively, at a tapping frequency of 4 Hz. These 2.2 µF and 4.7 µF capacitors obtained peak-to-peak 5.48 V and 3.52 V, respectively, at a tapping frequency of 4 Hz. The stored charge in the capacitor could be effectively utilized as a power source for next-generation smart and portable electronics. The energy harvesting capability in distinguishable electrical responses to different interactions makes it a promising candidate for integration into wearable electronics, real-time health tracking, and gesture-based interface systems. Its ability to generate distinguishable electrical responses based on user interaction speed and force renders it suitable for wearable health monitoring, interactive input systems, and security detection interfaces. Due to its notable sensitivity, mechanical flexibility, and sustainability, the newly designed R-TENG offers a viable solution for the development of advanced biodegradable sensing platforms.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this research work introduces an eco-friendly, flexible bioplastic film fabricated from nature-derived materials (radish or turnip extract) reinforced with agar to design a high-performance, sustainable TENG for self-powered sensing applications. The unification of natural d-glucose-rich radish or turnip extract with agar polysaccharides yielded a mechanically flexible bioplastic film with enriched hydroxyl and carbonyl groups, facilitating promising surface charge density and triboelectric performance. The R-TENG demonstrated significantly superior electrical performance, delivering a higher output peak-to-peak voltage and higher power density, with enhanced sensitivity in both gait and tactile sensing applications compared to the T-TENG counterpart. The fabricated R-TENG delivered impressive triboelectric characteristics, reaching a peak-to-peak voltage of ∼344 V, a power density of ∼26.01 W m−2, and an exceptional pressure sensitivity of 15.28 V kPa−1 within ∼9–17 kPa pressure range. The R-TENG exhibited impressive self-powered sensing capabilities in both wearable gait and tactile sensing applications, with remarkable sensitivity, mechanical adaptability, and biodegradability, confirming biomechanical signal detection. This research work demonstrates a sustainable pathway to engineer high-performing biodegradable triboelectric materials, promoting environmental sustainability and reducing dependence on synthetic materials in future wearable and self-powered electronic platforms.
