Multi-hierarchical biofunctional polymeric biomaterial to promote wound closure
Authors: Zacharie Deruyver, Naïma Ahmed Omar, Claire de Lartigue, Noémie Petit, Louise Hosty, Aline Echalard, Chloé Eudier, Shane Browne, Christophe Egles, Anthony C. Duncan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic wounds constitute a significant medical, societal, and economic issue due to the significant delay in wound closure, leading to the increased risk of infection, which extends patients’ time spent in the hospital and negatively impacts overall health. Biofunctional biomaterials have emerged as powerful tools to promote tissue regeneration by mimicking extracellular matrix properties and delivering therapeutic agents. Furthermore, wound closure is a complex and multi-stage process that requires coordination of molecular and cellular factors, such as growth factor signaling and matrix remodeling agents. To address this issue, hierarchically structured and compartmentalized multifunctional biomaterials that include hydrogels have emerged as an effective solution to promote wound healing. In the context of chronic wounds, Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS)-responsive hydrogels are interesting models which specifically trigger a response (e.g., gel opening followed by drug release) in the vicinity of high levels of ROS – specific biomarkers of a chronic state. In this study, we developed a hierarchical multi-layer hybrid biomaterial (HMHB) via the sequential combination of melt electrowriting (MEW) and electrospinning techniques. This HMHB will contain two parts: (i) a macro/micro structured polycaprolactone (PCL) support (MSS) printed via MEW onto which (ii) a polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) electrospun (micro)fibrous film (EFF) was deposited, resulting in a ROS-responsive bioactive antibacterial hydrogel meshwork upper layer that allows for further biofunctionalization. The final construct exhibited a hierarchical architecture composed of a mechanically robust PCL framework and a PVA nanotextured layer. This configuration ensured both structural integrity and homogeneous fiber coverage. Incorporation of Nisin (an antibacterial agent) directly into the EFF prior to the electrospinning step conferred effective antimicrobial activity against Staphylococcus epidermidis, without the need for additional chemical modification. These results establish the HMHB as a promising proof-of-concept for modular and multifunctional wound dressing elaboration.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Chronic wounds affect millions of people worldwide and represent a major healthcare burden. In Europe, over 60 million individuals live with diabetes, and diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) represent one of its most severe and costly complications [1]. Cost treatment for a single DFU patient is estimated to reach up to €30,000, while conventional methods such as debridement and standard dressings often fail to achieve full wound closure and healing [2, 3]. The latter results in prolonged hospitalization, risk of amputation, increased patient suffering, and higher mortality, negatively impacting a patient’s quality of life and therefore placing a severe burden on healthcare systems [4]. Wound healing is a complex and multi-phase process involving hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and maturation, which involves tissue remodeling as well as angiogenesis [5–7]. In chronic wounds, this sequence is disrupted by the blockage of the wound healing process in the inflammation state, characterized by abnormally high levels of Reactive Oxidative Species (ROS) [5–13] in the wound environment. Other chronic wound issues include dysfunctional angiogenesis, a process essential for adequate delivery of vital oxygen and nutrients to the regenerating tissue [14]. Persistent hypoxia and microbial infection further hinder healing by worsening inflammation and inhibiting skin barrier recovery [4, 15]. To overcome these challenges, tissue engineering has focused on developing biomaterials capable of mimicking the extracellular matrix (ECM) while providing mechanically, structurally, and bio-functionally adequate substrates capable of enhancing tissue regeneration [16–18]. Electrospun fibrous films (EFFs) have shown promise due to their (nano)fibrous architecture and ability to promote cell migration and infiltration [19]. Melt electrowriting (MEW) provides another level of structural control, allowing for the fabrication of micro-patterned supports with defined mechanical properties [20, 21]. The combination of both techniques allows for the elaboration of a hierarchical biomaterial with spatial compartmentalization [20]. Furthermore, new developments in processing techniques have enabled a greater enhancement of scaffold complexity through the incorporation of biofunctional hydrogels [19, 22].

Additionally, breakthroughs in the field of ROS stimuli-responsive release systems of therapeutic agents have allowed for adaptive behaviour of the material to the evolving wound environment during the healing process. To that effect, a wide variety of polymers, both natural (e.g., silk fibroin, hyaluronic acid) and synthetic (e.g., polycaprolactone, polyvinyl alcohol), can be used to construct these advanced hierarchically structured biomaterials with multiple levels of organisation/structure and functions [17, 23, 24].

In this study, we developed a hybrid wound dressing combining a melt electrowritten scaffold and an electrospun ROS-responsive PVA hydrogel upper layer. The potential of the latter to respond to an ROS-rich environment was investigated. The electrospun layer is designed to provide antimicrobial bio-responsive activity to the wound in the chronic state with a view to overcoming the chronic inflammation impediment. In turn, the MEW scaffold is designed to provide mechanical support to the electrospun layer. It may also be used as a reservoir for the liberation of other bioactive agents, although this is not within the scope of this paper.

To our knowledge, such an approach using such materials, for the elaboration of a multi-hierarchical/multifunctional adaptive wound dressing system, as reported here, has never been published before.

Materials and methods

Preparation of PVA solution

PVA (115 kDa, 86,5-89% hydrolysed, VWR BDH Chemicals, USA) solution for electrospinning (ES) was prepared as follows: 48 g of ultrapure water and 3.6 g of ethanol were mixed in a sealed glass bottle with a magnetic stir bar at room temperature for at least 15 min. Once the water-ethanol solution was homogeneous, 8.4 g of PVA were added to obtain a final PVA-water-ethanol solution with the respective mass fractions of 14–80–6%. The sealed flask was then heated in a water bath at 80–90 °C under continuous magnetic stirring to ensure complete dissolution of PVA. Care was taken not to exceed 90 °C, the deemed optimal temperature range for good PVA dissolution [25], and to avoid unwanted PVA alteration as witnessed by observed PVA-water solution colour change from transparent to yellow-orange after long duration heating at high temperatures ( > 90°).

Once the solution became transparent and free of suspended crystals, it was cooled down to room temperature. The final mass was adjusted with the solvent to compensate for eventual evaporation losses (when required). Finally, these solutions were stored at 4 °C (for up to two weeks maximum) and allowed to reach room temperature before the electrospinning step. PVA solution containing the antibacterial agent Nisin (Anhui Minmetals Development, China) was prepared as above, and the drug was further dissolved at an amount equivalent to a 0.5% mass fraction.

Electrospun fibrous films fabrication

The electrospun fibrous films (EFFs) were obtained via electrospinning using the E-FIBER EF200 (SKE Research Equipment, Italy) within a closed chamber equipped with ventilation and camera monitoring. The setup included a 10 mL Luer-Lock syringe (Terumo), PTFE tubing ( ~ 10 cm), stainless steel needles (17 G) and male/female Luer-lock connectors. The electrospinning process was conducted using a programmable syringe pump to control the polymer solution flow. The optimized electrospinning conditions were as follows: an applied voltage ranging from 15 to 20 kV, a flow rate between 0.4-0.7 mL/h, and a needle-to-collector distance set at 15 cm. This process was carried out at an ambient temperature of 23–24 °C with a relative humidity rate ranging from 18 to 40%. The electrospun fibers were collected on a flat metallic plate covered with aluminum foil serving as the collector surface, for approximately 2 to 3 h per run. ES was performed at higher collection durations (up to 4 h) when a thicker film was required. No pre-treatment of the PVA solution or post-treatment of the fibers was applied.

Macro/micro structured support fabrication via the melt electrowriting (MEW) procedure

The macro/micro structured support (MSS) was obtained via MEW. It was performed using the Axolotl MEW printing system (Axolotl Biosystems, Turkey) equipped with a temperature-controlled printhead, a high-voltage generator and a flat collector platform. Poly(ε-caprolactone) (PCL, Axolotl Biosystem, Turkey) was used as the printing material. It was loaded into the metallic syringe and heated up to 140 °C for an hour. A pneumatic pressure of 55 kPa was first applied to initiate flow, then reduced to 14-28 kPa for the rest of the deposition process. The print head voltage was set at 4 kV, with a printing speed of 14 mm/s and an offset surface (nozzle-to-substrate distance) of 4 mm. Scaffold fabrication was conducted by printing 3–5 layers of parallel and orthogonally oriented microfilaments with an 80% infill density. All structures were deposited on a flat collector under ambient environmental conditions. The printed constructions were stored in a sterile container at room temperature.

After MEW printing, as described above, the PCL scaffolds were cooled to room temperature and used as substrates for electrospinning. Electrospinning was performed directly onto the MEW-printed scaffolds over a 2-hour period using the aqueous PVA solution described above, according to the aforementioned conditions.

The electrospun layer conformed to the MEW template, producing a dual-composition scaffold with dual-scale architecture consisting of both mechanical support and high surface area for potential biological interactions.

Biomechanical testing of HMHB

Tensile Testing Uniaxial assays were performed on HMHBs using a T-SERIES universal testing machine (Rycobel). Samples were clamped vertically between two flat grips, each securing 3 mm of material at both ends. The effective gauge length (L₀) was 22 mm for a total specimen height of 28 mm. Tensile loading was applied at a constant speed of 5 mm/min until sample failure. Force displacement rate was recorded in real time. Stress-strain curves were subsequently derived based on the measured initial gauge length and cross-sectional area data.

Assessment of antimicrobial activity of HMHB

Under a laminar hood, nutrient agar (Biolab NUA20500) was poured into two Petri dishes, filling them two-thirds full, and then left to stand for 2 min while slightly open.

Next, in a sterile hemolysis tube containing 4.5 mL of sterile physiological saline solution (NaCl, Sigma-Aldrich, S9625 at 9 g/L), three colonies of Staphylococcus epidermidis (Pasteur collection, CIP 53.124) were deposited using a loop to obtain a bacterial suspension to be inoculated onto the agar.

The swab was dipped into the suspension and then wrung out. The bacterial suspension was spread uniformly on the agar. PVA fibers were functionalised with a Nisin Z solution (Anhui Minmetals Development, China) by co-electrospinning the two molecules. Using sterile forceps, PCL samples coated with PVA fibers functionalized with Nisin Z were carefully placed on the bacterial cultures on the agar. Light pressure was applied to each sample to ensure that all the material was in contact with the agar. A PCL sample coated with PVA fibers without Nisin will serve as a negative control as well as a positive control of Nisin alone. The Petri dishes were then incubated at 37°C for 24 h.

Cytotoxicity assessment of HMHB

L929 cell line (American Type Culture Collection ATCC, CCL-1TM), derived from subcutaneous tissue of a male mouse, was used to perform an indirect cytotoxicity assay in accordance with ISO standard 10,993–5:2009 [26]. Cells were cultured in complete Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Sigma Aldrich, D5796) supplemented with 10% (v/v) foetal bovine serum (Gibco, 10270-106) and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin (Corning, 30-002-C1) under a controlled atmosphere (5% CO2). Square EFFs of 5 mm were placed in a 24-well plate and covered with 1.2 mL of culture medium overnight. Sample extracts (PCL, PVA, and PVA+Nisin, n = 3/condition) were collected, and 200 µL of culture medium for each sample was added to the L929 cells layer (American Type Culture Collection ATCC, CCL-1TM), which had been seeded beforehand at 30,000 cells/cm². The plate was placed in the incubator (37°C, 5% CO2) for 24 h.

Metabolic activity was assessed by alamarBlueTM assay (Invitrogen, A50101), which was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, alamarBlueTM reagent was diluted 1:10 in a complete DMEM medium and heated to 37 °C. The culture medium was removed from the wells containing the cells and replaced with the diluted alamarBlueTM reaction medium. The cells were incubated (37 °C, 5% CO2) for 3 h, and the supernatants were then collected for each sample and analysed in a microplate reader (Biotek®, Synergy TM 2). Absorbances were read at 570 and 600 nm. The metabolic activity of the cells was expressed as a percentage of reduced alamarBlue™ relative to the negative control (cells alone).

Reactive oxygen species (ROS)

This experiment was conducted in a randomized, double-blind manner to ensure unbiased results and reproducibility. To evaluate the scaffold’s responsiveness to reactive oxygen species (ROS), EFFs were first treated for 24 h in an ethanol solution of phenyldiboronic acid (DBA)—known to be an ROS-sensitive crosslinker with respect to PVA hydrogels, at varying concentrations (0.5, 1, 3, 5 and 10%(w/v)). The purpose of this step is to render the PVA hydrogel resistant to water dissolution in the absence of ROS, by forming a 3D PVA macromolecular stable network in pure water. However, in the presence of ROS, the DBA crosslinked PVA hydrogel structure is expected to collapse due to the destruction of its ROS-sensitive crosslinks holding it together and would thus return to a liquid state (dissolution), potentially liberating any drug previously loaded within.

To validate such an effect, after treatment, the samples were immersed in an ROS, the hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) solution or distilled water as a control, and their degradation was monitored over time.

Testing of ROS responsiveness for the DBA-treated PVA fiber samples

As aforementioned, a set of EFFs was treated via exposure for various durations to ethanol containing an ROS-sensitive crosslinker (DBA). This was also performed at different DBA concentrations. The ROS sensitivity of these treated samples was then evaluated via exposure of the latter to a hydrogen peroxide (ROS) solution. If ROS-responsiveness was present, the treated samples were expected to be partially or totally degraded (dissolved) upon exposure to the ROS-containing solution.

Indeed, this was the case for all DBA-treated EFFs, with visible signs of partial to total degradation within 3 h of exposure to the ROS solution (hydrogen peroxide). Total degradation was observed within 24 h for all DBA-treated EFFs irrespective of the DBA concentration used during treatment. In contrast, the same DBA-treated EFFs immersed in pure water (absent of ROS) remained stable and preserved their integrity (no dissolution observed). Discussion

This study establishes proof of concept for the design of a multistructural/multifunctional wound dressing that combines appropriate architectural, physical, chemical, mechanical, antimicrobial, and ROS-responsiveness wound healing functions.

The HMHB presented here is composed of a controlled double-layer structure : a PCL melt electro-written MSS underlayer topped with an electrospun PVA EFF overlayer. The latter conferred adequate mechanical properties with high tailoring versatility as well as high spatial control precision [28].

Our mechanical results reflect the composition and structural patterns of our HMBC. The PCL MSS star- and square-patterned exhibited a Young’s modulus in the same order of magnitude range. However, the former exhibited higher elongation at rupture than the latter. This star-patterned MSS also ruptured in a different way since the rupture line appeared slanted and chaotic (Fig. 4D) compared to a quasi-straight, clean-cut rupture line for the square-patterned MSS (Fig. 4C). This observation, combined with the higher elongation at break, suggests better stress redistribution within the sample (leading to higher mechanical resistance) for the star-patterned MSS compared to that for its square-patterned homolog.

The PVA EFF exhibited lower Young’s modulus and higher elongation at break compared to the MSS substrate, suggesting a softer, more flexible and ductile material. This may be partly explained by mechanical differences in PVA and PCL polymer mechanical properties (i.e., PVA being, for instance, more ductile than PCL). This resistance to brutal fracture for the PVA EFF is imparted to the whole when assembled to the underlying star-patterned or square-patterned PCL-based MSS. Additionally, the ESS PVA layer added to the MSS layer may likely modify load distribution within the whole structure, resulting in lower stress concentration on MSS filaments. PVA being less brittle than PCL also likely allows for better internal stress relaxation within the sample when backed to the MSS layer compared to the latter alone.

In that respect, wound dressing mechanical properties are highly important to ensure appropriate physical protection, patient comfort, and conformability with skin biomechanics. Optimal performance requires an adequate balance between parameters such as stiffness, mechanical strength, and flexibility to conform with native skin characteristics [29]. For instance, excessively high dressing stiffness ( > 300 kPa) is known to increase peri-wound tissue-damaging risks. On the other hand, excessively low stiffness yields poor wound protection and may lead to dressing failure. Although skin stiffness depends on factors such as patient age, skin hydration rate and body localisation, literature reports values in the range of 5 kPa to 140 MPa [30, 31]. Our results are promising since the stiffness values for our HMHB and its individual components fall within that range. Furthermore, another advantage of our design approach is that the final HMHB stiffness values may be tuned by playing with the ratio of PCL MSS to PVA EFF. A lower latter ratio would be expected to lead to lower overall HMHB stiffness values (and vice versa), since MSS exhibits a stiffer nature (higher Young’s modulus) compared to that for the EFF layer. Our approach could thus allow our HMHB, via structural and polymer nature composition, to optimally match the required biomechanical specifications (i.e., stiffness, mechanical resistance, elongation at break) for any given desired biomaterial application.

Furthermore, electrospinning is a cost-effective, versatile, and relatively simple way of obtaining high surface ratio structures at a nano-level scale [16, 32]. The EFF obtained here allowed a high specific surface area. This is relevant for the incorporation of high amounts of bioactive agents (e.g., antibacterial and/or antibiofilm agents). These may be incorporated and maintained within the EEF via weak interactions or covalently linked onto the fibres (via a stimuli-responsive bond, for instance, if required).

In this work, we were able to impart ROS-responsive function to the EFF layer, to allow for adaptability to the rapidly evolving microenvironment of the wound, in particular that of chronic wounds [14] where oxidative stress is substantial. In that respect, ROS-triggered disintegration of the EFF may be desirable for appropriate on-demand release of bioactive agents in the context of tissue regeneration improvement and development of smart chronic wound dressings.

This ROS-responsive function was transmitted to the EFF after exposure to an ROS-sensitive crosslinking agent—the phenyldiboronic acid agent (DBA). The latter rendered the EFF insoluble in water alone but soluble in the presence of the ROS chronic state biomarker. Furthermore, the antimicrobial function was imparted thanks to the integration of an antibiotic peptide (Nisin) within the PVA polymer solution before the electrospinning step, consistent with previous studies [33].

One other important advantage of the electrospinning approach is the preservation of the bioactivity of incorporated agents - small [34] or large molecules (peptides, proteins) [35–37] and even particles [38].

Combining these techniques allows for great design versatility – enabling adaptability of our system to a large array of wound types, whether acute or chronic, and at different healing stages, rendering it amenable to tissue regeneration applications [20, 39–42]. This design approach conveys a high degree of control over both final architecture and bioactivity compartmentalisation.

Furthermore, it allows for great flexibility via rational material selection during the different elaboration steps. This was also central to our design strategy. In this work, PCL was selected due to its ease for MEW processing into the MSS layer, its printability, and mechanical robustness [21, 43]. PVA was chosen for its hydrophilic properties, high water content, and its ease to be integrated into an electrospinning process, thus offering a semi-controlled fiber mesh structure (EFF) akin to the natural extracellular matrix [19, 23].

Additionally, PVA, due to its numerous backbone hydroxyl functions, allows for multiple biofunctionalizations, either via weak H-bonding or via covalent grafting onto these functional groups, thus offering a versatile system adaptable to multiple therapeutic requirements [44–47].

Furthermore, to address the issue of potential external or internal infection, particularly in the field of chronic wounds, the Nisin antibacterial peptide was incorporated. Its selection was motivated by its strong inhibition of Gram-positive activity, particularly against dermal bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis, as well as methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) strains [48], its low toxicity [49] and its compatibility with polymeric solutions [50]. Nisin’s antibacterial activity mode of action is based on rapid bacterial membrane disruption and inhibition of cell wall synthesis (via binding to lipid II), making it a highly effective bactericidal agent against a broad range of Gram-positive bacteria. Unlike other antibiotics, Nisin is also less likely to contribute to bacterial resistance and can be directly incorporated into the electrospun solution before EFF elaboration without the requirement for further chemical modification [50, 51].

With respect to the final HMHB preparation, the dual process platform remains modular and adaptable, offering possibilities for adjustments and versatility on fiber density, architecture, and material composition, to name a few. These adjustments may be tailored to suit the specific clinical application.

Overall, our results in this paper demonstrate good complementarity between both MEW and ES techniques and respond to a growing need in the field of wound care: the shift from passive to active and adaptive dressings [52, 53]. Next-generation dressings should do more than just protect; they must also interact with the wound, deliver and respond to inner cues, prevent infection, and encourage tissue regeneration [2, 20, 42, 52, 53].

Although hierarchal wound dressings have already been reported in the literature [54–58], to our knowledge the approaches and materials used here to design a multi-hierarchical/multifunctional adaptive wound dressing have never been published before. To this effect, our hybrid MSS-EFF structure offers a basis system capable of evolving and adapting to various therapeutic objectives, fitting into this rapidly moving field. Looking forward, one major area of interest is the further addition of functionalized layers. To match the various steps of a multi-hierarchical elaboration, the final step for bioactive functionality integration may be achieved via loading bioactive molecules directly into the EFF matrix. Future design evolutions, based on our concept of a biologically active layer playing as a drug reservoir for sustained stimuli-controlled release, is a very useful feature for a wide range of chronic wound treatments.

Our results also confirmed the ROS-responsive ability of the DBA-treated EFF network (DBA-EFF). Indeed, experimental results confirmed that, regardless of studied DBA concentrations (0.5% to 10%), the DBA-EFF degraded consistently within 3 h of ROS exposure to H2O2, while remaining stable in water. This finding highlights the material’s potential for controlled, on-demand, ROS-responsive therapeutic drug release in oxidative environments, such as inflamed and infected chronic wounds [20]. This PVA-based ROS-responsiveness, as we described previously [27], provides an additional functionality layer and opens pathways for targeted delivery of therapeutic agents in future versions.

It is noteworthy to mention here that while this study primarily focuses on structural design and antimicrobial activity characterization, further biological investigations will be considered. These include evaluating the scaffold’s influence on fibroblast migration and proliferation using scratch assays and growth factor-enriched environments, epidermal growth factor (EGF) in particular. These studies will aim to provide a deeper understanding of our multiple-hierarchical system’s regenerative capacities. In parallel, we are exploring the interaction of bioactive antibacterial agent-loaded foams (with Nisin in particular). These foams will be intended to occupy the internal compartments of the MSS structures, offering both volume filling and enhanced biological functionality [43]. This strategy aims to mimic the hierarchical nature of native tissue environments while locally delivering therapeutic drug activity, especially in highly infected or high-risk wound sites.

It is worth noting that beyond wound healing applications, the MEW approach employed in this study allows the possibility of fabricating electroconductive devices for a broader range of tissue regeneration applications. Although we have not yet explored electroconductive integration in this paper, conductive polymers such as polyaniline (PANi) or poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) polystyrene sulfonate (PEDOT: PSS) are being investigated for their ability to modulate cellular behaviours, particularly in nerve or muscle-rich tissues [2, 23, 59]. The surface coating of conductive polymers could enable the development of smart wound dressings capable of promoting cell proliferation and/or differentiation using external electrical stimulation [60, 61]. In that respect, Liu et al. fabricated a PEDOT: PSS/chitosan-coated PLGA electrospun fiber as a nerve guide conduit. It helped enhance axonal regeneration, Schwann cells alignment, and functional recovery in a sciatic nerve crush rat model [62]. In addition to their electroconductive properties, some conductive polymers, like PANi, possess intrinsic antibacterial activity, which is being investigated against common wound pathogens, including Staphylococcus aureus [60]. Moreover, emerging electroconductive elastomer composites based on bio-derived components are under investigation as sustainable alternatives to offer greener synthesis routes [63]. Regarding the PCL backbone, the incorporation of short oligomer units derived from natural sources, such as triarylmethane, can impart a range of biological activities, including anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidant and/or antibacterial effects, which are needed to enhance wound repair [64].

In summary, while this study presents a first proof-of-concept, the system is highly customizable, and its modularity opens the door to future developments in smart wound dressing and tissue regeneration applications. Whether through integrating sensors, multiple functionality, or biostimuli-controlled release strategies, this platform could serve as a basis for next-generation therapies tailored for complex wound treatment, tissue regeneration or tissue engineering [20, 51, 65, 66].

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a hybrid wound dressing that combines structural support, antimicrobial activity and ROS-responsiveness.

A macro/micro structured polycaprolactone (PCL) support (MSS) was printed via MEW onto which (ii) a polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) electrospun macro-/microfibrous film (EFF) was deposited. The latter could be made ROS-responsive by treatment with an ROS-sensitive crosslinker (DBA). This allowed the fabrication of a hierarchical multi-layer hybrid biomaterial (HMHB) into which multiple bioactive agents can be incorporated in a spatially controlled fashion.

Furthermore, while allowing further biofunctionalization, our modular HMHB additionally has the ability to mimic the native extracellular matrix. Together, these features establish a promising foundation for the next generation of wound care and/or tissue regeneration systems. Future iterations may integrate controlled release of various therapeutic drugs and/or electroconductive elements, opening new pathways for dynamic and personalized healing approaches. In that respect, integration of bioactive agents into nanostructured platforms presents great potential to address growing challenges in human health issues [67–73]. This study is the base platform for more elaborate multifunctional multi-hierarchical systems exhibiting the capacity to adapt to the challenge of diverse, rapidly evolving healing environments.

Thus, our approach proposed here is promising as it paves the way to a highly versatile path for the flexible controlled elaboration of adaptive biomaterials not only as dressings for better chronic wound healing, but also of new generations of bioactive multi-hierarchical scaffolds for tissue repair and regeneration.
