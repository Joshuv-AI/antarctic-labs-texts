Exploring Parents' Values in Healthcare Decision‐Making for Rare Genetic Neurodevelopmental Disorders: A Qualitative Study to Inform Guideline Development
Authors: Mirthe J Klein Haneveld, Louise Cox, Petri J C M Embregts, Alistair R Niemeijer, Martina C Cornel, Charlotte M W Gaasterland, Agnies M van Eeghen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHealthcare decision‐making for individuals with rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorders associated with intellectual disabilities can be complex and value‐laden, in which parents often play a central role. To ensure that clinical practice guideline recommendations align with the perspectives of parents, it is essential to incorporate their values into the GRADE Evidence‐to‐Decision framework used in guideline development.MethodsThe aim of this qualitative study was to explore the values of parents of individuals with rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorders in healthcare decision‐making. We explored what mattered to parents in relation to healthcare decisions and in the process of healthcare decision‐making. Eighteen parents of individuals with 15 (ultra)rare genetic conditions participated in semi‐structured interviews. Reflexive thematic analysis was used to generate themes from the data.ResultsImportant values for making healthcare decisions were proportionality, considering balancing harms against the need to treat and feasibility for the family, and equality, with a need for inclusive, accessible and continuous healthcare. The process of decision‐making in healthcare was described as a collaborative effort, in which acknowledging parental knowledge and respecting and supporting autonomy were important themes.ConclusionsThe findings support the applicability of the Evidence‐to‐Decision framework to guideline development for rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorders, as proportionality and equality are reflected in its criteria. Implications for practice include explicit attention to family‐level impacts and incorporating lived experience of parents to inform value judgements. Healthcare decision‐making was described as a collaborative process, with a need for support and space for parents to clarify and communicate their values.Keywords: clinical practice guidelines, healthcare decision‐making, intellectual disabilities, parents, rare conditions, values

Introduction

Individuals with rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorders associated with intellectual disabilities often require lifelong, multidisciplinary care (Rosenberg et al. 2021). Healthcare providers may have limited expertise in these rare conditions and rely on caregivers for information about patient‐specific presentations, communication and care context (Rosenberg et al. 2021; Bindels‐de Heus et al. 2013; Cooper et al. 2004). Therefore, parents often play an active role in healthcare decision‐making. Parents frequently experience significant distress and personal impact (Fitzgerald and Gallagher 2021; Finless et al. 2024; Currie and Szabo 2020) while balancing caregiving responsibilities, serving as experts on their child's condition and acting as proxies in decision‐making. Decision‐making can be particularly complex due to uncertainty about the individual's preferences, divergent views among families and professionals and the need to navigate competing values, such as autonomy and health (van den Driessen Mareeuw et al. 2022; van Hooren et al. 2005; Zaal‐Schuller et al. 2025).

Clinical practice guidelines can support decision‐making by providing evidence‐based recommendations for healthcare (Graham et al. 2011). Guideline development inherently involves subjective judgements as guideline developers weigh the benefits and harms of interventions, diagnostic tests or monitoring approaches. To ensure that these judgements reflect the perspectives of parents, it is crucial to take into account their values, that is, what matters most to them when making healthcare decisions (Witteman et al. 2021). If recommendations conflict with what is important to parents, their implementation may be difficult and could strain healthcare provider–family relationships.

A scoping review on values of individuals with rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorders and their family/caregivers in healthcare found that, although extensive research on healthcare experiences exists, values are rarely explicitly reported (Klein Haneveld et al. 2026). Few studies explicitly address values in decision‐making (van Hooren et al. 2005; van den Driessen Mareeuw et al. 2020; Duarte et al. 2022) or guideline development (Verstraten‐Oudshoorn et al. 2024), and none have interpreted findings within the context of guideline methodology. Instead, qualitative studies implicitly point to values including autonomy, person‐centredness and feasibility; competence of and connection with the healthcare professional; and system‐level factors such as accessibility, coordination and stigma (Klein Haneveld et al. 2026). Decision‐making involves balancing benefits and harms under uncertainty, prioritising among health problems and considering family and identity‐related factors (Klein Haneveld et al. 2026). These values are recurrent across conditions and contexts and largely align with established frameworks for the general population; however, autonomy, stigma, care coordination and identity may take on specific nuances for people living with lifelong conditions, including intellectual disabilities and/or complex condition‐associated health problems (Klein Haneveld et al. 2026).

The Grading of Recommendation, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) Evidence‐to‐Decision framework is a commonly used methodological framework for formulating guideline recommendations (Alonso‐Coello, Oxman, et al. 2016; Alonso‐Coello, Schunemann, et al. 2016; Meneses‐Echavez et al. 2023). This framework contains criteria, including (un)desirable effects, certainty of the evidence, values, resource use, equity, acceptability and feasibility. By using this framework, guideline developers can systematically evaluate these factors and ensure that the considerations are integrated into the resulting recommendations. The framework is meant to be flexible, allowing for modifications in terminology, criteria and guidance to suit different contexts. Given the unique challenges in healthcare decision‐making faced by families living with rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorders, we believe it is critical to assess whether the existing framework incorporates their values.

The aim of this qualitative study is to explore the values of parents of individuals with rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorders in healthcare decision‐making. Specifically, the study addresses two key questions: (1) what matters to parents in healthcare decisions and (2) what matters to parents in the healthcare decision‐making process? These findings are used to evaluate the applicability of the Evidence‐to‐Decision framework and identify any necessary adjustments to make guidelines for this population more acceptable and family‐centred, in particular those developed by the European Reference Network (ERN) ITHACA (Intellectual Disability, TeleHealth, Autism and Congenital Anomalies) on Rare Congenital Malformations and Rare Intellectual Disability (Who we are n.d.).

Methods

Study Design

In this qualitative study, we conducted semi‐structured interviews with parents of individuals with rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorders and used reflexive thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke 2019) to inductively and iteratively develop themes that reflect parents' values in healthcare decision‐making for consideration in the Evidence‐to‐Decision framework.

Study Population and Recruitment

The study population consisted of parents of individuals with rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorders associated with intellectual disabilities. We approached 12 patient associations for relevant rare genetic conditions, nine of which responded and agreed to facilitate recruitment. A (print or social media) poster and email with information on the research aim, interview content, estimated time commitment and contact information of the interviewer were shared. A convenience sample was recruited, with purposive selection to ensure diversity in condition, age and gender of affected individuals and parental gender. Of 31 individuals who expressed interest in participating, 18 participated based on these criteria, response to follow‐up emails and availability. No compensation was provided.

Participant Characteristics

Eighteen parents (13 mothers and five fathers) of 18 individuals with 15 different rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorders participated. Both parents of one individual participated together (P8 and P9); one parent had two affected children (P6).

One parent had been diagnosed with the same genetic condition themselves. Among the affected individuals, 10 were female and eight were male. Eleven were children under 18, whereas seven were adults, two of whom were deceased. Their genetic conditions included Chung–Jansen syndrome, Coffin–Siris syndrome, dup15q syndrome, Kleefstra syndrome, Noonan syndrome, Rett syndrome, Smith–Magenis syndrome, tuberous sclerosis complex, Williams syndrome, Wolf–Hirschhorn syndrome and two monogenic and three chromosomal conditions (a duplication, a microdeletion and a mosaic trisomy syndrome) that are not further specified to protect privacy.

The group was heterogeneous regarding intellectual functioning, ranging from average or borderline IQ with developmental disabilities to profound intellectual disabilities. Parents reported co‐occurring challenges, including challenging behaviour, mental health problems and physical health problems, such as epilepsy, scoliosis, motor impairments, congenital heart defects, respiratory infections, gastrointestinal problems, endocrine disorders, tumour growth and obesity. Parents' descriptions of their children's conditions are provided in Table S1.

Data Collection

Semi‐structured interviews guided by an interview guide (Supporting Information) were conducted between March 2024 and February 2025. Semi‐structured interviews were chosen for their flexibility in exploring complex and sensitive topics while maintaining consistency across participants. The interview guide was designed to elicit empirical reference points by exploring both positive and negative experiences, allowing participants to reflect on concrete situations that revealed their values in healthcare contexts. The interview guide was developed by L.C. (medical student), M.K. (PhD candidate trained in medicine and care ethics) and C.G. (guideline methodologist) with contributions from all co‐authors. It was pilot tested to refine the guide and train L.C. in conducting interviews. M.K. and C.G. had prior experience in qualitative research; L.C. was trained through pilot interviews and post‐interview debriefing.

Interviews lasted 1–1.5 h and were conducted by L.C. via videoconferencing (n = 14), at the hospital (n = 2) or at participants' homes (n = 1), based on participant preference to accommodate caregiving responsibilities. Only the interviewer and participant(s) were present, except in one case where a parent was caring for their sick child during the interview. Field notes supported reflexivity during data analysis. Interviews were audio‐recorded and transcribed verbatim. Although we initially planned for approximately 10 interviews, the sample was expanded to enhance information power considering the broad aim and diverse population of the study (Malterud et al. 2016).

Data Analysis

Data were analysed inductively and iteratively using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke 2019; Braun and Clarke 2006). L.C. and M.K. read the transcripts shortly after the interviews. Both had full access to the data and no therapeutic relationships with the participants. L.C. coded each interview using initial, descriptive codes in MAXQDA 2024 software, with M.K. independently coding a subset of five interviews to facilitate reflexive discussions on the interpretation of the transcripts. Similar codes were grouped into potential themes by L.C., refined first in discussion with M.K., and then through multiple discussions with the wider interdisciplinary research team: C.G., M.C. (professor in community genetics), P.E. (professor in psychopathology and treatment in people with intellectual disabilities), A.N. (care ethicist and father of a child with Down syndrome and epilepsy) and A.E. (intellectual disability physician) (Figures S1 and S2). In these discussions, the researchers' conceptions of values, guideline development and their experiences in receiving or providing healthcare influenced the interpretation of findings. No member reflections were conducted.

Ethical Approval

The Medical Ethical Committee of the Amsterdam UMC assessed the study protocol and confirmed that the study was exempt from ethical approval according to the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO) on 27 March 2023 (reference W23_065#23.171). Participants provided verbal informed consent before the interview and could withdraw at any time. Anonymity and privacy were ensured by assigning pseudonyms, omitting identifiable or contextually traceable information in citations and participant descriptions and securely storing all data following institutional guidelines.

Results

Below, we present the findings of our reflexive thematic analysis. The study addresses two questions: (1) what matters to parents in healthcare decisions and (2) what matters to parents in the healthcare decision‐making process. With regard to healthcare decisions, parents' accounts reflected proportionality, balancing potential benefits and harms of interventions in the child's (family) context, and equality, referring to being treated as any other member of society and having access to inclusive, accessible and continuous care. In the process of making healthcare decisions, parents highlighted the importance of acknowledging their knowledge from lived experience and of respecting and supporting their child's autonomy, often understood in relational terms.

What Mattered in Healthcare Decisions

Proportionality

The theme of proportionality captures the recurring balancing in parents' accounts between medical necessity and harms and feasibility. Many parents described interventions or diagnostic procedures as involving disproportionate harm compared with expectations in the general population, often with enduring consequences for well‐being and later engagement with healthcare, which could outweigh uncertain benefits or medical need. Parents also described the profound impact of healthcare interventions on the home and family context, suggesting that feasibility within daily family life was seen as a precondition.

Parents emphasised that procedures generally considered minimally invasive could be traumatic or harmful for their child, with healthcare professionals sometimes failing to recognise this: ‘[If my child's ears] need to be looked at, the doctor should look in their ears, not first an assistant, because this is a child that you have to stranglehold completely before you can look in their ears’ (P18). Negative healthcare experiences often had significant impact on both individuals and their parents, for example, leading to children refusing any further healthcare visits: ‘The hospital may have a tough afternoon, but afterwards, I have [my child] at home who I can never get to go to a hospital or a dentist again […] so I face the consequences’ (P4). Consequently, parents stressed the importance of avoiding unnecessary procedures:

When you are finally at the hospital, after insisting on it, [my child's] blood has to be tested [for signs of a recurrent infection], and then they always also look in [their] ears, but [my child] hates that, everything close to [their] face, […] I think because [they have] been on a ventilator […] Just take blood, because that is what we are here for, and if that [confirms the recurrent] infection, [my child] just has to go on antibiotics, and does not need to have [their] throat and ears looked at. (P12)

Treatment responses were sometimes different or unknown for particular rare genetic conditions, as described by one parent: ‘How [my child] responds to medication, how [my child] responds to anaesthesia, everything is different from what [the doctors] are used to […], it's really just guessing: how does he react? So that's really been an uncertain time’ (P8). Uncertainty about benefits and harms often required a trial‐and‐error approach, which could be experienced as stressful:

[My child] experienced side effects of the antipsychotics, [then] we tried Ritalin but that […] gave [them] even more anxiety […] What does it do to your child, how does it work out physically [because] it can have side effects [like] movement disorders. […] [We] try to give [our] child tools to regulate [themselves] instead of medicalising everything. But you know there comes a time that [their] behaviour might be out of control, and it will be necessary. (P16)

In some cases, the benefits of certain interventions were not seen to outweigh their potential harms, for example, in one parent's positive experience of deciding whether to fit a scoliosis brace:

A cut‐out would have to be made for the mic‐key button [gastrostomy] in [their] belly, and the corset would have to extend from the armpits to the hips. [My child] […] has a hard time accepting things. […] Fitting a corset like that, the question is, is it going to pay off? […] The spinal team […] fortunately considered the full situation, all the X‐rays that were taken, [their] development, and also [their] difficulty in accepting things. And they concluded: [for now], we won't do anything, it's too stressful for [my child] to do this relative to its benefit. (P17)

Moreover, parents described caring for their child as intense and demanding, involving the whole family. As such, healthcare decisions also affected the whole family.

Care for [these children] is lifelong […] and siblings and parents also adapt, adjust their lives because of the disability […] of the family member in need of care. But that also means that […] if a child doesn't sleep all night, then the rest of the family suffers too. If a child has behavioural problems, the rest of the family suffers too. So apart from the effect on the child, the effect of treatment on the rest of the family is also important, they also potentially experience health problems because of that. So, I think that's really inextricably linked. (P2)

Consequently, parents underlined the need to ensure that medical procedures that require their involvement are realistic and feasible within their home circumstances: ‘You can shout […], for example, the child has to practice eating every day for four hours. But who is going to do that? […] Someone has to do it, and is that feasible? […] I can't do magic either’ (P15). They also highlighted that healthcare professionals should consider that parents may prioritise differently than providers, often focusing on issues with the greatest impact on daily life. For example, one parent prioritised addressing behavioural problems over monitoring other health problems: ‘At hospitals, the focus is very much on […] the medical tests and the blood levels, whereas the burden of disease […] is on behaviour’ (P4).

Equality

Across the interviews, the notion of equality was brought up by parents both within and outside of healthcare contexts. In their accounts, it most commonly referred to receiving equal treatment compared to the general population and not being disadvantaged in healthcare settings. Parents also described experiences related to inclusivity, accessibility and continuity that reflect broader principles of equity, as these are perceived as necessary requirements for achieving equal treatment in practice.

Many parents addressed experiences of inequality and the importance of inclusivity in society: ‘We should make it known from an early age that people with disabilities […] are people just like you and me’ (P3). These experiences extended to healthcare, where several parents perceived insufficient attention for their child's needs. Reported factors contributing to this inequality included the rarity of their child's condition, limited knowledge among healthcare providers and their child's inability to fend for themselves. For example, one parent recalled visiting their child, who was unable to eat independently, in the hospital and discovering that all the meals from their previous visit were still untouched on the bedside table. They perceived this as a clear sign of neglect and unequal treatment:

In the hospital, […] in the case of my [child], who can't talk, […], you still see that someone who is restricted to a wheelchair and who can't talk receives a different treatment compared to a child that walks around and can talk and can ask for things. (P10).

Several parents experienced insufficient accessibility of healthcare. Several parents indicated that taking their child to the hospital was difficult. Physical environments were sometimes not set up for people with disabilities: ‘There is an accessible toilet and shower room, but no bath stretcher, which [my child] needs, and before you can even enter the bathroom, you have to move aside about twelve pieces of equipment, because it's basically used for storing medical aids’ (P10). Some found it impossible to bring their child to consultations: ‘[The doctor] rarely if ever sees my [child] these days, because it's very difficult to go to the hospital with my [child], […] now, I do a lot by phone’ (P13). Others stressed they only attend the hospital where their child's medical history is known, even when relevant expertise is located elsewhere: ‘If we go to [another hospital], I can go and tell the whole story all over again. But if he's very distressed, […] I want him to go straight to the emergency room for oxygen’ (P7).

Finally, continuity was crucial in healthcare: ‘Having continuity, being able to think together [about] what we see now [compared to] last year, […] to collaborate over time’ (P7). Parents faced challenges especially after receiving the diagnosis and during the transition to adult care. They reported dealing with many healthcare professionals and a lack of coordinated, specialised care, especially after their child turns 18, because expertise centres for ultrarare conditions are often unavailable: ‘For adults with [more common conditions], often there is an [expertise] centre, so [healthcare] continues also when you're an adult. But if you don't fit in anywhere […] then it's searching by yourself’ (P8).

What Mattered in the Healthcare Decision‐Making Process

Healthcare decision‐making was described as a collaborative effort to determine the most appropriate treatment, which can be challenging for all involved:

It also requires something from the person who is dealing with [my child] to […], on the one hand, focus on [my child], but on the other hand focus on me and see how I deal with the situation […] It's difficult for everybody. It's often difficult for me, but for doctors or others who barely know [my child], it's also very difficult. But together, I think, in general, you figure it out. (P14)

In relation to healthcare decision‐making, key themes were acknowledging parental knowledge, referring to the experiential knowledge of parents as caregivers, and respecting and supporting autonomy, which reflects the importance of considering the individual's preferences and the support required to express and enact them.

Acknowledging Parental Knowledge

Whereas some parents experienced themselves as active participants in healthcare decision‐making and others struggled to advocate for their child, parents consistently emphasised the importance of their experiential knowledge in healthcare decisions. Parents described that they bring a distinctive, non‐medical perspective to the table. Healthcare professionals who only see the individual in a medical setting may not fully comprehend what issues matter most in daily life:

[The doctors] don't know what it's like to have a child like that. Of course they see them in the outpatient clinic, but to struggle with this every day, 24/7, it's a completely different thing. And we obviously don't look at them as medics, we do look at them as our child, which is a different perspective. (P17)

In addition, parental involvement was deemed necessary as their understanding of their child's needs may extend beyond that of the healthcare professional, especially when their child is unable to express themselves verbally:

Our children cannot talk, they cannot tell where they are hurting. […] We see it in our children's behaviour: something is wrong. [They] walk differently, [they] eat less, or [they] only eat certain things, or the defecation doesn't go well—these are very small things—or the colour of the urine is not right even though [they are] drinking, such things. (P13)

Consequently, parents emphasised the importance of being allowed to contribute to decision‐making, including to divert from healthcare professional's advice. Many had both positive and negative experiences in this regard. For example, one parent recalled a decision on preventative antibiotics use:

Until two years ago, [my son] had quite a few infections which make him stuffy. He uses low‐dose antibiotics preventively throughout the winter months and because of that, he hasn't been sick often. [When] the paediatrician said: ‘[…]You could also omit it,’ I said: ‘Well, I'd rather not, because the amount [of antibiotics] is minimal and I don't want to go through that again, […] let's just go ahead and use it during the winters.’ Well, they were receptive to that […], and I like that. (P5)

Some parents reported feeling that their own empowerment enabled having a say in decision‐making:

I would like to feel the space, as parents, and I think I can feel [that space] but that's because I'm empowered enough, […] that if [the doctors] say: ‘We really do recommend’, that we are still allowed to say: ‘Yes, we have read up, we have heard you and yet we choose not to do it’. (P17)

Other parents indicated the need for support to facilitate involvement, for example, in clarifying what matters when making a decision:

As a parent, you're not really prepared for [having a child with disabilities] and you meet new challenges every time. And, [ …] there is little support […] anyway, but also little support for parents to find out: what do I want and why? […] How do you discover what your values are? And how do you bring those into all the conversations you have? I think that more support could be offered to parents on this. (P15)

For some individuals with intellectual disabilities, parents take on the full (legal) responsibility to represent their child. To ensure that decision‐making is in line with the best interest of the individual, it was considered important that healthcare professionals verify that parents are making informed decisions: ‘I think it is [important] that [doctors] also assess whether parents take decision[s] consciously and also realise what their impact is’ (P14). This connects to the second theme, namely, respecting and supporting the autonomy of the individual.

Respecting and Supporting Autonomy

Although autonomy was a recurrent theme across interviews, it was sometimes articulated in more individual terms and at other times in relational terms. Several parents mentioned the importance of directly involving their child in healthcare decision‐making, emphasising the importance of respecting their autonomy and acknowledging their own perspective. For example, one parent described that their child used to say: ‘I may be different, and I may not understand some things, but I am still no less than them. They should not decide about me, they should consult with me’ (P1). Another parent described how, although their child was unable to comprehend certain things, they still preferred healthcare professionals to address them directly:

It's about [my child] and [my child] may have an intellectual disability and may not be able to comprehend certain things, […] but still, [my child] is just a human being, just ask [them] things. It may all feel very different to [them] than it seems to me. And it's all about [them], isn't it? (P11)

Beyond highlighting the importance of direct involvement of their child, many parents discussed supporting them in healthcare decision‐making, especially in case of more severe intellectual or communication disabilities. Several parents noted that their child was not able to understand or adequately express their opinion in decision‐making processes. For example, one parent explained that their child is ‘just not very adequate in her answers, so [they] can say “yes” or “no” very convincingly, but it is not necessarily true’ (P12). In such cases, parents recommended involving parents or others to support the individual in making decisions:

The doctor [should] insist on having someone there who can support, […] a partner, family member, or a volunteer […] because the likelihood that [people with our syndrome] have a reduced IQ is increased […] and they can come across very naturally, as if there's nothing wrong, […] and patients often don't even realise it themselves, […], so to what extent do they understand exactly what a doctor tells them? (P6)

This reflects a more relational view of autonomy, where parents and professionals attempt to support the autonomy of individuals with intellectual disabilities as much as possible through their involvement.

Discussion

This study describes important values for guideline development based on interviews with 18 Dutch parents of individuals with (ultra)rare conditions associated with intellectual disabilities. The results show that proportionality and equality should be explicitly considered in the formulation of clinical practice guideline recommendations. Key themes related to the process of healthcare decision‐making were acknowledging parental knowledge and respecting and supporting the autonomy of the affected individual.

Proportionality involved weighing uncertain benefits against potential harms, with an emphasis on what is feasible for families. Prior qualitative research also highlighted that treatment burden may differ for individuals with rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorders, for example, due to sensory processing difficulties; health complaints and side effects may also be overlooked in individuals unable to verbally express their complaints, especially when combined with limited knowledge of underlying rare conditions (Klein Haneveld et al. 2026; van den Driessen Mareeuw et al. 2020; McDonagh et al. 2022; Xanthopoulos et al. 2023; Bohnstedt et al. 2023; Minnes and Steiner 2009; Soto Jansson et al. 2024; Weng et al. 2012). Moreover, in line with the broader qualitative research in this area, benefits and harms as well as feasibility were considered on a family level, given the broader impact on family well‐being (Fitzgerald and Gallagher 2021; van Hooren et al. 2005; Klein Haneveld et al. 2026).

Equality was expressed in parents' emphasis on being treated as equal and linked to the need for inclusive, accessible and continuous care. Parents felt that such conditions were often lacking, especially for ultrarare conditions as included in this study; their accounts therefore point to issues of equity in healthcare, where equal treatment alone does not ensure fair access or appropriate care provision. These findings align with studies reporting challenges in organising lifelong multidisciplinary care (van den Driessen Mareeuw et al. 2020; O'Donoghue et al. 2023; Kleinendorst et al. 2020; Kerin et al. 2020; Both et al. 2018; Peters et al. 2022; Rietman et al. 2018; Van Remmerden et al. 2020; Boyce et al. 2020) and experiences of stigma, biased expectations and unequal treatment of people with intellectual disabilities (Steffensen et al. 2024; Griffith et al. 2011; Carrion et al. 2022; Celik and Kara Uzun 2023; Hamlington et al. 2015; Huiracocha et al. 2017; Uhlenbusch et al. 2019; Boonman et al. 2022).

Healthcare decision‐making was seen as a collaborative process involving the individual, their caregivers and healthcare professionals. Parents emphasised the importance of their involvement, noting that they bring a unique perspective and understanding of their child's needs, which has been previously defined as tacit knowledge (Zaal‐Schuller et al. 2025; Kruithof et al. 2024). In line with previous research, parents recognised the importance of respecting the autonomy of their child alongside a more relational interpretation of autonomy, involving others to support individuals in understanding information or expressing their perspective (Mastebroek et al. 2016). As such, our findings align with previous literature on decision‐making in both the general population and in individuals with intellectual disabilities, which conceptualises autonomy as relational rather than strictly individualistic (Mastebroek et al. 2016; van Nistelrooij et al. 2017; Bjornsdottir et al. 2015).

Implications for Clinical Practice Guideline Development

The main themes identified in this study are represented within the GRADE Evidence‐to‐Decision framework (Alonso‐Coello, Oxman, et al. 2016; Alonso‐Coello, Schunemann, et al. 2016), supporting its applicability to this population while providing several suggestions for its use in practice.

Proportionality is addressed through criteria that assess the balance between benefits and harms, the priority of the health problem and the acceptability and feasibility of the intervention. As parents in this study reported perceiving the proportionality of a given intervention differently compared to healthcare providers, people with lived experience should be involved in the Evidence‐to‐Decision process. Parents' accounts also indicate that the balance between benefits and harms should be considered not only at the individual level but also at the family level, recognising families as important stakeholders in healthcare.

The Evidence‐to‐Decision framework, which takes a population perspective, also includes consideration of impacts on equity (Alonso‐Coello, Oxman, et al. 2016; Alonso‐Coello, Schunemann, et al. 2016). In the context of rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorders, this implies not only providing equal treatment but also ensuring preconditions such as accessible and continuous healthcare and considering the potential for recommendations to reinforce or mitigate stigma experienced by people living with disabilities (Cooper et al. 2004). If the Evidence‐to‐Decision framework is used, the above considerations should receive significant attention.

Implications for Clinical Practice Guideline Use in Healthcare

Our findings show healthcare decision‐making for rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorders as a collaborative process, in which healthcare providers and parents work together to identify the most appropriate course of action. It is important to recognise that guideline recommendations are never dictates: no recommendation will apply to all circumstances and all patients (Guyatt et al. 2008) and for rare conditions with scarce evidence, recommendations are even more likely to be conditional.

As different choices will be appropriate for different individuals, healthcare providers should support individuals in arriving at a decision consistent with their values, ensuring that care is personalised to each unique situation. This study supports a relational view of autonomy, in which individual autonomy is understood as embedded within family and social contexts and where parents and others may play an important role in supporting individuals to identify and express their values (Mastebroek et al. 2016; van Nistelrooij et al. 2017; Bjornsdottir et al. 2015). The findings also highlight a need for support for some parents in articulating their values, thereby facilitating shared decision‐making.

Strengths and Limitations

The study included a heterogeneous population, including parents of individuals with various ultrarare genetic conditions, who are relatively under‐represented in qualitative research (Klein Haneveld et al. 2026). Given the wide variability between and among these conditions, findings are not intended to be exhaustive or representative, but rather to provide an interpretation of the shared meanings across parents' accounts. This variability may nonetheless shape how the findings should be interpreted; for example, perspectives on individual autonomy seemed to vary with the level of cognitive and adaptive functioning of the individual. To enable the reader to assess the transferability to other contexts, we provide the participants' descriptions of their children's conditions in the Supporting Information.

Our recruitment and data collection strategy likely excluded parents with low literacy or limited digital skills. This may have shaped the perspectives captured in this study, as such barriers often intersect with other axes of difference, including socio‐economic position and cultural background. In turn, these may influence how parents experience equality in healthcare services and engage in healthcare decision‐making processes (Oueslati et al. 2026; Roodbeen et al. 2021).

Finally, this study focused on parents' perspectives and only included one parent who had been diagnosed with the same syndrome themselves. People with intellectual disabilities remain under‐represented in health research (Klein Haneveld et al. 2026; Kaehne et al. 2019; Strnadova et al. 2022) and may hold different views from their caregivers. Parents in this study also emphasised the importance of autonomy and including the perspective of individuals with rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorders in healthcare decisions. Future research should therefore explore their values and how these can be integrated into clinical practice guidelines.

Conclusion

Interviews with 18 Dutch parents of individuals with rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorders highlighted two key values that are relevant to Evidence‐to‐Decision processes: proportionality and equality. In individuals with (ultra)rare conditions associated with intellectual disabilities, healthcare decisions may involve uncertainty about benefits and harms compared with expectations in the general population and compounded health inequities. The identified values align with the GRADE Evidence‐to‐Decision framework, supporting its applicability but also the additional importance of explicitly discussing these values together with affected individuals, parents and caregivers when formulating recommendations for this population. Acknowledging parental knowledge and respecting and supporting autonomy were important in the process of healthcare decision‐making, which was described as a collaborative effort requiring adequate support, for example, in value clarification.
