Center of distances and Bernstein sets
Mateusz Kula

Abstract
. We show that for any subset A ⊂ [ 0 , ∞ ) A\subset[0,\infty) , where 0 ∈ A 0\in A , there exists a Bernstein set X ⊂ ℝ X\subset\mathbb{R} such that A A is the center of distances of X X .



1.
Introduction

The notion of center of distances has been introduced in
[
5
]
as follows. If
(X,d)
is a metric space, then the set

S(X):=\{a\in[0,\infty)\colon\forall_{x\in X}\exists_{y\in X}d(x,y)=a\}

is called the
center of distances
of
X
.
Although this notion is quite general, most applications concern subsets of real numbers
\mathbb{R}
.
For example, the center of distances has been successfully used in proofs that some sets are not achievement sets of any series, see
[
5
]
or
[
4
]
. Exact calculation of the center of distance is sometimes complicated, for example for central Cantor sets, see
[
2
]
and
[
1
]
.

In
[
3
]
, the question to characterize sets
S(X)
for subsets of the real line has been raised. At the conferences ‘Inspirations in Real Analysis II’ (2024) and ‘46th Summer Symposium in Real Analysis’ (2024), M. Filipczak asked the following.

Question.
Given a subset
A\subset[0,\infty)
with
0\in A
, does there exist
X\subset\mathbb{R}
such that
S(X)=A
?

We give a positive answer to Filipczak’s question, see Theorem
2
, and improve this result showing that in addition
X
can be chosen to be a Bernstein set, see Theorem
4
. It is worth noting that the answer to Filipczak’s question was already known for compact
A
and in that case
X
can be chosen to be closed, see
[
3
, Corollary 4.11]
.

We use the standard notation. Throughout this paper
2^{\leqslant n}
,
2^{<n}
and
2^{n}
denote the set of all finite binary sequences of length
\leqslant n
,
<n
and
=n
, respectively.
If
s=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
and
t=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})
are finite sequences, then, as usual,
s^{\frown}t
is the concatenation
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n},t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})
. Also,
\operatorname{lin}_{\mathbb{Q}}(X)
denotes the linear span of
X
over the rationals
\mathbb{Q}
.

2.
On the average operator

Given a subset
C\subset\mathbb{R}
, let
T_{C}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
be the operator given by the formula

T_{C}(Y):=\left\{\frac{1}{2}(y+y^{\prime})\colon y,y^{\prime}\in Y\right\}\setminus C,\text{ where }Y\subset\mathbb{R}.

Let

T^{n}_{C}(Y):=\underset{n\text{ times}}{\underbrace{(T_{C}\circ\dots\circ T_{C})}}(Y)\,\text{ and }\,T^{\infty}_{C}(Y):=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}T^{n}_{C}(Y).

Note that if
C\cap Y=\emptyset
, then

Y\subset T_{C}^{\infty}(Y)
,
C\cap T_{C}^{\infty}(Y)=\emptyset
and

(1)

T_{C}(T^{\infty}_{C}(Y))=T^{\infty}_{C}(Y).

Indeed, if
v\in T_{C}(T^{\infty}_{C}(Y))
, then there exist
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
u,u^{\prime}\in T^{n}_{C}(Y)
such that
v=\frac{1}{2}(u+u^{\prime})\notin C
, and hence
v\in T_{C}^{n+1}(Y)\subset T_{C}^{\infty}(Y)
.

Lemma 1
.

Given
C\subset\mathbb{R}
,
Y\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus C
and
v\in T^{n}_{C}(Y)
, there exists a function
d\colon 2^{\leqslant n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

such that

(I)

d({\emptyset})=v
;

(II)

d(s)=\frac{1}{2}(d(s^{\frown}0)+d(s^{\frown}1))
for
s\in 2^{<n}
;

(III)

d(s)\in Y
for
s\in 2^{n}
;

(IV)

d(s)\notin C
for
s\in 2^{\leqslant n}
.

Moreover, if
Y=\{x-b,x+b\}
and
x\in C
, then
d(s)=x-b
for all
s\in 2^{\leqslant n}
or
d(s)=x+b
for all
s\in 2^{\leqslant n}
.

Proof.

Let
C\subset\mathbb{R}
and
Y\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus C
. The proof is by induction on
n\in\mathbb{N}
. If
n=0
, there is nothing to do. Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and assume the hypothesis is fulfilled for
n
.
Fix

v\in T^{n+1}_{C}(Y)=T_{C}(T^{n}_{C}(Y)).

By the definition of
T_{C}
there exist
v_{0},v_{1}\in T^{n}_{C}(Y)
such that
v=\frac{1}{2}(v_{0}+v_{1})\notin C
. By induction hypothesis there exist functions
d_{0},d_{1}\colon 2^{\leqslant n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
d_{i}(\emptyset)=v_{i}
and conditions
(II)
–
(IV)
with
d_{i}
in place of
d
are fulfilled. Define
d\colon 2^{\leqslant n+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
d(\emptyset)=v
,
d({0^{\frown}s})=d_{0}(s)
and
d({1^{\frown}s})=d_{1}(s)
for any
s\in 2^{\leqslant n}
.

If
Y=\{x-b,x+b\}
and
x\in C
, then
T^{n}_{C}(Y)=Y
. Thus the function
d
must be constant.
∎

In particular, if
v\in T^{n}_{C}(Y)
, then

v=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{s\in 2^{n}}d(s),

where
d\colon 2^{\leqslant n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is as in Lemma
1
.

3.
Surjectivity of the center of distances operator

Now we prove a theorem, which answers Filipczak’s question.

Theorem 2
.

For any set
A\subset[0,\infty)
, where
0\in A
, there exists
X\subset\mathbb{R}
such that
S(X)=A
.

Proof.

Let
0\in A\subset[0,\infty)
and consider an enumeration

[0,\infty)\setminus A=\{b_{\alpha}\colon\alpha<\kappa\},

where
\kappa=\operatorname{card}([0,\infty)\setminus A)\leqslant\mathfrak{c}
. Any Hamel basis of
\mathbb{R}
over
\mathbb{Q}
has cardinality
\mathfrak{c}
, so we inductively choose a transfinite sequence
(x_{\alpha})_{\alpha<\kappa}
of real numbers such that

(2)

x_{\alpha}\notin\operatorname{lin}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\{b_{\beta}\colon\beta\leqslant\alpha\}\cup\{x_{\beta}\colon\beta<\alpha\}\right).

Put
C:=\{x_{\alpha}\colon\alpha<\kappa\}
,
Y:=\bigcup_{\alpha<\kappa}\{x_{\alpha}+b_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}-b_{\alpha}\}
and

X:=\mathbb{R}\setminus T_{C}^{\infty}\left(Y\right).

By definitions,
C\subset X
and for each
\alpha<\kappa
the number
x_{\alpha}\in X
witnesses that
b_{\alpha}\notin S(X)
, therefore

S(X)\subset[0,\infty)\setminus\{b_{\alpha}\colon\alpha<\kappa\}=A.

To prove the opposite inclusion, fix
b\in[0,\infty)\setminus S(X)
. Then there exists
x\in X
such that
x+b,x-b\notin X
.
Since

x\notin T_{C}^{\infty}(Y)=T_{C}(T_{C}^{\infty}(Y))

(recall formula (
1
)) and
x
is the middle of points
x+b,x-b\in T_{C}^{\infty}(Y)
, it follows that
x\in C
, so
x=x_{\alpha}
for some
\alpha<\kappa
.
Apply Lemma
1
to
x+b
and
x-b
, so there exist functions
d_{0},d_{1}\colon 2^{\leqslant n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
d_{0}(\emptyset)=x-b
,
d_{1}(\emptyset)=x+b
and conditions
(II)
–
(IV)
with
d_{i}
in place of
d
are fulfilled.

By condition
(III)
(with
d
replaced by
d_{0}
and
d_{1}
) for each
s\in 2^{n}
there exist
\alpha_{s},\beta_{s}<\kappa
and
\delta_{s},\varepsilon_{s}\in\{-1,1\}
such that

(3)

d_{0}(s)=x_{\alpha_{s}}+\delta_{s}b_{\alpha_{s}}\in Y\text{ and }d_{1}(s)=x_{\beta_{s}}+\varepsilon_{s}b_{\beta_{s}}\in Y.

By conditions
(I)
and
(II)
we have

(4)

2x_{\alpha}=x+b+x-b=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{s\in 2^{n}}\left(x_{\alpha_{s}}+x_{\beta_{s}}+\delta_{s}b_{\alpha_{s}}+\varepsilon_{s}b_{\beta_{s}}\right).

Using condition (
2
), we get

\alpha=\alpha_{s}=\beta_{s}\text{ for all }s\in 2^{n}.

Otherwise, if

\gamma=\max\left(\{\alpha\}\cup\{\alpha_{s}\colon s\in 2^{n}\}\cup\{\beta_{s}\colon s\in 2^{n}\}\right)

and we move all
x_{\gamma}
to the left hand side and
x_{\alpha}
to the right hand side if necessary, we get a contradiction with the choice of
x_{\gamma}
.

We have
d_{1}(s)\in\{x_{\alpha}-b_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}+b_{\alpha}\}
for all
s\in 2^{n}
, hence by (the last part of) Lemma
1
, we get
x+b=x+\varepsilon b_{\alpha}
for some
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
. Consequently
b=b_{\alpha}\notin A
. ∎

4.
Bernstein sets and center of distances

A
Bernstein set
on the real line is a subset
B\subset\mathbb{R}
such that both
B
and its complement meet every uncountable closed subset of
\mathbb{R}
. Any uncountable closed set on the real line has cardinality
\mathfrak{c}
and there are exactly continuum many such sets.
Improving a classical construction of a Bernstein set, we get the following.

Proposition 3
.

There exists a Bernstein set
X
such that
S(X)=[0,\infty)
.

Proof.

Let
\{F_{\alpha}\colon\alpha<\mathfrak{c}\}
be an enumeration of all uncountable closed subsets of
\mathbb{R}
. Choose sequences
(x_{\alpha})_{\alpha<\mathfrak{c}}
,
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha<\mathfrak{c}}
of real numbers such that

x_{\alpha}\in F_{\alpha}\setminus\operatorname{lin}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{x_{\beta}\colon\beta<\alpha\}\cup\{y_{\beta}\colon\beta<\alpha\}),

y_{\alpha}\in F_{\alpha}\setminus\operatorname{lin}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{x_{\beta}\colon\beta\leqslant\alpha\}\cup\{y_{\beta}\colon\beta<\alpha\}).

Put
Y:=\{y_{\alpha}\colon\alpha<\mathfrak{c}\}
and
X=\mathbb{R}\setminus T^{\infty}_{\emptyset}(Y)
. If
a,b\in T_{\emptyset}^{\infty}(Y)
, then
\frac{1}{2}(a+b)\in T_{\emptyset}^{\infty}(Y)
, hence
S(X)=[0,\infty)
.
Also
Y\subset T^{\infty}_{\emptyset}(Y)
and

T_{\emptyset}^{\infty}(Y)\cap\{x_{\alpha}\colon\alpha<\mathfrak{c}\}\subset\operatorname{lin}_{\mathbb{Q}}(Y)\cap\{x_{\alpha}\colon\alpha<\mathfrak{c}\}=\emptyset,

so
X\supset\{x_{\alpha}\colon\alpha<\mathfrak{c}\}
is a Bernstein set.
∎

Modifying the proof of Theorem
2
, we get a final theorem.

Theorem 4
.

If
0\in A\subset[0,\infty)
, then there exists a Bernstein set
X\subset\mathbb{R}
such that
S(X)=A
.

Proof.

If
A=[0,\infty)
, then the result follows from Proposition
3
. Assume
0\in A\subsetneq[0,\infty)
and consider an enumeration

\emptyset\neq[0,\infty)\setminus A=\{b_{\alpha}\colon\alpha<\mathfrak{c}\},

where terms
b_{\alpha}
are repeated if necessary.
Let
\{F_{\alpha}\colon\alpha<\mathfrak{c}\}
be an enumeration of all uncountable closed subsets of
\mathbb{R}
.
Inductively choose transfinite sequences
(x_{\alpha})_{\alpha<\mathfrak{c}}
,
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha<\mathfrak{c}}
such that

(5)

x_{\alpha}\in F_{\alpha}\setminus\operatorname{lin}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\{b_{\beta}\colon\beta\leqslant\alpha\}\cup\{x_{\beta}\colon\beta<\alpha\}\cup\{y_{\beta}\colon\beta<\alpha\}\right),

(6)

y_{\alpha}\in F_{\alpha}\setminus\operatorname{lin}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\{b_{\beta}\colon\beta\leqslant\alpha\}\cup\{x_{\beta}\colon\beta\leqslant\alpha\}\cup\{y_{\beta}\colon\beta<\alpha\}\right).

Put
C:=\{x_{\alpha}\colon\alpha<\mathfrak{c}\}
,
Y:=\bigcup_{\alpha<\mathfrak{c}}\{y_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}+b_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}-b_{\alpha}\}
and

X:=\mathbb{R}\setminus T_{C}^{\infty}\left(Y\right).

Since
C\subset X
and
\{y_{\alpha}\colon\alpha<\mathfrak{c}\}\subset T_{C}^{\infty}(Y)
, so
X
is a Bernstein set.
For each
\alpha<\mathfrak{c}
the number
x_{\alpha}\in X
witnesses that
b_{\alpha}\notin S(X)
, therefore
S(X)\subset A
.

Fix
b\in[0,\infty)\setminus S(X)
. As in the proof of Theorem
2
, check that there exists
\alpha<\mathfrak{c}
such that
x_{\alpha}+b,x_{\alpha}-b\in T^{\infty}_{C}(Y)
and there exist functions
d_{0},d_{1}\colon 2^{\leqslant n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
d_{0}(\emptyset)=x_{\alpha}-b
,
d_{1}(\emptyset)=x_{\alpha}+b
and conditions
(II)
–
(IV)
with
d_{i}
in place of
d
are fulfilled. We have

(7)

2x_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{s\in 2^{n}}(d_{0}(s)+d_{1}(s))

and, by condition
(III)
, for each
s\in 2^{n}
there are some
\alpha_{s},\beta_{s}<\mathfrak{c}
such that

d_{0}(s)\in\{y_{\alpha_{s}},x_{\alpha_{s}}+b_{\alpha_{s}},x_{\alpha_{s}}-b_{\alpha_{s}}\}\text{ and }d_{1}(s)\in\{y_{\beta_{s}},x_{\beta_{s}}+b_{\beta_{s}},x_{\beta_{s}}-b_{\beta_{s}}\}.

Let

\gamma:=\max\left(\{\alpha\}\cup\{\alpha_{s}\colon s\in 2^{n}\}\cup\{\beta_{s}\colon s\in 2^{n}\}\right).

Check that, by (
6
) and (
7
), if
d_{0}(s)=y_{\alpha_{s}}
, then
\alpha_{s}<\gamma
, and similarly, if
d_{1}(s)=y_{\beta_{s}}
, then
\beta_{s}<\gamma
. It follows that
\gamma=\alpha
, because otherwise
\gamma=\alpha_{s}
or
\gamma=\beta_{s}
for some
s\in 2^{n}
, and
d_{0}(s)=x_{\gamma}\pm b_{\gamma}
or
d_{1}(s)=x_{\gamma}\pm b_{\gamma}
, which together with (
7
) would contradict (
5
). Finally, again by (
5
),
d_{0}(s)\neq y_{\alpha_{s}}
,
d_{1}(s)\neq y_{\beta_{s}}
and
\alpha=\alpha_{s}=\beta_{s}
for all
s\in 2^{n}
(because otherwise the rational coefficient at
x_{\alpha}
in (
7
) would be non-zero).

Further reasoning proceeds as in the proof of Theorem
2
, that is, since
d_{1}(s)\in\{x_{\alpha}-b_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}+b_{\alpha}\}
for all
s\in 2^{n}
, we obtain
b=b_{\alpha}\notin A
.
∎
