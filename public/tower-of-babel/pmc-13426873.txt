Arthroscopic Autologous Iliac Crest Bone Augmentation for Restoring Engaging Reverse Hill‐Sachs Lesion With Anterior Capsular Tightening in Posterior Shoulder Dislocation
Authors: Shiqi Xiang, Yini Zhang, Ding Zhou, Yuchen He, Junliang Liu, Weihong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPosterior shoulder dislocation is often accompanied by a reverse Hill‐Sachs lesion that may engage the posterior glenoid rim and drive recurrent instability. Lesions involving >25% of the humeral articular surface frequently require surgery. We describe an all‐arthroscopic, joint‐preserving technique that combines humeral reconstruction with posterior stabilization. A curved iliac‐crest corticocancellous bone block is inlaid to restore the humeral defect and fixed with suture anchors using a double‐pulley compression construct. Anterior capsular soft‐tissue compression is added extra‐articularly to augment stability. In the same setting, the posterior labrum‐capsuloligamentous complex is repaired with suture anchors to re‐establish glenoid containment. This single‐stage approach addresses both bony and soft‐tissue pathology to prevent engagement and preserve native joint function.

Posterior shoulder dislocation commonly results in a reverse Hill‐Sachs lesion (rHSL), an anteromedial humeral head impaction that may engage the posterior glenoid rim during internal rotation and contribute to recurrent instability. rHSLs have been reported in up to ~86% of posterior dislocations. 1 , 2 , 3 Small, acute defects may be managed nonoperatively, 4 whereas larger or engaging lesions are more likely to remain unstable and require surgery. 5 Recurrent instability is influenced by both soft‐tissue injury and humeral head morphology. 6 The gamma angle and defect size are clinically useful predictors: a gamma angle >90° and rHSL involvement >25% of the articular surface increase the risk of engagement and are associated with inferior results after conservative treatment. A gamma angle greater than 90° significantly increases the likelihood of defect engagement with the glenoid rim, particularly during internal rotation, which can perpetuate instability and lead to poorer outcomes with conservative treatment. 1 In lesions of this size, nonoperative management is often ineffective, necessitating surgical intervention to restore joint stability and prevent further dislocations. 5 , 7 , 8

Surgical options for rHSLs are varied and depend on the size, location, and nature of the defect. Techniques range from tendon‐based procedures like McLaughlin or modified McLaughlin, to structural bone grafting (either autograft or allograft), and in more severe cases, arthroplasty may be considered. 2 Autologous iliac‐crest grafting restores the articular arc, and various fixation methods have been described, including screw fixation in open surgery. 9 , 10 Here, we describe an arthroscopic technique that uses suture anchors and a double‐pulley fixation technique for securing the iliac crest bone block. Additionally, we enhance stability by applying external pressure to the anterior joint capsule soft tissue. This method combines bone grafting with soft tissue stabilization to address both the bony defect and the associated soft tissue insufficiency, thereby improving joint stability and reducing the risk of recurrent instability.

Step 3: Autologous Iliac Crest Bone Graft Harvest and Preparation

Through a ~3‐cm incision along the anterior‐superior iliac crest, a curved corticocancellous bone block is harvested (approximately 2.2 × 1.0 × 1.0 cm, adjusted to defect size) (Figure 3A‐C). The graft is contoured to the humeral footprint with the cortical surface preserved to restore humeral contour. 4 tunnels (2 superior, 2 inferior) are drilled in a rectangular configuration, maintaining ≥2 mm bone bridge from each hole to the edge to reduce fracture risk (Figure 3D).

Step 4: Posterior Capsulolabral Repair

Posterior stabilization is performed before humeral grafting. The posterior labrum/capsule is mobilized and the posterior glenoid rim is lightly prepared. Three to four knotless PushLock anchors (2.9 mm; Arthrex, Naples, FL) are placed at the posterosuperior, mid‐posterior, and posteroinferior rim (additional anchor as needed), advancing and securing the capsulolabral complex to recreate the posterior bumper without intra‐articular knots (Figure 4C).

(B) Delivery and seating of the arc‐shaped iliac‐crest bone block flush within the reverse Hill‐Sachs crater; superior limbs are passed through the superior tunnels and inferior same‐color limbs through the inferior tunnels (B, bone graft). (C) Posterior stabilization—repair of the posterior labrum and capsule using suture anchors, restoring the posterior bumper and capsular tension (P, Posterior rim of glenoid). (D) Anterior stabilization—extracapsular tightening: the remaining limbs from the superior and inferior anchors are tied outside the joint over the anterior capsule/subscapularis, creating two low‐profile compression knots that reinforce the inlay and add anterior stability (F, anterior capsule/front; H, humeral head).

Step 5: Humeral Defect Preparation and Anchor Placement

Attention is then turned to the humeral defect. Viewing can be switched to an anterior portal to optimize visualization of the anteromedial humeral head. The rHSL crater is debrided to a flat, bleeding bed to improve graft contact and healing. Two 4.5‐mm double‐loaded suture anchors (Helicoil; Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) are inserted perpendicular to the defect surface at the superior and inferior borders of the central portion of the defect. This anchor position is chosen to create broad opposing compression vectors across the graft footprint (Figure 4A).

Step 6: Suture Retrieval and Allocation

All limbs are retrieved anteriorly through separate capsular windows and organized outside the joint. For graft fixation, select one same‐color suture from the superior anchor and one same‐color suture from the inferior anchor (4 limbs total) to create the double‐pulley compression construct. The remaining suture from each anchor is reserved for subsequent anterior soft‐tissue reinforcement and is not passed through the graft.

Step 7: Anterior Portal Dilation and Cannula Placement

The anterior working portal is dilated to allow graft passage. A coaxial metal delivery cannula is introduced through the anterior portal and aligned directly with the humeral defect. Establishing a straight, coaxial path is important for controlled graft insertion and to reduce the risk of graft rotation or suture entanglement during passage.

Step 8: Graft Suture Passage and Delivery

With the graft outside the joint, the selected graft‐fixation sutures are passed through the predrilled tunnels as follows: the chosen limbs from the superior anchor are passed through the two superior tunnels (cancellous‐to‐cortical), and the chosen same‐color limbs from the inferior anchor are passed through the two inferior tunnels. The reserved sutures for anterior soft‐tissue reinforcement remain outside the graft. The graft is then grasped with a clamp and delivered through the anterior metal cannula. During insertion, the graft is oriented so that the cortical surface faces outward (away from the joint) according to the planned seating geometry. Suture crossing is avoided throughout passage. Under arthroscopic visualization, the graft is advanced into the defect and gently manipulated until it docks flush with the prepared bed (Figure 4B).

Step 9: Double‐Pulley Graft Fixation

Once the graft is seated, fixation is achieved by tying the superior and inferior constructs using a double‐pulley technique. The superior and inferior suture pairs are tensioned in a controlled fashion to compress the graft broadly against the defect bed, and the constructs are secured with additional half‐hitches. Arthroscopic probing is used to confirm that the graft is stable and seated flush without prominent edges (Figure 4B).

Step 10: Anterior Soft‐Tissue Reinforcement

After graft fixation, the reserved sutures (one remaining suture from each anchor) are used for anterior soft‐tissue reinforcement. These sutures are tied extra‐articularly over the anterior capsule and subscapularis, creating two focused extracapsular compression knots (superior and inferior). This step provides additional anterior soft‐tissue support while avoiding broad tendon transfer into the defect (Figures 3E and 4D). Postoperative radiological results are shown in Figure 5A‐C.

DISCUSSION

Posterior shoulder dislocation is uncommon (2%‐4%) and frequently overlooked; when present, an rHSL and posterior capsulolabral injury often render the joint unstable. 2 , 11 , 12 For medium, engagement‐prone rHSLs—driven more by orientation (e.g., adverse gamma angle) than simple percent loss—there remains no consensus; pathways generally divide into anatomic reconstruction of the joint surface 13 versus nonanatomic tendon “filling” (McLaughlin variants, capsulotenodesis). 7 , 14

We describe an all‐arthroscopic, joint‐preserving technique for large, engaging rHSLs: a curved iliac‐crest inlay compressed with two double‐loaded anchors in a double‐pulley construct, a structured posterior capsulolabral repair, and targeted extracapsular anterior capsular/subscapularis compression using the remaining limbs. Anchor placement at the superior and inferior borders of the central defect creates opposing compression vectors and broad graft contact without intra‐articular screws. A coaxial anterior portal with metal cannula streamlines delivery of a predrilled four‐tunnel graft. The selective extracapsular fixation confines the checkrein to the graft face, minimizing constraint on the subscapularis and anterior capsule while preserving internal rotation.

The surgical technique addresses both sides of the pathology: the inlay restores the humeral defect and eliminates engagement, anterior soft‐tissue tightening adds support, and the posterior repair re‐establishes the bumper. This combination is suited to high‐demand patients who require stability without the trade‐offs of arthroplasty or the motion loss sometimes seen after tendon‐based techniques. However, the technique also has disadvantages, including iliac‐crest donor‐site morbidity, technical complexity with a substantial learning curve (portal alignment, suture management, and graft passage), and the risk of graft‐related complications such as fracture, malposition, nonunion, or resorption. In addition, anchor fixation may be less reliable in poor bone quality, and the procedure requires specialized instrumentation (including a delivery cannula) and may increase operative time.
