First report using near-infrared spectroscopy to age-grade Phlebotomus papatasi [Scopoli, 1786] sand flies
Authors: Catherine M. Flanley, Taina N. Ferreira, Douglas A. Shoue, Floyd E. Dowell, Caroline J. Isemann, Michelle S. Huang, Fernando A. Genta, Mary Ann McDowell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Vector age and survival directly influence vectorial capacity and disease transmission potential. Current age-grading techniques for phlebotomine sand flies are time-intensive and destructive as they require dissection by highly skilled laboratory personnel that groups hematophagous females as young or old depending on parity status. This study examined the potential of near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) to age-grade Phlebotomus papatasi sand flies, a primary vector of Leishmania major, a causative agent of cutaneous leishmaniasis. Female (n = 746) and male (n = 497) adult P. papatasi were collected 7, 14, and 21 days post-emergence. The NIR spectra from individual sand flies were used to develop a partial least squares regression model using a leave one out cross-validation approach. Independent test sets of P. papatasi were used to test the model prediction accuracy. When categorized as < 7 or ≥ 7 days old, the overall predictive accuracy was 85% for females and 87% for males. Sand flies reared in slightly cooler insectary conditions were also predicted with greater than 80% accuracy on average. For the first time, this study validates the use of NIRS as a non-destructive and rapid age-grading technique for P. papatasi

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization, leishmaniasis remains one of the most neglected diseases affecting some of the most vulnerable populations in impoverished and developing countries1. In recent years, leishmaniasis has started to become endemic to the United States, with 59% of reported cases from 2007 to 2017 originating in the U.S.2. Globally, two million cases of leishmaniasis occur each year with 1 million of those being cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL)1,2. Experts predict that by 2080, those exposed to leishmaniasis will double2. CL is the most prevalent clinical outcome of leishmaniasis, resulting in ulcerative skin lesions in exposed areas of skin such as the face, neck, arms, and hands. Although the lesions typically self-heal, the lifelong scarring can lead to disfigurement and social prejudice3. CL is caused by several species of Leishmania parasites, including Leishmania major, vectored by the Phlebotomus papatasi [Scopoli, 1786] sand fly1.

Vector survival is a principal determinant of vectorial capacity and transmission potential. Hematophagous arthropods, like sand flies and mosquitoes, must survive past the parasite developmental period in the vector, known as the extrinsic incubation period, to be able to transmit the parasite to a new host. Female P. papatasi sand flies must be older than 8 days post-emergence to transmit Le. major parasites, similarly to Anopheles mosquitoes that must be older than 10 days post-emergence to transmit malaria parasites4,5.

Nulliparous females are considered young, and parous females are considered old. However, parity is not an accurate predictor for infection status, as oviposition occurs before the Leishmania parasites complete their life cycle development in the sand fly. Adult female sand flies, including P. papatasi and P. dubosqci, another competent Le. major vector, exhibit gonotrophic discordance that increases their potential to transmit Leishmania parasites over their lifetime. Gonotrophic discordance increases sand fly vectorial capacity because the females can take multiple blood meals during each gonotrophic cycle6. Although a second blood meal may increase mortality in female sand flies, the second blood meal may increase the vectorial capacity of the surviving adult females7–9. Elnaiem et al. demonstrated that the Leishmania metacyclic promastigotes, or infectious forms, develop in the gut of the New World sand fly Lutzomyia longipalpis and migrates to the proboscis more rapidly after the second blood meal when compared to the first blood meal8. After the second blood meal, an increase in infectivity due to rapid parasite development was also observed in P. papatasi with a 125-fold increase in parasite load found in the female sand fly and a fourfold increase in lesion frequency upon infection in the murine model9. Disrupting the transmission of Leishmania parasites is of paramount importance and one way to accomplish this is by employing control strategies aimed at reducing the age of sand fly populations.

To properly quantify the impact of new vector management strategies that target natural population age reduction, a fast, easy, cost-effective, and nondestructive method to estimate the age of wild-caught sand flies is imperative, but is currently nonexistent for this species. The current methods for age grading P. papatasi and other phlebotomine species are time consuming as they are labor-intensive and require highly skilled technicians to dissect female sand flies. Thus, limiting the number of samples that can be processed. One current method determines parity by examining the presence or absence of crease marks in the genital atrium armature10,11. The examination of ovarian accessory gland secretion, where transparent secretions indicate nulliparous females while opaque, yellowish, granulated secretions indicate parous females, was found to be an unreliable method in many species including sand flies12. Another method determines age by examining cuticular growth lines, but requires dissection and proper mounting of specimens, resulting in the loss of 30% of individuals13.

In this study, we examined the potential of near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) to age-grade laboratory-reared P. papatasi sand flies. A wide variety of NIRS applications have been validated including the identification of stored grain insect pests14, differentiation of male and female tsetse fly pupae15, identification of termite species16, quantification of blood meal size in two different mosquito species17, and Zika infection status in Aedes aegypti mosquitoes18. Additionally, NIRS has been established as an age-grading tool for house flies19, stored-product beetles20, the biting midge, Culicoides sonorensis21, fruit flies22, and several mosquito species23–26. NIRS offers a rapid, inexpensive, nondestructive approach to age estimation. Individual insects can be properly positioned and scanned in less than one minute allowing for high-throughput data collection of large volumes of individuals while allowing for future downstream experiments with the same specimens.

NIRS measures the absorption of near-infrared energy (400–2500 nm) by the bending and stretching of organic compound functional groups; for example, O–H, N–H, and C–O. A unique spectrum for each age class is expected as cuticular hydrocarbons change with age in other important vectors such as mosquitoes and biting midges21,23,24. Although cuticular hydrocarbon changes related to age have not been extensively studied in phlebotomine species, cuticular changes have been identified27–29. Data collection is also possible for freshly caught and anesthetized or preserved insects30,31.

We previously demonstrated value in NIRS as an age-grading technique for Lu. longipalpis sand flies32, finding that NIRS is able to accurately predict sand flies into three age groups with accuracy of 58–82%, and into young (< 8 days) or old (> 8 days) with an accuracy of 82–92%2. Lu. longipalpis sand flies transmit visceral leishmaniasis and are distributed across Central and South America32. Here we validate the use of NIRS as a method to determine the age of laboratory-reared female and male P. papatasi sand flies. These sand flies are responsible for the majority of CL cases distributed across Europe, the Middle East, and North Africa. The ability to age grade P. papatasi vector populations can provide insights into transmission intensity, help to evaluate population control efforts aimed at reducing vector lifespan, and bring a greater understanding of disease epidemiology.

Discussion

Vector survival is a critical element that influences transmission intensity and vectorial capacity. Female vectors must survive past the extrinsic incubation period of the parasite to transmit to a new host. For P. papatasi, females must live longer than 8 days to be viable vectors4. Understanding the age structure of P. papatasi populations provides epidemiological information, such as the proportion of potentially infectious vectors, and offers a quantitative measurement that determines the efficacy of age reduction and vector survival control strategies. Current age-grading techniques for sand flies categorizes nulliparous females as young and parous females as old, but parity does not correlate accurately to infection status6. In this study, we validate the use of NIRS to age-grade female and male laboratory-reared P. papatasi sand flies. As our experimental set up was such that we knew the actual age of the sand flies and that parity can only classify sand flies as young and old, we did not use morphological characteristics to cross-validate the NIRS results. NIRS has been employed in various agricultural and vector-related studies ranging from the identification of agricultural pests in stored grain to vector sibling species identification, and age-grading insect species14,19–21,23. We recently reported the usefulness of NIRS to age-grade phlebotomine species, using Lu. longipalpis. Here we extend these studies to age-grade the P. papatasi phlebotomine species and its potential for further applications.

The PLS regression model, developed using a CV leave-one-out approach, predicted the age of independent tests of sand flies with an overall accuracy of 85% for females and 87% for males when categorized as young or old. The model was less accurate in predicting individuals into their actual age groups. The same limitation was observed in the age-grading of Lu. longipalpis, and a similar categorization (young vs old) also had to be used. One possible reason for the low accuracy of the model was the lower number of insects used in each category, when compared to the same kind of calibration curve when performed with mosquitoes, where typically 500–1000 individuals in each category were used24–27. In this respect, the difficulty in obtaining high numbers of insects, especially in older cohorts (either in the field or in laboratory conditions), is a clear limitation in applying NIR to sand flies. Although a model that could predict the age of sand flies with finer resolution (e.g. 0–2, 3–5, 6–8, etc.) would be ideal, a young versus old identification provides useful epidemiological information, such as the proportion of potentially infectious vectors in populations.

PLS regression coefficients and differences in spectral data clarify which wavelengths have the greatest impact on the PLS regression model. Many different variables influence the spectral data collected, but one of the major factors affecting the predictive capability of the PLS regression model is the change in chemical moieties of cuticular hydrocarbons. The changes in functional groups composed of C, H, O, and N create the positive and negative peaks in the regression coefficient plots.

Cuticular lipids function to prevent desiccation in insect species but also play important roles in pheromone mate attraction and species identification. The composition of cuticular lipids may include wax esters, glycerides, free fatty acids, sterols, alcohols, aldehydes, and hydrocarbons in varying amounts depending on species and even individuals within a species. Cuticular hydrocarbons are commonly found in insect species but their concentration varies greatly between species from as little as 2% to 95% of total lipid concentration33. To determine if cuticular lipids absorb a significant portion of NIR energy when scanned, rice weevils were stripped of their cuticular lipids using hexane extraction, and the resulting calibration model was then unable to accurately classify individuals by age20.

The NIR wavelengths contributing to our PLS regression models distinguishing age for female and male sand flies, 1200–1285, 1405–1485, 1685–1725, and 1910–1935 nm, align with important spectral regions in previous studies attributed to common C–H, O–H, or N–H overtones19–21,23,26. The spectral regions presented here do not align exactly with these previous studies, but this is expected as the cuticular lipid composition and particular concentrations of different lipid classes differ between species and the interactions of the different functional groups has the potential to shift the peaks33.

Cuticle deposition over time has not been widely studied in phlebotomine species but has been in other dipteran insects such as the house fly, Musca domestica, and the mosquitoes Anopheles and Aedes spp.34–37. These studies indicate age and sex differences in cuticular hydrocarbons that may help may help to explain the considerable variability we observed in the day 0 predicted P. papatasi individuals and the ability of the PLS regression model to more accurately predict young versus old sand flies.

Counting daily cuticle growth on apodemes is another age-grading technique used in dipteran insects but is quite labor-intensive and destructive to specimens38,39. In anopheline mosquitoes, daily cuticle deposition ended after 10 days with the maximum number of days being 1439. This observation supports the leveling-off effect of predicted ages found in older sand flies. In particular, the PLS model has difficulty predicting the age of females older than 14 days, and was unable to interpret the independent test sets of 21-day old females, suggesting that the cuticle was not significantly changing in older adults, as seen in many other related species.

For this study, it was vitally important to mimic the habitats and conditions in which specimens are collected, preserved, and received from wild populations for future experiments. All of the samples scanned were stored in RNAlater™, a validated preservation method with minimal adverse effect on NIRS model prediction capabilities in other insects31. Insects collected in field settings are often preserved via desiccation or in ethanol, but these methods can cause specimens to become brittle making dissection for additional analyses difficult. Additionally, NIRS on ethanol-preserved specimens is less accurate than using it on fresh samples. Although RNAlater™ is significantly more expensive than other preservation methods, it keeps specimens in a condition suitable for dissection, as well as for extraction of both RNA and DNA. In addition, a major benefit for surveillance workflows is that samples can be stored at room temperature for one week, making transport from remote locations to laboratory settings easier.

P. papatasi exhibit a high degree of environmental flexibility as they inhabit a variety of ecotopes from desert-like conditions to cooler Mediterranean climates40. Sand flies were reared in two different conditions, designated normal and cool, to simulate P. papatasi specimens collected from populations found in endemic countries with diverse environmental conditions. Although the cooler conditions decreased overall prediction accuracy into individual age groups, the model differentiated young and old sand flies with greater than 80% accuracy on average. Temperature manipulates cuticular growth. Higher temperatures accelerate layer deposition, while lower temperatures decrease it. Extremely low temperatures can result in cessation41. This temperature dependency is likely responsible for the decrease in prediction accuracy observed. The sand flies reared in cooler conditions were not used to train the PLS regression model as this purposely introduced an additional variable to see how the model would respond and could account for the lower classification accuracy observed. This mimicked variable temperatures in field settings to see how the model would handle predication capabilities. Further studies are needed in this area including calibration in diverse field settings. Lower temperatures may delay cuticle development or change the composition, which may skew the predicted age of an individual sand fly to be younger than its actual age. Several other factors (biodiversity of fungal and bacterial species present on the cuticle of P. papatasi, composition and concentration of surface polar lipids, female gravidity) could also influence PLS regression by absorbing NIR energy23,29,42. Future follow-up studies could control for these variables to increase prediction accuracy.

NIRS is reagent-free, high throughput technique, and personnel can be trained to scan and analyze data in just a few hours. Thus, this method can easily be incorporated and scale sand fly population monitoring in field settings. Although not as accurate as DNA-based test methods, recent studies estimate that NIRS is 18 times faster and 110 times cheaper than RT-qPCR, allowing for rapid surveillance of vector populations18. Currently, a cheap, efficient, and non-destructive method to age sand flies does not exist and NIRS offers the sand fly research community a missing tool for surveillance and further downstream experiments such as determination of infection status using PCR.

Differences in cuticular hydrocarbon profiles have been studied in phlebotomine species to distinguish allopatric and sympatric populations as well as vector species from non-vector species using gas chromatography27,28,43–45. NIRS can support these studies by identifying morphologically indistinguishable species. Then, the same samples can be used for genetic and molecular testing, elucidating gene flow between populations and potential insecticide resistance development.

Conclusions

The age structure and survival of vector species influence important epidemiological factors such as transmission potential and vectorial capacity. The current methods for age-grade sand flies are labor-intensive, requiring skilled personnel and destroying samples so they cannot be used in further experiments. The present study confirms NIRS as a non-destructive and rapid age-grading technique for laboratory-reared P. papatasi aged 0, 7, 14, and 21 days post-emergence. Overlapping of sand fly ages occurred, but the PLS regression model accurately distinguished between young (< 7 days old) and old (≥ 7 days old) sand flies. The PLS regression model predicted independent test sets of female sand flies into their actual age groups with 38% accuracy that increased to 85% when categorized as young or old. Similarly, male prediction accuracy improved from 66 to 87% when grouped as young or old. Further study is required to validate this technique on field-captured sand flies.

Methods

Laboratory-reared sand flies

All P. papatasi (Israeli strain) sand flies used in this experiment were reared at the University of Notre Dame (Notre Dame, Indiana) under the following conditions: 28 °C, 80% RH, and a 12:12 light–dark cycle with a 60-min dawn and dusk period. The sand fly colony was maintained with blood-feeding on anesthetized rats once per week and adult sand flies were provided with 20% (w/v) sucrose solution to feed ad libitum. Sand fly larvae were fed a mixture of rabbit feces and rabbit chow46. Females were collected at 0, 7, 14, and 21 days post emergence. Males were collected at 0, 7, and 14-days post emergence. Over the collection window, only 11 males lived to 21 days. Thus, this time point was removed from the experimental design. Individuals categorized as 0-days old are sand flies that are less than 24 h old post emergence. To assess if the calibration model could accurately predict P. papatasi flies collected from a different environmental condition, some larval pots were moved to a temperature-controlled chamber where the temperature was slightly cooler at 26 °C with 60% humidity.

All adults were collected and anesthetized with chloroform, washed using a 10% (v/v) Alconox detergent solution, and stored fully submerged in RNAlater™ at room temperature until scanning. The actual number of adults collected for each time point and environmental condition (normal or cool) can be found in Table 1.

NIR spectrometer scanning

All sand flies included in the experiment were transferred to the United States Department of Agriculture, Center for Grain and Animal Health Research, Stored Product Insect and Engineering Research, Manhattan, Kansas, for scanning. Immediately before scanning, adult sand flies were removed from the RNAlater™ and blotted on paper towels to eliminate any residual liquid. A maximum of 10 sand flies at a time were positioned on their sides on a Spectralon diffuse reflectance plate with the head and thorax of the sand fly centered under the 3 mm fiber-optic probe (Model 135325 Rev A, ASD, Inc.). NIR spectra were collected using a LabSpec 4i Standard Res Lab Analyzer (Malvern Panalytical, Longmont, CO) as outlined in established protocols21,23. Twenty spectra were collected for each sand fly and stored as an average spectrum using ASD software RS3, with the entire process taking less than one minute. After scanning, sand flies were returned to their respective tubes and fully submerged in the RNAlater™.

Data analysis

Spectra were converted using ASD ViewSpecPro (Malvern Panalytical) to a format supported by the GRAMS Plus/IQ software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tewksbury, MA). Data was analyzed according to previously established protocols22,24. The methods section in Mayagaya et al. (2009) provides a detailed explanation of spectra processing and categorization23. All spectra were analyzed between 500 and 2350 nm wavelengths to minimize noise in the data set and mean-centered before using the GRAMS software to perform Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression and develop calibrations. Cross-validation (CV) was employed to develop the calibrations. The number of PLS factors was chosen from the information in the regression coefficient plot and the predicted residual error sum of squares (PRESS) plot. In the model, spectra from newly emerged sand flies were assigned a value of “0”, spectra from one week-old sand flies were assigned a value of “7”, spectra from 2-week-old sand flies were assigned a value of “14”, and 3-week-old sand flies were assigned a value of “21.” Each age group of sand flies had a calibration set and at least two independent test sets.

The age of individual sand flies was predicted based on their collected spectrum. Both female and male sand flies were evaluated according to the following predicted age cut-off values for the four age groups: ≤ 3.5 were considered 0 days old, > 3.5–10.5 were considered 7 days old, > 10.5–17.5 were considered 14 days old, and > 17.5–24.5 were considered 21 days old. Both female and male sand flies were further evaluated according to following cut-off values for the two age groups: < 7 were considered young, and ≥ 7 were considered old. The calibration model was developed using sand flies reared in normal conditions only. Statistical differences between age groups of females and males were detected using analysis of variance (ANOVA) in GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Specifically, Kruskal–Wallis test and Dunn’s multiple comparison test were employed.
