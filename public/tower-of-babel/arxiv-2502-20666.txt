{preface}

Recent advancements in this field have paved the way for an exploration of intricate phenomena that characterize the behavior of dynamical systems. This book, delves into key concepts and emerging trends in the study of linear dynamical systems, offering a comprehensive overview of some of the most significant developments in this dynamic and evolving field.

In Chapter 1, we explore some basic facts from the Banach contracting
principle to the equivalence between hyperbolicity, shadowing and expansivity for invertible operators on Banach spaces.

In Chapter 2, we embark on the fundamental principles that underlie the understanding of hyperbolicity, expansivity, and shadowing phenomena within linear dynamical systems. This chapter serves as a foundational exploration of crucial concepts, providing readers with the necessary groundwork to delve deeper into subsequent chapters.

Chapter 3, extends the discussion beyond the traditional realms of hyperbolicity, exploring the nuances and implications of generalized hyperbolic obehavior in linear dynamical systems
[
6
]
. This chapter illuminates the broader spectrum of dynamics that arise when considering generalized hyperbolic structures, opening new avenues for research and discovery.

In Chapter 4, the focus shifts to a critical aspect of dynamical systems—their stability under perturbations. Structural stability is a key theme, and the chapter provides insights into the conditions that ensure the persistence of qualitative properties in the face of small perturbations, a crucial consideration in the study of linear dynamics. We present a proof of the Bernardes-Messaoudi Stability theorem about the structural stability of generalized hyperbolic operators
[
4
]
.

The exploration continues in Chapter 5, where the attention turns to the intricate dynamics surrounding homoclinic points. This chapter delves into the rich behavior exhibited by systems near homoclinic points, shedding light on their significance and implications in the broader context of linear dynamics.

Chapter 6, presents a captivating departure from conventional topics. The exploration of a peculiar operator and its applications widens the scope of linear dynamics, revealing unexpected connections and potential applications in diverse scientific and engineering fields.

Throughout this book, the aim is to provide a cohesive and accessible resource for researchers, academics, and students interested in the cutting-edge developments within linear dynamics. Each chapter is designed to contribute to a deeper understanding of the intricate dynamics exhibited by linear systems and to inspire further exploration into the fascinating realm of linear dynamics. As we embark on this journey, it is our hope that this monograph serves as both a guide and a catalyst for continued advancements in this dynamic and vibrant field of study.

Chapter 1
Basics

Let
X
be a metric space. We say that
X
is separable if it has a dense sequence.
A sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
Cauchy
if

\lim_{m,n\to\infty}d(x_{n},x_{m})=0.

More precisely, for every
\epsilon>0
there is
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

d(x_{n},x_{m})<\epsilon
for all
m,n\geq N
.
We say that
X
is complete if every Cauchy sequence of
X
is convergent i.e. there is
p\in X
such that

\lim_{n\infty}x_{n}=p.

Definition 1

A map
f:X\to X
is a contraction if there is
0<\lambda<1
such that

d(f(x),f(y))\leq\lambda d(x,y),\quad\quad\forall x,y\in X.

(1)

A
fixed point
of
f:X\to X
is a point
x\in X
such that
f(x)=x
.
The set of fixed points of
f
is denoted by
Fix(f)
.

Theorem 1.1
(Banach Contracting Principle)

Every contracting map of a complete metric space has a fixed point.

Proof

Let
0<\lambda<1
be satisfying (
1
).
Fix
x\in X
.

For every
n\geq 0
we define
f^{0}=id_{X}
(the identity of
X
) and

f^{n}=f\circ\overset{(n)}{\cdots}\circ f
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
It follows that

d(f^{n}(x),f^{n}(y))\leq\lambda d(f^{n-1}(x),f^{n-1}(x))\leq\overset{(n)}{\cdots}\leq\lambda^{n}d(x,y),\quad\forall x,y\in X,\,n\in\mathbb{N}.

Now, fix
x\in X
.
Since for all
m>n
one has

\displaystyle d(f^{m}(x),f^{n}(x))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d(f^{m}(x),f^{n+1}(x))+d(f^{n+1}(x),f^{n}(x))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d(f^{m}(x),f^{n+2}(x))+d(f^{n+2}(x),f^{n+1}(x))+

\displaystyle d(f^{n+1}(x),f^{n}(x))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m-n-1}d(f^{n+k+1}(x),f^{n+k}(x))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k=0}^{m-n-1}\lambda^{n+k}\right)d(f(x),x)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\lambda^{n}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{k}\right)d(f(x),x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda^{n}}{1-\lambda}d(f(x),x)\to 0\quad\quad\mbox{ as }\quad\quad n\to\infty

thus

\lim_{n,m\to\infty}d(f^{m}(x),f^{n}(x))=0

hence
(f^{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
is Cauchy. Since
X
is complete, there is
p\in X
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}f^{n}(x)=p.

Since
f
is contracting,
f
is continuous.
So,

f(p)=f(\lim_{n\to\infty}f^{n}(x))=\lim_{n\to\infty}f^{n+1}(x)=p

thus
p\in Fix(f)
completing the proof.
∎

Note that if
f
is a contraction of a complete metric space, then the above proof says that there is
p\in Fix(f)
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}f^{n}(x)=p,\quad\quad\forall x\in X.

In other words, the positive orbit
(f^{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
of every point
x\in X

converges to
p
.
Therefore, the point
p
is an
attractor
in the sense that it

attracts
the
positive orbit
of every point.

Now, we introduce some dynamical formalism.
Let
f:X\to X
be a map of a metric space.
We say that
x\in X
is

•

Fixed if
f(x)=x
;

•

Periodic if
f^{k}(x)=x
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
;

•

Nonwandering
if for every neighborhood
U
of
x
there is
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{n}(U)\cap U\neq\emptyset
;

•

Chain recurrent
if for every
\delta>0
there is a sequence

(x_{i})_{i=0}^{k}
such that
x_{0}=x=x_{k}
and
d(f(x_{i}),x_{i+1})<\delta
for all
0\leq i\leq k-1
.

The corresponding sets are denoted by
Fix(f)
,
Per(f)
,
\Omega(f)
and
CR(f)
.
The following relationships between these sets holds:

Fix(f)\subset Per(f)=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}Fix(f^{n})\subset\Omega(f)\subset CR(f).

None of the reversed of the above inclusions holds.
We also have for any
x\in X
,

•

Positive orbit:
(f^{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
;

•

Negative orbit (when
f
invertible):
(f^{-n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
where
f^{-n}=(f^{-1})^{n}
;

•

Orbit (when
f
invertible):
(f^{n}(x))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
.

Of fundamental importance is the knowledge of the limit points of the orbits of
x
. In this vein, we define

\omega(x)
as the limit points of the positive orbit of
x
namely

\omega(x)=\{y\in X:y=\lim_{i\to\infty}f^{n_{i}}(x)\mbox{ for some sequence }n_{i}\to\infty\}.

In the invertible case we define
\alpha(x)
as the
\omega
-limit set of
f
under the negative orbit.

The Banach principle can be reformulated as follows:

If
fX\to X
is a contraction of a complete metric space, then there is
p\in X
such that

Fix(f)=\Omega(f)=\{p\}
and
\omega(x)=\{p\}
for all
x\in X
.

In other words, we know what will happen with any orbit:
They will be attracted to a fixed point.
This kind of result is the prototype of what we search in dynamics.
More precisely, to understand the asymptotic behavior of the orbits or, more precisely, to determinate the structure of the omega-limit sets, or, the nonwandering or chain-recurrent points.

One more structure in dynamics is as follows.

•

We say that
x\in X
is a homoclinic point of a map
f:X\to X
, associated to
p\in X
, if

\lim_{n\to\pm\infty}f^{n}(x)=p.

The set of homoclinic points associated to
p
is denoted by
H_{f}(p)
.
Clearly
p\in Fix(f)

\iff

H_{f}(p)\neq\emptyset
.

Definition 2

We say that
f:X\to X
is:

•

Expansive: There is
\epsilon>0
(called expansivity constant) such that if

d(f^{n}(x)-f^{n}(y)\leq\epsilon
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}

\Longrightarrow

x=y
.

•

We say that
f:X\to X
is shadowing (or that has the shadowing property) if
for every
\epsilon>0
there is
\delta>0
such that for every sequence

(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
with
d(f(x_{n}),x_{n+1})\leq\delta
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
(called
\delta
-pseudo orbit) there is

x\in X
such that
d(f^{n}(x),x_{n})\leq\epsilon
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Next, we make some comments about the origin and motivations of the Linear Dynamics Theory.

We say that
X
is a
Banach space
over
K=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
if it is a vector space over
K
equipped with a norm
\|\cdot\|
whose induced metric is complete.

Example 1

X=\mathbb{C}^{n}
with the norm
\|z\|=\sqrt{|z_{1}|^{2}+\cdots+|z_{n}|^{2}}
or, for a given
p\in[0,\infty)

X=l^{p}(\mathbb{C})=\{z=(z_{i})_{i\geq 0}:z_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
and
\sum_{i\geq 0}|z_{i}|^{p}<\infty\}
with the norm
\|z\|=(\sum_{i\geq 0}|z_{i}|^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}
.
Similarly
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
l^{p}(\mathbb{R})
.

Example 2

Let
(X,\mu)
be a measure space. Given
p\in[1,\infty)
we define

L^{p}=L^{p}(X,\mu)=\{f:X\to\mathbb{R}:f\mbox{ is measurable and }\int|f|^{p}d\mu<\infty\}.

This is a Banach space (with the usual sum and scalar product of functions) and the
L^{p}
-norm

\|f\|_{p}=\left(\int|f|^{p}d\mu\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

A Banach space is
Hilbert
if there is an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
such that

\|x\|^{2}=\langle x,x\rangle,\quad\quad\forall x\in X.

Of the above examples, only
\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
l^{2}
and
L^{2}
are Hilbert.

Definition 3

A map of a Banach space
L:X\to X
is linear if
L(x+y)=L(x)+L(y)
and
L(\lambda x)=\lambda L(x)
for all
x,y\in X
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.

Definition 4

A linear operator of a Banach space is a linear map which is also continuous.

Following properties are equivalent for every linear map of a Banach space

L:X\to X
,

•

L
is a linear operator (i.e. continuous);

•

L
is continuous at
x=0
.

•

L
is
bounded
i.e.
there is
K\in(0,\infty)
such that

\|L(x)\|\leq K\|x\|,\quad\quad\forall x\in X.

•

\sup_{x\neq 0}\frac{\|L(x)\|}{\|x\|}<\infty

We can then define the
norm
of a bounded linear operator
L:X\to X
by

\|L\|=\sup_{x\neq 0}\frac{\|L(x)\|}{\|x\|}

and one has

\|L\|=\sup_{\|x\|=1}\|L(x)\|.

The space
Op(X)
of linear operators of a Banach space
X
is a Banach space if endowed with the norm
\|L\|
for
L\in Op(X)
.
We also consider those
L\in Op(X)
such that
L^{-1}
exists and is continuous. This is the set of
invertible linear operators
denoted by

GL(X)
.

The
spectrum
of
L\in Op(X)
is the set
\sigma(L)\subset\mathbb{C}
defined by
\lambda\in\sigma(L)

\iff

\lambda-L\notin GL(X)
.
As a result,
\sigma(L)
is precisely the set of
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
such that the linear operator
\lambda-L
is not bijective i.e. not injective or not surjective. In the first case
\lambda
is called
eigenvalue
of
L
.
The set of eigenvalues is denoted by
\sigma_{p}(L)
(this is the so-called the point spectrum of
L
).

The origin of the Linear Dynamics can be traced back to the
Invariant subspace problem
(ISP):

Proposition 1.2

Does every linear operator of a complex Banach space
L:X\to X

exhibit a closed subspace
M
different from
X
and
\{0\}
such that

L(M)\subset M
?

The answer is immediately positive if
X
is not separable.

In fact, by taking any
x\in X\setminus\{0\}
the closure
M
of subspace generated by the positive orbit of
x
under
L
namely

M=\overline{Span\{L^{n}(x):n\geq 0\}}

(2)

satisfies the requirements of the ISP.

The question therefore makes sense in the separable case only.

In such a case a negative answer was given by Enflo.
However, the ISP is still open on separable Hilbert spaces.
The identity (
2
) put in evidence the relationship between the dynamics of a linear operator (represented by the positive orbit) and the ISP.
Many authors have been studying the dynamics of linear operators on Banach spaces.

This is what the subject Linear Dynamics is about.
We first analyze conditions for chain recurrence, homoclinic points and topological transitivity for linear operators.

Theorem 1.3

If
L\in Op(X)
for a Banach space
X
, then
CR(L)
is a closed subspace
and
L(CR(L))\subset CR(L)
.

Proof

Suppose that
x\in CR(L)
. If
\lambda\neq 0
is an scalar and
\epsilon>0
, there is a sequence
(x_{i})_{i=0}^{k}
such that
x_{0}=x_{k}=x
and
\|L(x_{i})-x_{i+1}\|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{|\lambda|}
for all
0\leq i\leq k-1
. It follows that
(\lambda x_{i})_{i=0}^{k}
is a sequence
with
\lambda x_{0}=\lambda x_{k}=\lambda x
and
\|L(\lambda x_{i})-\lambda x_{i+1}\|\leq\epsilon
for all
0\leq i\leq k
. Hence
\lambda x\in CR(L)
for
\lambda\neq 0
.
Since
0=0x
always belong to
CR(L)
we obtain
Spam(x)\subset CR(L)
.

If
x,y\in CR(L)
there are sequence,
(x_{i})_{i=0}^{k}
and
(y_{i})_{i=0}^{l}
with

x_{0}=x=x_{k}
,
y_{0}=y=y_{l}
,
\|L(x_{i})-x_{i+1}\|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}
for

and
\|L(y_{j})-y_{j+1}\|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}
for

. Assuming that

the sequence

(z_{i})_{i=0}^{l}=\{x_{0}+y_{0},x_{1}+y_{1},\cdots,x_{k}+y_{k},0+y_{k+1},\cdots,0+y_{l}\}

and

for all

.
Therefore,

.

Definition 5

A map of a metric space
f:X\to X
is topologically transitive
if for all open sets
U,V\subset X
there is
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

f^{n}(U)\cap V\neq\emptyset
.
Topologically transitive linear operators are often called hypercyclic operators.

Since Banach spaces are complete, to be topologically transitive is equivalent to the existence of dense orbits.
The natural question is if there are hypercyclic operators on finite dimensional spaces. However,

Theorem 1.5

There are no hypercyclic operators on finite dimensional Banach spaces.

Since every linear operator of a finite-dimensional Banach space has eigenvalues, this theorem is a direct consequence of the following
lemma which is interesting by its own. We learn it from the nice book by Bayart and Matherson
[
2
]
.
Remember that the dual of a linear operator of a Banach space
L:X\to X
is the linear operator
L^{*}(\varphi)=\varphi\circ L
defined on the dual space
X^{*}
i.e. the space of continuous linear mappings
\varphi:X\to\mathbb{C}
.

Lemma 1.6

If
L\in Op(X)
is hypercyclic, then its dual operator
L^{*}
has no eigenvalues.

Proof

Otherwise, there is
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
such that

L^{*}(\varphi)=\lambda\varphi
for some continuous non-zero linear mapping
\varphi:X\to\mathbb{C}
.
Since
L
is hypercyclic, there is
x\in X
such that

(L^{n}(x)_{n\in\geq 0}
is dense in
X
.
Since
L^{*}(\varphi)=\lambda\varphi
, we have
\varphi\circ L=\lambda\varphi
so
\varphi(L^{n}(x))=\lambda^{n}\varphi(x)
for every
n\geq 0
.
Since
\varphi
is continuous and
(L^{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
is dense in
X
, we have that
(\lambda^{n}\varphi(x))_{n\geq 0}
is dense in
\varphi(X)
.
But
\varphi
is non-zero so
\varphi
is onto i.e.
\mathbb{C}=\varphi(X)
thus
(\lambda^{n}\varphi(x))_{n\geq 0}
is dense in
\mathcal{C}
which is absurd. This completes the proof.

Theorem 1.9

Every hyperbolic
L\in GL(X)
is expansive and shadowing.

We split the proof in some lemmas.

Lemma 1.10

Every hyperbolic
L\in GL(X)
is expansive.

Proof

Indeed, suppose
L
is not expansive.
Then, there is some
x\neq 0
such that
the sequence
(\|L^{n}(x)\|)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is bounded.
We write
x=x^{S}+x^{U}\in S\oplus U
.
Since

\|L^{n}(x^{U})\|\leq\|L^{n}(x)\|+\|L^{n}(x^{S})\|

(for

), the sequence
(\|L^{n}(x^{U})\|)_{n\geq 0}
is bounded.
This implies

.
Then,
x=x^{S}\in S
so

is bounded thus

hence

a contradiction. Therefore,

Lemma 1.11

Every contraction of a complete metric space

f:X\to X
has the shadowing property.

Proof

Let
\lambda
be the contracting constant of
f
.
Given
\epsilon>0
we choose
\delta=(1-\lambda)\epsilon
.
Let
(x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a
\delta
-pseudo orbit.

Define
E=\{\xi\in X^{\mathbb{Z}}:d(\xi_{i},x_{i})\leq\epsilon\,\forall i\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.

E
is a complete metric space if equipped with the supremum distance

D(\xi,\eta)=\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}d(\xi_{i},\eta_{i}).

Define
\Gamma:E\to E
by

\Gamma(\xi)_{i}=f(\xi_{i-1}),\quad\quad\forall i\in\mathbb{Z}.

We must prove
\Gamma
is well-defined.
Since

d(f(\xi_{i-1}),x_{i})\leq d(f(\xi_{i-1}),f(x_{i-1}))+d(f(x_{i-1}),x_{i})\leq\lambda\epsilon+\delta=\epsilon,

we have
\Gamma(E)\subset E
.

Since

D(\Gamma(\xi),\Gamma(\eta))=\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}d(f(\xi_{i}),f(\eta_{i}))\leq\lambda D(xi,\eta),

for all
\xi,\eta\in E

Suppose that there is a Banach space

\delta>0
from this property for
\epsilon=1
,

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|y_{n}\|\leq K\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|z_{n}\|\quad\mbox{ and }\quad y_{n+1}=Ly_{n}+z_{n},\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}.

We define

The lemma below justifies the term ”shadowableness constant” in this definition.

Lemma 3.3

L\in GL(X)
has the shadowing property
\iff

Shad(L)<\infty
.

Proof

First assume that
L
has the shadowing property and take
\delta>0
from this property for
\epsilon=1
. We shall prove that
K=\frac{1}{\delta}
is a shadowableness constant.
Let
(z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a bounded sequence and define
M=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|z_{n}\|
. We can assume that
M\neq 0
. Take a sequence

(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
such that

x_{n+1}=Lx_{n}+\frac{\delta}{M}z_{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}

(this sequence is completely determined by
x_{0}
).
Clearly
\|Lx_{n}-x_{n+1}\|\leq\delta
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
hence there is
x\in X
such that

\|L^{n}(x)-x_{n}\|\leq 1
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Define
y_{n}=\frac{M}{\delta}(x_{n}-L^{n}x)
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Then,

\|y_{n}\|\leq\frac{M}{\delta}=K\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|z_{n}\|
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
hence

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|y_{n}\|\leq K\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|Z_{n}\|.

Also

y_{n+1}=\frac{M}{\delta}(x_{n+1}-L^{n+1}x)=\frac{M}{\delta}(Lx_{n}+\frac{\delta}{M}z_{n}-L^{n+1}x)=Ly_{n}+z_{n},

so
y_{n+1}=L(y_{n})+z_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore,
K
is a shadowableness constant.

Conversely,
suppose that there is a shadowableness constant
K
. Fix the given
\epsilon>0
we set

\delta=\frac{\epsilon}{K}
. Let
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
such that

\|Lx_{n}-x_{n+1}\|\leq\delta
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Take
z_{n}=Lx_{n}-x_{n+1}
so
(z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
satisfies

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|z_{n}\|\leq\delta
and so it is bounded.
Then, there is
(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
such that

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|y_{n}\|\leq K\delta
and
y_{n+1}=Ly_{n}+z_{n}
.
Set
x=y_{0}-x_{0}
.
Since

y_{n+1}=Ly_{n}+Lx_{n}-x_{n+1}
,
y_{n+1}-x_{n+1}=L(y_{n}+x_{n})=\cdots=L^{n+1}(x)
for all

hence

L^{n}x=y_{n}+x_{n},\quad\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}.

Then,

\|L^{n}x-x_{n}\|=\|y_{n}\|\leq\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|y_{n}\|\leq K\delta=\epsilon,\quad\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}

proving the shadowing property for
L
. This ends the proof.
∎

An application of the above lemma is the following result.

Theorem 3.5

Let
X
a Banach space and
L\in GL(X)
.
If there is a direct sum
X=X_{1}\oplus X_{2}
by closed subspaces
X_{1},X_{2}
with

L(X_{i})=X_{i}
for
i=1,2
, then
L
has the shadowing property if and only if
L|_{X_{1}}
and
L|_{X_{2}}
do.

Proof

Let
P_{i}:X\to X_{i}
be the projections (
i=1,2
) associated to the splitting
X=X_{1}\oplus X_{2}
.
Define
H:X\to X_{1}\times X_{2}
by
H(x)=(P_{1}(x),P_{2}(x))
.
Clearly
H
is a linear map.
Since
X=X_{1}\oplus X_{2}
we can see that
H
is bijective with inverse

H^{-1}(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}+x_{2}
for
(x_{1},x_{2})\in X_{1}\times X_{2}
.
Then,

\|H^{-1}\|\leq 2.

Also, since

\|H(x)\|=\max\{\|P_{1}(x)\|,\|P_{2}(x)\|\}\leq\max\{\|P_{1}\|,\|P_{2}\|\}\|x\|,\quad\quad\forall x\in X,

H
is a bounded operator with

\|H\|\leq\max\{\|P_{1}\|,\|P_{2}\|\}.

Then,
H
is a linear homeomorphism.
On the other hand,

\displaystyle H^{-1}\circ(L|_{X_{1}}\times L|_{X_{2}})\circ H(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H^{-1}((L|_{X_{1}}\times L|_{X_{2}})(P_{1}(x),P_{2}(x)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H^{-1}(L(P_{1}(x)),L(P_{2}(x)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L(P_{1}(x))+L(P_{2}(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L(P_{1}(x)+P_{2}(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L(x),\quad\quad\forall x\in X

proving

H^{-1}\circ(L|_{X_{1}}\times L_{X_{2}})\circ H=L.

Then,

\max\{Shad(L|_{X_{1}}),Shad(L|_{X_{2}})\}\leq 2\max\{\|P_{1}\|,\|P_{2}\|\}Shad(L)

and

Shad(L)\leq 2\max\{\|P_{1}\|,\|P_{2}\|\}\max\{Shad(L|_{X_{1}}),Shad(L|_{X_{2}})\}

by Lemma
3.4
.
Therefore,
L
has the shadowing property
\iff

Shad(L)<\infty
(by Lemma
3.3
)

\iff

Shad(L|_{X_{i}})<\infty
for
i=1,2

\iff

L|_{X_{i}}
has the shadowing property for
i=1,2
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3

Since the identities
L(X_{1})=X_{1}
and
L(X_{2})=X_{2}
hold,

(L(P_{1}(x)),L(P_{2}(x)))\in X_{1}\times X_{2}

so
H^{-1}(L(P_{1}(x)),L(P_{2}(x)))=L(P_{1}(x))+L(P_{2}(x))
.
The question arise what if we change these identities by inclusions.

In the following result we estimate the shadowableness constant.

Theorem 3.6

Let
X
be a Banach space and
L\in GL(X)
.
If
X=S\oplus U
is a splitting into closed subsets
S
,
U
of
X
such that

L(S)\subset S
and
L^{-1}(U)\subset U
, then

Shad(L)\leq\|P_{S}\|\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|L^{k}|_{S}\|+\|P_{U}\|\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|L^{-k}|_{U}\|,

(11)

where
P_{S}:X\to S
and
P_{U}:X\to U
are the projections associated to the splitting
X=S\oplus U
.
If additionally
\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{k}|_{S}
and
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}L^{-k}|_{U}
are strongly convergent, then

\max\left\{\left\|\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{k}|_{S}\right\|,\left\|\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{-k}|_{U}\right\|\right\}\leq Shad(L).

(12)

Proof

To prove the first inequality we can assume

A=\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|L^{k}|_{A}\|<\infty\quad\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\quad B=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|L^{-k}|_{B}\|<\infty.

We assert that

K=\|P_{S}\|A+\|P_{U}\|B

is a shadowableness constant of
L
.

Take a bounded sequence

(z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
and define the sequence

(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
by

y_{n}=\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{k}P_{S}(z_{n-k-1})-\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}L^{-k}P_{U}(z_{n+k-1}),\quad\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}.

We have

\displaystyle\|y_{n}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|L^{k}|_{S}\|\|P_{S}(z_{n-k-1})\|+\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|L^{-k}|_{U}\|\|P_{U}(z_{n+k-1})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(A\|P_{S}\|+B\|P_{U}\|)\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|z_{n}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|z_{n}\|,\quad\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}

so

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|y_{n}\|\leq K\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|z_{n}\|.

Also

\displaystyle y_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{k}P_{S}(z_{n-k})-\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}L^{-k}P_{U}(z_{n+k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}L^{k}P_{S}(z_{n-k})-\left(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}L^{-k}P_{U}(z_{n+k})+P_{U}(z_{n})\right)+z_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}L^{k}P_{S}(z_{n-k})-\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{-k}P_{U}(z_{n+k-1})+z_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L\left(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}L^{k-1}P_{S}(z_{n-k})-\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{-(k+1)}P_{U}(z_{n+k-1})\right)+z_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L\left(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{k}P_{S}(z_{n-k-1})-\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}L^{-k}P_{U}(z_{n+k-1})\right)+z_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Ly_{n}+z_{n},\quad\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}

so
K
is a shadowableness constant.
This proves (
11
).

Now we assume that
\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{k}|_{S}
and
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}L^{-k}|_{U}
are strongly convergent. We can assume that
Shad(L)<\infty
.
Fix
\epsilon>0
, a shadowableness constant
K<Shad(L)+\epsilon
and

x\in S
. The constant sequence
(x)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is clearly bounded so
there is a sequence
(y_{n})_{\in\mathbb{Z}}
in
X
with
y_{0}\in S
such that

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|y_{n}\|\leq K\|x\|\quad\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\quad y_{n+1}=L(y_{n})+x,\quad\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(13)

It follows that

y_{n}=L^{n}(y_{0})+\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}L^{k}(x),\quad\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

But since
\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{k}|_{S}

Then, the inequality in (

Since

\left\|\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{k}|_{S}\right\|\leq Shad(L)+\epsilon.

Letting

Likewise

\left\|\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{-k}|_{U}\right\|\leq Shad(L)

so (

) holds.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{k}P_{S}(z_{n-k})-\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}L^{-k}P_{U}(z_{n+k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}L^{k}P_{S}(z_{n-k})-\left(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}L^{-k}P_{U}(z_{n+k})+P_{U}(z_{n})\right)+z_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}L^{k}P_{S}(z_{n-k})-\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{-k}P_{U}(z_{n+k-1})+z_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L\left(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}L^{k-1}P_{S}(z_{n-k})-\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{-(k+1)}P_{U}(z_{n+k-1})\right)+z_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L\left(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{k}P_{S}(z_{n-k-1})-\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}L^{-k}P_{U}(z_{n+k-1})\right)+z_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Ly_{n}+z_{n},\quad\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}

so
K
is a shadowableness constant.
Therefore,
L
has the shadowing property by Lemma
3.3
and the proof follows.
∎

The converse of Theorem

was questioned by D’Aniello et al
[
7
]
. More precisely,
they proposed the following question.

Question 3.10

Is every linear homeomorphism with the shadowing property of a Banach space generalized hyperbolic?

About this question we can make the following remark.

Remark 3.11

It follows from the proof od Theorem
3.9
that every
L\in GL(X)

exhibiting a splitting
X=S\oplus U
by closed subspaces
S
and
U
such that
L(S)\subset S
,
L^{-1}(U)\subset U
;

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|L|_{S}\|<\infty\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|L^{-1}|_{U}\|<\infty

has the shadowing property. For the moment such homeomorphisms will be referred to as weakly hyperbolic.
It is therefore tempting to obtain negative answer for the question as soon as we find a weakly hyperbolic linear homeomorphism which is not generalized hyperbolic.
Nevertheless, both concepts of hyperbolicity for linear homeomorphisms are indeed equivalent.

Let us present a partial positive answer for the question.
We follow part of the recent work
[
13
]
.
To motivate
we first give a consequence of Theorem
3.9
.
Recall that
L\in GL(X)
is
equicontinuous
if for every
\epsilon>0
there is
\delta>0
such that
if
x\in X
and
\|x\|\leq\delta
, then
\|L^{n}(x)\|\leq\epsilon
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The
central direction
of
L
is defined by
E^{c}=E^{cs}\cap E^{cu}
namely

E^{c}=\{x\in X:\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|L^{n}x\|<\infty\}.

This is clearly a linear subspace and
L(E^{c})=E^{c}
. Moreover,

L
is expansive if and only if
E^{c}=\{0\}
In particular, if
L
is expansive, then
E^{c}
is a closed subspace.
The converse is true for generalized hyperbolic operators namely the following result holds.

Theorem 3.12

If
L\in GL(X)
is generalized hyperbolic and
E^{c}
is closed, then
E^{c}=\{0\}
.
In particular,
L
is expansive (hence hyperbolic).

Proof

First we note that

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|L^{n}(z^{c})\|<\infty,\quad\quad\forall z^{c}\in E^{c}.

So, the Banach-Steinhouse theorem implies

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|(L|_{E^{c}})^{n}\|<\infty.

Then, it follows that
L|_{E_{c}}
is equicontinuous.
Now, let
P_{S}:X\to S
and
P_{U}:X\to U
be the projections
associated to the generalized hyperbolic splitting
X=S\oplus U
.
Given
z^{c}\in E^{c}
we write

z^{c}=P_{S}z^{c}+P_{U}z^{c}
.
Since

\|L^{-n}(z^{c}-P_{S}z^{c})\|=\|L^{-n}P_{U}z^{c}\|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
,
we have

\sup_{n\geq 0}\|L^{-n}(z^{c}-P_{S}z^{c})\|<\infty
. Also

\sup_{n\geq 0}\|L^{n}(z^{c}-P_{S}z^{c})\|\leq\sup_{n\geq 0}\|L^{n}z^{c}\|+\sup_{n\geq 0}\|L^{n}P_{S}z^{c}\|<\infty

so

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|L^{n}(z^{c}-P_{S}z^{c})\|<\infty
proving

P_{U}z^{c}=z^{c}-P_{S}z^{c}\in E^{c}.

Likewise,
P_{S}z^{c}\in E^{c}
proving

E^{c}=(E^{c}\cap S)\oplus(E^{c}\cap U).

Clearly
E\cap S
and
E\cap U
are closed subspaces,
L(E^{c}\cap S)\subset E^{c}\cap S
,
L^{-1}(E^{c}\cap U)\subset E^{c}\cap U
,
r(L|_{E^{c}\cap S})<1
and
r(L|_{E^{c}\cap U})<1
.
All together imply that
L|_{E^{c}}\in GL(E^{c})
is generalized hyperbolic. Then, it has the shadowing property by Theorem
3.9
.
This implies that
E^{c}=\{0\}
.
Therefore,
L
is expansive (see the remark before Lemma
2.3
) and the proof follows.
∎

In light of Question
3.10
, it is natural to think that Theorem
3.12
holds for linear homeomorphisms with the shadowing property too.
Indeed, we will prove that this is the case.

Theorem 3.13

If
L\in GL(X)
has the shadowing property and
E^{c}
is closed, then
E^{c}=\{0\}
.
In particular,
L
is expansive (hence hyperbolic).

At first glance we can try to prove this result by repeating the proof of Theorem
3.12
with the
generalized hyperbolic hypothesis replaced by shadowing.
Indeed, everything go well till the assertion that
L|_{E^{c}}
has the shadowing property.
When
L
is generalized hyperbolic this follows because the generalized hyperbolicity of
L
implies that of
L|_{E^{c}}
(and then the shadowing of
L|_{E^{c}}
follows from Theorem
3.9
). In the shadowing case we cannot assert that the shadowing of
L
implies that of
L|_{E^{c}}
. Instead, we will prove that the shadowing property of
L
will induce a related property on
L|_{E^{c}}
. This is the idea behind the next definition.

Definition 3.14

A homeomorphism of a metric space
g:Y\to Y
has the bounded shadowing property if for every
\epsilon>0
there is
\delta>0
such that for every bounded sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
with

d(g(x_{n}),x_{n+1})\leq\delta
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
there is
x\in Y
such that

d(g^{n}(x),x_{n})\leq\epsilon
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

In other words
g
has the bounded shadowing property if only the
bounded
pseudo orbits can be shadowed.
Clearly both the shadowing and the bounded shadowing properties are equivalent on bounded metric spaces and further every homeomorphism with the shadowing property has the bounded shadowing property too.
We don’t know however if these two properties are equivalent on
every
metric space.

The main motivation for this definition comes from the following elementary lemma.
It asserts that the shadowing property of a linear homeomorphisms induces the bounded shadowing property on its central direction. More precisely, we have the following result.

Lemma 3.15

If
X
is a Banach space and
L\in GL(X)
has the shadowing property, then
L|_{E^{c}}
has the bounded shadowing property.

Proof

Let
\epsilon>0
be given and
\delta
be from the shadowing property of
L
.
Let
(x^{c}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a bounded sequence in
E^{c}
such that

\|L(x^{c}_{n})-x^{c}_{n+1}\|\leq\delta
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Then, the shadowing property provides
y\in X
such that

\|L^{n}(y)-x^{c}_{n}\|\leq\epsilon,\quad\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}.

It follows that

\|L^{n}(y)\|\leq\epsilon+\|x^{c}_{n}\|
and so

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|L^{n}(y)\|\leq\epsilon+\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|x^{c}_{n}\|<\infty.

Then,
y\in E^{c}
and so
L|_{E^{c}}
has the bounded shadowing property.
∎

To apply this lemma to prove Theorem
3.13
we need the
following lemma about the invariance of the bounded shadowing property under topological conjugacy.

Lemma 3.16

Let

,

To prove the result we just need to assume that

is a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism

is uniformly continuous with uniformly continuous inverse

).

and let

For this

we take
\delta^{\prime}
from the bounded shadowing property of

and, for this
\delta^{\prime}
, we take
\delta>0
such that

Now let
(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a bounded sequence in
Y

Then,

\|P(H^{-1}(y_{n}))-H^{-1}(y_{n+1})\|\leq\delta^{\prime}

. Since

\|H^{-1}(y_{n})\|\leq\|H^{-1}(y_{n})-H^{-1}(0)\|\leq Lip(H^{-1})\|y_{n}\|+\|H^{-1}(0)\|,\quad\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}

(H^{-1}(y_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a bounded sequence of

.
Then, the bounded shadowing property provides

such that

\|L^{n}(z)-H^{-1}(y_{n})\|\leq\epsilon^{\prime},\quad\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}.

It follows that

\|H(L^{n}(z))-y_{n}\|\leq\epsilon
and so

satisfies

\|(H\circ L\circ H^{-1})(y)-y_{n}\|=\|H(L^{n}(z))-y_{n}\|\leq\epsilon,\quad\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}

proving the result.

It was proved in [Axiom] that an equicontinuous linear homeomorphism of a Banach space has the shadowing property only if
X=\{0\}
. As a direct corollary of the above proof
we obtain the similar result for the bounded shadowing property. More precisely, we have the following result.

Corollary 3.17

An equicontinuous linear homeomorphism of a Banach space
X
has the bounded shadowing property if and only if
X=\{0\}
.

Since every subspace of a finite dimensional Banach space is closed we also obtain the following well-known corollary from Theorem
3.13
.

Corollary 3.18

Every linear homeomorphism with the shadowing property of a finite dimensional Banach space is
expansive (hence hyperbolic).

Let

be a

-map of a Banach space.
We say that a fixed point

is a

if its derivative

is a generalized hyperbolic linear homeomorphism of

For such fixed points we have the following result.

Corollary 4.6
(Generalized Grobman-Hartman Theorem)

For every
C^{1}
diffeomorphism of a Banach space

and every generalized hyperbolic fixed point
p
of
F
there are a homeomorphism

H:X\to X
and a neighborhood
U
of
p
in
X
such that

H\circ F=DF_{p}\circ H
on
U
.

Proof

First we reduce the proof to the case
p=0
.
Consider the diffeomorphism
G(y)=F(y+p)-p
for
y\in X
.
Then,
G(0)=0
and
DG_{0}=DF_{p}
so
0
is a generalized hyperbolic fixed point of
G
.
If the result is valid for
p=0
, there is a homeomorphism

K:X\to X
and an open neighborhood

of

in
X
such that

K\circ G=DG_{0}\circ K\quad\mbox{ on }\quad V.

Take
U=p+V
and

for
x\in X
.
If
x\in U
,
y=x-p\in V
and so

\displaystyle H(F(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K(F(x)-p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K(F(y+p)-p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K(G(y))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle DG_{0}(K(y))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle DF_{0}(H(x))

proving

in
U
.
It remains to prove the result when

Put

.
It follows from Theorem

that

is strong structural stable.
Take
\delta>0
from this property for

and

.
We have

.
Then, there is a neighborhood

From this we can extend

\displaystyle DF_{0}(H(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L(H(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H(L(x)+\beta(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H(L(x)+F(x)-L(x))

\displaystyle H(F(x)),\quad\quad\forall x\in U

Remark about the strong structural stability

We start with the following result.

Theorem 4.8

The following properties are equivalent for every

1.

2.

there is

(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}

We shall prove that

has the shadowing property.
Indeed, we show that

in the statement of Item (2) is a shadowableness constant.
Take a bounded sequence

and define

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|y_{n}\|\leq\gamma\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|z_{n}\|.

Also,

\displaystyle y_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{k}(z_{n-k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}L^{k}(z_{n-k})\right)+z_{n}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{k+1}(z_{n-k-1})+z_{n}

\displaystyle L\left(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{k}(z_{n-k-1})\right)+z_{n}

\displaystyle L(y_{n})+z_{n},\quad\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z},

proving the assertion about

Next we observe that
\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{k}
converges strongly and so

.
Then,

L^{m}P_{B}(x)=P_{A}L^{m}P_{B}(x)+P_{B}L^{m}P_{B}(x)

P_{B}(x)=L^{-m}P_{A}L^{m}P_{B}(x)+L^{-m}P_{B}L^{m}P_{B}(x).

Since
P_{B}(x),L^{-m}P_{B}L^{m}P_{B}\in B
, we have

L^{-m}P_{A}L^{m}P_{B}(x)\in B.

But also

P_{A}L^{m}P_{B}(x)\in A

L^{-m}P_{A}L^{m}P_{B}(x)\in L^{-m}(A)

thus

L^{-m}P_{A}L^{m}P_{B}(x)\in B\cap L^{-m}A,\quad\quad\forall m\geq 0.

(23)

Again

x=P_{A}(x)+P_{B}(x)
so
L^{m}x=L^{m}P_{A}(x)+L^{m}P_{B}(x)
.
Since
x\in H
and
L^{m}|_{A}\to 0
strongly, we get

\lim_{m\to\infty}L^{m}P_{B}(x)=\lim_{m\to 0}(L^{m}x-L^{m}P_{A}(x))=0.

Again we observe that
L^{-m}|_{B}\to 0
strongly so
\sup_{n\geq 0}\|L^{-m}|_{B}\|<\infty
by the Banach-Steinhouse theorem thus

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\|P_{B}(x)-L^{-m}P_{A}L^{m}P_{B}(x)\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\|L^{-m}P_{B}L^{m}P_{B}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\sup_{m\geq 0}\|L^{-m}|_{B}\|)\|P_{B}\|\lim_{m\to\infty}\|L^{m}P_{B}(x)\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

Then, (
23

P_{B}(x)\in\overline{\left(\bigcup_{m\geq 0}(B\cap L^{-m}(A))\right)}\subset\overline{\hat{H}}.

(24)

Finally,
\hat{H}
is a subspace so
\overline{\hat{H}}
also is and so
\overline{\hat{H}}+\overline{\hat{H}}=\overline{\hat{H}}
.
Then, (
22
) and (
24
) imply

completing the proof.
∎
