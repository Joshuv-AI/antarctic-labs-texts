Cardiometabolic medicine in China: Burden, challenges, and strategic priorities
Authors: Rui-Xiang Zeng, Sha Li, Xiao Wang, Ke-Fei Dou, Jian-Jun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of cardiometabolic medicine (CMM) reflects a shift in cardiovascular disease (CVD) management from organ-based care toward greater cross-system integration. CMM integrates cardiovascular, endocrine, renal, and public health domains to address the bidirectional interplay between metabolic dysfunction and CVD. In this review, we trace the conceptual evolution from metabolic syndrome to cardiometabolic disease (CMD), synthesize evidence for the biological integration of cardiovascular, metabolic, and renal disease, individual cardiometabolic therapies, and emerging integrated care models, and discuss the epidemiological transition and implementation barriers in China, including service fragmentation, specialty silos, and limited patient engagement. Strategic priorities include a coordinated CMM framework, multidisciplinary care, standardized training, primary-tertiary care linkage, and digital implementation, together with measurable indicators for implementation and outcomes. China's experience may offer relevant lessons for other Asian settings navigating similar transitions, suggesting that CMM may be viewed not simply as a new organizational construct, but as a potential realignment of chronic disease management toward integrated, patient-centered, and life-course cardiovascular health.

Introduction

The development of cardiometabolic medicine (CMM) reflects a broader shift in the management of cardiovascular diseases (CVD), moving from organ-based specialization toward system-level integration. Over the past century, the subdivision of internal medicine into distinct specialties has enabled major advances in disease-specific care, but has also fragmented the understanding of complex chronic diseases, particularly in the context of multimorbidity [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. As our understanding of disease pathophysiology has deepened, the limitations of isolated specialty-based care have become increasingly apparent. The traditional specialty-centric approach fails to address the complex multi-system crosstalk underlying multiple chronic diseases, particularly the intimate association between CVD and metabolic disorders, thus no longer meeting the demands of modern complex clinical practice [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Against this background, CMM provides an integrated clinical framework that views the heart as a central target organ dynamically modulated by systemic metabolic homeostasis, enabling a heart-centric, multisystem approach to disease management. It underscores the fundamental pathophysiological link between CVD and metabolic disorders and supports a continuum of care extending from specialty-specific diagnosis and treatment to cross-disciplinary collaboration and ultimately to system-level integration. In China, CMM should be viewed not merely as a new subspecialty, but as a systems-level response to the growing burden, complexity, and fragmentation of cardiometabolic disease management [6,7]. Accordingly, this review examines the biological and therapeutic evidence underpinning CMM, evaluates the evidence for integrated or multidisciplinary cardiometabolic care as a healthcare-delivery model and the remaining evidence gaps, explores how China's epidemiological burden and healthcare-system characteristics shape CMM implementation, and distinguishes implementation priorities supported by current evidence from proposals requiring prospective evaluation.

Literature selection

This article was conducted as a narrative review rather than a systematic review. Relevant literature was identified through searches of PubMed, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Wanfang Data from database inception to 30 September 2026, supplemented by references from selected articles. Searches combined terms related to cardiometabolic medicine, metabolic syndrome, cardiometabolic disease, cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic health, and cardiometabolic risk factors with terms such as integrated care, multidisciplinary care, implementation, and China/Chinese. No formal language restriction was applied. Priority was given to guidelines, scientific statements, expert consensus documents, randomized trials, meta-analyses, nationally representative epidemiological studies, registries, and studies of integrated-care models. China-specific policy documents and professional-society initiatives were additionally identified through targeted searches of relevant official government and professional-organization websites. Publications were selected according to relevance and methodological quality, while clearly irrelevant, duplicate, or insufficiently informative reports were excluded. In synthesizing the literature, we distinguished evidence for biological integration, the efficacy of individual therapies, the effectiveness of integrated care delivery models, and health-system implementation. Where direct evidence for CMM-specific care models was limited, recommendations were framed as evidence-informed proposals rather than established standards.

From metabolic syndrome to cardiometabolic disease

The conceptualization of cardiometabolic disease (CMD) has evolved through a progressive transition, from the initial separation of cardiovascular and metabolic disorders to the recognition of their shared and integrated pathophysiological basis [2]. In the early stages of modern medicine, CVD and metabolic diseases were classified as distinct entities within different specialties, reflecting an organ-based framework of disease understanding. This separation began to be challenged in the mid-20th century, when the Framingham Heart Study provided important systematic epidemiological evidence linking metabolic abnormalities to increased cardiovascular risk. Early descriptions of the clustering of metabolic abnormalities emerged in the 1960s, with obesity, dyslipidemia, and hyperglycemia frequently occurring within the same individuals [8]. In 1988, Reaven proposed “syndrome X″ and identified insulin resistance as a unifying pathophysiological mechanism linking glucose intolerance, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and cardiovascular risk [9]. The World Health Organization subsequently introduced a formal diagnostic framework for metabolic syndrome (MetS) in 1998, representing an important step toward its clinical standardization [10]. Subsequent definitions from the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III and the International Diabetes Federation further promoted the clinical use and international harmonization of MetS criteria [11,12]. However, despite these advances, the MetS framework remained largely descriptive and was not fully incorporated into mainstream cardiovascular guidelines for decades, which limited its clinical impact [13]. By the early 21st century, a more mechanistic perspective had emerged, emphasizing abdominal obesity as a marker of dysfunctional adipose tissue and linking adiposity, chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and cardiovascular risk [14]. Building on these developments, the term CMD emerged as a more comprehensive construct in the medical literature. Unlike earlier frameworks that focused primarily on risk factor clustering, CMD emphasizes the continuum of cardiovascular outcomes driven by systemic metabolic dysfunction. In this sense, the evolution from MetS to CMD reflects a conceptual shift from descriptive clustering to integrated pathophysiological understanding [2]. More recently, the cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) health framework has further broadened this integrated perspective by explicitly incorporating kidney dysfunction into the continuum linking metabolic abnormalities and CVD [15]. The 2026 CKM guideline further operationalized this framework through CKM staging, PREVENT-based risk assessment, systematic evaluation of metabolic and kidney risk factors, and interdisciplinary care coordination [16]. Although CMD and cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome describe overlapping biological and clinical continua, CMM serves a different but complementary function. Whereas the CKM guideline primarily focuses on disease staging, risk assessment, prevention, and evidence-based treatment, CMM additionally emphasizes healthcare-delivery organization, multidisciplinary coordination, referral pathways, workforce development, digital infrastructure, and implementation evaluation. Thus, the CKM framework and staging system should not be viewed as replacing CMM, but rather as reinforcing the need for a transdisciplinary model capable of translating integrated disease concepts into clinical practice [15,16].

Evidence base and evidence gaps for CMM

Biological integration of cardiovascular, metabolic, and renal disease

The biological rationale for CMM is supported by converging evidence that cardiovascular, metabolic, and renal disorders are interconnected through shared and bidirectional pathophysiological pathways. Dysfunctional adiposity, insulin resistance, chronic low-grade inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and kidney disease interact to promote cardiovascular injury and disease progression [14,15]. The cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic framework further formalizes this continuum by integrating metabolic risk factors, chronic kidney disease, and subclinical or clinical CVD within a staged disease model [15]. These observations provide a biological basis for considering cardiometabolic disorders within an integrated framework rather than as isolated comorbidities. However, biological integration alone does not establish that a specific integrated CMM healthcare-delivery model improves clinical outcomes.

Evidence from individual pharmacological interventions

At the therapeutic level, robust evidence demonstrates that modification of individual cardiometabolic pathways can reduce cardiovascular and renal risk. One of the best-established examples is the causal role of dyslipidemia in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD). Large-scale meta-analyses of randomized trials have shown that lowering low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) reduces major vascular events in a broadly proportional manner, establishing lipid-lowering therapy as a cornerstone of ASCVD prevention and management [17,18]. Over recent decades, improved control of major risk factors and evidence-based therapies have contributed substantially to declines in cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, although these gains have become increasingly challenged by the rising prevalence of obesity, diabetes, and hypertension [19,20].

More recently, cardiovascular outcome trials of novel glucose-lowering agents, including sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs), have demonstrated that targeting metabolic pathways can confer cardiovascular and renal benefits beyond glycemic control. SGLT-2 inhibitors reduce hospitalization for heart failure and the progression of kidney disease, with benefits extending beyond glucose lowering [21,22]. GLP-1 RAs reduce major adverse cardiovascular events in patients with type 2 diabetes and high cardiovascular risk, and semaglutide further reduced cardiovascular events in patients with overweight or obesity and established CVD but without diabetes in the SELECT trial [23,24]. Together, these trials establish the efficacy of important therapeutic components of cardiometabolic management. However, they evaluate individual pharmacological interventions within clinical-trial settings and do not test whether multidisciplinary CMM care delivery is superior to conventional specialty-based care.

The expanding therapeutic landscape also supports a broader cardiometabolic framework. Obesity is increasingly recognized as a modifiable disease target rather than merely a risk marker, while chronic kidney disease has emerged as both a major cardiovascular risk amplifier and a therapeutic target [15,25]. Contemporary cardiometabolic care therefore extends beyond isolated control of lipids, glucose, blood pressure, or body weight, and instead prioritizes treatment selection according to the patient's combined atherosclerotic, heart failure, renal, and metabolic risk profile [[21], [22], [23], [24], [25]].

Evidence for integrated cardiometabolic care delivery

In contrast to the extensive evidence for individual therapies, direct evidence evaluating CMM as an integrated healthcare-delivery model remains comparatively limited but is expanding. A 2024 scoping review identified 18 publications evaluating organizational initiatives for cardiometabolic care. Cardiometabolic and multidisciplinary services were associated with improvements in intermediate outcomes, including body weight, waist circumference, blood pressure, HbA1c, and cholesterol levels, as well as greater adherence to recommended care processes and guideline-directed therapies. However, the interventions varied substantially in patient populations, staffing, organizational structure, and outcome definitions, limiting direct comparisons and generalizability [26].

Several observational studies provide more direct evidence for dedicated cardiometabolic clinics. In a propensity-matched evaluation of the Saint Luke's Cardiometabolic Center of Excellence, 129 patients with type 2 diabetes and CVD managed in the cardiometabolic clinic were compared with 387 patients receiving conventional care. Guideline-directed medical therapy was more frequently achieved in the cardiometabolic clinic, including substantially greater use of SGLT-2 inhibitors and/or GLP-1 receptor agonists [27]. A separate team-based program, the Center for Integrated and Novel Approaches in Vascular-Metabolic Disease (CINEMA), also reported increased uptake of evidence-based therapies and improvements in HbA1c, LDL-C, and body mass index among patients with type 2 diabetes at high cardiovascular risk [28].

A subsequent retrospective study of 400 patients with type 2 diabetes and ASCVD treated in a dedicated cardiometabolic clinic similarly reported increased use of GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT-2 inhibitors and greater achievement of LDL-C treatment targets [29]. More recently, a prospective registry of 2223 patients across nine Cardiometabolic Center Alliance sites found improvements in body weight, blood pressure, LDL-C, HbA1c, insulin requirements, and use of guideline-directed therapies over a median follow-up of 6.8 months [30]. These findings support the feasibility of coordinated CMM care and suggest potential improvements in implementation and intermediate risk factors. However, the available CMM-specific studies are predominantly observational, follow-up is relatively short, and effects on myocardial infarction, stroke, cardiovascular death, kidney failure, and other long-term clinical outcomes have not yet been established.

Evidence from broader integrated-care models provides additional, although indirect, support. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of 54 randomized trials involving 12,976 participants with type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, chronic respiratory disease, or combinations thereof found improvements in several outcomes, including adverse events, quality of life, self-management, and blood pressure control, although evidence for some outcomes remained uncertain [31]. In the Beacon model of integrated primary-secondary diabetes care, a pragmatic randomized trial showed that community-based care led by general practitioners with advanced diabetes expertise and supported by an endocrinologist and diabetes educator was non-inferior to hospital specialist care for HbA1c control and was associated with greater patient satisfaction, while blood pressure, lipid levels, and quality of life were similar between groups [32]. A subsequent costing analysis estimated a mean health-system saving of A$365 per patient course of treatment, although economic findings were sensitive to local service volume and operational arrangements [33]. These studies suggest that coordinated chronic-disease care can be clinically feasible and, in selected settings, economically favorable, but they were not designed specifically as CMM programs and therefore provide indirect rather than definitive evidence for CMM.

Overall, the evidence hierarchy for CMM remains asymmetric. Biological integration and the efficacy of individual cardiometabolic therapies are supported by substantial evidence, whereas evidence for CMM as a distinct integrated care-delivery model is newer and relies largely on observational studies, heterogeneous clinic models, and surrogate or intermediate outcomes. Formal economic evaluations of dedicated cardiometabolic clinics remain limited, and evidence on scalability across different healthcare systems is still sparse [34]. Future studies should prospectively compare integrated CMM models with usual care using standardized measures of guideline implementation, cardiometabolic risk-factor control, cardiovascular and renal events, patient-reported outcomes, healthcare utilization, cost-effectiveness, and health equity. These evidence gaps are particularly relevant when considering adaptation of CMM to the Chinese healthcare system.

Cardiometabolic burden and implementation gap in China

In China, rapid economic development and profound societal transformations over recent decades have reshaped population structure and lifestyle patterns, resulting in a sustained rise in cardiometabolic risk factors. More importantly, this epidemiological expansion has been accompanied by persistently low rates of disease awareness, treatment initiation, and effective control. During the same period, the cardiovascular disease burden in China has risen substantially [7]. Contemporary real-world data further demonstrate that the implementation gap extends beyond disease prevalence. In the iCaReMe China registry, which enrolled 9000 patients with type 2 diabetes from 60 hospitals in 2023-2024, only 29.0% achieved HbA1c <7.0%, 27.8% achieved blood pressure <130/80 mmHg, and 43.0% had LDL-C <2.6 mmol/L [35]. These findings indicate that control of multiple major cardiometabolic risk factors remains suboptimal even among patients already engaged with hospital-based care. This growing burden is particularly evident in the major modifiable risk factors driving ASCVD. National data indicate that the prevalence of overweight among Chinese adults increased from 16.4% in 1992 to 34.3% in 2015-2019, while obesity increased from 3.6% to 16.4% during the same period. Abdominal obesity also rose substantially, from 20.2% in 1993 to 35.4%-46.9% in 2015-2017. Of particular concern, the rise in obesity is no longer confined to adults. Childhood and adolescent obesity have also increased substantially in China, suggesting that cardiometabolic risk accumulation may begin decades before the onset of clinically overt CVD [36]. Diabetes prevalence has increased more than tenfold, from 0.67% in 1980 to 10.9% in 2013 and 12.4% in 2018, accompanied by a high prevalence of prediabetes, reaching 38.1% in 2018 [7,37]. Dyslipidemia remains highly prevalent, affecting approximately one third to nearly half of middle-aged and older adults in recent surveys, while hypertension increased from approximately 15.3%-15.7% in 1991 to 23.2%-25.6% in 2012-2015, with persistently low rates of effective control [7].

China-specific estimates from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2023 analysis further illustrate the magnitude of the cardiovascular burden in China. In 2023, China had approximately 135.8 million prevalent CVD cases, 4.5 million CVD deaths, and 89.3 million CVD disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [38]. This burden is characterized by three defining features: a very large absolute number of affected individuals, rapid increases in major cardiometabolic risk factors, and a substantial gap between disease prevalence and effective management [7,38]. Although recent Chinese guidelines have increasingly incorporated metabolic factors into ASCVD risk assessment and emphasized lifetime risk assessment, their implementation in routine clinical practice remains limited [7,39]. This gap is not solely a matter of guideline dissemination, but reflects deeper structural challenges within the healthcare system, including fragmentation of care, insufficient integration across specialties, and disparities in health literacy. As a result, the translation of evidence-based cardiometabolic strategies into real-world practice remains a major challenge in China [39]. In this sense, China provides a large-scale real-world setting in which to examine the feasibility and scalability of the CMM framework, while also underscoring the need for a coordinated strategy that links disciplinary development, clinical integration, and population-level prevention. Within this epidemiological context, the development of CMM in China should be understood not merely as the introduction of a new subspecialty, but as a necessary response to a changing disease landscape.

Systemic barriers and emerging implementation models in China

China-specific evidence indicates that fragmentation occurs both across levels of care and between specialties. Reviews of the Chinese primary healthcare system have identified insufficient coordination between primary care and hospitals, separation between clinical and public-health services, uneven workforce capacity, and fragmented health-information systems as persistent barriers to chronic disease management [40,41]. A multicenter Chinese registry further demonstrated specialty-specific treatment gaps among patients with coexisting hypertension and diabetes: 32.9% of patients treated for hypertension in cardiology and 58.9% of patients treated for diabetes in endocrinology had both conditions, yet treatment patterns differed substantially between the two specialties [42]. These findings provide empirical support for the concern that organ- or specialty-based care may result in incomplete management of overlapping cardiometabolic risks.

China has also developed several organizational models that may inform future CMM implementation. One prominent example is the Metabolic Management Center (MMC) program, launched by the Chinese Medical Doctor Association in 2016 as a standardized, one-stop model for the management of diabetes and related metabolic disorders [43]. In an early comparative study, MMC-based care was associated with greater improvement in HbA1c and selected lipid measures, higher patient satisfaction, and more favorable projected long-term outcomes than conventional management [44]. More recently, a propensity-matched study of 1114 Chinese adults with type 2 diabetes found that participation in the MMC program was associated with greater improvements in fasting glucose, diastolic blood pressure, body mass index, and estimated 10-year cardiovascular risk than usual primary care [45]. However, the MMC model remains primarily diabetes- and metabolism-centered, and its existing evidence base is largely observational. It should therefore be regarded as an important Chinese implementation platform relevant to CMM, rather than as a validated national CMM model.

Professional-society coordination has also begun to emerge. In 2021, the Metabolic Cardiovascular Disease Group of the Chinese Society of Cardiology issued an expert consensus on comprehensive cardiovascular-risk management in patients with cardiovascular disease and abnormal glucose metabolism [46]. In 2024, the Endocrinology and Metabolism Physician Branch of the Chinese Medical Doctor Association and the National Society of Cardiometabolic Medicine jointly published a consensus on lipid management in patients with diabetes [47]. These initiatives demonstrate increasing cross-specialty academic coordination. However, published evidence describing standardized CMM-specific curricula, fellowships, competency frameworks, or certification pathways in China remains sparse. More broadly, deficiencies in the training and capacity of primary healthcare professionals have been repeatedly identified as barriers to high-quality chronic disease management [41]. Thus, professional organization has advanced more rapidly than formalized CMM workforce development.

Patient-level barriers are similarly heterogeneous. National assessments indicate that health literacy in China improved from 6.48% in 2008 to 23.15% in 2020, but substantial geographic, urban-rural, age, and educational disparities persisted [48]. Studies of diabetes self-management in Chinese adults further suggest that self-efficacy, health beliefs, social support, provider-patient communication, and access to chronic-disease resources influence engagement in long-term care [49]. These findings suggest that patient engagement should not be treated simply as an individual behavioral deficit, but as an outcome shaped by health literacy, communication, social context, and healthcare-system support.

Digital infrastructure represents both a limitation and an opportunity. Earlier assessments of Chinese primary care identified fragmented health-information systems and limited availability of routinely useable clinical data [40]. However, several large implementation studies demonstrate that digital platforms can support more integrated chronic-disease management. In the ROADMAP cluster-randomized trial, 19,601 patients with type 2 diabetes from 864 communities across 25 provinces were enrolled; the mHealth-enabled hierarchical-care intervention increased HbA1c control by 7.0 percentage points and produced a modest improvement in combined glycaemic, blood-pressure, and LDL-C target attainment at 12 months [50]. The SMARTDiabetes cluster-randomized trial in 80 communities subsequently showed that a combined patient self-management application, family health promoters, and primary-care clinical decision support improved achievement of multiple risk-factor targets at 24 months, although benefits differed between rural and urban settings [51]. These studies support the feasibility of digitally enabled integrated care in China, but they remain disease-specific and do not establish that digital infrastructure alone can deliver comprehensive CMM. More recently, the CKM-CARE initiative has begun leveraging regional electronic health records from more than 550,000 adults in Weinan to establish longitudinal CKM surveillance, illustrating the potential of routinely collected data for future CMM evaluation [52].

Patient-centered reorientation of cardiometabolic care

From a clinical perspective, CMD management is increasingly shifting toward a patient-centered model. Concepts for CVD prevention have evolved from managing myocardial ischemia in established disease to preventing atherosclerotic plaque formation, and further to modulating metabolic homeostasis at earlier stages of disease development [53,54]. This trajectory reflects a forward shift in the focus of cardiovascular prevention and treatment, from late-stage disease intervention to earlier risk identification and long-term health maintenance. This paradigm shift requires clinicians to move beyond passive, encounter-based care and toward proactive identification of unmet cardiometabolic needs in both clinical and community settings. In this model, clinicians are not only providers of disease-specific treatment, but also partners in risk communication, lifestyle guidance, shared decision-making, and long-term disease management. At the same time, individuals should be supported to take an active role in maintaining cardiometabolic health, since lifestyle modification remains the foundation of CMD prevention and management, even in the era of increasingly effective pharmacological and interventional therapies.

In China, this patient-centered reorientation also requires attention to health-system and patient-level factors. Chinese evidence suggests that effective self-management depends not only on patient knowledge, but also on self-efficacy, provider-patient communication, social support, and access to chronic-disease resources [48,49]. In contemporary cardiometabolic care, however, clinicians should be regarded as partners in long-term risk assessment, communication, and care planning, providing evidence-based expertise to support shared decision-making. Meanwhile, many clinicians work within high-intensity, hospital-centered service systems, with limited time and institutional support for community engagement, preventive counseling, and longitudinal risk management. These constraints may limit the transition from disease-centered treatment to health-centered care. This issue is particularly relevant in an era of rapid technological advancement in cardiology. As Tona recently cautioned, clinical practice should continue to ask not only what can be done, but also what should be done for patients [55]. For CMM, this reminder is especially important. Its core value lies not in adding another subspecialty to an already fragmented system, but in reorienting cardiovascular care toward integrated, patient-centered, and transdisciplinary prevention and management. Taken together, these epidemiological, institutional, and cultural challenges indicate that the advancement of CMM in China requires a coordinated strategy rather than isolated clinical initiatives.

Strategic priorities for advancing CMM in China

To address these challenges, we propose a staged CMM implementation strategy that links disciplinary development, institutional integration, guideline standardization, professional training, and population-level prevention, as summarized in Table 1 and Fig. 3. These priorities should be interpreted as evidence-informed implementation proposals, informed by contemporary cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic frameworks, multidisciplinary-care concepts, and the broader integrated-care literature [15,31,56], rather than as components of a validated national CMM model. Their feasibility, effectiveness, and cost-effectiveness require prospective evaluation in Chinese healthcare settings. Existing academic committees, clinical research centers, and national chronic disease programmes provide an important foundation for CMM development in China, but these resources remain insufficiently integrated into a unified implementation framework. First, a national framework for CMM should be established to define its scope, core competencies, clinical pathways, and outcome indicators. Proposed framework for CMM implementation and evaluation in China

China-specific implementation challenges include a substantial cardiometabolic burden, gaps in risk-factor control, fragmented care delivery, and the still-emerging development of integrated implementation platforms. The proposed framework emphasizes strategic planning and governance, care integration and referral, standardized pathways, workforce development and training, and digital implementation. Evaluation should encompass risk assessment and risk-factor control, cardiovascular and renal outcomes, care coordination and implementation, patient-reported outcomes, healthcare utilization and costs, and health equity. The framework represents an evidence-informed implementation proposal rather than an established or validated national CMM model.

Second, institutional integration should be promoted through dedicated cardiometabolic clinics, multidisciplinary teams, and shared care pathways. These models should focus on early identification and integrated management of major ASCVD-related risk factors, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, obesity, chronic kidney disease, and unhealthy lifestyle patterns. Professional societies or dedicated working groups may serve as coordinating platforms for academic development, clinical training, guideline formulation, quality control, and implementation research in CMM.

Third, China needs a structured training system for CMM professionals. Standardized curricula, fellowship programs, continuing medical education, and competency-based certification should be developed to cultivate clinicians capable of managing cardiometabolic risk across the continuum of cardiometabolic care. Training should emphasize not only pharmacological and interventional therapies, but also risk prediction, lifestyle intervention, long-term disease management, shared decision-making, and community-based prevention.

Finally, guideline development and implementation science should become central components of CMM practice in China. National guidelines or expert consensus documents are needed to define risk stratification algorithms, treatment pathways, follow-up models, and quality indicators for CMD management. Digital health platforms and real-world data systems may further support population risk screening, patient follow-up, adherence monitoring, and outcome evaluation. Operationally, CMM could be implemented through a tiered model linking primary care with tertiary cardiometabolic centers. Primary care would provide initial cardiometabolic risk assessment, routine control of major risk factors, adherence support, and longitudinal follow-up, with referral of patients with complex multimorbidity or inadequate risk-factor control to tertiary multidisciplinary teams. Tertiary centers would provide integrated specialist assessment and treatment, support complex decision-making, and serve as regional hubs for referral, training, and quality improvement. Professional societies could develop competency standards, care pathways, and quality indicators, whereas public-health authorities could support population-level prevention, surveillance, digital infrastructure, and equitable access to care.

Implementation should be evaluated across multiple domains rather than by clinical outcomes alone. Suggested indicators include the proportion of eligible patients receiving integrated cardiovascular-metabolic-renal risk assessment, LDL-C, blood-pressure and HbA1c target attainment, use of guideline-directed therapies in eligible patients, referral completion, treatment adherence, retention in longitudinal follow-up, cardiovascular and renal events, patient-reported outcomes, healthcare utilization and costs, and disparities in access and outcomes across geographic and socioeconomic groups. Implementation outcomes such as acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, feasibility, fidelity, implementation cost, penetration, and sustainability should also be considered [57].

Although CMM has the potential to improve integrated prevention and management, its implementation should avoid creating another isolated specialty silo. The goal of CMM should not be to replace existing cardiology, endocrinology, nephrology, rehabilitation, or primary care services, but rather to provide a coordinating framework that connects them through shared pathways, common risk assessment, and longitudinal follow-up. In some settings, well-organized multidisciplinary team models may achieve many of the practical goals of CMM without requiring the immediate establishment of a formal subspecialty. Therefore, CMM implementation should be staged and adapted to local healthcare capacity. Resource-limited and primary-care settings may initially prioritize simplified risk assessment, referral pathways, digital follow-up, and targeted continuing education, whereas tertiary centers can serve as regional hubs for complex cardiometabolic care, workforce training, and quality improvement.

Implications for Asia

The implementation of CMM in China may also provide broader insights for other Asian countries facing similar epidemiological and health-system transitions. Across Asia, rapid urbanization, population ageing, dietary changes, physical inactivity, and increasing obesity have accelerated the growth of cardiometabolic risk [58]. In addition, many Asian populations develop diabetes, abdominal obesity, and ASCVD risk at relatively lower BMI levels, underscoring the need for region-specific risk assessment and prevention strategies [58]. China's experience highlights both the opportunities and challenges of translating integrated cardiometabolic concepts into real-world practice within a large, heterogeneous healthcare system. Its emerging efforts in academic coordination, multidisciplinary care, primary care linkage, digital implementation, and life-course prevention may offer a practical reference for other Asian settings. However, CMM models should not be uniformly applied across countries. Their implementation should be adapted to local disease patterns, healthcare infrastructure, reimbursement systems, workforce capacity, and cultural expectations regarding prevention and self-management.

Conclusion

CMM embodies a patient-centered and forward-looking model for CVD prevention and control, aligned with broader public health priorities in China, particularly chronic disease prevention and population health promotion. Its implementation requires the integration of cross-disciplinary medical resources and the coordination of multiple stakeholders, including clinicians, public health professionals, policymakers, and patients, to support the development of a more cohesive and efficient cardiometabolic care system. In this context, further advancement of CMM may provide a framework for testing more integrated approaches to chronic cardiometabolic disease management in China and generate implementation evidence relevant to other healthcare systems. Rather than representing a purely localized framework, its evolution may contribute to a broader understanding of how CMM can be operationalized across diverse healthcare systems worldwide. However, the strength of evidence differs across components of CMM. Biological evidence supporting cardiometabolic integration and therapeutic evidence for individual interventions are substantial, whereas direct evidence that CMM-specific care-delivery models improve long-term cardiovascular and renal outcomes, cost-effectiveness, or health equity remains limited. These outcomes should therefore be regarded as priorities for prospective evaluation rather than assumed benefits of CMM implementation. CMM may therefore be viewed as a potential systems-level response to the growing complexity of chronic cardiometabolic disease rather than simply as a new organizational construct.
