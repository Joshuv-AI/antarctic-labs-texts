Diagnostic Determinants of Thyroid Dysfunction in Southern Saudi Arabia: Identifying High-Risk Populations Through Integrated Risk Factor Analysis
Authors: Jalal H Abu Halimah, Mohammad A Jareebi, Zakaria Y Shubayli, Mohammad Y Shubayli, Mohammad A Zaeri, Khalid A Bakri, Ahmed Y Sabi, Abdulaziz F Zaylaee, Zuhair A Almunawwar, Eyad Z Omar, Rakan M Hakami, Abdullah A Khudier, Abdulaziz A Aqdi, Rayim A Oraybi, Khalid O Alyahyawi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesEarly and accurate diagnosis of thyroid disorders remains challenging in resource-variable settings, with limited diagnostic algorithms tailored to regional populations. Understanding the constellation of risk factors for thyroid disease can enhance diagnostic accuracy and inform targeted screening strategies.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional diagnostic study among 1,250 adults (≥18 years) in southern Saudi Arabia (May 2024) to identify diagnostic determinants of thyroid dysfunction. Participants completed structured assessments of demographic profiles, anthropometric measurements, family and medical histories, lifestyle factors, and current thyroid status. Statistical analyses included chi-square tests, Fisher’s exact tests, and independent-samples t-tests to establish diagnostic associations.ResultsSelf-reported physician-diagnosed thyroid disorders were reported by 10% of participants, with hypothyroidism predominating (7%). Diagnostic determinants showed distinct clustering patterns: hypothyroidism demonstrated the strongest associations with female sex (OR-equivalent difference: 81% vs 41%, p < 0.001), elevated BMI (30 ± 6.6 vs 26 ± 7.7 kg/m2, p < 0.001), advanced age (40 ± 11 vs 34 ± 13 years, p < 0.001), family history (p < 0.001), and prior radiation exposure (p < 0.001). Hyperthyroidism exhibited different diagnostic signatures, notably physical inactivity and hereditary factors (p < 0.001). One-quarter of diagnosed individuals presented with multiple concurrent thyroid pathologies, complicating diagnostic interpretation.ConclusionThis study identifies patterns of self-reported thyroid dysfunction and associated factors that may help guide future screening strategies integrating demographic, anthropometric, and clinical determinants to identify high-risk populations requiring thyroid function testing. The findings support development of risk-stratified diagnostic algorithms and may improve early detection rates in comparable settings.

Introduction

Thyroid disorders encompass a spectrum of conditions affecting thyroid gland structure and function, leading to disruptions in the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis and alterations in essential hormones triiodothyronine (T3) and thyroxine (T4).1,2 These conditions, including hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, thyroiditis, goiter, and thyroid cancer, represent significant public health concerns due to their high prevalence and potential for serious cardiovascular, metabolic, and neuropsychiatric complications if left untreated.3,4 The diagnosis of thyroid disorders is based on a combination of clinical assessment, biochemical testing, and imaging. Serum thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) is the recommended first-line screening test, followed by free thyroxine (FT4) and, when indicated, free triiodothyronine (FT3) to distinguish overt from subclinical thyroid dysfunction.2

Global epidemiological studies demonstrate substantial geographic variation in thyroid disorder prevalence. A national Chinese survey reported an overall adult hypothyroidism rate of 13.95%, particularly affecting women and older individuals,5 while Australian community-based studies found approximately 13% prevalence of thyroid dysfunction.6 Women are disproportionately affected, with thyroid dysfunction rates up to ten times higher than men, attributed to hormonal, genetic, and immunological factors.4 The Middle East region faces a particularly substantial burden, with a meta-analysis estimating that approximately 19% of adults are affected, rising from 15% in 2000 to over 30% by 2022.7 Saudi Arabia reports among the highest rates globally.7 Thyroid cancer ranks as the second most common cancer among Saudi women after breast cancer, with an age-standardized incidence rate of 11.3 per 100,000 and dramatic increases of 15-fold in women and 22-fold in men over the past three decades.8 Despite this substantial burden, comprehensive epidemiological data remain limited in Saudi Arabia. Local studies show considerable regional variation—research in Al-Baha found 15–16% prevalence9—but most studies focus on specific regions or populations. Well-established risk factors include female sex, older age, family history, autoimmune thyroiditis, and iodine intake patterns.4,10 Modifiable factors such as obesity have emerged as important contributors, with large Asian cohort studies demonstrating significant associations between elevated BMI and thyroid cancer risk.11 Obesity is heavily associated with numerous metabolic and endocrine comorbidities, including insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia.12 The profound impact of obesity on metabolic regulation is further highlighted following interventions like bariatric metabolic surgery, which, while effective for managing severe obesity, induces significant biochemical changes such as altered absorption of essential nutrients like vitamin B12 and folate. These metabolic shifts can subsequently disrupt homocysteine metabolism, demonstrating the intricate biochemical disturbances and potential cardiovascular risks that can accompany obesity-related endocrine challenges.13 Public awareness remains limited, with recent studies revealing considerable gaps in understanding thyroid disease risk factors and lifestyle associations.14 The Saudi Cancer Registry reported 1,045 new thyroid cancer cases among women in 2018, accounting for 8.4% of all female cancers, with highest incidence in the Eastern and Riyadh regions.15

The southern region of Saudi Arabia presents a vital setting for studying thyroid diseases due to its distinctive geographic location, demographic composition, and environmental factors. This study aimed to determine thyroid disorder prevalence among adults in southern Saudi Arabia and identify key demographic, lifestyle, and clinical risk factors. By examining multiple conditions simultaneously and assessing comprehensive risk factors, we sought to provide evidence guiding targeted public health interventions and clinical screening protocols in the region.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Sample Size

This cross-sectional study utilized convenience sampling among Saudi adults aged 18 years and above residing in southern Saudi Arabia. Participants were recruited through online dissemination of the survey questionnaire via social media platforms and community networks, targeting eligible individuals who voluntarily agreed to participate.

Data Collection Tools

The data collection instrument was systematically developed to capture essential variables related to thyroid disorders and associated factors. A literature review was first conducted to identify established risk factors for thyroid disease, selecting key variables based on epidemiological evidence and clinical relevance.4,11 The initial instrument was then reviewed by domain experts in endocrinology, general surgery, and public health to assess item clarity, content validity, and cultural appropriateness for the Saudi population, with modifications made accordingly. Thyroid status was determined exclusively based on participant self-report of prior physician diagnosis, and no biochemical validation (TSH, FT3, FT4) or medical record verification was performed.

The final questionnaire comprised three main sections: (1) Demographic characteristics including age, sex, nationality, marital status, educational attainment, employment status, and geographic residence (urban vs rural); (2) Medical and family history including physician-diagnosed thyroid conditions (hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, thyroid nodules, thyroid cancer), family history of thyroid disease in first-degree relatives, history of radiation exposure to the neck, chest, or head, use of thyroid-affecting medications (lithium, amiodarone, interferon), comorbid endocrine conditions (adrenal or pituitary disorders), history of thyroid surgery, and for female participants, reproductive history including pregnancy and postpartum thyroiditis; and (3) Lifestyle and anthropometric factors including self-reported height and weight for body mass index (BMI) calculation, smoking status (never, current, former), and physical activity patterns assessed using standardized questions about frequency and intensity of exercise.

Physical activity was categorized as: (1) moderate to vigorous physical activity for at least 30 minutes, five times per week; (2) moderate to vigorous physical activity for less than 30 minutes, five times per week; or (3) no regular physical activity during the week. All questions used clear, standardized language with response options designed to minimize recall bias and improve consistency of participant responses; however, self-reported data remain subject to reporting inaccuracies. Furthermore, questionnaire was not formally validated through pilot testing, test-retest reliability assessment, or psychometric evaluation, and responses were not cross-checked against medical records or other independent sources.

Data Collection Process

Data was compiled in Microsoft Excel and analyzed using RStudio (version 4.2.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Descriptive statistics included means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages. Between-group comparisons used independent samples t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests (or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts <5) for categorical variables. Prevalence rates were calculated as proportions of participants reporting physician-diagnosed conditions. Given multiple comparisons, associations with p < 0.01 provided stronger evidence, while p-values between 0.01 and 0.05 were interpreted cautiously. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 with two tailed tests.

Ethical Approval

The study received approval from the Local Committee for Research Ethics at Jazan University (reference No.: REC-46/09/1417, dated March 5, 2024). Informed consent was obtained electronically from all participants. Data confidentiality was maintained through password-protected databases accessible only to the research team.

Use of Generative Artificial Intelligence

Generative AI was used solely for language editing. Study design, data, analysis, and interpretation were entirely conducted by the author.

Discussion

Key Findings

This cross-sectional study identified a 10% prevalence of self-reported physician-diagnosed thyroid disorders among participants, which provides preliminary epidemiological insight into thyroid disease burden in southern Saudi Arabia, with hypothyroidism most common (7%), followed by hyperthyroidism (3%), thyroid nodules (2%), and thyroid cancer (1%). However, because diagnoses were self-reported without biochemical confirmation, these estimates should not be interpreted as definitive estimates of clinically confirmed thyroid dysfunction. Female sex, older age, higher BMI, and family history showed the strongest associations with thyroid conditions. Notably, 25% of affected individuals reported multiple concurrent conditions, highlighting thyroid pathology complexity. Our findings align with Al-Baha findings (15–16%)9 and falls within Middle Eastern estimates,7 though lower than some national Saudi estimates exceeding 30%. This variation likely reflects our focus on physician-diagnosed conditions rather than laboratory screening, which may miss subclinical disease but ensures practical relevance for healthcare planning by identifying clinically significant disease requiring medical attention.

Gender, Age, and Reproductive Factors

The female predominance—81% of hypothyroid and 60% of hyperthyroid patients—reinforces global patterns showing up to ten-fold higher thyroid dysfunction in women.4 This disparity reflects hormonal fluctuations related to reproductive cycles, pregnancy, and menopause, combined with autoimmune thyroid condition predisposition.16 Postpartum thyroiditis affected 3% of women with pregnancy history, underscoring the importance of peripartum thyroid monitoring. Healthcare providers should maintain heightened vigilance for postpartum thyroid dysfunction, as early detection can prevent progression to permanent hypothyroidism. The age association—hypothyroid patients averaging 40 versus 34 years in unaffected individuals—aligns with established patterns of increasing prevalence with advancing age,4,10 likely reflecting cumulative autoimmune processes and declining thyroid reserve. These patterns support age-stratified screening approaches, with more intensive monitoring for individuals over 40, particularly women.

Family History and Genetic Predisposition

Family history showed one of the strongest associations with thyroid conditions: 61% of hypothyroid and 43% of hyperthyroid patients reported positive family history, substantially exceeding background rates (10–13%). For structural diseases, clustering was even more pronounced—63% of thyroid nodule and 31% of thyroid cancer patients reported family history versus 1–2% background rates. These findings align with international studies showing 4.6-fold increased thyroid cancer risk with positive family history.17,18, The strength of familial clustering suggests important hereditary factors, potentially reflecting shared genetic variants, common environmental exposures, or both. However, the observational nature of our study precludes determination of whether familial clustering reflects primarily genetic susceptibility, shared environmental exposures within families, or both mechanisms operating simultaneously. Clinical implications include: (1) implementation of family history-based screening protocols, with affected relatives warranting earlier monitoring; (2) potential value of genetic counseling for families with multiple affected members; and (3) opportunities for genetic epidemiology research identifying population-specific susceptibility variants. Future studies employing multivariable analyses controlling for shared household exposures and environmental factors would help clarify the relative contributions of genetic versus environmental factors to familial clustering.

Obesity, Physical Activity, and Metabolic Factors

Significant BMI associations—mean 30 kg/m2 among hypothyroid versus 26 kg/m2 in unaffected individuals, with 33% obesity prevalence among thyroid patients versus 18% unaffected—parallel Asia Cohort Consortium findings linking elevated BMI to thyroid cancer risk.11 The obesity-thyroid relationship is likely bidirectional: obesity may contribute through inflammation and altered leptin signaling,19 while hypothyroidism promotes weight gain through metabolic slowing. This complex interplay emphasizes integrated clinical approaches addressing both conditions simultaneously. However, our cross-sectional design and bivariate analytical approach preclude determination of temporal sequence or control for potential confounders such as diet, medication use, or comorbid conditions that may influence both obesity and thyroid function. With 55% overweight/obesity prevalence overall, substantial prevention opportunities may exist through lifestyle interventions, though longitudinal studies with multivariable adjustment are needed to establish whether obesity represents a true modifiable risk factor or primarily reflects thyroid disease consequences. Physical inactivity associations (64% sedentary among thyroid patients versus 48% unaffected) warrant particularly cautious interpretation. The cross-sectional design cannot distinguish whether physical inactivity precedes thyroid disease development or results from thyroid-related symptoms such as fatigue, muscle weakness, and exercise intolerance. Reverse causation is highly plausible, as these symptoms commonly reduce physical activity capacity. Despite interpretive limitations, systematic reviews show exercise interventions improve thyroid hormone profiles,20 suggesting potential benefits of physical activity for thyroid health. However, these findings support exercise promotion primarily for symptom management and general health rather than as definitively established primary prevention. Prospective cohort studies with repeated measures of both physical activity and thyroid function, employing multivariable analyses to control for confounding factors, are essential to clarify directionality and establish whether physical inactivity genuinely contributes to thyroid disease risk. The observed association between elevated BMI and self-reported thyroid dysfunction should be interpreted cautiously. Individuals experiencing weight gain may be more likely to suspect thyroid disease or seek medical evaluation, potentially introducing detection bias. Additionally, age-related physiological increases in TSH and transient thyroid function abnormalities may contribute to misclassification in the absence of repeated biochemical testing. However, it is important to consider that individuals with a high BMI are more likely to utilize healthcare services, increasing the likelihood that various conditions, including thyroid diseases, are detected at an early or indolent stage. This phenomenon is referred to as detection bias.21 Lastly, another concern is that hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis is more active with higher levels of circulating leptin, since obese people have high levels of adipose tissue and hence leptin its expected for TRH and TSH to be high, this results in another bias called misclassification bias.19

Medical Risk Factors and Environmental Exposures

Strong associations between thyroid-affecting medications and thyroid dysfunction—18% of hypothyroid and 17% of hyperthyroid patients reported lithium, amiodarone, or interferon use versus 1–2% in unaffected individuals—align with established mechanisms whereby these medications disrupt thyroid function.22,23 While these associations are well-supported by mechanistic evidence and clinical observations, our findings emphasize their relevance in this population and underscore the importance of systematic thyroid function monitoring in patients receiving these medications. The magnitude of observed associations suggests monitoring protocols may need reinforcement in clinical practice, with particular attention to testing schedule compliance. Radiation exposure showed significant associations across all conditions, particularly for hyperthyroidism (26% exposed vs 2%) and thyroid cancer (38% vs 2%). Though absolute exposure prevalence was low (2.8%), the magnitude of differences confirms radiation as an important factor associated with thyroid disease. These findings have implications for medical imaging protocols, occupational safety standards, and patient counseling regarding radiation risks. However, our assessment of radiation exposure was limited to self-reported history without quantification of exposure dose, timing, or duration, limiting our ability to characterize dose-response relationships. Associations between endocrine comorbidities (adrenal or pituitary disorders) and thyroid dysfunction likely reflect multiple endocrine neoplasia or autoimmune polyendocrine syndromes,24 supporting comprehensive endocrine screening in patients with any endocrine disorder. However, the small numbers of participants with these conditions (n=24, 2%) limit statistical precision and prevent detailed characterization of these relationships.

Public Health and Clinical Implications

The 10% prevalence translates to substantial healthcare needs—applied nationally to Saudi Arabia’s 35 million population, this suggests over 3 million affected individuals requiring coordinated services. Clear high-risk groups (women, older adults, elevated BMI, positive family history) support targeted screening protocols that could enhance early detection while optimizing resources compared to universal screening. Strong modifiable factor associations—particularly obesity and physical inactivity—indicate primary prevention opportunities. Culturally adapted public health campaigns promoting healthy weight and physical activity, integrated with broader non-communicable disease prevention strategies, could potentially reduce disease burden. Healthcare systems should implement systematic medication monitoring protocols for patients receiving thyroid-disrupting drugs, leveraging electronic health records to trigger automated testing. The pronounced familial clustering supports routine three-generation family history collection during clinical encounters and cascade screening when thyroid disease is diagnosed. Individuals with affected first-degree relatives should receive earlier screening, particularly when multiple family members are affected.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths include the large sample size (n = 1,250), comprehensive assessment of multiple thyroid conditions and associated risk factors, and inclusion of participants from both urban and rural areas. The questionnaire was developed based on published literature and reviewed by clinical and public health experts to ensure clarity, content validity, and cultural appropriateness.

Limitations include the cross-sectional design, which prevents causal inference and limits interpretation of the observed associations, particularly those involving BMI and physical activity. Physical activity data were self-reported and may not accurately reflect actual exercise frequency, duration, or intensity, potentially affecting the observed associations related to physical inactivity. In addition, analyses were primarily bivariate and did not include multivariable adjustment, preventing identification of independent predictors and adjustment for potential confounders.

The present analysis was exploratory in nature and focused on bivariate associations; multivariable regression analysis was not performed, which limited adjustment for potential confounding variables.

A major limitation of this study is the reliance on self-reported physician-diagnosed thyroid disorders without biochemical or laboratory confirmation. Because a single abnormal thyroid function test does not necessarily indicate persistent thyroid disease, and thyroid hormone abnormalities may normalize upon repeat testing, some degree of misclassification is possible. Additionally, thyroid nodule history was self-reported without imaging verification, and some participants may have reported incidental or clinically insignificant benign nodules, potentially leading to overestimation of clinically relevant nodular thyroid disease.25 In addition, serum TSH levels may physiologically increase with advancing age, particularly among older adults, which may lead to overestimation of subclinical hypothyroidism.26 Furthermore, the observed association between higher BMI and thyroid dysfunction should be interpreted cautiously, as individuals experiencing weight gain may be more likely to suspect thyroid disease or seek medical evaluation, potentially introducing detection bias.21 Therefore, the reported prevalence estimates and observed associations should be interpreted as preliminary findings rather than clinically confirmed disease burden.

Future Research Directions

Prospective cohort studies following individuals over time would clarify temporal relationships and enable stronger causal inference, particularly for bidirectional obesity-thyroid dysfunction relationships. Population-based laboratory screening studies would provide more accurate prevalence estimates, including subclinical disease. Genetic epidemiology research investigating familial clustering could identify population-specific susceptibility variants enabling personalized risk prediction. Environmental epidemiology examining dietary iodine, contaminants, and occupational exposures would identify region-specific modifiable factors. Finally, randomized controlled trials of culturally adapted lifestyle interventions targeting obesity and physical inactivity would provide definitive evidence regarding prevention potential.

Conclusions

Approximately one in ten participants reported self-reported physician-diagnosed thyroid disorders. Female sex, advancing age, elevated BMI, and family history showed the strongest associations with thyroid conditions. The observed associations involving factors such as obesity and physical inactivity highlight important areas for future research; however, the cross-sectional design and bivariate analytical approach preclude causal interpretation. The observed familial clustering suggests that family history may warrant further investigation as a potentially relevant factor in future risk assessment and screening research. These findings provide descriptive epidemiological data characterizing self-reported thyroid disorder prevalence and associated factors in this population. Future prospective studies employing objective diagnostic measures and multivariable analyses are warranted to establish independent risk factors and clarify temporal relationships in the Saudi population.
