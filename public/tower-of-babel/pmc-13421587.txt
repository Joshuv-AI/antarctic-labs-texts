A detailed investigation of a-IGZO thin film-based MSM UV photodetector
Authors: Tarik Asar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmorphous Indium–Gallium–Zinc–Oxide (a-IGZO) thin films were deposited on silicon and glass substrates using a magnetron sputtering system at room temperature. The structural, morphological, optical, and electrical properties of the films were systematically investigated to evaluate their suitability for ultraviolet photodetection. Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry and Atomic Force Microscopy were employed to characterize the film composition and surface topology, while Hall Effect and Current–Voltage measurements were used to assess the electrical performance. The optical properties were analyzed in the 200–1100 nm spectral range using UV–VIS spectroscopy. A Metal–Semiconductor–Metal (MSM) photodetector was fabricated using a-IGZO film. The average transmittance of the film is 80%, an estimated optical energy gap value is 3.1 eV, and the band tail width is 382 meV. The electrical results for carrier density, mobility, and resistivity were determined to be 4.88 × 1019 cm− 3, 7.83 cm2/V.s, and 1.64 × 10− 2 Ω.cm, respectively. The FoM is calculated as 1.27 × 10− 4 Ω−1. a-IGZO MSM photodetector output parameters at 385 nm ultraviolet illumination, such as responsivity, detectivity, noise equivalent power, photo-to-dark current ratio, and normalized photo-to-dark current ratio at the critical voltage are determined as 12.72 A/W, 1.13 × 1013 Jones, 7.69 × 10− 13 W/Hz1/2, 0.386, and 3.96 × 105 W− 1, respectively. The results demonstrate that the a-IGZO MSM device exhibits behaviors comparable to those in the literature of ultraviolet photodetectors, confirming the potential of a-IGZO as a promising material for advanced optoelectronic applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52983-w.Keywords: Indium–Gallium–Zinc–Oxide, Metal–Semiconductor–Metal, UV photodetector, Thin film characterization, Device characterizationSubject terms: Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introductıon

Transparent conducting oxide (TCO) is essential for variety of optoelectronic applications devices1,2, such as at-panel displays3, touch screens4, gas sensors5, organic light emitting diodes6, electrochromic devices7, transistor8, lenses9, transparent electrodes in LCD screens10, and photodetectors (PDs)11. Among TCOs, Amorphous Transparent Oxide Semiconductors (ATOSs) have attracted considerable attention due to several promising features, including high transparency in the visible light region, high free-carrier mobility, and large-area, uniform deposition even at room temperature12–17. ATOSs are generally classified into two groups: single cation systems, such as Zinc-Oxide (ZnO)18, Tin-Oxide (SnO)19, and multi-cation systems that include Zinc-Indium-Oxide (ZnInO)20, Zinc-Tin-Oxide (ZnSnO)18, Gallium Zinc Oxide (GaZnO)21, and Indium-Gallium-Zinc-Oxide (InGaZnO)16,18,22. Because mixing two or more cations with different ionic sizes and charges greatly promotes the formation of the amorphous phase, using a multi-cation system helps ensure that thin films remain amorphous during processing18,23. Amorphous-InGaZnO (a-IGZO) is one of the most promising semiconductor oxide materials because of its high electron mobility, low leakage current, good film uniformity, optical transparency, and compatibility with low-temperature, large-area fabrication24–26. Additionally, a variety of optoelectronic applications, including transparent electronics, neuromorphic vision, sensors, and UV PDs, have been enabled by IGZO’s ultraviolet (UV) light absorption properties, owing to its broad optical band gap (Eg) of approximately 3.3–3.5 eV27–29. Among various deposition techniques for a-IGZO thin films, RF sputtering, which has the advantages of good film adhesion, reproducibility, high deposition rate, good homogeneity, and suitability for large-area processing, is one of the most widely used in many industries to prepare transparent and conductive thin films30–34. The sputtering method allows for the accurate transfer of target material stoichiometry to the deposited film35. By modulating parameters such as the sputtering power and the oxygen partial pressure during deposition, researchers can precisely control the concentration of oxygen vacancies, which are the primary source of free carriers in IGZO36–38. Lowering the carrier concentration through controlled oxidation is essential to ensure that the Schottky contact depletion region extends sufficiently across the channel to suppress dark current36–39. Furthermore, the high kinetic energy of atoms arriving at the substrate during sputtering (typically 1–10 eV) promotes adatom migration, resulting in dense, compact, and smooth amorphous structures36–40. Unlike solution-processed methods, sputtering produces films with low surface roughness and fewer structural defects, which is crucial for forming reliable, reproducible Schottky interfaces in Metal-Semiconductor-Metal (MSM) devices37–40. A significant advantage of magnetron sputtering is also its ability to deposit high-quality films at room temperature or low processing temperatures17,35–40. Thus, the magnetron sputtering systems provide the necessary control over material density, carrier concentration, and interface morphology to realize high-performance, stable, and scalable a-IGZO-based MSM photodetectors for next-generation UV sensing technologies. These properties make a-IGZO suitable for a variety of applications, including PDs41–44. PDs are well-suited optoelectronic devices for monitoring the exposure limit to UV radiation. Their extensive use has enabled various applications, including space communications, medical applications, flame detection and surveillance, missile tracking, environmental condition assessment, and UV emission identification45,46. These advantages make it highly promising for a wide range of applications in both military and civilian fields48. Therefore, it is important to develop an effective sensor for UV light detection28. UV PDs are crucial in advancing science and technology by transforming optical signals into electrical signals using semiconductor materials45. One notable application of a-IGZO is in MSM photodetectors. The MSM photodetector architecture, characterized by its fast photoresponse, benefits substantially from the integration of IGZO, enabling its use in advanced optoelectronic systems that require both high performance and stability44,47. The basic structure of an MSM photodetector typically consists of a semiconductor layer sandwiched between two metal electrodes, often configured as interdigitated fingers to optimize carrier collection48–50. This interdigitated design enhances the electric field in the semiconductor, facilitating the efficient separation and collection of photogenerated electron-hole pairs and thereby improving responsivity and speed49. For IGZO MSM photodetectors, a-IGZO films are often used due to their uniform properties and suitability for low-temperature processing, including room-temperature fabrication47. The judicious selection of metal contacts is crucial, as the metal-semiconductor interface properties, particularly the Schottky barrier height, significantly influence device performance, including dark current and photoresponse51,52. For instance, asymmetric electrode designs using different metals can yield varying Schottky barrier heights, enabling self-powered photodetection even without an applied bias53. Self-powered photodetection devices offer several advantages, including low power consumption, reduced system complexity, compact size, and simplified fabrication. Those devices can be divided into two groups according to the method of energy conversion: one type includes photoconductive devices, which convert UV radiation into electrical energy; the other type includes photovoltaic (built-in electric field) devices containing p–n junctions, heterojunctions, or Schottky junctions54–60. In photovoltaic mode, which means the incoming light signal induces spontaneous diffusion of free-carriers through the space charge region and electron-hole separation that leads to current flow at zero-bias voltage61. Usually, a built-in electric field, which separates the photogenerated electron-hole pairs and gives rise to a circuit current, is formed due to the spontaneous diffusion of electrons driven by the work function difference between the contact metal and the semiconductor. Unfortunately, the semiconductor’s surface state can significantly affect the diffusion process. The photoresponse at zero bias can also occur from a nonuniform distribution of trap densities at the metal/semiconductor interface. If the barrier heights at the surface states can be modulated, an asymmetric Schottky barrier forms, providing an additional driving force for the spontaneous diffusion of photocurrent in MSM PDs62. Asymmetric contacts receive significant recognition for enhancing the self-assembly of the built-in field to separate electron-hole pairs without an external bias, with significant Schottky contact differences, and supporting the long-term operation of the device63. Additionally, the photoresponse at zero bias can be attributed to hole trapping. The hole-trapping process at the metal electrode/a-IGZO interface plays an important role in achieving unbiased photoresponse in a-IGZO UV PDs. It provides insight into the operation of an a-IGZO UV PD at zero bias using a simple MSM structure47. Furthermore, the spectral response of IGZO MSM photodetectors can be tuned, for example, to enhance sensitivity in the solar-blind UV window by optimizing the deposition environment64. In terms of specific performance metrics, IGZO MSM photodetectors have achieved notable results. A maximum responsivity of 120 A/W and a noise equivalent power of 0.2 nW/(Hz)1/2 with a response time of 30 ms have been reported for real-time UV photodetectors fabricated with a-IGZO and gold electrodes44. This performance highlights the potential of a-IGZO in UV sensing and related optical applications. Therefore, the development of IGZO MSM photodetectors expands the range of photodetector technologies, which are essential in fields such as imaging, self-powered systems, remote communications, security, and wearable electronics65. Ongoing research into materials such as IGZO, along with advancements in device architecture and integration, promises further improvements in the performance and versatility of these devices.

The characterization of a-IGZO thin films and IGZO MSM UV photodetectors, including their structural, morphological, optical, and electrical properties, has significant implications. In this study, a-IGZO thin films were deposited at room temperature on both silicon and glass substrates using magnetron sputtering. The structural, morphological, and optical characterizations of the deposited a-IGZO thin film and electrical behaviors of the IGZO MSM UV Photodetector were determined by Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometer, Atomic Force Microscope, Hall Effect, and Current-Voltage measurement systems. The optical characterization of the a-IGZO thin film was determined in the spectral region of 200–1100 nm by a UV-Vis spectrometer. The electrical output parameters of the fabricated IGZO MSM UV Photodetector, such as Responsivity (R), Detectivity (D*), Noise Equivalent Power (NEP), Photo-to-Dark Current Ratio (PDCR), and Normalized Photo-to-Dark Ratio (NPDR), were determined to assess the effect of electrical parameters on device performance.

Experimental

a-IGZO thin films were deposited on both the n-type Si substrate (Si stands out as a more cost-effective option and owing some advantages32, having a resistivity range of 1–10 Ωcm, and the Corning glass substrate by means of the RF magnetron sputtering system (Nanovak NVTS-500), having an InGaZnO4 target (Kurt J. Lesker, 99.99% Pure, 2.00” Diameter x 0.125” Thick). Before the deposition process, n-type Si and Corning glass substrates were cleaned with an inorganic solvent of CH3COCH3 in an ultrasonic bath for 10 minutes, followed by a second cleaning with an inorganic solvent of CH3CH(OH)CH3. After rinsing with deionized (DI) water, the n-type Si substrate was finally dried with N2 and then loaded into the magnetron sputtering system. After that, the a-IGZO thin films were deposited on the front surfaces of both substrates without oxygen. The deposition parameters are given in Table 1.

Then, an IGZO MSM UV photodetector was fabricated on 10 × 10 mm2 Si-based sample. The fabrication process included a native oxide removal step and an MSM diode-forming step that involves two back-to-back Schottky interdigital electrodes. First, the native oxide on the Si substrate surfaces was removed using a wet-chemical etch solution of 1H2SO4:1H2O2:80 DI-H2O for 1 minute. The samples were rinsed in DI-H2O and dried with nitrogen gas. After that, Ag interdigital electrodes with a 100 μm line width, 100 μm spacing, and 800 μm length were deposited onto an a-IGZO thin film-coated n-Si substrate using the Aerosol Jet Printing System (Aerosol Jet 5X Systems; Optomec, USA). The optical characterization of the a-IGZO thin film was determined in the spectral region of 200–1100 nm by an UltraViolet-Visible (UV-Vis) spectrometer (PerkinElmer Lambda 2 S). The electrical output parameters of the fabricated photodetector, including Responsivity (R), Detectivity (D*), Noise Equivalent Power (NEP), and Normalized Photo-to-Dark Ratio (NPDR), were determined by analyzing the results obtained from Current-Voltage measurements performed under ultraviolet light at room temperature.

a-IGZO thin film characterization

The deposited IGZO thin film was confirmed to be amorphous in our previous study using Grazing Incidence X-Ray Diffraction (GIXRD), in which the X-Ray beam incidence angle was kept at a very low angle to focus solely on the thin-film surfaces66. It is also well known that surface morphology is a crucial factor in determining the performance of electro-optical devices, such as MSM photodetectors, solar cells, LEDs, and sensors67. In devices such as MSM photodetectors, which will be produced on a rough, thin-film surface, distortions at the metal-semiconductor interface may occur, potentially affecting device performance. For this reason, it is essential to examine thin-film surfaces using surface analysis methods, such as AFM, to achieve successful surface functionalization and to determine the Root Mean Square (RMS) roughness values using suitable software68. Thus, the surface morphology of the structure over a 3 μm × 3 μm area was characterized using the obtained AFM measurements. Figure 2 shows the two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional (3D) AFM images of the a-IGZO thin film surface, illustrating its morphology and surface roughness. The sample’s surface RMS roughness is 0.90 nm. However, the a-IGZO thin film surface has poles with heights of 6.35 nm distributed across the film surface. The formation of these poles could be due to the rapid sublimation of starting materials before decomposition69.

(a) 2D and (b) 3D AFM images of a-IGZO thin film.

The surface appears smooth, which enhances the device’s high electrical performance70,71, as surface defects are primary candidates for causing cracks in the film under external stresses71. The smooth, thin film surface played a crucial role in high-performance devices72. The surface construction is nearly identical to that in the literature for oxide-based thin films17,73.

The interface characteristics of an a-IGZO thin film were analyzed using Secondary Ion Mass Spectroscopy (SIMS) depth profile measurements. SIMS is a destructive technique used to analyze a material’s composition. This method involves removing material by sputtering and then analyzing the ejected particles with a mass analyzer67,74. SIMS depth profile of the In, Ga, Zn, O2, and Si atoms in the sample is given in Fig. 3.

In the SIMS graph, it is evident that there is a sharp transition between the atoms forming the a-IGZO thin film and the n-Si substrate. This transition between the atoms and the substrate also indicates that the a-IGZO thin film is approximately 95 nm thick. Additionally, it is worth noting that O2 diffusion into the substrate layer is negligible at the end of thin film deposition. At the interface between the a-IGZO thin film layer and the substrate, the number of materials forming the thin film decreases. In contrast, the amount of Si as the substrate material increases significantly. This good interfacial formation also demonstrates that the thin film forms on the substrate with high quality and surface compatibility. This situation is encountered in many thin films produced from high-quality semiconductor substrates in literature.

IGZO MSM UV photodetector characterization

When the IGZO thin film is under UV illumination (typically with photon energy greater than the IGZO bandgap), several processes occur simultaneously, including photogeneration, oxygen desorption, and ionization of oxygen vacancies27,28. UV photons excite electrons from the valence band to the conduction band, generating electron-hole pairs28,46. The electron–hole pairs are generated, and the photogenerated holes are captured by adsorbed oxygen ions. Then, these oxygen ions become oxygen molecules again and desorb from the IGZO thin film ()21. This process “releases” the previously trapped electrons back into the conduction band27. UV light also ionizes oxygen vacancies (), promoting electrons to the conduction band ()27,46. The trapping of photogenerated holes by surface oxygen ions delays carrier recombination. Because holes are consumed at the surface via desorption, the lifetime of photogenerated electrons is extended, allowing them to circulate through the circuit multiple times21. This results in photoconductive gain and transition efficiency21,28. A model using an energy band diagram to explain the working principle of a self-powered PD at zero bias is shown in Fig. 9.

Conclusıons

The amorphous Indium–Gallium–Zinc–Oxide (a-IGZO) thin films were deposited at room temperature. The structural, morphological, optical, and electrical properties of the films were systematically investigated to evaluate their suitability for ultraviolet photodetection. It was found that the average transmittance of the film is 80%, an estimated optical energy gap value is 3.1 eV, and the band tail width is 382 meV. The electrical results for carrier density, mobility, and resistivity were determined to be 4.88 × 1019 cm− 3, 7.83 cm2/V.s, and 1.64 × 10− 2 Ω.cm, respectively. The FoM is calculated as 1.27 × 10− 4 Ω−1. The electrical output parameters of the fabricated a-IGZO MSM UV photodetector were also evaluated, including responsivity, detectivity, noise-equivalent power, photo-to-dark current ratio, and the normalized photo-to-dark current ratio. The ultraviolet light exposure leads to a current increase of 6.375 µA. The critical voltage for the device is 3.02 V. The highest measured responsivity, 48.99 A/W, was measured at room temperature at the critical voltage. The maximum detectivity was found to be 4.37 × 1013 Jones under an applied bias of 0.79 V. The minimum NEP is 2.29 × 10− 13 W/Hz1/2 obtained at 0.79 V. The maximum PDCR and NPDR are observed at 0.14 V, corresponding to 31.207 and 9.202 × 106 W− 1, respectively. Responsivity, detectivity, noise equivalent power, photo-to-dark current ratio, and normalized photo-to-dark current ratio at the critical voltage are 12.72 A/W, 1.13 × 1013 Jones, 7.69 × 10− 13 W/Hz1/2, 0.386, and 3.96 × 105 W− 1, respectively. The results demonstrate that the a-IGZO MSM UV photodetector exhibits behaviors comparable to those reported in the literature for ultraviolet photodetectors, confirming the potential of a-IGZO as a promising material for advanced optoelectronic applications. In addition, this comprehensive study makes a significant contribution to the literature on a-IGZO MSM UV photodetectors, offering easy fabrication processes and low production costs.
