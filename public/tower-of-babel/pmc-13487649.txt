Secular Trends in Physical Activity, Sedentary Behavior, and Weight Status Among South Korean Adolescents During 2017–2024
Authors: Yunmin Han, Younghwan Choi, Sungjae Yoon, Doyoung Gwak, Eun‐Young Lee, Yeon Soo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundWe examined trends in physical activity (PA), sedentary behavior (SB), and weight status among Korean adolescents across the COVID‐19 period and assessed subgroup disparities.MethodsWe analyzed nationally representative data from the 2017 to 2024 Korea youth risk behavior web‐based survey (N = 417,243; mean age, 15.0 years). PA adherence followed World Health Organization recommendations; SB adherence was defined as ≤ 2 h/day of recreational screen time; and weight status used the 2017 Korean Growth Chart BMI percentiles. Weighted estimates and Joinpoint regression quantified annual percent changes (APCs).ResultsUnderweight and obesity both increased. Underweight rose particularly among girls (APC, 6.7%), whereas obesity was more prevalent among boys (14.7%; APC, 3.2%) and high school students (13.3%; APC, 3.2%). PA adherence increased over the study period (APC, 4.0%) but remained low, especially in girls (2.4% vs. 8.6% in boys). SB adherence declined from 36.1% in 2017 to 19.2% in 2020 and partially recovered by 2024 (24.5%).Implications for School Health Policy, Practice, and EquitySchools should adopt gender‐sensitive strategies to increase daily PA and reduce recreational SB, prioritizing girls and high school students.ConclusionsFrom 2017 to 2024, weight polarization worsened among Korean adolescents, underscoring the need for equitable school‐based interventions targeting PA, SB, and weight‐related disparities.Keywords: exercise, health equity, obesity, underweight

Introduction

The COVID‐19 pandemic significantly disrupted daily routines and lifestyles worldwide, presenting unprecedented challenges to public health [1]. In South Korea (Korea hereafter), rigorous public health measures to prevent the spread of infection were implemented, including school closures, and social distancing, which have substantially altered lifestyle patterns, particularly among children and adolescents [2]. These disruptions were known to have impacted adolescent health behaviors, notably physical activity (PA), sedentary behavior (SB), and consequently, weight status [3, 4]. Understanding these changes is crucial, as 24‐h movement behaviors affect adolescent obesity risk, and school‐based PA interventions can effectively improve health outcomes [5, 6].

Even prior to the COVID‐19 pandemic, Korean adolescents experienced considerable health challenges related to weight status, with both obesity and underweight being significant public health concerns [7]. Between 2009 and 2019, the prevalence of obesity among Korean boys increased from 6.6% to 13.8%, while among girls it rose from 3.5% to 8.1% [8]. At the same time, underweight status, particularly among girls, showed an increasing trend. Specifically, in 2022, 9.9% of Korean girls were classified as underweight, highlighting the continued presence of both obesity and underweight as major weight‐related health issues [9].

These sex‐specific weight trends are closely linked to two critical modifiable health behaviors: PA and SB [10]. Biologically and behaviorally, insufficient PA and prolonged SB can independently disrupt energy homeostasis and increase obesity risk, whereas excessive SB combined with psychological stress may also aggravate underweight‐related problems [11]. Despite the World Health Organization (WHO) recommending that adolescents engage in at least 60 min of moderate‐to‐vigorous PA daily, over 90% of Korean adolescents failed to meet this recommendation prior to the pandemic, alongside high levels of SB [12, 13]. Importantly, these behaviors are socially patterned: boys tend to report higher PA yet higher obesity prevalence, whereas girls report higher sedentary time and a higher proportion of underweight [14]. PA also declines markedly from middle to high school, consistent with Korea's strong academic demands and time constraints on leisure‐time activity [15].

While prior international and Korean studies have reported short‐term changes in adolescents' PA, SB, and weight status during the early pandemic, evaluating these variables concurrently is crucial for establishing a strong rationale for comprehensive interventions. Evidence spanning the full COVID‐19 timeline (pre‐, during‐, and post‐pandemic) remains limited [16, 17, 18]. This gap limits our ability to distinguish short‐term disruption from longer‐term shifts and to identify which groups experienced the most persistent deterioration.

Therefore, this study investigated trends in weight status (including obesity, overweight, and underweight), PA, and SB among Korean adolescents across three periods: pre‐pandemic (2017–2019), during the pandemic (2020–2021), and post‐pandemic (2022–2024). Based on previous findings showing sex‐ and age‐related differences in PA, SB, and weight status among adolescents [15, 19], we examined whether (1) PA and SB guideline adherence and weight status changed across these periods and (2) these trends differed by sex and school level.

Methods

Data and Study Population

This study analyzed data from the Korea youth risk behavior web‐based survey (KYRBS), an annual, nationally representative, anonymous, and self‐administered online survey conducted by the Korea disease control and prevention agency (KDCA) [20]. The KYRBS employs a stratified, multistage cluster sampling design to ensure representation of middle and high school students across the country. The survey systematically monitors a range of health‐related behaviors among Korean adolescents. The KYRBS is administered in participating schools, and students complete the anonymous web‐based questionnaire voluntarily during a designated survey period. The survey is typically conducted in early summer (generally June–July), which helps reduce seasonal variation across survey years. The present study was a secondary analysis of anonymized, publicly available KYRBS data. Ethical review was waived by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University (IRB No. E2507/003‐018). For the original KYRBS, informed consent for participation was obtained by the KDCA prior to data collection.

A total of 448,798 student responses were recorded across the eight annual survey waves. The KYRBS is an annual, nationally representative repeated cross‐sectional survey, in which different samples of students in Grades 7 through 12 (typically aged 12–18 years) are selected each year. The annual response rate consistently exceeded 90%, underscoring the reliability and robustness of the dataset. For the present analysis, we included participants with complete data on key study variables, including PA, SB, and body mass index (BMI). Adolescents with missing or implausible values for relevant covariates (e.g., socioeconomic status, geographic region, or academic performance) were excluded from the final sample. After applying these inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 417,243 adolescents were retained for analysis (Table S1). Based on the classification criteria provided by the Korea Central Disease Control Headquarters, the study period was categorized into three phases: pre‐COVID‐19 (2017–2019), during COVID‐19 (2020–2021), and post‐COVID‐19 (2022–2024) [21].

Measures

Physical Activity

PA was assessed using two items: (1) “During the past 7 days, on how many days were you physically active for a total of at least 60 minutes per day? (Activities that increased your heart rate or made you breathe harder than usual)” and (2) “During the past 7 days, on how many days did you engage in vigorous PA for at least 20 minutes per day? (Activities that made you sweat or breathe much harder than usual).” Responses were recorded as the number of days (0–7) in the past week and were dichotomized as meeting versus not meeting a guideline‐based definition of recommended adolescent PA. Given the structure of the KYRBS items (which do not capture average daily moderate‐to‐vigorous physical activity (MVPA) minutes across the week), we applied a conservative operational definition based on the WHO recommendation [22]. Specifically, adolescents were classified as meeting this operational definition if they reported: (1) engaging in at least 60 min per day of moderate‐to‐vigorous PA on 7 days in the past week and (2) performing vigorous‐intensity PA for at least 20 min per day on three or more days in the past week [22]. Accordingly, participants reporting ≥ 60 min/day of MVPA on all 7 days and vigorous‐intensity PA on ≥ 3 days/week were classified as meeting this operational definition. Because the survey only assessed the number of days with ≥ 60 min of activity, adolescents reporting fewer than 7 days were classified as not meeting the guideline.

Sedentary Behavior

SB was assessed using a single self‐reported item: “During the past 7 days, how many hours per day did you spend sitting for non‐academic purposes (e.g., watching TV, playing video games, using a smartphone or computer for leisure)?” Responses were collected as average hours per day and were dichotomized as meeting versus not meeting the SB guideline using the ≤ 2 h/day recreational screen‐time threshold from the Canadian 24‐Hour Movement Guidelines [23]. Because the WHO guidelines do not specify a quantitative sedentary‐time cut‐off for adolescents, this threshold was used for classification.

Weight Status Classification

BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2). Height and weight were self‐reported by participants through the KYRBS online questionnaire. Weight status was classified using the 2017 Korean National Growth Charts, which provide age‐ and sex‐specific BMI percentiles for children and adolescents [24]. Underweight was defined as having a BMI below the 5th percentile for age and sex. Obesity was defined as having a BMI at or above the 95th percentile for age and sex. To ensure accurate classification, each participant's exact age in months was calculated based on the participant's date of birth, allowing for precise alignment with developmental benchmarks outlined in the growth charts.

Sociodemographic Variables

The following sociodemographic variables were included to examine trends in PA, SB, and weight status across population subgroups. School level consisted of two groups: middle school (Grades 7–9) and high school (Grades 10–12). Sex consisted of two groups: male or female. Perceived economic status was assessed by asking adolescents to evaluate their household's financial situation with response options on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from very high to very low, which were grouped into three categories: high, middle, and low (high = very high/high; middle = middle; low = low/very low). Region of residence was classified based on the level of urbanization: urban (metropolitan cities), other cities, and rural areas. Urban areas were defined as metropolitan cities (special/metropolitan cities) according to the administrative classification used in the KYRBS; no additional population‐size threshold was applied. Academic performance was assessed by asking adolescents to report their academic achievement over the past 12 months with response options on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from very high to very low, which were categorized into high, middle, or low for analyses (high = very high/high; middle = middle; low = low/very low). These variables were used to stratify the prevalence estimates and assess trends across different sociodemographic groups.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses accounted for the complex survey design of the KYRBS by incorporating sampling weights, stratification, and clustering to ensure nationally representative estimates. Descriptive statistics were first used to summarize the sociodemographic characteristics and key study variables—including PA, SB, and weight status—by survey year and grade level.

Baseline characteristics were summarized using unweighted values. Categorical variables were presented as unweighted frequencies and proportions (%), while continuous variables were presented as unweighted means and standard deviations (mean ± SD). To examine time trends, we estimated the annual prevalence of PA and SB guideline adherence and weight status categories using weighted prevalence estimates for each survey year.

Temporal trends were further assessed using Joinpoint regression analysis, which identified statistically significant changes in the slope of trends over time. The annual percent change (APC) and corresponding 95% CIs were reported for each segment to describe the magnitude and direction of changes across different time periods. All analyses were conducted using Stata version 18 (Stata Corp LLC, College Station, TX, USA), which supports complex survey data procedures. Joinpoint regression analyses were performed using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 417,243 Korean adolescents were eligible for and included in this study, spanning the years 2017 to 2024. The sample was stratified into three distinct periods relative to the COVID‐19 pandemic: pre‐pandemic (2017–2019; n = 163,149), during the pandemic (2020–2021; n = 103,050), and post‐pandemic (2022–2024; n = 151,044). The overall cohort comprised a slightly higher proportion of middle school students (52.6%) and boys (51.2%). The distribution of participants by sex, school level, socioeconomic status, region of residence, and academic performance is described in detail in Table S1.

Trends in Weight Status

Table 1 presents weighted prevalence estimates (%) of underweight from 2017 to 2024. Overall, underweight prevalence increased from 6.9% in 2017 to 7.5% in 2024 (total prevalence 7.7%), corresponding to a significant upward trend over the study period (APC = 3.0%, 95% CI = 0.7–5.4). This increase was particularly pronounced among girls, whose underweight prevalence rose from 6.4% in 2017 to 8.7% in 2024, with a marked increase during the pandemic that persisted into the post‐pandemic period (APC = 6.7%, 95% CI = 3.4–10.2).

Trends in PA and SB Guidelines Adherence

Discussion

Using nationally representative data from Korean adolescents, we examined secular trends in PA and SB guideline adherence and weight status across the pre‐, during‐, and post‐pandemic periods and by key sociodemographic characteristics. By applying APC estimates, we quantified and compared behavioral and health‐related transitions over time. Our stratified analyses by different sociodemographic characteristics indicated that, in addition to sex, trends in PA, SB, and weight status varied by school level, household economic status, region of residence, and academic performance. These subgroup differences highlight the potentially heterogeneous impact of the pandemic on adolescent health behaviors and the need for tailored public health strategies.

The increasing prevalence of adolescent obesity has been consistently documented across many countries, with a particularly pronounced rise among boys [25]. Our findings align with prior Korean studies reporting a steady increase in obesity rates during the COVID‐19 pandemic [16, 19, 26]. Similar global patterns have been observed, especially during the pandemic period [25]. In contrast to the global decline in underweight prevalence, our study identified a divergent pattern among Korean girls, who showed persistent or even increasing rates of underweight [9, 27]. This sex‐specific discrepancy highlights a distinct public health concern in the Korean context, potentially shaped by sociocultural factors such as body image norms and weight control behaviors among female adolescents [28].

Our findings also showed a notable increase in both underweight and obesity after the onset of the pandemic, reflecting a widening polarization of weight status by sex. Obesity was more pronounced among boys, whereas underweight was especially evident among girls. These differences are shaped not only by biological factors but also by gendered sociocultural influences [29, 30]. Prior studies in Korea have documented high levels of body dissatisfaction and distorted weight perception among female adolescents, often driven by media portrayals and social norms that idealize thinness [31]. This internalization of unrealistic body ideals contributes to unhealthy weight‐control behaviors, particularly among high school girls, and helps explain why underweight prevalence in Korean girls diverges from global trends. In contrast, boys may be more likely to experience weight gain, partly because of lower engagement in dietary restriction and, in some cases, higher consumption of energy‐dense foods [31, 32, 33].

International studies have also reported a sharp decline in adolescent PA during the early pandemic period [34, 35]. For example, a systematic review of global data found a 20% reduction (90% CI = −34% to −4%) in total daily PA among children and adolescents [36]. Notably, the sex difference in this decline was minimal (approximately −1%). In contrast, our study found that the reduction in PA among Korean adolescents during the pandemic was relatively modest, and activity levels rebounded in 2022, suggesting a degree of resilience or policy‐mediated mitigation. This may reflect Korea's unique policy response, including relatively shorter school closures, access to outdoor spaces, and an early return to structured school routines.

Our findings also reinforce previous Korean studies showing markedly lower PA adherence among girls than among boys, with sex disparities in MVPA greater than those reported in many Western countries [15, 36]. Although activity levels have modestly rebounded, adherence remains far below global recommendations. SB also worsened during the pandemic, with Korean adolescents showing a marked decline in adherence to sedentary guidelines and only partial recovery thereafter. Given its link to obesity risk, this trend highlights the need for integrated strategies targeting both PA and screen time reduction [37]. School contexts may shape adolescents' movement behaviors and weight outcomes; recent evidence links school‐level factors (including sport facility parameters) to youth overweight/obesity, supporting school‐based approaches to address post‐pandemic disparities in activity and weight status [38]. In addition, sleep duration has been associated with overweight/obesity risk in school‐aged youth, suggesting that school health strategies may benefit from considering sleep alongside PA and sedentary time [39].

It is also important to distinguish between sex—a biological categorization based on physiological characteristics—and gender, which encompasses the social, cultural, and behavioral roles, expectations, and norms associated with being male, female, or nonbinary [40]. Although our data were reported by sex, we interpreted the observed patterns through a gendered sociocultural lens because adolescents' weight‐related behaviors, opportunities for PA, and sedentary lifestyles are strongly shaped by societal expectations and gender norms [41]. For example, cultural ideals around body image, participation in sports, and engagement in active play often differ for boys and girls, influencing both weight trajectories and activity patterns [10].

Implications for School Health Policy, Practice, and Equity

Although PA guideline adherence improved modestly after the pandemic, overall adherence among Korean adolescents remained very low, particularly among girls, indicating persistent sex‐related disparities in PA participation. Girls consistently reported lower levels of PA than boys, and this gap remained pronounced after the pandemic, likely due to sociocultural influences such as gendered expectations around sports participation, safety concerns, and body image pressures [42, 43]. At the same time, Korea received an “A” grade in the 2022 Global Matrix 4.0 Report Card for government policy efforts to support youth PA, reflecting broad national and local initiatives [44]. The pandemic may also have created more opportunities for outdoor play given continued access to public open spaces, highlighting the potential to embed outdoor activity within school routines [13]. However, these policy advances contrast with actual adolescent behaviors: the overall PA grade remained at “D−,” underscoring the persistent gap between institutional intent and real‐world outcomes. Furthermore, Korean adolescents spend an average of 6–7 h per day in academic study—among the highest globally—which reduces opportunities for PA and contributes to prolonged sedentary time [45]. Together, these factors suggest that both sociocultural pressures and structural barriers have reinforced inequities in PA participation, particularly among girls, and limited the impact of otherwise strong policy support. Furthermore, the proliferation of digital devices and increased smartphone use—especially after the pandemic—has further exacerbated SB [46]. Screen time has become a dominant form of leisure and social interaction, often replacing outdoor play and active commuting [47]. The shift toward digital environments may have long‐term implications for both PA patterns and weight status.

The simultaneous rise in underweight and obesity among Korean adolescents highlights the need for targeted, sex‐specific interventions that combine nutrition education with PA promotion [48, 49]. Relying solely on BMI thresholds risks overlooking nuanced, population‐specific weight issues; therefore, public health surveillance should incorporate additional markers such as body composition, weight perception, and behavioral risk factors [50]. Importantly, our findings show that while PA adherence improved modestly, sedentary behavior adherence declined sharply during the pandemic and only partially recovered thereafter. Given the well‐established link between prolonged screen time and obesity risk, SB warrants equal emphasis alongside PA. Policies should not only promote structured exercise but also actively reduce sedentary time through school‐ and community‐based programs [51, 52]. To reduce disparities, interventions should consider socioeconomic context. Expanding affordable after‐school and community activity opportunities, integrated with education and health systems, could narrow the gap. In addition, family‐level strategies are critical; Korean data indicate that parental PA strongly influences adolescent behavior, underscoring the need for family‐based education and role modeling [53]. A multi‐level approach involving schools, families, and communities will be essential to achieve sustainable health gains [54]. However, school‐based PA programs often show limited durability once structured requirements end, and sustaining participation beyond compulsory school time remains a challenge [55]. Future strategies should therefore incorporate elements that promote long‐term engagement—such as enjoyment, autonomy (e.g., offering choice), and peer/social support—particularly for girls and high school students who showed persistently low adherence in our study [56, 57].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the use of self‐reported data may have introduced reporting bias (e.g., recall and social desirability) for PA and SB and may also have led to some misclassification of BMI‐based weight status because height and weight were self‐reported. In addition, dichotomizing PA and SB into guideline adherence may reduce information on dose (e.g., how close participants were to meeting recommendations) and may introduce some misclassification. Second, the behavioral variables were limited in detail: PA was assessed using self‐reported items on the frequency of daily MVPA and vigorous‐intensity PA, which did not capture other important dimensions such as activity type, intensity beyond the threshold, duration, and setting. Third, because of the repeated cross‐sectional design, this study cannot establish causal relationships or track individual‐level behavioral changes over time. Finally, the findings should be interpreted within the specific sociocultural context of Korea, including intense academic pressure, unique pandemic policies, and high digital connectivity, which may limit generalizability to other countries.

Conclusion

This study identified distinct trends in PA, SB, and weight status among Korean adolescents across the COVID‐19 pandemic timeline. While obesity and underweight both increased, particularly among boys and girls respectively, adherence to PA guidelines showed a modest post‐pandemic rebound. However, overall activity levels remained low, with notable disparities by sex, grade, and socioeconomic status. These findings underscore the need for tailored, equity‐focused health policies that address both ends of the weight spectrum. Continued monitoring and multi‐level interventions are essential to support healthy behaviors in the post‐pandemic era.
