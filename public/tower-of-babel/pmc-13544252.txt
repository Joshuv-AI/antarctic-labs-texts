Upconverted nanoparticle for 2D nanomapping of excitation energy transfer in conjugated polymer
Authors: Syoji Ito, Hirotaka Kageyama, Takato Mizoguchi, Bhagya Lakshmi S. B., Hikaru Sotome, Ali Eftekhari, Aude Bouchet, Michel Sliwa, Hiroshi Miyasaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Real-space evaluation of exciton migration in a nanodomain potentially provides substantial information in advanced material design, that is, however, still challenging as optical excitation and detection are diffraction limited to submicrometer resolutions. To explore the real-space evaluation, we used rare-earth core-shell upconverting nanoparticles, with thulium Tm3+ as emitters and Yb3+ as sensitizers (TmUCNP), for nanometric photoexcitation of conducting polymer, poly[2-methoxy-5-(2-ethylhexyloxy)-1,4-phenylenevinylene] (MEH-PPV). The TmUCNPs in MEH-PPV solid were photoexcited with a near-infrared laser. The visible emission of the Tm ions allowed for the production of excited states of MEH-PPV through excitation energy transfer. The emission of MEH-PPV spectrally separable from Tm ions permitted individual imaging of their emission spots and single-particle emission dynamics measurement. The luminescence images of the TmUCNPs and MEH-PPV film, analyzed using the localization method, provided two-dimensional (2D) nanoscale distributions of the emissive sites in MEH-PPV surrounding the TmUCNPs. From the 2D map of the emissive sites, we determined the length of excitation energy transfer in the MEH-PPV solid to be ≦55 nanometers.

INTRODUCTION

Excitation energy transfer (EET) is a photophysical process in which a molecule in an excited state (the energy donor) transfers its excitation energy to a neighboring molecule (the energy acceptor) (1). For the characterization of the EET, two representative mechanisms have been theoretically formulated: well known as the Förster (2, 3) and Dexter (4) ones.

The EET plays one of the major roles in photoconversion of various organic systems, such as natural photosynthetic circuits, organic photovoltaic cells, and organic light-emitting diodes. Elucidating the EET dynamics in such assembled molecular systems can hence potentially lead to a guiding principle of design for photoresponsive materials. Researchers have conducted extensive studies using time-resolved spectroscopy, providing information on the rate and length of EET for the ensembles of the systems such as multichromophoric nanoparticles (5), dendrimers (6), conjugated polymer solids (7, 8), and supramolecular polymers (9). These time-resolved studies have estimated the exciton migration length in these systems to be several tens of nanometers. For instance, in a conjugated polymer blend with ∼50 nm–sized microscopic phase-separation structures, more than 70% of donor polymer underwent charge separation within a subpicosecond timescale at the phase boundary (10). In addition to such time-resolved approaches, evaluations of EET lengths based on single-molecule/particle fluorescence spectroscopy have also been reported. For example, spectroscopic measurements of single poly[2-methoxy-5-(2-ethylhexyloxy)-1,4-phenylenevinylene] (MEH-PPV) chains have revealed EET lengths of up to 75 nm (11), while single-particle measurements of nanometer-sized MEH-PPV aggregates have estimated exciton migration lengths of ∼60 nm (12). Single-particle fluorescence spectroscopy also revealed that perylenediimide nanoparticles ranging from several tens to 100 nm emitted single photons per photoexcitation, despite containing many perylenediimide molecules within a single nanoparticle (13). Because this single-photon emitting behavior (photon antibunching) results from efficient S1-S1 annihilation among multiple excited states within a nanoparticle—ultimately leading to the survival of a single excited state—the result implies a highly efficient EET process with an EET length sufficient to cover the entire volume of the nanoparticles (14).

Although time-resolved spectroscopy can provide information on EET rate and characteristic migration length as described above, such measurements are spatially averaged over macroscopic samples with intrinsic nanoscale heterogeneity. Despite its potential impact on the rational design of advanced materials, evaluating EET lengths on a spatial scale of several tens of nanometers remains challenging because of the diffraction limit of light and the coexistence of multiexciton processes, such as exciton-exciton annihilation, combined with energy migration via the Förster and Dexter mechanisms (8). In solids of conjugated dye molecules, the energy of a photon absorbed by a dye molecule migrates among surrounding molecules and is ultimately trapped by a lower-energy site, followed by fluorescence emission, as illustrated in Fig. 1 (D and E). A previous real-space study of exciton diffusion has visualized the temporal broadening of fluorescent area sizes following diffraction-limited excitation (15). Such approaches provide ensemble-averaged information on exciton diffusion within a diffraction-limited excitation area through the temporal evolution of the width of the corresponding emission spot. To evaluate the EET length in real space with a higher precision, it is necessary to determine both the photoexcitation position and the corresponding emission (low-energy) site with nanometric accuracy. To precisely locate such a low-energy site, a two-dimensional (2D) localization method (16–19) can be performed by fitting the emission spot of the low-energy site using a 2D Gaussian function. Under ambient condition (i.e., in the air at room temperature), this method can achieve localization accuracy within several nanometers on the lateral objective plane. However, attaining nanometric accuracy in the localization of the excitation position is substantially more difficult. Even when laser light is focused using an objective lens with the highest numerical aperture (NA), dye molecules are excited randomly in the diffraction-limited focal spot (200 to 300 nm in diameter for visible excitation). Consequently, the localization accuracy of the excitation position is also diffraction limited, one order of magnitude larger than the typical EET length, thereby hindering nanometric evaluation of EET processes.

For photoexcitation of a nanometer-sized spot beyond the diffraction limit, we can consider several potential approaches, such as the use of optical near-field and downsizing photoexcitation area based on the stimulated emission depletion (STED) concept (20–22). Performing aperture-type or scattering-type scanning near-field optical microscopy (SNOM) (23–25) enables photoexcitation of a small area (∼10 to 100 nm). However, localized photoexcitation is achievable only in the vicinity of the SNOM tip, and the optical near field decays rapidly with increasing distance from the tip. Consequently, achieving nanometric photoexcitation inside a conjugated-material film is challenging. In contrast, upconversion nanoparticles (UCNPs) can function as embedded nanoscale excitation sources directly within the material. Although STED microscopy can also achieve local photoexcitation comparable to that of SNOM, efficient depletion of excited states generally requires intense depletion beams, which may induce photodamage or additional nonlinear effects in conjugated materials. In contrast, UCNP-based excitation enables localized excitation without requiring strong depletion light fields. In addition, both SNOM and STED typically require relatively complex optical systems and alignment procedures, whereas UCNP-based excitation can be implemented using a comparatively simple wide-field optical setup.

To achieve nanometric photoexcitation and real-space investigation of EET in a solid of conjugated compounds using a simple optical setup, in the present study, we used rare-earth UCNPs (26–29) for local excitation, as illustrated in Fig. 1, and demonstrated their performance as nanoscale excitation sources for the measurement of energy migration length in a conjugated material, MEH-PPV. Local photoexcitation of materials using nanoparticles as nanometric localized excitation sources has previously been reported for the study of triplet exciton diffusion in molecular crystals (30). Only an ensemble-averaged diffusion behavior was however characterized through diffusion-induced broadening of upconversion emission profiles. In contrast, the present study combines nanometric localized excitation of single rare-earth UCNPs with 2D localization analysis of emission positions, enabling 2D spatially resolved mapping of EET behaviors in conducting polymer films. A key advantage of UCNPs is their ability to quantitatively evaluate EET through lifetime analysis, as the emitters exhibit a long lifetime of ∼500 μs. In addition, UCNPs minimize the occurrence of S1-S1 annihilation process, given that their emission quantum yield is few percentages, resulting in a small number of excited MEH-PPV molecules.

The emitters in the UCNPs used in this study are thulium (Tm) ions (Fig. 1, A and B). Those located at the surface are generally quenched, whereas Tm ions within a core region, ranging 20 to 24 nm in diameter, are typically considered for fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) (27). The UCNPs were covered by solid films of a known conducting polymer, MEH-PPV, and photoexcited using continuous-wave (CW) laser light at 976 nm. Given that the UCNPs emit discrete ultraviolet (UV) and visible (VIS) emission bands, nanometer-sized light sources emitting VIS photons could be produced by near-infrared (NIR) irradiation without direct photoexcitation of the conjugated polymer. These nanoscale light sources enabled local photoexcitation of the MEH-PPV film through EET from the UCNPs to the surrounding conjugated polymer chains, followed by energy migration (Fig. 1, C to E). Spectrally selected luminescence microscopy (Fig. 1F) provided emission images of individual UCNPs and corresponding emission spots of the conjugated polymer film. In the following report, we evaluate the local excitation process through the EET from the UCNP to the surrounding conjugated polymer as well as emission behaviors of the emitters in single UCNPs as nanoscale excitation sources. We then demonstrate real space evaluation of EET length, ranging from a few nanometers up to 55 nm, from the spatial distribution of individual emission spots of the conjugated polymer solid.

RESULTS

Figure 2A shows the steady-state luminescence spectrum of Yb3+- and Tm3+-doped core-shell upconverting nanoparticles (TmUCNP) in cyclohexane sandwiched with two well-cleaned coverslips excited at 976 nm (the spectrum was obtained using the wide-field setup with the imaging spectrometer; see Materials and Methods). The two narrow main emission bands at 450 and 474 nm overlap well with the broad absorption spectrum of the MEH-PPV film, as shown in Fig. 2B. EET from the TmUCNP to MEH-PPV can thus be expected. Considering that the TmUCNPs are covered by a 3- to 5-nm inert shell and with oleic acid, Förster-type (through space) EET is anticipated as the most plausible mechanism. The fluorescence spectrum of MEH-PPV in the solid state is red-shifted from that in solution (31), implying the formation of emission sites at lower energy originating from the aggregation of the chromophores of MEH-PPV and energy migration within the MEH-PPV film from a photoexcited chromophore to such an emissive site. The fluorescence decay curve of the MEH-PPV film at 610 nm is shown in Fig. 2C with fitting result using a triexponential function, providing lifetimes of 0.12 (29%), 0.25 (59%), and 0.60 (12%) ns with a χ2 value of 1.1. The amplitude-weighted averaged lifetime of the MEH-PPV film is 0.26 ns. A fluorescence decay curve of MEH-PPV in a chloroform solution (before drop-casting) is also shown in Fig. 2C as a green line. Although the fluorescence decay curve of the solution is monoexponential like, it required biexponential function for accurate fitting with lifetimes of 0.18 (19%) and 0.32 (81%) ns, and the corresponding average lifetime is 0.29 ns. Compared to the fluorescence decay curve of the solution, that of the solid film exhibits both shorter and longer lifetimes resulting from aggregation. The shorter lifetime suggests the migration of excitation energy within the solid film. To spectrally confirm that energy migration can take place in the film of MEH-PPV, we measured the time-resolved fluorescence spectra of the MEH-PPV film using the time-correlated single-photon counting (TCSPC) setup described above. Figure 2D shows the fluorescence spectra of the MEH-PPV film up to 5 ns after photoexcitation. Corresponding normalized spectra up to 1 ns are also shown in Fig. 2E, indicating that the center of mass (COM) of these fluorescence spectra shift to the longer-wavelength region with time. Figure 2F shows the time course of the COM of these fluorescence spectra in wave number. The spectral COM shifts from 16200 to 15900 cm−1 within 2 ns, the dynamics of which is roughly divided into faster and slower ones. The shift of the COM rapidly proceeds up to 200 ps after photoexcitation, followed by relatively slow spectral shift up to 2 ns. This shift of the spectral COM qualitatively indicates that the averaged photon energy of the emission exhibited a red shift with time, suggesting energy migration in the MEH-PPV film. Thus, the detection of fluorescence from a low-energy emission site after energy migration in the MEH-PPV solid is expected to occur as illustrated in Fig. 1 (D and E).

For further analysis of the EET from the single TmUCNPs to the surrounding MEH-PPV in the solid phase, we separately detected luminescence from the UCNPs and MEH-PPV under 976-nm CW photoexcitation by using optical bandpass filters. Figure 3D shows a luminescence image of single TmUCNPs surrounded by the MEH-PPV film, obtained using the EMCCD camera with an exposure time of 31 ms and a detection wavelength range of <532 nm. Figure 3E shows the luminescence image for the same area in the 545- to 620-nm range, obtained using the EMCCD camera with an exposure time of 500 ms, which corresponds to the fluorescence from the MEH-PPV solid film. The positions of individual bright spots in Fig. 3 (D and E) correspond to each other, confirming that the NIR excitation of the UCNPs leads to the luminescence of MEH-PPV. Figure 3F shows time traces of the luminescence intensities of a TmUCNP and the corresponding emission spot for MEH-PPV. As typically shown in the figure, the TmUCNPs and corresponding emission spots of MEH-PPV did not show blinking. These nonblinking luminescence behaviors can be ascribed to the presence of multiple Tm ions in a single TmUCNP and multiple neighboring chromophores in MEH-PPV. Figure 3G shows a correlation plot between luminescence intensities of single TmUCNPs and the corresponding MEH-PPV emissive spots, yielding a negative correlation coefficient of −0.40. Although the absolute value of the correlation coefficient is not very large, the overall negative correlation suggests that a dim TmUCNP exhibited efficient EET to MEH-PPV and vice versa. However, the weak negative correlation implies that, in addition to EET, other photophysical processes contribute to the change in the luminescence intensity of the UCNPs and the corresponding MEH-PPV emissive spots. Individual emitters (Tm ions) involved in the EET near the surface of the nanoparticles can experience different microenvironments owing to the intrinsic heterogeneity of the nanoparticles, including variations in the concentration of neighboring sensitizers, lattice mismatches, and defects acting as energy traps. The MEH-PPV film, which serves as the energy acceptor, can also exhibit intrinsic inhomogeneity, such as spatial variations in the concentration of chromophores and trap sites acting as quenchers. Owing to the aforementioned heterogeneous nature of both the UCNP and MEH-PPV film, the luminescence intensities of a TmUCNP and the corresponding emissive spot of the MEH-PPV film vary from particle to particle. These variations can further lead to differences in the EET efficiency from individual TmUCNPs to the surrounding MEH-PPV. As a result, the magnitude of the correlation coefficient was relatively small.

To further evaluate the EET from TmUCNPs to the MEH-PPV film, we measured the luminescence lifetime of TmUCNPs on a coverslip (without MEH-PPV) and those surrounded by the MEH-PPV film (with MEH-PPV). Figure 4A shows emission decay curves at 473 nm for the ensemble of TmUCNPs on a glass surface and those embedded in an MEH-PPV solid film. The emission of the TmUCNPs in the presence of MEH-PPV exhibited faster decay than that without MEH-PPV. As these decay curves include contributions from multiple TmUCNPs, they are intrinsically multiexponential. The curves were analyzed with multiple exponential functions (typically, three components), and average luminescence lifetimes were determined. The corresponding emission lifetimes of the TmUCNPs in Fig. 4A thus obtained are 672 and 539 μs, respectively. The shortened emission lifetime of the TmUCNPs in the presence of MEH-PPV is consistent with the EET from the nanoparticles to the conjugated polymer solid. It should be stressed that changes in the refractive index of the surrounding medium can affect the luminescence lifetime of TmUCNPs, and such effects are therefore inherently included in the present lifetime measurements. To evaluate this effect in the present system, we measured the luminescence lifetimes of TmUCNPs deposited on cover glasses in different surrounding media: air (refractive index, n = 1.0), water (n = 1.33), and oil (n = 1.52). The corresponding results are presented in section S8, indicating that changes in the refractive index slightly modify the luminescence lifetime. The magnitude of the effect is however much smaller than the lifetime changes observed in the MEH-PPV films. By using the rate constants for luminescence (kl), nonradiative relaxation (knr), and EET (kEET), the ensemble-averaged luminescence lifetime of the TmUCNPs with the MEH-PPV film [with conjugated polymer (wCP)], τwCP, can be expressed by τwCP=1/(kl+knr+kEET). Meanwhile, the ensemble-averaged luminescence lifetime of the TmUCNPs without MEH-PPV, τwoCP, can be expressed by τwoCP=1/(kl+knr). From the above values of luminescence lifetime with and without MEH-PPV, kEET is determined to be 3.7 × 102 s−1.

Figure 4B shows typical luminescence decay curves of single TmUCNPs with and without MEH-PPV. As expected from the data of the particle ensemble shown in Fig. 4A, the single TmUCNPs covered by the MEH-PPV film exhibited shorter luminescence lifetimes than bare TmUCNPs on the coverslip (without MEH-PPV). The luminescence lifetime of the bare TmUCNPs without MEH-PPV varied from particle to particle, associated with the nonlinear response of the upconversion emission (34, 35). In addition, as shown in Fig. 3, individual UCNPs surrounded by MEH-PPV appeared to exhibit different EET efficiencies that can be ascribed to the inhomogeneity of the microscopic properties of the MEH-PPV film, as well as the intrinsically nonlinear excited-state dynamics of the UCNPs. Owing to these heterogeneities, the distribution of lifetimes for single UCNPs is scattered as shown in Fig. 4E. However, the average luminescence lifetime with MEH-PPV, τw (= 400 μs), is shorter than that without MEH-PPV, τwo (= 465 μs), suggesting EET from single TmUCNPs to the surrounding MEH-PPV film, which is consistent with the data shown in Fig. 3C. Using the average lifetimes, 400 and 465 μs, the average rate constant of EET, kEET_sp is determined to be 3.5 × 102 s−1 for the single particles.

Figure 4 (C and D) shows the typical time courses of luminescence intensity of the TmUCNPs without (Fig. 4C) and with (Fig. 4D) MEH-PPV. As described earlier in Fig. 3F, the single TmUCNPs did not show photoblinking, regardless of the presence of MEH-PPV. Figure 4F shows the distribution of temporally averaged luminescence intensity (counts/0.1 s) for the single TmUCNPs with and without MEH-PPV. The average luminescence intensity for TmUCNPs with MEH-PPV (112 counts/0.1 s) is smaller than that without MEH-PPV (124 counts/0.1 s), which is consistent with the shorter luminescence lifetimes of the UCNPs surrounded by the MEH-PPV film. The ratio of the averaged luminescence lifetimes of the single TmUCNPs with and without MEH-PPV is 400/465 = 0.86, in agreement with the ratio of the luminescence intensity with and without MEH-PPV that is 112/124 = 0.90. This consistency supports the validity of the measurements and is consistent with the occurrence of EET.

To then evaluate the EET length from the individual TmUCNPs to the surrounding MEH-PPV, we analyzed the spectrally separated luminescence images using the localization method. Before analyzing luminescence spots of TmUCNPs in MEH-PPV films, we first analyzed luminescence images of bare TmUCNPs on a coverslip to investigate their intrinsic emission behavior at the single-particle level; an image of this sample is shown in Fig. 3A. Figure 5A shows localized positions for 200 video frames of a single bare TmUCNP on a coverslip analyzed with 2D Gaussian, and the centroids of the luminescence spots were determined for each video frame; the centroids of the 2D distribution for the 200 localized points were set at the origin (x, y) = (0, 0) in Fig. 5 (A and B). An example of this localization process is shown in sections S3 and S4 and movie S1. All of the localized positions are plotted in Fig. 5A; analyzed data for every 20 frames of emission video are exhibited with different colors. Compared with a 2D distribution of the localized positions for a single quantum dot in the polymer solid dispersed within a 9-nm diameter for the same setup (standard deviation of x and y positions are 2.0 and 2.2 nm, respectively, as shown in section S2) (36), the xy distribution in Fig. 5A shows a wider spatial distribution, suggesting that the TmUCNP contains multiple emission sites. Figure 5B shows the localized positions of the single TmUCNP for the initial 20 frames. The numbers, 1 to 20, next to each localized point correspond to the number of the video frames. The localized emission position of the TmUCNP fluctuated randomly from frame to frame, suggesting stochastic variations in the emissive regions within the nanoparticle.

These results further support the achievement of localized nanoexcitation in the present system. On the basis of the emission positions distributed within a circle of ∼30 nm in diameter, the effective excitation area is expected to be comparable to the size of the UCNP. In other words, the size of the TmUCNP is expected to be comparable to the spatial extent of the local photoexcitation.

To discuss the value of EET length, we define the EET length in the present study as follows. The spatial distribution of the emission positions of a TmUCNP is localized within a circular area with a diameter of ∼30 nm. Considering this, the excitation region of MEH-PPV projected onto two dimensions should be located somewhere within the 30-nm circle. Given that it is not possible to determine the exact position within the circle where the EET to MEH-PPV occurred, we here represent the starting point of EET by the centroid of the TmUCNP emission points. Assuming that the centroid position of the MEH-PPV emission spots corresponds to that of the TmUCNP emission, the resulting distribution of EET distances is given by the histogram shown in red bars in Fig. 6C. According to this definition, the EET length obtained in this study reaches a maximum of ∼55 nm.

Given that the localization error depends on the SNR of a luminescence spot, the localization error is inversely proportional to the SNR; we compared xy distributions of localization positions for MEH-PPV emission spots with different SNRs. The emission spots of MEH-PPV with a higher SNR (8.4; Fig. 6D) exhibited a wider spatial distribution than those with a lower SNR (6.3; Fig. 6E), indicating that the observed distribution is unlikely to originate from localization uncertainty associated with SNR and more likely reflects differences in EET length. This control experiment further supports the interpretation that the observed spatial distribution of MEH-PPV emission spots reflects excitation energy migration. The distributions of the distance from the origin (centroids of xy distributions) to each localization point are also shown in Fig. 6C for 20 nanoparticles. Although the sizes of the single TmUCNPs slightly vary particle by particle, the EET length can be estimated from several nanometers to 55 nm.

DISCUSSION

We have shown that TmUCNPs worked as nanometric light sources for local photoexcitation to study EET in the solid films of MEH-PPV. The TmUCNPs selectively photoexcited with the 976-nm CW laser via stepwise multiphoton excitation exhibited strong blue (at 450 and 474 nm) and weak red (at 640 nm) narrow peak luminescence in the MEH-PPV film. The fluorescence of the MEH-PPV films was detected only from the positions corresponding to the TmUCNPs, suggesting that the excitation energy of the TmUCNPs was transferred to the surrounding chromophores of the MEH-PPV, and fluorescence attributed to the low-energy sites in the MEH-PPV films was observed. This EET interpretation was further supported by the time-resolved luminescence lifetime measurements of both the ensemble and single-particle TmUCNPs. The time-resolved measurements determined the rate of EET from TmUCNP to MEH-PPV to be ∼4 × 102 s−1. Under the condition where the donor-acceptor distance is equal to the Förster radius, the EET rate kEET is expressed by the following equation, kEET=1/τD (1). Here, τD is the luminescence lifetime of an energy donor. This simple equation estimates the EET rate from the TmUCNPs to the MEH-PPV solid films at 2 × 103 s−1 with a value of 500 μs as τD; the value is much, almost one order, larger than the experimental data. The relatively small rate constant observed in the experiments may be related to the limited number of Tm ions participating in the EET process within each TmUCNP. One possible interpretation is that Tm ions located near the particle surface contribute preferentially to the EET, whereas Tm ions inside the particles are less affected. As a result, the overall luminescence lifetime of each TmUCNP may not be strongly influenced by the EET process.

We also discuss the localized positions of the emissive sites in the TmUCNPs and in the MEH-PPV films. Figure 5 shows the temporal evolution of the localized position in a single TmUCNP. It should be noted here that each localized position does not necessarily correspond to a single emitting Tm ion. A TmUCNP contains multiple Tm ions, and the excited states of the Tm ions are produced through stepwise multiphoton absorption of the NIR photons. Because this excitation process is expected to occur stochastically, the luminescence observed in each video frame is considered to be a superposition of photons emitted from different Tm ions. The temporal fluctuation of the localized position is therefore ascribed to the temporal change of the COM of Tm ions emitting within the exposure time of charge-coupled device (CCD) detection. The same discussion applies to the emission spots of the MEH-PPV solid film. The localized position of each luminescence spot does not necessarily correspond to the single emissive (low-energy) site of the MEH-PPV film. As shown in Fig. 5, the emission position of the TmUCNP fluctuates randomly over time, suggesting that the MEH-PPV chromophores are also excited randomly around the nanoparticle. Given that the MEH-PPV film consists of many aggregated polymer chains containing numerous closely spaced chromophores, a large number of possible energy-transfer pathways may exist. The positions of the red circular symbols in Fig. 6 may represent either localized emissive sites reached through individual pathways or averaged positions of multiple emitting chromophores located in close proximity to one another. This interpretation is supported by the fact that simultaneous emission from chromophores separated by large distances would produce a superposition of two point spread functions, resulting in a distorted spot shape and an increased spot width compared to that of a single chromophore. However, such behavior was not observed experimentally. On the other hand, the spot width is strongly affected by fluctuations in the focal position along the z direction, and therefore, when luminescence images from chromophores that were separated by several nanometers to several tens of nanometers overlap spatially, it is practically difficult to accurately estimate the interchromophore distances solely from the spot width. Considering these points, the observed temporal changes in the MEH-PPV emission spot positions are likely to contain spatial information related to the energy-transfer pathways, including their temporal evolution. Thus, the 2D map of multiple localized emission positions can provide spatial information related to the EET length in the conducting polymer.

The EET length in the MEH-PPV film was thus determined in the range from several nanometers to ∼55 nm from the superresolved imaging results. In the present measurements, three-dimensional EET processes are projected onto a 2D plane, suggesting that the EET lengths along the optical axis are underestimated and that the averaged EET length derived from the histogram in Fig. 6C may therefore also be underestimated. However, even if such underestimation modifies the EET-length histogram, the maximum EET length is expected to remain essentially unchanged, because it is primarily determined by EET pathways parallel to the objective (xy) plane in wide-field imaging. Therefore, the average EET length should still be shorter than 55 nm. This EET length is somewhat shorter than the previously reported EET distance of 60 nm for solid MEH-PPV films (12). One possible reason is that, in previous reports, fluorescence from chromophores emitting around 630 nm was measured and generally recognized as trap sites of MEH-PPV. On the other hand, it was not detected in the present measurements. In other words, because the present study predominantly detects emission from intermediate steps of energy migration toward more stabilized trap sites emitting at 630 nm, the EET lengths obtained in our study are considered to be somewhat shorter than the values reported previously.

Using UCNP is a very simple and effective approach for nanometric excitation of materials in the solid phase. Wavelength can be modulated by the choice of emitter and NIR irradiation power. We foresee a wide variety of applications of this nanometric excitation method combined with superresolved localization microscopy to visualize excitation migration in the nanospace, such as the measurement of EET length and direction for supramolecular systems, molecular assemblies, or organic/hybrid solar cells, leading to the detailed understanding of the EET in such molecular systems toward the rational design of highly efficient photofunctional chemical systems.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Samples

TmUCNPs dispersed in cyclohexane [Hefei Fluonano Biotech, NaReYF4:Yb, Tm/NaYF4, upconversion nanocrystals, emission λem at 450 and 474 nm (main peaks), 5 mg/ml, particle diameter of 30 nm including the shell, shell thickness of 3 to 5 nm] were used as nanometer-sized excitation sources. The TmUCNPs are covered with oleic acid to stabilize their dispersion. Multiple Yb3+ in the ground state in a TmUCNP absorb photons at 976 nm and transfer their excitation energy to neighboring Tm ions. Individual Tm ions receive the excitation energy from the surrounding Yb3+, and higher excited states such as 1G4, 1D2, and 1I6 are produced, followed by the discrete emission resulting from radiative transitions from these excited states in the UV (343 nm) and VIS (450, 474, 510 and 642 nm) regions, as shown in Fig. 1A. (27, 28) The conjugated polymer MEH-PPV [average Mn (number-average molecular weight) = 40,000 to 70,000; Mw (weight-average molecular weight)/Mn = ∼6] was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used without further purification.

The coverslips used in this study were cleaned in the following manner. Coverslips (Matsunami NEO) soaked in acetone underwent ultrasonic cleaning for 30 min. The coverslips were then rinsed with ultrapure water and soaked in a 5% aqueous solution of sodium hydroxide for 60 min, followed by rinsing with ultrapure water and drying with Ar (argon) gas blow. The surfaces of the dried coverslips were further cleaned using a UV-ozone cleaner (SEN Lights Corp.) for 60 min.

The colloidal solution of the TmUCNP (25 μl) was thus spin-casted onto a cleaned coverslip at 2000 rpm for 120 s, resulting in sparsely dispersed TmUCNPs on the coverslip. A saturated toluene solution of the MEH-PPV (20 μl) was drop-casted onto the cleaned coverslip with the TmUCNPs and dried under ambient condition for several hours to obtain an ∼50- to 100-μm-thick MEH-PPV solid film covering the TmUCNPs on the coverslip.

Optical setups

The emission of the TmUCNP and MEH-PPV was observed by a laboratory-built wide-field microscope consisting of an inverted microscope (Olympus IX71) and a CW NIR laser at 976 nm (Thorlabs BL976-SAG300). An objective lens with a high NA [UPlanFLN Oil Iris (100×/NA = 1.30), Olympus] was used for focusing the excitation NIR laser and collecting the emission from the sample. The NIR laser beam was introduced to the optical microscope through a pair of plano-convex lenses and reflected toward the objective lens by a dichroic mirror (Semrock, FF647-SDi01) for epi-illumination of an ∼40-μm area in the sample plane. The excitation intensity of the NIR laser at the samples was typically 4.7 kW cm−2. The emission of the TmUCNPs and the MEH-PPV film was detected as video images with an EMCCD camera (ImagEM, C9100-13, Hamamatsu Photonics). To block the NIR light propagating to the EMCCD camera, we used two short-pass filters (Semrock, SP01-785RU, and Semrock, FF01-950SP). For spectrally separated emission imaging in the wavelength ranges of <532 nm and 545 to 620 nm, we used the following optical filters: a short-pass filter for 532 nm (Semrock, SP01-532RU) and a band-pass filter for 545 to 620 nm (Semrock, FF01-582/75). The localization accuracy of the imaging setup was evaluated by imaging a single quantum dot embedded in a poly(methyl methacrylate) film. The standard deviations of the localized x and y positions of the quantum dot, shown in fig. S2 of the Supplementary Materials, were 2.0 and 2.2 nm, respectively, suggesting that the imaging system is suitable for evaluating EET lengths on the order of several nanometers to several tens of nanometers. Details of this evaluation are provided in section S2. When measuring the emission spectra of the single TmUCNPs and surrounding MEH-PPV excited through the energy transfer from the TmUCNPs, an imaging spectrometer (CLP-50, Bunkoukeiki) was used in the detection pathway.

The emission lifetime of the ensemble of TmUCNPs was measured using the same inverted microscope above. The CW NIR (976 nm) laser beam was slightly focused by an objective (UPlanFLN Oil Iris, 100×, NA = 1.30, Olympus) to TmUCNPs on a coverslip. The sample was prepared by drop-casting to increase the concentration of the TmUCNPs for bright luminescence. The intensity of the NIR laser was temporally modulated to square-wave pulses at 100 Hz by inputting corresponding square-wave pulses to the driver of the laser using a function generator. The width of each square pulse was 5 ms, and its rising and falling time was ∼2 μs, which was short enough for measuring emission lifetime in the order of several hundreds of microseconds. The time profile of luminescence intensity for the TmUCNPs under photoirradiation with the 976-nm square-wave pulses was detected and recorded with a photomultiplier tube (H9307-03, Hamamatsu Photonics) connected with a digital oscilloscope (DS-5652A, Iwatsu Electric).

The single-particle emission dynamics was measured by a custom-made hyperspectral lifetime imaging setup. For excitation of UCNPs, an LDH laser head at 980 nm is used, which can be operated in CW mode or pulse squared mode with the Taiko PDL M1 controller (PicoQuant). For the generation of millisecond pulse square excitation, the Taiko controller was triggered (fast gate mode) by a square pulse generated by a delay generator (DG; SRS, model DG535). The same DG was used as a trigger signal (every 5 ms) (i) for the synchronization channel of a Time Correlate Single Photon Card (Time Harp 260, long-range mode with a bin resolution of 1 ns) working with multistop capability mode and (ii) to gate an intensified EMCCD (emICCD) camera. The laser beam is directed into an Olympus microscope (IX71), and the light emitted, reflected, or scattered from the sample is collected with a dichroic mirror (Semrock, 670-nm edge BrightLine multiphoton short-pass dichroic beamsplitter) through an oil-immersion objective lens (Olympus, UPLSAPO 60×, NA = 1.42). Emitted light is collected through the same objective and dichroic and filtered using a 720 nm short-pass (Semrock, 720-nm blocking edge BrightLine multiphoton short-pass emission filter) filter before being redirected (10/90 beam-splitter cube) to a single-photon counting avalanche photodiode (MPD, 50 μm) and a multigrating versatility monochromator [Princeton Instruments IsoPlane 160 equipped with two gratings (300 g/mm) blazed at 300 and 750 nm] coupled with an intensified CCD camera (emICCD; 1024 by 1024 pixels, PI Max4 Princeton Instruments). To acquire confocal images, a piezo-scanning stage (P-562.3CD, Digital controller E-725.3CD, Physik Instrument), controlled via Symphotime 64 software, is used to scan the sample in x, y, and z directions over an area of 160 μm by 160 μm. After scanning the sample and imaging single UCNPs, time-resolved luminescence measurements were performed by time-gating the emICCD camera (triggered by the DG) with an excitation duration of 2 ms every 5 ms.

Time-resolved fluorescence spectra of MEH-PPV films were obtained through TCSPC measurement for different detection wavelengths. The TCSPC setup was already described in our previous report (37). Here, a femtosecond Ti:sapphire laser (Tsunami BB, Spectra Physics, pulse duration of 70 fs, repetition rate of 80 MHz, wavelength tunable range of 700 to 1000 nm) was used as an excitation light source. The output wavelength from the femtosecond laser was tuned to 880 nm, and its repetition rate was decreased to 8 MHz using an electro-optic modulator (Conoptics, M360-80 & 25D). These 880-nm pulses were focused into a photonic crystal fiber (NL-PM-750, NKT Photonics) to generate white light continuum with a peak at 540 nm. Using an optical band-pass filter transmitting from 519 to 542 nm (Semrock, FF02-531/22), green laser pulses ranging from 519 to 542 nm were prepared and used for photoexcitation of the MEH-PPV films. The fluorescence photons were detected at a magic angle configuration using a polarizer and a half-wave plate. A photomultiplier tube (Hamamatsu, R3809U-50) with an amplifier (Hamamatsu, C5594) and a TCSPC card (PicoQuant, PicoHarp 300) were used for the signal detection. A monochromator (Princeton Instruments, Acton 2150) was placed in front of the photomultiplier tube for acquiring spectrally selected fluorescence decay curves. The system response time was determined to be ∼40 ps (full width at half maximum) from scattered light of a scratched glass plate. For construction of time-resolved fluorescence spectra, the fluorescence decay of MEH-PPV films was measured at 25 different wavelengths with intervals of 10 nm from 550 to 800 nm.
