Adverse childhood experiences and ICD-11 complex posttraumatic stress disorder in Poland: a population-based study
Authors: Marcin Rzeszutek, Małgorzata Dragan, Maja Lis-Turlejska, Katarzyna Schier, Joanna Kowalkowska, Katarzyna Drabarek, Angelika Van Hoy, Paweł Holas, Dominika Maison, Gabriela Wdowczyk, Elżbieta Litwin, Julia Wawrzyniak, Wiktoria Znamirowska, Szymon Szumiał, Małgorzata Desmond
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Research on the prevalence of ICD-11-based complex posttraumatic stress disorder (CPTSD) in nationally representative samples is lacking, with no such studies conducted considering national Polish samples until now.Objectives: The primary aim of this study was to explore profiles of study participants in terms of either PTSD or CPTSD symptoms regarding to ICD-11 diagnosis with the aid of latent profile analysis. We also investigated the relationship between the types and cumulative number of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) with the probability of CPTSD rate in a national sample of Poles.Methods: A representative sample of Polish adults (n = 3,557) participated in this study. ACEs were assessed using the Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaire, while the core features of PTSD and CPTSD were measured using the International Trauma Questionnaire.Results: The findings showed a 11% prevalence rate of probable CPTSD in the studied sample of Poles. Sexual abuse, emotional neglect, physical neglect and having the experience of a mentally ill household member were the most significant predictors of potential diagnosis. Furthermore, significant differences concerning lifestyle data were observed in the group of participants with CPTSD.Conclusions: The current rate of probable CPTSD in Poland is substantially higher than the respective data reported for other countries. We also noted that the ICD-11 diagnosis of CPTSD may not apply to all cultural environments. Finally, it was observed that the ACEs – CPTSD association may depend both on the ACEs types as well as on their overall cumulative character.KEYWORDS: Adverse childhood experiences, trauma, complex posttraumatic stress disorder, posttraumatic stress disorder, ICD-11

The 11th edition of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11; World Health Organization [WHO], 2018) introduced a new diagnosis of complex posttraumatic stress disorder (CPTSD). This is regarded as a milestone in psychotraumatology because, after decades of controversial debates on the unique consequences of experiencing complex traumatic events, this edition established CPTSD as a distinct clinical entity, separate from single-event PTSD (Brewin et al., 2017; Cloitre et al., 2013; Herman, 1992; Maercker et al., 2022). Specifically, in ICD-11, there are now two sibling diagnoses: PTSD and CPTSD, with substantial differences in their underlying symptoms (WHO, 2018). In particular, PTSD comprises six symptoms that can be grouped into three clusters: (a) re-experiencing trauma in the here and now (Re), (b) deliberate avoidance of traumatic reminders (Av), and (c) a sense of current threat (Th). In contrast, CPTSD encompasses the PTSD symptoms but also includes three additional symptoms grouped under the disturbances in self-organisation (DSO) dimension. DSO describes posttraumatic reactions following chronic and repetitive traumatisation, often interpersonal in nature and experienced in childhood, such as child abuse. These symptoms are: (a) affective dysregulation, (b) negative self-concept, and (c) disturbed relationships (WHO, 2018). Several authors have provided empirical evidence on the discriminant validity of CPTSD, highlighting its distinctiveness from PTSD (e.g. Barbieri et al., 2019; Choi & Cho, 2020; Hyland et al., 2018; Rink & Lipinska, 2020). However, the construct validity of CPTSD remains a matter of ongoing debate. On one hand, the most common factorial solution from confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) points to a two-factor higher-order model (i.e. PTSD and DSO; see Ben-Ezra et al., 2018; Vallières et al., 2018). On the other hand, studies using latent profile analysis (LPA) and latent class analysis (LCA) have proposed a three-class solution (Kazlauskas et al., 2018), a four-class structure (Perkonigg et al., 2016), and even a six-factor model for CPTSD (Ho et al., 2019). Other studies have failed to identify a unitary structure of CPTSD (Eidhof et al., 2019; Rzeszutek et al., 2020). Authors who applied factor mixture modelling approaches have also provided rather mixed findings on this issue (e.g. Redican et al., 2022; Wolf et al., 2015). A recent review identified that one reason for the ongoing issues with confirming the construct validity of CPTSD is cross-cultural variation in its phenomenology, particularly the versatile understanding of the DSO concept across different cultures (Heim et al., 2022). Therefore, the fundamental question of whether the inclusion of CPTSD has actually reduced diagnostic comorbidity and led to greater parsimony in the diagnostic criteria remains unanswered (Rod & Schmidt, 2021; Resick et al., 2012).

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) refer to a wide range of inter-correlated negative or potentially traumatic life events that occur during childhood and adolescence (see e.g. child maltreatment, abuse and neglect, household dysfunctions; Felitti et al., 1998). They are a well-established risk factor for numerous mental health problems in adulthood, including depression, anxiety, substance abuse, and personality disorders (e.g. Heim et al., 2010; Herzog & Schmahl, 2018; Karatzias et al., 2020; Merrick et al., 2017). There is also ample evidence linking ACEs to elevated risk of PTSD and CPTSD in adulthood (e.g. Frewen et al., 2019; Ho et al., 2021; Karatzias et al., 2020; Sölva et al., 2020). In this regard, a recent meta-analysis by Leiva-Bianchi et al. (2023) showed that, out of various ACEs, the main risk factors for CPTSD are sexual and physical abuse and emotional neglect during childhood.

However, the nature of the ACEs – CPTSD relationship – whether particular ACE types or the cumulative effect of ACEs impact CPTSD probability in adulthood – is yet to be verified (Cloitre, 2021; Leiva-Bianchi et al., 2023). Also, what remains to be examined are gender and age differences with respect to ACEs – CPTSD association (Lonnen & Paskell, 2024; McGinty et al., 2021). Moreover, since the majority of previous studies were conducted using small, convenience samples, there is a paucity of population-based studies on the ACE – CPTSD association (Choi et al., 2021; Cloitre et al., 2020).

Finally, the empirical evidence also shows that ACEs can be closely related to enduring brain dysfunctions that, in turn, affect physical and mental health throughout the lifespan (Herzog & Schmahl, 2018). More specifically, ACEs were found to be a prominent risk factor for many physical health problems; for example, functional somatic syndromes or cardiovascular diseases (Baldwin et al., 2021; Qiao et al., 2024). Due to their strong emotional impact that persists throughout the lifespan and influences people’s behaviours, ACEs may constitute an important risk factor for adopting unhealthy lifestyles in adulthood. For example, Ho et al. (2021) found that ICD-11 CPTSD symptoms mediate the associations between ACEs and physical health problems during adulthood. Furthermore, Brugiavini et al. (2023), in the SHARE study conducted in 19 European countries, showed that ACEs are related to i.e. adult smoking and obesity. Thus, there is a need for further studies on the long-term health-related impact of ACEs in adulthood.

Current study

Taking the aforementioned research gaps into consideration, the main aim of this study was to explore profiles of the participants in terms of either PTSD or CPTSD symptoms with respect to ICD-11 diagnosis, as well as to investigate the relationship between the types and number of ACEs and the probability of CPTSD in a national sample of Poles. The aforementioned associations were accounted for with respect to sociodemographic variables, as well as selected data on the lifestyle patterns of the participants (see: professional activity, obesity, active smoking, and sleep deprivation; see Table 1). Method

Participants and procedure

Data was collected from January to February 2024. The participants were recruited through a nationwide research panel in Poland. The study questionnaires were distributed among the participants through an online platform. Notably, participation was both voluntary and anonymous. All participants provided informed consent and earned points for their participation, which they could exchange for money later if they completed more questionnaires, or they could choose an eBook as a reward.

Overall, the representative sample included 3557 Polish adults aged 18–99 (M = 46.90; SD = 15.93). Table 1 summarises the sociodemographic characteristics and the selected data on lifestyle patterns of the participants.

More than half of the sample (56%) were female. The majority of the participants lived in a village and were fully employed, with the most prevalent level of education being secondary. In most cases, the number of inhabitants in a household was two.

To ensure representativeness, the sample structure was stratified to reflect the overall population demographics of Poland, as noted in the annual report of Statistics Poland (Główny Urząd Statystyczny [GUS], 2023). Measures

Adverse childhood experiences

To investigate the number and the types of ACEs experienced by the participants we used the Polish version of the Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaire (ACE-10; Felitti et al., 1998), adapted to Polish by Makaruk et al. (2018). ACE-10 is a retrospective self-report tool designed to assess potentially traumatic childhood experiences. This 10-item survey assesses 10 specific types of childhood adversity, with the questions divided into two main categories. The first category addresses child maltreatment experiences, including physical abuse, verbal abuse, sexual abuse, physical neglect, and emotional neglect. The second category pertains to household dysfunctions, such as having an alcoholic parent, a mother subjected to domestic violence, a family member who is incarcerated, a family member with a mental illness, and the loss of a parent due to divorce, death, or abandonment. ACE-10 determines a total score by summing the number of ‘yes’ responses, resulting in an index ranging from 0 to 10, which indicates the accumulation of different adversities experienced during childhood. The questionnaire has been validated in nonclinical and clinical samples and demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency and evidence of its convergent validity with the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ; Schmidt et al., 2020). The reliability of ACE-10 in the current study, as measured by Cronbach’s alpha, was found to be satisfactory (see Table 3).

International trauma questionnaire

The International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ; Cloitre et al., 2018; Polish by adaptation by Draczynska et al. (2023)) was used to evaluate the core features of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and complex PTSD (CPTSD). This questionnaire was adapted to Polish by Draczynska et al. (2023). Participants filled out the questionnaire just as they did for the previous tool, i.e. the Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaire, which surveyed the potential ACEs experienced by them. The ITQ contains 18 items: avoidance (Av), re-experiencing (Re), feelings of fear (Th), and symptoms of complex post-traumatic stress disorder (CPTSD), such as emotional dysregulation, negative self-image, and disruptions in relationships. Respondents are asked to rate statements based on the extent to which they experienced these typical PTSD symptoms on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (definitely no) to 4 (definitely yes). A potential diagnosis of PTSD (no clinical diagnosis was conducted) requires the individual to score at least 2 points for at least one question from the Re category, one question from the Av category, and one question from the Th category. Therefore, with regard to this questionnaire, PTSD symptom scores range from 0 to 24 (i.e. sum of the six items from each subscale), and CPTSD symptom scores range from 0 to 48 (i.e. sum of the 12 ITQ items). Furthermore, according to the ICD-11 diagnostic criteria, an individual can only receive a diagnosis of PTSD or CPTSD, not both. The reliability of this questionnaire, indicated by Cronbach’s alpha, was found to be adequate as well (see Table 3).

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in six stages. First, the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants were analysed. Second, the descriptive statistics of the analysed interval variables and Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficients were assessed. In addition, Pearson’s correlation coefficients between all analysed variables were estimated. Third, latent profile analysis (LPA; Vermunt, 2017) was performed to examine the extracted profiles of the participants in terms of either elevated PTSD or CPTSD symptoms. The number of profiles to extract was decided on the basis of the lowest possible AIC (Akaike Information Criterion), BIC (Bayesian Information Criterion), and KIC (Kullback Information Criterion) fit indices values and the highest possible value of entropy. Fourth, based on the study sample and the data on the Polish population provided by the annual report of Statistics Poland (Główny Urząd Statystyczny [GUS], 2023), the maximum margins of error were calculated to verify the representativeness of the study sample (Moore & McCabe, 1999). The margins of error were employed to estimate the prevalence rate of ACEs and the CPTSD level in the Polish general population, controlling for the gender and age of the participants. These values, when added to and subtracted from the point estimate, formed the 95% confidence interval for the estimate pertaining to the entire population. The fifth stage of analysis was devoted to examining the types and number of ACEs as predictors of the probable CPTSD diagnosis using logistic regression analysis (Garson, 2016). Odds ratios (OR) were employed for interpreting the results, with a statistically significant OR value higher than 1 indicating increased odds of CPTSD. The final stage of analysis focused on investigating associations between the CPTSD rate and the selected data on lifestyle patterns of the participants (professional activity, obesity, active smoking, and sleep disturbance), aided by Pearson’s chi-squared test for independence. The calculations were performed with the use of IBM SPSS Statistics 29.0 and Jamovi 2.3.28 software.

CPTSD construct assessment

The CPTSD construct in the context of the measurement conducted using the ITQ questionnaire was examined by implementing latent profile analysis (LPA). Based on the values of the fit indices, a three-class solution was achieved, with the values of the fit indices being AIC = 82468, BIC = 82628 and KIC = 82497. As a reference for extracting just one profile the fit indices were equal to AIC = 93936, BIC = 94010 and KIC = 93951 and for extracting two profiles the fit indices were equal to AIC = 84685, BIC = 84802 and KIC = 84707. The high value of entropy, i.e. 0.86, justified the solution further. In simulation studies, an entropy value of 0.84 or higher ensured at least 90% accuracy in correct assignments (Wang et al., 2017). Figure 1 depicts the acquired profiles.

Latent profiles of PTSD/CPTSD symptoms in the studied sample (n = 3557).

The three profiles were found to be distinct with regard to all PTSD/CPTSD symptoms, starting from the profile with all symptoms elevated (Class 1, n = 549), through the profile with all symptoms at the average level (Class 2, n = 1417), to the profile depicting low intensity of all symptoms (Class 3, n = 1591). No profiles with high CPTSD and low PTSD symptoms or with high PTSD and low CPTSD symptoms were detected.

This finding applies to both the entire sample (χ2 = 17.65, p < .001) and the male (χ2 = 8.78, p = .003) and female groups (χ2 = 63.76, p < .001). Furthermore, sleeping difficulty was significantly higher in the group of men with CPTSD (χ2 = 14.96, p < .001), while active smoking was significantly higher in the group of women with CPTSD (χ2 = 4.57, p = .033). The number of participants with obesity was also significantly higher in the group of participants with CPTSD. This also applied to the general sample (χ2 = 71.42, p < .001), as well as to both male (χ2 = 14.88, p < .001) and female participants (χ2 = 63.76, p < .001).

Discussion

Our study is among the few in the CPTSD literature so far (e.g. Choi et al., 2021; Cloitre et al., 2020) to examine the prevalence of ICD-11 CPTSD in a nationally representative population. The results showed an extraordinarily high level of CPTSD in the Polish population, estimated to be 11% (with 9%–13% confidence intervals), which is worth highlighting. This percentage is substantially higher than the global CPTSD prevalence, which ranges between 1% and 8% based on the results obtained from different countries (Maercker et al., 2022). The aforementioned concerning data are consistent with the findings of a recent population-based study conducted by Rzeszutek et al. (2023a) on PTSD prevalence in Poland, which estimated the prevalence to be 18.8% – a percentage that is significantly higher than those reported in comparable studies on PTSD prevalence worldwide (e.g. Atwoli et al., 2013; Gaviria et al., 2016; Kilpatrick et al., 2013; Trautmann & Wittchen, 2018). While there may be numerous reasons for such discrepancies, some authors have highlighted the role of the massive human and material losses experienced by Poles during World War II (WWII) as well as the repression and insecurity encountered during the subsequent Communist regime (Snyder, 2010) in this regard, indicating the potential, transgenerational transmission of trauma from that period (Rzeszutek et al., 2023b). In other words, Polish survivors of WWII may have faced significant challenges in sharing their traumatic memories with their communities, resulting in a lack of social acknowledgment for their WWII experiences. This, in turn, may have prevented them from mourning their losses properly, potentially passing unresolved trauma to subsequent Polish generations (Lis-Turlejska et al., 2018). However, this hypothesis requires further empirical investigation.

The results of this study did not support the first hypothesis, since we failed to identify PTSD and CPTSD profiles consistent with those postulated in the ICD-11 diagnosis (WHO, 2018). Notably, the separate diagnosis of CPTSD sparked much controversy several years before its formal introduction into ICD-11 (Maercker et al., 2013; Resick et al., 2012). One of the core arguments in favour of including CPTSD, apart from highlighting the unique consequences of experiencing complex traumatic events, has been the need to reduce diagnostic comorbidity above the DSM – 5 PTSD diagnosis (Cloitre et al., 2014). Although numerous researchers have proved the ICD-11 construct validity of CPTSD (Barbieri et al., 2019; Ben-Ezra et al., 2018; Hyland et al., 2018; Tian et al., 2020), others did not reach the same conclusions (Eidhof et al., 2019; Glück et al., 2016; Rzeszutek et al., 2020). In this context, a decade ago, Wolf et al. (2015) argued that PTSD and CPTSD profiles extracted through latent class analyses usually differ only in terms of the severity of symptoms and not necessarily by its specific types. In other words, the extent to which people with a CPTSD diagnosis are distinct from those suffering from PTSD because of displaying more ‘complex’ symptom profiles is still not entirely known (Brewin et al., 2017; Rod & Schmidt, 2021). Perhaps CPTSD and PTSD should be operationalised as two dimensions of the broad spectrum of trauma disorders, rather than as discrete clinical entities (Resick et al., 2012). However, in this context, one should bear in mind the cross-cultural variations in the phenomenology of CPTSD related to different understandings of DSO (Heim et al., 2022), which may shed additional light on the contradictory results related to the ICD-11 diagnosis of CPTSD.

The findings of our research were consistent with the second and third hypotheses, highlighting the association between the types and cumulative number of ACEs with the probability of CPTSD diagnosis in participants. In particular, the most significant predictive ACEs for CPTSD were found to be sexual abuse, emotional neglect, physical neglect and the experience of a household member being mentally ill. From the very beginning of the debate on the nature of CPTSD, it has been emphasised that this syndrome originates because of severe adverse stressors of a prolonged and repeated nature from which there is no possibility of escape (Herman, 1992). However, no consensus could be reached on whether separate complex traumatic events should be examined as distinct CPTSD predictors or if the focus should be on its multiple constellations (Maercker et al., 2013). Recently, it has been accepted that, rather than a single type of ACE, cumulative ACEs and their specific patterns are the primary risk factors for developing CPTSD (Brewin et al., 2017). The results of this study align also with those of a meta-analysis conducted by Leiva-Bianchi et al. (2023), who found that the main risk factors for CPTSD are sexual abuse and emotional neglect during childhood. However, we also detected the destructive role of physical neglect by early caregivers in childhood, along with the experience of a household member being mentally ill. In addition, the cumulative number of ACEs was linked to a higher risk of developing CPTSD compared to situations characterised by unitary ACEs in participants. These data are in line with two recent studies that evaluated the link between cumulative traumatic experiences and CPTSD, where also distinct classes of traumatic experiences were detected with respect to their associations with CPTSD/PTSD symptoms. Namely, Eidhof et al. (2019) demonstrated that individuals in the CPTSD class were more likely to report higher number of traumatic experiences compared to the low CPTSD endorsement class. Similarly, Sölva et al. (2020) reported the relationship between cumulative maltreatment and higher probability of being in PTSD and CPTSD classes. Lastly, it should be underscored that the prevalence rates for various types of ACEs observed in our participants (see Table 4) may be compared to the results of a previous study conducted in Poland, which revealed a similarly high prevalence of parental alcohol problems in the household and a sense of threat in the family (Brian et al., 2013; Hardt et al., 2011). More recently, the European Society of Traumatic Stress Studies (ESTSS) ADJUST COVID-19 pan-European study revealed a similar total rate of ACEs estimated at 30% for the Polish sample, 25% for abuse, and 20% for neglect (Dragan et al., 2020; Lotzin et al., 2020).

We also found that some sociodemographic factors (gender, age) and selected data on the lifestyle patterns of the participants modified our results, which corresponds to the fourth and final hypothesis of this study. First, we did not observe gender differences in CPTSD intensity, which contrasts with the findings of other studies highlighting higher CPTSD in females (McGinty et al., 2021). However, we noticed gender differences in ACEs types as predictors of CPTSD. For example, in the group of males, sexual abuse posed the greatest risk of causing CPTSD, which contradicts previous results on the topic. This further emphasises that while women have higher sexual trauma rates, men may exhibit similarly severe responses (e.g. Guina et al., 2019; Makaruk et al., 2018). Second, we noticed age differences with regard to CPTSD levels, with the highest level observed in younger groups (18–24) and the lowest CPTSD intensity among the oldest (70+). The latter observation is consistent with several theories pointing to decreasing PTSD/CPTSD levels with age (McGinty et al., 2021). Some of these theories underscore greater resilience and more optimal cognitive reappraisal capacities in the wake of trauma among seniors compared to younger adults (MacLeod et al., 2016). Other authors have highlighted that old age is usually related to the increased acceptance of earlier life events, including traumatic and adverse events from the past (Fox et al., 2020). However, older adults may also be more reluctant to report mental health concerns, as they exhibit a rather high tendency of transferring posttraumatic symptoms into somatic complaints (Bottche et al., 2012). Finally, we noticed significant negative lifestyle consequences of CPTSD in the studied sample. More specifically, the participants with CPTSD diagnosis were largely unemployed, suffered from obesity (as defined by BMI index), declared facing sleep problems more often, and were often dependent on smoking. These findings were partly expected, as previous studies have identified a link between interpersonal trauma exposure/ACEs and poor physical health (Ho et al., 2021; López-Martínez et al., 2018). Nevertheless, our study showed at a national level that CPTSD may interfere with social and physical well-being (see also Choi et al., 2021).

Finally, it must be mentioned that our study was conducted in a specific global context around the Polish borders, i.e. the Russian aggression against Ukraine. The war in Ukraine lasting from 2022 has significantly affected the psychological well-being and daily lives of people in many countries. However, so far, there is little research on the impact of this situation on the mental condition of Polish society (Chudzicka-Czupała et al., 2023). Earlier global crisis related to the COVID-19 pandemic led to elevated rates of various mental health problems on a global scale, including many regions of Europe (Olff et al., 2021). Therefore, it should be taken into account that both, previously pandemic, as well as the current tense situation resulting from the war in Ukraine could impact the severity of symptoms of emotional disorders observed in Poland.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including being the large Poland-based assessment of complex PTSD rate and ACEs prevalence in a nationally representative sample along with control of sociodemographic and lifestyle factors. Nevertheless, a few limitations should be mentioned. First, we used a self-reported measurement of ACEs and CPTSD instead of any form of clinician-administered evaluation of CPTSD, although the latter is usually treated as the gold standard in posttraumatic diagnosis and assessment. However, this kind of measurement would have been very difficult to conduct in an epidemiological study. Second, the retrospective and cross-sectional design of this study precludes the drawing of causal conclusions on the link between CPTSD, ACEs and the lifestyle data of the participants. Third, we did not control for previous treatments in assessing the possible effect of current or past trauma-focused psychotherapy on our participants. Finally, we did not control the possible impact of the current tense situation affecting Polish society resulting from the Russian aggression against Ukraine.

Conclusion

Despite the above-mentioned limitations, our study makes important contributions to research on complex PTSD and ACEs in both the Polish and global contexts. We found that the current rate of probable CPTSD in Poland is substantially higher than the corresponding data pertaining to other countries. This points to the requirement for further studies based in Poland, ideally using a longitudinal design, involving more diverse and psychotherapy-seeking samples. We also noticed that the ICD-11 diagnosis of CPTSD may not apply to all cultural environments. Furthermore, it is noted that the ACEs – CPTSD association may depend not necessarily on ACEs types but rather on their overall cumulative character. Finally, at the national level, we observed that CPTSD may interfere with the social and physical functioning of those diagnosed with it.
