Dementia prevention strategies for India: From risk reduction to brain health across the life-course
Authors: Mathew Varghese, C.T. Sudhir Kumar
Published: 2026

In 2017, the World Health Organization (WHO) published the Global Action Plan on the Public Health Response to Dementia 2017–2025, in recognition of dementia as a growing public health challenge in an ageing world.1 Its vision was a world where dementia could be prevented and people with dementia and their carers could live well with dignity, support, autonomy, and equality. Yet, eight years later, most countries still lack a dementia-specific national strategy, policy, plan, or framework.2

Every September, Alzheimer’s Disease International (ADI) marks World Alzheimer Month by releasing evidence-based reports on key themes. The 2023 report on reducing dementia risk emphasised that although much is known about reducing risk through lifestyle modification, little has been done by countries and communities to translate this knowledge into policy, planning, and behaviour change.3 The theme for this year ‘The Earlier You Know, The More You Can Do: A Dementia Diagnosis Matters’ will focus on the benefits of timely diagnosis ( https://www.alzint.org/get-involved/world-alzheimers-month/).

The magnitude of the problem

Nationally representative data from the Longitudinal Ageing Study in India-Diagnostic Assessment of Dementia (LASI-DAD), estimated dementia prevalence among Indians aged over 60 years.4 About 8.8 million people (7.4%) were estimated to be affected, placing India among the countries with the largest dementia burden. Dementia in India shows a clear social gradient, with higher burden among rural older adults and those with limited education. With no affordable cure available, prevention remains the viable strategy, particularly as the global burden of disease projections estimate around 11 million people with dementia in India by 2050.5

The 2024 Lancet Commission estimated that nearly 45% of dementia cases worldwide could be prevented or delayed by modifying 14 life-course risk factors.6 These included 5% from low education in early life; 33% from midlife risks such as hearing loss, high LDL cholesterol, depression, traumatic brain injury, inactivity, diabetes, smoking, hypertension, obesity, and excess alcohol; and 7% from later-life risks including social isolation, air pollution, and untreated vision loss. The LASI-DAD estimates for India suggest that up to 70% of dementia cases in India may be preventable through context-specific risk-factor modification -- especially low education, visual impairment, physical inactivity, and social isolation.7

The WHO approach: A life-course perspective to dementia prevention

The recently published WHO guidelines on ‘Risk reduction of cognitive decline and dementia’ emphasise a life-course approach, recognising that many modifiable risk factors for dementia overlap with those for non-communicable diseases.8 These guidelines broadly discuss interventions as risk reduction strategies in the following domains: (i) healthy behaviours, including cognitive, social and physical activity, reduced harmful alcohol use, tobacco cessation, and a balanced diet; (ii) management of obesity, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, hearing loss, depression, stroke, traumatic brain injury, vision impairment, sleep problems, HIV, and menopausal hormone therapy; (iii) environmental risks, especially air pollution; and (iv) multidomain interventions targeting multiple risk factors.

In addition to the 14 modifiable risk factors identified by the 2024 Lancet Commission, the WHO (2026) guidelines address a few other factors like management of sleep, stroke, HIV and menopausal hormone therapy.8 It concluded that older adults with normal cognition or mild cognitive impairment should be encouraged to engage in cognitive training and cognitively stimulating activities, have a healthy, balanced dietary pattern, that may reduce the risk of cognitive decline and dementia. Stroke is a significant risk factor for dementia, with the risk increasing further after recurrent strokes. For India, the modifiable risk factors highlight a shift toward proactive, comprehensive multidomain dementia prevention strategies tackling both lifestyle, existing health conditions and environmental factors.

A life-course Approach to brain health -what India can do to prevent dementia

The emerging literature on modifiable risk factors highlights the need for a public health approach to the problem of dementia, especially in Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMIC) regions like India. This necessitates identifying primary, secondary, and tertiary prevention strategies that could be used to mitigate the growing burden of dementia.

Primary prevention

The National Academy of Medicine framework classifies primary prevention as universal, selective, or indicated, based on population risk.9 Widely used in public and mental health, it supports risk stratification and early intervention where universal strategies target the general population, selective strategies address modifiable risk groups, and indicated strategies focus on those with earliest disease signs. This approach may reduce dementia incidence through earlier risk reduction.

Universal interventions

Universal prevention should begin before dementia risk factors emerge, with stronger attention to children and youth through policies that address social determinants and promote brain health across the life course.10 Rising physical inactivity, especially among adolescents, women and older adults, highlights the need for community exercise programmes aligned with WHO recommendations. Tobacco control and brain health messaging in public education campaigns can promote healthy living, ageing, and self-care at low cost. In India, lack of education contributes substantially to dementia risk, with a population attributable fraction above 20%.7 Improving access to education, adult literacy, and lifelong learning may strengthen cognitive resilience, particularly among women and rural populations. Stronger road safety laws, fall prevention, pollution control, healthy ageing programmes, and non-communicable diseases prevention policies can support universal dementia prevention. These measures could encourage healthier lifestyles, improve social and environmental conditions, reduce future dementia risk, and build cognitive reserve.

Selective interventions

India’s rising diabetes and hypertension burden offers an opportunity to integrate dementia risk reduction into community-based non-communicable diseases screening and management programmes.11 Better detection, treatment, and long-term control of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidaemia through the National Programme for Prevention and Control of non-communicable diseases (NP-NCD) should remain a priority, alongside action on obesity, unhealthy diets, physical inactivity, and harmful alcohol use. Addressing these risks through policy, programmes, and individual care may delay dementia onset and reduce incidence.

Late-life depression, often missed because of stigma, isolation, and limited geriatric mental health services, requires better primary care, detection and treatment. Hearing and vision impairment are also common among older Indians because of limited screening and poor access to aids. Integrating sensory and elder care health services through programmes like the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Deafness and Blindness and National Programme for the Health Care of the Elderly (NPHCE) ( https://dghs.mohfw.gov.in/national-programme-for-the-health-care-of-the-elderly.php ) could support earlier dementia risk identification. Social isolation, increasingly relevant as younger family members migrate, remains under-studied despite its likely dementia risk. Biomass-fuel exposure, particularly among rural women, may also contribute to neuroinflammation and cognitive decline, reflecting intersecting risks of poverty, gender inequity, and rural residence.

Indicated interventions

These interventions target individuals with early disease signs who do not yet meet diagnostic criteria. Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is a key target, as affected individuals have higher dementia risk but retain potential for intervention.12 Multidomain approaches combining exercise, diet, cognitive training, and vascular risk management may maintain cognition and delay progression. Anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies such as lecanemab and donanemab, now approved in India, may qualify as indicated interventions, but require further study because eligibility is narrow, costs are high, and adverse effects need regular monitoring.10 Opportunistic cognitive screening in primary care and NCD clinics, especially among older adults with vascular risks, could support timely intervention.

Secondary prevention

These interventions focus on early detection and treatment to delay progression to severe disease. Early diagnosis enables appropriate use of cholinesterase inhibitors, NMDA receptor antagonists, and psychosocial interventions for patients and family caregivers. This is being practiced in many clinics managed by psychiatrists, neurologists and geriatricians.13 Benefits of early treatment were clearly demonstrated in the World Alzheimer’s Report 2011,14 yet over 75% of people with dementia globally remain undiagnosed, with only about 10% diagnosed in India.15 Stigma, fear, and shortage of specialised healthcare professionals hinder early identification, underscoring the need to train more professionals. Psychosocial interventions for caregiver training and management of different symptoms are beneficial. Some programmes are available online, as in ‘iSupport for dementia caregivers’16 or videos on ‘Moving Pictures India.’17 Strengthening memory clinics, digital patient tracking, and task-sharing with community health workers could offer scalable, cost-effective models for secondary dementia prevention.

Tertiary prevention

aims to reduce disability, manage functional and behavioural symptoms, support caregivers, and improve quality of life for moderate to severe dementia. The WHO rehabilitation package outlines evidence-based interventions for dementia rehabilitation,18 while the World Alzheimer Report 2025 offers a roadmap for integrating goal-oriented rehabilitation into routine care.19 Multidisciplinary community-based care, day-care, respite and palliative services, stigma reduction, dementia-friendly policies leading to dementia-friendly communities, may reduce disease burden and improve quality of life for people with dementia and their caregivers.20

Overall, recognising dementia as an emerging public health concern and strengthening health systems to detect cognitive impairment would be an important step for dementia prevention in India. Strategies should be regionally tailored, especially where cardiovascular risk is high, and integrated into existing non-communicable disease clinics. Delaying dementia by even one or two years could substantially reduce healthcare costs and caregiver burden, making prevention an economic imperative. Stronger primary care, expanded health and wellness centres, better insurance coverage, and lower out-of-pocket expenditure are essential for equitable access to prevention, screening, and long-term management of modifiable risk factors.
