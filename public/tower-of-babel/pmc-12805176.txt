Noninvasive Cardiac Imaging in Older Adults
Authors: Krishna K. Patel, Jordan Strom, Mushabbar A. Syed, Roosha Parikh, Leslee J. Shaw, Julius M. Gardin, Michael W. Rich, Radmila Lyubarova
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Noninvasive cardiac imaging plays a critical role in the diagnosis and risk stratification of cardiovascular disease in older adults, a population marked by clinical heterogeneity, multimorbidity, and age-related physiologic changes. This review outlines the strengths and limitations of commonly used imaging modalities including echocardiography, transesophageal echocardiography, cardiac computed tomography, nuclear imaging tests, and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging in the context of aging. We highlight diagnostic challenges such as limited exercise capacity, image quality artifacts, reduced specificity in the setting of multivessel, or microvascular disease and intolerance to longer scan protocols. Advances in imaging technology, including artificial intelligence and hybrid protocols, offer opportunities to improve accuracy, access, and individualized decision-making. The review emphasizes the importance of tailoring test selection to patient comorbidities and goals of care. Addressing current evidence gaps through trials inclusive of older adults and geriatric-focused imaging guidelines is essential to delivering equitable, high-value cardiovascular care to older adults.

In the United States, adults ≥65 years account for the majority of hospitalizations due to coronary artery disease (CAD), heart failure (HF), and valvular heart disease.1, 2, 3 They present with increasingly complex cardiovascular pathologies, underscoring the need for tailored approaches to diagnosis and management. Age-related structural, functional, and metabolic changes, such as increased left ventricular (LV) wall thickness, concentric remodeling, myocardial fibrosis, impaired diastolic relaxation, alterations in contractility, chamber size, and vascular stiffness, contribute to heightened cardiovascular risk. These changes can also mimic or obscure pathology, complicating the interpretation of standard imaging if age-specific norms are not considered.

Advances in noninvasive imaging now offer many options: transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA), coronary artery calcium (CAC) scoring, myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI) using single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) or positron emission tomography (PET), and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR). Each modality offers unique advantages and challenges in the aging population. As older patients often present with overlapping conditions (eg, CAD, valvular disease, HF can all cause exertional dyspnea), a multimodality approach tailored to individual needs and limitations is essential.

In this review, we aim to: 1) outline physiological and practical challenges in cardiac imaging for older adults; 2) evaluate the strengths, limitations, and clinical roles of key imaging modalities; 3) discuss barriers to image acquisition and interpretation; 4) propose innovations to enhance imaging in this population; and 5) identify research gaps and future directions to better meet the needs of an aging population.

Normal physiological and structural changes with age

Aging is associated with characteristic structural and physiologic changes in the cardiovascular (CV) system that is often considered to be part of normal aging but may confound disease recognition and risk stratification. With advancing age both diastolic and systolic LV volumes, as well as stroke volume, progressively decline, even after accounting for body size, while left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) tends to increase.4 Global longitudinal strain (GLS) does not change substantially with age, suggesting that the increase in LVEF is due to greater circumferential shortening.4 LV wall thickness also increases with age. Basal septal wall thickening is common, occurs in 4 to 6% of those aged 70 to 84 years, and in up to 18% in those ≥85 years.5 This may contribute to LV outflow tract ellipticity and intracavitary obstruction, particularly in small hyperdynamic LVs.5,6 Basal septal hypertrophy is more common in individuals with mitral annular calcification and hypertension and may result in overestimation of LV mass.7 Changes in LV mass with age remain uncertain; some echo studies show an increase, while some using CMR show a decrease or no change with age.4,8, 9, 10, 11

Right ventricle volumes also decrease and right ventricular ejection fraction increases with aging.8,12,13 While left atrial volumes increase, right atrial size shows no significant change with aging.14 With age, septal and lateral e’ velocities decrease, and the E/e’ ratio increases, the latter however remains below the pathological threshold.15 Transmitral filling becomes increasingly dependent on atrial contraction after age 60.15 These changes occur alongside progressive mitral annular and aortic calcification, valvular thickening, regurgitation and stenosis, and increased peak tricuspid regurgitant velocity, which can be attributed to normal aging or reflect age-associated diseases.8,16,17 At least mild mitral annular calcification is present in ∼40% of older adults and is associated with microtears in the mitral annulus from high chamber pressure conditions which are prevalent in older age like hypertensive heart disease, aortic stenosis (AS), chronic kidney disease, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.18,19 It can cause significant mitral stenosis when encroaching on the inlet and regurgitation when extending to the left atrium.20 Figure 1 highlights some age-related findings on TTE.Figure 1Age-Related Echocardiographic Findings in Older Adult Echocardiographic Images of an 84-Year-Old Man Without Heart Disease(A) Apical 3-chamber view demonstrating basal septal hypertrophy. Chronotropic response declines with aging due to reduced beta-adrenergic sensitivity, forming the basis for the maximum predicted heart rate formula (220–age), which serves as a target for achieving adequate effort during exercise stress testing. CAC is a marker of biological age.21 In recent studies, 62.5% of adults over the age of 75 years had CAC scores >100 Agatston units.22 A CAC score of zero is seen in only 11% in those ≥75 and just 5% in those ≥90 years in another study with notable differences by race and sex—zero CAC is more common in Black older women and less common in White older men.22,23 There are also physiologic changes in absolute stress myocardial blood flow (MBF) and myocardial blood flow reserve (MBFR) with age. Stress MBF and MBFR decrease with advancing age in both men and women, primarily due to age-related alterations in endothelium-mediated coronary vasodilation.24 Rest MBF remains relatively stable.24 Utilizing single cutoff of MBFR to define abnormality in perfusion may lead to overdiagnosis of obstructive CAD in older adults.

Sex-related differences in cardiovascular aging

With aging, women often develop concentric remodeling with smaller LV sizes, greater contractility, and more prominent diastolic decline than men.25,26 The reasons for sex-related differences are unclear, but may involve higher rates of myocardial apoptosis and fibrosis with aging in men and physiologic adaptation in women to maintain cardiac output with smaller LV cavities.26, 27, 28 CMR T1 mapping shows a higher degree of myocardial fibrosis with aging, especially in men. While younger women have higher extracellular volume (ECV) fraction, they demonstrate less ECV increase over time.29 Left atrial reservoir and conduit strains decrease while contractile strain increases in older women.30 Coronary calcification burden progresses differently, with lower prevalence of zero CAC in older men vs women, particularly Black women.22,23 Younger males exhibit higher MBFR compared to females, but this difference diminishes with age, resulting in similar MBFR values in men and women in older adults.24

These physiological changes highlight the importance of revisiting the definitions of normality in older populations. In the MESA (Multiethnic Study of Atherosclerosis) study, 81.6% of healthy older adults had ≥1 abnormal finding using current guideline cutoffs.8 This may lead to overdiagnosis and misclassification. In the ARIC (Atherosclerosis Risk In Communities) study, applying age-adjusted norms reclassified 42% as having normal diastolic function, this group had lower risks of HF and mortality.31 Similarly, lower limits for normal LVEF may be higher in older adults, and thus so-called low-normal values (ie, 50% to 55%) may be pathologically low in an older population, especially in older women. Thus, age- and sex-specific normal ranges are critically needed in older adults. Echocardiography

TTE is the first-line imaging modality for structural and functional cardiac assessment in all adults due to its wide availability, lack of ionizing radiation, and relatively low cost. An estimated 95% of Medicare beneficiaries live within 16 miles of TTE-capable center, underscoring its utility for older adults.39 TTE provides key information on systolic and diastolic function, valvular heart disease, aortic size, and pericardial disease. Pathology is common in older adults: screening studies report depressed LVEF in up to 5.3% and clinically significant valvular heart disease in up to 6.4%.40,41 While routine TTE screening of middle-aged adults does not reduce myocardial infarction, death, or stroke, evidence for its use in older adults remains limited.42 One study in centenarians found echocardiographic abnormalities in 88%, but 72% saw no management changes, questioning the value of routine screening in older adults.43

TEE is essential for diagnosis, procedural planning, and guidance in structural heart interventions. While safe overall, older adults are an increased risk of sedation-related complications and esophageal injury. One study showed transient hypotension was 3.5 times more common in adults >70 years compared to younger individuals (<50 years), though life-threatening complications were rare (<0.01%).44 Longer procedural time and poor image quality are associated with higher risk for esophageal injury in older adults.45 Preventative strategies include dysphagia or odynophagia screening, use of conscious sedation vs general anesthesia, use of smaller diameter TEE probes or intracardiac echocardiography, and avoidance of prolonged anteflexion.45 When TEE poses procedural risks, cardiac CT or CMR may be suitable alternatives.

With age, the utility of exercise stress echocardiography declines due to limited exercise capacity and difficulty achieving target heart rate. In one study, only 53% of individuals over 80 reached the target heart rate, with the mean exercise time was 3 minutes 46 seconds.46 Consequently, pharmacologic stress testing, particularly with dobutamine stress echo (DSE) is increasingly used. While generally safe, DSE carries risks such as arrhythmias and blood pressure fluctuations. A large cohort study reported atrial fibrillation during DSE in 0.83% overall, rising to 1.3% among those aged 70 to 79 years, and 4% in those 80 years and older.47 DSE maintains strong diagnostic and prognostic value in older adults, with 90% accuracy reported in patients ≥70 years. Furthermore, abnormal stress perfusion and stress wall motion were predictive of adverse outcomes at 21-month follow-up.48

Aortic stenosis

TTE is the main tool for initial diagnosis and staging, with cardiac CT, CMR and TEE are useful in select clinical scenarios. In older adults, the prevalence of low flow severe AS with preserved or reduced EF is higher and additional diagnostic considerations are important.49 Assessing the outflow tract to aortic valve flow ratio by TTE, and aortic valve CAC score by CT are important to define severity.50 In those with low EF, DSE can aid to distinguish true vs pseudo-severe AS. Myocardial fibrosis, important prognostic marker of LV decompensation or post-TAVR outcomes, can be detected with CMR and cardiac CT via ECV assessment or inferred from echocardiographic GLS (71).51 Imaging for transthyretin (TTR) amyloidosis should be considered in paradoxical low flow AS due to higher coexistence in these patients.

Limitations and practical considerations of echocardiography in older adults

Suboptimal acoustic windows with TTE occur in ∼10 to 20% of the population, this rate rises with age—those ≥65 years of age have a 52% greater likelihood of requiring ultrasound contrast (Table 3).52,53 Obesity, chest wall deformities, critical illness, and chronic lung disease, all more frequent in older adults, can make it challenging to visualize the endocardial border without using ultrasound contrast.52 Age-related declines in cognition and hearing also affect cooperation with positioning, breath holding, and maneuvers. Involving caregivers may help achieve optimal imaging results. Body surface area may not reflect cardiac size when normalizing chamber dimensions in obese or underweight patients, alternative scaling measures such as height or allometric height should be considered.54

Interpretation of findings can also be challenging. First, chronological age alone does not capture the heterogeneity of cardiovascular aging. Thus, age must be interpreted in the context of associated cardiac structural and functional changes. Echocardiographic parameters including LV hypertrophy, concentric remodeling, left atrial enlargement, impaired diastolic indices, and aortic root dilation reflect biological rather than chronological aging and may improve risk stratification.55 Recent work has proposed distinct cardiac aging phenotypes (“slow,” “normal,” and “accelerated”), which independently predict adverse outcomes, with the highest event rates in the accelerated group.55 In addition, artificial intelligence (AI)-based models estimating biological age from echocardiography show that a predicted age ≥5 years above chronological age is associated with significantly higher mortality risk.56 Together, these findings suggest that echo-derived biological age may serve as a powerful tool to define cardiac aging phenotypes and identify high-risk substrates in older adults. Second, age-related changes may not imply pathology. For example, while aging is associated with changes in mitral inflow patterns and diastolic variables, invasive studies suggest preserved LV relaxation (measured using Tau) until at least the 8th decade of life.57 Findings from the Cardiovascular Health Study demonstrated a U-shape association between E/A ratio and incident HF in older adults, with the lowest risk observed in individuals with an intermediate E/A ratio 0.7 to 1.5.58 While normative data exist, the thresholds that define high-risk older adults remain unclear.8,54 Third, competing risks in older adults may reduce the utility of certain findings. For example, identifying patent foramen ovale in older adults with cryptogenic stroke is less informative given the higher likelihood of atrial fibrillation or vascular disease and that older adults were excluded from several patent foramen ovale closure trials.59 These factors illustrate the need to interpret echocardiographic results in context, along with multimorbidity and other competing risks.

Technological advances in echocardiography

In addition to conventional echo parameters, 3D and speckle tracking echocardiography of LV and left atrium are emerging tools with diagnostic and prognostic value. 3-dimensional (3D) echo provides more accurate and reproducible quantification of chamber volumes, ejection fraction and provides additional information in valvular disease, particularly estimation of severity of regurgitant jets. LV GLS can aid to identify subclinical systolic dysfunction and infiltrative cardiomyopathy such as amyloid, as well predictive of adverse CV outcomes.60 Still data on age-specific normal ranges are limited and more studies are needed to define prognostic value in older adults. Left atrial reservoir strain, recently incorporated in the diastolic function guidelines, has greater sensitivity in diagnosing LV filling pressures than E/e′ and left atrial volume index and is an important prognostic marker in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction and atrial fibrillation.38,61,62

Emerging technologies such as miniaturization of cardiac ultrasound, AI-guided acquisition, and wearables may improve diagnosis, especially in underserved older adults.63 Older adults are more likely to live in rural locations and have lower household incomes, which may themselves present barriers to accessing care.64 AI-enabled point-of-care ultrasound devices used by nonexperts could expand access to vulnerable, elderly individuals65 living in skilled nursing and assisted living facilities, improving triage and reducing hospital visits. Similar models have succeeded in Indian Health Services and rural Australia.66,67 Wearable ultrasound patches may enable continuous cardiac monitoring in nonhospital settings, improving both early detection and reducing admissions.68 While promising, these tools must be validated and carefully implemented to avoid propagating existing biases in care.

Cardiac computed tomography

Cardiac CT is increasingly used to evaluate various cardiovascular conditions in older adults. In addition to evaluating coronary atherosclerosis, it supports preprocedural planning for structural interventions, left-atrial appendage (LAA) closure, arrhythmia management, and myocardial tissue characterization, offering anatomic and functional insights in complex cases frequent in older adults.

Coronary artery disease

Diagnostic value

CCTA is accurate and feasible in older patients with stable chest pain (example in Figure 2). Although heavy calcifications may reduce diagnostic accuracy due to blooming artifacts, modern scanners often provide good image quality. In one study of 1,011 > 75-year-olds using a 320-row system, CCTA was interpretable in 68% of cases and of acceptable image quality in 83%.69 The positive predictive value for detecting obstructive CAD reached 89% vs invasive angiography in those older than 70 years.70 Among older adults with a median age of 68.5 years and high CAC score, fractional flow reserve (FFR)-CT demonstrated low specificity, likely due to limited visualization of the lumen size from calcium blooming, thus despite high sensitivity may result in frequent false positives.71 Misregistration and malalignment between axial data sets can further degrade accuracy, and metal (stents, prostheses) and motion can render segments nonanalyzable or spuriously abnormal. In addition, CT-FFR primarily estimates epicardial pressure loss and does not account for microvascular dysfunction, which is common with aging and may decouple symptoms from epicardial physiology. Prognostic value

CCTA predicts adverse cardiovascular outcomes beyond traditional risk factors and CAC scoring, in both symptomatic and asymptomatic older adults.73,74 Its ability to quantify plaque burden, identify high-risk plaque features, and assess vessel-specific ischemia using FFR-CT improves prognostication and helps guide management decisions.75 In the DISCHARGE (Diagnostic Imaging Strategies for Patients with Stable Chest Pain and Intermediate Risk of Coronary Artery Disease) trial, adults >75 years undergoing CCTA had comparable outcomes and procedure-related complications to those undergoing invasive angiography.76 The FFR-CT technology comes with an added cost, with mixed reports on cost-effectiveness.77,78 In older adults, particularly those with high CAC or suspected microvascular disease, FFR-CT is best used as an adjunct to high-quality CCTA within a multimodality pathway rather than a stand-alone gatekeeper.

CAC score remains predictive with age.79, 80, 81 Studies show it predicts mortality over 5.6 to 15 years in adults ≥70 years.82,83 Notably, 5.6-year survival among the older adults ≥75 years and younger adults with CAC of zero was similar.82,83

Non-CAD applications of CCTA in older adults

Cardiac CT is used for preprocedural planning of percutaneous mitral and tricuspid valve therapy, transcatheter electrosurgical procedures in patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy at high surgical risk, and procedural execution and postprocedural follow-up of transcatheter LAA occlusion.84,85 CT plays an important role in AS management and vascular access evaluation that are essential for transcatheter valve replacement as described above. Cardiac CT is used for pulmonary vein mapping and evaluation of the left atrial appendage for exclusion of thrombus prior to cardioversion or ablation procedures. A meta-analysis showed delayed CT imaging had 100% sensitivity and 99% specificity for LAA thrombus detection.86

Limitations and practical considerations of cardiac CT in older adults

While noninvasive, CT requires careful patient selection. Frailty and physical conditions may limit appropriate positioning on the scanner table. Heavy calcifications cause blooming artifacts on CCTA, potentially exaggerating luminal narrowing and impairing image quality. Arrhythmias, particularly atrial fibrillation, can degrade image quality or necessitate repeated acquisitions. Also, many older patients may not tolerate the medications needed for heart rate control or have contraindications. Iodinated contrast increases risk of contrast-induced acute kidney injury among those with low glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in a population already at elevated risk for renal insufficiency.87 Additionally, while radiation risk is often less of a lifetime concern in advanced age, it is important to minimize dose through optimized protocols.

Technological advances in cardiac CT

Innovations such as Photon Counting Detector-CT offer improved spatial resolution (up to 0.2 mm), reduced electronic noise, and decreased blooming artifacts due to the novel detector leading to reduced heart rate dependency and lower contrast/radiation doses with improved diagnostic accuracy.88,89 However, validation in older adults remains limited. Key challenges such as arrhythmias, renal dysfunction, heavy coronary calcification, and limited mobility may attenuate its applicability in this population. Ongoing studies are needed before routine integration into clinical pathways for older adults.

Nuclear myocardial perfusion imaging

Nuclear MPI is an established technique for evaluating CAD in older patients, who often present with multiple comorbidities and atypical symptoms. SPECT is less expensive, widely accessible and supported by extensive prognostic data, while PET offers superior resolution, lower radiation, and the ability to quantify MBF and MBFR, which enhances detection of diffuse atherosclerosis and microvascular disease. Due to mobility limitations, most older adults undergo pharmacologic rather than exercise stress testing, necessitating careful consideration of potential side effects.

Coronary artery disease

Diagnostic value

SPECT retains high diagnostic performance in older adults, but specificity can be reduced by certain age-related factors like smaller cardiac size, attenuation artifacts, and limited exercise capacity. In octogenarians, SPECT MPI demonstrated 92% sensitivity for triple-vessel disease, comparable to younger patients.90 However, a multicenter registry showed a slight drop in diagnostic accuracy in older adults (area under the curve: 0.72 vs 0.78; P = 0.03), mainly due to smaller LV volumes that degrade image quality.91

PET overcomes many of these limitations. Prognostic value

Both SPECT and PET provide valuable prognostic information in older adults.90,96, 97, 98, 99 In 2 large cohorts of >13,000 patients (including 517 ≥80 years of age), the predictive value of abnormal MPI scans was similar, or even stronger, in patients aged ≥80 years.90,97 While a normal MPI scan still carries favorable prognostic value, its “warranty period” may be shorter in the very old due to age and comorbidities.97,98 For instance, one study showed a 6.1% event rate over 3 years in individuals ≥80 with normal SPECT vs 2.2% in those <65.98 The high competing risk of noncardiac death may decrease the prognostic importance of ischemia in very old patients. In one study, the extent of perfusion defects on PET lost prognostic significance beyond age 85 years, but it was limited by small sample size.96 However, PET-derived quantitative metrics like reduced MBFR and diminished LVEF reserve (defined as stress LVEF–rest LVEF) remain prognostic, even when perfusion appears normal.92,100,101 These quantitative markers offer incremental risk stratification and can guide decisions about revascularization in select older adults.

Despite procedural concerns, data support that older adults with significant ischemic burden (>15%) may derive survival benefit from invasive management.97 PET-derived thresholds for ischemia severity (≥10%), low MBFR (≤1.8), or reduced LVEF reserve (≤0) define subgroups that may experience improved outcomes after revascularization.100,102, 103, 104 Still, referral rates are lower in octagenarians compared with younger patients, with physicians citing frailty, cognition, and patient preferences as deterrents.99,105 Integration of MPI with clinical judgment and geriatric assessment is key to individualized decision-making.

Limitations and challenges of nuclear MPI in older adults

Older adults present technical challenges for MPI due to smaller LV volumes and anatomical factors such as kyphosis or diaphragmatic elevation, particularly in older women. These can produce attenuation artifacts and partial volume effects, potentially leading to false positives or missed ischemia. Additionally, lying flat for prolonged imaging may be difficult for those with arthritis, back pain, or orthopnea, and motion during scans can blur images. Advances in faster scan acquisition with newer SPECT cameras, gated SPECT, and CT-based attenuation correction have significantly mitigated these limitations. Chronotropic incompetence is another challenge; older patients often have an insufficient increase in heart rate with exercise, necessitating administration of vasodilators. Despite advancements, SPECT may still miss balanced ischemia from multivessel disease, a limitation addressed by PET’s quantitative capabilities.

Cardiac amyloidosis

Wild-type TTR amyloidosis (ATTR) is increasingly recognized in older adults, especially those with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, LV hypertrophy, or AS.106,107 Tc-99m pyrophosphate (PYP) SPECT imaging has emerged as a noninvasive and highly specific diagnostic tool for ATTR. When combined with lab tests to exclude light-chain amyloidosis, a positive PYP scan (grade 2-3 uptake, elevated heart-to-contralateral lung ratio) confirms ATTR, enabling timely initiation of treatments like tafamidis or acoramidis (Figure 3, Panel B).108 Higher myocardial tracer uptake correlates with more advanced disease and worse prognosis.109 However, interpretation can be confounded by blood pool activity, skeletal uptake, or recent infarcts.110 These issues are more common in older populations and may lead to false positives or indeterminate scans. Tomographic views and attenuation correction improve accuracy and help differentiate true myocardial uptake from artifacts, supporting more reliable diagnosis and management.

Technological advances in nuclear imaging

AI and machine learning hold promise for enhancing MPI interpretation in older adults. AI models have outperformed conventional quantitative tools in identifying obstructive CAD and screening for cardiac amyloidosis, offering higher diagnostic accuracy and reducing interobserver variability.111,112 These systems can incorporate imaging data alongside patient-specific variables such as age, frailty, and comorbidities to deliver personalized risk assessments. AI tools can also improve image quality through motion correction and noise reduction and streamline workflow by standardizing interpretation across centers.113 As these technologies mature, validation in very old populations is essential to avoid algorithmic bias. AI may help tailor invasive decisions and therapeutic strategies more precisely to individual older patients, improving outcomes in ischemic heart disease and cardiac amyloidosis.112,114,115

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging

CMR provides a comprehensive assessment of cardiac structure and function, making it especially valuable for older adults with complex cardiovascular diseases. It provides high spatial resolution, 3D visualization, comprehensive tissue characterization, and avoids ionizing radiation exposure. However, clinical use is limited by availability, specialist expertise, and patient barriers like claustrophobia or device contraindications.

Diagnostic value

CMR is useful for evaluating CAD, cardiomyopathies, valvular heart disease, vascular disease, and pericardial disease. Though extensively studied, evidence in older patients is limited; most data are derived from use in a general population. A standard exam integrates sequences tailored to specific clinical scenarios, including morphology, function, and myocardial tissue characterization with late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) and parametric mapping techniques.

CMR is the gold standard for quantifying ventricular volumes, EF, and LV mass with superior reproducibility to echocardiography.116 LGE identifies myocardial scar/fibrosis of ischemic and nonischemic etiologies. A meta-analysis of 12 studies confirmed its diagnostic accuracy in ischemic cardiomyopathy (area under the curve: 0.95), supporting its use in new-onset HF.117 Stress perfusion CMR, utilizing vasodilators like adenosine or regadenoson, is a robust tool for diagnosing CAD,118,119 however routine use in older adults may be limited by access, cost, scan duration, and patient tolerance. Supporting evidence is largely derived from selected cohorts, which may not reflect frail or multimorbid older populations. In those >70 years, it was safe (96%), identified myocardial ischemia and/or infarction in 42% and excluded it in 58% with a <1 hour exam.120 Accordingly, stress CMR should be considered a high-value tool in selected older adults particularly those with equivocal results from other modalities or contraindications to CT or nuclear imaging rather than as a first-line test.

Parametric mapping (T1, T2, T2∗, ECV) characterizes myocardial tissue and aids diagnosis of aging-associated conditions. LGE detects focal fibrosis, while native T1/ECV assesses diffuse fibrosis. Prognostic value

Normal stress CMR predicts low event rates (99.3% 5-year survival).123 In 3 separate cohorts of older adults (110 adults >70 years; 2,496 adults with mean age 76 years, and 1780 patients with mean age 73 years), ischemia severity correlated with higher adverse cardiac events.124, 125, 126 CMR-derived scar burden informs implantable cardioverter defibrillator implantation decisions in ischemic cardiomyopathy.127 CMR-derived LVEF and microvascular obstruction improved post-ST elevation myocardial infarction risk prediction in older adults.126 Unrecognized MI was found in 17% of older adults by LGE on CMR and was associated with higher mortality.128 Elevated ECV suggesting diffuse interstitial fibrosis predicted adverse outcomes in various conditions, including severe AS.129,130 Post-TAVR regurgitation ≥30% on CMR predicts worse outcomes.131 4-Dimensional flow magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) enhances risk stratification in aortic disease.132

Challenges and limitations of CMR in older adults

Longer CMR protocols lasting 45 to 60 minutes may be difficult for older patients. Updated protocols reduce scan time to under 30 minutes, and addition of free-breathing sequences may help older adults with limited breath holding capacity.133 Concerns of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis with gadolinium-based contrast agents in chronic kidney disease are mitigated using group II agents.134 Modern cardiac implantable devices manufactured post-2000 are generally safe for CMR scans at 1.5 T with appropriate safety protocols.135,136 Access remains limited by center specialization and preauthorization. Claustrophobia is manageable with mild sedation, wide-bore scanners, and shorter scan protocols. Absolute contraindications to CMR include metallic foreign bodies in critical locations, insulin pumps, temporary pacing leads, and certain aneurysm clips.

Technological advances in CMR

Advances in CMR focus on rapid acquisition, automated scanning, image quality, and enhanced clinical workflow. AI holds promise for streamlining acquisition and interpretation.137 Quantitative perfusion imaging methods and measurement of MBF and perfusion reserve enhance detection of coronary and microvascular disease.138

Newer advances and future directions

Technological advances are improving feasibility, image quality, and diagnostic precision across modalities. Automated contouring and quantification tools reduce operator variability, while machine learning may surpass traditional metrics in predicting outcomes in older adults. AI-driven decision-support systems that integrate clinical, biomarker, and imaging data offer promise for individualized risk assessment, particularly valuable given the heterogeneity in aging populations. Key research gaps include:1.Inclusive imaging trials: Older adults remain underrepresented in imaging trials.139 Large studies are needed to inform test selection, define age- and sex-specific thresholds, and assess outcomes.2.Older adult-specific imaging guidelines: Current guidelines lack specificity for older adults due to limited evidence. Guideline development must integrate patient-centered considerations such as frailty, comorbidities, and life expectancy through multidisciplinary collaboration among geriatricians, radiologists, and cardiologists.3.Shared decision-making tools: More data are needed to support shared decision-making, incorporating frailty status and comprehensive geriatric assessment into both imaging selection and treatment choices.4.Protocol optimization: Modifications such as free-breathing or shortened CMR protocols may improve tolerability. Hybrid protocols combining functional (PET/SPECT) and anatomic (CCTA/MRI) imaging could streamline diagnostics but require supportive infrastructure.5.Point-of-care imaging and AI integration: Portable echo and simplified CMR systems, and AI-based interpretation,140,141 could expand access for homebound or institutionalized older adults. Faster scanners may reduce procedural burden and enhance equity. However, equity remains a critical concern in AI applications for older adults. Underrepresentation of older adults in training data sets, coupled with algorithmic bias, may limit performance in this population. Cognitive impairment can affect data acquisition and interpretation, further underscoring the need for validation in age-diverse cohorts. Importantly, AI tools should augment not replace clinical judgment, particularly in complex geriatric care.

Conclusions

As the population ages, optimizing cardiac imaging for older adults is critical. Physiologic changes, comorbidities, and functional limitations introduce diagnostic and prognostic challenges across all modalities. A multimodality, patient-centered approach that incorporates geriatric assessment can improve accuracy, guide care, and enhance outcomes. Future priorities include older adult inclusive imaging trials, protocol refinement for tolerability, and use of AI to deliver individualized, equitable cardiovascular care for this growing population. System-level reform is essential including investment in portable imaging technologies to expand access, interdisciplinary collaboration to optimize care, and guideline revision to incorporate the unique needs of older adults.
