Abstract
We investigate a qualitatively new regime of inflationary models with small and rapid oscillations in the potential—resonant non-Gaussianity. In contrast to the standard scenario, where most of the observable information is encoded in the power spectrum, in this regime the oscillatory signal predominantly appears in higher-order correlation functions with large n n . This behavior emerges when the oscillation frequency ω \omega exceeds the naive cutoff of the theory, 4 ​ π ​ f 4\pi f . However, as noted by Hook and Rattazzi [ 1 ] , the actual cutoff is somewhat higher—though only logarithmically—when the amplitude of the oscillations is small. We identify a phenomenologically relevant window in which n n -point functions with 3 ≲ n ≲ 9 3\lesssim n\lesssim 9 are potentially observable. In this regime, the signal exhibits 350 - 1000 oscillations per decade in k k .

Introduction and setup

It is generally understood that the signal encoded in
n
-point primordial correlation functions with large
n
is subdominant compared to that of the power spectrum.
Specifically, in a single-field model of inflation with oscillatory potential, it was shown in
[
2
]
that within the regime of validity of the effective field theory (EFT), the oscillatory signal contained in the
2
-point function dominates over the signal contained in higher-order
n
-point functions.
Note that
n
-point correlation functions of the curvature perturbation have been extensively studied in the literature, see e.g.
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
.
However, the analysis done in
[
2
]
relied on the fact that the EFT is expected to break down at the
naive
UV cutoff
4\pi f
, beyond which the theory becomes strongly coupled.
This implies that if one goes beyond such a scale, there might be phenomenologically interesting signals one has not encountered before.
Recently in
[
1

, the perturbative unitarity bound for a canonical scalar-field model with oscillatory potential was reconsidered, revealing that the UV cutoff of the EFT exceeds the scale
4\pi f
.
Therefore, this opens up a window in which the oscillatory signal encoded in higher-order
n
-point functions may become potentially observable and dominate that of the
2
-point function.
Scrutinizing this is the main goal of this paper.

In the resonant model, we assume that a canonical scalar field
\phi

\displaystyle V(\phi)=V_{\rm sr}(\phi)+\Lambda^{4}\cos(\phi/f)\;,

(1.1)

where

is a slow-roll potential,

The detailed analysis of axion monodromy inflation with

[
6

Here we keep the slow-roll potential generic, as it was done in
[
5

4

{\rm d}s^{2}=-\,\mathrm{d}t^{2}+a(t)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}^{2}

Following the analysis in

\displaystyle\dot{H}(t)=-\epsilon_{0}H_{0}^{2}\big[1-\tilde{b}\cos(\phi_{0}/f)\big]\;,

(1.2)

where
0

\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}\equiv-\frac{\dot{H}_{0}}{H_{0}^{2}}=\frac{\dot{\phi}_{0}^{2}}{2M_{\rm Pl}^{2}H_{0}^{2}}\;,\qquad\tilde{b}\equiv\frac{2\Lambda^{4}}{\dot{\phi}_{0}^{2}}\;.

Note that it is straightforward to analytically obtain the solution for

In this paper, we are interested in the regime where the frequency of oscillations is large compared to the Hubble rate and the oscillation amplitude is small, i.e.

\displaystyle\alpha\equiv\frac{|\dot{\phi}_{0}|}{H_{0}f}=\frac{\omega}{H_{0}}\gg 1\;,\qquad\tilde{b}\ll 1\;,

(1.4)

Having discussed the dynamics of the background, we now focus on the analysis of perturbations around it.
In this paper, for simplicity, we are interested in scalar fluctuations in the decoupling limit where the slow-roll parameter

This implies that during inflation the metric becomes exactly de Sitter, for which the scale factor is

2
Calculation of the unitarity cutoff

In this section we revisit the derivation of the unitarity cutoff derived in
[
1
]
. In the single-field model with the oscillatory potential
V(\phi)=\Lambda^{4}\cos(\phi/f)
, the
n\to n
scattering amplitude
\mathcal{M}_{n\to n}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{n\rightarrow n}=\frac{\lambda_{2n}}{8\pi}\bigg(\frac{E}{4\pi}\bigg)^{2n-4}\frac{1}{n!(n-1)!(n-2)!}\;,

(2.1)

where
\lambda_{2n}=(-1)^{n}\Lambda^{4}/f^{2n}
and
E
is the center-of-mass energy.
Note that in App.
A
we explicitly show the detailed derivation of Eq. (
2.1
) (see also
[
14
]
).
Written in terms of the resonant-model parameters (
\tilde{b}
and
\omega
), Eq. (
2.1
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{n\rightarrow n}=(-1)^{n}\tilde{b}\pi\bigg(\frac{\omega}{4\pi f}\bigg)^{2n-2}\frac{1}{n!(n-1)!(n-2)!}\;,

(2.2)

where we have identified the energy
E
with the oscillation frequency
\omega
and used
\tilde{b}=2\Lambda^{4}/(\omega^{2}f^{2})
.
3
3

3

Note that since the scattering amplitude (
2.1
) with energy
E
is an object defined on flat space, identifying
E
with
\omega
(oscillation frequency) is valid when the mode functions are inside the horizon. This is consistent with the fact that the saddle point at which
n
-point functions of curvature perturbation are peaked is at
-k_{\rm t}\eta_{\rm s}\simeq\alpha\gg 1
, where
k_{\rm t}
is the total momentum and
\eta_{s}
is the saddle point. If
\eta_{s}
moves to later times due to large
n
, one then expects corrections in the identification of
E
and
\omega
.

The perturbative unitarity bound is obtained by imposing
|\mathcal{M}_{n\rightarrow n}|<1
.
The maximum of
|\mathcal{M}_{n\rightarrow n}|
over
n
is obtained by solving
\partial_{n}|\mathcal{M}_{n\rightarrow n}|=0
. By expanding the latter equation for large
n
we obtain that the maximum
n
is attained at

n_{\rm max}\simeq\left(\frac{\omega}{4\pi f}\right)^{2/3}\;.

(2.3)

Notice that we obtain a different prefactor compared to Eq. (2.4) in
[
1
]
. We checked that our result gives a better approximation to the true maximum of the amplitude.
From Eq. (
2.3
) it is clear that when
\omega/(4\pi f)
is large,
n_{\rm max}
is also large.
The condition
|\mathcal{M}_{n\rightarrow n}|<1
evaluated at
n_{\rm max}
gives a maximum values for
\omega<\Lambda_{\rm cutoff}
.
Therefore, in the large-
n
limit, we obtain the implicit expression

\frac{\Lambda_{\rm cutoff}}{4\pi f}\simeq\bigg\{\frac{1}{3}\log\Big[\frac{2}{\tilde{b}}\Big(\frac{f}{\Lambda_{\rm cutoff}}\Big)^{2}\Big]\bigg\}^{3/2}\;,

(2.4)

where we have dropped terms that are not enhanced by the logarithmic factor.
From the expression (
2.4
), it is now evident that
\Lambda_{\rm cutoff}
is larger than the naive cutoff
4\pi f
by a logarithmic factor that depends on
\tilde{b}
.
Notice that, as
\tilde{b}\to 0
, this cutoff approaches infinity, i.e. the theory becomes free as oscillations in the potential disappear.
Thus, this allows us to explore physics beyond the scale
4\pi f
up to
\Lambda_{\rm cutoff}
using our EFT.
However, for the values of
\tilde{b}
and
\omega
relevant for our results, the approximation (
2.4
) does not suffice to have a good accuracy. For this reason in our results (Sec.
4
) we instead evaluate the cutoff numerically: for a given
\tilde{b}
the allowed
\omega
’s have to satisfy
|\mathcal{M}_{n\to n}|<1
for all integer
n\geq 2
.

Let us now consider the approximate cutoff (
2.4
) as a proxy for the actual numerical cutoff.
As described above, we are now allowed to go beyond the scale
4\pi f
up to
\Lambda_{\rm cutoff}
.
However, this statement holds true as long as one remains agnostic about possible UV completions of the model.
In fact, an explicit partial UV completion of the effective model studied in
[
1
]
gives rise to a parametrically smaller cutoff than
\Lambda_{\rm cutoff}
derived above.
4
4

4

See also
[
15
]
for a UV completion of the partial UV model in
[
1
]
. This UV theory involves
n
vector-like fermions and a complex scalar field.

This suggests that the EFT calculation of
n
-point functions may receive
\mathcal{O}(1)
corrections from heavy particles even before the energy scales reaches
\Lambda_{\rm cutoff}
, unless one can show that all such contributions are always subleading for
\omega<\Lambda_{\rm cutoff}
.
In this paper, we restrict our analysis to the unitarity cutoff given by Eq. (
2.4
) (or its numerical counterpart) and remain agnostic regarding the UV completion discussed above.
In that case, we apply the EFT to compute
n
-point correlation functions and their corresponding signal-to-noise ratios, aiming to identify the parameter space where such signals can be detectable.

Signal-to-noise ratio of the
n
-point functions

The connected
n
-point correlation function of
\zeta
can be analytically computed at first order in
\tilde{b}
. Their explicit expressions for large
\alpha
are
[
4
,
2
]

\displaystyle\braket{\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}\cdots\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}}=(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{k}_{\rm t})A_{n}B_{n}(k_{i})\;,

(3.1)

where

\displaystyle A_{n}=(-1)^{n+1}\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\tilde{b}}{2^{n+1}}\alpha^{2n-7/2}P_{\zeta}^{n-1}\;,

(3.2)

and

\displaystyle B_{n}(k_{i})=\frac{1}{k_{\rm t}^{n-3}\prod_{i}k_{i}^{2}}\bigg[\sin(\alpha\log(k_{\rm t}/k_{\star}))+\frac{1}{\alpha}\cos(\alpha\log(k_{\rm t}/k_{\star}))\sum_{i,j}\frac{k_{i}}{k_{j}}\bigg]\;,

(3.3)

with
\boldsymbol{k}_{\rm t}\equiv\sum_{i}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}
,
k_{\rm t}\equiv\sum_{i}k_{i}
,
P_{\zeta}=H^{4}/(2M_{\rm Pl}^{2}|\dot{H}_{0}|)
and
k_{\star}
is a pivot scale. For
n=2
, notice that the power spectrum also has a smooth component besides this resonant signal. The second term in the square bracket of
B_{n}
, suppressed by
1/\alpha
, will be neglected in the following.
These
n
-point functions are the main ingredient we will use below in order to compute the signal-to-noise ratio.
5
5

5

The oscillatory time dependence of the Hubble rate leads to a parametric resonance in the linear equations for
\pi
. Let us show that in the parameter space of interest this growth is not relevant. Neglecting momentarily the expansion of the universe, the linearised equation in momentum space for
\pi_{\boldsymbol{k}}(t)
at linear order in
\tilde{b}
is
\ddot{\pi}_{\boldsymbol{k}}+(\boldsymbol{k}^{2}/a^{2})\pi_{\boldsymbol{k}}+\omega\tilde{b}\sin(\omega t)\dot{\pi}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=0
.
We can recast this into a Mathieu equation by defining
\varphi_{\boldsymbol{k}}\equiv(1+\tilde{b}/2\cos(\omega t))\pi_{\boldsymbol{k}}
. Introducing
z\equiv\omega t/2
, indeed we have
\varphi_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime\prime}+[A_{\boldsymbol{k}}+2q\cos(2\tau)]\varphi_{\boldsymbol{k}}=0
, where
A_{\boldsymbol{k}}\equiv 4\boldsymbol{k}^{2}/(\omega^{2}a^{2})
,
q\equiv-\tilde{b}
and
′
here stands for a derivative with respect to
z
.
For
q\ll 1
, the equation is in the narrow-resonance regime and it develops instability bands around
A_{\boldsymbol{k}}=n^{2}
for
n
integer. The largest growth is for
n=1
, where the Floquet exponent is
\mu_{\boldsymbol{k}}\sim|q|/2
and the width of the band
A_{\boldsymbol{k}}\simeq 1\pm|q|
.
Then, the width translates into
k/a\simeq\omega(1\pm\tilde{b})/2
.
Neglecting the expansion of the universe and evaluating the growth after one Hubble time
\Delta t\sim 2\Delta z/\omega\sim H^{-1}
, we would obtain a growth
\exp(\mu_{\boldsymbol{k}}\Delta z)\sim\exp(\tilde{b}\,\omega/(4H))=\exp(\alpha\tilde{b}/4)
. This is not quite correct since the momentum
\boldsymbol{k}
redshifts and quickly exits the resonant band. This happens when
(\Delta k/a)/(k/a)\sim H\Delta t\sim\tilde{b}
, where the last requirement is the width of the band. Therefore, the accurate estimate for the growth is
\sim\exp(\alpha\tilde{b}^{2}/4)
.
We now argue that we are always in the regime where this growth is small. To see this, let us consider the correction to the power spectrum of
\zeta
in the resonant model at order
\tilde{b}
: it scales as
\sqrt{\alpha}\,\tilde{b}
, see Eq. (
3.1
), and observationally this is bounded by
\sqrt{\alpha}\,\tilde{b}\lesssim 10^{-2}

[
2
]
.
Therefore, we are always in the regime where
\exp(\alpha\tilde{b}^{2}/4)\simeq 1
. Notice that the estimate for the growth depends on the same parameter as the correction to the power spectrum, as expected.

Note that the dimensionless power spectrum whose value is constrained to
2\times 10^{-9}
by CMB observations reads
\Delta_{\zeta}^{2}=P_{\zeta}/(4\pi^{2})
.

These correlators are obtained by evaluating the
in
-
in
time integrals through a saddle-point approximation
[
4
]
. More specifically, in terms of conformal time
\eta
, the saddle is located at
(-k_{\rm t}\eta_{s})=\alpha
. Given that
\alpha
is large, this can be understood as a resonance where the time-dependent interaction
\sim\cos(\omega t)
produces
n
quanta when their frequency matches
\omega
. This implies that the total energy of the quanta can be identified exactly with
\omega
. The latter is then required to be below the perturbative unitarity cutoff found in the previous section.
To make this identification even sharper notice that if there is another state of mass
m
coupled to
\pi
, this state will be generically produced when
\omega\sim m
, making the EFT invalid.

3.1
Optimal estimator

Given the
n
-point functions above, we would like to obtain the optimal estimator for the parameter
\tilde{b}
.
To proceed in this direction, we first define appropriate estimators
\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{n}
for each
n
-point function as follows

\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\equiv\frac{1}{N_{n}n!}\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bigg[\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\bigg](2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{k}_{\rm t})\braket{\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}\cdots\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}}^{\prime}_{|\tilde{b}=1}\frac{\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}\cdots\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}}{P(k_{1})\cdots P(k_{n})}\;.

(3.4)

Here and in the following,
P(k)\equiv\braket{\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k}}\zeta_{-\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime}=P_{\zeta}/(2k^{3})
is the power spectrum and prime denotes the correlators without the factor
(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\sum_{i}\boldsymbol{k}_{i})
.
The normalisation factor
N_{n}
, which we will compute later, is defined such that the estimator is unbiased, i.e.
\langle\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\rangle=\tilde{b}
. The integral over momenta in (
3.4
) is restricted to non-degenerate polygons, i.e. there is no subset of the wavevectors
\boldsymbol{k}_{i}
whose sum vanishes.
6
6

6

Keeping the integral unrestricted would give additional contributions to, e.g. the average and variance of
\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{n}
, involving internal collinear limits.

The expectation values of these estimators read

\braket{\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{n}}=\frac{\tilde{b}\,\mathcal{V}}{N_{n}n!}\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bigg[\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\bigg](2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{k}_{\rm t})\frac{[\braket{\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}\cdots\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}}^{\prime}_{|\tilde{b}=1}]^{2}}{P(k_{1})\cdots P(k_{n})}\;,

(3.5)

where we defined
\mathcal{V}\equiv(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{0})
as the comoving volume (here the Dirac delta is in momentum space).

The optimal estimator is the linear combination of the individual estimators
\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{n}
:

\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm opt}\equiv\sum_{n\geq 2}\mathcal{W}_{n}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\,,

(3.6)

with weights
\mathcal{W}_{n}
such that
\braket{\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm opt}}=\tilde{b}
and the estimator has the minimal variance
\braket{\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm opt}^{2}}-\braket{\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm opt}}^{2}
. The corresponding total signal-to-noise ratio squared is then naturally defined as
(S/N)^{2}_{\rm tot}\equiv\braket{\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm opt}}^{2}/\braket{\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{2}_{\rm opt}}
, and similarly for each individual estimator
\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{n}
. By virtue of restricting estimators to non-degenerate configurations, off-diagonal terms in the covariance matrix
\braket{\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{m}}
vanish.
7
7

7

For the sake of interest, in App.
B
, we show explicitly the effect of including degenerate configurations on the off-diagonal terms of the covariance matrix.

Following the discussion in App. A of
[
16
]
, this makes the derivation of the optimal estimator much easier. First, notice that at the lowest order in
\tilde{b}
,
\braket{\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{2}_{n}}
can be obtained by treating the fields
\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k_{i}}}
in (
3.4
) as Gaussian. One then obtains

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{n}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{V}}{N_{n}^{2}n!}\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bigg[\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\bigg](2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{k}_{\rm t})\frac{[\braket{\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}\cdots\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}}^{\prime}_{|\tilde{b}=1}]^{2}}{P(k_{1})\cdots P(k_{n})}\;,

(3.7)

and the signals-to-noise in each
n
-point function:

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{S}{N}\bigg)^{2}_{n}\equiv\frac{\braket{\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{n}}^{2}}{\braket{\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{2}_{n}}}=\frac{\tilde{b}^{2}\mathcal{V}}{n!}\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bigg[\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\bigg](2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{k}_{\rm t})\frac{[\braket{\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}\cdots\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}}^{\prime}_{|\tilde{b}=1}]^{2}}{P(k_{1})\cdots P(k_{n})}\,,

(3.8)

which are simply related to the normalisation factor as
(S/N)^{2}_{n}=\tilde{b}^{2}N_{n}
.

Now, minimizing
\braket{\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm opt}^{2}}=\sum_{n\geq 2}\mathcal{W}_{n}^{2}\braket{\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{n}^{2}}
subject to
\sum_{n\geq 2}\mathcal{W}_{n}=1
can be straightforwardly done by introducing a Lagrange multiplier to enforce the constraint and taking the derivative with respect to
\mathcal{W}_{n}
. This gives
\mathcal{W}_{n}=N_{n}/(\sum_{m\geq 2}N_{m})
, and the very intuitive result that the total signal-to-noise is the sum in quadrature of the signals-to-noise in each
n
-point function:

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{S}{N}\bigg)^{2}_{{\rm tot}}=\sum_{n}\bigg(\frac{S}{N}\bigg)^{2}_{n}\,,

(3.9)

where, from (
3.1
), one explicitly has

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{S}{N}\bigg)^{2}_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{V}\,\frac{2^{n}A_{n}^{2}}{n!P_{\zeta}^{n}}\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bigg[\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}\,k_{i}}\bigg](2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}\big(\boldsymbol{k}_{\rm t}\big)\,\frac{\sin^{2}(\alpha\log(k_{\rm t}/k_{\star}))}{k_{\rm t}^{2n-6}}\;,

(3.10)

with
A_{n}
given in (
3.2
).

3.2
Evaluation of
(S/N)

Our aim is to evaluate the signal-to-noise ratio (
3.10
).
First we notice that in the limit of rapid oscillations of interest, we can replace the function
\sin^{2}
by its average
1/2
. Indeed, writing
\sin^{2}z=(1-\cos 2z)/2
, one sees that corrections are suppressed at large
\alpha
due to oscillations in the momentum integrals
\sim\exp(-\alpha\pi)
.
The idea then is to factorize the
n
integrals over momenta, in such a way that each integral can be computed separately. At first sight, the presence of
k_{\rm t}
prevents us from proceeding.
We therefore use a trick to replace
1/k_{\rm t}^{2n-6}
with an integral over the Feynman variable
\tau
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k_{\rm t}^{\beta}}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\beta)}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\,(-\tau)^{\beta-1}{\rm e}^{k_{\rm t}\tau}\;,

(3.11)

with
\beta>0
and
k_{\rm t}>0
.
8
8

8

For
n=2
and
n=3
, one can straightforwardly evaluate the
(S/N)
ratio without using the formula (
3.11
).
We thus obtain

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{S}{N}\bigg)^{2}_{n}=\mathcal{V}\,\frac{2^{n}A_{n}^{2}}{n!P_{\zeta}^{n}}\frac{1}{\Gamma(2n-6)}\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bigg[\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}\,k_{i}}\bigg]\int\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{x}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\sum_{i}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\,(-\tau)^{2n-7}{\rm e}^{k_{\rm t}\tau}\sin^{2}(\alpha\log(k_{\rm t}/k_{\star}))\;,

(3.12)

where we wrote

(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}\big(\boldsymbol{k}_{\rm t}\big)=\int\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{x}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\sum_{i}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}}
. In an observation, the range of integration of momenta is limited by some cutoff
k_{\rm max}
that depends on the experiment:
without this cutoff one would be measuring an infinite number of data and therefore the
(S/N)
ratio would be divergent.
In particular, an experiment will have some characteristic window function going to zero above
k_{\rm max}
. In our calculation we assume an exponential window function of the form
\sim\exp(-k_{i}/k_{\rm max})
, which enters in each
\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}

4.1
Measurable
n
-point functions

When interpreting the result (

\tilde{b}
. The smaller
\tilde{b}

Considering a background frequency

Despite having a

that peaks for a certain

To do so, we write the approximate expression (
3.23
) as an exponential
(S/N)^{2}_{n}=\exp(\Phi(n))

around

to second order in

.
Indeed, what controls the width is the second derivative of
\Phi(n)
with respect to
n
evaluated at
n=n_{\rm max}
.
It is straightforward to obtain
\Phi^{\prime\prime}(n_{\rm max})\simeq-3/n_{\rm max}.

Therefore, the signal can be approximated around its peak by a Gaussian of width
\sigma^{2}=-1/\Phi^{\prime\prime}(n_{\rm max})\simeq n_{\rm max}/3
.
This result indicates that the total signal cannot be simply approximated by a single
n
-point function but rather it is given by
\sim n^{1/2}_{\rm max}
correlators, all contributing similarly.
The total signal-to-noise (
3.9

(S/N)^{2}_{\rm tot}\simeq n^{1/2}_{\rm max}\,(S/N)^{2}_{\rm max}
proving an additional (small) boost to the signal.

4.2
Reaching high frequency

To enter the regime of interest of this paper, one needs fast oscillations
\omega>4\pi f
corresponding to
\alpha>210
.
This is a quite high frequency, but that can be constrained by current and future surveys.
Let us analyze this point, concentrating for simplicity on the analysis of the power spectrum described by the following expression:

\frac{k^{3}}{2\pi^{2}}\braket{\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k}}\zeta_{-\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime}=\Delta_{\zeta}^{2}\left[1+A_{\textrm{log}}\cos(\alpha\log k/k_{\star}+\varphi)\right]\;,

(4.3)

where we neglect the tilt
n_{s}
for simplicity,
k_{\star}=0.05\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}/h
is the pivot scale,
A_{\textrm{log}}
and
\alpha
are, respectively, the amplitude and frequency of the resonant feature and
\varphi
is a phase.
Comparing this parameterisation with the expression (
3.1
), we see that
A_{\textrm{log}}
is related to
\alpha
and
\tilde{b}
as
A_{\textrm{log}}=\sqrt{\alpha\pi/2}\,\tilde{b}
.

In order to observe the oscillation of a signal, one needs at least few data points within a single oscillation (Nyquist-Shannon frequency).
The resolution in Fourier space is limited by the size of the survey
L
.
For the model at hand, the frequency of oscillations in Fourier space is not fixed but depends logarithmically on the scale. In particular, around a given point
k
, the frequency of oscillation is of order
\sim\alpha/k
. We want this frequency to be smaller than the limit discussed above:
\alpha/k<L
. This is easier to achieve if one looks at the shortest modes in the survey, so that one gets
\alpha/(k_{\rm max}L)<1
.

Current observational bounds are limited to regions where
0\lesssim\alpha\lesssim 100
and
A_{\textrm{log}}\lesssim 0.04
(see for instance
[
18
,
19
]
). As explained earlier, exploring higher values for
\alpha
requires surveys with larger values of
k_{\rm max}L
. For the current bounds,
\alpha\simeq 100
corresponds to
\omega/(4\pi f)\simeq 0.2
(taking
\Delta_{\zeta}^{2}\simeq 2\times 10^{-9}
).
In this regime, we are at frequencies much lower than the naive cutoff
4\pi f
and, as confirmed in Fig.
2
, the signal is dominated by the correction to the power spectrum.
Upcoming and future surveys will reach
\Delta k\simeq 0.001\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}
, and possibly higher
k_{\rm max}
, which allows to reach
\alpha\simeq 360

[
20
,
21
]
. This would allow to explore the region
\omega/(4\pi f)\simeq 3
, where the bispectrum starts to dominate the signal.

5
Conclusions and future directions

In this paper, we identified a new regime of resonant non-Gaussianity in which the impact on the power spectrum is negligibly small, while higher-point correlation functions can be measurable.

A natural question that arises is how to experimentally probe such a signal, given that a brute-force analysis of an
n
-point function for large
n
appears unfeasible. It will be interesting to explore whether other approaches, not directly based on
n
-point functions (simulation-based inference and field-level inference: see for instance
[
22
]
and references therein) are better tools to constrain exotic initial statistics.

The signal also features very fast oscillations, presenting a significant experimental challenge.
In the standard case (
\omega\lesssim 4\pi f
), when the oscillations of the power spectrum are the most relevant feature, the maximum frequency is
\alpha\simeq 210
: this is an important target in the search of oscillations in the power spectrum. Our new regime features even faster oscillations, up to
\alpha\sim 1000
.

From a theoretical perspective, it is important to systematically explore models that produce large, potentially measurable
n
-point functions. Some such models have already been proposed, typically based on multi-field dynamics (see, e.g.
[
23
,
24
,
16
]
). One promising direction is to investigate localized features to determine if there are regimes with a peculiar hierarchy of correlators, as in the case studied here. In this context, the concept of “boundaries” in field space, introduced in
[
25
]
, is worth further exploration.

While we did not attempt to derive an explicit UV completion of the theory in this paper, we adopted the unitarity limit as a bound. This approach may be overly permissive, and explicit UV completions could impose additional constraints. For instance, the models studied in
[
1
]
suggest a parametrically smaller cutoff. An explicit UV completion could also introduce interesting phenomenological features, see e.g.
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
for recent related analyses.

Another question that arises is the relationship between the perturbativity of the theory and its non-Gaussianity. Naively, the two concepts are closely related: a theory becomes strongly coupled when it becomes fully non-Gaussian, i.e. when the free theory is no longer a good approximation. However, for the model under consideration, these concepts diverge slightly. Indeed, as one moves along the line that describes “strong coupling” in Fig.
2
, the theory remains cosmologically rather Gaussian, at least for small enough
\tilde{b}
. The simple interaction
\dot{\pi}^{n}
studied in App.
C
can be a good starting point for investigating this phenomenon.

Appendix A
S-matrix calculation in flat space

Following
[
1
]
, in this appendix we compute the
n\rightarrow n
matrix element
\mathcal{M}_{n\rightarrow n}
in flat space.

Phase-space integral

Before proceeding to the calculation, let us first evaluate the phase-space integral that usually appears in scattering-amplitude computations, see also
[
14
]
. We define

\displaystyle\Omega_{n}

\displaystyle\equiv\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{p}_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}2E_{\boldsymbol{p}_{i}}}\right)\,(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{(4)}\big(P^{\mu}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}^{\mu}\big)\;,

(A.1)

where
n
is the number of particles in the final state,
p^{\mu}_{i}
and
E_{\boldsymbol{p}_{i}}
are the four-momentum and the energy of particle
i
, and
P^{\mu}
is the total four-momentum of the final state. Then, replacing the delta function in the above expression with exponentials gives

\displaystyle\Omega_{n}

\displaystyle=\int\,\mathrm{d}^{4}x\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{p}_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}2E_{\boldsymbol{p}_{i}}}\right){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}P\cdot x}{\rm e}^{+{\rm i}\sum_{i}^{n}p_{i}\cdot x}

(A.2)

\displaystyle=\int\,\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}P\cdot x}\bigg[\int\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{p}_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}2E_{\boldsymbol{p}_{i}}}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}p_{i}\cdot x}\bigg]^{n}\;,

(A.3)

where the symbol dot here refers to the four dimensional inner product with Minkowski metric.
Note that, for massive particles, we have
E_{\boldsymbol{p}}=\sqrt{\boldsymbol{p}^{2}+m^{2}}
.
We notice that the integral in the square bracket is nothing but the Wightman function
D(x)
for a massive scalar in four dimensions, defined as

\displaystyle D(x)

\displaystyle\equiv\int\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{p}_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}2E_{\boldsymbol{p}_{i}}}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}p_{i}\cdot x}=\frac{m}{(2\pi)^{2}\sqrt{x^{2}}}K_{1}(m\sqrt{x^{2}})\;,

(A.4)

where in the second equality we have assumed that
x^{2}>0
(spacelike separation) and
K_{1}
denotes the modified Bessel function of the second kind (see e.g. Ch. 5.2 of
[
31
]
). Note that for timelike separation (
x^{2}<0
), one instead obtains
D(x)=imH_{1}^{(2)}(m\tau)/(8\pi\tau)
, where
\tau
is a proper time and
H_{1}^{(2)}
is the Hankel function of the second kind. In the massless limit, which is the one relevant for us, the expression (
A.4
) becomes

\displaystyle D(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}[\boldsymbol{x}^{2}-(t-{\rm i}\varepsilon)^{2}]}\;,

(A.5)

where we have properly added the
{\rm i}\varepsilon
prescription for the un-ordered (Wightman) two-point function.
Then, using (
A.5
) in (
A.3
) we have

\displaystyle\Omega_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2n}}\int\,\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\frac{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}P\cdot x}}{\left[\boldsymbol{x}^{2}-(t-{\rm i}\varepsilon)^{2}\right]^{n}}

(A.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2n}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\,\mathrm{d}t\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}P^{0}t}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\,\mathrm{d}r\,\frac{4\pi r^{2}}{\left[r^{2}-(t-{\rm i}\varepsilon)^{2}\right]^{n}}\;,

(A.7)

where in the second line we used
P^{\mu}=(P^{0},\boldsymbol{0})
. The integral over
r
can be performed in closed form, with the
{\rm i}\varepsilon
providing the prescription to avoid the singularities.
We have

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\,\mathrm{d}r\,\frac{4\pi r^{2}}{\left[r^{2}-(t-{\rm i}\varepsilon)^{2}\right]^{n}}=-{\rm i}(-1)^{n}\frac{\pi^{3/2}\,\Gamma(n-3/2)}{\Gamma(n)}(t-{\rm i}\varepsilon)^{3-2n}\;.

(A.8)

The final integral over
t
can be performed using Cauchy’s theorem by noticing the presence of a pole at
t={\rm i}\varepsilon
. Moreover, since
P^{0}>0
the integrand decays exponentially in the region
{\rm Im\,}{(t)}>0
.
We therefore deform the contour to a patch
\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon}
that encloses the pole and write
t={\rm i}\varepsilon+z
around the pole:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\,\mathrm{d}t\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}P^{0}t}(t-{\rm i}\varepsilon)^{3-2n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-P^{0}\varepsilon}\frac{({\rm i}P^{0})^{k}}{k!}\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon}}\,\mathrm{d}z\,z^{3-2n+k}

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{-P^{0}\varepsilon}\frac{({\rm i}P^{0})^{2n-4}}{(2n-4)!}2\pi{\rm i}\;.

(A.11)

In the last line we selected the simple pole, i.e.

Combining Eqs. (

) and (

) in (

(A.12)

Writing the energy

\displaystyle\Omega_{n}=\frac{1}{8\pi}\left(\frac{E}{4\pi}\right)^{2n-4}\frac{1}{(n-1)!(n-2)!}\;,

(A.13)

where we used the property

This result agrees for instance with
[

]
, even though it is obtained in an independent manner.

Scattering amplitude of normalisable states

Plane-wave states are delta-function normalisable and therefore are not suitable for being used in evaluating the perturbative unitarity bound. Instead of using standard partial-waves amplitudes, one can consider the more convenient states:

\displaystyle\ket{P,n}=C\int\,\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}P\cdot x}\big[\phi^{(-)}(x)\big]^{n}\ket{0}\;,

(A.14)

where
C
is a normalisation factor,
P^{\mu}
is the total four-momentum of the state,
\phi^{(-)}(x)
contains the creation operator
a_{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\dagger}
and

is a positive integer. These correspond to
n
-particle states that are normalisable:

\displaystyle\braket{P^{\prime},n^{\prime}|P,n}=(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{(4)}(P-P^{\prime})\delta_{n,n^{\prime}}\;.

As pointed out in
[
14
]
, the states (

) are defined such that they have the largest overlap with the interaction we will consider below.
We can write the scalar field in terms of creation and annihilation operators as usual

A.15

From Eq. (

\displaystyle\braket{P^{\prime},n^{\prime}|P,n}

(A.19)

a_{\boldsymbol{p}}
and

(A.21)

where in the first line we have evaluated the integrals over
x
and
y

A.1

A.13

(A.22)

At this point we are ready to evaluate our scattering amplitude.

\displaystyle\braket{P^{\prime},n^{\prime}|{\rm i}T|P,n}

\displaystyle=\braket{P^{\prime},n^{\prime}|{\rm i}\int\,\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\frac{\lambda_{2n}\phi(x)^{2n}}{(2n)!}|P,n}
