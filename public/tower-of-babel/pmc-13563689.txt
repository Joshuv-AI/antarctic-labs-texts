Posaconazole Target Attainment in Critically Ill Children: A 15‐Year Single‐Center Retrospective Analysis
Authors: Kathryn Pavia, Addison Pritchett, Jennifer Goldman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPosaconazole is a triazole antifungal agent used for prophylaxis and treatment of invasive fungal diseases. Posaconazole has high inter‐individual variability in dose‐exposure relationship and benefits from therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM). Adult studies report particularly high rates of subtherapeutic exposure among intensive care unit (ICU) patients related to changes in physiology during critical illness. The rates of subtherapeutic posaconazole exposure among critically ill children in ICU's are unknown. This single‐center retrospective analysis at a large freestanding children's hospital used TDM results from 2010 to 2025 obtained while patients were undergoing care in a pediatric ICU. Therapeutic targets were ≥ 1.0 mg/L for treatment of infection and ≥ 0.7 mg/L for prophylaxis. A general linear mixed model (GLMM) was used to assess factors which differed between instances with therapeutic and subtherapeutic posaconazole concentrations. Sixty‐two posaconazole concentrations from 30 ICU patients were analyzed, of which 24 (39%) concentrations were below target based on their therapeutic indication. Both univariate analysis and stepwise GLMM identified an association between liquid formulation and subtherapeutic targets (OR 29.6, CI 2.2–398.2, p = 0.01). However, even among critically ill children with IV posaconazole administration 13/46 (28%) measured concentrations remained subtherapeutic. Rates of subtherapeutic posaconazole exposure among critically ill children are high, though lower than reported from adult ICU cohorts. Liquid formulation use was associated with subtherapeutic concentrations, however appreciable instances of subtherapeutic exposure persist in patients receiving IV administration. Further research is needed to identify characteristics of critically ill patients at risk of subtherapeutic posaconazole exposure and to develop strategies to optimize attainment of appropriate medication exposure.

Introduction

Invasive fungal diseases (IFD) confer high morbidity and mortality, and disproportionately affect immunocompromised individuals [1, 2, 3]. Adequate antifungal treatment, together with source control and immune reconstitution, is crucial to supporting survival. Posaconazole is a second generation triazole antifungal agent with activity against several opportunistic yeasts and molds including Candida and Aspergillus species via inhibition of ergosterol synthesis via lanosterol‐14α‐demethylase [4, 5]. Posaconazole liquid suspension was initially approved for adult use in 2006, followed by delayed release tablets in 2013 and intravenous (IV) formulation in 2014. Pediatric licensing initially approved only oral formulations in patients aged 13–18, however as of 2021 approval has been extended to both IV and oral formulations for patients ≥ 2 years of age, and a delayed release powder for oral suspension has been approved [6, 7]. Over the past two decades there has been substantial utilization of oral suspension [8], delayed release tablet [9], and IV [10] formulations in the pediatric population.

Posaconazole exhibits formulation‐dependent variability in the dose‐exposure relationship. The liquid formulation exhibits saturable absorption requiring 3–4 daily doses, and bioavailability is substantially affected by fed state, fat content of the meal, and gastric pH [11, 12, 13]. The delayed release tablet significantly reduces the effect of fed state and fat content on absorption and allows for once daily dosing [14]. Absorption of the delayed release tablet is affected by clinical factors such as diarrhea, mucositis, and co‐administration of proton pump inhibitors [15, 16]. Posaconazole has a large volume of distribution [14, 17], extensive tissue penetration [18], and circulates primarily bound to albumin [7]. Posaconazole is primarily excreted unchanged in feces [7], while minority is glucuronidated via urine diphosphotase glucuronyltransferase (UGT) 1A4 prior to elimination in the urine or feces [19]. Posaconazole is also a substrate for P‐glycoprotein efflux transporters [7]. Even with delayed release and IV formulations, interindividual variability remains appreciable and therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) is recommended to ensure adequate medication exposure particularly in patients with known risk factors, physiologic instability, or invasive disease [20].

While the pharmacokinetic parameter most closely associated with fungicidal activity is the relationship between the area‐under‐the‐curve to the fungal minimum inhibitory concentration (AUC/MIC), this is challenging to calculate at bedside. Instead, trough concentrations which correspond to an adequate AUC against common organisms are used for day‐to‐day TDM. For prophylaxis against fungal infections, a trough serum concentration of 0.7 mg/L has demonstrated efficacy, while concentrations of at least 1 mg/L are required when treating IFD [20, 21].

In adults, risk factors for subtherapeutic posaconazole concentrations following oral administration have included diarrhea [16], severe mucositis [15], and co‐administration of proton pump inhibitor [16] and tacrolimus [22, 23]. Medication administration via nasogastric tubes has also been shown to decrease absorption [24]. Dexamethasone is known to induce UGT enzyme activity [25], and a single study has associated glucocorticoid use with lower posaconazole concentrations [26]. Low albumin concentrations affect total but not unbound posaconazole concentrations [27], which affects TDM interpretation since it is standard for clinical laboratories to report total concentration only. Systemic inflammation measured by C‐reactive protein has been reported to affect posaconazole concentrations in a retrospective evaluation by Jia et al. [8], but this association was not supported in prospective evaluation my Märtson et al. [28].

Critical illness is a time of physiologic derangements, which may affect pharmacokinetics. Critically ill patients commonly experience circulatory dysfunction, organ failure, changes in body composition, and polypharmacy interactions. These physiologic changes can affect medication absorption, distribution, metabolism, and elimination, thereby changing the ability for standard dosing to provide the necessary total medication exposure. Both single center [29] and multicenter [30] studies have shown high rates of failed posaconazole target attainment following IV administration in adult ICU patients.

In general, children demonstrate high variability in posaconazole bioavailability and clearance compared to adults [8, 31]. Critically ill children have the same risks for illness‐related alterations in medication pharmacokinetics, in addition to the developmental differences and dosing challenges related to their age group. Altered pharmacokinetics during pediatric critical illness has been demonstrated for medications such as amoxicillin‐clavulinate [32] and cefepime [33]. Despite the significant consequences of failing to achieve adequate antifungal exposure, rates of posaconazole target attainment among critically ill children have not been described.

Here, we performed a retrospective chart review of posaconazole concentrations obtained for TDM during periods of critical illness to describe rates of therapeutic target attainment among critically ill children at a single pediatric center.

Methods

Study Location and Approval

This study was conducted at a 390‐bed freestanding pediatric hospital with 67 pediatric critical care beds. Transplantation of bone marrow, livers, kidneys, and hearts is performed at this institution. The current electronic health record (EHR) has been in use since 2008. This study was approved by the institutional review board and was granted a waiver of consent due to its retrospective nature. All research activities were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Research design and reporting were conducted in accordance with STROBE best practices [34].

Patient Selection

Patients ≤ 18 years of age treated with posaconazole between 2008 and 2025 were identified by an EHR search using a TriNetX (TriNetX LLC, USA) query for records with (1) RxNorm or CPT codes for posaconazole medication ingredient and/or (2) LOINC codes for posaconazole concentration laboratory measurement. All identified patients underwent dedicated chart review, and patients with one or more posaconazole concentration measurements and clear documentation of posaconazole medication administration in the medication administration record (MAR) were identified for inclusion in the study.

Chart Review

Demographic information including age, sex, race/ethnicity, and primary diagnoses were extracted from the EHR. Leukemia and lymphoma as well as myelodysplastic syndrome with abnormalities were categorized as hematologic malignancies. Nonmalignant blood disorders included sickle cell anemia, alpha or beta thalassemia, and aplastic anemia. Both primary immunodeficiencies (chronic granulomatous disease, severe combined immunodeficiency) and primary immunodysregulatory disorders (primary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, X‐linked lymphoproliferative disease) were categorized as primary immunologic disorders. Age and weight were recorded on the day of ICU admission. Length of stay (LOS) was recorded for both ICU stay and total hospitalization. Each posaconazole concentration measurement was recorded as a separate datapoint. If more than one posaconazole concentration was measured on the same calendar day, only the concentration measured furthest after posaconazole administration was used as trough concentration. Posaconazole dose and route of administration were recorded for the 5 doses prior to each measurement. Standard dosing guidance [7, 35] is provided in Table S1. On the day of each posaconazole concentration, the patient location (outpatient, inpatient, or ICU) was identified. On days of admission, transfer, or discharge, the highest level of acuity on the calendar day was chosen.

Additional data recorded from the medical record included laboratory values, administration of supplemental albumin (25% or 5%), proton pump inhibitors (pantoprazole, omeprazole, lansoprazole, or esomeprazole), H2 blockers (ranitidine, famotidine, cimetidine), dexamethasone, tacrolimus, and use of extracorporeal therapies (extracorporeal membrane oxygenation [ECMO], continuous renal replacement therapy [CRRT], hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, or plasmapheresis) on the day of or day prior to the posaconazole concentration. When multiple laboratory values were available on a single calendar day, the earliest one was recorded. Posaconazole dose, route, and time of administration were confirmed from the medication administration record in the EHR. Patients who received supplemental albumin within ±24 h from the lab draw had their albumin concentrations censored due to insufficient temporal resolution to determine the order of draw and supplementation.

Characteristics of Posaconazole Course and Associated Fungal Infections

Dates, indication, and culture results related to the posaconazole course containing the ICU concentration were collected. Notes from infectious diseases specialists were reviewed when available to identify indication for therapy and infection characteristics. Target trough concentrations were defined as ≥ 0.7 mg/L for prophylaxis indications and ≥ 1.0 mg/L for treatment indications [17, 20, 21]. Invasive fungal disease was classified according to revised European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer and the Mycoses study group (EORTC/MSG) criteria [36]. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) of cultured organisms against posaconazole were reported from the EHR.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R (version 4.2.3). Categorical variables with > 2 categories and low counts in multiple categories collapsed into dichotomous variables: white/nonwhite for race, any extracorporeal support/none. Univariate comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables and the Pearson chi‐squared test or Fisher's Exact Test for categorical variables.

Prior to generalized linear mixed modeling (GLMM), continuous variables were assessed for skew and missingness. Skew was calculated using R package “e1071”, version 1.7.17, and variables with an absolute skew value > 1.5 were log transformed. Missingness was only observed in the four lab value covariates of interest: albumin, total bilirubin, direct bilirubin, and CRP, with missingness of 35%, 25%, 24%, and 41%, respectively. As a complete‐case approach would have reduced the sample size from 62 to 26, imputation was deemed appropriate for use with these variables. Missing data was imputed using the method “2l.pan” in R package “mice”, version 3.18.0. This method utilizes a Bayesian multilevel modeling approach to account for within and between cluster variation, which is suited to the repeated‐measures structure of this data. Subject ID was specified as a clustering variable in the imputation model, and other covariates (transplant, indication, sex, race, route of admin, age, current weight, receipt of supplemental albumin, white blood cell count, platelet count, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, alkaline phosphatase, alanine aminotransferase, potassium, and bicarbonate) were used as fixed effects, also allowing the model to account for a variety of subject‐level covariates to impute the lab values of interest more accurately.

GLMMs were fit across 20 imputed datasets using the with () function in the mice R package. Parameter estimates were combined according to Rubin's Rules via the pool() function to assess model performance across all imputations. A series of nested multilevel logistic regression models (binomial distribution, logit link) were fitted using the lme4 package version 1.1.34 to evaluate the contribution of predictors to a dichotomous outcome of “subtherapeutic posaconazole threshold”. Results

Of 166 patients identified with posaconazole medication administration codes starting in 2010, 101 patients had at least one posaconazole concentration recorded, 30 of whom had at least one concentration measured in an ICU (Figure 1). Of the 30 patients with ICU concentrations, the most common medical comorbidities were hematologic malignancies, followed by nonmalignant blood diseases and primary immune disorders (Table 1). Forty percent of patients (12/30) had undergone bone marrow transplantation. There was a high representation of adolescents, with a median age of 13.2 years (range 2.5–17.5) and median weight of 49.0 kg at ICU admission (range 11.4–96.5). Median ICU length of stay was 13.5 days, with only 61% of patients surviving to hospital discharge.

Sixty‐two posaconazole concentrations were available from the 30 patients, with a median of 2 samples per patient (range 1–7). Features related to posaconazole measurements are presented in Table 2. While the most common route of administration preceding a concentration measurement in the ICU was intravenous (n = 46, 74%), there were cases of both liquid suspension (n = 11, 18%) and delayed release tablets (n = 5, 8%) used during critical illness. The EHR did not consistently differentiate oral versus feeding tube routes for enteral administration. Most concentrations (46/62, 74%) were measured in patients for whom posaconazole was intended to treat suspected or confirmed fungal infection. The median trough concentration across all measurements was 1.1 mg/L. Therapeutic trough concentrations were defined as ≥ 0.7 mg/L for prophylactic indication and ≥ 1 mg/L for treatment indication [17, 21]. Based on these thresholds, each concentration dichotomized as “subtherapeutic” or “therapeutic” outcome, revealing that 24/62 (39%) of concentrations were subtherapeutic (Table 2). Univariate comparison identified route of administration (p = 0.0014) and albumin (p = 0.02) as the only factors that differed significantly between measurements with therapeutic vs. subtherapeutic concentrations (Table 3). The incidence of significant hypoalbuminemia was low (albumin < 2.5 g/dL in 4/40) and the distribution of albumin concentrations were narrow (median 3.3 g/dL, IQR 3.0–3.5).

The intraclass correlation (ICC) coefficient for the random subject intercept was 0.213, indicating that 21.3% of the variance is due to between‐subject differences. The final model demonstrated acceptable discrimination based on fixed‐effects alone (marginal AUC = 0.71). When subject‐specific random intercepts were incorporated (ICC = 0.21), model discrimination improved substantially (conditional AUC = 0.88), justifying the mixed‐effects nature of the modeling approach. The final model was well‐calibrated, with marginal calibration metrics of intercept 0.084 of and a slope of 0.956 identified. Only liquid formulation emerged as having a significant association with subtherapeutic concentration compared to IV administration (OR 29.6, CI 2.2–398.3, p = 0.012) (Table 4). Notably, the wide confidence interval secondary to small sample size indicates the true magnitude of effect is uncertain. This result was also reflected in bootstrap resampling of OR estimates, with sparse counts of tablet and liquid administration in the initial dataset leading to unstable bootstrap resampling and thus identifying a significant association for liquid dosing with an uncertain magnitude.

We performed a subanalysis only of patients receiving IV posaconazole to better reflect a reliable route of delivery. Among 46 concentrations following IV administration, 13 (28%) were subtherapeutic despite similar patient characteristics (Table S3). Consistent with the observed incidence in this cohort, the intercept from the final model corresponded to an estimated baseline probability of 26.4% of subtherapeutic concentrations in the IV reference group.

Finally, we described the fungal infections represented within this cohort. Sixty‐three percent (19/30) of ICU patients received posaconazole with the intent to treat suspected or proven fungal infection. Among these patients, only 2 met criteria for proven invasive fungal disease, and 7 were categorized as probable disease (Table 5). Two patients had proven localized disease without evidence of systemic dissemination. The remaining 8 patients had clinical concern for or invasive fungal disease but workup which did not meet EORTC criteria. Sites of infection and causative organisms were diverse, and many of the identified fungal organisms do not have consensus breakpoints for posaconazole susceptibility interpretation. Many patients, including those with higher MIC and those with CNS disease, received posaconazole in combination with other antifungal agents. Rates of IV posaconazole use were similar in the group being treated for suspected or confirmed disease compared to the total population (Table S4).

Discussion

Nearly 40% of posaconazole trough concentrations in our critically ill pediatric cohort failed to meet therapeutic targets for their indication. While this study is unable to directly assess rates of microbiologic failure and thus validate pharmacodynamic targets, optimizing antifungal concentrations represents a crucial treatment factor in a disease with high morbidity and mortality. As such, the frequency of subtherapeutic concentrations reported here, particularly among patients being treated for suspected or proven IFD, are concerning and represent an urgent area for ongoing research and improvement.

Overall rates of subtherapeutic concentrations in our cohort were lower than the 56%–74% subtherapeutic rates reported following IV administration in adult ICU studies. One single center adult study followed the pharmacokinetics of IV posaconazole at a dose of 300 mg daily over the first week of treatment in 10 critically ill adults and found that overall 74% of patients failed to achieve adequate concentrations to treat IFD including 56% who remained subtherapeutic by day ≥ 6 [29]. A subsequent multicenter adult study of antifungal pharmacokinetics during critical illness found that 62.5% of patients receiving posaconazole failed to reach treatment targets on Days 1–3, but had limited numbers for analysis after Day 4 [30].

Pediatric patients may have different features of critical illness such as different rates of acute or chronic hepatobiliary dysfunction, different nutritional status, or differences in plasma binding proteins. Pediatric patients in this study also may have had different severity of illness than those included in the adult studies, since ICU admission criteria are not standardized. While we did not detect an effect of weight on risk of subtherapeutic concentrations, it is notable that this cohort is skewed toward adolescent patients, possibly due to the licensing history of posaconazole formulations during the study period. Adolescents > 40 kg receiving fixed doses of posaconazole (Table S5) in this study are smaller than the adult patients receiving fixed doses in the ICU studies (median weight 61 kg in our cohort vs. 77 [30]–82 [29] kg in adult literature). As a result, these “standard doses” may provide greater systemic exposure in our adolescents compared to the typical adult. One study of critically ill adults found higher BMI to be associated with lower rates of target attainment [27], so it follows that low weights in adolescents could have aided target attainment. Most of the ICU concentrations in this cohort did not represent newly initiated posaconazole, since TDM concentrations are frequently obtained 5–7 days following initiation or dose adjustment. These TDM measurements may more accurately represent accumulation to steady state compared to studies which measured concentrations within the first 24–72 h of initiation. This aligns with the single center adult findings which showed improved rates of therapeutic concentrations from 0% to 44% between Day 2 and Day ≥ 6 [29]. Finally, and possibly most importantly, TDM is routine regardless of formulation at this institution. Therefore patients with multiple TDM measurements likely had dose adjusted in response to previous concentrations, which may have improved rates of therapeutic concentrations. Fourteen of the concentrations included here reflected patients with weight ≥ 40 kg receiving IV regimens of ≥ 400 mg daily (Table S5), dosing which was predicted to provide superior target attainment in the adult popPK model of critical illness [37].

The association between liquid formulation and subtherapeutic concentrations is consistent with adult ICU literature. Two studies have demonstrated significant challenges in achieving adequate posaconazole concentrations when the liquid suspension is administered to critically ill adults via feeding tubes. A 2011 study interrogating optimal enteral dosing schedules found that only 1 out of 27 patients was able to achieve therapeutic posaconazole concentrations via this route [38]. A 2022 study of posaconazole prophylaxis against aspergillosis in adults with COVID‐19 demonstrated that all patients exhibited Day 2 posaconazole concentrations below projected targets for prophylaxis [39]. These studies have been used as rationale that intravenous posaconazole administration is indicated for critically ill adults.

The findings in our pediatric ICU cohort are similar to those reported in several general pediatric populations. General pediatric studies have reported rates of subtherapeutic concentrations ranging from 25% to 50% [40, 41, 42, 43]. Liquid formulation has consistently shown more variable bioavailability than delayed release tablets or the powder for suspension among healthy patients or patients on general oncology floors [44, 45]. We were unable to detect an impact of tacrolimus co‐administration on likelihood of subtherapeutic trough, as has been reported in other pediatric studies [22, 23, 42], but co‐administration was uncommon in our cohort. Similarly, the impact of dexamethasone co‐administration did not achieve statistical significance in univariate analysis, but the relatively small number of cases with this co‐administration may render this study under‐powered to detect such an effect, if present. Larger studies would be useful to evaluate effects of the diverse range of co‐administered medications in critical care populations.

The use of retrospective TDM data is a limiting factor in this study. TDM concentrations are typically collected at trough timepoints and therefore provide limited information about the absorption and distribution phase. Sampling during the absorption phase in future studies would be helpful to elucidate alterations related to delayed motility, intestinal edema, feeding tube delivery to gastric or postpyloric locations, and other alterations which may affect absorption of enteral routes of administration during critical illness. Prospective studies may wish to pay particular attention to the location of delivery of enteral medications, as many ICU patients are unable to take medications by mouth and instead have medications delivered to gastric or postpyloric locations via feeding tubes. Given the known impacts of fed/fasted state, fat content, and pH on posaconazole absorption, administration to the duodenum or jejunum would be expected to have important effects on posaconazole absorption. Studying patients who transition from enteral formulations to IV administration could provide information about altered bioavailability.

The ability to detect abnormal laboratory values associated with subtherapeutic concentrations in this study may have been limited by patients lacking lab values on the day of posaconazole concentration as well as low rates of clinically significant hypoalbuminemia in this cohort. Posaconazole is a highly protein bound medication, and an adult ICU study found that patients with hypoalbuminemia had a lower likelihood of achieving target total posaconazole concentrations, but not no change in unbound posaconazole concentrations [27]. Limited numbers of patients receiving extracorporeal support as well as an absence of dense sampling limits the ability to assess its impact on pharmacokinetics. Adult case reports indicate minimal posaconazole removal by the CRRT circuit [46, 47], while a single pediatric case series found no sequestration of posaconazole in ECMO circuits [48], and an early adult licensing study reported less than 3% removal of posaconazole during outpatient dialysis sessions [49].

Clinical prescribing practices for pediatric posaconazole likely changed significantly during the course of this study. Only the liquid formulation of posaconazole was available until 2013. Furthermore, between 2013 and 2021, only the oral liquid and delayed release tablet were FDA approved for pediatric use in the United States, with IV formulations being used off‐label for patients under 18 years of age. While pediatric centers have gained significant experience with off‐label use of IV formulations throughout the past decade, this approval history may partially account for the prevalence of adolescents who more closely resemble adult physiology in this study cohort. The prevalence of adolescents in this cohort may mask the ability to evaluate features specific to smaller patients, such as weight‐based dosing and the cutting or crushing of delayed release tablets. No instances of the delayed release powder for suspension (approved in 2021) were identified in this critically ill cohort since it is not on formulary at our institution.

Among the patients receiving posaconazole for treatment indications, a minority met criteria for proven or probable invasive fungal disease according to EORTC/MSC. Furthermore, while recommended trough concentration targets based on prevention and treatment of aspergillosis are routinely generalized to diverse IFI [20], none of the patients in this cohort had isolated aspergillus infection. Indeed, most patients with identified organisms had infectious agents without a defined posaconazole susceptibility breakpoint. While the identified prophylaxis and treatment targets reflect those used for clinical decision making, it is possible that specific non‐aspergillus infections have different optimal posaconazole exposure. This limits our ability to relate failed posaconazole target attainment to microbiological failure. The medical complexity of this population limits the ability to attribute mortality directly to fungal infections rather than to concurrent viral/bacterial infections or underlying disease.

There are several limitations to this study. Due to the nature of retrospective chart review, information regarding race/ethnicity and past medical history is subject to accuracy of charting; therefore, the EHR may lack patient‐reported identifiers and/or conditions diagnosed outside of the current hospital system. TDM concentrations in real use scenarios are not always collected at optimal trough timepoints due to clinical constraints or feasibility, although early collection would be expected to underestimate rates of subtherapeutic troughs. Due to the long half‐life of posaconazole, information about concurrent medications and extracorporeal therapies used within the 24 h prior to a concentration measurement may incompletely represent the factors affecting the steady state reached over 5–7 days. Ability to assess correlations with albumin concentration, inflammatory markers, or bilirubin may be affected by incomplete data, as patients who did not have laboratory values measured on the day of posaconazole concentration might differ significantly from those who did. TDM data reported at our institution reflect total posaconazole concentration, therefore limiting our ability to interrogate the relationship between total and unbound concentration associated with changes in albumin concentration.

Overall, this study demonstrates that subtherapeutic posaconazole concentrations affect a significant number of critically ill children. While subtherapeutic concentrations in this pediatric ICU cohort were less frequent than those reported in adult ICU studies, there remains room for improvement in treatment strategies for this highly vulnerable group. Liquid formulation was the only risk factor for subtherapeutic concentrations identified in this cohort; however, more than one quarter of patients receiving IV posaconazole also had concentrations which were subtherapeutic for indication. Overall inter‐individual variability remains significant and worthy of further study.
