Factors Associated With Willingness to Support Organ Donation in Trinidad and Tobago
Authors: Gloria Ramdeen-Mootoo, Tamar Rodney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionOrgan donation and transplantation remain underutilized in Trinidad and Tobago despite the rising burden of chronic kidney and end-stage renal disease. Limited public understanding, low awareness of existing legislation, and persistent sociocultural and religious concerns contribute to low donation rates. This study examines knowledge, attitudes, and perceived barriers toward organ donation within the country's sociocultural and legislative context.Research QuestionsWhat demographic, psychosocial, and systemic factors influence willingness to donate organs, and how do knowledge, attitudes, cultural beliefs, and family discussions shape support for organ donation?DesignA national cross-sectional household survey was conducted using stratified cluster sampling across 1047 households. A modified version of the 2012 U.S. National Survey of Organ Donation assessed public awareness, willingness to donate, and influencing factors. Analyses included 1-way analysis of variance and Pearson correlation to evaluate relationships between demographic variables, knowledge, and willingness to donate.ResultsAmong 1047 respondents, 80.4% reported no knowledge of the Human Tissue Transplant Act. Higher education was positively associated with willingness to donate (P < .05). Misconceptions, fear, and religious concerns were major barriers. Family discussion strongly predicted willingness, explaining 31.7% of the variance. Although 70% opposed financial incentives, those who supported incentives showed higher willingness to donate (P < .05).ConclusionOrgan donation readiness in Trinidad and Tobago remains limited due to low legal awareness and sociocultural barriers. Promoting informed family discussions and implementing culturally responsive education campaigns may enhance willingness to donate and strengthen the national transplantation framework.

Introduction

The increasing prevalence of chronic kidney disease and end-stage renal disease presents a growing public health challenge in Trinidad and Tobago. Organ transplantation remains one of the most effective treatments for patients with organ failure and improves survival, quality of life, and long-term healthcare costs.1,2 Despite these benefits, organ donation rates remain limited in many regions.

Several barriers to organ donation have been identified globally, including medical mistrust, cultural beliefs, misinformation, and emotional decision-making by family members. 3 Legislative frameworks may also influence donation practices. In Trinidad and Tobago, the Human Tissue Transplant Act (2000) established the legal foundation for organ donation and transplantation; however, consent withdrawal by family members and limited public awareness continue to affect donation rates. 4 Research examining attitudes toward organ donation has primarily been conducted in North America and Europe, with relatively limited data available from the Caribbean region.5-7 Existing studies suggest that knowledge, cultural norms, and family discussions significantly influence willingness to donate.8-11 Understanding these factors is essential for developing targeted health promotion strategies. This study aimed to examine determinants of organ donation and transplantation in Trinidad and Tobago by assessing public knowledge, attitudes, and factors associated with willingness to support organ donation.

Methods

Design

A cross-sectional quantitative survey design was used to examine knowledge, attitudes, and willingness to support organ donation among adults in Trinidad and Tobago. The target population for this study consisted of adults aged 18 years and older residing in private households in Trinidad and Tobago. According to national census estimates, Trinidad and Tobago has a population of approximately 1.33 million, with adults aged 18 and older accounting for approximately 72% of the population. The national population has a median age of approximately 36 years, and the gender distribution is relatively balanced, with approximately 50.5% female and 49.5% male residents. Trinidad and Tobago is characterized by considerable ethnic diversity. National census data indicate that the population includes individuals of East Indian descent (approximately 37%), African descent (approximately 31%), and mixed ancestry (approximately 24%), with smaller proportions identifying as European, Chinese, Middle Eastern, or other ethnic backgrounds. Religious affiliation is similarly diverse, with Christianity representing the largest religious group (approximately 60%), followed by Hinduism (approximately 22%), Islam (approximately 6%), and smaller proportions reporting other religions or no religious affiliation. Educational attainment varies across the population, with the majority of adults completing secondary-level education, and smaller proportions completing technical, vocational, or tertiary education. These demographic characteristics provide a reference framework for assessing the representativeness of the study sample described in the Results section.

Sampling Strategy

A multistage stratified cluster sampling approach was used. Enumeration districts (EDs) served as primary sampling units and were selected using probability proportional to size sampling. The EDs were pre-existing geographic sampling units established and used by the Central Statistical Office (CSO) of Trinidad and Tobago and were not created specifically for this study. The CSO supported sample selection and data collection. Using a multistage, stratified cluster sampling approach, EDs served as the primary sampling units and households as the secondary sampling units. EDs were selected using probability proportional to size, followed by selection of households within each selected ED. Data were collected by trained enumerators using the study questionnaire. The sample size calculation incorporated an adjustment for anticipated nonresponse, and the multistage design was intended to capture population variability across the country's geographic areas. A total of 103 EDs were selected across municipalities. Within each district, households were randomly selected and 1 adult respondent aged 18 years or older was interviewed. Sample size calculations considered estimated willingness to donate, a margin of error of 0.05, and a design effect to account for cluster sampling. The calculated sample size was increased to ensure adequate statistical precision, resulting in a final sample of 1047 respondents.

Measures

Data were collected using a modified version of the 2012 U.S. National Survey of Organ Donation Attitudes and Behaviors. 12 The national survey built on earlier U.S. surveys of organ donation attitudes and behaviors and assessed public knowledge, beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors related to organ donation and transplantation. The instrument was selected because these domains were relevant to the study objectives. The questionnaire was adapted to enhance its relevance to the sociocultural and organ donation and transplantation context of Trinidad and Tobago. Independent variables included knowledge of organ donation and transplantation, attitudes toward organ donation, and demographic characteristics such as age, education level, marital status, employment status, and religious affiliation. The primary outcome variable was willingness to support organ donation.

Instrument adaptation was informed by a review of the literature, the investigator's experience, and consultation with subject-matter experts. Expert review included 2 sociologists, 2 nephrologists, a transplant procurement manager/anesthetist, and 2 senior nurse educators. The experts reviewed the content and format of the questionnaire and its appropriateness for assessing organ donation knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, and willingness within the local context. The original 65-item instrument was subsequently modified to 53 items.

The final questionnaire comprised 3 sections: (1) a 38-item interviewer-administered knowledge questionnaire and attitude scale addressing organ donation and transplantation; (2) 8 items assessing respondents’ health status; and (3) 7 items assessing demographic characteristics. The instrument assessed knowledge and awareness of organ donation and transplantation, willingness to donate, beliefs regarding living and deceased donation, family communication and decision-making, religious and cultural beliefs, misconceptions about donation and transplantation, financial considerations, and attitudes toward organ donation policies.

Before the main study, the modified questionnaire was pretested with a convenience sample of 10 adults in Trinidad and Tobago who were similar in characteristics to the intended study population. The pretest was conducted approximately 3 weeks before initiation of the main data collection and assessed whether questionnaire items and response options were clearly understood as intended, as well as the clarity of item wording and the feasibility and practicality of administering the questionnaire. Feedback from the pretest was used to refine the instrument before final administration. The willingness-to-support-organ-donation scale demonstrated good internal consistency in the study sample (Cronbach's α = .864), with higher scores reflecting greater willingness to support organ donation.

Data Collection

Data were collected through face-to-face household interviews in collaboration with the CSO of Trinidad and Tobago. The CSO provided technical and logistical support for the national survey, including assistance with the sampling design and access to national maps identifying municipalities, EDs, and households within each district. The Chief Census Officer served as the institutional liaison between the CSO and the research team throughout the survey process. Enumerators were trained by the principal investigator before data collection. Training focused on standardized administration of the questionnaire, with all survey questions, terminology, and response options reviewed in detail to promote consistency in interpretation and administration across households. Standardized procedures were followed during household data collection. The study was supported by a research grant from the Graduate Studies Department of The University of the West Indies.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from The University of the West Indies (UWI) Campus Ethics Committee (CEC360) and the Ministry of Health, Trinidad and Tobago. Before participation, respondents were informed of the nature, aims, and objectives of the study, and informed consent was obtained from all individuals who agreed to participate. Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality and anonymity were maintained throughout the study. Completed questionnaires were stored securely in a locked location accessible to the researcher, and electronic study data were maintained on a password-protected computer.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 23. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and study variables. Frequencies and percentages were calculated for categorical variables and means and standard deviations for continuous variables. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. Bivariate associations with willingness to support organ donation were examined using Pearson or Spearman correlation, independent-samples t tests, and 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate. Pearson correlation was used for normally distributed continuous variables and Spearman correlation when normality assumptions were not met. Independent-samples t-tests were used to compare willingness across independent variables with 2 categories, and 1-way ANOVA was used for variables with 3 or more categories. The dependent variable was assessed for normality, and homogeneity of variance across groups was evaluated using Levene's test. When the homogeneity-of-variance assumption was violated, Welch's ANOVA was used. For Pearson correlation analyses, squared correlation coefficients (r2) were used to quantify the proportion of variance in willingness associated with the relationship. For ANOVA analyses, effect-size estimates were used to characterize the magnitude of observed group differences, including the proportion of variation associated with the grouping variable where available.

Variables significantly associated with willingness at the bivariate level were subsequently entered into a forced-entry multiple linear regression model to examine their independent associations with willingness to support organ donation. Categorical predictors were dummy coded. Regression assumptions were evaluated using residual diagnostics, Cook's distance, the Durbin-Watson statistic, variance inflation factors, and tolerance statistics. Item-level nonresponse resulted in variation in the number of valid observations across variables and analyses. Missing values were not imputed; analyses were conducted using available observations with complete data for the variables included in each respective analysis. Accordingly, the analytic sample size (n) is reported for each analysis where applicable.

Results

Participant Characteristics

The total study sample included 1047 participants; however, item-level nonresponse resulted in varying analytic sample sizes. For the principal analysis, valid observations ranged from 494 to 894. Analyses of knowledge and willingness included 886 participants, legislative awareness included 894, financial compensation included 865, and beliefs in organ donation myths included 494. Analyses were based on available observations for each respective set of variables. Variable-specific denominators are reported in Table 1. Participants included 49.8% males (n = 521) and 50.2% females (n = 526). The majority were married (45.9%), followed by single (26.1%). Most respondents reported being parents (79%). Educational attainment varied, with the largest group reporting completion of secondary education. Religious affiliation was primarily Christian (60.9%), followed by Hindu (24.3%).

Attitudes and Beliefs Regarding Organ Donation

Overall, respondents expressed several favorable attitudes toward organ donation while also demonstrating important misconceptions. Most respondents agreed that an individual's wish to donate should be honored even over the objections of surviving family members (83.2%), that organ donation may help families cope with grief (70.6%), and that physicians do everything possible to save a person's life before organ donation is considered (71.1%). In addition, 88.8% recognized that individuals on the national waiting list may die because an organ does not become available on time. Nevertheless, substantial misconceptions remained. Nearly half of respondents agreed that a brain-dead person could recover from their injuries (45.5%), 43.6% believed that physicians might be less likely to save the life of a person who intended to become an organ donor, and 44.4% believed that families could incur additional medical expenses following donation. Perceptions of inequity in organ allocation were also evident, with 50.4% disagreeing with the opinion that a person with limited financial resources has the same chance as a wealthy person of receiving an organ transplant. In contrast, only 8.2% agreed that organ and tissue donation was against their religion. Valid sample sizes varied across individual items because of item-level nonresponse (Table 3).

Factors Associated with Willingness to Support Organ Donation

Several factors were significantly associated with willingness to support organ donation. Awareness of organ donation legislation was associated with differences in willingness, F (2, 891) = 5.394, P = .005, accounting for approximately 1.2% of the observed variation. Respondents who were aware of the legislation reported greater willingness (M = 18.06, SD = 4.92) than those who were unaware (M = 16.59, SD = 5.49) or uncertain about their awareness (M = 15.30, SD = 5.83). Knowledge of organ donation and transplantation services was moderately and positively associated with willingness to support organ donation (Pearson's r = .395, 95% CI [.338, .449], P < .001, n = 886). The corresponding r2 = .156 indicating that approximately 15.6% of the variance in willingness was associated with knowledge. Belief in organ donation myths was inversely associated with willingness (r = −.310, P < .001), with the squared correlation (r2 = .096) indicating that myths accounted for approximately 9.6% of the variance in willingness. Attitudes toward financial compensation were also associated with willingness, Welch's F (2, 197.14) = 78.94, P < .001, accounting for approximately 12% of the observed variation. Respondents who indicated that compensation would increase their likelihood of donation reported greater willingness (M = 20.28, SD = 3.85) than those who reported no influence of compensation (M = 16.33, SD = 5.28) or were undecided (M = 13.67, SD = 5.65). Family communication demonstrated the largest bivariate association with willingness. Willingness differed significantly according to respondents’ inclination to discuss organ donation wishes with family members, Welch's F (2, 151.37) = 173.70, P < .05. Respondents willing to discuss their wishes had higher willingness scores (M = 18.85, SD = 4.38) than those who were neutral (M = 14.26, SD = 4.44) or unwilling to discuss their wishes (M = 12.18, SD = 4.77). Differences in family communication were associated with approximately 31.7% of the observed variation in willingness.

In the multivariable analysis, the overall regression model was statistically significant, F (10, 722) = 53.71, P < .001, and explained 42.7% of the variance in willingness to support organ donation (R2 = .427). After adjustment for the other variables in the model, family communication remained the factor most strongly associated with willingness. Respondents who were unwilling to discuss their organ donation wishes with family members had willingness scores 5.49 points lower than those willing to discuss their wishes (B = −5.491, β = −.466, P < .001). Greater knowledge of organ donation and transplantation was positively associated with willingness (B = 1.090, β = .177, P < .001). Attitudes toward financial compensation and having less than a secondary education were also significantly associated with willingness (Table 4), whereas secondary education, trade/technical/vocational training, perceived family health, and perceived self-health were not statistically significant in the adjusted model (Table 5).

Discussion

Public Knowledge and Awareness of Organ Donation

The findings from this study highlight substantial gaps in public knowledge and awareness regarding organ donation and transplantation in Trinidad and Tobago. Despite the enactment of the Human Tissue Transplant Act in 2000, which established the legal framework for organ donation and led to the creation of the National Organ Transplant Unit, public awareness of the legislation remains limited. Less than a fifth of the respondents reported awareness of the Act. Although awareness of organ donation legislation was significantly associated with willingness, the magnitude of this association was small, corresponding to approximately 1.2% of the observed variation in willingness. These findings suggest that while legislative awareness contributes to positive attitudes, knowledge of policy alone may not be sufficient to substantially influence donation behavior. Similar observations have been reported in other settings where legal frameworks exist, but public awareness remains limited. Similar observations have been reported within the Caribbean, where the existence of organ donation infrastructure has not necessarily translated into uniformly favorable public attitudes or sufficient awareness. In Trinidad and Tobago, Onuoha et al 13 identified persistent gaps between knowledge and attitudes toward organ donation and emphasized the need for targeted public education to address unfavorable attitudes and strengthen community engagement with organ donation. Further examination of respondents’ knowledge of organ donation terminology, services, and the national organ donor registry revealed persistent misconceptions. Many participants associate organ donation with organ trafficking and other myths, reflecting a significant barrier to public trust and support. Only about one-third of respondents had heard of the national organ donor registry, indicating that exposure to information about organ donation remains limited. Knowledge of organ donation services explained 15.6% of the variance in willingness to support donation, highlighting the importance of health literacy and public awareness in shaping attitudes toward donation. These findings are consistent with previous research demonstrating that knowledge deficits are a major barrier to organ donation and that increased education is associated with greater willingness to donate. 14

Belief in myths surrounding organ donation also negatively influenced willingness to donate. Respondents who endorsed misconceptions about organ trafficking, allocation practices, or bodily disfigurement were significantly less likely to support donation, with myths accounting for 9.6% of the variation in willingness. These findings align with previous studies demonstrating that misinformation and superstition can reduce trust in healthcare systems and discourage individuals from considering organ donation. 15 Addressing these misconceptions through culturally appropriate public education campaigns and transparent communication about transplantation processes may therefore be essential for improving public trust and donor participation.

Willingness to Support Organ Donation

Trinidad and Tobago currently operates under an opt-in system for organ donation, regulated by the Human Tissue Transplant Act. This approach differs from opt-out (presumed consent) systems implemented in several European countries that have achieved higher donor rates. When respondents in this study were asked about their willingness to support a transition to a presumed consent system, approximately half expressed opposition to such a change. These findings suggest that public acceptance of presumed consent may be limited in the current sociocultural context. Similar findings were reported by Onuoha and colleagues, 13 who observed resistance to presumed consent in Trinidad and Tobago even among individuals who demonstrated relatively high knowledge of organ donation. Consequently, efforts to improve organ donation rates in Trinidad and Tobago may be more effective if they focus on strengthening the existing opt-in framework through public education, improved donor registration systems, and enhanced communication strategies rather than advocating for immediate legislative change.

Organ donation rates in Trinidad and Tobago remain considerably lower than those reported in countries with more developed transplantation systems such as the United States, Spain, and Turkey. 16 The results of this study also indicate that living donation appears to be more prevalent than deceased donation within the national context. This pattern suggests that living donation may represent an important opportunity for expanding transplantation services while deceased donor systems continue to develop. Approximately one-third of respondents expressed willingness to register as organ donors, and many participants indicated willingness to donate specific organs such as kidneys, bone marrow, liver, and pancreas. Notably, respondents expressed greater willingness to donate organs to family members compared with strangers. These findings are consistent with research demonstrating that altruistic motivations within family networks often influence organ donation decisions and may represent an important pathway for expanding living donor programs.

Factors Influencing Willingness to Support Organ Donation

Contrary to expectations and some prior research, sociodemographic factors such as age, gender, ethnicity, and religion were not significant predictors of willingness to support organ donation in this study. Education level, however, emerged as a significant determinant; respondents with higher levels of education demonstrated greater willingness to support organ donation, which is consistent with previous studies linking higher educational attainment and socioeconomic status to increased support for donation.17,18 Higher levels of education may contribute to improved health literacy and greater exposure to accurate information about transplantation, thereby reducing fears and misconceptions related to organ donation. Family communication demonstrated the strongest bivariate association with willingness to support organ donation, with differences in family communication associated with approximately 31.7% of the observed variation in willingness. Respondents who reported discussing organ donation with family members were substantially more willing to support donation. This finding supports the hypothesis that family dialogue plays a central role in organ donation decision-making. Previous studies have similarly emphasized the importance of family consent and communication in successful organ donation programs. 19 Encouraging individuals to discuss their wishes regarding organ donation with family members may therefore represent an important strategy for reducing uncertainty during end-of-life decision-making and improving donation consent rates.

Religious beliefs, while not statistically significant predictors of willingness to donate in this study, may still influence attitudes toward organ donation in certain contexts. Respondents identifying as non-religious reported the highest levels of willingness to donate, followed by Christians and Hindus. Although these differences were not statistically significant, the findings suggest that further research may be warranted to explore how religious values and cultural norms influence perceptions of organ donation in the Caribbean context. Engagement with faith leaders and religious organizations may provide opportunities for addressing misconceptions and promoting culturally sensitive education about organ donation. Finally, the potential role of financial compensation in organ donation was explored. While the majority of respondents (approximately 70%) opposed financial incentives for donation, individuals who supported compensation demonstrated greater willingness to donate. Attitudes toward financial compensation were significantly associated with willingness to support organ donation, with group differences associated with approximately 12% of the observed variation in willingness. These findings suggest that although altruism remains a primary motivator for donation, economic considerations may influence some individuals’ willingness to participate in donation programs. However, the introduction of financial incentives in organ donation systems raises significant ethical concerns and must be approached with caution. Any policies involving financial compensation would require careful regulation to ensure transparency, equity, and protection against exploitation. 20

Practice Implications for Transplant Programs

The findings from this study have important implications for the development and implementation of organ donation programs in Trinidad and Tobago. Increasing public awareness of organ donation legislation, transplantation services, and donor registration processes may strengthen national transplantation systems. Educational initiatives that address common misconceptions, particularly those related to organ trafficking, allocation fairness, and post-donation body integrity, may help build public trust and improve willingness to register as donors. In addition, integrating discussions about organ donation into routine healthcare encounters and community health outreach initiatives could increase awareness and normalize conversations about donation. Family communication also emerged as a critical factor influencing willingness to donate. Transplant programs may benefit from incorporating family-centered education strategies that encourage individuals to discuss their donation preferences with relatives. Healthcare professionals, including nurses and transplant coordinators, may play a key role in facilitating these conversations and providing accurate information about organ donation processes. Strengthening these communication strategies could help improve consent rates and support the development of a more sustainable organ donation system.

Overall, the findings of this study underscore the importance of public knowledge, health literacy, and family communication in shaping attitudes toward organ donation in Trinidad and Tobago. Although legislative frameworks provide an essential foundation for organ donation systems, increasing public understanding of donation processes and addressing persistent misconceptions remain critical priorities. Educational initiatives and community engagement strategies that promote transparent information and encourage family dialogue may enhance public support for organ donation. Strengthening these efforts may ultimately contribute to increased donor registration, improved transplant availability, and better health outcomes for individuals awaiting organ transplantation. Future research should explore culturally tailored educational interventions aimed at improving public knowledge of organ donation and transplantation in Trinidad and Tobago. Longitudinal and mixed-methods studies may also provide deeper insight into how cultural beliefs, trust in healthcare systems, and family dynamics influence organ donation decision-making within Caribbean populations.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between knowledge, attitudes, family communication, and willingness to support organ donation. While significant associations were identified, the directionality of these relationships cannot be determined. Second, data were collected through self-report interviews and may therefore be subject to social desirability bias. Participants may have reported more favorable attitudes toward organ donation than they genuinely hold, particularly when discussing socially valued behaviors such as altruism and helping others. Third, although the probability-based, multistage stratified cluster sampling strategy and inclusion of households across Trinidad and Tobago strengthened the geographic coverage of the study, these features do not ensure complete population representativeness. The household sampling frame excluded individuals living in hostels, shelters, hotels, boarding houses, and institutions, and only 1 respondent was interviewed per household. Because organ donation decisions may involve family members, interviewing a single household respondent may not fully capture family-level perspectives and decision-making processes. In addition, nonparticipation and item-level nonresponse may have introduced nonresponse bias if individuals who did not participate or who had incomplete responses differed systematically from those included in the analyses. Analytic sample sizes consequently varied across study variables. These factors may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader Trinidad and Tobago population, particularly for analyses with smaller analytic samples. Fourth, willingness to support organ donation was measured as an attitudinal outcome rather than actual donor registration or donation behavior. Therefore, greater reported willingness should not be interpreted as evidence that respondents would subsequently register as donors, authorize donations, or donate an organ. The findings characterize factors associated with willingness rather than actual donation behavior. Fifth, although the questionnaire was adapted for the Trinidad and Tobago context, underwent multidisciplinary expert review and pretesting, and demonstrated good internal consistency for the willingness-to-support-organ-donation scale (Cronbach's α = .864), the adapted instrument did not undergo comprehensive psychometric validation in the Trinidad and Tobago population. Accordingly, additional evaluation of the measurement properties of the adapted questionnaire is warranted. This study confirms good internal consistency for the willingness measure but does not establish comprehensive psychometric validation of the adapted instrument. Finally, certain cultural, religious, and socioeconomic influences on organ donation may not have been fully captured by the survey instrument. For example, broad measures of religious affiliation may not reflect religiosity, specific doctrinal beliefs, or the influence of religious leaders on donation decisions. Similarly, quantitative measures may not fully capture medical mistrust, family dynamics, or culturally specific understandings of death and donation. Future mixed-methods and longitudinal studies could provide a more nuanced understanding of these factors and examine whether changes in knowledge, family communication, and attitudes are associated with subsequent donor registration or donation-related behaviors.

Conclusions

This study provides one of the most comprehensive assessments of public attitudes toward organ donation and transplantation in Trinidad and Tobago. The findings demonstrate that willingness to support organ donation is strongly influenced by knowledge, awareness of transplantation services, and, most importantly, family communication. While support for organ donation was generally favorable, substantial gaps in public understanding and persistent misconceptions regarding organ trafficking, organ allocation, and donation procedures continue to represent significant barriers to donor participation. The results suggest that increasing public awareness alone may be insufficient unless educational efforts are accompanied by strategies that promote trust, address misinformation, and encourage discussions about donation within families. Family communication emerged as the strongest predictor of willingness to donate, underscoring the importance of normalizing conversations about organ donation before end-of-life decisions occur. As Trinidad and Tobago continues to strengthen its transplantation infrastructure, investments in culturally responsive public education, donor registration initiatives, healthcare provider training, and community engagement may substantially improve donor participation and transplantation outcomes. By addressing knowledge deficits and fostering informed family decision-making, policymakers and healthcare leaders have an opportunity to enhance organ donation rates, expand access to life-saving transplantation services, and improve the health and quality of life of individuals living with organ failure throughout the country.
