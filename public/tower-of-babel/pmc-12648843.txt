Chilaiditi syndrome with platypnea: a case report

ABSTRACT

Platypnea describes breathlessness that worsens in an upright position and improves when lying down. Introduction

Chilaiditi sign usually affects the transverse colon or hepatic flexure and impacts 0.25–0.28% of the population [1]. Its definition is the bowels’ interposition between the right diaphragm and the liver [2]. Chilaiditi sign is the radiologic finding of hemidiaphragmatic interposition of the colon; Chilaiditi syndrome is the term used to describe a symptomatic occurrence. The syndrome may present as a chronic illness or resolve independently [1]. The most prevalent symptoms are gastrointestinal (such as nausea, vomiting, constipation, and abdominal pain), which are followed less commonly by respiratory distress and chest discomfort that resembles angina [3].

The incidence is four times higher in men than women [4]. With a frequency of about 1%, it is more prevalent among the older population [5]. Diaphragmatic eventration is known as an aberrant elevation of part or all of the hemidiaphragm resulting from a loss of muscle or nerve function while preserving its anatomical connection. A section of the diaphragm is thinned and weak, resulting in decreased function in both congenital and acquired forms. Patients may or may not have respiratory symptoms, depending on the severity. Radiographic imaging confirms the diagnosis. Treatment mainly comprises supportive care and, if necessary, surgical plication [6–8].

Case presentation

A 70-year-old Sudanese male with known hypertensive and benign prostatic hyperplasia on treatment presented to the emergency department in Dongola Police Hospital with breathlessness in a sitting position with gradual onset and epigastric palpitations, low-grade fever that responded well to oral paracetamol, and suprapubic pain with burning micturition and constipation. He had no chest pain, lower limb swelling, or cough. A systemic review was performed, including cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, genitourinary, and neurological systems, and it was unremarkable. Notably, there was no report of orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, wheezing, hematuria, or neurological deficits.

The patient had a medical history of a similar condition 4 months before, which was treated conservatively. High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) was performed, showing an eventration of the diaphragm.

On examination, he looked unwell and pale, not jaundiced, afebrile, with a blood pressure of 110/60 mmHg, a pulse rate of 77 beats/minute, a respiratory rate of 30 beats/minute, and oxygen saturation of 91% in a supine position and 88% in the sitting position at room air condition. On physical examination, the cardiovascular system was unremarkable. He had absent air entry in the right lower lobe with a dull percussion note. His abdomen had visible pulsation in the epigastrium; it was soft, not tender, and there were no signs of guarding or ascites.

Basic laboratory examinations revealed a mild hyponatremia (132 mmol/dL). A hepatic panel was within normal limits. Complete blood count demonstrated a normal white blood cell (WBC) and platelet count and a hemoglobin level of 9.8 mg/dL, which is associated with anemia. His urinalysis showed pus cells (Table 1). Urine culture was unremarkable. X-ray imaging of the chest showed a homogenous opacity in the right lower zone of the lung (Fig. 1). Further computed tomography (CT) imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis showed a loop of colon interposition between the liver and right hemidiaphragm (Fig. 2). No evidence of bowel obstruction was reported. The patient was managed conservatively with oxygen therapy via nasal cannula, lactulose syrup for bowel regulation, antibiotics for suspected urinary tract infection, and paracetamol for fever and discomfort. Given the absence of bowel obstruction, peritonitis, or persistent respiratory compromise, surgical intervention was not indicated at this stage. However, the surgical team was consulted to evaluate the possibility of diaphragmatic plication should symptoms recur or worsen.

He was closely monitored for signs of deterioration, including increasing respiratory distress, worsening abdominal symptoms, or hemodynamic instability. Over the course of his hospital stay, his symptoms gradually improved, and imaging showed no progression of colonic interposition. He was discharged ten days later, after full resolution of constipation and stabilization of his respiratory status. His oxygen saturation at discharge was 96% in the recumbent position and 93% while sitting.

At follow-up, the patient was advised to maintain a high-fiber diet, ensure adequate hydration, and avoid straining during defecation to reduce recurrence risk. Education was provided regarding symptoms that would warrant urgent medical review. This case emphasized the importance of early recognition of Chilaiditi syndrome, careful assessment for surgical indications, and the value of multidisciplinary collaboration in complex cases. The decision to manage conservatively was supported by clinical stability and improvement with supportive care alone.

Discussion

Chilaiditi syndrome is a rare condition in which the colon becomes positioned between the liver and diaphragm, leading to various gastrointestinal and respiratory symptoms. This condition differs from Chilaiditi’s sign, which is basically a radiological finding without symptoms. The combination of Chilaiditi syndrome and platypnea, a rare respiratory symptom, marked by difficulty breathing when upright and improvement when lying down, poses unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Such a presentation is extremely rare, though a similar case involving Duchenne muscular dystrophy had been reported, which was treated conservatively with platypnea–orthodeoxia [9]. Notably, colonic pseudo-obstruction (Ogilvie syndrome), which has been reported as Chilaiditi syndrome in some literature, was not observed in this patient [10].

The pathogenesis of Chilaiditi syndrome is influenced by multiple factors, including anatomical variations, congenital abnormalities, or acquired conditions like colonic redundancy, phrenic nerve dysfunction, chronic lung disease, or diaphragmatic paralysis. The presence of platypnea suggests potential diaphragmatic dysfunction, changes in intrathoracic pressure dynamics, or a vascular shunt contributing to positional dyspnea. In this case, the displacement of abdominal organs may have mechanically compressed the diaphragm, disrupting pulmonary ventilation and circulation, thereby worsening respiratory symptoms when upright.

Common symptoms of Chilaiditi syndrome include abdominal pain, bloating, nausea, and occasionally respiratory distress. Platypnea adds complexity to the clinical presentation, necessitating differential diagnoses that include hepatopulmonary syndrome, cardiac shunts, or pulmonary embolism. Imaging studies, particularly chest and abdominal X-rays or CT scans, are essential for confirming the presence of colonic interposition. Additional assessments like arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis, echocardiography with contrast (bubble study), and pulmonary function tests may help identify the underlying cause of platypnea.

Management of Chilaiditi syndrome typically involves conservative treatments aimed at symptom relief, including bowel decompression, prokinetic agents, and lifestyle changes. However, when severe respiratory distress is present, as seen in the studied case with platypnea, a more intensive approach may be required. Noninvasive ventilation or diaphragmatic plication might be considered if diaphragmatic dysfunction is identified. In refractory cases, surgical interventions such as colopexy or colon resection may be necessary. Identifying Chilaiditi’s sign is crucial to avoid complications during medical procedures such as liver biopsy or colonoscopy [11].

Conclusion

The coexistence of Chilaiditi syndrome with platypnea is exceptionally rare and poses diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. This case highlights the importance of considering diaphragmatic mechanics and vascular shunting in patients with positional dyspnea. A multidisciplinary approach involving gastroenterology, pulmonology, and surgery is essential to optimize patient outcomes. Future research and case reports may further elucidate the underlying mechanisms and guide more tailored management strategies.
