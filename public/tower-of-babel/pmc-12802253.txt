HNRNPC reprograms cancer metabolism to drive acquired chemoresistance in colorectal cancer by repressing of Nrf2/SLC7A11-dependent ferroptosis
Authors: Xiaojian Zhu, Shanshan Lin, Lingling Ruan, Jianyong Xiong, Dengke Yao, Hongtao Wan, Yanglin Chen, Zhijiang Huang, Weiming Qiu, Dan Liu, Bo Yi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chemotherapeutic efficacy in colorectal cancer (CRC) is primarily driven‌ by adaptive mechanisms that circumvent therapy-induced ferroptosis. Despite the established role of ROS-mediated lipid peroxidation in initiating ferroptosis, the molecular regulators enabling cancer cells to escape this iron-dependent demise remain elusive. Here, we ‌identify‌ Heterogeneous Nuclear Ribonucleoprotein C (HNRNPC) as a critical orchestrator of chemoresistance in CRC via metabolic reprogramming of ferroptosis pathways. ‌Clinically, elevated HNRNPC expression correlates with poor chemotherapy outcomes‌ by conferring resistance to irinotecan (CPT-11). Mechanistically, HNRNPC augments the Nrf2/SLC7A11 axis-associated transcript stability by shielding Nrf2 and SLC7A11 mRNAs from degradation machinery, thereby enhancing the longevity of their RNAs. Concurrently, HNRNPC forms a direct protein interactome with ferroptosis gatekeepers Nrf2 and SLC7A11, enforcing the post-translational stabilization of these cytoprotective effectors. This bimodal regulation establishes HNRNPC as a master ferroptosis suppressor that drives acquired chemoresistance through redox homeostasis reprogramming in colorectal carcinogenesis, thereby enhancing cellular redox buffering capability. This RNA–protein interaction amplifies the Nrf2/SLC7A11 signaling axis, which scavenges lethal lipid peroxides and attenuates CPT-11 cytotoxicity. Genetic depletion or pharmacological inhibition of HNRNPC restored ferroptotic vulnerability and resensitized CRC models to chemotherapy. Our findings establish HNRNPC as a post-transcriptional regulator of ferroptosis defense and reveal a therapeutic strategy for overcoming chemoresistance by co-targeting HNRNPC in CRC‌‌.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02577-2.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) persists as a major contributor to cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide [1, 2]. Despite significant advancements in therapeutic strategies, including chemotherapy, targeted therapies, and immunotherapy, a significant proportion of patients develop treatment resistance, resulting in poor clinical outcomes [3, 4]. Among the myriad mechanisms implicated in chemoresistance, iron metabolism and lipid peroxidation have recently garnered attention as critical areas of investigation [5, 6]. Dysregulated iron homeostasis has been implicated in cancer progression and therapeutic resistance, particularly through the induction of ferroptosis-a regulated form of cell death characterized by the accumulation of lipid peroxides [7–9].

Current research has illuminated the complex interplay between iron metabolism and lipid peroxidation, demonstrating how elevated intracellular iron levels can catalyze the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby enhancing lipid peroxidation and promoting ferroptosis under certain conditions [10, 11]. However, the relationship between ferroptosis, lipid peroxidation metabolism, and CRC chemoresistance remains insufficiently characterized. Most studies have predominantly focused on the isolated contributions of iron and lipid metabolism without integrating these pathways into a comprehensive understanding of their collective impact on chemoresistance [12–14]. Furthermore, the potential for therapeutically targeting these metabolic pathways remains underexplored, which leaves a significant gap in our understanding of how to effectively mitigate chemoresistance in CRC patients.

Despite growing evidence linking RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) to cancer progression, the precise molecular mechanisms by which HNRNPC modulates ferroptosis, lipid peroxidation, and chemoresistance in CRC remain poorly understood. While HNRNPC's well-established role as a regulator of alternative splicing has been extensively documented across malignancies, emerging studies suggest broader functional dimensions: its interaction with long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) in maintaining genomic stability, and previously unrecognized contributions to chemotherapy resistance pathways, particularly those involving topoisomerase inhibitors [15–17]. Here, we demonstrate that aberrant overexpression of HNRNPC critically regulates CPT-11 chemosensitivity in CRC cells through a mechanism intricately connected with ferroptotic signaling. Our findings not only bridge the gap between HNRNPC's known splicing functions and its newly identified roles in metabolic reprogramming but also provide mechanistic insights into its potential as a therapeutic target in refractory CRC.

Our research newly have identified that the aberrant overexpression of HNRNPC significantly influences the chemosensitivity of CRC cells to the chemotherapeutic agent CPT-11. This differs from the traditional biological functions of HNRNPC in cells, such as mRNA splicing [18, 19], metastasis [20, 21], and ferroptosis [22, 23]. We demonstrate that HNRNPC impairs the CRC cell response to treatment by disrupting ferroptosis-lipid peroxidation pathways. Aligning with established mechanisms, CPT-11 ‌activates‌ ferroptosis ‌through dual pathways‌: ROS generation and glutathione (GSH) depletion, ‌which collectively drive‌ iron-catalyzed lipid peroxidation-‌a defining feature of ferroptosis [24]. Mechanistically, elevated HNRNPC levels extend the mRNA half-life of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) and solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11), key players in ROS elimination and reducing agent supply [25–27]. Furthermore, HNRNPC directly interacts with and upregulates Nrf2 and SLC7A11 expression, thereby attenuating the efficacy of CPT-11 through the activation of the Nrf2/SLC7A11 signaling pathway.

By elucidating this novel intracellular signaling pathway, our study not only addresses a critical gap in the current literature but also proposes that targeting HNRNPC could potentially reverse CPT-11 resistance in CRC. These findings hold promise for advancing therapeutic strategies and contribute to the broader effort of overcoming drug resistance in cancer treatment. Through this research, we aim to provide a comprehensive framework that integrates iron metabolism and lipid peroxidation pathways, offering new insights into the molecular mechanisms underpinning chemoresistance and paving the way for more effective CRC therapies.

Materials and methods

Study cohort and design

This retrospective cohort study utilised a randomly selected sample of 16 treatment-naïve CRC patients who underwent curative surgical resection at Jiangxi Cancer Hospital between June 2022 and March 2024. Paired tissue specimens (tumour and adjacent normal mucosa, collected ≥ 5 cm from tumour margin) were obtained under sterile conditions within 30 min of surgical resection. Following the implementation of standard cryopreservation protocols, all samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C in hospital-affiliated biobank facilities until analysis.The study protocol (No.2022ky296) received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of Jiangxi Cancer Hospital, with written informed consent obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Patient selection exclusion criteria included a history of preoperative chemotherapy/radiotherapy and the presence of synchronous malignancies.

Cell culture and cell line construction

The cell bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences in Shanghai provided the SW480 (RRID: CVCL_0546) and HCT116 (RRID: CVCL_0291) human CRC cell lines. Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Gibco, #11965092) medium was employed for the cultivation of SW480 cells, whereas Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 (RPMI 1640; Gibco, #11875119) medium was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, #10099–141), 100 μ/mL penicillin, and 100 mg/mL streptomycin for HCT116 cells. The cell cultures were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ in a humidified environment.

To generate LoVo-Irinotecan-resistant (LoVo/IR-R) cell lines, parental LoVo cells (RRID: CVCL_0399) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. Resistance to CPT-11 was induced through a stepwise dose-escalation protocol: the drug concentration was initially set at 50% of the parental IC50 (5 μM) and increased by 20% every 2 weeks over 5 cycles until reaching 150% of the parental IC50 (15 μM). Resistant cells were selected based on viability assays, demonstrating > 50% survival at the parental IC50 compared to untreated controls. Single-cell clones were isolated by limiting dilution, and stable resistant clones were identified by maintaining an IC50 > threefold higher than that of parental cells after 3 passages. Resistance mechanisms were validated using Western blotting and RT-qPCR.

To achieve efficient and stable gene knockdown, lentiviral transduction was employed instead of transient transfection. The specific procedure was as follows: CRC cells were first seeded at 30–40% confluency 24 h prior to transduction. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with lentiviral particles carrying shRNA targeting the gene of interest, which were diluted in serum-free medium to an appropriate Multiplicity of Infection (MOI = 10) and supplemented with 8 μg/mL polybrene to enhance infection efficiency. Subsequently, the cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection. The specific shRNA (hnRNP C1/C2 shRNA plasmid, #sc-35578-SH, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and cDNA ORF Clone plasmid (pLV-C-GFPSpark, #HG18882-ACGLN, SinoBiological) sequences used in the transfection process are detailed in Table S1.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

To identify the downstream targets of HNRNPC in CRC cells, we performed RNA-seq on HCT116 cells transfected with either sh-HNRNPC or a negative control sh-NC. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit, and RNA quality was assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. The RNA library was then prepared using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with paired-end reads of 150 bp. Raw data were processed using FastQC and Trimmomatic, aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using STAR, and gene expression was quantified with featureCounts. Differential expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 package, with thresholds set at |Log2 fold change|≥ 1 and adjusted P-value < 0.05 to identify significant downstream targets of HNRNPC. The Bioconductor package limma (v3.36.0) was used to analyze the differential gene expressions (DEGs), and the Bioconductor package clusterProfiler (v3.18.1) was used for Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment analysis and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA).

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from CRC tissues and cell lines using Trizol reagent (TRIzol Reagent, #15596026) following the manufacturer's instructions. Reverse transcription was performed using the TB Green real-time PCR kit (Takara, Japan, #RR820A) to determine mRNA expression levels, with three replicates per measurement. All primers were obtained from Guangzhou RibBio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (RibBio, Guangzhou, China). Table S2 provides a list of primers for RT-qPCR amplification. Further experimental details can be found in our previous literature with more specificity [28].

Western blotting

Following transfection, cell lysates were prepared using RIPA lysate and PMSF (Thermo Scientific, #36978, 100:1). The total protein content of the lysates was quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, #P0012S), which was boiled for 10 min at 100°C. Subsequently, the protein extracts were fractionated by 10% SDS-PAGE (Solarbio, #P1200-50T) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Thermo Scientific, #88518). Following the blocking step with 5% defatted milk, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibodies, followed by a 2-h incubation at room temperature with the secondary antibodies. The enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; Solarbio, #PE0020) method was used for detection, and the data were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Cell proliferation assay

To assess cell viability, the MTT assay (Promega Biotech, #G5421) was utilized. 6-well plates (Thermo Scientific, #140675) were seeded with 500 cells per well and transfected with appropriate constructs for HCT116 and SW480 cells. Following the manufacturer's instructions, relative cell viability was determined. For colony formation assays, cells were plated onto 6-well plates containing 10% FBS. The cells were cultured and media were replenished every 5 days. After two weeks of growth, the cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Thermo Scientific, #FB002) for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Thermo Scientific, #R40052) before manual counting. Results were normalized to plating efficiencies.

Intracellular ROS measurement

Cells were harvested and washed with PBS, then resuspended with PBS containing 5 μM C-11 BODIPY dye (#D3861, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Cells were then washed twice with PBS followed by resuspending in 200 μL PBS. As per the manufacturer's guidelines, the levels of ROS were evaluated using a ROS Fluorometric Assay Kit (Invitrogen, #EEA019). The cells were treated with a final concentration of 10 μM dihydroethidium (DHE) in a serum-free medium at 37 °C for 15 min. Following this, the fluorescence intensity was quantified to determine the cellular ROS levels.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) assay

To quantify the levels of MDA in cellular samples, the Cell MDA Colorimetric Assay Kit (Invitrogen, #EEA015) was employed. This assay kit utilizes the reaction between MDA and thiobarbituric acid (TBA). Following treatment, CRC cells were collected, lysed, and sonicated. The lysates were then quantified, and MDA working solution was added. The MDA content was determined by measuring the 532 nm absorbance using a microplate reader.

Iron assay

The concentration of Fe2⁺ was determined using an iron assay kit (Biovision, #MA-0103). Following cell lysis and centrifugation, protein quantification was conducted, and the resulting supernatant was used to determine the iron ion concentration. Blank control tubes, standard quality control tubes, and sample tubes were prepared according to the provided guidelines. The samples were incubated in a water bath at 60 °C for one hour. Subsequently, the reagent for iron ion detection was thoroughly mixed and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 593 nm following centrifugation.

Glutathione (GSH) and glutathione oxidized (GSSG) assays

The GSH and GSSG levels were assessed using the GSH and GSSG Colorimetric Assay Kit (Invitrogen, #EEA020). This assay kit employs a colorimetric method in which the formation of a yellow product (5-thio-2-nitrobenzoic acid) occurs. The GSH content can be indirectly quantified by measuring the optical density (OD) at 450 nm.

Immunoprecipitation

Cells were lysed in lysis buffer [20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 0.5% NP40, 10 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA] supplemented with complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, #04693132001). Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. Supernatants were pre-cleared with protein A/G agarose (#sc-2003, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for 1 h at 4 °C to reduce non-specific binding. After removal of agarose beads by brief centrifugation, pre-cleared lysates were incubated with anti-Flag, anti-Nrf2, or anti-SLC7A11 antibodies overnight at 4°C. Protein A/G agarose was then added to each sample and incubated for 2 h at 4 °C to capture antibody-protein complexes. Immunoprecipitates were washed five times with ice-cold lysis buffer, followed by elution in 2 × SDS loading buffer with heating at 95 °C for 5 min. Samples were resolved by 8% or 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes for immunoblotting with specific antibodies.

Immunohistochemistry

The samples for IHC microarray tissues were collected from 35–75 years old male and female CRC patients derived from of Jiangxi Cancer Hospital. The study complied with the Ethics Committee of Jiangxi Cancer Hospital, and the informed consent was obtained from involved patients. Immunohistochemistry was carried out to explore the relevance of HNRNPC in cancers and Subcutaneous tumourigenic model in nude mice. Briefly, sections were soaked in xylene for dewaxing and graded alcohol for rehydrating. Antigen retrieval was performed by placing in citrate buffer and high pressure heating to expose epitopes, and 5% BSA was added to block nonspecific epitopes. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies HNRNPC (1:50, #ab133607, Abcam, USA), Nrf2 (1:250, #ab76026, Abcam, USA), SLC7A11 (1:250, #ab243066, Abcam, USA) and β-actin (1:200, #4970S, CST, USA) at 4 °C overnight. Sections were cultured with biotin-labeled secondary antibody (ZSGB-BIO, 1:1000, #PV-6001; Thermo Scientific, 1:500, #A11004) for 1 h according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Hematoxylin dye were used for antigen detection. The images were photographed by Multispectral fully electric scanning microscopy imaging system (TissueGnostics, Austria), and calculated mean optical density (MOD) values by Image Pro-plus software. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jiangxi Cancer Hospital. n = 16 paired CRC tissues and corresponding non-cancerous colorectal tissues.

Animal in vivo experiments

The CRC cell lines HCT116 were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS. After trypsin digestion, the cells were combined with 50% Matrigel (BD, #354234) to achieve a final concentration of 5 × 106 cells/200 μL. To establish a xenograft mouse model of CRC, commonly known as a cell-derived xenograft or CDX model, a cell suspension from HCT116 was subcutaneously injected into the axilla region of 40 five-week-old BALB/c Nude mice. These mice were procured from SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Hunan, China), and were acclimated to specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions for one week prior to the experiment. The animal experimentation in this study was conducted in compliance with ethical standards and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Jiangxi Cancer Hospital (No.NCULAE-20220726006), adhering to institutional guidelines and regulations. Tumor size and volume were monitored until the conclusion of the experimental tumor harvesting period, after which the tumors were surgically excised for subsequent analysis and research purposes.

RNA stability assay

To assess RNA stability, cells were exposed to 5 μg/ml actinomycin D (Sigma, #A9415). HCT116 or SW480 cells were transfected with either sh-NC or sh-HNRNPC. Subsequently, total RNA was extracted at 0, 3, 7 and 12 h post-treatment with actinomycin D at a concentration of 5 μg/mL for qPCR analysis.

Molecular docking

The structural model of human HNRNPC, predicted by AlphaFold, served as the basis for identifying proteins with structural or sequence homology through FoldSeek and BLAST analyses. Molecular docking was conducted using the CB-Dock2 online server, which employs curvature-based cavity prediction, followed by AutoDock Vina for docking simulations. The substrate compounds, Nrf2 and SLC7A11, were prepared in SDF format, while the AlphaFold-derived HNRNPC structure was formatted as a PDB file. Ligand-receptor docking was executed in accordance with standard protocols provided by CB-Dock2. The docking results were assessed for biochemical plausibility, prioritizing configurations with the lowest Vina scores for further analysis. Predicted docking orientations were visualized and scrutinized using PyMOL (v3.7), enabling the identification of key active-site residues for subsequent mutagenesis studies.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

GSEA was carried out using GSEA tool v3.0, with the input gene sets sourced from gene sets collections, including Hallmarks, C5, and C7 from MsigDB v6.2. Enrichment scores were calculated through analyses performed on the GenePattern platform, employing the classical GSEA method. The results of the enrichment analysis were visualized using ggplot2. In short, GSEA was performed using the ‌sh-HNRNPC vs. sh-NC‌ comparison (n = 3 biological replicates per group) to specifically interrogate HNRNPC-dependent transcriptional changes. The input gene expression matrix was normalized by TPM (Transcripts Per Million) and log2-transformed. Genes were ranked by log2 fold change (sh-HNRNPC/sh-NC) to prioritize HNRNPC-regulated transcriptional alterations.

Statistical analysis

All values were presented as means ± standard deviation (SD), and statistical analyses and visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism 7 (GraphPad Prism, RRID: SCR_002798). The significance of differences between groups was assessed using one-way ANOVA and Student's paired or unpaired t-tests. The experiments were conducted as independent biological replicates and repeated at least three times. Results were labelled as follows: P < 0.05 was denoted as statistically significant, ns indicates not significant.

Results

HNRNPC is a key determinant of chemosensitivity to CPT-11 in CRC cells

We previously demonstrated that the m6A-reading protein HNRNPC inhibits the degradation of AC145207.5, stabilizes its RNA abundance, and subsequently activates the Nrf2/GPX4 axis [29]. This activation leads to a significant upregulation of GPX4 expression, which inhibits GPX4-mediated ferroptosis and ultimately promotes the malignant progression of CRC. To elucidate the role of HNRNPC in CRC and its potential impact on chemosensitivity to CPT-11, we commenced our investigation by examining HNRNPC expression in CRC tissues. Our analysis utilized immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, which revealed a heterogeneous expression pattern of HNRNPC across the CRC samples. Quantitative analysis of the IHC staining intensity demonstrated significantly elevated HNRNPC expression in CRC tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1a). Complementary to these findings, Western blotting confirmed increased HNRNPC protein levels in CRC tissues relative to normal controls (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, we investigated the expression levels of HNRNPC protein in irinotecan-resistant HCT116 cells and observed a significant upregulation compared to the parental cell line (Fig. 1c). Additionally, we performed bioinformatics analysis using two independent public datasets (GSE62332, and TCGA-CRC) to validate these findings in patient-derived tumor samples, which consistently showed elevated HNRNPC mRNA expression in CPT-11-resistant tumors compared to sensitive counterparts (Fig. S1a, b).Fig. 1HNRNPC plays a key role in chemosensitivity of CRC cells to CPT-11. a Representative immunohistochemical staining image of HNRNPC in CRC tissues and quantification of immunohistochemical results. n = 16, two-sided Mann–Whitney U-test. b Western blotting analysis of HNRNPC protein expression and quantification in CRC tissues. n = 8, two-tailed Student's t-test. c Western blot analysis was performed to assess HNRNPC protein levels in LoVo/CPT-11-resistant (R) and sensitive (S) cells. To further investigate the functional implications of HNRNPC expression, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to identify pathways associated with HNRNPC [30]. The analysis revealed significant enrichment of key phenotypes, including pathways related to drug metabolization and pyrimidine metabolism (Fig. 1d). These findings suggest that HNRNPC may play a pivotal role in CRC cell biology and therapeutic responsiveness. Building on these insights, we examined the effects of modulating HNRNPC expression on CRC cell viability and sensitivity to CPT-11. Specifically, HNRNPC expression was silenced in SW480 and HCT116 CRC cell lines (Fig. S1c, d), and cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay under varying concentrations of CPT-11. Notably, silencing HNRNPC significantly enhanced CRC cell sensitivity to CPT-11, as evidenced by reduced cell viability and lower IC50 values (Fig. 1e, f). Moreover, to assess the impact of different time points on chemosensitivity after HNRNPC knockdown, we performed MTT assays to evaluate cell viability following exposure to CPT-11 at a concentration of 25 µM. As depicted in Fig.S1e, f, HNRNPC knockdown resulted in a marked decrease in cell viability in both SW480 and HCT116 cells compared to control. These results underscore the role of HNRNPC in modulating chemosensitivity to CPT-11 in CRC cells. To further explore the implications of these findings, we conducted clonogenic assays to assess the long-term survival capacity of CRC cells with altered HNRNPC expression following CPT-11 treatment. Consistent with our previous observations, CRC cells with HNRNPC knockdown exhibited significantly reduced colony formation after a 14-day treatment with 25 μM CPT-11 (Fig. 1g). These data further support the role of HNRNPC in mediating resistance to CPT-11 in CRC cells.

In summary, our results indicate that HNRNPC serves as a critical regulator of chemosensitivity to CPT-11 in CRC. Elevated HNRNPC expression in CRC tissues correlates with resistance to CPT-11, and its modulation significantly influences CRC cell responses to this chemotherapeutic agent. These findings highlight HNRNPC as a potential therapeutic target for overcoming chemoresistance in CRC.

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which HNRNPC modulates chemosensitivity to CPT-11 in CRC cells, we conducted GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on genes upregulated following CPT-11 treatment in HNRNPC knockdown cells compared to control cells. To further investigate this potential link, we performed a correlation analysis utilizing data from the BioMart database [31]. This analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between HNRNPC expression levels and ferroptosis scores in CRC tissues (Fig. 2c), indicating that elevated HNRNPC expression may potentially suppress ferroptotic activity. To validate these findings, we conducted GSEA using the gene set associated with HNRNPC in CRC from TCGA database [32]. The results corroborated the involvement of HNRNPC in pathways that positively regulate ferroptosis, reinforcing the hypothesis that HNRNPC suppresses ferroptotic cell death in CRC (Fig. 2d). Building on these insights, we assessed the therapeutic potential of targeting ferroptosis in conjunction with CPT-11 treatment. We screened for the optimal concentrations of the CPT-11 and determined that treatment with CPT-11 at 10 μM, in combination with Fer-1, significantly enhanced cell death in HNRNPC knockdown cells compared to treatment with CPT-11 alone. This finding underscores the role of ferroptosis in mediating the increased sensitivity of CRC cells to CPT-11 when HNRNPC is silenced (Fig. 2e-h).

In conclusion, our results indicate that HNRNPC compromises chemotherapeutic sensitivity to CPT-11 in CRC cells by inhibiting ferroptosis. The significant positive association between HNRNPC expression levels and ferroptosis activity metrics, coupled with the synergistic cytotoxic effects observed upon HNRNPC silencing combined with CPT-11 administration, strongly suggests that dual targeting of HNRNPC and ferroptosis regulatory mechanisms may represent a promising therapeutic approach to reverse chemotherapy resistance in CRC.

HNRNPC impairs chemosensitivity to CPT-11 via impedes ferroptosis-lipid peroxidation metabolic pathways in CRC cells

To further elucidate the mechanisms by which HNRNPC regulates ferroptosis and chemosensitivity in CRC cells, we examined the impact of HNRNPC knockdown on reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and lipid peroxidation. Using confocal microscopy, we observed that CRC cells with HNRNPC knockdown, stained with the ROS Reagent kit and Liperfluo, exhibited a significant increase in both total ROS (red) and lipid ROS (green) levels compared to control cells (Fig. 3a, b, S2a, b). To quantify these observations, we measured cellular malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, a byproduct of lipid peroxidation, using the Thiobarbituric Acid (TBA) method. Consistent with our microscopy findings, HNRNPC knockdown cells exhibited significantly higher MDA concentrations, indicating elevated lipid peroxidation (Fig. 3c, d). To further elucidate the role of ‌HNRNPC‌ in ferroptosis, we systematically evaluated key cellular parameters associated with iron metabolism and redox homeostasis. First, we assessed intracellular Fe2+ levels using the Cellular Fe2+ Test Kit-Fluorescence method. Our results revealed that HNRNPC knockdown led to a significant increase in Fe2+ concentration, a pivotal driver of ferroptosis induction (Fig. 3e, f). Next, we investigated the expression of ‌TFRC (Transferrin Receptor 1)‌ in SW480, HCT116, and HCT116-irinotecan-R cells following HNRNPC knockdown. Western blot analysis demonstrated a ‌significant downregulation of TFRC‌ upon HNRNPC silencing (Fig. S2c), suggesting impaired iron uptake and further contributing to intracellular iron accumulation. To assess the impact on cellular redox status, we quantified ‌total GSH‌ and GSSG‌ levels using the ‌GSH/GSSG Assay Kit‌. Discussion

In this study, we elucidate the critical role of HNRNPC in modulating chemosensitivity to CPT-11 in CRC cells. Our findings indicate that elevated expression of HNRNPC in CRC tissues correlates with resistance to CPT-11, and its modulation significantly alters the response of CRC cells to this chemotherapeutic agent. Specifically, we demonstrate that HNRNPC impairs chemosensitivity by inhibiting ferroptosis [9, 39, 40], a regulated form of cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation. The observed inverse correlation between HNRNPC expression and ferroptosis scores, coupled with increased cell death following HNRNPC knockdown, underscores the potential of targeting HNRNPC and ferroptosis pathways as a strategy to overcome chemoresistance in CRC.

Our study presents a comprehensive theoretical model linking HNRNPC with the Nrf2/SLC7A11 signaling pathway, highlighting its role in regulating ferroptosis and lipid peroxidation metabolic pathways. The interaction between HNRNPC and key regulatory proteins such as Nrf2 and SLC7A11 suggests that HNRNPC positively influences their expression [41–43], thereby contributing to CRC progression and chemoresistance. This model offers a novel perspective on the molecular mechanisms underlying chemoresistance, potentially impacting the scientific community by fostering new research directions focused on ferroptosis and redox homeostasis in cancer therapy.

From a practical standpoint, our findings suggest that targeting HNRNPC could enhance the efficacy of CPT-11 by promoting ferroptosis in CRC cells. The synergistic effect observed when combining ferroptosis inducers (e.g., erastin) or Nrf2/SLC7A11 axis inhibitors with CPT-11 underscores the therapeutic potential of this combinatorial approach. While these findings advance our understanding of HNRNPC's role in ferroptotic regulation, the precise molecular interactions between HNRNPC and the Nrf2/SLC7A11 pathway remain to be elucidated. Future studies will employ RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (RIP-seq) and site-directed mutagenesis to systematically map their binding interfaces, thereby establishing causal relationships at the molecular level. Clinically, our discovery holds particular significance for CRC patients with elevated HNRNPC expression, as these individuals may derive maximal benefit from the proposed therapeutic strategy. The preclinical framework established here provides a foundation for subsequent validation in patient-derived organoid models and clinical trials, ultimately aiming to improve therapeutic outcomes in this challenging disease setting.

Looking ahead, future research should delve deeper into the molecular interactions and regulatory networks involving HNRNPC, Nrf2, and SLC7A11.Notably, the present study primarily examines the functional consequences of HNRNPC depletion. It remains to be determined whether further increasing HNRNPC levels is sufficient to actively potentiate Nrf2/SLC7A11 signaling or confer additional ferroptosis resistance.

Investigating the broader implications of HNRNPC modulation on other forms of cell death and its potential interactions with additional signaling pathways could unveil new therapeutic targets. Moreover, clinical studies assessing HNRNPC expression levels in CRC patients and their correlation with treatment outcomes will be crucial for validating its potential as a biomarker for chemosensitivity.

Conclusion

This groundbreaking study establishes HNRNPC as a master regulatory node governing CRC chemosensitivity to CPT-11, mediated principally through ferroptotic pathway orchestration and lipid peroxidation modulation. Our mechanistic dissection reveals a previously unrecognized molecular dialogue between HNRNPC and the Nrf2/SLC7A11 antioxidant axis, establishing an innovative conceptual paradigm for combating chemoresistance in CRC therapeutics (Fig. 9). The findings illuminate the therapeutic potential of pharmacological HNRNPC modulation to amplify ferroptotic vulnerability through lipid peroxide accumulation, thereby redefining current strategies for chemotherapy optimization. Beyond advancing fundamental understanding of cancer cell survival mechanisms under pharmacological pressure, this work bridges critical knowledge gaps in redox homeostasis manipulation for precision oncology. Concurrently, HNRNPC forms a direct protein interactome with ferroptosis gatekeepers Nrf2 and GPX4, enforcing the post-translational stabilization of these cytoprotective effectors. This bimodal regulation establishes HNRNPC as a master ferroptosis suppressor that drives acquired chemoresistance through redox homeostasis reprogramming in colorectal carcinogenesis
