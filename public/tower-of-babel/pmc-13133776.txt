Stable and Active p‑Block Metal-Doped Cu2O Catalysts for the Electrochemical Reduction of CO2 into CO
Authors: Karl Adrian Gandionco, Juwon Kim, Alexander Ryabin, Hyunji You, Sebastian Kunze, Subin Choi, Jeongho Cho, Gibeom Kim, Jiseok Kim, Hwiho Kim, Jongwoo Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Incorporating a secondary metal into a host material is an effective strategy to modulate its electronic structure and enhance electrocatalytic performance. Low-concentration doping, which minimizes perturbations to the host lattice and surface, enables precise material engineering while reducing synthesis complexity and costs. However, achieving stable low-concentration doping is challenging due to potential dopant leaching under dynamic electrochemical conditions. Here, we demonstrate that dilute incorporation of p-block elements (≤2.50 wt %) into Cu2O significantly enhances its CO2-to-CO conversion efficiency and operational stability. The lowest effective doping concentrations were 0.37 wt % for tin and 2.03 wt % for indium, both exhibiting negligible leaching. Remarkably, these dopants decrease the work function of Cu2O even at such low concentrations, contributing to improved catalytic performance. Moreover, both tin and indium suppress the reduction of Cu2O to metallic copper, maintaining high CO conversion efficiency over extended operation. Our findings demonstrate that low-concentration doping with p-block metals can effectively tune the electronic structure and enhance the electrocatalytic performance of copper-based catalysts.

Introduction

Aside from being a viable strategy in combating climate change, the electrochemical carbon dioxide reduction reaction (CO2RR) is also a feasible alternative process for the manufacturing of value-added chemicals and carbon-neutral fuels. Although the technology has been widely reported to be capable of converting CO2 into hydrocarbons and oxygenates, the efficient electrochemical reduction of CO2 to C2+ products remains a big challenge that is yet to be overcome. − In view of this and taking into account techno-economics, the two-electron reduction of CO2 into carbon monoxide (CO) is considered one of the most cost-effective routes for industrial application. − Silver-based materials, gold-based materials, and metal and nitrogen-doped carbon materials (nickel-based and iron-based) are the state-of-the-art catalysts for the electrochemical reduction of CO2 into CO. ,, However, recent studies show that incorporating a p-block metal, such as indium (In) or tin (Sn), into copper (Cu)-based materials changes the product selectivity of Cu and results in comparable CO selectivity relative to state-of-the-art catalysts. In the reported studies, the In or Sn-modified Cu-based catalysts generally have two forms, namely indium or tin oxide on Cu-based materials − and In or Sn-doped Cu-based materials. ,− The general observation is that the product selectivity changes depending on the amount of In or Sn in the modified Cu-based materials, where CO is formed preferentially when the In or Sn concentration is lower than Cu whereas formate is the main product when In or Sn concentration is higher than Cu. Density functional theory (DFT) suggests that increasing the concentration of In or Sn weakens the adsorption energy for the *COOH intermediate and strengthens the adsorption energy for the *OCHO intermediate. The carbophilic (*COOH) and oxophilic (*OCHO) adsorption modes lead to the favorable production of CO and formate, respectively. ,, The above generalizations were derived from studies that synthesized p-block metal-modified Cu-based catalysts with p-block metal concentrations ranging from 5 to 70 wt %. They do not comprehensively consider the effect of the addition of lower p-block metal doping concentrations. Dilute concentrations of p-block metal may cause a limited change in the electronic structure and electrochemical performance of Cu-based catalysts, but the extent of their effect remains unexplored in literature. Low-level doping offers an effective means to enhance catalytic activity and durability by precisely tuning the electronic structure without altering the overall host lattice. Nevertheless, dopants at such dilute concentrations are often susceptible to leaching under electrochemical conditions, underscoring the need for a strategy that ensures both electronic-structure control and structural robustness. At higher concentrations, doping may transition into bimetallic alloying, leading to strong lattice distortion and phase separation into element-rich domains. Although such alloying may enhance catalytic activity, it often requires large amounts of expensive dopants and complicates the understanding of the intrinsic structure–property relationship.

It has been recently reported that the presence of a p-block metal in copper oxide catalysts can stabilize the Cu+ or Cu2+ during the electrochemical CO2RR. , Ma et al. reported a lead-doped copper­(I) oxide (Pb–Cu2O) catalyst and showed that doping Cu2O with Pb hindered the complete reduction of Cu+ into Cu during reaction conditions. The retention of Cu+ is significant for the electrochemical conversion of CO2 into CO because Cu+ has a more positive free energy for the *CO intermediate as compared to Cu, indicating that the desorption of CO from the surface of Cu2O is more favorable than that of Cu. Moreover, Zhang et al. employed operando X-ray absorption fine structure spectroscopy to provide concrete proof of the survival of the Cu+ species during and after the electrochemical CO2 reduction reaction. Through operando infrared spectroscopy, the group shows the crucial role of stabilized Cu+ species in improving electrocatalytic performance. That is, Cu+ species on the catalyst surface promote the adsorption of *CO during the electrochemical CO2RR, which improves the CO2 to CO conversion. The stabilizing effect on Cu+ or Cu2+ species during the electrochemical CO2RR has not been fairly investigated in the case of In or Sn-modified Cu-based catalysts as studies focused mainly on the selectivity of the catalysts. Moreover, there has been no reported attempt to compare the stabilizing effect of different p-block metals, and no effort has been made to determine the effect of varying p-block metal doping concentrations on the stability of Cu-based catalysts.

In this work, we synthesized In–Cu2O and Sn–Cu2O catalysts with low In or Sn loadings (<2.50 wt %) using a simple room-temperature wet chemical reduction method. We confirmed that both catalysts are active and selective for the electrochemical reduction of CO2 to CO at doping concentrations between 1 and 2 wt %. At lower doping concentrations (0.32–0.37 wt %), we observed distinct effects on CO selectivity. The Sn–Cu2O catalyst maintained significantly higher CO selectivity than undoped Cu2O, even when we decreased the Sn concentration from 1.44 to 0.37 wt %. In contrast, when we decreased the In concentration from 2.03 to 0.32 wt %, the CO selectivity of In–Cu2O resembled that of undoped Cu2O. We found that the CO Faradaic efficiency of both undoped and doped Cu2O catalysts increases as their work function decreases, showing a strong correlation. Sn decreased the work function more effectively than In, even at very low concentrations (0.32–0.37 wt %), which indicates that Sn exerts a stronger influence on the electronic structure of Cu. We further confirmed that both In and Sn stabilize Cu+ species during electrochemical CO2 reduction. Post-mortem characterization after long-term electrolysis showed that In–Cu2O and Sn–Cu2O catalysts prevented the complete reduction of Cu2O to metallic Cu. As a result, both catalysts maintained higher Faradaic efficiency and partial current density for CO compared to undoped Cu2O, while retaining the dopants and Cu2O crystallographic structures during extended operation, which demonstrates enhanced stability. Our findings provide new insights into how In and Sn doping concentrations modulate the electronic structure and stability of Cu-based catalysts, offering guidance for the rational design of copper oxide systems doped with other p-block metals.

Experimental Section

Chemicals

The chemicals used in this work were of reagent grade and were employed without further purification. Copper­(II) sulfate pentahydrate (98.0–102.0%), indium­(III) nitrate (99.99%, metals basis), and tin­(IV) chloride (98%, metals basis) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Sodium citrate dihydrate, sodium hydroxide (98%), and L-(+)-ascorbic acid (99+ %) were also bought from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Potassium hydrogen carbonate (99.7–100.5%) was obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Carbon dioxide gas (99.999%) and argon gas (99.999%) were procured from KS TECH Co., Ltd. and KLCC, respectively. The aqueous solutions of the reagents for the synthesis of catalysts and the electrolyte for the electrochemical measurements were prepared using deionized water (18.2 MΩ·cm) processed from the Merck Millipore Water Purification System.

Synthesis of Catalysts

In–Cu2O and Sn–Cu2O were prepared through a wet chemical reduction method adapted from elsewhere. Briefly, 0.0018 mol of 0.90 M sodium citrate tribasic dehydrate was added to 150 mL of deionized water. After stirring for 5 min, 0.0012 mol of 1.20 M copper­(II) sulfate pentahydrate and 0.0012 mol of 0.12 M indium­(III) nitrate or tin­(IV) chloride were added to the solution. After 10 min, 0.0048 mol of 4.80 M NaOH was added, and the resulting solution was stirred for 10 more minutes. The precipitates of Cu2O-based catalysts were formed by adding 0.0012 mol of 1.20 M L-(+)-ascorbic acid and stirring the above solution for 30 min. The precipitates were collected by centrifugation. Afterward, the precipitates were washed three times with deionized water and once with acetone and then dried under vacuum for 6 h. The synthesized catalysts were labeled as Cu2O, In–Cu2O (x), and Sn–Cu2O (x), where x refers to the weight percentage of the doped p-block metal. The amount of the metal precursors was varied to obtain different loadings of p-block metals in the Cu2O.

Material Characterization

Electron microscopy techniques were employed to determine the morphology of the synthesized catalysts and the distribution of the In or Sn dopant in the Cu2O catalyst. Transition electron microscopy (TEM) images were obtained through Thermo Fisher’s Themis Z electron microscope. Aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscopy (Cs-STEM) equipped with a high-angle annular dark field detector and energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectrometer was also carried out in Thermo Fisher’s Themis Z electron microscope to corroborate the successful synthesis and integration of p-block metal into the Cu2O. X-ray diffraction was conducted in a Bruker D8 Advance to confirm the crystalline structure of the undoped Cu2O and p-block metal-doped Cu2O catalysts. Rietveld refinement was performed using the GSAS-II software to determine the lattice parameter of the undoped and doped Cu2O catalysts. The concentrations of p-block metal and copper in powder form and electrode form are measured through inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) in PerkinElmer’s Optima 8300, and their concentrations in the electrolyte after long-term electrolysis are measured using inductively coupled plasma-mass spectroscopy (ICP–MS) in PerkinElmer’s NexION 350D. Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) were conducted using an Axis Supra+ model from Kratos to determine the work functions of the pristine and used catalyst materials, as well as the chemical state of the elements in the catalysts, respectively. The Cu LMM Auger spectra of the used cathode materials were obtained using AXIS-His from Kratos and VersaProbe III from ULVAC-PHI, Inc. to evaluate the relative Cu+/(Cu + Cu+) ratio at different electrolysis times.

Electrode Preparation

To prepare the catalyst ink for the working electrode, a solution comprised of the catalyst material, 20 μL Nafion solution (5% w/w in water and 1-propanol), and 1 mL acetone was sonicated for at least 30 min. The catalyst loading on the carbon paper (SGL Carbon, Sigracet 39 BB GDL) was set at 1 mg/cm2. The catalyst ink was drop-coated on the carbon paper substrate, and the electrode was dried in the atmosphere. No succeeding treatment was done to the electrode before the electrolysis.

Electrochemical Measurements

The electrolysis experiments in this work were performed in an H-cell (redox. me). An anion exchange membrane (Fumasep FAA-3-PK-130) was used to separate the cathode and anode compartments. The electrolysis was conducted in a 0.10 M potassium bicarbonate (KHCO3) solution with an Ag/AgCl (3 M NaCl, ALS, RE-1B) as a reference electrode and a platinum wire (ALS, 002233) as a counter electrode. The electrolyte was bubbled with an ultrahigh-purity CO2 gas for at least 30 min before starting the electrochemical CO2RR. The purging of CO2 gas was sustained at 20 sccm throughout the electrolysis to ensure enough CO2 concentration for the reaction. All electrochemical measurements were performed using an Autolab potentiostat (Metrohm, PGSTAT302N), and all potential values were reported considering the solution resistance (Rs). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy was employed to measure the Rs. The iRs-compensated potential values were converted to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) scale using the following equation 1 Vvs.RHE=Vvs.Ag/AgCl+0.209+0.059pH

Product Analysis

The gas products were analyzed online through Young In’s YL6500 gas chromatography (GC) system. The Faradaic efficiency (FE) of the gas products and the generation rate of the product were calculated using the following equations 2 FE=rate×z×Fjtotal×100% where the rate is the generation rate of the product (mol/cm2·s), z is the charge transfer of each product, F is the Faradaic constant (96, 485 C/mol), and j total is the total current density during CO2 bulk electrolysis (A/cm2). The generation rate of the product is calculated as follows 3 rate=V˙×CA×VM where V̇ is the CO2 gas flow rate (L/s), C is the concentration of the product detected by GC in volume fraction, A is the submerged geometric area of the electrode (cm2), and V M is the molar volume of an ideal gas at ambient conditions.

The sample for the liquid products was obtained after electrolysis. The liquid products were analyzed using Bruker’s 500 MHz nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectrometer. The NMR sample was prepared by mixing 500 μL of liquid sample, 100 μL of 100 ppm DMSO solution, and 200 μL of D2O. The FE of the liquid product is calculated as follows 4 FE=α×n×FQ×100% where α is the number of electrons required for each product, n is the number of products (mol), F is the Faradaic constant, and Q is the total charge passed in the entire duration of electrolysis.

In Situ Raman Analysis

The in situ Raman analysis was performed in a commercially sourced flow-type Raman cell (Shanghai Fanyue Electronic Technology Co., Ltd.). The working electrode was prepared in the same manner as in the H-cell experiments. An anion exchange membrane (Fumasep FAA-3-PK-130) was used to separate the cathode and anode compartments. The reference and counter electrodes were Ag/AgCl and platinum wire (Shanghai Fanyue Electronic Technology Co., Ltd.), respectively. In situ Raman spectroscopy was conducted using a Raman spectrometer (Renishaw, InVia Qontor) coupled with a portable potentiostat (BioLogic, SP-200). The spectrometer was calibrated using a Si standard. A 633 nm excitation laser was employed, and scattered light was collected using a nonwater immersion objective (Leica, 50×). Electrolysis was carried out in CO2- saturated 0.10 M KHCO3 solution circulated through the Raman cell using a peristaltic pump (JIHPUMP). Raman spectra were acquired every 10 s throughout the electrolysis.

Results and Discussion

Catalyst Synthesis and Characterization

In–Cu2O (x = 0.32, 0.50, 1.50, 2.03, and 2.21) and Sn–Cu2O (x = 0.37, 1.44, 2.96, 3.50, and 4.06) catalysts were synthesized using a room-temperature wet chemical reduction method. The x indicates the weight percentage of the p-block metal that is doped into the Cu2O. ICP-AES was used to measure the elemental concentrations of all the as-synthesized catalysts (Table S1). XRD patterns of all samples show no observable peaks corresponding to metallic In or Sn, or their oxide phases, suggesting successful incorporation of In and Sn into the Cu2O lattice (Figures S1 and S2). To focus the investigation, comprehensive electrochemical CO2RR characterization was performed (Figures S3 and S4), based on which In–Cu2O (x = 0.32 and 2.03) and Sn–Cu2O (x = 0.37 and 1.44) were selected for further study. Accordingly, all subsequent results and discussion focus on these catalysts. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images (Figures and S5) show a lattice spacing of approximately 0.25 nm for all as-synthesized catalysts, corresponding to the interplanar distance of the (111) plane of cubic Cu2O. Considering the ionic radii of Cu+ (77 pm), In3+ (80 pm), and Sn4+ (69 pm), lattice expansion is expected when In3+ is incorporated, whereas Sn4+ doping could induce lattice contraction. However, no significant change in lattice spacing was observed, which can be attributed to the low doping concentrations. Notably, the morphological and structural features are consistent with those reported in a previous study from which our synthesis method was adapted. That study observed limited lattice distortion at low doping concentrations. STEM shows the general reduction of particle size upon the introduction of In and Sn dopants into Cu2O. The differences in particle size between undoped and doped Cu2O are attributed to dopant-mediated perturbations in crystal growth during synthesis. Previous studies on doped Cu2O systems have shown that heteroatom incorporation can modify surface growth behavior rather than proceeding via uniform lattice substitution. , In particular, dopants introduced during synthesis can accumulate at or near the particle surface, forming dopant-enriched regions that alter local surface energies and facet-dependent growth rates. This effect has been directly observed in bimodified Cu2O, where the formation of a rough, nanostructured outer layer reflects dopant-regulated surface growth. Consistent with these insights, the incorporation of In3+ or Sn4+ in the present system is expected to disrupt uniform crystal growth by modifying nucleation and surface propagation kinetics, leading to heterogeneous growth rates across different facets. As a result, the doped samples exhibit a broader particle size distribution and a reduced average particle size compared to undoped Cu2O. Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping confirms the successful incorporation of In and Sn into the Cu2O lattice. The elemental maps show a uniform distribution of Cu, O, and the dopant elements throughout the samples (Figures and S5), indicating homogeneous doping without observable phase segregation. X-ray diffraction (XRD) further supports this conclusion. The diffractograms of both undoped and doped Cu2O catalysts (Figures S1 and S2) exhibit only the peaks at 2θ = 29.51°, 36.37°, 42.26°, 61.36°, 73.66°, and 77.38°, which correspond to the (110), (111), (200), (220), (311), and (222) planes of Cu2O, respectively. The absence of additional peaks indicates that the catalysts are phase-pure, with negligible formation of secondary or impurity phases under the investigated conditions. To further strengthen our analysis, we performed extended XRD measurements using an internal standard (LaB6), followed by Rietveld refinement to accurately determine the lattice parameters and unit cell volumes of the undoped and doped Cu2O samples (Figures S6–S12 and Table S2). The refined lattice parameter of undoped Cu2O is 4.2641(3) Å. For In–Cu2O, the lattice parameters increase systematically to 4.2646(4) Å (0.32 wt %) and 4.2679(3) Å (2.03 wt %). In contrast, Sn–Cu2O exhibits a monotonic decrease to 4.2631(6) Å (0.37 wt %) and 4.2626(5) Å (1.44 wt %). These composition-dependent and opposing trends are consistent with the differences in ionic radii between Cu+ (77 pm), In3+ (80 pm), and Sn4+ (69 pm), and strongly support substitutional incorporation within the Cu2O lattice. Importantly, the observed lattice variations cannot be attributed solely to microstrain, particle size effects, or instrumental artifacts, as they exhibit a clear and reproducible dependence on dopant concentration. Furthermore, the absence of additional diffraction peaks, together with the homogeneous elemental distribution observed by STEM-EDS, excludes the formation of segregated In2O3, SnO, or SnO2 phases. XPS further verifies the oxidation states of the constituent elements. In both undoped and doped samples, Cu exists predominantly in the monovalent state (Figure S13a,b). Meanwhile, the dopant species are present as In3+ and Sn4+, respectively (Figure S13c,d). Taken together, these results confirm that at low doping concentrations, In and Sn are successfully incorporated into the Cu2O lattice without disrupting the host structure. The catalysts generally retain the crystallographic features of phase-pure Cu2O, while the dopants are uniformly distributed and maintain stable oxidation states. Collectively, these complementary structural and chemical analyses provide strong and self-consistent evidence for the successful incorporation of In and Sn into the Cu2O lattice at low doping concentrations, while preserving the phase purity and crystallographic integrity of the host material.

Electrochemical CO2RR Performance

As shown in Figures a–c and S14, the current response of the as-synthesized catalysts in the CO2-saturated KHCO3 solutions shows a higher value than that in the Argon-saturated solution, indicating active CO2 conversion in a CO2-rich environment. The main electrolysis experiments were conducted through chronoamperometry. The complete details for the experimental setup and the calculations of the Faradaic efficiency for the gaseous and liquid products from the voltammetry and analytical technique measurements are provided in the Experimental Section. The positive effect of incorporating In or Sn into Cu2O is evident in the enhanced selectivity for CO and the suppression of the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), as shown in Figures d–i and S15. The maximum CO Faradaic efficiency was found to be 78.8% for the Sn–Cu2O (1.44) catalyst at an applied potential of −0.98 V vs RHE. At moderate potential range (i.e., −0.74∼−0.8 V), the In–Cu2O (2.03) and Sn–Cu2O (1.44) catalysts showed a Faradaic efficiency of 72.8% at −0.74 V vs RHE and partial current density of −8.27 mA/cm2 and 73.2% at −0.80 V vs RHE and partial current density of −7.85 mA/cm2, respectively. These CO selectivities are much higher than that of Cu2O, which has a Faradaic efficiency of 20.3% at −0.76 V vs RHE and a partial current density of −1.68 mA/cm2. The lower-doping catalysts, namely the In–Cu2O (0.32) and Sn–Cu2O (0.37), showed a Faradaic efficiency for CO of 33.8% and 68.6%, respectively, which are substantially higher than that of undoped Cu2O for the same potential range. All the p-block metal-doped Cu2O catalysts are more active toward the electrochemical CO2RR over the HER across all the applied potential values, but both In–Cu2O (2.03) and Sn–Cu2O (1.44) catalysts can remarkably suppress HER as evident by their Faradaic efficiency for hydrogen gas (Figure g–i). At a potential range of −0.55 to −0.57 V vs RHE, the selectivity of Cu2O for hydrogen gas is two times higher than that of both In–Cu2O (2.03) and Sn–Cu2O (1.44) catalysts. The remarkable HER suppression of the In–Cu2O and Sn–Cu2O catalysts is in congruence with the DFT results of studies on In or Sn-modified Cu-based catalysts, wherein they found that these catalysts have higher energy barriers for generating *H relative to the unmodified Cu catalysts. ,

To rationalize the electrochemical performance of our catalysts, we performed post-mortem structural and electrochemical characterizations after electrolysis. STEM images (Figure ) reveal morphological and structural changes in the catalysts, indicating that the cubic morphology of the as-synthesized materials is not fully retained. To determine whether these changes are responsible for the observed electrocatalytic performance, we measured the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) of the catalysts after electrolysis (Figure S16). Table S3 summarizes the double-layer capacitance and ECSA values. Further post-mortem analysis using STEM-EDS (Figure ) confirms that the p-block metals in the doped Cu2O catalysts remain largely retained after electrolysis. Comparison of ECSA and STEM-EDS results between In–Cu2O, Sn–Cu2O, and undoped Cu2O shows that the particle size and surface-to-volume ratio are comparable for all catalysts after CO2RR. Detailed STEM-EDS analysis indicates that the p-block metals persist in the Cu2O lattice, consistent with ICP-AES measurements (Figure f,g). Moreover, the retained Sn and In appear to help preserve oxygen in the Cu2O host, as indicated by EDS analysis of the O to Cu ratio (Figure h). However, EDS is not an ideal technique for quantitative oxygen determination due to its limited sensitivity to light elements and uncertainties arising from ex situ measurements and air exposure. Accordingly, the EDS data are intended only to provide a semiquantitative, comparative assessment of relative O to Cu ratio trends between undoped and doped Cu2O catalysts (Figure h). The persistence of Cu+ species, which are critical for high CO selectivity, is further supported by XRD phase analysis, as discussed later in the manuscript. Overall, these results suggest that the incorporation of p-block metals into Cu2O primarily influences the electronic structure, enhancing selectivity and stability of In–Cu2O and Sn–Cu2O catalysts, rather than morphology or surface-to-volume effects, which are similar across all catalysts.

All p-block metal-doped Cu2O catalysts exhibited superior stability in CO production compared to undoped Cu2O. Specifically, the doped catalysts retained 86–97% of their initial CO Faradaic efficiency, whereas undoped Cu2O retained only 61%. The extent of retention depends on both the dopant identity and concentration (Tables S4 and S5). Sn–Cu2O (1.44) and Sn–Cu2O (0.37) retained 93% and 92%, respectively, while In–Cu2O (2.03) and In–Cu2O (0.32) retained 97% and 86%, respectively. These results suggest that incorporating p-block metals not only enhances activity but also improves catalyst stability under extended operation. The stronger retention observed for In-doped systems suggests that In may stabilize the Cu2O lattice more effectively at higher doping levels, whereas the consistent performance of Sn-doped catalysts across concentrations indicates a robust stabilization effect that is less sensitive to dopant loading. Our results suggest that the p-block metal dopants are persistently incorporated into the Cu2O host lattice and stabilize cationic Cu+ species during extended CO2RR operation. STEM-EDS analyses corroborate the stability of the doped catalysts and highlight the crucial role of p-block metals in maintaining CO selectivity, as evidenced by the presence of In or Sn even after long-term electrolysis (Figures S17–S26). Consistent with this, ICP-AES results (Figure S27) confirm the retention of doped metals within the catalysts, while ICP–MS measurements (Figure S28) detect only trace amounts of In or Sn in the electrolyte after prolonged electrolysis. Furthermore, the XRD patterns in Figures a and S29 show that the incorporation of p-block metals into Cu2O suppresses its complete reduction to metallic Cu. The characteristic Cu2O peak at 2θ = 36.37° (highlighted in yellow) remains after electrolysis for all doped catalysts, whereas the Cu0 peak at 2θ = 43.30° (highlighted in orange) appears in all samples. As shown in Figure a, the ratio of Cu+ relative to total Cu (Cu+ + Cu) is higher for all p-block metal-doped Cu2O catalysts than for undoped Cu2O, indicating enhanced Cu+ retention. This finding is significant because the sustained presence of Cu+ species contributes to improved CO2-to-CO selectivity in the doped Cu2O catalysts, consistent with previous reports. , To further corroborate the XRD results, we analyzed the Cu LMM Auger spectra of the catalyst materials at different electrolysis times to evaluate the relative Cu+/(Cu + Cu+). As shown in Figures S30–S33, the doped Cu2O catalysts exhibit a higher Cu+ fraction compared to undoped Cu2O. The temporal evolution of the relative Cu+ concentration, normalized to total Cu, is consistent with the XRD results and supports the conclusion that the incorporation of In and Sn promotes stabilization of the Cu+ oxidation state under CO2RR conditions. Although the Auger measurements were conducted ex situ, careful sample handling was employed to minimize air exposure prior to analysis. Nonetheless, these ex situ results are insufficient to conclusively determine the actual catalytic state of copper under CO2RR conditions, due to possible surface oxidation and phase transformations during sample handling and exposure to ambient atmosphere. To address this limitation, in situ Raman spectroscopy was conducted to provide evidence for the persistence of the Cu+ (Cu2O) oxidation state in In-doped Cu2O and Sn-doped Cu2O catalysts. The characteristic Raman bands of Cu2O are observed at 150, 218–220, 415, 523–528, and 623–630 cm–1. The sustained presence of these features throughout electrolysis indicates the stability of the Cu+ oxidation state during operation. As shown in the in situ Raman spectra (Figures S34 and S35), the characteristic Cu2O bands at 150 and 218–220 cm–1 remain clearly observable in the doped catalysts, particularly in In–Cu2O (2.03) and Sn–Cu2O (1.44), demonstrating that In and Sn doping effectively stabilizes the Cu+ oxidation state under CO2RR conditions.

We measured the work function of the catalysts through ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (Figure S36), as this parameter is strongly correlated to the catalytic activity of a material. ,,, As depicted in Figure b, all the p-block metal-doped Cu2O catalysts have a lower work function relative to the undoped Cu2O catalyst. The measured work function of the catalyst aligns with the obtained electrochemical performance. The In–Cu2O (2.03) catalyst shows the lowest work function among all the catalyst materials, and it gives the best selectivity for the electrochemical conversion of CO2 into CO at less negative potential values. Seemingly, the identity and concentration of the p-block metal that is doped into the Cu2O are significant factors in decreasing the work function of the Cu2O base material. The obtained work function values of the p-block metal-doped Cu2O catalysts also confirm our hypothesis that Sn brings a more substantial change in the electronic structure of Cu and support the obtained CO selectivity. The work function of the p-block metal-doped Cu2O catalysts decreases relative to that of Cu2O since In and Sn introduce three and four electrons to the Cu2O lattice, respectively. The work function values in Figure b are those of the pristine states of all the catalyst materials. Considering that the catalysts may have undergone structural and compositional changes during CO2RR, we also measured their work function values after 1.5 and 24 h of electrolysis. As shown on Figures S37 and S38, the work function values exhibit the same overall trend across pristine, short-term, and long-term electrolysis. The undoped Cu2O catalyst consistently shows a higher work function than all p-block-metal-doped Cu2O catalysts. These work function values correlate well with the enhanced CO selectivity observed for the p-block-metal-doped Cu2O catalysts relative to the undoped Cu2O. Figure c presents the surface models of the catalyst systems, including the proposed incorporation sites of In and Sn within the Cu2O lattice. These models were adapted from the work of Liu et al. Based on a careful evaluation of their study and the consistency between their reported structural features and our experimental observations, we employed these to represent the probable dopant configurations in our catalysts. The incorporation of In and Sn into the Cu2O lattice in ionic states (Figure d) represents n-type doping, wherein the dopants donate electrons to the host lattice, shifting the Fermi level closer to the conduction band. This results in a higher electron density near the Fermi level, increasing the likelihood of electron occupancy in conduction band states. Such an enhancement in electron density facilitates electron transfer to CO2, thereby promoting its activation for the reduction reaction. To further rationalize the enhanced CO selectivity of the p-block metal-doped Cu2O catalysts relative to the undoped catalysts, we considered the theoretical framework established by Pu et al. and Yang et al. , Pu et al. reported that the rate-determining step for the CO2-to-CO conversion on Cu2O is the desorption of CO from the surface. Moreover, they showed that doping In into Cu2O significantly reduces the desorption energy barrier for *CO intermediates. These findings explain why In-doped Cu2O preferentially forms CO and why Cu2O can convert CO2 into more reduced products. Consistent with this, similar effects have been reported for Sn-doped copper-based materials. Yang et al. demonstrated that the incorporation of Sn into copper-based materials modifies the CO2-to-CO conversion reaction pathway in such a way that the desorption of CO becomes more facile. Additionally, they found that Sn incorporation destabilizes the *H intermediate, increasing the Gibbs free energy for its formation and thereby suppressing the hydrogen evolution reaction. Taken together, these theoretical studies provide a mechanistic framework that is in good agreement with our experimental observations, supporting the role of p-block dopants in modulating the electronic structure of Cu2O, facilitating CO desorption, and suppressing competing hydrogen evolution, thereby leading to the enhanced CO selectivity observed in our doped catalysts.

Conclusions

In summary, we successfully synthesized In–Cu2O and Sn–Cu2O catalysts that are stable, selective, and active for the electrochemical reduction of CO2 into CO through a simple room-temperature wet chemical reduction method. Even with very low concentrations of In or Sn, the p-block metal-doped Cu2O catalysts exhibited a very competitive electrochemical performance (Tables S4 and S5). We highlight that In and Sn bring about varying degrees of change to the electronic structure of Cu. Our work provides further information and new insights into the effect of the addition of a p-block metal (In or Sn) on the electrochemical performance of Cu-based materials that are relevant and could be applied to studies about other p-block metal-modified Cu-based catalysts.
