The Hsd17b13P260S patient variant knock-in male mouse model provides translational insights into HSD17B13 biology
Authors: Coenraad F Slabber, Felina Lenkeit, Kerri Grove, Alexandra Aebi, Vanessa Orsini, Alejandro Reyes, Vanessa Ludwig, Christophe Crochemore, Sebastian Bergling, Sophia Wild, Ulrike Naumann, Karyn Enos, Austin S Abrams, Hansjoerg Zeller, Raj Bhol, Louis Wang, Emily Redmond, Kristie Wetzel, Paola Capodieci, Hanna Heikelä, Guillermo Martinez-Nieto, Laura Mairinoja, Petra Sipilä, Leena Strauss, Birgit U Baumgarten, Elizabeth L George, Markus Heim, Luigi Terracciano, Antonius Bouwmeester, Jan S Tchorz, Matti Poutanen, Heinz Ruffner, Mattia R Bordoli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a prevalent liver disease that can progress to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), a condition marked by inflammation and fibrosis that increases the risk of cirrhosis, liver failure, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Loss-of-function (LoF) variants of hydroxysteroid 17-β dehydrogenase 13 (HSD17B13), a lipid droplet–associated enzyme, protect against progression from MASLD to MASH and more severe types of liver disease.MethodsWe generated a knock-in mouse model harbouring the human orthologue of the HSD17B13 P260S patient variant and investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying its protective phenotype upon dietary challenge, by applying comprehensive spatial transcriptomics, histological analysis, matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionisation (MALDI) imaging, and in vitro cellular characterisation.FindingsMale Hsd17b13P260S mice showed reduced liver fibrosis (p < 0.0001) upon dietary challenge, reflecting the protective effect seen in patients and supporting the model's translatability. Despite similar transcript levels, HSD17B13 P260S protein in the liver was significantly lower compared to wild-type (WT). Interestingly, HSD17B13 WT protein lost its periportal zonation when switching mice from a chow to a high trans-fat diet (HTF), whereas the HSD17B13 P260S protein retained periportal expression. Male Hsd17b13P260S mice showed increased steatosis (p < 0.05), while spatial transcriptomics revealed the suppression of pro-fibrotic and immune response-related genes. MALDI imaging identified significant differences in phospholipids across genotypes and diets. Both HSD17B13 WT and P260S variant protein localised to lipid droplets, but the latter showed reduced enzymatic activity. Lastly, we observed reduced expression of CCND1 (p < 0.05) and an increase in multinucleated hepatocytes (p < 0.0001) in livers from male Hsd17b13P260S compared to WT upon HTF feeding.InterpretationOur study suggests that the Hsd17b13P260S mouse model is translatable towards human physiology and useful for understanding the protective mechanisms of the human HSD17B13 P260S LoF variant. Lower HSD17B13 P260S protein abundance, reduced enzymatic activity and retained periportal localisation may contribute to protection from liver disease progression by maintaining zonated transcriptomic and lipidomic profiles. These findings provide key insights into HSD17B13 biology and its role in MASLD to MASH progression.FundingNovartis Biomedical Research Postdoctoral program, Sigrid Jusélius Foundation, Forendo Pharma.Keywords: Hsd17b13, Liver function, MASH, Fibrosis, Zonation, Spatial transcriptomics, Spatial lipidomics

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Published genetic studies consistently demonstrate that loss-of-function (LoF) variants in HSD17B13 are strongly associated with protection against various liver diseases, including progression from metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH). HSD17B13 is a lipid droplet-associated enzyme expressed in periportal hepatocytes. Its physiological substrate(s), function in liver pathophysiology, and role in disease are still largely unknown. The current lack of appropriate mouse models recapitulating HSD17B13 patient genetics limits our understanding of HSD17B13 biology.

Added value of this study

In this study, we aimed at generating a translatable knock-in mouse model (Hsd17b13P260S) replicating the HSD17B13 P260 patient-protective point mutation. Male Hsd17b13P260S mice were protected against fibrosis in a diet-induced model of liver disease. Spatial transcriptomics confirmed reduced levels of immune-response and pro-fibrotic genes in livers of Hsd17b13P260S mice, and spatial lipidomics suggested differences in phospholipid abundances in line with published patient data. Despite lower protein levels and reduced enzymatic activity, the HSD17B13 P260S protein localises to lipid droplets, similarly to the WT. Interestingly, the characteristic HSD17B13 periportal zonation is lost in livers from patients with MASH as well as in Hsd17b13 WT mice fed a high-trans-fat diet. Importantly, the HSD17B13 P260S variant protein retains its periportal zonation upon high-trans-fat diet feeding. CCND1 levels were reduced, while multinucleated hepatocytes increased in Hsd17b13P260S livers after dietary challenge, suggesting enhanced liver function. Our findings provide molecular evidence for the protective effect of the HSD17B13 LoF variant in diet-challenged male mice, consistent with observations in humans.

Implications of all the available evidence

Findings in our Hsd17b13P260S mice suggest translatability of this model and therefore provide a suitable tool to address the complex HSD17B13 biology. Characterisation of Hsd17b13P260S by extensive spatial transcriptomics and lipidomics approaches combined with broad immunostaining and cellular studies led to novel insights into HSD17B13 biology. Further research is essential to elucidate the respective contributions of enzymatic activity, expression levels and zonation patterns to the protective phenotype observed in carriers of the HSD17B13 LoF variant.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most prevalent metabolic disease of the liver.1,2 MASLD is a multifactorial, chronic liver disease that follows a progressive path, ranging from simple hepatic steatosis to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH). MASH is characterised by liver inflammation and deposition of fibrotic tissue. Patients with MASH are at higher risk of progressing to fibrosis, cirrhosis and liver failure, as well as developing hepatocellular carcinoma.3, 4, 5 Therefore, various efforts have focused on elucidating the molecular basis of MASLD to MASH progression to identify novel therapeutic strategies and targets. To date, MASH with moderate to advanced fibrosis clearly remains a high unmet clinical need.

Hydroxysteroid 17-β dehydrogenase 13 (HSD17B13) is a lipid droplet-associated enzyme predominantly expressed in periportal hepatocytes in the liver. The localisation of HSD17B13 to lipid droplets suggests a role for the protein in lipid metabolism. Furthermore, it has been suggested that master regulators of lipid metabolism such as Liver X Receptors induce the expression of Hsd17b13.6 Likewise, increased levels of phospholipids have been observed in patients carrying LoF HSD17B13 variants.7 HSD17B13 has also been shown to be upregulated in patients with MASLD and is thought to promote lipogenesis.8,9 However, its physiological function as well as the disease-relevant substrate(s) remain unknown.10

Interestingly, several genetic studies have linked LoF variants in HSD17B13 to protection from steatosis to MASH progression. An initial genome-wide association study (GWAS) has identified a protective protein-truncating variant in HSD17B13 (rs72613567).11, 12, 13, 14 Subsequently, other LoF HSD17B13 variants have been identified, all of which seem to convey protective effects upon patients with MASH.12,15, 16, 17, 18

One LoF variant (rs62305723G>A) is characterised by a single point mutation, leading to a proline to serine change at amino acid position 260 (P260S). This P260S point mutation does not appear to affect gene expression but has been associated with reduced inflammation, hepatocyte ballooning, and Mallory-Denk bodies, evidenced by histological analyses in patients.17 Importantly, the molecular basis underlying the protective mechanism remains unaddressed. Although protein-truncating variants are more common in the global population, studying the P260S variant, resulting in a full-length but apparently non-functional protein, is particularly suited for understanding HSD17B13 biology.

The liver simultaneously performs various metabolic functions, a vital feat which is enabled through spatiotemporal metabolic zonation, whereby hepatocytes are broadly classified into three main liver zones (periportal, mid- and pericentral zone) along the porto–central axis, enabling each zone to efficiently perform distinct metabolic tasks within the same organ.19 Furthermore, liver zonation allows the liver to dynamically respond to changing caloric intake to supply sufficient energy to the organism.20,21 MASH is characterised by disrupted liver zonation and impaired metabolic homoeostasis.22, 23, 24 Whether the zonation of HSD17B13 is affected in MASH and whether it correlates with disease progression is currently unknown.

Under physiological conditions, lipid metabolism is carefully balanced between liver zones, whereby periportal hepatocytes are primarily responsible for lipid uptake and β-oxidation, and pericentral hepatocytes are involved in lipogenesis. The disruption of this zonal lipid metabolism balance is a hallmark of MASH, resulting in lipid accumulation in the liver that can lead to lipotoxicity and inflammation.19 Whether HSD17B13 gene variants influence zonal lipid species composition is unclear.

Here, we generated Hsd17b13 P260S knock-in mice (Hsd17b13P260S) to mimic the human HSD17B13 P260S variant and recapitulate the molecular mechanisms of the protective phenotype observed in patients. We confirmed the variant's protective effect against fibrosis using a model of diet-induced liver disease, validating the model and supporting the protective HSD17B13 P260S phenotype suggested by human GWAS. We then applied an integrated spatial -omics approach to investigate zone–specific effects of Hsd17b13 LoF. The HSD17B13 P260S variant showed reduced expression in the liver and possesses lower catalytic activity compared to the wild-type enzyme, as evidenced in an in vitro activity assay. We also showed that whereas the wild-type HSD17B13 protein loses its periportal zonation upon spreading into neighbouring liver zones in response to high-fat diet feeding, the P260S point mutant maintained its original periportal zonation. Furthermore, using MALDI imaging, we observed genotype-dependent differences in phospholipid abundances. Finally, we report improved liver function in Hsd17b13P260S compared to WT mice. These findings indicate that HSD17B13 P260S ameliorates liver lipid composition and prevents disease progression.

Methods

Generation of the mouse lines

As the Hsd17b13 knockout mice (KO) we utilised the mouse line B6; 129S5-Hsd17b13tm1Lex/Mmucd (RRID:MMRRC_032367-UCD) obtained as sperm from the Genentech collection from Mutant Mouse Resource & Research Centers (MMRRC Repository, UC Davis, USA). The colony was established by IVF and subsequent embryo transfer using standard procedures. In these mice, exons 1 and 2 are replaced by homologous recombination to a LacZ/Neo cassette (NCBI accession BC019427.1).

Two independent mouse lines expressing the Hsd17b13P260S variant were generated by Novartis Pharma AG and the Turku Center for Disease Modeling, University of Turku, using the CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology. The P260S mutation was introduced into the exon 6 of the Hsd17b13 gene (ENSMUSE00000316982) in mouse zygotes using a single CRISPR RNA (crRNA, 5′-GATCAGAGAAATATTGATAT-3′) to induce double-strand DNA break, and a single-strand donor DNA (ssDNA) containing the targeted mutation and two silent mutations to facilitate the genotyping of the mutant mice (5′-GAAGAAGTTGCAAGGAGTCTGATCAATGGAATACTTACCAACAAGAAAATGATCTTCGTTTCATCGTATATTAATATAAGCTTGATCCTGGAAAAGTAAGTTCCTCGCAGAACCCTGAAACACTCAAACACAAATGTTAGAAATGTTGCTAT -3′). Fertilised mouse zygotes (C57Bl/6NCrl, Scanbur) were obtained after mating superovulated females and the CRISPR mix (crRNA–25 ng/ml, ssDNA–50 ng/ml, Cas9–50 ng/ml) was microinjected into the pronuclei of these zygotes. The embryos were transferred into the oviducts of pseudopregnant female mice. The offspring obtained were screened for the mutation of interest.

Hsd17b13 KO mice were genotyped using the PCR protocol provided by MMRRC using two sets of primer pairs to distinguish between wild-type (Forward 5′-GGCGTCATCATCTACTCCTACCTG-3′, Reverse 5′-TGGCTCCTGCGTCTCTTGTTATCA-3′) and mutated (Forward 5′-GCAGCGCATCGCCTTCTATC-3′, Reverse 5′-CTGGGCCAAACTTTTTAGACAC-3′) mice.

Hsd17b13P260S knock-in mice were genotyped by PCR using primers surrounding the target mutation (Forward 5′-GGGTTTTACCTCAACATCAGGG-3′ and Reverse 5′-AGGGAATCAAAGACTCATGGGT-3′). The insertion of two silent mutations into homozygous mice hence facilitates the identification of mutant genotypes by digestion of the PCR product with HindIII or VspI/AseI.

Ethics

Hsd17b13 wild-type (WT), Hsd17b13 KO, or Hsd17b13P260S mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions at Novartis Pharma AG or at the Central Animal Laboratory (University of Turku, Finland). All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and regulations and approved by either the Novartis Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) (Ref:120191; 20 DAX 019 and 23 DAX 012) or The Regional State Administrative Agency of Southern Finland (ESAVI 41729/2019). These policies fully meet the requirements of the National Institutes of Health (Bethesda, MD, USA) guidelines on animal experimentation. The mice were subjected to a 12 h light/dark cycle at constant temperature (21 ± 3 °C) and humidity (55 ± 15%), with water and food ad libitum, and were individually identified by tail or ear markings and housed with littermates (1–6 mice per cage). For specimen collection, the mice were euthanised using CO2 asphyxiation and cervical dislocation.

Dietary interventions

For dietary interventions, the mice received either chow diet (D09100304, Research Diets), choline-deficient and 0.1%-methionine-added high-fat diet with 60% fat (CDAHFD, A06071302, Research Diets) or high trans-fat (HTF.103, D17010103, Research Diets) diet. Mice used in the dietary studies were 8–12 weeks old. Following acclimatisation, a total of 52 male mice were used for the primary CDAHFD study: 10 Hsd17b13P260S on CDAHFD, 10 Hsd17b13 KO on CDAHFD, 20 WT on CDAHFD, and as a control for the diet 6 Hsd17b13P260S and 6 WT on chow. The phenotype of the Hsd17b13P260S mice on CDAHFD was confirmed with an independently generated line in a second study involving 11 Hsd17b13 WT and 14 Hsd17b13P260S mice. A total of 56 male mice were used for the HTF study divided into the following 7 groups with 8 mice each: WT on normal chow for 6 weeks, WT on HTF for 6 weeks, WT on HTF for 12 weeks, Hsd17b13P260S on normal chow for 6 weeks, Hsd17b13P260S on normal chow for 12 weeks, Hsd17b13P260S on HTF for 6 weeks, Hsd17b13P260S on HTF for 12 weeks. Experimental unit was a single animal, and mice were allocated to groups by simple randomisation. Group size was determined from a priori power estimates using pilot study variability. Mice with a clinical assessment scoring of ≤3 and/or a body condition scoring of ≤2 were euthanised.

Clinical biochemistry

Approximately 100 μl of blood was collected via the saphenous vein at the beginning of the experiment (age 8–12 weeks) and then biweekly. Blood samples were collected in BD Microtainer SST TubesTM (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and serum was extracted according to manufacturer's instructions. Serum samples were diluted 4-fold, and ALT and AST activities were measured in Movet Oy (Kuopio, Finland) using a Thermo Konelab 60i Chemistry analyser (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Shapiro–Wilk test was used to test for normal distribution. The statistical significance between two groups was tested with Mixed-effects model (REML) (two-way ANOVA for non-parametric sample sets) in GraphPad Prism (RRID:SCR_002798) 8.1.2 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Alternatively, clinical biochemistry parameters were determined with a Cobas Pure c303 analytical unit (Roche Diagnostics GmbH) applying the following codes: ALT, 20130; AST, 20220; ALB, 20090; TRIG, 21130; CHOL, 20411.

Isolation and purification of mouse primary hepatocytes

Mice were euthanised via CO2 inhalation. Livers were perfused with Liver perfusion medium (17701-038, Gibco) and cells were isolated with Collagenase A (11088793001, Roche) diluted to 120 CDU/ml in perfusion solution [DMEM-F12 (11039-047, Life Technologies) supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S; 15140122, Life Technologies), 3 mM CaCl2 (C7905, Sigma) and 10 mM HEPES (276638, Life Technologies)]. Isolated cells were washed with DMEM-F12 supplemented with 1% P/S, 12 mM HEPES and 20% heat-inactivated FBS (10270-106, Life Technologies).

Isolated liver cells were centrifuged for 5 min, at 50 g at 4 °C. When non-parenchymal cells (NPCs) were collected the supernatant was transferred into a separate tube and centrifuged for 5 min, at 60 g at 4 °C. The resulting NPC pellet was resuspended in 1 ml attachment medium [William's E-Medium (1-48F02-I, BioConcept) supplemented with 1% P/S, 1x GlutaMAX™ (35050-038, Life Technologies), 10 mM HEPES (276638, Life Technologies), 10% FBS (10270-106, Life Technologies), 0.17 μM Insulin (I 5500, Sigma) and 30 nM Dexamethasone (D4902, Sigma)], centrifuged for 5 min, at 60 g at 4 °C and stored at −80 °C until further processing.

The hepatocyte pellet was resuspended in attachment medium and filtered through a 100 μm pluriStrainer® (43-50100-01, PluriSelect) before centrifugation for 5 min, at 50 g at 4 °C. Cell pellets were resuspended in attachment medium and overlayed onto Percoll® PLUS (17-5445-01, GE Healthcare) supplemented with 1x HBSS (14185-045, Life Technologies) before mixing via tube inversion. The cells were centrifuged for 10 min, at 50 g at 4 °C, and the cell pellets were resuspended in appropriate volumes of attachment media to determine cell number before seeding cells on Collagen I-coated plates (A1048301, Gibco). Hepatocytes were allowed to attach before attachment medium was exchanged with Matrigel® Matrix (356234, Corning) diluted 1:20 in cultivation medium [(William's E-Medium supplemented with 1% P/S, 1x GlutaMAX™, 10 mM HEPES, 0.17 μM Insulin, 30 nM Dexamethasone and 10 ng/ml Oncostatin M (300-10-500UG, Peprotech)]. The culture medium was replaced daily throughout the culture period.

Adenovirus infection

Primary mouse hepatocytes were grown as 3D spheroids in 96-well ultra-low attachment plates and infected with Adenovirus 5 encoding Hsd17b13 WT or P260S. Protein expression in lysates from eight pooled spheroids was assessed by Western blot analysis using the Odyssey scanner (LICORbio). The following antibodies were used: anti-HSD17B13 (ab122036, Abcam, RRID: AB_11130824) and anti-GAPDH (G8795, Sigma–Aldrich, RRID: AB_1078991).

Lentivirus generation

HEK293 cells (ACC 305, DSMZ) were cultured in DMEM high glucose (31966, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (16140, Gibco) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Transfection of the ViraPower™ Lentiviral Packaging Mix (K497500, Invitrogen) containing pLP1 (32.5 μg), pLP2 (7.5 μg), VSV-G (9.4 μg) and the HSD17B13 sequences cloned into a pLenti vector with blasticidin resistance was performed using a DNA-CaCl2-H2O mixture. The supernatant containing the viruses was collected two days post-transfection.

Huh7 cells stably expressing HSD17B13

Parental Huh7 cells (JCRB0403, JCRB Cell Bank, RRID: CVCL_0336) were cultured in DMEM low glucose (31885, Gibco) with 10% FBS (16140, Gibco) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cells were transduced with 100 μl of the lentivirus-polybrene (0.8 μg/ml; TR-1003-G, Merck Millipore) mixture and cultured in selection medium containing 5 μg/ml Blasticidin S (A1113903, Gibco).

Generation of Jump In™ T-REx™ HEK293 cell lines

Jump In™ T-REx™ HEK293 cells expressing either HSD17B13 WT or HSD17B13 P260S were generated according to the manufacturer's protocol (A15008, Life Technologies). Briefly, cells were transfected in a 6-well plate using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000-008, Life Technologies) with the pJTITM R4 Int plasmid and pLAF expression vectors containing either HSD17B13 WT or the HSD17B13 P260S variant. After transfection, cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 for 24 h. Cells were then washed with calcium- and magnesium-free PBS (14190-094, Life Technologies), detached using 0.25% Trypsin (25200-056, Life Technologies), and pooled into a T75 flask for selection and further culture. Cells were maintained in DMEM (31966, Gibco) supplemented with 10% dialysed FBS (2-01F17-I, AMIMED), 0.1 mM NEAA (11140-035, Invitrogen), 25 mM HEPES (15630-056, Gibco), 2 mg/ml Geneticin (10131, Gibco), and 5 μg/ml Blasticidin S (A11139-03, Gibco).

Fluorescence intensity assay

HEK293 cells expressing either HSD17B13 WT or the HSD17B13 P260S variant were seeded at a density of 1.3 × 104 cells per well in 384-well black, flat-bottom, uClear, sterile plates (781091, Greiner) containing 45 μl OptiMEM (11058-021, Gibco). Doxycycline (D3447, Sigma–Aldrich) was added to a final concentration of 1 μg/ml to induce expression. Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h in the presence of 20 μM surrogate substrate (3-(6-methoxynaphthalen-2-yl) cyclohex-2-en-1-ol). After the initial incubation, fluorescence was measured hourly for an additional 6 h using a PerkinElmer EnVision 2104 Multilabel Reader (excitation filter: Umbelliferone 355/40; emission filter: TRF Emission 520/25).

Human tissue samples

Human tissue needle biopsies were obtained from the University Hospital Basel Tissue Bank in accordance with the Swiss Federal Human Research Act (HRA). Overall, four control and six MASH liver needle biopsies were analysed. Control samples were obtained from peritumoral normal liver tissue collected during cancer resections and did not show signs of MASH based on histopathological assessment.

RNA isolation and qRT-PCR analysis

Procedure at Novartis: Total cellular RNA was isolated from primary hepatocytes or homogenised liver tissue by following the manufacturer's instructions of a RNeasy Mini Kit (74104, Qiagen). 500–1000 ng DNase I (79254, Qiagen)-treated RNA was reverse-transcribed using an iScript™ cDNA synthesis kit (1708891, Bio Rad). RT-qPCR was performed using LightCycler® 480 SYBR Green I Master (4887352001, Roche) and RT-qPCR signals were detected on a QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR System (A34322, Applied Biosystems). Gapdh or Ubc CT values were used for normalisation.

Procedure at University of Turku: Total RNA was isolated from approximately 30 mg of frozen liver tissue using Trisuretm (Bioline, London, UK). About 3 mg of RNA was treated with DNase I using a DNase I kit (Sigma Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA) and reverse transcribed using SensiFAST™ cDNA Synthesis kit (Bioline). The cDNA samples were diluted 100-fold prior to RT-qPCR analysis. SYBR Green kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to perform RT-qPCR analysis. The results were analysed using Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (RRID:SCR_018064) (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) together with CFX Manager (RRID:SCR_017251) software (Bio-Rad) and normalised to a reference gene (Gapdh). Shapiro–Wilk test was used to test for normal distribution. An unpaired t-test or nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test was used to determine the statistical significance between the genotypes using Graphpad Prism (RRID:SCR_002798) 8.1.2 software (GraphPad Software). Primers used for RT-qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S11.

SDS-PAGE and Western blot analysis

Protein from tissue or cells was extracted using Pierce™ RIPA buffer (89961, Thermo Fischer Scientific) supplemented with Halt™ Protease & Phosphatase inhibitor (78447, Thermo Fischer Scientific). For tissue samples, 50 mg snap-frozen tissue was homogenised in Lysing Matrix D tubes (6913100, MP) using an MP FastPrep-24™ 5G Homogeniser (116005500, MP) with the following settings: 6.0 m/s, 40 s. Tissue debris was removed by centrifugation at 17000g for 10 min at 4 °C. The protein concentration was determined using a BCA Protein assay kit (23225, Thermo Scientific). Protein samples were prepared for SDS-PAGE with NuPAGE™ LDS Sample Buffer (NP0007, Invitrogen) and NuPAGE™ Sample Reduction Agent (NP0009, Invitrogen). 10–20 μg protein was loaded onto precast 4–12% gradient gels (NP0336BOX, Invitrogen) and run in 1x MES SDS Running buffer (NP0002, Novex) at 120 V until the desired separation was achieved. Protein separation was monitored using Precision Plus Protein All Blue Prestained Protein Standards (1610373, Bio Rad). Semi-dry blotting was performed onto methanol-activated Transfer Membrane Immobilon-FL (IPFL00010, Millipore) at 15 V in 2x NuPAGE™ Transfer Buffer (NP006-1), 20% (v/v) methanol (320390, Sigma Aldrich) supplemented with NuPAGE™ Antioxidant (NP0005, Invitrogen). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk powder (70166, Fluka) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table S12) diluted in 0.5% milk, TBS-Tween 20 buffer (28360, Thermo Fischer Scientific). Membranes were washed 3x in 1x TBS-Tween 20 before secondary antibodies (NA934V, CiteAb) were applied. Following membrane washing, chemiluminescence signal was generated with Pierce ECL Plus Western Blotting Substrate (32132, Thermo Fischer Scientific) or SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (34094, Thermo Fischer Scientific) and acquired on a ChemiDoc XRS+ System (Bio-Rad).

Haematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining

H&E staining was performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections using a Tissue Stainer (HT110232, Sigma Aldrich). The sections (3.5 μm thick) were deparaffinised in xylene (82510-156, Brenntag) and rehydrated in serial steps of decreasing ethanol (82343-156, Brenntag) concentrations. The sections were then stained with Mayer's Haematoxylin (MHS128, Sigma Aldrich) and Eosin (HT110232, Sigma Aldrich) and dehydrated for mounting with Eukitt (03989, Sigma Aldrich) and coverslipping. The stained slides were scanned at 20x magnification using an Aperio AT2 digital slide scanning system (Biosystems), and histological analysis was performed with the HALO software (Indica Labs, v4.0.5107.407).

Picro Sirius Red staining

Picro Sirius Red staining was performed on FFPE tissue sections using a Tissue Stainer (HT110232, Sigma Aldrich). Sections (3.5 μm thick) were deparaffinised in xylene (82510-156, Brenntag) and rehydrated in serial steps of decreasing ethanol (82343-156, Brenntag) concentrations. The sections were then stained with Sirius Red (0.1% in picric acid; SO-674, Rowley Biochemicals) and dehydrated for mounting with Eukitt (03989, Sigma Aldrich) and coverslipping. The stained slides were scanned at 20x magnification using an Aperio AT2 digital slide scanning system (Biosystems) and histological analysis was performed with the HALO software (Indica Labs, v4.0.5107.407). At the University of Turku, the stained microscope slides were scanned with a Pannoramic 1000 Flash digital slide scanner (3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary) with 20X magnification and a resolution of 0,24 μm/pixel, and histological analysis was performed using QuPath (version 0.2.0).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissue sections (3.5 μm thick) were stained using standard protocols on a Roche Diagnostics Discovery ULTRA instrument. The following reagents were employed: OmniMap anti-Rb HRP (760-4311, Roche Diagnostics), DISCOVERY ChromoMap DAB kit (760-159, Roche Diagnostics), DISCOVERY Purple kit (760-229, Roche Diagnostics), DISCOVERY Teal HRP kit (760-247, Roche Diagnostics). Antibodies used for IHC (see Supplementary Table S13) were diluted in Dako Antibody Diluent (S3022, Dako) and applied manually to tissue sections in a volume of 150 μl. Counterstaining was performed with Haematoxylin II (790-2208, Roche Diagnostics) and Bluing reagent (760-2037, Roche Diagnostics). The slides were dehydrated using a Tissue Stainer (HT110232, Sigma Aldrich) and coverslipped with Eukitt (03989, Sigma Aldrich). Stained slides were scanned at 20x magnification using an Aperio AT2 digital slide scanning system (Biosystems) and histological analysis was performed with the HALO software (Indica Labs, v4.0.5107.407). Whole-slide liver images were imported into HALO, where random forest classifiers were trained to identify periportal and pericentral protein-stained regions. The quantified protein-positive areas were exported to Excel and normalised to the total tissue area to calculate the percentage of tissue covered by each protein.

Multiplex immunofluorescence (IF)

FFPE (3.5 μm) or snap frozen (7 μm) liver sections were stained using standard protocols on a Roche Diagnostics Discovery ULTRA instrument. Snap-frozen tissues were manually washed in PBS (10010-023, Gibco) and fixed in formalin (HT501640, Merck) for 15 min before staining. The following reagents were employed: DISCOVERY Inhibitor (7017944001, Roche Diagnostics), OmniMap anti-Rb HRP (760-4311, Roche Diagnostics), Opal 520 reagent pack (FP1487001KT, Akoya Bioscience), Opal 570 reagent pack (FP1488001KT, Akoya Bioscience), Opal 690 reagent pack (FP1497001KT, Akoya Bioscience). The Opal reagents were diluted 1:100 in 1x Plus Amplification Diluent (FP1498, Akoya Bioscience). Antibodies used for multiplex IF (see Supplementary Table S13) were diluted in Dako Antibody Diluent (S3022, Dako) and applied manually to tissue sections in a volume of 150 μl. Counterstaining was performed with DAPI (D1306, Invitrogen). The slides were washed with soapy water before manual coverslipping with FluorSave™ Reagent (345789, Merck). Slides were scanned with a PhenoImager HT (Akoya Biosciences) at a magnification of 20x and image analysis was performed with the HALO software (Indica Labs, v4.0.5107.407, see IHC).

RNAscope

RNAscope was performed on 5 μm FFPE sections following the instructions of the RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent v2 Assay Kit (323100, ACD). Sections were dried overnight at 37 °C and subjected to proteasome digestion. Next, probe hybridisation (RNAscope™ 2.5 LS Probe- Mm-Hsd17b13, 460408) and signal amplification was performed. RNAscope images were taken using a PhenoImager HT (Akoya Biosciences) slide scanner at a magnification of 20x and image analysis was performed with the HALO software (Indica Labs, v4.0.5107.407, see IHC).

Spatial transcriptomics

Spatial gene expression profiling was performed using the 10x Genomics Visium CytAssist Spatial Gene Expression for FFPE following the manufacturer's protocol (CG000518 Rev C). FFPE mouse liver sections (5 μm thick) were mounted onto positively charged slides (Epredia SuperFrost Plus Adhesion Microscope Slides, Thermo Fisher). High resolution H&E imaging was performed on Panoramic MIDI II at 37x magnification. The 6.5 mm × 6.5 mm capture area was chosen, and manufacturer's workflow was followed until the library preparation. Libraries were sequenced with paired-end dual-indexing (28 cycles Read 1, 10 cycles i7, 10 cycles i5, 90 cycles Read 2) on NovaSeq™ 6000. Spatial transcriptomics data were processed with Space Ranger v2.1.1 using the 10x Genomics mouse reference mm10-2020-A. Image registration and spatial alignment were performed in Space Ranger using the CytAssist and microscopy images.

MALDI imaging

MALDI imaging experiments were acquired using a Bruker timsTOF fleX mass spectrometer. Snap-frozen livers were sectioned at 10 μm thickness using a CM3050 S cryostat (Leica Biosystems). Sections were thaw-mounted onto indium tin oxide (ITO) coated microscope slides for MALDI IMS and serial sections were collected on glass microscope slides for multiplex IF (see multiplex IF materials and methods section). Prior to matrix application, MALDI slides were dipped 3-times in chilled 150 mM ammonium acetate for 5 s each to remove salts from the tissue. 2,6-dihydroxyacetophenone (DHA) matrix was prepared at 8 mg/ml in 80% methanol and was applied using a TM-Sprayer instrument (HTX Technologies) with the following settings: matrix flow rate of 0.10 ml/min, stage speed of 1300 mm/min, 10 passes, and a 2.5 mm track spacing at 50 °C. Spectra were collected in positive ion and negative ion modes in the range of m/z 300–1500. Image resolution was set at 40 μm. SCiLS Lab (Bruker) was used for data analysis and image visualisation. For identification, m/z values extracted from the MALDI imaging data were run through LIPID MAPS and the HMDB databases to obtain putative IDs of the detected peaks. Not all m/z values had a database match and furthermore, not all m/z values selected for fragmentation yielded a confirmed identification. We selected m/z values with a database match of less than 5 ppm and sufficient intensity for on-tissue MS/MS fragmentation to confirm the lipid identification.

Results

The Hsd17b13P260S mouse model recapitulates the human liver disease protective phenotype

In the present study we generated two independent mouse lines harbouring an amino acid change (Hsd17b13P260S) that corresponds to the patient-protective HSD17B13 P260S variant. We initially assessed whether these models recapitulate the liver fibrosis protective effects observed in patients carrying LoF variants and compared them to the previously published Hsd17b13 knockout (Hsd17b13 KO) mice.25 For this purpose, 8–12-week-old Hsd17b13P260S and Hsd17b13 KO male mice were fed a choline-deficient and 0.1%-methionine-added high-fat diet (CDAHFD) that induces a MASH-like phenotype with liver fibrosis as a consequence of impaired VLDL secretion.26 CDAHFD-induced liver fibrosis was measured by quantifying Picrosirius red staining, a well-established marker for fibrosis in preclinical mouse models. The analysis revealed a significant decrease in fibrosis levels in both Hsd17b13P260S (p < 0.0001) and Hsd17b13 KO (p < 0.01) mice compared to WT controls, thus suggesting translatability of both these Hsd17b13 LoF mouse models (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. S1A and S1B). The Picrosirius red staining data were further supported by lower expression of Col1a1 and Tnfα in both the Hsd17b13P260S and Hsd17b13 KO males (p < 0.0001 vs. Hsd17b13 P260S and p < 0.01 vs. Hsd17b13 KO for Col1a1; p < 0.05 vs. Hsd17b13 P260S and p < 0.05 vs. Hsd17b13 KO for Tnfa) (Supplementary Fig. S1C–F). Furthermore, serum analysis of liver damage-associated markers revealed reduced aspartate transaminase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) in Hsd17b13P260S mice compared to WT controls over a 12-week CDAHFD feeding insult (Supplementary Fig. S1G and H), suggesting an improved ability of the liver to tolerate CDAHFD-induced liver damage. Given that the protection against fibrosis was consistent across both LoF genotypes, we subsequently focused on the Hsd17b13P260S model due to its significance for human translation.

Spatial transcriptomics identifies suppressed pro-fibrotic genes in liver zones of Hsd17b13P260S mice on both chow and HTF diets

Postulating that anti-fibrotic protection arises early in disease progression, we examined the Hsd17b13P260S phenotype under a mild dietary challenge. For this purpose, we fed 8–12-week-old Hsd17b13P260S and WT mice a chow or a high trans-fat (HTF) diet for 12 weeks, which induced an obesogenic liver insult. Body weight increased similarly over time in both genotypes during HTF feeding, and liver weight was likewise comparable across groups (Supplementary Fig. S1I and J). Serum levels of ALT, AST, albumin (ALB), and triglycerides (TRIGL) did not differ between genotypes (Supplementary Fig. S1K–N). Interestingly, however, Hsd17b13P260S mice displayed elevated serum cholesterol (CHOL) levels after 12 weeks on the HTF diet (p < 0.05) compared to WT controls (Supplementary Fig. S1O).

We then performed spatial transcriptomics to identify differentially regulated genes among both Hsd17b13 genotypes for each diet, using an adjusted p-value ≤0.05 and log2 fold-change (FC) ≥ 1 or ≤ −1 as cutoffs. Our pseudo bulk analysis identified 33 genes to be differentially expressed in Hsd17b13P260S compared to WT mice on the chow diet, of which 27 were down-regulated and 6 were up-regulated (Supplementary Table S1). Gene set enrichment analysis of the top down-regulated genes suggested altered gene expression in pathways related to immune system activation, inflammatory response, and adipocyte differentiation (Supplementary Table S2). On the HTF diet 61 genes were differentially expressed in Hsd17b13P260S mice compared to control animals, of which 51 genes were decreased and 10 increased (Supplementary Table S3). In addition to immune response regulation, gene set enrichment analysis revealed down-regulation of genes involved in extracellular matrix organisation and collagen fibril organisation in the Hsd17b13P260S mice compared to WT mice on HTF diet, with genes falling into the two categories including Col1a1 and Col1a2 (Supplementary Table S4).

Overall, only a few genes revealed strong differential expression in the pseudo bulk RNA analysis comparing Hsd17b13P260S vs WT mice. Furthermore, when plotting the ratio of gene expression in HTF and chow diets for WT and Hsd17b13P260S mice, most genes fell along the diagonal, suggesting an overall similar response to the HTF diet in the livers of WT and Hsd17b13P260S animals (Fig. 1B and C).

Cell clustering analysis of the liver spatial transcriptomics identified 12 distinguishable cell clusters that overall seemed to behave similarly in both Hsd17b13 genotypes when the mice were switched from chow to the HTF diet for 12 weeks (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Next, we arranged the liver cell clusters along a periportal-to-pericentral axis, enabling us to group the clusters into the periportal, mid-, or pericentral zones using zone-specific marker genes (Supplementary Fig. S2B and C). We then analysed the differentially expressed genes in the liver zones of Hsd17b13P260S compared to WT mice, under both chow and HTF diet conditions. Most of the top down-regulated genes (p-value ≤0.05 and log2(FC) ≤ −1) in the different liver zones of Hsd17b13P260S mice on the chow diet play a role in the immune response, such as the attraction of immune cells. These genes included Cxcl1 in the mid- and periportal zone, Lcn2 across all liver zones, Cebpd and Nfkb2 in the pericentral zone, and Il1r1 in the periportal zone, suggesting reduced recruitment of immune cells to the liver in chow-fed Hsd17b13P260S mice. Additionally, Cers6, involved in sphingolipid metabolism, was among the most down-regulated transcripts identified in the periportal zone of Hsd17b13P260S mouse livers, suggesting altered metabolic composition. Contrariwise, most overexpressed genes were linked to fatty acid metabolism. These genes included Acot1 in the mid- and periportal zone, Cyp2j9 and Cyp4a14 in the midzone, and Pdk4, Cyp4a14 and Acot3 in the pericentral zone (Supplementary Tables S5–7).

Next, we analysed the liver zones of Hsd17b13P260S and WT mice fed an HTF diet for 12 weeks. In doing so, we identified genes involved in extracellular matrix deposition and maturation as among the most down-regulated genes in Hsd17b13P260S mice, including Col1a1 and Col1a2 in the mid- and pericentral zone, Col5a2 in all liver zones, Col6a2 in the midzone, Mmp12 in the pericentral zone, and Col6a3 in the periportal and pericentral liver zones. Furthermore, we also noted immune cell-related transcripts such as Trem2 and Ly6 in the mid- and pericentral zone of the liver to be suppressed in Hsd17b13P260S mice. On the other hand, factors involved in fatty acid metabolism, such as Cyp2c54 (in the periportal and midzone) and Cyp2a4 (in the midzone), and nutrient uptake (Slc2a5) were among the most elevated genes in the liver of Hsd17b13P260S mice on the HTF diet (Supplementary Fig. S2D–F, Supplementary Tables S8–10). Overall, these gene expression changes suggested that Hsd17b13P260S mice fed a chow or HTF diet exhibited reduced expression of genes involved in immune response. Furthermore, the reduced expression of extracellular matrix components likely reflected early signs of the fibrosis protection attributable to the Hsd17b13P260S allele, in particular upon CDAHFD challenge (Fig. 1A).

Spatial transcriptomics reveals Hsd17b13 expression in liver midzone clusters in response to HTF insult

Interestingly, in livers of both WT and Hsd17b13P260S mice, we observed increased expression of periportal and pericentral zone genes in the midzone upon HTF compared to chow feeding (Fig. 1D, E, Supplementary Fig. S2G). Additionally, we observed an HTF-associated trend towards upregulation of periportal-associated marker genes in clusters belonging to all zones in both WT and Hsd17b13P260S mice (Supplementary Fig. S2H). The average pericentral zone marker expression, on the other hand, showed a trend to a mild decrease in certain clusters associated with the midzone upon HTF feeding (Supplementary Fig. S2I). Average periportal and pericentral gene expression of three independent mice suggest that, despite minor alterations, the overall zonation pattern was consistently maintained across all conditions (Supplementary Fig. S2J and K). Hsd17b13 was detected in the midzonal clusters upon HTF diet feeding in Hsd17b13 WT males (Fig. 1F). We considered a gene differentially expressed if it passed a significance false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of 5% and an absolute log2 FC of 1 or more upon HTF diet challenge. Hsd17b13 WT expression showed a trend of induction in all three zones. However, the difference did not meet the FC criteria; the log2 FC was 0.25 in the periportal zone; 0.5 in the midzone, and 0.96 in the pericentral zone. Taken together, these findings indicate that a 12-week HTF dietary challenge preserved overall liver zonation.

Intrigued by the observation that the expression of both WT and Hsd17b13P260S was detected under HTF conditions in clusters falling outside of the periportal zone, we next subjected our mouse models to histological and protein level characterisation upon physiological (chow diet) and steatotic (HTF diet) feeding.

Protein levels of the HSD17B13 P260S variant are strongly reduced compared to WT HSD17B13

We observed no obvious histological differences in the livers of 8–12-week-old WT and Hsd17b13P260S mice on a chow diet (Fig. 2A). RT-qPCR analysis also indicated similar expression levels of the WT transcript and the Hsd17b13P260S gene variant in liver homogenates, whereby they are mainly expressed in hepatocytes and not in non-parenchymal cells (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2B and C). In situ hybridisation via RNAscope confirmed the periportal localisation for the expression of both WT and the Hsd17b13P260S variant in chow-fed mice (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Interestingly, despite identical expression of the WT and Hsd17b13P260S transcripts in the mouse liver, Western blot analysis revealed a strongly reduced level of the HSD17B13 P260S variant protein in the liver compared to the WT protein (Fig. 2D). This finding was confirmed by immunohistochemistry of WT and Hsd17b13P260S mice where we observed a strong staining for the WT protein and only a faint staining for the Hsd17b13P260S variant in periportal liver zones (Fig. 2E). The periportal expression of HSD17B13 in chow-fed mice was confirmed by fluorescent-activated cell sorting followed by Western blot with a polyclonal HSD17B13 antibody of WT primary hepatocytes (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Hepatocyte populations were sorted based on the expression of the pericentral marker CD73. In these experiments Hsd17b13 KO samples were used as control for antibody specificity (Fig. 2D and E and Supplementary Fig. S3B). We also determined whether changes in the underlying liver periportal zonation might account for the reduced Hsd17b13P260S protein levels. Therefore, we quantified the periportal zone across Hsd17b13 genotypes by immunostaining the livers for histidine ammonia-lyase (HAL) and glutamine synthetase, two established markers of periportal or pericentral zones, respectively.27 No major, genotype-dependent changes in the size of the HAL-positive zone were detected (Fig. 2F). Overall, these findings indicated that the reduced protein levels of the Hsd17b13P260S variant are protein-specific and not driven by differential gene expression or genotype-dependent changes in general liver zonation.

HSD17B13 P260S localises to lipid droplets but has reduced in vitro enzymatic activity

To further confirm the disconnect between Hsd17b13P260S transcript and the corresponding protein levels, we transduced primary mouse hepatocytes of Hsd17b13 KO mice via adenoviral gene transfer to rescue either HSD17B13 P260S or WT protein expression. Analysis revealed similar transcription levels between WT and Hsd17b13P260S transcripts (Supplementary Fig. S3C). However, HSD17B13 P260S protein levels were markedly lower compared to the WT protein (Fig. 3A). These findings are consistent with the observations in vivo (Fig. 2B and D) suggesting potential differences in protein biophysical stability, translation, and/or turnover between WT and HSD17B13 P260S in mouse primary hepatocytes.

Next, we investigated the enzymatic activity of HSD17B13 WT and the HSD17B13 P260S variant. For this purpose, we employed the Jump-in™ T-REx™ HEK293 system, which enables targeted integration of a single gene copy into a specific locus in the genome, resulting in comparable protein levels between HSD17B13 WT and HSD17B13 P260S (Fig. 3B). We measured the turnover of a surrogate substrate in cells expressing either HSD17B13 WT or the HSD17B13 P260S variant. HSD17B13 WT was able to efficiently convert the substrate, whereas the point mutant showed reduced enzymatic activity (Fig. 3C). These data suggested that the HSD17B13P260S variant display reduced enzymatic capacity in vivo. Intriguingly, the notion that similar protein levels between HSD17B13 WT and P260S were achieved in the Jump-in™ T-REx™ HEK293 system, which is in striking contrast to primary mouse hepatocytes, suggested hepatocyte-specificity in regulating translation, stability, or turnover of the HSD17B13 P260S variant. We confirmed this hypothesis by achieving similar protein levels of WT and HSD17B13 P260S in stably transduced HEK293 cells but reduced protein levels of HSD17B13 P260S compared to WT in Huh7 cells (Fig. 3D). Moreover, this finding also confirmed that the HSD17B13 antibody used in this study recognised both variants with equal efficiency.

As HSD17B13 is known to be lipid droplet-associated, we sought to determine whether HSD17B13 P260S shares the same localisation. We transfected Huh7 cells with plasmids expressing either WT HSD17B13 or the HSD17B13P260S variant and subsequently performed immunofluorescence staining of HSD17B13 and lipid droplets using BODIPY. Both the WT and point mutant protein co-localised with lipid droplet membranes (Fig. 3E). Taken together, these findings indicated that the HSD17B13 P260S variant was present on lipid droplets, but exhibited reduced enzymatic activity, and might therefore still perform a potential scaffolding function on the membrane.

HSD17B13 P260S maintains periportal zonation in steatosis, while WT HSD17B13 spreads into the mid- and pericentral zones

Next, we examined the consequences of HTF diet-mediated liver steatosis on the Hsd17b13 transcript and HSD17B13 protein expression in vivo. RT-qPCR and RNAscope revealed that HTF diet feeding for 6 or 12 weeks had minimal effects on Hsd17b13 mRNA levels in livers from WT and Hsd17b13P260S mice and the periportal zonal expression pattern was retained (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. S4A). Interestingly, however, HTF increased both WT and HSD17B13 P260S protein levels in livers of male mice compared to chow-fed animals (Fig. 4B). We confirmed this finding by immunostaining for HSD17B13 on the livers of mice on either chow or 6- and 12-week HTF diet. Importantly, we observed a marked, time-dependent spread of WT HSD17B13 across liver zones during the 12-week HTF diet feeding, suggesting a gradual loss of HSD17B13 WT protein zonation (Fig. 4C, top and Fig. 4D). In contrast, the HSD17B13 P260S variant did not spread across liver zones and rather retained its original periportal zonation pattern even under the HTF diet. The HSD17B13 P260S staining was, however, increased under HTF diet feeding in a time-dependent manner, as evidenced by immunohistochemistry analysis (Fig. 4C, bottom), thereby accounting for the increased protein levels observed in the Western blot analysis (Fig. 4B). Corresponding Hsd17b13 KO control samples were run side by side (Fig. 4B and C). To assess whether the spread of the HSD17B13 WT protein was a consequence of general changes in the underlying liver zonation, we performed immunohistochemical staining of HAL and GS on livers of WT and Hsd17b13P260S mice following a 12-week HTF diet. We observed no major genotype-dependent difference in the size of the HAL-positive area, indicating that factors associated with the periportal zone in general were not differently affected by the HTF diet among the different mouse genotypes (Fig. 4E). To further investigate the general zonation pattern under chow versus HTF diet, we stained for Argininosuccinate Synthase 1 (ASS1), CYP2E1, and Hepcidin Antimicrobial Peptide 2 (HAMP2), serving as additional periportal, pericentral, and midzonal markers, respectively. The localisation of each protein did not change after 12 weeks on the HTF diet among the WT and Hsd17b13P260S indicating that overall zonation was preserved (Supplementary Fig. S4B and C). Simultaneous detection of HSD17B13 WT or HSD17B13 P260S with HAL and GS by immunofluorescence staining further showed that in WT mice on chow diet HSD17B13 and HAL signals exhibited a nearly perfect overlap. In contrast, the area stained for the HSD17B13 P260S variant was smaller than that of HAL, indicating that the variant was fully encompassed by the periportal zone (Fig. 4F, left, and 4G). However, when feeding WT mice an HTF diet, we observed a time-dependent spread of HSD17B13 from the HAL-positive area into neighbouring liver zones, which extended to the pericentral zone at the 12-week timepoint, encompassed by the GS-positive area. On the other hand, the periportal-specific localisation of HSD17B13 P260S was preserved even under the 12-week HTF diet feeding (Fig. 4F, right, and 4G).

Importantly, HSD17B13 protein expression beyond the periportal zone was also observed in liver biopsies of patients with MASH. In control liver samples not presenting any histopathological signs of MASH, HSD17B13 colocalised with HAL in the periportal zone and was absent from the pericentral region marked by GS (Supplementary Fig. S4D, top, and Supplementary Fig. S4E). In patients with MASH, however, HSD17B13 lost its zonated expression pattern and overlapped with that of GS (Supplementary Fig S4D, bottom, and Supplementary Fig. S4E).

These findings indicated the extension of HSD17B13 protein localisation beyond the periportal zone in both mouse and human diseased livers. Intriguingly, these data suggested a causal link between the loss of HSD17B13 zonation and MASLD progression.

Hsd17b13P260S mice present with increased liver steatosis

Next, we analysed the extent and pattern of steatosis across Hsd17b13 genotypes in mice on HTF diet. In line with the increased expression of fatty acid metabolic enzymes observed in the spatial transcriptomics analysis (Supplementary Fig. S2D–F) we detected increased steatosis in the livers of Hsd17b13P260S mice compared to WT controls upon 12-week HTF diet (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. S5A). Interestingly, the overall increase in steatosis in Hsd17b13P260S mice originated from increased levels of macro-steatosis (p < 0.01), whereas levels of micro-steatosis were comparable across genotypes (Fig. 5B, top and bottom panels, respectively). The observed increase in macro-steatosis aligns with previously reported data in male Hsd17b13 KO mice, both in chow25 and high-fat diet.17

Furthermore, we noticed a zonated distribution of lipid droplets within the liver. We therefore quantified the amount of lipid droplets associated with different liver zones by co-staining with the periportal and pericentral markers HAL and GS, respectively. After a 6-week HTF diet, most lipid droplets localised to the periportal region of both WT and Hsd17b13P260S mice (Fig. 5C, left panel). In contrast, upon 12 weeks of HTF diet feeding, lipid droplets exhibited a more homogeneous distribution across the livers of both WT and Hsd17b13P260S mice (Fig. 5C, right panel). As HSD17B13 is a lipid-droplet-associated enzyme, we next assessed the spatial correlation between HSD17B13 protein expression and steatosis. Immunohistochemistry defined that after both 6- and 12-week HTF diets, most lipid droplets co-localised with HSD17B13-positive areas in WT mice (Fig. 5D, left and right panels, respectively). Intriguingly, after a 6-week HTF diet, the mice carrying the Hsd17b13P260S variant exhibited a nearly even distribution of lipid droplets between HSD17B13P260S-positive and -negative areas of the liver. Furthermore, after 12 weeks of HTF feeding, the majority of lipid droplets localised outside the HSD17B13P260S-positive areas (Fig. 5D, left and right panels, respectively). These data suggested that lipid droplet composition but not formation could be affected by the presence or absence of functional HSD17B13.

This workflow enabled us to assign lipid species across the porto–central axis of the liver (Fig. 6A). Overall lipid changes were modest between the genotypes with most lipids showing less than 30% change between the groups (Fig. 6B and C). The lipid profile primarily reflected the effects of the diet, whereas genotype-driven differences are expected to be more subtle. Due to insufficient annotation in lipid mass databases, the cluster of top hits that differed between WT and Hsd17b13P260S livers on chow diet could not be identified (Fig. 6B). However, several phospholipids were identified to be significantly altered in the chow or HTF diets, including long-chain polyunsaturated phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidylserine (PS), phosphatidylinositol (PI) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) species (Table 1). Hepatic phospholipid levels, especially PCs, have been linked to MASH progression and fibrosis in both patients and mice.7,28, 29, 30 Among others, PE 40:7 was enriched predominantly in the pericentral zone in both WT and Hsd17b13P260S mice under the chow diet. However, under HTF diet, its level was higher in the periportal area relative to the pericentral area (Fig. 6D). Additionally, PE 40:7 was increased in both the periportal and pericentral zones in Hsd17b13P260S livers compared to WT under chow diet conditions, with a trend to be increased upon HTF diet feeding (Fig. 6E). Conversely, PS 38:4 levels were decreased in Hsd17b13P260S livers compared to WT controls, in the periportal zone under chow conditions, and in the pericentral zone after HTF feeding. Under chow diet conditions, PS 38:4 showed a clear zonated pattern, which was largely lost after HTF diet feeding (Fig. 6D and F). LPC 18:0 displayed a similar zonal distribution across diets, but its levels were lower in Hsd17b13P260S livers than in WT under chow conditions and higher in Hsd17b13P260S livers than in WT after HTF diet feeding (Fig. 6G). Overall, these data validate our serial tissue sectioning workflow coupled to MALDI as a sensitive approach for detecting changes in lipid zonation that were missed by bulk lipidomics approaches.

Livers of Hsd17b13P260S mice on HTF diet revealed decreased proliferation and improved molecular markers of liver function

To further determine the ability of the liver to manage the HTF challenge, we first focused on assessing midzone recovery, as the midzone is the primary contributor to liver regeneration following injury. We revisited our spatial transcriptomics analysis of WT and Hsd17b13P260S livers on chow and HTF diet. Cyclin D1 (Ccnd1) was upregulated in the mid- and periportal zone of WT, and to a lesser extent in Hsd17b13P260S mouse livers on 12-week HTF diet (Fig. 7A). We further performed a multiplex CCND1 staining, along with HAL and CYP2E1 as periportal and pericentral markers, respectively, and the data revealed a reduced number of CCND1-positive nuclei in mid- and periportal zones of the livers in Hsd17b13P260S mice compared to the WT mice (p < 0.05) (Fig. 7B). Conversely, the expression level of the classical proliferation marker Ki-67 was generally low upon 12-week HTF diet, with high variability across mice. This likely reflected differences in the expression kinetics of Ki-67 compared with CCND1 (Supplementary Fig. S6A).

Next, we determined the status of hepatocyte multinucleation in both genotypes. Polyploid hepatocytes are a cellular source of liver regeneration and metabolic function under stress conditions.31 To specifically quantify hepatocytes and distinguish between adjacent cells, we co-stained with the hepatocyte marker HNF4a and the plasma membrane marker ATPase. Interestingly, we observed a lower number of multinucleated hepatocytes in the livers of WT animals on HTF diet compared to the mice carrying the protective Hsd17b13P260S variant (p < 0.0001), whereas there was no difference under chow diet conditions (Fig. 7C). These observations, coupled with the fibro-protective effect of the Hsd17b13P260S variant, suggested that Hsd17b13P260S livers are more resilient to a HTF diet challenge and/or that their recovery is more efficient than Hsd17b13 WT livers.

Discussion

LoF variants in HSD17B13 are linked to protection against the progression of steatosis to MASH with fibrosis,11 making HSD17B13 an attractive therapeutic target. However, advances in understanding its physiological role and the mechanisms underlying this protective effect have been limited by the absence of mouse models that accurately reflect human genetics. In fact, existing Hsd17b13 knockout and knockdown models have produced conflicting results regarding whether loss of Hsd17b13 prevents disease progression.32,33

Here we report a preclinical mouse model carrying the P260S patient-protective variant in the endogenous Hsd17b13 locus. Despite an overall lower expression compared to the WT protein, HSD17B13 P260S is detectable in the periportal zone, and its protein levels are markedly increased upon HTF feeding, thus distinguishing this model from the knock-out setting. Interestingly, Hsd17b13 mRNA levels in the liver did not significantly differ across genotypes, neither on chow nor on HTF diets, whereas HSD17B13 P260S protein levels were dramatically reduced compared to WT controls upon all diet conditions. We did not detect a meaningful increase in Hsd17b13 mRNA following the HTF diet, in contrast to previous reports of diet-induced upregulation.29,34 Differences in diet type, study duration, or mouse genetic background may explain this discrepancy. Of note, we observed comparable protein expression levels between WT HSD17B13 and HSD17B13 P260S in a non-hepatocyte Jump-in™ T-REx™ HEK293 cell system. This suggests a cell-type-specific and previously uncharacterised regulation of HSD17B13 at the protein level. The increased protein levels observed among both genotypes upon HTF feeding, combined with the localisation of HSD17B13 to lipid droplets, suggest that lipid droplets are involved in stabilising the protein. The recently resolved crystal structure provided the structural basis for HSD17B13 localisation to lipid droplets.35

Importantly, Hsd17b13P260S mice on CDAHFD were protected from liver fibrosis comparable to Hsd17b13 KO mice and showed lower serum AST and ALT levels relative to WT mice, supporting the translatability of the patient-protective phenotype. This fibro-protective effect has been observed in various HSD17B13 LoF variants in humans, including rs6531975, rs143404524 and rs72613567.11,36,37 It remains to be determined how the different features of HSD17B13 variants, such as zonation, expression levels, and enzymatic activity, individually contribute to protection against fibrotic liver disease progression.

By using a milder dietary challenge (HTF), we focused on the steatosis phase of MASLD, reasoning that early-stage changes may prime later stages of inflammation and fibrosis. In these studies, we observed loss of the WT HSD17B13 zonation upon HTF feeding, whereby the protein expression extended from the periportal zone into the midzone and, in a time-dependent manner, into the pericentral zone. We also observed a similar tendency toward extended zonation of HSD17B13 protein in patients with MASH compared to control human liver tissue. The observed spatial extension is one of the plausible reasons for the increased levels of HSD17B13 protein reported in patients with MASLD and MASH.8 In future studies, larger cohort sizes, detailed information on genetic background, and demographic data will be needed to further elucidate the correlation between HSD17B13 protein expression across liver zones and disease stages. Interestingly, the HSD17B13 P260S protein maintains its periportal zonation upon HTF diet in mice, despite a relative increase in expression comparable to that of the WT enzyme. Our spatial transcriptomics analysis indicated that the expression of Hsd17b13 transcripts beyond the original periportal zone is correlated with changes in gene expression in different liver zones. Of note, many of the most down-regulated transcripts in Hsd17b13P260S mouse liver compared to WT controls were found to be related to immune activation (e.g. Lcn2, Cxcl1) and extracellular matrix composition (e.g. Col1a1, Col1a2) in both chow- and HTF diet-fed animals. These findings align with histological data, showing reduced liver inflammation in Hsd17b13 LoF models.38

In line with the predicted enzymatic activity of HSD17B13, patients carrying the HSD17B13 rs72613567 LoF variant have been described to have higher grades of liver steatosis compared to WT carriers.11,39 Accordingly, we observed an increased degree of steatosis, specifically macro-steatosis, in Hsd17b13P260S mice on HTF diet, which mirrored previous findings in Hsd17b13 KO mice.25 Interestingly, unlike carriers of HSD17B13 rs72613567, carriers of HSD17B13 P260S (rs62305723) do not show an association with steatosis in humans.17 The discrepancy may arise from species differences or from score-based human data versus machine-learning-based quantification in mice.

In WT mice on the HTF diet, most lipid droplets were located in the HSD17B13-positive area, suggesting a potential role for HSD17B13 in affecting lipid droplet composition. Because the MALDI imaging technology used in this study did not provide subcellular resolution, it was not possible to definitively attribute observed lipid changes specifically to lipid droplets, other organelles, or cellular membranes. However, the Hsd17b13P260S mouse model enabled comparative analysis of lipid distribution and abundance across different liver zones and Hsd17b13 genotypes. Spatial MALDI analysis showed only mild genotype-dependent differences in lipid composition, likely because of the prominent effect of the HTF diet in both WT and Hsd17b13P260S mice. Translatability of our mouse model was, however, supported by genotype-dependent differences in several individual hepatic phospholipid species, including increased levels of long-chain polyunsaturated PCs in Hsd17b13P260S mice. Previous studies have likewise reported that elevated hepatic PC levels correlate with milder fibrosis both in patients carrying HSD17B13 variants and in mouse models with altered Hsd17b13 expression.29,33 Moreover, reduced hepatic PCs and lower PC/PE ratios are strongly linked to advanced forms of liver disease and fibrosis progression, independent of HSD17B13.28,30

Physiological regeneration and restoration of liver function are driven by the insulin-like growth factor binding protein 2-CCND1 axis, being an indirect target of rapamycin.40,41 Of note, CCND1 has been identified as a key regulator of liver regeneration following partial hepatectomy in rodents.42 Ccnd1 transcript and CCND1 protein levels were higher in WT than in Hsd17b13P260S mice after 12 weeks of HTF diet feeding, suggesting that WT livers may still be recovering from diet-induced insult, whereas Hsd17b13P260S livers may have already recovered. Furthermore, we noted an increased number of bi- or multinucleated hepatocytes in the livers of Hsd17b13P260S mice fed with a HTF diet. Binucleated hepatocytes support liver polyploidisation and enhance metabolic and detoxifying capacity, contributing to hepatoprotection.43,44 Polyploid, binucleated hepatocytes can re-enter the cell cycle after injury and, due to their low turnover, are less susceptible to senescence or replicative stress.45,46 Hepatocyte bi- and multinucleation are recognised features of liver regeneration in human pathophysiology and pre-clinical models.47,48 These findings indicate that Hsd17b13P260S livers either rapidly recover polyploid hepatocytes after HTF challenge or maintain hepatocyte ploidy despite the dietary challenge. Further studies are needed to confirm genotype-specific injury and recovery kinetics.

In summary, we generated an Hsd17b13P260S patient variant-relevant mouse model that provided translatable insights into HSD17B13 biology. We observed reduced levels of the HSD17B13 P260S variant in the liver and lower catalytic activity in vitro. Furthermore, unlike the variant protein, WT HSD17B13 spread beyond its typical periportal localisation under high-fat diet challenge. These differences, together with associated changes in gene expression and midzonal metabolite turnover, may contribute to enhanced liver regeneration and recovery in Hsd17b13P260S mice and in humans carrying the HSD17B13 P260S variant. Future comparative studies with different HSD17B13 patient variants and additional mutants (e.g. catalytically inactive mutants) in the same mouse model setting should provide preclinical evidence about how zonation, enzymatic activity, and expression levels contribute to the protective phenotype of HSD17B13 LoF variants in humans.
