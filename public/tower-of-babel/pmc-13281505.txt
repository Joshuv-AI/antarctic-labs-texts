Individual, interpersonal, organizational, and community contexts: factors associated with alcohol consumption among Brazilian adolescents
Authors: Lidiane Moura Coelho, Mery Natali Silva Abreu, Erika Gisseth Leon Ramirez, Laura de Souza Cury, Alanna Gomes da Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescence is a development period characterized by biological, psychological, and social changes that may lead to risk behaviors, such as alcohol consumption. Family, school, and community contexts, together with exposure to different communication media, may contribute to adolescents’ vulnerability to alcohol use.ObjectiveTo identify individual, interpersonal-, organizational-, and community-level factors associated with current alcohol consumption among Brazilian adolescents.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study using data from the 2019 National Survey of School Health (PeNSE). The outcome was current alcohol consumption (use in the past 30 days). Poisson regression with robust variance was employed to estimate prevalence ratios (PR) and their respective 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), including factors from individual, interpersonal, organizational, and community levels.ResultsTwenty-eight point one percent of Brazilian adolescents reported alcohol consumption. At the individual level, alcohol use was positively associated with age 16–17 years (PR = 1.54), cigarette smoking (PR = 1.47), use of other tobacco products (PR = 2.04), illicit drug use (PR = 1.21), regular consumption of sugar-sweetened foods (PR = 1.20), feeling sad (PR = 1.29), and thought that life was not worth living (PR = 1.24). At the interpersonal level, alcohol consumption was associated with parental alcohol consumption (PR = 1.66), parental concern about the adolescent (PR = 1.05), intrafamilial physical violence (PR = 1.09), sexual violence (PR = 1.15), and truancy (PR = 1.23). At the community level, higher prevalence was observed in the South (PR = 1.27).ConclusionsWe observed a high prevalence of current alcohol consumption among Brazilian adolescents, associated with factors across multiple contextual levels, highlighting the complexity and multidimensional nature of this behavior.Keywords: Alcohol drinking, Adolescent health, Adolescent behavior, Risk factors, Social vulnerability

Introduction

Adolescence is a developmental stage characterized by significant physical, cognitive, and psychosocial changes, which shape decision-making processes [1, 2]. During this transitional period, increases in risk behaviors and the adoption of unhealthy lifestyle patterns are commonly observed, including low consumption of fruits and vegetables, physical inactivity, high intake of ultra-processed foods, and the use of alcohol and tobacco products [3], particularly waterpipe (hookah) and electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes) [4].

In this context, alcohol consumption constitutes a major challenge to adolescent health, reflecting both the pursuit of social acceptance and integration and the influence of contextual and environmental determinants, such as household behaviors and a permissive digital environment. This environment is characterized by targeted marketing, favorable portrayals of alcohol on social media, and increasing commercialization through websites and applications, which collectively enhance accessibility and normalize alcohol use among adolescents [5–8].

Evidence from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study 2019 demonstrates the substantial burden of alcohol consumption on the health of adolescents and young people. In 2019, an estimated 59,855 deaths worldwide among individuals aged 10–24 years were attributable to alcohol use, in addition to approximately 5.9 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in this age group. Pronounced sex disparities were observed, with a greater alcohol-attributable burden in males. Furthermore, the composition of alcohol-attributable causes varies across regions, reflecting differences in social and epidemiological contexts. Among adolescents and young people, alcohol consumption contributed to a wide spectrum of diseases and injuries, particularly alcohol use disorders, self-harm, and interpersonal violence, as well as road traffic injuries, high and upper-middle levels of the Human Development Index [9].

Within the Southern Common Market (Mercosur), the prevalence of past-30-day alcohol consumption among students was 54.6% in Uruguay (2019) and 54.1% in Argentina (2018). In Paraguay, the prevalence was 35.7% (2017), whereas in Brazil it was 28.1% (2019) [10]. In the Brazilian context, 34.6% of adolescents reported initiating alcohol use before the age of 14 years, suggesting early exposure to alcohol use [11].

Epidemiological data underscore the magnitude and widespread distribution of alcohol consumption among adolescents at global and regional levels, establishing it as a salient public health concern characterized by marked cross-national variation.

Alcohol consumption among adolescents should be analyzed within a multifactorial and ecological framework, given the frequent co-occurrence of multiple risk behaviors in this population. Evidence indicates that alcohol use tends to occur in conjunction with other health-risk behaviors, such as the use of psychoactive substances and illicit drugs, sedentary behavior, and unhealthy dietary patterns, thereby potentiating its adverse health effects [1, 12].

The consequences of alcohol consumption during adolescence span physical, mental, and social domains of health. Alcohol use at this stage may impair brain development, affecting both the structure and function of the central nervous system [13]. It is frequently associated with a complex interplay involving anxiety symptoms and other emotional disorders and may serve as a maladaptive strategy for coping with stress [14]. Moreover, alcohol consumption is recognized as one of the main risk factors for the development of noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) [15].

In Brazil, alcohol consumption among adolescents is a major concern, reinforced by Federal Law No. 13,106/2015, which prohibits the sale, supply, and consumption of alcoholic beverages by individuals under 18 years of age [16].

In addition to this prohibition, Brazil has implemented other measures aimed at reducing alcohol consumption in the general population, including partial restrictions on advertising, applicable to beverages with an alcohol content above 13 degrees Gay-Lussac, hereby excluding most beers and wines, as well as health education initiatives aimed at preventing early alcohol use. More recently, there has been growing discussion around the adoption of pricing and taxation policies for alcoholic beverages in the country. However, these strategies still have limited scope and require further strengthening, particularly regarding their effectiveness in preventing alcohol use among adolescents.

Strengthening public policies aimed at preventing early alcohol use is essential, including not only enforcing compliance with existing legislation but also effectively implementing regulatory measures. Structural interventions, such as stricter regulation of alcohol advertising, increased taxation, and reduced alcohol availability, are widely recognized as effective strategies for protecting children and adolescents. Evidence indicates that education and awareness initiatives achieve greater impact when integrated with robust and effectively implemented public policies, whereas interventions based exclusively on informational campaigns tend to produce limited effects [17–21].

Although alcohol consumption during adolescence is widely recognized as a public health problem in Brazil, population-based evidence among Brazilian school adolescents regarding the factors influencing this behavior remains limited. Most studies focus on isolated aspects, without integrating the multiple dimensions involved in the initiation and maintenance of alcohol use in this age group [4, 10]. There is, therefore, a lack of research addressing multiple levels of influence and approaching adolescent alcohol use as a complex, socially embedded phenomenon. Investigating factors that favor or protect against alcohol consumption among adolescents is essential to strengthen health surveillance and to broaden the understanding of risk behaviors in this population. In addition to supporting continuous monitoring, such knowledge can guide the formulation, refinement, and implementation of public policies, as well as support more context-sensitive and effective prevention and health promotion strategies. These investigations are crucial to inform evidence-based actions and to strengthen the health system’s response to the specific needs and vulnerabilities of this age group.

Accordingly, this study aimed to identify individual, interpersonal, organizational, and community-level factors associated with current alcohol use among Brazilian adolescents.

Methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional, school-based study using secondary data from the 2019 edition of the National Survey of School Health (PeNSE).

Data from the 2019 National School Health Survey (PeNSE), conducted by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE), are publicly available from: https://www.ibge.gov.br/estatisticas/sociais/saude/9134-pesquisa-nacional-de-saude-do-escolar.html?edicao=31442&t=resultados.

Study context

PeNSE is part of the Brazilian National Surveillance System for Risk and Protective Factors for Noncommunicable Diseases (NCDs). Data were collected through a structured, self-administered questionnaire developed and standardized by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE) for the National School Health Survey (PeNSE). The questionnaire has been previously published by IBGE and is publicly available. Detailed methodological information about the instrument and data collection procedures is provided in the official PeNSE 2019 report [11].

Sampling design and study population

The survey adopted a two-stage, cluster sampling design, in which schools constituted the primary sampling units and classes of enrolled students represented the secondary sampling units. All students from the selected classes were eligible and comprised the analytic student sample [11].

The PeNSE 2019 sample included students enrolled from the 7th grade of elementary school (formerly 6th grade) through the 12th grade of high school, including technical programs integrated with secondary education and teacher training tracks. This sampling frame covered most adolescents aged 13 to 17 years, who constituted the target population of the present study. The survey was designed to estimate population parameters at the national level, as well as for major geographic regions, Federative Units (states), capital cities, and the Federal District [11].

In 2019, PeNSE included 4,242 schools and 6,612 classes, totaling 189,857 enrolled students, of whom 183,264 were present at the time of data collection. Of these, 159,245 questionnaires were considered valid according to criteria established by IBGE, which included student consent recorded in the mobile data collection device, completion of sex and age information, and minimum class-level response thresholds. For the present analysis, 124,898 adolescents were included, corresponding to students with valid information for the primary outcome variable—current alcohol consumption (past 30 days), which accounted for the additional reduction in sample size.

Study variables

The outcome variable was current alcohol consumption, defined as the self-reported intake of at least one glass or one serving of an alcoholic beverage on one or more days during the past 30 days, based on the question: “In the past 30 days, on how many days did you drink at least one glass or one serving of an alcoholic beverage?” The variable was dichotomized as “No” (0 days in the past 30 days) and “Yes” (≥ 1 day).

The selection of explanatory variables was guided by the social-ecological model, which posits that human behavior results from dynamic interactions between individual attributes and multiple social contexts, such as family, peers, school, and community, that reciprocally influence one another [22]. Within this framework, adolescent alcohol consumption is understood as a multicausal phenomenon, shaped by personal characteristics as well as relational and structural determinants, including alcohol consumption by family members and peers, school engagement, and the broader community environment. This approach allows for a more comprehensive and integrative understanding of the conditions that promote or inhibit substance use during adolescence. The social-ecological model is structured into five analytical levels, as illustrated in Fig. 1 [23].

Following the social ecological model, variables available in PeNSE 2019 were selected according to their conceptual correspondence to the individual, interpersonal, organizational, and community levels, as the survey does not include measures pertaining to the public policy level.

Individual-level variables: Age group (13–15; 16–17 years); race/skin color (White; Black); Sex (female; male); cigarette smoking during the past 30 days (no; yes); use of other tobacco products, including electronic cigarettes and waterpipe (hookah), during the past 30 days (no; yes); illicit drug use (marijuana and crack) (no; yes); regular consumption of fruits, vegetables, and greens and consumption of sugar-sweetened foods (≥ 5 days; <5 days/week); physical activity (≥ 300; <300 min/week); feeling sad (no; yes); and having thought that life was not worth living (no; yes).

Data analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted initially, including the calculation of absolute and relative frequencies and their respective 95% confidence intervals (95%CI).

For inferential analyses, Poisson regression with robust variance estimation was used to estimate prevalence ratios (PR) and 95%CI. First, bivariate analyses were performed, and variables with p-values < 0.05 were considered eligible for inclusion in the multivariable model. Subsequently, independent variables were entered hierarchically based on the social-ecological framework. Variables were included sequentially according to their theoretical proximity to the outcome (individual, interpersonal, organizational, and community levels), as illustrated in Fig. 1. Variables with p-values < 0.05 were retained in the final adjusted model.

Additionally, a descriptive assessment of access to alcoholic beverages was conducted, considering places and sources of acquisition (markets, friends, family members, and social events), stratified by adolescents’ sociodemographic characteristics. Distributions were presented as percentages with 95% CI, and between-group differences were assessed using the chi-square test of independence.

All analyses were performed using Stata (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA): Software for Statistics and Data Science, version 14.0, employing the survey (svy) module to account for the complex sampling design.

Ethical considerations

The National Survey of School Health (PeNSE) was conducted in accordance with national ethical guidelines for research involving human subjects and was approved by the National Research Ethics Commission of the Brazilian Ministry of Health (CONEP/MS), under approval number 3,249,268, issued on April 8, 2019. As PeNSE data are publicly available and de-identified, approval by an institutional Research Ethics Committee was not required for the present analysis.

Results

The prevalence of alcohol consumption among Brazilian adolescents was 28.1% (95% CI: 27.4–28.8). Among those who reported alcohol consumption, the most frequent source of access was social events/parties (35.5%; 95% CI: 34.4–36.6), followed by markets/retail outlets (27.4%; 95% CI: 26.2–28.5), friends (22.9%; 95% CI: 21.8–23.9), and family members (14.3%; 95% CI: 13.4–15.2) (data not shown in tables).

Forms of access to alcoholic beverages differed according to sociodemographic characteristics. Adolescents aged 16–17 years reported greater access via markets/retail outlets (58.6%) and social events/parties (41.7%), whereas those aged 13–15 years more frequently obtained alcohol from friends (61.0%) and family members (66.5%). Male adolescents reported higher access via markets/retail outlets and social events/parties, while female adolescents more often accessed alcohol through friends and family members. Access was also more frequent among Black and Brown adolescents, those living in urban areas, and students attending public schools, particularly in the Southeast and Northeast regions of Brazil (Table 1).

Alcohol consumption was higher among adolescents aged 16–17 years (38.9%), females (30.1%), and White adolescents (29.9%). Higher prevalence was also observed among those who reported cigarette smoking (78.7%), use of other tobacco products (72.4%), illicit drug use (81.4%), regular consumption of sugar-sweetened foods (34.8%), and indicators of psychological distress, such as feelings of sadness (29.7%) or thoughts that life was not worth living (34.7%). Regarding interpersonal factors, higher alcohol consumption was observed among adolescents whose parents consumed alcohol (34.9%), those who experienced intrafamilial violence (36.6%) or sexual violence (41.5%), and those who reported school truancy (41.6%). At the regional level, the highest prevalences were observed in the South (38.4%) and Southeast (30.8%) regions of Brazil (Table 2).

At the individual level, a higher prevalence of alcohol consumption was observed among adolescents aged 16–17 years (PR = 1.54; 95% CI: 1.47–1.60) compared with younger adolescents. Alcohol consumption was also associated with cigarette smoking (PR = 1.47; 95% CI: 1.38–1.56), use of other tobacco products (PR = 2.04; 95% CI: 1.89–2.11), and illicit drug use (PR = 1.21; 95% CI: 1.13–1.31). In addition, adolescents who reported regular consumption of sugar-sweetened foods showed a higher probability of alcohol consumption (PR = 1.20; 95% CI: 1.16–1.25). Positive associations were also observed between alcohol consumption and indicators of psychological distress, including reported sadness (PR = 1.29; 95% CI: 1.18–1.40) and thoughts that life was not worth living (PR = 1.24; 95% CI: 1.20–1.29). Conversely, male adolescents presented a lower probability of alcohol consumption compared with female adolescents (PR = 0.94; 95% CI: 0.91–0.98) (Table 3).

At the interpersonal level, a higher probability of alcohol consumption was observed among adolescents whose parents consumed alcohol (PR = 1.66; 95% CI: 1.58–1.74), as well as among those who reported experiencing intrafamilial violence (PR = 1.09; 95% CI: 1.04–1.15) or sexual violence (PR = 1.15; 95% CI: 1.10–1.20). In addition, adolescents who reported school truancy showed a higher probability of alcohol consumption in the past 30 days (PR = 1.23; 95% CI: 1.18–1.29) (Table 3).

Regarding the community level, adolescents living in the South region had a higher probability of alcohol consumption (PR = 1.27; 95% CI: 1.20–1.35), whereas those residing in the Northeast (PR = 0.91; 95% CI: 0.86–0.97) and North (PR = 0.75; 95% CI: 0.69–0.80) regions showed a lower probability of alcohol consumption compared with adolescents living in the Southeast region (Table 3).

Discussion

This study found a high prevalence of current alcohol consumption among Brazilian adolescents and identified patterns of access and multilevel correlates distributed across individual, interpersonal, organizational, and community contexts. The analysis demonstrates that alcohol consumption is concentrated among older adolescents and co-occurs with other behavioral risks and indicators of psychological distress, while also being strongly related to family practices and regional heterogeneity.

The observed prevalence of 28.1% is notable given legal prohibitions on alcohol consumption by minors and the enhanced biological susceptibility of adolescents to alcohol-related harms. Alcohol consumption during adolescence is linked to neurodevelopmental impairment, increased propensity for risk-taking, poorer mental-health outcomes, and the potential establishment of problematic consumption trajectories that persist into adulthood. The predominance of access through social events, commercial retail outlets, peers, and family members points to persistent shortcomings in enforcement of age-restriction policies and to normative permissiveness at household and social levels. These findings indicate that the high prevalence cannot be interpreted solely as the result of individual choices; rather, it reflects an enabling social and commercial environment that facilitates availability, exposure, and normalization of alcohol consumption among adolescents.

The higher prevalence of alcohol consumption among adolescents aged 16–17 years may be related to the biopsychosocial maturation process characteristic of this life-course stage. As adolescents grow older, they develop new cognitive skills, greater decision-making autonomy, and begin to occupy new social roles, which expands their participation in social spaces and increases exposure to situations in which alcohol consumption is socially encouraged [24, 25]. Cognitive and social development during adolescence fosters the search for new experiences, including exploratory and risk behaviors. Moreover, older adolescents tend to experience lower parental supervision, greater circulation in party environments, and broader peer networks, factors that may facilitate alcohol consumption [26]. Thus, age emerges as an important marker of vulnerability to alcohol consumption, reflecting not only individual developmental changes but also transformations in social relationships and everyday contexts.

The concurrent pattern of alcohol consumption and use of other licit and illicit substances during adolescence can be interpreted in light of the “Gateway Theory”, which describes a progression in substance use across development, typically beginning with licit and socially accepted substances such as alcohol and subsequently advancing to other drugs [27, 28]. This progression often occurs within a psychosocial context marked by increased autonomy, expanded social interactions, and greater exposure to peer norms and behaviors (Steinberg, 2008). Initial contact with alcohol frequently takes place in peer-related settings, such as friends’ homes, highlighting the role of social groups in the initiation and normalization of alcohol consumption during adolescence [29, 30].

In this context, behaviors related to unhealthy dietary patterns and physical inactivity may be understood as part of broader and co-occurring lifestyle patterns that tend to coexist during adolescence. National evidence indicates substantial clustering of behavioral risk factors among Brazilian adolescents, with the most prevalent combination being low consumption of fruits and vegetables coupled with physical inactivity, affecting approximately 66% of this population. Additionally, certain behaviors show strong interrelationships, with the highest observed-expected prevalence ratio identified for the combination of smoking and alcohol consumption, indicating a synergistic interaction between these risk behaviors [12]. These findings reinforce that alcohol consumption during adolescence does not occur in isolation, but rather as part of a set of interrelated and early established practices that may persist throughout the life course [12, 31].

Evidence consistently demonstrates an association between alcohol exposure and adverse outcomes among adolescents, including mental health problems, problematic behaviors, and suicidal ideation [32]. This relationship may be linked to the use of alcohol as a maladaptive coping strategy for emotional regulation in the presence of negative affect, particularly in contexts of psychological distress. Studies indicate that difficulties in emotion regulation are associated with more problematic patterns of substance use, including alcohol, suggesting that deficits in self-regulatory processes may influence how individuals cope with negative emotions through consumption. Traits related to low self-regulation and reduced self-control have been associated with a higher occurrence of alcohol-related problems, such as impaired control over intake and adverse behaviors during consumption, regardless of overall levels of consumption, underscoring the limitations of alcohol consumption as a coping mechanism [33, 34]. Furthermore, the presence of shared underlying factors, such as limited social support and difficulties in emotional management, may simultaneously influence mental health and alcohol-related behaviors [35–37]. Given the cross-sectional design of the study, causal relationships cannot be established; nevertheless, the findings reinforce the need for preventive approaches that integrate mental health promotion with strategies to reduce alcohol consumption among adolescents.

At the interpersonal level, the family environment was strongly associated with alcohol consumption among adolescents. The presence of alcohol in daily household routines, particularly when parents or caregivers frequently consume alcoholic beverages, contributes to the normalization of this behavior. Observing alcohol consumption as a common practice within the family environment may lead adolescents to perceive it as acceptable, thereby facilitating experimentation and early consumption [38, 39].

Adolescents who reported low parental supervision, represented in this study by a lack of parental concern and unauthorized school absenteeism, showed higher prevalence ratios for alcohol consumption. Weak monitoring of daily activities, prolonged absence of caregivers, and limited communication within the family environment increase adolescents’ exposure to health-risk behaviors. Evidence from public schools indicates a high frequency of family conflicts and parental absence in adolescents’ daily lives, factors associated with increased vulnerability to alcohol consumption [40, 41].

Complementarily, adolescents who reported experiencing intrafamilial physical violence perpetrated by parents or caregivers showed higher levels of alcohol consumption. Exposure to violence within the household may function as a chronic stressor, favoring alcohol consumption as a strategy for emotional coping. In addition, family contexts marked by violence often present greater disorganization, lower supervision, and higher permissiveness toward risk behaviors, creating an environment conducive to the initiation and maintenance of alcohol consumption [41–43].

Taken together, these findings indicate that alcohol consumption during adolescence is strongly related to specific family practices, including exposure to adult alcohol consumption, lack of supervision, and experiences of intrafamilial violence. The family environment therefore emerges as a central context for prevention strategies, underscoring the importance of active caregiver involvement, the establishment of clear boundaries, and the promotion of safe and non-violent family relationships as means of reducing adolescents’ vulnerability to early alcohol consumption.

The higher prevalence ratio of alcohol consumption observed among adolescents who reported experiences of sexual violence is consistent with the literature indicating a strong association between sexual violence and risk behaviors during adolescence [44, 45]. Previous studies suggest that alcohol consumption may increase exposure to risky sexual contexts and, consequently, to sexual violence [46]. Conversely, evidence also shows that experiences of sexual violence are associated with adverse mental health outcomes, such as depressive symptoms, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress, which are in turn related to a higher prevalence of alcohol consumption and other psychoactive substance use [47, 48]. Moreover, sexual violence frequently occurs in contexts of social and familial vulnerability, where other risk factors overlap, such as intrafamilial violence, neglect, and low parental supervision, further increasing adolescents’ exposure to harmful health-related behaviors [49]. Thus, the association observed between sexual violence and alcohol consumption should be understood within a broader framework of cumulative adversities, reinforcing the need for intersectoral strategies focused on prevention, protection, and comprehensive care for affected adolescents.

At the community level, higher alcohol consumption was observed among adolescents residing in the South region of Brazil, highlighting territorial heterogeneity in patterns of alcohol consumption. However, the data from this study do not allow the identification of the specific mechanisms underlying the observed regional differences, as broader contextual characteristics are not directly captured by the survey. These findings therefore point to the need for further investigations that more comprehensively explore regional determinants of alcohol consumption during adolescence.

Overall, the findings of this study reinforce that alcohol consumption during adolescence is a multifactorial phenomenon, influenced by individual, interpersonal, and community-level determinants, and strongly shaped by the familial, social, and environmental contexts in which adolescents are embedded.

Given this multifactorial nature of alcohol consumption and the identified patterns of access, the findings highlight the need to strengthen and effectively implement structural and regulatory measures in line with the World Health Organization’s SAFER initiative and recommended “Best Buys”. Priority actions include strengthening enforcement of minimum legal purchase age laws; restricting alcohol marketing and sponsorship (including digital platforms); implementing pricing and taxation policies that reduce affordability for young people; limiting outlet density and hours of sale; and regulating online sales and delivery with robust age-verification mechanisms. These measures include increasing prices through taxation and pricing policies for alcoholic beverages; comprehensive restrictions on advertising, promotion, and sponsorship, including in digital environments; and reducing alcohol availability in physical retail settings as well as in digital and remote channels. These structural measures should be integrated with family- and school-centered prevention programs to address upstream determinants and enhance protective capacities within households and communities, for example parenting support, school-based socioemotional learning, and accessible mental-health services. These measures should be complemented by continuous, intersectoral, and evidence-based educational actions that go beyond purely informational approaches and address the development of socioemotional skills, the promotion of mental health, the prevention of violence, and the strengthening of protective contexts within family, school, and community environments.

In the Brazilian context, however, the implementation of these measures remains uneven, with persistent challenges related to the enforcement of minimum legal age laws, the regulation of alcohol advertising, particularly in digital environments, and the control of availability across different social contexts. These gaps contribute to maintaining a permissive environment for early alcohol use and underscore the importance of studies that examine the multiple factors associated with alcohol consumption among adolescents.

This study has some limitations. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the use of self-reported data may be subject to recall and social-desirability bias, as well as potential misinterpretation of questions. In addition, the exclusion of adolescents not enrolled in school may underestimate the burden among more vulnerable populations. Despite these limitations, the study benefits from a large, nationally representative sample and design-based analyses.

Future studies should employ longitudinal designs and incorporate contextual indicators, such as local outlet density, enforcement intensity, and socioeconomic characteristics, as well as qualitative approaches to better understand the mechanisms underlying regional differences and to evaluate the effectiveness of policy and programmatic interventions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that current alcohol consumption among Brazilian adolescents is both prevalent and socially patterned, clustering with other behavioral risks and indicators of psychological distress. Older adolescents and female sex were associated with higher prevalence of consumption, as were exposure to tobacco and illicit substances, low parental supervision, experiences of intrafamilial and sexual violence, and truancy. Patterns of acquisition, notably through social events, commercial retail outlets, peers, and family members, point to pervasive availability and normative acceptance that undermine existing protective mechanisms.
