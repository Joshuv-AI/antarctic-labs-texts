Prevalence and associated factors of hypertensive emergency among hypertensive crisis patients admitted to the emergency department at Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Ethiopia
Authors: Teshome Shanko, Desalegn Dawit Assele, Zemedkihun Motera, Yitayew Ewnetu Mohammed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertension is a major global health problem that affects approximately 1.4 billion people worldwide, and 1–2% of this population experience hypertensive crisis. Hypertensive emergency is a subtype of hypertensive crisis associated with target organ damage and is linked with increased morbidity and mortality. In our setting, the prevalence of hypertensive emergencies and the characteristics of patients with a hypertensive crisis are not certainly known yet. This study aimed to assess the prevalence and associated factors of hypertensive emergency among hypertensive crisis patients admitted to the emergency department at Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Ethiopia.MethodsAn institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 379 randomly selected medical records of hypertensive crisis patients admitted at Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital from July 2021 to July 2024. Data were extracted from September 1 to 30, 2024. A structured checklist was used to collect the data from charts through the Kobo toolbox. The descriptive statistics were presented in tables, frequencies, percentages, and graphs. Binary logistic regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with hypertensive Emergency. An adjusted odds ratio along with a 95% confidence interval was reported. The statistical significance level was declared at a p-value ≤ 0.05.ResultsA total of 369 patients with hypertensive crisis were included in the study. The prevalence of hypertensive Emergency was 40.7% (95% CI: 36, 46%). In patients with hypertensive emergencies, cerebrovascular injuries accounted for 25.7% of the hypertension-mediated organ damage, followed by acute renal failure (22%), and cardiovascular emergencies (15.4%). New-onset hypertension [AOR:2.5; 95%CI: 1.52, 4.15] and diabetes mellitus [AOR: 2.92; 95% CI: 1.63, 5.24] were independently associated with a hypertensive emergency.ConclusionThe study found that in four out of ten hypertensive crises, patients experienced hypertensive emergencies. Cerebrovascular injuries were the most commonly identified target organ damage. New-onset hypertension and diabetes mellitus were independently associated with hypertensive emergency. Routine hypertension screening, monitoring, and counseling of hypertensive individuals could reduce the risk of target organ damage.

Introduction

Hypertensive emergency (HE) is a persistent elevation in systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 180 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 120 mmHg accompanied by acute or ongoing target-organ damage (TOD) [1]. Hypertensive crisis is a broader term used to describe patients with rapid and severe blood pressure (BP) elevation (SBP ≥ 180 and/or DBP ≥ 120), with or without TOD. As a consequence of untreated or poorly controlled hypertension, 1% to 2% of people with chronic hypertension are expected to suffer from a hypertensive crisis [2]. The pathophysiology involves sudden increases in vascular resistance and endothelial injury, leading to impaired autoregulation and organ ischemia. Hypertensive crisis is a serious acute complication of hypertension, and timely intervention tailored to the patient’s clinical context is essential for stabilizing blood pressure and mitigating risk.

Based on the presence or absence of TOD, a hypertensive crisis can be classified as hypertensive urgency or emergency, which determines the management options and prognosis of patients. Hypertensive emergency is a sudden, severe blood pressure elevation accompanied by TOD. Common hypertension-mediated organ damage in patients with HE includes cerebrovascular injuries (such as ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, hypertensive encephalopathy), hypertensive retinopathy, cardiovascular emergencies (such as acute heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, aortic dissection), and acute renal failure [3]. In one systematic review that assessed types of hypertension-mediated organ damage in patients with HE, ischemic stroke (28.1%) was the most prevalent TOD, followed by pulmonary edema/acute heart failure (24.1%), hemorrhagic stroke (14.6%), acute coronary syndrome (10.8%), renal failure (8.0%), subarachnoid hemorrhage (6.9%), encephalopathy (6.1%), and aortic dissection (1.8%) [4].

The prevalence of hypertensive emergencies varies globally. The prevalence of hypertensive emergency among patients presenting with hypertensive crisis was found to be 3.66% in Thailand [5], 28% in the United States of America [6], 58.9% in Cameroon [7], 68% in Tanzania [8], 61.7% in Sudan [9], 76% in Congo [10], and 19.1% in Brazil [11]. The prevalence of HE among hypertensive crisis patients in Ethiopia was reported to be 29.8% at the Ayder Comprehensive Specialized Hospital [12] and 34.1% at the University of Gondar Specialized Hospital [13].

Understanding risk factors associated with the development of hypertensive crisis is of paramount importance to improve healthcare delivery to patients with hypertension while reducing the significant healthcare burden. Studies reported that several factors, such as age, male gender, unemployment, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, new onset of HTN, regular follow-up and monitoring, medication adherence, family history of HTN, chronic alcohol use, smoking, and use of illicit drugs, were associated with HE [9, 14–16].

The rapid rise in noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) such as hypertension is a threat to the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, which includes a target of reducing premature deaths from NCDs by one-third by 2030 [17]. It is also an obstacle to poverty reduction initiatives in low-income countries, particularly by increasing household costs associated with health care [18]. Hypertension is one of the leading causes of morbidity and death worldwide, particularly in resource-limited settings [19].

The prevention and control of hypertension is focused on controlling modifiable risk factors, such as lifestyle modification, managing obesity, smoking cessation, and reducing salt and alcohol consumption [20]. A significant proportion of hypertensive patients with a positive history of alcohol use and smoking have a substantial risk of developing severe, uncontrolled blood pressure and subsequent organ damage [7]. Hence, lifestyle modification in the form of regular exercise and avoidance of high-risk behaviors like smoking and alcohol consumption would significantly reduce the risk of hypertensive emergencies [21, 22].

Ethiopia launched a hypertension prevention and control project in 74 health facilities and 200 health posts in seven regions of the country in 2019, to improve hypertension prevention and control at the primary health care level. Identifying factors associated with hypertensive emergencies is the crucial step towards reducing hypertension-related premature deaths and disability-adjusted life years. There have been a few studies conducted in other regions of Ethiopia that reported inconsistent results on the magnitude and risk factors of hypertensive emergencies [23–25]. There is limited evidence on the prevalence of hypertensive emergency and its associated factors in the study area. As population characteristics and study setting could significantly contribute to the observed variation, a representative study in our study area is crucial. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of and factors associated with hypertensive emergencies among patients with hypertensive crisis admitted to the emergency department at Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Sidama, Ethiopia.

Methods

Study design, setting, and period

An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 379 randomly selected medical records of hypertensive crisis patients admitted at Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital from July 2021 to July 2024. Hawassa is located 273 km south of the capital city of Ethiopia, Addis Ababa. The city serves as the capital of the Sidama Regional State. HUCSH started healthcare delivery services in 2006. The emergency department is one of the major service areas at HUCSH. The emergency department is led by Emergency and Critical Care specialists. Hypertensive crisis patients receive personalized care from multidisciplinary teams, including subspecialty consultations with neurologists, cardiologists, and nephrologists. Between July 2021 to July 2024, a total of 963 patients experiencing hypertensive crisis were admitted to the hospital’s emergency department. Data were extracted from September 1 to 30, 2024.

Population

All adult patients admitted to the Emergency Department of HUCSH with hypertensive crisis were the source population. All adult patients admitted to the Emergency Department of HUCSH with hypertensive crisis from July 2021 to July 2024 were the study population. Patients with incomplete or lost records for essential variables were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

The sample size was calculated based on a single population proportion formula by using open Epi info version 7 and considering the following assumptions: 34.1% proportion of hypertensive emergency taken from a study done at the University of Gondar Specialized Hospital [13], 95% confidence interval, 5% margin of error, and 10% for incompleteness and the final sample size was 379. A total of 963 patients with hypertensive crisis were admitted to this hospital from July 2021 to July 2024. OpenEpi software was used to generate a random sample. Then, using the subsequent unique number from the database registry, a random sample of 379 patient cards was selected.

Study variables

The outcome variable of the study was hypertensive emergency. The independent variables were Socio-demographic characteristics (age, sex), clinical and behavioral characteristics: previous history of hypertension, comorbidities (DM, CKD, dyslipidemia, cardiac disease, previous stroke), smoking, chronic alcohol use, chewing khat, and drug non-adherence.

Data collection methods

The data collection checklist was prepared from different literature [13, 14, 26] and locally adapted after being validated by Emergency and Critical Care Medicine (ECCM) consultants. The data extraction tool containing study variables of interest was prepared in the English language to extract relevant information about age, sex, smoking, chewing khat, drug abuse, dyslipidemia, clinical presentation, duration of the disease, and diagnosis on admission. Data sources were the Emergency Room Logbook, patients’ record books, and patients’ charts. Patients’ medical records, documented in the respective log books and charts, were collected. The data collection process was done by three medical interns. All data collection activities were supervised by a trained ECCM Resident.

Data quality control

One day of training was given to the data collectors by the principal investigator about the objective of the study, the variables on the questionnaire, and how to collect data. The supervisor and principal investigator checked the collected data for completeness, clarity, and accuracy on the same day of collection. A pretest was conducted on 5% of the sample size. Based on the pretest findings, necessary modifications were made to the data extraction checklist before the actual collection of data was made.

Data processing and analysis

Data were collected using the Kobo Toolbox Collect and exported to Stata version 16 for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including proportions, tables, and charts, were used to describe the characteristics of the study participants. Binary logistic regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with HE. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bi-variable analysis were candidates for the final multivariable logistic regression model. An adjusted odds ratio along with the 95% confidence interval was used to show the strength of the association. Multicollinearity diagnostics showed no significant correlation among independent variables, with a mean Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) of 1.30, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern in the logistic regression model. The level of significance was declared at a p-value < 0.05. The Hosmer-Lemeshow Test was used for checking the model fitness of logistic regressions (p = 0.801).

Variables definition

Hypertensive crisis defined as an admission SBP record of above 180 mmHg and/or DBP of over 120 mmHg with or without target organ damage, in the emergency department. Hypertensive crisis is classified into hypertension urgency and emergency [27].

Hypertensive emergency is defined as cases of hypertensive crisis accompanied by the presence of at least one target organ damage [27].

Targeted organ damages are defined as the presence of evidence of hypertensive encephalopathy, intracerebral hemorrhage, acute ischemic stroke, acute myocardial infarction, acute heart failure with pulmonary edema, unstable angina pectoris, dissecting aortic aneurysm, acute renal failure, and eclampsia [27].

Hypertensive urgency is defined as cases of hypertensive crisis with no evidence of target organ damage [27].

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

The study included a total of 369 patients, yielding a 97.3% response rate. More than half (55.3%) were aged between 45 and 64 years, with a mean age of 53.9 (SD ± 12.6) years. Nearly two-thirds (64.8%) were males, and 239 (66.4%) came from the Sidama region. Half (49.6%) had a family history of hypertension, while 50.4% had no family history of hypertension (Table 1).

Clinical and behavioral characteristics

Nearly three-fourths of the patients, 272 (73.7%), had a previous history of hypertension and were on antihypertensive medication, whereas 97 (26.3%) were newly diagnosed with hypertension. Out of the 272 hypertensive patients on antihypertensive agents, 104 (38.4%) patients took a single drug, 157 (57.7%) took a two-drug combination, and 11 (4.0%) took a three- or more-drug combination. The adherence to hypertensive medication was poor for 185 patients (68%), while 87 (32%) had good adherence.

Diabetes mellitus (17.1%) was the most commonly identified comorbidity among patients presenting with hypertensive crisis, followed by chronic kidney disease (CKD) (15.2%) and cardiac disease (6.5%). A quarter of the patients (24.1%) had a smoking history, and 111 (30.1%) had a history of chronic alcohol use (Table 2).

Factors associated with hypertensive emergencies

In bivariable logistic regression analysis, age, smoking, alcohol consumption, newly diagnosed hypertension, and diabetes mellitus were found to have a p-value < 0.25. In multivariable regression models, newly diagnosed hypertension and diabetes mellitus were independently associated with hypertensive emergencies. The odds of hypertensive emergencies were 2.5 [AOR:2.5; 95%CI: 1.52, 4.15] times more likely for those who had newly diagnosed hypertension than those who had been previously hypertensive. Similarly, diabetes mellitus was 2.9 [AOR: 2.92; 95% CI: 1.63,5.24] times more likely to be associated with hypertensive emergencies as compared to those patients without diabetes (Table 4).

Discussion

The prevalence of hypertensive emergency among patients admitted to the emergency department with hypertensive crisis was 40.7%. The finding was higher than studies conducted at Gondar (34.1%) [13], Mekelle (29.8%) [12], on African Americans in the USA (28%) [6], and at Harar Town, Eastern Ethiopia (30.5%) [28]. The observed variation could be due to differences in patient population, the health care setting, or participant selection. For instance, the study conducted in Gondar included patients admitted to both the Emergency department and medical ward, whereas our study only included hypertensive crisis patients admitted to the emergency department. In contrast, the prevalence of HE in our study was lower than in studies conducted in Cameroon (58.9%) [7], Tanzania (68%) [8], Sudan (61.7%) [9], and Congo (76%) [10]. The significant variation in the prevalence of HE across these studies could be attributed to variations in the socio-demographic profile of patients, the burden of comorbidities in patients, long-term follow-up and management of hypertension, and adherence to antihypertensive medications. For instance, the study conducted in Cameroon found that only 24.2% of patients with preexisting hypertension were on treatment, indicating uncontrolled blood pressure might be the primary cause of HE [7]. Moreover, the comprehensiveness of patient assessment might also affect the identification of patients with TOD. In our study, only 2.7% of patients presenting with hypertensive crisis were assessed with fundoscopy.

Preexisting hypertension was reported in 73.7% of patients with hypertensive crisis. The finding was supported by other studies conducted in Ethiopia, which reported that most patients who presented with hypertensive crisis commonly had preexisting hypertension [8, 26, 28]. Poor adherence to antihypertensive medications was observed in patients with preexisting hypertension, with poor adherence observed in two-thirds of patients. A study conducted in Harar Town showed that 87.7% of hypertensive urgency patients were non-compliant with their medication, which increases the odds of a poor treatment outcome by 2.7-fold [28].

Headache accounted for 72.9% of the presenting signs and symptoms among patients admitted with hypertensive crisis, followed by confusion, nausea, and vomiting, which is consistent with a study done at the University of Gondar Specialized Hospital [26], at Ayder Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Mekelle, Ethiopia [12], Harar Town, Eastern Ethiopia [28], and Brazil [11]. Headaches are common symptoms in HE patients due to ammonia build-up in the brain, caused by liver and renal damage. This leads to encephalopathy, resulting in a range of neuropsychiatric and neurological symptoms [29]. Nausea and vomiting are also common complaints, often causing dehydration and exacerbating headaches.

Cerebrovascular emergencies (25.7%), such as ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, and hepatic encephalopathy, were the most common types of TODs among patients with hypertensive emergencies, followed by acute renal failure and cardiovascular emergencies. This finding was consistent with studies conducted at the University of Gondar Specialized Hospital and Ayder Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, which reported neurovascular emergencies as the most common TOD in patients with HE [12, 13].

This study found that patients with a new onset of hypertension had 2.5 times higher odds of HE when compared to known hypertensive patients. The finding was consistent with a study conducted in Tigray, Ethiopia [12]. In contrast, a study conducted in public hospitals in Addis Ababa reported that the likelihood of having a hypertensive crisis was 3.6 times higher among participants with a history of hypertension compared to those without a history of hypertension [14]. The inconsistent findings across the studies might be attributed to differences in the long-term care of chronic hypertensive patients, healthcare access, and patient adherence to pharmacological and non-pharmacological management. Poor healthcare-seeking behavior and inadequate routine health checkups might lead patients with undetected longstanding hypertension to have an initial presentation with hypertensive crisis or emergency.

In this study, patients with diabetes mellitus were three times more likely to develop hypertensive emergencies as compared to patients without diabetes. The finding was supported by a study done at public hospitals in Addis Ababa, which showed that the odds of HE were fourfold higher for diabetic patients [14]. The pathophysiologic role diabetes plays in the development and progression of atherosclerosis, which in turn causes high blood pressure, through damaging and altering the walls of the small blood arteries, explains the observed association. Additionally, diabetes could damage the kidneys by causing scars, which causes water and salt retention, which in turn causes blood pressure to rise.

Limitations of the study

Since this study design is cross-sectional and relies on the revision of medical records, its conclusions may be compromised by the absence of some factors and the inability to capture others. The study design also hinders establishing a causal relationship between the independent and dependent variables. Underutilization of some standard patient assessment tools in hypertensive crisis patients, such as fundoscopy, might lead to missing patients with additional TODs like hypertensive retinopathy.

Conclusion

The study found that four out of ten hypertensive crisis patients had experienced hypertensive emergencies. Cerebrovascular injuries, followed by acute renal failure and cardiovascular diseases, were the most common types of hypertension-mediated TODs. New-onset hypertension and diabetes mellitus were independently associated with HE. Routine hypertension screening, preventive measures, and long-term management of hypertension should be employed to mitigate the increased burden of hypertensive emergencies observed in the study. Health care providers should recognize the risk factors and commonly affected organs in patients with HE to identify and manage patients promptly.
