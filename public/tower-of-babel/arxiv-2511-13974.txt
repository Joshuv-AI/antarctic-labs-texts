Quadrature for Singular Integrals over convex Polytopes
Johannes Tausch

Abstract
A new algorithm for the efficient numerical approximation of weakly singular integrals over convex polytopes is introduced. Such integrals appear in the Galerkin discretizations of integral equations and nonlocal partial differential equations. The polytope is decomposed into a number of convex hulls of a singular and regular face. This expresses the singularity in a single variable which is effectively handled by Gauss-Jacobi quadrature. The decomposition algorithm is applicable to general finite polytopes. The Cartesian product of two simplices and two cubes will be discussed as special cases and numerical examples will be presented to illustrate the convergence of the resulting quadrature scheme.



keywords

Numerical integration, singular integral, high dimensional integral,
Gauss-Jacobi quadrature, decomposition of polytopes

Introduction

This paper is concerned with the numerical evaluation of integrals of the form

\int_{P_{x}}\int_{P_{y}}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}.

(1)

Here
P_{x},P_{y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
are
convex polytopes (triangles, quadrilaterals, tetrahedra, cubes, prisms, etc),
and the kernel
k:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

is smooth when
\mathbf{x}\not=\mathbf{y}
but singular when
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}
.

Integrals of the type (
1
) arise in boundary
integral reformulations of elliptic PDEs
[
14
,
17
,
20
,
21
,
24
]
, parabolic and
hyperbolic PDE
[
16
,
19
]
and fractional
PDEs
[
1
,
7
,
9
]
.
Besides differential equations, integrals of the type
(
1
) also arise in the evaluation of the Riesz
minimal energy
[
18
,
13
]
and in
certain applications in quantum mechanics

[
11
]
.

We assume that
P_{x}
and
P_{y}
are conforming in the sense that either

P_{x}=P_{y}
or that the intersection
P_{x}\cap P_{y}
is a common
face. Because of the singularity of the integrand it is challenging to
find suitable quadrature methods, and is a topic of current research.

The original motivation to devise efficient quadrature schemes for
(
1
) came from Galerkin boundary element methods,
where
P_{x}
and
P_{y}
are panels in three dimensional space. For
the case of triangles and parallelograms Sauter and co-workers
constructed singularity removing transformations that map the four
dimensional unit cube to the Cartesian product
P_{x}\times P_{y}
, see

[
8
,
20
]
. Later, this methodology
was generalized to tetrahedra and parallelotopes in arbitrary dimensions
in a sequence of papers by Chernov, Petersdorff and Schwab, see

[
4
,
3
,
2
,
5
]
.

The construction in the aforementioned papers is specific to the type
of polytope, and is therefore limited to pairs of simplices and pairs
of parallelotopes. The primary goal of the present paper is develop a
framework to handle general convex and bounded polytopes. The
fundamental principle here is a simple algorithm that decomposes any
finite polytope into a collection of generalized pyramids. This
algorithm will then be applied to the Cartesian product

P_{x}\times P_{y}
which will result in a decomposition of convex hulls
of certain faces of
P_{x}
and
P_{y}
. The singularities in the
integrals over the convex hulls can be handled with Gauss-Jacobi
rules.

The reminder of this paper is organized as
follows. Section
2
provides some background material
on convex polytopes. Section
3
discusses
integration of singular functions over the convex hull of two faces.
The following two Sections
4
and
5

describe the pyramidal decomposition algorithm for general polytopes
and its application to the case of Cartesian products of two
polytopes. Section
6
presents an analysis for
simplices and cubes and compares the present method with the previously
published work for these special cases. Finally,
Section
7
presents a few numerical illustrations of
the method.

Convex Polytopes

Convex polytopes are a well studied topic in discrete geometry. The
standard references are
[
12
]
and
[
26
]
and
comprehensive overviews of the computational aspects of polytopes are
given in
[
10
]
and
[
15
]
. In this
section we collect some basic concepts that will be used later on.
The proofs for some of the less obvious statements will be provided.

2.1
Basic Concepts

There are two ways to
specify bounded convex polytopes. The first is to consider a polytope as the
intersection of half spaces bounded by affine hyperplanes. This leads
to a system of linear inequalities

P=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon A\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{b}\right\}

where the each row represents a half space. A point in
P
that
achieves equality in
d
independent rows is a vertex of
P
. A

j
-face is the subset of
P
that achieves equality in
d-j

independent rows of the hyperplane representation.

In the second description one starts with the vertices and specifies

P
as their convex hull. That is

P=[\mathbf{v}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{n}]:=\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}\colon\lambda_{i}\geq 0,\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}=1\right\}.

Any face of
P
is the convex hull of a subset
of the vertices of
P
. Faces are distinguished based on their
dimension. The 0-faces are the vertices, the 1-faces
are the edges and the faces with dimension one less than the polytope
are facets.

The affine hull of some face
F=[\mathbf{v}_{i_{0}},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{i_{k}}]
of

P
is the
hyperplane of smallest dimension that contains
F

\operatorname{aff}(F)=\left\{\sum_{j=0}^{k}\lambda_{j}\mathbf{v}_{i_{j}}:\,\sum_{j=0}^{k}\lambda_{j}=1\right\}.

For
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\operatorname{aff}(F)
the set

H_{F}=\operatorname{aff}(F)-\mathbf{x}_{0}

is a linear space, which is independent of the choice of
\mathbf{x}_{0}
. If

F
is a
j
-face then the dimension of
H_{F}
is
j
.

The face lattice is the set of all faces of a polytope which is
partially ordered by inclusion. It is represented by a
graph whose nodes correspond to faces and
edges represent the inclusion of a
(j-1)
-face in a
j
-face. Two
polytopes are combinatorially equivalent if they have isomorphic face
lattices.

2.2
Iterated Pyramids

Pyramids are
an important special type of polytopes, where all vertices
except for one are in the same affine hyperplane. The vertex that is
not in the hyperplane is called the apex. The convex hull of the other
vertices is the base and is one of the facets of the
pyramid. The notation for a pyramid with apex
\mathbf{v}_{0}
and base

B_{0}
is

P=\operatorname{pyr}(\mathbf{v}_{0},B_{0}):=\left\{(1-\lambda)\mathbf{v}_{0}+\lambda\mathbf{x}_{0}:\;\mathbf{x}_{0}\in B_{0},\;\lambda\in[0,1]\right\}.

If the base is also a pyramid,
i.e.,
B_{0}=\operatorname{pyr}(\mathbf{v}_{1},B_{1})
, then we use the notation

\operatorname{pyr}(\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2},B_{2})
to indicate such an iterated pyramid. By
recursion, one obtains a sequence of bases
B_{j}
and apices
\mathbf{v}_{j}

and the
k
-fold pyramid

P=\operatorname{pyr}(\mathbf{v}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{k},B_{k}).

as long as
\mathbf{v}_{j}\not\in\operatorname{aff}(B_{j})
,
j\in\{0,\dots,k\}
.
The
convex hull of the apices is a
k
-dimensional simplex, denoted by

A_{k}:=[\mathbf{v}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{k}]
.

If the final base of an
iterated pyramid is a vertex,
then
\operatorname{pyr}(\mathbf{v}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{k},\{\mathbf{v}_{k+1}\})
is the

k+1
-dimensional simplex with
vertices
\mathbf{v}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{k+1}
.

2.3
Convex Hull of two Polytopes

We now turn to the convex hull of two polytopes

A
and
B
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. It is defined as

\operatorname{conv}(A,B)=\Big\{(1-\lambda)\mathbf{x}_{A}+\lambda\mathbf{x}_{B}:\lambda\in[0,1],\;\mathbf{x}_{A}\in A,\;\mathbf{x}_{B}\in B\Big\}

(2)

While this concept can be defined for any two polytopes, we are only interested in
situations where for
\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{conv}(A,B)
the points
\mathbf{x}_{A}
and
\mathbf{x}_{B}

and the parameter
\lambda
are uniquely defined. This turns out to be
true if the following condition is met.

Assumption 2.1
.

\operatorname{aff}(A)\cap\operatorname{aff}(B)=\emptyset\quad\mbox{and}\quad H_{A}\cap H_{B}=\{\mathbf{0}\}

Lemma 1
.

If Assumption
2.1
holds, then
\mathbf{x}_{A}\in A
and

\mathbf{x}_{B}\in B

and
\lambda\in[0,1]
are unique.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{conv}(A,B)
such that

\mathbf{x}=(1-\lambda)\mathbf{x}_{A}+\lambda\mathbf{x}_{B}=(1-\mu)\mathbf{y}_{A}+\mu\mathbf{y}_{B}

(3)

with
\mathbf{x}_{A}
,
\mathbf{y}_{A}
in
A
,
\mathbf{x}_{B}
,
\mathbf{y}_{B}
in
B
, and
\lambda
,

\mu
in
[0,1]
. Assume first that
\lambda\not=\mu
. Rearranging
and dividing by
\lambda-\mu
reveals that the point

\frac{1-\mu}{\lambda-\mu}\mathbf{y}_{A}-\frac{1-\lambda}{\lambda-\mu}\mathbf{x}_{A}=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-\mu}\mathbf{x}_{B}-\frac{\mu}{\lambda-\mu}\mathbf{y}_{B}

is in
\operatorname{aff}(A)
and in
\operatorname{aff}(B)
since the coefficients on both
sides add up to unity. This contradicts the first part of Assumption
2.1

and thus
\lambda=\mu
. In the latter case we obtain from
(
3
) that

(1-\lambda)(\mathbf{y}_{A}-\mathbf{x}_{A})=\lambda(\mathbf{x}_{B}-\mathbf{y}_{B}).

Since the vectors
\mathbf{y}_{A}-\mathbf{x}_{A}
and
\mathbf{x}_{B}-\mathbf{y}_{B}
are in
H_{A}

and
H_{B}
, respectively, it follows from the second part of Assumption
2.1

that
\mathbf{x}_{A}=\mathbf{y}_{A}
and
\mathbf{x}_{B}=\mathbf{y}_{B}
and the assertion is shown.

We conclude the section with the following observation.

Lemma 2
.

If
A_{k}
is the convex hull of the apices
\{\mathbf{v}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{k}\}

of an iterated pyramid, then

\operatorname{pyr}(\mathbf{v}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{k},B_{k})=\operatorname{conv}(A_{k},B_{k}).

(4)

Further,
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
satisfy Assumption
2.1
.

Proof.

The statement is trivial for
k=0
. We use induction to show that it
holds for any
k
.
Since
B_{k-1}=\operatorname{pyr}(\mathbf{v}_{k},B_{k})
it follows from the definitions and
the induction assumption that

\operatorname{pyr}(\mathbf{v}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{k},B_{k})=\operatorname{pyr}(\mathbf{v}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{k-1},B_{k-1})=\operatorname{conv}(A_{k-1},B_{k-1})

To demonstrate that the latter set is equal to
\operatorname{conv}(A_{k},B_{k})
we need to
show that for all
\mathbf{a}_{k-1}\in A_{k-1}
,
\mathbf{b}_{k}\in B_{k}

(1-\lambda_{1})\mathbf{a}_{k-1}+\lambda_{1}\Big((1-\lambda_{2})\mathbf{v}_{k}+\lambda_{2}\mathbf{b}_{k}\Big)=(1-\mu_{1})\Big((1-\mu_{2})\mathbf{a}_{k-1}+\mu_{2}\mathbf{v}_{k}\Big)+\mu_{1}\mathbf{b}_{k}

holds for some
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
and
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\in[0,1]^{2}
. This is equivalent to the system

\displaystyle 1-\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=(1-\mu_{1})(1-\mu_{2})

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(1-\lambda_{2})

\displaystyle=(1-\mu_{1})\mu_{2}

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}.

Indeed, for
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in[0,1]^{2}
given, the solution
of the system is

\mu_{1}=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{2}=\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}{1-\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}

which is again in
[0,1]^{2}
. Vice versa, for
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\in[0,1]^{2}

\lambda_{1}=\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{2}=\frac{\mu_{1}}{\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}

will do the job. This proves the first assertion (
4
).

To show the second assertion, assume
\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{aff}(A_{k})\cap\operatorname{aff}(B_{k})
. Denote the vertices of
B_{k}
by
\mathbf{w}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{w}_{n}
, then

\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\mu_{i}\mathbf{w}_{i},

(5)

where
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}=\sum_{i}\mu_{i}=1
. Assume first that

\lambda_{k}\not=1
. Rearranging and
dividing by
1-\lambda_{k}
leads to

\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\lambda_{i}^{*}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\mu_{i}^{*}\mathbf{w}_{i}-\lambda_{k}^{*}\mathbf{v}_{k}.

(6)

where
\lambda_{i}^{*}=\lambda_{i}/(1-\lambda_{k})
and
\mu_{i}^{*}=\mu_{i}/(1-\lambda_{k})
.
The left hand side in (
6
) is an affine combination of
the vertices of
A_{k-1}
and the right hand hand side is an affine
combination of vertices of
B_{k-1}
. This contradicts the
induction assumption and thus we are left to check
\lambda_{k}=1
. In this case
it follows from (
5
) that
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{v}_{k}
and
\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{aff}(B_{k})
contradicting the assumption that the apex of the pyramid

B_{k-1}
is not in the affine plane of its base.

It remains to show that
H_{A_{k}}\cap H_{B_{k}}=\{0\}
. To that end,
consider
\mathbf{x}\in H_{A_{k}}\cap H_{B_{k}}
. Since
\mathbf{x}\in H_{A_{k}}
there are coefficients

\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{v}_{i}-\mathbf{v}_{0})+\lambda_{k}(\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{v}_{0}).

(7)

If
\lambda_{k}=0
then this implies that
\mathbf{x}\in H_{A_{k-1}}\cap H_{B_{k}}
. This is a subspace of
\mathbf{x}\in H_{A_{k-1}}\cap H_{B_{k-1}}
, which by induction assumption is trivial. Hence
\mathbf{x}=0
. If
\lambda_{k}\not=0
we can rearrange (
7
) as

\mathbf{v}_{k}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{v}_{0}-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{k}}(\mathbf{v}_{i}-\mathbf{v}_{0}).

Since
-\mathbf{x}/\lambda_{k}\in H_{B_{k}}=\operatorname{aff}(B_{k})-\mathbf{v}_{k}
it follows that
the left hand side is in
\operatorname{aff}(B_{k-1})
. On the other hand the right hand side
is in
\operatorname{aff}(A_{k-1})
which contradicts the induction assumption. Thus the
zero vector is the only vector in
H_{A_{k}}\cap H_{B_{k}}
.

2.4
Distance Formula

The distance of a point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
from
\operatorname{aff}(A)
is given by

\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf{x},A):=\min\limits_{\mathbf{a}\in\operatorname{aff}(A)}\left|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a}\right|=\left|\Pi_{A}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a}_{0})\right|.

Here,
\left|\cdot\right|
is the Euclidean norm and
\Pi_{A}
is the orthogonal projector into the subspace
H_{A}^{\perp}
,
and
\mathbf{a}_{0}
is an arbitrary point in
\operatorname{aff}(A)
.
If
\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{conv}(A,B)
then a simple calculation with
\mathbf{a}_{0}=\mathbf{x}_{A}

shows that

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf{x},A)

\displaystyle=\left|\Pi_{A}\Big((1-\lambda)\mathbf{x}_{A}+\lambda\mathbf{x}_{B}-\mathbf{x}_{A}\Big)\right|=\lambda\left|\Pi_{A}\Big(\mathbf{x}_{B}-\mathbf{x}_{A}\Big)\right|

(8)

\displaystyle=\lambda\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf{x}_{B},A).

Integrals over Faces and Convex Hulls

Integrals over a
j
-face can be carried out using an orthogonal basis
for the parameterization.
If the columns of the matrix
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times j}
are orthogonal
basis vectors of
H_{F}
and
\mathbf{v}\in\operatorname{aff}(F)
then

\int_{F}f(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}=\int_{F_{Q}}f(\mathbf{v}+Q{\boldsymbol{\xi}}))\,d{\boldsymbol{\xi}},

(9)

where the parameter space
F_{Q}\subset\mathbb{R}^{j}
is a polytope with
nonempty interior. If
F
is
parameterized by a basis
T
of
\operatorname{aff}(F)
then

\int_{F}f(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}=\int_{F_{T}}f(\mathbf{v}+T{\boldsymbol{\xi}}))\,d{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\,\det(R).

Here
T=QR
is the qr-factorization and
R\in\mathbb{R}^{j\times j}
is upper triangular.

We now turn to integrals over the convex hull of two polytopes
A
and

B
that satisfy Assumption
2.1
. The dimension of

\operatorname{conv}(A,B)
is
n=s+r+1
where
s=\dim(A)
and
r=\dim(B)
.
Further, if
\mathbf{x}_{A}=\mathbf{v}+T_{A}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
and
\mathbf{x}_{B}=\mathbf{w}+T_{B}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
are
parameterizations of
A
and
B
,
then the Jacobian of
\mathbf{x}=(1-\lambda)\mathbf{x}_{A}+\lambda\mathbf{x}_{B}
is

J=\Big[(1-\lambda)T_{A},\lambda T_{B},\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{v}+T_{B}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}-T_{A}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\Big]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}.

This matrix can be factored as

J=QR\begin{bmatrix}(1-\lambda)I_{s}&&\\
&\lambda I_{r}\\
&&1\end{bmatrix},

where
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
is orthogonal
R\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is
upper triangular.
Here only the last column of
R
depends on
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
, but
not its last entry. This follows from the fact that the last column of

Q
is in the orthogonal complement of the range of
[T_{A},T_{B}]
. Thus
the determinant of
R
is a constant. Reverting the parameterizations
of
A
and
B
one can see that

\int\limits_{\operatorname{conv}(A,B)}\!\!\!f(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}=\delta_{AB}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{A}\int_{B}f\big((1-\lambda)\mathbf{x}_{A}+\lambda\mathbf{x}_{B}\big)d\mathbf{x}_{B}d\mathbf{x}_{A}(1-\lambda)^{s}\lambda^{r}d\lambda\,

where

\delta_{AB}=\frac{\det(R)}{\det(R_{A})\det(R_{A})}

(10)

and
R_{A}
and
R_{B}
are the triangular factors in the
qr-factorizations of
T_{A}
and
T_{B}
. It is easy to verify that

\delta_{AB}
is independent of the choice of bases. The particular
form of this integral can be exploited when the integrand is singular
on the face
A
but smooth otherwise. Specifically, we assume that

Assumption 3.1
.

There is
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
with
\alpha<r+1
and a smooth function

such that

f(\mathbf{x})=g(\mathbf{x})\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf{x},S)^{-\alpha}.

From the distance formula (

) it follows that

\int\limits_{\operatorname{conv}(A,B)}\!\!\!f(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}=\\
\delta_{AB}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{A}\int_{B}g\big((1-\lambda)\mathbf{x}_{A}+\lambda\mathbf{x}_{B}\big)\operatorname{dist}^{-\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{B},A)\,d\mathbf{x}_{B}d\mathbf{x}_{A}(1-\lambda)^{s}\lambda^{r-\alpha}d\lambda.

(11)

Since
\operatorname{dist}(x_{B},A)>0
the

-integral is smooth and the singularity
appears only in the
\lambda
variable. To approximate (
11
)
by numerical integration, one can use quadrature rules for smooth
functions over the faces
A
and
B
. The singularity in

can be treated
effectively by using the Gauss-Jacobi quadrature rule for the weight

.

4
Pyramidal Decomposition Algorithm

The strategy to handle singular integrals over arbitrary polytopes is
to construct a decomposition of the polytope into convex hulls and
apply integration formula (
11
). To that end,
let
P=[\mathbf{v}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{n}]
be a convex polytope where the
integrand is singular on the vertices
\{\mathbf{v}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{k}\}
, where

k<n
. Further, the integrand satisfies Assumption
3.1
with
the the affine plane
S=\operatorname{aff}(\mathbf{v}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{k})
. For the following
discussion, we make the following assumption about the faces of the
polytope

Assumption 4.1
.

All faces
F
of
P
either have a singular vertex or satisfy

\operatorname{dist}(F,S)>0.

The assumption states that if
S
intersects with a face then the
intersection contains at least one vertex of
F
.
If the convex hull of all singular vertices is a
face of
P
, then it is easy to see that Assumption
4.1
is
satisfied. However, the assumption includes more general situations. An
example of a polytope that is singular in its interior and still
satisfies the assumption is shown in Figure
1
.

The basic building block of the pyramidal decomposition algorithm is
the simple observation that any polytope can be decomposed into
pyramids by selecting any vertex as the apex. To describe this fact
more formally, we denote by
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{v},P)
the set of facets of the
polytope
P
that do not contain the vertex
\mathbf{v}
. Then the following
lemma holds.

Lemma 3
.

If
\mathbf{v}
is a vertex of a finite convex polytope
P
then

P=\bigcup\limits_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{v},P)}\!\!\operatorname{pyr}(\mathbf{v},F),

(12)

which is a union with disjoint relative interiors.

Proof.

For a point
\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{relint}(P)
consider the line that contains both

\mathbf{x}
and the vertex
\mathbf{v}
. Since
P
is finite, the intersection of
this line with
P
is a line segment
[\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}]
, where
\mathbf{w}
is a
point on a facet
F
of
P
. If this facet contained the vertex
\mathbf{v}

then by convexity of
F
the entire line segment
[\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}]
would be
in
F
contradicting the assumption that
\mathbf{x}
is in the
interior. Hence
F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{v},P)
and
\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{pyr}(\mathbf{v},F)
. Taking the closure shows that the left hand side in (
12
)
is contained in the right hand side.
Since the opposite inclusion is obvious the equality in
(
12
) follows.

To show the second statement note that for two distinct facets

F,F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{v},P)

\operatorname{pyr}(\mathbf{v},F)\cap\operatorname{pyr}(\mathbf{v},F^{\prime})=\operatorname{pyr}(\mathbf{v},F\cap F^{\prime})
. This is a
lower dimensional face of both pyramids, hence their interiors are
disjoint.

4.1
Decomposition Algorithm

The strategy is to select a singular vertex of
P
and break up
P

into pyramids as in (
12
). If in this decomposition a base
has no further singular vertices, the decomposition is stopped. If a
base in (
12
) contains another singular vertex, then the
base will be split using (
12
) with the next singular
vertex as the apex. The process is repeated until all bases have no
singular vertex. The resulting recursive algorithm is described
in
1
. We summarize some obvious facts about
Algorithm
1
.

1.

The bases generated by the algorithm form a partially

which will be referred to as the pyramidal lattice

In every step of the pyramidal decomposition, the dimension of

5
Singular Kernel Integrals

We now turn to the main motivation of this work which is to construct
a suitable decomposition
for the integral (
1

the following assumption.

Assumption 5.1
.

There is
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and a smooth function

The power

The polytopes

and

are in

.

and

intersect in a common face.

This assumption includes the case that
P_{x}=P_{y}
.
The polytopes are the convex hull of their vertices

The domain of integration in (

are Cartesian products of

(\mathbf{v}_{k},\mathbf{v}_{k}),\quad k\in\{1,\dots s\},

Lemma 4
.

If

Consider the face

of

is empty then

. If

is nonempty then it is

and
F_{y}
. Then

\mathbf{v}
of

is a vertex of
F_{x}
and

.

is a singular vertex of

4.1
.

The following is an application of Lemma

Proof.

The first assertion follows from the fact that the facets of

and

For the second assertion note that

chosen according to the rule

\sigma_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if }F_{i}\in\{E_{2},E_{3}\},\\
0&\mbox{if }F_{i}\in\{S,T\}.\end{cases}

(20)

With this choice of apex the set
\mathcal{F}(F,\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},
) consists of
faces
F^{\prime}
of the following form

\exists i:\;F^{\prime}_{i}=\begin{cases}E_{2}\mbox{ or }E_{3}&\mbox{if }F_{i}=S,\\
\mathbf{v}_{2}&\mbox{if }F_{i}=E_{2},\\
\mathbf{v}_{3}&\mbox{if }F_{i}=E_{3},\\
G_{2}\mbox{ or }G_{3}&\mbox{if }F_{i}=T,\\
\end{cases}\quad\mbox{and}\quad F^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}=F_{i^{\prime}}\quad\mbox{for}\;i^{\prime}\not=i.

(21)

If in the above formula
F_{i}=S
then
F^{\prime}
is a singular face in

\mathcal{S}^{({\ell+1})}
. If
F_{i}=E_{2}
or
F_{i}=E_{3}
then
F^{\prime}

is nonsingular and in the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{({\ell+1})}_{1}=\Big\{F_{1}\times

\displaystyle\dots\times F_{d}\,:\,F_{i}\in\{S,E_{2},E_{3},\mathbf{v}_{2},\mathbf{v}_{3}\},\;i\leq k,\;F_{i^{\prime}}=T,\;i^{\prime}>k,

\displaystyle\mbox{ and }n_{V}(F)=1,\;n_{E}(F)=\ell-1\Big\}.

If in (
21
)
F_{i}=T
then
F^{\prime}
is
nonsingular and in the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{({\ell+1})}_{2}=\Big\{F_{1}\times

\displaystyle\dots\times F_{d}\,:\,F_{i}\in\{S,E_{2},E_{3}\},\;i\leq k,\;F_{i}\in\{T,G_{2},G_{3}\},\;i^{\prime}>k,

\displaystyle\mbox{ and }n_{G}(F)=1,\;n_{E}(F)=\ell\Big\}.

By induction, one sees that the singular faces in the pyramidal
lattice are the union of sets
\mathcal{S}^{({\ell})}
.
Moreover, the bases are the union of the sets
\mathcal{B}^{({\ell})}_{1}
and

\mathcal{B}^{({\ell})}_{2}
,
1\leq\ell\leq k
.
The cardinality of all bases in the pyramidal lattice can now be
determined with simple counting. We find that

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{B}_{1}

\displaystyle=k\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\binom{k-1}{\ell-1}2^{\ell}=2k3^{k-1},

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{B}_{2}

\displaystyle=2(d-k)\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\binom{k}{\ell-1}2^{\ell}=2(d-k)3^{k}.

Consider now
F\in\mathcal{B}^{({\ell})}_{1}\cup\mathcal{B}^{({\ell})}_{2}
. Denote by

{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{d})
the positions of the edges and
vertices, that is,

\sigma_{i}=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{ if }F_{i}\in\{S,T\},\\
1&\mbox{ if }F_{i}\in\{E_{2},E_{3},G_{2},G_{3},\mathbf{v}_{2},\mathbf{v}_{3}\}.\end{cases}

Then all paths of apices in the pyramidal lattice that lead from
P
to
F
are
of the form
\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{0}},\mathbf{v}_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\ell}}
,
where
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{j}=(\sigma_{j1},\dots\sigma_{jd})
,
\sigma_{ji}\in\{0,1\}
and

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{ji}=j,\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sigma_{j+1i}\geq\sigma_{ji}.

As described in Section
4.3
these vertices are a
triangulation of

A_{F}=I_{\sigma_{1}}\times\dots\times I_{\sigma_{d}},

(22)

where
I_{0}=\{(\mathbf{v}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{0})\}
and
I_{1}=[(\mathbf{v}_{0}\mathbf{v}_{0}),(\mathbf{v}_{1}\mathbf{v}_{1})]
.
This is a
\ell
-dimensional cube and a subset of (
18
).

The construction in this section proves the following result.

Theorem 8
.

The Cartesian product of the cubes

C_{x}=[0,1]^{d}
and
C_{y}=[0,1]^{k}\times[-1,0]^{d-k}
has the following
decomposition of convex hulls of singular and nonsingular faces.

C_{x}\times C_{y}=\bigcup_{\ell=2}^{k+1}\bigcup_{F\in\mathcal{B}^{({\ell})}_{1}}\operatorname{conv}(A_{F},F)\quad\cup\;\quad\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{k+1}\bigcup_{F\in\mathcal{B}^{({\ell})}_{2}}\operatorname{conv}(A_{F},F).

The total number of terms is
(6d-4k)3^{k-1}
.

7
Numerical Examples

The method has been implemented for the case of two simplices of
arbitrary dimension with any number of common vertices. As discussed
in Section
6.1
,
the faces
A_{F}
,
F_{x}
and
F_{y}
in the integrals of
Theorem
6
are all simplices of various dimensions. If

T=[\mathbf{v}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{n}]
is such a simplex then

\mathbf{x}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})=\mathbf{v}_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{v}_{k-1})\hat{x}_{k}

is a parameterization
\mathbf{x}:\hat{T}_{n}\to T
where
\hat{T}_{n}
is the

n
-dimensional standard simplex

\hat{T}_{n}=\{\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:0\leq\hat{x}_{n}\leq\dots\leq\hat{x}_{1}\leq 1\}.

We consider two methods to construct quadrature rules for
\hat{T}_{n}
. The first is based on the well known Duffy transform, which maps
the unit cube
[0,1]^{n}
to
\hat{T}_{n}
via

\hat{\mathbf{x}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=(\xi_{1},\,\xi_{1}\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{1}\xi_{2}\cdots\xi_{n}).

The Jacobian of this transformation is
J({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\xi_{1}^{n-1}\xi_{2}^{n-2}\cdots\xi_{n-1}
.
The transformed integral is approximated by
a tensor product Gauss-Jacobi rule where the
J({\boldsymbol{\xi}})
is included in
with the weight function. This leads to

\int\limits_{\hat{T}_{n}}\varphi(\hat{\mathbf{x}})\,d\hat{\mathbf{x}}\approx\sum_{q_{1}=1}^{p}\cdots\sum_{q_{n}=1}^{p}\varphi\left(\xi_{1q_{1}},\,\xi_{1q_{1}}\xi_{2q_{2}},\dots,\xi_{1q_{1}}\cdots\xi_{nq_{n}}\right)w_{1q_{1}}\cdots w_{nq_{n}}

(23)

where
\xi_{jq}
and
w_{jq}
are the nodes and weights of the
Gauss-Jacobi rule for the weight function
w_{j}(\xi_{j})=\xi_{j}^{n-j}
.
The tensor product form of (
23
) implies that the quadrature
rule is exact for monomials of the form
\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}
, with

\alpha_{i}\leq 2p-1
, which is more than what is necessary to integrate
multivariate polynomials of degree
2p-1
exactly.

Thus the tensor product rule suboptimal in the number of nodes, and
accumulates quadrature points near the origin. To obtain rules with
fewer nodes and better node distributions one has to solve a
polynomial system for the nodes
\mathbf{x}_{k}\in\hat{T}_{n}
and weights

w_{q}>0
such that the rule

\int\limits_{\hat{T}_{n}}\varphi(\hat{\mathbf{x}})\,d\hat{\mathbf{x}}\approx\sum_{q=1}^{N_{q}}\varphi\left(\mathbf{x}_{q}\right)w_{q}

(24)

is exact for all polynomials up to a given degree. This is known as
generalized Gauss quadrature. Except for some special cases it is
unknown what the least number of nodes is
[
6
]
. The tensor
product rule is exact, but has more nodes than the number of
conditions in (
24
) suggests. More efficient rules can be
obtained by a elimination procedure
[
25
]
. This
process iteratively eliminates nodes and applies a nonlinear solver to
satisfy the exactness condition for the reduced rule. In our numerical
experiments we have used the rules that were generated by
implementation in
[
22
]
which is described
in
[
23
]
.

5
displays the error versus the degree of

1
) is six and ten, respectively. In
Assumption
5.1
, we set
g(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\exp(\sum_{i}x_{i}+y_{i})

and
\alpha=1
. One can see that in all cases the error decays at
the same exponential rate. The tensor product for the odd order
2p+1

is identical to order
2p
, which explains the staircase behavior in
the plot. The generalized Gauss rules for even orders appear to give

The reference value

8
Conclusions

The decomposition algorithm combined with the
Gauss-Jacobi quadrature for the resulting convex hulls is a simple
method to construct effective quadrature rules for general polytopes
with singular functions. Previous constructions are limited to special
cases. The exponential convergence of the quadrature for analytic
kernels can be explained by approximation properties of multivariate
polynomials. However, a more thorough analysis is still needed, for
instance, to optimize the quadrature of the iterated integrals of the
convex hulls and to handle adaptivity for polytopes with poor shape
quality.
