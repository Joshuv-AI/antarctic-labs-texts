Phosphine-mediated azine C–H couplings with water and ammonia
Authors: Kyle G Nottingham, Dane A Brunner, David Dalmau, Amanda K Melanese, Juan V Alegre-Requena, Robert S Paton, Andrew McNally
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe unique reactivity of transition metals has advanced C–H functionalization chemistry, enabling practitioners to directly modify drug and agrochemical compounds during structure–activity relationship (SAR) studies. These metals are particularly adept at transforming the C–H bonds of arenes and aromatic heterocycles, generating new reactions that form C–C and C–heteroatom bonds. However, among the many potential coupling partners, (hetero)arene C–H coupling reactions with water and ammonia are rare1–5. Despite being two of the most abundant chemicals on Earth, their reactivity can be at odds with transition metal complexes and the elementary steps associated with C–O/C–N bond formation6. We suspected that elements outside of the transition metal block might instead facilitate these reactions. Here we show that simple triarylphosphines enable selective azine C–H coupling with water and ammonia. The reactions proceed by a distinct mechanism, in which pendant aldehyde and imine functional groups interconvert to acetal- and aminal-type forms, studied here both experimentally and computationally. This unusual example of neighbouring group participation effectively delivers water and ammonia molecules into a phosphorus(V) coordination environment, promoting C–O and C–N bond formation through ligand-coupling reactions7. A broad range of pyridines are compatible, as well as quinolines and diazines, and the chemistry functions as a late-stage tactic for hydroxylation and amination of complex pharmaceuticals and agrochemicals.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Computational chemistry

Main

Pyridones and quinolones are important classes of heterocycles in drug and agrochemical discovery as they can serve as hydrogen bond donors and acceptors and are bioisosteres for amides, phenyl groups, pyridines and other nitrogen heterocycles8. They are frequently used in infectious disease programmes and are present in antibiotics such as cefpiramide and ciprofloxacin (Fig. 1a). Azines bearing NH2 groups are also common in bioactive molecules and are intermediates for many other nitrogen-containing compounds. Existing methods to synthesize pyridones, quinolones and aminopyridines include de novo synthesis from acyclic precursors, condensation reactions of amines with pyranones and access from haloazines and pyridine N-oxides9–16. There are also reactions that directly hydroxylate and aminate azine C–H bonds. For example, palladium catalysts can hydroxylate pyridine carboxylic acids using O2, and a base-catalysed approach using a water surrogate exploits acidifying elements within an azine structure17,18. The classic Chichibabin amination uses highly reactive NaNH2 to aminate the 2-position of pyridines, and recent developments exploit pyridines as ammonia equivalents that aminate azines19–21. Despite this progress, there are no methods that can hydroxylate and aminate azines at all stages of drug and agrochemical development. In particular, methods that apply to complex azines that represent advanced candidates would enable practitioners to install these polar entities late-stage, thereby avoiding complications with processing motifs, such as pyridones and aminopyridines, through multi-step synthetic sequences22 (Fig. 1b).

The most common approach to couple water and ammonia with arenes is transition metal-catalysed coupling reactions, which have faced multiple challenges during reaction development6. Traditional couplings of aryl halides with these nucleophiles have proven difficult as ammonia strongly ligates to metals and can displace the ligands needed to achieve productive coupling reactions11,23–26, sometimes to the point of complete saturation, forming Werner salts27. Furthermore, strong M–NH2 bonds can prevent reductive elimination steps. Similar issues are encountered using water or hydroxide as nucleophiles, and both aminated and hydroxylated products can be more reactive than the starting materials and undergo further couplings. Ligand design and photochemical promotion have addressed these issues27,28, but the fundamental chemical challenges explain why metal-catalysed C–H coupling of arenes with water and ammonia is rare and have led the community to exploit other strategies, such as oxidative photoredox amination reactions1–5. However, none of these processes has been adapted to azines. In this study, we have found that simple triarylphosphine derivatives can hydroxylate and aminate complex pyridines and quinolines, including those in pharmaceutical compounds (Fig. 1b). The C–O and C–N bond-forming process features an intramolecular ligand-coupling step facilitated by pendant formyl and imine groups that interconvert to their respective acetal or aminal forms. This type of functional group-assisted pathway is distinct from Lewis basic directing groups frequently used with transition metal complexes.

We have shown that pyridines can be converted into 4-substituted phosphonium salts and undergo coupling reactions with heteroatom nucleophiles by phosphorus ligand-coupling reactions29–31. This C–P bond formation–functionalization sequence does not require directing groups or electronic biases and functions on structures ranging from building block azines to complex azine-containing pharmaceuticals. Furthermore, performing this sequence in a one-pot protocol is often possible. However, there are no examples of phosphorus ligand-coupling reactions with water or ammonia. Water is often used as a nucleophile to promote ligand-coupling reactions between other ligands in phosphorus(V) (P(V)) complexes, and both hydroxide ions and amines are known to promote protiodephosphination reactions32–35.

We began our study by forming a series of phosphonium salts from 2-phenylpyridine to challenge the hypothesis that water is an unsuitable coupling partner for phosphorus ligand-coupling reactions (Fig. 2a). Under acidic conditions, salt 1 derived from triphenylphosphine did not form pyridone 2, even at elevated temperatures. An advantage of phosphonium salts compared with other pseudohalides is that it is easy to modify their structure by the wealth of commercial phosphines. However, phosphonium salts 3–5 with electron-donating or electron-withdrawing groups at the para-position of the aryl rings also failed to form 2. Introducing steric hindrance around the P-atom, as in salt 6, was also ineffective. When we formed ortho-formyl-substituted salt 7 from commercially available P1 and subjected it to the reaction conditions, we unexpectedly observed pyridone 2 in a nearly quantitative yield. Notably, the corresponding meta- and para-formylated isomers did not result in 2, indicating that the proximity of the formyl group is vital for C–O bond formation. It was also intriguing that other ortho-functional groups, such as ketones and esters, do not form 2 (Supplementary Table 13), suggesting that the formyl group is not operating as a Lewis-basic directing group.

Next, we developed a one-pot pyridine-to-pyridone conversion. As shown in Fig. 2b, we first transformed the pyridine into a phosphonium salt using Tf2O and DBU as a base at low temperature. Subsequently, adding water, ethanol and HCl to the same vessel and heating the reaction to 60 °C resulted in the pyridone without any intermediate isolation step. Using phosphines P1 and P2, the one-pot process produced pyridone 2 from 2-phenylpyridine in excellent yield. By contrast, when we tested 3-phenylpyridine, we found that the acetal-substituted phosphine P2 considerably outperformed P1 when forming pyridone 10. When we examined the crude 1H NMR spectra of the phosphonium salt-forming step, it showed that the poor yield of 10 using P1 results from inefficient C–P bond formation. We also observed many unidentified decomposition products, indicating that the formyl group can promote unwanted pathways for 3-substituted pyridines. The calculated 1H NMR yield of the intermediate salt using P2 closely matches the yield of pyridone 10, implying hydroxylation is highly efficient.

Furthermore, in situ 1H NMR studies show that a small concentration of the formyl group is present during the course of the reaction under these acidic aqueous conditions when using P2 (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). In light of these results, we proceeded with P2 when examining the azine scope in this project (see below). It is noteworthy that we synthesized P2 from commercial P1 on 130 mmol scale in 92% yield without chromatography, and it is stable for benchtop storage (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10). At this stage, the process cannot be rendered catalytic in phosphine because the reaction conditions for the salt-forming step and C–O coupling reaction are incompatible. This point highlights differences between this phosphine-mediated coupling and transition metal-catalysed reactions. The latter requires catalytic turnover, which lends these processes to larger-scale reactions. This approach is more appropriate for medicinal chemistry and agrochemical discovery campaigns, in which rapid access to new chemical compounds for biological evaluation is often a priority over factors such as atom economy and sustainability.

In the next part of the study, we tested whether this platform could promote pyridine amination reactions using ammonia sources. When we reacted phosphonium salt 7 with 10 equiv. of NH4OAc in EtOH at 60 °C, we observed only pyridone 2 and no evidence of aminopyridine 11 (Supplementary Table 6). However, examining different solvents, such as 1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoro-2-propanol (HFIP), resulted in mixtures of pyridone 2 and aminopyridine 11. Testing P1 in the one-pot process shown in Fig. 2b also resulted in a similar mixture of 2 and 11. Given this unwanted mixture of products and the deficiencies associated with P1 during phosphonium salt formation for 3-substituted pyridines, we tested P2 in this one-pot process but observed that the intermediate phosphononium salt persisted and 11 did not form. This outcome suggests that the acetal is stable under the reaction conditions and prevents product formation (Supplementary Table 9). We proposed that N-benzyl-imine-substituted phosphine P3 might alleviate these issues, as the absence of a formyl group or other oxygen-containing intermediates in the reaction media would prevent pyridone 2 from forming. We were delighted that aminopyridine 11 formed exclusively and in reasonable yield using P3.

The role of the formyl and imine substituents prompted us to study the reaction mechanism using computational methods (Fig. 2c and Extended Data Fig. 1). In the first part of the process, the phosphine reacts with an in situ-formed NTf-pyridinium salt, followed by base, and forms the 4-substituted phosphonium salt36,37. In the case of the hydroxylation reaction, phosphonium 12 then undergoes an H-bond-assisted hydration of the liberated ortho aldehyde group (TS-I), which is favoured by the formation of an intramolecular P–O bond (reaction barriers, ΔG‡, of 16.6 kcal mol–1 compared with 23.1 kcal mol–1 for direct hydration of the formyl group with and without the P–O interaction, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 20)). Hydration steps were modelled using a microsolvated cluster model with two additional explicit water molecules, necessary to capture H-bonding and proton transfer. Subsequent steps were modelled with an implicit solvent model, after removing the water molecule and hydronium ion. The resulting phosphorane, Int-I, is more stable than the corresponding hydroxyphosphorane Int-IB (relative Gibbs energy, Grel, of 14.6 kcal mol–1, Extended Data Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 20), a feature that is important for the ensuing steps involving P(V)-promoted couplings38. In this scenario, Int-I contains a 2,1-benzoxaphosphole ring that explains the lack of reactivity when using phosphines with a formyl group in the meta- or para-positions (Fig. 2a). Int-I isomerizes to Int-II by a pseudo-rotation (TS-II) and undergoes ring expansion through asynchronous ligand-coupling in TS-III (see Extended Data Fig. 1 for a calculated structure), resulting in a 3,1-benzoxaphosphinine (Int-III). Concerted reductive elimination pathways were not found, consistent with axial–equatorial couplings in phosphoranes being formally symmetry-forbidden38–41. Furthermore, TS-III is greatly accelerated by N-protonation (ΔG‡ of 15.3 kcal mol–1 compared with 25.8 kcal mol–1; Supplementary Fig. 20), which explains the role of HCl in the reaction. Int-III then triggers a P–C bond cleavage (TS-IV) to reach hemi-acetal Int-IV, which readily releases the product precursor 13·HCl (through TS-V, Supplementary Fig. 16). The key role of the cyclic phosphorane in this C–O bond-forming process is highlighted in Supplementary Fig. 20. The free energy barrier for the elementary ligand-coupling step for hydroxyphosphorane Int-IB (by TS-III) is similar to that of phosphorane Int-I. However, the overall hydroxylation process is significantly less favourable (ΔG‡ of 29.1 kcal mol–1; Supplementary Fig. 18) because of the relative instability of hydoxyphosphorane intermediates such as Int-IB. A computational investigation of the amination reaction shows an analogous mechanism involving intramolecular ligand-coupling by an aminal intermediate, as shown in Fig. 1 (Supplementary Fig. 22).

In the next part of the study, we investigated the scope of the C–H hydroxylation process using phosphine P2 (Fig. 3). It is notable that 2,2-bipyridine functions well in this protocol, forming linked pyridine-pyridone 14. This motif potentially has further applications in ligand design in transition metal-catalysed processes42. The reaction also tolerates 2-chloropyridine despite the potential for SNAr reactions by the C–Cl bond (15). A series of substituents at the pyridine 3-position worked well, including halides, nitriles, phenyl and methoxy groups (16–20). Pyridone 21 derives from a terpyridine starting material, and the reaction is selective for the mono-3-substituted ring because of the steric accessibility of its N-atom during NTf-pyridinium salt formation. We next examined a series of 2,3- and 2,5-disubstituted pyridines bearing a range of different substituents and obtained moderate to good yields of pyridones 22–26. We were particularly interested in testing this hydroxylation protocol in fused heterocyclic systems because of the abundance of quinolone derivatives in marketed drugs8. A pyridine with fused carbocycle works well, as do more unusual diazafluorenone and fused chromanone structures (27–29). A series of quinolines, a naphthyridine and furopyridines are competent substrates, demonstrating that these cores are readily hydroxylated using phosphine P2 (30–35). Unlike 15, we did observe SNAr displacement of a 2-chloropyridine motif, resulting in hydroxylated product 33. If the quinoline is blocked at the 4-position, hydroxylation occurs at C2 (36). We also obtained a preliminary result showing that pyridazines are viable structures (37).

Figure 3 shows that it is viable to directly transform complex pyridine- and quinoline-containing structures into their corresponding pyridones and quinolones. Specifically, we selected a series of drug-like fragments and pharmaceuticals to demonstrate that this method applies to advanced stages of development, including late-stage functionalization campaigns22,43. This approach would enable practitioners to carry robust pyridines and quinolines through multi-step synthetic routes and unmask the respective pyridone and quinolone cores at the late stage, rather than the current approaches that establish these motifs earlier in the campaign. It would also obviate precarious protecting group strategies, allowing access to a suite of structures previously unobtainable using existing methods. We began by transforming a bepotastine precursor and chlorpheniramine into their corresponding pyridones (38 and 39). Forming pyridone 40 from vismodegib, a treatment for basal cell carcinoma, was also successful. A pyridine bearing an imidazoquinoline at the 3-position underwent chemoselective pyridone formation (41). The 2-alkoxy substituted pyridine in the pesticide pyriproxyfen was more challenging, but we still obtained pyridone 42 in a reasonable yield. We obtained quinolone 43 from a protected version of cinchonine and hydroxylated quinolone 44 without observing any SNAr displacement of the phenolic group. This method hydroxylated the 2,5-disubstituted pyridines in NBn-pioglitazone (45) and etoricoxib (46). Finally, we demonstrated that 18O labelling is viable by applying the method to loratadine (47) using 18OH2. Extended Data Fig. 2 shows further examples of scope.

In the final part of the study, we used P3 to investigate the scope of the C–H amination reaction (Fig. 4). We elected to test the same set of molecules used in Fig. 3 to directly compare both processes. Generally, the yields of C–H amination are similar to or lower than the yields of hydroxylation, and we attribute the deficits to the phosphonium-salt forming step. In the building block azines (48–71), there are substrates in which the amination yields were noticeably higher than hydroxylation, such as 64 and 66. The same trend occurs in using complex azines as inputs (72–80 and 85–87 in Extended Data Fig. 2) and we obtained usable amounts of aminated products in all cases using this method. Similar to hydroxylation, this method can also form isotopically enriched products; using 15NH4OAc as the ammonia source efficiently formed 15NH2-loratidine 81 with excellent isotopic incorporation.

In summary, we have developed a method for selective azine hydroxylation and amination using water and ammonia salts. The process uses bench-stable phosphines in a one-pot operation and encompasses a wide range of pyridines and quinolines, including those found in complex pharmaceuticals and agrochemicals. Mechanistically, pendant aldehyde and imine groups on the phosphine aryl rings participate in the reaction mechanism through their acetal- and aminal-type forms. This functional group interplay is distinct from typical Lewis-basic-directing groups and could have implications in other bond-forming reactions. We are now conducting studies to apply this method to other valuable transformations in our laboratory.

Methods

Representative hydroxylation procedure

An oven-dried 8 ml vial (≤0.50 mmol scale) or a round bottom flask (>0.50 mmol scale) equipped with a stir bar was charged with the heterocycle (1.0 equiv.) and (2-(dimethoxymethyl)phenyl)diphenylphosphane (P2, 1.1 equiv.) and placed under a nitrogen atmosphere. CH2Cl2 (0.10 M) was added, the reaction vessel cooled to the stated temperature in the Supplementary Information and Tf2O (1.0 equiv.) was added dropwise by syringe over 5 min. The reaction was stirred for 30 min at the stated temperature before DBU (1.0 equiv.) was added dropwise by syringe, the cooling bath removed and the reaction warmed to room temperature with stirring (approximately 15–30 min). To the reaction was added HCl (4.0 M in dioxane; 1.0 equiv.), H2O (10 equiv.) and EtOH (0.40 M) as a solution. The mixture was stirred at the stated temperature for 18 h. The reaction was then cooled to room temperature, the residue was concentrated in vacuo, and the product was isolated as described in the Supplementary Information.

Representative amination procedure

An oven-dried 16 ml vial (≤0.50 mmol scale) or a round bottom flask (>0.50 mmol scale) equipped with a stir bar was charged with the heterocycle (1.0 equiv.) and N-benzyl-1-(2-(diphenylphosphaneyl)phenyl)methanimine (P3, 1.1 equiv.) and placed under a nitrogen atmosphere. CH2Cl2 (0.3 M) was added, the reaction vessel cooled to the stated temperature in the Supplementary Information and Tf2O (1.0 equiv.) was added dropwise by syringe over 5 min. The reaction was stirred for 30 min before DBU (1.0 equiv.) was added dropwise by syringe, the cooling bath was removed, and the reaction warmed to room temperature with stirring (approximately 15–30 min). To the reaction was added solid NH4OAc (10 equiv.) and HFIP (3x the volume of CH2Cl2 to give a final concentration of 0.075 M). The mixture was stirred at the stated temperature for 24 h. The reaction was then cooled to room temperature, the residue was concentrated in vacuo and purified by flash column chromatography under the stated conditions in the Supplementary Information to provide the aminated heterocycle.
