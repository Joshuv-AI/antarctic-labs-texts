Explicit desingularisation of Kummer surfaces in characteristic two via specialisation Thanks: Website: https://alvarogohe.github.io
Alvaro Gonzalez-Hernandez

Abstract
. We study the birational geometry of the Kummer surfaces associated to the Jacobian varieties of genus two curves, with a particular focus on fields of characteristic two. In order to do so, we explicitly compute a projective embedding of the Jacobian of a general genus two curve and, from this, we construct its associated Kummer surface. This explicit construction produces a model for desingularised Kummer surfaces over any field of characteristic not two, and specialising these equations to characteristic two provides a model of a partial desingularisation. Adapting the classic description of the Picard lattice in terms of tropes, we also describe how to explicitly find completely desingularised models of Kummer surfaces whenever the p p -rank is not zero. In the final section of this paper, we compute an example of a Kummer surface with everywhere good reduction over a quadratic number field, and draw connections between the models we computed and a criterion that determines when a Kummer surface has good reduction at two.

1.
Introduction

Kummer surfaces are quotients of abelian surfaces by the involution that sends any point to its inverse with respect to the group law on the surface. They are one of the most classical examples of K3 surfaces, and they have been studied extensively, due to their remarkable number of singular points and their deep connections with the geometry of abelian varieties
[
33
]
.

In this article, we study the geometry of Kummer surfaces associated with Jacobians of genus two curves. In this setting, one can always obtain explicit equations for the Kummer surface, realised as a singular quartic surface in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
. If the characteristic of the field of definition is not two, the associated quartic model has sixteen nodes corresponding to the
2
-torsion points
[
6
]
. An explicit model of the desingularisation is known: it arises as the intersection of three quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{5}
, and this model has connections with the computation of explicit equations of the Jacobian variety as the intersection of
72
quadrics inside
\mathbb{P}^{15}
(Section
2
).

Outside the realm of geometry, one motivation to study Kummer surfaces comes from cryptography. In addition to elliptic curve cryptography, there has been a recent interest in studying cryptographic protocols that involve abelian varieties of higher dimension, for instance, in isogeny-based protocols like the Supersingular Isogeny Diffie-Hellman (SIDH)
[
7
,
8
]
. However, many of these protocols do not work in characteristic two, as in that case, Kummer surfaces behave quite differently
[
22
]
. This is because in characteristic two the
2
-torsion of the abelian surface is a subgroup of
(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{2}
, and its associated Kummer surface therefore has fewer singular points, but of higher complexity (Section
3
).

As in the characteristic zero case, there is a way to construct an explicit model for the Kummer surface associated to the Jacobian of a genus two curve as a quartic in
\mathbb{P}^{3}

[
29
]
. However, following this construction does not generate a smooth model of the desingularisation of this quartic as the intersection of three quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{5}
. In principle, this could suggest that over a field of characteristic two, Jacobians of genus two curves and Kummer surfaces behave completely differently compared to how they behave over a field of any other characteristic.

Our main result demonstrates that, despite these differences, the classical constructions can be adapted to work in characteristic two.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a genus-two curve over a perfect field
k
of characteristic two. Then, its Jacobian variety
\Jacc
admits an embedding as the intersection of
72
quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{15}
and we provide explicit equations for this embedding. Furthermore, we computed a projective embedding for a partial desingularisation of the Kummer surface associated to
\Jacc
as the complete intersection of three quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{5}
.

These two results are made precise in Theorems
3.1
and
4.1
. As we shall see, both embeddings arise naturally by specialising suitable models from characteristic zero (Section
4
).

Recently, Katsura and Kondō
[
21
]
used the theory of quadric line complexes to also describe equations for partial desingularisations of Kummer surfaces as the intersection of quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{5}
. Through different methods, we extend their results by showing that simpler models for these equations can always be computed over the field of definition of the curve (Section
5
).

In characteristic zero, there are sixteen special curves called tropes going through the singular points of a Kummer surface, and we will see how the specialisation of these curves provides a natural way to study the desingularisation of Kummer surfaces in characteristic two (Section
6
).

This general theme of studying Kummer surfaces in positive characteristic from the reduction of a model in characteristic zero will play an even bigger role in Section
7
of the paper.

There is a well-known result of Fontaine
[
17
]
(see also Abrashkin
[
1
]
) which asserts that there does not exist any abelian scheme over
\mathbb{Z}
and, as a consequence of this, there cannot exist abelian varieties defined over
\mathbb{Q}
with everywhere good reduction. In a similar fashion, a lesser-known result, also due to Abrashkin
[
2
]
and Fontaine
[
18
]
independently, shows that there cannot exist K3 surfaces defined over the rationals that have everywhere good reduction.

Since Tate provided in the late sixties one of the first examples of elliptic curves with good reduction everywhere, the curve
E\,/\,\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{29})
defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}:y^{2}+xy+\big(\tfrac{5+\sqrt{29}}{2}\big)^{2}y=x^{3},

many different techniques and methods have been developed in order to find elliptic curves with everywhere good reduction over number fields. In the case of abelian surfaces, it is relevant the work of Dembélé and Kumar
[
10
]
, Dembélé
[
11
]
, and Dąbrowski and Sadek
[
9
]
who all found explicit examples defined over quadratic fields of genus two curves whose Jacobians have everywhere good reduction over a quadratic number field.

After seeing that the question has a positive answer for abelian surfaces, one would naturally ask if it is then possible to find examples of K3 surfaces with everywhere good reduction over a number field. In Section
7
, we will give a positive answer to this question by proving the following:

Theorem 1.2
.

There exist examples of Kummer surfaces with everywhere good reduction over a number field.

More concretely, we construct an explicit example of a Kummer surface with everywhere good reduction over a quadratic field. To check that the Kummer surface has good reduction at all primes, we apply a criterion of Lazda and Skorobogatov
[
25
]
to study the reduction at two of an abelian surface with good reduction at all places, which is defined over a quadratic number field. This criterion involves studying the action of the absolute Galois group of the base field on the
2
-torsion points, and sheds light on the conditions that have to be met for a smooth model of a Kummer surface to also reduce to a smooth surface modulo two.

This paper comes with code that supports all the calculations and allows us to compute explicit equations for all the varieties that have been described. The latest updates of this code can be found
here
, whereas the version of the code at the time of publication can be found
here
.

preserved by the Galois group

is defined over the field of definition of

The tropes for these special examples have been computed in

Consider the blow-up

is a line in

\displaystyle E_{O}\cdot\hat{T}_{i}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle E_{O}\cdot\hat{T}_{ijk}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle E_{ij}\cdot\hat{T}_{i}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle E_{ij}\cdot\hat{T}_{k}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle E_{ij}\cdot\hat{T}_{ijk}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle E_{ij}\cdot\hat{T}_{ikl}

\displaystyle=0.

The translation
\tau_{ij}
also acts on the exceptional divisors by the rules

\displaystyle\tau_{ij}(E_{O})

\displaystyle=E_{ij},

\displaystyle\tau_{ij}(E_{ij})

\displaystyle=E_{O},

\displaystyle\tau_{ij}(E_{ik})

\displaystyle=E_{jk},

\displaystyle\tau_{ij}(E_{kl})

\displaystyle=E_{mn},

where

Let

be the pull-back of a hyperplane section of

under the blow-up map. Then, in

, we can express the tropes in terms of

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{i}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}(H-E_{O}-E_{ij}-E_{ik}-E_{il}-E_{im}-E_{in}),

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{ijk}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}(H-E_{ij}-E_{ik}-E_{il}-E_{lm}-E_{ln}-E_{mn}).

The Picard number of a Kummer surface is always
\rho+16
where

is the Picard number of the abelian surface of which it is the quotient. It is therefore possible to prove that, for a sufficiently general Kummer surface,

is generated over

by the classes of the sixteen exceptional curves, the sixteen tropes, and the hyperplane section

]

Kummer surfaces over fields of characteristic two

Let
\mathcal{C}

•

•

(also in the sense of Artin).

•

In the supersingular case,

has one elliptic singularity of type
\mathchoice{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\makebox[10.44444pt]{\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\put(0.0,0.0){\circle{0.0}}}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\put(-2.5,0.0){$\displaystyle 4$}}}}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\makebox[10.44444pt]{\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\put(0.0,0.0){\circle{0.0}}}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\put(-2.5,0.0){$\textstyle 4$}}}}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\makebox[8.51111pt]{\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\put(0.0,0.0){\circle{0.0}}}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\put(-1.99306,0.0){$\scriptstyle 4$}}}}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\makebox[7.40283pt]{\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\put(0.0,0.0){\oval(0.0,0.0)}}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\put(-1.70142,0.0){$\scriptscriptstyle 4$}}}}}^{1}_{\,0,1}

(in which case, the Kummer surface associated to

For a general abelian surface

has an elliptic double singularity of type
\mathchoice{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\makebox[14.00003pt]{\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\put(0.0,0.0){\oval(0.0,0.0)}}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\put(-5.00002,0.0){$\displaystyle 19$}}}}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\makebox[14.00003pt]{\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\put(0.0,0.0){\oval(0.0,0.0)}}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\put(-5.00002,0.0){$\textstyle 19$}}}}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\makebox[11.97226pt]{\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\put(0.0,0.0){\oval(0.0,0.0)}}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\put(-3.98613,0.0){$\scriptstyle 19$}}}}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\makebox[10.80566pt]{\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\put(0.0,0.0){\oval(0.0,0.0)}}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\put(-3.40283,0.0){$\scriptscriptstyle 19$}}}}}_{\,0}
. This situation cannot happen for Kummer surfaces associated to Jacobians of curves of genus two

In order to understand the resolution of singularities in these cases, Schröer observed that blowing up the schematic image of

inside

generated a crepant partial resolution of the singularities

.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a genus-two curve over a perfect field of characteristic two, and let

. Inside the subspace
\mathcal{L}(2(\Theta_{+}+\Theta_{-}))(-2\Jacc[2])
of sections vanishing on the

is ordinary,

•

is almost ordinary,

•

in Laufer’s notation

As mentioned in the introduction, Katsura and Kondō used the theory of line complexes to obtain similar results for Kummer surfaces that do not necessarily come from the Jacobians of genus two curves

The advantages of the method described in this article are that the scheme models that have been computed are defined over the field of definition of the curve, which is not always the case for the models of Katsura and Kondō, they have simpler equations, and also work for Jacobians of supersingular genus two curves.

Theorem
3.1
.

The proof is constructive: given a genus two curve in characteristic two, we explicitly compute the equations of its Jacobian, the corresponding Kummer surface

, and its partial desingularisation

. In section

and in section

4.
Computing a projective embedding of the Jacobian of a genus two curve

In this section, we will prove the following theorem:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a genus-two curve over a perfect field

of characteristic two.
Then the linear system associated to the divisor
2(\Theta_{+}+\Theta_{-})
defines an embedding over
k
of the Jacobian variety
\Jacc
as the intersection of
72
quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{15}

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Following the notation of Section
2
, in characteristic
2
, given a Weierstrass point

, the divisor
\Theta_{w}
is ample, and therefore,

is very ample
[

. Since
\Theta_{+}+\Theta_{-}\sim 2\Theta_{w}
and

, the linear system

defines a projective embedding of

into

.

In order to compute this embedding we first compute a basis of

for a general genus two curve. Next, we determine all relations among the elements of this basis, thus obtaining the

The two software packages that have been used to perform these computations have been Mathematica
[

]
for computing the majority of equations and Magma
[

to perform the more geometric operations on the Kummer surfaces such as checking the configuration of singularities described in Theorem

.
All the relevant code is available

.

4.1.
Computing a basis of
\mathcal{L}(2(\Theta_{+}+\Theta_{-}))

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a general genus–two curve of the form

y^{2}+\Bigl(\sum_{i=0}^{3}g_{i}x^{i}\Bigr)y\;=\;\sum_{i=0}^{6}f_{i}x^{i},

which we assume satisfies
\deg(g)=3
; the relevance of this assumption will become apparent in the next section.

The idea of obtaining explicit models of Kummer surfaces in characteristic
2
by specialising from characteristic
0
goes back to Müller
[
29
]
.
In his work, he computed the equations of a basis
\{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\}
of
\mathcal{L}(\Theta_{+}+\Theta_{-})
in characteristic zero such that, upon reducing the coefficients modulo two, form a basis
\{\overline{k}_{1},\overline{k}_{2},\overline{k}_{3},\overline{k}_{4}\}
of
\mathcal{L}(\Theta_{+}+\Theta_{-})
for a general genus two curve defined over a field of characteristic two.

The basis
\{\overline{k}_{1},\overline{k}_{2},\overline{k}_{3},\overline{k}_{4}\}
is given by the following expressions:

\displaystyle\bar{k}_{1}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\bar{k}_{2}

\displaystyle=x_{1}+x_{2},

\displaystyle\bar{k}_{3}

\displaystyle=x_{1}x_{2},

\displaystyle\bar{k}_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{S(x_{1},x_{2})+y_{2}g(x_{1})+y_{1}g(x_{2})}{\left(x_{1}+x_{2}\right)^{2}},

where

\displaystyle S(u,v)

\displaystyle=f_{0}+f_{1}(u+v)+f_{3}uv(u+v)^{2}+f_{5}u^{2}v^{2}(u+v)^{2}.

Since these are linearly independent, and the product of any two elements of
\mathcal{L}(\Theta_{+}+\Theta_{-})
lies in
\mathcal{L}(2(\Theta_{+}+\Theta_{-}))
, we obtain ten elements of the basis of
\mathcal{L}(2(\Theta_{+}+\Theta_{-}))
, which in analogy with the characteristic zero case, we will denote by
\overline{k}_{ij}
.

As
\ell(2(\Theta_{+}+\Theta_{-}))=16
, we still need to compute six more independent elements of the basis, for which we will specialise from characteristic zero. While it is not known what the elements of these basis are for models of curves of the form
y^{2}+g(x)y=f(x)
, for genus two curves defined by equations of the form
y^{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{6}\tilde{f}_{i}x^{i}
we know equations for a basis of
\mathcal{L}(2(\Theta_{+}+\Theta_{-}))
, and, more specifically, for a basis
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{6}\}
that generates all odd functions
[
14
, Section 3]
.

By considering the morphism
(x,y)\mapsto(x,y+\tfrac{1}{2}g(x))
, any curve
\mathcal{C}
of the form

y^{2}+g(x)y=f(x)

can be mapped over
k
to a curve
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
of the form
y^{2}=\tilde{f}(x)
where
\tilde{f}(x)=f(x)+\tfrac{1}{4}g(x)^{2}
. Through this change of coordinates, we can find a basis
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{6}\}
for the odd functions of
\mathcal{L}(2(\Theta_{+}+\Theta_{-}))
for models of curves of the form
y^{2}+g(x)y=f(x)
in characteristic zero.

Although one might expect the reductions modulo two
\{\bar{b}_{1},\dots,\bar{b}_{6}\}
to remain independent, this fails in general. However, we can easily construct a basis that reduces well modulo two via the Algorithm
1
, which amounts to computing the Smith normal form associated to the basis.

Since the relations among the
b_{i}
form a finitely generated
\mathbb{Z}
-module, the Smith normal form always exists, and the algorithm necessarily terminates after finitely many row and column operations. Thus, once the Smith normal form produces a primitive
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of this module, its reduction modulo two forms a basis of
\mathcal{L}(2(\Theta_{+}+\Theta_{-}))
over the residue field. In particular, this guarantees that the basis obtained by the algorithm specialises well to characteristic two.

Algorithm 1

- Computation of a basis that reduces well modulo two

Input:
A basis
\mathcal{B}=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\}
defined over
k
.

Output:
A modified basis
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
whose reduction modulo two is a basis of a subspace of
\mathcal{L}(2(\Theta_{+}+\Theta_{-}))
of the reduced curve
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
.

1:

Multiply each
b_{i}\in\mathcal{B}
by the smallest power of two that clears all denominators.

2:

Reduce the coefficients of the resulting elements modulo two to obtain a new set
\{\bar{b}_{1},\dots,\bar{b}_{n}\}
.

3:

Compute all linear relations among the
\bar{b}_{i}
by determining the kernel of the associated matrix over the reduced field.

4:

Lift these relations to
k
to produce new elements in
\mathcal{B}
that vanish modulo two.

5:

Divide by the appropriate powers of two to obtain new elements whose reductions modulo two are independent of the previous ones.

6:

Repeat steps 1–5 until obtaining a basis of odd functions whose reductions are linearly independent and belong to
\mathcal{L}(2(\Theta_{+}+\Theta_{-}))
.

Applying this algorithm, we obtain a basis
\mathcal{B}
such that the reductions of the elements of this basis modulo two are the following:

\displaystyle\bar{b}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{g(x_{1})+g(x_{2})}{x_{1}+x_{2}},

\displaystyle\bar{b}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{2}g(x_{1})+x_{1}g(x_{2})}{x_{1}+x_{2}},

\displaystyle\bar{b}_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{2}^{2}g(x_{1})+x_{1}^{2}g(x_{2})}{x_{1}+x_{2}},

\displaystyle\bar{b}_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{y_{2}g(x_{1})+y_{1}g(x_{2})}{x_{1}+x_{2}},

\displaystyle\bar{b}_{5}

\displaystyle=\frac{(g_{1}+g_{2}(x_{1}+x_{2})+g_{3}x_{1}x_{2})(y_{2}g(x_{1})+y_{1}g(x_{2}))}{(x_{1}+x_{2})^{2}}+\frac{T(x_{1},x_{2})}{x_{1}+x_{2}},

\displaystyle\bar{b}_{6}

\displaystyle=\frac{g_{3}(g_{0}g_{3}+g_{2}^{2}(x_{1}+x_{2})+g_{2}g_{3}x_{1}x_{2})(y_{2}g(x_{1})+y_{1}g(x_{2}))}{(x_{1}+x_{2})^{2}}+\frac{g_{3}^{2}R(x_{1},x_{2})}{x_{1}+x_{2}}.

where
T(u,v)
and
R(u,v)
are the following bivariate polynomials:

\displaystyle T(u,v)

\displaystyle=f_{1}g_{1}+(f_{3}g_{1}+f_{1}g_{3})uv+(f_{5}g_{1}+f_{3}g_{3})u^{2}v^{2}+f_{5}g_{3}u^{3}v^{3}+(f_{3}g_{0}+f_{1}g_{2})(u+v)

\displaystyle+g_{0}g_{2}g_{3}uv(u+v)+(f_{5}g_{2}+g_{2}^{2}g_{3})u^{2}v^{2}(u+v)+f_{1}g_{3}(u+v)^{2}+(f_{5}g_{1}+g_{1}g_{2}g_{3})uv(u+v)^{2}

\displaystyle+(f_{5}g_{0}+g_{0}g_{2}g_{3})(u+v)^{3},

\displaystyle R(u,v)

\displaystyle=f_{1}g_{0}+(f_{3}g_{0}+f_{1}g_{2})uv+(f_{5}g_{0}+f_{3}g_{2})u^{2}v^{2}+f_{5}g_{2}u^{3}v^{3}+(f_{1}g_{1}+g_{0}^{2}g_{2})(u+v)

\displaystyle+(g_{0}g_{2}^{2}+g_{0}g_{1}g_{3})uv(u+v)+(f_{5}g_{1}+f_{3}g_{3}+g_{1}g_{2}g_{3})u^{2}v^{2}(u+v)+f_{1}g_{2}(u+v)^{2}

\displaystyle+(f_{5}g_{0}+g_{1}^{2}g_{3}+g_{0}g_{2}g_{3})uv(u+v)^{2}+(f_{1}g_{3}+g_{0}g_{1}g_{3})(u+v)^{3}.

However, working in characteristic two introduces an additional difficulty: the reductions
\overline{b}_{i}
of the newly constructed elements
b_{i}
turn out to be linearly dependent on the previously computed elements
\overline{k}_{jr}
. The underlying reason for this phenomenon is the following. The involution
\iota
acts on
\mathcal{L}(2(\Theta_{+}+\Theta_{-}))
with eigenvalues
\pm 1
, and our chosen basis is diagonal with respect to this action. After reducing modulo two, the eigenvalues

1

\iota
on the reductions of these basis elements becomes trivial. This contradicts the known fact that
\mathcal{L}(2(\Theta_{+}+\Theta_{-}))
contains elements that are not fixed by
\iota

This degeneration does not render the construction of the

useless. Indeed, the reductions
\overline{b}_{i}
still play a key role in describing partial desingularisations of Kummer surfaces in characteristic two, as will be discussed in Section
5

\mathcal{L}(2(\Theta_{+}+\Theta_{-}))
in characteristic zero.

Since each
\overline{b}_{i}
can be written as a linear combination of the
\overline{k}_{jr}

\mathcal{L}(2(\Theta_{+}+\Theta_{-}))
whose reductions vanish in characteristic two and hence must be divisible by a power of two. Applying Algorithm

, we obtain a basis
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{6}\}
whose reductions modulo two, together with the

\mathcal{L}(2(\Theta_{+}+\Theta_{-}))
. The full procedure and the resulting equations are implemented in the Mathematica notebook

6.

7.
Kummer surfaces with everywhere good reduction over a quadratic field

Let
F
be a number field and

such that

is a complete, discretely valued field with ring of integers

and residue field

if there exists a scheme or algebraic space

smooth and proper over
\mathcal{O}_{K}
with generic fibre
\mathcal{X}_{K}\cong X

has
potentially good reduction at

such that for all places

lying above
v
,

has good reduction at
w
.
A variety
X/F
is said to have
everywhere good reduction
if it has good reduction at every non-Archimedean place.

As stated in the introduction, we are interested in studying whether there exist K3 surfaces
X/F
with everywhere good reduction. We will see that this is indeed the case, as we can find a scheme model of Kummer surface with everywhere good reduction, as a consequence of the following.

Let
A
be an abelian surface over a number field
F
and let
v
be an non-Archimedean place.

•

If
v
does not lie above two, then the Kummer surface associated to
A
has good reduction at
v
if and only if there exists a quadratic twist
A^{\chi}
of
A
such that
A^{\chi}
has good reduction. This is a consequence of the work of Matsumoto
[
26
]
and Overkamp
[
30
]
.

•

If
v
lies above two, then the Kummer surface associated to
A
has potentially good reduction if
A
has good reduction at
v
. This is a consequence of Lazda and Skorobogatov
[
25
]
in the ordinary and almost ordinary case and Matsumoto
[
27
]
in the supersingular case.

Starting with an abelian surface with everywhere good reduction, these results show that over possibly a field extension, its associated Kummer surface has everywhere good reduction. The goal of this section is to show that it is possible to explicitly construct an example of a Kummer surface with everywhere good reduction over a quadratic number field.
