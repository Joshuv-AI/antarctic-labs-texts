Evaluating compliance with quality indicators of HIV and AIDS clinical care and analyzing those associated with mortality in the Democratic Republic of Congo
Authors: Tambwe Willy Muzumbukilwa, Bihehe Masemo Dieudonne, Riziki Ghislain Manimani, Aganze Gloire Mushebenge, Rajesh Vikram Vagiri, Manimbulu Nlooto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSince human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) infection have been identified, significant improvements have been made concerning its diagnosis and treatment. Few contributions have been made in the area of quality indicators in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). To address this gap, a recent study conducted in the said area in 2023 resulted in the development of a comprehensive list of 88 indicators for assessing the quality of clinical care for HIV and AIDS. Out of these, 66 were identified as a core set of quality indicators for the assessment of HIV and AIDS clinical care. The aim of this study was to evaluate compliance with these quality indicators and determine those that are associated with mortality among people living with HIV and AIDS (PLWHA) in the DRC.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted at Panzi Hospital in Bukavu, South Kivu province of the DRC. The study population included PLWHA adult patients who were followed for a period of five years at the antiretroviral (ARV) clinic from January 2017 to December 2021. Descriptive analysis was performed to evaluate essential characteristics, and inferential statistics were performed including a multivariable logistic regression analysis performed to assess the association between selected clinical care indicators and PLWHA mortality, to estimate the odds ratios of mortality based on various factors, and to control for confounding variables.ResultsA total of 884 PLWHA were included. The level of compliance with the quality indicators was 78.79%. In the multivariate analysis, the quality-of-care indicators associated with mortality in the follow-up PLWHA on treatment were items 12 (Late diagnosis of HIV infection in specialized care), 24 (Assessment of resistance in cases of virologic failure), and 38 (Evaluation of Cardiac risk). Other factors associated with mortality were male sex, lower CD4 (< 500mm3) and high viral load (> 50 copies/ml).ConclusionThe level of compliance with the indicators was moderate. Some quality indicators were associated with mortality. These indicators are associated with delayed diagnosis of HIV infection, assessment of resistances in case of virologic failure, and evaluation of cardiac risk. Addressing gaps in adherence to these quality of care indicators (clinical care standards) could potentially reduce mortality rates and enhance the overall management of HIV and AIDS, particularly at Panzi Hospital in Bukavu, South Kivu Province, and in general in the DRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10253-y.

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) continue to pose significant public health challenges, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where healthcare systems often struggle with limited resources [1]. In the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), the HIV epidemic remains a critical concern, with 520,000 people living with HIV and AIDS (PLWHA) requiring consistent clinical care to manage the disease effectively [2]. Ensuring quality care for PLWHA is crucial for prolonging lives, reducing transmission and mortality rates, and improving overall population health outcomes [3]. One of the central strategies to enhance the quality of HIV and AIDS care is the implementation of standardized clinical care indicators [4]. These indicators serve as benchmarks to evaluate the effectiveness, consistency, and comprehensiveness of care provided to PLWHA [5]. Monitoring compliance with these indicators enables healthcare providers and policymakers to assess gaps in care delivery, address system inefficiencies, and improve treatment outcomes. In this regard, the study published by Muzumbukilwa et al. (2023) [6] identified 88 indicators for quality clinical care for HIV and AIDS in the literature review. Of these, 66 were selected as a fundamental set of quality indicators for evaluating HIV and AIDS clinical care. The selection of these indicators was informed by their widespread use in various studies and their endorsement by both the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines on HIV management [7] and the South African national guidelines for HIV care [8]. To ensure relevance within the context of low- and middle-income countries, priority was given to selecting indicators that were common to both sets of guidelines.

In resource-constrained settings like the DRC, maintaining high standards of care can be challenging. Various factors, such as insufficient infrastructure, lack of trained personnel, and limited access to medications, may hinder the consistent application of HIV and AIDS care guidelines [9]. Understanding the relationship between compliance with these indicators and PLWHA outcomes, particularly mortality, is crucial for targeted interventions that can enhance healthcare delivery in these settings [10]. In addition, mortality rates serve as foundational indicators guiding the development of pertinent health policy frameworks [11, 12]. While certain studies have addressed HIV and AIDS indicators concerning quality clinical care across diverse contexts and their associations with various other variables [6, 13], the influence of these indicators on mortality remains unexplored and warrants further analysis. In this context, it is pertinent to determine whether a correlation exists between the quality of care achieved through compliance with established quality standards and mortality rate. This study evaluated compliance with indicators of HIV and AIDS clinical care and determine those that are associated with mortality.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

In this retrospective cohort study, adult PLWHA were recruited from the Antiretroviral (ARV) clinic at Panzi Hospital in Bukavu, located in the Sud Kivu province of the Democratic Republic of Congo. The study covered a 5-year period from January 2017 to December 2021. This period was chosen to capture the depth and breadth of data necessary for a robust analysis of compliance with HIV care indicators and their association with mortality. The study site provides daily care for PLWHA, serves as a primary point of contact for approximately 3000 PLWHA, and accommodates an average of 4,500 visits annually. Among these PLWHA, 2428 are currently receiving ART from the study site. Furthermore, an average of 12 PLWHA are initiated on ART at the clinic each month.

Study population

In the analysis of compliance with quality indicators and those associated with mortality, all adult PLWHA were identified by reviewing the medical records from Panzi Hospital. The inclusion criteria encompassed the case files of both PLWHA in-patients and out-patients who were initiated on ART in 2017 or later, with complete medical records, including baseline demographic information, ART initiation date, and follow-up data on clinical indicators. Moreover, only case files of PLWHA aged 18 years at the time of ART initiation were considered. PLWHA who initiated ART at least 6 months before the start of the study period were included. Exclusions were made for medical case notes that were unaccounted for because of reasons such as loss or misfiling, incomplete information, or PLWHA being transferred to another level of care. In addition, PLWHA who had been receiving ART for less than six months were excluded from the study because this timeframe was deemed the minimum requirement for assessing treatment response [14, 15].

Quality indicators

Indicators for evaluating the quality of HIV and AIDS clinical care proposed by Muzumbukilwa et al. were used. This document published in 2023, recommended 88 quality indicators, which were selected considering the local context of low- and middle-income countries [6]. Out of the 88 indicators in this study, 66 were deemed relevant for evaluating compliance, as indicated in Table 1. Among these, 23 indicators were used to assess the correlation with mortality, as presented in Table 2.

Description of HIV and AIDS Quality indicators (the indicators considered relevant to assess correlation with mortality appear in italics)

The research team analyzed compliance with indicators in accordance with the standards established in the GeSIDA quality indicators document, as delineated in Table 2 [16]. The requisite data for this assessment were procured from electronic medical records, and physical patient files were archived in paper folders.

Study variables

The variables studied were age at the time of starting follow-up, gender, harmful behaviors as a risk factor for HIV infection, death during study period, CD4 + cell count, and HIV viral load after 6 months of ART. In this study, “harmful behaviors” are defined as substance abuse, including drug use, alcohol abuse, and smoking.

Statistical analysis

A database using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 29.0 was established. A descriptive analysis of the PLWHA’ essential characteristics was performed. Continuous variables are depicted in terms of their means and medians, whereas categorical variables are represented as percentages or proportions. The chi-square test was used to assess the equality of categorical variables. Sixty-six quality indicators met the standard intervals established by experts and were thus considered relevant for clinical care. These indicators were assessed using a 95% parametric confidence level (CIs). An indicator was considered fulfilled if the 95% CI of the compliance percentage contained the value defined as the standard. In evaluating clinical care, compliance score ranges typically assess how well healthcare providers follow established clinical guidelines or standards. These ranges often fall into four categories: 0–59%: Low compliance; 60–89%: Moderate compliance; 75–89%; 90–100%: High compliance [17]. The odds ratio (OR) was computed alongside 95% confidence intervals for each indicator to gauge the correlation between non-compliance of indicators and mortality. Subsequently, multivariate regression models were developed to examine the independent associations between quality indicators and mortality. These models incorporated additional independent variables, such as age, gender, harmful behaviors, CD4 + cell count, and HIV viral load. Variables exhibiting a significance level of p < 0.05 were retained within the model.

Validity of the instrument and reliability of the data

Each questionnaire was developed through a comprehensive literature review and consultations with healthcare experts to ensure clinical relevance. Content Validity was assured by conducting a panel of experts to evaluate each questionnaire, ensuring that all selected questions accurately reflected the constructs of interest. Data reliability was assessed using the interrater reliability method. The calculated Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was around 0.87, which was the greater score [18]. Data accuracy was maintained through audits and error-checking in SPSS.

All researchers involved in the survey underwent comprehensive training to ensure consistency and reliability in data gathering. The training included an overview of study objectives, an understanding of questionnaires, data collection procedures, ethical considerations, practice sessions, and monitoring and feedback.

Informed consent was not required for this study as it involved a retrospective analysis of anonymized PLWHA records with no direct contact. According to national regulations and ethical guidelines, minimal-risk research using pre-existing data does not necessitate individual consent.

Results

In this study, an examination of the clinical records of 884 PLWHA was undertaken and subsequently used for comprehensive analysis. The sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of PLWHA are defined and presented in Table 3. In the cohort of 884 PLWHA studied, 308 (34.8%) were male and 576 (65.2%) were female. Regarding age distribution, 468 (52.9%) fell within the range of 18–39 years, with 416 (47.1%) encompassing ages exceeding 40 years. In addition, a subset of 291 PLWHA (32.9%) exhibited harmful behaviours, notably substance use. Regarding viral load, favorable suppression of viral load was observed in 299 (33.8%) PLWHA who showed a viral load of less than 50 copies/ml, and 585 PLWHA (66.2%) exhibited a viral load > 50 copies/ml. Regarding the CD4 cell count, 312 (35.3%) PLWHA exhibited CD4 cell counts exceeding 500 cells/mm3, and 572 (64.7%) patients had a CD4 cell count less than 500 cells per mm3 (Table 3).

As shown in Table 4, the quality indicators associated with an increase in the risk of mortality were 12 (Late diagnosis of HIV infection in specialized care), 24 (Assessment of resistances in case of virologic failure), and 38 (Evaluation of Cardiac risk). Multivariate analysis showed that the above indicators had higher odds of being associated with mortality. Therefore, indicator 12 had an OR of 20.764 (95% CI 10.912–39.510), indicator 24 had an OR of 13.501 (95% CI 6.477–28.143), and indicator 38 had an OR of 11.429 (95% CI 6.161–21.201).

The other factors associated with mortality were male sex with an OR of 1.929 (95% CI 1.279–2.909), the group of CD4: <500 mm3 with an OR of 13.277 (95% CI 5.347–32.967), and VL: > 50 with an OR of 31.881 (95% CI 7.809- 130.151) (Table 5).

Discussion

The primary objective of this investigation was to assess compliance with indicators of clinical care for HIV and AIDS, with a secondary aim to discern any correlations between these indicators and mortality. The findings can help shape evidence-based guidelines for HIV and AIDS clinical care, ensuring that policies are grounded in PLWHA care and outcomes realities, and help allocate resources effectively to areas needing urgent attention.

Of the 884 clinical records of PLWHA evaluated in this study, female sex predominated in 65.2% of cases. This finding corroborates a survey conducted by Johnson et al. (2012) [19] in South Africa from 2004 to 2011, which acknowledged that females seemed to have greater access to counselling opportunities for adherence to HAART. Pregnancy and breastfeeding serve as pivotal moments for women to undergo HIV testing and start ART [18].

The findings from this study delineated that the percentage of PLWHA who exhibited a viral load of more than 50 copies/ml was high (66.2%) at 6 months after starting ART, in accordance with the study conducted by Edet A et al. (2019) [20]. This high percentage of no suppressed viral load can firstly be attributed to the prevalence of viral suppression rising over time. Literature has confirmed that the prevalence of viral suppression has demonstrated a consistent upward trajectory with prolonged ART intervention [21, 22]. Secondly, the high percentage of no suppressed viral load could appear in PLWHA who may have inadequate adherence to medication schedules or irregular drug intake, which could lead to incomplete suppression of the virus [23].

In this study, we observed a high percentage of PLWHA with CD4 cell counts 500 mm3, with an average of 64.7% in the sixth month following the initiation of ART. A similar pattern was observed in a Mongolian study conducted by Bayarsaikhan et al. (2021) [24], which showed a high percentage of PLWHA with CD4 cell counts 500 mm3 at 6 months after initiation of ART compared with those with CD4 cell counts > 500 mm3.

This finding is because, in general, the increase in CD4 cell count during ART can continue even during the second year and over time [25].

While adherence to quality indicators exhibited a high level in this study, there are areas for improvement. Concerning certain indicators where the perceived adherence is notably strong, the observed outcomes for these indicators fell below the prescribed standard, which was likely attributed to the need for consistent documentation within the clinical records of the executed actions. These indicators include 11 (Delay in referral to specialized care), 57 (Monitoring toxicity of ARVs), 64 (Follow-up in the outpatient clinic after hospital discharge), and 82 (Undetectable viral load (< 50 copies/mL) at week 48 of treatment).

Another cluster of indicators of the comorbidities present among PLWHA exhibited a level of compliance that was less than optimal: 17 (Bilirubin assessment), 20 (A chest X-ray was performed on the patient at the initial assessment), 21 (ECG performed in patients and Cardiovascular risk assessment), 37 (Detection of metabolic syndrome in the HIV population), 38 (Evaluation of Cardiac risk).

A group of indicators concerning vaccination of PLWHA with which the level of compliance was suboptimal: 49 (Vaccination against hepatitis B), 50 (Vaccination against pneumococcal infection), 52 (Vaccination against Covid-19).

The lack compliance with indicators 5 (Availability of Voluntary Medical Male Circumcision) and 9 (Diabetes and hypertension must be integrated with HIV services) is due to the fact that those services are not available in the HIV services program offered at Panzi Hospital.

Among the relevant quality indicators evaluated, multivariate analysis showed that some were associated with an increase in the risk of mortality. These indicators include 12 (Late diagnosis of HIV infection in specialized care), 24 (Assessment of resistances in case of virologic failure), 38 (Evaluation of Cardiac risk).

This indicator, which refers to the late diagnosis of HIV infection, is associated with a high risk of mortality. This result was confirmed by a study by Gabre et al.(2023) [26]. This retrospective cohort investigation conducted within public hospitals situated in North Ethiopia identified that PLWHA with late diagnosis of HIV exhibited a three-fold higher mortality rate attributable to HIV when compared with those diagnosed at an earlier phase. The result was also established by Bakewell et al., (2022) [27] in their study estimating the risk of mortality attributable to recent late HIV diagnosis. A delay in HIV diagnosis is associated with a high mortality rate. In their study, they found a mortality rate of 38% for PLWHA who had a late HIV diagnosis versus 22% for those without a late diagnosis.

The indicator, which refers to assessing resistance in cases of virologic failure, was related to mortality. This corroborates with the literature confirming that virological failure and HIV drug resistance in PLWHA with HIV are strongly associated with mortality [28, 29].

The continual presence of HIV viremia among ART recipients significantly accelerates the onset of viral resistance, consequently culminating in treatment failure [30].

In the case of our study, the resistance to ARV in virologic failure could be due to the effect that some PLWHA may have inadequate adherence to medication schedules or irregular drug intake, which could lead to incomplete suppression of the virus. This incomplete suppression could create an environment in which the virus could mutate and develop resistance to the drugs being used, fostering an increase in mortality rates. HIV resistance testing and screening are advised for these PLWHA.

The research results indicate that the indicator, which refers to screening for cardiac risk, was associated with mortality. These findings were confirmed by Touloumi et al. (2020) [31], Grand et al. (2020) [32], and Vos et al. (2021) [33]. The authors mentioned that PLWHA presenting with cardiac risk factors and cardiovascular diseases face a higher mortality risk compared to those without cardiac risk. Therefore, regular screening and early management of cardiovascular risk factors in PLWHA should be highly prioritized in follow-up patients.

The gastrointestinal tract assumes a pivotal role in the initial stages of HIV infection pathogenesis, given the virus’s predilection for infecting CD4 + T cells, most of which—approximately 80%—reside within the gut mucosa. Consequently, the depletion of these T cells inflicts damage upon the gut mucosa, leading to heightened gut permeability and subsequent microbial translocation. This sequence of events precipitates chronic inflammation and immune activation. Despite the implementation of antiretroviral therapy, which does not eradicate HIV infection, immune irregularities persist. These persistent abnormalities are correlated with both the occurrence of cardiovascular events and mortality rates [34].

Although the mechanisms for HIV-related cardiovascular disease might be the same in all PLWHA, the distribution of cardiovascular disease risk factors varies by geographical location [35]. In this investigation, we believe that the strong association between cardiovascular risk and mortality is because more PLWHA have access to antiretroviral therapy with more benign cardiovascular disease side-effect profiles. Indoor air pollution and smoking should also be mentioned as cardiovascular risks that increase mortality rates.

Our study indicates that some factors were associated with mortality. These factors included male gender, higher VL (VL > 50 copies/ml) at 6 months, and lower CD4 (CD4 < 500 mm3) at 6 months of ART.

As specified in the study conducted by Gao et al., (2019) [36], our study also identified that mortality was associated with male gender. The first reason is that men exhibit the highest incidence of HIV and shoulder a disproportionate burden of HIV infection [31]. Additionally, certain high-risk sexual behaviours, including inconsistent condom usage, engagement in multiple sexual partnerships, and drug or alcohol abuse, significantly elevate the susceptibility of males to HIV infection, leading to high mortality rates [37, 38]. The second explanation for their high mortality is that men exhibit adverse effects associated with ART and demonstrate lower adherence to treatment protocols than women [39]. The third reason is that many HIV prevention and treatment programs are designed around maternal and child health, leading to a limited focus on men. This leaves gaps in targeted counselling, education, and intervention for males [40]. Additionally, men may face barriers such as inflexible clinic hours, which conflict with work schedules, reducing their ability to attend medical appointments regularly and leading to a high rate of mortality [41].

In this study, mortality was also associated with low CD4 cell count and high VL. This analysis was consistent with the study conducted by Cobre et al., (2020) [42], which indicated that high baseline VL and low baseline CD4 levels increased the risk of mortality [43]. The literature confirms a robust correlation between early mortality and the extent of immunodeficiency at the initiation of ART [44], as was the case in our study. Therefore, strategies to reduce mortality must include earlier HIV diagnosis and enhanced longitudinal care preceding ART initiation.

Study limitations

Our study had a few limitations: our investigation was single-site clinic-based, so the results may not be generalizable to the overall population. As a retrospective study, the data were collected from PLWHA’ files, these are mostly incomplete; some critical variables were missing. Therefore, these samples were excluded from the study. This data loss and reduced sample size can weaken statistical power, making it more difficult to detect significant effects, ultimately affecting the results. In future research, this issue should be addressed by:

Only the PLWHA’ cases that follow-up at Panzi Hospital were analyzed, and some PLWHA could have been followed to other hospital centres. The usefulness of these quality indicators as predictors of mortality would require validation in a second, larger cohort and an assessment to determine whether the indicators for the calculation of the risk rate should be weighted.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the need to analyze the clinical practice concerning the care of PLWHA to identify a correlation between the quality of care achieved through compliance with established quality standards and mortality rate. The research uniquely contributes by highlighting how poor adherence to clinical standards impacts PLWHA outcomes in low-resource settings. By focusing on specific quality indicators in practice, healthcare providers can enhance clinical consistency, improve training, and strengthen the health system’s support for HIV and AIDS care. The compliance rate with quality indicators was moderate, 78.79%, with 52 indicators meeting the established standards out of 66 evaluated. The quality indicators associated with an increased risk of mortality were 12 (Late diagnosis of HIV infection in specialized care), 24 (Assessment of resistances in case of virologic failure), and 38 (Evaluation of Cardiac risk). The other factors associated with mortality included male sex, lower CD4 (< 500 mm3), and high VL (> 50 copies/ml). All these quality indicators should be considered a priority in the healthcare of PLWHA. Implementing these findings into policy and patient education can ultimately improve PLWHA outcomes, reduce mortality, and foster a more effective, sustainable healthcare environment for managing HIV and AIDS. For future research, innovative care models like task-shifting and decentralized care, along with examining social determinants, are recommended to address these gaps and improve HIV care quality.
