Abortion care preferences and trade-offs when traveling out-of-state for facility-based services: findings from a discrete choice experiment
Authors: Gracia Sierra, Klaira Lerma, Gabriela Alvarez Perez, Anitra Beasley, Kari White
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPregnant people living in states that banned abortion after the US Supreme Court’s decision overturning Roe v Wade (Dobbs v Jackson Women’s Health Organization -Dobbs decision) may evaluate multiple factors when deciding where to obtain facility-based abortion care in another state. We examine Texans’ stated preferences for out-of-state facility-based abortion care and quantify the trade-offs they would make when choosing between out-of-state facilities following a 2022 abortion ban.MethodsIn August 2022, we surveyed Texans ≥ 16 years old seeking abortion at in-state facilities or who were searching online for information about accessing abortion care. We used a Bayesian discrete choice experiment to analyze 12 choice sets for out-of-state facility-based abortion care and to assess preferences for three care attributes: wait time to appointment, distance to facility, and cost. We estimated conditional, multinomial, and mixed logit models to examine respondents’ attribute preferences and how these differed across subgroups. We used marginal rates of substitution to express Texans’ tradeoffs in terms of willingness to pay and willingness to travel for different attributes.ResultsAmong 136 respondents (1,362 observations), time to next appointment was the most important attribute (β = -0.887, p < 0.001), followed by cost (β = -0.006, p < 0.001) and distance (β = -0.001, p < 0.001). Respondents were willing to pay an additional $248 (95% CI: $220, $278) and travel 917 miles (95% CI: 711, 1123) further to get an appointment one week sooner. The willingness to pay for a one-week reduction in wait time to appointment was higher among respondents who did not report any economic hardships ($313, 95% CI: $255, $371) than among those who had one or more economic hardships ($175, 95% CI: $142, $208).ConclusionThis discrete choice experiment examining Texans’ preferences for out-of-state facility-based abortion care demonstrates that people seeking abortion prioritize wait time to appointment when deciding where to obtain care. Efforts to make timely care a more feasible option for all those seeking abortion care are needed to mitigate the potential widening of disparities in access to care now that more states have banned abortion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-12005-9.

Background

In June 2022, the United States (US) Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade (Dobbs v Jackson Women’s Health Organization -Dobbs decision), which has allowed 14 states to implement laws prohibiting nearly all abortions. Prior to the Court’s ruling, approximately 10% of all people who obtained an abortion—and 15% of those living in states with restrictive abortion laws – traveled across state lines for care [1]. Following the Supreme Court’s decision, the overall number of people who obtained out-of-state abortion care doubled from one in ten to one in five [2].

Because facility-based abortion care is no longer available in many states, pregnant people who prefer in-person, clinician-provided care or who require such care if they want or need a procedural abortion will have to travel long distances and overcome significant financial and logistical barriers [2, 3]. In states where abortion remains legal, facilities that provide this care may have longer wait times for appointments as they try to accommodate more out-of-state patients, while still providing care to in-state patients [4–6]. The costs of long-distance travel along with appointment delays may result in people obtaining care later in pregnancy when it is more expensive, and lack of insurance coverage for abortion – along with travel costs—will increase people’s out-of-pocket expenses [7, 8].

Previous studies have reported that people seeking abortion consider multiple factors when deciding where to obtain care. These factors include cost, referrals, facility name recognition, gestational limits, state abortion restrictions, wait times to appointment, and distance [3, 9–14]. However, there is limited data demonstrating which factors are most important in people’s decision making. As the share of people in the US living farther away from abortion facilities increases, additional evidence about how people seeking abortion evaluate their options for out-of-state facility-based care is needed to inform patient navigation efforts so people can obtain timely and affordable abortion care.

In a 2014 national survey of abortion patients, Fuentes and Jerman found that 32% of patients chose the abortion facility where they received care because it was the closest [13]. People might prioritize getting care at the nearest facility because their individual circumstances may make it difficult for them to travel. Pregnant people who have children – who account for nearly two-thirds of US abortion patients –other caregiving responsibilities, or constraints taking time off from work may prefer to get care at the nearest facility to minimize their time away from home. Additionally, travel-related expenses, including the cost of gas or airline tickets, lodging for overnight stays and food, may increase with longer travel distances, and people living on low incomes may try to minimize these costs by getting care at the nearest facility. Notably, not all people who travel for abortion care obtain their abortion at the nearest facility [12, 13, 15, 16], likely reflecting the relevance of other facility and individual characteristics in their decision decision-making. Moreover, most of the prior studies about people’s facility decision-making were conducted prior to the overturning of Roe v. Wade, which significantly increased travel distances for many US residents. The relative importance of distance may be different in the current context as people consider facilities that are now hundreds of miles away [17].

Costs may also be an important factor in people’s decision making, particularly for people living on low incomes, who account for the majority of people who obtain abortion care in the US [18, 19]. The median cost for medication abortion (available up to 11 weeks of pregnancy) is $560 and for a second-trimester procedural abortion is $895 [7]. Medicaid cannot be used outside of an individual’s home state, and some states restrict coverage for abortion by private insurance plans, forcing people to pay out of pocket for care, on top of any travel-related expenses. Because many patients seeking abortion care are living on low incomes, they may search for facilities that offer care at a lower cost [10, 12]. Indeed, the aforementioned national survey of abortion patients reported that 15% chose their facility because it was the most affordable [13]. In a recent survey of 887 US residents assigned female sex at birth and aged 18 to 55 years, Seymour and colleagues found that respondents placed a high value on cost of services in a hypothetical search for out-of-state abortion care [11]. To help offset their costs, people may also seek financial assistance from national and local abortion funds, non-profit organizations that provide funding for abortion and travel to people needing abortion care. However, these organizations may not cover all of a person’s costs and cannot support all those seeking assistance [20].

A third factor people seeking abortion consider is time to the next available appointment. In 2014, 13% of abortion patients reported that they chose the facility at which they received care because it could schedule them the soonest [13]; however, recent research suggests that time to appointment may weigh more heavily in people’s decision making and be prioritized over distance and cost [11, 12]. A discrete choice experiment study assessing abortion preferences demonstrated that wait time to appointment was highly influential in the abortion care decision-making process [11]. In a qualitative study of Texas residents seeking abortion care following the September 2021 implementation of Senate Bill 8 (SB8), which prohibited abortions after detectable embryonic cardiac activity, many pregnant Texans prioritized shorter wait times to appointment over cost of care and distance when deciding where to obtain their abortion [12]. They were frequently motivated by their intense pregnancy symptoms (e.g., nausea, vomiting and fatigue) and strong abortion method preference-particularly for medication abortion. Additionally, people may prioritize time to appointment because they worry that longer wait times will push them later in pregnancy, which can increase their costs, or may prevent them from getting an abortion at all owing to differences in facility and state gestational limits for abortion [21, 22].

In sum, the reasons people seeking abortion care choose a facility and may prefer one facility over another based on distance, cost and wait times are multifaceted. Moreover, people may have stronger preferences about where to go based on their individual characteristics, including their parenting status, abortion method preference, and economic circumstances. Examining key sources of heterogeneity in preferences and tradeoffs among people seeking abortion care can identify which groups may need additional resources to obtain out-of-state care.

In this study, we build on the extant literature and examine pregnant Texans’ stated preferences for different features of out-of-state facility-based abortion care and quantify the trade-offs they are willing to make when deciding where to go. Specifically, we use a Bayesian discrete-choice experiment (DCE) approach to assess how study respondents valued different characteristics (or attributes) of out-of-state facilities over others, focusing on wait time to appointment, distance to facility, and cost. We also investigated heterogeneity in preferences for attributes of facility-based abortion care and variability in tradeoffs in terms of cost and travel across respondents’ characteristics.

Methods

Study sample

We conducted this study in August 2022 – the same month that Texas’ total abortion ban went into effect following the overturning of Roe v. Wade and nearly one year after Texas implemented a law prohibiting most abortions after detectable embryonic cardiac activity (or 5 to 6 weeks after a person’s last menstrual period). In the preceding 11 months, thousands of pregnant Texans who could not obtain in-state abortion care traveled long distances to surrounding states, and the numbers and burdens of travel were expected to increase once abortion care was no longer available in Texas and many neighboring states also implemented total abortion bans [23].

We recruited respondents who had considered abortion and participated in one of two rapid-response survey studies to specifically examine their decision-making about where to obtain abortion care. Study 1 enrolled 300 English- and Spanish-speaking pregnant people ages ≥ 18 years who presented for an ultrasound visit at eight Texas abortion facilities and were seeking abortion care in June and July 2022 [24]. After providing informed consent, Study 1 respondents completed a one-time, self-administered tablet-based survey about their preferences for abortion care. Study 2 enrolled 450 English- and Spanish-speaking Texas residents who were ≥ 16 years of age, pregnant and searching online for information about accessing abortion care between September 2021 and July 2022 [12, 25]. After providing informed consent, Study 2 respondents used their own electronic device to complete a self-administered baseline and 6-week follow-up survey about their experiences seeking abortion care in Texas and other states; in the follow-up survey, respondents reported whether they had obtained an abortion. Surveys for both studies were available in English and Spanish and collected information on respondents’ demographic characteristics, indicators of socioeconomic status, and abortion method preference. At the end of the facility-based survey (Study 1) and follow-up online survey (Study 2), we asked respondents whether they would be willing to be contacted for future studies and, if so, they provided contact information where they could be reached.

Overall, 125 (42%) of Study 1 respondents and 283 (63%) of Study 2 respondents agreed to be contacted for future studies. In August 2022, we reached out to 125 Study 1 respondents who agreed to be re-contacted and 86 Study 2 respondents who completed the follow-up survey between May and August 2022, agreed to be re-contacted, and reported they had not obtained an abortion. The emails and text messages we sent to respondents who had agreed to be contacted for future studies directed them to a secure link where they could read more about the current study’s purpose and, if interested in participating, provide consent and complete the survey in English or Spanish on their own device. We contacted participants up to six times to remind them about the study opportunity. Those who completed the survey received a $40 electronic gift card. The University of Texas at Austin institutional review board, which provides ethical oversight of research studies, approved procedures for informed consent and other study activities. All participants ≥ 18 years of age provided informed consent to participate in the study. In the case of minors, we only obtained participant assent. Parental consent was not obtained since requiring this may have put minors at risk if they had not disclosed their abortion decision and completely precluded them from participating in the study.

Survey design: discrete choice experiment

Because we had already collected information on respondents’ sociodemographic characteristics, the survey for this study primarily consisted of a discrete choice experiment (DCE) about their preferences for out-of-state facility-based abortion care. We used a DCE to identify the relative importance of different facility characteristics and the trade-offs people were willing to make between these characteristics [26–28]. In a DCE, respondents are presented with several hypothetical choice sets (or questions) and asked to choose their preferred option between two or more discrete (i.e., mutually exclusive) alternatives. The alternatives differ in attributes (e.g., characteristics or features of an abortion facility) and in the levels (or values) of the attributes. DCEs are based on the assumption that when someone faces a choice between two or more alternatives, they will choose the one that provides them with the highest utility or value. The utility of each alternative is assumed to be a function of the observed attribute levels, preference weights for each attribute, and unobserved attributes of each alternative [29–31].

Respondents in this study were asked to choose between two alternatives in each choice set, and the instructions preceding the choice sets stated, “please select the clinic you would most likely choose. Think about what was happening in your life when you were first considering abortion while you choose”. We did not include an opt-out option as we were specifically interested in the trade-offs that abortion patients who decide to travel for care make between available facilities.

Selection of attributes and levels

We selected attributes for the DCE based on a review of the published quantitative and qualitative literature about abortion travel and facility decision-making and findings from in-depth interviews that we conducted with 65 pregnant Texas residents seeking out-of-state abortion care following the implementation of Senate Bill 8 (SB8) [12]. The attributes we initially considered were: distance to a facility, wait time to appointment, cost and insurance, facility gestational duration limits, type of abortion procedure offered, referrals, state abortion restrictions, facility name recognition, and prior visit for care. As outlined by Coast et al. [32], we narrowed the list by focusing on attributes that were most important to people seeking abortion (i.e., mentioned most frequently in the literature and our interviews) and were amenable to changes in the service environment: one-way travel distance to a facility, out-of-pocket costs for care and travel, and wait time to next available appointment. We excluded type of procedure offered or gestational duration limits at facilities as attributes because our knowledge of the service environment indicated that abortion methods and higher gestational duration limits were available in the settings where people were most likely obtain care. We limited the attributes to three in order to minimize the cognitive burden of the choice tasks [33, 34].

For each of the attributes, we selected levels that would best reflect the range of options that respondents were likely to encounter once abortion was no longer available in Texas and many of its neighboring states. For the levels of one-way distance to an abortion facility, we used the average one-way distance between Texas metropolitan areas and the nearest cities that had abortion facilities in other states where abortion care was expected to remain legal following the Supreme Court’s decision [35, 36]: 400 miles, 800 miles, 1,200 miles (Table 1). As described to survey respondents, out-of-pocket costs referred to the amount of money they would have to pay for their abortion and travel after receiving any financial assistance: $500, $750, $1000. We generated these estimates based on the cost of an abortion before 12 weeks of gestation, the cost of gas for the above driving distances and price per gallon at the time of the study, and abortion clients’ reported amounts of financial assistance offered by non-profit abortion funds [7, 12, 37]. The levels for wait time to next available appointment were: next week, two weeks, and three weeks. These corresponded to the range of wait times to next appointment based on monthly mystery client calls we placed to out-of-state facilities between September 2021 and June 2022 [4, 5]. Creating the choice sets

Respondents who have to compare alternatives with numerous attributes and levels can experience cognitive burden, which can lead to biased results [33, 34]. Therefore, in designing our questionnaire, we aimed to limit the overall number of choice sets and the number of attributes included in each choice set. To do so, we used a statistically optimal design approach. Statistically optimal designs identify a smaller number of choice sets that force respondents to make trade-offs between alternatives, thereby maximizing the information gained from each observed choice and minimizing the variance of the parameter estimates. Specifically, we used a Bayesian D-efficient (DB-efficient) design to generate 12 choice sets with two alternatives per choice (Table 2); had we used a full factorial design that incorporated all possible combinations of facility attributes and levels, respondents would have needed to complete 351 choice sets (e.g.,., 27!/(2!*(27–2)!). The DCE survey questionnaire designed for this study consisted of 12 choice sets presented in one block so that each respondent had to answer 12 questions in which they were asked which facility they would choose between two alternatives when deciding where to obtain out-of-state facility-based abortion care. Everything about the facilities being compared was the same except for the varied attribute levels in the alternatives. DB-efficient designs specify a prior preference distribution that incorporates the researchers’ prior knowledge of the likely sign and/or magnitude of the parameter values (i.e., coefficients of the attributes) while acknowledging uncertainty about the true parameters. Drawing on qualitative data from in-depth interviews, quantitative data on Texans who traveled out-of-state for abortion care following the implementation of other abortion restrictions and our subject matter expertise, we assumed increases in wait time, distance, and out-of-pocket cost would have a negative effect on the value (or utility) people placed on an alternative. As such, we assigned negative signs to the priors corresponding to the parameter coefficients for each of these attributes. We specified the prior distribution as a truncated multivariate normal distribution, in which each distribution was centered around the mean vector, had a standard deviation of 1, and the upper boundary for the distribution was truncated at 0. To quantify the priors for the parameter coefficients (i.e., means) corresponding to the attributes of wait time to next appointment and distance to facility, we used existing data on Texas residents who traveled out of state for care between February 2020-May 2020, when in-state abortion facilities suspended services in response to a state executive order [15]. In specifying a prior distribution, we also accounted for uncertainty in our point estimates. Overall, this distribution of priors provided us with a range within which we believed the true parameter coefficients for each attribute were located and was consistent with existing research showing that people seeking abortion care may prioritize wait time to appointment over distance to facility and cost when deciding where to obtain out-of-state abortion care [11, 12].

Using Monte Carlo simulations, the idefix package, and the Modfed (modified Fedorov algorithm) and CEA (coordinate exchange algorithm) functions in R [26], we selected the design that resulted in the lowest DB-error (DB-error = 1.01) for the 12 choice sets and maximized the relevant information on preferences and trade-offs obtained from the information matrix (i.e., was the most efficient). We included a choice set with a strictly dominant alternative that was superior in all three attributes (i.e., shorter wait time, shorter travel distance, and lower cost) as an internal consistency check to make sure people understood the experiment (Table 2, Choice Set #2).

We assessed the robustness of our design in two ways. First, we examined the average probability that each alternative would be chosen based on the prior preference distribution so that there was no alternative that would always be chosen and respondents would have to make trade-offs (Supplementary Table A1). Second, we examined the balance of attribute levels to ensure that some levels were not overrepresented (or underrepresented), leading to biased estimates of the level preferences (Supplementary Table A2).

Sample size estimation

To determine our sample size, we followed the approach proposed by Johnson and Orme [38]. In this approach, the minimum sample size (N) required for the attributes’ main effects depends on the number of choice sets (t), the number of alternatives per choice set (a), and the number of analysis cells (c) according to the following equation: N > 500c/(t*a). When considering main effects, “c” is equal to the largest number of levels for any of the attributes. When considering all two-way interactions between the different attributes, “c” is equal to the largest product of levels of any two attributes. Because we were interested in the main effects and not in the two-way interactions between attributes, “c” was equal to three, which was the largest number of levels for any of our attributes. For a DCE with 12 choice sets and two alternatives per choice set, we chose a target sample size of 126, which was twice the required minimum sample size of N = 63 (e.g., N > 500(3)/ (12*2)) for a DCE with the attribute-level specifications of our design and allowed us to explore heterogeneity in the effects of attributes and trade-offs across different subpopulations.

Statistical analysis

We examined the overall distribution of respondents’ sociodemographic characteristics and used chi-squared tests to assess differences between the respondents in Study 1 (recruited at Texas abortion facilities) and Study 2 (recruited online). To estimate preferences for the three facility attributes and heterogeneity in preferences, we used the mlogit (multinomial logit) and the gmnl (multinomial logit models with random parameters) packages in R to fit a series of regression models, all of which specified a linear additive utility function specification without interactions between attributes [27]. First, we fit conditional logit models in which respondents’ choice of facility (the dependent variable) is explained only by the three facility attributes (distance, wait time to next appointment, cost). We also estimated these models using a non-linear utility specification in which wait time and distance were treated as categorical variables to assess whether there was a non-linear effect of attributes on utility. These models assume homogeneity in respondents’ preferences and did not include any individual characteristics. To assess heterogeneity in preferences, we next estimated multinomial logit models that included interactions between facility attributes and the following respondent characteristics: parenting status (had one or more children or no children), abortion method preference (medication or procedure), and recent economic hardship (any or none). We considered respondents to have a recent economic hardship if they experienced one or more of the following in the last 12 months: did not pay the full amount of rent or mortgage, did not pay the full amount of the gas or electricity bill, had phone service turned off because payments were not made, did not have enough to cover essential expenses, borrowed money from friends or family to help pay bills. In these models, the choice for each alternative is a function of attributes and individual-level characteristics. Statistically significant interaction terms identify differences between groups of respondents in the marginal utility (i.e., preference) of each attribute. However, these models assume differences in preferences are explained only by differences in the observed covariates and there is no heterogeneity due to unobserved characteristics across respondents.

In the final set of models, we used correlated mixed logit regression models to account for variations in preferences due to unobserved heterogeneity [39]. In these models the choice for each alternative is a function of attributes, random parameters for attributes of interest, and individual-level characteristics. We limited our mixed logit analyses to examining overall heterogeneity in preferences for longer wait time to next appointment by economic status because these were consistently statistically significant in the multinomial models.

To provide further insights into the relative importance of different attributes in respondents’ decision-making process of where to obtain out-of-state abortion care, we computed two marginal rates of substitution (MRS): the marginal willingness to pay (WTP) and the marginal willingness to travel (WTT). Using the model coefficients, we calculated the MRS between two attributes as the ratio of their marginal utilities (MRSx1/x2 = β1/β2), holding all other attributes constant. The calculations for WTP for wait time (WTP = βwait time/−1*(βcost)) and distance (WTP = βdistance/−1(βcost)) estimate the amount of money people are willing to pay for a one-week reduction in wait time or to reduce distance to facility by one mile (scaled to 100 miles to make results meaningful), respectively. Similarly, the WTT for wait time (WTT = βwait time/βdistance) and for cost (WTT = βcost/βdistance) estimates how much further people are willing to travel (in miles) to get a one-week earlier appointment or to reduce cost by $100.

We first estimated the overall WTP and WTT using the marginal utilities from the conditional logit model. We then used the marginal utilities from the multinomial models with interactions between attributes and respondent characteristics to estimate group-specific WTP and WTT. We also estimated the corresponding overall WTP and WTT, which we calculated as the weighted average of the group-specific values where the weights were given by the percentage of respondents in each subgroup. To estimate the standard deviation of the WTP and WTT, we used the Delta method involving a first-order Taylor series expansion of the WTP and WTT expressions [40]. We computed confidence intervals (CIs) for each MRS to describe the uncertainty associated with these estimates.

We estimated all models using the dataset that included all respondents. Additionally, we performed sensitivity analyses in which we estimated the models using a dataset that excluded respondents who failed the strictly dominance test.

Results

Of the 211 prior study respondents we invited to complete the DCE survey, 136 completed it: 67 Study 1 respondents and 69 Study 2 respondents (Table 3). The remaining 75 did not respond to our invitation after multiple requests. Most respondents were Hispanic (44%), had some college education or earned a college degree (59%), had children (60%), had paid employment (58%), experienced one or more economic hardship (74%), and had a preference for medication abortion (70%). Compared to Study 2 respondents, a higher percentage of those from Study 1 identified as Hispanic (55% vs 32%, p = 0.009) and were less likely to experience one or more economic hardship (66% vs 82%, p = 0.04). Participants contributed 1,632 observations (136 participants * 12 choice sets) for the analyses. The magnitude of the coefficients also indicated that wait time to next appointment (β = −0.887, p < 0.001) was the attribute people most valued when choosing where to obtain abortion care, followed by cost (β = −0.003, p < 0.001) and distance to facility (β = −0.001, p < 0.001). The conditional logit model coefficients under a non-linear utility specification showed no evidence of non-linear effects of attributes on utility. In the multinomial logit models that included respondent characteristics, the main effects for the facility attributes remained negative and significant, consistent with the conditional logit models and respondents’ preference for shorter wait times and distances and lower out-of-pocket costs (Table 5). Respondents who did not have children had a stronger preference for shorter wait time to next appointment compared to people who had children in Model 1 assessing non-financial attributes (wait time and distance; β = −0.046; < 0.05) and Model 2 assessing non-distance attributes (wait time and out-of-pocket costs; β = −0.046 p < 0.05). Discussion

People seeking abortion consider a variety of factors when deciding where to obtain care [10–13]. Our study adds to the limited body of literature that has assessed how people evaluate and prioritize different characteristics when obtaining out-of-state facility-based abortion care [11, 12]. As in those studies, we found that respondents prioritized getting care sooner over cost and distance to a facility. Indeed, we estimated that study respondents were willing to pay an additional $250 and travel 900 miles further to get an appointment one week sooner. The sizeable tradeoffs that respondents were willing to make demonstrate that people seeking abortion consider their care to be time sensitive. The reasons that getting care sooner may be more important than cost and distance are likely related to pregnant people’s preference for a particular abortion method, as well as their desire to resolve nausea, fatigue, other physical symptoms of pregnancy that affect their day-to-day well-being, and the psychosocial burden of being pregnant [12, 41].

Although we consistently found that all respondents wanted to obtain abortion care sooner, our novel modeling approach enabled us to illuminate heterogeneity in preferences across respondent subgroups and determine that the degree of tradeoffs in terms of cost and distance varied for some groups. Specifically, respondents who did not report any economic hardships during the last year were willing to pay more and travel further for an earlier appointment, compared to those who had at least one hardship. This difference is likely due to the fact that, prior to the implementation of abortion bans, economically disadvantaged pregnant people experiencing economic strain already had to delay paying routine expenses and borrow money to pay for care [42], and they have fewer resources available to realize their preferences. As a result, they may be forced to wait longer for care if there is congestion at the facilities that are closer to them or that offer care at lower costs, unless they are able to obtain additional financial and practical support to go elsewhere. Such delays may make those with fewer resources ineligible for their preferred method, need an abortion later in pregnancy when procedures are more costly, or forego having an abortion altogether [7, 10–13, 43]. These results suggest that as more states restrict abortion access, disparities in access to care will widen.

In our models, we also found that willingness to pay and willingness to travel for shorter wait times to appointments did not vary by respondents’ parenting status or by abortion method preference. The lack of significant differences by parenting status might be due to our variable being an imprecise measure of caregiving responsibilities; more nuanced measures that assess children’s age or the availability of other caregiving supports may yield different results. The absence of significant differences by abortion method preference could be attributed to the composition of our sample, in which all people were early in their pregnancy and were not approaching clinically meaningful thresholds that would make them ineligible for their preferred method. Although both medication and procedural abortion were available in geographic areas where Texas residents would travel, not all facilities in the US provided both methods, with 40% of them offering only medication abortion [44].

Implications of the findings

Given that respondents in our study prioritized appointment wait time over distance and cost, timeliness of care should be central in efforts to support pregnant people’s care navigation. This is especially pertinent for people seeking care in states with mandatory waiting period and two-appointment requirements, which extend the time to abortion beyond the wait time information provided by facilities [45]. In addition to including information on state restrictions (e.g., mandatory waiting periods), information about the next available appointment in web-based facility directories would allow people to easily identify the locations with the shortest wait times and enhance referral making among healthcare providers and navigators who help guide people to care. Also, people seeking abortion would benefit from additional financial and logistical (e.g., travel, childcare) support so they can get timely care. This would be particularly relevant for people facing economic hardships – who account for the majority of those who have historically obtained abortion care—but who may experience significant challenges gathering the resources needed [2, 11–13, 18, 42]. Although abortion funds have played an important role helping pregnant people to cover their abortion and travel costs [46], the escalating cost of travel, decrease in donations, and change in state laws in the past year may limit the number of people these organizations can support and the financial assistance they can provide [47, 48]. This can further restrict access to care, particularly for people residing in states that prohibit public and private insurance plans from covering abortion.

Strengths and limitations

Among the many strengths of our study include our use of DCE to assess preferences for out-of-state care in a sample of Texans who were considering (or recently considered) abortion. As such, respondents’ feelings about pregnancy and the evaluation of facility options resembled a more realistic abortion decision-making scenario and their preferences for care less hypothetical. Other unique strengths include our estimation of trade-offs and meaningful measures of willingness to pay and willingness to travel, as well as our examination of heterogeneity in preferences for facility attributes, which have not been used in other studies of abortion facility-decision making [11].

Our study also has limitations. Our findings might not be generalizable to the Texas population. Although we used diverse recruitment strategies, we may not have captured the preferences of people who are less likely to participate in research studies. Additionally, because half of our sample included respondents recruited at abortion facilities, our results may be affected by post-rationalization bias: these respondents may have selected alternatives that had attributes that were most similar to the location where they presented for care.

Although we based our DCE attributes on those that most commonly appeared in our and other’s research, there are other attributes that people seeking abortion care value when deciding where to obtain care that we did not assess; we also did not examine respondents’ preferences for other modes of obtaining an abortion, such as through telehealth, which has become increasingly common [49]. Future studies with larger samples of people considering abortion could assess additional attributes and options for obtaining abortion care and would provide greater precision in estimates for sub-group differences than we had in our study.

Conclusion

Our findings show that pregnant Texans seeking an abortion prioritize shorter wait time to appointment over distance and cost when deciding where to obtain out-of-state facility-based abortion care. These results highlight the relative importance that people seeking abortion place on getting care as soon as possible, as well as how the willingness to make financial and travel tradeoffs was more limited among those with fewer economic resources, despite their overall preference for timely care. Efforts to make timely care a more feasible option for all those seeking abortion are needed to mitigate the potential widening of disparities in access and to support people’s reproductive autonomy now that more states have banned abortion.
