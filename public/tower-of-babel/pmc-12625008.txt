Relationship between C-reactive protein triglyceride glucose index and cardiovascular disease risk: a cross-sectional analysis with machine learning
Authors: Ruwen Zheng, Tianyi Wang, Min Liu, Xuedan Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular disease (CVD) continues to be a leading cause of disease burden and mortality worldwide. Identifying reliable biomarkers for CVD risk assessment is essential. This study investigates the association between the C-reactive protein–triglyceride–glucose (CTI) index and CVD, evaluating its potential value in CVD classification.MethodsThis study included 14,899 participants aged 20 years and older from the 1999–2020 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. Regression analysis and restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to examine the relationship between the CTI and CVD, along with its five specific outcomes. An interaction test assessed the impact of different subgroups on the association between CTI and CVD. Furthermore, the potential of CTI to assess CVD risk was evaluated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, decision curve analysis, and SHAP analysis, with machine learning models, including XGBoost, LASSO, random forest, support vector machine, and Naive Bayes, used for evaluation.ResultsFor each 1-unit higher CTI, the odds of having CVD were 18% higher (OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.10–1.28, P < 0.01). Specific associations include congestive heart failure (29%) (OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.14–1.47, P < 0.001), heart attack (29%) (OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.15–1.44, P < 0.001), coronary heart disease (20%) (OR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.07–1.35, P < 0.01), angina (21%) (OR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.06–1.36, P < 0.01), and stroke (13%) (OR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.01–1.27, P < 0.05). Age influences the association between CTI and CVD, with individuals under 60 years being more affected. Machine-learning models achieved AUC > 0.70, indicating moderate discriminatory ability; these findings suggest promising potential for risk stratification. SHAP analysis indicated that CTI showed larger SHAP contributions to CVD classification than CRP and TyG within our models.ConclusionHigher CTI levels were associated with higher odds of prevalent CVD, indicating that CTI may serve as a marker of CVD status and aid cross-sectional discrimination of prevalent CVD. Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to establish temporality and to evaluate whether CTI adds predictive value in longitudinal risk assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-025-03265-4.Keywords: CTI, Cardiovascular disease, NHANES, Cross-sectional study, Machine learning

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a broad category encompassing all disorders that affect the circulatory system, including the heart and vasculature, such as heart failure, stroke, and coronary heart disease (CHD) [1, 2]. The incidence of CVD has more than doubled from 1990 to 2019, and during the same period, global CVD mortality increased from 12.1 million deaths in 1990 to 18.6 million in 2019 [3]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), CVD is the leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for approximately one-third of all deaths [4]. With the aging global population, both the prevalence and mortality associated with CVD are expected to rise [5]. Consequently, minimizing, or even reducing, the prevalence of CVD—particularly in the context of rapid aging—has become a critical public health issue, presenting significant challenges to global health.

Adopting healthy lifestyles, such as regular physical activity and a balanced diet, can help mitigate the burden of CVD [6, 7]. Effective management of comorbid conditions, including hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease, is also crucial for reducing the risk of CVD [8–10]. Insulin resistance (IR), characterized by decreased sensitivity of target tissues (e.g., liver, skeletal muscle, and adipose tissue) to insulin, is a well-recognized correlate of CVD risk [11]. This condition impairs the ability to respond to normal insulin levels and disrupts glucose and lipid homeostasis, often leading to metabolic disturbances such as hyperglycemia, hypertension, and dyslipidemia, which collectively increase the risk of CVD [12, 13]. Recent studies have highlighted the Triglyceride-Glucose (TyG) index as a reliable marker of IR, with a strong correlation to the gold-standard hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp technique [14]. The TyG index has demonstrated favorable sensitivity and specificity in some studies compared with traditional indices such as HOMA-IR and is consistently linked to adverse cardiovascular outcomes [15–17]. However, the TyG index is not without its limitations. While it correlates well with IR, it does not fully capture the inflammatory components that contribute to CVD.

Inflammation is another critical risk factor for CVD, directly contributing to vascular inflammation and being positively correlated with the development of atherosclerosis (AS) [18, 19]. Among various inflammatory markers, C-reactive protein (CRP) is the most extensively studied and has emerged as a key biomarker for assessing CVD risk, given its strong association with both inflammation and the progression of CVD [20, 21]. However, CRP also has its limitations as a biomarker. While it is a reliable indicator of systemic inflammation, CRP alone does not directly reflect metabolic disturbances, such as IR, which are central to CVD development.

In recent years, the assessment of CVD risk has become increasingly important in clinical practice. One widely used tool for this purpose is the Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease (ASCVD) calculator, which estimates an individual’s 10-year risk of experiencing atherosclerotic cardiovascular events based on factors such as age, cholesterol levels, blood pressure, smoking status, and diabetes [22]. Although CRP and the TyG index are well-established independent risk factors for CVD, relying on a single biomarker does not provide sufficient predictive value for assessing CVD risk [23]. The C-reactive protein–triglyceride–glucose index (CTI), a novel composite measure combining CRP and TyG, has been proposed as a composite risk marker that captures both inflammatory status and metabolic disturbances, which are implicated in the pathogenesis of CVD [24]. CTI integrates two complementary biological axes. CRP reflects systemic inflammation, whereas the TyG index reflects metabolic dysregulation and insulin resistance; together they may address residual risk that neither marker captures alone.

Accordingly, CTI may aid risk stratification and screening by integrating complementary information from these pathways. Previous studies have reported associations between elevated CTI levels and the incidence of stroke [25]. However, there is a paucity of research exploring the quantitative relationship between CTI and overall CVD risk. Therefore, the primary objective of this study is to examine cross-sectional associations between CTI and CVD risk using data from the 1999–2020 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), to evaluate CTI’s potential utility as a risk marker, and to motivate future longitudinal investigations that can establish temporality and inform any intervention-oriented recommendations.

Methods

Study population

The data for this study were sourced from NHANES, a nationally representative health survey conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) under the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). NHANES employs a complex survey design with sampling weights, strata, and primary sampling units to address the multi-stage sampling methodology. These weights adjust for unequal selection probabilities, non-response, and post-stratification, ensuring that the sample accurately represents the U.S. population. NHANES aims to assess the health and nutritional status of U.S. adults and children through a combination of household interviews, physical examinations at Mobile Examination Centers (MEC), and laboratory tests, collecting multi-dimensional data on demographics, social factors, health behaviors, and nutrition to provide a comprehensive overview of the nation’s health [26, 27]. All participants provided informed consent, and the study protocol was reviewed and approved by the CDC and NCHS Institutional Review Board, in compliance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [28, 29].

For this analysis, we included participants aged ≥ 20 years from the 1999–2020 NHANES cycles, totaling 58,744 individuals. Participants who were pregnant at the time of examination, had incomplete data on CTI or CVD variables, or had missing covariate data were excluded. The final analysis included 14,899 eligible participants (see Fig. 1). See Supplementary Table S1 for comparisons of age, gender, and race between included and excluded participants.

Assessment of CTI and CVD

The CTI was calculated based on the formula established by previous studies [24]: CTI = 0.412 × Ln (CRP) + Ln (TG × FPG × 0.5), where CRP is measured in mg/L and reflects the level of inflammation in the body; triglycerides (TG) are measured in mg/dL and also reflect inflammation; and fasting plasma glucose (FPG) is measured in mg/dL and serves as an indicator of glucose metabolism.

The assessment of CVD in this study was based on self-reported data. Participants were asked, ‘Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you have Congestive Heart Failure (CHF), CHD, angina, heart attack, or stroke?’ If the answer to at least one of these questions was ‘yes,’ the participant was considered to have CVD [30].

Covariates

Consistent with prior studies. The covariates considered in this study included gender (male and female), age, race/ethnicity (Mexican American, other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, and other races), educational level (Below 9th grade, 9th-11th grade, High school graduate/General Educational Development (GED), Some college/AA degree, College graduate or higher), poverty-income ratio [31], Low-density lipoprotein (LDL; measured in fasting venous blood collected at the Mobile Examination Center after a 6–8 h fast), and body mass index (BMI), categorized as normal (< 25 kg/m²), overweight (25 to < 30 kg/m²), and obese (≥ 30 kg/m²).

Hypertension was diagnosed based on self-reports and whether participants were taking antihypertensive medications. Diabetes was defined by meeting any of the following criteria: self-reported diagnosis, HbA1c level ≥ 6.5%, Fasting Plasma Glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L after 8 h of fasting, 2-hour plasma glucose level ≥ 11.1 mmol/L in an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), or current use of antidiabetic medications or insulin [32]. Alcohol consumption was classified into two categories based on whether participants consumed at least 12 alcoholic drinks per year, with one drink defined as 12 ounces of beer, 5 ounces of wine, or 1.5 ounces of spirits [33]. Smoking status was divided into two groups: (1) Never smoker: participants who have smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime, and (2) Current/Former smoker: participants who have smoked 100 or more cigarettes in their lifetime.

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics for continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD, while categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages. Between-group differences for categorical variables were assessed using the χ² test, and the differences for continuous variables were evaluated using the Student’s t-test (for normally distributed data) or the Mann–Whitney U test (for non-normally distributed data). All analyses incorporated NHANES sampling weights and survey design variables (strata and primary sampling units).

Weighted logistic regression models were employed to assess the relationship between CTI levels and quartiles with CVD and specific cardiovascular outcomes. These models were adjusted for potential confounding factors in three stages: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for age, gender, and race), and Model 3 (adjusted for age, gender, race, education level, PIR, LDL, alcohol consumption, smoking status, BMI, hypertension, and diabetes).

Additionally, restricted cubic splines (RCS) analysis was performed to explore potential non-linear relationships between CTI levels and CVD or specific cardiovascular outcomes, as well as to assess the presence of any threshold effects. This analysis allows us to explore the possibility that the effect of CTI on cardiovascular outcomes may not follow a linear pattern across the full range of values. Interaction tests were conducted to evaluate whether the association between CTI and CVD varied across different subgroups. The results of these interaction analyses were visualized using forest plots to facilitate interpretation of subgroup differences.

A total of 14,899 participants were included, with 10,430 assigned to the training set and 4,469 to the test set (a stratified 70/30 split by CVD status to preserve outcome prevalence). To mitigate overfitting, we applied 10-fold cross-validation on the training set (identical folds across algorithms); in each iteration, one-fold served as validation and the remaining nine as training, repeated until every fold had been used once. We assessed robustness by testing multiple random seeds during split randomization to ensure results were not sensitive to a single split. After tuning, models were refit on the full training set and evaluated once on the held-out test set. All five algorithms—XGBoost, LASSO, Random Forest, SVM, and Naive Bayes—used the same training/test split and cross-validation scheme for fair comparison. Detailed cross-validation settings and hyperparameter search grids are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Model performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves; AUC > 0.70 was interpreted as indicating acceptable discrimination in this dataset. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to estimate net benefit across threshold probabilities within this cross-sectional sample; findings provide an exploratory appraisal of potential clinical usefulness rather than prospective performance. Finally, we used SHAP values to interpret the contribution of individual features to the model’s evaluation of CVD risk.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3). A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Weighted logistic regression results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

A total of 14,899 participants were included in this study. Based on CVD status, participants were divided into two groups: those with CVD (1,614 participants) and those without CVD (13,285 participants). As shown in Table 1, there were significant differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups (P < 0.001). Compared with those without CVD, participants with prevalent CVD were older and more often male; they also had higher CTI levels, greater smoking exposure, and higher prevalences of hypertension, diabetes, and obesity.

Weighted logistic regression models were used to assess the association between CTI levels and its quartiles with CVD (Table 2). In the fully adjusted Model 3, CTI was significantly positively associated with CVD: for each 1-unit higher CTI, the odds of having CVD were 18% higher (OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.10–1.28, P < 0.001). Additionally, compared with Q1, participants in Q4 had 38% higher odds of CVD (OR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.13–1.68, P < 0.01).

Interaction test

Figure 3 presents the results of an interaction test conducted across different subgroups to examine the relationship between CTI and CVD. This analysis considered various subgroup characteristics, including age, gender, alcohol consumption, smoking status, BMI, and the presence of hypertension and diabetes. A significant interaction between CTI and CVD was observed only in the age subgroup (interaction P < 0.05). In contrast, no significant interaction was found in the other subgroups (interaction P > 0.05).

Development and validation of machine learning models for assessing CVD risk using CTI

Figure 4 displays ROC curves for five models trained using CTI together with age, gender, race, education level, PIR, LDL, alcohol use, smoking status, BMI, hypertension, and diabetes. Test-set AUC indicated moderate-to-good performance for CVD status assessment, with Naive Bayes performing best (AUC = 0.825) and XGBoost, LASSO, and Random Forest showing comparable performance (AUC = 0.819); the SVM was slightly lower but remained above 0.70. Supplementary Figure S1 presents ROC results after excluding CTI; across all five algorithms, AUC decreased, indicating that including CTI improves model performance for CVD status assessment. Figure 5 shows DCA comparing the model-based strategies with the “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies across threshold probabilities. Consistent with a low-prevalence setting, the treat-all strategy dominates at very low thresholds. Within clinically plausible thresholds for primary prevention (approximately 0.10–0.40), the model-based strategies yield positive net benefit and generally outperform both extremes.

Interpretability of machine learning models for CVD risk assessment using SHAP values

To examine the relative contributions of study variables to classification of prevalent CVD in this cross-sectional dataset, we applied SHAP to the Naive Bayes model, which achieved the highest AUC among the candidate algorithms. For visualization, Fig. 6 displays SHAP results from an exploratory expanded feature set comprising CTI, CRP, TyG, and the prespecified covariates (age, gender, race, education level, PIR, LDL, alcohol use, smoking status, BMI, hypertension, and diabetes). Figure 6A shows the mean absolute SHAP values, ranking variables by their overall contribution to prevalent CVD classification. Figure 6B presents beeswarm plots illustrating how low-to-high feature values influence the model output. Age and hypertension showed the largest contributions, consistent with clinical knowledge. Within this exploratory model, CTI had a higher mean SHAP value than either CRP or TyG, suggesting that the composite may capture more information than either component alone; however, causal inference cannot be made from a cross-sectional analysis, and prospective cohort studies are needed for confirmation.

Discussion

This study systematically examined the relationship between CTI and CVD, as well as its specific outcomes. Our findings show that higher CTI levels are associated with higher odds of prevalent CVD. Specifically, for each 1-unit increase in CTI, the odds of having CVD were 18% higher, the odds of CHF and heart attack were 29% higher, the odds of CHD were 20% higher, the odds of angina were 21% higher, and the odds of stroke were 13% higher. RCS analyses were consistent with a significant linear association between CTI and the aforementioned cardiovascular outcomes.

In contrast, age was found to be the only subgroup showing evidence of effect modification, with the association between CTI and prevalent CVD appearing stronger in individuals aged < 60 years. Although CVD is traditionally thought to be more common in older individuals, the incidence of early-onset CHD and heart attacks in younger age groups (especially < 50 years) has increased significantly in recent years. This trend has been associated with younger individuals who have risk factors such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and obesity [34, 35]. In addition, IR and inflammatory responses in younger individuals may be more likely to lead to metabolic disorders and increased cardiovascular risk [36]. These considerations may help explain the stronger association observed for those aged < 60 years.

Previous studies have found a significant positive linear relationship between CTI and the incidence of stroke [25], which is consistent with our findings. Furthermore, using machine-learning models, we evaluated the discriminatory performance for prevalent CVD associated with CTI. These models were consistent with the association between CTI and CVD, with AUC >0.70 indicating moderate discrimination for classifying prevalent CVD. Decision curve analysis suggested potential net benefit in this cross-sectional sample, particularly for individuals with a lower predicted risk of CVD (threshold probability < 0.4). Prospective studies with external validation are needed to assess generalizability and clinical utility. Additionally, SHAP analyses indicated that CTI contributed more to model predictions than CRP or TyG individually. In practice, CTI could help prioritize individuals for further evaluation, while integration with established risk models (e.g., Framingham Risk Score or ASCVD calculator) may improve risk stratification; intervention decisions should be guided by prospective evidence rather than cross-sectional associations.

TyG is a key marker for evaluating IR, and elevated TyG levels have been associated with an increased risk of CVD, with this association being particularly stronger when combined with obesity indicators, such as TyG-BMI [37]. Thus, the relationship between TyG and CVD reflects the broader connection between IR and CVD. AS is a major cause of CVD, and inflammation and endothelial dysfunction are implicated in its progression [38]. In IR, the response of endothelial cells to insulin may be diminished, potentially impairing the regulation of nitric oxide (NO) production, which may contribute to reduced vascular relaxation and endothelial dysfunction [39]. IR has been linked to increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which may contribute to inflammatory cascades and vascular injury [40]. Given the cross-sectional design of our study, we cannot establish temporality or infer causality; longitudinal investigations are warranted.

Furthermore, IR is closely associated with the classic lipid triad (high TG, low high-density lipoprotein, and small-dense LDL), which accelerates lipid deposition in the vascular wall, exacerbates AS, and promotes the development of CVD [40]. Increased levels of free fatty acids in IR activate signaling pathways like protein kinase C and nuclear factor kappa B, enhancing reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, reducing NO availability, and ultimately contributing to vascular dysfunction and endothelial injury [41]. Overall, IR may contribute to CVD predominantly through pathways involving AS and endothelial dysfunction— including endothelial cell dysfunction, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic disturbances.

CRP, an acute-phase reactant protein primarily synthesized by the liver, responds to inflammatory signals, especially those triggered by cytokines such as Tumor Necrosis Factor Alpha (TNF-α), Interleukin-6 (IL-6), and Interleukin-1 Beta (IL-1β) [42]. As a biomarker of systemic inflammation, CRP reflects sustained inflammatory states, which are pivotal in the development of AS and CVD [43]. Beyond being a marker of inflammation, CRP also directly drives CVD progression by promoting the formation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, fostering plaque growth and instability, and contributing to thrombosis [44]. CRP activates endothelial adhesion molecules, facilitating leukocyte adhesion and migration, and increases ROS generation, inducing oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction, thus accelerating the pathological progression of CVD [45].

Furthermore, there is a complex bidirectional relationship between inflammation and IR. On one hand, inflammatory cytokines interfere with insulin signaling, activate toll-like receptors, and induce oxidative stress, thereby impairing insulin signaling and leading to IR [46]. On the other hand, IR promotes the polarization of macrophages toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, which secretes large amounts of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α), further driving the inflammatory response [47]. This vicious cycle exacerbates both IR and inflammation, accelerating the development of metabolic disorders.

Despite the significant contributions of this study, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the reliance on self-reported CVD outcomes introduces potential bias, as self-reported data are prone to misreporting or underreporting of events. This could affect the accuracy and validity of the findings. Second, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to infer causal relationships, and longitudinal studies are needed to explore the temporal association between CTI and the development of CVD. Third, despite adjustment for multiple sociodemographic and clinical covariates, residual confounding remains possible because several key cardiovascular risk determinants—habitual physical activity, dietary quality, medication use (e.g., lipid-lowering and antihypertensive therapy), and family history—were unavailable in the analytic dataset. To the extent that these factors are associated with both CTI and CVD, effect estimates may be biased upward (if higher CTI co-occurs with adverse lifestyles) or downward owing to treatment-related confounding (confounding by indication). Future work should incorporate these variables and, where they remain unmeasured, apply quantitative bias analyses (e.g., E-values or probabilistic sensitivity analyses) to gauge the magnitude and direction of potential bias. Finally, although our machine-learning models and SHAP summaries were informative, generalizability and clinical transportability should be established through external validation in independent, prospectively ascertained cohorts.

Additionally, the U.S.-based sample used in this study may limit the generalizability of the findings to populations with different racial/ethnic composition or demographic profiles. Further research should explore the potential clinical applications of CTI, including identifying clinically informative thresholds and evaluating its incremental value alongside existing cardiovascular risk prediction tools, such as the Framingham Risk Score or the ASCVD calculator. Moreover, future studies should aim to evaluate the utility of CTI in more diverse populations and examine whether strategies targeting inflammatory and metabolic pathways reflected by CTI are associated with improved cardiovascular outcomes in prospective studies.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study observed a significant association between CTI and CVD, with higher CTI levels associated with higher odds of prevalent CVD. Machine learning models showed moderate discrimination for classifying prevalent CVD using CTI in this dataset; findings are exploratory, require external validation in independent cohorts, and CTI performed better than TyG or CRP alone within this dataset. However, due to the cross-sectional design, we cannot infer temporality or causality, and longitudinal studies are needed to explore the long-term prognostic value of CTI for CVD outcomes. Further research should also assess the incremental value of adding CTI to existing CVD risk prediction tools (such as the Framingham Risk Score or ASCVD calculator), using discrimination, calibration, reclassification, and decision-analytic measures.
