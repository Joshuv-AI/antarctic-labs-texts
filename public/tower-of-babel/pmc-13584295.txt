From M1/M2 polarization to disease-associated microglial states: refining the role of NIK in diabetic retinopathy
Authors: Jing Chen
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

I read with interest the recent study by Li et al. [1], who linked NF-κB-inducing kinase (NIK) activation to microglial inflammatory responses and blood-retinal barrier (BRB) dysfunction in diabetic retinopathy (DR). Using retinal transcriptomics, AGE-treated HMC3 cells, NIK knockdown, microglia-endothelial co-culture, and pharmacological inhibition with B022, the authors provide evidence that NIK participates in inflammatory signaling relevant to retinal vascular injury. The main issue, however, is how these effects should be interpreted biologically. The study frames them largely as an M1/M2 polarization shift, yet the authors’ own marker data do not show the reciprocal pattern expected under a simple binary model. That pattern is important because it arises from the study itself, not from a nomenclature debate imposed from outside. Together with current microglial state concepts and recent DR single-cell data [2–5], the findings suggest that NIK may remodel disease-associated microglial states rather than simply move cells along a binary M1/M2 axis. This distinction changes both the proposed role of NIK and the therapeutic question that follows.

The strongest reason for this reinterpretation is the reported marker pattern. In AGE-treated HMC3 cells, CD68-positive and CD206-positive cells both increased, while higher TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels were accompanied by increased IL-10 [1]. Concurrent changes in markers assigned to opposing phenotypes are not what would be expected from a simple reciprocal switch. They are more consistent with overlapping or coexisting activation programs. At the population level, these measurements cannot resolve whether apparently opposing markers are co-expressed in the same cells, arise from distinct cellular subsets, or reflect transitions between transcriptional states; in retinal tissue, bulk measurements also cannot separate resident microglia from infiltrating myeloid cells. Nor does a change in a small set of canonical markers define a complete transcriptional state. Accordingly, the reduction in M1-associated markers and increase in M2-associated markers after NIK inhibition support a shift in inflammatory phenotype, but do not by themselves establish conversion between fixed M1 and M2 identities. The authors’ results are therefore compatible with a mixed activation landscape and raise the possibility that NIK affects one or more disease-associated microglial programs.

Single-cell studies in DR provide relevant context for this interpretation. Current microglial nomenclature emphasizes multidimensional, context-dependent states rather than fixed M1/M2 identities [2]. Wang et al. reported marked microglial heterogeneity and dynamic subtype changes in early experimental DR [3]. Geng et al. identified four microglial subtypes in an NPDR rat model, including an SPP1-high population associated with oxidative stress, apoptosis, and inner BRB injury [4]. In human proliferative DR, Gu et al. described MARCO-positive microglia with pro-angiogenic and pro-fibrotic properties [5]. These studies differ in species, tissue source, and disease stage and do not define a single longitudinal trajectory. Nor do they establish NIK as the driver of these states. They do, however, show why a binary framework may miss biologically relevant heterogeneity in DR. This matters because changes in state abundance and changes within a state are biologically different and could imply different therapeutic effects. The more precise question raised by Li et al. is therefore whether NIK changes the abundance, persistence, or transcriptional program of selected disease-associated microglial states that interact with retinal endothelial cells and contribute to BRB dysfunction.

This distinction also affects therapeutic interpretation. If a state-based model is correct, the goal of NIK inhibition would not simply be to suppress “M1” and promote “M2,” but to reduce harmful NIK-dependent programs while preserving homeostatic or reparative microglial functions. Moreover, because B022 was delivered intravitreally without microglia-specific targeting, improved BRB integrity cannot establish that retinal microglial NIK is the cell-specific mediator of the in vivo effect [1]. The pharmacological benefit observed in this model supports further evaluation of NIK as a therapeutic target, but is not equivalent to microglia-specific causality. A practical next step would be to compare the existing retinal transcriptomic data with published DR microglial state signatures, followed by single-cell or spatial transcriptomic profiling after NIK inhibition. Microglia-restricted Map3k14/NIK manipulation, combined with lineage-resolved analysis, would more directly test cell-specific causality and distinguish resident microglia from infiltrating macrophages. Such work would not diminish the significance of the original findings. It could refine the role of NIK as a regulator of pathogenic microglial state remodeling and provide a more precise framework for NIK-directed therapy in DR.
