Introduction: balance theorems

Probabilities associated with uniform distribution on

Following a standard abuse, we identify a poset

We use

Our first goal in this paper is to say something new

Possibly the best known of all poset problems

Conjecture 1.1
.

If the finite poset P is not a chain then

(1)

This was first proposed by Kislitsyn

This existence was shown

What’s particularly striking about

There has also been a substantial amount of work proving Conjecture

While Conjecture

3.
Geometry

We use
body
to mean a full-dimensional compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}

(for some
d
),
and denote Euclidean volume by
|\cdot|
, with dimension
given by context.

We will be interested in two polytopes associated with
P
. The first of these,
the
order polytope
, is

{\mathcal{O}}(P)=\{v\in[0,1]^{P}:x<y\Rightarrow v_{x}\leq v_{y}\},

and the second, the
chain polytope
, will be discussed shortly.
(See
[
40
]
for background on both.)
We will usually capitalize
continuous counterparts of discrete quantities, so to begin use

F
for a uniform point of
{\mathcal{O}}(P)
.

The extensions of
P
naturally decompose

{\mathcal{O}}(P)
into simplices of volume
1/n!
; namely,
f\in E(P)
, say with
f(x_{i})=i

corresponds to the simplex

(15)

\{0\leq v_{x_{1}}\leq\cdots\leq v_{x_{n}}\leq 1\},

yielding in particular
\mathbb{P}(F(x)<F(y))=\mathbb{P}(f(x)<f(y))
and

(16)

|{\mathcal{O}}(P)|=e(P)/n!

If we think of choosing
f
and then
F
uniformly from the corresponding simplex,
then we see that
\mathbb{E}[F(x_{i})|f]=i/(n+1)
and the centroid of
{\mathcal{O}}(P)
is

(17)

H(x):=\mathbb{E}F(x)=\mathbb{E}f(x)/(n+1)=h(x)/(n+1).

We also use

(18)

Q(x)=\max_{y<x}F(y)~
and
~R(x)=\min_{y>x}F(y)

(with
Q(x)=0
if
x
is minimal and
R(x)=1
if
x
is maximal; cf. (
7
)), and

(19)

{\rm Win}(x)=\mathbb{E}[R(x)-Q(x)]=\mathbb{E}[r(x)-q(x)]/(n+1)={\rm win}(x)/(n+1).

For a given setting of
(F(z):z\neq x)
,
F(x)
is uniform from
[Q(x),R(x)]
, whence

(20)

H(x)=\mathbb{E}[Q(x)+R(x)]/2

—in particular

(21)

H(x)={\rm Win}(x)/2~
if
~x\in\min(P)
,

since in this case
Q(x)\equiv 0
—and

(22)

\mathbb{E}[F(x)-Q(x)]=\mathbb{E}[R(x)-F(x)]={\rm Win}(x)/2.

Moreover, since
\xi
chosen uniformly from
[0,a]
satisfies
\mathbb{E}|\xi-b|\geq a/4
(for any
b
) and

{\rm var}(\xi)=a^{2}/12
, we have

(23)

\mathbb{E}|F(x)-F(y)|\geq\mathbb{E}[R(x)-Q(x)]/4={\rm Win}(x)/4

(equivalently,
\mathbb{E}|f(x)-f(y)|\geq{\rm win}(x)/4
) for any
y\neq x
, and

(24)

{\rm Var}(F(x))\geq\mathbb{E}\{{\rm Var}[F(x)|Q(x),R(x)]\}\geq\mathbb{E}(R(x)-Q(x))^{2}/12\geq{\rm Win}^{2}(x)/12.

The discrete version of (
24
) is

(25)

\sigma^{2}(x)\geq({\rm win}(x)-1)^{2})/12,

which justifies
our assertion (in (
11
)) that
{\rm win}\leadsto\sigma
.
(The justification is not immediate from (
24
), due to the last expression in

(26)

{\rm Var}(F(x))=\frac{\sigma^{2}(x)}{(n+1)(n+2)}+\frac{H(x)(1-H(x))}{n+2}

[routine derivation omitted], which roughly corresponds to the residual variance in
F(X)
given
f(x)
.)

Remark.

Though we don’t have a concrete use for it at the moment,
the following counterpart of (
24
)
seems basic,
but has proved remarkably difficult to attack.

Conjecture 3.1
.

There is a fixed
\varepsilon>0
such that for every
P
and x,
{\rm Win}(x)>\varepsilon{\rm Var}(F(x))
.

This is best possible (up to the value of
\varepsilon
;
see also Conjecture
12.8
) when
P
is a chain with
x
not too near the ends.
On the other hand, we don’t even know that one can’t simultaneously have

{\rm Var}(F(x))=\Omega(1)
and
{\rm Win}(x)=O(1/n)
(respectively the
largest and smallest possible values of these parameters).

We now turn to the chain polytope,

{\mathcal{C}}(P)=\{v\in[0,1]^{P}:\mbox{$\sum_{x\in C}v_{x}\leq 1$ for every chain $C$ of $P$}\}.

Though first considered as an object of particular interest
only in
[
40
]
,
{\mathcal{C}}(P)
is
also the case
G=G(P)
of the
vertex-packing polytope
of a graph
G
,

(27)

{\rm conv}(\{\mathbbm{1}_{I}:I\in{\mathcal{I}}(G)\})\subseteq[0,1]^{V(G)},

where
{\mathcal{I}}(G)
is the collection of independent sets of
G

(e.g.
{\mathcal{I}}(G(P))={\mathcal{A}}(P)
).

In what follows we will sometimes work with the more general
notion of a
convex corner
(we will say simply
corner
);
that is, a compact convex
\mathcal{K}\subseteq(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{n}

with nonempty interior, satisfying

\underline{0}\leq v\leq\underline{w}\in\mathcal{K}

\Rightarrow

v\in\mathcal{K}

(where “
\leq
” is product order).
Where valid, we will usually give results for corners, since
this costs nothing and may be helpful in showing what we actually use.
(Of course a reader may just think of
{\mathcal{C}}(P)
.)
In all cases the ground set is
V
, with
|V|=n
;
x
,
y
are in
V

and
v
is in
\mathbb{R}^{V}
;
and, with
\mathcal{K}
the body in question,

c
is the centroid of
\mathcal{K}
and

d=(d_{x}:x\in V)
is given by

(28)

d_{x}=|\mathcal{K}|/|\mathcal{K}-x|,

where
\mathcal{K}-x
is the intersection of
\mathcal{K}
with the hyperplane
\{v_{x}=0\}
.

For posets, a basic observation of Stanley
[
40
]
is that there is a
bijective, piecewise-linear, volume-preserving
“transfer map”
F\mapsto F^{*}
from
{\mathcal{O}}(P)
to

{\mathcal{C}}(P)
given by
F^{*}(x)=F(x)-Q(x)
. This implies

(29)

|{\mathcal{C}}(P)|=|{\mathcal{O}}(P)|\,\,(=e(P)/n!),

says that the centroid of
{\mathcal{C}}(P)
is given by

(30)

c_{x}={\rm Win}(x)/2

(see (
22
)), and gives an alternate expression for
d
:

(31)

d_{x}=|{\mathcal{C}}(P)|/|{\mathcal{C}}(P-x)|=e(P)/(ne(P-x)).

As we will see in Lemma
5.3
,

d
turns out to be a good proxy for

, so also for Win if
\mathcal{K}={\mathcal{C}}(P)
.

4.
Log-concavity

The geometric viewpoint gets much of its power from the tractability of
log-concave functions. Here we briefly review what we need from
this subject. (See also the lucid account in
[
31
]
.)

Recall that
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
is
log-concave
if

g(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\geq g(x)^{\lambda}g(y)^{1-\lambda}

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
.
We call a random
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

lc-distributed

if its density function is log-concave.
We also say a random
\mathbb{Z}
-valued
f
is lc-distributed
if the sequence
\{\mathbb{P}(f=k)\}
is log-concave; that is,

\mathbb{P}(f=k)^{2}\geq\mathbb{P}(f=k-1)\mathbb{P}(f=k+1)
for all
k
.

The following version of the Prékopa-Leindler Inequality,
is contained in Theorem 5.1 of
[
31
]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

If
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
is log-concave, then its projection on
any subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is log-concave.

(We will use only one-dimensional projections, but see
[
20
]

for a higher-dimensional poset application.)

For the rest of this section
Z
is an
\mathbb{R}
-valued
r.v. with density
g
.
The first part of the next lemma is trivial and the second is
contained in
[
31
, Lemma 5.3(c,d)]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For nonnegative
Z
:

(a)
If
g
is nonincreasing on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
, then

\mathbb{E}Z\geq 1/(2g(0))
.

(b)

If g is log-concave then

\mathbb{E}Z\leq 1/g(0)
and
\mathbb{E}^{2}Z\leq 2\mathbb{E}^{2}Z.

We briefly depart from geometry
to
recall two observations in the same vein but for the discrete setting.
(These are standard, but we don’t know a reference.)

Proposition 4.3
.

If
f
takes values in the positive integers
and is lc-distributed, then

(a)

\mathbb{E}f\leq 1/\mathbb{P}(f=1)
;

(b)

{\rm Var}(f)=O((\max_{k}\mathbb{P}(f(x)=k))^{-2})
.

We return to
Z
.
The next result (perhaps in more precise form) is presumably not new.

Lemma 4.4
.

If Z is lc-distributed then for any
\varepsilon
,

\mathbb{P}(|Z|\leq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}|Z|/3)\leq\varepsilon
.

Proof.

It is enough to show this when
Z
has compact support and
\mathbb{E}|Z|=1
;
so we assume this,
let
Z
maximize
\mathbb{P}(|Z|\leq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}|Z|/3)
,
and
use
p
for the density of
Z
(so
\log p
is concave).
We claim that
\log p
is linear on each of
(-\infty,-\varepsilon/3]
,
[\varepsilon/3,\infty)
.
For example, if the second linearity fails, then we may replace
p
by a
density
q
that agrees with
p
on
(-\infty,\varepsilon/3)
, while on
[\varepsilon/3,\infty)
,
\log q

is linear and
q
has the same integral as
p
.
Then
Z^{\prime}
with density
q
has
\mathbb{E}|Z^{\prime}|>\mathbb{E}|Z|=1
and

\mathbb{P}(|Z^{\prime}|\leq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}|Z^{\prime}|/3)>\mathbb{P}(|Z^{\prime}|\leq\varepsilon/3)=\mathbb{P}(|Z|\leq\varepsilon/3)
,
contradicting our choice of
Z
.

Let
p(t_{0})=\max_{t}p(t)
; by the above linearity,
t_{0}\in[-\varepsilon/3,\varepsilon/3]
.
If
p(t_{0})\leq 3/2
, then

\mathbb{P}\left(|Z|\leq\varepsilon/3\right)\leq(3/2)(2\varepsilon/3)=\varepsilon;

and otherwise, Lemma
4.2
(b) gives another contradiction:

\mathbb{E}|Z|\leq|t_{0}|+\mathbb{E}|Z-t_{0}|\leq|t_{0}|+1/p(t_{0})\leq\varepsilon/3+2/3<1.

∎

The following fundamental fact is Lemma 5.4 of
[
31
]
.

Theorem 4.5
.

For any lc-distributed
Z
,
\mathbb{P}(Z\geq\mathbb{E}Z)\geq 1/e
.

In view of Theorem
4.1
, this generalizes
the result of Grünbaum mentioned in Section
1
,
which we will also use below
and now state formally:

Theorem 4.6
.

For a body
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
H^{+}

one side of a hyperplane H through the centroid of K,

|H^{+}\cap K|\geq|K|/e.

The following variant of Theorem
4.5
will be crucial (see Lemma
5.7
).

Lemma 4.7
.

If
\mathbb{E}|Z|\gg|\mathbb{E}Z|
then
\mathbb{P}(Z>0)~~\mbox{\raisebox{-3.87495pt}{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textstyle{>}}}{{\sim}}$}}~~1/e
.

Proof.

We first observe that the hypothesis of the lemma implies
\sigma_{Z}\gg|\mathbb{E}Z|
, since

\sigma_{Z}^{2}=\mathbb{E}|Z|^{2}-\mathbb{E}^{2}Z\geq\mathbb{E}^{2}|Z|-\mathbb{E}^{2}Z\sim\mathbb{E}^{2}|Z|\gg\mathbb{E}^{2}Z.

So it’s enough to show

(32)

\sigma_{Z}\gg|\mathbb{E}Z|\,\,\Rightarrow\,\,\mathbb{P}(Z>0)~~\mbox{\raisebox{-3.87495pt}{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textstyle{>}}}{{\sim}}$}}~~1/e.

Here
Theorem
4.5
says we may assume
\mu:=\mathbb{E}Z<0
, and that, with

I=[\mu,0]
, it’s enough to show

\mathbb{P}(Z\in I)\ll 1.

To see this, set
\mathbb{P}(Z\in I)=\gamma
and
let
x_{0}
maximize
g
on
I
(so
g(x_{0})\geq-\gamma/\mu
).
With
h
the restriction of
g
to
[x_{0},\infty)

scaled to integrate to 1,
Lemma
4.2
gives

E[Z^{2}|Z\geq x_{0}]\leq 2\mathbb{E}^{2}[Z|Z\geq x_{0}]\leq 2h(x_{0})^{-2}\leq 2g(x_{0})^{-2}\leq 2(\mu/\gamma)^{2},

and the same argument (with a different
h
) gives
E[Z^{2}|Z\leq x_{0}]\leq 2(\mu/\gamma)^{2}
.
So
\sigma_{Z}\leq\sqrt{2}|\mu|/\gamma
,
which with

\sigma_{Z}\gg|\mu|

(as in (
32
))
gives
the desired
\gamma=o(1)
.

∎

Observation 9.2

10.
A more general Komlós Theorem

This will be brief since the main ideas are already in
[
24
]
.

Proof of Theorem
8.1
.

To avoid pointless precision, we fix
\eta>0
significantly smaller than
\varepsilon^{2}

and use
a\approx b
for
|a-b|=O(\eta)
(with the implied constant independent of
\varepsilon
).

An easy application of Ramsey’s Theorem allows us to assume,
with
M
as large as needed, that

there are numbers
r(\underline{j})
for
\underline{j}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell})\in[K]^{k}
such that
for all
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}
in
[M]
,

(54)

\mathbb{P}(X_{i_{\ell}}=j_{\ell}\,\,\forall\ell\in[k])\approx r(\underline{j}).

It is then enough to show that (for large enough
M
)

r(\underline{j})\approx r(j_{\pi(1)},\ldots,j_{\pi(k)})\,\,\,\forall\underline{j}\in[K]^{k}
and
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{[k]}
,

or, since adjacent transpositions generate
\mathfrak{S}_{[k]}

(here “adjacent” is just notationally convenient),

(55)

\mbox{$r(\underline{j})\approx r(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s-1},j_{s+1},j_{s},j_{s+2},\ldots,j_{k})\,\,\,\forall\underline{j}\in[K]^{k}$ and $s\in[k-1]$}.

Proof of (
55
).

We may assume
M=km
and let
I_{1}\cup\cdots\cup I_{k}
partition
[M]

into intervals of size
m
. Set

a(\ell,j)=m^{-1}\sum_{i\in I_{\ell}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{i}=j\}}

and notice that (
54
) implies

(56)

\mathbb{E}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}a(\ell,j_{\ell})\approx r(\underline{j})\,\,\,\forall\underline{j}\in[K]^{k}.

Fix
\underline{j}
and set
\psi=\prod\{a(\ell,j_{\ell}):\ell\in[k]\setminus\{s,s+1\}\}
.
A second application of (
54
) gives, for any
j
,

(57)

\mathbb{E}[\psi\cdot(a(s,j)-a(s+1,j))^{2}]=O(\eta).

[
Because (briefly)
: The l.h.s. is

m^{-k}\left\{\sum^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[\psi\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{i}=X_{i^{\prime}}=j\}}]-\sum^{\prime\prime}\mathbb{E}[\psi\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{i}=X_{i^{\prime}}=j\}}]\right\},

where the
(i,i^{\prime})
’s in
\sum^{\prime}
range over
I_{s}^{2}\cup I_{s+1}^{2}
and those in

\sum^{\prime\prime}
over
(I_{s}\times I_{s+1})\cup(I_{s+1}\times I_{s})
.
Of the
m^{k}
terms in each sum, all but the diagonal terms in
\sum^{\prime}
are
within
O(\eta)
of
r(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s-1},j,j,j_{s+2},\ldots,j_{k})
.
This gives (
57
) if we pretend
all
terms are non-diagonal;
and if, say,
m>1/\eta
, the effect of the
2m^{k-1}

diagonal terms is negligible.]

For (
55
) we now have,
setting
\underline{j}^{\prime}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s-1},j_{s+1},j_{s},j_{s+2},\ldots,j_{k})
,
writing
\|f\|
for
\sqrt{\mathbb{E}f^{2}}
, and with the initial “
\approx
” given by (
56
):

\displaystyle|r(\underline{j})-r(\underline{j}^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}\left[\psi\cdot\left\{a(s,j_{s})a(s+1,j_{s+1})-a(s,j_{s+1})a(s+1,j_{s})\right\}\right]|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\psi\cdot\left\{a(s,j_{s})(a(s+1,j_{s+1})-a(s,j_{s+1}))\right\}|

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,+\mathbb{E}|\psi\cdot\left\{a(s,j_{s+1})(a(s,j_{s})-a(s+1,j_{s}))\right\}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{\psi}\cdot a(s,j_{s})\|\|\sqrt{\psi}(a(s+1,j_{s+1})-a(s,j_{s+1}))\|

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,+\|\sqrt{\psi}\cdot a(s,j_{s+1})\|\|\sqrt{\psi}(a(s,j_{s})-a(s+1,j_{s}))\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{\psi}(a(s+1,j_{s+1})-a(s,j_{s+1}))\|+\|\sqrt{\psi}(a(s,j_{s})-a(s+1,j_{s}))\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(\sqrt{\eta})

(where the last “
\leq
” holds because
\psi
and
a(\cdot,\cdot)
are
in
[0,1]
), and the final bound follows from (
57
).

∎

Example 11.1
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there is a poset
