A quasi-experimental study on conservative nursing measures for preventing phlebitis in chemotherapy patients
Authors: Warda Mohamed Henedy, Fatmah Ahmed Alamoudi, Salwa Abd El Gawad Sallam, Mahmoud Abdel Hameed Shahin, Gehan Elmadbouh, Amany Tawfik Elfakhrany, Shereen Hussein Deep
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhlebitis is a common and potentially serious complication among cancer patients receiving peripheral intravenous chemotherapy, leading to patient discomfort, treatment interruptions, and increased healthcare costs. Preventative nursing interventions, including normal saline flush, cold application, and magnesium sulfate application, have shown promise in reducing the incidence and severity of phlebitis. However, evidence regarding their effectiveness remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of conservative nursing measures on the incidence of phlebitis among patients undergoing chemotherapy. A quasi-experimental post-test-only control group design was conducted at the oncology department of the Oncology Institute of Menoufia University Hospital. A convenience sample of 100 adult male and female patients on chemotherapy was randomly assigned to either the intervention group (n = 50), receiving the conservative nursing interventions, or the control group (n = 50), receiving standard care. Phlebitis severity was assessed using the Visual Infusion Phlebitis scale over three consecutive days post-intervention. The findings revealed a significant reduction in phlebitis severity in the intervention group compared to the control group on the second and third post-intervention days, as measured by the Visual Infusion Phlebitis scale (p < 0.01). The incidence of phlebitis was also markedly lower in the intervention group compared to the control group (12% vs. 38% on day two; 10% vs. 34% on day three). Additionally, the first occurrence of phlebitis occurred significantly later in the intervention group (37.28 ± 19.98 h) than in the control group (22.52 ± 16.11 h). Catheterization patency duration was also prolonged in the intervention group compared to the control group (69.48 ± 9.58 h vs. 52.50 ± 23.20 h), indicating improved catheter site viability. In conclusion, conservative nursing measures significantly reduce the incidence and severity of phlebitis in chemotherapy patients. Incorporating these interventions into routine oncology nursing care may enhance patient safety, treatment adherence, and overall clinical outcomes.Keywords: Cryotherapy, Chemotherapy, Conservative treatment, Nursing care, Intravenous infusions, PhlebitisSubject terms: Cancer, Cancer, Diseases, Health care, Oncology

Introduction

Cancer continues to represent a critical global health challenge, with incidence and mortality rates steadily rising each year1. In the United States alone, the American Cancer Society projects that cancer cases will exceed 2,001,140 in 2024, up from 1,958,310 in 2023 1,2. Several factors contribute to this increase, including an aging population, genetic susceptibility, environmental exposures, and lifestyle-related risks such as smoking, unhealthy diets, and sedentary behavior3. Similarly, in Egypt, cancer has emerged as a major public health concern, with more than 320,000 patients receiving government-funded treatment in 2019 4.

Among available treatment modalities, chemotherapy remains a cornerstone for both curative and palliative care in oncology5. While initially perceived as selectively targeting malignant cells, chemotherapy is now understood to impact healthy tissues as well, leading to a spectrum of adverse effects, including fatigue, nausea, vomiting, hair loss, depression, and phlebitis6,7. Phlebitis, or inflammation of the vein, is particularly troubling for patients receiving chemotherapy via peripheral intravenous (IV) catheters. Its occurrence is linked to mechanical trauma during catheter insertion, chemical irritation from cytotoxic drugs, and potential bacterial contamination due to inadequate aseptic technique7.

Reported incidence rates of phlebitis among chemotherapy patients vary widely, ranging from 20 to 80% 8. This variability stems from multiple factors, including medication type, catheter size, insertion technique, and patient-specific characteristics9,10. Clinically, phlebitis presents with local pain, erythema, edema, warmth, and, in severe cases, palpable venous cords and thrombosis11,12. If left untreated, phlebitis can escalate to serious complications, including bloodstream infections and deep vein thrombosis, ultimately leading to chemotherapy interruption, extended hospital stays, and increased healthcare costs13.

Given these risks, nurses play a pivotal role in both preventing and managing phlebitis. Recognizing risk factors, conducting thorough assessments, and ensuring continuous monitoring during and after catheterization are integral to nursing practice7,14. Early detection and timely intervention can reduce the incidence and severity of phlebitis, which is particularly critical for oncology patients who often receive highly irritating chemotherapy agents7,8.

A variety of interventions have been explored to prevent or treat phlebitis, including pharmacological agents (such as anti-inflammatories and anticoagulants), phytotherapeutic products (e.g., Chamomilla Recutita, Notoginseng, Magnesium sulfate, Aloe vera), and physical measures like cold or heat application15. Despite extensive research, no universal consensus has been established regarding the most effective approach16,17. However, conservative nursing measures—such as normal saline flushes, cold compresses, and topical application of Magnesium sulfate (MgSO₄)—have shown promise for their simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and minimal side effects18–21.

Magnesium sulfate exerts anti-inflammatory, analgesic, and vasodilatory effects that help mitigate phlebitis. It functions by preventing calcium entry into smooth muscle cells, enhancing intracellular calcium uptake, and ultimately reducing muscle contractions and inflammatory responses22,23. Topical MgSO₄—often combined with glycerin—provides localized anti-inflammatory action, reduces edema, and relieves pain associated with phlebitis23–26.

Additionally, cold applications offer physiological benefits by inducing vasoconstriction, reducing blood flow, and limiting the migration of inflammatory cells, which collectively lower local edema and inflammation27. It also reduces cellular metabolism and nerve conduction velocity, which helps alleviate pain28,29. The combination of cold compresses with topical MgSO₄ has been shown to effectively decrease phlebitis severity26,30,31.

Normal saline flushes, meanwhile, help maintain catheter patency and mechanically clear irritants from the vein wall, thereby reducing chemical irritation18,20,32. Wang and Ni 21 further demonstrated that cold compresses, when used alongside saline flushes, effectively reduce inflammation and discomfort at the infusion site.

Despite these promising findings, most studies have examined these measures separately rather than in combination16,18,25,30,32–37. The concurrent application of MgSO₄, cold compresses, and normal saline flushes as an integrated, conservative nursing protocol has not been thoroughly investigated, especially among cancer patients undergoing peripheral IV chemotherapy. This gap is particularly relevant in Egypt, where resource constraints and high patient volumes necessitate cost-effective and evidence-based nursing practices.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the combined effect of these conservative nursing interventions on the incidence of phlebitis in chemotherapy patients. By addressing this gap, the study seeks to inform nursing practice and contribute to the development of standardized care protocols, ultimately improving patient outcomes and healthcare efficiency in oncology settings.

This study aimed to evaluate the effect of conservative nursing measures (normal saline flush, cold application, and magnesium sulfate application) on the incidence of phlebitis among patients receiving chemotherapy. Method and materials

Study design

This study employed a quantitative research methodology using a post-test-only quasi-experimental control group design. In this design, participants were divided into two groups: an intervention group that received the intervention and a control group that either did not receive the intervention or received standard care. Both groups were then assessed for phlebitis outcomes after the intervention had been applied to the intervention group. This approach aimed to evaluate the effect of the intervention by comparing the post-treatment outcomes of the two groups, without conducting pre-tests.

Study setting

The study was carried out at the oncology institute’s chemotherapy department in Shebin El-Kom, Menoufia Governorate, Egypt. There are two units in the department, one for male and one for female patients.

Sampling technique

The study recruited the accessible population receiving chemotherapy at the oncology department using a convenience sampling technique. To lessen selection bias, eligible inpatients were screened daily based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria after obtaining informed consent. The participants were considered for randomization and were randomly assigned into two equal groups (n = 50) using a straightforward randomization technique.

Conservative nursing interventions (cold application, magnesium sulfate application, and normal saline flush) were provided to the intervention group, while the control group was given routine nursing care, which included administering chemotherapy per protocol with follow-up and assessment of the veins without using any further treatment.

Randomization was conducted with sealed opaque envelopes, each carrying a label of either “study” or “control” to guarantee impartial group assignment. The group assignments were placed into sequentially numbered, sealed opaque envelopes prepared in advance. After consent, each participant selected one envelope to determine their group, ensuring allocation concealment and reducing selection bias. Patients were unaware of their group assignment; however, the nurses performing the interventions were cognizant of the treatment procedure.

Intervention

To mitigate chemotherapy-induced phlebitis, the following routine care protocol was provided to all patients in the department:

IV Catheter procedures: Peripheral IV catheters (e.g., 20–22G) were placed in large forearm veins under an aseptic technique to minimize mechanical irritation. Also, for patients with unilateral breast cancer, catheterization was performed on the contralateral arm, and no catheters were inserted on the affected side throughout the study. This ensured patient safety and maintained consistency with the hospital and established clinical guidelines. The experimental protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Nursing at Menoufia University (Decision No. 1026, September 20, 2023) and the Oncology Institute at Menoufia University prior to implementation.

Drug Administration Guidelines: Vesicant drugs (e.g., doxorubicin, vinorelbine) were diluted per institutional protocols41. Infusion rates were adjusted based on drug-specific recommendations (e.g., slow infusion for irritants). Moreover, chemotherapy drugs were administered per institutional guidelines, including standardized dilution, infusion rates, and cannula size to minimize phlebitis risk.

The implementation phase included the delivery of the chemotherapeutic regimen and the execution of interventions based on group allocations. In addition to the mentioned routine care protocol, the intervention group received the following conservative nursing interventions:

Normal saline flush: The IV catheter was irrigated with 5–10 mL of normal saline before and after chemotherapy delivery to ensure catheter patency and reduce irritation from chemotherapeutic drugs.

Normal saline flush: The IV catheter was irrigated with 5–10 mL of normal saline before and after chemotherapy delivery to ensure catheter patency and reduce irritation from chemotherapeutic drugs.

Cold application: An ice pack was applied for 15 min at the beginning of chemotherapy administration at the IV site and for 15 min three consecutive days at the time of the chemotherapy doses to mitigate inflammation and edema.

Cold application: An ice pack was applied for 15 min at the beginning of chemotherapy administration at the IV site and for 15 min three consecutive days at the time of the chemotherapy doses to mitigate inflammation and edema.

Application of MgSO₄: A solution comprising 20 g of MgSO₄ diluted in 100 mL of glycerin and a sterile dressing soaked with this combination was applied twice daily with a sterile gloved hand for 15 min (during and after chemotherapy session) on the distal area of the catheter up to 10 cm on the route of intravenous injection from the first to the third day of chemotherapy cycle in the intervention group. The region was cleaned with normal saline before each treatment.

Applying cold compresses followed by magnesium sulfate was based on their complementary mechanisms and practical clinical considerations. Cold application was used first to induce immediate vasoconstriction, reducing local blood flow and limiting the initial inflammatory response, edema, and pain at the cannula site. Following this, magnesium sulfate was applied for its osmotic and anti-inflammatory effects, which helps draw out residual fluid, further reduce swelling, and promote resolution of inflammation.

This sequence was chosen to maximize therapeutic benefit: the cold compress controls acute inflammation in the early phase, while magnesium sulfate supports sustained reduction of edema and promotes healing31. The control group received conventional hospital nursing care. This included IV catheter insertion protocol, chemotherapeutic delivery, and monitoring without further treatments. The application of cold compresses (Cryotherapy) was only in the event of problems, according to hospital care guidelines.

Data collection tools

Tool I: Structured interview questionnaire: This tool was developed by researchers for the collection of demographic data and medical data. The questionnaire was divided into two main sections.

The first section focused on demographic characteristics, including patient age, gender, marital status, level of education, occupation, and place of residence.

The second section of the questionnaire was dedicated to medical data, capturing vital details such as the patient’s primary cancer diagnosis, the specific chemotherapy regimen they were receiving, and their history of previous IV complications. This section also gathered information regarding the use of anti-inflammatory medications, disease stage, the first occurrence of phlebitis, and the duration of catheterization. By systematically collecting these variables, the study aimed to establish baseline characteristics for both the study and control groups.

Tool 2: Visual Infusion Phlebitis (VIP) Scale: The VIP Scale was used to objectively assess the severity of phlebitis among patients throughout the intervention. It’s an internationally recognized tool originally developed by Jackson42 and later refined by Gallant and Schultz43. It is widely used in oncology and IV therapy research due to its high reliability and validity in grading the severity of phlebitis. It classifies phlebitis into six grades, ranging from Grade 0 (no signs of phlebitis) to Grade 5 (severe thrombophlebitis requiring immediate intervention). A score of 0 indicates that the IV site appears healthy, whereas a score of 1 suggests mild discomfort or slight redness. As the score increases, symptoms become more severe, with Grade 3 indicating pain along the cannula path with noticeable swelling, and Grade 4 representing the presence of a palpable venous cord, a hallmark of advanced phlebitis. The highest score, Grade 5, is given when thrombophlebitis occurs, characterized by significant pain, redness, and swelling.

The VIP Scale was chosen for this study due to its proven validity and reliability44, as demonstrated in previous research where its inter-rater agreement exceeded 85% (Cohen’s kappa >0.85). Gallant and Schultz43 concluded that their version of the VIP scale is a reliable and valid measure for assessing PVC phlebitis. Additionally, studies have reported high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha exceeding 0.80, confirming the scale’s robustness in assessing IV-related complications26,43–46. The scale is already part of routine practice in the oncology department at Shebin El-Kom, Menoufia Governorate, Egypt. Additionally, before data collection, nurses of the department received a briefing session focused on the correct application of the VIP scale to ensure consistent and standardized assessment across all participants.

Data collection procedure

The data-gathering procedure was executed according to a systematic methodology to guarantee uniformity, precision, and dependability over a four-month duration, from June to October 2024. The process was segmented into four primary phases: assessment, planning, implementation, and evaluation. Each step was meticulously crafted to methodically collect data, reduce biases, and uphold ethical and therapeutic norms.

Assessment phase

In this phase, patients who met the inclusion criteria were contacted, and the study’s goal was explained to them comprehensively. Patients were apprised of the research procedures, possible hazards, and benefits before acquiring signed informed consent. This guaranteed that participation was completely voluntary. Upon obtaining permission, patients were randomly allocated to either the intervention group or the control group by a sealed envelope procedure to ensure impartial distribution.

To account for differential phlebitis risks across drug classes, we employed stratification by Drug Risk Category. Patients were stratified into high-risk (vesicants, e.g., anthracyclines) and moderate-risk (irritants, e.g., taxanes) groups. Randomization ensured a balanced distribution of these strata between study/ control groups.

Participants in both cohorts had an initial interview using a predefined questionnaire to gather baseline demographic and clinical information, including age, gender, cancer type, chemotherapy regimen, and previous IV problems. The interview was held in a secluded and tranquil environment to guarantee patient comfort and anonymity. None of the study participants received any premedication vesicant or irritant that can trigger phlebitis before chemotherapy.

Planning phase

The study team created instructional resources, i.e., PowerPoint presentations, films, and live demonstrations, to standardize treatments. The materials were used to educate oncology nurses on the correct application of conservative nursing practices employed in the intervention group. The instructions included aseptic IV insertion methods, proper protocols for normal saline flushing, cold application, MgSO₄ application, and the use of the VIP Scale for phlebitis evaluation.

The planning phase also included collaboration with the hospital pharmacy to formulate the necessary MgSO₄ and glycerin solution in a sterile setting. Pre-prepared ice packs were organized for convenient access. Comprehensive written directives delineating the intervention procedures were disseminated among nursing personnel to guarantee uniform execution.

Monitoring phase

After implementing the routine care and the conservative nursing interventions, nurses in the department were instructed to monitor patients for signs of phlebitis and report any cases to the research team. Additionally, dedicated research team members working in the clinical setting were assigned to systematically assess all study participants. These assessments included documenting and recording any symptoms of phlebitis. Evaluations were conducted daily at consistent time intervals, beginning on the first day of chemotherapy administration and continuing for three consecutive days.

All patients were meticulously observed throughout the treatment regimen. Patients of both groups were monitored for the first indicators of phlebitis, including erythema, edema, discomfort, or increased temperature at the IV site. Patients displaying indications of Grade 3 phlebitis or more, according to the VIP Scale, had their catheters promptly removed and were entitled to medical assessment.

Evaluation phase

The VIP Scale was used to evaluate phlebitis over three successive days after chemotherapy treatment. The phlebitis assessment was performed by the researchers on Day 1, Day 2, and Day 3 after the first chemotherapy injection. The IV site of each patient was assessed, and a phlebitis severity score (0–5) was provided according to the clinical symptoms seen.

Participants were followed up daily for three consecutive days post-chemotherapy to monitor the progression of phlebitis. The evaluation was performed at the same time every day using the VIP scale. Any delayed signs of inflammation, like redness and pain at the infusion site, were recorded by the researchers. The follow-up period was chosen based on clinical guidelines, which indicate that phlebitis typically manifests within 48–72 h after chemotherapy infusion.

In instances where phlebitis severity reached Grade 3 or higher, the IV catheter was promptly removed, and cold compresses were administered to the afflicted region following hospital procedures. Patients exhibiting pronounced phlebitis symptoms were referred for further medical treatment, including the provision of anti-inflammatory drugs if required.

During the assessment phase, all data were recorded in standardized patient records, providing precise monitoring of phlebitis development and intervention efficacy. The conclusive data set was gathered and examined to evaluate the incidence and severity of phlebitis and IV catheter patency between the study and control groups.

Ethical considerations

This research was carried out in complete compliance with ethical norms to safeguard the rights and welfare of all participants. Ethical permission was secured from the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Nursing at Menoufia University before the commencement of the research (Research Ethics Committee No: 1026). An official authorization letter was obtained from the administration of the Oncology Institute at Menoufia University, permitting the researchers to do the study at the hospital’s chemotherapy unit. All eligible participants were thoroughly briefed on the study’s purpose, goals, methods, possible hazards, and benefits before providing their signed informed consent. Participants were guaranteed that their involvement was completely voluntary, and they could leave the research at any moment without any adverse repercussions or effects on their medical care. To maintain security and safeguard data, all patient information was anonymized, with each participant given a unique identification number instead of personal identifiers. The gathered data was securely kept and available only to the study team. The study complied with the standards established in the Declaration of Helsinki (2024) for medical research involving human beings, guaranteeing that participants were treated with dignity and respect47.

Data analysis

Data was analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, summarized the demographic and clinical characteristics of patients in both the study and control groups. Independent sample t-tests assessed baseline comparability across groups for continuous variables (e.g., age and catheterization duration), while chi-square (χ²) tests compared categorical data such as gender, cancer type, and chemotherapy regimen. Results were considered statistically significant when p-values were below 0.05. Drug-related phlebitis risk was controlled by stratification during randomization and included as a covariate in regression models.

To evaluate the efficacy of the interventions, chi-square tests compared the occurrence and severity of phlebitis (using the VIP Scale) between groups. Relative Risk (RR) with 95% confidence intervals quantified the intervention effect. Additionally, independent sample t-tests compared the initial occurrence time of phlebitis (in hours) and the mean duration of catheterization, assessing whether conservative nursing interventions delayed phlebitis onset and prolonged catheter patency. All statistical tests used a significance threshold of p < 0.05, and effect sizes were calculated when applicable to assess the clinical relevance of findings.

As presented in Table 3, the distribution of patients according to the Visual Infusion Phlebitis (VIP) scale demonstrated progressive improvement in the intervention group compared to the control group across all post-intervention days. Day 1 post-intervention, 72% of the intervention group showed no signs of phlebitis versus 60% of controls. This difference became more pronounced over time: by the second day, 88% of the intervention group remained phlebitis-free compared to 62% of controls, increasing to 90% versus 66% by the third day. Statistical analysis confirmed significantly lower phlebitis severity scores in the intervention group during days 2 and 3 post-intervention (p < 0.05).

Discussion

Phlebitis is an expected complication associated with peripheral IV catheters, especially among patients receiving chemotherapy6,7. This quasi-experimental study aimed to determine the effect of conservative nursing measures, including normal saline flush, cold application, and MgSO₄, on the prevention and early management of phlebitis in patients with cancer undergoing peripheral IV chemotherapy. The study findings revealed that the patients in the intervention group who received conservative nursing measures showed significantly lower phlebitis incidence and severity than those in the control group on the second- and third-day post-intervention. Moreover, the onset of phlebitis was delayed, and the catheter remained in place longer in the intervention group compared to the control group, suggesting that these measures support vascular health and improve patient outcomes.

The observed temporal reduction in phlebitis incidence during the three-day monitoring period represents a clinically significant deviation from the expected natural progression of chemotherapy-induced vascular inflammation. This phenomenon can be attributed to the study’s structured intervention protocol, which fundamentally altered the typical phlebitis trajectory through three key mechanisms. First, the implementation of stringent escalation criteria created a critical inflection point in the progression of phlebitis cases. Patients meeting Grade III + phlebitis criteria (VIP Scale) underwent immediate catheter removal and medical evaluation, effectively truncating the severe end of the phlebitis spectrum. This protocol-mandated intervention served as an artificial ceiling preventing the accumulation of advanced cases that would otherwise be expected in longitudinal observation.

Concurrently, the graded intervention approach for milder presentations (Grades I and II) demonstrated measurable therapeutic efficacy. Both cohorts received hospital routine care, including cold compresses application, aseptic techniques, dressing changes, monitoring signs of phlebitis using VIP scale, reevaluation of phlebitis, documenting and reporting, avoiding further irritation of the vein, and educating the patient to report pain, redness, or swelling. In addition to that, the intervention group benefited from the implemented conservative nursing care protocol. This tiered management system produced two clinically relevant outcomes: (1) complete resolution of early-stage phlebitis in some cases, and (2) stabilization, preventing progression to higher severity grades. The efficacy of this approach is quantitatively evidenced by the Day 2 outcomes, where the intervention group maintained 88% phlebitis-free status compared to 62% in the control group (χ²=14.689, p = 0.005), with only 2% progressing to medium-grade inflammation versus 10% in standard care.

Additionally, some patients in the control group who developed early-stage phlebitis may have used herbal remedies (e.g., sesame oil) or over-the-counter topical creams like Hemoclar cream without formally reporting this to the clinical team—a behavior commonly observed in the Egyptian community. These unreported self-care practices could have contributed to the observed reduction in phlebitis symptoms over time, independent of the study intervention.

Methodologically, the truncated observation window represents both a study limitation and an explanatory factor. Existing natural history data48,49 demonstrate that phlebitis independently increased with increasing catheter duration, being highest after 96 h, suggesting our 72-hour endpoint may have preceded the expected zenith of inflammatory progression. Furthermore, the Hawthorne effect inherent in clinical trials likely enhanced adherence to catheter care protocols in both groups, potentially attenuating the background incidence rate. These findings demonstrate that early intervention can reverse phlebitis, structured vascular care prevents progression, and comprehensive conservative measures improve outcomes. Though longer follow-ups may yield different patterns, this study confirms that targeted nursing interventions can disrupt inflammation within the critical 72-hour window post-chemotherapy.

Aligning with the reported findings of Bayoumi, Khonji14, the current results underscore the important role of regular nursing assessments before, during, and after catheterization, along with close monitoring for any signs of phlebitis. These routine practices are important mainly in oncology care, where early detection and timely intervention can significantly improve patient outcomes and prevent complications. Consistent with the research conducted by Mali, Chendake20, the current study findings support that flushing an IV catheter with 0.9% normal saline before and after medication administration can help reduce the risk of phlebitis by clearing irritant substances from the venous wall. Additionally, the findings of the current study align with the conclusions of the review conducted by Sotnikova, Fasoi36, which found that flushing with either 0.9% normal saline or heparin solution helps maintain catheter patency and prevent complications. However, it should also be noted that most researchers favor 0.9% normal saline for its safety, decreased risk of mistakes, efficiency, ease of use, more economic, and lack of adverse effects36.

The current results concerning the effectiveness of cold compresses in reducing the severity of phlebitis are supported by the quasi-experimental study by Chauhan, Sen50. Their study compared hot and cold compresses in managing phlebitis among patients in medical and surgical wards, concluding that cold compresses are particularly effective in reducing phlebitis severity50. Moreover, the current findings align with those of El-Sayad, Aboalizm22, who investigated the effects of preventive measures such as sesame oil and cold compresses on chemotherapy-induced phlebitis. Their results revealed that these preventive measures effectively reduced the incidence of chemotherapy-induced phlebitis in this population22.

In patients receiving amiodarone infusions, 15-minute cold gel pack applications every 2 h significantly lowered phlebitis rates and severity27. To standardize assessment, a VIP scale was developed for patients receiving chemotherapy, demonstrating high inter-rater reliability and correlation with patient-reported severity42,43. These findings suggest that cold compress application is an effective, non-invasive method for preventing and managing chemotherapy-related phlebitis, while the VIP scale offers a reliable tool for assessing phlebitis severity in clinical settings.

Concerning pharmacological interventions, the current findings align with those of Hasanien, Shereif24, who found that magnesium sulfate with glycerin was more effective than ice chips in reducing peripheral IV cannula-induced phlebitis. In a comparative study among 110 patients, magnesium sulfate with glycerin was also found to be more effective than heparinoid ointment51. Similarly, the current investigations are consistent with Metagudda, Pujari30, who evaluated the effectiveness of magnesium sulfate with glycerin in managing superficial thrombophlebitis and found it effective in reducing thrombophlebitis severity. Magnesium sulfate, particularly in combination with glycerin, is commonly used in both ointment and solution forms. This combination acts as a cleanser, moisturizer, anti-inflammatory agent, and hydrator. It minimizes water loss from the skin, prevents crystallization of the stratum corneum’s layered structure, and, in turn, lowers the risk of infection34,52.

In the same vein, a comparative analysis by Sharma and Patidar35 revealed that magnesium sulfate outperformed cold compresses in managing superficial thrombophlebitis, despite both modalities showing clinical benefits. A scoping review of topical treatments for peripheral venous catheter-related phlebitis identified physical measures, phytotherapeutic treatments, and pharmacological interventions as potential options19. Among these, magnesium sulfate, with or without glycerin, emerged as one of the best-performing pharmacological treatments. However, the review noted that available evidence is limited and often lacks methodological rigor, highlighting the need for further research in diverse clinical contexts19.

Phlebitis is correlated with some factors, including irritant drugs, cannula size, insertion site, duration of use, age, gender, and underlying health conditions7. Preventive measures included regularly assessing the insertion site, securing the cannula with proper dressings, choosing appropriate catheter sites, and following strict aseptic techniques14. Furthermore, strict adherence to institutional protocols for systematic phlebitis monitoring enables early detection, prevents clinical deterioration, and mandates timely cannula replacement upon reaching Grade 3 phlebitis - all critical components of effective vascular access management. These nursing interventions play a key role in preserving vein health, delaying the onset of phlebitis, and prolonging catheter use, ultimately enhancing patient care14. The current study demonstrated that strictly adhering to these nursing interventions and implementing conservative measures can preserve venous integrity and delay the onset of phlebitis. As a result, the catheter lifespan is extended, potential treatment interruptions are reduced, and the patient’s overall experience is improved. Maintaining consistent vascular access is essential for keeping chemotherapy on schedule, directly impacting treatment efficacy and continuity.

Study limitations

While this study offers valuable insights into the effectiveness of conservative nursing measures for preventing phlebitis, several limitations should be noted. First, its single-center design in an oncology department, along with convenience sampling and a relatively small sample size with the presence of confounding variables, and heterogeneity in routine care practices, may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future multi-center studies with larger, randomized samples across diverse clinical settings are needed to enhance external validity. Second, the three-day follow-up period restricts the detection of late-onset phlebitis, suggesting the need for longer observation periods. Third, the exclusion of patients with pre-existing vascular conditions or MgSO₄ allergies narrows the applicability of the results to broader populations. Finally, the absence of patient-reported outcomes, such as pain levels or satisfaction, underscores the importance of incorporating qualitative measures in future research to enable a more comprehensive evaluation.

Conclusions

This study provides strong evidence that conservative nursing measures, such as normal saline flush, cold application, and MgSO₄ application, can effectively manage phlebitis among patients receiving peripheral IV chemotherapy. These measures help delay phlebitis onset, extend catheter lifespan, and improve patient comfort.

Including these simple techniques in standard chemotherapy protocols can enhance patient safety, minimize complications, and improve IV therapy outcomes. The findings reinforce the critical role of oncology nurses in preventing phlebitis through regular assessment, timely intervention, and adherence to best practices. Although the study has some limitations, it lays the groundwork for future research to explore the long-term impact of these interventions in diverse healthcare settings.

Implications of the study

The current study findings emphasize the critical role of oncology nurses in preventing and managing phlebitis. Regularly checking IV catheter sites, identifying early signs of phlebitis, and using simple yet effective interventions such as saline flushing, cold compresses, and MgSO₄ application can help protect veins and reduce complications. Integrating these measures into daily nursing care can lead to better patient outcomes and safer IV therapy.

The study validated the concept of phlebitis reversibility through early intervention, demonstrated the preventive capacity of protocolized vascular access care, and highlighted the superior outcomes achievable with comprehensive conservative measures. While longer follow-up might reveal different progression patterns in untreated populations, these results establish proof-of-concept for interrupting the inflammatory cascade through targeted nursing interventions during the critical 72 h of chemotherapy.

Beyond clinical practice, this study underscores the need for standardized hospital guidelines that incorporate these preventive measures. Establishing clear institutional guidelines can decrease healthcare costs, minimize catheter replacements, and improve patient safety. Moreover, nurse training programs should emphasize education on IV catheters related conservative care techniques to ensure their consistent and effective application. Given these benefits, hospital administrators and policymakers should prioritize implementing these strategies in oncology care to enhance both patient safety and treatment outcomes.
