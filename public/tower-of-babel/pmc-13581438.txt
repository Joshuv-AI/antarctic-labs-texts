Radiological pattern of hip osteoarthritis is associated with one-year patient-reported outcomes but not long-term mortality or revision after total hip arthroplasty
Authors: Conor J McCann, Nick D Clement, John M Bayram, Gillian Leitch, Chloe E H Scott
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThis study aimed to determine the effect of the radiological patterns of hip osteoarthritis (OA) on patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) at one year following total hip arthroplasty (THA) and their impact on long-term mortality and survivorship.MethodsThis retrospective study assessed the outcomes of 445 patients with primary hip OA undergoing THA. Plain radiographs were assessed to determine the Kellgren–Lawrence grading, the radiological pattern of arthritis (hypertrophic, atrophic, or mixed) and the predominant location of wear. PROMs assessed included the EQ-5D-3L and Oxford Hip Score (OHS) preoperatively and at 12 months postoperatively. The minimum follow-up for surviving patients was 14.7 years. Across the cohort, including patients who died during follow-up, the mean follow-up was 12.3 years (1.0 to 15.6), with patients censored at death. Multivariable linear regression models were used to evaluate independent predictors of postoperative PROM scores, mortality, and revision.ResultsIn all, 284 females (63.5%) and 161 males (36.5%) were included. Females were more likely to have atrophic OA (n = 120; (42.3%) vs n = 47; (29.2%); p < 0.001). Males were more likely to have hypertrophic OA (n = 52; (32.3%) vs n = 47; (16.5%); p < 0.001). One-year PROM improvement differed by OA pattern: hypertrophic OA had the greatest mean improvement in OHS (+22.6 (SD 7.4)) and EQ-5D-3L (+0.45 (SD 0.32)), compared with mixed (+18.6 (SD 9.5), +0.35 (SD 0.32)) and atrophic OA (+17.3 (SD 11.3), +0.33 (SD 0.32)) OHS (p < 0.001) and EQ-5D-3L (p = 0.005). After adjustment for covariates, atrophic OA remained independently associated with lower postoperative OHS and EQ-5D-3L scores compared with hypertrophic OA. Mortality and revision rates did not differ between OA pattern groups.ConclusionRadiological pattern of hip OA was associated with differences in one-year PROMs following THA. Patients with atrophic OA had smaller improvements following surgery compared with counterparts with hypertrophic OA. No differences were observed in long-term mortality or revision. Introduction

Primary hip osteoarthritis (OA) is increasingly recognized as a heterogeneous condition, with variation in disease onset and the rate of clinical and radiological progression.1 When conservative measures are exhausted, total hip arthroplasty (THA) represents a clinically effective and cost-effective treatment for hip OA.2 Differences in postoperative outcomes exist, and multiple factors have been implicated, including age, sex, frailty, preoperative function, and radiological severity of OA.3-5 Variation in outcomes persists despite accounting for these factors, suggesting that the understanding of hip OA and its treatment remains incomplete.

Distinct patterns of hip OA are recognized,6-8 and may be associated with demographic factors such as age and sex.9 One method of classification is based on the presence or absence of osteophytes (hypertrophic or atrophic, respectively).10 Atrophic OA has been associated with the female sex,11 faster disease progression/joint destruction,12,13 and higher fracture risk, even when adjusted for bone mineral density.14 Structural and metabolic differences have been identified between atrophic and hypertrophic OA; atrophic OA has microarchitectural disorganization, lower bone volume, and thinner trabeculae,14 as well as reduced type 2 collagen synthesis compared with hypertrophic OA.10 As both biological and radiological factors have been associated with differences in outcomes following THA, it is therefore plausible that OA pattern may influence functional recovery; however, evidence on the impact of OA phenotype on postoperative outcomes remains limited.

Increasing radiological severity of OA has been associated with greater postoperative improvements in patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs),3,15 although this relationship is not consistently reported.16 Qualitative radiological classifications, such as the Kellgren–Lawrence (KL) grade,17 are commonly used to guide management and demonstrate fair inter- and intraobserver reliability.7 However, these classifications are limited in their ability to capture the heterogeneity of OA, and may not capture all features relevant to postoperative outcome.

The primary aim of this study was to investigate the association between the pattern of OA seen on preoperative radiographs and PROMs one year after THA. Secondary aims included examining any association with severity grading, direction of wear, comorbidities, and long-term outcomes including mortality and revision.

Methods

Data collection was completed under Ethics Committee approval. All patients undergoing primary THA at the study centre (Royal Infirmary of Edinburgh) over one calendar year, from 1 January 2010 to 31 December 2010, were considered for inclusion. This year was the first in which all patient radiographs were reliably uploaded to the NHS Scotland national archive system, enabling complete long-term radiological follow-up and revision capture. Patients with idiopathic primary OA were included; other THA indications, including inflammatory arthropathy or secondary OA, were excluded. Long-term follow-up for mortality and revision was undertaken through regional electronic patient records and national imaging archives, with final record review performed at a minimum of 14.7 years post-THA.

Operations were performed by, or under the direct supervision of, one of 14 fellowship-trained arthroplasty surgeons. A posterior or modified Hardinge approach was used, based on the surgeon’s preference. Implants were also based on surgeon preference, with the majority in our centre at the time using a cemented Exeter v40 femoral component and a cemented Contemporary acetabular component (both Stryker Orthopaedics, USA). Sex (male/female) was recorded as documented in the electronic patient record at the time of surgery; 284/451 (63.0%) were female and 167/451 (37.0%) were male. Mean age across the entire cohort was 67.8 years old (range 40-91) . Patient data was collected preoperatively, including sex (biological), demographic characteristics, comorbidities, baseline Oxford Hip Score (OHS)18,19 and EQ-5D-3L20 scores. Patients completed PROMs independently by post (with a telephone call follow-up to prompt postal form completion if required) preoperatively and at one year postoperatively as part of routine follow-up. Those who conducted telephone calls were independent of the surgical team and not involved in any radiological assessment. PROMs were not assessed over the telephone.

The OHS is a widely used PROM that measures hip pain and function.21 The original OHS, which scores from 12 (best) to 60 (worst) was collected and linearly transformed to the 0 to 48 scale (48 best) to improve interpretability for analysis and reporting. The EQ-5D-3L provides a summary measure of health-related quality of life with scores ranging from below 0 (states considered worse than death) to 1 (perfect health).22

Radiological data

All patients underwent standardized preoperative supine anteroposterior pelvic radiographs according to institutional protocol; both lower limbs were rotated internally to standardize femoral neck profile, beam alignment was centred on the symphysis pubis with a standardized source-to-image distance, and images were reviewed for adequate pelvic rotation and tilt. Radiographs were classified by a single observer (CJM) according to OA pattern (hypertrophic or atrophic), as previously described with an additional study-defined ‘mixed’ category for cases not clearly meeting either definition (Figure 1).11,12 Atrophic OA was defined by absent or very minimal osteophyte formation in the presence of other degenerative features (e.g. joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis/cysts or femoral head collapse).11 Hypertrophic OA was defined by definite, marked osteophyte formation, consistent with prior descriptions.11 Radiographs not meeting either definition were classified as mixed. Direction of wear (superolateral, medial, or global) and KL grade were also recorded.

Preoperative supine anteroposterior radiographs. a) Right hip of a 71-year-old male, classified asshowing a hypertrophic pattern of osteoarthritis (OA), global direction of wear, and Kellgren–Lawrence (KL) grade 4. b) Right hip of a 67-year-old female, classified as showing an atrophic pattern of OA, global direction of wear, and KL grade 3. c) Left hip of a 65-year-old male, classified as showing a mixed pattern of OA, medial direction of wear, and KL grade 3.

Direction of wear (superolateral or medial) was determined based on the predominant location of joint space narrowing and relative femoral head migration, as described by Conrozier et al11 and Ledingham et al.9 Cases not clearly fitting these patterns were classified as ‘global’ wear. KL grade was assigned using a modification of the original KL classification.17 The KL criteria attach weight to the presence of osteophytes as a part of its grading system; therefore, to allow for grading in cases with minimal osteophyte formation, atrophic OA was graded based on other features (e.g. joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis/cysts, and femoral head collapse). Classification uncertainties were resolved by group consensus with the senior author (CEHS) following the initial classification, and inter- and intraobserver reliability were assessed on the final classifications.

Interobserver reliability was assessed in a random sample of 100 radiographs independently reviewed by a second orthopaedic surgeon (JMB), who was blinded to the primary assessor’s (CJM) classifications; these data were used solely for reliability analysis and not included in the primary dataset. Agreement was quantified using percentage agreement and Cohen’s kappa (quadratic-weighted for KL grade). To assess intrarater reliability, a separate random subset of radiographs (n = 100) was re-evaluated by the primary assessor (CJM) after a minimum interval of three months (3 to 5) without access to the original classifications.

To ensure complete capture of revision procedures performed outside the study centre, the National Picture Archiving and Communication System,23 which stores all radiographs obtained within Scotland, was searched. Postoperative radiographs suggestive of revision surgery were cross-referenced with clinical records by a single observer (CJM) to confirm the procedure and indication. Revision events were identified throughout follow-up, and mortality status was confirmed at final data collection; shorter observed times therefore reflect earlier death rather than incomplete follow-up. The type of femoral/acetabular component and use of cement in the primary procedure was also recorded.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was completed using R v. 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria). Bivariate analysis was used to compare the age, sex, comorbidity, KL grade, direction of wear, and OHS and EQ-5D-3L scores between OA pattern groups. Normally distributed continuous variables were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and non-normally distributed variables using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test where expected cell counts were less than five. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons following Kruskal–Wallis testing were performed using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni adjustment. For categorical variables analyzed using chi-squared testing, pairwise comparisons were performed with Bonferroni correction.

Multivariable linear regression models were constructed with one-year OHS and EQ-5D-3L scores as the dependent variables, adjusting for baseline PROM score, age, sex, KL grade, direction of wear, and comorbidity. This approach represents an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), which adjusts postoperative scores for baseline values and is recommended when analyzing baseline and follow-up measurements of continuous outcomes;24 model assumptions were assessed and no major violations were observed. Long-term mortality was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and compared between groups using the log-rank test. A Cox proportional hazards model was constructed to adjust for potential confounders. Revision was analyzed using cumulative incidence functions, accounting for death as a competing event. Group differences were assessed using Gray’s test. Multivariable subdistribution hazard modelling was performed using the Fine–Gray method, adjusting for age, sex, and comorbidity. Results are reported as subdistribution hazard ratios (SHRs) with 95% CIs. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A patient flow diagram is shown in Figure 2. A total of 445 patients were included, of whom 431 (96.9%) had complete one-year OHS data, 413 (92.8%) had complete EQ-5D-3L data, and 403 (90.6%) had complete PROMs. Baseline characteristics are presented in Table I. Females were more likely to have atrophic OA (n = 120/284, 42.3% vs n = 47 out of 161, 29.2%), while males were more likely to have hypertrophic OA (n = 52/161, 32.3% vs n = 47/284, 16.5%, Table I). The majority of both femoral and acetabular components were cemented (Table II). Inter- and intrarater reliability of radiological assessment was good (Supplementary Table 1). Most patients had KL grade 4 OA (n = 256; 57.5%), followed by grade 3 (n = 169; 38.0%) and grade 2 (n = 20; 4.5%; Table III). The distribution of KL grades did not differ significantly between males and females (p = 0.263, Fisher’s exact test).

OA pattern and KL severity grade

Significant differences in KL grading were observed between OA pattern groups (p < 0.001, Fisher’s exact test; Table I). The mean KL grade was higher in the hypertrophic group than in the atrophic or mixed groups 3.79 (SD 0.41) vs 3.35 ( SD 0.67) and 3.55 (SD 0.52), respectively; p < 0.001, Kruskal-Wallis test). Pairwise analysis between the grouped OA patterns demonstrated significant differences between each group (Table IV) after post-hoc testing, with the largest difference seen between atrophic and hypertrophic groups (adjusted p-value < 0.001, Dunn’s post-hoc pairwise with Bonferroni correction).

Direction of wear

Direction of wear differed by sex (p < 0.001, chi-squared test; Supplementary Table 2). Pairwise comparisons confirmed significant differences between all categories (all p < 0.005, chi-squared test), with males more likely to exhibit superolateral wear and females more likely to exhibit medial and global wear (Supplementary Table 2). Significant variation in the direction of wear was also seen between the three OA pattern groups (Table I). On pairwise analysis, the distribution of wear direction differed significantly only between the atrophic and hypertrophic group (p = 0.011, chi-squared test; Bonferroni corrected adjusted p-value = 0.033; Table IV), with a higher rate of observed superolateral wear in the atrophic group (hypertrophic n = 28/99 (28.3%) vs atrophic n = 70/167 (41.9%)) and more global wear in the hypertrophic group (hypertrophic n = 58/99 (58.6%) vs atrophic n = 66/167 (39.5%)). The mixed pattern group did not significantly differ in wear direction from either the atrophic (p = 0.110, chi-squared test; Bonferroni corrected adjusted p-value = 0.329) or hypertrophic groups (p = 0.398, chi-squared test; Bonferroni corrected adjusted p-value > 0.999).

Comorbidity

No significant differences were observed in the rates of individual comorbidities between OA pattern groups, apart from lung disease, which was more common in the atrophic group (n = 20 (12.0%)) compared with hypertrophic (n = 2 (2.0%)) and mixed patterns (n = 17 (9.5%); p = 0.018, Fisher’s exact test; Table I). The prevalence of any comorbidity did not differ between OA pattern groups; however, comorbidity (defined as two or more comorbidities) was more common in patients with atrophic OA (n = 41 (24.6%)) compared with mixed (n = 35 (19.6%)) and hypertrophic patterns (n = 12 (12.1%); p = 0.048, chi-squared test).

Patient-reported outcome measures

Preoperative OHS was not significantly different between the three groups (Table V). The hypertrophic group had a significantly larger mean score improvement compared with atrophic or mixed (22.61 (SD 7.36) vs 17.31 (SD 11.34) and 18.64 (SD 9.50), respectively; p < 0.001, one-way ANOVA). Across pain, function, and total score domains, the hypertrophic group had the greatest overall improvement, with the mixed group demonstrating intermediate improvement, and the atrophic group having the least. Pairwise comparisons confirmed significantly greater improvement in the hypertrophic group compared with atrophic (adjusted p-value = 0.001, Dunn’s Pairwise) and mixed patterns (adjusted p-value = 0.008, Dunn’s pairwise comparison), while the difference between mixed and atrophic groups was not statistically significant (adjusted p-value > 0.999, Dunn’s pairwise comparison; Table IV). Preoperative EQ-5D-3L did not differ between OA patterns, while one-year improvement was greatest in the hypertrophic group, intermediate in the mixed group, and lowest in the atrophic group (Table V).

No significant difference was seen in overall preoperative score or preoperative pain between the three KL groups (Table III). Mean preoperative function was significantly worse in patients with lower KL grades (KL 2: 12.63 vs KL 3: 11.21 and KL 4: 10.09; p = 0.005, ANOVA). Increasing KL grade was associated with greater overall mean improvement in OHS (KL 2: 12.8; KL 3: 17.5; KL 4: 20.5; p < 0.001, ANOVA) No significant difference in preoperative mean EQ-5D-3L scores was seen between the three different KL grades (Table III). Patients with KL grade 2 showed the smallest improvement in mean EQ-5D-3L score (KL 2: 0.23; KL 3: 0.33; KL 4: 0.40; p < 0.001, Kruskal-Wallis test).

Multivariable linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate independent predictors of one-year improvement in OHS and EQ-5D-3L (Table VI). Compared with the hypertrophic pattern, the atrophic pattern was associated with significantly lower postoperative OHS (β −4.56 (95% CI −6.94 to −2.18); p < 0.001, multivariable linear regression), where β represents the adjusted regression coefficient (mean difference relative to the reference group) and CI the confidence interval. Similar findings were observed for EQ-5D-3L scores; the atrophic pattern was associated with lower postoperative scores compared with hypertrophic OA (β −0.074 (95% CI −0.139 to −0.008); p = 0.027, multivariable linear regression).

Mortality

Patients were followed for mortality and revision until 8 August 2025. Mean follow-up was 12.3 years (1.0 to 15.6), with no significant difference between OA pattern groups (p = 0.487, Kruskal-Wallis test; Table II). There was no significant difference in mortality between the different OA pattern groups on Kaplan–Meier analysis (p = 0.207, log-rank test; Figure 3). In a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model adjusting for age, sex, and comorbidity, OA pattern was not independently associated with mortality (atrophic vs hypertrophic hazard ratio (HR) 1.20 (95% CI 0.81 to 1.80); p = 0.363, multivariable Cox regression), mixed vs hypertrophic HR 1.35, (95% CI 0.92 to 1.97; p = 0.123, multivariable Cox regression; Table VII). Increasing age was associated with higher mortality risk, while female sex was associated with lower mortality risk.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that the radiological pattern of OA was associated with differences in one-year PROM scores following THA. Atrophic OA was more frequent in females and linked to lower KL grade, superolateral wear, and higher rates of comorbidity, and was associated with poorer OHS and EQ-5D-3L outcomes compared with hypertrophic or mixed patterns. Long-term reoperation and mortality rates were similar across groups. Direction of wear also differed between males and females. Importantly, after adjusting for age, sex, KL grade, and comorbidity, OA pattern and direction of wear remained independently associated with postoperative PROM scores. This suggests that radiological phenotype and wear pattern may capture prognostic information beyond structural severity alone; however, baseline differences between groups, including sex and wear distribution, may represent residual confounding, and these findings should therefore be interpreted as associative rather than causal.

Radiological OA severity grading is widely used in both clinical and epidemiological settings,7,9 and remains a practical tool in routine care; increasing KL grade has been associated with THA requirement and larger postoperative PROM improvements.3,25-27 Similar findings are reported using the Tonnis classification,15 but evidence is mixed, with some studies showing no association with postoperative PROMs.28 Although useful, KL is a broad severity score and does not capture several radiological features that may be relevant, including osteophyte phenotype and wear direction. The KL system relies on osteophyte formation as a key component of grading, which is strongly associated with cartilage damage,29 but may not consistently reflect the extent of underlying cartilage degeneration, as demonstrated by atrophic OA phenotypes which have marked joint space narrowing despite minimal osteophyte formation.12 In our cohort, greater (modified) KL severity was associated with larger PROM improvements in unadjusted analyses but was not an independent predictor after multivariable adjustment. In contrast, OA pattern remained independently associated with postoperative PROM scores, suggesting that radiological phenotype may provide prognostic information beyond structural severity.

Differences in radiological phenotype may reflect underlying variation in bone and cartilage biology, although evidence is inconsistent.10,11,30 Histomorphometric studies have demonstrated structural differences between hypertrophic and atrophic hip OA. Schnitzler et al30 reported that patients with atrophic OA had lower trabecular bone volume, thinner trabeculae, and a higher prevalence of vertebral fractures compared with hypertrophic OA, suggesting that atrophic disease may represent a more osteoporotic bone response. Differences in cartilage metabolism have also been described; Conrozier et al10 demonstrated reduced serum levels of type II collagen synthesis in patients with atrophic OA, indicating reduced cartilage synthetic activity compared with hypertrophic disease. These biological differences may contribute to the absence of osteophyte formation and the more destructive radiological pattern (more rapid joint space narrowing) observed in atrophic OA.12,31 However, other work has shown no statistically significant differences in circulating biological markers between atrophic/hypertrophic phenotypes, suggesting that systemic biomarker profiles may not reliably distinguish these patterns.11 Evidence is also emerging suggesting differences in clinical response to intra-articular treatments between OA pattern phenotypes, with atrophic generally responding worst.31 Epidemiological and radiological differences between phenotypes have also been reported, with atrophic OA occurring more frequently in older females and is associated with reduced femoral head coverage and diffuse superior migration.11 Collectively, these findings suggest that hypertrophic and atrophic OA may represent differing skeletal responses to joint degeneration, which could plausibly influence joint biomechanics, disease progression, and the magnitude of symptomatic improvement following arthroplasty. However, the biological mechanisms underlying these radiological patterns remain incompletely understood.

The mixed phenotype demonstrated intermediate improvement between hypertrophic and atrophic patterns, consistent with the possibility that these radiological appearances represent points along a spectrum of disease rather than entirely discrete categories. Notably, most patients across all radiological patterns experienced substantial improvement following THA. Radiological phenotype should therefore be interpreted as influencing the magnitude of postoperative improvement rather than the likelihood of benefit from surgery. Recognition of OA pattern on routine preoperative radiographs may therefore assist in patient counselling and expectation setting.

As well as hypertrophic/atrophic pattern, direction of femoral head migration (direction of wear) has previously been associated with differences between sex groups.9,32 Ledingham et al9 report similar findings to ours with superolateral wear more common in males (58%) than females (39%), and medial/superomedial wear more frequent in females (24%) compared to males (7%). We found medial and global wear patterns demonstrated smaller one-year PROM improvements than superolateral wear, suggesting that variation in hip morphology and joint loading patterns may influence postoperative functional recovery following THA.

This study has several limitations. Although radiological classifications were performed independently by two observers with assessment of inter-rater reliability, subjectivity remains in pattern and wear-direction classification. The ‘mixed’ OA pattern group may represent a heterogeneous group rather than a distinct phenotype. Despite multivariable regression modelling adjusting for key confounders, residual confounding from baseline differences between groups as well as from unmeasured variables cannot be excluded. No a priori sample size calculation was performed, and the study may therefore have been underpowered for certain secondary or long-term outcomes. While the direction of wear was included in regression models for one-year PROM improvement, its independent association with long-term mortality and implant survivorship was not specifically examined. Although electronic records and national imaging archives were reviewed to identify revision procedures, revisions performed outside Scotland may not have been captured, and the low number of revision events means the study was not powered to detect differences in revision risk between groups; therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, outcomes for the patients lost to follow-up (lacking PROM data) are unknown. In addition, a modified KL grading system was used, placing less emphasis on osteophyte formation and greater reliance on joint space narrowing, and was based on supine (non-weightbearing) radiographs, which may limit comparability with standard grading approaches. Finally, PROMs were assessed only at one year, and longer-term functional trajectories were not available; whether benefits are sustained or offset by late complications or functional decline remains unclear.

In patients with primary hip OA undergoing THA, preoperative radiological OA pattern was associated with differences in one-year PROMs. Hypertrophic OA demonstrated greater improvement in OHS and EQ-5D-3L compared with atrophic and mixed patterns. Direction of wear was also independently associated with outcome, with medial and global wear patterns demonstrating smaller improvements in OHS compared with superolateral wear. These associations persisted after adjustment for age, sex, KL grade, and comorbidity. OA pattern was not associated with long-term mortality or implant survivorship.

Take home message

- Radiological phenotype (hypertrophic/atrophic osteoarthritis) and direction of wear are independently associated with one-year patient-reported outcome measure improvement following total hip arthroplasty, suggesting that radiological phenotype may provide additional prognostic information beyond structural severity alone.
