Effects of a brief aging simulation suit intervention on clinical empathy in healthcare professionals working in long-term care: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Sergio Serrada-Tejeda, Paula Obeso-Benítez, Lucía Hernández-Hernández, Fernando Martínez-Cuervo, Marta Pérez-de-Heredia-Torres, Patricia Sánchez-Herrera-Baeza, Aitor Yusta-Ceacero, Rosa M Martínez-Piédrola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical empathy refers to a healthcare professional’s ability to understand a patient’s experiences and emotions through cognitive and affective perspective taking, and to communicate that understanding through compassionate and appropriate professional behaviors. Aging simulation suits are experiential educational tools designed to replicate the sensory and physical limitations associated with aging. However, evidence regarding their effectiveness in enhancing clinical empathy among active healthcare professionals remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of an aging simulation suit on clinical empathy among healthcare professionals working in long-term care settings.MethodsA randomized controlled trial was conducted with 82 healthcare professionals from four nursing homes in Madrid and Asturias (Spain). Participants were randomly assigned to an experimental group (EG) (n=41) or a control group (CG) (n=41). Both groups received the same structured educational session on empathy and aging. The experimental group additionally participated in an immersive experience using the GERT aging simulation suit, whereas the control group did not receive the simulation component. Self-reported empathy were measured pre- and post-intervention using the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI) and the Jefferson Scale of Empathy–Health Professions version (JSPE-HPS).ResultsNo significant differences were found between groups in IRI scores. However, the experimental group showed significant improvements in total JSPE-HPS scores and in the subscales Perspective Taking and Compassionate Care (p < 0.05), compared with the control group. These findings suggest that the immersive intervention enhanced both cognitive and affective components of clinical empathy.ConclusionsThe use of an aging simulation suit was associated with improvements in specific dimensions of clinical empathy among healthcare professionals working in long-term care. This educational tool offers a valuable experiential approach that enhances understanding and compassion toward older adults. However, these findings are limited to short-term, self-reported measures, and no behavioral or patient outcome data were collected. Further longitudinal studies are needed to determine the long-term sustainability of these effects and their translation into clinical practice.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, Unique Protocol ID: 2711201916919; ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT07280689. Introduction

Aging brings physiological and structural changes [1], in addition to a decline in functional abilities that affect movement, sensory and psychosocial functions, and ultimately, the ability to interact with the environment [2]. Older people living in nursing homes require long-term care and represent a highly diverse population [3, 4] as both the amount and type of assistance required may vary depending on the aging process. From a social and healthcare perspective, the role of professionals and caregivers is essential, as they enable the physical, social, and emotional needs of older adults to be met, helping them to remain functional for longer in their everyday environment [5, 6]. The relationship between the professional and the older person is decisive in the quality of care and has been identified as an essential aspect of the therapeutic relationship [7–10]. In this context, empathy emerges as one of the main factors in developing a quality therapeutic relationship, understood as the ability to perceive another person’s feelings and emotions and convey that understanding through active listening, respect, and both verbal and nonverbal communication [11]. Furthermore, it has been shown that a higher level of empathy in healthcare professionals reduces anxiety, depression, and hostility, while improving satisfaction and self-concept in older people [12]. Understanding and responding to the emotional and functional needs of older adults is essential to improving their well-being and promoting humane treatment [9].

Despite the growing incorporation of training methods that include empathy in the teaching of healthcare professionals, there are no specific protocols for training them, nor is there any information on what content is most effective for their training. Cunico et al. [13] have shown that educational interventions such as training talks, role-playing, analysis of recordings, and interactions with real patients are frequently used to improve healthcare professionals’ empathy towards the elderly population. In this context, the use of simulation methodologies at the clinical level and the training process for social and healthcare professionals have been employed as a strategy for improving and training empathic skills. Studies that have used platforms such as The Aging Game [14] and the Geriatric Medication Game® [15] have enabled medical and nursing students to put themselves in the shoes of elderly patients, increasing their sensitivity and understanding of the limitations of aging. Likewise, training programs in geriatric clinical communication, which include educational sessions, role-playing with standardized patients, and video recordings, have been shown to improve communicative self-efficacy and reduce ageist attitudes among healthcare professionals [15]. In addition, the analysis of actual recordings of medical consultations has facilitated the identification of person-centered strategies that strengthen the therapeutic bond with older adults [16, 17].

The use of aging simulation suits is an innovative methodology that allows participants to experience firsthand the physical and sensory limitations associated with advanced age, enabling a greater understanding of the experiences, behavior patterns, and requirements of older people. By simulating aspects related to aging, such as restricted mobility, impaired visual and auditory perception, and decreased manual dexterity, these devices offer a realistic experience of the daily challenges faced by older adults, establishing themselves as a valuable resource for guiding and enriching learning [18].

However, despite their usefulness, there is limited scientific evidence regarding the effectiveness of aging simulation suits in developing empathic attitudes toward older adults, particularly as measured by validated self-report instruments such as the Jefferson Scale of Empathy. Various studies have analyzed the effectiveness of using aging simulation suits in educational contexts, especially in the field of health. One possible explanation for these inconsistent findings relates to methodological heterogeneity across studies. Most investigations have relied primarily on clinician or student self-report questionnaires to assess empathy, whereas relatively few have incorporated patient-reported outcomes or observational assessments of empathic behavior. Differences in measurement approach may therefore partially explain variability in reported effectiveness, as self-perceived empathy does not always correlate with patient-experienced empathy. In this regard, some studies indicate that the impact of these interventions may be predominantly cognitive, with improvements in understanding the difficulties associated with advanced age, but without a significant increase in self-reported clinical empathy scores [19]. This difference suggests that the suit may promote awareness and knowledge more than the emotional response.

Moreover, studies conducted among undergraduate students in social and health sciences programs have generally reported more positive outcomes following exposure to aging simulation suits. In these cases, improvements have been identified in self-reported attitudes and empathy toward older adults after using the suit, supporting its value as an educational tool in the training of future social and healthcare professionals [20–22]. These findings may reflect the greater receptiveness of students, who are still developing their professional identity, to experiential learning interventions. Nonetheless, the need for further research to optimize the effectiveness of these interventions is emphasized [23].

In summary, although aging simulation suits show promise in improving knowledge, attitudes, and empathic behavior toward older adults, evidence regarding their impact on clinical empathy in practicing professionals remains limited and methodologically heterogeneous. Further rigorously designed trials are required to clarify their specific contribution and underlying mechanisms. Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate the impact of an aging simulation suit on clinical empathy, including its cognitive and affective dimensions, in social and healthcare professionals working in nursing homes.

Materials and methods

Participants and recruitment

A randomized controlled trial was designed with a sample of healthcare professionals (nurses, nursing assistants, occupational therapists, doctors, psychologists, and physiotherapists) recruited from four nursing homes: two in the Community of Madrid and two in the Principality of Asturias. The distribution of participants across centers (final analyzed sample, n = 82) was as follows: Study Center 1 (n = 36), Study Center 2 (n = 27), Study Center 3 (n = 12), and Study Center 4 (n = 7). All participants were invited to take part in the study during their working hours, on a voluntary basis and without any financial or other form of advantage or disadvantage. To be eligible, participants had to meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) being a healthcare professional and (2) working at the assigned center with direct contact with residents. The exclusion criterion was: (1) failure to sign the written informed consent to participate in the study.

Participants were randomly allocated to either the experimental group (EG) or the control group (CG). The unit of randomization was the individual healthcare professional, not the participating nursing home. Participants from the four nursing homes were individually assigned to either group using a computer-generated allocation sequence created with the “blockrand” package in R, employing block randomization (block size = 10) and a 1:1 allocation ratio to ensure balanced group sizes throughout the recruitment process. The allocation sequence was generated prior to participant enrollment by a member of the research team who was not involved in outcome assessment.

After providing written informed consent, participants were enrolled at each participating center and assigned sequentially according to the pre-generated randomization list. The allocation list was stored securely and was not accessible to personnel involved in outcome assessment, thereby reducing the risk of selection bias. In addition, both groups followed the same assessment schedule and were evaluated under equivalent conditions, which contributed to reducing potential measurement bias and ensuring comparability between groups. Due to the nature of the intervention (educational session alone versus educational session combined with simulation), blinding of participants and interventionists was not feasible.

All questionnaires were securely stored in a locked research cabinet that only the research team members could access. Data were collected solely for academic research and publication purposes. This study adheres to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines. The study was approved by the ethics committee of the Universidad Rey Juan Carlos. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the ICH Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice and in full compliance with the relevant regulations (Biomedical Research Act 14 /2007). Clinical trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov, Unique Protocol ID: 2,711,201,916,919; ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT07280689. Date of registration: October 10, 2025. The trial was registered retrospectively due to prolonged technical disruptions associated with the migration from EudraCT to the EU Clinical Trials Information System (CTIS), under EU Regulation 536/2014, which prevented prospective registration during the project initiation period (September 2022). The protocol was submitted to ClinicalTrials.gov on October 10, 2025, and the identifier NCT07280689 was assigned on December 1, 2025. Although registration occurred after study initiation, all objectives, methods, and outcome measures had been defined a priori, as evidenced by the Ethics Committee approval obtained in September 2022, ensuring the transparency and integrity of the research.

Sample size

Sample size was determined through a priori power analysis conducted using G*Power based on a mixed-design ANOVA with one within-subject factor (Time: pre vs. post) and one between-subject factor (Group: experimental vs. control), focusing on the interaction effect (Group × Time). A medium effect size (f = 0.25), according to Cohen’s conventions, was assumed. The analysis was performed with a significance level of α = 0.05 and a statistical power of 0.80. A correlation of 0.5 between repeated measures and a sphericity correction of 1 were assumed. The required total sample size was estimated at 54 participants (27 per group). The final analyzed sample (n = 82; 41 per group) exceeded this requirement. No attrition occurred after randomization, and group allocation remained balanced throughout the study. Although participants were recruited from four nursing homes, randomization was conducted at the individual level. Given the relatively small cluster sizes, clustering effects were not modeled. Given the relatively small and unequal number of participants per center, the estimation of the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was not considered appropriate.

Intervention

The main objective of the intervention was to improve the clinical empathy of social and healthcare professionals working with older people in long-term care facilities (nursing homes). Adverse events were monitored throughout the intervention session. Participants were instructed to report any discomfort, dizziness, instability, or other physical symptoms experienced while using the aging simulation suit. Study staff directly supervised all activities to ensure safety. Any incidents such as falls or physical injury were to be recorded immediately.

Intervention group

Participants in the EG received a structured educational session focused on aging, functional limitations, and, in particular, the relationship between empathy and social and health care. This theoretical training included concepts on the importance of empathy in the quality of care, its cognitive and emotional components, and strategies to improve it in clinical and nursing home settings. Additionally, an experiential activity was implemented using the GERT (Niederstotzingen, Germany) aging simulation suit. This suit is designed to replicate the physical and sensory limitations associated with the aging process, allowing professionals to directly experience the difficulties faced by older adults in their daily activities. Participants were first provided with instructions regarding the correct use of the suit and safety considerations. Once equipped, they performed a predefined and standardized sequence of activities representative of daily tasks in nursing home settings under the supervision of the trained researcher to ensure consistency across centers. These activities included going up and down stairs, moving around the facility, performing activities of daily living (such as getting in and out of bed, sitting down and standing up from the toilet, and putting clothes away in the closet), and interacting with other individuals in the environment.

The total duration of the intervention session was approximately two hours. The theoretical educational component lasted one hour and was followed by a one-hour experiential activity using the GERT aging simulation suit (mean exposure time = 60 min). Both components were delivered consecutively during the same session.

Control group

Participants allocated to the CG attended a structured, instructor-led educational session lasting approximately 60 min. The session included: (1) an overview of physiological and sensory changes associated with aging; (2) common functional limitations in older adults; (3) theoretical foundations of empathy in healthcare; and (4) practical communication strategies aimed at enhancing empathic interactions in long-term care settings.

The session was delivered in a classroom-based format using a standardized slide presentation and guided discussion. Participants remained seated throughout the session and did not engage in any experiential, role-playing, or simulation-based activities.

To ensure methodological consistency across centers, the same trained researcher delivered all sessions following a predefined structure and standardized content. Due to the nature of the intervention (educational session alone versus educational session combined with simulation), blinding of participants and the interventionist was not feasible.

This control condition was designed to account for exposure to empathy-related theoretical material while isolating the added effect of the immersive simulation component.

Measurements

Each participant completed an ad hoc questionnaire to collect socio-demographic and professional data, including age, gender, educational level, place of birth, native language, years of employment in the institution, and years of experience working with geriatric patients. No specific information was gathered regarding previous formal training in communication skills or empathy. Although participants were healthcare professionals with prior academic and clinical education, prior exposure to structured empathy training was not systematically recorded.

All outcome measures were administered at two time points for both study groups: (1) a baseline assessment conducted after informed consent was obtained and prior to randomization and intervention, and (2) an immediate post-intervention assessment performed after completion of the educational session (and the simulation activity in the experimental group). Both groups followed the same assessment schedule. All assessments were conducted following a standardized procedure across participating centers. Questionnaires were administered in the same order and under comparable conditions for both groups, and data collection was supervised by members of the research team to ensure consistency in administration.

Two complementary instruments were used to assess empathy. The Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI) was included to measure dispositional (trait) empathy as a multidimensional personality construct in the general population. In contrast, the Jefferson Scale of Empathy–Health Professions version (JSPE-HPS) was selected to specifically assess clinical empathy as a professional competency in healthcare settings. The use of both instruments allowed us to differentiate between general empathic disposition and context-specific clinical empathy. However, both the IRI and the JSPE-HPS are self-report instruments, which may introduce response bias or social desirability effects.

Empathy is a multidimensional construct encompassing dispositional, affective, cognitive, and context-specific components. For this reason, two complementary instruments were employed. The Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI) was used to assess dispositional empathy, reflecting relatively stable empathic tendencies in the general population, whereas the Jefferson Scale of Empathy–Health Professions version (JSPE-HPS) was selected to capture empathy specifically within the clinical and professional context. The use of both instruments allowed for a comprehensive assessment of empathy by distinguishing between dispositional and context-specific clinical components. However, as both measures rely on self-report, they may be subject to response biases such as social desirability or subjective perception of change.

Davis Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI) [20]: This scale is especially useful in research on the multidimensionality of the empathic process in the general population. It is adapted to Spanish [21] and consists of 28 items distributed in four subscales that measure four dimensions of the integrative concept of empathy: perspective taking, fantasy, empathic concern, and distress or personal discomfort, with seven items each. The perspective taking (dimension 1), and fantasy (dimension 3) subscales assess the more cognitive processes, while the empathic concern (dimension 2) and personal distress (dimension 4) subscales measure people’s emotional reactions to the experiences of others. The Spanish version of the IRI has demonstrated adequate psychometric properties in adult populations, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from 0.56 to 0.70 across subscales and evidence of construct validity [21].

Jefferson Scale of Empathy - Health Professions version (JSPE-HPS) [22]: this scale assesses empathy in the context of the clinical relationship from a cognitive approach and from the perspective of emotions and is composed of 20 items within three dimensions: dimension 1 perspective taking, dimension 2 compassionate care, and dimension 3 putting oneself in the patient’s place. They are scored according to a Likert scale from one (strongly disagree) to seven (strongly agree). Scores range from a minimum of 20 to a maximum of 140 and the higher the score, the higher the participant’s level of empathy. The reliability and validity of the JSPE-HPS have previously been demonstrated [23–27]. The Spanish version of the scale also shows adequate psychometric properties, with a Cronbach’s Alpha of 0.786; and an ICC of 0.90 (95% CI = 0.86–0.93; p < .0001). The resulting model showed an acceptable fit (χ2/gl = 1.97, p < .001; comparative fit index [CFI] = 0.93; Root means square error of approximation [RMSEA] = 0.05).

Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was the change in clinical empathy as measured by the total score of the Jefferson Scale of Empathy–Health Professions version (JSPE-HPS), as the intervention was specifically designed to enhance empathy in professional healthcare settings.

Secondary outcomes included changes in dispositional empathy as measured by the total Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI) score. Analyses of individual subscales from both instruments were considered exploratory.

Although the trial was registered retrospectively, the JSPE-HPS total score was defined a priori as the primary outcome, given the intervention’s specific focus on empathy within professional healthcare practice.

Several procedures were implemented to ensure consistency and data quality throughout the study. The same trained researcher delivered all intervention sessions using standardized content and structure across centers. Data collection followed a uniform protocol, and questionnaires were securely stored and handled exclusively by the research team. No attrition occurred after randomization, and baseline equivalence between groups was confirmed, supporting the internal consistency of the study procedures.

Statistical analysis

For the descriptive statistical analysis of the sample, the basic descriptive methods were used: for qualitative variables, the number of cases present in each category, and the corresponding percentage were calculated, and for quantitative variables, the mean and standard deviation were calculated.

Baseline comparability between the control and experimental groups was assessed prior to the main analyses. Chi-square (χ²) tests were performed to examine potential differences in sociodemographic categorical variables between groups following randomization. No statistically significant baseline differences were observed. In addition, independent-samples t-tests were conducted to verify the absence of pre-intervention differences in empathy scores.

To examine the potential differences in pre- and post-intervention scores on the IRI and JSPE-HPS scales, two-factor ANOVA tests with repeated measures on one factor were conducted using the General Linear Model procedure. Pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. Given the absence of baseline group differences in IRI total and subscale scores, adjustment for dispositional empathy was not deemed necessary in the primary analysis. Although trait empathy may theoretically act as a covariate influencing clinical empathy scores, the repeated-measures ANOVA approach was therefore retained. Future studies may consider modeling trait empathy as a covariate to further isolate the specific effect of simulation-based interventions.

Effect sizes were estimated using partial eta-squared (ηp²) for ANOVA analyses and interpreted according to conventional thresholds (small: 0.01, medium: 0.06, large: 0.14).

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 29.0.2.0 for Windows. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < .05.

Results

No adverse events, including falls, injuries, or significant physical discomfort, were reported during the intervention sessions. Discussion

The aim of this study was to examine the effects of an immersive aging simulation suit experience on self-reported clinical empathy levels among social and healthcare professionals working in nursing homes, compared with conventional theoretical training.

It is important to acknowledge the conceptual complexity of empathy, as there is no single, universally accepted definition. Recent literature has highlighted the overlap between empathy, empathic concern, and compassion, particularly in self-report instruments. Accordingly, the IRI and the JSPE-HPS operationalize empathy from different theoretical perspectives, and their findings should be interpreted as reflecting distinct, albeit related, constructs rather than as indicators of the superiority of one measure over another [27].

Empathy and compassion, although related, are distinct constructs. Empathy generally refers to the ability to understand and share another person’s emotional state, whereas compassion includes an additional motivational component directed toward alleviating that suffering. Some subscales within commonly used instruments, such as “Compassionate Care” in the JSPE-HPS or “Empathic Concern” in the IRI, may partially capture compassion-related aspects. This conceptual overlap should be considered when interpreting the results of self-report measures of empathy [27].

Historically, most interventions aimed at training empathy have focused on health science students, using simulation-based modalities to introduce and reinforce these skills in controlled classroom settings [28, 29]. However, although such approaches have shown considerable potential for novice trainees, there is growing concern about the lack of evidence exploring empathy training in practicing professionals who, despite being immersed in direct patient care, also face challenges such as burnout and empathy erosion due to the demands of clinical practice [29, 30]. Immersive interventions incorporating simulation suits have the potential to reverse or mitigate this decline by offering experiential reminders of patient vulnerability and the importance of compassionate care, as shown in the work by Foster et al. [30] and Can et al. [31].

In educational settings, especially among students who have not yet experienced the complexity of direct patient care, immersive tools are highly valued for their ability to provide a “safe space” in which to experience, reflect on, and internalize the emotional components of clinical encounters without risk of harm to the patient [29, 32]. However, since these interventions are often conducted in controlled classroom or laboratory settings, they lack the complexity and immediacy of actual clinical practice, an aspect that is particularly relevant when students transition from academic programs to actual professional roles [33].

In contrast, practicing healthcare professionals already have extensive direct contact with patients, and their levels of empathy are influenced not only by prior training but also by the daily challenges of clinical practice, including time pressure and emotional exhaustion [29]. These professionals may have developed deeply ingrained clinical habits and knowledge bases that, while beneficial in many ways, can lead to a decline in spontaneous empathetic responses over time, especially in the face of high clinical volumes or stressful working conditions [30]. As reported by Levett-Jones et al. [34] in their study, the dynamic nature of empathy requires that training be viewed not as a single intervention, but as a continuum extending from the early stages of education to professional practice.

Delivering the intervention within the clinical workplace may facilitate contextualized reflection and immediate integration of empathic attitudes into routine professional interactions. Furthermore, it not only contributes to filling the current evidence gap, but also proposes a scalable and sustainable approach to empathy education that can be generalized across diverse healthcare settings and professional disciplines. The experimental design of our study included a CG that received a structured theoretical educational session focused on aging and functional limitations, with particular emphasis on the relationship between empathy and social and health care. This session addressed the importance of empathy in the quality of care, its cognitive and emotional components, and specific strategies to enhance empathic skills in clinical and nursing home settings. The EG received the same theoretical training, which was integrated with practical experience using the simulation suit, ensuring that the immersive component complemented rather than replaced the theoretical session. This design provided a clear comparison that allowed us to evaluate the impact of experiential learning on empathy. Studies such as that by Han and Kim [35] support the validity of this experimental design, as they compared an experiential intervention based on simulation with a theoretical intervention among social workers, finding significant improvements in cognitive empathy and stress reduction in the group that experienced the simulation.

Our results indicated that, although participants in the EG showed significant improvements in clinical empathy as measured by the JSPE scale, with the exception of a single item, no significant differences were observed in IRI scores between groups. This divergence likely reflects the fact that the two instruments assess different dimensions of empathy. While dispositional empathy, as measured by the IRI, may be relatively stable and less susceptible to short-term interventions, the JSPE-HPS focuses on context-specific, professionally oriented empathy, which may be more amenable to change through experiential training. In line with this finding, Hojat et al. [36] and Maximiano-Barreto et al. [37] highlight the difference in the way these instruments capture changes in clinical empathy scores. The divergent findings between the IRI and the JSPE-HPS may be explained by differences in construct sensitivity and contextual framing. The IRI measures relatively stable, trait-like empathic dispositions that are not specifically anchored to professional practice, making them less susceptible to short-term educational interventions. In contrast, the JSPE-HPS is explicitly framed within the healthcare context and assesses modifiable professional attitudes and orientations toward patient care. The experiential nature of the simulation suit may therefore have activated context-specific cognitive and affective processes directly relevant to clinical interactions, without substantially altering broader personality-based empathic tendencies.

These findings have relevant practical implications. The improvement observed in JSPE-HPS, but not in IRI scores, suggests that brief immersive interventions may be particularly effective in producing short-term changes in context-specific, clinically enacted empathy, such as perspective taking and compassionate care in daily practice. However, the absence of changes in dispositional empathy highlights that more sustained or repeated interventions may be required to achieve longer-term modifications in stable empathic traits. Therefore, aging simulation suits may be best conceptualized as catalysts for immediate empathic activation, whose effects should be reinforced over time through longitudinal training strategies to ensure durability and integration into routine clinical care.

A growing number of studies support the idea that immersive simulation tools, such as aging simulation suits, promote experiential learning and enhance more cognitive forms of empathy linked to patient care, leading to improvements in JSPE-HPS scores in both students and healthcare professionals [38]. This pattern is consistent with our results and those of the study by Gerhardy et al. [39], suggesting that, while the simulation suit improved participants’ ability to understand and appreciate the challenges faced by older adults, these improvements were specifically reflected in their professional empathic orientation rather than in general dispositional traits of empathy.

Thus, the lack of significant changes in IRI scores after the intervention may indicate that, while simulation interventions can influence clinical and context-specific empathic skills, they may not induce substantial transformations in broader and more stable aspects of empathy [40]. Although baseline JSPE-HPS scores were relatively high in both groups, this finding is consistent with previous research reporting elevated self-perceived clinical empathy scores among practicing healthcare professionals. Rather than indicating that the population was unsuitable, this suggests that the intervention was tested in a context where empathy is already valued, making the observed improvement particularly noteworthy despite a potential ceiling effect. These results highlight the importance of selecting empathy measures that are sensitive to change and capable of capturing modifiable, context-specific dimensions, particularly in samples with high baseline levels.

Regarding exposure and maintenance of results, throughout the study, scores on the JSPE-HPS scale increased in both groups, suggesting a general trend toward the development of greater clinical empathy over time, possibly as a result of exposure to the theoretical educational sessions received.

When analyzing the results by group, participants in the CG showed no statistically significant changes in their scores on the JSPE-HPS scale from pre- to post-intervention, whereas those in the EG demonstrated a significant increase. This finding, consistent with the results of previous research [41, 42], supports the hypothesis that the immersive experience provided by the use of the simulation suit promotes the development of empathic attitude in social and healthcare professionals who work with older people. Although the absolute mean difference (7.49 points) may appear modest within a 140-point scale, the observed partial eta squared (η² = 0.145) corresponds to a large effect according to conventional benchmarks. Converting this effect size into a standardized mean difference yields an estimated Cohen’s d of approximately 0.82, indicating a substantial intervention impact. Although Cohen’s d and Hedges’ g are not identical—Hedges’ g includes a small-sample correction factor—they are conceptually equivalent measures of standardized mean differences and converge in samples of this size. Notably, this magnitude exceeds the pooled average effect (Hedges’ g ≈ 0.58) reported in recent meta-analytic research on empathy training in healthcare professionals by Paulus and Meinken [43]. This positioning above the pooled mean suggests that even a relatively brief training session may produce a meaningful and practically relevant improvement in clinical empathy.

Although the increase observed in JSPE scores may appear modest in absolute terms, recent evidence indicates that clinician empathy is meaningfully associated with patient outcomes and care quality. Licciardone et al. [44] reported that higher physician empathy was linked to improved pain outcomes and health-related quality of life in chronic pain patients over 12 months. Similarly, Keshtkar et al. [45], in a systematic review of randomized trials, found that practitioner empathy significantly improved patient satisfaction—an outcome closely related to adherence, trust, and overall quality of care. Together, these findings support the view that even moderate increases in clinical empathy scores are not merely reflective of self-perception but correspond to clinically relevant improvements in patient–professional interactions [44–46].

In line with these findings, Bas-Sarmiento et al. [47] reported in a recent systematic review that experiential interventions are generally more effective than educational strategies based solely on a humanistic approach. However, the current scientific literature includes few studies [48] that specify which dimensions of the JSPE-HPS scale show significant changes after the use of an aging simulation suit.

In this regard, and as various authors have pointed out [49, 50], it is essential to analyze the different dimensions of clinical empathy separately, especially the cognitive and affective components, given that these do not evolve in a homogeneous manner [51]. Various validations by the JSPE-HPS identify that the Perspective Taking and Standing in the Patient’s Shoes subscales correspond to the cognitive domain, while Compassionate Care is related to the emotional or affective domain [52]. In accordance with the findings of Ozdemir et al. [48], our intervention with the simulated aging suit showed statistically significant differences in Perspective Taking and Compassionate Care. This could be explained by the fact that wearing the suit facilitates an immediate and tangible understanding of the physical and sensory limitations associated with aging, directly impacting the Perspective Taking dimension, while also activating a regulated affective response that awakens compassion towards others, thus influencing Compassionate Care.

Furthermore, the absence of significant changes in the Standing in the Patient’s Shoes dimension may be explained by the nature of this construct, which represents a deeper and more internalized cognitive empathy, often associated with a more existential or experiential experience [49]. Development of this type of empathy likely requires more prolonged interventions to foster meaningful change [48]. The duration of the intervention carried out in this study may not have been sufficient to promote changes in this subscale, which requires a more complex and sustained process of empathic internalization.

This study is not without limitations. First, participants were not blinded to group allocation, as those in the experimental group were aware of wearing the aging simulation suit. This lack of blinding may have introduced performance bias, potentially influencing post-intervention responses. Additionally, the exclusive use of self-reported measures (IRI and JSPE-HPS) may have introduced response biases, such as social desirability or perceived expectations regarding the intervention, possibly leading to an overestimation of changes in empathy. The repeated administration of the same instruments may also have contributed to test–retest effects.

Furthermore, no objective outcome measures—such as patient-reported outcomes or observational assessments of professional–patient interactions—were included. As a result, it remains unclear whether increases in self-reported empathy translate into meaningful changes in real-world clinical practice. Previous research has shown that clinicians’ self-assessed empathy does not necessarily align with patients’ perceptions or externally observed behaviours; therefore, the present findings should be interpreted with caution.

Contamination between groups cannot be ruled out, as participants from both groups worked within the same four nursing homes and may have communicated informally about the intervention. Additionally, as participants were recruited from a limited number of centres, potential clustering effects were not accounted for in the analysis, which may have introduced intra-centre similarities influencing the observed outcomes. The inclusion of only four centres may also limit the generalizability of the findings to other long-term care settings. Therefore, findings should be interpreted with caution, and future studies should consider multilevel approaches or estimation of intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) to better account for clustering effects. Furthermore, the high baseline empathy scores observed in both groups suggest a potential ceiling effect, which may have reduced the ability to detect meaningful changes.

From an analytical perspective, multiple subscales from both instruments were examined, and findings from secondary and exploratory analyses should therefore be interpreted cautiously due to the risk of multiplicity and inflation of Type I error. However, the primary outcome was pre-specified at the total scale level, reducing concerns regarding multiplicity for the main effect. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

The intervention consisted of a single-session experience, and no longitudinal follow-up was conducted. Consequently, the durability of the observed improvements in clinical empathy remains uncertain. In addition, prior formal training in communication skills or empathy was not specifically assessed. Although participants were qualified healthcare professionals, previous exposure to empathy-related training may have influenced baseline scores and responsiveness to the intervention.

Future research should address these limitations by incorporating objective outcome measures, including patient-reported evaluations and observational assessments, as well as systematically collecting data on prior empathy training. Longitudinal designs with repeated or sustained interventions are needed to determine whether improvements in empathy are maintained over time and translate into lasting behavioural changes in clinical practice. Additionally, future studies could examine the role of individual differences, such as baseline empathy traits, as covariates, and explore whether professional background or years of experience moderate responsiveness to simulation-based interventions.

Finally, although this study did not directly assess long-term retention or transfer to practice, it is important to consider that empathy is influenced not only by individual-level training but also by broader organisational and environmental factors, such as workload, institutional culture, and resource availability [53]. Future research should adopt a more systemic perspective, examining how these contextual factors interact with immersive interventions to optimise empathic practice in healthcare settings.

Although several methodological strategies were implemented to enhance internal consistency—such as standardized procedures, allocation concealment, and equivalent assessment conditions—these factors should be considered when interpreting the findings. In particular, the reliance on short-term, self-reported outcomes and the specific study context may limit the generalizability of the results to other settings or to long-term behavioral changes.

Despite these limitations, this study offers relevant implications for clinical practice, particularly in the field of promoting empathy among healthcare professionals. The use of aging simulation suits, by providing an immersive and tangible experience of the limitations associated with aging, can promote the development of greater understanding towards older people. This training tool could contribute to strengthening the therapeutic relationship and, ultimately, to more person-centered care.

Conclusion

This randomized controlled trial indicates that adding an aging simulation suit to a brief theoretical training program can enhance specific dimensions of clinical empathy among long-term care professionals. While dispositional empathy remained unchanged, improvements were observed in perspective taking and compassionate care following the immersive experience. These findings support the use of immersive simulation as a complementary strategy to traditional didactic approaches in empathy training for long-term care professionals.
