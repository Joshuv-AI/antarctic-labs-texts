AI-driven collision risk prediction and debris optimization in low earth orbit
Authors: Emad Alkhazraji, Mohamed E. Yahia, R. E. Tolba, N. S. Alharthi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Debris congestion and collision in low Earth orbit have become a major point of concern due to the rapid expansion of activities in that region. In this work, an AI-driven framework is developed and deployed to predict, analyze, and optimize collision risk across the altitude inclination space. Local debris object population, mass density distribution, and conjunction derived risk are computed. Then, an AI surrogate model, viz., gradient-boosted decision trees, was trained to predict collision conjunction relative probabilities and risk. Thereafter, data analysis and explain ability techniques are utilized to uncover underlying risk drivers and reveal how altitude, inclination, mass loading, and crowding interact across the orbital space. The analyses showed how the AI surrogate model reliably captures underlying nonlinear risk behavior and interactions. More importantly, it suggested that meaningful capacity increases are achievable with only a moderate rise in predicted risk. Finally, the AI surrogate model was employed in a multi-objective evolutionary optimization to quantify the trade off between minimizing collision risk and maximizing usable orbital capacity, revealing a set of optimal, feasible, and well-balanced points of operation in this regime. Ultimately, the proposed pipeline offers a computationally efficient pathway for prediction and interpreting orbital safety, guiding constellation deployment, and supporting sustainable space traffic management strategies.

Introduction

The proliferation of human-made objects in the low Earth orbit (LEO) has led to a significant increase in space debris, posing substantial risks to operational spacecraft. Space debris encompasses non-functional objects that range from defunct satellites and spent rocket stages to smaller fragments resulting from collisions or disintegration events1–3. In 2022, over 70% of all objects in the LEO were classified as debris, underscoring its critical density in this orbital region2,4. The primary concern associated with this accumulation is the potential for collisions, which can generate even more debris and potentially trigger Kessler syndrome, a cascade effect whereby collisions with debris create more debris, leading to further collisions and rendering certain orbital regions unusable2. This is particularly relevant in the LEO, which is heavily utilized for various space activities, including the deployment of large satellite constellations5,6. No doubt that the explosive increase in man-made objects in low Earth orbit (LEO) has led to a substantial increase in space debris. This poses a significant risk to operational spacecraft, satellite missions, and the long-term sustainability of space activities1,2,7,8. The international community is also grappling with the legal and governance challenges space debris poses. Increasing congestion in near-Earth orbits, driven by the rapid growth of satellites and debris, directly threatens the sustainable use of space9. Legal frameworks such as the 1967 Space Treaty are being examined to understand their applicability to managing and mitigating satellite debris in the LEO8. There is a recognized urgency for addressing space debris, with international bodies like the United Nations Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space taking steps to inform states about the importance of preventing debris generation10.

Various approaches have been taken to address the challenge of space debris, including detection and tracking. Traditional ground-based equipment faces limitations in observing fast-moving LEO debris due to atmospheric interference and restricted fields of view11. To overcome these challenges, new technologies, such as optical space-based surveillance networks for tracking LEO debris, are being explored11. Debris removal is also crucial. One promising area involves using miniaturized satellites, such as CubeSats, with robotic arms to deorbit larger debris objects4. This approach is economically viable and offers shorter design-to-deployment times compared to larger missions4. The guidance for such missions is being optimized through convex-optimization-based model predictive control algorithms, which account for factors such as J2 perturbation, drag, and eclipses12. Moreover, risk management and control remain paramount pre- and during missions13,14.

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) techniques are being developed to improve the accuracy of orbital lifetime estimation for LEO satellites, which is required to comply with debris mitigation guidelines, such as the 25-year deorbit rule established by the Inter-Agency Space Debris Coordination Committee (IADC)15. Traditional numerical orbit propagation methods, while accurate with high-precision force models, can be computationally intensive. ML approaches are faster and comparably accurate, thus enabling a global assessment of guideline compliance15. In collision avoidance, AI-driven optimization frameworks are essential for managing the inherent uncertainty in collision risk assessment16. Space debris poses a significant threat, increasing the likelihood of mission failure or catastrophic damage. AI assists in optimizing collision avoidance maneuvers by accounting for uncertainties in factors such as conjunction probabilities and orbital perturbations16. For the detection and tracking of space debris, AI has been integrated into various advanced systems, including space-based surveillance networks11, radar systems17, LiDAR sensors18, density model optimization19, mission planning for active removal20, and rendezvous trajectory generation21.

In this work, we employ an ML model and an AI surrogate to predict the conjunction probability of and risk from LEO debris. The surrogate model acts as a fast, efficient way to predict newly detected debris objects. Furthermore, the model is then utilized to further analyze different parameters that affect the conjunction factors using AI analysis techniques, namely principal component analysis (PCA) and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). Lastly, enabled by its fast and efficient nature, the AI surrogate model is then utilized to perform a genetic algorithm to optimize different conditions and parameters. The contribution of this work is not the introduction of new individual methodologies, but rather their structured integration into a unified workflow that links prediction, interpretation, and optimization within a single consistent framework. This integration enables a more comprehensive analysis of orbital risk by connecting model outputs with interpretable feature contributions and decision-oriented trade-off exploration. While prior studies have addressed collision risk estimation, data-driven modeling, or optimization independently, the proposed approach provides a cohesive pipeline that enables these components to be applied jointly, allowing for improved interpretability and decision support.

Methodology

In this work, we utilized the LEO−Catalog dataset cordially provided by LEOLABS22. The dataset catalogued 25,000+ debris objects in LEO, categorized into fragments, payloads, and rocket bodies, detailing each object’s launch date, country of origin, apogee, perigee, and inclination. The catalog includes records spanning from 1959 to 2025, reflecting the historical evolution of the orbital environment. However, the present analysis is conducted using a static snapshot of the dataset at the time of acquisition to ensure consistency in model training and evaluation. In other words, temporal dynamics are not explicitly modeled in this work. This approach allows the study to focus on spatial and population-based risk patterns rather than temporal forecasting. Missing values were handled using a combination of filtering and simple imputation strategies. Non-critical missing entries were replaced using default or zero-filled values where appropriate, while ensuring that all key variables required for risk estimation were retained and validated.

Despite these simplifications, the proxy preserves the relative variation of collision risk across different regions of the orbital environment, which is the primary objective of this study. Surrogate modeling

The next step in our analysis is to build and train surrogate models that predict the collision probability proxy and relative collision risk at each altitude and inclination bin, based on the variables (features) discussed in subsection 2.1, namely altitude, inclination, object density, count, and combined mass. In particular, the XGBoost (eXtreme Gradient Boosting) machine learning model was employed. The benefit of using the surrogate model is twofold: (1) Pose as a fast and efficient surrogate (replacement) model that can predict the conjunction probability proxy and risk of any newly detected debris object. (2) Open the door to further analysis of the dependencies and correlation between the given features and conjunction probability proxy and relative risk (as discussed in the next section). XGBoost is a highly efficient, machine learning model for supervised tasks like classification and regression, known for its speed, performance, and scalability, especially with large datasets like the one at hand. XGBoost was selected as the surrogate modeling approach due to its strong performance on structured tabular datasets and its ability to capture nonlinear relationships and feature interactions. Compared to traditional ensemble methods such as Random Forests, gradient boosting models typically provide improved predictive accuracy and better handling of complex dependencies. In addition, XGBoost offers efficient training and seamless integration with explainability frameworks such as SHAP, which are essential for interpreting model predictions in this study. While alternative models such as LightGBM and Random Forests are also widely used, the focus of this work is not on benchmarking different machine learning algorithms, but rather on demonstrating an integrated framework that combines prediction, interpretation, and optimization. Therefore, XGBoost is adopted as a representative high-performance model suitable for this application. Initially, the LEO Catalog dataset was divided as 80–20% between training and test datasets, respectively, for validation. The proposed framework is designed as a decision-support tool for analyzing orbital congestion and guiding satellite deployment strategies. The workflow consists of three main stages: data preprocessing, surrogate model prediction, and multi-objective optimization. Model training is performed offline and requires standard computational resources for gradient boosting models. Once trained, the surrogate model enables rapid inference, with prediction times on the order of milliseconds per sample. This allows efficient evaluation of large design spaces during optimization. The optimization process introduces additional computational cost, but remains tractable for moderate population sizes and generation counts. The framework is therefore suitable for scenario analysis and constellation design studies, where near real-time performance is sufficient. It is important to note that the proposed approach is not intended for real-time collision avoidance at the individual object level, but rather for higher-level risk assessment and strategic planning.

SHAP interpretability

SHAP is a technique that assigns a contribution value to each input feature for each prediction made by a surrogate model. It is based on cooperative game theory and provides local interpretability by aggregating feature contributions across all inputs. SHAP analysis is used here to interpret the behavior of the trained surrogate model by quantifying the contribution of each feature to the predicted output. It is important to note that these explanations reflect the internal structure of the model and do not, by themselves, establish causal relationships within the physical orbital environment. While SHAP does not provide causal inference, the identified feature contributions are consistent with established physical intuition, such as the influence of object density and population on collision likelihood. This agreement supports the credibility of the surrogate model while maintaining a clear distinction between correlation and causation. Figure 4 depicts the SHAP summary plot showing the effect each feature has on the predicted output (Risk). Features higher on the plot are generally more important, wherein each point represents a single instance’s SHAP value for a specific feature. The SHAP value for a given feature represents its contribution to the model prediction, and is computed as the average marginal contribution of the feature across all possible feature subsets27.Fig. 4SHAP summary plot quantifying the influence of a feature on the predicted risk across the entire design dataset.

SHAP summary plot quantifying the influence of a feature on the predicted risk across the entire design dataset.

While PCA revealed that the physical structure of the LEO is governed mainly by two axes: (1) congestion and mass concentration, and (2) orbital geometry defined by altitude and inclination, SHAP analysis complements this by quantifying how each feature affects the collision risk at the local (micro) level. In addition, the SHAP decision plots shown in Fig. 5 provide further insight into how each variable pushes the predicted risk for a sampled orbital bin. Specifically, Figs. 4 and 5 show that the combined mass, followed by count, then density, are the strongest drivers of risk. Particularly, congestion and mass concentration factors create distinct high-risk corridors in given altitude–inclination regimes. This corroborates the dominance of PC1 that was observed in Fig. 3. However, what SHAP analysis provides is a more detailed breakdown of how each feature ranks in affecting the overall output. Nonetheless, together with the PCA, SHAP provides a holistic explanation of risk formation, enabling interpretable AI-driven forecasting, hotspot detection, and targeted capacity-planning strategies for orbital debris management. Furthermore, underlying nonlinear effects are becoming clear; For instance, doubling mass more than doubles the risk, and the nonlinear factor of Density × Count interaction is strong as Risk surges when both are high. Moreover, the localized spread exhibited by altitude and inclination shows that Risk is not monotonic, but rather concentrated at specific operational regimes. Multi-objective optimization

We conclude this study with formulating a multi-objective optimization problem and solving it using the Non-Dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II). The trained surrogate model enables rapid prediction of collision risk, allowing it to reliably replace computationally heavy physics-based risk simulations during optimization iterations28,29. In particular, two conflicting objectives were adopted: (1) minimizing the collision risk, and (2) maximizing the usable orbital capacity, expressed as the available room for additional spacecraft with respect to a nominal capacity limit per orbital bin (in our case, 120% of the maximum observed count per bin). This capacity constraint is introduced as a relative measure of orbital crowding based on the observed population distribution. This reference value represents a controlled extrapolation beyond current occupancy levels, enabling the exploration of near-future congestion scenarios without extending into unrealistic regimes. This formulation does not imply a strict physical limit, but rather provides a normalized framework for comparing solutions within a bounded and physically interpretable design space. Similarly, the allowable variation range in object count is selected to prevent degenerate solutions while maintaining sufficient flexibility to explore meaningful trade-offs between population density and collision risk. Without constraints, the optimizer converges to trivial solutions in terms of unreasonable altitudes/inclinations, producing low-risk but unrealistic orbital configurations. Thus, we imposed physically motivated constraints that restrict the allowable deviation from the baseline to within a relative window of -50% to +200%. Conclusion

This work presented a holistic AI-powered framework that predicts, analyzes, and optimizes conjunction probability proxy and risk in Earth’s LEO. We demonstrated how AI can act as a robust pathway for both interpretability and computational scalability in space debris risk assessment. The surrogate model’s predictive fidelity is essential for large-scale case exploration, capacity planning, and optimization, where traditional physics-based models would be prohibitively slow or outright unfeasible. The subsequent data analysis methodologies we utilized exposed the dominant drivers shaping the LEO environment. The first component, responsible for ~37% of the total variance, reflected congestion (high population and high mass concentration), while the second component (~29% of the variance) captures orbital configuration. Later on, these findings were reinforced on a micro level by identifying combined mass, count, and density as the strongest local contributors to predicted risk. They also revealed important nonlinear interactions in play, such as the amplified risk when both density and mass rise simultaneously. This all culminated in the final stage, exploiting the practical and computational utility of the surrogate model multi-objective optimization. Competing goals were explored, namely minimizing conjunction risk and maximizing usable orbital capacity. The resulting Pareto frontier provides a rich map of feasible trade-offs. At the extreme low-risk end, the predicted risk values reached ~\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-10}$$\end{document} but required drastically reducing the population density. On the other end (high-capacity end), increasing the usable capacity towards the upper limit only increased the risk incrementally. Between these two extremes lies a sweet middle ground, or rather space, particularly suitable for sustainable constellation deployment. Overall, the proposed holistic framework offers a robust and interpretable decision tool, with the scalability and efficiency needed for real-time what-if analysis for regulators, constellation planners, and space traffic management entities.
