Stochastic Bandits for Crowdsourcing and Multi-Platform Autobidding
François Bachoc, Nicolò Cesa-Bianchi, Tommaso Cesari, Roberto Colomboni

Abstract
Motivated by applications in crowdsourcing, where a fixed sum of money is split among K K workers, and autobidding, where a fixed budget is used to bid in K K simultaneous auctions, we define a stochastic bandit model where arms belong to the K K -dimensional probability simplex and represent the fraction of budget allocated to each task/auction. The reward in each round is the sum of K K stochastic rewards, where each of these rewards is unlocked with a probability that varies with the fraction of the budget allocated to that task/auction. We design an algorithm whose expected regret after T T steps is of order K ​ T K\sqrt{T} (up to log factors) and prove a matching lower bound. Improved bounds of order K ​ ( log ⁡ T ) 2 K(\log T)^{2} are shown when the function mapping budget to probability of unlocking the reward (i.e., terminating the task or winning the auction) satisfies additional diminishing-returns conditions.

Introduction

The multi-armed bandit, a mathematical model for sequential decision-making with partial feedback, has been applied to a wide range of domains, including clinical trials, product recommendation, online advertising, digital markets, and process optimization. A multi-armed bandit consists of an action space and an unknown reward process associated with each action. At every decision round, a bandit algorithm selects an action and observes the reward produced at that round by the process associated with the chosen action. In this work we focus on a setting where rewards are stochastic and actions have a linear structure. However, unlike the classical linear bandit model—where the expected reward is a linear function of the selected action
x
—in our setting the expected reward is
\bigl\langle\mu,F(x)\bigr\rangle
, where
\mu\in[0,1]^{K}
are unknown coefficients and
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{K}\to[0,1]^{K}
is some (possibly nonlinear) function. More specifically, we assume that actions
x
are elements of the
K
-dimensional probability simplex and
F(x)
are the means of
K
Bernoulli random variables. The realizations of these random variables, which the algorithm observes after each round, tell us which of the
K
components of our action contributed to the total reward in that round. This simple variant of linear bandits allows us to analyze new interesting scenarios that were not fully captured by previous analyses:

•

A platform for crowdsourcing splits in each round a fixed sum of money among
K
workers who must complete a set of tasks. Workers will accept and complete their tasks only with a certain probability, defined by the vector
F(x)=\big(F_{1}(x_{1}),\ldots,F_{K}(x_{K})\big)
and depending on each worker’s preferences and reliability that are being affected by the payments
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{K})
, where
x_{k}
is the fraction of money received by the
k
-th worker. The platform’s expected reward in that round,
\bigl\langle\mu,F(x)\bigr\rangle
, depends on the productivity of each worker modeled via
K
random variables with means
\mu\in[0,1]^{K}
.

•

An autobidding agent spends in each round a fixed budget to bid in
K
simultaneous auctions. Auctions are won with probabilities
F(x)=\big(F_{1}(x_{1}),\ldots,F_{K}(x_{K})\big)
depending on the agent’s bids
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{K})
and the competing bids in each auction. The agent’s expected reward in the round,
\bigl\langle\mu,F(x)\bigr\rangle
, depends on the unknown value of each auctioned item, which is modeled via
K
random variables with means
\mu\in[0,1]^{K}
.

Other applications include online model selection, where a computing time budget is split among several instances of a learning system (e.g., a LLM trained with different hyperparameter values) and the expected reward is computed based on the quality of the output produced by those instances that terminated training (or inference) within the allocated time—see, e.g.,
xia2024llm
for work along these lines.

1.4
Techniques and challenges

In this section, we highlight the main challenges arising from the analysis of our setting and the techniques employed to overcome them.

Why off-the-shelf bandit techniques fail.

We first notice that the censored feedback we receive is sufficient to reconstruct bandit feedback, since playing a budget allocation
X_{t}\in\Delta_{K}
at time
t
reveals the learner’s (noisy) instantaneous reward
\bigl\langle G_{t},B_{t}(X_{t})\bigr\rangle
.
Hence, in principle, bandit techniques could be applied to our setting.
However, bandits algorithms have regret guarantees of the order of
\sqrt{HT}
, where
H
is the number of arms, and, in our case, the number of arms is infinite.
This problem is usually tackled in
\mathcal{X}
-armed bandits with discretization methods when the associated (expected) reward function is Lipschitz.
However, in our case, this is
not
necessarily the case since the budget-to-success curves are not Lipschitz in general.
Furthermore, even if they were
L
-Lipschitz, given that the
\frac{1}{n}
-covering number of the unitary simplex
\Delta^{K}
scales as
\Theta(n^{K-1})
, we need a discretization grid with
\Theta(n^{K-1})
points to have every other (potentially optimal) point in the simplex to be at most
\frac{1}{n}
away from some point in the discretization grid.
Hence, the regret guarantees would scale at best as
O\bigl(\frac{L}{n}T+\sqrt{n^{K-1}T}\bigr)
, which, after an optimal tuning for
n
, leads to non-parametric regret guarantees of order
O(L^{(K-1)/(K+1)}T^{K/(K+1)})
.

Worst-case upper bound.

We avoid this non-parametric regret rate by exploiting two key features of our problem.
First, the total reward is the sum of
K
rewards coming from different tasks.
Second, the budget we allocate on each single task leads to a probability of observing the reward associated with the task that is, in expectation, proportional to the reward we earn from it.
Taken together, these facts point us toward an optimistic strategy: the moment a component looks promising, we are incentivized to funnel more budget into it.
That extra budget increases the probability of generating more observations, which either confirm our choice or quickly expose our mistake, letting us reallocate before the damage spreads.
This intuition is formally proven showcasing a structure reminiscent of the elliptical potential lemma
(
lattimore2020bandit
, Lemma 19.4)
, which implies that by following the optimistic strategy the regret coming from each task scales at most as the square root of the number of observations of the reward associated with that task, for an overall worst-case regret of
K\sqrt{T}
(
Theorem

1
).

Worst-case lower bound.

To prove that this is the best we can hope for in general,
even with full feedback access
, poses another significant challenge. To establish this bound, we rely on a series of successive reductions, leveraging techniques coming from statistical hypothesis testing and information theory. This approach allows us to eventually lower bound the worst-case cumulative regret of our problem by translating the instantaneous regret into a sum of binary decision errors across
K
distinct tasks over a total of
2K
possible tasks, each contributing a non-negligible amount to the final regret bound. The high-level intuition is that one can (essentially) embed
K
independent instances of hard
2
-armed online-learning-with-expert-advice problems in our framework. Since the worst-case lower bound of each of these
K
instances is of order
\sqrt{T}
, the intuition (that we prove formally in
Theorem

2
) is that the worst-case lower bound of our problem should be of order at least
K\sqrt{T}
.

Speed-ups.

Several significant challenges arise in proving logarithmic regret rates when the budget-to-success curves exhibit strongly concave properties.

The first challenge is to leverage a self-bounding technique to show that if the optimal budget allocation lies in the interior of the simplex, selecting a budget allocation that maximizes the reward computed from estimated task rewards leads to instantaneous regret that scales quadratically with the distance between our estimated and the true reward vectors.

A second, more subtle challenge is removing the explicit assumption that the optimal allocation is in the interior.
We demonstrate that interior optimality is not an assumption; instead, it follows naturally and non-trivially from the fact that the reward associated with each task is strictly positive.

The final challenge is quantifying precisely how quickly estimation errors vanish under our optimistic allocation rule.
By leveraging concentration inequalities, we show that once the cumulative probability mass allocated to a given task reaches a logarithmic threshold in the time horizon, the number of completed tasks is, with high probability, of the same order as this cumulative mass.
This result implies that the estimation error for each task scales as
\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}
, thereby delivering the desired logarithmic regret guarantee.

Algorithm and worst-case analysis

In this section, we introduce
Algorithm

2
, a UCB-style algorithm designed to operate under censored feedback.
At each round, the learner selects an allocation that optimistically maximizes estimated rewards based on past feedback.
Since rewards are observed only for completed tasks, the algorithm maintains task-wise upper confidence bounds (UCBs) that are updated using importance-weighted estimators based on realized completions.
The confidence width evolves over time as a function of the cumulative completion count and required confidence level.

To lighten the notation, from

0/0\coloneqq 0

1/K
results in an automatic failure.
We now introduce a family of “hard” reward distributions so that each full-feedback algorithm will suffer high regret under at least one of them.
Letting, for all
q\in[0,1]
,

be a Bernoulli distribution with parameter

, we define, for any
j_{1},\dots,j_{K}\in[2]
, a probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{K}}
such that, for each
t\in[T]
and each

:

•

If

, the reward random variables in
(G_{t,2i-1})_{t\in[T]}
have a
\mathcal{B}\bigl(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{T}}}\bigr)
distribution and those in
(G_{t,2i})_{t\in[T]}
have a
\mathcal{B}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})
distribution.

•

If
j_{i}=2
, the reward of the random variables in
(G_{t,2i-1})_{t\in[T]}
have a
\mathcal{B}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})
distribution and those in
(G_{t,2i})_{t\in[T]}
have a
\mathcal{B}\bigl(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{T}}}\bigr)
distribution.

At a high level, we can think of the
2K
tasks
\{1,2,3,4,\dots,2K-1,2K\}
as
K

pairs
of tasks
\bigl\{(1,2),(3,4),\dots,(2K-1,2K)\bigr\}
, and, for each pair
i\in[K]
,
j_{i}=1
means that the first element of the pair is optimal, while if
j_{i}=2
, the second one is.
Indeed, note that, for any
j_{1},\dots,j_{K}\in[2]
and any
i\in[K]
, if
j_{i}=1
, we have that
\mu_{2i-1}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{T}}}

\mu_{2i}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, while, if
j_{i}=2
, it holds that
\mu_{2i-1}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
and
\mu_{2i}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{T}}}
.
Throughout the proof, for any
j_{1},\ldots,j_{K}\in[2]
, when the underlying probability measure is
\mathbb{P}_{j_{1},\dots,j_{K}}
, we refer to the regret up to the time horizon

as

.

Fix any full-feedback algorithm
\mathscr{A}
.
To prove the theorem, we will show that there exists
j_{1},\ldots,j_{K}\in[2]
such that

R_{j_{1},\dots,j_{K},T}=\Omega\bigl(K\sqrt{T}\bigr)
.
We only outline the main steps of the proof here and defer all missing details to
Appendix

B
.
For each
t\in[T]
and
k\in[2K]
, let
I_{t,k}\coloneqq F_{k}(X_{t,k})\in\{0,1\}
.

Step 1.

Due to the specific binary nature of the budget-to-success curves, we can assume (without loss of generality) that
\mathscr{A}
’s budget allocations
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
satisfy
\sum_{k=1}^{2K}I_{t,k}=K
for all
t\in[T]
.
In words,
\mathscr{A}
is never under- or over-investing, but always allocating either
0
or
1/K
budget in each task.

Step 2.

Due to the symmetric way in which, for each
i\in[K]
, a task of the
i
-th pair
(2i-1,2i)
is always better than the other task in the pair, it can be shown with a non-trivial derivation that
\mathscr{A}
gains nothing by investing
1/K
in both tasks of a pair.
Thus, we can assume (without loss of generality) that
\mathscr{A}
’s budget allocations
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
satisfy
I_{t,2i-1}+I_{t,2i}=K
for each
i\in[K]
and
t\in[T]
.
In words,
\mathscr{A}
always invests a budget of exactly
1/K
in one and only one task of each pair
(2i-1,2i)
.

Step 3.

Using the previous two steps along with a careful derivation, we can show that, for any
j_{1},\dots,j_{K}\in[2]
, when the underlying probability measure is
\mathbb{P}_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{K}}
, the regret
R_{T}
of
\mathscr{A}
satisfies

R_{T}\geq\frac{\sqrt{T}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{j_{1:K}}(i),\text{ where }p_{j_{1:K}}(i)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}_{j_{1},\dots,j_{K}}\left[E_{i}^{c}\right],&\text{ if }j_{i}=1,\\
\mathbb{P}_{j_{1},\dots,j_{K}}\left[E_{i}\right],&\text{ if }j_{i}=2,\end{cases}\text{ and }E_{i}\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{T}I_{t,2i-1}\geq\nicefrac{{T}}{{2}}\right\}.

At a high level, this step says that
\mathscr{A}
’s regret can be lower bounded in terms of the probability that
\mathscr{A}
invested in suboptimal tasks for too long.

Step 4.

Finally, through a long and detailed information-theoretic argument, we can prove that, if the environment draws a reward instance
(J_{1},\dots,J_{K})
uniformly at random from
[1,2]^{K}
, then, on average, the terms
p_{j_{1:K}}(i)
in the previous step can be lower bounded by a numerical constant (independent of
T
and
K
), which, together with the previous step, yields
R^{\star}_{T}=\Omega\bigl(K\sqrt{T}\bigr)
.
∎

4
Speed-ups

We now investigate the impact of additional structure on the function space where

live.
In particular, we will show that strong concavity plays a pivotal role in allowing logarithmic rates.
We flesh out the general case in
Appendix

This special case suffices to present some of the main ideas of the general analysis in

Furthermore, this is a special case of particular interest as it models many important behaviors like

Assumption 1

For any

Step 1:

5
Conclusions, limitations, and future work

We introduced a new stochastic bandit framework, which captures applications ranging from crowdsourcing markets to multi-platform autobidding, and to computing allocations in large-scale model training.
We design an algorithm and establish its minimax optimality with a regret guarantee of
\widetilde{\Theta}\bigl(K\sqrt{T}\bigr)
. The same algorithm with no
ad-hoc
tuning enjoys an exponential speed-up when the budget-to-success curves satisfy a natural diminishing-returns property. To the best of our knowledge, these are the first tight guarantees for sequential budget allocation with semi-bandit feedback.

Beyond matching the leading-order rates, our analysis yields two structural insights. First, semi-bandit feedback lets the learner decouple exploration across coordinates from reward estimation, giving a clean
\sqrt{T}
frontier in the worst case. Second, strong concavity acts as an implicit regularizer that yields logarithmic learning rates analogous to bandits with strongly-convex losses.

A minor limitation of our work is that our bounds are only tight up to logarithmic terms.
Other interesting variants, which are not captured by our theory, are when the per-round budget varies with time (stochastically or adversarially), or when tasks (with deterministic or random completion times) can run for multiple time steps, and the feedback is only observed with some delay, at the completion of the tasks.
Lastly, one could consider a contextual version of this problem where some context related to the tasks is revealed to the learner before they are asked to allocate budgets at each time step.
We leave these refinements and extensions open for future work.

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{K\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{1+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t=1}^{T}B_{t,k}(X_{t,k})}\right]

(Jensen’s inequality)

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{K\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}\sqrt{1+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\bigl[B_{t,k}(X_{t,k})\bigr]}

\displaystyle=4\sqrt{K\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}\sqrt{1+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\bigl[F_{k}(X_{t,k})\bigr]}\;.

Hence

\displaystyle T\bigl\langle\mu,F(x^{\star})\bigr\rangle-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\bigl\langle\mu,F(X_{t})\bigr\rangle\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\bigl\langle\mu,F(x^{\star})\bigr\rangle-\bigl\langle\mu,F(X_{t})\bigr\rangle\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq K^{2}T^{2}\delta+4\sqrt{K\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}\sqrt{1+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\bigl[F_{k}(X_{t,k})\bigr]}\;.

Since
x^{\star}
was chosen arbitrarily, we can take the
\sup
over
x^{\star}\in\Delta_{K}
, and the conclusion thus follows by substituting
\delta\coloneqq\frac{1}{(KT)^{2}}
.

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\bigg[0\times\mathrm{1}\{U_{\ell}=a_{\ell},V_{\ell}=a^{\prime}_{\ell}\}+0\times\mathrm{1}\{U_{\ell}=b_{\ell},V_{\ell}=b^{\prime}_{\ell}\}

\displaystyle R_{T}

\displaystyle=T\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}}\right)K-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[I_{t,2i-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}}\mathbb{I}\{j_{i}=1\}\right)+I_{t,2i}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}}\mathbb{I}\{j_{i}=2\}\right)\right]

Hence there is

Appendix C
Speed-up upper bound: missing details

For the remainder of this section, we define, for
t=1,\ldots,T
,

\mathcal{F}_{t}\coloneqq\sigma\Bigl(\bigl(B_{s,1}(X_{s,1}),\ldots,B_{s,K}(X_{s,K}),B_{s,1}(X_{s,1})G_{s,1},\ldots,B_{s,K}(X_{s,K})G_{s,K}\bigr)_{s=1}^{t}\Bigr).

(18)

The next lemma and its two corollaries show that successive rewards observed for a given arm can be controlled as if they were i.i.d. variables, despite the rewards themselves depending on past choices of the algorithm.

Lemma 1
.

Fix
k\in[K]
. Let
N\in[T]
be deterministic and such that

\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}B_{t,k}(X_{t,k})\geq N\right]=1.

Let
(Y_{1,k},\ldots,Y_{N,k})\coloneqq(G_{t_{1},k},\ldots,G_{t_{N},k})

where
t_{1}<\cdots<t_{N}
are the (random)
N
first indices
t\in\mathbb{N}
such that
B_{t,k}(X_{t,k})=1
. Then
Y_{1,k},\ldots,Y_{N,k}
are i.i.d. and distributed as
G_{1,k}
.

Proof.

Let
\psi
be a bounded continuous function on
[0,1]
.
Let
A_{1}=\bigl\{B_{1,k}(X_{1,k})=1\bigr\}=\{t_{1}=1\}

and for
t\geq 2
,
A_{t}=\bigl\{B_{1,k}(X_{1,k})=0,\ldots,B_{t-1,k}(X_{t-1,k})=0,B_{t,k}(X_{t,k})=1\bigr\}=\{t_{1}=t\}
. We have, using the law of total expectation,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(Y_{1,k})\bigr]=\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(Y_{1,k})\mid A_{t}\bigr]\mathbb{P}[A_{t}]=\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(G_{t,k})\mid A_{t}\bigr]\mathbb{P}[A_{t}].

(19)

Then, using
\mathcal{F}_{t}
from (
18
), noting that
A_{t}
is a measurable event of
B_{t,k}(X_{t,k})
and the variables in
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, and using the law of total expectation,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(G_{t,k})\mid A_{t}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(G_{t,k})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},B_{t,k}(X_{t,k})\bigr]\mid A_{t}\Bigr].

Since
G_{t,k}
is independent of
B_{t,k}(X_{t,k})
and
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(G_{t,k})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},B_{t,k}(X_{t,k})\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(G_{1,k})\bigr].

Hence

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(G_{t,k})\mid A_{t}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(G_{1,k})\bigr]\;.

It follows from (
19
) that

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(Y_{1,k})\bigr]=\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(G_{1,k})\bigr]\mathbb{P}[A_{t}]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(G_{1,k})\bigr].

Hence
Y_{1,k}
is distributed as
G_{1,k}
.

Next, for
\ell\in[N]
, let

\mathcal{S}_{\ell}=\left\{(s_{1},\dots,s_{\ell})\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}:s_{1}<\dots<s_{\ell}\right\}.

Note that
\mathcal{S}_{n+1}

is the set of values for
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n+1})
that can be reached. Consider any two functions
\phi\colon[0,1]^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\psi\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
. Define

T(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n+1})\coloneqq\left\{(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n+1})=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n+1})\right\}.

Then we have, using the law of total expectation,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi(Y_{1,k},\ldots,Y_{n,k})\psi(Y_{n+1,k})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n+1})\in\mathcal{S}_{n+1}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi(Y_{1,k},\ldots,Y_{n,k})\psi(Y_{n+1,k})\mid T(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n+1})\bigr]\mathbb{P}\bigl[T(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n+1})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n+1})\in\mathcal{S}_{n+1}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi(G_{s_{1},k},\ldots,G_{s_{n},k})\psi(G_{s_{n+1},k})\mid T(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n+1})\bigr]\mathbb{P}\bigl[T(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n+1})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n+1})\in\mathcal{S}_{n+1}}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi(G_{s_{1},k},\ldots,G_{s_{n},k})\psi(G_{s_{n+1},k})\mid\mathcal{F}_{s_{n+1}-1},B_{s_{n+1},k}(X_{s_{n+1},k})\bigr]\mid T(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n+1})\Bigr]

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\times\mathbb{P}\bigl[T(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n+1})\bigr],

where the last equality holds by the law of total expectation since

T(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n+1})
is a measurable event of
B_{s_{n+1},k}(X_{s_{n+1},k})
and the variables in
\mathcal{F}_{s_{n+1}-1}
.

Then, conditionally to
\mathcal{F}_{s_{n+1}-1}
and
B_{s_{n+1},k}(X_{s_{n+1},k})
, only
G_{s_{n+1},k}
remains random (and keeps its unconditional distribution). Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi(Y_{1,k},\ldots,Y_{n,k})\psi(Y_{n+1,k})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n+1})\in\mathcal{S}_{n+1}}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\phi(G_{s_{1},k},\ldots,G_{s_{n},k})\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(G_{s_{n+1},k})\mid\mathcal{F}_{s_{n+1}-1},B_{s_{n+1},k}(X_{s_{n+1},k})\bigr]\mid T(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n+1})\Bigr]

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\times\mathbb{P}\bigl[T(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n+1})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(G_{1,k})\bigr]\sum_{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n+1})\in\mathcal{S}_{n+1}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi(G_{s_{1},k},\ldots,G_{s_{n},k})\mid T(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n+1})\bigr]\mathbb{P}\bigl[T(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n+1})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(G_{1,k})\bigr]\sum_{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n+1})\in\mathcal{S}_{n+1}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi(Y_{1,k},\ldots,Y_{n,k})\mid T(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n+1})\bigr]\mathbb{P}\bigl[T(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n+1})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(G_{1,k})\bigr]\mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi(Y_{1,k},\ldots,Y_{n,k})\bigr],

where the last equality follows from the law of total expectation.

Hence, by induction it follows that for any functions
\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N}
from
[0,1]
to
\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{N}\psi_{i}(Y_{i,k})\right]=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\psi_{i}(G_{1,k})\right].

This concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 1
.

Fix
N,T\in\mathbb{N}
with
N\leq T
. Fix
k\in[K]
. Let
E_{N,T}
be the event

E_{N,T}\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{T}B_{t,k}(X_{t,k})\geq N\right\}.

Under the event
E_{N,T}
, define
t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}
as the sequence of the
N
first random times
t\in[T]
where
B_{t,k}(X_{t,k})=1
, with
1\leq t_{1}<\dots<t_{N}\leq N
.
Then, for any subset
A\subset[0,1]^{N}
,

\mathbb{P}\bigl[E_{N,T}\cap\left\{\left(G_{t_{1},k},\dots,G_{t_{N},k}\right)\in A\right\}\bigr]\leq\mathbb{P}\bigl[\left(G_{1,k},\dots,G_{N,k}\right)\in A\bigr].

Proof.

Consider the budget-to-success curves
F_{1},\dots,F_{K}
.
Consider new (time-dependent) functions with, for
t\in[T]
,
k\in[K]
, and
x\in[0,1]
,

\widetilde{F}_{t,k}(x)=\begin{cases}F_{k}(x)&~~\text{if $t\leq T$}\\
1~\text{(constant function)}&~~\text{if $t\geq T+1$}.\end{cases}

These new
\widetilde{F}_{t,1},\dots\widetilde{F}_{t,K}
define a new multi-platform setting, for which we denote probabilities with
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
. The behavior of the gains and
algorithm choices
is unchanged from times
t=1
to
t=T
between the two settings. Hence

\mathbb{P}\bigl[E_{N,T}\cap\left\{\left(G_{t_{1},k},\dots,G_{t_{N},k}\right)\in A\right\}\bigr]=\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\bigl[E_{N,T}\cap\left\{\left(G_{t_{1},k},\dots,G_{t_{N},k}\right)\in A\right\}\bigr].

Conside
n\in[T-1]
.
On the other hand, under the new setting, each arm is infinitely explored over times
t\in\mathbb{N}
and so we can apply
Lemma

1
(with
t_{1},\dots,t_{N}
defined as in this lemma with probability one) yielding

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\bigl[E_{N,T}\cap\left\{\left(G_{t_{1},k},\dots,G_{t_{N},k}\right)\in A\right\}\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\bigl[\left(G_{t_{1},k},\dots,G_{t_{N},k}\right)\in A\bigr]

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\bigl[\left(G_{1,k},\dots,G_{N,k}\right)\in A\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\bigl[\left(G_{1,k},\dots,G_{N,k}\right)\in A\bigr].

∎

Corollary 2
.

With the notation of
1
, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, we have, for
N,T\in\mathbb{N}
,
N\leq T
,

\mathbb{P}\left[E_{N,T}\cap\left\{\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}G_{t_{i},k}-\mu_{k}\right|>\sqrt{\frac{\log(\frac{2}{\delta})}{2t}}\right\}\right]\leq\delta.

Proof.

We apply
1
to the set

A=\left\{(g_{1},\ldots,g_{N})\in[0,1]^{N}:\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}g_{i}-\mu_{k}\right|>\sqrt{\frac{\log(\frac{2}{\delta})}{2t}}\right\}

and then we apply Hoeffding’s inequality.
∎

The next crucial lemma shows that the number of times a task has been completed is not much smaller than the total amount of probability invested in the task (once at least a polylogarithmic amount of probability has been invested in the task).

Lemma 2
.

For any
T\geq 2
, with probability at least
1-\frac{K}{T}
, the following event holds:
For
t\in[T]
and
k\in[K]
, if

\sum_{s=1}^{t}F_{k}(X_{s,k})\geq 100\log(T)^{2}

then

\sum_{s=1}^{t}B_{s,k}(X_{s,k})\geq\frac{\sum_{s=1}^{t}F_{k}(X_{s,k})}{2}.

Proof.

For
k\in[K]
, the sequence

\bigl(F_{k}(X_{t,k})-B_{t,k}(X_{t,k})\bigr)_{t\in\mathbb{N}}

is a martingale difference sequence, together with the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
, recalling
\mathcal{F}_{t}
from (
18
) and defining
\mathcal{F}_{0}\coloneqq\sigma(\{\varnothing\})
. Indeed, for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
F_{k}(X_{t,k})-B_{t,k}(X_{t,k})
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable and

\mathbb{E}\bigl[F_{k}(X_{t,k})-B_{t,k}(X_{t,k})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\bigr]=0

since conditionally to
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
,
F_{k}(X_{t,k})
is fixed and
B_{t,k}(X_{t,k})
follows a Bernoulli distribution with parameter
F_{k}(X_{t,k})
.

For the same reason,

\mathrm{Var}\bigl[F_{k}(X_{t,k})-B_{t,k}(X_{t,k})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\bigr]=F_{k}(X_{t,k})\bigl(1-F_{k}(X_{t,k})\bigr).

Let us define

v_{t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathrm{Var}\bigl[F_{k}(X_{s,k})-B_{s,k}(X_{s,k})\mid\mathcal{F}_{s-1}\bigr]\leq\sum_{s=1}^{t}F_{k}(X_{s,k}).

Hence from Corollary 16 in
[
6
]
(a version of Bernstein’s inequality for martingales), we have, for
\delta>0
and
t\in[t]
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{s=1}^{t}F_{k}(X_{s,k})-\sum_{s=1}^{t}B_{s,k}(X_{s,k})>\sqrt{2(v_{t}+1)\log(t/\delta)}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}\log(t/\delta)\right]\leq\delta.

Let us take
\delta=\frac{1}{T^{2}}
and replace
v_{t}
by its upper bound yielding

\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{s=1}^{t}F_{k}(X_{s,k})-\sum_{s=1}^{t}B_{s,k}(X_{s,k})>\sqrt{2\left(\sum_{s=1}^{t}F_{k}(X_{s,k})+1\right)3\log(T)}+\frac{3\sqrt{2}}{3}\log(T)\right]\leq\frac{1}{T^{2}}.

If
\sum_{s=1}^{t}F_{k}(X_{s,k})\geq 100\log(T)^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\left(\sum_{s=1}^{t}F_{k}(X_{s,k})+1\right)3\log(T)}+\frac{3\sqrt{2}}{3}\log(T)

\displaystyle\leq\left(3+\frac{3\sqrt{2}}{3}\right)\sqrt{\sum_{s=1}^{t}F_{k}(X_{s,k})}\log(T)

\displaystyle\leq 5\log(T)\sqrt{\sum_{s=1}^{t}F_{k}(X_{s,k})}.

Hence,

\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{s=1}^{t}F_{k}(X_{s,k})\geq 100\log(T)^{2}~\mbox{and}~\sum_{s=1}^{t}F_{k}(X_{s,k})-\sum_{s=1}^{t}B_{s,k}(X_{s,k})>5\log(T)\sqrt{\sum_{s=1}^{t}F_{k}(X_{s,k})}\right]\leq\frac{1}{T^{2}}.

Then, if

\sum_{s=1}^{t}F_{k}(X_{s,k})\geq 100\log(T)^{2},

we have

\frac{\sum_{s=1}^{t}F_{k}(X_{s,k})}{2}\geq 5\log(T)\sqrt{\sum_{s=1}^{t}F_{k}(X_{s,k})}\;.

Hence we have

\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{s=1}^{t}F_{k}(X_{s,k})\geq 100\log(T)^{2}~~\mbox{and}~~\sum_{s=1}^{t}B_{s,k}(X_{s,k})<\frac{\sum_{s=1}^{t}F_{k}(X_{s,k})}{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{T^{2}}.

We conclude the proof with a union bound.
∎

Assumption 2
.

1.

For each

(0,1]

Note that Assumption

1
(in

4
), since it is implied by but it does not imply

.

Note also that, under

:

•

There exists a function

and for each

•

There exists a function

•

—indeed, the set in the

) and

For the remainder of this section, we assume fixed such functions

,

,

, and

.

We now state our generalized version of the speed-up analysis of

in the general diminishing-returns setting.

Theorem 4
.

Under

The last crucial lemma establishes the stability of the minimizer of the average reward under concavity and for means and budgets bounded away from zero.

Lemma 5
.

Let
2
hold.
Let
u_{\inf}\in(0,\frac{1}{K}]
and
0<m_{\inf}<m_{\sup}<\infty
.
Consider mean vectors
m,\widetilde{m}\in[m_{\inf},m_{\sup}]^{K}
.
Assume that
x^{\star}(m)\in(u_{\inf},1]^{K}
and
x^{\star}(\widetilde{m})\in(u_{\inf},1]^{K}
.
Then

\Phi_{m}\bigl(x^{\star}(m)\bigr)-\Phi_{m}\bigl(x^{\star}(\widetilde{m})\bigr)\leq\frac{2M(u_{\inf})^{2}}{m_{\inf}\alpha(u_{\inf})}\left\lVert m-\widetilde{m}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

For conciseness, write
\alpha=\alpha(u_{\inf})

and
M=M(u_{\inf})
.
Notice that since
m\in[m_{\inf},m_{\sup}]^{K}
we have that
\Phi_{m}\colon[u_{\inf},1]^{K}\to[0,K]
is
m_{\inf}\alpha
-strongly
concave with respect to the Euclidean norm.
In fact, for each
u,v\in[u_{\inf},1]^{K}

\displaystyle\Phi_{m}(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[K]}m_{k}F_{k}\bigl(v_{k}\bigr)\leq\sum_{k\in[K]}m_{k}\Bigl(F_{k}\bigl(u_{k}\bigr)+F_{k}^{\prime}\bigl(u_{k}\bigr)\bigl(v_{k}-u_{k}\bigr)-\frac{\alpha}{2}\bigl(v_{k}-u_{k}\bigr)^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\Phi_{m}(u)+\sum_{k\in[K]}m_{k}F_{k}^{\prime}\bigl(u_{k}\bigr)\bigl(v_{k}-u_{k}\bigr)-\frac{m_{\inf}\alpha}{2}\left\lVert v-u\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\;.

Similarly,

\Phi_{\widetilde{m}}\colon[u_{\inf},1]^{K}\to[0,K]
is
m_{\inf}\alpha
-strongly
concave with respect to the Euclidean norm.

For conciseness, write
\Phi^{\star}=\Phi_{m}

and
\widetilde{\Phi}=\Phi_{\widetilde{m}}
. Write also
x^{\star}=x^{\star}(m)
and
\widetilde{u}=x^{\star}(\widetilde{m})
.
For
v\in[0,\frac{1}{K})
, write

\Delta_{K}(v)=\Delta_{K}\cap[v,1]^{K}.

Then since it is assumed

x^{\star}(m)\in(u_{\inf},1]^{K}
and
x^{\star}(\widetilde{m})\in(u_{\inf},1]^{K}
, we have

x^{\star}\in\argmax_{u\in\Delta_{K}(u_{\inf})}\Phi^{\star}(u)~~\text{and}~~\widetilde{u}\in\argmax_{u\in\Delta_{K}(u_{\inf})}\widetilde{\Phi}(u).

Since
x^{\star}
is in the relative interior of
\Delta_{K}
with respect to
\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{K}:\sum_{k=1}^{K}u_{k}=0\}
and it maximizes
\Phi^{\star}
on
\Delta_{K}
,
for each
u\in\Delta_{K}
we have

\big\langle\nabla\Phi^{\star}(x^{\star}),u-x^{\star}\big\rangle=0.

(29)

Then, leveraging first the
m_{\inf}\alpha
-concavity of
\Phi^{\star}
, and then the
M
-Lipschitzness of
F_{1},\dots,F_{K}
on
