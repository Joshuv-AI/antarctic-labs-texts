Prognostic significance of pretreatment systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) in patients with cervical cancer: a meta-analysis
Authors: Hao Shan, Qingsong Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe pretreatment systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) has been previously investigated for its potential in predicting the prognostic outcomes of cervical cancer (CC), but findings remain inconsistent. This meta-analysis aimed to clarify the prognostic value of SII in patients with CC.MethodsWeb of Science, PubMed, the Cochrane Library, and Embase databases were systematically searched up to October 15, 2025. The prognostic performance of SII in CC was evaluated by calculating pooled hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The primary outcome was overall survival (OS), and the secondary outcome was progression-free survival (PFS).ResultsSixteen studies with a total of 2,840 patients were included. Fifteen studies comprising 2,735 cases were analyzed for OS, and nine studies including 1,349 patients were analyzed for PFS. Elevated SII was significantly associated with poorer OS (HR = 2.13, 95% CI = 1.60–2.85, p < 0.001) and PFS (HR = 1.81, 95% CI = 1.26–2.60, p < 0.001). Sensitivity analysis indicated stable results, confirming the robustness and reliability of the findings for both OS and PFS. No significant publication bias was detected according to Begg’s test.ConclusionsElevated pretreatment SII is significantly associated with poorer OS and reduced PFS in patients with CC. SII may serve as a reliable and efficient prognostic biomarker for CC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-04167-5.

Introduction

Cervical cancer (CC) represents a major global health challenge, ranking as the fourth most common malignancy among women [1]. According to the latest GLOBOCAN statistics, there were 661,021 newly diagnosed CC cases and 348,189 CC-related deaths worldwide in 2022 [2]. The main treatment modalities for CC include surgery and radiotherapy, with chemotherapy serving as an important adjunct [3]. Cisplatin-based concurrent chemoradiotherapy combined with brachytherapy remains the standard treatment for locally advanced CC [4]. The prognosis for early-stage CC is generally favorable; however, it worsens when the disease invades adjacent tissues or metastasizes to lymph nodes [5]. Several factors influence the prognosis of patients with CC, including age, tumor size, histological type, grade, lymph node metastasis, tumor stage, anemia, squamous cell carcinoma antigen levels, and type of radiotherapy [6]. The five-year survival rate for CC varies by disease stage, ranging from approximately 92% in stage I to about 56% in stage IV [7]. Consequently, improving prognostic outcomes in patients with advanced CC highlights the need for identifying reliable and effective prognostic biomarkers.

Current evidence indicates that immune responses and inflammation play crucial roles in tumor development and metastasis [8]. Several inflammation-based biomarkers, such as the lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio [9], platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio [10], fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio [11], lymphocyte-to-C-reactive protein ratio [12], and C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio [13], have demonstrated significant prognostic value across various cancers. The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) is calculated as follows: SII = (neutrophil count × platelet count)/lymphocyte count [14]. SII has been reported as an important prognostic indicator in multiple solid tumors, including prostate cancer [15], colorectal cancer [16], melanoma [17], renal cell carcinoma [18], and glioblastoma [19]. However, previous studies investigating the prognostic effect of SII in CC have yielded inconsistent [20–35]. Some studies have suggested that elevated SII is significantly associated with a poorer prognosis in CC [21–23, 31, 33], whereas others have reported no significant correlation between SII and CC prognosis [24, 25, 32]. Accordingly, this meta-analysis assessed the specific role of SII in forecasting the prognosis of CC.

Materials and methods

Study guideline

This study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines (Supplemental File 1) [36]. The meta-analysis protocol was registered in INPLASY (registration No. INPLASY2025120020), and the protocol is accessible at https://inplasy.com/inplasy-2025-12-0020/.

Literature search

Web of Science, PubMed, the Cochrane Library, and Embase databases were systematically searched up to October 15, 2025, using the following search terms: (“systemic immune-inflammation index” OR “SII” OR “systemic immune-inflammatory index”) AND (“cervical carcinoma” OR “cervical cancer”). Both free-text keywords and Medical Subject Headings terms were used to maximize search coverage. The detailed search strategies for each database are presented in Supplemental File 2. Only articles published in English were included. Additionally, the reference lists of relevant studies were manually screened to identify any additional eligible publications.

Study selection

The inclusion criteria were defined according to the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, and Study Design (PICOS) framework: (1) Population: patients with pathologically confirmed CC; (2) Intervention: studies that investigated the association between pretreatment SII and survival outcomes in CC, using a defined cutoff value to categorize patients into high and low SII groups; (3) Comparison: CC patients with normal or lower SII levels; (4) Outcome: studies reporting valid or calculable hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs); and (5) Study design: cohort studies, including both prospective and retrospective designs. Only English-language studies were considered.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) conference abstracts, comments, reviews, letters, and case reports; (2) studies including duplicate patient data; and (3) non-human (animal) studies.

Data acquisition and quality evaluation

Two reviewers (H.S. and Q.X.) independently collected data from eligible studies, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion until a consensus was reached. The extracted data included the following: first author, country, publication year, study design, sample size, study center, study duration, patient age, International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage, treatment method, follow-up period, survival outcomes, cutoff value for SII, method of cutoff determination, type of survival analysis, and HRs with corresponding 95%CIs. The primary endpoint was overall survival (OS), and the secondary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS). The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [37] (https://www.ohri.ca/programs/clinical_epidemiology/oxford.asp), which ranges from 0 to 9 points. A score of ≥ 6 indicated high methodological quality.

Statistical analysis

The prognostic value of the SII in CC was evaluated by calculating pooled HRs and 95% CIs. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic. When I2 exceeded 50% or the p-value was below 0.10, indicating significant heterogeneity, a random-effects model was applied; otherwise, a fixed-effects model was used. Subgroup analyses were performed to further explore the prognostic value of SII across different patient subgroups. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of the OS and PFS findings. Meta-regression was also performed to identify potential sources of heterogeneity. Publication bias was examined using funnel plots and Begg’s test. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 12.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA), and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Process of literature retrieval

The initial database search identified 308 studies. After removing duplicates, 243 records were retained (Fig. 1). Following a review of titles and abstracts, 220 studies were excluded because they were animal experiments or unrelated to the research topic, leaving 23 studies for full-text assessment. Of these, seven studies were excluded due to unavailable survival data (n = 6) or missing SII cutoff values (n = 1). Finally, 16 studies comprising 2,840 patients [20–35] were included in this meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Included study features

The publication years of the included studies [20–35] ranged from 2019 to 2025 (Table 1). Eight studies were conducted in China [21–24, 27, 29, 31, 32], three in Italy [25, 26, 34], two in Turkey [33, 35], and one each in Spain [20], Canada [28], and Poland [30]. The sample sizes ranged from 90 to 328 (median: 165); therefore, a cutoff of 160 was used for subgroup analyses stratified by sample size. Fifteen studies had a retrospective design [20–31, 33–35] and one was a prospective study [32]. Twelve studies were single-center studies [20–23, 25, 26, 28–33], while four were multicenter trials [25, 27, 34, 35]. Regarding the FIGO stage distribution, six studies included patients with stages I–IV [20, 28–30, 33, 34], four recruited stages I–II [21, 22, 24, 26], two included stages II–IV [25, 27], two involved stages III–IV [32, 35], and one each covered stages I–III [31] and II–III [23]. The cutoff values for SII ranged from 385 to 1,377 (median: 868.5); therefore, a cutoff of 900 was applied in subgroup analyses based on SII cutoff values. Thirteen studies determined cutoff thresholds using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis [20–22, 24–31, 33, 34], two used X-tile software [23, 32], and one used the median value [35]. Fifteen studies evaluated the prognostic value of SII for OS [20–26, 28–35], and nine examined its association with PFS [22, 25–29, 32–34] in CC. Nine studies obtained HRs and 95% CIs from univariate analysis [20, 22, 25, 26, 31–35], while seven derived them from multivariate analysis [21, 23, 24, 27–30]. All studies were of high methodological quality, with NOS scores ranging from 7 to 9 (Table 1).

SII and OS

Fifteen studies comprising 2,735 patients [20–26, 28–35] reported data on the prognostic value of the SII for OS. Given the presence of significant heterogeneity (I2 = 53.9%, p = 0.007), a random-effects model was applied. The pooled results showed that elevated SII was significantly associated with poorer OS in patients with CC (HR = 2.13, 95% CI = 1.60–2.85, p < 0.001) (Table 2; Fig. 2). Subgroup analyses were performed based on country, study design, study center type, sample size, treatment, cutoff value, cutoff determination method, and type of survival analysis (Table 2). The prognostic value of SII for OS remained consistent across subgroups, and it was not affected by sample size, study center, cutoff value, or survival analysis method (p < 0.05; Table 2). Meta-regression analysis indicated that none of the assessed factors — including country, sample size, study design, study center, FIGO stage, treatment, cutoff value, cutoff determination method, or type of survival analysis — were significant sources of heterogeneity in OS (Table 2).

SII and PFS

Nine studies including 1,349 patients [22, 25–29, 32–34] investigated the association between SII and PFS in CC (Table 3). Using a random-effects model, elevated SII was found to be significantly associated with poorer PFS (HR = 1.81, 95% CI = 1.26–2.60, p < 0.001) (Table 3; Fig. 3). Subgroup analyses demonstrated that high SII remained a significant prognostic biomarker for PFS in the following subgroups: sample size ≥ 160 (p < 0.001), retrospective design (p < 0.001), single-center studies (p = 0.001), chemoradiotherapy treatment (p = 0.017), cutoff SII < 900 (p = 0.007), cutoff determined using ROC curve analysis (p < 0.001), and multivariate survival analysis (p < 0.001) (Table 3). Meta-regression revealed that variables such as country, sample size, study design, study center, FIGO stage, treatment, cutoff value, cutoff determination method, and type of survival analysis were not significant contributors to heterogeneity in PFS (Table 3).

Discussion

SII has been widely investigated for its prognostic value in CC, although findings have varied across studies. The present meta-analysis combined data from 16 studies involving 2,840 patients [20–35] and demonstrated that elevated SII is significantly associated with poorer OS and PFS in patients with CC. The robustness of these results was confirmed through subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, and publication bias assessments. Collectively, these findings identify SII as a reliable and promising prognostic biomarker for CC. To the best of our knowledge, this meta-analysis provides the first comprehensive evidence supporting the prognostic value of SII in CC.

SII is calculated using neutrophil, platelet, and lymphocyte counts [14]. Therefore, an elevated SII likely reflects increased neutrophil and platelet counts and/or a decreased lymphocyte count. Although the precise mechanisms underlying the association between SII and CC prognosis remain to be fully elucidated, several biological explanations have been proposed. First, emerging evidence suggests that neutrophils play a critical role in tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis by impairing epithelial cell repair, promoting angiogenesis, and facilitating immune evasion [38]. Pro-tumor N2 neutrophils can produce reactive nitrogen and oxygen species, leading to genetic instability and DNA damage, which may promote tumorigenesis [39, 40]. Second, platelets contribute to tumor growth and dissemination by inducing epithelial–mesenchymal transition in cancer cells, enhancing their motility and resistance to apoptosis, and promoting tumor cell extravasation [41]. When interacting with tumor cells, platelets release cytokines and growth factors that stimulate angiogenesis and establish a microenvironment favorable to tumor development [42, 43]. Third, lymphocytes are essential components of the host antitumor immune response, as they exert cytotoxic effects and suppress cancer cell proliferation and invasion [44]. In the tumor microenvironment, monocytes can differentiate into dendritic cells or tumor-associated macrophages, which support tumor progression and inhibit immune activity [45]. Lymphocytes play a crucial for the immune system by facilitating the destruction of cytotoxic cells and inhibiting the growth and spread of tumor cells, thereby contributing significantly to tumor defense [46]. A decrease in lymphocyte counts leads to reduced antitumor activity, increasing the risk of tumor recurrence and metastasis [47–49]. Therefore, SII is considered a biologically reasonable prognostic marker on neutrophil, platelet, and lymphocyte levels.

Notably, the cutoff values for SII among the included studies varied considerably, ranging from 385 to 1,377, with a median value of 868.5. A cutoff of 900 was therefore applied in the subgroup analysis, and the results demonstrated that SII ≥ 900 remained a significant prognostic indicator for both OS and PFS (Tables 2 and 3). Based on these findings, we propose 900 as a potential standardized SII cutoff value for patients with CC.

Recently, numerous meta-analyses have reported the prognostic significance of SII across various cancer types [50]. In a meta-analysis by Zhang et al., which included 2,342 patients, higher pretreatment SII was associated with poorer OS and PFS among non-small cell lung cancer patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors [50]. Similarly, Li et al. found that elevated SII predicted worse OS, recurrence-free survival (RFS), and PFS in hepatocellular carcinoma patients undergoing monotherapy or combination therapy, based on a meta-analysis of 39 studies [51]. Another recent meta-analysis involving 8,267 patients demonstrated that, in prostate cancer patients undergoing radical prostatectomy, high SII was correlated with reduced OS, biochemical RFS, and cancer-specific survival [52]. Furthermore, Wang et al. observed that elevated SII was significantly associated with poorer OS in osteosarcoma based on a meta-analysis of 1,015 cases [53]. In addition, a meta-analysis including 12 studies reported that high SII was strongly correlated with unfavorable OS and PFS in metastatic renal cell carcinoma [54].

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, most of the included studies were retrospective in design, which may have introduced selection bias due to the inherent limitations of retrospective analyses. However, the subgroup analysis revealed that study design did not significantly affect the prognostic value of SII for OS or PFS (Tables 2 and 3). Similarly, the meta-regression analysis indicated that study design was not a major source of heterogeneity (Tables 2 and 3). Second, the SII cutoff values were not consistent among the included studies. Third, the pooled HRs and 95% CIs were extracted from published articles rather than from individual patient data. Fourth, when fewer than ten studies are included, publication bias assessments may lack sufficient statistical power to accurately detect asymmetry (https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook). Therefore, the results regarding publication bias should be interpreted with caution. In light of these limitations, large, prospective, multicenter studies are required to further validate and refine the prognostic utility of SII in patients with CC.

Conclusion

In summary, this meta-analysis demonstrates that elevated SII is significantly associated with poorer OS and reduced PFS in patients with CC. These findings suggest that SII may serve as a reliable and clinically useful prognostic biomarker for CC.
