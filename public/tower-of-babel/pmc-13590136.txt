In Vitro Anti‐Inflammatory Activity of Sour Cherry (Prunus cerasus L.) Seed Oil and Computational Safety Profiling
Authors: Tuğba Aydin, Tuğba Günbatan, Aysel Berkkan, Onur Kenan Ulutaş, Melike Sucu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study aimed to evaluate the in vitro anti‐inflammatory activity of sour cherry seed oils, including samples obtained by Soxhlet extraction and maceration, as well as ten commercial samples. The results demonstrated that the 15‐lipoxygenase inhibitory activity of the oil samples ranged from 6% to 80%. Regarding cyclooxygenase (COX) inhibition, while most samples showed negligible activity, the oil produced by Soxhlet extraction inhibited COX‐1 and COX‐2 by 38% and 32%, respectively. Similarly, the oil obtained via maceration showed inhibition rates of 25% and 23% for COX‐1 and COX‐2, respectively. In the trypsin inhibition assay, the Soxhlet‐extracted and macerated oils exhibited 42% and 25% inhibition, respectively. Two market‐supplied samples also showed considerable trypsin inhibitory activity, at 39% and 35%. In contrast, none of the sour cherry seed oil samples had a significant effect on chymotrypsin. Gas chromatography with flame ionization detection analysis revealed that linoleic acid, oleic acid, and palmitic acid were the major fatty acid components in all oil samples. Additionally, in silico ADMET profiling and molecular docking studies of the major fatty acids revealed favorable predicted safety profiles and strong binding affinities against the target enzymes, which, alongside the observed in vitro inhibitions, support its potential for further development.Keywords: ADMET, cyclooxygenase, inflammation, lipoxygenase, Prunus cerasus

Introduction

Inflammation is a complex immune response to harmful stimuli such as pathogens, damaged cells, toxic compounds, or radiation. It is a protective mechanism aimed at eliminating the injurious stimuli and initiating the healing process [1, 2]. As such, inflammation is a vital defense mechanism for maintaining health [3]. At the tissue level, it is characterized by redness, swelling, heat, pain, and loss of function, resulting from local immune, vascular, and inflammatory cell responses to infection or injury [4]. Key microcirculatory events during inflammation include changes in vascular permeability, leukocyte recruitment, and the release of inflammatory mediators [2, 5].

Arachidonic acid is a key precursor in inflammatory pathways. Upon activation by various extrinsic and intrinsic factors, the phospholipase A2 enzyme catalyzes the release of arachidonic acid from the cell membrane, making it available for the cyclooxygenase (COX) and lipoxygenase (LOX) pathways [6]. COX and LOX are well‐known pro‐inflammatory enzymes essential for the physiological production of eicosanoids, such as prostaglandins and leukotrienes. The COX enzyme oxidizes arachidonate to form prostaglandin G2 (PGG2). The released arachidonate can also be utilized for the synthesis of other prostaglandins, including PGH2 and thromboxane [7, 8]. Leukotrienes, another class of arachidonic acid derivatives synthesized by LOX, also play a significant role in the inflammatory process [9, 10].

LOX enzymes exist in different isoforms, such as 5‐LOX, 8‐LOX, 12‐LOX, and 15‐LOX. These enzymes catalyze the formation of 5‐, 8‐, 12‐, and 15‐hydroperoxyeicosatetraenoic acid (HPETE) derivatives, leading to the production of leukotrienes, hepoxilins, and lipoxins [10]. 5‐LOX, a well‐characterized isoform, initiates a critical biosynthetic pathway in inflammation by triggering the production of 5‐HPETE, which is converted to leukotriene A4 (LTA4). LT‐A4 serves as the precursor for other leukotrienes, including LTB4, LTC4, LTD4, and LTE4 [11, 12]. LTB4, the end product of the 5‐LOX pathway, is implicated in the pathogenesis of various inflammatory and allergic diseases, such as atherosclerosis, cancer, and cardiovascular disorders [13, 14]. Additionally, 12‐LOX and 15‐LOX isoforms also play significant roles in leukotriene synthesis.

Given that COX and LOX enzymes are rate‐limiting steps in eicosanoid production, they are widely used as targets for screening and evaluating anti‐inflammatory drugs [15, 16]. Most nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs currently on the market primarily inhibit COX‐1 or COX‐2 [17]. However, selective COX inhibition can lead to an overproduction of leukotrienes (due to an un‐suppressed LOX pathway), and elevated leukotriene levels are associated with adverse effects. Therefore, a multi‐target inhibitor capable of simultaneously suppressing both COX and LOX is considered a more favorable therapeutic approach than existing NSAIDs. Furthermore, selective COX‐2 inhibitors have been linked to cardiovascular risks (e.g., hypertension, myocardial infarction) and nephrotoxicity, attributed to a reduction in vasodilatory and anti‐aggregatory prostacyclin (PGI2) alongside unopposed pro‐thrombotic thromboxane A2 [18, 19, 20]. Therefore, the cardiovascular side effects of selective COX‐2 inhibitors could be mitigated by the concurrent inhibition of COX‐1, which prevents platelet aggregation. Conversely, the gastrointestinal side effects associated with COX‐1 inhibition might be reduced by simultaneous LOX inhibition. But simultaneously inhibiting both COX‐1 and COX‐2 can lead to other complications. Consequently, dual inhibition of COX‐1 and LOX has been proposed as a potentially superior strategy [17]. Despite this rationale, no such dual inhibitor is currently available. Zileuton remains the only FDA‐approved LOX inhibitor [21], and 5‐Loxin is another example used for treating inflammatory conditions like osteoarthritis [19]. This underscores a significant unmet need for the development of safe, cost‐effective anti‐inflammatory agents that can concurrently inhibit COX‐1 and LOX.

Serine proteases (EC 3.4.21), which constitute over one‐third of all known proteolytic enzymes and are grouped into 13 clans and 40 families, are also pivotal in inflammation. This class includes digestive enzymes (trypsin and chymotrypsin), leukocyte proteases, and proteins involved in coagulation, fibrinolysis, and complement activation [22]. Their functions are diverse and critical, encompassing protein metabolism, digestion, blood coagulation, apoptosis, development, immune regulation, and fertilization [23]. Serine proteases are key components of the inflammatory response, released by activated leukocytes and mast cells or generated via the coagulation cascade. They act as immune modulators by regulating the production of cytokines and chemokines. For instance, they induce the pro‐inflammatory activity of IL‐32, stimulate interferon‐gamma (IFN‐γ) production in T‐cells, and promote antigen‐specific humoral responses. They also enhance TNF‐α activity by cleaving its membrane‐bound form and activate the zymogens of IL‐1β and IL‐18. Additionally, they induce the release of IL‐6 and IL‐8 from monocytes and process and regulate the activity of mature IL‐33 [24]. Therefore, inhibiting or modulating serine protease activity represents a potential therapeutic strategy for inflammatory diseases [25].

Sour cherry (Prunus cerasus L.) is widely used in products like juice, jam, marmalade, and sauces, classifying it as an industrial fruit. During processing, the peel and seeds are typically removed as by‐products. However, these by‐products, particularly the pomace and seeds, have been recognized as valuable sources of functional compounds. In the last decade, sour cherry by‐products have garnered increasing interest due to their functional properties, becoming a focus of new research [26].

Within the scope of this research, we aimed to determine the in vitro anti‐inflammatory activity of sour cherry seed oils. This included oils we obtained using two different techniques (Soxhlet extraction and maceration) and ten commercial samples from Türkiye. The activity was assessed through COX and 15‐LOX enzyme inhibition assays. Furthermore, considering their role in inflammation, the inhibitory effects of these oils on chymotrypsin and trypsin were also evaluated. In the final phase of the study, the fatty acid composition of all oil samples was determined by gas chromatography analysis. The biological activities of both the laboratory‐prepared and commercial oils were then compared and evaluated in relation to their chemical profiles. Additionally, in silico ADMET profiling and molecular docking studies were performed on the major fatty acids.

Results and Discussion

The extraction yields from sour cherry seeds were determined as 4.4 g (5.5% w/w) using the Soxhlet method and 1.1 g (2.4% w/w) using maceration. Our findings demonstrate that the sour cherry seed oils obtained by Soxhlet extraction and maceration inhibited 15‐LOX by 80% and 57%, respectively. Among the commercial samples, “6” and “3” numbered oils also exhibited significant 15‐LOX inhibitory activity, with inhibition rates of 62% and 42%. Regarding COX inhibition, while most samples showed low activity, the Soxhlet‐extracted oil inhibited COX‐1 and COX‐2 by 38% and 32%, respectively. The oil obtained via maceration showed inhibition rates of 25% and 23% for COX‐1 and COX‐2, respectively. The “6” numbered commercial oil also displayed notable COX‐1 and COX‐2 inhibition (31% and 28%, respectively) (Table 1). Chymotrypsin inhibitory activity was negligible for all tested sour cherry seed oil samples (10%>) and was not statistically significant. This indicates that sour cherry seed oils do not target this enzyme. However, in the trypsin inhibition assay, the Soxhlet‐extracted and macerated oils exhibited 42% and 25% inhibition, respectively. The “2” and “5” numbered commercial oils also demonstrated considerable trypsin inhibitory activity (39% and 35% inhibition, respectively) (Table 2).

To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has investigated the chymotrypsin, trypsin, and 15‐LOX inhibitory activities of sour cherry seed oil. However, a few studies have evaluated its anti‐inflammatory and antinociceptive effects through different mechanisms. For instance, Mahmoud et al. reported that a hexane extract of sour cherry seeds (20 and 50 µg/mL) significantly reduced the mean channel fluorescence (MCF) for IL‐8 and TNF‐α expression in CD3+ cells (p < 0.05). The study further investigated the extract's effects on cells from rheumatoid arthritis patients. When LPS‐stimulated cells were treated with the extract across a concentration range of 1–50 µg/mL, a significant dose‐dependent reduction was observed in the cytokine signaling indices for both CD3+IL‐8+ and CD3+TNF‐α+ cells compared to the control (p < 0.05–0.001). Moreover, ELISA analysis revealed another crucial dimension of the extract's activity. Treatment with the same concentration range (1–50 µg/mL) increased the content of heme oxygenase‐1 in cells from both rheumatoid arthritis patients and healthy subjects (p < 0.05—0.001). Based on these collective findings—the suppression of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐8, TNF‐α) and the induction of the protective heme oxygenase‐1 enzyme—the authors concluded that the sour cherry seed extract mitigates oxidative stress and ameliorates pro‐inflammatory signaling pathways [27]. Further compelling evidence for the anti‐inflammatory potential of sour cherry seeds comes from an in vivo study by Raafat et al. The researchers demonstrated that an ethyl acetate extract of sour cherry seeds and its bioactive constituent, linoleic acid—isolated through bioactivity‐guided fractionation—exerted significant anti‐inflammatory and antinociceptive effects in carrageenan‐induced rat models. The ethyl acetate extract, at a dose of 200 mg/kg, markedly suppressed carrageenan‐induced paw edema by 88.8%. Notably, the isolated linoleic acid achieved a comparable efficacy, reducing edema by 86.6% at a much lower dose of 20 mg/kg, highlighting its role as a primary active component. The study further elucidated the underlying mechanism of action, revealing that both the extract and linoleic acid significantly modulated key inflammatory and oxidative pathways. The treatment led to a marked downregulation of pro‐inflammatory mediators, including TNF‐α and IL‐6, and reduced lipid peroxidation, a key marker of oxidative stress. Concurrently, it enhanced the anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant defenses by elevating levels of the anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐10 and boosting serum catalase and reduced glutathione [28]. This multi‐target action, suppressing pro‐inflammatory signals while amplifying protective responses, provides a robust pharmacological basis for the traditional use of sour cherry seeds and aligns with the 15‐LOX inhibitory activities observed in our current investigation.

No prior research has evaluated the effect of sour cherry seed oil on COX enzymes. However, studies on the fruit and its anthocyanins have reported COX inhibitory activity. Seeram et al. (2001) evaluated the cyclooxygenase inhibitory potential of aqueous extracts from two distinct sour cherry varieties, Balaton and Montmorency. Their findings demonstrated a significant and selective inhibition of both COX isoforms. The aqueous extract of the Balaton variety inhibited COX‐1 by 26.6% and COX‐2 by 38.3%. Similarly, the extract from the Montmorency variety exhibited comparable activity, inhibiting COX‐1 by 24.9% and COX‐2 by 36.6% [29]. Reddy et al. isolated cyanidin‐3‐O‐glucoside from sour cherry fruit and investigated its effects on COX‐1 and COX‐2 enzymes. At a concentration of 100 µg/mL, the compound demonstrated a potent and notably selective inhibitory profile. It inhibited COX‐2 by 59%, while its inhibition of COX‐1 was significantly lower at 29.5% [30]. Wojdyło et al. also reported COX‐1 and COX‐2 inhibition by methanolic extracts of cherry fruits and leaves [31].

GC‐FID analysis revealed that the major fatty acids in all oil samples were linoleic acid (31.28%–56.96%), oleic acid (22.16%–46.24%), and palmitic acid (5.37%–18.23%). The “4” numbered sample was notable for its high palmitic and stearic acid content. Linolenic acid was detected only in the laboratory‐prepared oils and commercial samples numbered “2” and “8”, while arachidonic acid was found exclusively in sample numbered “2”. These results are consistent with previous studies reporting similar fatty acid profiles in sour cherry seed oils [32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37]. Some researchers have analyzed the fatty acids in extracts obtained using more polar solvents. Saleh et al. reported that an ethyl acetate extract was rich in oleic acid (30.19%), followed by linoleic acid (23.65%) and stearic acid (10.67%) [38]. In a contrasting finding from another study, Raafat et al. also used ethyl acetate but found a different composition in their extract, which was dominated by linoleic acid (17.81%), with lower amounts of palmitic acid (11.99%) and stearic acid (7.70%) [28]. Furthermore, research by Deineka et al. using acetone revealed yet another distinct composition, characterized by a nearly equal and high concentration of oleic acid (41.1%) and linoleic acid (40.8%), along with the presence of α‐eleostearic acid (7.9%) and palmitic acid (5.5%) [39].

An analysis of the correlation results (Figure 1) was performed to explore potential relationships between the fatty acid profiles (analyzed as FAMEs, by GC‐FID) and the biological activities of the whole oils. However, it is important to acknowledge a methodological consideration when interpreting these results. The biological assays were conducted on the whole oils, where fatty acids are predominantly esterified in triacylglycerol structures. In contrast, the GC‐FID analysis required hydrolysis and methylation of these triacylglycerols to produce FAMEs for quantification. The biochemical properties and steric accessibility of a bulky triacylglycerol molecule can differ significantly from those of its constituent free fatty acids. Therefore, the correlation analysis presented here should be viewed as an exploratory tool to generate hypotheses, rather than a direct demonstration of cause‐and‐effect relationships between individual fatty acid concentrations and enzyme inhibition. The lack of strong correlation [a comparatively stronger correlation was identified only between eicosenoic acid and oleic acid content and trypsin inhibitory activity (r = 0.701 and 0.686, respectively) may support our hypothesis that the observed anti‐inflammatory activity is likely influenced by a combination of factors, including the specific triacylglycerol structure and, more importantly, the presence of minor bioactive components (such as sterols, tocopherols, and phenolic compounds) which were not quantified in this study.

Despite the general similarity in fatty acid composition, significant variations were observed in 15‐LOX inhibitory activity. The reason for this situation is thought to be the possibility that GC‐FID analyses can only be performed with a limited number of standards and that not all components in the extracted oils can be volatilized using the methylation method employed and analyzed by GC. Previous studies have identified additional components in sour cherry seed oil, such as phospholipids (e.g., phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylethanolamine), sterols (e.g., cholesterol, β‐sitosterol, stigmasterol, campesterol), tocopherols (e.g., δ‐tocopherol, γ‐tocopherol, α‐tocopherol), carotenoids (e.g., zeaxanthin, cryptoxanthin, β‐carotene), phenolic compounds (e.g., gallic acid, p‐hydroxybenzoic acid, vanillin, p‐coumaric acid, cinnamic acid), and flavonoids (e.g., rutin, apigenin, catechin, ellagic acid, naringenin, kaempferol) [32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37]. Physical differences among the oil samples—such as variations in color, viscosity, and solidification behavior—further support the likelihood of differing minor component profiles. These non‐fatty acid constituents may be responsible for the observed variations in bioactivity. Literature evidence supports this hypothesis, identifying several compound classes with documented COX and LOX inhibitory activity. Sterols, particularly β‐sitosterol, demonstrate potent dual inhibition of both COX‐2 (IC50: 2.6 µM) and 5‐LOX (IC50: 2.0 µM) in experimental assays, supported by strong computational binding affinities [40, 41, 42, 43]. Stigmasterol and campesterol also exhibit high COX‐2 binding affinities (−10.7 and −10.5 kcal/mol, respectively) [44], indicating the sterol fraction warrants further investigation. Flavonoids represent another promising group. Rutin shows substantial inhibition of both COX‐2 (81%) and 15‐LOX (80.43%) [45], with exceptional computational binding scores reaching −12.30 kcal/mol against 5‐LOX [46]. Catechin demonstrates robust dual inhibition experimentally (COX‐2 IC50: 93.3 µM [47]; 15‐LOX IC50: 17 µg/mL [48]) and computationally (−12.23 kcal/mol against COX‐2 [37]; −12.63 kcal/mol against 5‐LOX [46]). Phenolic compounds also contribute significantly. Gallic acid inhibits COX‐2 (IC50: 38.94 µg/mL) and LOX (IC50: 17.85 µg/mL) [49], while p‐coumaric acid shows particular potency against LOX (IC50: 14.79 µM) [50]. Cinnamic acid exhibits remarkable COX‐1 selectivity with an IC50 of 0.16 µg/mL [51]. Tocopherols present a more complex mechanism, showing indirect COX‐2 inhibition in cell‐based models despite lacking direct enzyme activity [52, 53]. β‐Carotene demonstrates high COX‐2 binding affinity (−10.29 kcal/mol) and may inhibit through radical scavenging mechanisms [54, 55]. Based on compiled evidence, β‐sitosterol, rutin, and catechin emerge as the most promising candidates for future research due to their robust dual inhibitory profiles supported by both experimental and computational data.

The substantial variability observed among commercial sour cherry seed oil samples in both enzyme inhibitory activities (especially for LOX inhibition) and fatty acid profiles warrants further consideration. While the authenticity of these samples could not be independently verified, several intrinsic and extrinsic factors are known to influence the chemical composition and, consequently, the bioactivity of seed oils. First, cultivar (varietal) differences within P. cerasus species have been shown to significantly affect the phytochemical profile, particularly the levels of sterols, tocopherols, and phenolic compounds, even when fatty acid profiles remain broadly similar. Second, geographic origin and climatic conditions during fruit development can alter the secondary metabolite accumulation in seeds. Third, processing methods play a critical role; cold‐pressing, solvent extraction (as used for our laboratory samples), and refining processes can differentially extract or degrade minor bioactive components such as phospholipids, sterols, and polyphenols. Fourth, storage conditions (temperature, light exposure, duration) may lead to oxidative degradation of sensitive compounds, potentially reducing bioactivity over time. Finally, the possibility of adulteration or mislabeling in commercial products cannot be entirely excluded. Therefore, the wide range of bioactivities observed in this study likely reflects a complex interplay of these factors, all of which ultimately influence the concentration and composition of the minor constituents (e.g., sterols, flavonoids, phenolic acids) that we hypothesize to be the primary drivers of the observed COX and LOX inhibition [32, 34, 37].

The integration of computational approaches such as molecular docking and ADMET profiling with experimental enzyme inhibition data is becoming a standard paradigm in natural product research to identify and prioritize bioactive lead compounds [56]. Therefore, to complement the enzyme inhibition data and provide preliminary safety insights, we performed computational ADMET profiling of the four major fatty acid constituents identified by GC‐FID analysis (Table 4). ProTox‐3.0 analysis revealed favorable acute toxicity profiles for all fatty acids. Linoleic acid exhibited the highest predicted LD50 value of 10 000 mg/kg (Toxicity Class 6: non‐toxic), while palmitic and stearic acids demonstrated LD50 values of 900 mg/kg (Toxicity Class 4: harmful if swallowed). Notably, oleic acid showed a predicted LD50 of 48 mg/kg, (not given in the Table 4) as this value appears anomalously low compared to its well‐established dietary safety profile and GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) status. This discrepancy likely reflects limitations in the computational model for highly lipophilic fatty acids and emphasizes the necessity of experimental validation. All four fatty acids were predicted to be non‐mutagenic (Mutagenicity: Inactive, probability 1.0) and exhibited low cytotoxicity risk (Cytotoxicity: Inactive, probability 0.71–0.74).

The pkCSM analysis corroborated the favorable safety profiles, with all fatty acids testing negative for AMES mutagenicity and hepatotoxicity. hERG cardiotoxicity predictions indicated minimal risk, with oleic acid showing a positive prediction for hERG II inhibition, while linoleic, palmitic, and stearic acids tested negative for both hERG I and II inhibition. Interestingly, all four fatty acids were predicted as potential skin sensitizers, which may warrant consideration for topical applications but is not relevant for oral consumption. The predicted oral rat acute toxicity (LD50) values from pkCSM ranged from 1.406 to 1.849 mol/kg, further confirming low acute toxicity potential.

The analysis demonstrated that all major fatty acids exhibited high predicted gastrointestinal (GI) absorption, supporting their bioavailability following oral administration. However, blood‐brain barrier (BBB) permeability predictions varied: linoleic acid was predicted to cross the BBB, while oleic, palmitic, and stearic acids were predicted as non‐BBB permeant, which is consistent with their role as peripheral metabolic substrates rather than central nervous system‐active compounds. Drug‐likeness assessment revealed minimal Lipinski rule violations, with bioavailability scores ranging from 0.85 to 0.55, indicating favorable oral pharmacokinetic properties. The consensus lipophilicity values (Log Po/w) ranged from 5.45 to 6.33, reflecting the highly lipophilic nature of these fatty acids, which may contribute to their membrane permeability and potential for topical applications.

To validate the accuracy of the grid box parameters and docking methodology, a redocking procedure was initially evaluated. All redocking validations yielded RMSD values below 2.0 Å, successfully confirming the reliability of the computational protocol. Subsequently, the docking simulations revealed robust binding affinities for the evaluated fatty acids. Remarkably, both linoleic acid and oleic acid demonstrated an exceptional binding affinity of −7.5 kcal/mol against COX‐2 (PDB: 5F19). Against COX‐1 (PDB: 1DIY), linoleic acid exhibited an outstanding binding affinity of −6.6 kcal/mol, displaying a highly competitive profile closely approaching that of the reference drug ASA (−6.9 kcal/mol). This strong interaction within the predicted catalytic cavities underscores the molecular capacity of these unesterified fatty acids to effectively accommodate within the active sites of cyclooxygenases, strongly aligning with the observed in vitro COX inhibition. For 15‐LOX (PDB: 2IUJ), oleic and linoleic acids exhibited favorable binding affinities of −6.2 and −6.0 kcal/mol, respectively. While the highly potent reference inhibitor NDGA expectedly showed the highest affinity (−9.2 kcal/mol), validating the functional integrity of the detected catalytic cavity, the significant binding scores of the major unsaturated fatty acids provide solid structural support for the robust 15‐LOX inhibitory activity noted in the enzymatic assays. Palmitic acid exhibited slightly weaker binding affinities across all target enzymes compared to its long‐chain unsaturated counterparts. Overall, this structural compatibility provides a rigorous molecular basis, demonstrating that the major fatty acid components possess the structural conformations required to successfully occupy and target the active cavities of these key pro‐inflammatory enzymes. Notably, celecoxib, a selective COX‐2 inhibitor, exhibited a markedly stronger predicted binding affinity (−10.9 kcal/mol) than the non‐selective inhibitors diclofenac (−8.0 kcal/mol) and ASA (−6.2 kcal/mol), consistent with its established structural selectivity for the COX‐2 isoform's larger secondary binding pocket.

These comprehensive in silico predictions provide a strong preliminary evidence base supporting the safety of the major fatty acid constituents in sour cherry seed oil, particularly regarding mutagenic potential and hepatotoxicity. However, the unexpected low LD50 prediction for oleic acid underscores the importance of integrating computational predictions with existing experimental safety data and further in vitro validation.

To provide a preliminary toxicological risk‐benefit assessment of the bioactive constituents identified in this study, we conducted comprehensive in silico ADMET profiling using validated computational platforms (ProTox‐3.0, SwissADME, pkCSM). This represents, to our knowledge, the first systematic computational toxicology evaluation specifically targeting the major fatty acid components of P. cerasus seed oil in the context of their anti‐inflammatory applications. The integration of predictive computational toxicology with experimental enzyme inhibition data exemplifies a contemporary paradigm in natural product drug discovery, wherein in silico modeling accelerates safety assessment, reduces experimental animal use in accordance with the 3Rs principles (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement), and guides evidence‐based prioritization of compounds for subsequent in vitro and in vivo studies [57, 58]. Recent literature strongly supports this integrated methodology, emphasizing that combining computational screening (such as ADMET and molecular docking) with in vitro enzymatic validation provides a robust framework for elucidating the polypharmacological potential, binding mechanisms, and safety margins of plant‐derived lipids and phytochemicals [56, 59, 60].

The in silico toxicity predictions revealed highly favorable safety profiles for the major fatty acid constituents, with particularly encouraging findings regarding mutagenic potential and hepatotoxicity. All four fatty acids—linoleic acid, oleic acid, palmitic acid, and stearic acid—were unanimously predicted to be non‐mutagenic (Mutagenicity: Inactive, ProTox‐3.0 probability = 1.0) and exhibited negative AMES toxicity predictions (pkCSM), indicating no genotoxic risk. This is a critical safety parameter, as mutagenicity represents a primary concern in the early‐stage risk assessment of bioactive natural products intended for long‐term consumption [61]. Furthermore, all fatty acids demonstrated negative hepatotoxicity predictions (pkCSM), suggesting minimal risk of liver damage—an essential consideration given that hepatotoxicity is one of the most common causes of drug attrition in preclinical and clinical development [62]. The predicted low cytotoxicity probabilities (0.71–0.74, ProTox‐3.0) further support the expectation that these fatty acids, at physiologically relevant concentrations, would not induce non‐selective cellular damage.

The predicted acute oral toxicity values merit careful interpretation within the broader context of established nutritional and pharmacological data. Linoleic acid exhibited the most favorable acute toxicity profile, with a predicted LD50 of 10 000 mg/kg (Toxicity Class 6: non‐toxic), aligning with its status as an essential omega‐6 fatty acid widely consumed in the human diet without adverse effects [63]. Palmitic and stearic acids demonstrated predicted LD50 values of 900 mg/kg (Toxicity Class 4: harmful if swallowed), which, while appearing moderate in the classification system, are substantially higher than therapeutic doses and consistent with their extensive dietary consumption history. However, oleic acid presented an anomalous prediction of LD50 = 48 mg/kg (Toxicity Class 2: fatal if swallowed), which starkly contradicts its well‐documented safety profile as a primary component of olive oil, its GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) designation by the FDA, and decades of safe human consumption at gram‐per‐day levels [64, 65]. This discrepancy likely reflects a known limitation of QSAR models when applied to highly lipophilic long‐chain fatty acids, wherein structural similarity searches may retrieve training set compounds with distinct toxicological profiles. Therefore, this specific ProTox‐3.0 prediction for oleic acid should be entirely disregarded. Instead, safety evaluations must rely on the more pharmacokinetically plausible pkCSM prediction, its FDA GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) designation, and its extensive history of safe human dietary consumption. Indeed, the pkCSM model provided a more realistic prediction for oleic acid (LD50 = 1.849 mol/kg, approximately 522 000 mg/kg), underscoring the importance of cross‐validating predictions across multiple computational platforms and integrating them with experimental literature data [57]. This example illustrates a critical principle in computational toxicology: in silico predictions serve as hypothesis‐generating tools to prioritize experimental testing, rather than as definitive safety determinations, particularly when predictions conflict with robust historical safety evidence.

The pharmacokinetic predictions from SwissADME provide valuable insights into the potential bioavailability and distribution of these fatty acids. All four compounds demonstrated high predicted gastrointestinal (GI) absorption, which is consistent with their lipophilic nature (consensus Log Po/w = 5.45–6.03) and established mechanisms of dietary lipid absorption via enterocyte uptake and chylomicron‐mediated transport [66]. The bioavailability scores ranged from 0.55 to 0.85, with linoleic and oleic acids receiving the highest scores, suggesting favorable oral bioavailability profiles. Interestingly, only linoleic acid was predicted to cross the blood‐brain barrier (BBB permeant: Yes), while oleic, palmitic, and stearic acids were predicted as BBB non‐permeant. This differential BBB permeability may have implications for potential central nervous system‐related anti‐inflammatory effects, as linoleic acid and its metabolites have been implicated in neuroinflammatory modulation [67]. However, the peripheral anti‐inflammatory activities observed in this study—particularly 15‐LOX, COX, and trypsin inhibition—do not require CNS penetration, and thus the BBB non‐permeability of most fatty acids does not detract from their therapeutic potential for systemic inflammatory conditions.

The hERG cardiotoxicity predictions warrant brief consideration. While oleic acid was predicted as a potential hERG II inhibitor, this is unlikely to pose a clinically significant risk given the extensive cardiovascular safety data for oleic acid‐rich diets, particularly the Mediterranean diet, which is associated with reduced cardiovascular mortality [68]. Nonetheless, this prediction highlights a theoretical consideration for high‐dose concentrated formulations, and future in vitro hERG channel assays could provide experimental clarification if pharmaceutical development is pursued.

An important finding from the pkCSM analysis was the prediction of skin sensitization potential for all four fatty acids. While this may warrant consideration for topical cosmetic or dermatological applications, it is not directly relevant to the oral or systemic anti‐inflammatory applications proposed for sour cherry seed oil in this study. Furthermore, skin sensitization predictions from in silico models often exhibit lower specificity compared to mutagenicity or hepatotoxicity predictions, and experimental patch testing would be required to definitively assess dermal allergenicity [69].

When contextualizing these in silico findings within the broader literature, it is noteworthy that previous experimental toxicity studies on related cherry seed products support the favorable safety predictions generated in this study. Chen et al. reported that Chinese cherry (Cerasus pseudocerasus L.) seed oil, which shares a similar fatty acid composition with P. cerasus seed oil, exhibited an oral LD50 exceeding 4000 mg/kg in rodents, with no mutagenic effects in Ames tests, micronucleus assays, or sperm abnormality tests, and no adverse effects in 28‐day repeated‐dose toxicity studies [70]. Similarly, Bak et al. demonstrated that sour cherry seed kernel meal was well‐tolerated at oral doses up to 3000 mg/kg in mice, with no alterations in liver or kidney function markers or histopathological changes [71]. These experimental findings align closely with the favorable in silico predictions generated in the present study, providing converging evidence from both computational and experimental approaches that sour cherry seed oil constituents exhibit a wide margin of safety.

Moreover, the potent 15‐LOX inhibitory activity observed in this study (up to 80% inhibition for Soxhlet‐extracted oil) takes on added significance when considered alongside the favorable toxicity predictions. Selective LOX inhibitors have been actively pursued as anti‐inflammatory drug candidates, yet concerns regarding off‐target toxicity, particularly hepatotoxicity and cardiovascular effects, have limited their clinical translation [72]. The current findings suggest that sour cherry seed oil, as a complex mixture of naturally occurring fatty acids with demonstrated LOX inhibitory activity and favorable predicted toxicity profiles, may circumvent some of these limitations. The multi‐component nature of the oil may also confer additional advantages through synergistic or additive effects among bioactive constituents, a phenomenon frequently observed in whole‐plant extracts [73].

It is essential to emphasize that while the convergence of favorable in silico predictions from multiple independent QSAR platforms (ProTox‐3.0, SwissADME, pkCSM), combined with supportive experimental toxicity literature on related cherry seed products, provides a robust preliminary safety assessment, these findings must be experimentally validated prior to clinical translation. The next logical step in the development pathway would be to conduct standardized in vitro cytotoxicity assays (e.g., MTT, neutral red uptake, lactate dehydrogenase release) using relevant cell lines, including hepatocytes (HepG2), renal epithelial cells (HEK‐293), and gastrointestinal cells (Caco‐2), to experimentally confirm the predicted low cytotoxicity and assess selectivity indices (ratio of cytotoxic IC50 to enzyme inhibitory IC50). Subsequently, acute and subchronic in vivo toxicity studies conducted in accordance with OECD guidelines (Test No. 420, 407) would be necessary to determine the no‐observed‐adverse‐effect level (NOAEL), assess potential dose‐dependent organ toxicity, and establish safe exposure limits for human consumption [74, 75].

In conclusion, the integration of computational toxicology predictions with experimental enzyme inhibition data in this study has generated a comprehensive preliminary risk‐benefit profile for sour cherry seed oil. The favorable in silico safety predictions, particularly the absence of mutagenic potential and hepatotoxicity risk, combined with the demonstrated potent 15‐LOX inhibitory activity, position sour cherry seed oil as a promising candidate for further preclinical development as a natural anti‐inflammatory agent. This work establishes a foundational dataset that will guide the design of subsequent in vitro and in vivo toxicity studies, thereby advancing the evidence‐based development of P. cerasus seed oil toward potential therapeutic or nutraceutical applications.

Conclusions

This study represents the first comprehensive investigation of the effects of sour cherry seed oil on trypsin, chymotrypsin, 15‐LOX, COX‐1, and COX‐2 enzymes, coupled with computational toxicological profiling of its major fatty acid constituents. The results highlight the notable 15‐LOX inhibitory potential of sour cherry seed oil (up to 80% inhibition for Soxhlet‐extracted oil), which provides a mechanistic basis for the anti‐inflammatory and analgesic effects previously reported for sour cherry seed extracts in the literature. Fatty acid analysis revealed similar profiles across all samples, dominated by linoleic acid (31%–57%), oleic acid (22%–46%), and palmitic acid (5%–18%), though their relative proportions varied with extraction method and source. The lack of a strong correlation between the fatty acid profiles (analyzed as methyl esters) and the enzyme inhibition activities of the whole oils suggests that the observed bioactivities are not simply a function of individual fatty acid concentrations. This finding points to two important considerations: first, the biological activity of triacylglycerols—the predominant form of fatty acids in the oils—may differ from that of their constituent free fatty acids due to differences in steric accessibility and molecular structure; and second, other minor components, such as phospholipids, sterols, tocopherols, and phenolic compounds, may significantly contribute to the overall anti‐inflammatory effects. Consequently, targeted analysis on characterizing the triacylglycerol profiles and these minor constituents, along with in vivo animal studies or cell culture experiments to investigate anti‐inflammatory and analgesic mechanisms, will be essential to fully elucidate the pharmacological potential of sour cherry seed oil.

Importantly, the comprehensive in silico ADMET profiling conducted in this study provides strong preliminary evidence supporting the safety of the major fatty acid constituents. All four major fatty acids (linoleic, oleic, palmitic, and stearic acids) were predicted to be non‐mutagenic, exhibited negative hepatotoxicity predictions, and demonstrated minimal cytotoxicity risk according to converging predictions from ProTox‐3.0, pkCSM, and SwissADME platforms. These computational findings, which align with previously published experimental toxicity studies on related cherry seed products reporting oral LD50 values exceeding 4000 mg/kg and absence of genotoxicity [70, 71], establish a favorable preliminary risk‐benefit profile for sour cherry seed oil. The integration of experimental enzyme inhibition data with predictive computational toxicology in this study exemplifies a contemporary, evidence‐based approach to natural product drug discovery, wherein in silico modeling accelerates safety assessment, reduces animal testing requirements, and guides prioritization for subsequent preclinical validation [57, 58].

Nevertheless, while the convergence of favorable in silico predictions and supportive literature provides a strong foundation, experimental validation through in vitro cytotoxicity assays (e.g., MTT, LDH release) on hepatocyte, renal, and gastrointestinal cell lines, followed by acute and subchronic in vivo toxicity studies in accordance with OECD guidelines (Test No. 420, 407), remains essential prior to therapeutic application [74, 75]. Additionally, quality control measures including analysis of residual solvents, heavy metal contamination, pesticide residues, and microbiological purity should be established, particularly for commercial samples, to ensure consumer safety and product standardization.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the findings are based on in vitro enzyme inhibition assays, and the translation to in vivo anti‐inflammatory efficacy requires validation through animal models and clinical studies. Second, the bioavailability and pharmacokinetic properties of the bioactive constituents following oral administration have not been experimentally determined and warrant investigation to establish appropriate dosing regimens. Third, the authenticity and processing conditions of the commercial samples could not be independently verified; therefore, results are interpreted under the assumption that products contain authentic P. cerasus seed oil as labeled. Future studies should prioritize authenticated samples with full quality control documentation, including analysis of residual solvents, heavy metals, and pesticide residues.

Based on the collective data obtained—potent 15‐LOX inhibition, moderate COX and trypsin inhibitory activities, and favorable predicted toxicity profiles—sour cherry seed oil demonstrates considerable potential for development as a safe, multi‐target anti‐inflammatory component in functional foods, dietary supplements, or pharmaceutical formulations. This research addresses a critical knowledge gap regarding the anti‐inflammatory mechanisms of sour cherry seed oil and provides a valuable foundation for future translational studies aimed at clinical validation of its therapeutic potential.

Instrumentation

Absorbance measurements were performed using a Molecular Devices SpectraMax ABS Plus microplate reader. Plant Material and Oil Samples

Ten different commercial sour cherry seed oil samples from various brands were procured from online retailers and local herbalists in Türkiye.

Sour cherry (Prunus cerasus L.) fruits were harvested from the Yukarı Çavundur Village in Çubuk District, Ankara, Turkey (coordinates: 40°23'53.5“N, 33°05'29.8”E) on July 6, 2024. The seeds were immediately separated from the fruits, washed with water, and blotted dry with paper towels. The seeds were then air‐dried at room temperature in the shade. A voucher specimen was collected from the same tree and deposited at the Herbarium of the Faculty of Pharmacy, Gazi University (Ankara, Turkey), with the voucher number GUEF‐3932.

Extraction

Soxhlet Extraction: Eighty grams of coarsely ground sour cherry seeds were placed into an extraction thimble and extracted with 1 L of n‐hexane in a Soxhlet apparatus for 6 h. The resulting extract was concentrated under reduced pressure at 38°C using a rotary evaporator.

Maceration: Forty‐five grams of coarsely ground sour cherry seeds were macerated with 200 mL of n‐hexane at room temperature for 48 h. The mixture was then filtered, and the residue plant material was re‐extracted three more times with fresh 200 mL portions of n‐hexane, each for 48 h. All filtrates were combined and concentrated under reduced pressure at 38°C using a rotary evaporator.

In Silico ADMET Prediction

Computational toxicity assessment of the major fatty acid constituents (linoleic acid, oleic acid, palmitic acid, and stearic acid) was performed using three complementary web‐based platforms to predict absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) parameters. Canonical SMILES (Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System) structures for each fatty acid were retrieved from the PubChem database [85].

Acute oral toxicity (LD50), organ‐specific toxicity (hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, cardiotoxicity), cytotoxicity, and mutagenicity were predicted using ProTox‐3.0 (https://tox.charite.de/protox3/), a validated quantitative structure‐activity relationship (QSAR) model with reported prediction accuracies exceeding 80% for rodent acute toxicity endpoints [58].

Drug‐likeness parameters, including Lipinski's rule of five violations, gastrointestinal absorption, blood‐brain barrier permeability, and bioavailability scores, were assessed using SwissADME (http://www.swissadme.ch/) [86].

Additional toxicity endpoints, including AMES mutagenicity, skin sensitization, and hERG‐mediated cardiotoxicity, were evaluated using the pkCSM platform (https://biosig.lab.uq.edu.au/pkcsm/), which employs graph‐based signatures to predict pharmacokinetic and toxicity properties [87]. All predictions were conducted using default parameters, and results were compiled for comparative analysis.

Molecular Docking Studies

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the observed in vitro enzyme inhibitions and to investigate the binding affinities of the major fatty acid constituents (linoleic acid, oleic acid, and palmitic acid), molecular docking simulations were performed using the CB‐Dock2 web server (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb‐dock2/). CB‐Dock2 is a structure‐based blind docking approach integrating cavity detection and AutoDock Vina. The protein targets were carefully selected from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) to reflect the biological sources utilized in the in vitro enzymatic assays. Accordingly, soybean 15‐lipoxygenase (PDB ID: 2IUJ) was selected to represent the plant‐derived 15‐LOX enzyme, while ovine cyclooxygenase‐1 (PDB ID: 1DIY) was selected to match the ovine‐derived COX‐1 enzyme. Since a high‐resolution crystal structure for ovine cyclooxygenase‐2 is currently unavailable in the PDB, the highly homologous human cyclooxygenase‐2 (PDB ID: 5F19) was utilized as the validated standard model for COX‐2 interactions. To validate the computational docking protocol and provide a relative benchmark for binding potencies, the reference inhibitors used in the wet‐lab assays—acetylsalicylic acid (ASA) for COX‐1 and nordihydroguaiaretic acid (NDGA) for 15‐LOX—were also docked into their respective targets. The 3D structures of all ligands (SDF format) were retrieved from the PubChem database. During the automated blind docking procedure, the algorithm scanned the entire protein surfaces to identify putative binding cavities and calculated the lowest Vina binding scores (kcal/mol) for the representative poses within the primary catalytic domains. To further strengthen the comparative analysis for COX‐2, celecoxib, a selective COX‐2 inhibitor, and diclofenac, a non‐selective NSAID, were additionally docked into 5F19 as positive controls alongside ASA, allowing a direct comparison of binding potencies within the same catalytic cavity. To confirm the reliability of the docking protocol, redocking validation was performed using the RMSD Calculator tool on the Tamarind Bio platform (https://www.tamarind.bio). For COX‐2, since the 5F19 structure used in the blind docking studies lacks a suitable co‐crystallized small‐molecule ligand for redocking validation, the co‐crystallized ligand of celecoxib was instead redocked into the homologous structure PDB ID: 3LN1; for COX‐1, redocking was performed directly on 1DIY using its own co‐crystallized ligand; and for 15‐LOX, since the 2IUJ structure lacks a co‐crystallized organic ligand, a homologous soybean lipoxygenase‐1 structure in complex with 13(S)‐hydroperoxyoctadecadienoic acid (PDB ID: 1IK3) was used as a surrogate template for validation.
