A novel transparent visual channel translates into enhanced surgical safety and efficiency for prolapsed lumbar disc herniation: a comparative clinical study
Authors: Junwei Zhang, Gen Li, Yang Sun, Shuo Feng, Bin Pan, Maji Sun, Feng Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe standard metal working channel in percutaneous translaminar endoscopic discectomy (PTED) creates a significant visual “blind spot,” presenting a particular challenge during surgery for prolapsed lumbar disc herniation (PLDH). This obstruction can result in semi-blind surgical maneuvers, elevating the risk of neural injury and incomplete fragment removal. To overcome this fundamental limitation, we developed a novel transparent visual channel (TVC) and assessed its clinical performance.MethodsIn a retrospective comparative study, we analyzed 65 patients with PLDH who underwent PTED, utilizing either the novel TVC (n = 29) or the conventional metal working channel (n = 36). Key metrics for comparison included surgical field of view (quantified using the structural similarity index measure [SSIM]), total operation time, frequency of intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring alerts, standard perioperative clinical indicators, complication rates, and patient-reported outcomes (Visual Analog Scale [VAS] for pain, Oswestry Disability Index [ODI], and MacNab criteria).ResultsThe TVC group demonstrated a significantly larger and more consistent surgical field of view, evidenced by a markedly higher SSIM (98.2% ± 1.1% vs. 54.6% ± 8.3%, P < 0.05). Operative efficiency was improved in the TVC group, with a significantly shorter mean operation time (83.2 ± 9.7 min vs. 97.5 ± 8.9 min, P < 0.05). Critically, the use of the TVC was associated with enhanced intraoperative safety, as indicated by a significant reduction in the number of intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring alerts during neural decompression (2 vs. 11, P < 0.05). Both groups exhibited significant and comparable improvements in VAS scores, ODI scores, and MacNab outcomes at follow-up, with no statistically significant differences between the groups (P > 0.05).ConclusionThe novel transparent visual channel effectively converts the PTED procedure from a semi-blind technique to a fully visualized one. This material innovation delivers direct clinical advantages by improving intraoperative safety through reduced neural irritation and increasing procedural efficiency, all while maintaining the excellent clinical outcomes associated with standard PTED. This study substantiates the TVC as a significant advancement in endoscopic spine surgery instrumentation, directly addressing a core visual constraint of the established technique.Keywords: Spine endoscopy, Transparent visual channel, Surgical safety, Procedural efficiency, Percutaneous translaminar endoscopic discectomy, Lumbar disc herniation

Introduction

For patients with prolapsed lumbar intervertebral disc herniation (PLDH) who have failed conservative treatment, surgical removal of the herniated nucleus pulposus is the most effective approach [1]. Conventional open surgery has gradually been replaced by the less-invasive endoscopic surgical methods [2–4]. One such method, percutaneous translaminar endoscopic discectomy (PTED), has advantages such as relatively simple intraoperative puncture, short fluoroscopy time, and direct visualization of anatomical structures under endoscopy [5, 6]. The PTED technique has been applied to most types of PLDH [7, 8]. However, this method is associated with technical difficulties [9, 10]. The objective lens and the working channel of the endoscope used for PTED are coaxial, resulting in a limited surgical operating space [5]. Because the working channel is composed of metal materials, the visual field is relatively narrow. Consequently, the direction and angle of the channel and endoscope must be constantly adjusted during the operation [11]. In PLDH, this often makes it difficult to reach the target point or conduct a full exploration, potentially compromising complete removal of the prolapsed nucleus pulposus tissue [12]. This limitation not only increases the risk of residual fragments but also elevates the potential for neural injury due to necessary but “blind” manipulations. Moreover, severe prolapsed disc herniation often reduces the effective area of the spinal canal and may displace the nerve roots to an abnormal position, increasing the risk of nerve root and dural injury during laminar decompression or placement of the working channel [13].

To address these limitations, we designed a novel transparent visual channel (TVC), which provides a larger intraoperative visual field than current endoscopes used in PTED surgery. The aim of this study is to systematically analyze the clinical data of 65 patients with PLDH who underwent spinal endoscopic surgery using either a conventional metallic endoscope or the novel TVC and to evaluate the impact of the TVC on surgical visualization, intraoperative safety, and procedural efficiency. This study was designed to bridge this translational gap by clinically evaluating the TVC. The translational pathway involved: (1) identifying the clear clinical limitation (metal channel visual obstruction); (2) developing a technical solution (TVC with high-transmittance polymer); and (3) clinically validating its performance against the standard of care. We hypothesized that the TVC, by eliminating the visual obstruction of the metal channel, would (1) provide a larger and more consistent surgical field of view, (2) reduce intraoperative neural irritation, thereby enhancing safety, (3) improve procedural efficiency, and (4) achieve clinical outcomes non-inferior to the conventional technique.

Materials and methods

Properties of the TVC

The TVC was fabricated using the polypropylene/polyamide (PA6) blend system and molecular chain topological cross-linking technology. It has a light transmittance of > 80%, a tensile strength of ≥ 45 MPa, and a wear rate of < 0.2 mm3/N·m. It has passed the in vitro cytotoxicity test according to the ISO 10,993 standard (report no.: STI − 20231109–026 N). The material selection (polypropylene/polyamide blend) was driven by the dual clinical requirements of high light transmittance for visualization and sufficient mechanical strength (tensile strength ≥ 45 MPa) to withstand surgical forces. The molecular chain topological cross-linking technology was employed to achieve a low wear rate (< 0.2 mm3/N·m), ensuring durability and resistance to abrasion during use, a critical property for clinical practicality and safety. The TVC is designed for reusable use and can be sterilized using standard sterilization methods (e.g., ethylene oxide, plasma sterilization) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Surgical field of view (FOV) analysis

The endoscopic system used in this study features an objective lens with a 30° angled view and an inherent FOV of 90° (based on manufacturer specifications). However, when the conventional metal working channel is employed, the effective FOV is significantly reduced due to physical obstruction by the channel wall. In contrast, the TVC, with its > 80% light transmittance, preserves nearly the full inherent FOV of the objective lens, allowing for unobstructed peripheral visualization. This is particularly critical during maneuvers such as nerve root retraction and migrated fragment retrieval, where anatomical structures often lie outside the immediate line of sight of the metal channel.

Factors affecting intraoperative visibility

Intraoperative visual obstruction in conventional PTED arises from several factors: (1) anatomical crowding due to hypertrophic ligamentum flavum or osteophytes; (2) instrument crowding when multiple tools occupy the narrow channel; (3) blood and tissue debris adhering to the channel wall; and (4) channel-wall shadowing, which creates a “blind arc” around the periphery. The TVC mitigates these issues by enabling visualization through the channel wall, thus allowing the surgeon to monitor instruments and anatomy beyond the direct optical axis, thereby reducing semi-blind maneuvers.

Patient groups

A total of 65 patients with PLDH who were admitted to our hospital between February 2023 and February 2024 were included in this retrospective study. Patients were assigned to the TVC group (n = 29) or the conventional PTED group (n = 36) based on the chronological order of admission and surgical scheduling, without intentional selection bias. As the TVC was introduced after a period of conventional PTED surgeries, this resulted in the TVC group cases being performed chronologically after those in the PTED group.

Inclusion criteria

The following inclusion criteria were applied: (1) Low back pain accompanied by varying degrees of radiating pain, numbness in the lower limbs, or other symptoms in the areas innervated by the affected nerves; (2) imaging data indicating that the prolapsed nucleus pulposus conforms to the prolapsed type of lumbar disc herniation in the Lee classification and is consistent with clinical symptoms and signs; (3) no history of lumbar spine surgery; and (4) no improvement in symptoms after 3 months of conservative treatment or the presence of nerve damage symptoms, meeting the indications for surgery.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) PLDH requiring fusion and internal fixation due to multi-level herniation and multi-level stenosis (involving ≥ 2 segments), lumbar spondylolisthesis, or instability; (2) lumbar disc herniation accompanied by severe annulus fibrosus calcification; and (3) PLDH complicated with infection, spinal tumors, or tuberculosis.

Ethics statement

The research protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (ethics approval no.: XYFY2023-KL498-01). All participants obtained informed consent.

Surgical expertise

All procedures were performed by a single senior spine surgeon (F.Y.) with more than 10 years of dedicated experience in percutaneous endoscopic lumbar surgery, including over 300 PTED cases prior to the study period.

Surgical methods

TVC Group: Patients were placed in a prone position under general anesthesia. The surgical segment was accurately located and the puncture point was marked using imaging guidance. According to the involved segment, electromyogram electrodes were placed on the muscles to be monitored. The surgical area was disinfected and draped. Then, under C-arm fluoroscopic guidance, an accurate puncture was performed with a Kirschner wire, and an 8-mm-long skin incision was made at the puncture point. A series of dilators were sequentially inserted to gradually establish the surgical channel. The TVC was inserted, and an endoscopic system was placed (Fig. 2). After the bone structure was exposed, a drill, a laminar rongeur, and an endoscopic trephine were used to enlarge the laminar interspace window to expose the ligamentum flavum, which was bluntly dissected under the endoscope. The working channel was rotated into the spinal canal under direct vision. A radiofrequency electrode was used to remove the fascia and epidural fat, exposing the dural sac, annulus fibrosus, nerve root, and nucleus pulposus. The nerve was rotated and retracted through the TVC to expose the herniated nucleus pulposus. The integrity of the nerve was observed through the transparent wall of the working channel, as was the presence of any residual nucleus pulposus outside the channel wall. With the entire nerve visible, the nucleus pulposus in the spinal canal was removed with forceps. The nerve root was explored, and free and adherent inflammatory tissues attached around the nerve root were resected to ensure sufficient decompression (Fig. 3). At the end of the operation, the nerve root was freely movable. The endoscope and TVC were then removed sequentially, and the incision was closed with interrupted sutures. The wound was covered with a sterile dressing.

PTED Group: The steps for patient positioning and puncture localization were identical to those in the TVC group. The conventional metal working channel and endoscope were placed, and the spinal endoscope was connected to the camera monitoring system. The remaining surgical steps were the same as those in the TVC group (Fig. 3).

Intraoperative monitoring

In both groups, continuous electromyography (EMG) monitoring was routinely used throughout the surgical procedure for all patients. Electrodes were placed on the muscles (e.g., tibialis anterior, gastrocnemius) innervated by the nerves corresponding to the involved vertebral segment, either L4/L5 or L5/S1. During the operation, both the healthy and affected sides were monitored, with the healthy side serving as the control, to observe the peak value, waveform, and motor units of the surface EMG signals [15]. A continuous abnormal response on the intraoperative EMG or changes in the EMG waveform were used as early warning indicators. In such an event, the surgeon was to immediately stop the operation, identify the cause, and continue after the EMG returned to normal to avoid spinal nerve injury.

Postoperative management

Patients were allowed to ambulate 2 days after surgery, wearing a rigid lumbar brace when out of bed. They were instructed to begin lumbar and back muscle exercises 1 month postoperatively and to avoid lumbar load-bearing or excessive fatigue for 3–6 months. The use of a rigid lumbar brace in the early postoperative period reflects our institutional preference for providing temporary external support, reducing paraspinal muscle strain, and promoting patient confidence during mobilization after endoscopic decompression.

Outcome measures

The primary endpoints of this study were chosen to reflect procedural safety and efficiency, given the hypothesized advantages of the TVC. We recorded the duration of intraoperative fluoroscopy, operation time, postoperative hospital stay, incidence of nerve root injury, incidence of cerebrospinal fluid leakage, and number of intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring alarms. We evaluated pain and function using the visual analogue scale (VAS) and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), and assessed clinical efficacy using the modified MacNab criteria. The structural similarity index measure (SSIM) was used to compare surgical field coverage between the two surgical techniques. We extracted three representative high-quality frames from each surgical video at key stages: (1) initial spinal canal entry, (2) nerve root exposure, and (3) final decompression. These frames were compared against a standardized reference template image using MATLAB v2022a. The reference template image was selected from a high-quality intraoperative view obtained during an uneventful TVC-assisted procedure, representing optimal visualization of the neural structures and surgical field. The SSIM index ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater similarity to the reference in terms of luminance, contrast, and structure. A total of 195 images (3 per patient × 65 patients) were analyzed.

Follow-up time

Relevant indicators of clinical efficacy were evaluated at 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months after the surgery.

Statistical analysis

SPSS v26.0 was used for statistical analyses. Measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s), and compared between groups by using the independent-samples t-test. Categorical data were expressed as percentages (%) and compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Surgical field coverage

The SSIM is an objective image-analysis metric used to assess the structural similarity between two images. In this study, representative high-quality endoscopic images were used as reference templates, and the intraoperative images of each group were compared with them. SSIM analysis showed that 98.2% of surgical field images in the TVC group were similar to the reference templates, compared to 54.6% in the PTED group (P < 0.001; Fig. 4). A higher SSIM value indicates better clarity, exposure, and visual consistency of the surgical field. The images in the TVC group had higher fidelity, and the field of view was more stable and unobstructed throughout the surgery (Table 2). Operative parameters

The TVC group had a significantly shorter operation time than the PTED group (P < 0.05). The average postoperative hospital stay and the duration of intraoperative fluoroscopy did not significantly differ between the two groups (P > 0.05; Table 2). The number of intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring alarms when placing the working channel and exploring the nerves also did not differ between the two groups (P > 0.05). However, the number of alarms during intraoperative decompression was significantly lower in the TVC group than in the PTED group (P < 0.05; Table 3).

Incidence of complications

No instances of nerve injury, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, or surgical-site infection occurred in either group. One case (3.44%) of paresthesia (e.g., numbness and burning pain) occurred in the TVC group, while 4 cases (11.11%) occurred in the PTED group; the difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05, Fisher’s exact test). Postoperative recurrence was rare in both groups, with 1 case (3.45%) in the TVC group and 2 cases (5.56%) in the PTED group (P > 0.05, Table 4).

Discussion

The management of prolapsed lumbar disc herniation (PLDH) demands exceptional visualization due to the frequent migration and concealment of disc fragments. The principal finding of this translational study is that a simple yet fundamental change in the composition of the working channel—from opaque metal to transparent polymer—can effectively address a long-standing visual limitation in endoscopic spine surgery. Our results demonstrate that the TVC successfully translates a conceptual design into a clinical tool that not only significantly expands the surgical field of view but, more importantly, represents a meaningful advancement in surgical visualization, transitioning the procedure from a largely tactile-dependent approach to one with substantially enhanced visual feedback.

This direct visual feedback translated into two critical clinical benefits: a substantial reduction in intraoperative neural irritation and improved procedural efficiency. The expanded surgical field effectively eliminated the blind zone, transitioning the procedure from a “semi-blind” to a fully visualized technique. This improvement, where visual feedback rather than tactile sensation drives intraoperative decisions, represents a fundamental improvement in the safety philosophy of endoscopic decompression [16]. By enabling better identification of anatomical structures, the TVC directly helped to minimize stimulation of the nerve roots and surrounding tissues [17] and to more effectively detect hidden nucleus pulposus tissue, thereby conclusively improving surgical safety.

The PTED technique was first proposed by Ruetten in 2006 [18]. Studies suggest that PTED is suitable for the treatment of PLDH at the L5/S1 level, but it often has unsatisfactory results for the L4/5 and above segments due to the narrow interlaminar space [19]. The introduction of the under-microscope trephine and power system has increased the scope of indications for the interlaminar approach, but specialized instruments are still needed, and there is a risk of damage to the dura mater and nerve roots [20, 21]. Cheng et al. [22] improved the puncture position of the lamina to address the problem of difficult intraoperative puncture, which reduced puncture difficulty and shortened operation time, but it could not effectively change the surgical field of view. Huang et al. [23] established a channel with a drill at the inner edge of the pedicle of the lower vertebral body in order to fully expose the nerve root, which partially expanded the under-microscope field of view during PTED. Although many new surgical strategies have been proposed, all of them focus on the positioning, approach, and laminoplasty during PTED, and a critical limitation remained unaddressed: the endoscopic working channel itself. Our study directly targets this unaddressed core limitation. While previous innovations focused on optimizing the path to the target, the TVC revolutionizes the experience at the target. It addresses the fundamental visual obstruction caused by the channel itself, which is the root cause of ‘semi-blind’ manipulation. Since the working channel is made of metal, it fundamentally blocks the surgical field of view, causing improper operation of the approach instruments by the surgeon during PTED due to the limited intraoperative field of view [5, 24, 25]. Especially during the key step of pulling the nerve root to expose the nucleus pulposus, a limited field of view makes it impossible to observe the nerve root in real time, which may result in stimulation of or even damage to the nerve root. To overcome this core problem of visual obstruction, we designed the TVC. In the context of PLDH surgery, the TVC’s transparency is transformative. It allows for continuous, real-time visualization of the nerve root during retraction and the surrounding area during fragment retrieval. This not only minimizes the risk of iatrogenic injury (enhancing safety) but also streamlines the process of locating and removing migrated fragments (improving efficiency). The expanded field of view and increased brightness under endoscopy facilitate the identification of anatomical structures and the observation of changes in the nerve root while exposing the protruding nucleus pulposus, thereby making the procedure both safer and more efficient, which is particularly valuable when dealing with the complex pathology of PLDH. The instrument enables full visualization in real time during surgery, which reduces the difficulty of the surgical operation and may be especially useful for inexperienced surgeons.

A key step in spinal endoscopic surgery is comprehensive and effective electrocoagulation hemostasis to improve the clarity of the endoscopic field of view [26, 27]. While quantitative blood loss comparison was unfeasible due to irrigation fluid, the TVC’s transparency conferred a distinct advantage in managing bleeding, a challenge accentuated in PLDH surgery where exploration for migrated fragments can lead to vascular irritation. Unlike the conventional metal channel which obscures the periphery, the TVC enables real-time visualization of vessels and bleeding points beyond the immediate working area. This facilitates precise and efficient electrocoagulation, preventing the visual obscuration and potential complications that can compromise safety and disrupt surgical flow. In addition, during nerve decompression, the working channel will be closely attached to the nerve root [5, 28]. The conventional metal working channel conducts electricity, which may cause slight irritation of a nerve root that is in proximity to the metal working channel [17, 24]. Furthermore, the non-conductive nature of the TVC material eliminates the theoretical risk of electrical current transmission to adjacent neural tissues during radiofrequency use—a potential concern with metal channels. These properties collectively enhance the safety profile and procedural fluency of PTED for PLDH.

While the reduction in intraoperative EMG alerts in the TVC group suggests decreased mechanical irritation of neural structures, it should be interpreted with caution. EMG activity can be influenced by multiple factors, including anesthetic depth, muscle baseline activity, and electrocautery interference. Therefore, EMG alerts serve as a surrogate indicator of intraoperative neural proximity rather than a direct measure of neurological injury. Nonetheless, the consistent reduction in alerts during critical steps such as decompression supports the improved visual control afforded by the TVC.

The most significant finding of this comparative study lies in the equivalence of patient-reported outcomes between the two groups. This equivalence in patient-centered outcomes is of paramount importance: it demonstrates that the TVC achieves the gold-standard therapeutic goal of PTED not by altering the endpoint, but by optimizing the pathway to it. The TVC provides a safer, more controlled, and efficient route to the same excellent result. For PLDH, where the risk of residual fragment or neural injury is a primary concern, this superior procedural pathway translates to a more reliable and confident surgical strategy, potentially reducing the ‘cost’—in terms of intraoperative risk and surgeon anxiety—of achieving a successful outcome.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Its retrospective design and the sample size, while reasonable for an initial comparative study of a novel device, may limit the generalizability of the findings. It is important to emphasize that patients with true PLDH requiring surgery represent a select clinical subgroup, making large-scale recruitment inherently challenging and underscoring the value of this preliminary investigation. However, the fact that statistically significant and clinically meaningful advantages in visualization and procedural safety were demonstrated even in this cohort underscores the robust effect size of the TVC. The single-surgeon experience, while controlling for technical variability, may also introduce bias. Future prospective, multi-center randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes are warranted to confirm these findings and to explore the impact of the TVC on the learning curve for endoscopic spine surgery.

The TVC cases were performed chronologically after the conventional PTED cases in this series, raising the possibility that improved outcomes could reflect the surgeon’s increasing experience rather than the device itself. To address this, we note that the operating surgeon had already performed over 300 PTED procedures prior to this study and had reached a stable performance plateau. Moreover, the TVC’s advantages—such as the immediate expansion of the visual field and reduced neurophysiological alerts—were observed from the very first case, suggesting an intrinsic device benefit rather than a learning curve effect. Nevertheless, we acknowledge this chronological sequence as a study limitation and recommend future randomized trials to further control for this variable.

This study exemplifies a direct patient-to-bench-to-patient translational research cycle. The initial clinical problem (poor visualization) was identified in the operating room, leading to the design and fabrication of a new device (TVC) incorporating material science. It was then reintroduced into the clinical setting, where its benefits were rigorously quantified. The successful translation of the TVC paves the way for future research directions: (1) Exploring the use of even more advanced biomaterials to enhance mechanical and optical properties; (2) Integrating imaging or navigation technologies within the transparent channel for a hybrid visualization platform; and (3) Conducting large-scale, multi-center studies to validate its impact on surgeon learning curves and long-term patient outcomes. The TVC therefore serves not only as an improved tool but also as a potential platform for future technological integration in endoscopic spine surgery, such as incorporating augmented reality or navigation cues directly within the channel.

In conclusion, the TVC achieves the excellent clinical outcomes of conventional PTED for PLDH through a superior surgical process—one that is safer, more efficient, and fully visualized. The TVC represents more than a technical advancement; it is a meaningful advancement in endoscopic spine surgery instrumentation. By providing a reliable solution to the core problem of visual obstruction, it sets a new standard for safety and efficiency in PTED for PLDH. The promising results here provide a strong rationale for the next translational step: large-scale, multi-center studies to validate its impact on surgeon learning curves and long-term patient outcomes, and to explore its integration with augmented reality or navigation systems directly within the transparent channel.
