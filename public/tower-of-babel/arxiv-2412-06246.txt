1.1.
Two-side least singular value estimates

The main contribution of this paper is a lower bound for
\sigma_{min}(X)
that is asymptotically sharp up to a log factor.

Theorem 1.5
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,2),

\delta>1
, and let
N=\lceil\delta n\rceil
. Let
\xi
be a random variable with a symmetric distribution
\xi\overset{\operatorname{law}}{=}-\xi
satisfying that, for some
C_{u}>C_{\ell}>0
and some
T_{0}>0
, we have that for any
t\geq T_{0}
,

(1.1)

C_{\ell}t^{-\alpha}\leq\mathbb{P}(|\xi|\geq t)\leq C_{u}t^{-\alpha}.

Let
X=(x_{ij})_{1\leq i\leq N,1\leq j\leq n}
be a rectangular random matrix with independent identically distributed (i.i.d.) entries having the distribution
\xi
.
Then for any
D>0
we can find
C_{1},C_{2}>0
and some
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
such that, whenever
N\geq N_{0}
,

(1.2)

\mathbb{P}\left(C_{1}n^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\log n)^{\frac{2(\alpha-2)}{\alpha}}\leq\sigma_{min}(X)\leq C_{2}n^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\log n)^{\frac{\alpha-2}{2\alpha}}\right)\geq 1-n^{-D},

where
C_{1},C_{2}
depend only on
C_{\ell},C_{u},\alpha,\delta,D
, while

N_{0}
may additionally depend on the tail threshold
T_{0}
in
(
1.1
).

The upper bound stated in Theorem
1.5
was established in a recent paper by Bao,
Lee and Xu
[
7
]
. We give a self-contained small-column proof of this bound, using the same truncation idea. The authors asked in
[
7
]
whether a matching lower bound for
\sigma_{min}(X)
can be derived, and in this paper we partially answer this problem by deriving a lower bound which almost matches the upper bound except for a log factor.

The proof of Theorem
1.5
and other main results in this work are based on a surprising interplay between two distinctive powerful techniques: a recent matrix universality principle by Brailovskaya and Van Handel
[
13
]
, and a careful adaptation of an earlier argument by Tikhomirov
[
37
]
. Such a successful combined usage seems completely new: we need to consider both anti-concentration phenomena and the increased variance due to heavy-tail, and negligence on either side would not lead to our almost optimal estimate in (
1.2
).

Recall that a random variable
Z
is said to be
(0,\sigma)

\alpha
-stable for some
\alpha\in(0,2)
and
\sigma>0
, if

\mathbb{E}[e^{itZ}]=\exp(-\sigma^{\alpha}|t|^{\alpha})\text{ for all }t\in\mathbb{R}.

By standard properties of stable distribution, the estimate (
1.1
) is satisfied by all
\alpha
-stable laws for all
\alpha\in(0,2)
.

Another example illustrates which tail exponent governs the scale under
independent random mixing. Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
be symmetric laws with
tails comparable to
t^{-\alpha_{1}}
and
t^{-\alpha_{2}}
, where

0<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<2
. For fixed
p\in(0,1)
, the mixture

p\mu_{1}+(1-p)\mu_{2}
has tail comparable to
t^{-\alpha_{1}}
.
Thus, for i.i.d. entries sampled from this mixture, Theorem

1.5
gives the leading power
n^{1/\alpha_{1}}
for

\sigma_{min}(X)
, up to logarithmic factors. This observation concerns
independent random mixing, not a prescribed arrangement of the two types.

The lower-bound argument also applies to symmetric random variables
with regularly varying tails, after choosing a distribution-dependent
truncation level and normalization. More precisely, we have the following.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\zeta
have a symmetric distribution and suppose that, for some

\alpha\in(0,2)
and some positive slowly varying function
L
,

(1.3)

\mathbb{P}(|\zeta|\geq t)=L(t)t^{-\alpha},\qquad t\geq 1.

Fix
\delta>1
, set
N=\lfloor\delta n\rfloor
, and let

X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times n}
have i.i.d. entries of distribution
\zeta
.
For every
D>0
, there exist a positive slowly varying function
L_{D}^{\prime}

and an integer
n_{0}
, depending on
D,\delta
and the law of
\zeta
,
such that, for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{min}(X)\geq n^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}L_{D}^{\prime}(n)^{-1})\geq 1-n^{-D}.

Remark
.

Theorem
1.5
shows that typically the smallest singular value
\sigma_{min}(X)
is concentrated in a very small window. We believe that the lower bound in (
1.2
) can be improved to
n^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\log n)^{\frac{\alpha-2}{2\alpha}}
, i.e. the upper bound should give the optimal magnitude. Currently the discrepancy of the upper and lower bound by a log factor is due to the technique we use, see Remark
2.6
, (4) for more details. Removing the log factor discrepancy may require considerable effort and innovative ideas, which we leave for future work. Despite this mismatch of log factors, Theorem
1.5
appears to be the first time that we can capture the effect of heavy tails on
\sigma_{min}(X)
that is not existing for Gaussian distribution. Another major advantage is that the proof is robust to perturbations, even without any control on operator norm of perturbation, see Theorem
1.7
. A further open problem is to derive the exact distribution of
\sigma_{min}(X)
, but we feel this problem is far beyond the scope of existing techniques.

While the estimate (
1.2
) holds with polynomially good probability
1-O(n^{-D})
for any
D>0
, it does not hold with exponentially good probability
1-e^{-\Omega(n)}
. Indeed, fix
c\in(0,1)
and put
b_{n}=n^{(1-c)/\alpha}
. By (
1.1
), the event
\mathcal{E}_{n}:=\{\max_{i\in[N]}|x_{i1}|<b_{n}\}
has probability at least
e^{-Kn^{c}}
for some constant
K>0
and all sufficiently large
n
. Conditionally on
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, the expected squared norm of the first column is
O(nb_{n}^{2-\alpha})=o(n^{2/\alpha}(\log n)^{4(\alpha-2)/\alpha})
. Markov’s inequality therefore shows that this column norm is below the lower threshold in (
1.2
) with conditional probability tending to one. Since
\sigma_{min}(X)\leq\|X_{\cdot 1}\|_{2}
, the failure probability is at least
(1-o(1))e^{-Kn^{c}}
, ruling out an exponentially small failure probability. For dense random matrices with uniformly anti-concentrated entry distribution, we often have least singular value bounds with exponentially good probability, see
[
25
]
,
[
33
]
,
[
27
]
. For sparse random matrices, small singular value estimates usually hold with probability
1-e^{-\Omega(np)}
where

is the sparsity, see
[
9
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
17
]
. This is no surprise given that heavy-tail random matrix ensembles share some common features with sparse matrix ensembles.

1.2.
Diverging aspect ratio and general perturbations

The lower bound in Theorem
1.5

is valid in a much wider generality, as it continues to hold when the random variables are no longer i.i.d. and the matrix is perturbed by some other deterministic matrix
B
. It is also important to consider unbounded aspect ratio
N/n
, which we discuss here.

Lower bounds for rectangular random matrices in the sub-Gaussian setting were obtained by Rudelson and Vershynin
[
33
]
, while Tikhomirov obtained estimates uniform over arbitrary deterministic perturbations under only anti-concentration assumptions
[
37
]
. Thus, the point of Theorem
1.7
is not the presence of

alone, but the heavy-tail scale together with the inhomogeneous coefficients and the possibly unbounded aspect ratio.

In the next theorem, we (1)consider possibly unbounded aspect ratio

; (2)remove the i.i.d. assumption and (3)consider a general perturbation

.

.

Fix

\delta>1

1.1

A_{1}\leq a_{ij}\leq A_{2}\quad\forall i=1,\cdots,N,j=1,\cdots,n.

Let

be an
N\times n

and let

be any deterministic

such that,

D,\delta,\alpha,C_{\ell},C_{u},A_{1},A_{2}

In Theorem

An upper bound for

(Upper bound, diverging

) Fix
\alpha\in(0,2)

Although Proposition

2.
Proof outline of main results

To study the heavy-tail random matrix
X
, an (by now standard, see
[
3
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
28
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
22
]
) approach is to resample the entries of
X
. Specifically, we first assign a label taking value in
\{0,1\}
to each entry, where a label 1 is assigned to site
(i,j)
meaning that
x_{ij}
takes a small value, and value 0 assigned to
(i,j)
meaning that
x_{ij}
takes a large value. Then we sample the small and large entry parts of
X
separately via conditional laws of
x_{ij}
conditioned to be small or large. Conditioned on the label, the entries of
X
remain independent.

We outline this resampling procedure for

The diverging aspect ratio case is dealt in Section

Definition 2.1
.

The sampling procedure (for bounded aspect ratio) has the following steps:

(1)

Fix

(2)

Consider

(2.1)

\psi_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,\quad\text{with probability }\mathbb{P}(|x_{ij}|<n^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}}),\\
0,\quad\text{with probability }\mathbb{P}(|x_{ij}|\geq n^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}}).\end{cases}

(3)

Define two classes of random variables

and

and

\mathbb{P}(y_{ij}\in\tilde{I})=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(x_{ij}\in\tilde{I}\cap(-n^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}},n^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}})\right)}{\mathbb{P}(x_{ij}\in(-n^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}},n^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}}))},

(2.3)

\mathbb{P}(z_{ij}\in\tilde{I})=\frac{\mathbb{P}(x_{ij}\in\tilde{I}\cap((-\infty,-n^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}}]\cup[n^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}},\infty)))}{\mathbb{P}(x_{ij}\in(-\infty,-n^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}}]\cup[n^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}},\infty))}.

(4)

It is not hard to check that the matrix

has the same distribution as

X\overset{\operatorname{law}}{=}{\Psi}\circ Y+(\mathbf{1}-{\Psi})\circ Z,

where

and

Theorem

is the bounded-aspect-ratio consequence of the

.
The same lemma also yields Proposition

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
N\geq n\geq 2
, and let
X
have i.i.d. real entries satisfying

\mathbb{P}(|x_{ij}|\geq t)\leq C_{u}t^{-\alpha}
for
t\geq T_{0}
,
where
\alpha\in(0,2)
. For every
\mathbf{b}\in(0,1)
, there exist

C_{\rm up}>0
and
N_{\rm up}\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for

N\geq N_{\rm up}
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left\{\sigma_{min}(X)\geq C_{\rm up}N^{1/\alpha}(\log n)^{(\alpha-2)/(2\alpha)}\right\}\leq e^{-n^{\mathbf{b}}}.

Here
C_{\rm up}
depends only on
\alpha,C_{u},\mathbf{b}
, and

N_{\rm up}
depends additionally on
T_{0}
. Symmetry is not required.

Proof.

Write
R=|x_{11}|
. Integrating the upper-tail bound and absorbing

T_{0}^{2}
into
B^{2-\alpha}
gives, for
B\geq b_{0}(\alpha,C_{u},T_{0})
,

p:=\mathbb{P}(R\geq B)\leq\tfrac{1}{2},\qquad\mathbb{E}[R^{2}\mid R<B]\leq K_{0}B^{2-\alpha},\quad K_{0}:=2+\frac{4C_{u}}{2-\alpha}.

Set

\kappa:=\frac{1-\mathbf{b}}{2},\qquad\eta:=1-2^{-\kappa},\qquad B:=\left(\frac{2C_{u}N}{\kappa\log n}\right)^{1/\alpha}.

For sufficiently large
N
, the preceding bounds hold uniformly over

2\leq n\leq N
, since

B^{\alpha}\geq 2C_{u}N/(\kappa\log N)\to\infty
.
For
\mathcal{E}_{j}:=\{|x_{ij}|<B\text{ for every }i\in[N]\}
,

\log(1-p)\geq-2p
gives

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{j})=(1-p)^{N}\geq e^{-2Np}\geq n^{-\kappa}.

Set
L:=\sqrt{K_{0}/\eta}\,\sqrt{N}\,B^{1-\alpha/2}
.
Conditional Markov’s inequality yields

\mathbb{P}\{\|X_{\cdot j}\|_{2}<L\}\geq(1-\eta)\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{j})\geq 2^{-\kappa}n^{-\kappa}.

The columns are independent and

\sigma_{min}(X)\leq\min_{j}\|X_{\cdot j}\|_{2}
, so

\mathbb{P}\{\sigma_{min}(X)\geq L\}\leq\exp(-2^{-\kappa}n^{1-\kappa})\leq\exp(-n^{\mathbf{b}}),

because
1-\kappa-\mathbf{b}=\kappa
and
n\geq 2
.
Substituting
B
into
L
gives the claimed threshold with

C_{\rm up}
depending only on
\alpha,C_{u},\mathbf{b}
.
∎

For bounded
N/n
, the truncation level in this proof can be written

B=n^{1/\alpha-\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}}
, where

(2.5)

n^{\alpha\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}}=\frac{\kappa n\log n}{2C_{u}N}\asymp\log n.

2.3.
The lower bound

For the lower bound, we use the same decomposition

X=\Psi\circ Y+(\mathbf{1}-\Psi)\circ Z
, but choose the truncation
level differently. Put
R=|x_{ij}|
and
\bar{F}(t)=\mathbb{P}(R\geq t)
.
For every
b>0
, the exact truncated-moment identity is

(2.6)

\mathbb{E}[R^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{R<b\}}]=2\int_{0}^{b}t\bar{F}(t)\,dt-b^{2}\bar{F}(b).

This identity remains valid when the distribution has an atom at
b
.
For the upper bound, (
1.1
) gives, for
b\geq T_{0}
,

\mathbb{E}[R^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{R<b\}}]\leq T_{0}^{2}+\frac{2C_{u}}{2-\alpha}b^{2-\alpha}.

For a positive lower bound, set

\theta=(C_{\ell}/(2C_{u}))^{1/\alpha}\in(0,1)
.
Whenever
b\geq T_{0}/\theta
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[R^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{R<b\}}]

\displaystyle\geq\theta^{2}b^{2}\mathbb{P}(\theta b\leq R<b)

\displaystyle\geq\theta^{2}b^{2}(C_{\ell}\theta^{-\alpha}-C_{u})b^{-\alpha}=\theta^{2}C_{u}b^{2-\alpha}.

More explicitly, set

B_{0}:=\max\left\{1,\frac{T_{0}}{\theta},(2C_{u})^{1/\alpha},T_{0}^{2/(2-\alpha)}\right\},\qquad c_{0}:=\theta^{2}C_{u},\qquad C_{0}:=2+\frac{4C_{u}}{2-\alpha}.

For
b\geq B_{0}
, we have
\bar{F}(b)\leq 1/2
and

T_{0}^{2}\leq b^{2-\alpha}
. Division by

\mathbb{P}(R<b)=1-\bar{F}(b)
therefore gives

c_{0}b^{2-\alpha}\leq\mathbb{E}[R^{2}\mid R<b]\leq C_{0}b^{2-\alpha}.

The constants
c_{0},C_{0}
depend only on
\alpha,C_{\ell},C_{u}
, whereas
the starting point
B_{0}
also depends on
T_{0}
.
Apply this with
b=n^{1/\alpha-\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}}
, where

\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}=o(1)
. Symmetry gives
\mathbb{E}y_{ij}=0
, and
the normalization yields

\mathbb{E}[|n^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}+(1-\frac{\alpha}{2})\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}}y_{ij}|^{2}]=C_{n}\cdot n^{-1}

with
c_{0}\leq C_{n}\leq C_{0}
for all sufficiently large
n
.
No convergence of
C_{n}
is asserted under the two-sided tail bounds.
We summarize these facts as follows.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\xi_{n}
have the distribution of

n^{-1/\alpha+(1-\alpha/2)\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}}y_{ij}
. Then, for all
sufficiently large
n
,

\mathbb{E}\xi_{n}=0,\qquad\mathbb{E}|\xi_{n}|^{2}=C_{n}/n,\quad c_{0}\leq C_{n}\leq C_{0},\qquad|\xi_{n}|\leq n^{-\alpha\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}/2}\quad\text{a.s.}

Instead of choosing
\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}
as in (
2.5
), we will choose
\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}
in the proof of lower bound as

(2.7)

n^{\alpha\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}}=\mathbf{c}(\log n)^{4}

where
\mathbf{c}>0
will later be set to be a sufficiently large constant. This change is made for a purely technical purpose, so that we can use Theorem
2.5
. See Remark
2.6
, (4) for more discussions on the optimality of
\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}
.

We let
\mathcal{F}_{\Psi}
be a sigma subalgebra of the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
spanned by all the random variables
\psi_{ij}
and the events
\{\mathbf{\psi}=W\}
for any
W\in\{0,1\}^{N\times n}
. Then conditioning on the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{\Psi}
, the random variables
y_{ij}
and
z_{ij}
are mutually independent.

Now we outline a heuristic argument to prove Theorem
1.5
.
For any fixed choice of label
{\Psi}
, we first sample the entries
Z=(z_{ij})
, and we show that no matter what value
z_{ij}
takes, we can always use the randomness of
y_{ij}
to derive high probability lower bounds for
\sigma_{min}(X)
that are independent of
Z
.

Via Lemma
2.4
, the additional factor
C_{n}^{-1/2}
makes
the truncated entry variance equal to
n^{-1}
. A naive application
of the classical Bai–Yin theorem (Theorem
1.1
) would
therefore suggest that

\sigma_{min}(C_{n}^{-1/2}n^{-1/\alpha+(1-\alpha/2)\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}}X)

is close to
\sqrt{\delta}-1
.

This heuristic argument has several flaws. First, even the unit-variance variables
\eta_{n}:=\sqrt{n/C_{n}}\,\xi_{n}
are not uniformly anti-concentrated. Since
\alpha<2
,
\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}=o(1)
and
C_{n}\geq c_{0}>0
, the multiplier relating
\eta_{n}
to
y_{ij}
tends to zero, while the conditional laws of
y_{ij}
are tight. Thus
\eta_{n}\to 0
in probability, so
\mathbb{P}(|\eta_{n}|\leq r)\to 1
for every fixed
r>0
. Theorem
1.3
therefore cannot directly give a uniform lower bound at this normalization. Second, by our sampling procedure there are still
\gg n
entries
(i,j)
of
X
where
\psi_{ij}=0
and thus the randomness at site
(i,j)
cannot be used directly. With high possibility each row has such entries, so we have to deal with
X
with some randomness removed. Third, we have very weak control on the operator norm of the conditionally non-random part
\|(\mathbf{1}-\Psi)\circ Z\|
containing the large entries, and we need a method to lower bound the smallest singular value for a rectangular matrix with normalized entries perturbed by a large deterministic matrix. This is very similar to the setting of
[
37
]
.

2.4.
A two-moment replacement principle for minimal singular value

We now address the first caveat raised in the last section. After fixing the label and the large-entry part, we compare the normalized matrix with a Gaussian matrix having the same entry variances. In terms of the unit-variance variables
\eta_{n}=\sqrt{n/C_{n}}\,\xi_{n}
, the replacement is by a standard real Gaussian
\gamma
; equivalently,
\xi_{n}
is replaced by
\sqrt{C_{n}/n}\,\gamma
.

The next theorem is essentially a corollary of
[
13
]
, Theorem 2.6 and 3.16.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
N\geq n
.
Let
T
be an
N\times n
matrix with decomposition
T=T_{0}+T_{1}
, where
T_{0}
is deterministic,
T_{1}
has independent, mean zero coordinates
(t_{ij})
such that
\sup_{i,j}\mathbb{E}[|t_{ij}|^{2}]\leq\frac{M^{2}}{n}
for some fixed
M>1
and let
q>0
be such that

\sup_{i,j}|t_{ij}|\leq q,\quad a.s.

Let
G=G_{0}+G_{1}
be another
N\times n
matrix where
G_{0}=T_{0}
,
G_{1}=(g_{ij})
has independent mean zero Gaussian entries and share the same variance profile as
T_{1}
i.e.
\mathbb{E}[g_{ij}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[t_{ij}^{2}]
for each
i,j
. Then on a common probability space supporting an independent copy of
G
and
T
, we have: for any
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}(|\sigma_{min}(T)-\sigma_{min}(G)|\geq Cn^{-\frac{1}{2}}t^{\frac{1}{2}}+Cq^{\frac{1}{3}}t^{\frac{2}{3}}(\frac{N}{n})^{\frac{1}{3}}+Cqt)\leq 8Ne^{-t},

where
C>0
is a universal constant that only depends on
M>0
.

When the aspect ratio
N/n
is bounded, we take
t=(D+2)\log N\asymp\log n
so that the failure probability is polynomially small. To make the comparison error smaller than the fixed positive lower bound for
\sigma_{min}(G)
obtained below, it suffices to take
q\leq c(\log n)^{-2}
with
c>0
sufficiently small: this controls
q^{1/3}t^{2/3}(N/n)^{1/3}
, while the other two terms vanish as
n\to\infty
. This is the reason we set (
2.7
). The proof of Theorem
2.5
is deferred to Section
3.5
. When the aspect ratio
N/n
is diverging, we will set the magnitude of
q
differently, see Section
2.7
.

Remark 2.6
.

We give several important remarks before applying this theorem.

(1)

Theorem
2.5
does not require that entries of
T_{1}
, or any of its normalized version, are uniformly anti-concentrated.

(2)

All parameters in Theorem
2.5
depend only on the random part
T_{1}
and do not depend on the deterministic part
T_{0}
. This is very important for our application.

(3)

We can also compare
T
to its free probability version
T_{\text{free}}
and compute
\sigma_{min}(T_{\text{free}})
using Lehner’s formula
[
24
]
. This innovative approach is proposed and developed carefully in
[
6
]
. However, we have almost no control over the very large deterministic part
T_{0}
, and some randomness is removed from the random part
T_{1}
. This means that the computation of
\sigma_{min}(T_{\text{free}})
will be very difficult. Instead, we take the other approach by lower bounding
\sigma_{min}(G)
, which draws us on the techniques in the subfield of quantitative invertibility of random matrices (see review
[
39
]
) and in particular in a very similar situation as
[
37
]
.

(4)

The choice of
\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}
in (
2.7
) is made only for Theorem
2.5
to produce meaningful quantitative estimates. This choice of
\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}
is the source of the mismatch in Theorem
1.5
: the lower bound is smaller than the upper bound by a factor of
(\log n)^{\frac{3(2-\alpha)}{2\alpha}}
. We expect the choice
n^{\alpha\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}}\asymp\log n
, equivalently
q\asymp(\log n)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, as in (
2.5
), to be optimal for proving a similar comparison theorem to Theorem
2.5
. Currently, there exists another method to bound the least singular value of a sparse rectangular matrix, which is
[
17
]
. The work
[
17
]
remarkably achieves the optimal sparsity scale for a Bai-Yin type result to hold, but the proof requires the entries to have zero mean. Informally, the method of Theorem
2.5

works only for inhomogeneous sparse matrix models with sparsity (suitably defined) at least
(\log n)^{4}/n
,
while for the homogeneous case we can go down to sparsity
(\log n)/n
. In our setting, considering a large chunk of fixed non-zero entries is fundamentally important, and it is unclear how to generalize the proof of
[
17
]
to a matrix with arbitrary mean, so we do not take this approach.

2.5.
When anti-concentration takes in

Having Theorem
2.5
in hand, the next step is to lower bound
\sigma_{min}(G)
for a rectangular random matrix
G
with Gaussian entries. As some randomness of
G
is removed, the most direct proof does not work. Also, it appears that assuming a Gaussian entry does not simplify much the proof, so we make a more general statement for independent sub-Gaussian entries with uniform anti-concentration.

Recall that a real random variable
\xi
is said to be
K
-sub-Gaussian for some
K>0
if

\mathbb{E}[\exp(|\xi|^{2}/K^{2})]\leq 2.

Definition 2.7
.

For a given label
\Psi=(\psi_{ij})\in\{0,1\}^{N\times n}

\Psi

The matrix
G

G_{0}
and the label
{\Psi}

to simplify the notations and keep the dependence on

{\Psi}

has a zero column, then
\sigma_{min}(G)=0

\Psi

For any deterministic label

When

\Psi=W
.

3.5.
Proof of universality results

This section contains the proof of Theorem
2.5
. We begin with some notations.

The Hausdorff distance of two subsets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}
is defined via

d_{H}(A,B):=\inf\{\epsilon>0:A\subset B+[-\epsilon,\epsilon]\text{ and }B\subset A+[-\epsilon,\epsilon]\}.

For any bounded operator
X
on a Hilbert space we use the notation
\operatorname{sp}(X)
to denote the spectrum of
X
.

We will use the following spectrum universality result from
[
13
]
, Theorem 2.6.

The comparison replaces the centered random matrix by a Gaussian matrix with the same covariance structure. Its proof uses a cumulant expansion together with high-order trace and resolvent estimates; the resulting errors are controlled by
\sigma_{*}(X)
,
\sigma(X)
, and
R(X)
. Concentration then converts these estimates into high-probability Hausdorff control of the spectra. After self-adjoint linearization, this spectral comparison yields a comparison of the smallest singular values.

Let
Z_{0}
be an
d\times d
self-adjoint deterministic matrix, and let
Z_{1},\cdots,Z_{n}

be independent
d\times d
self-adjoint random matrices with
\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]=0
. Consider

(3.8)

X:=Z_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i},

and let
\operatorname{Cov}(X)
be the
d^{2}\times d^{2}
covariance matrix such that

(3.9)

\operatorname{Cov}(X)_{ij,kl}:=\mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}X)_{ij}\overline{(X-\mathbb{E}X)}_{kl}].

Let
G
be a
d\times d
self-adjoint random matrix where
\{\Re G_{ij},\Im G_{ij}:i,j\in[d]\}
are jointly Gaussian, satisfying that
\mathbb{E}[G]=\mathbb{E}[X]
and
\operatorname{Cov}(G)=\operatorname{Cov}(X)
. Then
G
can be expressed as

G=Z_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}g_{i}

for deterministic matrices
A_{1},\cdots,A_{N}\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C})_{sa}
and for i.i.d. real Gaussians
g_{1},\cdots,g_{N}
.

Following
[
13
]
we introduce matrix parameters

\sigma(X):=\|\mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}X)^{2}]\|^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\sigma_{*}(X):=\sup_{\|v\|_{2}=\|w\|_{2}=1}\mathbb{E}[|\langle v,(X-\mathbb{E}X)w\rangle|^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}},

R(X):=\left\|\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|Z_{i}\|\right\|_{\infty}.

Theorem 3.14
.

[
[
13
]
] There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that for any
t>0
,

(3.10)

\mathbb{P}[d_{H}(\operatorname{sp}(X),\operatorname{sp}(G))>C\epsilon(t)]\leq de^{-t},

where

\epsilon(t)=\sigma_{*}(X)t^{\frac{1}{2}}+R(X)^{\frac{1}{3}}\sigma(X)^{\frac{2}{3}}t^{\frac{2}{3}}+R(X)t.

The following lemma (for deterministic matrices) is similar to
[
6
]
, Lemma 3.13 and has a similar proof.

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
N\geq n
and let
H,G\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times n}
be deterministic.
For
\epsilon>0
, set
A_{\epsilon}:=4\epsilon^{2}I_{n}
, where
I_{n}
is
the
n
-dimensional identity matrix, and define

\widetilde{G}_{\epsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&G^{*}&A_{\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
0&0&0&0\\
G&0&0&0\\
A_{\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}&0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\widetilde{H}_{\epsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&H^{*}&A_{\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
0&0&0&0\\
H&0&0&0\\
A_{\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

The four block sizes are
n,n,N,n
, so these are self-adjoint matrices
of dimension
N+3n\leq 4N
.
Then
\operatorname{sp}(\widetilde{H}_{\epsilon})\subseteq\operatorname{sp}(\widetilde{G}_{\epsilon})+[-\epsilon,\epsilon]

implies that

\begin{cases}\lambda_{+}(H^{*}H+A_{\epsilon})^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\lambda_{+}(G^{*}G+A_{\epsilon})^{\frac{1}{2}}+\epsilon,\\
\lambda_{-}(H^{*}H+A_{\epsilon})^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq\lambda_{-}(G^{*}G+A_{\epsilon})^{\frac{1}{2}}-\epsilon.\\
\end{cases}

The same implication holds after interchanging
G
and
H
in both
the hypothesis and the conclusion. We write

\lambda_{+}(Z):=\sup\operatorname{sp}(Z)
and

\lambda_{-}(Z):=\inf\operatorname{sp}(Z)
for self-adjoint
Z
.

Furthermore,
\operatorname{sp}(\widetilde{H}_{\epsilon})\subseteq\operatorname{sp}(\widetilde{G}_{\epsilon})+[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
implies that

\sigma_{min}(H)\geq\sigma_{min}(G)-3\epsilon.

Proof.

The nonzero eigenvalues of
\widetilde{G}_{\epsilon}
are the positive
and negative singular values of the matrix

(G^{*}\ \ A_{\epsilon}^{1/2})
. Consequently,

\operatorname{sp}(\widetilde{G}_{\epsilon})=\operatorname{sp}((G^{*}G+A_{\epsilon})^{\frac{1}{2}})\cup-\operatorname{sp}((G^{*}G+A_{\epsilon})^{\frac{1}{2}})\cup\{0\},

and the same holds for
\widetilde{H}_{\epsilon}
.
The assumed spectral inclusion immediately gives

\lambda_{+}(H^{*}H+A_{\epsilon})^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\lambda_{+}(G^{*}G+A_{\epsilon})^{\frac{1}{2}}+\epsilon.

Every positive eigenvalue of either linearization is at least

2\epsilon
. In particular, the smallest positive eigenvalue of

\widetilde{H}_{\epsilon}
cannot lie within distance
\epsilon
of zero
or of a negative eigenvalue of
\widetilde{G}_{\epsilon}
. Its matching
eigenvalue must therefore be positive, which gives

\lambda_{-}(H^{*}H+A_{\epsilon})^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq\lambda_{-}(G^{*}G+A_{\epsilon})^{\frac{1}{2}}-\epsilon.

Finally,

\sigma_{min}(H)\leq\lambda_{-}(H^{*}H+A_{\epsilon})^{\frac{1}{2}}=\sqrt{\sigma_{min}(H)^{2}+4\epsilon^{2}}\leq\sigma_{min}(H)+2\epsilon.

Combining this with the analogous inequalities for
G
proves

\sigma_{min}(H)\geq\sigma_{min}(G)-3\epsilon.

∎

Let
T
and
G
be as in Theorem
2.5
, and form their
linearizations as above. The centered random part has
T_{1}^{*}
in block

(1,3)
and
T_{1}
in block
(3,1)
; all other blocks are zero.
The two nonzero diagonal blocks of its expected square are

\mathbb{E}(T_{1}^{*}T_{1})
and
\mathbb{E}(T_{1}T_{1}^{*})
.
Their norms are bounded by
M^{2}N/n
and
M^{2}
, respectively.
Independence of the entries and Cauchy–Schwarz bound the variance of
every unit-vector bilinear form by
M^{2}/n
. Moreover, each
self-adjoint increment corresponding to an entry

has norm

|t_{ij}|\leq q
. Thus, for every
\epsilon>0
,

\sigma(\widetilde{T}_{\epsilon})\leq M\sqrt{\frac{N}{n}},\quad\sigma_{*}(\widetilde{T}_{\epsilon})\leq\frac{M}{\sqrt{n}},\quad R(\widetilde{T}_{\epsilon})\leq q.

We have collected necessary ingredients for proving Theorem
2.5
.

Proof of Theorem
2.5
.

We define random matrices
\widetilde{T}_{\epsilon(t)},

\widetilde{G}_{\epsilon(t)}
as in the statement of Lemma (
3.15
), where we replace
\epsilon
by

where the constant

Since
