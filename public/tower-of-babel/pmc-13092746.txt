Identification of MYC co-expression gene: POLR3G is associated with cell senescence, immunotherapy, chemotherapy responses, and clinical prognosis in bladder cancer patients
Authors: Li Zhu, Yang Liu, Jianjun Lan, Erdong Cai, Dongmei Sun, Jibin Liu, Li Zong, Zhirong Cong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•POLR3G is a key MYC-co-expressed gene and independent prognostic biomarker in BLCA.•A five-gene MYC network signature stratifies BLCA risk and predicts therapy response.•High POLR3G links to immunosuppression and resistance to chemo/immunotherapy in BLCA.•POLR3G may regulate bladder cancer cell senescence via p53 signaling.Keywords: MYC, POLR3G, BLCA, Biomarker, Immunotherapy

Introduction

In China, bladder cancer (BLCA) ranks as the most common malignancy within the genitourinary system [1]. According to the extent of invasion through the bladder wall, BLCA is divided into non-muscle-invasive (NMIBC) and muscle-invasive (MIBC) subtypes [2]. Although therapeutic strategies, including surgical intervention and chemotherapy, have evolved and contributed to better patient outcomes [3], a considerable number of individuals still suffer from disease recurrence and metastasis after initial treatment [4]. Patients with advanced BLCA face a highly aggressive disease course and poor survival rates [5]. Therefore, identifying robust biomarkers is clinically essential to accurately distinguish high-risk cases and to facilitate personalized treatment planning.

The MYC gene is widely recognized for its critical functions in various tumors, including BLCA. As a central oncogene, its elevated expression is strongly linked to the initiation and advancement of numerous malignancies. In the context of BLCA, MYC drives tumor growth and dissemination through diverse biological processes. MYC’s influence in BLCA involves complex crosstalk with other molecular pathways. For instance, research has revealed that the long non-coding RNA GClnc1 enhances malignant behaviors of BLCA cells by modulating the LIN28B/let-7a/MYC axis [6]. Moreover, POLD1 contributes to MYC protein stabilization, accelerating cancer proliferation and metastasis, and establishes a POLD1-MYC feedback circuit that amplifies oncogenic outcomes [7]. These findings illustrate the multifaceted regulatory network orchestrated by MYC. Beyond its cell-autonomous functions, MYC also affects the tumor microenvironment. It contributes to immune evasion, angiogenesis, and migratory processes—all of which support local invasion and distant spread [8]. MYC also engages in a positive feedback circuit with FGFR3, collectively accelerating bladder carcinogenesis [9]. This underscores the broad involvement of MYC across different stages of disease progression. Elevated MYC expression is further correlated with chemoresistance in BLCA. For example, DLGAP5 stabilizes the MYC protein, increasing resistance to gemcitabine [10]. Similarly, high MYC levels are linked to cisplatin resistance, indicating its potential role as a therapeutic target to reverse treatment failure [11]. In summary, MYC contributes to BLCA pathogenesis by regulating tumor development, metastatic spread, and treatment resistance through multiple signaling axes. Given its central role, targeting MYC may open new avenues for improving BLCA therapy.

Bioinformatics plays a pivotal role in the identification of modern tumor biomarkers. By integrating various bioinformatics tools and databases, researchers can identify potential biomarkers from vast genomic and transcriptomic datasets [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. These biomarkers not only facilitate early cancer diagnosis but also provide a foundation for personalized treatment. This study investigates the MYC gene, with a specific emphasis on the function of its key downstream targets within the immune microenvironment of BLCA. We have identified POLR3G as the most significant MYC-related gene in this malignancy. Our findings demonstrate that POLR3G expression is linked to patient outcomes and may represent a novel target for immunotherapeutic strategies in BLCA.

Materials and methods

Datasets and patient samples

This study utilized clinical information from BLCA patients in the TCGA database [16]. The TCGA-BLCA dataset comprises 406 BLCA samples and 19 normal bladder tissue samples. Additionally, we collected 4 pairs of BLCA tissue and adjacent normal tissue from patients at Nantong Tumor Hospital.

Immunohistochemical analysis of POLR3G in BLCA tissue

BLCA tissues were processed following a standardized immunohistochemical protocol. Briefly, sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Heat-induced antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer in an autoclave. After cooling and rinsing with PBS, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubation with hydrogen peroxide. The sections were then incubated with a primary antibody against POLR3G for 2 h at room temperature. Following washes with PBS, a secondary antibody was applied for 20 min. After additional washes, immunoreactivity was visualized using DAB, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Finally, the sections were dehydrated through graded alcohols, cleared in xylene, and mounted. BLCA.

Gene enrichment analysis

Differential mRNA expression analysis was performed using the Limma package in R (v3.40.2). Transcripts with an adjusted P-value 〈 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change 〉 2 were considered statistically significant. To explore the potential oncogenic functions of POLR3G, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses using the ClusterProfiler package [[17], [18], [19]]. Additionally, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was employed to identify significantly enriched biological pathways [20].

Immune infiltration analysis

Immune cell infiltration levels were assessed using the immunedeconv R package, which integrates multiple deconvolution algorithms [21]. Among these, the XCELL method was selected for its ability to evaluate a broad spectrum of immune cell types [22]. To further identify immune subsets with prognostic value, we applied the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) algorithm. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, 2020).

Consensus clustering analysis

Consensus clustering was applied to the TCGA BLCA dataset, integrating clinical and RNA-seq data, to identify robust molecular subtypes [23,24]. This analysis was performed using the ConsensusClusterPlus R package (v1.54.0). We configured the algorithm to evaluate up to six clusters (k = 6), with 100 resampling iterations wherein 80% of the samples were drawn each time. The clustering was executed using the hierarchical method (clusterAlg = "hc") with 'ward.D2′ as the internal linkage. The resulting consensus matrix and sample clusters were visualized in a heatmap generated with the pheatmap package (v1.0.12). To assess the prognostic relevance of the identified subtypes, Kaplan-Meier survival curves were plotted for each cluster, and between-group differences in survival were compared using the log-rank test. All analyses were conducted in R (v4.0.3).

Statistical analysis

The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to compare POLR3G expression levels between BLCA and normal tissue samples. Patient survival outcomes were assessed using the log-rank test. A two-sided p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Identification of MYC co-expressed genes

We utilized the LinkedOmics platform to analyze the TCGA BLCA (BLCA) dataset and identify genes co-expressed with MYC. Fig. 1A presents a volcano plot of all co-expressed genes. Subsequently, we employed the STRING database to analyze protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks for the top 50 genes positively and negatively correlated with MYC (Fig. 1B–C). The PPI analysis revealed a greater number of interactions among MYC-positive correlated genes compared to the negatively correlated set. Heatmaps further illustrated the co-expression patterns of these top 50 positive and negative genes (Fig. 1D–E). Functional enrichment analysis indicated that these genes are involved in biological processes such as metabolic processes, response to stimulus, and localization. They were also associated with cellular components including membrane, nucleus, membrane-enclosed lumen, and protein-containing complex, as well as molecular functions like protein binding, ion binding, and nucleic acid binding (Fig. 1F).

(D) Heatmap of the top 50 positively correlated genes. (E) Heatmap of the top 50 negatively correlated genes. (F) Functional enrichment of MYC co-expressed genes.

Subtyping of BLCA based on MYC co-expressed genes

To investigate the role of MYC co-expressed genes in BLCA heterogeneity, we performed consensus clustering. The optimal cluster number (k = 2) was determined by analyzing the proportion of ambiguous clusters (PAC) metric, which was most stable at k = 2 (u1=0.1, u2=0.9) (Fig. 2A). Heatmap results confirmed clear separation between the two clusters (Fig. 2B–C). Survival analysis revealed that patients in Cluster 1 had significantly worse overall and disease-specific survival compared to Cluster 2 (Fig. 2D–E). KEGG pathway analysis of differentially expressed genes between clusters showed that genes upregulated in Cluster 1 were significantly enriched in NF-kappa B signaling, TNF signaling, IL-17 signaling, and JAK-STAT signaling pathways. In contrast, Cluster 2 was characterized by upregulation of the PPAR signaling pathway (Fig. 2F–G).

(D) Differences in overall survival between clusters. (E) Differences in disease-specific survival between clusters. (F-G) KEGG enrichment analysis for Cluster 1 (F) and Cluster 2 (G).

Association of MYC co-expression signature with immunotherapy and chemotherapy

We explored the immune landscape of the two clusters using the xCell algorithm. Fig. 3A displays the differential infiltration levels of various immune cells between clusters. The abundance of tumor-infiltrating immune cells for each sample is shown in Fig. 3B Furthermore, significant differences were observed in the expression of all immune checkpoint genes between the two clusters (Fig. 3C). Subsequent analysis using the TIDE algorithm predicted that patients in Cluster 1 would exhibit significantly poorer response to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy (Fig. 3D), potentially explaining their inferior survival. Finally, we found significant differences in the estimated IC50 values for common BLCA chemotherapeutic agents between the clusters (Fig. 3E).

Construction of a prognostic model

We identified 17 MYC co-expressed genes that were differentially expressed between tumor and normal tissues and also prognosis-related (Fig. 4A). Among these, 7 were protective factors and 10 were risk factors for BLCA prognosis (Fig. 4B). The LASSO regression algorithm was applied to these 17 genes, leading to a final prognostic model incorporating five genes: ETF1, MTHFD1L, TPD52L1, POLR3G, and ZSCAN16 (Fig. 4C–D). A risk plot based on this model is shown in Fig. 4E. Patients classified into the high-risk group based on the model signature had significantly poorer prognosis compared to the low-risk group (Fig. 4F). The time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves demonstrated good predictive accuracy for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival (Fig. 4G).

Construction of the prognostic model. (A) Venn diagram of prognostic MYC co-expressed genes. (B) Forest plot of the 17 prognostic genes. (C-D) LASSO coefficient profiles and selection process. (E) Risk plot of the five-gene signature. (F) Kaplan-Meier survival analysis between risk groups. (G) Time-dependent ROC curves.

Correlation of the prognostic signature with therapy

The risk score derived from our model showed a positive correlation with the infiltration levels of most immune cells, as assessed by both xCell and TIP algorithms (Fig. 5A). Consistent with this, expression of most immune checkpoint genes differed significantly between the risk groups (Fig. 5B). TIDE analysis further predicted that high-risk patients would respond less favorably to ICB therapy (Fig. 5C). Finally, we observed significant differences in IC50 values for most chemotherapeutic drugs between the risk groups, with the exception of rapamycin (Fig. 5D).

Association of the risk score with therapy. (A) Correlation between risk score and immune cell infiltration. (B) Differential expression of immune checkpoint genes between risk groups. (C) Predicted response to ICB therapy between risk groups. (D) Differences in chemotherapeutic drug IC50 scores between risk groups.

POLR3G is a key prognostic gene among MYC co-expressed genes

We evaluated the five model genes (ETF1, MTHFD1L, TPD52L1, POLR3G, ZSCAN16) across disease stages and grades. Expression of POLR3G, MTHFD1L, and TPD52L1 was significantly higher in advanced-stage tumors, while ZSCAN16 was higher in early-stage tumors (Fig. 6A). In high-grade tumors, ETF1, MTHFD1L, TPD52L1, and POLR3G expression was significantly elevated compared to low-grade tumors (Fig. 6B). Given their differential expression across stages and grades, MTHFD1L, TPD52L1, and POLR3G were subjected to multivariable Cox regression analysis. All three were independent prognostic factors (Fig. 6C–E). When included together in a multivariable model, POLR3G emerged as the most significant independent prognostic marker among the three (Fig. 6F). Kaplan-Meier analysis confirmed that high POLR3G expression was associated with worse overall and disease-specific survival (Fig. 6G–H), which was further supported by time-dependent ROC curves (Fig. 6I–J).

POLR3G as a prognostic biomarker in BLCA. (A) Expression of the five genes across tumor stages. (B) Expression of the five genes across tumor grades. (C-F) Multivariable Cox regression analyses for MTHFD1L, TPD52L1, POLR3G, and all three combined. (G, I) Kaplan-Meier curves for overall and disease-specific survival based on POLR3G expression. (H, J) Time-dependent ROC curves for POLR3G predicting overall and disease-specific survival.

POLR3G expression correlates with therapy response

POLR3G expression showed a positive correlation with the infiltration of most immune cells (xCell and TIP algorithms, Fig. 7A). Significant differences in immune checkpoint gene expression were observed between POLR3G-high and POLR3G-low groups (Fig. 7B). TIDE analysis predicted that patients with high POLR3G expression would be less responsive to ICB therapy (Fig. 7C). Similarly, the IC50 values for most chemotherapy drugs, except rapamycin, differed significantly between POLR3G expression groups (Fig. 7D).

Association of POLR3G with therapy response. (A) Correlation between POLR3G and immune cell infiltration. (B) Differential immune checkpoint expression between POLR3G-high and -low groups. (C) Predicted ICB response between POLR3G expression groups. (D) Differences in chemotherapeutic drug IC50 scores between POLR3G expression groups.

POLR3G is associated with BLCA cell senescence and MYC interaction

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) in BLCA samples revealed that high POLR3G expression was significantly associated with MYC activity targets, PD-1 signaling, intestinal immune network for IgA production, p53 downstream pathway, DNA damage/telomere stress-induced senescence, and cellular senescence pathways (Fig. 8A–F). Analysis of shared transcriptional regulators identified p53 as a common upstream regulator for both POLR3G and MYC (Fig. 8G). Given its role as a master regulator of senescence, this suggests POLR3G's influence on cellular senescence may involve the p53 signaling pathway. Furthermore, analysis of shared perturbagens indicated that Cisplatin, a first-line chemotherapy drug for BLCA, is a compound potentially co-regulated by POLR3G and MYC (Fig. 8H).

Functional analysis of POLR3G in BLCA. (A-F) Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) for POLR3G. (G) Shared transcriptional regulators of POLR3G and MYC. (H) Shared perturbagens of POLR3G and MYC.

Experimental validation of POLR3G expression in BLCA

We collected 4 pairs of BLCA and matched normal bladder tissues to validate POLR3G expression. The results confirmed that POLR3G protein expression levels were significantly higher in tumor tissues compared to normal adjacent tissues (Fig. 9).

Discussion

The oncogene MYC is a well-established master regulator of tumorigenesis, driving cell proliferation, metabolic reprogramming, and metastasis across a wide spectrum of cancers, including BLCA [[25], [26], [27], [28]]. However, a systematic investigation into the collective role of its co-expressed gene network in BLCA pathophysiology and therapy resistance has been lacking. This study comprehensively delineates the landscape of MYC co-expressed genes in BLCA, revealing their significant implications for tumor subtyping, prognosis, and therapeutic sensitivity. Furthermore, we identified and validated POLR3G, a RNA polymerase III subunit, as a pivotal MYC-associated gene with robust prognostic power and potential involvement in regulating cellular senescence.

Our consensus clustering analysis, based on MYC co-expression profiles, successfully stratified BLCA patients into two distinct subtypes (Cluster 1 and Cluster 2) with markedly different clinical outcomes. The aggressive Cluster 1, characterized by poor survival, exhibited significant enrichment in pro-tumorigenic and inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB, TNF, IL-17, and JAK-STAT signaling [[29], [30], [31], [32]]. This molecular signature is consistent with a hyper-proliferative and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), providing a plausible explanation for the observed therapeutic resistance and unfavorable prognosis in this group. Conversely, the more indolent Cluster 2 was associated with the PPAR signaling pathway, which has been implicated in promoting differentiation and inhibiting inflammation in other cancer contexts. This clear molecular demarcation underscores the utility of MYC co-expression patterns in refining BLCA molecular taxonomy and identifying high-risk patients.

The five-gene prognostic signature (ETF1, MTHFD1L, TPD52L1, POLR3G, ZSCAN16) derived from the MYC network further enhances clinical translatability. The model's effectiveness in risk stratification was corroborated by its strong association with patient survival in the TCGA cohort. The tumor immune microenvironment plays a crucial role in the occurrence, development, and treatment response of tumors. It is not only the "soil" for tumor growth but also a key regulator determining the fate of tumors [33,34]. Notably, the risk score was not only prognostic but also intimately linked to the immune landscape and therapy response. The positive correlation between the risk score and immune infiltration, coupled with the elevated expression of multiple immune checkpoints in the high-risk group, paints a picture of an "immune-hot" but functionally exhausted TME. This is further supported by the TIDE analysis, which predicted poorer responses to ICB in high-risk patients, suggesting that despite the presence of immune cells, the TME is likely suppressive, rendering ICB ineffective. This paradox highlights the complexity of the immune response in BLCA and suggests that our signature could help identify patients who are unlikely to benefit from ICB monotherapy.

Among the model genes, POLR3G emerged as the most potent independent prognostic factor. Its expression increased with tumor stage and grade, and high POLR3G levels consistently predicted shorter overall and disease-specific survival. While traditionally studied for its role in transcription by RNA polymerase III, emerging evidence links POLR3G to MYC-driven oncogenesis. Our GSEA results significantly expand its potential function in BLCA, linking it to MYC targets, PD-1 signaling, and—most intriguingly—cellular senescence pathways and the p53 signaling cascade. The finding that p53 is a shared transcriptional regulator of both POLR3G and MYC offers a compelling mechanistic hypothesis: POLR3G may cooperate with MYC to modulate the p53-dependent senescence barrier, a critical anti-cancer mechanism that cells must bypass for malignant progression. The evasion of senescence is a hallmark of cancer, and its association with POLR3G opens new avenues for understanding BLCA aggressiveness.

The therapeutic implications of our findings are substantial. The consistent differential response to common chemotherapy agents between the risk groups and POLR3G expression levels suggests that our model could guide chemotherapeutic choices. The identification of Cisplatin as a potential shared perturbagen of POLR3G and MYC through perturbagen analysis provides a direct molecular link to a first-line therapy, meriting further experimental validation. Finally, the upregulation of POLR3G in our local BLCA patient samples provides crucial preliminary translational evidence, strengthening the clinical relevance of our bioinformatics discoveries.

A limitation of the present study is the use of bulk transcriptomic data, which does not allow for the definitive attribution of the five-gene signature expression to specific cell populations. While POLR3G’s association with MYC activity and cellular proliferation suggests a predominantly malignant cell origin, the high correlation between our risk score and immune infiltration indicates that these genes may also reflect or influence the state of the infiltrating immune cells. Future single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) validation is warranted to resolve the spatial and cell-type-specific expression of this signature.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study establishes the MYC co-expression network as a critical determinant of BLCA heterogeneity, progression, and treatment response. We developed and validated a practical five-gene prognostic signature that effectively stratifies patients and correlates with immunotherapy and chemotherapy outcomes. The central role of POLR3G within this signature, along with its association with cellular senescence and p53 signaling, positions it as a novel and compelling biomarker and a potential therapeutic target in BLCA. Future research should focus on elucidating the precise molecular mechanisms by which POLR3G influences BLCA cell senescence and drug resistance, both in vitro and in vivo.
