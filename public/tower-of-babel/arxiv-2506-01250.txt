Neural Variance-aware Dueling Bandits with Deep Representation and Shallow Exploration
Youngmin Oh, Jinje Park, Taejin Paik, Jaemin Park

Abstract
In this paper, we address the contextual dueling bandit problem by proposing variance-aware algorithms that leverage neural networks to approximate nonlinear utility functions. Our approach employs a variance-aware exploration strategy , which adaptively accounts for uncertainty in pairwise comparisons while relying only on the gradients with respect to the learnable parameters of the last layer. This design effectively balances the exploration–exploitation tradeoff under both the Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) and Thompson Sampling (TS) frameworks. As a result, under standard assumptions, we establish theoretical guarantees showing that our algorithms achieve sublinear cumulative average regret of order 𝒪 ~ ​ ( d ​ ∑ t = 1 T σ t 2 + d ​ T ) , \widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(d\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t}^{2}}+\sqrt{dT}\right), for sufficiently wide neural networks, where d d is the contextual dimension, σ t 2 \sigma_{t}^{2} the variance of comparisons at round t t , and T T the total number of rounds. We also empirically validate that our approach offers reasonable computational efficiency and achieves sublinear regret on both synthetic tasks with nonlinear utilities and real-world tasks, outperforming existing methods.



1
Introduction

Contextual dueling bandits
[
11
,
31
]
extend the stochastic contextual multi-armed bandit framework by enabling the learner to select pairs of arms (duels) and receive preference feedback, indicating which arm is preferred, rather than a single numeric reward. In each round
t
, the learner obtains contextual information from the environment, selects two arms for comparison, and observes a preference outcome. This outcome follows the Bernoulli distribution with a probability determined by a link function, such as the Bradley–Terry–Luce (BTL) model
[
14
,
23
]
, which characterizes the likelihood of one arm being preferred over the other based on their latent utilities.

Many existing studies on the contextual dueling bandit framework
[
3
,
26
,
25
,
22
,
5
]
assume a linear utility function.
However, the linearity assumption imposes a significant limitation on its ability to model real-world preferences, as real-world user preferences often exhibit complex, nonlinear interactions among features that a simple linear model may fail to capture. To address this limitation,
Verma et al. 2024
recently proposed neural dueling bandit algorithms under the Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) and Thompson Sampling (TS) frameworks. These algorithms employ a neural network to approximate a general nonlinear utility function, demonstrating sublinear cumulative average regret when the network width is sufficiently large. Despite these theoretical advancements, their approach requires constructing a Gram matrix based on the gradients with respect to all learnable parameters of the neural network. This results in substantial computational overhead, posing practical challenges in large-scale applications.

In the multi-armed bandit (MAB) literature, similar computational challenges have been mitigated by adopting shallow exploration strategies
[
5
,
30
]
, which use Gram matrices based only on the gradients of the last layer. Concurrently, variance-aware methods have been actively studied in linear MABs and dueling bandits
[
10
,
37
,
39
,
34
,
35
]
, and recently extended to neural MABs
[
5
]
. However, to the best of our knowledge, variance-aware approaches combined with shallow exploration have not yet been explored in the context of dueling bandits.

Motivated by these gaps, we propose the
Neural Variance-Aware Linear Dueling Bandits
(NVLDB). Following
Xu et al. 2020
,
Bui et al. 2024
, our approach constructs the Gram matrix from the gradients with respect to the parameters in the last layer, thereby improving computational efficiency compared to the method of
Verma et al. 2024
. To incorporate variance-awareness, NVLDB estimates the variance of a Bernoulli distribution using the neural network as a nonlinear utility approximator. Furthermore, NVLDB accommodates various arm selection strategies—including one asymmetric and two symmetric variants—under both the UCB and TS frameworks.

Assuming a sufficiently large neural network width
m
,
we prove that, under standard assumptions, the expected cumulative average regret for each arm selection strategy is bounded by

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(d\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t}^{2}}+\sqrt{dT}\right),

where
d
denotes the contextual dimension,
\sigma_{t}^{2}
represents the variance of the Bernoulli distribution for the selected pair of arms at round
t
, and
T
is the total number of rounds.
Additionally, we establish variance-agnostic variants of NVLDB by demonstrating that its expected cumulative average regret remains sublinear, bounded by
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(d\sqrt{T}\right)
under the same assumptions.

We emphasize that the regret analysis for NVLDB under each arm selection strategy involves significant technical challenges. One of the key difficulties lies in deriving a concentration inequality for
\theta_{t-1}
with respect to
V_{t-1}
, which we formally establish in
Lemma

1
. Unlike in neural linear bandits
[
30
,
5
]
,
\theta_{t-1}
in neural linear dueling bandits lacks a closed-form expression and is further biased due to shallow exploration during representation learning (see, e.g., Lemma A.4 in
[
30
]
). In the neural setting,
these difficulties are individually addressed by
Verma et al. 2024
and
Bui et al. 2024
, with each focusing on only one; neither considers both challenges simultaneously. In our setting, both challenges must be addressed simultaneously to avoid overly loose upper bounds. To overcome this, we introduce a bootstrap argument
[
12
]
on
\|\theta_{t-1}\|_{2}
for iterative refinement, supported by matrix analysis techniques such as the Courant–Fischer theorem
[
13
,
4
]
. Starting from an initial loose upper bound, we iteratively refine it to establish a tighter concentration inequality. To the best of our knowledge, such techniques have not been used in prior work on neural (dueling) bandits. We provide further discussion in Section
5
, and defer full proofs to the supplementary material.

Contributions.

Our contributions are as follows: First, we introduce the first
neural variance-aware linear dueling bandits
(NVLDB), which leverages a Gram matrix constructed solely from the gradients with respect to the final-layer parameters of a neural network, significantly reducing computational overhead compared to prior approaches
[
29
]
. Second, under standard assumptions and assuming a sufficiently wide neural network, we prove that NVLDB achieves sublinear expected cumulative average regret for both variance-aware and variance-agnostic variants, regardless of the arm selection strategy. Finally, empirical evaluations demonstrate that NVLDB exhibits sublinear cumulative average regret and outperforms existing methods on both synthetic and real-world datasets.

Related Results.

Linear Contextual Dueling Bandits

Contextual dueling bandits with a linear utility function have been the subject of active study in recent years
[

,
25
,
10
,
22
]
. The UCB-based approaches remain effective in this formulation.
Bengs et al. 2022
and

proposed UCB-based methods and showed that the cumulative regret is bounded by
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d\sqrt{T})
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{dT})
.
Di et al. 2023
introduced a variance-aware action-elimination method and showed that the cumulative regret remains bounded.
Li et al. 2024
utilized Thompson Sampling and proposed an algorithm called FGTS.CDB, showing that the cumulative regret is bounded by
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d\sqrt{T})
. In addition, there exist lower bounds of order
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{dT})

[
25
]
for both the cumulative average and weak regret.

Neural Bandits.

Neural networks were first employed as nonlinear reward estimators in multi-armed bandits
[
38
,
33
,
9
,
2
]
.

Ban et al. 2021
adds a second network for potential
-
gain estimation but must retain all past checkpoints, whereas
Deb et al. 2023
rely on perturbed
-
prediction regression oracles that require several forward passes per round.
Zhou et al. 2020
and
Zhang et al. 2021a
proposed neural UCB and TS algorithms, respectively, both achieving sublinear cumulative average regret with a sufficiently large network width and bounded effective dimension. To address computational challenges of previous results,
Xu et al. 2020
introduced shallow exploration approach that leverages the gradients on the last layer’s parameters to construct a Gram matrix. Building on these pioneering works, neural networks have been widely adopted in various bandit settings, such as combinatorial bandits
[
15
]
and federated bandits
[
8
]
. Moreover,
Verma et al. 2024
proposed a method utilizing neural networks to address contextual dueling bandits with nonlinear utility functions, incorporating both UCB and TS-based strategies.

Variance-aware Linear Bandits.

Variance-aware approaches for linear contextual bandits can be categorized into those that require knowledge of the true variance at each round
[
39
,
40
]
and those that do not
[
36
,
18
,
34
]
. Before the introduction of the method in
Zhao et al. 2023
,
methods that did not rely on the true variance often exhibited worse regret bounds compared to their variance-known counterparts. In contrast,
Zhao et al. 2023
achieves computational efficiency while matching the regret bounds of methods that assume variance knowledge, thus bridging the gap between these two lines of work. Such variance-aware approaches have also been applied to neural bandits
[

10

, yielding sublinear cumulative average regret. To the best of our knowledge, there has been no prior work on neural variance-aware dueling bandits.

3
Problem Setting

Contextual Dueling Bandits.

A learner observes a context set
X_{t}=\left\{x_{t,1},\cdots,x_{t,K}\right\}\subset\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
at each round
0<t\leq T
, where
T
is the total number of rounds,
K
is the number of arms, and
\mathcal{X}
is the overall context space. Then the learner chooses two contexts
\left(x_{t,k_{t,1}},x_{t,k_{t,2}}\right)
in
X_{t}
where
k_{t,1},k_{t,2}\in[K]
, which is equivalent to pull a tuple of arms
\left(k_{t,1},k_{t,2}\right)
.
As a result, the learner observes a binary preference feedback signal
o_{t}
, a binary signal.
The feedback
o_{t}
is sampled from a Bernoulli distribution
\mathcal{B}(p_{t})
, where
p_{t}
depends on the chosen pair of contexts.
We assume that the probability
p_{t}
is determined by a link function
g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,1]
applied to the difference of latent utilities.
The latent utility is defined by an unknown function
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, which takes a context as input. In other words,
p_{t}=\mathbb{P}\left(o_{t}=1\mid x_{t,k_{t,1}},x_{t,k_{t,2}}\right)=g\left(\Delta u_{t}\right),
where
\Delta u_{t}=u\left(x_{t,k_{t,1}}\right)-u\left(x_{t,k_{t,2}}\right).

Link Function.

We assume that the link function belongs to the exponential family
[
3
]
.
The exponential family contains various link functions, including the Bradley-Terry-Luce (BTL) model
[
14
,
23
]
, the Thurstone-Mosteller model
[
27
]
, and the Exponential Noise model
[
17
]
.
The gradient of the log-likelihood loss within this family has a particular form, which is utilized in the theoretical analysis of cumulative regret.

Utility Function.

The linear utility function
u
has been extensively studied in contextual dueling bandits
[
25
,
3
,
10
,
22
]
. However, real-world utility structures are often far more complex. To address this, recent studies have explored dueling bandit algorithms under nonlinear utility functions by approximating the utility using neural networks
[
29
]
. Building on this idea and following a similar approach to that of
Xu et al. 2020
, we assume that the utility function
u
can be expressed as an inner product between a feature map
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and an unknown true parameter
\theta_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle u\left(x_{t,k}\right)=\theta_{*}^{\intercal}\phi\left(x_{t,k}\right),

(1)

which generalizes the linear setting when the feature map is the identity function.

3.1
Objective

The objective of this problem is to minimize the expected cumulative regret. The two most common types of instantaneous regret are average regret
r^{a}_{t}
and weak regret
r^{w}_{t}
:

\displaystyle r^{a}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{2u\left(x_{t,k_{t}^{*}}\right)-u\left(x_{t,k_{t,1}}\right)-u\left(x_{t,k_{t,2}}\right)}{2},\quad r^{w}_{t}=u\left(x_{t,k_{t}^{*}}\right)-\max\left\{u\left(x_{t,k_{t,1}}\right),u\left(x_{t,k_{t,2}}\right)\right\},

where
k_{t}^{*}=\arg\max_{k\in[K]}u\left(x_{t,k}\right)
.
Then we define the cumulative average and weak regrets
as

R^{a}_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}r^{a}_{t},\>R^{w}_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}r^{w}_{t}.

An efficient algorithm for the stochastic contextual dueling bandits guarantees the sublinear increase of
R_{t}^{a}
and
R_{t}^{w}
on
T
, e.g.,
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T}).

The weak and average cumulative regrets have individual advantages. For instance, the latter is more useful than the former when selecting the inferior action has significant side effects, e.g., clinical trials.
In this paper, our problem focuses mainly on the cumulative average regret
R_{T}^{a}
as
[
3
]
.

4
Variance-Aware Neural Dueling Bandits

4.1
Nonlinear Utility Function Estimator

To achieve a sublinear expected cumulative average regret, accurately estimating the unknown utility function
u
is crucial. While many existing works have relied on a linear structure
[
3
,
22
,
25
,
10
]
, i.e.,
u\left(x_{t,k}\right)=\theta^{\intercal}_{*}x_{t,k}
, real-world utility functions are often
inherently nonlinear. To approximate such nonlinear utility functions, we employ a fully connected neural network
f
with the ReLU activation function
\sigma(x)=\max\{x,0\}
, as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} f\left(x;\theta,W\right)&=\theta^{\intercal}\phi\left(x;W\right),\quad\phi\left(x;W\right)=\sqrt{m}\sigma\left(W^{L}(\sigma(\cdots\sigma\left(W^{1}x\right))\cdots)\right),\end{aligned}

(2)

where
W=(\textrm{vec}(W^{1}),\cdots,\textrm{vec}(W^{L}))
consists of learnable parameters as
W^{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
,
W^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
for
2\leq l\leq L-1
, and
W^{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
. The parameter
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is initialized as
\theta=\theta_{0}
, and the weight matrices are initialized as
W=W^{i}_{0}
for
1\leq i\leq L
, following the setup of prior neural bandit frameworks
[
30
]
. In detail, for
1\leq l\leq L-1
, we initialize the learnable parameters as

\displaystyle W_{0}^{l}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}w&0\\
0&w\end{bmatrix},\quad W_{0}^{L}=\begin{pmatrix}\omega^{\intercal}\\
-\omega^{\intercal}\end{pmatrix}^{\intercal},\quad\theta_{0}\sim N\left(0,\frac{1}{d}\right),

(3)

where
w\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,4/m\right)
and
\omega\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,2/m\right)
. After observing the binary signal
o_{t}
from the arm selection
\left(k_{t,1},k_{t,2}\right)
at each round
t
, the neural network
f
is trained on historical data
\{x_{t,k_{i,1}},x_{t,k_{i,2}},o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t}
. The parameters
\theta_{t}
and
W_{t}
are updated by minimizing the regularized log-likelihood loss
\mathcal{L}_{t}
, defined as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{t}\left(\theta,W\right)&=-\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\frac{\log\left(g\left(\left(-1\right)^{1-o_{i}}\Delta f_{i}\right)\right)}{\zeta_{i}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\lambda\left\|\theta-\theta_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2},\end{aligned}

(4)

for some
\lambda>0
, where

\Delta f_{i}=f\left(x_{i,k_{i,1}};\theta,W\right)-f\left(x_{i,k_{i,2}};\theta,W\right)
and

\displaystyle\zeta_{i}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\max\{\widehat{\sigma}_{i},\epsilon\}&\quad\textrm{variance-aware,}\\
&1&\quad\textrm{variance-agnostic,}\end{aligned}\right.

(5)

with the estimated variance
|\widehat{\sigma}_{i}|^{2}
and a parameter
\epsilon>0
. In the variance-aware setting, the estimated variance is given by
|\widehat{\sigma}_{i}|^{2}=g(\Delta f_{i})(1-g(\Delta f_{i})).
The positive constant
\epsilon
is essential in practice to prevent the loss from diverging and to incorporate prior knowledge about the minimum variance in the problem if possible.

4.2
Arm Selection Methods.

We propose two types of methods: UCB-based
[
1
]
and Thompson Sampling
[
33
]
strategies, following the approach of
[
29
]
. We assume access to a set of parameters
(\theta_{t-1},W_{t-1})
learned from the previous round
t-1
, as well as the Gram matrix
V_{t-1}
:

\displaystyle V_{t-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\frac{\Delta\phi_{i,k_{i,1},k_{i,2}}\Delta\phi_{i,k_{i,1},k_{i,2}}^{\intercal}}{\zeta_{i}^{2}}+\lambda I,

(6)

where
\Delta\phi_{i,k,k^{\prime}}:=\Delta\phi_{i,k,k^{\prime},i}
, with

\Delta\phi_{i,k,k^{\prime},j}=\phi\left(x_{i,k};W_{j-1}\right)-\phi\left(x_{i,k^{\prime}};W_{j-1}\right)
for any
k,k^{\prime}\in[K]
and
i,j\in[T]
. The value
\zeta_{i}
in
Equation

6
is identical to that in
Equation

4
.
Based on the Gram matrix, we propose three arm selection strategies: one asymmetric arm selection and two symmetric arm selections, applicable to both UCB- and Thompson Sampling-based methods.

4.2.1  UCB-based Methods.

Asymmetric Arm Selection (UCB-ASYM).

This strategy selects the first arm
k_{t,1}
greedily based on
\left(\theta_{t-1},W_{t-1}\right)
:

\displaystyle k_{t,1}=\argmax_{k\in[K]}\theta_{t-1}^{\intercal}\phi(x_{t,k};W_{t-1}).

(7)

The second arm
k_{t,2}
is determined as the best competitor of
k_{t,1}
with an exploration bonus, expressed as the upper confidence width
\left\|\Delta\phi_{t,k,k_{t,1}}\right\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}
as

k_{t,2}=\argmax_{k\in[K]}\theta_{t-1}^{\intercal}\phi(x_{t,k};W_{t-1})+a_{t}\left\|\Delta\phi_{t,k,k_{t,1}}\right\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}},

where
a_{t}>0
is a confidence width coefficient.
The asymmetric arm selection strategy has been studied in
[
3
]
and
[
29
]
, in the contexts of linear and nonlinear utility functions, respectively.

Optimistic Symmetric Arm Selection (UCB-OSYM).

The first and second arms, which maximize the sum of utilities including the exploration bonus, are selected as follows:

\displaystyle k_{t,1},k_{t,2}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{k,k^{\prime}\in[K]\times[K]}\theta_{t-1}^{\intercal}\phi_{t,k,k^{\prime}}+a_{t}\left\|\Delta\phi_{t,k,k^{\prime}}\right\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}},

with the confidence width coefficient
a_{t}>0
and
\phi_{t,k,k^{\prime}}=\phi_{t,k,k^{\prime},t}
, where

\phi_{i,k,k^{\prime},j}=\phi\left(x_{i,k};W_{j-1}\right)+\phi\left(x_{i,k^{\prime}};W_{j-1}\right)
for
i,j\in[T]
and
k,k^{\prime}\in[K].

This strategy has been studied in the linear setting, as in
[
10
]
.

Candidate-based Symmetric Arm Selection (UCB-CSYM).

This strategy first constructs a confidence set
\mathcal{C}_{t}
for each round
t
for strong candidates as potential optimal arms, as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{t}=\left\{k\mid a_{t}\left\|\Delta\phi_{t,k,k^{\prime}}\right\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}>\theta_{t-1}^{\intercal}\Delta\phi_{t,k^{\prime},k},\forall k^{\prime}\in[K]\right\}.

(8)

for the confidence width coefficient
a_{t}>0
.
A tuple of arms with the maximal exploration bonus is then selected as follows:

k_{t,1},k_{t,2}=\argmax_{k,k^{\prime}\in[K]\times[K]}\left\|\Delta\phi_{t,k,k^{\prime}}\right\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}.

This strategy type has been studied in the linear setting
[
25
]
.

4.2.2  Thompson Sampling Methods.

Asymmetric Arm Selection (TS-ASYM).
In this strategy, the first arm
k_{t,1}
in round
t>0
is greedily selected as in
Equation

7
.
Then, we sample each arm’s relative utility compared to the first arm from a Gaussian distribution with mean

\theta_{t-1}^{\intercal}\Delta\phi_{t,k,k_{t,1}}
and variance

a_{t}^{2}\left\|\Delta\phi_{t,k,k_{t,1}}\right\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}^{2}
,
where
a_{t}>0
is the confidence width coefficient, i.e.,

\widetilde{u}_{t,k_{t,1},k}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\theta_{t-1}^{\intercal}\Delta\phi_{t,k,k_{t,1}},a_{t}^{2}\left\|\Delta\phi_{t,k,k_{t,1}}\right\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}^{2}\right)
.
Then, the second arm
k_{t,2}
in round
t
is selected as
k_{t,2}=\argmax_{k\in[K]}\widetilde{u}_{t,k_{t,1},k}.

This strategy was also studied in
[
29
]
.

Optimistic Symmetric Arm Selection (TS-OSYM).

This strategy constructs a Gaussian distribution, where the mean is defined as the sum of the estimated utilities of the pair of arms
\left(k,k^{\prime}\right)
, and the variance is determined by the exploration bonus for the pair. Then, a value
\widetilde{u}_{t,k,k^{\prime}}
is drawn from a Gaussian distribution as
\widetilde{u}_{t,k,k^{\prime}}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\theta_{t-1}^{\intercal}\phi_{t,k,k^{\prime}},a_{t}^{2}\|\Delta\phi_{t,k,k^{\prime}}\|^{2}_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}\right)
for
k,k^{\prime}\in[K]
where
a_{t}>0
is the confidence width coefficient. Then, the arms
k_{t,1}
and
k_{t,2}
are finally selected as
k_{t,1},k_{t,2}=\argmax_{k,k^{\prime}\in[K]\times[K]}\widetilde{u}_{t,k,k^{\prime}}.

Candidate-based Symmetric Arm Selection (TS-CSYM).
In the same manner as the UCB regime, a confidence set
\mathcal{C}_{t}
is constructed as in
Equation

8
for the confidence width coefficient
a_{t}>0
.
Then the pair
(k_{t,1},k_{t,2})
of arms is chosen from the set
\mathcal{C}_{t}
as
(k_{t,1},k_{t,2})=\argmax_{(i,j)\in C_{t}\times C_{t}}\widehat{\sigma}_{i,j},
where

\widehat{\sigma}_{i,j}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\|\Delta\phi_{t,i,j}\|^{2}_{V_{t-1}^{-1}},\frac{\|\Delta\phi_{t,i,j}\|^{4}_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}}{4\log(Kt^{2})}\right).

While we consider different arm selection methods, we show that the expected cumulative average regret for each method remains comparable up to constant factors under standard assumptions.
The equivalence of UCB-based arm selection strategies in the linear setting was discussed in
[
10
]
.

Regret Analysis

In this section, we provide theoretical results for the upper bounds of cumulative average regret for each arm selection method.
To facilitate the analysis of our algorithm, we assume the following structure for the context set
\mathcal{X}
, utility function
u
and the initial parameters
(\theta_{0},W_{0})
.

Assumption 1
.

For all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the context vector is bounded in

7
Conclusion

We propose a family of neural variance-aware linear dueling bandit algorithms, collectively referred to as NVLDB. Our approach employs a neural network to approximate nonlinear utility functions and estimates uncertainty by constructing a Gram matrix from the gradients with respect to the learnable parameters of the last layer. These gradients are weighted by the estimated variance of the Bernoulli distribution, computed via exponential-family link functions. We theoretically show that both NVLDB and its variance-agnostic variant achieve sublinear cumulative average regret across various arm selection strategies under both the UCB and TS frameworks. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to introduce variance-aware neural dueling bandits with shallow exploration. Furthermore, we empirically validate our method against existing baselines and demonstrate its superior performance on widely used synthetic and real-world tasks.

For a more refined estimation, we provide the following results based on spectral perturbation analysis.

Lemma B.6
.

Let
V,W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be symmetric positive definite (SPD) matrices. Then for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the following inequality holds:

\lambda_{m}(W^{-1}V)\|x\|_{W}^{2}\leq\|x\|_{V}^{2}\leq\lambda_{M}(W^{-1}V)\|x\|_{W}^{2}.

Proof.

Since
V
and
W
are SPD, the matrix
W^{-1}V
is also SPD and hence diagonalizable with positive real eigenvalues. Consider the generalized Rayleigh quotient:

R(x):=\frac{x^{\intercal}Vx}{x^{\intercal}Wx}=\frac{\|x\|_{V}^{2}}{\|x\|_{W}^{2}}.

By the Courant–Fischer Minimax theorem
[
13
]
, the following inequality holds for all nonzero
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\lambda_{m}(W^{-1}V)\leq R(x)\leq\lambda_{M}(W^{-1}V).

Multiplying both sides by
\|x\|_{W}^{2}=x^{\intercal}Wx
gives:

\lambda_{m}(W^{-1}V)\cdot\|x\|_{W}^{2}\leq\|x\|_{V}^{2}\leq\lambda_{M}(W^{-1}V)\cdot\|x\|_{W}^{2}.

Taking square roots (since all terms are positive), we obtain:

\sqrt{\lambda_{m}(W^{-1}V)}\cdot\|x\|_{W}\leq\|x\|_{V}\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{M}(W^{-1}V)}\cdot\|x\|_{W}.

∎

Lemma B.7
.

Let
y_{1},\dots,y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be vectors satisfying
\|y_{i}\|_{2}\leq B
. Define
z_{i}:=y_{t}-y_{i}
, and assume that
\|z_{i}\|_{2}\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(m^{-1/6})
for all
i
. Let the regularized Gram matrices be

V:=\lambda I+\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}y_{i}^{\intercal},\qquad W:=\lambda I+\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}z_{i}^{\intercal},

with
\lambda>0
.
Then the following bound holds:

\|WV^{-1}\|=\sup_{x\neq 0}\frac{x^{\intercal}Wx}{x^{\intercal}Vx}\leq 1+\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{tm^{-1/3}}{\lambda}\right).

Proof.

Since
\|z_{i}\|_{2}\leq Cm^{-1/6}
, we have

\|z_{i}z_{i}^{\intercal}\|=\|z_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\leq C^{2}m^{-1/3}.

Therefore,

\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{t}x_{i}x_{i}^{\intercal}\right\|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t}\|x_{i}x_{i}^{\intercal}\|\leq tC^{2}m^{-1/3}.

Let
A:=\sum_{i=1}^{t}y_{i}y_{i}^{\intercal}
and
B:=\sum_{i=1}^{t}z_{i}z_{i}^{\intercal}
. Then we write

WV^{-1}=(\lambda I+B)^{-1}(\lambda I+A).

We consider the Rayleigh quotient:

\lambda_{M}(WV^{-1})=\max_{\|x\|=1}\frac{x^{\intercal}(\lambda I+B)z}{x^{\intercal}(\lambda I+A)x}.

Now, for all unit vectors
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

x^{\intercal}(\lambda I+B)x\leq\lambda+\|B\|\leq\lambda+tC^{2}m^{-1/3},

for some constant
C>0

and

x^{\intercal}(\lambda I+A)x\geq\lambda.

Therefore,

\frac{x^{\intercal}(\lambda I+B)x}{x^{\intercal}(\lambda I+A)x}\leq\frac{\lambda+tC^{2}m^{-1/3}}{\lambda}=1+\frac{tC^{2}}{\lambda m^{1/3}}.

We conclude that

\lambda_{M}(W^{-1}V)\leq 1+\frac{tC^{2}}{\lambda m^{1/3}},

E.2.1  Hyperparameters.

We report the hyperparameters used in the main manuscript. To evaluate NVLDB, we employ a fully connected deep neural network with ReLU activations, consisting of

d
. We set the regularization parameter
\lambda=1.0
, the confidence width coefficient
a_{t}=1.0
, the number of gradient steps per round

, and the number of episodes
\mathcal{H}=1
. Implementing an exact algorithm to find
\theta_{t}
that satisfies
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t}}{\partial\theta}(\theta_{t},w_{t})=0

(\theta_{t},w_{t})
via gradient descent on the loss function using a learning rate of
\gamma=0.01
at each round

Computational Efficiency.

We compared the wall-clock times of our method (NVLDB-UCB-ASYM) and NDB
[
29
]
over 2,000 rounds across 20 random seeds, measured on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 GPU paired with an AMD Ryzen 9 5950X 16-core CPU. As a result, the proposed method significantly outperforms NDB in terms of computational efficiency. Specifically, NVLDB-UCB-ASYM completes 2,000 rounds in an average of
1.71\pm 0.78
minutes, while NDB requires
48.8\pm 15.5
minutes under the same conditions. These large gaps make sense because NDB utilizes the gradients with respect to full learnable parameters in a given neural network, but our method utilizes the gradients with respect to only the learnable parameters of the last layer in a given neural network to construct a Gram matrix.
