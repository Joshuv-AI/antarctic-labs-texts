Road crashes: a scourge on African lives!
Authors: Timothy Craig Hardcastle
Published: 2025

The recent article by Mulugeta et al again directs attention to the trauma burden of the low-and-middle-income (LMIC) regions of the world and in particular highlights sub-Saharan Africa, where almost 70% of the world’s LMIC trauma-related deaths occur.1 They highlight considerable road crash injuries at their facility, mention the prolonged emergency department (ED) length of stay and postulate rurality and lack of step-down facilities as reasons for this. They also decry the lack of trauma registries in LMICs and the difficulties maintaining those that do exist.1 Furthermore, article-based records, with a corroborating log-book system for continuous records present challenges in data retrieval from potentially missing files.

Almost 30% of patients had an ED stay exceeding 24 hours, with some approaching 10 days (median 5), often awaiting family to fetch them postdischarge. This is a huge difference from the accepted norms in wealthier countries where the ED stay is addressed in hours (4–6 hours being the average) with some variance if a short-stay ward is part of the ED infrastructure for observation purposes, yet not dissimilar to the experience in other African countries.25

What appears to be in line with universal experience is that almost 70% of patients were discharged home from the ED after excluding severe injury requiring admission, whereas the main mortality was from brain trauma.1 4 5 Similar to middle-income counties in Africa more advanced diagnostics and imaging are available in dedicated trauma centers, but often delays to arrival result from poor emergency medical service infrastructure.5 6

The lack of reliable trauma registries in LMICs has prompted the WHO to develop a number of minimum data sets for trauma data accrual (see https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/who-dataset-for-injury), for intensive care management (in development by the WHO/World Federation of Intensive and Critical Care collaboration), as well as operative care (https://www.who.int/teams/integrated-health-services/clinical-services-and-systems/emergency--critical-and-operative-care). This together with the development of in-patient capacity, patient holding areas for those awaiting family and improved post-trauma rehabilitation for inpatients will reduce the ED overcrowding and length of stay backlogs.7

Hopefully the global surgery movement can partner with LMICs and move from the “surgical mission outreach” mindset to in-country human capacity and structural capacity development enabling lasting, cost-efficient, context-relevant trauma systems that reduce road crash incidence, management and related mortality.
