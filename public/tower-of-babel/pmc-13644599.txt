Bispecific antibodies that engage pIgR enrich in mucosa in cynomolgus monkeys
Authors: Kelsie Marks, Nirav Shah, Ly Pham, Jun Chen, Ian White, Krista Burke, Peter Garrett, Mathieu Marella, Prathap Nagaraja Shastri, Brian Geist, Partha Chowdhury, Vinicius Carreira, Wan Cheung Cheung, Adam Zwolak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTherapeutic antibodies have limited access to barrier‑protected compartments such as the brain, lung and gastric mucosa, and kidney lumen, constraining their effectiveness in many diseases. Approaches using a bispecific antibody that targets a transcytosing receptor such as the polymeric Ig receptor (pIgR) can result in significant enrichment of IgG-based therapeutics across from blood into mucosal or mucosal lining. The polymeric Ig receptor is expressed in epithelial layers in many tissues, but differences in expression patterns across species represent a challenge in validating preclinical in vivo models. Here, we compare RNA and protein expression across an array of human, cynomolgus monkey, and mouse tissues. We show that pIgR expression in cynomolgus monkeys is similar to expression in humans. We further evaluate the ability of pIgR-targeted bispecific antibodies to transcytose into lung and gastrointestinal mucosa in cynomolgus monkeys. We show that pIgR-targeted bispecific antibodies rapidly deplete from serum, while enriching 5–10-fold in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid compared to normal IgG antibodies. These results support the use of anti-pIgR bispecific antibodies to treat diseases requiring targeting in mucosal areas.

Introduction

IgG-based therapeutics offer significant advantages over small molecule therapeutics, including longer half-life, immune effector functions, and generally lower toxicities. However, antibodies fail to reach therapeutically relevant exposure levels in certain immune-excluded niches, such as brain parenchyma, lung and gastric mucosa, and renal tubule and Bowman’s capsule or bladder lumen, which are often sites of disease pathologies. Thus, strategies to deliver antibodies across cellular barriers into immune-excluded niches represent an important therapeutic need. Acute respiratory infection, kidney disease, and gastrointestinal inflammation are among mucosal diseases in tissues characterized by high expression of the polymeric Ig receptor (pIgR). Antibodies that target transcytosis receptors, such as the transferrin receptor, CD98, or others, show increased enrichment across the blood-brain barrier in mouse and primates.1–4 Likewise, antibodies targeting pIgR show enhanced transcytosis across epithelial layers.5–8 These studies support the possibility of delivering therapeutically efficacious doses of antibody across cellular barriers.

Humans transcytose ~3–5 g/day of dimeric IgA (dIgA) across the mucosal barrier via engagement with pIgR.9,10 pIgR is highly expressed in mucosal epithelial tissues, including gastric, kidney, and lung, where it localizes primarily on the basolateral surface. PIgR features 5 immunoglobulin (Ig) domains in its extracellular region and can bind dIgA and IgM via a conformational change upon binding.11 Binding to dIgA precedes transcytosis to the apical surface, where the extracellular domain of pIgR is cleaved to release the dIgA:pIgR, also called secretory IgA (sIgA).

Respiratory infection remains a threat to human health, and antibody therapeutics have limited efficacy due primarily to their low exposures at mucosal sites of infection. Therapeutic IgG display low exposure in the lung mucus (~1,000 times lower than the level in serum) and slow accumulation in the mucosa due to the tight epithelial barriers and lack of an IgG-specific active transport mechanism. These characteristics of IgG manifest as high costs of goods and narrow therapeutic windows, leading to only limited application for these diseases. Short therapeutic windows, coupled with the need for high doses to attain therapeutic efficacy (typically in the range of 50 mg/kg for hemagglutinin-targeting antibodies), limit the utility of IgG for treatment of acute respiratory infection.12,13 Additionally, chronic kidney disease, including both autosomal dominant and recessive polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD and ARPKD, respectively), involves aberrant growth of renal tubule epithelial cells inaccessible to circulating therapeutic antibodies.14 Kidney cyst growth is in part driven by signaling through growth factor receptors, such as the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), but antibodies that target EGFR can cause significant toxicity in skin and other tissues where EGFR is expressed.15 Interestingly, an anti-EGFR antibody on a dimeric IgA isotype showed the ability to slow cyst growth in preclinical models, highlighting the potential of a targeted delivery approach to treat luminal or mucosal disease.16

We reasoned that an IgG-based bispecific antibody (bsAb) that can bind pIgR might likewise efficiently transcytose into the mucosal lumen where it could bind a second target. pIgR-based transcytosis could overcome the challenges of low exposure and slow enrichment in the mucosal lumen, enabling therapeutic IgG to treat acute respiratory disease or other mucosal diseases with IgG. Indeed, we and several other groups showed that targeting pIgR could enhance mucosal enrichment, using either peptides, single domain (VHH) antibodies, or even dIgA itself.6,7,17,18 Furthermore, we showed that enrichment occurs via directional transcytosis across cell monolayers and human tissues through pIgR engagement, although studies in rodents were precluded by high expression of pIgR in the liver.7,17

Here, we characterize the RNA and protein expression patterns of pIgR in human, cynomolgus monkey, and mouse tissues. These data confirm observations that studies of pIgR-based mucosal enrichment in mice are confounded by uniquely higher liver expression in mice compared to primates, and they show that cynomolgus monkey is a relevant species based on its similar pIgR expression patterns to humans.19,20 We used VHH domains that we previously characterized to design bsAbs that simultaneously bind pIgR and hemagglutinin to evaluate the pharmacokinetics (PK) of a pIgR-targeted bsAb in cynomolgus monkeys, showing that the bsAb displays fast enrichment in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid compared to its IgG counterpart and that this enrichment occurred on therapeutically relevant timescales. The bsAb maintained high exposure in BAL fluid for ~1 week, consistent with the mucosal half-life of dIgA, suggesting such a design may enable IgG-based intervention for acute respiratory illnesses. These designs may also prove useful in other conditions requiring IgG enrichment in the mucosa, including inflammatory bowel diseases and polycystic kidney disease.

Results

Transcriptomic analysis of pIgR expression in mouse, cynomolgus monkey, and human tissues using bulk RNA-sequencing

To compare pIgR expression across species, we first performed bulk RNAseq on human, cynomolgus monkey, and mouse tissues (Figure 1). The expression was normalized to transcripts per kilobase per million reads sequenced (TPM) for easy cross-study comparison. In mouse, high expression (>500 TPM) was observed in most of the digestive-related tissues (cecum, colon, duodenum, esophagus, gallbladder, ileum, jejunum, liver, and rectum) followed by the prostate and uterus (>40 TPM). In the cynomolgus monkey, high expression was observed in the colon and stomach (>500 TPM) and <500 TPM for the jejunum, liver, cervix, mammary gland, and kidney. In humans, high expression was observed in the salivary gland, colon, jejunum, and smooth muscle (>500 TPM) and <500 TPM in the kidney, lung, ilium, stomach, colon, pancreas, and mammary gland. Although there are some differences in the expression level and tissues between species, overall, plgR is expressed in many of the digestive- and reproductive system-related tissues for mouse, monkey, and humans. The largest difference in pIgR expression was seen in the liver, where mouse expressed > 500 TPM but cynomolgus monkey and human liver showed very low expression. In human tissue, pIgR protein was detected in epithelial cells of the duodenum mucosa, renal tubules, and urinary bladder. Minimal immunoreactivity was observed in the hepatocellular compartment, but some apical staining was noted in the biliary duct epithelium. High intensity immunoreactivity was also observed in a rare subpopulation of incidental leukocytes in the tonsil and gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT). Evidence of cross-reactivity with cynomolgus monkey tissue was identified in similar anatomical locations, such as, the kidney tubules and duodenum mucosa, as expected. Overall, the intensity of pIgR positive cells in the cynomolgus monkey was less than that observed in humans. Consistent with human tissue findings, pIgR immunoreactivity was present in a rare subpopulation of incidental leukocytes in the cynomolgus monkey tonsil and GALT. No immunoreactivity was observed in the cynomolgus monkey liver, as expected.

pIgR expression in human FFPE tissue

In human normal tissue, immunoreactivity with the rabbit monoclonal anti-pIgR antibody clone [EPR23314-256] was predominantly observed as a membranous/cytoplasmic pattern across multiple epithelial cell types. In the gastrointestinal tract, strong positive pIgR labeling was observed in the mucosal epithelium of the colon and small intestines and concentrated toward the lower regions of the gastric pits in the stomach, as well as in glandular epithelium and other epithelial linings. (Figure 3). Additionally, pIgR labeling was observed in the epithelium of the pancreatic interlobular ducts and the gall bladder. Some apical labeling was observed within the biliary duct epithelium of the liver. In the urinary system, patches of high‑intensity labeling were noted in the apical urothelial surface epithelium of the urinary bladder and renal tubules. Mid-to-high intensity labeling was observed in a subpopulation of epithelium in the Bowman’s capsule of the kidney (Figure 3, Supplementary Figure S1, Supplementary Table S2). In the reproductive organs, strongly positive pIgR labeling was observed in select regions of the endometrial epithelial cells, while only apical labeling was noted in the remaining endometrial epithelium. Positive labeling was also detected in the cervical epithelium and in a very rare subset of cells of the prostate tubular epithelium. Within the tonsil and lymph node tissue, a very rare subset of incidental leukocytes demonstrated strongly positive pIgR labeling. Positive labeling was observed in the alveolar macrophages of the lung and in squamous and glandular epithelium of the larynx, pharynx, oropharynx, parotid gland, and tongue. In the nervous system, low levels of vesicular cytoplasmic labeling were observed in a subset of neurons of the midbrain and in the meninges of the sella turcica , as well as the corneal epithelium. pIgR expression in cynomolgus monkey FFPE tissue

In cynomolgus monkey tissue, immunoreactivity with the rabbit monoclonal anti-pIgR antibody clone [EPR23314-256] presented an overall intensity of pIgR positive cells that was less than what was observed in humans (Figure 4, Supplementary Table S2). Considering the nervous system, low levels of vesicular cytoplasmic labeling were observed in a subset of neurons of the midbrain and hippocampus. In digestive tissues, cytoplasmic labeling of the stomach, small intestine, and colon mucosal epithelium was observed in a bull’s‑eye/multi-ring pattern concentrated toward the gastric pits, with labeling also observed in a subset of leukocytes within the lamina propria. In pancreas, diffuse cytoplasmic labeling was observed in the islets of Langerhans. Considering the urinary system, positive labeling was observed in the tubule epithelium of the kidneys. Within the tonsil and lymph node tissue, a very rare subset of incidental leukocytes demonstrated strongly positive pIgR labeling. Positive labeling was observed in a rare subset of alveolar macrophages of the lung and in the apical epithelial surface of the trachea. Low‑level diffuse cytoplasmic labeling was observed in the glomerulosa of the adrenal gland, and vesicular cytoplasmic labeling was observed in the sebaceous glands of the skin. Except for the tubule epithelium of the kidney, neurons of the midbrain, and leukocytes of the tonsil and lymph nodes, human and cynomolgus monkey did not display similar staining patterns, likely due to antibody non-specificity in the monkey. Characterization of antibodies

Quantitative mRNA and IHC analysis showed that pIgR displays similar expression profiles in cynomolgus monkeys and human and that pIgR is expressed in respiratory epithelium. Thus, we designed antibodies to evaluate the ability of pIgR engagement to enrich antibody in lung and gastric mucosa in cynomolgus monkeys. A monoclonal antibody (mAb) that binds hemagglutinin (FM1B108) was compared to the same mAb with the anti-pIgR VHH2 fused to the N-terminus of its heavy chain (FM1B104) (Figure 5A).7 We selected this anti-pIgR VHH domain based on our previous characterization of its high ability to mediate transcytosis in cell and human tissue-based models.7,17 The current study was designed to evaluate whether this previously characterized transcytosis platform could produce enhanced mucosal enrichment in a translationally relevant nonhuman primate species. Both antibodies featured the anti-hemagglutinin variable region from the antibody CR9114.21 Similarly designed control antibodies featured a variable region that lacked binding to hemagglutinin (MSCB847 and B23B307). Both FM1B108 and FM1B104 bound similarly to hemagglutinin from H1N1 influenza, while only FM1B104 bound to pIgR (Figure 5B,C). Control antibodies failed to bind hemagglutinin, but B23B307 bound to both human and cynomolgus monkey pIgR, while MSCB847 failed to bind pIgR from either species (Figure 5 5D,E). PK and biodistribution in cynomolgus monkeys

We evaluated the ability of the pIgR-targeted bsAb, FM1B104, to transcytosis from serum into the mucosa of the pIgR-expressing lung, kidney, and gastrointestinal tissues. Specifically, we collected BAL to determine the biodistribution of the pIgR bsAb across the lung epithelium relative to its serum levels (Figure 6, Tables 1 and 2, and Supplementary Table S3). We measured serum and BAL PK, and we also collected various sections of the GI tract – duodenum, jejunum, and ileum as well as lung and kidney tissue to determine the biodistribution of pIgR bsAb across the epithelium (Supplementary dataset). There was rapid clearance of FM1B104 from serum, with a terminal half-life of 1.5 days, relative to FM1B108 whose serum half-life was 9.5 days. This observation is consistent with uptake of FM1B104 in tissues expressing pIgR and is directionally aligned with the previously demonstrated transcytosis activity of the anti-pIgR VHH platform.7,17 Both antibodies reached maximum concentration (Cmax) in BAL at 72 h. The overall exposure (AUCall) showed that FM1B104 exhibited a 5.5-fold higher exposure in BAL when compared with FM1B108. The ratio of serum to BAL showed a significant difference as well. At 72 h post dose, the concentration of FM1B104 in BAL was 0.78 μg/mL (serum/BAL ratio 28), while for FM1B108, it was 0.14 μg/mL (serum/BAL ratio 396). This indicates that at Cmax the ability of the pIgR bsAb to bind pIgR conferred a 6-fold increase in BAL exposure. The levels of IgG in healthy adult serum and BAL are 11 mg/mL and 0.0092 mg/mL (serum/BAL ratio 1,195), respectively.22,23 The steady-state levels of secretory IgA (sIgA) in healthy adult serum and BAL are 1.9 mg/mL and 0.013 mg/mL (serum/BAL ratio 146), respectively.23 Thus, FM1B104 showed significantly enhanced mucosal enrichment compared to IgG and IgA, even at similar overall concentrations. FM1B104 levels in the lung tissue were almost 30-fold lower than those of FM1B108 at 312 h, suggesting the trafficking of the pIgR bsAb from tissue into the mucosal space.

Similar observations were seen in the gastric tissues – duodenum, jejunum and ileum – where distribution of both FM1B104 and FM1B108 was comparable at 48 h. However, at 312 h FM1B104 levels in the tissues were below the limit of quantification (BLQ), suggesting transcytosis of the pIgR bsAb from across the tissue into the mucosal layer. In kidney tissue, FM1B104 levels were likewise BLQ at 312 h; however, unlike gastric tissues, more pronounced clearance was already evident at 48 h, with levels of FM1B104 ~ 3.4-fold lower than those of FM1B108. This accelerated clearance may reflect the high pIgR expression in the epithelial lining of primate (and human) renal tubules, as indicated by the uniform IHC staining of tubular epithelial cells (Supplementary Figure S1). Across all tissues evaluated, FM1B104 demonstrated more rapid clearance relative to FM1B108, supporting efficient transcytosis of pIgR-targeted bsAb across epithelial barriers, including renal epithelium.

Discussion

Targeting therapeutic antibodies to immune-excluded compartments represents a significant unmet need. We previously showed that anti-pIgR VHH domains could mediate transcytosis in cell-based and human microtissue-based assays.7,17 Previous studies showed that high pIgR expression in the liver of rodents makes these models challenging for translational studies. One way to avoid the liver-based clearance of antibodies is to use GalNAc-siRNA conjugates for hepatocyte-specific knockdown of targets, but the time-course of knockdown still presents challenges for analysis.24 Additionally, our RNA expression data show that mice uniquely display high expression of pIgR in the esophagus mucosa, distinct from cynomolgus monkeys and humans. Mice also display lower pIgR expression in stomach compared to cynomolgus monkeys and other primates. Here we show that cynomolgus monkeys are a relevant model for evaluating transcytosis in humans. Cynomolgus monkeys display somewhat lower RNA levels of pIgR in the lung compared to humans, suggesting that the level of enrichment in cynomolgus monkeys may under-represent enrichment in humans, while expression of pIgR RNA is higher in the colon of cynomolgus monkeys compared to humans. We further validated RNA analysis by IHC analysis in human and cynomolgus monkey tissue. Staining of pIgR was highest in human duodenum, with expression being confined to the mucosal epithelial cells. This staining was consistent, albeit somewhat less intense, in the duodenum of cynomolgus monkeys. Epithelial staining was also observed in the urinary bladder epithelium of both species. Intense staining was observed in human stomach, colon, and respiratory tissue, suggesting pIgR-targeted antibodies could efficiently transcytose to these tissues.

Overall, the IHC analysis showed high concordance with transcriptomic analysis. The tissues with highest RNA expression: jejunum, salivary gland, colon, ileum, kidney, stomach, and lung all showed strong staining by IHC. Cardiac tissue and most neuronal tissues were negative for pIgR expression by both RNA-seq and IHC. One discrepancy was that bladder tissue showed strong staining by IHC but only low transcript levels. Although the exact cause of the difference was not known, it may have been due to the small number of cells expressing pIgR within bladder epithelium. Human and cynomolgus monkey liver displayed low transcript numbers of pIgR (~14 and ~23 TPM, respectively) while human liver tissue displayed intense staining in a subset of bile duct epithelial cells, which was absent in cynomolgus monkey liver. Lastly, while pIgR expression was highest in GI tissue (e.g., colon, jejunum, ileum, stomach), the relative RNA levels and staining varied somewhat.

Based on these analyses, we quantitatively evaluated the enrichment of a pIgR-targeted bsAb in cynomolgus monkey serum, BAL fluid, lung, and intestinal mucosa (duodenum, ileum, and jejunum). This study demonstrated efficient uptake of the pIgR-targeted bsAb in tissues expressing pIgR. FM1B104 was rapidly cleared from serum relative to the control antibody FM1B108 and exhibited substantially greater exposure in BAL fluid.

Importantly, FM1B104 and FM1B108 share the same anti-hemagglutinin binding domain and overall molecular architecture, with the principal functional difference being the addition of the pIgR-binding VHH domain in FM1B104. Consistent with this design, FM1B104 demonstrated reduced serum exposure, increased BAL exposure, and accelerated depletion from pIgR-expressing tissues relative to FM1B108. These findings are further supported by prior studies demonstrating pIgR-mediated transcytosis using the same anti-pIgR VHH platform.7,17

The PK differences between FM1B104 and FM1B108 appear to be driven primarily by increased clearance rather than altered distribution. FM1B104 demonstrated an approximately 4.5-fold higher systemic clearance than FM1B108 (0.9 versus 0.2 mL/h/kg), while the volume of distribution remained comparable between the two molecules (54.8 versus 72.8 mL/kg). These findings argue against broadly increased tissue distribution as the primary explanation for the shorter serum half-life of FM1B104 and are consistent with the expected biological consequences of pIgR engagement and transcytosis.

While the current study was not specifically designed to quantitatively distinguish among receptor-mediated transcytosis, target-mediated disposition, altered tissue distribution, and other contributing mechanisms, the collective PK and biodistribution findings support pIgR engagement as a major contributor to the observed profile. Furthermore, the use of an anti-hemagglutinin antibody was intended to minimize the likelihood of target-mediated clearance in healthy animals. Additional mechanistic studies will be required to fully define the relative contribution of individual disposition pathways. There was approximately a 5.5-fold increase in BAL exposure of the pIgR-targeted bsAb relative to the control antibody. The maximum BAL exposure of FM1B104 was 770 ng/mL at 72 h after administration, corresponding to approximately 1% of total antibody, compared with approximately 10 μg/mL total secretory IgA in human BAL. Because BAL samples were not corrected for epithelial lining fluid dilution, the reported BAL concentrations should be interpreted as relative measures of mucosal exposure rather than absolute epithelial lining fluid concentrations. Nevertheless, the substantially higher BAL exposure observed for FM1B104 relative to FM1B108 under identical collection conditions is consistent with enhanced delivery of the pIgR-targeted bsAb to the pulmonary mucosal compartment.

Together, these findings support the potential utility of pIgR-targeted antibodies for enhancing mucosal exposure, although additional efficacy and mechanistic studies will be required to establish the therapeutic relevance of the observed concentrations.

Interestingly, a recent study showed that an anti-cMet dIgA molecule was effective in reducing renal cyst growth in preclinical ADPKD model, even without hepatic knockdown of pIgR, possibly due to significant upregulation of pIgR in cystic kidneys.16 This work showed that after a single intraperitoneal (IP) injection of 20 mg/kg of antibody, approximately 10% of the total antibody could be recovered from cystic fluid after 72 h. These data elegantly highlight that pIgR engagement-based enrichment can be used to safely administer antibodies that would otherwise pose dose limiting systemic toxicity to tissues of therapeutic interest. Kidney cysts are reported to be driven by growth factor receptor signaling, in particular by EGFR, but systemic administration of anti-EGFR antibodies like cetuximab results in significant toxicity due to high expression in skin and other healthy tissues.14,25 Thus, targeting anti-growth factor receptor antibodies to cyst lumen via pIgR engagement represents an attractive therapeutic option that can simultaneously limit toxicity and overcome manufacturing challenges associated with IgA-based antibodies. Although pIgR expression was observed in renal tubule epithelium of both humans and cynomolgus monkeys, staining in Bowman’s capsule was more prominent in human tissue. Although our study suggests that the use of pIgR-engaging bsAbs could be used to target a number of mucosal diseases, further studies using ex vivo disease models will further inform the utility of this approach.

This work shows that pIgR-targeting bsAbs can enrich in the mucosa bounded by pIgR expressing tissues rapidly and to therapeutically relevant levels. Specifically, we show that pIgR-targeting bsAbs enrich in lung mucosa and rapidly deplete from tissues where pIgR is highly expressed, including gastrointestinal and kidney tissue. These results suggest that the enrichment can enable therapeutically relevant exposure levels at low doses, which can enable safer, more efficacious treatment of mucosal diseases. The rapid rates of clearance of pIgR-engaging bsAbs suggest a possible therapeutic mechanism of clearing pathogenic IgG or other toxic species from blood.

Materials and methods

Transcriptomic analysis of pIgR expression

All transcriptomic data and analysis went through the standard data processing and pipeline, unless indicated differently in the species-specific sections. Standard RNA isolation techniques were used to extract RNA from tissues. RNA quality was assessed using RNA Integrity Number (RIN) score (to assess RNA degradation; score > 7) and spectrophotometric measurements (to assess RNA purity; A260/A280 ratio > 1.7). Raw fastq files were mapped to the species-specific refence genome and gene model using the standard Omicsoft pipeline. The filtered and trimmed reads (100 bp) were aligned and quantified using Omicsoft’s Array Studio NGS module. The underlining alignment algorithm Omicsoft Sequence Aligner: OSA4.0, and the gene/transcript quantification module is an Omicsoft implementation of the RSEM algorithm. The gene expression of each bulk RNA-Seq sample was reported as TPM. Raw fastq files from public data sets were obtained from GTEx V8 or GEO (Gene Expression Omnibus) (GSE99248, GSE112155, GSE137619, GSE149099) using fastq-dump from SRA Toolkit. RNA-Seq alignment and quantification utilized the human B38 genome build from Omicsoft, and Omicsoft GenCode. Bulk RNA-seq was performed on 5 males and 5 female health C57BL/6J mouse from the Jackson Labs (Bar Harbor, ME). Animals were approximately 7–8 weeks of age. Additional data from GEO (GSE54651, GSE90755, GSE125483 and GSE135415) using fastq-dump from SRA Toolkit were incorporated. RNA-Seq alignment and quantification utilized the Mouse.B38 genome and OmicsoftGenCode.V24 gene model. Bulk RNA-seq was performed on 5 sexually matured male and females Chinese or Cambodian Cynomolgus monkey sourced from Charles River Laboratories. All animal procedures were performed under Charles River – Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC)-approved protocol and in accordance with applicable institutional and regulatory animal welfare requirements. RIN scores for some tissues had to be lowered to >6 to maintain enough samples. RNA-Seq alignment and quantification utilized the Macaca_fascicularis_6.0 genome and Enaembl.R106 gene model.

IHC assay method development

Six cell lines were selected based on endogenous pIgR RNA expression levels from the publicly available Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (https://sites.broadinstitute.org/ccle/) and European Genome-phenome Archive’s Genentech (https://ega-archive.org/dacs/EGAC00001000055) data sets (Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Figure S1). The chosen cell lines serve as qualified reagent controls with either positive or negative pIgR expression. For preparation of the cell line controls, cultures were grown to subconfluence, harvested non-enzymatically, and pelleted by centrifugation. Cell pellets were fixed with 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 hours before routine processing and paraffin embedding. All cell pellets were then arrayed into a single paraffin block. Endogenous tissue controls were selected from the Johnson and Johnson Innovative Medicine Investigative Pathology tissue repository due to the presence or absence of pIgR expressing cells. These included samples of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) human normal duodenum, kidney, urinary bladder, lymph node, tonsil, and liver. Equivalent tissue regions from cynomolgus monkey were used to confirm cross-reactivity of the antibody with this species.

Qualified FFPE human cell pellets and quality controlled normal human and monkey tissues were used to characterize the specificity and sensitivity of the rabbit monoclonal anti-pIgR antibody clone [EPR23314-256] (Abcam, cat. Ab275020). The exact peptide sequence that was used to generate the rabbit monoclonal ant-pIgR antibody clone [EPR23314-256] is proprietary information to Abcam. Abcam published that the rabbit recombinant monoclonal antibody was generated using their RabMAb® technology and targets a sequence corresponding to UniProt: P01833. A bioinformatic alignment of the human and cynomolgus monkey pIgR protein showed 89% identity between the two species (BLAST-P, NIH, Uniprot ID P01833: human pIgR, and accession number EHH50590.1: cynomolgus monkey pIgR). The IHC assay was developed on the Leica Bond RX autostainer (Leica Biosystems, Buffalo Grove, IL). The anti-pIgR antibody clone [EPR23314-256] presented acceptable IHC specificity and sensitivity and, therefore, was used for the detection of endogenous expression of pIgR protein in tissue sections. FFPE cells and tissues were sectioned at 4 µm and mounted onto SuperFrost Plus glass microscopy slides (VWR, cat. 48,311–703). Briefly, unbaked glass slides were loaded into the autostainer and deparaffinized following the default Leica deparaffinization protocol. Heat-mediated antigen retrieval was performed using an EDTA-based solution (pH ~ 9.0) at 100°C for 20 minutes, followed by pretreatment of the slides with endogenous peroxidase blocking solution for 10 minutes. Slides were incubated with the rabbit monoclonal anti-pIgR [EPR23314-256] primary antibody at a dilution of 1/3,000 (final concentration 0.164 µg/mL) in Primary Antibody diluent (Leica Biosystems, cat. AR9352) for 30 minutes. After extensive washing steps, the primary antibody was detected using the chromogenic Leica Refine DAB detection kit (Leica Biosystems, cat. DS9800), according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The slides were mounted with glass coverslips and examined using bright-field microscopy.

The pIgR expression profile in human and monkey tissue was conducted on an extensive list of quality-controlled human and monkey normal tissue microarrays (TMA) composed of n ≥ 3 donors per tissue location. In total, 180 human and 240 monkey tissue cores were included in the assessment. In addition to the TMAs, full-format tissue sections of human normal larynx, pharynx, and oropharynx were included in the assessment due to the presence of mature squamous epithelium. Each human FFPE tissue was commercially sourced from Johnson and Johnson approved vendors, including ProteoGenix, BioIVT, and BioOptoins. Human tissues were obtained as de-identified biospecimens with supplier-documented ethical procurement and donor consent, as applicable, and checked for anatomical location accuracy and lack of histopathology by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining prior to inclusion in the assessment. Cynomolgus monkey tissues were obtained from Charles River Laboratories. All animal procedures were performed under Charles River – Nevada Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC)-approved protocol and in accordance with applicable institutional and regulatory animal welfare requirements. Proteins and analysis

All recombinant antibodies, as described in Figure 5, were expressed transiently in ExpiCHO-S cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Culture supernatants were harvested and filtered, and titers were measured by surface biolayer interferometry using protein A-based biosensor tips (Octet, Sartorius) according to the manufacturer’s protocols and using a wild-type IgG1 antibody (anti-RSV clone B21M) as a control.26 Antibodies were affinity-purified using MabSelect SuRe resin (Cytiva) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Analytical SEC was performed using a G3000SWxl column (Tosoh Bioscience) in phosphate-buffered saline.

Binding was measured between Abs and either pIgR or hemagglutinin by immobilizing antibodies to anti-Fc biosensors (Sartorius). Antibody-coated tips were then exposed to each antigen for a 90 ‑second association step, followed by a 90 ‑second dissociation step. Association and dissociation rates were measured by the shift in wavelength (nm). All proteins and antigens were diluted to a concentration of 10 mg/mL in 1 X phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 25°C. Data were collected with Octet Data Acquisition (Sartorius) and analyzed using Octet Data Analysis (Sartorius). Data were processED using Graphpad Prism software.

Cynomolgus monkey PK and biodistribution

We evaluated pIgR-based transcytosis of the bsAb in the mucosa of lung, kidney, and GI tissues of cynomolgus monkey. Animals were given a single 5 mg/kg intravenous dose of antibody. We collected BAL to determine the biodistribution of the pIgR bsAb across the lung epithelium relative to its serum levels. Additionally, we also collected lung, various sections of the GI tract – duodenum, jejunum and ileum, and harvested kidneys at 48 and 312 h post dose to determine the biodistribution of pIgR bsAb across the tissue epithelium. In this study 14 cynomolgus macaques were split into two groups − 8 received the pIgR bsAb (FM1B104) and 6 received the control bsAb (FM1B108) via slow bolus intravenous administration. For PK serum sample collection, 0.5 mL blood was collected into 2.5 mL BD Vacutainer® SSTTM gel tubes pre-dose and at 0.5, 6, 24, 48, 72-, 168-, 240-, and 312-hours post-dose. Serum was isolated by centrifuging the tubes at room temperature at a speed of 2700 g for 10 minutes. The BAL procedure was performed on the sedated animals by the “chair method.” The animal was placed in dorsal recumbency in the chair channel. The handle at the chair base was slid up against the animal to keep the animal from sliding down the chair channel. Once the handle is in position supporting the animal’s weight, the handle was then tightened into position. A red rubber feeding tube was premeasured and marked before placement. The animal head was tilted back and down below the edge of the chair channel. A red rubber feeding tube was then inserted into the animal’s trachea via a laryngoscope during inspiration. The tube was placed into correct positioning, and the BAL wash procedure was executed as normal procedure would indicate. A total of 10 mL saline was flushed through the tube. The collected BAL samples were placed immediately onto wet ice and processed for isolation of fluid. For this procedure, the tube was centrifuged (8 min. 300–400 ×g) at 4°C, the supernatant removed, and aliquots were prepared (3× 1 mL in Saline and the remaining as a single aliquot) and cryopreserved until shipment. The animals were staggered in two groups to alternate BAL collection resulting in combined timepoints at pre-dose and at 24, 48, 72, 168, 240, and 312- hours post-dose. BAL concentrations were reported as measured concentrations obtained from the recovered lavage fluid. No correction for epithelial lining fluid dilution or lavage recovery was applied. All animals were subjected to the same BAL collection and processing procedures to enable relative comparisons between treatment groups. The two groups were also alternated to perform scheduled necropsies for the following tissue collections – lungs, liver, kidney, GI (duodenum, ileum, and jejunum). The tissues were excised, rinsed with saline and blotted dry, weighed, and placed in suitable screw Nunc tubes with O-rings. Samples were snap‑frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C.

The concentrations of FM1B104 and FM1B108 in cynomolgus monkey serum, BAL fluid, and tissues were determined using MSD immunoassays on MSD GOLD 96-well Streptavidin SECTOR Microplate (MSD, Cat# L15SA). Fresh standard curves were prepared by serial dilution for each antibody in 100% naïve matrix (serum, BAL fluid and 8–10 mg/mL tissue homogenate). Frozen quality controls prepared in 100% naive matrix were diluted and tested with each assay. Briefly, plates were blocked with Superblock buffer (Scytek, Cat# 65,276) for 60 min and washed with 0.05% Tween-20 in PBS. A master mix containing 0.5 µg/mL biotinylated goat anti-human IgG (Bethyl Labs, Cat# A80-319B-15) and 0.5 µg/mL sulfo-tag conjugated goat anti-human IgG (Southern Biotech, Cat# 2049–01) was prepared in the assay buffer (1% bovine serum albumin and 0.5% Tween-20 in PBS). 120 µL/well of master mix was combined with 30 µL/well reference standards, quality controls, and samples and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with shaking in a transfer plate. From this mixture, 50 µL/well was transferred to the MSD plate in duplicate and incubated for 1 hr. After washing, 150 µL/well 1X Read buffer (MSD, Cat# R92TC-1) was added, and signal was acquired using a Meso Sector S600 imager. Data was analyzed with Watson LIMS 7.4.2 (Thermo Scientific) using a five-parameter logistic fit with 1/Y2 weighting. The quantifiable curve ranges were 5–5000 ng/mL for serum (assay sensitivity of 5 ng/mL), and 5–2000 ng/mL for BAL fluid (assay sensitivity of 5 ng/mL). Tissue samples were weighed and homogenized in radio-immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Thermo, Cat# 89,901) with protease inhibitor (Thermo, Cat# A32955), and total protein concentration was determined with a BCA assay (Thermo, Cat# 23,227). Tissue lysates were normalized to 8–10 mg/mL before MSD measurement. The quantifiable curve range for tissue lysates was 5–2000 ng/mL. To calculate the tissue drug concentration, we multiplied the antibody concentration measured in the tissue lysates by the total volume used to generate the final normalized sample. The resulting total amount of antibody per sample was then divided by the tissue wet weight to determine the antibody concentration in the tissue (µg/g).

PK analysis

PK parameters were derived by noncompartmental analysis using Phoenix WinNonlin (Certara, Princeton, NJ). Terminal half-life (t½) was estimated from the terminal log-linear phase of the concentration-time profile. Area under the concentration-time curve (AUC) was calculated using the linear-up/log-down trapezoidal method. Clearance (CL) and volume of distribution (Vd) were determined using standard noncompartmental methods for intravenous administration. Concentrations below the lower limit of quantitation were excluded from parameter estimation as appropriate.
