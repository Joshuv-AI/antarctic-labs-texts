Imaging thick objects with deep-subangstrom resolution and deep-subpicometer precision
Authors: Wenfeng Yang, Haozhi Sha, Jizhe Cui, Rong Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Size effects are ubiquitous in the structural, mechanical, and physical properties of materials, making it highly desirable to study the intrinsic properties of thick objects through high-resolution structural analysis in transmission electron microscopy. Although deep-subangstrom resolution has been achieved with multislice electron ptychography, the sample thickness is typically very limited. By combining energy filtering and extended local-orbital ptychography (eLOP) that retrieves varying aberrations during electron beam scanning, here, we report ptychographic reconstructions for silicon as thick as 85 nanometers, approximately three times larger than usual thickness threshold for conventional multislice electron ptychography. The elimination of aberration variations contributes to accurate reconstructions with an information limit of 18 picometers and atomic position precision of 0.39 picometers. Accurate ptychographic reconstructions for thick objects can facilitate the discovery or interpretation of intrinsic structural and physical phenomena in solids, which is of great importance in physics, chemistry, materials science, and semiconductor device engineering.

INTRODUCTION

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (1) is a widely used technique for real-space imaging of solids at high spatial resolution. With the development of ptychographic algorithms (2–7), TEM is increasingly used with ptychographic phase retrieval for ultrahigh spatial resolution and high phase accuracy. On a modern transmission electron microscope equipped with an aberration corrector (8, 9), the information limit of electron ptychography reached 39 pm in 2018 (10), 23 pm in 2021 (11), and 14 pm in 2023 (7). Recently, an information limit of 41 pm for two-dimensional (2D) materials was achieved using an uncorrected electron microscope (12). Deep-subangstrom-resolution (<0.5 Å) imaging with high phase accuracy helps to accurately characterize the microstructure of materials (13), e.g., orientation mapping around dislocation kinks in a metal oxide (14), lattice-resolved antiferromagnetic imaging (15), subangstrom resolution imaging of beam-sensitive materials (16–18), measuring atomic positions with subpicometer precision in a polar metal (19), measuring the concentration of oxygen vacancies in a superconductor (20), imaging polar vortex in twist SrTiO3 (21), and counting and positioning single atoms of interstitial oxygen in a metal (22).

Atomic resolution imaging usually requires very thin samples, typically less than 10 nm for aberration-corrected TEM imaging and 20 nm for scanning TEM (STEM) imaging. High-quality ptychographic reconstructions also necessitate a sample thickness less than 20 nm for dense solids such as metals, ceramics, and semiconductors. The thickness threshold of electron ptychography of dense solids reached 21 nm in 2021 (11), 34 nm in 2024 (14), and 50 nm in 2024 (7). Size effects are pervasive in the structural, mechanical, and physical properties of materials. The reduction of sample thickness often results in altered structural or dynamical behaviors due to strain relaxation, surface diffusion and damage, or even phase transitions. Excessively thin samples pose substantial challenges for the reliability of structural analysis in TEM. To obtain intrinsic structures of solids, it is highly desirable to use samples that are as thick as possible for accurate structural analysis.

In this study, we developed a method named extended local-orbital ptychography (eLOP) that enables the variation of aberrations as the electron beam scans across the sample. It should be noted that eLOP and its precursor (LOP) (7) are phase retrieval methods of unknown structures that starts from scratch. They differ from the method proposed in the reference (23), which involve the structural refinement of known initial models. By combining the eLOP method with energy-filtering, ptychographic reconstructions are realized for 85-nm-thick Si and 60-nm-thick SrTiO3, with the information limit of 18 pm for Si and 16 pm for SrTiO3. The atomic position precisions for Si and SrTiO3 are 0.39 and 0.42 pm, respectively, both reaching the deep-subpicometer (<0.5 pm) region.

RESULTS

Probe aberrations negatively affect high-resolution imaging of objects. In general, the problem is more severe for thick objects due to the larger focus range of the probe within the objects in the STEM mode. Conventional pixelated ptychography (CPP) describes the probe as pixel arrays and optimizes the probe to eliminate the influence of aberrations, which are assumed to be constant during probe scanning. However, aberrations often vary in experiments. eLOP describes the probe as position-dependent aberration functions, effectively eliminating the influence of aberration variations on ptychography (see Materials and Methods for details). An accurate probe description is essential for reconstructing thick samples and precisely determining atomic positions.

Simulated datasets were used to demonstrate the ability of the eLOP method for the reconstruction of thick objects. For comparison, reconstructions using the CPP method were also performed. The multislice method (25) as implemented in the μSTEM code (26) was used. The 4D-STEM datasets for various thicknesses were generated, and ptychographic reconstructions were carried out. Typical diffraction patterns are shown in fig. S6. Each diffraction pattern was padded to 256 × 256 pixels for better real-space sampling during the reconstructions. For the CPP method, we used the EMPTY program, which implements the multislice ptychography with adaptive propagator (6).

The maximum thickness reconstructed by eLOP is at least 60 nm, whereas by CPP is 40 nm, as shown in Fig. 2 (A and B). The reason of larger thickness threshold of eLOP can be attributed to the robustness of the parameterized probe and object. The probe in CPP is composed of uncorrelated pixels. For 50- and 60-nm-thick objects, the probe cannot converge to a well-defined shape during the optimization. In contrast, the probe reconstructed via eLOP consistently maintains a well-defined shape, which benefits from the parameterization of the probe. Additionally, the object in eLOP is more robust than that in CPP. Although a 40-nm-thick object can be reconstructed using CPP, the sample thickness must be known a priori and fixed during the reconstruction; otherwise, the method diverges, resulting in unsuccessful reconstruction. In contrast, eLOP allows the sample thickness to be optimized during reconstruction. This capability is highly advantageous, as the sample thickness is usually unknown in experiments.

DISCUSSION

By combining eLOP with energy filtering, deep-subangstrom resolution and deep-subpicometer precision have been achieved for dense solids as thick as 85 nm. This method alleviates the demanding requirements of electron ptychography on sample thickness, paving the way for ultrahigh-resolution imaging and accurate analysis of intrinsic structures and phenomena in engineering materials and semiconductor devices.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Details of eLOP

Because the aberration of the probe will change during the experiments, the variation of aberration coefficients at different scanning positions needs to be considered in the LOP algorithm. In this work, the probe at the jth position is expressed asPj(r)=FA(k)eiχ(k,j)(1)where A(k) is the aperture function. The scanning position–dependent aberration function χ(k,j) is written as (8, 28, 29)χ(k,j)=ℝ12ωω¯C1(j)+12ω¯2A1(j)+ω2ω¯B2(j)+13ω¯3A2(j)+14ω2ω¯2C3(j)+…(2)where ℝ{·} means taking the real part of the matrix in the bracket. ω=kx+i∗ky, where kx and ky are coordinates in reciprocal space, respectively. ω¯ is the complex conjugate matrix of ω. C1(j),A1(j),B2(j),A2(j), and C3(j) are the mapping of aberration coefficients.

To account for the noncircular aperture shape, an elliptical aperture was used in this study. Additionally, acquiring a diffraction pattern of the vacuum region helps obtain a more accurate aperture shape.

Materials and sample preparation

The SrTiO3, Si, and BaTiO3 single crystals were purchased from Hefei Kejing Materials Technology Co. Ltd. The STEM samples were prepared using the standard lift-out method by focus ion beam (FIB) in a Zeiss Auriga FIB instrument.

Experimental details and data processing

EELS experiments were carried out using an FEI Titan3 Themis 60-300 instrument. The spectrum image data and the annular dark-field signal were acquired with a beam current of about 50 pA, a convergence semiangle of 25 mrad. The measured thickness-to-λ ratio of area 1 in Fig. 3A is 0.26 nm−1. Given that the reconstructed thickness is 30 nm, the used inelastic mean free path of SrTiO3 is 115 nm.

The 4D-STEM diffraction datasets were acquired using an electron microscope pixel array detector (K3) installed on a probe aberration–corrected JEM-ARM300F2 GRAND ARM2 atomic resolution analytical microscope operated at 300 kV. An energy-selecting slit with a width of 5 eV was inserted with the zero loss peak at the center to exclude most inelastically scattered electrons in diffraction patterns. The convergence semiangle was set to 24.3 mrad. Each diffraction pattern has a dimension of 128 × 128, and the camera length is 1.5 cm, giving a reciprocal pixel size of 0.035 Å−1 (0.68 mrad). To achieve substantial overlap, the defocus and scanning step size of both SrTiO3 and silicon are 30 and 0.03 nm, respectively. The thickness of SrTiO3 is 30 to 60 nm. The thickness of silicon is 85 nm.

The defocus, thickness, scanning step size, and reciprocal pixel size of BaTiO3 dataset are 25 nm, 25 nm, 0.04 nm, and 0.031 Å−1, respectively. All data that support the findings of this study have been deposited in Zenodo (30).
