Background

Cancer continues to pose a significant global health challenge, with nearly 20 million new cases reported worldwide in 2020 alone, maintaining its status as one of the leading causes of death (Cyclotrons 2024). The pivotal role of diagnostic radiology and nuclear medicine in cancer screening, therapy planning, response evaluation, and long-term surveillance cannot be overstated. Annually, over 3600 million diagnostic radiology examinations are conducted worldwide (Cyclotrons 2024).

In the field of medical and scientific research, radionuclides are used to investigate various physiological and pathological processes. These radionuclides find applications in diagnostic imaging, therapeutic interventions, and translational research, bridging the gap between laboratory discoveries and clinical applications. In Europe, the demand for novel radionuclides (R&D in progress, pre-clinical, clinical trials) has grown significantly, driven by the continuous evolution of medical technologies and the increasing emphasis on personalized and precision medicine.

Radionuclide production involves the creation of radioactive nuclei through various methods, such as via the use of nuclear reactors, particle accelerators or radionuclide generators. The regular production and supply of radionuclides is essential for routine molecular imaging techniques like Positron Emission Tomography (PET) or Single-Photon Emission Computed Tomography (SPECT), as well as for Targeted Radionuclide Therapy (TRT). However, until a radionuclide becomes available commercially, the routine production of a novel radionuclide is a complex process that requires a close collaboration between nuclear physicists, engineers, radiochemists, biologists and medical professionals to reach and maintain a sustainable, safe and efficient source of supply.

Addressing the demand for high-purity radionuclides is crucial in medical applications. For that purpose, PRISMAP - Production of high purity Isotopes by mass Separation for Medical Application - the European medical radionuclide programme (prismap.eu) was created to bring together production facilities including intense neutron sources, an isotope mass separation facility, high-power accelerators, biomedical research institutes, and hospitals. This collaborative effort, involving 23 beneficiaries from 13 countries and including one international organization, strives to simplify access for a diverse user community, mirroring the USA National Isotope Development Centre (NIDC). Notably, key associations such as the European Association of Nuclear Medicine (EANM) and the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) provide support to the PRISMAP programme.

The main goal of PRISMAP is to provide a sustainable source of high purity grade new radionuclides for medicine, involving from the onset upcoming major European infrastructures, to provide a single-entry point for all researchers active in this field including SMEs, global pharma, nuclear centres, hospitals and universities, using standardised access procedures. The new isotope enrichment and standardisation techniques triggered in PRISMAP will expand our services and provide them to yet unreachable remote European laboratories. PRISMAP thus strives to create a paradigm shift in the early phase research on radiopharmaceuticals, targeted drugs for cancer – one of the major diseases in Europe – theranostics and personalised medicine, shaping the European isotope landmark as a gold standard to accelerate the development of the pharma industry and ultimately of a better healthcare for the improvement of our citizens’ life.

Survey methodology

A survey targeting the latest trends mainly focused on the demand, the production and the distribution of non-routinely used medical radionuclides for use in research, and for pre-clinical and clinical trials was created. This survey has been disseminated through the PRISMAP community, to professional associations of radiology, nuclear medicine, as well as to preclinical and clinical end users across Europe. PRISMAP brings together a consortium of 23 beneficiaries from 13 countries, one European Research Laboratory and an International Organisation, but the survey was disseminated also to other institutions and companies that are not official members of PRISMAP. Therefore, the situation of innovative radionuclides production in Europe was identified from these institutions which completed questionnaires.

The PRISMAP survey was initially distributed from January to September 2022, with ongoing data collection extended beyond this period. The survey aimed to pinpoint significant participants in the industrial, research, pre-clinical, and clinical sectors related to PRISMAP. Its goal was to explore the requirements of end users in nuclear medicine from industrial, scientific, and clinical backgrounds. The findings were employed to assess existing facility details, geographical presence, capacities, licensing, operational aspects, ongoing and upcoming research and development plans, obstacles, and possibilities for enhanced cooperation and advancement.

For each audience questions were divided in several parts. There were some common questions but other parts of questions were for each group separately. For radiopharmaceutical producers general topics were - general information, current status of radionuclide production, future plans of radionuclide production, logistics and distribution, research and development activities.

For preclinical/clinical users and research institutions questions were - general information, information about equipment they have and radiopharmaceuticals and theranostic pairs they used routinely, and also radionuclides and theranostic pairs they would be interested to use in the next 2–5 years; research and development activities.

The current status and needs of clinical end-users in nuclear medicine were identified from 114 respondents among which 104 European companies and institutions completed the PRISMAP survey, representing 30 countries. Although the survey gathered data about pre-clinical and clinical research and applications, the current report discusses only the radionuclide demand, production and distribution for research to pre-clinical research purposes only (Radzina 2023).

Novel radionuclides

Typical or conventional medical radionuclides in this article are defined as those currently routinely produced by well-established technologies in the industry. The most commonly used PET radionuclides are 11C, 13N, 15O, 18F (Synowiecki et al. 2018). More recently68Ga has found its way widely into clinical practice and is becoming another standard for PET imaging (Radzina 2020). Typical SPECT radionuclides that have already found their way into daily clinical use are,99mTc, 111In, 123I. 90Y, 131I, 177Lu, 223Ra (Synowiecki et al. 2018; Ziadi et al. 2010; Jia 2022). Although there is a vast number of radionuclides suitable for medical applications, this article mainly focuses on innovative radionuclides offered via the PRISMAP consortium. The PRISMAP portfolio consists of 28 radionuclides, which have been identified for their potential in diagnostic applications or in targeted radionuclide therapy, in particular targeted alpha therapy and Auger-electron therapy. As proven by the end-user survey, there is (amongst the research user community) an increasing demand for the alpha emitting radionuclide 225Ac, which has been indicated as being produced by three of the survey respondents. The PRISMAP consortium members offer 225Ac to the users from two sites - JRC Karlsruhe and CERN-MEDICIS. The aim to indicate more sites that would offer such increasing demand for radionuclides is still an open objective with high importance.

Respondents of 44 institutions mentioned additional radionuclides they consider producing or obtaining in following years by external suppliers for any of their research and development activities. Most often mentioned radionuclides are − 44Sc, 47Sc, 64Cu, 67Cu, 89Zr, 149Tb, 152Tb, 155Tb, 161Tb, 165Er/169Er, 177Lu, 188Re, 211At, 225Ac.

In recent years, the Tb radionuclide “family” and 47Sc/44Sc, 64Cu, 67Cu, 89Zr, 177Lu, 211At and others have become the main research topics towards theranostic/therapeutic use. Also identified as important is 225Ac as an alpha emitter in radionuclide therapy and radiopharmaceutical development, or as generator for 213Bi, and the interest for such is only increasing. Therefore, supply of these radionuclides should address the growing demand.

Current status of production of radionuclides in the European region

The production of radionuclides in the European region holds paramount significance in advancing medical diagnostics and therapeutic applications, particularly within the realm of nuclear medicine. Understanding the current status of radionuclide production is essential for assessing the region’s capabilities and addressing the burgeoning demands for cutting-edge medical radionuclides.

According to the survey (Fig. 2), as expected, the main approach for radionuclide production includes cyclotrons (17 respondents), radionuclide generators (7 respondents) and nuclear reactors (8 respondents). Additionally, 3 respondents indicated the use of non-conventional production techniques, such as the high-energy proton activation coupled with off-line mass separation used at CERN-MEDICIS, as well as extraction from natural thorium and accelerator-driven subcritical neutron sources. None of the surveyed respondents indicated any other source for radionuclide production such as high-energy electron linear accelerators.

Radionuclide production source among respondents

Radionuclide production by cyclotrons

According to the IAEA database (Cyclotrons 2024) there are at least 348 cyclotrons in Europe, with maximal energy ranging from 3 MeV (for deuterons) up to 590 MeV for protons, however, not all of them are used for medical radionuclide production.

Cyclotrons in the proton energy range of 16–18 MeV dedicated for conventional medical radionuclide production of 11C, 15O, 18F, 68Ga and other standard PET radionuclides are the most common. Main manufacturers of cyclotrons are IBA, GE, Siemens and NIIEFA.

Among the surveyed European accelerators, a total of 40 present the capability to generate proton beams of 30 MeV or higher energies. This characteristic renders them well-suited for the unconventional production of radionuclides. Additionally, the survey identifies seventeen cyclotrons that possess the capacity to generate proton beams within the 60–100 MeV range. These specific cyclotrons are ideal for producing more specialized radionuclides that necessitate elevated incident particle energies.

One noteworthy example is the high-energy proton accelerator at the Paul Scherrer Institute (PSI) in Switzerland, which can achieve proton energies of up to 590 MeV. However, it is important to mention that this survey does not encompass high-energy accelerators like synchrotrons, such as CERN’s Proton Synchrotron Booster (PSB) operating at 1.4 GeV.

According to the survey results main radionuclides produced are 68Ga, 64Cu, 11C, 18F.

Radionuclide distribution and logistics

According to this study, the predominant mode of transportation for radioactive materials is by road, accounting for 54% of respondents, closely followed by air transport, which constitutes 43% of the responses. The utilization of sea transportation is relatively limited, with only a small contribution of 3% from three respondents. The infrequent use of sea transport could be potentially attributed to challenges in establishing time-efficient logistical chains. Sea transportation might only prove effective when connecting two ports nearby, such as countries situated around the Baltic Sea or within intricate supply networks. The survey highlights that road transport predominantly occurs on a national level and is occasionally extended to short-distance international distribution. For longer distances or the transportation of short-lived radionuclides, air transport emerges as the most efficient method.

Several key airports across Europe have been identified that serve as pivotal points for radioactive material transport. These include AMS, BHX, BLQ, BRU, CDG, CPH, GVA, FCA, HAJ, HEL, LEJ, LHR, LIS, MAD, OSL, PRG, RIX, TLL, VCE, VNC, ZRH and some in the USA. These airports are significant for establishing supply chains, as they accommodate shipments of radioactive packages.

Survey results show that half of respondents that supply institutes externally already have international distribution routes established (Fig. 3). The longer distance distribution routes, such as between different continents are especially noteworthy. Substantial part of respondents, totaling 31, subcontract the transportation of class 7 radioactive materials to specialized transport companies. This includes both, institutions that produce and receive the radioactive packages.

Radionuclide distribution from European manufacturers to recipients (by countries)

Moreover, according to a Lancet Oncology Commission data, accessibility of theranostic capabilities is insufficient. This commission was created with the aim to address the important issue of access to the promising field of theranostics at a global level.

Despite advances of theranostic pharmaceuticals, there is little information regarding the access and avail- ability of theranostic capabilities in countries around the world. It is found out that he production of radioisotopes for imaging and therapy is concentrated in a few countries (e.g., 99Mo, 111In, 131I, 177Lu, and 225Ac in countries including the USA, Canada, South Africa, Australia, Germany, Belgium, Russia, and the Netherlands), creating supply chain challenges. The main reasons for the low availability of theranostic treatments are primarily access to radio- pharmaceuticals, trained workforce, little infrastructure available for treatments, and insufficient funding for treating patients with theranostics (Abdel-Wahab et al. 2024).

Out of 24 respondents that export the produced radionuclides, radionuclide generators or radiopharmaceuticals, 10 respondent institutions indicated the use of an external company as distributor, for example, Curium, PerkinElmer, Terumo, Nucligen. Tendency for larger radionuclide manufacturing facilities to sub-contract towards external distributors is observed. Such approach eases the acquisition of radionuclides by the users and operations by the manufacturers.

Most of the novel radionuclides within the PRISMAP portfolio do not benefit yet from the experience already gained on the more common medical radionuclides. However, the existing experience can be used as a backbone to build knowledge, identify gaps and possibilities, and create opportunities. The first PRISMAP projects have given the opportunity to test the logistic chains across Europe between multiple production facilities and end users. The findings have been reported by the PRISMAP consortium (Jensen 2023).

Identified distribution and legislation limitations

Radioactive material shipment, particularly of short-lived (few hours to days) medical radionuclides, is difficult and a time critical procedure. The international transport of novel or previously unused radionuclides between a production site and research centers poses even more difficulties due to incomplete or sometimes even absent national legislation on import/export, allowed activity limits, operator licensing, information regarding the radionuclide and its impurities and decay products. One of the possible approaches to address this is to proceed via regional clusters where all facilities are in reach of each other, such as the PRISMAP Alpine cluster (CERN, CHUV, ILL, PSI), the Belgian cluster (KU Leuven, SCK CEN), or the Polish cluster (NCBJ).

Fragmented and unharmonized procedures for import/export legislation of radionuclides across Europe and beyond were identified in the survey by 9 respondents as well as complexities in supply chain by 6 and licensing by 5 respondents respectively. Two respondents indicated other constraints such as cost of transportation. For longer international radionuclide shipments, customs clearance was also mentioned as an issue and due to some radionuclide short half-lives, such delay can limit their use in nuclear medicine applications. Harmonized legislation and standards not only for radionuclide distribution but also for clinical translation therefore is one of main issues already indicated, discussed, and targeted within the PRISMAP consortium (Decristoforo 2022).

Exploring multiple possible distribution methods within PRISMAP showed the possibility of supplying mass-separated radionuclide sample “door to door” from CERN-MEDICIS to a recipient in Denmark within 4 h, which includes shipment of the sample as well as preparation of necessary documents and customs clearance. However, such a distribution mechanism is rather expensive if not optimized with multiple sample transports.

Some constraints on the availability and distribution of radionuclides/radiopharmaceuticals after 1st of March, 2022 after a Russian invasion in Ukraine were reported by 11 respondents, mostly indicating the shutdown of partner institutes that supply them with radionuclides.

Respondents also indicate the main problems with air cargo transport, which are limitations due to the dose rate of the radiation, shielding of the radioactive source and packaging.

Target material supply constraints

The production of medical radionuclides used in theragnostic applications is mainly performed by irradiating target materials with charged particles accelerated in cyclotrons, linear accelerators, or synchrotrons or by neutrons in reactors. A key element for pure medical radionuclide production is the choice of appropriate target material minimizing or excluding unwanted contaminant production. Novel medical radionuclide production strongly depends on the availability of enriched materials. Some enriched target material availability is currently scarce, and radionuclide manufacturers in Europe face strong dependency on external suppliers and manufacturers (Cocolios 2022). This is reflected in the survey as the most common issue for 28 respondents. And due to availability subsequently the delivery time is indicated as secondary bottleneck for 15 respondents.

Due to limited enriched target material availability, radionuclide production sites either focus on different radionuclide production, radiotracer synthesis or outsource desired radionuclide precursor supply from other institutions within reasonable distances and optimized supply routes. Alternative enrichment method investigation could decrease the shortage. A method for enrichment of calcium and titanium isotopes by laser-enhanced isotopically selective condensation technique for the production of 43,44,47Sc radionuclides for theragnostic applications is currently being explored within PRISMAP (Bernerd et al. 2023; Cocolios 2023).

Future perspective

Out of 64 respondents from research institutions and manufacturing facilities, 19 respondent companies/institutions plan to export or extend the already existing export region of produced radionuclides in the following years. Ten respondents were interested within Europe only, 8 respondents were interested not only in Europe but also in the rest of the world and 1 respondent - outside Europe only. Involvement in an early stage of establishing these routes may help foster the radionuclide distribution as well as give the ability for users to gain access to desired radionuclides in a more foreseeable timeframe.

Furthermore, 30 respondent companies/institutions plan to increase manufactured volume of radionuclides in the following years. Even without plans to expand, main limitations that respondents face or foresee are limitations in infrastructure (15 respondents), shortage of staff, expertise (15 respondents), production capacity and target material supply (11 respondents each) and other financial or managerial issues (3 respondents). Possibilities to teach and train students, staff and to transfer knowledge, as indicated by responders, is one of the main limitations, which can be addressed through more open communication between institutions. As such, this is one of the PRISMAP objectives currently being addressed. Some respondents do not foresee any issues yet due to low or manageable demand at the moment.

Fifty respondents are involved in radiopharmaceutical synthesis, production and/or research. Out of these 50 respondents, 20 companies/institutions distribute radiopharmaceuticals to external research institutes or users (pre-clinical and clinical research/applications). Four from these 20 respondents distributed radiopharmaceuticals to international recipients within EU and outside EU borders.

In general, radiopharmaceutical distribution is even more demanding in terms of legal compliance than radionuclide or radiopharmaceutical precursor distribution. Often, radiopharmaceuticals for pre-clinical and clinical applications are produced in-house. Therefore, we can conclude that the radiopharmaceuticals distributed at national and international level are labelled mainly with radionuclides outside the scope of PRISMAP. Nevertheless, the existing distribution routes and expertise in legislation and regulations are of great interest for the emerging radiopharmaceuticals coming into clinical trial phase.

From responses of the respondents, we can conclude the necessity of PRISMAP not only as a web-based entry platform for the production and dispatch of non-conventional radionuclides, but also as a long-term platform for aiding an advancement in, at the moment, emerging radionuclide distribution and use in research, translational research, pre-clinical/clinical research and nuclear medicine.

Collaboration in radionuclide research and development activities

Out of all respondents, 80% (90 respondents) indicated that their research and development activities would benefit from a platform such as PRISMAP, which offers the possibility of collaboration with the consortium and obtaining emerging radionuclides with centralized and harmonized procedures and legislation. 18% of surveyed respondents indicated the interest in collaborating with PRISMAP to foster the expertise in pre-clinical and clinical applications of novel radionuclides. 10% of respondents see benefit in joining the PRISMAP community to have contacts and support on their future research or clinical activities.

More than a third of the respondents have shown the interest in novel radionuclides either for radiopharmaceutical development and labelling studies for already ongoing projects or new project applications. On top of translational research preclinical/clinical study equipment optimization with the novel radionuclides is also expressed to match upcoming novel radionuclide use in clinical practice.

Main benefit for R&D activities in collaboration with PRISMAP is the availability of emerging medical radionuclides and access to mass-separated, pure medical radionuclides. At present, mass-separated medical radionuclides with high molar activity for research in Europe are available only from CERN-MEDICIS and exceptionally CERN-ISOLDE (Duchemin 2021). However, multiple mass-separation facilities are emerging in the coming years in Europe through projects such as TATOOS at PSI (Switzerland) (Hartmann et al. 2023), SPES at LNL-INFN (Italy) (De Angelis 2016), ISOL@MYRRHA at SCK-CEN (Belgium) (Hurier et al. 2022)and SMILES at ARRONAX (France) (SMILES 2024). The core founder of PRISMAP– CERN-MEDICIS is a consortium of experts in medical radionuclide production, nuclear medicine, radiochemistry and nuclear research (Cocolios 2023). The CERN-MEDICIS facility was commissioned in December 2017 and has provided non-conventional, high molar activity radionuclides for medical research ever since. MEDICIS is a unique facility due to its mass-separator and modes of operation that utilizes both 1.4 GeV protons delivered by the CERN PSB as well as external samples irradiated at any of the partner institutes. Mass separation at MEDICIS is a physical, single-chemical-element isotope purification process characterized by ionizing isotopes or molecules of interest followed by their separation in a magnetic field, according to their mass to charge ratio. Followed by radiochemical separation, high molar activity and purity radionuclides for research could be obtained (Zabolockis 2023). Since the first mass-separated155Tb ion beam at MEDICIS in 2017, non-exhaustive list of radionuclides such as 43,44,47Sc, 47Ca/47Sc, 128Ba/128Cs, 129Cs, 153Sm, 149,152,155Tb, 155Dy/155Tb, 165,167Tm, 169Er, 191Pt, 175Yb, 224Ra/212Pb, 225Ra, 225Ac have been investigated, mass-separated collected, and delivered to research institutions across Europe and Asia with activities from up to 1 GBq per batch (Bernerd 2023).

As the proposed research projects to PRISMAP increase, members of the PRISMAP consortium in the survey also indicated the benefit of additional collaborating institutes, as additional sites to carry out research activities related to radionuclide production and purification, harmonization of quality control procedures and pre-clinical/clinical studies.

It is seen that there is a need for certain radionuclides and radiopharmaceuticals. For example, respondents mentioned the following needs − 211At for therapy study by labelled affibody; radionuclides for alpha emitter radionuclide therapy; longer half-life positron emitters; access to radionuclides such as terbium; supply of medical Sc radionuclides for labelling studies; 175Yb and some radionuclides from Tb.

Conclusions

The data from the PRISMAP survey found common aspects from all producer-facility respondents: the biggest challenge for the producers is the availability of target materials, which goes hand-in-hand with their purity/enrichment grade. Respondents did indicate the most common issues with the enriched material supply/stock that companies/institutions experienced – availability, delivery time, purity/enrichment grade, quantity and price as well as transport and regulatory limitations, including lack of harmonized import/export legislation and complexity of supply chains. Increased demand and focus on higher isotopic purity, as well as current geopolitical developments strain the supply chain, leading to price increases and availability issues. Ultimately these factors can limit the future output of some of the novel radionuclide products.

The results show that there are sufficient national or international distribution routes and methods established, although having reported challenges due to legislation constraints, especially for novel radionuclides. For longer international radionuclide shipments, the customs clearance was indicated as obstacle due to medical radionuclide short half-life. Such delays must be mitigated. The elaboration of a list of potential transport companies able to deal with the trans-national transport of non-conventional radionuclides on the web access platform, together with efforts from PRISMAP to provide data towards regulation authorities would address some of these findings.

Furthermore, 30 respondent companies/institutions plan to increase manufactured volume of radionuclides in following years with certain limitations in infrastructure, shortage of staff and expertise, production capacity, target material supply and other financial or managerial issues.

Main novel radionuclides that would be of interest for this end user community in the next 2–5 years were 44Sc, 47Sc, 64Cu, 67Cu, 89Zr, 149Tb, 152Tb, 155Tb, 161Tb, 165Er/169Er, 177Lu, 188Re, 211At, 225Ac. Quite interestingly, there was no interest expressed in other PRISMAP portfolio radionuclides from respondents such as: 111Ag, 135La, 153Sm, 213Bi in the course of the survey dissemination. Increased demand for 225Ac, terbium radionuclides and 67Cu production in the following years can, therefore, be expected. We must stress that none (except for iodine radiopharmaceuticals) of the current responder institutions yet are interested in possibilities of “matched pair” from terbium and scandium radionuclides in the near future. This reflects still insufficient pre-clinical data and/or availability of the aforementioned radionuclides.

There is an evident necessity for PRISMAP not only as a web-based entry platform for the production and dispatch of non-conventional radionuclides, but also as a long-term network for aiding in the advancement of emerging radionuclide research and distribution. This is developed through centralized and harmonized procedures and legislation, and the use of the novel radionuclides in translational research, pre-clinical/clinical research and nuclear medicine, offered by efforts of the PRISMAP consortium and future initiatives. Hands-on and targeted training should also become an integral part of the PRISMAP activities to match the needs of the community.

Even though respondent activity has been considered optimal, there is still a need for reaching out to more responders from South-Eastern European regions (e.g., Balkan countries). The continuous data acquisition is crucial to maintain actual radionuclide map across Europe, therefore aiding radionuclide producer and user groups now and in the future.

This study was preliminary and should be extended outside the PRISMAP consortium.
