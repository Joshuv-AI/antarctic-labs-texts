. 1.
Introduction

Let
(x_{n})\subseteq[0,1)
be a sequence. We say that
(x_{n})
is uniformly distributed (UD) if

\frac{1}{N}\#\{n\leq N:x_{n}\in[a,b)\}\to b-a,\text{ as }N\to\infty

for any
0\leq a<b\leq 1.
The study of uniform distribution dates back to the seminal work of Weyl
[
11
]
, and has been well studied over the last century. One of the well-known examples of UD sequence is
(\{n\alpha\})
for any irrational
\alpha.

Let
s>0
be a real number and
N
be a natural number. The pair correlation function
R_{2}(s,(x_{n}),N)
is defined by

R_{2}(s,(x_{n}),N):=\frac{1}{N}\#\{1\leq n\neq m\leq N:\|x_{n}-x_{m}\|\leq s/N\},

where
\|x\|=\inf_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|x+k|
denotes the nearest integer distance. We say that
(x_{n})
has Poissonian pair correlation (PPC) if for any
s>0

R_{2}(s,(x_{n}),N)\to 2s,\text{ as }N\to\infty.

This notion was first studied by Rudnick and Sarnak
[
9
]
, where they showed that for any
d\geq 2,

(\{n^{d}\alpha\})
has PPC for almost all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}.
The pair correlation statistics is a stronger property than uniform distribution, in the sense that if a sequence has PPC, then it is UD (see
[
2
,
7
,
10
]
).

Let
(a_{n})
be an increasing sequence of natural numbers. Let us denote the pair correlation function of
(\{a_{n}\alpha\})
by
R_{2}(s,\alpha,N),
and throughout the article.

Definition
.

We say that
(a_{n})
has metric Poissonian pair correlation (MPPC), if for any
s>0,

R_{2}(s,\alpha,N)\to 2s\text{ as }N\to\infty,

for almost all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be a finite subset. The additive energy
E(A)
of
A
is defined by

E(A):=\#\{a,b,c,d\in A:a+b=c+d\}.

One can check that it satisfies

|A|^{2}\leq E(A)\leq|A|^{3}.

The representation function
r_{A}
is defined in the following way:

r_{A}(v):=\#\{a,b\in A:a-b=v\},

where
v\in\mathbb{Z}.
Then it is easily seen that the additive energy of
A
satisfies the equality

E(A)=\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}}r_{A}(v)^{2}=\|r_{A}\|_{2}^{2}.

Let
A_{N}:=\{a_{n}:n\leq N\}
be the first
N
terms of the sequence
(a_{n}).
Recently, Aistleitner, Larcher and Lewko
[
1
]
showed that if

E(A_{N})\ll N^{3-\delta}

for some
\delta>0,
then
(a_{n})
has MPPC. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to relate the metric Poissonian pair correlation property to the notion of additive energy. This result covers many sequences in one place, such as the result of Rudnick and Sarnak
[
9
]
, because the additive energy of
(n^{d})
is
\ll N^{2+\epsilon}
for any
\epsilon>0
(see
[
1
]
for more examples). Later, Bloom and Walker
[
3
]
improved this result by relaxing the upper bound of the additive energy. They showed that if

E(A_{N})\leq N^{3}/(\log N)^{C}

for some large
C,
then
(a_{n})
has MPPC. They conjectured that this holds for any
C
strictly greater than
1.
They have not provided any specific lower bound for
C.
The purpose of this article is to give a lower bound of
C.
Our main result is as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(a_{n})
be a strictly increasing sequence of natural numbers. Let
C\geq 13.155
be a real number. Suppose the additive energy of
(a_{n})
satisfies

E(A_{N})\leq N^{3}/(\log N)^{C};

then
(a_{n})
has MPPC.

Remark 1
.

The theorem holds true for any
C
satisfying the inequality
C-\beta-2\sqrt{2C+1}>1,
where
\beta=-2\sqrt{3}-6\log(1-1/\sqrt{3}).
That is,
C>(\beta+1)+4+2\sqrt{2(\beta+1)+5}\approx 13.155
, which is far from the conjectural value
1+\epsilon
of
C.

Note that for any
K\geq 1,
additive energy of
([n(\log n)^{K}])
is
\ll N^{3}/(\log N)^{K-1}
(see
[
6
, Corollary 2]
). As an application of Theorem
1.1
, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
K\geq 14.155.
Then
([n(\log n)^{K}])
has MPPC.

2.
Random Model

In this section, we discuss a random model introduced by Lewko and Radziwiłł in
[
8
]
, which is used to study the bounds of GCD sums (see section
3
). Let
\{X(p):p\text{ is prime}\}
be a collection of independent random variables which are uniformly distributed in
\mathbb{S}^{1}.
Let
\sigma>1/2
be a real number. We define the random zeta function in the following way:

\zeta_{X}(\sigma)=\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{X(p)}{p^{\sigma}}\right)^{-1}.

For any
\sigma>1/2,
this series converges almost everywhere by the Kolmogorov three series theorem. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
we define

X(n)=\prod_{p^{r}||n}X(p)^{r}.

Then in
L^{p}
sense (with
p>0
) for
1/2<\sigma<1

\zeta_{X}(\sigma)=\sum_{n}\frac{X(n)}{n^{\sigma}},

and

\mathbb{E}\left[X(n)\overline{X(m)}\right]=\begin{cases}1\quad&\text{ if }m=n\\
0\quad&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

where
\mathbb{E}(Y)=\int Y
denotes the mean of a random variable
Y.

In the next lemma, we provide a more explicit (quantitative) upper bound of the
2l
-th moment of the random zeta function than that given in
[
8
, Lemma 6]
.

Lemma 2.1

(moment lemma)
.

Let
1/2<\sigma<1/2+1/4
and
l\geq 4.
Then,

\log\mathbb{E}[|\zeta_{X}(\sigma)|^{2l}]\leq(l^{2}+\beta l)\log((\sigma-1/2)^{-1}).

This plays a crucial role in establishing an upper bound for the GCD sum (see section
3
). We state the following results, which will be used in the proof of Lemma
2.1
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
0<a\leq 1/\sqrt{3}
be a real number and
l\in\mathbb{N}.

Then

\int_{0}^{2\pi}1/\left(1+a^{2}-2a\cos x\right)^{l}dx\leq\int_{0}^{2\pi}\exp(\beta la^{2}+2la\cos x)dx

for any
\beta\geq-2\sqrt{3}-6\log(1-1/\sqrt{3})\approx 1.7032
.

Proof.

We show that for all
x\in[0,2\pi],

\exp(\beta la^{2}+2la\cos x)\geq 1/\left(1+a^{2}-2a\cos x\right)^{l},

which ensures the lemma. It is sufficient to prove the inequality for
l=1.

Let

f(x)=\exp(\beta a^{2}+2a\cos x)\left(1+a^{2}-2a\cos x\right)-1.

The Lemma boils down to showing that
f(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in[0,2\pi].
Since
f(x)=f(2\pi-x),
it reduces to show this for
x\in[0,\pi].
Taking the first derivative, we get

f^{\prime}(x)=\exp(\beta a^{2}+2a\cos x)2a\sin x(2a\cos x-a^{2}).

So,

f^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in[0,\cos^{-1}(a/2)],
and
f^{\prime}(x)\leq 0
for all
x\in[\cos^{-1}(a/2),\pi]
,
i.e.
f
increases in
[0,\cos^{-1}(a/2)],
and
f
decreases in
[\cos^{-1}(a/2),\pi].
Therefore, it is sufficient to prove that
f(0),f(\pi)\geq 0.

Let
F(a)=\log(f(0)+1)
. That is,
F(a)=2a+\beta a^{2}+2\log(1-a).

Then
F^{\prime}(a)=\frac{a}{1-a}(2\beta-2-2\beta a).
Therefore,
F
increases in
[0,1-1/\beta],
and
F
decreases in
[1-1/\beta,1).
In the first case, i.e. for any
a\in[0,1-1/\beta],\ F(a)\geq F(0)=0.

Considering the second interval where
F
decreases, we try to choose the value of
\beta
to be
<2
such that
F(1/\sqrt{3})\geq 0,
since
a\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}
. Thus,

\displaystyle F(1/\sqrt{3})

\displaystyle=\beta/3+2/\sqrt{3}+2\log(1-1/\sqrt{3})\geq 0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\beta\geq-2\sqrt{3}-6\log(1-1/\sqrt{3})\approx 1.7032.

which implies that
F(a)\geq 0
for all
0<a\leq 1/\sqrt{3}.
And this gives us
f(0)\geq 0
for this choice of
\beta.

Now we show that
f(\pi)\geq 0.

\displaystyle f(\pi)=

\displaystyle\exp(\beta a^{2}-2a)(1+a^{2}+2a)-1\geq 0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle(\beta-1)a^{2}\geq 0.

Since
\beta>1,

f(\pi)\geq 0.

∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
1/2<\alpha<1/2+1/4.
Then

\log((1-1/2^{\alpha})^{-1})\leq 2.56/2^{2\alpha}.

Proof.

Let
f(\alpha)=2.56/2^{2\alpha}+\log(1-1/2^{\alpha}).
Then,

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\alpha)=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2\alpha}-5.12(2^{\alpha}-1)}{2^{2\alpha}(2^{\alpha}-1)}\log 2\leq 0

in
[1/2,1/2+1/4].
Therefore,
f
is decreasing in
[1/2,1/2+1/4],
i.e.
f(\alpha)\geq f(3/4)>0.
This completes the proof.
∎

Before starting the proof of Lemma
2.1
, we fix the value of
\beta.
Throughout the subsequent sections
\beta=-2\sqrt{3}-6\log(1-1/\sqrt{3})\approx 1.7032.

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

Let
l
be a natural number. We set

E_{l}(p):=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left(1-X(p)/p^{\sigma}\right)^{-2l}\right|\right].

Note that

\log\mathbb{E}[|\zeta_{X}(\sigma)|^{2l}]=\sum_{p}\log\mathbb{E}_{l}(p).

We aim to bound
E_{l}(p),
which eventually leads to a bound of the
2l
-th moment of the random zeta function.
Since
X(p)
is uniformly distribute in
\mathbb{S}^{1},
for any
l\in\mathbb{N}
and
p
prime we have

\displaystyle E_{l}(p)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(1-\frac{e^{i\theta}}{p^{\sigma}}\right)^{-l}\left(1-\frac{e^{-i\theta}}{p^{\sigma}}\right)^{-l}d\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\frac{p^{2\sigma}}{p^{2\sigma}+1-2p^{\sigma}cos\theta}\right)^{l}d\theta.

By Lemma
2.2
, for any prime
p\geq 3
and
l\in\mathbb{N}
we obtain

\displaystyle E_{l}(p)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\exp(\beta l/p^{2\sigma}+2l/p^{\sigma}\cos x)dx

(2.1)

\displaystyle=I_{0}(2l/p^{\sigma})\exp(\beta l/p^{2\sigma}),

where
I_{0}
is the
0
-th modified Bessel function.
Using
\log I_{0}(t)\leq t^{2}/4
for all
t\geq 0
in (
2
), for any
l\in\mathbb{N}
and prime
p\geq 3,
we get

(2.2)

\displaystyle\log E_{l}(p)\leq\frac{l^{2}+\beta l}{p^{2\sigma}}.

Now for
p=2
and for any
l\in\mathbb{N},
we use the trivial upper bound of
E_{l}(2),
which is
(1-1/2^{\sigma})^{-2l}.
Thus, for
1/2<\sigma<1/2+1/4,
the lemma
2.3
ensures that

(2.3)

\displaystyle\log E_{l}(2)\leq 5.12l/2^{2\sigma}.

Invoking (
2.2
), and (
2.3
) with
l\geq 4
we get

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}[|\zeta_{X}(\sigma)|^{2l}]

\displaystyle\leq(l^{2}+\beta l)\sum_{p}\frac{1}{p^{2\sigma}}

\displaystyle\leq(l^{2}+\beta l)\sum_{p}\log((1-p^{-2\sigma})^{-1})

(2.4)

\displaystyle=(l^{2}+\beta l)\log\zeta(2\sigma).

Thus proving
\log\zeta(2\sigma)\leq\log((\sigma-1/2)^{-1})
would conclude the lemma.
Recall that

\zeta(2\sigma)=\frac{1}{2\sigma-1}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(1-2\sigma)^{n}}{n!}\gamma_{n},

where
\gamma_{0}
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant and
\gamma_{n}
is the
n
-th Stieltjes constant, which
satisfy the bound
|\gamma_{n}|\leq n^{n}/e^{n}.
Using these bounds and Stirling’s formula
we have

|\zeta(2\sigma)-1/(2\sigma-1)|<1

in the given range
1/2<\sigma<1/2+1/4.
Therefore,

\log\zeta(2\sigma)\leq\log((\sigma-1/2)^{-1}).

∎

3.
GCD Sums

Given a function
f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
with finite support and
\sigma\in(0,1]
, the GCD sum is defined as

S_{f}(\sigma):=\sum_{a,b}f(a)\overline{f(b)}\frac{\gcd(a,b)^{2\sigma}}{(ab)^{\sigma}}.

This sum plays a major role in finding the large values of the Riemann zeta function (see
[
4
,
5
]
), and in the theory of Poissonian pair correlation (see
[
1
,
3
]
). In our next result, we provide an upper bound of
S_{f}(1/2)
similar to Bloom and Walker
[
3
, Theorem 2]
, but with an explicit constant.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a function of finite support with
\log\|f\|_{1}\geq 4
and
K
be a parameter such that
K\geq(\log\|f\|_{1})^{4}.
Let

\sum_{ab=cd}f(a)f(b)f(c)f(d)\leq K\|f\|_{2}^{4}.

Then,

\displaystyle S_{f}(1/2)\ll

\displaystyle\frac{(\log\|f\|_{1})^{\beta}}{\log\|f\|_{1}+\text{O}(1)}\exp\left(5/2(\log K)^{1/2}(\log(\log\|f\|_{1}))^{1/2}\right)\|f\|_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to that of
[
3
, Theorem 2]
, which uses the ideas of Lewko and Radziwiłł
[
8
]
. Let
\sigma=1/2+1/\log\|f\|_{1}.
Then, by applying Hölder’s inequality we get

S_{f}(1/2)\ll S_{f}(\sigma).

We define a random variable
D(X)
as

D(X)=\sum_{a}f(a)X(a).

Note that

\mathbb{E}[|D(X)|^{2}]=\|f\|_{2}^{2}\;,

and

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|D(X)|^{4}]=\sum_{ab=cd}f(a)f(b)f(c)f(d)\leq K\|f\|_{2}^{4}.

It can be shown that

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|D(X)\zeta_{X}(\sigma)|^{2}]=\zeta(2\sigma)\sum_{a,b}f(a)f(b)\frac{\gcd(a,b)^{2\sigma}}{(ab)^{\sigma}}.

Let
V
be a positive real number and
l
be a natural number to be chosen later. Dividing the integral in (
3.2
) into two parts depending on whether
|\zeta_{X}(\sigma)|\leq V

and applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in the second part, we deduce that

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|D(X)\zeta_{X}(\sigma)|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{2}^{2}V^{2}+V^{-2l}(\mathbb{E}[|D(X)|^{4}])^{1/2}(\mathbb{E}[|\zeta_{X}(\sigma)|^{4+4l}])^{1/2}.

Invoking the moment Lemma
2.1
and applying (
3.1
) to RHS of the above inequality (
3.3
) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|D(X)\zeta_{X}(\sigma)|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{2}^{2}\left(V^{2}+V^{-2l}K^{1/2}\exp((2(l+1)^{2}+\beta(l+1))\log((\sigma-1/2)^{-1}))\right)

\displaystyle=\|f\|_{2}^{2}\left(V^{2}+V^{-2l}K^{1/2}\exp((2(l+1)^{2}+\beta(l+1))\log\log\|f\|_{1})\right).

Choosing

V=\exp\left((\beta/2+5/4(l+1))\log\log\|f\|_{1}\right),

l+1=(\log K)^{1/2}(\log\log\|f\|_{1})^{-1/2}

we get

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|D(X)\zeta_{X}(\sigma)|^{2}]\leq\|f\|_{2}^{2}(\log\|f\|_{1})^{\beta}\exp\left(5/2(\log K)^{1/2}(\log\log\|f\|_{1})^{1/2}\right).

It is to be noted that
l\geq 1,
because of the assumption on
K.

Therefore, (
3.4
) and (
3.2
) together with
\zeta(2\sigma)=1/(2\sigma-1)+\text{O}(1)
prove the theorem.
∎

Now we prove a particular case of the above theorem, which will be used to prove Theorem
1.1
. The derived explicit exponent of
\log N
in Theorem
3.3
leads to a lower bound for
C.
Such a conclusion was not attainable in
[
3
, Theorem 5]
due to the absence of explicit constants in
[
3
, Theorem 4]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

[
3
, Theorem 3]

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be a finite subset and
r=r_{A}
be the representation function. Then for some large constant
C_{1}

\sum_{ab=cd}r(a)r(b)r(c)r(d)\leq C_{1}|A|^{6}\log|A|.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\epsilon>0
and
C\geq 15/2.
Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be a finite subset with
|A|=N.
Assume that
E(A)\leq N^{3}/(\log N)^{C}.
Then for large
N
the GCD sum satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{i},n_{j}\in A-A\\
n_{i},n_{j}>0\end{subarray}}\frac{(n_{i},n_{j})}{\sqrt{n_{i}n_{j}}}r(n_{i})r(n_{j})\ll N^{3}(\log N)^{\beta-1+2\sqrt{2C+1}-C.}

Proof.

Until equation (
3.3
) of Theorem
3.1
, computations remain unchanged. By applying Theorem
3.2
and Lemma
2.1
with

f=r_{A}\chi_{\mathbb{N}}
in (
3.3
) we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|D(X)\zeta_{X}(\sigma)|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq E(A)V^{2}+V^{-2l}(C_{1}N^{6}\log N)^{1/2}\exp(((2l+2)^{2}+\beta(2l+2))\log\log N)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq V^{2}N^{3}/(\log N)^{C}+V^{-2l}(C_{1}N^{6}\log N)^{1/2}\exp((2(l+1)^{2})+\beta(l+1))\log\log N).

We choose

l=\frac{\sqrt{2C+1}-2}{2},\text{ and }V=(\log N)^{\beta/2+\sqrt{2C+1}}.

This ensures that

V^{2}N^{3}/(\log N)^{C}\geq V^{-2l}(C_{1}N^{6}\log N)^{1/2}\exp((2(l+1)^{2})+\beta(l+1))\log\log N).

Therefore, by (
3.2
) and the choice of
\sigma
imply that the GCD sum is

\ll N^{3}(\log N)^{\beta-1+2\sqrt{2C+1}-C}.

∎

Remark 2
.

The moment lemma (Lemma
2.1
) with a lesser value of
\beta
would decrease the exponent of
\log N
in Theorem
3.3
, resulting in a better lower bound of
C
in Theorem
1.1
. With this method, we were unable to further reduce the value of

Proof of Theorem
1.1

Let

\text{Var}(R_{2}(s,\cdot,N)):=\int_{0}^{1}\left(R_{2}(s,\alpha,N)-2s(N-1)/N\right)^{2}d\alpha

be the variance of
R_{2}(s,\cdot,N).
It is enough to show that

\text{Var}(R_{2}(s,\cdot,N))\ll 1/(\log N)^{1+\delta^{\prime}}

for some
\delta^{\prime}>0.
The rest of the arguments are very standard, see the proof of
[
3
, Theorem 5]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

[
1
, Lemma 3]

If
s\asymp 1,
then

\text{Var}(R_{2}(s,\cdot,N))\ll\frac{\log N}{N^{3}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{i},n_{j}\in A-A\\
n_{i},n_{j}>0\end{subarray}}\frac{(n_{i},n_{j})}{\sqrt{n_{i}n_{j}}}r(n_{i})r(n_{j}).

Applying Theorem
3.3
in the Lemma
4.1
we have

\displaystyle\text{Var}(R_{2}(s,\cdot,N))\ll

\displaystyle(\log N)^{\beta+2\sqrt{2C+1}-C}.

Hence, the choice of
C
for which
C-\beta-2\sqrt{2C+1}>1
yields the theorem.
