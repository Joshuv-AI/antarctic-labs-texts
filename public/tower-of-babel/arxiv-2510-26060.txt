Performance Analysis of Dynamic Equilibria in Joint Path Selection and Congestion Control
Sina Keshvadi

Abstract
Path-aware networking, a cornerstone of next-generation architectures like SCION and Multipath QUIC, empowers end-hosts with fine-grained control over traffic forwarding. This capability, however, introduces a critical stability risk: uncoordinated, greedy path selection by a multitude of agents can induce persistent, high-amplitude network oscillations. While this phenomenon is well-known, its quantitative performance impact across key metrics has remained poorly understood. In this paper, we address this gap by developing the first axiomatic framework for analyzing the joint dynamics of path selection and congestion control. Our model enables the formal characterization of the system’s dynamic equilibria-the stable, periodic patterns of oscillation-and provides a suite of axioms to rate their performance in terms of efficiency, loss avoidance, convergence, fairness, and responsiveness. Our analysis reveals a fundamental trade-off in protocol design between predictable performance (efficiency, convergence) and user-centric goals (fairness, responsiveness). We prove, however, that no such trade-off exists among efficiency, convergence, and loss avoidance, which can be simultaneously optimized through careful parameter tuning. Furthermore, we find that agent migration can, counter-intuitively, enhance stability by de-synchronizing traffic, a theoretical result validated by our simulations. These findings provide a principled design map for engineering robust, high-performance protocols for the future path-aware Internet.



I
Introduction

The principle of end-host-driven path selection is experiencing a significant resurgence, forming the bedrock of next-generation network architectures designed to overcome the rigidity of today’s Internet.

In the inter-domain, path-aware architectures like SCION are enabling enterprises to achieve unprecedented levels of security and availability by allowing endpoints to explicitly select network paths that bypass threats and performance bottlenecks
[
1
]
. Concurrently, within data centers and across the mobile edge, transport protocols such as Multipath QUIC (MPQUIC) leverage path diversity to deliver tangible performance gains, enhancing connection resilience and throughput by dynamically scheduling traffic over multiple available links
[
2
]
. Yet, this powerful capability introduces a critical, systemic risk: when a large population of self-interested agents independently and greedily selects paths based on perceived load, their uncoordinated actions can trigger persistent, high-amplitude load oscillations. This phenomenon threatens to undermine the very stability these advanced architectures aim to provide, a concern whose relevance is only amplified by the increasing scale and dynamism of modern network environments
[
3
]
.

While this stability challenge is well-recognized, the vast body of prior research has predominantly concentrated on designing
suppression mechanisms
—strategies intended to guide selfish agents toward a stable equilibrium and prevent oscillations from occurring in the first place
[
4
]
. Although critical, this focus on mitigation has left the underlying performance trade-offs of the instability itself largely unquantified. Key questions often remain unexplored: by precisely how much does oscillation degrade network efficiency during periods of traffic migration? How does it impact the fairness experienced by competing end-hosts? And what is the resulting cost in terms of convergence time and performance predictability? Answering these questions requires a framework capable of characterizing not just static equilibria, but the dynamic, and potentially non-convergent, behavior of the system. We argue that an axiomatic approach, which has been used to derive fundamental truths in areas from economics to routing protocol design, provides the ideal lens for this investigation
[
5
]
. Our work, therefore, is the first to adapt and significantly extend the formal axiomatic method for single-path congestion control to the multi-path domain, providing a rigorous foundation for analyzing the joint dynamics of path selection and rate adaptation
[
6
]
.

Our axiomatic analysis yields several insights that challenge the conventional understanding of path selection dynamics. Perhaps most notably, we establish that there is no fundamental performance trade-off between network efficiency, loss avoidance, and the stability of traffic flows. These three critical goals, which collectively define predictable network operation, can be simultaneously optimized through principled protocol design. This co-optimization, however, reveals a deeper, inherent tension: we demonstrate that these performance metrics are fundamentally at odds with inter-agent fairness and a protocol’s responsiveness. Specifically, tuning a protocol for higher responsiveness—enabling agents to rapidly migrate to better paths—improves fairness but necessarily introduces greater load volatility and reduces overall throughput. This work therefore replaces the monolithic view of path selection’s instability with a nuanced, multi-faceted perspective, quantifying the precise conditions under which its effects are beneficial versus detrimental.

In summary, this paper makes the following primary contributions:

•

A Generalized Analytical Framework.
We develop a tractable model of joint path selection and congestion control that integrates key behavioral parameters—namely, agent responsiveness (
m
) and reset softness (
r
)—into a multi-path axiomatic system.

•

Equilibrium Characterization.
We provide a complete analytical characterization of the dynamic equilibria that emerge from greedy, uncoordinated agent behavior, mathematically describing the predictable, periodic patterns of both lossless and lossy network oscillations.

•

Axiomatic Rating Methodology.
We extend the axiomatic method to the path-aware domain, defining a rigorous set of metrics for quantifying protocol performance across the dimensions of efficiency, stability, loss avoidance, and fairness.

•

Derivation of Fundamental Trade-offs.
We use this framework to formally derive the core design trade-offs, proving that while efficiency, loss avoidance, and convergence can be simultaneously optimized, they stand in a fundamental trade-off with fairness and responsiveness.

II
Related Work

The architectural principle of vesting path-selection intelligence in network endpoints has gained significant traction across a diverse array of networking domains, driven by the demand for greater performance, security, and flexibility. This trend is evident in proposals for multi-criteria routing, which empower applications to choose paths satisfying complex objectives beyond simple latency, such as economic cost or specific security postures
[
7
]
. In massive-scale data centers, fine-grained, source-driven traffic management is increasingly used to optimize for ultra-low latency and minimize flow completion times for demanding applications
[
8
]
. The principle is also central to emerging network topologies; for instance, the highly dynamic nature of LEO satellite constellations necessitates source-based mechanisms for adapting to rapid changes in path availability and quality
[
9
]
. Finally, in next-generation inter-domain architectures such as SCION, end-host path control is a foundational element, enabling explicit trust establishment and resilience against prefix hijacks and routing anomalies
[
1
]
. While their specific goals vary, these systems are unified by the transfer of path-selection agency to the network edge. This architectural shift, however, invariably reintroduces the fundamental challenge of maintaining global network stability in the face of uncoordinated, greedy decisions made by a multitude of self-interested agents.

The literature aimed at mitigating these load oscillations can be broadly classified into two main paradigms: centralized and end-host-based. Centralized approaches, typified by Software-Defined Networking (SDN)-based traffic engineering, rely on a logically unified controller with a global network view. This controller computes stable traffic distributions and installs forwarding rules that prevent the uncoordinated, greedy behavior that leads to oscillations in the first place
[
10
]
. In contrast, end-host-based approaches seek to achieve stability without central coordination by modifying the decision-making logic of the agents themselves. Such strategies include introducing inertia to discourage frequent path changes, using randomized ”hedging” strategies, or implementing more sophisticated adaptive algorithms that allow agents to converge to a stable state over time
[
11
]
. Despite their architectural differences, both research thrusts share the primary goal of
oscillation suppression
. Our work adopts a fundamentally different and complementary posture: rather than designing new mechanisms to prevent instability, our objective is to formally characterize and quantify its multifaceted performance consequences when it does occur, thereby providing a rigorous understanding of the system’s dynamic, oscillatory behavior.

From a methodological standpoint, prior efforts to model path selection dynamics have navigated a trade-off between abstraction and specificity. Foundational work in selfish routing has used the classic Wardrop model to analyze static traffic distributions, their efficiency (often termed the Price of Anarchy), and convergence properties
[
12
]
. However, these models cannot appropriately represent the congestion-control dynamics central to our analysis. At the other end of the spectrum, applied research in Multipath TCP, such as the design of protocols like LIA and OLIA, captures oscillatory phenomena but relies on ad-hoc reasoning and experimental validation, limiting its generality
[
13
]
. Our work adopts the axiomatic method, first applied to congestion control by Zarchy et al., as a compelling alternative that balances these extremes
[
6
]
. This aligns with a broader research program that has successfully applied axiomatic reasoning to other areas in computer science, including routing protocols and recommendation systems
[
14
]
.

Finally, our work is distinguished from prior theoretical analyses of multi-path routing, such as the work by Wang et al. on the ’cost of not splitting in routing’
[
15
]
. Their research captures the difference in network utility between selecting a single path versus concurrently using multiple paths. However, our work differs in several key aspects: we investigate the dynamic evolution of traffic rates rather than static allocations; we evaluate performance using a suite of axioms rather than a single utility metric; and our comparison is between a system with path selection and one with pre-determined paths, rather than between different modes of path selection.

III
Background: The Axiomatic Method in Congestion Control

The axiomatic method for congestion control, introduced by Zarchy et al., provides a formal framework for analyzing and comparing the fundamental behavior of transport protocols
[
6
]
. The approach is designed to move beyond ad-hoc, scenario-specific evaluations by establishing a set of first principles—or axioms—that represent universally desirable properties for any congestion control algorithm. In the canonical single-path context, these foundational axioms allow for a multi-dimensional characterization of protocol performance.
They include:
Efficiency
, which requires a protocol to make full use of available bottleneck capacity;
Fairness
, which dictates how that capacity should be equitably shared among competing flows;
Convergence
, which demands that flow rates settle within a tight range of a steady-state value; and
Loss Avoidance
, which mandates the minimization of packet drops.
Together, these axioms provide a formal basis for articulating and quantifying the inherent, and often conflicting, design trade-offs at the core of congestion control.

The method borrows its philosophical underpinnings from classical economics and game theory, where axiomatic frameworks have long been used to formally define the properties of a “fair” or “desirable” outcome in systems with competing agents
[
16
]
. The evaluation process for a given protocol is methodical: first, a mathematical model is constructed to represent the interactions of agents universally adopting that protocol. Second, this model is analyzed to identify its long-term equilibrium state—the stable pattern of behavior to which the system dynamics converge. Finally, the properties of this equilibrium are measured against the predefined axioms, yielding a quantitative rating of the protocol’s intrinsic performance characteristics. For instance, to analyze a classic protocol such as TCP Reno, one would first formalize its well-known additive-increase, multiplicative-decrease (AIMD) behavior as follows:

w_{i}(t+1)=\begin{cases}w_{i}(t)+1&\text{if }L_{\pi}(t)\leq C_{\pi}\quad\text{(no loss)}\\
\gamma\cdot w_{i}(t)&\text{if }L_{\pi}(t)>C_{\pi}\quad\text{(loss event)}\end{cases}

(1)

cwnd_{i}(t+1)=\begin{cases}cwnd_{i}(t)+\alpha(\tau_{i}(t))&\text{if }f_{\pi}(t)\leq C_{\pi}\quad\text{(no loss)}\\
\beta\cdot cwnd_{i}(t)&\text{if }f_{\pi}(t)>C_{\pi}\quad\text{(loss event)}\end{cases}

(2)

While this framework is powerful for analyzing protocols on a single, shared resource, its core assumptions are fundamentally challenged in a path-aware network. The ability of agents to dynamically select and migrate between paths introduces a new degree of freedom that has no analogue in the single-path model. This agent migration, driven by the rational, self-interested pursuit of uncongested links, intrinsically couples the load dynamics across all available paths. As a result, the system may not converge to a static equilibrium at all. Instead, the uncoordinated actions of agents can drive the network into a
dynamic equilibrium
characterized by persistent, periodic load oscillations. Such behavior lies outside the scope of the original axiomatic framework, which was designed to rate the properties of a fixed-point convergence, thus necessitating a direct extension to accommodate the unique dynamics of path-aware systems.

Building on this foundation, our work introduces the necessary extensions to apply this rigorous method to the path-aware domain. Our primary adaptation is to shift the analytical focus from static fixed-points to the properties of the
dynamic equilibria
that characterize oscillatory systems. This allows us to reason formally about persistently fluctuating network states. In doing so, we also elevate
Responsiveness
—an agent’s propensity to migrate to a better path—to a first-class property, treating it not as an externality but as a core, tunable parameter of a joint path-selection and congestion control protocol.
Consequently, our extended framework will evaluate protocol performance across a comprehensive suite of five axioms: the canonical properties of
Efficiency
,
Fairness
,
Convergence
, and
Loss Avoidance
, which we reinterpret for the dynamic context, alongside the newly incorporated dimension of
Responsiveness
.

IV
Modeling Path-Aware Dynamics

IV-A

System Setup and Assumptions

We develop a tractable analytical model of a path-aware network consisting of a set of competing agents and shared resources. Let
A

N

To construct a model that isolates the fundamental properties of oscillation, our analysis focuses on a scenario designed to elicit worst-case stability conditions. This scenario rests on the following three core assumptions:

1.

Greedy, Load-Adaptive Selection.
We assume agents are myopic and act greedily, always migrating to the path with the lowest instantaneous utilization. This behavior is a well-understood driver of instability in competitive routing games, as it encourages ”herding” dynamics where agents rush
en masse
to a newly attractive resource, thereby congesting it and restarting the cycle
[
17
]
.

2.

Sequential Multi-path Usage.
When an agent switches paths, we assume it migrates its entire traffic flow at once, rather than gradually splitting traffic. This maximizes the impulse of each migration event and thus the amplitude of the resulting load oscillations. This assumption is also a pragmatic approximation of real-world protocol behavior, as schedulers for Multipath TCP, for example, often concentrate the vast majority of traffic onto the single best-performing path at any given time
[
18
]
.

3.

Disjoint and Similar Paths.
We model the
P
paths as being resource-disjoint and homogeneous in terms of their bottleneck capacity and round-trip time. Path homogeneity serves as a powerful synchronizing factor, ensuring that agents receive performance feedback and react in a highly correlated fashion—a key catalyst for coherent, network-wide oscillations.

IV-B

System Dynamics

The state of our model, defined by the per-path agent sets
\{A_{\pi}(t)\}
and loads
\{L_{\pi}(t)\}
, evolves in discrete time steps according to a stochastic process driven by agent migration. At each step
t
, the path with the minimum utilization,
\pi_{min}(t)
, is identified. For every other path
\pi\neq\pi_{min}(t)
, each agent
i\in A_{\pi}(t)
independently decides to migrate to
\pi_{min}(t)
with our responsiveness probability,
\rho
. This process determines a random set of migrating agents,
M_{\pi}(t)
, for each suboptimal path.

Consequently, the load on a path
\pi
that experiences departures is reduced by the aggregate congestion windows of the agents in
M_{\pi}(t)
, while the windows of the remaining, non-migrating agents continue to evolve according to their CC algorithm. Concurrently, the load on the destination path
\pi_{min}(t)
increases by the sum of the congestion windows of all arriving agents—each first scaled by the reset softness factor
\sigma
—in addition to the window growth of its own incumbent agent population.

This complete stochastic process is captured by the following recursive formulation:

IV-C

Model Limitations

The clarity and analytical tractability of our model are predicated on several simplifying assumptions that necessarily define its scope. Our focus on a topology of homogeneous, parallel paths results in a system with implicitly synchronized feedback, which, while creating a tractable worst-case scenario for coherent oscillations, abstracts away the complexities of asynchrony and shared bottlenecks found in real networks. Furthermore, our framework is presently specified for the broad class of loss-based congestion control protocols. Extending this axiomatic analysis to capture the fundamentally different dynamics of modern latency-based and model-based algorithms, such as BBR
[
20
]
, represents a compelling and important direction for future research. Despite these limitations, our model provides a robust and essential foundation for deriving the first-order principles that govern the performance of path-aware systems.

V
Equilibrium Characterization

Having established the deterministic equations governing the system’s expected dynamics, we now analyze their long-term behavior. Our analysis reveals that the interplay between greedy path selection and congestion control does not drive the system toward a static fixed point, but rather toward a
dynamic equilibrium
. In the language of dynamical systems, such an equilibrium corresponds to a stable periodic orbit, or limit cycle, in which the system state—the vector of per-path agent counts and loads—evolves through a finite, repeating sequence of values
[
21
]
. Our objective in this section is to formally identify and characterize these periodic patterns. We will show that the system converges exponentially fast to one of two classes of dynamic equilibria:
lossless
equilibria, where path capacities are always respected, and
lossy
equilibria, where they are periodically breached. The structure of both equilibrium types is determined by a remarkably consistent pattern of agent migration, which we formalize next.

The structure of these equilibria is governed by a remarkably consistent and self-organizing pattern of collective agent behavior, which we term a
P
-step oscillation
. This is a dynamic pattern with a period of
P
time steps, in which the utilization-based ranks of the
P
available paths permute in a deterministic, round-robin fashion. The oscillation is driven by two coupled migration dynamics. First, the in-migration to the least-utilized path is consistently over-reactive: the sudden influx of agents is so large that this path invariably becomes the
most
utilized in the subsequent time step. This confirms that the myopic greedy strategy is not a Nash Equilibrium, as agents acting on momentarily correct information achieve a collectively pessimal outcome
[
17
]
. Second, the corresponding out-migration of agents from all other paths is
order-preserving
, meaning their relative utilization ranks are maintained. The composite effect is a perfectly predictable rank permutation: the path at rank
p\in[0,P-2]
moves to rank
p+1
, while the path at rank
P-1
jumps to rank
0
. We formalize this cyclical rank evolution as follows:

First, we define the rank of a path
\pi
at time
t
based on its expected load, which orders all paths from most utilized (rank 0) to least utilized (rank
P-1
):

\text{rank}(\pi,t)=p\iff|\{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi\mid\hat{L}_{\pi^{\prime}}(t)>\hat{L}_{\pi}(t)\}|=p.

(9)

With this, we can formally define the system’s core oscillatory pattern.

Definition 1

(
P
-step Oscillation)
.

A system’s expected dynamics exhibit a
P
-step oscillation
if there exists a time
t_{0}
after which the rank of any path
\pi\in\Pi
evolves according to the following rule for all
T\geq 0
:

\text{If }\emph{rank}(\pi,t_{0})=p,\text{ then }\emph{rank}(\pi,t_{0}+T)=(p+T)\pmod{P}.

(10)

A natural question is whether this
P
-step oscillation
is a universal property of our model or a fragile artifact. We can test this by checking for logical consistency: if the hypothetical equilibrium flow volumes that result from assuming a
P
-step pattern are themselves inconsistent with that pattern (e.g., a rank-0 path has less flow than a rank-1 path), then our assumption must be invalid for those system parameters. We performed this consistency check across a wide range of parameters for responsiveness (
\rho
) and reset softness (
\sigma
). The results, shown in Figure
3
, demonstrate that the
P
-step pattern is remarkably robust. Inconsistencies only arise in a small, corner-case fraction of the parameter space, justifying our use of the
P
-step oscillation as the foundational dynamic pattern for the remainder of our analysis.

This
P
-step oscillation pattern provides a deterministic map of agent migration, which allows us to solve for the agent equilibrium. Consider a path
\pi^{(0)}
that is at rank 0 at time
t_{0}
. For
P-1
consecutive time steps, it is not the least-utilized path, so it only experiences agent outflow, governed by the first case of Eq. (
8a
). At time
t_{0}+P-1
, it reaches rank
P-1
(the minimum). In the next step,
t_{0}+P
, it receives all agent inflow per the second case of Eq. (
8a
), returning it to rank 0. This complete cycle is characterized by the following first-order difference equation, which models the agent count for a specific path
\pi
at intervals of
P
time steps:

\hat{a}_{\pi}(t_{0}+P)=(1-\rho)^{P}\hat{a}_{\pi}(t_{0})+\rho N

(11)

The predictable structure of the
P
-step oscillation allows for a full analytical characterization of the system’s lossless dynamic equilibrium.
We find the fixed point of this dynamical system by solving Eq. (
11
) for
\hat{a}_{\pi}(t_{0}+P)=\hat{a}_{\pi}(t_{0})
, which yields the equilibrium agent count for the rank-0 path. By extension, the expected number of agents
\hat{a}^{(p)}
on any path of rank
p
converges to a unique, closed-form solution determined solely by the total number of agents
N
, paths
P
, and the responsiveness parameter
\rho
:

\hat{a}^{(p)}=\frac{(1-\rho)^{p}\cdot\rho\cdot N}{1-(1-\rho)^{P}}

(12)

Furthermore, we can prove that the system converges to this unique equilibrium exponentially fast. We define a
trajectory function

\hat{a}_{\pi}^{(p)}(t)
as an explicit function that interpolates the agent count for a path
\pi
at all times
t
when it has rank
p
. This function is given by:

\hat{a}_{\pi}^{(p)}(t)=(\hat{a}_{\pi}(t_{\pi p})-\hat{a}^{(p)})\cdot(1-\rho)^{t-t_{\pi p}}+\hat{a}^{(p)}

(13)

where
t_{\pi p}
is the first time
t\geq t_{0}
that path
\pi
has rank
p
. As
t\to\infty
, the first term vanishes, proving that
lim_{t\to\infty}\hat{a}_{\pi}^{(p)}(t)=\hat{a}^{(p)}
.

This flow equilibrium is a more complex function of all system parameters, including the reset softness
\sigma
, which dictates the magnitude of the traffic impulse that fuels the cycle.
Solving this system for its fixed points yields the expected load
\hat{L}^{(p)}
on a path of rank
p
. The solutions for the maximum (rank 0) and minimum (rank
P-1

\hat{L}^{(0)}=\frac{((1+\rho\sigma z(\cdot))\cdot(\sum_{p=0}^{P-2}\hat{\alpha}^{(p)})+\hat{\alpha}^{(P-1)})\cdot\hat{a}^{(P-1)}}{1-(1+\rho\sigma z(\cdot))\cdot(1-\rho)^{P-1}}

(16)

\hat{L}^{(0)}\leq C/P
. If the peak flow exceeds the capacity, the system transitions into a lossy state.

\pi
at rank
p

\hat{L}_{\pi}^{(p)}(t)

VI
Axiomatic Performance Metrics for Path-Aware Protocols

Having analytically characterized the system’s dynamic equilibria, we now introduce the set of axioms used to quantitatively evaluate their performance. This operationalizes the core of the axiomatic method, providing a rigorous, multi-dimensional framework for rating the emergent behavior induced by a given MPCC protocol. A key contribution of our work is the re-interpretation of these axioms for the context of a perpetually oscillating system. Unlike in a single-path model where metrics are evaluated at a static fixed point, our ratings are derived from the properties of the entire periodic cycle, such as its amplitude, minimum, and maximum values. We now proceed to formalize a metric for each of the five axioms previewed in our background: Efficiency, Loss Avoidance, Convergence, Fairness, and Responsiveness. We begin with Efficiency, which captures the productive use of network capacity.

An MPCC protocol’s efficiency is determined by its ability to consistently utilize the available network capacity, ensuring that traffic migration does not lead to periods of severe under-utilization. We formalize this as follows:

Axiom 1

(Efficiency)
.

An MPCC protocol is said to be
\epsilon
-efficient if, once the system has converged to its dynamic equilibrium (for
t\geq t_{0}
), the utilization on any path
\pi\in\Pi
is guaranteed to never drop below the value
\epsilon
. The efficiency rating is therefore the minimum sustained utilization across all paths in the equilibrium cycle:

\epsilon=\min_{t\geq t_{0},\pi\in\Pi}\frac{\hat{L}_{\pi}(t)}{C/P}

(20)

Higher values of
\epsilon\in[0,1]
are more desirable.

The axiom of Loss Avoidance quantifies a protocol’s ability to respect link capacity limits, measuring the worst-case load overshoot that occurs during the peak of an oscillation.

Axiom 2

(Loss Avoidance)
.

An MPCC protocol is said to be
\lambda
-loss-avoiding if, in its dynamic equilibrium, the load
\hat{L}_{\pi}(t)
on any path
\pi
never exceeds its capacity
C/P
by more than a fraction
\lambda
. The loss avoidance rating is therefore the maximum fractional overshoot observed across all paths during the equilibrium cycle:

\lambda=\max_{t\geq t_{0},\pi\in\Pi}\left\{0,\frac{\hat{L}_{\pi}(t)-C/P}{C/P}\right\}

(21)

Lower values of
\lambda\geq 0
are more desirable, with
\lambda=0
indicating a perfectly lossless protocol.

The Convergence axiom, which we can also consider a measure of stability, quantifies the predictability of the network by measuring the relative amplitude of the load oscillations. A protocol with poor convergence exhibits high-amplitude swings in traffic, leading to high performance volatility for the end-user.

Axiom 3

(Convergence)
.

An MPCC protocol is said to be
\gamma_{\text{conv}}
-convergent if, in its dynamic equilibrium, the ratio of the minimum sustained load to the maximum peak load across all paths is given by
\gamma_{\text{conv}}
. The convergence rating is therefore:

\gamma_{\text{conv}}=\frac{\min_{t\geq t_{0},\pi\in\Pi}\hat{L}_{\pi}(t)}{\max_{t\geq t_{0},\pi\in\Pi}\hat{L}_{\pi}(t)}

(22)

Values of
\gamma_{\text{conv}}\in[0,1]
closer to 1 indicate a more stable and predictable protocol with lower-amplitude oscillations.

The Fairness axiom evaluates the equity of bandwidth allocation among competing agents, which is reflected in the distribution of their congestion window sizes at equilibrium. Simple metrics such as the ratio of minimum to maximum window size are ill-suited to a path-aware system where recent migrants naturally have small windows; we therefore use variance as a more robust statistical measure of distribution equality.

Axiom 4

(Fairness)
.

An MPCC protocol is said to be
\eta
-fair if, in the dynamic equilibrium, the variance of the congestion window sizes across the entire population of agents is equal to
\eta
. The fairness rating is thus:

\eta=\mathrm{Var}_{i\in A}\left[w_{i}(t)\right]_{t\geq t_{0}}

(23)

Finally, we introduce Responsiveness as a new axiom, unique to the path-aware context. It captures the agility with which agents can discover and exploit better-performing paths, a core capability of path-aware networking.

Axiom 5

(Responsiveness)
.

Unlike metrics derived from equilibrium properties, Responsiveness is an intrinsic parameter of the MPCC protocol itself. A protocol is said to be
\rho
-responsive, where
\rho

\text{Responsiveness}=\rho

While not an outcome of the system dynamics, we treat it as an axiom because it represents a desirable property that, as our analysis will show, stands in fundamental tension with other performance goals. Higher values of
\rho\in(0,1]
correspond to a more agile and adaptive protocol.

VII
Theoretical Insights and Trade-offs

Our axiomatic analysis of the system’s equilibria yields its first major theoretical insight, which challenges a common assumption in network protocol design. We formally establish that there exists
no fundamental trade-off between Efficiency (
\epsilon
), Loss Avoidance (
\lambda
), and Convergence (
\gamma_{\text{conv}}
)
. These three axioms, which collectively define a state of predictable, high-performance network operation, can be simultaneously optimized by a principled choice of protocol parameters. This optimum is achieved when the system is tuned to operate in a specific, critically-stable lossless equilibrium where the peak of the traffic oscillation,
\hat{L}^{(0)}
, precisely matches the available path capacity,
C_{\pi}
. In this state, the protocol is perfectly lossless (
\lambda=0
) by definition, while the oscillation’s minimal amplitude ensures both a high floor on utilization (maximizing
\epsilon
) and a high ratio of minimum-to-maximum load (maximizing
\gamma_{\text{conv}}
). Our analysis demonstrates that this optimal state is attainable through the joint tuning of a hard reset policy (i.e., a reset softness
\sigma\to 0
) and a carefully calibrated agent responsiveness (
\rho
).

This co-optimization, however, exposes a more fundamental and unavoidable trade-off that lies at the heart of path-aware protocol design: the inherent tension between predictable performance (Efficiency and Convergence) and the twin goals of
Fairness
and
Responsiveness
. Our analysis reveals that a protocol tuned for high responsiveness (
\rho\to 1
) naturally achieves a high degree of fairness. The constant flux of agent migration forces frequent congestion window resets across the population, preventing significant long-term window disparities and resulting in a compact distribution with low variance (
\eta\to 0
). Yet, this same mechanism is profoundly detrimental to performance, as the perpetual window resets lead to chronic link under-utilization (low
\epsilon
) and large-amplitude oscillations (low
\gamma_{\text{conv}}
). Conversely, a protocol with low responsiveness (
\rho\to 0
) is highly efficient and stable, as ”sticky” agents can maintain large windows that saturate link capacity. This performance comes at the direct cost of fairness, as it creates a highly bimodal distribution of window sizes between long-tenured agents and the few recent migrants, yielding a large variance (
\eta
). Responsiveness (
\rho
) is therefore the primary axis along which a protocol designer must navigate this core dilemma.

Beyond these internal trade-offs, our framework allows us to address a more fundamental question: what is the net performance impact of enabling end-host path selection compared to a static network with an optimal, fixed allocation of agents to paths? Our analysis reveals a nuanced answer, demonstrating that dynamic path selection can be both detrimental and, counter-intuitively, highly beneficial.

VII-A

Static Baseline for Comparison

To quantify these effects, we first define the axiomatic ratings for our baseline: a
static network
without path selection, where
N
agents are optimally distributed, with
a_{\pi}=N/P
agents on each path.

Efficiency (
\epsilon
):
In this static, synchronized system, the lowest flow volume occurs after a loss event, when the aggregate load
C_{\pi}
is reduced by
\gamma
.

\epsilon(Static)=\frac{\gamma\cdot C/P}{C/P}=\gamma

(28)

Loss Avoidance (
\lambda
):
The worst-case loss occurs when all
a_{\pi}
agents are at capacity and increase their window by the maximum possible additive increase,
\alpha^{\max}=\max_{\tau}\alpha(\tau)
.

\lambda(Static)=\frac{\alpha^{\max}\cdot a_{\pi}}{C_{\pi}}=\frac{\alpha^{\max}\cdot N}{C}

(29)

Convergence (
\gamma_{\text{conv}}
):
The convergence is the ratio of the minimum flow (from Eq. (
28
)) to the maximum flow (the peak of the loss event from Eq. (
29
)).

\gamma_{\text{conv}}(Static)=\frac{\gamma\cdot C/P}{C/P+\alpha^{\max}\cdot a_{\pi}}=\frac{\gamma\cdot C}{C+\alpha^{\max}\cdot N}

(30)

\eta
):
Assuming all agents start simultaneously and experience the same loss events, their windows remain perfectly synchronized.

\eta(Static)=0

(31)

These baseline ratings provide the formal ”ground truth” against which we compare the performance of our dynamic MPCC protocols.

VII-B

Performance Impact of Dynamic Selection

The primary peril occurs in inefficient, lossy equilibria, where the out-migration of agents following a loss event compounds the window reduction, deepening the trough in utilization and degrading efficiency below that of the static baseline, as validated in Figure
8
. However, our most striking finding is that path selection can offer a profound benefit by
de-synchronizing
agent behavior. In a static system, co-located agents tend to act in lockstep, creating highly correlated window increases that result in severe, periodic load overshoots. Agent migration fundamentally disrupts this phase-locking. By constantly remixing the population on each path, it averages out the agents’ continuity times (
\tau
), thereby smoothing the aggregate traffic growth. This de-synchronization can dramatically dampen peak loads, leading to significantly better loss avoidance and stability than the more brittle, synchronized static system.

VII-C

Design Implications

Taken together, these insights provide a formal design map for engineering next-generation, path-aware transport protocols. Our work suggests that designers should not view efficiency, stability, and loss avoidance as mutually exclusive goals to be compromised; instead, an optimal, lossless operating point exists and should be the target of a well-tuned protocol. The primary and unavoidable design choice, therefore, is not whether to achieve high performance, but how to position a protocol along the fundamental trade-off axis between that performance and the goals of fairness and responsiveness. The optimal setting for the responsiveness parameter,
\rho
, is thus context-dependent: a data center fabric might favor a low-
\rho
protocol to maximize stable throughput, whereas a mobile device could benefit from a high
\rho
to rapidly abandon failing links. Finally, our de-synchronization finding offers a new design principle: a controlled, non-zero level of agent migration can be viewed as a valuable tool for enhancing network stability and burst tolerance, reframing responsiveness from a purely end-user feature to a mechanism for improving collective network health.

For example, performance studies in production SCION networks have observed that dynamic, performance-driven path selection can, in certain cases, induce significant traffic volatility on inter-domain links, particularly when multiple sources react to the same network event

. This finding provides a real-world analogue to the synchronized, over-reactive ”herding” behavior that drives the oscillations in our model. Furthermore, the operational challenges reported in such studies reflect the essential trade-off between Responsiveness and Stability that our axiomatic framework formally derives. The practical need to balance the agility afforded by path selection against the imperative for predictable network performance confirms that our model, despite its abstractions, captures the first-order forces that govern these next-generation systems.

Discussion and Future Directions

The theoretical insights and empirical validations presented in this paper carry significant implications for the future design and deployment of path-aware network protocols. Our central contribution is the formalization of the performance trade-offs governed by agent responsiveness, which suggests that parameters like
\rho
(Responsiveness) and
\sigma
(Reset Softness) should be elevated from latent implementation artifacts to first-class, explicit design parameters for any multi-path protocol. This points toward a future of tunable transport systems, where network operators or even applications can select a performance profile—for instance, prioritizing maximum stable throughput for data center workloads versus agile responsiveness for mobile clients—based on a clear, quantitative understanding of the consequences. Such a move aligns with the broader community’s push toward application-aware and policy-driven networking, where the monolithic, one-size-fits-all model of transport is superseded by more adaptable, context-specific solutions

While our model provides a foundational understanding, its abstractions also define clear boundaries and motivate several rich avenues for future work. A primary direction is to extend our analysis to more complex and realistic network topologies, relaxing our assumptions to include heterogeneous paths with varying capacities and latencies, as well as scenarios with shared, non-disjoint bottlenecks. X
Conclusion

In this work, we addressed the critical stability challenge presented by the widespread adoption of end-host-driven path selection in next-generation network architectures. Motivated by a gap in the quantitative understanding of path-selection-induced oscillations, we developed and applied an axiomatic framework for joint path selection and congestion control. By modeling the system’s expected dynamics, we were able to analytically characterize the properties of the resulting dynamic equilibria. Our analysis culminates in the formal derivation of a fundamental trade-off, proving that a protocol’s fairness and responsiveness are intrinsically at odds with its ability to deliver efficient and stable performance. Our framework further reveals that efficiency, stability, and loss avoidance are not in conflict, and that agent migration itself can serve as a powerful de-synchronizing mechanism to improve, rather than degrade, network predictability.

Ultimately, the contribution of this work is a shift from a qualitative intuition to a quantitative science for the design of path-aware protocols. By providing a formal language and an analytical framework to reason about these complex, dynamic systems, we have replaced ambiguous notions of ”instability” with a precise, multi-dimensional map of the core performance trade-offs. The impact of this formalization is both direct and practical: it equips protocol designers and network operators with the principled tools needed to engineer next-generation systems that are not only flexible and powerful, but also robustly stable and performant. As the Internet continues its evolution toward a more user-centric and programmable architecture, this foundational understanding of how to balance the freedom of decentralized path selection with the imperative for collective stability will be essential.
