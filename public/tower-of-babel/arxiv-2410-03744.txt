Coval description of the boundary of a numerical range and the secondary values of a matrix
Petr Blaschke

Abstract
. The boundary of a numerical range of a finite matrix is always a nice curve (algebraic, closed and simple), but the equation it satisfies is often very complicated. We will show that, furthermore, there is no hope of describing these curves in terms of distances from the eigenvalues – as the dimension 2, where the numerical range is just an ellipse, would suggest. But, as we will show, there is a remarkably simple “coval” description in terms of distances to tangent lines . Provided that one measures these distances not only to the eigenvalues but also to additional points, the most important of which are secondary values – which we will define and describe their algebraic and geometric properties.

There are other types of “ovals”, but instead of points, the distances to given poles are measured to

Case 3).
The polynomial

D
EFINITION
68
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, where
n\geq 3
. A
secondary value of
A

x\in\mathbb{C}
is a solution of

(76)

{\rm tr}\,\left({\rm adj}(xI-A)(A^{\star}-\bar{A})\right)=0.

T
HEOREM
69
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
has the following block form

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\lambda\\
0\end{array}\begin{array}[]{c}b^{\star}\\
D\end{array}\right),

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is an eigenvalue of
A
,
b\in\mathbb{C}^{(n-1)\times 1}
,
D\in\mathbb{C}^{(n-1)\times(n-1)}
.

(1)

If
b=0
then
\lambda
is also a secondary value of
A
.

(2)

If
\lambda\not\in W(D)
and it is a secondary value, then
b=0
.

R
EMARK
70
.

In other words, the matrix
A
decomposes into a direct sum of
\lambda
and
D
(i.e.
b=0
) only if
\lambda
coincides with some secondary value. This condition is necessary but not sufficient. For that we need
\lambda
being outside the numerical range of
D
.

\star

Proof.

The block form of
A
implies

{\rm tr}\,{\rm adj}\left(xI-A\right)(A^{\star}-\bar{A})=b^{\star}{\rm adj}\left(xI-D\right)b+(x-\lambda){\rm tr}\,{\rm adj}\left(xI-D\right)(D^{\star}-\bar{D}).

Therefore

{\rm tr}\,{\rm adj}\left(\lambda I-A\right)(A^{\star}-\bar{A})=b^{\star}{\rm adj}\left(\lambda I-D\right)b.

For
b=0
we thus have

{\rm tr}\,{\rm adj}\left(\lambda I-A\right)(A^{\star}-\bar{A})=0,

proving the first claim.

For the remaining claim, suppose
b\not=0
. Since
\lambda\not\in W(D)
the eigenvalue
\lambda
is simple and
\lambda I-D
is an invertible matrix.

Denote
c:=(\lambda I-D)^{-1}b\not=0
so that
b=(\lambda I-D)c
and
b^{\star}=c^{\star}\left(\bar{\lambda}I-D^{\star}\right)
. We have

0={\rm tr}\,{\rm adj}\left(\lambda I-A\right)(A^{\star}-\bar{A})=b^{\star}{\rm adj}\left(\lambda I-D\right)b=\left|\lambda I-D\right|c^{\star}\left(\bar{\lambda}I-D^{\star}\right)c.

Discarding the non-zero determinant and taking the complex conjugate of both sides of the equation we obtain

0=c^{\star}(\lambda I-D)c,

rearranging

\lambda=\frac{c^{\star}Dc}{c^{\star}c}.

Therefore
\lambda\in W(D)
. A contradiction.
∎

P
ROPOSITION
71
.

The centroid of the secondary values is inside the numerical range. More precisely,
let
\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{n-2}
be the secondary values of
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, i.e. solutions of (
76
). Then

(77)

\chi:=\frac{\chi_{1}+\dots+\chi_{n-2}}{n-2}\in W(A).

Proof.

Note that by (
62
) we have

\chi=\frac{1}{n-2}\left({\rm tr}\,(A)-\frac{t_{2}(A)}{t_{1}(A)}\right).

We are going to show that
\chi
is a convex combination of elements of
W(A)
. To that end assume that
A
is upper triangular, i.e.

A_{j,k}=0
for
j>k
. Denote its values
A_{j,k}=:a_{j,k}
for
j<k
and the eigenvalues by
\lambda_{j}:=A_{j,j}
.

For every triple of distinct natural numbers
j<k<l
we define the following column vector
u_{jkl}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
:

\left(u_{jkl}\right)_{r}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{k,l}&r=j\\
-a_{j,l}&r=k\\
a_{j,k}&r=j\\
0&\text{else}\end{array}\right.

There are exactly
\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\
3\end{array}\right)
of such vectors each containing
3
off-diagonal elements of
A
out of
\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\
2\end{array}\right)
possible.
Obviously

u_{ijk}^{\star}u_{ijk}=|a_{jk}|^{2}+|a_{jl}|^{2}+|a_{kl}|^{2}.

In the sum

\sum_{j<k<l}u_{jkl}^{\star}u_{jkl}

we have
3\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\
3\end{array}\right)
summands and each off-diagonal element
|a_{j,k}|^{2}
appears exactly
3\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\
3\end{array}\right)/\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\
2\end{array}\right)=(n-2)
times. Therefore it holds

\sum_{j<k<l}u_{jkl}^{\star}u_{jkl}=(n-2)t_{1}(A).

Also

u_{jkl}^{\star}Au_{jkl}=\lambda_{j}|a_{k,l}|^{2}+\lambda_{k}|a_{j,l}|^{2}+\lambda_{l}|a_{j,k}|^{2}-a_{j,k}a_{k,l}\overline{a_{j,l}}.

Let us compute

\displaystyle\sum_{j<k<l}u_{jkl}^{\star}Au_{jkl}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j<k<l}\left(\lambda_{j}|a_{k,l}|^{2}+\lambda_{k}|a_{j,l}|^{2}+\lambda_{l}|a_{j,k}|^{2}\right)-\sum_{j<k<l}a_{j,k}a_{k,l}\overline{a_{j,l}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j<k<l}+\sum_{k<j<l}+\sum_{k<l<j}\right)\lambda_{j}|a_{k,l}|^{2}-\sum_{j<k<l}a_{j,k}a_{k,l}\overline{a_{j,l}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j}\sum_{k<l}-\sum_{j=k<l}-\sum_{k<j=l}\right)\lambda_{j}|a_{k,l}|^{2}-\sum_{j<k<l}a_{j,k}a_{k,l}\overline{a_{j,l}}

\displaystyle={\rm tr}\,(A)t_{1}(A)-\sum_{k<l}\lambda_{k}|a_{k,l}|^{2}-\sum_{k<l}\lambda_{l}|a_{k,l}|^{2}-\sum_{j<k<l}a_{j,k}a_{k,l}\overline{a_{j,l}}

\displaystyle={\rm tr}\,(A)t_{1}(A)-\sum_{j\leq k\leq l}a_{j,k}a_{k,l}\overline{a_{j,l}}+\sum_{j=k=l}a_{j,k}a_{k,l}\overline{a_{j,l}}

\displaystyle={\rm tr}\,(A)t_{1}(A)-{\rm tr}\,\left(A^{2}A^{\star}\right)+{\rm tr}\,\left(A^{2}\bar{A}\right)

\displaystyle={\rm tr}\,(A)t_{1}(A)-t_{2}(A).

Dividing bots sides by
(n-2)t_{1}(A)
we obtain

\chi=\sum_{j<k<l}\frac{u_{jkl}^{\star}Au_{jkl}}{u_{jkl}^{\star}u_{jkl}}\gamma_{j},\qquad\gamma_{j}:=\frac{u_{jkl}^{\star}u_{jkl}}{(n-2)t_{1}(A)}.

Since
\sum_{j}\gamma_{j}=1
the number
\chi
is a convex combination of elements of
W(A)
and therefore
\chi\in W(A)
.
∎

Missing case of Theorem

When an eigenvalue

in the notation of Theorem

This easy to satisfy. Suppose

for some

Then

In fact

and since the matrix

is positive definite (at least for

Can we still draw some conclusions about numerical range of

in this case?

Properties of a ”2-normal” matrix

For a normal matrix
A
we have

\det\left(p(A)\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}p(\lambda_{j}),

i.e. in our greedy expansion formula (
57
) only single term survives.

What are the properties of a matrix
A
for which
two
terms survive?

D
EFINITION
72
.

We will call a matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}

2-normal
if

(78)

\det\left(p(A)\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}p(\lambda_{j})-\frac{t_{1}(A)}{4}\prod_{j=1}^{n-2}p(\chi_{j}).

Two-normal matrices are very nice, geometrically, since their numerical range can be described using eigenvalues and the secondary values only.
Furthermore, the secondary values
\chi_{j}
, in this case, are truly behaving like an “antiparticles” to eigenvalues exactly the same way as in dimension 3 – when one coincides with the other they “annihilate” and the numerical range is reduced to a lower dimensional one (plus a possible point). No further condition needed.

There is a simple counting argument from which we can get a rough idea how many 2-normal matrices there are. An upper triangular
n\times n
complex matrix with fixed eigenvalues has precisely
n(n-1)/2
free complex parameters, or
n^{2}-n
free real parameters.

From (
78
) we know that any line connecting an eigenvalue
\lambda
with some secondary value
\chi
is a solution to
\det\left(p(A)\right)=0
. Therefore computing the tangential angle
\theta
and the pedal coordinate
p
of such line, we have

\det\left(pI-\frac{e^{{\rm i}\theta}}{2{\rm i}}A^{\star}+\frac{e^{-{\rm i}\theta}}{2{\rm i}}A\right)=0.

A single (non-linear) equation in parameters of
A
.
There are
n(n-2)
such lines, therefore we have
n(n-2)
equations. Therefore – roughly – the space of 2-normal matrices should have at most
n
real parameters.

In the literature, there exists so-called
binormal matrices
– see
[
21
]
. A matrix
A
is binormal if
A^{\star}A
,
AA^{\star}
commute.
The anonymous referee has suggested the following example of a 2-normal matrix which is not binormal. Consider

A(b):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&1&b&1\\
0&0&1&b\\
0&0&0&1\\
0&0&0&0\end{array}\right).

Easy calculation shows that

\det{p(A(b))}=p^{4}-\frac{2|b|^{2}+4}{4}p(\chi_{+})p(\chi_{-})-\frac{\gamma_{2}}{16},

where

\chi_{\pm}:=\frac{-(b+\bar{b})\pm\sqrt{b^{2}+{\bar{b}}^{2}-4}}{2|b|^{2}+4},

and, crucially,

\gamma_{2}=4-(b+\bar{b})^{2}+|b|^{4}=b_{2}^{4}+2b_{1}^{2}b_{2}^{2}+(b_{1}^{2}-2)^{2},\qquad b=b_{1}+{\rm i}b_{2}.

We can see that
\gamma_{2}\geq 0
and
\gamma_{2}=0
if and only if
b=\pm\sqrt{2}
. Interestingly, these values of
b
are precisely those for which the secondary values coincides, i.e.
\chi_{+}=\chi_{-}
. This might be a coincidence, though.
