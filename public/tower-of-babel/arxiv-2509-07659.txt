Sparse domination via the Calderón-Zygmund decomposition: the example of Dini-smooth kernels
Fernando Ballesta-Yagüe, José M. Conde-Alonso

Abstract
. In this expository article, we briefly survey the main known schemes of proof of sparse domination principles within harmonic analysis. We then use the one based on the Calderón-Zygmund decomposition to prove a dual sparse domination estimate for Calderón-Zymgund operators with Dini-smooth kernels, with an eye on the difficulties that arise when trying to transfer the argument to spaces with nondoubling measures.



Introduction

The last decade has seen the rapid development of the sparse domination technique within harmonic analysis. Given a measure space
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
and
0<\eta<1
, a family
\mathcal{S}\subset\Sigma
is called
\eta
-sparse if for each
Q\in\mathcal{S}
, there exists
E_{Q}\subset Q
with two properties:

•

\mu(E_{Q})\geq\eta\mu(Q)
for each
Q\in\mathcal{S}
.

•

For each
Q\neq R\in\mathcal{S}
,
E_{Q}
and
E_{R}
are disjoint.

In the simplest version, a sparse operator is an averaging one of the form

\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}f(x)=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}\langle f\rangle_{Q}\mathds{1}_{Q}(x),\quad\mbox{where}\quad\langle f\rangle_{Q}=\frac{1}{\mu(Q)}\int_{Q}fd\mu,

and the family
\mathcal{S}
is
\eta
-sparse. Whenever
\mathcal{S}
is composed of reasonable sets,
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}
is a self-adjoint operator which is bounded on
L^{p}(\mu)
for
1<p<\infty
and which is of weak-type
(1,1)
. A sparse domination for an operator
T
refers to an inequality of one of the kinds below:

\textbf{(i)}\;\|Tf\|_{\mathbb{X}}\lesssim\|\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}f\|_{\mathbb{X}},\quad\textbf{(ii)}\;\left|\langle Tf,g\rangle\right|\lesssim\langle\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}|f|,|g|\rangle,\quad\textbf{(iii)}\;|Tf(x)|\lesssim\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}|f|(x)\;a.e.

\mathbb{X}
is some appropriate Banach space of functions in inequality
(i)
, which is weaker than
(ii)
, while that is clearly implied by
(iii)
. In all the above, the family
\mathcal{S}
is
\eta
-sparse for some fixed valued of
\eta
, but the family itself does depend on
f
and
g
. A sparse domination –of one of the kinds
(ii)
and
(iii)
, which are the ones of more interest to us here– can be viewed as a somewhat strong quantification of the weak-type behavior of the operator
T
, if the geometry of the sets in
\mathcal{S}
is nice enough. In this paper, we will only consider families of cubes with sides parallel to the coordinate axes.

Sparse domination has been first studied in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with its importance coming from weighted inequalities. This is so because
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}
–when
\mathcal{S}
is a family of cubes– can be shown to easily satisfy the
A_{2}
conjecture: if
w
belongs to the Muckenhaupt class
A_{2}
, then a beautiful argument from
[
CUMP12
]
implies that

\|\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}f\|_{L^{2}(w)}\lesssim[w]_{A_{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}(w)},

with implicit constant independent of
w
. This matches the sharp weighted bound for Calderón-Zygmund singular integral operators, proved in full generality in
[
Hyt12
]
. Therefore, a sparse domination for a Calderón-Zygmund operator
T
of any of the kinds
(i)-(iii)
above yields a new proof of the
A_{2}
theorem, which was a major motivation in the beginning of the theory. Later, sparse domination results have been used to prove sharp weighted inequalities beyond the
A_{2}
conjecture, including weighted weak-type estimates or estimates in non-Euclidean contexts; some weighted estimates can even be attained without the use of extrapolation, using only sparse domination techniques
[
Moe12
]
. Even –previously unknown– unweighted weak-type estimates have been shown as a consequence of delicate sparse domination arguments, proving the applicability of the theory beyond its original goals. A very nice account of the development of the field can be found in
[
Per19
]
.

In this note, we focus on sparse domination principles for Calderón-Zygmund singular integral operators on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A Calderón-Zygmund operator is an
L^{2}
-bounded linear map that formally admits the representation

Tf(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(x,y)f(y)\,dy,

for appropriate functions
f
and points
x
, and whose kernel
K
satisfies
|K(x,y)|\lesssim|x-y|^{-d}
when
x\neq y
plus an additional smoothness condition, that we will make precise below and which can be the source of meaningful technical difficulties. There are several ways in which one can achieve sparse domination for these operators:

•

Sparse domination based on median oscillation decomposition:
Let
m_{Q}(f)
denote a median of a function
f
over a cube
Q
, and
\omega_{\lambda}(f;Q)
its oscillation off a
\lambda
-fraction, that is

\omega_{\lambda}(f;Q)=\inf_{A\subset Q;|A|\geq(1-\lambda)|Q|}\sup_{x,y\in A}|f(x)-f(y)|.

Lerner’s median oscillation formula states the following:

|f(x)-m_{Q}(f)|\lesssim\sum_{R\in\mathcal{S}}\omega_{\lambda(d)}(f;Q),

for some sparse family
\mathcal{S}
and some dimensional quantity
0<\lambda(d)<1
. Applying the above to
T
yields a pointwise bound for it in terms of certain non-cancellative Haar shifts. This allows one to prove sparse domination in the sense
(i)
, yielding the aforementioned simplification of the
A_{2}
theorem
[
Ler13a
,
Ler13b
]
. Later, the same technique allowed the first sparse domination proofs in the pointwise sense
(iii)

[
CAR16
,
LN19
]
. In all these results, the kernel smoothness is required to be the so-called logarithmic Dini condition, a somewhat strong one.

•

Sparse domination based on grand maximal functions:
In
[
Lac17
]
and the works that followed it, variants of the following (local) grand maximal operator are introduced:

\mathcal{M}_{Q}f(x)=\sup_{x\in R\subset Q}\esssup_{y\in R}|T(f\mathds{1}_{3Q\setminus 3R})(y)|.

This grand maximal operator is of weak-type
(1,1)
, and so it can be used to run a stopping time argument that simplifies the proof above and eliminates the passage through Haar shift operators. Using this scheme, Lacey was able to achieve sparse domination for operators with kernels satisfying Dini smoothness. The method was further streamlined in subsequent works
[
Ler16
,
LO20
]
.

•

Sparse domination based on the Calderón-Zygmund decomposition:
One scheme of proof of sparse domination of the kind
(ii)
uses infinitely many Calderón-Zygmund decompositions and was initially introduced to study non-kernel operators
[
BFP16
]
. Later, it was used to study dyadic variants of Calderón-Zygmund operators
[
CDPO18
]
and rough singular integrals
[
CACDPO17
]
. This scheme has later evolved using local, single-scale behavior of the operators under consideration as a tool to achieve the estimates
[
BRS24
]
. This class of single-scale gains of the operators is usually termed
L^{p}
-improving.

There are of course many other recent developments that carry the theory much further, and we refer again to
[
Per19
]
for a more systematic account of sparse domination for objects of highly oscillatory nature, Banach/operator valued settings, the vector valued theory with matrix weights, and other settings. All in all, a large part of the analysis of interesting operators near the
L^{1}
endpoint has been transferred to the new language of sparse domination. One outstanding remaining question of interest to us, however, is that of nonhomogeneous spaces, that we discuss in more detail in Section
3
. We want to contextualize some of the difficulties encountered in that setting in our proof below. Indeed, in this note we use the scheme of proof, neatly outlined in
[
CDPO18
]
, to study smooth Calderón-Zygmund operators. Our main result below is strictly contained in many others cited above, one of them being
[
CACDPO17
]
, where a similar approach is taken. We also follow arguments from
[
BRS24
]
at times.

Theorem A
.

Let
T
be a Calderón-Zygmund operator whose kernel satisfies the Dini condition, and
0<\eta<1
small enough. For each pair
(f_{1},f_{2})
of nonnegative, bounded functions supported on some cube
Q_{0}\in\mathscr{D}
, there exists an
\eta
-sparse family of cubes
\mathcal{S}
such that

\left|\left\langle Tf_{1},f_{2}\right\rangle\right|\lesssim\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}\left\langle f_{1}\right\rangle_{Q}\left\langle f_{2}\right\rangle_{Q}|Q|.

The proof of Theorem
A
splits the kernel
K
of the Calderón-Zygmund operator
T
using a radial Littlewood-Paley decomposition. This was already used by
[
HRT17
]
in order to prove weighted inequalities for rough singular integrals, and is the point of view adopted in
[
CACDPO17
]
as well. Then, a careful sequence of Whitney decompositions of the relevant level sets allows one to estimate a piece of the operator in each step using the simultaneous Calderón-Zygmund decomposition of the two functions and is used to construct
\mathcal{S}
. As has been noted elsewhere
[
LMA17
]
, the family
\mathcal{S}
in the statement must depend on the pair of functions
(f_{1},f_{2})
, but does not depend on
T
.

The scheme of proof described above has been carried over several times in the literature. We have chosen to present the (smooth) Calderón-Zygmund case –with pointwise smoothness bounds– because we find that it illustrates the method in a clear way, without the additional technicalities of other settings, like the rough one of
[
CACDPO17
]
. On the other hand, it does incorporate geometric difficulties, like interactions between cubes that are near one another, which are not present in the dyadic setting of
[
CDPO18
]
. Therefore, we believe that it is better to display them because it is often a source of difficulties when one tries to transfer the full scheme to other settings or the analysis of other operators. Our proof is reasonably self-contained, relying only on the classical Whitney decomposition, while all other details are provided. Finally, the presentation of said details allows us to discuss the nonhomogeneous case, where significant challenges remain open and where it is not even clear that the sparse strategy is the right one to deal with weighted inequalities.

The rest of this note is structured as follows: in Section
1
we explain the two main (classical) tools that appear in the proof of the sparse domination result: Whitney coverings and a modification of the Calderón-Zygmund decomposition. We also introduce Calderón-Zygmund singular integral operators and the decomposition in scales of their kernels that we are going to use in our analysis. Section
2
contains the proof of Theorem
A
via an iterative scheme. In Section
3
we analyze possible extensions of the main result to nonhomogeneous contexts, where partial results have been achieved, and the challenges that one must overcome to obtain a more satisfactory result than the existing ones.

1.
Calderón-Zygmund decomposition, Whitney covering and singular integrals

We use the standard dyadic system
\mathscr{D}
of half-open cubes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as a tool below, even if a martingale structure is not strictly necessary in order to prove a sparse domination result (see, for example,
[
CADPPV22
]
). Indeed, the sparse family
\mathcal{S}
that appears in the statement of Theorem
A
is not a subfamily of
\mathscr{D}
.

1.1.
The Whitney covering theorem

The Whitney decomposition is a clever way to partition an open set
\Omega
into dyadic cubes whose size is proportional to their distance to the boundary of
\Omega
. We will use the following version of the theorem, which has features that we shall use in our estimates below and the constants that will be needed below. To that end, define
k(d)
as the smallest positive integer such that
2^{k(d)-1}>\sqrt{d}
, and set

\Lambda_{d}\coloneqq 2^{k(d)+2}+1.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be open and fix a Whitney constant
C_{\mathcal{W}}>\sqrt{d}
. There exists a family
\mathcal{P}=\{L_{j}\}\subset\mathscr{D}
such that

(1)

\bigcup_{j}L_{j}=\Omega
, and the cubes
L_{j}
are pairwise disjoint.

(2)

(C_{\mathcal{W}}-\sqrt{d})\cdot\ell(L_{j})\leq\operatorname{dist}\left(L_{j},\Omega^{c}\right)\leq 2C_{\mathcal{W}}\cdot\ell(L_{j})
.

Moreover, if we take
C_{\mathcal{W}}>\sqrt{d}(2\Lambda_{d}+1)
, then

2\Lambda_{d}L_{j}\subset\Omega\text{ for all }j,

and

\text{if }\Lambda_{d}L_{j}\cap\Lambda_{d}L_{k}\neq\varnothing,\text{ then }\ell(L_{j})\sim\ell(L_{k}).

The proof of the first two properties can be found in
[
Ste70
, Ch. IV, Th. 1]
, if we define the sets
\Omega_{k}
that appear in that proof by

\Omega_{k}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:C_{\mathcal{W}}2^{-k}<\operatorname{dist}\left(x,F\right)\leq C_{\mathcal{W}}2^{-k+1}\right\}.

1.2.
Calderón-Zygmund decomposition with respect to a family of cubes

As above, let
\Omega
be open and let
\mathcal{P}=\{L_{j}\}_{j\in J}\subset\mathscr{D}
be a pairwise disjoint family such that
\bigcup L_{j}=\Omega
. Let
f\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Define

\displaystyle b_{L_{j}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(f-\langle f\rangle_{L_{j}})\mathds{1}_{L_{j}},\quad\mbox{and}\quad b\coloneqq\sum_{j\in J}b_{L_{j}};

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle\coloneqq f\mathds{1}_{\Omega^{c}}+\sum_{j}\langle f\rangle_{L_{j}}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}}.

The decomposition
f=g+b
is called the Calderón-Zygmund decomposition of
f
adapted to
\mathcal{P}
. Calderón-Zygmund decompositions are useful when the Calderón-Zygmund cubes are maximal with respect to the behavior of a certain maximal operator. We use the standard uncentered one:

\mathcal{M}f(x)=\sup_{Q\ni x}\langle|f|\rangle_{Q},

with the supremum running over half-open cubes with sides parallel to the coordinate axes. We provide the details of the properties of the Calderón-Zygmund decomposition in this formulation.

Lemma 1.2
.

Let
\lambda>0
be fixed and
0\leq f\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be such that
\operatorname{supp}\left(f\right)\subset Q
. Let
\Omega\subset Q
be open. Let
\{L_{j}\}
be the Whitney decomposition of
\Omega
. If

(1.1)

\mathcal{M}(f)\mathds{1}_{\Omega^{c}}\lesssim\lambda,

where
\Omega^{c}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus{\Omega}
, then

(1.2)

\|g\|_{\infty}\lesssim\lambda,

(1.3)

\sum_{j}\|b_{L_{j}}\|_{1}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{1}(Q)},

and if
R
is a cube such that there exists
L_{j_{0}}\subset R
, then

(1.4)

\langle|b|\rangle_{R}\lesssim\lambda.

Proof.

We start proving (
1.2
). Since the sets
\Omega^{c}
and
\{L_{j}\}_{j}
are all pairwise disjoint, we first use (
1.1
) to get

g\mathds{1}_{\Omega^{c}}=f\mathds{1}_{\Omega^{c}}\leq\mathcal{M}(f)\mathds{1}_{\Omega^{c}}\lesssim\lambda,

On the other hand, by Theorem
1.1
, there exists
\mu\sim 1
such that
\mu L_{j}\cap\Omega^{c}\neq\varnothing
. Therefore,

\langle f\rangle_{L_{j}}\lesssim\langle f\rangle_{\mu L_{j}}\leq\mathcal{M}f(x_{j})\quad\text{for some }x_{j}\in(\mu L_{j})\cap\Omega^{c}.

By (
1.1
),
\mathcal{M}f(x_{j})\lesssim\lambda
, so
\langle f\rangle_{L_{j}}\lesssim\lambda
. Therefore,

g\mathds{1}_{L_{j}}=\langle f\rangle_{L_{j}}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}}\lesssim\lambda.

To prove (
1.3
), we just compute

\|b_{L_{j}}\|_{L^{1}}=\|(f-\langle f\rangle_{L_{j}})\mathds{1}_{L_{j}}\|_{L^{1}}\leq\|f\mathds{1}_{L_{j}}\|_{L^{1}}+\langle f\rangle_{L_{j}}\|\mathds{1}_{L_{j}}\|_{L^{1}}=2\|f\mathds{1}_{L_{j}}\|_{L^{1}}.

Since the
\{L_{j}\}
are pairwise disjoint and
\bigcup L_{j}=\Omega\subseteq Q
, we have

\sum_{j}\|b_{L_{j}}\|_{1}\leq\sum 2\|f\mathds{1}_{L_{j}}\|_{L^{1}}=2\int_{\bigcup L_{j}}|f|\leq 2\|f\|_{L^{1}(Q)}.

To prove (
1.4
), let
R
be such that
L_{j_{0}}\subset R
for some
j_{0}
. We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|R|}\int_{R}|b(x)|\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{R}\int_{R}\left|\sum(f(x)-\langle f\rangle_{L_{j}})\mathds{1}_{L_{j}}(x)\right|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|R|}\sum_{j}\int_{R\cap L_{j}}|f(x)|\,dx+\frac{1}{|R|}\sum_{j}\langle f\rangle_{L_{j}}|R\cap L_{j}|

Since
\mu L_{j}\cap\Omega^{c}\neq\varnothing
for every
j
and
L_{j_{0}}\subset R
, then
\mu R\cap\Omega^{c}\neq\varnothing
. So if
x_{i}\in\mu R\cap\Omega^{c}
, then

\frac{1}{|R|}\sum_{j}\int_{R\cap L_{j}}|f|\leq\frac{1}{|R|}\int_{R}|f|\lesssim\frac{1}{|\mu R|}\int_{\mu R}|f|\leq\mathcal{M}f(x_{i})\lesssim\lambda.

To bound the other sum, we just use that
\langle f\rangle_{L_{j}}\lesssim\lambda
and the disjointness of
\{L_{j}\}
:

\frac{1}{|R|}\sum_{j}\langle f\rangle_{L_{j}}|R\cap L_{j}|\lesssim\frac{1}{|R|}\sum_{j}\lambda|R\cap L_{j}|\leq\lambda\frac{1}{|R|}|R|=\lambda.\qed

1.3.
Dini-smooth Calderón-Zygmund operators

A Calderón-Zygmund operator (abbreviated CZO) is a linear operator
T
, initially defined over Schwartz functions, such that:

•

T
can be extended to a bounded linear operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

•

For all
f\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
Tf
is given in integral form by a kernel
K\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{(x,y):x=y\}\to\mathbb{C}
, in the sense that

Tf(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(x,y)f(y)\,dy,\quad\text{ for all }x\not\in\operatorname{supp}(f).

•

K
satisfies the size condition

|K(x,y)|\lesssim\frac{1}{|x-y|^{d}},\quad x\neq y.

We assume additional smoothness on
K
: a modulus of continuity
\omega
is a function
\omega\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
which is continuous and non-decreasing. We say that it satisfies the Dini condition if

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\omega(t)}{t}\,dt<\infty.

We require that there exists such a modulus of continuity
\omega
so that, if
2|x-x^{\prime}|\leq|x-y|
, then

|K(x,y)-K(x^{\prime},y)|+|K(y,x)-K(y,x^{\prime})|\lesssim\frac{1}{|x-y|^{d}}\omega\left(\frac{|x-x^{\prime}|}{|x-y|}\right).

We break
K
into scales using a standard Littlewood-Paley type decomposition. Let
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,1]
be a radial function in
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\operatorname{supp}\left(\phi\right)\subset\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:0<|x|<2\right\}
and such that
\phi\equiv 1
on
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:0\leq|x|\leq 1\}
. We set
\psi(x)\coloneqq\phi(x)-\phi(2x)
, so that
\operatorname{supp}\left(\psi\right)\subset\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:1/2<|x|<2\right\}
. The family
\left\{\operatorname{Dil}_{2^{k}}^{\infty}\psi\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}=\left\{\psi\left(\frac{\cdot}{2^{k}}\right)\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
forms a partition of unity, in the sense that

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi\left(\frac{x}{2^{k}}\right)=1,\quad x\neq 0.

We next truncate
K
using
\psi
. Set

K_{s}(x,y)\coloneqq\psi\left(\frac{x-y}{2^{s}}\right)K(x,y),\quad s\in\mathbb{Z},

and define the smooth single-scale operator

T_{s_{0}}f(x)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K_{s_{0}}(x,y)f(y)\,dy,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

and the smooth multi-scale truncations

T_{(s_{0})}f(x)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\sum_{s\geq s_{0}}K_{s}(x,y)\right)f(y)\,dy,\quad T^{(s_{0})}f\coloneqq Tf-T_{(s_{0}+1)}f.

The integrals defining
T_{(s_{0})}f
and
T_{s}f
converge absolutely for every
f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
1\leq p<\infty
, by Hölder’s inequality. Therefore, the truncations are well-defined in the pointwise sense. For each
s_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
, the operators
T_{s_{0}}
,
T_{(s_{0})}
and
T^{(s_{0})}
are Calderón-Zygmund operators with kernels
K_{s_{0}}(x,y)
,
\sum_{s\geq s_{0}}K_{s}(x,y)
and
\sum_{s\leq s_{0}}K_{s}(x,y)
respectively, with Dini smoothness given by the modulus of continuity
\widetilde{\omega}(t)\coloneqq t+\omega(t)
. Moreover, all these operators are bounded in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
uniformly with respect to
s_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
. The adjoint operator of
T_{s}
is given by

(T_{s})^{\ast}g(x)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K_{s}(y,x)g(y)\,dx.

Therefore,
(T_{s})^{\ast}
has the same structure as
T_{s}
.
T_{s}
satisfies the
L^{p}
-improving-type inequality

(1.5)

\|T_{s}(f)\|_{\infty}\lesssim_{d}2^{-sd}\|f\|_{L^{1}}.

Indeed, if
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we can apply the size condition to obtain

|T_{s}(f)(x)|=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K_{s}(x,y)f(y)\,dy\right|\leq\int_{|x-y|\in[2^{s-1},2^{s+1}]}\frac{|f(y)|}{|x-y|^{d}}\,dy\lesssim 2^{-sd}\|f\|_{L^{1}}.

The same estimate is valid for
(T_{s})^{\ast}
. We end this section with two important localization estimates. Given
Q_{0}\in\mathscr{D}
, define
s_{Q_{0}}=\log_{2}(\ell(Q_{0}))
and denote
S(Q_{0})=s_{Q_{0}}+k(d)
. For
s>S(Q_{0})
, we define the annulus

A_{s}(Q_{0})\coloneqq\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x-x_{Q_{0}}|\in[2^{-2}\cdot 2^{s},2^{k(d)}\cdot 2^{s}]\right\},

where
x_{Q}
denotes, here and throughout, the center of a cube
Q
.

Lemma 1.3
.

Let
Q_{0}\in\mathscr{D}
and
s>S(Q_{0})
. Then

(1.6)

\text{if }\quad K_{s}(x,y)\neq 0\quad\text{ for }\quad y\in Q_{0},\quad\text{ then }\quad x\in A_{s}(Q_{0}).

As a consequence,
\int_{Q_{0}}K_{s}(x,y)f(y)\,dy\neq 0
only if
x\in A_{s}(Q_{0})
. Therefore

(1.7)

Tf(x)=T^{(S(Q_{0}))}f(x)+\sum_{s>S(Q_{0})}\left(\int_{Q_{0}}K_{s}(x,y)f(y)\,dy\right)\mathds{1}_{A_{s}(Q_{0})}(x).

Proof.

Let
s>S(Q_{0})
, that is,
s=s_{Q_{0}}+k(d)+\delta
for some integer
\delta\geq 1
. Notice that
K_{s}(x,y)\neq 0
implies that
|x-y|\in[2^{s-1},2^{s+1}]
.
Notice also that, since
y\in Q_{0}
, then

|x_{Q_{0}}-y|\leq\frac{\operatorname{diam}\left(Q_{0}\right)}{2}=\frac{2^{s_{Q_{0}}}\sqrt{d}}{2}<2^{s_{Q_{0}}+k(d)-2}.

Therefore, we obtain the upper bound

\displaystyle|x-x_{Q_{0}}|\leq|x-y|+|y-x_{Q_{0}}|<2^{s+1}+2^{s_{Q_{0}}+k(d)-2}\leq 2^{s_{Q_{0}}+k(d)+\delta}(2+2^{-2})\leq 2^{s}\cdot 2^{k(d)},

where the last step is true because
k(d)\geq 2
for
d\geq 1
. We also obtain the lower bound

\displaystyle|x-x_{Q_{0}}|\geq|x-y|-|x_{Q_{0}}-y|\geq 2^{s-1}-2^{s_{Q_{0}}+k(d)-2}=2^{s_{Q_{0}}+k(d)+\delta}[2^{-1}-2^{-2-\delta}]\geq 2^{s}\cdot 2^{-2}.

∎

Lemma 1.4
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
Q\in\mathscr{D}
. Then

(1.8)

\operatorname{supp}\left(T^{(S(Q))}(f\mathds{1}_{Q})\right)\subset\Lambda_{d}Q.

Proof.

Let
x\not\in\alpha Q
for some
\alpha>1
. Then,
x\not\in\operatorname{supp}\left(f\mathds{1}_{Q}\right)
, so

T^{(s_{Q})}(f\mathds{1}_{Q})(x)=T(f\mathds{1}_{Q})(x)-T_{(s_{Q})}(f\mathds{1}_{Q})(x)=\int_{Q}\left(\sum_{s\leq s_{Q}}K_{s}(x,y)\right)f(y)\,dy.

Since
K_{s}(x,y)=\psi(\frac{x-y}{2^{s}})K(x,y)
and
\operatorname{supp}\left(\psi(\frac{\cdot}{2^{s}})\right)\subset[2^{s-1},2^{s+1}]
, we want to choose
\alpha
in such a way that we guarantee
|x-y|>2^{S(Q)+1}
, so that
|x-y|>2^{s+1}
for every
s\leq S(Q)
and hence
T^{(S(Q))}(f\mathds{1}_{Q})(x)=0
. For every
y\in Q
and
x\not\in\alpha Q
, we have

|x-y|\geq\frac{1}{2}\ell(\alpha Q)-\frac{1}{2}\ell(Q)=\frac{1}{2}\ell(Q)[\alpha-1]=2^{s_{Q}-1}[\alpha-1].

So to assure that
|x-y|\geq 2^{S(Q)+1}
for every
x\not\in\alpha Q
and
y\in Q
, it is enough that

2^{s_{Q}-1}[\alpha-1]\geq 2^{S(Q)+1}\iff\alpha-1\geq 2^{k(d)+2}\iff\alpha\geq 2^{k(d)+2}+1=\Lambda_{d}.\qed

2.
Sparse domination

2.1.
The main estimate

The proof of Theorem
A
is based on the following lemma, that will be applied in an iterative way.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
Q\in\mathscr{D}
. Let
0\leq f_{1},f_{2}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\operatorname{supp}\left(f_{1}\right)\subset Q
and
\operatorname{supp}\left(f_{2}\right)\subset\Lambda_{d}Q
. Let
\mathcal{P}=\left\{L_{j}\right\}_{j\in J}
be the family of Whitney cubes of some open set
\Omega\subset 2\Lambda_{d}Q
chosen with Whitney constant
C_{\mathcal{W}}>\sqrt{d}(2\Lambda_{d}+1)
, and suppose that

(\mathcal{M}f_{i})\mathds{1}_{\Omega^{c}}\lesssim\left\langle f_{i}\right\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q},\quad i=1,2.

Then

\left|\left\langle T^{(S(Q))}(f_{1}),f_{2}\right\rangle\right|\leq C\left\langle f_{1}\right\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\left\langle f_{2}\right\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}|\Lambda_{d}Q|+\sum_{j\in J}\left|\left\langle T^{(S(L_{j}))}(f_{1}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}}),f_{2}\right\rangle\right|.

Proof.

Let

T_{\mathcal{P}}f(x)\coloneqq\sum_{j\in J}T^{(S(L_{j}))}(f\mathds{1}_{L_{j}}).

Then we can split

\displaystyle\langle T^{(S(Q))}(f_{1}),f_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle T^{(S(Q))}(f_{1})-T_{\mathcal{P}}(f_{1}),f_{2}\rangle+\langle T_{\mathcal{P}}(f_{1}),f_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle T^{(S(Q))}(f_{1})-T_{\mathcal{P}}(f_{1}),f_{2}\rangle+\sum_{j\in J}\langle T^{(S(L_{j}))}(f_{1}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}}),f_{2}\rangle.

It is enough to prove the bound

|\langle T^{(S(Q))}(f_{1})-T_{\mathcal{P}}(f_{1}),f_{2}\rangle|\lesssim\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\langle f_{2}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}|\Lambda_{d}Q|.

We split the left-hand side using the Calderón-Zygmund decomposition from Lemma
1.2
, applied to each function
f_{i}
and the collection
\mathcal{P}
(corresponding to a level
\lambda_{i}=\langle f_{i}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}
). By the linearity of the operators involved, we get

\displaystyle\langle(T^{(S(Q))}-T_{\mathcal{P}})(f_{1}),f_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(T^{(S(Q))}-T_{\mathcal{P}})(g_{1}),g_{2}\rangle+\langle(T^{(S(Q))}-T_{\mathcal{P}})(g_{1}),b_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\langle(T^{(S(Q))}-T_{\mathcal{P}})(b_{1}),g_{2}\rangle+\langle(T^{(S(Q))}-T_{\mathcal{P}})(b_{1}),b_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{II}+\mathrm{III}+\mathrm{IV}.

We estimate the four terms in turn.

Estimate of
\mathrm{I}
:
First we split

|\mathrm{I}|\leq|\langle T^{(S(Q))}(g_{1}),g_{2}\rangle|+|\langle T_{\mathcal{P}}(g_{1}),g_{2}\rangle|\leq|\langle T^{(S(Q))}g_{1},g_{2}\rangle|+\sum_{j}|\langle T^{(S(L_{j}))}(g_{1}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}}),g_{2}\rangle|.

For the first term, we use Cauchy-Schwarz, the uniform
L^{2}
-boundedness of
\{T^{(s)}\}_{s\in\mathbb{Z}}
and (
1.2
):

\displaystyle|\langle T^{(s_{Q})}(g_{1}),g_{2}\rangle|\lesssim_{T}\|g_{1}\|_{2}\|g_{2}\|_{2}\leq\|g_{1}\|_{\infty}|\Lambda_{d}Q|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|g_{2}\|_{\infty}|\Lambda_{d}Q|^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\langle f_{2}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}|\Lambda_{d}Q|.

For the second one, using the localization of Lemma
1.4
, we have

|\langle T^{(S(L_{j}))}(g_{1}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}}),g_{2}\rangle|=|\langle T^{(S(L_{j}))}(g_{1}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}}),g_{2}\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}}\rangle|,

and so

|\langle T^{(S(L_{j}))}(g_{1}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}}),g_{2}\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}}\rangle|\leq\|T^{(S(L_{j}))}(g_{1}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}})\|_{2}\|g_{2}\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}}\|_{2}\lesssim\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\langle f_{2}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}|L_{j}|,

arguing as before. Since
\{L_{j}\}
are disjoint with
\bigcup L_{j}=\Omega\subseteq 2\Lambda_{d}Q
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j}|\langle T^{(S(L_{j}))}(g_{1}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}}),g_{2}\rangle|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j}\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\langle f_{2}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}|L_{j}|\lesssim\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\langle f_{2}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}|\Lambda_{d}Q|.

Estimate of
\mathrm{II}
:
Since
g_{1}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}}=\langle f_{1}\rangle_{L_{j}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{II}

\displaystyle=\langle(T^{(S(Q))}-T_{\mathcal{P}})(g_{1}),b_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle T^{(S(Q))}(f_{1}\mathds{1}_{\Omega^{c}}),b_{2}\rangle+\langle T^{(S(Q))}(g_{1}\mathds{1}_{\bigcup L_{j}}),b_{2}\rangle-\langle T_{\mathcal{P}}(g_{1}),b_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle T^{(S(Q))}(f_{1}\mathds{1}_{\Omega^{c}}),b_{2}\rangle+\sum_{j}\langle(T^{(S(Q))}-T^{(S(L_{j}))})(\langle f_{1}\rangle_{L_{j}}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}}),b_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\mathrm{II}_{1}+\mathrm{II}_{2}.

We begin with
\mathrm{II}_{1}
. To use the smoothness of the kernel, we pass to the adjoint to take advantage of the mean
0
atoms
b_{i,L_{j}}
. We have

\mathrm{II}_{1}=\langle f_{1}\mathds{1}_{\Omega^{c}},(T^{(S(Q))})^{\ast}(b_{2})\rangle=\sum_{j}\int_{\Omega^{c}}f_{1}(x)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\sum_{s\leq S(Q)}K_{s}(y,x)\right)b_{2,L_{j}}(y)\,dy\right)\,dx,

using the integral expression of
T
. Since
C_{\mathcal{W}}>\sqrt{d}(2\Lambda_{d}+1)=\sqrt{d}(2^{k(d)+3}+2)
,

\operatorname{dist}\left(L_{j},\Omega^{c}\right)\geq(C_{\mathcal{W}}-\sqrt{d})\ell(L_{j})>2^{S(L_{j})+1}.

This means that
|x-y|>2^{S(L_{j})+1}
for every
x\in\Omega^{c}
and
y\in L_{j}
. By definition,
K_{s}(x,y)=0
for all
s\leq S(L_{j})
and
x\in\Omega^{c}
,
y\in L_{j}
. Therefore, the sum in
\mathrm{II}_{1}
can truncated accordingly, and due to the support and mean
0
properties of
b_{2,L_{j}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle|\mathrm{II}_{1}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j}\sum_{s=S(L_{j})+1}^{S(Q)}\int_{\Omega^{c}}|f_{1}(x)|\left(\int_{L_{j}}|K_{s}(y,x)-K_{s}(x_{L_{j}},x)|\cdot|b_{2,L_{j}}(y)|\,dy\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\sum_{s=S(L_{j})+1}^{S(Q)}\left(\int_{L_{j}}|b_{2,L_{j}}(y)|\,dy\right)\left(\int_{\Omega^{c}}|f_{1}(x)|\sup_{z\in L_{j}}|K_{s}(z,x)-K_{s}(x_{L_{j}},x)|\,dx\right)

(2.1)

\displaystyle\lesssim\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\sum_{j}\|b_{2,L_{j}}\|_{L^{1}}\sum_{s=S(L_{j})+1}^{S(Q)}\int_{\Omega^{c}\cap A_{s}(L_{j})}\sup_{z\in L_{j}}|K_{s}(z,x)-K_{s}(x_{L_{j}},x)|\,dx,

having used in the last step that, since
s>S(L_{j})
, then
K_{s}(z,x)
can only be non-zero for
z\in L_{j}
if
x\in A_{s}(L_{j})
by formula (
1.6
) in Lemma
1.3
. To bound the remaining sum of integrals, we use the smoothness of the kernels
K_{s}
. Indeed,
K_{s}
has Dini smoothness with modulus of continuity given by
\tilde{\omega}(t)=t+\omega(t)
. Therefore, for
x\in A_{s}(L_{j})
and
z\in L_{j}
,

(2.2)

|K_{s}(x_{L_{j}},x)-K_{s}(z,x)|\lesssim\frac{1}{|x-x_{L_{j}}|^{d}}\left[\frac{|z-x_{L_{j}|}}{|x-x_{L_{j}}|}+\omega\left(\frac{|z-x_{L_{j}|}}{|x-x_{L_{j}}|}\right)\right],

applying the smoothness condition because
|x-x_{L_{j}}|\geq 2|z-x_{L_{j}}|
. Moreover, on the one hand
x\in A_{s}(L_{j})
implies
|x-x_{L_{j}}|\geq 2^{s-2}
. On the other hand,
|x_{L_{j}}-z|\lesssim_{d}\ell(L_{j})
if
z\in L_{j}
, so

(2.3)

|K_{s}(x_{L_{j}},x)-K_{s}(z,x)|\lesssim 2^{-d[s-2]}\left[\frac{\ell(L_{j})}{2^{s-2}}+\omega\left(\frac{\ell(L_{j})}{2^{s-2}}\right)\right].

(
2.3
) allows us to sum up:

\displaystyle|\mathrm{II}_{1}|

\displaystyle\lesssim\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\sum_{j}\|b_{2,L_{j}}\|_{1}\sum_{s=S(L_{j})+1}^{S(Q)}|A_{s}(L_{j})|2^{-d[s-2]}\left[\frac{\ell(L_{j})}{2^{s-2}}+\omega\left(\frac{\ell(L_{j})}{2^{s-2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\sum_{j}\|b_{2,L_{j}}\|_{1}\sum_{s=S(L_{j})+1}^{S(Q)}2^{sd}2^{-d[s-2]}\left[2^{s_{L_{j}}-s}+\omega\left(2^{s_{L_{j}}-s-2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\sum_{j}\|b_{2,L_{j}}\|_{1}\left[\sum_{s=S(L_{j})+1}^{S(Q)}2^{s_{L_{j}}-s}+\sum_{s=S(L_{j})+1}^{S(Q)}\omega\left(2^{s_{L_{j}}-s-2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\sum_{j}\|b_{2,L_{j}}\|_{1}\left[1+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\omega(t)}{t}\,dt\right],

using
|A_{s}(L_{j})|\sim 2^{sd}
. So, by (
1.3
),

\displaystyle|\mathrm{II}_{1}|\lesssim_{\omega}\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\sum_{j}\|b_{2,L_{j}}\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\|f_{2}\|_{L^{1}(\Lambda_{d}Q)}=\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\langle f_{2}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}|\Lambda_{d}Q|.

We now estimate
\mathrm{II}_{2}
. As for
\mathrm{II}_{1}
, to use the smoothness of the kernel we pass to the adjoint operator:

\displaystyle\mathrm{II}_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\langle\langle f_{1}\rangle_{L_{j}}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}},\left(T_{(S(L_{j})+1)}^{(S(Q))}\right)^{\ast}(b_{2})\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\sum_{s=S(L_{j})+1}^{S(Q)}\langle\langle f_{1}\rangle_{L_{j}}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}},(T_{s})^{\ast}(b_{2})\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\sum_{s=S(L_{j})+1}^{S(Q)}\sum_{j^{\prime}}\langle\langle f_{1}\rangle_{L_{j}}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}},(T_{s})^{\ast}(b_{2,L_{j^{\prime}}})\rangle.

To apply the smoothness of
K_{s}
to
b_{2,L_{j^{\prime}}}
we need
s\geq S(L_{j^{\prime}})+1
as before, so we split the sum as

(2.4)

\mathrm{II}_{2}=\sum_{j}\langle f_{1}\rangle_{L_{j}}\sum_{j^{\prime}}\sum_{s=S(L_{j})+1}^{S(L_{j^{\prime}})}\langle\mathds{1}_{L_{j}},(T_{s})^{\ast}(b_{2,L_{j^{\prime}}})\rangle+\sum_{j}\langle f_{1}\rangle_{L_{j}}\sum_{j^{\prime}}\sum_{s=S(L_{j^{\prime}})+1}^{S(Q)}\langle\mathds{1}_{L_{j}},(T_{s})^{\ast}(b_{2,L_{j^{\prime}}})\rangle,

where the first sum is considered empty when
S(L_{j})+1>S(L_{j^{\prime}})
, i.e., when
s_{L_{j}}+1>s_{L_{j^{\prime}}}
. Now, for the second term all computations are the same as for
\mathrm{II}_{1}
. We just need to notice that we can get rid off the sum in
j
because the
\{L_{j}\}
are disjoint:

\sum_{j}\int_{L_{j}\cap A_{s}(L_{j^{\prime}})}\left|\int_{L_{j^{\prime}}}K_{s}(y,x)b_{2,L_{j^{\prime}}}(y)\,dy\right|\,dx\leq\int_{A_{s}(L_{j^{\prime}})}\left|\int_{L_{j^{\prime}}}K_{s}(y,x)b_{2,L_{j^{\prime}}}(y)\,dy\right|\,dx.

Therefore, we are left to estimate the first sum in (
2.4
) when
s_{L_{j^{\prime}}}>s_{L_{j}}
.
But the number of terms that we need to estimate is controlled by a constant that depends only on
C_{\mathcal{W}}
and
d
. Indeed, we next check that the scales of
L_{j}
and
L_{j^{\prime}}
have to be comparable in order for the corresponding term to be non-zero. Suppose that
s\in\mathbb{Z}
is such that
S(L_{j})+1\leq s\leq S(L_{j^{\prime}})
, with
s_{L_{j}}<s_{L_{j^{\prime}}}
, and that
\langle\mathds{1}_{L_{j}},(T_{s})^{\ast}(b_{2,L_{j^{\prime}}})\rangle\neq 0
.
In particular,
\operatorname{supp}(\mathds{1}_{L_{j}})\cap\operatorname{supp}(T_{s})^{\ast}(b_{2,L_{j^{\prime}}})\neq\varnothing.

Recall that
\operatorname{supp}\left(\mathds{1}_{L_{j}}\right)\subset L_{j}
and, by (the proof of) Lemma
1.4
,
\operatorname{supp}(T_{s})^{\ast}(b_{2,L_{j^{\prime}}})\subset\Lambda_{d}L_{j^{\prime}}
for
s\leq S(L_{j^{\prime}})
.
So
L_{j}\cap\Lambda_{d}L_{j^{\prime}}\neq\varnothing
. Therefore,

\operatorname{dist}\left(L_{j^{\prime}},\Omega^{c}\right)\leq\operatorname{diam}\left(\Lambda_{d}L_{j^{\prime}}\right)+\operatorname{diam}\left(L_{j}\right)+\operatorname{dist}\left(L_{j},\Omega^{c}\right).

Then, by Theorem
1.1
and using
C_{\mathcal{W}}>\sqrt{d}(2\Lambda_{d}+1)
we get

\operatorname{dist}\left(L_{j^{\prime}},\Omega^{c}\right)\geq(C_{\mathcal{W}}-\sqrt{d})2^{s_{L_{j^{\prime}}}}

and

\displaystyle\operatorname{diam}\left(\Lambda_{d}L_{j^{\prime}}\right)+\operatorname{diam}\left(L_{j}\right)+\operatorname{dist}\left(L_{j},\Omega^{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{d}\Lambda_{d}\ell(L_{j^{\prime}})+\sqrt{d}\ell(L_{j})+\operatorname{dist}\left(L_{j},\Omega^{c}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{d}\Lambda_{d}2^{s_{L_{j^{\prime}}}}+\sqrt{d}2^{s_{L_{j}}}+2C_{\mathcal{W}}2^{s_{L_{j}}}.

Therefore,

[C_{\mathcal{W}}-\sqrt{d}(1+\Lambda_{d})]2^{s_{L_{j^{\prime}}}}\leq[\sqrt{d}+2C_{\mathcal{W}}]2^{s_{L_{j}}},

which implies

\ell(L_{j^{\prime}})\lesssim_{C_{\mathcal{W}},d}\ell(L_{j}).

Since
s_{L_{j}}<s_{L_{j^{\prime}}}
, there is
N_{0}
such that
\ell(L_{j})<\ell(L_{j^{\prime}})\leq 2^{N_{0}}\ell(L_{j})
. Additionally, since
L_{j}\cap\Lambda_{d}L_{j^{\prime}}\neq\varnothing
with
\ell(L_{j})\sim\ell(L_{j^{\prime}})
, there exists
\mu>0
(independent of
j
and
j^{\prime}
) such that
L_{j^{\prime}}\subset\mu L_{j}
. We also have the
L^{1}\to L^{\infty}
estimate (
1.5
) for
(T_{s})^{\ast}
, which yields

|\langle\mathds{1}_{L_{j}},(T_{s})^{\ast}(b_{2,L_{j^{\prime}}})\rangle|\leq\|\mathds{1}_{L_{j}}\|_{1}\|(T_{s})^{\ast}(b_{2,L_{j^{\prime}}})\|_{\infty}\lesssim|L_{j}|\cdot 2^{-sd}\|b_{2,L_{j^{\prime}}}\|\lesssim|L_{j}|\cdot 2^{-sd}\|f_{2}\mathds{1}_{L_{j^{\prime}}}\|_{L^{1}}.

We plug it in the estimate that remains to get

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j}\langle f_{1}\rangle_{L_{j}}\sum_{j^{\prime}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\sum_{s=S(L_{j})+1}^{S(L_{j^{\prime}})}\langle\mathds{1}_{L_{j}},(T_{s})^{\ast}(b_{2,L_{j^{\prime}}})\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,j^{\prime}\\
\ell(L_{j})<\ell(L_{j^{\prime}})\leq 2^{N_{0}}\ell(L_{j})\\
L_{j^{\prime}}\subset\mu L_{j}\end{subarray}}\sum_{s=S(L_{j})+1}^{S(L_{j})+N_{0}+1}\left|\langle\mathds{1}_{L_{j}},(T_{s})^{\ast}(b_{2,L_{j^{\prime}}})\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,j^{\prime}\\
\ell(L_{j})<\ell(L_{j^{\prime}})\leq 2^{N_{0}}\ell(L_{j})\\
L_{j^{\prime}}\subset\mu L_{j}\end{subarray}}\sum_{s=S(L_{j})+1}^{S(L_{j})+N_{0}+1}|L_{j}|\cdot 2^{-sd}\|f_{2}\mathds{1}_{L_{j^{\prime}}}\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,j^{\prime}\\
\ell(L_{j})<\ell(L_{j^{\prime}})\leq 2^{N_{0}}\ell(L_{j})\\
L_{j^{\prime}}\subset\mu L_{j}\end{subarray}}\sum_{s=S(L_{j})+1}^{S(L_{j})+N_{0}+1}|L_{j}|\langle f_{2}\rangle_{L_{j^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\langle f_{2}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\sum_{j}|L_{j}|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j^{\prime}\\
\ell(L_{j})<\ell(L_{j^{\prime}})\leq 2^{N_{0}}\ell(L_{j})\\
L_{j^{\prime}}\subset\mu L_{j}\end{subarray}}\sum_{s=S(L_{j})+1}^{S(L_{j})+N_{0}+1}1

\displaystyle\lesssim\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\langle f_{2}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}|\Lambda_{d}Q|.

The last step above is justified by the fact that the
L_{j}
are pairwise disjoint and contained in
2\Lambda_{d}Q
, and because the two inner sums run over boundedly many terms: the innermost runs over
N_{0}
terms, while
\mu
just depends on
C_{\mathcal{W}}
and
d
, so

\#\{j^{\prime}:\ell(L_{j})<\ell(L_{j^{\prime}}),\hskip 2.84544ptL_{j^{\prime}}\subset\mu L_{j}\}\lesssim_{C_{\mathcal{W}},d}1.

Estimate of
\mathrm{III}
:
By definition and the
L^{2}
-continuity and linearity of the operators and the scalar product,

\mathrm{III}=\langle(T^{(S(Q))}-T_{\mathcal{P}})(b_{1}),g_{2}\rangle=\sum_{j}\langle T^{(S(Q))}(b_{1,L_{j}}),g_{2}\rangle-\sum_{j}\sum_{j^{\prime}}T^{(S(L_{j^{\prime}}))}(b_{1,L_{j}}\mathds{1}_{L_{j^{\prime}}}),g_{2}\rangle.

Since
\operatorname{supp}\left(b_{1,L_{j}}\right)\subset L_{j}
and
L_{j}\cap L_{j^{\prime}}=\varnothing
if
j\neq j^{\prime}
, then
b_{1,L_{j}}\mathds{1}_{L_{j^{\prime}}}\equiv 0
if
j\neq j^{\prime}
. So the above sum is

\sum_{j}\langle(T^{(S(Q))}-T^{(S(L_{j}))})(b_{1,L_{j}}),g_{2}\rangle=\sum_{j}\sum_{s=S(L_{j})+1}^{S(Q)}\langle T_{s}(b_{1,L_{j}}),g_{2}\rangle.

We have the scalar product of a good function against the operators
T_{s}
with
s>S(L_{j})
acting on the mean
0
functions
b_{1,L_{j}}
Therefore, the estimate is exactly the same as in
\mathrm{II}
, with the only differences that the indices
i=1,2
are interchanged and we have
T_{s}
instead of
(T_{s})^{\ast}
. Hence,

|\mathrm{III}|\lesssim\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\langle f_{2}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}|\Lambda_{d}Q|.

Estimate of
\mathrm{IV}
:
By definition of
T_{\mathcal{P}}
and
b_{1}
, and continuity and linearity of the operators and the scalar product,

\mathrm{IV}=\langle(T^{(S(Q))}-T_{\mathcal{P}})(b_{1}),b_{2}\rangle=\sum_{j}\langle T^{(S(Q))}(b_{1,L_{j}}),b_{2}\rangle-\sum_{j}\sum_{j^{\prime}}\langle T^{(S(L_{j^{\prime}}))}(b_{1,L_{j}}\mathds{1}_{L_{j^{\prime}}}),b_{2}\rangle.

As reasoned in
\mathrm{III}
,
b_{1,L_{j}}\mathds{1}_{L_{j^{\prime}}}\equiv 0
if
j\neq j^{\prime}
. So

\mathrm{IV}=\sum_{j}\langle(T^{(S(Q))}-T^{(S(L_{j}))}(b_{1,L_{j}}),b_{2}\rangle.

Therefore, the estimate is of the same form as
\mathrm{III}
, with the small difference that now we have
b_{2}
instead of
g_{2}
. We write the details to show the minor changes that need to be made in the argument.

\displaystyle\mathrm{IV}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\sum_{s=S(L_{j})+1}^{S(Q)}K_{s}(x,y)\right)b_{1,L_{j}}(y)\,dy\right)b_{2}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\sum_{s=S(L_{j})+1}^{S(Q)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\int_{L_{j}}[K_{s}(x,y)-K_{s}(x,x_{L_{j}})]b_{1,L_{j}}(y)\,dy\right)b_{2}(x)\,dx.

Introducing absolute values, we have

|\mathrm{IV}|\leq\sum_{j}\sum_{s=S(L_{j})+1}^{S(Q)}\left(\int_{L_{j}}|b_{1,L_{j}}(y)|\,dy\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{z\in L_{j}}|K_{s}(x,z)-K_{s}(x,x_{L_{j}})|b_{2}(x)|\,dx\right).

Again, we have operators
T_{s}
acting on
b_{1,L_{j}}
with scales
s>S(L_{j})
. So we can restrict the integral over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
A_{s}(L_{j})
by Lemma
1.3
and apply the smoothness estimate as in
II_{1}
, obtaining

\displaystyle\int_{A_{s}(L_{j})}\sup_{z\in L_{j}}|K_{s}(x,z)-K_{s}(x,x_{L_{j}})|\cdot|b_{2}(x)|\,dx

(2.5)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{A_{s}(L_{j})}\frac{1}{|x-x_{L_{j}}|^{d}}\left[\frac{|z-x_{L_{j}}|}{|x-x_{L_{j}}|}+\omega\left(\frac{|z-x_{L_{j}}|}{|x-x_{L_{j}}|}\right)\right]|b_{2}(x)|\,dx,

using again
2|z-x_{L_{j}}|\leq|x-x_{L_{j}}|
to apply the smoothness.
Since
x\in A_{s}(L_{j})
,
|x-x_{L_{j}}|\geq 2^{s-2}
, and since
x_{L_{j}},z\in L_{j}
,
|z-x_{L_{j}}|\leq\sqrt{d}2^{s_{L_{j}}-1}\leq 2^{S(L_{j})-2}
. So

\frac{1}{|x-x_{L_{j}}|^{d}}\lesssim 2^{-sd}\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{|z-x_{L_{j}}|}{|x-x_{L_{j}}|}\leq\frac{1}{2^{s-S(L_{j})}}.

Hence, using that
\omega
is non-decreasing, (
2.1
) can be bounded by

\eqref{smoothnessIV}\lesssim 2^{-sd}\left[\frac{1}{2^{s-S(L_{j})}}+\omega\left(\frac{1}{2^{s-S(L_{j})}}\right)\right]\int_{A_{s}(L_{j})}|b_{2}(x)|\,dx.

Denote
B_{s}(L_{j})=B(x_{L_{j}};2^{s+k(d)+1})
the smallest ball that contains
A_{s}(L_{j})
. Then

\int_{A_{s}(L_{j})}|b_{2}(x)|\,dx\leq\int_{B_{s}(L_{j})}|b_{2}(x)|\,dx=|B_{s}(L_{j})|\langle|b_{2}|\rangle_{B_{s}(L_{j})}\sim 2^{sd}\langle|b_{2}|\rangle_{B_{s}(L_{j})}\lesssim 2^{sd}\langle f_{2}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q},

by (
1.4
). Therefore,

\displaystyle|\mathrm{IV}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j}\sum_{s=S(L_{j})+1}^{S(Q)}\left(\int_{L_{j}}|b_{1,L_{j}}(y)|\,dy\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{z\in L_{j}}|K_{s}(x,z)-K_{s}(x,x_{L_{j}})|b_{2}(x)|\,dx\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\langle f_{2}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\sum_{j}\|b_{1,L_{j}}\|_{L^{1}}\sum_{s=S(L_{j})+1}^{S(Q)}\left[\frac{1}{2^{s-S(L_{j})}}+\omega\left(\frac{1}{2^{s-S(L_{j})}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\langle f_{2}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\sum_{j}\|b_{1,L_{j}}\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\langle f_{2}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\|f_{1}\|_{L^{1}(\Lambda_{d}Q)}=\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\langle f_{2}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}|\Lambda_{d}Q|,

having applied (
1.3
) in the last inequality.∎

2.2.
Construction of the sparse family

We can now finish the proof of Theorem
A
.

Proof of Theorem
A
.

The idea of the proof is to apply Lemma
2.1
iteratively, for which we qualitatively assume that
f_{1},f_{2}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We will denote by
\mathcal{P}_{n}
the family of cubes on which we will localise the estimate at the
n
-th iteration. We know that
\operatorname{supp}\left(f_{1}\right)\subset Q_{0}
, so we let
\mathcal{P}_{0}\coloneqq\{Q_{0}\}
.

Fix
\eta_{0}\in(0,1)
. We will build a
(C,\eta_{0})
-sparse family such that the estimate in Theorem
A
holds, for some constant
C\in(0,1)
. We do it following an iteration procedure. For the first iteration, let

\Omega_{i}^{Q_{0}}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\mathcal{M}f_{i}(x)>c_{0}\langle f_{i}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q_{0}}\},\quad i=1,2,

for some
c_{0}
to be chosen later. Define the open set

\Omega_{Q_{0}}\coloneqq\Omega_{1}^{Q_{0}}\cup\Omega_{2}^{Q_{0}},

which is contained in
2\Lambda_{d}Q_{0}
and gives

\mathcal{M}f_{i}(x)\leq c_{0}\langle f_{i}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q_{0}}\quad\text{ if }x\in\Omega_{Q_{0}}^{c},

for
i=1,2
. Let
\{L_{j}^{(1)}\}_{j\in J_{1}(Q_{0})}
be the Whitney decomposition of
\Omega_{Q_{0}}\subset 2\Lambda_{d}Q_{0}
with Whitney constant
C_{\mathcal{W}}>\sqrt{d}(2\Lambda_{d}+1)
as in Theorem
1.1
. Since
\operatorname{supp}\left(f_{1}\right)\cup\operatorname{supp}\left(f_{2}\right)\subset Q_{0}
, we can apply Lemma
2.1
(notice that
\langle Tf_{1},f_{2}\rangle=\langle T^{(S(Q_{0}))}f_{1},f_{2}\rangle
) and obtain the following estimate:

(2.6)

\left|\left\langle Tf_{1},f_{2}\right\rangle\right|=\left|\left\langle T^{(S(Q_{0}))}(f_{1}),f_{2}\right\rangle\right|\leq C\left\langle f_{1}\right\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q_{0}}\left\langle f_{2}\right\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q_{0}}|\Lambda_{d}Q_{0}|+\sum_{j\in J_{1}(Q_{0})}\left|\left\langle T^{(S(L_{j}^{(1)}))}(f_{1}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}^{(1)}}),f_{2}\right\rangle\right|.

Assume without loss of generality that
Q_{0}
is dyadic. Since
\operatorname{supp}\left(f_{1}\right)\subset Q_{0}
, the non-zero terms in the sum will be those such that
Q_{0}\cap L_{j}^{(1)}\neq\varnothing
. By the choice of
C_{\mathcal{W}}
,
2\Lambda_{d}L_{j}\subset\Omega_{Q_{0}}\subset 2\Lambda_{d}Q_{0}
, so by dyadic nesting it must be
L_{j}^{(1)}\subset Q_{0}
. Hence, the non-zero terms in the sum correspond to the cubes

\mathcal{P}_{1}\coloneqq\{L_{j}^{(1)}:j\in J_{1}(Q_{0}),L_{j}^{(1)}\subset Q_{0}\}.

If we denote
\mathcal{P}_{1}=\{L_{j}^{1}\}_{j\in J_{1}}
, estimate (
2.6
) can be written as

\left|\left\langle T^{(S(Q))}(f_{1}),f_{2}\right\rangle\right|\leq C\left\langle f_{1}\right\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q_{0}}\left\langle f_{2}\right\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q_{0}}|\Lambda_{d}Q_{0}|+\sum_{j\in J_{1}}\left|\left\langle T^{(S(L_{j}^{1}))}(f_{1}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}^{1}}),f_{2}\right\rangle\right|.

Now we repeat the process for each of the terms in the sum. Define

\Omega_{i}^{L_{j}^{1}}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\mathcal{M}(f_{i}\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{1}})(x)>c_{0}\langle f_{i}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{1}}\},\quad i=1,2.

Then
\Omega_{L_{j}^{1}}\coloneqq\Omega_{1}^{L_{j}^{1}}\cup\Omega_{2}^{L_{j}^{1}}
is contained in
2\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{1}
and we have

\mathcal{M}(f_{i}\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{1}})(x)\lesssim\langle f_{i}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{1}}\quad\text{ if }x\in\Omega_{L_{j}^{1}}^{c},\quad i=1,2.

Taking the Whitney decomposition
\{L_{k}^{(2)}\}_{k\in J_{2}(j)}
of
\Omega_{L_{j}^{1}}
, we can apply Lemma
2.1
and obtain

\displaystyle|\langle T^{(S(L_{j}^{1})}(f_{1}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}^{1}},f_{2}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq C\langle f_{1}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}^{1}}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{1}}\langle f_{2}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{1}}|\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{1}|

(2.7)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\sum_{k\in J_{2}(j)}|\langle T^{(S(L_{j_{2}}^{(2)}))}(f_{1}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}^{1}}\mathds{1}_{L_{k}^{(2)}},f_{2}\rangle|.

for each
j\in J_{1}
. Again, for the terms in the last sum to be non-zero we need
L_{j}^{1}\cap L_{k}^{(2)}\neq\varnothing
. Since
2\Lambda_{d}L_{k}^{(2)}\subset\Omega_{L_{j}^{1}}\subset 2\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{1}
, then
L_{k}^{(2)}\subset L_{j}^{1}
. Hence, the sum in (
2.2
) can be taken over the cubes in

\mathcal{P}_{2}(j)\coloneqq\{L_{k}^{(2)}:j_{2}\in J_{2}(j)\text{ for some }j\in J_{1}\text{ and }L_{k}^{(2)}\subset L_{j}^{1}\},\quad j\in J_{1}.

The collection of second generation cubes is

\mathcal{P}_{2}\coloneqq\bigcup_{j\in J_{1}}\mathcal{P}_{2}(j)\eqqcolon\{L_{k}^{2}\}_{k\in J_{2}}.

\mathcal{P}_{1}=\{L_{j}^{1}\}_{j\in J_{1}}
is a pairwise disjoint family. Likely, the cubes in
\mathcal{P}_{2}
are also pairwise disjoint. Indeed, let
L,L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}
. This means that
L=L_{k}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(j)
and
L^{\prime}=L_{k^{\prime}}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(j^{\prime})
for some
j,j^{\prime}\in J_{1}
. If
j=j^{\prime}
, then
L_{k}^{(2)}
and
L_{k^{\prime}}^{(2)}
are disjoint because they are Whitney cubes of the open set
\Omega_{L_{j}^{1}}
. If
j\neq j^{\prime}
, then
L_{k}^{(2)}
and
L_{k^{\prime}}^{(2)}
are disjoint because
L_{k}^{(2)}\cap L_{k^{\prime}}^{(2)}\subset L_{j}^{1}\cap L_{j^{\prime}}^{1}=\varnothing
because
L_{j}^{1}
and
L_{j^{\prime}}^{1}
are Whitney cubes of
\Omega_{Q_{0}}
. We can now combine estimates (
2.6
) and (
2.2
) to obtain

(2.8)

|\langle Tf_{1},f_{2}\rangle|\leq C\sum_{i=0,1}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{i}}\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\langle f_{2}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}|\Lambda_{d}Q|+\sum_{L\in\mathcal{P}_{2}}|\langle T^{(S(L))}(f_{1}\mathds{1}_{L}),f_{2}\rangle|.

To perform the
n
-th iteration,
n\geq 3
, we have from the
(n-1)
-th iteration that

(2.9)

|\langle Tf_{1},f_{2}\rangle|\leq C\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{i}}\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\langle f_{2}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}|\Lambda_{d}Q|+\sum_{L\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1}}|\langle T^{(S(L))}(f_{1}\mathds{1}_{L}),f_{2}\rangle|.

We write
\mathcal{P}_{n-1}\eqqcolon\{L_{j}^{n-1}\}_{j\in J_{n-1}}
. For
j\in J_{n-1}
, we perform the Whitney decomposition of
\Omega_{L_{j}^{n-1}}\coloneqq\Omega_{1}^{L_{j}^{n-1}}\cup\Omega_{2}^{L_{j}^{n-1}}
, with

\Omega_{i}^{L_{j}^{n-1}}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\mathcal{M}(f_{i}\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{n-1}})(x)>c_{0}\langle f_{i}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{n-1}}\},\quad i=1,2.

obtaining a collection
\{L_{k}^{(n)}\}_{k\in J_{n}(j)}
for each
j\in J_{n-1}
. Again,
\Omega_{L_{j}^{n-1}}\subset 2\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{n-1}
and
\mathcal{M}(f_{i}\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{n-1}})(x)\lesssim\langle f_{i}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{n-1}}
if
x\not\in\Omega_{L_{j}^{n-1}}
. Hence, applying Lemma
2.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle|\langle T^{(S(L_{j}^{n-1})}(f_{1}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}^{n-1}}),f_{2}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq C\langle f_{1}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}^{n-1}}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{n-1}}\langle f_{2}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{n-1}}|\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{n-1}|

(2.10)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k\in J_{n}(j)}|\langle T^{(S(L_{k}^{(n)}))}(f_{1}\mathds{1}_{L_{j}^{n-1}}\mathds{1}_{L_{k}^{(n)}}),f_{2}\rangle|.

Reasoning as above, we define

\mathcal{P}_{n}(j)\coloneqq\{L_{k}^{(n)}:k\in J_{n-1}(j)\text{ for some }j\in J_{n-1},L_{k}^{(n)}\subset L_{j}^{n-1}\},

and

\mathcal{P}_{n}\coloneqq\bigcup_{j\in J_{n-1}}\mathcal{P}_{n}(j)\eqqcolon\{L_{j}^{n}\}_{j\in J_{n}}.

The cubes in
\mathcal{P}_{n}
are pairwise disjoint and

(2.11)

|\langle Tf_{1},f_{2}\rangle|\leq C\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{i}}\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\langle f_{2}\rangle_{\Lambda_{d}Q}|\Lambda_{d}Q|+\sum_{L\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\langle T^{(S(L))}(f_{1}\mathds{1}_{L}),f_{2}\rangle|.

Estimate (
2.11
) is enough to prove the statement, as we next see. We put
\mathcal{P}\coloneqq\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}_{i}
, and we define

\mathcal{S}\coloneqq\{\Lambda_{d}Q\}_{Q\in\mathcal{P}}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{S}(i)

where
\mathcal{S}(i)\coloneqq\{\Lambda_{d}Q\}_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{i}}
. To check that
\mathcal{S}
is indeed sparse, we make a choice of disjoint subsets
E_{Q}
corresponding to each
Q\in\mathcal{S}
. We first do so for
\mathcal{S}(0)\coloneqq\{\Lambda_{d}Q_{0}\}
. By the weak
(1,1)
-boundedness of
\mathcal{M}
,

|\Omega_{Q_{0}}|\leq\frac{2\|\mathcal{M}\|_{L^{1}\to L^{1,\infty}}}{c_{0}}|\Lambda_{d}Q_{0}|.

Taking

c_{0}=\frac{2\|\mathcal{M}\|_{L^{1}\to L^{\infty}}(\Lambda_{d})^{d}}{1-\eta_{0}},

then

|\Omega_{Q_{0}}|\leq\frac{1-\eta_{0}}{2\|\mathcal{M}\|_{L^{1}\to L^{\infty}}(\Lambda_{d})^{d}}2\|\mathcal{M}\|_{L^{1}\to L^{1,\infty}}|\Lambda_{d}Q_{0}|=(1-\eta_{0})|Q_{0}|,

which allows us to choose the major subset

E_{\Lambda_{d}Q_{0}}\coloneqq Q_{0}\mathbin{\big\backslash}{\bigcup_{j}L_{j}^{1}}.

It satisfies

\left|E_{\Lambda_{d}Q_{0}}\right|\geq\frac{\eta_{0}}{(\Lambda_{d})^{d}}|\Lambda_{d}Q_{0}|,

because

\left|Q_{0}\setminus{\bigcup_{j\in J_{1}}L_{j}^{1}}\right|\geq|Q_{0}|-\sum_{j\in J_{1}}|L_{j}^{1}|=|Q_{0}|-|\Omega_{Q_{0}}|\geq|Q_{0}|-(1-\eta_{0})|Q_{0}|=\eta_{0}|Q_{0}|.

To the cubes in
\mathcal{S}(1)\coloneqq\{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{1}\}_{j\in J_{1}}
, we assign the major subsets

E_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{1}}\coloneqq L_{j}^{1}\setminus{\bigcup_{k\in J_{2}(j)}L_{k}^{(2)}}=L_{j}^{1}\setminus{\bigcup_{L\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(j)}L}=L_{j}^{1}\setminus{\bigcup_{L\in\mathcal{P}_{2}}L},\quad j\in J_{1},

which again satisfy
|E_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{1}}|\geq\frac{\eta_{0}}{(\Lambda_{d})^{d}}|\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{1}|
. And in general, to the cubes in
\mathcal{S}(n)\coloneqq\{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{n-1}\}_{j\in J_{n-1}}
we associate the major subset

E_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{n-1}}\coloneqq L_{j}^{n-1}\setminus{\bigcup_{k\in J_{n}(j)}L_{k}^{(n)}}=L_{j}^{n-1}\setminus{\bigcup_{L\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}L},

and we have
|E_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{n-1}}|\geq\frac{\eta_{0}}{(\Lambda_{d})^{d}}|\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{n-1}|
. Therefore, if we prove that the sets
\{E_{Q}\}_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}
are disjoint, then
\mathcal{S}
is
\frac{\eta_{0}}{(\Lambda_{d})^{d}}
-sparse. To see that
\{E_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\}_{Q\in\mathcal{P}}
are pairwise disjoint, let
Q=L_{j}^{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
. It is enough to show that
E_{\Lambda_{d}Q}
is pairwise disjoint with
E_{\Lambda_{d}Q^{\prime}}
for every
Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
,
0\leq m\leq n
. First, as we have already observed, the cubes in
\mathcal{P}_{n}=\{L_{j}^{n}\}_{j\in J_{n}}
are pairwise disjoint. Hence
E_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{n}}\cap E_{\Lambda_{d}L_{k}^{n}}=\varnothing
if
j\neq k
, so the major subsets of the cubes of the
n
-th level of
\mathcal{S}
are disjoint. To pass to the above levels, we observe the following: each cube
L_{j}^{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
is really some
L_{j^{\prime}}^{(n)}
with
j^{\prime}\in J_{n-1}(k)
for some
k\in J_{n-1}
. That is,

L_{j}^{n}=L_{j^{\prime}}^{(n)}\subset L_{k}^{n-1}.

Therefore, since
L_{j}^{n}
has been removed from
E_{\Lambda_{d}L_{k}^{n-1}}
and
E_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{n}}\subset L_{j}^{n}
, we have that

E_{\Lambda_{d}L_{k}^{n-1}}\cap E_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{n}}=\varnothing.

But, at the same time, the cube
L_{k}^{n-1}
has been removed from the major subsets of the cubes
L_{l}^{n-2}
,
l\in J_{n-2}
. Hence, the cube
L_{j}^{n}
, which is contained in
L_{k}^{n-1}
, has also been removed. Hence, iterating this procedure to reach level
0
, we have that

E_{\Lambda_{d}L_{j}^{n}}\cap E_{\Lambda_{d}L_{k}^{m}}=\varnothing

for every
k\in J_{m}
,
0\leq m\leq n-1
. Letting

\mathcal{S}_{n}\coloneqq\bigcup_{j=0}^{n}\mathcal{S}(n),

the estimate (
2.11
) at step
n
can be written as

|\langle Tf_{1},f_{2}\rangle|\lesssim\langle\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}_{n}}f_{1},f_{2}\rangle+\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\langle T^{(S(Q))}(f_{1}\mathds{1}_{Q}),f_{2}\rangle|.

Taking the limit as
n\to\infty
, we obtain the desired estimate. Indeed, the families
\mathcal{S}_{n}
are increasing, and therefore
\langle\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}_{n}}(f_{1}),f_{2}\rangle
is an increasing sequence that converges to

\langle\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}(f_{1}),f_{2}\rangle=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}\left\langle f_{1}\right\rangle_{Q}\left\langle f_{2}\right\rangle_{Q}|Q|.

The sum in
\mathcal{P}_{n}
vanishes when
n\to\infty
, since

\left|\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\langle T(f_{1}\mathds{1}_{Q}),f_{2}\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\rangle\right|\lesssim\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\|f_{1}\mathds{1}_{Q}\|_{2}\|f_{2}\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{d}Q}\|_{2}\lesssim_{d}\|f_{1}\|_{\infty}\|f_{2}\|_{\infty}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|Q|^{\frac{1}{2}}|Q|^{\frac{1}{2}}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

To justify that the limit is
0
, notice that

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|Q|=\sum_{j\in J_{n-1}}\left(\sum_{L_{k}^{(n)}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}(j)}|L_{k}^{(n)}|\right)\leq\sum_{j\in J_{n-1}}|\Omega_{L_{j}^{n-1}}|\leq\sum_{j\in J_{n-1}}(1-\eta_{0})|L_{j}^{n-1}|=(1-\eta_{0})\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1}}|Q|.

On the right-hand side we obtain an identical expression to the one on the left-hand side, but now with
n-1
instead of
n
. Iterating this estimate, we obtain

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|Q|\leq(1-\eta_{0})^{n}\sum_{j\in J_{1}}|L_{j}^{1}|=(1-\eta_{0})^{n}|\Omega_{Q_{0}}|\lesssim(1-\eta_{0})^{n+1}|Q_{0}|,

which tends to
0
as
n\to\infty
because
\eta_{0}\in(0,1)
. ∎

3.
Remarks and complements: nonhomogeneous sparse domination

We end this note commenting on the differences and challenges that one encounters when transferring the statement of Theorem
A
to other measure spaces, and with some open problems. We assume now that our Calderón-Zygmund operator is an operator with integral representation

Tf(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}K(x,y)f(y)\,d\mu(y),

where
(\mathcal{X},d,\mu)
is a metric measure space and the kernel
K
has a local behavior
|K(x,y)|\sim|x-y|^{-n}
for some integer
n
.

3.1.
Homogeneous spaces

(\mathcal{X},d,\mu)
is a space of homogeneous type if
\mu
is doubling with respect to
d
, that is, for each point
x\in\mathcal{X}=\operatorname{supp}\left(\mu\right)
and every
r>0
, there holds

(3.1)

\mu(B(x,2r))\leq C_{\mathrm{doub}}\;\mu(B(x,r)).

In that situation, if one formulates the right smoothness condition for
K
then Theorem
A
holds, as shown for example in
[
Lor21
]
. Moreover, the scheme of proof that we used in Section
2
can be used, even without postulating the existence of a nice replacement of the dyadic system, as shown in
[
CADPPV22
]
.

3.2.
Calderón-Zygmund operators with nondoubling measures

When (
3.1
) fails, the situation changes dramatically and the challenges that appear if one tries to formulate Theorem
A
in this situation are significant. Let us focus on the case
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
. There is a rich Calderón-Zygmund theory which was developed for measures of polynomial growth. What is postulated in that case is that there exists
0<n\leq d
so that

\mu(B(x,r))\leq r^{n}

holds for all
x\in\operatorname{supp}\left(\mu\right)
and all
r>0
. The size growth of the singularity of Calderón-Zygmund kernels matches that of the measure, and a fairly complete transference of the classical results in Calderón-Zygmund theory is available, including weak-type estimates
[
NTV97
,
Tol01b
]
, BMO spaces
[
Tol01a
]
, and
Tb
theorems
[
NTV03
]
. A more general theory is even possible, with measures satisfying milder assumptions
[
Hyt10
]
. There are two results that transfer sparse domination to this setting, each following one of the methods laid out in the Introduction:

•

Median oscillation formula:
The median oscillation formula was extended to general measures by Hänninen
[
H1̈7
]
. Using that and a system of dyadic cubes adapted to
\mu

[
DM00
]
one can follow the original scheme of
[
Ler13a
]
, obtaining a pointwise bound

|Tf(x)|\lesssim\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}(\tilde{\mathcal{M}}f)(x),

for some adequate modified maximal function
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}

[
CAP19
]
. The drawback of this approach is that, even when
\mu
is doubling, one does not recover the
A_{2}
theorem, for the quantitative weighted dependence is suboptimal (one gets a quadratic dependence on the
A_{2}
constant of the weight).

•

Grand maximal function:
Using once more the David-Mattila dyadic system from
[
DM00
]
,
[
VZK18
]
follows the proof of
[
Ler16
]
to obtain a pointwise estimate of the form

|Tf(x)|\lesssim\sum_{j}a_{j}\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{S}_{j}}|f|(x),

where
\{a_{j}\}_{j}
is summable and the modified sparse operators are of the form

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{S}}f(x)=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}\langle f\rangle_{\alpha Q}\mathds{1}_{Q}(x),

for some dilation parameter
\alpha
. The result does recover the
A_{2}
theorem in the doubling case, but is not comparable with
[
CAP19
]
in the general case, which shows that it is also suboptimal.

It is natural to wonder whether the arguments from Section
2
can be carried over to this setting. There exists a dyadic Calderón-Zygmund decomposition that works for general measures
[
CCAP22
]
, so an estimate like the one in the iteration Lemma
2.1
cannot be ruled out. However, it is unclear whether the geometric technique of the Whitney covering, which is critically used to run the main stopping-time argument, admits an adequate replacement.

Problem 3.1
.

Find an iteration scheme to prove a version of Theorem
A
for measures of polynomial growth using the Calderón-Zygmund decomposition.

One can avoid the sparse domination technique altogether and look for a path to generalize the
A_{2}
theorem using different methods. Hytönen’s representation theorem
[
Hyt12
]
states that a Calderón-Zygmund operator can be written as an average of certain dyadic operators, called dyadic shifts, which are generally considered to be discrete models of singular integrals. Haar shifts defined with respect to general measures were considered in
[
LSMP14
]
, where a characterization of the class of measures
\mu
for which they are of weak-type
(1,1)
was found. The corresponding class of measures fails to include all polynomial growth measures, which is an indication that a direct generalization of Hytönen’s representation is unlikely (see also
[
GH18
]
, where a representation theorem is proved that allows one to prove a
T1
theorem, but not sharp weighted inequalities).

Problem 3.2
.

Find the right generalization of Hytönen’s representation theorem for Calderón-Zygmund operators defined with respect to measures of polynomial growth, and prove a nonhomogeneous
A_{2}
theorem.

Even for Haar shifts that are of weak-type
(1,1)
sparse domination fails, and more complicated averaging operators need to be considered in the right hand side of the sparse domination inequality in order to obtain a positive result
[
CAPW25
]
. This yields interesting weighted inequalities as a corollary and it suggests that one can try a similar approach for Calderón-Zygmund operators.

Problem 3.3
.

Define an averaging operator
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{S}}
so that the result

|Tf(x)|\lesssim\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}|f|(x)+\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{S}}|f|(x)

holds and entails quantitatively sharp weighted inequalities.

Remark 3.1
.

When looking for weighted inequalities in the nonhomogeneous setting, it may be relevant to notice that the class of
A_{p}
