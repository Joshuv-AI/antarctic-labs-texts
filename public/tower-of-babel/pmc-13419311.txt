The relationship between academic stress and depression in college students: the moderating effect of parent-child relationships
Authors: Zhuang Liu, Siyan Chen, Yike Yan, Liqing Zeng, Xinyao He, Jingmin Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to examine the relationships among college students’ depression, academic stress, and parent-child relationship quality, and to inform the development of effective depression prevention strategies.MethodsParticipants were recruited from two sources: college students at a university in Hubei Province (via the Hubei College Student Health Survey, HCSHS) and the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS). The study employed multiple linear regression analysis to explore the associations between these variables.ResultsA total of 871 college students were included in the final analysis, comprising 569 from HCSHS and 302 from CFPS. The sample yielded a mean depression score of 14.08 ± 3.90 and a mean perceived academic stress score of 3.19 ± 0.96. Multiple linear regression analyses revealed that academic stress (β = 0.22, P < 0.001) and parent-child relationship quality (categorized as average or distant: β = 0.37, P < 0.001) emerged as key predictors of depression in college students. In addition, a significant interaction effect was observed between academic stress and parent-child relationship quality on depression in college students (β = -0.24, P = 0.02).ConclusionAmong college students, both academic stress and average or distant parent-child relationships were found to significantly increase the risk of depression, with a significant interaction observed between these two factors. Specifically, a close parent-child relationship was found not to exert a stress-buffering effect against academic stress; rather, it was associated with heightened sensitivity to such stress, thereby amplifying its adverse impact on depressive symptoms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04851-5.

Introduction

Depression is one of the most common mental health conditions among adolescents in China and around the world [1]. It has become a significant public health issue, garnering significant attention from the international research community. Depression is expected to become the primary contributor to the global burden of disease by 2030, according to the World Health Organization (WHO) [2]. Depression is one of the most prevalent mental health disorders among college students, with a prevalence of 30.6%. This rate is significantly higher than that of the general population [3].

There is evidence to suggest that mental health challenges among Chinese adolescents are becoming more severe. The 2022 National Blue Book on Depression specifically states that students are responsible for approximately 50% of all clinically diagnosed depression cases in China [4]. Additionally, the average detection rate of depressive symptoms among Chinese college students has consistently increased over the past year [5]. It is crucial to note that this matter is not exclusive to China. Depressive symptoms are prevalent among college students in the United States, with a prevalence rate of approximately 20% [6], 32% in Eastern Europe [7], and 31% in Germany [8]. This has been consistently documented in international studies. Collectively, these results emphasize the global severity and widespread nature of depression among college students and underscore the pressing necessity for coordinated interventions by public health and education authorities.

Stress has been identified as a significant risk factor for the development of depressive symptoms [9, 10]. The current body of evidence suggests that college students generally experience higher levels of overall stress than the general population [11]. Additionally, academic stress is significantly and positively associated with the development of depressive symptoms. It is important to mention that the family environment moderates this association, with the character of the parent-child relationship being a critical factor. The parent-child relationship is regarded as a critical protective factor that affects the susceptibility of college students to depression. Additionally, college students with differing degrees of depressive symptoms have been observed to exhibit substantial variations in the characteristics of their parent-child relationships [12].

Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory posits that the family microsystem is fortified by the robust protective factor of sustained support from parents and other primary caregivers. Research has demonstrated that this type of support can alleviate the negative psychological consequences of adverse life events for adolescents and is a crucial defense against the onset of mental health disorders, such as depression [13]. This theory also establishes the fundamental theoretical framework for the conceptualization of the parent-child relationship as a stress-buffering mechanism, a concept that is intricately linked to social support theory and attachment theory. This mechanism is founded on a secure attachment bond that is established through positive, long-term interactions between parents and children. This bond allows individuals to cultivate a consistent sense of psychological security and the perceived availability of social support, which in turn promotes effective emotion regulation, resilience to adversity, and the tendency to seek assistance when necessary. In the context of stress, these attributes collectively establish the fundamental personality and behavioral foundation for psychological buffering.

The synergistic interaction between parental presence and parent-child communication is believed to be the primary mechanism by which academic stress is alleviated in parent-child relationships [14]. Parental presence is believed to create an emotionally secure environment that stabilizes cognition and prevents the accumulation of anxiety, while catastrophic cognitive appraisals of academic stress may be rectified through communication. In addition, communication is believed to facilitate the immediate emotional discharge of negative affect, while parental presence is believed to enhance psychological security through emotional approval, thereby reducing the risk of internalization. Furthermore, communication offers strategic guidance that enhances coping efficacy, and parental presence directly promotes academic efficacy by facilitating collaborative engagement in tasks such as studies methodologies discussions. In conjunction, these processes are believed to constitute a closed-loop support system that is cognitive, affective, and behavioral. Notably, academic stress is a unique, persistent, and developmentally normative stressor for college students; it is closely tied to self-evaluation, social comparison, future prospects, and parental expectations, making its interaction with family factors more prominent than other daily stressors.

A supportive parent-child relationship is regarded as a critical protective factor for the mental health of college students within the family system, as it has the potential to effectively counteract the negative emotions that are induced by stress when confronted with stressful situations [15]. The character of the parent-child relationship has also been demonstrated to interact with risk factors, thereby moderating the association between stressful life events and depression [16]. Consequently, the quality of the parent-child relationship may possess a substantial moderating effect on the relationship between academic stress and melancholy, serving as a potential protective factor. The precise interactive mechanism between academic stress and parent-child relationships remains obscure, despite the fact that existing research has confirmed that academic stress positively predicts depression and that parent-child relationships influence mental health outcomes. The stress-buffering effects of intimate parent-child bonds have been the primary focus of the majority of prior studies, with limited attention being paid to the conditional nature and complexity of this relationship.

The current study specifically examines the moderating role of parent-child relationship quality in the association between academic stress and depression among college students, drawing on two complementary data sources and rigorously controlling for potential confounding variables. This was done to address the research gap. The results are anticipated to address a critical lacuna in the current literature and to serve as empirical evidence to guide the development of family-integrated, targeted interventions for student mental health.

Study population and methods

Study population

This study incorporates data from two sources: the 2020 phase of the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS) and the Hubei College Student Health Survey (HCSHS), a field survey conducted among undergraduate students at a university in Hubei Province [17]. Supplement 1 contains the complete questionnaire of the HCSHS.

Logically inconsistent responses, missing data on key variables, implausible values, and statistical outliers were all exclusionary criteria for the CFPS sample. Participants were included if they reported “undergraduate” as their current educational level in response to question QC3 (“What is your current level of education?“).

The following inclusion criteria were met by eligible participants in the HCSHS: (1) they were 17 to 27 years old, (2) they are currently enrolled as a full-time undergraduate student, (3) they were fully conscious and capable of effective communication, and (4) they possessed adequate literacy and expressive ability, with no indication of cognitive impairment. Participants were excluded if they (1) failed to complete the questionnaire, (2) had lived on campus for fewer than six consecutive months, or (3) submitted questionnaires that contained significant missing items or obvious errors.

Research instruments

General demographic questionnaire

Participants’ background characteristics, such as gender, age, ethnicity, height, weight, self-reported health status, monthly household per capita income, academic year, major field of study, average weekly study hours, extracurricular participation, perceived academic stress, dietary habits, parent-child relationship quality, family values, parental educational level, and smoking status, were collected using a self-administered sociodemographic questionnaire.

Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D)

In the 2020 wave of the CFPS, the 8-item abbreviated form of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) was employed to evaluate depressive symptoms. The CES-D, which was initially devised by Radloff (1977), quantifies the frequency of depressive symptoms that were experienced in the previous week. Zhang (2010) validated a Chinese-adapted variant of the scale, which has been demonstrated to possess cultural validity and robust psychometric properties among Chinese populations [18]. The eight-item abbreviated form consists of four fundamental dimensions: depressive affect, positive affect (reverse-coded), somatic symptoms, and interpersonal problems. The participants rated each item on a 4-point Likert scale (1–4), with scores corresponding to the frequency of symptom occurrence over the preceding week: 1 = rarely or less than 1 day, 2 = 1 to 2 days, 3 = 3 to 4 days, and 4 = 5 to 7 days. Higher scores suggest a more severe depressive symptomology and a worse mental health status, with a total score ranging from 8 to 32. The HCSHS participants were administered this 8-item scale in the present study, and a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.901 was obtained, indicating outstanding internal consistency reliability.

College academic stress scale

A single-item self-report measure was employed to evaluate academic stress, which posed the question, “How much academic stress do you currently feel?” Responses were evaluated on a five-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from one (no stress) to five (excessive stress). Scores that were higher were indicative of a higher level of perceived academic stress.

Parent-child relationship

A solitary self-report item was employed to evaluate the quality of parent-child relationships: “How would you characterize your relationship with your parents over the past six months?” Responses were evaluated on a five-point ordinal scale, with anchors ranging from “not at all” to “very close.” Responses were dichotomized into a binary variable for analytical purposes. The first three categories (“not close at all,” “not close,” and “average”) were collapsed and coded as “average/not close,” while the final two categories (“close” and “very close”) were pooled and coded as “close.”

Statistical analysis

R software, version 4.5.0, was employed to conduct all statistical analyses. Continuous variables with an approximately normal distribution are reported as mean ± SD, while categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. For comparisons between two independent groups, independent-samples t tests were implemented, while 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was implemented for comparisons between three or more groups.

In order to investigate the relationship between depressive symptoms (dependent variable) and key predictors, such as academic stress, parent-child relationship quality, and their interaction term, a multiple linear regression model was developed. This model was adjusted for a range of demographic and behavioral covariates that are known to independently influence depression among college students. In particular, the model factored in gender, as female students are more likely to report higher levels of depressive symptoms as a result of biological and sociocultural factors. It also considered residence type (urban vs. rural), which reflects socioeconomic disparities related to access to mental health resources and academic expectations [19]; participation in student organization activities, a proxy for social engagement that may serve as a buffer against feelings of isolation and stress [20], and Body Mass Index (BMI), which has been established to be associated with both physical and mental health outcomes [21]. Well-established theoretical and empirical pathways that connect these covariates to depressive outcomes guided the selection of these covariates. The statistical significance level was established at P ≤ 0.05, and all statistical tests were 2-tailed.

Outcome

Description of basic characteristics

The final analytical sample was consisted of 871 college students, with 302 participants from the CFPS and 569 from the HCSHS. The mean age of the participants was 19.99 (SD, 2.63) years, and the majority were female (n = 558 [64.06%]) and freshmen (n = 490 [56.26%]). In terms of perceived academic stress, the mean score was 3.19 (SD, 0.96), while the mean score for depressive symptoms was 14.08 (SD, 3.90). In terms of the quality of the parent-child relationship, 672 participants (77.15%) reported a close relationship, while 199 (22.85%) designated their relationship as average or distant. Multiple linear regression analysis

After accounting for demographic covariates such as age and gender, the multiple linear regression analysis (Table 2) revealed that BMI was significantly positively associated with depressive symptoms among college students (B = 0.08, P < 0.05). This implies that each 1-unit increase BMI was associated with a 0.08-point increase in the mean depressive symptom score. See Table 2 for the results. Furthermore, the mean depressive symptom score of students in the HCSHS sample was 2.24 points higher than that of students in the CFPS sample, as the study sample affiliation (HCSHS vs. CFPS) was significantly associated with depressive symptoms (B = 2.24, P < 0.001). Students who reported average or poor health status in early adolescence had a mean depressive symptom score 1.38 points higher than those who reported good health. Additionally, health status in early adolescence (before age 14 years) exhibited a significant association with depressive symptoms (β = 1.38, P < 0.05).

The regression model encompassed academic stress, parent-child relationship quality, and their interaction term, all of which demonstrated statistically significant associations with depressive symptoms (β = 0.22, P < 0.001; β = 0.37, P < 0.001; interaction term: β = -0.24, P = 0.02). Additionally, stratified analyses demonstrated that the character of the parent-child relationship influenced the association between academic stress and depressive symptoms. There was a 0.23-point increase in mean depressive symptom scores for each 1-unit increase in academic stress among students with an average or distant parent-child relationship, and a 0.90-point increase in mean depressive symptom scores for each 1-unit increase in academic stress among students with a close parent-child relationship (B = 0.90, P < 0.001). The positive correlation between academic stress and depressive symptoms was less pronounced among students with average or distant parent-child relationships, as confirmed by simple slope analysis. Conversely, this correlation was significantly more pronounced among students with close parent-child relationships. These results indicate that, in this sample, a close parent-child relationship does not serve as a protective factor against academic stress; rather, it increases individuals’ susceptibility to it, which in turn exacerbates its detrimental effects on depressive symptoms (refer to Table 2; Fig. 1). We conducted the same regression analyses separately in two independent samples (CFPS and HCSHS) to evaluate the robustness of these results. Table 3 displays the findings: the predictive effects of academic stress and parent-child relationships on depressive symptoms, as well as their interaction, were significant in both samples after controlling for pertinent confounding variables. This suggests that the research conclusions are highly robust.

Discussion

This investigation concentrated on a sample of college students to conduct a systematic analysis of the correlations between academic stress, the quality of parent-child relationships, and their interaction with the severity of depressive symptoms. Covariates, such as BMI and early-life health status, were controlled for. The results elucidate the distinctive contributions of each variable to depressive symptoms, thereby providing empirical evidence to inform the development of targeted mental health interventions for college student populations.

Among Chinese college students, academic stress is regarded as the most primary stressor that contributes to depressive symptoms [22]. Academic stress was discovered to be a positive predictor of depressive symptoms in college students in the current study. Specifically, higher levels of academic stress were associated with a greater likelihood of experiencing depressive symptoms. This discovery is in accordance with previous empirical investigations [23–25]. A potential underlying mechanism is the dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis in response to external stressors, which results in the excessive secretion of stress hormones and the consequent damage to brain regions involved in emotional regulation [26].

Academic performance is widely recognized by the job market as a critical criterion for talent selection among college graduates in an employment landscape that is becoming increasingly competitive. Many students believe that maintaining consistently high academic performance is essential for securing desirable employment and gaining a competitive advantage. This perception necessitates a significant investment of time and energy in their scholastic pursuits, which in turn heightens their psychological and academic burdens. Many students are predisposed to feelings of anxiety, exhaustion, and helplessness as a result of prolonged exposure to a highly competitive academic environment. In this population, self-doubt may arise under persistent stress when academic performance fluctuates or fails to meet personal or external expectations. This is sometimes accompanied by intense frustration, which exacerbates the severity of depressive symptoms.

A significant positive predictor of depressive symptoms among college students was identified as a average or distant parent-child relationship in the current study (β = 0.37, P < 0.001). This finding is consistent with previous empirical research [27, 28]. Survey data suggests that college students who report positive parent-child relationships have a lower likelihood of experiencing depressive symptoms, while those with average or distant relationships face a higher risk and greater symptom severity [29]. The parent-child bond is widely recognized as a key determinant of adolescent and young adult mental health [30]. In accordance with this, Cheng et al. (2022) indicated that college students’ scores for depression, anxiety, and perceived tension were lower when they exhibited higher levels of adaptability and intimacy in the parent-child relationship [31]. One potential explanation for this association is that college students who have close parent-child relationships, on average, maintain consistent, in-depth communication with their parents. Parents may provide timely psychological support and guidance, which may prevent the chronic accumulation of negative emotions, and repressed emotional distress may be conveyed through such communication. Additionally, the development of a well-integrated, healthy personality and the maintenance of long-term psychological well-being and emotional stability are facilitated by the harmonious family environments in which students are reared who have positive parent-child relationships. Conversely, students who have average or distant parent-child relationships frequently develop in family environments that are emotionally constrained or conflictual, which impedes the development of a healthy personality and compromises the capacity to maintain positive mental health. Collectively, these results demonstrate that a positive parent-child relationship has the potential to improve the mental health of college students and reduce their susceptibility to psychological distress [32].

Previous research has suggested that a positive parent-child relationship is an essential protective factor against psychological distress. Nevertheless, the interaction term analysis conducted in the current study uncovered a unique moderating pattern: a close parent-child relationship was discovered to amplify the emotional response of college students to the adverse effects of a specific level of academic stress, thereby strengthening the association between academic stress and depressive symptoms. Conversely, an average or distant parent-child relationship was associated with a weakened emotional response, resulting in a nonsignificant association between academic stress and depressive symptoms. Notably, students with close relationships still exhibited substantially lower levels of depressive symptoms under high academic stress in the overall sample, despite the fact that close parent-child relationships intensified the negative effects of academic stress. This discovery implies that the protective properties of close parent-child relationships are diminished—rather than eliminated—in the presence of elevated academic stress. It does not imply that average or distant relationships serve as a buffer against the detrimental effects of academic stress. The complexity of parent-child relationship dynamics is underscored by this moderating effect, which deviates from traditional family support theory and contradicts the hypothesized direction.

A perceived tension filtering effect may be one potential underlying mechanism. College students with close parent-child relationships may experience heightened emotional reactivity to academic stress that is provoked by unmet parental expectations, as a result of a given level of academic stress being strongly associated with parental expectations [33]. When academic setbacks occur or parental expectations are not met, this amplified psychological sensitivity may escalate into significant psychological distress, as subpar academic performance may be interpreted as a failure to live up to family trust. The character of the parent-child relationship has a substantial moderating effect on perceived academic stress within the context of Chinese family culture [34]. In contrast, students who have distant parent-child relationships tend to adopt a more neutral cognitive assessment of the same level of academic stress, perceiving it as an individual issue rather than one related to family expectations. The psychological response to the stressor is dampened, and the likelihood of stress developing into depressive symptoms is reduced, as this neutral appraisal alleviates the family-related emotional burden prompted by academic stress. The immediate transmission of parental expectations is further intensified by the proliferation of digital platforms, such as WeChat parent groups and real-time academic feedback applications, according to digital ecological systems theory [35]. Consequently, the aforementioned psychological reactions are amplified. Inevitably, children experience academic stress induced by parental educational expectations, as demonstrated in existing research [36]. Higher parental educational expectations are linked to improved academic performance among students; however, they also subject students to increased academic stress, a dynamic that may jeopardize the mental health of college students. In contrast, parents who maintain scientifically grounded and reasonable expectations for their children may encourage the development of a positive, healthy self-concept in adolescents. This, in turn, helps to maintain positive mental health in the face of academic stress and promotes adaptive appraisal and perception of academic challenges [37]. Therefore, the psychological and emotional response to a specific level of academic stress is exacerbated by heightened sensitivity to parental expectations in college students with close parent-child relationships. This, in turn, increases the vulnerability to depressive symptoms due to the same academic stressor.

Another potential explanatory pathway is the emotional dependency differentiation moderating hypothesis. This hypothesis elucidates the manner in which the quality of parent-child relationships affects the intensity of individuals’ emotional responses and buffering behaviors in response to a specific level of academic stress. In general, college students who have close parent-child relationships have higher expectations for family support. When confronted with a particular degree of academic stress, they are more inclined to request emotional support and problem-solving assistance from their parents. Nevertheless, the initial academic stress response may be further exacerbated by the inability to procure such need-concordant support, which can result in the development of secondary negative emotions. However, if parents neglect to offer timely, need-concordant support—for example, if academic achievement is prioritized over emotional guidance and empathic understanding—a secondary adverse effect of unmet expectations may be triggered, thereby exacerbating the emotional response to academic stress and worsening depressive affect. Furthermore, depressive affect is considered a prototypical internalizing emotional disorder [38], and its development is extremely susceptible to parenting styles. In particular, parents who are of the opinion that negative emotions impede child development frequently implement neglectful or distraction-based strategies to assist their children in rapidly disengaging from negative emotions. The development of emotional regulation competencies is not effectively promoted by such parental practices, and college students’ capacity to process and integrate emotional information is impeded, thereby increasing their susceptibility to internalizing disorders.

Previous research has demonstrated that the development of depressive disorders is exclusively linked to parental neglect of the melancholy affect of college students [39]. The onset of depressive affect among college students has been demonstrated to be substantially and positively predicted by both maternal and paternal neglect [40]. In contrast, college students who have parent-child relationships that are relatively distant or low-closeness tend to develop a more independent stress-coping style throughout their developmental years. Students who are exposed to a specific degree of academic stress are more inclined to attribute it to personal effort than external support. This may lead to relatively mild emotional fluctuations and a reduced likelihood of experiencing depressive symptoms. This explanatory mechanism is a post-hoc inference that is based on cross-sectional data and is an unverified hypothesis that requires confirmation through subsequent causal research. These two hypothetical pathways offer a plausible interpretive framework for the observed reverse moderating effect, and they both strictly adhere to the definition of a moderating effect. Specifically, the quality of the parent-child relationship acts as a conditional variable that modifies the intensity of the association between academic stress and depressive symptoms. In order to directly test these hypotheses and elucidate the causal mechanisms underpinning the moderating role of parent-child relationship quality in the association between academic stress and depressive symptoms, it is imperative that future research employ longitudinal follow-up or experimental designs, as neither pathway has been empirically validated in a causal context.

Targeted interventions are encouraged to reduce the incidence of depressive symptoms among college students, as indicated by the results of this investigation. Academic evaluation systems should be improved at the institutional level to alleviate the tension that is associated with an excessive reliance on single-score assessments. It is also recommended that specialized courses on academic stress management and emotional regulation be provided, with a particular emphasis on assisting students with close parent-child relationships in the development of rational cognitive appraisals of academic stress and the decoupling of it from excessive parental expectations. This will facilitate the development of adaptive coping strategies. Parents should be advised to establish realistic educational expectations, refrain from overly associating their children’s academic performance with family affective recognition, and receive targeted training in effective parent-child communication skills at the familial level. This training should enable parents to balance constructive guidance with empathic emotional engagement, thereby reducing the amplified emotional reactivity of college students to a specific level of academic stress in the context of close parent-child relationships and preventing the secondary adverse effects of unmet support expectations. Concurrently, the establishment of a school-family joint intervention mechanism and the implementation of routine mental health assessments may be facilitated through collaboration between universities and families. In an effort to guarantee the comprehensive protection of the mental health and psychological well-being of college students, these endeavors should be tailored to specifically address the risk of depressive symptoms that arise from the intricate dynamics of parent-child relationships.

This investigation is not without its constraints. Initially, single-item measures were implemented to evaluate both academic stress and the character of parent–child relationships. Although this method alleviates respondent burden in large-scale surveys, and existing research has supported the validity of this single-item stress measure [41], such single-item indicators are limited in their ability to capture overall subjective perceptions. This can result in inadequate differentiation across specific dimensions and heightened vulnerability to transient mood states or response bias. This can obfuscate or distort true associations. Secondly, a cross-sectional research design does not permit causal inferences. The two putative mechanisms proposed to account for the reverse moderation effect represent post-hoc interpretations without empirical support for causality. To further validate these exploratory findings and clarify the potential causal mechanisms underlying the moderating role of parent–child relationship quality in the link between academic stress and depressive symptoms, future research should employ validated multidimensional scales along with longitudinal or experimental designs to enable more robust measurement and causal inference.

Summary

The risk of depressive symptoms among college students was substantially increased by academic stress and non-close or average parent-child relationship quality in this study. Additionally, a statistically significant interaction effect was identified between these two variables. In this cross-sectional sample, students with close parent-child relationships did not exhibit a stress-buffering effect; rather, they exhibited a stronger association between academic stress and depressive symptoms, indicating a pattern of increased sensitivity. This innovative discovery enhances the current comprehension of the joint association between the quality of parent-child relationships and the mental health of young adults in the context of academic stress. It also offers new empirical evidence to substantiate the development of testable hypotheses regarding the multifactorial correlates of depressive symptoms among college students. Direct validation of the proposed explanatory pathways is necessary in subsequent causal and longitudinal research.
