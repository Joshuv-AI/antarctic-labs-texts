SDG driven carbon aware machine learning model recommendation framework
Authors: Kurla Uday Kiran Reddy, B. A. Sabarish, P. Bagavathi Sivakumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding the carbon footprint is crucial for sustainable AI development. This work presents a framework called “carbon aware optimistic machine learning recommendation framework” (CAOMLF) that aims to balance machine learning performance with environmental sustainability by analyzing carbon emissions and energy consumption. This framework applies a multi-objective approach to balance between the predictive performance and corresponding environmental impact of carbon footprint using a trade-off score. This work evaluates basic machine learning tasks (classification, regression, and clustering) across various data types (text, image, and tabular) in a variety of scenarios and gives recommendations based on the trade-off score. For each of these tasks, a suite of five distinct model architectures have been systematically evaluated. This proposed framework consists of a training phase and a recommendation phase. In the training phase, the system captures data including accuracy and carbon emission for machine learning tasks with varying size of datasets in dimension. In the recommendation phase, the train once approach is used to suggest a better machine learning model with optimal hyperparameters for balancing performance and environmental impact.

Introduction

A carbon footprint represents the total volume of greenhouse gases—most notably carbon dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4), and nitrous oxide (N2O) emitted directly or indirectly by human activities or products. These emissions are converted to carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2eq) to provide a uniform measure of the global warming potential. In software development, this environmental footprint consists of the operational emissions resulting from the electricity consumption of servers, as well as the embodied carbon of the hardware manufacturing. This effect is now measured by the Software Carbon Intensity (SCI) standard (ISO/IEC21031:2024)1, which quantifies the carbon emissions per functional unit, such as an ML training run or an API call.

During the last ten years, the carbon footprint of the Information and Communication Technology (ICT) sector has changed quite a lot and at present it is responsible for about 1.4% to 3.9% of the world’s total greenhouse gas emissions2. From 2010 to 2020, the industry saw a huge divergence when, despite data traffic increasing 1,000%, total energy consumption stayed at the same level because of the huge efficiency gains in hyperscale data centers and hardware3. Now that we are entering the era of software defined emissions, the efficiency saturation is being questioned by the move to cloud, native architectures. The environmental impact of the majority of developers today is found mainly in “Scope 3” indirect emissions, where the energy cost of code execution is often hidden due to the lack of hardware transparency4.

Besides, Large Language Models (LLMs) and Machine Learning (ML) developments have deepened the crisis, resulting in more than 56% of worldwide organizations currently implementing AI-based applications5. Initially, sustainability in AI was mainly about finetuning a single model, so that a model like BLOOM would only produce 50 tonnes of CO2eq during training6, but now the AI sector is at a major turning point, with 80–90% of the total energy used by AI being attributed to the inference phase7. Moreover, one AI-generated query can use up to almost ten times the electricity of a conventional web search, showing a move from the era of general software to high-intensity “resource-heavy” applications8.

Nowadays, machine learning plays a central role in innovation and automated system design in almost all sectors, healthcare, finance, agriculture, climate science, education, etc. The performance of machine learning lies in its ability to learn patterns from large-scale datasets and use them to make accurate predictions, thereby enabling improved decision-making and system performance. Recent developments in these sectors have enhanced the scope of automation in critical software engineering phases, such as requirements gathering9, automated resource allocation10, and test case prioritizing11. Selecting and tuning a machine learning model and model configuration is a very important step in the development of an efficient machine learning solution. This important step is called hyperparameter optimization, which involves exploring a big search space of possible configurations to find one that gives the best performance.

The process of this search has a direct impact on the usage of project resource usage, including developer hours and the compute budget. Also, this process produces a considerable carbon footprint due to its high energy usage.

Despite its recognized importance, the prevailing model selection methodology remains fundamentally inefficient. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the traditional machine learning workflow relies on a reactive, “trial-and-error” strategy where models are trained without prior measurement of carbon emissions. This sequence results in a significant sustainability gap; when a model is found to be inefficient post-hoc, the development cycle including the wasted CO2 emissions, must be repeated to find a better configuration.

Standard development processes and present automated tools are predominantly focused on performance metrics, such as accuracy and F1 score measures, and ignore this environmental factor. This lack of pre-emptive visibility creates a critical void for a complete approach that can predict both accuracy and sustainability before the resource intensive training phase begins. To address this, we propose a framework named Carbon Aware Optimistic Machine Learning Framework (CAOMLF), as shown in Fig. 2. The proposed CAOMLF workflow shifts the analysis to the pre-training phase by first evaluating dataset characteristics. By utilizing this framework carbon emission and accuracy is predicted upfront. The system can select the most efficient model and configuration prior to execution. This transition ensures a “train once” approach, resulting in a workflow that is both fast and sustainable.

The framework differs from traditional AutoML frameworks using its pre-training predictive logic. While AutoML often depends on iterative execution phase running multiple trials under a compute budget this system uses a “train once” strategy. It forecasts carbon emissions and performance before any training occurs, eliminating the high energy usage underlying in trial-based searches.

This research study resolves the issue of excessive use of resources in traditional model and configuration selection by forecasting model performance and selection steps which have an environmental impact before the actual training of models. This study employs Random Forest Regressor metamodels to predict model performance and model’s environmental impact and derives the trade-off Score based on user defined priorities to identify the best machine learning model for the given application. The study subsequently offers a ranked list of the most sustainable model and its configurations.

Besides raising the accuracy, a model choice plays a key role in making sustainable applications development fit for the Global Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). The approach cuts down the environmental impact of machine learning (ML) workflows by picking models that don’t only work well but also lower carbon emissions thus supporting SDG 13 (Climate Action). " On top of that, sustainable and efficient model selection leads to resource optimization, energy, and computational cost reduction, and thus, to the achievement of SDG 7 (Affordable and Clean Energy). By considering the factors of sustainable development into the model selection process, machine learning research and applications are guaranteed to support innovation in technology and environmental goals.

The main goal of this research is to address the low efficiency and high resource process of selecting appropriate machine learning models. This system will recommend the appropriate machine learning model and its hyperparameters by balancing high performance and low carbon footprint. The specific objectives are to create a set of specialized predictive models to predict accuracy and carbon emissions for different algorithms, apply a trade-off scoring system that prioritizes configurations with high accuracy and low environmental impact, and construct a working prototype that transparently provides these recommendations.

Literature survey

In applied machine learning, a lot of energy is needed to train a machine learning model, leading to significant carbon footprints, high resource consumption, and environmental impact. The main part of the energy is used up in the trial-and-error phase to discover the best settings for a model, also referred to as hyperparameter optimization. To solve this issue, the concept of green AI was brought up which basically emphasizes making artificial intelligence and machine learning environmentally friendly. Scientists are looking into various methods to develop energy, efficient and sustainable models. As part of this effort, several tools are now available to calculate the carbon footprints of model training. For instance, popular Python libraries like CodeCarbon, CarbonTracker, Carbon-AI, Emissions-Tracker, Machine Learning Impact Calculator and Experiment Impact Tracker are designed to be integrated directly into training scripts. They run in the background, monitoring the energy consumption of hardware and using real-time data on the local power grid’s carbon intensity to produce a report on the final carbon emissions. The problem here is that this entire class of existing tools is reactive. They calculate the carbon footprint after the model has been built, which means that the energy has alreadpy been consumed and the carbon has already been emitted. Although valuable for reporting and creating awareness, they do not prevent the environmental cost from being incurred in the first place. We reviewed existing research studies to better understand the problem and tried to recommend a better design for an effective greener solution.

After reviewing many papers in the field of Green AI, we discovered that ML systems’ energy consumption is influenced by a variety of factors. Niccolo Marini et al.12, compares the energy consumption of AI algorithms in five different languages: C++, Java, Python, MATLAB, and R using the CodeCarbon framework. The trade-off between ease of coding and energy efficiency is highlighted by the results, which show that compiled languages like C + + and Java are up to 54× more efficient than interpreted ones. Abhishek Anilkumar et al.13, compares various deep learning models, such as long short-term memory (LSTM), convolutional neural network-long short-term memory (CNN-LSTM), and Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD), to estimate the levels of carbon dioxide emission using only data from 1870 to 2021. The study highlights that the DMD model stands out in identifying non-linear trends and accurately forecasting future CO2 levels across five European countries. Vincenzo De Martino et al.14, conducted a study on usage of large language models to analyse the adoption of 30 green architectural tactics through mining 168 GitHub projects. It finds that many energy-efficient design tactics, like using pre-trained libraries, are common, but advanced ones such as energy-aware simplifying remain neglected. Damiano Torre et al.15, performed a large-scale systematic mapping of 116 primary studies on Deep Learning techniques used for detecting cybersecurity attack detection, reviewing the type of attack involved. Finds that most of the common Deep Learning techniques used in Network Security domains are CNN and LSTM. Jieke Shi et al.16, presented Avatar, a framework for optimization that can reduce the size, latency, and energy consumption of large language models of code such as CodeBERT model by 180% while maintaining accuracy. Samarth Sikand et al.17, evaluated whether AI-generated code follows sustainable coding guidelines by analysing ChatGPT, BARD, and GitHub Copilot. Nearly all generated snippets ignore energy-efficient patterns and show non-green choices. Koushik Reddy P et al.18, highlighted the advantage of combining model accuracy with lower computational overhead by effectively predicting cardiovascular disease risk using machine learning, ensemble classifiers, and feature selection. Veronica Bolon-Canedo et al.19, classified green AI into two categories: “green-by AI” (using AI for environmental benefit) and “green-in AI” (making AI itself more sustainable). It discusses algorithmic, and hardware, focusing on carbon footprints tracking, efficiency tools, and the function of recent international laws such as the European Union Artificial Intelligence Act. Negar Alizadeh and Fernando Castor20, analyzed Energy Consumption in DL Models Across Different Runtime Infrastructures, this research experimentally investigates the execution time and energy consumption of Deep Learning (DL) models using various runtime providers (CUDA, TensorRT) and runtime infrastructures (PyTorch, TensorFlow, MXNet, and ONNX). While TensorRT regularly performs better than CUDA in efficiency, it does not identify a clear winner because performance varies greatly by model, batch size, and environment. This shows the unpredictability of DL performance and energy consumption. Santiago del Rey21, examined how the installation of the framework and training environment of machine learning systems impact energy consumption using CodeCarbon framework. To help developers to create the software systems that are sustainable and energy-efficient, it suggests a predictive tool for predicting energy consumption. Christian Birchler et al.22, proposed a method SDC-Scissor, using Machine Learning to predict and avoid irrelevant simulation test cases of self-driving cars prior to execution, enhancing cost-effectiveness of testing and reducing unnecessary testing efforts. Mohammed Alswaitti et al.23, suggested a data centric approach that trains models using elite samples instead of full datasets. Using CodeCarbon framework the authors measure energy use and carbon emissions during training. Results report reductions of up to 98% in energy and carbon impact while maintaining or improving model performance. Buddhadev Pusti et al.24, addressed Security and Energy-Aware Resource Allocation in Mobile Edge Computing (MEC) and proposed a flexible security model for MEC that establishes secure links between IoT devices and gateways using stream ciphers like Grain v1 and Rabbit. To complement this, they developed a Power-Aware VM allocation policy that reduces energy consumption by 6.43% compared to existing strategies while minimizing Service Level Agreement (SLA) violations. Tim Yarally et al.25, Studied Energy-Efficient Practices in Deep Learning Training Green AI, mainly focusing specifically on the training phase, uncovering energy-efficient practices related to hyperparameter tuning and network architecture design. These studies offer specific tactics to reduce energy use but often address isolated parts of the problem. Samarth Sikand et al.26, proposed the “Green AI Quotient,” which is a new metric for evaluating how “green” an AI based system is. This measure is an important step forward in the attempt to quantify sustainability. However, similarly to various other instruments, it is still mainly a tool for post hoc assessment that allows reviewing a system once it has already been developed. Yuyi Mao et al.27, published a Contemporary Survey on Green Edge AI Moreover, it highlights that although there are various energy, efficient design principles in different areas, incorporating them at the development level remains a challenging task for developers. Lei Jiang and Fan Chen28, introduced CarbonScaling, which is a framework to extend neural scaling laws by considering both operational and embodied carbon footprints. The plotting of the results from the experiments of different model training frameworks on scale and energy analyses demonstrates a clear power, law scaling of the train energy with the number of model parameters while showing a strong correlation between energy and time as well. Kostas Ordoumpozanis et al.29, Reviewed the Carbon Footprint of Finetuning Pre-trained Deep Learning Models in Medical Imaging, the research delved into the environmental impact of finetuning Vision Transformer (ViT) models in medical image analysis only. Various aspects like the size of the model, the type of hardware architecture, as well as the selection of the cloud service provider are factors that strongly affect the total energy usage and greenhouse gas emissions, according to the paper’s analysis.

Table 1 offers a systematic summary of the reviewed literature and research work is categorized into three main categories: metrics, architecture, and training. It reflects a move in methods from single metric to multi-metric frameworks that consider performance of the model, its environmental impact, to generate green architectures.

The focus of current green AI research is on how to measure the environmental impact of a model after its training. Tools such as CodeCarbon, Experiment Impact Tracker, and CarbonTracker are certainly valuable for post training reporting, however, they do not prevent the initial carbon emissions that occur when models are selected through trial and error. The study builds a framework for this limitation by providing pre-training guidance (train once). The system utilizes a dual Random Forest meta-model framework to independently predict model performance and carbon emissions for various configurations before any training begins. By calculating a trade-off score (as described in Sect. 4) the system provides a ranked list of sustainable model configurations allowing developers to select high-performing, eco-friendly architectures at the start of the development cycle, effectively eliminating the carbon-intensive trial-and-error phase.

Dataset generation/experimental setup

By giving equal priority to each scenario like image, text and tabular data, the dataset is effectively built to make predictions of optimal machine learning configurations. The dataset generation process begins by defining specific scenarios, which represent unique combinations of data type, dataset size, and dataset features; for instance, an image scenario is defined by a specific volume, such as 10,000 images, and a fixed image shape, such as 28 × 28 × 1. To cover a broad range of architectures and learning algorithms, distinct machine learning models suited to each task were chosen, to avoid bias in dataset generation, each scenario was trained using 100 distinct hyperparameter combinations, encompassing learning rate, number of layers, and regularization strength.

For dataset generation we chose CodeCarbon30 because it is a lightweight tool that is much easier to use than other options like Experiment Impact Tracker31 or CarbonTracker32, which often require complex setups or “admin” (sudo) permissions to access hardware data. CodeCarbon overcomes these hurdles by working seamlessly on both local computers and cloud platforms (like Google Colab) without special permissions, by using real-time local energy data to give much more accurate and up-to-date results.

By applying exactly 100 hyperparameter runs for each algorithm and dataset size, the predictive metamodels derived from these experiments are essentially unbiased in terms of distribution. For each type of data, the model hyperparameters are set and kept constant for a given algorithm across all dataset sizes. This experiment is well controlled and makes it possible to isolate the dataset as the variable that determines how computational cost and carbon emissions increase with data volume, without being affected by changes in configuration. Apart from that, in order to conduct a thorough examination of carbon emissions from different computing platforms, the dataset was created by running the experiments on local laptops, high performance cloud environments, and laboratory systems, as summarized in Table 2. In total, 5400 training runs were carried out for dataset generation. For experimental purposes, larger datasets were split into several size buckets in order to create experimental points that show the effect of dataset size on model performance and carbon emissions.

After each experimental run, metadata that are typical of data types, dataset size, dataset features, machine learning task, model trained, model hyperparameters, model performance, and model carbon emissions (kg) are saved as a CSV file for meta, model training purposes. The output measures include the carbon emissions generated during the training which have been measured by the CodeCarbon library and subsequently converted into equivalent CO2 emissions and performance metrics. By making sure that each scenario contributes a balanced number of experimental data points, the dataset does not lead to a biased representation. Such a design facilitates the creation of intelligent recommendation systems that can equitably evaluate the accuracy and sustainability trade-off in different project contexts, which, in the end, helps promote sustainable AI by embedding the environmental impact directly into the model selection process.

Methodology

This study establishes a framework that can recommends optimal machine learning model and its configurations for efficiency and sustainability by forecasting model performance as well as carbon emissions from given data set description. Instead of relying on multiple disparate external datasets, a unified and systematic experimental dataset was constructed through a well-planned data set construction process. Large, representative datasets from three different data types (text, image, and tabular) were chosen for this process, and these datasets were then methodically sampled and down-sampled to produce a number of variants that represent a broad range of conditions. These variations include differences in data types, feature counts (low, medium, and high), dataset sizes (small, medium, and large), and machine learning tasks like classification. An individual experimental run that combines a particular data set sample, data type, feature count, task, and chosen model architecture with a specific hyperparameter configuration is represented by each record in this extensive data set. All of these elements have been combined to create combinations that reflect carbon emissions and performance. Information such as dataset Features (Image shape, number columns), Machine Learning Task (Classification, Regression, and Clustering) Model Trained, Model Hyperparameters, Model performance measured and Model Carbon Emissions kg produced during model training are recorded. The output measurements include two metrics: the carbon emissions produced during model training, which is measured using the CodeCarbon library, and the Model performance metric, which evaluates the model’s performance in predicting tasks. CodeCarbon library tracks energy consumption and converts it into equivalent CO2 emissions, which are then measured in kilograms. By making sure that each scenario contributes the same number of experimental data points and that they are evenly distributed across all tested models and hyperparameter sets, the dataset eliminates biased representation that could cause the predictive models built on top of it to be misleading. This library first records the amount of energy utilized by the computational resources during each run of training and then converts that energy into kilos of CO2 emissions.

The first step in building the dataset was choosing base datasets that would be large enough for each type of data to thoroughly study the influence of data set size on model performance and carbon emissions. Next, these datasets were divided into different sample sizes. For every combination of these factors, data type, task category, dataset size, and feature count, multiple machine learning models were carefully chosen. These models were selected so as to ensure comprehensive coverage of the pertinent solution spaces by including a variety of algorithms and architectures suitable for the task and data. Every model that was chosen was trained and assessed using 100 distinct hyperparameter setups. To ensure high-quality training data, 100 hyperparameter combinations per model were selected via Random Search, providing a wide and unbiased coverage of the search space compared to fixed grid methods.

The generated extensive experimental data, which included the observed model performance and carbon emissions from all 100 hyperparameter setups for every combination of data type, task, size, and features, formed the foundation for the next step. In order to train specific Random Forest Regressor metamodels for the predictive modeling component, the data set was separated and arranged according to both data type and model architecture.

The selection of Random Forest Regressors for the metamodels was based on the non-linear behavior of the experimental data, as linear models failed to represent the non-linear relationships between dataset size, feature counts, and carbon emissions. Random Forest was selected over gradient boosting due to its better ability to handle high-dimensional hyperparameter spaces without overfitting33.

Two distinct sets of metamodels were created, one for estimating carbon emissions and the other for predicting the expected accuracy. These metamodels use the machine learning task and the data set properties (size, number of features, and type) as input. Trained on the extensive experimental data, the metamodels can predict complex nonlinear relationships and Correlations instantly, by avoiding the need of exhaustive hyperparameter searches for every new prediction task.

The Trade-off Score was chosen for its mathematical transparency and interpretability. Because model performance and carbon emissions exist on different scales, a weighted average of Min-Max Normalized values allows for a balanced comparison. This simple formulation acts as a controllable decision framework, allowing developers to prioritize sustainability or performance by adjusting weights (Wper and Wemi) without the energy-heavy trial-and-error execution.

During prediction and recommendation, users specify the fundamental characteristics of their dataset, including data type, machine learning task, number of samples, and feature count. The system continues to use the suitable metamodels on all possible candidate models and their hyperparameter configurations to obtain accuracy and carbon emissions prediction values according to the user’s problem setting. In order to help users make decisions a composite trade-off score is derived from the combination of accuracy and carbon emission prediction with weights being set at user’s discretion to reflect preference for performance, sustainability, or a compromise between the two. The system ranks the configurations based on this trade-off measure and provides the leading recommendations per model type as well as the best options overall.

All model training and experimental runs were done with a CPU. Besides, hardware-oriented experiments on GPU and TPU systems were carried out separately to assess the CO2 emissions at the infrastructure level. This way, the experiments can be reproduced and are accessible for practitioners not having specialized hardware like GPUs or TPUs. By taking this design decision, variation caused by different hardware has been minimized, and the results are based on settings that are generally available in both industry and academia.

Results

This sustainable machine learning optimizer fills a significant gap in the field by offering a model selection approach that considers both the environmental impact of training and inference alongside predictive performance. As machine learning becomes increasingly crucial for automated systems, industry decision making, and scientific discovery, researchers and society are concerned about the energy requirements of massive energy consumption of large-scale experimentation and model tuning. The approach change from performance (accuracy and F1 score) at any costs to a more balanced and conscientious model development cycle is facilitated by embedding sustainability objectives right into the configuration stage. Accuracy and carbon emissions (kg CO2-equivalent) were methodically measured for every experiment. This makes it possible to choose the best configurations, resulting in high predictive power and low emissions.

The metamodels were systematically tested using a 5-fold cross-validation method on an 80/20 train-test split of the 5,400 experimental runs. This ensures that the models were tested on completely unseen test data, with balanced sampling applied to maintain balanced distribution across image, text, and tabular domains.

The CAOMLF framework evaluated image classification tasks using 100,000 samples with an image shape of 28 × 28 × 1. Figure 4 shows that when priorities are balanced, the framework recommends CNN as ideal - accuracy hits 99.23%, while emissions stay at just 0.002708 kg. This specific configuration utilizes 64 and 128 filters across two layers with 10 training epochs. If priority shifts toward minimizing carbon emissions (70%) as shown in Fig. 5, the framework still recommends CNN but now much lighter ones - with 32 then 64 filters, reduces training by half. What stands out is the 98.57% accuracy held steady even as emissions drop sharply to just 0.000979 kg - cutting the footprint by around two-thirds. These results highlight that the framework effectively balances the trade-off between model performance and carbon emissions, selecting CNN architectures as the optimal option that maintains high accuracy while minimizing carbon emissions.

The CAOMLF framework evaluated text classification tasks using 650,000 samples and a feature space size of 5000 by balancing high accuracy and low carbon emissions. Figure 6 shows that when priorities are balanced, the framework recommends SVM as best with predicted accuracy of 92.89% and predicted carbon emissions of 0.003802 kg. Logistic Regression holds the second position with predicted accuracy of 89.57% and predicted carbon emissions of 0.001099 kg. If priority shifts toward minimizing carbon emissions (70%) as shown in Fig. 7, the frameworks still recommend SVM and the recommendation ranking for the remaining models remains unchanged. At the same time, the ranking highlights Multinomial Naive Bayes as a key Green AI alternative, with predicted accuracy of 88.56% and predicted carbon emissions of 0.000015 kg.

The figures show that there was a close match between the CAOMLF model predictions and the actual data. As an example, the system forecasted an accuracy of 92.89% for the SVM model, whereas the real execution resulted in 92.12%. Besides that, the system accurately predicted the carbon footprint of the various settings and thus, it brought out XGBoost as the most energy-consuming option and SVM as a very energy saving alternative. Beyond model and hyperparameter choices, the computational infrastructure significantly influences resource consumption and CO2 emissions, a clear compromise between computational speed and environmental sustainability is observed in Table 4 which presents a comparison of seven hardware specifications and provides evidence that training time and carbon emissions demonstrate non-linear relationship with compute power. The basic difference between traditional AutoML and the proposed framework exists in the elimination of iterative search iterations. As shown in Fig. 2, AutoML approaches undergo a repeated trial-and-error iteration, where multiple models are trained blindly before identifying inefficiencies. As shown in Fig. 16, the actual execution of an unlimited FLAML search experimentally results to 0.03222861 kg carbon emission, representing the total search footprint of testing seven distinct models. Conclusion

The proposed recommendation framework helps in building environment friendly machine learning model selection. This model selection is achieved through CAOMLF by estimating the trade-off score, and it lists models and their associated hyperparameters, along with carbon emissions and performance metrics, sorted in descending order. The model with the highest trade-off is recommended as the best model and configuration. It also allows the user to select the preferences for performance or sustainability, generating a trade-off score for model selection. The system recommends models based on a balance of accuracy and carbon emissions, encouraging greener machine learning practices. Future work can be extended for advanced machine learning models like Deep learning and quantum machine learning.
