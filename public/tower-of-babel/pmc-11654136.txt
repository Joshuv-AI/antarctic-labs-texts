Progressive decrease in partial pressure of end-tidal CO2 during posterior spinal fusion surgery in a child with idiopathic scoliosis and pectus excavatum: a case report
Authors: Yan Xu, Yanjun Lin, Chunling Jiang, Li Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundScoliosis surgery performed in a prone position may result in thoracic anatomical compression and alter local hemodynamics, increasing surgical risk, especially in patients with pectus excavatum. Most commonly, refractory hypotension is the first symptom of these circulatory changes. Here, we report a case with scoliosis and pectus excavatum under posterior spinal fusion that presented as a progressive decrease in the partial pressure of end-tidal CO2 (PETCO2) as the first symptom in the prone position. The probable reasons are analyzed, and solutions are suggested.Case presentationWe presented a case of a 17-year-old child suffering from idiopathic scoliosis and pectus excavatum who underwent elective posterior spinal fusion and developed a progressive decrease in PETCO2 accompanied by refractory hypotension while in the prone position. Background

Posterior spinal fusion surgery stands as a cornerstone in the treatment for severe idiopathic scoliosis among pediatric patients [1]. The prone position is necessary during this procedure, yet it may lead to hemodynamic changes due to increased mediastinal compression or obstructive cardiogenic shock [2, 3]. Consequently, profound hypotension has emerged as the predominant clinical manifestation among previous cases [4, 5]. In this report, we presented a case of a pediatric patient with scoliosis and pectus excavatum who experienced a progressive decrease in partial pressure of end-tidal CO2 (PETCO2) as the initial symptom, followed by profound hypotension shortly after the prone position. Written consent for publication was obtained from the patient’s legal guardian.

Case presentation

A 42-kg, 17-year-old female (height, 152 cm) with idiopathic scoliosis (Cobb’s angle 68°) and pectus excavatum was scheduled for posterior spinal fusion surgery. She had no history of cardiovascular disease and no self-reported discomfort in both supine and prone positions during sleep. Physical examination revealed a significant right-sided thoracic lateral spine deformity. Normal heart sounds were auscultated, and there were no cardiac murmurs. Preoperative standing anterior-posterior and lateral spine radiography and computed tomography chest imaging presented marked narrowing of the anteroposterior diameter, with the Haller index measured at (maximal transverse diameter / narrowest anterior posterior length of chest) approximately 5.3 [6]. This imaging indicated pressure exerted by the sternum and anterior vertebra on the right heart (Fig. 1). Laboratory blood tests, vascular ultrasound, electrocardiogram, transthoracic echocardiography (ejection fraction: 60%) and pulmonary function testing showed no significant abnormalities. Additionally, preoperative airway evaluation showed no evident abnormalities.

Intravenous midazolam was administered as premedication, followed by Propofol, sufentanil, and rocuronium to induce anaesthesia. After an uncomplicated endotracheal intubation was performed, an arterial line was placed for arterial blood pressure monitoring. Total intravenous anaesthesia with Propofol and remifentanil was initiated under somatosensory and motor-evoked potential monitoring. When the patient was positioned prone at 09:27 AM, almost immediately, a gradual decline in PETCO2 from 32 mm Hg to 17 mm Hg was observed alongside a reduction in systolic blood pressure from 120 to 70 mm Hg and an increase in heart rate from 75 to 100 bpm, without accompanying arrhythmias or ST segment alterations. During this period, oxygen saturation and airway pressure remained stable, without signs of obstructive ventilation. Moreover, there was no signs of flushing, hive, or wheezing on auscultation. Despite rapid infusion of 250 mL of crystalloid, intravenous boluses of metaraminol (0.2 mg × 3) and norepinephrine (5 µg × 2), the hypotension persisted, and PETCO2 remained below 20 mmHg. Arterial blood gas analysis indicated a PaCO2 of 41 mm Hg. Considering the absence of embolic risk factors and the suspicion of right heart compression due to chest deformity, the patient was promptly returned to the supine position. Subsequently, an immediate improvement in PETCO2 and resolution of hypotension were observed.

To confirm our hypothesis of significant compression of the right heart in the prone position, the patient was cautiously repositioned prone at 09:43 AM. Once again, a progressive decline in PETCO2 and hypotension happened, which was similar to the previous occurrence. Both parameters improved immediately upon manually relieving pressure on the patient’s chest (Fig. 2). Subsequently, adjustments were made to use longitudinal bolsters on either side of her chest instead of a transverse chest pad to minimize cardiac compression (Fig. 3), with surgical assistants prepared to promptly lift the patient as required. Following these measures, the patient remained prone for approximately 5 h without further complications, except for transient PETCO2 decreases during pedicle screw insertion.

Intraoperatively, the patient received 2 L of crystalloids, 1 L of colloid, and 0.2–0.4 mg of metaraminol intravenous intermittently. A nadir haemoglobin concentration was 7.3 g/dL, prompting the administration of 4 U blood. Adequate urine output of 4.7 mL/kg/h was maintained throughout the procedure. With a satisfactory neurological examination, the patient was extubated in the operating room and transferred to the Post anesthesia Care Unit for close neuromonitoring and discharged from the hospital on day 11.

Discussion and conclusion

While transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) has been recognized as a valuable tool for detailed cardiac assessment in cases of profound hypotension [4], it may be not routinely used in clinical practice. Our observations reminded the importance of PETCO2 decrease in alerting anesthesiologists to heart compression, particularly right heart compression. PETCO2 is a fundamental monitoring parameter during general anaesthesia. It reflects the concentration of CO2 at the end of exhalation, measured via a capnograph. PETCO2 levels are influenced by three main factors: ventilation, perfusion, and metabolism. Decreased PETCO2 may indicate hypoventilation, reduced cardiac output, or a ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) mismatch, which can occur in conditions such as pulmonary embolism or severe hypoxia. Conversely, increased PETCO2 can be a sign of hyperventilation, improved circulation, or heightened metabolic activity, such as in fever or sepsis. Variations in PETCO2 provide valuable insights into the efficiency of gas exchange and the balance between CO2 production and elimination, making it an essential parameter for evaluating patient condition during anesthesia. A sudden decline in PETCO2 and simultaneous rise in PaCO2 in intubated and ventilated patients, without changes in ventilation parameters or metabolism disturbances, may indicate a reduction in pulmonary blood flow, potentially preceding arterial hypotension [7]. This correlation has been well-established in both animal models and human studies [8, 9]. For instance, in a swine model, PETCO2 showed a significant correlation with cardiac index, with a best-fit line derived by least-squares analysis produced the equation: PETCO2 = 4.98 + 0.012 [cardiac index] (r 2 =0.82) [10].

Considering the preoperative condition of our patient, characterized by scoliosis and pectus excavatum resulting in leftward displacement of the heart, with the right atrium and right ventricle situated between the vertebral bodies and sternum, prone positioning with a transverse chest pad could exacerbate bony structural compression, leading to compression of the right heart system and consequent reduction in pulmonary blood flow. Therefore, we postulated that the observed decrease in PETCO2 was primarily attributable to reduced pulmonary blood flow, resulting in a reduction in CO2 exchange and subsequent decline in PETCO2. This reduction in pulmonary blood flow might have decreased left ventricular preload, eventually leading to decreased cardiac output, hypotension and reflex sinus tachycardia. Importantly, vigilance for pulmonary embolism during anaesthesia was warranted in cases of unexplained PETCO2 decreases and PaCO2 increases, particularly in the presence of deep vein thrombosis or gas embolism evidence [11, 12]. However, vascular ultrasound findings in our case revealed no abnormalities, and there was no supporting evidence for gas embolism. Furthermore, PETCO2 levels and hemodynamic status improved immediately upon supine repositioning, which was rarely associated with pulmonary embolisms.

Additionally, similar to spinal surgery, mechanical stresses associated with prone positioning may induce fluctuations in PETCO2 and hemodynamics. Notably, in our case, hypotension followed the decrease in PETCO2, contrary to previous reports where hypotension typically preceded PETCO2 decline [3, 13]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the patients’ spinal curvature, which inclined towards the right, resulting in anterior displacement of the vertebras and a narrow anteroposterior diameter between the sternum and anterior vertebra, proximal to the right cardiac system. Conversely, in a structurally normal thorax, prone positioning primarily compresses the left heart system, leading to initial hypotension. Moreover, transverse chest pads exerted additional pressure on the abnormal thoracic cavity, exacerbating the compression. Hence, although the patient tolerated the prone position during sleep, severe cardiac compression occurred during surgery, with subsequent recovery upon return to a supine position. Although reposition the transverse chest pads to the subcostal region may improve hemodynamics and allow surgery to proceed, subcostal bracing may be associated with inferior vena cava /liver compression and epidural venous engorgement, resulting in an increased risk of bleeding [5, 14, 15]. Furthermore, prior case reports of patients with flexible chest walls or pectus excavatum who developed hemodynamic instability after surgery in the prone position using transverse bolsters have highlighted the advantages of longitudinal bolsters [5, 13]. These bolsters reduce compression on the thoracic cage and allow prone surgeries to proceed successfully. Similarly, a study [2] involving healthy adult patients undergoing TEE assessment of five prone positioning systems commonly used in spine surgery found that longitudinal bolsters caused the least hemodynamic disturbance. Thus, we used longitudinal bolsters on either side of her chest instead of a transverse chest pad to minimize cardiac compression.

This case also had two Limitations. First, this is the description of a single patient, as well as the lack of direct visualization and cardiac monitoring during the episode of hemodynamic collapse. Nonetheless, the successful completion of the surgery with adjustment of bolster positioning leads us to believe that the cardiac compression from the transverse chest bolster was responsible for the event. Second, TEE guidance was not used in this case, because it is not routinely application in this type of surgery. However, we still recommend that patients with scoliosis and pectus excavatum undergoing scoliosis repair should receive intraoperative TEE monitoring whenever available.

Herein, patients with scoliosis and pectus excavatum are at increased risk of hemodynamic instability during spinal surgery in the prone position. A decrease in PET CO2 should attract the attention of the surgical team to a possible cardiac compression and that the solution may be the use of longitudinal bolsters in this type of case when TEE is not available. It is worth mentioning that, when there is a hemodynamic problem occurring directly after the prone position, the first reflex is to turn the patient back to the supine position.
