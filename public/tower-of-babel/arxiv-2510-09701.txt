Throughout the paper, we use a very important theorem connecting the

Definition 1.8

(Strong Open Set Condition)
.

Let the IFS
\{S_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
satisfies the open set condition. It is said to satisfy the strong open set condition if there exists a bounded open set
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
V\cap E\neq\emptyset
,
S_{i}(V)\subset V
for all
i
, and
S_{i}(V)\cap S_{j}(V)=\emptyset
while
d\big(S_{i}(V),S_{j}(V)\big)>0
for all
i\neq j
. Specifically, we use the distance (not a metric) here as:

d(X,Y):=\inf\left\{d(x,y):x\in X,y\in Y\right\},

for all
X,Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

With the strong open set condition, in Theorem 3.3 in
[
5
]
, the main result is:

Theorem 1.9
.

Given a self similar set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying the strong open set condition. Let
s
be the Hausdorff dimension of
E
, then:

(4)

\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)=\min\left\{\frac{|A|^{s}}{\mu(A)}:A\mbox{ is convex and }c\leq|A|\leq|E|\right\},

where
\mu
is the probability measure defined as:

\mu(A)=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{s}(A\cap E)}{\mathcal{H}^{s}(A)},

and
c:=\min_{i\neq j}d\big(S_{i}(E),S_{j}(E)\big)
.

Notice that we have
\min

Definition 1.10

(Optimal Set)
.

The set
A
in Theorem
1.9
that achieves the minimum in equation (
4
) is called an optimal set.

Main results on Upper Bounds

The discussion on the upper bound is relatively more straightforward since, by the definition of the upper bounds with
minimum
, as long as we can find a set, it automatically casts an upper bound.

Through the computation, we exploit the symmetry with the Cantor sets to approximate
\mu(A)
for any potential
optimal set
A
. Specifically, we consider a
d
-dimensional sphere centered at
(1/2,1/2,\cdots,1/2)
and varying the radius
radius whose circumference exactly touches one of the corner closest to
(0,0,\cdots,0)
of one of the fundamental part on some
k
-th depth. Here, we provide the pseudo-code of the experiment procedure.

Algorithm 2.1
.

We use the following procedure to compute the upper bound of the Hausdorff measure with
\mathcal{C}^{d}
up to depth
k
.

Due to symmetry about all
x_{i}=\frac{1}{2}
planes, our simulation shall just focus on
\mathcal{C}_{L}=\mathcal{C}\cap[0,\frac{1}{2}]
and its produced.

1:

We start with the set
S=\{0\}
, then we iteratively apply the iterated function system to
S
with a desired number of iteration (as our threshold
k
) so we can get an approximation of left endpoints of remaining intervals of
S\approx\mathcal{C}_{L}
.

2:

Generate the lattice of the coordinates
S^{d}
by iterating all coordinates.

3:

For all the points in
S^{d}
, compute its squared distance from
(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},\cdots,\frac{1}{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

4:

Given a fixed distance
r
, for each squared distance, count the number of lattice of coordinates that is within
r
. This is a lower bound of how much basic cubes are covered on this interval up to this threshold, record this number as
N
.

5:

Hence, we can compute the lower bound of the probability measure as:

\mu(A)\geq\frac{N}{2^{kd}}.

6:

This hence leads to the upper bound of the optimal set using equation (
4
).

As a side note, all computations in the first 4 steps utilizes the
Fraction
class in the programming language to prevent rounding errors that could impact the accuracy of
N
.

Remark 2.2
.

Algorithm
2.1
is a modification and generalization from

]
which deals with the two-dimensional case, which also trade-off some complexity for a more precise result.

•

Given a fixed depth

will be of

•

In the computation, the lower Cartesian products can be computed within reasonable time (
\leq 10

Foundations to the Lower Bounds

Remark 3.1
.

With the definition of Quarants and enumeration of the IFS, we can enumerate the basic sets of the
d
-th dimensional and level
1
Cantor set by inputting
D:=[0,1]^{d}
into the
S_{i}
function for
i=1,2,\cdots,2^{d}
.

Additionally, for a string of length
k
,
(i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{k})\in\{1,2,\cdots,2^{d}\}^{k}
, we consider the contraction as the composition of contractions,
i.e.
:

S_{i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{k}}:=S_{i_{1}}\circ S_{i_{2}}\circ\cdots\circ S_{i_{k}}

Since our main concern is the contractor of the set that we start from
D:=[0,1]^{d}
, we denote:

D_{i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{k}}:=S_{i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{k}}(D).

By definition,
\mu
is the probability measure so we have
\mu(B)\leq 1
naturally. Therefore, if we would want to find some tighter lower bound, we can improve by computing a better lower bound on the diameter of the optimal set.

By definition, we can develop the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

An optimal set
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
must intersect at least two basic sets on level 1,
i.e.
,
B
intersect at least two sets from
\{D_{1},D_{2},\cdots,D_{2^{d}}\}
, thus
|B|\geq 1/3=\min\{d(S_{i}(D),S_{j}(D):i\neq j\}
.

This is directly by
1.9
, while one can prove this otherwise by scaling the one intersected basic square to the whole domain.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the optimal set of
\mathcal{C}^{d}
,
|B|>\frac{5}{9}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
B
intersects 2 fundamental sets in level
1
, without loss of generality, we may assume that
B
intersects
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
. For the sake of contradiction, we suppose that
B\cap\big((\bigcup_{x:\text{odd}}D_{1,x})\cup(\bigcup_{y:\text{even}}D_{2,y})\big)=\emptyset
.

Then, we construct a scaling and translation map
\varphi
such that for all odd
x
and even
y
, it maps:

D_{1,y}\mapsto D_{y}\mbox{ and }D_{2,x}\mapsto D_{x}.

Concretely, we can define the map
\varphi:D_{1}\cup D_{2}\to\mathcal{C}^{d}
as follows:

\varphi(x)=\begin{cases}3x,&\mbox{ when }x\in D_{1},\\
3\big(x-(\frac{2}{3},0,0,\cdots,0)\big),&\mbox{ when }x\in D_{2}.\end{cases}

Then, we consider sets
T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{2^{d}}
constructed by:

T_{j}=\begin{cases}\varphi(D_{1,j}\cap B)\mbox{ when }j\mbox{ is even},\\
\varphi(D_{2,j}\cap B)\mbox{ when }j\mbox{ is odd}.\end{cases}

Now, denote
T:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{2^{d}}T_{i}
. Note that the scaling is exactly by 3 and is the exact duplicate of the self similar sets, we have:

\mu(T)=2^{d}\mu(B).

Now, we consider that the scaling factor is by
3
, so we have:

|T|\leq 3|B|.

However, we shall not be satisfactory with the inequality. We need a strict inequality. We observe that for the
\varphi
map, if we break into each dimension, they:

•

Either has the mapping with a negative translation factor of
-\frac{2}{3}
and
-\frac{4}{3}
, or

•

has the maximum possible distance in that that dimension smaller than
\frac{1}{3}
.

Hence, either way, the diameter of the shape after the transition would be strictly bounded by 3 times of the original diameter. Thus,
|T|<3|B|
.

Now, if we use
T
as as the optimal set in equation
4
:

\frac{|T|^{s}}{\mu(T)}<\frac{3^{s}|B|^{s}}{\mu(T)}=\frac{2^{d}|B|^{s}}{2^{d}\mu(B)}=\frac{|B|^{s}}{\mu(B)},

which implies that
T
is smaller than
B
, and thus
B
cannot be the optimal set, which contradicts with our assumption that
B
is optimal set.

Therefore,
B
must intersect at least one of the sets on the corner, specifically,
B\cap\big((\bigcup_{x:\text{odd}}D_{1,x})\cup(\bigcup_{y:\text{even}}D_{2,y})\big)\neq\emptyset
, and since it already intersects
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
, so it must at least intersect some
D_{2,y}
with
D_{1}
or some
D_{1,x}
with
D_{2}
. Thus, such distance is guaranteed to be
\frac{5}{9}
.
∎

For the better approximation procedure, we shall consider the repulsive lemma and repulsive pairs.

Definition 3.4

(Repulsive Pairs)
.

\{U,V\}
is defined to be a repulsive pair of order
k
with distance
d
if
U
and
V
are basic sets of level
k
for
\mathcal{C}^{d}
and
d_{\rm H}(U,V)>d
.

Lemma 3.5

(Repulsive Lemma)
.

Suppose
\{U,V\}
is a repulsive pair of order
k
with distance
|B|
, then:

\mu\big(B\cap(U\cup V)\big)\leq 2^{-dk}.

Proof.

Suppose
U=S_{I}(D)
and
V=S_{J}(D)
, where
I,J\in\{1,2,\cdots,2^{d}\}^{k}
. Since
|B|<d_{\rm H}(U,V)
, there exists
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that for all
L\in\{1,2,\cdots,2^{d}\}^{m}
:

d_{\rm H}\big(B\cap\big(S_{L,I}(D)\cup S_{L,J}(D)\big)\big).

Hence, by the monotonicity of the measure:

\displaystyle\mu\big(B\cap(U\cup V)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\mu\left(\bigcup_{L\in\{1,2,\cdots,2^{d}\}^{m}}\big(B\cap\big(S_{L,I}(D)\cup S_{L,J}(D)\big)\big)\right)

\displaystyle=(2^{d})^{m}\cdot 2^{-d(k+m)}=2^{-dk}.

∎

Now, we can use this notation to generalize a tighter lower bound by using the following proposition.

Proposition 3.6
.

Suppose
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an optimal set of
\mathcal{C}^{d}
such that
|B|\leq L

(5)

\mu(B)\leq 1-N_{k}(L)2^{d-kd-1},

where
N_{k}(L)
is the maximum number of matching (vertices that do not share a common edge) over all edges that connect between:

\displaystyle I\in

\displaystyle X_{k}=\{1,i_{2},\cdots,i_{k}:i_{t}=1,2,\cdots,2^{d}\mbox{ for }2\leq t\leq k\},

\displaystyle J\in

\displaystyle X_{k}=\{2^{d},i_{2},\cdots,i_{k}:i_{t}=1,2,\cdots,2^{d}\mbox{ for }2\leq t\leq k\},

in which
|S_{I}(0)-S_{J}(0)|>L
, where
0:=(0,\cdots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

Consider that there is a total of
N_{k}(L)
disjoint repulsive pairs of order
k
for
[0,\frac{1}{2}]^{d}\cup[\frac{1}{2},1]^{d}
whose Hausdorff distances are bounded below by
L
, and note that the diameter of
B
is bounded above by
L
so the optimal set covers at most one of those fundamental sets there, hence:

\mu\left(B\cap\left(\left[0,\frac{1}{2}\right]^{d}\cup\left[\frac{1}{2},1\right]^{d}\right)\right)\leq\frac{1}{2^{d-1}}-\frac{N_{k}(L)}{2^{kd}}.

Now, by symmetry, there will be a total of

\mu(B)\leq 1-2^{d-1}\cdot\frac{N_{k}(L)}{2^{kd}}=1-N_{k}(L)2^{d-kd-1}.\vskip-18.0pt

∎

Remark 3.7
.

The computation of

0\leq N_{k}(L)\leq 2^{(k-1)d},

and by plugging it in equation

which aligns with the maximum possible probability measure.

Definition 3.8

Let

and
d\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
be fixed, we define the function
W_{d,k}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
as:

W_{d,k}(x)=\big(1-N_{k}(x)\cdot 2^{d-kd-1}H_{d}\big)^{1/s_{d}},

where
N_{k}(x)
is defined as of in Proposition
3.6
,
H_{d}
is the upper bound of Hausdorff dimension of
\mathcal{C}^{d}
and
s_{d}
is the Hausdorff dimension of
\mathcal{C}^{d}
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Here, we can give some properties of the
W_{d,k}
function:

(1)

For fixed
d
and
k
,
W_{d,k}
is monotonic,
i.e.
,
W_{d,k}(x)\leq W_{d,k}(y)
if
x\leq y
.

(2)

If
W_{d,k}(c)<c
, then
|B|\neq c
, where
B
is an optimal set of
\mathcal{C}^{d}
.

Proof.

(1)

The monotonicity is by the fact that
N_{k}(x)\geq N_{k}(y)
when
x\leq y
, since there will be at least that many repulsive pairs when the distances are shorter.

(2)

For the sake of contradiction, suppose
W_{d,k}(c)<c
and
|B|=c
, with Proposition
3.6
, we have:

\displaystyle\mu(B)\leq 1-2N_{k}(c)4^{-k}<\frac{c^{s}}{H_{d}}\leq\mu(B),

which is a contradiction.

∎

Note that the
W_{d,k}(x)
function allows more possibilities in finding stricter bounds for the Hausdorff measure. However, it is very much constrained by the computation capabilities of the computer. When the dimension
d
and/or the layer
k
gets larger, it requires exponentially longer time to compute the number of repulsive pairs.

4.
Computation of Lower Bounds

With a few lower dimensions, however, we may utilize the feature on very low levels to computer better lower bounds on the Hausdorff measure.

For dimension 3, recall from Theorem
2.3
, we have:

\mathcal{H}^{s_{3}}(\mathcal{C}^{3})\leq 2.352741546983966.

Also, by Theorem
3.3
, let
B
be the optimal set, we know that
|B|>5/9
, so we can consequently have:

\mu(B)\geq\frac{(5/9)^{3\log_{3}2}}{2.352741546983966}>0.139716>\frac{1}{8}.

Now, we can claim that
|B|\geq\frac{2\sqrt{11}}{9}
, which happens to be the Hausdorff distance between the following eight repulsive pairs or order 2:

\displaystyle\{D_{1,1},D_{2,7}\},\{D_{1,2},D_{2,8}\},\{D_{1,3},D_{2,5}\},\{D_{1,4},D_{2,5}\},

\displaystyle\{D_{1,5},D_{2,4}\},\{D_{1,6},D_{2,3}\},\{D_{1,7},D_{2,1}\},\{D_{1,8},D_{2,2}\}.

That is,

d_{\rm H}(D_{1,1},D_{2,7})=\cdots=d_{\rm H}(D_{1,8},D_{2,2})=\sqrt{\frac{2^{2}+2^{2}+6^{2}}{9^{2}}}=\frac{2\sqrt{11}}{9}.

Hence, if
|B|<\frac{2\sqrt{11}}{9}
, there will be 8 repulsive pairs and

\mu(B)\leq 8\times\frac{1}{64}=\frac{1}{8},

which contradicts the fact that
\mu(B)>\frac{1}{8}
, implying
|B|\geq\frac{2\sqrt{11}}{9}
.

With the updated diameter, we have:

\mu(B)\geq\frac{(2\sqrt{11}/9)^{3\log_{3}2}}{2.352741546983966}>0.238561>\frac{12}{64}=\frac{3}{16}.

Now, we claim that
|B|\geq\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}
, which happens to be the Hausdorff distance between the following four repulsive pairs of order 2:

(6)

\{D_{1,1},D_{2,8}\},\{D_{1,3},D_{2,6}\},\{D_{1,5},D_{2,4}\},\{D_{1,7},D_{2,2}\},

which we have:

d_{\rm H}(D_{1,1},D_{2,8})=\cdots=d_{\rm H}(D_{1,7},D_{2,2})=\sqrt{\frac{2^{2}+2^{2}+8^{2}}{9^{2}}}=\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}.

Hence, if
|B|<\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}
, there will be 4 repulsive pairs and

\mu(B)\leq(8+4)\times\frac{1}{64}=\frac{3}{16},

which contradicts
\mu(B)>\frac{3}{16}
, implying
|B|\geq\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}
.
Again, with the updated diameter, we have:

\mu(B)\geq\frac{(2\sqrt{2}/3)^{3\log_{3}2}}{2.352741546983966}>0.380202,

and this turns out to be the best approximations give the upper bound and level 2.

Then, we can think of some consequences of such result.

Lemma 4.1
.

An optimal set of
\mathcal{C}^{3}
must intersect four basic sets of level 1.

Proof.

First, for the sake of contradiction, we suppose that all optimal sets of
\mathcal{C}^{3}
covers at most two basic sets of level 1.

However, from (
6
), we found repulsive pairs such that
\mu(B)\geq 0.380202>\frac{3}{8}
, there must have been an optimal set that intersects at least three of the basic sets.

Then, suppose that all optimal sets of
\mathcal{C}^{3}
covers at most three basic sets of level 1.

Similarly, we can suppose that it intersects
D_{1}
,
D_{2}
, and
D_{3}
. We can use (
6
) from
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
, and we can assume all basic sets of level 2 from
D_{3}
are included. Hence, we have
\mu(B)>0.380202>\frac{3}{8}
, there must have been an optimal set that intersects at least four of the basic sets.
∎

Since we now have four basic sets of level 1 includes, we can try to find better bounds from that.

With four basic sets of level 1 included, we claim that
|B|\geq\frac{2\sqrt{26}}{9}
where we have to consider two cases, without loss of generality:

•

When the intersection is
D_{1},D_{2},D_{3},D_{4}
, or the four basic sets belong to the same face.

Here, this aligns with the Hausdorff distance between the following eight repulsive pairs of order 2:

\displaystyle\{D_{1,1},D_{4,7}\},\{D_{1,2},D_{4,8}\},\{D_{1,3},D_{4,5}\},\{D_{1,4},D_{4,5}\},

(7)

\displaystyle\{D_{1,5},D_{4,4}\},\{D_{1,6},D_{4,3}\},\{D_{1,7},D_{4,1}\},\{D_{1,8},D_{4,2}\}.

•

When the intersections are
D_{1},D_{2},D_{3},D_{5}
, or the four basic sets spans all faces.

Here, we consider the Hausdorff distance between the following eight repulsive pairs of order 2:

\displaystyle\{D_{2,1},D_{5,7}\},\{D_{2,2},D_{5,8}\},\{D_{2,3},D_{5,6}\},\{D_{2,4},D_{5,5}\},

(8)

\displaystyle\{D_{2,6},D_{3,1}\},\{D_{2,8},D_{3,2}\},\{D_{3,3},D_{5,1}\},\{D_{3,4},D_{5,2}\}.

Specifically, here we have:

\displaystyle d_{\rm H}(D_{1,1},D_{4,7})

\displaystyle=\cdots=d_{\rm H}(D_{3,8},D_{2,2})=d_{\rm H}(D_{2,1},D_{5,7})=\cdots=d_{\rm H}(D_{3,4},D_{5,2})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2^{2}+6^{2}+8^{2}}{9^{2}}}=\frac{2\sqrt{26}}{9}.

Hence, by if
|B|<\frac{2\sqrt{26}}{9}
, there will be (at least) eight repulsive pairs (for both cases), and hence:

\mu(B)\leq(16+8)\times\frac{1}{64}=\frac{3}{8},

which is a contradiction to that
\mu(B)>\frac{3}{8}
, which implies that
|B|\geq\frac{2\sqrt{26}}{9}
.

With the updated diameter, we have:

\mu(B)\geq\frac{(2\sqrt{26}/9)^{3\log_{3}2}}{2.352741546983966}>0.538462>\frac{1}{2}.

Now, we may use this as the conclusion to a stronger result:

Proposition 4.2

The optimal set of
\mathcal{C}^{3}
that covers five basic sets of level 1.

Proof.

For the sake of contradiction, we suppose that all optimal sets of
\mathcal{C}^{3}
covers at most four basic sets of level 1.

However, from (
7
) or (
8
), we found repulsive pairs such that
\mu(B)\geq 0.538462>\frac{1}{2}
, there must have been an optimal set that intersects at least five of the basic sets.
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

\mathcal{H}^{s_{3}}(\mathcal{C}^{3})>(2\sqrt{26}/9)^{3\log_{3}2}\geq 1.811621
.

This is directly from the lower bound of
