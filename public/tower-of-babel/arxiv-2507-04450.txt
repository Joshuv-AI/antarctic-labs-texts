Characterising buried objects in metal detection
P.D. Ledger, W.R.B. Lionheart

Abstract




Abstract

Current mathematical models for identifying highly conducting buried objects in metal detection assume that the soil is non-conducting and has the same permeability as free space. However, although the electrical conductivity of soil is low, it is not negligible and depends on factors such as soil type and salinity.
Moreover, the magnetic permeability of soil varies with its iron content and is often described as uncooperative.
Depending on the ground conditions, these soil’s properties can influence the induced voltages in the measurement coils of metal detectors and becomes of increasing importance as the frequency of the exciting current source is increased. In this work, we develop a new asymptotic expansion for the perturbed magnetic field due to the presence of a highly conducting magnetic buried object as its size tends to zero, which takes account of the ground conditions. The leading order term of this expansion can be expressed in terms of a complex symmetric rank-2 magnetic polarizability tensor, which characterises the object, can assist in its identification, and we provide conditions under which this tensor’s coefficients can be computed independently of the ground conditions. We demonstrate the improved accuracy of our new result, which takes account of the ground conditions, over the situation where the soil’s conductivity and permeability are not considered.

Keywords:
Metal detection; Polarizability tensors; Eddy current; Buried objects; Uncooperative soil.

1
Introduction

Metal detectors provide a low-cost portable solution for identifying buried metallic objects located close to the ground’s surface. Applications include identifying unexploded ordnance, anti-personnel and anti-vehicle landmines in former conflict zones, mineral prospecting and finding metallic items of archeological or forensic significance. They are also popular with hobbyist treasure hunters. Metal detectors work on the principle of magnetic induction and often consist a simple audible tone obtained from the induced voltage, which an operator uses to identify the presence of a conducting body. Key challenges for existing metal detectors include identifying the shape of a conducting body and distinguishing between a small object buried close to the surface and a larger object buried at greater depths. However, since the voltage signal induced in a measurement coil contains object characterisation information, there is scope to use this information to help to better identify hidden objects.

Metallic objects have a high electrical conductivity (typically of the order of
10^{6}
S/m or higher) and magnetic metals have a relative magnetic permeability that is substantially greater than
1
. Given the high conductivities of metals and the low frequencies of metal detectors (typically
10-100
kHz), mathematical models of metal detection use the eddy current approximation of Maxwell’s equations, where the displacement currents are neglected due to the dominance of the Ohmic currents
[
2
]
. It is also common to assume that the ground is non-conducting and have to the same magnetic permeability as free space. In this work, this important restriction is removed and new results on object characterisation are obtained.

While the conductivity of the soil is much smaller than that of the metallic object, its electrical conductivity is not zero and depends on the specific nature of the soil. Strongly saline soils, which includes dissolved salts that are naturally occurring as well as contamination from pollution or fertilisers, have an electrical conductivity of around 1.6 S/m
[
27
]
, but those soils with a low salt content have a lower electrical conductivity. Clay soils tend to have a higher electrical conductivity than those primarily made of silt or sand. While the relative magnetic permeability of many soils is typically close to 1 (typical value 1.0006), uncooperative soils and rocks with high iron content can have higher values e.g. volcanic rock and soil (typical value 1.021), granite (typical value 1.076) and rock in some iron mining areas (typical value 1.1)
[
26
]
. Depending on the ground conditions, the electrical conductivity and magnetic permeability of the soil can influence the signal produced and are of increasing importance as the frequency is increased.

Using the eddy current approximation of Maxwell’s equations, and assuming the conducting object is placed in a non-conducting background, Ammari, Chen, Chen, Garnier and Volkov
[
2
]
have developed the leading order term in an asymptotic expansion of the perturbed magnetic field due to the presence of a highly conducting object as its size tends to zero. Ledger and Lionheart
[
11
]
have shown that this leading order term can be written in terms of a complex symmetric rank-2 magnetic polarizability tensor (MPT), which is independent of the object position and provide explicit expressions for the computation of its coefficients.
The authors have also obtained a complete asymptotic expansion of the perturbed magnetic field where the higher order terms are written in terms of generalised MPTs
[
13
,
15
]
, which provide additional object characterisation information. The (generalised) MPTs are related to the generalised polarisation/polarizability tensors introduced by Ammari and Kang
[
4
]
for applications in electrical impedance tomography and composite materials, but require the solution of vectorial, rather than scalar, transmission problems and their coefficients depend on the object’s size, shape, electrical conductivity and magnetic permeability as well as the exciting frequency.
The MPT coefficients as a function of exciting frequency, known as its spectral signature
[
14
]
, also provide additional characterisation information and computational approaches
[
29
,
10
]
have been developed to efficiently compute the MPT spectral signature. Dictionaries of computed MPTs have been obtained
[
17
]
and machine learning approaches applied to identify classes of objects for applications in security screening
[
20
,
21
,
18
]
.

The novelties of this work are as follows:

•

The development of a new asymptotic expansion for the perturbed magnetic field evaluated in free space due to the presence of highly conducting (magnetic) buried object as its size tends to zero and the soil is a low-conducting medium.

•

Providing conditions under which the object can be characterised by a rank-2 MPT whose coefficients can be computed independently of the ground conditions.

•

Illustrating the improved accuracy obtained by the new asymptotic expansion, which incorporates information about the ground conditions, compared to a previous formulation that assumes the properties of the ground are the same as free space.

Fundamental to our work, will be the use of an appropriate Green’s function to take account of the soil-air interface. In general this will necessitate the use of a dyadic Green’s function rather than the scalar Laplace Green’s function considered in previous work
[
2
,
11
]
. To aid with this, we will draw on the work in the area of non-destructive testing where Green’s functions have been obtained for half-space problems involving a conductor-air interface
[
6
,
9
]
.

The material will be presented as follows: In Section
2
, the mathematical model for a buried highly conducting permeable object using the eddy current approximation of the Maxwell system is described. Then, in Section
3
, new energy estimates are obtained extending those presented in
[
2
]
to the situation considered in this work. Section
4
presents transmission problems that are independent of the object’s position. Then, in Section
5
, integral representation formulae are derived where the aforementioned dyadic Green’s function that takes account of the soil-air interface plays an important role. Section
6
derives the new asymptotic formula for the perturbed magnetic field as the size of the buried object tends to zero and Section
7
presents this in terms of a magnetic polarizability tensor representation that can be computed independently of the soil’s properties and the object’s position. In Section
8
we include a series of numerical results to illustrate our proposed approach.

\displaystyle\text{in ${B}_{\alpha}$},

.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
w
be the solution to (
10
). Then, by assuming that (
19
) holds, there exists a constant
C
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\times\left({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}\leq

\displaystyle C\alpha^{7/2}\mu_{r,*}(|\mu_{r,s}^{-1}-\mu_{r,*}^{-1}|+\nu)\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}\|_{W^{2,\infty}(B_{\alpha})},

(20a)

\displaystyle\left\|{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}-\left(\frac{\sigma_{*}-\sigma_{s}}{\sigma_{*}}\right)\nabla\phi_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}\leq

\displaystyle C\alpha^{9/2}\mu_{r,*}(|\mu_{r,s}^{-1}-\mu_{r,*}^{-1}|+\nu)\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}\|_{W^{2,\infty}(B_{\alpha})}.

(20b)

Proof.

First we construct

\displaystyle(

\displaystyle\mu_{0}^{-1}\nabla\times({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}),\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{\Omega_{fs}}+(\mu_{0}^{-1}\mu_{r,s}^{-1}\nabla\times({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}),\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{\Omega_{s}\setminus\overline{B_{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle+(\mu_{0}^{-1}\mu_{r,*}^{-1}\nabla\times({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}),\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{B_{\alpha}}-\mathrm{i}\omega\left(\sigma_{s}\left({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}\right),{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\right)_{\Omega_{s}\setminus\overline{B_{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\omega\left(\sigma_{*}\left({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}-\left(\frac{\sigma_{*}-\sigma_{s}}{\sigma_{*}}\right)\nabla\phi_{0}\right),{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\right)_{{B_{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}^{-1}\left((\mu_{r,s}^{-1}-\mu_{r,*}^{-1})\nabla\times({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}),\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\right)_{B_{\alpha}}-\mathrm{i}\omega((\sigma_{s}-\sigma_{*})({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}+\nabla\phi_{0}),{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{{B_{\alpha}}},

then we choose
{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}-\left(\frac{\sigma_{*}-\sigma_{s}}{\sigma_{*}}\right)\nabla\phi_{0}&\text{in $B_{\alpha}$}\\
{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}&\text{in ${\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\overline{B_{\alpha}}$}\end{array}\right.
and multiply by
\mu_{0}
so that

\displaystyle\|\nabla\times({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{fs})}+\mu_{r,s}^{-1}\|\nabla\times({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{s}\setminus\overline{B_{\alpha}})}

\displaystyle+\mu_{r,*}^{-1}\|\nabla\times({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}-\mathrm{i}\omega\mu_{0}\sigma_{s}\left\|{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{s}\setminus\overline{B_{\alpha}})}

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\omega\mu_{0}\sigma_{*}\left\|{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}-\left(\frac{\sigma_{*}-\sigma_{s}}{\sigma_{*}}\right)\nabla\phi_{0}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}({B_{\alpha}})}

\displaystyle=(\mu_{r,s}^{-1}-\mu_{r,*}^{-1})\left(\nabla\times({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}),\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\right)_{B_{\alpha}}-\mathrm{i}\omega\mu_{0}((\sigma_{s}-\sigma_{*})({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}+\nabla\phi_{0}),{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{{B_{\alpha}}},

and by the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality

\displaystyle\mu_{r,*}^{-1}

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\times\left({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}-\left(\frac{\sigma_{*}-\sigma_{s}}{\sigma_{*}}\right)\nabla\phi_{0}\right)\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}=\mu_{r,*}^{-1}\|\nabla\times({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left((\mu_{r,s}^{-1}-\mu_{r,*}^{-1})\nabla\times({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}),\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\right)_{B_{\alpha}}\right|+\omega\mu_{0}\left|((\sigma_{s}-\sigma_{*})({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}+\nabla\phi_{0}),{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{{B_{\alpha}}}\right|.

Now

\displaystyle\left|(\mu_{r,s}^{-1}-\mu_{r,*}^{-1})\left(\nabla\times({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}),\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\right)_{B_{\alpha}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq|\mu_{r,s}^{-1}-\mu_{r,*}^{-1}|\|\nabla\times({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}})\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}

\displaystyle\leq C|\mu_{r,s}^{-1}-\mu_{r,*}^{-1}|\alpha^{3/2}\alpha^{2}\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}\|_{W^{2,\infty}(B_{\alpha})}\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}

\displaystyle\leq C|\mu_{r,s}^{-1}-\mu_{r,*}^{-1}|\alpha^{7/2}\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}\|_{W^{2,\infty}(B_{\alpha})}\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})},

and

\displaystyle\omega\mu_{0}\left|((\sigma_{s}-\sigma_{*})({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}+\nabla\phi_{0}),{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{{B_{\alpha}}}\right|\leq

\displaystyle\omega\mu_{0}|\sigma_{s}-\sigma_{*}|\|{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}+\nabla\phi_{0}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}\|{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\omega\mu_{0}|\sigma_{s}-\sigma_{*}|\alpha\|\nabla\times({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}})\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}\alpha\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\alpha^{2}\omega\mu_{0}|\sigma_{s}-\sigma_{*}|\alpha^{3/2}\alpha^{2}\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}\|_{W^{2,\infty}(B_{\alpha})}\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\alpha^{11/2}\omega\mu_{0}|\sigma_{s}-\sigma_{*}|\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}\|_{W^{2,\infty}(B_{\alpha})}\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\alpha^{7/2}\nu\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}\|_{W^{2,\infty}(B_{\alpha})}\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})},

which follows from (
17
),

\displaystyle\|\nabla\times({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}})\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}\leq C\alpha^{3/2}\alpha^{3}\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}\|_{W^{2,\infty}(B_{\alpha})},

(21)

and
\|{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}\leq C\alpha\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}
and
\alpha^{2}\omega\mu_{0}|\sigma_{s}-\sigma_{*}|\leq\nu
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\times\left({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}-\left(\frac{\sigma_{*}-\sigma_{s}}{\sigma_{*}}\right)\nabla\phi_{0}\right)\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}\leq C\mu_{r,*}\alpha^{7/2}(|\mu_{r,s}^{-1}-\mu_{r,*}^{-1}|+\nu)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}\|_{W^{2,\infty}(B_{\alpha})}\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})},

(22)

which leads to (
20a
). Using (
18
) gives (
20b
).
∎

3.2
Improved energy estimates for
\sigma_{s}\neq 0

To circumvent the assumption (
19
), a different approach is followed below. This is motivated by considering the soil as a form of regularisation to the non-conducting background case. We illustrate this for
{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}
below. Defining the bilinear forms

\displaystyle a({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}},{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}):=

\displaystyle(\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}},\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{\Omega_{fs}\cup\Omega_{s}},

\displaystyle a^{\varepsilon}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}},{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}):=

\displaystyle(\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}},\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{\Omega_{fs}}+(\mu_{r,s}^{-1}\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}},\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{\Omega_{s}}-\mathrm{i}\omega\mu_{0}(\sigma_{s}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}},{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{\Omega_{s}},

then denoting
{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}^{\varepsilon}\in\tilde{X}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}^{0}\in\tilde{X}
as the solutions to

\displaystyle a^{\varepsilon}({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}^{\varepsilon},{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})=\mathrm{i}\omega\mu_{0}f({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}),\qquad\text{and}\qquad a({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}^{0},{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})=\mathrm{i}\omega\mu_{0}f({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}),

with and without soil, respectively, where the linear form
f({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}):=({\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}^{s},{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{\Omega_{fs}}
,
we can estimate

\displaystyle|a({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}},{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})-a^{\varepsilon}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}},{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})|\leq

\displaystyle|1-\mu_{r,s}^{-1}||(\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}},\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{\Omega_{s}}+\omega\mu_{0}(\sigma_{s}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}},{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{\Omega_{s}}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|1-\mu_{r,s}^{-1}|\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{s})}\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{s})}+\omega\mu_{0}\sigma_{s}\|{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{s})}\|{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{s})}.

Since
\tilde{X}
involves a divergence constraint that can be imposed through a mixed formulation, we have following
[
30
]
[pg 30-31],
[
5
]
,

\displaystyle\|{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}^{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}^{\varepsilon}\|_{Y}\leq C\inf_{{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\in\tilde{X}}\left(\|{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}^{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\|_{Y}+\sup_{{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}\in\tilde{X}}\frac{|a({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}},{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}})-a^{\varepsilon}({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}},{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}})|}{\|{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}\|_{Y}}\right),

where
Y={\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}(\hbox{curl},\Omega_{s}\cup\Omega_{fs})
. Noting
{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}^{0}\in\tilde{X}
, we get

\displaystyle\|{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}^{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}^{\varepsilon}\|_{Y}\leq C\varepsilon\|{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}^{0}\|_{Y},

where

\varepsilon=\omega\mu_{0}\sigma_{s}D^{2}
(note that
|1-\mu_{r,s}^{-1}|\omega\mu_{0}\sigma_{s}D^{2}\leq\varepsilon
since
1\leq\mu_{r,s}\leq 1+\frac{\alpha}{D}
) and
D
is an appropriate length scale, which is introduced so that
C
is dimensionless.
As
{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}^{0}
is the unique solution to
a({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}^{0},{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})=\mathrm{i}\omega\mu_{0}({\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}^{s},{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{\Omega_{fs}},
we have, via a stability estimate
[
30
]
[pg 30-31],
[
5
]
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla\times({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}^{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}^{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{s})}\leq\|{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}^{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}^{\varepsilon}\|_{Y}\leq C\varepsilon\omega\mu_{0}\|f\|_{Y^{\prime}},

where
C
is independent of
\varepsilon
. Note that
\|f\|_{Y^{\prime}}=\displaystyle\sup_{{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\in Y\setminus\{{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}\}}\frac{|f({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})|}{\|{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\|_{Y}}
.

In a similar way, introducing the bilinear forms

\displaystyle b({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}},{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}):=

\displaystyle(\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}},\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{\Omega_{fs}\cup\Omega_{s}\setminus\overline{B_{\alpha}}}+(\mu_{r,*}^{-1}\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}},\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{B_{\alpha}}-\mathrm{i}\omega\mu_{0}(\sigma_{*}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}},{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{B_{\alpha}},

\displaystyle b^{\varepsilon}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}},{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}):=

\displaystyle(\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}},\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{\Omega_{fs}}+(\mu_{r,s}^{-1}\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}},\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{\Omega_{s}\setminus\overline{B_{\alpha}}}+(\mu_{r,*}^{-1}\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}},\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{B_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\omega\mu_{0}(\sigma_{s}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}},{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{\Omega_{s}\setminus\overline{B_{\alpha}}}-\mathrm{i}\omega\mu_{0}(\sigma_{*}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}},{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{B_{\alpha}},

and writing
{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{\varepsilon}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}-\left(\frac{\sigma_{*}-\sigma_{s}}{\sigma_{*}}\right)\nabla\phi_{0}&\text{in $B_{\alpha}$}\\
{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}&\text{in ${\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\overline{B_{\alpha}}$}\end{array}\right.
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{0}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}-\nabla\phi_{0}&\text{in $B_{\alpha}$}\\
{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}&\text{in ${\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\overline{B_{\alpha}}$}\end{array}\right.
, which satisfy

\displaystyle b^{\varepsilon}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{\varepsilon},{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})=g({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})\qquad\text{and}\qquad b({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{0},{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})=g({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}};\sigma_{s}=0,\mu_{r,s}=1),

for all
{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\in\tilde{X}
with and without soil, respectively. In the above,

\displaystyle g({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}):=\left((\mu_{r,s}^{-1}-\mu_{r,*}^{-1})\nabla\times({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}),\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\right)_{B_{\alpha}}-\mathrm{i}\omega\mu_{0}((\sigma_{s}-\sigma_{*})({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}+\nabla\phi_{0}),{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})_{{B_{\alpha}}},

which simplifies accordingly if
\mu_{r,s}=1
and
\sigma_{s}=0
. Then, following similar steps to the above,
we can estimate

\displaystyle\|\nabla\times({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}\leq

\displaystyle\|{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{\varepsilon}\|_{Y}\leq C\varepsilon\|g({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}};\sigma_{s}=0,\mu_{r,s}=1)\|_{Y^{\prime}}

(23)

where again
\varepsilon=\omega\mu_{0}\sigma_{s}D^{2}
and
D
corresponds to the depth of the object. We use the above to establish the following.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
w
be the solution to (
10
). Provided that
\varepsilon\leq\nu
, there exists a constant
C
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\times\left({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}\leq

\displaystyle C\alpha^{7/2}\mu_{r,*}(|1-\mu_{r,*}^{-1}|+\nu)\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}\|_{W^{2,\infty}(B_{\alpha})},

(24a)

\displaystyle\left\|{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}-\left(\frac{\sigma_{*}-\sigma_{s}}{\sigma_{*}}\right)\nabla\phi_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}\leq

\displaystyle C\alpha^{9/2}\mu_{r,*}(|1-\mu_{r,*}^{-1}|+\nu)\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}\|_{W^{2,\infty}(B_{\alpha})}.

(24b)

Proof.

By the triangle inequality

\displaystyle\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}\leq\|\nabla\times({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{\varepsilon}-{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{0})\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}+\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})},

where we choose
{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{\varepsilon}={\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}-\left(\frac{\sigma_{*}-\sigma_{s}}{\sigma_{*}}\right)\nabla\phi_{0}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{0}={\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}-\nabla\phi_{0}
in
B_{\alpha}
, and
{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{\varepsilon}={\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{0}={\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\overline{B_{\alpha}}
, with and without the soil present, respectively. Then, from Lemma
3.1
for the non-conducting soil,

\displaystyle\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}=\left\|\nabla\times\left({\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}\leq

\displaystyle C\alpha^{7/2}\mu_{r,*}(|1-\mu_{r,*}^{-1}|+\nu)\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}\|_{W^{2,\infty}(B_{\alpha})},

and from the proof of this result

\displaystyle|g({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}};\sigma_{s}=0,\mu_{r,s}=1)|\leq C\mu_{r,*}\alpha^{7/2}(|1-\mu_{r,*}^{-1}|+\nu)\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}\|_{W^{2,\infty}(B_{\alpha})}\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}.

So, using (
23
) we obtain (
24a
) provided that
\varepsilon\leq\nu
.
To obtain, (
24b
), we again use the triangle inequality

\displaystyle\|{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}\leq\|{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{\varepsilon}-{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}+\|{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})},

where, from Lemma
3.1
for the non-conducting soil,

\displaystyle\|{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}=\left\|{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{\alpha}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}-\nabla\phi_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}\leq

\displaystyle C\alpha^{9/2}\mu_{r,*}(|1-\mu_{r,*}^{-1}|+\nu)\|\nabla\times{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}\|_{W^{2,\infty}(B_{\alpha})}.

Noting that

\displaystyle\|{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}

\displaystyle\leq C\alpha\left(\|\nabla\times\left({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{\varepsilon}\right)\|_{L^{2}(B_{\alpha})}+\|{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}\cdot\left({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}-{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{\varepsilon}\right)\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{*})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\alpha\|{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{0}-{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}^{\varepsilon}\|_{Y}

\displaystyle\leq C\alpha\varepsilon\|g({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}};\sigma_{s}=0,\mu_{r,s}=1)\|_{Y^{\prime}},

we obtain the desired result provided that
\varepsilon\leq\nu
.

3.3
Comparison of problems for
w
with and without soil

We denote
{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{fs}\in\tilde{X}
as the solution to

9
Conclusions

In this paper we have derived an asymptotic expansion for the perturbed magnetic field in the presence of a
small, arbitrarily shaped, highly conducting buried object with a smooth boundary and constant material parameters. Previous work assumed that the ground is non-conducting and with the same permeability as free space. We have removed this key restriction, which is important for buried objects like landmines and UXO, and obtained new results on object characterisation.
We have provided conditions for the permeability and conductivity of the uncooperative soil in order to allow the object to be characterised by a complex symmetric rank-2 MPT, whose coefficients can be computed independently of the object’s position.
We have illustrated the importance of including the soil’s material properties and the usefulness of our new result through a series of computational experiments. Future work will include the extension to characterisation of buried objects by generalised MPTs allowing for discrimination of more subtle classes of objects.
