Complex Heavy Quarkonium Potential in an Anisotropic Collisional Quark-Gluon Plasma
Manas Debnath,, Lata Thakur,, Najmul Haque,

Abstract
We compute the complex heavy-quark potential in an anisotropic quark-gluon plasma (QGP) using kinetic theory with a Bhatnagar-Gross-Krook (BGK) collision kernel to incorporate collisions via gluon collective modes. By modifying the medium’s dielectric permittivity with momentum anisotropy and finite collisional rates, we derive both the real and imaginary components of the potential. While collisions have minimal impact on the real part and binding energy, they significantly amplify the imaginary part, modulating the effects of anisotropy. This enhancement increases quarkonium dissociation rates in a nonequilibrium QGP, offering deeper insights into suppression mechanisms.



I
Introduction

The study of the quark-gluon plasma (QGP) is a central focus of current experiments at the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider (RHIC) at Brookhaven National Laboratory (BNL) and at the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) at CERN. These experiments aim to uncover the properties of QGP, a state of matter that is expected to emerge at extremely high temperatures, where quarks and gluons exist in a deconfined state. At sufficiently high temperatures, QGP exhibits the behavior of a weakly interacting system, a feature that can be understood through hard thermal loop (HTL) resummation techniques
Weldon 1982
;
Braaten and Pisarski 1990
;
Frenkel and Taylor 1990
;
Braaten and Pisarski 1992
;
Haque et al. 2014
;
Haque and Mustafa 2025
.

Heavy quarkonium suppression serves as a crucial signal for the presence of QGP
Matsui and Satz 1986
. In a vacuum, quarkonium states are well characterized by non-relativistic potential models, which exhibit a Coulomb-like behavior at short distances and a linear confining potential at larger distances
Eichten et al. 1975
;
Eichten et al. 1978
;
Eichten et al. 1980
;
Buchmuller and Tye 1981
;
Koma et al. 2006
;
Brambilla et al. 2005
. However, in a hot medium, the quark-antiquark potential is modified due to Debye screening, leading to the dissociation of quarkonium bound states above the crossover temperature
Matsui and Satz 1986
.
The quarkonium states’ binding energy ranges from a few MeV to a few GeV, which could result in a “sequential suppression”
Karsch et al. 2006
as the weakly bound states melt near transition temperature, and the more tightly bound states melt at higher temperature.
This sequential melting of quarkonium states has been observed in experiments at RHIC and LHC, where higher-mass states of bottomonia and charmonia
Chatrchyan et al. 2011
;
Chatrchyan et al. 2012
exhibit stronger suppression.

While earlier studies of heavy quarkonium in QGP assume an isotropic medium
Agotiya et al. 2009
, the early stages of heavy-ion collisions introduce momentum-space anisotropies caused by the rapid expansion of the plasma
Romatschke and Strickland 2003
;
Romatschke and Strickland 2004
;
Mrowczynski et al. 2004
. Non-central collisions, in particular, generate an anisotropic momentum distribution, leading to differential pressures in longitudinal and transverse directions, resulting in a momentum space anisotropy, and it causes a kinetic instability
Mrowczynski et al. 2017
. The instability has a significant effect on the system’s evolution, leading to faster isotropization
Romatschke and Rebhan 2006
;
Mrowczynski et al. 2017
. This anisotropy influences collective gluon modes
Romatschke and Strickland 2003
;
Dumitru et al. 2009a
, which in turn modify the heavy-quark potential
Dumitru et al. 2008
;
Burnier et al. 2009
;
Dumitru et al. 2009b
;
Dumitru et al. 2009a
;
Thakur et al. 2013
;
Dong et al. 2022
.
In addition to the momentum anisotropy, the collisions among plasma constituents are usually ignored at high temperatures as the hard collision (large momentum exchange) time scale is much longer than the time scale for the soft momentum exchange
Zhao et al. 2023
. When the temperature of the system decreases, it becomes necessary to
consider both hard and soft collisions.

The gluon collective modes in an isotropic thermal QGP with collisional effects have been studied using a Bhatnagar-Gross-Krook (BGK) collisional kernel
Carrington et al. 2004
, and this formalism has been extended to anisotropic plasmas
Schenke et al. 2006
in which the authors have considered only those modes which are propagating parallel to the anisotropy
vector. In Ref.
Schenke et al. 2006
, the authors found a single unstable mode and a few other stable modes. Later, in Ref.
Kumar et al. 2018
, the authors considered
the modes that can propagate in all possible directions
with respect to the (small) anisotropy vector, where they considered the
interacting medium in terms of the effective quasiparticle
model. Recently, in Ref.
Zhao et al. 2023
, the authors extended the existing results for the collective modes by incorporating the full angular dependence of gluon propagation while considering general anisotropy strength.

Previous studies have explored quarkonium properties in an anisotropic QGP under collisionless scenarios
Dumitru et al. 2008
;
Dumitru et al. 2009a
;
Thakur et al. 2013
;
Thakur et al. 2014
, while Ref.
Zhao et al. 2023
examined collective modes with collisions. In this work, we extend these efforts by systematically deriving the complex heavy-quark potential in a momentum-anisotropic QGP with finite collisional effects. Using the collective modes of Ref.
Zhao et al. 2023
, for the first time, we compute the real and imaginary components of the potential, incorporating both general anisotropy and the BGK collision kernel. Our studies reveal how these effects influence quarkonium thermal widths and dissociation rates in a nonequilibrium QGP, providing valuable insights into quarkonium suppression in relativistic heavy-ion collisions.

In addition to the quarkonium potential in an anisotropy system, the quarkonium properties are also studied in various non-trivial situations relevant to HIC.
As a strong magnetic field appears in non-central HIC, the heavy quarkonium properties are studied in the presence of an external magnetic field
Sebastian et al. 2023a
;
Singh et al. 2018
. Additionally, the quarkonium properties are also studied when there is relative motion between the medium and quarkonium
Thakur et al. 2017
;
Sebastian et al. 2023b
and
in a bulk viscous medium
Du et al. 2017
;
Thakur et al. 2020
;
Thakur:2021vbo
.

The paper is structured as follows: Section
II
discusses the gluon propagator in a collisional anisotropic QGP medium. In Section
III
, we discuss the dielectric permittivity of the collisional anisotropic QGP medium. In section
IV
, we present the derivation of the complex heavy-quark potential and examine the impact of collisions and momentum-anisotropy on the real and imaginary components of the potential. In Section
V
, we study the thermal width of the ground state of charmonium. Finally, we summarize our results and discuss their implications in Section
VI
.

II
Gluon propagator in a collisional anisotropic medium

In this section, we discuss the gluon self-energy and propagator in the presence of a collisional anisotropic QGP medium, which we later use to compute the permittivity of the medium and eventually use that to determine the in-medium heavy quark-antiquark potential.

The momentum-space anisotropic correction is introduced by deforming the distribution functions of constituent particles
Romatschke and Strickland 2003
. The deformed distribution function is parametrized in the following spheroidal form

\displaystyle f(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=f_{\text{iso }}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\sqrt{k_{T}^{2}+(1+\xi)k_{L}^{2}}\right)

(1)

\displaystyle=f_{\text{iso }}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\sqrt{\bm{k}^{2}+\xi(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{n})^{2}}\right),

where
f(\bm{k})
is derived from
f_{iso}(k)
by filtering out particles with a large momentum component along the anisotropic direction,
\bm{n}

Romatschke and Strickland 2003
.
Here,
k_{T}
and
k_{L}
denote the components of momentum transverse and longitudinal to a given anisotropy axis
\bm{n}
with
k=|\bm{k}|=\sqrt{k_{T}^{2}+k_{L}^{2}}
. Here, in Eq. (
1
), the parameter
\xi
quantitatively measures the strength of momentum-space anisotropy. The quantity
\lambda
serves as a typical characteristic momentum scale of plasma particles. Here, it is relevant as the temperature is only in thermal equilibrium. Considering a spheroidal distortion, only one anisotropy axis is being considered. As a result, the modes of the gluon polarization tensor depend on the propagation angle relative to
\bm{n}
. We first calculate the gluon self-energy and propagators in a collisional anisotropic QGP medium. Subsequently, we determine the inverse dielectric permittivity of the anisotropic medium with collision, which will be used to compute the real and imaginary parts of the quarkonium static potential.

II.1
Gluon self-energy and resummed propagator

In the collisionless case, the gluon self-energy is typically obtained by the hard thermal loop (HTL) resummation technique from the Feynman diagrams.
However, for the collisional QGP, the gluon self-energy is usually obtained using the Kinetic theory
Carrington et al. 2004
;
Schenke et al. 2006
;
Zhao et al. 2023
.
Within the framework of kinetic theory, the phase-space distribution of partons (viz. gluons, quarks, and antiquarks) in the QCD plasma is characterized using gauge-covariant Wigner functions,
W^{i}(\bm{k},X)
with
i\in\{g,q,\bar{q}\}
and
\bm{k}
and
X
denote the three momentum and four position respectively. These particles are treated as massless and satisfy the mass-shell constraint.
To obtain the linearized transport equation, we expand
W^{i}(\bm{k},X)=W^{i}(\bm{k})+\delta W^{i}(\bm{k},X)
. After applying a gauge-covariant gradient expansion, the generalized Boltzmann equation governing quarks and gluons takes the form

\displaystyle V\cdot\partial_{X}\delta f^{i}_{a}(\bm{k},X)+g\theta_{i}\,V_{\mu}F^{\mu\nu}_{a}\partial_{\nu}^{(K)}f^{i}(\bm{k})=\mathcal{C}^{i}_{a}(\bm{k},X),

(2)

where
V=(1,\bm{v})
with
\bm{v}\equiv\bm{k}/k
and
\theta_{g}=\theta_{q}=1,\theta_{\bar{q}}=-1
.
In Eq. (
2
), the weak-coupling limit is taken, and higher order terms of coupling
g
are neglected. So the field tensor becomes
F^{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}+\mathcal{O}(g)
. In this limit, the theory becomes effectively Abelian, and one may consider there is no coupling between different color channels. The distribution function
f^{i}(\bm{k)}
and its color fluctuations
\delta f^{i}_{a}(\bm{k},X)
are related via the Wigner function as

\displaystyle f^{q/\bar{q}}(\bm{k})=\frac{1}{N_{c}}\operatorname{\text{Tr}}[W^{q/\bar{q}}(\bm{k},X)],\qquad f^{g}(\bm{k})=\frac{1}{N_{c}^{2}-1}\operatorname{\text{Tr}}[W^{g}(\bm{k},X)],

(3)

and

\displaystyle\delta f^{q/\bar{q}}_{a}(\bm{k},X)=2\operatorname{\text{Tr}}[t_{a}\,\delta W^{q/\bar{q}}(\bm{k},X)],\qquad\delta f^{g}_{a}(\bm{k},X)=\frac{1}{N_{c}}\operatorname{\text{Tr}}[T_{a}\,\delta W^{g}(\bm{k},X)],

(4)

where
t_{a}
is the
SU(N_{c})
group generators in the fundamental representation and
T_{a}
is the corresponding adjoint representations.
Now, for the collisionless case, the right-hand side of Eq. (
2
) is simply zero, that is,
\mathcal{C}^{i}_{a}(\bm{k},X)=0
. But when we include collision,
\mathcal{C}^{i}_{a}(\bm{k},X)
can be given by a BGK-type collision kernel,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{i}_{a}(\bm{k},X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nu\,\bigg[f^{i}_{a}(\bm{k},X)-\frac{N^{i}_{a}(X)}{N^{i}_{eq}}f^{i}_{eq}(k)\bigg],

(5)

with
f^{i}_{a}(\bm{k},X)=f^{i}(\bm{k})+\delta f^{i}_{a}(\bm{k},X)
, and

\displaystyle N^{i}_{a}(X)=\int_{\bm{k}}f^{i}_{a}(\bm{k},X),\qquad N^{i}_{eq}=\int_{\bm{k}}f^{i}_{eq}(k).

(6)

The BGK-type collision term in Eq. (
5
) characterizes the equilibration of the system through collisions, occurring over a timescale inversely proportional to
\nu
.
In this work, the collision parameter,
\nu
, is taken to be a free parameter, with no dependence on particle momentum and particle species. The advantage of the BGK kernel is that the particle number conservation is instantaneously followed; that is,

\int_{\bm{k}}\mathcal{C}^{i}_{a}(\bm{k},X)=0.

(7)

In Ref.
Schenke et al. 2006
, it is estimated that
\nu
lies in the range
\nu\sim 0.1m_{D}-0.2m_{D}
, but it is possible that the collision rate could even be larger than this. However, in this article, we have assumed a small
\nu
. The induced current for each color channel is given by,

\displaystyle J^{\mu}_{ind,a}=g\int_{\bm{k}}V^{\mu}\{2N_{c}\,\delta f^{g}_{a}(k,X)+N_{f}[\delta f^{q}_{a}(k,X)-\delta f^{\bar{q}}_{a}(k,X)]\}.

(8)

The gluon self-energy for the collisional case can be obtained following the Ref.
Zhao et al. 2023
;
Schenke et al. 2006
as

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu\nu}_{ab}(P)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\delta J^{\mu}_{ind,a}(P)}{\delta A^{b}_{\nu}(P)}

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g^{2}\delta_{ab}\int_{\bm{k}}V^{\mu}\partial_{l}^{\bm{k}}f(\bm{k})\frac{g^{l\nu}(\hat{\omega}-\bm{\hat{p}}\cdot\bm{v})-\hat{P}^{l}V^{\nu}}{\hat{\omega}-\bm{\hat{p}}\cdot\bm{v}+i\hat{\nu}}+g^{2}\delta_{ab}(i\hat{\nu})\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{V^{\mu}}{\hat{\omega}-\bm{\hat{p}}\cdot\bm{v}+i\hat{\nu}}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\int_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\partial_{l}^{(\bm{k}^{\prime})}f(\bm{k}^{\prime})\,\frac{g^{l\nu}(\hat{\omega}-\bm{\hat{p}}\cdot\bm{v}^{\prime})-\hat{P}^{l}V^{\prime\nu}}{\hat{\omega}-\bm{\hat{p}}\cdot\bm{v}^{\prime}+i\hat{\nu}}\mathcal{W}^{-1}(\hat{\omega},\hat{\nu}),

where

\int_{\bm{k}}\equiv\int\,d^{3}\bm{k}/(2\pi)^{3}
. For convenience, we define the dimensionless collision rate
\hat{\nu}=\nu/p
.

\hat{P}=(\hat{\omega},\hat{\bm{p}})
with
\hat{\omega}=\omega/p
and
\hat{\bm{p}}=\bm{p}/p
. The function
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\omega},\hat{\nu})
is defined as

\mathcal{W}(\hat{\omega},\hat{\nu})=1-\frac{i\hat{\nu}}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dy}{\hat{\omega}-y+i\hat{\nu}}=1-\frac{i\hat{\nu}}{2}\ln\frac{\hat{\omega}+i\hat{\nu}+1}{\hat{\omega}+i\hat{\nu}-1}.

(10)

In Eq. (
9
),
f(\bm{k})=N_{f}[f^{q}(\bm{k})+f^{\bar{q}}(\bm{k})]+N_{c}f^{g}(\mathbf{k})
, where the distribution function of partons is anisotropic as defined in eq. (
1
). The gluon self-energy still satisfied the transversality condition even after introducing the collisional term. The spatial component of gluon self-energy can be obtained after substituting the Eq. (
1
) into Eq. (
9
) as

\displaystyle\Pi^{ij}(P)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{D}^{2}\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}v^{i}\frac{v^{l}+\xi(\bm{v}\cdot\bm{n})n^{l}}{(1+\xi(\bm{v}\cdot\bm{n})^{2})^{2}}\frac{\delta^{lj}(\hat{\omega}-\bm{\hat{p}}\cdot\bm{v})+\hat{p}^{l}v^{j}}{\hat{\omega}-\hat{\bm{p}}\cdot\bm{v}+i\hat{\nu}}+i\hat{\nu}m_{D}^{2}\int\frac{d\Omega^{\prime}}{4\pi}\frac{v^{\prime i}}{\hat{\omega}-\hat{\bm{p}}\cdot\bm{v^{\prime}}+i\hat{\nu}}

(11)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{v^{l}+\xi(\bm{v}\cdot\bm{n})n^{l}}{(1+\xi(\bm{v}\cdot\bm{n})^{2})^{2}}\frac{\delta^{lj}(\hat{\omega}-\bm{\hat{p}}\cdot\bm{v})+\hat{p}^{l}v^{j}}{\hat{\omega}-\hat{\bm{p}}\cdot\bm{v}+i\hat{\nu}}\,\mathcal{W}(\hat{\omega},\hat{\nu})^{-1},

where

m_{D}^{2}=-\frac{g^{2}}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\,\,k^{2}\frac{df_{iso}(k)}{dk}.

(12)

The gluon self-energy given in Eq. (
11

.
Therefore, the symmetric tensor basis for anisotropic systems in the collisionless limit is unsuitable for the decomposition in in Eq. (
11
). Instead, we adopt a more general tensor basis with five structure functions to decompose the gluon self-energy.

\Pi^{ij}(P)=\alpha A^{ij}+\beta B^{ij}+\gamma C^{ij}+\delta D^{ij}+\rho E^{ij},

(13)

with the tensor basis defined as

\displaystyle A^{ij}=\delta^{ij}-\hat{p}^{i}\hat{p}^{j},\quad B^{ij}=\hat{p}^{i}\hat{p}^{j},\quad C^{ij}=\frac{\tilde{n}^{i}\tilde{n}^{j}}{\tilde{n}^{2}},\quad D^{ij}=\tilde{n}^{i}\hat{p}^{j},\quad E^{ij}=\tilde{n}^{j}\hat{p}^{i}

(14)

Here,
\tilde{n}^{i}\equiv A^{ij}n^{j}
, which is orthogonal to
\hat{p}^{i}
. Note that the basis functions follow the following orthonormal conditions:

\displaystyle A^{ij}A^{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2,\ B^{ij}B^{ij}=1,\ C^{ij}C^{ij}=1,\ D^{ij}D^{ij}=0,\ E^{ij}E^{ij}=0,

(15a)

\displaystyle\hat{p}^{i}A^{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\ \hat{p}^{i}B^{ij}=\hat{p}^{j},\ \hat{p}^{i}C^{ij}=0,\ \hat{p}^{i}D^{ij}=0,\ \hat{p}^{i}E^{ij}=\tilde{n}^{j},

(15b)

\displaystyle\tilde{n}^{i}A^{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{n}^{j},\ \tilde{n}^{i}B^{ij}=0,\ \tilde{n}^{i}C^{ij}=\tilde{n}^{j},\ \tilde{n}^{i}D^{ij}=\tilde{n}^{2}\hat{p}^{j},\ \tilde{n}^{i}E^{ij}=0.

(15c)

Using the orthonormal properties of the tensor basis, the five structure functions can be determined from Eq. (

\displaystyle\hat{p}^{i}\Pi^{ij}\hat{p}^{j}

\displaystyle=\beta,

(16a)

\displaystyle\quad\tilde{n}^{i}\Pi^{ij}\tilde{n}^{j}

\displaystyle=\tilde{n}^{2}(\alpha+\gamma),

(16b)

\displaystyle\tilde{n}^{i}\Pi^{ij}\hat{p}^{j}

\displaystyle=\tilde{n}^{2}\delta,

(16c)

\displaystyle\hat{p}^{i}\Pi^{ij}\tilde{n}^{j}

\displaystyle=\tilde{n}^{2}\rho,

(16d)

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\Pi^{ij}

\displaystyle=2\alpha+\beta+\gamma.

(16e)

By utilizing gluon self-energy, the inverse of the resummed gluon propagator
\Delta^{ij}(P)

\displaystyle(\Delta^{-1})^{ij}(P)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(p^{2}-\omega^{2}+\alpha)A^{ij}+(\beta-\omega^{2})B^{ij}+\gamma C^{ij}+\delta D^{ij}+\rho E^{ij}.

Upon inversion, the resummed gluon propagator becomes

\displaystyle\Delta^{ij}(P)=\Delta_{A}[A^{ij}-C^{ij}]+\Delta_{G}[(p^{2}-\omega^{2}+\alpha+\gamma)B^{ij}+(\beta-\omega^{2})C^{ij}-\delta D^{ij}-\rho E^{ij}],

with

\displaystyle\Delta_{A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega^{2}-p^{2}-\alpha},

\displaystyle\Delta_{G}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(p^{2}-\omega^{2}+\alpha+\gamma)(\beta-\omega^{2})-\tilde{n}^{2}\delta\rho}.

The dispersion relation can be determined by finding the poles of the propagator

\displaystyle\Delta^{-1}_{A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega^{2}-p^{2}-\alpha=0,

\displaystyle\Delta^{-1}_{G}

\displaystyle(p^{2}-\omega^{2}+\alpha+\gamma)(\beta-\omega^{2})-\tilde{n}^{2}\delta\rho=(\omega^{2}-\Omega_{+}^{2})(\omega^{2}-\Omega_{-}^{2})=0,

(20)

where

2\Omega^{2}_{\pm}=\bar{\Omega}^{2}\pm\sqrt{\bar{\Omega}^{4}-4[(\alpha+\gamma+p^{2})\beta-\tilde{n}^{2}\delta\rho]},

(21)

with

where
s(y,t,\hat{\nu})\equiv\sqrt{\left(y^{2}-\hat{\nu}^{2}+t^{2}-1\right)^{2}+4y^{2}\hat{\nu}^{2}t^{2}}
. So the mass scales become

(29b)

\displaystyle m_{\gamma}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\xi\,m_{D}^{2}}{2(1-t^{2})}\Big[(1+t^{2})f_{1}(\xi)-t(1-t^{2}+2\hat{\nu}^{2})\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{[1]}(\xi,t,\hat{\nu})-i\hat{\nu}(1+3t^{2})\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{[2]}(\xi,t,\hat{\nu})+2t\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{[3]}(\xi,t,\hat{\nu})\Big],

(29c)

\displaystyle m_{\delta}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{D}^{2}}{2\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}\Big[\hat{\nu}tf_{0}(\xi)-i\hat{\nu}^{2}t\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{[0]}(\xi,t,\hat{\nu})+\hat{\nu}(1-\xi t^{2})\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{[1]}(\xi,t,\hat{\nu})-i\xi t\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{[2]}(\xi,t,\hat{\nu})\Big],

(29d)

\displaystyle m_{\rho}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{m_{D}^{2}}{2\mathcal{W}(0,\hat{\nu})\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}\Big[\xi\hat{\nu}\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{[1]}(\xi,t,\hat{\nu})+i\xi t\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{[2]}(\xi,t,\hat{\nu})\Big],

(29e)

where the factor

(30a)

(30b)

III
Dielectric Permittivity

In this section, we deduce the color dielectric permittivity in a collisional anisotropic QGP medium, which is later used to determine the in-medium heavy quarkonium potential.
In the temporal axial gauge, this permittivity can be computed from the gluon propagator in the static limit as
Thakur et al. 2013

\displaystyle\epsilon^{-1}(p)=-\lim_{\omega\to 0}\omega^{2}\frac{p^{i}p^{j}}{p^{2}}\Delta_{ij}(\omega,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\omega\to 0}\omega^{2}\frac{(p^{2}-\omega^{2}+\alpha+\gamma)}{(\omega^{2}-\Omega_{+}^{2})(\omega^{2}-\Omega_{-}^{2})}.

(31)

The dielectric permittivity depends on the gluon propagator, which has both the real and imaginary parts. Hence, it is a complex quantity with both real and imaginary parts, which can be written as

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,\epsilon^{-1}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lim_{\omega\to 0}\omega^{2}\frac{p^{i}p^{j}}{p^{2}}\text{Re}\Delta_{ij}(\omega,p).

(32)

In the static limit, the real part of the dielectric permittivity can be written in terms of the mass scales as

\displaystyle{\rm Re}\,\epsilon^{-1}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{p^{2}(p^{2}+m_{\alpha}^{2}+m_{\gamma}^{2})}{\left(p^{4}+p^{2}\left(m_{\alpha}^{2}+m_{\beta}^{2}+m_{\gamma}^{2}\right)+m_{\alpha}^{2}m_{\beta}^{2}+m_{\beta}^{2}m_{\gamma}^{2}-m_{\delta}^{2}m_{\rho}^{2}\right)},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{p^{2}(p^{2}+m_{\alpha}^{2}+m_{\gamma}^{2})}{(p^{2}+m_{+}^{2})(p^{2}+m_{-}^{2})},

where

2m^{2}_{\pm}=\left(m_{\alpha}^{2}+m_{\beta}^{2}+m_{\gamma}^{2}\right)\pm\sqrt{\left(m_{\alpha}^{2}+m_{\beta}^{2}+m_{\gamma}^{2}\right)^{2}-4\left\{m_{\beta}^{2}(m_{\alpha}^{2}+m_{\gamma}^{2})-m_{\delta}^{2}m_{\rho}^{2}\right\}}\ .

(34)

Additionally, the imaginary part of the dielectric permittivity is given by

\displaystyle\text{Im}\,\epsilon^{-1}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lim_{\omega\to 0}\omega^{2}\frac{p^{i}p^{j}}{p^{2}}\text{Im}\Delta_{ij}(\omega,p).

(35)

Based on the spectral function approach
Weldon 1990
, the imaginary part of the gluon propagator can be expressed as

\displaystyle\text{Im}\Delta^{ij}=-\pi(1+e^{-\beta\omega})\sum_{m}\mathrm{R}_{m}\mathcal{P}_{m}^{ij},

(36)

where
\mathcal{P}_{m}^{ij}=A^{ij},B^{ij},C^{ij},D^{ij},E^{ij}
- are the projection operators and

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}_{m}=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{e^{\beta\omega}}{e^{\beta\omega}-1}\rho_{m}(\omega,p),

(37)

where

\displaystyle\rho_{m}(\omega,p)=\lim_{\omega\to 0}\text{Im}[(p^{2}-\omega^{2}+\alpha+\gamma)\Delta_{G}].

(38)

In the static limit
(\omega\rightarrow 0)
, the imaginary part of the dielectric permittivity given in Eq. (
35
)) reduces to

\displaystyle\text{Im}\,\epsilon^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lim_{\omega\to 0}\frac{e^{\beta\omega}+1}{e^{\beta\omega}-1}\omega^{2}\;\text{Im}\left(\frac{p^{2}-\omega^{2}+\alpha+\gamma}{(\omega^{2}-\Omega_{+}^{2})(\omega^{2}-\Omega_{-}^{2})}\right)

(39)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\lim_{\omega\to 0}\frac{2\omega T}{(\Re\Omega_{+}^{2})^{2}\ \left(\omega^{2}-\Re\Omega_{-}^{2}\right)^{2}}\bigg[\left(p^{2}+m_{\alpha}^{2}+m_{\gamma}^{2}\right)\left\{\Re\Omega_{+}^{2}\ \Im\Omega_{-}^{2}-\Im\Omega_{+}^{2}\ (\omega^{2}-\Re\Omega_{-}^{2})\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt+\left(\Im\alpha+\Im\gamma\right)\Re\Omega_{+}^{2}\left(\omega^{2}-\Re\Omega_{-}^{2}\right)\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\omega\to 0}\frac{2T}{(\Re\Omega_{+}^{2})^{2}\ \left(1-\frac{\Re\Omega_{-}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\right)^{2}}\bigg[\left(p^{2}+m_{\alpha}^{2}+m_{\gamma}^{2}\right)\left\{\Re\Omega_{+}^{2}\ \frac{\Im\Omega_{-}^{2}}{\omega^{3}}-\frac{\Im\Omega_{+}^{2}}{\omega}\ \left(1-\frac{\Re\Omega_{-}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt+\left(\frac{\Im\alpha}{\omega}+\frac{\Im\gamma}{\omega}\right)\Re\Omega_{+}^{2}\left(1-\frac{\Re\Omega_{-}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\right)\bigg].

To determine
\text{Im}\epsilon^{-1}
, we need to evaluate the following quantities in the static limit as

\displaystyle\lim_{\omega\to 0}\Re\Omega_{+}^{2}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle(p^{2}+m_{\alpha}^{2}+m_{\gamma}^{2})+\mathcal{O}\left(\omega\right),

\displaystyle\lim_{\omega\to 0}\Re\Omega_{-}^{2}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{\omega^{2}}{p^{2}}\left[-m_{\beta}^{2}+\frac{2m_{\rho}^{2}m_{\delta}^{2}}{p^{2}+m_{\alpha}^{2}+m_{\gamma}^{2}}\right]+\mathcal{O}\left(\omega^{3}\right),

\displaystyle\lim_{\omega\to 0}\Im\Omega_{+}^{2}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle(\Im\alpha+\Im\gamma)+\mathcal{O}\left(\omega^{2}\right),

\displaystyle\lim_{\omega\to 0}\Im\Omega_{-}^{2}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\Im\beta+\mathcal{O}\left(\omega^{4}\right).

(40)

Using the static limit from Eq. (
III
) in Eq. (

\displaystyle\text{Im}\,\epsilon^{-1}(p,\xi,\nu,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\omega\to 0}\frac{2\Im\beta\ p^{4}T\left(p^{2}+m_{\alpha}^{2}+m_{\gamma}^{2}\right)^{2}}{\omega^{3}\left(p^{4}+p^{2}\left(m_{\alpha}^{2}+m_{\beta}^{2}+m_{\gamma}^{2}\right)+m_{\alpha}^{2}m_{\beta}^{2}+m_{\beta}^{2}m_{\gamma}^{2}-m_{\delta}^{2}m_{\rho}^{2}\right)^{2}}

(41)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\pi\,m_{i\beta}^{2}\,pT\left(p^{2}+m_{\alpha}^{2}+m_{\gamma}^{2}\right)^{2}}{\left(p^{4}+p^{2}\left(m_{\alpha}^{2}+m_{\beta}^{2}+m_{\gamma}^{2}\right)+m_{\alpha}^{2}m_{\beta}^{2}+m_{\beta}^{2}m_{\gamma}^{2}-m_{\delta}^{2}m_{\rho}^{2}\right)^{2}},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\pi\,m_{i\beta}^{2}\,pT\left(p^{2}+m_{\alpha}^{2}+m_{\gamma}^{2}\right)^{2}}{\left(p^{2}+m_{+}^{2}\right)^{2}\left(p^{2}+m_{-}^{2}\right)^{2}}.

In Eq. (
41

m_{i\beta}
as

\displaystyle m_{i\beta}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{\pi}\lim_{\omega\to 0}\frac{p^{3}}{\omega^{3}}\Im\beta

(42)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi\mathcal{W}\left(0,\hat{\nu}\right)}\Bigg[\frac{\hat{\nu}}{1+\hat{\nu}^{2}}m_{\beta}^{2}-\frac{m_{D}^{2}}{2}\left\{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(\xi,t,\hat{\nu})-i\,\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{[0]}(\xi,t,\hat{\nu})\right\}\Bigg],

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(\xi,t,\hat{\nu})=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{(\hat{\nu}+itx)(i\hat{\nu}+\xi tx)\ \text{sgn}(tx)}{\left(1+\xi x^{2}\right)^{2}\left[(tx-i\hat{\nu})^{2}-(1-t^{2})(1-x^{2})\right]^{3/2}}\,dx.

(43)

In the collision-less medium
(\nu=0)
, the new mass scale

becomes

\displaystyle m_{i\beta,0}^{2}

\displaystyle\lim_{\hat{\nu}\to 0}\frac{m_{D}^{2}}{\pi}\left[i\,\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{[0]}(\xi,t,\hat{\nu})-\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(\xi,t,\hat{\nu})\right]

(44)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m_{D}^{2}}{\left(1+\left(1-t^{2}\right)\xi\right)^{3/2}}\left[1+\frac{\xi}{2}\frac{\left(1+t^{2}\right)+\xi\left(1-t^{2}\right)\left(1-2t^{2}\right)}{1+\left(1-t^{2}\right)\xi}\right].

With the analytic form of mass scales in Eq. (

in the analytic form using Eq. (

Lastly, in the limit
\xi=\nu=0,
all the mass scales becomes

\displaystyle m_{\alpha}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{\gamma}^{2}=m_{\delta}^{2}=m_{\rho}^{2}=m_{-}^{2}=0,

\displaystyle m_{\beta}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{i\beta}^{2}=m_{+}^{2}=m_{D}^{2}.

(45)

\displaystyle\Gamma(\xi,\nu,T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbf{r}}|\psi(\mathbf{r},T)|^{2}\text{Im}V(\mathbf{r},\xi,\nu,T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\pi\int_{r=0}^{\infty}dr\,r^{2}\,\int_{\Theta=0}^{\pi}d\Theta\,\sin(\Theta)\,|\psi(r,T)|^{2}\,\text{Im}V(r,\Theta,\xi,\nu,T)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left[\cos\left(p\,r\,t\cos\Theta\right)J_{0}(pr\,\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\sin\Theta)-1\right]\left(\alpha+\frac{2\sigma}{p^{2}}\right)\text{Im}\epsilon^{-1}(p,\xi,\nu,T),

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\frac{2}{a_{0}},

\displaystyle p\,r\,t\cos\Theta,

VI
Summary and discussions

In this article, we studied the complex heavy quarkonium potential in a QGP medium in the presence of a momentum-space anisotropy, considering the effect of collisions among the plasma constituents.
The anisotropic correction in momentum space is applied by considering the Romatschke-Strickland anisotropy distribution function of the thermal partons. Consequently, the gluon self-energy is rederived by solving linearized kinetic equations of the Boltzmann-Vlasov type, incorporating the BGK collision kernel.
We further computed the resummed gluon propagator, based on which we calculated the
real and imaginary parts of the dielectric permittivity of the medium in the static limit. Using this dielectric permittivity, we derived the complex potential for heavy quarkonia and analyzed how this potential is affected in the presence of a collisional anisotropic medium.
We observed that the momentum-space anisotropic correction, parameterized by
\xi
, reduces the screening effect. The anisotropy in momentum space makes the potential anisotropic that depends on the angle
\Theta
between the
Q\bar{Q}
dipole axis and the direction of anisotropy.
Though the momentum-space anisotropy reduces the screening, the effect of the collision in the real part of the potential is nominal.
Additionally, the magnitude of the imaginary part of the potential is enhanced with the collisional parameter
\nu
. The magnitude of the imaginary part of the potential is suppressed with
\xi
when
\nu=0
, whereas it is enhanced for a non-zero value of
\nu
.
Further, we calculated the decay width of
J/\psi
. We found that the decay width decreases with the increase in anisotropy for a collisionless plasma. However, the decay width increases with the increase of anisotropy in the presence of the collision among the plasma constituents. So, the charmonium dissociates faster in a collisional plasma.

We look forward to using our complex potential to calculate the binding energy and dissociation temperature in the near future.
