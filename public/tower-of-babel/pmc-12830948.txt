Association of the triglyceride-glucose series indices with hypertension and pre-hypertension in Anhui China
Authors: Aoyu Wang, Rongrong Li, Xiaoqing Li, Li Zhang, Hao Zhu, Li Shu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInsulin resistance (IR) and hypertension have a pivotal part in the pathophysiology of cardiovascular disease. The triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index and its derivatives—TyG–body mass index (BMI), TyG–waist circumference (WC), and TyG–WC-to-height ratio (WtHR)—have appeared as simple, cost-effective substitutes for evaluating IR. This research seeks to explore the associations between TyG combined with these obesity indicators and hypertension and pre-hypertension. Data of 9,132 participants in Bengbu (Anhui Province, China) 2019 was collected. The dose–response relationship between these parameters and hypertension or pre-hypertension were analyzed utilizing restricted cubic spline models with multiplex logistic regression (LR). The performance of these parameters in predicting hypertension or pre-hypertension was evaluated using LR and XGBoost model. LR analysis revealed that the TyG index and its derivatives were significantly associated with an increased risk of hypertension and pre-hypertension (P < 0.05). Notably, the TyG-WtHR index demonstrated superior potential predictive capacity (AUC = 0.631, OR = 3.133, 95% CI = 2.688–3.652). Dose–response relationships were predominantly nonlinear (P for nonlinear < 0.05), with some linear trends and sex-based differences. The XGBoost model outperformed the traditional LR model (AUC: LR—0.617, XGBoost—0.662), identifying the TyG–WC and TyG–WtHR indices as potential risk factors of hypertension. Exploratory interaction analysis shows that, high meat consumption was associated with higher hypertension likelihood in individuals with elevated TyG values. The TyG index and its derivatives, are contributive, sex-specific factors of hypertension in middle-aged and elder participants. Introduction

Hypertension (HTN) significantly contributes to the worldwide burden of disease, affecting numerous adults worldwide, and there is an increase in its prevalence1. This hallmark of this illness is characterized by elevated blood pressure (BP)2, which leads to alterations in the construction or functionality of the vascular system and the organs it serves3,4. Recent data indicate that there are now 1.28 billion persons aged 30 to 79 who have high blood pressure, up from 650 million in the previous 30 years5, which contributes to premature mortality, cardiovascular disease(CVD), and cardiovascular disease-associated disabilities, indicating hypertension has emerged as an important public global health issue6. Pre-hypertension often progresses to hypertension, leading to early-stage atherosclerosis and left ventricular dysfunction7. Moreover, the risks of HTN and CVD are higher in individuals with pulmonary pre-hypertension than in those with normotension8.

Insulin resistance (IR), a major contributor to vascular diseases in humans and has a strong correlation with HTN9. It can induce oxidative stress with varying intensities, disrupting endothelial function and promoting low-grade inflammation10,11. Inflammation serves as a crucial element in the progression of arterial stiffness and is instrumental in the development of HTN12. The Hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic Clamp (HEGC) test is the only method deemed to be the gold standard to judge IR13. Nevertheless, hurdles and invasiveness of this test present cost and feasibility-related challenges in both clinical research and large-scale public health studies4,14,15. Guerrero-Romero and colleagues put forward and confirmed a novel index designed to calculate and identify IR using metrics related to fasting blood glucose (FBG) and triglyceride (TG), known as the TyG index16. Subsequently, Er et al. reported that TyG can be combined with body mass index (BMI) to reliably and effectively evaluate blood glucose, lipid profiles, and visceral adiposity, which offers the advantages of simplicity, robustness, and clinical efficacy17. Therefore, the efficacy and accuracy of IR assessment may improve when the TyG index is integrated with obesity-related measures like as WC, BMI, and WtHR18.

TyG index and the chance of getting CVD are significantly correlated, including ailments like atherosclerosis, coronary artery disease, and stroke19. However, the connection between the TyG index, its derivatives and hypertension, pre-hypertension issues are still not well clear20. Furthermore, gender difference and dietary patterns are significant to hypertension and pre-hypertension, the influence that TyG index and its derivatives combined with different dietary patterns or gender-stratified analyses on hypertension and pre-hypertension are not clear, neither.

Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) is a machine learning (ML) algorithm which is grounded in decision tree ensembles21. It leverages an approach to gradient boosting that was designed by Chen and Guestrin22. This algorithm is widely applied in diverse ML tasks to perform advanced analyses, exhibiting excellent exactness and high Area Under the Curve(AUC) values23,24.

The goal of this cross-sectional research is to look at the associations of TyG index and its derivatives with hypertension and pre-hypertension. For this, we analyzed data from a public health survey carried out in Bengbu City, located in eastern Anhui Province, China. Moreover, we sought potential dose–response relationships through restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis and utilized it to investigate the disparities caused by gender. Logistic regression (LR) and XGBoost model were used to identify the indicators that had an impact on hypertension. We further highlighted the limitations of the TyG indices and proposed potential improvements.

Results

Baseline attributes of participants

This cross-sectional research included 9132 individuals. They were stratified into three groups by BP: normotension (n = 1215), pre-hypertension (n = 5277), and hypertension (n = 2640). Table 1 presents the participants’ demographic characteristics, health-related behaviors, physical examination results, laboratory test results, and metabolic parameters. The mean age increased across the normotension, pre-hypertension, and hypertension groups—62.33 ± 6.08, 62.61 ± 5.98, and 63.64 ± 6.31 years, respectively (P < 0.01). Age was adjusted for gender, marital status, education level, health-related behaviors and physical examination. The proportion of balanced diet, meat-oriented diet and vegetarians-oriented diet in each group were of significant difference (P < 0.01). Furthermore, metabolic parameters such as the TyG index and its derivatives and BMI progressively increased across the normotension, pre-hypertension, and hypertension groups (P < 0.01). Notably, intergroup discrepancy was noted in WC, triglyceride level, FBG level, and weight (P < 0.01).

Performance of the five metabolic parameters in predicting hypertension and pre-hypertension

Table 3 and Fig. 1 presented the ROC curve analysis findings of hypertension and pre-hypertension. The results included cut-off value, sensitivity, specificity, Youden index, and the AUC values. The AUC values for the five metabolic parameters exceeded 0.50, indicating their potential in recognizing hypertension and pre-hypertension. The cut-off value of TyG-WtHR was 4.79 for females and 4.42 for males in hypertension. Fig. 1 shows the AUC curve of hypertension and pre-hypertension in gender stratification. In both conditions, the TyG–WtHR index revealed the optimal AUC values in males and females, its performance was better for hypertension (AUC values, male: 0.612, 95% CI 0.593–0.630; female: 0.631, 95% CI 0.614–0.647) than for pre-hypertension (AUC values, male: 0.574, 95% CI 0.556–0.592; female: 0.573, 95% CI 0.557–0.588). The five metabolic parameters exhibited higher predictive performance for hypertension in female than in male, which is the reverse in pre-hypertension.

Risk factors for hypertension and pre-hypertension

The variables for both hypertension and pre-hypertension are shown in Fig. 2. Utilizing LR analysis, the risk factors were identified, the results revealed a link between the TyG indices and hypertension and pre-hypertension (P < 0.05). A significant dose–response association was observed across all metabolic parameters (P < 0.05). For hypertension, the ORs for the metabolic parameters increased consistently as the quartile became higher. However, this trend was less evident for pre-hypertension, where the OR for the TyG–WC index was lower in Q4 (OR: 1.297; 95% CI 1.085–1.551) than in Q3 (OR: 1.345; 95% CI 1.132–1.599) and that for the TyG–WtHR index was lower in Q4 (OR: 1.124; 95% CI 0.943–1.340) than in Q3 (OR: 1.346; 95% CI 1.130–1.603).

Interactions of metabolic parameters with dietary habits

As indicated in Fig. 5, the prevalence of hypertension was higher in the participants whose metabolic parameters exceeded corresponding median values. This phenomenon was most pronounced in the participants with a balanced dietary and those with a meat-based dietary, with the exception of a subgroup of participants with elevated TyG values who consumed a meat-based dietary, among whom the prevalence of hypertension was 1.4%. A comparison of the ORs indicated that the meat-oriented group faced optimal likelihood of hypertension, particularly those with high TyG–WtHR values, among whom the prevalence of hypertension was 31.5% (OR: 3.759; 95% CI 2.469–5.723).

Interaction effects of metabolic parameters and dietary habits on prevalence of hypertension.

Predictive performance of XGBoost model

Figure 6 presents the ROC curves for the XGBoost and LR models. Data obtained from a confusion matrix indicate that the XGBoost model achieved a predictive accuracy of 76.631%. LR models for the TyG–WtHR index, obtained the highest AUC values of 0.617. By contrast, the XGBoost model attained AUC value of 0.662. Hence, the XGBoost model behaved better than the LR model.

Discussion

We investigated the associations of the TyG index and its derivatives with hypertension and pre-hypertension in middle-aged and elder adults in Bengbu and evaluated the identification ability of these 5 indices. The BMI of the population is in the normal range, which is carried out on the premise of excluding the huge health risks caused by obesity itself. Our findings indicate that TyG derivatives, particularly the TyG–WtHR index, exhibit superior distinguish power for hypertension than traditional obesity markers, such as BMI. Sex-stratified analyses utilizing ROC analysis revealed that in hypertension, the TyG index and its derivatives exhibited a higher predictive performance in women than in men; however, a reverse condition was observed in pre-hypertension. The associations between the five metabolic parameters and two conditions were investigated through RCS and LR analyses. The findings show dose–response relationships between the metabolic parameters and the likelihood of pre-hypertension and hypertension that are specific to gender. To provide a more comprehensive direction for clinical decision-making for the residents of Anhui province, we utilized the interaction analysis to explore the potential association between the TyG indices combined with dietary patterns and hypertension, which revealed that individuals with higher TyG combination indices values who consumed a meat-based diet may be related to a higher risk of hypertension. Furthermore, we compared the capacity of XGBoost machine model and LR model in determining TyG and its variants in predicting hypertension. The findings suggested that the XGBoost model was slightly surpassed the LR model.

Numerous studies have associated excess visceral adiposity with metabolic irregularities that increase the risks of IR and cardiometabolic conditions, including hypertension26. The TyG index, utilized in conjunction with obesity markers such as WC, BMI, and WtHR, superior identifies IR, given that obesity is a key driver of IR27. Although our research revealed that the TyG index and its derivatives were independently connected with both hypertension and pre-hypertension, there is still much to learn about the processes relating IR to hypertension. IR may lead to hyperinsulinemia, which enhances the dynamics of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system28,29, increasing renal sodium retention. This can therefore indirectly improve sodium and water retention, in addition to raising blood vessels activity through angiotensin II, leading to HTN30,31. Furthermore, IR may activate the sympathetic nervous system, promoting the release of emission such as adrenaline and noradrenaline32,33, which might lead to endothelial dysfunction, swelling of smooth muscle cells in the blood vessels, and increased peripheral arterial resistance, all of which add to the onset of hypertension34,35.

A cohort study (December 2024) reported that the TyG–WtHR index outperformed other indicators in predicting all-cause mortality. The predictive performance was most pronounced among individuals in the highest TyG–WtHR quartile36. These findings are aligned with ours. In the present study, multivariate LR models, covariate-adjusted forest plots, and sex-stratified ROC analysis indicated that higher TyG–WtHR quartile group revealed an elevated likelihood of hypertension and that the TyG–WtHR index exhibited better predictive power than the other derivatives of the TyG index. Additionally, XGBoost model identified TyG–WtHR and TyG-WC as 2 factors that contributed the most.

Based on the results of our study, the RCS model with gender stratification indicates that female exhibit more non-linear dose–response relationships. This gender difference can be considered from two factors: First, female have more abdominal subcutaneous fat, while men have more visceral fat, which may be the basis of aging of the human cardiovascular system and indirectly affect changes in BP37. Second, female estrogen can improve insulin sensitivity and inhibit liver gluconeogenesis, resulting in this gender difference38.

This study observed an unexpected trend for pre-hypertension: a paradoxical reduction in pre-hypertension was noted in high quartiles of the TyG–WtHR and TyG-WC index. Having examining a number of relevant articles, no research has been found to date that explores the connection between TyG-WtHR, TyG-WC and pre-hypertension. The paradoxical phenomenon we observed may be attributable to many effects, we proposed that individuals with high TyG-WtHR and TyG-WC in the pre-hypertension group might represent a survivor cohort. Those most susceptible to its adverse effects may have already progressed to full hypertension or developed cardiovascular complications, thus being selectively excluded from the pre-hypertension group.

Middle-aged and elder individuals tend to have higher baseline HDL-C levels and greater exposure to medical interventions than younger individuals. Moreover, age-related biological factors may conceal the connections of the TyG index and pre-hypertension39. Individuals receiving a diagnosis of pre-hypertension may adopt healthier lifestyles by, for example, reducing their salt intake and engaging in regular exercise. These adjustments may improve BP control40. CDKL1 may mediate the complex associations of the TyG indices with IR-induced hyperglycemia, thereby elevating BP41.

The XGBoost model combines numerous weak learners to iteratively enhance predictive accuracy. It incorporates regularization and parallel processing methods to improve performance and computational efficiency22. Because of its high accuracy, scalability, and flexibility, XGBoost is widely adopted in medical prediction tasks42,43.

In January 2025, Zheng et al. developed 6 ML models for forecasting the threat of mortality in sufferers with hypertension. The models incorporate a range of clinical indicators. Among the various models, XGBoost exhibited the highest predictive accuracy regarding the danger risk in patients with HTN (all-cause mortality rate: AUC = 0.826; cardiovascular disease mortality rate: AUC = 0.769)20. Building on their approach, we used both LR and XGBoost models to recognize key risk factors influencing hypertension and pre-hypertension in Bengbu middle-aged and elder residents. The results are visualized using violin plots and variable importance histograms. SHAP value highlighted the TyG–WC and TyG–WtHR as the two most influential variables associated with hypertension. Although the AUC value of the model is 0.662, which is relatively low, it provides new methods for improving the predictive model’s ability. The low AUC value may be attributed to several theoretical factors related to the model’s learning dynamics and data properties. For example, the model’s predictive performance might have been limited by inherent structural deficiencies in the feature space, where predictors lack sufficient nonlinear interaction with the outcome variable to form distinct decision boundaries44. Furthermore, the model’s overly stringent regularization parameters might have introduced a bias-variance trade-off, which reduced model sensitivity, excluding potentially informative features while preventing overfitting45.

At present, there are relatively few prediction tools for hypertension and prehypertension, resulting in people not knowing the risks they develop, it will result in inefficient management and cannot be extended to the whole population46. In contrast, TyG and its derivative indicators are not only easy to obtain from the calculation data, but also can be widely applied in various places. This study found that in Bengbu City, as the risk factors, the above indicators were different between the residents with hypertension and pre-hypertension, especially TyG-WtHR and TyG-WC, and we still observed significant different relationships for different genders. This can provide a basis for the facilitate identification and prediction of hypertension and pre-hypertension in the future. In terms of cost-effectiveness, the cost of each TyG test was about $1.64, while the cost of Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR), a classic indicator of insulin resistance, was $4.26, which reduced the cost of TyG by 61.5%47. Regarding nutrition guidance, the exploratory study on the interaction between TyG indices and dietary pattern in hypertension in this text provides a feasibility alternative, it may provide a basis for the prevention and early control of hypertension.

This study is innovative in using gender stratified analysis, RCS analysis, the interaction between indicators and dietary patterns, as well as LR and XGBoost models to explore the effects of TyG index and its derived indicators on hypertension and pre-hypertension in the population of Bengbu. Upon examining the research outcomes, it is important to recognize several limitations. First, Bengbu residents do not fully represent the entire demographic of Anhui Province, may limit the generalizability of findings to other populations with different ethnic, dietary, or lifestyle characteristics. Multi-center studies across diverse populations would strengthen the external validity. Second, although trained interviewers used standardized methods to administer the survey questionnaires, the data were self-reported, which might have introduced errors and biases. Third, due to the lack of fasting insulin data, it was not possible to use HOMA-IR or HEGC methods to evaluate IR in relation to hypertension scores, which resulted in the inability to compare TyG index and its derivatives with such directly measured indicators, reducing the comprehensive comparison of the study. Fourth, the broad classification of FFQ of foods precluded the precise identification of diverse and specific dietary patterns, which reduced the specificity of later nutritional guidance, but the significant interaction observed with high TyG indices nonetheless provides a strong, pragmatic public health message. It underscores that a dietary pattern characterized by high meat consumption—irrespective of the specific type—may be particularly detrimental for BP in individuals with underlying insulin resistance. Fifth, a key finding of our study is that, despite showing a significant association and the AUC values of each indicator being statistically significant, the TyG-related indices (such as TyG-WC and TyG-WtHR) demonstrated only modest predictive accuracy (AUC ~ 0.66). This clearly indicates that they cannot satisfactorily classify individuals into hypertension or prehypertension groups by themselves, which limits their immediate standalone clinical diagnostic utility. In this study, we have centered our interpretation on those with the largest effect sizes (e.g., OR > 1.3 or < 0.8) that are most likely to be clinically meaningful, as suggested by the SHAP value rankings. Finally, given the cross-sectional design of our study, the reported AUC and cut-off values reflect the diagnostic or discriminative ability of TyG indices for prevalent hypertension/pre-hypertension, rather than longitudinal prediction of future risk. The associations observed herein require validation in longitudinal or prospective cohort studies to determine their temporal sequence and their predictive performance for hypertension and pre-hypertension.

Conclusion

This research indicates that the TyG index and its derivatives were independently associated with hypertension and pre-hypertension, and provided new perspectives for future clinical work and nutrition guidance. These findings should be integrated with previous studies to make targeted interventions, such as personalized lifestyle programs for high-risk individuals identified by TyG-WC or TyG-WtHR and different diet patterns, helping decelerate the progression of hypertension-related diseases among Bengbu residents. In future research, utilizing more longitudinal and omics data on the research of the abnormal phenomenon that the OR of pre-hypertension reduced in high TyG-WtHR and TyG-WC quartiles and validating the indices’ power through multi-center cohorts across China would be a direction.

Methods

Study population

The collection of the data was part of the Cardiovascular and Health Promotion Study (COCHPS2019), a cross-sectional investigation conducted in a community situated in the Longzihu District of Bengbu City, Anhui Province, China, from September to December 201948. Three-phase stratified cluster sampling was performed: communities were first categorized based on socioeconomic quartile49. Subsequently, residential clusters were randomly selected. Finally, eligible households were enlisted. All participants contributed comprehensive data across four key dimensions.

First, in-person interviews were conducted using a validated questionnaire, which exhibited satisfactory internal consistency (Cronbach’s α: from 0.7 to 0.8). Second, a 24-h dietary recall was administered using standardized food models to ensure accurate reporting of dietary intake. Third, various anthropometric parameters were measured, including height (cm), weight (kg), WC (cm), systolic BP (mmHg), and diastolic BP (mmHg)50. Finally, laboratory tests were conducted to evaluate several health-related biomarkers, such as triglycerides, which can point to the blood’s fat content, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) known for its role in heart health, fasting blood glucose is important for understanding glucose metabolism, and total cholesterol, which provides an overall measure of cholesterol in the bloodstream.

A cohort of 10,258 individuals was initially recruited. Of them, 9132 met the inclusion prerequisites. The prerequisite for exclusion were well-defined to uphold the study’s integrity. Specifically, the following individuals were excluded: those aged < 45 years (n = 274), those with missing or invalid sleep data (n = 28), those with incomplete dietary records (n = 63), and individuals who had missing or abnormal demographic data regarding age, sex, marital status, and education (n = 761). Fig. 8 presents a flowchart depicting participant selection. Before beginning the trial, each participant gave written informed consent. This research endeavor was duly sanctioned by the Ethics Committee of Bengbu Medical University. The surgical procedures were all executed in strict adherence to the ethical standards stipulated in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participant selection process.

Data collection

Trained researchers conducted in-person interviews with the participants by using revised and standardized questionnaires51. The questionnaire included the following components: demographic characteristics, lifestyle habits, and medical history. The following demographic data were collected: name, sex, age, education level (nil, elementary school, junior high school, or high school or above), and marital status (unmarried, married, or separated)49. The following lifestyle habits were assessed: smoking habits (never-smoker, current, or former-smoker), alcohol consumption (nondrinker, occasional, regular), physical activity (regular was defined as engaging in such activities ≥ 5 days per week; occasional as 1–4 days per week; and none as no regular moderate-to-vigorous physical activity), sleep duration (short, standard, or long), and dietary habits52. Dietary preferences were assessed on the basis of the diversity of food groups ingested within the last 24 h53,54. The “meat” category was defined to include red meat (e.g., pork, beef, lamb) and poultry, the “Vegetarian” category was defined to include vegetable, fruit and grain. The following medical information was obtained: presence of conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, or hyperlipidemia and use of medications.

Physical examination

Measurements were conducted by trained evaluators in accordance with established protocols. Participants were weighed and gauged for height using a weighing scale and a standard height gauge, respectively, with the attendees were attired in lightweight clothing and were barefoot. The point midway from the lowest rib to the iliac crest was where WC was measured by using a nonelastic measuring tape with consistent tension; two readings were taken and averaged. BP was measured after a 10-min rest by using an Omron HBP-1300 monitor (Omron Healthcare, Hoffman Estates, IL, USA), three consecutive readings were taken and averaged.

Laboratory tests

Triglyceride level was measured using an enzymatic colorimetry assay, whereas HDL-C level was measured using a direct elimination method. FBG level was determined using the hexokinase method. After a fasting period of ≥ 8 h, biochemical analyses and lipid profiling were performed using a biochemical test with full automation (Roche Modular P800; Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). All blood samples were collected in the morning after an overnight fast at designated local clinics or community health centers by trained nurses. All procedures adhered to stringent laboratory quality control standards.

Definitions and calculations

Definitions

Hypertension was defined in accordance with the following criteria: a systolic BP of ≥ 140 mmHg and/or a diastolic BP of ≥ 90 mmHg55; or current antihypertensive drug is to be used56,57. Pre-hypertension is characterized by a systolic BP of 120–139 mmHg or a diastolic BP of 80–89 mmHg58. The TyG index measures IR by evaluating FBG and triglyceride levels59,60. This index has been amalgamated with various obesity markers to develop the TyG–WC, TyG–BMI, and TyG–WtHR indicators35,61. When the fasting plasma glucose level was more than 7.0 mmol/L, diabetes was diagnosed62. The collection of dietary intake data was accomplished by means of a food frequency questionnaire (FFQ)63. As mentioned, dietary was classified as balanced, meat-based, or vegetarian on the basis of the quality, quantity, variety, and habitual frequency of food and beverage intake64. Information regarding the length of sleep was collected using a self-reported survey, which inquired about the specific time intervals during which the participants typically slept65. On the basis of their responses, the involved were categorized as short (< 6 h per day), normal (6–8 h per day), or long (> 8 h per day)35,66. On the basis of their physical activity habits, two groups were formed from the participants: those engaging in physical activity and those not engaging in it67.

Calculations

The following calculations were employed to compute the TyG, TyG-WC, TyG-WtHR and TyG-BMI indices and WtHR, BMI68:

TyG index = ln[triglyceride level (mg/dL) × glucose level (mg/dL)/2]

TyG − WC index = TyG × WC (cm)

TyG − WtHR index = TyG × WtHR

TyG − BMI index = TyG × BMI (kg/m2)69

WtHR = WC/height

BMI = body mass (kg)/height2 (m2)70

Statistical analysis

The data entry process was executed using EpiData (Odense, Denmark’s EpiData Association, version 3.1), while the subsequent data analysis was in conjunction by the cooperation of SPSS (version 26.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA) and R software (version 4.4.3). To figure out the regularity distribution, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used for the data. The five metabolic parameters analyzed in the research—the TyG indices and BMI—were separated into four quartiles (Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4). Measurement data meeting the criteria for an expression for a normal distribution would be mean ± standard deviation ( ± s), in instances where the data are non-normal, they are to be represented graphically as the intermediate values of Interquartile range (IQR). Categorical data were stated in quantitative form (%) values. Direct standardization method was used to adjust the age of Diet patterns, Sleep duration, Physical habits, Drinking and Smoking data. Take the age distribution of the total data population (n = 9132) as a reference to ensure that the confounding effect of age is eliminated. In the process of comparing continuous variables, the Kruskal–Wallis test was utilized for data that did not distribution normally and one-way analysis of covariance for data that was. The chi-square test was leveraged to make a comparison within categorical variables. Logistic regression (LR) and restricted cubic spline analyses were accustomed to recognize potential dose–response relationships between the metabolic parameters and the risks of hypertension and pre-hypertension. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve was used to analyze the sensitivity, specificity and Youden index difference of each index for hypertension and prehypertension. The AUC values were compared to evaluate the predictive effect of different indicators on hypertension and prehypertension risk. AUCs were tested and analyzed by Z test. The standard defined for assessing statistical significance was established as a P-value of below 0.05, indicating that any outcomes falling below this threshold would be considered statistically meaningful in the context of the study. The nonlinear correlations among metabolic parameters and hypertension or pre-hypertension were examined using restricted cubic spline analysis. Five knots were positioned at the 5th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 95th percentiles71,72. Basis functions were generated using the rms package in R, and data were processed through LR analysis. Statistical models were modified to account for age, gender, marital status, Education level, smoking, drinking, dietary patterns and habit of exercise. Moreover, restricted cubic spline functions were stratified by gender, and both linear and nonlinear P values were calculated for comparative evaluation. The likelihood ratio test (LRT) was implemented to ascertain the presence of nonlinearity.

XGBoost is a distributed gradient-boosting algorithm that is utilized for developing efficient, flexible, and portable ML models73. Compared with the traditional statistical methods of SPSS or R statistical package, XGBoost has two main advantages. It can not only reveal a non-linear, threshold-based relationship between indicators and hypertension risk that was not apparent in the initial linear models, but, most importantly, the importance ranking of each variable calculated based on the SHAP value quantifies the comprehensive contribution of each variable in the overall prediction model, which has a practical significance. The performance of the classification model was evaluated on the testing set using a confusion matrix, which tabulates the counts of True Positives (TP), True Negatives (TN), False Positives (FP), and False Negatives (FN). Key metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score, were then derived from this matrix to comprehensively assess the model’s classification efficacy. In the present study, the XGBoost model was implemented in R (version 4.4.3). The data set was separated into testing and training sets using a 3:7 ratio74. The total dataset is 9132, with 6393 data points in the training set and 2739 data points in the testing set. A total of 150 trees were used, and the learning rate was set at 0.1 to ensure convergence stability. Each tree’s maximum depth was limited to 4 to lessen the possibility of overfitting, while a total of 5 cross-validation folds were applied to evaluate the effectiveness of each set of parameters.

After parameter optimization, predictive outputs were generated, and the AUC value and 95% confidence interval (95% CI) were computed applying the pROC and ROCit packages. A corresponding ROC curve was generated. Finally, SHapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) values were calculated using the TreeSHAP algorithm, and violin plots and variable importance histograms were generated to assess variable relevance.
