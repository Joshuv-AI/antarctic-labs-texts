Nutritional enrichment of eggs through essential oil–supplemented laying hen diets and associations with intestinal microbiota
Authors: Tulay Elal Mus, Tulay Ozcan, Tugçe Necla Selvi, Gokce Keser, Hakan Ustuner, Onder Canbolat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the effects of oregano essential oil alone and in combination with cinnamon and peppermint essential oils on intestinal microbiota, the sensory and textural properties of cooked egg’s and fatty acid profile. Intestinal microbiota/fatty acid profile correlations and health index were evaluated. Lohmann LSL-LITE strain laying hens were randomly divided into four groups and fed for 28 days: a control group receiving a basal diet (C); a group supplemented with 250 mg/kg oregano essential oil (O), a group receiving 125 mg/kg oregano plus 125 mg/kg cinnamon essential oils (O + C), and a group receiving 125 mg/kg oregano plus 125 mg/kg peppermint essential oils (O + P). Sensory evaluation showed that the distinctive flavors and aromas of oregano, cinnamon, and peppermint essential oils were not detected. Essential oil supplemented diet did not affect the eggs’ hardness, adhesiveness, cohesiveness, and resilience properties. Shear force was found to be significantly different in group O. Assessment of nutritional indices showed that high Linoleic Acid/α-Linolenic Acid ratio has given O and O + P groups eggs potential functional properties. While the coliforms, Enterobacteriaceae, and Enterococcus spp. in the essential oil supplemented groups were reduced, and Lactobacillus spp. increased. Also, essential oil feed achieved a correlation between lactic acid bacteria and Linoleic Acid/α-Linolenic Acid, omega6/omega3 ratio. Furthermore, behenic, palmitoleic, and palmitic fatty acids positively correlated with intestinal Lactobacillus spp. As a result, laying hens feed essential oil supplementation results in potential functional eggs that offer better nutritional indices for the consumer health, which were obtained without affecting the egg’s characteristics.Keywords: Egg, Essential oil, Gut microbiota, Oregano, Laying hen

Introduction

The poultry industry is critically important; it provides sources of animal protein such as eggs and meat. The intestinal microbiota is among the practical factors in the productivity and health of laying hens (Choi & Kim, 2023). The intestinal microbiota is actively involved in the health system, produces antimicrobial products, and prevents colonization of pathogens in the intestine, producing micronutrients, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), and amino acids (Khan et al., 2020). Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) are the most important members of the probiotic microorganism family that provide all these positive effects and are colonized in the intestines of poultry (Sirisopapong et al., 2023). Probiotic microorganisms in this group carry out fermentation reactions in the colon and produce metabolites such as acetate, butyrate, and propionate. These metabolites are absorbed from the intestine and exchanged with the blood, ensuring positive effects on health (Liu et al., 2021). Acetate protects against colibacillosis, which is frequently seen in poultry. The chicken that received acetate-supplemented diet exhibited reduced lung inflammatory response and the air-blood permeability induced by colibacillosis (Peng et al., 2021). Propionate, which supresses fat accumulation and decreases triglyceride content in abdominal fat tissue by reducing transcription of various lipogenic genes in liver and abdominal fat tissues (Li et al., 2021). The amount of butyrate synthesized by probiotics can be increased with the essential oils in the diet, thus improving intestinal health by providing energy to the colon mucosa (Tiihonen et al., 2010). These beneficial metabolites produced by intestinal probiotic microorganisms affects egg quality in laying hens (Xu et al., 2023).

Essential oils have become alternative natural products to antibiotics for health advancement in poultry. Oregano oil supplementation in laying hens’ diet has many favorable effects such as promoting longevity, which is essential for breeders (Johnson et al., 2025) and egg quality which is remarkable for consumers (Xu et al., 2024). The structural characteristics of eggs provide objective, quantifiable, exact, and reproducible instrumental texture quality (Liu et al., 2022) and are important in consumers’ egg purchasing preferences (Berkhoff et al., 2020). While the effects of essential oils on egg quality (weight, width/length, albumen height, shell characteristics, etc.) have been demonstrated in detail by previous researchers (Ghanima et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022; Orzuna-Orzuna & Lara-Bueno, 2023; Xu et al., 2023), possible changes in the sensory, textural properties and health-promoting potential of these eggs which are important to consumers, have not been investigated. The current study aimed to investigate the potential effects of dietary oregano essential oil supplementation and its combined use with cinnamon and peppermint essential oils on the feces microbiota, egg’s sensory, textural properties, and fatty acid profile. The possible association between the intestinal microbiota of laying hens and egg fatty acid profile was evaluated. The fatty acid-based nutritional indices of eggs were calculated to determine their effects on egg consumers’ diet-related diseases.

Materials and methods

Animals, management, treatments

The research was conducted at the Agricultural Application and Research Centre of Bursa Uludag University (Bursa, Turkiye). A total of 360 hens were randomly selected from a 3000-bird flock. Randomization was performed using initial body weight as a blocking factor. Birds were weighed individually at 57 weeks of age and allocated to treatment groups to ensure similar mean body weight and distribution. The age range of the flock (37–57 week prior to trial start) represented normal flock variation; however, all birds enrolled in the study were exactly 57 weeks of age at allocation. The mean body weight on the initial day of the study, at 57 weeks of age, was 1.58 ± 0.15 kg for the C group, 1.55 ± 0.16 kg for the O group, 1.57 ± 0.16 kg for the O + C group, and 1.52 ± 0.14 kg for the O + P group. Laying hens were raised in a traditional cage system and they were randomly allocated to the experimental cages according to their initial body weights. The study included four treatment groups of 90 birds each: a control group without essential oils (C), a group supplemented with 250 mg/kg oregano essential oil (O; W28281-2, Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA), a group supplemented with 125 mg/kg oregano and 125 mg/kg cinnamon essential oils (O + C; W229202, Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA), and a group supplemented with 125 mg/kg oregano and 125 mg/kg peppermint essential oils (O + P; W28482-3, Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA). The essential oils were pulverized onto the feed at the specified doses. The prepared experimental feeds were stored in special bags and provided to the laying hens daily throughout the study. The diet provided to animals consisted of 42% corn kernels, 26% wheat kernels, 14% soybean meal, 2.5% sunflower meal, 3% vegetable oil, 0.02% sodium chloride, 9.1% limestone, 2.35% di-calcium phosphate, dl-methionine 0.16%, l-lysine 0.19%, and premix 0.5%. The composition of the diet consumed was designed to contain 2820 kcal/kg metabolic energy, 16.8% crude protein, 4.8% crude fat, 8.2% crude ash, 41% starch, 0.72% phosphorus, and 3.6% calcium. The diet and water were supplied for ad libitum. All egg samples were collected at the end of the 28th day of feeding. The obtained egg samples were brought to the laboratory for analysis and stored in the fridge for 24 h.

Analysis of feces microbiota

In this research, each group of laying hens was divided into three cages containing 30 hens, and five fresh feces samples were collected from different locations of each cage on the 1 st, 14th, and 28th days using sterile stool containers and tools. The samples were immediately cooled and stored at −80 °C for bacteriological analysis until the last sample was obtained. 10 g of feces sample was homogenized in 90 mL Maximum Recovery Diluent (MRD) (112535, Merck, Germany) using a Stomacher Lab Blender (Model 400, Seward, UK) for at least two minutes. Serial decimal dilutions were prepared with MRD. Violet Red Bile agar (101406, Merck, Germany) and Violet Red Bile Glucose agar (70189, Merck, Germany) were used to isolate Coliforms and Enterobacteriaceae, respectively, with overnight (20–24 h) incubation at 35 ± 1 °C. Slanetz and Bartley medium (105262, Merck, Germany) was used for Enterococcus spp., and petri dishes were incubated at 35 ± 1 °C for 48 h. Lactobacillus spp. was grown on de Man Rogosa Sharpe agar (110660, Merck, Germany) under an anaerobic atmosphere at 35 ± 1 °C for 72 h. (Elal Mus & Ak Sonat, 2015) Lactococcus spp. were enumerated on M17 Agar (115108, Merck, Germany) at 30 ± 1 °C for 48 h without aeration (Kaczmarek et al., 2021). The microbiological analysis was repeated for three times for each sample.

Fatty acid analysis of the egg yolk

The extraction of lipids in the egg yolk samples was performed using Bligh and Dyer’s (1959) technique. The mixture of 20 mL methanol: chloroform (1:2, v/v; Merck) was used. The extraction was performed in a rotary vacuum evaporator (Laborota 4001, Germany). The fatty acid methyl esters were obtained by transesterification with 100 µL oil, and 1.5 mol/L HCl dissolved in methanol was mixed for 15 min. Oil was methylated at 80 °C for two hours, and then 1 mL hexane was added to obtain the methyl esters. The fatty acid account was assigned by gas chromatography (Agilent GC, USA) accoutred with a mass spectrometry detector (QP5050, Japan). An HP 88 column (100 × 0.250 × 0.20 μm) (Agilent GC, USA) and a GC–MS Real-Time Analysis Software (Shimadzu, Japan) system were activated to identify the fatty acids in egg yolk samples (Perveen et al., 2021). Fatty acid-based nutritional indices were determined by the description of Chen and Liu (2020) using fatty acid composition data (%).

Texture profile analysis of the cooked eggs

The cooked egg texture profile was measured using a TA.XT plus Texture Analyzer with a cylindrical probe with a 36-mm diameter (Stable Micro Systems, UK). Texture profile analysis was carried out with a trigger force of 0.05 N, two-cycle deformation of 40%, and a 30 mm/min speed. The resulting force-time curves determined resilience, cohesiveness, hardness, and adhesiveness (Dong et al., 2021). The whole egg sample without a shell was placed on a Zwick/Roell (Instron, USA) device attached by the Warner-Bratzler blade for the shear force (N) determination (Ekiz et al., 2012).

Sensory analyses of the cooked eggs

A sensory panel consisting of 10 semi-trained assessors was used in this study. Panelists received a brief orientation session covering scale use, attribute definitions, and sample evaluation procedures to ensure consistent basic assessment. (ISO 8586:2012). The results were considered acceptable for exploratory analysis. Each assessor provided written informed consent after the experimental specifications were explained. Furthermore, the assessors were informed about the safety of the samples and participants’ rights before the sensory analysis commenced. The panelists evaluated the eggs according to different criteria such as color, odor, hardness, flavor, thyme, cinnamon, mint aroma, aftertaste, and general acceptability of the product in the given forms. (According to the hedonic scale, 9- Extremely liked, 1- Strongly disliked) (Baxter et al., 2024).

Statistical analysis

One-way analysis of variance (One-Way ANOVA) was performed to evaluate differences among groups. When significant differences were detected, pairwise comparisons were conducted using Fisher’s Least Significant Difference (LSD) post-hoc test (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01). All statistical analyses were carried out using MINITAB 17 (Statistical Software). The Principal Component Analysis and correlation were identified using OriginPro 2024b (OriginLab Corporation, USA).

Results and discussion

Feces microbiota

The relationship between intestinal microbiota and the feed utilization ratio of laying hens has been a popular topic in recent years. Intestinal microbiota-modulation has become an alternative to antimicrobials to improve poultry health and increase production efficiency (Yang, 2019). The study investigated the effect of oregano essential oil alone and in combination with cinnamon and peppermint essential oils on intestinal microbiota and the sensory and textural properties of cooked eggs, and their fatty acid profile. The effects of O, O + C, and O + P essential oils added to the diet on the coliforms, Enterobacteriaceae, Enterococci, Lactobacillus spp., and Lactococcus spp. are summarized in Table 1. The coliforms and Enterobacteriaceae counts decreased (p < 0.05) in experimental groups on the 14th and 28th days. The coliforms decreased on the 14th (p > 0.05) and increased on the 28th (p < 0.05) days, and Enterobacteriaceae decreased on both sampling days (p < 0.05) in the C group. A difference (p < 0.05) was detected in the enterococci in the O and O + C groups on the 14th and 28th days and in the O + P group on the 28th day. The essential oil feed supplementation did not affect Lactococci count, but Lactobacillus spp. increased in the O, O + C and O + P groups (p < 0.05). SCFAs, which are also produced by lactobacilli, can inhibit the growth of pathogenic bacteria by decreasing intestinal pH. Particularly, butyrate suppresses the expression of nitric oxide synthase, which encodes inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), in intestine cells through PPAR-γ. Thus, it leads to a significant decrease in iNOS and nitrate. Then, it prevents the growth of Enterobacteriaceae to continue general intestinal homeostasis (Liu et al., 2021). Microbiological analyses indicated that the use of oregano essential oil single or in combination had a positive effect on the Lactobacillus spp. population and an inhibitory effect on the coliform and Enterobacteriaceae populations. The combined dietary use of encapsulated oregano, cinnamon, and clove essential oils has been shown to decrease Enterobacteriaceae and increase Lactobacillus spp. and Bifidobacterium spp. in broiler chicken (Meligy et al., 2023). Similarly, Abouelezz et al. (2019) reported decreased coliform and Enterobacteriaceae counts due to oregano and enviva essential oil supplementation on the duck diet.

The weights of the egg samples varied between 57.99 and 70.15. The highest weight was determined in sample O (Table 2). According to the egg shape index (SI) classification, SI < 72 for sharp eggs, SI = 72–76 for standard eggs, and SI > 76 for round eggs (Duman et al., 2016). When the SI values ​​​​in egg samples were examined, sample O + C was in the sharp egg (67.8%) category, while the other samples were classified as standard eggs. The shell surface values ​​were determined between 71.07 and 80.62. The highest shell surface values ​​were determined in the O and O + C groups. While the Haugh Unit (HU) values ​​of the eggs ranged from 61.56 to 79.70 in C and experimental groups (p < 0.05), the highest value was determined in C and followed by O + P group eggs. It was determined that the HU values ​​of eggs produced in various rearing systems were 66.9–71.4 and that higher values ​​were acquired in young layer flocks than in old flocks (Oke et al., 2014). The egg weight increased in O and O + C groups eggs while HU value was determined to be lower than C. Otherwise O + P group eggs have the lowest weight but HU value was high among the experimental groups. Albumen quality was negatively affected in essential oil supplemented groups laying hens eggs (p < 0.05).

The color of the egg yolk is linked to level of xanthophylls in poultry feed and antioxidant activity of xanthophyll and carotene (Gül et al., 2012). Yellow color, considered as a health and quality component in eggs, is another important sensory property consumer’s evaluate (Zurak et al., 2022). In raw eggs, DSM yolk color scale was 12 in C and O + C samples and it was 11 in O and O + P samples (Table 1), but no statistical significance was found (p < 0.05). There are consistent results that oregano essential oil supplementation did not generate a significant difference in yolk color value (Migliorini et al., 2019; Feng et al., 2021).

Fatty acids of egg yolk

The fatty acid characterization results of egg samples obtained from hens fed with diets including different essential oils are given in Table 3. Differences between egg samples regarding short and medium-chain fatty acid content were insignificant (p > 0.05). Differences were found between samples in terms of long-chain fatty acids such as myristic, palmitoleic, stearic, oleic, α-linolenic acid, arachidic, eicosenoic, eicosadienoic, arachidonic acid and very long chain fatty acids such as behenic, docosahexaenoic, and nervonic acid. Palmitoleic and behenic acid levels were determined to be higher in eggs produced from hens fed with essential oil. Depending on the classification of fatty acids detected in egg samples according to the double bonds in their chemical structures, including essential oils in the chickens’ diets, did not cause differences in egg samples’ saturated fatty acid content. In contrast, the content of monounsaturated fatty acids was higher in the O + C sample, and the amount of polyunsaturated fatty acids was higher in the control (C) sample than in other samples. Similar to our current study, in the survey conducted by Filipiak-Florkiewicz et al. (2024), they reported that there was no significant change in the saturated fatty acid levels of eggs of chickens fed with pomegranate seed oil. Still, there were minor changes in the amounts of myristic acid, palmitoleic acid, and stearic acid. They also found that there was no significant change in monounsaturated fatty acids.

The eggs’ fatty acid-based nutritional indices values ​​are calculated and given in Table 4. In general, the PUFA/SFA rate is used to evaluate the effect of dietary fats on cardiovascular diseases (Chen & Liu, 2020). A high PUFA/SFA rate is an indicator of healthy fat consumption. Reducing the amount of saturated fat and increasing the quantity of unsaturated fatty acids in diets reduces cardiovascular disease risk factors in individuals (Froyen & Burns-Whitmore, 2020). It is reported that the amounts of saturated fatty acids and monounsaturated fatty acids in chicken eggs are almost not affected by the lipids included in the feed, similar to our study, but it causes changes in polyunsaturated fatty acids (Gladkowski et al., 2014; Bondoc et al., 2023). This work determined that the PUFA/SFA ratio in eggs obtained from chickens given different oil supplements did not change at a high level. The highest value in the MUFA/SFA ratio was obtained in the O + C sample. The active role of ω−9 MUFAs, including oleic acid, in preventing and treating various chronic diseases such as cardiovascular diseases, neurodegenerative diseases, cancer, and obesity has been proven (Wang et al., 2024). LA/ALA ratio is an index considered in new-born and infant nutrition. Infant formulas LA content varied from 8.5% to 26.9% of total fat, and the LA/ALA ratio was at a minimum of 5.4% and 52.5% worldwide. (Einerhand et al., 2023). A high ratio of LA/ALA leads to a higher transformation of LA to other n-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids. In contrast, a low ratio tends to a greater conversion of ALA into other n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (Klevebro et al., 2020). In our study, LA/ALA ratio varied between 44.36 and 54.06. It was found that the highest value belonged to the O sample. LA/ALA ratios were determined to be 55.89, 90.59, 26.88, and 21.17 in table eggs from four different farms. Laying hens’ feed composition impacts the lipid index quality of the eggs (Kralik et al., 2025). The highest ɷ6/ɷ3 ratio, which evaluated the effect of polyunsaturated fatty acids and inflammatory diseases, was determined to belong to the O sample. The LA/ALA and ɷ6/ɷ3 ratios detected in the eggs of laying hens fed with essential oils positively correlate with the number of lactic acid bacteria in the fecal microbiota. It was also determined that these bacteria have a positive relationship with behenic, palmitoleic, and palmitic fatty acids. Following the purpose of this work, it was revealed that the diet’s composition causes differences within the fecal microbiota and fatty acids. However, further studies are needed to reveal the biological and metabolic basis of these changes.

Sensory characteristics of cooked egg samples

The sensory panel was conducted by presenting four groups of hard-cooked egg samples (including the control sample) to untrained panelists and asking them to evaluate them in terms of color, hardness-softness, taste-flavor, thyme aroma, cinnamon aroma, mint aroma, mouthfeel, and general acceptability parameters. The results of the evaluations are given in Fig. 2. The results are presented in a PCA graph and explain 85.2% of the total variance. Consumer feedback determined that the tastes and aromas specific to essential oils were not felt in the eggs, and the samples were found to be similar to the control sample. Hard-boiled cooking for ten minutes may cause loss of volatility of essential oils.

PCA plot of sensorial properties of cooked egg samples

One of the main limitations of this study is the small size of the panel (n = 10). However, this group received key training on the subject before the assessment. Future studies could be conducted with larger groups for comparative sensory evaluations. Feed intake and body weight changes were not monitored throughout the 28-day feeding period. Although this does not affect the microbiota–fatty acid correlation results, next studies should integrate performance parameters to provide a more comprehensive evaluation. Furthermore, the human health benefits of potential functional eggs were not confirmed by consumer clinical tests.

Conclusion

As a result, oregano essential oil can be used single or in combination with other essential oils in the diet of laying hens, and it has been shown to modulate the feces microbiota and increase egg weight positively. It has been shown that dietary essential oil supplementation intensifies the yolk color of the cooked eggs. The feed with oregano and cinnamon essential oil changed the shape of the eggs. Essential oil supplementation in the diet did not affect the sensory and textural structure but reduced the shear force of the cooked eggs. An increase in the amount of MUFA was observed in the O + C sample. Dietary use of oregano essential oil alone and combined with peppermint oil displayed increases in LA/ALA and ɷ6/ɷ3 values while its combination with cinnamon oil led to increases in MUFA/SFA, ɷ6/ɷ3 values of nutritional indices related to human health. The high LA/ALA ratio in the O and O + P groups may have given these eggs possible functional properties. In this research, enriched eggs with altered microbiota and fatty acid profiles were obtained by adding essential oils to the diets of laying hens, in which the egg’s characteristics were not affected, and the fatty acid health index parameters were positively affected in a dietetic sense.
