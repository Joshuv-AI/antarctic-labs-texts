Intervention to Improve Running and Physical Activity Participation in Children With Generalised Joint Hypermobility: A Feasibility Study
Authors: Annie Chappell, Beverly Jepson, Gaela Kilgour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChildren with symptomatic paediatric generalised joint hypermobility (pgGJH) have limitations in physical activity and participation, yet there is a lack of research to guide intervention. In Australia, these children do not receive funding support. The objective of this study was to investigate the feasibility of a programme delivered in a private practice to improve running and associated goals of children with pgGJH from the perspectives of children, parents and staff.MethodsChildren with pgGJH undertook a weekly group running intervention and home exercise programme (HEP) for 10 weeks. Feasibility of delivery in a private paediatric therapy clinic was determined using physical and self‐reported measures for acceptability, demand, implementation, practicality, integration and intervention efficacy. Semi‐structured interviews with participants, therapists and managers explored multiple aspects of feasibility of the programme.Results Acceptability: satisfaction was rated between 6.5 and 9/10. Demand: 155 of 175 survey respondents were interested in the programme. Recruitment saw 24 participants invited, 10 excluded and 6 declined. Eight children aged 10–15 years (two female) participated and attended ≥ 90% of sessions. The HEP was completed at least twice per week by 62% of participants. Implementation: the intervention was completed according to the protocol with one minor adverse event. Practicality: the intervention was well equipped but expensive to run with a therapist to participant ratio of 1:3. Integration: interventionists perceived the programme to fit well within the practice infrastructure and culture. Efficacy: participants and parents reported improved running technique and increased confidence in physical activity. Leg stiffness improved and kinesiophobia was reduced.ConclusionA running intervention for children with pgGJH was acceptable, in demand, practical to deliver and able to be integrated and implemented in a private clinic. Cost was the main challenge identified by families and management.

Introduction

Running is an important skill that enables children to participate in physical activity (PA) (Robinson et al. 2015). PA is critical for life‐long social, emotional and physical well‐being (Gao et al. 2018; Moeijes et al. 2019). Children with paediatric generalised joint hypermobility (pgGJH) have excessive mobility in multiple joints due to increased ligamentous laxity (Jensen et al. 2016). These children often present to physiotherapy services because of difficulty with running and PA due to factors including reduced lower limb power, kinesiophobia (fear of movement), pain or fatigue (Van Meulenbroek et al. 2021; Tofts et al. 2023; Celletti et al. 2013). This impacts participation in PA and everyday life (Van Meulenbroek et al. 2021; Holloway and Long 2019; Maarj et al. 2021).

The incidence of pgGJH with a Beighton score ≥ 6 is 11% (Williams et al. 2024), meaning that in every school class there are likely to be three children having difficulty keeping up with their peers because of pgGJH. In Australia, children with pgGJH are not eligible for the National Disability Insurance Scheme and usually receive very limited, if any, services through the public health system. These families often seek support from private service providers when finances permit.

A running intervention has been demonstrated to assist children with cerebral palsy to achieve running goals (Gibson et al. 2018), but it is unknown whether such an intervention would be safe and effective in children with hypermobility. We therefore undertook a feasibility study of this running intervention for children with pgGJH. This paper will report on the feasibility domains; a more in‐depth analysis of the intervention efficacy and qualitative experiences of the children and families while participating in the programme, and at 6 months post‐completion, is published separately (Chappell et al. 2026).

Methods

Study Design

This was a mixed‐methods study utilising the feasibility domains of Bowen et al. (Bowen et al. 2009), employing a single subject research design to assess the efficacy of the intervention (Krasny‐Pacini and Evans 2018). The study was prospectively registered with ANZCTR (ACTRN12623000249651), and ethical approval was granted by Curtin University (HRE2023‐0415). Parents provided written consent and children provided written assent. Participants were randomised to start the intervention after 4, 5 or 6 weeks of baseline measures by drawing numbers out of hat (A.C.). Participants then undertook the running intervention for 10 weeks, followed by a withdrawal phase (6 weeks), and follow‐up at 6 months.

Participants

Children 9–18 years old with asymptomatic or symptomatic generalised joint hypermobility were recruited through Western Kids Health (WKH), a paediatric allied health provider. Inclusion criteria included a Beighton score ≥ 6/9, a goal to improve their running and availability to attend one training session each week for 10 weeks. Children were excluded if they had previously attended running group at WKH, were unable to undertake strenuous exercise for medical reasons, had cognitive or behavioural problems that hindered working in a group, had coexisting neurological or orthopaedic conditions or had an injury in the past 6 months that impacted their ability to run.

Intervention

The intervention protocol was adapted from a published protocol (Gibson et al. 2018), but in brief consisted of an individualised programme conducted in a group setting, which utilised a hierarchy of exercises based on the biomechanical requirements of normal running, focusing on low‐load plyometric exercises for the plantarflexors, hip flexors and hip extensors. The group was conducted once per week and participants received an individualised home exercise programme (HEP), estimated to take approximately 15 min, to be completed at home at least twice per week. The group utilised a therapist: child ratio of 1:3 and was conducted at WKH. Participants in the research study did not pay to attend. Equipment used included a TotalGym Power Tower, rebounders, 15 cm hurdles, cones, steps, agility ladders and theraband. The therapists received 8.5 h of training in the intervention and basic education in psychologically‐informed physiotherapy.

Qualitative Outcomes

Parents and children undertook semi‐structured interviews with the overarching question: ‘What could children with symptomatic hypermobility tell us about the value of participation in a targeted running intervention programme for their physical performance, attendance, involvement and kinesiophobia?’ Interviews were conducted with each child and the same key parent/carer who had been involved in the intervention. Questions were directed at the child initially and then each parent for further support, clarification and their perspectives. Interviews were not time limited and occurred within 2 weeks of the intervention finishing and at 6 months post intervention. All interviews occurred over Zoom and were conducted by a researcher not involved with the delivery of the intervention but experienced in interviewing and PA participation research (G.K.). Supporting quotations are presented from children (C1–8), parents (P1–8), interventionists (I‐1–3) and manager (M1) from WKH.

Limited Efficacy Testing—Quantitative Measures

The primary outcome measure was goal attainment scaling (GAS) (Krasny‐Pacini et al. 2013), measured weekly. Secondary outcomes included visual analogue scale (VAS) (Le May et al. 2018) for pain and fatigue, also measured weekly, and pre‐ post‐ measures including Muscle Power Sprint Test (Steenman et al. 2016), Lower Extremity Grading System (Smith et al. 2018), 2‐dimensional running gait analysis (Fernández‐González et al. 2020), Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia (Dupuis et al. 2023), actigraphy (Romanzini et al. 2012), Adolescent Physical Activity Recall Questionnaire (Booth et al. 2002) and KIDSCREEN‐10 (Ravens‐Sieberer et al. 2010). The number and type of adverse events were recorded in the participant's clinical notes after every session as applicable, and the participant followed up the next day by phone by one of the interventionists.

Analysis

Quantitative data was represented graphically and analysed visually using published minimal clinically important differences where available. Kendell's Tau (Brossart et al. 2018) was used to test for trend in mean for each phase. A qualitative descriptive approach was employed (Bradshaw et al. 2017), and interview data was transcribed verbatim. Two researchers immersed themselves in the data, undertaking repeated reading and analysis using sorting and coding to develop meaning statements, sub‐themes and themes. Methodological quality and trustworthiness were developed by employing recommended methods of maintaining an audit trail and reflective journal, member checking and peer discussions.

Acceptability

Children's rating of the programme ranged from 6.5 to 9, with a mean of 7.25. Children reported enjoying the group in that it was more motivating to work with other children, and it was more fun than exercising alone. Children also reported that the programme helped them feel more confident in their body and ability and they liked how this felt. Some children commented that the programme seemed too easy or too slow to change activity.

It's helped me learn how to run properly and better so that I don't hurt myself … when I'm running and I can go for longer. (C5)

It's helped me learn how to run properly and better so that I don't hurt myself … when I'm running and I can go for longer. (C5)

Parents discussed the positive influence the programme had on their children's confidence and outlook on PA and recognised changes in physical ability. Challenges for parents included time constraints, making it difficult to complete the HEP or to drive to the group in a busy week.

I feel definitely it helps having a third party to help and with these ideas and show that there is a possibility that things can improve. (P3)

I feel definitely it helps having a third party to help and with these ideas and show that there is a possibility that things can improve. (P3)

Interventionists were very satisfied and rated the programme 7–8/10. They reported it was positive to be offering a novel intervention and offering care that was in demand. Staff satisfaction was also linked with seeing the children engage, develop confidence, connect with other children and they perceived changes in physical ability in the participants. Staff challenges related to balancing the need for individualised progression of exercises and planning (pre/post) which was non‐billable time.

Practicality

The intervention was safe to run and did not increase pain or fatigue levels in the participants (Figure 1). There was one collision between two participants resulting in a bruised shin, but both children completed the session and all subsequent sessions.

Pain and fatigue visual analogue scale scores by participant.

Attendance was excellent, four children had 100% attendance, and four children had 90% attendance. HEP adherence was variable (Table 2), with 62% of participants completing their HEP at least twice per week to meet minimum training guidelines of three sessions per week (Faigenbaum et al. 2009).

The group was staffed by two senior physiotherapists and one graduate physiotherapist for the 10 weeks of the intervention, including 1 h for the intervention and 30 min for set up and pack down. The 8.5 h of training was considered the minimum required to run this intervention. Direct staffing costs totalled AUD $5428.50. To buy all the basic equipment from major domestic retail/sports outlets, including three rebounders, 60 cones, six hurdles, three agility ladders, an aerobics step and three boxes of theraband costs a total of AUD $691.00 at the time of writing. The TotalGym Power Tower was considered helpful but not essential; this costs AUD $11 815.00 for the powered version or AUD $7269.00 for the manual version plus AUD $380 for delivery to Perth, Western Australia. The intervention could be run on a local oval or at a community hall and has been run in these situations in WA with a different population group. A local oval costs approximately $40 per hour to hire.

Integration

Interventionists considered the ratio of 1:3 to be ideal to balance safety, efficacy and cost, whereas clinic management felt a ratio of 1:4 might make the group more cost effective. Alternatively, having more junior staff lead the group would be more cost effective but require more investment in training. While having two sessions per week would make it more likely that more children would meet recommended training loads, families considered coming in more than once per week difficult both from a time and cost perspective.

Discussion

This study provides proof‐of‐concept that low‐load plyometric training targeted at running skill is safe and feasible to deliver to children with pgGJH in a private paediatric allied health practice by physiotherapists. The programme was able to be implemented with appropriate fidelity in a new population and was practical in a private practice setting.

The demand survey, with subject ‘Hypermobility’, was sent to a diverse population of the target age group associated with WKH, of these approximately 150 respondents expressed some interest in learning to improve running skill. Considering each group is likely limited to eight participants, there is easily sufficient demand to justify a consistent clinical service. The participants recruited to the study had a high incidence of kinesiophobia and high incidence of neuromuscular difficulties, so the programme was also justified from a clinical perspective.

Regarding service delivery, the main concern from a clinic management perspective was the cost, which was also a concern for families. Staff felt that a therapist: participant ratio of 1:3 would ensure a high degree of service quality; however, this would mean the cost would be a barrier to many families. Strategies that could potentially lower the cost would include having one senior staff member working with junior staff members or students, collaboration with school PE teachers, exercise physiologists, personal trainers or sports clubs or seeking external funding support. In Australia, school‐aged children with pgGJH do not receive disability funding, nor do they generally receive services through the public health system for movement difficulties. A gap therefore exists between those who can afford to pay privately and those who cannot. This is an issue that needs to be addressed by the Australian Health and Disability sectors.

In this study the intervention was led by physiotherapists in a clinic setting; however the interventionists discussed the potential for the programme to be delivered by physical education teachers in schools if they received appropriate training. Families supported the idea of early involvement of schools and Physical Education teachers so running was integrated earlier into their child's lives as an early day PA. This suggests that there is a large population of children with pgGJH (Morris et al. 2017) who would benefit from this type of intervention delivered as a before‐ or after‐school programme, similar to spelling remediation. This would also reduce the barrier families identified of distance away from home.

Limitations

Children in this study were aged 10–15 years; results cannot be generalised to other age groups. This age range was chosen to minimise the effect of normal development, as running should be mature at 9 years of age (Ulrich 2013). Selection bias may have occurred as a goal to improve running was an inclusion criterion, meaning this group of children were more likely to have symptomatic pgGJH. Many of the children recruited were known to the service which may also have introduced a bias and increased likelihood of having symptomatic pgGJH. The study was conducted over an extended period of time which included the Christmas holidays. This may have impacted the results as children were not engaged in their usual activities on school holidays.

Clinical Implications

Children with pgGJH have a high incidence of kinesiophobia and neuromuscular deficits. This study indicates that a low‐load plyometric programme to improve running may be safe and feasible to deliver in a private practice setting, but cost may be a barrier to many families. This service should be considered for funding by public health services. We also recommend investigating the potential for implementation by physical education teachers in an educational setting.
