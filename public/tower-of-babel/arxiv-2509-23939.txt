Efficient Douglas-Rachford Methods on Hadamard Manifolds with Applications to the Heron Problems
D. R. Sahu, Shikher Sharma, Pankaj Gautam

Abstract
Our interest lies in developing some efficient methods for minimizing the sum of two geodesically convex functions on Hadamard manifolds, with the aim to enhance the convergence of the Douglas-Rachford algorithm in Hadamard manifolds. Specifically, we propose two types of algorithms: inertial and non-inertial algorithms. The convergence analysis of both algorithms is provided under suitable assumptions on algorithmic parameters and the geodesic convexity of the objective functions. This convergence analysis is based on fixed-point theory for nonexpansive operators. We also study the convergence rates of these two methods. Additionally, we introduce parallel Douglas-Rachford type algorithms for minimizing functionals containing multiple summands with applications to the generalized Heron problem on Hadamard manifolds. To demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed algorithms, we present some numerical experiments for the generalized Heron problems.



keywords
Inertial methods, Douglas-Rachford methods, Fixed point methods, Minimization problems, Generalized Heron problems

Introduction

The Douglas-Rachford algorithm is among the most celebrated splitting algorithms in convex analysis and operator theory
Combettes and
Pesquet 2008
;
Boţ et al. 2015
;
Dixit et al. 2022
;
Bergmann et al. 2016
;
Sahu et al. 2024
;
Thao 2018
, originally introduced by Douglas and Rachford
Douglas and Rachford 1956
for the numerical solution of partial differential equations. It was later generalized by Lions and Mercier
Lions and Stampacchia 1967
and independently by Passty
Passty 1979
to solve the more general problem of finding a zero of the sum of two maximally monotone operators in Hilbert spaces. Eckstein and Bertsekas
Eckstein and Bertsekas 1992
provided further insights into its convergence under relaxation schemes and inexact computations.

Beyond operator inclusions, the algorithm also finds wide application in convex optimization, particularly for problems involving the sum of two proper, lower semicontinuous, and convex functions; see
Combettes and
Pesquet 2008
.
Given a Hilbert space
X
and two proper, convex, lower semicontinuous functions
\varphi,\psi:X\to(-\infty,+\infty]
, the Douglas–Rachford algorithm aims to solve the following convex minimization problem:

\min_{x\in X}\varphi(x)+\psi(x).

(1)

The strength of the Douglas-Rachford algorithm lies in its splitting structure, which allows one to compute the proximal mappings of
\varphi
and
\psi
separately. This structure makes the algorithm especially useful in large-scale and structured optimization problems
Combettes and
Pesquet 2008
.

Recently, these algorithms were successfully applied in image processing mainly for two reasons: the functional to minimize allows simple proximal mappings within the method and it turned out that the algorithms are highly parallelizable; see
Combettes and
Pesquet 2008
. Therefore, Douglas-Rachford methods became some of the most popular variational methods for image processing. Continued interest in Douglas-Rachford iteration methods is also due to its excellent performance on various nonconvex problems; see
Bauschke et al. 2002
;
Bauschke et al. 2011
.

Motivated by applications where data naturally reside on nonlinear spaces such as manifolds, common in computer vision, machine learning, and information geometry, there has been growing interest in extending convex optimization methods to manifold settings. Hadamard manifolds, in particular, offer a natural framework for such generalizations due to their geometric properties, including unique geodesics between points
Bačák 2014
.
In the past two decades, several results from linear spaces have been extended to manifold settings because of their applications in many areas of science, engineering, and management (see
Li et al. 2009
;
Li et al. 2011
;
Ferreira and Oliveira 2002
;
Ferreira et al. 2005
).

In this context, a natural generalization of classical convex optimization problems to Hadamard manifolds involves minimizing the sum of two geodesically convex functions.
Let
\mathcal{M}
be a Hadamard manifold and let
\phi,\psi\colon\mathcal{M}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
be proper geodesically convex lower semicontinuous functions. Consider the following minimization problem:

\min_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\varphi(x)+\psi(x).

(2)

To compute the solution to problem (
2
), Bergmann et al.
Bergmann et al. 2016
proposed the following Douglas-Rachford algorithm on Hadamard manifold:

x_{n+1}=\gamma(x_{n},R_{\lambda\phi}R_{\lambda\psi}(x_{n});\alpha_{n}),

(3)

where
\{\alpha_{n}\}
is a sequence in
[0,1]
with
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}(1-\alpha_{n})=\infty
and
\lambda>0
.
Bergmann et al.
Bergmann et al. 2016
showed that if
R_{\lambda\phi}R_{\lambda\psi}
is nonexpansive, then the sequence generated by method (
3
) converges to an element
v
of
\fix(R_{\lambda\phi}R_{\lambda\psi})
such that
u=\prox_{\lambda\psi}(v)
is solution of problem (
2
).

It is worth noting that by setting
T={R}_{\lambda\phi}{R}_{\lambda\psi}
, the Douglas-Rachford algorithm (
3
) can be seen as a Krasnoselski-Mann iteration given by

x_{n+1}=\gamma(x_{n},Tx_{n};\alpha_{n}),

where
\{\alpha_{n}\}
is a sequence in
[0,1]
with
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}(1-\alpha_{n})=\infty
and
\lambda>0
. This observation connects optimization and fixed point theory and hence problem (
1
) can now be viewed as a fixed point problem.

This naturally leads to the framework of fixed point theory.
Various problems across diverse domains, such as image reconstruction
Byrne 2004
, signal processing
Byrne 2004
, variational inequality problems
Mercier 1980
, and convex feasibility problems
Bauschke and Borwein 1996
can be formulated as fixed point problems. Let
C
be a nonempty, closed and convex subset of a real Hilbert space
X
and
T:C\to C
be a nonlinear operator. Then, the fixed point problem can be stated as:

\text{ find }x\in C\text{ such that }Tx=x.

(4)

To solve such problems, numerous iterative techniques have been developed. One of the most widely used is the Mann iterative method, introduced by Mann
Mann 1953
in 1953.
The Mann iteration method has its limitations, as it may not effectively approximate the fixed points of pseudocontractive mappings (see
Chidume and Mutangadura 2001
).
To tackle this challenge, Ishikawa
Ishikawa 1974
proposed an iterative approach, extensively investigated by multiple authors across different spaces to compute the fixed point of pseudocontractive and nonexpansive mappings (see
Takahashi et al. 2008
;
Acedo and Xu 2007
;
Dotson 1970
).

Building on this, Agarwal et al.
Agarwal et al. 2007
introduced the S-iteration method as follows:

\begin{cases}y_{n}=(1-\beta_{n})x_{n}+\beta_{n}Tx_{n},\\
x_{n+1}=(1-\alpha_{n})Tx_{n}+\alpha_{n}Ty_{n}\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N},\end{cases}

(5)

where
\{\alpha_{n}\}
and
\{\beta_{n}\}
are sequences in
(0,1)
with
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}(1-\beta_{n})=\infty
.
In 2011, Sahu
Sahu 2011
proposed a modified version of the S-iteration method (
5
), called the normal S-iteration method, also known as the hybrid Picard-Mann iteration method, which is defined as follows:

x_{n+1}=T((1-\alpha_{n})x_{n}+\alpha_{n}Tx_{n})\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N},

(6)

where
\{\alpha_{n}\}
is a sequence in
(0,1)
.
Continuing in this direction, Sahu
Sahu 2020
observed that applying the operator multiple times in each iteration can significantly enhance the convergence.
This led to the
p
-accelerated normal S-iteration method
Sahu 2020
, defined as follows:

x_{n+1}=T^{p}((1-\alpha_{n})x_{n}+\alpha_{n}Tx_{n})\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N},

(7)

where
\{\alpha_{n}\}
is a sequence in
(0,1)
and
p
is the power of acceleration. For
p=1
, the
p
-accelerated normal S-iteration method reduces to the normal S-iteration method defined by (
6
). It is observed in
Sahu 2020
that the
p
-accelerated normal S-iteration method has better convergence performance than the Mann iterative method and normal S-iterative method for the fixed point of nonexpansive operators with convex domain in infinite dimensional Hilbert space. In fact in most cases
p
-accelerated normal S-iteration method works better than the inertial Mann iteration method due to the parameter
p
.

Inertial-type extrapolation techniques play a critical role in accelerating iterative methods. The foundation of such methods can be traced back to Polyak
Poljak 1964
, who introduced the heavy ball method. Alvarez and Attouch
Alvarez and Attouch 2001
extended the concept to improve the performance of the proximal point algorithm, proposing the inertial proximal point algorithm for solving inclusion problems.

In the existing literature, the utilization of inertial extrapolation-based methods has been thoroughly explored and implemented in various contexts
(see
Ochs et al. 2014
;
Boţ and Csetnek 2016
;
Chen et al. 2015
;
Adly et al. 2023
;
Attouch and Cabot 2019
and references therein). Recently, researchers have developed several iterative algorithms by incorporating inertial extrapolation techniques, such as the fast shrinkage thresholding algorithm
Beck and Teboulle 2009
, inertial forward-backward algorithm
Lorenz and Pock 2015
and inertial Douglas-Rachford splitting algorithm
Boţ et al. 2015
.

Building upon the development of inertial and accelerated iterative methods, Mainge
Maingé 2008
proposed a novel approach in 2008 by combining inertial extrapolation with the Mann method, aiming to compute the fixed point of nonexpansive mappings on a real Hilbert space. The inertial Mann algorithm is defined as follows:

\begin{cases}y_{n}=x_{n}+\theta_{n}(x_{n}-x_{n-1}),\\
x_{n+1}=(1-\alpha_{n})y_{n}+\alpha_{n}Ty_{n}\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N},\end{cases}

(8)

where
\{\theta_{n}\}
and
\{\alpha_{n}\}
are sequences in
[0,1)
.
Mainge
Maingé 2008
studied the weak convergence of inertial Mann algorithm (
8
) under the conditions that

\theta_{n}\in[0,\theta]
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\theta\in[0,1)
,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\theta_{n}\|x_{n}-x_{n-1}\|^{2}<\infty
, and
\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{n}>0
and
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{n}<1
.

The second condition that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\theta_{n}\|x_{n}-x_{n-1}\|^{2}<\infty
, taken by Mainge
Maingé 2008
is often difficult to verify in practice. Boţ et al.
Boţ et al. 2015
proposed a modification in algorithm (
8
)
by replacing this condition with an alternative criterion
that the sequence
\{\theta_{n}\}
is nondecreasing in
[0,\theta]
with
\theta_{1}=0
and
\theta\in[0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\delta>\frac{\alpha^{2}(1+\alpha)+\alpha\sigma}{1-\alpha^{2}}\text{ and }0<\lambda\leq\lambda_{n}\leq\frac{\delta-\alpha[\alpha(1+\alpha)+\alpha\delta+\sigma]}{\delta[1+\alpha(1+\alpha)+\alpha\delta+\sigma]}\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N},

(9)

where
\alpha,\delta,\sigma>0
are some suitable parameters.

Recently, Dixit et al.
Dixit et al. 2020
proposed the inertial normal S-iteration method for the solution of problem (
4
) for a nonexpansive operator, which in practice converges faster than the Mann iteration method, inertial Mann iteration method and normal S-iteration method.

The verification of condition (
9
), as considered by
Boţ et al. 2015
, is a challenging task. To address this issue, Sahu
Sahu 2020
proposed a new convergence criterion for the inertial Mann iteration. This criterion assumes that the sequences
\{\alpha_{n}\}
and
\{\theta_{n}\}
satisfy the following conditions: there exist constants
0<a\leq\alpha_{n}\leq b<1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the sequence
\{\theta_{n}\}\subset[0,1)
is nondecreasing, and there exists
\theta\in(0,1)
such that
0\leq\theta_{n}\leq\theta<\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon+\max\{1,\epsilon\}}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\epsilon=\frac{1-b}{b}
. These conditions are easier to verify in practice.

Motivated by the work of Sahu
Sahu 2020
and Bergmann et al.
Bergmann et al. 2016
, the main purpose of this paper is to introduce an inertial Douglas-Rachford method and
p
-accelerated normal S-Douglas-Rachford method for solving the problem (
2
) and study its convergence analysis.
We give application of these algorithms to minimizing functionals containing multiple summands by introducing a parallel inertial Douglas-Rachford method and parallel
p
-accelerated Douglas-Rachford method. We also apply our parallel Douglas-Rachford methods to solve the generalized Heron problems and provide numerical experiments.

Preliminaries

Let
C
be a nonempty subset of a metric space
(X,d)
and let
T\colon C\to C
be an operator. Then
T
is said to be nonexpansive if

d(Tx,Ty)\leq d(x,y)\text{ for all }x,y\in C.

We denote the set of fixed points of an operator
T\colon C\to C
by
\fix(T)
, thus,

\fix(T)=\{x\in C\colon Tx=x\}.

We denote the set
\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
by
\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

For a sequence
\{x_{n}\}
in
C
, define
R_{T,\{x_{n}\}}:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
by

R_{T,\{x_{n}\}}(n)=\min_{1\leq i\leq n}d(x_{i},Tx_{i})\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

(10)

Observe that
R_{T,\{x_{n}\}}(n)\leq d(x_{i},Tx_{i})
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,n
and
\{R_{T,\{x_{n}\}}(n)\}
is decreasing.

We use the notation
o
means that
s_{n}=o(1/t_{n})
if and only if
\lim_{n\to\infty}s_{n}t_{n}=0
.

Definition 1

(
Sahu et al. 2016
)
.

Let
C
be a nonempty subset of a metric space
(X,d)
and let
T\colon C\to C
be an operator with
\fix(T)\neq\emptyset
. Let
\{x_{n}\}
a sequence in
C
. We say that
\{x_{n}\}
has

(a)

the limit existence property (for short, the LE property) for
T
if
\lim_{n\to\infty}d(x_{n},v)
exists for all
v\in\fix(T)
;

(b)

the approximate fixed point property (for short, the AF point property) for
T
if

\lim_{n\to\infty}d(x_{n},Tx_{n})=0;

(c)

the LEAF point property if
\{x_{n}\}
has both LE property and AF property.

(
Sahu 2020

Let
X
be a Hilbert space and
\alpha\geq 0
. Then,

\|x-\alpha y\|^{2}\geq(1-\alpha)\|x\|^{2}-\alpha(1-\alpha)\|y\|^{2}\text{ for all }x,y\in X.

There exists

For the basic definitions and results regarding the geometry of Riemannian manifolds, we refer to
do Carmo 1992
;
Sakai 1996
;
Udrişte 1994
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be an
m
-dimensional differentiable manifold and let
x\in\mathcal{M}
. The set of all tangent vectors at the point
x
, denoted by
\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}
, is called the tangent space of
\mathcal{M}
at
x
. It forms a real vector space of dimension
m
. The tangent bundle of
\mathcal{M}
is defined as the disjoint union
\mathcal{T}\mathcal{M}=\bigcup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}
, which is a smooth manifold of dimension
2m
.

We suppose that
\mathcal{M}
is equipped with a Riemannian metric, i.e., a smoothly varying family of inner products on the tangent spaces
\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}
. The metric at
x\in\mathcal{M}
is denoted by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{x}:\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
.
A differentiable manifold
\mathcal{M}
with a Riemannian metric
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is said to be a Riemannian manifold.
The induced norm on
\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}
is denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{x}
.

Let
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
and let
\gamma\colon[a,b]\to\mathcal{M}
be a piecewise smooth curve joining
x
to
y
. Then the length of the curve
\gamma
is defined by

L(\gamma)\coloneq\int_{a}^{b}\|\gamma^{\prime}(t)\|dt
. The minimal length of all such curves joining
x
to
y
is called the Riemannian distance, denoted by
d(x,y)
, i.e.,
d(x,y)\coloneq\inf\{L(\gamma)\colon\gamma\in C_{xy}\}
, where
C_{xy}
denotes the set of all piecewise smooth curves
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to\mathcal{M}
such that
\gamma(0)=x
and
\gamma(1)=y
.

A single-valued vector field on a differentiable manifold
\mathcal{M}
is a smooth mapping
V\colon\mathcal{M}\to T\mathcal{M}
such that, for each
x\in\mathcal{M}
,
V(x)\in\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}
.
Let
\nabla
be the Levi-Civita connection associated with the Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
.
A vector field
V
along
\gamma
is said to be parallel if
\nabla_{\gamma^{\prime}}V=0
.
If
\gamma^{\prime}
is parallel along
\gamma
, i.e.,
\nabla_{\gamma^{\prime}}\gamma^{\prime}=0
, then
\gamma
is said to be geodesic. In this case,
\|\gamma^{\prime}\|
is a constant. Moreover, if
\|\gamma^{\prime}\|=1
, then
\gamma
is called a unit-speed geodesic. A geodesic joining
x
to
y
in the Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
is said to be a minimal geodesic if its length is equal to
d(x,y)
. We denote geodesic joining
x
and
y
by
\gamma(x,y;\cdot)
, i.e.,

\gamma(x,y;\cdot)\colon[a,b]\to\mathcal{M}
is such that, for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
\gamma(x,y;a)=x
and
\gamma(x,y;b)=y
.

The parallel transport on the tangent bundle
\mathcal{T}\mathcal{M}
along the geodesic
\gamma
with respect to Riemannian connection
\nabla
is defined as
P_{\gamma,\gamma(b),\gamma(a)}\colon\mathcal{T}_{\gamma(a)}\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{T}_{\gamma(b)}\mathcal{M}
such that

P_{\gamma,\gamma(b),\gamma(a)}(v)=V(\gamma(x,y;b))\text{ for all }a,b\in\mathbb{R},v\in\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M},

where
V
is the unique vector field satisfying
\nabla_{\gamma^{\prime}(x,y;t)}V=0
for all
t\in[a,b]
and
V(\gamma(x,y;a))=v
.
A Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
is said to be complete if for any
x\in\mathcal{M}
all geodesic emanating from
x
are defined for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. By Hopf-Rinow Theorem
Sakai 1996
, if
\mathcal{M}
is complete, then any pair of points in
\mathcal{M}
can be joined by a minimal geodesic. Moreover,
(\mathcal{M},d)
is a complete metric space. If
\mathcal{M}
is a complete Riemannian manifold, then the exponential map
\exp_{x}\colon\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
at
x\in\mathcal{M}
is defined by
\exp_{x}v=\gamma_{x,v}(1)
for all
v\in\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}
,
where
\gamma_{x,v}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{M}
is a unique geodesic
starting from
x
with velocity
v
, i.e.,
\gamma_{x,v}(0)=x
and
\gamma^{\prime}_{x,v}(0)=v
. It is known that
\exp_{x}(tv)=\gamma_{x,v}(t)
for
each real number
t
. For the exponential map, we have the following assertions:

(i)

\exp_{x}\mathbf{0}=\gamma_{x,\mathbf{0}}(1)=\gamma_{x,v}(0)=x
, where
\mathbf{0}
is the zero tangent vector

(ii)

Inverse of
\exp_{x}
,
\exp_{x}^{-1}\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}
is given by

\exp_{x}^{-1}y=v,

where
\gamma_{x,v}(1)=\exp_{x}v=y
.

(iii)

\exp_{x}
is a diffeomorphism on
\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}
and for any
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
, we have
d(x,y)=\|\exp_{x}^{-1}y\|
.

(iv)

Let
y\in\mathcal{M}
and
v=\exp_{x}^{-1}y\in\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}
. Then

\exp_{x}(tv)=\exp_{x}(t\exp_{x}^{-1}y)=\gamma_{x,\exp_{x}^{-1}y}(t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

A complete simply connected Riemannian manifold of nonpositive sectional
curvature is called a Hadamard manifold.

(

)

Let

Remark 9
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a Hadamard manifold and

be a geodesic triangle in
\mathcal{M}
. Then, we have the following:

(i)

Let
\varsigma_{y}

\langle\exp_{y}^{-1}x,\exp_{y}^{-1}z\rangle=d(x,y)d(y,z)\cos\varsigma_{y}

(ii)

Efficient Douglas-Rachford Algorithms on Hadamard Manifolds

In this section, we introduce two algorithms for finding fixed points of nonexpansive operators on Hadamard manifolds. We also discuss the consequences of these algorithms for solving the minimization problem (

First, we introduce an method that incorporates an inertial extrapolation acceleration term, given as follows:

Algorithm 21

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a Hadamard manifold and

Initialization:

\begin{cases}y_{n}=\exp_{x_{n}}(-\theta_{n}\exp_{x_{n}}^{-1}x_{n-1}),\\

(12)

where
\{\alpha_{n}\}

(0,1)
and
\{\theta_{n}\}
is a sequence in
[0,1)

Note that Method (

) from Hilbert spaces to the setting of Hadamard manifolds. Therefore, we also refer to it here as the inertial Mann iteration method.

For the convergence analysis of inertial Mann method (

\displaystyle\leq-a(1-b)d^{2}(y_{n},Ty_{n})+\theta_{n}(1+\theta_{n})d^{2}(x_{n},x_{n-1}).

(28)

From (

\displaystyle(d^{2}(x_{n+1},v)-d^{2}(x_{n},v))-\theta_{n}(d^{2}(x_{n},v)-d^{2}(x_{n-1},v))

\displaystyle\leq-a(1-b)d^{2}(y_{n},Ty_{n})+\theta_{n}(1+\theta_{n})d^{2}(x_{n},x_{n-1}).

(29)

Let
\delta_{n}=\theta_{n}(1+\theta_{n})d^{2}(x_{n},x_{n-1})
,
\phi_{n}=d^{2}(x_{n},v)
,
V_{n}=\phi_{n}-\phi_{n-1}
and
[V_{n}]_{+}=\max\{V_{n},0\}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. From (

\displaystyle a(1-b)d^{2}(y_{n},Ty_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\phi_{n}-\phi_{n+1}+\theta_{n}(\phi_{n}-\phi_{n-1})+\delta_{n}

\displaystyle=\phi_{n}-\phi_{n+1}+\theta_{n}V_{n}+\delta_{n}.

\displaystyle\leq\phi_{n}-\phi_{n+1}+\theta_{n}[V_{n}]_{+}+\delta_{n}.

(30)

From (
27
), we have

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}d^{2}(x_{n+1},x_{n})\leq\frac{\psi_{1}}{K(1-\theta)}.

(31)

Note that
x_{0}=x_{1}
. Then
\psi_{1}=d^{2}(x_{1},v)-\theta_{1}d^{2}(x_{0},v)+K_{1}d^{2}(x_{0},x_{1})\leq d^{2}(x_{0},v)
. From (
31
), we have

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}d^{2}(x_{n+1},x_{n})\leq\frac{d^{2}(x_{0},v)}{K(1-\theta)}.

It follows from condition (
C3
) that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\delta_{n}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\theta_{n}(1+\theta_{n})d^{2}(x_{n},x_{n-1})\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\theta(1+\theta)d^{2}(x_{n},x_{n-1})\leq\frac{\theta(1+\theta)d^{2}(x_{0},v)}{K(1-\theta)}.

(32)

From (
29
), we have

\displaystyle V_{n+1}\leq\theta_{n}V_{n}+\delta_{n}\leq\theta[V_{n}]_{+}+\delta_{n}.

Therefore

\displaystyle[V_{n+1}]_{+}\leq\theta[V_{n}]_{+}+\delta_{n}.

Applying the recurrence repeatedly, we get

\displaystyle[V_{n+1}]_{+}\leq\theta^{n}[V_{1}]+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta^{j-1}\delta_{n+1-j}.

(33)

Since
x_{0}=x_{1}
, we get
V_{1}=[V_{1}]_{+}=0
and
\delta_{1}=0.

From (
33
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}[V_{n}]_{+}\leq\frac{1}{1-\theta}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\delta_{n}=\frac{1}{1-\theta}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\delta_{n}\leq\frac{\theta(1+\theta)d^{2}(x_{0},v)}{K(1-\theta)^{2}}.

(34)

Since
\phi_{1}=\phi_{0}=d^{2}(x_{0},v)
. From (
30
), (
32
) and (
34
), we get

\displaystyle a(1-b)\sum_{i=1}^{n}d^{2}(y_{n},Ty_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\phi_{1}-\phi_{n}+\theta\sum_{i=1}^{n}[V]_{+}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\delta_{i}

\displaystyle\leq d^{2}(x_{0},v)+\frac{\theta^{2}(1+\theta)d^{2}(x_{0},v)}{K(1-\theta)^{2}}+\frac{\theta(1+\theta)d^{2}(x_{0},v)}{K(1-\theta)}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{\theta^{2}(1+\theta)}{K(1-\theta)^{2}}+\frac{\theta(1+\theta)}{K(1-\theta)}\right)d^{2}(x_{0},v).

This implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}d^{2}(y_{n},Ty_{n})\leq\frac{1}{a(1-b)}\left(1+\frac{\theta^{2}(1+\theta)}{K(1-\theta)^{2}}+\frac{\theta(1+\theta)}{K(1-\theta)}\right)d^{2}(x_{0},v).

(35)

By using the nonexpansivity of
T
,
(
12
)
and Proposition

, we have

\displaystyle d^{2}(x_{n+1},Tx_{n+1})

\displaystyle=d^{2}(\gamma(y_{n},Ty_{n};\alpha_{n}),Tx_{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{n})d^{2}(y_{n},Tx_{n+1})+\alpha_{n}d^{2}(Ty_{n},Tx_{n+1})-\alpha_{n}(1-\alpha_{n})d^{2}(y_{n},Ty_{n})

\displaystyle\leq(d(y_{n},Ty_{n})+d(Ty_{n},Tx_{n+1}))^{2}+d^{2}(Ty_{n},Tx_{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq 2(d^{2}(y_{n},Ty_{n})+d^{2}(Ty_{n},Tx_{n+1}))+d^{2}(Ty_{n},Tx_{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq 2d^{2}(y_{n},Ty_{n})+3d^{2}(y_{n},x_{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq 2d^{2}(y_{n},Ty_{n})+3d^{2}(y_{n},\gamma(y_{n},Ty_{n};\alpha_{n}))

\displaystyle\leq 2d^{2}(y_{n},Ty_{n})+3\alpha_{n}^{2}d^{2}(y_{n},Ty_{n})

\displaystyle\leq 5d^{2}(y_{n},Ty_{n}).

From (

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}d^{2}(x_{i+1},Tx_{i+1})\leq 5\sum_{i=1}^{n}d^{2}(y_{i},Ty_{i})\leq\frac{5}{a(1-b)}\left(1+\frac{\theta^{2}(1+\theta)}{K(1-\theta)^{2}}+\frac{\theta(1+\theta)}{K(1-\theta)}\right)d^{2}(x_{0},v).

Thus

(R_{T,\{x_{n}\}}(n))^{2}=\min_{1\leq i\leq n}d^{2}(x_{n+1},Tx_{n+1})\leq\frac{5}{a(1-b)}\left(1+\frac{\theta^{2}(1+\theta)}{K(1-\theta)^{2}}+\frac{\theta(1+\theta)}{K(1-\theta)}\right)\frac{d^{2}(x_{0},v)}{n}.

Next, we introduce another accelerated method that does not involve an inertial term. Unlike the inertial Mann iteration method, which requires working in the entire space, the following method can be applied on a convex domain.

Algorithm 26
.

Let
C
be a nonempty, closed and geodesically convex subset of a Hadamard manifold
\mathcal{M}
,
p\in\mathbb{N}
and let

be an operator.

Initialization:
Choose

arbitrary.

Iterative step:
For the current
n^{th}
iterate, compute the
(n+1)^{th}
iteration as follows:

\begin{cases}y_{n}=\gamma(x_{n},Tx_{n};\alpha_{n}),\\
x_{n+1}=T^{p}(y_{n})\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\end{cases}

(36)

where
\{\alpha_{n}\}
is a sequence in
(0,1)
.

Note that this method is inspired from the
p
-accelerated normal S-iteration method (

We now study the convergence of the
p
-accelerated normal S-iteration method (

Theorem 27
.

Let
C
be a nonempty, closed and geodesically convex subset of a Hadamard manifold
\mathcal{M}
and let
T\colon C\to C
be a nonexpansive operator such that
\fix(T)\neq\emptyset
. Let
\{x_{n}\}
be the sequence generated by
p
-accelerated normal S-iteration method (
36

\{\alpha_{n}\}
is a sequence in
(0,1)
satisfying the condition
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}(1-\alpha_{n})=\infty
. Then
\{x_{n}\}
converges to an element of
\fix(T)
with the convergence rate gives as follows:

d(x_{n},Tx_{n})=o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}(1-\alpha_{i})}}\right).

Proof.

Let
v\in\fix(T)
. From (
36
) and Proposition
8
, we have

\displaystyle d(y_{n},v)

\displaystyle=d(\gamma(x_{n},Tx_{n};\alpha_{n}),v)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{n})d(x_{n},v)+\alpha_{n}d(Tx_{n},v)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{n})d(x_{n},v)+\alpha_{n}d(x_{n},v)=d(x_{n},v).

(37)

From (
36
), (
37
) and nonexpansivity of
T
, we have

d(x_{n+1},v)=d(T^{p}(y_{n}),v)\leq d(T^{p-1}y_{n},v)\leq\cdots\leq d(y_{n},v)\leq d(x_{n},v).

Hence,
\lim_{n\to\infty}d(x_{n},v)
exists and
\{x_{n}\}
is bounded.

Next we show that
\lim_{n\to\infty}d(x_{n},Tx_{n})=0
.
From Proposition
13
, we have

\displaystyle d^{2}(x_{n+1},v)

\displaystyle=d^{2}(T^{p}(y_{n}),v)

\displaystyle\leq d^{2}(y_{n},v)

\displaystyle=d^{2}(\gamma(x_{n},Tx_{n};\alpha_{n}),v)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{n})d^{2}(x_{n},v)+\alpha_{n}d^{2}(Tx_{n},v)-\alpha_{n}(1-\alpha_{n})d^{2}(x_{n},Tx_{n})

\displaystyle=d^{2}(x_{n},v)-\alpha_{n}(1-\alpha_{n})d^{2}(x_{n},Tx_{n}).

It follows that

\alpha_{n}(1-\alpha_{n})d^{2}(x_{n},Tx_{n})\leq d^{2}(x_{n},v)-d^{2}(x_{n+1},v).

(38)

Since
\lim_{n\to\infty}d(x_{n},v)
exists, from (
38
) we have

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}(1-\alpha_{n})d^{2}(x_{n},Tx_{n})<\infty,

(39)

which implies that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}d(x_{n},Tx_{n})=0,

(40)

because otherwise
d(x_{n},Tx_{n})\geq K
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
for some
K>0
and then

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}(1-\alpha_{n})d^{2}(x_{n},Tx_{n})\geq K\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}(1-\alpha_{n})=\infty,

which is a contradiction to the condition (
39

On the other hand, using the nonexpansivity of
T
and Proposition
8
, we have

\displaystyle d(x_{n+1},Tx_{n+1})

\displaystyle=d(T^{p}(y_{n}),T^{p+1}(y_{n}))

(41)

\displaystyle\leq d(y_{n},Ty_{n})

\displaystyle\leq d(y_{n},Tx_{n})+d(Tx_{n},Ty_{n})

\displaystyle\leq d(y_{n},Tx_{n})+d(x_{n},y_{n})

\displaystyle=d(\gamma(x_{n},Tx_{n};\alpha_{n}),Tx_{n})+d(\gamma(x_{n},Tx_{n};\alpha_{n}),x_{n})

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha_{n})d(x_{n},Tx_{n})+\alpha_{n}d(Tx_{n},Tx_{n})+(1-\alpha_{n})d(x_{n},x_{n})+\alpha_{n}d(Tx_{n},x_{n})

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha_{n})d(x_{n},Tx_{n})+\alpha_{n}d(Tx_{n},x_{n})=d(x_{n},Tx_{n}).

(42)

This means that
\{d(x_{n},Tx_{n})\}
is a monotone sequence. Combining this with (

\lim_{n\to\infty}d(x_{n},Tx_{n})=0

\{x_{n}\}
satisfies the LEAF point property. Therefore, from Proposition
12

\{x_{n}\}
converges to an element of
\fix(T)
.

Again from (
38

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}(1-\alpha_{i})d^{2}(x_{i},Tx_{i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}(d^{2}(x_{i},v)-d^{2}(x_{i+1},v))=d^{2}(x_{0},v).

(43)

From (
42

\{d(x_{n},Tx_{n})\}
is decreasing. It follows from (

\displaystyle d^{2}(x_{n},Tx_{n})\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}(1-\alpha_{i})\leq d^{2}(x_{0},v).

This implies that

\displaystyle d(x_{n},Tx_{n})\leq\frac{d(x_{0},v)}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}(1-\alpha_{i})}}.

Hence,
d(x_{n},Tx_{n})=o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}(1-\alpha_{i})}}\right).

∎

Remark 28

If in addition, we take

Remark 29
.

While both Inertial Mann method (
12
) and
p
-accelerated normal S-iteration method (
36
) aim to compute a fixed point of the nonexpansive mapping
T
, their convergence behavior differs significantly in practice.

The
p
-accelerated normal S-iteration method (
36
) generates a relatively stable sequence, and the fixed-point residual
d(Tx_{n},x_{n})
typically decreases in a monotonic fashion. As such, the value of
d(Tx_{n},x_{n})
itself serves as a reliable indicator of the convergence rate.

On the other hand, the inertial Mann iteration method (
12
), which incorporates momentum via previous iterates, produce more oscillatory trajectories due to the presence of momentum terms. As a result, the standard residual
\{d(Tx_{n},x_{n})\}
may not monotonically decrease, and may not reflect the actual rate at which the iterates approach the fixed point set. To better assess convergence behavior, we therefore consider the best iterate criterion

R_{T,\{x_{n}\}}(n)=\underset{{1\leq i\leq n}}{\min}d(Tx_{i},x_{i})
, which is more appropriate for methods with inertial components.

Now we introduce two accelerated Douglas-Rachford-type algorithms for solving the minimization problem (
2
) on Hadamard manifolds.
By defining
T=R_{\lambda\phi}R_{\lambda\psi}
, the classical Douglas-Rachford method can be seen as a special case of the Krasnoselskii-Mann iteration method. The proposed algorithms extend this idea to Hadamard manifolds and incorporate acceleration techniques. Specifically, both algorithms are constructed using the composition of the reflected resolvents
R_{\lambda\phi}
and
R_{\lambda\psi}
,
following the structure of the inertial Mann iteration method (
12
) and the
p
-accelerated normal S-iteration method (

).

In this section, we perform numerical experiments on a well-known nonconvex optimization problem, the Rosenbrock problem, defined as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}a(x_{1}^{2}-x_{2})^{2}+(x_{1}-b)^{2},

(49)

and

x_{2}-\frac{8\lambda(x_{1}+2\lambda b)(x_{1}-b)}{(1+2\lambda)^{2}}\end{pmatrix}\text{ for all }x=(x_{1},x_{2})^{T}\in\mathcal{M}.

(50)

Applications

In this section, we present applications of the proposed algorithms for solving the problem of minimizing a functional involving multiple summands. To this end, we develop parallel versions of the proposed algorithms and apply them to find the solution of generalized Heron problem on Hadamard manifolds.

5.1
Minimization of functional containing multiple summands

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a Hadamard manifold. Let
\varLambda_{N}=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
and let
f_{k}\colon\mathcal{M}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
,
k\in\varLambda_{N}
be proper, geodesically convex and lower semicontinuous functions.
Consider the following problem of minimization of functionals containing multiple summands:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{k\in\varLambda_{N}}f_{k}(x).

(52)

Now, consider the product manifold
\mathcal{M}^{N}=\{\textbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\colon x_{k}\in\mathcal{M},k\in\varLambda_{N}\}
. Define

\textbf{D}=\{\textbf{x}\in\mathcal{M}^{N}\colon x_{1}=\dots=x_{N}\in\mathcal{M}\}.

(53)

Then
D
is a nonempty, closed and geodesically convex set. Hence,
\iota_{\textbf{D}}
is a proper, geodesically convex and lower semicontinuous function; see
Bačák 2014
. For

\textbf{x }=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\in\mathcal{M}^{N}

\prox_{\iota_{\textbf{D}}}(\textbf{x})=P_{\textbf{D}}(\textbf{x})=\Bigl(\amin_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{k\in\varLambda_{N}}d^{2}(x_{k},x),\ldots,\amin_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{k\in\varLambda_{N}}d^{2}(x_{k},x)\Bigl)\in\mathcal{M}^{N}.

(54)

Define
F\colon\mathcal{M}^{N}\to(-\infty,\infty]
by

F(\textbf{x})=\sum_{k\in\varLambda_{N}}f_{k}(x_{k})\text{ for all }\textbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\in\mathcal{M}^{N}.

(55)

We are interested in minimization of
F
over
D
. For this, we consider the following unconstrained minimization problem:

\min_{\textbf{x}\in\mathcal{M}^{N}}F(\textbf{x})+\iota_{\textbf{D}}(\textbf{x}).

(56)

A detailed explanation of the above formulation is given in
Bergmann et al. 2016
. Note that for
\lambda>0

\displaystyle\prox_{\lambda F}(\textbf{x})

\displaystyle=\left(\prox_{\lambda f_{1}}(x_{1}),\ldots,\prox_{\lambda f_{N}}(x_{N})\right)\text{ for all }\textbf{x}\in\mathcal{M}^{N}.

It follows that the reflection of
F
is just given by the componentwise reflections of
\prox_{\lambda f_{k}}
, i.e.,

R_{\lambda F}(\textbf{x})=\left(R_{\lambda f_{1}}(x_{1}),\ldots,R_{\lambda f_{N}}(x_{N})\right)\text{ for all }\textbf{x}\in\mathcal{M}^{N}.

We now present two methods based on the inertial Douglas-Rachford method (
44
) and the
p
-accelerated normal S-Douglas-Rachford method (
45
) for finding a solution to problem (
52
).

5.2
The generalized Heron problem in Hadamard manifolds

Heron from Alexandria (10-75 AD) was “a Greek geometer and inventor whose writings preserved for posterity a knowledge of the mathematics and engineering of Babylonia, ancient Egypt, and the Greco-Roman world” (from the Encyclopedia Britannica). One of the geometric problems he proposed in his Catroptica was as follows: find a point on a straight line in the plane such that the sum of the distances from it to two given points is minimal.

In 2012, Mordukhovich et al.
Mordukhovich et al. 2012a
generalized the Heron problem.
In the examination of the generalized Heron problem, as discussed in reference
Mordukhovich et al. 2012a
;
Mordukhovich
et al. 2012b
;
Boţ et al. 2015
;
Dixit et al. 2022
;
Sahu et al. 2024
, the conventional notions of points and straight lines are substituted with nonempty, closed and convex sets. Within this context, the objective is to locate a point within a predetermined nonempty, closed and convex set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
in such a way that the combined distance to specified nonempty, closed and convex sets
C_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m},k\in\varLambda_{N}
is minimized. Mathematically, we can write

\text{minimize }\sum_{k\in\varLambda_{N}}d(x,C_{k})\text{ subject to }x\in C\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}.

(59)

Now, we consider the generalized Heron problem in the setting of Hadamard manifold
\mathcal{M}
, where the objective is to locate a point within a predetermined nonempty, closed and geodesically convex set
C\subset\mathcal{M}
in such a way that the combined distance to specified nonempty, closed and geodesically convex sets
C_{k}\subset\mathcal{M},k\in\varLambda_{N}
is minimized. Mathematically, the generalized Heron problem can be formulated as:

\text{minimize }\sum_{k\in\varLambda_{N}}d(x,C_{k})\text{ subject to }x\in C\subset\mathcal{M}.

(60)

The constrained problem (
59
) can be formulated into the following constrained problem:

\min_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{k\in\varLambda_{N}}\phi_{k}(x)+\iota_{C}(x),

(61)

where
\phi_{k}(x)=d(x,C_{k})
for all
k\in\varLambda_{N}
.
In the view of (
56
), problem (
61
) can be written as

\min_{\textbf{x}\in\mathcal{M}^{N+1}}F(\textbf{x})+\iota_{\textbf{D}}(\textbf{x}),

(62)

where
F(\textbf{x})=\sum_{k\in\varLambda_{N}}d(x_{k},C_{k})+\iota_{C}(x_{N+1})
and

D
=
\{\textbf{x}\in\mathcal{M}^{N+1}\colon x_{1}=\dots=x_{N+1}\}
.

Example 38
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}^{m}_{++}=\{x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\colon x_{i}>0,i=1,\ldots,m\}

The Riemannian distance

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have proposed new accelerated algorithms–the inertial Mann method and the
p
-accelerated normal S-method for finding fixed points of nonexpansive operators on Hadamard manifolds. These methods were further utilized to minimize the sum of two geodesically convex functions on Hadamard manifolds. For the minimization problem, we have derived the inertial Douglas-Rachford method and the
p
-accelerated normal S-Douglas-Rachford method, which aim to improve the convergence speed of the classical Douglas-Rachford method in the context of Hadamard manifolds. We have studied the convergence analysis of these methods and analyzed their asymptotic behavior under mild conditions on the iteration parameters.

Additionally, we introduced parallel versions of these algorithms–the inertial parallel Douglas-Rachford method and the
p
-accelerated normal S-parallel Douglas-Rachford method for finding the solution of minimization problems involving sum of more than two functions. We applied these parallel methods to find the solution of the generalized Heron problem in the context of Hadamard manifolds. We conducted numerical experiments which showed the efficiency of the proposed methods.
