Commutative Quantale and Localization
Bangzheng Li, Yichen Xiao

Abstract
. In this paper we introduce the localization construction for quantales. A quantale is a complete semilattice combined with a multiplication. We mimic the notion of filter in a lattice to define multiplicative filters in a quantale, and construct the localization of the quantale at a multiplicative filter. We prove theorems with similar structure as “ Spec ⁡ R \operatorname{Spec}R is a sheaf” and use them to obtain several results in algebra and geometry, including the Baire Category Theorem and some of its generalizations. We also present an algebraic version of Baire Category Theorem, which we believe has not appeared in literature.

Roughly speaking, a quantale is a complete (join-)semilattice that carries a multiplication operation.
In the regular definition of quantale, we impose the existence of bottom element.
However, in this paper we will drop this assumption, so that our theory becomes more general.
There are two main sources for quantales: the collection of opens in a topological space and the collection of ideals in a ring.
The former one is more geometric and the latter one is more algebraic.

Aside from ideals and filters are well studied in lattice theory, there’s also few mentions about these concepts in multiplicative lattices, such as quantales. Similar with the various ideals mentioned in

The primary objective for this paper is to develop a localization theory inside quantales, using a multiplicative filter point of view.

1.2.
Three Theorems in Algebra/Geometry

We introduce three theorems that may be well-known to the audiences.

One sheaf axiom for
\operatorname{Spec}R
:
let
R
be a commutative unital ring and
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in R
such that
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})=R
.
Then the
R
-linear map
R\to\prod_{i=1}^{n}R_{f_{i}}
is injective.

0 is a local property:
let
R
be a commutative unital ring and
M
an
R
-module.
If
M_{\mathfrak{m}}={\left\{0\right\}}
for all maximal ideals
\mathfrak{m}\subset R
, then
M={\left\{0\right\}}
.

Baire Category Theorem:
a locally compact regular (which is equivalent to locally compact Hausdorff) topological space is not the union of countably many nowhere dense subsets.

Among the three theorems, the first two come from algebra, while the last one comes from geometry.
You may think that they are three independent theorems, at least the first two and the last one has no relation with each other.
However, using the language of quantale, all of these three theorems can be unified into

Filter Merging Theorem:
under some assumptions, for a
Q
-module
M
and multiplicative filters
{\left\{\mathscr{F}_{i}\right\}}_{i\in I}
, the map

M_{\mathscr{F}}\to\prod_{i\in I}M_{\mathscr{F}_{i}}

is injective, where
\mathscr{F}=\bigcap_{i\in I}\mathscr{F}_{i}
is the “merged multiplicative filter.”

Using this unified theorem, the proof idea of Baire Category Theorem can be summarized by the following procedure:

(a)

We regard everything up to a nowhere dense subset.

(b)

Then each nowhere dense subset becomes empty set.

(c)

However, the whole space is not the empty set since the whole space is not nowhere dense.

(d)

We conclude by the observation that a nonempty set is not the union of countably many empty sets.

At first glance, this “proof” makes no sense.
Here are some potential concerns:

(a)

Why can we regard everything up to a nowhere dense subset?

(b)

A nonempty set is not the union of arbitrarily many empty sets, so why Baire Category Theorem does not work for arbitrarily many nowhere dense subsets?

(c)

Where did we use the locally compact Hausdorff condition?

All these questions will have a satisfactory answer in this paper, so that the above “proof” becomes a rigorous one.

Also, recall the other condition for the sheaf axiom of
\operatorname{Spec}R
: for
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{n}\in R
such that
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})=R
, the image of
R\to\prod_{i=1}^{n}R_{f_{i}}
is precisely those
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\prod_{i=1}^{n}R_{f_{i}}
such that the image of
a_{i}
under map
R_{f_{i}}\to R_{f_{i}f_{j}}
agrees with the image of
a_{j}
under map
R_{f_{j}}\to R_{f_{i}f_{j}}
.
We will exhibit a similar statement for the case of quantales and their localization.
Furthermore, we will remove the somewhat superfluous condition “
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})=R
.”

1.3.
Overview

We now present an overview toward this paper.
In
Section

2
we talk about basic definitions and propositions that is necessary to understand the main text of this paper.
The key definitions are quantales and their modules, Noetherian, compactness.

In
Section

3
we define the notion of multiplicative filters in a quantale and construct the localization of a quantale at a multiplicative filter.
It is notable that after doing localization the result may not always be a quantale/module, so we define what it means for a multiplicative filter to be localizable, under which assumption the resulting localization will indeed be a quantale/module.

In
Section

4
we introduce shrinkability, a notion that serves important role in the study of localization of quantales.
A byproduct is the suspension of a quantale, which is used to define the shrinkability and has interest in its own.
We will also prove the first filter merging theorem.

In
Section

5
we first recall the precoherence for a quantale.
With the help of suspension, we then define bloomingness for a quantale/module, and use such ideas to present the second filter merging theorem.
We also show that a very important class of topological spaces, locally compact Hausdorff spaces, induce blooming quantales.
Then we go ahead and define special classes of multiplicative filters, and use them to give the third filter merging theorem.

However, a lot of theorems about localization is based on the assumption that our multiplicative filters are localizable (or some stronger conditions), and using the original definition to show a multiplicative filter is localizable is not an easy task.
In
Section

6
we present what it means for a multiplcative filter to be normal, a condition that is easy to check and can guarantee that our multiplicative filter is localizable.
Similarly we define conormal, the dual notion of normal, and show that it shares some similar properties as the normalness.
As an application of these notions, we show that some special multiplicative filters are normal/conormal, and they will serve an important role in the proof of Baire Category Theorem.

Section

7
is divided into three parts.
In the first part we compare our approach of defining localization of quantale with other author’s.
In the second part we present several applications of our theory about localization of quantales.
We show that various algebraic/geometric facts can be deduced from localization of quantales, including the Baire Category Theorem.
We also give two generalizations of Baire Category Theorem, which can be proved in a few lines using the language of quantales.
In the end, we give an algebraic version of Baire Category Theorem, which can be proved exactly the same way as the Baire Category Theorem.
We think this is the strength of the theory about localization of quantales: it connect algebra and geometry together, so that a definition in algebra can be used to give intuition for geometric world, a theorem in algebra can be transferred to a theorem in geometry, and vice versa.
In the third part we give some conjectures and questions for future studies.

In this paper, we take nonzero join instead of arbitrary (so a bottom element may not exist), which drops a necessary condition for the quantale structure (and generalizes the theory of quantale).
Still, the results work for a quantale with original definition.

.

A (commutative integral)

First let us prove some basic properties of quantale.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
(Q,\vee)
be a semilattice.
Then

(a)

For
a,b\in Q
, we have

if and only if
a+b=b
.

(b)

If

is a prequantale, then for

a\leq a^{\prime},b\leq b^{\prime}
, then
ab\leq a^{\prime}b^{\prime}

In particular,
ab\leq a
and
ab\leq b
.

(c)

If
Q
is a quantale, then for

, their meet
a\wedge b=\sum{\left\{x\in Q:x\leq a,x\leq b\right\}}
exists.
The operations
\vee
and
\wedge
on

make

into a lattice.

Proof.

Part (a):
\Rightarrow
: for
q\in Q
, we have
q\geq a
and
q\geq b
if and only if
q\geq b
, so
a+b=b
.

\Leftarrow
: we have
b\geq b

.

Part (b): it suffices to show
ab\leq ab^{\prime}
.
To see this, just note that
ab+ab^{\prime}=a(b+b^{\prime})=ab^{\prime}
, so by Part (a) we see
ab\leq ab^{\prime}
.
Now
ab\leq a\cdot 1=a
and
ab\leq 1\cdot b=b
.

Part (c): by Part (b) we have

is not empty.
Now pick any

such that
q\leq a
and
q\leq b
, then

, so

, from which we see

exists, hence

is a lattice.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

Thus, given the addition structure on a semilattice, we can recover the partial order by
2.6
Part (a).

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
Q
be a quantale.
The following are equivalent:

(a)

For all
q\in Q
, we have
q^{2}=q
.

(b)

For all

ab=a\wedge b
.

Proof.

\Rightarrow

On the other hand, we have

\Leftarrow

2.2.
Noetherian and Compactness

In commutative algebra, Noetherian is one of the most important notion, saying that an ascending chain of ideals in
R
eventually stabilizes.
Since ideals in
R
corresponds exactly to elements in
\operatorname{Id}(R)
, we have a natural definition for Noetherian in a poset.

Definition 2.18
.

Let
P
be a poset.
Then we say
P
is
Noetherian
if every ascending chain of element
p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq\cdots
in
P
eventually stabilizes, i.e., there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
p_{N}=p_{N+1}=p_{N+2}=\cdots

The next lemma shows that under Noetherian assumption, a semilattice is equivalent to a complete semilattice.

Lemma 2.19
.

A Noetherian semilattice is complete.

Proof.

Let
L
be a Noetherian semilattice and pick
S\subset L
.
We claim that
\sum S
exists and is equal to
\sum S_{0}
for some finite subset
S_{0}\subset S
.
Assume the contrary, then we produce an ascending chain that does not stabilize in the following procedure.

Pick any
s_{1}\in S
.
If
s_{1}\geq s
for all
s\in S
, then
\sum S=s_{1}
and we arrive at a contradiction.
Thus, there exists
s_{2}\in S
such that
s_{2}\not\leq s_{1}
.

If
s_{1}+s_{2}\geq s
for all
s\in S
, then
\sum S=s_{1}+s_{2}
and we arrive at a contradiction.
Thus, there exists
s_{3}\in S
such that
s_{3}\not\leq s_{1}+s_{2}
.

Repeat this procedure, we get an ascending chain
s_{1}<s_{1}+s_{2}<s_{1}+s_{2}+s_{3}<\cdots
that does not stabilize, contradicts the assumption that
L
is Noetherian.
∎

Thanks to
Lemma

2.19
, when we talk about Noetherian in a semilattice we can assume the lattice is complete.
Now let us give some equivalent characterizations of Noetherian.
To simplify our notations, let us introduce a convention first.

Definition 2.20
.

Let
L
be a complete semilattice and
x\in L,{\left\{x_{i}\right\}}_{i\in I}\subset L
.
Then we write
x\leq^{*}\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}
if
x\leq\sum_{i\in I_{0}}x_{i}

I_{0}\subset I
.
Also, we write
x=^{*}\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}
if
x\geq x_{i}
for all

and
x=\sum_{i\in I_{0}}x_{i}
for some finite subset
I_{0}\subset I
.

Proposition 2.21
.

Let
L
be a complete semilattice.
The following are equivalent:

(a)

L
is Noetherian.

(b)

Every subset
S\subset L
has a maximal element, i.e., there exists
s\in S
such that for

, the condition

implies
s=t
.

(c)

For
x\in L,{\left\{x_{i}\right\}}_{i\in I}\subset L

x\leq\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}
, then
x\leq^{*}\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}
.

(d)

For
x\in L,{\left\{x_{i}\right\}}_{i\in I}\subset L
, if
x=\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}
, then
x=^{*}\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}
.

Proof.

(a)
\Rightarrow
(b): let
S\subset L
be a subset.
Assume the contrary,
S
does not admit a maximal element, i.e., for all
s\in S
, there exists
t\in S
such that
s<t
.

Pick any
s_{1}\in S
, then there exists
s_{2}\in S
such that
s_{1}<s_{2}
, and there exists
s_{3}\in S
such that
s_{2}<s_{3}
.
Repeat this procedure, we get an ascending chain
s_{1}<s_{2}<s_{3}<\cdots
that does not stabilze, contradicts the assumption that
L
is Noetherian.

(b)
\Rightarrow
(c): let
S={\left\{\sum_{i\in I_{0}}x_{i}:I_{0}\subset I\text{ is finite}\right\}}
.
Then
S
has a maximal element
\sum_{j\in J}x_{j}
, where
J\subset I
is finite.
Now for all
i\in I
, if
x_{i}\not\leq\sum_{j\in J}x_{j}
, then
\sum_{j\in J\cup{\left\{i\right\}}}x_{j}>\sum_{j\in J}x_{j}

\sum_{j\in J}x_{j}
.

Thus,

for all

\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\leq\sum_{j\in J}x_{j}

(c)
\Rightarrow

.
Now

Thus,

(d)
\Rightarrow
(a): pick an ascending chain

Let
x=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}x_{i}

.
Now let

, then

since
x_{i}\leq x_{i+1}
for all

.
Thus,
x_{N}\geq x_{i}
for all

, hence the chain

∎

3.
Multiplicative Filters and Localization

Localization is one of the most fundamental construction in commutative algebra.
Thus, we want to carry it to the realm of quantales and quantale modules.

Throughout this section, we assume
Q
is a quantale.

3.1.
Definitions of Multiplicative Filters

Similar to the multiplicative subsets used in localization in rings, we perform the definition of a multiplicative filter:

Definition 3.1
.

A
multiplicative filter
, or in short
m-filter
, of a quantale
Q
is a subset
\mathscr{F}\subset Q
satisfying:

(a)

(Nonempty)
1\in\mathscr{F}
.

(b)

(Upper closeness) If
a\in\mathscr{F},b\in Q_{\geq a}
then
b\in\mathscr{F}
.

(c)

(Multiplicative closeness) If
a,b\in\mathscr{F}
, then
ab\in\mathscr{F}
.

Definition 3.2
.

For a set
S\subset Q
, the
multiplicative filter generated by

S
is the smallest multiplicative filter (with respect to inclusion)
\mathscr{F}(S)
containing all elements in
S
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For
S\subset Q
, we have

\mathscr{F}(S)={\left\{q\in Q:q\geq\prod_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\text{ for some }n\in\mathbb{N},s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\in S\right\}}.

Proof.

The set defined above is clearly upper closed. Suppose
p,q
are both in the set, with
q\geq\prod_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}
,
p\geq\prod_{j=1}^{m}t_{j}
,
s_{i},t_{j}\in S
, then
pq\geq\prod_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}
, with right hand side being finite product of element in
S
, thus we have the set being closed under multiplication. Moreover, for
S\neq{\left\{0\right\}}
, we have
1
in the set.

On the other hand, as
S\subset\mathscr{F}(S)
, we know all finite product of element in
S
must lie in
\mathscr{F}(S)
, and as
\mathscr{F}(S)
is upperclosed we know the set described above lies in
\mathscr{F}(S)
, and as it is indeed a multiplicative filter, it is
\mathscr{F}(S)
.
∎

There are two natural operations on the multiplicative filters: taking the set-theoretic product
\mathscr{F}({\left\{ab:a\in\mathscr{F}_{1},b\in\mathscr{F}_{2}\right\}})
, and the set-theoretic sum
{\left\{a+b:a\in\mathscr{F}_{1},b\in\mathscr{F}_{2}\right\}}
. The following lemma shows that the set-theoretic sum is the same as intersection.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mathscr{F},\mathscr{G}
be m-filters of
Q
.
Then
\mathscr{F}\cap\mathscr{G}={\left\{a+b:a\in\mathscr{F},b\in\mathscr{G}\right\}}
.

Proof.

\subset
: pick
x\in\mathscr{F}\cap\mathscr{G}
, then
x=x+x
and
x\in\mathscr{F},x\in\mathscr{G}
, so LHS is in RHS.

\supset
: pick
a\in\mathscr{F},b\in\mathscr{G}
, then
a+b\in\mathscr{F},a+b\in\mathscr{G}
, hence
a+b\in\mathscr{F}\cap\mathscr{G}
.
Thus, RHS is in LHS.
∎

The notion of m-filter also gives us a way to construct new quantales from the existing ones.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\operatorname{mF}(Q)
be the set of multiplicative filter on a quantale
Q
, then
\operatorname{mF}(Q)
admits a natural quantale structure under
\subset
and multiplication
\mathscr{F}\cdot\mathscr{G}=\mathscr{F}\cap\mathscr{G}
.
The addition is given by
\sum_{i\in I}\mathscr{F}_{i}
being the m-filter generated by
\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathscr{F}_{i}
.
Moreover,
\operatorname{mF}(Q)
is an idempotent quantale with least element
{\left\{1\right\}}
.

Proof.

Commutativity and complete semilattice structure follows from the definition, and the element
Q\in\operatorname{mF}(Q)
acts as the multiplicative unit. Thus, we only need to consider distributivity.

Suppose
q\in\mathscr{G}\big(\sum_{i\in I}\mathscr{F}_{i}\big)
, then there exists
I_{0}\subset I
finite, elements
b\in\mathscr{G}
and
a_{i}\in\mathscr{F}_{i}
for
i\in I_{0}
, satisfying
q\geq b+\prod_{i\in I_{0}}a_{i}
. Thus,
\prod_{i\in I_{0}}(b+a_{i})=bc+\prod_{i\in I_{0}}a_{i}\leq b+\prod_{i\in I_{0}}\leq 1
for some
c
, and
q\in\sum_{i\in I}\mathscr{G}\mathscr{F}_{i}
.

Conversely, suppose
q\in\sum_{i\in I}\mathscr{G}\mathscr{F}_{i}
, then there exists
I_{0}\subset I
finite, elements
b_{i}\in\mathscr{G}
and
a_{i}\in\mathscr{F}_{i}
for
i\in I_{0}
, satisfying
q\geq\prod(b_{i}+a_{i})
. Since
\prod_{i\in I_{0}}b_{i}+\prod_{i\in I_{0}}a_{i}\leq\prod_{i\in I_{0}}(b_{i}+a_{i})\leq q
and
\prod_{i\in I_{0}}b_{i}\in\mathscr{G}
, we have
q\in\mathscr{G}\big(\sum_{i\in I}\mathscr{F}_{i}\big)
.

Thus,
\operatorname{mF}(Q)
satisfies all axioms for quantale, thus it is a quantale.
Finally, for
\mathscr{F}\in\operatorname{mF}(Q)
, we have
\mathscr{F}\cdot\mathscr{F}=\mathscr{F}\cap\mathscr{F}=\mathscr{F}
, hence
\operatorname{mF}(Q)
is an idempotent quantale by
2.8
.
Obviously
{\left\{1\right\}}\in\operatorname{mF}(Q)
is less than all other elements of
\operatorname{mF}(Q)
.
∎

We would now give some classes of m-filters.

Example 3.6
.

The

is
{\left\{1\right\}}\subset Q
.

Example 3.7
.

The whole quantale
Q
is a m-filter of
Q
.

When we specify at rings, here’s also multiplicative filter generated from multiplicative sets:

Example 3.8
.

For a ring
R
and a multiplicative closed subset

\mathscr{N}={\left\{J\in\operatorname{Id}(R):J\cap S\neq\varnothing\right\}}

3.2.
Examples of Filters

Definition 3.9
.

Let
f\in Q
.
Then we use
\mathscr{F}_{f}
to denote the
minimal filter
containing
f
.
More concretely,
\mathscr{F}_{f}={\left\{q\in Q:f^{n}\leq q\text{ for some }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}}
.

Example 3.10
.

Let
Q
be an idempotent quantale, then
\mathscr{F}_{f}=Q_{\geq f}
(as sets).

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
f,g\in Q
, we have
\mathscr{F}_{f}\mathscr{F}_{g}=\mathscr{F}_{f+g}
and
\mathscr{F}_{f}+\mathscr{F}_{g}=\mathscr{F}_{fg}
.

Proof.

Suppose
h\in\mathscr{F}_{f}\mathscr{F}_{g}
, then
h\geq f^{n},h\geq g^{m}
for some
m,n\geq 0
, thus
h\geq(f+g)^{n+m}=f^{n}P_{1}(f,g)+g^{m}P_{2}(f,g)
for some polynomials
P_{1},P_{2}
. Suppose
h\in\mathscr{F}_{fg}
, then
h\geq(f+g)^{n}
gives
h\geq f^{n}
and
h\geq g^{n}
.

Suppose
h\in\mathscr{F}_{f}+\mathscr{F}_{g}
, then
h=h_{f}h_{g}
such that
h_{f}\geq f^{n},h_{g}\geq g^{m}
, thus
h\geq f^{n}g^{m}\geq(fg)^{n+m}
. Suppose
h\in\mathscr{F}_{fg}
, we have
h\geq(fg)^{n}
, thus
h+f^{n}\geq f^{n},h+g^{n}\geq g^{n}
, thus
(h+f^{n})(h+g^{n})=h(h+f^{n}+g^{n})+f^{n}g^{n}\in\mathscr{F}_{f}+\mathscr{F}_{g}
, thus
h\in\mathscr{F}_{f}+\mathscr{F}_{g}
as it is a m-filter.
∎

Example 3.12
.

Let
R
be a commutative unital ring and
Q=\operatorname{Id}(R)
.
Then for
s\in R
, we have
\mathscr{F}_{(s)}={\left\{J\in Q:s\in\sqrt{J}\right\}}
.

Recall two ideals
I,J
in a ring
R
are comaximal if
I+J=R
.
It turns out that the notion of comaximal actually gives a m-filter in a quantale.
To see this, we first need a lemma.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
a,b,c\in Q
.
If
a+b=1,a+c=1
, then
a+bc=1
.

Proof.

We have
1=(a+b)(a+c)=a(a+b+c)+bc\leq a+bc
.
∎

This simple lemma has some surprising applications.

Corollary 3.14
.

Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in Q
.
If
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=1
, then
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{m}=1
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

First
a_{1}+(a_{2}+a_{3}+\cdots+a_{n})=1
, so
a_{1}^{m}+(a_{2}+a_{3}+\cdots+a_{n})=1
by
Lemma

3.13
.
Now
a_{2}+(a_{1}^{m}+a_{3}+a_{4}+\cdots+a_{n})=1
, so
a_{2}^{m}+(a_{1}^{m}+a_{3}+a_{4}+\cdots+a_{n})=1
by
Lemma

3.13
.
Repeat this procedure, we will finally get
a_{1}^{m}+\cdots+a_{n}^{m}=1
.
∎

Corollary 3.15
.

Let
R
be a ring and
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in R
such that
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})=R
.
Then
(f_{1}^{m},\ldots,f_{n}^{m})=R
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Apply
3.14
to
Q=\operatorname{Id}(R)
and
a_{i}=(f_{i})
.
∎

Definition 3.16
.

Let
a\in Q
.
Then we define the
comaximal filter

\mathscr{F}_{\perp a}={\left\{q\in Q:q+a=1\right\}}
.
We have
\mathscr{F}_{\perp a}
is a filter by Lemma
3.13
.

Example 3.17
.

Let
X
be a topological space
Q=\mathscr{O}(X)
.
Then for
U\in Q
, we have
\mathscr{F}_{\perp U}={\left\{V\in Q:U\cup V=X\right\}}={\left\{V\in Q:U^{c}\subset V\right\}}
.

We now exhibit a class of m-filters that will play a crucial role in the proof of Baire Category Theorem.

Definition 3.18
.

Let
a\in Q
.
Then we define the
codense filter

\mathscr{F}_{\nmid a}={\left\{q\in Q:qx\leq a\text{ implies }x\leq a\text{ for all }x\in Q\right\}}.

Lemma 3.19
.

The codense filter
\mathscr{F}_{\nmid a}
is a m-filter in
Q
for all
a\in Q
.

Proof.

To see upper closeness, note that if
q\in\mathscr{F}_{\nmid a},q^{\prime}\geq q
, and
q^{\prime}x\leq a
, then
qx\leq q^{\prime}x\leq a
, so
x\leq a
.
To see multiplicative closeness, note that if
q,q^{\prime}\in\mathscr{F}_{\nmid a}
and
qq^{\prime}x\leq a
, then
q^{\prime}x\leq a
, so
x\leq a
.
∎

There is a special case where the codense filter is easy to describe.

Definition 3.20
.

An element
p\in Q
is
prime
(or a
prime element
) if
p\neq 1
and
ab\leq p
implies
a\leq p
or
b\leq p
for all
a,b\in Q
.

Example 3.21
.

Let
R
be a ring.
Then
\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Id}(R)
is prime if and only if
\mathfrak{p}
is a prime ideal.

Proposition 3.22
.

Let
p\in Q
be a prime element.
Then
\mathscr{F}_{\nmid p}={\left\{q\in Q:q\not\leq p\right\}}
.

Proof.

\subset
: pick
q\in\mathscr{F}_{\nmid p}
.
If
q\leq p
, then
q\cdot 1\leq p
, so
1\leq p
, contradicts with the definition of prime element.
Thus,
q\not\leq p
.

\supset
: pick any
q\not\leq p
.
If
qa\leq p
, then either
q\leq p
or
a\leq p
.
However,
q\not\leq p
, so
a\leq p
.
Thus,
q\in\mathscr{F}_{\nmid p}
.
∎

In particular, if we consider m-filter associated with a ring, then
\mathscr{F}_{\nmid\mathfrak{p}}
is the usual localization at
\mathfrak{p}
:

Example 3.23
.

For a ring
R
and prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Id}(R)
, the m-filter
{\left\{J\in\operatorname{Id}(R):J\cap(R-\mathfrak{p})\neq\varnothing\right\}}
is just

.

The name codense comes from topology.

Example 3.24
.

Let
X
be a topological space and consider the quantale
\mathscr{O}(X)
.
Then
\mathscr{F}_{\nmid\varnothing}={\left\{U\in\mathscr{O}(X):U\text{ is dense in }X\right\}}
.
Thus,
Lemma

3.19
tells us the intersection of two open dense subsets is again open dense.

We can generalize this observation to any codense filter in
\mathscr{O}(X)
.

Proposition 3.25
.

Let
X
be a topological space and
V\in\mathscr{O}(X)
.
Then

\mathscr{F}_{\nmid V}={\left\{U\in\mathscr{O}(X):U\cap V^{c}\text{ is dense in }V^{c}\right\}}.

Proof.

\subset
: pick
U\in\mathscr{F}_{\nmid V}
.
If
U\cap(X-V)
is not dense in
X-V
, then there exists
W\in\mathscr{O}(X)
such that
(U\cap V^{c})\cap(W\cap V^{c})=\varnothing
and
W\cap V^{c}\neq\varnothing
.
Then
(U\cap W)\cap V^{c}=\varnothing
, hence
U\cap W\subset V
.
However,
W\cap V^{c}\neq\varnothing
tells us
W\not\subset V
, so
U\not\in\mathscr{F}_{\nmid V}
, a contradiction.

\supset
: if
U\cap W\subset V
, then
(U\cap V^{c})\cap(W\cap V^{c})\subset V\cap V^{c}=\varnothing
, so because
U\cap V^{c}
is dense in
V^{c}
we see
W\cap V^{c}=\varnothing
, or
W\subset V
.
∎

3.3.
Localization

After defining the multiplicative filter, we could give the definition of localization. Before it, we first give the relation of local order:

Definition 3.26
.

For
a,b\in M
, we say
a
is
locally less than

b

in one step
, or
a\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}b
, if there exists index set
I
,
a_{i}\in Q,s_{i}\in\mathscr{F}
for
i\in I
, such that
a\leq\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}
and
s_{i}a_{i}\leq b
for all
i\in I
.

We say
a
is
locally less than

b

in
n
step
, or
a\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{n}b
, if there exists
c_{1},\cdots,c_{n-1}\in Q
such that
a\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}c_{1}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}\cdots\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}c_{n-1}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}b
, and
a
is
locally less than

b
, or
a\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}b
, if there exists such
n
. When there’s no confusion, we could omit the
\mathscr{F}
subscript.

Proposition 3.27
.

The local order is a preorder.

Proof.

Clearly we have
a\preceq a
. Suppose
a\preceq b
and
b\preceq c
, we have
a\preceq^{n}b
and
b\preceq^{m}c
for some
n,m
, thus
a\preceq^{n+m}c
and
a\preceq c
.
∎

Definition 3.28
.

The
localization
of
M
at
\mathscr{F}
, written
M_{\mathscr{F}}
is the set
M/\sim
, where
a\sim b
if and only if
a\preceq b
and
b\preceq a
.

Lemma 3.29
.

Suppose
a_{i}\preceq^{1}b_{i}
for
i\in I
, then
\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\preceq^{1}\sum_{i\in I}b_{i}
.

Proof.

By definition of local order, we have
a_{i}\leq\sum_{j\in J_{i}}a_{ij},s_{ij}a_{ij}\leq b_{i}
for index set
J_{i}
and
s_{ij}\in\mathscr{F}
. Thus, we have
\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\leq\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{j\in J_{i}}a_{ij},s_{ij}a_{ij}\leq b_{i}\leq\sum_{i\in I}b_{i}
, which shows
\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\preceq^{1}\sum_{i\in I}b_{i}
.
∎

Lemma 3.30
.

Suppose
b\preceq^{1}c
, then
ab\preceq^{1}ac
for any
a\in Q
.

Proof.

We have
b\leq\sum_{i\in I}b_{i}
with
s_{i}b_{i}\leq c
for some
s_{i}\in\mathscr{F}
. Thus, we have
ab\leq\sum_{i\in I}ab_{i}
with
s_{i}(ab_{i})\leq ac
.
∎

Lemma 3.31
.

We have

(a)

Q_{\mathscr{F}}
is a prequantale under addition
\overline{p}+\overline{q}=\overline{p+q}
and multiplication
\overline{p}\cdot\overline{q}=\overline{pq}
.

(b)

M_{\mathscr{F}}
is a
Q_{\mathscr{F}}
-premodule under addition
\overline{a}+\overline{b}=\overline{a+b}
and multiplication
\overline{q}\cdot\overline{a}=\overline{qa}
.

Proof.

Part (a): for addition in both case, suppose
x\preceq z
and
y\preceq z
, we have
x\preceq^{1}x_{1}\preceq^{1}\cdots\preceq^{1}x_{n-1}\preceq^{1}z
and
y\preceq^{1}y_{1}\preceq^{1}\cdots\preceq^{1}y_{m-1}\preceq^{1}z
, thus we have
x+y\preceq^{1}x_{1}+y_{1}\preceq^{1}\cdots\preceq^{1}z+z=z
in at most
\max(m,n)
step, thus
\overline{z}\geq\overline{x+y}
is equivalent to
\overline{z}\geq\overline{x}
and
\overline{z}\geq\overline{y}
, so
\overline{x}+\overline{y}=\overline{x+y}
.

For multiplication, it suffices to show
ab\preceq ac
provided that
b\preceq c
, since distribution comes from definition.
After writing
b\preceq^{1}b_{1}\preceq^{1}b_{2}\preceq^{1}\cdots\preceq^{1}b_{n-1}\preceq^{1}c
, it suffices to show
ab\preceq^{1}ac
provided that
b\preceq^{1}c
, which is fulfilled in
Lemma

3.30
.
∎

Since this paper’s main focus is quantale/module, we wonder when
M_{\mathscr{F}}
is complete.

Proposition 3.32
.

Let
M
be a
Q
-module and
\mathscr{F}\subset Q
be a m-filter.
The following are equivalent:

(a)

For all
b\in M,{\left\{a_{i}\right\}}_{i\in I}\subset M
, if
a_{i}\preceq b
for all
i\in I
, then
\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\preceq b
.

(b)

For all
b\in M
, there exists
n_{b}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}b
implies
a\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{n_{b}}b
for all
a\in M
.

Proof.

(a)
\Rightarrow
(b): Assume for an element
b\in M
, there is no such
n_{b}
, then there exists a sequence of elements
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset M
, such that
a_{i}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}b
, but
a_{i}\not\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{i-1}b
for all
i
.

Consider
a=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}a_{i}
, then
a\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}b
by assumption. Suppose
a\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{n}b
, then
a_{n+1}\leq a
, thus
a_{n+1}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{n}b
, a contradiction.

(b)
\Rightarrow
(a): Suppose for
i\in I
, we have
a_{i}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}x_{i1}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}\cdots\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}x_{i(n_{b}-1)}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}b
, then
\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}\sum_{i\in I}x_{i1}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}\cdots\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}\sum_{i\in I}x_{i(n_{b}-1)}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}\sum_{i\in I}b=b
.
∎

Definition 3.33
.

When a m-filter
\mathscr{F}\subset Q
satisfies any of the equivalent condition in
3.32
we say
\mathscr{F}
is
localizable
over
M
.
In case of
M=Q
we omit “over
M
” and simply say
\mathscr{F}
is localizable.

In fact, although most of multiplicative filters we meet would be localizable, there exist non-localizable filters.

Example 3.34
.

Let
Q
be the quantale
L_{0}\sqcup L_{1}\sqcup L_{2}
, where
L_{0}\cong\mathbb{L}_{\omega}
,
L_{1}
be the collection of all ordinals at most
\omega^{2}
, and
L_{2}={\left\{\mathbf{0}\right\}}
being the bottom. The addition is defined by a total order, with
x>y
for
x\in L_{i},y\in L_{j},i<j
, and the order in each layer is naturally defined. The multiplication is defined as
xy\in L_{\min(i+j,2)}
for
x\in L_{i},y\in L_{j}
, and for
0\geq x\in L_{0}
,
y=a\omega^{2}+b\omega+c\in L_{1}
(either
a=0
or
a=1,b=c=0
), we have
xy=a\omega^{2}+b\omega+\max(c+x,0)
.

Proposition 3.35
.

Let
\mathscr{F}=L_{0}
be a multiplicative filter inside the above quantale
Q
, then
\mathscr{F}
is not localizable over
Q
.

Proof.

Consider
x_{n}=n\omega\in L_{1}
, then suppose
x_{n}\leq\sum_{i\in I}x_{ni},s_{ni}x_{ni}\leq y
for
y\leq x_{n}
, then we must have
x_{ni}\in L_{1}\cup L_{2}
, thus there exists
i\in I
such that
x_{ni}\geq(n-1)\omega
. (otherwise, the supremum would be at most
(n-1)\omega
.) However,
s\big((n-1)\omega\big)=(n-1)\omega
for all
s\in\mathscr{F}
, thus
y\geq x_{n-1}
, and we indeed have
x_{n}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}x_{n-1}
.

Thus, by writing out a chain, we have
x_{n}\preceq^{n}x_{0}
and
x_{n}\not\preceq^{n-1}x_{0}
, thus
\mathscr{F}
is not localizable over
Q
by
3.32
.
∎

We would now show that in case of localizable

is indeed a quantale and

its module.

Lemma 3.36
.

We have

(a)

If
\mathscr{F}
is localizable, then

is a quantale under addition
\sum_{i\in I}\overline{q_{i}}=\overline{\sum_{i\in I}q_{i}}

.

(b)

\mathscr{F}
is localizable over

, then

is a
Q

\sum_{i\in I}\overline{a_{i}}=\overline{\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}}
and action

.

(c)

If
\mathscr{F}
is localizable over

and

, then
M_{\mathscr{F}}
is a
Q_{\mathscr{F}}
-module under action
\overline{q}\cdot\overline{a}=\overline{qa}
.

Proof.

For addition in all cases, suppose the elements be
x_{i}
. With
x_{i}\preceq y
, we have
x_{i}\preceq^{n_{y}}y
, and so
\sum_{i}x_{i}\preceq^{n_{y}}y
by using

\sum_{i\in I}\overline{x_{i}}=\overline{\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}}

For multiplication, we just need to check

, since the distribution comes from definition. Similar with the prequantale case, we could break it into a chain and apply

3.30
.
∎

Example 3.37
.

We have
{\left\{1\right\}}\in\operatorname{mF}(Q)
is localizable over any
Q
-module
M
, and
M_{{\left\{1\right\}}}\cong M
(as
Q
-modules) via isomorphism
M\xrightarrow{\sim}M_{{\left\{1\right\}}},x\mapsto\overline{x}
.

Example 3.38
.

We will see later that when
R
is a ring,
M
is an
R
-module, and
S\subset R
a multiplicatively closed subset, then
\mathscr{N}={\left\{J\in\operatorname{Id}(R):J\cap S\neq\varnothing\right\}}\in\operatorname{mF}(\operatorname{Id}(R))
is localizable over
\operatorname{Id}(R)
and
\operatorname{Sub}_{R}(M)
, and
\operatorname{Id}(R_{S})\cong\operatorname{Id}(R)_{\mathscr{N}}
and
\operatorname{Sub}_{R_{S}}(M_{S})\cong\operatorname{Sub}_{R}(M)_{\mathscr{N}}
(as

-module).
In particular, for prime ideal

.

Lemma 3.39
.

Let
M
be a
Q
-module and pick
\mathscr{F},\mathscr{G}\in\operatorname{mF}(Q)
such that
\mathscr{F}\subset\mathscr{G}
.
For
x,y\in M
such that
x\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}y
, we have
x\preceq_{\mathscr{G}}^{1}y
.

Proof.

We have
x\leq\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}
with
s_{i}x_{i}\leq y
for some

.
Thus,
x\leq\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}
and
s_{i}x_{i}\leq y
, where
s_{i}\in\mathscr{G}
, showing
x\preceq_{\mathscr{G}}^{1}y
, as desired.
∎

Proposition 3.40
.

Let
M
be a
Q
-module and pick
\mathscr{F},\mathscr{G}\in\operatorname{mF}(Q)
such that
\mathscr{F}\subset\mathscr{G}
.
Then

(a)

There is a
Q
-premodule homomorphism
\varphi:M_{\mathscr{F}}\to M_{\mathscr{G}}
.

(b)

If both
\mathscr{F}
and
\mathscr{G}
are localizable over
M
, then
\varphi
is a
Q
-module homomorphism.

Proof.

Part (a): we first show
\varphi
is well-defined.
To see this, pick
a,b\in M
such that
a\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}b
.
Then
a\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}x_{1}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}x_{2}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}\cdots\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}x_{n-1}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}b
.
Thus,
a\preceq_{\mathscr{G}}^{1}x_{1}\preceq_{\mathscr{G}}^{1}x_{2}\preceq_{\mathscr{G}}^{1}\cdots\preceq_{\mathscr{G}}^{1}x_{n-1}\preceq_{\mathscr{G}}^{1}b
by
Lemma

3.39
, showing
a\preceq_{\mathscr{G}}b
, hence
\varphi
is well-defined (as map between sets).

To check that
\varphi
preserves finite addition, just note that for
a,b\in M
, we have
\varphi(\overline{a}+\overline{b})=\varphi(\overline{a+b})=\overline{a+b}=\overline{a}+\overline{b}=\varphi(\overline{a})+\varphi(\overline{b})
, as desired.

To check that
\varphi
preserves multiplication, just note that for
a\in M,q\in Q
, we have
\varphi(q\overline{a})=\varphi(\overline{qa})=\overline{qa}=q\overline{a}=q\varphi(\overline{a})
, as desired.

Part (b): by (the proof of) Part (a)
\varphi
is well defined and preserves multiplication.

To check that
\varphi
preserves addition, just note that for
a_{i}\in M
, we have
\varphi(\sum_{i\in I}\overline{a_{i}})=\varphi(\overline{\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}})=\overline{\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}}=\sum_{i\in I}\overline{a_{i}}=\sum_{i\in I}\varphi(\overline{a_{i}})
.
∎

Let
\mathscr{F},\mathscr{G}\in\operatorname{mF}(Q)
.
If
\mathscr{F}
is localizable over
M
, then we obtain a
Q
-module
M_{\mathscr{F}}
, so we can localize it at
\mathscr{G}
to obtain
(M_{\mathscr{F}})_{\mathscr{G}}
.
Similarly, if
\mathscr{G}
is localizable over
M
, then we obtain
(M_{\mathscr{G}})_{\mathscr{F}}
.
A natural question to ask is when they are
Q
-module and if so do they agree?
We are now going to show that when all
\mathscr{F},\mathscr{G},\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}
are localizable over
M
, then we have an isomorphism between
Q
-modules
(M_{\mathscr{F}})_{\mathscr{G}}\cong(M_{\mathscr{G}})_{\mathscr{F}}
.

Lemma 3.41
.

Let
M,N
be
Q
-modules with
Q
-linear map
\varphi:M\to N
.
Pick
\mathscr{F}\in\operatorname{mF}(Q)
such that
sv=v
for all
s\in\mathscr{F}
and
v\in N
.
Then

(a)

\varphi
factors through
M_{\mathscr{F}}
to give a homomorphism between
Q
-premodules
\widetilde{\varphi}:M_{\mathscr{F}}\to N,\overline{x}\mapsto\varphi(x)
.

(b)

If
\mathscr{F}
is localizable over
M
, then
\widetilde{\varphi}
is a
Q
-module homomorphism.

Proof.

It suffices to show that when
\overline{x}\leq\overline{y}
in
M_{\mathscr{F}}
, we have
\varphi(x)\leq\varphi(y)
.
It suffices to show this when
x\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}y
.
However, then
x\leq\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}
and
s_{i}x_{i}\leq y
for some
s_{i}\in\mathscr{F}
, so
\varphi(y)\geq\sum_{i\in I}s_{i}\varphi(x_{i})=\sum_{i\in I}\varphi(x_{i})\geq\varphi(x)
, as desired.
∎

Lemma 3.42
.

Let
\mathscr{F},\mathscr{G}\in\operatorname{mF}(Q)
such that
\mathscr{F}
is localizable over
M
.
For
x,y\in M
, if
\overline{x}\preceq_{\mathscr{G}}\overline{y}
in
M_{\mathscr{F}}
, then
x\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}y
in
M
.

Proof.

We have
\overline{x}\preceq_{\mathscr{G}}^{1}\overline{x_{1}}\preceq_{\mathscr{G}}^{1}\cdots\preceq_{\mathscr{G}}^{1}\overline{x_{n-1}}\preceq_{\mathscr{G}}^{1}\overline{y}
in
M_{\mathscr{F}}
for some
x_{i}\in M
.
Thus, it suffices to show that for
a,b\in M
satisfy
\overline{a}\preceq_{\mathscr{G}}^{1}\overline{b}
in
M_{\mathscr{F}}
, we have
a\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}b
.
We have
\overline{a}\leq\sum_{i\in I}\overline{a_{i}}
and
\overline{t_{i}a_{i}}\leq\overline{b}
in
M_{\mathscr{F}}
for some
s_{i}\in\mathscr{G}
.
Thus,
a\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}
and
t_{i}a_{i}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}b
.
Then since
\mathscr{F}
is localizable, we see
\sum_{i\in I}t_{i}a_{i}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}b
.

Now we have
a\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}z_{1}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}z_{2}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}\cdots\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}z_{m-1}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\preceq_{\mathscr{G}}^{1}\sum_{i\in I}t_{i}a_{i}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}z_{1}^{\prime}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}z_{2}^{\prime}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}\cdots\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}z_{m^{\prime}-1}^{\prime}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}b
for some
m,m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N},z_{j},z_{j}^{\prime}\in M
.
Now by
Lemma

3.39
we have
a\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}^{1}z_{1}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}^{1}z_{2}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}^{1}\cdots\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}^{1}z_{m-1}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}^{1}\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}^{1}\sum_{i\in I}t_{i}a_{i}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}^{1}z_{1}^{\prime}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}^{1}z_{2}^{\prime}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}^{1}\cdots\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}^{1}z_{m^{\prime}-1}^{\prime}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}^{1}b
, hence
a\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}b
, as desired.
∎

Proposition 3.43
.

Let
\mathscr{F},\mathscr{G}\in\operatorname{mF}(Q)
.
If both
\mathscr{F}
and
\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}
are localizable over
M
, then
\mathscr{G}
is localizable over
M_{\mathscr{F}}
and we have isomorphism between
Q
-modules
M_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}\xrightarrow{\sim}(M_{\mathscr{F}})_{\mathscr{G}},\overline{x}\mapsto\overline{\overline{x}}
.

Proof.

Using the notation as in
3.32
, for
y\in M
we will show we can pick
n_{\overline{y}}^{\mathscr{G}}=n_{y}^{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}
, where the left hand side corresponds to
\overline{x}\preceq_{\mathscr{G}}\overline{y}
in
M_{\mathscr{F}}
and right hand side corresponds to
x\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}y
in
M
.
To see this, pick
x,y\in M
such that
\overline{x}\preceq_{\mathscr{G}}\overline{y}
in
M_{\mathscr{F}}
.
Then by
Lemma

3.42
we have
x\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}y
.

Thus, we have
x\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}^{1}w_{1}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}^{1}w_{2}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}^{1}\cdots\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}^{1}w_{n_{0}-1}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}^{1}y
, where
n_{0}=n_{y}^{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}
.
We now claim that for
a,b\in M
, we have
a\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}^{1}b
implies
\overline{a}\preceq_{\mathscr{G}}^{1}\overline{b}
in
M_{\mathscr{F}}
, which finishes the first part of the proof that
\mathscr{G}
is localizable over
M_{\mathscr{F}}
.
However, this claim is true because we have
a\leq\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}
with
s_{i}t_{i}a_{i}\leq b
for some
s_{i}\in\mathscr{F},t_{i}\in\mathscr{G}
.
Then in
M_{\mathscr{F}}
, we have
\overline{a}=\sum_{i\in I}\overline{s_{i}a_{i}}
and
t_{i}\overline{s_{i}a_{i}}\leq\overline{b}
, so
\overline{a}\preceq_{\mathscr{G}}\overline{b}
in
M_{\mathscr{F}}
, as desired.

We now prove the second part of this proposition.
Let
Q
-linear map
\varphi:M\to(M_{\mathscr{F}})_{\mathscr{G}}
be the composition
M\to M_{\mathscr{F}}\to(M_{\mathscr{F}})_{\mathscr{G}}
(valid by
3.40
).
Then for any
q\in\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}
and
x\in M
, we claim that
q\overline{\overline{x}}=\overline{\overline{x}}
in
(M_{\mathscr{F}})_{\mathscr{G}}
.
It suffices to show
\overline{x}\preceq_{\mathscr{G}}\overline{qx}
in
M_{\mathscr{F}}
.
However,
q\geq st
for some
s\in\mathscr{F},t\in\mathscr{G}
, so
\overline{x}=\overline{sx}
and
t\overline{sx}\leq\overline{qx}
in
M_{\mathscr{F}}
, as desired.

Therefore, by
Lemma

3.41
we obtain a
Q
-linear map
\widetilde{\varphi}:M_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}\to(M_{\mathscr{F}})_{\mathscr{G}},\overline{x}\mapsto\overline{\overline{x}}
.
We have
\widetilde{\varphi}
is surjective because both
M\to M_{\mathscr{F}}
and
M_{\mathscr{F}}\to(M_{\mathscr{F}})_{\mathscr{G}}
are surjective (so that
\varphi
is surjective).
Thus, to conclude the proof, it suffices to show
\varphi
is injective.

Now pick any
x,y\in M
such that
\overline{\overline{x}}=\overline{\overline{y}}
in
(M_{\mathscr{F}})_{\mathscr{G}}
, then
\overline{x}\preceq_{\mathscr{G}}\overline{y}
in
M_{\mathscr{F}}
, so by
Lemma

3.42
we have
x\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}y
.
Similarly,
y\preceq_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}x
, hence
\overline{x}=\overline{y}
in
M_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}
.
As a result,
\varphi
is injective, as desired.
∎

Now, we exhibit a special kind of multiplicative filters that will be useful later.

Definition 3.44
.

A localizable multiplicative filter
\mathscr{F}
is
1-step over
M
if
a\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}b
implies

, or equivalently,

.

4.
Shrinkable and Suspension

In this section,
Q
is a quantale and
M
a
Q
-module.

4.1.
Definitions and Basic Properties

In many places we have inequalities like
x\leq\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}
.
We wonder when we can “shrink”
x_{i}
’s to make it become an equality.
We want to say that in a “shrinkable” module such inequality can be made into an equality, and we want the usual modules to be shrinkable in order for our theory to be general.

Example 4.1
.

Let
X
be a topological space and
U,U_{i}(i\in I)
be open sets with
U\subset\bigcup_{i\in I}U_{i}
, then
U=\bigcup_{i\in I}(U\cap U_{i})
.

Definition 4.2
.

A map
f:A\to B
between posets is
order-preserving
if
f(a_{1})\leq f(a_{2})
for all
a_{1},a_{2}\in A
such that
a_{1}\leq a_{2}
.

Definition 4.3
.

We say a surjective order-preserving map
f:A\to B
between posets is
shrinkable
if for all
a\in A,b\in B
such that
b\leq f(a)
, there exists
a^{\prime}\in f^{-1}(b)
such that
a^{\prime}\leq a
.

This condition can be explained by the following diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f(a)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{b}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\geq}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\geq}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{-1}}

We now introduce the suspension of a semilattice in order to define what is a shrinkable complete semilattice.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
L
be a semilattice.
Then there is a preorder on the collection of all nonempty subsets of
L
, defined by
S\leq T
if and only if for all
s\in S
, there exists finite
T_{s}\subset T
such that
s\leq\sum T_{s}
.

Proof.

The only nontrivial part is transitivity.
Pick nonempty susbsets
S,W,T
of
L
such that
S\leq W
and
W\leq T
.
Then for all
s\in S
, there exists
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\in W
such that
s\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}
, and for each
1\leq i\leq n
, there exists finite
T_{i}\subset T
such that
w_{i}\leq\sum T_{i}
.
Now take
T_{s}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}
, then
s\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum T_{i}=\sum T_{s}
, so
S\leq T
, as desired.
∎

Definition 4.5
.

Let
L
be a semilattice.
The
suspension
of
L
, denoted by
\Sigma L
, is the quotient
{\left\{\text{nonempty subsets of }L\right\}}/\sim
, where the equivalence is defined by
S\sim T
if and only if
S\leq T
and
T\leq S
, where
\leq
is as in
Lemma

4.4
.

Proposition 4.6
.

We have

(a)

If
L
is a semilattice, then
\Sigma L
is a complete semilattice under addition
\sum_{i\in I}S_{i}=\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i}
.

(b)

If
L
is a complete semilattice, then we have a surjective homomorphism between complete semilattices
\sigma:\Sigma L\to L,S\mapsto\sum_{s\in S}s
.

(c)

If
Q
is a prequantale, then
\Sigma Q
admits a quantale structure, where multiplication is
S\cdot T={\left\{st:s\in S,t\in T\right\}}
.

(d)

If
Q
is a quantale, then
\sigma
is a homomorphism between quantales.

(e)

If
M
is a
Q
-module, then
\Sigma M
is a
\Sigma Q
-module via
S\cdot A={\left\{sa:s\in S,a\in A\right\}}(S\in\Sigma Q,A\in\Sigma M)
.

Proof.

Part (a): Since the partial order in
\Sigma L
is element wise, we have
\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i}\leq T
equivalent to
S_{i}\leq T
for all
T
.

Part (b): We need to check
\sigma(\sum_{i\in I})=\sum_{i\in I}\sigma(S_{i})
. This follows by
\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{s\in S_{i}}s=\sum_{s\in\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i}}s
.

Part (c): Consider
\sum_{i\in I}(S_{i}\cdot T)
and
\left(\sum_{i\in I}S_{i}\right)\cdot T
, they are both in shape
{\left\{st:s\in\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i},t\in T\right\}}
, thus the multiplication is distributive, and
\Sigma Q
is a quantale.

Part (d): As
\sigma(ST)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in S\\
t\in T\end{subarray}}st=\sum_{s\in S}\sum_{t\in T}st=\sigma(S)\sigma(T)
,
\sigma
is indeed a quantale homomorphism given it is a complete semilattice homomorphism.

Part (e): We only need to check the distribution, which is clear by similar proof with part (c).
∎

Definition 4.7
.

Let
L
be a complete semilattice. Then we use
\sigma_{L}:\Sigma L\to L
to denote the canonical map from
\Sigma L
to
L
.
We would omit the subscript if there is no confusion.

For a complete semilattice
L
, note we have a homomorphism between (non-complete) semilattices
\iota:L\to\Sigma L,x\mapsto{\left\{x\right\}}
.
When
L
is a quantale, then
\iota
is a homomorphism between prequantales.
Also, we have
\sigma\circ\iota=\mathrm{id}
and
\iota\circ\sigma(S)\geq S
for all
S\in\Sigma L
.

Finally, let us see the definition for a complete semilattice to be shrinkable.

Definition 4.8
.

We say a complete semilattice
L
is
shrinkable
if
\sigma_{L}:\Sigma L\to L
is shrinkable.

Remark 4.9
.

Unpacking the definition we see a complete semilattice
L
is shrinkable if for
x,x_{i}\in L
with
x\leq\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}
, there exist
{\left\{y_{j}\right\}}_{j\in J}\subset L
such that
x=\sum_{j\in J}y_{j}\leq^{*}\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}
.

Most of the quantales we encounter would be shrinkable.
The rule of thumb is, when you see a “naturally arising” quantale/module, then with high probability it is shrinkable.

Example 4.10
.

Let
Q
be an idempotent quantale, then
Q
is shrinkable.
This is because if
x\leq\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}
, then
x=x^{2}=\sum_{i\in I}(xx_{i})
and
xx_{i}\leq x_{i}
.
In particular,
\mathscr{O}(X)
is shrinkable for all topological space
X
.

Example 4.11
.

Let
P
be an
R
-module.
Then
\operatorname{Sub}_{R}(P)
is shrinkable.
This is because if
N\subset\sum_{i\in I}N_{i}
, then
N=\sum_{n\in N}(n)
and for all
n\in N
, there exists finite
I_{n}\subset I
such that
n\in\sum_{i\in I_{n}}N_{i}
, so that
(n)\subset\sum_{i\in I_{n}}N_{i}
.

Example 4.12
.

Let
L
be a shrinkable semilattice and
x\in L
.
Then
L_{\geq x}
is shrinkable.

In fact, the shrinkable property could simplify the condition for localization.

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
M
be a shrinkable
Q
-module and
\mathscr{F}\in\operatorname{mF}(Q)
.
For
a,b\in M
, if
a\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}b
, then
a=\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}
and
s_{i}a_{i}\leq b
for some
a_{i}\in M,s_{i}\in\mathscr{F}
.

Proof.

We have
a\leq\sum_{j\in J}a_{j}^{\prime}
with
s_{j}^{\prime}a_{j}^{\prime}\leq b
for some
a_{j}^{\prime}\in M,s_{j}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{F}
.
Now since
M
is shrinkable
a=\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}
and
a_{i}\leq^{*}\sum_{J}a_{j}^{\prime}
for some
a_{i}\in M
.
For
i\in I
, pick finite subset
J_{i}\subset J
such that
a_{i}\leq\sum_{j\in J_{i}}a_{j}^{\prime}
and let
s_{i}=\prod_{j\in J_{i}}s_{j}^{\prime}
.
Then
a=\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}
and
s_{i}a_{i}\leq(\prod_{j\in J_{i}}s_{j}^{\prime})(\sum_{j\in J_{i}}a_{j}^{\prime})=\sum_{j\in J_{i}}(s_{j}^{\prime}a_{j}^{\prime}\prod_{j^{\prime}\in J_{i}-{\left\{j\right\}}}s_{j^{\prime}}^{\prime})\leq\sum_{j\in J_{i}}b=b
, as desired.
∎

Proposition 4.14
.

Let
M
be a shrinkable
Q
-module and
\mathscr{F}\in\operatorname{mF}(Q)
.
For
a,b\in M,n\in\mathbb{N}
, if
a\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{n}b
, then there exists
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in M
such that
a\geq x_{1}\geq x_{2}\geq\cdots\geq x_{n},a\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}x_{1}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}x_{2}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}\cdots\preceq_{\mathscr{F}}^{1}x_{n}
, and
x_{n}\leq b
.

Proof.

We use induction on
n
.
If
n=1
, then by
Lemma

4.13

a=\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}
with
s_{i}a_{i}\leq b
for some
a_{i}\in M,s_{i}\in\mathscr{F}
, so we can take
x_{1}=\sum_{i\in I}s_{i}a_{i}
.
Thus, we assume
n>1
.

We have
a\preceq^{1}y_{1}\preceq^{1}y_{2}\preceq^{1}\cdots\preceq^{1}y_{n-1}\preceq^{1}b
for some
y_{k}\in M
.
Then by
Lemma

4.13

a=\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}
with
s_{i}a_{i}\leq y_{1}
for some
a_{i}\in M,s_{i}\in\mathscr{F}
.
We let
x_{1}=\sum_{i\in I}s_{i}a_{i}
, then
a\geq x_{1}
and
a\preceq x_{1}
.
Also,
x_{1}\leq y_{1}
, so
x_{1}\preceq y_{2}
.
Thus, we can apply induction hypothesis to

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{M_{\mathscr{F}+\mathscr{G}}}}

we see the image of

under map

Useful Structures on Quantales, Modules, and Multiplicative Filters

In this section

5.1.
Precoherence

Let us recall the definition of precoherent quantales from
[
1
]
.

Definition 5.1
.

For a complete semilattice
L
and
c\in L
, we say
c
is a
compact element
if
c\leq\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}
implies
c\leq^{*}\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}
for all
x_{i}\in L
.
The collection of compact elements in
L
form a subset called
K(L)
.

Example 5.2
.

We have
1\in K(L)
if and only if
L
is compact.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
L
be a complete semilattice and
a,b\in K(L)
.
Then
a+b\in K(L)
.

Proof.

Suppose
a+b\leq\sum_{i\leq I}c_{i}
, then
a\leq\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}
and
b\leq\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}
. Thus,
a\leq^{*}\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}
and
b\leq^{*}\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}
.
By taking the union of the finite sets, we get
a+b\leq^{*}\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}
, thus
a+b
is compact.
∎

Definition 5.4
.

A complete semilattice
L
is
algebraic
if it is compactly generated, i.e., we have
x=\sum{\left\{c\in K(L):c\leq x\right\}}
for all
x\in L
.

Definition 5.5
.

A quantale
Q
is
precoherent
if

(a)

Q
is algebraic.

(b)

K(Q)
is closed under multiplication, i.e.,
ab\in K(Q)
for all
a,b\in K(Q)
.

Definition 5.6
.

A quantale
Q
is coherent if
Q
is both precoherent and compact.

We can generalize precoherence to
Q
-modules.

Definition 5.7
.

Let
Q
be a precoherent quantale.
A
Q
-module
M
is
precoherent
if

(a)

M
is algebraic.

(b)

cm\in K(M)
for all
c\in K(Q)
and
m\in K(M)
.

As the next proposition shows, we do not have to verify multiplicative closedness for all elements in
K(Q)
in order to show

is precoherent.

Proposition 5.8
.

Suppose
A\subset K(Q)
satisfies:

(a)

x=\sum{\left\{a\in A:a\leq x\right\}}
for all
x\in Q
, and

(b)

aa^{\prime}\in A
for all
a,a^{\prime}\in A
,

then
Q
is precoherent and
c\in K(Q)
if and only if
c=a_{1}+a_{2}+\cdots+a_{n}
for some
a_{i}\in A
.
Let
M
be a
Q
-module with
B\subset K(M)
such that

(a)

x=\sum{\left\{b\in B:b\leq x\right\}}
for all
x\in M
, and

(b)

ab\in B
for all
a\in A,b\in B
,

then
M
is a precoherent
Q
-module and
m\in K(M)
if and only if
m=b_{1}+\cdots+b_{n}
for some
b_{i}\in B
.

Proof.

We first prove the first part.
First pick
c\in K(Q)
.
Then
c=\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}
for some
a_{i}\in A
.
As
c\in K(Q)
, we have
c=^{*}\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}
, so
c=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
for some
a_{i}\in A
.
Conversely, by
Lemma

5.3
we see
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\in K(Q)
provided that
a_{i}\in K(Q)
.

To show

is precoherent, it suffices to show
K(Q)
is closed under multiplication.
However, for
c,c^{\prime}\in K(Q)
, we have
c=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
and

for some

, so

cc^{\prime}=\sum{\left\{a_{i}a_{j}^{\prime}:1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq m\right\}}\in K(Q)
by
Lemma

5.3
.

We now prove the second part.
By the same reason we have
m\in K(M)
if and only if
m
is the summation of finitely many elements in
B
.
Now for
c\in K(Q),m\in K(M)
, we have
c=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
and
m=\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{j}
for some
a_{i}\in A,b_{j}\in B
.
Then
cm=\sum{\left\{a_{i}b_{j}:1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq m\right\}}\in K(M)
by
Lemma

5.3
.
∎

5.2.
Blooming

Let us recall the definition of left adjoint for a order-preserving map.

Definition 5.12
.

Let
A,B
be posets and

be order-preserving maps.
Then we say
L
is
left adjoint
to
R
(or
R
is
right adjoint
to
L
) if for all
a\in A,b\in B
, we have
L(a)\leq b
if and only if
a\leq R(b)
.

We record the following theorem from
[
8
]
.

Theorem 5.13
.

[
8
, Proposition 1.8]

Left adjoint preserves all joins and right adjoint preserves all meets.

Definition 5.14
.

Let
A,B
be posets with order-preserving map
f:B\to A
.
If
f
admits a left adjoint, then we will denote its left adjoint by
f^{\flat}
.

Lemma 5.15
.

Let
A,B
be posets and
f:B\to A
be a surjective order-preserving map.
The following are equivalent:

(a)

f
admits a left adjoint.

(b)

\min f^{-1}(A_{\geq a})
exists for all
a\in A
and
f(\min f^{-1}(A_{\geq a}))=a
.

(c)

f
is shrinkable and
\min f^{-1}(a)
exists for all
a\in A
.

Moreover, if any of these condition is met, then
f^{\flat}(a)=\min f^{-1}(a)
.

Proof.

(a)
\Rightarrow
(b): Consider the adjoint
f^{\flat}
, with
f^{\flat}(a)\leq b
if and only if
a\leq f(b)
. Thus, we have the minimal element inside
f^{-1}(A_{\geq a})
is exactly
f^{\flat}(a)
, and it would map to
a
since
f
is surjective.

(b)
\Rightarrow
(c): Given
f(\min f^{-1}(A_{\geq a}))=a
,
\min f^{-1}(A_{\geq a})
is also the minimal in
f^{-1}(a)
. Moreover, since for all
a^{\prime}\leq f(b)
we have
b^{\prime}=\min f^{-1}(a)
,
b^{\prime}\leq b
, we would have
f
shrinkable.

(c)
\Rightarrow
(a): We define
f^{\flat}
as
\min f^{-1}(a)
, thus by the shrinkable property we know for
a^{\prime}\leq f(b)
we have
f^{\flat}(a^{\prime})\leq b
, and by taking
b=f^{\flat}(a)
we have the order preserving property. Moreover, for
f^{\flat}(a)\leq b
we have
a\leq f(b)
given that
f\circ f^{\flat}
is identity, thus
f^{\flat}
is indeed a left adjoint.
∎

For
\mathscr{F}\in\operatorname{mF}(Q)

.

(b)

Part (b): let

For all

, we have

where all

’s are compact elements.
Then by assumption there exists some finite

.

Let

be a blooming quantale.

Proof.

Clearly the identity

. We just need to check the closedness of addition and product.

Let
\mathscr{F},\mathscr{G}
be solid quantales, then for

, we have

and

with

,

.

Let

be solid quantales for

Suppose

.

Let

We use Zorn’s lemma to pick a maximal

p\geq q
.
To see this is doable, pick a chain

such that

and we want to show

.
Assume the contrary, then there exists finite

\sum C_{0}\in\mathscr{F}
by Proposition

.
However, there exists a maximal element

, from which we see

, a contradiction.

Now we show such an element

is prime.
Assume the contrary, then there exists
a,b>p
such that

.
However, then from maximality of

we see
a,b\in\mathscr{F}
, hence
ab\in\mathscr{F}
, so
p\in\mathscr{F}
, a contradiction.
∎

5.4.
Locally Solid Multiplicative Filters

We are now going to introduce a local version of solid m-filters.

Definition 5.32
.

We say
\mathscr{F}\in\operatorname{mF}(Q)
is
locally solid
if there exists
W\in\sigma^{-1}(1)
such that for all
w\in W
and
S\in\sigma^{-1}(\mathscr{F})
, we have
\iota(tw)\leq S
(in
\Sigma Q
) for some
t\in\mathscr{F}
.

Lemma 5.33
.

For locally solid
\mathscr{F}\in\operatorname{mF}(Q)
, if
W\in\sigma^{-1}(1)
meets the condition in the definition of locally solid and
W^{\prime}\leq W
in
\Sigma Q
(such that
\sigma W^{\prime}=1
), then
W^{\prime}
also meets the condition in the definition of locally solid.

Proof.

For each
w^{\prime}\in W^{\prime}
, we have
w^{\prime}\leq w_{1}+\cdots+w_{n}
for some
w_{i}\in W
.
Pick any
S\in\sigma^{-1}(\mathscr{F})
, then
\iota(t_{i}w_{i})\leq S
for some
t_{i}\in\mathscr{F}
, meaning
t_{i}w_{i}\leq\sum S_{i}
for some finite
S_{i}\subset S
.
Thus, if we take
t=\prod_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}
, then
tw^{\prime}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\prod_{j\neq i}t_{j})t_{i}w_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}w_{i}\leq\sum\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}
, whence
\iota(tw^{\prime})\leq S
in
\Sigma Q
, as desired.
∎

Thus, when
Q
is continuous, in the definition of locally solid it is safe to take
W=\sigma_{Q}^{\flat}(1)
.
Obviously a solid m-filter is locally solid, and the next corollary says the converse holds for compact quantale.

Corollary 5.34
.

Let
Q
be a compact quantale.
Then a locally solid m-filter of
Q
is solid.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{F}\in\operatorname{mF}(Q)
be locally solid and
W\in\sigma^{-1}(1)
witnesses the locally solidity of
\mathscr{F}
.
Then
{\left\{1\right\}}\leq W
since

is compact, hence by
Lemma

5.33
we can take
W={\left\{1\right\}}
.
However, then the condition for locally solid becomes solid.
∎

Proposition 5.35
.

We have
{\left\{1\right\}}\in\operatorname{mF}(Q)
is locally solid if and only if
\min\sigma^{-1}(1)
exists in
\Sigma Q
.

Proof.

\Rightarrow
: let

witnesses the locally solidity of
{\left\{1\right\}}\in\operatorname{mF}(Q)
.
We claim

, which finishes the proof.
To see this, pick any

, then by the definition of locally solid we see

for all

in

\Leftarrow
: let

.
Then for all

Example 5.36
.

Let

be a locally compact topological space, i.e., every point has a compact neighborhood.
Then
{\left\{1\right\}}\in\operatorname{mF}(\mathscr{O}(X))
is locally solid.
This is because every point

admits open

and compact

.
Now

.

{\left\{U_{x}\right\}}_{x\in X}\leq{\left\{V_{i}\right\}}_{i\in I}
in
\Sigma\mathscr{O}(X)

Furthermore, in the setting of regular space

being locally compact is the same as
{\left\{1\right\}}\in\operatorname{mF}(\mathscr{O}(1))
being locally solid, in which case
\mathscr{O}(X)
is blooming.

Theorem 5.37
.

Let
X
be a regular topological space.
The following are equivalent:

(a)

X
is locally compact.

(b)

\mathscr{O}(X)
is blooming.

(c)

{\left\{1\right\}}\in\operatorname{mF}(\mathscr{O}(X))

Proof.

(a)
\Rightarrow
(b): pick any

For all

.
Then we claim

.

.
Thus,

We now argue that for

\sigma^{\flat}(U^{1})\sigma^{\flat}(U^{2})=\sigma^{\flat}(U^{1}\cap U^{2})
.
For

like above (corresponding to

).

Now take any

.
Then for each

, if

, then obviously
V_{x}^{1}\cap V_{y}^{2}\leq^{*}\sum_{i\in I}U_{i}
.
Thus, assume

is compact since it is a closed subset in

.
Thus, there exists finite

such that

, meaning

, as desired.

(b)
\Rightarrow
(c): this is a direct consequence of
5.35
.

(c)
\Rightarrow
(a): by
5.35
we see
W=\min\sigma^{-1}(1)
exists in
\Sigma\mathscr{O}(X)
.
For each
x\in X
, there exists
U\in W
such that
x\in U
.
Then because

is regular we see there exists open neighborhood

and closed neighborhood

such that

(since there are two open sets separating

and

We claim that

is compact, so we are done.

Pick any open cover
C\subset\bigcup_{i\in I}U_{i}

we see

for some finite

.
Then we have

, showing

is compact, as desired.
∎

The Spec like

Lemma 5.38

Let

for all

(where

Let

witnesses the locally solidity of

.
It suffices to show that

for each

.
Let

.
For each

and

We take

to be minimal such positive integer.

Our key claim is that
a=\sum_{i\in I}a_{m_{i}}(i_{\leq m_{i}})
.
Obviously
\sum_{i\in I}a_{m_{i}}(i_{\leq m_{i}})\leq a
, so we now show

.
Assume the contrary,
a\not\leq\sum_{i\in I}a_{m_{i}}(i_{\leq m_{i}})

Then we have
\sum_{i\in I}a_{m_{i}}(i_{\leq m_{i}})=\sum_{j_{1}\in I_{1}}\sum_{i\in I(j_{1})}a_{m_{i}}(i_{\leq m_{i}})

j_{1}\in I_{1}
such that

.

We now have

for each

Now let

However, for

As a result, we see

However, we have

∎

An important example of conormal filter is the dense filter in reduced quantale.

Definition 6.36
.

Let
Q
be a quantale with bottom
0
.
Then we say
Q
is
reduced
if
a^{2}=0
implies
a=0
.
Equivalently,
a^{n}=0
implies
a=0
for all
a\in Q,n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Example 6.37
.

An idempotent quantale is reduced.

Example 6.38
.

Let
R
be a ring, then
\operatorname{Id}(R)
is reduced if and only if
R
is reduced.

Theorem 6.39
.

Let
Q
be a reduced quantale with bottom
0
.
Then the m-filter
\mathscr{F}_{\nmid 0}
is conormal over
Q
.
Moreover,
\overline{x}=\overline{y}
in
Q_{\nmid 0}
if and only if
\hbar(x)=\hbar(y)
, where
\hbar:Q\to Q,q\mapsto\sum{\left\{q^{\prime}\in Q:qq^{\prime}=0\right\}}
.

Proof.

First if
x\leq\sum x_{i}
and
s_{i}x_{i}\leq y
for some
s_{i}\in\mathscr{F}_{\nmid 0}
, then we claim
\hbar(y)\leq\hbar(x)
.
Pick
q\in Q
such that
qy=0
.
Then
s_{i}qx_{i}=0
, so
qx_{i}=0
from definition of
\mathscr{F}_{\nmid 0}
, so
q\sum x_{i}=0
, so
qx=0
, which shows
h(y)\leq h(x)
.
Thus, we see if
\overline{x}=\overline{y}
in
Q_{\nmid 0}
, then
\hbar(x)=\hbar(y)
.

Now pick
x,y\in Q
such that
\hbar(x)=\hbar(y)=q
.
Then we claim

.
If
p(x+q)=0
, then

, so
p\leq q
, so

shows

, hence

since

is reduced.
Thus,

and similarly

.

Now

, and

, which shows
\mathscr{F}_{\nmid 0}
is conormal over

and
\overline{x}=\overline{y}
if and only if
\hbar(x)=\hbar(y)
, as desired.
∎

.

Let

.

7.
Comparisons, Applications, and Conjectures

7.1.
Comparison with Existing Literatures

The idea of localization of quantales has appeared in several literature.
In
[
16
,
18
,
19
]
, for precoherent quantale
Q
(in
[
16
]

Q
is assumed to be an r-lattice, which is stronger than precoherent) and multiplicatively closed subset
S\subset K(Q)
, the authors define a map
Q\to Q,x\mapsto x_{S}
, where
x_{S}=\sum{\left\{c\in K(Q):sc\leq x\text{ for some }s\in S\right\}}=\sum{\left\{y\in Q:y\preceq_{\mathscr{F}(S)}^{1}x\right\}}
.
Since
\mathscr{F}(S)=\sum_{s\in S}\mathscr{F}_{s}
and each
\mathscr{F}_{s}
is solid (by the assumption
S\subset K(Q)
and
5.29
), we see
\mathscr{F}(S)
is solid by
5.30
, hence normal over
Q
by
Theorem

6.13
, so is 1-step over
Q
by
6.17
.
Thus,
x_{S}=\sum{\left\{y\in Q:y\preceq_{\mathscr{F}(S)}x\right\}}
and we see this is precisely the localization of
Q
at
\mathscr{F}(S)
.
Similarly, in
[
13
]
, for coherent quantale
Q
and prime element
p\in Q
, Georgescu define a map
Q\to Q,x\mapsto x_{p}
, where
x_{p}=\sum{\left\{y\in Q:y\preceq_{\mathscr{F}_{\nmid p}}x\right\}}
, so we see this is precisely the localization of
Q
at
\mathscr{F}_{\nmid p}
(which is again solid).

In summary, when defining localization people are mainly concerned with precoherent quantales (and localize at solid m-filters), which is a strong condition and can provide nice properties.
Also, their main focus is quantale rather than their modules.
Therefore, in this paper we generalize the idea of localization to any quantale and their modules.
Since most precoherent quantales arise from algebraic objects, our generalization allows us to apply the theory of localization to geometric objects like topological spaces, and produce some applications as we will immediately see.

For each
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
X\preceq_{\mathscr{F}_{\nmid 0}}U_{i}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}_{\perp U_{i}}}\varnothing
, showing
(Q_{\nmid 0})_{\perp U_{i}}={\left\{\ast\right\}}
, so
Q_{\nmid 0}={\left\{\ast\right\}}
, which contradicts
6.40
.
∎

We recall the non-rigorous “proof” of Baire Category Theorem and show where each point corresponds in the above proof:

(a)

We regard everything up to a nowhere dense subset: consider
\mathscr{O}(X)_{\nmid\varnothing}
.

(b)

Then each nowhere dense subset becomes empty set:
(\mathscr{O}(X)_{\nmid\varnothing})_{\perp U_{i}}={\left\{\ast\right\}}
.

(c)

However, the whole space is not the empty set since the whole space is not nowhere dense:
\mathscr{O}(X)_{\nmid\varnothing}\neq{\left\{\ast\right\}}
.

(d)

We conclude by the observation that a nonempty set is not the union of countably many empty sets:
\mathscr{O}(X)_{\nmid\varnothing}\to\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(\mathscr{O}(X)_{\nmid\varnothing})_{\perp U_{i}}
is injective.

We also address the concerns:

(a)

Why can we regard everything up to a nowhere dense subset?
Because
\mathscr{F}_{\nmid\varnothing}
is conormal (in particular, localizable) over
\mathscr{O}(X)
by
Theorem

6.39
.

(b)

A nonempty set is not the union of arbitrarily many empty sets, so why Baire Category Theorem does not work for arbitrarily many nowhere dense subsets?
Because
Theorem

5.39
only works for countably many m-filters.

(c)

Where did we use the locally compact Hausdorff condition?
Because we want
{\left\{1\right\}}\in\operatorname{mF}(\mathscr{O}(X))
to be locally solid (so that the condition in
Theorem

5.39
is met), so that
X
should be locally compact.
Moreover, we want each
(\mathscr{O}(X)_{\nmid\varnothing})_{\perp U_{i}}
to be trivial, so we want
X
to be regular (and locally compact Hausdorff space is locally compact regular).

We now present two generalizations of Baire Category Theorem.

Theorem 7.10
.

Let
X
be a locally compact space (i.e., every point has a compact neighborhood) and
Y\subset X
a regular subspace.
Then for arbitrary subspaces
{\left\{Z_{k}\right\}}_{k=1}^{\infty}
in
X
, if
\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}Z_{k}=X
, then there exists
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that
Z_{\ell}\cap Y
is not nowhere dense in
Y
.

Proof.

The embedding
Y\hookrightarrow X
induces a quantale homomorphism
\mathscr{O}(X)\to\mathscr{O}(Y),U\mapsto U\cap Y
.
We have
\mathscr{O}(Y)_{\nmid\varnothing}
is a
\mathscr{O}(Y)
-module, so can be pulled back to a
\mathscr{O}(X)
-module with action
U\cdot\overline{V}=\overline{U\cap V\cap Y}
for
U\in\mathscr{O}(X),V\in\mathscr{O}(Y)
.

Now for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathscr{F}_{k}={\left\{U\in\mathscr{O}(X):Z_{k}\subset U\right\}}\in\operatorname{mF}(\mathscr{O}(X))
, then
\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathscr{F}_{k}={\left\{1\right\}}
is locally solid.
Assume the contrary, then inside
\mathscr{O}(Y)
, we have
Y\preceq_{\mathscr{F}_{\nmid\varnothing}}^{1}Y-\operatorname{cl}_{Y}(Z_{k}\cap Y)\preceq_{\mathscr{F}_{k}}^{1}\varnothing
since
Y
is regular, so
Y\preceq_{\mathscr{F}_{k}}^{1}\varnothing
in
\mathscr{O}(Y)_{\nmid\varnothing}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Thus, inside
\mathscr{O}(Y)_{\nmid\varnothing}
we have
\overline{Y}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}_{k}}\overline{\varnothing}
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, hence
\overline{Y}\preceq_{{\left\{1\right\}}}^{1}\overline{\varnothing}
by
Lemma

5.38
, which contradicts
6.40
.
∎

The proof of next theorem follows from very similar routine.

Theorem 7.11
.

Let
X
be a locally compact Hausdorff space with closed subspaces
{\left\{C_{k}\right\}}_{k=1}^{\infty}
such that
X=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}C_{k}
.
Let
f:Y\to X
be a continuous map, then there exists
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{-1}(C_{\ell})\subset Y
is not nowhere dense.

Proof.

f
induces a quantale homomorphism
\mathscr{O}(f):\mathscr{O}(X)\to\mathscr{O}(Y),U\mapsto f^{-1}(U)
, so we can view
\mathscr{O}(Y)_{\nmid\varnothing}
as a
\mathscr{O}(X)
-module.
For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathscr{F}_{k}={\left\{U\in\mathscr{O}(X):C_{k}\subset U\right\}}\in\operatorname{mF}(\mathscr{O}(X))
, then
\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathscr{F}_{k}={\left\{1\right\}}
is locally solid.

Assume the contrary, then inside
\mathscr{O}(Y)
we have
Y\preceq_{\mathscr{F}_{\nmid\varnothing}}^{1}Y-f^{-1}(C_{k})
, and we claim
Y-f^{-1}(C_{k})\preceq_{\mathscr{F}_{k}}^{1}\varnothing
.
It suffices to show
\bigcap_{U\supset C_{k}}\operatorname{cl}f^{-1}(U)=f^{-1}(C_{k})
, where in the intersection
U\subset X
is open.
Obviously
f^{-1}(C_{k})\subset\bigcap_{U\supset C_{k}}\operatorname{cl}f^{-1}(U)
.
For the other direction, just note that
\operatorname{cl}f^{-1}(U)\subset f^{-1}(\operatorname{cl}U)
and
\bigcap_{U\supset C_{k}}\operatorname{cl}U=C_{k}
since
X
is regular.
Thus,
\bigcap_{U\supset C_{k}}\operatorname{cl}f^{-1}(U)\subset\bigcap_{U\supset C_{k}}f^{-1}(\operatorname{cl}U)=f^{-1}(\bigcap_{U\supset C_{k}}\operatorname{cl}U)=f^{-1}(C_{k})
, as desired.

As a result, we see
\overline{Y}\preceq_{{\left\{1\right\}}}^{1}\overline{\varnothing}
in
\mathscr{O}(Y)_{\nmid\varnothing}
by
Lemma

5.38
, which contradicts
6.40
.
∎

Mimic
Theorem

7.9
, we can get an algebraic version of Baire Category Theorem.
Note that the proof is pretty much the same as
Theorem

7.10
and
Theorem

7.11
.

Theorem 7.12
.

Let
R
be a ring with radical ideal
\mathfrak{b}\varsubsetneq R
and countably many ideals
{\left\{I_{k}\right\}}_{k=1}^{\infty}
.
Suppose for all
r\in R-\mathfrak{b}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
x\in R
such that
rx\in R-\mathfrak{b}
and
(1-a)x\in\mathfrak{b}
for some
a\in I_{k}
, then there exists maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}\varsubsetneq R
such that
I_{k}\not\subset\mathfrak{m}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

The ring homomorphism
R\to R/\mathfrak{b}
induces quantale homomorphism
\operatorname{Id}(R)\to\operatorname{Id}(R/\mathfrak{b}),J\mapsto J(R/\mathfrak{b})
, so we can view
\operatorname{Id}(R/\mathfrak{b})_{\nmid(0)}
(note that this is a
\operatorname{Id}(R/\mathfrak{b})
-module by
Theorem

6.39
) as a
\operatorname{Id}(R)
-module.

Assume the contrary, then
\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathscr{F}_{\perp I_{k}}={\left\{1\right\}}\in\operatorname{mF}(\operatorname{Id}(R))
is locally solid (it is even solid since
\operatorname{Id}(R)
is compact).
Now for
k\in\mathbb{N}
let
J_{k}=(x\in R:(1-a)x\in\mathfrak{b}\text{ for some }a\in I_{k})(R/\mathfrak{b})\in\operatorname{Id}(R/\mathfrak{b})
.
Consider
x\in R
along with
a\in I_{k}
such that
(1-a)x\in\mathfrak{b}
, then we have
(1-a)+I_{k}=R
and
(1-a)(x)(R/\mathfrak{b})=(0)(R/\mathfrak{b})
, so
J_{k}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}_{\perp I_{k}}}^{1}(0)
.

Thus, by our assumption
1\preceq_{\mathscr{F}_{\nmid(0)}}^{1}J_{k}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}_{\perp I_{k}}}^{1}(0)
, so
\overline{R/\mathfrak{b}}\preceq_{\mathscr{F}_{\perp I_{k}}}^{1}\overline{(0)}
in
\operatorname{Id}(R/\mathfrak{b})_{\nmid(0)}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
As a result,
\overline{R/\mathfrak{b}}\preceq_{{\left\{1\right\}}}^{1}\overline{(0)}
in
\operatorname{Id}(R/\mathfrak{b})_{\nmid(0)}
by
Lemma

5.38
, which contradicts
6.40
.
∎

Corollary 7.13
.

Let
R
be a ring with countably many maximal ideals.
Pick radical ideal
\mathfrak{b}\varsubsetneq R
.
Then there exist maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}\subset R
and
r\in R-\mathfrak{b}
satisfying
rx\in\mathfrak{b}
for all
x\in R
such that
(1-a)x\in\mathfrak{b}
for some
a\in\mathfrak{m}
.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of
Theorem

7.12
after letting
{\left\{I_{k}\right\}}_{k=1}^{\infty}
enumerate all maximal ideals.
∎

Note that in the proof of

.

Let

be a topological space.
Then

is locally compact if and only if

The condition that

.

Let
Q
be a quantale with bottom
0
.
Then
\mathscr{F}_{\nmid 0}
is localizable over
Q
.

Note that if
\mathscr{F}_{\nmid 0}

,

, and

Another natural question to ask is

Problem 7.16
.

For which

?
