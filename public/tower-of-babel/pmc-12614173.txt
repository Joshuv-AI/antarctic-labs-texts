Assessing the Impact of Wearing a Weighted Vest on Balance, Stability, and Quality of Life in Older Adults: A Pilot Study
Authors: Isabel Paniak, Diya Soni, Lia Tsotsos, Shelagh Stoneham
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsFalls are a significant public health concern among older adults, with profound impacts on health, independence, and healthcare costs. There is a growing need for innovative fall prevention tools and strategies, and weighted vests have the potential to be used as a fall‐prevention tool. This study aimed to assess the effects of 10‐pound and 20‐pound weighted vests on balance and stability in adults aged 55 and older, as well as the usability and acceptability of these vests for daily use.MethodsA cross‐sectional pilot study was conducted in 2024 among thirty‐four participants who completed an in‐person assessment of objective balance tests (4‐Stage Balance Test, Timed Up and Go, Berg Balance Scale, Functional Reach) and subjective stability measures (Rate of Perceived Stability Scale, Functional Abilities Confidence Scale, Falls Efficacy Scale) under three conditions: no vest, 10‐pound vest, and 20‐pound vest. Seventeen participants volunteered to take the vests home for daily use for 2 weeks. Multiple linear and ordinal linear regression analyses were performed using SPSS 29.ResultsAlthough the weighted vest conditions were not significantly correlated with measures of balance and stability, increased age and body weight were inversely related to these outcomes. In the in‐lab condition, 73% of participants responded positively to the vests, citing improved posture, and feeling more stable. In the take‐home phase, the vests were well‐received, with 53% indicating willingness to continue use.ConclusionThis study suggests that while weighted vests may not directly enhance balance in the short‐term, they could be beneficial as part of a broader strategy, particularly in weight management and exercise programs. Future research should explore the long‐term effects of weighted vests, especially in more diverse populations, to better understand their potential in fall prevention.Keywords: balance, falls, stability, weight management, weighted vests

Introduction

Falls are a major public health concern worldwide for adults aged 65 and older [1]. Approximately one‐third experience a fall annually, with rates increasing to 42% after age 70 [1]. Falls contribute significantly to emergency department visits, injury‐related hospitalizations, disability, decreased quality of life and independence, and even death [2, 3]. In 2021, the United States' fall‐related mortality rate was roughly 78 per 100,000 people [4], with associated healthcare costs projected to reach upwards of $240 billion USD by 2040 [5].

Population trends indicate that the number of individuals aged 65 years and older is outpacing those aged 20 years or younger [6]. By 2050, Americans aged 65 and older are expected to increase 47%, comprising nearly one‐fourth of the American population [7]. According to the U.S. Public Health Service, approximately two‐thirds of fall‐related deaths are preventable [8]. Current fall prevention strategies include assistive devices, protective equipment, environmental modifications, education, exercise and mobility training [1]. Given the aging population, rising healthcare costs, and the increasing incidence of musculoskeletal disorders, like osteoporosis [9], there is a growing need for innovative fall prevention tools and strategies.

Weighted vests have gained attention as a potential therapeutic tool. Their applications span a range of uses, including, but not limited to, reducing stress and anxiety in adolescents [10] and improving health outcomes in older adults with obesity when combined with calorie restriction [11]. These devices are portable, relatively inexpensive, and adaptable to various conditions. Despite their demonstrated versatility, research examining the effects of weighted vests on older adults, and more specifically, their effects on balance and stability, is limited. Therefore, the first aim of this cross‐sectional pilot study was to assess the impact of 10‐pound (lbs) and 20‐pound (lbs) weighted vests on objective measures of balance and perceptions of stability in adults aged 55 and older. Subsequently, the second aim was to assess the usability, acceptability, and feasibility of the vests in daily use for community‐dwelling older adults.

Methods

A total of 50 participants were screened using an online eligibility screening form and by phone to assess eligibility. Participants who self‐reported osteoporosis, osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and/or vertigo (n = 7), self‐reported a broken bone, injury and/or surgery in the last 6‐months (n = 7), or self‐reported as sedentary (n = 2) were excluded. A total of 34 participants met the inclusion criteria and were invited to participate in the research study. All participants provided electronic or written informed consent and were compensated for their participation in the study. The study was approved by Sheridan's Research Ethics Board (SREB No. 2002‐06‐005‐007).

In‐Lab Condition

Participants attended a 1‐h in‐person assessment at CER, completing four objective balance tests (4‐Stage Balance Test, Timed Up and Go (TUG), Berg Balance Scale (BBS), and Functional Reach (FR)), and three subjective stability measures (Rate of Perceived Stability Scale (RPSS), Functional Abilities Confidence Scale (FACS), and Falls Efficacy Scale (FES)). These assessments were conducted under three conditions: without a weighted vest (0‐lbs), with a 10‐lbs vest, and 20‐lbs vest, and the order of conditions and tests were randomized. This is illustrated in Figure 1. The selection of 10‐ and 20‐lbs vests was informed by previous literature employing loads up to 20% of body weight, with an emphasis on ensuring participant safety.

Flowchart of in‐lab tests across weighted vest conditions.

Take‐Home Condition

Half of the participants volunteered for a 2‐week take‐home phase, during which they incorporated weighted vests into their daily routine for at least 1 h per day. Participants were encouraged to wear the vest in whatever way best suited their needs, with no restrictions on time of day, location (e.g., at home) and type of activity (e.g., exercise). Participants selected their own weight load, which could be adjusted daily to optimize feasibility, safety, and comfort, up to a maximum of 30‐lbs. Each participant received a questionnaire booklet to record daily ratings of their balance using the RPSS upon waking (before wearing the vest), and again after vest use. They also maintained a daily log documenting the weight worn, duration of wear, activities performed, challenges encountered, and any notable events. One week after completing the take‐home phase, a 30‐min structured phone interview was conducted to gather qualitative and quantitative feedback on the usability and feasibility of the weighted vests as a home‐based health support tool.

Statistical Analysis

This study employed an exploratory analysis using SPSS Version 29 on data collected from the in‐lab assessments. Multiple linear regressions were performed on the BBS total score, FR distance, TUG total time, FES total score and FACS total score, and an ordinal linear regression was performed on the 4‐stage balance test level and RPSS rating to examine the impact of the weighted vest conditions on objective and subjective measures of balance and stability. Independent variables included age, gender, body weight, height, and vest condition. All hypothesis tests were two‐tailed, with a significance level set at p < 0.05. A follow‐up 15‐min structured phone interview was conducted 1‐week post‐assessment to gather qualitative data and feedback on participant′s experience with weighted vests in a lab setting.

Results

The weighted vest conditions were not significantly correlated with the scores on the tests of balance and measures of stability. Results from the multiple linear regression analyses with weighted vest conditions are presented in Table 2, and results from the ordinal linear regression analyses with weighted vest conditions are presented in Table 3. Of the objective tests of balance, the BBS total had an inverse relationship with age (β = −0.470, p < 0.001) and body weight (β = −0.006, p < 0.001). TUG completion time was positively correlated with age (β = 0.130, p < 0.001) and body weight (β = 0.087, p < 0.001). FR distance was positively correlated with height (β = 0.129, p = 0.005). The 4‐stage balance test had an inverse relationship with age (β = −0.202, SE = 0.046, p < 0.001) and body weight (β = −0.103, SE = 0.024, p < 0.001). Of the subjective measures of stability, the FES total was positively correlated with age (β = 0.819, p = 0.01) and body weight (β = 0.339, p = 0.05), while the FACS total was inversely correlated with age (β = −18.221, p < 0.001) and body weight (β = −8.940, p < 0.001). The RPSS rating was positively correlated with age (β = 0.076, SE = 0.033, p = 0.02) and body weight (β = 0.042, SE = 0.018, p = 0.02). When compared to the two weighted vest conditions, no weight (0‐lbs) was inversely correlated with the RPSS (β = −1.654, SE = 0.482, p < 0.001).

Among participants in‐lab, 73% responded positively to the vests, citing improved posture, confidence in stability, and feeling more grounded, though some reported worsened balance and discomfort with the additional weight.

In the take‐home phase, almost all (94%) reported the vests to be comfortable, over half (53%) expressed willingness to incorporate them into daily life, three‐quarters (76%) reported that the vests were at least somewhat functional, and over half (53%) would recommend the vest to someone they know.

Discussion

Given the aging population and the prevalence of falls and fall‐related injuries among older adults, there is an ongoing need for effective fall prevention tools and strategies. Our study explored the potential of weighted vests to improve balance and stability in older adults. While the vests were not significantly correlated with the objective and subjective measures of balance and stability, we found that performance on these tests declined in relation to other physiological factors. That is, balance and stability decreased as age and body weight increased, underscoring the importance of maintaining a healthy body weight to preserve balance and stability as we age.

Previous studies have found significant weight loss in older adults with obesity who participated in dietary programs coupled with weighted vest interventions [12], and the use of weighted vests as an adjunct to strength training, stair climbing and balance exercise programs [13]. Feedback from participants in our study echoed these findings, with one participant noting, “I want to keep the weight off. Wearing a 20‐lbs vest reminded me how much extra weight I used to carry and how much harder it was to move around.” (Participant 003). These observations align with broader fall prevention recommendations, that advocate for multifaceted strategies that combine education, environmental modification, assistive devices and exercise [14]. Given that 68% of our cohort had a body mass index (BMI) categorized as “overweight” or “obese,” future studies could explore weighted vests as part of targeted, age‐specific interventions to manage weight and reduce fall risk. Understandably, caution is necessary with intentional weight loss in older adults, as it can lead to decreased bone mass density (BMD), a significant risk factor for fractures [12]. Around 39% of fall‐related injuries result in fractures [14]. More specifically, low BMD of the proximal femur and fall propensity are two major risk factors for hip fractures [15]. From 2019 to 2020, approximately one‐third of fall‐related hospitalizations were the result of a hip fracture in adults 65 and older, which led to longer hospital stays [14]. This further highlights the need for strategies that can both reduce fall‐risk while also maintaining bone health. Interestingly, weighted vests have the potential to offer a dual benefit: supporting weight loss while preserving, or even enhancing, bone density. Short‐term use of weighted vests loaded with 8% of body weight coupled with aerobic exercise were sufficient in loading the skeletal system, resulting in increased bone synthesis and decreased bone resorption in postmenopausal women with osteoporosis after 18 30‐min sessions [16]. Similarly, after a 32‐week intervention involving weighted vest use during strength training, stair climbing, and balance exercises, this not only resulted in significant weight loss but improved femoral neck density in older women [13]. This suggests that weighted vests could be a safe alternative to traditional weight loss methods, without compromising skeletal health.

Generally, adults over the age of 65 do not meet physical activity guidelines, with less than 10% meeting recommended activity guidelines [12]. Compliance with exercise programs is hindered by barriers such as limited access to gyms, equipment and transportation. Over half of the take‐home cohort (53%) indicated that they would be willing to incorporate the weighted vests into their daily routines, citing weight loss, perceived improvements in core and leg strength, balance and stability, and overall comfort. Weighted vests are portable, relatively accessible, safe and low‐risk devices, making them a viable option for home use, potentially overcoming some of the barriers to exercise adherence for older adults.

Declines in muscle strength, power, and postural stability are three determinants of fall propensity [17]. Often, resistance training and aerobic exercise are suggested as solutions. The observed relationship between age, weight and declining performance on the TUG test further emphasizes the potential utility of weighted vests. Previous studies have demonstrated that in postmenopausal older women, progressively loading vests during a 9‐month exercise regimen improved lateral stability, lower‐body muscular strength, muscular power, and leg mass [17]. Similarly, short‐term functional exercise interventions combining vests yielded significant improvements in lower body power and chair rise performance [18], and all measures of strength, sit‐to‐stand performance, and aerobic capacity [19]. Jessup found that wearing a weighted vest while strength training, stair climbing, and performing balance exercises during a 32‐week regimen resulted in improvements in balance, more specifically, the near tandem and star‐execution [13]. Similarly, our study found the 4‐stage balance test, which includes the tandem stand, was inversely correlated with age and body weight. These findings suggest two possible mechanisms to improve sit‐to‐stand and the tandem stand: (1) weighted vests as a weight loss tool, (2) weighted vests as a muscle strengthening tool for lower body strength.

Older adults in our study reported feeling more stable in the 0‐lbs (no weight) condition, as indicated by lower RPSS scores, which reflect greater subjective stability. This aligns with our finding that higher body weight was correlated with higher RPSS scores, suggesting greater perceived instability. It is possible that the novelty of wearing a weighted vest with loads of 10‐ or 20‐lbs created an initial sense of awkwardness and imbalance, requiring additional effort to maintain stability. For instance, participant 019 stated, “initially the vests felt really heavy, and wearing them made me feel more unstable and unbalanced—especially the 20‐lbs; it was uncomfortable and threw off my balance.” Given the randomized order of condition, some participants experienced the 20‐lbs load immediately after the no‐weight condition, which could have contributed to increased perceived instability. Future studies should collect RPSS measurements at multiple timepoints to more accurately capture short‐term and task‐specific perceptions of stability.

A limitation of our study, and one that has been noted by other researchers, was identifying the optimal weight and duration of wear to achieve significant improvements in balance, stability, weight loss, or bone health. Weighted vest protocols in the literature range from 0% to 20% of body weight [17, 18, 20, 21]. For example, high‐functioning community‐dwelling older adults who wore vests loaded at 5% of body weight experienced minimal benefits [20], while loads closer to 20% of body weight posed challenges related to safety and usability [21]. Our study employed vests weighing 10‐lbs and 20‐lbs during in‐lab conditions, equivalent to 5%–12% of the cohort's average body weight (166.7‐lbs). While most participants tolerated these weights well, one participant was unable to complete the 20‐lbs condition due to a shoulder injury. On average, participants in the take‐home intervention wore weighted vests weighing approximately 12‐lbs, with ranges from 5‐lbs to 30‐lbs, for an average of 2 h daily. Weight and duration were subject to the participant′s preference and needs, ensuring autonomy of the device. Future research should focus on determining the minimum weight load and duration necessary to elicit significant functional improvements.

Our study's cross‐sectional design and relatively healthy cohort could have limited our ability to detect significant effects of weighted vests on the objective measures of balance. Previous longitudinal studies have shown that long‐term use of weighted vests, especially when combined with exercise or dietary interventions, can improve weight and BMD. While our study did not track changes in balance and stability over time, future studies should aim to assess the effects of weighted vest use on balance and stability longitudinally. While the randomized order of test conditions minimized potential biases, the weight loads employed were insufficient to induce detectable changes in balance and stability.

Sensors offer a cost‐effective, user‐friendly, and wireless solution with significant potential for integration into weighted vests to enable real‐time monitoring and feedback [22, 23]. Bio‐signal sensors have been employed in several studies to detect physiological signs of stress [22], as well as gases such as nitric oxide and oxygen, which are important for monitoring physiological processes [24, 25, 26]. Future studies should explore the integration of such sensors into weighted vests to provide continuous, real‐time data on physiological changes associated with fall‐related anxiety or to predict fall risk by detecting biological markers indicative of postural instability and loss of balance.

Additionally, the present study's 1‐h, single‐session assessment did not adequately capture the long‐term effects of weighted vest use on balance and stability. The study's exclusion criteria limited the sample size to relatively healthy older adults, restricting the generalizability of the study results to the broader older adult population. To strengthen validity and generalizability, longitudinal studies, with extended interventions and control groups are needed to assess the sustained impact and safety of vest use over time. Repeated measurements and randomized controlled trials would further identify the efficacy of weighted vests in improving balance and stability in older adults. Furthermore, future studies should aim to focus on more diverse older adult populations that include those with balance impairments, vestibular dysfunction or musculoskeletal disorders. Given the aging population, there is an increasing prevalence of osteoporosis, affecting more than 200 million people worldwide [27]. This condition significantly increases older adults' susceptibility to fractures caused by falls, which often result in serious complications and elevated mortality risk [27]. Therefore, future research should evaluate the long‐term effects and repeated use of weighted vests specifically in older adults with sarcopenia and osteoporosis, focusing on outcomes such as bone density, perceived stability, and fall risk.

In conclusion, weighted vests can serve as a low‐risk intervention, and promising tool to address barriers to physical activity, and to encourage older adults to engage in regular exercise. However, further research is needed to optimize vest protocols, weight loads, wear durations, and integration with exercise regimens. Introducing these interventions earlier in life and maintaining their use across the lifespan may yield the best outcomes for reducing fall risk and fall‐related injuries. By addressing these gaps, weighted vests have the potential to become comprehensive fall prevention strategies.
