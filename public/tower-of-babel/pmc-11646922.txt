Extensive Myocardial Calcification Rapidly Progressing After Orthotopic Liver Transplantation
Authors: Enock Adjei, Maro M. Doce, Anish Sarma, Arman Fijany, Wali R. Johnson, Mary P. French, Jiayang Li, Song Ang, Hasan Siddiqi, Bruno Lima, Muhammad Ameen Rauf
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Orthotopic liver transplantation has been rarely associated with myocardial calcification; no prior cases of asymptomatic calcification have been documented. We present the unique finding of asymptomatic myocardial calcification following orthotopic liver transplantation.

A 41-year-old woman with end-stage liver disease underwent a laparoscopic cholecystectomy. On postoperative day 7, she presented with septic shock, acute decompensation of her cirrhosis, acute kidney injury (AKI) requiring continuous renal-replacement therapy (CRRT), and a type II non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction with normal cardiac function and no wall motion abnormalities. Despite the sepsis resolution, her liver function failed to improve, necessitating orthotopic liver transplantation (OLTx). Post-OLTx, she developed sinus tachycardia, and cardiac work-up revealed acute myocarditis with myocardial calcifications of the basal to mid-left ventricle. This case raises the concern about potential long-term cardiac sequelae. Given the importance of further research to understand the potential implications over time, we recommend the need for continued intermittent cardiac surveillance, despite the patient being asymptomatic.

Liver transplantation can have multisystem postoperative sequelae, including kidney injury, cardiovascular disease, and infection.1,2 Metastatic calcifications after OLTx have been described in the published reports, where elevated parathyroid hormone levels, hypercalcemia, and associated renal failure were attributed as the probable causes.3 Only a single case of acute onset of myocardial calcification in a post-OLTx patient has been reported.4 The prevalence, etiologies, and consequences of post-OLTx myocardial calcifications are poorly understood. We present the first asymptomatic rapid-onset myocardial calcification case in a post-OLTx patient.

Case Presentation

A 41-year-old woman with a past medical history notable for alpha-1-antitrypsin deficiency, ulcerative colitis, and cirrhosis secondary to primary sclerosing cholangitis underwent an uncomplicated laparoscopic cholecystectomy for a low-grade dysplastic gallbladder polyp. Her pre-op Model for End-stage Liver Disease (MELD)-Na score was 15 (sodium: 137 mmol/L, bilirubin: 3.2 mg/dL, serum creatinine: 0.61 mg/dL, and international normalized ratio: 1.2), with a Child-Pugh Class A Score. On postoperative day 7 following her laparoscopic cholecystectomy, she presented to the emergency department in septic shock with E-Cloacae bacteremia, AKI (requiring CRRT), acute hepatic decompensation with a MELD-Na score of 38 (sodium: 124, bilirubin: 8, serum creatinine: 4.82 and INR: 2.1), and non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction with elevated troponin of 14.38.

Transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) (Figure 1) at initial presentation showed an average left ventricular ejection fraction (55%-65%) with normal wall motion and thickness. The right ventricle also showed normal systolic function and a mildly dilated left atrium. No significant valvular disease was noted, although the estimate of RV systolic pressure was not possible (Figure 1). The IVC demonstrated normal size and collapse, suggesting 5 to 10 mm Hg right atrial pressure. A second pretransplant TTE was obtained 3 days after hospitalization, given her increasing troponin levels (with the highest level at 44.99), reaffirming the previously noted normal findings. She subsequently underwent a diagnostic coronary angiography on hospital day 4, which showed normal coronary vessels (Figure 1). The patient received continuous renal replacement therapy for oliguric AKI secondary to sepsis and continued broad-spectrum antibiotic treatment for her septic shock secondary to E. faecalis bacteremia. After 12 days of hospitalization, there was resolution of her bacteremia and septic shock. However, there was no improvement in her liver function, with a MELD-Na score of 34. Thus, she was listed for a liver transplant on hospital day 12. On hospital day 15, she underwent a deceased brain donor OLTx without intraoperative complications. Immunosuppressive therapy included tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, and prednisone. On post-transplantation day 3, she had an up-trending total bilirubin and underwent ERCP that showed an anastomotic bile duct leak. She returned to the operating room on that day to revise the duct anastomosis.

On post-transplantation day 6, the patient’s routine liver duplex demonstrated tardus parvus. A CT angiography of the abdomen and pelvis was obtained to assess the hepatic vessels and rule out concerns for hepatic artery thrombus. Although the computed tomography angiogram (CTA) was unremarkable, there was an incidental finding of “extensive myocardial calcification” in the left ventricle (Supplemental Figure 1). A transthoracic echocardiogram this time was notable for a new extensive myocardial calcification in the basal to mid-septum and basal to mid-inferior and inferior lateral walls, with normal biventricular function, size, and wall motion (Supplemental Figure 1). With the guidance of our cardiologists, further cardiac imaging with coronary magnetic resonance imaging CMR and coronary CTA were obtained. The CTA showed CAD RADS (Coronary Artery Disease Reporting and Data System) 0, “progressive calcification of the left ventricular myocardium with relative sparing of the apex,” and hypokinesis of the affected myocardial walls (Supplemental Figure 2). The coronary magnetic resonance imaging with parametric mapping showed acute inflammation and mildly or moderately hypokinetic basal, inferoseptal, and anterolateral left ventricular walls with nonischemic myocardial injury and edema (Figure 2), fulfilling the Lake Louise Criteria for acute myocarditis. With the unclear etiology of the patient’s asymptomatic acute myocarditis and myocardial calcification, an endomyocardial biopsy was considered. Throughout this patient’s post-transplantation course, she maintained serum calcium levels within the normal range, and she had normal cardiac examination and function, except for mild sinus tachycardia, without any associated symptoms. She was discharged to an inpatient rehabilitation facility on post-transplantation day 28. Her prolonged hospital course was caused by physical debilitation and AKI requiring CRRT and intermittent HD. She is recovering well and continues to remain asymptomatic from her myocardial calcifications. Follow up TTE (on post-transplantation day 40), demonstrates stable myocardial calcifications in the basal segments of her left ventricle (Supplemental Figure 3).

Discussion

This case highlights the unusual occurrence of asymptomatic acute myocarditis leading to extensive myocardial calcifications following liver transplantation. It also highlights the need for further research to understand the potential implications and to identify which subsets of patients may benefit from close cardiac monitoring.

Metastatic tissue calcification can result from a variety of conditions including hyperparathyroidism, hypercalcemia, renal failure, acid-base changes, infectious, and neoplastic processes among others.4 It is a common complication after liver transplantation, with a prevalence rate reported to be approximately 47% to 84%, and often correlated with renal failure and hypercalcemia.4,5 A 1992 autopsy study of 25 patients who had received liver transplants found microscopic tissue calcification in 21 patients, suggesting that asymptomatic tissue calcification after liver transplant is surprisingly common.6

There have been very few cases of extensive left ventricular calcification in OLTx and non-OLTx patients that have been described. And in those cases, the patients had life-threatening symptoms, including arrhythmias and heart failures.

In one report, a 20-year-old man developed myocardial calcifications and ultimately fatal heart failure after 2 successive liver transplants.5 That patient was found to have hypercalcemia that subsequently resulted in myocardial calcifications, as well as calcium deposition in the skeletal muscles and lungs, indicative of disseminated metastatic tissue calcification. Hemodialysis with low calcium fluid, calcitonin, pamidronate, and a RANKL inhibitor were unsuccessful. Although our patient had acute renal failure secondary to her pretransplant septic shock requiring HD, she did not have hypercalcemia.

In a report by Rob et al,7 acute pericarditis and subsequent development of extensive myocardial calcification of the left ventricle was observed in a 56-year-old man in India who developed acute perimyocarditis secondary to Shigella sepsis. In a systemic review study by Ioannou et al,8 looking at endocarditis caused by Enterobacter Cloacae, they found that it was prevalent in patients who had undergone cardiac surgery within 30 days (37.5%), prosthetic cardiac valve (27.7%), central venous catheters (18.8%), IV drug history (17.6%), pacemaker/defibrillator (15%), and ESRD undergoing HD (6.3%). Our patient initially presented in septic shock secondary to E. Cloacae and had biochemical evidence of nonischemic myocardial injury with markedly elevated CRP and troponin levels. However, she did not have any evidence of acute myocarditis on her pretransplant cardiac work-up. Thus, it remains unclear whether her acute extensive myocardial calcifications after her liver transplantation could be caused by her autoimmune disease or transplantation or previous cardiac insult from her initial acute illness. It highlights the potential for a complex interplay of factors contributing to the development of asymptomatic acute myocarditis with extensive myocardial calcification in a post-liver transplant recipient. Disentangling the precise etiology remains a challenge. The patient's nonischemic myocardial injury, arising from the context of septic shock, acute renal failure requiring CRRT, and acute hepatic decompensation, may have played a role. Alternatively, the observed findings could represent a sequela of liver transplantation.

In contrast to the few cases of extensive left ventricular calcification in OLTx and non-OLTx patients that have been described (which likely reflect a selection bias, where more dramatic cases are prioritized for reporting), our patient presents with a favorable outcome, demonstrating asymptomatic myocardial calcification post-transplantation. This case underscores the importance of further research into the potential long-term cardiac sequelae in post-OLTX patients with myocardial calcification, even in the absence of symptoms. Thus, continued intermittent cardiac surveillance is recommended to determine if such patients will develop a restrictive CMP or systolic dysfunction over time.

Conclusions

This case underscores the importance of further research into the potential long-term cardiac sequelae in post-transplant patients, even in the absence of symptoms. Thus, there is the need for close cardiac surveillance to help determine the potential implications of asymptomatic myocardial calcification post-OLTX and identify which subsets of patients may benefit from such monitoring.VISUAL SUMMARY.
