Incorporating neutron star physics into gravitational wave inference with neural priors
Thibeau Wouters, Peter T. H. Pang, Tim Dietrich, Chris Van Den Broeck

Abstract
Bayesian inference, widely used in gravitational-wave parameter estimation, depends on the choice of priors, i.e., on our previously existing knowledge. However, to investigate neutron star mergers, priors are often chosen in an agnostic way, leaving valuable information from nuclear physics and independent observations of neutron stars unused. In this work, we propose to encode information on neutron star physics into data-driven prior distributions constructed with normalizing flows, referred to as neural priors . These priors take input from constraints on the nuclear equation of state and neutron star population models. Applied to GW170817, GW190425, and GW230529, we highlight two contributions of the framework. First, we demonstrate its ability to provide source classification and to enable model selection of equation of state constraints for loud signals such as GW170817, directly from the gravitational-wave data. Second, we obtain narrower constraints on the source properties through these informed priors. As a result, the neural priors consistently recover higher luminosity distances compared to agnostic priors. Our method paves the way for classifying future ambiguous low-mass mergers observed through gravitational waves and for continuously incorporating advances in our understanding of neutron star properties into gravitational-wave data analysis.

II.2
Normalizing flows

Normalizing flows (NFs) are a class of generative machine learning models that enable flexible density estimation, and are therefore often referred to as neural density estimators

The core idea of

p_{\rm{\mathcal{X}}}(x)=p_{\mathcal{Z}}\left(f^{-1}(x)\right)\left|\det\left(\frac{\partial f^{-1}(x)}{\partial x}\right)\right|\,.

(3)

Discussion

IV.1
Source classification

Inferring the nature of compact objects observed in
GW
events from their inspiral is challenging for objects with masses close to the
TOV
mass
130
;
172
.
One approach relies on the mass estimates derived from
GW
inferences.
For instance, by computing the overlap between an object’s mass posterior and the posterior on
M_{\rm{TOV}}
, the probability that the object is a
BH
or an
NS
can be estimated
96
;
110
;
98
;
14
;
254
;
260
;
102
;
2
;
153
;
178
.
However, this implicitly assumes that the
NS
and
BH
populations are non-overlapping
110
, ignoring the possibility that compact objects in the
NS
mass regime could be (primordial)
BH

156
;
53
;
52
;
75
;
63
;
259
;
237
or exotic compact objects
187
;
66
;
168
.

Besides the masses, one can hope to identify compact objects through their tidal signature
51
;
234
;
143
.
In practice, however, the difference in the waveforms due to tidal deformations is small
185
, such that this approach only works well for sufficiently loud signals such as observed by future ground-based detectors
59
;
47
;
255
;
114
;
76
;
67
;
148
;
40
, for low-mass
NS
with large tidal deformabilities
71
;
120
;
70
, or after observing a population of mergers
106
;
194
.

In this work, we presented a method for Bayesian source classification under the assumption that
NS
and
BH
have non-overlapping mass distributions.
In future work, we will explore how well this approach can distinguish compact objects when this assumption is relaxed, relying on neural priors to constrain the relation between mass and tidal deformability
a priori
.

IV.2
Prior to posterior information gain

Since the neural priors, by construction, restrict the parameter space explored during inference, it is important to determine to what extent the posteriors in Sec.
III.2
are shaped by the prior or by the information provided by the signal.
To assess this, we compute the information gain between the prior and posterior distributions for the tidal deformability.
We construct the priors by sampling the
EOS
from the sets discussed in Sec.
II.3.1
and evaluating their predictions for the tidal deformabilities at the source-frame component masses of the posterior samples.
We quantify the degree of similarity between these priors and the posteriors through the
KL
(
KL
) divergence
44
.
The comparison is shown visually in Fig.
7
, and the
KL
divergences (measured in bits) between prior and posterior pairs are given in the brackets in the legend.
We note that, as expected, the
KL
divergence is larger if the
SNR
of the signal is larger, as there is more information content in the signal driving the inference.
Nevertheless, we still find noticeable shifts in the posteriors due to the signal in other events and neural priors, showing that the analyses are not simply reproducing the informed priors.

IV.3
Equation-of-state-informed GW inference

Previous works have considered different methods to use
EOS
information during
GW
parameter estimation for
BNS
systems.
One example is the use of universal relations
286
;
9
;
57
.
However, these relations have residuals that have to be accounted for
57
, and can lead to conceptual or technical problems when used in Bayesian inference
146
.
Moreover, these relations are designed without assuming any constraints on the
EOS
, and would therefore need to be modified to explicitly incorporate such information.

Instead, the
EOS
can be sampled on the fly in two ways.
First, one can choose a parametrization for the
EOS
and jointly sample the
GW
and
EOS
parameters, using machine learning emulators of the
TOV
solver to accelerate the likelihood evaluation
177
;
221
;
241
;
220
.
However, this leads to an increase in the number of parameters to sample, which makes convergence less efficient.
Second, one can use a discrete set of tabulated
EOS
, which is either sampled during
GW
inference
13
;
204
;
230
;
266
;
152
;
284
, or used in the postprocessing of
GW
posteriors that relied on agnostic priors
119
;
41
;
147
;
145
.
However, this requires more memory to store the tabulated
EOS
and might lead to a low number of posterior samples when used to analyze high
SNR
signals
230
.

Finally, another option is to emulate the distributions of the relevant parameters for
GW
inference as predicted by the
EOS
constraints, which alleviates the issues described above.
To this end, Ref.
176
recently introduced physics-informed priors based on
EOS
information for
GW
inference, making it conceptually similar to the present study.
However, their method was limited to modeling the conditional distribution of
\tilde{\Lambda}
given the source-frame chirp mass.
Our approach instead models the joint distributions of both component masses and tidal deformabilities, offering a more general framework to directly incorporate
EOS
constraints into
GW
inference.

IV.4
Future work

Before concluding, we highlight a few of our assumptions and how these present themselves as opportunities to extend the methodology in future work.

First, we note that this work focused on the application of neural priors in
GW
inference.
For multimessenger events like GW170817, joint inference on all messengers could further constrain parameters and aid classification.
However, when using agnostic priors in such a joint analysis, the Bayes factors still favor the
BNS
hypothesis only mildly
69
.
In the future, we will extend our neural priors to additionally model parameters of the
EM
counterpart.
For instance, by making use of the fits in Refs.
22
;
157
;
82
;
45
, the
GW
binary parameters can be linked to the mass ejecta of kilonovae
204
;
45
(although the fits might be subject to systematic errors
227
).
In this way, the neural priors can be used for informed multimessenger analyses.

Second, we note that we have neglected phenomena that can soften or stiffen the
EOS
, such as dark matter
205
;
74
;
93
;
196
;
213
, pressure anisotropy
131
;
132
;
203
, phase transitions
207
;
195
;
129
;
217
, modelling of the nuclear physics
230
;
166
, or various compositions
248
;
167
;
223
;
128
;
108
;
42
.
Therefore, the preference for soft
EOS
could arise from (a combination of) the aforementioned effects rather than a purely hadronic, soft
EOS
.
However, the neural priors, being data-driven, can perfectly accommodate any set of
EOS
in the construction (see Sec.
II.3.3
).
Future work will investigate whether the model selection introduced in this work can generalize to discern such effects in sufficiently loud
BNS
mergers or a collection of
BNS
mergers.

Finally, future work will consider, with a simulation study, whether the use of informed priors in
GW
inference results in improved constraints on the
EOS

176
.

V
Conclusion

Data analysis of
GW
signals from mergers involving
NS
typically employs agnostic priors on the source parameters.
In this work, we propose a flexible, data-driven method to incorporate external knowledge of
NS
physics into
GW
parameter estimation.
In particular, we train
NF
, a type of neural density estimator, on predictions of masses and tidal deformabilities for
BNS
and
NSBH
systems from
NS
population models and existing constraints on the
EOS
.
The flows can be used as priors in stochastic samplers, and we refer to them as
neural priors
.

We construct such neural priors informed by existing
EOS
constraints and population models.
Applying them to inferences of GW170817, GW190425, and GW230529, we highlight two benefits from using the neural priors over agnostic priors.
First, we demonstrate that we can perform Bayesian model selection by computing Bayes factors between hypotheses encoded as different neural priors.
We classify GW170817 as a
BNS
merger, and find substantial evidence for a soft
EOS
to explain the tidal deformation in the signal.
GW190425 is classified as a
BNS
merger, with strong to decisive evidence, while GW230529 is decisively characterized as an
NSBH
merger.
While these findings are in line with previous works, we assess them based on data-driven priors directly from analyzing the
GW
data.

Second, the neural priors result in narrower posteriors on the
GW
source parameters.
We find that the informed constraints derived for GW170817 from the
GW
data alone are consistent with multimessenger analyses of the merger that jointly model the
GW
data and
EM
counterpart.
In particular, we find a preference for nearly-equal mass systems, constrain
\tilde{\Lambda}\gtrsim 300
, and recover a luminosity distance consistent with information from the host galaxy.
For GW190425, our informed posteriors favor less equal masses compared to agnostic priors, with almost no posterior support for mass ratios above
0.9
.
Regarding GW230529, we find narrower constraints on the mass ratio and therefore the spins of the binary, while the tidal deformation posterior is more prior-dominated due to the termination of the waveform model at low frequencies.
For GW190425 and GW230529, we recover a significantly larger luminosity distance compared to agnostic inferences, due to a more constrained source-frame chirp mass.

Looking ahead, we envision that our data-driven methodology can easily incorporate information from additional sources and can be generalized to inform other parameters of interest.
Therefore, it can easily accommodate the advances in our understanding of both the
NS
population and dense nuclear matter in the coming years.

Appendix B
Normalizing flow implementation

In this section, we provide more details on the numerical implementation of the
NF
.
The flows are trained with
glasflow

273
;
92
, using the
CouplingNSF
architecture, which is a type of neural spline flow
91
.
We use
4
layers with widths of
256
neurons and a depth of
3
blocks for the conditioner networks.
While this results in fairly large networks, we have focused on robustness of the flows across all
18
configurations shown in Fig.
2
, and leave further optimization to future work.

We use the procedure outlined in Sec.
II.3.3
to generate
{200\ 000}
samples, of which
80\%
is used as training data, and the remaining
20\%
as validation data.
We found that training of the flows is improved when trained on the source-frame chirp mass
\mathcal{M}_{c}^{\rm{src}}
and mass ratio
q=m_{2}/m_{1}
rather than source-frame component masses.
The training data is rescaled to the domain
[0,1]
, and we apply a Jacobian factor to the flow’s log likelihood calculation during sampling to account for this operation.

The flows are trained with the Adam optimizer
149
, with a fixed learning rate of
10^{-4}
and a batch size of
1024
for a maximum of
2000
epochs, and early stopping is employed if no improvement in the loss is observed after
250
epochs.

To assess the accuracy of the flows, we evaluate the
JSD

171
for each parameter between the original distribution of training samples and a distribution generated from the flow.
Across all
18
neural priors that we consider, we find that the highest
JSD
value is
6.2\times 10^{-4}
bits.
Therefore, we can conclude that the trained
NF
are an accurate representation of the underlying distributions.

The trained flows are then used inside a
bilby
prior class called
NFPrior
, which subclasses the
JointPrior
class.
By design, generating samples and evaluating the density of this
JointPrior
class are treated by the
NF
.
Certain nested samplers, like
dynesty
, sample a unit hypercube from which physical samples are obtained by rescaling with the appropriate transformations.
In our case, this is achieved by defining a joint multivariate Gaussian distribution object internally in the
NFPrior
class of the same dimensionality.
By first mapping samples from the unit hypercube to the unit Gaussian, they can then be transformed into the physical space by using the bijections learned by
NF
.
While this work shows applications with nested samplers, we have verified that the neural priors also work in MCMC-based samplers such as
jim

281
;
282
.

After training, the flows do not necessarily respect the physical boundaries of the training data.
For example, the flow has a negligible, but non-zero, probability to generate samples with a mass ratio above
1
.
We have found that in less than
1\%
of the
NF
samples, the generated sample is unphysical.
This behavior can be corrected by clipping samples generated from the
NF
, as well as setting the probability of a sample to
0
if it violates any physical bounds.
While this technically makes the
NFPrior
class unnormalized (even if the
NF
is, by design), we have computed the normalization constant of one flow with
dynesty
, finding that the distribution is normalized within the errors of the evidence calculation.
Therefore, we consider the neural priors to be normalized for the purposes of this work.
In the future, this issue can be alleviated by either transforming the flow before fitting, to ensure its support matches the target distribution, or by transforming the data to an unbounded space support before fitting to match the
NF

271
.
