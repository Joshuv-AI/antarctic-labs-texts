System-Bath Approach to Rotational Brownian Motion Preprint: .
Ashot Matevosyan 1,2) and Armen E. Allahverdyan 2)

Abstract
Rotational equilibrated systems are widespread, but relatively little attention has been devoted to studying them from the first principles of statistical mechanics. Here we bridge this gap, as we look at a Brownian particle coupled with a rotational thermal bath modeled via Caldeira-Leggett oscillators. We show that the Langevin equation that describes the dynamics of the Brownian particle contains (due to rotation) long-range correlated noise. In contrast to the usual situation of non-rotational equilibration, the rotational Gibbs distribution is recovered only for a weak coupling with the bath. However, the presence of a uniform magnetic field disrupts equilibrium, even under weak coupling conditions. In this context, we clarify the applicability of the Bohr-van Leeuwen theorem to classical systems in rotational equilibrium, as well as the concept of work done by a changing magnetic field. Additionally, we show that the Brownian particle under a rotationally symmetric potential reaches a stationary state that behaves as an effective equilibrium, characterized by a free energy. As a result, no work can be extracted via cyclic processes that respect the rotation symmetry. However, if the external potential exhibits asymmetry, then work extraction via slow cyclic processes is possible. This is illustrated by a general scenario, involving a slow rotation of a non-rotation-symmetric potential.

Introduction

I.1
Gibbs distribution

Thermodynamic systems are composed of microscopic constituents with well-defined quantum or classical dynamics. Statistical mechanics bridges the gap between macro and micro descriptions and accounts for phenomenological thermodynamics and non-equilibrium phenomena in physics
Terletski 1971
;
Landau 2002
;
Reichl 1999

Problems in statistical mechanics typically consider a large system divided into two unequal parts: a smaller subsystem of interest, referred to as the “system,” and a larger quasi-equilibrium subsystem known as the “thermostat,” “reservoir,” or “bath”
Landau 2002
. The system interacts with the bath, exchanging energy, particles, linear momentum, and, as we will explore later, angular momentum
Matevosyan 2024
;
Matevosyan and Allahverdyan 2023
. A fundamental concept in equilibrium statistical mechanics is the Gibbs distribution, which links the probability of a system in phase space with its Hamiltonian. Thermodynamic quantities are deduced as averages over the Gibbs distribution.

The convergence of the system’s dynamics toward the Gibbs distribution is less universal, as it depends on the details of the system and bath. The generalized, classical Langevin equation emerged recently as a sufficiently general paradigm for non-equilibrium dynamics and relaxation toward the Gibbs distribution
Tuckerman 2010
. It is also deduced systematically from the system-bath approach
Tuckerman 2010
. In particular, the projection operator formalism provides a powerful framework for deriving the generalized Langevin equation directly from the Hamiltonian of a closed system by examining the dynamics of slow degrees of freedom
Mori 1965
;
Zwanzig 2001
;
Ayaz et al. 2022
. The Caldeira-Leggett (CL) model can be viewed as a special case of this formalism: the bath is modeled as a collection of independent harmonic oscillators, each representing a mode of the thermal environment
Magalinskii 1959
;
Zwanzig 1973
;
Caldeira and Leggett 1983
;
Tuckerman 2010
.
This model has a closed-form solution, reproduces the generalized Langevin equation for an initially equilibrium bath, and hence leads to the (non-rotational) Gibbs distribution for the system in the long-time limit. Oscillators in the CL model can represent phonons or photons, or (more generally) refer to the degrees of freedom of a weakly perturbed bath
Caldeira and Leggett 1983
;
Tuckerman 2010
.

The usual Gibbs distribution includes only energy and temperature. Here, we focus on a system that interacts with a rotational thermal bath. All interactions are assumed to be pairwise and rotationally symmetric; that is, the total angular momentum is conserved for the overall system. Examples of Rotational Equilibrium

Rotational equilibration is prevalent in various contexts. Rotary motors play a crucial role in energy storage and transmission in molecular machines

I.3
Our results and the structure of the paper

Here we aim to describe the Brownian motion for a particle coupled with a rotational bath. As opposed to previous phenomenological approaches
Bonitz et al. 2012
;
Kählert et al. 2012
;
Matevosyan 2024
, we take a more microscopic stance. We start from the bath of harmonic oscillators
Magalinskii 1959
;
Zwanzig 1973
;
Caldeira and Leggett 1983
;
Tuckerman 2010
(Caldeira-Leggett model), where the rotation is imposed in the initial state of the bath via the Gibbsian density (
1
). The advantage of harmonic oscillators is that the bath dynamics is exactly solvable.
As a result, Brownian particle dynamics are exactly like Langevin equation dynamics (exact means without further approximations). While the friction force of this Langevin equation is standard, e.g., it admits localization towards the usual Ohmic limit, the noise is long-range correlated due to rotation, even in the Ohmic limit. Both these aspects differ from the phenomenological description of rotational Brownian motion proposed in Refs.
Bonitz et al. 2012
;
Kählert et al. 2012
;
Matevosyan 2024
.

The rotational Langevin equation is similar to the quantum Langevin equation, where (even for Ohmic friction) the noise is not white and shows long-range correlations due to quantum fluctuations; see, e.g., Ref.
Allahverdyan and Nieuwenhuizen 2002
. Importantly, the long-time limit of the rotational Langevin equation reduces to the rotational Gibbsian state (i.e. the analog of (
1
)) of the Brownian particle only in the weak coupling limit, an aspect which is also similar to the quantum Langevin equation
Allahverdyan and Nieuwenhuizen 2002
. Additional non-Gibbsian features for the stationary state exist whenever (for a rotational bath) an external homogeneous, time-independent magnetic field acts on the charged Brownian particle. We show that, despite the stationary state of a Brownian particle in a harmonic potential being non-Gibbsian
3

3

III

, it still admits a well-defined free energy: no work can be extracted by slowly and cyclically varying the system’s external parameter. The existence of the free energy is, however, closely tied to the rotational symmetry of the potential acting on the Brownian particle: cyclic work extraction is possible if the external potential for the Brownian particle is non-rotation-symmetric.

Recall that when the rotation is absent, the classical Langevin equation always relaxes to the classical Gibbs density for the Brownian particle (provided only that the external potential is confining, as we shall always assume in this work)

;
Breuer and Petruccione 2002a

Matevosyan and Allahverdyan 2023
.

The paper is structured as follows: In the next section, we define our microscopic model of the rotational thermal bath as an extension of the Caldeira-Leggett model. There we also discuss stability features of the bath and deduce the Langevin equation for the rotational Brownian motion. In section

To make the presentation self-contained, section

Section
VI.2

VI.3
shows that the notion of free energy still applies to the stationary state of the Brownian particle interacting with the rotational thermal bath and external magnetic field. The condition of isotropic potential is lifted in section
VII

We summarize in the last section.

II.3
Stability of Rotational Thermal Bath

As we emphasized, each oscillator in (
15

\Omega_{k}
. This is because low-frequency oscillators, while irrelevant for the bath’s thermodynamics, are crucial for the long-term behavior of the Brownian particle. A constant rotation frequency
\Omega_{k}
would lead to instability,
inverting the harmonic potential. Therefore,
\Omega_{k}
must decrease with
\omega_{k}

A

\displaystyle\omega_{k}>\Omega_{k},\quad{\rm for~all}\quad k.

(21)

The instability of low-frequency harmonic modes under rotation is a well-known phenomenon, closely related to superradiance
Bekenstein and Schiffer 1998
;
Alicki and Jenkins 2018
. Recently, Ref.
Alicki and Jenkins 2018
examined a weak-coupling master equation for a system interacting with a rotational quantum thermal bath composed of effectively finite-dimensional modes. In the classical scenario, addressing these instabilities under
\Omega_{k}=\Omega
will demand introducing a non-linear confining potential for the oscillators. This, combined with the inverted harmonic potential due to rotation, would create a double-well non-linear potential. Hence, the bath dynamics will no longer be exactly solvable, and the model would lose its primary advantage in exploring non-weak coupling scenarios.

For strong coupling (

Thus, we conclude that strong coupling with the bath prevents the establishment of the rotational Gibbs equilibrium at the same temperature as the bath. Recall that for a non-rotational bath, i.e.,
\Omega(w)=a=0
, we do get the non-rotational Gibbs distribution from the Langevin equation (

These conclusions are obtained for bath angular velocities (

), which is less realistic than (

32
).

In the quantum domain (i.e., for the quantum Langevin equation), a similar conclusion holds: the quantum Gibbs distribution is not achieved for a quantum Brownian oscillator strongly coupled to a quantum thermal bath
Allahverdyan and Nieuwenhuizen 2002
. In the quantum case, angular momentum is not involved, and the reason for not establishing the proper Gibbs distribution at a non-weak coupling is the quantum entanglement between the system and the bath.
Classically, the angular momentum is involved, and the lack of the rotational Gibbs distribution (
1
) is due to specific features of angular momentum transfer between the bath and the Brownian particle. Note in this context that the angular momentum is more additive than energy, since (in contrast to the concept of interaction energy), there is no concept of “interaction angular momentum,” at least in non-relativistic physics.

VI
Work and free-energy: rotationally invariant situation

A focus of the paper is on slow (thermodynamic) work in the stationary state, and the existence of a potential for this work, i.e., free-energy.
The general definition of work will be commented relatively briefly.

VI.1
Free-energy without magnetic field: reminder

The system+bath is thermally isolated and is described by the overall time-dependent Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H(t)=H_{S}(\lambda(t))+H_{\text{int}}+H_{B},

(77)

where the separate terms refer to (resp.) the system, interaction, and bath, and
where
\lambda(t)
is an externally varying parameter (external field) that directly influences the system’s Hamiltonian. The work
W
is defined as

\displaystyle\frac{\delta W}{\delta t}=\left\langle\frac{\partial H}{\partial\lambda}\dot{\lambda}\right\rangle=\left\langle\frac{\partial H}{\partial\lambda}\right\rangle\dot{\lambda},\quad\dot{\lambda}=\frac{{\rm d}\lambda}{{\rm d}t},

(78)

where
\langle...\rangle
denotes the average over the (generally time-dependent) distribution of the system+bath;
\dot{\lambda}
is taken out of the average, since it is an externally controlled variable that does not depend on the coordinates and velocities of the system+bath. For the considered thermally isolated situation, and
using the Liouville equation of motion for the system+bath, one relates the work to the total energy change of the system+bath
Allahverdyan and Nieuwenhuizen 2007
:

\displaystyle\frac{\delta W}{\delta t}=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left\langle{H}(\,{\lambda}(t)\,)\right\rangle.

(79)

Since
\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}H=\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}H_{S}
, the work in (
79
) can be expressed solely in terms of the system’s properties:

\displaystyle\frac{\delta W}{\delta t}=\left\langle\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial\lambda}\right\rangle\dot{\lambda},

(80)

where the average is now taken over the distribution of the system, which is the marginal distribution of the system+bath. Note that while the work is equal to the energy change of the total system+bath, it is not anymore equal to the energy change of the system. The difference between these two quantities is just the
heat, which is exchanged between the system and the bath.

Eq. (
78
) generalizes to multiple parameters:

\displaystyle\delta W=\sum_{\alpha}\left\langle\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}\right\rangle{\rm d}\lambda_{\alpha}.

(81)

For slow changes of
\lambda
(
\lambda_{\alpha}
), the system’s distribution remains close to its stationary value, which is time-dependent solely due to the dependence of
\lambda(t)
on time. Let us now assume that this stationary distribution is given by the rotational Gibbs distribution for the system [cf. (
1
) where for simplicity the rotation axis is
z
]:

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{1}{Z(\lambda)}e^{-\beta H(\lambda)+\beta\Omega L_{z}}

(82)

where
\rho
is the phase-space stationary density for the system (i.e., it is written in terms of canonical coordinates and momenta),
Z(\lambda)
is the partition function. Hence, all externally changeable parameters
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots)

enter into
\rho
only via
H(\lambda)
(and the corresponding term in
Z
). Hence, the slow work in (
78
,
81
) is written via the free-energy
F(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots)
:

\displaystyle\delta W={\rm d}F=\sum_{i}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}{\rm d}\lambda_{\alpha},

\displaystyle F=-T\ln Z(\lambda),

(83)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=\left\langle\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\right\rangle_{\rm eq},

\displaystyle W=\Delta F,

(84)

where
\left\langle...\right\rangle_{\rm eq}
indicates that the averaging is done via the equilibrium density (
82
), and where the final message of (
84
) is that the work is given by the free-energy difference. This conclusion is important, also because it refers to the concept of thermodynamic reversibility: according to (
84
), cyclic changes of parameters not only leave the state (
82
) intact (this will be the case for any stable stationary distribution), but also nullify the work.

VI.2
Work by Time-Dependent Magnetic Field and Free Energy

VI.2.1
General definition of work done by time-dependent and homogeneous magnetic field

A magnetic field leads to several conceptual difficulties that should be discussed carefully. To apply (
79
) with
\lambda\equiv{\bm{B}}
, we need to introduce the Hamiltonian of a charged particle in an external (time-independent and homogeneous) magnetic field. To this end, start from (
2
) and standardly define the canonic momentum
{\bm{P}}
, and Hamiltonian
H
(
{\bm{R}}^{\intercal}\equiv(X,Y,Z)
):

\displaystyle{\bm{P}}=\frac{\partial{\cal L}_{S}}{\partial{\bm{V}}}={\bm{V}}+{\bm{A}},\quad{\bm{V}}=\dot{\bm{R}}

(85)

\displaystyle H={\bm{P}}{\bm{V}}-{\cal L}_{S}=\frac{1}{2}({\bm{P}}-{\bm{A}})^{2}+V({\bm{R}}).

(86)

The work is now defined by differentiating
H
in (
86
) over time (entering via the time-dependent magnetic field), while
{\bm{P}}
and
{\bm{R}}
are held fixed [cf. (
78
)]:

\displaystyle\dot{H}=({\bm{A}}-{\bm{P}})\dot{\bm{A}}=-{\bm{V}}\dot{\bm{A}}=-\frac{\dot{\bm{B}}{\bm{L}}}{2},\quad{\bm{L}}={\bm{R}}\times{\bm{V}},

(87)

where for the last equality we employed the Coulomb gauge-expression (
4
), and where
{\bm{L}}
and
\frac{1}{2}{\bm{L}}
are (resp.) angular momentum and magnetic moment; recall that in (
2
,
87
) we assumed unit mass and unit charge. Eq. (
87
) applies to non-stationary situations, where the work is defined from (
79
,
87
) as

\displaystyle\delta W=-\frac{1}{2}\left\langle{\bm{L}}\right\rangle\delta{\bm{B}},

(88)

where
\left\langle{\bm{L}}\right\rangle
is the average of the non-stationary probability density of the system. Eq. (
88
) generalizes to an interacting system of
N
charges
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
with masses
\{m_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
under homogeneous magnetic field:

\displaystyle\delta W=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{e_{i}}{2}\left\langle{\bm{R}}_{i}\times\dot{\bm{R}}_{i}\right\rangle\delta{\bm{B}},

(89)

i.e., from the viewpoint of
\delta W
the magnetic moments add up additively
Landau and Lifshitz 2013
, not the orbital moments
m_{i}{\bm{R}}_{i}\times\dot{\bm{R}}_{i}
.

Eq. (
89
) formally coincides with the work defined by an external magnetic field in thermo-electrodynamics
Landau and Lifshitz 2013
; see
Narayan and Young 2004
for a recent review. However, those applications refer mostly to quasi-equilibrium (slow) work, whereas (
89
) applies more generally, also to non-equilibrium situations. This is relevant because a thermodynamic expression is deduced from classical Hamiltonian mechanics.

VI.2.2
The issue of gauge-dependence

In the context of (
87
), we should address the following two questions.
First, the very Coulomb gauge
{\rm div}{\bm{A}}=0
is not unique for a homogeneous magnetic field
{\bm{B}}
: we can change

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}({\bm{R}})\to{\bm{A}}({\bm{R}})+\partial_{\bm{R}}\,\chi({\bm{R}}),

(90)

where
\chi({\bm{R}})
is a smooth function satisfying the Laplace equation
\Delta\chi=0
. Then both
{\rm rot}{\bm{A}}={\bm{B}}
and
{\rm div}{\bm{A}}=0
are preserved, but the expression for work (
87
) will obviously change; see Ref.
Giuliani 2010
for a related discussion. This freedom of the Coulomb gauge is unphysical in the following sense. When
{\bm{B}}({\bm{R}})
is inhomogeneous and decays sufficiently quickly at
|{\bm{R}}|\to\infty
, such behavior is also required for
{\bm{A}}({\bm{R}})
, and hence for
\chi({\bm{R}})
. Smooth and non-singular solutions of
\Delta\chi=0
decaying at
|{\bm{R}}|\to\infty
are zero
Allahverdyan and Babajanyan 2016
. The homogeneous
{\bm{B}}
is unphysical, e.g. because it will lead to infinite magnetic energy, since its energy density
\propto{\bm{B}}^{2}
is constant. Appendix
I
shows that if the magnetic field
{\bm{B}}
is properly regularized (so that it decays at
|{\bm{R}}|\to\infty
), (
4
) emerges in a region
|{\bm{R}}|\sim 0
, where
{\bm{B}}
is approximately constant. This relates to the fact that (
4
) is linear over
{\bm{R}}
. Now,
ad hoc
generalizations are possible, e.g.,
{\bm{A}}({\bm{R}})=\frac{\zeta(|{\bm{R}}|)}{2}{\bm{B}}\times{\bm{R}}
, where
\zeta(|{\bm{R}}|)
goes to zero for
|{\bm{R}}|\to\infty
, and
\zeta(|{\bm{R}}|)
is constant for
|{\bm{R}}|\sim 0
. Appendix
I
shows that (
4
) emerges in a more physical way.

The second question is more general: why should the Coulomb gauge be preferred given the gauge freedom
(
90
) with arbitrary smooth
\chi
? This is answered in
Allahverdyan and Babajanyan 2016
, where it is shown in the context of relativistic electrodynamics (and with the proper modeling of sources of work) that the Lorenz gauge is suitable for defining the work done by a time-dependent electromagnetic field on charged particles. For magnetic phenomena, the electric potential is neglected, and the Lorenz gauge reduces to the Coulomb gauge; see also
Allahverdyan and Karakhanyan 2018
in this context.

VI.2.3
Free energy with magnetic field

For slow changes of the magnetic field
{\bm{B}}=\bm{e}_{z}b
, the averaging in (
89
) refers to the rotational Gibbs distribution [for a single particle for simplicity; cf. (
13
)]:

\displaystyle\rho({\bm{V}},{\bm{R}})\propto e^{-\beta[\frac{{\bm{V}}^{2}}{2}+V({\bm{R}},\lambda_{\alpha})-\Omega[({\bm{R}}\times{\bm{V}})_{z}+\frac{b}{2}(X^{2}+Y^{2})]]}.

(91)

We change to canonic variables
{\bm{R}}
and
{\bm{P}}
via (
85
); note that the corresponding Jacobian is
1
:

\displaystyle\rho({\bm{P}},{\bm{R}};b,\lambda_{\alpha})=\frac{1}{Z(b,\lambda)}e^{-\beta[\frac{({\bm{P}}-{\bm{A}})^{2}}{2}+V({\bm{R}},\lambda_{\alpha})-\Omega({\bm{R}}\times{\bm{P}})_{z}]},

(92)

where the rotation axis is
\bm{e}_{z}
[see (
3
)], and where
{\bm{A}}
is given by (
4
). Eq. (
92
)
has the form of the rotational Gibbs distribution [see (
1
) or (
82
)]
but with the canonical orbital momentum
{\bm{R}}\times{\bm{P}}
.

For slow changes of the magnetic field which started at (
92
), the averaging in (
89
) refers to the quasi-equilibrium (
92
), i.e., to
\rho({\bm{P}},{\bm{R}};b(t),\lambda_{\alpha}(t))
. Eq. (
89
) now refers to one component of work in (

83
,
84

-T\ln Z(b,\lambda)
from (

We emphasize that for defining the free energy, it is necessary to have (

{\bm{B}}
are aligned, as already assumed in (

VI.2.4
The mantra ”magnetic field does no work”

As we discussed above, work is done by a time-dependent magnetic field. However, everybody knows and frequently repeats that the magnetic field does not do (mechanical) work on charges, since the (magnetic) Lorentz force acting on any given charge is perpendicular to the velocity of that charge
Landau and Lifshitz 1975
. It is therefore understandable that people wondered how these two statements are consistent. They are consistent, and there is an interesting quest to find microscopic mechanisms behind this consistency; see Refs.
Mosca 1974
;
Coombes 1979
;
Deissler 2008
;
McDonald
;
Allahverdyan and Karakhanyan 2018
;
Schmidt and Bröcker 2024
;
Allahverdyan and Babajanyan 2016
;
Veetil et al. 2023
for discussions on such mechanisms.

VI.3
Free energy of the rotational stationary state

We return to the system (non-weakly) coupled to the rotational thermal bath; see sections
III
,
IV.1
, and
V
. Now the stationary state is characterized by an effective temperature and rotational velocity; see (
55
), (
76a
), and Fig.
4
. These effective parameters depend on the external parameters of the system. Therefore, the standard proof for the existence of free energy outlined above cannot be directly applied. Nonetheless, Appendix
F
shows that the free energy does exist under the following conditions:

– The system coupled to the rotational thermal bath model (
5
) is a Brownian oscillator, i.e., the Hamiltonian
H({\bm{R}},{\bm{P}};\,\lambda_{\alpha})
is a quadratic function of the canonic variables
{\bm{R}}
and
{\bm{P}}
for all values of the external parameters
\{\lambda_{\alpha}\}
(including the magnetic field).

– The Hamiltonian is invariant under rotations along a fixed axis (we choose it to be
\bm{e}_{z}
), which is aligned with the magnetic field
{\bm{B}}
; see (
3
). This leads to 7 parameters
\lambda_{\alpha}
that can be varied externally. The system with Lagrangian (
2
) with harmonic potential
V({\bm{R}})=\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{2}{\bm{R}}^{2}
is a special case of our theorem, where there are two external parameters:
\omega_{0}
of the external potential and the strength of the magnetic field. We continue to maintain mass and charge as unities, since they are not easy to control externally for real Brownian particles.

Two main differences between the present result and the free energies discussed in sections
VI.1
and
VI.2
are as follows. First, sections
VI.1
and
VI.2
addressed a general rotational invariant potential
V({\bm{R}};\lambda_{\alpha})
, but were restricted to the rotational Gibbs distribution for the system. Here we consider only harmonic systems, but relax the restriction of the rotational Gibbs. Second, we only show the existence of the free energy, without providing its explicit form. However, this suffices to assert that any sufficiently slow, cyclic perturbation of the system in the parameter space
\{\lambda_{\alpha}\}
results in zero net work. Consequently, work cannot be extracted from this system, despite it being only in effective equilibrium.

The main similarity of the two results is that we assume a rotationally invariant system Hamiltonian. Looking for the genesis of the existence of free energy and its possible extensions, we can attempt to trace it back to the possibility that the non-Gibbsian stationary state for the Brownian oscillator (with the rotationally invariant Hamiltonian) is a partial trace of the rotational Gibbs distribution (
1
) for the system+bath. Once the free energy exists for the rotational Gibbs, it also exists for its subsystem due to the fundamental feature (
80
) of work. This argument does not imply any weak-coupling limit, since (
80
) does not imply it. While the argument needs further exploration, we now move to the next section, where we relax the assumption of rotational symmetry for the system, while maintaining it for the bath, and demonstrate the emergence of a non-zero cyclic work.

VII
Work Extraction with Non-Rotation-Symmetric Potentials

In the preceding sections, we assumed that the overall system (system+bath) exhibits rotational symmetry. When the system is coupled to the thermal bath, it relaxes to an effective rotational Gibbs distribution. However, an equilibrium rotational Gibbs distribution is achieved only under specific conditions (Section
IV.2
). Additionally, we demonstrated in the previous section that, despite the effective equilibrium, the system has a free energy, which implies that no work can be extracted through a cyclic slow process.

In this section, we consider the same rotational thermal bath described by (
5
), now coupled to a Brownian particle in an non-rotation-symmetric potential:

\displaystyle V({\bm{R}};\,\varphi)=V_{a}\big(\mathsf{R}(-\varphi){\bm{R}}\big)

(93)

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}\left(\varphi\right)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\varphi&-\sin\varphi&0\\
\sin\varphi&\cos\varphi&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},

(94)

where the external parameter
\varphi
refers to the rotation of the non-rotation-symmetric potential
V_{a}
around the
z
-axis by angle
\varphi
.

Suppose the parameter
\varphi(t)
changes over time.
We calculate the work related to this, which is defined as:

\displaystyle\frac{\delta W}{\delta t}=

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{\partial H}{\partial\varphi}\dot{\varphi}\right\rangle_{\bm{R}}=\left\langle\frac{\partial V}{\partial\varphi}\dot{\varphi}\right\rangle_{\bm{R}}

(95)

where
\left\langle~\right\rangle_{\bm{R}}
denotes the average over the non-stationary distribution of the system. In order to calculate the average, we write the Langevin equation (
8
,
27
,
31
):

\displaystyle\ddot{\bm{R}}=-\partial_{\bm{R}}V\big({\bm{R}};\,\varphi(t)\big)-\gamma\dot{\bm{R}}+{\bm{\xi}}(t),

(96)

\displaystyle\left\langle{\bm{\xi}}(t){\bm{\xi}}(t^{\prime})^{\intercal}\right\rangle={\rm C}_{\bm{\xi}}(t-t^{\prime}),

(97)

The noise spectrum is a feature of the bath and is unaffected by the asymmetry of the potential. Thus,
{\rm C}_{\bm{\xi}}(t)
is given by (
27
).

First, we will show that the work can be expressed in terms of the average torque exerted on the particle by the external potential:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V}{\partial\varphi}=M_{z}\qquad M_{z}=-\left[{\bm{R}}\times\partial_{\bm{R}}V({\bm{R}};\,\varphi)\right]_{z}

(98)

Then, we will show that this average does not depend on
\varphi
when
\varphi(t)
is a slow process. This is because the quasi-stationary distribution of the particle in the co-rotational frame of reference is time-independent.

Denoting
{\bm{R}}^{\prime}=\mathsf{R}(-\varphi){\bm{R}}
, we get

\displaystyle\partial_{\bm{R}}V({\bm{R}};\,\varphi)=\mathsf{R}(\varphi)\partial_{{\bm{R}}^{\prime}}V_{a}\big({\bm{R}}^{\prime}\big)

(99)

\displaystyle\partial_{\varphi}V({\bm{R}};\,\varphi)={\bm{R}}^{\intercal}\partial_{\varphi}\mathsf{R}(\varphi)\partial_{{\bm{R}}^{\prime}}V_{a}\big({\bm{R}}^{\prime}\big).

(100)

Using
\partial_{\varphi}\mathsf{R}(\varphi)=\mathsf{P}\!{}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\perp}\!}\mathsf{R}(\pi/2)\mathsf{R}(\varphi)
, where
\mathsf{P}\!{}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\perp}\!}
is the projection to the
x
-
y
plane, we obtain from (
98
–
100
):

\displaystyle\partial_{\varphi}V({\bm{R}};\,\varphi)={\bm{R}}^{\intercal}\,\mathsf{P}\!{}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\perp}\!}\mathsf{R}(\pi/2)\,\partial_{\bm{R}}V({\bm{R}};\,\varphi)

(101)

\displaystyle={\bm{R}}^{\intercal}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\partial_{\bm{R}}V({\bm{R}};\,\varphi)=M_{z}.

(102)

If
\varphi(t)
changes slowly, the system remains in a quasi-stationary state, and the work is given by:

\displaystyle\frac{\delta W}{\delta t}\approx\dot{\varphi}\left\langle\partial_{\varphi}V\right\rangle_{{\bm{R}},\rm ss}=\dot{\varphi}\left\langle M_{z}\right\rangle_{{\bm{R}},\rm ss},

(103)

where the average
\left\langle~\right\rangle_{{\bm{R}},\rm ss}
is taken over the stationary distribution of (
96
) with a constant
\varphi
.
Both
M_{z}
and the steady-state distribution depend on
\varphi
, but this dependence will cancel out in
\left\langle M_{z}\right\rangle_{{\bm{R}},\rm ss}
. To show this, recall
{\bm{R}}^{\prime}=\mathsf{R}(-\varphi){\bm{R}}
and transform (
96
) to
{\bm{R}}^{\prime}
-coordinates:

\ddot{{\bm{R}}}^{\prime}\!=-\partial_{{\bm{R}}^{\prime}}V_{a}({\bm{R}}^{\prime})\!-\!\gamma\dot{{\bm{R}}}^{\prime}\!+\!{\bm{\xi}}^{\prime},\quad~{\bm{\xi}}^{\prime}(t)=\mathsf{R}(-\varphi){\bm{\xi}}(t),

(104)

where
\partial_{{\bm{R}}}=\mathsf{R}(-\varphi)^{\intercal}\partial_{{\bm{R}}^{\prime}}=\mathsf{R}(\varphi)\partial_{{\bm{R}}^{\prime}}
. Recall that the autocorrelation matrix
{\rm C}_{\bm{\xi}}(t)
in (
27
,
97
) is invariant under rotations around the
z
axis. Therefore,

\displaystyle\left\langle{\bm{\xi}}^{\prime}(t){\bm{\xi}}^{\prime}(t^{\prime})^{\intercal}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathsf{R}(-\varphi)\left\langle{\bm{\xi}}(t){\bm{\xi}}(t^{\prime})^{\intercal}\right\rangle\mathsf{R}(\varphi)

(105)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{R}(-\varphi){\rm C}_{\bm{\xi}}(t-t^{\prime})\mathsf{R}(\varphi)={\rm C}_{\bm{\xi}}(t-t^{\prime}).

Thus, the spectrum of
{\bm{\xi}}^{\prime}(t)
and
{\bm{\xi}}(t)
is the same in all the rotated frames.
This implies that Langevin equation (
104
) and the corresponding stationary distribution of
{\bm{R}}^{\prime}
do not depend on
\varphi
, in contrast to the original Langevin equation (
96
).

The torque
M_{z}
in
{\bm{R}}^{\prime}
-coordinates reads:

\displaystyle M_{z}=-\left[{\bm{R}}^{\prime}\times\partial_{{\bm{R}}^{\prime}}V_{a}({\bm{R}}^{\prime})\right]_{z}

(106)

where we applied
{\bm{R}}^{\prime}=\mathsf{R}(-\varphi){\bm{R}}
to (
102
).
Therefore,
\left\langle M_{z}\right\rangle_{{\bm{R}},\rm ss}=\left\langle M_{z}\right\rangle_{{\bm{R}}^{\prime},\rm ss}
and does not depend on
\varphi
.
The total work associated with one complete cycle of the potential, corresponding to a
2\pi
change in
\varphi
, is given by:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\delta W}{\delta\varphi}{\rm d}\varphi=2\pi\left\langle M_{z}\right\rangle=-W_{c},

(107)

where we introduced the minus work
W_{c}
, because
W_{c}>0
means work-extraction, in which we are interested.

We calculate
W_{c}
for a harmonic non-rotation-symmetric potential:

\displaystyle V_{a}({\bm{R}})=\frac{1}{2}\omega_{x}^{2}X^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\omega_{y}^{2}Y^{2}~+\frac{1}{2}\omega_{0}^{2}Z^{2},

(108)

\displaystyle\omega_{x}=\omega_{0}(1+\epsilon),\qquad\omega_{y}=\omega_{0}(1-\epsilon).

(109)

where
V_{a}
has a form of an ellipse with axes matching the coordinate axes, and the external parameter
\varphi
in (
93
) rotates the ellipse by angle
\varphi
.

The torque
M_{z}
in the co-rotational frame evaluates to:

\displaystyle\left\langle M_{z}\right\rangle=4\omega_{0}^{2}\epsilon\left\langle X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}\right\rangle.

(110)

We evaluate
\left\langle X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}\right\rangle
using (
60
) (generalizing (
57
) for the non-rotation-symmetric potential (
108
)):

\displaystyle\left\langle X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{2{\rm i}\,T\omega\Omega(\omega){\rm sgn}(\omega)}{\omega^{2}-\Omega(\omega)^{2}}\,\times

\displaystyle\qquad\times\frac{\gamma}{(\omega_{x}^{2}-\omega^{2}+{\rm i}\gamma\omega)(\omega_{y}^{2}-\omega^{2}-{\rm i}\gamma\omega)}\;{\rm d}\omega.

(111)

This integral vanishes when
\epsilon=0
, as the integrand becomes an odd function, as
\Omega(\omega)
is an odd function; see (
26
).
In Appendix
G
, we evaluate the integral (
111
) in the limit
\gamma\ll\omega_{0}
and
\epsilon\ll 1
, i.e., weak coupling and small asymmetry (see (
211

\displaystyle W_{c}=\frac{\epsilon^{2}\,\frac{\gamma}{2\omega_{0}}}{\epsilon^{2}+\left(\frac{\gamma}{2\omega_{0}}\right)^{2}}\frac{8\pi T\omega_{0}\Omega(\omega_{0})}{\omega_{0}^{2}-\Omega(\omega_{0})^{2}}.

(112)

Note that the friction
\gamma
must be finitely small to achieve non-zero work. For a fixed small friction
\gamma
, at weak asymmetries, the cyclic work increases quadratically with the degree of asymmetry
\epsilon
. However, it saturates at the value

\displaystyle W_{c,\max}=\frac{4\pi\gamma T\Omega(\omega_{0})}{\omega_{0}^{2}-\Omega(\omega_{0})^{2}}.

(113)

This behavior is illustrated in Figure
6
(solid line and dashed line).
Thus, work extraction from a single rotational thermal bath is possible via a slowly rotational asymmetric potential.

VIII
Phenomenological Langevin Equation for a Brownian Particle Interacting with a Rotational Bath

Several phenomenological approaches have been developed to study Brownian motion in a rotational bath
Ryskin 1988
;
Gotoh 1990
;
Miyazaki 1995
; see also
Matevosyan 2024
for the most recent approach.
Here, a phenomenological Langevin equation was proposed that modifies only the friction term of the usual Langevin equation (
{\dot{\bm{R}}}={\bm{V}}
, unit mass):

\displaystyle{\dot{\bm{V}}}=-\partial_{\bm{R}}V({\bm{R}})-\gamma({\bm{V}}-{\bm{\Omega}}\times{\bm{R}})+\sqrt{2\gamma T}{{\bm{\xi}}}(t).

(114)

where
{\bm{R}}
and
{\bm{V}}
are the position and velocity of the particle,
V({\bm{R}})
is the potential,
\gamma
is the friction coefficient,
T
is the temperature, and
{\bm{\xi}}(t)
is the (most usual) white noise with uncorrelated components. As in (
1
), the thermal bath is characterized by two parameters: the temperature
T
and the average rotational velocity
{\bm{\Omega}}
. Eq. (
114
) differs from the standard Langevin equation only in the friction term, which depends on the relative velocity between the particle and the rotational thermal bath,
{\bm{V}}-{\bm{\Omega}}\times{\bm{R}}
. Ref.
Matevosyan 2024
provides phenomenological arguments for deriving (
114
), and fits this equation to numerical experiments. For rotationally symmetric potentials,
V({\bm{R}})=V(|{\bm{R}}|)
, (
114
) reproduces the rotational Gibbs distribution (
1
) in the long-time limit.

The main difference between (
114
) and (
8
) (assuming (
8
) is in the local friction limit for the sake of comparison, i.e. (
31
) holds) is that (
8
) features long-range correlated noise with standard friction, whereas (
114
) employs white noise with a modified friction term. The white-noise limit in (
114
) allows for a convenient analysis using the Fokker-Planck equation for any potential
V({\bm{R}})
, unlike (
8
), where the long-range correlated noise complicates such descriptions for non-linear potentials. However, (
8
) has clearer microscopic foundations.

Regardless of the initial conditions, the system described by (
114
) reaches (for a confining potential) a stationary state, which for non-rotation-symmetric potential differs from the equilibrium rotational Gibbs distribution (
1
). For a harmonic confining potential (
108
), the exact stationary distribution of (
114
) is worked out in
Matevosyan 2024
. Specifically, we are interested in the cyclic work:

\displaystyle W_{c}=

\displaystyle-2\pi\left\langle M_{z}\right\rangle=\frac{\epsilon^{2}\,\frac{\gamma}{2\omega_{0}}}{\epsilon^{2}+\big(1-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{\omega_{0}^{2}}\big)\big(\frac{\gamma}{2\omega_{0}}\big)^{2}}\frac{8\pi T\Omega}{\omega_{0}},

(115)

where the last expression is derived under the limits
\gamma\ll\omega_{0}
and
\epsilon\ll 1
. The full expression is detailed in Appendix
G.4
and visualized in Figure
6
.

We compare the two expressions (
112
) and (
115
) by setting
\Omega=\Omega(\omega_{0})

(notice the phenomenological thermal bath does not have a rotational spectrum
\Omega(\omega)
). It is evident that the work extracted from our thermal bath model is larger than that from the phenomenological bath, as illustrated by the dashed lines in Figure
6
.

The solid lines representing the exact values of
W_{c}
intersect at larger
\epsilon
values (note that the solid blue line is evaluated numerically). It appears that at sufficiently large asymmetries, more work can be extracted from the phenomenological thermal bath. However, the assumption
\Omega=\Omega(\omega_{0})
in (
115
) is valid only in the weak coupling limit; see sections
III

IX
Summary

Our primary goals in this paper were to analyze the microscopic, solvable, oscillator Caldeira-Leggett model for Brownian motion subject to a rotational thermal bath, and to study (mostly slow) work-exchange processes taking place in the rotational stationary state. Here are our results, along with their perspectives and limitations.

– The advantage of the Caldeira-Leggett model is that it is solvable and allows us to deduce exactly the Langevin equation for the Brownian particle. However, the bath composed of harmonic oscillators is unstable for homogeneous rotation. This instability is due to low-frequency oscillators, which are important for the long-time behavior of the Brownian particle. This instability was already noted in the literature
Bekenstein and Schiffer 1998
;
Alicki and Jenkins 2018
.

Our recipe for preventing this instability is to assume that the angular rotation velocity varies with the frequency of the oscillator; see (
21
). An alternative (and perhaps more physical) way of resolving this problem is to assume that bath oscillators are non-linear, at least for sufficiently low frequencies. This will likely change the bath conceptually and lead to new effects, but the model will lose solvability and will be much more difficult to analyze.

One of such effects is related to the phenomenon of sedimentation, where a free Brownian particle is subject to a rotational bath. The model holding (
21
) predicts that for long times
t
the rotation for the free Brownian particle scales as
\sim\ln t
, in contrast to the coordinate, which scales as
\sqrt{t}
; see Appendix
H
. Thus, for the free particle (in contrast to the bound particle we studied here) the rotation is irrelevant at large times. In other words, the intricate Brownian physics of sedimentation processes is still out of reach for microscopic models

Ryskin 1988
;
Gotoh 1990
;
Miyazaki 1995
.

– The main difference of the Langevin equation driven by the rotational bath is that the noise becomes long-range (
\sim t^{-1}
) correlated in the off-diagonal sector due to the rotation; see (
34
–
36
). This feature of the rotational Langevin equation will stay in more general bath models. Its conceptual importance stems from the fact that ordinary Fokker-Planck methods do not apply to the long-range correlated noise. Most likely, this should mean the rotation will lead to new effects in e.g. the particle escape from a non-linear, metastable potential.

– In contrast to the ordinary Langevin equation, the stationary state of the rotational Langevin equation is not the rotational Gibbs distribution (
1
). This point is important to emphasize, since phenomenological approaches to the rotational Brownian motion were proposed with an apparently desirable feature that the stationary state is the rotational Gibbs distribution; see section
VIII
. The latter is recovered only for a vanishingly weak coupling with the bath.

However, this state inherits certain Gibbsian features if the external potential acting on the Brownian particle is rotation symmetric. The most important of them is that there is a free energy, i.e., work-exchange via cyclic, slow processes is impossible. In other words, the concept of reversibility is defined despite the rotation, long-range correlation and non-Gibbsian stationary state; see section
VI
. If the potential is not rotationally symmetric, there are cyclic, slow processes that extract work. A generic class of such processes was shown in section
VII
. It should be interesting to understand to what extent these or similar processes are involved in the work-extraction from rotational parts of ATP; see section
I.2
.

– A rotational charged Brownian particle is an interesting example of a classical system that feels the external magnetic field in its stationary state; see section
V
. All such systems have to be out of the standard Gibbsian equilibrium in one way or another; see
Matevosyan and Allahverdyan 2021
;
Matevosyan and Allahverdyan 2023
for recent discussions.
A surprising point is that under a magnetic field, one needs more stringent conditions for recovering the rotational Gibbs distribution in the weak coupling limit (than with zero magnetic field); cf. Section
V
.

– In this work, we also clarified the concept of work done for a time-dependent magnetic field. This was an unclear subject, but our results presented in section
VI
show that it is nearly as well defined as the ordinary work.

Appendix B
Cauchy Principal Value

The Cauchy Principal Value (CPV) is a technique used to assign finite values to certain improper integrals that would otherwise be undefined due to singularities. For instance, the integral of
{1}/{x}
over the real line has a singularity at
x=0
, and the CPV regularizes this by excluding an infinitesimal interval around
x=0
. Specifically, the principal value of the integral is defined as the limit where a small symmetric region around the singularity is removed. Formally, the CPV of an integral is given by:

\displaystyle{\cal P}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{f(x)}{x}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{-\epsilon}\frac{f(x)}{x}{\rm d}x+\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}\frac{f(x)}{x}{\rm d}x\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}\frac{f(x)-f(-x)}{x}{\rm d}x.

(119)

where
f(x)
decays sufficiently fast at infinity.
This definition ensures that integrals with singularities are well-defined by carefully handling the regions near
x=0
and isolating the “principal” part of the contribution.
For example,

\displaystyle{\cal P}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{1}{x}\,dx=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left(\int_{-1}^{-\epsilon}\frac{1}{x}\,dx+\int_{\epsilon}^{1}\frac{1}{x}\,dx\right)=0.

(120)

One of the most important results involving the Cauchy Principal Value is the Sokhotski-Plemelj formula
Breuer and Petruccione 2002b
, which characterizes the behavior of the complex function
\frac{1}{x\pm i\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
. The formula states that:

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{x\pm i\varepsilon}=\mathcal{P}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)\mp i\pi\delta(x),

(121)

where
\delta(x)
represents the Dirac delta function. Here,
\mathcal{P}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)
denotes the Cauchy Principal Value of
\frac{1}{x}
; see (
119
). Neither
\delta(x)
nor
\mathcal{P}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)
are ordinary functions; instead, they are distributions that act on test functions in integrals
Lighthill 1958
.
In the context of (
121
), note
Lighthill 1958
:

\displaystyle\int{\rm e}^{-i\omega t}\frac{{\rm d}\omega}{2\pi}=\delta(t),~~\int{\rm e}^{-i\omega t}\text{sgn}(\omega)\frac{i\,{\rm d}\omega}{2}={\cal P}\Big(\frac{1}{t}\Big).

(122)

This result is significant in various fields, including complex analysis, mathematical physics, and quantum field theory. The Cauchy Principal Value provides a method to handle singularities in integrals, while the delta function term captures the localized nature of the singularity at
x=0
.

Thus, the most general rotationally symmetric quadratic Hamiltonian has seven independent parameters
\lambda_{\alpha}
. In the specific case where
c=c_{z}=0
,
m_{z}=m=1
, and
b
represents the magnitude of a uniform magnetic field along the
z
-axis, the Hamiltonian (
158
) corresponds to the Lagrangian (
2
) with a harmonic, rotationally symmetric external potential
V({\bm{R}})=\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{2}(X^{2}+Y^{2})+\frac{\omega_{0z}^{2}}{2}Z^{2}
and the gauge (
4
).

We now determine the stationary state distribution of the system, following the approach outlined in Section
IV
, which will be utilized in (
81
). Let
{\rm S}(\omega)
represent the
2d\times 2d
spectral density of the row vector
\left({\bm{R}}(t)^{\intercal}\,{\bm{P}}(t)^{\intercal}\right)
. The second moment is given by

\displaystyle\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{R}}^{\intercal}~{\bm{P}}^{\intercal}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{{\bm{R}}}\\
{{\bm{P}}}\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle={\rm C}(0)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm S}(\omega)\,{\rm d}\omega,

(161)

where
{\rm C}(t)
denotes the
6\times 6
autocorrelation function. The averages can be expressed in terms of
{\rm S}(\omega)
as follows:

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}\right\rangle=\int{\rm d}\omega\Tr\left({\rm S}\frac{\partial\Sigma}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}\right),

(162)

where
\Tr
denotes the trace of the matrix.
Note that only
\Sigma
in (
158
) depends on
\lambda_{\alpha}
.

To establish a relationship between
{\rm S}
and
{\rm S}_{\bm{\xi}}
, consider the Fourier transform of (
157
):

\displaystyle{\rm i}\omega\tilde{{\bm{P}}}=

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial{\bm{R}}}(\tilde{{\bm{R}}},\tilde{{\bm{P}}})-{\rm i}\omega\tilde{\zeta}(\omega)\tilde{{\bm{R}}}+\tilde{\bm{\xi}},

(163a)

\displaystyle{\rm i}\omega\tilde{{\bm{R}}}=

\displaystyle+\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial{\bm{P}}}(\tilde{{\bm{R}}},\tilde{{\bm{P}}}),

(163b)

Solving these equations for
\tilde{{\bm{R}}}
and
\tilde{{\bm{P}}}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{{\bm{R}}}\\
\tilde{{\bm{P}}}\end{pmatrix}={\rm M}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{{\bm{\xi}}}\\
0\end{pmatrix},

(164)

\displaystyle\text{with}\quad{\rm M}(\omega)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}{\rm i}\omega\tilde{\zeta}\,\underline{{\rm I}}\,&{\rm i}\omega\,\underline{{\rm I}}\,\\
-{\rm i}\omega\,\underline{{\rm I}}\,&0\end{pmatrix}+\Sigma\equiv{\rm K}(\omega)

(165)

Note that
{\rm M}^{-1}
is linear in
\Sigma
, such that
{\rm d}({\rm M}^{-1})={\rm d}\,\Sigma
.

Since
\zeta(t)
is real, its Fourier transform satisfies the conjugate symmetry condition:
\tilde{\zeta}(-\omega)=\tilde{\zeta}(\omega)^{\ast}
. Consequently,
{\rm M}(-\omega)={\rm M}(\omega)^{\dagger}
. The spectral density can then be expressed as:

\displaystyle{\rm S}(\omega)={\rm M}(\omega)\bar{\rm S}_{\bm{\xi}}(\omega){\rm M}(\omega)^{\dagger},

(166)

\displaystyle\text{with}\quad\bar{\rm S}_{\bm{\xi}}(\omega)=\begin{pmatrix}{\rm S}_{\bm{\xi}}(\omega)&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(167)

Substituting this into (
162
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\delta W}{\delta\lambda_{\alpha}}=

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}\right\rangle=\int{\rm d}\omega\Tr\left({\rm M}\bar{\rm S}_{\bm{\xi}}{\rm M}^{\dagger}\frac{\partial{\rm M}^{-1}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}\right),

(168)

where we utilized the property
\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}{\rm M}^{-1}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\Sigma
, as seen in (
165
). Recall the identity for the differential of the inverse matrix:

\displaystyle\tfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\lambda}{\rm M}^{-1}=-{\rm M}^{-1}\left(\tfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\lambda}{\rm M}\right){\rm M}^{-1}.

(169)

Using this, we can rewrite the expression as

\displaystyle\frac{\delta W}{\delta\lambda_{\alpha}}=

\displaystyle-\int{\rm d}\omega\Tr\left({\rm M}\bar{\rm S}_{\bm{\xi}}{\rm M}^{\dagger}{\rm M}^{-1}\frac{\partial{\rm M}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}{\rm M}^{-1}\right)

(170)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int{\rm d}\omega\Tr\left(\bar{\rm S}_{\bm{\xi}}{\rm M}^{\dagger}{\rm M}^{-1}\frac{\partial{\rm M}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}\right)

(171)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int{\rm d}\omega\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}{\rm S}^{\xi}_{ij}\left[{\rm M}^{\dagger}{\rm M}^{-1}\frac{\partial{\rm M}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}\right]_{ij},

(172)

where in the second line we applied the cyclic property of the trace. Note that
{\rm S}_{\bm{\xi}}
corresponds to the non-zero upper left block of
\bar{\rm S}_{\bm{\xi}}
, as defined in (
167
).

We claim that the term within the square brackets forms a complete differential for any
1\leq i,j\leq d
:

\displaystyle{\rm d}f=\sum_{\alpha}\left[{\rm M}^{\dagger}{\rm M}^{-1}\frac{\partial{\rm M}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}\right]_{ij}\,{\rm d}\lambda_{\alpha}

(173)

for some function
f(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots;\omega)
. This claim implies the existence of a free energy
F(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots)
such that
\frac{\delta W}{\delta\lambda_{\alpha}}=\frac{\partial F}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}
. We now proceed to prove (
173
).

For convenience, define the following
2d\times 2d
block matrix:

\displaystyle{\rm U}=\begin{pmatrix}\,\underline{{\rm I}}\,&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(174)

Equivalently, we need to show that the following
2d\times 2d
matrix is a complete differential:

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}{\rm U}{\rm M}^{\dagger}{\rm M}^{-1}\frac{\partial{\rm M}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}{\rm U}\,{\rm d}\lambda_{\alpha}

(175)

We further simplify (
175
) using the relation

\displaystyle{\rm M}^{-1}=\left({\rm M}^{\dagger}\right)^{-1}+{\rm i}\omega\left(\tilde{\zeta}+\tilde{\zeta}^{\ast}\right){\rm U}

(176)

which follows from the definition (
165
) and the property
\left({\rm M}^{\dagger}\right)^{-1}=\left({\rm M}^{-1}\right)^{\dagger}
.
We substitute this into (
175
) to obtain:

\displaystyle{\rm U}{\rm M}^{\dagger}{\rm M}^{-1}\frac{\partial{\rm M}}{\partial\lambda}{\rm U}=

\displaystyle{\rm U}\left(\,\underline{{\rm I}}\,+{\rm M}^{\dagger}{\rm i}\omega\left(\tilde{\zeta}+\tilde{\zeta}^{\ast}\right){\rm U}\right)\frac{\partial{\rm M}}{\partial\lambda}{\rm U}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm U}\frac{\partial{\rm M}}{\partial\lambda}{\rm U}+{\rm U}{\rm M}^{\dagger}{\rm U}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\left({\rm U}{\rm M}{\rm U}\right),

(177)

where in the last line we used the identity
{\rm U}^{2}={\rm U}
. Note that the first term is already a complete differential,
{\rm U}\frac{\partial{\rm M}}{\partial\lambda}{\rm U}{\rm d}\lambda_{\alpha}={\rm U}({\rm d}{\rm M}){\rm U}
, so it remains to show that the second term is also a complete differential. First, note that
{\rm U}{\rm M}{\rm U}\equiv{\rm N}
is the upper left block of
{\rm M}
. From (
164
), the matrix
{\rm N}
relates the position and noise

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{R}}}={\rm N}\tilde{{\bm{\xi}}}.

(178)

By substituting
\tilde{{\bm{P}}}
from (
163b
) into (
163a
), we find the expression for
{\rm N}
:

\displaystyle{\rm N}^{-1}=

\displaystyle\left[\left({\rm i}\omega\,\underline{{\rm I}}\,+\Sigma_{RP}\right)\Sigma_{PP}^{-1}\left({\rm i}\omega\,\underline{{\rm I}}\,-\Sigma_{RP}^{\intercal}\right)+\Sigma_{RR}+{\rm i}\omega\zeta\,\underline{{\rm I}}\,\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[{\rm H}+{\rm i}\omega\zeta\,\underline{{\rm I}}\,\right],

(179)

where
{\rm H}={\rm H}^{\dagger}=\left({\rm i}\omega\,\underline{{\rm I}}\,+\Sigma_{RP}\right)\Sigma_{PP}^{-1}\left({\rm i}\omega\,\underline{{\rm I}}\,-\Sigma_{RP}^{\intercal}\right)+\Sigma_{RR}
is a Hermitian matrix. Note that (
178
) and the rotational symmetry of the overall system imply that the matrix
{\rm N}
is invariant under rotations around the symmetry axis.

In (
177
) remains to show that
{\rm N}^{\dagger}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}{\rm N}\,{\rm d}\lambda_{\alpha}
is a complete differential. Here we will employ another approach: we will show that the mixed derivatives are equal:

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\;\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{\beta}}\left({\rm N}^{\dagger}\frac{\partial{\rm N}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}\right)-\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}\left({\rm N}^{\dagger}\frac{\partial{\rm N}}{\partial\lambda_{\beta}}\right),

(180)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{\partial{\rm N}^{\dagger}}{\partial\lambda_{\beta}}\frac{\partial{\rm N}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}-\frac{\partial{\rm N}^{\dagger}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}\frac{\partial{\rm N}}{\partial\lambda_{\beta}}.

(181)

Applying the inverse formula (
169
) to the derivative of
{\rm N}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\rm N}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}\left[{\rm H}+{\rm i}\omega\zeta\,\underline{{\rm I}}\,\right]^{-1}=-{\rm N}\frac{\partial{\rm H}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}{\rm N}

(182)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\rm N}^{\dagger}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}=

\displaystyle-{\rm N}^{\dagger}\frac{\partial{\rm H}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}{\rm N}^{\dagger}

(183)

where we used the fact that only
{\rm H}
in (
179
) depends on the system parameters and
{\rm H}
is Hermitian. Substituting these into (
181
), we get:

\displaystyle{\rm N}^{\dagger}\frac{\partial{\rm H}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}{\rm N}^{\dagger}{\rm N}\frac{\partial{\rm H}}{\partial\lambda_{\beta}}{\rm N}-{\rm N}^{\dagger}\frac{\partial{\rm H}}{\partial\lambda_{\beta}}{\rm N}^{\dagger}{\rm N}\frac{\partial{\rm H}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}{\rm N}.

(184)

Up to this point, we have not utilized the rotational symmetry of the overall system. All matrices in (
184
) are invariant under rotation around a fixed axis. In
d=3
dimensions, such rotations are represented by a tensor product of a 2D rotation and the identity matrix. Since 2D rotations commute, the same property holds for the tensor product. Therefore,
{\rm N}
,
{\rm N}^{\dagger}
,
\frac{\partial{\rm H}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}
, and
\frac{\partial{\rm H}}{\partial\lambda_{\beta}}
commute with each other. Consider the first term in (
184
):

\displaystyle{\rm N}^{\dagger}\frac{\partial{\rm H}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}{\rm N}^{\dagger}{\rm N}\frac{\partial{\rm H}}{\partial\lambda_{\beta}}{\rm N}=

\displaystyle{\rm N}^{\dagger}{\rm N}^{\dagger}{\rm N}\frac{\partial{\rm H}}{\partial\lambda_{\beta}}\frac{\partial{\rm H}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}{\rm N}

(185)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm N}^{\dagger}\frac{\partial{\rm H}}{\partial\lambda_{\beta}}{\rm N}^{\dagger}{\rm N}\frac{\partial{\rm H}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}{\rm N}.

(186)

This makes (
184
) zero, thus proving (
181
). Consequently, we have shown that
{\rm N}^{\dagger}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}{\rm N}\,{\rm d}\lambda_{\alpha}
is a complete differential, as well as (
177
), and finally (
81
).

Importantly, this result is independent of the specific form of the noise spectrum (
167
). We have demonstrated that each summand in (
172
) is a complete differential, which depends solely on the form of the dynamical equations (
157
) and the rotational symmetry of the system.

Appendix H
Free Brownian Motion Coupled to Rotational Thermal Bath

In this section, we investigate the transient distribution of a free Brownian particle coupled to a rotational thermal bath:

\displaystyle\ddot{{\bm{R}}}=-\gamma\dot{{\bm{R}}}+{\bm{\xi}}.

(219)

where
{\bm{R}}=(X,\,Y,\,Z)
. For analytical calculations, we consider a linear angular velocity spectrum of bath oscillators, as in (
37
), so the autocorrelation of noise
{\bm{\xi}}
is (
III
). We assume that at time
t=0
the particle starts from position
{\bm{R}}(0)=0
and with velocity
\dot{\bm{R}}(0)=0
. The moments depending on only one coordinate are easy to calculate due to white noise on the diagonals of (
III
):

\displaystyle\left\langle X^{2}(t)\right\rangle=\left\langle Y^{2}(t)\right\rangle=\frac{q}{\gamma^{2}}t-\frac{q}{\gamma^{3}}\left(1-{\rm e}^{-\gamma t}\right)\left(3-{\rm e}^{-\gamma t}\right)

\displaystyle\left\langle V_{x}^{2}(t)\right\rangle=\left\langle V_{y}^{2}(t)\right\rangle=\frac{q}{2\gamma}\left(1-{\rm e}^{-2\gamma t}\right)

\displaystyle\left\langle X(t)V_{x}(t)\right\rangle=\left\langle Y(t)V_{y}(t)\right\rangle=0

(220)

where
q=\frac{2\gamma T}{1-a^{2}}
is the intensity of the white noise.
From the rotational symmetry of the system, we have:

\displaystyle\left\langle X(t)Y(t)\right\rangle=\left\langle X(t)V_{y}(t)\right\rangle=\left\langle Y(t)V_{x}(t)\right\rangle=0.

(221)

Below we calculate the remaining moments
\left\langle X(t)V_{y}(t)\right\rangle=-\left\langle Y(t)V_{x}(t)\right\rangle
. We start with (
123
):

\displaystyle\left\langle XV_{y}\right\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{0}^{t}\!K(t-u)K^{\prime}(t-u^{\prime})\left\langle\xi_{x}(u)\xi_{y}(u^{\prime})\right\rangle{\rm d}u{\rm d}u^{\prime}

\displaystyle~=-\frac{2\gamma T}{\pi}\frac{a}{1\!-\!a^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{0}^{t}\!K(t\!-\!u)K^{\prime}(t\!-\!u^{\prime}){\cal P}\left(\tfrac{1}{u-u^{\prime}}\right){\rm d}u{\rm d}u^{\prime}

\displaystyle~=\frac{2\gamma T}{\pi}\frac{a}{1-a^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{0}^{t}K(u)K^{\prime}(u^{\prime}){\cal P}\left(\tfrac{1}{u-u^{\prime}}\right){\rm d}u{\rm d}u^{\prime}

(222)

where Laplace kernel
K(t)
is

\displaystyle K(t)=\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(1-{\rm e}^{-\gamma t}\right),\quad K^{\prime}(t)={\rm e}^{-\gamma t}

(223)

This consists of integrals of the form:

\displaystyle F(t;\alpha,\beta)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}{\rm e}^{-\alpha x}{\rm e}^{-\beta y}{\cal P}\left(\frac{1}{x-y}\right){\rm d}x{\rm d}y

(224)

So, the moment
\left\langle X(t)V_{y}(t)\right\rangle
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\left\langle X(t)V_{y}(t)\right\rangle=

\displaystyle\textsection\frac{2T}{\pi}\frac{a}{1-a^{2}}\left[F(t;0,\gamma)-F(t;\gamma,\gamma)\right]

(225)

In order to evaluate (
224
), recall the following elementary functions:

\displaystyle\text{Exponential Integral:}\quad\text{Ei}(x)=-\int_{-x}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t}{\rm e}^{-t}{\rm d}t,

(226)

\displaystyle\text{Sinh Integral:}\quad\text{Shi}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\sinh t}{t}{\rm d}t

(227)

\displaystyle\hskip 94.72192pt=\frac{1}{2}\left(\text{Ei}(x)-\text{Ei}(-x)\right),

(228)

where
\frac{1}{t}
in the integrals are interpreted as the Cauchy Principal Value, as defined in (
119
).

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}{\rm e}^{-\alpha x}{\cal P}\left(\frac{1}{x-y}\right){\rm d}x=

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-\alpha y}\int_{-y}^{t-y}{\rm e}^{-\alpha x}{\cal P}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right){\rm d}x

(229)

So for
F(t;\alpha,\beta)
we have:

\displaystyle F(t;\alpha,\beta)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}{\rm e}^{-\beta y}{\rm e}^{-\alpha y}\left[\text{Ei}(-\alpha(t-y))-\text{Ei}(\alpha y)\right]{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,{\rm e}^{-(\alpha+\beta)t}\int_{0}^{t}{\rm e}^{(\alpha+\beta)y}\text{Ei}(-\alpha y){\rm d}y

(230)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}{\rm e}^{-(\beta+\alpha)y}\text{Ei}(\alpha y){\rm d}y

(231)

We apply integration by parts to each of these integral. The second integral reads:

\displaystyle\int_{\epsilon}^{t}{\rm e}^{-(\alpha+\beta)y}\text{Ei}(\alpha y){\rm d}y=

(232)

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{\alpha\!+\!\beta}\Big[\!-{\rm e}^{-(\alpha+\beta)y}\,\text{Ei}(\alpha y)\Big|_{\epsilon}^{t}+\!\int_{\epsilon}^{t}\frac{{\rm e}^{-\beta y}}{y}{\rm d}y\,\Big]

(233)

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}\Big(-{\rm e}^{-(\alpha+\beta)t}\text{Ei}(\alpha t)+\text{Ei}(-\beta t)

(234)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\Big[{\rm e}^{-(\alpha+\beta)\epsilon}\text{Ei}(\alpha\epsilon)-\text{Ei}(-\beta\epsilon)\Big]\Big)

(235)

where we used
\text{Ei}^{\prime}(x)={{\rm e}^{x}}/{x}
. Then, we take the limit
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
. For small negative arguments,
\text{Ei}(-x)=\gamma_{E}+\ln x+O(x)
, where
\gamma_{E}
is the Euler constant. Using (
228
), we change positive arguments to negative ones:
\text{Ei}(\alpha\epsilon)=\text{Ei}(-\alpha\epsilon)+2\text{Shi}(\alpha\epsilon)
, and note that
\text{Shi}(0)=0
. Thus, we have:

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\left[{\rm e}^{-(\alpha+\beta)\epsilon}(\text{Ei}(-\alpha\epsilon)+2\text{Shi}(\alpha\epsilon))-\text{Ei}(-\beta\epsilon)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\left[\log(\alpha\epsilon)-\log(\beta\epsilon)+O(\epsilon)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\log\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)

(236)

We evaluate the first integral (
230
) in a similar way:

\displaystyle\int_{\epsilon}^{t}{\rm e}^{(\beta+\alpha)y}\text{Ei}(-\alpha y){\rm d}y=

(237)

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}\Big[{\rm e}^{(\alpha+\beta)y}\text{Ei}(-\alpha y)\Big|_{\epsilon}^{t}-\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}\int_{\epsilon}^{t}\frac{{\rm e}^{\beta y}}{y}{\rm d}y\Big]

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}\Big[{\rm e}^{(\alpha+\beta)t}\text{Ei}(-\alpha t)-\text{Ei}(\beta t)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad-\Big({\rm e}^{(\alpha+\beta)\epsilon}\text{Ei}(-\alpha\epsilon)-\text{Ei}(\beta\epsilon)\Big)\Big]

(238)

The limit is also similar:

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\left[{\rm e}^{(\alpha+\beta)\epsilon}\text{Ei}(-\alpha\epsilon)-\text{Ei}(-\beta\epsilon)-2\text{Shi}(\beta\epsilon)\right]=

\displaystyle~~~=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\left[\log(\alpha\epsilon)-\log(\beta\epsilon)+O(\epsilon)\right]=\log\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)

(239)

Thus, we obtain:

\displaystyle F(t;\alpha,\beta)=\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}\Big[\text{Ei}(-\alpha t)-\text{Ei}(-\beta t)

(240)

\displaystyle+{\rm e}^{-(\alpha+\beta)t}\left[\text{Ei}(\alpha t)-\text{Ei}(\beta t)\right]-\log(\alpha/\beta)(1+{\rm e}^{-(\alpha+\beta)t})\Big]

We will also need this for
\alpha=0
or
\beta=0
. Taking the corresponding limits:

\displaystyle F(t;0,\beta)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\Big[(\gamma+\log(\beta t))(1+{\rm e}^{-\beta t})

(241)

\displaystyle\quad-\text{Ei}(-\beta t)-{\rm e}^{-\beta t}\text{Ei}(\beta t)\Big],

(242)

\displaystyle F(t;\alpha,0)=

\displaystyle\,-F(t,0,\alpha)

(243)

Turning back to the moment (
225
), we have:

\displaystyle\left\langle X(t)V_{y}(t)\right\rangle=\frac{2T}{\pi}\frac{a}{1-a^{2}}\left[F(t;0,\gamma)-F(t;\gamma,\gamma)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{2T}{\pi}\frac{a}{1-a^{2}}\left[-\text{Ei}(-t\gamma)-{\rm e}^{-\gamma t}\text{Ei}(\gamma t)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+(1+{\rm e}^{-\gamma t})\left(\gamma_{E}+\log(\gamma t)\right)\right]

(244)

where
\gamma_{E}\approx 0.58
is the Euler constant. For small times, we get:

\displaystyle\left\langle X(t)V_{y}(t)\right\rangle=\frac{2T}{\pi}\frac{a}{1-a^{2}}\frac{\gamma t^{2}}{2}

(245)

and for large times it scales logarithmically:

\displaystyle\left\langle X(t)V_{y}(t)\right\rangle\sim\frac{2T}{\gamma\pi}\frac{a}{1-a^{2}}\log(t).

(246)

Appendix I
Vector Potential in Coulomb Gauge for Circular Current Loop

The purpose of this Appendix is to evaluate the vector potential
{\bm{A}}({\bm{R}})
in the Coulomb gauge
{\rm div}{\bm{A}}=0
for a magnetic field
{\bm{B}}({\bm{R}})
which goes to zero for
|{\bm{R}}|\to\infty
, but is
approximately homogeneous for
|{\bm{R}}|\sim 0
.

To this end, we recall
that cylindrical coodinates
r
,
\phi
and
z
are defined via the
rectangular coordinates
X
,
Y
and
Z
:
X=r\cos\phi
and
Y=r\sin\phi
. The correspoding unit vectors are

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{r}=\cos\phi\,\bm{e}_{x}+\sin\phi\,\bm{e}_{y},\qquad\bm{e}_{\phi}=-\sin\phi\,\bm{e}_{x}+\cos\phi\,\bm{e}_{y},

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{r}\times\bm{e}_{\phi}=\bm{e}_{z},\quad\bm{e}_{z}\times\bm{e}_{r}=\bm{e}_{\phi},\quad\bm{e}_{\phi}\times\bm{e}_{z}=\bm{e}_{r}.

(247)

Any vector expands as

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}=A_{r}\bm{e}_{r}+A_{\phi}\bm{e}_{\phi}+A_{z}\bm{e}_{z}.

(248)

Next, let us recall that
{\bm{A}}({\bm{R}})
in the Coulomb gauge directly relates to the electric current
\bm{J}({\bm{R}})
, which generates the magnetic field
Landau and Lifshitz 1975
[we take
c=1
]:

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}({\bm{R}})=\int{\rm d}^{3}R^{\prime}\,\frac{\bm{J}({\bm{R}}^{\prime})}{|{\bm{R}}-{\bm{R}}^{\prime}|}.

(249)

We consider
\bm{J}
that within (
248
) has only non-zero component
J_{\phi}(r,z)
, which does not depend on
\phi
. The conservation condition
{\rm div}\bm{J}=0
is automatically satisfied since:

\displaystyle{\rm div}\bm{J}=\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial(\rho J_{\rho})}{\partial\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial J_{\phi}}{\partial\phi}+\frac{\partial J_{z}}{\partial z}=0.

(250)

Using (
247
,
248
) we transform (
249
) to cylindrical coordinates and find

\displaystyle A_{x}({\bm{R}})=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int r{\rm d}r\,{\rm d}\phi\,{\rm d}z

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{{J}_{\phi}(r,z)\sin\phi}{\sqrt{(X-r\cos\phi)^{2}+(Y-r\sin\phi)^{2}+(Z-z)^{2}}},

(251)

\displaystyle A_{y}({\bm{R}})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int r{\rm d}r\,{\rm d}\phi\,{\rm d}z

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{{J}_{\phi}(r,z)\cos\phi}{\sqrt{(X-r\cos\phi)^{2}+(Y-r\sin\phi)^{2}+(Z-z)^{2}}},

(252)

where
{\bm{R}}=(X,Y,Z)
and
A_{z}=0
. The simplest way of evaluating (
I
,
252
) is to assume that
J_{\phi}(r,z)
as a function of
r
and
z
is localized:

\displaystyle J_{\phi}(r,z)=\delta(r-R_{0})\delta(z)\hat{J}_{\phi},

(253)

and then look at (
I
,
252
) in the domain
|{\bm{R}}|\ll R_{0}
:

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}=\frac{\hat{J}_{\phi}\pi}{2R_{0}}[-Y,X,0]+{\cal O}(|{\bm{R}}|^{2}/R_{0}^{2}),

(254)

which agrees with (
4
).
