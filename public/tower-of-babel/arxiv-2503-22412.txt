Casimir pressure with dissipation and quantum corrections from axion dark matter
Philippe Brax, Pierre Brun

Abstract
We study the effects of the oscillating axion field present in our environment on the Casimir pressure between two metallic plates. We take into account the finite conductivity of the boundary plates and model the interactions between matter and photons in the Schwinger-Keldysh formalism. This allows us to take into account dissipation in the quantum field description of this open quantum system and retrieve the Lifschitz results for the Casimir interaction between two metallic plates. We then compute the leading correction to the Lifschitz theory in inverse powers of the axion suppression scale and show that the Casimir pressure receives oscillating corrections depending on the product of the axion mass and the distance between the plates. This contribution is repulsive at large distance compared to the axion Compton wavelength as a consequence of the breaking of parity invariance by the axion dark matter background.

The Casimir system with two metallic plates where dissipation and dispersion takes place is a testbed for dissipative open quantum system. Indeed, as soon as the plates are not perfect, the reflection coefficients of the walls differ from unity and therefore energy flows from the vacuum cavity to the plates implying that the cavity is not a closed quantum system anymore. There is an exchange of energy with the plates which is reinforced in the dissipative case where currents inside the plaques can be excited and the Joule effect prevents the existence of thermal equilibrium if the transfer of heat into radiation is not taken into account.

II
Photons in matter coupled to axions

II.1
The action

As we are interested in the axion in the presence of metallic plates, we will calculate the quantum Casimir effect using the vacuum Lagrangian and its modified version taking into account the interaction of photons with matter. In vacuum we have

\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}-\frac{\phi}{4M}F^{{\mu\nu}}\tilde{F}_{\mu\nu}

(1)

where
\tilde{F}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}F^{\rho\sigma}
is the dual field strength and
M
is an energy scale of the order of the Peccei-Quinn symmetry breaking scale. We define
\epsilon^{0123}=1
. The pseudo-scalar field
\phi
will be assumed to be time dependent, as representing the oscillations of the dark matter field in our environment. Notice that when the axion field becomes constant, the coupling between the axion and photons becomes irrelevant as
F\tilde{F}
is a derivative term and we do not consider non-trivial topological effects. We work in the Coulomb gauge where
A^{0}=0
and
\partial_{i}A^{i}=0
which selects two transverse polarisations. We are interested in situations where there is a background magnetic field parallel to the plates
[
14
]

a^{\mu}
of the potential vector where

A^{\mu}=\bar{A}^{\mu}+a^{\mu}
and
\bar{A}^{\mu}

[

S=\int d^{4}x\left(\frac{1}{2}(\vec{e}^{2}-\vec{b}^{2})+\frac{\phi}{M}\vec{e}.\vec{b}-\vec{\cal J}.\vec{a}\right)

(2)

where the have introduced the current

{\cal J}^{\mu}=\frac{1}{2M}\partial_{\nu}\phi\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\bar{F}_{\rho\sigma}.

(3)

In a time-dependent axionic background this corresponds to the current induced by the axion and the external background field

\vec{\cal J}=-\frac{\dot{\phi}}{M}\vec{B}.

(4)

This action reproduces the equations of motion for the photon field in the presence of the axion field.

When coupled to matter, in particular a metal where dissipation takes place, we would like to find an action which reproduces the phenomenological field equations
of motion

\partial_{0}\vec{d}-\vec{\nabla}\wedge\vec{b}=-\frac{\dot{\phi}}{M}\vec{b}+\vec{\cal J}.

(5)

which corresponds to

-\partial_{0}(\epsilon\star_{t}\partial_{0}\vec{a})+\Delta\vec{a}=-\frac{\dot{\phi}}{M}\vec{b}+\vec{\cal J}.

(6)

where we work in the Coulomb gauge with
a^{0}=0

(\Delta+\epsilon(\omega)\omega^{2})\vec{a}=0

The propagation speed is now
1/\sqrt{\epsilon(\omega)}

II.3.2
The Feynman propagator

In the quantum field theoretic treatment of the Casimir effect which will be expounded below, the Feynman propagator corresponding to the
T
-product of the field is required. The Feynman propagator can be obtained using an adaptation of the Schwarz reflection principle where holomorphic functions are extended from the upper half plane to the lower one. In the right half place with
\Re\omega>0

\Re\omega\geq 0:G_{F}(\omega)=G(\omega)

(50)

where we have omitted the dependence on the other variables to simplify the notation. In the left half plane we define

\Re\omega\leq 0:G_{F}(\omega)=\bar{G}(\bar{\omega})=G(-\omega).

(51)

The Feynman propagator is obtained through a reflection with respect to the origin which sends the resonances in the right half plane to the left half plane above the negative real axis.
This defines the Feynman propagator in the whole
\omega
complex plane.
In terms of integrals, the Feynman propagator reads explicitly

G_{F}(t,\vec{x})=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\not{d}\omega\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\cos\omega t~e^{i\vec{p}_{\parallel}.\vec{x}_{\parallel}}G(\omega,\vec{p}_{\parallel},z)

which satisfies the same partial derivative equation as the retarded propagator

implying that
-\partial_{t}(\epsilon(t)\star_{t}\partial_{t})G_{F}+\Delta G_{F}=\delta^{(4)}(x^{\mu})
.
.

(57)

This definition of the Feynman propagator is such that the function

which is only valid when

. In this case the resonances are displaced from the real axis by an infinitesimal amount. When this is not the case, the Feynman propagator has a branch cut on the imaginary axis apart from the origin where it is continuous. This is discussed in the next section.

II.3.3
The Feynman propagator for finite dissipation

When
\gamma\neq 0
and is not taken to be infinitesimal, the Feynman propagator defined in the complex
\omega
-plane by a reflection across the origin is not continuous along the imaginary axis apart from the origin. When calculating the Fourier transform as an integral along the real line
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
, this is not an issue. When going to the complex plane and closing the contour in the lower half plane for instance, this requires to modify the integration path by including a contribution going from
-i\infty
to
0
and back to
-i\infty
along both sides of the branch cut which are joined at the origin where the propagator is continuous. This gives a new contribution to
G_{F}^{+}
which reads

\delta G_{F}^{+}=\int_{-i\infty}^{0}\not{d}\omega\int\not{d}^{2}{p_{\parallel}}e^{-i\omega(t-t_{0})}e^{i\vec{p}_{\parallel}.(\vec{x}_{\parallel}-\vec{x}_{0\parallel})}(G(\omega)-G(-\omega))Y(t-t_{0})

(59)

This is necessary to lift the obstruction to constructing the Feynman propagator as a continuous function across the imaginary axis. As expected, this new contribution preserves the structure of the Feynman propagator as a T-product.

In practice, we will see that it is easier to calculate the Feynman propagator at equal times by first performing a Wick’s rotation sending the real axis to the imaginary axis via a rotation of a
\pi/2
angle. As the axis is rotated, it does not cross poles and branch cuts. The equality of the two integrals over
\omega
is guaranteed as long the integrand converges to zero on a large circular quadrant joining the two axes. For very large
|\omega|
the permittivity is close to unity and the Feynman propagator behaves like the one in empty space in
1/|\omega|
. This justifies the validity of Wick’s rotation. Instead of using the contour drawn in Fig.
4
, the integral can be performed very close to the positive part of the imaginary axis after Wick’s rotation, as schematically shown in Fig.
5
. Because of the parity on the imaginary axis, this gives half the desired result.

Figure 5:
The Feynman propagator can be Wick-rotated to the vicinity of the imaginary axis. After this Wick’s rotation the integral is computed on a line infinitely close of the positive part of the imaginary axis.

After the rotation the integral for the Feynman propagator lies to the right of the imaginary axis in the upper half plane and to its left in the lower half. Analytically we have
G_{F}(i\omega)=G(i\omega)
along this path. As a result, we can use the expressions for the retarded Green’s function to obtain the Casimir pressure for instance as we shall see below.

III
Path integral quantisation

III.1
The two polarisations

A direct approach to quantisation bypasses the interpretation of the field
a_{i}
in terms of particles, which is flawed as Poincaré invariance is broken and there is dissipation in the system.
We first decompose the vectors
a_{i},\ i=(x,y,z),
as

a_{i}(\vec{x},t)=\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\int\not{d}^{3}ke_{\alpha i}(k)a_{\alpha}(k,t)e^{i\vec{k}.\vec{x}}=\sum_{\alpha}e_{\alpha i}\star_{x}a_{\alpha}

(60)

where
\star_{x}
is the convolution operator in space. The polarisation vectors are complex such that
\bar{e}_{i\alpha}(k)=e_{i\alpha}(-k)
and satisfy the familiar orthogonality relations

\sum_{\alpha}\bar{e}_{\alpha i}(k)e_{\alpha j}(k)=\delta_{ij}-\frac{k_{i}k_{j}}{k},\ \ k^{i}e_{\alpha i}(k)=0,\ \ \bar{e}_{\alpha i}(k)e^{i}_{\beta}(k)=\delta_{\alpha\beta}

(61)

where
k^{2}=k_{i}k^{i}
. The momenta are real. The vector field is Hermitian as long as the two scalar polarisation fields satisfy
a_{\alpha}^{\dagger}=a_{\alpha}
, i.e. they are real scalar fields. Similarly we introduce two scalar field
\tilde{a}_{\alpha}
for the shadow vector field
\vec{\tilde{a}}
.
We define the Fourier tranform for each polarisation scalar as

a_{\alpha}(\vec{x},t)=\int\not{d}^{3}ka_{\alpha}(k,t)e^{i\vec{k}.\vec{x}}.

(62)

As a consequences we have for the displacement vector

d_{i}=-\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\int\not{d}^{3}ke_{\alpha i}(k)(\epsilon\star_{t}\dot{a}_{\alpha})(k,t)e^{i\vec{k}.\vec{x}}=\sum_{\alpha=\pm}e_{\alpha i}\star_{x}\dot{d}_{\alpha}

(63)

and we have used the property
\partial_{0}(\epsilon\star_{t}a_{\alpha})(k,t)=(\epsilon\star_{t}\dot{a}_{\alpha})(k,t)
.

III.2
The Hamiltonian and dissipation

Let us now come back to the quantisation procedure.
We can also calculate the associated Hamiltonian using the conjugate momenta

\vec{\pi}_{a}=\hat{\epsilon}\star_{t}\partial_{0}\vec{\tilde{a}}-\frac{\phi}{M}\vec{\tilde{b}},\ \ \vec{\pi}_{\tilde{a}}=\epsilon\star_{t}\partial_{0}\vec{a}-\frac{\tilde{\phi}}{M}\vec{b}

(64)

and then obtain

H=\int d^{3}x(\partial_{0}\vec{\tilde{a}}(\epsilon\star_{t}\partial_{0}\vec{a})+\partial_{i}\vec{a}.\partial_{i}\vec{\tilde{a}}+\vec{\cal J}.\vec{\tilde{a}}+\vec{\tilde{\cal J}}.\vec{a}))

(65)

where the dependence on the axion field only appears in the source terms.
From the quantum action
S_{\rm qu}
we get the Hamiltonian

H_{\rm qu}=\int d^{3}x(\partial_{0}\tilde{a}^{i}(\epsilon\star_{t}\partial_{0}a^{i})+\partial_{i}\tilde{a}_{j}\partial_{i}a_{j})

(66)

where we will suppress the
{\rm qu}
index for convenience below as we are only dealing with the quantum properties from now on.
Notice that the explicit axion interactions do not enter the Hamiltonian. They appear in the definition of the canonical momenta.

We will separate the Hamiltonian corresponding to the free theory in vacuum and the part containing the matter permittivity. We have

H_{0}=\int d^{3}x(\partial_{0}\tilde{a}_{i}\partial_{0}a_{i}+\partial_{i}\tilde{a}_{j}\partial_{i}a_{j})=\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\int d^{3}x(\partial_{0}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}\partial_{0}a_{\alpha}+\partial_{i}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}\partial_{i}a_{\alpha})

(67)

corresponding to two systems of independent free scalar fields.
The free Hamiltonian is corrected by interaction terms

H_{I}=\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\int d^{3}x((\partial_{0}\tilde{a}_{\alpha})\chi\star_{t}\partial_{0}a_{\alpha})

(68)

where we have defined

\chi(\omega)=\epsilon(\omega)-1.

(69)

Associated to the free Hamiltonian are the free conjugate momenta

\vec{\pi}_{a}^{0}=\partial_{0}\vec{\tilde{a}},\ \ \vec{\pi}_{\tilde{a}}^{0}=\partial_{0}\vec{a}

(70)

such that

H_{0}=\int d^{3}x(\vec{\pi}^{0}_{a}.\vec{\pi}^{0}_{\tilde{a}}+\partial_{i}\tilde{a}_{j}\partial_{i}a_{j})

(71)

and the interaction Hamiltonian

H_{I}=\int d^{3}x(\vec{\pi}^{0}_{\tilde{a}}.\chi\star_{t}\vec{\pi}_{a}^{0}).

(72)

In the schrödinger picture, the free conjugate momenta are associated to the functional derivatives
\vec{\pi}_{a}^{0}=\frac{1}{i}\frac{\delta}{\delta\vec{a}}
and similarly for
\vec{\pi}^{0}_{\tilde{a}}=\frac{1}{i}\frac{\delta}{\delta\vec{\tilde{a}}}
. As operators the two momenta commute. Now
let us take the Hermitian conjugate of the interaction Hamiltonian density

[(\partial_{0}\tilde{a}_{\alpha})(\chi\star_{t}\partial_{0}a_{\alpha})]^{\dagger}=(\hat{\chi}\star_{t}\partial_{0}a_{\alpha})(\partial_{0}\tilde{a}_{\alpha})

(73)

where
\hat{\chi}(t)=\chi(-t)
.
We see that

H_{I}^{\dagger}\neq H_{I}

(74)

as soon as
\chi(-\omega)\neq\chi(\omega)
,
i.e. the interaction Hamiltonian is not Hermitian. This is a consequence of dissipation.

III.3
Schwinger-Keldysh formalism

We are interested in vacuum expectation values in this theory with dissipation. In the Heisenberg representation of quantum mechanics where
operators evolve and states are fixed, we shall quantise by taking as states (and the full Hilbert space of states) the one of the free theory. The operators evolve in time and the generator of the time evolution is the Hamiltonian. The free evolution operator satisfies

\partial_{t}U_{0}=-iH_{0}U_{0}

(75)

whose solution is taken as the time ordered product

U_{0}(t)=T(e^{-i\int_{-\infty}^{t}H_{0}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}).

(76)

It is convenient to define the operators in the interaction representation where this free evolution is taken into account

a_{\alpha I}(t,\vec{x})=U_{0}^{\dagger}(t)a_{\alpha I}(-\infty,\vec{x})U_{0}(t).

(77)

The free evolution operator is unitary as
H_{0}
is Hermitian.

The time evolution of the operators involves the total Hamiltonian which is not Hermitian hence the evolution operator is not unitary. It satisfies

\partial_{t}U=-iHU

(78)

whose solution is again the time ordered product, i.e. operators are ordered from left to right from the greatest time to the lowest

U(t)=T\left(e^{-i\int_{-\infty}^{t}H(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}\right).

(79)

In the interaction picture we introduce

U_{I}(t)=U_{0}^{\dagger}(t)U(t)

(80)

which satisfies the equation

\partial_{t}U_{I}=-iU_{0}^{\dagger}H_{I}(a_{\alpha},t)U_{0}U_{I}.

(81)

We define

{\cal H}_{I}(a_{I\alpha})=U_{0}^{\dagger}H_{I}(a_{\alpha},t)U_{0}

(82)

as the interaction Hamiltonian in the interaction picture, i.e.

{\cal H}_{I}(a_{\alpha I})=\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\int d^{3}x(\partial_{0}\tilde{a}_{\alpha I})(\chi\star_{t}\partial_{0}a_{\alpha I}).

(83)

As result we have the T-product exponentiation

U_{I}=T\left(e^{-i\int_{-\infty}^{t}{\cal H}_{I}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}\right)

(84)

which satisfies
U_{I}(-\infty)={\cal I}
where
{\cal I}
is the identity operator.
Similarly, we have the time evolution

a_{\alpha}(t)=U_{I}^{\dagger}(t)a_{\alpha I}(t)U_{I}(t)

(85)

between the operators in the interaction picture
a_{\alpha I}
and the quantum field operators
a_{\alpha}
.
The same is true for any operator

{\cal O}(t)=U_{I}^{\dagger}(t){\cal O}_{I}(t)U_{I}(t)

(86)

where
{\cal O}_{I}=U_{0}^{\dagger}{\cal O}U_{0}
.
Notice that we have

\partial_{t}U_{I}^{\dagger}=iU_{I}^{\dagger}{\cal H}_{I}^{\dagger}(a_{\alpha},t).

(87)

whose solution is

U_{I}^{\dagger}=\bar{T}\left(e^{i\int_{0}^{t}{\cal H}_{I}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}\right)

(88)

where the
\bar{T}
time ordering puts all the fields from left to right in increasing time order.

We will be interested in simple observables involving only one time and defined by the vacuum expectation value of operators
{\cal O}(t)
. For instance we will take

{\cal O}(t)=a_{\alpha}(\vec{x},t)\tilde{a}_{\beta}(\vec{y},t)
. We will consider the steady state situation reached after bringing the two plates together and waiting for a long time. In this case, we will have access to

\langle 0|{\cal O}(+\infty)|0\rangle=\langle 0|T_{C}\left[{\cal O}_{I}(t)U_{I}(-\infty,-\infty)\right]|0\rangle

(89)

where we have identified the time-ordered product along the double line from
-\infty
to
+\infty
and back, see appendix
B
for details.

\langle 0|T_{C}\left[{\cal O}_{I}(+\infty)U_{I}(-\infty,-\infty)\right]|0\rangle\equiv\langle 0|U_{I}^{\dagger}(+\infty){\cal O}_{I}(+\infty)U_{I}(+\infty)|0\rangle.

(90)

Along the upper part of the path
C
the evolution operator involves
{\cal H}_{I}
and on the lowest part
{\cal H}_{I}^{\dagger}
.
This representation allows one to write this vacuum expectation value as the path integral

\langle 0|{\cal O}(+\infty)|0\rangle=\int{\cal D}a^{1}_{\alpha}{\cal D}a^{2}_{\alpha}{\cal D}\tilde{a}^{1}_{\alpha}{\cal D}\tilde{a}^{2}_{\alpha}{\cal O}_{I}e^{iS(a_{\alpha}^{1},\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1})-iS^{\dagger}(a_{\alpha}^{2},\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{2})}

(91)

involving two copies of the two polarisation scalars
a^{a}_{\alpha},\tilde{a}^{a}_{\alpha}\ a=1,2
labelling the upper and lower parts of the Schwinger-Keldysh contour.
The operator
{\cal O}_{I}
is inserted on the upper path.
Of course as a boundary condition we must impose that
a_{\alpha}^{1}(\infty)=a_{\alpha}^{2}(\infty)
and similarly
\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}(\infty)=\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{2}(\infty)
which imposes constraints on the Green’s functions.

The action in the Schwinger-Keldysh formalism is defined by

S(a^{a}_{\alpha},\tilde{a}^{a}_{\alpha})=S(a_{\alpha}^{1},\tilde{a}^{1}_{\alpha})-S^{\dagger}(a_{\alpha}^{2},\tilde{a}^{2}_{\alpha}).

(92)

The quantum action can be written explicitly as

S\left(a^{1}_{\alpha},\tilde{a}^{1}_{\alpha}\right)=\int d^{4}x\left(\partial_{0}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}\epsilon\star_{t}\partial_{0}a_{\alpha}^{1}-\partial_{i}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}\partial^{i}a_{\alpha}^{1}+\frac{\phi}{M}\partial_{0}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}P_{i\alpha\beta}\star_{x}\partial^{i}a_{\beta}^{1}+\frac{\tilde{\phi}}{M}\partial_{0}a_{\alpha}^{1}P_{i\alpha\beta}\star_{x}\partial^{i}\tilde{a}_{\beta}^{1}\right)

(93)

and similarly on the lower branch where we have defined the coupling tensor

P_{i\alpha\beta}=\int\not{d}kP_{i\alpha\beta}(k)e^{i\vec{k}.\vec{x}}

(94)

where

P_{i\alpha\beta}(k)=\epsilon_{ijk}\bar{e}_{j\alpha}e_{k\beta}

(95)

is the vector product of the polarisation vectors. This is obviously zero if
\alpha=\beta
by antisymmetry and orthogonal to the two polarisation vectors.
This coupling introduces a mixing between the two polarisation scalars. We will separate the quantum action into the free part

S_{\rm free}(a^{a}_{\alpha},\tilde{a}^{a}_{\alpha})=\int d^{4}x(\partial_{0}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{a}\epsilon\star_{t}\partial_{0}a_{\alpha}^{a}-\partial_{i}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{a}\partial^{i}a_{\alpha}^{a})

(96)

with

S_{\rm free}=S_{\rm free}(a^{1}_{\alpha},\tilde{a}^{1}_{\alpha})-S^{\dagger}_{\rm free}(a^{2}_{\alpha},\tilde{a}^{2}_{\alpha})

(97)

and the interaction part with the axion

S_{\rm int}(a^{a}_{\alpha},\tilde{a}^{a}_{\alpha})=\int d^{4}x\left(\frac{\phi}{M}\partial_{0}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{a}P_{i\alpha\beta}\star_{x}\partial^{i}a_{\beta}^{a}+\frac{\tilde{\phi}}{M}\partial_{0}a_{\alpha}^{a}P_{i\alpha\beta}\star_{x}\partial^{i}\tilde{a}_{\beta}^{a}\right)

(98)

such that

S_{\rm int}=S_{\rm int}(a^{1}_{\alpha},\tilde{a}^{1}_{\alpha})-S^{\dagger}_{\rm int}(a^{2}_{\alpha},\tilde{a}^{2}_{\alpha})

(99)

This allows one to use perturbation theory techniques when calculating vacuum expectation values of operators depending on a single time.

III.4
The propagators and the two-point functions

III.4.1
Polarisations and boundary conditions

So far we have not distinguished the two polarisations and the associated scalar fields
a_{\alpha}
. This is valid in empty space when the permittivity is
uniformly equal to unity in space. When the permittivity is not a constant like in the case of the Casimir experiment set-up, we must specify the boundary conditions at the interface between two media. To simplify the discussion, let us consider a planar interface at
z=0
between two media of permittivities
\epsilon_{1}=1
and
\epsilon_{2}=\epsilon(\omega)
in Fourier space. There are two types of electromagnetic waves which can be transmitted across the surface. The first one is the TE, transverse electric, polarisation when the electric field is parallel to the surface, say in the
x
direction. The other polarisation is the TM, transverse magnetic, polarisation where the magnetic field is parallel to the interface, again say in the
x
direction. The two modes correspond to the two panels of Fig.
6
.

Figure 6:
Illustration of the field’s vectors in the TE and TM modes.

Across the surface, both the transverse electric and magnetic fields must be continuous.
Let us first consider the TE case. We can write

e_{x}=-\partial_{0}a_{x}

(100)

implying that
a_{x}
must be continuous across the interface. Maxwell’s equation reads

\partial_{0}\vec{b}=-\vec{\nabla}\wedge\vec{e}

(101)

and implies that the parallel component of
\vec{b}
is
b_{y}
which is proportional to
\partial_{z}a_{x}
. The boundary conditions imply that for the TE polarisation we must impose

[a_{x}]=0,\ \ [\partial_{z}a_{x}]=0

(102)

where
[f]=f_{2}-f_{1}
is the jump of
f
across the interface.
Considering the case of an incident wave with momentum
\Delta
along the
z
direction being transmitted to a wave of momentum
\xi
, we have the relations between the amplitudes of the reflected and transmitted waves

\displaystyle A_{+}+A_{-}=B_{+}

\displaystyle\Delta(A_{+}-A_{-})=\xi B_{+}

where
A_{\pm}
are the amplitudes of the incident and reflected waves, and
B_{+}
the transmitted one. We get that the reflection coefficient
r_{TE}=\frac{A_{-}}{A_{+}}
is

r_{\rm TE}=\frac{1-\frac{\xi}{\Delta}}{1+\frac{\xi}{\Delta}}.

(104)

Let us now turn to the case where the magnetic field is along the
x
direction. This implies that
b_{x}=-\partial_{z}a_{y}
and
the Maxwell equation

\partial_{0}\vec{d}=\vec{\nabla}\wedge\vec{b}

(105)

implies that in Fourier modes

e_{y}=-i\frac{\partial^{2}_{z}a_{y}}{\epsilon\omega}.

(106)

Now integrating this equation between
z=-\delta
and
z=\delta
, and upon using the continuity of
e_{y}
across the interface implies that
\frac{1}{\epsilon}\partial_{z}a_{y}
must be continuous. As a result we find that the TM polarisation boundary condition is

[a_{y}]=0,\ \ \left[\frac{1}{\epsilon}\partial_{z}a_{y}\right]=0.

(107)

For incident waves we find that amplitudes of the transmitted and reflected waves must satisfy

\displaystyle A_{+}+A_{-}=B_{+}

\displaystyle\Delta(A_{+}-A_{-})=\frac{\xi}{\epsilon}B_{+}

implying that the reflection coefficient for the TM modes is

r_{\rm TM}=\frac{1-\frac{\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}}{1+\frac{\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}}.

(109)

for a wave of energy
\omega
. We will see that these boundary conditions follow from the action and influence the calculations of the propagators.
In the following we will denote by
+
the TE polarisation and
-
the TM polarisation.

III.4.2
The TE propagators

The two polarisations with their specific boundary conditions can be related to the operators defining the action. This leads to the appropriate propagators with the TE and TM boundary conditions.
In order to simplify the notation we define the electric doublet

A_{+}^{a}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}a_{+}^{a}\\
\tilde{a}^{a}_{+}\\
\end{array}\right)

(110)

associated with the TE polarisation.
The free action for the TE polarisation is obtained after integration by parts

S_{\rm free}=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{4}x(A_{+}^{a})^{T}{\bf{D^{E}}}_{ab}A^{b}_{+}.

(111)

The differential operator is off diagonal

{\bf{D}}^{E}_{ab}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\hat{\cal D}^{E}_{ab}\\
{\cal D}^{E}_{ab}&0\\
\end{array}\right)

(112)

where
a=1,2
labels the two Keldysh branches of the
{\cal C}
path. We have introduced the differential operators

\displaystyle{\cal D}^{E}_{11}={\cal D}_{E},{\cal D}^{E}_{22}=-\tilde{\cal D}_{E}

\displaystyle\hat{\cal D}^{E}_{11}=\tilde{\cal D}_{E},\hat{\cal D}^{E}_{22}=-{\cal D}_{E}

and all the other entries of the matrix vanish, i.e. there is no coupling between the upper and lower branches of the Schwinger-Keldysh contour.
The minus signs originate from the complex conjugation of
e^{iS}
on the lower contour.
More precisely the pair
({\cal D},\tilde{\cal D})
is such that

{\cal D}_{E}=-\partial_{0}(\epsilon\star_{t}\partial_{0})+\Delta,\ \tilde{\cal D}_{E}=-\partial_{0}(\hat{\epsilon}\star_{t}\partial_{0})+\Delta,

(114)

or in Fourier space (for the time part only)

{\cal D_{E}}=\epsilon(\omega)\omega^{2}+\Delta,\ \tilde{\cal D}_{E}=\epsilon(-\omega)\omega^{2}+\Delta,

(115)

where we can now see that
\hat{\cal D}_{E}={\cal D}_{E}^{\dagger}
upon using
\bar{\epsilon}(\omega)=\epsilon(-\omega)
. As a result the differential operator
{\bf D}^{E}
is Hermitian. We also define the contraction
x^{a}y_{a}=c_{ab}x^{a}y^{a}
where
c_{11}=1,\ c_{22}=-1
and zero otherwise. This is the ”metric” in the Schwinger-Keldysh formalism allowing one to raise and lower indices. This also allows one to treat the fields on the two parts of the Schwinger-Keldysh path simultaneously.
We can invert the operator
{\bf D}^{E}
by defining

{\bf D}^{E}_{ab}{\bf\Delta}^{bc}_{E}(x;y)=i\delta^{(4)}(x^{\mu}-y^{\mu})\delta^{c}_{a}

(116)

or more explicitly as

{\bf{\Delta}}^{ab}_{E}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&{\Delta}^{ab}_{E}\\
{\hat{\Delta}}^{ab}_{E}&0\\
\end{array}\right).

(117)

Let us define the two propagators

{\cal D}_{E}\Delta_{E}(x;y)=i\delta^{(4)}(x^{\mu}-y^{\mu})

(118)

and

\tilde{\cal D}_{E}\tilde{\Delta}_{E}(x;y)=-i\delta^{(4)}(x^{\mu}-y^{\mu}).

(119)

As a result we get the set of propagators

\displaystyle\Delta^{11}_{E}\equiv\Delta_{E},\ \ \Delta^{22}_{E}\equiv\tilde{\Delta}_{E}

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}^{11}_{E}=\tilde{\Delta}_{E},\ \ \hat{\Delta}^{22}_{E}=\Delta_{E}

corresponding to the fact that the propagators on the second branch of the Schwinger-Keldysh path are the time reversed of the propagators on the first branch.
In the following we will use

\Delta_{E}=iG_{EF}

(121)

where
G_{EF}
satisfies

-\partial_{0}(\epsilon\star_{t}\partial_{0}G_{EF})+\Delta G_{EF}=\delta^{(4)}(x^{\mu}-y^{\mu})

(122)

This is the Feynman propagator where we will retrieve its T-product decomposition, see below.
Finally notice that across a boundary where
\epsilon
jumps, like in the case of the transition between the vacuum and the plates in Casimir experiments, we can integrate both sides between
z=-\delta
and
z=\delta
where
z=0
is the interface and get that

\left[\frac{dG_{EF}}{dz}\right]_{z=0}=0

(123)

i.e. the first derivative of
G_{F}
does not jump across the interface. This is the TE boundary condition applied to the Green’s function.

Let us connect these propagators with correlation functions. As the path integral of the free theory is Gaussian, we can immediately obtain

e^{W(J)}=\int{\cal D}A^{1}_{+}{\cal D}A^{2}_{+}e^{iS_{\rm free}+\int d^{4}xA_{+}^{Ta}{\bf J}_{a+}}

(124)

where
{\cal D}A^{a}_{+}={\cal D}a^{a}_{+}{\cal D}\tilde{a}^{a}_{+}
and

{\bf J}_{a+}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}J_{a+}\\
\tilde{J}_{a+}\\
\end{array}\right)

(125)

Here
W(J)
is the generating functional of connected correlation functions.
The Gaussian integral is obtained by solving

{\bf D}^{E}_{ab}A_{+}^{b}=i{\bf J}_{a+}

(126)

corresponding to

A^{a}_{+}={\bf\Delta}_{E}^{ab}{\bf J}_{b+}

(127)

and substituting back in the action

W(J)=\frac{1}{2}{\bf J}_{a+}^{T}({\bf\Delta}^{ab}_{E})^{T}{\bf J}_{b+}.

(128)

Here the transpose operators acts on the discrete matrix indices.
As a result we get for the two-point functions obtained by Gaussian integration

\langle A_{+}^{a}(x)A_{+}^{b}(y)\rangle={\bf\Delta}_{E}^{ab}(x,y)

(129)

and we denote by

\langle A_{+}^{a}(x)A_{+}^{b}(y)\rangle=\int{\cal D}A^{1}_{+}{\cal D}A^{2}_{+}\;A_{+}^{a}(x)A_{+}^{b}(y)\;e^{iS_{\rm free}}.

(130)

These two-point functions are the TE two-point functions.

In a more explicit fashion, only the two-point functions between
a^{a}_{\alpha}
and
\tilde{a}^{a}_{\alpha}
are non-vanishing with

\langle a^{1}_{+}(x)\tilde{a}^{1}_{+}(y)\rangle=\Delta_{E}(x;y),\ \ \langle\tilde{a}^{1}_{+}(x)a^{1}_{+}(y)\rangle=\tilde{\Delta}_{E}(x;y).

(131)

We can now decompose the two-point functions as T-products. For instance
the boundary condition at infinity
a^{1}(\infty)=a^{2}(\infty)
implies that

\langle a_{+}^{1}(x)\tilde{a}_{+}^{1}(y)\rangle=Y(x^{0}-y^{0})\langle a_{+}^{2}(x)\tilde{a}_{+}^{1}(y)\rangle+Y(y^{0}-x^{0})\langle a_{+}^{1}(x)\tilde{a}_{+}^{2}(y)\rangle

(132)

where
Y
is the Heaviside distribution.
For instance as
x^{0}\to\infty
, the field located at
x^{\mu}
on the first branch reaches infinity where it becomes the field on the second branch. This implies that
the propagator for
x^{0}>y^{0}
should be identified with
\langle a_{\alpha}^{2}(x)a_{\beta}^{1}(y)\rangle=\Delta_{E}^{21}\delta_{\alpha\beta}
by continuity. This defines
\Delta_{E}^{21}
.
We have also the identification between the path integration and the quantum vacuum expectation values

\langle 0|T\left[a_{+}^{a}(x)\tilde{a}_{+}^{b}(x)\right]|0\rangle=\lim_{x^{\mu}\to y^{\mu}}\langle a_{+}^{a}(x)\tilde{a}_{+}^{b}(y)\rangle

(133)

where the limit has to be understood after the appropriate renormalisation procedure.

III.4.3
The TM propagator

We now define the magnetic doublet as

A_{-}^{a}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}a_{-}^{a}\\
\hat{\epsilon}\star_{t}\tilde{a}^{a}_{-}\\
\end{array}\right)

(134)

associated with the TM polarisation. Notice that the second component is convolved with the time-reversed permittivity function.
The free action for the TM polarisation is obtained after integration by parts

S_{\rm free}=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{4}x(A_{-}^{a})^{T}{\bf{D}}_{ab}^{M}A^{b}_{-}

(135)

and involves
the off-diagonal magnetic differential operator

{\bf{D}}_{ab}^{M}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\hat{\cal D}_{ab}^{M}\\
{\cal D}_{ab}^{M}&0\\
\end{array}\right)

(136)

where we have introduced the differential operators

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{11}^{M}={\cal D}_{M},{\cal D}_{22}^{M}=-\tilde{\cal D}_{M}

\displaystyle\hat{\cal D}_{11}^{M}=\tilde{\cal D}_{M},\hat{\cal D}_{22}^{M}=-{\cal D}_{M}.

The pair of magnetic operators
({\cal D}_{M},\tilde{\cal D}_{M})
is such that

{\cal D}_{M}=-\partial_{0}^{2}+\epsilon^{-1}\star_{t}\Delta,\ \tilde{\cal D}_{M}=-\partial_{0}^{2}+\hat{\epsilon}^{-1}\star_{t}\Delta,

(138)

or in Fourier space (for the time part only)

{\cal D}_{M}=\omega^{2}+\frac{\Delta}{\epsilon(\omega)},\ \tilde{\cal D}_{M}=\omega^{2}+\frac{\Delta}{\epsilon(-\omega)},

(139)

i.e. the operator
\epsilon^{-1}\star_{t}
is the convolution with the function whose Fourier transform is
1/\epsilon(\omega)
, respectively
\hat{\epsilon}^{-1}\star_{t}
the convolution with the function of Fourier transform
1/\epsilon(-\omega)
.
We see that
\hat{\cal D}_{M}={\cal D}^{\dagger}_{M}
upon using
\bar{\epsilon}(\omega)=\epsilon(-\omega)
. As a result the differential operator
{\bf D}_{M}
is Hermitian.

We can invert the operator
{\bf D}^{M}
by defining the propagators

{\bf D}_{ab}^{M}{\bf\Delta}^{bc}_{M}(x;y)=i\delta^{(4)}(x^{\mu}-y^{\mu})\delta^{c}_{a}

(140)

or more explicitly as

{\bf{\Delta}}^{ab}_{M}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&{\Delta}^{ab}_{M}\\
{\hat{\Delta}}^{ab}_{M}&0\\
\end{array}\right).

(141)

This requires the two magnetic propagators

{\cal D}_{M}\Delta_{M}(x;y)=i\delta^{(4)}(x^{\mu}-y^{\mu})

(142)

and

\tilde{\cal D}_{M}\tilde{\Delta}_{M}(x;y)=-i\delta^{(4)}(x^{\mu}-y^{\mu}).

(143)

Hence we get the set of propagators

\displaystyle\Delta^{11}_{M}\equiv\Delta_{M},\ \ \Delta^{22}_{M}\equiv\tilde{\Delta}_{M}

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}^{11}_{M}=\tilde{\Delta}_{M},\ \ \hat{\Delta}^{22}_{M}=\Delta_{M}

We will use in the following the magnetic Green’s function

\Delta_{M}=iG_{MF}

(145)

where
G_{MF}
satisfies

-\partial_{0}^{2}G_{MF}+\epsilon^{-1}\star_{t}\Delta G_{MF}=\delta^{(4)}(x^{\mu}-y^{\mu})

(146)

or in Fourier space

\omega^{2}G_{MF}+\frac{\Delta}{\epsilon(\omega)}G_{MF}=\delta^{(3)}(\vec{x}-\vec{y}).

(147)

Again across an interface where
\epsilon
jumps, like at the interface between the vacuum and the plates in a Casimir experiment, we have

\left[\frac{1}{\epsilon(\omega)}\frac{dG_{MF}}{dz}\right]_{z=0}=0

(148)

which is the TM boundary condition applies to the Green’s function.

Just like in the TE case, we can connect these propagators with the correlation functions. The same reasoning leads to

\langle A_{-}^{a}(x)A_{-}^{b}(y)\rangle={\bf\Delta}_{M}^{ab}(x,y)

(149)

where we denote by

\langle A_{-}^{a}(x)A_{-}^{b}(y)\rangle=\int{\cal D}A^{1}_{-}{\cal D}A^{2}_{-}A_{-}^{a}(x)A_{-}^{b}(y)e^{iS_{\rm free}}.

(150)

These two-point functions are the TM two-point functions.
More explicitly, the only non-vanishing two-point functions between
a^{a}_{-}
and
\tilde{a}^{a}_{-}
are

\langle a^{1}_{-}(x)\hat{\epsilon}\star_{t}\tilde{a}^{1}_{\beta}(y)\rangle=\Delta_{M}(x;y),\ \ \langle\hat{\epsilon}\star_{t}\tilde{a}^{1}_{-}(x)a^{1}_{-}(y)\rangle=\tilde{\Delta}_{M}(x;y).

(151)

with the T-product decomposition

\langle a_{-}^{1}(x)\hat{\epsilon}\star_{t}\tilde{a}_{-}^{1}(y)\rangle=Y(x^{0}-y^{0})\langle a_{-}^{2}(x)\hat{\epsilon}\star_{t}\tilde{a}_{-}^{1}(y)\rangle+Y(y^{0}-x^{0})\langle a_{-}^{1}(x)\hat{\epsilon}\star_{t}\tilde{a}_{-}^{2}(y)\rangle

(152)

where
\langle a_{-}^{2}(x)\hat{\epsilon}\star_{t}\tilde{a}_{-}^{1}(y)\rangle=\Delta^{21}_{M}
.
Finally we identify

\langle 0|T\left[a_{-}^{a}(x)\hat{\epsilon}\star_{t}\tilde{a}_{-}^{b}(x)\right]|0\rangle=\lim_{x^{\mu}\to y^{\mu}}\langle a_{-}^{a}(x)\hat{\epsilon}\star_{t}\tilde{a}_{-}^{b}(y)\rangle

(153)

with the appropriate renormalisation procedure.

III.5
The link with electrodynamics

We will elaborate further the link between the action involving the two fields
a_{\alpha}^{1}
and
\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}
and classical electrodynamics. We focus on the part of the action which does not contain the axion field as the axion coupling to photon does not contribute to the energy-momentum tensor.
All in all we consider eight fields. The two physical fields
a_{+}
and
a_{-}
are copied and time reversed for accounting for dissipation, and a second doubling of the field is necessary for integrating along the Schwinger-Keldysh contour, as shown in Fig.
7
where the example of the TE field is considered.

Figure 7:
Illustration of the Keldysh contour in the case of the TE field.

We have seen that classically the shadow field is nothing but the time-reversed electromagnetic field. We first decompose the electromagnetic energy momentum tensor obtained from the action

S(a_{\alpha}^{1},\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1})=\int d^{4}x\;{\cal L}

(154)

where we only focus on the fields on the first branch on the Keldysh-Schwinger contour.
This leads to the decomposition into two separate parts

{\cal T}^{\alpha}_{\mu\nu}=T_{\mu\nu}^{\alpha}+\hat{T}_{\mu\nu}^{\alpha}

(155)

for each polarisation
where we have

T_{\mu\nu}^{\alpha}=\partial_{\nu}a_{\alpha}^{1}\frac{\partial{\cal L}}{\partial\partial^{\mu}a^{1}_{\alpha}}-\frac{\eta_{\mu\nu}}{2}{\cal L},\ \hat{T}_{\mu\nu}^{\alpha}=\partial_{\nu}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}\frac{\partial{\cal L}}{\partial\partial^{\mu}\tilde{a}^{1}_{\alpha}}-\frac{\eta_{\mu\nu}}{2}{\cal L}.

(156)

Notice that this energy-momentum tensor is not symmetric and is conserved.
More explicitly we have

\displaystyle T_{00}^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{0}a_{\alpha}^{1}(\hat{\epsilon}\star_{t}\partial_{0}\tilde{a}^{1}_{\alpha})+\partial_{i}a_{\alpha}^{1}\partial^{i}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}\right)

\displaystyle\ T_{0i}^{\alpha}=\partial_{i}a_{\alpha}^{1}(\hat{\epsilon}\star_{t}\partial_{0}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}),\ T_{i0}=\partial_{0}a_{\alpha}^{1}\partial_{i}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}

\displaystyle\ T_{ij}^{\alpha}=\partial_{j}a_{\alpha}^{1}\partial_{i}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}-\frac{\delta_{ij}}{2}\left(-\partial_{0}a_{\alpha}^{1}(\hat{\epsilon}\star_{t}\partial_{0}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1})+\partial_{i}a_{\alpha}^{1}\partial^{i}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}\right)

and

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{00}^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{0}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}(\epsilon\star_{t}\partial_{0}a_{\alpha}^{1})+\partial_{i}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}\partial^{i}a_{\alpha}^{1}\right)

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{0i}^{\alpha}=\partial_{i}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}(\epsilon\star_{t}\partial_{0}a_{\alpha}^{1}),\ \hat{T}^{\alpha}_{i0}=\partial_{0}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}\partial_{i}a_{\alpha}^{1}

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{ij}^{\alpha}=\partial_{j}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}\partial_{i}a_{\alpha}^{1}-\frac{\delta_{ij}}{2}\left(-\partial_{0}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}(\epsilon\star_{t}\partial_{0}a_{\alpha}^{1})+\partial_{i}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}\partial^{i}a_{\alpha}^{1}\right)

The
axion does not play a role in the energy momentum tensor as the original
F\tilde{F}
term in the action of electrodynamics coupled to axions is topological, i.e. does not depend on the metric.

This energy-momentum tensor has interesting properties in the classical case and in the absence of dissipation
\gamma=0
. There is no need for the doubling of the electromagnetic fields and in fact one can take
\tilde{a}(t)=a(t)
as they both satisfy the same Maxwell equation. This implies that we have the relation

T_{\mu\nu}=T^{\rm mink}_{\mu\nu}+\partial_{\lambda}\Theta_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}

(159)

where the Minkowski energy-momentum tensor of electromagnetism in matter
[
32
]
is given by

\displaystyle T^{\rm mink}_{00}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\vec{d}.\vec{e}+\vec{b}.\vec{b}\right)

\displaystyle T_{0i}^{\rm mink}=\left(\vec{d}\wedge\vec{b}\right)_{i},\ T_{i0}^{\rm mink}=\left(\vec{e}\wedge\vec{b}\right)_{i}

\displaystyle T_{ij}^{\rm mink}=-e_{i}d_{j}-b_{i}b_{j}+\frac{\delta_{ij}}{2}\left(\vec{e}.\vec{d}+\vec{b}^{2}\right)

The correction tensor is given explicitly by

\displaystyle\Theta^{\lambda}_{0i}=-\epsilon\star_{t}F^{\lambda}_{0}a_{i},\ \Theta^{\lambda}_{i0}=-F^{\lambda}_{0}a_{i}

\displaystyle\Theta^{0}_{ij}=-\epsilon\star_{t}F^{0}_{i}a_{j},\ \Theta^{k}_{ij}=-F^{k}_{i}a_{j}.

When
\epsilon=1
, i.e. in the case of electrodynamics in vacuum, this transformation is similar to the one between the Noether energy momentum tensor and the symmetric energy momentum tensor
[
33
]
. The two energy momentum tensors
T^{\mu\nu}
and
T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm mink}
define the same conserved charge, i.e. the electromagnetic momentum

{\cal P}^{\mu}=\int d^{3}x\;T^{0\mu}.

(162)

Indeed using the explicit antisymmetry of
F^{\mu\nu}
we have

\Theta^{00\mu}=0
and therefore

\int d^{3}x\;\partial_{\lambda}\Theta^{\lambda 0\mu}=\int d^{3}x\;\partial_{i}\Theta^{i0\mu}=0

(163)

In this sense, the two energy momentum tensors are equivalent
and we can identity
T^{\mu\nu}
as the energy momentum tensor of electrodynamics in matter.
Of course, the total derivatives in (
159
) would play a role when boundaries are present like in the Casimir setup.
In the following section, we will show that the tensor
T^{\mu\nu}
leads to the correct description of the Casimir effect in the presence of matter.

In the dissipative case when
\gamma\neq 0
we have classically
\tilde{a}(t)=a(-t)
and we find

\hat{T}_{\mu\nu}(t)=T_{\mu\nu}(-t),

(164)

i.e. the total energy-momentum tensor is the sum of the energy-momentum tensor
T_{\mu\nu}
and its time-reversed
\hat{T}_{\mu\nu}
. In particular the energy density of the system is the sum of the energy density of the forward and backward energy densities

{\cal T}_{00}^{\alpha}(t)=T_{00}^{\alpha}(t)+T_{00}^{\alpha}(-t).

(165)

This energy density is conserved which confirms that the dissipated energy by the forward process is brought back from infinity by the time-reversed dynamics.
As a result, we identify
T_{\mu\nu}
with the energy-momentum tensor of electrodynamics in the presence of dissipation.
It involves the shadow field in a crucial way.

In the following we will calculate the quantum vacuum expectation values of
T_{\mu\nu}
and identify this as the quantum vacuum contribution to the energy and pressure of electromagnetism in matter. We will confirm this identification by calculating the Casimir pressure when the plates are not ideal and retrieve the Lifschitz theory, i.e. the well known results
[
20
,
21
]
.
The vacuum energy is obtained as

E=\int d^{3}x\;\langle 0|T_{00}|0\rangle

(166)

or equivalently

E=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\int d^{3}x\;\langle 0|(\partial_{0}a_{\alpha}^{1}\hat{\epsilon}\star_{t}\partial_{0}\tilde{a}^{1}_{\alpha}+\partial_{i}a_{\alpha}^{1}\partial_{i}\tilde{a}^{1}_{\alpha})|0\rangle

(167)

Similarly for the pressure we have

P_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\langle 0|(\partial_{z}a_{\alpha}^{1}\partial_{z}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}+\partial_{0}a_{\alpha}^{1}\hat{\epsilon}\star_{t}\partial_{0}\tilde{a}^{1}_{\alpha}-\partial_{x}a_{\alpha}^{1}\partial_{x}\tilde{a}^{1}_{\alpha}-\partial_{y}a_{\alpha}^{1}\partial_{y}\tilde{a}^{1}_{\alpha})|0\rangle

(168)

coming from
T_{zz}
and
involving the electric and magnetic two-point functions.
In the following section, we will show that this pressure coincides with the result from Lifschitz’s theory in the absence of axionic field. When the axion is taken into account, we will calculate the first correction to the quantum pressure due to the coupling to axions.

IV
The Lifschitz theory

Before calculating the effects of axions on the Casimir effect, let us revisit the case with non-trivial permittivities in the plaques and no axions. We will use the previous energy and pressure defined from the action involving the two polarisation scalars and their shadow fields. We will retrieve the results of the Lifschitz theory which are commonly derived using different approaches
[
20
,
21
]
.

IV.1
The vacuum energy with no axions

We will calculate the vacuum expectation value of
T^{00}
as obtained in the previous section.
Let us evaluate the potential energy first

E_{P}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\langle 0|\int d^{3}x\;\partial_{i}a^{1}_{\alpha}(x)\partial_{i}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}(x)|0\rangle

(169)

where the energy is the vacuum expectation value defined in the previous section. Here we consider the fields as living on the upper path of the Schwinger-Keldysh contour.
The kinetic energy is given by

E_{K}=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{x^{\mu}\to x_{0}^{\mu}}\int d^{3}x\;\langle 0|\partial_{t}a_{\alpha}^{1}(x)(\hat{\epsilon}\star_{t_{0}}\partial_{t_{0}}\tilde{a}^{1}_{\alpha}(x_{0})|0\rangle.

(170)

As the expectation values are taken at coincident points, the product of distributions is ill-defined and is understood in a limiting sense
using the point-splitting method with
x^{\mu}\to x_{0}^{\mu}
.
After an integration by parts the potential energy becomes

E_{P}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\langle 0|\int d^{3}x\;\partial^{2}_{x}a^{1}_{\alpha}(x)\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}(x)|0\rangle

(171)

which is understood as the limit

E_{P}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\lim_{x^{\mu}\to x_{0}^{\mu}}\int d^{3}x\;\partial_{x}^{2}\langle a^{1}_{\alpha}(x)\tilde{a}^{1}_{\alpha}(x_{0})\rangle.

(172)

We first focus on the TE polarisation.
Now we use the identity

\langle a^{1}_{+}(x)\tilde{a}^{1}_{+}(x_{0})\rangle=iG_{EF}(x;x_{0})

(173)

As
x\neq x_{0}
in the point-splitting limit we have also the equality

\lim_{x^{\mu}\to x_{0}^{\mu}}\int d^{3}x\;\partial_{x}^{2}G_{EF}(x;x_{0})=\lim_{x^{\mu}\to x_{0}^{\mu}}\int d^{3}x\;\epsilon\star_{t}\partial_{t}^{2}G_{EF}(x;x_{0}).

(174)

Now as the Green’s function is time-translation invariant, i.e. it only depends on
t-t_{0}
, we have
\partial^{2}_{t}G_{EF}(x,x_{0})=-\partial_{t}\partial_{t_{0}}G_{EF}(x;x_{0})
.
This implies that the potential energy and the kinetic energy for the TE polarisation are equal

E_{P}^{+}=E_{K}^{+}=\frac{i}{2}\lim_{x^{\mu}\to x_{0}^{\mu}}\int d^{3}x\;\partial_{t}\partial_{t_{0}}(\epsilon\star_{t}G_{EF})(x;x_{0}).

(175)

The total energy is then

E_{+}=i\lim_{x^{\mu}\to x_{0}^{\mu}}\int d^{3}x\;\partial_{t}\partial_{t_{0}}(\epsilon\star_{t}G_{EF})(x;x_{0}).

(176)

Translation invariance in the parallel direction to the plates and time translation invariance leads to

G_{EF}(x;x_{0})=G_{EF}(t-t_{0},\vec{x}_{\parallel}-\vec{x}_{\parallel 0},z;0,\vec{0},z_{0})

(177)

implying that the integral over the surface area of the plates factors out and we get finally
in terms of Fourier modes

\frac{E_{+}}{A}=i\lim_{x\to x_{0}}\int dz\;\not{d}\omega\;\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;e^{-i\omega(t-t_{0})}\epsilon(\omega)\omega^{2}G_{EF}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z_{0}).

(178)

where we have defined the Fourier transform

G_{EF}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z_{0})=\int dt\;d^{2}x_{\parallel}\;e^{-i\omega t}e^{i\vec{x}_{\parallel}.\vec{p}_{\parallel}}G_{EF}(t,\vec{x}_{\parallel},z;0,\vec{0},z_{0})

(179)

which is given the appendix
D
for the retarded Green’s function and by the extension procedure in section
II.3.2
for the Feynman propagator.

The TM polarisation can be dealt with in the same fashion, see the appendix
E
, using

\langle a^{1}_{-}(x)\tilde{a}^{1}_{-}(x_{0})\rangle=i\epsilon^{-1}\star_{t}G_{MF}(x;x_{0})

(180)

implying that the energy for the TM polarisation is given by

E_{+}=i\lim_{x^{\mu}\to x_{0}^{\mu}}\int d^{3}x\;\partial_{t}\partial_{t_{0}}G_{MF}(x;x_{0})

(181)

or equivalently

\frac{E_{-}}{A}=i\lim_{x^{\mu}\to x_{0}^{\mu}}\int dz\;\not{d}\omega\;\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;e^{-i\omega(t-t_{0})}\omega^{2}G_{MF}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z_{0}).

(182)

Hence the total energy can be obtained from the following the integrals

\frac{E}{A}=i\lim_{x^{\mu}\to x_{0}^{\mu}}\int dz\;\not{d}\omega\;\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;e^{-i\omega(t-t_{0})}\omega^{2}\left(\epsilon(\omega)G_{EF}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z_{0})+G_{MF}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z_{0})\right)

(183)

leading to the vacuum energy.

IV.2
The ideal case

Before focusing on the case with metallic plates, let us retrieve the Casimir energy when the plates are ideal with a vanishing Green’s function apart from
inside the vacuum. This corresponds to the limit
\epsilon\to\infty
, see appendix
D
for instance.
In this case we have
G_{F}=0
for
z<0
and
z>d
whilst in between the plates

G_{EF}(z;z_{0},\omega,p_{\parallel})=\frac{1}{\Delta}\frac{\sin\Delta(z_{0}-d)\sin\Delta z}{\sin\Delta d},\ G_{MF}(z;z_{0},\omega,p_{\parallel})=\frac{1}{\Delta}\frac{\cos\Delta(z_{0}-d)\cos\Delta z}{\sin\Delta d}.

(184)

Explicitly we have

\int_{0}^{d}dz\;\left(G_{F}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z)+G_{MF}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z)\right)=\frac{d\cos\Delta d}{\Delta\sin\Delta d}.

(185)

We will change variable from
\omega
to
\Delta
which is a double cover of the real line into the positive real line. In order to take into account the double counting we extend
\Delta
to the negative real line by taking
\Delta={\rm sign}(\omega)\sqrt{\omega^{2}-p^{2}_{\parallel}}
. Then we get

\frac{E}{A}=i\frac{d}{2\pi}\lim_{t\to t_{0}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;e^{-i\omega(t-t_{0})}\;\frac{\omega^{2}\cos\Delta d}{\Delta\sin\Delta d}.

(186)

In the complex
\Delta
plane, the integrand has poles for

\Delta_{n}=\frac{n\pi}{d}\Rightarrow\omega_{n}={\rm sign}(n)\left(\frac{\pi^{2}n^{2}}{d^{2}}+p_{\parallel}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

(187)

for
n\neq 0
. As
t>t_{0}
, the integral can be performed by closing the integration contour in the lower half-plane where
\omega
has a negative imaginary parts guaranteeing that the exponential factor
e^{-i\omega(t-t_{0})}
decays at infinity. As a memory of the shift of the poles when dissipation is present, we take the Feynman prescription that the poles are shifted from the real axis by

\omega_{n}={\rm sign}(n)\left(\left(\frac{\pi^{2}n^{2}}{d^{2}}+p_{\parallel}^{2}\right)^{1/2}-i\delta\right)

(188)

where
\delta
is infinitesimal.
As shown in Fig.
8
the poles are integrated over circles with residues
{\rm Res_{n}}=\frac{\omega_{n}}{d}

we find that

\frac{E}{A}=i\frac{d}{2\pi}\times\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;(-2\pi i)\;\sum_{n>0}\frac{\omega_{n}}{d}

(189)

leading to

\frac{E}{A}=\sum_{n>0}\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;{\omega_{n}}.

(190)

Figure 8:

This is the vacuum energy for the two polarisations, i.e. we retrieve that the vacuum energy is due to the presence of photon modes between the ideal plaques.
Notice the complementary roles that the
TE
and
TM
polarisations play, i.e. they contribute equally to the vacuum energy. Moreover if we had shifted the poles below the real axis as for retarded Green’s functions, the energy would have vanished. Using the Feynman prescription is crucial.

IV.3
Metallic plates

From now on and to simplify the notation, all the propagators are the Feynman propagators and we drop the
F
index for simplicity.
In the case of metallic plates, the integrals of the Green’s function inside the plates converges provided the two Green’s functions
G_{E}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z)
and
G_{M}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z)
vanish when
z
is very large. It turns out that
\lim_{z\to\infty}G_{E}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z)=-\frac{1}{2\xi}
and
\lim_{z\to\infty}G_{M}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z)=-\frac{\epsilon}{2\xi}
. This means that one must renormalise the Green’s functions according to

G_{ER}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z)=G_{E}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z)+\frac{1}{2\xi},G_{MR}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z)=G_{M}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z)+\frac{\epsilon}{2\xi}

(191)

Physically this amounts to subtracting the pressure exerted on the plate by the vacuum when one regularises the plate to be of finite and very large width
L
. The plate at
z=d+L
compensates part of the pressure exerted at
z=d
. This is the origin of this renormalisation.
In practice this implies that

G_{ER}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z)=\frac{\Theta_{E}(\omega,p_{\parallel})}{2}\left(\frac{\xi}{\Delta}+\frac{\Delta}{\xi}\right)\left(\sin\Delta d+\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\left(\cos\Delta(2z-d)-\cos\Delta d\right)\right),\ 0\leq z\leq d

(192)

and

G_{MR}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z)=\frac{\Theta_{M}(\omega,p_{\parallel})}{2}\left(\frac{\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}+\frac{\epsilon\Delta}{\xi}\right)\left(\sin\Delta d+\frac{\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}\left(\cos\Delta(2z-d)-\cos\Delta d\right)\right),\ 0\leq z\leq d

(193)

In the plates we find

\displaystyle G_{ER}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z)=\frac{\Theta_{E}(\omega,p_{\parallel})}{2}\sin\Delta d\left(\frac{\xi}{\Delta}+\frac{\Delta}{\xi}\right)e^{-2\xi(z-d)}\ \ z\geq d

\displaystyle G_{ER}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z)=\frac{\Theta_{E}(\omega,p_{\parallel})}{2}\sin\Delta d\left(\frac{\xi}{\Delta}+\frac{\Delta}{\xi}\right)e^{2\xi z)}\ \ z\leq 0

and

\displaystyle G_{MR}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z)=\frac{\Theta_{M}(\omega,p_{\parallel})}{2}\sin\Delta d\left(\frac{\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}+\frac{\epsilon\Delta}{\xi}\right)e^{-2\xi(z-d)}\ \ z\geq d

\displaystyle G_{MR}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z)=\frac{\Theta_{M}(\omega,p_{\parallel})}{2}\sin\Delta d\left(\frac{\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}+\frac{\epsilon\Delta}{\xi}\right)e^{2\xi z)}\ \ z\leq 0

As these expressions correspond to the renormalised Feynman propagators, they are defined in the right hand plane where
\Re\omega>0
. In the left half plane the Feynman propagators are simply obtained by exchanging
\omega\to-\omega
. This implies that integrals involving the Feynman propagator along the real line are such that

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\;G_{EF}(\omega)=2\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\;G_{EF}(\omega)=2\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\;G_{E}(\omega)

(196)

as
G_{EF}(\omega)=G_{E}(\omega)

for
\omega>0
. We will use this below.

IV.3.1
The electric energy

The contribution from the plaques to the energy is given by

\displaystyle\frac{E_{\rm plaque}^{+}}{A}=i\lim_{t\to t_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\not{d}\omega\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;e^{-i\omega(t-t_{0})}\epsilon(\omega)\omega^{2}\frac{\Theta_{E}(\omega,p_{\parallel})}{2\xi}\left(\frac{\xi}{\Delta}+\frac{\Delta}{\xi}\right)\sin\Delta d

\displaystyle+i\lim_{t\to t_{0}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\not{d}\omega\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;e^{-i\omega(t-t_{0})}\epsilon(-\omega)\omega^{2}\frac{\Theta_{E}(-\omega,p_{\parallel})}{2\xi(-\omega)}\left(\frac{\xi(-\omega)}{\Delta}+\frac{\Delta}{\xi(-\omega)}\right)\sin\Delta d

(197)

where on the second line all the contributions are evaluated at
-\omega
and we do not specify
\Delta(-\omega)
as
\Delta
is an even function.
The contribution from the vacuum between the plaques is

\displaystyle\frac{E_{\rm V}^{+}}{A}=i\lim_{t\to t_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\not{d}\omega\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;e^{-i\omega(t-t_{0})}\omega^{2}\frac{\Theta_{E}(\omega,p_{\parallel})}{2\Delta}\xi d\left(\frac{\xi}{\Delta}+\frac{\Delta}{\xi}\right)\left(-\cos\Delta d+\left(\frac{\Delta}{\xi}-\frac{1}{\Delta d}\right)\sin\Delta d\right)

\displaystyle+i\lim_{t\to t_{0}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\not{d}\omega\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;e^{-i\omega(t-t_{0})}\omega^{2}\frac{\Theta_{E}(-\omega,p_{\parallel})}{2\Delta}\xi(-\omega)d\left(\frac{\xi(-\omega)}{\Delta}+\frac{\Delta}{\xi(-\omega)}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(-\cos\Delta d+\left(\frac{\Delta}{\xi(-\omega)}-\frac{1}{\Delta d}\right)\sin\Delta d\right)

.

(198)

Adding the two integrals we get

\displaystyle\frac{E^{+}}{A}=\frac{i}{2}\lim_{t\to t_{0}}\Big[\int_{0}^{\infty}\not{d}\omega\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;\Theta_{E}(\omega,p_{\parallel})F(\omega,p_{\parallel})e^{-i\omega(t-t_{0})}

\displaystyle+\int_{-\infty}^{0}\not{d}\omega\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;\Theta_{E}(-\omega,p_{\parallel})F(-\omega,p_{\parallel})e^{-i\omega(t-t_{0})}\Big]

(199)

where we have introduced the even function of
\omega

F_{E}(\omega,p_{\parallel})=\omega^{2}\left(\frac{\xi}{\Delta}+\frac{\Delta}{\xi}\right)\left[\frac{\epsilon(\omega)}{\xi}\sin\Delta d+\frac{\xi d}{\Delta}\left(-\cos\Delta d+\left(\frac{\Delta}{\xi}-\frac{1}{\Delta d}\right)\sin\Delta d\right)\right].

(200)

In these integrals, the limit
t\to t_{0}
is taken to be with
t\leq t_{0}
when
\omega\geq 0
and
t\geq t_{0}
when
\omega\leq 0
. This allows one to reduce
the electric energy to a single integral with
t\leq t_{0}

\frac{E^{+}}{A}=i\lim_{t\to t_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\not{d}\omega\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;\Theta_{E}(\omega,p_{\parallel})F_{E}(\omega,p_{\parallel})e^{-i\omega(t-t_{0})}

(201)

which will be calculated after Wick’s rotation.

IV.3.2
The magnetic energy

The calculation runs similarly to the electric case
with the magnetic energy in the plaques given by

\frac{E_{\rm plaque}^{-}}{A}=i\lim_{t\to t_{0}}\int\not{d}\omega\;\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;e^{-i\omega(t-t_{0})}\omega^{2}\frac{\Theta_{M}(\omega,p_{\parallel})}{2\xi}\left(\frac{\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}+\frac{\epsilon\Delta}{\xi}\right)\sin\Delta d

(202)

whereas the contribution from the vacuum between the plaques is

\frac{E_{\rm V}^{-}}{A}=i\lim_{t\to t_{0}}\int\not{d}\omega\;\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;e^{-i\omega(t-t_{0})}\omega^{2}\frac{\Theta_{M}(\omega,p_{\parallel})}{2\Delta\epsilon}\xi d\left(\frac{\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}+\frac{\epsilon\Delta}{\xi}\right)\left(-\cos\Delta d+\left(\frac{\epsilon\Delta}{\xi}-\frac{1}{\Delta d}\right)\sin\Delta d\right).

(203)

Adding the two integrals we get, where here
t\leq t_{0}
,

\frac{E^{-}}{A}=i\lim_{t\to t_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\not{d}\omega\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;\Theta_{M}(\omega,p_{\parallel})F_{M}(\omega,p_{\parallel})e^{-i\omega(t-t_{0})}

(204)

where we have introduced

F_{M}(\omega,p_{\parallel})=\omega^{2}\left(\frac{\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}+\frac{\epsilon\Delta}{\xi}\right)\left[\frac{1}{\xi}\sin\Delta d+\frac{\xi d}{\epsilon\Delta}\left(-\cos\Delta d+\left(\frac{\epsilon\Delta}{\xi}-\frac{1}{\Delta d}\right)\sin\Delta d\right)\right]

(205)

The integrals giving the energy and defined along the real axis such as (
204
) are oscillatory and difficult to manipulate. It is more convenient to perform a Wick’s rotation in the complex plane and integrate along the imaginary axis.

IV.4
Wick’s rotation

IV.4.1
The electric energy

The oscillating integrals we found in the previous section can be evaluated after performing Wick’s rotation. This is only possible as the Feynman propagators does not have obstructions by poles and branch cuts when rotating the
\omega
-integral by an angle of
\pi/2
in the complex plane.
In taking the limit
t\to t_{0}
, we consider
t\leq t_{0}
implying that the integral along a large circle in the first quadrant is exponentially convergent to zero.
Then, after Wick’s rotation
\omega\to i\omega
we have

\frac{E^{+}}{A}=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\;\Theta_{E}(i\omega,p_{\parallel})F_{E}(i\omega,p_{\parallel})

(206)

where now

\Delta=\left(\omega^{2}+p_{\parallel}^{2}\right)^{1/2},\ \xi=\left(p_{\parallel}^{2}+\omega^{2}\epsilon(i\omega)\right)^{1/2}

(207)

where in the metallic case

\epsilon(i\omega)=1+\frac{\omega_{\rm pl}^{2}}{\omega^{2}+\gamma\omega}

(208)

which has no singularity on the positive imaginary axis where
\omega>0
. This transformation implies that

\Theta_{E}(i\omega,p_{\parallel})=-\frac{1}{\Delta\left(\left(1+\frac{\xi^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}\right)\sinh\Delta d+\frac{2\xi}{\Delta}\cosh\Delta d\right)}

(209)

and

F_{E}(i\omega,p_{\parallel})=\omega^{2}\left(-\frac{\xi}{\Delta}+\frac{\Delta}{\xi}\right)\left[\frac{\epsilon(i\omega)}{\xi}\sinh\Delta d+\frac{\xi d}{\Delta}\left(\cosh\Delta d+\left(\frac{\Delta}{\xi}-\frac{1}{\Delta d}\right)\sinh\Delta d\right)\right]

(210)

which is real.
Finally we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{E^{+}}{A}=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{\omega^{2}}{\Delta\left(\left(1+\frac{\xi^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}\right)\sinh\Delta d+\frac{2\xi}{\Delta}\cosh\Delta d\right)}\left(\frac{\xi}{\Delta}-\frac{\Delta}{\xi}\right)\times

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\epsilon(i\omega)}{\xi}\sinh\Delta d+\frac{\xi d}{\Delta}\left(\cosh\Delta d+\left(\frac{\Delta}{\xi}-\frac{1}{\Delta d}\right)\sinh\Delta d\right)\right].

Let us take the large
d
limit corresponding to the infinite space case. We have

\frac{E_{\infty}^{+}}{A}=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{\omega^{2}}{\left(1+\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\right)^{2}}\left(\frac{\xi}{\Delta}-\frac{\Delta}{\xi}\right)\left[\frac{\epsilon(i\omega)}{\xi}+\frac{\xi d}{\Delta}\left(1+\left(\frac{\Delta}{\xi}-\frac{1}{\Delta d}\right)\right)\right].

(212)

The energy of empty space-time must be renormalised to zero, as we assume that locally space-time is of the Minkowski type corresponding to a vanishing cosmological constant. As a result we define the renormalised energy

\frac{E_{\rm R}^{+}}{A}=\frac{E^{+}}{A}-\frac{E_{\infty}^{+}}{A}.

This turns out to be

\frac{E_{\rm R}^{+}}{A}=-\frac{d}{\pi}\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\;\frac{\omega^{2}}{\Delta}\frac{1}{\left(\frac{1+\frac{\xi}{\Delta}}{1-\frac{\xi}{\Delta}}\right)^{2}e^{2\Delta d}-1}\left(1-\frac{2p_{\parallel}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\frac{1}{\xi d}\right)

(213)

where the factor
\left(1-\frac{2p_{\parallel}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\frac{1}{\xi d}\right)
corresponds to the contribution from the boundary plates, i.e. it reduces to unity when concentrating on the cavity of size
d
only.

IV.4.2
The magnetic energy

After Wick’s rotation
\omega\to i\omega
, the magnetic energy becomes

\frac{E^{-}}{A}=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\;\Theta_{M}(i\omega,p_{\parallel})F_{M}(i\omega,p_{\parallel})

(214)

with

\Theta_{M}(i\omega,p_{\parallel})=-\frac{1}{\Delta\left(\left(1+\frac{\xi^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}\right)\sinh\Delta d+\frac{2\xi}{\Delta}\cosh\Delta d\right)}

(215)

and

\displaystyle F_{M}(i\omega,p_{\parallel})

\displaystyle=\omega^{2}\left(-\frac{\xi}{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta}+\frac{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta}{\xi}\right)\times

(216)

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{\xi}\sinh\Delta d+\frac{\xi d}{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta}\left(\cosh\Delta d+\left(\frac{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta}{\xi}-\frac{1}{\Delta d}\right)\sinh\Delta d\right)\right]

which is real.
Finally we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{E^{-}}{A}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\;\frac{\omega^{2}}{\Delta\left(\left(1+\frac{\xi^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}(i\omega)\Delta^{2}}\right)\sinh\Delta d+\frac{2\xi}{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta}\cosh\Delta d\right)}\times

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\xi}{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta}-\frac{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta}{\xi}\right)\left[\frac{1}{\xi}\sinh\Delta d+\frac{\xi d}{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta}\left(\cosh\Delta d+\left(\frac{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta}{\xi}-\frac{1}{\Delta d}\right)\sinh\Delta d\right)\right].

Let us take the large
d
limit corresponding to the infinite space case again. We have

\displaystyle\frac{E_{\infty}^{-}}{A}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\;\frac{\omega^{2}}{\left(1+\frac{\xi}{\epsilon^{2}(i\omega)\Delta}\right)^{2}}\times

(218)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\xi}{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta}-\frac{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta}{\xi}\right)\left[\frac{1}{\xi}+\frac{\xi d}{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta}\left(1+\left(\frac{\Delta}{\xi}-\frac{1}{\Delta d}\right)\right)\right].

We define the renormalised energy

\frac{E_{\rm R}^{-}}{A}=\frac{E^{-}}{A}-\frac{E_{\infty}^{-}}{A}.

This turns out to be

\frac{E_{\rm R}^{-}}{A}=-\frac{d}{\pi}\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\;\frac{\omega^{2}}{\Delta}\frac{1}{\left(\frac{1+\frac{\xi}{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta}}{1-\frac{\xi}{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta}}\right)^{2}e^{2\Delta d}-1}\left(1-\frac{2p_{\parallel}^{2}}{\epsilon(i\omega)\omega^{2}}\frac{1}{\xi d}\right)

(219)

This can be explicitly evaluated in the ideal case.

IV.4.3
The ideal case

The ideal case is easily retrieved by sending
\epsilon\to\infty
and
\xi\to\infty
implying that

\frac{E_{\rm R}}{A}=-\frac{2d}{\pi}\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\;\frac{\omega^{2}}{\Delta}\frac{1}{e^{2\Delta d}-1}

(220)

which can be written by parity as

\frac{E_{\rm R}}{A}=-\frac{d}{\pi}\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\;\frac{\omega^{2}}{\Delta}\frac{1}{e^{2\Delta d}-1}

(221)

Notice that
\vec{\Delta}=(\omega,\vec{p}_{\parallel})
is a 3-vector whose norm is
\Delta
. Rotation invariance of the integral for the vector
\vec{\Delta}
implies that

\frac{E_{\rm R}}{A}=-\frac{d}{3\pi(2\pi)^{2}}\int d^{3}\Delta\frac{\Delta}{e^{2\Delta d}-1}=-\frac{4d}{3(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\Delta\frac{\Delta^{3}}{e^{2\Delta d}-1}=-\frac{1}{12(2\pi)^{2}d^{3}}\Gamma(4)\zeta(4)

(222)

or more concisely

\frac{E_{\rm R}}{A}=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{720\,d^{3}}

(223)

as
\Gamma(4)=6
and
\zeta(4)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{90}
.
The pressure is then given by taking the derivative with respect to
d

P=-\frac{d{(E_{R}/A)}}{dd}=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{240\,d^{4}}

(224)

as expected.

In the non-ideal case, the vacuum energy comprises the energy due to the fluctuations in the empty cavity and in matter. The fact that the reflection coefficients for waves coming from the cavity and leaving it into the plates are not strictly equal to unity implies that the cavity as seen as a closed system of dimension
d
is in fact an open system where energy flows into the plate continually. As a result, the energy in the cavity and the pressure exerted by the quantum fluctuations on the interface betweeen the cavity and matter are not simply related by one being the derivative of the other. We must calculate the pressure directly. This is done in the following section.

IV.5
The vacuum pressure

We will evaluate directly the pressure acting on the plate at
z=d
. For this we calculate the vacuum expectation value of the
T_{zz}
part of the energy momentum tensor.
This is given by

P_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\langle 0|\left(\partial_{z}a_{\alpha}^{1}\partial_{z}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}^{1}+\partial_{0}a_{\alpha}^{1}\hat{\epsilon}\star_{t}\partial_{0}\tilde{a}^{1}_{\alpha}-\partial_{x}a_{\alpha}^{1}\partial_{x}\tilde{a}^{1}_{\alpha}-\partial_{y}a_{\alpha}^{1}\partial_{y}\tilde{a}^{1}_{\alpha}\right)|0\rangle

(225)

which can be conveniently written in terms of the electric and magnetic contributions

P_{z}^{+}=\frac{i}{2}\lim_{z\to z_{0}}\int\not{d}\omega\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;\left(\partial_{z}\partial_{z_{0}}-p_{\parallel}^{2}+\omega^{2}\right)G_{EF}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z_{0})

(226)

and

P_{z}^{-}=\frac{i}{2}\lim_{z\to z_{0}}\int\not{d}\omega\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;\left(\partial_{z}\partial_{z_{0}}-p_{\parallel}^{2}+\omega^{2}\right)G_{MF}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z_{0})

(227)

evaluated at
z=z_{0}=d
after the appropriate renormalisation.
As we are interested in the pressure due to the vacuum on the plate, the electric contribution is given by

P_{z}(d)^{+}=\frac{i}{2}\lim_{z\to d-,\ z_{0}\to d-}\int\not{d}\omega\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;\left(\partial_{z}\partial_{z_{0}}+\omega^{2}-p_{\parallel}^{2}\right)G_{EF}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z_{0})

(228)

where the limit is taken with
z,z_{0}\leq d
. A similar result holds for the magnetic pressure

P_{z}(d)^{-}=\frac{i}{2}\lim_{z\to d-,\ z_{0}\to d-}\int\not{d}\omega\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;\left(\partial_{z}\partial_{z_{0}}+\omega^{2}-p_{\parallel}^{2}\right)G_{MF}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z_{0}).

(229)

Let us evaluate these integrals.

IV.5.1
The electric pressure

Let us start with the term involving the two derivatives in the electric case

\partial_{z}\partial_{z_{0}}G_{E}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z_{0})|_{z=z_{0}=d}=\Theta(\omega,p_{\parallel})\;\xi\;\Delta\left(\sin\Delta d-\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\cos\Delta\right)

(230)

where
G_{E}
is the Fourier transform of the Green’s function which can be then transformed into the Feynman propagator by the Schwarz reflection procedure.
The same derivative expression evaluated as
z\to\infty
gives

\partial_{z}\partial_{z_{0}}G_{E}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z_{0})|_{z=z_{0}\to\infty}=\Theta(\omega,p_{\parallel})\;\xi^{2}\;\left(-\cos\Delta d+\frac{\sin\Delta d}{2}\left(\frac{\Delta}{\xi}-\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\right)\right).

(231)

Obviously this term corresponds to a contribution to the pressure exerted on the outer side of the plaque and we must subtract the two quantities to get

\partial_{z}\partial_{z_{0}}G_{ER}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z_{0})=\Theta(\omega,p_{\parallel})\;\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}\;\left(\frac{\Delta}{\xi}+\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\right)\sin\Delta d.

(232)

where
R
stands for renormalised.
Similarly the term in
\left(\omega^{2}-p^{2}_{\parallel}\right)G_{E}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z)
must be understood as
(\omega^{2}-p^{2}_{\parallel})G_{ER}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z)

where
G_{R}
vanishes as
z\to\infty
, i.e. the pressure coming from the outer edge of the plaque has been removed.
Here notice that we have taken the outer pressure as coming from vacuum, i.e. we have taken
\epsilon(\omega)=1
for the evaluation of the Green’s functions. We have then

G_{ER}(d,\omega,p_{\parallel};d)=\frac{\Theta_{E}(\omega,p_{\parallel})}{2}\left(\frac{\Delta}{\xi}+\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\right)\sin\Delta d.

(233)

As a result the contribution to the pressure can be written as

P_{z}^{+}=\frac{i}{4}\int\not{d}\omega\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;\Theta_{E}(\omega,p_{\parallel})\left(\frac{\Delta}{\xi}+\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\right)\left(\xi^{2}-p_{\parallel}^{2}+\omega^{2}\right)\sin\Delta d.

(234)

This can be evaluated after Wick’s rotation as

P_{z}^{+}=\frac{1}{4}\int\not{d}\omega\;\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;\frac{\xi^{2}-\Delta^{2}}{\Delta}\left(\frac{\xi}{\Delta}-\frac{\Delta}{\xi}\right)\frac{\sinh\Delta d}{\left(1+\frac{\xi^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}\right)\sinh\Delta d+\frac{2\xi}{\Delta}\cosh\Delta d}

(235)

Again this result does not vanish when
d\to\infty
. This would correspond to the pressure in empty space-time which is taken to vanish as we assume that
locally space-time is Minkowski. After
removing the pressure in empty space-time when
d\to\infty
we get

P_{z}^{+}=-\int\not{d}\omega\;\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;\frac{\Delta}{{\left(\frac{1+\frac{\xi}{\Delta}}{1-\frac{\xi}{\Delta}}\right)^{2}e^{2\Delta d}-1}}.

(236)

This is the Lifschitz’s formula for the pressure coming from the
TE
modes with two plates of the same permittivity
\epsilon
. Notice that this integral can be written as

P_{z}^{+}=-\int\not{d}\omega\;\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;\frac{\Delta}{{r_{TE}^{-2}\,e^{2\Delta d}-1}}.

(237)

where
r_{TE}
is the reflection coefficient for the
TE
modes evaluated for imaginary frequencies
r_{TE}(i\omega)=\frac{1-\frac{\xi}{\Delta}}{1+\frac{\xi}{\Delta}}
where
\xi=\left(p_{\parallel}^{2}+\omega^{2}\epsilon(i\omega)\right)^{1/2}
depends on the permittivity for imaginary frequencies.

IV.5.2
The magnetic pressure

The two derivative term of the magnetic Green’s function is

\partial_{z}\partial_{z_{0}}G_{M}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z_{0})|_{z=z_{0}=d}=\Theta_{M}(\omega,p_{\parallel})\;\frac{\xi\Delta}{\epsilon}\;\left(\sin\Delta d-\frac{\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}\cos\Delta d\right).

(238)

The same derivative expression evaluated as
z\to\infty
using the expressions in the plates gives

\partial_{z}\partial_{z_{0}}G_{M}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z_{0})|_{z=z_{0}\to\infty}=\Theta_{M}(\omega,p_{\parallel})\xi^{2}\left(-\cos\Delta d+\frac{\sin\Delta d}{2}\left(\frac{\epsilon\Delta}{\xi}-\frac{\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}\right)\right).

(239)

Now we must remove the pressure exerted by the vacuum outside the plates as the size of the plates goes to infinity. This is obtained by subtracting
\epsilon^{-2}\partial_{z}\partial_{z_{0}}G_{M}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z_{0})|_{z=z_{0}\to\infty}
. Indeed we know that for the
TM
polarisation
\lim_{\delta\to 0}\partial_{z}G_{M}|_{L+\delta}=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\epsilon^{-1}\partial_{z}G_{M}|_{L-\delta}
for a plate of size
L
and the derivatives are taken at the right boundary of the plates.
The renormalised contribution to the pressure is then

\partial_{z}\partial_{z_{0}}G_{MR}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z_{0})=\Theta_{M}(\omega,p_{\parallel})\;\frac{\xi^{2}}{2\epsilon^{2}}\;\left(\frac{\epsilon\Delta}{\xi}+\frac{\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}\right)\sin\Delta d.

(240)

where
R
stands for renormalised.
Similarly the term in
\left(\omega^{2}-p^{2}_{\parallel}\right)G_{M}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z)
must be understood as
\left(\omega^{2}-p^{2}_{\parallel}\right)G_{MR}(z,\omega,p_{\parallel};z)

where
G_{MR}
vanishes as
z\to\infty
, i.e. the pressure coming from the outer edge of the plaque has been removed. Hence we have

G_{MR}(d,\omega,p_{\parallel};d)=\frac{\Theta_{M}(\omega,p_{\parallel})}{2}\left(\frac{\epsilon\Delta}{\xi}+\frac{\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}\right)\sin\Delta d.

(241)

As a result the contribution to the pressure can be written as

P_{z}^{-}=\frac{i}{4}\int\not{d}\omega\;\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;\Theta_{M}(\omega,p_{\parallel})\left(\frac{\epsilon\Delta}{\xi}+\frac{\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}\right)\left(\frac{\xi^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}-p_{\parallel}^{2}+\omega^{2}\right)\sin\Delta d.

(242)

This can be evaluated after Wick’s rotation as

P_{z}^{-}=\frac{1}{4}\int\not{d}\omega\;\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;\frac{\frac{\xi^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}(i\omega)}-\Delta^{2}}{\Delta}\left(\frac{\xi}{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta}-\frac{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta}{\xi}\right)\frac{\sinh\Delta d}{\left(1+\frac{\xi^{2}}{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta^{2}}\right)\sinh\Delta d+\frac{2\xi}{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta}\cosh\Delta d}.

(243)

Again this result does not vanish when
d\to\infty
. This would correspond to the pressure in empty space-time which is taken to vanish as we assume that
locally space-time is Minkowski. After
removing the pressure in empty space-time obtained when
d\to\infty
we get

P_{z}^{-}=-\int\not{d}\omega\;\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;\frac{\Delta}{{\left(\frac{1+\frac{\xi}{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta}}{1-\frac{\xi}{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta}}\right)^{2}e^{2\Delta d}-1}}.

(244)

This is Lifschitz formula for the pressure coming from the
TM
modes with two plates with the same permittivity
\epsilon
. Notice that the same integral can be written as

P_{z}^{-}=-\int\not{d}\omega\;\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;\frac{\Delta}{{r_{TM}^{-2}\,e^{2\Delta d}-1}}.

(245)

where
r_{TM}
is the reflection coefficient for the
TM
modes evaluated for imaginary frequencies.

IV.5.3
The ideal case

In the ideal case where
r_{TE}=r_{TM}=1
, the electric and magnetic contributions to the Casimir pressure are equal and the pressure reduces to

P_{z}=-2\int\not{d}\omega\;\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;\frac{\Delta}{e^{2\Delta d}-1},

(246)

which can be evaluated as

P_{z}=-\frac{2\times 4\pi}{16(2\pi)^{3}d^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\frac{x^{3}}{e^{x}-1}=-\frac{2\times 4\pi}{16(2\pi)^{3}d^{4}}\Gamma(4)\zeta(4)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{240\,d^{4}}.

(247)

This is the usual Casimir pressure in the ideal case.

Now that we have retrieved the classical Lifschitz theory in the Schwinger-Keldysh formalism, we can explore the effects of the axion coupling to photons on the Casimir pressure.

here extended to the Schwinger-Keldysh case relevant to the dissipative case.

We can now concentrate on the corrections to the Green’s function at coinciding points as illustrated in Fig.

V.3
The vanishing dissipation limit
\gamma\to 0

Let us recall some of the properties of the propagators before discussing the different contributions to the axionic Casimir pressure. We will restrict ourselves to the weak dissipation limit
\gamma\to 0
as this simplifies the calculation and as for the ideal situation in the electromagnetic Casimir case, this provides a useful estimate of the effect without too many calculational complications.

For the diagonal propagators involving the first part of the Keldysh path we
can use the decomposition defined in section
II.3

\Delta^{11}_{\gamma}(z;z_{0},\omega,p_{\parallel})=i\Theta_{\gamma}(\omega,p_{\parallel})H_{\gamma}(z;z_{0},\omega,p_{\parallel})

(271)

when
\Re(\omega)>0
and we replace
\omega\to-\omega
when
\Re(\omega)<0
. This corresponds to the definition of the Feynman propagator.
Similarly we have

\Delta^{21}_{\gamma}(z;z_{0},\omega,p_{\parallel})\supset iY(\Re(\omega))\;\not{\delta}\left(\Theta^{-1}_{\gamma}(\omega,p_{\parallel})\right)\;H_{\gamma}(z;z_{0},\omega,p_{\parallel})

(272)

corresponding to the positive energy resonances, i.e. the resonances whose real parts are positive.
This is complemented by a contribution coming from the continuum with pulsations above
\omega_{\rm Pl}
.
A similar result holds for
\Delta^{12}_{\gamma}
with the negative frequency resonances.
In the Feynman integrals, we also need the modified propagators
\check{\Delta}_{\gamma}
. They involve the modified Fourier transform
\check{H}
which is defined like
\check{\Delta}_{\gamma}
.
Finally, we notice that the Green’s functions
\tilde{\Delta}
exchange the positive and negative frequency parts with the ones of
\Delta
.
Using this,
we see that
\delta G_{II}
vanishes as it is only non-vanishing when
\Re(\omega)=0
and this does not contribute to the pressure which involves an integral over
\omega
. Similarly, the contribution
\delta G_{III_{b}}
is only non-vanishing when
\Re(\omega)\in[-m,0]
and
\delta G_{III_{b}}
when
\Re(\omega)\in[0,m]
.

In the following, we will only be interested in axion masses
m\lesssim\omega_{\rm Pl}
corresponding to resonances for distances larger than the London length. In this regime, we see that the
\delta G_{III}
channels do not receive contributions from the continuum. Only the resonances can contribute. Collecting everything we have therefore

\displaystyle\delta G_{I}(z;z_{0},\omega,p_{\parallel})

\displaystyle\supset\frac{m^{2}\phi_{0}^{2}}{8M^{2}}\;\Theta_{\gamma}(\omega,p_{\parallel})\;\tilde{\Theta}_{\gamma}(\omega,p_{\parallel})\times

\displaystyle\int dz_{1}dz_{2}H_{\gamma}(z;z_{1},\omega,p_{\parallel})\check{\tilde{H}}_{\gamma}(z_{0};z_{2};\omega,p_{\parallel})

\displaystyle\Big[\Theta_{\bar{\gamma}}(\omega-m,p_{\parallel})\check{H}_{\bar{\gamma}}(z_{1};z_{2},\omega-m,p_{\parallel})+\Theta_{\bar{\gamma}}(\omega+m,p_{\parallel})\check{H}_{\bar{\gamma}}(z_{1};z_{2},\omega+m,p_{\parallel})\Big].

The third contributions give

\displaystyle\delta G_{III_{a}}(z;z_{0},\omega,p_{\parallel})

\displaystyle\supset-\frac{m^{2}\phi_{0}^{2}}{8M^{2}}\;Y\left(-\Re(\omega)\right)\;Y\left(\Re(\omega)+m\right)\;\Theta_{\gamma}(\omega,p_{\parallel})\times

\displaystyle\not{\delta}\left(\Theta^{-1}_{\bar{\gamma}}(\omega+m,p_{\parallel})\right)\;\not{\delta}\left(\tilde{\Theta}^{-1}_{\gamma}(\omega,p_{\parallel})\right)\times

\displaystyle\int dz_{1}dz_{2}H_{\gamma}(z;z_{1},\omega,p_{\parallel})\check{\tilde{H_{\gamma}}}(z_{2};z_{0};\omega,p_{\parallel})\check{H}_{\bar{\gamma}}(z_{1};z_{2},\omega+m,p_{\parallel}),

and similarly

\displaystyle\delta G_{III_{b}}(z;z_{0},\omega,p_{\parallel})

\displaystyle\supset-\frac{m^{2}\phi_{0}^{2}}{8M^{2}}\;Y\left(\Re(\omega)\right)\;Y\left(m-\Re(\omega)\right)\;\Theta_{\gamma}(\omega,p_{\parallel})\times

\displaystyle\not{\delta}\left(\Theta^{-1}_{\bar{\gamma}}(\omega-m,p_{\parallel})\right)\;\not{\delta}\left(\tilde{\Theta}^{-1}_{\gamma}(\omega,p_{\parallel})\right)\times

\displaystyle\int dz_{1}dz_{2}H_{\gamma}(z;z_{1},\omega,p_{\parallel})\check{\tilde{H}}_{\gamma}(z_{2};z_{0};\omega,p_{\parallel})\check{H}_{\bar{\gamma}}(z_{2};z_{1},\omega-m,p_{\parallel}).

As can be seen, the last two terms are only non-vanishing when two conditions are satisfied, i.e. either

\Theta^{-1}_{\bar{\gamma}}(\omega+m,p_{\parallel})=0,\quad\tilde{\Theta}^{-1}_{\gamma}(\omega,p_{\parallel})=0

(276)

or

\Theta^{-1}_{\bar{\gamma}}(\omega-m,p_{\parallel})=0,\quad\tilde{\Theta}^{-1}_{\gamma}(\omega,p_{\parallel})=0.

(277)

These equations must have solutions for either
\Re(\omega)\in[-m,0]
or
\Re(\omega)\in[0,m]
. When solutions exist, they would be in a finite number.
The resonances are given by (
35
) with an infinitesimal
\gamma
. The condition (
276
) must have solutions with the same imaginary parts. This implies that the integer numbers labeling the resonances must be equal. This is not compatible with the real parts being equal implying that there is no resonance when the system is weakly dissipative.
This implies that

\delta G(z;z_{0},\omega,p_{\parallel})=\delta G_{I}(z;z_{0},\omega,p_{\parallel})

(278)

which can be explicitly calculated analytically.

V.4
The axionic pressure in the ideal case

We will simplify the calculation of the correction to the Green’s function by approximating the propagators by their ideal expressions. This is a good approximation when the penetration length is much smaller than the distance between the plates in such a way that one can neglect the penetration of the modes into the plaques.
In this case the two Green’s functions reduce to

G_{E}(z;z_{0},\omega,p_{\parallel})=\frac{1}{\Delta}\frac{\sin\Delta(z_{0}-d)\sin\Delta z}{\sin\Delta d},\ G_{M}(z;z_{0},\omega,p_{\parallel})=\frac{1}{\Delta}\frac{\cos\Delta(z_{0}-d)\cos\Delta z}{\sin\Delta d}

(279)

and vanish outside the interval
[0,d]
. As a result the quantum correction to the Green’s functions becomes

\displaystyle\delta G_{E}(z;z_{0},\omega,p_{\parallel})

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{2}\phi_{0}^{2}}{8M^{2}}\int dz_{1}\;dz_{2}\;G_{E}(z;z_{1},\omega,p_{\parallel})\check{G}_{E}(z_{2};z_{0};\omega,p_{\parallel})\times

\displaystyle\left[\check{G}_{E}(z_{1};z_{2},\omega-m,p_{\parallel})+\check{G}_{E}(z_{1};z_{2},\omega+m,p_{\parallel})\right]

for the electric case. We have used
\tilde{G}(x;y)=G(y,x)
. The correction to the magnetic Green’s function is given by

\displaystyle\delta G_{M}(z;z_{0},\omega,p_{\parallel})

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{2}\phi_{0}^{2}}{8M^{2}}\int dz_{1}\;dz_{2}\;G_{M}(z;z_{1},\omega,p_{\parallel})\check{G}_{M}(z_{2};z_{0};\omega,p_{\parallel})\times

\displaystyle\left[\check{G}_{M}(z_{1};z_{2},\omega-m,p_{\parallel})+\check{G}_{M}(z_{1};z_{2},\omega+m,p_{\parallel})\right].

An easy calculation gives that for the modified propagators,

\check{G}_{E}(z;z_{0},\omega,p_{\parallel})=\omega G_{E}(z;z_{0},\omega,p_{\parallel});\quad\check{G}_{M}(z;z_{0},\omega,p_{\parallel})=\omega G_{M}(z;z_{0},\omega,p_{\parallel}).

(282)

The explicit evaluation of the integrals gives for the corrections

\displaystyle\delta G_{E}(z;z_{0},\omega,p_{\parallel})

\displaystyle=-\frac{m^{2}\omega\phi_{0}^{2}}{2M^{2}}\frac{\sin\Delta z\sin\Delta(z_{0}-d)}{\sin^{2}\Delta d}\times

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\left(\cos\Delta d-\cos\Delta^{-}d\right)^{2}}{\left(\Delta^{2}-(\Delta^{-})^{2}\right)^{2}}\frac{\omega-m}{\Delta^{-}\sin\Delta^{-}d}+\frac{\left(\cos\Delta d-\cos\Delta^{+}d\right)^{2}}{\left(\Delta^{2}-(\Delta^{+})^{2}\right)^{2}}\frac{\omega+m}{\Delta^{+}\sin\Delta^{+}d}\right]

where
\Delta^{\pm}
are associated to
\omega\pm m
. For the magnetic Green’s function we have

\displaystyle\delta G_{M}(z;z_{0},\omega,p_{\parallel})

\displaystyle=-\frac{m^{2}\omega\phi_{0}^{2}}{2M^{2}}\frac{\cos\Delta z\cos\Delta(z_{0}-d)}{\Delta^{2}\sin^{2}\Delta d}\times

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\left(\cos\Delta d-\cos\Delta^{-}d\right)^{2}}{\left(\Delta^{2}-(\Delta^{-})^{2}\right)^{2}}\frac{\Delta_{-}(\omega-m)}{\sin\Delta^{-}d}+\frac{\left(\cos\Delta d-\cos\Delta^{+}d\right)^{2}}{\left(\Delta^{2}-(\Delta^{+})^{2}\right)^{2}}\frac{\Delta_{+}(\omega+m)}{\sin\Delta^{+}d}\right].

Notice that the Green’s functions are simply corrected by momentum-dependent form factors.
Using these expressions we can get the pressure after summing over the two polarisations. Let us first consider the correction to the electric pressure

\displaystyle\delta P_{E}(d)

\displaystyle=-i\frac{m^{2}\phi_{0}^{2}}{4M^{2}}\int\not{d}\omega\;\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;\frac{\omega\Delta^{2}\cos\Delta d}{\sin^{2}\Delta d}\times

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\left(\cos\Delta d-\cos\Delta^{-}d\right)^{2}}{\left(\Delta^{2}-(\Delta^{-})^{2}\right)^{2}}\frac{\omega-m}{\Delta^{-}\sin\Delta^{-}d}+\frac{\left(\cos\Delta d-\cos\Delta^{+}d\right)^{2}}{\left(\Delta^{2}-(\Delta^{+})^{2}\right)^{2}}\frac{\omega+m}{\Delta^{+}\sin\Delta^{+}d}\right].

This can be evaluated after Wick’s rotation
\omega\to i\omega
where we have

\Delta^{\pm}=\sqrt{(\omega\mp im)^{2}+p^{2}_{\parallel}}
leading to

\displaystyle\delta P_{E}(d)

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{2}\phi_{0}^{2}}{2M^{2}}\times

(286)

\displaystyle\Re\left(\int\not{d}\omega\;\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;\omega(\omega+im)\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\Delta^{-}(\Delta^{2}-(\Delta^{-})^{2})^{2}}\frac{\cosh\Delta d(\cosh\Delta d-\cosh\Delta^{-}d)^{2}}{\sinh^{2}\Delta d\sinh\Delta^{-}d}\right).

This expression can be simplified as

\delta P_{E}=\frac{\phi_{0}^{2}}{2M^{2}}\;\frac{1}{d^{4}}\;f_{E}(md),

(287)

where the form factor is given by the integral

f_{E}(md)=\Re\left(\int\not{d}x\;\not{d}^{2}\vec{y}\;x(x+imd)\frac{\hat{\Delta}^{2}}{\hat{\Delta}^{-}(2x+imd)^{2}}\frac{\cosh\hat{\Delta}\left(\cosh\hat{\Delta}-\cosh\hat{\Delta}^{-}\right)^{2}}{\sinh^{2}\hat{\Delta}\sinh\hat{\Delta}^{-}}\right),

(288)

where we have defined
x=\omega d
and
\vec{y}=\vec{p}_{\parallel}d
and obtain

\hat{\Delta}=(x^{2}+\vec{y}^{\;2})^{1/2},\ \hat{\Delta}^{-}=((x+imd)^{2}+\vec{y}^{\;2})^{1/2}.

(289)

The same method yields for the magnetic contribution

\displaystyle\delta P_{M}(d)

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{2}\phi_{0}^{2}}{2M^{2}}\times

(290)

\displaystyle\Re\left(\int\not{d}\omega\not{d}^{2}\vec{p}_{\parallel}\omega(\omega+im)\frac{\Delta_{-}}{\left(\Delta^{2}-(\Delta^{-})^{2}\right)^{2}}\frac{\cosh\Delta d\left(\cosh\Delta d-\cosh\Delta^{-}d\right)^{2}}{\sinh^{2}\Delta d\sinh\Delta^{-}d}\right),

corresponding to

\delta P_{M}=\frac{\phi_{0}^{2}}{2M^{2}}\;\frac{1}{d^{4}}\;f_{M}(md).

(291)

where the form factor is given by the integral

f_{M}(md)=\Re\left(\int\not{d}x\;\not{d}^{2}\vec{y}\;x(x+imd)\frac{\hat{\Delta}_{-}}{(2x+imd)^{2}}\frac{\cosh\hat{\Delta}\left(\cosh\hat{\Delta}-\cosh\hat{\Delta}^{-}\right)^{2}}{\sinh^{2}\hat{\Delta}\sinh\hat{\Delta}^{-}}\right).

(292)

Notice that the complete form factor
f_{E}+f_{M}
vanishes when
m\to 0
corresponding to the vanishing of the effect of the axion in the static limit. As expected the correction to the pressure has a dependence in
1/d^{4}
and corrections in
md
.

V.5
The Axionic pressure

The form factors
f_{E,M}
are ultraviolet (UV) sensitive and dominated by the largest momenta and energies.
In this case and in the limit
md\ll\omega_{\rm Pl}d
, we find that the electric form factor behaves like

f_{E}\simeq\frac{1}{4}\int\not{d}^{3}{\hat{\Delta}}\;\frac{\hat{\Delta}}{\tanh^{3}\hat{\Delta}}\left(1-e^{imdx/\hat{\Delta}}\right)^{2},

(293)

where we have defined the vector
\vec{\hat{\Delta}}=(x,\vec{y})
. This integral is infrared convergent but diverges quartically in the UV where the cut-off scale is of the order of
\omega_{\rm Pl}d
as no pressure is exerted at higher frequencies as the metal becomes transparent. Using this explicit cut-off, the form factor behaves like

f_{E}\simeq\frac{\pi}{8}\left(\omega_{\rm Pl}d\right)^{4}\left(2-\frac{4}{md}\sin md+\frac{1}{md}\sin 2md\right).

(294)

This gives a contribution to the pressure in

\delta P_{E}\simeq\frac{\omega_{\rm Pl}^{4}}{128\pi^{2}}\frac{\phi_{0}^{2}}{M^{2}}\left(2-\frac{4}{md}\sin md+\frac{1}{md}\sin 2md\right).

(295)

This leads to a constant pressure in the limit
d\to\infty
which must be substracted as before.
Doing so, the renormalised form factor becomes

f_{E}\simeq\frac{1}{4}\int\not{d}^{3}\;{\hat{\Delta}}\hat{\Delta}\left(\frac{1}{\tanh^{3}\hat{\Delta}}-1\right)\left(1-e^{imdx/\hat{\Delta}}\right)^{2}\simeq\frac{3}{2}\int\not{d}^{3}{\hat{\Delta}}\frac{\hat{\Delta}}{e^{2\hat{\Delta}}-1}\left(1-e^{imdx/\hat{\Delta}}\right)^{2}.

(296)

This gives explicitly

f_{E}\simeq 3\left(1-\frac{2}{md}\sin md+\frac{1}{2md}\sin 2md\right)\int\not{d}^{3}{\hat{\Delta}}\;\frac{\hat{\Delta}}{e^{2\hat{\Delta}}-1}.

(297)

The last factor reconstructs the TE casimir pressure in the ideal case.
As a result we have

\delta P_{E}\simeq-\frac{3}{2}\left(1-\frac{2}{md}\sin md+\frac{1}{2md}\sin 2md\right)\frac{\phi_{0}^{2}}{M^{2}}P_{E}.

(298)

There is also the magnetic contribution implying that the correction to the Casimir pressure due to the axion is given by

\frac{\delta P}{P}\simeq-\frac{3}{2}\left(1-\frac{2}{md}\sin md+\frac{1}{2md}\sin 2md\right)\frac{\phi_{0}^{2}}{M^{2}}.

(299)

At short distance we have

\frac{\delta P}{P}\simeq\frac{\phi_{0}^{2}}{2M^{2}}(md)^{2}.

(300)

The dependence of the correction with
md
is plotted in Fig.
11
. At short distance the correction is positive and evolves quadratically with
d
for a fixed mass, as shown in the inset of the figure. Interestingly we find that that for
md={\cal O}(1)
, the pressure changes sign and the plates repulse. This type of behaviour is similar to the one obtained when the axion depends on time or space linearly
[
27
,
29
]
. For larger distances, the correction to the axion-induced Casimir pressure oscillates and becomes constant. The pressure itself vanishes as
1/d^{4}
so that for large distances the usual CDM equation of state
p=0
is retrieved. The suppression of the quantum effect by
\phi_{0}^{2}/M^{2}
implies that for dark matter in the galactic halo this contribution is negligible.
On the other hand, for axion clumps of very large densities, we may hope that the quantum effects could be relevant. The analysis of this possibility is left for future work.

VI
Conclusion

We have described the effects of the axion oscillations in the environment of a Casimir experiment on the pressure exerted on the metallic plates. We have focused on metallic plates with dissipation and retrieved the Casimir pressure when axions are neglected, i.e. the Lifschitz theory, and then corrected this result at leading order in the axion coupling to the photons. We have developed these calculations in the Schwinger-Keldysh formulation of the
in-in
formalism as it is particularly suited to evaluate the vacuum expectation value of operators even in the case of dissipative systems. We have also used a field-doubling trick in order to describe dissipative systems in a Lagrangian way. For this we have introduced a “shadow” field whose role is to run history in reverse and bring back energy from infinity. This guarantees that a sound quantum field theory formulation can be defined. This doubling trick makes the connection with classical electrodynamics non-trivial. However we have shown how the conserved energy momentum tensor of the doubled theory can be written as the sum of a well defined energy momentum tensor and its time-reversed. Identifying the energy momentum tensor of electrodynamics in the presence of dissipation in this manner, we have shown how the pressure on the plates deduces from the spatial components of this tensor exactly reproduce the Lifschitz theory.

We have then calculated the corrections to the Green’s function induced by the axion field at second order in the axion coupling constant. As expected, the resulting pressure vanishes for massless axions. Indeed in this case, the axion term disappears from the action for trivial topologies and no axion effect is expected. On the other hand, we find that for small distances compared to the axion’s Compton wavelength, the induced pressure varies quadratically with the distance and is attractive. For larger distances and a given mass, the axion contribution changes sign and oscillates with respect to the distance. This is a breaking of the Kenneth-Klich theorem
[
23
]
which happens when parity is violated
[
22
]
. Here the role of parity breaking medium is played by the dark matter background with an oscillating axion.
We have not considered the phenomenology associated to this new effect. In particular, the axion-induced pressure is small due to the tiny coupling and the small background dark matter density in the galactic halo unless we take into account the possibility of enhanced effects in axion clumps. This is left for further investigation.

\epsilon(t)=\not{\delta}(t)+\int\not{d}\omega\;e^{-i\omega t}\left(\epsilon(\omega)-1\right)

\epsilon(t)=\not{\delta}(t),\ t<0
.
When
\epsilon(\omega)
has a pole at the origin as for metals, we define the integral by drawing a small circle around the origin in the complex plane as shown in Fig.

\epsilon(t)-\delta(t)

t\geq 0
.

H_{0}=\int d^{3}x\;\left(\partial_{0}\tilde{a}_{i}\partial_{0}a_{i}+\partial_{i}\tilde{a}_{j}\partial_{i}a_{j}\right)=\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\int d^{3}x\;\left(\partial_{0}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}\partial_{0}a_{\alpha}+\partial_{i}\tilde{a}_{\alpha}\partial_{i}a_{\alpha}\right)

Appendix B

in-in
vs.
in-out

In this section we will recall the link between the
in-in
and
in-out
formulations of quantum field theory when all the usual assumptions used in particle physics are respected. In particular, we assume that the Hamiltonian is Hermitian implying that the evolution operator is unitary. We also consider the case where Poincaré invariance is respected implying that the notion of particle state is well-defined. The ground state of the theory is also assumed to be unique and corresponds to the absence of particles.
We are interested in quantum averages in the Schrödinger picture

\left\langle{\cal O}\right\rangle_{\psi}(t)\equiv\langle\psi(t)|{\cal O}|\psi(t)\rangle

(310)

where the ket
|\psi\rangle
evolves with time
|\psi\rangle(t)=U(t,-\infty)|\psi\rangle
. The system is prepared at time minus infinity and then evolves with the unitary evolution operator.
We focus on vacuum averages where
|\psi\rangle=|0\rangle
is the vacuum of the non-interacting theory at time
t=-\infty
before the interactions are switched on. This vacuum is the
in
vacuum of the theory
|0\rangle=|{\rm in}\rangle
. We can then use the fact that the free Hamiltonian
H_{0}|0\rangle=0
has vanishing energy in vacuum to write

\langle{\cal O}\rangle_{\rm vac}(t)\equiv\left\langle{\rm{in}}\left|U^{\dagger}_{I}(t,-\infty){\cal O}_{I}(t)U_{I}(t,-\infty)\right|{\rm{in}}\right\rangle

(311)

in the terms of the operator
{\cal O}_{I}(t)
and the evolution operator in the interaction picture.
We will be interested in the vacuum expectation value (vev) of
{\cal O}
when all the transient effects have disappeared corresponding to
t=\infty
.
Now we can identify the
out
vacuum as

|{\rm out}\rangle=S|{\rm in}\rangle

(312)

where the scattering
S
-matrix is defined as
S=U_{I}(\infty,-\infty)

As a result we have

\langle{\cal O}\rangle_{\rm vac}(\infty)=\left\langle\rm{out}\left|{\cal O}_{I}(\infty)S\right|\rm{in}\right\rangle

(313)

This is the origin of the
in-out
evaluation of vevs using explicit
S
matrix elements.
Stability of the vacuum and unitarity imply that

|{\rm out}\rangle=e^{i\alpha}|{\rm in}\rangle

(314)

where

e^{i\alpha}=\langle{\rm in}|S|{\rm in}\rangle

(315)

leading to

\langle{\cal O}\rangle_{\rm vac}(\infty)=\frac{\langle\rm{int}|{\cal O}_{I}(\infty)S|\rm{in}\rangle}{\langle{\rm in}|S|{\rm in}\rangle}.

(316)

This is the Gellmann-Low formula. Now for Lagrangian field theories, we can use the Dyson formula

S=T\left(e^{i\int d^{4}x{\cal L}_{I}}\right)

(317)

where
{\cal L}_{I}
is the interaction Lagrangian. This leads to the usual result in terms of path integrals

\langle{\cal O}\rangle_{\rm vac}(\infty)=\frac{\int{\cal D}\phi{\cal O}(\infty)e^{i\int d^{4}x{\cal L}_{I}(\phi,\partial_{\mu}\phi)}}{\int{\cal D}\phi e^{i\int d^{4}x{\cal L}_{I}(\phi,\partial_{\mu}\phi)}}

(318)

where we have assumed that the field theory is defined by the field
\phi
.

None of these results stand in the dissipative case as the evolution operator is not unitarity. Moreover, the notion of particle is ill-defined in the Casimir setup as translation invariance is broken. As a result, the vev’s must be evaluated in the Schwinger-Keldysh formalism where such assumptions are not made.
All this implies that there are memory effects which cannot be cancelled, for instance

\langle 0|a_{\alpha}(t_{1})a_{\beta}(t_{2})|0\rangle=\langle 0|U_{I}^{\dagger}(t_{1},-\infty)a_{\alpha I}(t_{1})U_{I}(t_{1},-\infty)U^{\dagger}(t_{2},-\infty)a_{\beta I}(t_{2})U_{I}(t_{2},-\infty)|0\rangle.

(319)

In the absence of dissipation this reads

\langle 0|a_{\alpha}(t_{1})a_{\beta}(t_{2})|0\rangle=\langle 0|U_{I}(-\infty,t_{1})a_{\alpha I}(t_{1})U(t_{1},t_{2})a_{\beta I}(t_{2})U_{I}(t_{2},-\infty)(t_{2})|0\rangle

(320)

which can be represented as

\langle 0|a_{\alpha}(t_{1})a_{\beta}(t_{2})|0\rangle=\langle 0|T_{C}(a_{\alpha I}(t_{1})a_{\beta I}(t_{2})U_{I}(-\infty,-\infty))|0\rangle

(321)

where the time ordering is along the path
C
which goes from
-\infty
to
\infty
and back to
-\infty
.
When dissipation is present we obtain

U_{I}(t_{1},-\infty)U_{I}^{\dagger}(t_{2},-\infty)\neq U_{I}(t_{1},t_{2})

(322)

where
U(t_{1},t_{2})=T\left(e^{-i\int_{t_{2}}^{t_{1}}{\cal H}_{I}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}\right)
due to
H_{I}^{\dagger}\neq H_{I}
.
As as result the Schwinger-Keldysh representation of the correlators does not
work because of dissipation. This is why in the main text, we only consider the time evolution of single operators.

Appendix C
Quantum dissipation

We focus on the interior of the cavity of size
d
seen as an open system when the reflection coefficients are not unity. Its energy is given by the two contributions

\frac{E^{+}}{A}=-\frac{d}{\pi}\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\;\frac{\omega^{2}}{\Delta}\frac{1}{\left(\frac{1+\frac{\xi}{\Delta}}{1-\frac{\xi}{\Delta}}\right)^{2}e^{2\Delta d}-1}

(323)

for the TE polarisation
and

\frac{E^{-}}{A}=-\frac{d}{\pi}\int\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\;\frac{\omega^{2}}{\Delta}\frac{1}{\left(\frac{1+\frac{\xi}{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta}}{1-\frac{\xi}{\epsilon(i\omega)\Delta}}\right)^{2}e^{2\Delta d}-1}

(324)

for the TM case.
When dissipation is present, the first law of thermodynamics gives

dE=-PdV+\delta Q

(325)

where
\delta Q
is the heat associated with the change of volume. This tells us that the pressure is not simply the derivative of
E
. For the cavity of size
d

we can define the quantum work
W_{\pm}
for the two polarisations as

P_{\pm}=-\partial_{d}W_{\pm}

(326)

which reads

W_{\pm}=\frac{A}{2}\int\not{d}\omega\not{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}\;\ln\left(1-r_{TE/TM}^{2}e^{-2\Delta d}\right).

(327)

With this we can identify the loss of energy from the cavity as the energy penetrating the plates

Q_{\pm}=E_{R}^{\pm}-W_{\pm}

(328)

which is a function of the distance
d
.
In the ideal case, the reflection coefficients are unity and the cavity behaves like a closed system. In this case no energy flows from the cavity to the plates, i.e.
Q_{\pm}=0
. This is non-vanishing for real metals as the reflection coefficients are not equal to unity anymore and the cavity behaves like an open system. Some of the energy will disappear and
Q_{\pm}
should account for some of the energy flowing away to infinity and some being dissipated in the metal when the imaginary part of
\epsilon
is non-vanishing.

Appendix D
The electric Green’s function

In this appendix we will give explicit formulae for the Green’s function satisfying

-\partial_{0}(\epsilon\star_{t}\partial_{0}G_{E})+\Delta G_{E}=\delta^{(4)}(x^{\mu}-y^{\mu}).

(329)

In the following we will use time-translation invariance and space-translation invariance in the
(x,y)
plane along the plaques to choose
y^{\mu}=(\vec{0},z_{0},0)
.
In terms of Fourier decomposition the Green’s function satisfies

\left(\partial_{z}^{2}-p_{\parallel}^{2}+\epsilon(\omega)\omega^{2}\right)G_{E}=\delta(z-z_{0}).

(330)

We will separate the
z
-axis into three intervals and give the solution in each case using continuity of the Green’s function at the boundaries

z=0
and
z=d
. The first derivative is continuous there too whilst there is a jump
\left[\frac{dG_{E}}{dz}\right]_{z=z_{0}}=1
of the first derivative when
G_{E}
itself is continuous at
z=z_{0}
. Moreover we impose that
\lim_{|z|\to\infty}G_{E}=0
. This determines a unique solution.

D.1
z_{0}\in[0,d]

When
0\leq z_{0}\leq d
is between the plates
the Green’s function is then defined by

G_{E}=G^{E}_{-}e^{\xi z},\ z<0

(331)

and

G_{E}=G^{E}_{+}e^{-\xi(z-d)},\ z>d

(332)

where we have now

G^{E}_{-}=\Theta_{E}(\omega,p_{\parallel})\left(\cos\Delta(z_{0}-d)-\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\sin\Delta(z_{0}-d)\right)

(333)

together with

G^{E}_{+}={\Theta_{E}(\omega,p_{\parallel})}\left(\cos\Delta z_{0}+\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\sin\Delta z_{0}\right)

(334)

and the Green’s function between the plates

\displaystyle 0\leq z\leq z_{0},\ G_{E}=G^{E}_{-}\left(\cos\Delta z+\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\sin\Delta z\right)

\displaystyle z_{0}\leq z\leq d,\ G_{E}=G^{E}_{+}\left(\cos\Delta(z-d)-\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\sin\Delta(z-d)\right)

.

(335)

where

\Theta_{E}(\omega,p_{\parallel})=\frac{1}{\Delta\left((1-\frac{\xi^{2}}{\Delta^{2}})\sin\Delta d-2\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\cos\Delta d\right)}.

(336)

D.2
z_{0}<0

This case is different from the previous case as

G_{E}=G^{E}_{-}e^{\xi z},\ z\leq z_{0}

(337)

and

G_{E}=Ae^{\xi z}+Be^{-\xi z},\ z\in[z_{0},0]

(338)

whilst

\displaystyle 0\leq z\leq d,\ G_{E}=G^{E}_{+}\left[\left(\sin\Delta d-\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\cos\Delta d\right)\sin\Delta z+\left(\cos\Delta d+\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\sin\Delta d\right)\cos\Delta z\right]

\displaystyle z\geq d,\ G_{E}=G^{E}_{+}e^{-\xi(z-d)}

.

(339)

We find that

\displaystyle A=\frac{G^{E}_{+}}{2}\left[\cos\Delta d+\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\sin\Delta d+\frac{\Delta}{\xi}\left(\sin\Delta d-\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\cos\Delta d\right)\right]

\displaystyle B=\frac{G^{E}_{+}}{2}\left[\cos\Delta d+\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\sin\Delta d-\frac{\Delta}{\xi}\left(\sin\Delta d-\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\cos\Delta d\right)\right]

and we have

G^{E}_{+}=\Theta_{E}(\omega,p_{\parallel})e^{\xi z_{0}}.

(341)

Finally we find that

G^{E}_{-}=\Theta_{E}(\omega,p_{\parallel})\left[\cosh\xi z_{0}\left(\cos\Delta d+\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\sin\Delta d\right)+\frac{\Delta}{\xi}\sinh\xi z_{0}\left(\sin\Delta d-\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\cos\Delta d\right)\right]

(342)

D.3
z_{0}>d

We have now

z<0,\ G_{E}=G^{E}_{-}e^{\xi z}

(343)

and

z\in[0,d],\ G_{E}=G^{E}_{-}\left(\cos\Delta z+\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\sin\Delta z\right)

(344)

whilst we have

z\in[d,z_{0}],\ G_{E}=\frac{G^{E}_{-}}{2}\left[\sin\Delta d\left(\frac{\xi}{\Delta}+\frac{\Delta}{\xi}\right)e^{-\xi(z-d)}+\left(2\cos\Delta d+\sin\Delta d\left(\frac{\xi}{\Delta}-\frac{\Delta}{\xi}\right)\right)e^{\xi(z-d)}\right]

(345)

and finally

z>z_{0},\ G_{E}=G^{E}_{+}e^{-\xi(z-d)}

(346)

where we find

G^{E}_{-}=\Theta_{E}(\omega,p_{\parallel})e^{-\xi(z_{0}-d)}

(347)

and

\displaystyle G^{E}_{+}

\displaystyle=\Theta_{E}(\omega,p_{\parallel})\Big[\cosh\xi(z_{0}-d)\left(\cos\Delta d+\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\sin\Delta d\right)

(348)

\displaystyle+\frac{\Delta}{\xi}\sinh\xi(z_{0}-d)\left(-\sin\Delta d+\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\cos\Delta d\right)\Big]

Appendix E
The magnetic Green’s functions

E.1
The link to the electric Green’s function

The magnetic Green’s function satisfies the identity

-\partial_{0}^{2}G_{M}+\epsilon^{-1}\star_{t}\Delta G_{M}=\delta^{(4)}(x^{\mu}-y^{\mu})

(349)

Away from the point
x=x_{0}
, it is a solution of the homogeneous equation and therefore admits the same expansion in exponentials for
z<0
and
z>d
whilst it is
a linear combination of
\cos\Delta z
and
\sin\Delta z
between
0
and
d
. The only difference between the two Green’s function comes from the boundary conditions involving the continuity of
\partial_{z}G_{E}
for the TE case and
\epsilon^{-1}(\omega)\partial_{z}G_{M}
for the TM polarisation. This is enough to deduce the expression for the Green’s function. Indeed we only have to substitute

\xi\to\frac{\xi}{\epsilon}

(350)

in the coefficients of the electric function to find the magnetic function.
For instance when
0\leq z_{0}\leq d
is between the plates
the magnetic Green’s is function obtained as

G_{M}=G^{M}_{-}e^{\xi z},\ z<0

(351)

and

G_{M}=G^{M}_{+}e^{-\xi(z-d)},\ z>d

(352)

where

G^{M}_{-}=\Theta_{M}(\omega,p_{\parallel})\left(\cos\Delta(z_{0}-d)-\frac{\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}\sin\Delta(z_{0}-d)\right)

(353)

and

G^{M}_{+}={\Theta_{M}(\omega,p_{\parallel})}\left(\cos\Delta z_{0}+\frac{\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}\sin\Delta z_{0}\right).

(354)

The magnetic Green’s function between the plates is

\displaystyle 0\leq z\leq z_{0},\ G_{M}=G^{M}_{-}\left(\cos\Delta z+\frac{\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}\sin\Delta z\right)

\displaystyle z_{0}\leq z\leq d,\ G_{M}=G^{M}_{+}\left(\cos\Delta(z-d)-\frac{\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}\sin\Delta(z-d)\right)

.

(355)

where

\Theta_{M}(\omega,p_{\parallel})=\frac{1}{\Delta\left((1-\frac{\xi^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}\Delta^{2}})\sin\Delta d-2\frac{\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}\cos\Delta d\right)}.

(356)

Similar expressions are valid in the other two intervals. All this can be checked by an explicit calculation.

E.2
Convolution and the magnetic Green’s function

Some properties of the TM two-point functions are useful.
The magnetic two-point function are given by

\Delta_{M}(x;y)=\left\langle a^{1}_{-}(x)(\hat{\epsilon}\star_{t_{y}}\tilde{a}^{1}_{-})(y)\right\rangle

(357)

where the permittivity function is convolved with the second field
\tilde{a}^{1}_{\alpha}
, and the convolution is expressed in time leading to

\Delta_{M}(x;y)=\int dt\;\epsilon(t-t_{y})\left\langle a^{1}_{-}(\vec{x},t_{x})\tilde{a}^{1}_{-}(\vec{y},t)\right\rangle.

(358)

As the two-point functions respect time translation invariance,, we have
\Delta_{M}(x;y)=\Delta_{M}(\vec{x},t_{x}-t_{y};\vec{y},0)
and therefore

\Delta_{M}(x;y)=\int dt\;\epsilon(t-t_{y})\Delta_{M}(\vec{x},t_{x}-t;\vec{y},0).

(359)

Changing variable to
\tilde{t}=t_{x}+t_{y}-t
we find

\Delta_{M}(x;y)=\int d\tilde{t}\;\epsilon(t_{x}-\tilde{t})\Delta_{M}(\vec{x},\tilde{t}-t_{y};\vec{y},0)=\int d\tilde{t}\;\epsilon(t_{x}-\tilde{t})\left\langle a^{1}_{-}(\vec{x},\tilde{t})\tilde{a}^{1}_{-}(\vec{y},t_{y})\right\rangle

(360)

and therefore

\Delta_{M}(x;y)=(\epsilon\star_{t_{x}}\Delta^{M}_{-})(x;y)=\left(\hat{\epsilon}\star_{t_{y}}\Delta^{M}_{-}\right)(x;y)=(\Delta^{M}_{-}\star_{t_{y}}\epsilon)(x;y).

(361)

where

\Delta^{M}_{-}(x;y)=\left\langle a^{1}_{-}(\vec{x},\tilde{t})\tilde{a}^{1}_{-}(\vec{y},t_{y})\right\rangle.

(362)

In regions of space where
\epsilon(\omega)=1
, we will identify
\Delta_{M}
and
\Delta^{M}_{-}
.

E.3
Magnetic resonances

The resonance equation for the
TM
polarisation is

\Theta_{M}(\omega,p_{\parallel})=0

(363)

where

\Theta(\omega,p_{\parallel})=\frac{1}{\Delta\left((1-\frac{\xi^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}\Delta^{2}})\sin\Delta d-2\frac{\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}\cos\Delta d\right)}.

(364)

This has solutions for

\tan\Delta d=\frac{2\frac{\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}}{1-\frac{\xi^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}\Delta^{2}}}.

(365)

We consider the cases when
\gamma\ll|\omega|\ll\omega_{\rm Pl}
implying that
\Delta\ll\omega_{\rm Pl}
and

\frac{\xi}{\Delta}\simeq\frac{\omega_{\rm Pl}}{\Delta}\left(1-i\frac{\gamma}{2\omega}\left(1+\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\omega_{\rm Pl}}\right)\right)

(366)

which is large and therefore

\tan\Delta d=\frac{2\xi}{\epsilon\Delta}\simeq-2\frac{\omega^{2}}{\omega_{\rm Pl}\Delta}\left(1+\frac{i\gamma}{2\omega}-\frac{\Delta^{2}}{2\omega_{\rm Pl}^{2}}\right).

(367)

The solutions are obtained by iteration and are

\Delta_{n}d=n\pi-2\frac{(\omega_{n}^{(0)})^{2}d}{n\pi\omega_{\rm Pl}}\left(1+i\frac{\gamma}{2\omega_{n}^{(0)}}\right),\ n\neq 0

(368)

where to leading order

\omega_{n}^{(0)}={\rm sign}(n)\sqrt{\frac{n^{2}\pi^{2}}{d^{2}}+p_{\parallel}^{2}}.

(369)

The imaginary part of the resonance frequency is obtained by squaring (
32
) implying that

\omega_{n}^{2}=\frac{n^{2}\pi^{2}}{d^{2}}+p_{\parallel}^{2}-2i\frac{\gamma\omega_{n}^{(0)}}{\omega_{\rm Pl}d}

(370)

Similarly to the
TE
case, the resonances are below the real axis. Above the plasma frequency, the resonance condition
is the same as the
TE
case and there are no solutions.
