Characterization of Angle-Angle Diameter Relationship with Corneal Diameter, Axial Length, and Intraocular Pressure in Pediatric Glaucoma
Authors: Taylor Kolosky, Reshmi Talwar, He Eun Forbes, Andrew Lee, Moran R Levin, Janet L Alexander
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo characterize the relationship of angle-angle diameter (AA) with intraocular pressure (IOP), corneal diameter (CD), and axial length (AL) in pediatric patients with glaucoma.DesignProspective cross-sectional observational study.ParticipantsNineteen participants (34 eyes) aged 0.12 to 12.5 years (median = 1.5 years) with pediatric glaucoma.MethodsUltrasound biomicroscopy (UBM), A-scan biometry, and IOP and CD measurement were performed under general anesthesia prior to intraocular surgery. A masked observer measured AA in each UBM image using ImageJ. Pearson analysis and multivariate regression were performed using R.Main Outcome MeasuresCorrelation and age-adjusted regression relationships between AA and CD, AL, and IOP.ResultsTwenty-three UBM images from 12 primary congenital glaucoma (PCG) participants, 6 from 3 participants with glaucoma following cataract surgery (GFCS), and 5 from 4 participants with Sturge-Weber syndrome–associated glaucoma were analyzed. Angle-angle diameter showed a strong positive correlation with CD (r = 0.93, P < 0.001) across most age groups and glaucoma subtypes except Sturge-Weber syndrome. Angle-angle diameter was also strongly correlated with AL in PCG and GFCS (r = 0.73, P < 0.001; r = 0.87, P = 0.02), with similar associations seen in participants aged <1 year (r = 0.75, P = 0.002). Corneal diameter demonstrated parallel correlations with AL in PCG, GFCS, and infants (r = 0.79, P < 0.001; r = 0.88, P = 0.02; r = 0.78, P < 0.001). No significant correlations were identified between IOP and either AA or CD. In age-adjusted models, AA was not significantly associated with AL or IOP in the overall cohort. However, among PCG eyes, each 1-mm increase in AA corresponded to a significant 1.63-mm increase in AL (P = 0.002). No additional significant associations were observed in other glaucoma subtypes or age groups.ConclusionsAngle-angle diameter may be clinically useful for diagnosing and monitoring glaucoma in pediatric patients, as it tends to increase proportionally with AL. Further studies in larger populations will improve understanding of how these relationships change with age and disease severity.Financial Disclosure(s)Proprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.Keywords: Angle-angle diameter, Pediatric glaucoma, Ultrasound biomicroscopy, Primary congenital glaucoma

Pediatric glaucoma is a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by increased intraocular pressure (IOP), leading to optic nerve damage and potential vision loss. Unlike adult glaucoma, which is often associated with open or narrow angles and progressive nerve damage over time, pediatric glaucoma presents with distinct anatomical and physiological changes. These include increased axial length (AL), corneal diameter (CD) enlargement, and deep anterior chambers due to elevated IOP.1, 2, 3 Accurate and reliable assessment of these structural parameters is important for the diagnosis and management of pediatric glaucoma.

Ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) is a valuable imaging modality for evaluating anterior segment anatomy. Ultrasound biomicroscopy has shown particularly strong clinical utility in the pediatric population given its capability for high-resolution visualization even in eyes with significant corneal opacity or clouding.4 Among its capabilities is the ability to measure the angle-angle diameter (AA) or the anterior chamber width at the level of the trabecular-iris angle. Angle-angle diameter may serve as a useful structural parameter in children with limbal physiology that can complicate CD measurement, such as aniridia-associated keratopathy, Peters anomaly, and sclerocornea. Several prior studies have shown a consistent relationship between AA and CD in children and adults.5, 6, 7 Other studies have reported on the relationship between CD and key glaucoma-associated parameters, including IOP and AL.3,8,9 The relationship between AA and these parameters remains insufficiently characterized in pediatric populations. Understanding these relationships could aid in refining diagnostic criteria, assessing disease severity, and monitoring disease progression in young patients.

This study was conducted to investigate the associations between AA, CD, IOP, and AL in pediatric patients with pediatric glaucoma using UBM and standardized biometric assessments. We hypothesize that (1) AA has a strong positive correlation with CD, (2) AA increases proportionally with AL and IOP, and (3) these relationships will allow use of AA as a marker of glaucoma progression in infants and children.

Methods

Participants

This study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki as amended in 2013. The University of Maryland Baltimore Institutional Review Board has approved this protocol and the corresponding consent forms. Written informed consent for participation in UBM imaging was obtained from the parents of each participant. Collection and evaluation of protected health information was compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996.

Thirty-four eyes from 19 pediatric participants (median age: 16.2 months; range: 1.4–150 months) were included in this study: 23 eyes from 12 participants with primary congenital glaucoma (PCG) (median age: 10.5 months; range: 1.4–150 months), 6 eyes from 3 participants with glaucoma following cataract surgery (GFCS) (median age: 69 months; range: 20–107 months), and 5 eyes from 4 participants with Sturge-Weber syndrome (SWS)–associated glaucoma (median age: 26 months; range: 5–117 months). Participants were divided into 3 age subgroups: between 0 and 1 year (8 participants, 14 eyes), between 1 and 3 years (5 participants, 9 eyes), and ≥3 years (6 participants, 11 eyes). Our cohort was composed of participants who underwent UBM imaging at the University of Maryland Medical Center or Washington University Medical Center between November 2014 and May 2024. The diagnosis of pediatric glaucoma was based on the Childhood Glaucoma Research Network guidelines,10 which require ≥2 of the following in a patient aged <18 years: (1) IOP >21 mmHg; (2) reproducible visual fields defect consistent with glaucomatous optic neuropathy with no other observable reason for the defect; (3) progressive myopia or myopic shift with increased ocular dimensions that outpace normal growth; (4) increased CD (>11.0 mm in newborns or >12.0 mm in children aged <1 year or >13.0 mm in children aged >1 year); and (5) progressive increase in cup-to-disc ratio, cup-disc asymmetry of ≥0.2 when optic discs are of similar size, and focal rim thinning. Subjects with a history of anterior segment trauma were excluded. Primary congenital glaucoma was diagnosed when a child met the criteria for pediatric glaucoma diagnosis and had isolated angle anomalies and no history of eye surgery or trauma. Primary congenital glaucoma can be further classified by subtype based on age of diagnosis: (1) neonatal onset (0–1 month), (2) infantile onset (1–24 months), or (3) late onset (>24 months). Glaucoma following cataract surgery was defined as pediatric glaucoma that was diagnosed after cataract surgery. Sturge-Weber syndrome–associated glaucoma was defined as pediatric glaucoma diagnosed in a child with SWS.

Imaging and Image Analysis

All measurements were obtained while participants were supine under general anesthesia prior to examination under anesthesia or planned surgical procedure. An eyelid speculum was used, and cotton-tipped applicators were used for globe positioning as needed. Intraocular pressure was obtained in each eye using a Tono-Pen handheld tonometer (Reichert Technologies). Corneal diameter was measured using calipers and a ruler. Only horizontal CD was measured, as normative data and diagnostic thresholds are typically based on horizontal CD or white-to-white distance. In addition, our prior study demonstrated horizontal measurements to have greater reproducibility than vertical measurements.5

Immersion A-scan biometry was performed next using the Aviso Ultrasound Platform A/B/UBM with a 10-MHz linear transducer (Quantel Medical) or Ellex ultrasound. A-scan biometry was performed by an experienced operator, a board-certified pediatric ophthalmologist, on each eye. A scleral immersion shell was used to support the probe, and normal saline was used as the coupling fluid. The readings were recorded when the operator observed a satisfactory scan, defined as having well-defined echoes corresponding to the cornea, the retina, and the anterior and posterior poles of the lens in phakic or pseudophakic patients. An automated sequence of 10 readings was taken, with 1 application of the shell and probe per eye. Immersion A-scan AL measurements were based on the average of 10 consecutive automatic values.

Ultrasound biomicroscopy imaging was performed by the same operator using the Aviso Ultrasound Platform A/B UBM with a 50-MHz linear transducer (Quantel Medical) or Ellex ultrasound. For subjects enrolled at the University of Maryland Medical Center, the transducer was covered with a single-use ClearScan UBM probe cover filled with deionized water. Hypromellose ophthalmic solution (2.5%) was applied to the ocular surface as lubrication. For subjects enrolled at Washington University, imaging was performed without probe cover, using a scleral shell filled with balanced salt solution. For all images, the operator collected ≥1 horizontal axial ultrasound image per eye, concurrent with predetermined landmarks and probe positions from an established protocol.11

The manual image analysis protocol uses ImageJ 1.48v (National Institutes of Health), a Java-based open access image processing program. Reliability and repeatability analysis of the current imaging and analysis protocol has been previously published.11 Images were deidentified and reviewed for eligibility. Ultrasound images were selected based on centration of the pupil, ability to identify the angles, and overall quality. Ultrasound biomicroscopy images that were not optimally aligned were excluded. Images were considered optimally aligned when both iridocorneal angles were clearly visualized, and both the reflections of anterior and posterior poles of the lens were visible and on the same line perpendicular to the lens surface, for phakic and pseudophakic patients. Angle-angle diameter was defined as the diameter between the 2 iridocorneal angles’ opposing recesses (Fig 1). Two independent trained observers (T.K. and H.F.) measured AA in each UBM image, and the average measurements were used for analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Pearson correlation and multivariate regression were performed using R, version 3.5.3 (R Core Team). Subgroup analysis was performed by age group, glaucoma subtype, and IOP control. Interobserver agreement for AA measurements was calculated using 2-way mixed effects interclass correlation coefficients.

Results

Twenty-three UBM images (1 image per eye) from 12 PCG participants, 6 UBM images from 3 GFCS participants, and 5 UBM images from 4 SWS participants were analyzed to obtain horizontal AA for each eye. The SWS group had the largest CD (median = 12.84 mm), largest AA (median = 13.02 mm), and highest IOP (median = 24.0 mmHg) compared with the other 2 groups. The GFCS group had the longest AL (median = 26.24 mm). There were no significant differences in CD, AA, IOP, or AL measurements among the subgroups. Interobserver agreement for AA measurements was excellent (interclass correlation coefficient = 0.92; 95% confidence interval: 0.86–0.96). Descriptive statistics for all parameters are in Table 2.

In age-adjusted models, each 1-mm increase in AA was associated with a 0.55-mm increase in AL and a 1.49-mm increase in IOP, although these associations were not statistically significant (P = 0.10 and 0.18, respectively). In contrast, when restricting analysis to PCG eyes and adjusting for age, each 1-mm increase in AA was associated with a significant 1.63-mm increase in AL (P = 0.002). No additional significant associations with IOP or within other glaucoma subtypes or age groups were identified in multivariate regression.

Discussion

We conducted a prospective, cross-sectional observational study to investigate the relationships among AL, IOP, and AA, a parameter previously shown to correlate strongly with CD.5,12 Because both CD and AL can be important indicators of pediatric glaucoma progression,8,13 we aimed to assess whether AA, measured via UBM image analysis, could serve as an additional internal metric. Angle-angle diameter could be particularly useful for approximating CD in eyes with limbal physiology that complicates external CD measurement. Developing a formula to characterize the relationship between AA and AL, and potentially IOP, could further enhance its utility by enabling clinicians to estimate globe enlargement or pressure-related changes from AA measurements. This approach may complement traditional external measurements of CD obtained with calipers and a ruler or of AL obtained with immersion A-scan biometry, potentially improving monitoring and assessment of pediatric glaucoma.

Increased CD is often one of the initial signs of elevated IOP in infants and children and can serve as an important marker in diagnosing and monitoring pediatric glaucoma.14 This is due to the increased scleral and corneal elasticity of the pediatric eye, which allows for globe enlargement in response to increased IOP, a phenomenon not seen in adults, where ocular tissues are more rigid.1, 2, 3 As a result, structural parameters that reflect this distensibility, such as AL and CD, often correspond with pediatric glaucoma progression and severity.8,13

Across the full cohort, there was a strong positive correlation between AA and CD, which is consistent with previously published work5,12 and further indicates the close relationship between the 2 parameters. This relationship is anatomically intuitive, as AA represents the internal diameter corresponding to CD. Corneal diameter would be expected to equal the AA diameter plus the thickness of the sclera, cornea, and potentially the trabecular meshwork and Schlemm’s canal. If these layers are relatively consistent in thickness, a strong correlation between AA and CD would be expected.

Although AA is strongly correlated with CD, which is already widely used, AA may serve as a valuable alternative metric in situations where external CD cannot be measured reliably. Specifically, this could pertain to eyes with significant corneal opacity or limbal physiology that can complicate CD measurement, such as aniridia-associated keratopathy, Peters anomaly, and sclerocornea, anterior staphyloma, or congenital hereditary endothelial dystrophy. In these cases, visualization of limbal landmarks may be limited, reducing the accuracy of CD measurements. Therefore, AA obtained by measuring internal anatomical landmarks from UBM images may provide a more reliable and objective assessment in eyes with limbal physiology.

The strong positive correlation between AA and CD was present in most age groups and glaucoma subtypes with the exception of SWS, where smaller sample size and distinct pathophysiology might limit interpretability.15 In SWS, elevated episcleral venous pressure and diffuse ocular vascular malformations could contribute to globe enlargement through mechanisms that differ from those seen in PCG or GFCS. For example, altered scleral biomechanics and venous congestion may play a role, potentially resulting in nonuniform patterns of ocular enlargement and disproportionate changes in external dimensions relative to internal anterior chamber measurements. However, these interpretations remain speculative, as additional structural parameters (e.g., anterior chamber depth, angle opening distance, or scleral/corneal thickness) were not systematically assessed in the present study. Additionally, anterior chamber anatomical abnormalities and variations in corneal edema may further contribute to measurement variability and a weaker observed correlation between AA and CD in this subgroup.

Notably, the SWS subgroup had the largest CD and AA and the highest IOP. This group was also the smallest, comprising 4 participants (5 eyes), limiting the ability to draw generalized conclusions. While these preliminary observations suggest potential differences in ocular enlargement patterns in SWS, further investigation in a larger cohort is needed. In particular, future studies incorporating additional structural parameters, such as anterior chamber depth, angle opening distance, or scleral/corneal thickness, could help clarify whether the relationship between AA and CD reflects distinct biomechanical or developmental mechanisms in SWS compared with other pediatric glaucoma subtypes. Angle-angle diameter was strongly positively correlated with AL in participants with PCG and GFCS, and among infants aged <1 year. These relationships parallel known associations between AL and corneal enlargement in early childhood glaucoma8 and support the concept that AA increases, at least in part, as a manifestation of overall globe expansion. The similar patterns observed between CD and AL reinforce this interpretation.

No significant associations were observed between IOP and either AA or CD. This is consistent with the recognized limitations of single IOP measurements16 given that IOP fluctuates substantially, is influenced by examination conditions, and may not accurately reflect cumulative pressure exposure and globe size,17,18 particularly in eyes with longstanding disease.19

Multivariate age-adjusted analysis revealed trends toward increased AL and IOP with increasing AA, although these relationships did not reach statistical significance in the full cohort. This may reflect heterogeneity in age groups, diagnosis, and baseline ocular biomechanics, which could dilute associations present in more uniform disease groups. For instance, there was a significant association between AA and AL in the PCG subgroup, suggesting that AA might be particularly informative in classic congenital glaucoma, where elevated IOP leads to biomechanical stress and structural remodeling.20,21

Limitations of our study include the relatively small sample size and lower representation in glaucoma subgroups other than PCG, such as GFCS and SWS, which may limit the generalizability of subgroup analyses. Additionally, although this study frames AA as a clinically useful parameter in cases where CD cannot be reliably measured, such as sclerocornea, aniridia, or Peters anomaly, our cohort did not directly represent this intended clinical use case. Furthermore, our cross-sectional design precludes assessment of longitudinal changes in AA over time. Future research should prioritize longitudinal studies that track AA changes in pediatric glaucoma to clarify how this parameter correlates with disease progression and severity across different ages and clinical subgroups. Such studies may also help define how AA relates to AL and CD, particularly in the context of medical and surgical interventions. Investigating the relationship between AA and other anterior segment properties, such as corneal hysteresis and scleral rigidity, may provide deeper insights into the biomechanical properties of glaucomatous eyes in pediatric populations. Further validation of AA as a clinical tool could enhance its utility in surgical planning, particularly in determining the optimal timing and type of intervention for patients with progressive disease.

Conclusions

This study provides preliminary evidence that AA correlates with established markers of disease severity and progression, CD and AL, in pediatric glaucomatous eyes. These findings support the potential utility of AA as an additional internal measure of globe enlargement in pediatric glaucoma, particularly in PCG. Given the challenges of obtaining high-quality, reliable measurements in young children, the ability to extract meaningful structural data from UBM images obtained during examination under anesthesia could aid in disease monitoring. Longitudinal multicenter studies are needed to validate these associations and to determine the broader clinical applicability of AA.
