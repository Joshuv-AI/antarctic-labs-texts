Abstract
. Digital transformation in the cultural heritage sector has produced vast yet fragmented collections of artefact data. Existing frameworks for museum information systems struggle to integrate heterogeneous metadata, unstructured documents, and multimodal artefacts into a coherent and queryable form. We present MuseKG, an end-to-end knowledge-graph framework that unifies structured and unstructured museum data through symbolic–neural integration. MuseKG constructs a typed property graph linking objects, people, organisations, and visual or textual labels, and supports natural language queries. Evaluations on real museum collections demonstrate robust performance across queries over attributes, relations, and related entities, surpassing large-language-model zero-shot, few-shot and SPARQL prompt baselines. The results highlight the importance of symbolic grounding for interpretable and scalable cultural heritage reasoning, and pave the way for web-scale integration of digital heritage knowledge.



1.
Introduction

Digitisation efforts across the cultural heritage sector have created vast but fragmented repositories of artefact information, spanning text-based catalogues, image archives, and multimedia documentation. Yet, despite the abundance of data, museum collections remain difficult to search, connect, and interpret holistically. The challenge lies not merely in data volume, but in semantic disconnection; object descriptions, relationships, and contextual metadata often exist in isolation across heterogeneous systems, limiting cross-collection discovery and interpretive reasoning.

Recent advances at the intersection of Knowledge Graphs (KGs)
(
10
)
, and Large Language Models (LLMs)
(
1

11

offer a new pathway toward semantically enriched museum infrastructures. For example, the Metadata Enrichment Model
(
7
)
integrates fine-tuned neural networks with semantic KGs to detect and represent visual features within digitised manuscripts, enabling dynamic ontology expansion and richer metadata. Similarly, KG-driven cultural heritage frameworks
(
6
)
automate entity-relation extraction and knowledge completion to improve reasoning and interoperability across museum information systems. Together, these works illustrate a broader paradigm shift toward intelligent, multimodal, and semantically integrated cultural heritage management.

However, these approaches fall short of providing an end-to-end system that bridges data collection, KG construction, and user-facing natural language interaction. Current frameworks typically rely on static ontologies or manual curation, which limits their scalability and the ability to handle unstructured or multi-modal inputs, such as object images, production notes, or historical documents. Moreover, retrieval pipelines in museum contexts remain narrowly defined and are often focused on keyword or metadata matching rather than reasoning over interconnected relationships.

In this paper, we present

MuseKG

To evaluate the system, we construct a benchmark using both KG-grounded and LLM-guided question–answer generation, rather than relying on pre-defined queries or manual annotations. Our contributions are threefold:

(1) We propose a system
MuseKG
that enables KG construction and natural language-based queries over museum collections.

(2) At the technical level, we propose a unified construction pipeline for transforming museum collection records into a semantically normalised, extensible KG, and a natural-language query processor that maps questions to executable graph operations for attribute lookup, relation retrieval, and one-hop reasoning.

(3) We conduct an empirical study that demonstrates
MuseKG
’s consistent accuracy and advantage over baseline models, validating the benefits of symbolic grounding in cultural-heritage reasoning.

KG Constructor.

Our KG constructor maps source data records to graph nodes and edges through the following steps:

(1)
Normalisation:
Textual fields are lower cased, whitespace collapsed, and punctuation/quotes stripped to stabilise matching during entity resolution and querying.

(2)
Entity Identification and Node Creation:
For each distinct entity identified from the source data, a corresponding node is created in the KG. An “entity identifier” in
MuseKG
refers to the unique value used to represent a distinct entity as a node.
Nodes are created for the following entities: (i) each
opacObjectId
from the main object records; (ii) entities referenced in
relationshipsCollection
(e.g., persons, organisations, or related objects), using their provided Id (

relatedRecordId
) as identifiers; (iii) imageId extracted from
imagesCollection
; and (iv) named entities (e.g., places, persons, concepts) detected and linked from textual fields (e.g., descriptions).

(3)
Node Type Assignment:
The node type mapping
\tau
assigns a specific type to each created node:

object
for nodes created from
opacObjectId
;

person
or
organisation
for nodes constructed from objects in the
relationshipsCollection
, as indicated by their
relatedRecordType
field;

ImageLabel
for nodes derived from image labels;

Place
,
Person
, or
Concept
for nodes created from entities linked from textual fields.

(4)
Relation Labelling and Edge Creation:
Relationship identifiers (e.g.,
object_prod_pri_person
) are mapped to a fixed vocabulary
\mathcal{R}
(for simplification). Edges are then created between the corresponding nodes based on these normalised relation labels.

(5)
Deduplication:
Nodes are initially created for each unique entity identifier. Duplicate nodes, identified by canonical titles or accession numbers, are then merged, retaining the first-seen attribute payload. Duplicate edges are detected and removed based on their source-relation-target triples.

(6)
Schema Validation:
Extracted attributes for each node are restricted to a predefined schema
\mathcal{K}
. Other values are dropped.

Natural Language Query Interface

Our
MuseKG
query interface answers NL queries by Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
(
9
)
over the KG in two main stages:

Context Retrieval from KG.

Upon receiving a query,
MuseKG
first identifies key entities in the question. An LLM is employed to extract key entities (e.g., an object title) from the NL question. For each identified entity,
MuseKG
then retrieves a textual context from the KG. This context is formed by all attributes associated with the entity’s node and its relations to one-hop neighbours. This step effectively grounds the query within the rich, interconnected data of the KG, providing relevant factual evidence. Note that
MuseKG
can extend to multi-hops to answer more complex queries.

For example, given a query “What are the measurements for the Long Scale Galvanometer?",
MuseKG
first identifies ‘Long Scale Galvanometer’ as the key entity. It then retrieves its attributes (including ‘measurements’) and any directly connected entities. This collected information forms the context:

3.
Experiments and Discussion

This section documents an empirical evaluation of
MuseKG
. Here, we describe the experimental setup, detail the datasets, outline the evaluation protocol, describe implementation details, and finally report results with discussion.

Evaluation Dataset.

We construct a 150-question benchmark (50 per category) from the source museum JSON records. For each sampled object, we call
gemini-2.5-flash-lite

(
3
)
with a category-specific prompt that shows the raw JSON and asks for a single question-answer pair plus a structured KG query description in JSON.
Category 1 prompts require attribute-lookup questions (e.g., retrieving an object’s measurements).
Category 2 prompts require relation-neighbour questions and use
find_related
with an explicit relationship from the KG schema.
Category 3 prompts require harder questions that first establish a relationship and then retrieve an attribute of the related node (e.g., the birth date of a producer or the accession number of a related object). We parse the model output as JSON, discard malformed responses, and keep the remaining question-answer pairs together with their
query_details
, which can be compiled directly into executable KG queries.

Baseline Methods.

We compare three baselines: (1)
LLM Zero-shot

(
8
)
: the model directly answers the question without any KG interaction; (2)
LLM Few-shot

(
2
)
: the model is given a small set of QA exemplars and is prompted to answer the question directly in natural language; and (3)
LLM SPARQL-prompt

(
4
)
: the model is instead prompted to output a SPARQL query, which we run on the KG to obtain the answer.

Evaluation Protocol.

We conduct human evaluations to measure answer correctness; LLM-based evaluation could be used in future for scalability.

Implementation Details.

We use Python for scripting and data processing; KG operations run on NetworkX
(
5
)
. QA pairs are produced by gemini-2.5-flash-lite
(
3
)
that verbalises queries, expected answers, and structured query parameters from object metadata with few-shot prompts. For baselines, we run open-source models (google/gemma-3-4b-it, google/gemma-3-12b-it
(
11
)
, and GPT-OSS-20B
(
1
)
). These serve as the model in the baselines and
MuseKG
for answer generation. All experiments are executed on a single H100 GPU for local inference.

Error Analysis.

Analysis of evaluation logs reveals error patterns across the baseline methods. The
LLM Few-shot
method exhibited significant challenges, primarily failing to convert NL questions into structured queries, often resulting in “None” or “Failed to parse question” answers. Similarly, the
LLM Zero-shot
method showed very low accuracy, frequently returning factually incorrect or hallucinated information, or generating answers that were too general, incomplete, or overly verbose, indicating its struggle without direct KG interaction. In contrast,
MuseKG
and
LLM SPARQL-prompt
demonstrated considerably higher accuracy, with their errors typically stemming from minor inaccuracies in retrieved data (e.g., “Circa 1900” instead of “Circa 1920”), incorrect specific data points (e.g., wrong accession numbers), or, in the case of LLM SPARQL-prompt, generated query failing to extract specific attributes, leading to empty answers. These suggest that while RAG and generation approaches are more robust, they still face challenges in precision and comprehensive data retrieval for all query types.

Computational Efficiency.

We report end-to-end wall-clock latency from question to final answer. With Gemma-12B, MuseKG answers in about 0.36 s per query, essentially matching the simple LLM Few-shot pipeline (0.37 s). In contrast, LLM SPARQL-prompt is slower at 3.38 s, and LLM Zero-shot is slowest at 8.96 s (25× slower than MuseKG), because the Zero-shot setting generates reasoning steps before answering, especially when there are no relevant contexts.
These results show that MuseKG is efficient, as it confines expensive LLM computation to a lightweight synthesis step over a pre-filtered KG context.

4.
Conclusion

We introduced
MuseKG
, an end-to-end pipeline for constructing a museum knowledge graph and answering queries.
MuseKG
achieves strong accuracy for attribute lookup, relation retrieval, and one-hop reasoning tasks, outperforming baselines. Our current benchmark covers 150 questions over a single institution’s collections; future work includes scaling to larger and more diverse museum partners and supporting deeper multi-hop queries.
