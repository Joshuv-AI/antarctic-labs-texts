Machine Learning, scRNA-Seq and Biological Experiments Identify Hub Genes Responsible for Ischemia-Reperfusion Injury in Steatotic Liver Allografts
Authors: Kai Lu, Yi-Ming Zhao, Kui-Yuan Chai, Yi Lyu, Xu-Feng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLiver transplantation is the standard treatment for end-stage liver disease, but donor organ shortage has increased the use of marginal donor livers (eg, steatotic grafts). These livers are highly sensitive to liver ischemia/reperfusion injury (LIRI), worsening transplant outcomes, with unclear molecular mechanisms limiting targeted therapies.MethodsWe adopted an integrative bioinformatics approach combining transcriptomic analysis of public datasets (GSE151648, GSE135251, GSE15480, GSE23649 and GSE193764), machine learning (LASSO, SVM-RFE), and scRNA-Seq analysis (GSE171539, CRA004061) to identify hub genes and pathways. Key findings were validated in vitro (AML12 hepatocytes under hypoxia/reoxygenation) and in vivo (murine model of steatotic liver under I/R).ResultsWeighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) identified LIRI-, NAFLD-, and NASH-associated modules enriched in immune/inflammatory pathways. Machine learning pinpointed distinct hub genes for NAFLD-related LIRI (JUN, CCL2) and NASH-related LIRI (PHLDA1, PNRC1, GADD45B, NFKBIA, JUND; all AUC > 0.7). CCL2 emerged as the top predictor via SHAP analysis. scRNA-seq revealed cell-type-specific expression patterns and distinct immune infiltration signatures between NAFLD and NASH cohorts, with remodeled cell-cell communication via CCL/CXCL pathways. In vitro/in vivo models confirmed the upregulation of key genes under LIRI, and CCR2 inhibition significantly attenuated liver injury, pathological damage, and pro-inflammatory cytokine (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6) expression.ConclusionOur study identifies distinct molecular signatures and hub genes for LIRI in NAFLD versus NASH allografts, revealing differential immune landscapes between these two conditions. The findings further support the CCL2-CCR2 axis as a therapeutically targetable pathway driving LIRI in steatotic livers and provide potential biomarkers to improve marginal liver transplantation outcomes.

Introduction

Liver transplantation is an effective treatment method for individuals with end-stage liver disease. Nonetheless, there remains a gap between the “demand” for transplantation and the “supply” of available organs. Numerous patients have lost their lives during the waiting period, primarily due to the inability to allocate a donated liver.1 Consequently, this situation has prompted a growing trend in the utilization of various marginal donor livers, including steatosis liver, older donors’ liver and livers infected with hepatitis viruses.2,3

In recent years, alterations in dietary patterns have led to a persistent increase in the prevalence of Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease (NAFLD).4 As a result, the potential donors exhibiting steatosis has also risen, rendering steatosis liver as an unavoidable option in transplantation considerations. NAFLD is characterized by a liver fat content that surpasses 5% of the liver’s wet weight, whereas a liver with fatty degeneration exceeding 30% is classified as marginal donor liver.5

Ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) refers to the tissue damage that arises when transplanted organs undergo a period of insufficient blood flow (ischemia) followed by the restoration of blood flow and oxygen supply (reperfusion).6 The underlying mechanisms of IRI are intricate and encompass various biological processes, such as cellular damage, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses.7 Fatty degeneration in donor livers is particularly vulnerable to liver ischemia-reperfusion injury (LIRI), which significantly impairs liver function and potentially lead to the early graft dysfunction or primary graft failure.8 Steatotic livers exhibit heightened susceptibility to IRI due to several interrelated metabolic vulnerabilities, including impaired mitochondrial function and ATP depletion, increased oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation driven by elevated reactive oxygen species production, enhanced endoplasmic reticulum stress, and sinusoidal microcirculatory dysfunction that exacerbates the “no-reflow” phenomenon.9–11 Furthermore, while NAFLD and NASH share a common pathogenic basis, they represent distinct clinical and histopathological entities—NASH is characterized by inflammatory infiltrates and hepatocellular injury, whereas simple steatosis is considered a more benign state—with NASH exhibiting a distinct hepatic immune-related gene signature and altered immune cell populations compared to simple steatosis.12,13

The heightened vulnerability of steatotic livers to IRI can be explained by a “two-hit” model specific to transplant settings: (1) Hepatic steatosis (the “first hit”) disrupts liver homeostasis via lipid accumulation, chronic low-grade inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress, priming the graft for subsequent injury; (2) Ischemia during organ preservation and reperfusion after transplantation (the “second hit”) amplifies these pre-existing impairments, triggering massive hepatocyte apoptosis, necroptosis, and excessive inflammatory responses. We hypothesize that this stepwise injury is driven by a subset of genes dysregulated in steatosis and further amplified during IRI14–16 - these genes represent key mediators of IRI in steatotic allografts, and their identification is critical for addressing unmet clinical needs in liver transplantation.

However, the exact mechanisms governing the development of LIRI in steatosis liver remain inadequately elucidated, necessitating additional research to pinpoint critical molecules and pathways implicated in disease progression. Therefore, in this study, we utilized bioinformatics, machine learning and biological experiments to analyze hub genes associated with susceptibility to IRI in NAFLD and NASH donor liver, aiming at informing the development of clinical molecular targets and exploring potential therapeutic strategies.

Materials and Methods

Data Collection and Analyzation

The mRNA expression profiles of GSE151648, GSE135251, GSE15480, GSE23649 and GSE193764 were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) database. Specifically, GSE151648 contains 40 pre-transplant (representing ischemic specimen) and 40 post-transplant (representing reperfused specimen) human liver samples. GSE135251 included liver tissue samples from 10 healthy controls, 51 patients with NAFLD and 155 patients with NASH. The GSE15480 dataset comprises 12 liver tissues from pre-ischemic donors and 12 liver tissues from reperfused allograts. In the GSE23649 dataset, samples were categorized into two groups: pre-transplant (n = 33) and post-transplant (n = 33). Additionally, the GSE193764 dataset includes 12 samples collected before liver transplantation and 12 samples collected after transplantation from rats with liver macrosteatosis. The Details of the Datasets

Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA)

To identify the disease-related key genes in LIRI, NAFLD and NASH, we conduct a WGCNA analysis of GSE151648 and GSE135251. Based on selecting all genes, we converted the correlation matrix to the adjacency matrix, and the maximum R2 was conducted using the soft threshold of an unweighted network. Then, the coefficients between the different modules and the phenotypes were calculated. For LIRI, the turquoise module was identified as the key module. The black, red, green and grey modules were selected as key modules in NAFLD, as well as the red and yellow modules were identified in NASH patients.

Analysis of Differentially Expressed Genes

All the gene sets were normalized with the “limma” package before identifying differentially expressed genes. And the DEGs were selected by p-value < 0.05 and |logFC|≥1. Venn diagrams were used to identify the key genes in LIRI with NAFLD or NASH by the “VennDiagram” package. The location of these genes on chromosomes was visualized through the “RCircos” R package. And the Spearman correlation analysis of these genes was visualized by the “corrplot” and “circlize” R package.

Functional Enrichment Analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional enrichment analysis were conducted by the “clusterProfiler” R package to explore biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), cellular components (CC), and gene-related signaling pathways.

Screening Hub Genes by Machine Learning

To filter out the significant genes in LIRI with NAFLD or NASH, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) logistic regression analysis and Support Vector Machine-Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE) analysis were performed to identify the optimal core genes. Subsequently, 2 hub genes in LIRI with NAFLD and 5 genes in LIRI with NASH were selected. The diagnostic capacity of the hub genes was identified by ROC curves in GSE151648 and 2 external datasets, GSE15480 and GSE23649.

Immune Infiltration Analysis

CIBERSORT was used to explore the discrepancy in immune cells between different phenotypes of patients in GSE151648 and GSE135251. Moreover, the identified hub genes were explored with the association with different immune cells scores.

GSEA Analysis

GSEA (Gene Set Enrichment Analysis) is a computational method used to interpret gene expression data by focusing on gene sets rather than individual genes. This approach involves selecting one or more predefined functional gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb). By analyzing the correlation between gene expression profiles and specific phenotypes, GSEA assesses the collective impact of gene set alterations on phenotypic changes. We performed a single-gene GSEA analysis to investigate the possible roles of hub genes in LIRI with NAFLD or NASH.

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) Analysis

The dataset GSE151648 was split into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets using stratified sampling (“createDataPartition” from the caret package). Multiple supervised learning models were trained, including random forest, support vector machine (SVM), xgboost, k-nearest neighbors (KNN), and gradient boosting machines. Models were validated using 5-fold repeated cross-validation (trainControl). Performance was evaluated via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC) using the “pROC” package, with the optimal model selected based on the highest AUC. For the optimal model, SHAP values were computed to quantify feature contributions to predictions. Permshap (from kernelshap) was used for SHAP calculation, with kernelshap employed for datasets with larger gene sets. SHAP-based feature importance was derived from mean absolute SHAP values.

Single Cell Analysis in GSE171539

The single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset GSE171539 from patients with liver ischemia-reperfusion was obtained from the GEO database and analyzed by the “Seurat” package. The number of detected genes and the percentage of mitochondrial genes were calculated to filter low-quality cells. After quality control, scRNA-seq data were normalized using the “NormalizeData” function, and highly variable genes were identified via the “FindVariableFeatures” function. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the “RunPCA” function, followed by dimensionality reduction via t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) using the “RunTSNE” function. Clustering analysis was conducted with the “FindNeighbors” and “FindClusters” functions at a resolution of 0.5. For cell type annotation, the human primary cell atlas reference dataset was obtained from the “celldex” package. Cell type identities were visualized using the “TSNEPlot” function. Differential gene expression analysis was performed with the “FindAllMarkers” function to identify marker genes for each cell type. Finally, the expression levels of core genes across different cell types were further analyzed.

Single Cell Analysis in CRA004061

The scRNA-seq data of fatty liver graft injury after liver transplantation was downloaded from China National Center for Bioinformation/Beijing Institute of Genomics, Chinese Academy of Sciences, under accession number CRA004061 (publicly accessible at https://bigd.big.ac.cn/gsa).

The whole analysis was conducted in R using the “Seurat” package, supplemented with Harmony (batch correction) and clustree (clustering evaluation). Data were normalized via the LogNormalize method (scale factor = 10,000), followed by identification of 2000 highly variable features (HVFs) using the “vst” method. All genes were scaled, and PCA was performed on HVFs. Batch effects were corrected with Harmony, grouped by the “Type” metadata. UMAP and tSNE were used for nonlinear dimensionality reduction and visualization. Upregulated cluster-specific markers were identified using “FindAllMarkers”. Cell types were manually annotated with known cell type markers from the previous study.17 Cell-cell communication was analyzed using the “CellChat” package. Data preprocessing included identifying over-expressed genes and ligand-receptor pairs, with projection onto a PPI network. Communication probabilities were computed and filtered (≥5 cells per group). Pathway-level communication was aggregated. Pseudotime analysis was performed using “Monocle” package.

Construction of the Liver Ischemia Reperfusion Injury Model

AML12 mouse hepatocytes were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (CellMax, CGM104.05) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, VivaCell, C04001) and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin solution (VivaCell, C3421-0100). For lipid accumulation, cells were treated with 0.25 mM palmitic acid (PA, Sigma-Aldrich, P0500) and 0.5 mM (OA, Sigma-Aldrich, O1383) for 24 h, followed by induction of hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/I/R) via exposure to hypoxia (1% O2, 5% CO2, 94% N2) for 6 h and re-oxygenation (1% O2, 5% CO2) for 1 h. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release in cell supernatants was measured using an LDH cytotoxicity assay kit (Beyotime, C0016).

For in vivo hepatic ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) model, male C57BL/6 mice (8–10 weeks old) were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility at the Animal Center of Xi’an Jiaotong University under controlled environmental conditions (temperature 22 ± 2°C, relative humidity 50±10%, 12h light/dark cycle with lights and had free access to food and water). To induce hepatic steatosis, the mice were fed a choline-deficient, high fat diet (CDHF, Dyets, D200420) for 4 weeks. For the hepatic ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) model, partial hepatic ischemia was induced by clamping the vessels to the left and middle lobes with a microvascular clip for 60 min, after which the clip was released to allow 12-h reperfusion. Sham controls received identical laparotomy but no vascular occlusion. To assess the potential effect of CCR2 inhibition, the CCR2 inhibitor (MedChemExpress, HY-103362) was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 30mg/kg 30min prior to the ischaemic insult; control animals received an equal volume of vehicle. At 12 h after reperfusion, the mice were deeply anaesthetised with isoflurane and euthanised by exsanguination via the abdominal aorta. Liver tissues and serum were collected for subsequent analyses. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Health Science Center, Xi’an Jiaotong University (approval No. XJTUAE20253758) and were carried out in strict accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Total RNA was extracted from cells/tissues via TRIzol (Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan), AG21102, ChangSha, China), reverse-transcribed to cDNA with 5X Evo M-MLV RT Reaction Mix Ver.2 (Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan), AG11728, ChangSha, China), and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed with SYBR Green Pro Taq HS (Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan), AG11701, ChangSha, China), with expression normalized to Actb. Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses in the present study were implemented using R software version 4.3.2. For in vitro experiments, 4 independent biological replicates were performed, each with 3 technical replicates; data are presented as mean ± SEM. For in vivo experiments, n=6 mice per group were used at least. P values were determined by an independent sample t-test or the Mann–Whitney U-test, as appropriate, and P < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Identification of Key Gene Modules in LIRI by WGCNA

The GSE151648 dataset, comprising 80 samples, was subjected to Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA). Scale independence and mean connectivity analyses (Figure 1A and B) indicated that a soft threshold of 6 yielded a network conforming to scale-free topology. Using this threshold, we constructed a co-expression network, identified gene modules, and generated a clustering dendrogram. The turquoise module showed a strong positive correlation with the trait of LIRI (MEturquoise, r = 0.86, P = 2e-22; Figure 1C), and module membership was significantly correlated with gene significance within this module (r = 0.96, P < 1e-200; Figure 1D). Functional enrichment analysis indicated that genes in the turquoise module were primarily associated with immune-related processes, including chemotaxis, leukocyte migration, and response to chemokine (Figure 1E). KEGG pathway analysis further highlighted key signaling pathways such as cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, PI3K-Akt signaling, and MAPK signaling (Figure 1F). Gene Enrichment Analysis of Hub Genes in LIRI with NAFLD

GSEA of Hub Genes in LIRI and Their Correlation Analysis in NAFLD/NASH

According to the above findings, the genes associated with NAFLD liver in LIRI were JUN and CCL2. The CCL2 high expression group was highly enriched in the pentose phosphate pathway (Figure S5A). Meanwhile, the JUN high expression group was highly enriched for folate biosynthesis and focal adhesion (Figure S5B), which is acknowledged as an intracellular glucose metabolic pathway primarily responsible for producing NADPH, a crucial component for various cellular metabolic processes.

The genes related to NASH liver in LIRI included PHLDA1, PNRC1, GADD45B, NFKBIA, and JUND. As illustrated in Figure S5C–G, the group exhibiting high expression of GADD45B was predominantly involved in the regulation of autophagy (Figure S5C). The group characterized by high expression of JUND showed a strong association with both the VEGF signaling pathway and the B cell receptor signaling pathway (Figure S5D). Additionally, the NFKBIA high expression group was significantly enriched in the Notch signaling pathway (Figure S5E), while PHLDA1 chiefly involved in the regulation of autophagy associated with the biosynthesis of valine, leucine, and isoleucine (which pertains to the synthesis of non-essential amino acids) as well as the regulation of autophagy (Figure S5F). To further analysis of the hub genes with steatotic degree, we have demonstrated the correlation between their expression and NAS scores in NAFLD/NASH. Single-Cell Trajectory and Functional Analyses Uncover Dynamic Cellular Responses to Hepatic I/R Injury

To delineate dynamic cellular states and functional alterations during I/R injury in steatotic livers, we performed trajectory inference, pseudotime analysis, and functional enrichment. Diffusion map-based trajectory analysis revealed lineage relationships among liver cell types and coordinated state transitions following I/R (Figure 8E). Pseudotime analysis for each cell type further captured their progression along inferred trajectories, illustrating temporal shifts in cell states post-injury (Figure 8F). Key genes exhibited cell-type-specific expression dynamics along pseudotime (Figure 8G): Ccl2 was upregulated in myeloid cells; Gadd45b peaked at mid-pseudotime in hepatocytes; Jun was sustained across multiple lineages; Phlda1 increased in stromal cells; and Pnrc1 showed late-phase upregulation in hepatocytes.

A heatmap of cell type-specific gene enrichment across pseudotime stages illustrated the activation of distinct transcriptional programs in neutrophils, macrophages, hepatocytes, and other lineages over time (Figure 8H). UMAP visualization further highlighted spatial clustering of cells based on key gene expression, indicating segregated dynamic states within the liver cell population (Figure S6E and F). Finally, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of DEGs between high- and low-risk groups highlighted processes such as response to external stimulus, myeloid cell-mediated immunity, and hypoxia response, underscoring the activation of inflammatory and stress-responsive pathways during I/R in steatotic livers (Figure S6G and H). Furthermore, violin plots (Figure S6I) confirmed these cell-type-specific expression patterns.

Together, these results uncover dynamic cellular state transitions and the coordinated activation of pro-inflammatory and stress-response programs during I/R injury in steatotic liver.

CCR2 Inhibition Alleviated Hepatic I/R Injury in Steatotic Mice

Given previous analysis in Figure 5H, it was confirmed that CCL2 had the highest mean absolute SHAP values among candidate genes. Specifically, CCL2 (also known as MCP-1) is a key chemokine that exerts its biological effects by binding to the receptor CCR2. The CCL2-CCR2 axis orchestrates the recruitment and activation of immune cells (eg, monocytes, macrophages), thereby exacerbating inflammation and hepatocellular damage in hepatic I/R. Thus, CCR2 inhibitors represent a potential strategy to target the CCL2-CCR2 axis and alleviate hepatic I/R injury in seatotic livers.

As illustrated in the experimental schematic (Figure 10A), steatotic mice receiving a CCR2 inhibitor prior to I/R exhibited attenuated liver damage in histological analysis and immune infiltration (decreased F4/80+CCR2+ cells) (Figure 10B and C) compared with the I/R-only group. Correspondingly, serum ALT and AST levels were significantly reduced in the inhibitor-treated group (Figure 10D). Furthermore, qRT-PCR analysis revealed that hepatic mRNA levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines Il-1b, Tnf-α, and Il-6 were markedly decreased in mice treated with the CCR2 inhibitor compared to those in the I/R control group (Figure 10E), indicating an effective suppression of the inflammatory response. Discussion

Liver transplantation remains the definitive therapeutic intervention for end-stage liver disease, yet the persistent shortage of donor organs has necessitated the use of marginal grafts, including those with steatosis.1,2 Steatotic livers, particularly from donors with NAFLD or NASH, exhibit heightened susceptibility to LIRI, which significantly compromises graft function and survival.18 In this study, we employed an integrative bioinformatics approach combined with machine learning and single-cell transcriptomics to identify and validate key molecular biomarkers and pathways implicated in LIRI within steatotic liver allografts.

Through integrative bioinformatics and machine learning analyses, we identified distinct molecular signatures underlying LIRI in steatotic liver allografts. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) revealed key modules strongly associated with LIRI, NAFLD, and NASH, enriched in immune and inflammatory pathways. By combining differential expression analysis with LASSO and SVM-RFE algorithms, we pinpointed two hub genes (JUN and CCL2) for LIRI in NAFLD livers and five (PHLDA1, PNRC1, GADD45B, NFKBIA, and JUND) for LIRI in NASH livers, all demonstrating high discriminatory capacity in retrospective cohorts (both internal and external datasets). Clinically, per the American Society of Transplant Surgeons (ASTS) Donor Selection Guidelines,19,20 donor livers with steatosis <30% are safe for transplantation, those with 30–60% require cautious evaluation, and those with >60% are not recommended for routine use. Notably, the NAFLD stage correlates with mild steatosis (≤33%), and hub genes (JUN and CCL2) identified from NAFLD livers may serve as molecular markers with low steatosis burden, supporting their suitability for transplantation. In contrast, the five hub genes (PHLDA1, PNRC1, etc) associated with LIRI in NASH livers - where steatosis is more severe and pathological lesions are prominent - prompt extreme caution in donor selection.

Notably, SHAP analysis identified CCL2 as the one of most influential predictors among identified candidate biomarkers. Single-cell RNA sequencing further elucidated cell-type-specific expression patterns of these genes and revealed extensive remodeling of intercellular communication, particularly through CCL and CXCL chemokine pathways. In vitro and in vivo models confirmed the upregulation of key genes under LIRI stress, and therapeutic inhibition of CCR2 (the receptor for CCL) significantly attenuated liver injury and inflammation in steatotic mice.

Steatotic liver disease (including NAFLD and NASH) increases susceptibility to liver LIRI,5 primarily through microcirculatory impairment, enhanced lipid peroxidation in hepatocytes, and mitochondrial dysfunction-induced energy failure.14–16 Beyond these shared mechanisms, NAFLD and NASH differ fundamentally: NAFLD is defined by isolated macrovesicular steatosis,21,22 while NASH involves lobular inflammation, spotty necrosis, and additional stresses such as ER stress and oxidative damage.16–18 Upon LIRI, NASH livers release more pro-inflammatory mediators, amplifying injury and highlighting their greater vulnerability.7,12

Consistent with these distinctions, our enrichment analyses revealed that LIRI in NAFLD mainly involved innate immune cells (eg, macrophages, mast cells), whereas NASH-related LIRI engaged adaptive immune populations (dendritic cells, B cells, T cells). This indicates that the chronic inflammatory microenvironment in NASH primes an adaptive immune response upon LIRI, potentially explaining its heightened susceptibility and poorer transplant outcomes.23,24

Further immune infiltration analysis showed dynamic changes in immune cell composition during LIRI, including decreased resting NK cells, M2 macrophages, and resting mast cells, along with increased M1 macrophages, activated mast cells, activated NK cells, and activated dendritic cells. These shifts align with the pro-inflammatory immune landscape characteristic of LIRI.25,26 However, we acknowledge that the distinction between innate and adaptive immune involvement is primarily derived from correlative immune infiltration analyses and lacks direct functional validation. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as suggestive rather than definitive, and future studies employing cell depletion or neutralizing antibodies are needed to establish causal relationships. Moreover, GSEA indicated that the hub genes were significantly enriched in immune-related and autophagy pathways, supporting their roles as potential prognostic immune markers.27 Given the established role of the gut–liver axis in modulating hepatic inflammation, recent work by Li et al demonstrated that probiotic-derived OMVs (GDO@CM) restore intestinal barrier integrity and modulate inflammation via the APP/CD74 axis, highlighting the therapeutic potential of gut-targeted strategies in inflammatory conditions. Such OMV-based platforms, therefore, merit further investigation in future translational studies for steatotic liver I/R injury.28

Single-cell transcriptomics provided spatial and temporal resolution of hub gene expression across liver cell types. CCL2 was highly expressed in neutrophils and fibroblasts, GADD45B in NK cells, and PHLDA1 in endothelial cells and hepatocytes. Trajectory analysis revealed pseudotime-dependent regulation of these genes, underscoring the dynamic transcriptional reprogramming during LIRI progression. Cell–cell communication analysis highlighted extensive rewiring of intercellular signaling, particularly through CCL and CXCL chemokine pathways, which likely exacerbates inflammatory recruitment and tissue damage.29,30 The scRNA-seq data from GSE171539 reflects IRI in non-steatotic human livers, while CRA004061 is a steatotic rat transplant model. These datasets offer complementary insights into cellular responses but have inherent limitations: human data lacks the steatosis background, and the rat model involves species-specific biology and does not allow for definitive distinction between donor-derived and recipient-infiltrating immune cells.

Among all candidate molecules, CCL2 emerged as a particularly critical mediator.31,32 Our multi-faceted analytical approach consistently highlighted its central role: not only was CCL2 selected as a key diagnostic marker in retrospective cohorts through machine learning, but it also ranked as the most influential predictor in our SHAP analysis. This computational prominence is biologically significant, as CCL2 functions as a master orchestrator of the inflammatory cascade post-LIRI.33,34 The CCL2-CCR2 axis, a well-established pathway for monocyte and macrophage recruitment—was found to be critically amplified in the context of LIRI in steatotic livers.32,35 Recent work by Zuo et al developed a liver-targeted exosomal nanoplatform for H2S delivery that combats hepatic IRI through synergistic anti-oxidative, anti-inflammatory, and anti-ferroptotic effects, offering a comparative perspective that complements our CCR2 inhibition approach.36 Single-cell transcriptomics provided spatial resolution to this process, demonstrating that CCL2 mediates extensive intercellular crosstalk, with heightened signaling originating from hepatocytes and fibroblasts toward immune cells such as neutrophils and T cells in post-reperfusion livers.

The predictive potential and translational potential of targeting CCL2 signaling was substantiated by our experimental validation. Both in vitro and in vivo models confirmed significant upregulation of CCL2 under LIRI conditions, establishing a direct link between its expression and hepatocellular damage. However, we acknowledge that the bioinformatics predictions for GADD45B and NFKBIA were not consistently validated across all experimental settings. Such discrepancies may arise from inherent differences between transcriptional profiling and functional assays. Further mechanistic investigations are warranted to clarify the precise roles of these candidates in steatotic LIRI. Most importantly, therapeutic intervention using a CCR2 inhibitor37 prior to I/R injury in steatotic mice resulted in markedly attenuated liver damage, as evidenced by improved histology, reduced serum ALT/AST levels, and suppressed expression of key pro-inflammatory cytokines (Il-1b, Tnf-α, Il-6). These findings collectively affirmed that the CCL2-CCR2 axis is not merely a bystander but an active and therapeutically targetable driver of LIRI pathology in fatty livers. Our pre-ischemic administration of the CCR2 inhibitor provides initial proof-of-concept that targeting this axis can attenuate IRI in steatotic livers. Determining the therapeutic window, including efficacy of post-reperfusion administration, is a critical next step for clinical translation.

Some limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Our analyses were derived entirely from public datasets without in‑house sequencing validation, largely due to the scarcity of dedicated transcriptomic data for steatotic liver ischemia‑reperfusion injury, which necessitated a co‑analysis strategy intersecting LIRI with NAFLD/NASH to identify shared candidates—an approach that warrants confirmation in future cohort‑specific sequencing studies. Additionally, the CCR2 inhibitor was administered only prior to ischemia, and further investigation of alternative therapeutic time windows will be required to evaluate its full translational potential. Despite these limitations, the convergence of multi‑omic screening, external validation, and experimental evidence supports the robustness of our major conclusions.

In conclusion, our study systematically identifies and validates hub genes and pathways associated with LIRI in steatotic liver allografts. We provide evidence supporting the roles of innate and adaptive immunity in NAFLD- and NASH-related IRI, respectively, and highlight the CCL2-CCR2 axis as a viable therapeutic target. These findings not only enhance our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying LIRI in fatty livers but also offer potential biomarkers and targets for improving the outcomes of marginal liver transplantation.
