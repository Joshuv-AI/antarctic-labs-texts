Quantum simulation of general spin-1/2 Hamiltonians with parity-violating fermionic Gaussian states
Michael Kaicher, Joseph Vovrosh, Alexandre Dauphin, Simon B. Jäger

Abstract
We introduce equations of motion for a parity-violating fermionic mean-field theory (PV-FMFT): a numerically efficient fermionic mean-field theory based on parity-violating fermionic Gaussian states (PV-FGS). This work provides explicit equations of motion for studying the real- and imaginary-time evolution of spin-1/2 Hamiltonians with arbitrary geometries and interactions. We extend previous formulations of parity-preserving fermionic mean-field theory (PP-FMFT) by including fermionic displacement operators in the variational Ansatz. Unlike PP-FMFT, PV-FMFT can be applied to general spin-1/2 Hamiltonians, describe quenches from arbitrary initial spin-1/2 product states, and compute local and non-local observables in a straight-forward manner at the same modest computational cost as PP-FMFT—scaling as 𝒪 ⁡ ( N 3 ) \mathcal{O}(N^{3}) in the worst case for a system of N N spins or fermionic modes. We demonstrate that PV-FMFT can exactly capture the imaginary- and real-time dynamics of non-interacting spin-1/2 Hamiltonians. We then study the post quench-dynamics of the one- and two-dimensional Ising model in presence of longitudinal and transversal fields with PV-FMFT and compute the single site magnetization and correlation functions, and compare them against results from other state-of-the-art numerical approaches. In two-dimensional spin systems, we show that the employed spin-to-fermion mapping can break rotational symmetry within the PV-FMFT description, and we discuss the resulting consequences for the calculated correlation functions. Our work introduces PV-FMFT as a benchmark for other numerical techniques and quantum simulators, and it outlines both its capabilities and its limitations.

Introduction

Understanding basic principles such as ground state and thermal properties, as well as the time evolution of interacting quantum many-body systems lies at the heart of quantum simulation. Spurred by the progress made in controlling and manipulating atomic, molecular and optical systems
13
;
18
;
47

9

37

N

While strongly correlated methods are indispensable in regimes where quantum states obey area- or volume-law entanglement
15
(at least for dimensions
>1
), it is not always clear a priori whether a given system truly requires such methods, or whether a weakly correlated approach suffices. Moreover, what constitutes a “successful” numerical simulation may vary by context: qualitative reproduction of key mechanisms, as in superconductivity
3
, may be adequate in some settings, while quantitative agreement, as in quantum chemistry
22

Tensor-network approaches
11
;
2

56

61

66

\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{log}(D))

14

15

N
. The ultimate aim of numerical methods for quantum simulation is to balance computational tractability with an accurate and comprehensive description of both static and dynamically generated quantum correlations.

Possibly one of the simplest examples of an efficient simulation method is spin mean-field theory (SMFT), which belongs to the class of methods that do not capture any correlations or entanglement, but nonetheless has been successful in describing certain phase transitions and order parameters
68
;
41

Going beyond SMFT to describe quantum correlations, weakly correlated methods based on Gaussian variational states
59
;
19
can capture limited forms of correlation and entanglement. Their main representatives are bosonic and fermionic mean-field theory (FMFT), whose variational wave functions are bosonic and fermionic Gaussian states (FGS), respectively. Although far less expressive than tensor-network methods, their approximations and limitations are well understood and can therefore allow for a simple physical interpretation of the efficiently simulated numerical results

;
52
;
19
. Depending on the system and parameter regime, these methods can yield qualitative and even quantitative agreement with more strongly correlated methods, but of course can become unreliable whenever the main approximations break down. Importantly, Gaussian mean-field approaches remain valuable for identifying beyond-classical-simulatability regimes where discrepancies between mean-field predictions and experiment or other classical benchmarks may indicate the emergence of genuinely quantum and strongly correlated behavior
67

62

35

In this work we derive explicit, numerically stable real- and imaginary-time EOMs for parity-violating fermionic Gaussian states (PV-FGS)—referring to the resulting framework as parity-violating fermionic mean-field theory (PV-FMFT). As an application, we discuss how PV-FMFT applies to general spin-1/2 systems. PV here corresponds to mixing states with even and odd fermionic occupation number. Such mixing appears naturally in bosonic Gaussian states via displacement operators after spin-to-boson mappings such as via a Holstein–Primakoff mapping
26

Parity-preserving fermionic mean-field theory

We define the fermionic creation and annihilation operators

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_{\text{FGS}}}=\hat{U}_{\text{FGS}}\ket{\mathbf{0}},

II.2
Parity-violating fermionic mean-field theory

II.2.1
Motivation

While many realistic fermionic Hamiltonians can be represented as even polynomials as in Eq. (
1
) this is not true for every Hamiltonian. An important class are generic spin-1/2 Hamiltonians. One can map any spin-1/2 Hamiltonian onto a fermionic Hamiltonian by an exact mapping such as the Jordan-Wigner (JW) transformation
30
. However, when mapping the spin Hamiltonian to a fermionic Hamiltonian via JW, this generally results in a polynomial with even and odd monomials

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\text{poly}(\hat{\bf a}).

(15)

This means that the fermionic parity can be broken (due to the presence of odd monomials of fermionic operators). As a consequence, the FMFT applied to spin Hamiltonians requires the consideration of the larger family of PV-FGS, as illuatrated in Figure
1
.

In what follows, we will show how including the PV fermionic displacement operator to the variational Ansatz of Eq. (
3
), followed by the introduction of an auxiliary mode-mediated mapping, leads to a consistent set of EOMs of the same form as Eqs. (
12
)-(
14
). This allows us to derive a FMFT that can be applied to any spin-1/2- and PV fermionic Hamiltonian. In the context of simulating quantum quenches of arbitrary spin-1/2 Hamiltonians, our approach further allows to include quantum quenches of arbitrary spin-1/2 Hamiltonians, which for instance allows us to start from arbitrary uncorrelated spin states.

II.2.4
The Colpa mapping

We can map any PV-Hamiltonian as in Eq. (
15
) to a PP-Hamiltonian of the form of Eq. (
1
), and any PV-FGS as in Eq. (
27
) to a linear combination of two PP-FGS by introducing an auxiliary mode (originally referred to as a ‘ghost particle’). We will reserve the mode
0
—i.e. Majorana operators
\hat{a}_{0}
and
\hat{a}_{1}
—for this ghost particle and define the Colpa mapping
12
,

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{2j}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Colpa}}}{{\rightarrow}}-i\hat{a}_{1}\hat{a}_{2j}

(28)

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{2j+1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Colpa}}}{{\rightarrow}}-i\hat{a}_{1}\hat{a}_{2j+1},

(29)

where
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
denotes the modes of the original Hamiltonian. The absence of the operator
\hat{a}_{0}
in Eqs. (
28
)-(
29
) will become important when deriving the EOMs of the PV-FMFT later on. The Colpa mapping has the property that even monomials of fermionic operators remain unaffected, while odd monomials are turned into even monomials, i.e.

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{(N)}=\text{poly}(\hat{a})\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Colpa}}}{{\rightarrow}}\ \hat{H}^{\prime}{}^{(N+1)}=\text{poly}_{\text{even}}(\hat{a}),

(30)

where the superscript indicates that the left (right)-hand side Hamiltonian is described by
N
(
N+1
) fermionic modes, and the prime is used in order to distinguish operators and states before and after the mapping. Figure
2
summarizes the main steps leading from the initial spin-1/2 Hamiltonian to a PP fermionic Hamiltonian through the Colpa mapping.

which projects onto the

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{M^{\prime}})\ket{C(\mathbf{I})^{\prime}}

(32)

where we defined the even fermionic Gaussian operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{M^{\prime}})=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{0}^{\dagger}&\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{\dagger}&\hat{c}_{0}&\hat{\mathbf{c}}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{M}^{\prime}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{0}\\

(33)

and, writing the original matrix of Eq. (

\displaystyle\mathbf{M^{\prime}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbf{v}^{T}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{u}^{\dagger}\\

0&-\mathbf{v}^{T}&\mathbf{0}&-\mathbf{u}^{\dagger}\\

(34)

Note, that the fermionic creation and annihilation operators in Eq. (

(35)

II.2.5
Equations of motion of the parity-violating fermionic mean-field theory

The goal of this work is to present EOMs which allow to describe PV fermionic Hamiltonians with a FMFT based on FGS. In this subsection, we will present our main result: By restricting Eq. (
26
) to unitary transformations, using the strategy developed in Ref.
59
, we can show that the EOMs for a PV-FMFT are analogue to Eqs. (
12
)-(
14
).

We consider the following unitary PV-FGS Ansatz

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_{\text{PV}}^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\text{FGS}}^{\prime}\ket{C(\mathbf{0})^{\prime}},

(37)

where
\ket{C(\mathbf{0})^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0,0^{\otimes N}}+\ket{1,0^{\otimes N}}\right)
follows from Eq. (
31
). Strictly speaking, Eq. (
37
) is a sum of two FGS and thus no longer a FGS, but we will still call it a PV-FGS in slight abuse of language. Just like in Eq. (
4
), we have a PP-FGS generator in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{(N+1)}

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\text{FGS}}^{\prime}=e^{\frac{i}{4}\mathbf{A}^{\prime T}\bm{\xi}^{\prime}\mathbf{A}^{\prime}},

(38)

where

\displaystyle\bm{\xi^{\prime}}=-\frac{i}{2}(\mathbf{W}^{\prime})^{*}\mathbf{J}^{\prime}\mathbf{M}^{\prime}(\mathbf{W}^{\prime})^{\dagger}

(39)

describes a purely imaginary and skew-symmetric matrix, and
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}
in Eq. (
39
) is a special case of Eq. (
34
),

\displaystyle\mathbf{M^{\prime}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\mathbf{u}^{T}&0&\mathbf{u}^{T}\\
\mathbf{u}&\mathbf{M}^{(11)}&-\mathbf{u}&\mathbf{M}^{(12)}\\
0&\mathbf{u}^{T}&0&-\mathbf{u}^{T}\\
-\mathbf{u}&\mathbf{M}^{(21)}&\mathbf{u}&\mathbf{M}^{(22)}\end{pmatrix},

(40)

which follows from the unitarity condition
\mathbf{v}=-\mathbf{u}
. We further introduced the matrix which describes how to transform between fermionic creation and annihilation operators and Majorana operators,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{\prime}=\mathbf{W}^{\prime}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{0}\\
\hat{\mathbf{c}}\\
\hat{c}_{0}^{\dagger}\\
\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix},

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathds{1}_{N+1}&\mathds{1}_{N+1}\\
-i\mathds{1}_{N+1}&i\mathds{1}_{N+1}\end{pmatrix}.

(41)

Similar to Eq. (
5
), Eq. (
38
) describes a linear canonical transformation of the fermionic operators in the extended Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{(N+1)}
,
\hat{U}_{\text{FGS}}^{\prime}{}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\hat{A}}^{\prime}\hat{U}_{\text{FGS}}^{\prime}=\mathbf{R}^{\prime}\mathbf{\hat{A}}^{\prime}
, where
\mathbf{R}^{\prime}=e^{i\bm{\xi}^{\prime}}
and
\text{det}(\mathbf{R}^{\prime})=1
. Thus, using the Colpa mapping has simplified the transformation of the PV-generator from a quadratic canonical transformation in
\mathcal{H}^{(N)}
to a PP-generator describing a linear canonical transformation in the extended Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{(N+1)}
. Analog to Eq. (
6
), we define the covariance matrix
\bm{\Gamma^{\prime}}
and mean-field matrix
\mathbf{H}_{m}^{\prime}=4\frac{d\braket{\hat{H}^{\prime}}_{\text{PV}}}{d\bm{\Gamma^{\prime}}}
in the extended Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{(N+1)}
.

The EOMs that describe the imaginary time evolution of the PV-FGS of Eq. (
37
) is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}{d\tau}=

\displaystyle-\mathbf{H}_{m}^{\prime}-\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}\mathbf{H}_{m}^{\prime}\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}.

(42)

while the real-time evolution is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}{dt}=

\displaystyle[\mathbf{H}_{m}^{\prime},\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}].

(43)

A detailed proof for the validity of Eqs. (
42
)-(
43
) is provided in Appendix
A
. The computational cost for simulating PV-FMFT is asymptotically the same as simulating a PP-FMFT, since only a single auxiliary fermionic mode has to be added, with the most costly numerical operation being the evaluation of the Pfaffian of a
((2N+2)\times(2N+2))
-matrix. We provide the explicit expressions for the energy expectation values
\braket{\hat{H}^{\prime}}_{\mathrm{PV}}
of the studied Hamiltonians in Appendix
B
, and detail how to efficiently compute
\mathbf{H}_{m}^{\prime}
even in cases of ill-behaved covariance matrices in Appendix
C
.

III.2
Transverse-field Ising Hamiltonian

We now study the TFIM with power-law interactions that can be realized in Rydberg-atom arrays with an interaction range determined by the Rydberg blockade radius
28
;
55
. With uniform Rabi frequency
\Omega
(transverse field) and detuning
\Delta
(longitudinal field), the Hamiltonian reads

H=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\neq l}J_{kl}\,\hat{n}_{k}\hat{n}_{l}+\frac{\Omega}{2}\sum_{k}\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{x}-\Delta\sum_{k}\hat{n}_{k},

(47)

where
\hat{n}_{k}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathds{1}-\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{z})
and we assume arbitrary units of energy, where
\text{max}(J_{kl})=1
.
Motivated by experimental constraints, we take
\Omega
and
\Delta
to be spatially uniform and choose
\Delta=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l\neq c}J_{lc}
, where
c
denotes a central lattice site. This sets the site-dependent longitudinal field

\displaystyle\zeta_{k}\equiv\Delta-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{l\neq k}J_{kl}

(48)

to zero at the center and nonzero elsewhere, with the largest magnitude near the boundaries; see Figure
4
.

We use power-law couplings
J_{kl}=r_{kl}^{-\alpha}
with lattice spacing set to unity, where
r_{kl}
is the Euclidean distance between sites
k
and
l
.
Most simulations employ van der Waals interactions (
\alpha=6
), relevant for Rydberg platforms. Because the large exponent yields a rapidly decaying tail, we expect PV-FMFT to approximate the one-dimensional TFIM ground state well, consistent with Ref.
32
. In contrast, in the strong long-range regime (characterized by small
\alpha\leq 1
) the accuracy of PP-FMFT deteriorates; we expect a similar limitation for PV-FMFT, which we illustrate on small system sizes.

\displaystyle(-1)^{q-p}\text{Pf}\left(\left.\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{2p+1,\dots,2q}\right)

\displaystyle-(-1)^{p+q}\text{Pf}\left(\left.\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{1,\dots,2p}\right)\text{Pf}\left(\left.\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{1,\dots,2q}\right)

(50)

8

Influence of spin-basis representation:

In one dimension and with open boundary conditions (OBC), the TFIM with NN couplings and no longitudinal field is exactly solvable after a JW transformation: within the
XX
-representation

H=J\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{x}\sigma_{i+1}^{x}+h\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{z}

the JW image is a quadratic fermionic operator. Consequently, the ground state—and real-time dynamics whenever the initial state is a PP-FGS—are reproduced exactly by PP-FMFT. By contrast, the
ZZ
-representation

H=J\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{z}\sigma_{i+1}^{z}+h\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{x}

is related to the
XX
-representation by a uniform spin rotation, but under the standard JW-transformation of Eqs. (
16
)-(
18
) the fermionic interaction terms are no longer quadratic. Hence PP-FGS ceases to be exact in that basis. More generally, although the two spin Hamiltonians are unitarily equivalent, different spin bases lead—after fermionization—to different variational problems: single-spin rotations are not Gaussian transformations, so they move the model closer to or farther from the ‘best’ fermionic-Gaussian manifold. This is why the choice of spin basis (before JW) measurably affects the quality of an FMFT approximation for interacting cases, see e.g. Refs.
23
;
24
. To see the effect of the chosen spin basis, we study PV-FMFT for both the
XX
- and
ZZ
-representation for the one- and two-dimensional TFIM.

Numerical PV-FMFT solver:

A direct real-time update similar to Eq. (
45
) was numerically unstable for the TFIM. We therefore integrate in time with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) method using a fixed step size
\Delta tJ=0.001
, which let to stable results across all simulated system sizes, dimensions and parameter regimes of the TFIM.

MPS details:

The matrix product state (MPS) simulations presented in this work were performed using the
emu_mps
Python package
7
;
6
, employing a two-site time-dependent variational principle (TDVP) algorithm. For one-dimensional systems, the MPS mapping follows the standard linear chain representation, while for two-dimensional systems a horizontal snaking pattern was used, mapping each row from left to right. Time evolution was carried out with a time step of
\Delta tJ\approx 0.01
. To ensure numerical reliability, the bond dimension was chosen according to system size and computational constraints: for systems of 81 sites in both one and two dimensions, we utilized a bond dimension of 900, the largest available in multiples of 100, that could be supported by a 40 GB NVIDIA A100 GPU, whereas for smaller systems a fixed bond dimension of 1000 was sufficient to produce reliable dynamical properties. It is worth noting that for large systems and long-time evolutions, MPS calculations may lose quantitative accuracy when
h_{x}=\mathcal{O}(J)
due to the rapid growth of entanglement, particularly in two-dimensional systems
67
.

TW details:

We consider a semiclassical numerical method based on the discrete truncated Wigner transformation (TW) introduced in Ref.
54
. The EOMs are solved using RK4 with step size
\Delta tJ=0.001
and a varying number of samples.

IV
Summary and outlook

We introduced a fermionic mean-field framework based on parity-violating fermionic Gaussian states (PV-FGS) and derived explicit equations of motion for both real- and imaginary-time evolution of the variational parameters; we refer to the resulting scheme as parity-violating fermionic mean-field theory (PV-FMFT). A key observation is that PV-FMFT on a system of
N
modes can be recast as a parity-preserving (PP) FMFT for a state that is a linear combination of two FGS on an enlarged Hilbert space with one auxiliary fermionic mode. This makes FMFT applicable to arbitrary spin-1/2 models and to fermionic Hamiltonians which do not have to preserve the fermionic parity, and it naturally accommodates quenches from generic spin-1/2 product states. Our implementation is numerically stable and, in the worst case, scales as
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
with the number of spins/fermionic modes.

As a first validation, we numerically demonstrated that PV-FMFT yields the exact ground state for arbitrary non-interacting spin Hamiltonians and thus can represent any spin-1/2 product state. We further showed that post-quench single-site magnetization from arbitrary initial product states are also exactly captured by PV-FMFT.

We then benchmarked the one-dimensional TFIM in presence of a longitudinal field and show that for a chain of 81 spins the considered observables computed with PV-FMFT agree well with MPS results for van der Waals (
\alpha=6
) interactions, where
\alpha
describes the coupling strength
J_{kl}=1/r_{kl}^{\alpha}
. However, when considering strong long-range interactions (
\alpha=1
) and a small chain containing nine spins, we observe deviations between the PV-FMFT and results from exact diagonalization in the quench dynamics. However, the qualitative behavior of the time-evolved observables is still described well by PV-FMFT.

In a two-dimensional square lattice containing
(9\times 9)
spins, we study the post-quench dynamics under van der Waals interactions (
\alpha=6
) in presence of a longitudinal field and compare our results against two state-of-the-art numerical methods: MPS and a semi-classical method based on the discrete truncated Wigner approximation (TW). In the low transverse field regime
h_{x}/J=0.5
, we find good agreement between PV-FMFT and the MPS solution, while TW leads to qualitatively different results. However, when approaching the dynamical phase transition
h_{x}/J\approx 2.5
, PV-FMFT only coincides with MPS and TW for a short time. We find that TW coincides with the MPS solution over longer evolution times as the transversal field increases and the system becomes weakly coupled.

A noteworthy limitation of the current formulation is the breaking of rotational symmetry visible in connected correlators of the JW-based PV-FMFT solutions: horizontal and vertical bonds map to different fermionic operator structures (parity strings) under a snake ordering, leading to anisotropic errors. Consistently, we find that the choice of spin representation (
XX
vs
ZZ
) significantly impacts accuracy across all TFIM settings.

Our formulation of PV-FMFT is closed in the sense that it discusses how to study arbitrary spin-1/2 Hamiltonians with PV-FGS. However, it is not optimal and we believe that the quality of the PV-FMFT solution can be improved by optimizing the chosen spin basis for representing the spin Hamiltonian. Also the labeling of the lattice has a measurable effect for FMFT and should be investigated
23
;
25
;
59
;
31
.

The breaking of rotational invariance we reported is due to the JW-transformation and is a non-physical artifact of the underlying method. Other mappings exist, which could potentially mitigate the effect of this symmetry breaking
65
;
50
;
42
;
36
.

It was recently shown that the PV-FGS Ansatz we employ can be implemented efficiently on digital quantum computing devices
43
and our work thus can be employed as a means of state initialization. Another interesting question is a direct comparison of PV-FMFT with PP-FMFT based on the PP Kitaev-mapping
36
;
51
;
38
;
72
. Also, a comparison of PV-FMFT with their bosonic counterpart, i.e. a mean-field theory based on bosonic Gaussian states
10
;
59
-both in terms of expressibility and computational runtime across a range of different physical models could be an interesting topic of future research.

\displaystyle-\frac{d\mathbf{R}}{d\tau}\bm{\Upsilon}\mathbf{R}^{T}-\mathbf{R}\bm{\Upsilon}\frac{d\mathbf{R}^{T}}{d\tau},

(87)

which using Eqs. (
85
)-(
86
) leads to the following EOM for the covariance matrix,

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{\Gamma}}{d\tau}=

\displaystyle-\mathbf{H}_{m}-\bm{\Gamma}\mathbf{H}_{m}\bm{\Gamma}.

(88)

We now need to make sure that the derived EOMs which follow from the considerations of the quadratic contributions are consistent with the constant contributions. We show that this is indeed the case by using Eqs. (
70
) and Eq. (
85
) and the orthogonality of
\mathbf{R}
,

\displaystyle\ket{L_{0}}=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{4}\Tr\left(\frac{d\mathbf{R}}{d\tau}\mathbf{R}^{T}\bm{\Gamma}\right)\ket{0,\mathbf{0}}=\frac{i}{8}\Tr\left(\mathbf{H}_{m}\bm{\Gamma}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{T}\bm{\Gamma}\right)\ket{0,\mathbf{0}}-\frac{i}{8}\Tr\left(\bm{\Gamma}\mathbf{H}_{m}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{T}\bm{\Gamma}\right)\ket{0,\mathbf{0}}=0,

(89)

where the last line follows from the cyclic property of the trace.

Using the definition of the covariance matrix in Eq. (
65
), we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}_{pq}=

\displaystyle-\left(\mathbf{R}\bm{\tilde{\Upsilon}}\mathbf{R}^{T}\right)_{pq}=-\left(\mathbf{\tilde{R}}\bm{\Upsilon}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}^{T}\right)_{pq}.

(90)

With Eq. (
90
), we now study what follows from the condition
\ket{\tilde{L}_{2}}=\ket{\tilde{R}_{2}}
using Eqs. (
73
) and (
79
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}:\mathbf{A}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{R}}^{T}\frac{d\tilde{\mathbf{R}}}{d\tau}\mathbf{A}:\ket{0,\mathbf{0}}=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{4}:\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{T}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}^{T}\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}\hat{\mathbf{A}}:\ket{0,\mathbf{0}}.

(91)

Using the same strategy as before, we note that due to the normal-ordering, only the fermionic creation operator part of the Majorana operators remain, leading to

\displaystyle:\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{0}^{\dagger}&\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{\dagger}&i\hat{c}_{0}^{\dagger}&i\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}^{T}\frac{d\mathbf{\tilde{R}}}{d\tau}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{0}^{\dagger}\\
\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{\dagger}\\
i\hat{c}_{0}^{\dagger}\\
i\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}:\ket{0,\mathbf{0}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}:\left(-\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{0}^{\dagger}&\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{\dagger}&i\hat{c}_{0}^{\dagger}&i\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}^{T}\bm{\tilde{\Gamma}}\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{0}^{\dagger}\\
\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{\dagger}\\
i\hat{c}_{0}^{\dagger}\\
i\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{0}^{\dagger}&\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{\dagger}&i\hat{c}_{0}^{\dagger}&i\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}^{T}\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m}\bm{\tilde{\Gamma}}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{0}^{\dagger}\\
\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{\dagger}\\
i\hat{c}_{0}^{\dagger}\\
i\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}\right):\ket{0,\mathbf{0}}.

(92)

Equation (
92
) then leads to

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbf{\tilde{R}}}{d\tau}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m}\bm{\tilde{\Gamma}}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}-\bm{\tilde{\Gamma}}\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}\right).

(93)

Since
\mathbf{\tilde{R}}
and
\mathbf{R}
are directly related [see Eq. (
55
)] it is important to check if the differential equations for both are consistent. This is done by verifying if the EOMs derived in Eq. (
93
) are consistent with the EOMs derived in Eq. (
85
). We will use the matrix form of Eq. (
55
),

\displaystyle\mathbf{\tilde{R}}=

\displaystyle-\mathbf{K}\mathbf{R}

(94)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf{1}}_{N+1}&\mathbf{0}_{N+1}\\
\mathbf{0}_{N+1}&{\mathbf{1}}_{N+1}\end{pmatrix},

(95)

where we defined the
(N+1)\times(N+1)
-diagonal matrix

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{1}}_{N+1}=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&\cdots&0\\
&&\ddots&\\
0&0&\cdots&1\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(96)

For the matrix defined in Eq. (
95
), we have

\bm{\tilde{\Upsilon}}=\mathbf{K}\bm{\Upsilon}\mathbf{K}
,
\bm{\tilde{\Gamma}}=-\mathbf{\tilde{R}}\bm{\Upsilon}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}^{T}=-\mathbf{KR}\bm{\Upsilon}\mathbf{R^{T}}\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{K}\bm{\Gamma}\mathbf{K}
, and we write

\displaystyle\frac{dE}{d\tilde{\Gamma}_{pq}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{kl}\frac{dE}{d{\Gamma}_{kl}}\frac{d\Gamma_{kl}}{d\tilde{\Gamma}_{pq}},

(97)

where, using the skew-symmetry of the covariance matrix,

\displaystyle\frac{d\Gamma_{kl}}{d\tilde{\Gamma}_{pq}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{d\Gamma_{kl}}{d\tilde{\Gamma}_{pq}}-\frac{d\Gamma_{lk}}{d\tilde{\Gamma}_{pq}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{d}{d\tilde{\Gamma}_{pq}}\sum_{rs}K_{kr}\tilde{\Gamma}_{rs}K_{sl}-\frac{d}{d\tilde{\Gamma}_{pq}}\sum_{rs}K_{lr}\tilde{\Gamma}_{rs}K_{sk}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(K_{kp}K_{ql}-K_{lp}K_{qk}\right),

(98)

which, if inserted into Eq. (
97
) and using
\mathbf{H}_{m}=4dE/d\bm{\Gamma}
, leads to

\displaystyle\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}\mathbf{H}_{m}\mathbf{K}.

(99)

Using the identity
\mathbf{K}^{2}=\mathds{1}_{2N}
, we can then use Eq. (
94
) and Eq. (
85
) to get

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbf{\tilde{R}}}{d\tau}=

\displaystyle-\mathbf{K}\frac{d\mathbf{R}}{d\tau}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{H}_{m}\bm{\Gamma}\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{K}\bm{\Gamma}\mathbf{H}_{m}\mathbf{R}\right)=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m}\mathbf{K}\bm{\Gamma}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{K}\bm{\Gamma}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{R}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m}\bm{\tilde{\Gamma}}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{R}-\bm{\tilde{\Gamma}}\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{R}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m}\bm{\tilde{\Gamma}}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}-\bm{\tilde{\Gamma}}\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}\right),

(100)

which is identical to Eq. (
93
). Therefore, the EOMs of Eq. (
85
) are consistent with the ones derived in Eq. (
93
). Let us now check whether the condition
\ket{\tilde{L}_{0}}=0
is also satisfied. Using Eq. (
72
), we compute

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\left[\frac{d\mathbf{\tilde{R}}}{d\tau}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}^{T}\bm{\tilde{\Gamma}}\right]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\left[\left(\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m}\bm{\tilde{\Gamma}}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}^{T}\bm{\tilde{\Gamma}}-\bm{\tilde{\Gamma}}\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}^{T}\bm{\tilde{\Gamma}}\right)\right]=\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\left[\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m}\bm{\tilde{\Gamma}}\bm{\tilde{\Gamma}}\right]-\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\left[\bm{\tilde{\Gamma}}\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m}\bm{\tilde{\Gamma}}\right]=0,

(101)

where we made use of the orthogonality of
\mathbf{\tilde{R}}
and used the cyclic property of the trace. We have thus shown that the imaginary time evolution is described by Eq. (
85
) and that these EOMs are consistent with the odd-parity subspace EOMs in Eq. (
93
), and satisfy the condition that the zeroth-order normal ordering expressions
\ket{L_{0}}
and
\ket{\tilde{L}_{0}}
disappear.

A.2
Real-time evolution

In order to derive the RTE EOMs, we proceed similarly. The underlying differential equation we need to solve is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\Psi_{\text{FGS}}}=-i\hat{H}\ket{\Psi_{\text{FGS}}}.

(102)

We first consider the quadratic contribution. From Eqs. (
70
) and (
77
) the condition
\ket{L_{2}}=\ket{R_{2}}
with

\displaystyle\ket{R_{2}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}:\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{H}_{m}\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{A}}:\ket{0,\mathbf{0}}

(103)

\displaystyle\ket{L_{2}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}:\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{T}\frac{d\mathbf{R}}{d\tau}\hat{\mathbf{A}}:\ket{0,\mathbf{0}}.

(104)

leads to

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbf{R}}{dt}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{m}\mathbf{R}+b\mathbf{R}\bm{\Upsilon}.

(105)

Here,
b
is an undetermined real-valued parameter which represents a gauge degree of freedom. This parameter has however no effect on the EOMs for the covariance matrix, which for the even parity sector are given by

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{\Gamma}}{dt}=

\displaystyle[\mathbf{H}_{m},\bm{\Gamma}],

(106)

which follows directly from Eq. (
105
). The constant-terms are

\displaystyle\ket{L_{0}}=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{4}\Tr\left(\frac{d\mathbf{R}}{d\tau}\mathbf{R}^{T}\bm{\Gamma}\right)\ket{0,\mathbf{0}},

(107)

\displaystyle\ket{R_{0}}=

\displaystyle-iE\ket{0,\mathbf{0}},

(108)

and we can choose
b
to ensure that the constant term condition
\ket{L_{0}}=\ket{R_{0}}
is met.

We now move on to the odd parity sector and consider the cubic and linear contributions,

\displaystyle\ket{\tilde{L}_{0}}=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{4}\text{Tr}\left[\frac{d\mathbf{\tilde{R}}}{d\tau}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}^{T}\right]\ket{0,\mathbf{0}}

(109)

\displaystyle\ket{\tilde{L}_{2}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}:\mathbf{A}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{R}}^{T}\frac{d\tilde{\mathbf{R}}}{d\tau}\mathbf{A}:\ket{0,\mathbf{0}},

(110)

and

\displaystyle\ket{\tilde{R}_{0}}=

\displaystyle-iE\ket{0,\mathbf{0}}

(111)

\displaystyle\ket{\tilde{R}_{2}}=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{4}:\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{T}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}^{T}\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}\hat{\mathbf{A}}:\ket{0,\mathbf{0}}.

(112)

By choosing

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbf{\tilde{R}}}{d\tau}=

\displaystyle-\mathbf{K}\frac{d\mathbf{R}}{d\tau}=-\mathbf{K}\mathbf{H}_{m}\mathbf{R}-b\mathbf{K}\mathbf{R}\bm{\Upsilon}=-\mathbf{K}\mathbf{H}_{m}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{R}-b\mathbf{K}\mathbf{R}\bm{\Upsilon}=\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}+b\mathbf{\tilde{R}}\bm{\Upsilon},

(113)

we have

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{\tilde{\Gamma}}}{dt}=

\displaystyle-\frac{d\mathbf{\tilde{R}}}{dt}\bm{\Upsilon}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}^{T}-\mathbf{\tilde{R}}\bm{\Upsilon}\frac{d\mathbf{\tilde{R}}^{T}}{dt}=-\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}\bm{\Upsilon}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}^{T}-b\mathbf{\tilde{R}}\bm{\Upsilon}\bm{\Upsilon}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}^{T}+\mathbf{\tilde{R}}\bm{\Upsilon}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}^{T}\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m}+b\mathbf{\tilde{R}}\bm{\Upsilon}\bm{\Upsilon}\mathbf{\tilde{R}}^{T}=[\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m},\bm{\tilde{\Gamma}}],

(114)

and we can ensure that the condition on the constant (linear) contribution in the even (odd) parity sectors is satisfied by choosing the gauge degree of freedom to be

\displaystyle b=\frac{\text{Tr}\left[\mathbf{H}_{m}\bm{\Gamma}\right]+4E(\bm{\Gamma})}{2N}=\frac{\text{Tr}\left[\mathbf{K}^{2}\mathbf{H}_{m}\mathbf{K}^{2}\bm{\Gamma}\right]+4E(\bm{\Gamma})}{2N}=\frac{\text{Tr}\left[\mathbf{\tilde{H}}_{m}\bm{\tilde{\Gamma}}\right]+4E(\bm{\Gamma})}{2N}.

(115)

We note, that it is also possible to solve the EOMs for the variational parameters
\bm{\xi}
directly through

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{\xi}}{dt}=

\displaystyle-i\left(\mathbf{H}_{m}-b\bm{\Gamma}\right),

(116)

where we used
\bm{\Gamma}=-\mathbf{R}\bm{\Upsilon}\mathbf{R}^{T}
.

Appendix B
Energy expectation values and their derivatives

In order to implement the PV-FMFT, we need to compute the expectation value
E^{\prime}=\braket{\Psi_{\text{PV}}^{\prime}|\hat{H}^{\prime}|\Psi_{\text{PV}}^{\prime}}
of the Hamiltonians in the extended Hilbert space w.r.t. the PV-FGS Ansatz of Eq. (
37
), as well as their respective derivatives
\mathbf{H}_{m}
. The energy expectation values can be computed using Wick’s theorem through Eq. (
9
). The energy expectation value of the non-interacting spin Hamiltonian defined in Eq. (
35
) is given by

\displaystyle E^{\prime}=

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{N}\left[\frac{J_{p}^{x}}{2}(-1)^{p}\text{Pf}\left(\left.\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{1,2,\dots,2p-1,2p}\right)+\frac{J_{p}^{y}}{2}(-1)^{p}\text{Pf}\left(\left.\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{1,2,\dots,2p-1,2p+1}\right)-\frac{J_{p}^{z}}{4}\left(\Gamma^{\prime}_{2p,2p+1}-\Gamma^{\prime}_{2p+1,2p}\right)\right].

(117)

while the energy of the TFIM Hamiltonian defined in Eq. (
36
) is given by

\displaystyle E^{\prime}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k<l\\
k,l>0\end{subarray}}^{N}J_{kl}\text{Pf}\left(\left.\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{2k,2k+1,2l,2l+1}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\Omega_{k}^{x}}{2}(-1)^{k}\text{Pf}\left(\left.\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{1,2,\dots,2k-1,2k}\right)-\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\zeta_{k}}{2}\Gamma^{\prime}_{2k,2k+1}.

(118)

Similarly, the energy of the TFIM Hamiltonian defined in Eq. (
24
) where interactions are of the form
XX
is given by

\displaystyle E^{\prime}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p<q\\
p,q>0\end{subarray}}^{N}J_{pq}(-1)^{q-p}\text{Pf}\left(\left.\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{2p+1,2p+2,\dots,2q}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p=1}^{N}\Omega_{p}\Gamma_{2p,2p+1}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p=1}^{N}\zeta_{p}(-1)^{p}\text{Pf}\left(\left.\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{1,2,\dots,2p-1,2p}\right).

(119)

The expressions for
\mathbf{H}_{m}^{\prime}=4\frac{d\braket{H^{\prime}}_{\text{PV}}}{d\bm{\Gamma^{\prime}}}
follow from analytical formulas for the derivative of a Pfaffian.

The computation of the Pfaffian and its derivative is the most costly operation in FMFT, in particular when dealing with ill-behaved (non-invertible) submatrices
\bm{\Gamma}|_{I}
, which for instance appear naturally when considering quenches of the TFIM model with spins initialized along the
z
-direction.

Appendix C
Computing the gradient of the Pfaffian of ill-behaved matrices

We will consider two ways for computing the derivative of the Pfaffian of a (
2n\times 2n
) skew-symmetric matrix
\mathbf{A}
which depends on some variables
x_{j}
. If the matrix
\mathbf{A}
is singular for some variable
x_{j}
, the derivative of the Pfaffian can be computed through

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\text{Pf}(\mathbf{A})}{\partial x_{k}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 2n}(-1)^{i+j+1}\frac{\partial A_{ij}}{\partial x_{k}}\text{Pf}\left(\mathbf{A}|_{\hat{i}\hat{j}}\right),

(120)

where we assume
i<j
and where
\mathbf{A}|_{\hat{i}\hat{j}}
is the submatrix of
\mathbf{A}
with rows
i,j
and columns
i,j
removed. We stress, that Eq. (
120
) can be applied also to special cases, where e.g. entire rows of a matrix
\mathbf{A}
are zero, but it is numerically quite expensive since it involves having to compute
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
Pfaffians of size
(2(n-1)\times 2(n-1))
.

If
\mathbf{A}
on the other hand is not singular w.r.t.
x_{j}
, one can use the following identity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Pf}(\mathbf{A})}\frac{\partial\text{Pf}(\mathbf{A})}{\partial x_{k}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\left[\mathbf{A}^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial x_{k}}\right],

(121)

which allows one to compute the all entries of the gradient in a single step provided one has a closed formula for
\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial x_{k}}
. If we consider the special case
x_{k}=A_{lm}
, we can write Eq. (
121
) as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Pf}(\mathbf{A})}\frac{\partial\text{Pf}(\mathbf{A})}{\partial A_{lm}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\left[\mathbf{A}^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial A_{lm}}\right].

(122)

The derivative of the Pfaffian is linked to the adjugate matrix. The adjugate matrix is the transpose of the cofactor matrix
\bm{\Delta}(\mathbf{A})^{T}=\text{adj}(\mathbf{A})
,
which is defined as
27

\displaystyle\Delta(\mathbf{A})_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j+1+\theta(i-j)}\text{Pf}\left(\mathbf{A}|_{\hat{i}\hat{j}}\right)\text{Pf}(\mathbf{A}).

(123)

The adjugate matrix satisfies the relation
\mathbf{A}\text{adj}(\mathbf{A})=\text{adj}(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{A}=\text{det}(\mathbf{A})\mathds{1}
, which in case the matrix
\mathbf{A}
is well behaved relates the matrix inverse with the adjugate,

\displaystyle\text{adj}(\mathbf{A})=\text{det}(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{A}^{-1}.

(124)

In the context of the adjugate matrix, it was shown that
\text{adj}(\mathbf{A})
can be accurately computed from the inverse
\mathbf{A}^{-1}
, even when the ladder has been inaccurately computed
60
. Let us at first assume that the matrix
\mathbf{A}
is well-behaved and thus its inverse exists. Then, we can use Eqs. (
122
) and (
124
) to compute

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\text{Pf}(\mathbf{A})}{\partial A_{lm}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\text{Pf}(\mathbf{A})}\text{Tr}\left[\text{adj}(\mathbf{A})\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial A_{lm}}\right]=\frac{1}{2\text{Pf}(\mathbf{A})}\text{adj}(\mathbf{A})_{ml}=\frac{1}{2\text{Pf}(\mathbf{A})}\Delta(\mathbf{A})_{lm}=\frac{1}{2}(-1)^{l+m+1+\theta(l-m)}\text{Pf}\left(\mathbf{A}|_{\hat{l}\hat{m}}\right),

(125)

where we made use of the fact that we are taking the derivative of a structured skew-symmetric matrix, where
\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial A_{lm}}=\mathbf{J}^{lm}-\mathbf{J}^{ml}
, where
\mathbf{J}^{lm}
is the matrix which has zeros everywhere except for a 1 at row
l
and column
m
.

In order to compute the derivative of an ill-behaved matrix, following Ref.
60
we use the Schur decomposition

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{Q}^{T},

(126)

where
\mathbf{Q}
is a real orthogonal matrix and
\mathbf{T}
has the block diagonal form

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}=\begin{pmatrix}0&t_{1}&\\
-t_{1}&0&\\
&&0&t_{2}&\\
&&-t_{2}&0&\\
&&&&\ddots\\
&&&&&0&t_{n}&\\
&&&&&-t_{n}&0&\end{pmatrix},

(127)

where
t_{k}\in\mathds{R}
are the imaginary values of the purely imaginary eigenvalue pairs of
\mathbf{A}
. When at least one of those pairs is very close (or identical) to zero, the matrix inverse
\mathbf{A}^{-1}
cannot be computed. However, the adjoint (and therefore the derivatives of the Pfaffian) can still be accurately computed when using an inaccurately computed matrix inverse
\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon}
, where

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{Q}^{T},

(128)

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}0&t_{1}+\varepsilon&\\
-(t_{1}+\varepsilon)&0&\\
&&0&t_{2}+\varepsilon&\\
&&-(t_{2}+\varepsilon)&0&\\
&&&&\ddots\\
&&&&&0&t_{n}+\varepsilon&\\
&&&&&-(t_{n}+\varepsilon)&0&\end{pmatrix},

(129)

and the derivative of the Pfaffian can be computed to an accuracy
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
. Numerically, we are then able to compute the entire gradient in parallel, avoiding
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
computations of the individual sub-Pfaffians
\text{Pf}(\mathbf{A}|_{\hat{l}\hat{m}})
through Eq. (
125
). This is achieved by computing the derivative of the Pfaffian through the approximate matrix inverse of Eq. (
128
),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\text{Pf}(\mathbf{A})}{\partial\mathbf{A}}\approx

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\text{Pf}(\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon})\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}=-\frac{1}{2}\text{det}(\mathbf{Q})\left(\prod_{k=1}^{n}(t_{k}+\varepsilon)\right)\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}^{T},

(130)

where we used the Pfaffian properties
\text{Pf}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{Q}^{T})=\text{det}(\mathbf{Q})\text{Pf}(\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon})
and
\text{Pf}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&a\\
-a&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)=a
.

Note, that in general we will be dealing with expressions of the form
\frac{\partial\text{Pf}(\mathbf{A}|_{I})}{\partial\mathbf{A}}
, where
I=\{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{2k}\}
with
k\leq n
denotes some subindex set. In that case, Eq. (
130
) can still be used, and the resulting
(2k\times 2k)
matrix needs to be embedded into the
(2n\times 2n)
matrix (all entries which contains rows or columns that are not in
I
are set to zero).

Appendix D
Arbitrary spin-1/2 product states lie in the family of PV-FGS

Following the discussion in Ref.
24
, we consider a general product state of two spin-
1/2
particles,

\ket{\Psi}=(1+\eta_{1}\sigma_{1}^{+})(1+\eta_{2}\sigma_{2}^{+})\ket{00},\qquad\eta_{k}\in\mathds{C},

(131)

where
\ket{0}
denotes a spin-down state. Under the JW transformation,
\sigma_{k}^{+}=\hat{c}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{S}_{k}
, with the string operator
\hat{S}_{k}
defined in Eq. (
19
), the corresponding fermionic state is

\ket{\Phi}=(1+\xi_{1}\hat{c}_{1}^{\dagger})(1+\xi_{2}\hat{c}_{2}^{\dagger}\hat{S}_{1,1})\ket{\mathbf{0}}=(1+\xi_{1}\hat{c}_{1}^{\dagger})(1+\xi_{2}\hat{c}_{2}^{\dagger})\ket{\mathbf{0}},

(132)

since the vacuum
\ket{\mathbf{0}}
has even fermionic parity. Due to the anti-commutation relations of fermionic operators, we may rewrite this state as a fermionic Gaussian operator acting on the vacuum:

\displaystyle\ket{\Phi}

\displaystyle=e^{\xi_{1}\hat{c}_{1}^{\dagger}}e^{\xi_{2}\hat{c}_{2}^{\dagger}}\ket{\mathbf{0}}=e^{\xi_{1}\hat{c}_{1}^{\dagger}+\xi_{2}\hat{c}_{2}^{\dagger}+\xi_{1}\eta_{2}\hat{c}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{2}^{\dagger}}\ket{\mathbf{0}},

(133)

where we made use of the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula. The construction extends directly to three spins: The state

\ket{\Psi}=(1+\eta_{1}\sigma_{1}^{+})(1+\eta_{2}\sigma_{2}^{+})(1+\eta_{3}\sigma_{3}^{+})\ket{000}

(134)

maps under the JW transformation to

\displaystyle\ket{\Phi}

\displaystyle=e^{\xi_{1}\hat{c}_{1}^{\dagger}+\xi_{2}\hat{c}_{2}^{\dagger}+\xi_{1}\xi_{2}\hat{c}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{2}^{\dagger}}e^{\xi_{3}\hat{c}_{3}^{\dagger}}\ket{\mathbf{0}}=e^{\xi_{1}\hat{c}_{1}^{\dagger}+\xi_{2}\hat{c}_{2}^{\dagger}+\xi_{3}\hat{c}_{3}^{\dagger}+\xi_{1}\xi_{2}\hat{c}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{2}^{\dagger}+\xi_{1}\xi_{3}\hat{c}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{3}^{\dagger}+\xi_{2}\xi_{3}\hat{c}_{2}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{3}^{\dagger}}\ket{\mathbf{0}}.

(135)

The pattern for larger number of spins follows from the above considerations. Thus every spin-1/2 product state can be constructed from a PV fermionic Gaussian operator acting on the fermionic vacuum state.

The resulting state is not necessarily normalized, but normalization can always be restored, as done for example in imaginary-time evolution (ITE) algorithms.
Importantly, the appearance of linear terms in the exponent is precisely what distinguishes PV-FGS from PP-FGS: Since PP-FGS lack linear fermionic terms, they cannot represent generic spin-
1/2
product states.
In contrast, the Gaussian-filter Ansatz used in our ITE formulation (Sec.
II
) naturally includes these PV contributions, and the corresponding PV-FGS will be obtained by solving the EOM in Eq. (
42
).

Appendix E
PV-FMFT can reproduce SMFT

We want to show that the dynamics of a system of
N
non-interacting spin-1/2 Hamiltonians can be described by PV-FMFT. The real-time EOMs for a variational wave function
\ket{\Psi(\bm{\theta})}
derived in Section
II
were derived from the time dependent variational principle
59

\displaystyle d_{t}\theta_{j}=

\displaystyle-i\sum_{k}(\mathbf{G}^{-1})_{jk}\braket{\Psi_{k}|\hat{H}|\Psi},

(136)

where
