Hepatocyte Piezo1 regulates glycogen metabolism via the FGF21-STAT3 pathway
Authors: Tian Tao, Ke Yang, Zhenming Li, Mengyuan Zhang, Zhejiao Zhang, Jinghui Guo, Hui Chen, Geyang Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The metabolism of glycogen in the liver plays a crucial role in regulating blood glucose levels. Previous studies have underscored the critical involvement of Piezo1 in glucose metabolism. However, the role of Piezo1 in hepatic glycogen metabolism remain unexplored. We induced liver-specific deletion of Piezo1 in Piezo1fl/fl mice using Cre adenovirus via tail vein injection. Compared to Piezo1fl/fl treated with GFP adenovirus, those treated with AAV-Cre exhibited impaired glucose tolerance, elevated GYS2 and PYGL levels, and decreased FGF21 levels, implicating inhibition of the STAT3 signaling pathway. Furthermore, mice with hepatocyte-specific Piezo1 deletion (Alb-Piezo1−/−) on a high-fat diet experienced exacerbated blood glucose levels, decreased hepatic FGF21 production, and enhanced PYGL expression and activity. These effects were alleviated by PF-05231023, an FGF21 analog. Additionally, intraperitoneal injection of Yoda1, a Piezo1 agonist, raised FGF21 levels, activating the STAT3 pathway and suppressing PYGL activity, thereby improving glucose tolerance. In vitro studies showed that knockdown of Piezo1 or Stat3 increased PYGL expression. Conversely, activation of Piezo1 led to decreased PYGL expression. Our study reveals a previously unexplored regulatory mechanism of glycogen metabolism by Piezo1 in liver cells, offering new insights into diabetes treatment.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05921-4.

Introduction

The liver serves as a crucial metabolic organ within the organism, primarily responsible for maintaining energy balance [1]. Among its various functions, the metabolism of glycogen, particularly its synthesis and breakdown, is an indispensable component in maintaining stable blood glucose levels [2]. Imbalances in glucose homeostasis are frequently connected to the development and advancement of various metabolic disorders, including diabetes and fatty liver disease [3]. Glycogen, a branched polysaccharide composed of numerous glucose molecules linked by glycosidic bonds, serves as a crucial energy reserve in mammals, primarily stored in skeletal muscle and the liver for regulating blood glucose levels [2]. The proper functioning of hepatic glycogen metabolism relies on the accurate coordination and regulation of a variety of metabolic enzymes, such as glucokinase, glycogen synthase 2 (GYS2), debranching enzymes, liver glycogen phosphorylase (PYGL), phosphorylase kinase, and glucose-6-phosphatase (G6PC) [2]. Among these, GYS2 and PYGL are key rate-limiting enzymes for hepatic glycogen synthesis and breakdown, respectively, and they directly affect the rate and efficiency of hepatic glycogen metabolism, thereby influencing glucose stability [4]. Additionally, hepatic glycogen metabolism is intricately regulated by several hormones, including insulin and glucagon. These hormones work together to maintain glucose homeostasis by modulating the activities of enzymes involved in glycogen synthesis and breakdown [5].

Piezo1 is a mechanosensitive ion channel that converts mechanical signals into electrical and chemical signals by allowing calcium to flow across the cell membrane [6]. It is found in many non-sensory tissues, including the lungs [7], endothelial cells [8], red blood cells [9], urinary bladder [10], liver [11], and kidneys [12], where it plays important roles in various physiological processes [7–13]. Prior investigations have underscored the critical involvement of Piezo1 in the modulation of glucose and lipid metabolism, specifically by participating in the regulation of pancreatic insulin and glucagon secretion, thereby orchestrating systemic glucose homeostasis. Additionally, in the gastrointestinal tract, Piezo1 has been implicated in the regulation of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and ghrelin secretion, as well as in the modulation of glucose-lipid metabolism and nutrient absorption [14–18]. Considering the crucial role of liver in glucose metabolism, the expression profile and potential functional role of Piezo1 in hepatic glycogen metabolism remain unexplored. In the present study, we demonstrate that Piezo1 is expressed in the liver. Furthermore, the hepatocyte-specific deletion of Piezo1 was found to enhance the expression of PYGL, leading to increased glycogenolysis and contributing to elevated blood glucose levels. These novel findings contribute to our expanding understanding of the complex regulatory networks that govern glucose homeostasis, and may offer valuable insights into the development of innovative therapeutic strategies for metabolic disorders.

Results

Piezo1 ablation in mouse liver leads to deteriorating blood glucose and increased hepatic glycogen content

The expression levels of Piezo1 protein and mRNA were observed in the livers of mice (Fig. S1A, B). To investigate the role of Piezo1 in regulating hepatic glycogen metabolism, we administered an adeno-associated virus (AAV) carrying the Cre recombinase (AAV-Tbg-NLS-Cre virus) via tail vein injection into Piezo1fl/fl mice that were fed a high-fat diet (HFD). This approach aimed to generate mice with a liver-specific knockout of Piezo1 (Fig. 1A). Our findings indicate a significant increase in liver weight (Fig. 1B). Additionally, Piezo1fl/fl mice receiving AAV-Cre exhibited a more severe glucose intolerance than mice receiving AAV-GFP (Fig. 1C, D). Further, the expression of Pygl, a gene involved in hepatic glycogenolysis, was markedly upregulated, whereas the expression levels of glycogen synthesis-related genes such as Glut2 and Gys2 were also increased. Interestingly, no significant alterations were detected in genes associated with gluconeogenesis, such as glucose-6-phosphatase（G6pc）,phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase 1 (Pck1) and pyruvate carboxylase (Pcx), or glycolysis including phosphofructokinase (Pfk). Additionally, we observed a concomitant enhancement in PYGL activity in Piezo1fl/fl mice receiving AAV-Cre (Fig. 1E-L). Hepatocyte-specific Piezo1 knockout mice exhibit abnormal liver glycogen metabolism

In order to further enhance the specificity of Piezo1 knockout specifically in the hepatocyte, the Piezo1fl/fl mice were crossed with the Alb-Cre mice to generate liver cell-specific Piezo1 deletion mice (Alb-Piezo1−/−) (Fig. 3A, B). Our results demonstrated that the Alb-Piezo1−/− mice fed with the high-fat diet exhibited significantly higher liver weight compared to control mice (Fig. 3C). Notably, these Alb-Piezo1−/− mice also displayed a marked glucose intolerance and hepatic glycogen accumulation (Fig. 3D-F). Analyses using glycogen assay kits and Periodic Acid-Schiff stain (PAS) revealed a substantial increase in hepatic glycogen content in the gene-knockout mice (Fig. 3E, F). The mRNA levels of Piezo1 and FGF21 were downregulated, whereas those of Glut2, Gys2, and Pygl were upregulated in the livers of Alb-Piezo1−/− mice (Fig. 3G-K). Additionally, hepatic activity of PYGL was elevated in Alb-Piezo1−/− mice (Fig. 3L). However, no significant changes were observed in genes related to gluconeogenesis or glycolysis between control and Alb-Piezo1−/− mice (Fig. 3M-P). Similarly, the protein levels of Piezo1 and FGF21 in the livers were significantly reduced in Alb-Piezo1−/− mice. Exogenous infusion of FGF21 analog rescues impaired glycogen metabolism in Alb-Piezo1−/− mice

To examine whether the disruption of glycogen metabolism in Alb-Piezo1−/− mice is due to the absence of FGF21, we conducted further experiments by administering intraperitoneal injections of PF-05231023, a long-acting analog of FGF21, to both high-fat diet-fed Alb-Piezo1−/− mice and control littermates. The infusion of PF-05231023 improved liver weight, glucose metabolism, and hepatic glycogen storage in Alb-Piezo1−/− mice (Fig. 4A-D). The expression levels of Piezo1 and Fgf21 were significantly reduced in the livers of Alb-Piezo1−/− mice compared to Piezo1fl/fl mice. Furthermore, PF-05231023 stimulated the phosphorylation of STAT3 and inhibited the expression and activity of both GYS2 and PYGL in Alb-Piezo1−/− mice. Discussion

The liver, as the pivotal organ for glycogen storage within the organism, plays a crucial role in maintaining energy homeostasis and physiological activities [21]. Glycogen, serving as an immediate energy reserve, is rapidly converted from glucose and stored postprandially, preparing the body for rapid energy supply during periods of starvation or sudden energy demands [22]. Consequently, precise regulation mechanisms governing glycogen synthesis and breakdown are indispensable for sustaining glycemic control and overall metabolic health. During hepatic glycogen synthesis, GYS2 serves as a rate-limiting enzyme, with its catalytic activity tightly regulated to ensure the timely and appropriate conversion of glucose into hepatic glycogen for storage [23]. This process not only helps to reduce fluctuations in postprandial blood glucose levels but also offers a steady supply of glucose for the body during periods of fasting [24]. PYGL, as the rate-limiting enzyme for hepatic glycogenolysis, catalyzes the breakdown of glycogen into glucose-1-phosphate. Subsequently, glucose-1-phosphate can be converted to glucose-6-phosphate, which may then enter the pentose phosphate pathway, participating in the generation of NADPH and fatty acid synthesis. This process represents a crucial pathway for the rapid acquisition of glucose when energy demands increase, vital for maintaining energy supply and lipid metabolism balance [25]. Under physiological conditions, such as fasting or intense exercise, blood glucose levels decrease, leading to a significant increase in PYGL activity. This enhancement facilitates the breakdown of hepatic glycogen, replenishing blood glucose and ensuring a stable energy supply. Conversely, after a meal, when blood sugar levels rise, insulin production increases as well, inhibiting PYGL activity through a complex network of signaling pathways. This, in turn, reduces the breakdown of hepatic glycogen and helps prevent elevated blood sugar levels after meals [26, 27]. This precise regulatory mechanism ensures the maintenance of glucose homeostasis across diverse physiological states. In our study, injecting the Cre virus into the tail vein of Piezo1fl/fl mice or generating hepatocyte-specific Piezo1 gene knockout mice both lead to the depletion of Piezo1 in liver cells. This is accompanied by a significant increase in the expression and function of GYS2 and PYGL. Concurrently, hepatic glycogen accumulation and worsened blood glucose levels, suggesting that liver Piezo1 may play a role in maintaining blood glucose balance by regulating glycogen metabolism.

FGF21, a hormone secreted by the liver, plays a critical role in regulating hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism [19, 28]. Our study revealed a significant decrease in FGF21 levels in liver-specific Piezo1 knockout mice, while systemic administration of the Piezo1 agonist Yoda1 led to increased FGF21 levels. These findings enhance our understanding of the complex role of Piezo1 in hepatic metabolism, indicating that it may play a crucial role in coordinating and balancing various metabolic pathways by regulating cytokines such as FGF21. Furthermore, treatment with FGF21 analogs markedly improved glucose metabolism in Alb-Piezo1−/− mice, indicating that targeting the Piezo1-FGF21 axis could be a promising strategy for managing glycogen metabolism and glucose homeostasis.

In addition, Piezo1 is vital for regulating the synthesis and secretion of insulin and glucagon in pancreatic islets [14, 15], as well as modulating gastrointestinal hormones such as GLP-1 [16] and ghrelin [17]. Our findings uncover a novel aspect of Piezo1’s role by showing its impact on FGF21 secretion in the liver, a key metabolic regulator. This discovery expands the understanding of Piezo1’s role in hormonal regulation and reinforces its significance in maintaining hormonal balance throughout the body.

Piezo1 serves as a mechanosensor for stiffness in hepatic macrophages, and its activity is influenced by mechanical stiffness. Pharmacological activation of Piezo1 enhances the capacity of macrophages for efferocytosis and accelerates the resolution of liver inflammation and fibrosis [29, 30]. Our study focuses on elucidating the function of Piezo1 in hepatocytes, contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of its physiological roles within the liver. This convergence of findings underscores the diverse roles of Piezo1 in both immune and parenchymal cells, emphasizing its crucial significance in maintaining hepatic homeostasis. The absence of a detailed characterization of mechanical signals in hepatocytes represents a limitation of our current study. Physiological and pathological mechanical signals (e.g., changes in cell tension, extracellular matrix stiffness, or hemodynamic forces) that modulate Piezo1 activity in hepatocytes represent a valuable direction for future research, as they would help better clarify its role in hepatic metabolism.

Previous research has highlighted the crucial role of STAT3 in maintaining glucose homeostasis by inhibiting the expression of gluconeogenic genes in the liver [31]. Moreover, STAT3 is involved in glycogen metabolism during motor neuron development, where it regulates the expression of Gysl and PygB through cis-regulatory sequences. This demonstrates its role in controlling glycogen synthesis and breakdown across different tissues [32]. Notably, a study has demonstrated that hepatic FGF21 knockdown enhances glycogenolysis through the STAT3 pathway [20]. In our research, we found that the mechanosensitive ion channel Piezo1 further influences the STAT3 signaling pathway through FGF21, thereby modulating the expression of GYS2 and PYGL and participating in the regulation of glycogen metabolism.

In summary, this study reveals that liver cells possess the ability to sense mechanical stimuli and precisely regulate glycogen metabolism through the Piezo1-mediated FGF21-STAT3 pathway. This finding provides new insights into hepatic glycogen metabolism and blood glucose regulation.

Methods

Animals and treatments

All experimental animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility, in compliance with the guidelines of the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Jinan University. Environmental conditions were strictly controlled as follows: temperature maintained at 22 ± 2 °C, relative humidity at 50%−60%, and a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle; animals were provided with ad libitum access to a sterile experimental diet and sterile drinking water. The animal experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Jinan University, with the ethical approval number: IACUC-20241119-01.

AAV-Cre injection

Six-week-old male Piezo1fl/fl mice received a tail vein injection of either AAV-Tbg-NLS-Cre virus (Obio Technology, Shanghai, China) or AAV-Tbg-GFP at a dosage of 1011 viral genome copies (vg). One month post-injection, the tissues were collected for further analysis.

Liver cell-specific Piezo1 deletion mice (Alb-Piezo1-/-)

Piezo1fl/fl mice and Alb-cre mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). To generate hepatocyte-specific Piezo1 deletion mice (Alb-Piezo1−/−), the Piezo1fl/fl mice were crossed with the Alb-Cre mice. PCR is used to identify the genotype of mice during the subsequent mating and breeding process. The primers required for mouse genotyping are shown in the Table S1.

Male mice aged 4–5 weeks were placed on a high-fat diet for a period of 10 weeks and used as experimental subjects in this study.

PF-05231023 treatment

After 10 weeks of HFD feeding, PF-05231023 was administrated to Piezo1fl/fl and Alb-Piezo1−/− mice intraperitoneally(i.p.) at a dose of 10 mg/kg twice a week for 4 weeks [33].

Yoda1 treatment

After 10 weeks of HFD feeding, Yoda1 was administrated to C57BL/6J mice intraperitoneally (i.p.) at a dose of 80 µg/kg once daily for a week.

Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT)

After being fasted overnight, mice were injected intraperitoneally with glucose (1.5 g/kg). Blood sample was collected from the tail vein at various time points (0, 15, 30, 60, 90, 120 min) for glucose determination using the Accu-Chek performa glucometer (Roche).

Periodic acid-schiff stain (PAS)

After dewaxing and hydrating the sections, they are immersed in Periodic Acid for denaturation for 15 min and then rinsed with purified water three times. The sections are then placed in Schiff’s reagent for staining in a dark environment. Once staining is complete, rinse the sections under running water, and then counterstain them with hematoxylin to visualize the nuclei. After differentiation and further bluing, dehydrate the sections through a series of graded ethanol solutions, and finally mount them with neutral balsam. The images were captured using a Leica SP8 microscope (Leica, Germany).

Cell culture and transfection

The mouse AML-12 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA) and were maintained in DMEM/F-12 containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% Insulin-Transferrin-Selenium-A (ITS-A) supplement at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere. Prior to various treatments, the cells used in this study were first treated with oleic acid (OA, 40 µM) for 24 h.

For stable knockdown of Piezo1 in AML-12 cells, shRNA sequences targeting mouse Piezo1 were first cloned into the pLKO.1 vector. Subsequently, a co-transfection of psPAX2, pMD2G, and either pLKO.1 or pLKO.1-shPiezo1 plasmids (siPiezo1: CCAACCTTATCAGTGACTT) was performed in 293 T cells for 48 h to generate lentiviral supernatants. Following collection and filtration, the lentiviral supernatants were then added to the culture medium of AML-12 cells for 24 h. The AML-12 cells were subsequently subjected to puromycin selection at a concentration of 5ug/mL for 48 h.

Wild-type AML-12 cells and AML-12 cells with stable Piezo1 knockdown were treated with the FGF21 analog PF-05231023 (500 ng/mL) or vector control for 24 h.

To downregulate Stat3 expression in AML-12 cells, small interfering RNA (siRNA) was used. Briefly, cells were seeded at an optimal density and transfected with either control siRNA or Stat3 siRNA using lipofectamine reagent for 24 h. Subsequently, the Stat3 siRNA-transfected AML-12 cells were treated with the FGF21 analog PF-05231023 (500 ng/mL) or vector control for an additional 24 h.

Real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Takara, Shiga, Japan) and reversely transcribed to cDNA. qPCR was performed as previously described [18]. The primers used are detailed in Table S2.

Western blotting

Western blot analysis was performed as described previously [34, 35]. Briefly, the proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and detected with specific antibodies. Key antibodies are listed in Table S3.

Statistical analyses

All data are expressed as mean ± S.E.M. Statistical differences were assessed by one-way ANOVA or Student’s t-test (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001).
