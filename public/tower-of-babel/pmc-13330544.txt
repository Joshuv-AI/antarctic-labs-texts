Environmental shaping of tolerance failure in autoimmune liver diseases: a phenotype-specific framework with primary biliary cholangitis as the strongest model
Authors: Krystian Mirowski, Krzysztof Ciszowski, Malgorzata Zwolinska-Wcislo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutoimmune liver diseases (primary biliary cholangitis, PBC; primary sclerosing cholangitis, PSC; autoimmune hepatitis, AIH) are three distinct, organ-specific autoimmune disorders that share loss of tolerance as the central pathophysiological mechanism but differ in target tissue, age of onset, genetic background, and environmental susceptibility. This review examines how environmental exposures modulate tolerance failure and tissue-directed injury across the three phenotypes. The signal is most coherent in PBC, where epidemiological evidence (smoking, recurrent urinary tract infections), neoantigen-focused mechanistic work on xenobiotic modification of PDC-E2, cholangiocyte apotope biology, and emerging exposomic data converge. PSC is better understood through gut-liver-microbiome interactions. AIH, including pediatric AIH, shows a weaker and more heterogeneous environmental signature complicated by overlap with drug-induced autoimmune-like hepatitis (DI-ALH). We outline the limitations of current exposomic methodology and propose phenotype-resolved research priorities. Introduction

Autoimmune liver diseases (AILD) - autoimmune hepatitis (AIH), primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), and primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) - are three distinct, organ-specific autoimmune disorders. They share loss of immune tolerance as the central pathophysiological mechanism but differ in target tissue, age of onset, genetic background, and clinical course [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. We do not treat them here as a single environmental entity. They are discussed side by side because comparing how environmental signals contribute to tolerance failure across three different forms of liver autoimmunity is informative, even where the underlying biology diverges.

Genetic susceptibility is well established. Genome-wide association studies have identified loci across the HLA region and pathways involved in antigen presentation, IL-12/JAK-STAT signaling, NF-kB, and lymphocyte activation [1,6]. Modelling studies suggest that identified variants explain less than one-third of total susceptibility, and concordance studies in monozygotic twins for PBC (approximately 63% pairwise concordance versus 0% in dizygotic pairs) provide some of the strongest evidence that non-genetic factors contribute substantially to disease liability [7]. This is consistent with the broader view of organ-specific autoimmunity, where heritability accounts for part but not all of disease risk and where environmental triggers shape both incidence and tissue specificity.

Disease incidence and prevalence vary by region, with latitude-related gradients and spatial clustering reported for PBC and PSC [8,9]. These geo-epidemiological patterns do not establish causality but suggest that environmental inputs - low-dose chemical mixtures, infections, microbial communities, diet, and pharmaceuticals - influence whether genetic susceptibility translates into clinically manifest autoimmunity. For PBC and PSC, the interval between presumed exposure and clinical disease is often long, which complicates retrospective attribution. AIH is more heterogeneous. AIH also affects children, in whom prolonged adult-pattern chemical or pharmaceutical exposures are unlikely to apply, and where viral, perinatal, and immune-developmental factors may dominate [5]. The pediatric phenotype is therefore an important corrective to any framework that assumes AILD pathogenesis is uniformly driven by cumulative adult-life exposures.

Our synthesis is interpretive rather than novel: the idea that environmental exposures contribute to AILD pathogenesis has been discussed for decades [[10], [11], [12]]. What we attempt here is a phenotype-resolved reading. PBC provides the most internally consistent example because epidemiology, neoantigen biology, cholangiocyte immunobiology, and bile-centred exposure retention converge in the same direction. PSC is best understood through gut-liver-microbiome biology. AIH retains a more heterogeneous exposure profile, with the additional diagnostic problem of drug-induced autoimmune-like hepatitis (DI-ALH), which is the currently preferred nomenclature for autoimmune-like hepatic injury following identifiable drug or supplement exposure [13,14]. Throughout this review we attempt to distinguish epidemiologically established associations from mechanistically plausible but unproven extensions, and to flag where statements reflect our interpretation rather than consensus.

Why AILD warrant an exposome-informed framework

Single-exposure epidemiology has limited explanatory value in AILD because patients accumulate low-dose mixtures of chemicals, drugs, dietary components, and microbial metabolites over years to decades [10,15,16]. Questionnaire-based ascertainment is susceptible to recall bias and misclassification, particularly for consumer products whose formulations change over time. The exposome framework, which aims to characterise total environmental exposure across the lifespan, offers a methodological alternative through untargeted measurement in serum, urine, bile, and tissue [[15], [16], [17], [18]].

High-resolution exposomics combined with exposome-wide association studies (EWAS) has identified differential plasma chemical profiles in patients with PBC and PSC, with detectable signatures of pesticides, food additives, and persistent organic pollutants [17]. Complementary work has shown that bile carries a measurable chemical fingerprint reflecting geography and individual exposure history [18]. These observations are biologically encouraging because they indicate that cumulative low-dose exposures may leave detectable molecular traces in disease-relevant compartments. Exposomics, however, has well-recognised limitations that should be acknowledged. Untargeted mass spectrometry detects chemical features whose identity, biological activity, and disease relevance are often uncertain; only a minority of features can be confidently annotated against reference libraries [15,16]. Cross-sectional designs cannot establish temporality, and biological half-lives differ widely across chemical classes, so a single measurement may not adequately represent past exposure. Replication across independent cohorts is still limited. Causal inference from EWAS signals requires integration with mechanistic data, animal models, and ideally interventional evidence, none of which are easily generated for low-dose mixtures. We therefore treat exposomic findings as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

In our view, the most useful contribution of the exposome framework is conceptual: it forces explicit consideration of gene-environment interaction. Disease emerges not when susceptibility variants act alone or when exposures act alone, but when both align in time and target tissue. In PBC, the same HLA risk variants are present in many individuals who never develop disease; conversion of susceptibility into clinical autoimmunity probably requires environmental triggers acting on antigen presentation, barrier function, or regulatory T cell biology [1,6,7]. Modelling exposure and genotype jointly, rather than separately, is the appropriate next step.

PBC: the strongest model of environmentally modulated liver autoimmunity

Among the three AILD phenotypes, PBC has the most coherent environmental narrative. Meta-analytic evidence identifies cigarette smoking as the strongest modifiable association. The pooled odds ratio for PBC among ever-smokers versus non-smokers was 1.31 (95% CI, 1.03-1.67), with a stronger signal in former smokers (pooled OR 1.36; 95% CI, 1.01-1.84) and a non-significant trend in current smokers (pooled OR 1.18; 95% CI, 0.94-1.50), albeit with substantial heterogeneity (I2 89%) [19]. The attenuation of risk in current smokers compared with former smokers is counterintuitive and may reflect survivor bias, diagnostic timing, or the dual pro-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects of tobacco. A separate meta-analysis reported approximately three-fold higher risk of advanced fibrosis among PBC patients who were ever-smokers (pooled OR 3.00; 95% CI, 1.18-7.65), suggesting that smoking may influence progression in addition to initiation [20,21].

The controlled interview study by Gershwin et al. enrolled 1032 PBC patients and 1041 matched controls and identified past smoking (adjusted OR 1.569; 95% CI, 1.292-1.905), history of urinary tract infections (UTI; AOR 1.511; 95% CI, 1.192-1.915), use of hormone replacement therapy (AOR 1.548; 95% CI, 1.273-1.882), and a first-degree family history of PBC (AOR 10.736; 95% CI, 4.227-27.268) as independent risk factors [22]. The UTI signal is mechanistically interpretable. Escherichia coli, the predominant uropathogen, expresses a PDC-E2 homologue (ePDC-E2) whose lipoyl domain shares conformational features with the immunodominant human PDC-E2 lipoyl domain. Yang et al. provided direct experimental evidence that human-PDC-E2-lipoyl-domain affinity-purified antibodies from PBC patients cross-react with ePDC-E2, and identified spreading determinants beyond the immunodominant epitope, strengthening the case that E. coli exposure in genetically susceptible hosts can contribute to AMA generation [23]. This is one of the more concrete examples in human autoimmunity in which an environmental trigger has been linked mechanistically to a specific organ-targeted autoantibody response.

Hair dye use has been a recurrent association in interview-based studies and is acknowledged in clinical guidance [2,3,22,24,25]. We treat it cautiously. Permanent oxidative hair dyes contain aromatic amines (mainly p-phenylenediamine, PPD) that undergo oxidative coupling to form reactive intermediates capable of binding proteins and generating immunologically relevant adducts [26,27]. PPD is also an established contact sensitiser with documented T-cell-mediated reactivity [28]. The most defensible interpretation - which we offer as interpretation rather than proof - is that hair dyes serve as markers of repeated exposure to mixtures of reactive aromatic chemicals rather than as causal agents in themselves. Whether such mixture exposure contributes to PBC pathogenesis or reflects unmeasured lifestyle confounding cannot be resolved from current data.

Mechanistic work on xenobiotic modification of PDC-E2 provides the closest existing example of biologically plausible chemical mimicry in AILD. 2-Octynoic acid, found in cosmetics and food flavourings, can be conjugated to the lipoyl domain of PDC-E2, generating a neoepitope recognised by AMA with higher affinity than the native structure [29]. 2-Nonynoic acid, also present in cosmetic formulations, has been identified as a potential trigger [30]. Organic mimeotopes of PDC-E2 elicit cross-reactive immune responses in serologic and T-cell proliferation assays [31].

A separate line of work clarified why this immunological assault is biliary-specific. Lleo et al. demonstrated that during apoptosis, human intrahepatic biliary epithelial cells fail to glutathionylate PDC-E2 and translocate it immunologically intact into apoptotic bodies (so-called apotopes), making the autoantigen accessible to AMA without permeabilisation [32]. Other epithelial cell types either degrade or shield PDC-E2 during apoptosis. The apotope is therefore the immunological link between a ubiquitous mitochondrial autoantigen and tissue-selective injury limited to small intrahepatic bile ducts.

This compartment-specific vulnerability has direct consequences for environmental exposure. Bile is a physiologic reservoir in which exogenous chemicals, their reactive metabolites, and microbially transformed compounds (including bile acids) are concentrated through enterohepatic circulation [17,18,33]. Cholangiocytes are immunologically active: they express toll-like receptors, present antigen via MHC class II, and secrete cytokines and chemokines [33,34]. The link between the preceding sentences is the following: when immunologically intact PDC-E2 apotopes appear in a microenvironment enriched with reactive xenobiotics and microbial metabolites, the local conditions favour chronic, compartment-amplified antigen presentation. The biliary tree functions simultaneously as the target of immune attack and as the site where environmental exposure burden is locally amplified. PBC is, in this sense, the AILD in which compartmental exposure retention, antigen accessibility, and tissue specificity coincide most clearly. Fig. 2 summarises this mechanistic chain. PSC: a distinct, gut-centred environmental architecture

PSC is not a weaker version of PBC. Its environmental architecture differs in kind, not in degree. The dominant conceptual frame is the gut-liver-microbiome axis, comprising intestinal barrier function, microbial composition, microbial metabolites, bile acid signaling, and hepatic immune activation [[35], [36], [37], [38], [39], [40]].

A meta-analysis of seven case-control studies (over 2.3 million participants) reported decreased risk of PSC among current smokers (pooled OR 0.31; 95% CI, 0.18-0.53) and former smokers (pooled OR 0.52; 95% CI, 0.44-0.61), with the inverse association persisting in non-IBD subgroups [[41], [42], [43]]. This is not evidence that smoking protects against PSC. The more plausible explanations are confounding by inflammatory bowel disease, treatment effects, selection bias, and as-yet uncharacterised effects of nicotine or tobacco metabolites on intestinal immunity. The interpretive point - and we acknowledge it as interpretation - is that the exposure logic in PSC differs fundamentally from PBC, where smoking is consistently associated with increased risk.

Microbiome-oriented studies are central in PSC because environmental inputs probably act indirectly - by shaping microbial composition, intestinal permeability, metabolite flux, and bile acid transformation - rather than through direct hepatobiliary toxicity [[37], [38], [39], [40]]. PSC microbiomes differ from healthy controls in alpha diversity and in enrichment of specific genera linked to mucosal inflammation. Microbial dysbiosis may contribute actively to pathogenesis through secondary bile acids, FXR and TGR5 signaling disruption, and enhanced portal delivery of pathogen-associated molecular patterns [[37], [38], [39], [40]]. Diet, antibiotics, smoking, and chronic intestinal inflammation all influence the microbiome over years. The gut-liver-microbiome axis therefore provides a biologically coherent route by which cumulative environmental pressures can translate into hepatobiliary inflammation without requiring direct biliary chemical toxicity. Cross-national bile exposomic profiling supports this view, revealing geography-dependent biliary chemical signatures that may interact with disease-specific pathways [17,18]. Integrating microbiome sequencing, bile acid profiling, and exposomic measurement within the same patient cohorts is a logical, but methodologically demanding, next step.

AIH: heterogeneity, the DI-ALH problem, and pediatric AIH

AIH also has environmental associations, but the signal is less uniform. An English registry-based case-control study of 987 AIH cases and 6767 matched controls found that ever-smokers had a significantly increased risk of AIH, with the association persisting after adjustment for age, sex, deprivation index, and region [44]. A US-based questionnaire study by Lammert et al. reported a higher proportion of current smokers among AIH cases (18.9% versus 7.4%), though ever-smoker prevalence was similar between groups [45]. These conflicting patterns reflect both genuine biological heterogeneity and methodological limitations.

A critical diagnostic boundary is drug-induced autoimmune-like hepatitis (DI-ALH), the currently preferred term for hepatic injury that resembles AIH serologically and histologically but follows identifiable drug or supplement exposure [13,14]. DI-ALH can present with the same autoantibody profiles, histology, and biochemistry as idiopathic AIH, making differentiation difficult without detailed temporal exposure history. The clinical importance of this overlap is practical: patients with DI-ALH may improve on drug withdrawal alone and may not require long-term immunosuppression [13,14]. Herbal and dietary supplement exposures add further complexity because they are often underreported and contain immunologically active constituents. For epidemiology, the implication is uncomfortable: signals previously attributed to environmental AIH risk may partly reflect undetected DI-ALH, particularly when exposure timing is incompletely captured.

The aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) pathway offers a mechanistic bridge between environmental ligands and immune regulation in AIH. AhR is a ligand-activated transcription factor that senses exogenous compounds (polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, dietary indoles, microbial tryptophan metabolites) and endogenous ligands (kynurenine, bilirubin derivatives) and influences the balance between regulatory T cells (Tregs) and Th17 cells. Vuerich et al. showed that Tregs and Th17 cells from AIH patients display impaired AhR-driven CD39 upregulation, attributable to elevated AhR repressor (AHRR) and oestrogen receptor alpha in Tregs and elevated HIF-1 alpha in Th17 cells, with AhR preferentially binding oestrogen receptor alpha over ARNT in AIH Tregs [46]. Silencing AHRR in vitro restored Th17 responsiveness to AhR ligands, limited Th17 differentiation, prevented Treg-to-Th17 conversion, and attenuated liver injury in a humanised mouse model [47]. These findings position AhR as a candidate molecular interface between environmental chemicals and the Treg/Th17 balance in an organ-specific autoimmune context.

Pediatric AIH adds an important corrective. AIH affects children, in whom long-term adult-pattern xenobiotic or pharmaceutical exposure is generally not relevant, and where DI-ALH is uncommon outside specific drug classes [5]. Children with AIH more frequently present as a sclerosing-cholangitis overlap (autoimmune sclerosing cholangitis), and the genetic, perinatal, and viral context may be more determinative than chronic chemical exposure. The pediatric phenotype is therefore difficult to reconcile with any framework that treats environment-driven AIH as primarily a cumulative adult-life exposure problem. It is more compatible with a model in which environmental factors modulate tolerance failure on a permissive genetic background, and in which the relevant environmental inputs differ across the life course: perinatal, infectious, and immune-developmental in early-onset disease; chemical, pharmaceutical, and microbial-metabolic in adult-onset disease. Any environmental framework for AIH must accommodate both.

From association to mechanism: cholestatic phenotypes, mechanistic bridges, and the wider context of organ-specific autoimmunity

The environmental signal appears strongest in cholestatic AILD because compartment biology, antigen exposure, and exposure retention coincide in the same tissue. Cholangiocytes combine epithelial stress responses with antigen-presenting capacity; bile retains xenobiotics, microbial metabolites, and reactive intermediates; and the apoptotic apotope makes PDC-E2 immunologically accessible in PBC [17,18,[32], [33], [34]]. Several mechanistic bridges are relevant across AILD: reactive xenobiotics can modify self-structures and generate neoepitopes; structural similarity between environmental motifs and self-antigens can enable molecular mimicry; and stress-related antigen externalisation and innate immune activation can amplify the immunogenic context in which altered structures are encountered [[10], [11], [12],23,[29], [30], [31], [32], [33],48,49]. These pathways are unlikely to operate in isolation. Their importance probably depends on host genotype, regulatory tone, and the timing of exposure relative to barrier dysfunction or inflammatory priming. Table 3 summarises these bridges.

It is worth placing this in the broader landscape of organ-specific autoimmunity, since the AILD environmental story has structural parallels with non-hepatobiliary disorders. In type 1 diabetes, enterovirus infection, early-life dietary factors, and intestinal microbiota are implicated alongside HLA susceptibility, with gut-pancreas immunological interactions structurally analogous to the gut-liver axis in PSC. In multiple sclerosis, Epstein-Barr virus, vitamin D status, smoking, and obesity converge as environmental risk factors on a strong HLA-DRB1∗15:01 background, illustrating how a single dominant environmental trigger (EBV) becomes integral to disease etiology only when other modifiers are present. Rheumatoid arthritis demonstrates a clear smoking-by-genotype interaction (shared epitope plus smoking and protein citrullination), which is the closest non-hepatic parallel to the smoking-PDC-E2-haptenisation story in PBC. Autoimmune thyroiditis is shaped by iodine intake, selenium status, and infection. The common thread across these examples is gene-environment interaction operating on tissue-specific antigen handling, immune regulation, and barrier biology. AILD fit comfortably within this framework and do not require a separate explanatory paradigm.

A second observation worth making explicit is that environmental modulation does not displace loss of tolerance as the central pathophysiological mechanism in AILD, particularly in AIH. Loss of immune tolerance to self-antigens, mediated by defective central and peripheral regulatory mechanisms, remains the defining feature of these disorders. The contribution of the environmental framework is to specify how, where, and when tolerance failure becomes durable and tissue-selective. Compartment-specific cholangiocyte biology and bile-based exposure retention explain why PBC injury localises to small intrahepatic bile ducts. Gut-liver-microbiome biology explains why PSC injury localises to large ducts at the intestinal interface. The environmental framework complements rather than competes with the tolerance-failure paradigm.

Clinical implications, methodological limitations, and research priorities

For clinical practice, the hierarchy of actionability is uneven. Smoking has the strongest epidemiologic support and is the most defensible target for counseling, particularly in PBC [[19], [20], [21]]. Hair dyes and other consumer-product exposures should be discussed as hypothesis-relevant rather than proven causal factors [22,24,25]. In AIH, careful exposure history (including prescription drugs, over-the-counter agents, and herbal or dietary supplements) is clinically important because identification of DI-ALH changes both classification and management [13,14].

The methodological weaknesses of the field are substantial. Most data are observational. Exposure ascertainment is often retrospective and questionnaire-based, with limited validation. Low-dose chemical mixtures are poorly measured, and product formulations change over time. Latency between exposure and clinical disease can span years to decades, making retrospective attribution unreliable. Exposomic technologies, while promising, have their own limitations: feature annotation remains incomplete; analytical reproducibility across platforms is imperfect; only a fraction of detected features can be confidently identified; biological half-lives vary widely; cross-sectional designs cannot establish temporality; and replication of EWAS signals across independent cohorts is still in early stages [15,16]. Causal inference from any single exposome study is therefore weak. Statements about environmental causation in AILD should explicitly distinguish empirically supported associations (such as smoking and PBC, or UTI and PBC) from mechanistically plausible but currently unproven extensions (such as specific cosmetic xenobiotics or bile chemical signatures). We have attempted that distinction throughout this review.

Priorities for the field should include: prospective cohorts with repeated exposure capture and biospecimen banking; integrated multi-omics combining exposomics, metabolomics, microbiome sequencing, and immunophenotyping (including Treg/Th17 profiling, AhR pathway readouts, and CD39 expression); phenotype-resolved analytic design, avoiding pooling of PBC, PSC, and AIH; standardised DI-ALH adjudication to limit cohort contamination; explicit modelling of gene-environment interaction rather than separate genetic and environmental analyses; and inclusion of pediatric and early-onset AIH populations to test which environmental architectures generalise across the life course. Conclusions

AILD are three distinct, organ-specific autoimmune diseases in which loss of tolerance is the central pathophysiological mechanism. Environmental factors do not replace this paradigm; they contextualise it. Environmental modulation appears most coherent in PBC, where smoking, recurrent UTI, xenobiotic-driven neoepitope formation on PDC-E2, cholangiocyte apotope biology, and bile-based exposure retention align mechanistically [22,23,[29], [30], [31], [32]]. PSC reflects a distinct environmental architecture centred on the gut-liver-microbiome axis [[35], [36], [37], [38], [39], [40]]. AIH, including pediatric AIH, has a heterogeneous exposure profile complicated by DI-ALH and is best understood as a tolerance-failure disease in which environmental inputs differ across the life course [5,13,14,44,45]. The most productive next step is not another catalogue of candidate risk factors but the construction of phenotype-resolved, mechanistically anchored, gene-environment-aware studies capable of testing how environmental signals translate into stable, tissue-selective autoimmune phenotypes.
