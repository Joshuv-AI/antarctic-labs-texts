Application of a flipped classroom incorporating modified team-based learning in molecular biology laboratory teaching: a mixed methods study
Authors: Meng Qu, Qinlong Hou, Xushen Li, Chunyan Yu, Jichen Xia, Zhiheng Dong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Flipped Classroom with Team-Based Learning (FC-TBL) approach has gained traction in medical education in recent years, yet a comprehensive assessment of its instructional efficacy remains limited. This study sought to elucidate the effectiveness of FC-TBL in molecular biology laboratory instruction and assess its influence on subsequent related courses’ learning outcomes.MethodsA total of 109 students were randomly assigned to either the traditional Lecture-Based Learning (LBL) group or the FC-TBL group. Students’ academic performance and satisfaction were gauged through final examinations and surveys, while the enduring impact of FC-TBL was evaluated using online assessments and tracking students’ scores in follow-up courses.ResultsThe findings indicate that FC-TBL significantly enhanced students’ academic achievements in molecular biology labs and garnered higher satisfaction rates among participants compared to traditional methods. Furthermore, in terms of sustained benefits, FC-TBL demonstrated a marked improvement in the learning outcomes of subsequent related courses.ConclusionIn conclusion, this study underscores the efficacy of FC-TBL in fostering both immediate and lasting learning effects, positioning it as a promising pedagogical model for adoption in laboratory instruction across various medical education disciplines.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06450-7.

Background

The molecular biology laboratory course stands as a cornerstone in the undergraduate medical curriculum, functioning as a pivotal bridge between theoretical insights into molecular biology and practical laboratory proficiency. These laboratory techniques represent fundamental tools in medical research and are essential in clinical diagnostics and therapeutic interventions. Currently, Lecture-Based Learning (LBL) remains the dominant instructional paradigm for molecular biology laboratory courses in a multitude of medical institutions in China. While this methodology facilitates students’ systematic mastery of molecular biology techniques, it falls short in fostering self-directed learning, critical thinking, innovation, and the effective application of acquired knowledge to future endeavors in science and clinical practice [1].

Previous educational reforms have witnessed the integration of teaching content and the transformation of traditional confirmatory experiments into inquiry- and innovation-driven comprehensive experiments by the basic medicine teaching team at Beihua University. This alignment with scientific research and clinical practice is aimed at fostering graduates with robust scientific acumen. However, our observations indicate that the implementation of comprehensive experimental teaching within the conventional Lecture-Based Learning (LBL) framework has not fully engaged students or enhanced their motivation, analytical skills, and problem-solving abilities. Challenges emerged due to the intricate sequencing of experimental steps, a more convoluted experimental process, and delays in obtaining experimental results. Therefore, identifying effective teaching strategies for molecular biology laboratory courses is crucial for enhancing students’ experimental proficiency and overall practical competencies.

The Flipped Classroom (FC) represents an innovative teaching approach that integrates traditional pedagogy with digital information technology. Within the flipped classroom model, there is a shift in the role of learners, transitioning the focus of education from being teacher-centered to student-centered [2]. This change also alters the learning process from the conventional “teaching before learning” to a more progressive “learning before teaching” approach [2, 3]. In this framework, students first acquire the necessary knowledge through pre-class activities, subsequently delving deeper and applying this knowledge during in-class sessions. For instance, students accomplish lower-order learning objectives by independently acquiring fundamental knowledge before class, and then advance to higher-order learning goals by actively applying and honing their analytical and problem-solving skills under the guidance of the instructor [2, 4].

During the pre-class phase of FC, students are provided with reading materials or instructional videos via an online platform, enabling them to manage their study time autonomously and at their preferred pace. This approach liberates ample classroom time for fostering higher-order thinking skills [4, 5]. The FC teaching model underscores independent learning and knowledge internalization, fostering a culture where students are motivated to become self-reliant and accountable learners [6, 7]. However, challenges arise in the implementation of FC, such as the absence of adaptive assessment mechanisms (e.g., immediate feedback) and low levels of pre-class engagement, leading to limited pre-learning opportunities which may impede the effectiveness of FC deployment [8, 9].

To address these issues, it is imperative to introduce active learning strategies within the FC framework, including immediate feedback and collaborative teamwork [3, 10]. These enhancements can enhance student engagement, promote deeper understanding, and maximize the benefits of the flipped classroom approach in educational settings. In this context, Team-based Learning (TBL) emerges as a promising solution to meet the above objectives. TBL is a student-centered instructional approach, characterized as “an active learning and small-group teaching strategy” [11, 12]. It offers students a diverse array of activities encompassing individual learning, collaborative teamwork, and immediate feedback, presenting itself as a structured and active form of team-based group learning [12]. TBL not only fosters positive attitudes towards class preparation and pre-class activities but also serves as a motivational force for FC, thereby mitigating some of its inherent shortcomings. When compared to traditional lecture-based learning (LBL), both FC and TBL have demonstrated the ability to enhance student learning outcomes and cultivate a heightened enthusiasm for learning, particularly within the realm of medical education in recent times [3, 8, 13–16].

The Flipped Classroom with Team-Based Learning (FC-TBL) is a student-centered blended teaching approach that integrates the team-based learning model into the flipped classroom, combining the strengths of both FC and TBL instructional models. Nevertheless, there exists a dearth of research on the efficacy of FC-TBL in medical education. Existing studies have primarily concentrated on short-term evaluations of course effectiveness [12, 13, 17–19], with limited exploration into the enduring impact of FC-TBL. Thus, further evidence is imperative to elucidate the true effectiveness of this blended instructional model [20]. The exploration of synergistic active teaching strategies to optimize learning outcomes and knowledge retention is crucial for fostering more dynamic laboratory courses [6]. Our study aims to elucidate the effectiveness of FC-TBL in the context of molecular biology laboratory instruction and its sustained impact over the long term.

Methods

Study participants

This study was conducted at the College of Basic Medical Sciences, Beihua University, involving 109 clinical medical students for the comparison of two teaching methodologies. The molecular biology laboratory course was conducted during the sixth semester. The participants exhibited a slightly higher representation of females (59.6%) compared to males (40.4%), with an age range of 19 to 20 years. These third-year university students were randomly allocated to either the LBL group (n = 54), utilizing traditional lecture methods, or the FC-TBL group (n = 55), incorporating the flipped classroom model with team-based teaching strategies. The mean Grade Point Averages (GPA) on a 4-point scale were obtained for both groups, resulting in 3.45 ± 0.26 for the LBL group and 3.42 ± 0.23 for the FC-TBL group. The GPA represents the average of students’ exam grades in all previous courses post-enrollment, sourced from the university’s official website. Statistical analysis employed an independent samples t-test, indicating no significant difference in mean GPA between the two groups (P = 0.585). All participants volunteered for the study, providing written informed consent, and were guaranteed anonymity and confidentiality throughout the research process.

Study design

The medical molecular biology laboratory course offered at Beihua University consists of four practical modules: nucleic acids extraction, quantitative analysis of nucleic acids, polymerase chain reaction, and electrophoresis techniques. These modules are designed to equip students with essential experimental skills, deepen their understanding of experimental theories, and enhance their problem-solving abilities. Both groups of students were exposed to identical lecture settings, instructors, and lecture materials, although the sessions took place on different dates. Each session lasted 180 min, or 3 h, and occurred once a week over a span of 4 weeks. The instructor distributed the same textbook and syllabus to all students, ensuring consistency across the groups.

Prior to the class, in order to ensure equitable distribution of gender and learning abilities within the learning teams, the instructor divided the students into 8 learning groups for the FC-TBL based on their GPA and gender, with each group comprising 6–7 students. Utilizing the “Rain Classroom” app, an online teaching platform accredited by Beihua University and independently developed by Tsinghua University, the instructor provided pre-class resources such as assigned reading materials, instructional videos, teaching slides, and guiding questions. Students could learn at their own pace, but were required to complete the Individual Readiness Assurance Test (iRAT) within the specified time frame. The test comprised 8–10 closed-book multiple-choice questions, each with five options (one correct/best answer and remaining four distractors), designed to assess students’ understanding of basic concepts and key points. Upon completion of the test, students transitioned into a group learning phase. During these group meetings, students engaged in discussions centered around the aforementioned questions. Based on their discussions, they created a PowerPoint presentation that summarized the key points and insights gained from their collective analysis. This presentation was subsequently utilized as part of the Team Readiness Assurance Test (tRAT), a tool designed to evaluate the readiness and collaboration skills of student teams.

In the classroom setting, the instructor began by outlining the course objectives and reviewing basic concepts (15 min). Subsequently, during the 15-minute Team Readiness Assurance Test (tRAT), a random selection method was used to present each team’s consensus and problem-solving process through a PPT. Following the presentations, a 30-minute classroom discussion took place, during which the instructor intervened promptly to provide guidance and clarification in cases of dispute or uncertainty. The instructor then summarized key points and demonstrated basic experimental procedures (10 min). Students engaged in experimental activities for 100 min, completing the knowledge application phase, and subsequently participated in intra-group discussions on the experimental results, focusing on 3–5 questions based on the 4 S principles (significant problem, same problem, specific choice, simultaneous response) for 5 min. The instructor allocated an additional 5 min to provide feedback based on each group’s performance.

After the class session, students were expected to submit their lab reports and engage in further learning by reviewing materials provided by the instructor, such as journal articles on recent advancements in molecular biology. Additionally, students were required to actively participate in group discussions and knowledge exchange sessions lasting no less than 30 min. Throughout this learning phase, the instructor addressed student queries either through the “Rain Classroom” App or a dedicated WeChat group established beforehand.

Structure of LBL

In contrast to FC-TBL, which emphasizes interactive learning, the LBL group received only the teaching PowerPoint slides before class. Students prepared for class using the designated textbook and teaching materials (Fig. 1). During the class, a conventional teaching approach focused on the teacher’s lecture was employed, covering topics such as experimental principles, operational procedures, safety precautions, and equipment usage. Prior to conducting solo experiments, students observed operational demonstrations provided by the teacher. Experiment reports were completed by students at the conclusion of the class. However, although there was no group study or discussion prior to the class, students had the opportunity to communicate with the teacher via the “Rain Classroom” App to address any issues encountered during pre-class preparation and post-class review.

Evaluation methodology

At the conclusion of the course, all participants underwent a final examination and completed an anonymous questionnaire. The test results were utilized to evaluate the efficacy of FC-TBL instruction, while the questionnaire aimed to gauge students’ perceptions regarding the effectiveness and satisfaction with FC-TBL teaching. Students’ satisfaction survey on FC-TBL

Seven months post-course completion (at the end of the seventh semester), all students participated in an online test for the molecular biology laboratory course. A comparison of final test scores between the two student groups was conducted for subjects such as Genetic Diagnosis and Gene Therapy, Medical Biotechnology and Applications, Introduction to Medical Science Research in the seventh semester, and Hospital Infection in the eighth semester. The molecular biology experiment in the sixth semester of the clinical medicine major served as a preparatory course for the aforementioned program. It involved learning the application of PCR and nucleic acid hybridization methods to diagnose diseases at the genetic level in gene therapy and diagnosis. Additionally, molecular biology techniques were also required for pathogen detection in hospital infection control, among other medical applications. This comparison was carried out to assess the long-term impact of FC-TBL teaching.

Data analysis

In this study, SPSS version 21 software was used to analyze the data. The normality of continuous variables was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test. If the data demonstrated a normal distribution, intergroup differences were assessed using independent sample t-tests. If the data exhibited non-normal distribution, intergroup differences were evaluated using the Mann-Whitney U-test. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Cohen’s d calculator was used to compute the effect size for each result to assess the magnitude of differences.

Descriptive statistics were employed to evaluate students’ perceptions of FC-TBL teaching. Due to the exploratory and descriptive nature of the study, a qualitative thematic analysis approach was used for data analysis. The questionnaire responses were evaluated using a 5-point Likert scale, where 1 indicated “strongly disagree” and 5 denoted “strongly agree.” The scale’s reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. The survey results on the capabilities of students in the FC-TBL and LBL groups were analyzed and compared. The analysis was conducted using the chi-square test, with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05.

Results

Comparison of examination scores

One week following the course conclusion, all students participated in a simultaneous examination comprising experimental theory, case studies, and experimental design. Subsequently, the test scores of the LBL and FC-TBL student groups were compared and analyzed. The test scores for students in the LBL group in the molecular biology laboratory course averaged 85.89 ± 4.26, while those in the FC-TBL group averaged 90.27 ± 3.51. Illustrated in Fig. 2, the FC-TBL group achieved significantly higher test scores than the LBL group, indicating that the FC-TBL teaching model enhanced the teaching effectiveness of the molecular biology laboratory course.

Student competency comparison

A total of 109 students (100% response rate) completed the questionnaire. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the questionnaire’s seven items was calculated as 0.806. Survey data, presented in quantitative and percentage formats, were utilized to depict students’ attitudes and were analyzed using the chi-square test. As depicted in Table 1, compared to the LBL group, a majority of students believed that the FC-TBL teaching model notably stimulated their motivation for independent learning, enhanced their self-learning capabilities, fostered independent thinking, improved problem-solving and analytical skills, leading to increased learning outcomes. Additionally, the FC-TBL teaching model was noted to enhance communication, expression skills, and teamwork abilities.

Students’ perceptions of FC-TBL teaching model

A total of 55 students from the FC-TBL group participated in a survey to evaluate their experience with the FC-TBL approach, yielding a 100% response rate. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the eight questionnaire items was calculated as 0.758. Results from the questionnaire (refer to Table 2) indicated that 78.2% of students felt that the FC-TBL teaching model sparked their interest in molecular biology experiments. Furthermore, over 81% of students mentioned that they could effectively communicate with both the instructor and their peers, receiving timely and valuable feedback. More than 83% expressed satisfaction with their contributions to the team and their individual accomplishments.

While 20% of students expressed concerns about the FC-TBL method demanding excessive time outside of class, over 74% appreciated the well-designed pace of the class and expressed a fondness for the FC-TBL teaching model, indicating their willingness to choose it again in the future.

The impact of FC-TBL on long-term knowledge retention

To evaluate the enduring effects of the FC-TBL teaching model on the retention of knowledge regarding molecular biology experiments, we conducted an exam seven months post-course (at the end of the seventh semester). The exam comprised 15 multiple-choice questions, each carrying a value of 1 point for a total of 15 points. Covering basic concepts and fundamental theories, the results indicated that while there was no significant disparity between the scores of the FC-TBL group (11.89 ± 1.82) and the LBL group (11.38 ± 1.86) (refer to Fig. 3), the FC-TBL group’s scores were marginally higher than those of the LBL group.

Discussion

Developing innovative talents with robust scientific research capabilities and effective self-learning skills stands as a crucial objective in the ongoing reforms within medical higher education in China [21]. The undergraduate phase of basic medical education represents an opportune period for nurturing students’ innovative prowess and scientific acumen. Presently, laboratory course instruction serves as the primary avenue for fostering innovation and research acumen among undergraduate students in Chinese colleges and universities [21]. However, conventional experimental teaching predominantly revolves around verification experiments following teacher-led lectures, characterized by simplistic experimental procedures, fixed operations, and singular outcomes. Within traditional experimental teaching paradigms, students assume passive roles, lacking opportunities for self-exploration and innovation. Hence, the quest for novel teaching strategies in laboratory courses emerges as an imminent challenge necessitating resolution.

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that higher levels of learner participation and autonomy in learning activities are associated with increased perceptions of worth in the learning environment and higher motivation [9, 10, 22]. In contrast to traditional, instructor-centered lecture formats, FC-TBL provides participants with flexible pre-class learning strategies, allowing them maximum autonomy in learning and fostering engagement through team-based learning discussions [6, 9, 10]. Additionally, the incorporation of the classroom Team Readiness Assurance Test (tRAT) and post-class reflective exchanges for FC-TBL participants enables them to take on more active roles in the learning process. This not only enhances learning effectiveness but also further boosts participants’ sense of achievement. Research conducted by Feng has demonstrated the effectiveness of FC-TBL in enhancing learning activities within clinical laboratory immunology courses [22]. FC-TBL has been shown to improve learning effectiveness in acute-care nursing courses for nursing students [23], leading to improvements in academic performance [17]. These findings collectively suggest that the FC-TBL teaching approach positively impacts short-term learning outcomes in fundamental medical courses.

In our study, we implemented the FC-TBL teaching mode in molecular biology laboratory courses for 55 clinical medicine students at Beihua University in 2021. Our results showed that FC-TBL had a short-term positive impact on the learning effectiveness of molecular biology laboratory courses, enhancing students’ comprehensive skills across multiple dimensions. This finding may be attributed to the collaborative nature of team-based learning, which enhances learner engagement and achievement, resulting in better preparation before class and improved problem-solving skills during and after class, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of FC-TBL. These findings are consistent with those reported by Shen [6].

Learning effectiveness encompasses enhancements in cognitive aspects as well as changes in affective dimensions, including advancements in knowledge, skills, perceptions, and attitudes [24]. Current evaluations of learning impacts have primarily focused on short-term effects, such as the influence on learning outcomes in courses employing specific instructional approaches and their effects on concurrent courses within the same academic term, as previously discussed. However, extensive research on the long-term effects of learning effectiveness—such as its influence on subsequent courses or even future professional endeavors—is notably scarce.

In a study assessing the efficacy of FC in physiology courses, it was demonstrated that FC not only elevated students’ performance in physiology classes but also facilitated learning in subsequent courses, such as pathology, pathophysiology, pharmacology, and diagnostics [5]. Similarly, Shen discovered in an investigation of a comprehensive laboratory course in basic medical science that FC-TBL led to improved final exam scores one month later [6]. However, contrasting viewpoints exist; some scholars contend that FC lacks sustained impacts and does not offer any advantage in terms of academic performance compared to alternative active learning strategies [2]. Notably, upon students’ return to a traditional classroom setting, the influence of FC appeared negligible [2, 25]. These divergent findings may primarily stem from the absence of a standardized assessment framework. Variables such as the duration between assessments, evaluation criteria (e.g., exam scores, self-directed learning capabilities, communication and presentation proficiencies, creativity, analytical and problem-solving skills), and student characteristics (including diverse learning styles) can significantly influence the evaluation of long-term effects.

In our evaluation of the long-term effects of FC-TBL, we found that the retention of basic concepts and fundamental theories of molecular biology experiments did not exhibit statistically significant differences over time between the two student groups. However, retention levels were higher in the FC-TBL group compared to the LBL group. Notably, the FC-TBL group engaged in significant self-study activities before classes, utilizing resources such as textbooks, videos, and supplementary materials. This suggests that, while FC-TBL did not demonstrate a substantial positive impact on the long-term retention of foundational knowledge, the integration of pre-class self-study within the FC-TBL framework resulted in a better understanding of the subject matter, likely due to the reinforcing effect of the Individual Readiness Assurance Test (iRAT) and collaborative team discussions in TBL.

The cultivation of lifelong learning skills stands as a pivotal objective in medical higher education. As aspiring clinicians poised to confront professional challenges, the acquisition of lifelong learning skills can yield enduring benefits in their future clinical and research endeavors. Regrettably, students in certain institutions may lack critical thinking, analytical prowess, and problem-solving abilities due to deficiencies in fostering innovation and practical skills. Consequently, these students are mostly trained to be qualified medical graduates rather than empowered individuals equipped with the lifelong learning capabilities. Notably, lifelong learning capabilities are essential for continual improvement and excellence in their future professional undertakings [26].

In our study, we observed that the final grades in Gene Diagnosis and Gene Therapy, Medical Biotechnology and Applications, and Introduction to Medical Science Research courses during the 7th semester were significantly higher in the FC-TBL group compared to the LBL group. Additionally, the final grade in the Hospital Infection course in the 8th semester was also superior in the LBL group. These courses necessitate a strong foundation in experimental theories of molecular biology, which are crucial for understanding clinical diagnosis and therapy, biopharmaceuticals, and the design of scientific research. The results indicate that students in the FC-TBL group exhibit enhanced abilities to construct new knowledge based on their existing understanding and effectively bridge theoretical concepts with practical applications. It is evident that the positive effects of the FC-TBL approach on subsequent related course learning may not be solely attributed to improved memory. The FC-TBL approach may have facilitated participants in developing essential lifelong learning skills, including critical thinking, improved analytical capabilities, and enhanced problem-solving skills. These acquired skills likely contributed to the superior performance in subsequent courses. The cultivation of lifelong learning skills through FC-TBL appears to have a positive ripple effect on students’ learning outcomes in follow-up courses. By fostering a deeper understanding and application of knowledge, FC-TBL holds the potential to equip students with the necessary tools to effectively acquire and apply new knowledge in real-world contexts, thereby preparing them for continued learning and success in their future endeavors.

Furthermore, students expressed a high level of satisfaction with the FC-TBL approach. They appreciated the autonomy afforded by individual pre-class preparation and the collaborative peer-to-peer discussions during FC-TBL, noting that it enhanced their engagement and sparked their interest in learning. Most students agreed or strongly agreed that “This teaching method stimulated my interest in learning molecular biology laboratory courses” (Table 2). The efficacy of pre-class preparation was notably enhanced by the increased interest, heightened engagement, and immediate feedback received both from team members and instructors. Students highlighted the value of feedback with most students agreeing or strongly agreeing that “The feedback from the team members helped me a lot” and “I received timely feedback from teachers”. The majority of the students agreed or strongly agreed that “The course did not take up too much time outside of class” (Table 2).

Beyond enhancing student engagement, FC-TBL fostered a sense of pride and accomplishment through pre-class and in-class discussions, encouraging deeper reflection. Students expressed satisfaction with their contributions to group activities, with most students agreeing or strongly agreeing that " I made a significant contribution to the group’s activity” and " I was satisfied with my personal achievements” (Table 2).Students favored the FC-TBL approach and believed that this blended teaching method was beneficial in enhancing independent learning, teamwork skills, and practical application abilities, while also facilitating communication among peers and between students and instructors. A substantial 74.5% of students indicated that “The classroom pace is suitable for me” and expressed willingness to choose this teaching method again (Table 2).

While our study demonstrates the positive outcomes of FC-TBL instruction, there are inherent limitations in the study design that warrant consideration. Firstly, concerning the construction of the team-based learning groups, our approach primarily relied on GPA as a criterion. Each group included 1–2 high-GPA students who could assist their peers as needed. However, essential skills such as communication, presentation, and collaboration were not factored into the group composition criteria. Secondly, the assessment of the long-term impacts of FC-TBL was limited to tracking final exam results. This approach overlooked crucial factors that influence lifelong learning, such as students’ self-directed learning capabilities, communication and presentation skills, creativity, as well as analytical and problem-solving skills. Moreover, the study’s scope was constrained by the low number of course hours and participants exposed to FC-TBL, which could potentially diminish the assessment of its long-term effects. Additionally, factors like the type and duration of video materials used in pre-course activities were not thoroughly explored, despite their potential influence on learners’ motivation to engage.Given the unique characteristics of each course, implementing and optimizing a new teaching model like FC-TBL to enhance teaching and learning quality will necessitate sustained, dedicated efforts on the part of educators over an extended period.

Conclusions and future perspectives

This study has demonstrated that the FC-TBL blended learning model positively impacts students’ learning outcomes in the molecular biology laboratory course. While this effect may not prolong the retention of acquired knowledge, it consistently enhances learning effectiveness in subsequent related courses. Students have shown a strong preference for FC-TBL, indicating its potential to stimulate interest, motivation, and improve lifelong learning skills.The FC-TBL teaching approach aligns well with the current need to train innovative talents, supports students’ self-directed learning, and offers distinct advantages in fostering teamwork, innovation, and lifelong learning abilities. The cultivation of these skills appears to be even more crucial for students accustomed to the LBL teaching method. Moving forward, we plan to further explore and refine team-building guidelines in our teaching practices to enhance motivation. Additionally, we aim to fine-tune pre-class materials, such as videos and micro-courses, to better engage students and stimulate their learning interests.

Our goal is to optimize the FC-TBL pedagogy to facilitate its broader implementation in medical higher education practices, thereby contributing to a more effective and engaging learning environment for students.
