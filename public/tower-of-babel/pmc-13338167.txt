Graph-based analysis of frequency response measurements for assessment of winding faults in power autotransformers
Authors: Vahid Tamjidi Azad, Hadi Afsharirad, Hamed Alizadeh Ghazijahani, Amin Safari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate assessment of winding faults in large power autotransformers using frequency response analysis (FRA) remains challenging due to the complex electromechanical coupling between winding geometry and the measured response. This paper proposes a graph-based diagnostic framework that links physics-based FRA modeling with probabilistic machine learning for fault type identification, localization, and severity estimation. First, a RLC state-space model of a 125 MVA, 230/132/20 kV power autotransformer is developed and validated against measured FRA traces in the healthy state. Subsequently, simulation-based faulty FRA datasets are then generated by applying mechanical deformations, including axial displacement (AD) and radial deformation (RD), and an electrical turn-to-turn short-circuit (TTSC) fault through corresponding geometry/parameter variations. Each FRA trace is converted into a graph representation using the weighted visibility-graph approach, and informative features are extracted from the resulting graph-domain spectrum. A Gaussian-process (GP) pipeline is subsequently employed, where Type-GPC performs multi-class fault type identification (Healthy/AD/RD/TTSC), Location-GPC localizes TTSC faults, and fault-specific GPR models estimate severity with quantified uncertainty. Experimental results show overall accuracies of 92.9 % for fault type identification and 92.0 % for TTSC localization. Introduction

The ongoing monitoring of power network equipment is crucial for maintaining the reliability and stability of the electrical grid. Power transformers, as key components, are integral to voltage regulation and energy transmission. Given their significant cost and essential role, any failure in power transformers can result in considerable operational interruptions and financial repercussions. Although transformers are generally reliable, increasing electrical demand and prolonged exposure to electrical, mechanical, and environmental stresses raise the likelihood of faults over time. Research has shown that faults related to windings represent a significant proportion of transformer failures, highlighting the need to develop effective diagnostic methods to detect and classify these faults accurately1. Among these, axial displacement (AD), radial deformation (RD), and turn-to-turn short circuit (TTSC) of transformer windings are defects that, if not detected correctly in their early stages, can propagate and give rise to other faults, eventually causing transformer failure2.

For detecting electrical and mechanical faults in in-service transformers, a wide range of online and offline electrical and non-electrical techniques has been investigated. Electrical methods include short-circuit impedance measurement, partial discharge (PD) measurement, leakage flux sensing, and monitoring of terminal current/voltage signatures3. However, many such methods lack sufficient sensitivity to small changes in winding structure and tend to indicate problems only after the fault has progressed to severe damage4. Among non-electrical methods, dissolved gas analysis (DGA) of transformer oil is a common technique for detecting such faults. Nevertheless, DGA also typically requires significant fault development, and overlapping gas patterns with other fault types can complicate interpretation.

Recently, frequency response analysis (FRA) has become a popular and practical method for detecting several issues in transformer windings due to its non-invasive, cost-efficient, and highly sensitive nature5. In FRA, the measured frequency response of a transformer is compared against a healthy reference; because the response is tightly linked to the winding’s physical configuration, any structural change alters the winding parameters and, consequently, the frequency response. The offline FRA measurement procedure is well established and documented in international standards6,7, but interpreting the results still requires dedicated studies. Hence, recent research has focused on enhancing FRA interpretation using mathematical indices or data-driven approaches, such as machine-learning models and advanced signal-processing techniques. However, despite the capabilities of these approaches, a significant limitation is their dependence on extensive labeled datasets that include healthy and faulty transformer FRA traces. Moreover, implementing controlled fault scenarios with different severities and locations on operating power transformers is often impractical due to technical and financial constraints. Consequently, researchers have increasingly adopted computational modeling methods to simulate winding defects and investigate their impact on FRA responses. In this context, many studies, such as8,9, simulate FRA traces using equivalent circuit models of one or more transformers, and then investigate the influence of various electrical and mechanical faults on the FRA response by modifying the corresponding circuit parameters. To achieve a more accurate simulation of different fault conditions, several other works, such as10,11, have employed FEM-based modeling of both healthy and faulty transformer states. This approach enables a much more precise investigation of the impact of various fault types on transformers with different winding structures and geometrical configurations.

To quantify differences between FRA traces and enable condition assessment, researchers have widely used numerical indices. For instance, in10,12,13, various electrical and mechanical winding faults were investigated using mathematical and FEM models of a transformer, and different numerical indices were assessed to identify fault types. It was also shown that some indices are better suited to AD detection, whereas others are more suitable for RD. A recent comprehensive study14 combined multiple indices and frequency-band selection to detect TTSCs and estimate severity effectively. Similarly, the work in15 applied several numerical indicators within a k-means–GOA clustering framework to group different winding fault conditions directly from FRA signatures.

Some studies have also used image processing in combination with classification methods to analyze faults. In16, FRA traces were mapped to two-dimensional images, and a convolutional neural network (CNN) was trained for short circuit fault localization. Reference17 combined digital image processing with evidence theory for mechanical-fault detection and classification, while18 fused the magnitude and phase information of FRA responses to quantify faults via image processing.

To improve interpretability19, applied various machine-learning techniques together with an examination of significant indices for fault classification, utilizing data derived from 70 measurements. In another study20, several intelligent classifiers—including probabilistic neural network (PNN), decision tree (DT), support vector machine (SVM), and k-nearest neighbors (k-NN)—were used to classify fault types, and the performance of each method was evaluated for different fault categories. In21, a laboratory winding model was subjected to a range of electrical and mechanical faults; frequency-band sensitivities were analyzed using numerical indicators, and multiple classifiers were benchmarked. A similar line of work in22 partitioned FRA data into various bands and applied deep learning models. Reference23 combined numerical indicators with a random forest (RF) classifier to identify electrical and mechanical faults, using measurements from more than 80 transformers. In24, a laboratory setup together with a mathematical model was used to generate faults on a transformer, and an SOA-KELM classifier was trained, demonstrating superior performance compared with other ML-based classifiers. In another approach, an image-based classification method using the AlexNet deep-learning model is proposed in25. Simulated FRA data from two transformers under different mechanical deformation conditions are converted into image representations and, via transfer learning, are used to classify winding faults in a third real transformer.

Among these emerging approaches, graph signal processing (GSP) stands out as a promising methodology for analyzing FRA data. GSP extends traditional signal-processing techniques to signals defined on irregular graph structures, allowing FRA measurements to be represented as graphs via the weighted visibility graph (WVG) approach. Examining FRA data in the graph spectral domain enables the effective extraction of distinctive features while reducing data dimensionality26, which can significantly enhance the performance of classifiers used for transformer fault detection and classification. Moreover, due to the complexity of real transformer structures, many investigations rely on simplified single-winding models rather than full power-transformer implementations. In contrast, this study deliberately considers a large three-winding autotransformer that is widely used in the transmission network, so that the results are more representative for real high-voltage applications and not restricted to small-scale laboratory setups. Accordingly, this work addresses these gaps by developing a detailed model of a 125 MVA, 230/132/20 kV power autotransformer and validating it against measured FRA responses in the healthy condition. The simulation model incorporates all relevant electrical parameters extracted from measurements, manufacturer design data, and finite-element analysis. Winding fault scenarios involving AD, RD, and TTSC are then applied to this model with different severity levels. Physically imposing such faults on high-power units would be technically risky and economically impractical, making a validated model-based framework a more feasible and safe alternative.

In this work, a probabilistic Gaussian-process (GP) based diagnostic pipeline is developed to provide a comprehensive FRA assessment for both mechanical and electrical winding faults. After converting the FRA traces into a graph representation and extracting informative features, a Gaussian process classification (GPC) model is first employed for multi-class fault type identification among Healthy, AD, RD, and TTSC conditions. For the cases identified as TTSC, a second GPC model is then applied to localize the fault within the winding structure. Finally, fault-specific Gaussian process regression (GPR) models are used to estimate the fault severity. This GP framework enables interpretable and reliable decision-making by jointly addressing fault type, location (for TTSC), and severity within a unified probabilistic learning approach.

Classification results are analyzed in Section "Results and discussion", followed by conclusions in Section “Conclusion”.

Autotransformer modeling

Power transformers consist of multiple components, such as windings with various configurations and connections, core laminations, insulation systems, tanks, and mechanical supports, forming a complex structure. This structure creates an RLC network characterized by resistance (R), capacitance (C), and inductance (L) parameters, which depend significantly on the physical characteristics of the transformer. Consequently, electrical and mechanical faults can impact these RLC values, modifying the transformer’s behavior across different frequency ranges.

FRA involves applying a low-voltage signal with variable frequency, typically 20 Hz to 2 MHz, to the transformer windings and measuring both input and output signals at each frequency. The ratio of these signals produces the transfer function, containing amplitude and phase information. The transfer function at each frequency represents the effective impedance derived from the internal electrical parameters of the transformer. Hence, FRA can detect small physical changes within the transformer’s internal RLC parameter network.Fig. 1A simple, detailed model for a single phase of the autotransformer.

Development of the autotransformer model

In this study, we developed a detailed autotransformer model based on state-space equations, incorporating series capacitances (between turns and disks), parallel capacitances between sections, and both self and mutual inductances among all winding sections. Each phase of the autotransformer under investigation consists of three winding sections: the series winding (HV), the common winding (MV), and the tertiary winding (LV). The HV and MV windings are disc-type, while the LV winding has a layered structure composed of two layers, A and B. Consequently, each phase comprises four sections.

To achieve precise high-frequency modeling, the windings were subdivided into smaller segments, each represented by equivalent RLC circuits. For simulating mechanical deformations, a relatively small number of winding sections is sufficient to capture these defects. In contrast, inter-turn faults must be analyzed with a finer spatial resolution. Therefore, to model TTSC faults, the windings were segmented into 64 sections in one case and 82 sections in another case, based on the number of disks in the HV and MV windings. Autotransformer specifications

The studied autotransformer has an HV winding that combines simple and interleaved disk structures, with 64 disks and 535 turns per phase. The MV winding has a simple disk structure, comprising 82 disks and 720 turns. The LV winding is structured in two layers, totaling 189 turns. Model validation

To assess the model’s accuracy, it is essential to evaluate and validate the simulated frequency response. Figure 3 displays the FRA traces for the HV and MV windings of phase A of the autotransformer, recorded under healthy conditions using the end-to-end open-circuit configuration recommended in IEEE Std C57.1496. The measurements were performed with a commercial FRA test set over the frequency range 20 Hz–1 MHz, using 1024 logarithmically spaced frequency points. This figure illustrates that both traces exhibit similar positive and negative slope patterns, aligning well in terms of resonance and anti-resonance frequencies within the low-to-middle frequency range. Additionally, the characteristic double-peak feature (DPF), a prominent aspect of autotransformers28, is accurately represented in the simulated traces. To quantify the model’s accuracy, the correlation coefficient (CC) index was used, which primarily reflects variations in trace shapes and is recommended in6, together with the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE). The calculated CC values for the frequency responses of the HV and MV windings were 0.9788 and 0.9642, respectively, while the corresponding MAPE values were 3.8 % and 3.5 %. These values indicate a high level of agreement between the measured and simulated FRA traces and confirm the model’s capability in accurately reproducing the transformer’s frequency response.

It should be noted that FRA is fundamentally a comparative approach, with its primary modeling objective being to generate response signatures under various fault conditions to aid in fault interpretation and identification. Moreover, achieving a perfect match between simulated and measured responses is not practically possible due to several factors, including restricted access to detailed transformer design information due to manufacturer confidentiality, the inherent complexity of accurately modeling intricate winding configurations, insulation systems, mechanical assemblies, and additional influences such as aging and contamination of transformer insulation materials.

Fault simulation

In this section, simulations of AD, RD, and TTSC are conducted on the power autotransformer model, utilizing different intensities for each type of deformation fault. Then, the effect of each fault on the autotransformer FRA trace is investigated.

To simulate the deformations, a new FEM model with geometric modification for each condition was constructed and all parameters were recalculated using the electrostatic and magneto-static finite element solutions combined with analytical formulas. These updated parameters were subsequently integrated into the autotransformer model to compute the FRA trace for each deformation condition. These three fault mechanisms were deliberately selected because they constitute the most representative and diagnostically influential winding failure modes reported in FRA practice, capturing both mechanical deformation patterns and early-stage electrical insulation breakdowns that are known to initiate the majority of real transformer winding failures. This defect arises from the influence of two unequal axial electromagnetic force components acting on the winding29. Winding radial deformation (RD)

Radial deformation is induced by electromagnetic forces from circumferential currents circulating through the windings29. To explore the effects of the RD fault, the deformation is applied to the FEM model of the MV winding in phase A in 30 incremental steps, where the affected portion of the winding perimeter is gradually increased up to the entire circumference. For clarity, three representative severity levels are highlighted in this paper (RD1–RD3): one-quarter, one-half, and the entire perimeter of the MV winding.

When RD occurs in the MV winding, it causes an uneven alteration in the spacing between the windings. Specifically, in some areas, the gap between the HV and MV windings increases, while simultaneously, the distance between the MV and LV windings decreases. Consequently, the capacitance between the HV and MV windings diminishes, whereas it rises between the MV and LV windings. These changes in spacing also influence the mutual inductances among the windings. Proposed methodology

The primary objective of the proposed method is to utilize GSP techniques to analyze FRA data effectively, enabling accurate detection and classification of mechanical deformation faults in transformer windings. The procedure involves converting FRA data into a network of interconnected vertices (nodes) and edges (links), thereby enabling mathematical and computational methods to highlight the small changes in the FRA traces under different faults. The selection and construction of a right graph structure is a crucial initial step in GSP, since the definitions of frequency and node-domain operations depend on this choice. To extract the underlying structure of the FRA graph, this study adopts the WVG method to transform a one-dimensional FRA signal into a network that preserves the signal’s dynamics30. In this construction, each data point from the FRA trace is represented as a vertex, and edges are established according to visibility criteria among samples. Consequently, the WVG captures intrinsic topological characteristics of the FRA trace. A complete flowchart outlining these steps is provided in Fig. 8.Fig. 8The proposed FRA interpretation method.

In this study, after the measurement and calculation of the autotransformer model parameters and its construction, several winding defects with varying severities and locations were simulated to obtain FRA traces in both healthy and faulty states. Subsequently, different fault patterns were extracted via graph spectral analysis of these responses. Finally, pattern similarity was modeled and faults were directly inferred via a complete Gaussian-process framework, utilizing a kernel defined over the PSD feature vectors to assess fault types, severity, and its location. For mechanical faults, 30 samples were produced for each fault type, including AD and RD, covering a range of displacement magnitudes. For the electrical fault scenario, 100 samples corresponding to TTSC conditions were generated by varying both the fault location and the fault severity. Measured FRA responses under healthy conditions for the HV and MV windings were used as reference traces to validate the transformer model and to generate the simulated faulty FRA datasets.

The GP framework is a non-parametric, Bayesian approach to machine learning that defines a probabilistic distribution over functions. A GP is fully specified by a mean function, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m({\textbf {x}})$$\end{document}, and a kernel function, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k({\textbf {x}}, {\textbf {x}}')$$\end{document}, as follows34:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} f(\textbf{x}) \sim \mathscr{G}\mathscr{P}(m(\textbf{x}), k(\textbf{x}, \textbf{x}')). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The mean function, often assumed to be zero for simplicity, represents the expected value of the function. The kernel function is more critical, as it defines the similarity between any two input points (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textbf {x}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textbf {x}}'$$\end{document}), which in this study are the GDFT feature vectors. A common and flexible choice is the Squared Exponential (SE) kernel, also known as the Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel, as34:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} k(\textbf{x}_i, \textbf{x}_j) = \sigma _f^2 \exp \left( -\frac{||\textbf{x}_i - \textbf{x}_j||^2}{2l^2}\right) . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _f^2$$\end{document} (the signal variance) and l (the characteristic length-scale) are the kernel’s hyper-parameters. These are not set manually but are optimized during the training phase, typically by maximizing the marginal log-likelihood on the training data. This optimization process allows the model to learn the appropriate smoothness and scale of the function directly from the data. This GP framework can be adapted for both classification (for fault type and location identification) and regression (for fault severity estimation).

Based on this GP formulation, we design a diagnostic pipeline in which GPC is first employed for multi-class fault type identification (Healthy/AD/RD/TTSC). Then, fault severity is quantified using fault-specific GPR models (for AD, RD, and TTSC), and for the detected TTSC cases, an additional GPC model is further used to identify the fault location.

Fault type and location identification

Gaussian process classification (GPC) is employed in the proposed framework to perform probabilistic classification tasks, including (i) multi-class fault type identification and (ii) TTSC fault location identification. To this end, two separate GPC models are constructed: a Type-GPC model for fault type classification and a Location-GPC model that is activated only when TTSC is detected. Although these two models address different label spaces, they share the same probabilistic formulation and training procedure.

In GPC, a latent function is defined for each class and a GP prior is placed over these latent functions. If the most probable class is TTSC, the Location-GPC model is subsequently applied for TTSC location identification, where the location classes are defined as top, middle, bottom, or cross-winding. In both stages, the final predicted class is obtained by selecting the class c that maximizes the posterior probability. This probabilistic output improves interpretability by reporting not only the most likely decision (fault type or TTSC location) but also the associated confidence compared to alternative classes.

Results and discussion

To validate the efficacy of the proposed GSP–GP framework, the models were evaluated on a comprehensive test dataset that was kept separate from the training data. The performance of GPC in fault type identification and TTSC localization, as well as the performance of GPR in severity estimation, is reported separately.

As outlined in Table 4, methods such as CNNs require massive datasets, which are practically unattainable in scenarios relying on sparse field measurements and controlled physical models. Conversely, classical methods (SVM, RF) and numerical indices lack rigorous uncertainty quantification. Directly applying GP to raw FRA signals suffers from the curse of dimensionality and fails to capture the geometric properties of the frequency response. The proposed framework overcomes these limitations by utilizing graph signal processing to map the FRA trace into a lower-dimensional, structurally informative domain, enabling the GP to provide reliable, interpretable, and probabilistic diagnostic outputs even with moderately sized datasets. Comprehensive empirical benchmarking against these baselines remains a valuable direction for future head-to-head comparative studies.

Conclusion

This paper presented a physics-informed and data-driven diagnostic framework for autotransformer FRA interpretation, capable of identifying fault type, localizing TTSC faults, and estimating fault severity with quantified uncertainty. A validated RLC state-space model was used to reproduce the healthy FRA response and to generate consistent faulty datasets for mechanical deformations (AD and RD) and electrical TTSC faults. By converting FRA traces into weighted visibility graphs and extracting graph-domain spectral features, the proposed GP pipeline enabled probabilistic assessment through Type-GPC (Healthy/AD/RD/TTSC), Location-GPC for TTSC localization, and fault-specific GPR models for severity estimation. The method achieved 92.9 % accuracy in fault type identification and 92.0 % accuracy in TTSC localization. Moreover, the severity estimators provided low prediction errors, with MAE/RMSE values of 1.00 %/1.34 % for AD/RD and 5.30 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega$$\end{document}/5.91 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega$$\end{document} for TTSC, while also delivering confidence intervals that improve interpretability for maintenance decisions. Future work will focus on extending the fault library and further validating the proposed framework using FRA measurements from transformers with documented winding faults. In the present study, fault scenarios were generated through simulations applied to a validated transformer model, which represents a practical limitation of transformer fault experimentation. Future investigations will therefore aim to incorporate real faulty transformer data and evaluate the proposed approach under a wider range of measurement conditions and transformer configurations.
