WaveMamba-Net: Dual-frequency adaptive wavelet state-space network for real-time pulse signal classification in wearable health monitoring
Authors: Jiajia Wu, Yanyan Bai, Liuliu Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryContinuous cardiovascular monitoring via wearable devices is critical for early disease detection, yet existing pulse signal analysis methods struggle to achieve both high accuracy and real-time performance under noisy, imbalanced conditions. We propose WaveMamba-Net, a deep learning framework integrating wavelet-based multi-scale decomposition with state-space modeling for patient status classification from photoplethysmography signals. Discrete wavelet transform decomposes pulse signals into frequency-specific components, processed by dual-view Wavelet Temporal and Spatial Sliding Enhance Modules capturing interperiod rhythm and intraperiod morphological features. Selective state-space Mamba blocks enable efficient long-range dependency modeling, while Multi-Wavelet Condition Convolution adaptively integrates decomposition levels via input-dependent kernel modulation. Evaluated on Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III (MIMIC-III) Waveform Database and PPG-DaLiA Dataset, WaveMamba-Net achieves 91.84% accuracy and 87.52% Macro-F1 for arrhythmia classification, and 88.26% accuracy with 86.91% Macro-F1 for stress assessment, outperforming nine baselines with 12 ms inference latency suitable for edge deployment.

Introduction

The global healthcare landscape is experiencing a fundamental transformation driven by the convergence of wearable sensing technologies and artificial intelligence.1,2 Cardiovascular diseases remain the leading cause of mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 17.9 million deaths annually, which underscores the critical need for continuous, accessible patient monitoring systems.3,4 Traditional clinical assessment methods, while accurate, are constrained by their episodic nature and inability to capture the dynamic physiological changes that occur throughout daily life.5 Wearable devices have emerged as a promising solution to bridge this gap, offering the potential for real-time health surveillance in non-clinical settings. Among various physiological signals, the pulse signal provides a particularly rich information source,6 reflecting not only cardiac function but also offering insights into vascular health, autonomic nervous system activity, and overall hemodynamic status.

Despite the proliferation of commercial wearable devices capable of monitoring heart rate and related metrics, current systems face significant limitations that restrict their clinical utility and adoption in medical decision-making.7 Most existing devices focus on relatively simple measurements such as heart rate and step counting, failing to extract the deeper physiological information embedded within pulse waveform morphology.8 Furthermore, the majority of these systems operate as passive data collectors, transmitting raw measurements to cloud servers or smartphone applications without providing immediate, actionable feedback to patients or healthcare providers.9,10 This limitation is particularly problematic in scenarios requiring timely intervention, such as detecting arrhythmias, monitoring post-surgical recovery, or managing chronic cardiovascular conditions.11 The disconnect between data collection and real-time clinical response represents a critical barrier to the integration of wearable technology into mainstream healthcare practice. The challenge of transforming raw pulse signals into clinically meaningful insights in real-time requires addressing several technical and methodological complexities.12,13 Pulse signals captured by wearable devices are inherently susceptible to motion artifacts, variations in sensor contact pressure, and individual physiological differences, all of which can compromise signal quality and interpretation accuracy.14 Traditional signal processing approaches often rely on handcrafted features and threshold-based detection algorithms, which struggle to generalize across diverse patient populations and varying environmental conditions.15,16 Machine learning techniques have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in pattern recognition and predictive modeling across medical domains, yet their application to real-time wearable health monitoring remains underexplored.17,18 The fundamental question becomes how to leverage advanced computational methods to extract reliable diagnostic information from noisy, continuous pulse signals while maintaining the computational efficiency necessary for edge device implementation.19,20

Despite the remarkable success of structured state-space models—particularly the Mamba architecture21—in sequence modeling, their direct application to physiological biosignals such as photoplethysmography (PPG) reveals several non-trivial limitations. Standard state-space model (SSM) formulations process input at a uniform temporal resolution, with state-transition matrices operating identically across all time-steps. This is poorly suited to cardiovascular signals, which are inherently multi-scale: low-frequency components (below 1 Hz) encode hemodynamic trends and respiratory modulation, while high-frequency components (1–30 Hz) carry the morphological micro-structure—systolic peaks, dicrotic notches, and arrhythmia-discriminative fine variations—most critical for clinical classification. Moreover, although Mamba’s selective-scan mechanism enables input-dependent parameter adaptation, this selectivity operates exclusively in the time domain and lacks explicit frequency-aware gating. Wearable PPG recordings are further complicated by non-stationary motion artifacts whose spectral content overlaps substantially with physiologically relevant features, making it difficult for a purely time-domain SSM to distinguish artifact-induced energy bursts from genuine pathological deviations such as ectopic beats. Finally, prior SSM-based approaches to biosignal analysis adopt single-stream architectures that discard a fundamental structural duality in pulse waveforms: the interperiod dimension—capturing rhythm regularity and autonomic modulation across successive cardiac cycles—and the intraperiod dimension—encoding individual waveform morphology reflective of vascular compliance and myocardial function.

These three limitations—uniform temporal resolution, frequency-agnostic selectivity, and single-view processing—jointly motivate the Dual-Frequency Adaptive Temporal-Spatial Wavelet Mamba (DFATS-WaveMamba) framework proposed in this work. We first decompose the PPG signal into frequency-specific sub-bands via the discrete wavelet transform (DWT), aligning the model’s inductive bias with the physiological structure of cardiovascular dynamics prior to any learnable processing. A subsequent dual-view architecture then dedicates separate Mamba-based pathways to interperiod temporal dynamics and intraperiod spatial morphology, enabling complementary feature extraction that single-stream SSMs fundamentally cannot achieve. In response to these challenges, we propose an intelligent wearable wristband system that integrates pulse signal sensing with real-time machine learning-based patient status assessment and feedback mechanisms in Figure 1. Our approach employs a multi-stage framework that combines adaptive signal preprocessing, feature extraction from pulse waveform characteristics, and a lightweight machine learning model optimized for on-device inference. Unlike conventional systems that depend on cloud connectivity and post-processing analysis, our device architecture enables immediate patient status evaluation and provides direct feedback through the wearable terminal. This design philosophy addresses the critical need for autonomous, real-time health monitoring systems that can function independently in various settings, including remote areas with limited network infrastructure. Overall architecture of WaveMamba-Net for pulse signal classification

The framework consists of four primary stages: (1) wavelet-based signal decomposition separating low-frequency and high-frequency components through 1D-DWT, and 2D-DWT for temporal and spatial views respectively; (2) energy-oriented high-frequency picking module with element-wise energy calculation and softmax-based attention for diagnostic feature enhancement; (3) dual-view feature extraction through Wavelet Temporal Sliding Enhance Module (WTSEM) capturing interperiod variations and Wavelet Spatial Sliding Enhance Module (WSSEM) capturing intraperiod morphology, both processed through stacked Mamba blocks for efficient state-space modeling; (4) Multi-Wavelet Condition Convolution (MWCC) for adaptive frequency integration with Hadamard product and gating mechanism, culminating in classification through dense layers with dropout regularization. Residual connections preserve information flow throughout the network hierarchy.

The technical architecture of our proposed system is built upon a novel Dual-Frequency Adaptive Temporal-Spatial Wavelet Mamba (WaveMamba-Net) framework, specifically designed to address the challenges of real-time pulse signal analysis in resource-constrained wearable devices. The detailed architecture is illustrated in Figure 2. Central to our approach is the integration of DWT with state-space models to simultaneously capture both temporal dynamics and spatial characteristics of pulse waveforms. The notation and symbols used throughout the methodology are summarized in Table 1. The system employs a one-dimensional (1D) DWT as the initial processing stage, decomposing raw pulse signals into low-frequency and high-frequency components that respectively represent baseline physiological trends and fine-grained variations critical for detecting subtle abnormalities. This frequency-domain decomposition enables our model to selectively focus on diagnostically relevant signal components while suppressing noise artifacts that commonly plague wearable sensor data. The wavelet coefficients then feed into parallel processing pathways consisting of Wavelet Temporal Sliding Enhance Modules (WTSEMs) and Wavelet Spatial Sliding Enhance Modules (WSSEMs), which employ sliding window operations combined with energy-oriented high-frequency picking mechanisms to identify and amplify physiologically significant signal segments. These enhancement modules utilize element-wise energy calculation and softmax-based attention mechanisms to dynamically weight temporal variations within cardiac cycles and spatial patterns across different pulse measurement locations. The core computational engine comprises multiple Mamba blocks, which provide efficient long-range dependency modeling essential for capturing the sequential nature of cardiovascular dynamics while maintaining computational efficiency suitable for edge deployment. The dual-view architecture processes both temporal variations between consecutive cardiac cycles and spatial relationships within individual pulse waveforms, enabling comprehensive feature extraction that captures both interperiod and intraperiod variations. A Multi-Wavelet Condition Convolution module further refines these representations through learnable convolutional kernels conditioned on wavelet coefficients, adaptively adjusting feature extraction based on signal frequency characteristics. The integration pathway employs residual connections and Hadamard products to merge information from low-frequency and high-frequency branches, preserving both global trends and local details throughout the network hierarchy. Results

The detailed hyperparameter configuration is provided in Table 2. Table 3 presents the comprehensive evaluation results of our proposed WaveMamba-Net against nine baseline methods across both MIMIC-III and PPG-DaLiA datasets. The results demonstrate that WaveMamba-Net consistently achieves superior performance across all evaluation metrics, establishing new state-of-the-art results for pulse signal-based patient status classification. On the MIMIC-III dataset, our method attains an accuracy of 91.84%, which represents a substantial improvement of 3.69 percentage points over the previous best baseline Multivariate Long Short-Term Memory Fully Convolutional Network (MLSTM-FCN). More importantly, the Macro-F1 score reaches 87.52%, surpassing MLSTM-FCN by 4.26 percentage points, which is particularly significant given that this metric equally weights performance across all classes including rare but clinically critical arrhythmias. The Cohen’s Kappa coefficient of 0.893 indicates strong agreement beyond chance, substantially exceeding the 0.8 threshold for clinical reliability. The performance gains are even more pronounced when examining the class imbalance scenario inherent in the MIMIC-III dataset. Traditional machine learning methods such as support vector machine (SVM) and Random Forest achieve reasonable overall accuracy (72.35% and 74.68% respectively) but suffer significantly on the Macro-F1 metric (58.42% and 61.27%), revealing their inability to effectively handle minority classes despite careful feature engineering. This discrepancy between accuracy and Macro-F1 indicates that these methods predominantly succeed on the majority normal sinus rhythm class while failing to capture the subtle patterns distinguishing rare arrhythmias. Deep learning baselines show marked improvement, with 1D-convolutional neural network (CNN) achieving 81.24% accuracy and 72.15% Macro-F1, demonstrating that learned representations outperform handcrafted features. The introduction of temporal modeling through long short-term memory network (LSTM) further improves performance to 83.47% accuracy and 75.63% Macro-F1, confirming the importance of capturing sequential dependencies in cardiovascular signals. Temporal convolutional network (TCN) and Transformer architectures continue this trend, reaching 84.92% and 85.63% accuracy respectively, with TCN showing particular effectiveness through its dilated convolutions that efficiently capture multi-scale temporal patterns.

Among the state-of-the-art time series models, InceptionTime, TST, and MLSTM-FCN represent the strongest baselines, leveraging sophisticated architectural designs specifically tailored for temporal data. InceptionTime’s multi-scale convolutional modules achieve 86.28% accuracy by processing signals at multiple temporal resolutions simultaneously, while TST’s enhanced attention mechanisms with learnable positional encodings reach 87.42% accuracy by better capturing long-range dependencies. MLSTM-FCN’s hybrid architecture combining recurrent and convolutional pathways proves most competitive among baselines, attaining 88.15% accuracy and 83.26% Macro-F1. However, WaveMamba-Net’s 3.69% accuracy improvement and 4.26% Macro-F1 improvement over MLSTM-FCN highlight the advantages of our wavelet-based frequency decomposition coupled with efficient state-space modeling. On the PPG-DaLiA dataset, which presents different challenges including motion artifacts from daily activities and inter-subject variability, WaveMamba-Net again demonstrates superior robustness with 88.26% accuracy and 86.91% Macro-F1. The 3.53% accuracy improvement and 4.23% Macro-F1 improvement over MLSTM-FCN are comparable to those observed on MIMIC-III, indicating that our method’s advantages generalize across different data distributions and classification tasks. The Cohen’s Kappa of 0.843 and AUROC of 0.947 further confirm strong performance. Notably, the absolute performance values on PPG-DaLiA are lower than MIMIC-III across all methods, reflecting the inherent difficulty of this dataset due to motion artifacts and the physiological stress classification task requiring more subtle pattern recognition compared to overt arrhythmia detection. Traditional machine learning methods struggle more severely on PPG-DaLiA, with SVM and Random Forest achieving only 68.12% and 70.45% accuracy respectively, and their Macro-F1 scores barely exceeding 65%, highlighting the limitations of handcrafted features when dealing with noisy, motion-contaminated signals.

The progressive improvement from simple deep learning architectures to sophisticated state-of-the-art models follows a similar trajectory on PPG-DaLiA as on MIMIC-III. The 1D-CNN baseline achieves 76.82% accuracy, establishing that learned convolutional features provide substantial robustness to motion artifacts compared to traditional features. LSTM’s bidirectional processing improves accuracy to 78.95%, while TCN’s dilated causal convolutions reach 80.34%, demonstrating the value of efficient long-range temporal modeling for capturing stress-related heart rate variability patterns across extended signal segments. The Transformer architecture achieves 81.27% accuracy, though its advantage over TCN is more modest on PPG-DaLiA than on MIMIC-III, possibly because the self-attention mechanism’s global receptive field is less critical for the shorter, more localized patterns characteristic of stress-induced physiological changes. Among advanced baselines, MLSTM-FCN’s 84.73% accuracy represents the strongest competition, yet WaveMamba-Net’s 88.26% accuracy demonstrates a clear advantage.

The standard deviations reported across five independent runs reveal important insights about model stability and reliability. WaveMamba-Net consistently exhibits lower variance compared to baseline methods, with standard deviations of 0.39% and 0.58% for accuracy on MIMIC-III and PPG-DaLiA respectively. This reduced variance is even more pronounced for Macro-F1 scores (0.64% and 0.76% standard deviation), Cohen’s Kappa (0.005 and 0.008), and AUROC (0.003 and 0.005). In contrast, the best-performing baseline MLSTM-FCN shows higher variance with accuracy standard deviations of 0.46% and 0.71%, while earlier baselines like SVM and Random Forest exhibit substantially higher variance exceeding 1.0% in many cases. This improved stability suggests that WaveMamba-Net’s wavelet-based decomposition and state-space modeling provide more consistent feature extraction and learning dynamics, reducing sensitivity to random initialization and training data ordering in Figure 4. The most substantial performance degradation occurs when removing the Mamba blocks entirely, replacing them with standard feedforward layers while maintaining the same parameter budget. This variant achieves only 85.71% accuracy and 79.48% Macro-F1 on MIMIC-III, representing drops of 6.13 percentage points and 8.04 percentage points respectively compared to the full model. On PPG-DaLiA, the performance similarly degrades to 81.82% accuracy and 78.36% Macro-F1, with drops of 6.44 percentage points and 8.55 percentage points. These dramatic reductions demonstrate that the selective state-space modeling capability of Mamba blocks is fundamental to our architecture’s success. The Mamba mechanism’s ability to dynamically adjust its receptive field based on input characteristics proves essential for capturing the variable-length temporal patterns inherent in cardiovascular signals, where critical diagnostic features may span different time scales ranging from individual heartbeat morphology to inter-beat variability patterns. The linear computational complexity of Mamba blocks, achieved through efficient state-space formulations, enables processing of longer signal segments without the quadratic memory and computation costs associated with standard attention mechanisms, which becomes particularly important when analyzing continuous pulse recordings from wearable devices.

Removing the wavelet decomposition component and directly processing raw signals with the dual-view architecture results in 87.23% accuracy and 81.34% Macro-F1 on MIMIC-III, representing performance drops of 4.61 percentage points and 6.18 percentage points in Figure 5. The degradation is similarly pronounced on PPG-DaLiA with 83.47% accuracy and 80.15% Macro-F1, showing drops of 4.79 percentage points and 6.76 percentage points. These results confirm that explicit frequency decomposition provides substantial benefits beyond what the neural network can learn implicitly from raw signals. The wavelet transform’s ability to separate low-frequency baseline trends from high-frequency morphological details aligns well with the physiological reality that different cardiovascular phenomena manifest at distinct frequency bands. Baseline wander and respiratory influences dominate low frequencies, while fine-grained pulse waveform features such as dicrotic notches and systolic peaks reside in higher frequencies. By decomposing signals into these components before neural processing, we enable the model to apply specialized processing strategies to each frequency band, effectively increasing the model’s inductive bias toward physiologically meaningful representations. The WTSEM and WSSEM contribute 2.92 percentage points and 2.50 percentage points in accuracy on MIMIC-III respectively, with the temporal module showing slightly larger impact. This pattern reflects the inherently temporal nature of pulse signals where sequential dependencies across consecutive heartbeats carry important diagnostic information about rhythm regularity and heart rate variability. The WTSEM’s sliding window attention mechanism captures interperiod variations by aggregating context across neighboring cardiac cycles, enabling detection of subtle rhythm disturbances that manifest as abnormal beat-to-beat relationships. Conversely, the WSSEM focuses on intraperiod variations within individual pulse waveforms, capturing morphological features such as pulse amplitude, rise time, and waveform shape that reflect vascular compliance and cardiac contractility. On PPG-DaLiA, the spatial module shows relatively stronger contribution (3.18 percentage points versus 3.28 percentage points), which aligns with the stress classification task’s greater dependence on subtle waveform morphology changes associated with autonomic nervous system modulation. The complementary nature of these two modules is further evidenced by their non-additive contribution: removing both simultaneously results in greater performance degradation than the sum of individual removals, indicating synergistic interaction between temporal and spatial enhancement pathways.

Concatenation Fusion retains both branches but merges their outputs via linear projection of the concatenated features, replacing the Hadamard interaction. Hadamard without Gate retains the element-wise interaction but removes the sigmoid gate, fixing the balance between temporal and spatial contributions. Temporal only (the existing single-view variant) removes the spatial branch entirely. The three variants yield 90.13%, 90.89%, and 89.47% accuracy on MIMIC-III respectively, compared to 91.84% for the full model. The monotonic improvement across this chain confirms that all three design decisions—dual branching, Hadamard interaction, and adaptive gating—each contribute independently, with the gate alone accounting for 0.95 percentage points. Processing temporal information alone achieves 89.47% accuracy and 84.91% Macro-F1 on MIMIC-III, substantially outperforming spatial-only processing, which reaches 87.68% accuracy and 81.92% Macro-F1. This gap of 1.79 percentage points in accuracy confirms that temporal dynamics dominate pulse signal classification, particularly for arrhythmia detection where rhythm irregularities manifest primarily as temporal pattern disruptions. However, the full dual-view model’s 91.84% accuracy exceeds even temporal-only processing by 2.37 percentage points, demonstrating that spatial morphological features provide non-redundant complementary information. The spatial pathway’s ability to capture waveform shape variations proves especially valuable for distinguishing between arrhythmia types with similar temporal patterns but different underlying hemodynamic mechanisms. On PPG-DaLiA, the gap between temporal-only (86.12%) and spatial-only (83.25%) narrows to 2.87 percentage points, reflecting stress classification’s greater dependence on subtle morphological features compared to overt temporal irregularities. The fusion mechanism’s gated architecture, which dynamically balances temporal and spatial contributions based on input characteristics, enables the model to adaptively emphasize the most informative view for each specific signal segment.

Discussion

The experimental results and ablation studies collectively demonstrate that WaveMamba-Net achieves state-of-the-art performance for pulse signal-based patient status classification through the synergistic integration of wavelet-based frequency decomposition and efficient state-space modeling. The consistent superiority across multiple evaluation metrics and datasets validates our core hypothesis that explicit multi-scale signal decomposition combined with adaptive temporal-spatial feature extraction provides fundamental advantages over end-to-end learning approaches that process raw signals directly. The wavelet transform’s ability to separate physiologically meaningful frequency components aligns the model’s inductive bias with domain knowledge about cardiovascular signal characteristics, enabling more sample-efficient learning particularly for rare but clinically critical conditions. The Mamba blocks’ selective state-space mechanism proves especially effective for pulse signals, where diagnostic patterns span variable time scales and the ability to dynamically adjust receptive fields based on signal characteristics becomes crucial. The dual-view architecture’s complementary processing of temporal dynamics and spatial morphology reflects the multifaceted nature of cardiovascular information, where rhythm irregularities and waveform shape variations provide independent diagnostic evidence that must be jointly considered for accurate assessment.

This paper presents WaveMamba-Net, a novel deep learning framework for real-time pulse signal analysis in wearable health monitoring devices. By integrating wavelet-based multi-scale decomposition with efficient state-space modeling through Mamba blocks and dual-view temporal-spatial processing, our approach achieves significant performance improvements over existing methods. Extensive experiments on MIMIC-III and PPG-DaLiA datasets demonstrate state-of-the-art results with 91.84% accuracy and 87.52% Macro-F1 on arrhythmia classification, and 88.26% accuracy with 86.91% Macro-F1 on stress level assessment, substantially outperforming nine competitive baseline methods. Comprehensive ablation studies validate the contribution of each architectural component, confirming that wavelet decomposition, energy-oriented enhancement, adaptive sliding attention mechanisms, and state-space modeling synergistically enable robust feature extraction from noisy physiological signals. The framework’s linear computational complexity and superior performance on imbalanced datasets make it particularly suitable for clinical deployment in wearable devices, enabling continuous patient monitoring with real-time feedback. Future work will focus on multi-modal sensor fusion, model compression for edge deployment, and extension to broader cardiovascular assessment tasks, advancing toward comprehensive AI-powered personalized health monitoring systems.

Limitations of the study

Despite these promising results, several limitations warrant discussion and suggest directions for future work. The model’s performance degradation under severe motion artifacts, while partially mitigated through data augmentation, indicates that explicit artifact detection and rejection mechanisms could provide additional robustness. Incorporating multi-modal sensor data such as accelerometer readings for motion compensation or developing adversarial training strategies with more diverse artifact patterns represents a promising avenue for improvement. The computational efficiency of WaveMamba-Net, while superior to transformer-based approaches due to Mamba’s linear complexity, still requires approximately 12 ms per inference on current hardware, which may pose challenges for ultra-low-power wearable devices with strict energy budgets. Future research should explore model compression techniques including knowledge distillation, pruning, and quantization to enable deployment on resource-constrained embedded systems. Additionally, while our current framework focuses on classification tasks, extending the architecture to support regression tasks such as continuous blood pressure estimation, or multi-task learning frameworks that jointly predict multiple physiological parameters could enhance clinical utility. The integration of uncertainty quantification mechanisms beyond simple softmax probabilities, such as evidential deep learning or Bayesian neural networks, would provide more reliable confidence estimates crucial for safety-critical medical applications where knowing when the model is uncertain is as important as the predictions themselves.

Method details

System architecture overview

Our proposed intelligent pulse monitoring system, termed WaveMamba-Net (Wavelet-enhanced Mamba Network for pulse analysis), integrates advanced signal processing with efficient deep learning architectures to enable real-time physiological assessment on wearable devices. Our framework comprises four primary components: wavelet-based signal decomposition, dual-view feature extraction, adaptive frequency-aware enhancement, and hierarchical state-space modeling. The overall pipeline transforms raw pulse signals x∈RT into diagnostic predictions yˆ through a series of learnable transformations preserve both temporal coherence and spatial morphological characteristics.22

Data preprocessing

Pulse signals acquired from photoplethysmography (PPG) sensors inherently contain multi-scale information spanning different physiological processes in Figure 2. We employ the discrete wavelet transform (DWT) as the foundational preprocessing stage to decompose input signals into frequency-specific subbands.23 For a given pulse signal segment x(t), the one-dimensional DWT decomposes it into approximation coefficients aj and detail coefficients dj at decomposition level j:(Equation 1)aj[n]=∑kh[k−2n]⋅aj−1[k],dj[n]=∑kg[k−2n]⋅aj−1[k],where h[k] and g[k] represent the low-pass and high-pass decomposition filters respectively, with a0 = x. The approximation coefficients aj capture low-frequency components reflecting baseline cardiovascular trends, while detail coefficients dj contain high-frequency variations corresponding to pulse waveform fine structure and potential arrhythmic events.

We implement a three-level decomposition to obtain multi-resolution representations. The energy distribution across frequency bands is quantified as:(Equation 2)Ej=∑n|dj[n]|2,Etotal=∑j=1JEj+‖aJ‖2

The relative energy ratio ρj = Ej/Etotal provides a quantitative measure of signal power distribution, enabling adaptive processing based on frequency content characteristics.

Several signal decomposition frameworks are available for PPG analysis, including Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD)24 and its ensemble variant (EEMD), Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT)-based spectrograms, and learnable filterbank approaches. The DWT is preferred over these alternatives for four interconnected reasons. First, using a compactly supported orthogonal wavelet (Daubechies-4 in our implementation), the DWT yields a complete, non-redundant decomposition in which approximation and detail coefficients exactly reconstruct the original signal with no information loss, and subband energies are strictly additive—making the energy ratio ρj = Ej/Etotal a well-defined, interpretable quantity. EMD, by contrast, produces non-orthogonal intrinsic mode functions with ambiguous energy attribution, complicating the energy-oriented attention design of our EOHFP module. Second, the DWT runs in O(T) time via the fast Mallat algorithm, whereas EEMD incurs O(T2) complexity per mode and requires hundreds of ensemble realisations to suppress mode-mixing—approximately 40× more computation than DWT for a 30-second segment at 125 Hz, rendering it incompatible with edge-deployment inference constraints.

Third, the Daubechies filters are fixed and differentiable, allowing the DWT to be implemented as a parameter-free convolutional layer through which gradients flow cleanly into upstream embedding layers. EMD’s iterative, data-driven sifting procedure is not differentiable with respect to its decomposition output, precluding end-to-end training of any gating mechanism conditioned on its coefficients. Fourth, the dyadic frequency bands of the DWT align naturally with the known spectral structure of PPG signals: the approximation subband covers the VLF/LF haemodynamic baseline (0–1 Hz), the first detail level captures the fundamental cardiac frequency and its harmonics (1–4 Hz), and higher detail levels isolate fine morphological features and motion artifacts (4–15 Hz). This physiological alignment is inherent to the DWT’s fixed basis and does not require empirical re-calibration across datasets, in contrast to data-adaptive methods such as EMD whose mode boundaries shift with signal statistics and thus reduce robustness to inter-individual variation.

Model selection and implementation

Before detailing the mathematical operations, it is worth clarifying the physiological rationale for converting the one-dimensional PPG sequence into a two-dimensional matrix representation. A PPG signal recorded at sampling frequency fs exhibits a quasi-periodic structure governed by the cardiac cycle. When a segment of length T is reshaped into a matrix X∈RH×W—where each row spans approximately one cardiac cycle (H ≈ fs/f0, with f0 the nominal heart rate) and each column indexes successive heartbeats—the resulting layout places intraperiod morphology along the row axis and interperiod temporal variation along the column axis. This construction is analogous to the lag-matrix and Hankel-matrix representations widely used in nonlinear dynamics and recurrence quantification analysis,25 and makes the periodic relatedness between samples explicit in the array geometry before any learnable computation occurs—encoding a strong physiological inductive bias that a purely 1D sequential model must otherwise recover from data alone.

The key advantage of this 2D representation is that it renders the two orthogonal sources of cardiovascular variation simultaneously accessible to a 2D discrete wavelet transform (2D-DWT). The LL subband retains the global haemodynamic baseline and coarse waveform shape; the LH subband captures slow beat-to-beat morphological changes such as those induced by respiratory sinus arrhythmia; the HL subband highlights sharp intraperiod transitions including the systolic upstroke and dicrotic notch; and the HH subband isolates localised transient deviations characteristic of arrhythmic events and motion artifacts. Together, these subbands decompose physiological structure that is largely invisible to 1D operators, which must scan the signal as a flat time series without explicit awareness of cycle boundaries. The row and column dimensions of X are not geometric in nature, but constitute a genuine structured dual-axis representation grounded in cardiovascular periodicity—with H and W determined adaptively per segment via an initial peak-detection step to align matrix boundaries as closely as possible with true cardiac cycle boundaries.

Following wavelet decomposition, we construct dual-stream embeddings to process temporal and spatial views independently. For the temporal stream, we apply a sliding window operation with window size w and stride s to segment the signal into overlapping patches {pi}i=1N, where N = ⌊(T − w)/s⌋ + 1. Each patch pi∈Rw is projected into a D-dimensional embedding space through a linear transformation:(Equation 3)eitemp=Wembedpi+bembed,Wembed∈RD×w

To preserve positional information crucial for temporal ordering, we incorporate learnable positional encodings PEi:(Equation 4)zitemp=eitemp+PEi

For the spatial stream, we apply two-dimensional DWT (2D-DWT) to the reshaped signal representation X∈RH×W obtained through reorganization operations. The 2D-DWT generates four subbands: LL (approximation), LH (horizontal details), HL (vertical details), and HH (diagonal details):(Equation 5){LL,LH,HL,HH}=DWT2D(X)

These subbands are concatenated and embedded into spatial representations zspat∈RN′×D through convolutional operations that preserve local spatial correlations.

High-frequency components contain critical diagnostic information but are often contaminated with motion artifacts and sensor noise. We design an energy-oriented selection mechanism to identify and amplify physiologically relevant high-frequency features. For the detail coefficients at each decomposition level dj, we compute element-wise energy:(Equation 6)E[n]=|dj[n]|2+ϵwhere ϵ is a small constant for numerical stability. The energy distribution is normalized through softmax operation to obtain attention weights:(Equation 7)α[n]=exp(E[n])∑mexp(E[m])

A natural concern with energy-based attention in wearable PPG settings is whether high-energy coefficients correspond to diagnostically relevant features or to motion-artifact contamination. We address this concern on three complementary levels: architectural design, training regularisation, and empirical validation.

Structured energy attention versus broadband artifact energy

Motion artifacts in wrist-worn PPG sensors typically manifest as broadband, temporally irregular energy bursts that span multiple wavelet detail levels simultaneously.27 In contrast, physiologically meaningful high-frequency features—such as the dicrotic notch, the systolic peak gradient, and ectopic-beat morphological distortions—exhibit level-specific, quasi-periodic energy concentrations that repeat with the cardiac rhythm. The softmax normalisation operates independently within each decomposition level j, so the attention weights α(j)[・] form a relative distribution across the temporal positions within level j rather than a cross-level comparison. As a result, a broadband artifact that simultaneously elevates energy across levels j = 1, 2, 3 will produce moderate attention in each level separately, whereas a diagnostically significant morphological feature confined to a specific level will generate sharply peaked attention at that level. Furthermore, the gated enhancement applies a sigmoid-activated linear transformation Wgate to the attention-weighted coefficients; this gating layer is learned end-to-end and acquires the ability to suppress non-discriminative energy patterns during training.

To further discourage the module from fixating on artifact-induced energy peaks, we introduce a periodicity consistency loss Lper that penalises attention-weight sequences whose autocorrelation profile deviates from that expected of a quasi-periodic cardiac signal. A single ectopic beat in a 30-second window produces a kurtosis value significantly elevated above the Gaussian baseline (κj ≈ 0), even when the standard deviation remains only modestly increased. Experimental design and hyperparameter tuning

The proposed WaveMamba-Net is implemented in PyTorch with the following architectural specifications: embedding dimension D = 256, number of Mamba blocks L = 6, sliding window size w = 128 samples, stride s = 64 samples, and dropout probability p = 0.3. The wavelet decomposition employs Daubechies-4 (db4) wavelets with three decomposition levels. For training, we use batch size B = 32, initial learning rate ηmax = 1 × 10−3, weight decay λ = 1 × 10−4, and center loss weight α = 0.5. The model is trained for 100 epochs with early stopping based on validation loss. Data augmentation includes random amplitude scaling within [0.8, 1.2], time warping with warping parameter σ = 0.2, and additive Gaussian noise with signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) ranging from 15 to 25 dB. All experiments are conducted on NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs with mixed-precision training enabled for computational efficiency.

All experiments are implemented using PyTorch 2.1.0 framework with CUDA 12.1 on a computing platform equipped with NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs (24GB memory). We employ mixed-precision training with automatic mixed precision (AMP) to accelerate computation while maintaining numerical stability. The model architecture follows the specifications detailed in the STAR Methods section with minor adjustments for dataset-specific characteristics.

Network configuration

The embedding dimension is set to D = 256 for both temporal and spatial streams. We stack L = 6 Mamba blocks in each processing pathway, with each block containing a state-space dimension of 16. The sliding window size for patch extraction is configured as w = 128 samples with stride s = 64 samples for MIMIC-III (125 Hz sampling rate) and w = 64 samples with stride s = 32 samples for PPG-DaLiA (64 Hz sampling rate), ensuring approximately 1-second temporal coverage per patch. The wavelet decomposition employs Daubechies-4 (db4) wavelets with J = 3 decomposition levels, which effectively separates baseline cardiovascular trends from high-frequency morphological details. For the Multi-Wavelet Condition Convolution module, we use kernel size k = 5 with 128 output channels. The classification head consists of two fully connected layers with dimensions [256, 128] and [128, C], where C is the number of target classes in Table 2.

Training protocol

We train the model for a maximum of 100 epochs with early stopping patience of 15 epochs based on validation loss. The batch size is set to B = 32 for MIMIC-III and B = 16 for PPG-DaLiA due to longer signal segments. We employ the AdamW optimizer with initial learning rate ηmax = 1 × 10−3, weight decay λ = 1 × 10−4, and momentum parameters β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999. The learning rate follows a cosine annealing schedule with warm restarts every 30 epochs, decaying to ηmin = 1 × 10−6. A linear warmup phase of 5 epochs is applied at the beginning of training. The dropout probability is set to p = 0.3 for regularization. The composite loss function weights are configured as α = 0.5 for center loss and λ = 1 × 10−4 for L2 regularization. To address class imbalance in MIMIC-III, we apply focal loss with focusing parameter γ = 2.0 instead of standard cross-entropy.

Data augmentation

During training, we apply several augmentation techniques to improve model robustness. Random amplitude scaling uniformly samples scaling factors from [0.85, 1.15] to simulate sensor gain variations. Time warping applies smooth random distortions with warping parameter σ = 0.2 using cubic spline interpolation. Additive Gaussian noise is injected with signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) randomly sampled from 15 to 25 dB to emulate real-world sensor noise. For PPG-DaLiA, we additionally apply random baseline wander by adding low-frequency sinusoidal components with frequencies between 0.1 and 0.5 Hz to simulate motion artifacts. Each training sample undergoes augmentation with probability 0.6.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Performance evaluation metrics

We employ four comprehensive metrics to assess classification performance from different perspectives, addressing both overall accuracy and class-specific effectiveness in imbalanced scenarios.

Accuracy (ACC)

Accuracy measures the proportion of correctly classified samples among all predictions, providing a straightforward assessment of overall model performance. It is computed as:(Equation 37)ACC=∑i=1N⊮(yˆi=yi)N=TP+TNTP+TN+FP+FN,where N is the total number of test samples, ⊮(⋅) is the indicator function, yˆi and yi denote predicted and ground-truth labels respectively, and TP, TN, FP, FN represent true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives. While intuitive, accuracy can be misleading in imbalanced datasets where high accuracy may result from correct predictions on the majority class while failing on minority classes.

Macro-averaged F1 score (Macro-F1)

To address class imbalance and evaluate performance across all categories equally, we compute the macro-averaged F1 score. For each class c, precision and recall are defined as:(Equation 38)Precisionc=TPcTPc+FPc,Recallc=TPcTPc+FNc

The F1 score for class c is the harmonic mean of precision and recall:(Equation 39)F1c=2⋅Precisionc⋅RecallcPrecisionc+Recallc

The macro-averaged F1 score computes the unweighted mean across all C classes:(Equation 40)Macro-F1=1C∑c=1CF1c

This metric treats each class equally regardless of sample size, making it particularly suitable for evaluating performance on rare but clinically significant conditions such as critical arrhythmias.

Cohen’s kappa (κ)

Cohen’s Kappa coefficient measures inter-rater agreement while accounting for agreement occurring by chance, providing a more robust evaluation than raw accuracy. It is calculated as:(Equation 41)κ=po−pe1−pe,where po is the observed agreement (equivalent to accuracy) and pe is the expected agreement by chance:(Equation 42)po=1N∑c=1Cncc,pe=1N2∑c=1Cnc+⋅n+c

Here, ncc denotes the number of samples correctly classified as class c, while nc+ and n+c represent the total number of samples with ground-truth label c and predicted label c respectively. Kappa values range from -1 to 1, with κ > 0.8 indicating strong agreement, 0.6 < κ ≤ 0.8 indicating moderate agreement, and κ ≤ 0.6 indicating weak agreement.

Baseline methods

We compare our proposed WaveMamba-Net against a comprehensive set of baseline methods spanning traditional machine learning approaches, deep learning architectures, and state-of-the-art models specifically designed for physiological signal analysis.

Support vector machine (SVM)

As a classical machine learning baseline, we implement SVM with radial basis function (RBF) kernel. Features are extracted from pulse signals using a combination of time-domain statistics (mean, standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis), frequency-domain features obtained via Fast Fourier Transform (power spectral density in different frequency bands), and wavelet-based features (energy distribution across decomposition levels). A total of 156 handcrafted features are computed for each signal segment. The SVM hyperparameters (regularization parameter C and kernel width γ) are optimized through 5-fold cross-validation on the training set. This baseline establishes the performance ceiling achievable with traditional feature engineering approaches.

Random Forest (RF)

Random Forest provides a robust ensemble learning baseline that can capture non-linear relationships in feature space. We employ the same 156 handcrafted features as used for SVM. The forest consists of 500 decision trees with maximum depth limited to 20 to prevent overfitting. Each tree is trained on bootstrap samples with feature subsampling ratio set to 156≈12 features per split. This baseline demonstrates the effectiveness of ensemble methods for pulse signal classification.

1D convolutional neural network (1D-CNN)

We implement a deep 1D-CNN architecture as a pure deep learning baseline. The network consists of 6 convolutional blocks, each containing a 1D convolutional layer (kernel size 7, stride 1), batch normalization, ReLU activation, and max pooling (kernel size 2). The number of filters progresses as [64, 128, 128, 256, 256, 512]. Global average pooling aggregates spatial features, followed by two fully connected layers [512, 256] before the classification layer. This architecture has approximately 2.8 million parameters and demonstrates the capability of purely convolutional approaches for temporal signal processing.

Long short-term memory network (LSTM)

As a recurrent neural network baseline, we employ a bidirectional LSTM to capture long-range temporal dependencies in pulse signals. The architecture comprises 3 stacked bidirectional LSTM layers with hidden dimensions [256, 256, 128], followed by an attention mechanism that computes weighted aggregation of hidden states across time steps. The attention weights are derived from a single-layer feedforward network. Dropout with probability 0.4 is applied between LSTM layers for regularization. This baseline has approximately 2.1 million parameters and represents classical sequence modeling approaches.

Temporal convolutional network (TCN)

TCN employs dilated causal convolutions to achieve large receptive fields while maintaining computational efficiency. Our implementation consists of 8 residual blocks with dilation factors [1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128], each containing two dilated convolutional layers (kernel size 3) with residual connections. The number of channels is fixed at 256 across all blocks. Weight normalization and spatial dropout (probability 0.3) are applied for regularization. TCN has approximately 1.9 million parameters and represents modern convolutional approaches for sequence modeling without recurrence.

Transformer

We implement a standard Transformer encoder architecture adapted for univariate time series classification. The input signal is segmented into patches of 64 samples with 50% overlap and embedded into 256-dimensional vectors with sinusoidal positional encodings. The encoder consists of 6 layers, each containing multi-head self-attention (8 heads, dimension 256) and a position-wise feedforward network (hidden dimension 1024). Layer normalization and residual connections follow the standard pre-norm configuration. This baseline has approximately 3.5 million parameters and demonstrates the effectiveness of pure attention mechanisms for pulse signal analysis.

InceptionTime

InceptionTime is an ensemble of deep convolutional networks specifically designed for time series classification. Each network in the ensemble uses Inception modules with parallel convolutional operations at multiple scales (kernel sizes [10, 20, 40]) and a bottleneck layer for dimension reduction. We employ an ensemble of 5 networks, each with 6 Inception modules and 128 filters per scale. Residual connections span every 3 modules. This baseline represents state-of-the-art convolutional architectures for time series and has approximately 2.4 million parameters per network.

TST (time series transformer)

TST enhances the standard Transformer with learnable positional encodings and a specialized attention mechanism for time series. The architecture employs 6 encoder layers with multi-head attention (8 heads) and incorporates temporal convolutional layers within each encoder block to capture local patterns. The embedding dimension is 256 with feedforward dimension 1024. TST introduces a learnable linear projection for positional encodings instead of fixed sinusoidal functions, allowing the model to adapt to dataset-specific temporal structures. This baseline has approximately 3.8 million parameters.

MLSTM-FCN

Multivariate Long Short-Term Memory Fully Convolutional Network (MLSTM-FCN) combines recurrent and convolutional processing through a dual-pathway architecture. One pathway consists of a single LSTM layer (128 units) with attention, while the parallel pathway employs three temporal convolutional blocks (kernel size 8, 5, 3) with filters [128, 256, 128]. Features from both pathways are concatenated and fed to the classification head. Despite being designed for multivariate time series, we adapt it to univariate signals by treating wavelet decomposition levels as multiple channels. This baseline has approximately 1.6 million parameters and demonstrates hybrid recurrent-convolutional approaches.

Mamba (vanilla)

To provide a direct SSM baseline without wavelet integration, we implement a standard Mamba encoder21 applied to linearly-projected temporal patches of the raw PPG signal. The architecture consists of six stacked Mamba blocks with state-space dimension 16 and embedding dimension 256, matching the configuration of our proposed model. No frequency decomposition or dual-view processing is applied. This baseline isolates the contribution of the selective state-space mechanism itself from the wavelet and dual-view components of WaveMamba-Net, and has approximately 3.1M parameters.

S4 (structured state space)

We implement the S4 model of Gu et al.31 using diagonal-plus-low-rank parameterisation of the state matrix. The network follows the same depth and embedding configuration as vanilla Mamba for comparability. S4 serves as a representative of the earlier generation of continuous-time SSMs whose computational efficiency has since been surpassed by Mamba’s hardware-aware selective scan, and provides a reference point for evaluating the role of input-dependent selectivity.

TSCMamba

TSCMamba32 is a recently proposed architecture that combines continuous wavelet transform with the Mamba state-space model for multi-view time series classification, integrating spectral, temporal, local, and global features through a novel tango scanning mechanism.

TimesNet

TimesNet33 is a state-of-the-art method for general time series analysis that transforms 1D temporal variations into 2D space by folding the signal according to dominant frequencies identified via FFT, then applying 2D convolutions to capture within-period and cross-period patterns. This design shares a structural motivation with our spatial reshape operation (Section 3.3) and provides a direct point of comparison for assessing the value of 2D representations in cardiovascular signal analysis. We follow the authors’ recommended configuration with kernel size 3 and top-k = 3 dominant frequencies.

DLinear

DLinear34 applies a simple trend-seasonal decomposition using a moving average and then feeds each component through an independent linear layer. Despite its simplicity, DLinear has been shown to be a surprisingly competitive baseline for time series tasks. We include it here as a representative of lightweight decomposition-linear methods to contextualise the gains provided by more complex architectures.

BioMamba

BioMamba35 is the most directly relevant prior work, combining spectro-temporal embedding with bidirectional Mamba for biosignal (ECG/EEG) classification. Unlike WaveMamba-Net, BioMamba does not incorporate explicit wavelet decomposition, dual-view architecture, or adaptive frequency conditioning.

Statistical analysis

All classification performance metrics (Accuracy, Macro-F1, Cohen’s Kappa, and Macro-AUROC) are reported as the mean ± standard deviation derived from 5 independent runs with different random seeds. For the MIMIC-III Waveform Database, results are obtained under a fixed temporal 70%/15%/15% train/validation/test split, while for the PPG-DaLiA dataset, a leave-one-subject-out cross-validation procedure across all 15 subjects is employed to provide an unbiased estimate of inter-subject generalizability.

To assess whether the performance gains of WaveMamba-Net over each baseline were statistically significant, pairwise comparisons of Macro-F1 scores across the 5 independent runs were conducted using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. A corrected p-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference. Effect sizes were additionally quantified using Cohen’s d to complement the significance testing. For the ablation study, each ablated variant was evaluated under identical experimental conditions and random seeds as the full model, and McNemar’s test was applied to paired sample-level predictions to determine whether the performance degradation attributable to each removed component was statistically significant (p < 0.05). All statistical analyses were conducted using Python (v3.10.12) with scipy (v1.11.0) and numpy (v1.24.0).
