One Health – One Planet – One Mind: integrating eco-anxiety into the One Health paradigm
Authors: Saifullahi Idris Umar, Sadiq Muhammad Maaji, Ibrahim Isma'il Beli, Zara Muhammad Nura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Climate change and environmental degradation affect not only ecosystems and physical health but also psychological well-being. Eco-anxiety—defined as chronic or anticipatory distress related to perceived environmental decline—has emerged as a growing public mental health concern. Although One Health frameworks recognise the interdependence of human, animal, and environmental health, psychosocial processes shaping behavioural responses to environmental change remain underdeveloped within One Health discourse.This conceptual review positions eco-anxiety as a psychosocial determinant operating within the One Health–One Planet–One Mind continuum. Rather than treating eco-anxiety solely as a mental health outcome, we conceptualize it as a mediating and bidirectional process through which environmental disruption influences human behavior, multispecies health outcomes, and system-level resilience. Drawing on interdisciplinary evidence from environmental psychology, planetary mental health, and One Health literature, we synthesise pathways through which eco-anxiety may promote adaptive behaviors—such as environmental stewardship and community mobilization—or, when unaddressed, contribute to maladaptive responses including disengagement and reduced resilience.We propose a conceptual framework that embeds eco-anxiety within One Health architecture, illustrating feedback loops linking planetary change, psychosocial responses, behavioural pathways, and multispecies health outcomes. We conclude by outlining implications for One Health policy, monitoring, and intervention, highlighting the value of integrating psychosocial determinants into multisectoral strategies addressing climate-related health challenges.

Introduction

Climate change increasingly threatens the stability of ecological systems, human health, and global development at an accelerating pace [1]. While physical and environmental consequences are well documented, a growing body of evidence underlines a profound psychological dimension: rising levels of climate-related worry, distress, and anticipatory fear, collectively known as eco-anxiety [2], [3]. Eco-anxiety represents a pervasive emotional response to perceived environmental decline and future ecological risks; it is now recognized by major mental health and climate bodies as an emerging public health issue [2].

Despite growing research in environmental psychology and climate mental health, eco-anxiety remains largely absent from the One Health discourse. Although classical One Health highlights the interdependence of human-animal-environmental health, its framing has focused mainly on biomedical threats, such as zoonotic diseases, food safety, and antimicrobial resistance [4]. The evolving extensions -One Planet and One Mind-attempt to broaden the paradigm toward planetary sustainability and mental well-being. However, neither of these frameworks has sufficiently conceptualized how the psychological responses to environmental change drive One Health outcomes, or integrated eco-anxiety as a driver of behavior, resilience, and multi-sectoral adaptation.

The gap is significant. Eco-anxiety sits uniquely at the interface of cognition, emotion, and environmental perception, shaping how individuals and communities interpret ecological risk [5], [6]. It may motivate conservation action and adaptive behavior, or it may generate paralysis, hopelessness, and disengagement—affecting not only human mental health but also ecosystem stewardship and animal welfare. By influencing decision-making, community preparedness, and risk mitigation, eco-anxiety becomes more than a mental health outcome; it emerges as a psychosocial determinant that modulates the human–animal–environment continuum [6], [7].

The One Health- One Planet- One Mind (OH-OP-OM) paradigm is an ideal platform to explore this intersection. It contends that planetary processes, ecological stability, and human mental health are self-reinforcing. However, even within this framework, eco-anxiety remains implicit rather than explicit, limiting its explanatory and operational potential. There is no structured model showing how eco-anxiety mediates and feeds back into the One Health cycle, nor guidance on how to integrate eco-anxiety into surveillance, resilience planning, or multisectoral health governance.

This article fills an important gap in the literature. We propose that ecological anxiety may serve as a psychosocial determinant in One Health – a factor that may either enhance or reduce resilience to health threats within all three components of One Health – humans, animals, and the environment [8].

In this article, we focus on:1.Defining Eco-Anxiety as a Bi-Directional Psychosocial Response, with Adaptive or Maladaptive Potential.2.Identifying Eco-Anxiety as a Component of the One Health–One Planet–One Mind Continuum.3.Developing a New Conceptual Framework for Eco-Anxiety Pathways and Feedback Loops.4.Developing a Practical Plan for the Inclusion of Eco-Anxiety into One Health Policy, Monitoring, and Intervention Strategies.

This article explicitly integrates eco-anxiety into the One Health–One Planet–One Mind framework, extending existing One Health perspectives by foregrounding psychosocial pathways that remain underdeveloped in current models. We develop a new theoretical pathway: Environmental Change → Eco-Anxiety → Behavior → One Health Outcomes; we also describe eco-anxiety as a predictor and a catalyst for behavior change within the One Health system. Additionally, we propose a dynamic feedback model to illustrate how eco-anxiety can be increased or decreased through the process of adjusting to, or maladapting to, environmental challenges over time.

The emerging area of planetary mental health has drawn attention to the mental health effects of environmental change; however, its focus is largely on describing mental health outcomes, rather than elucidating the various systems-level mechanisms by which behavioural and governance-related responses to the environment shape health dynamics across multiple sectors [6], [9]. This review proposes the consideration of eco-anxiety as a behavioural and governance-related systems-level mechanism, linked through specific pathways and feedback loops to both human and animal health and the health of the natural environment, across the One Health framework [2], [10].

By framing eco-anxiety as not simply an emotional response to environmental change; rather, as a systems-level determinant of resilience; the overall scope of One Health is expanded, and a more holistic, psychologically informed perspective on planetary health is promoted.

Eco-anxiety as an emerging psychosocial determinant

Eco-anxiety is broadly defined as chronic or anticipatory distress that arises out of perceived threats to the environment and future ecological stability [11]. It contrasts with transient stress responses to acute disasters in that eco-anxiety usually persists, being future-oriented and collective, influenced by ongoing exposure to climate narratives, lived environmental degradation, and uncertainty about planetary futures [12], [13]. As it is not a clinical disorder, though, it has measurable effects on emotional well-being, cognition, and behavior, especially within young people, indigenous communities, and populations relying directly on natural ecosystems [14].

Emerging evidence suggests that eco-anxiety operates as a psychosocial determinant of health, informing outcomes via behavioural, emotional, and social pathways [8], [15]. Population-based studies across Europe, North America, and low- and middle-income countries have documented high levels of climate-related worry, particularly among young people, with reported associations between eco-anxiety, depressive symptoms, sleep disturbance, and reduced perceived well-being [14], [16]. At the community level, eco-anxiety has been linked to altered risk perception, institutional trust, and engagement in adaptive or avoidant behaviors [5], [17]. Such findings position eco-anxiety alongside established psychosocial determinants-such as chronic stress and perceived lack of control-known to influence health beyond direct biological exposure.

According to empirical research, eco-anxiety produces a non-linear relationship with regard to behavior in that moderate levels of eco-anxiety are positively correlated with increases in pro-environmental behaviors and collective actions [18], [19]. In contrast, high levels of eco-anxiety that haven't been addressed (or mitigated) tend to produce withdrawal from pro-environmental behaviors, denial of environmental change, and numbing of emotions related to environmental changes [20], [21], [22]. Accordingly, moderate levels of eco-anxiety may act as protective factors by stimulating the development of environmentally sustainable behaviors, which may also support the overarching One Health goals. High levels of unaddressed eco-anxiety may be combined with feelings of powerlessness, leading to emotional numbing, disengagement (or withdrawal), and denial—none of which can support environmental stewardship or adaptive capacity [16], [18], [23].

These behavioural consequences, from the perspective of One Health, are extremely important. The ways in which humans use their land, manage their livestock, interact with wildlife, prepare for disasters, and protect their environment all depend upon information and resources; however, how we respond to our environment is also affected by our emotional state and our perception of risk [24], [25]. Therefore, eco-anxiety is one of several psychological determinants that connects emotional and psychological responses with ecological and biological outcomes [26], [27]. As such, eco-anxiety affects community responses to drought, floods, decreased biodiversity, and animal movement; thus, these community responses have a direct effect on the health of humans, the welfare of animals, and the integrity of the ecosystems [28], [29].

Although eco-anxiety is crucially important; it is still very rarely considered by health policy or surveillance systems. The mental health response to climate change is typically oriented around post-disaster stress and trauma; therefore, it ignores the chronic anticipatory distress, which is a significant characteristic of eco-anxiety; thus, this limits the capacity of health systems to anticipate how people will behave in response to environmental change, as well as designing interventions that utilize adaptation through emotional responses to the environment and mitigates harm [30], [31].

Including eco-anxiety in the psychosocial determinants of health is essential for a complete One Health framework. This means that mental health is not only affected by changes in our environment but also influences how well we can adapt, build resilience and implement multisectoral strategies to improve our health [32], [33]. By putting mental health on the same level as other aspects of environmental and public health, this will provide an opportunity to integrate the psychological aspect of environmental changes into the broader One Health-One Planet-One Mind continuum. Conceptualising eco-anxiety alongside established psychosocial determinants such as chronic stress, perceived control, and collective efficacy situates it within familiar public health and One Health analytic frameworks.

Eco-anxiety in the One Health–One Planet–One Mind interface

The One Health–One Planet–One Mind (OH–OP–OM) framework recognizes the fact that the health of the planet is inextricably interconnected with the ecological integrity and mental well-being of its inhabitants. Within this triad, eco-anxiety occupies a pivotal yet underexplored position, emerging at the intersection of environmental change (One Planet), psychological response (One Mind), and multispecies health outcomes (One Health) [34], [35]. Integrating eco-anxiety into the OH-OP-OM creates a roadmap for understanding the connection between how environmental disruption causes behavioural and health consequences across a range of distinct, yet interrelated systems.

From the One Planet perspective, climate change, loss of biodiversity, land degradation, and recurrent environmental crises are all chronic stressors in shaping collective perceptions of ecological instability [36], [37]. These are not merely physical exposures but also symbolic signals of planetary decline, stirring anticipatory distress without direct harm. Eco-anxiety thus stands out as a psychic incarnation of planetary disruption, converting environmental signals into emotional and cognitive reactions.

In the One Mind domain, eco-anxiety represents the way we psychologically process environmental risk at both the individual and the collective level. This is shaped by media narratives, cultural values, environmental identity and trust/distrust in institutions [38], [39]. Additionally, eco-anxiety has a strong social connection and depends on the context in which it occurs. It can be exacerbated by environmental injustice, livelihood insecurity, and weak governance [40], [41]. Therefore, eco-anxiety should be viewed as a population-level issue of mental health rather than an isolated individual condition.

The ramifications of eco-anxiety for One Health are far-reaching. Humans' psychobiological reaction to their changing environment governs their behavior with corresponding biological outcomes for the environment, animals as well as for the humans [3], [29]. Increased eco-anxiety will influence decisions about migration, land use practices, agricultural practice, interaction with wildlife and response to disasters. In some contexts, increased eco-anxiety may promote adaptive behaviors (i.e. conservation efforts, increasing use of sustainable resources, increasing community mobilization) while other instances of eco-anxiety may result in disengagement, fatalism or maladaptive coping strategies resulting in greater environmental deterioration and health risk [20], [28], [42].

These dynamics are evident in climate-affected settings where environmental disruption alters health-related decision-making. For instance, protracted drought and livestock loss have been associated with psychological distress that affects agricultural practices, decreases engagement with animal health services, and undermines long-term livelihood planning [43], [44], [45]. Similarly, repeated flooding events have been indicated to shape risk perception and preparedness behaviors, as heightened eco-anxiety fuels either proactive community adaptation or, when not managed, withdrawal and fatalism [46], [47], [48]. Such patterns demonstrate how psychological responses to planetary change can take a lead role in shaping multispecies health outcomes within the One Health continuum.

Evidence from climate-affected settings shows that environmental disruption bears measurable psychological and multispecies consequences. Research in the aftermath of extreme weather events has documented increased anxiety, depression, and distress not only with regard to personal loss but also related to ecosystem degradation and animal mortality [49], [50]. The loss of livestock, decline in wildlife, and displacement of pets during a climate-related disaster have been documented to exacerbate psychological distress and hinder recovery, pointing out the emotional interdependence between human and animal health within climate-vulnerable communities [51], [52]. Such bidirectional interactions underscore the relevance of eco-anxiety for the multispecies dimension of One Health. For instance, in climate-affected agrarian settings, psychological distress related to environmental uncertainty may influence livestock management decisions, engagement with veterinary services, and responses to animal disease outbreaks, with downstream consequences for zoonotic risk and livelihood stability [53], [54].

In addition, research shows that when people experiencing ecological grief and concern have social support and feel they can make a difference, their emotional response actually leads to increased engagement in environmental stewardship. This demonstrates that eco-anxiety may serve as a motivating force in helping individuals to engage in the adoption of One Health adaptive behaviors [55], [56].

Crucially, the OH–OP–OM framework has lacked a coherent psychosocial mechanism linking planetary change to health outcomes. Eco-anxiety fills this conceptual gap, serving as a mediator in translating environmental disruption into behavioural pathways with material consequences for One Health. It also serves as a feedback signal, where successful adaptation and ecosystem recovery can reduce distress and maladaptation can amplify psychological burden.

This review is situated explicitly within the One Health-One Planet-One Mind interface and goes further to develop a more dynamic and human-centered understanding of planetary health. It also brings into view the idea of mental health not just as an outcome but also as a key determinant-one that shapes resilience, adaptation, and multisectoral health outcomes in light of ecological change. This systems-based framing extends beyond planetary mental health by situating eco-anxiety within One Health decision-making, multispecies interactions, and resilience governance, rather than treating mental health solely as an endpoint of environmental change. This integrated perspective is essential to the development of One Health strategies capable of meeting the complex challenges of climate change at the heart of the Anthropocene.

Within this framework, eco-anxiety is conceptualized as a continuum rather than a binary exposure, with its effects shaped by contextual moderators such as governance quality, social support, and perceived agency. These moderators influence whether eco-anxiety facilitates adaptive behavioural responses—supporting multispecies health and resilience—or contributes to maladaptive feedback loops that exacerbate environmental degradation and health vulnerability

Environmental change as the primary driver

The framework starts with climate change, the degradation of the environment, focusing on extreme weather, loss of biodiversity, collapse of ecosystems, and security of resources. These conditions of the planet symbolize the One Planet factor and are both physical and symbolic causes of eco-anxiety [57]. It is important to acknowledge that the sense of environment degradation, developed by living experiences, exposure, and stories, plays a crucial part in influencing psychological reactions, even without having direct exposure.

Eco-anxiety as a psychosocial mediator

Eco-anxiety provides the central focus within the model, linking disruption in the environment to the subjective response – emotional, cognitive, and behavioural) (One Mind). Eco-anxiety is envisioned on a continuum, from adaptive anxiety to debilitating anxiety. While moderate in level, eco-anxiety can increase the sensitivity to risks, community engagement, and protective behaviors. At higher levels, it may interfere with the capacity for coping, decision-making, and community coherence [58], [59], [60]. This ambivalence again emphasizes its potential as a risk factor as well as a protective component in the One Health framework.

Behavioural and multispecies health pathways

Eco-anxiety influences behaviors that directly affect human, animal, and environmental health via several key pathways: changes in health-seeking behavior, disaster preparedness, and land and resource management, as well as livestock and wildlife interaction and conservation practices [7], [18], [47], [59]. For example, eco-anxiety may facilitate adaptive behaviors such as enhanced disaster preparedness, community-driven conservation, and protective livestock management. However, where eco-anxiety is high and unaddressed, it may be associated with disengagement from animal health programs, decreased environmental stewardship, and/or maladaptive coping behaviors that further accelerate ecological decline and health vulnerability. These behaviors represent the intersection where psychological processes meet tangible One Health outcomes.

Integration into One Health resilience

A key innovation of the model is this explicit step in integrating eco-anxiety into One Health practice. The integration involves recognition at a psychosocial determinant level, incorporation into monitoring and surveillance systems, translation into policy and governance frameworks, and development of cross-sectoral interventions. Thus, the embedding of eco-anxiety within One Health architecture allows for more anticipatory, responsive, and human-centered health strategies.

Feedback loops and dynamic resilience

This model has a feedback loop that connects eco-anxiety to One Health resilience. Success in adapting, restoring ecosystems, and providing inclusive governance may lessen the impact of eco-anxiety through increased adaptive engagement. On the other hand, failure to implement sustainable policies, environmental loss, and inequitable response may cause eco-anxiety to increase, which perpetuates maladaptive feedback loops. Furthermore, this dynamic view of climate–health interactions acknowledges the inherent instability of climate–health interactions and the role of mental health as an integral part of long-term resilience.

Together, these pathways position eco-anxiety as a system-level determinant within the One Health–One Planet–One Mind continuum. The model extends prior frameworks by considering mental health explicitly for multispecies resilience planning and shows how emotional responses to planetary change shape health outcomes across scale.

Longitudinal and qualitative research has found that community-level adaptation, ecosystem recovery, and shared governance can mitigate-distress associated with climate change, while recurring exposure to unmanaged loss of the environment increases anxiety and hopelessness, supporting the plausibility of the proposed feedback loop [61], [62].

When ecological restoration efforts have tangible effects in communities, like strengthening animal health and reducing the effects of disaster, it is likely that those communities will find their eco-anxiety diminished and feel greater agency over their surroundings. Conversely, continued environmental degradation with no effective solution will lead to greater levels of ecological stress and create or perpetuate maladaptive cycles for the community.

Operationalizing eco-anxiety within One Health research and surveillance

A central challenge in integrating eco-anxiety into One Health research thus lies not with issues of conceptual plausibility but rather with measurement and operationalization. To date, eco-anxiety has not been systematically integrated into the surveillance frameworks of One Health, which traditionally have emphasized biological, environmental, and epidemiological indicators. The absence of One Health-specific instruments, however, does not render eco-anxiety unmeasurable. Rather, it represents an important disciplinary gap between environmental psychology and multisectoral health research that can be productively bridged through a strategic use of existing tools.

Several psychometric instruments already exist to measure eco-anxiety and related constructs such as climate anxiety, ecological distress, and ecological grief. Those include Hogg's climate anxiety measures, the Climate Anxiety Scale by Clayton and Karazsia, and climate change worry scales that have been integrated into population-based surveys and youth-focused assessments [17], [63], [64], [65], [66]. Climate-related disability and impairment scales further complement these tools by quantifying functional impacts relevant to public health and resilience planning [67]. Even though these instruments were originally developed within psychological and public research domain, they provide standardized and reproducible means of measuring emotional, cognitive, and behavioural responses to perceived environmental change. Importantly, they conceptualize eco-anxiety not as a clinical disorder but as a spectrum of concerns arising from anticipation and distress, which is consistent with the One Health emphasis on population level determinants rather than individual pathology.

From a One Health perspective, the value of these measures rests in their potential to be analysed alongside routinely collected environmental and animal-health data streams, rather than to be used in isolation. Eco-anxiety indicators can be placed within the One Mind domain and examined in concert with One Planet indicators such as drought indices, land degradation metrics, biodiversity loss, water insecurity, and frequency or severity of extreme weather events [3], [68]. Simultaneously, One Health-relevant outcomes-including livestock morbidity and mortality, uptake of animal health services, disaster preparedness behaviors, migration patterns, and shifts in land or resource use-can be evaluated as downstream behavioural or multispecies correlates [13], [69]. Co-analysis on its own does not assume causality but allows for hypothesis-testing on the nature of psychosocial-environmental interactions within complex adaptive systems.

At the community or population level, inclusion of eco-anxiety in environmental health surveys, climate change risk assessment, or post-disaster assessment provides a feasible approach for examining whether psychological distress correlates with ecosystem degradation or multispecies health risk/survival. For instance, eco-anxiety scores might be examined in concert with prolonged drought and loss of livestock, flooding and recurring displacement of humans and animals, or loss of biodiversity impacting livelihoods dependent on that ecosystem. The analyses may help to identify psychosocial thresholds beyond which environmental concern shifts from adaptive engagement to maladaptive withdrawal, informing resilience planning, community risk communication, and intervention design.

From a surveillance and policy perspective, eco-anxiety indicators can act as sentinel psychosocial signals within One Health systems. Longitudinally monitored shifts in levels of eco-anxiety may signal not only environmental degradation per se but also governance effectiveness, institutional trust, and perceived adaptive capacity [18], [70]. In so doing, eco-anxiety extends conventional environmental and health metrics to capture how populations perceive, emotionally process, and respond to ecological risk-a set of processes influencing behaviors pertinent to human health, animal welfare, and ecosystem stewardship.

Importantly, this framework does not propose the development of new measurement instruments. Rather, it outlines a viable measurement agenda through integrating existing validated scales into interdisciplinary research and surveillance designs. Integrating the measures of eco-anxiety alongside environmental and animal-health data streams facilitates empirical testing of the pathways and feedback loops presented in this review and moves the development of eco-anxiety as a primarily conceptual construct toward one that is testable as a psychosocial determinant within One Health, while maintaining methodological rigor and disciplinary transparency. Policy implications, research directions, and conclusion

The One-Health, One-Planet, and One-Mind integrated framework includes the consideration of the potential for eco-anxiety (and related feelings) to affect people's mental and emotional well-being as a result of ongoing or expected negative impacts from climate change. The recognition of eco-anxiety as a psychosocial determining factor represents an opportunity for One Health systems to move beyond reacting to environmental disasters toward developing anticipatory, people-centered approaches to address eco-anxiety.

One Health surveillance systems may benefit from incorporating psychosocial indicators, including eco-anxiety, alongside environmental and biological metrics, to better anticipate behavioural responses to environmental change. Eco-anxiety will need to be integrated through four interrelated activities that will allow for improved recognition: First, eco-anxiety must be officially recognized and documented as a mental health consequence of environmental change within the context of national and global One Health Strategy; Second, the development and implementation of comprehensive and robust monitoring/surveillance systems will require eco-anxiety indicators to accompany existing environmental and health metrics in order to facilitate the identification of vulnerable populations and maladaptive behaviors associated with eco-anxiety; Third, action will be required for policy translation to ensure psychosocial influences are recognized in climate adaptation and biodiversity conservation, natural disaster preparedness, and animal health strategies; and Fourth, One-Health and climate-health integrated approaches to eco-anxiety should promote mental health support and direct actions linking environmental action with developing agency, social cohesion and adaptive opportunities instead of generating fear and paralysis.

These approaches have a practical application for equity and justice. Eco-anxiety is not evenly distributed and impacts young people, indigenous communities, and communities that experience environmental injustice and insecure living. Incorporating eco-anxiety into the One Health approach can ensure that mental health is considered in the same way that ecological and biological vulnerability are considered because it can help to build a more equal resilience in human and animal populations. Incorporating eco-anxiety into the One Health approach does not replicate a planetary mental health approach.

Several research priorities follow from this framework. Future research should work on developing instruments of eco-anxiety that can be used for surveillance in large populations, test its longitudinal association with adaptive and maladaptive strategies, and investigate multispecies correlations between psychological distress and ecosystem and animal welfare outcomes. Testing hypotheses about proposed feedback loops would be critical in sharpening interventions and in ascertaining eco-anxiety as One Health resilience factors.

Strengths and limitations

This review is conceptual in nature and does not seek to empirically test the proposed framework. Consequently, the pathways and feedback loops described are intended to synthesise and integrate existing evidence rather than establish causation per se. The framework draws on interdisciplinary literature; however, empirical evidence of the linkage between eco-anxiety and multispecies health outcomes remains uneven across contexts and settings. Moreover, cultural, socioeconomic, and governance contextual variation will influence the ways that eco-anxiety is manifested and how it drives behavioural change-a level of nuance that cannot be comprehensively accounted for in a single conceptual model. These limitations signal important directions for future empirical and context-specific research and are not weaknesses of the framework itself.

Conclusion

Eco-anxiety represents an important yet under-recognized psychosocial dimension of environmental change with implications for human, animal, and ecosystem health. By conceptualising eco-anxiety as a psychosocial determinant within the One Health–One Planet–One Mind framework, this review extends existing One Health perspectives by foregrounding the behavioural and emotional pathways through which environmental disruption influences multispecies health outcomes and system resilience.

Integrating eco-anxiety into One Health thinking offers a more holistic understanding of adaptation and resilience in the context of climate change, highlighting mental health not only as an outcome but also as a driver of behavioural responses that shape environmental stewardship and health across species. Recognising and addressing eco-anxiety within One Health frameworks may therefore strengthen multisectoral efforts to respond to the complex and interconnected health challenges of the Anthropocene.
