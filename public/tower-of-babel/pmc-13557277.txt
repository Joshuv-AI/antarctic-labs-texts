Dermatoscopic Assessment of Nail Fold Capillary Patterns in Patients of Color with Different Autoimmune Connective Tissue Diseases
Authors: Eman Ayesha, Sumera Hanif, Madeline Tchack, Emma Didzbalis, Maria Elisabetta Greco, Talat Akbar, Aliza Hashim, Rabia Shaukat, Hooryia Shafiq Butt, Noah Musolff, Babar Rao, Haroon Nabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNail fold capillaroscopy is a noninvasive tool for assessing early capillary changes in proximal nail fold capillaries in autoimmune connective tissue diseases. Handheld dermatoscopes have expanded its clinical application.ObjectivesTo characterize nail fold capillary patterns using dermatoscopy and evaluate their association with autoimmune connective tissue disease severity in patients with skin of color (Fitzpatrick skin types IV, V and VI).MethodsA six-month cross-sectional study was conducted at Ghurki Trust Teaching Hospital in Lahore, Pakistan. Ninety-six patients with Fitzpatrick skin types IV–VI and autoimmune connective tissue diseases underwent dermatoscopic evaluation (HEINE DELTA 30 Dermatoscope) and clinical assessment of disease activity.ResultsMost participants were female (84.4%). The diagnoses included rheumatoid arthritis (RA) (51%), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) (27%), systemic scleroderma (SSc) (17%), and dermatomyositis (DM) (5%). Capillary patterns varied significantly with disease duration (p = 0.001) and diagnosis. Normal patterns were observed in 27 patients (28.1%), nonspecific patterns in 28 patients (29.2%), and scleroderma patterns in 41 patients (42.7%). Scleroderma patterns were most frequent in those with SSC and SLE. Focal microhemorrhages (52.1%) and areas of discrete devascularization (50%) were the most prevalent microvascular findings. Capillary patterns were significantly associated with disease severity (p < 0.001).ConclusionsDermatoscopic assessment of nail fold capillaries may provide a valuable, noninvasive biomarker of disease activity in autoimmune connective tissue diseases. This study provides valuable data on skin of color, which remains underrepresented in dermatological research.Keywords: Dermatoscope, Scleroderma pattern, Skin of color, Nailfold capillaroscopy

Introduction

Autoimmune connective tissue disorders encompass a spectrum of disorders, including systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), systemic sclerosis (SSc), primary Sjögren’s syndrome, undifferentiated connective tissue disease, rheumatoid arthritis (RA), and dermatomyositis (DM) [1]. The American Autoimmune Related Disease Association has reported over 100 autoimmune disorders, collectively representing a significant global health concern, impacting approximately 5% to 10% of the worldwide population [2]. The vascular changes and abnormalities associated with these disorders can lead to distinct skin manifestations and nail changes. These local vascular alterations can be assessed through proximal nail fold capillary examination [1].

Nail fold capillaroscopy is a reproducible and noninvasive technique for evaluating proximal nail fold capillaries [3], capable of detecting vascular alterations that precede clinical symptoms and are often associated with the progression of connective tissue diseases marked by abnormal capillary morphology [4,5,6]. Although the gold standard for capillaroscopic evaluation is videocapillaroscopy, its widespread clinical use is hindered by high cost, time demands, lack of portability, and the requirement for specialized training. As a practical alternative, dermatoscopes offer a portable, user-friendly, and efficient method for capillary assessment [7], making them especially valuable in settings with limited access to advanced imaging modalities.

Nail fold capillary patterns are divided into three categories: normal pattern, scleroderma pattern, and nonspecific pattern. Normal capillaries are clearly visible, have an inverted “U” shape, with 7–12 capillaries per millimeter, a loop diameter of less than 20 μm, and no tortuosity, microhemorrhages, or avascular zones. The scleroderma pattern of nail fold capillaries, first identified by Maricq et al. in 1973, refers to a distinct set of nail fold capillaroscopy changes. These changes include dilated capillaries, a reduction in capillary loops, microhemorrhages and neoangiogenesis. Two or more nail folds must exhibit at least two of these characteristics to diagnose the scleroderma pattern, while those having only one capillary change are regarded as nonspecific pattern [8].

Integrating dermatoscopy into routine clinical practice may aid in early disease detection, severity assessment, and longitudinal monitoring, ultimately contributing to improved patient outcomes. This is particularly relevant given the underrepresentation of individuals with skin of color (SOC) in dermatological research and education, as documented in recent literature, highlighting gaps in academic resources, cosmetic dermatology publications, and clinical texts [9,10,11]. As there remains a notable lack of data on the application of nail fold capillaroscopy in SOC populations, further investigation is warranted.

Objectives

The objective of this study was to characterize nail fold capillary patterns using dermatoscopy in patients with autoimmune connective tissue disease and Fitzpatrick skin types IV – VI and to evaluate their association with disease severity.

Methods

A cross-sectional study was conducted in the outpatient Department of Dermatology at Ghurki Trust Teaching Hospital in Lahore, Pakistan. The study duration was six months after approval of the research synopsis. Patients diagnosed with SSc, SLE, DM, or RA, aged 16 years and older, were included in the study without gender restrictions. Eligible participants were of Fitzpatrick skin types IV, V, and VI. Participants were selected through non-probability convenience sampling. Exclusion criteria included current smokers, patients with ungual or periungual disease, and patients with diabetes mellitus. The sample size was 96, calculated using the WHO sample size calculator based on an estimated prevalence of scleroderma pattern in SLC of 52.9%, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) and a margin of error of 0.10 [12].

Data Collection Procedure

The study commenced following approval from the institutional ethics committee. Patients presenting in the dermatology outpatient department of Ghurki Trust Teaching Hospital who fulfilled the inclusion criteria were enrolled in the study. A detailed written proforma was completed for each participant, and informed consent was obtained prior to participation. Nail fold dermoscopy was performed after patients were seated for 15 minutes at room temperature to stabilize vascular responses and to ensure observations reflected underlying pathological changes rather than temporary physiological variations. Examination was carried out on the nail folds of digits 1 through 5 of each participant on each hand using handheld HEINE DELTA 30 dermatoscope. The dermatoscope was used in polarized mode at ten-fold magnification and was connected to an iPhone 15 for image viewing and documentation. All changes observed in the nail folds were documented and photographed for further analysis.

Disease severity was assessed using standardized scoring systems specific to each condition: the 28 Joint Disease Activity Score-C-reactive protein (DAS28-CRP) for RA, the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) for SLE, the Valentini Disease Activity Score (VDAS) for SSc, and the Myositis Disease Activity Assessment Tool (MYOACT) for DM.

Data Analysis

The data collected were entered and analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27.0. Descriptive statistics were applied for the demographic and disease-related variables. Disease severity was assessed using specific scoring systems for each disease category: the SLEDAI for SLE patients and the VDAS for patients with SSc. Chi-square tests were performed to compare categorical variables across different groups, with a p-value of ≤0.05 considered statistically significant.

To explore associations between demographic characteristics (age, sex, and disease duration) and nail fold capillary patterns, frequency distributions and chi-square tests were used.

Furthermore, the relationship between disease severity and capillary patterns was analyzed by comparing scoring system results (SLEDAI, VDAS, DAS28-CRP, and MYOACT) with observed dermatoscopic patterns.

Results

Among the 96 patients who underwent dermatoscopic assessment of nail fold capillary patterns in autoimmune connective tissue diseases, the majority were female (84.4%), with males comprising 15.6%. The mean age was 37.28 years, ranging from 17 to 75 years (Table 1). Fifty-six patients were Fitzpatrick skin type IV, 32 patients were type V, and the remaining eight were type VI. Among the autoimmune diseases, RA was the most common (51.0%), followed by SLE (27.1%), SSc (16.7%), and DM (5.2%). The majority of patients (65.6%) had a disease duration of 1–10 years. A significant association was observed between sex and nailfold capillary patterns (P=0.028). There was a highly significant association found between disease duration and capillary pattern (P=0.001). However, no significant association was observed between patient age and the nail fold capillary patterns.

Nail Fold Capillary Patterns

Three distinct patterns were identified: normal, nonspecific, and scleroderma patterns. The most prevalent was the scleroderma pattern, which was seen in 41 patients, representing 42.7% of the total group. The nonspecific pattern was seen in 28 patients (29.2%), while 27 patients (28.1%) exhibited normal pattern. In SSc, the predominant pattern was scleroderma (62.5), followed by SLE (50%). DM uniquely displayed a dominance of nonspecific patterns (80%). In patients with RA, both nonspecific and scleroderma patterns were equally frequent (34.7%), and normal patterns also accounted for 30.6%, making this disease unique in its balanced distribution across categories. Focal microhemorrhages were the most frequently observed finding, present in 52.1% of cases, followed by discrete devascularization, seen in 50% of cases (Figure 1). Relationship between Dermatoscopic Pattern and Disease Severity

Disease severity was assessed using specific scoring systems for each disease. For SSc, the VDAS showed a mean score of 3.44, indicating moderate severity. For SLE, the SLEDAI score had a mean of 5.69, reflecting moderate disease activity. In DM, the MYOACT visual analogue score averaged 13.60, indicating moderate severity. Lastly, RA severity, measured using the DAS28-CRP score, showed a mean of 2.65, indicating mild disease severity (range: 0.40–5.50). In SSc, capillary morphology changed significantly with increasing disease severity. In patients with mild disease severity, capillary morphology was predominantly normal (62.5%), with a smaller proportion showing tortuous loops (25%) and dilatations (12.5%). However, in those with moderate disease severity, tortuous loops were more frequent (50%). Dilatations were not observed in moderate cases.

In SLE, mild disease severity was characterized by entirely normal capillary morphology (100%), with no evidence of tortuous loops or dilatations. However, in moderate cases, there was a noticeable decrease in normal morphology (66.7%) and a slight increase in tortuous loops (20%) and dilatations (13.3%).

In RA, capillary morphology remained largely normal in remission (100%). In cases of low disease activity, a small proportion (9.1%) of capillaries showed tortuous loops, while 90.9% retained normal morphology. In those with moderate disease activity, there was a further increase in tortuous loops noted. Interestingly, high disease activity was associated with rare capillary dilatations and more prominent morphological changes.

All patients with DM showed moderate degree of severity.

These findings highlighted the differing extents of capillary involvement across the four diseases observed, with SSc exhibiting the most severe vascular remodeling in SOC patients.

Discussion

Dermoscopy offers a noninvasive, cost-effective method to detect proximal nailfold capillary patterns in different autoimmune connective tissue diseases [13]. Characteristic capillary changes such as enlarged capillaries, microhemorrhages, and avascular zones are frequently observed in these disorders, aiding in early diagnosis and monitoring disease progression [14].

In the present study of 96 patients, the SSc pattern was identified in 42.7% of the cohort, while Mawra et al. and Chanprapaph et al. reported a strikingly greater prevalence of scleroderma pattern in SSc and DM patients [12,15]. Chanprapaph et al.’s study specifies a patient population of Fitzpatrick skin types III–V, without a proportional breakdown. A potentially higher representation of Fitzpatrick type III patients in their cohort may have contributed to differences observed from the current study, as our study included exclusively patients of skin types IV, V, and VI [15]. In contrast, Murat et al. reported a much lower prevalence, identifying the scleroderma pattern in RA cases and with none observed in SLE patients [16]. Mohamed et al. observed that 60.4% SLE patients exhibited nonspecific patterns, almost in agreement with our study, where 50% of SLE patients showed scleroderma pattern [17]. These differences in the findings across studies suggest that factors such as patient selection, sample size, and disease severity may influence nail fold capillary pattern distribution.

The present study aligns with that of Chojer et al., where focal microhemorrhages and areas of discrete devascularization were the most consistent findings [4]. However, Mawra et al. and Rajaei et al. found tortuosity and a prominent subpapillary plexus as predominant findings in patients with RA [12,18]. In contrast, the current study revealed a lower degree of tortuosity, particularly in patients with SSc and SLE. This variability between studies may be due to the differences in the disease phenotypes, disease duration, treatment protocols, and environmental factors.

Unlike other studies, the present study showed a significant relationship between the dermoscopic patterns and sex as well as disease duration [12,16].

In SSc, we found a significant correlation between disease severity and the dermoscopic patterns, with normal capillary decreasing from 62.5% to 37.5% and an increase in tortuous loops from 25% to 50% as disease severity progressed. This aligns with other studies that showed that capillary density loss is associated with severe organ involvement and new interstitial lung disease [19,20]. This suggests that worsening capillary changes may reflect disease severity and organ damage.

In SLE, the present study showed that capillary morphology remained entirely normal (100%) in patients with mild disease severity, with a decrease in normal morphology and an increase in tortuous loops and dilations as disease severity increased to moderate level. Several studies showed a positive correlation between nailfold capillary changes and SLE disease activity, with more severe alterations linked to higher disease activity, including capillary density reduction and abnormal microvascular structures [21,22,23].

In DM, this study found increased capillary density, focal microhemorrhages, and discrete devascularization predominating with moderate disease activity in all the patients of this subgroup. In contrast, Paudyal et al. observed bushy capillaries in the majority of DM cases, along with enlarged capillaries and avascular areas [24]. However, tests of significance could not be applied to the current study due to its small sample size, thus limiting its statistical analysis.

In the present study, RA demonstrated the correlation between capillary morphology and disease severity, with normal capillaries observed in remission, and tortuous loops and morphological abnormalities became more prominent when disease activity rose to moderate and higher levels. However, other studies showed weak correlations between specific capillary patterns and disease duration or severity in RA, suggesting that while nailfold capillaroscopy can detect microvascular changes, it may not reliably reflect RA disease activity [5,25].

Notably, among all referenced studies, only one explicitly reported patient skin types using the Fitzpatrick scale, as was done in our study. The absence of this demographic detail could contribute to the observed differences noted between findings reported in the literature and our study. These differences may stem from true physiological variations across skin type classifications, although these differences may also be due to differences in the effectiveness of capillaroscopy techniques in those of different skin types, as skin pigmentation and optical contrast may influence dermatoscope visibility and interpretation. Greater inclusion of SOC populations is necessary to ensure equitable diagnostic accuracy and clinical applicability.

The study’s sample size of 96 patients was relatively small, limiting the ability to draw broad conclusions. As it was a cross-sectional study, it was not possible to assess the capillary changes over time or evaluate the effects of treatment interventions. Furthermore, while RA was the most common disease among participants, DM was underrepresented, reducing the vitality of results. The study also failed to consider possible confounding variables such as comorbid conditions, lifestyle changes, and environmental factors that may affect nailfold capillaries.

Conclusions

In conclusion, dermatoscopy can be used to detect nailfold patterns that may reflect underlying disease activity. This technique holds promise as a valuable tool for monitoring disease progression and evaluating treatment efficacy, contributing to improved clinical outcomes. The focus on patients with SOC in this study addresses a significant gap in the literature and provides further insights into how these autoimmune connective tissue diseases present within this population.
