Recurrent Radiculopathy Caused by an Epidural Cervical Plasmacytoma Revealing Plasma Cell Leukemia: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Luis Garibay, Jose-Carlos Sauri-Barraza, Eduardo Callejas Ponce, Jesus E Valdez-Aguilar, Ulises García-González, Jorge D. Pérez Ruiz, Carlo Enrico Bañuelos Aluzzi, Miguel A Rodriguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cervical radiculopathy in elderly patients is commonly associated with degenerative spine disease, which can make the diagnosis of less frequent epidural tumors challenging. Epidural cervical plasmacytoma is rare and may initially present with neck pain and radicular symptoms that closely resemble cervical spondylosis. We report the case of a 76-year-old woman with axial neck pain and left upper extremity radiculopathy initially managed as degenerative cervical disease, with temporary postoperative improvement followed by recurrence of symptoms. Further surgical evaluation revealed an epidural cervical mass involving the lower cervical nerve roots, and histopathological analysis confirmed plasmacytoma. Subsequent hematologic assessment established the diagnosis of plasma cell leukemia. This case highlights the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis in patients with recurrent, progressive, or atypical radicular symptoms, especially when the clinical course is not fully explained by degenerative pathology. Early tissue diagnosis and systemic evaluation are essential to guide appropriate multidisciplinary treatment.

Introduction

Plasmacytomas are clonal neoplasms of plasma cells that form a single mass, classified as solitary bone plasmacytomas (SBP) when arising from bone or extramedullary plasmacytomas when originating in soft tissues [1]. A biopsy-proven plasmacytoma with one or more additional lesions fulfills diagnostic criteria for multiple myeloma (MM) [2]. When circulating plasma cells comprise ≥5% of peripheral blood leukocytes, the diagnosis corresponds to plasma cell leukemia (PCL), an aggressive plasma cell dyscrasia associated with a poor prognosis [3-5].

Both SBP and MM frequently involve the axial skeleton and may rarely affect the cervical spine through osteolytic lesions driven by osteoclast activation and osteoblast suppression, leading to instability, axial neck pain, foraminal stenosis, or myelopathy [6-8]. These manifestations often overlap with degenerative spondylosis, delaying recognition of an underlying plasma cell neoplasm, and may often go overlooked till advanced disease stages [9,10]. However, to our knowledge, no case of PCL has been reported to debut with an extradural spinal plasmacytoma mimicking a recurrent degenerative radiculopathy.

Documenting this case is clinically important because cervical extradural plasmacytoma presenting as the sentinel manifestation of PCL has not, to our knowledge, been previously characterized, and its initial resemblance to degenerative cervical radiculopathy creates a tangible risk of diagnostic delay. This presentation is unusual because PCL typically arises de novo or as a leukemic transformation of advanced MM rather than being unveiled by a solitary extradural spinal lesion, and extradural plasmacytomas themselves account for only a small proportion of plasma cell neoplasms involving the spine. Early recognition of this deceptive clinical pathway, particularly when radiculopathy recurs or evolves atypically after surgery for presumed degenerative disease, may shorten the interval to systemic staging with PET-CT and peripheral blood immunophenotyping, thereby accelerating hematological diagnosis and initiating appropriate multimodal therapy. By reporting this case, we aim to broaden the recognized spectrum of PCL presentation, alert clinicians to an atypical but clinically significant mode of disease onset, and contribute a well-documented reference point in the existing literature on plasma cell neoplasms involving the cervical spine.

Case presentation

Clinical presentation

A 76-year-old woman presented in January 2026 with a two-month history of progressive axial neck pain and left arm paresthesias with triceps predominance. Her past medical history was unremarkable, with no significant comorbidities. Examination revealed limited cervical range of motion, left-sided Spurling’s sign, and weakness (Daniels grade 4/5) in the C5-C7 myotomes, with hypoesthesia in the corresponding dermatomes. Cervical MRI demonstrated multilevel degenerative spondylosis with T2-weighted disc protrusions at C4-C7, with left paracentral disc bulging producing neural foraminal narrowing and left nerve root compression (Figure 1). Neurophysiologic studies demonstrated irritation of the left C6 and C7 nerve roots, while preoperative laboratory workup was unremarkable.

Initial procedure and evolution

A C5-C6 and C6-C7 anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) was performed, achieving adequate decompression (Figure 2).

The patient experienced immediate improvement in neck pain and was discharged on postoperative day 1. At two-week follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic. However, at postoperative week 6, she returned with recurrent neck pain radiating to the left arm, rated 8/10 on the visual analog scale. Cervical CT revealed posterior osteophytic complexes and left-predominant facet arthropathy at C6-C7 and C7-T1 producing left neural foraminal stenosis, while repeat neurophysiologic studies indicated left C7 and C8 nerve root irritation (Figure 3) At this stage, the principal differential diagnoses considered included persistent degenerative cervical radiculopathy from recurrent foraminal stenosis, facet joint cyst, pseudoarthrosis of the prior arthrodesis, and, given the atypical early recurrence, an infiltrative or neoplastic process.

Surgical reintervention

Given the recurrent radiculopathy and facet-associated radicular compression, posterior cervical decompression at C6-C7 and C7-T1 was performed. Before finalizing the laminectomy and decompression, a gray, friable epidural mass encasing the left C7 and C8 roots was identified and completely resected (Figure 4). Trans-surgical histopathological evaluation suggested a plasma cell neoplasm.

Postoperative work up and status

The patient experienced significant postoperative improvement in radicular pain and arm strength and was discharged on postoperative day 3. Final histopathology showed sheets of atypical plasma cells with nuclear pleomorphism; immunohistochemistry was positive for CD138, CD38, and kappa light-chain restriction, with a Ki-67 index of 80% (Figure 5), confirming the diagnosis of plasmacytoma.

Whole-body PET-CT demonstrated multiple hypermetabolic osseous lesions involving the occipital condyle, cervicothoracic junction, thoracic and lumbar vertebrae, and pelvis (Figure 6), findings initially suggestive of MM. Subsequent systemic hematologic evaluation, including peripheral blood immunophenotyping by flow cytometry, revealed circulating plasma cells accounting for 26% of white blood cells. Together with the epidural lesion histopathology and immunohistochemistry (CD138 and CD38 positivity, kappa light-chain restriction, Ki-67 proliferation index of 80%), these findings established the diagnosis of PCL.

PET-CT, positron emission tomography-computed tomography.

Adjuvant treatment

Following the establishment of the systemic diagnosis, the patient was started on combination induction therapy with daratumumab, lenalidomide, denosumab, and dexamethasone, alongside involved-field radiotherapy (30 Gy delivered in 10 fractions) targeted at the T1 lesion. Denosumab was administered as supportive antiresorptive therapy in view of the extensive osseous involvement.

Treatment complications

No surgical or treatment-related complications were documented during the postoperative period or the early phase of systemic induction therapy.

Follow-up

At the most recent follow-up visit, following initiation of systemic induction therapy, the patient remained neurologically intact with sustained improvement in radicular pain and upper limb strength. Hematological and radiological disease response remained under ongoing assessment by the hematology-oncology team, with no disease progression documented at the time of this report.

Discussion

Plasma cell neoplasms involving the cervical spine pose a diagnostic challenge. While these dyscrasias frequently affect the axial skeleton, epidural involvement mimicking degenerative radiculopathy remains rare [11,12]. Consequently, neoplastic etiologies may be overlooked in elderly patients when initial imaging is initially interpreted as common age-related spondylosis, leading to delayed diagnosis or insufficient primary surgical interventions [13].

Our case is particularly unique as the epidural plasmacytoma was the sentinel finding of PCL, an aggressive and rare variant of plasma cell dyscrasias characterized by the systemic proliferation of plasma cells in the peripheral blood [4,5]. The high Ki-67 index (80%) observed in our patient’s histopathological analysis reflects the aggressive behavior of PCL.

The limited reports of cervical plasmacytomas in the literature show a heterogeneous presentation, ranging from myelopathy to isolated radiculopathy (Table 1).

As evidenced by our review, a high index of suspicion is required when symptoms recur rapidly or present atypically. For instance, Wegener et al. reported a patient in whom plasmacytoma was initially confirmed only after a targeted biopsy of a lytic lesion, followed by progression to MM [13]. Similarly, Ha et al. described cervicothoracic involvement presenting with acute myelopathy, where surgical decompression revealed a gray, friable epidural plasma cell tumor [12].

Across the reports summarized in Table 1, cervical plasmacytomas cluster in middle-aged adults, with mean ages in the mid-fifties in the larger cohorts [9,14], although individual reports span a wide range from the fourth to the eighth decade [6,13,16,17]. Our patient, aged 76 years, is therefore among the oldest reported, reinforcing that plasma cell neoplasms involving the cervical spine should remain a diagnostic consideration even in elderly patients whose imaging is dominated by age-related spondylosis. The anatomical distribution in the literature favors the subaxial cervical spine, with several cases involving the C6-C7 and cervicothoracic junction [12,16,17], the same region affected in our patient; this predilection likely reflects the greater mechanical load and degenerative remodeling at these levels, which can mask an underlying neoplasm. Presentations range from isolated radicular pain [6,13,16,17] to frank myelopathy [12], and our case aligns with the radicular end of this spectrum but is distinguished by the rapid postoperative recurrence that signaled an atypical process.

Several features of our case are consistent with prior reports. The intraoperative finding of a gray, friable epidural plasma cell tumor encasing the nerve roots closely mirrors the description by Ha et al. [12], and the therapeutic sequence of surgical decompression for tissue diagnosis and neural decompression followed by adjuvant therapy is in keeping with the strategy advocated by Huang et al. [14] and Chen et al. [18]. The occurrence of plasmacytoma after a previous cervical fusion also resonates with the recent report by Burket et al. [17], in which a de novo plasmacytoma arose at the site of a prior ACDF, suggesting that the postoperative cervical environment may sometimes harbor or unmask an underlying plasma cell neoplasm. Important differences are equally informative. Unlike the cases of Wegener et al. [13] and Huang et al. [14], in which progression from solitary plasmacytoma to systemic MM occurred over months to years, our patient exhibited simultaneous systemic disease at the time of the spinal tumor, indicating that the leukemic phase was already established rather than evolved subsequently. Whereas Belo et al. [6] described an elderly patient with cervical radiculopathy from MM who improved with radiotherapy alone, our patient’s disease was biologically more aggressive, as reflected by the Ki-67 proliferation index of 80% and the 26% circulating plasma cell burden, precluding a radiotherapy-only strategy and mandating systemic induction. These contrasts underscore that cervical plasmacytomas are heterogeneous in their biological behavior and that imaging and surgical findings alone are insufficient to define systemic extent.

Read as a whole, Table 1 highlights several recurring themes. Clinically, the predominant symptom is neck pain with or without radiculopathy, present in the majority of reported patients [6,9,13,16,17], while myelopathy denotes more advanced cord compromise [12]. Diagnostically, tissue sampling, whether through open resection or directed biopsy, remains the decisive step in every case, consistent with the central role of histopathology and immunohistochemistry (CD138/CD38 positivity and light-chain restriction) demonstrated in our patient. Imaging patterns are dominated by lytic or hypermetabolic osseous involvement of the cervical and cervicothoracic spine, with epidural extension producing neural compression; however, as our case illustrates, an epidural plasma cell mass may be underappreciated on initial imaging, which is interpreted as degenerative disease. Therapeutically, most authors combine surgery with radiotherapy and/or chemotherapy [13,14,16,17], and progression to systemic MM emerges as the principal prognostic concern, occurring in a subset of patients initially labeled as solitary plasmacytoma [13,14]. Prognostically, outcomes are favorable when neurological compromise is relieved early and systemic therapy is instituted promptly [9,16], whereas delays in recognizing systemic disease perpetuate undertreatment. Our case extends these trends by demonstrating that systemic disease can declare itself through the spinal lesion at the outset rather than during follow-up, and that the leukemic variant carries a more aggressive trajectory requiring immediate multidrug systemic therapy.

A comprehensive literature search was performed using PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar to identify reports of cervical extradural plasmacytoma associated with PCL. To the best of our knowledge, based on our literature search, this is the first reported case in which a cervical extradural plasmacytoma served as the presenting manifestation that led to the initial systemic diagnosis of PCL. The closest reported parallel is the case by Jankovic et al. [16], in which an intraforaminal C6 plasmacytoma produced persistent radiculopathy; however, that lesion arose in the context of known recurrent MM rather than as the sentinel finding of de novo PCL. Other reports describe cervical plasmacytomas in the setting of SBP or MM [6,9,12-15,17], but none document circulating plasma cells meeting PCL criteria at first presentation. The novelty of the present report, therefore, lies in documenting a previously undescribed clinical pathway extradural cervical plasmacytoma as the index event of PCL and in emphasizing that peripheral blood immunophenotyping and PET-CT should be incorporated early when an epidural plasma cell tumor is identified, even when the initial clinical focus is purely spinal.

From a therapeutic standpoint, PCL requires intensive multidrug induction regimens that combine a proteasome inhibitor, an immunomodulatory agent, and a monoclonal antibody due to its poor prognosis. However, no randomized prospective trials specifically address PCL, and most recommendations are extrapolated from MM studies, in which systemic therapy and radiotherapy play key roles for disease control [5,6,9,18]. Surgical intervention remains indicated for cases of neurological compromise or mechanical instability, providing both decompression and tissue diagnosis [6,8,15]. When stabilization is required, anterior approaches to the middle and lower cervical spine have been associated with greater effectiveness and lower morbidity than posterior approaches [19]. Our case illustrates that maintaining vigilance regarding neoplastic disease involving the cervical spine in elderly patients with refractory symptoms is crucial for timely and appropriate hematological management.

Several limitations and knowledge gaps deserve emphasis. The evidence base guiding PCL with spinal involvement derives almost entirely from small case series and individual reports rather than prospective trials, and treatment recommendations are largely extrapolated from MM [5,18], creating uncertainty regarding optimal induction intensity, the timing of radiotherapy relative to systemic therapy, and the durability of neurological recovery. Inconsistencies across reports, such as the variable interval between solitary plasmacytoma and systemic progression [13,14], and the heterogeneous use of biopsy versus resection, limit direct comparison and reflect the absence of a standardized diagnostic pathway for cervical plasma cell tumors. Our report, by documenting the simultaneous presentation of an extradural plasmacytoma and PCL, contributes a defined reference point that may help refine the systemic workup of patients with cervical plasma cell lesions and supports prospective evaluation of combined local and systemic therapy in this entity.

Conclusions

Epidural cervical plasmacytoma is a rare cause of neck pain and radiculopathy that can closely mimic degenerative cervical disease in elderly patients, leading to delayed recognition of an underlying neoplasm. In patients with recurrent or atypical symptoms, lesion-directed tissue diagnosis -- often achieved through surgical resection or directed biopsy -- followed by systemic staging with PET-CT and peripheral blood evaluation is essential to distinguish solitary plasmacytoma from systemic plasma cell disease, including aggressive entities such as PCL, and to initiate timely multimodal therapy.
