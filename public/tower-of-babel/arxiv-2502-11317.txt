Temporal structures of the X-ray photoemission problem
F. D. Picoli¹, G. Diniz 1,2 , M. P. Lenzarini¹, I. D’Amico³, L. N. Oliveira¹

Abstract
Theoretical studies of X-ray photoemission from simple metals have traditionally focused on the frequency domain, aiming to reproduce experimental spectra. Here, we investigate the same problem in the time domain in search of physical insight and methodological advances. Our results reveal prominent aspects of the problem that are inconspicuous in the frequency domain. The calculated ℱ ⁡ ( t ) \mathcal{F}(t) exhibits a weakly damped harmonic oscillation that modulates the Doniach-Sunjic power-law decay of the photoemission rate, a behavior arising from the coherent interference between two classes of particle-hole excitations. From a methodological perspective, we advance the time-dependent numerical renormalization-group (NRG) approach by exploring the eNRG method, a real-space variant that is more flexible than Wilson’s construction. Giving special attention to strong core-hole potentials, we compare the photocurrents obtained from two complementary time-dependent eNRG algorithms with (i) an analytical expression for ℱ ⁡ ( t ) \mathcal{F}(t) that becomes highly accurate at moderately long times and (ii) results from numerical diagonalization of a tight-binding Hamiltonian, which covers the time interval in which our analytical expression is less precise. Anticipating extensions to correlated-impurity models, we identify the sources of deviation and discuss the virtues and drawbacks of the two algorithms.

II
Summary of findings

Photoemission is a relatively simple phenomenon. X radiation ejects a core electron, leaving behind a hole whose potential attracts the conduction electrons. Experimentalists measure the photoemission current as a function of energy. The resulting spectrum shows a peak at the threshold energy, the minimal energy required to photoeject the core electron. The peak is asymmetric and somewhat broad. The asymmetry reflects the creation of electron-hole pairs above the threshold, while the breadth reflects the finite lifetime of the core hole.

In contrast, we focus on the time dependence of the photocurrent, given by the squared projection of the quantum state
\ket{\Psi(t)}
on the ground state before the x-ray creates the core hole. Insofar as this ground state constitutes our initial state
\ket{\Psi(0)}
, the photocurrent is mathematically equivalent to the
fidelity

\mathcal{F}(t)
of
\ket{\Psi(t)}
relative to its value at
t=0
. X-ray photoemission spectroscopy (XPS) measures the Fourier transform of the fidelity
[
33
,
53
,
46
]
. X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) complements XPS by measuring the probability of promoting a core electron to the conduction band
[
46
,
12
]
.

Interest in XAS and XPS motivated mathematical studies in the late 1960s
[
45
,
56
,
24
]
, which identified power laws with universal exponents in the near-threshold frequency dependence of the currents. The core potential shifts the phases of the conduction electrons, and the phase shift
\delta
of the conduction state at the Fermi level parameterizes the power laws. Nozières and De Dominicis determined the XAS exponent
[
56
]

\displaystyle\beta\equiv 2\left(\dfrac{\delta}{\pi}-1\right)^{2},

(1)

and Doniach and Sunjic found the XPS exponent
[
24
]

\displaystyle\alpha=2\left(\dfrac{\delta}{\pi}\right)^{2}.

(2)

Subsequent research determined the prefactors of the power laws and identified a secondary threshold at higher energy, associated with excitations from the
bound state
that splits off the bottom of the conduction band under the attraction of the core hole
[
14
,
57
,
16
]
. We are unaware of parallel developments in the time domain.

XAS and XPS proved instrumental in extending NRG to excitation properties in the frequency domain
[

In the time domain, the photoemission problem is more than a checkpoint in the algorithm-development itinerary, exhibiting features that merit physical discussion. As expected, the fidelity decreases following a Doniach-Sunjic power law, but we shall see that a sharply defined damped oscillation envelops the decay. This behavior evidences interference among transitions from the initial many-body state (the quantum state of the electron gas before absorption of the x-ray) to the final many-body states (the excited states generated by the sudden creation of a localized potential). The occupation of the bound state sorts the final states into two classes: the
plugged
many-body states, with an occupied bound state, and the
unplugged
ones, with a vacant bound state. The energy needed to vacate the bound state determines the frequency of the oscillation. The damping is due to the distinct decay rates: the amplitude of the interference diminishes with time because the unplugged states decay faster than the plugged ones. In summary, the time dependence of the fidelity displays rich physics, bringing to light features that are hardly discernible in plots of the photoemission rate as a function of frequency.

From an operational perspective, the computation of the fidelity is simpler than straightforward applications of the NRG procedure, such as the computation of thermodynamic properties for the Kondo model. The latter calculation relies on three controllable approximations, two of which arise in constructing the renormalization-group transformation: (i) the logarithmic discretization of the conduction band, followed by an exact Lanczos transformation, and (ii) the infrared truncation of the resulting infinite series.
The subsequent iterative diagonalization of the resulting Hamiltonian calls for an additional approximation: (iii) the ultraviolet truncation of the Hamiltonian matrix at each iteration.

The ultraviolet truncation introduces inaccuracies in the time-dependent properties, which calls for a more elaborate procedure
[
1
]
. By contrast, in the photoemission problem, the initial and final Hamiltonians are quadratic and dispense with iterative diagonalization and, consequently, with ultraviolet truncation. Only approximations (i) and (ii) are required. Approximation (ii) is not a concern, since a modest computational effort is sufficient to keep the resulting deviations under control. We will therefore focus our attention on the inaccuracies resulting from the discretization.

To gauge such imprecision, we have established two benchmarks. One is the time dependence resulting from brute-force numerical diagonalization of the model Hamiltonian. The other benchmark is an analytical expression for the time-dependent fidelity that is very accurate, except for short times. Therefore, the two benchmarks are complementary—the numerical diagonalization yields essentially exact results at short and moderately large times. Starting at moderately large times, the analytical expression is likewise precise. From the two, we can always choose a nearly exact value for the fidelity at any time
t
. We call this set of data the
reference
values. Armed with them, we focus our attention on the renormalization-group procedure.

Our study builds upon the real space formulation known as eNRG
[
27
]
instead of Wilson’s momentum space approach. This formulation is more flexible as it incorporates three dimensionless parameters rather than two: the

Subtraction of the reference fidelities from the output of an eNRG run with specific

\theta

These deviations are comparable to those encountered in NRG calculations of thermodynamic properties for the Kondo and Anderson models
[
83
,
40
,
41
]
, where irregular high-temperature behavior and small unphysical oscillations as a function of temperature were observed. We call
transitory
the first type of deviation and

the second. The transitory deviations are due to operators distinguishing the discretized conduction band Hamiltonian from the corresponding fixed point of the renormalization group transformation. These operators are irrelevant: under this transformation, they decay proportionally to the energy or even faster
[
40
]
.

Persistent deviations are mathematical analogs of the de Haas-van Alphen oscillations
[
4
]
. Just as a magnetic field bunches the continuum of conduction states into Landau levels and produces de Haas–van Alphen oscillations, the logarithmic discretization of the band introduces artificial oscillations in the thermal dependence of thermodynamic properties. The NRG parameter
z
shifts the logarithmic sequence uniformly and hence shifts the phase of the oscillatory deviations. Averaging the calculated thermodynamical property on a set of uniformly spaced
z

[
64
]
.

The same two sources of deviations arise in the calculations of the fidelity, with more damaging consequences. The irrelevant operators again give rise to transitory deviations, but their decay is slow because it is controlled by the uncertainty principle, not by the Boltzmann factor. The discretization of the conduction levels introduces persistent fluctuations analogous to the harmonic oscillations in the thermal dependence of the thermodynamic properties. Here, however, the persistent deviations are polychromatic, their Fourier spectrum extending to very low frequencies.

To minimize transitory and persistent deviations, we explore two smoothing algorithms. The first is simple, conceptually and operationally. We let the offset
\zeta
be a large integer, fix the other eNRG parameters, and compute the fidelity from the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the resulting Hamiltonian. This procedure yields essentially exact fidelities, free of transitory or persistent deviations, over an interval ranging from
t=0
to a maximum time
T_{\zeta}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
proportional to
\zeta
.

Despite this procedure being effective for quadratic Hamiltonians, its extension to correlated impurity models is limited by the exponential growth of the computational cost with
\zeta
. As a consequence, adjusting
{T}_{\zeta}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
to the screening timescale of the Kondo problem becomes prohibitive

1

1

This difficulty appeared, for example, in a recent calculation of spectral densities for the two-channel Kondo model

. Nevertheless, this rapid growth does not prevent calculations for smaller

The second algorithm combines averaging on

Our study of the time-dependent photoemission rate, therefore, comprises two complementary eNRG procedures. The first offers essentially exact results over a time interval with an extension limited by considerations of computational cost. The second is unable to deal with the transitory fluctuations, but becomes effective at long times because it washes out the persistent artifacts of the discretization. A combination of the two methods emerges as a promising tool.

t=0

\nicefrac{{E_{r}^{\phantom{\dagger}}t}}{{\hbar}}
of the exponentials in the sum on the right-hand side of Eq. (
29
) vanish. The sum then reduces to the normalization of
\ket{\Psi_0\z}
, and the equality to
\mathcal{F}(t=0)=1
. As
t
grows, the eigenstates
\ket{r}
satisfying
\nicefrac{{|E_{r}|^{\phantom{\dagger}}t}}{{\hbar}}\gtrsim 1
acquire significantly different phases, and the resulting destructive interferences reduce the fidelity. At very long times, one expects the first term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
30
) to approach zero and the fidelity to approach the following small constant:

\displaystyle c_{\mbox{res}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}=\sum_{r}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\absolutevalue{\braket{r}{\Psi_0\z}}^{4}.

(31)

In the continuum limit
L\to\infty
, the overlaps
\innerproduct{r}{\Psi_0\z}
become infinitesimal, and
c_{\mbox{res}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\to 0
, a consequence of the Anderson orthogonality catastrophe
[
3
]
. However, for finite lattices, the sum on the right-hand side of Eq. (
31
) is nonzero, and so
\mathcal{F}(t\to\infty)\neq 0
. The numerical results in the following sections will confirm these expectations.

Consider now the practical aspects of computing the fidelity. For small
L
, given the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{K}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
, computing the right-hand side of Eq. (
29
) is straightforward. However, the computational cost rapidly becomes prohibitive as the lattice size grows because the number of contributing many-body eigenstates is
N_{r}=\binom{{2L}}{L}
. In practice, therefore, an alternative numerical approach is preferable.

IV.3.2
Single-particle method

For quadratic Hamiltonians and large
L
, an alternative approach proves more practical
[
56
,
38
]
. We rewrite the projection on the right-hand side of Eq. (
6
) as

\displaystyle\innerproduct{\Psi_0\z}{\Psi_t\z}=\matrixelement{\emptyset}{a_{-1}\z\ldots a_{-L}\z a_{-L}^\dagger(t)\ldots a_{-1}^\dagger(t)}{\emptyset},

(32)

where
\ket{\emptyset}
is the vacuum state, and
a_{n}^{\dagger}(t)
(
n=-L,\ldots,L-1
) denotes the time evolution of the initial-Hamiltonian eigenstate
a_{n}^{\dagger}
, driven by the final-state Hamiltonian, that is,

\displaystyle a_{n}^{\dagger}(t)=e^{-i\mathcal{H}_{K}^{\phantom{\dagger}}t/\hbar}a_{n}^{\dagger}e^{+i\mathcal{H}_{K}^{\phantom{\dagger}}t/\hbar},

(33)

or after projection onto the basis of the final-Hamiltonian eigenstates
b_{\ell}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
(
\ell=-L,\ldots,L-1
),

\displaystyle a_{n}^{\dagger}(t)=\sum_{\ell=-L}^{L-1}\anticommutator{a_n^\dagger}{b_{\ell}\z}e^{-i\epsilon_{\ell}^{\phantom{\dagger}}t/\hbar}b_{\ell}^{\dagger}.

(34)

Wick’s theorem then turns Eq. (
32
) into the expression

\displaystyle\innerproduct{\Psi_0\z}{\Psi_t\z}=\det\underline{\underline{M}}(t),

(35)

where
\underline{\underline{M}}(t)
is the
L\times L
matrix with elements

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{M}}_{m,n}(t)=\sum_{\ell=-L}^{L-1}\anticommutator{a_m\z}{b_\ell^\dagger}e^{-i\epsilon_{\ell}t/\hbar}\anticommutator{b_\ell\z}{a_n^\dagger}.

(36)

To accurately compute the fidelity up to a maximum time
\bar{t}
, we must work with an energy splitting (
25
) small compared to
\hbar/\bar{t}
, so that
\exp(-\sfrac{i\Delta_L\z\bar{t}}{\hbar})\approx 1
. The condition, therefore, is
L=\nicefrac{{\tau\bar{t}}}{{(\hbar\eta)}}
, where
\eta\pi\ll 1
.

IV.4
Time scales

The coupling
\tau
fixes the conduction bandwidth, controls the short-time behavior of the fidelity, and, within the context of numerical calculations, makes it convenient to work with dimensionless times
\omega_{\tau}^{\ }t
, where
\omega_{\tau}^{\ }\equiv\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{\hbar}}
.

At long times, with
\omega_{\tau}^{\ }t\gg 1
, the characteristic time
T_{L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
, defined by the equality

\displaystyle\omega_{\tau}^{\ }T_{L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}=2L

(37)

sets the time scale.
Specifically, comparison with Eq. (
25
) shows that the smallest energy in the single-particle spectrum is inversely proportional to the characteristic time; namely,

\displaystyle\Delta_{L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}=2\pi\hbar/T_{L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}.

(38)

Since the energy differences in the spectra of
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{K}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
are proportional to
\Delta_{L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
, the periods of the oscillations that contribute to
\mathcal{F}(t)
are multiples of the characteristic time
T_{L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
. In other words, at
t=T_{L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
, the fidelity completes a cycle. It follows that
\mathcal{F}(T_{L}^{\phantom{\dagger}})=1
, a finite-size effect without physical equivalence in the continuum.

At moderately long times, such that
t\ll T_{L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
while
\omega_{\tau}^{\ }t\gg 1
, energies of
\order{\dl}
contribute insignificantly to the many-body energy in the exponent
-iE_{r}^{\phantom{\dagger}}t/\hbar
on the right-hand side of Eq. (
28
) and can be neglected. In this time regime, the fidelity is therefore independent of
T_{L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
. However, this changes as
t
increases. An example with
K=-2\tau
in Sect.
VI.2.2
(see Fig.
9
(d)) shows deviations of
0.6\%
at
t=T_{L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}/10
, in comparison to the fidelity calculated at the same instant in the limit
L\to\infty
.

K

\epsilon_{ph}
of one such particle-hole pair undistinguishable from zero at small times. More specifically, as long as

Still, the inset leaves two questions unanswered since it does not explain why the fidelity oscillates or why the amplitude of the oscillations decays, even after multiplication by

V.1
Plugged and unplugged many-body eigenstates

To track the origin of the damped oscillations, it is necessary to examine the eigenstates of the final-state Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{K}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
that contribute to the fidelity, that is, the many-body eigenstates comprising
L
occupied levels. The bound state
b_{-L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
, with energy
\epsilon_{-L}
, is special among the single-particle eigenstates. Since its occupation
n_{-L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\equiv b_{-L}^{\dagger}b_{-L}
is conserved, the many-body eigenstates
\ket{r}
of
\mathcal{H}_{K}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
can be divided into two classes, depending on whether
n_{-L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}=1
or
n_{-L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}=0
. The depiction in Fig.
2
suggests the adjectives
plugged
and
unplugged
to label the former and the latter eigenstates, respectively. The lowest-energy plugged state is the ground state
\ket{\Omega}
of the final Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{K}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
, with energy
E_{0}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
. The lowest unplugged state is
b_{0}^{\dagger}b_{-L}\ket{\Omega}
, since
b_{0}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
is the first single-particle level with positive energy, and its energy is
E_{0}^{\phantom{\dagger}}+\epsilon_{B}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
, where
\epsilon_{B}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
is the energy needed to promote an electron from the bound state to the first unoccupied level above the Fermi level, as the double-headed arrow in the figure indicates,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{B}^{\phantom{\dagger}}=\dfrac{\Delta_{L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}}{2}-\epsilon_{-L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}.

(39)

V.1.1
One-to-one correspondence between the plugged and unplugged eigenstates

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the set of all the plugged states and
\mathcal{U}
, the set of the unplugged states. There is a one-to-one correspondence between the states in
\mathcal{P}
and in
\mathcal{U}
. To verify this statement, take any plugged state
\ket{P}\in\mathcal{P}
and construct a many-body state
\ket{U}
so that (i) the bound state is vacant and (ii) the occupation of each level
\epsilon_{p}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
(
p=-L+1,\ldots,L-1
) is equal to the occupation of level
\epsilon_{p-1}
in
\ket{P}
.

As the arrows in Fig.
3
(a) indicate, the energy difference between
\ket{U}
and
\ket{P}
is

\displaystyle E_{U}^{\phantom{\dagger}}-E_{P}^{\phantom{\dagger}}=\epsilon_{-L+1}^{\phantom{\dagger}}-\epsilon_{-L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}+(L-1)\Delta_{L}^{\phantom{\dagger}},

(40)

and since
(L-1)\Delta_{L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
is the energy difference between
\epsilon_{-L+1}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
and
\epsilon_{0}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
, it follows from Eq. (
39
) that

\displaystyle E_{U}^{\phantom{\dagger}}-E_{P}^{\phantom{\dagger}}=\epsilon_{B}^{\phantom{\dagger}}.

(41)

V.1.2
Contributions from the plugged and unplugged states to the fidelity

The conclusion that the energy separation between each plugged eigenstate
\ket{P}
and the corresponding unplugged eigenstate
\ket{U}
is uniform suggests the following partition of the sum on the right-hand side of Eq. (
29
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(t)=\absolutevalue{\pp_P\z+ \exp(-i\omb t)\pp_U\z}^{2}.

(43)

Here,
\omega_{B}^{\ }\equiv\nicefrac{{\epsilon_{B}^{\phantom{\dagger}}}}{{\hbar}}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{P}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\equiv\sum_{P}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\absolutevalue{\braket{P}{\Psi_0\z}}^{2}\exp(-i \dfrac{E_P\z t}{\hbar}),

(44)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{U}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\equiv\sum_{U}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\absolutevalue{\braket{U}{\Psi_0\z}}^{2}\exp(-i \dfrac{E_{P(U)}\z t}{\hbar}),

(45)

where the sums span the sets of the plugged eigenstates
\ket{P}
and the unplugged eigenstates
\ket{U}
, respectively. In Eq. (
45
),
E_{P(U)}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\equiv E_{U}^{\phantom{\dagger}}-\epsilon_{B}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
denotes the energy of the plugged eigenstate associated with
\ket{U}
.

Although the time-dependent exponentials on the right-hand sides of Eqs. (
44
) and (
45
) are identical, the projections
\innerproduct{P}{\Psi_0\z}
and
\innerproduct{U}{\Psi_0\z}
are different and therefore deserve special attention. Consider a filled level
n
in the initial ground state
\ket{\Psi_0\z}
and a filled level
p
in the plugged state
\ket{P}
in Fig.
3
(a). Assuming that neither level lies too far from the Fermi level, so that Eqs. (
19
) and (
22
) are reliable, the initial energy
\varepsilon_{n}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
and the final energy
\epsilon_{p}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
differ by

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n}^{\phantom{\dagger}}-\epsilon_{p}^{\phantom{\dagger}}=\Delta_{L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\left(n-p+\dfrac{\delta}{\pi}\right).

(46)

Next, consider the levels occupied in the unplugged state
\ket{U}
. To each level
p
in the many-body state
\ket{P}
, the construction in Fig.
3
associates a filled level
u=p+1
in
\ket{U}
. The energy difference between the initial level
n
and the final level
u
is

\varepsilon_{n}^{\phantom{\dagger}}-\epsilon_{u}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
, which can be written as

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n}^{\phantom{\dagger}}-\epsilon_{u}^{\phantom{\dagger}}=\Delta_{L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\left(n-p+\left(\dfrac{\delta}{\pi}-1\right)\right).

(47)

The difference on the left-hand side of Eq. (
46
) is the denominator of the fraction on the right-hand side of Eq. (
26
) and hence determines the projection
\anticommutator{a_n^\dagger}{b_p\z}
between the two single-particle eigenstates. The projection
\innerproduct{P}{\Psi_0\z}
, of the plugged many-body state
\ket{P}
upon the initial state
\ket{\Psi_0\z}
is the determinant of the
L\times L
matrix whose elements are the projections
\anticommutator{a_n\dagger}{b_p\z}
, between the occupied levels
a_{n}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
in
\ket{\Psi_0\z}
and the occupied levels
b_{p}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
in
\ket{P}
.

Likewise, Eq. (
47
) determines the projection
\anticommutator{a_n^\dagger}{b_u\z}
, needed to compute the projection
\innerproduct{U}{\Psi_0\z}
of the unplugged many-body state
\ket{U}
upon
\ket{\Psi_0\z}
. The comparison between the right-hand sides of Eqs. (
46
) and (
47
) shows that the substitution
\delta\to\delta-\pi
transforms the former into the latter.
Since
\sin(\delta)
only changes sign under the same substitution, we can see from Eq. (
26
) that, except for levels close to the band edges, the construction in Fig.
3
maps the single-particle projections needed to compute
\innerproduct{P}{\Psi_0\z}
onto those entering the computation of
\innerproduct{U}{\Psi_0\z}
. In other words, the construction establishes the mapping

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\braket{P}{\Psi_0\z}}_{\delta}\mapsto\absolutevalue{\braket{U}{\Psi_0\z}}_{\delta-\pi},

(48)

where each subscript is the phase shift needed to calculate the associated many-body projection.

Inspection of the right-hand sides of Eqs. (
44
) and (
45
) then extends the mapping to the sums
\mathcal{P}_{P}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{U}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
, namely,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{P,\delta}^{\phantom{\dagger}}(t)\mapsto\mathcal{P}_{U,(\delta-\pi)}(t).

(49)

We only have to compare Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
) to identify the analogous mapping between the Doniach-Sunjic and the Nozières-De Dominicis exponents:

\displaystyle\beta\mapsto\alpha.

(50)

V.1.3
Interplay between the Doniach-Sunjic and Nozières-De Dominicis components of the fidelity

The fidelity, given by Eq. (
43
), results from the interference between two similar photoemission processes with distinct threshold energies, split by
\epsilon_{B}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
. The first process involves excitations to the plugged final states, has a lower threshold energy
E_{P}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
, and follows the Doniach-Sunjic law:

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\pp_{P,\delta}\z}^{2}\propto(\omega_{\tau}t)^{-\alpha}.

(51)

The second one comprises excitations to the unplugged states and has the threshold energy
E_{U}^{\phantom{\dagger}}=E_{P}^{\phantom{\dagger}}+\epsilon_{B}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
. Given the mappings (
49
) and (
50
), Eq. (
51
) implies that

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\pp_{U,(\delta-\pi)}\z}^{2}\propto(\omega_{\tau}t)^{-\beta}.

(52)

To interpret Eq. (
52
) physically, compare the unplugged many-body states to the final states in an XAS experiment. As illustrated by the electron distributions in states
\ket{U}
and
\ket{U'}
in Fig.
3
, each unplugged state contains an electron that has been promoted from the bound state to a single-particle state above the Fermi level. This process is analogous to the excitation of an electron from the core orbital to a vacant conduction level during the absorption experiment. Consequently, the current due to the unplugged states follows a power law with the XAS exponent (
1
).

In the phase-shift range of interest,
0<\delta<\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}}
, the exponent
\beta
always exceeds
\alpha
. Consequently, the contribution from
\mathcal{P}_{U,(\delta-\pi)^{\phantom{\dagger}}}
to the fidelity dies out faster, as does the amplitude of interference between the two processes. The partition of the final eigenstates into plugged and unplugged states thus explains the damping of the oscillations in Fig.
1
. The algebraic approach in the following section confirms this argument.

V.2
Analytical results

Appendix
A
treats Eq. (
43
) analytically, based on two approximations that produce accurate results at large times, when the dominant contribution to the fidelity comes from single-particle eigenstates close to the Fermi level: (i) in the initial Hamiltonian (
K=0
) the energy splitting
\Delta_{L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
between neighboring levels is assumed to be uniform throughout the conduction band; (ii) in the final Hamiltonian (
K<0
), the conduction-band levels above the bound state are assumed to be uniformly phase-shifted. A closed expression for the time-dependent fidelity is derived:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(t)=\dfrac{1+\mathcal{R}_{t}^{2}+2\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\cos(\omb t+\vartheta_t\z)}{(1+Q)^{2}}\mathcal{T}_{t}^{-\alpha}

(53)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{t}\equiv\exp(\int_{0}^{\pi\omtau t}\dfrac{1- \cos(x)}{x}\,\dd x),

(54)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\equiv Q\mathcal{T}_{t}^{{-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}}},

(55)

\displaystyle\vartheta_{t}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\equiv\dfrac{\beta-\alpha}{2}\sii(\pi\omega_{\tau}^{\ }t),

(56)

with
\sii(x)={\int_{0}^{x}du{\frac{\sin(u)}{u}}}
and

\displaystyle Q\equiv\frac{{\mathcal{P}}_{U}^{\phantom{\dagger}}(0)}{{\mathcal{P}}_{P}^{\phantom{\dagger}}(0)}.

(57)

The ratio on the right-hand side of Eq. (
57
) involves two ground-state projections and, therefore, depends only on the phase shift
\delta
. As explained in the following, the numerical computation of
{\mathcal{P}}_{P}^{\phantom{\dagger}}(0)
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{U}^{\phantom{\dagger}}(0)
is not demanding. Therefore, throughout this report, we have relied on Eq. (
57
) to determine
Q
and obtain the fidelity from the analytical expression (
53
). For example, to get the green curve in Fig.
1
, we have used
Q=0.17
.

V.2.1
Individual contributions of the plugged and unplugged many-body states

As mentioned previously, Eq. (
53
) is an algebraic statement of the conclusions drawn in Sec.
V.1
. To bring this point home, Appendix
A
also shows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{P,\delta}^{\phantom{\dagger}}(t)\equiv\dfrac{1}{1+Q}{\mathcal{T}_{t}}^{-{\frac{\alpha}{2}}}\exp(-i\frac{\alpha}2\sii(\pi\omtau t)),

(58)

and that the transformation
\delta\to\delta-\pi
implies
Q\mapsto 1/Q
. Therefore, under this transformation, Eq. (
58
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{U,(\delta-\pi)}^{\phantom{\dagger}}(t)\equiv\dfrac{Q}{1+Q}{\mathcal{T}_{t}}^{-{\frac{\beta}{2}}}\exp(-i\frac{\beta}2
\sii(\pi\omtau t)).

(59)

For more explicit comparison between Eqs. (
58
) and (
59
), with the help of Eq. (
55
) [Eq. (
56
)], we eliminate
Q
from the numerator of the fraction (
\alpha
from the argument of the exponential) on the right-hand side of Eq. (

). These substitutions show that

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{U,(\delta-\pi)}^{\phantom{\dagger}}(t)\equiv\dfrac{\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\phantom{\dagger}}e^{-i\vartheta_{t}^{\phantom{\dagger}}}}{1+Q}{\mathcal{T}_{t}}^{-{\frac{\alpha}{2}}}\exp(-i\frac{\alpha}2\sii(\pi\omtau t)).

(60)

Appendix

derives Eq. (

) independently. Equations (

) and (

) establish the mapping
\mathcal{P}_{P,\delta}\mapsto\mathcal{P}_{U,(\delta-\pi)}
, between the contributions of the unplugged states and the plugged states to the fidelity, and thus ratify the arguments presented in Sec.
V.1
.

V.3
Analytical versus numerical results

Returning the focus to the numerical results, the solid green line in Fig.

represents Eq. (

Under this condition, Eq. (

.

For large values of its argument, the cosine integral on the right-hand side is

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{t}=e^{\gamma}\pi\omega_{\tau}^{\ }t,

which reduces Eq. (

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(t)=\dfrac{\left(\pi e^{\gamma}\right)^{-\alpha}}{(1+Q)^{2}}\left(1+\mathcal{R}_{t}^{2}+2\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\cos(\omb t+\vartheta_t\z)\right)(\omega_{\tau}^{\ }t)^{-\alpha}.

(63)

Equation (

\displaystyle(\omega_{\tau}^{\ }t)^{\alpha}\mathcal{F}(t)=\dfrac{\left(\pi e^{\gamma}\right)^{-\alpha}}{(1+Q)^{2}}\left(1+\mathcal{R}_{t}^{2}+2\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\cos(\omb t+\vartheta_t\z)\right).

(64)

V.4
Physical interpretation

The trinomial within parentheses on the right-hand side of Eq. (
64
) is the law-of-cosines sum of two vectors with magnitudes unity and
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
whose phases differ by
(\omega_{B}^{\ }t+\vartheta_{t}^{\phantom{\dagger}})
. The function
\vartheta_{t}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
quickly approaches
\left[\frac{\pi}{4}\left(\beta-\alpha\right)\right]
as
\omega_{\tau}^{\ }t
increases; therefore, the sum oscillates with frequency
\omega_{B}^{\ }
and amplitude
2\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
. From the definition (
55
) and Eq. (
62
) we can see that
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
vanishes as
\omega_{\tau}^{\ }t\to\infty
: the decay of
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
abates the oscillation.

More physical considerations lead to the same conclusion.
\mathcal{P}_{P,\delta}
is the contribution of the plugged states to the fidelity, and so
\absolutevalue{\pp_{P,\delta}}^{2}
follows the Doniach-Sunjic power law
[
24
]
. Likewise,
\mathcal{P}_{U,(\delta-\pi)}
is the contribution of the unplugged states, and
\absolutevalue{\pp_{U,(\delta-\pi)}}^{2}
follows the Nozières-De Dominicis power law
[
56
]
. The energies of the plugged and unplugged states differing by
\epsilon_{B}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
, hence the phases of
\mathcal{P}_{P,\delta}(t)
and
\mathcal{P}_{U,(\delta-\pi)}^{\phantom{\dagger}}(t)
differ by
\omega_{B}^{\ }t
. Therefore, instead of adding up to
\absolutevalue{\pp_{P,\delta}}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\pp_{U,(\delta-\pi)}}^{2}
, the two contributions interfere. The interference gives rise to the oscillations in Fig.
1
. The more rapid decay (Nozières-De Dominicis-like) of
\mathcal{P}_{U,(\delta-\pi)}
relative to the (Doniach-Sunjic-like) decay of
\mathcal{P}_{P,\delta}
accounts for the damping of the oscillations.

To reinforce this interpretation with numerical evidence, consider now the individual contributions
{\mathcal{F}}_{P}^{\phantom{\dagger}}(t)\equiv|\mathcal{P}_{P}(t)|^{2}
and
{\mathcal{F}}_{U}^{\phantom{\dagger}}(t)\equiv|\mathcal{P}_{U}(t)|^{2}
, of the plugged and unplugged eigenstates to the fidelity. To determine them, note that the operator
b_{-L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}b_{-L}^{\dagger}
projects
\ket{\Psi_t}
on the subset of unplugged eigenstates; accordingly, the following expression determines the unplugged component:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{U}(t)^{\phantom{\dagger}}={\absolutevalue{\mel{\Psi_0\z}{b_{-L}\z b_{-L}^\dagger}{\Psi_t}}}^{2}.

(65)

The following equality expresses the matrix element on the right-hand side of Eq. (
65
):

\displaystyle\matrixelement{\Psi_0\z}{b_{-L}\z b_{-L}^\dagger}{\Psi_t}=

\displaystyle\qquad\matrixelement{\emptyset}{a_{-1}\ldots a_{-L} b_{-L}\z b_{-L}^\dagger a_{-L}^\dagger(t)\ldots a_{-1}^\dagger(t)}{\emptyset}.

(66)

Analogously to Eq. (
32
), Eq. (
66
) can be evaluated using the procedure in Sec.
IV.3.2
.

Since
b_{-L}^{\dagger}b_{-L}
projects
\ket{\Psi_t}
on the subset of plugged eigenstates, the following expression determines the plugged component of the fidelity:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{P}^{\phantom{\dagger}}(t)^{\phantom{\dagger}}={\absolutevalue{\mel{\Psi_0\z}{b_{-L}^\dagger b_{-L}\z}{\Psi_t}}}^{2},

(67)

The identity
b_{-L}^{\dagger}b_{-L}=1-b_{-L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}b_{-L}^{\dagger}
shows that the matrix element in Eq. (
65
) is the difference between the right-hand sides of Eqs. (
32
) and (
60
) and allows calculation of
{\mathcal{F}}_{P}^{\phantom{\dagger}}(t)
without additional computational effort.

Now that the physical oscillations observed in the photocurrent are well understood, we can turn our attention to reproducing the results discussed in this section using the eNRG method. The motivation here is methodological: to develop an effective smoothing procedure capable of performing time-dependent calculations for non-quadratic Hamiltonians.

VI
Renormalization-group approach

Three integers
\zeta\geq 0
,
\lambda>1
, and
\theta\equiv\log(w)/\log(\lambda)
(
w\in\mathbb{N}
) parameterize the construction of the eNRG, which divides the
L\to\infty
tight-binding lattice into an infinite chain of cells, or intervals. The first
\zeta
intervals
\mathcal{C}_{\ell}^{\zeta}
(
\ell=0,1,\ldots,\zeta
) contain a single site each. The following interval
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
starts a succession
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
(
n=0,1,\ldots
), each member of which encompasses
w\lambda^{n}
sites. For each cell
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
, a linear combination of the pertinent operators
c_{\ell}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
then defines a normalized Fermi operator
f_{n}^{\phantom{\dagger}}

[
27
]
. Figure
6
schematically illustrates this construction. Although the discretization parameters
\lambda
and
w
were defined as integers, Eqs. (
68
) and (
69
) can be rigorously extended to real
\lambda\geq 1
and
\theta\in[1,-1]

[
27
]
.

VI.1
Discretized Hamiltonian

Along with the
c_{\ell}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
(
\ell=0,1,\ldots,\zeta
), the
f_{n}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
(
n=0,1,\ldots
) form a basis, upon which we project the model Hamiltonian. The projection brings Eq. (
5
) to the form
[
27
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{K}^{\lambda,\zeta,\theta}=

\displaystyle\left(\tau\sum_{\ell=0}^{\zeta-1}c^{\dagger}_{\ell}c_{\ell+1}+\tau c^{\dagger}_{\zeta}f_{0}^{\phantom{\dagger}}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tau_{n}f_{n}^{\dagger}f_{n+1}+\mathrm{H.~c.}\right)

\displaystyle+Kc_{0}^{\dagger}c_{0}^{\phantom{\dagger}},

(68)

where

\displaystyle\tau_{n}^{\phantom{\dagger}}=\tau\lambda^{{-n-\theta-\frac{1}{2}}}.

(69)

The coefficients
\tau_{n}
(
n=0,1,\ldots
) in Eq. (
69
) decay exponentially with
n
. To compute the fidelity at time
t
, therefore, it is safe to truncate the infinite series on the right-hand side of Eq. (
69
) at
n=\mathcal{N}
, where
\mathcal{N}
is the smallest integer such that

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{N}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}t}{\hbar}<\eta,

(70)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{N}}\equiv\tau\lambda^{-\mathcal{N}-1/2},

(71)

is the smallest coefficient
\tau_{n=\mathcal{N}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
for
\theta=0
.
\eta\ll 1
is a fixed dimensionless parameter that controls the accuracy of the approximation.

Given
t
, the following equality then defines the truncated scaled Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{K}^{\mathcal{N}}
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{N}}}{\mathcal{H}_{K}^{\mathcal{N}}}=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\zeta-1}\tau c^{\dagger}_{\ell}c_{\ell+1}+\tau c^{\dagger}_{\zeta}f_{0}^{\phantom{\dagger}}+\sum_{n=0}^{\mathcal{N}-1}\tau_{n}f_{n}^{\dagger}f_{n+1}+\mathrm{H.~c.}\right)

\displaystyle+Kc_{0}^{\dagger}c_{0}.

(72)

This scaling ensures that the spectrum of
\mathcal{H}_{K}^{\mathcal{N}}
consists of positive eigenvalues ranging from unity to
2\lambda^{{\mathcal{N}+1}/2}
and negative eigenvalues ranging from
-1
to
-2\lambda^{\mathcal{N}+1/2}
.

Given the Hamiltonian (
72
), calculating the fidelity
\mathcal{F}_{\zeta,\theta}^{\phantom{\dagger}}(t)
requires first diagonalizing the square matrix of dimension
\mathcal{N}+\zeta+1
representing the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{K}^{\mathcal{N}}
. It is important to emphasize that this Hamiltonian is quadratic (noninteracting) and therefore does not require the iterative diagonalization procedure of standard NRG, allowing for efficient treatment within the single-particle procedure in Sec.
IV.3.2
. Condition (
70
), with
\tau_{\mathcal{N}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
given by Eq. (
69
), shows that the truncation number
\mathcal{N}
grows logarithmically with
t
, a dependence that contrasts with the linear growth of the tight-binding lattice in Sec.
IV
. The computational cost of an eNRG run covering a given time interval is, therefore, substantially smaller than that of a tight-binding computation of
\mathcal{F}(t)
in the same time range. Section
V
presents examples.

VI.1.1
Single-particle spectrum

With
K=0
, the right-hand side of Eq. (
72
) is particle-hole symmetric, and so is the resulting single-particle spectrum. Given
\zeta
, it is convenient to choose
\mathcal{N}
of the opposite parity so that
\mathcal{N}+\zeta+1
is an even number, henceforth denoted
2M
. Of the single-particle eigenvalues,
M
will be positive and
M
negative. The same partition results for
K\neq 0
, and the following approximate expressions describe the single-particle eigenvalues
[
27
]
:

\displaystyle E_{j}^{\pm}(K)=\pm\tau\lambda^{{2(j-1)-\theta\pm\frac{2\delta}{\pi}}}\qquad\left(j=1,2,\ldots,M\right),

(73)

with the phase shift
\delta
given by Eq. (
23
).

Equation (
73
) is accurate for the eigenvalues in the range
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{N}}\ll\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{N}}\absolutevalue{\erg{j}\z}\ll\tau
. The inaccuracy grows with
j
, and deviations of a few percent become visible close to the upper limit. For negative
K
, as
\absolutevalue{K}
grows past
2\tau
, the lowest eigenvalue
E_{-M}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
splits off the bottom of the conduction band. For
K<-2\tau
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{N}}E_{-M}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\approx K+\dfrac{\tau^{2}}{K}.

(74)

We will associate the Fermi operators
g_{j\pm}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
and
h_{j\pm}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
(
j=1,2,\ldots,M
) with the single-particle eigenstates corresponding to the eigenvalues
E_{j}^{\pm}(K)
and
E_{j}^{\pm}(0)
of the final and initial Hamiltonians, respectively.

Since Eq. (
68
) reduces to the tight-binding Hamiltonian (
5
) for
\lambda=1
, to justify the discretization, one must show that the physical properties resulting from the spectrum of (
68
) converge to the continuum limit as
\lambda\to 1
.

VI.1.2
Computation of the fidelity

The analysis leading from Eq. (
32
) to Eq. (
36
) can be repeated, with the discretized Hamiltonian (
72
) substituted for the tight-binding Hamiltonian (
21
). An expression analogous to Eq. (
35
) then determines the projection of the time-dependent state on the initial state:

\displaystyle\innerproduct{\Psi_t\z}{\Psi_0\z}=\det\underline{\underline{M}}^{\mathcal{N}}(t),

(75)

where the matrix elements of the
M\crossproduct M
matrix
\underline{\underline{M}}^{\mathcal{N}}(t)
are

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{M}}_{m,n}^{\mathcal{N}}(t)=\sum_{p}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\anticommutator{h_m}{g_p^\dagger}e^{-i\epsilon_{p}^{\pm}t/\hbar}\anticommutator{g_p}{h_n^\dagger}.

(76)

VI.2
Numerical procedure

Numerical diagonalization of the initial and the final Hamiltonians
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathcal{N}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{K}^{\mathcal{N}}
for a specific set of eNRG parameters
\lambda
,
\zeta
, and
\theta
and adequately chosen truncation number
\mathcal{N}
, followed by substitution of the right-hand side of Eq. (
75
) for the projection on the right-hand side of Eq. (
6
) yields the
raw
fidelity

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\zeta,\theta}^{\phantom{\dagger}}(t)={\absolutevalue{\det{\underline{\underline{M}}^\nn(t)}}}^{2}.

(77)

The eNRG method inherits a trait of the NRG procedure and intersperses plots of physical properties with non-physical oscillations. Thermodynamic properties computed with
\lambda>1
display sinusoidal functions of the temperature added to the exact thermal dependence, that is, the thermal dependence that a calculation with
\lambda=1
would produce. In this case, averaging two successive
\zeta
’s, and a few
\theta
’s in the interval
-1\leq\theta\leq 1
is enough to eliminate these artifacts
[
27
]
.

The computation of the time-dependent fidelity poses a more complex problem because it involves not one but two discrete spectra, those of the initial and final Hamiltonians. Instead of the uniform energy difference between the plugged and unplugged many-body excitations in Fig.
2
, we find a variety of splittings, the dispersion of which increases with the discretization parameter
\lambda
. The resulting fidelity is the sum of several artificially distinct frequencies. Gone is the harmony of the damped oscillations in Figs.
1
,
4
and
5
.

VI.2.2
Time scale
{T}_{\zeta}^{\phantom{\dagger}}

Why are the computed fidelities independent of
\lambda
at small times? This question prompts us to consider the time scales in the eNRG Hamiltonian.
Section
IV.4
associated
T_{L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
with the smallest energy in the single-particle spectrum of the tight-binding Hamiltonian. The analogous energy in the eNRG approach is
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{N}}
, which defines the time scale
T_{\mathcal{N}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\equiv\nicefrac{{\hbar}}{{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{N}}}}
. At times
t\ll T_{\mathcal{N}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
, the energy scales close to
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{N}}
will contribute insignificantly to the time-evolution operator
\exp(-i\dn\hh_K^\nn t)

\mathcal{F}(t)
will be independent of
\mathcal{N}
, although it will generally vary with
\lambda
,
\theta
and
\zeta
.

However, the latter parameter introduces an exception. The first term within parentheses on the right-hand side of Eq. (
68

\zeta

37

{T}_{\zeta}^{\phantom{\dagger}}

\displaystyle\omega_{\tau}^{\ }{T}_{\zeta}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\equiv\zeta.

(78)

In the interval

, the properties of the eNRG Hamiltonian are independent of
\lambda
and
\theta
, because the energy scales in that segment of the chain, which is not affected by discretization, governs their calculation. The independence of
\lambda
implies that the fidelities in this interval accurately represent the continuum limit.

VI.4
Smoothed fidelity procedure

As Sec.
VI.2.2
explained, the eNRG procedure yields essentially exact fidelities in the time range
0\leq t<{T}_{\zeta}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
. From the perspective of computational cost, the eNRG method is very efficient. Given a time interval, the effort needed to compute the fidelity is a small fraction of the effort to diagonalize the tight-binding Hamiltonian that yields comparable accuracy. For example, the eNRG run and the diagonalization of the
2L=5\,000
tight-binding Hamiltonian that provided the data in Fig.

These considerations refer to our model Hamiltonian, which reduces to two quadratic forms. The diagonalization of a quartic eNRG Hamiltonian would be substantially more demanding computationally. Large
\zeta

[
81
]

72

\zeta+\mathcal{N}+1

\zeta

\zeta

for

\mathbb{T}[\mathcal{H}_{K}^{\mathcal{N}}]=\mathcal{H}_{K}^{\mathcal{N}+2}

\mathbb{T}

[

increases,
\mathcal{H}_{K}^{\mathcal{N}}

\mathcal{H}_{\delta}^{*}

[

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathcal{N}}=\sum_{m=0}^{\mathcal{M}}w_{m}^{\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{O}_{m}^{\phantom{\dagger}},

In analogy to

[

To explain the relatively slow time decay of the fluctuations in all three curves, a comparison with equilibrium properties is expedient. The calculation of equilibrium properties deals with a single energy scale at a time: thermodynamic and equilibrium transport properties are associated with the thermal energy scale
k_{B}^{\phantom{\dagger}}T
; spectroscopy, with the photon energy
\hbar\omega
. This energy scale defines the truncation number
\mathcal{N}
, in analogy with Eq. (
70
), with the pertinent inverse energy,
\nicefrac{{1}}{{k_{B}^{\phantom{\dagger}}T}}
or
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\hbar\omega}}
, substituted for
\nicefrac{{t}}{{\hbar}}
. The deviation of a physical property caused by the leading operator
\mathcal{O}_{1}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
on a given energy scale is then proportional to its coefficient
w_{1}^{\mathcal{N}}
and therefore decays with energy. The deviations are, therefore, insignificant, except at energies comparable to the bandwidth.

Time-dependent properties pose a different problem. The uncertainty principle broadens the energy levels by
\Delta_{t}^{\phantom{\dagger}}=\nicefrac{{\hbar}}{{t}}
. Instead of an energy scale, this broadening defines a soft cutoff
[
1
]
: the energy scales above
\Delta_{t}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
contribute to
\bar{\mathcal{F}}(t)

\Delta\mathcal{F}

\mathcal{N}
increases, the coefficients of the irrelevant operators decrease, and the operator
\mathcal{O}_{1}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
becomes dominant. However, the uncertainty principle admits contributions from higher energy scales, which correspond to smaller truncation numbers
\mathcal{N}
and hence include other irrelevant operators; as a result,
\Delta\mathcal{F}
decays more slowly than
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\omega_{\tau}^{\ }t}}
, as illustrated by the plots in Fig.

VI.5
Retrospective overview

The two real numbers
\lambda
and
\theta
and the integer
\zeta
define the eNRG Hamiltonian. The real numbers are analogous to the discretization and twist parameters of the NRG method
\Lambda
and
z
, respectively. The integer
\zeta
, characteristic of the eNRG approach, defines two alternative ways to calculate time-dependent properties, which we briefly recapitulate. For definiteness, let
t_{\textrm{max}^{\phantom{\dagger}}}
specify the time interval
0\leq t\leq t_{\textrm{max}^{\phantom{\dagger}}}
of interest.

VI.5.1
Large
\zeta
procedure

Choosing
\zeta
somewhat larger than
\omega_{\tau}^{\ }t_{\textrm{max}^{\phantom{\dagger}}}
with moderately small
\lambda
(
\lambda=2
, for example) and
\theta=0
warrants uniform precision over the entire time interval. A single run being sufficient, this procedure is recommended for quadratic Hamiltonians, which can be easily diagonalized. For more complex models of correlated impurity systems, which require iterative diagonalization of the discretized Hamiltonian, the computational cost tends to be prohibitive. If
t_{\textrm{max}^{\phantom{\dagger}}}
is only moderately large, the tDMRG method applied for the eNRG Hamiltonian offers an attractive alternative.

VI.5.2
Integration over twist parameters

When the computational cost considerations rule out double-digit
\zeta
’s, it becomes convenient to carry out several runs, with
N_{\theta}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
uniformly spaced twist parameters in the interval
-1\leq\theta\leq 1
. To minimize discretization artifacts, it is expedient to average the computed time-dependent property on two subsequent
\zeta
’s and to integrate it numerically over
\theta
. The calculated properties tend to show artificial oscillations around
\omega_{\tau}^{\ }t\gtrsim\zeta
, which decay with time, resulting in accurate results for
\omega_{\tau}^{\ }t\lesssim N_{\theta}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
.

VII
Conclusions

We have studied the time dependence of the fidelity
\mathcal{F}(t)
, the Fourier transform of X-ray photoemission spectra. The two general approaches in Secs.
IV.3.1
and
IV.3.2
determine
\mathcal{F}(t)
. The former has led to the analytical expression derived in Appendix
A
, which is very accurate for
\omega_{\tau}^{\ }t\gg 1
. The latter, which is convenient for numerical computations, has allowed the calculation of
\mathcal{F}(t)
from the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the tight-binding Hamiltonian for the photoemission problem.

The analytical expression and numerical results of this calculation have served as benchmarks against which we have gauged the precision of the two time-dependent renormalization-group procedures defined in Sec.
VI
. The results and comparison offer physical and operational insight.

Our findings highlight the importance of the bound state created by the core-hole potential. As a function of time, the fidelity decays following a power law enveloped by a damped oscillation. The decay is the Doniach-Sunjic power law. The oscillations are due to interference between two kinds of excitation: excitations to plugged states, with the bound state occupied, and to unplugged states, with the bound state vacant. The oscillations are damped because the latter follows the Nozières-De Dominicis power law and, therefore, decays faster than the former, which follows the Doniach-Sunjic law.

We have rooted our developments in the real-space formulation of the renormalization-group approach, the eNRG method, which generates model Hamiltonians parametrized by two real numbers
\lambda
and
\theta
, and an integer
\zeta
. Comparison with benchmarks identified two sources of inaccuracy affecting the fidelities calculated by the eNRG procedures, both due to the discretization of the conduction band. The first source is the irrelevant operators that distinguish the eNRG model Hamiltonians from the fixed points to which the renormalization-group transformation drives them. The irrelevant operators cause slowly decaying transitory deviations. The second source is the reduction of the band continuum to a sequence of discrete levels, which spawns persistent fluctuations and shifts upward the calculated fidelities.

The large-
\zeta
procedure, which amounts to working with
\zeta>\omega_{\tau}^{\ }t_{\mbox{max}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
, where
t_{\mbox{max}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
is the longest time of interest, produces essentially exact results over the interval
[0,t_{\mbox{max}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}]
, and therefore avoids deviations.

The large-
\zeta
algorithm deals so efficiently with quadratic Hamiltonians and yields so precise fidelities that it has replaced the brute-force procedure and established a more reliable standard of accuracy in our study. We have checked the quality of the fidelities produced by the second eNRG method, the smoothing procedure, against this new benchmark. The second algorithm is valuable because it offers better perspectives for work targeting Hamiltonians with quartic terms. The inability to eliminate transitory deviations limits its range to medium and large times, to wit, the region that may be beyond the scope of the large-
\zeta
method.

The significant contribution of the high-energy states, referred to here as unplugged states, arises from the sudden change in a localized interaction and the presence of a bound state. These features are not exclusive to quadratic Hamiltonians and can also play an important role in interacting systems
[
16
]
. Discarding states based on energy is therefore inadequate in such cases. More sophisticated truncation schemes than the standard ultraviolet cutoff are required to compute their properties within the many-body iterative diagonalization procedure
[
1
]
. Combined with an appropriate truncation strategy, the second algorithm may provide a route to accurately access long-time dynamics in impurity models. Further developments are left for future work.

In summary, we studied the time dependence of the photoemission current as an object of physical interest in its own right and as a testing ground for the development of time-dependent procedures based on the eNRG method. The analytical expression for the fidelity and the large-
\zeta
algorithm offered (i) solutions to the problem, with results that are uniformly accurate over the one-dimensional parametric space of the model and (ii) a physical interpretation of all features of the solution. With future applications to interacting-impurity Hamiltonians in mind, we have also discussed the smoothing algorithm. Our results show that averaging on
\zeta
followed by integration over
\theta
eliminates persistent deviations and is therefore appropriate to calculate time-dependent properties after the transient deviations have died out.

A.1
Plugged eigenstates

In the many-body ground state of the initial Hamiltonian, all single-particle levels
\varepsilon_{n}^{\phantom{\dagger}}<0
, below the Fermi level (

), are occupied, while the other levels remain vacant. Therefore,

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_0\z}=\prod_{j=-L}^{-1}a_{j}^{\dagger}\ket{\emptyset}.

(89)

where
\ket{\emptyset}
is the vacuum state.

Likewise, in the many-body ground state
\ket{\Omega}
of the final Hamiltonian, all single-particle levels
\epsilon_{n}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
below the Fermi level (

) are occupied:

\displaystyle\ket{\Omega}=\prod_{j=-L}^{-1}b_{j}^{\dagger}\ket{\emptyset}.

(90)

By definition,
\ket{\Omega}
is a plugged state. Other plugged states consist of particle-hole excitations from the ground state that leave the bound level occupied. The number
k
of particle-hole excitations ranges from
k=1
to
k=L-1

With this in mind, we can associate each plugged state
\ket{\rp}
containing
k
particle-hole excitations (

k

\displaystyle\{p\}

\displaystyle\equiv\{p_{1}^{\phantom{\dagger}},p_{2}^{\phantom{\dagger}},\ldots,p_{k}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\}

\displaystyle(0\leq p_{1}^{\phantom{\dagger}}<

\displaystyle p_{2}^{\phantom{\dagger}}<\ldots<p_{k}\leq L-1),

(91)

and

\displaystyle\{h\}

\displaystyle\equiv\{h_{1}^{\phantom{\dagger}},h_{2}^{\phantom{\dagger}},\ldots,h_{k}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\}

\displaystyle(-1\geq h_{1}^{\phantom{\dagger}}

\displaystyle>h_{2}^{\phantom{\dagger}}>\ldots>h_{k}^{\phantom{\dagger}}>-L),

so that the energy levels indexed by the
p_{j}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
(
j=1,\ldots,k
) lie above the Fermi level and are filled, while the levels indexed by
h_{j}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
lie below and are vacant.

With
h_{k}^{\phantom{\dagger}}>-L
, the bound level is occupied. The labels in Eq. (
91

92

p_{1}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
and
h_{1}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
are closest in energy. If we exclude these two labels,
p_{2}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
and
h_{2}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
will be closest in energy, and so forth until
p_{k}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
and
h_{k}^{\phantom{\dagger}}

Given the sets
\{p\}
and
\{h\}
, the eigenstate
\ket{\rp}
can be written as

\displaystyle\ket{\rp}=\prod_{j=1}^{k}b_{p_{j}}^{\dagger}b_{h_{j}}\ket{\Omega},

(93)

\displaystyle E_{r_{P}^{\phantom{\dagger}}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}=\Delta_{L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(p_{j}^{\phantom{\dagger}}-h_{j}^{\phantom{\dagger}}).

(94)

where we used Eq. (
85

It will be helpful to define a third set
\{g\}
. To this end, let
\{g_{0}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\}\equiv\{-1,-2,\ldots,-L\}
and define
\{g\}
as the complement of
\{h\}
with respect to
\{g_{0}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\}

\{h\}
contains the
k
labels of the negative energy levels that are vacant in
\ket{\rp}

contains the labels of the
L-k

\{h\}

is a plugged state,

From Eq. (

\ket{\rp}
onto the initial ground state is

\displaystyle\innerproduct{r_P\z}{\Psi_0\z}=\matrixelement{\Omega}{\prod_{j=1}^kb_{h_j\z}\dg b_{p_j\z}}{\Psi_0\z}.

For

With

For

A.2
Unplugged eigenstates

The ground state
\ket{\Omega}

As explained in Sec.

\displaystyle\ket{\ru}=\prod_{j=1}^{k}b_{p_{j}^{\phantom{\dagger}}}^{\dagger}b_{h_{j}^{\phantom{\dagger}}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\ket{\Omega_U\z}.

\displaystyle E_{r_{U}^{\phantom{\dagger}}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}=\epsilon_{B}^{\phantom{\dagger}}+\Delta_{L}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(p_{j}^{\phantom{\dagger}}-h_{j}^{\phantom{\dagger}}),

(122)

\displaystyle\innerproduct{\ru}{\Psi_0\z}=\matrixelement{\Omega_U\z}{\prod_{j=1}^k{b_{h_j\z}\dg b_{p_j\z}}}{\Psi_0\z}.

with

;
