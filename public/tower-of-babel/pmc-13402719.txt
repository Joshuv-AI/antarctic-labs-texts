Merging bioinspired incubation with supramolecular photocatalysis for Michaelis CO2 reduction beyond enzymes
Authors: Xin Xu, Junkai Cai, Qiaojia Guo, Peng Wang, Chunying Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe conversion of carbon dioxide into chemicals and fuels via photoreduction offers a promising path toward a sustainable and carbon-neutral future, but the low concentration of photosensitized reactive species and their associated sluggish kinetics limit energy conversion. Herein, a bioinspired incubation strategy is developed to pretreat an artificial cofactor 1,3-dimethyl-2-phenyl-2,3-dihydrobenzimidazole (BIH) with a photocatalyst, enriching reactive species within an FeIII-porphyrin-modified capsule. Pre-incubation of Ir(ppy)3 with BIH produces an elevated concentration of anionic IrII(ppy)3 species, which are abstracted into the cationic capsule to enable a saturated enzymatic conversion of CO2-to-CO. This photocatalytic system achieves a turnover number of 180,000 and a turnover frequency of 165 s−1 for CO production, comparable to the reported most effective photocatalytic CO2-to-CO systems and typical CO2 reductases. This pseudo-enzymatic transformation allows concurrent intramolecular cyclic dehydrogenation of imines and CO2 reduction. Here, we show an opportunity for facilitating the conversion of high-energy-barrier photocatalysis under mild conditions.Subject terms: Chemistry, Molecular capsules, Inorganic chemistry

Introduction

The conversion of carbon dioxide into fuels and valuable chemicals through visible light-driven processes holds the promise of becoming a transformative technology with profound implications for future generations and a significant contribution toward a net-zero carbon economy1,2. Recent breakthroughs have led to the creation of numerous techniques, which skillfully integrate essential functional components to accomplish photocatalytic reduction of CO2 and innovative photoredox catalytic methods to synthesize organic chemicals3,4. Among the various CO2 reduction reactions, the formation of CO is notably one of the more straightforward processes to achieve during catalysis, as it requires fewer electrons and photons compared to other carbon-containing products5,6. Notwithstanding the fact that the best technology reported to date has yielded CO with a turnover number (TON) reaching up to 100,000 per mole of catalyst, these systems generally rely on ultra-low catalyst concentration (below nanomolar), which are constrained by the extremely low concentration (far below nanomolar) of the photosensitized transient intermediates with inherently short lifespans7–10. Fine-tuning the longevity and reactivity of the sequence of photoinduced transient intermediates is crucial for optimizing efficiency and selectivity at elevated catalyst concentration, while simultaneously addressing the complexities presented by competitive side reactions (Fig. 1a)11.

Natural photosynthesis converts carbon dioxide into energy-rich organic molecules such as sugars and carbohydrates by harnessing solar energy, providing foundational principles for photoredox catalysis12,13. Notably, there is no lack of oxidoreductases to pre-organize and incubate cofactors (e.g., NADH) around enzymatic active sites in the protein-constructed pocket for forming reactive intermediates, and pursuing exquisite selectivity and kinetic enhancement14,15. Chemists have crafted kinds of bioinspired capsules to integrate cofactors and/or photosensitizers in the confined pocket for the activation and conversion of inert molecules, including CO2 via photocatalysis16,17. We thought that incubating cofactor mimics with photosensitizer, combined with host-guest abstracting of the formed transient species, would guarantee an effective photocatalytic manifold via a saturated Michaelis conversion18–20. Considering that the opened capsules exhibited position-modified interactions and promising guest-exchanging kinetics, we envisioned that merging bio-inspired photo-incubation with dynamic, favorable enzymatic conversion would enhance substrate-product circulation with the kinetics beyond the typical concentration dependence of the transient species.

Herein, we report a pre-equilibrium approach to merge commercial photosensitizer, tris(2-phenylpyridine)iridium, Ir(ppy)3, within a FeIII-porphyrin decorated lantern-shaped capsule H1 for efficient CO2 photoreduction. Our approach includes the incubation of Ir(ppy)3 and 1,3-dimethyl-2-phenyl-2,3-dihydrobenzimidazole (BIH), under visible light irradiation to form an elevated concentration of anionic IrII(ppy)321, an active transient species that always has quite a lower concentration in typical photocatalytic systems (Fig. 1b). The lantern-shaped capsule abstracts the species to form a pre-equilibrium clathrate intermediate via π-stacking and electrostatic interactions. The system efficiently facilitates the CO2-to-CO conversion with the initial rate exhibiting zero-order dependence on the concentration of both Ir(ppy)3 and BIH, overcoming the typical sluggish kinetics of photosensitization in the photocatalytic process. The positively charged characteristic and open-interacted manner endow H1, facilitating rapid Ir(ppy)3-IrII(ppy)3 exchanges via a kick-kick manner, achieving a turnover frequency of about 165 s−1, competitive with formate dehydrogenase22, and a TON of 180,000, comparable to reported CO2-to-CO photoreduction systems23–25. The approach enables efficient intramolecular C − X (X = N or S) cyclic dehydrogenation, concurrently with CO2 reduction, providing a strategy for converting high-energy-barrier reactions under mild conditions.

Results and discussion

Characterization of cages and host-guest species

The coordination cage H1 was constructed by assembling an ortho-dicarboxylate modified FeIII-porphyrin-based ligand (denoted as H2LFe) and bis(cyclopentadienyl) zirconium dichloride in DMF/THF solution (Supplementary Figs. 1−11). X-ray structural analysis of H1 revealed a lantern-shaped capsule consisting of two C3-symmetric Cp3Zr3 (μ3-O)(μ2-OH)3 (Cp3Zr3) clusters distributed on the top and bottom, and three H2LFe ligands strategically positioned along the sides (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Data 1). H1 had a height of 21.1 Å, with an average Fe···Zr distance of 12.0 Å and an Fe···Fe distance of 15.7 Å. Crucially, the assembly features three large rhombic openings (15.7 × 16.2 Å2), readily accessible for the binding of suitable guests with high but dynamic active affinity that was favorable for fast pre-equilibrium between the association and dissociation (Supplementary Figs. 1−7)26. Electrospray ionization mass spectra analysis of H1 with an intense peak at m/z = 1720.4890 assigned to [(Cp3Zr3)2·LFe3]2+ species, exhibited high stability and integrity of H1 in solution (Supplementary Figs. 14−16). The addition of commercial photosensitizer Ir(ppy)3 into H1 solution, aroused a new peak at m/z = 2047.0673, which was assigned to the [H1·Ir(ppy)3]2+ complex species (HIr) by comparing the simulation results based on natural isotopic abundances (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 17).

UV−vis spectra of H1 (5.0 μM) solution upon the addition of Ir(ppy)3 resulted in a weakened Soret band of LFe modules on H1, and generated an isosbestic point at 420 nm. Hill plot fitting of the titration profile in a 1:1 binding model provided an association constant Kass as high as 1.33 × 105 M−1 (Fig. 2d)27. Ir(ppy)3 exhibited bright emission centered at 520 nm, and the emission intensity was quenched directly upon the addition of H1 into the solution, fitting of the titration profile gave a 1:1 host-guest complexation behavior with the association constant calculated as 1.05 × 105 M−1 (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. 25)27,28. Density functional theory calculations (DFT) of the host-guest complex suggested that the Ir(ppy)3 guest spontaneously positioned at the cleft binding sites of the host (Supplementary Data 2), and was stabilized by π-stacking interactions29. Remarkably, beyond the π-stacking separation (3.26 Å) between the phenyl-pyridine moiety on Ir(ppy)3 and the porphyrin plane on H1, C–H···O hydrogen bonds (2.70 Å) between the phenyl-pyridine moiety and an oxygen atom from the zirconium clusters (Supplementary Fig. 8) were found to stabilize the complexation. This strategic interplay of the conductive π-channel enhanced the electron communication between the photosensitizer and its active reduced form for CO2 reduction30,31, while it allowed the fast pre-equilibrium between guest desorption and complexation for catalytic purposes (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 8).

Cyclic voltammetry of H1 displayed three pairs of redox peaks at −0.25, −1.05, and −1.65 V (vs. Ag/AgCl), assigned to FeIII/FeII, FeII/FeI, and FeI/Fe0 couples32, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 18). Adding Et3N·HCl into the H1 solution induced a proton-reduction peak at −1.40 V (vs. Ag/AgCl) (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 19). Introducing CO2 into the H1 solution triggered the peak related to FeII/FeI couple toward the more anodic side, and exhibited that the current potential shifted with prolonged CO2 bubbling time and reached saturation after a short period (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Figs. 20, 21)33. Notably, the introduction of CO2 into the ligand LFe solution caused little variation of the potential (Supplementary Fig. 22). We deduced that the FeII/FeI couple on the capsule was able to activate CO2 through the capsule shielding effect, at the time, a suitable photosensitizer was conducted to avoid a high energy barrier for reducing the FeII/FeI couple23,24.

Supramolecular catalytic performance of host

We then displayed the reduction of CO2 upon addition of the electron donors into the acetonitrile solution containing Ir(ppy)3 (0.1 mM) and H1 (0.1 μM) with typical proton donor trifluoroethanol (TFE, 0.25 M) under a CO2 atmosphere, and the excess of Ir(ppy)3 ensures the saturation of the complexation species with H1. Among typical electron donors, including ascorbic acid, sodium ascorbate, triethylamine, triethanolamine, BIH (50 mM) aroused significant CO production (510 μL). Control experiments without H1, BIH, CO2, or light, respectively, manifested no CO generated (Supplementary Fig. 45, Supplementary Table 6), and a feeble catalytic performance was observed in the absence of Ir(ppy)3 or TFE. The optimized condition facilitated a turnover number (TON) of 45,000 after 30 min irradiation and an initial turnover frequency (TOF, within the first 10 min) of more than 40 s−1 for the selective CO2-to-CO conversion (Supplementary Fig. 46, Supplementary Table 6).

Luminescence titration of the Ir(ppy)3 solution upon the addition of BIH demonstrated a strong emission quenching with a Stern-Volmer constant28 calculated as about 1.78 × 104 M−1, but other electron donors exhibited lower quenching constants (Supplementary Figs. 28−32, Supplementary Table 5). Time-dependent emission measurements revealed a lifetime diminishing of Ir(ppy)3 upon addition of electron donors, wherein BIH (50 mM) aroused the shortest lifetime (0.42 μs) (Supplementary Figs. 33, 34). As capsule H1 exhibited lower quenching efficiency, we attributed the high efficiency to the quenching pathway that the excited state of Ir(ppy)3, reduced by the electron donor, dominated the photocatalysis, and the reductive Ir(ppy)3 further reduced iron centers for CO2 reduction.

When pre-reacting Ir(ppy)3 with BIH upon light for 15 min, no CO was observed, but the subsequent introduction of the cationic capsule H1 led to a four-fold enhancement in initial TOFCO compared to the one-pot reaction without pre-incubation (Supplementary Fig. 46, Supplementary Table 6). Under optimized conditions, about 1630 μL of CO was produced after 30 min of irradiation with >99% selectivity and an initial TOF ca. 165 s−1 based on the concentration of H1 (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 46 and Supplementary Table 6). Continuously exposing the reaction mixture for one hour generated 2075 μL CO, reaching a TON of 180,000 per H1 (amounting to 60,000 per Fe) (Supplementary Figs. 47, 50). This value represents a highly effective CO2-to-CO conversion with considerable selectivity, competitive with reported superior photocatalytic systems (Supplementary Fig. 48, Supplementary Table 16) and natural enzymes, including formate dehydrogenase (FDH, TOF: 5.6 s−1) and carbon monoxide dehydrogenase (CODH, TOF: 45 s−1)22,34. The quantum yield for CO production was determined as ΦCO = 5.8% for the optimized system with pre-incubation, compared to 1.8% under the condition without pre-incubation, highlighting the role of the incubation strategy in enhancing photon utilization efficiency. Experiments with 13C-labeled CO2 revealed a dominant signal at m/z = 29 exclusively corresponding to 13CO, confirming that CO2 was the ultimate carbon source of CO (Supplementary Fig. 49)25.

We also recognized that the reaction of Ir(ppy)3 (0.1 mM) with BIH (50.0 mM) upon light irradiation aroused obvious Ir(ppy)3 emission quenching and formation of BI+ which was detected by 1H NMR assay (Supplementary Figs. 12, 27, and 40). UV−vis spectral tracing of the reaction solution exhibited that the absorbance increased at 280 nm (the characteristic band of BI+) and decreased at 310 nm (the band of BIH) with an obvious isosbestic point at 300 nm (Fig. 3b)35,36. Concomitantly, a new absorption band emerged at 528 nm (Supplementary Fig. 41), matching the reported fingerprint of the reduced IrII(ppy)3 species37, suggesting the generation of IrII with elevated concentration during the pre-incubation. The variations of the two absorbances both exhibited a linear relationship with irradiation time, and reached a plateau after 15 min (Supplementary Fig. 42). While subsequent addition of the pre-reaction mixture to H1 solution unequivocally aroused a H1-Ir(ppy)3 combination characteristic isosbestic point at 415 nm, accompanied by new absorptions corresponding to FeII and even FeI species (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 43)38, we attributed these spectral changes to the pre-reaction of Ir(ppy)3 with BIH forming enriched anionic IrII(ppy)3, which was abstracted into the pocket of cationic H1 and proceeded to reduce the FeIII center for further driving CO2 reduction39.

Kinetic experiments revealed that the initial conversion rate (within 10 min) showed a first-order dependence on the TFE concentration, but a zero-order dependence on the Ir(ppy)3 concentration and BIH concentration (Fig. 3e, f, Supplementary Figs. 50−54) under standard conditions. Control experiments without pre-incubation revealed a first-order regime on the concentration of Ir(ppy)3. It is likely that this characteristic pre-activation process endowed the generation of anionic active IrII(ppy)3 species with elevated concentration, and enabled the formation of a saturated clathrate of IrII(ppy)3 with H1 via enhanced electrostatic attraction19,20. The concentration independence of the Ir(ppy)3 and the active intermediate IrII(ppy)3 delivered the sluggish kinetics of the photosensitization process that restricts the photocatalytic CO2 reduction efficiency (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Fig. 42). Control experiments of the catalytic performance of molecular Fe-porphyrin (i.e., the building block of H1, H2LFe) under identical conditions revealed significantly lower activity for CO2 reduction. The pre-incubation strategy enhanced the catalytic efficiency, but the photocatalysis kinetics of the Fe-porphyrin monomer suggested a first-order dependence of the initial rate on the Ir(ppy)3 concentration (Supplementary Figs. 55, 56). In all cases, the capsule H1 exhibited superior catalytic performance over equal amounts of the Fe-porphyrin fragment.

While the pre-incubated system gradually deactivated over 1 h of catalysis, control experiments by adding fresh components after reaction for 1 h (Supplementary Fig. 57) exhibited that the fresh Ir(ppy)3 or H1 added to the solution did not arouse catalysis recovery, but the presence of fresh BIH restored approximately 39% of the initial CO production. It is likely that the photosensitizer and catalyst remained structurally intact during catalysis, and the decrease in catalytic efficiency was possibly attributed to the product inhibition effect during the rapid catalytic conversion40, in which back electron transfer from the IrII(ppy)3 to the largely generated BI+ during the CO2 reduction led to a kinetic bottleneck (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 40).

We then engineered a recirculating continuous-flow photo-reactor featuring an integrated microfluidic system (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Figs. 58−60). Precise modulation of CO2 (0.8 mL·min−1) and liquid (2.0 mL·min−1) flow rates enabled a yield of 65 mL CO under standardized catalytic conditions: H1 (0.1 μM), Ir(ppy)3 (0.1 mM) with BIH (50.0 mM) in CH3CN upon a 15-min pre-incubation. Critically, this reactor achieved a TON of 362,000—surpassing tubular reactors in efficiency for 2 h irradiation. From the mechanism point of view, light incubation enabled the formation of sufficient IrII(ppy)3 species, generating a saturated IrII(ppy)3 involved clathrate with Fe-porphyrin decorated capsule, followed by the reduction of the Fe center and the reduction of CO2 into an intermediate FeIICOOH, and finally to gain a proton and ultimately generates H2O and CO (Supplementary Fig. 61)23,41. In situ Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy revealed a significantly increased signal at 1600 cm−1, which could be interpreted as a characteristic signal of the *COOH species, a common intermediate during photocatalytic CO2-to-CO processes (Supplementary Fig. 62)42.

Investigation into the mechanism and scope of C–X cyclization

The capability of this electron transfer mode enabled oxidative cyclizations of imine-based C − X coupling motifs to form N-substituted and S-substituted aromatic chemicals subsequently. These heterocycles are important structural motifs due to their biological and therapeutic activities. However, the preparation of benzimidazole and benzothiazole compounds from easily prepared substituted imines still faces marked challenges43. Irradiating Ir(ppy)3 (0.1 mM) with N-substituted substrate 1a ((E)−2-(benzylideneamino)aniline, 10.0 mM, Fig. 4g) under a CO2 atmosphere aroused an obvious emission quenching and the formation of a cycloaddition intermediate, which was detected by the 1H NMR tracing (Supplementary Figs. 13, 35). UV−vis spectral tracing of the reaction mixture exhibited the absorbance increase at 290 nm and decrease at 385 nm with an obvious isosbestic point at 320 nm (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 44). Notably, the analogous S-substituted substrate 2a ((E)−2-(benzylideneamino)benzenethiol) quenched the emission of Ir(ppy)3 significantly, but the O-substituted substrate 3a ((E)−2-(benzylideneamino)phenol) did not work (Fig. 4d, g, Supplementary Fig. 35).

The square wave voltammetry (SWV) provided the oxidation potential about 0.45, 0.22 and 0.71 V for the N-substituted substrate 1a, S-substituted substrate 2a and O-substituted substrate 3a, respectively, and presented a positive correlation with that of the quenching efficiency (Supplementary Figs. 23, 24). We deduced a typical photoinduced electron transfer (PET) reaction from the substrate to the excited state of Ir(ppy)3, forming IrII(ppy)3 for further reduction conversion (Supplementary Fig. 36)44. Most importantly, luminescence titration of dye-loaded capsule clathrate upon addition of 1a did not recover the emission of Ir(ppy)3, but led to further quenching of the emission (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Fig. 37). It is likely that the presence of 1a could not squeeze the dye in the cavity, but enforced a close proximity to dye-loaded capsule for an enhanced molarity of reactants, therefore benefiting the further reaction acceleration17.

Under optimized photocatalytic conditions, substrate 1a underwent efficient C–N cyclization to afford 4a in 82% yield, corresponding to a TON of 8200 based on H1 (Supplementary Table 17). Control experiments demonstrated that no product was detected in the absence of Ir(ppy)3, and only low yields were observed in the absence of H1, light, or TFE, respectively, confirming the necessity of each component for the cyclization reaction (Supplementary Table 18). Notably, replacing H1 with H2LFe (3.0 μM) resulted in a significantly reduced yield of 24%, highlighting the essential role of the supramolecular architecture in facilitating the cyclization. Moreover, omitting the pre-incubation step also led to a decreased yield (37%), indicating that pre-generation of the active IrII species is beneficial even for the cyclization.

When 2-NH2 substituted imine derivative substrates with different functional groups were used, the dehydrogenated C − N coupling took place, and the product yield was enhanced with electron-donating groups, but diminished when electron-withdrawing groups were present. The square wave voltammetry revealed that, compared to the unsubstituted substrate 1a (R = H), electron-donating groups caused a cathodic shift of the oxidation potential, while electron-withdrawing groups caused an anodic shift. Hammett plot suggested a statistically negative linear correlation between Hammett substituent constant σp values and substrate conversion (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 63)45, reflecting that the electronic effects governed the electron transfer process. This trend was mechanistically consistent with the reductive quenching pathway, wherein electron-donating groups boosted the quenching efficiency (Supplementary Fig. 64), enhancing the thermodynamic driving force for the electron communication between substrates and photosensitizers46.

When 2-SH substituted imine derivative substrate 2a (prepared in situ by mixing the aldehyde and amino compound) was chosen, the oxidation potential of the 2a involved clathrate was around 0.22 V (vs Ag/AgCl), which was the lowest among these imine substrates and led to the most efficient C − S coupling. Alternatively, the C − O coupling hardly took place when 2-OH substituted imine derivative substrate 3a was used (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. 65). We then inferred that the formation of substrate-involving clathrate was important to trigger the chemical conversion, and the oxidation potential of the substrate involving transition controlled the yield of the chemical conversion.

With the photon power energy increased from P1 = 40 mW·cm−2 to P2 = 80 mW·cm−2, we found a 4.1-fold increase in the 4a yield, showing a quadratic relationship dependence of the initial rate of the 1a oxidation on different photon power (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Fig. 66). These results suggested a combination of the two photons energy to successively drive two one-electron transfer per catalytic cycle: the first PET triggered the initial ring-forming reaction, while the second PET dehydrogenated the cyclized intermediate. In all cases, the IrII(ppy)3 species reduced the Fe-porphyrin site for CO2 reduction47. The efficient dual-photon process validates our strategy: the pre-incubation protocol ensures a high concentration of IrII(ppy)3, while the capsule H1 serves as a versatile platform that manages sequential electron transfers for concurrent conversions.

Collectively, a paradigm by merging bioinspired incubation with dynamic favorable enzymatic conversion was developed to overcome challenges of sluggish photo-sensitization kinetics for CO2 photoreduction, delivering efficient CO2-to-CO conversion with competitive metrics (>165 s−1 TOF, >99.9% selectivity). This seamless lab-to-process translation underscores the industrial viability of integrating enzymatic kinetics with light incubation for overcoming persistent energy barriers in challenging transformations.

(Energy Chemical). Methyl p-formylbenzoate (98%) was purchased from Bide Pharmatech Co., Ltd. Bis(cyclopentadienyl) zirconium dichloride (98%) was purchased from Accela ChemBio Co., Ltd. (Energy Chemical). CO2 and Ar were purchased from Nanjing Special Gas Factory Co., Ltd.

General methods and instrumentation

The crystal data were collected on the Bruker D8 Venture X-ray diffractometer in Bruker AXS, Germany. The emission spectra were measured on the Horiba FL-3 fluorescence spectrophotometer at room temperature. The emission lifetimes were measured employing time-correlated single-photon counting on an Edinburgh FLS1000 instrument. Ultraviolet−visible (UV−vis) data were collected by a Shimadzu UV3600 spectrometer. A typical three-electrode system was used to collect electrochemical data on a CHI 760 F electrochemical workstation. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) data were obtained on a Bruker Avance Ⅲ 400 M spectrometer, and the peak frequency was based on the internal standard (tetramethylsilane, TMS) shift of 0.0 ppm. Electrospray ionization mass (ESI-MS) spectra were obtained by a Thermo Fisher Q Exactive mass spectrometer, and methanol or acetonitrile was used as the mobile phase with a flow rate of 0.20 mL·min−1. The gas chromatography data were obtained by a Techcomp GC 7900 gas chromatograph. In situ FTIR experiments were conducted on a Nicolet IS50-FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) equipped with a mercury cadmium telluride detector and a custom-designed gastight reaction cell. Isotopically labeled reaction products were analyzed by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS, QP2020N, Shimadzu Corporation) equipped with a high-sensitivity quadrupole mass analyzer. The continuous-flow photoreactor system was engineered with technical support from Beijing Roger Tech, Ltd., featuring an integrated helical microfluidic module constructed from fluorinated ethylene propylene (FEP) tubing (standard o.d. 1.6 mm, i.d. 0.8 mm; residence volume: 8 mL). All schematics, structural figures, and chemical structures were prepared using Microsoft PowerPoint (basic shapes), Diamond, Origin, and ChemDraw, all licensed for commercial publication.

Synthesis of ligand LFe

Compound LH (0.17 g, 0.24 mmol; for synthesis see Supplementary Information, Section 1) and FeCl2·4H2O (0.48 g, 2.4 mmol) were dissolved in DMF (80 mL). The reaction mixture was heated at 130 °C for 12 h under a nitrogen atmosphere. After cooling to room temperature, the solvent was removed in vacuo. The crude product was washed with warm water (50 °C) until the filtrate was colorless and dried in vacuo. The resulting purple solid was dissolved in methanol, filtered, and concentrated in vacuo to afford LFe as a purple solid (0.15 g, 78.3%). Synthesis of metal-organic capsule H1

Cp2ZrCl2 (0.04 mmol, 11.7 mg) was dissolved in H2O (0.3 mL) under ultrasonication for 10 min. To this solution was added a mixture of ligand LFe (0.02 mmol, 15.9 mg) in DMF (2.0 mL) and THF (1.0 mL). After additional ultrasonication (10 min), the reaction mixture was heated at 65 °C for 12 h. Upon cooling to room temperature, the product was washed with DMF, affording purple block-shaped crystals H1. The crystals were subsequently washed with Et2O and dried in vacuo for a yield of 60.6%. General method of UV−vis absorption spectra and emission spectra tests

UV−vis and emission spectra were acquired in custom 1.0 cm × 1.0 cm quartz cuvettes sealed with a septum, containing a sample volume of 3.0 mL. The sample solution was saturated by bubbling with CO2 or Ar for 10 min to rigorously exclude oxygen. A micro stir bar was placed inside the cuvette prior to measurements, followed by the addition of analytes via a microsyringe.

General method of electrochemical measurements

Electrochemical measurements were performed in a standard three-electrode cell comprising a glassy carbon working electrode, an Ag/AgCl reference electrode (3.0 M KCl), and a Pt wire counter electrode (0.5 mm diameter), containing an electrolyte volume of approximately 5.0 mL. Prior to each measurement, the working electrode was thoroughly polished with 0.05 μm alumina powder on a polishing cloth. The electrolyte was a freshly prepared 0.10 M solution of TBAPF6 in anhydrous DMF or CH3CN, which was saturated by bubbling with CO2 or Ar for 10 min. Gas flow was maintained throughout experiments, with bubbling between scans and solvent blanketing during scans. All cyclic voltammograms were recorded at a scan rate of 100 mV/s unless otherwise stated. All potentials are reported as measured versus Ag/AgCl (3.0 M KCl) without iR correction.

General method of gas phase test

Gas-phase analysis was performed using a Techcomp GC 7900 gas chromatograph to quantify carbon monoxide (CO) generated from the photocatalytic reduction of carbon dioxide (CO2) in the photoreactor headspace. The system employed nitrogen as the carrier gas and a 0.6 m × 3.0 mm (i.d.) 5 Å molecular sieve column for separation. Hydrogen (H2) was detected via a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) operated at 100 °C, while CO and hydrocarbons were analyzed using a flame ionization detector (FID) coupled with a methane conversion unit. The injector port and chromatographic oven temperatures were maintained at 200 °C and 60 °C, respectively.

In situ FTIR spectra experiment

The catalyst was uniformly dispersed as a thin film on a substrate mounted at the focal point of the reaction chamber. Prior to initiating catalysis, the system underwent rigorous purging with high-purity N2 (30 mL·min−1) to eliminate the gases in the cell and the adsorbed species on the catalyst surface. Subsequently, a continuous CO2 stream (99.999%) was introduced to establish a well-defined CO2-dominated reactive atmosphere. Dynamic surface processes and transient intermediates were probed in real time by synchronizing mercury cadmium telluride detector-based time-resolved spectral acquisition with controlled photoexcitation conditions during photocatalytic reduction.

General method of 1H NMR yield determination

The yields of photocatalytic cyclization products were determined by 1H NMR spectroscopy using 1,3,5-trimethoxybenzene as an internal standard. At each designated time point (typically at 20 min intervals), a 300 μL aliquot was extracted from the photocatalytic reaction mixture (initial substrate concentration 10.0 mM) using a long-needle gastight syringe. From this aliquot, 200 μL was transferred to a 1.5 mL mass spectrometry vial. To this sample, 200 μL of a 10.0 mM stock solution of 1,3,5-trimethoxybenzene in acetonitrile was added as an internal standard. The mixture was then evaporated to dryness under reduced pressure at room temperature using a rotary evaporator with slow rotation to ensure gentle evaporation. The dried residue was redissolved in 600 μL of deuterated solvent (DMSO-d6 for benzimidazole products 4a–4f; CDCl3 for benzothiazole products 5a–5f) and transferred to a 5 mm NMR tube. 1H NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance III 400 MHz spectrometer. Yields were calculated by comparing the integral of a characteristic product peak to the integral of the internal standard signal.

Calculation for quantum yield

For a typical photocatalytic reaction under monochromatic LED irradiation (λ = 420 nm), the light intensity (measured by a calibrated optical power meter) was measured to be 80 mW·cm−2 with an illuminated area of 5.0 cm2 (cross-sectional area of the quartz reactor), giving a total incident power of 0.4 W. General methods for the catalytic CO2 reduction

Photocatalytic CO2 reduction experiments were performed in septum-sealed 15 mL quartz reactors equipped with a magnetic stir bar and water cooling. A standard reaction contained BIH (56.0 mg, 50.0 mM), trifluoroethanol (TFE, 90.0 μL, 0.25 M), and tris(2-phenylpyridine)iridium(III) (Ir(ppy)3, 0.10 mM) in CH3CN solution (4.91 mL). The reactor was sealed with a rubber plug and purged with CO2 for 20 min. The mixture was pre-irradiated (420 nm LED) under vigorous stirring at 25 °C for 15 min photoactivation prior to catalyst injection (named incubation). After activation, H1 (10.0 μL, 0.05 mM in methanol) was injected through the septum with a microsyringe. Headspace gas (100.0 μL aliquots) was periodically sampled using a gastight syringe and analyzed by GC-FID. Control experiments under identical conditions with Ar-purged systems confirmed CO2 as the exclusive carbon source (no CO detected). Control experiments with 13CO2 further confirmed the exclusive derivation of CO from carbon dioxide, as evidenced by the detection of 13CO (m/z = 29) via GC-MS.

General procedure for photocatalytic oxidative cyclization

All photocatalytic reactions were conducted under irradiation from blue LEDs (λ = 420 nm) and maintained at 25 °C through a circulating water bath. Reaction progress was quantitatively monitored by 1H NMR spectroscopy (400 MHz) with periodic sampling (200 μL aliquots withdrawn at 20 min intervals) using 1,3,5-trimethoxybenzene as an internal standard. Spectral analysis enabled the determination of oxidative cyclization product formation efficiency, with all 1H NMR measurements performed immediately after sample collection to ensure analytical fidelity. The C–X (X = O, N) coupling reaction was conducted in septum-sealed 15 mL quartz reactors equipped with a magnetic stir bar and water cooling. A solution of 2-substituted imine derivatives (10.0 mM, −OH or −NH2), Ir(ppy)3 (0.10 mM) and TFE (0.25 M) in CH3CN solution (5.0 mL) was degassed by CO2 purging 20 min. The mixture was photo-incubated upon 420 nm LED irradiation (15 min). H1 was then injected, and the irradiation was continued for 2 h. After the reaction, the products (4a–4f) were washed with chloroform, filtered, dried under vacuum, and dissolved in DMSO-d6 for 1H NMR analysis. The C–S coupling reaction followed the above procedure using substituted 2-aminothiophenol (10.0 mM) and benzaldehyde derivative (10.0 mM) as substrates. After the mixture was photo-incubated (15 min, vigorous stirring), H1 was subsequently injected, and continuously irradiated for 2 h. After completion, the products (5a–5f) were purified by flash column chromatography (2% EtOAc/hexane), dried under vacuum, and dissolved in CDCl3 for 1H NMR analysis. All yields are NMR yields determined by the internal standard method before isolation. Isolation was performed only for product characterization.
