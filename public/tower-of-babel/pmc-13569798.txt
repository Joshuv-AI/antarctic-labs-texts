Tuning the structural, optoelectronic, and thermoelectric performance of cesium based double perovskite halide under hydrostatic pressure
Authors: Abdullah Javed, Ahmed B M Ibrahim, Hudabia Murtaza, Quratul Ain, Junaid Munir, Amna Khan, Mohamed A Habib, Mokhtar S S Al-Salimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmong the perovskite material domain, halide double perovskites possess unique structural and optoelectronic properties which make them highly suitable for applications in solar cell and photonic devices. This manuscript reports a detailed study of the pressure effects on the physical attributes of Cs2ScInF6. The Wien2K code along with the FP-LAPW approach is used to assess the physical attributes of Cs2ScInF6 under pressure. The exchange–correlation effects are treated through the mBJ approximation. For the first principle analysis of the studied halide, each iteration is altered through a gap of 50 GPa till the pressure reaches 200 GPa. At ambient pressure, the material remains stable and possesses cubic symmetry without exhibiting major distortions in the lattice. The influence of pressure on electronic structures and TDOS plots of Cs2ScInF6 reports a significant decline in the bandgap from 4.14 eV (0 GPa) to 1.86 eV (200 GPa). The partial density of state for the studied halide reports strong hybridization of Sc-p and In-d states in the conduction band at higher pressures whereas In-d states are more prominent in the valence band at lower pressures. The optical traits reports significant shift in the dispersion and polarization peaks from UV region to visible region making Cs2ScInF6 suitable for visible light optoelectronics. The transport properties reports higher Seebeck coefficient at lower temperatures whereas the increase in the pressure slightly declines the thermoelectric parameters and at higher pressure and temperature the ZT value is noticed as 0.786 which reveals that this halide is a moderate contender for the production of green energy.Keywords: Pressure application, Thermoelectric, Optoelectronic, Renewable energy

Introduction

Coal and gas are two examples of conventional energy sources which are rapidly running out and are predicted to run out entirely by 2050 [1]. To address growing demand and limited supply of energy, technologists are creating technology to generate renewable energy by utilizing natural resources [2, 3]. Research is being done on solar radiation and thermal waste to find possible substitutes for current energy sources such as fossil fuels. Photovoltaic cell technology's high power altering efficiency makes it an affordable option for renewable energy source [4]. The exceptional phase, dynamic, thermal, electrical, structural, and optical steadiness of double halide perovskites has made them attractive candidates for photovoltaic and optoelectronic devices [5, 6]. The experimental applications of inorganic halide perovskites have significantly expanded throughout the years and among them photovoltaic cells, LEDs, radiation sensors, and lasers are a few examples [7–9]. Perovskite halides tends to have the upper edge for industrial applications than other materials due to their abundance and favorable physical properties, including a tunable band width, low reflectivity, intense absorption across a wide spectrum, long-period charge diffusion paths, exceptional photoconductivity, and expanded mobility despite low carrier efficiency [10–12]. Furthermore additional derivatives of perovskites (ABX3) of materials has been observed, including A2B1+B3+X6 [13], A2B4+X6 [14], and A3B2X9 [15]. A2BX6 type perovskites are considered a novel variety which possesses potential for environmental beneficial applications [16, 17]. Typically halide double perovskite has the general formula of A2BB'X6 which contains monovalent cation represented by ‘A’, 'B' and 'B'' depict distinct metal cations (monovalent is one of them, the other trivalent), and 'X' depicts a halide anion (such as Cl, F, Br or I) [18]. With tunable bandgap and environmentally benign characteristics, halide perovskites are attractive candidates for optoelectronic devices [19]. Their excellent stability and diversity in structure and function, offer appealing lead-free alternatives for a range of optoelectronic applications [20, 21]. The first principles analysis of Rb2AgMCl6 (M = As, Co, Rh) double perovskites possess bandgaps from 1.84 eV to 2.18 eV exhibiting high absorption in visible region. Moreover, the thermoelectric properties suggests that materials are p-type semiconductors [22]. Fluorine based materials are widely used as sensing materials [23]. Strong UV–vis absorption and lower energy loss is reported for A2TlRhF6 (A = K, Rb) [24]. The mBJ and ML prediction revealed a bandgap of 2.68 eV for Cs2GaAgF6 whereas high figure of merit at 600K suggest that this material exhibit high potential for thermoelectric application [25]. The first principle analysis of Cs2NaB′Cl6 (B′ = In, Cr, Y, Tb) suggest that these materials are exceptional contenders for tandem solar photovoltaics and detector applications [26]. SCAPS-1D simulations assess the physical attributes of Cs2TlAsI6 as solar cell absorber and it reveals that it is stable absorber for high-efficiency lead-free PSCs [27]. The DFT-based analysis of Rb2NaTlRBr6 suggests that the material achieves a (SLME) of 33%, making it a promising lead-free solar absorber [28]. A variety of double perovskites such as A2OsX6 (A = K, Rb; X = Cl, Br, I) [29], Rb2OsX6 (X = Cl, Br, I) [30], K2MCl6 [31], A2WBr6 [32], A2TeX6 (A = Cs, Rb; X = Cl, Br, I) [33], K2MBr6 (M = Sn, Ti, Pd, Pt) [34] and Cs2MCl6 (M = Mn, Mo) [35] exhibit excellent properties for diverse applications. In recent years, wide bandgap materials are gaining attention as these types of materials possess poor luminosity which significantly restricts their potential in optoelectronics. Many approaches have been attempted to address this problem [36–38], including altering the composition [39, 40], substituting cations and anions, and doping [41–44]. One powerful tool for changing the electrical and structural properties of materials without causing any chemical changes is pressure application [45]. Several halide perovskites has been investigated under the influence of pressure such as the emission spectra of halides Cs2NaBiCl6 and Cs2AgBiCl6 was successfully altered by pressure-based study [46, 47], and the band gap of Cs2AgBiBr6 was reduced from 2.2 eV to 1.7 eV by applying pressure [44]. Metallic transition is reported for Rb2AgSbCl6 when pressure is raised to 100 GPa [48]. The influence of pressure on Ba2GdBiO6 revealed that its electronic bandgap widens to 2.9 eV with pressure up to 20 GPa enabling UV and IR absorption for optoelectronic applications [49]. The pressure study of La2NiMnO6 reports metallic nature whereas its mechanical analysis suggests improved stiffness and ductility [50]. Phase transiting is noticed for Cs2TiBr6 and under pressure the material exhibits piezo-chromism (red → dark black) and photoluminescence redshift with broadening up to 12.6 GPa. pressure alters octahedral vibrations and self-trapped exciton states, enhancing its optical properties [51]. Many perovskite halides containing “Cl” and “F” at X-site are usually not explored due to their higher bandgaps when explored with appropriate approximation, so hydrostatic pressure is an appropriate technique through which halides based on “Cl” and “F” at X-site can be utilized after bandgap modifications. Through this approach many unexplored wide bandgap materials can be investigated for optoelectronic devices and green energy production. Although pressure dependent DFT studies on halide double perovskites are widely reported, this work demonstrates clear novelty by focusing on a comparatively unexplored Cs2ScInF6 and systematically analyzing its response under a wide pressure range. The study reveals distinct trends in structural stability, elastic behavior, and electronic bandgap modulation that are not inferred from literature. Moreover, the combined investigation of structural, electronic, and mechanical properties under pressure provides a more comprehensive understanding of pressure-driven tunability, thereby offering valuable insights for the design of advanced functional materials based on double perovskites.

Computational methods

The alteration of the physical attributes of Cs2ScInF6 under pressure are investigated using the Wien2K code [52]. The Wien2K accommodates the FP-LAPW technique to analyze the electrons in the muffin-tin and interstitial region [53]. This method is highly accurate and predicts results with higher precision by dividing the unit cell into non-overlapping spheres around the atoms and the remaining interstitial space, treating the wave function differently in each region to achieve high precision in solving the Kohn–sham equations. The pressure range is selected from 0 to 200 GPa with increment of 50 GPa in each calculation. The selection of pressures up to 200 GPa is justified to explore the full extent of structural and electronic stability limits, even beyond typical experimental ranges. Such an approach provides fundamental insight into extreme-condition behavior and helps identify potential phase transitions relevant for high-pressure applications and future experimental validation. Phonon dispersion curves

Phonon calculations are essential for assessing the dynamical stability of a material by identifying the presence or absence of imaginary frequencies. They also provide key insights into lattice vibrations, which govern thermal conductivity, phase stability, and thermodynamic properties. The Fig. 2 presents the phonon dispersion relations at 0 GPa and 200 GPa along the high-symmetry directions (W–L–Γ–X–W–K), providing insight into lattice dynamics and stability. At 0 GPa, all phonon modes exhibit positive frequencies, confirming the dynamical stability of the structure, with acoustic branches smoothly approaching zero at the Γ point and well-separated optical modes indicating relatively weaker interatomic interactions. At 200 GPa, the phonon spectrum remains predominantly positive over the entire Brillouin zone, confirming that the crystal retains its structural integrity under extreme compression. However, a few phonon modes exhibit very small negative frequencies near the L, Γ, and X symmetry points, indicating weak softening of certain lattice vibrations. These negative values are extremely small and localized rather than extending over large portions of the Brillouin zone. Such behavior suggests only a marginal dynamical instability and does not necessarily imply a structural collapse or phase transition. Overall, the structure remains effectively stable even at 200 GPa.

Electronic properties

Studying the electronic properties under pressure enables bandgap tuning of the material without the need for cation or anion substitution. Pressure modulation in the material is one of the most appropriate ways in assessing the material’s potential for optoelectronic devices. In this section, we have analyzed the pressure effect on the electronic attributes of Cs2ScInF6. Figure 3 demonstrates the bandgap of Cs2ScInF6 under pressure along high symmetry points (W L Γ X W K). The energy range of the x-axis is selected from − 5 eV to 6 eV for the studied halide to assess the effect of pressure appropriately. At ambient pressure, the valence band maximum (VBM) is located near the L-point, while the conduction band minimum (CBM) lies around the Γ-point, indicating an indirect band-gap semiconductor. As pressure increases, CBM gradually shift toward the Fermi level. The downward displacement of the CBM reduces the energy separation between them, leading to progressive band-gap narrowing. Figure 3a reports the indirect bandgap of 4.14 eV for Cs2ScInF6 under ambient pressure.

Band structures of Cs2ScInF6 under the impact of applied pressure from 0 to 200 GPa

A significant number of the dispersion curves are noticed in the valence and the conduction band. When the pressure reaches 50 GPa, the bandgap reduces to 3.62 eV as depicted in Fig. 3b. With increment of pressure from 100 to 200 GPa, the bandgap of the material reduces from 3.06 eV to 1.86 eV as depicted in Fig. 3c–e. Furthermore, the studied halide remains indirect as pressure is increased till 200 GPa. Notably the studied halide retains its indirect bandgap nature up to 200 GPa. It is also noticed that as pressure is increased the number of dispersion curves present in the conduction band becomes more prominent which indicates enhanced electronic and band de-localization and stronger orbital interactions and enhanced optoelectronic response under compression. The obtained results reveal electronic transitions from wide band gap semiconductors to moderate band gap semiconductor. The total and partial densities of states of Cs2ScInF6 under pressure are demonstrated in Fig. 4. The total DOS represents the number of available states that an electron can occupy in the material. The total density of state plots under pressure perfectly immaculate the dispersion curves present in the band structures for the studied halide. This implies that the pressure-dependent changes in the electronic structure of the studied halide are consistently captured across the band structure and TDOS, confirming the reliability of the computed electronic states. The partial density of state corresponds to the contribution of specific atoms or atomic orbitals to the total electronic states of the material. At ambient pressure, In-d and F-s reports major contribution in the valence band whereas Sc-p states reports majority contribution in the conduction band, as portrayed in Fig. 4a. As pressure is raised to 50 GPa, Sc-p state reports minor contribution in the conduction band whereas In-d and F-s reports majority contribution in the valence band as presented in Fig. 4b. Figure 4c–e reports the PDOS plot for Cs2ScInF6 at 100 GPa, 150 GPa and 200 GPa. At these pressure, the In-d and F-s states reports minor contribution in the valence band whereas in the conduction band Sc-p and In-d reports majority contribution along with the minor contribution of F-s. This suggests that increasing pressure modifies the orbital hybridization in Cs2ScInF6 which enhances the Sc-p and In-d states in the conduction band which aids the electronic transport properties of the studied material under pressure. The projected DOS shows that at low pressure (0–50 GPa) the valence band is dominated by In-d states with minor Sc-p/F-s contributions, while the conduction band is primarily Sc-p, leaving a clear gap at Fermi level. As pressure increases (100–200 GPa), the lattice compression enhances spatial overlap between neighboring orbitals, particularly Sc-p with In-d and Sc-p with F-s, leading to stronger hybridization. This is visible as a broadening of peaks and a shift of both valence states upward and conduction states downward toward Fermi level. The increased overlap delocalizes electrons, reduces orbital energy separation, and introduces mixed-character states near the Fermi level. Consequently, the band gap progressively narrows because the enhanced d–p interactions effectively pull the band edges closer together, eventually promoting a more moderate semiconductor electronic structure at higher pressures.

Optical attributes

The optical characteristics of a material are significant tool for gaining a more complete understanding of electron configuration in semiconductors and metallic materials as incident light interacts with the materials surface [68]. Optical features are crucial to understand the appropriateness of materials for advanced solar cell technologies. The effectiveness of optoelectronic devices can be enhanced by applying pressure [69]. In this section we have demonstrated pressure effect on the optical traits of Cs2ScInF6 whereas the range of pressure is selected from 0 to 200 GPa. The optical analysis for the studied halide is done by employing the Kramer-Kronig’s complex equations. These complex dielectric equations provide mathematical description of a material's dielectric characteristics that includes both real (ε1) and imaginary (ε2) parts [70], which are mathematically described as

The real part describes the materials ability to polarize the incident light when it interacts with light whereas the imaginary part describes the materials absorption power, energy loss and scattering. The energy axis is selected from 0 to 14 eV which covers IR, visible and UV region. Under pressure, the optical properties of Cs2ScInF6 are presented in Fig. 5. Figure 5a shows that at 0GPa, Cs2ScInF6 has the lowest static dielectric function relative to other pressures. The values of ε1(ω) increased from their original static values of 1.88 (0 GPa), 2.29 (50 GPa), 2.59 (100 GPa), 2.81 (150 GPa), and 3.07 (200 GPa). ε1(ω) reaches peak values of 4.88, 5.36, 5.73, 5.95, and 6.0 at the energies of 4.85 eV, 3.89 eV, 3.39 eV, 2.97 eV, and 2.72 eV, corresponding to pressures of 0, 50, 100, 150, and 200 GPa. Two of These peaks fall within the visible solar spectrum at 150 and 200 GPa while others fall in the ultraviolet region. This trend demonstrates that the increase in pressure causes the peak of real part of the studied halides to shift from high energy level (UV region) to lower energy region (visible region) indicating a redshift. Furthermore, the polarization ability of the material increases as pressure is increased which suggests that the studied halide offers higher dispersion of incident light [71]. The energy fluctuation in the imaginary factor ε2(ω) determines the amount of light attenuation within material, and for the studied halide it is presented in Fig. 5b. The electronic band gap values of Cs2ScInF6 were calculated utilizing the particular values of ε2(ω), which were found to be consistent with the band gap values attained from the electronic properties. The computed value of ε2(ω) for Cs2ScInF6 under pressure shows a rising trend, reaching peak values at pressure 0 GPa (4.94 eV), 50 GPa (4.1 eV), 100 GPa (3.55 eV), 150 GPa (3.33 eV), and 200 GPa (3.05 eV), respectively. This trend describes that as pressure is increased, the inter-band optical transitions shifts towards lower energy region and the overall scattering in the studied halide decreases. Figure 5(c-d) reports the plots for complex refractive index in which the real component is refractive index and the imaginary component is the extinction coefficient. The dispersion and transparency of light energy are measured using the refractive index n(ω), which corresponds to the real dielectric constant’s part ε1(ω) [72]. The main optical peaks in all graphs originate from interband electronic transitions between occupied valence states and unoccupied conduction states, primarily governed by orbital character and band dispersion. The low-energy peaks (≈2–5 eV) typically arise from transitions between hybridized p–d or p–p states near the band edges, reflecting the fundamental absorption edge and strong joint density of states. Intermediate features (≈5–9 eV) are linked to deeper valence bands transitioning into higher conduction bands, often involving more localized orbitals that produce sharper structures. High-energy peaks (> 10 eV) correspond to transitions from deeper core-like valence states to highly dispersive conduction bands, giving rise to broader, intense features seen in α(ω), σ(ω), and the loss function L(ω), where plasmon-related resonances may also appear. Under increasing pressure, band structures shrink and shift due to enhanced orbital overlap, leading to red-shifting (lower energy) of peaks, and sometimes peak merging due to band hybridization. The loss function peak shifts indicate changes in plasma frequency, while modifications in ε₁(ω) drive corresponding changes in refractive index and reflectivity, reflecting an overall pressure-induced stiffening of the electronic system and redistribution of optical spectral weight.

Thermoelectric attributes

Thermoelectric materials are proficient of transmitting heat, electricity generation, and photovoltaic cell technologies [74, 75]. The thermoelectric characteristics are essentially determined by the charge carriers accessible for band gap of material and conduction [72]. The energy can be generated from heat efficiently by using perovskite materials. These materials, with high absorption coefficient and good thermal efficiency, are essential in transport applications since these parameters are considered effective in converting heat energy into electricity [76]. Applying pressure can affect thermoelectric transport characteristics, leading to a smaller band gap and higher charge carrier density. The sensitivity of these features to higher pressures can provide information about their underlying lattice and electrical properties, leading to advancements in material responsiveness and potential for pressure sensors related sensory devices. In this section we have demonstrated the thermoelectric attributes of Cs2ScInF6 under the influence of hydrostatic pressure. The thermoelectric traits are computed through the semi-classical approach embedded in the BoltzTraP code, typically within the constant relaxation time approximation (CRTA), where the carrier scattering time is assumed to be energy-independent. Conclusion

In this manuscript reports the pressure effects on the physical characteristics of Cs2ScInF6 are thoroughly analyzed from DFT-based simulations. The physical properties of Cs2ScInF6 under pressure are evaluated using the Wien2K algorithm and the FP-LAPW method. Each iteration of the first principle analysis of the halide under study is modified by a 50 GPa gap until the pressure reaches 200 GPa. The studied halide has cubic symmetry and is stable at room pressure without showing significant lattice deformation. The bandgap of Cs2ScInF6 significantly decreased as 4.14 eV (0 GPa), 3.62 eV (50 GPa), 3.06 eV (100 GPa), 2.38 eV (150 GPa) and 1.86 eV (200 GPa). Strong hybridization of Sc-p and In-d states in the conduction band at higher pressures is reported by the partial density of state for the halide under study, whereas In-d states are more prevalent in the valence band at lower pressures. Dispersion and polarization peak shifts from the UV to the visible regions as pressure is increased making Cs2ScInF6 is a potential option for visible light optoelectronic applications. The thermoelectric properties of the investigated halide show a higher Seebeck's coefficient at lower temperatures, while an increase in pressure slightly reduces the thermoelectric properties. At higher temperatures and pressures, the ZT value is observed to be 0.786, indicating that this halide is a potential candidate for the production of green energy.
