Abstract
. We initiate a systematic study of spectral theory for bounded-degree Borel pmp graphs. Specifically, we study spectral properties of the associated adjacency and Laplacian operators. We start with proving a spectral characterization of approximate measurable bipartiteness. Next, we adapt classical theorems of Wilf and Hoffman to give novel upper and lower bounds on the approximate measurable chromatic number. Using similar techniques, we then show that the approximate measurable chromatic number of a pmp graph generated by n n bounded-to-one functions is at most 2 ​ n + 1 2n+1 . Next, concerning matchings, we introduce a measurable version of Tutte’s condition and show that a spectral assumption analogous to the one from a classical theorem of Brouwer and Haemers implies this measurable Tutte condition. Finally, we show that the spectrum is continuous under local-global convergence.



1.
Introduction

A major goal of research in descriptive combinatorics is the development of techniques for constructively solving classical graph theoretic problems – for instance, vertex coloring, edge coloring, and perfect matching – on infinite graphs. While many classical techniques go through for infinite graphs, the solutions that result are typically non-constructive, dependent on tools such as the axiom of choice. Thus, in descriptive combinatorics, additional definability or measurability constraints are imposed on the solutions, resulting in a deep theory with striking departures from classical combinatorics. For comprehensive surveys, we refer to
[

,
44
]
.

In this paper, we study the descriptive combinatorics of a certain class of definable graphs using tools from spectral theory. More precisely, given a standard probability space

probability-measure-preserving

The main inspiration for our work derives from the rich area of (finitary)

Concretely, given a finite graph, one may associate to it matrices, such as the adjacency matrix and the Laplacian matrix, that encode information about the graph. The spectra of these matrices then provide information about the combinatorial properties of the associated graph. For an overview of spectral graph theory, we refer to the surveys

In this paper, we contribute to the literature on bounded-degree Borel pmp graphs through a systematic study of the spectra of the associated adjacency and Laplacian operators; we then use spectral information to prove novel theorems in measurable combinatorics. Our results include a spectral characterization of approximate measurable bipartiteness, spectral upper and lower bounds on the approximate measurable chromatic number, and a spectral inequality that implies a measurable version of Tutte’s classical condition characterizing graphs that admit perfect matchings.

Concretely, given a bounded-degree Borel pmp graph
\mathcal{G}
on
(X,\mu)
, we define the
adjacency operator

T_{\mathcal{G}}:L^{2}(X)\to L^{2}(X)
by

(T_{\mathcal{G}}f)(x)=\sum_{y\sim x}f(y)

for all
f\in L^{2}(X)
,
x\in X
; here we use the notation
y\sim x
to indicate that
y
is adjacent to
x
. The second operator we study is the
Laplacian operator

L_{\mathcal{G}}:L^{2}(X)\to L^{2}(X)
, given by
L_{\mathcal{G}}=D_{\mathcal{G}}-T_{\mathcal{G}}
, where
D_{\mathcal{G}}\in L^{\infty}(X)
is the
degree function
given by
D_{\mathcal{G}}(x)=\deg(x)
for all
x\in X
. That is, the Laplacian operator satisfies

(L_{\mathcal{G}}f)(x)=\deg(x)f(x)-\sum_{y\sim x}f(y)

for all
f\in L^{2}(X)
,
x\in X
. Both of these operators have been studied already in the literature. In particular, it is a well-known fact that they are bounded and self-adjoint, thus allowing for the application of various tools from operator theory. We give a self-contained proof of this fact in Section
3
using
Borel transports
(see Subsection
2.3
), which are applied frequently throughout. Subsequently, after establishing some first properties of
T_{\mathcal{G}}
and
L_{\mathcal{G}}
, we extend the classical Perron-Frobenius theory, sometimes under the additional assumption of
spectral gap
(see Subsection
3.4
). Although many classical results generalize to our setting, the proofs often require new ideas. For example, for finite graphs, all spectral values are eigenvalues, and many arguments in finitary spectral graph theory involve the associated eigenvectors. For infinite graphs, however, spectral values are not always eigenvalues; nevertheless, because our operators are self-adjoint, each spectral value admits a sequence of
approximate
eigenfunctions, allowing for various approximation arguments.

Our first main result concerns bipartiteness, which we study in Section
4
. It is well-known in the classical setting (see, for instance,
[
46
, Chapter 4]
) that if a finite graph is bipartite, then the spectrum of its adjacency matrix is symmetric about
0
. If the graph is in addition connected, then a strong version of the converse holds: If the negative of the maximum eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix is also an eigenvalue, then the graph is bipartite. The combination of these results therefore gives a complete spectral characterization of bipartiteness for finite connected graphs. We adapt this characterization to the setting of bounded-degree Borel pmp graphs as follows:

Theorem A

(Theorems
4.4
and
4.9
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a bounded-degree Borel pmp graph on a standard probability space.

(1)

If
\mathcal{G}
is approximately measurably bipartite, then the spectrum of
T_{\mathcal{G}}
is symmetric about
0
.

(2)

Assume in addition that
\mathcal{G}
is
d
-regular and ergodic and that
T_{\mathcal{G}}
has spectral gap. If
-d
belongs to the spectrum of
T_{\mathcal{G}}
, then
\mathcal{G}
is approximately measurably bipartite.

While some of the ideas of the classical proof can be used, additional difficulties arise in the measurable setting. For item (1) above, the approximate measurable bipartition only gives a bipartition up to a specified
\varepsilon>0
, and the adjacency operator
T_{\mathcal{G}}
mixes the leftover “bad” set of measure
\varepsilon
with the sets of the bipartition. Nevertheless, given
\lambda
in the spectrum of
T_{\mathcal{G}}
, the standard idea of swapping the signs of an eigenvector on one part of the bipartition to show that
-\lambda
is in the spectrum extends to our setting. Indeed, provided that the error is carefully controlled, we can run a similar argument using approximate eigenfunctions and an approximate bipartition instead.
For item (2), analogously to the classical case, one would like to use the signs of a given approximate eigenfunction of
-d
to define an approximate bipartition. In order for this to work in the measurable setting, one needs the additional fact that approximate eigenfunctions of
-d
are approximately constant in absolute value, which is false in general (see Remark
3.20
). However, we show that this holds under the assumption of spectral gap, allowing us to complete the argument.

In Section
5
, we prove two novel upper bounds on the approximate measurable chromatic number of a bounded-degree Borel pmp graph. Our first bound generalizes a classical theorem of Wilf
[
54
]
, which gives an upper bound on the chromatic number of a finite graph in terms of the maximum eigenvalue of its adjacency matrix.

Theorem B

(Theorem
5.5
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a bounded-degree Borel pmp graph on a standard probability space. Then the approximate measurable chromatic number of
\mathcal{G}
is bounded above by
\lfloor M(T_{\mathcal{G}})\rfloor+1
, where
M(T_{\mathcal{G}})
is the maximum spectral value of
T_{\mathcal{G}}
.

In the notation of the above theorem,
M(T_{\mathcal{G}})
can alternatively be characterized as the spectral radius of
T_{\mathcal{G}}
, and it is bounded above by the maximum degree of
\mathcal{G}
(see Proposition
3.2
). Therefore, Theorem
B
provides at least as tight an upper bound on the approximate measurable chromatic number as the standard degree plus one bound (see
[
31
, Proposition 4.6]
). In general, when
\mathcal{G}
is non-regular, Theorem
B
gives an upper bound much tighter than the degree plus one bound and even tighter than the stronger upper bound from Brooks’s theorem (see
[
19
, Theorem 1.2]
) when it is applicable. For example, it is easy to show that
M(T_{\mathcal{G}})=\sqrt{ab}
for an
(a,b)
-biregular graph (see Corollary
3.5
), allowing quadratic improvement over the bound given by Brooks’s theorem. We however note that biregular graphs are measurably bipartite, and hence trivially admit measurable 2-colorings. It’s also worth pointing out that quadratic improvement is the best possible, since we always have
M(T_{\mathcal{G}})\geq\sqrt{d}
where
d
is the maximum degree (see Lemma
3.8
).

For the proof of Theorem
B
, we recursively construct a sequence of disjoint subsets exhausting the vertex space
X
by collecting all vertices with sufficiently small degree at each step. This depends on the observation that the average degree of
\mathcal{G}
is bounded above by
M(T_{\mathcal{G}})
(see Lemma
3.8
). The remainder of the proof then involves a list-coloring algorithm, which we use to color a
(1-\varepsilon)
-proportion of the graph by backtracking through the sequence of vertex sets.

Theorem C

Another fundamental result in finitary spectral graph theory is Hoffman’s theorem
[

Since the monochromatic sets in a coloring are independent, this yields a lower bound on the chromatic number as well, which in fact applies even to non-regular graphs. In Section

Theorem D

(Theorem
6.8
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a bounded-degree Borel pmp graph on a standard probability space. Then the approximate measurable chromatic number of
\mathcal{G}
is bounded below by
\lceil 1-\frac{M(T_{\mathcal{G}})}{m(T_{\mathcal{G}})}\rceil
, where
M(T_{\mathcal{G}})
and
m(T_{\mathcal{G}})
are the maximum and minimum spectral values of
T_{\mathcal{G}}
, respectively.

We note that the lower bound from the theorem is always at least
2

\mathcal{G}

When

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Borel graphs and descriptive combinatorics

Throughout, let
(X,\mu)
be a standard probability space, and let
\mathcal{G}=(X,E(\mathcal{G}))
be a
Borel graph
on
X
, i.e., a (simple, undirected) graph on
X
such that
E(\mathcal{G})\subseteq X^{2}
is Borel. We write
x\sim y
when
x
and
y
are adjacent in
\mathcal{G}
. The
degree
of a vertex
x\in X
, denoted
\deg_{\mathcal{G}}(x)
or simply
\deg(x)
when
\mathcal{G}
is clear, is the cardinality of the set
\{y\in X:y\sim x\}
. We say that
\mathcal{G}
is
locally finite
if
\deg(x)
is finite for each
x\in X
, that
\mathcal{G}
has
bounded degree
if there is
d\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\deg(x)\leq d
for each
x\in X
, and that
\mathcal{G}
is
d
-regular
if there is
d\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\deg(x)=d
for each
x\in X
. Note that since we work throughout in the measurable setting, we always implicitly discard sets of measure zero; for instance, we say
\mathcal{G}
is locally finite if it is locally finite on a conull set. The
minimum degree
of
\mathcal{G}
is given by
\min\{d:\mu(\{x\in X:\deg(x)=d\})>0\}
. If
\mathcal{G}
has bounded degree, then its
maximum degree
is given by
\max\{d:\mu(\{x\in X:\deg(x)=d\})>0\}
.

For a subset
A\subseteq X
, we write
\mathcal{N}(A)=\{x\in X:x\text{ has a neighbor in $A$}\}
; note that
\mathcal{N}(A)
could include vertices from
A
if they are adjacent to other vertices in
A
. We also define the
closed neighborhood
(or closed
1
-ball)
B_{1}(A)
of
A
as
B_{1}(A)=A\cup\mathcal{N}(A)=\{x\in X:d(x,A)\leq 1\}
. A subset
A\subseteq X
is called
\mathcal{G}
-invariant
if
A=B_{1}(A)
, i.e.,
A
contains all its neighbors. Finally, we note that, given a subset
A\subseteq X
, we may consider the
subgraph
\mathcal{G}\restriction A
of
\mathcal{G}
induced by
A
, i.e., the graph on
A
whose edge relation
E(\mathcal{G}\restriction A)
is defined by

E(\mathcal{G}\restriction A)=E(\mathcal{G})\cap A^{2}.

It is easy to see that, when
A\subseteq X
is Borel, then
\mathcal{G}\restriction A
is Borel. Additionally, we often wish to consider subgraphs induced by measurable sets
A\subseteq X

that are not necessarily Borel
, in which case the set
A
(endowed with the subspace topology) may fail to be a standard Borel space. However, it is well-known that, if
A
is measurable, then
A
is the union of a Borel set
B
with a null set. Since all our results concern the measurable setting where null sets may be discarded, we write
\mathcal{G}\restriction A
in this situation for convenience, but in actuality we implicitly consider
\mathcal{G}\restriction B
.

A set
A\subseteq X
is
(
\mathcal{G}
-)independent
if, whenever
x,y\in A
are distinct,
x\not\sim y
. Let
Z
be a set; a map

(

\mathcal{G}
-independent for each

. The

of
\mathcal{G}

\kappa

Z
-coloring of
\mathcal{G}
for some set
Z

\kappa
. If

, we refer to

.

As discussed in the introduction, in the descriptive combinatorics setting, definability or measurability restrictions are typically placed on colorings. Specifically, the

of
\mathcal{G}

for which there is a

-measurable
k

(if such a

Clearly

\chi(\mathcal{G})\leq\chi_{\mu}(\mathcal{G})\leq\chi_{B}(\mathcal{G})\text{ and }\chi_{\mu}^{\text{ap}}(\mathcal{G})\leq\chi_{\mu}(\mathcal{G}).

We refer to
[
29
]
for additional results.

We write
E_{\mathcal{G}}
for the

of
\mathcal{G}
, i.e., the equivalence relation whose classes are the connected components of
\mathcal{G}
. When
\mathcal{G}

A
.

By considering this associated Borel equivalence relation, we can make sense of various properties for
\mathcal{G}
from the theory of Borel equivalence relations. For instance, we say
\mathcal{G}
is
ergodic
if
E_{\mathcal{G}}
is ergodic. Additionally, the graphs under consideration here are graphs that interact nicely with the probability measure
\mu
on
X
. We say that the graph
\mathcal{G}
is
(
\mu
-)probability measure preserving
, or
pmp
for short, if
E_{\mathcal{G}}
is pmp, i.e., whenever
\theta
is a partial Borel bijection on
X
with
\text{graph}(\theta)\subseteq E_{\mathcal{G}}
,
\theta
preserves
\mu
in the sense that for any
\mu
-measurable set
A\subseteq X
,
\mu(A)=\mu(\theta^{-1}(A))
(equivalently, since
\theta
is injective,
\mu(A)=\mu(\theta(A))
). Equivalently,
\mathcal{G}
is pmp if
E(\mathcal{G})
can be generated by countably many measure-preserving Borel involutions.
As for subgraphs, we note that, if
\mathcal{G}
is pmp, then any subgraph of
\mathcal{G}
induced by a Borel set is also pmp with respect to the renormalized measure.

We finish this subsection by recording the easy observation that
invariance
of sets as defined above for graphs coincides with the usual notion of invariant sets for Borel equivalence relations. We will use this fact without mention.

Lemma 2.1
.

A measurable set
A\subseteq X
is
\mathcal{G}
-invariant if and only if
A
is
E_{\mathcal{G}}
-invariant. In particular,
\mathcal{G}
is ergodic if and only if every
\mathcal{G}
-invariant set
A
satisfies
\mu(A)\in\{0,1\}
.

Proof.

First, we note that if
A\subseteq X
is
E_{\mathcal{G}}
-invariant, i.e.,
A=[A]_{E_{\mathcal{G}}}
, then since
A\subseteq B_{1}(A)\subseteq[A]_{E_{\mathcal{G}}}
, we immediately have
A=B_{1}(A)
. In other words
E_{\mathcal{G}}
-invariant sets are
\mathcal{G}
-invariant.

Conversely, assume

is
\mathcal{G}
-invariant, i.e.,
A=B_{1}(A)
. An easy induction argument on the distance to
A
then shows that, whenever
x\in X
and

, we have

. Since

if and only if

, we conclude that
\mathcal{G}
-invariant sets are
E_{\mathcal{G}}
-invariant.
∎

Bounded operators on Hilbert spaces

In this paper, we study certain operators associated to bounded-degree Borel pmp graphs. In this subsection we collect some basic facts that we use throughout; these can be found in any standard reference on bounded operators on Hilbert spaces.

Let

on

4

4

Equivalently, it is well-known that these are exactly the operators that are continuous as functions on

We start with establishing some notation and terminology regarding bounded operators, as well as recalling standard facts. For

An operator

Let

(2)

.

Let

Let

Although interlacing of eigenvalues as in for instance

Lemma 2.5

(2)

Before continuing, we state one more basic result about the spectrum of an anti-diagonal self-adjoint operator for later reference.

Lemma 2.6
.

Assume
T\in B(H)
is a self-adjoint operator. Then the spectrum
\sigma(A)
of the operator

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&T\\
T&0\end{pmatrix}

is equal to
\sigma(T)\cup-\sigma(T)
.

Proof.

If
\lambda\in\sigma(T)
and
||Tf_{n}-\lambda f_{n}||\to 0

||A(f_{n}\oplus f_{n})-\lambda(f_{n}\oplus f_{n})||\to 0

. This proves one inclusion. For the other direction, assume

, and assume

is a sequence of approximate eigenfunctions for
\lambda
. Then

||Tf_{n}-\lambda g_{n}||\to 0\quad\text{and}\quad||Tg_{n}-\lambda f_{n}||\to 0.

In particular, it follows that

||T^{2}f_{n}-\lambda^{2}f_{n}||\to 0.

In other words,

, meaning that

(or both). This finishes the proof.

We end this subsection by establishing some basic facts regarding direct sum decompositions. Given an operator

and a decomposition

of

of

Note that this is just matrix multiplication with respect to the decomposition of

Tx=\begin{pmatrix}T_{11}&T_{12}&\cdots&T_{1n}\\
T_{21}&T_{22}&\cdots&T_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
T_{n1}&T_{n2}&\cdots&T_{nn}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\sum_{j=1}^{n}T_{1j}(x_{j})\\
\sum_{j=1}^{n}T_{2j}(x_{j})\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{j=1}^{n}T_{nj}(x_{j})\end{pmatrix}

Furthermore, if

is self-adjoint, then it is easy to see that for every

,
T_{ij}^{*}=T_{ji}
. In particular, for every
1\leq i\leq n
,

is self-adjoint.

Proposition 2.7
.

Using the above notation, let
T\in B(H)
be a self-adjoint operator. Then we have the following inequalities:

(1)

\forall 1\leq i\leq n
:
M(T_{ii})\leq M(T)
and
m(T_{ii})\geq m(T)
, i.e.,
\sigma(T_{ii})\subseteq[m(T),M(T)]
.

(2)

\forall 1\leq i,j\leq n
:
||T_{ij}||\leq||T||
.

Proof.

We start with proving (1). Fix
1\leq i\leq n
, and let
f\in H_{i}
. By definition, there exists
g\in H\ominus H_{i}
, namely
g=\sum_{k\neq i}T_{ki}(f)
, such that

Tf=T_{ii}f+g.

Hence

\langle Tf,f\rangle=\langle T_{ii}f+g,f\rangle=\langle T_{ii}f,f\rangle+\langle g,f\rangle=\langle T_{ii}f,f\rangle,

since
f\perp g
. In particular, we find

\displaystyle M(T_{ii})

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in H_{i},f\neq 0}\frac{\langle T_{ii}f,f\rangle}{\langle f,f\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in H_{i},f\neq 0}\frac{\langle Tf,f\rangle}{\langle f,f\rangle}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in H,f\neq 0}\frac{\langle Tf,f\rangle}{\langle f,f\rangle}

\displaystyle=M(T).

Similarly, upon replacing
\sup
by
\inf
, we get
m(T_{ii})\geq m(T)
, finishing the proof of (1).

We now prove (2). Fix
1\leq i,j\leq n
, and let
f\in H_{j}
. By definition,
||Tf||\leq||T||||f||
. Hence by the Pythagorean theorem, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}||T_{kj}f||^{2}

\displaystyle=||\sum_{k=1}^{n}T_{kj}f||^{2}

\displaystyle=||Tf||^{2}

\displaystyle\leq||T||^{2}||f||^{2}.

In particular,
||T_{ij}f||\leq||T||||f||
. Since this holds for every

, the result follows.

2.3.
Mass transport for pmp graphs

In this final preliminary section, we collect several useful results that we use throughout the paper. Fix a standard probability space
(X,\mu)
throughout. First, we state the following standard measure-theoretic fact, with a proof provided for completeness:

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
f:X\to[0,\infty)
be a
\mu
-measurable function with
\int_{X}f(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)<\infty
. Then for all
\varepsilon>0
, there is
\delta>0
such that, whenever
B\subseteq X
is
\mu
-measurable,

\mu(B)<\delta\implies\int_{B}f(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)<\varepsilon.

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that there is
\varepsilon>0
such that, for all
\delta>0
, there is a measurable set
B
with
\mu(B)<\delta
but
\int_{B}f(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)\geq\varepsilon
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\delta_{n}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}
, let
B_{n}
be a
\mu
-measurable set such that
\mu(B_{n})<\delta_{n}
but
\int_{B_{n}}f(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)\geq\varepsilon
, and let
f_{n}=f\restriction B_{n}
, so that
f_{n}\leq f
. Note that
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mu(B_{n})<\infty
, so that, by the Borel–Cantelli lemma,
\mu(\limsup_{n\to\infty}B_{n})=0
. Note that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}B_{n}=\{x\in X:x\in B_{n}\text{ for infinitely many $n$}\}.

Therefore, there is a conull set
A\subseteq X
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x)=0
for all
x\in A
. By the dominated convergence theorem, we thus get
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{X}f_{n}(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)=0
. But then there is
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for all
n>N
,
\int_{X}f_{n}(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)=\int_{B_{n}}f(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)<\varepsilon
, a contradiction.
∎

In the remainder of this subsection, we derive several useful corollaries from the theory of mass transport. We remark that applications of mass transport are ubiquitous in descriptive set theory (see, for instance,
[
30
]
) and other areas; the underlying ideas trace back to the seminal work of Feldman and Moore
[
22
]
, who introduced the left and right counting measures and proved that their equality is equivalent to
\mu
-invariance.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
E
be a countable Borel equivalence relation on
X
. A
Borel transport
in
E
is a Borel function
\varphi:E\to[0,\infty)
. Given a Borel transport
\varphi
in
E
, we define the functions
\mathrm{out}\,\varphi,\mathrm{in}\,\varphi:X\to[0,\infty)
by

\mathrm{out}\,\varphi(x)=\sum_{yEx}\varphi(x,y),\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{in}\,\varphi(x)=\sum_{yEx}\varphi(y,x)

The well-known
mass transport principle
demonstrates that, for any Borel transport
\varphi
, the integral over
\mathrm{out}\,\varphi
matches the integral over
\mathrm{in}\,\varphi
. For the formulation below, see for instance
[
52
, Proposition 5.3]
.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
E
be a Borel pmp equivalence relation on
(X,\mu)
, and let
\varphi:E\to[0,\infty)
be a Borel transport in
E
. Then

\int_{X}\mathrm{out}\,\varphi\operatorname{d}\!\mu=\int_{X}\mathrm{in}\,\varphi\operatorname{d}\!\mu

A first useful corollary is the following:

Corollary 2.11
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a bounded-degree Borel pmp graph on
(X,\mu)
with maximum degree equal to
d
, and let
A\subseteq X
be a measurable subset of
X
. Then

\mu(\mathcal{N}(A))\leq d\mu(A).

Proof.

Define the Borel transport
\varphi_{A}:E_{\mathcal{G}}\to[0,\infty)
by
\varphi_{A}(x,y)=1_{A}(y)
if
x\sim y
and
\varphi_{A}(x,y)=0
otherwise. Then we can easily compute

\mathrm{out}\,\varphi_{A}(x)=\sum_{y\sim x}1_{A}(y)=|\{y\sim x:y\in A\}|,

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{in}\,\varphi(x)=\sum_{y\sim x}1_{A}(x)=\begin{cases}\deg(x),&\text{ if }x\in A,\\
0,&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that, for any
x\in X
,
|\{y\sim x:y\in A\}|\geq 1
if and only if
x\in\mathcal{N}(A)
. Proposition
2.10
thus implies that

(2.12)

\displaystyle d\mu(A)\geq\int_{A}\deg(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)=\int_{X}|\{y\sim x:y\in A\}|\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)\geq\mu(\mathcal{N}(A)),

finishing the proof.
∎

The next result also follows easily from Proposition
2.10
.

Corollary 2.13
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a Borel pmp graph on
(X,\mu)
. For any positive measurable functions
f,f^{\prime}:X\to[0,\infty)
, we have the following equalities:

(1)

{\displaystyle\int_{X}\sum_{y\sim x}f(y)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)=\int_{X}\deg(x)f(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)}
.

(2)

{\displaystyle\int_{X}\sum_{y\sim x}f(y)f^{\prime}(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)=\int_{X}\sum_{y\sim x}f(x)f^{\prime}(y)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)}
.

Proof.

We start with (1). Define the Borel transport
\varphi_{f}:E_{\mathcal{G}}\to[0,\infty)
by
\varphi_{f}(x,y)=f(y)
if
x\sim y
and
\varphi_{f}(x,y)=0
otherwise. Note that

\mathrm{out}\,\varphi_{f}(x)=\sum_{y\sim x}\varphi_{f}(x,y)=\sum_{y\sim x}f(y),

and

\mathrm{in}\,\varphi_{f}(x)=\sum_{y\sim x}\varphi_{f}(y,x)=\sum_{y\sim x}f(x)=\deg(x)f(x).

Proposition
2.10
now immediately implies (1).

For (2), define the Borel transport
\varphi_{f,f^{\prime}}:E_{\mathcal{G}}\to[0,\infty)
by
\varphi_{f,f^{\prime}}(x,y)=f(y)f^{\prime}(x)
if
x\sim y
and
\varphi_{f,f^{\prime}}(x,y)=0
otherwise. Note that, for any
x\in X
,

\mathrm{out}\,\varphi_{f,f^{\prime}}(x)=\sum_{y\sim x}f(y)f^{\prime}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{in}\,\varphi_{f,f^{\prime}}(x)=\sum_{y\sim x}f(x)f^{\prime}(y).

Using Proposition
2.10
again, (2) follows, finishing the proof.
∎

Finally, we have the following technical inequality:

Corollary 2.14
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a Borel pmp graph on
(X,\mu)
, let
A\subseteq X
be a measurable subset of
X
, and suppose
f:X\to[0,\infty)
is a positive measurable function. Then

\int_{A}\sum_{y\sim x}f(y)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)=\int_{X}|\{y\sim x:y\in A\}|\cdot f(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)\leq\int_{\mathcal{N}(A)}\deg(x)f(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x).

Proof.

Define the Borel transport
\varphi_{A,f}:E_{\mathcal{G}}\to[0,\infty)
by

\varphi_{A,f}(x,y)=1_{A}(x)f(y)

We then compute

\displaystyle\mathrm{out}\,\varphi_{A,f}(x)=\sum_{y\sim x}1_{A}(x)f(y)=\begin{cases}\sum_{y\sim x}f(y),&\text{ if }x\in A,\\
0,&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

and

\mathrm{in}\,\varphi_{A,f}(x)=\sum_{y\sim x}1_{A}(y)f(x)=|\{y\sim x:y\in A\}|\cdot f(x).

Applying Proposition
2.10
once again finishes the proof.
∎

3.
Operators associated to Borel graphs

In this section, we define the two main operators we study, namely the adjacency operator and the Laplacian operator. We record some basic properties of these operators, as well as information about their spectral theory and how it connects to properties of the associated Borel graphs.

Throughout this section, let
(X,\mu)
be a standard probability space, and let
\mathcal{G}
be a bounded-degree Borel pmp graph on
X
with maximum degree
d
.

3.1.
The adjacency operator

The
adjacency operator

T_{\mathcal{G}}\in B(L^{2}(X))
is defined by

(T_{\mathcal{G}}f)(x)=\sum_{y\sim x}f(y)

for all
f\in L^{2}(X),x\in X
.

Example 3.1
.

For a finite graph
\mathcal{G}
with
n
vertices, one can identify
L^{2}(X)
with
\mathbb{C}^{n}
equipped with its usual inner product. In this case, the adjacency operator is merely the adjacency matrix (viewed as a linear operator on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
). Most of the results in this paper can be interpreted in this setting, recovering known facts from the spectral theory for finite graphs.

We begin with the following crucial fact:

Proposition 3.2
.

The adjacency operator
T_{\mathcal{G}}
is bounded and self-adjoint. Moreover, the operator norm of
T_{\mathcal{G}}
is bounded by the maximum degree
d
of
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

For notational simplicity, we write
T=T_{\mathcal{G}}
throughout. We first observe that for any function
f\in L^{2}(X)
and
x\in X
, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

|Tf(x)|^{2}=\bigg|\sum_{y\sim x}f(y)\bigg|^{2}\leq\deg(x)\sum_{y\sim x}|f(y)|^{2}.

Using this together with Corollary
2.13
(1) and the fact that
\deg(x)\leq d
for each
x\in X
, we compute

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}||Tf||^{2}&=\int_{X}|Tf(x)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)\\
&\leq d\int_{X}\sum_{y\sim x}|f(y)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)\\
&=d\int_{X}\deg(x)|f(x)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)\\
&\leq d^{2}||f||^{2}.\end{split}

Since this holds for any
f\in L^{2}(X)
, we conclude that
||T||\leq d
.

For self-adjointness, we observe that, for any
f,f^{\prime}\in L^{2}(X)
,

\langle Tf,f^{\prime}\rangle=\int_{X}Tf(x)\overline{f^{\prime}(x)}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)=\int_{X}\sum_{y\sim x}f(y)\overline{f^{\prime}(x)}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)

and similarly

\langle f,Tf^{\prime}\rangle=\int_{X}\sum_{y\sim x}f(x)\overline{f^{\prime}(y)}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x).

Since
T
is bounded by the first part of the proof, the integrals appearing in the above two equations are finite by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. By writing the functions
f,f^{\prime}
as linear combinations of positive functions, the desired result thus follows from Corollary
2.13
(2).
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

If
\mathcal{G}
is
d
-regular, then
||T_{\mathcal{G}}||=M(T_{\mathcal{G}})=d
. Moreover,
d
is an eigenvalue of
T
.

Proof.

Let
g:X\to\mathbb{C}
be the constant function defined by
g(x)=1
for all
x\in X
. Then we see that

\langle g,g\rangle=\int_{X}|g(x)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)=1,

and

T_{\mathcal{G}}g(x)=\sum_{y\sim x}g(y)=\deg(x)=d\text{\, for all $x\in X$.}

We conclude that
g
is an eigenfunction with eigenvalue
d
, and in particular
||T_{\mathcal{G}}||,M(T_{\mathcal{G}})\geq d
. Since Proposition
3.2
implies that
||T_{\mathcal{G}}||,M(T_{\mathcal{G}})\leq d
, this concludes the proof.
∎

For the next result, recall that for positive integers
a
and
b
, a Borel graph
\mathcal{G}
is called
Borel
(a,b)
-biregular
if there exists a partition
X=A\cup B
into independent Borel sets such that all vertices in
A
have degree
a
and all vertices in
B
have degree
b
.

Corollary 3.5
.

If
\mathcal{G}
is Borel
(a,b)
-biregular, then
||T_{\mathcal{G}}||=M(T_{\mathcal{G}})=\sqrt{ab}
. Moreover,
\sqrt{ab}
is an eigenvalue of
T_{\mathcal{G}}
.

Proof.

By assumption, we can bipartition
X=A\cup B
into independent Borel sets
A
and
B
containing the vertices of degree
a
and
b
respectively. By considering the integrals over
A
and
B
separately, a similar computation as in Proposition
3.2
then yields the desired upper bound; furthermore, it is easy to check that the piecewise constant function taking value
\sqrt{a}
on

and
\sqrt{b}
on

is an eigenfunction for
\sqrt{ab}
.
∎

Example 3.6
.

•

Let
\alpha
be an irrational number, and consider the graph
\mathcal{G}
on
X=[0,1)
such that
x\sim y
if and only if
x+\alpha\equiv_{1}y
or
y+\alpha\equiv_{1}x
. Then
\mathcal{G}
is a
2
-regular Borel
\mu
-pmp graph, where
\mu
is the usual Lebesgue measure. In particular,
||T_{\mathcal{G}}||=M(T_{\mathcal{G}})=2
, and
\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})\subseteq[-2,2]
. We show in Example
4.12
using bipartiteness that
-2\in\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})
. Later, in Example
8.3
, we show using local-global convergence that
\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})
is in fact exactly the interval
[-2,2]
.

•

Given any
d
-regular Borel pmp graph, we can always construct a
(2,d)
-biregular graph by “splitting the edges” as follows. Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
, let
(X,\mu)
be a standard probability space, and let
\mathcal{G}=(X,E)
be a
d
-regular Borel pmp graph. Here we view
E
as a set of unordered pairs; in particular, every edge in the original (undirected) graph
\mathcal{G}
is represented by a single point in
E
. Define a measure
\nu
on
E
by setting

\nu(S)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{X}|\{x^{\prime}\in X:\{x,x^{\prime}\}\in S\}|\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x).

We define a graph
\mathcal{H}
on the space
Y=X\sqcup E
as follows. For any
x\in X
and
e\in E
, we put
x\sim e
if and only if
e
is incident on
x
in
\mathcal{G}
. Note that the sets
X
and
E
are
\mathcal{H}
-independent and that, for any
x,x^{\prime}\in X
, there is a path between
x
and
x^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{G}
if and only if there is a path between
x
and
x^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{H}
. Define now
\eta=\frac{1}{d+1}(\mu+\nu)
and note that
\eta
is a Borel probability measure on
Y
. A straightforward but tedious exercise then demonstrates that
\mathcal{H}
is
\eta
-pmp. Moreover, it follows by construction that
Y=X\cup E
yields the desired Borel partition into the independent sets of vertices with degree
d
and
2
respectively. In particular, the previous corollary implies that
M(T_{\mathcal{H}})=\sqrt{2d}
. Since the graph is measurably bipartite, Theorem
4.4
moreover implies that
m(T_{\mathcal{H}})=-\sqrt{2d}
(which in this case can also easily be checked by exhibiting a concrete eigenfunction).

When
\mathcal{G}
is not regular, it is often useful to consider the average degree.

Definition 3.7
.

The
average degree
of
\mathcal{G}
, denoted
\deg_{\text{av}}(\mathcal{G})
, is defined by

\deg_{\text{av}}(\mathcal{G})=\int_{X}\deg(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x).

In the non-regular setting, as in the finite case, we have the following inequalities relating the maximum spectral value
M(T_{\mathcal{G}})
, the average degree, and the maximum degree:

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
d
be the maximum degree of
\mathcal{G}
. Then the following hold:

(1)

m(T_{\mathcal{G}})\leq\deg_{\text{av}}(\mathcal{G})\leq M(T_{\mathcal{G}})
.

(2)

\sqrt{d}\leq M(T_{\mathcal{G}})\leq d
.

Proof.

(1) Let
g:X\to\mathbb{C}
again be defined by
g(x)=1
for all
x\in X
. Since
\langle g,g\rangle=1,
and

\langle T_{\mathcal{G}}g,g\rangle=\int_{X}\bigg(\sum_{y\sim x}g(y)\bigg)\overline{g(x)}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)=\int_{X}\deg(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)=\deg_{\text{av}}(\mathcal{G}),

we immediately have that
m(T_{\mathcal{G}})=\inf_{f\neq 0}\frac{\langle T_{\mathcal{G}}f,f\rangle}{\langle f,f\rangle}\leq\deg_{\text{av}}(\mathcal{G})\leq M(T_{\mathcal{G}})=\sup_{f\neq 0}\frac{\langle T_{\mathcal{G}}f,f\rangle}{\langle f,f\rangle}
.

(2) The upper bound follows from Proposition
3.2
. For the lower bound, consider the set
A=\{x\in X:\deg(x)=d\}\subseteq X
. By assumption,
\mu(A)>0
. Note that the graph
\mathcal{G}^{4}
obtained from
\mathcal{G}
by placing an edge between two distinct vertices if their distance in
\mathcal{G}
is at most
4
has bounded degree. Therefore, by
[
31
, Proposition 4.6]
,
\mathcal{G}^{4}\restriction A
admits a finite Borel coloring, and at least one of the monochromatic sets
A_{0}\subseteq A
obtained from this coloring has
\mu(A_{0})>0
. Then since
\mathcal{G}\restriction A
is
d
-regular and distinct points of
A_{0}
are at distance greater than
4
from each other, the inequalities in Equation
2.12
when
A
is replaced with
A_{0}
become equalities, and we deduce that
\mu(\mathcal{N}(A_{0}))=d\mu(A_{0})
. Now define the function
f\in L^{2}(X)
as follows:

\displaystyle f(x)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{d},&\text{ if }x\in A_{0},\\
1,&\text{ if }x\in\mathcal{N}(A_{0}),\\
0,&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then we can compute

\langle f,f\rangle=\int_{X}|f(x)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)=d\mu(A_{0})+\mu(\mathcal{N}(A_{0}))=2d\mu(A_{0}),

and, again using that distinct points of
A_{0}
have distance greater than
4
,

\displaystyle\langle T_{\mathcal{G}}f,f\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\sum_{y\sim x}f(y)f(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{A_{0}}d\sqrt{d}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)+\int_{\mathcal{N}(A_{0})}\Big(\sqrt{d}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\sim x,\\
y\notin A_{0}\end{subarray}}f(y)\Big)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)

\displaystyle\geq 2d\sqrt{d}\mu(A_{0}).

We conclude that

M(T_{\mathcal{G}})\geq\frac{\langle T_{\mathcal{G}}f,f\rangle}{\langle f,f\rangle}\geq\frac{2d\sqrt{d}\mu(A_{0})}{2d\mu(A_{0})}=\sqrt{d},

finishing the proof.
∎

Next, again analogous to the finite setting, we show that
m(T_{\mathcal{G}})
is always negative (unless the measure of the set of vertices incident on at least one edge is zero, in which case the adjacency operator is the zero operator). For finite graphs, this follows easily from the observation that the trace of the adjacency operator is 0. In our setting, the operators are defined on the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space
L^{2}(X)
; since there is no well-defined trace on all of
B(L^{2}(X))
, we need a different proof.

Lemma 3.9
.

If
T_{\mathcal{G}}\neq 0
, then
m(T_{\mathcal{G}})<0
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
3.8
, we can find a positive-measure Borel set
A
consisting of vertices with degree at least
1
such that distinct vertices of
A
have distance greater than
4
. Consider the function
f\in L^{2}(X)
defined by

\displaystyle f(x)=\begin{cases}d,&\text{ if }x\in A,\\
-1,&\text{ if }x\in\mathcal{N}(A),\\
0,&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

We can then easily compute

\displaystyle\langle T_{\mathcal{G}}f,f\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\sum_{y\sim x}f(y)f(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{A}-d\cdot\deg(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)+\int_{\mathcal{N}(A)}-\Big(d+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\sim x,\\
y\notin A\end{subarray}}f(y)\Big)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x).

The first integrand is clearly strictly negative, and since the maximum degree of
\mathcal{G}
is equal to
d
, the second integrand is strictly negative as well. We thus conclude that

m(T_{\mathcal{G}})\leq\frac{\langle T_{\mathcal{G}}f,f\rangle}{\langle f,f\rangle}<0,

finishing the proof.
∎

Finally, suppose
A\subseteq X
is measurable and
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{G}\restriction A
. In light of the discussion on induced subgraphs in Subsection
2.1
,
the adjacency operator
T_{\mathcal{H}}
of
\mathcal{H}
may be viewed as having domain equal to
L^{2}(A)
. By considering the direct sum decomposition
L^{2}(X)=L^{2}(A)\oplus L^{2}(X\setminus A)
, the following is immediate from Proposition
2.7
(1):

Corollary 3.10
.

Let
A\subseteq X
be
\mu
-measurable, and let
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{G}\restriction A
. Then

m(T_{\mathcal{G}})\leq m(T_{\mathcal{H}})\leq M(T_{\mathcal{H}})\leq M(T_{\mathcal{G}}).

3.2.
The Laplacian operator

The
Laplacian operator

L_{\mathcal{G}}\in L^{2}(X)
is defined by

(L_{\mathcal{G}}f)(x)=\deg(x)f(x)-\sum_{y\sim x}f(y)=(D_{\mathcal{G}}f)(x)-(T_{\mathcal{G}}f)(x),

for all
f\in L^{2}(X)
and
x\in X
, where
D_{\mathcal{G}}:X\to\mathbb{C}
is the
degree function
defined by
D_{\mathcal{G}}(x)=\deg(x)
for
x\in X
. As in Example
3.1
, we note that in the case of a finite graph, we recover the usual Laplacian matrix. We start with the following easy observation:

Lemma 3.11
.

L_{\mathcal{G}}
is bounded, self-adjoint, and positive.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{G}
has bounded degree, the degree function
D_{\mathcal{G}}:X\to\mathbb{C}
is bounded and measurable, i.e.,
D_{\mathcal{G}}\in L^{\infty}(X)
, and it takes values in
\mathbb{R}
. In particular,
D_{\mathcal{G}}
defines a bounded self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(X)
by multiplication. Since
L_{\mathcal{G}}
is a linear combination of
D_{\mathcal{G}}
and
T_{\mathcal{G}}
, and
T_{\mathcal{G}}
is bounded and self-adjoint by Proposition
3.2
, we conclude that
L_{\mathcal{G}}
is also bounded and self-adjoint.

Finally, we show that
L_{\mathcal{G}}
is positive. We use the well-known fact that an operator
L
is positive if and only if
\langle Lf,f\rangle\geq 0
for every
f\in L^{2}(X)
. Before proceeding to
L_{\mathcal{G}}
, we first note that by Corollary
2.13
(1),

\langle D_{\mathcal{G}}f,f\rangle=\int_{X}\deg(x)|f(x)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)=\int_{X}\sum_{y\sim x}|f(y)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x).

Since we also trivially have
\deg(x)|f(x)|^{2}=\sum_{y\sim x}|f(x)|^{2}
for any
x\in X
, we can combine this with self-adjointness of
T_{\mathcal{G}}
to get

\displaystyle 2\langle L_{\mathcal{G}}f,f\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle D_{\mathcal{G}}f,f\rangle+\langle D_{\mathcal{G}}f,f\rangle-\langle T_{\mathcal{G}}f,f\rangle-\langle f,T_{\mathcal{G}}f\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\sum_{y\sim x}\big[|f(x)|^{2}+|f(y)|^{2}-f(y)\overline{f(x)}-f(x)\overline{f(y)}\big]\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\sum_{y\sim x}|f(x)-f(y)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)

\displaystyle\geq 0.

This finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.12
.

Suppose
\mathcal{G}
is
d
-regular. Then
\sigma(L_{\mathcal{G}})=d-\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})=\{d-\lambda:\lambda\in\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})\}
. In particular,
M(L_{\mathcal{G}})=d-m(T_{\mathcal{G}})\leq 2d
and
m(L_{\mathcal{G}})=d-M(T_{\mathcal{G}})=0
.

Proof.

First, we note that for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
and any bounded operator
T\in B(H)
for some Hilbert space
H
,

\displaystyle\sigma(\alpha I-T)

\displaystyle=\{\lambda:(\alpha I-T)-\lambda I\text{ is not invertible}\}

\displaystyle=\{\lambda:T-(\alpha-\lambda)I\text{ is not invertible}\}

\displaystyle=\{\lambda:\alpha-\lambda\in\sigma(T)\}

\displaystyle=\{\alpha-\eta:\eta\in\sigma(T)\}.

The result now follows immediately from the fact that
L_{\mathcal{G}}=dI-T_{\mathcal{G}}
when
\mathcal{G}
is
d
-regular.
∎

Note that the fact that
m(L_{\mathcal{G}})=0
always holds. Indeed,
\sigma(L_{\mathcal{G}})\subset[0,\infty)
since
L_{\mathcal{G}}
is positive, and it is easy to check that the constant functions are eigenfunctions with eigenvalue
0
.

Remark 3.13
.

Note that the fact that
L_{\mathcal{G}}
is positive also easily follows from Lemma
3.12
in case
\mathcal{G}
is
d
-regular, as the lemma shows that its spectrum is contained in
d-\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})\subseteq[0,2d]
. In fact, the inclusion
\sigma(L_{\mathcal{G}})\subset[0,2d]
, where
d
is the maximum degree, remains valid for non-regular graphs, since
L_{\mathcal{G}}
is positive and
||L_{\mathcal{G}}||=||D_{\mathcal{G}}-T_{\mathcal{G}}||\leq||D_{\mathcal{G}}||+||T_{\mathcal{G}}||\leq 2d
.

3.3.
First basic properties

We start with the observation that
|m(T_{\mathcal{G}})|\leq M(T_{\mathcal{G}})
; hence, in particular, all spectral values are bounded in absolute value by
M(T_{\mathcal{G}})
. This generalizes the classical Perron–Frobenius theory (see, for instance,
[
46
, Chapter 4]
).

Lemma 3.14
.

We always have
|m(T_{\mathcal{G}})|\leq M(T_{\mathcal{G}})
. In particular
\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})\subseteq[m(T_{\mathcal{G}}),M(T_{\mathcal{G}})]\subseteq[-M(T_{\mathcal{G}}),M(T_{\mathcal{G}})]
and
||T_{\mathcal{G}}||=M(T_{\mathcal{G}})
.

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
, write
T=T_{\mathcal{G}}
, and let
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of approximate eigenfunctions of norm 1 in
L^{2}(X)
for
m(T)
. By assumption, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq N
,

\displaystyle|m(T)|-\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq|\langle Tf_{n},f_{n}\rangle|

\displaystyle=\Bigg|\int_{X}\sum_{y\sim x}f_{n}(y)\overline{f_{n}(x)}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{X}\sum_{y\sim x}|f_{n}(y)||f_{n}(x)|\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\langle T|f_{n}|,|f_{n}|\rangle

\displaystyle\leq M(T).

As
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, this finishes the proof.
∎

The first corollary is the analogue of the classical fact that the maximum eigenvalue has a positive eigenvector.

Corollary 3.15
.

If
T_{\mathcal{G}}f=M(T_{\mathcal{G}})f
for some
f\in L^{2}(X)
, then
T_{\mathcal{G}}|f|=M(T_{\mathcal{G}})|f|
. In particular, if
M(T_{\mathcal{G}})
is an eigenvalue, then it has a positive eigenfunction.

Proof.

We write
T=T_{\mathcal{G}}
and
M=M(T_{\mathcal{G}})
. Assume without loss of generality that
||f||=1
. A computation analogous to the one in Lemma
3.14
shows that
\langle T|f|,|f|\rangle=M
. In particular,

0\leq||T|f|-M|f|||^{2}=\langle T|f|-M|f|,T|f|-M|f|\rangle=||T|f|||^{2}-M^{2}\leq 0.

We conclude that, indeed,
T|f|=M|f|
.

∎

In fact, when
M(T_{\mathcal{G}})
is not an eigenvalue, we note that the same proof yields the fact that there always exists a sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of positive approximate eigenfunctions for
M(T_{\mathcal{G}})
. A similar argument gives the following:

Corollary 3.16
.

Let
d
be the maximum degree of
\mathcal{G}
. If
-d\in\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})
and
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of functions in
L^{2}(X)
of norm
1
satisfying
||T_{\mathcal{G}}f_{n}+df_{n}||\to 0
, then
||T_{\mathcal{G}}|f_{n}|-d|f_{n}|||\to 0
. In particular,
d\in\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})
.

The next part of the classical Perron–Frobenius theory for finite graphs asserts that if
\mathcal{G}
is connected, then
the largest eigenvalue has multiplicity
1
and its eigenvector has strictly positive entries. We observe that this goes through in our setting upon replacing connectedness by its natural analogue, ergodicity.

Lemma 3.17
.

Assume
\mathcal{G}
is ergodic and
M(T_{\mathcal{G}})
is an eigenvalue with a positive eigenfunction
f
. Then
f>0
.

Proof.

Let
X_{0}=\{x\in X:f(x)=0\}
and assume
\mu(X_{0})>0
. Since
f\geq 0
and
M(T_{\mathcal{G}})f(x)=T_{\mathcal{G}}f(x)=\sum_{y\sim x}f(y)
for almost every
x\in X
, we see that for almost every
x\in X_{0}
and
y\sim x
,
f(y)=0
. In other words,
X_{0}=B_{1}(X_{0})
modulo null sets, i.e.,
X_{0}
is
\mathcal{G}
-invariant. By ergodicity, we conclude that
X_{0}=X
. This contradicts that
f\neq 0
.
∎

Lemma 3.18
.

Assume
\mathcal{G}
is ergodic and
M(T_{\mathcal{G}})
is an eigenvalue. Then
\ker(T_{\mathcal{G}}-M(T_{\mathcal{G}})I)=\mathbb{C}f_{0}
for some strictly positive function
f_{0}\in L^{2}(X)
.

Proof.

For convenience, we write
T=T_{\mathcal{G}}
and
M=M(T_{\mathcal{G}})
. We start with the following claim:

Claim 3.19
.

If
Tf=Mf
for some real-valued function
f\in L^{2}(X)
, then
f\geq 0
or
f\leq 0
.

Proof of Claim
3.19
.

Write
f=f_{+}-f_{-}
as its positive part minus its negative part. Note that
f_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}(|f|\pm f)
, and thus by Corollary
3.15
,
Tf_{\pm}=Mf_{\pm}
. However, by construction,
f_{+}f_{-}=0
; hence by Lemma
3.17
, we necessarily have
f_{+}=0
or
f_{-}=0
.
∎

We can now finish the proof. Using Corollary
3.15
, let
f_{0}
be a positive eigenfunction for
T
. From Lemma
3.17
, it follows that
f_{0}
is strictly positive. Assume
Tf=Mf
for some function
f
which is not a multiple of
f_{0}
. By passing to the real or complex part of
f
if necessary, we can assume
f
is real-valued and not a multiple of
f_{0}
. In particular, there exists
r\in\mathbb{R}
such that both
\mu(\{x\in X:f(x)-rf_{0}(x)>0\})>0
and
\mu(\{x\in X:f(x)-rf_{0}(x)<0\})>0
. However, since
f-rf_{0}
is an eigenfunction of
T
with eigenvalue
M
, this contradicts Claim
3.19
, finishing the proof.
∎

Remark 3.20
.

Since approximate eigenfunctions are natural for self-adjoint operators on infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, one might expect that, if
\mathcal{G}
is ergodic, then whenever
f_{n}\in L^{2}(X)
are functions of norm 1 satisfying
||T_{\mathcal{G}}f_{n}-M(T_{\mathcal{G}})f_{n}||\to 0
and
f_{n}\geq 0
, then
\mu(\{x\in X:f_{n}(x)=0\})\to 0
. However, this can never be true in general. Indeed, consider any free ergodic but not strongly ergodic measure-preserving action of a countable discrete group
\Gamma
on
(X,\mu)
, e.g., irrational rotation as an action of
\mathbb{Z}
on
S^{1}
. The Schreier graph of this action yields a
d
-regular Borel pmp graph on
X
. Because the action is not strongly ergodic, we can find a sequence of Borel sets
A_{n}\subseteq X
each having measure
\frac{1}{2}
that are approximately invariant for the action. It is then easy to check that the indicator functions
1_{A_{n}}
yield a sequence of approximate eigenfunctions for the (maximum) eigenvalue
d
. On the other hand, we will show below that the natural condition of spectral gap is in fact sufficient to deduce that any approximate eigenfunction of
d
is approximately constant.

Next, we record an easy generalization of the fact that eigenvectors of different eigenvalues are perpendicular to each other. We are mainly interested in the case where
\lambda_{1}=d
and the graph has spectral gap (see Subsection
3.4
).

Lemma 3.21
.

Let
T\in B(H)
be a self-adjoint operator. If
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}\in\sigma(T)
,
\lambda_{1}
is an eigenvalue, and
||Tf_{n}-\lambda_{2}f_{n}||\to 0
, then for any eigenfunction
g
of
\lambda_{1}
, we have
\langle f_{n},g\rangle\to 0
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\langle f_{n},g\rangle-\lambda_{2}\langle f_{n},g\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle f_{n},Tg\rangle-\lambda_{2}\langle f_{n},g\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle Tf_{n},g\rangle-\lambda_{2}\langle f_{n},g\rangle

\displaystyle\to 0,

from which the result follows immediately.
∎

Corollary 3.22
.

Assume
\mathcal{G}
is
d
-regular and
\lambda\in\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})
with
\lambda\neq d
. If
||T_{\mathcal{G}}f_{n}-\lambda f_{n}||\to 0
, then
\langle f_{n},1\rangle\to 0
.

Let
L^{2}_{0}(X)
denote the set of functions perpendicular to 1:

L^{2}_{0}(X)=L^{2}(X)\ominus\mathbb{C}1=\{f\in L^{2}(X):\int_{X}f(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)=0\}.

Since we can perturb any sequence of functions that are approximately perpendicular to
1
to a sequence of functions perpendicular to
1
, we obtain the following corollary:

Corollary 3.23
.

Assume
\mathcal{G}
is
d
-regular. Then
\sigma(T|_{L^{2}_{0}(X)})
is equal to either
\sigma(T)
or
\sigma(T)\setminus\{d\}
.

3.4.
Spectral gap

To deal with the subtleties arising from approximately invariant sets (as outlined for instance in Remark
3.20
), we sometimes assume that the adjacency operator has spectral gap, a property that has appeared in many forms in the literature and has seen important applications across various disciplines. Just as for finite graphs, it is known that spectral gap connects to expansion properties of the graph; see for instance
[
38
,
26
]
, as well as Section
7
. We work with the following definition of spectral gap in the case when
\mathcal{G}
is regular:

Definition 3.24
.

Assume
\mathcal{G}
is a
d
-regular Borel pmp graph. We say
\mathcal{G}
has
spectral gap
if there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})\subseteq[-d,d-\varepsilon]\cup\{d\}
. We refer to the largest such
\varepsilon
as the
spectral gap
of
\mathcal{G}
.

Remark 3.25
.

Since we restrict our attention to regular graphs, we may equivalently define the spectral gap using the Laplacian operator; in this case, the spectral gap is simply the largest
\varepsilon
such that
\sigma(L_{\mathcal{G}})\subseteq\{0\}\cup[\varepsilon,2d]
. Since
0
is always an eigenvalue of
L_{\mathcal{G}}
with the constant functions as eigenfunctions, this definition is sometimes more natural, although in our situation it is equivalent.

There are many natural examples of (regular) Borel pmp graphs with spectral gap, notably Schreier graphs of group actions with spectral gap; this includes Bernoulli actions of non-amenable groups and actions of Property (T) groups. In contrast, it is well-known that Schreier graphs of actions of amenable groups never have spectral gap.

Before proceeding, we note that any finite connected graph has spectral gap. It is therefore perhaps unsurprising that certain classical techniques extend naturally to Borel graphs with spectral gap, while it is not always clear if they extend in general (see for instance Remark
4.13
).

We end this section with a few easy observations regarding Borel pmp graphs with spectral gap; we will see further uses in Sections
4
and
7
. First, we note that it follows from Proposition
2.3
that if
\mathcal{G}
has spectral gap, then
\sigma(T|_{\ker(T-dI)^{\perp}})=\sigma(T)\setminus\{d\}
. If moreover
\mathcal{G}
is ergodic, then Lemma
3.18
implies the following:

Corollary 3.26
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an ergodic
d
-regular Borel pmp graph with spectral gap. Then we have
\sigma(T|_{L^{2}_{0}(X)})=\sigma(T)\setminus\{d\}
.

In this case, using Proposition
2.3
, it also follows easily that any sequence of approximate eigenfunctions for
d
is approximately constant (see the proof of Theorem
4.9
).

Finally, another classical result for finite connected graphs is the fact that
\mathcal{G}
is regular if the largest eigenvalue is equal to the maximum degree. We prove that this goes through when
M(T_{\mathcal{G}})
is a genuine eigenvalue, so for instance when
\mathcal{G}
has spectral gap, although we do not know whether this assumption is necessary.

Proposition 3.27
.

Assume
\mathcal{G}
is ergodic, the maximum degree of
\mathcal{G}
is
d
,
M(T_{\mathcal{G}})=d
, and
M(T_{\mathcal{G}})
is an eigenvalue. Then
\mathcal{G}
is
d
-regular.

Proof.

Write
T=T_{\mathcal{G}}
and let
f
be a function of norm 1 satisfying
Tf=df
. By Corollary
3.15
, we can assume that
f
is positive, and then Lemma
3.17
implies that necessarily
f>0
. Furthermore, from Corollary
2.14
, we deduce that

d\int_{X}f(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)=\int_{X}Tf(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)=\int_{X}\sum_{y\sim x}f(y)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)=\int_{X}\deg(x)f(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x).

Assume for contradiction that
X_{<d}=\{x\in X:\deg(x)<d\}
satisfies
\mu(X_{<d})>0
. Then the right-hand side of the above equation satisfies

\int_{X}\deg(x)f(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)\leq(d-1)\int_{X_{<d}}f(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)+d\int_{X\setminus X_{<d}}f(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)<d\int_{X}f(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x),

yielding the desired contradiction.
∎

4.
Bipartiteness

Recall that a graph
\mathcal{G}
on a set
X
is
bipartite
if
X
can be partitioned into two disjoint
\mathcal{G}
-independent sets, or equivalently if
\mathcal{G}
has no odd cycles. For a finite graph
\mathcal{G}
, it is well-known that if
\mathcal{G}
is bipartite, then the spectrum of its adjacency operator is symmetric about 0, and that if
\mathcal{G}
is moreover connected, then the converse holds as well. In fact, the negative of the maximum eigenvalue being in the spectrum is sufficient to deduce bipartiteness.

In this section, we show that this result goes through for bounded-degree Borel pmp graphs upon replacing bipartiteness with
approximate measurable bipartiteness
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a Borel graph on a standard probability space
(X,\mu)
. We say
\mathcal{G}
is
approximately measurably bipartite
if for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exist disjoint
\mathcal{G}
-independent measurable subsets
A,B\subseteq X
such that
\mu(A\cup B)>1-\varepsilon
.

Remark 4.2
.

Note that a Borel graph
\mathcal{G}
is approximately measurably bipartite if and only if
\chi^{\text{ap}}_{\mu}(\mathcal{G})\leq 2
. This is consistent with the well-known classical fact that bipartiteness and 2-colorability are equivalent.

For clarity, we make the following precise:

Definition 4.3
.

For a bounded, self-adjoint operator
T
, we say that the spectrum of
T
is
symmetric
if, whenever
\lambda\in\sigma(T)
, also
-\lambda\in\sigma(T)
.

We first show that the adjacency operator of any approximately measurably bipartite graph has symmetric spectrum. This argument requires no assumptions on
\mathcal{G}
beyond that it is a bounded-degree Borel pmp graph. For the converse (see Theorem
4.9
), we additionally assume that
\mathcal{G}
is ergodic and has spectral gap (although see Remark
4.13
).

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
(X,\mu)
be a standard probability space, and let
\mathcal{G}
be a bounded-degree Borel pmp graph on
X
. If
\mathcal{G}
is approximately measurably bipartite, then the spectrum of
T_{\mathcal{G}}
is symmetric.

Proof.

We write
T=T_{\mathcal{G}}
for convenience, and we denote by
d
the maximum degree of
\mathcal{G}
. Assume
\mathcal{G}
is approximately measurably bipartite. Let
\lambda\in\sigma(T)
, and fix
1>\varepsilon>0
. It is enough to show that there is
g\in L^{2}(X)
such that

||Tg+\lambda g||^{2}<\varepsilon.

Let
\varepsilon_{0}=\frac{\varepsilon}{1+2d^{2}+2\lambda^{2}}
. Since
\lambda\in\sigma(T)
, there is a unit vector
f\in L^{2}(X)
such that
||Tf-\lambda f||^{2}<\varepsilon_{0}
. By Lemma
2.8
, there is
\delta>0
such that for any
\mu
-measurable
B
,

(4.5)

\mu(B)<\delta\implies\int_{B}|f(x)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)<\varepsilon_{0}.

Since
\mathcal{G}
is approximately measurably bipartite, there are disjoint measurable
\mathcal{G}
-independent sets
A_{1},A_{2}\subseteq X
such that
\mu(A_{1}\cup A_{2})>1-\frac{\delta}{d(d+1)}
. Let
A_{3}=X\setminus(A_{1}\cup A_{2})
, so that
\mu(A_{3})<\frac{\delta}{d(d+1)}
.

By construction, there are
f_{1}\in L^{2}(A_{1}),f_{2}\in L^{2}(A_{2}),f_{3}\in L^{2}(A_{3})
such that
f=f_{1}+f_{2}+f_{3}
. Note that
||f_{1}||,||f_{2}||,||f_{3}||\leq 1
. Define
g=f_{1}-f_{2}+f_{3}
. In particular,
|g|=|f|
,
g=f
on
A_{1}\cup A_{3}
, and
g=-f
on
A_{2}
. We claim that
g
is our desired function.

First, define
B=A_{1}\cap\mathcal{N}(A_{3})
. By Corollary
2.11
, we have
\mu(B)\leq d\mu(A_{3})
. Furthermore, for every
x\in A_{1}\setminus B
, we have by construction that all its neighbors are in
A_{2}
; hence

Tg(x)=\sum_{y\sim x}g(y)=\sum_{y\sim x}-f_{2}(y)=-\sum_{y\sim x}f(y)=-Tf(x),

and in particular

Tg(x)+\lambda g(x)=-Tf(x)+\lambda f(x).

Similarly, for every
x\in A_{2}
, we get

Tg(x)=\sum_{y\sim x,y\in A_{1}}g(y)+\sum_{y\sim x,y\in A_{3}}g(y)=\sum_{y\sim x,y\in A_{1}}f_{1}(y)+\sum_{y\sim x,y\in A_{3}}f_{3}(y)=\sum_{y\sim x}f(y)=Tf(x)

and thus

Tg(x)+\lambda g(x)=Tf(x)-\lambda f(x).

Combining the above, we can now compute

(4.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}||Tg+\lambda g||^{2}&=\int_{X}|Tg(x)+\lambda g(x)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)\\
&=\int_{(A_{1}\setminus B)\cup A_{2}}|Tg(x)+\lambda g(x)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)+\int_{B\cup A_{3}}|Tg(x)+\lambda g(x)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)\\
&=\int_{(A_{1}\setminus B)\cup A_{2}}|Tf(x)-\lambda f(x)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)+\int_{B\cup A_{3}}|Tg(x)+\lambda g(x)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x).\end{split}

By assumption, the first summand is bounded by
\varepsilon_{0}
. For the second summand, we first note that the triangle inequality implies that
|a+b|^{2}\leq 2|a|^{2}+2|b|^{2}
, and thus similarly to (
3.3
), we get

(4.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{B\cup A_{3}}|Tg(x)+\lambda g(x)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)&\leq 2\int_{B\cup A_{3}}|Tg(x)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)+2\int_{B\cup A_{3}}|\lambda g(x)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)\\
&\leq 2d\int_{B\cup A_{3}}\sum_{y\sim x}|g(y)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)+2\lambda^{2}\int_{B\cup A_{3}}|g(x)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)\\
&=2d\int_{B\cup A_{3}}\sum_{y\sim x}|f(y)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)+2\lambda^{2}\int_{B\cup A_{3}}|f(x)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x).\end{split}

Since
\mu(B\cup A_{3})\leq(d+1)\mu(A_{3})<\delta
, line (
4.5
) tells us that the second summand is bounded above by
2\lambda^{2}\varepsilon_{0}
. In order to deal with the first summand, we observe that Corollary
2.11
implies that
\mu(\mathcal{N}(B\cup A_{3}))\leq d\mu(B\cup A_{3})\leq d(d+1)\mu(A_{3})<\delta
, and hence using Corollary
2.14
and line (
4.5
), we get

(4.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{B\cup A_{3}}\sum_{y\sim x}|f(y)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)&\leq\int_{\mathcal{N}(B\cup A_{3})}\deg(x)|f(x)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)\\
&\leq d\int_{\mathcal{N}(B\cup A_{3})}|f(x)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)\\
&<d\varepsilon_{0}.\end{split}

Combining (
4.6
), (
4.7
), and (
4.8
), we get

||Tg+\lambda g||^{2}<\varepsilon_{0}+2d^{2}\varepsilon_{0}+2\lambda^{2}\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon.

This finishes the proof of the theorem.
∎

The next theorem shows in particular that the converse holds if
\mathcal{G}
is ergodic and
d
-regular and has spectral gap.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
(X,\mu)
be a standard probability space, and let
\mathcal{G}
be an ergodic
d
-regular Borel pmp graph on
X
with spectral gap. If
-d\in\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})
, then
\mathcal{G}
is approximately measurably bipartite.

Proof.

Assume that
-d\in\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})
and fix
1>\varepsilon>0
. We show that there exist disjoint
\mathcal{G}
-independent Borel subsets
A,B\subseteq X
such that
\mu(A\cup B)>1-\varepsilon
. Let
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of functions in
L^{2}_{0}(X)
of norm 1 such that

||Tf_{n}+df_{n}||\to 0.

Note that we can assume that all the
f_{n}
are real-valued. Indeed, if
f_{n}=f_{n}^{(1)}+if_{n}^{(2)}
is the decomposition of
f_{n}
into its real and imaginary parts, then by definition of
T
, the decomposition of
Tf_{n}
into its real and imaginary parts is given by
Tf_{n}=Tf_{n}^{(1)}+iTf_{n}^{(2)}
.

Claim 4.10
.

Without loss of generality, we can assume
|f_{n}|
is a constant function for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof of Claim
4.10
.

By Corollary
3.16
, we have
||T|f_{n}|-d|f_{n}|||\to 0
, i.e.,
(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=(|f_{n}|)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of approximate eigenfunctions for
d
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we decompose
g_{n}=g_{n}^{(0)}+g_{n}^{(1)}
where
g_{n}^{(0)}=\int_{X}g_{n}(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)\in\mathbb{C}1
and
g_{n}^{(1)}\in L^{2}_{0}(X)
. Combining the assumptions with Lemma
3.18
and Proposition
2.3
, we obtain
c>0
such that

||Tg_{n}^{(1)}-dg_{n}^{(1)}||\geq c||g_{n}^{(1)}||

for all
n
. However, since
Tg_{n}^{(0)}=dg_{n}^{(0)}
, we also have
||Tg_{n}^{(1)}-dg_{n}^{(1)}||=||Tg_{n}-dg_{n}||\to 0
, and so we conclude that
||g_{n}^{(1)}||\to 0
.

Consider now the function
\tilde{f}_{n}
defined by

\tilde{f}_{n}(x)=f_{n}(x)-\mathrm{sgn}(f_{n}(x))g_{n}^{(1)}(x).

Then we have

\displaystyle|\tilde{f}_{n}(x)|=\begin{cases}|f_{n}(x)-g_{n}^{(1)}(x)|=|\,|f_{n}(x)|-g_{n}^{(1)}(x)|=|g_{n}^{(0)}(x)|,&\text{ if }f_{n}(x)\geq 0,\text{ and}\\
|f_{n}(x)+g_{n}^{(1)}(x)|=|-|f_{n}(x)|+g_{n}^{(1)}(x)|=|g_{n}^{(0)}(x)|,&\text{ if }f_{n}(x)<0.\end{cases}

In particular,
|\tilde{f}_{n}|
is constant. Moreover,

\displaystyle||T\tilde{f}_{n}+d\tilde{f}_{n}||

\displaystyle\leq||Tf_{n}+df_{n}||+||Tg_{n}^{(1)}||+d||g_{n}^{(1)}||

\displaystyle\leq||Tf_{n}+df_{n}||+2d||g_{n}^{(1)}||

\displaystyle\to 0.

This finishes the proof of the claim.
∎

Using the claim (and normalizing if necessary), we can thus find a sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of functions in
L^{2}_{0}(X)
such that
|f_{n}|=1
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
||Tf_{n}+df_{n}||\to 0
. Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
||Tf_{N}+df_{N}||\leq\varepsilon
. Then the set
Y=\{x\in X:|Tf_{N}(x)+df_{N}(x)|\geq\sqrt{\varepsilon}\}
necessarily has
\mu(Y)\leq\varepsilon
.

Without loss of generality (by passing to the real part of
f_{N}
if necessary), we have
f_{N}(x)\in\{\pm 1\}
for every
x\in X
. Fix
x\in X\setminus Y
and assume
f_{N}(x)=1
. We claim that every
y\in X
adjacent to
x
satisfies
f_{N}(y)=-1
. Indeed, since
x\notin Y
, we have

|Tf_{N}(x)+df_{N}(x)|=\sum_{y\sim x}f_{N}(y)+d\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

If there exists
y_{0}\sim x
with
f_{N}(y_{0})=1
, then

\sum_{y\sim x}f_{N}(y)+d=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\sim x\\
y\neq y_{0}\end{subarray}}f_{N}(y)+1+d\geq 2>\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

Similarly, if
x\in X\setminus Y
satisfies
f_{N}(x)=-1
, then every
y\in X
adjacent to
x
satisfies
f_{N}(y)=1
. In particular, if we define
A=\{x\in X\setminus Y:f_{N}(x)=1\}
and
B=\{x\in X\setminus Y:f_{N}(x)=-1\}
, then both
A
and
B
are
\mathcal{G}
-independent, and
\mu(A\cup B)=\mu(X\setminus Y)\geq 1-\varepsilon
. This finishes the proof.
∎

We note that Theorems
4.4
and
4.9
yield Theorem
A
from the introduction. The following is also immediate:

Corollary 4.11
.

Let
(X,\mu)
be a standard probability space, and let
\mathcal{G}
be an ergodic
d
-regular Borel pmp graph on
X
with spectral gap. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\chi_{\mu}^{\text{ap}}(\mathcal{G})\leq 2
, i.e.,
\mathcal{G}
is approximately measurably bipartite.

(2)

\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})
is symmetric.

(3)

-d\in\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})
.

Example 4.12
.

Let
\alpha
be an irrational number, and consider again the
2
-regular Borel pmp graph
\mathcal{G}
on
X=[0,1)
such that
x\sim y
if and only if
x+\alpha\equiv_{1}y
or
y+\alpha\equiv_{1}x
.

It is well-known that the approximate
\mu
-measurable chromatic number of
\mathcal{G}
is
2
; to see this, let
\varepsilon>0
, and let
C=[0,\gamma)
, where
\gamma<\min\{\alpha,1-\alpha,\varepsilon\}
is positive. Put
A=X\setminus C
, and define a measurable
2
-coloring on
\mathcal{G}\restriction A
by
c(x)=0
if the minimum number of rotations of
x
by
\alpha
to get into
C
is even,
c(x)=1
otherwise.

From the results in this section, it now follows immediately that
\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})
is symmetric. In particular,
m(T_{\mathcal{G}})=-M(T_{\mathcal{G}})=-2\in\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})
.

Remark 4.13
.

We note that ergodicity is a necessary assumption for the implication
(2)\Rightarrow(1)
. Indeed, it is easy to construct finite non-ergodic (i.e., disconnected) graphs with symmetric spectrum that are not
2
-colorable. For instance, one can take the graph on
9
vertices that is the disjoint union of a triangle, a square, and a single edge connecting two vertices. On the other hand, we do not know whether the
(2)\Rightarrow(1)
implication in Corollary
4.11
remains true without the assumptions of regularity and/or spectral gap. Indeed, for regularity, we simply observe that there exist finite non-regular bipartite graphs. As for spectral gap, we note that the graph
\mathcal{G}
arising from irrational rotation in Example

satisfies all conditions in Corollary
4.11
, but does not have spectral gap. Indeed, since
\mathbb{Z}
is amenable,
\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright S^{1}
is not strongly ergodic. In particular, there exists a sequence of almost invariant sets, and hence a sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{0,1\}
-valued approximate eigenfunctions for
2\in\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})
with
\mu(\{x\in X:f_{n}(x)=1\})=\frac{1}{2}
for each

. Compare also with
[

, which implies that whenever
\mathcal{G}
is hyperfinite,

, where

.

5.
Upper bounds on the chromatic number

In this section, we give novel upper bounds on the approximate measurable chromatic number of a bounded-degree Borel pmp graph. Before we proceed to the main results, we prove two lemmas. First, recall that, if
Y
is a Polish space, then the space
[Y]^{<\infty}
of finite subsets of
Y
is a standard Borel space. A Borel map
L:X\to[Y]^{<\infty}
is a
Borel (
Y
-)list assignment
. A
Borel
L
-coloring
of
\mathcal{G}
is a Borel proper
Y
-coloring
c
of
\mathcal{G}
with the property that, for each
x\in X
,
c(x)\in L(x)
. The proof of the following proposition requires just a small modification to the proof of
[
31
, Proposition 4.6]
; see also
[
19
, Proposition 3.8]
:

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally finite Borel graph on a standard Borel space
X
. Let
Y
be a Polish space, and suppose
L:X\to[Y]^{<\infty}
is a Borel list assignment such that
\deg(x)<|L(x)|
for each
x\in X
. Then
\mathcal{G}
admits a Borel
L
-coloring.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{G}
is locally finite, there are
\mathcal{G}
-independent Borel sets
A_{0},A_{1},\dots
such that
X=\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A_{n}
by
[
31
, Proposition 4.5]
. Let
<_{Y}
be a Borel linear ordering on
Y
, and for each
x\in A_{0}
, define
c(x)
to be the
<_{Y}
-least element of
L(x)
.

Now fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and assume that the partial Borel
L
-coloring
c
has been extended to the domain
A_{0}\cup\cdots\cup A_{n}
. For each
x\in A_{n+1}
, let

L_{n+1}(x)=L(x)\setminus\{c(y):y\in\mathcal{N}(x)\cap(A_{0}\cup\cdots\cup A_{n})\}.

Then
|L_{n+1}(x)|\geq 1
for each
x\in A_{n+1}
; indeed, if
x\in A_{n+1}
, then

|\{c(y):y\in\mathcal{N}(x)\cap(A_{0}\cup\cdots\cup A_{n})\}|\leq\deg(x),

and so

\displaystyle|L_{n+1}(x)|

\displaystyle=|L(x)|-|\{c(y):y\in\mathcal{N}(x)\cap(A_{0}\cup\cdots\cup A_{n})\}|

\displaystyle\geq\deg(x)+1-\deg(x)

\displaystyle=1.

So extend
c
to
A_{n+1}
by defining
c(x)
to be the
<_{Y}
-least element of
L_{n+1}(x)
for each
x\in A_{n+1}
. The map
c
that results from this inductive procedure is a Borel
L
-coloring of
\mathcal{G}
.
∎

We also prove the following technical lemma, which in fact can be strengthened (see Remark
5.3
):

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally finite Borel graph on a standard probability space
(X,\mu)
. Let
M
be a positive integer, let
r\in(0,1)
, and let
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of pairwise disjoint Borel subsets of
X
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
B_{n}=\bigcup_{k\leq n}A_{k}
,
\mathcal{G}_{n}=\mathcal{G}\restriction(X\setminus B_{n-1})
, and set
A_{-1}=B_{-1}=\emptyset
. Assume the following conditions hold for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

(1)

\mu(X\setminus B_{n})\leq r\mu(X\setminus B_{n-1})
, and

(2)

\deg_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}(x)\leq M
for all
x\in A_{n}
.

Then
\chi_{\mu}^{\text{ap}}(\mathcal{G})\leq M+1
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. By (1), for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mu(X\setminus B_{n})\leq r^{n+1}.

Since
r\in(0,1)
, there is
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for all
n\geq N
,

\mu(X\setminus B_{n})<\varepsilon,

so that
\mu(B_{n})>1-\varepsilon
.

For each
x\in X
, let
L_{N}(x)=\{0,\dots,M\}
. Note that, for each
x\in A_{N}
,
\deg_{\mathcal{G}_{N}}(x)\leq M
, and so by Proposition
5.1
, there is a Borel
L_{N}
-coloring
c_{N}
of
\mathcal{G}\restriction A_{N}
.

Now fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k<N
and assume that a Borel
(M+1)
-coloring
c_{N-k}
has been defined on
\mathcal{G}\restriction(A_{N-k}\cup\cdots\cup A_{N})
. Now, for each
x\in A_{N-(k+1)}
, define

L_{N-(k+1)}(x)=L_{N}(x)\setminus\{c_{N-k}(y):y\in\mathcal{N}(x)\cap(A_{N-k}\cup\cdots\cup A_{N})\};

then by (2),

\displaystyle|L_{N-(k+1)}(x)|

\displaystyle\geq|L_{N}(x)|-|\mathcal{N}(x)\cap(A_{N-k}\cup\cdots\cup A_{N})|

\displaystyle=(M+1)-|\mathcal{N}(x)\cap(A_{N-k}\cup\cdots\cup A_{N})|

\displaystyle\geq(\deg_{\mathcal{G}_{N-(k+1)}}(x)+1)-|\mathcal{N}(x)\cap(A_{N-k}\cup\cdots\cup A_{N})|

\displaystyle=(\deg_{\mathcal{G}_{N-(k+1)}}(x)-|\mathcal{N}(x)\cap(A_{N-k}\cup\cdots\cup A_{N})|)+1

\displaystyle\geq|\mathcal{N}(x)\cap A_{N-(k+1)}|+1

\displaystyle=\deg_{\mathcal{G}\restriction(A_{N-(k+1)})}(x)+1.

There is then a Borel
L_{N-(k+1)}
-coloring
\tilde{c}_{N-(k+1)}
of
\mathcal{G}\restriction(A_{N-(k+1)})
by Proposition
5.1
.

Note that
c_{N-(k+1)}=\tilde{c}_{N-(k+1)}\cup c_{N-k}
is an
(M+1)
-coloring of
\mathcal{G}\restriction(A_{N-(k+1)}\cup\cdots\cup A_{N})
; indeed, by pairwise disjointness of the sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
\tilde{c}_{N-(k+1)}\cup c_{N-k}
is well-defined. Let
x,y\in A_{N-(k+1)}\cup A_{N-k}\cup\cdots\cup A_{N}
be
\mathcal{G}
-adjacent. If
x,y\in A_{N-(k+1)}
, then because
\tilde{c}_{N-(k+1)}
is a coloring,
\tilde{c}_{N-(k+1)}(x)\neq\tilde{c}_{N-(k+1)}(y)
. Similarly, if
x,y\in A_{N-k}\cup\cdots\cup A_{N}
, then
c_{N-k}(x)\neq c_{N-k}(y)
. If
x\in A_{N-(k+1)}
and
y\in A_{N-k}\cup\cdots\cup A_{N}
, then since
c_{N-k}(y)\notin L_{N-(k+1)}(x)
,
\tilde{c}_{N-(k+1)}(x)\neq c_{N-k}(y)
.

Therefore, the map
c=c_{0}=\tilde{c}_{0}\cup\cdots\cup\tilde{c}_{N}
is a Borel
(M+1)
-coloring of
\mathcal{G}\restriction B_{N}
.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

In personal communication, Anton Bernshteyn pointed out that Lemma
5.2
can be strengthened as follows. Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally finite Borel graph on a standard probability space
(X,\mu)
, and let
M
be a positive integer. Assume that, for each Borel set
A\subseteq X
such that
\mu(A)>0
, the minimum degree of the induced subgraph
\mathcal{G}\restriction A
is at most
M
. Then
\chi_{\mu}^{\text{ap}}(\mathcal{G})\leq M+1
.

The proof is similar: First, recursively construct sequences
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of subgraphs of
\mathcal{G}
and
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of subsets of
X
by setting
\mathcal{G}_{0}=\mathcal{G},A_{0}=\{x\in X:\deg_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}(x)\leq M\}
, and, having defined
\mathcal{G}_{n},A_{n}
, setting

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}\restriction(X\setminus(A_{0}\cup\cdots\cup A_{n})),

\displaystyle A_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\{x\in V(\mathcal{G}_{n+1}):\deg_{\mathcal{G}_{n+1}}(x)\leq M\}.

Write
A_{\infty}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A_{n}
and
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{G}\restriction(X\setminus A_{\infty})
. We claim that, for each
x\in X\setminus A_{\infty}
, we have
\deg_{\mathcal{H}}(x)>M
; otherwise, there is
x\in X\setminus A_{\infty}
with
\mathcal{H}
-degree at most
M
. Since
\mathcal{G}
is locally finite, we have
\deg_{\mathcal{G}}(x)=k
for some nonnegative integer
k
. Then since
\deg_{\mathcal{H}}(x)\leq M
, there is
\ell\geq k-M
such that
\ell
neighbors
y_{0},\dots,y_{\ell-1}
of
x
belong to
A_{\infty}
. For each
i<\ell
, there is
n_{i}
such that
y_{i}\in A_{n_{i}}
. But then if
N=\max\{n_{i}:i<\ell\}
, then
\deg_{\mathcal{G}_{N+1}}(x)\leq M
, so that
x\in A_{N+1}
, a contradiction.
So
\mu(A_{\infty})=1
; otherwise,
\mathcal{G}\restriction(X\setminus A_{\infty})
has minimum degree greater than
M
, which contradicts the initial assumption on
\mathcal{G}
. It follows that, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there is
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mu(A_{0}\cup\cdots\cup A_{N})>1-\varepsilon
, and then the backwards list-coloring argument from the proof of Lemma
5.2
can be repeated.

5.1.
Wilf’s theorem

A classical theorem of Wilf gives a simple upper bound on the chromatic number of a finite graph in terms of the largest eigenvalue of its adjacency matrix.

Theorem 5.4
.

[
54
]
Let
\mathcal{G}
be a finite graph. Then

\chi(\mathcal{G})\leq\lfloor M(T_{\mathcal{G}})\rfloor+1.

We show that this theorem carries over to the pmp setting when the chromatic number is replaced by the approximate measurable chromatic number; this establishes Theorem
B
from the introduction.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a bounded-degree Borel pmp graph on a standard probability space
(X,\mu)
. Then

\chi_{\mu}^{\text{ap}}(\mathcal{G})\leq\lfloor M(T_{\mathcal{G}})\rfloor+1.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
, and write
\lambda=\lfloor M(T_{\mathcal{G}})\rfloor
. Put

r=\frac{\lambda+s}{\lambda+1},

where
s\in(0,1)
is such that
\lambda+s>M(T_{\mathcal{G}})
. Let
\mathcal{G}_{0}=\mathcal{G}
,
A_{0}=\{x\in X:\deg_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}(x)\leq\lambda\}
, and
A_{-1}=B_{-1}=\emptyset
.
We recursively define
B_{n}=\bigcup_{k\leq n}A_{k}
,
\mathcal{G}_{n+1}=\mathcal{G}\restriction(X\setminus B_{n})
, and
A_{n+1}=\{x\in X\setminus B_{n}:\deg_{\mathcal{G}_{n+1}}(x)\leq\lambda\}
.
We claim that the following conditions hold:

(1)

A_{n}\cap A_{m}=\emptyset
for all
m\neq n
,

(2)

\mu(X\setminus B_{n})\leq r\mu(X\setminus B_{n-1})
, and

(3)

\deg_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}(x)\leq\lambda
for all
x\in A_{n}
.

Conditions (1) and (3) are immediate from the construction, so we only need to prove (2).

Claim 5.6
.

\mu(X\setminus B_{n+1})\leq r\mu(X\setminus B_{n})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof of Claim
5.6
.

Suppose not. Then there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mu(X\setminus B_{n+1})>r\mu(X\setminus B_{n})
. So

\displaystyle\deg_{\text{av}}(\mathcal{G}_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu(X\setminus B_{n})}\int_{X\setminus B_{n}}\deg_{\mathcal{G}_{n+1}}(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\mu(X\setminus B_{n})}\int_{X\setminus B_{n+1}}\deg_{\mathcal{G}_{n+1}}(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\mu(X\setminus B_{n})}\mu(X\setminus B_{n+1})(\lambda+1)

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{\mu(X\setminus B_{n})}\Big(\mu(X\setminus B_{n})\frac{\lambda+s}{\lambda+1}\Big)(\lambda+1)

\displaystyle=\lambda+s>M(T_{\mathcal{G}}),

contradicting the facts from Lemma
3.8
and Corollary
3.10
that
\deg_{\text{av}}(\mathcal{G}_{n+1})\leq M(T_{\mathcal{G}_{n+1}})\leq M(T_{\mathcal{G}})
. This proves the claim.
∎

From the properties (1)–(3), we deduce by Lemma
5.2
that
\chi_{\mu}^{\text{ap}}(\mathcal{G})\leq\lambda+1=\lfloor M(T_{\mathcal{G}})\rfloor+1
, as desired.
∎

We note that by Corollary
3.5
this bound can improve on the bound from Brooks’s theorem quadratically (although that particular case of biregular graphs is not really of any interest as these are trivially 2-colorable). We also point out that Lemma
3.8
(2) implies that quadratic improvement is the best possible.

5.2.
Graphs generated by functions

Let
X
be a set, and let
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}:X\to X
be functions. The
graph generated by
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
, denoted
\mathcal{G}_{f_{1},\dots,f_{n}}
, is the graph on
X
such that
x,y\in X
are adjacent if
x\neq y
and there is some
i\leq n
such that either
f_{i}(x)=y
or
f_{i}(y)=x
. A well-known classical greedy algorithm argument demonstrates that
\chi(\mathcal{G}_{f_{1},\dots,f_{n}})\leq 2n+1
; this is best possible (see Remark
5.10
).

The
directed graph generated by
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
, denoted
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{G}}_{f_{1},\dots,f_{n}}
, is the directed graph on
X
such that there is a directed edge from
x
to
y
if
x\neq y
and
f_{i}(x)=y
for some
i\leq n
. For each
x\in X
, the
out-degree
of
x
, denoted
\deg^{+}(x)
, is the number of out-edges incident on
x
, and the
in-degree
of
x
, denoted
\deg^{-}(x)
, is the number of in-edges incident on
x
.

Next, using properties of the in-degree and out-degree in the directed graph
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{G}}_{f_{1},\dots,f_{n}}
, we prove that
2n+1
is an upper bound on the approximate measurable chromatics number for the underlying undirected graph
\mathcal{G}_{f_{1},\dots,f_{n}}
in the case when it is pmp, thereby establishing Theorem
C
from the introduction. The second part of the proof is similar to the proof of Theorem
5.5
, but we provide the details for completeness.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
(X,\mu)
be a standard probability space, and let
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}:X\to X
be bounded-to-one Borel functions such that the graph
\mathcal{G}_{f_{1},\dots,f_{n}}
is pmp. Then
\chi_{\mu}^{\text{ap}}(\mathcal{G}_{f_{1},\dots,f_{n}})\leq 2n+1
.

Proof.

For convenience, we write
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{f_{1},\dots,f_{n}}
and
\mathcal{D}=\overrightarrow{\mathcal{G}}_{f_{1},\dots,f_{n}}
. Define a Borel map
\varphi:E_{\mathcal{G}}\to[0,\infty)
by
\varphi(x,y)=1
if
x\neq y
and there is
i\leq n
such that
f_{i}(x)=y
and
\varphi(x,y)=0
otherwise. For each
x\in X
, we have
\mathrm{out}\,\varphi(x)=\deg^{+}(x)
and
\mathrm{in}\,\varphi(x)=\deg^{-}(x)
. Therefore, by Proposition
2.10
,

\deg^{+}_{\text{av}}(\mathcal{D})=\int_{X}\deg^{+}(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)=\int_{X}\deg^{-}(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)=\deg_{\text{av}}^{-}(\mathcal{D}).

Note that
\deg^{+}(x)\leq n
for all
x\in X
, so that
\deg_{\text{av}}^{+}(\mathcal{D})\leq n
. Therefore also
\deg_{\text{av}}^{-}(\mathcal{D})\leq n
. Note further that, if
A\subseteq X
is Borel, then
\deg_{\text{av}}^{+}(\mathcal{D}\restriction A)=\deg_{\text{av}}^{-}(\mathcal{D}\restriction A)
, as witnessed by the Borel transport
\varphi_{A}:E_{\mathcal{G}\restriction A}\to[0,\infty)
given by
\varphi_{A}(x,y)=1
if
x,y\in A
,
x\neq y
, and there is
i\leq n
such that
f_{i}(x)=y
and
\varphi_{A}(x,y)=0
otherwise; furthermore,
\deg_{\text{av}}^{+}(\mathcal{D}\restriction A)\leq n
, and so
\deg_{\text{av}}^{-}(\mathcal{D}\restriction A)\leq n
.

Let now
\mathcal{G}_{0}=\mathcal{G}
,
\mathcal{D}_{0}=\mathcal{D}
,
A_{0}=\{x\in X:\deg_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}^{-}(x)\leq n\}
, and
A_{-1}=B_{-1}=\emptyset
.
We recursively define
B_{m}=\bigcup_{k\leq m}A_{k}
,
\mathcal{G}_{m+1}=\mathcal{G}\restriction(X\setminus B_{m})
,
\mathcal{D}_{m+1}=\mathcal{D}\restriction(X\setminus B_{m})
, and
A_{m+1}=\{x\in X\setminus B_{m}:\deg_{\mathcal{D}_{m+1}}^{-}(x)\leq n\}
. We claim that the following conditions hold:

(1)

A_{m}\cap A_{m^{\prime}}=\emptyset
for all
m^{\prime}\neq m
,

(2)

\mu(X\setminus B_{m})\leq\frac{n}{n+1}\mu(X\setminus B_{m-1})
, and

(3)

\deg_{\mathcal{D}_{m}}^{-}(x)\leq n
for all
x\in A_{m}
.

Conditions (1) and (3) are immediate from the construction, so we only need to prove (2).

Claim 5.8
.

\mu(X\setminus B_{m+1})\leq\frac{n}{n+1}\mu(X\setminus B_{m})
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof of Claim
5.8
.

If not, then there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mu(X\setminus B_{m+1})>\frac{n}{n+1}\mu(X\setminus B_{m})
. So

\displaystyle\deg_{\text{av}}^{-}(\mathcal{D}_{m+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu(X\setminus B_{m})}\int_{X\setminus B_{m}}\deg_{\mathcal{D}_{m+1}}^{-}(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\mu(X\setminus B_{m})}\int_{X\setminus B_{m+1}}\deg_{\mathcal{D}_{m+1}}^{-}(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\mu(X\setminus B_{m})}\mu(X\setminus B_{m+1})(n+1)

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{\mu(X\setminus B_{m})}\Big(\mu(X\setminus B_{m})\frac{n}{n+1}\Big)(n+1)

\displaystyle=n,

contradicting that
\deg_{\text{av}}^{-}(\mathcal{D}_{m+1})\leq n
. This proves the claim.
∎

Finally, from (3) above, it follows that for all
x\in A_{m}
,
\deg_{\mathcal{G}_{m}}(x)=\deg_{\mathcal{D}_{m}}^{+}(x)+\deg_{\mathcal{D}_{m}}^{-}(x)\leq 2n
. Combining this with (1) and (2), we thus deduce from Lemma
5.2
that
\chi_{\mu}^{\text{ap}}(\mathcal{G})\leq 2n+1
, as desired.
∎

Remark 5.9
.

The statement of Theorem
5.7
is in fact equivalent to the following statement: Let
(X,\mu)
be a standard probability space, and let
\mathcal{D}
be a bounded-degree Borel directed graph on
X
such that the underlying undirected graph
\mathcal{G}
of
\mathcal{D}
is pmp. If
\deg_{\mathcal{D}}^{+}(x)\leq n
for each
x\in X
, then
\chi_{\mu}^{\text{ap}}(\mathcal{G})\leq 2n+1
. This follows immediately from the observation that any such
\mathcal{D}
is generated by
n

[
29
, Section 5.3]
.

Remark 5.10
.

It is well-known that the bound in Theorem
5.7
is best possible. For instance, consider the
Paley tournament

\mathcal{G}
on
\{0,\dots,6\}
generated by the functions

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=x+1\mod{7},

\displaystyle f_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=x+2\mod{7},

\displaystyle f_{3}(x)

\displaystyle=x+4\mod{7}.

Then it is easy to check that

.

6.
Lower bounds on the chromatic number

In this section, we give several new spectral lower bounds on the approximate measurable chromatic number of a bounded-degree Borel pmp graph. These bounds are connected to the following well-known classical theorem of Hoffman:

Theorem 6.1
.

[
27
]
Let
\mathcal{G}
be a finite graph. Then

\chi(\mathcal{G})\geq\lceil 1-\frac{M(T_{\mathcal{G}})}{m(T_{\mathcal{G}})}\rceil.

Many of the proofs in this section closely resemble the corresponding classical proofs; see
[
46
, Chapter 19]
. However, especially the proof of the main result, Theorem
6.8
, involves new ideas due to its approximate nature.

Recall that the
\mu
-independence number

i_{\mu}(\mathcal{G})
of
\mathcal{G}
is given by

i_{\mu}(\mathcal{G})=\sup\{\mu(A):A\text{ is a $\mu$-measurable, $\mathcal{G}$-independent set}\}.

Since monochromatic sets in a coloring have to be independent, it is easy to see that, for any
\alpha\in(0,1]
, if
i_{\mu}(\mathcal{G})\leq\alpha
, then
\chi_{\mu}^{\text{ap}}(\mathcal{G})\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}
(see also
[
18
]
).

We start with regular graphs, since the proofs in this case are considerably simpler. Our first main result is the following (compare with
[
18
, Proposition 4.16]
):

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a
d
-regular Borel pmp graph on a standard probability space
(X,\mu)
. Then for any
\mu
-measurable,
\mathcal{G}
-independent set
S\subseteq X
,

\mu(S)\leq\frac{-m(T_{\mathcal{G}})}{d-m(T_{\mathcal{G}})}=1-\frac{d}{M(L_{\mathcal{G}})}.

In particular,
i_{\mu}(\mathcal{G})\leq\frac{-m(T_{\mathcal{G}})}{d-m(T_{\mathcal{G}})}
.

Proof.

We write
L=L_{\mathcal{G}},T=T_{\mathcal{G}}
for convenience. Let
S\subseteq X
be
\mu
-measurable and
\mathcal{G}
-independent,
and let
1_{S}
be the characteristic function for
S
; note that
1_{S}
is
\mu
-measurable. Set

f=1_{S}-\mu(S)\cdot 1.

Notice that
L1=0
. Then since
L
is self-adjoint,

\displaystyle\langle Lf,f\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle L1_{S},1_{S}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\bigg(\deg(x)\cdot 1_{S}(x)-1_{S}(x)\cdot\sum_{y\sim x}1_{S}(y)\bigg)\cdot 1_{S}(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{S}(\deg(x)-\deg_{\mathcal{G}\restriction S}(x))\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{S}\deg(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)

\displaystyle=d\mu(S)

since
S
is
\mathcal{G}
-independent. A simple calculation shows also that

\langle f,f\rangle=\mu(S)(1-\mu(S)).

Hence,

M(L)\geq\frac{\langle Lf,f\rangle}{\langle f,f\rangle}=\frac{d\mu(S)}{\mu(S)(1-\mu(S))}=\frac{d}{1-\mu(S)}.

We conclude that

\mu(S)\leq 1-\frac{d}{M(L)}=\frac{M(L)-d}{M(L)}=\frac{-m(T)}{d-m(T)}.

∎

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a
d
-regular Borel pmp graph on a standard probability space
(X,\mu)
. Then

\chi_{\mu}^{\text{ap}}(\mathcal{G})\geq\lceil 1-\frac{d}{m(T)}\rceil.

Before showing that Corollary
6.3
in fact remains true for non-regular graphs (even though Theorem
6.2
in general does not), we note that for non-regular graphs, the exact same proof as for Theorem
6.2
gives the following slightly weaker statement:

Corollary 6.4
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a bounded-degree Borel pmp graph on a standard probability space
(X,\mu)
. Then for any
\mu
-measurable,
\mathcal{G}
-independent set
S\subseteq X
,

\mu(S)\leq 1-\frac{(1/\mu(S))\int_{S}\deg(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)}{M(L)}.

In particular, if
\delta
is the minimum degree of
\mathcal{G}
, then since
(1/\mu(S))\int_{S}\deg(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)\geq\delta
for any set
S\subseteq X
, we deduce the following:

Corollary 6.5
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a bounded-degree Borel pmp graph on a standard probability space
(X,\mu)
with minimum degree
\delta
. Then

i_{\mu}(\mathcal{G})\leq 1-\frac{\delta}{M(L)},

and thus also

\chi_{\mu}^{\text{ap}}(\mathcal{G})\geq\frac{M(L)}{M(L)-\delta}.

We next turn to the main result of this section, Theorem
6.8
below, which shows that Corollary
6.3
remains true in the general context of bounded-degree (as opposed to merely regular) graphs. We start with the following lemma:

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
T
be a bounded and self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(X)
. Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
(A_{i})_{1\leq i\leq k}
be a
\mu
-measurable partition of
X
. Then

\displaystyle(k-1)m(T)+M(T)\leq\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}M(T_{ii}),

where
(T_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{k}
is the block decomposition of
T
corresponding to the partition
X=\sqcup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction. If
k=1
, then the result is clear. We separate the
k=2
case into a distinct claim.

Claim 6.7
.

Suppose
X=A_{1}\sqcup A_{2}
is a
\mu
-measurable partition of
X
. Then
m(T)+M(T)\leq M(T_{11})+M(T_{22})
.

Proof of Claim
6.7
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Then there is a unit vector
f\in L^{2}(X)
such that
M(T)-\varepsilon<\langle Tf,f\rangle
. Since
L^{2}(X)=L^{2}(A_{1})\oplus L^{2}(A_{2})
, there are
f_{1}\in L^{2}(A_{1}),f_{2}\in L^{2}(A_{2})
such that
f=f_{1}+f_{2}
.

Suppose first that
N_{1}=||f_{1}||
and
N_{2}=||f_{2}||
are both nonzero. Set
g=\frac{N_{2}}{N_{1}}f_{1}-\frac{N_{1}}{N_{2}}f_{2}
. Then

\displaystyle\langle g,g\rangle

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\frac{N_{2}}{N_{1}}f_{1}-\frac{N_{1}}{N_{2}}f_{2},\frac{N_{2}}{N_{1}}f_{1}-\frac{N_{1}}{N_{2}}f_{2}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{N_{2}}{N_{1}}\Big)^{2}\langle f_{1},f_{1}\rangle-\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle-\langle f_{2},f_{1}\rangle+\Big(\frac{N_{1}}{N_{2}}\Big)^{2}\langle f_{2},f_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=N_{2}^{2}+N_{1}^{2}=||f||^{2}=1

since
f
is a unit vector. Therefore,

\displaystyle M(T)+m(T)

\displaystyle\leq(\langle Tf,f\rangle+\varepsilon)+\langle Tg,g\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle T_{11}f_{1},f_{1}\rangle+\langle T_{12}f_{2},f_{1}\rangle+\langle T_{21}f_{1},f_{2}\rangle+\langle T_{22}f_{2},f_{2}\rangle+

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\Big(\frac{N_{2}}{N_{1}}\Big)^{2}\langle T_{11}f_{1},f_{1}\rangle-\langle T_{12}f_{2},f_{1}\rangle-\langle T_{21}f_{1},f_{2}\rangle+\Big(\frac{N_{1}}{N_{2}}\Big)^{2}\langle T_{22}f_{2},f_{2}\rangle+\varepsilon

\displaystyle=(1+\Big(\frac{N_{2}}{N_{1}}\Big)^{2})\langle T_{11}f_{1},f_{1}\rangle+(1+\Big(\frac{N_{1}}{N_{2}}\Big)^{2})\langle T_{22}f_{2},f_{2}\rangle+\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle T_{11}f_{1},f_{1}\rangle}{\langle f_{1},f_{1}\rangle}+\frac{\langle T_{22}f_{2},f_{2}\rangle}{\langle f_{2},f_{2}\rangle}+\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq M(T_{11})+M(T_{22})+\varepsilon.

Since this inequality holds for all
\varepsilon>0
, we indeed have
M(T)+m(T)\leq M(T_{11})+M(T_{22})
in this first case.

Next, assume that
||f_{2}||=0
. (If
||f_{1}||=0
instead, then a similar argument applies.) Then
\langle Tf,f\rangle=\langle Tf_{1},f_{1}\rangle=\langle T_{11}f_{1},f_{1}\rangle
. So,

M(T)-\varepsilon\leq\langle Tf,f\rangle=\langle T_{11}f_{1},f_{1}\rangle\leq M(T_{11}),

since
||f_{1}||=||f||=1
. Then since also

m(T)\leq m(T_{22})\leq M(T_{22})

by Proposition
2.7
, we again have

M(T)+m(T)\leq M(T_{11})+\varepsilon+M(T_{22})

for all
\varepsilon>0
. This concludes the proof of the claim.
∎

Now let
k\geq 2
, and assume the inequality in the statement of the lemma holds for
k-1
. Let
T
be as in the statement of the lemma. Let moreover
S
be the operator appearing in the
(1,1)
-entry in the block decomposition of
T
over the measurable partition of
X
into the two sets
A_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup A_{k-1}
and
A_{k}
, and note that
T_{kk}
is the
(2,2)
-entry in this decomposition. By Claim
6.7
,

M(T)+m(T)\leq M(S)+M(T_{kk}).

By the inductive hypothesis,

(k-2)m(S)+M(S)\leq\sum_{1\leq i\leq k-1}M(T_{ii}).

So,

\displaystyle M(T)+(k-1)m(T)

\displaystyle\leq M(T)+m(T)+(k-2)m(S)

\displaystyle\leq M(S)+M(T_{kk})+(k-2)m(S)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}M(T_{ii}),

where the first inequality follows from Proposition
2.7
. This finishes the proof of the Lemma.
∎

We now turn to the main theorem of this section (Theorem
D
from the introduction):

Theorem 6.8
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a bounded-degree Borel pmp graph on a standard probability space
(X,\mu)
. Then
\chi_{\mu}^{\text{ap}}(\mathcal{G})\geq\lceil 1-\frac{M(T_{\mathcal{G}})}{m(T_{\mathcal{G}})}\rceil
.

Proof.

Let
T=T_{\mathcal{G}}
, and let
k=\chi_{\mu}^{\text{ap}}(\mathcal{G})
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
, there are
\mu
-measurable, pairwise disjoint,
\mathcal{G}
-independent sets
A^{\varepsilon}_{1},\dots,A^{\varepsilon}_{k}\subseteq X
such that
\mu(\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq k}A^{\varepsilon}_{i})>1-\varepsilon
. Let
B^{\varepsilon}=X\setminus(\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq k}A^{\varepsilon}_{i})
. Then
T
admits a block decomposition
(T^{\varepsilon}_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq k+1}
corresponding to this partition; we also write
T^{\varepsilon}_{B}
for the
(k+1,k+1)
-entry, rather than
T^{\varepsilon}_{k+1,k+1}
. Additionally, we consider the block decomposition of
T
corresponding to the partition of
X
into the two sets
A^{\varepsilon}_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup A^{\varepsilon}_{k}
and
B^{\varepsilon}
; we write
S^{\varepsilon}
for the
(1,1)
-entry of this decomposition,
R^{\varepsilon}_{12}
for the
(1,2)
-entry, and
R^{\varepsilon}_{21}
for the
(2,1)
-entry; note that the
(2,2)
-entry is
T^{\varepsilon}_{B}
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\varepsilon_{n}=\frac{1}{n}
. We claim that
\lim_{n\to\infty}M(S^{\varepsilon_{n}})=M(T)
. To see this, fix
\varepsilon>0
, and let

\varepsilon_{0}=\frac{\varepsilon}{3||T||+1}.

Then there is a unit vector
f\in L^{2}(X)
such that
\langle Tf,f\rangle>M(T)-\varepsilon_{0}
. By Lemma
2.8
, there is
\delta>0
such that, for any measurable
B\subseteq X
,
\mu(B)<\delta
implies
\int_{B}|f(x)|^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)<\varepsilon_{0}
. Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\frac{1}{N}<\delta
. Let
n\geq N
, and let
f_{1}\in L^{2}(A^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup A^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{k})
,
f_{2}\in L^{2}(B^{\varepsilon_{n}})
be such that
f=f_{1}+f_{2}
. Then by combining this with Proposition
2.7
(2), we get

\displaystyle|\langle Tf,f\rangle-\langle S^{\varepsilon_{n}}f_{1},f_{1}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq|\langle R^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{12}f_{2},f_{1}\rangle|+|\langle R^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{21}f_{1},f_{2}\rangle|+|\langle T^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{B}f_{2},f_{2}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq||R^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{12}||||f_{2}||||f_{1}||+||R^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{21}||||f_{1}||||f_{2}||+||T^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{B}||||f_{2}||^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3||T||||f_{2}||\leq 3||T||\varepsilon_{0}.

So,
M(S^{\varepsilon_{n}})\geq\langle Tf,f\rangle-3||T||\varepsilon_{0}\geq M(T)-(3||T||+1)\varepsilon_{0}=M(T)-\varepsilon
. Since also
M(S^{\varepsilon_{n}})\leq M(T)
by Proposition
2.7
(1), this shows that, indeed,
\lim_{n\to\infty}M(S^{\varepsilon_{n}})=M(T)
.

Finally, we claim

(k-1)m(T)+M(T)\leq 0.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
, and let
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
be such that
M(T)-M(S^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})<\varepsilon
. By Lemma
6.6
,

(k-1)m(S^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})+M(S^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})\leq\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}M(T^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}_{ii})=0

since
A_{i}
is
\mathcal{G}
-independent for each
i\leq k
. So

(k-1)m(T)+M(T)\leq(k-1)m(S^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})+(M(S^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})+\varepsilon)=\varepsilon.

Therefore, using that
m(T)<0
(see Lemma
3.9
),

\chi_{\mu}^{\text{ap}}(\mathcal{G})=k\geq 1-\frac{M(T)}{m(T)}.

This finishes the proof of the theorem.
∎

7.
Matchings

In this section, we prove that any bounded-degree Borel pmp graph whose spectral values meet a certain condition satisfies a property that we call the
strict measurable Tutte condition
. Our main result, Theorem
7.8
, is inspired by the following classical theorem of Brouwer and Haemers:

Theorem 7.1
.

[
12
, Theorem 2.3]
Let
\mathcal{G}
be a finite graph, and let
0=\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}
be the eigenvalues of the Laplacian
L_{\mathcal{G}}
. If
n
is even and
2\lambda_{2}\geq\lambda_{n}
, then
\mathcal{G}
admits a perfect matching.

For the remainder of this section, we fix an ergodic
d
-regular Borel pmp graph
\mathcal{G}
with spectral gap. We denote by
L=L_{\mathcal{G}}
the Laplacian operator, and define

m_{L}=\inf_{0\neq f\in L^{2}_{0}(X)}\frac{\langle Lf,f\rangle}{\langle f,f\rangle}\quad\text{and}\quad M_{L}=\sup_{0\neq f\in L^{2}_{0}(X)}\frac{\langle Lf,f\rangle}{\langle f,f\rangle}.

From the basic results in Sections
2.2
and
3.4
, we note that
0<m_{L}\leq M_{L}=M(L)\leq 2d
,
\sigma(L)\subseteq\{0\}\cup[m_{L},M_{L}]
, and the eigenspace of
0
is equal to
\mathbb{C}1
. Note that
m_{L}>0
is equivalent to
\mathcal{G}
having spectral gap; in particular, the condition
2m_{L}\geq M_{L}
, appearing in Theorem
7.8
below, automatically implies spectral gap.

We start with proving the following spectral inequality for subsets of
X
, which is the direct generalization to Borel pmp graphs of
[
24
, Lemma 6.1]
:

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
Y
and
Z
be disjoint subsets of
X
of positive measure such that there are no edges between
Y
and
Z
. Then

\frac{\mu(Y)\mu(Z)}{(1-\mu(Y))(1-\mu(Z))}\leq\left(\frac{M_{L}-m_{L}}{M_{L}+m_{L}}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

We write
M=M_{L}
and
m=m_{L}
for convenience, and we define
a=\frac{M+m}{2}
. Consider the operator

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&L-aI\\
L-aI&0\end{pmatrix}

on
L^{2}(X)\oplus L^{2}(X)
. From Lemma
2.6
, we get

\sigma(A)=\sigma(L-aI)\cup-\sigma(L-aI)\subseteq\{-a\}\cup\left[-\frac{M-m}{2},\frac{M-m}{2}\right]\cup\{a\}.

Similar to Proposition
2.3
and Corollary
3.26
, we see that the eigenspaces of
-a
and
a
equal
\mathbb{C}(1\oplus 1)
and
\mathbb{C}(-1\oplus 1)
, respectively, and

\sigma\left(A|_{L^{2}(X)\ominus(\mathbb{C}(1\oplus 1)\oplus\mathbb{C}(-1\oplus 1))}\right)\subseteq\left[-\frac{M-m}{2},\frac{M-m}{2}\right].

Consider the decomposition

(7.3)

L^{2}(X)\oplus L^{2}(X)=L^{2}(Z)\oplus L^{2}(X\setminus Z)\oplus L^{2}(X\setminus Y)\oplus L^{2}(Y).

Since there are no edges between
Y
and
Z
, the corresponding block decomposition of
A
has the form

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\ast&0\\
0&0&\ast&\ast\\
\ast&\ast&0&0\\
0&\ast&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

For notational convenience, we also write
X_{1}=Z,X_{2}=X\setminus Z,X_{3}=X\setminus Y,X_{4}=Y
, so that the decomposition (
7.3
) becomes
L^{2}(X)\oplus L^{2}(X)=\oplus_{i=1}^{4}L^{2}(X_{i})
. Additionally, we write
v_{i}=1_{X_{i}}
, i.e., the vector that is the constant function
1
on
L^{2}(X_{i})
and
0
elsewhere. Using this notation, we then construct the
4\times 4
matrix
B
with entries

b_{ij}=\frac{1}{\mu(X_{i})}\langle v_{i},Av_{j}\rangle.

We compute

\displaystyle b_{2,4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu(X_{2})}\langle v_{2},Av_{4}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu(X\setminus Z)}\int_{X}1_{X\setminus Z}(x)(\deg(x)1_{Y}(x)-\sum_{y\sim x}1_{Y}(y)-a1_{Y}(x))\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\mu(Z)}\left(\int_{Y}\deg(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)-\int_{X\setminus Z}\sum_{y\sim x}1_{Y}(y)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)-a\mu(Y)\right)

\displaystyle=-a\frac{\mu(Y)}{1-\mu(Z)}.

In the last line, we use the fact that there are no edges between
Y
and
Z
, together with the fact that
\int_{Y}\deg(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)=\int_{X}\sum_{y\sim x}1_{Y}(y)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)
; this follows from line (
2.12
), with
A
replaced by
Y
. Since
b_{21}=b_{22}=0
and the second row of
B
has to sum to

\frac{1}{1-\mu(Z)}\langle 1_{X\setminus Z},(L-aI)1\rangle=-a,

we immediately get that
b_{23}=-a+a\frac{\mu(Y)}{1-\mu(Z)}
. Using similar computations, it is then easy to check that
B
is given by

B=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-a&0\\
0&0&-a+a\frac{\mu(Y)}{(1-\mu(Z))}&-a\frac{\mu(Y)}{(1-\mu(Z))}\\
-a\frac{\mu(Z)}{(1-\mu(Y))}&-a+a\frac{\mu(Z)}{(1-\mu(Y))}&0&0\\
0&-a&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

A linear algebra exercise then tells us that the eigenvalues of
B
are given by
-a<-\lambda<\lambda<a
for some
\lambda>0
(namely
\lambda=\frac{a\sqrt{\mu(Y)\mu(Z)}}{\sqrt{(1-\mu(Y))(1-\mu(Z))}}
).

Claim 7.4
.

\lambda\leq\frac{M-m}{2}
.

Proof of Claim
7.4
.

Denote by

K=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\mu(X_{1})}&0&0&0\\
0&\sqrt{\mu(X_{2})}&0&0\\
0&0&\sqrt{\mu(X_{3})}&0\\
0&0&0&\sqrt{\mu(X_{4})}\end{pmatrix}

and let
\tilde{B}=KBK^{-1}
. Then it is clear that
\tilde{B}
and
B
have the same eigenvalues. Moreover, denoting by

S:\mathbb{C}^{4}\to\oplus_{i=1}^{4}L^{2}(X_{i}):(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4})\mapsto\oplus_{i=1}^{4}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu(X_{i})}}t_{i}1_{X_{i}},

it is easy to check that the adjoint of
S
is given by

S^{*}(f)=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu(X_{1})}}\int_{X_{1}}f(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x),\ldots,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu(X_{4})}}\int_{X_{4}}f(x)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)\right),

that
S^{*}S=1
, i.e.,
S
is an isometry, and that
\tilde{B}=S^{*}AS
. Since one can easily check that the conditions from Lemma
2.5
are satisfied, we indeed conclude that
\lambda\leq\frac{M-m}{2}
.
∎

Finally, from the claim, it follows immediately that

a^{2}\frac{\mu(Y)\mu(Z)}{(1-\mu(Y))(1-\mu(Z))}=\frac{\det(B)}{a^{2}}=\lambda^{2}\leq\left(\frac{M-m}{2}\right)^{2}.

Since
a=\frac{M+m}{2}
, dividing both sides by
a^{2}
gives the desired result.
∎

7.1.
Tutte’s condition

We now outline a condition in the pmp setting that is inspired by the classical Tutte’s condition for the existence of a perfect matching.

Let
A\subseteq X
be a measurable subset, and let

\mathcal{O}_{A}=\{x\in X\setminus A:x\text{ is contained in a finite odd component of $\mathcal{G}\restriction(X\setminus A)$}\}.

Note that

\mathcal{O}_{A}=\bigcup_{k\geq 0}\mathcal{O}_{A}^{(2k+1)},

where
\mathcal{O}_{A}^{(2k+1)}
denotes the points in
X\setminus A
contained in odd components of size
2k+1
. We note here that
\mathcal{O}_{A}^{(1)}
is
\mathcal{G}
-independent by construction.

Define a measure
\nu_{A,k}
on
\mathcal{O}_{A}^{(2k+1)}
by
\nu_{A,k}=\frac{1}{2k+1}\mu
, and let

\nu_{A}=\oplus\nu_{A,k},

i.e., for
Y\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{A}
,

\nu_{A}(Y)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\nu_{A,k}(Y\cap\mathcal{O}_{A}^{(2k+1)})=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{1}{2k+1}\mu(Y\cap\mathcal{O}_{A}^{(2k+1)}).

Intuitively,
\nu_{A}
“counts” the number of finite odd components that arise after
A
is removed from the vertex set. We now state our suggestion for a measurable (expanding) version of Tutte’s condition.

Definition 7.5
.

We say
\mathcal{G}
satisfies the
strict measurable Tutte condition
if there exists
c<1
such that for every measurable subset
A\subseteq X
, the set

\mathcal{O}_{A}\coloneqq\{x\in X\setminus A:x\text{ is contained in a finite odd component of $\mathcal{G}\restriction(X\setminus A)$}\}

satisfies
\nu_{A}(\mathcal{O}_{A})\leq c\mu(A)
.

We point out that, for finite graphs, Tutte’s condition is the same as the above with
c=1
.

In Theorem
7.8
below, we show that the spectral condition analogous to the one in the classical Brouwer-Haemers theorem implies the strict measurable Tutte condition. Before we embark on its proof, we first prove two useful lemmas.

Lemma 7.6
.

Assume
\mu
is nonatomic. Then for any measurable
A\subset X
and
0\leq t\leq\mu(\mathcal{O}_{A})
, there exists a subset
B\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{A}
with measure
\mu(B)=t
that is a union of components of
\mathcal{G}\restriction\mathcal{O}_{A}
.

Proof.

Consider the Borel graph
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{G}\restriction\mathcal{O}_{A}
. Since every component of
\mathcal{H}
is finite, we deduce that
E_{\mathcal{H}}
is a finite Borel equivalence relation; so in particular, there exists a Borel transversal
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{A}
meeting every component of
\mathcal{H}
exactly once.

If
t=\mu(\mathcal{O}_{A})
, we can obviously take
B=\mathcal{O}_{A}
to get the result, so assume
t<\mu(\mathcal{O}_{A})
. Since
\mathcal{O}_{A}
is equal to the disjoint union
\mathcal{O}_{A}=\bigcup_{k\geq 0}\mathcal{O}_{A}^{(2k+1)}
, there exists a unique
K\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\mu\big(\cup_{0\leq k\leq K-1}\mathcal{O}_{A}^{(2k+1)}\big)\leq t<\mu\big(\cup_{0\leq k\leq K}\mathcal{O}_{A}^{(2k+1)}\big).

Since
\mathcal{T}\cap\mathcal{O}_{A}^{(2K+1)}
is Borel and
\mu
is nonatomic, we can find a Borel subset
\mathcal{T}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{T}\cap\mathcal{O}_{A}^{(2K+1)}
of
\mu
-measure

\frac{1}{2K+1}\big(t-\mu\big(\cup_{0\leq k\leq K-1}\mathcal{O}_{A}^{(2k+1)}\big)\big).

Denoting by
T_{0}
the
E_{\mathcal{H}}
-saturation of
\mathcal{T}_{0}
, we find that
B=\cup_{0\leq k\leq K-1}\mathcal{O}_{A}^{(2k+1)}\cup T_{0}
satisfies the requirements, finishing the proof.
∎

Lemma 7.7
.

Assume
2m_{L}\geq M_{L}
and
\mu
is nonatomic. Then the measurable independence number of
\mathcal{G}
satisfies
i_{\mu}(\mathcal{G})<\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

We will show that under the assumptions of the lemma, we necessarily have
m(T_{\mathcal{G}})>-d
. Once we know this, the conclusion follows immediately from Theorem
6.2
.

Thus, we assume for contradiction that
m(T_{\mathcal{G}})=-d
. It follows that
M_{L}=M(L_{\mathcal{G}})=d-m(T_{\mathcal{G}})=2d
. Since
2m_{L}\geq M_{L}
, we conclude that
m_{L}\geq d
, and in particular,
\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})\subseteq[-d,0]\cup\{d\}
. From Corollary
4.11
, we then deduce that
\{-d,d\}\subseteq\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})\subseteq\{-d,0,d\}
. In particular, Proposition
2.3
implies that both
d
and
-d
are eigenvalues. Corollary
3.26
moreover tells us that the eigenspace of
d
is equal to
\mathbb{C}1
. Let
f
be an eigenfunction of
-d
, and assume without loss of generality that
f
is real-valued. Then the same argument as in the proof of Claim
4.10
implies that the absolute value of
f
is constant; furthermore, as in the proof of Theorem
4.9
, if we let
A=\{x\in X:f(x)<0\}
, then
A
and
B=X\setminus A
yield a measurable bipartition of
X
.

We claim that any other eigenfunction
g
of
-d
is a multiple of
f
, i.e., the eigenspace of
-d
is
1
-dimensional. Indeed, assume not, and let
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
be the parts of the measurable bipartition associated to
g
. By assumption, both
A_{0}=A^{\prime}\cap A
and
B_{0}=A^{\prime}\cap B
have positive measure, as do
A_{1}=B^{\prime}\cap A=A\setminus A_{0}
and
B_{1}=B^{\prime}\cap B=B\setminus B_{0}
. However, by construction, there are no edges between
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
(as they are subsets of
A^{\prime}
), and similarly there are no edges between
A_{0}
and
A_{1}
, between
B_{0}
and
B_{1}
, and between
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
. In particular,
A_{0}\cup B_{1}
and
A_{1}\cup B_{0}
have no edges between them, and thus are both invariant sets of positive measure, contradicting ergodicity.

We conclude that
L^{2}(X)=\mathbb{C}1\oplus\mathbb{C}f\oplus H
, where
f
is the eigenfunction of
-d
yielding the bipartition
X=A\cup B
, and
T_{\mathcal{G}}\restriction H=0
. Taking a subset
B_{0}\subseteq B
with
\mu(B_{0})=\alpha\mu(B)
, for some irrational
0<\alpha<1
, we can now consider the function

g=\mu(B)1_{B_{0}}-\mu(B_{0})1_{B}\in H.

Then for each
x\in A
, we have

(T_{\mathcal{G}}g)(x)=\sum_{y\sim x}g(y)=\sum_{y\sim x}\big(\mu(B)1_{B_{0}}(y)-\mu(B_{0})1_{B}(y)\big)=\sum_{y\sim x}\mu(B)1_{B_{0}}(y)-d\mu(B_{0}),

since all neighbors of
x\in A
lie in
B
and
\deg(x)=d
. As
T_{\mathcal{G}}\restriction H=0
, we conclude that for every
x\in A
,

\sum_{y\sim x}\mu(B)1_{B_{0}}(y)=d\mu(B_{0}),

and hence

\sum_{y\sim x}1_{B_{0}}(y)=d\alpha.

Since
\alpha
is irrational, this yields the desired contradiction.
∎

We are now ready for the main theorem of this section (Theorem
E
from the introduction).

Theorem 7.8
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an ergodic
d
-regular Borel pmp graph on a standard probability space
(X,\mu)
. If
2m_{L}\geq M_{L}
, then the strict measurable Tutte condition holds for
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

We again write
M=M_{L}
and
m=m_{L}
. If
\mu
is atomic, then ergodicity and the fact that
\mathcal{G}
is pmp imply that we can reduce to the case of a finite connected graph, and thus
[
12
, Theorem 2.3]
applies. Hence, we can assume that
\mu
is nonatomic.

Assume that
2m\geq M
and that the strict measurable Tutte condition fails, i.e., for all
c<1
, there exists a measurable
A\subseteq X
such that
\nu_{A}(\mathcal{O}_{A})>c\mu(A)
. We start with the following claim:

Claim 7.9
.

There exists some
\frac{1}{2}<c<1
that admits an associated subset
A\subseteq X
with
\nu_{A}(\mathcal{O}_{A})>c\mu(A)
satisfying
\mu(A)<\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof of Claim
7.9
.

Assume for contradiction that for every
c>\frac{1}{2}
, the associated set
A_{c}\subset X
with
\nu_{A_{c}}(\mathcal{O}_{A_{c}})>c\mu(A_{c})
satisfies
\mu(A_{c})\geq\frac{1}{2}
. Write
a_{c}=\mu(\mathcal{O}_{A_{c}}^{(1)})=\nu_{A_{c}}(\mathcal{O}_{A_{c}}^{(1)})
and
b_{c}=\nu_{A_{c}}(\mathcal{O}_{A_{c}}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{A_{c}}^{(1)})\leq\frac{1}{3}\mu(\mathcal{O}_{A_{c}}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{A_{c}}^{(1)})
. The assumptions imply that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&a_{c}+b_{c}=\nu_{A_{c}}(\mathcal{O}_{A_{c}})>c\mu(A_{c})\geq\frac{c}{2},\\
&a_{c}+3b_{c}\leq\mu(\mathcal{O}_{A_{c}})\leq 1-\mu(A_{c})\leq\frac{1}{2},\end{cases}

An easy computation then yields

a_{c}>\frac{3c}{4}-\frac{1}{4}.

Since
\mathcal{O}_{A_{c}}^{(1)}
is a
\mathcal{G}
-independent set, we thus have
i_{\mu}(\mathcal{G})>\frac{3c}{4}-\frac{1}{4}
. Therefore,

i_{\mu}(\mathcal{G})\geq\sup_{\frac{1}{2}<c<1}\Big(\frac{3c}{4}-\frac{1}{4}\Big)=\frac{1}{2}.

Since
\mu
is nonatomic and
2m\geq M
, Lemma
7.7
yields the desired contradiction.
∎

Using the claim, we now fix
\frac{1}{2}<c<1
and a corresponding subset
A
satisfying
\nu_{A}(\mathcal{O}_{A})>c\mu(A)
and
\mu(A)<\frac{1}{2}
. Write
s=\mu(A)
.

Case 1.

s\geq\frac{1}{1+2c}
. Then in particular
cs\geq\frac{1}{2}(1-s)
and thus
\mu(\mathcal{O}_{A})\geq\nu_{A}(\mathcal{O}_{A})>cs\geq\frac{1}{2}(1-s)
. In particular, we see that

\mu(X\setminus(A\cup\mathcal{O}_{A}))=\mu(X\setminus A)-\mu(\mathcal{O}_{A})<\frac{1}{2}(1-s).

Using Lemma
7.6
, we can thus define

Y=[X\setminus(A\cup\mathcal{O}_{A})]\cup Y_{0},

where
Y_{0}
is a union of components of
\mathcal{O}_{A}
and
\mu(Y_{0})=\frac{1}{2}(1-s)-\mu(X\setminus(A\cup\mathcal{O}_{A}))
, so that
\mu(Y)=\frac{1}{2}(1-s)
. Let
Z=\mathcal{O}_{A}\setminus Y_{0}
. Then
A\cup Y\cup Z=X
,
\mu(Z)=\mu(Y)=\frac{1}{2}(1-s)
, and by construction, there are no edges between
Y
and
Z
. Proposition
7.2
thus implies that

\left(\frac{M-m}{M+m}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{\mu(Y)\mu(Z)}{(1-\mu(Y))(1-\mu(Z))}=\frac{\mu(Y)\mu(Z)}{s+\mu(Y)\mu(Z)}=\frac{(1-s)^{2}}{(1+s)^{2}}.

Since
s<\frac{1}{2}
, we get

\frac{M-m}{M+m}\geq\frac{1-s}{1+s}>\frac{\frac{1}{2}}{\frac{3}{2}}=\frac{1}{3}.

Hence
2m<M
, contradicting the assumption from the theorem.

Case 2.

s<\frac{1}{1+2c}
. Since in this case
cs<\frac{1}{2}(1-s)
and
\mu(\mathcal{O}_{A})\geq\nu_{A}(\mathcal{O}_{A})>cs
, by using Lemma
7.6
to add components of
\mathcal{O}_{A}
to
X\setminus(A\cup\mathcal{O}_{A})
if necessary, we can find subsets
Y,Z\subseteq X
without edges between them such that
X=A\cup Y\cup Z
and
\min\{\mu(Y),\mu(Z)\}>cs
. In particular,
\mu(Y)\mu(Z)>cs(1-s-cs)
. Using that the function
x\mapsto\frac{x}{s+x}
is strictly increasing, Proposition
7.2
thus implies

\left(\frac{M-m}{M+m}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{\mu(Y)\mu(Z)}{s+\mu(Y)\mu(Z)}>\frac{cs(1-s-cs)}{s+cs(1-s-cs)}=\frac{c}{1+c}\left(1-\frac{s}{1-cs}\right).

Since the function
x\mapsto\frac{x}{1-cx}
is increasing as well, and
s<\frac{1}{1+2c}
, we deduce that

\left(\frac{M-m}{M+m}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{c}{1+c}\left(1-\frac{\frac{1}{1+2c}}{1-c\cdot\frac{1}{1+2c}}\right)=\left(\frac{c}{1+c}\right)^{2}.

We conclude that

\frac{M-m}{M+m}\geq\frac{c}{1+c}>\frac{1}{3},

where we use that
c>\frac{1}{2}
. Therefore, also in this second case, we get
2m<M
, contradicting the original assumption. This finishes the proof.
∎

Finally, similarly to the classical fact that Tutte’s condition implies Hall’s condition for bipartite graphs, we observe next that for bipartite (i.e., odd-cycle-free) Borel pmp graphs, the strict measurable Tutte condition implies strict expansion for independent sets. Here, using the terminology of
[
29
]
, we say that a Borel graph
\mathcal{G}
on a standard probability space
(X,\mu)
is
strictly expanding for independent sets
if there exists
c>1
such that, for each measurable
\mathcal{G}
-independent set
A\subseteq X
,
\mu(\mathcal{N}(A))\geq c\mu(A)
.

Lemma 7.10
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an ergodic
d
-regular Borel pmp graph on a standard probability space
(X,\mu)
and assume
\mathcal{G}
is odd-cycle-free. Then the strict measurable Tutte condition implies strict expansion for independent sets.

Proof.

We prove the contrapositive. Suppose
\mathcal{G}
is not strictly expanding for independent sets, and let
c<1
.

By assumption, there is a measurable
\mathcal{G}
-independent set
A\subseteq X
such that
\mu(\mathcal{N}(A))<(1/c)\mu(A)
. Then since
A
is
\mathcal{G}
-independent, each element of
A
is an isolated vertex in
\mathcal{G}\restriction(X\setminus\mathcal{N}(A))
. In particular, there is a subset of
X\setminus\mathcal{N}(A)
of measure at least
\mu(A)
that is a disjoint union of
\mathcal{G}\restriction(X\setminus\mathcal{N}(A))
-components of cardinality
1
. Therefore,

\nu_{\mathcal{N}(A)}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{N}(A)})\geq\mu(A)>c\mu(\mathcal{N}(A)).

Since
c<1
was arbitrary, this shows that the strict measurable Tutte condition is not satisfied. This finishes the proof.
∎

In particular, by combining Theorem
7.8
, Lemma
7.10
, and
[
38
, Remark 2.6]
, we deduce that the assumption
2m_{L}\geq M_{L}
implies the existence of a measurable perfect matching for
bipartite
ergodic regular Borel pmp graphs.

8.
Examples from local-global convergence

In general, it is difficult to compute the spectrum of the adjacency operator of a given bounded-degree Borel pmp graph
\mathcal{G}
. However, when
\mathcal{G}
arises as a suitable limit of finite graphs, it often becomes easier to compute (properties of) the spectrum of
\mathcal{G}
from those of the approximating finite graphs. A well-studied notion of convergence for which this is possible is
local-global convergence
. This notion was introduced by Bollobás and Riordan
[
8
]
under the name partition metric, while the identification of a bounded-degree Borel pmp graph as limit object was carried out later by Hatami, Lovász, and Szegedy
[
26
]
, who also obtained most currently known results. More precisely, the limit object of a sequence of finite graphs
\mathcal{G}_{n}
with uniformly bounded degree that converges in the local-global sense is a weak equivalence class of bounded-degree Borel pmp graphs (see
[
33
,
14
]
). While the limit is thus not determined up to isomorphism, all limits of the sequence
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
share various features, and in particular their adjacency operators have the same spectrum.

We may identify the space
G_{d}(X,\mu)
of Borel pmp graphs of degree bounded by
d
on a standard probability space
(X,\mu)
with
A(\mathbb{F}_{d},X,\mu)
, the standard Borel space of all Borel measure-preserving actions of the free group of order
d
on
(X,\mu)
(see for instance
[
33
]
). We can now consider the topology on
G_{d}(X,\mu)
induced by local-global convergence. One can show that this topology is induced by a pseudo-metric whose zero classes are weak equivalence classes, and that the metric quotient is compact
[
1
]
.

By combining
[
47
, Proposition 2.45]
and
[
21
, Proposition 3.4.2]
, one can then easily deduce the following theorem:

Theorem 8.1
.

The function
\delta:G_{d}(X,\mu)\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
given by
\delta(\mathcal{G},x)=\inf_{\lambda\in\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})}|x-\lambda|^{2}
is continuous.

Proof.

This is essentially
[
21
, Proposition 3.4.2]
, but we provide a short proof for completeness.

We claim that
\delta(\mathcal{G},x)
viewed as a formula is first-order definable (see for instance
[
21
]
). Once we prove this, the claim follows from
[
47
, Proposition 2.45]
.

For each
\mathcal{G}\in G_{d}(X,\mu),x\in\mathbb{R}
, let
S_{\mathcal{G},x}=(T_{\mathcal{G}}-xI)(T_{\mathcal{G}}-xI)^{*}
; we write
S=S_{\mathcal{G},x}
for convenience. Then
\sigma(S)=\{|\lambda|^{2}:\lambda\in\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}}-xI)\}
. We claim that
\delta(\mathcal{G},x)=||S||-||S-||S||\cdot I||
, and thus in particular it is first-order definable. To verify this, note that

||S-||S||\cdot I||=\sup_{\lambda\in\sigma(S-||S||\cdot I)}|\lambda|=||S||-\inf_{\lambda\in\sigma(S)}\lambda,

and that
\inf_{\lambda\in\sigma(S)}\lambda=\inf_{\lambda\in\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})}|x-\lambda|^{2}
. This completes the proof.
∎

A straightforward calculation then gives the following:

Corollary 8.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of graphs that local-global converges to
\mathcal{G}
. Then for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
x\in\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}})
if and only if there is a sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}=x
and
x_{n}\in\sigma(T_{\mathcal{G}_{n}})
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

This result can be used to construct a variety of bounded-degree Borel pmp graphs with prescribed spectral properties. For instance, an easy concrete example where we can compute the spectrum exactly is irrational rotation:

Example 8.3
.

Let
\theta
be an irrational number, and let
\mathcal{G}
be the graph on
[0,1)
obtained by placing an edge between vertices
x
and
y
if and only if
x=y\pm\theta\mod{1}
. Then
\mathcal{G}
arises as a local-global limit of the sequence
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n\geq 3}
, where for each
n\geq 3
,
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is the cyclic graph on
n
vertices (see for instance
[
26
, Section 12]
). It is easy to see that the spectrum of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is

\{2\cos\Big(\frac{2\pi i}{n}\Big):i\in\mathbb{N},0\leq i\leq n-1\},

the set of
x
-coordinates of the
n
th roots of unity. Therefore, by Corollary
8.2
, the spectrum of
\mathcal{G}
is the entire interval
[-2,2]
.

Another example concerns the persistence of spectral gap through local-global limits, connecting to the rich theory of
expander graphs
.

Example 8.4
.

Let
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of finite
d
-regular graphs such that
|V(\mathcal{G}_{n})|\to\infty
having a uniform lower bound on the spectral gap (in other words, a sequence of
d
-regular expander graphs). Upon passing to a subsequence if necessary, we may always assume that the sequence
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is local-global convergent. From Corollary
8.2
, we thus deduce that any limit graph
\mathcal{G}
of such a sequence has spectral gap, and its spectral gap is equal to the limit of the spectral gaps of the
\mathcal{G}_{n}
; see also
[
26
, Section 11]
.

As another related example, we note that the
cost
of the limit graph can also be computed given the costs of the approximating graphs; see
[
2
,
14
]
. Finally, we point out that the spectra of (
d
-regular)
random graphs
have been the subject of extensive study. While several questions concerning the local-global convergence of sequences of such graphs remain open, many (partial) results are known; see, for instance,
[
26
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
.
