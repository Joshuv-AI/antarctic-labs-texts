Robot-assisted vs. laparoscopic cholecystectomy in children: a meta-analysis with systematic review of comparative studies
Authors: Maria Klimeczek-Chrapusta, Kacper Stolarz, Bartosz Gęgała, Alicja Kot, Zofia Łubniewska, Maciej Preinl, Bartosz Bogusz, Maria Gruba, Anna Dąbrowska, Wojciech Górecki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis meta-analysis aimed to compare the safety and effectiveness of robot-assisted cholecystectomy (RAC) versus laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC). A comprehensive search of online databases was carried out to identify studies comparing laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) with robot-assisted cholecystectomy (RAC). Safety outcomes assessed included operative complications and conversion rates, while efficacy parameters comprised length of hospital stay, total operative time, postoperative analgesic use, and procedure cost. The meta-analysis was conducted using a random-effects model. A total of 157 articles were screened, 5 studies meeting the inclusion criteria, encompassing 548 children. Included studies reported instances of shorter LOS, reduced analgesic requirements, lower conversion and reintervention rates, and fewer postoperative complications in the RAC groups; however, none of these differences reached statistical significance. This meta-analysis found no meaningful differences between RAC and LC regarding safety and efficacy outcomes, suggesting that RAC is comparable to LC in the pediatric population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11701-025-03053-2.Keywords: Pediatric surgery, Robotic surgery, Cholecystectomy, Laparoscopy

Introduction

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) has been the standard treatment for children with symptomatic gallstones or biliary dyskinesia [1, 2]. Compared to traditional open surgery, it offers quicker recovery times and less common postoperative complications. In recent years minimally invasive surgery techniques have been revolutionized by rising access to robotic surgery. Robot-assisted cholecystectomy (RAC) seems to offer benefits in precision, better three-dimensional visualization and improved surgical control. Furthermore, in the studies included this following study, RAC was most commonly performed using a single-site procedure which compared to traditional multi-port laparoscopic techniques offers reduced post-surgical incision pain and more desirable cosmetic outcome [3]. While single-site procedures are also possible in LC, the multiport approach remains a gold standard in laparoscopic cholecystectomy [4, 5].

The objective of this meta-analysis was to assess the safety and efficacy of laparoscopic versus robotic cholecystectomy in pediatric patients, with particular emphasis on postoperative and intraoperative complications, conversion rates, duration of hospitalization, operative time and analgesic needs. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first published meta-analysis addressing this topic in children. Both surgical approaches have been shown to be effective; however, their respective advantages and limitations remain a subject of debate as researchers strive to identify which method offers superior outcomes [1–3]. By incorporating data from various studies, we hope to bring some insight into the aspects that may influence the choice of technique based on both clinical and financial factors.

Methods

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [6] criteria were closely followed by the authors of this meta-analysis. Additionally, the review was listed in the PROSPERO database under the registration number CRD42024584606. As a systematic review with meta-analysis, this study did not require ethical approval or participant informed consent to be conducted.

Search strategy

A thorough literature search was performed in October 2024 across three major electronic databases: MEDLINE/PubMed, EMBASE, and Web of Science. No limitations were placed on publication date or language. The search query applied in MEDLINE/PubMed was as follows:

("Surgery, Computer-Assisted"[MeSH] OR “robotics”[MeSH] OR “da Vinci” OR “telerobotic” OR “telesurgery” OR “robotic surgery”) AND (“Pediatrics”[MeSH] OR “Infant”[MeSH] OR “Child”[MeSH] OR “Adolescent”[MeSH]) AND (“Cholecystectomy”[MeSH] OR “Cholelithiasis” OR “gallbladder” OR “gallstones”[MeSH]).

Taking into account the specific syntax rules of each database, the search strategies for the remaining databases followed the same overall structure. Once the full-text articles were selected, their reference lists were manually examined to identify any additional relevant studies that could be eligible for inclusion.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The analysis encompassed pertinent research contrasting any aspects of traditional LC versus RAC in pediatric patients.

Eligible studies met the following criteria: (1) original clinical research directly comparing robot-assisted and conventional laparoscopic cholecystectomy in pediatric populations (< 18 years), and (2) reporting relevant outcomes such as operative duration, hospital stay length, morphine use, conversion to open surgery, and intraoperative or postoperative complications.

These were the criteria for exclusion: (1) presence of children and adults in the study population who cannot be distinguished from one another; (2) studies focusing solely on RAC or LC approach for cholecystectomy; (3) inappropriate study type, i.e., conference reports, case reports, reviews, and meta-analyses.

At least two authors independently conducted blind reviews of each paper deemed suitable for inclusion in this meta-analysis. If there were any disagreements, all authors had to discuss them thoroughly in order to reach a consensus.

Extraction strategy

Two authors independently extracted data from the included studies using a standardized electronic spreadsheet. For continuous variables, the analysis required the number of participants, mean values, and corresponding standard deviations (SD). In cases where SDs were not provided, they were approximated using established methods, such as SD = range / 4 or SD = interquartile range / 1.35.

Study quality appraisal

After the blind review, all papers that satisfied the inclusion criteria underwent a quality assessment. Randomized research was assessed using the Cochrane risk-of-bias instrument (RoB), whereas non-randomized studies were analyzed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Quality Assessment for Cohort Studies (NOS). The quality assessment procedure was carried out separately by two authors. No study was excluded from this meta-analysis due to its poor quality.

Outcomes of interest

Information extracted from each study included the research design (randomized controlled trial [RCT], cohort study [CS], or case–control study [CCS]), name of the first author, year of publication, and population details (sample size, country, age, and sex). Data on both the laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) and robot-assisted cholecystectomy (RAC) groups were also collected, encompassing patient demographics (number, age, sex, weight) and clinical parameters such as operative time (OT), hospital stay (LOS), morphine use, conversion rate to open surgery, and intra- or postoperative complication rates. Variables or complications reported in only a single study were excluded from the analysis.

Statistical methodology

The meta-analysis focused on the following outcomes: operative time, length of hospital stay, morphine consumption, conversion rate to open surgery, and rates of intraoperative and postoperative complications. All statistical analyses were conducted using PS IMAGE PRO 10.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics 29.0.2). For continuous variables, the standardized mean difference (SMD) served as the effect size, while dichotomous variables were analyzed using the log odds ratio (LogOR). Each outcome was presented with a 95% confidence interval (CI).Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using both the Chi2 and I2 statistics. Based on I2 values, heterogeneity was interpreted as follows: 0–40% likely insignificant, 30–60% moderate, 50–90% substantial, and 75–100% considerable. A random-effects model was applied throughout all analyses. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for comparisons of continuous and categorical data, and p < 0.1 for heterogeneity testing. The main results were visualized using forest plots.

Systematic literature review

A descriptive analysis was performed for all included studies. The evaluation covered multiple domains, such as study design and characteristics (including year of publication, country, sample size, study groups, duration, and follow-up period), type of robotic system utilized, investigated parameters, principal results, as well as reported complications and conversion rates.

Description of the population

A total of 548 patients (349 females, 111 males, and 88 participants without sex data) were included in the study. Conventional LC was performed in 376 patients, while 169 patients underwent RAC. The populations of the experimental (RAC) and control (LC) groups were homogeneous and comparable. The results showed no statistically significant difference in patient age between the two groups [Fig. 2] (SMD = -0.02; 95% CI: -0.27 to 0.23; p = 0.87). A statistically significant difference in patients’ weight was observed [Fig. 3] (SMD = 0.37; 95% CI: 0.10–0.64; p = 0.01); children who underwent robotic cholecystectomy had a significantly higher weight. Despite females constituting over a trifold of male participants, no significant difference in female [Fig. 4] (LogOR = -0.16; 95%CI: -0.65–0.33; p = 0.52) nor male [Fig. 5] (LogOR = 0.16; 95%CI: -0.33–0.65; p = 0.52) incidence was found between respective control and treatment groups.

Operating time, conversion to open surgery, complications, and reintervention rates

There was no statistically significant difference between the groups in the OT analysis [Fig. 6], which comprised 548 patients (SMD = 0.51; 95%CI: -0.27–1.28; p = 0.20). Furthermore, an analysis based on 329 patients revealed no noticeable difference in the conversion rate to open surgery (LogOR = -1.01; 95%CI: -3.17–1.14; p = 0.36) [Fig. 7]. Since there was only one study that documented intraoperative complications [3], it was not feasible to perform a meta-analysis on this outcome. Both groups experienced postoperative complications (LogOR = -0.13; 95%CI: -1.91–1.65; p = 0.89) [Fig. 8] and required reinterventions (LogOR = -0.63; 95%CI: -2.20–0.94; p = 0.43) [Fig. 9] at comparable rates.

Discussion

This meta-analysis found no statistically significant differences between robotic-assisted cholecystectomy (RAC) and laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) in any of the evaluated clinical outcomes, including operative time, conversion rate to open surgery, intra- and postoperative complications, hospital stay duration, and morphine consumption. These results indicate that robotic cholecystectomy offers comparable safety and efficacy to the traditional laparoscopic approach.

Although cholecystectomy is performed far less frequently in children than in adults, the growing prevalence of pediatric obesity has led to an increased occurrence of gallstone disease requiring surgical treatment. Additionally, the primary causes of cholelithiasis have shifted over time—from predominantly hemolytic disorders to diet-related factors [10].

Our meta-analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in operative time (OT) between robotic-assisted cholecystectomy (RAC) and laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC). Because several of the included studies [1, 3] did not differentiate between single- and multi-port approaches in their statistical analyses, the number of ports was not considered in our OT comparison. Nonetheless, most patients underwent single-site robotic procedures. Kulaylat et al. [3] analyzed both multi-port and single-site RAC and LC separately and together, finding that laparoscopic single-incision (LSI) procedures required significantly more time than robotic single-incision (RSI) ones. Conversely, Mattei et al. [7], who compared RSI and LSI exclusively, observed longer OT in robotic cases. Direct comparisons remain difficult, as LC was often performed by residents in training, whereas RAC tended to be done by more experienced surgeons. Additionally, single-incision techniques—often preferred for cosmetic reasons—may inherently extend operative time [3]. It should also be noted that robotic-assisted surgery is still relatively new in pediatric practice. Kudsi et al. [11] examined the RAC learning curve using cumulative sum analysis, identifying a marked improvement in operative efficiency after approximately 84 cases, representing a key threshold for proficiency.

We did not perform a formal cost analysis, as only one of the included studies provided cost-related data [3]. However, that study reported notably higher procedure costs for RAC compared with LC, suggesting a possible cost disadvantage of RAC that should be explored further in future research.

In the studies included in this meta-analysis, all reported single-site RAC procedures were performed using one incision of a size varying between 2 and 4 cm, peri- or transumbilically, allowing for the introduction of the main port (8.5 mm) and usually one or two assistant’s ports (5 mm). This opening size is considerably larger than the typical multiport LC incisions (generally 10 mm + 3 × 5 mm). Despite the larger incision, several studies in both adult and pediatric patients reported high patient satisfaction, with less postoperative pain and favorable cosmetic outcomes associated with having a single scar compared to three or four scars in standard multiport LC procedures [7, 8, 12, 13]. We believe it is worth mentioning that patient satisfaction, while inherently subjective, provides valuable insight even if it cannot be objectively measured.

Despite offering several advantages over conventional laparoscopy, robotic surgery has yet to gain widespread use in pediatric populations, primarily due to concerns about the smaller body size of children. However, gallstone disease in this group typically presents between 12.9 and 14.3 years of age, meaning that body size is less of a limiting factor for cholecystectomy [1–3, 7, 10, 11]. Although single-incision laparoscopic cholecystectomy (SILC) is considered both safe and feasible in children, it poses technical challenges, including limited working space, instrument crowding, reduced triangulation, longer operative times, and higher complication risks. Robotic-assisted techniques may help overcome these difficulties by providing superior visualization and improved instrument dexterity. The single-incision robotic cholecystectomy (SIRC) technique, for example, offers a 3D operative view and intuitive hand–instrument coordination, avoiding the counterintuitive movements required in SILC and allowing for greater precision in tight spaces [1, 3, 10]. Hey et al. [10], drawing on six years of experience with adolescent patients, reported that challenges related to the small operative field in pediatric robotic surgery can be mitigated through careful trocar placement and increased surgical experience.

Regarding the learning curve, a study by Sheetz et al. [14] on RAC in adults showed that while increased experience correlates with fewer intraoperative complications, most U.S. surgeons perform too few robotic cholecystectomies to reach the same level of proficiency achieved with traditional laparoscopy—an issue that may extend to other countries as well.

To our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis comparing the effectiveness and efficiency of RAC versus LC in pediatric patients. The most recent meta-analysis on this topic in adults, conducted by Delgado et al. [15] in 2024, included 13 studies with approximately 22,440 participants. Their findings indicated that RAC was associated with longer operative times but showed no significant differences in length of stay (LOS), intraoperative or postoperative complications, or bile duct injuries compared to LC. These results differ somewhat from ours, likely due to variability in how OT was defined and measured across studies [1–3, 7, 10, 11], particularly regarding inclusion of preparation time. Nonetheless, our findings on safety and perioperative outcomes in pediatric patients are consistent with those reported in adults. However, the latest cohort study by Mullens et al. [16] conducted on over 700 000 patients, reported a significantly higher risk of bile duct injury in patients undergoing robotic-assisted cholecystectomy compared with laparoscopic cholecystectomy, while overall postoperative complication, reoperations and readmissions were similar between the groups. Another meta-analysis by Sun et al. [17] comparing SIRC and multi-port laparoscopic (MLP) cholecystectomy in adults found higher rates of incisional hernia and greater overall costs in the SIRC group. None of the pediatric studies we reviewed reported incisional hernias, but similarly to findings in adult studies, one of the included studies found higher procedure costs in the RAC group. Overall, clinical outcomes were comparable and align with previous literature.

The main limitations of our meta-analysis stem from the designs of the included studies. Many were retrospective with small sample sizes, short follow-up durations, and reliance on historical controls. Another limitation is that one study included in our meta-analysis focused exclusively on acute patients (cholecystitis and/or choledocholithiasis) [8], and one study focused exclusively on elective cases [9], whereas the remaining studies either did not report whether and how many cases were acute or elective, or, even if they provided such data, analysed them together without differentiating into subgroups. It is possible that, had we been able to analyse acute and elective cases separately, the results might have differed. However, since most studies did not clearly distinguish between these groups, we analysed the cases together in accordance with the data provided by the original authors. A meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) would provide stronger evidence to evaluate this topic more definitively. Future research should prioritize RCTs, adopt standardized reporting methods with clearly defined outcome criteria, use the Clavien–Dindo classification for complications, and include extended follow-up beyond the immediate postoperative period.

Conclusions

Our analysis found no significant differences in available clinical outcome measures when comparing robotic-assisted surgery (RAS) to conventional laparoscopic minimally invasive surgery (MIS) for cholecystectomy in children. Based on the current literature, there is no conclusive evidence demonstrating clear clinical benefit of RAS over laparoscopic approach for cholecystectomy in pediatric patients. Similarly, no evidence-based contraindications have been documented. Therefore, the decision to use RAS should be based on the center’s capabilities, surgeon experience, and individual patient factors.
