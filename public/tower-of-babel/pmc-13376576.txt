Association between lactate-to-albumin ratio and 28-day mortality in critically ill patients with HFpEF: a retrospectively cohort study
Authors: Tuo Han, Jing Xiao, Ying Li, Bao-e Yan, Fang-yao Chen, Liang Bai, Jie Deng, Yan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Previous studies have established associations between elevated lactate-to-albumin ratio (LAR) and mortality in sepsis or critically ill populations. However, its prognostic significance specifically among heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) patients remains underexplored. This study aims to elucidate the relationship between LAR and 28-day all-cause mortality in critically ill patients with HFpEF. This retrospective cohort study derived 731 critically ill HFpEF patients from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV, v3.1) database. Demographic characteristics, comorbidities, laboratory parameters, clinical severity indices, and mortality outcomes were systematically extracted. Participants were subsequently stratified into LAR tertiles: T1 (< 0.363), T2 (0.363–0.593), and T3 (> 0.593). We employed Cox proportional hazards regression and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses to evaluate associations between LAR and 28-day mortality. Sensitivity analyses and comorbidity-stratified subgroup assessments verified result robustness and examined interaction effects. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis assessed LAR’s discriminative performance for 28-day mortality prediction. Within the final cohort of 731 HFpEF patients, 103 (14.1%) experienced 28-day mortality following ICU admission. A pronounced mortality gradient emerged across LAR tertiles (T1:10.3%; T2:10.7%; T3:21.3%; P < 0.001). Kaplan-Meier analysis confirmed significantly reduced survival probability with elevated LAR, persisting after adjustment for age, sex, race and BMI (log-rank P = 0.019). Unadjusted Cox regression demonstrated significant LAR-mortality associations. However, multivariable adjustment attenuated statistical significance for both continuous (HR 1.31, 95% CI 0.80–2.16; P = 0.290) and categorical comparisons (T3 vs. T1: HR 1.34, 95% CI 0.71–2.53; P = 0.373). RCS and sensitivity analyses corroborated these patterns. Significant interaction effects emerged between LAR and race, BMI, myocardial infarction, chronic pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, and hypertension (P < 0.05). ROC analysis indicated modest discriminatory capacity for 28-day mortality prediction (AUC 0.625, 95% CI 0.566–0.685). In this cohort of critically ill patients with HFpEF, it’s demonstrated a positive correlation between LAR and the risk of 28-day all-cause mortality. However, our study did not confirm LAR as an independent predictor of mortality in HFpEF after comprehensive adjustment. Instead, the findings position LAR as a composite biomarker reflecting global disease severity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44385-9.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) poses a significant global health challenge, manifested through escalating prevalence, substantial morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs. Among its phenotypes, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) now accounts for over 50% of all HF cases, with this disproportionate rise in HFpEF prevalence largely driven by aging populations and increasing comorbidities including obesity, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus1,2. Unlike heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), HFpEF is defined by a heterogeneous pathophysiology involving systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, microvascular dysfunction, and myocardial fibrosis, ultimately resulting in impaired ventricular relaxation and increased myocardial stiffness3,4. Critically, established therapies for HFrEF have consistently failed to improve major clinical outcomes in HFpEF patients5. This therapeutic impasse highlights the critical demand for deeper pathophysiological insights and novel prognostic markers to enable better risk stratification and identify potential therapeutic targets6. Compounding this challenge, acute decompensations in HFpEF carry a particularly poor prognosis, further emphasizing the paramount importance of identifying reliable early-warning biomarkers for adverse outcomes.

Emerging evidence solidifies the critical involvement of metabolic dysregulation and systemic inflammation in driving the progression and adverse outcomes of HFpEF3. While lactate was historically regarded primarily as a marker of tissue hypoperfusion and anaerobic metabolism, it is now understood to function as a significant signaling molecule7. Its elevation serves as an indicator of integrated stress responses—encompassing inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and adrenergic overdrive—pathways fundamentally implicated in HF pathophysiology8,9. Importantly, elevated lactate levels, even within conventionally defined ‘normal’ limits, are consistently associated with poorer prognosis in diverse cardiovascular settings, including both acute and chronic heart failure10. Parallel to this, hypoalbuminemia is a prevalent feature in HF, resulting from multifaceted etiologies such as malnutrition, chronic inflammation, cachexia, hepatic impairment, or capillary leakage11. Critically, reduced albumin levels constitute a robust, independent predictor of mortality in HF, a relationship plausibly explained by albumin’s essential contributions to antioxidant defense, endothelial integrity maintenance, and intravascular fluid regulation12,13. Given the frequent co-occurrence of lactate accumulation and albumin depletion in states of critical illness, and crucially, their reflection of convergent pathways involving metabolic stress and systemic inflammation, the lactate-to-albumin ratio (LAR) has gained traction as a composite biomarker offering potentially enhanced prognostic insight. Supporting this premise, initial investigations, largely within sepsis and critical care cohorts, demonstrate that LAR provides superior prognostic discrimination compared to either lactate or albumin alone14–16. Extending these findings to the cardiac domain, emerging evidence from studies on acute heart failure syndromes similarly points to a significant association between elevated LAR and heightened mortality risk15,17.

Although compelling pathophysiological rationale and promising preliminary data support LAR as a biomarker, substantial gaps persist in our understanding of its role specifically in HFpEF. A critical unresolved question is the prognostic validity of LAR within the distinctive pathophysiological landscape of HFpEF, which is typified by pervasive systemic inflammation and profound metabolic dysregulation. This gap is exacerbated by the limitations of existing studies on lactate or albumin in heart failure; they commonly aggregate HFrEF and HFpEF cohorts or omit dedicated analyses confined to HFpEF populations17–19. The composite nature of LAR, potentially capturing both the metabolic stress (via lactate) and the chronic inflammatory/nutritional frailty (via albumin) – dual processes intrinsically linked to HFpEF pathogenesis. Based on this mechanistic integration, we hypothesized that an elevated LAR at admission functions as a robust and pragmatic indicator of mortality risk in hospitalized patients with HFpEF. To address the identified knowledge gap, this study aims to determine the association between admission LAR and 28-day all-cause mortality in a well-characterized cohort of patients with HFpEF.

Methods

Data source

This was a retrospective cohort study based on the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV, version 3.1) database. Maintained by Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) in Boston, Massachusetts, this openly available, de-identified repository provides extensive patient demographics, laboratory results, medication histories, and comprehensive clinical documentation, serving as a vital asset for detailed healthcare investigations20. Author Jing Xiao gained full authorization to the MIMIC-IV resource and retrieved pertinent study data. The MIT and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center review boards approved the database’s use in research. Informed consent was waived due to the database’s de-identified and anonymous nature. Our study was reported adhering to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for observational research21, and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants selection

Study inclusion criteria (Fig. 1) comprised: (1) Adults aged 18 years or older. (2) Patients diagnosed with HFpEF regardless of whether HFpEF exacerbation was the primary reason for admission, ascertained via International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes as previously reported22: ICD-9 code 42830, 42831, 42832, 42833 and ICD-10 code I503, I5030, I5031, I5032, 15033; (3) First time ICU admission.

Flowchart of the study population selection.

Exclusion criteria encompassed: (1) Missing lactate or albumin measurements; (2) Abnormally elevated lactate concentrations (over than the 99th percentile) to exclude extreme values; (3) Active malignancy, metastatic solid tumors, or severe liver disease.

Following these criteria, 731 patients constituted the analytical cohort. Data extraction employed Structured Query Language (SQL). To mitigate selection bias, only the first ICU admission was analyzed for individuals with multiple hospitalizations.

LAR measurements

The LAR was calculated as serum lactate concentration (mmol/L) divided by serum albumin level (g/dL). To mitigate confounding effects from post-admission therapeutic interventions, the first lactate and albumin measurements typically obtained within a median of 2.1 h (IQR 1.0–4.5 h) after ICU admission were utilized. Patients were subsequently stratified into LAR tertiles: T1 (< 0.363), T2 (0.363–0.593), and T3 (> 0.593).

Covariables

The covariables extracted for analysis included demographics (age, gender, race and body mass index [BMI]); clinical comorbidities (myocardial infarction [MI], peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, dementia, chronic pulmonary disease [CPD], mild liver disease, diabetes mellitus [DM], chronic kidney disease [CKD], and hypertension; initial ICU laboratory results (hemoglobin, red blood cell count [RBC], white blood cell count [WBC], platelet count, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen [BUN], albumin, glucose, sodium, chloride, potassium, International Normalized Ratio [INR] an prothrombin time [PT]); medications (aspirin, clopidogrel, beta blocker, diuretic, heparin, new-oral-anticoagulants [NOAC], warfarin, amiodarone, digitalis, calcium channel blockers [CCB], and statin); vital signs (systolic and diastolic blood pressure [SBP/DBP], oxygen saturation [SpO₂], respiratory rate [RR], and temperature in Fahrenheit [Temperaturef]); and clinical severity indexes, including the Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) score, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, and Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II).

Primary endpoint

The primary endpoint of the study was all-cause mortality within 28 days following ICU admission.

Missing data management

Variables with a missing-data rate > 15% were dropped outright to reduce bias; while BMI, despite high rates of missingness (32.2%), were retained because of their clinical importance. ICU first glucose was replaced by ICU mean glucose owing to extensive missing values. Variables missed less than 15% were handled by multiple imputation.

Statistical analyses

Continuous data demonstrated normal distribution were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with group comparisons performed via Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous measures were reported as median with interquartile range (IQR) and analyzed employing the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical indicators were expressed as percentages (%) and assessed using chi-square testing. Application of the t-test was restricted to normally distributed variables, verified through Shapiro-Wilk normality assessment. Nonparametric comparisons utilized the Mann-Whitney U method. Survival disparities across LAR tertiles within 28 days post-ICU admission were evaluated with Kaplan-Meier methodology.

Cox proportional hazards modeling examined LAR’s prognostic significance for survival, treating it both continuously and categorically. Covariate selection for regression models followed a priori principles grounded in clinical pertinence, existing evidence, and dataset completeness. Six sequential Cox models were developed: an unadjusted baseline; Model 1 (demographic-adjusted); Model 2 (adding comorbidities); Model 3 (incorporating ICU-administered medications); Model 4 (including vital signs and key laboratory parameters); Model 5 (further adjusting for illness severity indices). Multicollinearity was quantified via variance inflation factors (VIF), where values exceeding 10 indicated substantive collinearity.

Complementary sensitivity investigations—comprising restricted cubic spline (RCS) regressions and subgroup evaluations—were executed to confirm result robustness. RCS analyses featuring four knots were implemented across all six Cox regression moles to explore the nonlinear associations. Incremental covariate adjustment in Cox models encompassed demographics, comorbidities, therapeutics, laboratory values, and severity metrics (SIRS, SOFA, GCS, SAPS II). Subgroup assessments were stratified by demographic features and comorbidity profiles. Sensitivity analyses included: (1) Cox regression utilizing non-imputed datasets; (2) Exclusion of mild hepatic impairment cases to minimize selection bias and hepatic influence on albumin; (3) Examination of LAR’s association with 365-day mortality. Additionally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis evaluated LAR’s discriminative capacity for predicting 28-day mortality in HFpEF patients.

Statistical significance was defined by a two-tailed P-value < 0.05. The R programming environment (Version 4.2.2; The R Foundation, http://www.R-project.org) and the Free Statistics analysis suite (Version 2.3 beta, Beijing, China) were utilized for conducting all statistical computations.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics stratified by LAR tertiles revealed significant differences across multiple clinical parameters (Table 1). Finally, 731 critically ill patients with HFpEF were included, with a mean age of 75.6 ± 12.4 years, and balanced gender distribution across LAR tertiles (P = 0.297). Significant intergroup differences emerged in hemodynamic and inflammatory parameters: higher LAR tertiles demonstrated elevated heart rate (T1:79.5 ± 14.0, T3:86.5 ± 16.1 beats/min; P < 0.001), reduced systolic (T1:119.7 ± 16.1, T3:114.3 ± 15.3 mmHg; P < 0.001) and diastolic blood pressure (T1:56.1 ± 9.7, T3:57.9 ± 10.5 mmHg; P < 0.001), increased white blood cell (WBC) counts (T1:10.7 ± 4.5, T3:15.0 ± 8.5 × 10³/µL; P < 0.001), and greater red cell distribution width (RDW, P = 0.006).

Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed a significant association between elevated LAR and reduced 28-day survival probability in HFpEF patients (log-rank P = 0.019, Fig. 2). Survival probability demonstrated an inverse gradient across LAR tertiles, with the highest tertile exhibiting markedly reduced survival compared to intermediate and lowest groups. Early curve divergence emerged post-admission, with progressive separation throughout follow-up. The highest LAR tertile maintained significantly diminished survival probability (log-rank P = 0.019).

Cox regression analysis

Cox regression analyses demonstrated significant associations between LAR and 28-day mortality in HFpEF patients within unadjusted models, irrespective of variable coding (Table 2). When modelled continuously, each unit increase in LAR corresponded to elevated mortality risk (HR 2.37, 95% CI 1.74–3.21; P < 0.001). Categorically, the highest LAR tertile exhibited significantly increased mortality versus the lowest tertile (HR 2.23, 95% CI 1.39–3.60; P = 0.001). These associations persisted following adjustment for demographic factors (Model 1) and comorbidities (Model 2). However, incremental adjustment for pharmacotherapies (Model 3), laboratory parameters (Model 4), and illness severity scores (Model 5) progressively attenuated the effects. In the fully adjusted Model 5, statistical significance was lost for both continuous (HR 1.31, 95% CI 0.80–2.16; P = 0.290) and categorical analyses (T3 vs. T1: HR 1.34, 95% CI 0.71–2.53; P = 0.373).

RCS analysis demonstrated a linear dose-response relationship between LAR and 28-day ICU mortality in unadjusted analyses (P for nonlinearity = 0.726), with mortality risk increasing monotonically across ascending LAR values (Fig. 3a). However, progressive attenuation of this relationship was observed in Models 3–5 after additional adjustment for pharmacotherapies, laboratory parameters, and clinical severity indices (Fig. 3d–f).

Subgroup analyses revealed consistent associations between LAR and 28-day mortality across most HFpEF patient strata in Model 1 (adjusted for age, sex, race and BMI; Fig. 4). Sensitivity analysis

To validate these findings, three sensitivity analyses were conducted: (1) Cox regression replication using non-imputed data (Supplementary Table 1); (2) Exclusion of mild hepatic impairment cases to minimize selection bias and potential confounding from hepatic dysfunction (Supplementary Table 2); (3) Investigation of LAR’s association with 365-day mortality (Supplementary Table 3). Consistently, significant associations emerged in unadjusted and partially adjusted models. However, statistical significance was lost following comprehensive adjustment for pharmacotherapies, laboratory parameters, and clinical severity indices.

ROC analysis

ROC analysis revealed modest discriminatory capacity of the LAR for predicting 28-day mortality in HFpEF patients (AUC 0.625, 95% CI 0.566–0.685; Fig. 5). The optimal LAR threshold for mortality prediction was 0.566, yielding 56.3% sensitivity and 66.7% specificity. While LAR surpassed lactate in predictive performance, it underperformed albumin (P = 0.006; Table 3).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the one of first dedicated analyses of LAR and 28-day mortality in critically ill patients with HFpEF. Cox regression demonstrated significant associations between elevated LAR and increased all-cause mortality risk in unadjusted and partially adjusted models. However, this relationship progressively attenuated following sequential adjusting for pharmacotherapies, laboratory parameters, and illness severity indices, culminating in loss of statistical significance in the fully adjusted model. RCS analyses corroborated this confounding-dependent relationship. ROC analysis further indicated only modest discriminative capacity of LAR for 28-day mortality prediction (AUC 0.625), underperforming serum albumin alone (P = 0.006). Significant interaction effects persisted between LAR and race, BMI, MI, chronic pulmonary disease, CKD, and hypertension across adjustment levels. Collectively, these findings position LAR as a biomarker reflecting integrated disease severity rather than an independent risk factor in decompensated HFpEF.

Current evidence positions the LAR as a robust prognostic indicator for all-cause mortality in critically ill HF populations. A retrospective MIMIC-IV cohort analysis of HF patients post-myocardial infarction23 demonstrated markedly higher admission LAR in non-survivors versus survivors [1.36 (1.20) vs. 0.62 (0.36), P < 0.05]. LAR achieved superior discriminative performance (AUC 0.780, 95% CI: 0.772–0.827) compared to lactate, SOFA score, and other biomarkers, with an optimal threshold of 0.671. Multivariable Cox regression established LAR as an independent predictor of in-hospital mortality, complemented by RCS analysis revealing a non-linear dose-response relationship23. External validation in a separate critical HF cohort reinforced these findings: the highest LAR quintile incurred 3.15-fold (95% CI: 2.47–4.01) and 2.46-fold (95% CI: 2.09–2.89) increases in 28-day and 1-year mortality risks, respectively, relative to the lowest quintile17. This inverse LAR-survival association extends to acute myocardial infarction and HF with comorbid diabetes18, CKD14, or sepsis15, collectively affirming LAR’s utility for short- and long-term mortality prediction. However, prior studies predominantly analyzed combined HFrEF and HFpEF cohorts without dedicated HFpEF stratification. To addressing this gap, our study utilized the well-characterized MIMIC IV database and observed significant unadjusted and partially adjusted associations between elevated LAR and 28-day mortality in critically ill HFpEF patients. Crucially, progressive adjustment for confounders—including pharmacotherapies, laboratory parameters, and illness severity indexes—attenuated this relationship, culminating in loss of statistical significance in the fully adjusted model. Furthermore, LAR’s discriminative capacity for 28-day mortality in HFpEF (AUC = 0.625) was moderate and exceeded by isolated serum albumin. The optimal ROC cutoff (0.566) lies close to the boundary between T2 and T3 (0.593). This overlap suggests that while the tertile stratification captures the high-risk group reasonably well, a continuous or different categorical threshold might offer alternative risk stratification approaches. Our results redefine LAR not as an independent risk factor, but as a composite biomarker mirroring global disease severity in decompensated HFpEF.

Subgroup analyses revealed consistent LAR-mortality associations persisted across most HFpEF subgroups after covariate adjustment for age, sex, race, and BMI. Despite attenuation in fully adjusted models, significant relationships endured in White patients, non-obese individuals, CPD-free subjects, and those with comorbid MI or CKD. Notably, LAR exhibited significant interactions with race, BMI, and MI, CPD, CKD, hypertension—conditions recognized as pathophysiological pillars of HFpEF1,3. Longitudinal data from the PREVEND cohort reveal that the population-attributable fraction of these factors exceeds 60% in women and 45% in men24, indicating that their removal could theoretically prevent one in every two incident HFpEF cases. Obesity is no longer viewed simply as a mechanical load. Visceral adipose tissue, particularly epicardial fat, secretes leptin, resistin and interleukin-6 that diffuse directly into the myocardium, impairing endothelial nitric-oxide signaling and stiffening the compliant left ventricle (LV)25,26. In community-based imaging studies, every 1-unit increase in waist-to-height ratio was associated with a 0.4%/year rise in LV end-diastolic elastance independent of blood pressure25. Systemic hypertension remains the most prevalent antecedent, present in > 80% of HFpEF patients at diagnosis. Chronic afterload elevation stimulates cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and collagen cross-linking, reducing early-diastolic longitudinal strain rate even before LV mass crosses the abnormal threshold27. Prior MI, although traditionally linked to reduced ejection fraction, also predisposes to HFpEF through epicardial adipose tissue dysfunction, segmental fibrosis and asynchronous relaxation28. Finally, CKD amplifies sodium avidity, systemic inflammation and arterial calcification, creating a low-output/high-afterload circulation that exhausts diastolic reserve29. In a pooled analysis, a 30% decline in eGFR doubled the risk of HF hospitalisation among obese hypertensive subjects, an effect only modestly attenuated after adjustment for neurohormonal blockade30. These findings highlight that the prognostic value of LAR varies across HFpEF subphenotypes defined by these comorbidities. Future researches are warranted to delineate context-specific lactate-albumin dynamics within these discrete HFpEF pathobiological domains.

Lactate elevation in HFpEF may originate from multiple sources: skeletal muscle hypoperfusion during exertion, pulmonary hypertension-induced right ventricular strain, hepatic congestion impairing clearance, or inflammatory-mediated aerobic glycolysis (so-called Warburg effect)8,10. Impaired lactate clearance, a recognized feature of HFpEF pathophysiology, associates with reduced exercise tolerance and skeletal muscle metabolic dysfunction, elevating circulating lactate independently of hypoperfusion31,32. A recent investigation enrolled 198 HFpEF patients, 103 with non-cardiac dyspnea (NCD), and 61 healthy volunteers, conducting right heart catheterization with mixed venous blood gas and lactate measurements at rest and during symptom-limited supine exercise33. Findings demonstrated significantly elevated exercise lactate indexed to workload in HFpEF patients (0.08 mmol/L/W [0.05–0.11]) compared to NCD (0.06 mmol/L/W [0.05–0.08]; P = 0.016) and volunteers (0.04 mmol/L/W [0.03–0.05]; P < 0.001). Impaired exercise tolerance in HFpEF arises from central and peripheral factors hindering oxygen utilization, driving hyperlactatemia. Meanwhile, hypoalbuminemia contributes to HF progression through reduced oncotic pressure exacerbating congestion, antioxidant depletion increasing oxidative stress, and impaired drug binding potentially diminishing therapeutic efficacy34. Evidences have linked both hyperlactatemia and hypoalbuminemia to adverse outcomes in HF populations. Our findings further extend this paradigm by demonstrating that the composite metric (LAR) enhances risk stratification specifically in decompensated HFpEF, potentially capturing the intersection of acute metabolic stress (lactate) and chronic vulnerability (albumin). The observed interactions with CKD and hypertension may reflect albumin’s role in diuretic responsiveness and volume regulation, while pulmonary interactions suggest tissue hypoxia amplifying lactate production10,11. However, the temporal dissociation between LAR’s early predictive capacity and later effect attenuation suggests its value lies primarily in reflecting cumulative physiological insults rather than driving pathogenesis. The stronger prognostic performance of albumin versus LAR in our analysis underscores the multifunctional roles of albumin beyond lactate contextualization13.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, residual confounding inherent to retrospective designs cannot be excluded. Unmeasured variables - particularly detailed fluid resuscitation protocols, diuretic strategies, and nutritional support - may influence lactate/albumin kinetics but were incompletely captured. Second, the absence of key pathophysiological markers (e.g., hsTnT, CRP, NT-proBNP) and organ support methods (such as mechanical ventilation, inotrope or vasopressor administration, renal replacement therapy, mechanical circulatory support) limits mechanistic exploration. Third, substantial missing lactate and albumin data (n = 2915) necessitated exclusion of 80% potentially eligible patients. Although mortality rates were comparable between included and excluded cohorts, the latter demonstrated significantly lower illness severity scores, shorter ICU stays, and more favorable lipid profiles - indicating selection bias toward less critically ill individuals. Fourth, single-centre derivation from MIMIC-IV constrains generalizability to diverse healthcare settings. Future prospective studies should incorporate serial LAR measurements, cardiopulmonary exercise testing, and inflammatory profiling to elucidate causal pathway.

Conclusion

In this cohort of critically ill HFpEF patients, elevated LAR was associated with increased short-term mortality risk in unadjusted and partially adjusted analyses. However, this association was attenuated and became non-significant after comprehensive adjustment for treatments, laboratory parameters, and illness severity scores, suggesting LAR serves as a biomarker of integrated disease severity rather than an independent risk factor. Its clinical utility may be most pronounced in early assessment prior to complete clinical characterization. Future research should validate these findings in prospective cohorts and explore dynamic LAR trajectories and their interaction with specific HFpEF phenotypes and therapies.
