Electrons as intermediate charge carriers in ion transport through nanoscale channels
Baptiste Coquinot, Mathieu Lizée, Lydéric Bocquet, Nikita Kavokine

Abstract
The ionic conductivity of a nanofluidic channel is one of its most basic properties, which is systematically characterized in experiments. While most studies to date have focused on the DC conductivity, there has recently been a surge of interest in the behavior of nanochannels under AC voltages. Here, we show that in nanochannels with electrically conducting walls, electrons can contribute to transporting ionic current through the channel. We identify a critical frequency and lengthscale above which electrons in the channel walls become the main charge carriers, resulting in a strongly increased conductivity. Our findings open the way to the engineering of nanofluidic functionalities based on ion-electron coupling.

The conducting wall of a nanochannel can be thought of as an electrode immersed in the solution. Electrodes are usually classified into two categories, according to the type of interaction they can have with the solution. Faradaic electrodes allow for the exchange of charges with the solution, whereby, through a chemical process, an ion in the solution is converted into an electron inside the electrode
Kim et al. 2007
;
Augustyn et al. 2014
. Non-faradaic or capacitive electrodes do not allow for such a chemical exchange of charges, and their interaction with the surrounding solution is purely electrostatic. When a potential is applied to such an electrode, counterions from the solution are attracted to its surface, forming an electric double-layer (EDL) of thickness on the order of the Debye length
\lambda_{\rm D}
. The electrode-electrolyte system thus behaves as a capacitor, of capacitance
\mathcal{C}_{\rm EDL}\sim\epsilon/\lambda_{\rm D}
, where
\epsilon
is the dielectric constant of the solution close to the electrode. The accurate prediction of EDL capacitance is a complex theoretical problem
Pireddu and Rotenberg 2023

\mathcal{C}_{\rm EDL}
typically ranges between 1 and 50
\mu
F/cm
2

Simon and Gogotsi 2008
;
Miller et al. 2010
;
Uesugi et al. 2013

;

Salanne et al. 2016
;

;

We consider a nanoscale channel of length
L
, width
W
and confinement (thickness)
h
, connecting two reservoirs filled with a salt solution (Fig. 1a). Electrodes immersed in the solution apply a (possibly time-dependent) voltage in between the two reservoirs. The ion-wall interactions inside the channel are characterized by the double-layer capacitance
\mathcal{C}_{\rm EDL}
, corresponding to an impedance
Z_{\rm EDL}=1/(i\omega\mathcal{C}_{\rm EDL})
. Under the assumption that electrostatic equilibration across the channel is faster than the variation of the applied potential, the channel’s impedance can be described by a modified transmission line model (TLM) – see Fig.
1
b. It is made of two resistive paths – ionic and electronic (the latter representing the channel wall), connected by the interfacial capacitor.
We denote
R_{i}=\rho_{i}L/hW
and
R_{e}=\overline{R}_{e}L/W
the ionic and electronic resistances, respectively.
The ionic resistivity
\rho_{i}=k_{B}T/qD_{i}c_{s}
can be estimated from the ionic diffusivity
D_{i}
and the salt concentration
c_{s}
(
q
is the ionic charge), while the electronic resistivity
\overline{R}_{e}
is determined by the channel wall material. Only the ionic path is connected to the external circuit, but the electronic path can contribute to the AC conductivity thanks to the interfacial capacity. We note that the complete circuit comprises a resistance and a capacitance due to the electrodes and the solution reservoirs in series with the nanochannel. In the following, we focus only on the nanochannel impedance
Z(\omega)
, keeping in mind that the electrodes impose a cutoff for the frequencies
\omega
that can be probed, typically ranging from 1 kHz to 1 MHz.

If the two paths were perfectly connected, the fraction of electric current going through the ionic path would be
I_{i}^{0}/I=R_{e}/(R_{i}+R_{e})
where
I
is the total current going through the transmission line. But with a non-trivial interfacial impedance, this fraction becomes dependent on the position
x
along the channel.
In the continuous limit of the TLM, the excess ionic current
J(x)=I_{i}(x)-I_{i}^{0}
solves a modified Telegrapher’s equation [SM]:

\ell(\omega)^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}J(x,\omega)=2iJ(x,\omega)

(1)

where we have introduced an exchange length:

\ell(\omega)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega\mathcal{C}_{\rm EDL}(\rho_{i}/h+\overline{R}_{e})}}.

(2)

Physically, it corresponds to the lengthscale over which current is exchanged between the two paths, as shown in Fig.
1
c where a typical solution of Eq. (
1
) is plotted. At distances
x\gtrsim\ell(\omega)
along the channel,
J(x)=0
so that
I_{i}=I_{0}
. Since the electronic resistance is typically much lower than the ionic resistance, this means that electrons become the main charge carriers far enough from the channel mouth. This transition is of course only possible if the channel length
L>\ell(\omega)
– a condition easiest to satisfy at higher frequencies.
At a given frequency,
\ell(\omega)
is shorter for stronger confinement: the larger the ionic resistance, the easier the transition to electronic conduction (Fig.
1
d).

We confirm this qualitative intuition by solving for the impedance
Z(\omega)
of the channel, using the boundary conditions
I_{i}(\pm L/2)=I
[SM]:

Z(\omega)=\frac{R_{e}R_{i}}{R_{e}+R_{i}}\left[1+\frac{R_{i}}{R_{e}}\frac{\textnormal{tanh}\left(\sqrt{2i\frac{\omega}{\omega_{c}}}\right)}{\sqrt{2i\frac{\omega}{\omega_{c}}}}\right]

(3)

Here, we have introduced a critical frequency
\omega_{c}
, defined by
\ell(\omega_{c})=L
, which reads explicitly (Fig.
1
e):

\omega_{c}=\frac{2}{\mathcal{C}_{\rm EDL}(\rho_{i}/h+\overline{R}_{e})L^{2}}.

(4)

At
\omega=0
,
Z=R_{i}
: the electronic path cannot contribute to the DC conductivity because the electrons cannot exit the channel wall. At
\omega\to\infty
,
Z\approx R_{e}
: the electronic path effectively short-circuits the ionic path.
\omega=\omega_{c}
marks the transition between these two regimes. At this frequency, the imaginary part of the impedance is largest and the channel exhibits capacitive behavior (see Fig.
2
a and b).

In practice, the electronic path is relevant if
\omega_{c}
is within the frequency range accessible to the ionic electrodes, typically between DC and
10^{3}-10^{6}~\rm Hz
. As shown in Fig.
1
e, this is the case for channels with high aspect ratio. For realistic values the resistivities and interfacial capacitance (see Fig. 1 legend), we find for instance
\omega_{c}=10~\rm Hz
for
L=100~\rm\mu m
at 1 nm confinement, or
L=1~\rm mm
at
1~\rm\mu m
confinement. Electron-mediated ionic conduction can thus occur across a vast range of scales, from nanochannels to microchannels. We note, however, that there is a significant effect on the overall conductivity only if the electronic resistance is much lower than the ionic resistance, which is not necessarily the case for larger channels (Fig. 2c).

;
Lizée et al. 2025

In conclusion, we have shown that nanofluidic channels with conducting walls can exhibit an enhanced AC conductivity, as the electrons inside the walls play the role of intermediate charge carriers for the ionic current. The effect is most significant for longer channels, such as those found in tortuous porous media
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Ghanbari and Esfandiar 2020
;
Yang et al. 2024
;
Gogotsi and Anasori 2019
. In practice, it could serve as a strategy for improving the efficiency of osmotic energy conversion through reverse electrodyalisis, by shortening transient regimes
Logan and Elimelech 2012
;
Siria et al. 2017
;
Chapuis and Bocquet 2025
. Fundamentally, our findings highlights the power of impedance spectroscopy as an analytical tool for nanofluidic channels: the proposed model allows interpreting the frequency-dependent impedance in terms of electrostatic processes occurring under nanoconfinement. Future work will involve extending our modeling to the case of nanochannels with electrochemical (faradaic) interfaces. We anticipate that nano-electrochemical channels may exhibit very subtle functionalities. For instance, applying an AC voltage to the electronic path instead of the ionic path, one could achieve ion transport against a concentration gradient, which is reminiscent of redox-powered biological proton pumps
Kim et al. 2007
.
