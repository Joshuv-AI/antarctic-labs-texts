Association of Subclinical Cardiac Remodeling With Incident Cancer in the MESA Cohort
Authors: Xinjiang Cai, Quinn White, Jordan Juarez, Omar Chehab, Bharath Ambale‐Venkatesh, W Craig Johnson, Spencer L Hansen, Kosuke Inoue, Joao AC Lima, Karol E Watson, Robyn L McClelland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with established cardiovascular disease have an increased risk of cancer; however, the association between subclinical cardiac remodeling and cancer development remains unclear. We aimed to examine the association between cardiac magnetic resonance imaging–derived measures of subclinical cardiac remodeling and incident cancer in the MESA (Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis) cohort.MethodsMultivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate the association between each cardiac magnetic resonance imaging measure of interest and the incidence of cancer, separately, for an all‐inclusive cancer end point as well as prostate, breast, female‐specific, colorectal, and lung cancer subtypes.ResultsOf 6214 participants with cancer data available (median age, 61.0 years; 52.7% women), 4595 had at least 1 cardiac magnetic resonance imaging–derived measure of interest, with 563 cancer events over a median follow‐up of 18.7 years. Overall cancer incidence was higher with greater left ventricular mass index or left ventricular mass/volume ratio, and lower with higher left atrial emptying fraction or peak left atrial longitudinal strain. In adjusted Cox proportional hazards models, left ventricular mass index was significantly associated with the hazard of breast cancer (hazard ratio [HR], 1.88 [95% CI, 1.19–2.97]; P=0.007), and peak left atrial strain was significantly associated with the hazard of colorectal cancer (HR, 0.52 [95% CI, 0.33–0.82]; P=0.005).ConclusionsIn the MESA cohort, elevated left ventricular mass index and worse (lower) peak left atrial strain were associated with an increased risk of breast cancer and colorectal cancer, respectively. Subclinical cardiac remodeling may serve as an early marker to identify individuals at risk for both cardiovascular disease and cancer.Keywords: cancer risk, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, cardio‐oncology, left atrial peak strain, left ventricular mass, prevention, risk predictionSubject Categories: Remodeling, Risk Factors, Clinical Studies, Epidemiology

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Our findings suggest that subclinical cardiac remodeling assessed by advanced cardiac imaging may help stratify individuals with subclinical cardiovascular disease for future incident cancer risk and integrate an interdisciplinary preventive approach toward risk prediction and prevention strategies for both preventive cardiology and oncology.

The bidirectional relationship between cardiovascular disease (CVD) and cancer, 2 leading global causes of death, is likely driven by shared risk factors, such as aging, smoking, diabetes, obesity, and physical inactivity, and common pathophysiological mechanisms, including chronic inflammation, immune dysregulation, and oxidative stress. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Cancer survivors have a high prevalence of coexisting CVD and are at elevated risk of developing CVD because of shared risk factors. 1 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Conversely, individuals with established CVD, such as heart failure (HF), 12 atherosclerotic CVD, 13 acute myocardial infarction, 14 or elevated levels of hs‐CRP (high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein), 15 , 16 have an increased risk of cancer development, 17 further highlighting the bidirectional crosstalk between CVD and cancer.

More recently, 3 large prospective cohort studies involving the PREVEND (Prevention of Renal and Vascular End‐Stage Disease) study, the FHS (Framingham Heart Study), and the MESA (Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis) have demonstrated that in participants without clinically overt CVD, mild elevation of baseline levels of cardiac biomarkers is associated with an increased risk of incident cancer. 15 , 18 , 19 Cardiac biomarkers are well established for predicting incident HF and CVD death, and for detecting early subclinical cardiac remodeling in asymptomatic CVD‐free individuals. 20 , 21 These findings suggest a potential link between subclinical cardiac remodeling and future cancer risk. Additional support for this association comes from animal models of pressure overload–induced cardiac hypertrophy. In these models, early cardiac remodeling, either with contractile dysfunction 22 or preserved ejection fraction, 23 has been shown to promote cancer growth and metastasis, even before the development of overt HF. However, evidence linking subclinical cardiac remodeling, particularly when assessed by advanced cardiac imaging, to cancer risk in humans remains limited. 2 , 17

MESA is a prospective, longitudinal cohort study of adults free of clinical CVD at baseline and is designed to investigate the prevalence and progression of subclinical CVD across 4 racial/ethnic groups recruited from 6 US field centers. 24 , 25 In this study, we investigated whether subclinical cardiac remodeling assessed by cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is associated with incident cancer in the ethnically and geographically diverse MESA cohort. Addressing this knowledge gap may advance our understanding of the structural and functional crosstalk between subclinical CVD and cancer beyond baseline cardiac biomarkers and provide novel insights for preventive cardio‐oncology.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the MESA Coordinating Center on approval of a manuscript proposal by the MESA Committees (https://mesa‐nhlbi.org/about/committees).

Study Population

The MESA enrolled 6814 men and women aged 45 to 84 years free of clinical overt CVD between 2000 and 2002 (examination 1), representing 4 racial and ethnic groups (non‐Hispanic White, Black, Hispanic/Latino, and Chinese) recruited across 6 urban study centers in the United States. 24 , 26 Of the 6814 participants enrolled, 5098 (75%) underwent cardiac MRI at examination 1. 27 The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards at all participating centers. All participants in the MESA were provided with informed consent. Comprehensive details on the study protocol are accessible on the MESA website: https://www.mesa‐nhlbi.org. Participants were followed up with serial clinical evaluations and laboratory testing to characterize the risk factors associated with the progression of subclinical to clinically overt CVD and incident cardiovascular events.

We excluded 536 participants with cancer at baseline and 29 participants lost to immediate follow‐up. In addition, 20 participants diagnosed with cancer within 180 days of enrollment and 11 participants diagnosed with cancer within 180 days after their first cardiovascular events (stroke, angina, myocardial infarction, and HF) were excluded.

Baseline Characteristics

Demographic information, CVD risk factors, and clinical characteristics were obtained at examination 1, as previously described, 24 , 28 , 29 including age, sex, race and ethnicity, education level, dietary patterns, physical activity, health insurance status, systolic blood pressure, lipid levels, renal function, and treatment with antihypertensive and lipid‐lowering drugs. Dietary patterns were evaluated using a self‐administered, 120‐item food frequency questionnaire. 30 , 31 The diet metric includes 5 factors: intake of fruits and vegetables, fish, sodium, sugar‐sweetened beverages, and whole grains. Participants were categorized as having a poor or not poor diet based on meeting 0 to 1 or 2 to 5 intake recommendations, respectively. 29 Diabetes was defined by a fasting plasma glucose level ≥126 mg/dL or the use of antidiabetic medications. 32 Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg, or the use of antihypertensive medications. Smoking status was evaluated through a self‐completed questionnaire, as previously reported. 33 Participants were categorized as current smokers, former smokers, or nonsmokers. For current or former smokers, smoking history was quantified in pack‐years. Physical activity was assessed using a semiquantitative survey adapted from the Typical Week Physical Activity Survey, as previously described. 34

Cardiac MRI Measures for Left Ventricular and Atrial Structure and Function

Cardiac MRI imaging was performed during examination 1 using 1.5‐T magnet scanners to assess left ventricular (LV) functions, volumes, and myocardial mass following the established protocol, as previously reported. 27 , 35 , 36 Left atrial (LA) volumetric function and the global longitudinal atrial strain were measured in 2‐ and 4‐chamber views, as previously described. 37 , 38 The peak longitudinal LA strain was calculated from the global longitudinal strain curve during the cardiac cycle. 37

Outcome Definitions and Cancer Events

In addition to baseline and in‐person examinations, participants or their proxies were contacted by telephone every 9 to 12 months during follow‐up. 24 , 39 Telephone interviewers collected available data on interim hospital admissions, CVD diagnoses and procedures, and deaths. Additional information was obtained through cohort clinic visits, participant‐initiated contacts, and medical record abstraction. Hospitalization records were reviewed for International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9), diagnosis codes, and cancer‐related codes were extracted and reclassified into specific cancer subtypes. 40 The term “all cancers” included all registered and defined cancer subtypes within MESA, except nonmelanoma skin cancers. The female‐specific cancer end point comprised ovarian, breast, and uterine cancers.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics are presented as median values with interquartile range (quartile 1–quartile 3) for continuous variables and frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. Comparisons of baseline characteristics by incident cancer status were performed using Wilcoxon rank‐sum test for continuous variables and χ2 test for categorical variables. Cumulative incidence rates were calculated for all cancers, specific cancer subtypes (lung cancer, colorectal cancer, and breast cancer), and sex‐specific cancers (prostate cancer for men and ovarian, uterine, and breast cancer for women) and were presented as events per 10 000 person‐years. The incidence rates were stratified based on (1) body‐size–adjusted LV mass indexed to body surface area (LVMI) in quartiles 27 , 36 ; (2) LV mass/LV volume ratio in quartiles 27 ; (3) LA emptying fraction (LAEF), LA maximum volume indexed to body surface area (LAVI), and peak longitudinal LA strain in tertiles 37 , 38 ; (4) the presence of LV hypertrophy; and (5) LV ejection fraction (LVEF) levels: <50% and ≥50%. 21 Kaplan–Meier curves were used to illustrate cumulative event rates for cancer incidence.

Separate Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate associations between each primary MRI predictor and the incidence of all cancers and cancer subtypes. Cardiac MRI measures were analyzed as continuous predictor variables as follows: (1) LVMI; (2) LV mass/LV volume ratio; (3) LVEF levels; and (4) LAEF, LAVI, and peak longitudinal LA strain. Modeling analyses of cancer subtypes used a cause‐specific competing risk approach, in which participants were censored at the time of diagnosis of a different cancer type. Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, race and ethnicity, education level, and health insurance status (private and public). Model 2 included all covariates from model 1 plus smoking status (current versus noncurrent; pack‐years over lifetime), diabetes, body mass index, antihypertensive medication use, systolic blood pressure, total and high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol levels, statin use, renal function, diet, and physical activity. For breast cancer and female‐specific cancer end points, additional female‐specific adjustments were made for menopausal stage, hormone replacement therapy, age at menarche, and age at first birth (21 years≤age<25 years, age≥25 years, and no children). Hazard ratios (HRs) were calculated per 1‐SD increment.

We also conducted a variable importance analysis, where, for each cancer outcome of interest, all primary predictors were included in the same model to identify the strongest contributor to the model. Proportional hazards tests were performed for all Cox models to identify potential violations to the proportional hazards assumption. A more conservative significance threshold of P<0.01 was considered statistically significant because of multiple comparisons with a large number of statistical models evaluated. All statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (version 4.5.1; R Core Team 2025; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; URL: https://www.R‐project.org/).

RESULTS

Study Population and Baseline Characteristics

A total of 6214 MESA participants who were free of CVD and self‐reported cancer at baseline (examination 1) and met the inclusion criteria were included in the analyses. The median age of participants at examination 1 was 61 years (quartile 1–quartile 3, 53.0–69.0 years), and 52.7% were women. The median follow‐up for MESA participants with cancer data was 18.7 years (quartile1–quartile 3, 12.9–19.5 years).

Tables 1 and 2 show baseline demographic and risk factor characteristics of 6214 participants and 3277 female participants categorized by cancer diagnosis status. Over the follow‐up period, there were 790 new first cancer events, with an incidence rate of 87.90 cases per 10 000 person‐years (Table 3) and a median time to cancer detection of 8.45 years (quartile 1–quartile 3, 4.61–12.67 years). Among those 4596 participants with at least 1 MRI measure of interest available, there were 563 cancer events. For LV and LA structural and functional measures, participants who developed incident cancer had higher LV mass and LV mass/volume ratio, lower peak LA strain and LAEF, and elevated high‐sensitivity cardiac troponin T and NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide) levels. Incidence Rate of All Cancers and Cancer Subtypes Stratified by Cardiac MRI Measures

Among participants who underwent cardiac MRI at examination 1, measures were available for 4596 for LVEF, LV mass, and LV mass/volume; 3872 for LAEF and peak longitudinal LA strain; and 3921 for maximal LAVI. The median (quartile 1–quartile 3) values of LVMI, LV mass/volume, LAEF, LAVI, and peak longitudinal LA strain at examination 1 were 78.4 (68.6–89.8) g/m2, 1.13 (1.00–1.28) g/mL, 60.81% (53.19%–68.49%), 29.18 (23.28–35.93) mL/m2, and 34.65% (29.25%–41.10%), respectively.

The incidence of cancer was higher among those with greater LVMI and LV mass/volume ratio, reduced LAVI, LAEF, and peak LA longitudinal strain, LVEF <50%, and presence of LV hypertrophy (Figure 1; Table S1). The incidence rates of all cancers per 10 000 person‐years differed markedly between the lowest and highest tertiles/quartiles of cardiac MRI measures. Specifically, for LVMI, rates increased from 67.62 (95% CI, 55.97–90.97) in the lowest tertile to 108.6 (95% CI, 92.98–126.1) in the highest. In contrast, measures of LA function, such as LAEF, LAVI, and peak LA strain, exhibited lower incidence rates in the highest tertiles: incidence rate for peak LA strain decreased from 106.94 (95% CI, 92.31–123.22) to 67.54 (95% CI, 56.62–79.94). The overall trend observed for all cancers was largely preserved for cancer subtype analyses, except for prostate cancer, and the trend was also likely affected by relatively low incidence rates in some subgroups. Differences in cancer incidence rate across LAVI tertiles were relatively modest.

Multivariable Cox Proportional Hazards Models by Cardiac MRI Measures

We used Cox proportional hazards models to assess the association between cardiac MRI measures and the risk of each specific cancer end point. Tests of the proportional hazards assumption suggested a violation for the model, including LV mass/volume ratio for the all‐cancer end point (proportionality test, P=0.024 for model 1, P=0.020 for model 2). Consequently, the coefficient of LV mass/volume ratio was modeled as time dependent by including a multiplicative interaction term between LV mass/volume ratio and follow‐up time. Our findings support that the positive association between baseline LV mass/volume ratio and incident cancer attenuates over time, although the attenuation was not significant at the 0.01 level. These results are shown in Table S2.

Figure 2; and Table S3 show the adjusted HRs between the incidence of cancer and each MRI measure predictor, where each cancer end point was the outcome in the Cox regression models. Table 4 includes the HRs of the primary predictors in each Cox model with P<0.05. When using a more conservative significance threshold of 0.01, we found a significant association between LVMI and hazard of breast cancer (HR in model 2: 1.88 [95% CI, 1.19–2.97]; P=0.007). We also found evidence for a significant association between peak LA strain and the hazard of colorectal cancer for model 1 (HR, 0.52 [95% CI, 0.33–0.82]; P=0.005). There was evidence (P<0.05) supporting an association of peak LA strain with breast cancer and lung cancer in both models, although neither was significant at the 0.01 level.

DISCUSSION

Our study offers several insights into the complex bidirectional relationship between subclinical CVD and cancer. Overall, cancer incidence differed by subclinical LV and LA structural and functional remodeling measures detected by cardiac MRI, particularly when comparing the highest with the lowest quartile/tertile categories. Higher LV mass was significantly associated with higher breast cancer risk and reduced peak LA strain, with greater risk of colorectal cancer after multivariable adjustment. Consistent with animal model findings linking cardiac hypertrophy to augmented cancer growth and metastasis, 22 , 23 our results provide direct human evidence that subclinical cardiac remodeling, assessed by advanced cardiac imaging in asymptomatic individuals without overt CVD, is associated with increased risk of incident cancer.

To address the human relevance of findings from mouse models of cardiac hypertrophy, a retrospective study examined patients with and without moderate to severe aortic stenosis (AS) and cancer incidence. 22 Over a median follow‐up of 5.4 years, cancer incidence was higher in patients with AS; however, the association was diminished and became nonsignificant after adjustment for cardiovascular risk factors. The association remained significant only among patients with AS aged 40 to 60 years and not in those aged >60 years. Although AS diagnosis might serve as a surrogate for cardiac remodeling, direct measurements of cardiac remodeling parameters, including LV mass and ventricular hypertrophy, at early or advanced stages were not assessed. Prospective clinical studies with structural and functional measures by advanced cardiac imaging are warranted to investigate whether cardiac remodeling, especially at the early stage before developing clinical CVD, increases the risk of incident cancer. 2 , 17

Ventricular remodeling is a heterogeneous and dynamic process involving structural and functional changes at the molecular, cellular, and tissue levels. 41 Individuals with ventricular hypertrophy, chamber enlargement, elevated filling pressures, or elevated cardiac biomarker levels are classified by the HF guideline as being at increased risk for symptomatic HF (stage B, pre‐HF). 42 Early identification of individuals at stage B is essential to start lifestyle modification, mitigate cardiovascular risk, and initiate pharmacologic therapy to prevent or delay progression into symptomatic HF. Mild elevation in cardiac biomarkers can serve as an early indicator of subclinical cardiac remodeling, including increased LV mass and fibrosis in asymptomatic individuals without clinical CVD. 20 , 21 More recently, these cardiac biomarkers are shown to be strong predictors for incident cancer in 3 large prospective cohort studies: PREVEND study, FHS, and MESA. 15 , 18 , 19

The MESA offers a unique opportunity to examine the relationship between subclinical cardiac remodeling and cancer risk beyond elevated biomarker levels, as it is the first large epidemiologic cohort to use advanced imaging techniques, including cardiac computed tomography 40 and MRI, 27 to detect subclinical CVD at baseline. 25 Among MESA participants, elevated LV mass and greater LV mass/volume ratio are positively associated with incident CVD and cardiovascular mortality. 27 , 36 , 43 We demonstrate an association of LV mass index with breast cancer with a stringent P‐value threshold of <0.01. Female patients with chemotherapy‐naïve breast cancer exhibit higher LV mass and relative LV hypertrophy, potentially driven by elevated endothelin‐1 released from breast cancer cells that modulates cardiomyocytes. 44 , 45 Our findings suggest the link between subclinical LV mass remodeling and future breast cancer risk may occur well before cancer diagnosis, analogous to stage B patients at risk for developing HF. This finding is independent of traditional cardiovascular risk factors, likely through cardiac secreted factors, such as periostin, 22 and other unidentified mechanisms.

LV global longitudinal strain has been widely used in cardio‐oncology to stratify and monitor patients with cancer at risk for systolic dysfunction and HF before, during, and after cancer treatment, 46 , 47 whereas evidence linking LA strain to cancer therapy–associated cardiotoxicities is also increasing. 48 The limited LV strain sample size in the MESA cohort precluded further analysis of LV strain with cancer events. 48 Here, we show that reduced peak LA strain is associated with increased risk of colorectal cancer, breast cancer, and lung cancer in all 3 models with P<0.05, and with model 1 demonstrating a P<0.01. Peak LA longitudinal strain may correlate with LV filling pressure and can be incorporated into the assessment of LV diastolic dysfunction, 49 , 50 identifies elevated risk in asymptomatic patients with moderate AS, 51 and predicts future cardiovascular events. 52 Therefore, peak LA strain may serve as an early marker for subclinical cardiac remodeling to identify individuals at higher risk for incident cancer.

One notable observation was that structural remodeling, as reflected by LVMI, and earlier LA dysfunction, as shown in peak LA strain, were associated with incident cancer. Conventional functional metrics, such as LVEF and LAEF, on the other hand, were not. Several considerations may explain this pattern. First, the MESA cohort enrolled individuals free of clinically overt CVD at baseline; therefore, relatively few participants exhibited significant LV or LA dysfunction (eg, LVEF <50%). The limited number of cancer events within these subgroups may have reduced statistical power to detect associations of LVEF and LAEF for cancers. Second, LV mass index and peak LA strain might capture subtle structural remodeling and early atrial dysfunction across the full cohort. These 2 parameters may reflect earlier stages of cardiac remodeling that precede overt dysfunction, potentially making them more sensitive early indicators of systemic processes linked to cancer risk. This interpretation is consistent with mouse models of pressure overload–induced cardiac hypertrophy, in which early remodeling before overt cardiac dysfunction is associated with enhanced tumor growth. Together, these findings suggest that early structural alterations with LV mass index and peak LA strain support the concept of early cardiac remodeling with increased cancer incidence, which is also shown in mild elevation of cardiac biomarker levels 15 , 18 , 19 and increased coronary artery calcium. 40

Current guideline for stage B (pre‐HF status) emphasizes risk factor modification to prevent progression into symptomatic HF. 42 Pharmacologic therapy, such as angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, and β‐blockers, is primarily recommended for patients with LVEF ≤40%, particularly in nonischemic settings. 42 Our findings suggest that subclinical cardiac remodeling in the absence of symptoms or significantly reduced LVEF may identify a higher‐risk phenotype associated with increased cancer risk, indicative of shared pathophysiological pathways between CVD and cancer. These observations support a more integrated approach to risk stratification that incorporates imaging‐based markers alongside traditional risk factors. They also reinforce the importance of aggressive management of lifestyle factors and comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes, and obesity, as well as efforts to reduce systemic inflammation. Collectively, these integrated prevention strategies may mitigate the risk for both CVD and cancer at an early stage.

Study Limitations

A key strength of our study is that advanced cardiac imaging studies were performed at baseline in a large prospective cohort; however, several limitations should be addressed. MESA participants without CVD and major medical conditions at enrollment are likely healthier than the general US population of similar age, with a lower cancer incidence. Detection bias may be present, as cardiac remodeling could increase CVD incidence and lead to more frequent hospitalizations or clinical evaluations. To mitigate this effect, we excluded individuals diagnosed with cancer within 180 days of their first cardiovascular events. Some participants without baseline MRI abnormalities might develop cardiac remodeling during the follow‐up period, potentially leading to an attenuation of HRs. To address temporality, we noted the extended follow‐up period of 18.7 years and the median time to cancer diagnosis of 8.45 years in MESA, which supports that subclinical cardiac remodeling occurred before cancer detection. Cancer events in the MESA cohort were identified using ICD‐9 codes from hospitalizations and inpatient procedures, which may have missed cancer diagnoses among participants who were never hospitalized. 19 , 40 Cancer events were not physician adjudicated, and data on cancer stage and treatment were not available in the MESA. Finally, the associations between subclinical cardiac remodeling and the development of incident cancers in a single observational study does not establish causality. Although we adjusted for a broad range of traditional risk factors, the possibility of residual or unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded. External validation of these findings in other large population‐based cohorts with available imaging data is warranted. Despite these limitations, baseline advanced cardiac imaging studies in MESA provide valuable insights into the association of subclinical cardiac remodeling with cancer risk in this large, geographically and ethnically diverse prospective cardiovascular cohort.

CONCLUSIONS

In the multiethnic adult MESA cohort free of clinical CVD and cancer at baseline, subclinical cardiac remodeling is associated with future cancer incidence (Figure 4). LVMI and peak LA strain, together with elevated baseline cardiac biomarkers 15 , 18 , 19 and coronary artery calcium score, 40 may help identify individuals at risk for both CVD and cancer and support improved risk prediction and prevention strategies at the intersection of preventive cardiology and oncology.
