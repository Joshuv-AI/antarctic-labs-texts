Imaging systematics induced by galaxy sub-sample fluctuation: new systematics at second order Preprint: APS/123-QED
Hui Kong, Nora Elisa Chisari, Boris Leistedt, Eric Gawiser, Martin Rodríguez-Monroy, Noah Weaverdyck, The LSST Dark Energy Science Collaboration

Abstract
Imaging systematics refers to the inhomogeneous distribution of a galaxy sample caused by varying observing conditions and astrophysical foregrounds. Current mitigation methods correct the galaxy density fluctuations n gal / n ¯ gal n_{\mathrm{gal}}/\bar{n}_{\mathrm{gal}} caused by imaging systematics assuming that all galaxies in a sample have the same n gal / n ¯ gal n_{\mathrm{gal}}/\bar{n}_{\mathrm{gal}} . Under this assumption, the corrected sample cannot perfectly recover the true correlation function. We name this effect sub-sample systematics . For a galaxy sample, even if its overall sample statistics (redshift distribution n ⁡ ( z ) n(z) , galaxy bias b ⁡ ( z ) b(z) ), are accurately measured, n ⁡ ( z ) , b ⁡ ( z ) n(z),b(z) can still vary across the observed footprint. It makes the correlation function amplitude of galaxy clustering higher, while correlation functions for galaxy-galaxy lensing and cosmic shear do not have noticeable change. Such a combination could potentially degenerate with physical signals on small angular scales, such as the amplitude of galaxy clustering, the impact of neutrino mass on the matter power spectrum, etc. sub-sample systematics cannot be corrected using imaging systematics mitigation approaches that rely on the cross-correlation signal between imaging systematics maps and the observed galaxy density field. In this paper, we derive formulated expressions of sub-sample systematics , demonstrating its fundamental difference with other imaging systematics. We also provide several toy models to visualize this effect. Finally, we discuss the estimation and mitigation of sub-sample systematics with a combination of synthetic source injection (SSI), analytical benchmarking on SSI, and self-organizing map.

Theory

II.1
Imaging Systematics Background Knowledge

In imaging systematics mitigation modeling, the observed galaxy density field is considered as

;
Ross et al. 2011
;

;

;

\rho_{\mathrm{obs}}=\rho_{\mathrm{no\,sys}}\prod_{i}(1+\mathcal{F}({\textbf{sys}^{i}}))

(2)

Here

Schlegel et al. 1998

\mathcal{F}

\rho_{obs}
is the observed galaxy density field.
\rho_{\mathrm{no\,sys}}
represents the galaxy density field observed without any imaging systematics. Note that in this model, we ignore

\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{obs}}
. For example, galaxies could be lost due to the noise in the images, reducing the number density. When the mean of
\mathcal{F}
is not 0, the mean galaxy density is shifted. In a realistic situation, we do not consider
\rho_{\mathrm{no\,sys}}
as the ‘truth catalog’. Instead, we just need a homogeneously distributed catalog with good knowledge of the redshift distribution
n(z)
and galaxy bias
b(z)
. Under these considerations, we define the ‘truth catalog’ to have the same mean number density as the ‘observed catalog’.
\mathcal{F}
is re-defined to
f
, where
1+f
is proportional to
1+\mathcal{F}
, and
f
has a mean of 0.

\rho_{\mathrm{obs}}=\rho_{\mathrm{truth}}\prod_{i}(1+f({\textbf{sys}^{i}}))

(3)

\rho_{\mathrm{truth}}
is not the same as the galaxy density field without any imaging systematics
\rho_{\mathrm{no\,sys}}
. Rather, it can be viewed as a re-selection of galaxies in
\rho_{\mathrm{obs}}
. This hypothetically selected sample is homogeneously distributed against all imaging systematics and has the same number density as
\rho_{\mathrm{obs}}
. In this particular case, we can set the mean of
f(\textbf{sys}^{i})

\bar{f}(\textbf{sys}^{i})=0,\,\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{obs}}=\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{truth}}

(4)

II.2
Imaging systematics with multiple sub-samples

In a more realistic configuration, a galaxy sample comprises galaxies with different redshift, luminosity, etc. The defined galaxy sample can be seen as an ensemble of galaxies with similar properties. However, one type of galaxy in this ensemble could be different from another due to having a different intrinsic flux, color and shape. We consider the selected galaxy sample to be an ensemble of galaxies
k
with various properties. Each
k
responds to survey property maps differently. Equation
3

\rho_{\mathrm{obs}}=\sum_{k}{\rho_{\mathrm{truth},k}}\prod_{I}(1+f_{k}(\textbf{sys}^{i}))

(5)

Each
f_{k}(\textbf{sys}^{i})
still has a mean of 0.

\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{truth},k}

\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{truth},k}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{obs},k}}{N_{\mathrm{obs},\mathrm{total}}}\cdot\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{obs}}

(6)

Similarly to the discussion in Section
II.1
,
\rho_{\mathrm{truth},k}
represents a galaxy sample that is homogeneously distributed against any imaging systematics, and has the same redshift and bias distribution as the observed galaxy sample. It is not the composition of the galaxy sub-sample
k
without any systematics. Under this definition, we have
\bar{f}_{k}(\textbf{sys}^{i})=0

We define the fraction of

h_{k}=\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{truth},k}/\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{obs}}

(7)

Enhancement of clustering amplitude at small
\alpha

We take a closer look at the 3rd term in Equation

\sum_{kk^{\prime}}h_{k}h_{k^{\prime}}\langle f_{k},f_{k^{\prime}}\rangle\langle\delta_{\mathrm{truth},k},\delta_{\mathrm{truth},k\prime}\rangle

This term can be decomposed into the product of two correlation functions (see Appendix

For convenience, we denote

\delta_{\mathrm{truth},k}
and

are both scalar maps that vary with sky position
\Omega
. If there are no

,
\langle\delta_{\mathrm{truth},k},\delta_{\mathrm{truth},k\prime}\rangle
would be the same for all

. Summing over all

is 0. However, this term is nonzero if we consider
sub-sample systematics
, especially for galaxy clustering: galaxies with similar apparent profiles (brightness, color, etc) are also similar in

and

. For these galaxies,
f_{k},f_{k^{\prime}}
are similar, leading to a positive
\langle f_{k},f_{k^{\prime}}\rangle
. Meanwhile,
\langle\delta_{\mathrm{truth},k},\delta_{\mathrm{truth},k\prime}\rangle
is also large due to their similarity in
n(z)
and
b(z)
. On the other hand, if the sub-sample

do not have similar apparent profiles,
\langle f_{k},f_{k^{\prime}}\rangle
could be small/negative and
\langle\delta_{\mathrm{truth},k},\delta_{\mathrm{truth},k\prime}\rangle
is small. Overall,
\langle\delta_{\mathrm{truth},k},\delta_{\mathrm{truth},k\prime}\rangle
serves as a ‘weight’ on
\langle f_{k},f_{k^{\prime}}\rangle
: it up-weights large/positive
\langle f_{k},f_{k^{\prime}}\rangle
and down-weights small/negative
\langle f_{k},f_{k^{\prime}}\rangle
, leading to a positive value on Equation

f_{k}
is particularly large for galaxies near the selection boundaries. The selection function of galaxy sub-sample
k
could generate large variations in
f_{k}
. For example, if
k
is detected in one region and undetected in another,
\langle f_{k},f_{k^{\prime}}\rangle
could be at the scale of
\sim O(1)
. On the other hand, galaxies far from selection boundaries have a small
\langle f_{k},f_{k^{\prime}}\rangle
.

Aside from the physically motivated reasoning above, we can also view this problem mathematically. The structure of this effect is the systematics map multiplied by the true galaxy density field. If we look at one

mode from the systematics map and another

mode from the true galaxy density field, the multiplication of these two mode is:

Y_{\ell m}Y_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}=\sum_{\ell^{\prime\prime}=|\ell-\ell^{\prime}|}^{\ell+\ell^{\prime}}C_{\ell m\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}^{\ell^{\prime\prime}m+m^{\prime}}Y_{\ell^{\prime\prime}m+m^{\prime}}

(19)

C_{\ell m\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}^{\ell^{\prime\prime}m+m^{\prime}}
is the

coefficient. It ranges from

to
\ell+\ell\prime
. Such structure suggests that for an observed

mode, it can get contaminated signal from all
\ell
s lower than, or similar to itself. At fixed

, the largest amplitude among all
\ell^{\prime\prime}
modes tend to occur at
\ell^{\prime\prime}=\ell+\ell^{\prime}
Edmonds 1996
. When we go to higher
\ell
s, more
\ell
modes contribute to the contamination signal, thus we see
|C_{\ell}|
biased high at large
\ell
. Since positive ones are up-weighted by
\langle\delta_{\mathrm{truth},k},\delta_{\mathrm{truth},k\prime}\rangle
, the stacked
C_{\ell}
for the whole galaxy sample is biased high.

Equation
19
suggests that low
\ell
modes in a systematics map contaminate more modes than high
\ell
modes. Indeed, the impact of
sub-sample systematics
is pattern dependent. In Appendix
D
, we provide toy models of
sub-sample systematics
assuming linear imaging systematics. The toy models suggest that the patterns of the systematics maps play an important role in determining the impact of
sub-sample systematics
.

II.2.3
Comparison with
1st order systematics
: additive vs Multiplicative

1st order systematics
that causes
n_{\mathrm{gal}}/\bar{n}_{\mathrm{gal}}
trends can be viewed as ‘additive’ to correlation function:
\delta^{\prime}\delta^{\prime}=\delta\delta+ff
. Here we naïvely denote the observed correlation function
\delta^{\prime}\delta^{\prime}
, the true correlation function
\delta\delta
, and the contribution of imaging systematics
ff
. Meanwhile,
sub-sample systematics
can be viewed as ‘multiplicative’:
\delta^{\prime}\delta^{\prime}=ff\cdot\delta\,\delta
.

The true clustering signal is exponentially small on large scales due to the nature of gravity, while
ff
does not have such a dramatic change with scales, leaving large-scale systematics mainly contributed by
1st order systematics
. The small-scale imaging systematics correction is typically a by-product of the correction of
1st order systematics
on large scales. Since the signal-to-noise of the small-scale clustering signal is much higher than the large-scale ones, the small-scale imaging systematics correction uncertainty induced by
1st order systematics
is typically considered small.

On the other hand,
sub-sample systematics
exerts a multiplicative effect on the correlation function. It scales with the true clustering signal so it is relatively small on large scales, potentially making it undetectable at those scales. However, it could cause a significant enough modification to the small-scale clustering signal. Without properly accounting for this effect, one may underestimate the error budget on small-scale clustering.

II.3
Anisotropic redshift and galaxy bias variation

We illustrate
sub-sample systematics
in Figure
1
: when we apply weights to a galaxy sample as a whole, the overall
n_{\mathrm{gal}}/\bar{n}_{\mathrm{gal}}
variation is properly corrected. However, the composition of each sub-sample still changes with the imaging systematics. This is parametrized as
f_{k}
in the previous section.

Figure 1
:
Illustration of how
sub-sample systematics
is formed. A galaxy sample is composed of multiple sub-samples defined by their intrinsic properties like flux, shape, etc. Here we show 3 sub-samples denoted as 1 for blue, 2 for black, and 3 for orange. Looking horizontally, the sub-samples respond differently to the imaging systematics map. The solid black line represents what we can observe: the
n_{\mathrm{gal}}/\bar{n}_{\mathrm{gal}}
fluctuation as a function of
x
. With an optimal imaging systematics correction treating the sample as a whole, this line becomes flat with
x
. However, the variation in sub-samples still exists. Looking vertically in the right figure, we see that the composition of different sub-samples changes with
x
. Since each sub-sample has a different redshift and galaxy bias distribution, this leads to a varying redshift and galaxy bias distribution across the entire footprint.

The galaxy sub-sample
k
is defined with a large number of parameters like the flux in each band, radius, ellipticity, etc. We can split one galaxy sample into numerous sub-samples. If we can accurately measure
f_{k}
for each sub-sample, then
sub-sample systematics
can be viewed as
multiplicative error
and properly corrected. However, measuring all
f_{k}
accurately is very challenging: if we split the galaxy sample into too many sub-samples, each sample has low density. The true galaxy clustering signal would leak into the measurement of
f_{k}
. The inability to measure
f_{k}
is the fundamental distinction between multiplicative error and
sub-sample systematics
. We need to find alternative ways to estimate and mitigate
sub-sample systematics
that does not require measuring all
f_{k}
.

For the interest of cosmological studies, we care about its impact on the measured galaxy correlation function. For a given galaxy tracer, only two parameters are of interest: the redshift distribution
n(z)
and the galaxy bias
b(z)
. Thus, we have an alternative way to describe
sub-sample systematics
:

The inhomogeneous distribution of galaxies’ redshift distribution
n(z,\textbf{sys})
and galaxy bias
b(z,\textbf{sys})

caused by imaging systematics
sys
The whole sample’s
n(z)
and
b(z)
distribution is still accurately measured
.

The two ways of describing
sub-sample systematics
can be seen in Figure
1
: looking horizontally, we see the density variation of different sub-samples
k
. This variation can be parameterized with
f_{k}
. Looking vertically, we see the composition of the galaxy sample changing with
sys
, inducing a spatially varying galaxy bias and redshift distribution. This can be parametrized with
n(z,\textbf{sys})
,
b(z,\textbf{sys})
.

Since we only care about measuring accurate correlation functions, parameterization with
n(z,\textbf{sys})
,
b(z,\textbf{sys})
is much easier to measure with real data. When dealing with real data, correlation functions are measured by counting all the galaxy-galaxy pairs
DD
at an angular separation
\alpha
. Then this value is normalized with uniform random pairs
RR
at the same footprint. When consider
sub-sample systematics
, the galaxy-galaxy pair counts
DD
at angular scale
\alpha
can be written as:

\sum_{IJ}D(\textbf{sys}(\Omega_{I}))D(\textbf{sys}(\Omega_{J}))\delta(||\Omega_{I}-\Omega_{J}||=\alpha)

(20)

Here the sum of I and J loops over the fully observed sky. The redshift and galaxy bias of
D
is a function of the systematics map value
sys
:

D(\textbf{sys}(\Omega_{I}))=D\left(b(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega_{I})),n(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega_{I}))\right)

(21)

The amplitude of the correlation function for galaxy clustering is proportional to:

b(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega_{I}))n(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega_{I}))b(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega_{J}))n(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega_{J}))

(22)

We can interpret Equation
20
as such: we go over full observed sky with
\Omega_{I}
. For each
\Omega_{I}
, we find all galaxies at sky positions
\Omega_{J}
that have angular scale
\alpha
with
\Omega_{I}
. The galaxy pair counts are reordered: when a galaxy finds its neighbors at a given separation
\alpha
, the expected sample statistics of these neighbors change from sample average
b(z),n(z)
to the systematics-altered sample statistics (
b(z,\textbf{sys}
),
n(z,\textbf{sys})
). We illustrate this effect in Figure
2
. The galaxy-galaxy pairs at
\alpha_{1}
are more similar to each other than the pairs at
\alpha_{2}
.

. The redshift and galaxy bias distribution changes with
\textbf{sys}(\Omega)

II.3.1
Galaxy correlation function: numerical expression

We assume that the
sub-sample systematics
is the only imaging systematics here. In other words, other systematics that produces
n_{\mathrm{gal}}/\bar{n}_{\mathrm{gal}}
trend have been properly corrected. We split the observed galaxy sample into N sub-samples based on how the imaging systematics shifts the selection function. As long as N is large enough, each sub-sample can be viewed as a clean, homogeneously distributed sample spanning its corresponding footprint.

We denote the observed tracers for each sub-region
A
as
D_{A}
. The region
A
confined by discretizing the systematics vector
sys
. Different
D_{A}
have different sample statistics. Its corresponding random sample is
R_{A}
.
R_{A}
is uniform random within the same footprint as
D_{A}
. The sum of all
R_{A}
is a uniform random sample across the whole observed footprint. Under this assumption, the observed galaxy sample is:

D_{\mathrm{obs}}=\sum_{\textbf{sys}=A}^{\textbf{sys}=N}D_{A}

(23)

We abbreviate
\sum_{I}

D(\textbf{sys}(\Omega_{I})=A)
as
D_{A}
. The corresponding randoms are:

R_{\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_{\textbf{sys}=A}^{\textbf{sys}=N}R_{A}

(24)

To elaborate,
D_{A},R_{A}
can be obtained in the following steps: first, we find the footprint
i
with imaging systematics value
\textbf{sys}=A
. Next, we populate uniform randoms in footprint and define the randoms as
R_{A}
. Meanwhile,
D_{A}
is all the galaxies within footprint.
R_{A}
is a uniform random sample having the same footprint as
D_{A}
. The correlation function of a galaxy sample is estimated with the Landy-Szalay
Landy and Szalay 1993
estimator:

w_{\mathrm{obs}}=\frac{D_{\mathrm{obs}}D_{\mathrm{obs}}-2D_{\mathrm{obs}}R_{\mathrm{tot}}+R_{\mathrm{tot}}R_{\mathrm{tot}}}{R_{\mathrm{tot}}R_{\mathrm{tot}}}

(25)

Replacing
D_{\mathrm{obs}}
and
R_{\mathrm{tot}}
with all the galaxy sub-samples with equation
23
and
24
:

w_{\mathrm{obs}}=\frac{\sum_{AB}^{N}(D_{A}D_{B}-D_{A}R_{B}-R_{A}D_{B}+R_{A}R_{B})}{R_{\mathrm{tot}}R_{\mathrm{tot}}}

(26)

We add
R_{A}R_{B}
in the denominator to construct correlation function for each sub-sample:

w_{obs}=\sum_{ij}^{N}\frac{R_{A}R_{B}}{R_{tot}R_{tot}}\frac{D_{A}D_{B}-D_{A}R_{B}-R_{A}D_{B}+R_{A}R_{B}}{R_{A}R_{B}}

(27)

Since each sub-sample is a clean and homogeneous sample,
(D_{A}D_{B}-D_{A}R_{B}-R_{A}D_{B}+R_{A}R_{B})/R_{A}R_{B}
is simply the true signal from cosmology, we denote it as
w_{AB}
. We define:

\mathrm{Win}_{AB}=\frac{R_{A}R_{B}}{R_{\mathrm{tot}}R_{\mathrm{tot}}}

(28)

\mathrm{Win}_{AB}
is the cross-correlation of the un-normalized window function for patch
A
and
B
. It serves as a ‘hypothetical window function’ that modifies the true correlation function signal as a function of
\alpha
. ‘Hypothetical’ comes from the fact that we do not measure this function for a real galaxy sample.

Equation
27
can be written in the form of:

w_{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha)=\sum_{AB}\mathrm{Win}_{AB}(\alpha)\cdot w_{AB}(\alpha)

(29)

w_{AB}(\alpha)
is the angular cross-correlation function of a galaxy sample with redshift
n(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega)=A)
and galaxy bias
b(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega)=A)
and another galaxy sample with
n(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega)=B)
,
b(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega)=B)
. The toy model in Section
III
is based on Equation
29
.

II.3.2
Galaxy correlation function: analytical expression

We can write the angular correlation function as:

w(\alpha)_{\mathrm{corrected}}=\\
\sum_{\ell}P_{\ell}(\cos\,\alpha)\int_{||\Omega_{1}-\Omega_{2}||=\alpha}dz\,d\Omega_{1}d\Omega_{2}M_{\ell}(z)\\
\cdot n(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega_{1}))n(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega_{2}))\\
\cdot b(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega_{1}))b(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega_{2}))/N(\alpha)

(30)

Here
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}
are sky angular coordinate (
\theta,\phi
).
M_{\ell}(z)
is a complicated function containing equations like the power spectrum P(k), Hubble parameter H(z), integrations over
k
, etc. It depends on the underlying cosmology, but it is independent of the properties of galaxy tracers.
N(\alpha)
is an normalization factor:

N(\alpha)=\int_{||\Omega_{1}-\Omega_{2}||=\alpha}d\Omega_{1}d\Omega_{2}

(31)

We define a parameter that quantifies
sub-sample systematics
:

\Delta K(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega))=\frac{n(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega))b(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega))}{n(z)b(z)}-1

(32)

This is effectively cross-correlating
\Delta K(z,\Omega_{1(2)})
with a constant field. If the
\Delta K
map does not contain
\ell=0
mode, then

\int_{||\Omega_{1}-\Omega_{2}||=\alpha}dz\,d\Omega_{1}d\Omega_{2}\cdot\Delta K\left(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega_{1(2)})\right)/N(\alpha)=0

(33)

\Omega_{1(2)}
means
\Omega_{1}
or
\Omega_{2}
. This is effectively cross-correlating
\Delta K(z,\Omega_{1(2)})
with a constant field.
\ell=0
corresponds to the mean of
\Delta K
. If it is non-zero, the result is a function independent of
\alpha

\int_{||\Omega_{1}-\Omega_{2}||=\alpha}dz\,d\Omega_{1}d\Omega_{2}\\
\cdot\Delta K(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega_{1(2)}))/N(\alpha)=\Delta\bar{K}(z)

(34)

Since we already assume that the mean of
n(z,\Omega
) is
n(z)
, and the mean of
b(z,\Omega)
is
b(z)
,
\Delta\bar{K}(z)
is small and likely negligible.
w(\alpha)_{\mathrm{corrected}}
can be re-written as:

w(\alpha)_{\mathrm{corrected}}=w(\alpha)_{\mathrm{truth}}+\sum_{\ell}P_{\ell}(\cos\,\alpha)\\
\int_{||\Omega_{1}-\Omega_{2}||=\alpha}dz\,d\Omega_{1}d\Omega_{2}M_{\ell}(z)\cdot n(z)^{2}b(z)^{2}\cdot\\
\left(2\Delta\bar{K}(z)+\Delta K(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega_{1}))\cdot\Delta K(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega_{2}))/N(\alpha)\right)

(35)

At fixed
z
, the
\theta,\phi
terms are the correlation function of
\Delta K(z)
:

w(\alpha)_{\mathrm{corrected}}=w(\alpha)_{\mathrm{truth}}+\\
\sum_{\ell}P_{\ell}(cos\,\alpha)\int dzM_{\ell}(z)\cdot n(z)^{2}b(z)^{2}\cdot\\
\left(2\Delta\bar{K}(z)+\langle\Delta K,\Delta K\rangle(z,\alpha)\right)

(36)

The second term shows that correlation function of
\Delta K
serves as a ‘weight’ inside the integral that computes the angular correlation function. Since the part related to underlying cosmology does not have a large variation, we can have a naïve estimate on the amplitude contributed by
sub-sample systematics
as:

A(\alpha)\sim\frac{\int dz\,n(z)^{2}b(z)^{2}\left(\langle\Delta K,\Delta K\rangle(z,\alpha)+2\Delta\bar{K}(z)\right)}{\int dz\,n(z)^{2}b(z)^{2}}

(37)

Practically, we can produce
\Delta K
maps for a set of redshift slices by estimating their redshift and galaxy bias variation as a function of imaging systematics. Then we use Equation
37
to have an order-of-magnitude estimate on
sub-sample systematics
.

As discussed in Section
II.2.2
,
sub-sample systematics
leads to an overestimation of the galaxy clustering correlation function amplitude. The same conclusion can be drawn more intuitively by modeling it with
\Delta K
, which reflects the anisotropic redshift and galaxy bias distribution. In Figure
2
, the systematics maps causing biased flux typically have a continuous, large-scale pattern. When a galaxy finds its corresponding pair, it tends to find galaxies similar to itself. At an angular scale
\alpha
of cosmological interest (
<5
degrees), the two
\Delta K
values at angular separation
\alpha
tend to have the same signs (both positive or both negative). As a result,
\langle\Delta K,\Delta K\rangle
tend to be positive. Consequently, the amplitude of the galaxy clustering correlation function is biased high. This effect is scale-dependent: it becomes more significant at smaller
\alpha
. Due to the patterned structure in the systematics map, galaxy pairs exhibit greater similarity on smaller scales. Its impact on cosmological inference, especially on neutrino mass estimation, is discussed in Section
V
.

Meanwhile, the galaxy-galaxy lensing (GGL) and the cosmic shear (CS) amplitude do not have a significant change with
sub-sample systematics
. Their clustering amplitude does not increase dramatically when changing the galaxy-galaxy pair ordering. We demonstrate with toy models in Section
IV.2
and Section
IV.3
.

IV
Analytical analysis of
sub-sample systematics
with toy models

If we draw a horizontal line in Figure
1
(right), this line could touch the dashed lines. The galaxy sample around this line is changed from one type to another as a function of the systematics map
sys
.
Sub-sample systematics
is formed by a collection of such change. Thus, we can investigate a ‘unit’ of
sub-sample systematics
: we consider a galaxy sample combining two sub-samples. The two types of galaxies in a galaxy sample have different galaxy bias and/or redshift distribution. The whole sample has no
n_{\mathrm{gal}}/\bar{n}_{\mathrm{gal}}
trend against the survey property map
sys
. However, only one sub-sample is seen at a given sky position, and it is determined by map
sys
. (Each sub-sample scales with a survey property map
sys
as a step function, and their trend is opposite). For simplicity, we also assume that the two tracers occupy the full sky.

Although the two sub-samples have
n_{\mathrm{gal}}/\bar{n}_{\mathrm{gal}}
trend against map
sys
, this trend is undetectable. We observe the two sub-samples as a whole. The observed sample, a combination of the two types of galaxies, does not exhibit a
n_{\mathrm{gal}}/\bar{n}_{\mathrm{gal}}
trend with this map
sys
. Imaging systematics mitigation methods that rely on probing
n_{\mathrm{gal}}/\bar{n}_{\mathrm{gal}}
trend against
sys
can not detect signals from this toy model.

In section
IV.1.1
, the two samples are correlated and share the same underlying matter density field. In section
IV.1.2
, the two samples are uncorrelated, meaning they are in different redshift ranges. Section
IV.2
considers the problem in the context of galaxy-galaxy lensing (GGL), and Section
IV.3
considers the problem in the context of cosmic shear (CS).

IV.1
redshift & galaxy bias shifts in Galaxy Clustering

IV.1.1
Shift in galaxy bias

We assume two galaxy tracers are in the same redshift range, but they have different galaxy bias
b_{1}
,
b_{2}
. For simplicity, we assume that the matter power spectrum of both tracers are proportional to the same power spectrum
P(k)
:
b_{1}^{2}P(k)
for tracer 1 and
b_{2}^{2}P(k)
for tracer 2.

If the two galaxy tracers are homogeneously distributed, the observed density field is:

\delta^{obs}_{tracers,v1}=h_{1}b_{1}\delta_{m}+h_{2}b_{2}\delta_{m}

(41)

h_{1}
,
h_{2}
are the fraction of the two tracers in the sample.
\delta_{m}
refers to the underlying matter density field. The same redshift distribution ensures that they have the same
\delta_{m}
. It is equivalent to a sample with a galaxy bias

b_{eff,v1}=h_{1}b_{1}+h_{2}b_{2}

(42)

On the other hand, if they are modified by map
sys
as a step function, the observed density field is

\delta^{obs}_{tracers,v2}=\begin{cases}b_{1}\cdot\delta_{m},&\textbf{sys}<=0\\
b_{2}\cdot\delta_{m},&\textbf{sys}>0\end{cases}

(43)

If we express the observed tracer, which is a sum of the two tracers, in the form of Landy-Szalay
Landy and Szalay 1993
estimator

w=\frac{(D_{1}+D_{2})^{2}-2(D_{1}+D_{2})(R_{1}+R_{2})+(R_{1}+R_{2})^{2}}{(R_{1}+R_{2})^{2}}

(44)

D_{1}
,
D_{2}
represents the associated tracers and
R_{1}
,
R_{2}
represents their corresponding randoms.

We define the window function between tracer
i
and
j
as:

\mathrm{Win}_{ij}=\frac{R_{i}R_{j}}{(R_{1}+R_{2})^{2}}

(45)

The window function has property:

\mathrm{Win}_{11}+\mathrm{Win}_{22}+\mathrm{Win}_{12}+\mathrm{Win}_{21}=1

(46)

\mathrm{Win}_{12}=\mathrm{Win}_{21}

(47)

\mathrm{Win}_{11}+\mathrm{Win}_{12}=\frac{R_{1}}{R_{1}+R_{2}}=h_{1}

(48)

\mathrm{Win}_{22}+\mathrm{Win}_{21}=\frac{R_{2}}{R_{1}+R_{2}}=h_{2}

(49)

We define

\Delta\mathrm{Win}_{1}=1-\mathrm{Win}_{11}/h_{1}

(50)

\Delta\mathrm{Win}_{2}=1-\mathrm{Win}_{22}/h_{2}

(51)

We have

h_{1}\Delta\mathrm{Win}_{1}=h_{2}\Delta\mathrm{Win}_{2}

(52)

Equation
44
can be expressed as:

w=\left(b_{1}^{2}\cdot\mathrm{Win}_{11}+b_{2}^{2}\cdot\mathrm{Win}_{22}\right.\\
\left.+b_{1}b_{2}\cdot(1-\mathrm{Win}_{11}-\mathrm{Win}_{22})\right)\cdot w_{m}

(53)

w_{m}
is the correlation function for the underlying matter density field.

The above equation can be re-written as

w=-(h_{1}b_{1}\Delta\mathrm{Win}_{1}-h_{2}b_{2}\Delta\mathrm{Win}_{2})(b_{1}-b_{2})\cdot w_{m}+\\
(h_{1}b_{1}^{2}+h_{2}b_{2}^{2})\cdot w_{m}

(54)

According to Equation
52
, this is

w=-h_{1}\Delta\mathrm{Win}_{1}(b_{1}-b_{2})^{2}\cdot w_{m}+(h_{1}b_{1}^{2}+h_{2}b_{2}^{2})\cdot w_{m}

(55)

If
h_{1}\Delta\mathrm{Win}_{1}
is small, this function is close to a galaxy sample with galaxy bias

b_{eff,v2}=(h_{1}b_{1}^{2}+h_{2}b_{2}^{2})^{1/2}

(56)

In fact, with this ‘hypothetical window’, the amplitude of the measured power spectrum is between
b_{eff,v1}
(Equation
42
) and
b_{eff,v2}
, and it depends on the distribution of tracer 1 and 2 in the footprint. We validate the derived equations with a toy mock in Appendix
E.1
.

IV.1.2
Shift in redshift

Following similar configurations as Section
IV.1.1
, we consider the two tracers with different redshift distribution. For simplicity, we assume the two tracers have no overlap in redshift space and thus are un-correlated with each other. If the two tracers are uniformly distributed, the observed density field is

\delta^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\mathrm{tracers}}=h_{1}b_{1}\delta_{1}+h_{2}b_{2}\delta_{2}

(57)

Here
\delta_{1}
,
\delta_{2}
are the underlying matter density field for tracer 1 and 2. For simplicity, we assume that the angular correlation function of

\delta_{2}
are equal.

\langle\delta_{1},\delta_{1}\rangle=\langle\delta_{2},\delta_{2}\rangle=w_{m}

This is not true for real galaxy samples, but we impose this assumption so that the conclusions in this Section can be compared to Section
IV.1.1
, where the tracers are correlated. Under our assumption, the angular correlation function of
\delta^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\mathrm{tracers}}
is

w=(h_{1}^{2}b_{1}^{2}+h_{2}^{2}b_{2}^{2})w_{m}

The effective galaxy bias for this combined sample is:

b_{eff,v3}=(h_{1}^{2}b_{1}^{2}+h_{2}^{2}b_{2}^{2})^{1/2}

(60)

On the other hand, if the tracers are modified by the survey property map

in the form of a step function, the observed density field is:

\delta^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\mathrm{{tracers}}}=\begin{cases}b_{1}\delta_{1},&\textbf{sys}<=0\\
b_{2}\delta_{2},&\textbf{sys}>0\end{cases}

(61)

We can express the angular correlation function in the same ways as Equation
44
(Landy-Szalay estimator). Since the two sample are uncorrelated,
w_{12}=w_{21}=0
, the angular correlation function is

w=w_{m}\cdot(b_{1}^{2}\mathrm{Win}_{11}+b_{2}^{2}\mathrm{Win}_{22})

(62)

A similar derivation as in Section
IV.1.1
gives us the observed angular correlation function

(63)

Here
\Delta\mathrm{Win}_{1}
is defined in Equation

. When

is small, the observed power spectrum is close to the power spectrum of a galaxy sample with bias

b_{eff,v4}=(h_{1}b_{1}^{2}+h_{2}b_{2}^{2})^{1/2}

(64)

Compared with

in Equation

sub-sample systematics
effect. For a more general case, the observed power spectrum has an amplitude between

and

For the toy model described in Equation

in Equation

IV.2

In the context of GGL

e_{t,ls}

Similar to the discussion in Section
IV.1.3
, if either the lens or source galaxies is a clean sample, or they do not have correlated ‘hypothetical window’, the signal would be the same as if they are homogeneously distributed. If they do have correlated ‘hypothetical window’, both lens and sources galaxies are modified by map

\gamma_{obs}=\sum_{ij=1}^{2}\mathrm{Win}_{ij}\gamma_{ij}

IV.2.1
Impact Estimation: negligible

Due to the structure of GGL, impact of

The power spectrum of GGL is

\chi

\Delta s_{i}^{t}/\chi_{j}^{{}^{\prime}0}

IV.4
Magnification and Intrinsic Alignment(IA)

The kernel used to compute the correlation function signal contributed by magnification is in a similar mathematical structure as GGL/CS. Thus, the impact of magnification is also negligible at first order. In addition, magnification is a small effect itself, so we can ignore the
sub-sample systematics
’s impact on magnification.

The kernel used to compute the correlation function signal contributed by IA is similar to galaxy clustering. Intuitively, we can think of a lens-source sample pair that has more radial overlap in some regions than others. This would produce larger intrinsic alignment signal than if they are homogeneously distributed. However, the impact of IA is small for standard cosmological probes. A percentage change on IA is negligible. Meanwhile, it could matter for new cosmological probes. For example,
Okumura and Taruya 2020
discusses the use of IA as a cosmological probe, and
Cross and Sánchez 2024
talks about inverse GGL, where IA is a dominant effect. It is necessary to estimate the impact of
sub-sample systematics
for these new cosmological probes.

VI
Estimation and Mitigation of
sub-sample systematics

It is important to have roughly estimate the impact of
sub-sample systematics
: is it a 0.1% impact or a 1% impact on the measurement of the correlation function? Having this level of estimation helps us determine whether it is necessary to incorporate it inside our cosmological inference. For example, we might have an error budget of 5% for a cosmological analysis, but
sub-sample systematics
, only contributes 0.1% of it. In this case, we can argue that
sub-sample systematics
is negligible for our analysis. Fully correcting
sub-sample systematics
is not possible because there are numerous types of galaxy sub-samples. However, we can make sure that the error contributed by
sub-sample systematics
is under control.

The toy model in Section
IV.1.2
is a good place to start. We can naïvely suppose that our sample have
h_{1}
‘outliers’ that only appear in some regions of the sky. These ‘outliers’ follow the toy model definition in Section
IV.1.2
. If
h_{1}=1\%
,
b_{1}=b_{2}
, the ratio of the clustering amplitude between the ‘observed’ sample and the ‘truth’ sample is:

\frac{w_{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha)}{w_{\mathrm{truth}}(\alpha)}=\frac{b_{\mathrm{eff,v4}}^{2}}{b_{\mathrm{eff,v3}}^{2}}=\frac{1}{h_{1}^{2}+h_{2}^{2}}\approx 1.02

(97)

The equations for galaxy bias are taken from Equation
60
and
64
. The clustering amplitude has a 2% change under this assumption.

In practice, we need more precise estimation on
sub-sample systematics
. Moreover, we need to mitigate such variation and control the impact of
sub-sample systematics
below a required error budget. The accuracy of
sub-sample systematics
determined by the estimation of galaxy bias
b(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega))
and redshift distribution
n(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega))
. Using these variables, we can have an order-of-magnitude estimation with Equation
37
or a more accurate estimation with Equation
29
.

The challenging part is to accurately estimate
n(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega))
and
b(z,\textbf{sys}(\Omega))
. In this Section, we discuss the methodology to measure these variables.

SSI produces synthetic galaxies that mimic the systematics in real sources. It can partially reproduce the

trends induced by the systematics that can be simulated by SSI. The trends that cannot be properly recovered are mainly due to uncertainties in the dust extinction map
E(B-V)
and zero-point calibration, since SSI does not know the ‘truth’ of these maps.
E(B-V)
and zero-point calibration error produce a shifted flux for all sources. If we do find some unrecoverable
n_{\mathrm{gal}}/\bar{n}_{\mathrm{gal}}
trends, we can empirically correct for the remaining trends by estimating the shifted flux as a function of relevant systematics maps.

VI.2
Estimating the amplitude of
sub-sample systematics

With synthetic sources produced from SSI, we can use them to benchmark analytic models that estimate the error for each galaxy. We can start from a ‘truth sample’, and add Gaussian noise and empirical flux shift to mimic the systematics trends seen in the synthetic galaxies produced by SSI. We do not expect these ‘analytical synthetic galaxies’ to perfectly mimic
n_{\mathrm{gal}}/\bar{n}_{\mathrm{gal}}
trends seen in real galaxies. As long as they resemble the
n_{\mathrm{gal}}/\bar{n}_{\mathrm{gal}}
trends, we can use these sources to estimate the variations in redshift
n(z,\Omega)
and galaxy bias
b(z,\Omega)
. We can get
n(z,\Omega)
trivially from the truth couterpart of the ‘analytical synthetic galaxies’. We further discuss the method to obtain
b(z,\Omega)
.

Both real and ‘analytical synthetic galaxies’ can be classified into different
Phenotypes
through self-organizing map (SOM)
Masters et al. 2015
. This technique is widely used in photometric redshift (photo-z) estimation
Buchs et al. 2019
. In the SOM photo-z method, synthetic galaxies before and after SSI are split into phenotypic cells
c,\hat{c}
.
c
is from a deep field which is used as ‘seed’ for sampling synthetic galaxies.
\hat{c}
is wide field. We can write the galaxy bias at cell
c
as:

p(b|c)=\sum_{\hat{c}}p(b|c,\hat{c})p(\hat{c}|c)

(98)

We approximate
p(b|c,\hat{c})
as
p(b|\hat{c})
, a parameter that can be measured. We do not need
p(b|c)
to be extremely accurate: we care about how galaxy bias varies across the footprint, and it is not a requirement to derive extremely accurate sky position independent parameter
p(b|c)
.

p(b|c)=\sum_{\hat{c}}p(b|\hat{c})p(\hat{c}|c)

(99)

p(\hat{c}|c)
is obtained from synthetic sources and it is called
survey transfer function
.
p(b|\hat{c})
can be obtained by the small-scale clustering amplitude in the wide data. Since cell
c
is narrow in redshift,
p(b|\hat{c})
can be approximated as redshift-independent. The galaxy bias at sky position
\Omega
is:

p(b(z)|\hat{s},\Omega)=\sum_{c,\hat{c}}p(b|c)p(c|\hat{c},\hat{s},\textbf{sys}(\Omega))p(\hat{c}|\hat{s},\textbf{sys}(\Omega))

(100)

With the measurement of
p(b(z)|\hat{s},\Omega)
,
n(z|\hat{s},\Omega)
,
we can estimate
\Delta K(z,\Omega)
discussed in Equation
32
.
sub-sample systematics
can be estimated numerically as discussed in Section
II.3.1
, or analytically as discussed in Section
II.3.2
.

Measuring cell
c
,
\hat{c}
has been a standard practice in the SOM photo-z method, as has already been implemented in the DESC photo-z pipeline RAIL
Busch et al. 2025
. This method leverages existing data products and requires minimal additional effort.

Finally, if
sub-sample systematics
, exceeded the error budget for any cosmological probes, we can split the galaxy sample into sub-samples, and correct the systematics trend for each sub-sample. Mitigating systematics trends for each sub-sample increases the risks of over-correcting the true clustering signal. Using synthetic galaxies instead of real galaxies can avoid this over-correction problem.

VII
Conclusion

We discussed
sub-sample systematics
: how a spatially varying galaxy bias and redshift distribution subtly change the observed correlation function. Unlike the more familiar 1st order imaging systematics, this effect does not produce a large, spurious signal on large scales. The amplitude of
sub-sample systematics
is proportional to the observed angular correlation function, making it more significant on angular scales with large correlation function amplitude. In contrast, the amplitude of the
1st order systematics
varies slowly across all angular scales. Since
sub-sample systematics
do not show alarming features on large scales, they are often ignored in current cosmological analysis.

We introduce the theory behind
sub-sample systematics
in Section
II
. Different galaxy types have different systematics trend. When correcting them as one sample, the corrected field has a variation in the composition of galaxy types. We demonstrate that
sub-sample systematics
can not be detected by cross-correlating the imaging systematics map with the observed galaxy density field. In other words, it does not show up as a trend in the plot of relative galaxy density
n_{\mathrm{gal}}/\bar{n}_{\mathrm{gal}}
versus systematics map value.
sub-sample systematics
always makes the observed galaxy clustering signal higher, and it scales up when going to smaller scales.

We define a parameter
\Delta K(z,\Omega)
(Equation
32
) which quantifies how
b(z),n(z)
varies with systematics.
\Delta K(z,\Omega)
can be used to estimate the impact of
sub-sample systematics
for any specific galaxy tracer.

Section
III
presents the methodology to estimate
sub-sample systematics
with a realistic galaxy sample. By measuring the hypothetical window functions defined in
28
, we derive the observed correlation function. We found that
sub-sample systematics
is only a concern for galaxy clustering, and it is more significant on small scales.

Section
IV
shows several toy models when two sub-samples have either different redshift or different galaxy bias. We discussed the situations for Galaxy Clustering, Galaxy-Galaxy Lensing (GGL), and Cosmic Shear (CS). We find that for GGL and CS, terms of
sub-sample systematics
cancels out until 2nd order. Meanwhile, the 2nd order terms galaxy clustering does not cancel out. This explains why
sub-sample systematics
is more significant for galaxy clustering.

Section
V
discusses impacts of the
sub-sample systematics
on cosmological measurements.
sub-sample systematics
can mimic the impact of massive neutrinos in the angular power spectra, which could, in principle, biase our results towards
m_{\nu}=0
, as shown in Figure
5
. This is particularly relevant in the context of DESI + CMB that have reported
m_{\nu}<0.07
, almost in tension with direct measurement of neutrino masses
Adame et al. 2025
. For
3\times 2PT
analysis,
sub-sample systematics
cause an inconsistent measurement of galaxy bias between galaxy-galaxy lensing and galaxy clustering. This is similar to the mysterious
X_{\mathrm{lens}}
factor found in the DES
redMaGiC
studies
Pandey et al. 2022
. It could bias
S_{8}
, a cosmological parameter that measures the clustering strength of matter in the universe.
S_{8}
is sensitive to the amplitude of the measured galaxy correlation function.
sub-sample systematics
produces scale-dependent bias similar to Primordial Non-gaussianity (PNG). The systematics of PNG could be dominated errors from
1st order systematics
or cosmic variance. However, it is necessary to estimate the error introduced by
sub-sample systematics
and determine whether it should be incorporated into the PNG parameter fitting.

Finally in Section
VI
, we discuss the question of ‘How do we estimate the impact of
sub-sample systematics
?’. Since estimating
sub-sample systematics
effect requires detailed knowledge about the imaging systematics effect for how
b(z),n(z)
varies with systematics, Synthetic Source Injection (SSI) is a promising tool to achieve this goal. We develop formalisims to estimate
sub-sample systematics
, utilizing the data products developed in the SOM photo-z method.

Appendix B
Weight-based systematics correction

Weight-based systematics correction is the most widely used method for imaging systematics correction. It derives a sky-position dependent imaging systematics weight
w_{\mathrm{sys}}
, and apply this weight to galaxies or their associated randoms to correct the density fluctuation caused by imaging systematics.

\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{corrected}}=w_{sys}\rho_{\mathrm{obs}}

(107)

\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{corrected}}
is the normalized corrected density field:

\langle\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{corrected}}\rangle=1

(108)

If the density fluctuation for all sub-samples are the same and
w_{\mathrm{sys}}

is accurate,
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{corrected}}
recovers the true galaxy density field. However, if different sub-samples have different systematics, the corrected galaxies density field is:

\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{corrected}}=w_{sys}\sum\rho_{\mathrm{truth},k}\prod_{i}(1+f_{k}^{i})

(109)

f_{k}^{i}
is defined in Equation
12
.
h_{k}
is the fraction of galaxy sub-sample k.

\rho_{\mathrm{truth},k}=h_{k}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{truth},k}

(110)

\sum h_{k}=1

(111)

\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{corrected}}=w_{sys}\sum_{k}\prod_{i}(1+f_{k}^{i})h_{k}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{truth},k}

(112)

Suppose that we have many realizations of true galaxies distributions (mocks), and these mocks have the same imaging systematics. For any given sky position, we take the average of all mocks:

\langle\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{corrected}}\rangle=w_{sys}\sum_{k}\prod_{i}(1+f_{k}^{i})h_{k}\langle\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{truth},k}\rangle

(113)

\langle\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{corrected}}\rangle=\langle\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{truth},k}\rangle=1

(114)

Thus, the systematics weight has a relationship with the systematics trends with sub-samples as:

w_{sys}=\frac{1}{\sum_{k}\prod_{i}(1+f_{k}^{i})h_{k}}

(115)

The density field for each corrected sub-sample is:

\rho_{\mathrm{corrected},k}=\frac{\prod_{i}(1+f_{k}^{i})}{\sum_{k}\prod_{i}(1+f_{k}^{i})h_{k}}\rho_{\mathrm{truth},k}

(116)

The systematics still exist for the sub-samples even after an ideal systematics correction.

Next, we compute the correlation function for this corrected galaxy density field:

\langle\delta_{\mathrm{corrected}},\delta_{\mathrm{corrected}}\rangle=\\
\langle w_{\mathrm{sys}}\sum_{k}h_{k}(\delta_{\mathrm{truth},k}+1)\prod_{i}(1+f_{k}^{i})-1,\\
w_{\mathrm{sys}}\sum_{k^{\prime}}h_{k^{\prime}}(\delta_{\mathrm{truth},k^{\prime}}+1)\prod_{i}(1+f_{k}^{i})-1\rangle

(117)

This equation can be decomposed into several terms:

\langle\delta_{\mathrm{corrected}},\delta_{\mathrm{corrected}}\rangle=\\
\langle w_{\mathrm{sys}}\sum_{k}h_{k}\prod_{i}(1+f_{k}^{i})\delta_{\mathrm{truth},k},w_{\mathrm{sys}}\sum_{k}h_{k}\prod_{i}(1+f_{k}^{i})\delta_{\mathrm{truth},k}\rangle\\
+\langle w_{\mathrm{sys}}\sum_{k}h_{k}\prod_{i}(f_{k}^{i}+1)-1,w_{\mathrm{sys}}\sum_{k}h_{k}\prod_{i}(f_{k}^{i}+1)-1\rangle

(118)

According to equation
115
, the second term is 0. This is the 1st order systematics term. It vanishes when
w_{\mathrm{sys}}
is accurate. We define a new term:

\mu_{k}=\frac{\prod_{i}(1+f_{k}^{i})}{\sum_{k}h_{k}\prod_{i}(1+f_{k}^{i})}=\frac{w_{\mathrm{sys}}}{w_{\mathrm{sys,k}}}

(119)

\mu_{k}
can also be seen as the global systematics weight
w_{\mathrm{sys}}
divided by the accurate systematics weight applied to sub-sample-
k

w_{\mathrm{sys,k}}
.
\mu_{k}
is internally related to each other:

\sum_{k}h_{k}(\mu_{k}-1)=0

(120)

According to equation
106
, un-correlated terms can be separated. Equation
118
is:

\langle\delta_{\mathrm{corrected}},\delta_{\mathrm{corrected}}\rangle=\\
\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}h_{k}h_{k^{\prime}}\langle\mu_{k},\mu_{k^{\prime}}\rangle\langle\delta_{\mathrm{truth},k},\delta_{\mathrm{truth},k^{\prime}}\rangle

(121)

We further isolate the true clustering signal:

\langle\delta_{\mathrm{corrected}},\delta_{\mathrm{corrected}}\rangle=\langle\delta_{\mathrm{truth}},\delta_{\mathrm{truth}}\rangle+\\
\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}h_{k}h_{k^{\prime}}\langle\mu_{k}-1,\mu_{k^{\prime}}-1\rangle\langle\delta_{\mathrm{truth},k},\delta_{\mathrm{truth},k^{\prime}}\rangle

(122)

We define:

f_{k}^{\mathrm{corrected}}=\mu_{k}-1

(123)

f_{k}^{\mathrm{corrected}}
is the systematics trend of galaxy sub-sample
k
after an optimal imaging systematics weight correction. In conclusion, the presence of sub-sample systematics trend modifies the corrected galaxy correlation function, and it can be parametrized with
f_{k}^{\mathrm{corrected}}
.

Appendix C
Correlation-function-based systematics correction

Correlation-function-based systematics correction applies imaging systematics correction on correlation-function level. The observed correlation function can be expressed as:

\langle\delta_{\mathrm{obs}},\delta_{\mathrm{obs}}\rangle=\\
\langle\delta_{\mathrm{truth}},\delta_{\mathrm{truth}}\rangle+\sum_{i,j}\langle f^{i},f^{j}\rangle+\sum_{i,j}\langle f^{i}\delta_{\mathrm{truth}},f^{j}\delta_{\mathrm{truth}}\rangle+...

(124)

We use superscript for
f^{i}
,
f^{j}
to reflect different types of systematics, e.g. depth, stellar density, etc. These methods relies on the fact that cross-correlating
f^{i}
with the observed galaxy density field gives the second term:

\langle f^{i},\delta_{\mathrm{obs}}\rangle=\sum_{j}\langle f^{i},f^{j}\rangle

(125)

The basic correction model ignores the 3rd terms and beyond, and assumes linear systematics
Elvin-Poole et al. 2018
;
Ross et al. 2011
;
Kalus et al. 2019
;
Alonso et al. 2019
;
Nicola et al. 2020
:

f^{i}(\textbf{sys}^{i})=a\cdot\textbf{sys}^{i}+b

(126)

Here
\mathrm{sys}_{i}
are the systematics values in survey property maps. Under these assumptions, the true galaxy density field
\delta_{\mathrm{truth}}
can be strictly solved. Several methods have been developed for such problem, and
Weaverdyck and Huterer 2021
proved that these methods are mathematically equivalent. For convenience, we only choose expression from Template Subtraction (TS,
Ho et al. 2012
) for further discussion. Within the TS context, the observed galaxy density field fluctuation is modified by imaging systematics map
i
with:

\delta_{\mathrm{obs}}=\delta_{\mathrm{TS}}+\sum_{i}\epsilon^{i}\cdot\textbf{sys}^{i}

(127)

\textbf{sys}^{i}
are the map values for a given systematics map, and its mean is shifted to 0.
\delta_{\mathrm{TS}}
is the corrected galaxy density field which is very similar to the truth.
\epsilon^{i}
is a constant value. Measuring the correlation function of
\delta_{\mathrm{obs}}
gives us:

C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{obs}}=C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{TS}}+\sum_{i,j}^{n}\epsilon^{i}\epsilon^{j}C_{\ell}^{ij}

(128)

Here
C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{obs}}
is the observed power spectrum,
C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{TS}}
is the TS-corrected power spectrum, and
C_{\ell}^{ij}
is the cross power spectrum between two survey property maps,
n
is the total number of used maps.
\epsilon^{i}
can be decomposed into different
\ell
modes, but it is typically considered as an
\ell
-independent parameter. The coefficients
\epsilon^{i}
are solved with a set of equations

C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{obs},i}=\sum_{j}^{n}\epsilon^{j}C_{\ell}^{ij}

(129)

C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{obs},i}
is the cross-power spectrum between observed galaxy density and survey property maps.
C_{\ell}^{TS}
need to be further de-biased
Elsner et al. 2016
to get the true power spectrum
C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{truth}}
:

C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{truth}}\sim C_{\ell}^{TS}\left(1-f^{2}_{\mathrm{sky}}\frac{n}{2\ell+1}\right)

(130)

Here
f_{\mathrm{sky}}
is the fractional area for this survey.
n
is still the total number of used maps. The solution with
\epsilon^{i}
depends on
\ell
. For linear imaging systematics mitigation, the mean of
\epsilon^{i}
across all
\ell
is chosen. Such a procedure avoids over-fitting the true cosmological fluctuations.

C.1
Template Subtraction with multiple sub-samples

If all galaxies respond to the survey property maps linearly,

\delta_{\mathrm{obs},k}=\delta_{\mathrm{TS},k}+\sum_{i}\epsilon_{k}^{i}\cdot\textbf{sys}^{i}

(131)

If we sum over all galaxy sub-samples
k
, and compute the power spectrum, we have

\langle\sum_{k}\delta_{\mathrm{obs},k},\sum_{k}\delta_{\mathrm{obs},k}\rangle=\\
\langle\sum_{k}h_{k}\delta_{\mathrm{TS},k}+\sum_{ki}h_{k}\epsilon_{k}^{i}\cdot\textbf{sys}^{i},\sum_{i}h_{k}\delta_{\mathrm{TS},k}+\sum_{ki}h_{k}\epsilon_{k}^{i}\cdot\textbf{sys}^{i}\rangle

(132)

h_{k}
is defined in Equation
7
.
\sum_{k}\delta_{\mathrm{obs},k}
is the observed galaxy density of the whole sample
\delta_{\mathrm{obs}}
. The above equation can be simplified as

\left\langle\delta_{\mathrm{obs}}\delta_{\mathrm{obs}}\right\rangle=\\
\left\langle\delta_{\mathrm{TS}}\delta_{\mathrm{TS}}\right\rangle+\sum_{ij}\left[\left\langle\textbf{sys}^{i},\textbf{sys}^{j}\right\rangle\sum_{k}h_{k}\epsilon_{k}^{i}\cdot\sum_{k^{\prime}}h_{k^{\prime}}\epsilon_{k^{\prime}}^{j}\right]

(133)

If we define a new coefficient

\epsilon^{i}=\sum_{k}h_{k}\epsilon_{k}^{i}

(134)

Then equation
133
can be transformed into the format for deriving the TS coefficient in equation
128
. Therefore, if all types of galaxies in a sample scale linearly with survey properly maps, then the validity of TS still holds, even if their response coefficient
\epsilon_{i}^{k}
is different. After applying linear imaging systematics weight to the galaxy sample as a whole, the slope of each sub-type galaxy and their densities have a certain relationship:

\sum_{k}h_{k}\epsilon_{k,\mathrm{corrected}}^{i}=0

(135)

In fact, this is a variant of equation
120
. In conclusion, if we ignore higher order terms in equation
124
,
sub-sample systematics
is also ignored as it belongs to higher order terms.

C.2
Multiplicative Error

Some methods also consider higher order terms in equation
124
. They are defined as multiplicative error. When considering one term higher, equation
124
becomes:

\langle\delta^{\mathrm{obs}},\delta^{\mathrm{obs}}\rangle=\\
\langle\delta_{\ell}^{\mathrm{truth}},\delta_{\ell}^{\mathrm{truth}}\rangle+\sum_{i,j}\epsilon^{i}\epsilon^{j}\langle\textbf{sys}^{i},\textbf{sys}^{j}\rangle+\\
\langle\delta^{\mathrm{truth}},\delta^{\mathrm{truth}}\rangle\sum_{ij}\epsilon^{i}\epsilon^{j}\langle\textbf{sys}^{i},\textbf{sys}^{j}\rangle

(136)

We cannot decouple this equation into distinct
\ell
modes due to the presence of the last term. We can replace the
C_{\ell}
s in the standard TS method with the angular correlation function
w(\theta)

Ross et al. 2011
. Solving this equation is non-trivial, but still possible.
Shafer and Huterer 2015
and
Weaverdyck and Huterer 2021
present methods to correct for the multiplicative error, and yield accurate estimates of
\epsilon^{i}
.

If we consider galaxy sub-samples, the last term in equation
136
becomes:

\sum_{ij}\left(\langle\textbf{sys}^{i},\textbf{sys}^{j}\rangle\sum_{kk^{\prime}}\epsilon^{i}_{k}\epsilon^{j}_{k^{\prime}}\langle\delta^{\mathrm{truth}}_{\mathcal{M}_{k}},\delta^{\mathrm{truth}}_{\mathcal{M}_{k^{\prime}}}\rangle\right)

(137)

Unlike equation
133
,
the subscript
k
,
k^{\prime}
here also exists inside the correlation function for sub-sample
\mathcal{M}_{k}
and
\mathcal{M}_{k^{\prime}}
. Thus, when considering higher order terms,
sub-sample systematics
can not be transformed into equation
136
. The correction methods that works for multiplicative error can not be applied to correct for
sub-sample systematics
.

In conclusion, the basic correction-function-based systematics correction methods correct the 1st order terms, leaving un-corrected 2nd order terms and beyond. By defining a new coefficient in Equation
134
,
sub-sample systematics
can be transformed into the original mathematical model. Thus, if 1st order accuracy is sufficient, it is unnecessary to consider
sub-sample systematics
. If we further consider 2nd-order terms,
sub-sample systematics
can not be mathematically transformed into the multiplicative error model.

Appendix D
Impact of
sub-sample systematics
with different systematics map patterns

sub-sample systematics
is a non-linear effect. Even if two different systematics maps cause the same level of galaxy density fluctuation, their impact on the observed correlation function can be every different. In this section, we use a toy model to visualize the impact of
sub-sample systematics
on a corrected sample with internal linear variation described by Equation
135
.

Suppose we have two galaxy samples produced by an underlying power spectrum
C_{\ell}
, and they are uncorrelated. They both have linear systematics trends against a systematics map. We perform a linear transformation to the systematics map to give a mean of 0 and a minimum of -1. We define this transformed map as
f
. The two galaxy samples are modified by
f
in an inverse direction:

\delta_{\mathrm{obs},\mathcal{M}_{1}}=\delta_{\mathrm{truth},\mathcal{M}_{1}}+f+f\cdot\delta_{\mathrm{truth},\mathcal{M}_{1}}

\delta_{\mathrm{obs},\mathcal{M}_{2}}=\delta_{\mathrm{truth},\mathcal{M}_{2}}-f-f\cdot\delta_{\mathrm{truth},\mathcal{M}_{2}}

\delta_{\mathrm{obs},\mathcal{M}_{12}}=\delta_{\mathrm{truth},\mathcal{M}_{12}}+f\cdot(\delta_{\mathrm{truth},\mathcal{M}_{1}}-\delta_{\mathrm{truth},\mathcal{M}_{2}})

(140)

We suppose that the observer can only see
\delta_{\mathrm{obs},\mathcal{M}_{12}}
and do not know that there are two galaxy samples internally varying. The observer can only check the

trend against

The correlation function of

\langle\delta_{\mathrm{obs},\mathcal{M}_{12}},\delta_{\mathrm{obs},\mathcal{M}_{12}}\rangle=\langle\delta_{\mathrm{truth},\mathcal{M}_{12}},\delta_{\mathrm{truth},\mathcal{M}_{12}}\rangle+\\
\langle f(\delta_{\mathrm{truth},\mathcal{M}_{1}}-\delta_{\mathrm{truth},\mathcal{M}_{2}}),f(\delta_{\mathrm{truth},\mathcal{M}_{1}}-\delta_{\mathrm{truth},\mathcal{M}_{2}})\rangle

(141)

The second term is non-linear and can not be trivially decomposed. We used a toy model to demonstrate its impact on the power spectrum.

We use a fiducial
C_{\ell}
to produce two uncorrelated galaxy fields. We test on two systematics maps. Systematics map 1 only has a large-scale mode at
\ell=2
, while systematics map 2 has small-scale modes. The power spectrum of the two systematics maps are in Figure

and

E.3
Discussion

That the window function for the ‘shift in redshift’ case has a larger impact than the ‘shift in galaxy bias’ case discussed in section
IV.1.1
. The first term divided by the second term here is

r_{z}=-h_{1}\Delta Win_{1}\cdot\frac{b_{1}^{2}+b_{2}^{2}}{f_{1}b_{1}^{2}+f_{2}b_{2}^{2}}

(145)

while for equation
55
, it is

r_{b}=-h_{1}\Delta Win_{1}\cdot\frac{(b_{1}-b_{2})^{2}}{f_{1}b_{1}^{2}+f_{2}b_{2}^{2}}

(146)

|r_{z}|>|r_{b}|
, meaning that this impact is always larger when the galaxy sample has a shift in redshift, rather than a shift in galaxy bias. This is also conceptually true. When there is a shift in redshift, the cross-correlation signals are all lost for the two tracers. When there is a shift in galaxy bias, the cross-correlation signal still partially remains. Since
r_{b}
and
r_{z}
scales with the ‘hypothetical window function’
h_{1}\Delta Win_{1}
, the model of two uncorrelated tracers is more sensitive to this window function.
