Latent neural architecture organising shared aesthetic evaluations of visual artworks
Authors: Xinyu Liang, Kaixiang Zhuang, Yun Wang, Yueting Su, Jianfeng Feng, Feng Zhou, Benjamin Becker, Deniz Vatansever
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAesthetic experience shapes behaviours ranging from everyday consumer choices to art appreciation. Although theoretical accounts propose that such experiences emerge from interactions among core brain systems, the dimensions organising this mental process remain unclear. Here, we introduce a data-driven framework to characterise the latent neural architecture of shared aesthetic evaluations. Participants viewed traditional paintings during 7T functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), and we applied dimensionality reduction to the similarity structure of their aesthetic ratings to identify the dominant axes of variation. Two principal dimensions emerged, visual semantics and hedonic valuation, each predicted by dissociable multivariate neural signatures. Category-selective regions along the ventral visual stream tracked variation in visual semantics, whereas medial prefrontal and subcortical circuitry tracked hedonic valuation. Moreover, individual differences in this latent aesthetic space, particularly within default mode network regions, scaled with visual art expertise. Together, these findings reveal how core brain systems synergistically organise shared aesthetic evaluations of visual artworks.Subject terms: Perception, Decision, Functional magnetic resonance imaging, Neural decoding

Introduction

Aesthetic experience arises when observers evaluate the aesthetic qualities of external stimuli, shaping behaviours as diverse as art appreciation, consumer choice, dietary habits, and engagement with social media platforms1–5. While individual differences in personal taste, artistic expertise, personality traits, and cultural backgrounds all give rise to a certain degree of variability in aesthetic experience6–9, particular domains of aesthetic evaluation consistently evoke similar responses across individuals and cultures. For instance, abstract or architectural stimuli elicit more variable responses, whereas natural landscapes or human faces often lead to consistent preference judgements10–12. Together, these findings suggest that, despite the influence of idiosyncratic factors, aesthetic evaluations and the experiences they facilitate might be grounded in shared neurocognitive processes.

In line with these behavioural observations, prior neuroimaging research has implicated value-sensitive regions, such as the medial prefrontal cortex and ventral striatum, in the aesthetic appreciation of diverse stimulus sets and across multisensory modalities, including visual art, music, and food13–18. Collectively, these findings support the hedonic valuation model, which conceptualises aesthetic experience as a form of reward processing localised in domain-general valuation circuits19,20. In addition, this model emphasises the integration of affective and perceptual information, suggesting that aesthetic value arises from interactions between brain regions computing hedonic value and those extracting sensory features from stimuli. Complementary to this account, the feature integration model proposes that visual artworks are initially parsed into discrete low- and high-level visual attributes (e.g., concreteness, symmetry, hue, contrast), which are then systematically integrated to generate an overall aesthetic response21,22. Across both models, core valuation circuitry is suggested to play a central role in assigning value to aesthetic stimuli based on the outcome of sensory and perceptual processing.

However, recent findings also suggest that aesthetic experience extends beyond the valuation of visual features to include the semantic content. For example, naturalistic scenes are often rated more positively than portraits or abstract images, and these preferences generalise across cultures and artistic media23,24. In contrast, abstract artworks show less intersubjective agreement, possibly due to variability in semantic interpretation6. These observations have led to emerging models that incorporate semantic cognition into aesthetic judgement, positing that meaning construction, grounded in memory, context, and conceptual knowledge, is integral to the evaluation of visual inputs25. Such models conceptualise aesthetic experience as an interaction between perceptual, semantic, and affective systems.

Despite varying emphases on specific neural circuits, a common thread across these current models is the view that aesthetic experience is an emergent property of the complex interactions among core neurocognitive systems involved in human mentation1,20,26, and that intersubjective agreement in evaluative judgements may provide a window onto the shared structure of that experience. Nevertheless, direct empirical evidence that decomposes aesthetic experience into its latent cognitive and neural components remains limited. Although traditional univariate approaches have allowed the localisation of brain activity related to aesthetic experience, they fail to disentangle overlapping cognitive processes or reveal how these processes jointly emerge from the shared structure of aesthetic experience across individuals.

To this end, recent developments in multivariate machine-learning methods offer powerful alternatives27,28, enabling the identification of distributed neural systems that predict complex mental states. In other contexts, these methods have been successfully applied to reveal robust neural signatures that predict pain29, fear30, disgust31, and pleasure32, while allowing researchers to distinguish highly interrelated processes, such as idiosyncratic experience and hardwired physiological responses33,34. Leveraging both dimensionality reduction and whole-brain modelling approaches, these techniques can effectively capture distinct neural representations underlying complex cognitive functions35,36. Thus, their application to neuroaesthetic investigations may allow for a more nuanced understanding of how distributed brain systems encode latent components of aesthetic experience in humans37.

Rather than assuming the existence of a specialised neural system, in this study we adopted a data-driven approach to identify the latent cognitive and neural dimensions that organise shared aesthetic evaluations of visual artworks. By characterising the similarity structure of aesthetic ratings and linking these dimensions to neural representations measured with 7T fMRI, our aim was to identify the dominant neurocognitive processes underlying participants’ aesthetic experience. Using methods inspired by online recommender systems38, we first constructed an aesthetic agreement matrix to capture the structure of shared evaluative consistency across participants. Decomposition of this matrix identified a low-dimensional architecture of shared aesthetic evaluations comprising two principal components: visual semantics and hedonic value. Multivariate decoding methods revealed that systematic variation along the visual semantics axis was largely predicted by category-selective regions in the ventral visual stream, whereas hedonic valuation recruited medial prefrontal, subcortical reward, and early visual regions. These neural signatures were highly specific and generalisable to predict category-selective and valence-related responses in independent datasets. Importantly, individual differences in the representation of this two-dimensional latent aesthetic space, particularly within key regions of the default mode network, was modulated by expertise in visual arts, highlighting both shared and idiosyncratic aspects of aesthetic evaluation. Collectively, these findings provide empirical support for a low-dimensional, distributed neural architecture underlying aesthetic evaluations of visual artworks. By bridging perceptual, semantic, and reward-related processes, our results refine existing neuroaesthetic models and advance a computational framework for understanding how shared aesthetic experiences are represented in the human brain.

Results

With the primary aim of investigating the latent neural architecture of shared aesthetic experiences, we curated 96 watercolour artworks depicting portraits and landscapes by four prominent Chinese artists (Supplementary Table 1). During ultra-high-field 7T fMRI scanning, participants passively viewed these artworks while attending to their aesthetic appeal.

After scanning, participants provided aesthetic appeal ratings for each image based on their aesthetic experience during fMRI scanning. Using an approach inspired by item-to-item collaborative filtering commonly employed in online recommender systems38, we then constructed an aesthetic agreement matrix based on between-image similarity of intersubjective ratings, which was reduced to low-dimensional latent components via principal component analysis (PCA). The top two components were quantised and used in multivariate neural decoding of image-specific fMRI responses to identify robust neural signatures of aesthetic experience. In addition to functional decoding, we tested the specificity and generalisability of these neural signatures in independent tasks and datasets. Finally, representational similarity analysis (RSA) assessed how individual differences in the neural encoding of this latent aesthetic space related to expertise in visual arts (Fig. 1a–e).

Decomposition of shared aesthetic experience reveals two core cognitive components

Building on the principle that stimuli eliciting similar aesthetic responses engage overlapping cognitive processes39, we adapted item-to-item collaborative filtering algorithms, widely used in online recommender systems for inferring latent user preferences, to characterise the structure of shared aesthetic experience. Specifically, we constructed an aesthetic agreement matrix that quantifies the similarity of aesthetic appeal ratings across all pairs of stimuli, based on participants’ subjective evaluations of artwork images. The Pearson correlation coefficient was employed for each image pair to index the degree of agreement across participants. Higher correlations indicate that participants tended to evaluate these images similarly, reflecting shared aesthetic appeal at the group-level, whereas lower or negative correlations suggest more divergent evaluative judgements. This matrix demonstrated high reliability across split-half and bootstrap resampling procedures (Supplementary Fig. S1). Importantly, it systematically aligned with objective measures of between-image similarity in visual (r = 0.15, p < 0.001), semantic (r = 0.34, p < 0.001), and affective features (r = 0.20, p < 0.001) extracted from independent AI algorithms (Supplementary Notes; Fig. S2), providing an indirect window onto the latent components that shape shared aesthetic experience.

The data-driven decomposition of this aesthetic agreement matrix using PCA revealed two significant, orthogonal (r = −2.30 × 10-¹⁷, p = 1) and stable cognitive components that jointly explained 27% and 21% of the total variance (eigenvalues = 1.15 and 0.90), respectively, as determined by parallel analysis and Jolliffe’s criterion (Supplementary Notes; Figs. S3–4). Qualitative inspection of this low-dimensional latent aesthetic space revealed that the identified components represented the artworks based on their perceived similarity in visual semantic (i.e. portraits to scenes) and hedonic value-based (i.e. commonly disliked to liked) attributes (Fig. 2a), highlighting the importance of these two dimensions in organising shared variance in aesthetic evaluative structure across participants.

To further validate the behavioural interpretation of these dimensions through triangulation, we conducted analyses investigating the association between the two core component scores and auxiliary measures. The results confirmed that the first component in this latent aesthetic space represents a dimension encoding visual semantics (Fig. 2b), showing significant association with the principal component of semantic similarity ratings obtained from an independent multi-arrangement task (r = 0.80, p < 0.001; Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. S5). For the second component, the results revealed a significant correlation with the group-average subjective appeal ratings (r = 0.40, p < 0.001; Fig. 2e), indicating a dimension of hedonic value that is shared across participants (Fig. 2c). These associations remained significant after controlling for nine canonical low-level features known to influence aesthetic judgements40,41, including root-mean-square contrast, lightness entropy, complexity, edge density, balance, mirror symmetry, sparseness, variability, and colour entropy (radj =0 .65, p < 0.001; radj = 0.31, p = 0.004). Importantly, the two dimensions showed a double-dissociation. No significant correlations were observed between the semantic dimension and group-averaged aesthetic ratings (r = 0.15, p = 0.14; Fig. 2e) or between the value dimension and semantic similarity scores from the multi-arrangement task (r = 0.01, p = 0.96; Fig. 2d).

In addition to these assessments, we also examined the relationship between the identified latent components with 831 objective image features, comprising low-level visual, high-level semantic, and affective descriptors derived from AI-based algorithms. We found that the first component was significantly associated with 15 low-level features (e.g., lightness entropy, edge density, colour, symmetry, 3D fractal dimension, self-similarity, homogeneity, and variability), four affective categories (related to awe, boredom, romance, and sexual desire), and 244 high-level semantic embeddings (31.77% of the total dimensions). In contrast, the second component showed no significant association with any of the extracted feature sets (Supplementary Fig. S6). Furthermore, we computed an independent aesthetic value metric for each image using a deep neural network (DNN) pre-trained on an independent dataset42 and examined their association with scores across the identified latent components of aesthetic experience. This analysis revealed a positive correlation between the DNN-based aesthetic value score and the hedonic value dimension (r = 0.23, p = 0.03; Supplementary Fig. S7), but not with the visual semantics dimension (r = −0.03, p = 0.74). Collectively, our findings highlight a latent aesthetic space underlying shared aesthetic evaluation across participants that is composed of visual semantics and hedonic value-based attributes of visual artworks, two mental processes that constitute crucial aspects of aesthetic experience.

Dissociable neural signatures predict latent components of aesthetic experience

Given the importance of the identified low-dimensional attributes in describing participants’ aesthetic experiences, our next objective was to reveal robust neural signatures that reliably predict systematic variations across the two latent components of aesthetic experience. For that purpose, we generated trial-specific fMRI signal amplitude estimates reflecting participants’ neural responses during aesthetic appreciation of artwork images at 7T, and employed a machine learning-based multivariate pattern analysis method to decode brain responses predictive of core components organising shared aesthetic evaluations across participants. Specifically, we used support vector regression (SVR) to identify whole-brain patterns of fMRI activity that predicted four quartile levels of both visual semantics (Fig. 3a) and hedonic value component scores (Fig. 3b), and employed a bootstrap method to reveal the brain regions that made significant contributions to the prediction model.

The results demonstrated that the trained SVR model could accurately predict the quartiles of semantic dimension in the sample set, ranging from Levels 1 to 4 (i.e. portraits to landscapes). The overall Pearson correlation between predicted and true values reached 0.83 (averaged across ten repetitions, 95% Confidence Intervals = [0.83, 0.83]; one-tailed permutation test, p < 0.001; overall root-mean-square error [RMSE] of 0.62) (Fig. 3c), with an internally computed correlation of 0.88 ± 0.24 and RMSE of 0.58 ± 0.24 among participants. To further determine the sensitivity of the prediction model, a two-alternative forced-choice test was applied, comparing pairs of activation maps within each subject and choosing the one with higher activity patterns as the higher level. The model accurately classified high (Level 4) versus moderate (average of Levels 2 and 3) and moderate versus low (Level 1) with 94 − 98% accuracy, and high versus low with 97% accuracy. Moreover, the model could distinguish each successive pair of semantic levels (e.g., Level 1 versus 2) with ≥ 82% accuracy, which was significantly better than chance (50%; p < 0.001).

Next, we applied the neural signature of visual semantics to time series data using the dot-product method to determine its specificity to the processing of semantic information. Inspection of the semantic signature reactivity at each time point following stimulus onset indicated that the semantic response began approximately 2 s following image onset and increased with increasing component levels during approximately 6–9 s. This finding provides further evidence that this neural signature captures semantic information processing within the overall aesthetic experience, as opposed to anticipation (pre-stimulus).

For the hedonic value attribute, we trained another SVR model to predict the four quartile levels of variation across the value dimension scores. This neural signature also demonstrated good predictive performance, with an overall correlation of 0.65 (95% Confidence Intervals = [0.64, 0.66]; one-tailed permutation test, p < 0.001) and an overall RMSE of 0.90 (Fig. 3d). The internally computed correlation among participants was 0.67 ± 0.37, with an average RMSE of 0.88 ± 0.20. Regarding the sensitivity of the prediction model, this model accurately classified high (Level 4) versus moderate (average of Level 2 and 3) and moderate versus low (Level 1) with 81 − 87% accuracy, and high versus low with 91% accuracy. Moreover, the model could distinguish the higher successive pair of value levels (e.g., Level 2 versus 3, and Level 3 versus 4) with ≥ 78% accuracy, which was significantly better than chance level (50%; p < 0.01). However, this model performed worse for the pair of lowest levels (Level 1 versus 2), with predictive performance remaining at chance level (50%). For the specificity of the hedonic value signature to aesthetic experience, we also explored its reactivity over the course of stimulus presentation. In line with the standard hemodynamic model, the signature response started approximately 2 s following the image onset and increased during 6–10 s, indicating the reactivity of this value signature to both aesthetic perception and evaluation.

Neural signatures of aesthetic experience generalise to core cognitive processes

In order to further validate the specificity of the identified neural signatures, we first conducted a cross-prediction analysis. In this analysis, the signature derived from one component was used to predict quartile levels of the other component. Based on a permutation test with 1000 iterations, we generated a null distribution of cross-prediction differences to assess the significance of the observed dissociation. The results showed significantly reduced performance in cross-prediction compared to that of the original prediction model for both conditions (semantic signature on value-based levels: 10-fold correlation of 0.08 ± 0.03, 95% Confidence Intervals = [0.06, 0.09]; difference compared to the original model: 95% Confidence Intervals = [-0.73,-0.77], t(9) = -94.25, p < 0.001; value signature on semantic levels: 10-fold correlation of 0.05 ± 0.03, 95% Confidence Intervals = [0.02, 0.07]; difference compared to the original model: 95% Confidence Intervals = [−0.58, −0.63], t(9) = -47.66, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. S8). These findings indicate that semantic and hedonic value signatures rely on distinct neural systems, which preferentially support predictions of their respective cognitive dimensions, with non-interchangeable roles.

An important feature of the identified neural signatures is that their performance can be independently assessed in other datasets and paradigms, not only providing an assessment of generalisability but also aiding in their cognitive interpretation. To this end, we used 100 unrelated participants from the Human Connectome Project (HCP)43 as a generalisation dataset. Specifically, we computed the expression of the identified neural signatures across contrasts of places versus faces and valence versus neutral conditions that were extracted from working memory and gambling tasks in the HCP, respectively (Supplementary Notes; Supplementary Fig. S9). These two contrasts were designed to assess category-selective representations and monetary reward processes in the human brain, which correspond to the two identified latent dimensions in this study, i.e. visual semantics and hedonic value.

For the places versus faces contrast, the expression was positive for both neural signatures (semantics: t(99) = 28.77, p < 0.001; value: t(99) = 9.96, p < 0.001), with a significantly stronger response observed for the visual semantics signature (t(99) = 20.04, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3f). By comparison, for the valence versus neutral contrast denoting the processing of monetary rewards, only the hedonic value signature exhibited a significantly positive response (t(99) = 3.59, p < 0.001). The response of the visual semantics signature was negative (t(99) = -4.41, p < 0.001) and significantly lower than that of the hedonic value signature (t(99) = -6.54, p < 0.001).

Overall, these findings indicate that in an independent generalisation dataset with cognitive paradigms designed to localise distinct neural processes, the neural signature of visual semantics responded specifically and robustly to the visual categories of places versus faces, while the neural signature for hedonic value responded to monetary valence.

Neural signatures of aesthetic experience are linked to distributed neural systems

Following the assessment of model specificity and generalisability, we next determined individual brain regions that consistently and reliably contributed to the whole-brain neural prediction models by using the bootstrap procedure (FDR q < 0.05; Supplementary Table 2)27. At the regional level, we identified brain areas that significantly contributed to the prediction of four distinct levels along the semantic dimension (Fig. 3a). These were primarily located within ventral visual pathway, which is implicated in category-selective classification of places versus faces44,45. Specifically, we observed positive contributions in the parahippocampal area (PHA) and ventral visual complex (VVC) and negative contributions were observed in the fusiform face complex (FFC). Positive contributions were also found in regions within the dorsal visual pathway associated with the processing of visuospatial information, including the inferior parietal cortex, medial intraparietal area, and intraparietal area, as well as retinotopic visual regions (V1) responsible for early visual processing.

In addition, the prediction model for visual semantics also emphasised the engagement of regions comprising the action observation network46. These included regions involved in eye movement-related processing, such as the ventral intraparietal complex in the parietal cortex and the premotor eye field (PEF) in the premotor cortex, as well as regions sensitive to motion, including the middle temporal area and medial superior temporal area. Beyond visual and somatosensory regions, however, several regions belonging to the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) and anterior insula also played a notable role in predicting semantic levels. Among subcortical regions, significant positive contributions were predominantly located in the right anterior hippocampus and the left posterior globus pallidus. In contrast, significant negative contributions were identified in the right nucleus accumbens, the left posterior caudate nucleus, and bilateral posterior globus pallidus (Supplementary Fig. S10a).

For the hedonic value signature, we identified brain regions that positively contributed to the prediction of four levels (FDR q < 0.05; Supplementary Table 3), primarily located in the prefrontal cortices associated with value-based decision-making (Fig. 3b). Specifically, positive contributions were observed in the ventral and anterior mPFC (areas 10r and 9 m) and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC; including areas a24, p24, p32 and s32). In contrast, regions in the posterior orbitofrontal cortex (pOFC) and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) exhibited negative contributions. As key nodes of the reward circuitry, regions in both the dorsal and ventral striatum were also engaged in predicting value levels. Significant positive contributions were found in the right nucleus accumbens, left posterior caudate nucleus, left putamen, and left anterior thalamus, while significant negative contributions were observed in the right anterior hippocampus and right posterior caudate nucleus (Supplementary Fig. S10b). The bilateral lateral amygdala, a major centre for emotion processing, also displayed significant negative contributions. Beyond regions implicated in reward and emotion processing, many areas within visual cortices were also involved in value prediction. Strong negative contributions were observed in early visual cortices, including V1, V2, V3 and V4, as well as the visual pathway for social perception, encompassing the temporo-parieto-occipital junction (TPOJ) and superior temporal visual area (STV). Conversely, positive contributions were found in regions of both the ventral and dorsal visual pathways, particularly in the intermediate areas, such as the VVC between the FFC and PHA, the ventromedial visual cortex (VMV) and the intra-parietal sulcus (IPS).

Certain brain features that significantly contribute to the multivariate models may capture sources of noise in the data rather than being directly related to cognitive processing47. To provide a more transparent comparison between univariate and multivariate results, we calculated Haufe-transformed activation patterns (i.e. forward models), which assess the relationship between each grayordinate and the response (fitted values) in the multivariate model30. Thus, regions that exhibit significant predictive weights and Haufe-transformed patterns are both considered important in the final multivariate model47,48. A formal assessment of the overlap showed that virtually all regions with consistent and significant model weights also showed significant activation patterns in the multivariate models (Supplementary Fig. S11).

Functional decoding of latent components underlying shared aesthetic experience

Having identified two robust neural signatures that predict component scores across the two latent dimensions of aesthetic experience, our next aim was to provide further validation to the interpretation of these neurocognitive components. To achieve this, we first examined the pattern of signature expression across artwork images within each quartile and within each thematic category. We then functionally decoded the identified neural signatures using an independent functional localiser task, topic-term-based meta-analyses, as well as large-scale intrinsic brain networks.

To begin with, specific artwork images that showed the highest reactivity to the identified neural signature within each quartile provided further cognitive interpretation to our results. For the visual semantics component, we found that Levels 1–4 ranged from: All portrait, Mostly portrait, Mostly landscape, All landscape images (Fig. 4a). While the bottom and top two levels consisted of all portrait and all landscape images, respectively, the middle two levels depicted both portraits and landscapes together with their relative contribution matching the associated quartile. For the hedonic value component, we found that the artwork images were divided into Disliked, Mostly disliked, Mostly liked, Liked images. Levels 3 and 4 contained artwork images of both landscapes and portraits with sparsity, warm colours and soft and fluent lines, in contrast to the dim colours and complex but short lines represented in Levels 1 and 2 (Fig. 4c). Overall, the results indicate that the identified neurocognitive components encode information that continuously varies across visual semantics and hedonic value.

To ensure that the recovered neural signatures were not driven solely by categorical differences between overall themes captured by the stimulus set, we conducted additional analyses evaluating signature expression within portrait and within landscape stimuli, separately. For the semantic axis, signature expression systematically increased along the first principal component both within portraits (r = 0.46, p = 0.001) and within landscapes (r = 0.59, p < 0.001). Similarly, the hedonic value signature expression also showed a linear increase along the second component both within portraits (r = 0.72, p < 0.001) and within landscapes (r = 0.74, p < 0.001). These results indicate that the recovered neural axes capture continuous variation within both themes of artworks included in this study rather than only reflecting differences between thematic categories (Supplementary Fig. S12).

When further evaluating the construct validity of the identified neural signatures, we employed the brain maps as positive and negative masks in a functional localiser task49 that investigated category-selective visual responses to faces, body parts, objects, and places (Supplementary Fig. S13). For the neural signature predicting visual semantics, positively contributing vertices showed significantly greater activity to places relative to all other categories, while showing stronger deactivation for faces than for the remaining categories (F(3,99) = 18.92, p < 0.001). The negatively contributing vertices, however, selectively responded to face and body categories while showing stronger deactivation to place category (F(3,99) = 30.37, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4b). The topic-term-based meta-analytical functional decoding corroborated these findings50. The positively contributing brain regions within the neural signature were mostly associated with visual cognition, such as visual scene, visual objects, and early visual function. In contrast, the negatively contributing brain regions were strongly associated with face recognition and bodily awareness. These results provide additional validation that the identified neural signature was primarily associated with visual semantics.

We performed the same analyses to evaluate the construct validity of the hedonic value signature. As expected, when applying the masks derived from hedonic value signature to the individual contrasts in the functional localiser task, the results did not show a clear dissociation in their category-specific activation patterns (Fig. 4d). Vertices in the positive mask showed minimal response to body, object and place categories but were strongly deactivated for face category (F(3,99) = 14.69, p < 0.001), while vertices in the negative mask showed no responses to any category (F(3,99) = 2.19, p = 0.09). We further explored the link between the hedonic value signature and topic-based meta-analytic maps. The positively contributing brain regions were mostly associated with cognitive and affective processes including decision making, problem solving, affective pictures, creative thinking, reward anticipation, judgement, working memory, and food and eating. In contrast, the negatively contributing brain regions were strongly associated with visual processes, including early visual, visual objects, visual scene, visual perception, face recognition, and sensory integration (Fig. 4f). These results suggest that the identified neural signature was primarily associated with valuation- and reward-related processes as well as primary sensory functions.

With the aim of further exploring the spatial pattern of signatures at the level of macro-scale neural organisation, we analysed the distribution of signature values using the widely adopted 7-network parcellation51,52. For both neural signatures, the visual and dorsal attention networks showed the widest distribution, contributing to both positive and negative model weights. Specifically, within the semantic signature, the somatosensory network showed relatively stronger contribution towards the positive end, while the default mode network was skewed towards the negative end. Within the hedonic value signature, however, the positive model weights were dominated by the default mode network, while negative weights were mostly within the ventral attention network.

Overall, the results of this functional decoding analysis highlighted that the two core cognitive components and the associated brain regions are robust and reliable predictors of visual semantic and hedonic value-based information, respectively, which collectively make a significant contribution to the expression of aesthetic experience.

Neural representation of the latent aesthetic space is modulated by expertise in arts

Thus far, our analyses have independently focused on two latent components underlying aesthetic experience. However, aesthetic appreciation is postulated to require the dynamic interaction of core cognitive components to handle multidimensional information, suggesting that the latent aesthetic space formed by these intrinsic dimensions may also be informative for uncovering, as a whole, the neural mechanisms underlying shared aesthetic experiences.

In order to further characterise the brain activity patterns representing this two-dimensional latent aesthetic space, we performed an RSA using a surface-based searchlight method28,53. The Euclidean distances between any pair of images in the aesthetic space were calculated, serving as a target representational dissimilarity matrix for the representational geometry. Using RSA applied to fMRI response patterns evoked by each artwork image, we found that the level of pattern dissimilarity in widespread cortical regions was robustly related to the pairwise Euclidean distances between images in the latent aesthetic space (Fig. 5a; see details in Supplementary Table 4). To account for both spatial autocorrelation and multiple comparisons across the searchlights, a permutation-based Threshold-Free Cluster Enhancement (TFCE) approach (5000 iterations) was used to identify significant clusters at the group-level54.

Significant brain regions were primarily located in the visual cortex, encompassing the dorsal pathway, the ventral pathway and the social perception pathway. We also observed a right-lateralized cortical pattern involving regions implicated in body-specific representations. This pattern included significant neural representation of the two-dimensional latent space within the inferior frontal junction (IFJ), IFS, FEF, PEF, and lateral premotor cortex (area 6) in the right hemisphere. Conversely, significant patterns with the opposite lateralisation were observed in the left posterior cingulate cortex (area 23). In addition, significant representations were also detected in memory-oriented and navigation-related regions, including the bilateral hippocampal cortices, the entorhinal (EC) and perirhinal entorhinal cortices (PeEC). For subcortical structures, we applied a regional RSA method based on the Melbourne Subcortex Atlas55. Significant representations of the geometry of latent aesthetic space (FDR q < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. S14 and Table 5) were observed in several bilateral subcortical regions, including the anterior and posterior thalamus, medial amygdala, and posterior hippocampus. Additional significant representations were found in the anterior and posterior putamen, anterior hippocampus, posterior caudate nucleus, and the core of nucleus accumbens in the left hemisphere, as well as in the lateral amygdala in the right hemisphere. These results were replicated using alternative distance metrics (e.g., Mahalanobis distance) and varying searchlight sizes, indicating the robustness of the observed patterns (Supplementary Notes; Figs. S14–S16, and Tables 6-7).

In addition to significant group-level results, the neural representation of this latent aesthetic space showed variation across individuals. In order to further investigate this, we performed an exploratory analysis to test whether this variation in neural representation of the latent aesthetic space was shaped by the participants’ expertise in visual arts. We adopted a self-report questionnaire to assess the art expertise of all participants (total questionnaire scores: mean ± SD = 6.65 ± 5.93, median = 5.0), including 10 questions about their background in art, art history and art theory, and frequency of appreciating visual arts56. We extracted the first principal component score (which explained 59% of total variance; mean ± SD = 0 ± 2.30, median = -0.60; Fig. 5b) to be used as the variable of interest in a general linear model (GLM), while correcting for age and sex of the participants.

The results showed that representations in key brain regions belonging to the default mode network, such as the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) and the mPFC, as well as in the ventral visual pathway, was positively influenced by art expertise (Fig. 5c; Supplementary Table 8). We further identified three peaks (FDR q < 0.05), located in the left inferior parietal cortex (area PFop, PGi and STV) and the right ACC (area s32), which were significantly correlated with individual differences in art expertise (Fig. 5d). Overall, these results not only indicate that the latent aesthetic space formed by core components of aesthetic judgements is dimensionally encoded, but also provide evidence that such representation is influenced by participants’ expertise in visual arts.

Discussion

In this study, our primary aim was to decompose the experience of visual aesthetics into dissociable cognitive components and uncover their corresponding neural substrates. Adapting methods employed in online recommender systems, we identified a low-dimensional architecture of shared aesthetic evaluations comprising two latent components: visual semantics and hedonic valuation. Using ultra-high-field 7T fMRI alongside multivariate decoding approaches, we mapped these components to distinct but interacting neural systems. While visual semantics was associated with category-selective regions along the ventral visual stream, hedonic valuation engaged the medial prefrontal cortex, subcortical reward structures, as well as primary visual regions. These robust neural signatures not only showed high specificity, but also generalised to the prediction of category-selective visual and valence responses in independent datasets. Importantly, individual variability in the neural representation of this two-dimensional latent aesthetic space, specifically within key nodes of the default mode network, was modulated by participants’ expertise with visual arts. Together, this evidence points to a nuanced interplay between shared neural computations and subjective experiences of visual aesthetics.

Captured in part by the distinction between landscapes and portraits in our study, the identification of a semantic dimension of aesthetic evaluation aligns with previous perspectives proposing that visual content meaningfully shapes aesthetic evaluations25. Following prior behavioural research12,24, the aesthetic appeal of landscapes in our study was rated higher than portraits, suggesting that semantic interpretation is a vital component of aesthetic experience. Based on this latent dimension, our ensuing neural decoding results revealed robust category-specific activity patterns within face- and body-selective areas (e.g., FFC, MST, MT) and place or object-selective areas (e.g., PHA, VMV), consistent with the known organization of the ventral visual stream44,45,57. In addition to the visual cortex, the results also highlighted the engagement of an expanded set of brain regions implicated in the aesthetic appreciation of the human body58. Together, these findings support generalised semantic frameworks25, which propose that aesthetic experience arises from the integration of perceptual input with stored conceptual knowledge. Such category-selective activations may facilitate associations between visual stimuli and emotionally or functionally relevant meanings, supported by observations on the PHA’s involvement in both scene recognition and memory-based interpretation of different contexts23,59.

In parallel to visual semantics, the second most important latent dimension in our study highlighted the key role of hedonic valuation in aesthetic experiences. This latent dimension was linked to distributed neural circuits involved in the processing of reward, affective appraisal, and decision-making as well as the extraction of features from visual stimuli. This discovery is in line with the sensory valuation model20, which posits that aesthetic judgement is a cognitive process of evaluation and assignment of value to different objects in our environment19,37. Specifically, our findings emphasise the role of mPFC subregions, including the ventral and anterior mPFC, medial orbitofrontal cortex, and subgenual anterior cingulate cortex, in encoding the subjective value of artworks. These regions are known to integrate affective, motivational, and self-referential signals during value-based decision-making60,61. In addition to these cortical areas, subcortical structures, such as the striatum, including the caudate, putamen, and nucleus accumbens, also contributed to the prediction of hedonic value dimension, reflecting their well-established role in reward anticipation and pleasure encoding1,62–65. Interestingly, while amygdala involvement in aesthetic evaluation has not been consistently reported, its role in signalling aversive or ambiguous stimuli may explain its contribution to negative hedonic responses in this context66. Collectively, these findings further highlight the necessity of integrating sensory, affective and evaluative processes that underlie aesthetic evaluation.

The accuracy of this network of brain regions in predicting hedonic valuation is also consistent with the affective workspace hypothesis67, which posits a flexible, valence-general system capable of supporting both positive and negative affective states. Unlike unidirectional emotional responses (e.g., fear or pain), aesthetic evaluation in our study spanned a broader hedonic range, with bidirectional valence shifts. This observation suggests that valuation-related regions operate within a dynamic, context-dependent framework rather than a fixed reward-only architecture. Supporting this view, valence-general signals in mPFC and associated structures have been observed in tasks ranging from economic choices to emotional imagery and self-relevant, value-based state representations68,69.

In addition to the engagement of trans-modal brain regions, our findings also demonstrate that sensory processing contributes directly to hedonic value assignment. The ventral visual complex, including the medial occipitotemporal sulcus, was implicated in value computation, consistent with its role in encoding colour, texture, and contour information70. Furthermore, early visual areas, such as V4 showed predictive contributions, particularly in negative evaluations. Prior studies have shown that abrupt contour transitions and irregular spatial structures, encoded in these regions, can elicit aversive responses71,72. These results suggest that even early sensory cortices contribute to the construction of affective experience through their sensitivity to specific visual features. In line with this finding, recent observations indicate that connectivity between visual and frontal valuation areas may facilitate the integration of low-level visual features into affective judgements73, which requires further investigation.

Beyond regional contributions, the distributed nature of these findings across the whole-brain supports the view that aesthetic processing arises from large-scale brain interactions rather than isolated regions13,20. In agreement with this assertion, the two latent dimensions, namely visual semantics and hedonic valuation, map well onto the aesthetic triad model, which proposes that aesthetic experiences emerge from the interaction of sensory-motor, emotion-valuation, and meaning-knowledge systems13. Our results expand on this model by showing that perceptual, sensory processes are not confined to a single functional module but rather operate across both semantic and valuation domains. This dual involvement underscores the importance of both bottom-up feature extraction and top-down conceptual modulation in shaping aesthetic experiences1,20,25.

These findings also speak to a broader debate about domain-specific versus domain-general mechanisms in aesthetic cognition. The consistent involvement of the mPFC, also implicated in moral reasoning, social evaluation, and intertemporal choice74–76, suggests that hedonic valuation during aesthetic appraisal may recruit domain-general valuation circuitry. Converging evidence across meta-analyses indicates that the mPFC, OFC, and ACC all show engagement across stimulus types, tasks, and cultural contexts17,18. In contrast, the reliance on category-selective visual regions reflects domain-specific representations, suggesting that conceptual valuation operates on top of perceptual scaffolding. This hierarchical architecture resonates with recent computational views, which propose that valuation is implemented via a flexible overlay on domain-specific representations20,77. Importantly, the present findings should not be interpreted as identifying cognitive processes unique to aesthetic experience. Instead, they demonstrate how domain-general core neurocognitive systems involved in visual semantics and hedonic valuation jointly structure aesthetic evaluations of artworks within the present stimulus set.

Situated atop such a functional organization lies the default mode network78, which has also been identified as a key contributor to the prediction of the latent dimension of hedonic valuation as well as the encoding of two-dimensional aesthetic space in our study79. The default mode network, traditionally linked to self-referential cognition80, has been increasingly implicated in aesthetic processing across visual and musical modalities14,16,59,65,81,82. In the context of aesthetic experience, the engagement of this network may reflect mental navigation across personally meaningful, autobiographical associations, or imagined scenes in response to visual art69,80,83,84. This supports the idea that aesthetic experience engages deeply reflective cognitive states, consistent with the contemplative nature of certain art forms.

In line with this interpretation, we found that the encoding of the aesthetic space in key default mode regions, such as, mPFC, posterior cingulate and posterior superior temporal regions, was modulated by individuals’ expertise in visual arts. Prior work has shown that experts show greater engagement of orbitofrontal and subgenual regions during aesthetic judgements, likely reflecting greater reliance on abstract or conceptual strategies7,85. Additionally, posterior superior temporal areas, including TPOJ and STV, are implicated in social perception and theory of mind86, further linking aesthetic judgement to socially grounded inference mechanisms5,87,88. These findings invite future research to explore how expertise sculpts neural representations of aesthetics, potentially through longitudinal training or cross-cultural comparisons.

While our study offers a detailed map of the neural architecture supporting visual aesthetic evaluation, several limitations remain. First, we examined static evaluations of aesthetic appeal, leaving their temporal evolution unexamined. Future work should employ time-resolved neuroimaging as well as continuous ratings to capture dynamic changes in aesthetic experience. Second, while our stimuli were limited to traditional Chinese watercolour paintings, this constraint allowed us to probe unique cognitive and affective features associated with contemplative art forms24,89. Nonetheless, future studies should test whether these findings generalise to Western art, abstract forms, or everyday aesthetic experiences. Third, we focused solely on the modality of visual arts. Expanding this research to music, dance, poetry, or multisensory experiences would help determine the generality of the observed neural dimensions.

Notwithstanding these limitations, our results provide compelling evidence that aesthetic experiences during evaluation of visual artworks emerge from a convergence of semantic interpretation and hedonic valuation, supported by coordinated activity across visual, reward, and default mode networks. This distributed architecture reflects both shared and individualised mechanisms of aesthetic cognition, and offers a neural framework for understanding how people derive meaning and pleasure from visual art. These findings have broad implications for cognitive neuroscience, computational aesthetics, and the growing interface between neuroscience and the arts.

Methods

Participants

All experimental procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki – Ethical Principles for Medical Research Involving Human Participants and were approved by the ISTBI institutional review board (AF/SC-19/20210604). In total, 34 healthy Mandarin-speaking individuals participated in the study (age range = 19 to 28 years, mean age = 23.21 years, SD = 2.41, female/male ratio: 22/12), who were all right-handed with normal or corrected-to-normal vision. None of the participants had any history of neurological or psychiatric disorders or contraindications to MRI scanning. All participants provided informed consent prior to study commencement and received monetary compensation for their participation (100 RMB/hour).

Experimental procedure

The experiment took place across two visits to the Zhangjiang International Brain Imaging Centre (ZIC), Fudan University. During their first visit, participants were informed about all the experimental procedures and scanned at 3 T MRI in order to obtain anatomical (T1w/T2w) and resting-state fMRI data. The second visit involved the acquisition of task fMRI data at 7T using the main Art Appreciation Task and the Functional Localiser Task paradigms. Subsequently, participants were taken to the Advanced Cognitive Testing Suite in order to complete a self-report Art Expertise Questionnaire and auxiliary behavioural assessments including the Art Rating Task and the Multi-Arrangement Task.

Stimuli

The main experimental paradigm was designed to assess the aesthetic experience of participants. For that purpose, we curated a set of Chinese traditional watercolour paintings using publicly available online resources. This set featured illustrations from four prominent Chinese painters active from the early-to-mid 20th century, namely Chen Shaomei, Lu Xiaoman, Pu Ru, and Zhang Daqian. The original images were cropped (425×425 pixels) to focus on two main themes that are commonly depicted in traditional artworks: portraits and landscapes. While portrait images showed people engaged in daily activities, highlighting faces and body parts, landscape images primarily featured nature, including mountains, rivers, and trees, as well as man-made objects, such as boats and houses. Contrast, luminance, and other visual properties of the images were unaltered. The final stimuli set for the main experimental paradigm included 96 images, consisting of 12 portrait and 12 landscape images extracted from individual artworks of each of the four Chinese artists. Figures depicting example stimuli show open-access works of representative subject matter sourced from institutional open-access collections. They do not represent the original experimental stimuli, which remain subject to copyright restrictions. The title, artist, and thematic category of each original painting are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Art appreciation task

In the main experimental paradigm, participants were presented with the full stimuli set of 96 artwork images in a random order without replacement using an event-related design90. In each trial, artwork images were presented in the middle of the screen on a grey background (6° visual angle) for 4 s, followed by a 1-s inter-stimulus interval (a red fixation dot). Participants were asked to passively view the images while focusing on the extent to which they found the artworks moving/compelling, probing the full depth of aesthetic experience while discouraging rapid, superficial judgements. Prior to fMRI scanning, participants were explicitly briefed about the task objectives and informed that they would later rate the same artworks based on their aesthetic experience inside the scanner. A total of four task fMRI runs were performed, with each run lasting 8 min 8 s.

Functional localiser task

Interleaved with the main experimental paradigm, we employed a robust and validated functional localiser task in order to delineate category-selective visual responses49. In this task, grayscale images from 10 categories were presented on a scrambled background. Images were grouped into five stimulus domains: faces (adult and child), body parts (whole body and limb), places (corridor and house), objects (car and instrument) and characters (pseudoword and number). In each 6-s-long trial, participants were presented with 12 images from a given category (0.5 s per image). Within each run, stimuli from each category domain, as well as blank trials, were presented six times in total. Two additional blank trials were included at the beginning and end of each run. Participants were asked to focus on the red fixation dot that was visible at the centre of the screen throughout the experiment, and press a button in the absence of a visible image (oddball task). In total, four runs were collected in the same session, lasting 4 min each.

Art rating task

Following fMRI scanning, participants provided subjective aesthetic appeal ratings to the full set of artwork images employed in the main experimental task fMRI paradigm. This behavioural task was performed individually on a computer screen in noise-controlled private testing rooms. Participants were explicitly instructed to base their evaluative judgements on their aesthetic experience during fMRI scanning. Images were presented and rated one at a time, which were later used to quantify the intersubjective similarity of aesthetic appeal ratings across participants. Using a trackball, participants recorded their responses on a visual slider below each image, providing ratings on a continuous scale from Dislike to Like81. There was no time limitation for the ratings (average rating time = 2.50 s, SD = 1.86).

Multi-arrangement task

In addition to the similarity in aesthetic evaluation of artwork pairs, we aimed to quantify semantic similarity using the multi-arrangement method91. This approach enables participants to efficiently evaluate multiple image pairs simultaneously while estimating perceived similarity. Using drag-and-drop, participants were instructed to arrange the artworks in a circular arena based on their similarity. Images perceived as similar were placed close together, while dissimilar images were positioned farther apart. Throughout the task, the system adaptively selected image subsets to optimise similarity measurements. Given the scope of this assessment (96 images creating 4560 possible pairs), the task took approximately one hour to complete. We then generated a single similarity matrix for each participant by combining multiple similarity representations.

Art expertise questionnaire

With the aim of characterising participants’ prior expertise with visual arts, we employed a self-report, pen-and-paper Art Expertise Questionnaire56. The questionnaire consisted of 10 questions related to the participant’s background in studio art, art history, art theory, and aesthetics, frequency of visits to museums and galleries, and average weekly time committed to making, reading about, and looking at visual art.

MRI data acquisition

In compliance with the data requirements of the Human Connectome Project (HCP) preprocessing pipeline92, a high-quality MRI data acquisition procedure was optimised for this study. While anatomical and resting-state fMRI data were collected at 3 T Magnetom Prisma MRI scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) with a 32-channel head coil using sequence protocols based on the HCP-Lifespan93, task-based fMRI data was acquired via 7T Magnetom Terra MRI (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) with a 1-channel transmit and 32-channel receive array (1Tx/32Rx) head coil (Nova Medical, Wilmington, MA, USA), using a site-specific version of the harmonised sequence protocols from the UK-7T Network94.

At 3 T, we collected whole-brain anatomical images using a T1-weighted MPRAGE sequence (0.8 mm isotropic resolution; TR = 2500 ms; TE = 2.22 ms; TI = 1000 ms; flip angle = 8°; bandwidth = 220 Hz/pixel; in-plane acceleration factor [iPAT] = 2; acquisition time [TA] = 6 min 40 s) and a T2-weighted SPACE sequence (0.8 mm isotropic; TR = 3200 ms; TE = 563 ms; bandwidth = 745 Hz/pixel; iPAT = 2; TA = 5 min 57 s). In addition, we acquired a pair of resting state fMRI data with both anterior and posterior phase encoding polarity using a 2D multiband (MB) gradient-recalled echo (GRE) echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence (2.0 mm isotropic; TR = 800 ms; TE = 37 ms; flip angle =52°; bandwidth = 2290 Hz/pixel; multiband factor = 8; 488 volumes; TA = 6 min 30 s). Moreover, we obtained a dual-echo gradient and a pair of spin echo images with AP/PA phase encoding polarity for post-hoc distortion correction of imaging data.

For the task fMRI runs at 7T, data acquisition was performed using a multiband GRE-EPI sequence with high spatial resolution (1.5 mm isotropic; TR = 1500 ms; TE = 25 ms; flip angle = 65°; multiband factor = 4; phase encoding = AP; TA = 8 min 8 s per Art Appreciation Task run and 4 min per Functional Localiser Task run). Associated dual-echo gradient field maps with matching geometry were also acquired for post-hoc distortion correction. During task fMRI runs, the experimental paradigm was presented on an MRI-compatible rear projection screen using a PROPixx projector (VPixx Technologies Inc., Canada). The display was situated inside the scanner bore, which participants viewed through a mirror attached to the head coil at a 45° angle. The display-to-mirror distance was about 202.5 cm (resolution 1920 × 1080 at 120 Hz, visual angle 6°), while any behavioural responses were recorded using a 4-button box.

MRI data preprocessing

Neuroimaging data were preprocessed following the HCP preprocessing pipeline92 as implemented in the Quantitative Neuroimaging Environment & Toolbox (QuNex) platform95. Specifically, structural images were first corrected for magnetic gradient distortion and registered to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) standard space. The brain tissues were then segmented into grey and white matter, and reconstructed as surfaces using Freesurfer96. Functional BOLD images were corrected for magnetic gradient distortion, head motion, as well as EPI distortion, and registered to the MNI space. All fMRI data were further denoised for spatially specific temporal artefacts using the ICA + FIX approach from FSL (FMRIB Software Library)97–99. Subsequently, BOLD time series were normalised for intensity and projected to the standard 32k fsLR grayordinate space92. To improve spatial alignment across subjects and to enhance group level statistics, we applied areal-feature-based cortical surface registration (MSMAll) using the multimodal surface matching algorithm100 with all the required modalities, i.e. cortical folding, resting-state connectivity, resting-state topography, and myelin-map architecture101. The final dense fMRI timeseries included 91,282 grayordinate samples (i.e., 59,412 cortical vertices and subcortical 31,870 voxels) across the whole-brain. The preprocessed BOLD images were further denoised with a 200 s high-pass filter using the clean function in the nilearn library. To improve spatial statistics, an additional spatial smoothing step was implemented by wb_command, achieving 4 mm full-width at half maximum (FWHM) Gaussian smoothing.

Trial-wise fMRI response estimation

For the purpose of estimating fMRI response amplitudes for each trial in the Art Appreciation Task, we employed the GLMsingle toolbox102. Following the calculation of baseline beta values using a standard GLM, this method generates additional beta versions that incorporate further denoising and optimisation strategies: (1) optimal choice of hemodynamic response functions from a library of 20 HRFs, (2) determination of data-driven nuisance regressors using GLMdenoise, and (3) regularisation of the single-trial betas with fractional ridge regression. In order to optimise the alignment between fMRI timepoints and trial onset in our task design, we first interpolated the preprocessed BOLD timeseries to 1 s intervals using the tseriesinterp function. Subsequently, we estimated trial-specific beta values for each grayordinate using the full set of strategies implemented in GLMsingle (TYPED-FITHRF-GLMDENOISE-RR). The four task runs were concatenated, resulting in beta maps that represented percent BOLD signal change for each participant. We subsequently averaged single-trial betas across four scan repetitions after z-scoring every grayordinate within each run to generate a comprehensive estimation of response amplitude for each stimulus image. This within-run normalisation was performed to eliminate potential non-stationarities and to equalise units across grayordinates.

Generalisation cohort

In order to test the validity of the identified neural signatures in our study, we employed data from the publicly available Human Connectome Project S1200 release103. Task fMRI data from 100 unrelated subjects (age range = 22 to 36 years, mean age = 29.11 years, SD = 3.68, female/male ratio: 54/46) were used, specifically focusing on the working memory (tfMRI_WM) and gambling task data (tfMRI_GAMBLING)43. In the working memory task, category-specific representations were embedded within the task design, which consisted of four visual categories: faces, places, tools, and body parts. The gambling task was designed to estimate neural responses to the anticipation of monetary reward and punishment.

Behavioural data analysis

The primary objective of our behavioural data analysis was to identify and validate the core cognitive components of shared aesthetic experience across the selected artworks employed in our main 7T fMRI task. For that purpose, we first constructed an aesthetic agreement matrix based on participants’ aesthetic appeal ratings, which was then decomposed into principal components, and subsequently validated via triangulation using auxiliary measures.

Construction of the aesthetic agreement matrix

In order to characterise the structure of shared aesthetic experience, we constructed an aesthetic agreement matrix quantifying the similarity of aesthetic appeal across stimulus pairs. This approach was informed by the principle that stimuli eliciting similar evaluative responses engage overlapping cognitive processes39, and was inspired by item-to-item collaborative filtering methods used in recommender systems38. For each image pair, we computed the Pearson correlation coefficient across participants’ subjective ratings to index intersubjective agreement. Higher correlations indicate that participants evaluated these images similarly, reflecting shared aesthetic processes, whereas lower or negative correlations suggest divergent evaluation patterns. We validated that this matrix exhibited systematic regularities associated with between-image similarity in visual, semantic, and affective properties using features extracted by independent AI-based models. The reliability of the aesthetic agreement matrix was confirmed through split-half and bootstrap resampling procedures (Supplementary Notes). While most participants’ responses followed approximately normal distributions, one individual who provided exclusively extreme ratings (0 or 1) for each image was identified as an outlier (SDratings = 0.50, exceeding two standard deviations from the group average SDratings = 0.19 ± 0.08), and was therefore excluded from behavioural analysis involving aesthetic ratings.

Decomposition of shared aesthetic experience

We applied principal component analysis (PCA) to the aesthetic agreement matrix in order to identify the latent components that best capture variance between artworks (Supplementary Fig. S3). PCA, as an orthogonal linear transformation, preserves the structure of the original high-dimensional data, while reducing its dimensionality by projecting it onto a lower-dimensional space defined by principal components that capture the maximum variance in the data. When determining the number of statistically meaningful components, we employed Jolliffe’s criterion (eigenvalues > 0.7)104 and results from a data-driven parallel analysis105,106. Empirical eigenvalues were compared against the 95th percentile of the corresponding eigenvalue distribution obtained from matrices created by 1000 simulations of random data with the same size as the original dataset. Moreover, further reliability analyses were performed on individual component scores and subspace similarity. The same PCA method was also applied to the group average semantic similarity matrix derived from the multi-arrangement task. For this matrix, we retained only the first principal component, which explained the large majority of variance and served as the dominant dimension (Supplementary Notes).

Characterising components of shared aesthetic experience

With the objective of evaluating the behavioural relevance of principal components derived from the aesthetic agreement matrix, we examined their relationships with two auxiliary measurements obtained in our experiment: (1) the first principal component score obtained from the group-averaged semantic similarity, and (2) the group average subjective value of each image based on aesthetic appeal ratings. Since these two measurements showed an inherent relationship (r = −0.24, p = 0.02), we conducted partial correlation analyses using one measurement as the predictor while controlling for the other as a covariate. In addition, we investigated the potential cognitive or psychological underpinnings of each principal component by relating component scores to a broad set of image properties and features extracted using AI-based algorithms and pretrained models. Specifically, we examined the relationships between the two component scores and 831 image features capturing low-level visual properties, high-level semantic embeddings and categorical affective scores. Moreover, we employed a deep learning neural network (MUSIQ)42, previously trained to predict objective aesthetic quality based on an independent image dataset, to compute an aesthetic value for each stimulus image, and tested its association with the component scores (Supplementary Notes).

Neuroimaging analyses

The neuroimaging analyses in this study aimed to derive neural signatures corresponding to the identified components of aesthetic experience using multiple analytical approaches: multivariate pattern analysis to decode brain responses to the dominant dimensions underlying the aesthetic agreement matrix, functional decoding to validate these neural signatures, and representational similarity analysis to examine how aesthetic relationships between images are represented in the human brain.

Multivariate neural decoding

With the aim of revealing neural signatures underlying the core components of aesthetic experience, we used a whole-brain multivariate pattern analysis approach27,30. We employed a linear support vector regression (SVR, C = 1) algorithm as the prediction model using CANlab core functions27. To maximise predictable variance among stimuli, we grouped all trials into four levels based on the quartiles of component scores from both PCA dimensions. For each of the two prediction models, we averaged individual beta maps in grayordinate space within each level and used these as features to predict component levels. The final features (predictors) comprised 136 beta maps, one per level for each participant (34×4), with component levels across participants serving as the outcome variable.

A rigorous cross-validation procedure with 10 repeats of 10-fold validation was used to evaluate the SVR algorithm’s performance. During this process, participants were randomly assigned to 10 subsamples of 3 or 4 using MATLAB’s cvpartition function. The optimal hyperplane was computed using the multivariate pattern in the training set and then evaluated on the excluded test set. Each subsample served as the testing set once across 10 iterations. To minimise potential bias from training-test splits, we repeated these cross-validation procedures 10 times with different splits, averaging the prediction performance to obtain a convergent estimation30.

For evaluating the predictive accuracy of the identified brain signature, we calculated Pearson correlations (r) between cross-validated predictions and actual component levels, using both overall (between- and within-subjects; 136 pairs) and within-subject (4 pairs per subject) measurements to quantify effect sizes. We calculated the root mean squared error (RMSE) within-subject to measure overall prediction error. To assess the significance of prediction performance, we performed a permutation test with 10,000 random shuffles. The randomisation was constrained within each participant, and performance estimates from the shuffled data were obtained using 10-fold cross-validation. Subsequently, we assessed classification accuracy between adjacent levels (level 2 versus level 1, level 3 versus level 2, and level 4 versus level 3) and between broader categories (high versus low, high versus moderate, and moderate versus low) using a forced-choice test that compared signature responses within participants. Two-sided binomial tests determined whether classification accuracies exceeded chance-level (50%).

Specificity and generalisability of neural signatures

We next tested whether the identified neural signatures showed specificity within our discovery dataset as well as generalisability to independent yet relevant cognitive tasks from the HCP generalisation dataset. For estimating the specificity of the predictive models, we repeated the same 10 repeats of 10-fold cross-validation with exchanged models and data. Specifically, we applied the semantic model to averaged individual beta maps within each level to predict value levels, and vice versa. To rigorously assess whether the observed dissociation in cross-prediction is statistically significant, we implemented a permutation test. Specifically, we randomly shuffled the predictive labels within each participant 1000 times to generate a null distribution of cross-prediction differences. For each permutation, we recomputed the self-prediction and cross-prediction accuracies and calculated their difference. The significance of observed dissociation was calculated based on this null distribution.

For quantifying generalisability of the identified neural signatures, we computed the cosine similarity between the signatures and contrast maps of interest. In the working memory task, we focused on the contrast between representation of two relevant categories, i.e., places versus faces43. In the gambling task, we focused on the combination of reward and punishment versus the neutral contrast as a general representation of monetary valence67. The signature response was estimated for each subject in the HCP by taking the dot product of vectorized contrast images with the neural signature pattern. The resulting data was then scaled by the l2 norms of activation images and signature patterns, to reduce differences in scaling across tasks107. The significance of each signature response was computed using a one-sample t-test, and the difference between signature responses was computed using a paired-sample t-test.

Quantification of regional importance

With the aim of delineating clusters of grayordinates that made the most reproducible contribution to the prediction models across subjects, we conducted bootstrap procedures with 10,000 samples (with replacement). For each bootstrap sample, we performed the prediction and calculated z-scores and two-tailed uncorrected p values at each grayordinate based on the bootstrap distribution of the population-level predictive pattern (neural signature). Regions with significant and consistent model weights were identified after correcting for multiple comparisons (one-sample t-test, FDR q < 0.05)27. In order to further highlight meaningful clusters in the prediction models, we applied minimum size thresholds of 40 mm² for cortical vertices and 80 mm³ for subcortical voxels (equivalent to 10 vertices or voxels).

Further evaluating the reliability of signature weights derived from the prediction models, we computed the Haufe transformation30,47. As suggested in previous studies, the Haufe transformation provides a more reliable and robust representation of neural patterns48. This transformation converts the population-level pattern (i.e., signature weights, also referred to as the backward model) into an activation pattern (forward model), which maps each grayordinate to the response (fitted values) in the multivariate model. The reconstructed activation pattern is also referred to as the structure coefficient in the statistical literature. Core regions that were most consistently correlated with and predictive of the target outcome (i.e., semantic or value levels) were defined as those containing grayordinates consistently observed across both backward and forward models (one-sample t-test, FDR q < 0.05).

Univariate task activation analysis

For the analysis of the functional localiser task, we used a standard GLM approach via FSL FEAT routines in order to identify category-specific visual responses. In the first-level analysis, five separate boxcar regressors modelled brain activity in response to the five stimulus domains, i.e., faces, bodies, places, objects and characters. All task regressors were convolved with the canonical HRF function using pre-whitening. Linear contrasts for these predictors were computed to estimate effects of interest: each stimulus domain versus all others. Following fixed-effects analyses that averaged four runs within each participant108, mixed-effect group level analyses were performed using non-parametric permutation testing (5000 iterations) via Permutation Analysis of Linear Models (PALM)109. The final results were corrected for multiple comparisons using FDR correction (q < 0.05).

Functional decoding of neural signatures

In order to provide an independent evaluation of the construct validity of the identified neural signatures, we used the thresholded signatures (FDR q < 0.05) from the bootstrap tests and performed two separate functional decoding analyses. First, we calculated the average level of category-selective activation in the functional localiser task within significant clusters in the identified neural signatures. For both positive and negative weighted masks, average task activation values were extracted for each individual in each stimulus domain including faces, bodies, objects and places. The domain of characters was excluded since no characters were used in any of the stimuli. For each signature mask, we performed a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA with stimulus domain as the within-subject factor. When a significant main effect was observed, post-hoc pairwise t-tests were carried out between all domain pairs, with Bonferroni correction applied to control for multiple comparisons.

In order to provide an intuitive cognitive interpretation of the predicted levels for each signature, we computed model-predicted expression for each image trial using cosine similarity between the neural signatures and beta maps averaged across repetitions within each participant. Expression values were then aggregated across participants using the median to yield a single predicted value for each artwork. For each signature, we visualised the images that elicited the strongest expression of the corresponding neural signature, stratified by quartiles. Owing to the bidirectional nature of each component, high expression corresponds to large positive values in the upper quartiles and large negative values in the lower quartiles. This expression was further used to evaluate whether the neural signature captured variance within each thematic category of artworks, namely portraits and landscapes by calculating the correlation coefficient between signature expression and dimensional scores within that category.

Furthermore, we employed topic-based meta-analytic decoding to reveal cognitive terms associated with significant regions within the neural signatures50,110. First, the 100 topics term related brain maps were derived from the Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic model (version 0.7). Anatomical and methodological topics were eliminated to focus solely on 50 terms related to cognitive and emotional processes (see details of terms in Supplementary Table 9). A total of 50 topic-specific activation maps were then projected to the grayordinate space and correlated with the positive and negative neural signature maps using Pearson correlation. Finally, for visualisation we retained the top 15 topics that were highly correlated with the neural signatures.

Representational similarity analysis

In addition to independently identifying the neural signatures of aesthetic experience using a multivariate decoding approach, we further assessed the neural representation of the two-dimensional aesthetic space, as a whole, via RSA. This analysis allowed us to identify regions that represent combined aesthetic features, and to examine whether the representation of this latent aesthetic space showed correspondence with expertise in visual arts. Specifically, we aimed to reveal brain regions in which the dissimilarity between neural activity patterns associated with each of the 96 images was explained by the pairwise Euclidean distance between images in the two-dimensional aesthetic space28.

To achieve this, we first employed a searchlight-based analysis method on the cortical surfaces. For each vertex on the midthickness surface, we extracted the closest 50 vertices (i.e., the neighbourhood) along the cortical ribbon constrained by pial and white surfaces as a searchlight53. Then, we performed RSA which included neural responses elicited from every trial. The representational dissimilarity matrices (RDMs) were quantified based on the Pearson correlation distance (1 - r) between neural activity patterns, as this metric is invariant to overall differences in activation magnitude and captures similarity in the relative spatial distribution of vertex-wise response profiles28,111. Using the pairwise Euclidean distances of images in the two-dimensional latent aesthetic space as a predictor, we then estimated the neural representational dissimilarity across searchlights with Spearman’s rho correlation. At the individual level, the effects of the Euclidean distances were further compared to a null distribution, which was estimated from 1000 permutations, while the positions of 96 images were randomly allocated in the two-dimensional latent space111. Specifically, each participant’s rho value was compared to the mean of the permuted distribution and then mapped back to the central vertex of each searchlight, which created an individual-specific continuous cortical map showing 2D representation of the aesthetic space. The calculated rho maps were all Fisher’s z-transformed and one-sample t-tests were performed for group-level analysis. Correction for multiple comparisons and autocorrelation across multiple searchlights was performed by using a permutation-based TFCE approach to identify significant clusters54. Specifically, a sign-flipping procedure with 5000 permutations was used to estimate the cluster-level significance at the group-level.

For subcortical regions, we used the Scale IV parcellation (54 regions) from the Melbourne Subcortex Atlas55 to extract regional activity patterns. The dissimilarity matrices were quantified via the Pearson correlation distance (1 - r) and further correlated with the target RDM, i.e. pairwise Euclidean distance of images in the two-dimensional aesthetic space, by using Spearman’s rho correlation. We further performed one-sample t-tests for group-level analysis. The results were corrected for multiple comparisons across the subcortical regions using FDR correction (q < 0.05).

We assessed the robustness of the RSA results by repeating the full RSA analyses using alternative distance metrics (e.g. Euclidean distances computed from z-normalized component scores and Mahalanobis distances) to estimate the structure of pairwise image relationships in the identified low-dimensional aesthetic space. We also reran the cortical RSA searchlight analyses using different searchlight sizes, ranging from 10 to 40 vertices in increments of 10. (Supplementary Notes).

Individual differences in art expertise

With the aim of exploring the potential influence of individual-specific expertise in visual arts on the representation of the two-dimensional latent aesthetic space, we employed a GLM-based analysis. First, a PCA was performed on the original 10 questions derived from the art expertise questionnaire. The resulting principal component score (explained 59% of total variance) was used as the indicator of individual art expertise, while age and sex were included as covariates. Statistical analysis was performed using non-parametric permutation testing via PALM. Cortical vertices below the uncorrected one-tailed p threshold of 0.05 was identified as clusters with minimum size thresholds of 40 mm², which was used for further visualisation. The significance was also corrected for multiple comparisons using FDR correction (FDR q < 0.05) to locate the peaks.

Equipment and software

The experimental task paradigms were implemented using PsychoPy software (v2021.1.4). Neuroimaging data were preprocessed following the HCP minimal preprocessing pipeline as implemented in the QuNex platform (v0.99.1). Trial-wise fMRI responses were estimated using GLMsingle (v1.1). GLM analysis was performed with FSL FEAT routines, and group-level statistical significance was assessed using PALM. A surface-based RSA searchlight procedure and permutation-based TFCE-correction were carried out using the CoSMoMVPA toolbox (v1.1.0)112 in MATLAB (version 2024b). To locate individual regions within the cortical maps, the multi-modal parcellation of the Human Connectome Project (HCP_MMP V1.0) served as the primary cortical atlas113. For subcortical nuclei, the Scale IV parcellation from the Melbourne Subcortex Atlas55 was employed. Cortical maps and subcortical regions were visualised using the Connectome Workbench (https://www.humanconnectome.org/software/connectome-workbench). Cluster identification and operation of CIFTI files were conducted using the wb_command functions in Connectome Workbench. All plots were generated using the Seaborn library in Python114.
