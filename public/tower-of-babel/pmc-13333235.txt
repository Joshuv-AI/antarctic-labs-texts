Programmable Bacterial Contractile Injection Systems for Ag Delivery and CD8+ T Cell Activation
Authors: Jeong-Yun Hwang, Sang-Hyun Kim, Jeong-Ki Kim, Soohwan Oh
Published: 2026

Bacteria have evolved sophisticated macromolecular complexes to sense, respond to, and manipulate their surrounding environments. Among these, bacterial secretion systems represent highly specialized molecular nanomachines that enable the transport of proteins, toxins, and signaling molecules across cellular membranes. Multiple secretion systems have been identified, including Type I through Type IX secretion systems and phage tail-derived extracellular contractile injection systems (eCISs) (1).

Recent studies by Kreitz et al. (23), entitled “Programmable protein delivery with a bacterial contractile injection system” and “Targeted delivery of diverse biomolecules with engineered bacterial nanosyringes” reported a programmable delivery platform based on the Photorhabdus virulence cassette (PVC), a naturally occurring eCIS, and demonstrated its potential for intracellular delivery of biomolecules to mammalian cells. The eCISs are phage tail-like macromolecular nanomachines encoded by diverse bacterial and archaeal genomes that mediate effector protein delivery to target cells (45). Structurally analogous to contractile bacteriophage tails, an eCIS consists of a baseplate complex, a contractile sheath, and an inner tube. PVC shares this structural architecture (Fig. 1A); however, it is uniquely released as an autonomous extracellular particle rather than being anchored to the cell surface (2). Representative atomic force microscopy images revealed elongated rod-shaped PVC particles in the pre-contracted state and shorter particles in the contracted state, consistent with the structural transition depicted in the schematic (Fig. 1A).

PVC was first identified in the insect-pathogenic bacterium Photorhabdus asymbiotica. PVC particles are approximately 100 nm-long extracellular injection systems encoded by Photorhabdus bacteria (6). The assembly and function of this system are governed by 16 core structural genes (pvc1–pvc16) organized within an approximately 20 kb operon (2). The capacity of PVC particles to function as autonomous extracellular injectors establishes them as a promising platform for the intracellular delivery of diverse biologics. A significant advancement was the identification of N-terminal packaging signal sequence that mediates selective cargo loading into the PVC inner tube (7). This mechanism facilitates the encapsulation of a broad range of proteins (10–130 kDa) with diverse isoelectric properties, while allowing the system to operate independently of the donor bacterial cell (7).

Building upon this modularity, Kreitz et al. (2) reported the programmable retargeting of eCIS for potential therapeutic applications. Utilizing AlphaFold-based structural predictions, the researchers performed a structure-guided redesign of the distal receptor-binding domain within the PVC tail fibers. The substitution of natural insect-targeting domains with heterologous proteins—such as the adenovirus serotype 5 knob domain or an epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-specific designed ankyrin repeat protein—redirected PVC specificity toward human cell-surface receptors, including coxsackievirus and adenovirus receptor and EGFR (Fig. 1B). PVC further enables direct intracellular delivery of active cargo proteins, unlike strategies that rely on nucleic acid intermediates or face stringent packaging constraints (2). In particular, Kreitz et al. (2) demonstrated delivery of Cas9 nucleases and base editors for genome editing in human cells, bypassing the size constraints of viral vectors such as adeno-associated virus.

A follow-up study by Kreitz et al. (3) expanded PVC cargo versatility by introducing spike engineering and retargeting (SPEAR), a modular adapter strategy enabling delivery of non-protein biomolecules. SPEAR enabled delivery of mRNA, single-stranded DNA templates, and ribonucleoproteins, supporting multi-modal genome editing such as combined Cas9 mRNA and repair template delivery (Fig. 1C).

While these studies established PVC as a programmable biomolecule delivery platform, its potential for Ag delivery and immune activation remains largely unexplored. To investigate this possibility, we optimized the culture conditions for PVC production. PVC particles were produced in bacterial cultures, purified by ultracentrifugation, and quantified, revealing that culturing at 24°C for 48 h significantly increased PVC yield compared with 30°C for 24 h (Fig. 1D; p=0.0246). The improved yield and purity of PVC particles provide a basis for evaluating PVC as a protein-based intracellular Ag delivery system. The bacterial origin of the PVC shell may confer intrinsic immunostimulatory properties, and its ability to deliver Ags into target cells is expected to favor MHC class I presentation and CD8+ T cell activation.

To experimentally validate the functional delivery and subsequent MHC class I presentation of PVC-encapsulated Ags, we utilized the ovalbumin (OVA) model system. We engineered PVC particles loaded with multiple repeats of the OVA-derived epitope (OVA257–264, SIINFEKL) and evaluated their ability to activate Ag-specific T cells. The murine dendritic cell (DC) line DC2.4 was incubated with varying concentrations of PVC particles loaded with repeated OVA257–264 peptide (PVC-OVA) for 2 h. Following the incubation, the DC2.4 cells were fixed to halt further Ag presentation and co-cultured with OVA257–264-specific CD8+ T cell hybridoma cells (CD8OVA), which express T-cell receptors specific for the OVA257–264 peptide presented by MHC class I (H-2K\\xe1\\xb5\\x87) molecules and produce IL-2 upon activation. The activation of CD8OVA cells was assessed by measuring IL-2 production after co-culture with fixed DC2.4 cells, revealing a clear dose-dependent response (Fig. 1E). Notably, PVC-delivered OVA257–264 peptide achieved a level of CD8OVA cell activation comparable to that of soluble OVA protein control at corresponding concentrations. These results suggest that PVC particles can efficiently deliver exogenous Ags into DCs for Ag processing and MHC class I presentation, highlighting the potential of the optimized PVC platform as a proof-of-concept Ag delivery system capable of inducing Ag-specific CD8+ T cell responses.

In conclusion, the engineering of eCIS/PVC systems represents a significant advance toward programmable intracellular delivery. Here, we optimized PVC production to improve PVC particle yield and purity and demonstrated that PVC-mediated delivery of antigenic peptides can promote functional MHC class I presentation and Ag-specific CD8+ T cell activation. Together with recent studies demonstrating receptor-guided retargeting of PVC for delivery in mammalian and plant systems (8), these findings highlight the versatility of engineered PVC as a programmable Ag delivery platform. Further studies will be required to evaluate in vivo efficacy and translational feasibility.
