Complement C3 inhibitory peptide AMY-101 ameliorates brain injury in a mouse model of NMOSD
Authors: Wenli Zhu, Fangping He, Fanxia Meng, Ruili Wei, Hui Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) is an autoimmune inflammatory demyelinating disease of the central nervous system, clinically characterized primarily by optic neuritis and transverse myelitis. This study aimed to investigate the therapeutic potential of the complement C3 inhibitory peptide AMY-101 in experimental models of NMOSD.MethodsIn vitro experiments were performed using AQP4-transfected cells exposed to AQP4-IgG and human complement to assess complement-dependent cytotoxicity and membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition. In vivo efficacy was evaluated in an intracerebral injection mouse model of NMOSD, in which histopathological changes after AMY-101 treatment were compared with those in untreated controls.ResultsAMY-101 significantly attenuated AQP4-IgG– and complement-mediated cytotoxicity and inhibited MAC formation in vitro. In the NMOSD mouse model, AMY-101 treatment markedly reduced astrocyte loss, AQP4 depletion, and demyelination compared with controls.ConclusionThese findings demonstrate that complement C3 inhibition with AMY-101 effectively ameliorates key pathological features of NMOSD in experimental models, supporting its potential as a therapeutic strategy and providing a rationale for further preclinical and clinical investigation.

Introduction

Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) is a severe autoimmune inflammatory demyelinating disease of the central nervous system (CNS), most commonly presenting as optic neuritis and transverse myelitis [1]. Serum autoantibodies against astrocyte water-channel protein aquaporin-4 (AQP4-IgG) are detected in most NMOSD patients and are considered central to disease pathogenesis [2]. Binding of AQP4-IgG to AQP4 on astrocytes triggers complement activation, leading to membrane attack complex (MAC) formation and subsequent inflammatory cascades that culminate in astrocyte injury, demyelination, and neuronal loss [3,4].

Current therapeutic approaches for NMOSD include immunosuppressive agents, plasma exchange, and targeted biologic therapies, such as eculizumab, satralizumab, inebilizumab, and rituximab [5–8]. Among the pathogenic mechanism involved in NMOSD, complement activation plays a pivotal role [1], making complement inhibition an effective therapeutic strategy [9]. Eculizumab, a 150-kDa humanized monoclonal antibody targeting complement C5, has been approved for the treatment of AQP4-IgG–positive adult patients with NMOSD and has demonstrated substantial clinical efficacy [10].

In recent years, peptide-based complement inhibitors have attracted increasing attention as potential therapeutic agents for complement-mediated diseases [11,12]. AMY-101 (1.789 kDa) is a novel complement-targeted therapeutic derived from the third-generation compstatin analog Cp40 and exhibits high affinity and selectivity for complement C3 (Kd = 0.5 nm) [13]. By inhibiting complement activation at the level of C3, AMY-101 blocks the downstream complement cascade and effectively prevents MAC formation [14]. In addition, AMY-101 can be administered subcutaneously and has been shown to be safe and well tolerated in clinical studies [15,16].

In the present study, we investigated the therapeutic potential of AMY-101 in NMOSD. Our findings demonstrate that AMY-101 attenuates AQP4-IgG- and complement-mediated cytotoxicity in vitro and reduces lesion severity in a mouse model of NMOSD, supporting complement C3 inhibition as a promising therapeutic approach for this disease.

Materials and methods

Lentivirus packaging and culture of M23-AQP4–overexpressing CHO cells

The open reading frame cDNA of Homo sapiens M23-AQP4 (NM_001364286.1) was synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and inserted into multiple cloning sites of pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1 (System Biosciences, Mountain View, CA). The construct was verified by DNA sequencing (Sangon Biotech) and a lentiviral vector lacking the M23-AQP4 sequence was used as the control. Lentivirus was concentrated to a titer of 3 × 10^8 IU/mL using Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter units (Millipore, Billerica, MA). A total of 50,000 Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells were infected with the lentivirus containing M23-AQP4 or the control vector. M23-AQP4–overexpressing and control CHO cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% L-glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) in 10-cm dishes at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

NMOSD antibodies and complement-dependent cytotoxicity

Following ethics approval and written informed consent, serum was obtained from two strongly AQP4-IgG–positive NMOSD patients (termed serumNMOSD) for experimental model generation. Patient 1 (female, 41 years) and Patient 2 (female, 38 years) provided single-time serum samples (15 mL each) on November 5, 2023, and December 23, 2023, respectively. Clinical details of the NMOSD patients are shown in Supplemental Table 1. Control serum was collected from age- and sex-matched healthy controls (termed SerumCtrl). Total IgG from patients and healthy controls was purified using Protein G resin (GenScript, Piscataway, NJ, USA), as previously described [17]. The purified IgG was subsequently concentrated to a final concentration of 10 mg/mL using Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter units (Millipore). Purity was confirmed by SDS-PAGE and was >95%. Following purification, a 10 μL aliquot of the IgG preparation was analyzed by a cell-based assay (CBA), confirming that AQP4-IgG reactivity remained strongly positive.

CHO cells stably expressing human M23-AQP4 were generated as previously described [18]. M23-AQP4-overexpressing CHO cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. Once confluent, cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and incubated at 30 °C for 30 min with serial dilutions of heat-inactivated NMOSD serum in RPMI 1640 medium. Serial dilutions of AMY-101(MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) and pooled normal human complement serum (hC; Innovative Research, Novi, MI, USA) were incubated at room temperature for 1 h and then added to the CHO cells. Cells were then incubated for 1 h at 27 °C [19,20], washed once with RPMI 1640 medium, and assayed for cytotoxicity using a LIVE/DEAD Viability/Cytotoxicity Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Cells were visualized using a fluorescence microscope (Leica DM6000B, Wetzlar, Germany).

Cell-based immunofluorescence assays

Single-cell suspensions of M23-AQP4–overexpressing and control CHO cells were seeded onto poly-L-lysine–pretreated coverslips in 24-well plates at a density of 50,000 cells per well. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Subsequently, Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with patient serum diluted 1:10 and a rabbit polyclonal anti-AQP4 primary antibody (1:100; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA; sc-20812), followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 594–conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody(1:200, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA, A-11012). Cell cultures was washed with PBS and mounted using Fluoroshield medium with 4′, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Cells were visualized using a fluorescence microscope. For assessment of MAC deposition, M23-AQP4–overexpressing CHO cells were incubated with 10% serumNMOSD and 5% hC in the presence or absence of AMY-101. A rabbit polyclonal anti-C5b-9 antibody (1:100; Abcam) was used as the primary antibody for immunofluorescence staining.

Intracerebral injection mouse model of NMOSD

Female C57BL/6 mice, aged 8–10 weeks and weight-matched, were purchased from Zhejiang Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. The intracerebral injection mouse model of NMOSD was established as previously described [20]. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions in an air-filtered environment with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and free access to food and water. The animal experiments were designed as exploratory studies based on previously published NMOSD animal models and comparable sample sizes reported in the literature [17–19]. Mice were randomly assigned to two experimental groups: 1) a control group, receiving 6 μl of IgGNMOSD, 4 μl of hC and 5 μl of PBS (n = 5); and 2) a treatment group, receiving 6 μl of IgGNMOSD, 4 μl of hC, and 5 μl of AMY-101 (n = 5). AMY-101 was prepared in PBS at a concentration of 20 mg/mL. The selected dose, 0.1 mg per mice, was based on evidence from previous studies [20,21] and our pilot experiments.

Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 10% chloral hydrate (3 ml/kg) and secured in a stereotactic frame (RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China). A midline scalp incision was made to expose the skull landmarks bregma and lambda. A burr hole (0.7 mm in diameter) was drilled 2.5 mm to the right of the bregma using a high-speed drill (RWD Life Science). A 33-gauge needle attached to a 25-μl gas-tight glass syringe (Hamilton, Reno, NV, USA) was inserted to a depth of 3 mm below the skull surface. IgGNMOSD (6 μl) and hC (4 μl), with or without AMY-101 (5 μl), were infused at a rate of 1 μl/min, with a total injection volume of 15 μl. Rectal temperature was maintained at 37 °C throughout the procedure. After injection, the scalp was disinfected and sutured. Seven days after injection, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under deep anaesthesia. Brain tissues were subsequently harvested and processed for cryosectioning. Mice were excluded if premature death precluded tissue collection.

For each mouse, four investigators were involved in the experimental procedures. The first investigator administered treatment according to the randomization table and was the only person aware of group allocation. The second investigator was responsible for anesthesia, whereas the third investigator performed the surgical procedure. The fourth investigator, who was also blinded to treatment allocation, assessed lesion status.

Immunohistochemical staining

Frozen brain sections, 6 μm thick, were permeabilized with cold acetone and then blocked with 5% BSA. Human serum samples diluted 1:10 were applied to the sections overnight at 4 °C. The following primary antibodies were used: mouse anti-AQP4 (1:100), goat anti-glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP; 1:1000), and rabbit anti-MBP (1:200, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Sections were washed and then incubated with the appropriate Alexa Fluor-conjugated anti-human secondary antibody (1:1,000; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). Tissue sections were examined using a fluorescence microscope (Leica DM6000B; Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Lesion areas were defined as regions showing complete or substantial loss of AQP4, GFAP and MBP immunoreactivity compared with the surrounding normal tissue. Quantification was performed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). Investigator were blinded to the experimental groups during pathological staining and analysis.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted in R, and visualizations were generated using the ggplot2 package. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), unless otherwise indicated. Given the relatively small sample size, nonparametric analyses were performed. Comparisons between two independent groups were conducted using the two-sided Mann–Whitney U test. Exact p-values were reported whenever possible, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

AMY-101 prevents AQP4-IgG–mediated complement-dependent cytotoxicity in vitro

CHO cells were transduced with lentivirus to overexpress M23-AQP4 (AQP4-CHO) or with an empty lentiviral vector as a control (null-CHO). Robust AQP4 expression was observed only in AQP4-CHO cells (Figure 1A). AMY-101 is a 13-amino-acid peptide with a molecular weight of 1.789 kDa (Figure 1B). Because binding of NMOSD-IgG to AQP4 can induce complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC) in AQP4-CHO cell cultures, we examined whether AMY-101 could reduce AQP4-IgG–mediated CDC in vitro. AQP4-CHO cells were cultured to confluence for 2 days and then incubated with 10% serumNMOSD or serumCtrl for 30 min, followed by the addition of 5% hC in the presence or absence of AMY-101. Cytotoxicity was assayed using LIVE/DEAD staining (Figure 1C). Quantitative analysis is shown in Figure 1D. serumNMOSD induced CDC in AQP4-CHO cells but not in control cells, whereas AMY-101 significantly reduced cytotoxicity.

AMY-101 prevents AQP4-IgG– and hC-mediated complement-dependent cytotoxicity in AQP4-CHO cells. (A) CHO-AQP4 cells stably expressing M23-AQP4 were incubated with the serum of AQP4-IgG positive patients (green) and a commercial anti–AQP4 antibody (red). Null-CHO cells were used as controls and did not express AQP4. (B) Chemical structure of AMY-101. (C) CHO-AQP4 cells were incubated with 10% serumNMOSD and 5% hC with 1.5 mM AMY-101 or control peptide. Cytotoxicity was assessed using LIVE/DEAD staining. Green indicates live cells, and red indicates dead cells. Scale bars in A and C, 200 μm. (D) The proportion of dead cells shown in D. Error bars indicate the 95% confidence interval (CI). n = 5/group; p = 0.0079.

The protective effect of AMY-101 is dependent on hC and inhibited MAC formation

To determine whether the protective effect of AMY-101 was influenced by hC concentration, AQP4-CHO cells were incubated with 10% serumNMOSD to induce CDC, followed by the addition of 0.5 mM AMY-101 together with different concentrations of hC. The proportion of dead cells increased with increasing hC concentration (Figure 2A). We fixed serumNMOSD concentration to 10% and measured CDC as a function of hC concentration. The EC50 of AMY-101 increased with increasing hC concentration, indicating that higher concentrations of AMY-101 are required to neutralize increasing amounts of C3 (Figure 2B). These results confirm that the protective effect of AMY-101 depends on hC concentration.

AMY-101 prevents AQP4-IgG and hC–mediated CDC by inhibiting MAC formation. (A) CHO-AQP4 cells were incubated with 10% serumNMOSD for 30 min, followed by the addition of 0.5 mM AMY-101 and varying concentrations of hC (2.5, 5, 10, or 15%). Complement-dependent cytotoxicity was assessed using LIVE/DEAD staining. (B) Quantification of the proportion of dead cells in CHO-AQP4 cells incubated with 10% serumNMOSD and different concentrations of AMY-101 and hC. n = 3/group. (C) CHO-AQP4 cells were preincubated with serumNMOSD and hC in the presence or absence of 1.5 mM AMY-101 for 30 min and then stained for C5b-9. Scale bars in A and C, 200 μm. (D) The proportion of MAC forming cells (red-to-blue fluorescence ratio) was statistically significant between the two groups. Error bars indicate the 95% confidence interval (CI). n = 5/group; p = 0.0079.

MAC formation was assessed by C5b-9 immunostaining. To determine the effect of AMY-101 on MAC formation, M23-AQP4–expressing CHO cells were incubated with serumNMOSD and hC in the presence or absence of AMY-101, followed by detection of C5b-9. Prominent C5b-9 deposition was observed on the plasma membranes of CHO cells treated with serumNMOSD and hC. In contrast, C5b-9–positive deposition was markedly reduced on the plasma membranes of AQP4-CHO cells treated with AMY-101 (Figure 2C). Compared with controls, AMY-101 treatment significantly decreased the proportion of cells with C5b-9–positive deposition (29.080 ± 6.725% vs 77.820 ± 6.635%, p = 0.0079; Figure 2D), confirming that AMY-101 inhibits MAC formation.

AMY-101 does not affect AQP4-IgG binding to AQP4

AMY-101 specifically binds to C3 and inhibits AQP4-IgG–mediated CDC. To further determine whether the protective effect of AMY-101 was related to AQP4-IgG, AQP4-CHO cells were incubated with different concentrations of serumNMOSD in the presence of fixed concentrations of hC (5%) and AMY-101 (0.5 mM). The proportion of dead cells was approximately 50% (Figure 3A). The EC50 of AMY-101 was unchanged (Figure 3B). Immunofluorescence staining using two different serumNMOSD samples showed that a high concentration of AMY-101 (2 mM) did not affect AQP4-IgG binding to AQP4 (Figure 3C). These results suggest that the protective effect of AMY-101 is independent of AQP4 IgG.

Complement-dependent cytotoxicity was assessed using LIVE/DEAD staining. (B) The proportion of dead cells (red) in CHO-AQP4 cells incubated with 5% hC and different concentrations of AMY-101 and serumNMOSD. n = 3/group. (C) CHO-AQP4 cells were incubated with 10% serumNMOSD in the presence or absence of 2 mM AMY-101, followed by immunostaining for AQP4 (red) and patient-derived serumNMOSD IgG binding (green). CHO-null cells were used as controls. Scale bars in A and C, 200 μm.

AMY-101 reduces brain injury in a mouse model of NMOSD

To evaluate the efficacy of AMY-101 in vivo, we used a parenchymal injection mouse model of NMOSD, as previously described [17,20]. Injection of IgGNMOSD and hC mimic the key pathological features of NMOSD, particularly demyelination and loss of astrocytes and AQP4. Seven days after intracerebral injection of IgGNMOSD and hC with or without AMY-101, brain tissues were analyzed by immunofluorescence staining. The primary outcome measure was lesion severity, assessed by histopathological analysis. Marked AQP4 and GFAP loss, together with demyelination, was observed in mice treated with IgGNMOSD and hC. AMY-101 treatment significantly reduced NMOSD-like lesions in this mouse model (Figure 4A). Quantification confirmed that AMY-101 reduced the areas of AQP4 loss (1.782 ± 0.287 vs 2.640 ± 0.287 mm2, n = 5, p = 0.0079), GFAP loss (1.143 ± 0.291 vs 1.927 ± 0.179 mm2, n = 5, p = 0.0079), and MBP loss (0.884 ± 0.196 vs 1.558 ± 0.115 mm2, n = 5, p = 0.0079; Figure 4B).

AMY-101 ameliorates lesions in the intracerebral injection model of NMOSD. (A) Mouse brains were injected with 6 μL IgGNMOSD and 4 μL hC with or without 5 μL AMY-101. Immunofluorescence staining for GFAP, AQP4, and MBP was performed 7 days after injection. Areas outlined in white indicate lesions characterized by loss of AQP4, GFAP, or MBP immunoreactivity. Scale bar, 500 μm. (B) Quantification of lesion areas shown in A. Error bars indicate the 95% confidence interval (CI). n = 5/group; p = 0.0079.

Discussion

NMOSD is a severe autoimmune inflammatory disorder of the central nervous system characterized by frequent relapses and progressive disability, with approximately 90% of untreated patients experiencing relapse within three years [22]. Accordingly, reducing the recurrence of NMOSD is often the focus of medical treatments [23]. Current disease-modifying therapies increasingly target key pathogenic mechanisms involved in NMOSD, among which complement activation plays a central role in AQP4-IgG-seropositive disease [24]. The clinical efficacy of complement-targeted therapies, including anti-complement monoclonal antibodies, has underscored the importance of complement inhibition as a therapeutic strategy for NMOSD [19,20]. In the present study, AMY-101 significantly reduced AQP4-CHO cell death and was associated with decreased MAC deposition in vitro. In addition, AMY-101 markedly reduced AQP4 and GFAP loss and ameliorated demyelination in a mouse model of NMOSD. These findings provide additional preclinical evidence supporting the therapeutic potential of the C3 inhibitory peptide AMY-101 in NMOSD.

Although reduced MAC deposition was observed following AMY-101 treatment, the therapeutic effects of C3 inhibition in NMOSD are unlikely limited to suppression of terminal complement activation. C3 occupies a central position in the complement cascade and contributes to multiple inflammatory processes, including opsonization, anaphylatoxin generation, and amplification of complement-mediated immune responses [15,25]. These pathways may also participate in astrocyte injury, inflammatory cell recruitment, and lesion formation in NMOSD [26]. Because the present study focused primarily on AQP4-IgG- and complement-mediated injury and MAC deposition, we could not determine the relative contribution of these downstream mechanisms. Further investigations are warranted to clarify how inhibition of individual complement effector pathways contributes to the overall therapeutic efficacy of AMY-101.

In 2019, eculizumab was approved for the treatment of AQP4-IgG–positive adult patients with NMOSD, representing a milestone in complement-targeted therapy for this disease [5]. However, monoclonal antibody therapies may have limitations related to molecular size, tissue distribution, and immunogenicity. In clinical studies, the concentration of eculizumab in cerebrospinal fluid was substantially lower than that in peripheral blood [27], suggesting limited CNS exposure. In addition, most monoclonal antibodies used in NMOSD clinical trials are murine-derived monoclonal antibodies that may generate HAMA neutralizing antibodies [28–30]. The HAMA neutralizing antibodies can decrease therapeutic effectiveness. Compared with monoclonal antibodies, lower-molecular-weight complement inhibitors may provide several pharmacological advantages, particularly with respect to reduced immunogenicity[31]. Nevertheless, their capacity for central nervous system penetration and their long-term immunogenicity remain to be established in NMOSD and warrant further investigation in both experimental models and clinical settings.

AMY-101 is a 13-amino-acid peptide that specifically targets complement C3, a convergence point for the classical, lectin, and alternative complement pathways [32]. Targeting C3 may provide broader upstream inhibition of complement activation than blockade of terminal complement components alone. Previous studies of C3 inhibition in complement-mediated diseases, including pegcetacoplan in paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria and AMY-101 in periodontal inflammation, support the broader therapeutic feasibility of this approach [33–35]. In this context, our findings suggest that AMY-101 may represent a candidate therapeutic strategy for NMOSD, particularly by attenuating AQP4-IgG– and complement-mediated injury.

This study has several limitations. First, although the number of animals included in each group was consistent with previously published exploratory studies using similar NMOSD animal models, the relatively small sample size remains a limitation. Future studies with larger-scale studies will be valuable to further strengthen the robustness of these findings. Second, although the intracerebral injection model is particularly useful for evaluating complement-mediated pathology, it does not fully recapitulate the spectrum of disease mechanisms in NMOSD, including peripheral immune activation, BBB dysfunction, chronic relapsing disease course, adaptive immune responses, and systemic complement dynamics. The present study therefore provides proof-of-concept evidence for AMY-101 as a potential therapeutic strategy for NMOSD. Passive-transfer and chronic NMOSD models may better reflect disease pathogenesis and therapeutic responses in patients [36,37], and future validation of AMY-101 in these models will be important to strengthen the translational relevance of our findings. Third, BBB penetration and immunogenicity of AMY-101 were not experimentally evaluated in the present study. Whether AMY-101 possesses superior BBB permeability and lower immunogenicity, remains to be further investigated.

Conclusion

AMY-101 effectively inhibited AQP4-IgG– and human complement-mediated injury in both in vitro and in vivo models of NMOSD. As a C3 inhibitory peptide, AMY-101 may offer potential pharmacological advantages, including low immunogenicity and improved CNS exposure; however, these properties require further validation in future studies. Taken together, our findings provide preclinical evidence supporting the therapeutic potential of AMY-101 in NMOSD and highlight complement C3 inhibition as a promising and potentially translatable therapeutic strategy for this disorder.
