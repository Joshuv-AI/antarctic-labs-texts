Quality of care, support and treatment services in Jharkhand’s Antiretroviral Therapy Center (ARTC): A mixed methods operational research
Authors: G. Jahnavi, Vinayagamoorthy Venugopal, Pratima Gupta, Rajesh Kumar, Saurabh Varshney
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:India has demonstrated an overall reduction of 57% in estimated annual new HIV infections. Assessing the quality of the ART Centers (ARTC) services would enable us to focus on the gaps and take necessary measures to provide high-quality ART services.Methods:An explanatory sequential mixed-methods design was adopted. A cross-sectional survey among PLHIVs and secondary data review with in-depth interview (IDI) among program staff and focus group discussion (FGD) among patients registered at the ARTC were conducted. One of each of the low, medium, and high case load centers was randomly selected. The Chi-square test compared major service indicators between ARTC.Results:Of the 150 patients interviewed from all three centers, 79 (52.7%) were males, and 66 (44%) were illiterate. Treatment adherence was high for 124 (82.7%) of the patients’ records checked. The IDIs resulted in three themes: infrastructure, manpower, and services. FGD yielded 15 statements on strengths. They were an adequate drug supply, no discrimination against PLHIVs attending the Center by the staff, and home care services.Conclusion:The quality of care provided to the patients registered was almost satisfactory. However, infrastructure deficiencies, incomplete records, and staff vacancies must be improved.

Introduction

The National AIDS and STD Control Programme (NACP) has embraced an evidence-based approach for program planning, prioritisation, and resource allocation to effectively steer India’s HIV response.[1] The ongoing NACP Phase V extends the national AIDS and STD strategy through the Financial Year 20252-2026, aligning with the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goal 3.3, to end the HIV/AIDS epidemic as a public health threat by 2030.[2]

In 2023, India’s adult HIV prevalence stood at 0.20%, indicating a low overall epidemic.[3] However, despite this low prevalence, the country continues to bear a significant HIV burden, with an estimated 25.44 lakh people living with HIV (PLHIV). Women aged 15 and older accounted for nearly 40% of new annual infections, while children comprised 3% of total infections.[3] Recent estimates indicate that Jharkhand had 23,794 PLHIV (2023), with an adult HIV prevalence of 0.07%. Nationally, HIV estimates confirm a continued decline in the epidemic, with India achieving a 57% reduction in estimated annual new infections, demonstrating the effectiveness of prevention and treatment initiatives.[4] Although Jharkhand is classified as a low-prevalence state, the Anti-Retroviral Therapy Centers (ARTC) of Jharkhand State AIDS Control Society must remain vigilant, ensuring all services are accessible to all, especially the most vulnerable populations.

Operational research (OR) plays a crucial role in generating scientific evidence to enhance the quality and adaptability of health and disease control programs as they expand.[5] The present operation research was designed to assess the quality and effectiveness of care provided under the Care Support and Treatment component in terms of (i) the client satisfaction toward the services provided namely accessibility of the center, availability of waiting area, counsellor communication skills, supportive and helpful, compliance of patients, (ii) the completeness of information captured in the ART treatment records, and (iii) to identify issues faced by the ART staff and PLHIVs and to identify best practices.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

An explanatory sequential mixed methods design was adopted for the project.[6] The quantitative component had a cross-sectional survey and a secondary data review. The qualitative component had in-depth interviews (IDI) and focus group discussions (FGD). The integration happened at the data collection and interpretation of the findings between the methods. Triangulation at different levels in research was followed to improve validity, credibility, and reduce bias. (i) Data triangulation: Collected data from register, patient, program staff; (ii) Methods triangulation: Mixed-methods design (quantitative survey, FGD, and IDI); and (iii) Investigator triangulation: multiple observers or researchers were involved to collect, process, or analyze qualitative data separately.

The Framework for Operations and Implementation Research in Health and Disease Control Programs was adopted to design, implement, and analyse the current OR.[7] There are a total of 12 ART centers in Jharkhand. The centers are divided into three categories, namely, low, medium, and high, based on the case load. The participants were recruited from three ARTCs, one from each category.

Study participants and sampling

The participants for the quantitative survey and Focus Group Discussions were PLHIVs registered at the selected ARTC. PLHIV belonging to various risk groups, namely pregnant mothers, men having sex with men, commercial sex workers, truck drivers, and migrants, were selected purposively. For the in-depth interview, healthcare personnel of ARTC, namely, staff nurses, pharmacists, counsellors, laboratory technicians, and data entry operators. One ARTC from each category was randomly selected. Thus, the low case load center at Deoghar, the medium case load center at Giridih, and the high case load center at Hazaribagh were selected for the study purpose. The first 50 PLHIVs who exited the center after obtaining their service were recruited from each of these ARTCs. Thus, a total of 150 PLHIVs were interviewed. For the qualitative interview, the participants who were vocal and ready to share their experiences were purposively selected.

Data collection tools and techniques

The exit interviews of the patients were held as part of a quantitative survey at the ARTC using a structured, pilot-tested questionnaire. The survey questionnaire had the following variables: (i) sociodemographic parameters namely age, gender, marital status, occupation, education, and community, (ii) quality of counselling services, (iii) infrastructure, (iv) treatment and care, (v) ART treatment record review related parameters, and (vi) adherence in %.

To measure the quality of counselling services provided at the center, the perspectives of PLHIV on counselling provided about HIV and ART-related issues were captured using a five-point Likert scale. The Likert scale was prepared based on the quality checklist reported under the NACP guidelines.[8] It was pilot tested and eventually finalized to capture data. While analyzing, five five-point scales were clubbed into three categories (satisfied, neutral and unsatisfied). The information was collected under the following headings. They were treatment preparedness, drug adherence, pill counting done (by a counsellor at each visit), follow-up visits, counselling before giving CD4 test report, correct and consistent use of condoms, testing of spouse/partner/children, nutrition, general hygiene, cough hygiene, counsellor used IEC material, social welfare schemes, and psychological support provided.

Details of the quality of the infrastructure of ARTC were assessed using the accessibility of the center and the availability of the waiting area. Treatment and care-related parameters included supportive and helpful staff, confidentiality, discrimination/stigma, care during the COVID-19 pandemic, and overall experience in the ARTC.

Completeness of the treatment card maintained at ARTC was assessed by checking the treatment and collecting data on identification data details at the entry point, personal history, family history, past antiretroviral treatment, clinical details, antiretroviral treatment summary, TB treatment during HIV care, pre-ART details, medical history, linkages to NGOs/care and support services, pediatrics details, investigations details under baseline tests, viral load and CD4 count, and patient follow up details.

Adherence in % was calculated based on the standard formula [Total number of pills the patient has taken (Number of pills given to the client minus number of pills balance in the bottle) divided by total number of pills that should have been taken in that period multiplied by 100]. Later, it was divided into three categories, namely, high (more than equal to 90%), moderate (90 to 75%), and low (<75%).

Qualitative interview: The qualitative data was collected using FGD and IDI. After performing the quantitative survey data analysis, the FGD and IDI were conducted. The counsellor helped the team to identify the participants by approaching them face-to-face. People who were nonvocal and felt uncomfortable sharing their views in the group opted out, and those who were vocal and willing to share their opinions and experiences about the care and services provided at ARTCs. FGD and IDI happened at the ARTCs in the absence of the service provider, to ensure privacy, in a place where all were comfortable. Authors VV, a male moderator and GJ, a female note-taker, conducted the interview. The interview guide was used to conduct the interviews, which had probes to explore the discussion deeper. VV was trained in qualitative research methods and had published qualitative studies previously, primarily carrying out the analysis with other members of the team. Each interview lasted 45 min. At the stage of data saturation, interviews were terminated. The information conveyed was paraphrased and confirmed with them toward the end of the interview. Informed written consent was obtained, and the conversation was audio-recorded after obtaining permission from them.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institute Ethical Committee for Human Studies (2022-37-EMP-02). Confidentiality was ensured at every step of data collection, compilation, analysis, and storage. All the reviewers had signed a copy of the data confidentiality form and were given specific instructions not to carry any original or photocopies of documents from the ARTC, click any photographs, or record any videos. Personal identifiers were excluded from the forms during the collection and reporting of beneficiary-level data.

Statistical analysis method

Quantitative: The data was entered in EpiData Manager software, and analysis was done using SPSS version 25.0. The analysis comprised a description of categorical data using frequency and percentage. Major indicators like adherence and laboratory tests were compared between ARTC using the Chi-square test.

Qualitative: Manual content analysis was performed on the responses given by those participants. Following the FGD and IDI, an audiotape of the discussions was carefully transcribed verbatim, and then they were translated into English by the team member who was good at both the vernacular and English languages. The authors, VV and GJ, independently coded significant text information in the transcripts. Next, the codes related to similar areas were clustered together to form the categories. Finally, similar categories were grouped to form themes to explore their perception of the quality of care and support provided at ARTCs. Statements in italics indicate direct quotations or verbatim from the respondents, and the quotations stated are either in support of or an addition to the description of results, helping to explain what respondents shared.

Of them, 79 (52.7%) were employed, 131 (87.3%) were married, and 105 (70%) belonged to other backward-class communities [Table 1].

In-depth Interview among Project Staff

Of the total 20 participants, three were staff nurses, medical social workers, lab technicians, pharmacists, care coordinators and data entry operators, and two were counsellors. Seven are from Deoghar and Hazaribagh each, and six are from Giridih. Of them, nine were males and 11 were females. Ages ranged from 24 to 51 years. The main purpose of this IDI was to capture the problems faced by them during the provision of care, support and treatment at ARTCs. The words and sentences mentioned in italics below were the exact verbatim of the participants (codes).

The content analysis of the transcript yielded three themes, namely, infrastructure, manpower, and services in all three centers. There were differences in the categories and codes formed at different centers.

Under the theme infrastructure, two categories, namely, (a) rooms and (b) basic amenities, were created. The codes related to the rooms related category were grouped under eight subheadings: (i) overcrowded (working space is inadequate, patient waiting area is small), (ii) ill ventilated (no electric fan/air conditioner was available), (iii) inadequate lighting (difficulty in writing registers and updating data in system), (iv) no cleanliness (sheep and goats are tied at the stairs many times), (v) no own electricity supply, (vi) no privacy possible, (vii) risk of air-borne diseases (TB patients crowded with others patients increasing risk of TB transmission), and (viii) no chamber for counsellor. The codes related to the basic amenities category were grouped into (i) nonavailability of toilet (We don’t have a toilet and use other toilets nearby), (ii) no drinking water, and (iii) furniture (no adequate chairs to sit).

The second theme, namely, manpower, had two categories: (a) nonavailability and (b) training of staff. The codes that contributed to nonavailability were (i) medical officer, (ii) safai walla/CHS staff, (iii) counsellor, and (iv) no replacement for workers. the codes under the training of staff were (i) type (induction training only) and (ii) frequency (not trained at frequent intervals).

The last theme of services had four categories: (a) basic services, (b) ART care, (c) care during COVID-19, and (d) best practices. Basic service had one category, which was biomedical waste management (different color bins are not available). The category of ART care had five groups of codes: (i) pharmacy (patient shout at us at times for shortage of drugs), (ii) laboratory (no CD4 kits available so temporarily managing from ICTC, now we stopped doing baseline investigations), (iii) medical service (patient reported being discriminated at labor room), (iv) lost to follow up (CHC staff pay home visit to avoid loss to follow up, we track them within 1 month of missing the visit, Long distance and financial burden on travel were the main reasons), and (v) reporting (computers are old and not connect to hotspot, registers are maintained properly; hence, we find no difficult to enter them in system and generate reports, poor internet hampers smooth reporting). In the category of Care during COVID-19, there was a single code that was drug delivery (through NGO/link ART center/outreach workers support, we delivered drugs block-wise at their home). Finally, under the best practices category, there were four groups of codes: (i) tracker list, (ii) drug delivery at home (our ART staff were sending drugs through visitors staying near to patients and sometimes we visited their home to deliver drugs), (iii) reducing defaulters, and (iv) patient welfare (previous Medical Officer arranged lunch and foods for patients and their kids, we maintain additional death register, we provide documentation related help in getting pension).

Focus group discussion among PLHIVs

Overall, 29 PLHIV participated in the FGD from all three centers. Of them, 11 were heterosexuals, 10 drivers, six antenatal mothers, and two MSM. Of these 29 patients, 19 (65.5%) were male, and 10 (34.5%) were female. The age in years ranged from 23 to 59 years. Focus group discussion with the patients registered with the ART center resulted in identifying the strengths and challenges faced in the provision of treatment, care and support at the center. The FGD lasted for nearly 45 minutes. They were all interviewed after obtaining written informed consent at the ART center, ensuring privacy and confidentiality.

Under the category of strengths, 15 statements were gathered from Deoghar ARTC, 9 from Giridih ARTC, and 16 from Hazaribagh. They were classified into three codes. They were an adequate drug supply, no discrimination against PLHIV attending the center by the staff, and home care services. One of the patients said that “ART staff are very friendly and never discriminate against us.” Another patient who was a housewife admitted that “During COVID, all drugs are provided at our doorstep.”

Under the category of challenges, five codes were evolved out of 25 statements in Deoghar, 36 in Giridih, and Hazaribagh ARTCs, each. There was a nonavailability of project staff, stigmatizing the patients at the District Hospital, minimal information on social welfare schemes, less frequent counselling, and difficulty in travelling to the center. Patients informed that because of the nonavailability of the Medical Officer, they visited the district hospital for their complaints and one of the female patients who attended the Obstetrics and Gynecology department said, “In the District hospital doctors are too discriminative,” “They are not doing surgeries for us they refer to private and they make us wait till the end of the OPD for the consultation.”

The positive aspects pointed out by the participants at the Deoghar ART center were adequate drug supply, no discrimination by the staff, and drugs provided at the doorstep during the COVID-19 pandemic. The challenges perceived by them were the nonavailability of MO, stigmatization by the doctors at the District Hospital, and not being aware of social welfare schemes provided to them, as they were counselled only once at the time of starting treatment. They requested during the discussion to dispense ART drugs for three months, which will help them reduce costs related to frequent travel.

Discussion

In the current study, we assessed the quality of ART services under the Care Support and Treatment component in the three selected ARTCs of Jharkhand. Almost all patients reported that they were satisfied with all sorts of care and support provided by the staff of the ARTC. Among those who conveyed unsatisfactory, 42 (28%) on social welfare schemes and 3 (2%) on psychological support provided. On the quality-of-care parameter, 40 (26.6%) were unsatisfied with the accessibility. Overall, they all rated their experience in the ARTC as satisfactory.

Studies previously done in South Africa assessing the quality of ART clinics revealed that patients’ satisfaction was poor related to clinician consultation and counselling-related services, and their accessibility and acceptability were better.[9] Another study conducted in Ethiopia showed that the availability of resources and the compliance of health care providers need improvement.[10] A study done in North Ethiopia with a similar objective revealed that increasing access to ART services, providing clean toilets, and ART drugs may further increase client satisfaction levels in the region.[11] The problems identified in our study were also similar to those of the abovementioned studies.

A previously done study among various ARTCs in India revealed that more than 80% of the clients reported overall satisfaction with the services availed from the center, and 60% reported that the quality of life has improved to a great extent after getting ARTC.[12] Another cross-sectional study previously done in Chandigarh, India at ARTC assessed the level of satisfaction and factors associated with it among PLHIVs registered at that center and revealed that 88% of the patients rated satisfaction level as satisfactory or above (30% very satisfactory, 58% satisfactory), 10% indifferent and 2% dissatisfied.[13] A study done in Andhra Pradesh, India, on client satisfaction with antiretroviral therapy service delivery revealed that the overall satisfaction rate among the clients was 92.6%.[14]

Our study findings related to satisfaction were almost the same as those for the previous studies mentioned above, except for a study done in Manipur that showed 60% satisfaction of clients.[15] The reason could be that the participants were injectable drug users. Their profile and their level of satisfaction with the ART services provided could be different from the other PLHIVs. The present study has no injectable drug users as participants.

The phenomenon of patient satisfaction with any health service provided is complex to understand. However, based on our understanding the factors associated with better satisfaction could be classified under the availability of human resources, length of time spent with the doctor, the average time required to get seen by the doctor, communication skills of various staff at ARTC, nonavailability of laboratory and pharmaceutical services, accessibility, and acceptability of services, and nondiscrimination of patients by any of the staff available.[16]

For a considerable time, it has been noted that surveys measuring patient contentment consistently show elevated levels of satisfaction.[1718] Assertions have been made that patients frequently mask a range of unfavorable encounters with their expressions of contentment.[1920] Instances where patients refrain from providing crucial feedback have been witnessed in countries like Indonesia and Lebanon.[21] Additionally, concerns about potentially distorted high levels of patient satisfaction have been brought up in a study conducted in South Africa by Harrison and colleagues.[18]

The human resource vacancy was common in all ARTCs, especially the Medical Officer. To overcome them, teleconsultation through e-Sanjeevini or the development of an AIDS/HIV intelligent web-based medical consulting system helps the current situation. A similar web-based model was developed, evaluated and approved by the Iranian Ministry of Health and Medical Education.[22] These interventions can be tried in our country as well. To overcome the shortcomings of patients’ self-care education,[23] it can be developed, especially using technology-based mobile applications, to clarify their doubts on treatment and improve counselling services.

Strengths and limitations

The current study had strengths and a few limitations. The novelty/major strength of the present study was to utilize a recent framework for conducting the OR and adopting the triangulation technique in research. Finally, this operational research was program consultant and manager-driven. Nevertheless, our study encountered certain constraints. Initially, the elevated levels of patient contentment about general services and staff performance should not automatically be interpreted as a reason for positivity. Moreover, the potential influence of recall bias in participants’ answers cannot be discounted. Due to its cross-sectional nature, the study couldn’t track changes in satisfaction over time, and while it was able to identify connections, it could not establish direct causality. The study’s limited sample size and its focus on a single location suggest caution in extending these findings to a broader context.

Recommendations for the program based on the findings

Separate counselling rooms or cabins at ARTCs have become important to ensure privacy and confidentiality for PLHIV. Overcrowding in the consultation area has to be avoided, as PLHIVs are immunocompromised. Training of counsellors, especially about public social protection schemes, is required. Linkage with social welfare schemes and other National Health Programs to be developed. Filling the vacant posts, periodic training of staff, availability of kits at the laboratory, and proper biomedical waste management are also required. Capacity building of PLHIV for mainstreaming them in society is the need of the hour. At Hazaribagh, a tracker list, CD4 register, and death register were maintained properly, which can be replicated in other ARTCs.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that the overall satisfaction with ART-related services is high at assessment sites in Jharkhand. Although it is clear that the state is capable of delivering high-quality ART care, significant geographic differences prevail among the centers. Our study findings provide a clear insight into the lacunae of the service provided at ARTC, namely human resource vacancy, especially Medical Officers, infrastructure facilities, mainly clean toilets and a separate room for the counsellor, uninterrupted testing kits to be ensured. The major points to be appreciated were no stigma and discrimination at the centers, continuous availability of drugs, and continuous drug delivery during the COVID-19 pandemic. Efforts should be made to further improve on the identified lacunae to improve the quality, thereby improving the adherence to ART care.
