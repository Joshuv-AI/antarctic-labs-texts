Very Special Relativity in Accelerated Frames: Non-relativistic Effects in Gravitational Spectroscopy of Ultracold Neutrons
Alessandro Santoni, Enrique Muñoz, Hartmut Abele, Benjamin Koch

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate the phenomenology of fermionic systems in uniform gravitational fields within the framework of very special relativity (VSR). We focus on the case of gravitational spectroscopy with ultracold neutrons, explored in experiments like q Bounce . Calculating the leading ( c 0 c^{0} ) and next-to-leading ( c − 1 c^{-1} ) order corrections to the non-relativistic Hamiltonian in an accelerated frame, we obtain the perturbed fermionic energy spectrum. At leading order, we do not find any modifications except for a trivial mass shift, thus preserving the equivalence between inertial and gravitational mass and particle-antiparticle sectors. The next-to-leading order corrections, instead, introduce time-dependent anisotropic contributions depending on the preferred spatial direction in VSR, and can then be used to probe novel Lorentz-violating signatures. Taking q Bounce sensitivity as a benchmark, we derive a first rough constraint for the neutron VSR parameter. Finally, we suggest alternative spin-flipping setups to better probe VSR effects and foresee potential future research directions.

I.1
VSR Dirac Equation in Inertial Frames

Before we move on to the technicalities of treating spinors in curvilinear coordinates, let us introduce some basic concepts of VSR in inertial frames. We will refer to its
SIM(2)
formulation
[
6
]
, implying no invariant tensors other than the usual Minkowski metric
\eta_{\mu\nu}
. Still, we shall assume the existence of a preferred null spacetime direction, labeled by
n^{\mu}
, which remains unaltered under
SIM(2)
transformations

n^{\mu}\underset{SIM(2)}{\Longrightarrow}\Omega\;n^{\mu}\,,\,\,\text{with }\,\Omega\in\mathbb{R}\,.

(8)

This implies that quotients of scalar products involving the vector
n^{\mu}
in both the numerator and the denominator are invariant under
SIM(2)
, since the rescaling parameter cancels out in the ratio. That allows us to write new non-local terms in the particle Lagrangians which break Lorentz symmetry down to the
SIM(2)
subgroup.

Given this background, the modified Dirac equation in VSR can be written as

\left(i\not{\partial}-m+i\lambda\frac{\not{n}}{n\cdot\partial}\right)\psi=0\,,

(9)

where we use the “slashed”-notation to represent contractions with gamma matrices. Note that
(n\cdot\partial)^{-1}
should be regarded as an inverse operator, which can be expressed, for example, in the integral representation

\frac{1}{n\cdot\partial}\psi=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\alpha\,e^{-\alpha n\cdot\partial}\psi\,.

(10)

Although the VSR term may seem daunting at first sight, in the absence of interactions its only effect is to shift the physical mass of the fermion to the new value

m_{f}=\sqrt{m^{2}+2\lambda}\,.

(11)

To avoid the encounter of runaway modes and remain consistent Appendix
C.4
, we require the argument of the square root to be positive, so that throughout the paper we take

\lambda>-m^{2}/2\,.

(12)

Moreover, it is customary and reasonable to think of
\lambda
as a perturbative parameter due to the LV nature of VSR. For this reason, approaching the non-relativistic limit in Section
IV
, we will further assume
|\lambda|\ll m^{2}
.

I.2
Hamiltonian Formulation of Inertial VSR

The time non-localities introduced by the new VSR operator in (
9
) make it difficult to directly identify a Hamiltonian for the fermion, as is usually done for Lorentz-invariant (LI) systems. To overcome these complications, we have developed an ad hoc approach which generally involves three steps
[
30
]
:

1.

Multiply the equations of motion (EOM) for
\psi
by
-in\cdot\partial
from the left.

2.

Use the “squared” version of the EOM to replace the second-order time derivatives of
\psi
.

3.

Collect on one side terms that are linear in the time derivative of
\psi
and write the EOM in the form of a Schrödinger equation with the appropriate Hamiltonian, i.e.,
i\partial_{0}\psi=\mathscr{H}\psi
.

Starting from (
9
), the first of the above steps leads us to the equation

(\,n\cdot\partial\,\not{\partial}+im\,n\cdot\partial+\lambda\not{n}\,)\,\psi=0\,.

(13)

Moving onto the second step, we have that the “squaring” procedure in the non-interacting case returns a Klein-Gordon (KG) equation for
\psi
with an effective mass
m_{f}

(\partial^{2}+m_{f}^{2})\psi=0\,\,\to\,\,\partial_{0}^{2}\psi=-(\partial_{i}\partial^{i}+m_{f}^{2})\psi\,.

(14)

Now, we replace this expression for the second-order time derivative into (
13
). As for the third step, by isolating all terms proportional to first-order time derivatives on the left side, we end up with

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{i}{m}(\gamma^{0}n^{i}+\gamma^{i})\partial_{i}\right)\,i\partial_{0}\psi=

(15)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{m}\gamma^{0}(\partial_{i}\partial^{i}+m^{2}_{f})-\frac{1}{m}\gamma^{j}n^{i}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}-i\,n^{i}\partial_{i}-\frac{\lambda}{m}\not{n}\right)\psi\,.

The operator on the left, which we define as

\mathscr{B}^{-1}_{\partial}\equiv 1-\frac{i}{m}(\gamma^{0}n^{i}+\gamma^{i})\partial_{i}\,,

(16)

satisfies the relation

\displaystyle m\gamma^{0}-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{i}\partial_{i}=

(17)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{B}_{\partial}\left(m\gamma^{0}+\frac{1}{m}\gamma^{0}\partial_{i}\partial^{i}-\frac{1}{m}\gamma^{j}n^{i}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}-i\,n^{i}\partial_{i}\right).

Thus, inverting
\mathscr{B}_{\partial}
on the right side of equation (
15
), we arrive to a time-evolution equation from which we identify the following Hamiltonian

\mathscr{H}_{VSR}=m\gamma^{0}-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{i}\partial_{i}+\frac{\lambda}{m}\mathscr{B}_{\partial}(\gamma^{0}-\gamma^{i}n_{i})\,.

(18)

Clearly, the first two terms are equivalent to those in the Dirac Hamiltonian, while the third one represents the VSR contribution. Therefore, in the limit
\lambda\rightarrow 0
, we recover the LI scenario, as expected.

Dividing and multiplying the operator
\mathscr{B}_{\partial}
on the right by
(1+\frac{i}{m}(\gamma^{0}n^{i}+\gamma^{i})\partial_{i})
, we can re-express it as

\mathscr{B}_{\partial}=\frac{1+\frac{i}{m}\gamma^{0}n^{i}\partial_{i}+\frac{i}{m}\gamma^{i}\partial_{i}}{1+\frac{1}{m^{2}}(n^{i}n^{j}-\delta^{ij})\partial_{i}\partial_{j}}\,.

(19)

Hence, defining
\sigma^{ij}\equiv\frac{1}{2}[\gamma^{i},\gamma^{j}]
and the non-local operator

\mathscr{R}_{\partial}^{-1}\equiv 1+\frac{1}{m^{2}}(n^{i}n^{j}-\delta^{ij})\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\,,

(20)

the final form of the VSR Hamiltonian in inertial frames is given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{VSR}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(m+\frac{\lambda\,\mathscr{R}_{\partial}}{m})\gamma^{0}-i(1+\frac{\lambda\,\mathscr{R}_{\partial}}{m^{2}})\gamma^{0}\gamma^{i}\partial_{i}

(21)

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda\,\mathscr{R}_{\partial}}{m}(\gamma^{i}n_{i}+\frac{i}{m}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}n^{i}n_{j}\partial_{i}+\frac{i}{m}\sigma^{ij}n_{j}\partial_{i})\,.

The momentum spectrum of the above Hamiltonian can be computed to be identical to the one of a free particle of mass
m_{f}
in special relativity

E=\sqrt{m^{2}_{f}+\bm{p}^{2}}\,,

(22)

in agreement with the spectrum anticipated by the KG-like dispersion relation in (
14
). Note that, even though
\mathscr{H}_{VSR}
may not seem to satisfy the traditional Hermiticity criteria, its spectrum (
22
) is still strictly real, suggesting that the VSR Hamiltonian formulation is well-posed and unitarity can be preserved. Indeed, there exists an extensive literature on the possibility of meaningfully working with non-Hermitian Hamiltonians in both quantum mechanics (QM) and quantum field theory
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
. In this context, the seeming lack of unitarity usually manifests when trying to naively interpret the behavior of certain open quantum systems with the toolkit of Hermitian QM (see, for example, the Lee model
[
35
,
36
,
31
]
). This also points to the possibility that VSR corrections effectively characterize specific classes of open systems, describing the presence of complex interactions with some background entities or the environment
[
37
]
.

II
Theoretical Framework for Curvilinear Coordinates

When dealing with fermions in curvilinear coordinates, there are many conventions that need to be specified and clarified. First, we introduce a set of local observers by defining an orthonormal frame field
\{\theta_{(a)}(x)\}
, which is connected to the coordinate basis
\{\partial_{\mu}\}
via the inverse tetrad
E_{a}^{\;\;\mu}(x)
as follows

\theta_{(a)}(x)=E_{a}^{\;\;\mu}(x)\,\partial_{\mu}\,.

(23)

The matrix
E_{a}^{\;\;\mu}
is the inverse of the tetrad matrix
e^{a}_{\;\mu}
, which is in turn defined by its relation to the spacetime metric
g_{\mu\nu}

g_{\mu\nu}=e^{a}_{\;\mu}e^{b}_{\;\nu}\,\eta_{ab}\,,

(24)

with the Minkowski tensor
\eta_{ab}
representing the flat metric for the tangent spaces. Throughout the rest of the paper, we denote spacetime indices with Greek letters and tangent-space indices with Latin ones, as usual. Starting from the “flat” gamma matrices
\gamma^{a}
, we can define their “curved” version

\underline{\gamma}^{\mu}\equiv E_{a}^{\;\;\mu}\,\gamma^{a},

(25)

which consistently satisfies the spacetime Clifford algebra

\{\underline{\gamma}^{\mu},\underline{\gamma}^{\nu}\}=2\,g^{\mu\nu}.

(26)

Additionally, we recall the definition of the antisymmetrized product
\sigma^{ab}
of two flat
\gamma-
matrices and introduce the block-diagonal four-by-four matrices
\bm{\Sigma}
as

\sigma^{ab}\equiv\frac{1}{2}[\gamma^{a},\gamma^{b}]\,\,\,,\,\,\,\Sigma^{i}\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\;\sigma^{i}&0\\
0&\;\sigma^{i}\end{array}\right),

(27)

with
\sigma^{i}
being the two-dimensional Pauli matrices.
When separately taking into account spatial and time components for both spacetime and tangent-space indices, we will prevent any ambiguity with the tetrad indices (which mix both typologies) by always placing Latin indices first when reading from left to right. For other objects, any possible confusion is avoided by considering their original geometric nature. For brevity, Dirac indices will typically be omitted.

Finally, since the inverse power of
c
is a natural parameter for performing formal expansions in the gravitational context, we shall retain the explicit
c
-units in our equations when needed.

II.1
Spinor Connection in Curvilinear Coordinates

To write down the Dirac equation in curvilinear coordinates
[
38
,
39
]
, it is crucial to define the action of the spacetime covariant derivative
\mathscr{D}_{\mu}
on a Dirac spinor
\psi

\mathscr{D}_{\mu}\psi=\partial_{\mu}\psi+\Gamma_{\mu}\psi\,.

(28)

Here, the spinor connection
\Gamma_{\mu}
is defined by the expression
[
40
]

\Gamma_{\mu}\equiv\frac{1}{4}\sigma^{ab}\,\omega_{\mu\,ab}=\frac{1}{4}\sigma^{ab}\,g_{\nu\rho}\,E_{a}^{\;\;\nu}(\partial_{\mu}E_{b}^{\;\;\rho}+\{^{\;\,\rho}_{\mu\,\alpha}\}\,E_{b}^{\;\;\alpha})\,,

(29)

where
\omega_{\mu\,ab}
is the Spin connection and
\{^{\;\,\rho}_{\mu\,\alpha}\}
are the Christoffel symbols defined in General Relativity (GR)

\{^{\;\,\rho}_{\mu\,\,\alpha}\}=\frac{1}{2}g^{\rho\beta}(\partial_{\mu}g_{\alpha\beta}+\partial_{\alpha}g_{\mu\beta}-\partial_{\beta}g_{\mu\alpha})\,.

(30)

We emphasize that both the curved gamma matrices and the tetrads are designed to be automatically covariantly constant
[
41
,
42
,
43
]

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{\mu}\underline{\gamma}^{\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\underline{\gamma}^{\nu}+\{^{\;\,\nu}_{\mu\,\,\rho}\}\underline{\gamma}^{\rho}-[\Gamma_{\mu},\underline{\gamma}^{\nu}]=0\,,

(31)

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{\mu}e^{a}_{\;\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}e^{a}_{\;\nu}-\{^{\;\,\rho}_{\mu\,\,\nu}\}\,e^{a}_{\;\rho}+{{\omega_{\mu}}^{a}}_{b}e^{b}_{\;\nu}=0\,,

(32)

which is something to keep in mind during the upcoming calculations.

II.2
Spacetime for Accelerated Frames

According to the GR perspective, a laboratory on Earth’s surface is constantly accelerating outward with a certain acceleration
\bm{a}
. This is because, instead of following a geodesic, it remains at a fixed distance from the center of the Earth. From the laboratory point of view, that leads to an “apparent” acceleration
\bm{g}=-\bm{a}
for freely falling objects, which is naturally ascribed to a gravitational force, as in the familiar Newtonian picture.

As long as we want to describe sufficiently small patches of spacetime, meaning small spatial volumes for small time periods
[
44
]
, and therefore deal with (almost) uniform gravitational fields, as happens in the context of spectroscopic experiments with UCNs
[
20
]
, the laboratory acceleration
\bm{a}
can be safely considered constant and homogeneous. Thus, a natural way to geometrically describe this accelerated setting is through the following spacetime line element
ds^{2}
and metric tensor
g_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle ds^{2}=\left(1+\frac{\bm{a}\cdot\bm{x}}{c^{2}}\right)^{2}d(ct)^{2}-d\bm{x}^{\,2}\,,

(33)

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}=diag\left\{\left(1+\frac{\bm{a}\cdot\bm{x}}{c^{2}}\right)^{2},-1,-1,-1\right\},

where we used
x^{0}=ct
,
\bm{a}\cdot\bm{x}=a^{i}x^{i}
and momentarily restored
c-
units. For convenience, in the rest of the article we take the
z-
axis to coincide with the acceleration direction
\bm{a}=a\,\bm{\hat{z}}=-\bm{g}
.

The
ds^{2}
in (
33
) agrees with the one obtained by charting flat spacetime with Kottler-Møller or Rindler coordinates
[
45
,
46
]
, which are the natural set of coordinates adopted by (non-inertial) observers undergoing uniformly accelerated motion. Hence, we should stress that the Riemann tensor associated with (
33
) is zero

{{R_{\mu\nu}}^{\alpha}_{\;\;\beta}}=\partial_{\mu}\{^{\;\,\alpha}_{\nu\,\,\beta}\}-\partial_{\nu}\{^{\;\,\alpha}_{\mu\,\,\beta}\}+\{^{\;\,\alpha}_{\mu\,\,\rho}\}\{^{\;\,\rho}_{\nu\,\,\beta}\}-\{^{\;\,\alpha}_{\nu\,\,\rho}\}\{^{\;\,\rho}_{\mu\,\,\beta}\}=0\,,

(34)

meaning that tidal effects
\propto|x|^{2}
are not depicted in this scheme. However, for the level of precision required in the present work, they would be irrelevant to the final result anyway. Neglecting
|x|^{2}
contributions, the geometric structure above is also approximately equivalent to the spacetime element measured by a static observer resting at some fixed distance
R
from the center of a Schwarzschild background. In fact, the latter represents a non-rotating and accelerating observer whose proper reference frame could be described through Fermi-Walker coordinates
[
47
,
48
]
.

The tetrads that naturally describe an observer standing still in the laboratory, i.e. one that is stationary relative to the accelerated coordinate chart
\{x^{\mu}\}
characterized by (
33
), are

\displaystyle e^{a}_{\;\;\mu}=diag\{V,1,1,1\}\,\,\text{with }\,\,V\equiv 1+\frac{\bm{a}\cdot\bm{x}}{c^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle E_{a}^{\;\;\mu}=diag\{\frac{1}{V},1,1,1\}\,,

(35)

leading to the curved gamma matrices below

\displaystyle\underline{\gamma}^{0}=E^{\;\;0}_{a}\gamma^{a}=\frac{1}{V}\gamma^{0}\,\,,\,\,\,\underline{\gamma}^{i}=E^{\;\;i}_{a}\gamma^{a}=\gamma^{i}\,.

(36)

In Appendix
A
, we include all other geometric quantities relevant to our calculations, such as the components of the spinor connection
\Gamma_{\mu}
and the Christoffel symbols.

III
VSR Dirac Equation in Curvilinear Coordinates

Following the usual prescription of General Relativity, the extension of equation (
9
) to the context of curvilinear coordinates is obtained through the replacement of partial derivatives
\partial_{\mu}
with covariant derivatives
\mathscr{D}_{\mu}

(i\not{\mathscr{D}}-m+i\lambda\frac{\not{n}}{n\cdot{\mathscr{D}}})\psi=0\,,

(37)

where the slashed notation is here used to indicate a contraction with the curved gamma matrices
\underline{\gamma}^{\mu}
. The constancy condition of
n^{\mu}
in inertial frames should also be adapted to curvilinear coordinates. The simplest way to do it is to assume its covariant derivative to vanish

\mathscr{D}_{\mu}n^{\nu}=0\,.

(38)

Although it may not be the unique way to generalize the concept of a constant
n^{\mu}
, in this paper we will use (
38
) as a working hypothesis. In fact, because of the zero curvature (
34
), we do not encounter no-go constraints that would forbid the existence of such a
n^{\mu}

[
49
,
20
]
, and we can always find solutions to the above equation. This also means that
n^{\mu}
would now be position-dependent in general, while still being lightlike.

Alternatively, we might take
n\cdot\mathscr{D}n^{\mu}=0
, the solution of which is a set of null geodesics. Both possibilities allow us to define the VSR equation (
37
) without any ambiguity, since they eliminate ordering problems with the numerator and the denominator of the non-local term. The preference for one over the other depends solely on the assumed fundamental origin of the preferred spacetime direction in VSR.

III.1
“Curved” Hamiltonian in VSR

The procedure to obtain the Hamiltonian associated with equation (
37
) is completely analogous to the one followed for the inertial case in Section
I.2
. Indeed, since the curvature tensor vanishes, the commutator of covariant derivatives acting on
\psi
vanishes accordingly

[{\mathscr{D}}_{\mu},{\mathscr{D}}_{\nu}]\psi=-\frac{1}{4}R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\underline{\gamma}^{\rho}\underline{\gamma}^{\sigma}\psi=0\,.

(39)

From that, it is straightforward to show that the “squared” EOM will have once again the KG structure, but now in curvilinear coordinates. Recalling the definition (
11
) of
m_{f}
, we find

(g^{\mu\nu}{\mathscr{D}}_{\mu}{\mathscr{D}}_{\nu}+m^{2}_{f})\psi=0\,,

(40)

implying the relation

{\mathscr{D}}_{0}^{2}\psi=-(g^{00})^{-1}({\mathscr{D}}_{i}{\mathscr{D}}^{i}+m^{2}_{f})\psi=-V^{2}({\mathscr{D}}_{i}{\mathscr{D}}^{i}+m^{2}_{f})\psi\,.

(41)

At this point, to follow steps 1 and 2 from Section
I.2
, we need to first expand the contractions appearing in the curvilinear equivalent of equation (
13
), i.e.

(n\cdot{\mathscr{D}}\not{\mathscr{D}}+imn\cdot{\mathscr{D}}+\lambda\not{n})\psi=0\,.

(42)

That gives the two following identities:

\displaystyle n\cdot{\mathscr{D}}\not{\mathscr{D}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{0}\underline{\gamma}^{0}{\mathscr{D}}_{0}^{2}+(\underline{\gamma}^{0}n^{i}+n^{0}\underline{\gamma}^{i}){\mathscr{D}}_{i}{\mathscr{D}}_{0}+\underline{\gamma}^{j}n^{i}{\mathscr{D}}_{i}{\mathscr{D}}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\,n^{0}}{V}\gamma^{0}{\mathscr{D}}_{0}^{2}+(\frac{\,n^{i}}{V}\gamma^{0}+n^{0}\gamma^{i}){\mathscr{D}}_{i}{\mathscr{D}}_{0}+\gamma^{j}n^{i}{\mathscr{D}}_{i}{\mathscr{D}}_{j}\,,

\displaystyle imn\cdot{\mathscr{D}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle im\,n^{0}{\mathscr{D}}_{0}+im\,n^{i}{\mathscr{D}}_{i}\,.

(43)

Replacing these expressions in (
42
) together with (
41
) and factorizing the time covariant derivative of
\psi
in the left-hand side, we obtain

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{i}{m}(\gamma^{0}\frac{n^{i}}{Vn^{0}}+\gamma^{i})\mathscr{D}_{i}\right){\mathscr{D}}_{0}\psi=

(44)

\displaystyle=\left(mV\gamma^{0}+\frac{V}{m}\gamma^{0}{\mathscr{D}}_{i}{\mathscr{D}}^{i}-i\frac{n^{i}}{n^{0}}{\mathscr{D}}_{i}-\frac{1}{m}\frac{n^{i}}{n^{0}}\gamma^{j}{\mathscr{D}}_{i}{\mathscr{D}}_{j}\right.

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\left.+\frac{\lambda}{m}V\gamma^{0}-\frac{\lambda}{m}\frac{n_{i}}{n^{0}}\gamma^{i}\right)\psi\,.

Evaluating the covariant derivatives along spatial directions we observe that, since
\Gamma_{i}=0
(see Appendix
A
), they always reduce to ordinary partial derivatives

\displaystyle{\mathscr{D}}_{i}\psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{i}\psi+\Gamma_{i}\psi=\partial_{i}\psi\,,

(45)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{D}}_{j}{\mathscr{D}}_{i}\psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{j}{\mathscr{D}}_{i}\psi+\Gamma_{j}{\mathscr{D}}_{i}\psi-\{^{\;\,\mu}_{j\,\,\,i}\}{\mathscr{D}}_{\mu}\psi=\partial_{j}\partial_{i}\psi\,,

except when time components are involved, e.g.

\mathscr{D}_{i}\mathscr{D}_{0}\psi=(\partial_{i}-\{^{\;\,0}_{i\,\,\,0}\}+\Gamma_{i})\mathscr{D}_{0}\psi=(\partial_{i}-\frac{1}{V}\partial_{i}V)\mathscr{D}_{0}\psi\,.

(46)

Taking that into account, we can write equation (
44
) as

\displaystyle i{\mathscr{D}}_{0}\psi=\mathscr{B}_{\mathscr{D}}

\displaystyle\left(mV\gamma^{0}+\frac{V}{m}\gamma^{0}{\partial}_{i}{\partial}^{i}-i\frac{n^{i}}{n^{0}}{\partial}_{i}-\frac{n^{i}\gamma^{j}}{m\,n^{0}}{\partial}_{i}{\partial}_{j}\right.

(47)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\left.+\frac{\lambda}{m}V\gamma^{0}-\frac{\lambda}{m}\frac{n_{i}}{n^{0}}\gamma^{i}\right)\psi\,,

where we defined the operator

\mathscr{B}^{-1}_{{\mathscr{D}}}\equiv 1-\frac{i}{m}(\gamma^{0}\frac{n^{i}}{Vn^{0}}+\gamma^{i})(\partial_{i}-\frac{1}{V}\partial_{i}V)\,.

(48)

The latter satisfies a relation analogous to (
17
)

\displaystyle mV\gamma^{0}-iV\gamma^{0}\gamma^{i}\partial_{i}=

(49)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{B}_{\mathscr{D}}\left(mV\gamma^{0}+\frac{V}{m}\gamma^{0}\partial_{i}\partial^{i}-i\frac{n^{i}}{n^{0}}\partial_{i}-\frac{1}{m}\frac{n^{i}}{n^{0}}\gamma^{j}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\right),

so that we recover the correct “curved” Dirac equation when

III.3
Restoring Hermiticity and Unitarity

Like in the inertial scenario, the non-inertial VSR Hamiltonian contains non-Hermitian terms, as shown in Appendix

\partial_{t}(\psi,\psi)=\partial_{t}\int d^{3}x\,\psi^{\dagger}\psi\neq 0\,.

However, the latter can be restored by introducing an appropriate spinorial metric

into the definition of the scalar product

(\psi,\psi)_{G}\equiv\int d^{3}x\,\psi^{\dagger}\,G\,\psi\,,

(67)

where

[

\partial_{t}G+i(\mathscr{H}^{g}_{VSR})^{\dagger}G-iG\,\mathscr{H}^{g}_{VSR}=0\,.

(68)

This procedure is applied, for example, in the case of non-homogeneous gravitational fields
[

]

\sqrt{-g}\,{E_{0}}^{0}
(factor that equals unity in our case). For more details, see
[

In the VSR scenario, we obtain the spinorial metric

which is Hermitian and positive definite, as expected for a good metric. More details on its derivation can be found in Appendix
C

\Delta
such that it holds the decomposition

G=\Delta^{\dagger}\Delta\,.

(70)

The latter suggests a redefinition

of the spinor, which implies the transformation of the original non-Hermitian Hamiltonian

into a new one

{\mathscr{H}}^{\Delta}=\Delta\mathscr{H}\Delta^{-1}+i\Delta^{-1}\partial_{t}\Delta\,,

(71)

that is Hermitian with respect to the standard inner product (i.e. using
G=1
) and describes the same dynamics. For our case, we obtain the following exact result

\Delta=\frac{1+\frac{\lambda\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}}{m^{2}}+\sqrt{1+\frac{2\lambda\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}}{m^{2}}}-\frac{\lambda\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}}{m^{2}}\,\gamma^{0}\gamma^{i}\tilde{n}_{i}}{2^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\sqrt{1+\frac{\lambda\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}}{m^{2}}+\sqrt{1+\frac{2\lambda\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}}{m^{2}}}}}\,.

In the hypothesis of VSR emerging as a minor departure from the LI setting, we can reasonably take
|\lambda|\ll m^{2}
, as already mentioned in Section
I.1
. Therefore, expanding formula (

values, we find

\Delta=1+\frac{\lambda}{2m^{2}}(1-\gamma^{0}\gamma^{i}\tilde{n}_{i})\,\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}+O(\lambda^{2})\,,

(73)

and replacing it back in (

\lambda

\displaystyle({\mathscr{H}}^{g}_{VSR})^{\Delta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle mV\gamma^{0}+\frac{\lambda}{m}(V\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}-\frac{1}{2}[\tilde{r}_{\partial},V]\tilde{\mathscr{R}}^{2}_{\partial})\,\gamma^{0}-\frac{\lambda}{2m}[\tilde{r}_{\partial},V]\tilde{\mathscr{R}}^{2}_{\partial}\gamma^{i}\tilde{n}_{i}

(74)

\displaystyle-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{i}(V\partial_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{i}V)-i\frac{\lambda}{m^{2}}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{i}(V\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}\partial_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\mathscr{\partial}_{i}V\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}-\frac{1}{2}[\tilde{r}_{\partial},V]\tilde{\mathscr{R}}^{2}_{\partial}\partial_{i})

\displaystyle-i\frac{\lambda}{m^{2}}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\tilde{n}^{i}\tilde{n}_{j}(V\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}\partial_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{i}V\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}-\frac{1}{2}[\tilde{r}_{\partial},V]\tilde{\mathscr{R}}^{2}_{\partial}\partial_{i})+i\frac{\lambda}{2m^{2}}\sigma^{ij}\tilde{n}_{j}(\partial_{i}V\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}-[\tilde{r}_{\partial},V]\tilde{\mathscr{R}}^{2}_{\partial}\partial_{i})\,,

IV.1
Foldy-Wouthuysen Transformation

The standard way to proceed with the FW transformation is the following. The first step is to divide the Hamiltonian into the so-called “even” and “odd” contributions labeled, respectively, by
\mathscr{E}
and
\Theta

\mathscr{H}=m\gamma^{0}+\mathscr{E}+\Theta\,.

(76)

These operators are identified according to the number of spatial gamma matrices involved and satisfy specific commutation relations

[\mathscr{E},\gamma^{0}]=0\,\,,\,\,\,\,\{\Theta,\gamma^{0}\}=0\,.

(77)

Then, the FW unitary transformation
U\equiv e^{iS}
consists of a change in the spinor and the Hamiltonian of the type

\psi^{\prime}=U\psi\,\,,\,\,\,\,\mathscr{H}^{\prime}=U\mathscr{H}U^{\dagger}-i\,U\partial_{t}U^{\dagger}\,.

(78)

The operator
S
in the exponent of
U
is defined as

S\equiv-\frac{i}{2mc^{2}}\gamma^{0}\Theta\,.

(79)

Working in the NR domain, where
mc^{2}
represents the largest energy scale of the system, we can perform a formal expansion of the transformed Hamiltonian (
78
) up to any order in
\nicefrac{{1}}{{m}}

\displaystyle\mathscr{H^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{iS}\mathscr{H}e^{-iS}-ie^{iS}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(e^{-iS})

(80)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}+i[S,\mathscr{H}]+\frac{i^{2}}{2!}[S,[S,\mathscr{H}]]+\frac{i^{3}}{3!}[S,[S,[S,\mathscr{H}]]]

\displaystyle+\frac{i^{4}}{4!}[S,[S,[S,[S,\mathscr{H}]]]

\displaystyle-\dot{S}-\frac{i}{2}[S,\dot{S}]+\frac{1}{6}[S,[S,\dot{S}]]+{\mathscr{O}}(m^{-4})\,.

In this way, the particle-antiparticle sectors of a Dirac system can be decoupled up to the desired precision by iteratively applying FW transformations. In fact, the operator (
79
) is specially designed to cancel out the leading-order odd contributions in each FW iteration.

\displaystyle H^{g\,\,\text{(}0.5PN)}_{VSR}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H^{g\,\,\text{(}0PN)}_{VSR}+\delta H^{g\,\,\text{(}0.5PN)}_{VSR}

(97)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(m+\frac{\lambda}{m})(1+v)\gamma^{0}+\frac{\gamma^{0}}{2m}(1-\frac{\lambda}{m^{2}})\partial_{i}\partial^{i}

\displaystyle-i\frac{\lambda}{2m^{2}}\sigma^{ij}\tilde{n}_{i}\partial_{j}v\,,

or in terms of the shifted mass
m_{f}
and the explicit content of
v=\bm{a}\cdot\bm{x}

\displaystyle H^{g\,\,\text{(}0.5PN)}_{VSR}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma^{0}\left(m_{f}+m_{f}\bm{a}\cdot\bm{x}+\frac{1}{2m_{f}}\partial_{i}\partial^{i}\right)

(98)

\displaystyle+i\frac{\lambda}{2m_{f}^{2}}\sigma^{ij}\tilde{n}^{i}a^{j}\,.

Contrary to what happened in the 0PN expansion, we now observe the existence of non-trivial VSR corrections, which involve the special direction
\bm{\tilde{n}}
. Those can then be used to experimentally test the VSR hypothesis.

V
Comparison with Experiments

At this point, we are ready to connect our previous results with
q
Bounce
-like experiments and configurations. To do that, we start by recognizing in (
98
) an unperturbed Hamiltonian
H_{0}
(equivalent to the one in (
2
)) and a perturbation
\Lambda
originating from VSR

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{f}+m_{f}\bm{a}\cdot\bm{x}+\frac{1}{2m_{f}}\partial_{i}\partial^{i}\,,

(99)

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2m^{2}_{f}}\bm{\sigma}\cdot(\bm{\tilde{n}}\times\bm{a})\,,

(100)

where we have specialized to matter particles, taking into consideration that

\sigma^{ij}\tilde{n}^{i}a^{j}=-i\epsilon^{ijk}\Sigma^{k}\tilde{n}^{i}a^{j}\underset{\text{matter}}{\Longrightarrow}-i\epsilon^{ijk}\sigma^{k}\tilde{n}^{i}a^{j}\,.

(101)

We should stress that the single term in
\Lambda
is analogous to the spin-orbit coupling in the electromagnetic case, where the spin operator is represented by
\bm{S}=\frac{\hbar}{2}\bm{\sigma}
.

As seen in (
123
), the timescale
t_{a}
of the
\bm{\tilde{n}}-
evolution is so large that the vector
\bm{\tilde{n}}
can effectively be considered constant over the duration (
\sim 10^{-2}\,s
) of each measurement. Thus, we can employ time-independent and degenerate quantum perturbation theory to calculate the 0.5PN corrections to the unperturbed energy levels defined by
H_{0}
. To do that, we first calculate the matrix elements
\Lambda^{N}_{\alpha\beta}
of the operator
\Lambda
in each degenerate eigenspace

\Lambda^{n}_{s_{1}s_{2}}=\bra{N,s_{1}}\Lambda\ket{N,s_{2}}\,,

(102)

where
N
is the quantum number labeling the unperturbed spectrum and
s_{1},s_{2}=\,\uparrow,\downarrow
represents the spin projection over the quantization direction, which in this case is the one of the acceleration, i.e.,
\bm{\hat{z}}
. Recalling
\bm{a}=(0,0,a)
, we use the relation

\bm{\sigma}\cdot(\bm{\tilde{n}}\times\bm{a})=a\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\;0&\tilde{n}^{2}+i\tilde{n}^{1}\\
\tilde{n}^{2}-i\tilde{n}^{1}&\;0\end{array}\right),

(103)

and the information on the unperturbed orthonormal energy levels
\ket{N,s}
of the quantum bouncing ball
[
59
]
, to find

\Lambda^{N}_{s_{1}s_{2}}=\frac{a\lambda}{2m^{2}_{f}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\;0&\tilde{n}^{2}+i\tilde{n}^{1}\\
\tilde{n}^{2}-i\tilde{n}^{1}&\;0\end{array}\right).

(104)

Finally, setting to zero the determinant of
\Lambda-\varepsilon\,\mathbf{1}_{2\times 2}
, we obtain the value of the energy corrections

{\varepsilon_{N}}=\pm\frac{a\lambda}{2m^{2}_{f}}\sin\theta\,,

(105)

which, remarkably, is independent of
N
, meaning that all energy levels are split in the same way.

Let us now present a rough estimate for the simplest case
\theta=\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}}
, assuming the neutron mass
m_{f}\simeq 939.565\,\nicefrac{{MeV}}{{c^{2}}}

[
63
]
and the acceleration value
a=9.8049\,m/s^{2}
measured next to the
q
Bounce
experiment utilizing a falling corner cube
[
64
]
. If we imagine an experiment with the same sensitivity reached by
q
Bounce
, which is at the moment around
\sim 10^{-16}\,eV
, deviations arising from VSR could be detected for values of
\sqrt{|\lambda|}\gtrsim 1\,{TeV}/{c^{2}}
. Nevertheless, the latter is evidently a range far outside our assumption
|\lambda|\ll m^{2}\simeq m^{2}_{f}
. Hence, additional clever ideas should be implemented in the future to increase
q
Bounce
sensitivity to this particular effect.

Interestingly enough, it is possible to classify the correction (
100
) according to the notation implemented by Kostelecký and collaborators in their work
[
20
]
, by simply identifying

(k^{NR}_{\sigma g})^{ij}=\frac{\lambda}{2m^{2}_{f}}\epsilon^{ikj}\tilde{n}^{k}\,.

(106)

However, the bounds found in Ref.
[
20
]
for the trace of such LV tensor are not helpful here, since the trace of (
106
) vanishes exactly.

VI
Discussion and Conclusions

In this work, we have taken an initial step toward analyzing the gravitational phenomenology of VSR fermionic systems by computing the leading and next-to-leading order corrections to the non-relativistic Hamiltonian in an accelerated frame. That is equivalent to the scenario of a uniform and homogeneous gravitational field, which can be considered as the first approximation to real gravitational backgrounds.

The absence of non-trivial modifications observed in the 0PN Hamiltonian further demonstrate the elusive nature of peculiar LV realizations like VSR. The new corrections found at 0.5PN depend instead on the preferred direction introduced in the VSR algebra. Therefore, they can be used to probe novel Lorentz-violating signatures and set new constraints on the VSR parameter for the neutron sector. However, current gravitational spectroscopy experiments, such as
q
Bounce
, only measure spin-conserving transitions, which seem not sensible to the VSR perturbations in the 0.5PN spectrum. Thus, new experimental configurations are needed to enable the investigation of these effects.

Another interesting aspect that emerged from our study is the peculiar time-dependent behavior of the preferred spatial direction
\bm{\tilde{n}}
in accelerated frames. In fact, while the presence of acceleration is what allows for non-trivial VSR terms, we have also shown that the greater the acceleration, the more rapidly
\bm{\tilde{n}}
tends to anti-align with
\bm{a}
, making the new VSR correction in (
105
) vanish and weakening the relevance of initial conditions. Furthermore, this evolution should also depend on the underlying nature that we assume for VSR. Indeed:

•

If
n^{\mu}
emerges from some new internal degree of freedom, its evolution might occur only during measurements, or more generally, during the neutrons’ lifetimes, as long as they remain in free fall.

•

In contrast, if it arises from global spacetime features, as usually considered, its evolution would start together with the appearance of acceleration, implying that in most cases
\bm{\tilde{n}}
may have had enough time to anti-align with
\bm{a}
.

One factor that we have neglected in this analysis is the presence of Earth’s rotation. The latter could alter the evolution of
\bm{\tilde{n}}
because the interplay between the ongoing rotational motion and the tendency to align with
\bm{a}
may give rise to a precession pattern. Moreover, when modeling laboratories on Earth’s surface, rotational effects are certainly relevant, since they can naturally lead to extra 0.5PN corrections. For these reasons, we wish to overcome this limitation in the future.

Appendix B
Evolution of
\tilde{n}^{i}

Consider the differential equation (
65
) that determines the time evolution of the unit vector
\bm{\tilde{n}}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{n}^{i}=\tilde{n}^{i}(\bm{\tilde{n}}\cdot\bm{a})-a^{i}\,,

(112)

where we remember that
a^{i}=\partial_{i}V
is the laboratory constant acceleration vector, which is equal and opposite to the local gravitational acceleration
\bm{g}
. To mimic conditions analogous to those on Earth’s surface, we choose the reference frame such that the acceleration points along the
\hat{z}
-axis, i.e.

\bm{a}=(0,0,a)\,\,,\,\,\,\text{with }a>0\,.

(113)

Moreover, since
\bm{\tilde{n}}
is a unit vector, it is convenient to parameterize it in spherical coordinates

\displaystyle\bm{\tilde{n}}=\left(\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\cos\theta\right).

(114)

Hence, we have

\bm{\tilde{n}}\cdot\bm{a}=a\cos\theta\,.

(115)

Employing these choices of frame and parameterization, the differential equation system (
112
) reduces to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\sin\theta\cos\varphi\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a\sin\theta\cos\theta\cos\varphi\,,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\sin\theta\sin\varphi\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a\sin\theta\cos\theta\sin\varphi\,,

(116)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\cos\theta\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a\cos^{2}\theta-a\,.

We start by explicitly solving the third differential equation in (
116
). Defining the variable
y(t)\equiv\cos\theta(t)
and assuming the initial condition
y(0)=\cos\theta_{0}
, we get

\displaystyle y(t)=\cos\theta(t)=-\frac{1-\beta e^{-2at}}{1+\beta e^{-2at}}\,,

(117)

where we introduced the parameter

\beta\equiv\frac{1+y(0)}{1-y(0)}=\frac{1+\cos\theta_{0}}{1-\cos\theta_{0}}\,.

(118)

Let us now turn to the solution of the first differential equation. Using the auxiliary variable
u(t)\equiv\sin\theta(t)\cos\varphi(t)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{du}{dt}=au(t)y(t)\,,

(119)

subject to initial condition
u(0)=\sin\theta_{0}\cos\varphi_{0}=u_{0}
. Eq. (
119
) can be solved by separation of variables, thanks to the knowledge of the explicit solution for
y(t)
in Eq. (
117
). The final result is

u(t)=u_{0}(1+\beta)\frac{e^{-at}}{1+\beta e^{-2at}}\,.

(120)

To find the explicit behavior on the equatorial plane we observe that

\displaystyle\sin\theta(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-y(t)^{2}}=\frac{2\sqrt{\beta}e^{-at}}{1+\beta e^{-2at}}\,,

\displaystyle\sin\theta_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\sqrt{\beta}}{1+\beta}\,.

(121)

Then, replacing it back in (
120
), we derive

\displaystyle\cos\phi(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos\phi_{0}(1+\beta)\frac{\sin\theta_{0}}{\sin\theta(t)}\frac{e^{-at}}{1+\beta e^{-2at}}

(122)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos\phi_{0}\,,

implying that the
\phi-
angle remains constant over time. Assuming
a=9.8049\,m/s^{2}
as in the main text and defining the acceleration time scale
t_{a}
as

\displaystyle t_{a}=\frac{c}{2a}\sim 10^{7}s\sim 115\,\,days\,,

(123)

the time-dependency of the azimuthal angle can instead be described by

\theta(t)=\cos^{-1}\left(-\frac{\frac{1}{\beta}-e^{-t/t_{a}}}{\frac{1}{\beta}+e^{-t/t_{a}}}\right)\,,

(124)

from which it becomes clear that
\theta=\pi
is a fixed point. Thus, during the evolution of the system, the space direction labelled by
\bm{\tilde{n}}
tends to anti-align with the local acceleration vector
\bm{a}
. The only exception to that is for
\theta_{0}=0
which looks like a point of unstable equilibrium, in the sense that it is stable over time evolution, but any small perturbation would lead to a flip of the
\bm{\tilde{n}}-
orientation.

B.1
Kottler-Møller Coordinates and Constant Vectors

One of the possible charting of a flat spacetime is given by Kottler-Møller coordinates, which naturally adapt to accelerating observers. Starting from a Minkowskian coordinate system
X^{\mu}=\{T,X,Y,Z\}
and taking the acceleration
\bm{a}//\bm{\hat{z}}
, we can define the new curvilinear one
x^{\mu}=\{t,x,y,z\}
through the following definitions

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a}\arctanh{\left(\frac{T}{Z+\frac{1}{a}}\right)}\,,

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X\,,

(125)

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Y\,,

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\left(Z+\frac{1}{a}\right)^{2}-T^{2}}-\frac{1}{a}\,,

which leads to a spacetime element
ds^{2}
analogous to the one seen in (
33
)

ds^{2}=dT^{2}-d\bm{X}^{2}=(1+az)^{2}dt^{2}-d\bm{x}^{2}\,.

(126)

The Jacobian transformation matrix which connects the two coordinate charts is given by

\Lambda_{\nu}^{\;\mu}(x)=\frac{\partial x^{\mu}}{\partial X^{\nu}}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\cosh at}{1+az}&0&0&-\frac{\sinh at}{1+az}\\
0&1&0&0\\
0&0&1&0\\
-\sinh at&0&0&\cosh at\end{pmatrix}.

(127)

At this point, the peculiar time dependence of
\tilde{n}^{i}
can be directly explained using the Kottler-Møller transformation (
125
): Let us start from a lightlike vector
v^{\mu}=(v^{0},\bm{v})
, constant in the old inertial coordinates
\partial_{X^{\mu}}v^{\nu}=0
. Transforming its components to the new coordinates using (
127
), we derive

\displaystyle v^{\mu^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mu}^{\;\mu^{\prime}}v^{\mu}\,,

(128)

\displaystyle v^{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{v^{T}\cosh at-v^{Z}\sinh at}{1+az}\,,

\displaystyle v^{x^{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(v^{X},v^{Y},v^{Z}\cosh at-v^{T}\sinh at)\,.

Now, defining for
v^{\mu}
a quantity analogous to
\bm{\tilde{n}}
in the main text, we obtain

\tilde{v}^{x^{i}}\equiv\frac{v^{x^{i}}}{v^{t}(1+az)}\,,

(129)

the components of which are given by

\displaystyle\tilde{v}^{x^{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{v^{T}\cosh at-v^{Z}\sinh at}\times

(130)

\displaystyle\times\,(v^{X},v^{Y},v^{Z}\cosh at-v^{T}\sinh at)\,.

Finally, considering (
130
) for very long times
t
, we get

\lim_{t\to\infty}\tilde{v}^{x^{i}}=(0,0,-1)\,,

(131)

meaning that
{\tilde{v}^{x^{i}}}
tends to anti-align with the acceleration, exactly as it happened for
\tilde{n}^{i}
. Once again, the only exception to that is for
\bm{v}//\bm{a}
, since it implies
v^{Z}=v^{T}
and then
\tilde{v}^{x^{i}}
is just constantly equal to

\displaystyle\tilde{v}^{x^{i}}=(0,0,1)\,.

(132)

Thus, this is another way to think about the origin of the time-dependent behavior of
\tilde{n}^{i}
.

Appendix C
Hermiticity Concerns and Spinorial Metrics

In this appendix, we include more details on the strategy followed to determine the spinorial metric
G
in VSR and its respective decomposition.

C.1
Adjoint of the VSR Hamiltonian

Initially, we must establish that (
III.1
) is not Hermitian by calculating its adjoint. The latter is defined by the relation

(\mathscr{H}^{g}_{VSR}\psi,\psi)=(\psi,(\mathscr{H}^{g}_{VSR})^{\dagger}\psi)\,.

(133)

In order to move the non-local operators in the Hamiltonian from one spinor to the other, we first observe that

\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}V\psi=V\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}\psi\,-[\tilde{r}_{\partial},V]\tilde{\mathscr{R}}^{2}_{\partial}\psi\,.

(134)

Moreover, exploiting the integral representation for
\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}
and integration by parts, we note that

\int d^{3}x\,\psi\,\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}\,\varphi=\int d^{3}x\,(\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}\psi)\,\varphi\,.

(135)

Defining the operator

\mathscr{K}\equiv(1-\gamma^{0}\gamma^{i}\tilde{n}_{i})\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}\,,

(136)

and dividing the Hamiltonian (
III.1
) into its parts at zero and first order in
\lambda
, we respectively obtain

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{(0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle mV\gamma^{0}-iV\gamma^{0}\gamma^{i}\partial_{i}-\frac{i}{2}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{i}\partial_{i}V\,,

(137)

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V\mathscr{K}\,(m\gamma^{0}-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{i}\partial_{i}+i\tilde{n}^{i}\partial_{i})\,.

It is straightforward to see that, while
\mathscr{H}_{(0)}
is Hermitian,
\mathscr{H}_{(1)}
is not. In fact, we have

\mathscr{H}_{(1)}^{\dagger}=\mathscr{K}\,V(m\gamma^{0}-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{i}\partial_{i}+i\tilde{n}^{i}\partial_{i})+i\mathscr{K}(\tilde{n}^{i}-\gamma^{0}\gamma^{i})\partial_{i}V\,.

(138)

This implies, as anticipated, that

(\mathscr{H}^{g}_{VSR})^{\dagger}=\mathscr{H}_{(0)}+\mathscr{H}_{(1)}^{\dagger}\neq\mathscr{H}^{g}_{VSR}\,.

(139)

C.2
Spinorial Metric
G
in VSR

Our starting assumption for the calculation of
G
is

G=1+\frac{\lambda}{m^{2}}G_{(1)}\,,

(140)

which is based on the following two observations:

•

In the limit
\lambda\to 0
, we must recover a trivial spinorial metric
G=1
.

•

Since the Hamiltonian (
III.1
) contains up to linear terms in
\lambda
, we expect the same for
G
.

Then, matching terms of equal order in
\lambda
, the defining relation (
68
) for the spinorial metric gets split into two separate ones

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}G_{(1)}+i\,[\mathscr{H}_{(0)},G_{(1)}]+i\left(\mathscr{H}^{\dagger}_{(1)}-\mathscr{H}_{(1)}\right)=0\,,\\
\\
[\mathscr{H}_{(1)},G_{(1)}]+\left(\mathscr{H}^{\dagger}_{(1)}-\mathscr{H}_{(1)}\right)G_{(1)}=0\,.\end{cases}

(141)

At this point, it is straightforward to check that taking
G_{(1)}=\mathscr{K}
both of the conditions above are satisfied, so that (
69
) is really the correct solution of (
68
).

C.3
Metric Decomposition

In order to achieve the decomposition of
G=\Delta^{\dagger}\Delta
, we try searching for a
\Delta
structured as follows

\Delta=b_{1}+b_{2}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{i}\tilde{n}_{i}\,,

(142)

which we also assume to be Hermitian. Therefore, from
G=\Delta^{2}
, we obtain the relations

\begin{cases}(b_{1})^{2}+(b_{2})^{2}=1+\frac{\lambda}{m^{2}}\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}\,,\\
2b_{1}b_{2}=-\frac{\lambda}{m^{2}}\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}\,\to\,b_{2}=-\frac{\lambda}{2\,b_{1}m^{2}}\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}\,.\end{cases}

(143)

Since we want
\Delta
to satisfy
\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\Delta=1
, meaning
\lim_{\lambda\to 0}b_{1}=1
and
\lim_{\lambda\to 0}b_{2}=0
, a possible solution when solving for
b_{1}
is

b_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{1+\frac{\lambda}{m^{2}}\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}+\sqrt{1+\frac{2\lambda}{m^{2}}\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}}}{2}}\,,

(144)

and consequently

b_{2}=-\frac{\frac{\lambda}{m^{2}}\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}}{2^{\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{1+\frac{\lambda}{m^{2}}\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}+\sqrt{1+\frac{2\lambda}{m^{2}}\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\partial}}}}\,.

(145)

Plugging these formulas into (
142
), we get to the expression (
72
) for
\Delta
in the main text.

C.4
Positive-Definiteness of
G

The positivity of the spinor norm induced by the generalized inner product requires to have

(\psi,\psi)_{G}>0\,\,\,\,\,,\,\,\forall\psi\neq 0\,.

(146)

To prove the latter statement, we work in momentum space to avoid unnecessary complications. In fact, by means of Fourier transformations, since the metric
G
does not contain
\bm{x}-
dependent coefficients, we can rewrite the scalar product as

(\psi,\psi)_{G}=\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})\hat{G}(\bm{p})\hat{\psi}(\bm{p})\,,

(147)

where we use the “hats” to denote the Fourier transforms of the spinors and the metric. Thus, the condition (
146
) reduces to

\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})\hat{G}(\bm{p})\hat{\psi}(\bm{p})>0\,\,\,\,\,,\,\,\forall\bm{p}\,,\,\,\forall\hat{\psi}(\bm{p})\neq 0\,,

(148)

which can be ensured by determining the positivty of the eigenvalues of
\hat{G}
. Defining the momentum
\bm{p}_{\perp}
perpendicular to
\bm{\tilde{n}}
as

\bm{p}_{\perp}\equiv\bm{p}-(\bm{\tilde{n}}\cdot\bm{p})\;\bm{\tilde{n}}\,,

(149)

we can write
\hat{G}(\bm{p})
as

\hat{G}=1+\frac{\lambda}{m^{2}+\bm{p}^{2}_{\perp}}(1-\gamma^{0}\gamma^{i}\tilde{n}_{i})\,.

(150)

Therefore, thanks to the algebra of Dirac matrices, we can easily calculate the determinant of
\hat{G}-\alpha\,Id_{4}
and find the eigenvalues of
\hat{G}
by solving

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left((1+\frac{\lambda}{m^{2}+\bm{p}^{2}_{\perp}}-\alpha)^{2}-(\frac{\lambda|\bm{\tilde{n}}|}{m^{2}+\bm{p}_{\perp}^{2}})^{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{2\lambda}{m^{2}+\bm{p}^{2}_{\perp}}-2\alpha\,(1+\frac{\lambda}{m^{2}+\bm{p}^{2}_{\perp}})+\alpha^{2}\right)^{2}.

The result is straightforward

\alpha=\begin{cases}1\,,\\
1+\frac{2\lambda}{m^{2}+\bm{p}^{2}_{\perp}}\,.\end{cases}

(152)

Both solutions are clearly always positive for
\lambda>-m^{2}/2
, demonstrating the positive-definiteness of the spinorial metric
G
and its respective norm, as expected.
