FASN for diffuse malignant peritoneal mesothelioma: a prognostic biomarker after CRS+HIPEC and a therapeutic target
Authors: Valentina Doldi, Chiara Maura Ciniselli, Claudia Aurelio, Enrica Favini, Silvio Marco Veronese, Fabio Bozzi, Shigeki Kusamura, Dario Baratti, Silvia Martini, Paolo Verderio, Marcello Deraco, Marco Folini, Paolo Gandellini, Sandro Pasquali, Nadia Zaffaroni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiffuse malignant peritoneal mesothelioma (DMPM) is a rare and aggressive malignancy with limited therapeutic options and poor clinical outcomes. While metabolic reprogramming, including altered lipid metabolism, is a recognized hallmark of cancer, its contribution to DMPM progression and treatment response remains poorly understood.MethodsGene expression profiling was performed on tumor samples from 45 DMPM patients treated with cytoreductive surgery and hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (CRS+HIPEC), comparing patients who developed recurrence within 30 months (N = 13) to those with later relapse or no recurrence (N = 32). Candidate prognostic markers were validated by immunohistochemistry in a cohort of 80 DMPM patients treated with CRS+HIPEC. Associations with progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were assessed using univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. To evaluate therapeutic potential, selective (cerulenin, C75) and non-selective (orlistat) fatty acid synthase (FASN) inhibitors were tested in patient-derived DMPM cell lines. Effects on cell proliferation, cell cycle progression, and apoptosis were assessed. Combination treatments with selinexor (XPO1/CRM1 inhibitor) or IAG-933 (inhibitor of FASN-derived palmitoyl-CoA activity) were also evaluated.ResultsFatty acid synthase (FASN) was significantly upregulated in tumors from patients with early recurrence. High FASN protein expression was associated with reduced PFS (HR = 1.93; p = 0.01) and OS (HR = 1.85; p = 0.02). Multivariate analysis confirmed FASN as an independent predictor of both PFS (HR = 2.24; p = 0.005) and OS (HR = 2.24; p = 0.014). In vitro, FASN inhibition significantly reduced DMPM cell growth, disrupted cell cycle progression, and induced apoptosis, with C75 showing the strongest effects. Combination treatment with FASN inhibitors and selinexor or IAG-933 resulted in enhanced growth inhibition and apoptosis compared with single-agent treatments.ConclusionsFASN represents a novel independent prognostic biomarker in DMPM patients undergoing CRS+HIPEC and a promising therapeutic target. Given the limited treatment options for DMPM, combination strategies incorporating FASN inhibitors with selinexor or TEAD-pathway–targeting agents, supported by existing clinical trial data, may offer a rapidly translatable therapeutic approach.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08281-0.

Background

Diffuse malignant peritoneal mesothelioma (DMPM) is a rare and aggressive primary peritoneal malignancy originating in the mesothelial surface of the peritoneum and typically associated with a dismal prognosis [1]. DMPM has three different histological subtypes, including epitheliod, the most common and associated with the best patient outcomes, sarcomatoid and biphasic [2]. In the past, patients have been treated with loco-regional approaches, including palliative surgery, intraperitoneal chemotherapy and abdominal irradiation. However, these methods proved ineffective, resulting in a patient median survival of only about one year [3]. The current standard of care for selected, eligible patients combines complete cytoreductive surgery (CRS) and hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) and has provided a clear long-term survival advantage, extending the median patient survival to 52–92 months. However, patient’s ineligible for CRS+HIPEC and those who develop a recurrence after this combined treatment regimen experience a severe prognosis, primarily due to the limited efficacy of current systemic treatment options [3, 4]. The search for new therapeutic targets to design of novel treatment strategies is an unmet need in DMPM. Furthermore, identifying new biomarkers is crucial for accurately selecting patients who could benefit from CRS+HIPEC.

The biology of DMPM remains largely unknown, mainly due to the disease’s rarity and the almost complete unavailability of experimental models capable to properly recapitulate clinical tumors. However, progresses have been made on some fronts. Studies on DMPM clinical samples have identified both germline and somatic mutations in key genes thought to play a critical role in the disease pathophysiology. These include the tumor suppressor genes BAP1, NF2, CDKN2A and TP53 as well as in the oncogene SETDB1 [5]. The altered activation of the PI3K and mTOR pathways has also been reported, suggesting a possible therapeutic value in targeting these pathways in DMPM patients [6]. Additionally, a dysregulation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway was observed in clinical samples and primary cell lines, providing insight into the relative chemoresistance of this disease [7, 8].

Dysregulated lipid metabolism, characterized by an accelerated de novo lipogenesis flux, is a common feature in cancer cells used to sustain a high proliferative rate [9]. While data on the contribution of altered fatty acid (FA) metabolism to DMPM progression are lacking, the reported activation of mTOR strongly suggest its relevance. mTOR promotes lipogenesis by activating SREBP1 transcription factor - a master regulator of lipogenic genes such as fatty acid synthase (FASN) [10]. FASN, which catalyzes the de novo synthesis of long chain FAs from acetyl-CoA, malonyl-CoA and NADPH, is overexpressed in many human cancers, including pleural mesothelioma and to be associated with late stages of disease and poor prognosis [11].

In the present study, utilizing annotated clinical specimens from two cohorts of DMPM patients who underwent CRS+HIPEC at our Institution, we analyzed FASN expression at mRNA and protein levels. Our findings demonstrated its relevance as a biomarker of poor prognosis. Moreover, exposing patient-derived DMPM cell lines to FASN inhibitors (FASNis) - orlistat, cerulenin or C75, resulted in the impairment of cell growth and the induction of apoptosis in two in-house developed patient-derived DMPM cell lines, suggesting FASN’s role as a novel therapeutic target for this disease. Moreover, we explored novel therapeutic strategies combining FASNis with clinically relevant drugs for DMPM. Specifically, we combined cerulenin or C75 with selinexor, an inhibitor of the nuclear export protein XPO1/CRM1 that represents a therapeutic target in DMPM [7], and with IAG-933, an inhibitor of TEADs family of transcription factors whose activation relies on FASN-provided palmitoyl-CoA [12, 13]. Both types of drug combinations demonstrated grater anti-growth activity than FASNis alone.

Methods

Patients’ series and gene expression analysis

This retrospective study analyzed data and tumor samples from consecutive patients with DMPM who underwent CRS+HIPEC at the Department of Surgery of Fondazione IRCCS Istituto Nazionale dei Tumori (INT, Milano, Italy) between October 1995 to February 2013 [14]. DMPM were sampled at surgery before the conduction of HIPEC. For cases where a frozen section from a DMPM was available, tumors were used for a gene expression analysis designed to identify biomarkers associated with tumor aggressiveness. Formalin-fixed paraffin samples from all patients were utilized for protein expression analysis with immunohistochemistry (IHC) to validate gene expression findings. All patients provided written informed consent to donate biological material for research purposes and the study was approved by the Institutional Ethical Committee (ID:INT 84/15).

Gene expression

Frozen DMPM tumors from 45 patients treated with CRS+HIPEC from 1995 to 2013 at INT as described above were available for gene expression profile analysis. Extracted RNA was processed for gene expression analysis using the Illumina HumanHT-12 v4. Briefly, 800 ng of total RNA were reverse transcribed, labeled with biotin and amplified overnight using the Illumina RNA TotalPrep Amplification kit according to manufacturer’s protocol. One μg of the biotinylated cDNA was mixed with the Hyb E1 hybridization buffer and then hybridized at 58 °C overnight. Array chips were washed with manufacturer’s E1BC solution, stained with 1 μg/mL Cy3-streptavidine and scanned with the Illumina BeadArray Reader. Raw data were generated using the Illumina BeadStudio 3.8 software and processed using the lumi package of Bioconductor [15]. After quality control, data were log2 transformed and normalized using the Robust Spline Normalization algorithm.

Tissue microarray (TMA) construction and FASN staining

Tumor tissues of 80 DMPM patients treated with CRS+HIPEC at INT from 1995 to 2013 as described above were selected for evaluating FASN expression. TMAs were constructed as previously described [16] by using a tissue-arraying instrument (Beecher Instruments, Silver Springs, MD, USA). For FASN staining, TMA sections were de-paraffinised in xylene, rehydrated through graded alcohols to water and subjected to immunohistochemical analysis using anti-FASN antibody (ab22759; RRID:AB_732316, Abcam, Cambridge, UK 1:100, incubation over-night). Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Adjacent sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin for anatomical reference. Images were acquired by Nikon Eclipse E600 microscope using ACT-1 software (RRID:SCR_018606, Nikon, Minato City, Tokyo, Japan). The FASN staining score was revised by a pathologist (S.V.). A scale ranging from 1 to 4 was defined, where 1 represents ‘undetectable/low staining’, 2 ‘weak staining’, 3 ‘medium staining’, and 4 the highest (strong) staining observed in the tissue sections.

Cell lines

The DMPM cell lines (MP-115 and SSL1) were established from surgical specimens of two patients who underwent surgery at INT, as previously described [17]. DMPM cell lines were maintained in DMEM F-12 medium (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) supplemented with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA), at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The human adult mesothelial MES-F cells (Zen-Bio Inc., Durham, NC, USA) were maintained in MSO-1 (Zen-Bio Inc.) medium supplemented with 10% of FBS, at 37 °C in a humidified incubator at 5% CO2. Cells were periodically monitored for DNA profile by short tandem repeats analysis using the AmpFISTR Identifiler PCR amplification kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) and checked for the lack of Mycoplasma contamination (MycoAlertTM Mycoplasma Detection Kit, Lonza) periodically.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated using QIAzol Lysis Reagent and miRNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) with DNase I digestion (QIAGEN), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA yield and A260/280 ratio were monitored with a NanoDrop ND-2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). cDNA was synthesized using a high-capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). Quantification of gene expression was assessed by qPCR using No AmpErase TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) and the following specific TaqMan gene expression assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) were used: FASN Hs01005622_m1. GAPDH (PN4326317E) (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) was measured as endogenous control for comparative analyses. RT-qPCR results were reported as relative quantity (RQ = 2−ddCt) with respect to a calibrator sample, using the comparative Ct (ddCt) method.

Drugs

Cerulenin (HY-12364; MedChemExpress, D.B.A. Italia S.r.l., Segarte, Italy), C75 (HY-12364/CS-35, MedChemExpress), selinexor (S7252; Selleckchem, Divbio Science Italy, Brenzone, Italy) and IAG-933 (HY-153811; MedChemExpress) were dissolved in dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MI, USA) and stored as stock solution at −80 °C. Working solutions were prepared in a complete cell culture medium just before use. Control cells were exposed to 0.25% (v/v) DMSO in complete culture medium, serving as the experimental vehicle control.

Growth inhibition assay

SSL1 and MP-115 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 4 × 103 cells/well to evaluate the antiproliferative activity of orlistat, cerulenin, C75, selinexor and IAG-933. Twenty-four h after seeding, cells were treated with increasing concentrations of each drug for 72 h, harvested with Trypsin-EDTA (Lonza) and counted with an automated cell counter (Beckman Coulter S.p.A., Milano, Italy). Untreated cells used as control were treated with solvent (DMSO) at 0.25% final concentration in culture medium. The concentration leading to 50% cell growth inhibition (IC50) was calculated from the dose−response curves [% inhibition of cell growth in treated vs untreated cells as a function of the Log10 of drug concentrations] using GraphPad Prism 10.1 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

For combination studies, the drugs were combined at fixed ratio in three concentrations and incubated for 72 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Cell growth inhibitory activity was assayed as described above. The type of pharmacological interaction was assessed by calculating the combination index (CI) at doses effecting 20, 50, and 80% reduction of cell growth, according to the Chou and Talalay’s method using CompuSyn software. A CI < 1 reflects a synergistic interaction; CI = 1 indicates an additive effect; CI > 1 evinces an antagonism.

Cell transfection and FASN knock-down

Cells seeded at the appropriate density were transfected for 4 h with 20 nM of siRNA molecules using Lipofectamine 2000 (RRID:SCR_010903, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Three different SiRNA targeting FASN (siFASN_1 sense strand 5’-AGG GAG ACC UGG UGA UGA ATT-3’; siFASN_2 sense strand 5’-GGG ACA ACC UGG AGU UCU UTT-3’; siFASN_3 sense strand 5’-CCA CCU ACG UAC UGG CCU ATT-3’) were designed using siMAX Design Software and synthesized by Eurofin MWG Operon (Ebersberg, Germany). A control siRNA with no homology to any known human mRNA was also used (5′-UUAACGAGGUGGACUAAGAdTdT-3′). Hereafter, FASN siRNA_1 will be referred to siFASN1, FASN siRNA_2 as siFASN2, FASN siRNA_3 as siFASN3 and control siRNA as siCTRL.

Coimmunoprecipitation

YAP immunoprecipitation was performed using 5 × 105 cells lysed with the Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) Kit (26149, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rotation in the presence of an anti-YAP antibody (D8H1X, RRID:AB_2650491 #14074, Cell Signaling Technology). Immune complexes were subsequently collected following the kit instructions, and eluates were quantified. Equal amounts of protein (100 ng per sample) were then subjected to Western blot analysis.

Protein extraction and western Blotting

Cells were lysed in lysis buffer prepared as described in [18] and collected with a scraper, then boiled for 1 minute and sonicated for 16 seconds with immersion probe at 10% amplitude, 400W/20 kHz (Branson 450 Digital Sonifier, Emerson Electric CO, Ferguson, MO, USA). Twenty micrograms of protein extracts were run on 4–12% precast Bis–Tris sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide or 3–8% precast Tris-acetate gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) and transferred onto Hybond nitrocellulose membranes (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Buckinghamshire, UK). Filters were blocked for 1 h at room temperature in 5% skim milk dissolved in 1× PBS-0.1% Tween 20 or 5% of BSA in 1× PBS and probed overnight at 4 °C with the following antibodies: anti-FASN (sc-20140, RRID:AB_2101095, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc. 10,410 Finnell Street Dallas, Texas 75,220 US), anti-CRM1 (ab24189, RRID:AB_2257217 Abcam), anti-PARP-1 (9542; RRID:AB_2160739, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, US), anti-CASP-3 (9662; RRID:AB_331439, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-mTORC1 (sc-517464, RRID:AB_3186240, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti-phospho mTORC1 (sc-293133, RRID:AB_2861149, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti-p70/S6K (9234, RRID:AB_2269803, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-SREBP1 (95879; RRID:AB_3717758, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-TEAD (13295, RRID:AB_2687902, Cell Signaling Technology). Anti-β-actin (ab8226; RRID:AB_306371, Abcam), anti-vinculin (V9131, RRID:AB_477629; Sigma) and anti-GAPDH (Ab9484, RRID:AB_307274; Abcam) antibodies were used to confirm equal protein loading on the gel. Filters were incubated with the secondary horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-mouse (NA931V, RRID:AB_772210; GE Health-care Life Sciences) or anti-rabbit (NA9340V, GE Healthcare Life Sciences) antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were revealed by the enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (WP20005, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). Membranes were cut to allow simultaneous incubation of different primary antibodies on the same samples. The original western blots were cropped to generate the appropriate figure panels with the relevant lanes. These cropped images were then subjected to uniform image enhancement of contrast and brightness. Molecular weights were determined using SeeBlue™ Plus2 or HiMark™ Pre-stained Protein Standards (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.).

Tunel assay

To evaluate apoptosis, SSL1 and MP-115 were seeded in 25 cm2 flasks at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells/flask and treated for 72 h with cerulenin, C75 or selinexor as single agents, or with selinexor in combination with either cerulenin or C75 at the IC50 of each compound. Floating and adherent cells were collected and processed by TUNEL (Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP Nick End Labeling) using In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit assay according to manufacturer’s instructions (#11684795910, Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Briefly, cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde for 45 minutes at room temperature, permeabilized with 0,1% Triton X-100 in 0,1% sodium citrate for 2 minutes in ice and then incubated with the TUNEL reaction mix for 1 h. Cell were centrifugated for 5 minutes at 1500×g, resuspended in PBS and subjected to FACS analysis (BD Accuri™ C6 Cytometer, Becton Dickinson, Basel, Switzerland). The analysis was performed using the Kaluza software (Software Version 2.1.2, C57477, Beckman Coulter S.p.A.). All the experiments were conducted at least three times.

Cell cycle analysis

SSL1 and MP-115 cells were seeded in 25 cm2 flasks at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells/flask and exposed for 24, 48, and 72 h to the IC50 of each drug as single agents or in combinations (cerulenin+selinexor or C75+selinexor). Both adherent and floating cells were fixed with 70% EtOH and incubated overnight at 4 °C in a staining solution containing 50 μg/mL of propidium iodide and 50 mg/mL of ribonuclease A in 1×PBS. Samples were analyzed with a BD Accuri C6 flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). At least 20,000 events were evaluated and analyzed using the Kaluza software (Software Version 2.1.2, C57477, Beckman Coulter S.p.A.). All the experiments were conducted at least three times.

Statistical analyses

For the 80 DMPM patients, the following clinical and pathological features were considered together with FASN expression: patient age (continuous variable) and sex (female vs male), patient ECOG Performance Status (0 vs 1–2), N stage (0 = negative, 1 = at least one positive), histopathologic subtype (epithelioid vs other), completeness of cytoreduction (categorical variable reported in a scale 0 to 3), proliferation according to ki67 (≤5 vs > 5), the peritoneal cancer index (PCI, continuous variable), and performance of preoperative chemotherapy (not performed vs performed). Standard descriptive statistics (medians and ranges for continuous variables and frequency tables for categorical variables) were used to describe the study cohort characteristics. Associations between FASN expression at IHC and categorical clinical and pathologic features were assessed using a chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, whereas the non-parametric Wilcoxon test was used for continuous variables. Progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were calculated as the time from CRS+HIPEC to the relapse and/or progression and death due to any cause, respectively. The patterns of PFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method Kaplan and Meier [19] and the survival curves were compared using log-rank tests. The prognostic role of each variable on PFS and OS was investigated using univariate and multivariate Cox regression models [20]. The relationship between continuous variables and either PFS or OS was investigated using a regression model based on restricted cubic splines. The initial multivariate model included all variables that achieved a statistically significant association with PFS or OS at univariate analysis to adjust the prognostic effect of FASN expression for known prognostic factors. A more parsimonious final model was then obtained using a backward selection procedure that retained only the variables maintaining the statistical significance. The C-statistic and its 95% Confidence Interval (CI) computed for each model according as described in [21] was used as pivotal measure for performance evaluation. All statistical analyses were performed with the SAS Studio (version 5.2; SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA) adopting a nominal α level of 5%. Graphical representations were created using the R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the survminer package. Data of in vitro experiments were analyzed trough the test of Mann–Whitney or ANOVA test to assess differences in continuous variables between groups of interest.

Results

FASN is an independent prognostic biomarker in patients with DMPM who undergo CRS+HIPEC

Based on this finding, the prognostic value of FASN was investigated in terms of PFS. The median follow-up time was 66 months (Interquartile range (IQR):46–90) with a PFS probability of 27% (95% CI: 14–41%). FASN is the most upregulated gene in relapsed DMPMs vs non-relapsed

To confirm the prognostic relevance of FASN, immunohistochemistry (IHC) analyses were performed on histological specimens from 80 DMPM patients using tissue microarrays (TMAs) (Table 3). The expression levels of this protein varied among specimens and patients were categorized according to their FASN levels using a 4-grade scoring system (Fig. 1 c and Supplementary Figure S1). Consistently with gene expression findings, tumors from patients who experienced a relapse predominantly showed high FASN staining (high score in the 71% of the cases). Conversely, tumors from patients who did not experience recurrence were characterized by low FASN staining (low score in 70% of cases) (Fig. 1 d). FASN staining did not correlate with other clinical-pathological variables, except for peritoneal cancer index (PCI, Wilcoxon p-value: 0.01). At a median follow-up of 163 months (IQR, 132–183 months), the of OS probability of this cohort was 21% (95% CI: 12–31%). The univariate Cox regression analysis revealed a significant association between FASN level for both PFS (low vs high: HR: 1.93, 95%CI: 1.144–3.249; p = 0.014) and OS (low vs high: HR: 1.854; 95%CI: 1.054–3.260; p = 0.032) probability (Fig. 1 e and f; Table 4). We conducted multivariate survival analysis to adjust the prognostic value of FASN for known clinical and pathological features. FASN is exert an anti-proliferative effect on DMPM cell lines

We evaluated the expression levels of FASN at both the mRNA and protein levels in two in-house-established DMPM cell lines (MP-115 and SSL1) derived from two patients with epithelioid DMPM. As shown in Fig. 2a, FASN was found to be significantly overexpressed in DMPM cell lines compared to normal mesothelial cells (MESF). This differential expression indicates its potential role as a therapeutic target. To assess the significance of lipid metabolism deregulation in DMPM, we investigated the effect of both selective and non-selective FASNis on cell proliferation. Exposure of both MP-115 and SSL1 cell lines to increasing concentrations of orlistat, cerulenin or C75 resulted in a dose-dependent reduction of cell growth (Fig. 2 b). These findings suggest that FASN plays a critical role in promoting the proliferation of DMPM cells. Combining FASNis and selinexor inhibits DMPM cell growth

As previously reported, XPO1/CRM1 was found to be significantly overexpressed in DMPM clinical samples compared to normal tissues [7]. Additionally, it represents a therapeutic target as indicated by findings showing that the clinically-approved XPO1/CRM1 inhibitor selinexor [22] induced significant anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic effects in DMPM cell lines and, most importantly, inhibited significantly the growth of subcutaneous and orthotopic DMPM xenografts [7]. Here, we tested the hypothesis that combining FASNis with selinexor could induce an enhanced therapeutic effect in DMPM cells.

According to previous finding [7, 22], western blotting analyses confirmed the overexpression of XPO1/CRM1 in MP-115 and SSL1 DMPM cells (Fig. 2 a). Notably, the exposure of DMPM cells for 72 h to increasing concentration of selinexor resulted markedly inhibition of the cell growth with IC50 values of 0.1 ± 0.06 nM and 0.3 ± 2.1 nM for SSL1 and MP-115, respectively (Fig. 2 c). To further corroborate our results, we employed a phenocopy strategy by using three specific siRNAs against FASN (siFASN1, siFASN2 and siFASN3). RNAi-mediated FASN knockdown markedly reduced FASN protein levels and significantly decreased cell growth in both MP-115 and SSL1 cells 72 h after transfection (Supplementary Figure S2 a-b).

In combination experiments we focused on the more specific FASN inhibitors cerulenin and C75. Cells were simultaneously exposed to increasing concentrations of the FASNi cerulenin or C75 combined with selinexor and the inhibition of the cell growth was assessed after 72 h. An enhanced cell growth inhibition was observed in both MP-115 and SSL1 cells treated with the drug combinations, as indicated by the shift of the dose-response curves compared to single agents (Fig. 3 a and b). Combining FASNis and selinexor induces apoptosis and impairs the cell cycle in DMPM cells

Treatment with cerulenin, C75 or selinexor at their IC50 as individual drugs induced apoptosis in MP-115 cells, with TUNEL-positive cell percentages of 22.5% for cerulenin, 35.1% for C75, and 45.1% for selinexor, respectively. Combined treatments further enhanced the apoptotic response, reaching 60 and 87% of TUNEL-positive cells when either cerulenin or C75 were combined with selinexor, respectively. Conversely, in SSL1 cells, both single agent treatments failed to induce an appreciable apoptotic response. Nevertheless, a modest induction of apoptosis was detected with the combination treatments, reaching 25.3% of TUNEL-positive cells for cerulenin + selinexor and 22.5% for C75 + selinexor (Fig. 4 a, Supplementary Figure S2 c). Western blot analysis further confirmed the induction of apoptosis in MP-115 cells following exposure to individual FASNis and combined treatments. Indeed, cleavage of PARP-1 (c-PARP-1) and caspase-3 (c-CASP-3) was observed in MP-115 cells after single-agent treatments and, to a greater extent, in the presence of combined treatments (Fig. 4 b). Since selinexor inhibits the nuclear export of several proteins involved in the regulation of the cell cycle, including p53, cyclin B1, cyclin D1, cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1a (CDKN1a), and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1b (CDKN1b), we sought to verify whether the cell growth inhibitory activity produced by the combination of FASNis and selinexor, in SSL1 cells, where a very modest apoptotic response was observed, was due to perturbation of the cell cycle. Flow cytometric analysis showed that, comparted to untreated cells, cerulenin alone did not affect the cell cycle of SSL1 cells over time, whereas, the exposure to selinexor resulted in the increase in the percentage of cells in G1 phase at early time points (Fig. 4 c). Notably, when cerulenin was combined with selinexor, a marked increase in the percentage of cells in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle was observed at all time points (Fig. 4 c). In contrast, C75 alone or in combination with selinexor significantly affected the distribution of SSL1 cells along the cell cycle phases, with a marked increase in the percentage of cells in G2/M phase that received the combined treatment (Fig. 4 d). Finally, the effect of these different treatments on mTORC1 was also investigated. Western blotting analysis indicated that the levels of the phosphorylated active form of mTORC1 (p-mTROC1) was downregulated by either cerulenin and C75, as well as by the combined treatments, in MP-115 cells and, more pronouncedly, in SSL1 cells. This downregulation was accompanied by a reduction in the nuclear form of SREBP1 in the treated compared to untreated cells, particularly following the combined treatments (Fig. 4 e and f, Supplementary Figure S3 a). Consistent with the effects observed following FASN inhibition, FASN-silenced cells also showed a significant downmodulation of p-mTORC1, as well as the nuclear and cytoplasmic forms of SREBP1 and P70/S6K (Supplementary Figure S3 c), although to different extents.

Given that YAP/TAZ-TEAD signaling has been recently identified as a critical regulator of the de novo lipogenic program driving proliferation and tumor growth [13], we aimed to evaluate the therapeutic potential of combining TEAD inhibitors (TEADis) with FASNis in DMPM cell lines. Firstly, a class comparison analysis was performed to test wherever selected genes related to YAP/TAZ-TEAD-dependent lipogenic program were differently expressed in DMPM patients who relapsed within 30 months compared to those who experienced a later relapse or remained relapse-free t. No significant differences were observed for NF2, YAP1, TAZ, TEAD1-4, SGK1 or SREBP1 between these two groups of patients (one-sided Mann–Whitney test, p > 0.05) (Supplementary Figure S4 a). Of note, TEAD2 and TEAD4 expression levels were positively – though weakly – correlated with FASN expression in DMPM clinical samples (Supplementary Figure S4 b).

Discussion

DMPM remains one of the most challenging and incurable cancer after failure of the CRS+HIPEC standard treatment, characterized by limited therapeutic options and low success rates of current therapeutics. Consequentially, identifying novel biomarkers to improve patients selection for CRS+HIPEC and uncovering new therapeutic targets are urgent clinical priorities [24]. In this study, we provide the first evidence of FASN potential as both a prognostic biomarker for DMPM patients treated with CRS+HIPEC, as well as a promising therapeutic target. Despite relying on a single institutional cohort, the extreme rarity of this malignancy makes our series uniquely valuable, representing one of the largest and most uniformly treated patient cohorts available. Exploratory gene expression analysis identified FASN as one of the most overexpressed gene in patients who experienced an early relapse. This prognostic relevance of FASN was further validated at the protein level, where IHC analysis on tissue microarray (TMA) confirmed that FASN overexpression is significantly associated with a worse patient prognosis (Fig. 1 and Table 1-6).

Metabolic dysregulation, specifically altered lipid metabolism, is a recognized hallmark of cancer [9]. Lipids are structural components of cell membranes, act as second messengers, and provide a key energy source to the cells. In cancer, lipid metabolism is reprogrammed through increased fatty acid uptake, enhanced de novo lipogenesis, accelerated beta-oxidation, and altered lipid storage [25]. FASN, a central enzyme driving de novo lipogenesis, is frequently upregulated in cancer cells. Beyond this metabolic role, it promotes proliferation, migration, and invasion, regulates key cell signaling pathways, and contributes to immune modulation [26]. These diverse functions highlight the multifaceted role of FASN in cancer progression and underscore its potential as a promising therapeutic target [10].

To date, the specific role of FASN in DMPM remains unexplored. In malignant pleural mesothelioma (MPM), FASN is known to be overexpressed compared to normal tissue, and its pharmacological inhibition has been shown to abolish tumor growth in MPM-bearing mice. Furthermore, a recent study identified FASN as a potential diagnostic and therapeutic target in NF2/p16-deficient MPM linking its activity to mitochondrial dynamics [11]. In both in vitro and in vivo NF2/p16-deficient MPM models, FASN inhibition produced marked antiproliferative effects, supporting its potential as a promising anticancer target [27].

In this study, we explored the potential of targeting FASN using a range of compounds - including orlistat, cerulenin and C75 - providing the first proof-of-concept for the therapeutic relevance of interfering with lipid metabolism in DMPM. These inhibitors act through distinct mechanisms depending on their binding sites and chemical properties. Specifically, orlistat, an FDA-approved anti-obesity drug, inhibits the thioesterase domain of FASN, thereby interfering with fatty acid synthesis [28]. Cerulenin, an antifungal antibiotic, targets the ketoacyl synthase (KS) domain of FASN, which catalyzes the condensation reaction during fatty acid chain elongation, effectively halting this process [29]. C75, a synthetic FASN inhibitor, interacts with multiple domains of the enzyme. It blocks the binding of malonyl-CoA to the β-ketoacyl synthase domain, preventing fatty acid elongation, and also inactivates other enzymatic functions, including enoyl reductase and thioesterase activities [30].

In our DMPM cell lines, which exhibit high baselined levels of FASN, treatment with either cerulenin or C75 markedly reduced cell growth and induced substantial cell cycle perturbation, as shown in Figs. 2 and 4.

Currently, TVB-2640 is the leading FASN inhibitor in clinical development, under evaluation for KRAS-mutant non–small cell lung cancer [31], ovarian and breast cancers either as a monotherapy or in combination with paclitaxel and trastuzumab [31, 32]. Since these studies suggest that FASN inhibitors exhibit only moderate efficacy as monotherapy in advanced tumors [31, 33], combining FASNis with anti-cancer drugs could represent a more effective therapeutic strategy to simultaneously target key molecular vulnerabilities in DMPM [34]. We investigated the synergy between FASNis and selinexor, a first-in-class inhibitor of the nuclear export protein XPO1/CRM1. Our results demonstrated significant anti-tumor activity in DMPM both in vitro and in vivo [7]. Here we showed that the combination of C75 or cerulenin with selinexor significantly enhances growth inhibition, apoptosis, and cell cycle disruption compared to single-agent treatment. Moreover, when we assessed the impact of the combined treatment on SREBP1, a key regulator of the de novo lipogenic pathway, we observed its downregulation—particularly of the nuclear form (n-SREBP1), which drives the transcription of lipid metabolism–related genes, including FASN (Fig. 4 e-f). These data suggest that selinexor may indirectly impair the nuclear trafficking of SREBP1, leading to the accumulation of its inactive cytoplasmic isoform (c-SREBP1) and thereby potentiating the antitumor effects of FASNis (Fig. 4e–f).

We further explored a dual-targeting approach combining FASNis and TEADis. Beyond its oncogenic role, FASN serves as the primary source of fatty acids for the post-translational modification of proteins, specifically protein acylation [26]. TEAD transcription factors can bind the co-factors YAP/TAZ and achieve the maximal tumorigenic activity when palmitoylated via their intrinsic palmitoylating enzyme–like activity. Because TEAD activation depends on the availability of palmitoyl-CoA supplied by FASN, inhibiting FASN may limit TEAD-driven oncogenesis [12, 35, 36]. Furthermore, TEADs regulate the cellular lipogenic program through the SGK1/mTORC1 axis, which controls the activation and the nuclear localization of SREBP1 [13]. Therefore, the combination of FASNis with TEADis may disrupt the FASN-mediated palmitoyl-CoA supply that enforce YAP-TEAD activation, while simultaneously suppressing the de novo lipogenesis program driven by YAP–TEAD through SREBP1 (Fig. 5 d).

Experimentally, we observed that IAG-933 exerted antitumor activity in DMPM models, which was significantly enhanced when combined with C75. This confirms a potential functional interaction between these pathways (Fig. 5 a-c). Mechanistically, the C75 + IAG-933 combination induced a superior reduction of FASN and TEADs expression compared with single agents, particularly evident in the MP-115 cell line.

This synergistic effect is likely driven by the downmodulation of the TEAD-dependent lipogenic regulator SREBP1 together with the disruption of the FASN-mediated TEAD palmitoylation (Fig. 5 e), although further investigation is required to fully elucidate these aspects. Moreover, while previous reports indicate that IAG-933 disrupts the YAP/TAZ–TEAD interaction without affecting TEAD protein levels [37], we observed a reduction in total TEAD protein following prolonged treatment, along with a moderate effect on the YAP–TEAD interaction, as assessed by co-immunoprecipitation (Supplementary Figure S3 c). This may reflect context-dependent effects, such as feedback regulation or decreased TEAD stability upon prolonged inhibition. Further studies are needed to better clarify this mechanism, including assessment of YAP–TEAD interaction and TEAD protein turnover.

In this study, we focused on IAG-993, a TEADis that disrupts the interaction between YAP/TAZ and TEADs. As a small molecule, IAG-993 has demonstrated robust in vivo tumor suppression and has gained considerable interest. It is currently under clinical investigation in advanced mesothelioma and other solid tumors, including tumors with strong YAP/TAZ-TEAD-dependent solid tumors, such as epithelioid hemangioendothelioma [38]. Furthermore, TEAD palmitoylation inhibitors – which also shown marked in vivo antitumor efficacy - represent an alternative therapeutic strategy, that may further amplify the disruption on the YAP–TEAD–dependent lipogenic pathway [37].

Conclusion

In this study, we have proposed FASN as an independent prognostic biomarker for patients with DMPM. Given that this tumor remains largely incurable—especially for patient’s ineligible to surgery or those who relapse after CRS+HIPEC and have extremely limited therapeutic options—we also identify FASN as a potential therapeutic target. While our findings are based on a unique institutional cohort, validation in an independent, ideally multicentric cohort would further strengthen the robustness and translational relevance of these results. We further demonstrate the rationale for combining FASNis with either selinexor or TEADis, which showed marked antitumor efficacy in our DMPM models. As these findings are currently limited to in vitro systems, in vivo validation would be essential to confirm their therapeutic efficacy. Nevertheless, our results provide a strong preclinical foundation for further investigation, and given that all these agents have been individually tested in clinical trials, the proposed strategy may represent a rapid path toward clinical translation, leveraging existing phase I data [38, 39] and offering a feasible therapeutic opportunity for DMPM patients.
