Improving Effective Prenatal Care for New Obstetric Patients Using a Standardized Intake Template and Pregnancy Risk Calculator
Authors: Morgan De Kleine, Sharanna S Johnson, Kaya S. Belknap
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionKenya has the 16th highest maternal mortality rate and the 58th highest infant mortality rate in the world. Most of these deaths are preventable and attributed to inadequate prenatal care. A chart audit at Africa Inland Church Kijabe Hospital revealed significant gaps in care at the initial prenatal visit. This project aimed to increase effective prenatal care for all new obstetric patients seen at the Maternal and Child Health Clinic to 75% within 8 weeks.MethodsThis quality improvement initiative consisted of two core interventions implemented over four Plan-Do-Study-Act cycles. Each 2-week cycle was guided by a test of change, and the data were analyzed at the end of each cycle. The first core intervention was implementing a new obstetric standard of care, based on 32 evidence-based guidelines from the World Health Organization and the Kenya Ministry of Health, which were embedded into a standardized intake template at the initial prenatal visit. The second core intervention was implementing a pregnancy risk calculator, using the Alberta Antenatal Risk Assessment, to classify each new obstetric patient’s pregnancy as low, moderate, or high risk.ResultsThis project achieved its aim statement and increased effective prenatal care from 21% to 77% in 8 weeks. Implementing the standardized intake template increased the mean standard-of-care score from 42% to 83%, with the greatest improvements seen in documenting a complete medical history and ordering prenatal labs. The number of high- or moderate-risk pregnancies identified was 33%, providing an opportunity for timely referral to the high-risk pregnancy clinic.ConclusionThe tools used in this quality improvement initiative provided a low-cost method to promote high-quality prenatal care. The standardized intake template can be adapted to other countries and contexts; further studies are needed to evaluate its impact on birth outcomes. Further research is needed to analyze its effectiveness and to develop and validate a pregnancy risk calculator for sub-Saharan Africa.

INTRODUCTION

Kenya has the 16th highest maternal mortality rate and the 58th highest infant mortality rate in the world.[1,2] Effective prenatal care plays a key role in preventing maternal and infant deaths, as it is estimated that 90% of maternal deaths in Kenya are due to suboptimal care.[3] In addition, 95% of neonatal deaths in Kenya are from preventable causes, predominantly related to pregnancy and birth complications.[4] In fact, just one prenatal visit in East Africa can reduce the neonatal mortality risk by 42%.[5] The World Health Organization currently recommends at least eight prenatal visits for optimal outcomes,[6] but in Kenya, only 4% of pregnant women attend at least eight visits, and only 66% attend at least four visits.[7] Two other challenges compound the issue of inadequate prenatal care in this context: only 33% of women in Kenya initiate prenatal care in the first trimester,[7] and only 66% receive complete care according to national guidelines at their initial visit.[8] Although the issue of inadequate prenatal care in Kenya is well-documented, fewer studies describe how to promote high-quality, effective prenatal care in this context.

The World Health Organization and the Kenya Ministry of Health have both published evidence-based guidelines, based on systematic reviews, that define the essential components of high-quality prenatal care.[6,9] The initial visit should include a complete history and physical examination, blood pressure and weight measurements, lab work, an early ultrasound, nutritional supplementation, and prenatal education.[6,9] Establishing the patient’s risk status at the first visit is also important, as women with high-risk pregnancies require closer monitoring for optimal outcomes.[10] Pregnancy risk calculators provide a systematic method to identify high-risk pregnancies, and various scoring systems have been developed and validated in the Netherlands, Canada, and Australia,[11–13] and also widely used in developing countries, such as India, Nepal, and Saudi Arabia.[14–16] The authors were unable to find any studies from sub-Saharan Africa that used pregnancy risk calculators to identify high-risk pregnancies at the initial visit.

Effective care was the framework for this quality improvement initiative, which focused on improving the quality of prenatal care for new obstetric patients at a Maternal and Child Health (MCH) clinic. According to the Institute of Medicine, effective care is evidence-based, grounded in scientific knowledge, and proven to be beneficial.[17] In this context, effective care was delivered by standardizing the initial prenatal visit using an intake template aligned with the World Health Organization’s and the Kenya Ministry of Health’s prenatal guidelines. Effective care was also promoted by using a validated pregnancy risk calculator at the initial prenatal visit to systematically identify high-risk pregnancies and facilitate timely referrals to the high-risk pregnancy clinic. Through these two core interventions, this project aimed to increase effective prenatal care for all new obstetric patients seen at the MCH clinic of Africa Inland Church (AIC) Kijabe Hospital to 75% within 8 weeks.

METHODS

This quality improvement initiative was deemed exempt from the institutional review board by the leadership of AIC Kijabe Hospital per hospital policy. Patient consent was waived because all information collected from chart audits was deidentified. The Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE) 2.0 guidelines were used to report this study’s outcomes,[18] providing a framework to ensure methodologic rigor and transparency.

Study Setting

AIC Kijabe Hospital was founded as a mission hospital in rural Kenya in 1915 and is now a nonprofit, 363-bed, tertiary teaching and major referral hospital in East Africa, where almost 2400 births occur annually.[19] The MCH clinic serves approximately 250 patients each week, including pregnant and postpartum women, as well as infants and children younger than 5 years. New obstetric patients comprise approximately 10% of the total clinic volume, and the majority are African, aged 18–45 years, married, and self-pay. During this quality improvement initiative, the project team included the MCH nurses (who obtained blood pressure, height, and weight measurements for patients), the clinical officers and medical residents (who provided prenatal care to the new obstetric patients), the family medicine consultant (who offered guidance to the clinical officers and residents), the head of the outpatient department (who ensured that clinical policies and protocols were followed during implementation), and the project leader.

Pre-Intervention Phase

A chart audit (N = 53) performed by the project leader at the MCH clinic in January 2024 found that many new and returning obstetric patients were not receiving complete care in accordance with the clinic’s guidelines.[20,21] Significant gaps were noted in history-taking skills, ordering lab work, performing physical examinations, prescribing recommended treatments, and providing patient education. Surveying pregnant patients (N = 30) at the clinic revealed many knowledge gaps. Interviews with clinical staff and administrators uncovered multiple causes for inadequate prenatal care, especially during the first visit. A team meeting was scheduled with all the staff at the MCH clinic, and a collaborative cause-and-effect (fishbone) diagram was created to reveal the root causes of inadequate care at the initial prenatal visit (see Supplemental Fig. S1, available online). In addition, the MCH team completed a survey with Likert-scale and open-ended questions about the strengths and challenges of providing prenatal care in this context. After gathering the above data, the hospital administration and the project leader made a collaborative decision to focus on improving the quality of prenatal care for new obstetric patients.

Interventions

This quality improvement initiative consisted of two core interventions that were implemented across four Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles. Each 2-week PDSA cycle was guided by a test of change (Table 1), and the data were summarized and analyzed at the end of each cycle. All new obstetric patients seen at the MCH clinic from April 15 to June 7, 2024, were included in this quality improvement initiative. Both core interventions were intended to address multiple root causes of inadequate prenatal care identified through the cause-and-effect (fishbone) diagram.

The first core intervention was the implementation of a new obstetric standard-of-care intake template, based on 32 best-practice standards derived from the prenatal care guidelines published by the World Health Organization and the Kenya Ministry of Health.[6,9] All components of prenatal care universally recommended by the World Health Organization were included. However, some context-specific recommendations were excluded if not part of the Kenya Ministry of Health guidelines.[6,9] The Kenya Ministry of Health’s recommended guidelines for the initial prenatal visit were also incorporated,[9] as were the MCH clinic’s detailed guidelines for history-taking.[21] These 32 best-practice standards included obtaining a complete medical history at the initial prenatal visit, screening for substance use and tuberculosis symptoms, and recording allergies, current medications, and tetanus vaccination status. These guidelines also included obtaining height, weight, and blood pressure measurements, as well as ordering appropriate prenatal labs to screen for blood type and Rhesus status, anemia, syphilis, HIV status, gestational diabetes, and urinary tract infections. These standards also incorporated performing a complete physical examination, ordering an early ultrasound, listening to fetal heart tones, and measuring fundal height (if indicated based on gestational age). Further standards included recording the estimated due data (with justification about whether it was based on the patient’s last menstrual period or an early ultrasound), determining the patient’s pregnancy risk status, reviewing pregnancy danger signs, providing counseling on a healthy diet and exercise in pregnancy, prescribing iron and folic acid supplements, administering the tetanus vaccine and deworming medication if indicated, and scheduling the next visit according to the World Health Organization’s guidelines.

These 32 best-practice standards were embedded into a standardized intake template for new obstetric patients. Because the existing electronic health record could not be easily modified to build templates, a Google extension program called Text Blaze was used to create the template. While using the electronic health record, the MCH providers entered a short phrase to open and complete the Text Blaze template, which could then be directly inserted into the patient’s chart. The project leader used a chart audit tool at the end of each clinic day to review all new obstetric charts for compliance with the 32 best-practice standards, and a mean standard-of-care score was generated based on the percentage of standards met. The purpose of this intervention was to increase staff adherence to the national and international prenatal care guidelines, while reducing time spent on documentation.

The second core intervention was the implementation of a new obstetric risk assessment tool. Because a pregnancy risk calculator has not yet been developed and validated for sub-Saharan Africa, the Alberta Antenatal Risk Assessment was used,[22] which has been validated in Canada and has also been successfully implemented in Saudi Arabia.[16] This tool was used to screen new obstetric patients for 46 risk factors across 4 categories, including prepregnancy history (e.g., advanced maternal age, current diabetes, chronic hypertension), past obstetrical history (e.g., previous stillbirth, previous preterm delivery, previous Cesarean section), problems in the current pregnancy (e.g., multiple pregnancy, gestational hypertension, anemia), and other risk factors (e.g., alcohol or drug use during the pregnancy). The pregnancy risk calculator was embedded into the Text Blaze standardized intake template. The MCH provider selected the relevant risk factors, and the patient’s pregnancy risk score was automatically calculated as low (0–2), moderate (3–6), or high (≥7) and entered into the patient’s chart. The purpose of this intervention was to provide a systematic method to identify high- and moderate-risk pregnancies at the initial prenatal visit and to facilitate timely referrals to the high-risk pregnancy clinic.

Before the start of these two interventions, the MCH clinicians opened a blank note in the electronic health record and were responsible for remembering and documenting all essential components of the initial prenatal visit. The MCH providers also had to rely on their best clinical judgment to identify high-risk pregnancies and determine when referrals to the high-risk pregnancy clinic were indicated. Before beginning this quality improvement initiative, staff were trained to use Text Blaze and given time to practice using all components of the template, including three built-in calculators for determining the estimated due date, the prepregnancy body mass index, and the pregnancy risk score. Drop-down menus and checkboxes helped staff complete various aspects of the history and physical in a timely fashion. During implementation, the project leader was on site at the start and end of each clinic day to answer questions.

Measures

Six core measures were used to evaluate this quality improvement initiative, including two process measures, two outcome measures, the aim measure, and the balancing measure. The process measures tracked the use of the two tools, and the outcome measures tracked their effectiveness, with the full operational definitions included in Table 2. The process measure for the first tool was the proportion of new obstetric visits in which the providers used the standard-of-care intake template. The outcome measure was the mean standard-of-care score generated by the chart audit tool, based on the percentage of the 32 best-practice standards that were met. The process measure for the second tool was the proportion of new obstetric visits in which the providers used the pregnancy risk calculator. The outcome measure for the pregnancy risk calculator was the proportion of new obstetric patients whose pregnancies were identified as high or moderate risk. The aim measure (which was the measure of effective prenatal care) was a composite of the mean standard-of-care score and the use of the pregnancy risk calculator.

Throughout this quality improvement initiative, the same project leader entered data from all new obstetric charts daily into the chart audit tool. This ensured consistency, reliability, and completeness of the data throughout the four PDSA cycles. To ensure the core interventions did not place an undue burden on the MCH staff, the balancing measure was the staff’s perception of their workload when caring for new obstetric patients, which was measured through a weekly Likert-scale survey. The MCH team’s overall response to the initiative was also evaluated using a pre- and post-implementation survey that included both Likert-scale and open-ended questions.

Data Analysis

Quantitative data were collected daily from the charts of all new obstetric patients seen at the MCH clinic using a standardized chart audit tool created in Google Forms, which was then exported to Google Sheets for analysis. The charts of all new obstetric patients presenting for routine prenatal care were included. During each PDSA cycle, the data were aggregated into summary tables and analyzed for demographic disparities and low-scoring metrics. The data were also plotted on run charts every 3 to 4 days and analyzed for shifts and trends to indicate special-cause variation. Qualitative data were collected by having the project leader record daily feedback from the MCH staff on the benefits and challenges of implementation. The project leader analyzed these daily field notes for common themes at the end of each PDSA cycle. The analysis of both quantitative and qualitative data helped to guide the iterative tests of change for each PDSA cycle.

RESULTS

Pre-Intervention Data

Before the implementation of this quality improvement initiative, baseline data for the mean standard-of-care score were established through a focused chart audit (N = 21) of all new obstetric patients over a 5-day clinic week. As seen in Table 3, the mean standard-of-care score for the 32 best-practice standards was 42% at baseline. Significant gaps in care were noted, as medical history was documented for 43% (n = 9) of new obstetric patients, surgical history was documented for 14% (3), allergies were documented for 38% (8), and current medications were documented for 14% (3). None of the patients were screened for substance use. Only 38% (8) received a urinalysis, and only 19% (4) had an estimated due date recorded with justification. None received a complete physical examination or counseling on a healthy diet and exercise in pregnancy. Only 33% (7) were given a tetanus vaccine if indicated, and none were given deworming medication if indicated. Because the pregnancy risk calculator was not yet being used, the baseline process measure for this tool was 0%. The baseline aim measure was 21% (the composite of the baseline mean standard-of-care score and the baseline use of the pregnancy risk calculator).

Interventions

Throughout this project, 177 new obstetric patients were seen, and effective prenatal care increased from 21% to 77% overall, surpassing the project’s aim of 75%. The aim measure was a composite of the mean standard-of-care score on the standardized intake template (83%) and the utilization of the pregnancy risk calculator (71%). The goal of 75% was selected because it was more than triple the baseline. As seen in Figure 1, although there were no shifts or trends on the aim run chart to indicate special-cause variation, the median exceeded the goal with an overall upward trajectory.

The demographic profile of the study’s participants mirrored the pregnant population seen at MCH. As shown in Table 4, all 177 new obstetric patients were African women aged 19–44 years, with 29% (n = 51) aged 35 years and older. Most were married (86%, 152) and self-pay (84%, 148). The most common occupations reported were businesswoman (33%, 59), housewife (16%, 28), and financial services employee (10%, 18). Of the new obstetric patients seen, 21% (38) presented in the first trimester, 24% (42) in the second trimester, and 55% (97) in the third trimester, highlighting a regional challenge.[23] The mean distance traveled to the clinic was 46 km, with a range of 1–735 km, underscoring the wide radius served by AIC Kijabe Hospital.

New Obstetric Standard of Care Tool

During implementation of this quality improvement initiative, the new obstetric standard-of-care intake template was used for 86% (153/177) of new obstetric patients, exceeding the 75% goal. The mean standard-of-care score increased from 42% to 83%, exceeding the 80% goal. The greatest improvements were seen in history-taking and ordering prenatal labs. More modest gains were seen in completing physical examinations and offering preventive treatment (e.g., dietary counseling, tetanus vaccines). Data from all new obstetric charts were included; no data were missing. When the template was implemented in PDSA cycle 1, use increased from 0% to 75% (40/53), with a mean standard-of-care score of 75%. This was the most impactful test of change for both the process and the outcome measures. After daily team feedback was obtained during PDSA 1, modifications were made to the intake template, including an expanded plan-of-care section for each trimester. During PDSA 2, template use increased to 90% (38/42), and the mean standard-of-care score rose to 85%. The lowest-scoring metric in PDSA 2 was documentation of a prepregnancy body mass index; therefore, all providers were trained on this calculation for PDSA 3, and the nurses were encouraged to record both height and weight. Although documentation of the prepregnancy body mass index nearly doubled from 32% (12/38) to 63% (20/32) in PDSA 3, this did not significantly affect the mean standard-of-care score, which remained at 85%. Template use also did not improve, as it was 89% (32/36). During PDSA 4, team huddles were held three times per week to promote team engagement and unity. Template use increased to 93% (43/46), and the mean standard-of-care score rose to 88% during this final PDSA cycle.

New Obstetric Risk Assessment Tool

The pregnancy risk calculator was used for 71% (125/177) of all new obstetric patients, meeting the benchmark goal of 75% in PDSA cycles three and four, although not for the project overall. One-third (33%; 41/125) of pregnancies were identified as high or moderate risk (henceforth referred to as the positivity rate), with 67% (84) classified as low risk, 26% (32) classified as moderate risk, and 7% (9) classified as high risk. The referral rate to the high-risk pregnancy clinic was 15% (18/122). Data from all new obstetric charts were included; no data were missing. When the pregnancy risk calculator was implemented in PDSA 1, use increased from 0% to 57% (30/53), which was the most impactful test of change for the process measure. The positivity rate was 17% (5/30), although chart audit data revealed that some pregnancy risk factors were overlooked by staff. During PDSA 2, the clinicians were given a small package of cookies each day they used the pregnancy risk calculator, which increased the use rate to 71% (30/42). In addition, the World Health Organization’s definitions of certain terms (e.g., stillbirth) were added to the pregnancy risk calculator to improve clarity, and the positivity rate increased to 37% (11/30). The focus of PDSA 3 was to improve staff familiarity with the pregnancy risk calculator. This was accomplished through case study practice at a team meeting and by posting a top 10 reference list on each provider’s desktop computer of the most common pregnancy risk factors seen at MCH. Use of the pregnancy risk calculator increased to 78% (28/36) in PDSA 3, with a positivity rate of 43% (12/28), which was higher than expected but seemed closely correlated with the fact that 42% (15/36) of new obstetric patients were aged 35 years and older in this cycle. To encourage team motivation during PDSA 4, daily progress toward benchmark goals was shared. Despite an influx of new staff during PDSA 4, pregnancy risk calculator use increased slightly to 80% (37/46), with a positivity rate of 35% (13/37) in this final PDSA cycle.

Staff Perception of Workload

The balancing measure for this project was the staff’s perception of their workload when caring for new obstetric patients. Unexpected challenges impacted this measure, including a nationwide doctors’ strike at public hospitals that lasted for 56 days and significantly increased the patient census at this nonprofit hospital at the start of this quality improvement initiative. In addition, unprecedented flooding and mudslides occurred, sadly claiming many lives from a nearby community and impacting staff morale. At baseline, staff reported their workload when caring for new obstetric patients as 4.6 on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not manageable to 5 = very manageable). As seen in Figure 2, this decreased to 3.1 during implementation, before returning to baseline at week 8. When surveyed about how well the MCH staff serve new obstetric patients, the staff rated pre-implementation care at 4.1 and post-implementation care at 4.6 on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not very well to 5 = very well), indicating improved overall satisfaction with the clinical care provided to new obstetric patients.

DISCUSSION

This project achieved its aim statement and increased effective prenatal care from 21% to 77% in 8 weeks. Using the new obstetric intake template increased the mean standard-of-care score from 42% to 83%, with improvements seen in the majority of the best-practice standards. Using the new obstetric risk calculator led to 33% of pregnancies being identified as high or moderate risk, providing an opportunity for enhanced care or referral to the high-risk pregnancy clinic.

Throughout this quality improvement initiative, 33% (41/125) of pregnancies were classified as high or moderate risk at the initial visit using the pregnancy risk calculator. An earlier study in Kenya categorized 26% (1148/4419) of pregnancies as high risk at the initial visit.[10] The slightly higher rate in this project is likely due to the more comprehensive pregnancy risk calculator used (with 46 risk factors evaluated), the inclusion of both high- and moderate-risk pregnancies, and the large volume of participants aged 35 years and older (29%; 51/177), a population known to have more pregnancy complications.[24] In the post-implementation team survey, several MCH providers reported that the pregnancy risk calculator was a useful tool for helping them to objectively identify high- and moderate-risk pregnancies. This is the first known report of the implementation of a pregnancy risk calculator in Kenya to identify high-risk pregnancies at the initial visit. Further studies are needed to determine whether early identification of high-risk pregnancies leads to improved birth outcomes in this context.

Although the definition of high-quality prenatal care varies across the literature,[25] the challenge of inadequate prenatal care in sub-Saharan Africa is well documented. A study of 79,725 pregnant women in 13 countries in this region found that only 54% of pregnant women received 4 basic components of prenatal care.[26] Another study analyzing 6 basic components of prenatal care among 87,068 pregnant women in 9 countries in East Africa found that only 21% received all 6 components.[27] A recent study of 1002 pregnant women in Kenya found that only 66% received complete care at the initial visit, as evaluated using 20 indicators of prenatal care.[8] This quality improvement initiative also identified gaps in prenatal care, but it went a step further than earlier studies by implementing a standardized intake template to address them.

Of the 32 best-practice standards measured in this study, significant gains were observed across all aspects of history-taking skills and ordering recommended prenatal labs. As shown in Table 3, adherence to each of the 11 metrics associated with taking a complete history was 91% or higher during PDSA 4. An increase was seen across all six prenatal labs ordered, with the greatest improvement noted in ordering a urinalysis (increasing from 38% at baseline to 87% overall). Documenting an estimated due date with justification also improved significantly (increasing from 19% at baseline to 72% overall). More modest gains were noted for performing a complete physical examination (increasing from 0% at baseline to 55% overall), calculating a prepregnancy body mass index (increasing from 0% at baseline to 48% overall), providing counseling on a healthy diet and exercise (increasing from 0% at baseline to 26% overall), administering the tetanus vaccine (increasing from 33% at baseline to 68% overall), and prescribing deworming medication (increasing from 0% at baseline to 44% overall). Further studies are needed to determine which components of high-quality prenatal care have the greatest impact on maternal and neonatal outcomes. More research is also needed on how to develop the infrastructure (e.g., supplies, technology, trained staff) to support these essential components of prenatal care.[28,29] Further studies may also want to explore how standardized templates might benefit other MCH clients, including postpartum, neonatal, and pediatric patients.

Templates have been shown to improve documentation, strengthen the quality of health care, increase adherence to clinical guidelines, promote efficiency, and boost provider satisfaction.[30,31] Using an intake template to standardize care was particularly effective in improving care in this context, as new staff (including clinical officers, residents, and medical interns) with various levels of experience rotated into the MCH clinic each week. Although there were variations in clinicians’ levels of competency when caring for new obstetric patients, the mean standard-of-care score increased from 42% to 83% during the project overall, which was a strength of this study. The staff also reported to the project leader that the intake template saved them time when documenting and increased their confidence that they were providing all necessary components of prenatal care at the initial visit.

Despite the observed improvements, the frequent rotation of staff into the MCH clinic also posed a challenge to the sustainability of this project because the project leader needed to be on site daily to orient new staff to these tools. To address this concern, a sustainability plan was developed and shared with the hospital administration, and the hospital applied for a grant to cover the cost of building and integrating standardized templates into the existing electronic health record. Further studies are needed to determine the most effective ways to help hospitals in resource-limited settings improve infrastructure and have access to digital tools to improve standardization of care and increase adherence to national and international guidelines.[28,29]

Quality improvement initiatives often face barriers, and studies indicate that low- and middle-income countries encounter more barriers than high-income countries due to less-developed infrastructure and technology, as well as fewer personnel trained in quality improvement.[32] During the implementation of this study, unforeseen obstacles (e.g., regional flooding, a nationwide doctors’ strike, an increase in patient census, difficulty modifying the electronic health record, and an influx of new staff) posed challenges. As seen in Figure 2, the MCH team’s perception of their workload worsened initially before returning to baseline. Despite all the difficulties, substantial gains in prenatal care were still achieved. As observed during the COVID-19 pandemic, quality improvement changes can occur rapidly, even in the face of ongoing challenges.[33,34] Therefore, other sites should not be deterred from improving the quality of care, even if unanticipated hurdles arise.

Limitations

This was a single-site quality improvement initiative, which may limit its generalizability to other settings in Kenya or sub-Saharan Africa, although the tools used are adaptable. The new obstetric standard-of-care tool was based on the best-practice standards of the World Health Organization and the Kenya Ministry of Health; however, it is not a validated tool, which is a limitation that could have affected the accuracy of this project’s results. As other studies have described, it is difficult to define high-quality or adequate prenatal care, as national and international guidelines vary on this topic.[8,25–27] The new obstetric risk assessment tool (the Alberta Antenatal Risk Assessment) was validated in Alberta, Canada, but not in Kenya. Some new obstetric patients seen during this study had risk factors not listed on the pregnancy risk calculator, which may have led to underdiagnosis of high-risk pregnancies, reinforcing the need to develop and validate a pregnancy risk calculator for sub-Saharan Africa. Because the new obstetric intake template could not be built into the existing electronic health record, Text Blaze became a feasible, low-cost workaround solution. However, some clinicians faced technical challenges, as it was difficult to view the patient’s electronic health record and the Text Blaze template simultaneously. As data were only measured throughout four PDSA cycles, further studies are needed to determine whether the results of this quality improvement initiative were sustained over time or if regression occurred. Obtaining accurate results for the balancing measure proved challenging because new staff rotated into the clinic weekly and may have had different responses to this quality improvement initiative. Although the MCH staff’s perception of their workload when caring for new obstetric patients returned to baseline by PDSA 4 (as depicted in Fig. 2), further studies are needed to determine whether this trend was sustained.

CONCLUSION

After the fourth PDSA cycle, this quality improvement initiative achieved an aim score of 84%, quadruple the baseline of 21%, through the implementation of a standardized intake template and a pregnancy risk calculator for new obstetric patients. These low-cost tools promoted high-quality prenatal care and may benefit other maternal and child health clinics, particularly in resource-limited settings. Although the tools must be tailored to fit local contexts, their simplicity and adaptability offer significant potential to advance high-quality prenatal care and reduce maternal and neonatal mortality. Further studies are needed to determine how standardized templates can help increase adherence to national and international guidelines and how they can best be developed and implemented in East Africa. Further research is also needed to develop and validate a pregnancy risk calculator for sub-Saharan Africa.
