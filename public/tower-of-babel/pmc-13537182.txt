Machine Learning–Based Prediction of Hemoglobin Variability in Patients with Chronic Kidney Disease Receiving Erythropoiesis-Stimulating Agents: A Real-World Retrospective Study
Authors: Pattanan Buranasaksathien, Wanjak Pongsittisak, Padoemwut Teerawongsakul, Solos Jaturapisanukul, Thananda Trakarnvanich, Sathit Kurathong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnemia is a common complication of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and is predominantly managed with erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs). Hemoglobin response to ESA therapy varies substantially among patients and remains difficult to predict owing to multiple interacting clinical and treatment-related factors. Machine learning approaches may help model hemoglobin dynamics using routinely collected clinical data.PurposeTo develop and evaluate machine learning models for predicting weekly hemoglobin changes in patients with CKD receiving ESA therapy.Patients and MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using electronic medical records from a tertiary care center in Thailand (January 2012 to December 2022). Adult patients with CKD stages 3B–5 and an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) below 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 receiving ESA therapy were included. Four machine learning algorithms (Decision Tree, Random Forest, XGBoost, and Support Vector Machine) were trained using data from 80% of patients and evaluated on an independent test set comprising the remaining 20% of patients, with all longitudinal observations from each patient kept within the same partition. Performance was assessed using root mean square error (RMSE) and Pearson correlation coefficient.ResultsA total of 834 patients contributing 10,335 clinical visits and 9,935 time-series observations were included. The median age was 71 years and 58.6% were female. RMSE values were 0.121, 0.115, 0.117, and 0.115 for Decision Tree, Random Forest, XGBoost, and Support Vector Machine, respectively. Pearson correlation coefficients ranged from 0.457 to 0.553 (all p < 0.001).ConclusionMachine learning models trained on routinely collected clinical data showed feasibility for predicting short-term hemoglobin variability in patients with CKD receiving ESA therapy. Larger multicenter and external validation studies are needed to refine predictive accuracy and evaluate clinical utility.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) represents a substantial and growing public health burden worldwide. In Thailand, recent epidemiological estimates indicate that approximately 11.6 million individuals, corresponding to 17.5% of the population, are affected by CKD, with the prevalence expected to increase further in coming years.1 Anemia is among the most prevalent and clinically consequential complications of CKD, typically emerging during stage 3 and becoming progressively more severe as kidney function declines toward end-stage kidney disease (ESKD). The estimated prevalence of anemia is approximately 15% among patients with CKD overall, rising to 40–50% among those with ESKD.2

CKD-associated anemia is linked to a range of adverse clinical outcomes. Affected patients frequently experience debilitating symptoms—including fatigue, insomnia, and headache—that significantly impair health-related quality of life.3 Moreover, anemia has been identified as a significant independent risk factor for cardiovascular morbidity, increased hospitalization, and premature mortality in patients with CKD.4 Given these consequences, effective management of anemia represents a critical component of comprehensive CKD care.

Current clinical practice guidelines, including those issued by Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO), recommend maintaining hemoglobin levels between 10.0 and 11.5 g/dL in patients with CKD receiving erythropoiesis-stimulating agent (ESA) therapy.5 ESAs represent the cornerstone of pharmacologic treatment for anemia of CKD in patients without absolute iron deficiency. However, despite routine hemoglobin monitoring and dose adjustments, treatment responses remain highly variable and frequently unpredictable. Prior studies have demonstrated a nonlinear relationship between ESA dose and hemoglobin response, with interindividual variability approaching 50%. Furthermore, the estimated lifespan of red blood cells in patients with CKD is approximately 64 days, with interindividual variability of nearly 30%, which further complicates prediction of hemoglobin response to ESA therapy.6

Hemoglobin variability in CKD-associated anemia is influenced by a wide array of clinical and treatment-related factors, including ESA formulation and route of administration, concurrent medications that interfere with erythropoiesis, iron deficiency, inflammatory activity, nutritional status, comorbid conditions, and dialysis adequacy.7–10 The complex interactions among these variables render accurate prediction of hemoglobin response challenging in routine clinical practice. Several studies have further reported that increased hemoglobin variability is associated with higher risks of hospitalization and mortality among patients with CKD.11,12

Artificial intelligence (AI) methods have recently been applied to complex clinical prediction tasks. Machine learning algorithms are capable of analyzing large clinical datasets and identifying nonlinear relationships among variables that may not be detectable using conventional statistical methods. Several studies have investigated machine learning and neural network–based approaches for anemia management in patients with CKD.

Barbieri et al developed an artificial neural network–based anemia control model to guide ESA dosing in 752 dialysis patients over a 12-month period. Their model reduced the median darbepoetin dose from 0.63 to 0.46 μg/kg/month, increased the proportion of hemoglobin values within the target range from 70.6% to 76.6%, and reduced hemoglobin variability from 0.95 g/dL to 0.83 g/dL.13 Similarly, Gabutti et al applied artificial neural networks to predict erythropoietin beta dosing required to achieve target hemoglobin levels, demonstrating superior performance compared with nephrologist predictions, with a specificity of 92% versus 83% for physician estimates.14

A further study involving 466 dialysis patients employed recurrent neural network models to recommend erythropoietin dosing. The AI-based approach reduced erythropoietin usage and produced more stable hemoglobin levels, with monthly hemoglobin variation decreasing from 0.8 g/dL under physician-directed dosing to 0.6 g/dL under AI-guided dosing, while the proportion of hemoglobin measurements within the target range improved from 62.9% to 79.5%.15

Despite these promising findings, most published studies have been conducted in Western populations or specialized research settings. In Thailand, anemia among patients with CKD remains a substantial clinical challenge; although a large proportion of dialysis patients receive ESA therapy, only approximately half achieve the recommended hemoglobin target of 10 g/dL.2 Furthermore, the application of AI-based prediction models for ESA management has not yet been investigated in the Thai CKD population.

Therefore, this study aimed to develop and evaluate machine learning models for predicting weekly hemoglobin changes in patients with CKD receiving ESA therapy using real-world clinical data, while exploring the feasibility of applying these techniques to support future clinical decision-support systems for anemia management.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This retrospective observational study was conducted using electronic medical records obtained from the Faculty of Medicine Vajira Hospital, Navamindradhiraj University, Bangkok, Thailand. Clinical data recorded between January 2012 and December 2022 were retrieved from the institutional hospital database for model development and evaluation.

Study Population

Adult patients with a diagnosis of anemia of CKD who received ESA therapy during the study period were identified from the hospital database. Patients were identified from five clinical services within the institution: the nephrology outpatient clinic, the hemodialysis unit, the continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis (CAPD) clinic, the CKD progression clinic, and the part-time outpatient nephrology clinic. These services represent the principal clinical settings responsible for CKD management at the institution and collectively provide a comprehensive representation of routine nephrology practice.

Eligibility Criteria

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met all of the following criteria: age 18 years or older, a confirmed diagnosis of CKD stage 3B to stage 5, an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) below 45 mL/min/1.73 m2, and documented receipt of ESA therapy during the study period for the treatment of anemia of CKD. Patients were excluded if they had hemoglobin levels below 7 g/dL, transferrin saturation below 20%, or serum albumin levels below 2.5 g/dL. These thresholds were applied to reduce the influence of severe hypoalbuminemia and profound iron deficiency on hemoglobin variability. Acute infection, malignancy, other potential causes of anemia, and hyperparathyroidism were not formal exclusion criteria because these conditions could not be consistently ascertained at each longitudinal observation in the retrospective electronic medical records. In addition, patients with fewer than three documented follow-up visits were excluded to ensure a minimum longitudinal dataset for time-series construction.

AI Model Development

The development of the machine learning models comprised four sequential stages: data collection, feature selection, data preprocessing, and model development.

Data Collection

Eligible patients were identified from the hospital information system using clinic codes corresponding to the five specified clinical services. Patients with an eGFR below 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 were initially screened, and those who had not received ESA therapy during the study period were excluded.

Clinical information extracted from the electronic medical record database included demographic characteristics (age, sex), comorbid conditions identified using ICD-10 diagnostic codes (hypertension and diabetes mellitus), laboratory parameters (hemoglobin, serum calcium, serum phosphorus, serum albumin, serum creatinine, and eGFR), kidney replacement therapy modality, ESA therapy details (dose and route of administration), and concomitant medications (angiotensin-converting enzyme [ACE] inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers [ARBs], and statins). Hemoglobin values were obtained from the five clinical services described above; laboratory values from emergency department or inpatient encounters were not included. For patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis, hemoglobin was sampled before the dialysis session and before ultrafiltration in accordance with unit protocol, and postdialysis or non-dialysis-day measurements were therefore not included. Laboratory codes used for data extraction are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Medication codes used to identify relevant drug exposures are provided in Supplementary Table S2. The exclusion variables—hemoglobin below 7 g/dL, serum albumin below 2.5 g/dL, and transferrin saturation below 20%—were applied prior to model development.

Feature Selection

Candidate variables were initially identified through a systematic review of the existing literature, clinical expert opinion from nephrologists, and evaluation of data availability within the institutional database. The final set of input features included demographic characteristics (age and sex), comorbid conditions (hypertension and diabetes mellitus), laboratory parameters (hemoglobin, serum calcium, serum phosphorus, serum albumin, serum creatinine, and eGFR), kidney replacement therapy modality (hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, or no kidney replacement therapy), ESA dose and route of administration (intravenous or subcutaneous), and concomitant use of ACE inhibitors or ARBs and statins. Variables with more than 15% missing values across the dataset were excluded from model development to ensure data quality and model stability. Intact parathyroid hormone and dialysis adequacy indices, including Kt/V and urea reduction ratio (URR), were initially considered as candidate variables but were excluded from model development because each had more than 15% missing data.

Data Cleaning and Preparation

Initial exploratory analysis of the dataset was performed to evaluate variable distributions and identify potential outliers. Outlier management was applied where appropriate to improve data consistency and model reliability. Variables with more than 15% missing values were excluded from the analytical dataset.

Missing values were handled using a sequential three-step imputation strategy. First, missing values were replaced using the last observation carried forward (LOCF) method. If missing values persisted after LOCF imputation, values from the baseline (first) visit were substituted. Any remaining missing values were then imputed using the median value of the corresponding variable. Estimated glomerular filtration rate values were calculated using the CKD-EPI equation when necessary to ensure consistency of kidney function measurements. To minimize potential data leakage, missing data imputation and preprocessing procedures were performed after partitioning the dataset into training and test sets.

Longitudinal clinical data were transformed into time-series observations using consecutive visit sequences. Because visit intervals varied among patients, hemoglobin change was normalized by the time interval between consecutive measurements and expressed as g/dL per week. At each index visit, clinical variables from the preceding and index visits were used as input features to capture temporal changes in patient status. The prediction target was the subsequent weekly change in hemoglobin (ΔHb), defined as the hemoglobin level at the next consecutive follow-up visit minus the hemoglobin level at the index visit, divided by the time interval between these two visits in weeks. The resulting time-series dataset was used to model short-term hemoglobin changes over time (Figure 1). Model Development and Selection

Following construction of the time-series dataset, data were randomly partitioned at the patient level into a training dataset (80% of patients) and an independent test dataset (the remaining 20%), ensuring that all longitudinal observations from a single patient were assigned exclusively to one partition to prevent data leakage (Figure 2). Hyperparameter optimization was performed using cross-validation within the training dataset. Optimal hyperparameters were identified using GridSearchCV and RandomizedSearchCV approaches (Supplementary Table S3). Four machine learning algorithms were evaluated:

Decision Tree

A supervised learning algorithm that models decision processes by partitioning the feature space through a hierarchical tree structure of nodes and branches representing decision rules and predicted outcomes.16

Random Forest

An ensemble learning method that constructs multiple independent decision trees using different bootstrap samples of the training data and averages their predictions to improve accuracy and reduce overfitting.17,18

Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)

An optimized gradient boosting algorithm that sequentially builds decision trees to minimize prediction error through gradient-based optimization, with regularization to control model complexity.19

Support Vector Machine (SVM)

A supervised learning algorithm that identifies the optimal hyperplane in a multidimensional feature space to minimize prediction error, with kernel functions (linear, polynomial, radial basis function, and sigmoid) enabling the capture of nonlinear relationships between variables.20,21

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the baseline characteristics of the study population. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed variables, or as median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed variables, as determined by inspection of distributional characteristics.

Model performance on the independent test dataset was evaluated using root mean square error (RMSE) and Pearson correlation coefficient (r) to assess the agreement between predicted and observed weekly hemoglobin changes. All statistical analyses and model development were performed using Python (version 3.9) with the scikit-learn and XGBoost libraries.

Results

Study Cohort

A total of 1,238 patients with an eGFR below 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 were initially identified from the hospital database (Figure 3). Following application of eligibility criteria and data preprocessing, 834 patients who received ESA therapy were included in the final analysis, contributing 10,335 clinical visits. After time-series construction and exclusion of patients with fewer than three follow-up visits, 9,935 longitudinal observations were derived and used for model development. Baseline Characteristics

The median age of the study population was 71 years (IQR 63–78; range 18–100), and 58.6% of participants were female (Table 1). Hypertension was the most common comorbidity, documented in 70.3% of longitudinal observations, while diabetes mellitus was recorded in 13.6% of observations. Kidney replacement therapy was documented in 46.0% of observations: hemodialysis in 42.6% and peritoneal dialysis in 3.4%; the remaining 54.0% of observations were from patients without kidney replacement therapy. ESA was administered subcutaneously in 57.4% of observations and intravenously in 42.6%. ACE inhibitor or ARB use was recorded in 30.3% of observations and statin use in 33.2%. The median number of follow-up visits was 10 (IQR 6–15; range 3–61), and the median follow-up duration was 9 weeks (IQR 5–12; range 0.1–321.1 weeks). Discussion

In this study, we developed and evaluated four machine learning models to predict weekly changes in hemoglobin levels among patients with anemia of CKD receiving ESA therapy using real-world clinical data. Using longitudinal clinical information from 834 patients and 9,935 time-series observations derived from routine nephrology practice, we demonstrated that multiple machine learning algorithms can be applied to model short-term hemoglobin variability using routinely available clinical variables. Random Forest, XGBoost, and Support Vector Machine demonstrated comparable predictive performance, with RMSE values ranging from 0.115 to 0.117 g/dL and Pearson correlation coefficients ranging from 0.500 to 0.553.

The best-performing models achieved an RMSE of 0.115 g/dL for predicting weekly hemoglobin change. In the clinical context of CKD anemia management, hemoglobin levels typically fluctuate by approximately 0.3–0.5 g/dL between visits as a result of biological variability, treatment response, and measurement variability. The prediction error observed in the present study therefore represents a relatively small deviation in comparison with the natural variability of hemoglobin in routine clinical practice. In addition, the predicted weekly hemoglobin change generally ranged between −0.2 and 0.2 g/dL across all models, indicating that the machine learning algorithms captured clinically meaningful trends in hemoglobin dynamics rather than random fluctuations. Because formal comparisons with simple benchmark models and calibration analyses were not performed, the incremental predictive value of the machine learning models over simpler strategies could not be established from the available metrics. Future studies should include formal comparisons with simple baseline predictors, such as mean-prediction or no-change models, together with calibration assessment, to quantify the incremental value of machine learning approaches over simpler prediction strategies.

The correlations between predicted and observed weekly hemoglobin changes were moderate across the evaluated models. This finding suggests that the models captured meaningful directional patterns in hemoglobin variability despite the well-recognized biological variability of hemoglobin response to ESA therapy in patients with CKD. Hemoglobin levels in this population are influenced by numerous interacting factors, including iron status, inflammatory activity, nutritional status, comorbid conditions, dialysis adequacy, and inter-visit variations in ESA dosing. Among patients receiving hemodialysis, only predialysis hemoglobin measurements obtained on dialysis days were included, thereby minimizing variability related to intradialytic fluid removal and hemoconcentration. The moderate correlation coefficients observed in this study likely reflect both the ability of the models to capture clinically relevant temporal patterns and the inherent biological variability of hemoglobin dynamics in patients with CKD receiving ESA therapy.

The structure of the prediction task should also be considered when interpreting these findings. Hemoglobin response to ESA therapy evolves over time rather than occurring immediately after dose administration.6 The models in the present study used the ESA dose recorded at the index visit, whereas hemoglobin measured at the subsequent visit may reflect cumulative ESA exposure across the entire inter-visit interval, during which the dose could have been modified. Expressing the outcome as a change per week also assumes an approximately linear trajectory between two measurements, an assumption that may not hold across intervals spanning the pharmacodynamic response to ESA therapy. These characteristics arise from clinically determined follow-up rather than protocolized sampling and may constrain the predictive accuracy achievable from the available longitudinal data.

A further consideration is that ESA dose in routine clinical practice is not assigned independently of the preceding hemoglobin trajectory. Clinicians commonly increase the dose when hemoglobin is falling and reduce or withhold treatment when hemoglobin approaches or exceeds the target range. Consequently, the association between ESA dose and subsequent hemoglobin change in this dataset reflects both the pharmacological effect of ESA therapy and the prescribing decisions that generated the observed dose. This does not invalidate the prediction objective pursued in the present study; however, the models should not be interpreted as estimating the causal effect of a specific ESA dose on subsequent hemoglobin change. Moreover, because prescribing policies, treatment thresholds, monitoring intervals, and dose-adjustment practices may differ across institutions, the relationships learned from these data may not transfer directly to other clinical settings.

Taken together, the pharmacodynamic response to ESA therapy, irregular inter-visit intervals, potential treatment changes during follow-up, and confounding by indication constrain both the interpretation and transportability of the predictions. Therefore, the present models should be interpreted as proof-of-concept prediction models rather than tools ready for direct clinical deployment. Prospective validation, model calibration, comparison with simple baseline predictors, and assessment of whether model-guided predictions improve ESA dose adjustment or patient outcomes will be required before clinical implementation.

Previous AI-based studies in CKD anemia addressed related but distinct clinical tasks. Barbieri et al evaluated the artificial intelligence–based Anemia Control Model in maintenance hemodialysis and reported lower darbepoetin consumption, a higher proportion of hemoglobin values within the target range, and reduced intrapatient hemoglobin fluctuation.13 Gabutti et al used artificial neural networks to predict the epoetin beta dose required to achieve the hemoglobin target and subsequent monthly dose adjustments in dialysis patients.14 Yun et al developed a recurrent neural network–based ESA dose-recommendation model for patients with kidney failure receiving replacement therapy.15 Collectively, these studies were conducted in dialysis populations and focused primarily on ESA dose recommendation, dose adjustment, hemoglobin target attainment, or treatment stability. In contrast, the present study included a broader CKD population, with 54.0% of longitudinal observations arising from patients not receiving kidney replacement therapy, and treated weekly hemoglobin change as a continuous prediction target using irregularly collected real-world data. Differences in study population, sampling structure, outcome definition, and prediction target preclude direct comparison of performance metrics. The principal contribution of the present study is therefore the evaluation of whether short-term hemoglobin trajectories can be modeled across routine CKD care rather than exclusively within dialysis-specific anemia-management programs.

Model interpretability is an important consideration for clinical implementation of machine learning tools. In the present study, the models were constructed using well-established determinants of hemoglobin dynamics in CKD, including hemoglobin levels from consecutive visits, ESA dose, kidney function parameters, and major comorbidities. The predictive patterns identified are therefore likely to reflect clinically meaningful relationships rather than purely statistical associations. Future investigations may further enhance model transparency by applying explainable AI techniques—such as feature importance analysis or SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) values—to quantify the individual contribution of each clinical variable to the predicted hemoglobin change.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the analysis was conducted using data from a single tertiary institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings or patient populations. Second, the relatively short median follow-up duration of 9 weeks may have limited the ability of the models to capture longer-term hemoglobin trajectories. Third, although the median age of the study population was 71 years, model performance was not evaluated separately in elderly and younger patients. Therefore, whether predictive performance differs according to age remains uncertain and should be examined in future age-stratified validation studies. Fourth, several clinically relevant variables—including inflammatory markers, reticulocyte count, erythropoietin resistance index, intact parathyroid hormone, and dialysis adequacy indices such as Kt/V and urea reduction ratio (URR)—were excluded because the proportion of missing data exceeded the prespecified threshold. Consequently, the models could not account for dialysis adequacy, which may influence ESA responsiveness and hemoglobin variability and may have contributed to residual variability in model predictions. Acute infection, active malignancy, other potential causes of anemia, and hyperparathyroidism were not systematically ascertained at each longitudinal observation and therefore could not be fully accounted for, leaving the possibility of residual confounding. In addition, the exclusion of patients with serum albumin levels below 2.5 g/dL or transferrin saturation below 20% resulted in a selected cohort and may limit the applicability of the models to patients with severe hypoalbuminemia or profound iron deficiency. Because inflammatory markers were not consistently available, inflammatory status could not be comprehensively characterized, and residual confounding by inflammation remains possible. Fifth, external validation was not performed, and therefore the generalizability of the developed models to other institutions or populations remains uncertain. Sixth, treatment patterns and ESA dosing strategies in Thailand may differ from those in other healthcare systems, which may further limit the external validity of the findings.

Despite these limitations, our findings provide initial evidence that routinely collected clinical data may be used to model hemoglobin variability using machine learning approaches. External validation in independent cohorts, incorporation of additional longitudinal variables, and application of more advanced model architectures—such as long short-term memory (LSTM) networks or transformer-based models—may further improve predictive performance and clinical applicability. Integration of such models into clinical decision-support systems could enable more personalized ESA dosing strategies and improve the overall efficiency of anemia management in patients with CKD.

Conclusion

The findings of this study demonstrate that machine learning models can be trained to estimate short-term weekly changes in hemoglobin levels among patients with CKD-related anemia receiving ESA therapy. Among the evaluated algorithms, Random Forest, XGBoost, and Support Vector Machine demonstrated comparable predictive performance, with RMSE values ranging from 0.115 to 0.117 g/dL and moderate positive correlations between predicted and observed weekly hemoglobin changes (Pearson r = 0.500–0.553; all p < 0.001).

Although overall predictive performance was moderate, these findings support the feasibility of applying machine learning approaches to model hemoglobin variability using routinely collected clinical data and suggest that such models may contribute to future clinical decision-support tools for anemia management in patients with CKD. These findings should be interpreted within the selected study population defined by the eligibility criteria, particularly the exclusion of patients with severe hypoalbuminemia or profound iron deficiency. Validation using larger multicenter datasets and independent patient cohorts will be necessary to further refine predictive accuracy and evaluate their potential clinical utility.
