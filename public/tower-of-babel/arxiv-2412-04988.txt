A unified framework for classical and quantum uncertainty relations using stochastic representations
Euijoon Kwon, Jae Sung Lee

Abstract
Thermodynamic uncertainty relations (TURs) and kinetic uncertainty relations (KURs) provide tradeoff relations between measurement precision and thermodynamic cost such as entropy production and activity. Conventionally, these relations are derived using the Cramér-Rao inequality, which involves an auxiliary perturbation in deterministic differential equations governing the time evolution of the system’s probability distribution. In this study, without relying on the previous formulation based on deterministic evolving equation, we demonstrate that all previously discovered uncertainty relations can be derived solely through the stochastic representation of the same dynamics. For this purpose, we propose a unified method based on stochastic representations for general Markovian dynamics. Extending beyond classical systems, we apply this method to Markovian open quantum systems by unraveling their dynamics, deriving quantum uncertainty relations that are physically more accessible and tighter in regimes where quantum effects play a significant role. This fully establishes uncertainty relations for both classical and quantum systems as intrinsic properties of their stochastic nature.



I
Introduction

Fluctuations arise from the inherent randomness in small systems interacting with their environment, limiting measurement accuracy.
Uncertainty relations in thermodynamics provide these fundamental limits on measurement precision in terms of thermodynamic costs. In this context, thermodynamic uncertainty relations (TURs)

Both relations can be expressed in a compact form as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{D}{J^{2}}\geq\frac{(1+\delta)^{2}}{\mathcal{R}_{\tau}}\;,

(1)

where
J
and
D
represent the mean and variance of an observable, respectively,
\delta
is a correction term that vanishes in the steady state, and
\mathcal{R}_{\tau}
denotes thermodynamic costs, corresponding to half of the total entropy production (EP) for TURs and to the total dynamical activity for KURs.
These inequalities not only provide fundamental limits on measurement precision
[

]
but have also been explored in various contexts, such as inferring EP

,

]
and deriving other thermodynamic relations

,

]
, including power-efficiency tradeoff
[

]
, speed limit
[

]
, and entropic bound
[

,

.

Following the initial discovery of TURs, various techniques have been employed to prove uncertainty relations in different stochastic systems, including large deviation theory
[

,

,

, the generating function method
[

,

, and the fluctuation-response inequality

.
One of the most widely used and comprehensive methods is using the Cramér–Rao inequality
[

,

,

,

,

,

, which states:

\displaystyle\frac{D_{\theta}}{[\partial_{\theta}J_{\theta}]^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}_{\theta}}\;,

(2)

where
\theta
represents a specific perturbation applied to the system.
Then,
J_{\theta}
and
D_{\theta}
denote the mean and variance of an observable under this perturbed dynamics, and
\mathcal{I}_{\theta}
represents the corresponding Fisher information. By comparing Eq. (

\partial_{\theta}J_{\theta}=J(1+\delta)
and
\mathcal{I}_{\theta}=\mathcal{R}_{\tau}
through an appropriate perturbation. A notable feature of the method using Cramér–Rao inequality is that it relies on deterministic equations governing the time evolution of the system’s probability distribution.

Recently, an alternative approach was proposed for deriving TURs in the relaxation dynamics of overdamped Langevin systems
[
9
]
.
Unlike conventional methods, this approach is based on a stochastic representation of the dynamics rather than deterministic equations.
Since it does not require an auxiliary perturbation, this method provides a more direct understanding of the origin of TUR as a consequence of inherent random fluctuations.

In this study, by generalizing the approach of Ref.

We first demonstrate that the previously discovered TURs and KURs for classical stochastic systems can be derived within this framework, thereby fully establishing this approach in the classical regime.
Furthermore, we apply this stochastic representation approach to Markovian open quantum systems via unraveling, successfully deriving uncertainty relations that are physically more accessible than previous quantum relations

II
Two representations and setup

Markovian dynamics are typically described by two representations. One is the stochastic representation, which generates a single stochastic trajectory of the system through random noise.
In this representation, the system state at time
t
is denoted by
z_{t}
, which can be a scalar number, a vector, or a matrix. Its time evolution is governed by the following stochastic differential equation:

\displaystyle\dot{z}_{t}=f(z_{t},t)+\sum_{k}g_{k}(z_{t},t)\xi_{t}^{k}\;,

(3)

where
f
and
g_{k}
denote the drift force and the strength of
k
-th noise source, respectively, and both are the same dimensional quantities as
z_{t}
.
\xi^{k}_{t}
represents the scalar white noise from the
k
th noise source, with zero mean and the covariance given by

\displaystyle\langle\xi^{k}_{t}\xi^{k^{\prime}}_{t^{\prime}}\rangle=h_{k}(z_{t},t)\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\;.

(4)

It is worth noting that various Markovian dynamics such as Langevin systems, Markov jump processes, and open-quantum Markov systems, can be described by Eq. (
3
). The detailed explanation on the stochastic representations of Markov jump processes and open-quantum systems is presented in Eqs. (
14
) and (
41
), respectively.

The other representation of Markovian dynamics is the deterministic representation, which describes the time evolution of the system’s probability distribution. This representation is expressed through deterministic equations, such as partial differential equations or master equations, without involving random noise.

Under this Markovian dynamics, we are interested in measuring a physical observable
\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}(\Gamma)
, where
\Gamma
denotes a system trajectory over the time interval from
0
to
\tau
and
\Lambda
represents a weight function for each transition, as shown in Eqs. (
15
) and (
44
). This observable can be generally decomposed into two parts, a diffusion part
\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,1}(\Gamma)
and a drift part
\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,2}(\Gamma)
, as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}(\Gamma)=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{k}M^{\Lambda,1}_{k}(z_{t},t)\xi^{k}_{t}+\int_{0}^{\tau}dtM^{\Lambda,2}(z_{t},t)\;,

(5)

where the first and the second integrals in Eq. (
5
) correspond to
\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,1}(\Gamma)
and
\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,2}(\Gamma)
, respectively.
Next, we introduce an auxiliary observable
Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}(\Gamma)
, defined as

\displaystyle Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}(\Gamma)=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{k}\zeta_{k}(z_{t},t)\xi^{k}_{t}\;.

(6)

Note that
\langle Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}\rangle=0
by construction. The variance of
Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}(\Gamma)
, denoted as
\mathcal{R}_{\tau}^{\zeta}\equiv\langle(Z_{\tau}^{\zeta})^{2}\rangle
, depends on the choice of
\zeta
. In a later section, we will select a specific
\zeta
such that
\mathcal{R}_{\tau}^{\zeta}
becomes either half of the entropy production,
\Sigma_{\tau}^{\text{tot}}/2
, or the total dynamical activity,
\mathcal{A}_{\tau}
.

III
Derivation of classical uncertainty relations using stochastic representations

III.1
General framework

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we can immediately obtain the following inequality:

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}\rangle^{2}=\langle(\mathcal{J}^{\Lambda}_{\tau}-\langle\mathcal{J}^{\Lambda}_{\tau}\rangle)Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}\rangle^{2}\leq\text{Var}(\mathcal{J}^{\Lambda}_{\tau})\mathcal{R}^{\zeta}_{\tau}\;.

(7)

Remaining task to derive uncertainty relations is calculating
\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}\rangle^{2}
. This can be achieved using either of the representations. In the deterministic representation, we need to introduce a perturbation to the dynamic equation by an amount controlled by the parameter
\theta
, where
\theta=0
indicates no perturbation, and a finite
\theta
signifies that the system is perturbed. For a specific perturbation with
\theta
, we take the auxiliary observable as
Z^{\zeta}_{\tau}(\Gamma)=\partial_{\theta}\log\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(\Gamma)\big|_{\theta=0}
, where
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(\Gamma)
is the probability of observing trajectory
\Gamma
under the perturbed dynamics. This leads to
\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}Z^{\zeta}_{\tau}\rangle=\partial_{\theta}\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle_{\theta}\big|_{\theta=0}
where

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle_{\theta}=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\int dzM^{\Lambda,2}(z,t)p_{\theta}(z,t)\;,

(8)

and
p_{\theta}(z,t)
is the probability distribution of
z_{t}
under the perturbed dynamics. By identifying
\partial_{\theta}\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle_{\theta}\big|_{\theta=0}=J(1+\delta)
with
J=\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle
, the uncertainty relation Eq. (
1
) is derived from Eq. (
7
). As this approach relies on the probability distribution governed by deterministic equations, we refer to it as the deterministic representation approach. This method is equivalent to using the Cramér–Rao inequality, where
\mathcal{R}_{\tau}^{\zeta}
corresponds to the Fisher information evaluated at
\theta=0
.

The second method to evaluate
\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}\rangle^{2}
utilizes the stochastic representation. Based on the decomposition of the observable, we obtain
\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,1}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}\rangle+\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,2}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}\rangle
. By requiring

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,1}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle\;

(9)

and defining the correction term as

\delta\equiv\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,2}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}\rangle/\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle\;,

(10)

Eq. (
1
) can be derived from Eq. (
7
). Evaluating
\delta
requires calculating the correlation of observables at different times, which necessitates the information of the underlying stochastic representation. Hence, we refer to this method as the stochastic representation approach.

Previously, various uncertainty relations were primarily derived using the deterministic representation approach, based on the Cramér–Rao inequality
[
6
,
7
,
25
,
26
,
8
,
10
]
. This method requires identifying a ‘suitable’ auxiliary perturbation to yield the desired expressions. More recently, TURs for Langevin systems in transient dynamics were proven using a stochastic representation approach
[
9
]
.

In this study, by going beyond the transient TUR in Langevin dynamics, we demonstrate that all types of TURs and KURs, including those involving time-dependent protocols, can be derived using the stochastic representation approach for both Langevin systems and Markov jump processes. This completes the establishment of the ‘stochastic representation approach’ concept in classical Markov systems. Detailed derivations are provided in subsequent sections.

III.2
Stochastic representation approach in Markov jump systems

We consider a continuous-time Markov jump dynamics described by the following master equation:

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{n}(t;\omega)=\sum_{m(\neq n)}j_{nm}(t;\omega)\;,

(11)

where
p_{n}(t;\omega)
is the probability distribution of state
n
and
j_{nm}(t;\omega)=R_{nm}(\omega t)p_{m}(t;\omega)-R_{mn}(\omega t)p_{n}(t;\omega)
is the probability current from state
m
to
n
. Here,
R_{nm}(\omega t)
denotes the transition rate from state
m
to
n
and
\omega
represents the rate of a time-dependent protocol. The microscopic state of the system at time
t
is denoted by
z_{t}\in\{1,2,\dots,S\}
, where
S
is the total number of states. During the next infinitesimal time interval
dt
, the system can either jump to another state
n(\neq z_{t})
with probability
R_{n,z_{t}}dt
, or remain in state
z_{t}
with probability
1+R_{z_{t},z_{t}}dt
, where
R_{n,n}\equiv-\sum_{m(\neq n)}R_{m,n}
.

Let
N_{nm}(t)
be the total number of jumps from the state
m
to
n
in the time interval
[0,t]
and define
\eta_{n}(t)\equiv\delta_{z(t),n}
. We now introduce a centered Poisson process
\tilde{N}_{nm}(t)
, whose time derivative is given by

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{N}}_{nm}(t)=\dot{N}_{nm}(t)-R_{nm}(\omega t)\eta_{m}(t)\;.

(12)

Note that
\tilde{N}_{nm}(t)
is ‘centered’ since
\langle\dot{\tilde{N}}_{nm}\rangle=0
. Furthermore, its autocorrelation function is given by

\displaystyle\langle\dot{\tilde{N}}_{nm}(t)\dot{\tilde{N}}_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle=R_{nm}(\omega t)p_{m}(t)\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\;.

(13)

From now on, we will omit
\omega
in the probability distribution for simplicity when there is no ambiguity. Thus,
\dot{\tilde{N}}_{nm}(t)
acts as the white noise
\xi_{t}^{nm}
in the stochastic representation (
3
), with
h_{nm}=R_{nm}p_{m}
. The derivation of Eq. (
13
) is presented in Appendix
A.1
.
Using this white noise
\dot{\tilde{N}}_{nm}(t)
, the Markov jump dynamics can be stochastically represented in the form of Eq. (
3
) as

\displaystyle\dot{z}_{t}=\sum_{n\neq m}(n-m)R_{nm}(\omega t)\eta_{m}(t)+\sum_{n\neq m}(n-m)\dot{\tilde{N}}_{nm}(t)\;.

(14)

The expression of an observable in this system is

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}(\Gamma)=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{n\neq m}\Lambda_{nm}(\omega t)\dot{N}_{nm}(t)\;.

(15)

It can be decomposed into the diffusion part

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,1}(\Gamma)=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{n\neq m}\Lambda_{nm}(\omega t)\dot{\tilde{N}}_{nm}(t)

(16)

and the drift part

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,2}(\Gamma)=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{n\neq m}\Lambda_{nm}(\omega t)R_{nm}(\omega t)\eta_{m}(t)\;.

(17)

The auxiliary observable
Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}(\Gamma)
is expressed as

\displaystyle Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}(\Gamma)=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{n\neq m}\zeta_{nm}(t)\dot{\tilde{N}}_{nm}(t)\;,

(18)

where
\langle Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}\rangle=0
and
\langle(Z_{\tau}^{\zeta})^{2}\rangle=\mathcal{R}_{\tau}^{\zeta}
. When
\zeta_{nm}=\zeta_{nm}^{\text{A}}\equiv 1
,
\mathcal{R}_{\tau}^{\zeta^{\text{A}}}
corresponds to total dynamical activity:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\tau}^{\zeta^{\text{A}}}=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{n\neq m}R_{nm}(\omega t)p_{m}(t)=\mathcal{A}_{\tau}\;.

(19)

When
\zeta_{nm}=\zeta_{nm}^{\text{E}}\equiv j_{nm}/a_{nm}
, where
a_{nm}=R_{nm}p_{m}+R_{mn}p_{n}
,
\mathcal{R}_{\tau}^{\zeta^{\text{E}}}
becomes half of the pseudo EP:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\tau}^{\zeta^{\text{E}}}=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{n>m}\frac{j_{nm}^{2}}{a_{nm}}=\frac{\Sigma_{\tau}^{\text{ps}}}{2}\;,

(20)

which measures the irreversibility of the dynamics and has been found very useful in deriving uncertainty relations in Markov jump systems
[
8
,
26
,
10
]
. The log-mean inequality,
2/(x+y)\leq(\ln x-\ln y)/(x-y)
for
x=R_{nm}p_{m}
and
y=R_{mn}p_{n}
, guarantees that
\Sigma_{\tau}^{\text{ps}}\leq\Sigma_{\tau}^{\rm tot}
, where

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\tau}^{\text{tot}}=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{n>m}j_{nm}\ln\frac{R_{nm}p_{m}}{R_{mn}p_{n}}\;.

(21)

Furthermore, we can show that for
\zeta=\zeta^{\rm A}

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,1}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta^{\text{A}}}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{n\neq m}\Lambda_{nm}R_{nm}p_{m}=\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle\;,

(22)

and for
\zeta=\zeta^{\rm E}

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,1}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta^{\text{E}}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{n\neq m}\Lambda_{nm}\frac{j_{nm}}{a_{nm}}R_{nm}p_{m}=\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle\;.

(23)

The second equality in Eq. (
23
) holds under the condition
\Lambda_{nm}=-\Lambda_{mn}
. Therefore, Eq. (
9
) is satisfied.

To derive uncertainty relations, the remaining task is to evaluate
\delta_{\zeta}=\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,2}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}\rangle/\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle
, which is calculated in the Appendix
A.2
. For both choice of
\zeta
, the correction term becomes

\displaystyle\delta_{\zeta}=\frac{\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,2}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}\rangle}{\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle}=\frac{(-1+\tau\partial_{\tau}-\omega\partial_{\omega})\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle}{\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle}\;,

(24)

Then, Eq. (
7
) becomes KUR when
\zeta=\zeta^{\rm A}

\displaystyle\frac{\text{Var}(\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda})}{\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle^{2}}\geq\frac{(1+\delta_{\zeta})^{2}}{\mathcal{A}_{\tau}}\;,

(25)

and TUR when
\zeta=\zeta^{\rm E}

\displaystyle\frac{\text{Var}(\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda})}{\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle^{2}}\geq\frac{2(1+\delta_{\zeta})^{2}}{\Sigma_{\tau}^{\text{ps}}}\geq\frac{2(1+\delta_{\zeta})^{2}}{\Sigma_{\tau}^{\text{tot}}}\;.

(26)

Note that these relations are identical to those derived in Refs.
[
26
,
8
,
10
]
, where they were proven using the Cramér-Rao inequality.

III.3
Stochastic representation approach in overdamped Langevin systems

We consider an
N
-dimensional overdamped Langevin dynamics, with the state described by the vector
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{N})^{\textsf{T}}
. The governing equation is given by

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x},\omega t)+\mathbb{B}(\mathbf{x},\omega t)\bullet\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\;,

(27)

where
\mathbf{A}=(A_{1},A_{2},\cdots,A_{N})^{\textsf{T}}
is a drift force,
\mathbb{B}
defines the diffusion matrix
\mathbb{D}
through the relation
\mathbb{D}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{B}\mathbb{B}^{\textsf{T}}
, and
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is a Gaussian white noise satisfying
\langle\xi_{i}\rangle=0
and
\langle\xi_{i}(t)\xi_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{ij}(t-t^{\prime})
. Throughout the calculation, we use the notation
\bullet
and
\circ
to denote the Itô product and the Stratonovich product, respectively. Equation (
27
) itself corresponds to the stochastic representation.
The Fokker-Planck equation of Eq. (
27
) is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p(\mathbf{x},t;\omega)=-\nabla^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x},t;\omega)\;,

(28)

where the probability current is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x},t;\omega)=\left\{\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x},\omega t)-\left[\nabla^{\textsf{T}}\mathbb{D}(\mathbf{x},\omega t)\right]^{\textsf{T}}\right\}p(\mathbf{x},t;\omega)\;.

(29)

In this system, the observable is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}(\Gamma)\equiv\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\textsf{T}}\!(\mathbf{x}(t),\omega t)\circ\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\;.

(30)

The observable can be decomposed into the diffusion part

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,1}=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbb{B}(\mathbf{x},\omega t)\bullet\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\;,

(31)

and the drift part

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,2}=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\left[\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{A}+\text{tr}(\mathbb{D}\nabla\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\textsf{T}})\right]\;.

(32)

The auxiliary observable
Z_{\tau}^{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(\Gamma)
is defined as

\displaystyle Z_{\tau}^{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\textsf{T}}(\mathbf{x},t)\bullet\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\;.

(33)

To derive TUR in this setup, we set
\boldsymbol{\zeta}=\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\text{E}}\equiv\mathbb{B}^{-1}\mathbf{J}/p
. Then, we can easily check that
\langle Z_{\tau}^{\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\text{E}}}\rangle=0
and

\displaystyle\left\langle\left(Z_{\tau}^{\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\text{E}}}\right)^{2}\right\rangle=\frac{\Sigma_{\tau}^{\text{tot}}}{2}\;,

(34)

where
\Sigma_{\tau}^{\text{tot}}
is the total EP given by

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\tau}^{\text{tot}}=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\int d\mathbf{x}\frac{\mathbf{J}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbb{D}^{-1}(\mathbf{x},\omega t)\mathbf{J}}{p(\mathbf{x},t;\omega)}\;.

(35)

One can easily show that

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,1}Z_{\tau}^{\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\text{E}}}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\int d\mathbf{x}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{J}=\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle\;.

(36)

As detailed in the Appendix
A.3
, the correction term becomes

\displaystyle\delta=\frac{(-1+\tau\partial_{\tau}-\omega\partial_{\omega})\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle}{\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle}\;,

(37)

which is the same expression as the Markov-jump case. Therefore, TUR for the Langvein system becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\text{Var}(\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda})}{\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle^{2}}\geq\frac{(1+\delta)^{2}}{\Sigma_{\tau}^{\text{tot}}}\;,

(38)

which corresponds to the relations derived using Cramér–Rao inequality in
[
6
,
7
,
25
,
10
]
. This is the generalization for arbitrary time-dependent protocol of the work done in
[
9
]
, where they proved TUR only for transient dynamics without time-independent protocol. We note that the TUR for underdamped Langevin systems can also be derived following a procedure similar to the one presented in this section.

IV
Application to Markovian open quantum system

The stochastic representation approach opens new avenues for deriving uncertainty relations in other systems, such as open quantum systems, which we will now explore. Given the success of proving uncertainty relations using the deterministic representation in classical systems, extending this idea to Markovian open quantum systems was a natural progression
[
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
]
. However, many such derivations lack a clear physical interpretation of the terms involved in the resulting uncertainty relations. The main challenge lies in finding a suitable perturbation that equates the Fisher information with thermodynamic costs, such as EP and dynamical activity. The naive perturbation often introduces non-measurable terms related to ‘quantum coherence’. However, as we will demonstrate, the stochastic representation helps reduce such terms, making the relations more physically accessible.

IV.1
Setup and derivation

We consider an open quantum system coupled to baths. In the weak-coupling limit, the time evolution of system’s density matrix
\rho(t)
at time
t
is governed by the Lindblad quantum master equation (QME):

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}(t)=-i[H,\rho(t)]+\sum_{k}\mathcal{D}[L_{k}(t)]\rho(t)\equiv\mathcal{L}(t)\rho(t)\;,

(39)

where
H
is the Hamiltonian of the system,
L_{k}
s are jump operators for channel
k=1,\dots,K
, and
\mathcal{D}[L]\bullet\equiv L\bullet L^{\dagger}-\{L^{\dagger}L,\bullet\}/2
. Assuming the bath is in equilibrium, the jump operators satisfy the local detailed balance condition,
L_{k}=e^{s_{k}/2}L_{k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}
, where
s_{k}
represents EP associated with a jump of type
k
, and
k^{\prime}
denotes the inverse jump of
k
.

Unraveling the QME involves treating
\rho(t)
as the ensemble average of
\rho_{c}(t)
, the ‘stochastic’ density matrix of the system conditioned on the measurement output
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
. By defining Kraus operators
M_{0}=1-iH_{\text{eff}}dt
, where
H_{\text{eff}}\equiv H-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{k}L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}
, and
M_{k}=\sqrt{dt}L_{k}
, unraveling the QME leads to the following evolution equation for
\rho_{c}(t)
:

\displaystyle\rho_{c}(t+dt)=

\displaystyle\left(1-\sum_{k}dN_{k}(t)\right)\frac{M_{0}\rho_{c}(t)M_{0}^{\dagger}}{\text{tr}[M_{0}\rho_{c}(t)M_{0}^{\dagger}]}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k}dN_{k}(t)\frac{M_{k}\rho_{c}(t)M_{k}^{\dagger}}{\text{tr}[M_{k}\rho_{c}(t)M_{k}^{\dagger}]}\;,

(40)

where
dN_{k}(t)
is a classical random variable that takes the value
1
when a jump occurs through channel
k
, and
0
otherwise. It satisfies
\mathbb{P}[dN_{k}(t)=1|\rho_{c}(t)]=\text{tr}(L_{k}\rho_{c}L_{k}^{\dagger})dt
, which is the probability of
dN_{k}(t)=1
conditioned on
\rho_{c}(t)
. In the limit
dt\rightarrow 0
, Eq. (
40
) reduces to the Belavkin equation
[
37
,
32
]
:

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{c}(t)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(t)\rho_{c}(t)+\sum_{k}\dot{\tilde{N}}_{k}\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}_{k}\rho_{c}}{\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{k}\rho_{c})}-\rho_{c}\right)\;,

(41)

where
\mathcal{L}_{k}\bullet\equiv L_{k}\bullet L_{k}^{\dagger}
and
\dot{\tilde{N}}_{k}dt=d\tilde{N}_{k}(t)
is a centered Poisson process defined as

\displaystyle d\tilde{N}_{k}(t)\equiv dN_{k}(t)-\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{k}(t)\rho_{c}(t))dt\;.

(42)

The key property of
\dot{\tilde{N}}_{k}
is that it has zero mean and an autocorrelation function given by

\displaystyle\left\langle\dot{\tilde{N}}_{k_{1}}(t_{1})\dot{\tilde{N}}_{k_{2}}(t_{2})\right\rangle=\delta(t_{1}-t_{2})\delta_{k_{1}k_{2}}\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{k_{1}}(t_{1})\rho(t_{1}))\;.

(43)

The derivation of Eq. (
43
) is presented in Appendix
B.1
. Thus,
\dot{\tilde{N}}_{k}(t)
corresponds to
\xi^{k}_{t}
in Eq. (
3
), with
h_{k}=\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{k}\rho_{c})
in Eq. (
4
). Therefore, Eq. (
41
) serves as the stochastic representation of the Lindblad QME, generating ‘stochastic trajectories’ for open quantum systems coupled to baths.

We are interested in the observable that measures the occurrence of transitions through channel
k
, weighted by
\Lambda_{k}
. In the stochastic representation, this observable from time 0 to
\tau
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}(\Gamma)=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{k}\Lambda_{k}\dot{N}_{k}(t)\;.

(44)

Then, following Eq. (
5
),
\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}(\Gamma)
can be divided into the two parts
\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,1}(\Gamma)=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{k}\Lambda_{k}\dot{\tilde{N}}_{k}
and
\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,2}(\Gamma)=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{k}\Lambda_{k}\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{k}\rho_{c})
.

Next, the auxiliary observable
Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}(\Gamma)
is written as

\displaystyle Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}(\Gamma)=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{k}\zeta_{k}(t)\dot{\tilde{N}}_{k}\;.

(45)

Note that
\langle Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}\rangle=0
. If we choose
\zeta_{k}
as
\zeta_{k}=\zeta_{k}^{\text{A}}\equiv 1
, the variance of
\mathcal{R}^{\zeta^{\text{A}}}_{\tau}=\langle(Z_{\tau}^{\zeta})^{2}\rangle
becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\zeta^{\text{A}}}_{\tau}=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{k}\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{k}\rho)=\mathcal{A}_{\tau}\;,

(46)

which corresponds to the quantum total dynamical activity. On the other hand, if we choose
\zeta_{k}
as

\displaystyle\zeta_{k}=\zeta_{k}^{\text{E}}\equiv\frac{\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{k}\rho)-\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}\rho)}{\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{k}\rho)+\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}\rho)}\;,

(47)

\mathcal{R}^{\zeta^{\text{E}}}_{\tau}
equals half of quantum pseudo EP, defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\zeta^{\text{E}}}_{\tau}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{k}\frac{\left[\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{k}\rho(t))-\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}\rho(t))\right]^{2}}{\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{k}\rho(t))+\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}\rho(t))}\equiv\frac{\Sigma_{\tau}^{\text{ps}}}{2}.

(48)

The pseudo EP is upper bounded by the quantum total EP, i.e.,
\Sigma_{\tau}^{\text{ps}}\leq\Sigma_{\tau}^{\text{tot}}

[
30
]
, where the EP rate is given by

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\text{tot}}_{\tau}

\displaystyle=-\text{tr}[\dot{\rho}(t)\ln\rho(t)]+\sum_{k}\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{k}\rho(t))s_{k}\;,

(49)

where the first term describes the change in the system’s von Neumann entropy, and the second term denotes the heat flow from the baths.

Based on this stochastic representation, we can derive quantum versions of the uncertainty relations.
For the quantum KUR, we choose
\zeta_{k}=\zeta_{k}^{\text{A}}
. Then, it follows directly from Eq. (
43
) that
\langle\mathcal{J}^{\Lambda,1}_{\tau}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta^{\text{A}}}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{J}^{\Lambda}_{\tau}\rangle
. By defining
\delta_{\text{KUR}}\equiv\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,2}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta^{\text{A}}}\rangle/\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle
, we obtain the quantum KUR:

\displaystyle\frac{\text{Var}(\mathcal{J}_{\tau})}{\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}\rangle^{2}}\geq\frac{(1+\delta_{\text{KUR}})^{2}}{\mathcal{A}_{\tau}}\;.

(50)

For the quantum TUR, we set
\zeta_{k}=\zeta_{k}^{\text{E}}
and impose an anti-symmetric condition on
\Lambda_{k}
between inverse channels
k
and
k^{\prime}
, such that
\Lambda_{k}=-\Lambda_{k^{\prime}}
. This setup allows us to show that
\langle\mathcal{J}^{\Lambda,1}_{\tau}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta^{\text{E}}}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{J}^{\Lambda}_{\tau}\rangle
. Similarly to the quantum KUR, by defining
\delta_{\text{TUR}}\equiv\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,2}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta^{\text{E}}}\rangle/\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\text{Var}(\mathcal{J}_{\tau})}{\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}\rangle^{2}}\geq\frac{2(1+\delta_{\text{TUR}})^{2}}{\Sigma_{\tau}^{\text{ps}}}\geq\frac{2(1+\delta_{\text{TUR}})^{2}}{\Sigma_{\tau}^{\text{tot}}}\;.

(51)

\delta_{\text{KUR}}
and
\delta_{\text{TUR}}
can be non-zero even in the steady state, as illustrated later. We refer to these as quantum corrections. In general, analytical forms of these quantum corrections are unattainable, so we need to rely on numerical calculations using stochastic Schrödinger equation. However, compact expressions for these corrections can be obtained in specific cases. The first case is the quantum reset process in the steady state, where the destination of a quantum jump is independent of the previous state. A detailed derivation of the quantum correction in this quantum reset process is provided in Appendix.
B
. The second case is the classical limit of the system, where the Hamiltonian matrix is diagonal and transitions occur only between energy eigenstates. In this case, the quantum corrections converge to those of classical systems, ultimately vanishing in the steady state.

IV.2
Comparing with earlier works

In this section, we compare the uncertainty relations in Eqs. (
50
) and (
51
) with those found in earlier works. In deriving uncertainty relations, Ref.
[
28
]
employed the quantum Cramér–Rao inequality in the steady state. Another study
[
30
]
applied the classical Cramér–Rao inequality to derive uncertainty relations for an arbitrary initial state. Both derivations based on the Cramér–Rao inequality involved specific perturbations to the system Hamiltonian and jump operators. The quantum uncertainty relations proposed in Ref.
[
30
]
can be summarized as

\displaystyle\frac{D}{J^{2}}\geq\frac{(1+\tilde{\delta})^{2}}{\mathcal{R}_{\tau}+\mathcal{Q}}\;,

(52)

where
\tilde{\delta}
represents a quantum correction distinct from
\delta
in our expression, and
\mathcal{Q}
denotes a quantum contribution arising from coherence. Here,
\mathcal{Q}
is given by the difference between
\mathcal{R}_{\tau}^{\zeta}
and the Fisher information. Since calculating
\mathcal{Q}
requires information from all quantum trajectories, which is numerically very costly, we will use its upper bound
\mathcal{Q}^{\text{u}}
in the next section for comparison with our bound. With this replacement, we refer to Eq. (
52
) as the Vu-Saito (VS) bound. Details of the VS bound are provided in Appendix.
C
.
Another quantum TUR for general open quantum system was derived in Ref.
[
29
]
. However, the terms representing thermodynamic cost in this TUR lacks a clear physical interpretation.
Finally,
\psi
-KUR was recently introduced as follows
[
31
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{D}{J^{2}}\geq\frac{(1+\psi)^{2}}{\mathcal{A}_{\tau}}\;,

(53)

where
\psi
denotes a quantity proportional to
[H,\rho_{\text{ss}}]
. Although this relation is valid only in the steady state, it does not include the quantum coherence contribution
\mathcal{Q}
. This absence of
\mathcal{Q}
can be achieved by perturbing only the jump operator but not the system Hamiltonian. In the next section, we will also compare our bound with the
\psi
-KUR.

V
Summary and outlook

We have introduced a general framework for deriving uncertainty relations for Markovian dynamics in both classical and quantum systems based on stochastic representations. By employing the stochastic decomposition of an observable, we have shown that TURs and KURs can be derived in a unified manner across various systems.
This approach offers an alternative way for understanding and deriving the uncertainty relations that inherently emerge from stochasticity in both the classical and quantum domains.
Through this approach, we successfully derive quantum uncertainty relations by representing open quantum systems as stochastic systems via unraveling. Notably, the derived quantum relations are more physically accessible, as they lack the additional quantum coherence contribution present in previous relations. Moreover, numerical examples demonstrate that our quantum relations provide more accurate precision estimates compared to previous ones, particularly in the regime where quantum effects are significant.

Promising avenues for future research include exploring the applicability of our quantum uncertainty relations in noise-induced coherence systems
[
41
,
42
]
and deriving both classical and quantum KURs for first passage time
[
43
,
44
,
45
,
30
,
46
]
using a stochastic representation, and estimating the measurement precision of quantum circuits, both experimentally and theoretically.

A.1
Derivation of Eq. (
13
)

Without loss of generality, we set
t\geq t^{\prime}
. Using Eq. (
12
), the autocorrelation function expands as

\displaystyle\langle d\tilde{N}_{nm}(t)d\tilde{N}_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle dN_{nm}(t)dN_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle-R_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\omega t^{\prime})\langle dN_{nm}(t)\eta_{m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle dt^{\prime}

\displaystyle~~~-R_{nm}(\omega t)\langle\eta_{m}(t)dN_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle dt

\displaystyle~~~+R_{nm}(\omega t)R_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\omega t^{\prime})\langle\eta_{m}(t)\eta_{m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle dtdt^{\prime}\;.

(54)

For
t>t^{\prime}
, the first term in Eq. (
54
) can be written as

\displaystyle\langle dN_{nm}(t)dN_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(dN_{nm}(t)=1|dN_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})=1)\mathbb{P}(dN_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})=1)\;.

(55)

Here, the two probabilities in the right-hand side are

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(dN_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})=1)=R_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\omega t^{\prime})p_{m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}\;,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(dN_{nm}(t)=1|dN_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})=1)=R_{nm}(\omega t)dt~\mathcal{U}_{mn^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime}),

(56)

where
\mathcal{U}_{nm}(t,t^{\prime})\equiv\mathbb{P}(x(t)=n|x(t^{\prime})=m)
is the transition kernel. For
t=t^{\prime}
, the first term becomes

\displaystyle\langle dN_{nm}(t)dN_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t)\rangle=\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}R_{nm}(\omega t)p_{m}(t)dt

(57)

since
\langle[dN_{nm}(t)]^{2}\rangle=\langle dN_{nm}(t)\rangle=R_{nm}(\omega t)p_{m}(t)dt
.
Next, the second term in Eq. (
54
) is

\displaystyle R_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\omega t^{\prime})\langle dN_{nm}\eta_{m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=R_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\omega t^{\prime})\times R_{nm}(\omega t)dt~\mathcal{U}_{mm^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})p_{m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\;.

(58)

The third term in Eq. (
54
) can be written as

\displaystyle R_{nm}(\omega t)\langle\eta_{m}(t)dN_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=R_{nm}(\omega t)\times\mathcal{U}_{mn^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})R_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\omega t^{\prime})p_{m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}\;.

(59)

Finally, the fourth term in Eq. (
54
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle R_{nm}(\omega t)R_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\omega t^{\prime})\langle\eta_{m}(t)\eta_{m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=R_{nm}(\omega t)R_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\omega t^{\prime})\times\mathcal{U}_{mm^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})p_{m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\;.

(60)

Thus, the first term (for
t>t^{\prime}
) cancels with the third term, and the second term cancels with the fourth term. The only remaining part is the first term for
t=t^{\prime}
, which confirms Eq. (
13
).

For both choices of
\zeta

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,2}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\int_{0}^{\tau}dt^{\prime}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\neq m\\

\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{n\neq m}\Lambda_{nm}R_{nm}p_{m}
. Therefore, the correction term becomes

\displaystyle\delta_{\zeta}=\frac{\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,2}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}\rangle}{\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle}=\frac{(-1+\tau\partial_{\tau}-\omega\partial_{\omega})\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle}{\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle}\;,

(71)

for both
\zeta=\zeta^{\rm A}
and
\zeta=\zeta^{\rm E}
.

A.3
Derivation of Eq. (
37
)

The first step in deriving Eq. (
37
) is to show the following relation for an arbitrary function
F
and matrix
\mathbf{G}
:

\displaystyle\left\langle F(\mathbf{x}(t),t)\mathbf{G}^{\textsf{T}}(\mathbf{x}(t^{\prime}),t^{\prime})\bullet\boldsymbol{\xi}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\Theta(t-t^{\prime})\int d\mathbf{x}d\mathbf{x}^{\prime}F(\mathbf{x},t)p(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime};\omega)\mathbf{G}^{\textsf{T}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\mathbb{B}^{\textsf{T}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}p(\mathbf{x},t|\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime};\omega)\;,

(72)

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}
denotes a Gaussian white noise, and
\Theta(t)=1
for
t>0
and zero otherwise. The proof of Eq. (
72
) follows the logic in Ref.
[
9
]
. First, for
t<t^{\prime}
, Eq. (
72
) vanishes due to the independence of the Wiener process from its past states. For
t\geq t^{\prime}
, we need to consider the correlation between
x(t)
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}(t^{\prime})
. In this case, the conditional average, given that
\boldsymbol{\xi}(t^{\prime})=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, can be expressed as

\displaystyle\left\langle F(\mathbf{x}(t),t)\mathbf{G}^{\textsf{T}}(\mathbf{x}(t^{\prime}),t^{\prime})\cdot\boldsymbol{\epsilon}|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\rangle=\int d\mathbf{x}d\mathbf{x}^{\prime}F(\mathbf{x},t)\mathbf{G}^{\textsf{T}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\cdot\boldsymbol{\epsilon}p(\mathbf{x},t|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+d\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime}+dt^{\prime})p(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\;,

(73)

where
d\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\omega t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}+\mathbb{B}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\omega t^{\prime})\cdot\boldsymbol{\epsilon}dt^{\prime}
. Expanding
p(\mathbf{x},t|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+d\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime}+dt^{\prime})
up to linear order in
dt^{\prime}
yields

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x},t|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+d\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime}+dt^{\prime})=p(\mathbf{x},t|\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})+d\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}p(\mathbf{x},t|\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})+dt^{\prime}\partial_{t^{\prime}}p(\mathbf{x},t|\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\;.

(74)

Substituting this expansion into Eq. (
73
) and averaging over
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
using
\langle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\textsf{T}}\rangle=\mathbb{I}/dt^{\prime}
, where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity matrix, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle F(\mathbf{x}(t),t)\mathbf{G}^{\textsf{T}}(\mathbf{x}(t^{\prime}),t^{\prime})\cdot\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\rangle=\int d\mathbf{x}d\mathbf{x}^{\prime}F(\mathbf{x},t)\mathbf{G}^{\textsf{T}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\mathbb{B}^{\textsf{T}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\omega t^{\prime})\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}p(\mathbf{x},t|\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})p(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\;.

(75)

Using this relation, we can show that

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,2}Z_{\tau}^{\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\text{E}}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int d\mathbf{x}d\mathbf{x}^{\prime}M(\mathbf{x},t)\mathbf{J}^{\textsf{T}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}p(\mathbf{x},t|\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int d\mathbf{x}d\mathbf{x}^{\prime}M(\mathbf{x},t)[\partial_{t^{\prime}}p(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})]p(\mathbf{x},t|\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int d\mathbf{x}d\mathbf{x}^{\prime}M(\mathbf{x},t)[\partial_{t^{\prime}}p(\mathbf{x},t|\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})]p(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int d\mathbf{x}M(\mathbf{x},t)[\partial_{t^{\prime}}\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})]p(\mathbf{x},t^{\prime}),

(76)

where
M\equiv\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{A}+\text{tr}(\mathbb{D}\nabla\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\textsf{T}})
is the short notation for the function inside the bracket of Eq. (
32
). Integration by parts and the Fokker-Planck equation are used for the second equalities. Integration by parts is once again used for the third equality.
In Eq. (
76
),
\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})
denotes the time-evolution operator, acting as
p(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})p(\mathbf{x},t^{\prime})
. If we write the Fokker-Planck equation as
\partial_{t}p(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathcal{L}(\omega t)p(\mathbf{x},t)
with the Fokker-Planck operator
\mathcal{L}
, the time-evolution operator can be explicitly expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})=\mathcal{T}\exp\left[\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}dt^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{L}(\omega t^{\prime\prime})\right]\;.

(77)

The final equality in Eq. (
76
) can be achieved as follows:

\displaystyle\int d\mathbf{x}^{\prime}[\partial_{t^{\prime}}p(\mathbf{x},t|\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})]p(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\int d\mathbf{x}^{\prime}[\partial_{t^{\prime}}\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})]\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})p(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=[\partial_{t^{\prime}}\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})]p(\mathbf{x},t^{\prime})\;.

(78)

Similarly to the case of Markov-jump dynamics, we can check that
\partial_{t}\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})=\mathcal{L}(\omega t)\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})
and
\partial_{t^{\prime}}\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})=-\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})\mathcal{L}(\omega t^{\prime})
. For the
\omega
derivative, we have the similar result:

\displaystyle\omega\partial_{\omega}\mathcal{U}(t,0)=\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})[t^{\prime}\partial_{t^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}(\omega t^{\prime})]\mathcal{U}(t^{\prime},0)

\displaystyle=t\mathcal{L}(\omega t)\mathcal{U}(t,0)-\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})\mathcal{L}(\omega t^{\prime})\mathcal{U}(t^{\prime},t)-\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\bigg[\{\partial_{t^{\prime}}\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})\}\mathcal{L}(\omega t^{\prime})\mathcal{U}(t^{\prime},0)+\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})\mathcal{L}(\omega t^{\prime})\{\partial_{t^{\prime}}\mathcal{U}(t^{\prime},0)\}\bigg]\;.

(79)

For the second equality in Eq. (
79
), integration by parts is used. Similarly to Eq. (
67
), the last integration in Eq. (
79
) vanishes. Following the process presented in Eqs. (
68
) and (
69
), we can get the following identity:

\displaystyle[t\partial_{t}-\omega\partial_{\omega}]p(\mathbf{x},t)=-\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}[\partial_{t^{\prime}}\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})]p(\mathbf{x},t^{\prime})\;.

(80)

By substituting Eq. (
80
) into Eq. (
76
), we get

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,2}Z_{\tau}^{\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\text{E}}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\int d\mathbf{x}[t\partial_{t}-\omega\partial_{\omega}]M(\mathbf{x},t)p(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\tau\int d\mathbf{x}M(\mathbf{x},\tau)p(\mathbf{x},\tau)-\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\int d\mathbf{x}M(\mathbf{x},t)p(\mathbf{x},t)-\omega\partial_{\omega}\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\int d\mathbf{x}M(\mathbf{x},t)p(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=(-1+\tau\partial_{\tau}-\omega\partial_{\omega})\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle\;,

(81)

where we use the fact that
M=M(\mathbf{x},\omega t)
in the first equality and
\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\int d\mathbf{x}M(\mathbf{x},t)p(\mathbf{x},t)
in the last equality.

Appendix B
Detailed calculation for open quantum system

B.1
Derivation of Eq. (
43
)

Expanding Eq. (
43
) gives

\displaystyle\left\langle d\tilde{N}_{k}(t)d\tilde{N}_{k^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle dN_{k}(t)dN_{k^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle-\langle\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{k}(t)\rho_{c}(t)]dN_{k^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle dt-\langle dN_{k}(t)\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rho_{c}(t^{\prime})]\rangle dt^{\prime}+\langle\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{k}(t)\rho_{c}(t))\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{{}^{\prime}k}(t^{\prime})\rho_{c}(t^{\prime}))\rangle dtdt^{\prime}\;.

(82)

Without loss of generality, we assume
t\geq t^{\prime}
. The first term in the expansion can be expressed as

\displaystyle\langle dN_{k}(t)dN_{k^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle=P(dN_{k}(t)=1,dN_{k^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})=1)+\delta(t-t^{\prime})\delta_{kk^{\prime}}P(dN_{k}(t)=1)dtdt^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\text{tr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{k}(t)\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rho_{c}(t^{\prime})}{\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rho_{c}(t^{\prime})]}\right)\right]dt\times\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rho_{c}(t^{\prime})]dt^{\prime}\right\rangle+\delta(t-t^{\prime})\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{k}(t)\rho(t)]dtdt^{\prime}\;,

(83)

where
\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})=\mathcal{T}\exp[\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}ds\mathcal{L}(s)]
denotes the time-evolution operator. The second term is

\displaystyle\langle\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{k}(t)\rho_{c}(t)]dN_{k^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle dt=\left\langle\text{tr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{k}(t)\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rho_{c}(t^{\prime})}{\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rho_{c}(t^{\prime})]}\right)\right]dt\times\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rho_{c}(t^{\prime})]dt^{\prime}\right\rangle\;.

(84)

The first part of Eq. (
83
) cancels out this second term.
Similarly, the third and fourth term are

\displaystyle\langle dN_{k}(t)\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rho_{c}(t^{\prime})]\rangle dt^{\prime}=\langle\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{k}(t)\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})\rho_{c}(t^{\prime})]\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rho_{c}(t^{\prime})]\rangle dtdt^{\prime}\;,

(85)

\displaystyle\langle\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{k}(t)\rho_{c}(t)]dt\times\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rho_{c}(t^{\prime})]dt^{\prime}\rangle=\langle\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{k}(t)\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})\rho_{c}(t^{\prime})]\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rho_{c}(t^{\prime})]\rangle dtdt^{\prime}\;.

(86)

Thus, the third and fourth terms cancel each other. Therefore, the noise correlation is reduced to Eq. (
43
).

B.2
Calculation of the correction term
\delta

To evaluate the correction term, it is necessary to calculate
\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,2}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}\rangle
:

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,2}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\Lambda_{k}(t)\zeta_{k^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\Delta_{k,k^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})\;,

(87)

where
\Delta_{k,k^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})
is defined as

\displaystyle\Delta_{k,k^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})\equiv\left\langle\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{k}\rho_{c}(t)](\dot{N}_{k^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})-\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}\rho_{c}(t^{\prime})])\right\rangle\;.

(88)

Using the same formulation as in Appendix
B.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta_{k,k^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})=\left\langle\text{tr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{k}\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})\left\{\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}\psi_{t^{\prime}}-\psi_{t^{\prime}}\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}\psi_{t^{\prime}})\right\}\right]\right\rangle\;,

(89)

where
\psi_{t^{\prime}}\equiv\rho_{c}(t^{\prime})
. Equation (
89
) can be evaluated in three specific cases. The first is the case where a system is in the steady state. In this case, since
\Lambda
and
\zeta
are time-independent, they can be taken out of the integration. Using the explicit expression
\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})=\exp[\mathcal{L}(t-t^{\prime})]
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\Delta_{k,k^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\text{tr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{k}\mathcal{L}^{\text{d}}(\mathcal{L}^{\text{d}}(e^{\mathcal{L}\tau}-1)-\tau)\left\{\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}\psi-\psi\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}\psi)\right\}\right]\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\tau\left[\Delta_{k,k^{\prime}}^{\text{ss}}+\mathcal{O}(1/\tau)\right]\;,

(90)

where
\mathcal{L}^{\text{d}}
represents the Drazin inverse of
\mathcal{L}
, and

\displaystyle\Delta_{k,k^{\prime}}^{\text{ss}}=-\left\langle\text{tr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{k}\mathcal{L}^{\text{d}}\left\{\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}\psi-\psi\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}\psi)\right\}\right]\right\rangle\;.

(91)

Thus,
\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,2}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}\rangle/\tau\rightarrow\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\Lambda_{k}\zeta_{k^{\prime}}\Delta_{k,k^{\prime}}^{\text{ss}}
as
\tau\rightarrow\infty
.

The second case is a quantum reset process in the steady state, where we can further calculate
\Delta_{k,k^{\prime}}^{\text{ss}}
. We first define a set of pure states,
\mathcal{S}\equiv\{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{K}\}
, where each
\varphi_{k}
denotes the destination of the corresponding jump operator
L_{k}
. The quantum reset process is defined as one in which the jump destination is independent of the pre-jump state.
This is formulated as
L_{k}\psi L_{k}^{\dagger}=\varphi_{k}\text{tr}(L_{k}\psi L_{k}^{\dagger})
. The steady-state distribution of
\psi
for this system is given by
[
35
]

\displaystyle P_{\text{ss}}[\psi]=\sum_{m}c_{m}\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,\,s_{m}(u)\delta(\psi-\varphi_{m}(u))\;,

(92)

where
c_{m}=\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{m}\rho_{\text{ss}})
with the steady-state density matrix
\rho_{\text{ss}}
.
s_{m}(u)
is the survival probability without jump defined as

\displaystyle s_{m}(u)=\text{tr}\left(e^{-iH_{\text{eff}}u}\varphi_{m}e^{iH_{\text{eff}}^{\dagger}u}\right)\;,

(93)

where
H_{\text{eff}}=H-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{k}L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}
, and
\varphi_{m}(u)
is the normalized survived state, given by

\displaystyle\varphi_{m}(u)=e^{-iH_{\text{eff}}u}\varphi_{m}e^{iH_{\text{eff}}^{\dagger}u}/s_{m}(u)\;.

(94)

Using the probability
P_{\text{ss}}[\psi]
, we evaluate
\Delta_{k,k^{\prime}}^{\text{ss}}
as

\displaystyle\Delta_{k,k^{\prime}}^{\text{ss}}

\displaystyle=-\int d\psi\;P_{\text{ss}}[\psi]\text{tr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{k}\mathcal{L}^{\text{d}}\left\{\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}\psi-\psi\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}\psi)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=-\sum_{m}c_{m}\int_{0}^{\infty}dus_{m}(u)

\displaystyle~~~\times\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}\varphi_{m}(u)]\text{tr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{k}\mathcal{L}^{\text{d}}\{\varphi_{k^{\prime}}-\varphi_{m}(u)\}\right].

(95)

For the second equality in Eq. (
95
),
\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}\psi=\varphi_{k^{\prime}}\text{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}\psi)
is used.

The third case considers a Hamiltonian that is diagonal in the energy eigenbasis, with
L_{nm}=\sqrt{R_{nm}}|n\rangle\langle m|
, where
R_{nm}
is the transition rate from
m
to
n
. Additionally, we assume
\rho_{c}(t)=\sum_{n}\eta_{n}(t)|n\rangle\langle n|
, where
\eta_{n}(t)=\langle n|\psi_{t}|n\rangle
.
This scenario corresponds to classical Markov jump systems.
By substituting
k\rightarrow nm
and
k^{\prime}\rightarrow n^{\prime}m^{\prime}
, we can calculate the two terms in
\Delta_{nm,n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})
of Eq. (
89
). The first term is

\displaystyle\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{nm}\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})\mathcal{L}_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\rho_{c}(t^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{nm}\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})R_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\eta_{m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})|n^{\prime}\rangle\langle n^{\prime}|]

\displaystyle=R_{nm}(t)\mathcal{U}_{mn^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})R_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\eta_{m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\;,

(96)

and the second term is

\displaystyle\langle\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{k}\rho_{c}(t)]\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{k^{\prime}}\rho_{c}(t^{\prime})]\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle R_{nm}\eta_{m}(t)R_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\eta_{m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=R_{nm}\mathcal{U}_{mm^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})R_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\eta_{m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\;.

(97)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}^{\Lambda,2}Z_{\tau}^{\zeta}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\sum\Lambda_{nm}(t)\zeta_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\Delta_{nm,n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})\;,

(98)

where
\Delta_{nm,n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})
is given by

\displaystyle\Delta_{nm,n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=R_{nm}(t)[\mathcal{U}_{mn^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})-\mathcal{U}_{mm^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})]R_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\eta_{m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\;.

(99)

Substituting Eq. (
99
) into Eq. (
87
) shows that the correction term is identical to the classical one in Eq. (
64
).

Appendix C
Brief note on the VS bound and the
\psi
-KUR

In this section, we briefly summarize the expressions for the VS bound (Eq. (
52
)) and the
\psi
-KUR (Eq. (
53
)) for the reader’s convenience. In Ref.
[
30
]
, Vu and Saito proved the following TUR:

\displaystyle\frac{D}{J^{2}}\geq\frac{(1+\tilde{\delta})^{2}}{\Sigma_{\tau}^{\text{tot}}/2+\mathcal{Q}_{1}}\;.

(100)

To express the terms in the above inequality, we first consider
\theta
-perturbed dynamics, applied to the Hamiltonian and jump operators as follows:

\displaystyle H_{\theta}=(1+\theta)H,\quad L_{k,\theta}=\sqrt{1+\ell_{k}\theta}L_{k}\;,

(101)

where
\ell_{k}=\zeta_{k}^{\text{E}}
defined in Eq. (
47
). Then, the expression of
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{1}=-\langle\partial_{\theta}^{2}\ln||\Psi_{\theta}(\Gamma)\rangle|^{2}\rangle_{\theta=0}\;,

(102)

where
|\Psi_{\theta}(\Gamma)\rangle
is defined as

\displaystyle|\Psi_{\theta}(\Gamma)\rangle:=U_{\theta}(\tau,t_{N})\prod_{j=1}^{N}L_{k_{j}}U_{\theta}(t_{j},t_{j-1})|n\rangle\;.

(103)

Here,
N
is the total number of jump during the interval
[0,\tau]
,
t_{j}
is the time of
j
-th jump,
|n\rangle
is the initial state, and
U(t^{\prime},t)=\exp[-iH_{\text{eff}}(t^{\prime}-t)]
. The correction term
\tilde{\delta}
is given by
\tilde{\delta}=J_{*}/J
, where

\displaystyle J_{*}=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{k}\Lambda_{k}\text{tr}[\mathcal{L}_{k}\phi(t)]\;

(104)

and
\phi(t)
is the solution of the following equation:

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}(t)=\mathcal{L}[\rho(t)+\phi(t)]+\sum_{k}[\ell_{k}-1]\mathcal{D}[L_{k}]\rho_{t}\;

(105)

with the initial condition
\phi(0)=0
. We are interested in the long-time, steady-state regime of the bound. In this regime, we can show
\mathcal{Q}_{1}\leq\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{\text{u}}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{\text{u}}=-4\tau[\text{tr}(\mathcal{F}_{1}\mathbb{P}\mathcal{L}^{\text{d}}\mathbb{P}\mathcal{F}_{2}\rho_{\text{ss}})+\text{tr}(\mathcal{F}_{2}\mathbb{P}\mathcal{L}^{\text{d}}\mathbb{P}\mathcal{F}_{1}\rho_{\text{ss}})]\;.

(106)

Here,
\mathbb{P}\rho=\rho-\text{tr}(\rho)\rho_{\text{ss}}
is the projection onto the complement of the steady-state space,
\mathcal{L}^{\text{d}}
denotes the Drazin inverse, and
\mathcal{F}_{1,2}
are the superoperators given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1}\rho

\displaystyle=-iH\rho+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\ell_{k}[L_{k}\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}-L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}\rho]\;,

(107)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2}\rho

\displaystyle=i\rho H+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\ell_{k}[L_{k}\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}-\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}]\;.

In the steady state,
\phi(t)
can be replaced with its steady-state form:

\displaystyle\phi_{\text{ss}}=\mathbb{P}\mathcal{L}^{\text{d}}\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum_{k}[1-\ell_{k}]\mathcal{D}[L_{k}]\rho_{\text{ss}}\right\}\;.

(108)

In Ref.
[
30
]
, Vu and Saito also derived the following KUR:

\displaystyle\frac{D}{J^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{\mathcal{A}_{\tau}+\mathcal{Q}_{2}}\;.

(109)

In this case, there is no correction term
\tilde{\delta}
in the numerator. To derive this relation, they used the perturbation as follows:

\displaystyle H_{\theta}=(1+\theta)H,\quad L_{k,\theta}=\sqrt{1+\theta}L_{k}\;.

(110)

Similarly to the VS TUR, the quantum coherence contribution satisfies
\mathcal{Q}_{2}\leq\mathcal{Q}_{2}^{\text{u}}
in the long-time limit, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{2}^{\text{u}}=-4\tau[\text{tr}(\mathcal{G}_{1}\mathbb{P}\mathcal{L}^{\text{d}}\mathbb{P}\mathcal{G}_{2}\rho_{\text{ss}})+\text{tr}(\mathcal{G}_{2}\mathbb{P}\mathcal{L}^{\text{d}}\mathbb{P}\mathcal{G}_{1}\rho_{\text{ss}})]\;,

(111)

with the superoperator
\mathcal{G}_{1,2}
defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{1}\rho

\displaystyle=-iH\rho+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}[L_{k}\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}-L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}\rho]\;,

(112)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{2}\rho

\displaystyle=i\rho H+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}[L_{k}\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}-\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}]\;.

In the examples of the main text, we use the expressions in Eqs. (
106
), (
108
), and (
111
) to estimate the VS bounds.

Recently, Prech et al. proved the
\psi
-KUR (Eq. (
53
)) in Ref.
[
31
]
using quantum Cramér–Rao inequality. They derived the relation with
\theta
-perturbed dynamics, where
L_{k,\theta}=\sqrt{1+\theta}L_{k}
. Note that this approach differs from Vu and Saito’s, as the system Hamiltonian is not perturbed. They show that
\psi=J_{\psi}/J
, with

\displaystyle J_{\psi}=\sum_{k}\Lambda_{k}\text{tr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{k}\mathcal{L}^{\text{d}}\mathcal{H}\rho_{\text{ss}}\right]\;,

(113)

where
\mathcal{H}\rho=-i[H,\rho]
.

Appendix D
Detailed calculations of the examples

In this section, we present the detailed expressions for thermodynamic quantities discussed in the main text.

D.1
Driven two-level system

Consider the open quantum system with the following Hamiltonian and jump operators:

\displaystyle H=\Delta(|0\rangle\langle 1|+|1\rangle\langle 0|)\;,

\displaystyle L_{0}=\sqrt{\gamma n}|1\rangle\langle 0|,\quad L_{1}=\sqrt{\gamma(n+1)}|0\rangle\langle 1|\;.

(114)

The steady-state density matrix of this system can be evaluated as

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{ss}}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\gamma^{2}(n+1)(2n+1)+4\Delta^{2}}{\gamma^{2}(2n+1)^{2}+8\Delta^{2}}&\frac{2i\gamma\Delta}{\gamma^{2}(2n+1)^{2}+8\Delta^{2}}\\
-\frac{2i\gamma\Delta}{\gamma^{2}(2n+1)^{2}+8\Delta^{2}}&\frac{\gamma^{2}n(2n+1)+4\Delta^{2}}{\gamma^{2}(2n+1)^{2}+8\Delta^{2}}\end{pmatrix}\;.

(115)

For the observable
\mathcal{J}_{\tau}=N_{1}(\tau)-N_{0}(\tau)
, the mean and variance are given by

\displaystyle J=\langle\mathcal{J}_{\tau}\rangle=\tau\frac{4\gamma\Delta^{2}}{\gamma^{2}(2n+1)^{2}+8\Delta^{2}}\;,

\displaystyle D=\text{Var}(\mathcal{J}_{\tau})=\tau[D_{\infty}+\mathcal{O}(1/\tau)]\;,

(116)

where
D_{\infty}
is expressed as

\displaystyle D_{\infty}=\frac{4(1+2n)\gamma\Delta^{2}[\gamma^{4}(2n+1)^{4}+8(8n^{2}+8n-1)\gamma^{2}\Delta^{2}+64\Delta^{4}]}{(\gamma^{2}(2n+1)^{2}+8\Delta^{2})^{3}}\;.

(117)

The calculation of these quantities relies on full counting statistics (FCS), a widely used approach in this field (see Ref.
[
36
]
for a review).

Since there is only one pair of jump operators satisfying detailed balance, the total EP is simply given by
\Sigma_{\tau}^{\text{tot}}=\ln\left(\frac{n+1}{n}\right)J
. From Eq. (
48
), the pseudo-EP is given by

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\tau}^{\text{ps}}=\tau\frac{8\gamma\Delta^{4}}{(1+2n)(\gamma^{2}n(n+1)+2\Delta^{2})(\gamma^{2}(2n+1)^{2}+8\Delta^{2})}\;.

(118)

Thus, in the long-time limit,
D
,
J
, and
\Sigma_{\tau}^{\text{tot}}\sim\Delta^{2}
, while
\Delta_{\tau}^{\text{ps}}\sim\Delta^{4}
for small
\Delta
. Additionally, we note that all physical quantities converge to finite values as
\Delta\rightarrow\infty
. From Eq. (
106
), the upper bound of the quantum co herence contribution in the VS bound is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{\text{u}}=\frac{8\Delta^{2}[2\gamma^{4}(2n+1)^{4}+32\gamma^{2}\Delta^{2}(2n+1)^{2}+128\Delta^{4}][\gamma^{4}n(n+1)(2n+1)^{2}+16\gamma^{2}\Delta^{2}n(n+1)+16\Delta^{4}]}{\gamma(2n+1)[\gamma^{2}n(n+1)+2\Delta^{2}][\gamma^{2}(2n+1)^{2}+8\Delta^{2}]^{3}}\;.

(119)

Therefore,
\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{\text{u}}\sim\Delta^{2}
for both
\Delta\rightarrow 0
and
\Delta\rightarrow\infty
.

D.2
Three-state quantum clock

We consider the open quantum system with the following Hamiltonian and jump operators:

\displaystyle H=\Delta(|0\rangle\langle 1|+|1\rangle\langle 0|)

\displaystyle L_{10}=\sqrt{\gamma n_{s}}|1\rangle\langle 0|,\quad L_{21}=\sqrt{\gamma n_{f}}|2\rangle\langle 1|,\quad L_{02}=\sqrt{\gamma n_{f}}|0\rangle\langle 2|\;.

(120)

The steady-state solution of this system is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{ss}}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{3}+\frac{2(n_{f}-n_{s})(n_{f}+n_{s})\gamma^{2}}{3[(n_{f}+2n_{s})(n_{f}+n_{s})\gamma^{2}+12\Delta^{2}]}&\frac{2i(n_{f}-n_{s})\gamma\Delta}{(n_{f}+2n_{s})(n_{f}+n_{s})\gamma^{2}+12\Delta^{2}}&0\\
-\frac{2i(n_{f}-n_{s})\gamma\Delta}{(n_{f}+2n_{s})(n_{f}+n_{s})\gamma^{2}+12\Delta^{2}}&\frac{1}{3}-\frac{(n_{f}-n_{s})(n_{f}+n_{s})\gamma^{2}}{3[(n_{f}+2n_{s})(n_{f}+n_{s})\gamma^{2}+12\Delta^{2}]}&0\\
0&0&\frac{1}{3}-\frac{(n_{f}-n_{s})(n_{f}+n_{s})\gamma^{2}}{3[(n_{f}+2n_{s})(n_{f}+n_{s})\gamma^{2}+12\Delta^{2}]}\end{pmatrix}\;.

(121)

We note that the system becomes classical when
n_{f}=n_{s}
or
\Delta=0
. The expression of the total dynamical activity, which corresponds to the mean value of the total number of ticks
\mathcal{J}_{\tau}=N_{10}(\tau)+N_{21}(\tau)+N_{02}(\tau)
, is

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\tau}=\gamma\tau\frac{3\gamma^{2}n_{f}n_{s}(n_{f}+n_{s})+4\Delta^{2}(2n_{f}+n_{s})}{\gamma^{2}(n_{f}+2n_{s})(n_{f}+n_{s})+12\Delta^{2}}\;.

(122)

The variance of the observable,
D=\tau(D_{\infty}+\mathcal{O}(1/\tau))
, can be calculated using FCS. The result is

\displaystyle D_{\infty}=\frac{\gamma[f_{0}\gamma^{6}+f_{2}\Delta^{2}\gamma^{4}+f_{4}\Delta_{4}\gamma^{2}+f_{6}\Delta^{6}]}{n_{f}[\gamma^{2}(n_{f}+2n_{s})(n_{f}+n_{s})+12\Delta^{2}]^{3}}\;,

(123)

where the expressions of
f_{0}
,
f_{2}
,
f_{4}
, and
f_{6}
are

\displaystyle f_{0}

\displaystyle=9n_{f}^{7}n_{s}+27n_{f}^{6}n_{s}^{2}+45n_{f}^{5}n_{s}^{3}+63n_{f}^{4}n_{s}^{4}+54n_{f}^{3}n_{s}^{5}+18n_{f}^{2}n_{s}^{6}\;,

\displaystyle f_{2}

\displaystyle=16n_{f}^{6}+28n_{f}^{5}n_{s}+496n_{f}^{4}n_{s}^{2}+604n_{f}^{3}n_{s}^{3}+136n_{f}^{2}n_{s}^{4}+16n_{f}n_{s}^{5}\;,

\displaystyle f_{4}

\displaystyle=-128n_{f}^{4}+1584n_{f}^{3}n_{s}+1136n_{f}^{2}n_{s}^{2}-32n_{f}n_{s}^{3}+32n_{s}^{4}\;,

\displaystyle f_{6}

\displaystyle=1280n_{f}^{2}+320n_{f}n_{s}+128n_{s}^{2}\;.

(124)

Thus,
\mathcal{A}_{\tau}\sim\Delta^{0}
and
D\sim\Delta^{0}
for large
\Delta
. The expression for the upper bound
\mathcal{Q}_{2}^{\rm u}
from Eq. (
111
) is too complicated to display here. Its numerical result, shown in the inset of Fig.
2
, verifies that
\mathcal{Q}_{2}^{\text{u}}\sim\Delta^{2}
.

D.3
Simple argument for
\mathcal{Q}\sim\Delta^{2}

In the quantum reset process described in Appendix.
B.2
,
L_{k}|\psi\rangle=|L_{k}|\psi\rangle||\psi_{k}\rangle
, where
|\psi_{k}\rangle
is the ket corresponding to the pure density matrix
\varphi_{k}\in\mathcal{S}
. Thus, the quantum coherence contribution in the VS bound can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}=-\langle\partial_{\theta}^{2}\ln|U_{\theta}(\tau,t_{N})|\psi_{k_{N}}\rangle|^{2}\rangle_{\theta=0}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\langle\partial^{2}_{\theta}\ln|L_{k}U_{\theta}(t_{j},t_{j-1})|\psi_{k_{j-1}}\rangle|^{2}\rangle_{\theta=0}\;.

(125)

In our models, the off-diagonal elements of the Hamiltonian are controlled by the parameter
\Delta
. Therefore, for large
\Delta
, the evolution governed by the effective Hamiltonian
U_{\theta}
is dominated by the rotation induced by
\Delta
.
Since the Hamiltonian is perturbed as
H_{\theta}=(1+\theta)H
in the formulation of the VS bound, the second derivative with respect to
\theta
introduces a factor of
\Delta^{2}
. Based on this reasoning, we expect the quantum coherence contribution in the VS bound to also scale as
\mathcal{Q}\sim\Delta^{2}
in the large
\Delta
regime.
