Toluene-2-monooxygenase expression and trichloroethene oxidizing activity of Burkholderia vietnamiensis G4 grown on benzyl alcohol and benzyl esters
Authors: Alyssa M. Saito, Alejandra P. Oyarzún, Michael R. Hyman, Lewis Semprini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBenzyl alcohol (BA) and two BA esters were examined for their ability to support growth of Burkholderia vietnamiensis G4 and expression of toluene-2-monooxygenase (T2MO) activity. Resting cell assays and an activity-based labeling (ABL) approach were used to quantify T2MO activity and levels of T2MO hydroxylase subunit in cells grown either on substrates already known to support T2MO expression (toluene, phenol); novel aromatic substrates (BA, benzyl acetate (BAc); benzyl butyrate (BBu)); and non-aromatics (lactate, acetate, and butyrate). Specific rates of TCE oxidation and levels of activity-based fluorescent labeling of T2MO hydroxylase α-subunits in cells grown on BA or BA esters were comparable to or greater than those of cells grown on toluene or phenol. In contrast, levels of activity and protein labeling of cells grown on acetate or butyrate were like those of lactate-grown cells. Cells grown on BA also degraded 1,2-cis-dichloroethene (cis-DCE), 1,1-dichloroethene (1,1-DCE), and vinyl chloride (VC). While toluene-grown cells generally exhibited higher specific rates of chloroethene transformation, BA-grown cells had consistently higher transformation capacities for these compounds. Stable accumulation of BA in batch cultures grown on BBu in the presence of propyne and the inhibitory effects of BA on TCE transformation both suggest BA is a T2MO substrate. Substrate-specific O2 uptake studies also suggest 2-hydroxybenzyl alcohol is a likely product of T2MO-dependent BA oxidation. Our results suggest that BA and benzyl esters could be useful for promoting cometabolic transformations, because unlike toluene or phenol, these are “generally regarded as safe” (GRAS) compounds with little or no human toxicity.Key points• Burkholderia vietnamiensis G4 cometabolizes TCE when grown on benzyl alcohol (BA).• Activity-based labeling showed BA and BA producing esters support T2MO expression.• BA and BA producing esters are safe compounds to promote aerobic TCE transformation. Introduction

Trichloroethylene (TCE) is a known human carcinogen, a US EPA Priority Pollutant, and a common organic groundwater contaminant due to its extensive historical use as an industrial solvent (Zogorski, 2006). Under anaerobic conditions, highly chlorinated ethenes like TCE and tetrachloroethene (PCE) can serve as terminal electron acceptors and can be biodegraded through microbially-catalyzed reductive dehalogenation reactions. This dehalorespiration results in the sequential production of cis−1,2-dichloroethene (cis-DCE) vinyl chloride (VC) and ethene, with 1,1-dichloroethene (1,1-DCE) and trans−1,2-dichloroethene (trans-DCE) as minor products (Semprini et al. 1995; Magnuson et al. 1998). Compared to anaerobic approaches for bioremediation, aerobic methods have some advantages. While anaerobic approaches can be applied to treat high concentrations of TCE (Yu and Semprini 2004), aerobic treatment has potential to treat low concentrations that may be required to meet regulatory requirements (Semprini 1997; Frascari et al. 2015). Also, aerobic processes would better maintain groundwater quality, as anaerobic treatment, being a reductive process, often leads to the reduction of other electron acceptors including sulfate, ferric iron, and carbon dioxide resulting in the production of sulfide, ferrous iron, and methane (Aulenta et al. 2006; Azizian et al. 2010).

Aerobic cometabolism involves the use of a typically benign primary substrate to promote the growth and activity of contaminant-degrading bacteria and in situ bioremediation based on this process is a potentially cost-effective treatment for groundwater contaminated with TCE and its lesser-chlorinated daughter products. A consistent feature of aerobic TCE cometabolism is that the catabolism of the primary substrate is initiated by a non-specific oxygenase enzyme which can also fortuitously degrade TCE and potentially other contaminants (Semprini 1997; Arp et al. 2001; Frascari et al. 2015; Hopkins and McCarty 1995; Nelson et al. 1987; Azizian et al. 2007).

A number of primary growth substrates have been shown to support the cometabolism of TCE and other chlorinated ethenes, such as cis-DCE, 1,1-DCE, trans-DCE, and VC. These primary substrates include, among others, toluene, phenol, methane, and propane (Semprini 1997; Frascari et al. 2015). Laboratory kinetic studies using pure cultures found the methanotroph Methylosinus trichosporium OB3b when expressing soluble methane monooxygenase (sMMO) transformed TCE at much higher rates than Pseudomonas cepacia G4 (Burkholderia vietnamiensis G4) which express T2MO when grown on toluene or phenol (Alvarez and Speitel, 2001). However, in situ demonstrations conducted at the same test site found native microorganisms stimulated by phenol or toluene were more effective at treating TCE than native organisms stimulated by methane (Hopkins and McCarty (1995). Other advantages of phenol and toluene are their higher aqueous solubilities compared to methane and propane, potentially permitting easier use in situ when adding to recirculating groundwater treatment systems (McCarty et al. 1998).

Both phenol and toluene have also been evaluated in pilot-scale field tests for TCE transformation (Hopkins et al. 1993a, b; Hopkins and McCarty 1995; McCarty et al. 1998). Although the field studies demonstrated phenol and toluene were both biodegraded to below regulatory levels, biostimulation of aquifers with these compounds remains controversial because they are both themselves US EPA Priority Pollutants. Thus, developing alternative substrates to promote the expression of the same monooxygenases observed during growth on toluene or phenol is of interest.

At least five distinct bacterial oxygenases have been described that initiate aerobic toluene catabolism (Parales et al. 2008). Three of these are soluble di-iron monooxygenases (SDIMOs) that selectively hydroxylate the aromatic ring of toluene at either the ortho (2)-, meta (3)-, or para (4)-positions. All three of these SDIMOs can oxidize TCE and other chlorinated ethenes and the model strains in which they have been most extensively studied are Burkholderia vietnamiensis G4 (T2MO), Ralstonia pickettii PKO1 (toluene-3-monooxygenase [T3MO]), and Pseudomonas mendocina KR1 (toluene-4-monooxygenase [T4MO], respectively (Parales et al. 2008).

Cometabolic transformation of TCE by B. vietnamiensis G4 has been particularly well studied as this bacterium exhibits high and sustainable rates of TCE transformation and relatively high threshold for toxic effects arising from cometabolic TCE oxidation (Yeager et al. 2001a & b). At the molecular level, T2MO consists of a 3 subunit ~ 210 kDa hydroxylase organized in an (αβγ)2 configuration. The masses of the 3 hydroxylase components are 61, 38, and 14 kDa, for the α, β, and γ subunits, respectively. The enzyme also contains a 40 kDa reductase that contains FAD and a Fe-S center and a 10.5 kDa regulatory protein that lacks cofactors (Newman & Wackett 1995). Phenol and toluene both support expression of T2MO and aerobic cometabolism of chlorinated ethenes by B. vietnamiensis G4 (Folsom et al. 1990). It has previously been shown that benzyl alcohol (BA) can support the cometabolic transformation of TCE, cis-DCE and VC in a mixed microbial culture (Tejasen 2003) but this compound has not been further characterized either in terms of the microorganisms or enzymes responsible for this activity.

Unlike either phenol or toluene, BA is a non-regulated food grade chemical and can also be generated from the hydrolysis of benzyl esters which are also widely used as flavoring agents in foods.

In this study, we demonstrate that BA and two of its esters are growth supporting substrates for B. vietnamiensis G4 and we have characterized the impacts of growth on these compounds on T2MO activity and T2MO hydroxylase α-subunit expression levels. The results have been interpreted in terms of their impacts on our understanding of TCE cometabolism by this model toluene-oxidizing bacterium and the potential use of BA and BA-generating esters as slow-release compounds suitable for promoting the aerobic cometabolic transformation of TCE and other chlorinated ethenes.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

AlexaFluor 647-azide (99% purity) was obtained from Invitrogen (Grad Island, NY). Benzyl alcohol (BA) (100%), and sodium lactate (60% syrup) and TCE (99.5% purity) were obtained from J.T. Baker. 3-butyn-1-ol (97% purity), BAc (99% purity), 1,4-diethynylbenzene (96% purity), 1,1-DCE (99% purity), 3-methylcatchol (98% purity), o-cresol (≥ 99% purity), 1,7-octadiyne (98% purity), propyne (≥ 99% purity), sodium acetate (99% purity), toluene (99.8% purity), and VC (99.5% purity) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. Inc (Milwaukee, WI). BBu (97% purity), cis-DCE (99% purity), 1,6-heptadiyne (97% purity), 1,8-nonadiyne (> 95% purity), and 1,9-decadiyne (> 95% purity) were obtained from TCI America (Portland, OR). 1,5-hexadiyne (50% v/v in pentane), phenol (99% purity), and sodium butyrate (98% purity) were obtained from Alfa Aesar (Ward Hill, MA). All other chemicals used were of reagent grade or better. Compressed gases (He, H2, N2, and air) were obtained from local industrial vendors.

Bacterial strain and cell growth

B. vietnamiensis G4 was routinely maintained on mineral salt media plates containing lactate (20 mM) as carbon and energy source as presented in the Supplementary File. The method for growing B. vietnamiensis G4 for the TCE and chlorinated ethene transformation tests are also presented in the Supplementary File.

Solvent stock solutions

Saturated stock solutions of chlorinated solvents were made by adding neat solvent (1 mL) to a glass serum vial (27 mL) filled with deionized water to give minimal headspace. The vials were sealed with butyl rubber septa and aluminum crimp seals. The day before use, the vials were vigorously shaken for 30 s to ensure saturation and were then allowed to settle overnight before experimental use. When used as a source of substrate in experiments with cells, the compounds mass was estimated using its saturated aqueous solubility at 20 °C.

Analysis of volatile chemicals

The concentrations of volatile chemicals in reactions were determined by gas chromatography (GC) through headspace analyses. Gas phase samples (100 µL) were obtained using gas-tight syringes. For TCE, cis-DCE and 1,1-DCE, samples were analyzed using a Hewlett Packard 6890 Series GC fitted with a micro electron capture detector and a capillary column (Agilent DB-624 UI 30 m × 0.53 mm). The GC was operated with a constant oven temperature of 50 °C, and helium was used as carrier gas at a flow rate of 15 mL/min. For VC and toluene, samples were analyzed using a Hewlett Packard 6890 Series GC fitted with a flame ionization detector and capillary column (Agilent DB-624 UI 30 m × 0.53 mm). Constant oven temperatures of 150 °C and 220 °C were used for the analysis of VC and toluene, respectively, and in both cases, helium was used as carrier gas at a flow rate of 15 mL/min. Each analyte was quantified by comparing peak areas to standard curves made from known amounts of authentic standards. The total mass of contaminants in experimental reactions was calculated assuming equilibrium between the liquid and headspace using dimensionless Henry’s constants at 20 °C (Gossett 1987).

Analysis of non-volatile chemicals

The concentrations of BA were determined using a Dionex 500X high performance liquid chromatograph (HPLC) equipped with an AD20 UV absorbance detector. Chromatographic separation was achieved with a HiChrom Prevail 5 μm organic acid column (250 × 4.6 mm). The HPLC was run in isocratic mode with a 0.06 M phosphate eluent at a flowrate of 1 mL/min for the first 5 min followed by a 1.5 mL/min flowrate for the following 15 min. Concentrations of BA were determined by developing a standard curve (at 254 nm) with external standards. The method has a detection limit of 0.05 mg L−1.

Growth tests with benzyl alcohol and benzyl butyrate

Batch tests were performed to demonstrate the growth of B. vietnamiensis G4 on either BA or BBu. The tests were performed in the presence and absence of propyne, a known inhibitor of T2MO (Yeager et al. 1999). The experiment was conducted in sterile Wheaton serum glass bottles (250 mL) with 175 mL of phosphate buffered mineral salt media (MSM) and 135 mL of headspace with plastic screw caps and butyl septa. For each substrate, two treatments, with or without propyne at 1.7% (v/v) in the headspace, were performed in triplicate and consisted of a low initial biomass of B. vietnamiensis G4 (0.1 mgTSS) in MSM with BA or BBu (0.50 mM; 250 mg/L). The presence of propyne ensures inhibition of T2MO and was at lower explosive limit of 1.7% (v/v gas phase) (Nassimi et al. 2017). The cultures were incubated in the dark at 30 °C in an environmental shaker operated at 200 rpm. Biomass growth was measured over time through OD600 measurements using an Orion Aquamate 8000 UV–Vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific).

Initial rates of contaminant transformation

Resting cell kinetic tests were conducted in sterile glass serum vials (27 mL) that contained MSM (10 mL). The vials were sealed with sterile butyl septa and aluminum crimp seals. The required volume of saturated stock solution to yield the desired mass of substrate was added to the sealed vials through the septum using glass microsyringes (Hamilton, Reno, NV). The vials were then incubated for 10 min on a shaker table operated at 200 rpm to allow equilibration of the contaminant between the aqueous and headspace phases. The initial headspace concentration of the contaminant was then measured by the gas GC method previously described. The reactions were initiated by the addition of a known mass of cells to the vials through the septa using a sterile plastic syringe. The vials were incubated at 20 °C at 200 rpm to maintain substrate equilibrium between the aqueous and headspace compartments.

Determination of transformation capacity (Tc)

The transformation capacity (Tc) is defined as the maximal mass of substrate that can be transformed by a given mass of resting cells (Alvarez-Cohen and McCarty 1991). In this study, this capacity was determined in the same experiments used to determine initial rates. Once the majority (≥ 98%) of the initial contaminant had been transformed, the contaminant was replenished in the reaction vial at the same or higher concentrations and continued transformation of the contaminant was monitored. This process was repeated until contaminant transformation ceased. The mass of contaminant transformed by resting cells was then compared to the mass lost in abiotic control reaction vials that contain MSM and the equivalent total introduced mass of contaminant but not cells. To verify contaminant transformation was due to T2MO activity, additional control incubations were conducted in reactions containing individual contaminants, cells, and propyne (2% v/v gas phase).

Inhibition tests

To determine whether BA affects T2MO activity in B. vietnamiensis G4, transformation of a fixed initial concentration of TCE was monitored in cell incubations containing toluene or BA (10 mg/L or 20 mg/L initial concentrations of each). The reactions were conducted in serum glass bottles (125 mL) that contained MSM (20 mL) and were sealed with butyl septa. As required, TCE was added from an aqueous stock solution to an initial dissolved concentration of 550 µg/L. Toluene was added from an aqueous stock solution (1 g/L) to obtain an initial dissolved concentrations of 10 mg/L or 20 mg/L while BA was added from a stock solution (5g/L) to achieve dissolved liquid concentrations of 10 mg/L and 20 mg/L. When required, propyne 1.7% propyne (v/v gas phase) was added to the reaction headspace. The reactions were initiated by adding cells to the vials which were then incubated at 20 °C on a shaker operated at 200 rpm. The rate of TCE transformation was then established from changes in the headspace concentration of TCE, as determined using the GC method previously described.

Activity-based labeling

Activity-based labeling (ABL) of T2MO components in B. vietnamiensis G4 was conducted on cells grown on individual substrates and harvested as described above. The method used was based on the approach described by Bennett et al. (2016). Details of the (ABL) method are provided in the Supplementary File. The major steps in the ABL of toluene-2-monooxygenase are shown in Figure S5.

Oxygen uptake measurements

O2 uptake measurements were made using a Clark-style oxygen electrode (Hansatech, King’s Lynn, Norfolk, UK) mounted in a glass water-jacketed reaction vessel operated at 30 °C. All reactions were conducted in a sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7) and had a final reaction volume of 2 mL. Specific rates of substrate dependent O2 uptake were determined for samples of cells (20 µL) incubated with either toluene (200 μM), o-cresol (200 μM), or 3-methylcatechol (400 μM). Other substrates were all tested at a concentration of 1 mM.

Suspended solids and protein determinations

The culture density (mg TSS/L) was determined gravimetrically through TSS analysis. A dry weight was obtained with a 0.45 µm Whatman ME 25/21 mixed cellulose filter in a 5.1-cm aluminum foil dish. Suspended cells were added to the filter and the retained cell mass was then dried at 103 °C for 30 min, cooled, and the weight change was used to calculate TSS. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay with bovine serum albumin as the standard.

Results

Growth of B. vietnamiensis G4 on BA

In batch cultures, growth on BA (250 mg/L) involved a lag phase of ~ 3 days followed by the rapid consumption of BA over the following 2–3 days (Fig. S1). The decrease in BA concentration was accompanied by a concurrent increase in culture cell density final cell density (~ OD600 of 0.45) and no further increases were observed after the BA had been fully consumed. These results suggest that B. vietnamiensis G4 grows directly on BA. The consumption of BA and increases in culture density were fully inhibited when propyne (1.7% v/v gas phase), a potent inactivator of T2MO activity in B. vietnamiensis G4 (Yeager et al. 1999), was added to the reactor headspace. The results indicate that T2MO acts directly on BA as a substrate to initiate BA catabolism in B. vietnamiensis G4.

The specific rate of TCE transformation by BA-grown cells (2.18 µmol/day/mg TSS) was ~ 25% greater than toluene-grown cells (1.75 µmol/day/mg TSS). Activity-based labeling

We have previously used 1,7-octadiyne (17OD) as a probe for fluorescent activity-based protein labeling of several bacterial monooxygenases (Bennett et al. 2016; Oyarzun and Hyman 2023). To investigate the potential use of 17OD as a probe to determine levels of T2MO expression in intact cells of B. vietnamiensis G4, we initially examined the specificity of 17OD by comparing substrate specific rates of O2 uptake for cells grown on either toluene or lactate. Toluene-grown cells exhibited high rates of toluene-, o-cresol-, and 3-methylcatechol-dependent O2 uptake activity (250–300 nmoles min mg protein) while these activities were all minimal (≤ 30 nmoles min mg protein) in cells grown on lactate (Fig. 2). In contrast, for toluene-grown cells treated with 17OD, both toluene- and o-cresol-dependent O2 uptake activities were strongly inhibited (≤ 90%), but there was a minimal effect of 17OD exposure on 3-methylcatechol-dependent O2 uptake activity. These results are compatible with a specific effect of 17OD on T2MO as this enzyme is typically only highly expressed in cells grown on aromatic substrates. Moreover, during toluene catabolism, this enzyme catalyzes both the initial oxidation of toluene to o-cresol and the further oxidation of o-cresol to 3-methylcatechol while other enzymes are responsible for the further oxidation of 3-methylcatechol. Pathway of BA catabolism

The time course growth of B. vietnamiensis G4 on BA described earlier (Fig. S1) indicated no BA consumption occurred in inoculated cultures containing BA and propyne, a putative mechanism-based inactivator of T2MO (Yeager et al. 1999). The role for T2MO in BA catabolism was further explored by examining the effects of propyne on growth of B. vietnamiensis G4 on benzyl esters. In cultures grown on BBu in the absence of propyne, there was an initial lag phase of ~ 1 day before increases in culture density were observed (Fig. 4). Over the following 5 days, culture density progressively increased and reached a stable maximum of ~ 0.40 after 6 days. During growth on BBu alone, BA accumulated in the culture medium and reached a maximum concentration of ~ 30 mg/L after 2 days. The concentration of BA then progressively decreased and was no longer detected in the culture medium when further changes in the culture density ceased after 7 days. In the cultures containing both BBu and propyne, the time course of changes in culture density (OD600) were similar to the cultures containing BBu alone except the final stable culture density obtained after 6 days of ~ 0.30 was ~ 25% lower. In the presence of propyne, the maximal concentration of BA (~ 50 mg/L) was also detected after ~ 2 days but remained stable for the remainder of the incubation. The concentration of BA that accumulated is in the range of 42 mg/L expected from the hydrolysis of 100 mg/L of BBU that was added to the batch reactors. Although the concentrations of BBu and butyrate were not determined, our results suggest BBu is initially hydrolyzed to a mixture of butyrate and BA. The pathway of BA catabolism in B. vietnamiensis G4 has not been previously established but was investigated by measuring substrate specific rates of O2 uptake for cells grown on a variety of different substrates. Cells grown on substrates that potentially (BA, BAc, BBu) or are known (toluene, phenol) to require T2MO activity all exhibited similar levels and ratios of toluene-, o-cresol-, and 3-methylcatachol-dependent O2 uptake (Fig. 6). The rates of O2 uptake exhibited by cells grown on acetate, butyrate, or lactate were low and none of the toluene-associated substrates stimulated detectable O2 uptake activity in cells grown on these acids. Low rates of O2 uptake activity were stimulated by benzaldehyde in cells grown on aromatics or esters and similarly low rates were also observed with BA as a substrate. In contrast, much higher rates of O2 uptake were consistently stimulated by 2-hydroxybenzyl alcohol. Acetate and butyrate also strongly stimulated O2 uptake in cells previously grown on BAc and BBu while these activities were substantially lower in cells grown on toluene or phenol. Discussion

Our results demonstrate that BA, and two benzyl esters (BBu and BAc) that can hydrolyze to yield BA, are novel growth substrates for B. vietnamiensis G4. Growth on these compounds consistently led to the expression of T2MO, as demonstrated by alkyne sensitive chlorinated co-substrate transformation assays and ABL of the subunit of the hydroxylase component of T2MO. The pathway of BA metabolism appears to involve a T2MO-catalyzed oxidation of BA with 2-hydroxy benzyl alcohol as a potential initial metabolite. Growth on BA and benzyl esters increased the transformation capacity for chlorinated co-substrates for T2MO. The low human toxicity of BA and benzyl esters suggests these compounds may be useful for promoting in situ cometabolic transformation of TCE and other chlorinated compounds as well as in novel cometabolic processes involving encapsulated cells.

The ability of B. vietnamiensis G4 to grow on BA and simple aromatic esters that can hydrolyze to yield BA has not been previously reported. The aromatic substrates utilized by this strain include toluene, phenol, o-, m-, p-cresol, and benzoate but none of these compounds are catabolized via BA as an intermediate. The genome of this strain consists of three chromosomes and five plasmids (Maida et al. 2014). Among these, the large, self-transmissible TOM plasmid encodes all of the enzymes required for growth on toluene (Shields et al. 1995). These enzymes include T2MO which catalyzes the two initial steps in toluene catabolism that generate 3-methylcatechol. Further oxidation of 3-methylcatechol is independent of T2MO activity and involves the meta cleavage pathway. Among the diverse microbial toluene-oxidizing pathways, BA is best known as the immediate product of toluene oxidation by xylene monooxygenase in strains such as P. putida mt-2 that utilizes the TOL plasmid-encoded pathway of toluene catabolism (Parales et al. 2008). Although xylene monooxygenase can oxidize BA (Bühler et al. 2000), it is normally sequentially oxidized to benzaldehyde and benzoic acid by dehydrogenases. Acinetobacter sp. strain ADP1 has also been reported to grow on several benzyl esters including BA, BBu, and benzyl propionate (Jones and Williams 2001). In these cases, the esters are initially hydrolyzed by an esterase and, like P. putida mt-2, the resulting BA is sequentially oxidized to benzaldehyde and benzoic acid by dehydrogenases (Jones and Williams 2001; Jones et al. 1999).

The results in this study build on earlier observations of TCE oxidation by B. vietnamiensis G4 through the activity of T2MO (Nelson et al. 1987; Folsom et al. 1990) and that T2MO is irreversibly inactivated by diverse alkynes (Yeager et al. 1999). Analyses of chlorinated hydrocarbon transformation rates and alkyne-based activity-based fluorescent labeling of T2MO components demonstrate that growth of B. vietnamiensis G4 on BA and BA-generating esters leads to high levels of expression of T2MO.

When grown on substrates including lactate, acetate, or butyrate, B. vietnamiensis G4 exhibited minimal rates of TCE oxidation while high rates of oxidation were observed for cells grown on toluene, phenol, BA, or the BA-generating esters, BAc and BBu (Table 1). Cells grown on these diverse aromatic substrates also oxidized other chlorinated alkanes examined (VC, 1,1-DCE, and cis-DCE) (Table 2). The role of T2MO in the transformation of TCE and other chlorinated ethenes has previously been established for both the native purified and heterologously expressed forms of this enzyme (Newman and Wackett 1997; Shim and Wood 2000). The rates of TCE transformation determined in this study agree well with those previously reported by Folsom et al. (1990) for phenol grown cells and toluene grown cells (Leahy et al. 1996) with toluene grown cells. In contrast, our Tc estimates are ~ twofold lower than those previously reported by Yeager et al. (2001a, b) which may reflect the earlier estimates were derived from short-term incubations (4 h) using high initial TCE concentrations (≤ 349 µM) whereas the present estimates were derived from longer-term incubations that used lower maximal TCE concentrations.

Our ABL studies also confirm that T2MO is highly expressed in cells grown on BA and BA-generating esters and that this is an effective method for detecting and quantifying the levels of catalytically active T2MO in cells. If the specific rates of TCE oxidation (Table 1) provide an estimate of the amount of T2MO in cells, our results comparing TCE transformation rates and labeling intensity for cells grown on different substrates (Fig. 3) suggest there is a close agreement between the two methods. However, as both approaches require T2MO activity, there may be other factors such as substrate-dependent variations in T2MO subunit ratios (e.g. hydroxylase vs regulatory protein ratios) that influence these measurements and lead to the apparent discrepancies we observed for cells grown on BAc versus other substrates (Fig. 3).

Although this study did not aim to characterize the pathway of BA catabolism in B. vietnamiensis G4, three lines of evidence support the conclusion that BA is a substrate for T2MO. First, in cells grown on BBu, the production of BA was observed in cultures grown both in the presence and absence of the T2MO-selective inactivator, propyne (Fig. 4). This observation is compatible with an initial hydrolysis of BBu to yield BA and butyrate. In the absence of propyne, the BA generated from BBu hydrolysis was consumed while in the presence of propyne, BA accumulated to concentrations expected from the hydrolysis of BBu and was not further consumed. Our transformation assays (Table 1) and activity-based labeling studies (Fig. 3) both indicate T2MO is expressed in BBu-grown cells. The lower overall biomass production and the stable accumulation of BA in the propyne-containing cultures suggest growth was only supported by T2MO-independent butyrate metabolism.

Second, our observation that BA, like toluene, inhibits T2MO-dependent TCE transformation is shown in Fig. 5. Further studies will be required to better define the kinetics of this interaction but in the resting cell assays used in this study, the inhibitory effect of BA on the rate of TCE transformation are weaker than those observed with toluene. Third, we observed that 2-hydroxybenzyl alcohol, a potential hydroxylation product of BA oxidation, stimulated O2 uptake activity in cells grown on aromatic substates to levels comparable to those observed with known intermediates in toluene catabolism including o-cresol and 3-methyl catechol (Fig. 6). This stimulation was not observed with another potential BA metabolite, benzaldehyde. Further investigation is required to determine if BA is oxidized to one or more hydroxybenzyl alcohols but as noted earlier, T2MO is already known to catalyze two sequential aryl ring hydroxylation reactions during toluene catabolism. Heterologously-expressed T2MO can also sequentially oxidize benzene to phenol, catechol, and 1,2,3-trihydroxybenzene (Tao et al. 2004). Further studies explicitly focused on determining the pathway of BA catabolism such as metabolite analyses and use of knock out mutants are needed to further explore our observations and to verify the conclusions we have drawn here.

Our results indicate that that the Tc values for BA-grown cells for TCE, VC, cis-DCE, and 1,1-DCE were between 34 and 150% higher than for toluene-grown cells (Table 2) and the Tc value for TCE for cells grown on BBu was over threefold higher than for toluene-grown cells (Table 1). The Tc value provides a comparison of the amount of co-substrate a specific cell type can transform but multiple factors including, but not limited to, the monooxygenase expression level of the cells, the toxicity of the co-substrate, and the level of endogenous storage compounds can impact these values. Based on specific transformation activities (Tables 1 and 2) and the results of our ABL reactions (Fig. 3), the increases in Tc we observed are not due to increases in the amount of active T2MO in cells. Compared to other TCE-cometabolizing strains, B. vietnamiensis G4 is relatively insensitive to toxic effects that arise during cometabolic TCE oxidation (Yeager et al. 2001a, b a, b). A more likely, although currently speculative explanation for the increases in Tc values is that growth of B. vietnamiensis G4 on BA and BA-generating esters increases the amounts of internal storage compounds, such as polyhydroxyalkonoates (PHAs) relative to cells grown on toluene. A clear role for PHAs in increasing Tc values for TCE transformation has previously been established for methanotrophs (Alvarez-Cohen and McCarty 1991). The possibility that the same effect occurs with B. vietnamiensis G4 warrants further study as Burkholderia strains frequently accumulate PHAs (Pan et al. 2012; Ramsay et al. 1989). Moreover, for energetic reasons, butyrate is often an effective growth substrate for PHA accumulating strains (Shi et al. 1997; Marang et al. 2013), and our results indicate the highest Tc values for TCE transformation were obtained for cells grown on BBu (Table 1). Future studies should measure PHA accumulation in cells grown on BA or BBU compared to toluene. Studies are also needed to determine if there is a relationship to slower rates of transformation being associated with high transformation capacities as we reported for cells grown on BAc.

A final important issue raised by this study is the potential use of BA and BA-generating esters for supporting in situ bioremediation of chlorinated solvents such as TCE. One attractive feature is that BA and BA-generating esters are “generally regarded as safe” (GRAS) compounds that are widely used in the food industry. Although this GRAS classification reflects the concentrations of these compounds used in food preparation and preservation, these compounds could potentially be useful as in situ stimulants for cometabolic transformation of compounds such as TCE through the activity of organisms such as B. vietnamiensis G4. Future research should examine whether the effects we have characterized in this study are specific for B. vietnamiensis G4 or whether TCE-cometabolizing activities of other toluene-oxidizing strains with different toluene-oxidizing oxygenases such as P. mendocina KR1 (T4MO), R. pickettii PK01 (T3MO), and P. putida F1 (toluene dioxygenase [TDO]) can also be supported by BA and BA-generating esters.

Future studies need to determine if native microorganisms stimulated in groundwater and soil microcosms fed BA and BA-generating esters can cometabolize TCE and other chlorinated ethenes, or whether bioaugmentation with B. vietnamiensis G4 grown on BBU that has a transformation capacity three times greater than cells grown on toluene has potential to promote in situ treatment. An alternative potential in situ application that would avoid the competitive consumption of BA and BA-generating esters by non-contaminant degrading microorganisms would be to use these compounds as slow-release substrates (SRCs) in systems in which specific microorganisms are encapsulated with these compounds. Our past research has demonstrated that Rhodococcus rhodochrous ATCC 21198 co-encapsulated with SRCs can sustainably oxidize several different important ground water contaminants (Rasmussen et al. 2020).
