Improving non-invasive glucose estimation with monthly calibrated photoplethysmography and implicit HbA1c
Authors: Justin Chu, Tung-Han Hsieh, Yao-Ting Chang, Shien-Kuei Liaw, Fu-Liang Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMost noninvasive blood glucose technologies, especially wearable photoplethysmography devices, require multiple calibrations and are often limited to narrow cohorts such as unmedicated or mild cases. We assess whether a single pretest once per month can meet clinical accuracy while broadening applicability through cohort-specific models.MethodsWe develop models for three groups: (i) individuals not using antidiabetic drugs, (ii) those using oral antidiabetic drugs only, and (iii) those using antidiabetic drugs in combination with other medications. Models are trained on cohort data with and without the monthly pretest and are then applied directly to personal testing without retraining. Inputs include dual-channel photoplethysmography signals and an inferred HbA1c (glycated hemoglobin) feature. Accuracy is summarized by mean absolute relative difference, clinical safety by the Parkes Error Grid, and improvements by a nonparametric rank-sum test.ResultsHere, we show that the best models using a single monthly pretest achieve mean absolute relative differences of 9.59, 12.23, and 16.40% for groups (i), (ii), and (iii), respectively. In the most complex group (iii), prediction errors are significantly lower than our earlier work according to the rank-sum test. The single-pretest models produce no clinically unacceptable readings on the Parkes Error Grid, likely due to dual-channel input and the inferred glycated-hemoglobin feature.ConclusionsA single monthly pretest enables accurate and clinically safe noninvasive glucose measurement across diverse patient groups. The approach operates without retraining or fine-tuning and can adapt to new users and devices through edge computing, supporting integration into current wearables for everyday diabetes management and public-health prevention.

Introduction

Diabetes is a chronic and metabolic disorder characterized by elevated blood glucose levels (BGL), resulting from either a deficiency in insulin production or impaired insulin response. Without proper insulin function, glucose accumulates in the bloodstream, leading to various health complications and potentially life-threatening consequences1.

Individuals with type 1 diabetes require lifelong insulin therapy. Type 2 diabetes, on the other hand, is more common and often linked to genetic and lifestyle factors, such as obesity and physical inactivity. For both types of diabetes, blood glucose (BG) monitoring is essential for effective disease management. Regular monitoring helps individuals with diabetes understand how their bodies respond to different factors such as food, exercise, and medication. Traditionally, blood glucose monitoring involves pricking the finger and using a glucose meter to measure BGLs. While this invasive method provides accurate results, it can be inconvenient, painful, and carries a risk of infection2. These downsides inspire the need for non- or minimally invasive blood glucose measurement methods. However, there have been limited in-depth studies on the methodology of NIBG measurement using datasets specifically focused on participants with diabetes or dedicated exclusively to diabetes patients. Thus, we exclusively use diabetes patients without prediabetes or healthy participants for this work.

For decades, there has been a growing interest in non- or minimally invasive blood glucose measurement methods. These technologies aim to measure BGLs without requiring frequent finger pricking. While the minimally invasive methods have had significant breakthroughs in recent years—especially FDA-approved continuous glucose monitors (CGMs)—these devices provide a promising indicator for diagnosis and tracking of diabetes progression3. The CGMs have the advantages of providing real-time data trend and the ability to alert the user of hypoglycemia events, which could significantly improve the quality of life for Type-1 diabetes or other patients needing intensive monitoring of their BGL. In contrast to CGMs, the non-invasive glucose measuring technique is still very much in a work-in-progress state, with no FDA-approved product in sight4. As Aminah Hina notes, various non-invasive approaches have been explored and can generally be categorized into three categories: enzymatic, transdermal, and electromagnetic wave sensing5. Enzymatic methods analyze the user’s bodily fluids, such as sweat, saliva, and tears, to measure the fluid’s glucose level and their correlation with the BGL6–8. Transdermal methods measure electrical bioimpedance based on the principle that blood glucose concentration affects one’s blood electrical properties9. Electromagnetic wave sensing methods encompass a variety of systems that utilize non-ionizing electromagnetic radiations, which cover a broad range of the electromagnetic spectrum10–13. A more recent review by Di Filippo, Daria et al. further categorized these methodologies into four main groups, and each with various unique sub-branches: optical, nanotechnology, electric/electromagnetic, and physiological14. The variety of methodologies based on different technologies and approaches shows the immense interest in solving this problem. A comprehensive review paper by Zanelli et al. included a figure showing a noticeable uptick in the number of research papers published on related topics since 201115. This suggests that research on NIBG measurement has experienced a surge of interest, likely driven by advancements in artificial intelligence over the past decade. Noninvasive blood glucose measurement techniques have the potential to provide more convenient and painless monitoring, which could significantly enhance users’ adherence to regular monitoring routines. However, developing accurate and reliable noninvasive methods remains challenging. Factors such as variation in skin properties, interference from other substances, and individual differences make it difficult to achieve consistent and accurate results.

Among the various different approaches to pursue non-invasive blood glucose (NIBG) measurement, the optical photoplethysmography (PPG) based systems are considered the most convenient, affordable, and widely available method16. PPG devices consist of a light emission source and a photodetector. The photodetector measures the changes in transmissive or reflective power from the light source caused by the variations in blood volume flow through peripheral capillaries.

While PPG-based NIBG measurement technologies have shown promise, the “holy grail” of the NIBG measurement has yet to be achieved. To be viable, an NIBG meter must meet the accuracy standard of medical devices and remain simple enough for practical use. However, this requirement remains unmet, largely due to errors induced by individual deviation. This problem can be resolved in a PPG-based system either through precision medicine by incorporating detailed features to represent individual characteristics, such as using a personally calibrated model, to address the individual deviations that hinder the prediction performance of models. Precision medicine is sometimes referred to as personalized medicine. It involves tailoring disease prevention and treatment to account for differences in individuals’ genes, environments, and lifestyles17. However, a personalized model often requires a dozen or more measurements to construct and achieve acceptable performance, which leads to inconvenience in use.

Previous NIBG studies using PPG technology-based methodology that uses a general model treat each measurement as an independent sporadic event, like an occasional finger-prick BGL test18–23. In contrast, research on using personalized models primarily focuses on continuous PPG measurements, like how CGMs collect data13,24. However, neither approach effectively aligns with the goal of long-term fasting blood glucose monitoring, where users measure their BG levels regularly but not continuously. A continuous NIBG monitoring device may provide the user with real-time changes in BGL and daily trends, but wearing the sensor 24/7 can lead to discomfort. While many studies introduced novel algorithms for leveling BG, considerable effort was also directed toward improving the measurement hardware for NIBG measurement25–29.

Unlike precision medicine therapy designed for a specific course of treatment, managing diabetes is a lifelong endeavor. While applying the concept of precision medicine to provide optimal performance of NIBG measurement, the repetitive measurement process required to obtain a usable personalized model may deter many potential users. Therefore, it is not only important but essential to strike a balance between using a generalized and a personalized approach to NIBG measurement, minimizing user effort while still achieving the benefits of personalization.

Here, we show that using one brief pretest each month with the generalized model effectively reduces the number of required pretests from twelve to one. The proposed model achieves 98.7, 87.9, and 78.3% of predictions in zone A of the Clarke Error Grid (CEG) and 98.7, 92.4, and 90.4% of predictions in zone A of the Parkes Error Grid (PEG) within 30 days of the pretest measurement for the three cohorts of diabetes who are not taking antidiabetic drugs (ADD), take oral ADD only, and take oral ADD combined with other drugs, respectively. The three cohorts also achieve mean absolute relative difference (MARD) of 9.59%, 12.23%, and 16.40% respectively. Together, these results indicate that the approach provides accurate and clinically safe noninvasive glucose estimation while substantially reducing user effort. This monthly calibration technology can be easily integrated into the current wearable devices’ ecosystem across a broad population spectrum for diabetes prognosis control or public health prevention.

In this work, we present an ameliorated application of the personalized deduction learning (DL) model in a generalized manner. This approach utilizes PPG signals alone, without other data inputs of personal profile and/or other sensors, to estimate fasting blood glucose concentration. The original DL model was developed as a fully personalized NIBG measurement solution with high accuracy, tested across a wide range of time intervals, up to 85 days30. While the DL approach demonstrates excellent performance, its fully personalized nature requires 12 rounds of pretest data for model training. This significant inconvenience limits its practicality and usability in broader applications.

The DL model utilizes accumulated comparisons of paired PPG signals as input to deduce changes in BGLs. The model structure follows the principle of our domain theory that changes in the signals must correlate with changes in BGLs. Thus, the model is designed and trained completely on the changes in PPG waveform and the corresponding change in BGL through a pairing mechanism that groups PPG waveform data from different time points for each individual. The model demonstrated a 20% improvement over conventional methods that did not incorporate the pairing mechanism between rounds of PPG measurements. With 12 rounds of training data, the DL model with automatic screening achieved a correlation coefficient of 0.81, an accuracy score of 93.5%, a root mean squared error (RMSE) of 13.93 mg/dL, a mean absolute error (MAE) of 12.07 mg/dL, and 100% predictions in zone A of the CEG30.

In a practical and precision-medicine-like scenario, we consider the boundary condition in which users are required to take only a single pretest before undergoing further formal testing. We apply the pairing mechanism from the DL model to effectively leverage pretest data from participants with multiple rounds of measurements. Our previous study also highlighted the negative impact that medications can have on NIBG measurement performance31. Therefore, in this work, the participants are divided into three different cohorts based on their antidiabetic drug usage, as illustrated in Fig. 1a, to evaluate their performance and better understand the effects of ADDs on our NIBG model. For users, the ideal outcome would be only a single pretest with PPG and a finger-prick BGL is needed, without any additional tests, to obtain accurate fasting blood glucose readings within 30 days.

Data source

The original dataset comprised 2632 records of data, with each record consisting of two measurements. The measurements are collected through over 20 different district healthcare centers across Taipei and Taoyuan City in Taiwan. A total of 598 measurements were extracted from the database. These 598 measurements were obtained from several different diabetes subjects (HbA1c > 6.5 % or taking ADDs) who had come back for multiple collection sessions, resulting in multiple rounds of data, allowing for the formation of pairs. For each subject, their consecutive rounds of data were sequentially grouped together to form pairs. From this subset, 974 unique pairs were created. Within each pair, the measurements are referred to as pretest and test rounds, respectively, based on their order. Schematic illustration of the data structure and how data are paired is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1a, b. The paired dataset was then sorted into three cohorts based on the subject’s ADD usage: the first cohort consists of subjects not taking any ADD (ADDNone), the second cohort includes subjects taking oral ADD only (ADDOral), and the third cohort comprises subjects taking ADD in combination with other medications (ADDMix). The dataset monolithically includes the Taiwanese (East-Asian) population, which falls within Type 3-Type 4 of the Fitzpatrick scale. The demographics of the three cohorts are presented in Table 1. The demographic data for Table 1 is included in Supplementary Data 1. Following a significant Kruskal–Wallis test, Dunn’s post-hoc test was used for no assumption on data normality or equal variances to conduct pairwise comparison between groups. The collection of samples used in this study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Academia Sinica, Taiwan (Application No: AS-IRB01-16081), and all procedures adhered to relevant guidelines and regulations. Following Taiwan IRB guidance and approved procedure, all subjects were fully informed about the study and provided written consent for the collection of data and its use in the research before the measurements. Participants are compensated with $200NTD (roughly USD 6.65) for each round of data collection. PPG signals were recorded with each measurement consisting of 60 s of PPG data at a 500 Hz sampling rate and later down-sampled to 250 Hz in actual use. Two measurements were taken for each record to ensure data validity and consistency. PPG signals were recorded using the TI AFE4490 Integrated Analog Front-End for oximeter, while invasive glucose values were obtained using the Roche Accu-Chek Mobile. Together with the invasive HbA1c was measured using Siemens DCA Vantage Analyzer. Blood pressure measurements were taken immediately before each PPG measurement by a cuff-based Omron model HEM-7320 automatic sphygmomanometer. Before all measurements, participants were asked to sit for at least five minutes, complete a brief questionnaire, and sign a written informed consent form. All measurements were conducted while the participants were in a relaxed sitting posture. For a detailed description of the experimental setup and procedure, please refer to our previous work32.

The data preprocessing of the PPG signal involves several steps. During pulse segmentation, 400 data points (equivalent to 1.6 s) are extracted backwardly from each pulse valley, forming a signal window. The pairing mechanism between two measurements is implemented by randomly pairing the segmented pulse windows one-to-one between the two measurements. In addition to this pairing process, several commonly used features, such as heart rate variation (HRV) and morphological features, are also extracted from the PPG signal and compiled into a feature vector to accompany the segmented pulse signal33,34. The HRV features consist of low-frequency power, high-frequency power, total power, standard deviation of the pulse interval difference, and the percentage of pulse interval differences exceeding 20 ms (pNN20). The morphological features include the width of the pulse at half height, the valley-to-peak time interval, the sum of the pulse areas (within the minute), the average of the pulse areas, the median of the pulse areas, and the pulse area of the window. Methods

In this study, we conducted comparisons on four different configurations. We first established a model that does not utilize any pretest (prior measurements for calibration) as a baseline. We also showcase the performance of the previously proposed implicit HbA1c method35, which employs an adjusted estimate of HbA1c value. The implicit HbA1c is derived by reversely applying a HbA1c-based fasting blood glucose estimation model31. The HbA1c model was trained with the subjects who were not taking any medication. The model learns the PPG waveform data and measured HbA1c value on their correlation to fasting BGL. The implicit HbA1c method then uses a single pretest data, including finger-prick blood glucose measurement and PPG signal, to conduct backcalculation on the HbA1c model to find the suitable implicit HbA1c. At the same time, we applied the DL model we published previously30, which is a solely personalized model, on the dataset of other similar subjects within the paired dataset to achieve generalization during model training. DL models utilizing two different rounds of measurements for paired 2-channel simultaneous input to achieve comparison-based model reasoning. This allowed us to compare the effects of 2-channel input and having a calibration round. Lastly, in the proposed methodology, we enhanced the generalized DL model by incorporating the aforementioned implicit HbA1c value, which has shown promising effects in addressing personal variations. This would showcase the combined effectiveness of adding implicit HbA1c while using a 2-channel input model structure. The comparison of the inclusion of these key enablers for each model is summarized in Table 2. The generalized DL model in a different application has also shown promising results while being applied to blood pressure estimation36. Detailed DL model structures and descriptions of each method’s workflow are presented in Figs. 2 and 3. A graphical presentation of the relation of the aforementioned previously published methods is presented in Supplementary Fig. 2. Figure 2a shows the schematic illustration of a simple HbA1c-gated baseline method. Similar to most traditional single-input feedforward deep learning models, it takes the PPG signal and feature vector, including the 11 HRV and morphological features plus heart rate and age, as input and sequentially passes them through multiple layers of a deep neural network to output a single prediction result. The training data are separated into two groups based on the HbA1c value of 7.5%, producing two different prediction models with the same structure. The test round signal is directly fed into the neural network for prediction, and the user selects the appropriate model based on their most recent HbA1c value. In this study, we simulate the scenario where the user has access to a recent HbA1c reading and can select the correct model, leading to a slightly better BGL estimation. This method is used as the baseline for representing a fully generalized (no pretest required) approach for assessing the difference in performance.

Figure 2b shows the schematic illustration of the previously proposed implicit HbA1c method, which requires a pretest round to assist with the testing round. The pretest measurement of PPG is taken alongside a corresponding finger-prick measurement. The pretest round is first used to retrieve an adjusted implicit HbA1c value using backcalculation with the PPG signal and finger-prick measured BGL. In an actual testing scenario, the implicit HbA1c is then applied with the test round PPG signal to complete the BGL prediction. The implicit HbA1c method relies on a single generalized neural network model trained with true HbA1c values. The HbA1c model takes PPG signals and HbA1c values as input and predicts its corresponding fasting BGL. When conducting the backcalculation procedure on pretest data, the pretest’s PPG signal is paired with different HbA1c values to get a varying range of fasting BGL estimation. The HbA1c value that resulted in the most accurate prediction is designated as the implicit HbA1c. The detailed model structure with parameters and methodology explanation can be found in our previous work published in Sensors and Bioengineering31,35, and the schematic of the model structure is also included in Supplementary Fig. 3. In this work, we directly used the already trained model from our previous study and applied it to the DLimp.H method, and also direct comparison for benchmarking.

Figure 2c shows the schematic illustration of the proposed DL method. The DLimp.H method uses a paired signal input of the pretest and test rounds, along with the pretest’s reference BGL and the pretest-determined implicit HbA1c from Fig. 2b, to enable the single pretest personalized DL model to deliver its BGL prediction. The models process the signal data from both the pretest and test simultaneously through its 2-channel input, where the test and pretest rounds of signals are packaged together. Compared to the implicit HbA1c methodology, which sequentially uses the paired dataset, this approach utilizes the pretest data before the testing data. The paired signals are input in a 2-channel configuration and processed by the neural network to generate a prediction based on their differences and similarities. The 1D-CNN takes in the 2-channel signal input in a manner similar to how a 2D-CNN handles a colored image input with 3 channels of RGB (red, green, and blue). In an actual use case scenario, the pretest can be repeatedly used with new testing measurements in pairs to conduct prediction until a new calibration is needed. New calibration data can simply swap out the old pretest data without any further fine-tuning and training of the prediction model.

The DLimp.H model structure, presented in Fig. 3, consists of 4 convolutional neural network (CNN) blocks followed by 2 dense blocks. For our baseline methods, a similar model structure is used, with the key difference being that the baseline methods have only one signal input and use more manually extracted features to assess the effectiveness of having a 2-channel input. For the DL model, the implicit HbA1c is simply removed from DLimp.H method to assess the effectiveness of adding implicit HbA1c. For both implicit HbA1c and DLimp.H method, the real HbA1c values were used during model training, while implicit HbA1c values were applied instead for testing. In contrast, the baseline model does not incorporate additional information on its convolution output before the data enters the dense blocks.

While the implicit HbA1c method directly applied the previously trained model, the training and testing for baseline, DL, DLimp.H models were conducted independently for each cohort. All methods were tested using identical datasets. For cohorts 1 and 2, the models were trained with data within the same cohort. For cohort 3, the model was trained using a combination of data from cohorts 1 and 2 due to the messiness of the data in cohort 3. While using data from cohort 3 exclusively for training, the model struggles to learn. We suspect it is due to the complex interference on biometric features from the cohort’s mixed medications resulted in conflicting information for the model. While using cohorts 1 and 2, the model was at least able to learn the proper and normal correlation between BGL and biometric features. For cohorts 1 and 2, the data were cycled through in a subject-based leave-one-out cross-validation manner to select the training and testing data. The test results from each subject were later compiled together to present as the final result due to the limited amount of available data. In each cycle, one test subject is designated as the testing subject, while the remaining subjects form the training data set. It is important to note that in each cycle, none of the test subjects’ data is utilized in any form during the model training phase. Supplementary Fig. 1c illustrates what the data composition would look like in each iteration. In the proposed methodology, the pretest data served as a foundation signal for the paired input comparison of the DL’s model after the model had been trained. Compared to the previous highly personalized configuration of the DL model, this proposed approach eliminates the need for multiple rounds of data from each individual to build the initial model and removes the requirement for retraining after each pretest. When new pretest data are required, the new measurement can simply replace the old pretest data without necessitating further modifications to the model.

Since the models were applied with a varied amount of 1.6 s of windows extracted from each 60-s measurement, each window generates an individual output value, forming a distribution of results. The median value of this distribution is selected to represent the prediction result for the measurement, while the standard deviation of the output values serves as a screening criterion to assess the validity of the prediction. If the standard deviation of the prediction result distribution within the minute exceeds 20% of the selected median, the result is deemed not consistent enough to be a valid and confident result. The high inconsistency between the prediction results within the 60-s measurement would be due to sub-ideal PPG signal quality and will prompt the user to retake the PPG measurement during practical use.

Statistics and reproducibility

All analyses were performed on a diabetes-only dataset that was paired into pretest–test pairs and partitioned into three medication-defined cohorts (Cohort 1–3). Tests are conducted using the scipy module version 1.9.1 on Python 3.9. Sample sizes were as follows: Cohort 1 (17 participants, 65 records, 182 pairs), Cohort 2 (10 participants, 67 records, 220 pairs), and Cohort 3 (20 participants, 167 records, 572 pairs). From the source database, 598 measurements from individuals with diabetes were selected and paired to form 974 unique pretest–test pairs.

For model evaluation, Cohorts 1 and 2 used subject-level leave-one-out cross-validation to avoid information leakage; for Cohort 3, models were trained on data from Cohorts 1–2 due to data complexity in Cohort 3. All methods were evaluated on identical testing datasets. Pretest–test intervals were grouped into 0–30 days and 30–90 days; pairs with intervals >90 days were excluded, and additional pairs were removed by a predefined consistency screen.

Replicates were defined at three levels: (i) technical replicates—multiple 1.6-s windows extracted from each 60-s PPG measurement, with the median window output reported as the measurement-level estimate and results were excluded if the within-measurement standard deviation exceeded 20% of the median; (ii) measurement replicates—two measurements per record to ensure consistency; and (iii) paired replicates—each measurement is paired with the other two measurements in the target record to form pretest–test rounds.

The primary performance metric was the distribution of estimates across zones of the Parkes error grid for Type-2 diabetes; secondary metrics included the Clarke error grid, accuracy score (RA), MAE, MARD, RMSE, and Pearson’s correlation. Normality was assessed using two-sided Shapiro–Wilk test. Between-method differences in absolute percentage error distributions were evaluated with two-sided Mann–Whitney U tests. Kruskal–Wallis H-test is performed on demographics for each cohort. Significant results were followed by two-sided Dunn’s post-hoc tests, with adjustment for multiple comparisons; exact p values are reported in the Supplementary Tables 2, 3, and 4.

Results

In this study, we used the PEG for Type-2 diabetes for analysis as the primary metric for presenting the model performance, while several other indices, including CEG, were also provided for easier comparison with previous works using the same metrics. The error grids are a graphical tool designed to evaluate the clinical accuracy of blood glucose monitoring systems37,38. The error grids categorize prediction results into five zones based on their clinical significance. Zone A represents the clinically accurate prediction results that can lead to proper and safe treatment decisions. Zone B includes the clinically acceptable prediction results that may not lead to optimal treatments, but are unlikely to cause significant harm. Zones C, D, and E, however, represent clinically unacceptable and potentially dangerous predictions, as they could lead to inaction or inappropriate treatment, thereby endangering the user.

In recognition of HbA1c’s ability to represent average BGLs for up to 90 days, this study utilizes paired data with intervals between them within the same 90-day timeframe. Due to the limited amount of data available, results are arranged into two sections of having 0 ~ 30 days and 30 ~ 90 days intervals between the data forming a pair. This distribution reflects the experimental design, wherein measurements were typically conducted at two- to three-week intervals. As a result, the 30-day boundary was chosen as an arbitrary yet practical division to facilitate the presentation of model performance.

Discussion

The DL model utilizes the paired input from the same subject to learn the differences and similarities between two signal segments and associate them with the change in BGL. In contrast, conventional models, which analyze each signal individually, attempt to associate the features of each signal with a reference BGL. This difference may allow the DL model to better form a regression-like model by learning what kinds of changes in the waveform would translate to how much difference in BGL. Whereas the conventional single-channel models approach the problem like a multi-class classification task during training, attempting to memorize some unique patterns for each sample. As shown in Fig. 1, there can be many vastly different waveforms for the same BGL. Since the shape and pattern of a PPG signal waveform are primarily influenced by individual biometric identity rather than BGL, the model could end up leveling the BGL by recognizing the test subject. With a larger sample size, there may be cases of different BGLs having waveforms with similar characteristics. These cases could easily lead to false predictions in conventional single-channel models with a classification-like behavior, as the model might be misled by similar waveform features.

Leveling BGL by assessing the changes from a provided pretest data provides the DL model with a better ability to determine unfamiliar signals accurately. Fundamentally, the PPG signal itself is a relative change in blood volume at the measured site. It is hard to give a definitive value on something that is measured relatively and without a reference point. DL methodology incorporating implicit HbA1c not only provided the relative difference between two signals by using a two-dimensional input but also provided the needed reference point of the pretest’s BGL and an adjusted estimation of HbA1c. This allows the model to better learn the difference between signals and associate it with the change in BGL during training to deduce the answer.

On one hand, comparing two signals from the same subject also removes the need to account for the variations in individual biometric identities in the PPG waveform, resulting in significantly fewer variables. On the other hand, the estimated implicit HbA1c from a previous HbA1c model serves as a bridge to level inter-personal differences between the subject and the generalized model. Implicit HbA1c values are derived from a true measured HbA1c value-based model using finger-prick BGL measurements from a pool of healthy subjects not affected by drugs. The process involves determining what the model predicts the subject’s HbA1c value to be based on the current PPG waveform and fasting BGL. In doing so, inter-personal differences that are not well documented or incorporated in the HbA1c model’s training are adjusted. Using this implicit HbA1c in our generalized DL model helps further provide a common basis to bridge the differences between training and testing subjects. Combining these effects, we can leverage the data from the general population to compensate for the deterioration of using less personal calibration data for increased usability.

Upon further analysis of the model, it shows that using a 2-channel input significantly increases the information available to the neural network. From Fig. 7, we can observe the difference between having a 1-channel and 2-channel input on the first convolution layer. The 1-channel CNN output only generates a series of uniform waveform shapes that resemble the original input waveform, but at different amplitudes. In contrast, the 2-channel CNN output generates four times more unique waveform shapes, including those resembling channel 1’s input, those resembling channel 2’s input, combinations of inputs from channels 1 and 2, and the differences between inputs from channels 1 and 2. This allows the 2-channel neural network to access not only all the information that a 1-channel network has, but also the waveform information from the second channel and all the combinations that result from it. When these new combinations of waveforms are passed on to the next CNN layer, even more unique waveform patterns’ permutations can be generated at a quadratic rate to represent the signal’s characteristics. These major differences highlight the advantage of using a 2-channel input over a 1-channel input. While trying to compare with other works, the fundamental differences in methodology such as comparing sporadic fasting BGL with continuous BGL estimation performance, combined with the difference in the complexity of the collected subject population such as using healthy database versus diabetic database, make it challenging to objectively and fairly compare the performance differences of different methods, as it won’t be an apple-to-apple comparison under the same premises. While we’ve provide the reviewed previous PPG-related works’ performance in Supplementary Table 1, we don’t feel comfortable drawing conclusions on performance differences solely based on reported performance.

The major benefit of the proposed DLimp.H model setup is its scalability. The model does not require retraining or fine-tuning with future pretests, which is highly beneficial and critical for its practical use. It simplifies and streamlines the procedure, potentially reducing the complexity of the system required to perform NIBG measurements. While the previous personalized DL model provides excellent prediction accuracy, it can be challenging to use due to the need for multiple personal measurements and the requirement to retrain the prediction model with each new set of calibration data. This limitation means that the calibration process cannot be performed locally by users; instead, the recorded data would likely need to be transferred to a powerful cloud server for model fine-tuning. With our proposed generalized method, we significantly improve both the usability and scalability when deploying the model on a larger scale.

PPG-based methodologies are often challenged by the difficulties in acquiring a good, long, continuous signal, as the devices in general are artifact-prone. The signal recorded can be heavily distorted due to the patient’s motion, nonideal posture obstructing blood flow, and other factors such as strong light exposure. The methods to denoise and filter the PPG raw data itself can be a research topic with depth. In this work, we have been particularly attentive to the recorded PPG signal quality during the data collection phase to acquire noise-free and artifact-free data. As mentioned in the “Data source” section, all collections of data were conducted in a static condition while the participants were seated and relaxed to mitigate any noise, interference, and artifacts. We also restart the 60-s recordings if we notice any visible artifacts occur during measurement. The study’s premise of working on fasting blood glucose only also follows the same rationale of mitigating the possible influence of different foods. Furthermore, in the data preprocessing stage, we have used a Butterworth filter with the cutoff frequency 0.75 Hz to separate the high frequency part and the low frequency part of the raw PPG signal, and proceeded the valleys and peaks identification of the high-frequency part PPG waveform by the Bigger-Fall-Side (BFS) algorithm (see the section “Methods”, Data Preprocessing of our previous work30). In order to suppress the noise that comes from even higher frequencies and maintain the smoothness and stability of the high-frequency part PPG waveform, we have applied the Z-score normalization39, and used another Butterworth filter to filter out the frequencies higher than 7.5 Hz before proceeding with the BFS algorithm.

The demographics across all three cohorts of subjects are very similar but distinctly reflect their drug use, as shown in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 3. The differences in performance across the cohorts are as expected. The conditions used to separate the cohorts are by categorizing subjects with the complexity of their ADD usage. Due to the lack of detailed medical records of the participants in our database, we are unable to further categorize and analyze them by any other comorbidities. Cohort 1 subjects are the most stable, without any diabetes medications affecting their metabolic stability and physiology. However, some may have taken other drugs that do not directly influence their blood glucose concentration. Subjects in this cohort are generally in the early stages and mild cases of diabetes, which may not yet require drug intervention for BGL control. As a result, they have the lowest average HbA1c and fasting BGL among the cohorts, which is reasonable and as expected. Cohort 2 subjects have their metabolic stability and physiology affected by oral ADD, but show a negative but limited impact on prediction performance, which we believe is due to the consistency of drug types used within the cohort. While cohort 2 subjects are affected by oral ADD, they are not under the influence of other drugs, such as hypertension-related medication. This suggests that the subjects in this cohort may not have hypertension, which is reflected in their normal average systolic and diastolic blood pressure. The absence of hypertension may also suggest that these subjects may not have advanced or long-term diabetes, as hypertension and diabetes often occur together. Cohort 2 subjects are believed to have more advanced diabetes compared to those in cohort 1, as they are already undergoing oral ADD treatment, resulting in a higher average fasting BGL. Cohort 3 consists of the most complex cases, with subjects affected by a combination of oral ADD and other medications, many of which are antihypertensive and antihyperlipidemic. This cohort includes individuals with the most advanced diabetic population with varying drug combinations and inconsistent medication routines, which may have influenced their metabolic stability and physiology in different ways. It is still unclear how to effectively account for the intertwined effects of mixed medications on the PPG signal, but it is known to contribute to poor NIBG performance.

Conclusion

To improve the usability of personalized neural network models, which typically require multiple personal data collection processes, this work introduces an easy-to-use personalized method that leverages general data. The proposed model utilizes data from other subjects taking similar drugs to train a generalized multi-channel CNN-based deep learning model and uses a single personal pretest measurement as a foundation to accurately estimate the fasting BGLs. This method requires the user to perform just one fasting pretest of PPG signals alongside a finger-prick blood glucose measurement for calibration prior to testing. The pretest data are verified to remain effective for up to 30 days. The models achieve prediction accuracies where 98.7, 92.4, and 90.4% of predictions in zone A of the PEG or 98.7, 87.9, and 78.3% of the results falls within CEG zone A while using a pretest taken within 30 days prior from testing for subjects not taking ADDs, subjects not mixing ADDs with other medications, and subjects using antidiabetic drug in conjunction with other types of medications, respectively. Notably, none of the prediction results within a 30-day interval from the pretest falls into hazardous zones C, D, and E. The three cohorts also achieve MARD of 9.59%, 12.23%, and 16.4% respectively. Detailed analysis of the first convolutional layer reveals that the 2-channel input generates four times more unique waveform features compared to the 1-channel input at this stage alone, and increases at a quadratic rate with each convolution layer. The single pretest model does not require retraining or further fine-tuning on the already built model, and thus can easily be adapted to new users/devices via edge computation. This monthly calibration technology can be simply added to current wearable devices for most of the diabetes cohorts’ prognosis control or public health prevention.
