The Pentatricopeptide Repeat Protein OsPPR7 Confers Cold Tolerance at the Booting Stage in Rice by Regulating ABA and ROS Homeostasis
Authors: Changsheng Liu, Jingwen Li, Yantao Li, Yingying Wang, Dongyuan Si, Jingguo Wang, Hongliang Zheng, Wei Xin, Detang Zou, Hualong Liu, Lei Lei, Luomiao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCold stress at the booting stage severely disrupts pollen development and drastically reduces grain yield in rice. Uncovering the genetic basis of cold tolerance is essential for breeding resilient varieties. Here, we identified a cold‑sensitive mutant, csb1, from an EMS‑mutagenized population of the cold‑tolerant japonica variety MK1, which exhibits significantly impaired seed‑setting under cold stress. Genetic analysis indicated that the phenotype is controlled by a single recessive nuclear gene. Using MutMap, we mapped the causal locus to a 26.3–27.0 Mb interval on chromosome 8. Among candidate genes, OsPPR7, encoding a PLS‑class pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) protein, showed strong cold‑induced expression in anthers. A promoter mutation in OsPPR7 was linked to the csb1 phenotype, as confirmed by genetic complementation. CRISPR/Cas9 knockout lines displayed increased cold sensitivity and severe pollen sterility, whereas overexpression enhanced tolerance. Cytological observations revealed that loss of OsPPR7 leads to defective anther development, abnormal pollen nuclei, and failed germination under cold stress. Mechanistically, OsPPR7, localized to mitochondria, regulates cold tolerance by fine‑tuning abscisic acid (ABA) biosynthesis and reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis in young panicles. Haplotype analysis across diverse germplasm identified natural variation in OsPPR7, with the superior cold‑tolerant haplotype Hap_I showing strong selective sweep signals in temperate japonica rice, indicating its role in adaptation to high‑latitude environments. Our study identifies OsPPR7 as a novel positive regulator of reproductive stage cold tolerance and provides a valuable genetic target for improving rice climate resilience.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12284-026-00916-6.Keywords: OsPPR7, Cold tolerance, Booting stage, ABA signaling, Pollen fertility

Introduction

Rice, native to tropical and subtropical regions, is highly sensitive to cold. Exposure to low temperatures during reproductive growth severely compromises yield and grain quality (Espe et al. 2017), making it a primary constraint on rice cultivation in cooler climates (Kuroki et al. 2007). While temperate japonica rice has acquired relatively stronger cold tolerance through natural selection (Liu et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2017), cold damage remains prevalent in major rice-growing areas of China, including the Northeast Plain, the Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau, and regions south of the middle-lower Yangtze River (Li et al. 2022a). This results in annual yield losses of 3–5 million tons (Zhu et al. 2015). In northern China alone, temperate japonica rice is planted on over 4.5 million hectares, where persistently low temperatures during the reproductive stage pose a serious threat to production (Guo et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2017). Therefore, identifying key cold-tolerance genes in temperate japonica rice is essential for breeding resilient cultivars.

Rice pollen are highly sensitive to low temperatures during the tetrad to uninucleate stages, particularly when the tapetum is developing. Cold stress disrupts the programmed degradation of the tapetum, leading to its abnormal expansion. This impairs nutrient supply to the pollen mother cells, alters carbohydrate metabolism, and ultimately causes anther and pollen abnormalities or sterility (Oda et al. 2010; Shimono et al. 2016). Low temperatures affect multiple stages of male reproductive development, including flowering time, pollen dehiscence, viability, pollination, and pollen tube growth (Barton et al. 2014; Boyer and McLaughlin 2007). In rice anthers, cold stress reduces endogenous gibberellin (GA) levels. In cold‑sensitive varieties, this results from the downregulation of GA biosynthetic genes GA20ox3 and GA3ox1, and exogenous GA application can restore fertility (Oda et al. 2010; Sakata et al. 2014). Conversely, WRKY53 represses GA synthesis, and its loss‑of‑function mutant maintains higher GA levels and enhanced cold tolerance (Tang et al., 2022). Abscisic acid (ABA) also plays a key role: overexpression of ABA synthesis genes OsZEP1 and OsNCED3 leads to pollen degradation, while cold‑tolerant varieties maintain low anther ABA levels (Oliver et al. 2007). These varieties further reduce ABA via the C‑8′ hydroxylation pathway, upregulating ABA8ox1 and ABA8ox2 (Ji et al. 2011; Nambara and Marion-Poll 2005). Moreover, bZIP73 Jap enhances cold tolerance by suppressing ABA synthesis genes OsNCED3 and OsNCED5 (Liu et al. 2019). Carbohydrates contribute to cold adaptation by stabilizing membranes, scavenging reactive oxygen species, mediating signaling, and interacting with hormonal pathways (Hincha et al. 1998; Peukert et al. 2014; Tarkowski Ł and Van den Ende 2015; Vereyken et al. 2001; Wim and El-Esawe 2014). Sucrose, starch, monosaccharides, and soluble sugars all influence rice cold‑tolerance under low temperatures (Huang et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2019; Oliver et al. 2007).

Cold tolerance is a complex quantitative trait controlled by multiple genetic loci and modulated by environmental factors, with distinct mechanisms acting at different developmental stages (Pan et al. 2015). Research cold tolerance at booting stage (CTB) has progressed more slowly than studies on seedling-stage tolerance, largely because phenotypic evaluation is complicated by variation in heading date and environmental fluctuations (Saito et al. 2010). Consequently, only a limited number of genes conferring CTB have been isolated via forward genetics. These include: Ctb1, which encodes an F-box protein potentially involved in the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, though its precise mechanism remains unclear (Saito et al. 2010); CTB4a, a leucine-rich repeat receptor-like kinase (LRR-RLK) that interacts with the ATP synthase β-subunit to maintain energy supply under low temperature (Zhang et al. 2017); CTB2, which enhances cold tolerance by increasing sterol glycoside levels and stabilizing membrane permeability (Li et al. 2021); LTT1, involved in maintaining ROS homeostasis during anther development under cold stress (Xu et al. 2020); CTB5, an HD-Zip transcription factor that interacts with OsHox12 to regulate GA metabolism and targets the ABA receptor gene PYL9, thereby improving tolerance through both GA signaling and ROS scavenging (Guo et al. 2025); and CTB6, a lipid transfer protein that stabilizes catalases to scavenge ROS and supports lipid accumulation in anthers (Gao et al. 2025). Although these findings provide a foundation for understanding CTB, the molecular regulatory network remains incomplete. Further studies are needed to elucidate the functional interactions among these genes, the evolutionary patterns of their natural variation, and their utility in molecular breeding.

This study aims to identify mutants from an EMS-mutagenized population of the cold-tolerant japonica rice variety MingKe-1 (MK1). We seek to elucidate the genetic basis of its cold-sensitive phenotype and to clone candidate genes conferring CTB using MutMap. The function of identified genes will be validated through genetic transformation. Furthermore, we will analyze the expression profiles, subcellular localization, and regulatory mechanisms of candidate genes, with particular emphasis on their interplay with hormone signaling pathways. Natural variation, haplotype diversity, and selection signatures of these genes across diverse rice germplasm will also be examined to identify superior cold-tolerant haplotypes. Ultimately, this work aims to provide genetic resources and a theoretical foundation for breeding cold-tolerant rice.

Materials and Methods

Materials

The cold-tolerant temperate japonica variety MK1, originating from Northeast China, was used in this study. A cold‑sensitive mutant, designated csb1 (Cold Sensitive at the Booting stage), was identified in the M2 generation of an ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS)-mutagenized MK1 population. For genetic analysis, csb1 (female parent) was crossed with MK1 (male parent) to produce 12 F1 plants, which were subsequently self‑pollinated to generate 1,773 F2 individuals. This F2 population was employed for χ² test for Mendelian segregation, genotyping, and QTL mapping. All plant materials were grown under standard field conditions at the experimental station of Northeast Agricultural University in Harbin, Heilongjiang Province, China. Data are presented as means of five biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and graphs were prepared with GraphPad Prism 7 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Identification of Cold Tolerance at the Booting Stage

To assess CTB, the genetic population and all transgenic plants were transplanted into rice fields at the Northeast Agricultural University experimental station, with a plant spacing of 10 cm and a row spacing of 30 cm. The main panicle was tagged during the booting stage when the distance from the flag leaf auricle to the auricle of the penultimate leaf reached between − 5 cm and 0 cm, corresponding to the period from meiosis to the uninucleate pollen stage. Subsequently, the marked plants were subjected to low-temperature treatment (LT, 17℃ for 7 day) using the intelligent irrigation system previously described (Yang et al. 2023), whilst the control plants were maintained under normal temperature (NT, 25–28℃) conditions. The relative seed-setting rate of mature seeds was used as an indicator of cold tolerance, calculated as: relative seed-setting rate = (seed-setting rate of panicles under cold treatment/seed-setting rate of panicles at room temperature) × 100%. Mature anthers were immersed in a 1% iodine-potassium iodide solution and imaged using a Nikon Eclipse 80i microscope to assess pollen viability. For in vivo analysis of pollen tube elongation, spikelets were harvested two hours post-pollination, fixed in FAA solution (100% ethanol: acetic acid: formalin = 14:1:2) for 24 h, sequentially dehydrated in 50%, 30%, and 10% alcohol solutions. Subsequently, the pistil tissue was treated with 2 mol/L NaOH for 12 h, stained with 0.05% aniline blue solution for an additional 12 h, and examined under a Leica DM4000B fluorescence microscope. Mature pollen nuclei were dyed with a 1 mg ml-l DAPI solution and visualized using a Revolve FL microscope, following the protocol by Huang et al. (2013). The processed samples were imaged with a scanning electron microscope (SU8010) to analyze the microstructure of the anthers and the morphology of the pollen grains.

Construction of Segregation Pools and Sequencing Data Analysis

In the MutMap analysis (Abe et al. 2012), cold-sensitive lines with seed-setting rates below 30% were selected from the F₂ population (1,773 individuals) to construct a mutant pool, with the wild-type MK1 serving as the cold-tolerant control. The mutant pool consisted of equimolar DNA from 68 sensitive individuals. A total amount of 0.5 µg DNA per sample was used as input material for the DNA library preparations. Sequencing library was generated using Truseq Nano DNA HT Sample Prep Kit (Illumina USA) following manufacturer’s recommendations. After quality assessment, Using Illumina’s library preparation and sequencing platform, a sequencing library with insert sizes of approximately 350 bp was constructed. The raw data was quality-assessed using the fastp software, and fragments shorter than 36 bp, after adapter removal and quality trimming, were discarded. Statistical analyses were performed on the quality-trimmed data to determine the number of reads, total base count, GC content and Q30%. Process the alignment results (BAM files) using GATK’s Best Practices workflow, and perform SNP detection using GATK’s Haplotyper method. The filtering criteria are based on the parameters recommended by GATK (McKenna et al. 2010). Variants were functionally annotated using SnpEff (Cingolani et al. 2012). Trait-linked intervals were identified based on SNP data. For filtered variant sites, SNP-index values were calculated separately for the mutant and wild-type pools. We employ a sliding window method to perform noise reduction on the ΔSNP-index values. To improve accuracy, we use the 0.995th percentile as a threshold on the sliding window results to identify candidate intervals.

RNA Extraction and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China), followed by reverse transcription to synthesize cDNA. The relative expression of OsPPR7 was quantified by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) with OsActin as the internal control; primer sequences are provided in Table S1. Transcript levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). Statistical significance was assessed by one-way ANOVA.

Subcellular Localisation of OsPPR7 Protein

For subcellular localisation of the OsPPR7 (PLS-like pentapeptide repeat protein 7), the mitochondrial marker was generated by fusing the mitochondrial targeting signal of Arabidopsis COXIV (cytochrome c oxidase subunit IV) to mCherry, and this construct was introduced into the pCAMBIA1305 vector under the control of the CaMV 35 S promoter. The 35 S: COXIV‑mCherry construct was used as a mitochondrial marker (Nelson et al. 2007). For co‑localisation, the 35 S: OsPPR7‑YFP construct was co‑expressed with the mitochondrial marker in Nicotiana benthamiana leaf epidermal cells. YFP fluorescence was excited at 514 nm and detected at 520–560 nm, while mCherry fluorescence was excited at 561 nm and detected at 590–630 nm. Fluorescence was visualised with a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP2).

Generation of Transgenic Lines

To induce CRISPR/Cas9-mediated mutations, two single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting the OsPPR7 exon were designed. The target sequences were GGAAGACACGATATTTCTCC and GCTCGCACACTGAAAACAAA, respectively. Each sgRNA was expressed independently under the control of a rice promoter. To construct the knockout line, the two sgRNA expression cassettes were assembled into a vector; the resulting construct was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 and transformed into MK1 callus tissue via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. For genotyping, PCR amplification was performed using the primers 5′-TCTCGGATTCTCAATGGGAAGAC-3′ and 5′-TGAGCAICAGAGTGACCCTC-3′, and the amplified fragments were Sanger sequenced to identify induced mutations. For overexpression, the OsPPR7 cDNA was placed under the control of the maize ubiquitin promoter and cloned into the binary vector pMDC32 via AscI/PacI sites using the In‑Fusion Advantage PCR Cloning Kit (Clontech, USA). The resulting construct was introduced into MK1 through Agrobacterium transformation. For complementation, a 4,949 bp genomic fragment containing the full OsPPR7 coding region and 2,000 bp of its native promoter was amplified and cloned into pCAMBIA1305 to generate the complementation vector pCAMBIA1305-OsPPR7.

Measurement of Physiological Indices Under Low Temperature Conditions

Malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) contents were determined in young panicles sampled after 0, 48, and 72 h of cold treatment. Extraction and quantification followed the respective assay kits (Plant MDA Assay Kit, A003‑3‑1; Hydrogen Peroxide Assay Kit; Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). For MDA, 200 mg of tissue was ground in liquid nitrogen, extracted with 2 mL of solution, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was reacted with thiobarbituric acid (TBA) at 100 °C for 20 min, centrifuged again, and absorbance was read at 532 nm. The determination of hydrogen peroxide was carried out in accordance with the protocol provided with the hydrogen peroxide assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Institute of Biological Engineering, China). Grind 100 mg of seedlings in a liquid nitrogen environment, resuspend in 1.0 mL of PBS, and centrifuge at 12,000×g for 12 min. The supernatant was then mixed with a colouring agent containing molybdate solution, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at a wavelength of 405 nm. For ABA analysis, panicles collected after 48 h of cold treatment were sent to Nanjing Ruiyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. ABA was extracted using a QuEChERS-based method, weigh out 10 g of sample, add 10 mL of acetonitrile and the internal standard, and shake vigorously; add NaCl, MgSO₄ and buffer salts to achieve phase separation and adjust the pH; after centrifugation, take an aliquot of the upper organic phase, mix it with magnesium sulphate and PSA, and perform dispersive solid-phase extraction. Quantitative analysis was ultimately performed using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with triple quadrupole mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS; Qsight LX 50/420, PerkinElmer, USA). To evaluate whether cold tolerance mediated by OsPPR7 was influenced by ABA, a 4 µM fluridone solution was sprayed three times daily on wild-type (WT) and knockout (KO) plants five days prior to treatment, after which the seed setting rate of the panicles was assessed.

Phylogenetic Analysis and Artificial Selection Evaluation of OsPPR7

Homologous sequences of OsPPR7 were identified using the BLASTp search programme of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI, www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Multiple sequence alignments were performed using DNAMAN. For haplotype and evolutionary analysis, OsPPR7 sequences were retrieved from 1,768 rice accessions (Table S2) (Yao et al. 2019). Haplotype analysis was performed using high‑density SNPs from the coding and promoter regions. Haplotype networks were constructed with PopArt 1.7 using the TCS algorithm (Leigh and Bryant 2015). A phylogenetic analysis of OsPPR7 was conducted across the entire dataset of 1,768 germplasm accessions using MEGA 11 software and the neighbour-joining method, with 1,000 bootstrap repetitions analysed. (Kumar et al. 2018). Nucleotide diversity (π) was estimated for the coding region and 30‑kb flanking sequences of OsPPR7 across 1,453 accessions using DnaSP 6 (Table S3).

Result

csb1 is a Cold-Sensitive Mutant Associated with Seed Set

Through screening of an EMS-mutagenized population of the cold-tolerant japonica variety MK1, we identified a mutant with severely reduced seed setting under cold stress, designated csb1 (Cold Sensitive at Booting stage 1; Fig. 1a). csb1 plants showed a seed-setting rate of only 14.3%, compared to 97.4% in the wild type (WT) under low-temperature treatment (Fig. 1b-c). I2-KI staining revealed that csb1 pollen was largely unstained and sterile, with a marked decrease in pollen grain number relative to the WT (Fig. 1d-e). To determine the genetic basis of the csb1 phenotype, a cross was made between the WT and csb1. All F₁ plants resembled the WT under cold stress (Figure S1). In the F₂ population, plants segregated into mutant and WT phenotypes at a ratio close to 1:3 (χ² = 0.277; Table S4, Figure S1), confirming that the cold-sensitive trait in csb1 is controlled by a single recessive nuclear gene.

Mapping of the csb1 Causal Locus

To identify the causal gene underlying the csb1 phenotype, we performed MutMap analysis (Abe et al. 2012) by aligning resequencing data to the Nipponbare reference genome. The sequencing depths for the wild-type and mutant pools were 55.99X and 52.09X, respectively, with genome coverage of 97.41% and 97.39%. Alignment achieved high efficiency, with > 99% of clean reads mapped and > 97% properly paired. Compared to the reference, the wild-type parent MK1 contained 562,557 SNPs and 109,673 InDels, while the F₂ mutant pool harbored 561,714 SNPs and 109,551 InDels (Table S5). The ΔSNP‑index plot revealed a distinct peak on chromosome 8, centered at 26.3–27.0 Mb (Fig. 2a). This region contains 21 SNPs distributed across eight annotated genes (Table S6). Excluding 11 intergenic variants, the remaining 10 variants are located within the exons, introns and promoters of four genes (Fig. 2b). Among these, the retrotransposon protein LOC_Os08g42140 and LOC_Os08g42160 exhibit amino acid changes. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that, of these four genes, only LOC_Os08g42610 was strongly induced by low temperature in anther tissue (Fig. 2c), and a single SNP was present in its promoter. The RAP-DB database annotates LOC_Os08g42610 as PLS-like pentapeptide repeat protein 7 (OsPPR7), identifying it as the most probable candidate gene responsible for the cold‑sensitive phenotype of csb1.

OsPPR7 is a Functionally Uncharacterized PLS-Class PPR Gene

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that OsPPR7 shares homology with orthologous proteins in Hordeum vulgare, Triticum spp., Lolium perenne, Zea mays and other plants (Fig. 3a). The protein comprises 581 amino acids and contains eight conserved PPR motifs, characteristic of the PLS subfamily (Fig. 3b, c). Cis‑regulatory element analysis of the OsPPR7 promoter identified motifs associated with abiotic stress, hormone signaling, and dehydration responses (Table S7, Figure S2). A promoter variant (− 1298 G > A) occurs within an ABRE motif (GCCACGTGGC → ACCACGTGGC), which may regulate the ABA-mediated OsPPR7 transcriptional response by affecting the binding capacity of the ABRE motif to transfactors. Expression data indicate that OsPPR7 is constitutively expressed, with highest levels in leaves, anthers, and panicles (Figure S3). As PPR proteins typically localize to organelles and occasionally to the nucleus(Hao et al. 2019; Lurin et al. 2004), we examined the subcellular distribution of OsPPR7 using a YFP fusion. Transient expression in N. benthamiana leaves showed that OsPPR7‑YFP fluorescence localized with mitochondrial markers (Fig. 3d). Together, these results establish OsPPR7 as a novel PPR protein with conserved sequence features and organellar localization.

OsPPR7 Positively Regulates Cold Tolerance of Rice at Booting Stage

To confirm that the promoter mutation in OsPPR7 causes the csb1 phenotype, a 4,949 bp genomic fragment containing the native promoter and full coding sequence was introduced into the csb1. In the complementation lines CP1 and CP2, cold tolerance was restored: the relative seed-setting rate under cold stress increased from 0.16 in csb1 to 0.91 (Fig. 4a, b), and pollen fertility was significantly improved (Fig. 4c). These results verify that the OsPPR7 promoter mutation is responsible for the cold‑sensitive phenotype. CRISPR/Cas9 was used to generate loss‑of‑function mutants KO1 and KO2 in the MK1 background. KO1 carries a 7‑bp deletion at position 41 of the first exon, and KO2 harbors a 4‑bp deletion at position 74, both predicted to cause premature translation termination (Figure S4a, b). Both knockout lines showed markedly reduced seed‑setting rates and severe pollen sterility under cold stress (Fig. 4d–f). In contrast, overexpression lines exhibited intermediate cold tolerance, with seed‑setting rates significantly higher than those of csb1 but lower than the wild type (Fig. 4g–i). I2-KI staining results were consistent with these phenotypic observations (Fig. 4j–l). Together, these data demonstrate that OsPPR7 positively regulates CTB.

OsPPR7 Influences Anther and Pollen Development to Govern Cold Tolerance

Pollen sterility is a primary cause of spikelet sterility (Oliver et al. 2007). To investigate how anther developmental defects affect pollen fertility under cold treatment, we compared the anther morphology of WT and KO plants under cold stress and control conditions (Fig. 5a–f). Under cold stress, KO anthers were smaller and malformed (Fig. 5e, f), whereas WT anthers developed normally (Fig. 5b, c). At room temperature, mature pollen from both WT and KO plants germinated on stigmas and penetrated the embryo sac, as shown by aniline blue staining (Fig. 5g–i). Under cold stress, however, KO pollen failed to germinate. DAPI staining further revealed that mature WT pollen grains contained three distinct nuclei: two densely stained sperm cells and one diffuse vegetative nucleus, exhibiting a plump morphology. In contrast, cold-stressed KO pollen grains showed poorly defined nuclei and a collapsed epidermis (Fig. 5j–m), indicating disrupted nuclear-cytoplasmic development. These cytological defects explain the reduced seed-setting rate in the osppr7 mutants.

OsPPR7 Regulates Cold Tolerance by Mediating ABA and Reactive Oxygen Species Signaling

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) and ABA signaling play critical roles in regulating cold tolerance in rice (Xiang et al. 2025). To examine the effect of low temperature on redox homeostasis, we measured malondialdehyde (MDA) and H₂O₂ levels in young panicles exposed to 17 °C for 0, 48, and 72 h. While no significant difference was observed at 0 h, the OsPPR7 knockout (KO) lines accumulated markedly higher levels of both MDA and H₂O₂ after 48 and 72 h of cold stress compared to the wild type (WT). In contrast, overexpression (OE) lines showed reduced accumulation, indicating that loss of OsPPR7 function elevates oxidative stress in young panicles under cold conditions (Fig. 6a-d). Since the OsPPR7 promoter mutation lies within an ABA-responsive element (ABRE), we quantified endogenous ABA in anthers under normal temperature (NT) and low temperature (LT) conditions. At NT, ABA levels did not differ significantly between WT and KO, but were elevated in the OE line. Under LT, however, ABA content increased sharply in KO anthers, exceeding the level in OE lines (Fig. 6e-f). Application of the ABA biosynthesis inhibitor fluridone significantly improved the seed-setting rate of KO plants under cold stress (Fig. 6g). Consistent with this, the expression levels of key ABA biosynthesis genes, including NCED3, NCED4, bZIP12, bZIP46, and ADR3, were substantially upregulated in KO lines under LT conditions. In OE lines, these genes were also upregulated compared to WT, but to a lesser extent than in KO lines (Fig. 6h-l). This moderate upregulation aligns with the elevated yet non-excessive ABA levels observed in OE lines and supports a model in which OsPPR7 modulates ABA biosynthesis in a dosage-dependent manner, thereby influencing cold tolerance at the booting stage through the maintenance of ROS homeostasis and proper ABA dynamics.

Natural Variation, Origins, and Selection Analysis of OsPPR7

To examine the natural variation of OsPPR7, haplotype analysis was performed on 1,768 rice accessions (Yao et al. 2019), identifying a total of 10 haplotypes (Hap_I–Hap_X; Table S2, Fig. 7a). Hap_I, Hap_VI, Hap_VII, and Hap_VIII were primarily distributed in japonica rice, whereas Hap_II, Hap_IV, Hap_IX, and Hap_X were prevalent in indica rice. Phylogenetic analysis separated the accessions into two major clades corresponding to indica and japonica subspecies, with Hap_I and Hap_II representing distinct haplotype groups within this divergence (Fig. 7b). These results support OsPPR7 as a gene associated with indica–japonica differentiation. Geographic analysis shows that the indica subclade is mainly distributed in southeastern China and Southeast Asia (Fig. 7c, Figure S5a), while japonica varieties are cultivated primarily in northeastern, northern, southeastern, and southwestern China (Fig. 7c, Figure S5b). To determine whether OsPPR7 was selected during domestication, we analyzed nucleotide diversity in the OsPPR7 region across 352 wild and 1,101 cultivated accessions (Fig. 7d, Table S8). The genetic diversity of indica (π = 0.10726, θ = 0.11549) was comparable to that of wild rice (π = 0.10474, θ = 0.14827) but significantly higher than in tropical japonica (π = 0.07168, θ = 0.09671) and temperate japonica (π = 0.01592, θ = 0.06301). Neutrality tests revealed a significantly negative Tajima’s D only in temperate japonica (D = − 2.18172, P < 0.01; Table S8), indicating that OsPPR7 has undergone strong selection in this subpopulation. To identify superior cold‑tolerant haplotypes, we analyzed sequence variation at the OsPPR7 locus across 248 northern Chinese japonica accessions (Table S9). Three haplotypes were identified: Hap_I (GG), Hap_VII (AG), and Hap_other (AT) (Fig. 7e). Hap_I, represented by MK1, occurred in 84 accessions, and Hap_VII in 67. Hap_other was not found in the broader panel of 1,768 germplasms, suggesting it may be a locally selected variant. The haplotypes are distinguished by two nonsynonymous SNPs at positions 26,933,953 bp (A→G) and 26,933,965 bp (T→G). Phenotypic evaluation under cold stress revealed significant differences in seed‑setting rates, ranked as Hap_I > Hap_other > Hap_VII (Fig. 7f). The A→G change at 26,933,953 bp is likely a key variant enhancing cold tolerance. Hap_other, present in 39% of the panel and exhibiting seed‑setting rates of 59.1–84.9%, may represent a recently selected allele favored in breeding. Haplotype network analysis indicates that Hap_I was derived from wild rice, with Hap_VII subsequently diverging from it (Fig. 7a). Geographically, Hap_VII is concentrated in central China, whereas the cold‑tolerant Hap_I has spread from lower to higher latitudes and become established (Figure S5c).

Discussion

Rice cold tolerance at the booting stage (CTB) is a complex quantitative trait controlled by multiple genes. Several key CTB genes have been cloned through forward genetics, primarily functioning through energy metabolism and hormone signaling pathways. For instance, Ctb1 operates via the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway (Saito et al. 2010), CTB4a enhances ATP synthase activity to support pollen viability (Zhang et al. 2017), CTB2 elevates sterol glycoside levels (Li et al. 2021), and bZIP73 reduces anther ABA content to improve tolerance (Liu et al. 2019). These findings collectively indicate that low-temperature stress during booting disrupts energy metabolism, which impairs male reproductive development at the cellular level and ultimately reduces panicle fertility. In this study, we isolated a cold-sensitive mutant, csb1, from an EMS-mutagenized population of the cold-tolerant japonica variety MK1, which showed severely reduced fertility under low temperature. MutMap analysis located the causal locus to a 26.3–27.0 Mb interval on chromosome 8. Among the annotated genes in this region, only four carried genomic mutations. qRT‑PCR revealed that LOC_Os08g42610 (OsPPR7), which contains a promoter mutation, was strongly induced by cold in anthers. OsPPR7 encodes a PLS-class pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) protein, and phylogenetic analysis indicates it is highly conserved among monocots. PPR proteins have been previously linked to temperature responses in plants. Examples include OsPUS1, which regulates mitochondrial superoxide levels to enhance cold tolerance in rice (Zu et al. 2023); CsPPR, which promotes cold tolerance during cucumber germination (Li et al. 2023); and OsPPR8, which influences grain quality and heat tolerance via mitochondrial RNA splicing (Yuan et al. 2025). Additionally, a genome-wide association study identified a haplotype in Os01g0357800 associated with seedling cold tolerance (Ham et al. 2021). To confirm that the OsPPR7 promoter mutation is causal, we introduced the wild-type genomic sequence including its native promoter from MK1 into the csb1 mutant. This complementation restored seed-setting under cold stress. Further characterization using CRISPR/Cas9 knockout and overexpression lines demonstrated that OsPPR7 acts as a positive regulator of CTB. These results establish OsPPR7 as a novel PPR protein essential for rice CTB.

Cold stress during rice reproductive growth leads to tapetum enlargement, abnormal pollen development, reduced pollen production, and increased male sterility (Satake 1991). Thus, cold injury primarily affects the development of male reproductive organs. Here, we show that OsPPR7 positively regulates CTB by influencing anther and pollen development. Cytological analysis revealed that under cold stress, OsPPR7 knockout (KO) plants exhibited smaller anthers, weaker pollen staining, reduced pollen number, and severely impaired fertility at the panicle stage. Pollen germination on stigmas was inhibited, preventing entry into the embryo sac and fertilization. DAPI staining further showed aberrant pollen morphology in KO plants after cold treatment, with both sperm and vegetative nuclei displaying developmental defects. Consistent with previous reports that loss of qCTB7 reduces pollen grain number under cold stress (Yang et al. 2023), our results indicate that failure of anther and pollen development under low temperature directly leads to decreased spikelet fertility. PPR proteins are known to regulate pollen development and male sterility through diverse mechanisms in plants. For example, the cytoplasmic PPR protein OsCPPR1 influences pollen viability by modulating transcript levels of the transcription factor OsGLK1, which is essential for anther parenchyma and chloroplast development (Zheng et al. 2022). Similarly, mutation of OsTMS19 causes fertility loss under high but not low temperatures (Zhou et al. 2024). We observed that loss of OsPPR7 function does not affect pollen development at normal temperatures, but results in nuclear sterility when subjected to cold stress. It seems that, under normal conditions, compensatory mechanisms can maintain mitochondrial respiration and energy homeostasis in the absence of OsPPR7. Cold stress, however, impairs mitochondrial electron transport and increases the production of ROS. As OsPPR7 is a mitochondrial PPR protein, it is likely involved in processing or maintaining the stability of transcripts encoding components of the electron transport chain. In OsPPR7 knockout plants, cold stress exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction, resulting in elevated levels of H2O2 and MDA in young spikes. This cold-specific requirement is consistent with the behaviour of other stress-responsive PPR genes, such as OsPUS1 (Zu et al. 2023). Therefore, OsPPR7 functions as a cold-regulated protective factor for mitochondria.

PPR proteins have emerged as important regulators of plant responses to biotic and abiotic stresses. In various species, their expression is modulated by salt, osmotic and oxidative stress, pathogen challenge, and signaling molecules such as SA, MeJA, and ABA (Che et al. 2022; Luo et al. 2022; Xing et al. 2018). Anther and pollen development are highly sensitive to ABA, where excessive or mistimed ABA signaling can induce pollen degeneration. In cold‑sensitive rice, upregulation of ABA biosynthetic genes OsZEP1 and OsNCED3 leads to rapid ABA accumulation and pollen abortion, whereas cold‑tolerant varieties maintain low endogenous ABA to preserve fertility (Oliver et al. 2007). In this study, under cold stress, ABA levels differed significantly among genotypes but did not change in a simple linear pattern. Wild-type plants maintained moderate ABA accumulation. Knockout lines showed excessive ABA elevation, whereas overexpression lines exhibited relatively controlled ABA increase. These observations indicate that OsPPR7 adjusts ABA levels in a genotype-dependent, dynamic manner to maintain homeostasis, rather than simply increasing or decreasing ABA abundance. In KO plants, ABA levels rose sharply and rapidly at the early stage of cold stress, likely overwhelming the capacity for signal desensitization and creating a positive feedback loop with ROS overproduction. In contrast, ABA accumulation in OE plants was more moderate, enabling a coordinated activation of protective pathways without triggering metabolic disruption. Consistent with this, application of the ABA biosynthesis inhibitor fluridone significantly rescued the seed-setting rate of KO plants under cold stress (Fig. 6g), indicating that the elevated ABA in KO is deleterious rather than protective—a hallmark of disrupted ABA signaling homeostasis. Excessive ROS can cause aberrant programmed cell death in the tapetum, disrupt lipid transport, impair pollen wall formation, and ultimately lead to pollen abortion (Gao et al. 2025; Pan et al. 2021; Zafar et al. 2020). ABA acts as a central hub that spatiotemporally modulates ROS dynamics, balancing signaling and toxicity while influencing reproduction‑related gene expression (Li et al. 2022b). Here, MDA, H₂O₂, and ABA levels rose sharply in KO anthers under cold, disrupting ROS homeostasis. By contrast, OE plants exhibited reduced oxidative damage, suggesting that their moderate ABA elevation effectively engages ROS scavenging mechanisms. In OE plants, moderate ABA upregulation helped scavenge excess ROS and maintain pollen fertility. Consistent with this, several ABA biosynthesis genes were upregulated by cold stress in a genotype‑dependent manner. Mitochondrial retrograde signalling (MRS) is a key molecular pathway regulating nuclear gene expression in response to mitochondrial dysfunction. Among the signalling molecules involved, the accumulation of mitochondrial ROS is the most conserved MRS signal in plants; it regulates expression of nuclear genes associated with stress responses and hormone synthesis by activating nuclear transcription factors such as bZIP and ABI4 (Li et al. 2022b). In this study, OsPPR7 deficiency led to mitochondrial dysfunction, subsequently triggering a massive accumulation of ROS in young spikelets (Fig. 6a-d). This ROS signal can activate the expression of key nuclear genes involved in ABA synthesis (such as OsNCED3 and OsNCED4), ultimately leading to excessive ABA accumulation. This regulatory mechanism is highly consistent with classical studies on mitochondrial-nuclear crosstalk in plants (Li et al. 2022b). Therefore, OsPPR7 may act as a homeostatic regulator involved in ABA biosynthesis, capable of modulating ABA biosynthesis and the efficiency of its signalling pathways in response to cold stress. Consequently, we propose that OsPPR7 may enhance cold tolerance by coordinating the dynamic changes in ABA levels and the homeostatic balance of ROS, thereby ensuring normal pollen development and fertilisation capacity under low-temperature stress conditions (Fig. 8). However, the specific molecular mechanisms by which OsPPR7 regulates ABA biosynthesis genes require further investigation, currently, its physiological role in cold tolerance has been primarily elucidated.

A proposed model for OsPPR7-mediated cold tolerance at the booting stage in rice. In wild-type (WT) anther cells, OsPPR7 is stably expressed in mitochondria under low-temperature conditions. This maintains reactive oxygen species (ROS) and abscisic acid (ABA) homeostasis, ensures normal development of anthers and pollen, and ultimately confers cold tolerance at the booting stage. In contrast, loss of OsPPR7 function impairs mitochondrial integrity, triggers ROS burst and excessive ABA accumulation, causes severe defects in anther morphology and pollen abortion, and consequently leads to compromised cold tolerance and significantly reduced seed setting rate under cold stress. Genetic variation is fundamental to biological evolution, the maintenance of genetic diversity, and crop domestication (Dong et al. 2025). In this study, we found that an EMS-induced mutation in the promoter region of OsPPR7 impairs cold tolerance. This specific mutation was not detected among 1,768 rice accessions. Sequence analysis defined ten major haplotypes, which clustered into two distinct groups corresponding to the indica and japonica subpopulations. Hap_I, represented by MK1, is widely distributed and demonstrates strong geographical adaptation. Among 248 northern Chinese varieties, only three haplotypes were identified. Hap_VII, a cold-sensitive type, is characterized by wild-type alleles at positions 26,933,953 bp (A) and 26,933,965 bp (T). The remaining two haplotypes are cold-tolerant and likely represent natural variants selected by breeders during long-term domestication; these constitute the predominant genotypes in temperate japonica rice. PPR proteins function as sequence-specific RNA-binding factors within organelles and are essential for RNA processing, editing and stability (Barkan and Small 2014). The non-synonymous SNP distinguishing cold-tolerant Hap_I from Hap_VII is likely to alter the RNA-binding affinity or specificity of OsPPR7. Given the extremely high energy demands during the germination stage (particularly in anthers and developing rachis), we hypothesise that this substitution enhances the stability or proper processing of transcripts involved in mitochondrial respiration or chloroplast function, thereby conferring the plant’s ability to withstand cold stress. However, further functional validation (e.g. via allelic replacement using single-base editing) is essential to definitively link this single amino acid change to the differences in seed-setting rates observed under cold stress. Phylogeographic analysis suggests that the cold-tolerant Hap_I originated from wild rice, whereas the cold-sensitive Hap_VII diverged from it. During domestication, Hap_I expanded from low to high latitudes, enabling adaptation to colder climates. In contrast, Hap_VII was gradually eliminated during northward migration and is now largely confined to warmer central regions of China, whereas tropical japonica genotypes are mainly found in southwestern and coastal areas (Figure S5d). This adaptive trajectory parallels that of known cold-tolerance genes such as CTB5, CTB4a, and HAN1, which together contribute to the cold adaptation of temperate japonica rice in high-latitude environments (Guo et al. 2025; Mao et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2017).

Conclusion

This study identifies OsPPR7, a pentatricopeptide repeat protein, as a positive regulator of cold tolerance at the rice booting stage. Genetic and complementation analyses confirmed that a recessive promoter mutation in OsPPR7 causes the cold-sensitive, low seed-setting phenotype of the csb1 mutant. Functionally, OsPPR7, localized to mitochondria, is required for normal anther and pollen development under cold stress. Mechanistically, OsPPR7 fine-tunes ABA biosynthesis and ROS homeostasis to sustain pollen fertility under low temperatures. Analysis of natural variation revealed distinct OsPPR7 haplotypes linked to indica–japonica differentiation and environmental adaptation. The superior cold‑tolerant haplotype Hap_I, prevalent in temperate japonica rice, bears molecular signatures of selection during domestication and latitudinal expansion. These results establish OsPPR7 as a key genetic factor underlying reproductive‑stage cold tolerance and provide a valuable resource for breeding cold‑resilient rice varieties.
