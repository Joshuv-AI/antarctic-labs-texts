The SHARE study – Survivorship After Head and Neck Cancer: evaluating patient care and adherence to follow up in Ontario, Canada: study protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Billy Tran, Agnieszka Dzioba, Jennifer Baker, Adam Mutsaers, Anthony C Nichols, Adrian Mendez, Chris D Goodman, David A Palma, Eric Winquist, Jennifer D Irwin, Kevin Fung, Paul Stewart, Pencilla Lang, Rohann J M Correa, Sara Kuruvilla, Sylvia Mitchell, Timothy Phillips, S Danielle MacNeil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurvivors of head and neck cancer (HNC) experience long-term physical and psychosocial effects post-treatment, however, often receive fragmented survivorship care. Evidence-based survivorship guidelines exist, but implementation remains limited. Poor coordination, insufficient communication, and lack of tailored support contribute to unmet patient needs. Treatment Summary and Survivorship Care Plans (TSSP) and Motivational Interviewing (MI) may improve self-efficacy and adherence to survivorship care recommendations. This study aims to evaluate whether a personalized TSSP along with a one-time MI counselling session improves physician implementation of survivorship care recommendations, patient satisfaction with post-treatment care, and quality of life (QoL) among HNC survivors.Methods/designThis study is a prospective, single-centre, two-arm, superiority randomized controlled trial (RCT) with a 1:1 allocation ratio. Outcomes will be evaluated at baseline, 3, 6, and 12 months post-baseline visit. A total of 252 HNC survivors (stage I–IVA, aged ≥ 18 years, 3–6 months post-definitive treatment, English-speaking, with no metastatic or residual disease) will be recruited by a trained research assistant through the Survivorship Clinic at Victoria Hospital in London, Canada. Recruitment began in May 2025. Participants will be randomized to either the intervention group (TSSP and MI session) or the control group (usual care only). The intervention consists of a 45-min MI session delivered by a trained nurse practitioner, focused on exploring the top 3 patient survivorship symptoms/concerns, providing resources and referrals, and goal-setting related to survivorship care recommendations. The primary outcome is the proportion of patient-identified survivorship symptoms/concerns addressed by primary care providers (PCP) at 12 months post-baseline between study groups, with secondary assessments at 3 and 6 months. Secondary outcomes include patient satisfaction with the TSSP and MI session, patient satisfaction with care and information, QoL, and PCP feedback on the utility of the TSSP. Descriptive statistics will be reported, and intention-to-treat analyses will be conducted using mixed-effects models to evaluate group differences over time.DiscussionThis study will contribute new evidence on the feasibility and effectiveness of integrating a scalable, combined TSSP and MI counselling intervention into routine HNC survivorship care. By promoting patient-centered communication, this approach may empower survivors to engage in self-management and improve long-term health outcomes. Findings will inform best practices for survivorship care planning and support the implementation of patient-tailored interventions across various oncology settings.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT06127784. Background

Head and neck cancers (HNCs) comprise a group of malignancies originating in the oral cavity, larynx, oropharynx, nasopharynx, and hypopharynx. Globally, HNC is the sixth most common cancer, accounting for approximately 890,000 new cases (roughly 4.5% of global cancer diagnoses) and 460,000 deaths (roughly 4.7% of global cancer deaths) in 2022 [1, 2]. This represents a notable rise compared to previous years, partially attributed to a change in risk factor prevalence. While tobacco and alcohol use have been the primary risk factors for HNC, in recent decades, an increasing incidence of human papillomavirus (HPV)-related oropharyngeal cancers has shifted the epidemiology of HNCs [3–5]. Globally, it is estimated that as high as 90% of oropharyngeal cancers are identified as HPV-positive [6, 7]. Patients with HPV-positive tumors have distinct clinical and demographic profiles which include being younger, non-smokers, and have better overall prognoses [8, 9]. The shift in HNC prevalence underscores the need for tailored survivorship care to address the unique needs of HNC patients, particularly those with HPV-related cancers. As the incidence of HPV-related HNC continues to rise, healthcare systems must adapt to provide comprehensive post-treatment support, encompassing medical, psychosocial, and behavioural interventions. Survivorship research aims to focus on health and life after an acute HNC diagnosis and treatment phase [10].

HNC survivors often face a multitude of long-term, complex, and debilitating challenges post-treatment. While overall survival has improved due to advancements in treatment modalities, these definitive treatments frequently result in physical, functional, and psychosocial impairments that significantly diminish quality of life (QoL) [11]. Common treatment-related sequelae include dysphagia, xerostomia, trismus, lymphedema, and speech and voice dysfunction [12–14]. Additionally, HNC survivors are at an increased risk of depression, anxiety, body image disturbances, and likely to avoid social spaces [15–17]. Furthermore, an additional burden many HNC survivors face is financial toxicity. These factors include out-of-pocket expenses, loss of income, and travel costs [18–20]. The cumulative impact of these challenges is further compounded by the fragmented nature of survivorship care. Patients often must navigate the transition from active treatment to follow-up with minimal guidance, inconsistent communication between multidisciplinary teams and primary care providers (PCP), and a lack of education on managing late effects or monitoring for recurrence [21].

Survivorship initiatives are beginning to recognize these issues; however, widespread implementation of survivorship care remains inconsistent [22]. Although clinical guidelines recommend structured treatment and survivorship care plans to facilitate patient self-management and improve communication between care teams, these tools are not routinely adopted in the context of HNCs [23]. Integrating evidence-based approaches may offer a promising path towards addressing gaps in survivorship care and improving outcomes for this vulnerable patient population.

Treatment Summary and Survivorship Care Plans (TSSPs) have been brought forward as a tool to improve the quality of survivorship care among HNC patients. This personalized document aims to enhance patient understanding of their diagnosis and treatment, facilitate communication between healthcare providers, and promote adherence to follow-up care. A recent pilot study demonstrated that TSSPs improved HNC survivors’ recall of their diagnosis and treatment details in the short term, suggesting that the TSSPs are a beneficial component of survivorship care. However, the adoption and evaluation of TSSPs in the HNC context remain limited. [24] Further research must establish the effectiveness of this intervention to optimize its implementation.

Motivational Interviewing (MI) is a patient-centered counselling approach designed to elicit behaviour change and has shown promise in the oncology setting. In the cancer care setting, MI has been found to be associated with positive outcomes including improved functional tasks, increased physical activity, better body mass index, reduced depression, and enhanced self-efficacy [25]. Within the context of HNC, only one study has utilized an MI-based intervention to improve nutrition and QoL among HNC patients undergoing radiation therapy. The authors reported that patients randomized to the intervention group were significantly better nourished, had fewer treatment interruptions, lower depression scores, and a higher QoL [26].

A dearth of clinical trial research examines the combined use of TSSPs and MI in HNC survivorship care [27–29]. Systematic reviews have identified few high-quality HNC survivorship studies that focused on integrated behavioural approaches [27], and while evidence suggests survivorship care plans (SCP) are more effective when accompanied by counselling [28], existing reviews of survivorship care models have largely excluded studies combining SCPs with behavioural interventions [29]. A preliminary feasibility randomized control trial (RCT) at the Verspeeten Family Cancer Centre (VFCC) of 18 patients with HNC evaluating the implementation of a one-on-one MI counselling session with a nurse practitioner found that patients assigned to the intervention group had a 15% higher rate of physician implementation of survivorship care needs compared to the usual care group (40% versus 25%, respectively) [30].

While TSSPs are widely endorsed by organizations such as the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) and the Canadian Partnership Against Cancer (CPAC), they have produced inconsistent outcomes [31, 32]. Existing survivorship research has largely overlooked the implementation context, failing to examine whether these interventions are feasible and scalable within real-world clinical settings. A recent scoping review found that survivorship interventions are rarely co-designed with patients or embedded within systems of care, which undermines their uptake and sustainability [29].

This RCT seeks to address these gaps by evaluating an integrated intervention combining TSSPs with a single-session MI approach, tailored specifically to the needs of HNC survivors. The study will assess implementation feasibility, acceptability, and effects on survivorship outcomes such as care plan adherence, satisfaction with care, and QoL. By focusing on a high-need, under-researched population within a tertiary care setting, this study has the potential to inform both practice and policy, setting the stage for scalable, survivor-centered models of care.

Methods/design

This study has been approved by the Western Health Sciences Research Ethics Board (REB# 125483), in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration. Research data will be stored on a password-protected computer on a secure hospital server, and a master list containing personal identifiers will be stored on REDCap, an online platform on a secure research institute server. The principal investigator, co-investigators, and institutional REB will have access to the data.

Study aims and hypotheses

The primary aim of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness of providing a personalized TSSP coupled with a one-on-one MI counselling session with HNC survivors to improve the implementation of recommendations compared to the usual standard of care. The TSSP will be developed for each patient in the intervention group in collaboration with a nurse practitioner who has been trained in MI.

The secondary aims of this study are to assess patient satisfaction with the delivery of the intervention, evaluate healthcare provider satisfaction with the TSSP, assess the effectiveness of the intervention to improve QoL and satisfaction with post-treatment care at 12 months compared to the usual care group, and examine longitudinal trajectories of survivorship symptom/concern implementation across the follow-up period.

We hypothesize that compared to the usual care group, the intervention group will report a statistically significant (1) higher rate of implementation of recommendations for patient’s needs, (2) improvement in QoL, and (3) improvement in satisfaction with post-treatment care from baseline to 12 months post-baseline. We hypothesize that patients and physicians will express satisfaction with the intervention.

Trial design

This study is a prospective, single-centre, two-arm, superiority randomized controlled trial (RCT) involving patients with a diagnosis of stage I–IVA HNC. A research assistant will randomly assign patients (1:1) to either the usual treatment or the intervention (TSSP and MI counselling session) group. The randomization allocation sequence will follow simple computer-generated random numbers. Participants will be randomly allocated to study groups immediately after providing informed consent and prior to completing baseline assessments. This is an open-label trial; blinding of participants, intervention providers, outcome assessors, or statisticians is not feasible due to the nature of the behavioural intervention. Eligible patients will be enrolled 3–6 months following completion of definitive HNC treatment. Data will be collected at four time points: baseline (first study visit), and 3, 6, and 12 months post-baseline (Fig. 1). The schedule of enrollment, interventions, and assessments is outlined in the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) figure (Fig. 2) [33].

Recruitment

Participants will be recruited through the Survivorship Clinic at the Multidisciplinary Head and Neck Cancer Clinic located at Victoria Hospital, London, Ontario, Canada. Patients deemed eligible for participation will be invited to enroll in the study, and informed consent will be obtained by a trained research assistant.

Treatment

Usual care

Patients assigned to the usual care group will receive treatment as usual, making no changes to any part of their clinical care. These patients will not meet with the nurse practitioner trained in MI, other than during the course of routine clinical care.

Usual clinical care for patients with HNC routinely includes undergoing physical exams, history taking at their visits, and a series of diagnostic tests including biopsy and imaging, where appropriate. Patients attend follow-up clinic appointments at a minimum of every 3–6 months and follow up with their health care providers on an as needed basis.

Intervention

Training

The nurse practitioner will undergo extensive MI training with an MI-trained PhD in the health sciences, who is also a Certified Professional Life Coach. Training will take the form of an extensive workshop-delivery experience for health providers. The interactive workshop will provide a model for health promotion and health behaviour change and will provide practical tools for working with patients with HNC for promoting health-related behaviour changes [34].

Treatment Summary and Survivorship Care Plan (TSSP)

We have developed a TSSP specific to HNC patients at the Verspeeten Family Cancer Centre (VFCC), London, Canada, through consultation with our HNC survivorship committee. The survivorship committee consists of our head and neck multidisciplinary team, patients, and their caregivers. This study and the TSSP have also been approved by the VFCC head and neck disease site team. The essential elements of the TSSP have been well delineated [35, 36]. The TSSPs for this study have two sections: (1) a survivorship care plan with individualized and general recommendations for follow-up HNC care for both the patient and all physicians involved in the patient’s care, and (2) the HNC treatment summary, including surgery type, chemotherapy received, and radiation therapy received.

Motivational Interview (MI) sessions

Each MI session will be a one-on-one, 45-minute meeting between the nurse practitioner and the patient. The four core principles of MI will be embedded into the session including the following: expressing empathy, developing discrepancy, rolling with resistance, and supporting self-efficacy [34]. The nurse practitioner will guide the patient through identifying personal survivorship concerns/goals, exploring ambivalence, formulating actionable plans, and providing resources to the patient.

Implementation of TSSPs and MI sessions

A research assistant will develop the individualized TSSP in consultation with the nurse practitioner. Individualized TSSPs will be developed from information abstracted from three sources—medical records (for the treatment summary), and information collected from interviews with patients and caregivers that comprehensively solicit individual survivorship needs and concerns. TSSPs will then be specified for each individual patient and distributed to the patient, the treating oncology team, and the PCP.

Following completion of the baseline questionnaires, patients assigned to the intervention group will undergo the intervention. The intervention will consist of a survivorship nurse practitioner-delivered MI counselling session.

Participants will be coached to identify and write down their three most important survivorship symptoms/concerns that they want to discuss with their physician, as a means of increasing empowerment in communication with their physicians [37–40]. The MI session will probe at what is important to each patient about having these discussions and having their survivorship care recommendations implemented. Furthermore, the session will aim to help patients deepen their motivation and commitment to bringing these topics up with their physician. Participants will then be invited to practice role playing these discussions. Some time following the intervention, patients will undergo their follow-up visits with their health care provider.

Safety and harms

This study involves a non-invasive behavioural intervention (MI and TSSP); no adverse physical events are anticipated. However, any unanticipated distress or psychological discomfort reported by participants during or following the intervention will be documented and reported to the PI.

Measures

Clinical and demographic characteristics

At baseline, participants will complete a demographic information form to record: age (continuous, in years); race/ethnicity (White, Black, Asian, Hispanic, Latino, Indigenous, other, prefer not to say); education level (some high school, high school, bachelor’s degree, master’s degree, Ph.D. or higher, trade school, other, prefer not to say); employment status (employed full-time, employed part-time, self-employed, unemployed, retired, other, prefer not to say); and marital status (single, married, common-law, other). Smoking history will be assessed, including current smoking status (yes, no, previously), amount smoked per day (packs or cigarettes), and duration of smoking (years) to calculate pack-years. Alcohol intake will be recorded as the number of alcoholic drinks consumed per week. Clinical information retrieved from patient charts will include the following: tumor characteristics (site, stage, pathology, HPV/EBV status, laterality), pathological features (margins, perineural invasion, lymphovascular invasion, extranodal extension), and treatment details (surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, immunotherapy with dates, doses, and regimens).

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is the proportion of patient-identified survivorship symptoms/concerns addressed by the patient’s PCP at 12 months post-baseline. At baseline, all patients will identify their top 3 survivorship symptoms/concerns via an investigator-developed questionnaire administered online through the London Health Sciences Centre Research Institute (LHSCRI) REDCap platform. At 3-, 6-, and 12-month follow-up visits, patients will complete questionnaires assessing whether each symptom/concern was addressed by their PCP (yes/no/no longer a concern). For each patient, the proportion of symptoms/concerns addressed will be calculated as follows: [(number of symptoms or concerns addressed/3) × 100]. The primary analysis will compare mean proportions between study groups at 12 months post-baseline.

Sample size

Our study sample size calculation is based on the primary study outcome, physician implementation of survivorship recommendations. Parameters for the sample size calculation are based on our feasibility clinical trial of 18 patients with HNC (9 in the intervention group and 9 in the control group) [27]. Based on a power calculation using G*Power 3.1.9.7, a total sample size of 176 (88 per study arm) will be required to provide 80% power to detect an effect size of 0.43 [mean (SD) of intervention group: 40.44 (32.30); mean (SD) of control group: 25.78 (36.32)].

At the VFCC, approximately 675 new HNC patients are seen within an 18-month period (450 per year). Of these, we anticipate 408 (60.5%) would be eligible to participate, 314 patients would agree to participate, and 252 would be recruited. Additionally, we would anticipate a 10% drop-out rate at each follow-up visit due to non-compliance with treatment, death from treatment, or palliative care, culminating in a 30% drop-out rate across all study time points. As such, we should be able to reasonably recruit 252 patients over an 18-month period, and it is expected that 176 patients will complete all study-related activities (88 per arm).

Statistical analyses

Descriptive summary statistics including mean, median, standard deviations, and frequency statistics will be calculated for demographic and clinical characteristics, scores on the MDASI-HN, Satisfaction with Care and Information Scale, and investigator-developed questionnaires, and physician-completed surveys. Chi-square or Fisher exact tests and t-tests will be conducted to compare baseline differences between the intervention and usual care groups on study outcomes. An intention-to-treat approach will be used for the primary analysis comparing the two groups. The primary analysis will use an independent samples t-test to compare the mean proportion of survivorship symptoms/concerns addressed by PCPs between the intervention and control groups at 12 months post-baseline. Secondary analyses will include two-group t-tests at 3- and 6-month timepoints, and a mixed-effects regression analysis to evaluate longitudinal trajectories of symptoms/concerns implementation across all timepoints, adjusting for baseline patient characteristics. For symptoms/concerns identified as addressed by PCPs, mean scores for extent of management and patient satisfaction will be compared between study groups using t-tests. An interim analysis of study outcomes will be conducted once 100 participants are recruited (50 in each group) to the study and have undergone the 12-month follow-up to assess for statistical power. If a high enough statistical power is reached (80%) by 12 months, the study team will stop recruiting further participants to the study. Data will be analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 30 [44]. An alpha level of 0.05 will be used to determine statistical significance. Study results will be published in an academic peer-reviewed otolaryngology journal.

Discussion

This study has the potential to greatly improve the care of HNC survivors and address the unmet needs of HNC patients. We expect that when patients are given information about their survivorship issues or symptoms, and when they are given the tools to empower them to speak to their physicians about their concerns, more recommendations will be implemented. We expect that the results will show that a combined TSSP and MI intervention will improve patients’ health outcomes and improve patient communication with their physicians.

Only one previous study has demonstrated the effectiveness of MI when used to facilitate behavioural change within the HNC setting. Building on the work of Britton et al. [26], our study seeks to provide further evidence for the utility of structured TSSPs in addition to one-on-one MI sessions to improve satisfaction with care and QoL among HNC survivors.

Several strengths of this study include its randomized controlled design, integration of patient-centered interventions, and focus on a population with significant unmet needs. However, our study is not without limitations which may include potential variability in MI session delivery and the generalizability of findings beyond the study setting.

Our study results could inform best practices for survivorship care in HNC patients for the use of TSSPs and MI counselling sessions. Additionally, should the intervention prove effective, it could be adapted for other cancer populations.
