SING: SDE Inference via Natural Gradients
Amber Hu, Henry Smith, Scott W. Linderman

Abstract
Latent stochastic differential equation (SDE) models are important tools for the unsupervised discovery of dynamical systems from data, with applications ranging from engineering to neuroscience. In these complex domains, exact posterior inference of the latent state path is typically intractable, motivating the use of approximate methods such as variational inference (VI). However, existing VI methods for inference in latent SDEs often suffer from slow convergence and numerical instability. Here, we propose S DE I nference via N atural G radients (SING), a method that leverages natural gradient VI to efficiently exploit the underlying geometry of the model and variational posterior. SING enables fast and reliable inference in latent SDE models by approximating intractable integrals and parallelizing computations in time. We provide theoretical guarantees that SING will approximately optimize the intractable, continuous-time objective of interest. Moreover, we demonstrate that better state inference enables more accurate estimation of nonlinear drift functions using, for example, Gaussian process SDE models. SING outperforms prior methods in state inference and drift estimation on a variety of datasets, including a challenging application to modeling neural dynamics in freely behaving animals. Altogether, our results illustrate the potential of SING as a tool for accurate inference in complex dynamical systems, especially those characterized by limited prior knowledge and non-conjugate structure.

Introduction

Stochastic differential equations (SDEs) are powerful tools for modeling complex, time-varying systems in scientific domains such as physics, engineering, and neuroscience
[
1
,
2
]
. In many real-world settings, we do not observe the state of the system directly, but instead must infer it from noisy measurements. For example, in neuroscience, we often wish to uncover continuously evolving internal brain states underlying high-dimensional neural recordings
[
3
]
.
Latent SDE models
address this problem by positing a continuous-time stochastic latent process that generates observations through a measurement model.

A key challenge in latent SDE modeling is posterior inference over the latent state trajectory. Accurate inference enables principled understanding and prediction of the underlying state, but it is often computationally intractable to perform analytically due to non-conjugate model structure. Methods for approximating the intractable posterior include Markov chain Monte-Carlo
[
4
,
5
]
, approximate Bayesian smoothing
[
2
,
6
]
, and variational inference (VI)
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
. In particular, VI offers a flexible and scalable framework for approximate inference in latent SDE models. In their seminal work,
Archambeau et al. 2007a
,
Archambeau et al. 2007b
formulate the VI problem for latent SDEs as constrained optimization over the family of linear, time-varying SDEs. While variants of this approach have been widely employed
[
9
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
, they often suffer from slow convergence and numerical instability. These shortcomings motivate the need for more efficient and robust VI methods in this setting.

In this work, we introduce SING: SDE Inference via Natural Gradients, a method that leverages natural gradient variational inference (NGVI) for latent SDE models. SING exploits the underlying geometry of the prior process and variational posterior, which leads to fast and stable latent state inference, even in challenging models with highly nonlinear dynamics and/or non-conjugate observation models. Our contributions are as follows:

(i)

We derive natural gradient updates for latent SDE models and introduce methods for computing them efficiently;

(ii)

We show how SING can be parallelized over time, enabling scalability to long sequences;

(iii)

We provide theoretical guarantees that SING approximately optimizes the continuous-time objective of interest; and

(iv)

We demonstrate that improved inference with SING enables more accurate drift estimation, including in models with Gaussian process drifts.

Background and problem statement

2.1
Latent stochastic differential equation models

We consider the class of models in which the latent states evolve according a stochastic differential equation (SDE) and give rise to conditionally independent observations. The prior on the latent states
\bm{x}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
takes the form

p(\bm{x}):d\bm{x}(t)=\bm{f}(\bm{x}(t))dt+\bm{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}d\bm{w}(t),\quad\bm{x}(0)\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\nu},\bm{V}),\quad 0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}

(1)

where
\bm{f}:\mdmathbb{R}^{D}\to\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
is the drift function describing the system dynamics,
\bm{\Sigma}
is a time-homogeneous noise covariance matrix, and
(\bm{w}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}}
is a standard
D
-dimensional Brownian motion.
\bm{\nu}
and
\bm{V}
are the mean and covariance of the initial state. For our exposition, we will model
\bm{f}
as a deterministic drift function with parameters
\bm{\theta}
. We will subsequently expand our scope in
section

3.5
to the case of a Gaussian process prior on
\bm{f}
. For simplicity of presentation, we assume
\bm{f}
is time-homogeneous; however, our framework can be readily extended to the time-inhomogeneous case as well.

While the latent trajectory
(\bm{x}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}}
is unobserved, we observe data
\mathcal{D}=\{t_{i},\bm{y}(t_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where
t_{i}\in[0,T_{\text{max}}]
are observation times and
\bm{y}(t_{i})\in\mdmathbb{R}^{N}
are corresponding observations modeled as

\mdmathbb{E}[\bm{y}(t_{i})~|~\bm{x}]=g\left(\bm{C}\bm{x}(t_{i})+\bm{d}\right).

(2)

Here,
g(\cdot)
is a pre-determined inverse link function and
\{\bm{C},\bm{d}\}
are learnable hyperparameters defining an affine mapping from latent to observed space. Of particular interest is the setting
D\ll N
, where the latent space is much lower-dimensional than the observations.

For the latent SDE model, we aim to solve two problems: (i) inferring a posterior over the latent trajectories
\bm{x}
, conditional on the data
\mathcal{D}
and (ii) learning the prior and output hyperparameters, collectively denoted by
\bm{\Theta}=\{\bm{\theta},\bm{C},\bm{d}\}
.

2.2
Natural gradient variational inference for exponential family models

SING leverages natural gradient variational inference (NGVI) to perform approximate posterior inference in latent SDE models. Before presenting our method, we first provide relevant background on NGVI for exponential families. In this section, we will consider the general VI problem in which we have a variational posterior
\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta})
with parameters
\bm{\eta}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{p}
. The goal of VI is to maximize a lower bound
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\eta})
to the marginal log likelihood
[
16
,
17
]
.

One way to maximize
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\eta})
is via natural gradient ascent
[
18
]
with respect to
\bm{\eta}
, which adjusts the standard gradient update to account for the geometry of the distribution space. In practice, this corresponds to the update rule

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}^{(j+1)}=\argmin_{\bm{\eta}}-\bm{\eta}^{\top}\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\eta}^{(j)})+\frac{1}{\rho}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\eta}-\bm{\eta}^{(j)})^{\top}\mathcal{F}(\bm{\eta}^{(j)})(\bm{\eta}-\bm{\eta}^{(j)})}_{\approx\textrm{KL}\left(\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}^{(j)})\;\|\;\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta})\right)}

(3)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}^{(j+1)}=\bm{\eta}^{(j)}+\rho[\mathcal{F}(\bm{\eta}^{(j)})]^{-1}\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\eta}^{(j)}),

(4)

where
\bm{\eta}^{(j)}
denotes the variational parameter at iteration
j
,
\rho
denotes the step size, and
\mathcal{F}(\bm{\eta})=\mdmathbb{E}_{\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta})}\left[\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}\log\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta})\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}\log\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta})^{\top}\right]
is the Fisher information matrix. The key idea of natural gradient methods
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
is to perform an update that both increases the ELBO and remains close to the current variational distribution (in KL distance). This interpretation relies upon a second-order Taylor expansion of the KL divergence, as denoted in
eq.

3
; we provide a derivation in
section

B.1
.

However, the inverse Fisher information in the update
eq.

4
is intractable to compute when
\bm{\eta}
is high-dimensional. This is common in models with temporal structure, including the latent SDE model presented in
section

2.1
. The key insight behind NGVI
[
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
is that
eq.

4
can be computed efficiently when
\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta})
constitutes a minimal exponential family:

\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta})=h(\bm{z})\exp\left(\left<\bm{\eta},\bm{T}(\bm{z})\right>-A(\bm{\eta})\right),\quad A(\bm{\eta})=\log\left(\int h(\bm{z})\exp\left(\left<\bm{\eta},\bm{T}(\bm{z})\right>\right)d\bm{z}\right).

(5)

Here,
\bm{\eta}
are called the natural parameters of the exponential family,
\bm{T}(\bm{z})
are the sufficient statistics,
\bm{\mu}=\mdmathbb{E}_{\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta})}[\bm{T}(\bm{z})]
are the mean parameters, and
A(\bm{\eta})
is the log-normalizer. The exponential family density
eq.

5
is defined for all
\bm{\eta}
for which
A(\bm{\eta})<\infty
. We refer the reader to
appendix

B
for an overview of exponential families.
Let
\bm{\mu}(\bm{\eta}):=\mdmathbb{E}_{\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta})}[\bm{T}(\bm{z})]
be the bijective mapping from natural to mean parameters. Using the exponential family identities
\mathcal{F}(\bm{\eta})=\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\eta}}A(\bm{\eta})=\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}\bm{\mu}(\bm{\eta})
, which we derive in
appendix

B
,
eq.

4
can be written as

\bm{\eta}^{(j+1)}=\bm{\eta}^{(j)}+\rho\nabla_{\bm{\mu}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\mu}^{(j)}).

(6)

This reparametrization avoids the need to compute or invert a large Fisher information matrix, allowing for tractable updates even for high-dimensional parameter spaces.

2.3
Variational inference in latent SDE models

Figure 1:
A latent SDE gives rise to conditionally independent observations. We use VI to approximate the intractable true posterior on latent variables with a time-varying linear SDE. To optimize the variational posterior, we marginalize both the prior and variational posterior to a time grid
\bm{\tau}
and replace the prior SDE with its Euler-Maruyama discretization (‘discretize’). On
\bm{\tau}
, the variational posterior is a multivariate normal distribution with block tridiagonal precision matrix.

In the latent SDE model in
section

2.1
, one can show that the true posterior
p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{D})
is a time-varying SDE with the same diffusion coefficient as the prior:

\displaystyle p(\bm{x}~|~\mathcal{D}):d\bm{x}(t)=\tilde{\bm{f}}(\bm{x}(t),t|\mathcal{D})dt+\bm{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}d\bm{w}(t),\quad\bm{x}(0)\sim p_{\mathcal{D}},\quad 0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}.

The drift
\tilde{\bm{f}}
of the posterior SDE is the solution to a linear partial differential equation (PDE)
[
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
]
. In the case where the prior
eq.

1
is a linear SDE and the observation model
eq.

2
is linear and Gaussian, both
\tilde{\bm{f}}
and the marginal density of the posterior
p(\bm{x}_{t}|\mathcal{D})
can be computed via a Kalman-Bucy smoother
[
32
]
. However, in non-conjugate settings, neither quantity admits a closed-form, and solving for them numerically using PDE solvers can be computationally expensive.

We propose a VI method for approximate inference on the latent trajectories
(\bm{x}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}}
. As in prior works
[
7
,
8
]
, we posit a variational family
q(\bm{x})
of linear time-varying SDEs,

q(\bm{x}):d\bm{x}(t)=(\bm{A}(t)\bm{x}(t)+\bm{b}(t))dt+\bm{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}d\bm{w}(t),\quad\bm{x}(0)\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}_{0},\bm{S}_{0}),\quad 0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}},

(7)

where the variational parameters are
(\bm{A}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}}
and
(\bm{b}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}}
. In other words, we are approximating the posterior drift
\tilde{\bm{f}}
with a linear function
\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x}(t),t):=\bm{A}(t)\bm{x}(t)+\bm{b}(t)
at each time
t
.
This yields the following evidence lower bound (ELBO) to the marginal log likelihood:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{cont}}(q,\bm{\Theta}):=\mdmathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\bm{y}|\bm{x},\bm{\Theta})]-\textrm{KL}\left(q(\bm{x})\;\|\;p(\bm{x}|\bm{\Theta})\right).

(8)

Our goal is to maximize
\mathcal{L}_{\text{cont}}(q,\bm{\Theta})
with respect to
q
, given
\bm{\Theta}
fixed. However, this objective is typically intractable to optimize directly. In practice, a common approach is to instead perform inference over a finite-dimensional distribution
q(\bm{x}_{0:T})
, where
\bm{x}_{i}:=\bm{x}(\tau_{i})
and
\bm{\tau}=\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=0}^{T}\subseteq[0,T_{\text{max}}],\ \tau_{0}=0,\ \tau_{T}=T_{\text{max}}
is a set of time points that includes observation times
\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

When the prior SDE
p(\bm{x})
is nonlinear, the corresponding finite-dimensional distribution
p(\bm{x}_{0:T})
does not have a closed-form expression. Therefore, we approximate
p
with its Euler-Maruyama discretization, which we call
\tilde{p}
. Since both
\tilde{p}
and
q
have Gaussian transition densities, this leads to a tractable approximation to
\mathcal{L}_{\text{cont}}(q,\bm{\Theta})
,

\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx}}(q,\bm{\Theta}):=\mdmathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\bm{y}|\bm{x},\bm{\Theta})]-\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log\frac{q(\bm{x}_{0:T})}{\tilde{p}(\bm{x}_{0:T}|\bm{\Theta})}\right].

(9)

We provide a schematic of the generative model and ELBO approximations in
fig.

1
. For derivations of the ELBOs
\mathcal{L}_{\text{cont}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx}}
, see Appendix
C
.

3
SING: SDE Inference via Natural Gradients

3.1
NGVI for latent SDE models

We propose SING, a method that uses NGVI to optimize the ELBO
\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx}}
in the latent SDE model. SING can be applied to any combination of prior drift and likelihood model and enjoys fast convergence by exploiting the geometry of the natural parameter space. SING builds upon recent work from
Verma et al. 2024
; however, it has several notable differences that we detail in Appendix
I
. We provide a general implementation of SING at:
https://github.com/lindermanlab/sing
.

We begin by recognizing that since
eq.

7
is Gaussian and Markovian, its marginalized distribution
q(\bm{x}_{0:T})
can be written as a minimal exponential family,

q(\bm{x}_{0:T}|\bm{\eta})=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{T}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{J}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}-\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\bm{x}_{i+1}^{\top}\bm{L}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}+\sum_{i=0}^{T}\bm{h}_{i}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}-A(\bm{\eta})\right).

(10)

In
eq.

10
, the natural parameters are
\bm{\eta}=\left[\{\bm{h}_{i},-\frac{1}{2}\bm{J}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{T},\{-\bm{L}_{i}^{\top}\}_{i=0}^{T-1}\right]
and the corresponding sufficient statistics are
\bm{T}(\bm{x}_{0:T})=\left[\{\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\}_{i=0}^{T},\{\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i+1}^{\top}\}_{i=0}^{T-1}\right]
. Since
q(\bm{x}_{0:T})
is Markovian, its full precision matrix is block tridiagonal, as depicted in
fig.

1
.

To apply the NGVI update
eq.

6
to
\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx}}
, we must first consider
q(\bm{x}_{0:T})
in terms of its mean parameters, which are simply the expectations of
\bm{T}(\bm{x}_{0:T})
under
q
. We denote these together as
\bm{\mu}:=\left[\{\bm{\mu}_{i,1},\bm{\mu}_{i,2}\}_{i=0}^{T},\{\bm{\mu}_{i,3}\}_{i=0}^{T-1}\right]
. From here,
eq.

6
simplifies to the following updates on the natural parameters, which decompose over time steps
i=0,\dots,T
:

\begin{aligned} (\bm{h}_{i}^{(j+1)},\bm{J}_{i}^{(j+1)})&=(1-\rho)(\bm{h}_{i}^{(j)},\bm{J}_{i}^{(j)})+\rho\nabla_{(\bm{\mu}_{i,1},\bm{\mu}_{i,2})}\mdmathbb{E}_{q^{(j)}}\left[\log p(\bm{y}_{i}|\bm{x}_{i})\delta_{i}+\log\tilde{p}(\bm{x}_{i+1},\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{x}_{i-1})\right]\\
\bm{L}_{i}^{(j+1)}&=(1-\rho)\bm{L}_{i}^{(j)}+\rho\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{i,3}}\mdmathbb{E}_{q^{(j)}}[\log\tilde{p}(\bm{x}_{i+1}|\bm{x}_{i})].\end{aligned}

(11)

In
eq.

11
,
q^{(j)}:=q(\bm{x}_{0:T}|\bm{\eta}^{(j)})
is the variational posterior at iteration
j
and
\delta_{i}
is an indicator denoting whether there is an observation at time
\tau_{i}
. We provide a derivation in
appendix

D
.

As written, the updates in
eq.

11
are challenging to compute because they involve (i) approximating intractable expectations and (ii) converting from natural to mean parameters between iterations. In the subsequent sections, we propose computational methods to address each of these problems.

3.2
ELBO approximation guarantee

Prior to detailing how we compute the SING updates
eq.

11
, we provide theoretical justification that maximizing the ELBO
\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx}}
will approximately maximize the true, continuous-time ELBO of interest
\mathcal{L}_{\text{cont}}
.
Moreover, on a subset of the variational parameter space, the approximation error tends to zero uniformly as the size of the grid
\bm{\tau}
tends to zero.

Theorem 1
.

Under mild regularity conditions (see
appendix

E
), there exists a constant
C
such that for any mesh size
\Delta t:=\max_{i=0}^{T-1}\Delta_{i},\ \Delta_{i}=\tau_{i+1}-\tau_{i}
of the time grid,
|\mathcal{L}_{\text{cont}}-\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx}}|\leq C(\Delta t)^{1/2}
.

Proof sketch.
One compares the continuous- and discrete-time log-densities and applies error bounds on the Euler–Maruyama approximation. The full statement and proof can be found in
appendix

E
.

To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first in the topic of variational inference for latent SDE models to provide an explicit rate of convergence for the discrete time ELBO to its continuous counterpart.

3.3
Computing expectations of prior transition densities

The updates in
eq.

11
rely on computing expectations of the form
\mdmathbb{E}_{q}[\log\tilde{p}(\bm{x}_{i+1}|\bm{x}_{i})]
. This is difficult in general because the prior drift
\bm{f}(\bm{x}(t))
can be nonlinear and the expectation is taken over the pairwise distribution
q(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{i+1})
, which has total dimension
\mdmathbb{R}^{2D}
. In the following proposition, we show using Stein’s lemma that we can rewrite the expectation over just
q(\bm{x}_{i})
, which reduces the integration dimensionality to
\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
. The proof can be found in
appendix

F
.

Proposition 1
.

The term
\mdmathbb{E}_{q}[\log\tilde{p}(\bm{x}_{i+1}|\bm{x}_{i})]
can equivalently be written in terms of the mean parameters
\bm{\mu}_{i}
,
\bm{\mu}_{i+1}
and the expectations
\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left[\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i})\right],\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left[\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i})^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i})\right]
, and
\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left[\bm{J}_{\bm{f}}(\bm{x}_{i})\right]
, where
\bm{J}_{\bm{f}}(\bm{x}_{i})
denotes the Jacobian of
\bm{f}(\cdot)
at
\bm{x}_{i}
.

The key implication of
proposition

1
is that the difficult expectations over the nonlinear prior transition terms can be reduced in dimensionality from
\mdmathbb{R}^{2D}
to
\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
. This makes these terms in SING’s updates significantly more tractable. In practice, there are many ways to compute these expectations; here, we focus on the setting where
eq.

1
is low-dimensional and use Gauss-Hermite quadrature. For higher-dimensional systems, one could use Taylor approximations to
\bm{f}
or Monte Carlo sampling.

3.4
Parallelizing SING

In many real-world settings, sequential inference on long time series can be computationally challenging. In this section, we show how SING can be fully parallelized, yielding efficient inference that scales logarithmically with the number of time steps.

The computational bottleneck of
section

3.1
is converting from natural parameters
\bm{\eta}
to mean parameters
\bm{\mu}
for the updates in
eq.

11
. By the identity
\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}A(\bm{\eta})=\bm{\mu}
, we can perform this conversion by first computing
A(\bm{\eta})
and then applying autodifferentiation with respect to
\bm{\eta}
. However, existing algorithms for this problem take
\mathcal{O}(D^{3}T)
time (see
section

G.1
for further details). While
D
is typically small in our setting, the linear scaling in
T
may be prohibitive for long sequences.

Our key insight is that the log-normalizer
A(\bm{\eta})
of
eq.

10
can be computed in parallel time by using associative scans
[
34
]
. Given a binary associative operator
\bullet
and a sequence
(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})
, the associative scan computes the prefix sum
(a_{1},(a_{1}\bullet a_{2}),\dots,(a_{1}\bullet a_{2}\bullet\dots\bullet a_{n}))
in logarithmic time using a divide-and-conquer approach. By recognizing the log-normalizer as

A(\bm{\eta})=\log\displaystyle\int\underbrace{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}_{0}^{\top}\bm{J}_{0}\bm{x}_{0}+\bm{h}_{0}^{\top}\bm{x}_{0}\right)}_{\displaystyle:=a_{-1,0}}\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{T}\underbrace{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{J}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{L}_{i-1}\bm{x}_{i-1}+\bm{h}_{i}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}\right)}_{\displaystyle:=a_{i-1,i}}\,d\bm{x}_{0:T}

(12)

we can apply the associative scan to compute
A(\bm{\eta})
. We define the unnormalized Gaussian potentials in
eq.

12
as the sequence elements
a_{i-1,i}
and marginalize out one variable at a time via the binary associative operator
a_{i,j}\bullet a_{j,k}=\int a_{i,j}a_{j,k}d\bm{x}_{j}:=a_{i,k}
. To marginalize out the variable
\bm{x}_{T}
, we also define the dummy element
a_{T,T+1}=1
. As a result, the log of the full scan
(a_{-1,0}\bullet a_{0,1}\bullet\dots\bullet a_{T,T+1})
evaluates exactly to
A(\bm{\eta})
. This algorithm has time complexity
\mathcal{O}(D^{3}\log T)
, resulting in significant speedups as sequence length increases (
fig.

4
). In
section

G.2
, we derive the analytical expression for the Gaussian marginalization operator and analyze our algorithm’s time complexity.

3.5
SING-GP: Drift estimation with Gaussian process priors

So far, we have assumed that the drift function
\bm{f}(\bm{x}(t)|\bm{\Theta})
is deterministic with learnable hyperparameters
\bm{\theta}
. However, in practice, we often have limited prior knowledge about the functional form of the drift but would still like to learn or infer its structure. A natural approach for this problem is to place a Gaussian process (GP)
[
35
]
prior over the drift,

\displaystyle f_{d}(\cdot)\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{GP}(0,\kappa_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot,\cdot)),\quad\text{for }d=1,\dots,D,

where
\bm{f}(\cdot)=\begin{bmatrix}f_{1}(\cdot),\dots,f_{D}(\cdot)\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
,
\kappa_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a kernel function, and
\bm{\theta}
are kernel hyperparameters. This results in a well-studied model called the GP-SDE
[
13
,
14
]
, which is appealing for its ability to encode rich prior structure in dynamics and provide posterior uncertainty estimates. However, the practical utility of GP-SDEs has been limited by the inference methods used to fit them
[
7
,
8
]
, which we show in
section

5.2
often exhibit slow convergence and numerical instability.

Here, we extend SING to enable accurate and reliable inference in GP-SDE models, resulting in a new method called SING-GP. While we defer technical details to
appendix

K
, we outline the key ideas here. Following
Duncker et al. 2019
, we introduce an augmented variational posterior of the form
q(\bm{x}_{0:T},\bm{f})
and use sparse GP techniques
[
36
]
to perform approximate inference over
q(\bm{f})
. This leads to convenient closed-form updates on
q(\bm{f})
. In
section

5.2
, we illustrate that SING-GP performs competitively to flexible parametric drift functions in low-data regimes, while also directly providing posterior uncertainty estimates on the drift. In
section

5.4
, we then use SING-GP on a challenging application of modeling latent neural dynamics in freely moving animals.

4
Related work

VI for latent SDE models
A number of VI methods for latent SDEs posit larger variational families than
eq.

7
, consisting of non-Gaussian diffusion processes
[
10
,
11
,
37
,
38
]
.
Wildner and Koeppl 2021
consider the variational family composed of controlled versions of the prior SDE.
Ryder et al. 2018
parameterize the variational family as the collection of diffusion processes with neural network drift and diffusion coefficients. Although more flexible, each of these methods is statistically less efficient than those that assume a smaller variational family.
Li et al. 2020
introduce a stochastic adjoint method to optimize a continuous
-
time ELBO corresponding to a general variational family. While memory efficient, the stochastic adjoint method requires sampling from the variational posterior at each gradient step.

In settings where statistical and computational efficiency are critical, many works have studied Gaussian diffusion process variational families of the form
eq.

7
. In their seminal work,
Archambeau et al. 2007a
,
Archambeau et al. 2007b
propose an algorithm VI for Diffusion Processes (VDP), which optimizes the continuous-time ELBO in
eq.

8
via Lagrange multipliers by incorporating the evolution of the marginal mean and covariance of
q(\bm{x}(t))
as constraints. A detailed overview of VDP is provided in
appendix

H
.
Duncker et al. 2019
use sparse GP approximations to extend VDP to GP-SDE models, a method we refer to as ‘VDP-GP’. Related to VDP,
Course and Nair 2023a
,
Course and Nair 2023b
express the continuous-time ELBO entirely in terms of the marginal mean and covariance and then approximate these functions using a neural network encoder-decoder architecture.

Most relevant to our work,
Verma et al. 2024
propose applying NGVI to the discrete-time ELBO from
eq.

9
in the case of a deterministic drift. Unlike SING,
Verma et al. 2024
do not address how to tractably compute
\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx}}
, and they do not suggest how to efficiently convert from natural to mean parameters. Moreover, no theoretical guarantees are given as to how well
\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx}}
approximates
\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}
. A complete comparison is given in
appendix

I
.

Connection to GP regression problems
Several works have applied NGVI for efficient inference in GP regression problems by exploiting the fact that many GPs admit equivalent representations as linear time-invariant SDEs
[
2
,
39
]
. For instance,
Chang et al. 2020
and
Dowling et al. 2023
consider GP regression problems with non-Gaussian likelihoods. To avoid cubic scaling in the number of data points, they reformulate the GP as a linear SDE and then apply NGVI to perform approximate inference in this non-conjugate model.
Hamelijnck et al. 2021
combine this approach with sparse GP approximations to perform inference in a spatio-temporal setting. While these works focus on linear SDE representations of GPs in regression problems, SING targets estimation of both the latent process and drift and applies to the broader class of
nonlinear
SDE priors.

Parallelizing sequential models
Associative scans have been successfully applied to parallelize inherently sequential computations in areas such as classical Bayesian inference and deep sequence modeling
[
42
,
43
,
44
,
45
,
46
,
47
]
.
Särkkä and García-Fernández 2020
and
Hassan et al. 2021
derived associative scans for parallelizing Bayesian filtering and smoothing algorithms, and
Hamelijnck et al. 2021
extended these algorithms to inference in non-conjugate GP regression. We note that our associative scan in
section

3.4
is reminiscent of the one in
Hassan et al. 2021
, which operates on potentials for inference in the hidden Markov model. However, SING differs in that it leverages these techniques to compute the log-normalizer in a Gaussian Markov chain with no observations, which enables efficient parameter conversion in its natural gradient-based updates.

Next, we simulate a dataset inspired by

in the hippocampus
[

. Place cells fire selectively when an animal occupies specific locations in space, and are often modeled using location-dependent tuning curves. We simulate 30 trials of latent states from a Van der Pol oscillator to represent animal trajectories through 2D space. We then construct tuning curves for

This setup is particularly challenging due to the non-conjugacy between the nonlinear dynamics and Poisson observations. Nevertheless, SING accurately infers the underlying latent states (

6
Discussion

We introduced SING, a method for approximate posterior inference in latent SDE models that harnesses the exponential family structure of the variational posterior to achieve rapid, stable convergence. By deriving tractable NGVI updates and parallelizing the natural-to-mean parameter conversion via associative scans, SING scales to long time series and handles nonlinear dynamics along with complex observation models with ease. Although we optimize a discrete-time ELBO, our theoretical bound (
Theorem

1
) ensures that SING closely approximates the continuous-time objective. We show via extensive experiments, including an application to real neural data, that SING outperforms prior methods in inference accuracy, drift estimation, and robustness to discretization.

However, SING makes simplifying assumptions that suggest several directions for future work. First, scaling SING’s updates to systems with higher latent dimensions will require efficient approximations, such as Monte Carlo sampling or approximations to the drift, that we have not yet explored empirically. Second, rather than fixing a discretization
\bm{\tau}
of
[0,T_{\text{max}}]
a priori, one could consider adaptively learning locations at which to discretize inference in order to better approximate rapidly changing dynamics. Nevertheless, our results already demonstrate SING’s effectiveness in a range of practical settings. Altogether, SING is positioned to be a reliable and broadly applicable tool for facilitating scientific discovery in complex dynamical systems.

Appendix A
Technical assumptions

Here, we present technical conditions that we assume throughout our paper. We consider probability measures defined on the measurable space
(C[0,T_{\text{max}}]^{D},\mathcal{B}_{C[0,T_{\text{max}}]^{D}})
, where
C[0,T_{\text{max}}]^{D}
is the Banach space of continuous functions endowed with the supremum norm.

In order to ensure the SDEs
eq.

1
and
eq.

7
admit unique, strong solutions, we will assume
\bm{f}
and
\bm{f}_{q}
satisfy the standard Lipschitz and linear growth conditions:

Assumption (Drift Regularity):
There exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}\geq 0
such that for all
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
and
t,s\in[0,T_{\max}]
:

\displaystyle\|\bm{f}(\bm{x},t)-\bm{f}(\bm{y},s)\|\;\leq\;C_{1}\bigl(\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|+|t-s|\bigr),\quad\|\bm{f}(\bm{x},t)\|\;\leq\;C_{2}\bigl(1+\|\bm{x}\|\bigr),

and the same bounds hold for
\bm{f}_{q}
.

In addition, we will assume all SDEs are sufficiently regular such that Girsanov’s Theorem (
Oksendal 2013
, Theorem 8.6.4) can be applied. See, for example, Novikov’s criterion. For the sake of completeness, we restate this result below:

Theorem 2

(Girsanov’s Theorem for SDEs)
.

Suppose the SDEs

\displaystyle\nu_{1}:d\bm{x}(t)=b_{1}(\bm{x}(t),t)dt+\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}d\bm{w}(t),\quad 0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}

(13)

\displaystyle\nu_{2}:d\bm{x}(t)=(b_{1}(\bm{x}(t),t)+b_{2}(\bm{x}(t),t))dt+\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}d\bm{w}(t),\quad 0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}

(14)

have the same initial law
\nu_{1}(\bm{x}(0))=\nu_{2}(\bm{x}(0))
and admit unique, strong solutions. Then
\nu_{2}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\nu_{1}
and the Radon-Nikodym density is

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{T_{\text{max}}}(\nu_{1}||\nu_{2}):=\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{T_{\text{max}}}\langle\bm{\Sigma}^{-1/2}b_{2}(\bm{x}(t),t),d\bm{w}^{\nu_{1}}(t)\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T_{\text{max}}}\|\bm{\Sigma}^{-1/2}b_{2}(\bm{x}(t),t)\|^{2}dt\right\}.

where
(\bm{w}^{\nu_{1}}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}}
is a
\nu_{1}
-Brownian motion. In particular, for any bounded functional
\Phi
defined on
C[0,T_{\text{max}}]^{D}
,

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{E}_{\nu_{2}}[\Phi(\bm{x})]=\mdmathbb{E}_{\nu_{1}}\left[\Phi(\bm{x})\mathcal{Z}_{T_{\text{max}}}(\nu_{1}||\nu_{2})\right].

In particular, Theorem
2
implies that the KL divergence between
eq.

1
and
eq.

7
is given by

\displaystyle\textrm{KL}\left(q\;\|\;p\right)

\displaystyle=\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log\left(\frac{q(\bm{x}(0))}{p(\bm{x}(0))}\mathcal{Z}_{T_{\text{max}}}(p||q)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\textrm{KL}\left(q(\bm{x}(0))\;\|\;p(\bm{x}(0))\right)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T_{\text{max}}}\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{f}(\bm{x}(t),t)-\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x}(t),t)\|_{\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}}dt.

Here, and throughout the appendix, we adopt the notation
\|\bm{z}\|_{\bm{M}}:=\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{M}\bm{z}
for
\bm{M}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{p\times p}
symmetric, positive semi-definite.

Appendix B
Exponential families and natural gradients

B.1
Overview of exponential families

Consider the collection of probability densities indexed by
\bm{\eta}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{p}

\displaystyle\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta})=h(\bm{z})\exp(\langle\bm{\eta},\bm{T}(\bm{z})\rangle-A(\bm{\eta})\rangle),\quad A(\bm{\eta})=\log\left(\int h(\bm{z})\exp(\langle\bm{\eta},\bm{T}(\bm{z})\rangle)\mu(d\bm{z})\right)

with respect to
\nu
, which we consider to be either (i)
p
-dimensional volume (i.e. Lebesgue measure) or (ii) counting tuples of
p
integers (i.e. counting measure).
1
1

1

Exponential families can be defined for an arbitrary measure
\nu
, but these are the two most common instances.

\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta})
is a well-defined probability density whenever
A(\bm{\eta})<\infty
. The set
\bm{\Xi}=\{\bm{\eta}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{p}:A(\bm{\eta})<\infty\}
is the natural parameter space of the exponential family, and
\bm{\eta}
are its natural parameters. We call
h(\bm{z})
the base measure of the exponential family;
\bm{T}(\bm{z})\in\mdmathbb{R}^{p}
are its sufficient statistics; and
A(\bm{\eta})
is its log normalizer or log partition function. Corresponding to the sufficient statistics are the mean parameters
\bm{\mu}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{p}
of the exponential family, which are simply the expectations of the sufficient statistics under the exponential family,
\bm{\mu}:=\mdmathbb{E}_{q}[\bm{T}(\bm{z})]
.

As an example, consider the
p
-dimensional multivariate normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}|\bm{\xi},\bm{\Lambda})
with unknown mean
\bm{\xi}
and unknown, positive-definite covariance
\bm{\Lambda}
. Then we can write this family in exponential family form as follows:

\displaystyle\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\xi},\bm{\Lambda})

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-p/2}|\bm{\Lambda}|^{-1/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{z}-\bm{\xi}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-p/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}\left(\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}\bm{z}\bm{z}^{\top}\right)+\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}\bm{\xi}-\frac{1}{2}\{\log|\bm{\Lambda}|+\bm{\xi}^{\top}\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}\bm{\xi}\}\right).

Let us identify
\bm{\eta}=(\bm{\eta}_{1},\bm{\eta}_{2})=(\text{vec}(\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}),\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}\bm{\xi})
as well as
h(\bm{z})=(2\pi)^{-p/2}
, where
\text{vec}(\bm{M}),\bm{M}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{p\times p}
represents the
p^{2}
-dimensional vector representation of the matrix
\bm{M}
. The natural parameter space is
\bm{\Xi}=\{\text{vec}(\bm{M}):\bm{M}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{p\times p}\ \text{symmetric, positive-definite}\}\times\mdmathbb{R}^{p}
. The sufficient statistics of the exponential family are
\bm{T}(\bm{z})=(-\frac{1}{2}\text{vec}(\bm{z}\bm{z}^{\top}),\bm{z})
and the mean parameters are
\bm{\mu}=(-\frac{1}{2}\text{vec}(\bm{\Lambda}+\bm{\xi}\bm{\xi}^{\top}),\bm{\xi})
. It remains to compute the log normalizer
A(\bm{\eta})
. In particular,

\displaystyle\log|\bm{\Lambda}|=-\log|\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}|=-\log|\bm{\eta}_{1}|

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}^{\top}\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}\bm{\xi}=\bm{\eta}_{2}^{\top}(\bm{\eta}_{1})^{-1}\bm{\eta}_{2}

which implies
A(\bm{\eta})=\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\eta}_{2}^{\top}(\bm{\eta}_{1})^{-1}\bm{\eta}_{2}-\log|\bm{\eta}_{1}|)
. Here we slightly abuse notation by treating
\bm{\eta}_{1}
interchangeably as a matrix and a vector.

This leads us to two important results regarding exponential families:

Lemma 1
.

The log normalizer
A(\bm{\eta})
is infinitely differentiable on the interior of the natural parameter space,
\text{int}(\bm{\Xi})
. Moreover, for
\bm{\eta}\in\text{int}(\bm{\Xi})
the log normalizer satisfies

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}A(\bm{\eta})=\mdmathbb{E}_{q}[\bm{T}(\bm{z})]=\bm{\mu},\quad\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}^{2}A(\bm{\eta})=\text{Cov}_{q}[\bm{T}(\bm{z})].

Proof.

A proof of the first statement can be found in
Lehmann et al. 1986
. For the second, differentiate the log normalizer twice

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}A(\bm{\eta})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}\log\left(\int h(\bm{z})\exp(\langle\bm{\eta},\bm{T}(\bm{z})\rangle)\mu(d\bm{z})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int T(\bm{z})h(\bm{z})\exp(\langle\bm{\eta},\bm{T}(\bm{z}))\mu(d\bm{z})}{\int h(\bm{z})\exp(\langle\bm{\eta},\bm{T}(\bm{z})\rangle)\mu(d\bm{z})}

\displaystyle=\int T(\bm{z})h(\bm{z})\exp(\langle\bm{\eta},\bm{T}(\bm{z})\rangle-A(\bm{\eta}))\mu(d\bm{z})

\displaystyle=\mdmathbb{E}_{q}[\bm{T}(\bm{z})]

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}^{2}A(\bm{\eta})

\displaystyle=\int\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}\left\{\bm{T}(\bm{z})h(\bm{z})\exp(\langle\bm{\eta},\bm{T}(\bm{z})\rangle-A(\bm{\eta}))\right\}\mu(d\bm{z})

\displaystyle=\mdmathbb{E}_{q}(\bm{T}(\bm{z})\bm{T}(\bm{z})^{\top})-(\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\bm{T}(\bm{z}))(\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\bm{T}(\bm{z}))^{\top}

\displaystyle=\text{Cov}_{q}[\bm{T}(\bm{z})].

∎

Define the set of mean parameters attainable by some distribution belonging to the exponential family

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}:=\left\{\bm{\mu}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{p}\ |\ \mdmathbb{E}_{\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta})}[\bm{T}(\bm{z})]=\bm{\mu}\ \text{for some}\ \bm{\eta}\in\bm{\Xi}\right\}.

Proposition
1
demonstrates that
\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}A(\bm{\eta})
maps from the interior of the natural parameter space
\bm{\Xi}
to the interior of the mean parameter space
\mathcal{M}
. Additionally, this mapping is surjective onto
\text{int}(\mathcal{M})
. A question of considerable interest is when, in addition, this map will be one-to-one (i.e. injective).

An exponential family is said to be minimal if its sufficient statistics do not satisfy any linear constraints. If a
p
-dimensional exponential family is not minimal, it can be reduced to a
(p-1)
-dimensional exponential family by rewriting one sufficient statistic as a linear combination of the others. In the multivariate normal family example, the exponential family as written is not minimal, since
\bm{z}\bm{z}^{\top}
is symmetric, and hence there are duplicate statistics along the off-diagonal. To reparameterize this exponential family in minimal form, one can define the symmetric vectorization operator
\text{svec}(\bm{M}):=(\bm{M}_{11},\sqrt{2}\bm{M}_{21},\bm{M}_{22},\sqrt{2}\bm{M}_{31},\sqrt{2}\bm{M}_{32},\bm{M}_{33},\ldots,\bm{M}_{pp})\in\mdmathbb{R}^{p(p+1)/2}
for
\bm{M}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{p\times p}
symmetric and then rewrite the multivariate normal density as

\displaystyle\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\xi},\bm{\Lambda})=(2\pi)^{-p/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\text{svec}(\bm{\Lambda}^{-1})^{\top}\text{svec}(\bm{z}\bm{z}^{\top})+\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}\bm{\xi}-\frac{1}{2}\{\log|\bm{\Lambda}|+\bm{\xi}^{\top}\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}\bm{\xi}\}\right).

The natural parameters of the minimal exponential family are
\bm{\eta}=(\bm{\eta}_{1},\bm{\eta}_{2})=(\text{svec}(\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}),\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}\bm{\xi})
. Note that in minimal exponential family form, the natural parameter space has dimension
p(p+1)/2+p
, whereas in the non-minimal form it has dimension
p^{2}+p
.

It is a well-known fact (see, for example,
Wainwright et al. 2008
) that when
\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta})
constitutes a minimal exponential family, then
\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}A(\bm{\eta})
is also one-to-one. Note that since
\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}A(\bm{\eta})
is then both surjective and one-to-one, the inverse mapping
[\nabla A]^{-1}:\text{int}(\mathcal{M})\to\text{int}(\bm{\Xi})
is well-defined.
This leads us to the following useful identity:

Lemma 2
.

Consider
\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta})
constituting a minimal exponential family. And let
\frac{d\bm{\mu}(\bm{\eta})}{d\bm{\eta}}=\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}^{2}A(\bm{\eta})
be the Jacobian of the mapping from natural to mean parameters. Then the following are equivalent:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}^{2}A(\bm{\eta})=\text{Cov}_{\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta})}\bm{T}(\bm{z})=\mathcal{F}(\bm{\eta}),\quad\bm{\eta}\in\text{int}(\bm{\Xi}),

where
\mathcal{F}(\bm{\eta})
denotes the Fisher information of the exponential family.

Proof.

The first identity is exactly the second statement in
lemma

1
. For the second, recall the identity

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\bm{\eta})=\text{Cov}_{\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta})}(\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}\log\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}))

for the Fisher information. Plugging in the density of the exponential family, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\bm{\eta})=\text{Cov}_{\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta})}[\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}\langle\bm{\eta},\bm{T}(\bm{z})\rangle]=\text{Cov}_{\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta})}[\bm{T}(\bm{z})].

∎

Note that as a consequence of the Inverse Function Theorem, we have that
for minimal exponential families, the inverse mapping
[\nabla A]^{-1}(\bm{\mu})
has Jacobian
(\text{Cov}_{\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}(\bm{\mu}))}[\bm{T}(\bm{z})])^{-1}=F(\bm{\eta}(\bm{\mu}))^{-1}
.

Finally, we give an expression for the KL divergence in an exponential family:

Lemma 3
.

Suppose
\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{1})
and
\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{2})
belong to the same
p
-dimensional exponential family with natural parameters
\bm{\eta}_{1}
and
\bm{\eta}_{2}
, respectively. Then

\displaystyle\textrm{KL}\left(\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{1})\;\|\;\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{2})\right)=\langle\bm{\eta}_{1}-\bm{\eta}_{2},\bm{\mu}_{1}\rangle-A(\bm{\eta}_{1})+A(\bm{\eta}_{2}).

Proof.

Write out the difference between the log densities of
\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{1})
and
\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{2})
. Then recognize that the contribution of the base measure
h(\bm{z})
cancels and that
\mdmathbb{E}_{q(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{1})}\bm{T}(\bm{z})=\bm{\mu}_{1}
.
∎

Recall that in
section

2.2
, we motivated natural gradient VI by claiming

\displaystyle\textrm{KL}\left(\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{1})\;\|\;\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{2})\right)\approx\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\eta}_{2}-\bm{\eta}_{1})^{\top}\mathcal{F}(\bm{\eta}_{1})(\bm{\eta}_{2}-\bm{\eta}_{1}).

We are now equipped to justify this claim by Taylor expanding
A(\bm{\eta}_{2})
about
\bm{\eta}_{1}
and invoking
lemma

3
:

\displaystyle A(\bm{\eta}_{2})=A(\bm{\eta}_{1})+\langle\bm{\eta}_{2}-\bm{\eta}_{1},\bm{\mu}_{1}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\eta}_{2}-\bm{\eta}_{1})^{\top}\mathcal{F}(\bm{\eta}_{1})(\bm{\eta}_{2}-\bm{\eta}_{1})+\mathcal{O}(\|\bm{\eta}_{2}-\bm{\eta}_{1}\|^{3})

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\underbrace{\langle\bm{\eta}_{1}-\bm{\eta}_{2},\bm{\mu}_{1}\rangle-A(\bm{\eta}_{1})+A(\bm{\eta}_{2})}_{=\textrm{KL}\left(\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{1})\;\|\;\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{2})\right)}=\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\eta}_{2}-\bm{\eta}_{1})^{\top}\mathcal{F}(\bm{\eta}_{1})(\bm{\eta}_{2}-\bm{\eta}_{1})+\mathcal{O}(\|\bm{\eta}_{2}-\bm{\eta}_{1}\|^{3}).

In the first line, we used the result
\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}A(\bm{\eta}_{1})=\bm{\mu}_{1}
from
lemma

1
as well as the result
\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}^{2}A(\bm{\eta}_{1})=\mathcal{F}(\bm{\eta}_{1})
from
lemma

2
.

B.2
Natural gradients and exponential families

Next, we discuss why the natural gradient VI update
eq.

4
simplifies to a gradient update of the ELBO
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\eta})
with respect to the mean parameters
\bm{\mu}
, rather than the natural parameters
\bm{\eta}
, when the variational posterior constitutes a minimal exponential family.

Recall (
eq.

4
) that the natural gradient VI update is

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}^{(j+1)}=\bm{\eta}^{(j)}+\rho[\mathcal{F}(\bm{\eta}^{(j)})]^{-1}\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\eta}^{(j)}).

From
lemma

2
, we have that in a minimal exponential family the Jacobian of the change-of-variable from natural to mean parameters is the Fisher information matrix
\mathcal{F}(\bm{\eta})
. This implies, by the Inverse Function Theorem, that the inverse Fisher information matrix is the Jacobian of the change-of-variable from mean to natural parameters. This allows us to rewrite the NGVI update as

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}^{(j+1)}

\displaystyle=\bm{\eta}^{(j)}+\rho[\mathcal{F}(\bm{\eta}^{(j)})]^{-1}\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\eta}^{(j)})

\displaystyle=\bm{\eta}^{(j)}+\rho\left(\frac{d\bm{\eta}(\bm{\mu})}{d\bm{\mu}}(\bm{\mu}^{(j)})\right)\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\eta}^{(j)})

\displaystyle=\bm{\eta}^{(j)}+\rho\nabla_{\bm{\mu}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\eta}^{(j)}).

In the final line, we used the chain rule. The equivalence between these two updates boils down to the fact that, in minimal exponential families, one can change between natural and mean parameters by multiplying by the Fisher information matrix.

Prior to concluding this section, we provide some helpful natural gradient identities that will enable us to compute our SING natural gradient VI updates.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose
\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{1})
and
\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{2})
belong to the same
p
-dimensional exponential family with natural parameters
\bm{\eta}_{1}
and
\bm{\eta}_{2}
, respectively. Then

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{1}}\textrm{KL}\left(\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{1})\;\|\;\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{2})\right)=\bm{\eta}_{1}-\bm{\eta}_{2}

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{1}}\mdmathbb{E}_{\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{1})}\log\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{1})=\bm{\eta}_{1}.

Proof.

Differentiating the expression for the KL divergence
lemma

3
, we obtain

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{1}}\textrm{KL}\left(\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{1})\;\|\;\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{1}}(\langle\bm{\eta}_{1}-\bm{\eta}_{2},\bm{\mu}_{1}\rangle-A(\bm{\eta}_{1})+A(\bm{\eta}_{2}))

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{d\bm{\eta}(\bm{\mu})}{d\bm{\mu}}(\bm{\mu}_{1})\right)\bm{\mu}_{1}+(\bm{\eta}_{1}-\bm{\eta}_{2})-\left(\frac{d\bm{\eta}(\bm{\mu})}{d\bm{\mu}}(\bm{\mu}_{1})\right)\underbrace{\frac{d}{d\bm{\eta}}A(\bm{\eta}_{1})}_{=\bm{\mu}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\eta}_{1}-\bm{\eta}_{2},

as claimed. As for the gradient of the negative entropy of an exponential family, we have by a nearly identical argument

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{1}}\mdmathbb{E}_{\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{1})}\log\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{1})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{1}}\left(\langle\bm{\eta}_{1},\bm{\mu}_{1}\rangle-A(\bm{\eta}_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{d\bm{\eta}(\bm{\mu})}{d\bm{\mu}}(\bm{\mu}_{1})\right)\bm{\mu}_{1}+\bm{\eta}_{1}-\left(\frac{d\bm{\eta}(\bm{\mu})}{d\bm{\mu}}(\bm{\mu}_{1})\right)\underbrace{\frac{d}{d\bm{\eta}}A(\bm{\eta}_{1})}_{=\bm{\mu}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\eta}_{1}.

∎

An important point is that while the natural gradient of the KL divergence and entropy admit simple, closed forms in an exponential family, the same is not true for the gradient with respect to the natural parameters. Differeniating the expression for the KL divergence from
lemma

3
, we obtain

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\eta}_{1}}\textrm{KL}\left(\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{1})\;\|\;\bar{q}(\bm{z}|\bm{\eta}_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{\eta}_{1}}(\langle\bm{\eta}_{1}-\bm{\eta}_{2},\bm{\mu}_{1}\rangle-A(\bm{\eta}_{1})+A(\bm{\eta}_{2}))

\displaystyle=\bm{\mu}_{1}+\left(\frac{d\bm{\mu}(\bm{\eta})}{d\bm{\eta}}(\bm{\eta}_{1})\right)(\bm{\eta}_{1}-\bm{\eta}_{2})-\bm{\mu}_{1}

\displaystyle=F(\bm{\eta}_{1})(\bm{\eta}_{1}-\bm{\eta}_{2}).

Notably, this expression requires multiplication by the Fisher information matrix, which, as we pointed out in
section

3.1
, is intractable in high-dimensional exponential families.

Appendix C
ELBO derivation

We first provide a derivation of the continuous-time ELBO,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{cont}}
. Using the notation from Theorem
2
, we have

\displaystyle\log p(\bm{y}|\bm{\Theta})=\log\int p(\bm{y}|\bm{x},\bm{\Theta})p(\bm{x}|\bm{\Theta})d\bm{x}

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{=}\log\int p(\bm{y}|\bm{x},\bm{\Theta})\mathcal{Z}_{T_{\text{max}}}(q||p)\frac{p(\bm{x}(0))}{q(\bm{x}(0))}q(\bm{x})d\bm{x}

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{\geq}\int\log\left(p(\bm{y}|\bm{x},\bm{\Theta})\mathcal{Z}_{T_{\text{max}}}(q||p)\frac{p(\bm{x}(0))}{q(\bm{x}(0))}\right)q(\bm{x})d\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\mdmathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\bm{y}|\bm{x},\bm{\Theta})]-\textrm{KL}\left(q(\bm{x})\;\|\;p(\bm{x}|\bm{\Theta})\right)

\displaystyle=\mdmathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\bm{y}|\bm{x},\bm{\Theta})]-\textrm{KL}\left(q(\bm{x}(0))\;\|\;p(\bm{x}(0))\right)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T_{\text{max}}}\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{f}(\bm{x}(t)|\bm{\Theta})-\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x}(t),t)\|_{\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}}^{2}dt.

Equality (i) follows from Girsanov’s Theorem and inequality (ii) follows from Jensen’s inequality. Here, the notation
p(\bm{x}|\bm{\Theta})d\bm{x}
is adopted for readability. Formally,
p(\bm{x}|\bm{\Theta})d\bm{x}
denotes integration with respect to the probability measure
p(\bm{x}|\bm{\Theta})
over paths
C[0,T_{\text{max}}]^{D}
.

Note that if the parameters of
p(\bm{x}(0))=\mathcal{N}(\bm{\nu},\bm{V})
are learned, then during the learning step one will set
p(\bm{x}(0))=q(\bm{x}(0))
, and the second term of the ELBO disappears.

Next, for the discrete-time ELBO, replacing the path measures
q(\bm{x})
and
p(\bm{x})
with
q(\bm{x}_{0:T})
and
p(\bm{x}_{0:T})
, respectively, and following an identical argument yields the lower bound on the marginal log likelihood

\displaystyle\log p(\bm{y}|\bm{\Theta})\geq\mdmathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\bm{y}|\bm{x},\bm{\Theta})]-\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log\frac{q(\bm{x}_{0:T})}{p(\bm{x}_{0:T}|\bm{\Theta})}\right].

Substituting
\tilde{p}(\bm{x}_{0:T}|\bm{\Theta})
for
p(\bm{x}_{0:T}|\bm{\Theta})
in the denominator of the second term yields
\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx}}
.

Theorem 1 (Precise Statement)
:

\bm{f}

\displaystyle\|\bm{f}(\bm{x},t)-\bm{f}(\bm{y},s)\|\leq L_{\bm{f}}(\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|+|t-s|)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{L}_{\text{cont}}(q,\bm{\Theta})-\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx}}(q,\bm{\Theta})|\leq(\Delta t)^{1/2}\cdot C(\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{x}(0)\|^{2},T_{\text{max}},M,D,L_{\bm{f}})

\bm{\tau}

Proof of
Theorem

For ease of notation, we will drop the dependence of
\bm{f}
on
\bm{\Theta}

\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{I}

\displaystyle|\mathcal{L}_{\text{cont}}(q,\bm{\Theta})-\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx}}(q,\bm{\Theta})|

Lemma 5
.

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}}\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{x}(t)\|^{2}\leq(\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{x}(0)\|^{2}+DT_{\text{max}}+M^{2})\exp\{(2M+1)T_{\text{max}}\}:=C_{0}(\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{x}(0)\|^{2},T_{\text{max}},M,D)

Proof.

By our assumptions on
(\bm{A}(u))_{0\leq u\leq T_{\text{max}}}
and

By Ito’s Lemma,

\displaystyle d\|\bm{x}(t)\|^{2}=2\langle\bm{x}(t),\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x}(t),t)\rangle dt+2\langle\bm{x}(t),d\bm{w}(t)\rangle+Ddt.

Taking the expectation of both sides and recognizing that the Ito integral is a bona fide martingale,

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{x}(t)\|^{2}=

\displaystyle\ \mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{x}(0)\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\{2\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\langle\bm{x}(u),\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x}(u),u)\rangle+D\}du

\displaystyle\ \mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{x}(0)\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\{2\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\{\|\bm{x}(u)\|(M\|\bm{x}(u)\|+M)\}+D\}du

\displaystyle\ \mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{x}(0)\|^{2}+D\cdot t+M^{2}t+\int_{0}^{t}(2M+1)\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{x}(u)\|^{2}du.

∎

Lemma 6

For all

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{x}(t)-\bm{x}(s)\|^{2}\leq C_{1}(\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{x}(0)\|^{2},T_{\text{max}},M,D)\cdot(t-s)

\bm{x}(s)

\displaystyle\|\bm{x}(t)-\bm{x}(s)\|^{2}=\left\|\int_{s}^{t}d\bm{w}^{q}(u)+\int_{s}^{t}\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x}(u),u)du\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigg\|\int_{s}^{t}d\bm{w}^{q}(u)+\int_{s}^{t}\bm{A}(u)(\bm{x}(u)-\bm{x}(s))du+\int_{s}^{t}(\{\bm{A}(u)-\bm{A}(s)\}\bm{x}(s)+\{\bm{b}(u)-\bm{b}(s)\})du

\displaystyle\leq 4\bigg\{\left\|\int_{s}^{t}d\bm{w}^{q}(u)\right\|^{2}+\left\|\int_{s}^{t}\bm{A}(u)(\bm{x}(u)-\bm{x}(s))du\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{x}(t)-\bm{x}(s)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\bigg\{D(t-s)+(t-s)^{2}\sup_{0\leq u\leq T_{\text{max}}}\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x}(u),u)\|^{2}+\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left\|\int_{s}^{t}\bm{A}(u)(\bm{x}(u)-\bm{x}(s))du\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left\|\int_{s}^{t}\bm{A}(u)(\bm{x}(u)-\bm{x}(s))du\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq M^{2}(t-s)\int_{s}^{t}\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{x}(u)-\bm{x}(s)\|^{2}du

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left\|\int_{s}^{t}(\{\bm{A}(u)-\bm{A}(s)\}\bm{x}(s)+\{\bm{b}(u)-\bm{b}(s)\})du\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(t-s)\int_{s}^{t}\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\{\bm{A}(u)-\bm{A}(s)\}\bm{x}(s)+\{\bm{b}(u)-\bm{b}(s)\}\|^{2}du

\displaystyle\leq 2(t-s)\int_{s}^{t}\mdmathbb{E}_{q}(\|\{\bm{A}(u)-\bm{A}(s)\}\bm{x}(s)\|^{2}+\|\bm{b}(u)-\bm{b}(s)\|^{2})du

\displaystyle\leq 2(t-s)^{3}M^{2}\int_{s}^{t}(\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{x}(s)\|^{2}+1)du

\displaystyle\leq 2(t-s)^{4}M^{2}\left(\sup_{0\leq u\leq T_{\text{max}}}\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{x}(u)\|^{2}+1\right).

Combining our above bounds and invoking Grönwall’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{x}(t)-\bm{x}(s)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\bigg\{D(t-s)+(t-s)^{2}\sup_{0\leq u\leq T_{\text{max}}}\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x}(u),u)\|^{2}

\displaystyle+2(t-s)^{4}M^{2}\left(\sup_{0\leq u\leq T_{\text{max}}}\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{x}(u)\|^{2}+1\right)\bigg\}\exp\left\{M^{2}(t-s)^{2}\right\}.

Recognizing the leading term as
(t-s)
and invoking the bound on
\sup_{0\leq u\leq T_{\text{max}}}\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{x}(u)\|^{2}
from
lemma

5
we obtain the result

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{x}(t)-\bm{x}(s)\|^{2}\leq C_{1}(\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\|\bm{x}(0)\|^{2},T_{\text{max}},M,D)\cdot(t-s).

∎

Lemma 7
.

Under
q
, the mean
\overline{\bm{m}}_{i,i+1}
and covariance
\overline{\bm{S}}_{i,i+1}
of
q(\bm{x}_{i+1}|\bm{x}_{i})

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{m}}_{i,i+1}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{i}+\Delta_{i}\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x}_{i},\tau_{i})+(\Delta_{i})^{2}(\bm{r}_{i}^{(1)}\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{r}_{i}^{(2)})

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{S}}_{i,i+1}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{i}\bm{I}+\frac{(\Delta_{i})^{2}}{2}(\bm{A}(\tau_{i})+\bm{A}(\tau_{i})^{\top})+(\Delta_{i})^{3}\bm{R}_{i}

where
\|\bm{r}_{i}^{(1)}\|,\|\bm{r}_{i}^{(2)}\|,\|\bm{R}_{i}\|\leq C_{2}(M)
are independent of

.

Proof.

Since

is the (strong) solution to the linear SDE

7

is Gaussian with mean and covariance

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{m}}_{i,i+1}

\displaystyle:=\bm{\Phi}(\tau_{i+1},\tau_{i})\bm{x}_{i}+\int_{0}^{\Delta_{i}}\bm{\Phi}(\tau_{i}+\Delta_{i},\tau_{i}+u)\bm{b}(u)du

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{S}}_{i,i+1}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{\Delta_{i}}\bm{\Phi}(\tau_{i}+\Delta_{i},\tau_{i}+u)\bm{\Phi}(\tau_{i}+\Delta_{i},\tau_{i}+u)^{\top}du,

\displaystyle\bm{\Phi}(t,s)

\displaystyle:=\bm{I}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{u_{1}}\cdots\int_{s}^{u_{k-1}}\bm{A}(u_{1})\cdots\bm{A}(u_{k})du_{1}\cdots du_{k},\ 0\leq s\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}

(16)

where the series form of the state transition matrix is known as the Peano-Baker series
[
56
]
. Truncation of the Peano-Baker series at its second term yields

\displaystyle\bm{\Phi}(t,s)

\displaystyle:=\bm{I}+\int_{s}^{t}\bm{A}(u)du+\bm{R}^{(1)}(t,s)

where

\displaystyle\|\bm{R}^{(1)}(t,s)\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{u_{1}}\cdots\int_{s}^{u_{k-1}}\bm{A}(u_{1})\cdots\bm{A}(u_{k})du_{1}\cdots du_{k}\right\|

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\leq}\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\left\|\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{u_{1}}\cdots\int_{s}^{u_{k-1}}\bm{A}(u_{1})\cdots\bm{A}(u_{k})du_{1}\cdots du_{k}\right\|

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{\leq}\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{(t-s)^{k}M^{k}}{k!}

\displaystyle=\exp((t-s)M)-1-(t-s)\leq\frac{M^{2}(t-s)^{2}}{2}\exp(M(t-s)).

In inequality (i) we used the fact that the series converges absolutely and for equality (ii) we used
\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{u_{1}}\cdots\int_{s}^{u_{k-1}}du_{1}\cdots du_{k}=(t-s)^{k}/k!
as well as our assumption
\|\bm{A}(t)\|\leq M
. Moreover, by the Mean-Value Theorem,

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\bm{A}(u)du=(t-s)\bm{A}(s)+\bm{R}^{(2)}(t,s),\quad\|\bm{R}^{(2)}(t,s)\|\leq M(t-s)^{2}

(17)

where we used our assumption that

is

, and hence its derivative is bounded in operator norm by
M
.
Plugging this into our expression

for the mean and covariance matrix,

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{m}}_{i,i+1}=\bm{x}_{i}+\Delta_{i}\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x}_{i},\tau_{i})+(\Delta_{i})^{2}(\bm{r}_{i}^{(1)}\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{r}_{i}^{(2)})

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{S}}_{i,i+1}=\Delta_{i}\bm{I}+\frac{(\Delta_{i})^{2}}{2}(\bm{A}(\tau_{i})+\bm{A}(\tau_{i})^{\top})+(\Delta_{i})^{3}\bm{R}_{i}

where

are independent of the index

.
∎

As an immediate consequence of lemmas

Appendix F
Computing expectations of prior transition densities

Here, we provide a proof of
proposition

1
from
section

3.3
, which says that
\mdmathbb{E}_{q(\bm{x})}[\log\tilde{p}(\bm{x}_{i+1}|\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\Theta})]
can be written as an expectation with respect to

only. To do this, we first prove the following statement, which is based off of Stein’s lemma.

Lemma 8
.

Suppose
\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{i}\\
\bm{x}_{i+1}\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\bm{m}_{i}\\
\bm{m}_{i+1}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{S}_{i}&\bm{S}_{i,i+1}\\
\bm{S}_{i,i+1}^{\top}&\bm{S}_{i+1}\end{bmatrix}\right)
where
\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{i+1}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
. Then, for any
\bm{f}:\mdmathbb{R}^{D}\to\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
,
\mdmathbb{E}\left[\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i})(\bm{x}_{i+1}-\bm{m}_{i+1})^{\top}\right]=\mdmathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}_{\bm{f}}(\bm{x}_{i})\right]\bm{S}_{i,i+1}
, where
\bm{J}_{\bm{f}}(\bm{x}_{i})
is the Jacobian of
\bm{f}
evaluated at
\bm{x}_{i}
.

Proof.

First, for notational simplicity, we denote

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{x}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{i}\\
\bm{x}_{i+1}\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{g}(\tilde{\bm{x}}):=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i})\\
\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i+1})\end{bmatrix},\quad\tilde{\bm{m}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{m}_{i}\\
\bm{m}_{i+1}\end{bmatrix},\quad\tilde{\bm{S}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{S}_{i}&\bm{S}_{i,i+1}\\
\bm{S}_{i,i+1}^{\top}&\bm{S}_{i+1}\end{bmatrix}.

Next, we apply Stein’s lemma to the Gaussian random vector
\tilde{\bm{x}}
and function
\bm{g}(\cdot)
. This yields the identity,

\mdmathbb{E}_{q(\tilde{\bm{x}})}\left[\bm{g}(\tilde{\bm{x}})(\tilde{\bm{x}}-\tilde{\bm{m}})^{\top}\right]=\mdmathbb{E}_{q(\tilde{\bm{x}})}\left[\bm{J}_{\bm{g}}(\tilde{\bm{x}})\right]\tilde{\bm{S}},

(18)

where
\bm{J}_{\bm{g}}(\tilde{\bm{x}})
is the Jacobian of
\bm{g}
evaluated at
\tilde{\bm{x}}
:

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{\bm{g}}(\tilde{\bm{x}})=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{J}_{\bm{f}}(\bm{x}_{i})&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\bm{J}_{\bm{f}}(\bm{x}_{i+1})\end{bmatrix}.

Reading off the bottom right block of matrices in
eq.

18
gives,

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{E}\left[\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i})(\bm{x}_{i+1}-\bm{m}_{i+1})^{\top}\right]=\mdmathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}_{\bm{f}}(\bm{x}_{i})\right]\bm{S}_{i,i+1}.

∎

Using this lemma, we can now prove
proposition

1
.

Proposition 1:

The term
\mdmathbb{E}_{q}[\log\tilde{p}(\bm{x}_{i+1}|\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\Theta})]
can equivalently be written in terms of the mean parameters (
\bm{\mu}_{i}
,
\bm{\mu}_{i+1}
) and the expectations
\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left[\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})\right],\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left[\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})\right]
, and
\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left[\bm{J}_{\bm{f}(\cdot|\bm{\Theta})}(\bm{x}_{i})\right]
, thereby reducing the dimensionality of integration from
\mdmathbb{R}^{2D}
to
\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
.

Proof.

Expanding the transition expectation term, we have

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{E}_{q}[\log\tilde{p}(\bm{x}_{i+1}~|~\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\Theta})]

\displaystyle=\mdmathbb{E}_{q}[\log\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}_{i+1}~|~\bm{x}_{i}+\Delta_{i}\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta}),\Delta_{i}\bm{\Sigma})]

\displaystyle=-\frac{D}{2}\log(2\pi\Delta_{i})-\frac{1}{2\Delta_{i}}\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left[\|\bm{x}_{i+1}-\bm{x}_{i}-\Delta_{i}\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})\|_{\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}}^{2}\right].

We can rewrite the expectation in the above line as

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left[\|\bm{x}_{i+1}-\bm{x}_{i}-\Delta_{i}\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})\|_{\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{E}_{q}[\|\bm{x}_{i+1}\|_{\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}}^{2}+\|\bm{x}_{i}\|_{\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}}^{2}+\Delta_{i}^{2}\|\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})\|_{\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}}^{2}-2\bm{x}_{i+1}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{x}_{i}-2\Delta_{i}\bm{x}_{i+1}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})

\displaystyle\quad+2\Delta_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})]

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\text{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{S}_{i+1}+\bm{m}_{i+1}\bm{m}_{i+1}^{\top}))+\text{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{S}_{i}+\bm{m}_{i}\bm{m}_{i}^{\top}))+\Delta_{i}^{2}\mdmathbb{E}_{q}[\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})]

\displaystyle-2\text{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{S}_{i,i+1}+\bm{m}_{i}\bm{m}_{i+1}^{\top}))-2\Delta_{i}\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left[\bm{x}_{i+1}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})\right]+2\Delta_{i}\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left[\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})\right]

Now, applying
lemma

8
to the second to last term, we have

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left[\bm{x}_{i+1}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})\right]=\text{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mdmathbb{E}_{q}[\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})]\bm{m}_{i+1}^{\top}+\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left[\bm{J}_{\bm{f}(\cdot|\bm{\Theta})}(\bm{x}_{i})\right]\bm{S}_{i,i+1})).

Applying Stein’s lemma to the last term, we have

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left[\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})\right]=\text{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mdmathbb{E}_{q}[(\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})]\bm{m}_{i}^{\top}+\bm{J}_{\bm{f}(\cdot|\bm{\Theta})}(\bm{x}_{i})\bm{S}_{i})).

Thus, we have shown that
\mdmathbb{E}_{q}[\log\tilde{p}(\bm{x}_{i+1}|\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\Theta})]
can be written in terms of
\bm{\mu}_{i}
,
\bm{\mu}_{i+1}
, and the expectations
\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left[\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})\right],\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left[\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})\right]
, and
\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left[\bm{J}_{\bm{f}(\cdot|\bm{\Theta})}(\bm{x}_{i})\right]
.
∎

Appendix G
Parallelizing SING

G.1
Sequential computation of log-normalizer

Here, we describe the naive sequential approach of converting from natural parameters
\bm{\eta}
to mean parameters
\bm{\mu}
of
q(\bm{x}_{0:T})
. The idea is to marginalize out one variable at a time in
eq.

12
, starting from
\bm{x}_{0}
and ending at
\bm{x}_{T}
.

We’ll first show how to marginalize out
\bm{x}_{0}
and
\bm{x}_{1}
, and then use this to derive a general recursive update for this sequential approach. To marginalize out
\bm{x}_{0}
, we focus on the following integral which contains all terms depending on
\bm{x}_{0}
.

\displaystyle\int\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}_{0}^{\top}\bm{J}_{0}\bm{x}_{0}+(\bm{h}_{0}-\bm{L}_{0}^{\top}\bm{x}_{1})^{\top}\bm{x}_{0}\right)d\bm{x}_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \underbrace{(2\pi)^{\frac{D}{2}}|\bm{J}_{0}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\bm{h}_{0}^{\top}\bm{J}_{0}^{-1}\bm{h}_{0}\right)}_{:=Z_{0}}\exp\left(\bm{h}_{1}^{(p)\top}\bm{x}_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}_{1}^{\top}\bm{J}_{1}^{(p)}\bm{x}_{1}\right),

where
Z_{0}
is the contribution to the log-normalizer from marginalizing out
\bm{x}_{0}
, and

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{1}^{(p)}=-\bm{L}_{0}\bm{J}_{0}^{-1}\bm{L}_{0}^{\top},\quad\bm{h}_{1}^{(p)}=-\bm{L}_{0}\bm{J}_{0}^{-1}\bm{h}_{0}.

Next, when marginalizing out
\bm{x}_{1}
, we should integrate the potential
\exp\left(\bm{h}_{1}^{(p)\top}\bm{x}_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}_{1}^{\top}\bm{J}_{1}^{(p)}\bm{x}_{1}\right)
from the previous step multiplied by the rest of the terms in
\bm{A}(\bm{\eta})
that depend on
\bm{x}_{1}
. This gives the following integral,

\displaystyle\int\exp\left(\bm{h}_{1}^{(p)\top}\bm{x}_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}_{1}^{\top}\bm{J}_{1}^{(p)}\bm{x}_{1}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}_{1}^{\top}\bm{J}_{1}\bm{x}_{1}-\bm{x}_{2}^{\top}\bm{L}_{1}\bm{x}_{1}+\bm{h}_{1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{1}\right)d\bm{x}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \int\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}_{1}^{\top}(\underbrace{\bm{J}_{1}+\bm{J}_{1}^{(p)}}_{:=\bm{J}_{1}^{(c)}})\bm{x}_{1}+(\underbrace{\bm{h}_{1}+\bm{h}_{1}^{(p)}}_{:=\bm{h}_{1}^{(c)}}-\bm{L}_{1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{2})^{\top}\bm{x}_{1}\right)d\bm{x}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \underbrace{(2\pi)^{\frac{K}{2}}|\bm{J}_{1}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\bm{h}_{1}^{\top}\bm{J}_{1}^{-1}\bm{h}_{1}\right)}_{:=Z_{1}}\exp\left(\bm{h}_{2}^{(p)\top}\bm{x}_{2}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}_{2}^{\top}\bm{J}_{2}^{(p)}\bm{x}_{2}\right)

where
Z_{1}
is the contribution to the log-normalizer from marginalizing out
\bm{x}_{1}
, and

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{2}^{(p)}=-\bm{L}_{1}\bm{J}_{1}^{(c)-1}\bm{L}_{1}^{\top},\quad\bm{h}_{2}^{(p)}=-\bm{L}_{1}\bm{J}_{1}^{(c)-1}\bm{h}_{1}^{(c)}.

Generalizing these recursions leads to the following sequential algorithm:

1.

Initialize
\bm{J}_{0}^{(p)}=\mathbf{0}
,
\bm{h}_{0}^{(p)}=\mathbf{0}
, and
\log Z=0
.

2.

For
i=0,\dots,T
:

(a)

Update
\bm{J}_{i}^{(c)}\leftarrow\bm{J}_{i}+\bm{J}_{i}^{(p)}
and
\bm{h}_{i}^{(c)}\leftarrow\bm{h}_{i}+\bm{h}_{i}^{(p)}
.

(b)

Update
\log Z\leftarrow\log Z+\log Z_{i}
.

(c)

Update
\bm{J}_{i+1}^{(p)}\leftarrow-\bm{L}_{i}\bm{J}_{i}^{(c)-1}\bm{L}_{i}^{\top}
and
\bm{h}_{i+1}^{(p)}\leftarrow-\bm{L}_{i}\bm{J}_{i}^{(c)-1}\bm{h}_{i}^{(c)}
.

3.

Return
A(\bm{\eta})=\log Z
.

Time complexity

In each iteration
i
, this sequential algorithm has time complexity
\mathcal{O}(D^{3})
which comes from the matrix inversion and multiplication in
-\bm{L}_{i}\bm{J}_{i}^{(c)-1}\bm{L}_{i}^{\top}
. Since each iteration relies on values computed in the previous iteration, this algorithm must be implemented sequentially, leading to a total time complexity of
\mathcal{O}(D^{3}T)
.

G.2
Parallelized computation of log-normalizer

Here, we present our new method of converting from natural to mean parameters in
parallel time
using associative scans, leading to substantial computational speedups on modern parallel hardware. As discussed in
section

3.4
, we accomplish this by defining elements in the scan as unnormalized Gaussian potentials
a_{i,j}
and combine them using the binary associative operator,

a_{i,j}\bullet a_{j,k}=\int a_{i,j}a_{j,k}d\bm{x}_{j}.

(19)

Note that
eq.

19
is indeed a binary associative operator, as it satisfies the identity:

\displaystyle(a_{i,j}\bullet a_{j,k})\bullet a_{k,l}

\displaystyle=\int\left(\int a_{i,j}a_{j,k}d\bm{x}_{j}\right)a_{k,l}d\bm{x}_{k}

\displaystyle=\int a_{i,j}\left(\int a_{j,k}a_{k,l}d\bm{x}_{k}\right)d\bm{x}_{j}

\displaystyle=a_{i,j}\bullet(a_{j,k}\bullet a_{k,l}).

In the following calculation, we show how to compute the binary associative operator between two potentials analytically. We will start with computing the operator between two
consecutive
potentials, and use this calculation to generalize to computing any pair of potentials with a shared variable to be marginalized.

Let
a_{i-1,i}
and
a_{i,i+1}
be two consecutive potentials. Then,

\displaystyle a_{i-1,i}\bullet a_{i,i+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \int\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{J}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{i-1}^{\top}\bm{L}_{i-1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{h}_{i}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}_{i+1}^{\top}\bm{J}_{i+1}\bm{x}_{i+1}-\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{L}_{i}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i+1}+\bm{h}_{i+1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i+1}\right)d\bm{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}_{i+1}^{\top}\bm{J}_{i+1}\bm{x}_{i+1}+\bm{h}_{i+1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i+1}\right)\int\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{J}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}+(\bm{h}_{i}-\bm{L}_{i-1}\bm{x}_{i-1}-\bm{L}_{i}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i+1})^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}\right)d\bm{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \underbrace{(2\pi)^{\frac{D}{2}}|\bm{J}_{i}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}}_{:=Z_{\text{local}}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{x}_{i-1}\\
\bm{x}_{i+1}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{J}_{1}^{(p)}&\bm{L}^{(p)\top}\\
\bm{L}^{(p)}&\bm{J}_{2}^{(p)}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{x}_{i-1}\\
\bm{x}_{i+1}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\bm{x}_{i-1}\\
\bm{x}_{i+1}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{h}_{1}^{(p)}\\
\bm{h}_{2}^{(p)}\end{pmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ a_{i-1,i+1}

where
Z_{\text{local}}
is the contribution to the log-normalizer from this operation, and

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{1}^{(p)}=-\bm{L}_{i-1}^{\top}\bm{J}_{i}^{-1}\bm{L}_{i-1},\quad\bm{J}_{2}^{(p)}=\bm{J}_{i+1}-\bm{L}_{i}\bm{J}_{i}^{-1}\bm{L}_{i}^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{h}_{1}^{(p)}=-\bm{L}_{i-1}^{\top}\bm{J}_{i}^{-1}\bm{h}_{i},\quad\bm{h}_{2}^{(p)}=\bm{h}_{i+1}-\bm{L}_{i}\bm{J}_{i}^{-1}\bm{h}_{i},

\displaystyle\bm{L}^{(p)}=\bm{L}_{i}\bm{J}_{i}^{-1}\bm{L}_{i-1}.

From the calculation above, we observe that the element
a_{i,j}
, where
i
and
j
are not necessarily consecutive, takes the general form

\displaystyle a_{i,j}=Z_{\text{local}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{x}_{i}\\
\bm{x}_{j}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{J}_{1}&\bm{L}^{\top}\\
\bm{L}&\bm{J}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{x}_{i}\\
\bm{x}_{j}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\bm{x}_{i}\\
\bm{x}_{j}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{h}_{1}\\
\bm{h}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right).

Using this form, we now derive the analytical result of the binary associative operator between any two Gaussian potentials with a shared marginalization variable.

\displaystyle a_{i,j}\bullet a_{j,k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \int Z_{\text{local}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{x}_{i}\\
\bm{x}_{j}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{J}_{1}&\bm{L}^{\top}\\
\bm{L}&\bm{J}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{x}_{i}\\
\bm{x}_{j}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\bm{x}_{i}\\
\bm{x}_{j}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{h}_{1}\\
\bm{h}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\tilde{Z}_{\text{local}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{x}_{j}\\
\bm{x}_{k}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\bm{J}}_{1}&\tilde{\bm{L}}^{\top}\\
\tilde{\bm{L}}&\tilde{\bm{J}}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{x}_{j}\\
\bm{x}_{k}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\bm{x}_{j}\\
\bm{x}_{k}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\bm{h}}_{1}\\
\tilde{\bm{h}}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right)d\bm{x}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ Z_{\text{local}}\tilde{Z}_{\text{local}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{J}_{1}\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{h}_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}_{k}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{J}}_{2}\bm{x}_{k}+\bm{x}_{k}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{h}}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\int\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}(\underbrace{\bm{J}_{2}+\tilde{\bm{J}}_{1}}_{:=\bm{J}^{(c)}})\bm{x}_{j}+\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}(-\bm{L}^{(p)}\bm{x}_{i}-\tilde{\bm{L}}^{(p)\top}\bm{x}_{k}+\underbrace{\bm{h}_{2}+\tilde{\bm{h}}_{1}}_{:=\bm{h}^{(c)}})\right)d\bm{x}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ Z_{\text{local}}^{(p)}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{x}_{i}\\
\bm{x}_{k}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{J}_{1}^{(p)}&\bm{L}^{(p)\top}\\
\bm{L}^{(p)}&\bm{J}_{2}^{(p)}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{x}_{i}\\
\bm{x}_{k}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\bm{x}_{i}\\
\bm{x}_{k}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{h}_{1}^{(p)}\\
\bm{h}_{2}^{(p)}\end{pmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ a_{i,k}

where

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{1}^{(p)}=\bm{J}_{1}-\bm{L}^{\top}\bm{J}^{(c)-1}\bm{L},\quad\bm{J}_{2}^{(p)}=\tilde{\bm{J}}_{2}-\tilde{\bm{L}}\bm{J}^{(c)-1}\tilde{\bm{L}}^{\top}

\displaystyle\bm{h}_{1}^{(p)}=\bm{h}_{1}-\bm{L}^{\top}\bm{J}^{(c)-1}\bm{h}^{(c)},\quad\bm{h}_{2}^{(p)}=\tilde{\bm{h}}_{1}-\tilde{\bm{L}}\bm{J}^{(c)-1}\bm{h}^{(c)}

\displaystyle\bm{L}^{(p)}=-\tilde{\bm{L}}\bm{J}^{(c)-1}\bm{L},

\displaystyle Z_{\text{local}}^{(p)}=Z^{\text{local}}\tilde{Z}^{\text{local}}(2\pi)^{\frac{D}{2}}|\bm{J}^{(c)}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\bm{h}^{(c)\top}\bm{J}^{(c)-1}\bm{h}^{(c)}\right)

In practice, instead of computing
Z_{\text{local}}^{(p)}
we instead compute
\log Z_{\text{local}}^{(p)}
. Since each binary operation marginalizes out one variable, the final result of the associative scan,

\displaystyle a_{-1,0}\bullet a_{0,1}\bullet\dots\bullet a_{T,T+1},

will be the result of accumulating all
\log Z_{\text{local}}
terms, and will hence be equal to the desired log-normalizer
A(\bm{\eta})
.

Time complexity

The time complexity of the associative scan, assuming
T
processors, is
\mathcal{O}(C\log T)
where
C
represents the cost of matrix operations within each application of the operator
[
34
,
45
]
. By the derivation above,
C=\mathcal{O}(D^{3})
which comes from the matrix inversion and multiplication in
\bm{L}^{\top}\bm{J}^{(c)-1}\bm{L}
and analogous terms. Therefore, the parallel time complexity is
\mathcal{O}(D^{3}\log T)
.

Appendix H
Background on Archambeau et al., 2007 (VDP)

In this section, we describe the variational diffusion process (VDP) smoothing algorithm proposed by
Archambeau et al. 2007a
. The namesake for this algorithm is adopted from
Verma et al. 2024
.

Rather than first approximating the continuous-time ELBO
\mathcal{L}_{\text{cont}}
,
eq.

8
with
\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx}}
,
eq.

9
and then maximizing with respect to the variational parameters, the VDP algorithm directly maximizes
\mathcal{L}_{\text{cont}}
. In particular, the VDP algorithm involves iteratively solving (continuous-time) ODEs. When implemented numerically, though, these ODEs are solved using a discrete-time scheme, such as Euler’s method. Therefore, like
Verma et al. 2024
and SING, VDP only approximately maximizes the true, continuous-time ELBO.

Archambeau et al. 2007a
rewrite the ELBO
\mathcal{L}_{\text{cont}}
in terms of
(\bm{A}(t),\bm{b}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}}
and
(\bm{m}(t),\bm{S}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}}
, where
\bm{m}(t)
and
\bm{S}(t)
denote the marginal mean and covariance, respectively, of
q(\bm{x})
at each time
0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}
. These parameters are related by the Fokker-Planck equations for linear SDEs

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{m}(t)}{dt}=\bm{A}(t)\bm{m}(t)+\bm{b}(t)

(20)

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{S}(t)}{dt}=\bm{A}(t)\bm{S}(t)+\bm{S}(t)\bm{A}(t)^{\top}+\bm{\Sigma}.

(21)

To ensure that, at the optimum, equations
eq.

20
and
eq.

21
are satisfied, VDP introduces the Lagrange multipliers
(\bm{\lambda}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}}
and
(\bm{\Psi}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}}
and incorporate
eq.

20
and
eq.

21
into the objective

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{cont}}((\bm{A}(t),\bm{b}(t),\bm{m}(t),\bm{S}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}},\bm{\Theta})+\int_{0}^{T_{\text{max}}}\bm{\lambda}(t)^{\top}\left(\frac{d\bm{m}(t)}{dt}-\bm{A}(t)\bm{m}(t)-\bm{b}(t)\right)dt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T_{\text{max}}}\text{tr}\left(\bm{\Phi}(t)^{\top}\left\{\frac{d\bm{S}(t)}{dt}-\bm{A}(t)\bm{S}(t)-\bm{S}(t)\bm{A}(t)-\bm{\Sigma}\right\}\right)dt.

Performing an integration by parts yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{cont}}((\bm{A}(t),\bm{b}(t),\bm{m}(t),\bm{S}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}},\bm{\Theta})-\int_{0}^{T_{\text{max}}}\bm{\lambda}(t)^{\top}\left(\bm{A}(t)\bm{m}(t)+\bm{b}(t)\right)dt

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T_{\text{max}}}\text{tr}\left(\bm{\Phi}(t)^{\top}\left\{\bm{A}(t)\bm{S}(t)+\bm{S}(t)\bm{A}(t)+\bm{\Sigma}\right\}\right)dt-\int_{0}^{T_{\text{max}}}\left(\text{tr}\left\{\frac{d\bm{\Phi}(t)}{dt}^{\top}\bm{S}(t)\right\}+\frac{d\bm{\lambda}(t)}{dt}^{\top}\bm{m}(t)\right)dt

\displaystyle+\text{tr}(\bm{\Phi}(T_{\text{max}})^{\top}\bm{S}(T_{\text{max}}))-\text{tr}(\bm{\Phi}(0)^{\top}\bm{S}(0))+\bm{\lambda}(T_{\text{max}})^{\top}\bm{m}(T_{\text{max}})-\bm{\lambda}(0)^{\top}\bm{m}(0).

(22)

The authors proceed by performing coordinate ascent on this objective with respect to the variables
(\bm{A}(t),\bm{b}(t),\bm{m}(t),\bm{S}(t),\bm{\lambda}(t),\bm{\Phi}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}}
. However, when taking the (variational) derivatives with respect to the functions
\bm{A}(t)
,
\bm{b}(t)
,
\bm{m}(t)
, and
\bm{S}(t)
, the dependence of
\bm{A}(t)
and
\bm{b}(t)
on
\bm{m}(t)
and
\bm{S}(t)
, and vice versa, is ignored.

Taking the derivative of
appendix

H
with respect to
\bm{m}(t)
and
\bm{S}(t)
yields the updates for the Lagrange multipliers

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{\Psi}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\bm{\Psi}(t)\bm{A}(t)-(\bm{A}(t))^{\top}\bm{\Psi}(t)+\frac{d}{d\bm{S}(t)}(-\textrm{KL}\left(q\;\|\;p\right)),\quad\bm{\Psi}(T_{\text{max}})=\bm{0}

(23)

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{\lambda}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\bm{A}(t)^{\top}\bm{\lambda}(t)+\frac{d}{d\bm{m}(t)}(-\textrm{KL}\left(q\;\|\;p\right)),\quad\bm{\lambda}(T_{\text{max}})=\bm{0}

(24)

subject to the jump conditions at observation times
\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T}

\displaystyle\bm{\Psi}(t_{i}^{+})

\displaystyle=\bm{\Psi}(t_{i})+\frac{d}{d\bm{S}(t_{i})}\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\log p(\bm{y}(t_{i})|\bm{x}(t_{i}))

(25)

\displaystyle\bm{\lambda}(t_{i}^{+})

\displaystyle=\bm{\lambda}(t_{i})+\frac{d}{d\bm{m}(t_{i})}\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\log p(\bm{y}(t_{i})|\bm{x}(t_{i})).

(26)

Taking the derivative of
appendix

H
with respect to
\bm{A}(t)
and
\bm{b}(t)
yields the updates

\displaystyle\bm{A}(t)

\displaystyle=\mdmathbb{E}_{q(\bm{x}(t))}\left[\frac{d\bm{f}(\bm{x}|\bm{\Theta})}{d\bm{x}}\right]-2\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\Psi}(t)

(27)

\displaystyle\bm{b}(t)

\displaystyle=\mdmathbb{E}_{q(\bm{x}(t))}[\bm{f}(\bm{x}|\bm{\Theta})]-\bm{A}(t)\bm{m}(t)-\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\lambda}(t).

(28)

Finally, for
p(\bm{x}(0))=\mathcal{N}(\bm{\nu},\bm{V})
, taking the derivative of
appendix

H
with respect to
\bm{\nu}
and
\bm{V}
yields

\displaystyle\bm{m}(0)

\displaystyle=\bm{\nu}-\bm{V}\bm{\lambda}(0)

(29)

\displaystyle\bm{S}(0)

\displaystyle=(2\bm{\Psi}(0)+\bm{V}^{-1})^{-1}.

(30)

Altogether, the VDP variational inference algorithm alternates between the following steps, until convergence

1.

Solve for
(\bm{m}(t),\bm{S}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}}
according to
eq.

20
and
eq.

21
.

2.

Update
(\bm{\lambda}(t),\bm{\Psi}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}}
, starting from
\bm{\lambda}(T_{\text{max}})=\bm{0}
and
\bm{\Psi}(T_{\text{max}})=\bm{0}
, according to
eq.

24
and
eq.

23
and taking into account the jump conditions
eq.

26
and
eq.

25
at observation times
\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T}
.

3.

Update
(\bm{A}(t),\bm{b}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}}
according to
eq.

27
and
eq.

28
.

4.

Update
\bm{m}(0)
and
\bm{S}(0)
according to
eq.

29
and
eq.

30
.

For stability,
Archambeau et al. 2007a
,
Archambeau et al. 2007b
propose replacing the update for
(\bm{A}(t),\bm{b}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}}
at iteration
\ell
with the “soft” updates

\displaystyle\bm{A}^{(\ell+1)}(t)=\bm{A}^{(\ell)}(t)+\omega(\widetilde{\bm{A}}^{(\ell)}(t)-\bm{A}^{(\ell)}(t))

(31)

\displaystyle\bm{b}^{(\ell+1)}(t)=\bm{b}^{(\ell)}(t)+\omega(\widetilde{\bm{b}}^{(\ell)}(t)-\bm{b}^{(\ell)}(t)),

where
\widetilde{\bm{A}}^{(\ell)}(t)
and
\widetilde{\bm{b}}^{(\ell)}(t)
are defined as in
eq.

27
and
eq.

28
. In our experiments, we observe that choosing
\omega
to be small (e.g.,
10^{-3}
) is often necessary to ensure convergence of VDP.

Unlike
Verma et al. 2024
and SING, VDP does not benefit from convergence guarantees. Indeed in
appendix

J
, we show that even in the setting where the prior SDE is linear and the observation model is Gaussian, VDP will not recover the true posterior in one step.

Appendix I
Comparison of SING to Verma et al., 2024

I.1
Overview of Verma et al., 2024

In this section, we outline
Verma et al. 2024
, the work upon which SING is built, and discuss key differences between the two variational EM algorithms.

Like SING,
Verma et al. 2024
maximizes the approximate ELBO
\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx}}
from
eq.

9
with respect the variational parameters
(\bm{A}(t),\bm{b}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}}
as well as the model parameters
\bm{\Theta}
. To accomplish this, they perform a change-of-measure

\displaystyle\tilde{p}(\bm{x}_{0:T}|\bm{\Theta})=p_{L}(\bm{x}_{0:T})\frac{\tilde{p}(\bm{x}_{0:T}|\bm{\Theta})}{p_{L}(\bm{x}_{0:T})},

where
p_{L}(\bm{x}_{0:T})=q(\bm{x}_{0:T}|\bm{\eta}_{L})
belongs to the same exponential family as the variational posterior and has natural parameters
\bm{\eta}_{L}=(\bm{h}^{L},\bm{J}^{L},\bm{L}^{L})
. Suppose
q(\bm{x}_{0:T}|\bm{\eta})
is initialized at
\bm{\eta}^{(0)}=\bm{\eta}_{L}
. Then the corresponding NGVI update steps can be written as

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{h}}_{i}^{(j+1)}

\displaystyle=(1-\rho)\tilde{\bm{h}}_{i}^{(j)}+\rho\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{i,1}}\mdmathbb{E}_{q^{(j)}}[\log p(\bm{y}_{i}|\bm{x}_{i})\delta_{i}+\Delta_{i}V(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{i+1})+\Delta_{i}V(\bm{x}_{i-1},\bm{x}_{i})]

(32)

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{J}}_{i}^{(j+1)}

\displaystyle=(1-\rho)\tilde{\bm{J}}_{i}^{(j)}+\rho\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{i,2}}\mdmathbb{E}_{q^{(j)}}[\log p(\bm{y}_{i}|\bm{x}_{i})\delta_{i}+\Delta_{i}V(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{i+1})+\Delta_{i}V(\bm{x}_{i-1},\bm{x}_{i})]

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{L}}_{i}^{(j+1)}

\displaystyle=(1-\rho)\tilde{\bm{L}}_{i}^{(t)}+\rho\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{i,3}}\mdmathbb{E}_{q^{(j)}}[\Delta_{i}V(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{i+1})]

\displaystyle\bm{h}_{i}^{(j+1)}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\bm{h}}_{i}^{(j+1)}+\bm{h}^{L},\ \bm{J}_{i}^{(j+1)}=\tilde{\bm{J}}_{i}^{(j+1)}+\bm{J}^{L},\ \bm{L}_{i}^{(j+1)}=\tilde{\bm{L}}_{i}^{(j+1)}+\bm{L}^{L}

Here,
\Delta_{i}V(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{i+1})=\log\left(\tilde{p}(\bm{x}_{i+1}|\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\Theta})/p_{L}(\bm{x}_{i+1}|\bm{x}_{i})\right)
,
0\leq i\leq T-1
is the log ratio of the transition probabilities from
\bm{x}_{i}
to
\bm{x}_{i+1}
under
\tilde{p}
and
p_{L}
.
The NGVI updates that appear in

Verma et al. 2024
are written in this unusual form to separate the contribution of
p_{L}
, which belongs to the same family as
q
, from the other terms that appear in the ELBO.

The authors propose alternating between (i) performing numerous NGVI updates (
32
) and (ii) updating
p_{L}
at iteration
j
according to a statistical linearization step. Specifically, statistical linearization of the prior drift computes the best linear approximation to the nonlinear prior drift under the current variational posterior according to

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{i}^{L},\bm{b}_{i}^{L}=\argmin\mdmathbb{E}_{q(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\eta}^{(j)})}\|\bm{A}_{i}^{L}\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{b}_{i}^{L}-\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})\|_{2}^{2}

(33)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{i}^{L}=\mdmathbb{E}_{q(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\eta}^{(\ell)})}\left[\frac{d\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})}{d\bm{x}}\right],\ \bm{b}_{i}^{L}=\mdmathbb{E}_{q(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\eta}^{(j)})}[\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})]-\bm{A}_{i}^{L}\bm{m}_{i},\quad i=0,\ldots T-1.

By the log-normalizer computation discussed in
appendix

G
, one can use
(\bm{A}_{i}^{L},\bm{b}_{i}^{L})_{i=1}^{T-1}

to obtain a new set of natural parameters
\bm{\eta}^{L,\text{new}}=(\bm{h}^{L,\text{new}},\bm{J}^{L,\text{new}},\bm{L}^{L,\text{new}})
. One then updates the natural parameters by setting

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{h}}_{i}^{(j)}\leftarrow\tilde{\bm{h}}_{i}^{(j)}+(\bm{h}_{i}^{L}-\bm{h}_{i}^{L,\text{new}}),\ \tilde{\bm{J}}_{i}^{(j)}\leftarrow\tilde{\bm{J}}_{i}^{(j)}+(\bm{J}_{i}^{L}-\bm{J}_{i}^{L,\text{new}}),\ \tilde{\bm{L}}_{i}^{(j)}\leftarrow\tilde{\bm{L}}_{i}^{(j)}+(\bm{L}_{i}^{L}-\bm{L}_{i}^{L,\text{new}})

(34)

and lastly
\bm{\eta}^{L}\leftarrow\bm{\eta}^{L,\text{new}}
. The updates
eq.

34
ensure that updating the parameters
\bm{\eta}^{L}
of
p_{L}
does not change the parameters of the
q
.

Importantly, the statistical linearization step appearing in
Verma et al. 2024
does not matter for inference, since it is apparent that by “undoing” the change-of-measure, one can rewrite the NGVI update (
32
) to exactly match the SING update (
11
). In fact, we point out that repeatedly performing statistical linearization, as is suggested by
Verma et al. 2024
, is unnecessary: one will obtain the same
\bm{\eta}^{L}
by performing only a single statistical linearization step at the end of inference.

The reason for introducing
p_{L}
is that the authors contend that it aids in hyperparameter learning, insofar as it allows one to couple the E and M-steps via
\bm{\eta}_{L}
, which from
eq.

33
, evidently depends on
\bm{\Theta}
. In other words, during the M-step
Verma et al. 2024
solve

\displaystyle\argmax_{\bm{\Theta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\eta}_{L}(\bm{\Theta})+\tilde{\bm{\eta}},\bm{\Theta}),\quad\tilde{\bm{\eta}}=(\tilde{\bm{h}},\tilde{\bm{J}},\tilde{\bm{L}}).

This idea is adopted from
Adam et al. 2021
. One can imagine there are many functions
\vartheta:\mdmathbb{R}^{|\bm{\Theta}|}\to\bm{\Xi}
that couple the E and M-steps by writing
\bm{\eta}=\vartheta(\bm{\Theta})+(\bm{\eta}-\vartheta(\bm{\Theta}))
. The authors argue that defining
\vartheta
as in
eq.

33
is desirable insofar as optimizing
\bm{\eta}-\vartheta(\bm{\Theta})
with respect to
\bm{\eta}
for
\bm{\Theta}
fixed is less dependent on
\bm{\Theta}
than for alternate
\vartheta
. One issue with this argument is that, unlike
Verma et al. 2024
, we are also optimizing the parameters of the likelihood
p(\bm{y}|\bm{x},\bm{\Theta})
, and so it is not evident that statistical linearization of the prior
eq.

33
best couples the E and M-steps.

I.2
Differences between SING and Verma et al., 2024

SING aims to simplify and improve upon the algorithm presented by
Verma et al. 2024
. First,
Verma et al. 2024
does not address how to compute the term

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{E}_{q}V(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{i+1})=\frac{1}{\Delta_{i}}\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\log\frac{p(\bm{x}_{i+1}|\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\Theta})}{p_{L}(\bm{x}_{i+1}|\bm{x}_{i})}=\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\bigg\{-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{A}_{t}^{L}\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{b}_{i}^{L}-\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})\|_{\bm{\Sigma}^{-}1}^{2}

\displaystyle+\left\langle\frac{\bm{x}_{i+1}-\bm{x}_{i}}{\Delta_{i}}-(\bm{A}_{t}^{L}\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{b}_{i}^{L}),\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}|\bm{\Theta})-(\bm{A}_{t}^{L}\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{b}_{i}^{L})\right\rangle_{\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}}\bigg\},

\displaystyle p(\bm{y}_{\geq t}|\bm{x}(t))\propto p(\bm{x}(t_{i})|\bm{x}(t))\left(\prod_{k=i}^{n-1}p(\bm{x}(t_{k+1})|\bm{x}(t_{k}))p(\bm{y}(t_{k})|\bm{x}(t_{k}))\right)p(\bm{y}(t_{n})|\bm{x}(t_{n})).

\displaystyle p(\bm{y}_{\geq t}|\bm{x}(t))=\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}(t)^{\top}\bm{G}(t)\bm{x}(t)+\bm{g}(t)^{\top}\bm{x}(t)+c(t)\right\},\quad t_{i-1}<t\leq t_{i}.

\displaystyle\psi(\bm{x},t)(\bm{A}^{\text{prior}}(t)\bm{x}+\bm{b}^{\text{prior}}(t))^{\top}(\bm{g}(t)-\bm{G}(t)\bm{x})+\psi(\bm{x},t)\frac{1}{2}\left\{(\bm{g}(t)-\bm{G}(t)\bm{x})^{\top}(\bm{g}(t)-\bm{G}(t)\bm{x})-\text{tr}(\bm{G}(t))\right\}

\displaystyle-\psi(\bm{x},t)\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}\frac{d}{dt}\bm{G}(t)\bm{x}+\frac{d}{dt}\bm{g}(t)^{\top}\bm{x}+\frac{d}{dt}c(t)\right)

\displaystyle(\bm{A}^{\text{prior}}(t)\bm{x}+\bm{b}^{\text{prior}}(t))^{\top}(\bm{G}(t)\bm{x}-\bm{g}(t))+\frac{1}{2}\left\{(\bm{G}(t)\bm{x}-\bm{g}(t))^{\top}(\bm{g}(t)-\bm{G}(t)\bm{x})+\text{tr}(\bm{G}(t))\right\}

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}\frac{d}{dt}\bm{G}(t)\bm{x}+\frac{d}{dt}\bm{g}(t)^{\top}\bm{x}+\frac{d}{dt}c(t)\right)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bm{G}(t)=-(\bm{A}^{\text{prior}}(t))^{\top}\bm{G}(t)-\bm{G}(t)\bm{A}^{\text{prior}}(t)+\bm{G}(t)^{\top}\bm{G}(t)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bm{g}(t)=-(\bm{A}^{\text{prior}}(t))^{\top}\bm{g}(t)+\bm{G}(t)^{\top}\bm{b}^{\text{prior}}(t)+\bm{G}(t)^{\top}\bm{g}(t)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bm{G}(t)=-(\bm{A}^{\text{prior}}(t))^{\top}\bm{G}(t)-\bm{G}_{t}\bm{A}^{\text{prior}}(t)+\bm{G}(t)^{\top}\bm{G}(t)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bm{g}(t)=-(\bm{A}^{\text{prior}}(t))^{\top}\bm{g}(t)+\bm{G}(t)^{\top}\bm{b}^{\text{prior}}+\bm{G}(t)^{\top}\bm{g}(t)

\displaystyle\bm{G}(t_{i})=\bm{G}(t_{i}^{+})+\bm{C}^{\top}\bm{R}^{-1}\bm{C}

\displaystyle\bm{g}(t_{i})=\bm{g}(t_{i}^{+})+\bm{C}^{\top}\bm{R}^{-1}(\bm{y}(t_{i})-\bm{d})

\displaystyle\bm{A}^{\text{prior}}(t)\bm{x}(t)+\bm{b}^{\text{prior}}(t)+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p(\bm{y}_{\geq t}|\bm{x}(t))=(\bm{A}^{\text{prior}}(t)-\bm{G}(t))\bm{x}(t)+(\bm{b}^{\text{prior}}(t)+\bm{g}(t)).

Now, though, we can directly compare

\bm{G}(t)
and

\displaystyle\frac{d(2\bm{\Psi}(t))}{dt}=-\bm{A}(t)^{\top}(2\bm{\Psi}(t))-(2\bm{\Psi}(t))\bm{A}(t)-(\bm{A}^{\text{prior}}(t)-\bm{A}(t))^{\top}(\bm{A}^{\text{prior}}(t)-\bm{A}(t)),

\displaystyle\frac{d(2\bm{\Psi}(t))}{dt}=-(\bm{A}^{\text{prior}}(t))^{\top}(2\bm{\Psi}(t))-(2\bm{\Psi}(t))\bm{A}^{\text{prior}}(t),\quad 2\bm{\Psi}(t_{i})=2\bm{\Psi}(t_{i}^{+})+\bm{C}^{\top}\bm{R}^{-1}\bm{C}.

SING-GP: Drift estimation with Gaussian process priors

Here, we present the GP-SDE generative model and full inference algorithm using SING-GP from

K.1

The GP-SDE model consists of the latent SDE model from
section

2.1
combined with Gaussian process priors on the output dimensions of the drift function

\displaystyle d\bm{x}(t)=\bm{f}(\bm{x}(t))dt+\bm{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}d\bm{w}(t),\quad\mdmathbb{E}[\bm{y}(t_{i})|\bm{x}]=g(\bm{C}\bm{x}(t_{i})+\bm{d})

\displaystyle f_{d}(\cdot)\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{GP}(0,\kappa_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot,\cdot)),\quad d=1,\dots,D.

\bm{\theta}

2.1

K.2

Following
Duncker et al. 2019
, we augment the generative model from
section

K.1
with

[
36
]
, which enable tractable approximate inference of
\bm{f}(\cdot)
. This technique allows the computational complexity of inference of
\bm{f}(\cdot)

is the number of inducing points. Let us denote the inducing locations as

and corresponding inducing values as
\{\bm{u}_{d}\}_{d=1}^{D}\subset\mdmathbb{R}^{M}

K.1

\displaystyle p(\bm{y},\bm{x},\bm{f},\bm{u}~|~\bm{\Theta})=p(\bm{y}~|~\bm{x},\bm{\Theta})p(\bm{x}~|~\bm{f})\prod_{d=1}^{D}p(f_{d}~|~\bm{u}_{d},\bm{\Theta})p(\bm{u}_{d}~|~\bm{\Theta}).

Treating

\displaystyle p(\bm{u}_{d}~|~\bm{\Theta})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{u}_{d}~|~\mathbf{0},\bm{K}_{zz})

is the Gram matrix corresponding to the batch of points

\displaystyle f_{d}(\cdot)~|~\bm{u}_{d}\sim\mathcal{GP}(\mu_{f_{d}|\bm{u}_{d}}(\cdot),\kappa_{f_{d}|\bm{u}_{d}}(\cdot,\cdot))

\displaystyle\mu_{f_{d}|\bm{u}_{d}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle q(\bm{x},\bm{f},\bm{u})=q(\bm{x})\prod_{d=1}^{D}p(f_{d}~|~\bm{u}_{d},\bm{\Theta})q(\bm{u}_{d}).

\displaystyle q(\bm{u}_{d})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{u}_{d}~|~\bm{m}_{u}^{d\ast},\bm{S}_{u}^{d\ast})

q(\bm{x}_{0:T})
is the finite-dimensional variational posterior on time grid
\bm{\tau}
and
\tilde{p}(\bm{x}_{0:T})
is the corresponding prior process on the same time grid discretized using Euler-Maruyama. We note that
\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx-GP}}
has two main differences from
\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx}}
: (1) the second term now involves an expectation with respect to
q(\bm{f})
, which comes from the Gaussian process prior on the drift, and (2)
\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx-GP}}
has an additional third term regularizing the posterior on inducing points towards its GP-derived prior.

K.3
Inference and learning using SING-GP

In a GP-SDE model, the two inference goals are (1) inferring a posterior over the latent states
q(\bm{x}_{0:T})
and (2) inferring a posterior over the drift function (i.e. dynamics)
q(\bm{f})
. In SING-GP, we propose to use SING as described in
section

3
to perform latent state inference, and we derive novel closed-form updates for
q(\bm{u}_{d})
based on
\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx-GP}}
. In the next section, we will show how Gaussian conjugacy allows for straightforward inference of
q(\bm{f})
given
q(\bm{u}_{d})
.

To update
q(\bm{u}_{d})
, we would like to maximize
\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx-GP}}(q(\bm{x}_{0:T}),q(\bm{u}),\bm{\Theta})
with respect to the variational parameters
\bm{m}_{u}^{d\ast},\bm{S}_{u}^{d\ast}
given the current estimates of
q(\bm{x}_{0:T})
and
\bm{\Theta}
. By direct differentiation of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx-GP}}(q(\bm{x}_{0:T}),q(\bm{u}),\bm{\Theta})
with respect to
\bm{m}_{u}^{d\ast}
and
\bm{S}_{u}^{d\ast}
, one can derive the following analytical updates for
\bm{m}_{u}^{d\ast}
and
\bm{S}_{u}^{d\ast}
:

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{u}^{d\ast}

\displaystyle=\bm{K}_{zz}\left(\bm{K}_{zz}+\Delta t\sum_{i=0}^{T}\mdmathbb{E}_{q(\bm{x}_{i})}[\bm{k}_{zx_{i}}\bm{k}_{x_{i}z}]\right)^{-1}\bm{K}_{zz}

(37)

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{u}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}_{u}^{d\ast}\bm{K}_{zz}^{-1}\left(\Delta t\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\mdmathbb{E}_{q(\bm{x}_{i})}[\bm{k}_{zx_{i}}]\left(\frac{\bm{m}_{i+1}-\bm{m}_{i}}{\Delta t}\right)+\Delta t\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\mdmathbb{E}_{q(\bm{x}_{i})}\left[\frac{d\bm{k}_{zx}}{d\bm{x}}\right]\left(\frac{\bm{S}_{i,i+1}-\bm{S}_{i}}{\Delta t}\right)\right).

In the above equation,
\bm{m}_{u}^{\ast}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{M\times D}
contains
\bm{m}_{u}^{d\ast}
in each column. The required kernel expectations in
eq.

37
have analytical expressions for, e.g., the commonly used radial basis function (RBF) kernel, and otherwise can be approximated via Gaussian quadrature for specialized kernels. Moreover, observe that since the dependence on
T
only appears in sums, this update can be computed in parallel across time steps.

Finally, the learning goal in the GP-SDE is to optimize the kernel hyperparameters
\bm{\theta}
. Prior work using GP-SDEs with structured GP kernels
[
14
]
found that alternating between updating
q(\bm{u})
and optimizing
\bm{\theta}
was prone to getting stuck in local maxima of the ELBO. Hence, we adopt the kernel hyperpararmeter learning approach proposed by
Hu et al. 2024
, which uses the update

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}^{\ast}=\argmax_{\bm{\theta}}\left\{\max_{q(\bm{u})}\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx-GP}}(q(\bm{x}_{0:T}),q(\bm{u}),\bm{\theta})\right\}.

The inner maximization can be performed via the closed-form updates as discussed above. We perform the outer maximization, which depends on both
q(\bm{u})
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx-GP}}(q(\bm{x}_{0:T}),q(\bm{u}),\bm{\theta})
, via Adam optimization. In practice, we find that this kernel hyperparameter learning method performs well on both synthetic and real datasets.

K.4
Posterior inference of the drift at unseen points

The sparse approximation
q(\bm{u})
allows us to compute the posterior distribution
\bm{f}^{\ast}:=\bm{f}(\bm{x}^{\ast})
at any unseen latent space location
\bm{x}^{\ast}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
. Recall that

\displaystyle q(\bm{f}^{\ast})=\prod_{d=1}^{D}p(f_{d}^{\ast}~|~\bm{u}_{d},\bm{\Theta})q(\bm{u}_{d})d\bm{u}_{d}.

Since
q(\bm{u}_{d})
is Gaussian and
p(f_{d}^{\ast}~|~\bm{u}_{d},\bm{\Theta})
is conditionally Gaussian, then
q(\bm{f}^{\ast})
is also a closed-form Gaussian distribution. This takes the form,

\displaystyle q(f_{d}^{\ast})=\mathcal{N}(f_{d}^{\ast}~|~\bm{k}_{x^{\ast}z}\bm{K}_{zz}^{-1}\bm{m}_{u}^{d},\kappa_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}^{\ast},\bm{x}^{\ast})-\bm{k}_{x^{\ast}z}\bm{K}_{zz}^{-1}\bm{k}_{zx^{\ast}}+\bm{k}_{x^{\ast}z}\bm{K}_{zz}^{-1}\bm{S}_{u}^{d}\bm{K}_{zz}^{-1}\bm{k}_{zx^{\ast}}).

K.5
Incorporating inputs

For our application of SING-GP to real neural data in
section

5.4
, we incorporate external inputs that may affect the underlying latent trajectories. Following
Hu et al. 2024
, we model this as

\displaystyle d\bm{x}(t)=(\bm{f}(\bm{x}(t))+\bm{B}\bm{v}(t))dt+\bm{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}d\bm{w}(t)

where
\bm{v}(t)\in\mdmathbb{R}^{I}
is a time-varying, known input signal and
\bm{B}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{D\times I}
is a linear transformation of the input into the latent space. We denote
\bm{v}_{i}:=\bm{v}(\tau_{i})
where
\tau_{i}
is a time point in the grid
\bm{\tau}
. We derive the optimal update
\bm{B}^{\ast}
that maximizes
\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx-GP}}
as

\displaystyle\bm{B}^{\ast}=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\left(\bm{m}_{i+1}-\bm{m}_{i}-\Delta t\mdmathbb{E}_{q(\bm{f}),q(\bm{x}_{i})}[\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i})]\right)\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}\right)\left(\Delta t\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}\right)^{-1}.

L.2
Drift estimation on synthetic data

Details on simulated data

The Duffing equation is a second-order ODE described by

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}v}{dt^{2}}+\gamma\frac{dv}{dt}-\alpha v+\beta v^{3}=0,\quad 0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}.

By setting
\bm{x}_{1}=v,\ \bm{x}_{2}=\frac{dv}{dt}
, we can convert the Duffing equation into a system of first-order ODEs. Moreover, we add an independent Brownian motion with noise
\sigma=0.2
to each coordinate. Altogether, this yields the two-dimensional SDE with drift

\displaystyle f_{1}(\bm{x}(t))=\bm{x}_{2}(t)

(38)

\displaystyle f_{2}(\bm{x}(t))=\alpha\bm{x}_{1}(t)-\beta\bm{x}_{1}(t)^{3}-\gamma\bm{x}_{2}(t).

It is straightforward to verify that the Duffing equation has three fixed points when
\alpha/\beta>0
: one at the origin and two at
(\pm\sqrt{\alpha/\beta},0)
.
We generate four trajectories from the Duffing SDE
eq.

38
with parameters
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(2,1,0.1)
and initial condition
\bm{x}(0)=(\sqrt{\alpha/\beta}-0.1,0.1)
, i.e. near the rightmost fixed point. We sample the solution to the SDE using the Euler-Maruyama discretization with
T_{\text{max}}=15
and
\Delta t=0.015
. All
10^{3}
timesteps are taken to define the grid
\bm{\tau}
. A visualization of two of the sampled trajectories is provided in
fig.

3
A.

From the
10^{3}
total timesteps, we sample
3\times 10^{2}
at random at which to include Gaussian observations
\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}(t_{i}),\bm{C}\bm{x}(t_{i})+\bm{d},\bm{R})
. The true emissions parameters
\bm{C}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{N\times D}
are
\bm{d}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
chosen by sampling
i.i.d.
standard normal random variables.
\bm{R}
is fixed to a diagonal matrix with entries
0.1
.

Details on learning

We simultaneously perform inference and learning in the latent SDE model according to the variational EM (vEM) algorithm. For each variational EM step, we take 10 E-steps (according to either VDP or SING) as well as 50 M-steps. In each M-step, we update both the drift parameters
\bm{\theta}
as well as the output parameters
\{\bm{C},\bm{d}\}
. We update the drift parameters by performing Adam on the approximate ELBO
\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx}}
, and we use a closed-form update for the output parameters (see next section). In the case of the GP prior drift, the only learnable parameters are the length-scale and output scale of the RBF kernel. The updates to the posterior over inducing points
p(\bm{u})
are performed during the E-step.

Figure 6:
Prior drift functions learned via vEM with SING and Adam. A: Learned neural network drift. B: Learned polynomial basis drift.

For the GP prior drift we use a
12\times 12
grid of inducing points on
[-6,6]^{2}
. To choose the size of this grid, we looked at the first two principal component scores of the observations
\{\bm{y}(t_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. For the neural-SDE, we use a neural network with two hidden layers of size 64 and ReLU activations. And for the polynomial basis drift, we consider all polynomials up to order three
\{1,\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\bm{x}_{1}\bm{x}_{2},\bm{x}_{1}^{2},\bm{x}_{2}^{2},\ldots\}
. Note that by the form of the Duffing equation
eq.

38
, this polynomial drift function can, in theory, perfectly represent the true drift.

We initialize the output parameters
\bm{C}
and
\bm{d}
by taking the first two principal components of the observations and the mean of the observations, respectively. The GP length scales and output scales are initialized to one; the neural network weight matrices are initialized by
i.i.d.
draws from a truncated normal distribution and the biases are initialized to zero; the coefficients on the polynomial basis are initialized by
i.i.d.
draws from
\mathcal{N}(0,(0.1)^{2})
.

When using SING during the E-step, we increase
\rho
on a log linear scale from
10^{-3}
to
10^{-1}
during the first ten iterations, and we then fix it at
10^{-1}
for the remaining
40
vEM iterations. We found this procedure of starting with
\rho
small and subsequently increasing it on a log-linear scale during the first
k
iterations to result in effective inference and learning
[
25
]
. As for VDP, we choose the soft update parameter
\omega
to be as large as possible while maintaining the stability of the algorithm. For the GP prior and neural-network drift classes, this selection procedure yielded
\omega=0.05
. For the polynomial basis drift, we were not able to obtain convergence with any reasonable choice of
\omega
(see
appendix

H
for a discussion of the parameter

). For this reason, it is omitted from
fig.

In
fig.

6

fig.

3
B. When performing inference with SING, both parametric drift functions, in addition to the GP posterior mean, match the drift
eq.

38
of the Duffing equation (
fig.

3
D). We measure the discrepancy between the learned and true drift by computing the dynamics root mean-squared error

\displaystyle\text{dynamics RMSE}=\left(\sum_{\bm{v}\in\bm{V}}w(\bm{v})\|\bm{f}(\bm{v})-\bm{f}(\bm{v}|\bm{\Theta})\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(39)

Here, we take a fine grid of cells, each with equal
D
-dimensional volume, covering the latent space; we denote the collection of their centers by
\bm{V}
. Then, we compute the proportion of true latent trajectories that fall into the cell with center
\bm{v}
, written
w(\bm{v})
, such that
\sum_{\bm{v}\in\bm{V}}w(\bm{v})=1
. Mathematically,
eq.

39
approximates

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\text{trials}\cdot T_{\text{max}}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\text{trials}}}\int_{0}^{T_{\text{max}}}\|\bm{f}(\bm{x}^{(j)}(t))-\bm{f}(\bm{x}^{(j)}(t)|\bm{\Theta})\|_{2}^{2}dt\right)^{1/2},

where
trials
represents the number of trials and
(\bm{x}^{(j)}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T_{\text{max}}}
is the true latent trajectory corresponding to the
j
th trial.

Closed-form updates for output parameters

Next, we derive closed-form updates for the output parameters
\bm{C}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{N\times D},\bm{d}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{N},\bm{R}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{N\times N}
in the Gaussian observation model

\displaystyle p(\bm{y}|\bm{x},\bm{\Theta})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}(t_{i})|\bm{C}\bm{x}(t_{i})+\bm{d},\bm{R}),

where
\bm{R}
is assumed to be diagonal. These same update equations appear in
Hu et al. 2024
. Although not directly mentioned in the main text, we can also learn the entries of the emissions covariance matrix
\bm{R}
.

Writing out the expected log-likelihood of
p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})
under
q(\bm{x})
, we have

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\bm{y}|\bm{x},\bm{\Theta})]

\displaystyle=-\frac{n}{2}\log|\bm{R}|+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mdmathbb{E}_{q}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{y}(t_{i})-\bm{C}\bm{x}(t_{i})-\bm{d}\|_{\bm{R}^{-1}}^{2}\right]+\text{const}

\displaystyle=-\frac{n}{2}\log|\bm{R}|-\frac{n}{2}\bm{d}^{\top}\bm{R}^{-1}\bm{d}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg(\bm{y}(t_{i})^{\top}\bm{R}^{-1}(\bm{C}\bm{m}(t_{i})+\bm{d})-\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}(t_{i})^{\top}\bm{R}^{-1}\bm{y}(t_{i})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}(\bm{C}^{\top}\bm{R}^{-1}\bm{C}\left\{\bm{S}(t_{i})+\bm{m}(t_{i})\bm{m}(t_{i})^{\top}\right\})-\bm{d}^{\top}\bm{R}^{-1}\bm{C}\bm{m}(t_{i})\bigg)+\text{const},

where
const
represents a generic constant with respect to
(\bm{C},\bm{d},\bm{R})
.
Differentiating with respect to
\bm{C}
,
\bm{d}
, and
\bm{R}^{-1}
yields the update equations

\displaystyle\bm{C}^{\ast}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\bm{S}(t_{i})+\bm{m}(t_{i})\bm{m}(t_{i})^{\top})\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\bm{y}(t_{i})-\bm{d}^{\ast})\bm{m}(t_{i})^{\top}

(40)

\displaystyle\bm{d}^{\ast}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\bm{y}(t_{i})-\bm{C}^{\ast}\bm{m}(t_{i}))

(41)

\displaystyle\bm{R}_{j,j}^{\ast}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\bm{y}_{j}(t_{i})^{2}-2\bm{y}_{j}(t_{i})(\bm{C}_{j}^{\ast})^{\top}\bm{m}(t_{i})+((\bm{C}_{j}^{\ast})^{\top}\bm{m}(t_{i}))^{2}+(\bm{C}_{j}^{\ast})^{\top}\bm{S}(t_{i})(\bm{C}_{j}^{\ast})\right)-(\bm{d}_{j}^{\ast})^{2},\ 1\leq j\leq D,

where
\bm{R}_{j,j}
is the
j
th diagonal entry of
\bm{R}
and
\bm{C}_{j}
is the
j
th row of
\bm{C}
.

By defining
\bar{\bm{y}}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{y}(t_{i})
as well as
\bar{\bm{m}}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{m}(t_{i})
and plugging
eq.

41
into
eq.

40
, we obtain

\displaystyle\bm{C}^{\ast}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\bm{S}(t_{i})+(\bm{m}(t_{i})-\bar{\bm{m}})(\bm{m}(t_{i})-\bar{\bm{m}})^{\top})+n\bar{\bm{m}}\bar{\bm{m}}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\bm{y}(t_{i})-\bar{\bm{y}})(\bm{m}(t_{i})-\bar{\bm{m}})^{\top}+n\bm{C}^{\ast}\bar{\bm{m}}\bar{\bm{m}}^{\top}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\bm{C}^{\ast}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\bm{y}(t_{i})-\bar{\bm{y}})(\bm{m}(t_{i})-\bar{\bm{m}})^{\top}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}((\bm{m}(t_{i})-\bar{\bm{m}})(\bm{m}(t_{i})-\bar{\bm{m}})^{\top}+\bm{S}(t_{i}))\right)^{-1}.

From the equation for
\bm{C}^{\ast}
, it is straightforward to solve for
\bm{d}^{\ast}
and
\bm{R}_{j,j}^{\ast},\ 1\leq j\leq D
.

Discretization experiments

In
fig.

3
E, we investigate the effect of the discretization size
\Delta t
on the performance of the vEM algorithm with SING and VDP. Recall that our true latent trajectories are sampled with Euler-Maruyama stepsize
0.015
, and so we would not expect
\mathcal{L}_{\text{approx}}
to better approximate
\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}
for smaller discretizations
\bm{\tau}
. In the case of VDP, though, we demonstrate that taking
\Delta t
smaller than
0.015
leads to improvements in the stability of the algorithm. Moreover, for large
\Delta t
, performing vEM with VDP is highly unstable and may diverge depending on the neural network initialization. For this reason, we exclude the dynamics RMSE corresponding to
\Delta t>0.15
.

In order to coarsen i.e. downsample the original grid
\bm{\tau}
, we fill in grid points where observations were not observed with zeroes. Then, we consider each
j
th observation in
\bm{\tau}
. If that observation is zero (i.e. there is not an observation at that grid point), we take the nearest observation, should there exist an observation in the original grid that lies between consecutive points in the coarsened grid. This procedure increases
\Delta t
to
j\cdot\Delta t
. In order to make the original grid
\bm{\tau}
finer i.e. upsample, we add
j
zeroes between the existing observations, again treating missing observations as zeroes. This procedure decreases
\Delta t
to
\Delta t/(j+1)
. Note that coarsening the grid may lead to fewer observations, but making it finer will neither increase nor decrease the number of observations.

, we provide additional results from runtime experiments on varying batch sizes. Here, we show wall clock time for batch sizes of
1
,
20
,
50

For larger batch sizes, our implementations of parallel SING and sequential SING both parallelize over the batch dimension. Thus, it is expected that parallel resources on a single GPU may be constrained for models that operate on both large batch sizes and long sequences. In

L.4
Application to modeling neural dynamics during aggression

Dataset specifications

We apply our method to a publicly available dataset from
Vinograd et al. 2024
, which can be found at:
https://dandiarchive.org/dandiset/001037
. We use the dataset labeled as ‘sub-M0L’ which consists of neural activity from one trial of a mouse exhibiting aggressive behavior towards two consecutive intruders. This dataset consists of calcium imaging from ventromedial hypothalamus neurons. In total, 56 neurons were imaged over 420.1 seconds at
10
Hz, resulting in a data matrix with
T=
4201 and
N=
56. Before fitting models, we
z
-score the data such that each neuron’s activity has mean 0 and standard deviation 1.

GP-SDE model details

We fit GP-SDE models with kernel function chosen to be the “smoothly switching linear” kernel from
Hu et al. 2024
. This kernel takes the form

\displaystyle\kappa(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\underbrace{((\bm{x}-\bm{c}_{j})^{\top}\bm{M}(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{c}_{j})+\sigma^{2}_{0})}_{\kappa_{\text{lin}}^{(j)}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})}\underbrace{\pi_{j}(\bm{x})\pi_{j}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}_{\kappa_{\text{part}}^{(j)}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})}

where

\displaystyle\pi_{j}(\bm{x})=\frac{\exp(\bm{w}_{j}^{\top}\phi(\bm{x})/\tau)}{1+\sum_{j=1}^{J-1}\exp(\bm{w}_{j}^{\top}\phi(\bm{x})/\tau)}.

Intuitively, this kernel encodes prior structure in the dynamics such that it “switches” between different linear dynamical systems based on location in latent space, but maintains smooth dynamics between different linear systems. This is enforced by the linear kernel
\kappa_{\text{lin}}^{(j)}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})
and partition kernel
\kappa_{\text{part}}^{(j)}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})
for each linear regime
j
. Above,
J
is the total number of discrete states that the function switches between; we choose
J=4
and
D=2
to be consistent with prior work
[
53
,
52
,
14
]
. We also choose
\phi(\bm{x})=\begin{bmatrix}1&\bm{x}_{1}&\bm{x}_{2}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
. The kernel hyperparameters are
\bm{\theta}=\{\bm{M},\sigma_{0}^{2},\{\bm{c}_{j},\bm{w}_{j}\},\tau\}
, which we optimize via Adam
[
49
]
in variational EM. In particular, for learning we use the approach proposed by
Hu et al. 2024
, which optimizes hyperparameters jointly with the closed-form posterior drift update. Finally, we model observations as

\displaystyle\bm{y}(t_{i})\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{C}\bm{x}(t_{i})+\bm{d},\bm{R})

for every observation time
t_{i}
. Unless otherwise specified, we fit models whose discretization matches the imaging rate; i.e.
\Delta t=0.1
.

Model fitting details

To fit GP-SDE models using both SING-GP and VDP-GP, we chose inducing points to be a uniformly spaced
8\times 8
grid in
[-8,10]\times[-4,12]
. These bounds were chosen by examining two-dimensional latent trajectories obtained by principal component analysis (PCA) on the neural data matrix. Similarly, we initialize
\bm{C}
and
\bm{d}
using PCA and keep them fixed during model fitting. We initialize kernel hyperparameters randomly from
i.i.d.
standard Gaussian distributions.

For both SING-GP and VDP-GP, we perform variational EM for 30 iterations. In each E-step we perform 15 updates (natural gradient steps for SING-GP and forward/backward passes for VDP-GP). In each M-step we perform 50 Adam iterations with initial learning rate 1e-4 to learn kernel hyperparameters.

For SING-GP, we again use a suggested learning rate schedule from
[
25
]
. In particular, we set the learning rates for the first 10 iterations to be log-linearly spaced between
10^{-1}
and
10^{0}
. We then keep the
10^{0}
learning rate for the rest of fitting. For VDP-GP, we use “soft updates” (
eq.

31
) and manually tune
\omega
to the largest value which still maintains numerical stability; in this case
\omega=0.005
.

Since the smoothly-switching linear kernel from
Hu et al. 2024
has several hyperparameters, we repeat our experiments for 5 different random initializations of
\bm{\Theta}
. In
fig.

5
D-E, we report average metrics and 2-standard error bars across these 5 initializations.

Computing posterior probability of slow points

In
fig.

5
C, we use the probabilistic formulation of the drift in the GP-SDE to identify high-probability regions with slow points. To do this, we compute a “posterior probability of slow point” metric for a fine grid of locations in latent space. For a particular
\bm{x}^{\ast}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
, we define this as:

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{P}(\bm{x}^{\ast}\text{is a slow point})=\prod_{d=1}^{D}\mdmathbb{P}_{q(f_{d}(\bm{x}^{\ast}))}[|f_{d}(\bm{x}^{\ast})|<\epsilon]

where
\epsilon>0
is chosen to be a small threshold. Intuitively, this probability is large if the inferred drift at
\bm{x}^{\ast}
is close to 0 in the posterior. Recall from
section

K.4
that
q(f_{d}(\bm{x}^{\ast}))
is a closed-form Gaussian distribution, so this quantity can be easily computed.

Experiment details for
fig.

5
D

In
fig.

5
D, we compare SING-GP and VDP-GP on two metrics as a function of discretization
\Delta_{t}
ranging from 0.06 to 0.1. We fit both methods using 100 variational EM iterations.

The first metric for model comparison is final expected log-likelihood; precisely, this is
\mdmathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\bm{y}~|~\bm{x})]
at iteration 100. We choose this metric to be able to directly compare the goodness-of-fit for each model; in contrast, the ELBOs are not directly comparable due to their slightly different formulations of the term
\textrm{KL}\left(q(\bm{x})\;\|\;p(\bm{x}|\bm{f})\right)
.

The second metric for model comparison is the number of iterations until convergence. Here, we say that the model has converged at iteration
j
if
|\mathcal{L}(q^{(j)},\bm{\Theta})-\mathcal{L}(q^{(j-1)},\bm{\Theta})|<
200 nats, where
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
denotes each model’s respective ELBO function. We note that other choices of convergence threshold yielded similar trends as the plot in
fig.

5
D.

Forward simulation experiment

Here, we provide details on the forward simulation experiment in
fig.

5
E. To compute the predictive
R^{2}
metric in
fig.

5
E, we carry out the following steps.

(a)

For a given model fit and discretization
\Delta t
, select 20 uniformly spaced time points
t_{0}
along the inferred latent trajectory, as well as their corresponding marginal means,
\bm{m}(t_{0}):=\mdmathbb{E}_{q}[\bm{x}(t_{0})]
.

(b)

For each
t_{0}
and
\bm{m}(t_{0})
, use the posterior mean of inferred dynamics
q(\bm{f})
to simulate forward the latent state for
s
seconds using discretization
\Delta t
, where
s=1,2,\dots,15
.

(c)

Forward simulation will yield a predicted latent state,
\hat{\bm{x}}(t_{0}+s)
. We compute the predictive
R^{2}
for each choice of
s
as,

\displaystyle R^{2}=1-\frac{\sum_{t_{0}}\|\hat{\bm{y}}(t_{0}+s)-\bm{y}(t_{0}+s))\|_{2}^{2}}{\sum_{t_{0}}\|\bm{y}(t_{0}+s)-\bar{y}\|_{2}^{2}},

where
\hat{\bm{y}}(t_{0}+s)=\bm{C}\hat{\bm{x}}(t_{0}+s)+\bm{d}
,
\bar{y}
is the mean of all observations in the trial, and the sums are taken over all 20 initial times
t_{0}
.

Additional results

In
fig.

8
, we present additional experimental results to supplement the SING-GP inferred flow field and line attractor from the main text,
fig.

5
C. These results provide new angles of analysis of the SING-GP model fit. Recall from above that the smoothly switching linear kernel from
Hu et al. 2024
encourages smoothly switching linear dynamics to be inferred.

In
fig.

8
A, flow field arrows are colored by most likely linear state. That is, each set of colored arrows represents a different linear dynamical system. Encoding this structure into the kernel allows for convenient downstream analysis of these inferred dynamics, in particular using established properties of linear systems. Moreover, the orange arrows in
fig.

8
represent the columns of the learned input effect matrix
\bm{B}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{D\times 2}
. We find that these input directions correspond to the direction of the latent trajectory at both timepoints at which an intruder enters.

In
fig.

8
B, we plot the inferred posterior variance of the same SING-GP model. We find that model uncertainty is low in the regions traversed by the inferred latent trajectory and high otherwise. In fact, this posterior variance is exactly the quantity that we use to compute posterior probability of slow points in
fig.

5
C. This demonstrates how the probabilistic formulation of the GP-SDE, which we can now perform reliable inference and learning in using SING-GP, allows us to better understand and interpret inferred flow fields.
