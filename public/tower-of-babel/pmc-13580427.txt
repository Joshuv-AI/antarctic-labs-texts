Acute Fulminant Systemic Granulocytic Vasculitis Involving Arteries and Veins in Vacuoles, E1 Enzyme, X‐Linked, Autoinflammatory, Somatic (VEXAS) Syndrome: An Autopsy Case Report
Authors: Kayo Ueda, Masakazu Fujimoto, Yumi Shiroishi, Masafumi Ito, Masahiko Osawa, Mayumi Inaba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVacuoles, E1 enzyme, X‐linked, Autoinflammatory, Somatic (VEXAS) syndrome is a disease concept characterized by UBA1 mutation that was first proposed in 2020. We report an autopsy case of VEXAS syndrome that was retrospectively diagnosed 11 years later through genetic analysis of formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) bone marrow tissue. The patient was a 65‐year‐old male with hepatitis C who presented with general fatigue followed by fever 2 weeks later. Shortly before death, phlebitis of the extremities and marked leukocytosis became evident. The patient died within 3 weeks of symptom onset. Autopsy revealed granulocytic vasculitis involving small‐ to medium‐sized arteries and phlebitis in various organs, including the lungs, kidneys, liver, and lymph nodes. Concomitant myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) was confirmed, leading to a diagnosis of MDS‐associated vasculitis. Sanger sequencing of FFPE specimens 11 years later revealed a UBA1 mutation (p. Met41Val), confirming VEXAS syndrome. Although VEXAS syndrome is typically characterized by chronic, treatment‐refractory inflammation and poor prognosis, autopsy reports are rare. Herein, we report an autopsy case of VEXAS syndrome associated with MDS and systemic vasculitis that presented with a fulminant clinical course.

Introduction

Vacuoles, E1 enzyme, X‐linked, autoinflammatory, and somatic (VEXAS) syndrome is an acquired autoinflammatory disorder first proposed by Beck et al. at the National Institutes of Health in 2020 [1]. Mutations in the ubiquitin‐like modifier activating enzyme 1 (UBA1) on the X chromosome result in loss of E1 ubiquitin‐activating enzyme function, leading to dysregulated innate immune pathway activation. VEXAS syndrome is characterized by vacuoles in immature myeloid and erythroid cells and is frequently associated with hematological abnormalities, including myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS). The disorder primarily affects males in late adulthood, with a median age at onset of 64 years [1]. UBA1 mutations have been reported in 0.02% (1/4269) of men > 50 years of age in North America [2]. Clinically, VEXAS syndrome is generally considered treatment‐refractory, prone to relapses, and typically follows a chronic course. Herein, we present an autopsy case of MDS with systemic granulocytic vasculitis that followed a rapidly progressive and fatal course. The diagnosis of VEXAS syndrome was established retrospectively 11 years after autopsy, following the recognition of this disease entity.

Clinical Summary

The patient was a 65‐year‐old male with a history of hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection. Eleven years before death, the patient had undergone a partial right hepatectomy for hepatitis C‐associated hepatocellular carcinoma, with no evidence of recurrence thereafter. The patient had been regularly treated for chronic hepatitis and hepatosplenomegaly and had no recurring inflammatory symptoms. Three weeks before death, the patient experienced persistent fatigue, followed 2 weeks later by a high fever (39°C). The patient was admitted to the hospital for further examination. Physical examination revealed no significant abnormalities of the chest, abdomen, or rectum. Laboratory testing on admission showed a white blood cell count of 6,400/μL (88% segmented neutrophils, 0% band neutrophils, 3% mononuclear cells, and 9% lymphocytes) and a platelet count of 142,000/μL. Normocytic normochromic anemia (hemoglobin 9.6 g/dL, MCV 88.7 fL) was observed. The C‐reactive protein level was elevated at 16.54 mg/dL. Complement levels showed normal C3 and CH50 levels, with decreased C4 levels. Proteinase 3 antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (PR3‐ANCA) and myeloperoxidase (MPO)‐ANCA tests were negative (< 1.0 U/mL). Rheumatoid factor and antinuclear antibody titers were negative (< 1:40). The patient was positive for HCV antibody (3+). Fecal occult blood testing and urinalysis were unremarkable. Computed tomography (CT) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis revealed worsening hepatosplenomegaly and mild lymphadenopathy, without mass lesions or abnormal lung shadows. Echocardiography showed no significant abnormalities. Blood cultures were negative, and no clear etiology of the persistent fever was identified. On the fifth day of hospitalization, painful subcutaneous phlebitis and erythema developed on the extremities [Figure 1a], prompting a skin biopsy. The following day, painful enlargement of the inguinal lymph nodes occurred, accompanied by marked leukocytosis (50,900/μL) (56% segmented neutrophils, 36% band neutrophils, 5% myelocytes, 0% mononuclear cells, and 39% lymphocytes). Bone marrow aspiration was performed immediately.

Bone marrow smear examination showed normal nucleated cell counts with moderately decreased megakaryocytes and erythroid cell counts. Granulocytic cell counts were significantly increased and were predominantly mature neutrophils. The proportions of each component cell of the myeloid lineage and monocytic cells were within normal ranges. No excess blasts were observed. Döhle bodies were observed in neutrophils, and cytoplasmic vacuoles were identified in myeloid cells, including neutrophils and erythroid cells [Figure 2a,b]. Despite the initiation of diagnostic evaluation, hepatic, renal, and respiratory functions worsened rapidly. The patient died the following day, before the pathological diagnosis from skin biopsy and bone marrow specimens could be finalized, and before steroid pulse therapy could be initiated. Autopsy was performed 5 h after death. Cytogenetic analysis revealed del (13) (q12q14) in four of 18 analyzed bone marrow cells.

Pathological Findings

Skin Biopsy

Antemortem skin biopsy demonstrated superficial subcutaneous phlebitis with fibrin thrombus formation within the vascular lumen and thickening of the venous wall [Figure 1b]. A variety of cells, neutrophils containing nuclear debris, lymphocytes, and round‐nuclear cells slightly larger than lymphocytes, infiltrated the vascular wall and surrounding adipose tissue [Figure 1c,d]. Numerous histiocytoid nuclear cells were also observed [Figure 1d]. Immunohistochemically, the round nuclear cells were MPO‐positive, consistent with granulocytic cells such as myelocytes. The large histiocytoid nuclear cells were positive for MPO and CD68/CD163 [Figure 1e,f] and negative for CD34. The Ki‐67 labelling index (LI) was high, indicating a proliferative population of immature myeloid cells expressing both monocytic and granulocytic markers.

Bone Marrow

Antemortem bone marrow histology demonstrated mild hyperplasia with three cell lineages. Dysplastic megakaryocytes were decreased in number, and erythroblastic islands were poorly formed [Figure 2c]. The number of myeloid cells was increased, with a markedly elevated myeloid‐to‐erythroid ratio (> 5:1) [Figure 2d]. Various myeloid cells, including numerous mature granulocytes, were observed [Figure 2c,d]. Immunohistochemistry revealed p53‐positive cells, HbF‐positive erythroblasts [Figure 2e], and CD42b‐positive small megakaryocytes [Figure 2f]. No CD34‐positive cells were detected. Based on smear findings, chromosome analysis, and pathological examination, the bone marrow was diagnosed as refractory hypoplasia with MDS [3] showing dysplasia in both erythroid and megakaryocytic lineages.

Autopsy Findings

Gross examination revealed hepatomegaly (2320 g), splenomegaly (860 g), and generalized lymphadenopathy involving the mediastinal, para‐aortic, and inguinal regions.

Histologically, granulocytic inflammation, primarily vasculitis, was observed in multiple organs (lungs, liver, kidneys, spleen, pancreas, heart, thyroid, adrenal glands, gastrointestinal tract, testes, and coronary arteries). Vasculitis affected both small‐ and medium‐sized arteries and veins. Various types of vasculitis, including whole or localized inflammation of the vessel wall, endotheliitis, perivascular infiltration, and lymphocyte‐predominant infiltration, were observed [Figure 3a–d]. Inflammatory activity was most pronounced in the lungs, where localized fibrin exudation surrounded alveolar small‐vessel vasculitis [Figure 3e]. Arterial inflammation extended into the terminal bronchiolar wall [Figure 3a], and interlobular phlebitis was associated with thrombosis and perivascular hemorrhage [Figure 3b]. Similar to the skin, immature histiocytoid myeloid cells infiltrated affected vessels along with neutrophils and nuclear debris. In the liver and kidneys, inflammatory cells were clustered around vessels [Figures 3f and 4a], while lymph nodes showed geographic necrosis with nuclear debris and micro‐hemorrhages [Figure 4b,c]. Endocarditis and myocarditis were observed [Figure 4d]. In the spleen and liver, the sinusoids were dilated because of infiltration of a variety of inflammatory cells, including granulocytes, myelocytes, lymphocytes, and plasma cells [Figure 4e,f]. Renal glomeruli and capillaries in the gastrointestinal mucosa were not inflamed [Figure 3f]. No fibrosis or granulomas were observed. Bridging fibrosis caused by HCV infection was observed in the liver [Figure 4a].

The final pathological diagnosis was systemic vasculitis with granulocyte dysfunction associated with MDS [4].

Genetic Analysis

Eleven years after autopsy, genetic analysis was performed on paraffin‐embedded bone marrow and skin specimens obtained antemortem. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification, followed by Sanger sequencing, identified a somatic UBA1 mutation, c.121 A > G (ATG > GTG; p. Met41Val), in the bone marrow specimen [Figure 5]. No mutations were detected in the skin samples, possibly owing to poor DNA quality or a low proportion of lesional cells. These findings established a retrospective diagnosis of VEXAS syndrome 11 years after autopsy.

Discussion

In VEXAS syndrome, UBA1 somatic mutations at the hematopoietic stem cell level [1] result in the overproduction of short UBA1 (UBA1c) [5]. This causes dysregulation of protein degradation by the ubiquitin‐proteasome system, resulting in endoplasmic reticulum stress and activation of innate immune pathways [5]. In mutated cells, inflammatory signals remain unchecked, resulting in excessive secretion of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines.1.5 Activated mutant neutrophils infiltrate blood vessel walls, causing systemic vasculitis.1.5 Furthermore, mutated hematopoietic stem cells undergo clonal proliferation,1.5 resulting in predominant myeloid differentiation and impaired production of erythroid, megakaryocytic, and lymphoid lineages [1] leading to MDS [6] cytoplasmic vacuoles [5] and macrocytic anemia [1].

MDS is the most frequent hematological abnormality in VEXAS syndrome, occurring in 30%–63% of cases [6]. Leukemic transformation is rare, and low‐risk groups are common [7]. Karyotypes are normal in 80% of cases. Even when abnormalities are present, including del(7q), del(11q), del(13q), del(20q), and Y, complex karyotypes have not been reported [6]. Although not specific, cytoplasmic vacuoles in bone marrow cells are characteristic of VEXAS syndrome [7]. In the present case, cytoplasmic vacuoles were observed not only in immature myeloid and erythroid cells but also in neutrophils. At autopsy, they were attributed to degenerative change owing to inflammation; however, considering the current understanding of VEXAS syndrome, these findings are now considered important clues to support the diagnosis. While macrocytic anemia is the most frequent clinical manifestation (96%) [1] the present case was normocytic. This may reflect microcytic anemia associated with active hepatitis C masking the reduction in red blood cell size, resulting in normal MCV.

VEXAS syndrome is characterized by the recurrence or overlap of multiple inflammatory symptoms. Fever is the most common symptom (92%), followed by skin involvement (88%), neutrophilic dermatosis and vasculitis, pulmonary infiltration (72%), chondritis (64%), venous thromboembolism (44%) [1]. The patient in the present study presented with nonspecific symptoms of fatigue and fever, and the autopsy findings suggested a vasculitis‐related condition. Vasculitis is often observed in VEXAS syndrome (23.6% of cases), most often affecting small‐sized vessels (19.1%); however, it has also been reported in medium‐ and large‐sized vessels (2.2% each) [8]. Skin biopsies are frequently performed, demonstrating leukocytoclastic vasculitis in 36% of skin lesions [9]. However, cases of immunoglobulin A vasculitis and ANCA‐associated vasculitis have also been reported [10]. Phlebitis is also seen, with venous wall thickening and thrombosis, particularly affecting medium‐sized veins, suggesting that it is a feature of VEXAS syndrome [11]. Nonetheless, phlebitis also occurs in other conditions, such as Behçet's disease. Recent pathological studies have demonstrated perivascular infiltration of immature myeloid cells with UBA1 mutations, including MPO‐positive histiocytoid cells [12].

The phenotype of inflammatory lesions may vary depending on genetic mutations, such as p.Met41 Leu/Thr/Val. The p.Met41Val genotype (23%) is associated with an increased incidence of vasculitis [9]. This genotype is also considered a poor prognostic factor, particularly when accompanied by pulmonary lesions, mediastinal lymph node enlargement, and multiple organ involvement [13, 14]. Furthermore, higher variant allele frequency (VAF) is correlated with disease severity [15]. In the present case, although VAF was not directly measured, it was presumed to be elevated because of increased UBA1 mutant cells in the bone marrow. Despite the influence of DNA fragmentation, VAF was estimated to exceed the 20% detection limit of Sanger sequencing. The p.Met41Val genotype and high inflammatory activity due to high VAF levels in multiple organs, including the lungs, likely contributed to the fatal clinical course. Although vasculitis is now recognized as a key manifestation of VEXAS syndrome, autopsy‐based reports remain extremely limited [16]. The first autopsy case reported disseminated vasculitis involving pulmonary, cardiac, and cutaneous vessels of various calibers. Although the affected organs and pathological findings are similar to those in the present case, phlebitis was not reported [16].

To our knowledge, this case represents only the second such report to date, providing rare and detailed insight into the systemic distribution and pathological features of VEXAS‐associated vasculitis. Therefore, this case adds important pathological evidence to the evolving understanding of this recently defined disease entity. Our findings highlight the importance of considering VEXAS syndrome in the differential diagnosis of vasculitis with MPO‐positive histiocytoid cell infiltration in older male patients.
