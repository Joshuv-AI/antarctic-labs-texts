Competition in the brain: a conserved principle with lifespan consequences
Authors: Dipanjan Roy, Arpan Banerjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Whole-brain models have long assumed purely cooperative brain connectivity, but Luppi et al. demonstrate that competitive couplings are a conserved feature of the mammalian connectome, markedly improving model accuracy across humans, macaques, and mice. This Comment examines the lifespan implications — from adolescent maturation of long-range competitive connections to their erosion in aging and neurodegeneration — and the framework’s translational potential for personalised brain models and clinical biomarkers.

Departure from cooperative convention

For decades, whole-brain computational models were based on an underlying assumption: that communication and coordination between different brain regions happen through purely positive, or cooperative, couplings only. This means that any positive correlations between brain areas were thought to be due to shared excitation, while negative correlations, when found, were dismissed as modelling artifacts or data noise. Luppi and co-authors1 provide rigorous and systematic counter-evidence to this assumption. They used an informed model of brain region dynamics: Stuart-Landau (Hopf) oscillators near critical bifurcation points, and inferred whole-brain generative effective connectivity (GEC) with both positive and negative values. Notably, in all three mammalian species examined – human, macaque, and mouse – 25–40% of all connections are negative in each individual, and models incorporating this competition consistently outperform cooperative-only counterparts.

The degree of improvement is notable. In humans, the Pearson correlation between modelled and actual functional connectivity improved from r = 0.42 to r = 0.87, while in mice, it improved to r = 0.95. Importantly, the enhancement does not appear to arise from overfitting or unconstrained optimisation. Structural connectivity, derived from diffusion tractography, is used as a biological prior to constrain the GEC, and L1 regularisation penalises complexity. Split-half reliability and ground truth recovery further suggest that competitive architecture is a stable, recoverable feature of individual brains, rather than noise. These findings support the interpretation that competitive interactions warrant systematic consideration as components of brain organisation, not merely as modelling artifacts.

Rooted in biology

The structure of these competitive connections is not arbitrary; rather, they are heavily concentrated along the principal gradient of cortical organisation, where they connect nodes or regions with opposing biological identity: parvalbumin-expressing inhibitory interneuron-dense nodes are negatively coupled to somatostatin-expressing cell-dense nodes, myelination-dense nodes are inhibitory to sparsely myelinated nodes, and so on, along these broadly canonical axes of sensorimotor to transmodal (or unimodal to heteromodal) organisation, which underpin cognition, development, and disease2,3. The biological relevance is further underlined by the cross-species robustness and consistency of these observations: competitive interactions have been shown to be present in humans, macaques, and mice, despite the tremendous interspecies differences in cortex size, cytoarchitectural complexity, and imaging modalities used. This cross-species robustness indicates that these patterns are not human or imaging modality specific but rather mammalian in principle – which the field has systematically failed to represent or model until now. The model does not take a specific stance on whether these connections are implemented via disynaptic inhibition, neuromodulatory gating, or long-range inhibitory projections – the macroscale structure is consistent and replicable regardless. This in particular is a key conceptual advancement from previous studies where researchers often tend to relate negative correlations and negative causal influences to biological inhibition. Departing from this trend the article provide a refreshing way to understand the brain dynamic organisation from a mechanisms of cooperation and competition which can help in construct of several new hypotheses4.

Competitive rather than cooperative mechanisms shapes the dynamic repertoire of brain across species

The most intellectually compelling results may be the degree to which the competitive model makes accurate predictions without specific instruction and training. Specifically, the GEC optimisation only optimises the functional connectivity matrix. Nevertheless, the models that include competition naturally exhibit more realistic metastability, the propensity of brain dynamics to reside briefly near a multitude of activity configurations rather than a fixed point or equilibrium, and a steeper local-to-global ignition hierarchy, whereby some brain regions readily spread activity globally, while others tend to stay local. These two characteristics of brain dynamics are generally regarded as hallmarks of flexible and adaptive brain computation5.

The results also reveal a higher level of synergistic information, a measure of the degree to which pairs of brain regions encode information that only becomes accessible when both brain regions are jointly observed, and cannot be accessed when either region is observed in isolation. Synergistic information has been associated with aspects of conscious awareness, cognitive flexibility, and the capacity of the global workspace to access and integrate distributed representations6,7. The fact that competition naturally increases the level of synergy suggests a fundamental mechanistic relationship between the suppressive coupling of functionally distinct brain regions and the need for the system to encode the information represented in the system as a whole, rather than in either region in isolation. Such a mechanistic hypothesis warrants specific attention in future theoretical work.

Furthermore, it is also found that there is greater similarity with respect to cognitive activation maps generated from Neurosynth meta-analysis for the competitive models than for the cooperative models (r = 0.35 versus r = 0.29; p < 0.001), and also a much higher level of differential identifiability, defined as the extent to which each individual’s functional connectivity profile is distinctive and can be reliably distinguished from those of other individuals This characteristic suggests that competitive architecture is critical for representing essential aspects of individual brain identity and fingerprinting, with significant implications for neuromorphic computing and individualised whole-brain modelling. Taken together, these three properties—increased synergy, a steeper local-to-global ignition hierarchy, and heightened differential identifiability—emerge spontaneously when competitive interactions are incorporated into whole-brain models, without being explicitly optimised, as summarised in Fig. 1f8,9.

A lifespan lens

For researchers interested in lifespan biology, perhaps the most compelling implication of this framework concerns the developmental origins, maturation profile, and eventual degradation of competitive connectivity across the lifespan. Those connections characterised as competitive—long-range, cross-gradient, stretching from the sensorimotor to the transmodal cortex—are precisely the ones most recently maturing in human development. Myelination of long cortico-cortical fibres continues into the third decade of life, and structural and functional segregation of unimodal and heteromodal systems peaks in early adulthood, defining a protracted window during which competitive architecture is progressively installed10. Should the competitive GEC follow this maturation profile, one would expect low levels of competitive coupling in the child, increasing levels of coupling in the adolescent, and peak levels in the young adult (Fig. 1e). As illustrated in Fig. 1e, connectivity rises steeply through childhood and adolescence, driven by the progressive installation of long-range competitive connections, and peaks in early adulthood (~age 20). It subsequently plateaus or gently declines through midlife, with the shaded band reflecting inter-individual variability. Individuals who develop and maintain healthy competitive brain architecture follow an Optimal/Cooperative Trajectory, in which intact competitive connectivity may serve as a buffer against age-related neural decline. A diverging Typical Aging/Compensatory Trajectory emerges around age 65, characterised by a steeper decline associated with two processes: erosion of competitive architecture, in which long-range competitive connections progressively break down, and accelerated neurodegeneration, in which loss of the competitive scaffold is hypothesised to precipitate more rapid neural decline. The widening variability band in later life reflects growing individual differences in the rate of this degeneration.

Normal adult aging, in contrast, shows a progressive decrease in the level of functional network segregation, white matter integrity in long-range tracts, and the distinction between canonical network communities11. While the mechanistic role of competitive coupling in age-related connectivity decline remains to be directly established, the GEC framework offers a natural testbed for such questions. Tracking how the balance between cooperative and competitive GEC weights shifts across the adult lifespan — and whether this trajectory differs between individuals with high versus low cognitive reserve—are among the most immediately addressable questions the framework opens.

Neurodegenerative conditions can accelerate the degradation of the connectivity profile. In Alzheimer’s disease, for example, the default mode network, a region of strong anticorrelations with the dorsal attention network in healthy adults, shows a loss of the antagonistic relationship with task-positive networks early in the course of the disease. Similarly, in frontotemporal dementia, the salience network and its competitive partners demonstrate differential vulnerability. Such a mechanistic approach to the study of connectivity disruption in neurodegenerative conditions using the GEC approach may distinguish between pathological and age-related decline with greater precision than traditional measures of functional connectivity12.

Translational potential and open questions

The clinical ramifications extend beyond dementia. Competitive GEC could potentially act as a principled framework for subject-specific virtual brains, which could be used to predict the outcomes of pharmacological or neuromodulatory interventions before their administration. In this regard, since the model has already demonstrated superior subject specificity, as captured by differential identifiability, it is more likely to account for subject differences in treatment response compared with a cooperative-only approach.

The improved neuromorphic computing capabilities of competitive models, as demonstrated by memory capacity metrics, also have important implications for a cognitive reserve hypothesis. If capacity in brain networks is related to the complexity of competitive models, then differences in competitive connectivity between individuals could potentially explain why some individuals appear to have greater reserve capacity before exhibiting signs of cognitive decline in response to significant neuropathological load. This hypothesis is amenable to direct testing by incorporating GEC estimation with cognitive and imaging data.

However, there are a number of issues that need further clarification. The biological basis of competitive coupling is currently unclear. Long-range GABA connections, disynaptic inhibition via local interneurons, and neuromodulatory suppression are all viable candidates. Laminar imaging, optogenetics, and pharmaco-fMRI, metabolic maps using magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) are likely required to distinguish between these possibilities. The relationship between structural and effective connectivity using multi-scale modelling is also worthy of further investigation. GEC is inferred from white-matter constrained dynamics, but this is likely a non-linear and non-static relationship. Finally, when moving towards pathological models, care must be taken to control confounding factors such as neurovascular decoupling, altered haemodynamics, and grey-matter atrophy. Nonetheless, this is a timely framework, and interspecies data are compelling.

Conclusion

Luppi and colleagues1 make a substantive contribution to the modelling and interpretation of brain interactions. The framework reframes suppression not as a lack of excitation but as a conserved organisational feature across mammalian species. From a biological aging perspective, the lifespan and translational implications are particularly salient: the GEC approach offers a principled computational lens for charting how competitive connectivity is installed during adolescence, maintained in healthy adulthood, and eroded by aging or neurodegeneration — and for linking these changes to individual differences in cognitive resilience and treatment response. Realising these possibilities will require longitudinal datasets, cross-disorder comparisons, and integration with multi-scale biological data, but the modelling framework is now available to the field to pursue these questions.
