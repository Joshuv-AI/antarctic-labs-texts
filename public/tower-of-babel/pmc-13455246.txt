Benefits and challenges of a Patient & Public Involvement (PPI) peer researcher
Authors: Nigel Westwood, Judith Tanner, Lyn Brierley-Jones, Ann Cheng, Melissa Rochon, Ricky Vaja, Jeremy Dearling, Keith Wilson, Gavin J. Murphy, Luke J. Rogers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Involving a patient and public involvement (PPI) peer researcher with lived experience in clinical research is a relatively new concept and to our knowledge, has not been described in adult cardiac surgery. The integration of a PPI peer researcher with a lived experience of a surgical site infection (SSI) was an exploratory component of work associated with an NIHR-funded, mixed-methods study evaluating patients and caregivers’ preferences for post-discharge SSI monitoring. The PPI peer researcher worked alongside an academic colleague, participating in patient observations, qualitative interviews, and thematic analysis. Their shared and personal experiences appeared to encourage some participants to disclose nuanced concerns that differed in tone and content from those shared with the academic researcher, offering an additional perspective to inform collaborative reflection. This approach did, however, present a series of practical challenges: inconsistent hospital approvals, fragmented site-based training requirements, and unclear approval processes created barriers and increased the workload of the academic team. This commentary aims to describe the practical feasibility, perceived benefits and challenges encountered when integrating a PPI peer researcher into this cardiac surgical research programme.

Introduction

Patient and public involvement is integral to clinical research [1]. This understanding has developed from an increasing appreciation that people should not be the passive recipients of the research that underpins their health [1, 2]. This has driven a PPI overhaul of how clinical trials are designed and delivered, altered journal publication criteria to ensure research is co-produced, and given rise to PPI engagement initiatives such as the James Lind Alliance [2]. Despite these well-overdue and positive changes, an increasing concern, certainly of the lay individuals on this project’s steering committee, is that PPI engagement has become more tokenistic and does not ultimately bring about the change desired by patients. This underpinned our decision to explore the involvement of a PPI peer researcher in this work.

Surgical site infections (SSIs) are among the most common healthcare-associated infections worldwide [3, 4]. The evidence base informing clinical practice is weak and therefore, significant variation in practice exists [5, 6]. An NIHR project was funded to explore patients’ and caregivers’ preferences for post-discharge SSI monitoring after adult cardiac surgery, and the factors identified have already been published [7–9]. An additional, exploratory component of this work, suggested by the lay members of the study’s leadership team, was integration of a PPI peer researcher with a lived experience of SSI alongside the academic researcher on site to undertake participant interviews, observe the patient journey through cardiac surgery, and collect data. It was advocated that this approach may enrich the perspective on patients’ and caregivers’ experiences. In this commentary, we highlight some of the unique challenges this presents and offer suggestions as to how these could be overcome.

Materials and methods

This work was an exploratory component of an explanatory mixed-method research project focusing on postoperative SSI wound monitoring [7–9]. The study involved observing individuals throughout their inpatient stay for adult cardiac surgery. Having an SSI was not a requirement. A survey focused on issues related to wound monitoring postoperatively, and in-depth qualitative interviews were conducted between September 2022 and February 2023. This was analysed thematically. Ethical permissions were approved by the East of Scotland Research Ethics Service (21/ES/0098).

Two researchers were involved in data collection: the ‘academic researcher’ (LBJ) was an experienced qualitative researcher with a background in sociology, and the ‘PPI peer researcher’ (NW) had a lived experience of his own SSI as a consequence of abdominal surgery. The PPI peer researcher received bespoke training in the following topic areas: SSI aetiology, risk factors and preventative interventions, confidentiality, data protection, hospital etiquette, writing field notes, and conducting interviews, including online tutorials and mock interviews provided by a senior member of the research team (JT). These findings are presented in accordance with the Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public (GRIPP) 2 short form [10].

Study - Target Wound Infection

Adult patients undergoing elective or urgent (not emergency) coronary artery bypass grafting at one of four hospitals in England were invited to participate in the study and were observed at various stages of their inpatient stay from admission, surgery itself and discharge. The observation field notes were unstructured, although expert input outlined the interventions patients were expected to receive at different stages throughout their hospital stay. The observations lasted from 15 to 270 min and were conducted by either LBJ or NW. Field note data were analysed and coded to generate themes.

In the second part of the study, patients and carers (defined as anyone looking after a family member, partner or friend who needed help due to their health) were invited to participate in an online survey. Recipients were invited to share the survey link with other patients and carers. The inclusion criteria were adults having, or previously having had any surgery or their carers. A wound infection or complication was not an inclusion criterion. The exclusion criteria included people under 18 years of age and individuals living outside of England. Participants completing the survey were subsequently invited to contact the research team if they wished to take part in the follow-up interviews. No interviews were excluded.

The interview schedule was developed following an analysis of the survey data and allowed for an in-depth exploration of issues identified through the survey [8]. The interviews were semi-structured and focused on patients’ experiences of caring for their surgical wound at home and the wound follow-up that may or may not have been provided. The interviews were conducted face-to-face by NW, JT and LBJ at the included sites, online via Microsoft Teams, or over the telephone. The consent process was always undertaken by the academic researcher and included permission to record the audio. Following this, the interviews were conducted over approximately one hour and independently transcribed by NW or LBJ.

Data analysis

Qualitative data from NW’s observation field notes and interview data were analysed to generate themes and categorised together via thematic analysis. All the data were read by a member of the research team (LBJ) and coded manually to generate initial codes driven by the data. The codes were discussed and reviewed with the peer researcher (NW) and other academic researchers (JT) and progressively refined into themes. A second researcher (JT) independently coded a sample of two transcripts to confirm the reliability of the analysis.

Reflexivity statement

NW was a Senior Royal Navy Officer obliged to retire on health grounds after extensive surgery. NW developed an SSI after abdominal surgery and required further emergency abdominal surgery, prolonged hospitalisation, frequent outpatient follow-up and readmissions. Consequently, he has a deep understanding of the impact an SSI can have on an individual and their family. Being self-employed at the time of his second operation, he is too aware of the impact being unable to work can have and the little support there was for people in his situation. Through his involvement as a National Trustee for a charity related to this, his voluntary roles expanded to include both the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NIHR) and the Royal Pharmaceutical Society. The role of a PPI peer researcher appealed because of his desire to assist in the development of a resource to support people whose surgery has been complicated by an SSI. He does not believe that he holds any conscious biases beyond a desire to strive to help others understand SSIs.

Results

The PPI researchers’ written reports documented participants’ specific circumstances, anxieties, and fears in a manner that provided more context than did the academic researchers’ reports. The written reports of the PPI peer researcher provided a narrative writing style that emphasised the participants’ experience as opposed to the more factual descriptions provided by the academic researcher. The following illustrative extracts from NW’s reports highlight some of the additional contextual detail captured;He was aware of the risk of surgical site infections and asked me where my colleague would be positioned in the OR; he was concerned about the risk of infection increasing with more people in the OR,” and “he was not naturally bald, but he had shaved his head as he thought that may help avoid falling hairs.

The semistructured interview format allowed the PPI peer researcher to adopt a more conversational approach, which was evident in the interview transcripts and characterised by equal dialogue between the PPI peer researcher and the participant. The academic researcher and participant interviews, on the other hand, more closely followed a traditional question‒response pattern. These contrasting views revealed some of the underlying context that aided the understanding of patients’ specific concerns and worries. The initial shock many patients experienced when learning of their need for surgery and the possible implications of their age and/or lifestyle were recurrent themes that the PPI peer researcher was able to elicit. It was also clear through the interview transcripts that several admissions made to the PPI peer researcher that were not made to the academic researcher. One example of this was when a participant explained, “I didn’t believe him [the doctor], because that’s what the doctors have to say.”

NW and LBJ were present at sites concurrently, although interviews were conducted independently; this may have influenced the differences in style and content. A further unanticipated benefit of the PPI peer researcher’s presence onsite, alongside the academic researcher, was the improvement it provided to the iterative process of the thematic analysis through immediate discussion of the differing perspectives. This facilitated early identification of key themes and enabled them to be explored in greater detail during the observations and interviews. This was not anticipated during the preparatory phase of this work and improved the data analysis and interpretation [8, 9].

Barriers to PPI peer researcher involvement

Several barriers to the involvement of a PPI peer researcher were recognised throughout this project. The majority of these will be universal to studies and trials trying to integrate a PPI peer researcher in the UK (role specific) although several are study specific. These can be classified accordingly; Site access, approval, and documentation (role specific), Mandatory employee site training (role specific), PPI peer researcher training requirements (role and study specific) and, Data analysis and write-up (study specific). Site access, approval and documentation

Site access presented significant challenges across all four study centres. Each institution requires different approval processes, with no standardised procedures existing for PPI peer researchers. Required documentation included Disclosure and Barring Service (DBS) checks (non-transferable between sites), occupational health clearances, and varied administrative approvals. One staff member commented, “We do not have patients who come onsite, which is why you are having so many problems. This is the first one we have come across.” Approval processes were frequently delayed, with confirmation sometimes received only the night before planned site visits. One site explicitly prohibited patient interaction, negating the purpose of the PPI peer researcher role entirely. This added a significant burden of work to the academic team, which was responsible for coordinating with the PPI peer researcher regarding travel, accommodation, a welcoming individual at each site to show them around, insurance, and supervision of the necessary documentation. This was coupled with the additional anxiety of ensuring everything ran smoothly on the day.

Mandatory site training

Site specific training was compulsory for NW’s attendance at these four sites and was delivered through online e-learning modules that appeared designed for individuals with minimal prerequisite knowledge, even to a lay individual. Healthcare providers required evidence that interviews can be conducted without giving medical advice or incorrect information while maintaining patient confidentiality. The repetitive nature of this training across multiple sites proved both time-consuming and uninspiring for the PPI peer researcher.

PPI peer training requirements

There is limited awareness of established guidelines and training modules for preparing individuals for the PPI peer researcher role, especially in adult cardiac surgery. Despite some excellent resources that exist [11].

Data analysis and write-up

The PPI peer researcher found involvement in data analysis and interpretation to be the most challenging aspect of the role. Without any prior experience in qualitative data analysis, uncertainty existed regarding the expectations and requirements for analysing the collected data and how reports should be written.

Dissemination

NW was invited to speak at several events following completion of this work, including a “research masterclass” and society annual conference, where his experience and learning were shared with a variety of healthcare professionals, patients, and carers. This illustrated that the bureaucratic challenges faced were not unique to NW when trying to co-produce research.

Discussion

This commentary describes the first documented instance of a PPI peer researcher with a lived experience actively undertaking data collection and analysis in an adult cardiac surgery setting. This approach appeared to provide a different, yet complementary perspective to that of the academic researcher. These differing perspectives appeared to improve the iterative process and thematic analysis, to enhance participant engagement and provide a more holistic context for survey responses and interview questions. Several factors appear key to understanding how the PPI peer researcher was able to elicit these differing, more contextual responses. Firstly, the PPI peer researchers’ own lived experience with an SSI and the impact sharing this experience seemed to have on participants’ ability to act as an ‘enabler to let them talk’ and possibly counterintuitively, the nonhealthcare professional status of the PPI peer researcher seems to have added a degree of authenticity and credibility that empowered participants to share their experiences more honestly. In addition, the PPI peer researchers’ relatability, as an individual of the same sex, ethnicity and similar age to the majority of study participants, appears to have bridged barriers that the academic researcher could not. This has implications for healthcare research methodology, suggesting that patient-researchers may elicit more authentic responses than traditional academic researchers or even that academic researchers, particularly those undertaking qualitative work, should share the same cultural and demographic background as the individuals being studied wherever possible. This highlights the value of incorporating patient perspectives as co-producers [11]. Furthermore, the reduced power differentials may also have led participants to feel more comfortable being open and willing to empathise and share their experiences with someone with a lived experience of SSI. The perceived difference in trust between the PPI peer researcher and patients could further inform patient-researcher communication strategies across clinical trials more generally.

This work highlights the need for research sponsors and policymakers to standardise protocols to enable the use of PPI peer researcher roles more widely across clinical research. The current ad hoc approval processes represent inefficient use of resources and create barriers to meaningful patient involvement in research. A national credentialing system, such as “PPI peer research passport or portal,” could streamline processes while maintaining the necessary safeguards.

Limitations

The exploratory nature of this work meant the methodology to demonstrate the impact of a PPI peer researcher on data quality or patient engagement was not utilised. A priori metrics of ‘depth’, ‘authenticity’ or ‘honesty’ were not defined, and an independent or blinded analysis was not undertaken. The experiences described, therefore, reflect the perceptions of the research team and a single PPI peer researcher rather than comparative evidence. The peer researcher’s specific background and lived experience are likely to have introduced biases, and the dual involvement of the same individuals in data collection, interpretation and commentary may have contributed to confirmation bias. Future studies should incorporate independent analytic input and explicit, prospectively defined criteria to evaluate the impact of PPI peer researchers.

Despite the outlined challenges, it is possible to integrate a PPI peer researcher in co-production, and it appears to provide a complementary perspective that would be valuable. Important questions remain unanswered regarding the optimal implementation of the PPI peer researcher role. Future research should examine whether different peer researchers with varied levels of lived experience produce similar benefits and how PPI peer researcher characteristics (e.g., communication skills, health literacy, and prior clinical experience) influence research quality and how this quality compares with that of academic researchers. Evidence-based training curricula and competency frameworks for PPI peer researchers across different clinical specialities are needed and, more importantly, culturally diverse populations should be involved. Furthermore, patients’ perspectives on being interviewed by peer researchers and academic researchers deserve systematic examination.

Conclusion

Integrating a PPI peer researcher with a lived experience of SSI is feasible, although challenging. It appeared to provide an alternative perspective that may not otherwise have been realised. These perceived benefits warrant systematic investigation. The siloed bureaucratic processes that healthcare providers require and the novel concept of a PPI peer researcher make this challenging. A “PPI peer researcher passport or portal” held with a formal research body could mitigate some of these by providing a means of centralising the necessary documentation and formalising national recognition of this role.
