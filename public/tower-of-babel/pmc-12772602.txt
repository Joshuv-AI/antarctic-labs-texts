Heading in football matches: descriptive analysis of heading events from 687 matches across age groups in English Academy and First Team men’s professional football
Authors: Michael Brownlow, Dominic Charles Townsend, Ian Varley, Bradley Sprouse, Mike Hughes, Arron Ackerman, David Taylor, Gill Myburgh, Paddy Riley, Sameer Patel, Antonio Belli, Matthew Green
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo describe the frequency, rates and contextual features of heading in matches across the English men’s elite football pathway.MethodsThis prospective observational cohort study coded purposeful heading events in English Premier League First Team (senior), Category 1 and Category 2 Academy matches played between April 2021 and June 2024. Using video analysis and predefined heading and match descriptors, the study focused on heading frequency, rates, preceding events and unintended head impacts occurring during heading events.ResultsA total of 687 matches were included in the analysis, yielding 26 510 purposeful heading events across 49 703 match minutes. Heading rates/min increased by 13% per age group, from U9s to senior levels, rising from 0.167 to 0.875 headers/min. Central defenders exhibited the highest heading frequency, accounting for 34.2%–36.3% of all heading events. The most common preceding event leading to a header was a long pass. Unintended head impacts during heading events were infrequent (31 occasions, <1% of all purposeful heading events), with the highest incidence (1 per 971 match minutes) observed in senior players and the lowest incidence (1 per 1852 match minutes) observed in foundation (under 9s to under 11s) players. Six players were removed from play following medical assessment.ConclusionsThis is the first published study to report heading rates across an entire elite, men’s football pathway. The findings support data-informed decisions regarding heading exposure in elite male footballers and provide valuable insights for those preparing players for the demands of the elite game at different age levels. Unintended head impacts during heading events occurred more frequently in senior players. Further research is needed to understand the variability in heading exposure throughout an individual player’s development pathway and any potential clinical ramifications.Keywords: Sports medicine, Sport, Football

Introduction

Association football (‘football’ or ‘soccer’) requires the skill of heading as it is permitted by the Laws of the Game. However, a standardised definition of heading does not currently exist.1 For the purpose of this study, heading is defined as an intentional, direct impact between the head and the football. The technical skill requirements to effectively perform a header are influenced by the preceding match event and phase of play, resulting in a variety of header types that require specific proficiencies to meet match demands.2 3 The importance of heading at the elite level is demonstrated by its frequency per player and per match in top European professional leagues, with factors such as league, playing position and level of play influencing heading exposure.2 4 Recent data from the Premier League (PL) also suggest heading is becoming increasingly impactful on the outcome of matches, with the proportion of goals scored via headers rising from 27.8% in the 2019/2020 season to 34.9% in the 2023/2024 season.5 These trends underscore the need for players to be adequately prepared for matches from both a physical and skill proficiency standpoint. However, the optimal approach to developing heading skills across different ages is not well established within the scientific literature.1 6

Balancing performance optimisation with a precautionary approach in relation to heading exposure has become an area of increasing interest, largely driven by European studies demonstrating an increased risk of neurodegenerative disease in former male professional players.7,11 Two of these studies found this risk is higher for outfield players than goalkeepers,8 9 and one reported a greater risk with longer playing careers.8 Although heading was not directly measured in these studies, it is suggested to be a contributing factor to the observed elevated risk. One cross-sectional study reported an association between higher self-reported heading exposure and cognitive impairment, but the odds of impairment were not increased with career duration.12 The reliability of self-reported heading measurements has also been questioned.13 The acute neurological implications of heading have been variably investigated.14,16 While several systematic reviews have concluded that, on balance, heading does not appear to significantly impact brain health in the short term, they also highlight methodological limitations across studies. Notably, deficiencies in study design could significantly influence results, and individual studies have reported transient negative neurological effects following heading exposure.14,16

Considering these findings, it is essential to have a precise understanding of context-specific, individual heading exposure in matches across all age groups within the elite football pathway. Such insight is critical for informing age-appropriate precautionary measures—a position supported by the Union of European Football Associations.17 Establishing individual heading exposure is most commonly and accurately achieved using direct observation or video analysis.1 13 18 Notably, matches are routinely filmed in elite football pathway competitions for broadcast or technical analysis purposes, which provides the opportunity for video analysis.

This study aims to describe the frequency, rates and contextual features of heading events in matches across the English men’s elite football pathway. A standardised video analysis approach will be used to describe heading events in matches (categorised by the competition age group), including an assessment of unintended head impacts associated with heading events.

Methods

Study design

This is a prospective, observational, cohort study describing purposeful heading events occurring in English PL First Team, Category 1 and Category 2 Academy matches. In England, football academies are independently audited and given a category status of 1–4, with 1 being the most elite. There are three tiers to audit: safe to operate, compliance and standards. Academy football is divided into development phases based on chronological age; foundation development phase (FDP; under 9s to under 11s), youth development phase (YDP; under 12s to under 16s) and professional development phase (PDP; under 18s to under 21s). The video analysis methodology is underpinned by predefined operational definitions, aligning with the standardised notational analysis systems described in previous research.19

Participants

After providing verbal and written information to the clubs, male players from PL First Teams, Category 1 and Category 2 Academies were recruited on a voluntary basis. A convenience sampling method was used.

Data were obtained from match footage provided by seven Category 1 and Category 2 Academies. This footage encompassed games played between 1 April 2021 and 15 June 2024, involving players from U9s to U18s. First Team (senior) match footage was routinely recorded and stored online in the digital video management system. To select a representative sample, 40 First Team games from the 2023/2024 PL season (complete season=380 games) were randomly selected using a random number generator (Calculator soup, calculatorsoup.com) and subsequently analysed.

Data capture and extraction

Footage of Academy games was captured using one of three camera systems: human-operated cameras, automated fixed cameras or human-operated internet protocol cameras. For PL First Team matches, standard broadcast feeds (a minimum of 10 per match) were used. All heading events in these matches were systematically identified and coded by trained coders using the video software Fulcrum Angles (Build #1141). Coder training followed a standardised process aligned with recommended practices for coder competency assessment and the optimisation of inter-tester and intra-tester reliability.20 Full details of the coder training programme are included in online supplemental material 1.

Heading event identification in matches was performed manually. Data extraction was conducted in a standardised manner in accordance with predefined fields and definitions. These included both match and heading descriptors. Match descriptors included game format, ball size and duration of the game in minutes as indicated by the referee’s official start and end times. Heading descriptors included the total number of headers and were further categorised by age group, playing position (or pitch zone location for U9–U12 age groups) and the preceding events leading to the header. Definitions for preceding events are provided in online supplemental material 2. Given that head injuries have been proposed as a potential unintended consequence of heading, any instance where a player’s head made contact with something other than the ball during a heading event was coded as an unintended head impact. Detailed definitions for these impacts can be found in online supplemental material 3. These were recorded irrespective of the need for subsequent medical attention from the highly trained medical professionals positioned pitch side throughout all levels of this elite pathway and mandated by rule. This training includes the identification and appropriate management of any head injuries.

To calculate the mean number of headers/min for each age group (heading rate), the total number of all heading events across all matches in that age group was divided by the total number of minutes played in those matches.

Player position

Six classifications of player position were chosen to understand differences in match play exposure: goalkeepers, central defenders, wide defenders, midfielders, attackers and wide attackers.

When coding U9s to U12s, the position the header occurred on the pitch was used as the ‘playing position’ to overcome the challenges of comparing positional demands in games with varying formats and player numbers. However, when formats reached 11 v 11 (U13s through to senior), the playing position was assigned based on how players lined up at the start of the game. Statistical analysis

A comparative analysis of heading events across age groups and development phases was conducted, with heading rate expressed as headers/min. To assess changes in heading rate across age groups, negative binomial regression was applied to account for overdispersion. In this model, the number of headers served as the dependent variable, with match minutes included as an offset variable. Percentage changes across age groups were calculated using the equation (exp(β)−1)*100, where β represents the slope coefficient. To compare heading rates between development phases, positions and preceding events, additional negative binomial regression models were used with false discovery rate-adjusted post hoc pairwise comparisons conducted via the emmeans function (from the emmeans package). Events with a heading rate <0.050 were excluded from analysis, resulting in six preceding events being retained for further comparison. Data on playing position and preceding event were further stratified by development phase, and comparative analyses were conducted using the same statistical approach. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Prism, V.10.2.3) and R software package (www.r-project.org).

Equity, diversity and inclusion statement

The player cohort in this study reflected the demographics, geographical locations and socioeconomic diversity within Academy and First Team football across the English men’s elite football pathway. Thus, findings may not be generalisable to girl and women football players. Our authorship team comprises both men and women from diverse medical and sports science backgrounds, representing a range of professional experiences and levels of publishing expertise.

Patient and public involvement

There was no direct involvement of the public, patients or players in the design or execution of this study.

Playing position

Heading frequency and rates by playing position are shown in table 2. The initial analysis combined data across age groups to compare playing positions. Goalkeepers were excluded from the comparative analysis, as they accounted for <1% of all heading events. Among outfield players, central defenders consistently exhibited the highest heading frequency, accounting for 34.2%–36.3% of all heading events across development phases. They also recorded the highest overall heading rate at 0.185 headers/min (95% CI 0.181 to 0.189), significantly higher than all other positions (all p<0.001). The lowest heading frequencies per phase were observed in wide attackers in the FDP, PDP and senior phase (8.5%–12.0%), and in central attackers in the YDP (9.3%) (table 2). Heading rate in the FDP was significantly lower than in all other phases (all p<0.05). In the YDP, heading rates were also lower compared with the PDP and senior phase across all positions (all p<0.05), except for the wide attackers, where no significant difference was found between YDP and PDP (p=0.170). Additionally, excluding central midfielders (p=0.330), all positions showed a lower heading rate in the PDP compared with the senior phase (all p<0.05).

Preceding event

The most common preceding event leading to a header was a long pass, with a rate of 0.102 headers/min (95% CI 0.099 to 0.105), significantly higher than short passes, throw-ins, corners and crosses (all p<0.001), but not significantly different from clearances (p=0.055). The proportions for all preceding events by age group can be found in online supplemental material 5. Heading rates at each development phase were categorised by open play and set-play preceding events, with the six most frequent preceding events analysed (table 2). Among set-play situations, throw-ins accounted for the highest proportion of heading events in the FDP (19.2%), YDP (9.9%) and PDP (8.4%), while corners were most common in the senior phase (7.2%). When comparing heading rates across phases, no significant differences were observed following throw-ins. However, heading rates following corners were significantly lower in the FDP, YDP and PDP compared with the senior phase (all p<0.05) (table 2).

In open play, heading rate following a long pass, clearance or cross was significantly lower in the FDP compared with all other phases (all p<0.001). The YDP also showed lower rates than both the PDP and senior phase (all p<0.001), while the PDP was lower than the senior phase (all p<0.05). For short passes, heading rates were lower in the FDP compared with all other phases (all p<0.001), but were significantly higher in the YDP (p=0.008) and PDP (p<0.001) compared with the senior phase. No significant difference was observed between the YDP and PDP (p=0.067) (table 2).

Unintended head impact

Unintended head impacts occurred in 31 of the 26 510 purposeful heading events, representing <1% of all headers. From the 31, 6 players were removed from play after medical assessment. The mechanisms of injury associated with these collisions were head-to-head (n=2), goalkeeper arm-to-head (n=2), foot-on-head (n=1) and leg-on-head (n=1). The age groups where removals occurred were U9 (n=1), U14 (n=4) and U15 (n=1).

The lowest incidence rate was observed in the FDP, with five occurrences across 180 matches (1 per 1852 match minutes). In contrast, the highest frequency was recorded in the senior phase, with four occurrences across 40 matches (1 per 971 match minutes) (table 3). Due to the limited number of events, formal statistical analysis was not performed on this dataset.

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive dataset describing heading rates, preceding events and unintended head impacts associated with heading events in matches across the English men’s elite football development pathway. It evidences a progressive increase in heading rates/min across age groups (rising by 13% with each age category) and identifies the playing positions undertaking the greatest heading rate in matches at different stages of the pathway. These findings are highly relevant for stakeholders wanting to make data-informed decisions related to heading exposure in elite men’s football and for practitioners preparing players to meet the physical and technical demands of the elite game at each developmental stage.

This dataset enables calculations of mean match-based heading exposure across all stages of the elite men’s football pathway. For example, we can estimate that ~1651 heading events will occur each season per senior team in PL matches alone (38 games). Of all heading events, an estimated 35% (n=578) would be performed by central defenders. Approximately 75% (n=1238) of heading events are expected to occur during open play and 25% (n=413) from set plays. Of all open play headers, 73% occur following short and long passes or crosses, whereas in set plays, they occur more frequently following corners or free kicks.

However, this data do not support precise estimates of ‘typical individual player heading exposure’ throughout their career due to several variables. The dataset reflects only match exposure and does not account for heading volume during training. There is substantial variability in frequency and rates of heading events during games within and across age groups and playing positions. Moreover, the numbers of matches played and minutes accumulated may vary significantly between players. Individual players may change positions throughout their career, exposing them to greater or lesser heading volumes, and coaches’ preferences on playing style and methods for skill development will also influence volumes of heading events. Therefore, there is likely to be a high degree of variability in heading exposure across individual player careers. Despite this variability, the data presents valuable opportunities to inform and manage heading exposure. For example, removing or modifying common preceding events before a header—such as throw-ins or corners—may present opportunities to manipulate heading rates. Robust evaluation of how changes to restarts or game formats affect heading rates is, however, required to determine the effectiveness of such interventions.

Heading a football has been suggested as a potential factor contributing to the increased risk of neurodegenerative diseases observed in former male professional footballers.7,11 However, research also highlights the multifactorial nature of neurodegenerative diseases, involving complex interactions between modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors.21 A precautionary approach to reduce heading exposure in younger children has been taken by some organisations. In 2015, the US Soccer Federation removed all heading up to age 10, and those aged 11–13 only permitted to head the ball in practice.22 In 2020, The Football Association (The FA) in England introduced similar guidance, removing heading from training for grassroots players up to and including U11, while declaring heading as ‘very low priority’ in the training of players up to and including U16.23 In 2024, The FA introduced additional guidance to further reduce heading exposure, initiating a phased removal of heading in matches for grassroots players aged up to and including U11.24 By studying academy players prior to the phased removal of heading changes implemented by The FA, this dataset will enable comparisons to be made and any impacts evaluated.

To illustrate, a team currently playing 30 matches per season at the U9 level (in 7 v 7 formats) would typically experience ~151 heading events per season. Since 2024, those now entering the Academy system (at U9) will have been prevented from heading within their grassroots settings, which may affect both the entry rate of heading and the subsequent exposure increases (currently 13% increase in heading rate per year from U9 to senior). Assuming the end rate of heading in senior football remains unchanged, it is reasonable to conclude players might face steeper age-specific increments in heading exposure later in their development to prepare for the demands of the elite game, and this curve may steepen further if they enter the Academy system at either U10 or U11. The impact of introducing heading in matches later, and the anticipated steeper increase in heading events, on players’ technical proficiency and long-term welfare remains unknown.

Unintended head impacts

The data highlight that unintended head impacts during heading events are an infrequent occurrence across this elite football pathway, occurring in less than 1% of all heading events. Observational analysis suggests these incidents may be more frequent in senior and YDP matches, but more data collection would be required to establish statistical significance. Also, the U14s is a notable outlier within the YDP data with 56% (9 out of 16 YDP unintended head impacts) being reported in this age group. The reasons for these age-specific rates remain speculative, and causation is likely multifactorial. However, the higher rate in senior football highlights a greater potential risk for concussive injuries at this level, despite no player removal being reported in this dataset. This finding supports the need for preventive strategies and availability of comprehensive concussion management protocols and stakeholder education to ensure optimal player care in the event of a concussive episode.

Limitations

The data presented are specific to elite men’s football within the English pathway and therefore generalisation to other levels of play within the UK, other countries or the women’s pathway is not possible. Additionally, the data up to PDP level are reflective of only seven Category 1 and Category 2 academies, and no data were collected over the age of 18 within the PDP.

Using the location of heading events on the pitch for players up to and including U12s offers a replicable solution for future research and enables positional comparisons across age groups. However, this approach could be subject to further critical review, as any player may perform a header in any pitch location.

Finally, while the analysis recorded the occurrence of unintended head impacts during heading, it did not assess the clinical outcomes of these events.

Future research

Further research is required to quantify individual player heading exposure and assess variability throughout the football pathway. Such understanding is critical for establishing dose-response relationships related to technical proficiency and for the development of evidence-informed guidelines. At present, the most reliable method of data collection involves trained observers systematically recording heading events during matches and training sessions. However, this approach is resource-intensive and may be impractical given the number of players, matches and training sessions that need to be captured. Accordingly, there is a clear opportunity to integrate technological solutions to facilitate more efficient and scalable data capture. Without such advancements, it remains challenging to accurately determine cumulative heading exposure across a player’s career—an important consideration when assessing potential long-term effects. In the interim, findings from this study may contribute to more individualised management of heading exposure, as well as the implementation of targeted educational and precautionary strategies for players.

Additionally, ongoing data collection is necessary to better investigate the clinical outcomes of unintended head impacts associated with heading events. This includes investigating how the impact of modifications to match formats and restarts affects both heading frequency and the likelihood of unintended contact. Addressing these research priorities will strengthen the evidence base and support the development of future guidelines and interventions aimed at optimising both player welfare and performance.

Conclusion

This is the first published study to report heading rates across an entire elite, men’s football pathway. The findings support data-informed decisions regarding heading exposure in this context and provide valuable insights for those preparing players for the demands of the elite game at different age levels. Unintended head impacts during heading events were infrequent but with a greater frequency in senior players. Further research is needed to understand the variability in heading exposure throughout an individual player’s development pathway and any potential clinical ramifications.
