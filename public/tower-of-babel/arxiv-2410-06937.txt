A Covariance Representation and an Elementary Proof of the Gaussian Concentration Inequality
Christian Houdré

Abstract




These simple notes provide a self-contained elementary proof of the
Gaussian concentration inequality which asserts that if
X\sim N(\mu,\Sigma)
and if

f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a Lipschitz function (with respect to the Euclidean distance), then

{\mathbb{P}}(f(X)-{\mathbb{E}}f(X)\geq x)\leq e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2\|\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f\|^{2}_{\infty}}},\qquad\forall\;x>0

(1)

where

\|\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f\|^{2}_{\infty}:=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f(x)\|^{2}=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\langle\nabla f(x),\Sigma\nabla f(x)\rangle.

(2)

(Throughout
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the
Euclidean inner product,
\Sigma^{1/2}
is a positive
semi-definite square root of
\Sigma
, and
\nabla
is the gradient operator).

The proof of (
1
) presented here depends on a covariance representation which
follows from interpolating characteristic functions, i.e.,
interpolating in the Fourier domain
.
This is formalized as:

Lemma 1
.

In
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, let
X\sim N(\mu,\Sigma)
and let
f,g:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be such that

\|\nabla f\|_{\infty}<+\infty
and
\|\nabla g\|_{\infty}<+\infty
.
Then

{\mathbb{E}}f(X)g(X)-{\mathbb{E}}f(X){\mathbb{E}}g(X)=\int^{1}_{0}{\mathbb{E}}\langle\Sigma\nabla f(X_{\alpha}),\nabla g(Y_{\alpha})\rangle d\alpha,

(3)

where

\begin{pmatrix}X_{\alpha}\\
Y_{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}\sim N\left(\begin{pmatrix}\mu\\
\mu\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma&\alpha\Sigma\\
\alpha\Sigma&\Sigma\end{pmatrix}\right),\qquad 0\leq\alpha\leq 1.

(4)

Remark 1.
(i) It is important to note that the first
d
-dimensional marginal of

\begin{pmatrix}X_{\alpha}\\
Y_{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}
has the same law as
X
and so does
its second
d
-dimensional marginal.

(ii) The representation formula (
3
) is well known (see the Bibliographical Comments
at the end of these pages). An elementary proof of it,
based on characteristic functions, will be shown below.
It can also be obtained as a direct consequence of It
{\hat{\rm o}}
’s formula
or, in a more elementary way, using the so-called Plackett’s identities.
For another approach, let
\gamma_{d}
denote the canonical Gaussian
measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with density
\gamma_{d}(dx)/dx=(2\pi)^{-d/2}e^{-\|x\|^{2}/2}
. Let

(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup
associated to
\gamma_{d}
and acting on the space
L^{1}(\gamma_{d})
via

(P_{t}g)(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}g(e^{-t}x+\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}y)\gamma_{d}(dy),x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Clearly, for
g
smooth,
\nabla P_{t}g=e^{-t}P_{t}\nabla g
, and an equivalent version
of (
3
) (for
\Sigma=I_{d}
, the
d\times d
identity matrix) is given by:

{\mathbb{E}}fg-{\mathbb{E}}f{\mathbb{E}}g=\int^{+\infty}_{0}{\mathbb{E}}\langle\nabla f,\nabla P_{t}g\rangle dt=\int^{+\infty}_{0}e^{-t}{\mathbb{E}}\langle\nabla f,P_{t}\nabla g\rangle dt,

(5)

since if
Y
is an independent copy of
X
,
\begin{pmatrix}X\\
e^{-t}X+\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}Y\end{pmatrix}
has the same
law as
\begin{pmatrix}X_{e^{-t}}\\
Y_{e^{-t}}\end{pmatrix}
.
The covariance representation (
3
) which, in contrast to
(
5
), hides the presence of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup
seems to be simpler and more amenable to our purposes, avoiding the study of actions of the semigroup on classes of functions.

Proof of the concentration inequality (
1
)

Let us now show how a simple application of (
3
) gives (
1
).
Again, note that
X_{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{{\cal L}}}X
and

Y_{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{{\cal L}}}Y
. Then, taking
g=e^{tf}
, with
f
bounded and
such that

\|\nabla f\|_{\infty}<+\infty
, with moreover
{\mathbb{E}}f(X)=0
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}f(X)e^{tf(X)}

\displaystyle=t\int^{1}_{0}{\mathbb{E}}\langle\nabla f(X_{\alpha}),\Sigma\nabla f(Y_{\alpha})e^{tf(Y_{\alpha})}\rangle d\alpha

\displaystyle=t\int^{1}_{0}{\mathbb{E}}\langle\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f(X_{\alpha}),\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f(Y_{\alpha})\rangle e^{tf(Y_{\alpha})}d\alpha

\displaystyle\leq t\int^{1}_{0}{\mathbb{E}}\|\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f(X_{\alpha})\|\|\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f(Y_{\alpha})\|e^{tf(Y_{\alpha})}d_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq t\|\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f\|^{2}_{\infty}\int^{1}_{0}{\mathbb{E}}e^{tf(Y_{\alpha})}d\alpha

\displaystyle=t\|\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f\|^{2}_{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}e^{tf(X)}.

(6)

Setting
h(t)={\mathbb{E}}e^{tf}
, the inequality (
A Covariance Representation and an Elementary Proof of the Gaussian Concentration Inequality
) reads as

\forall\;t>0,\qquad h^{\prime}(t)\leq t\|\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f\|^{2}_{\infty}h(t),

(7)

for any
f
bounded such that
{\mathbb{E}}f=0
and
\|\nabla f\|_{\infty}<+\infty
.
Thus,

{\mathbb{E}}e^{t(f-{\mathbb{E}}f)}\leq e^{{t^{2}\|\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f\|^{2}_{\infty}}/{2}}

(8)

for any
f
bounded such that
\|\nabla f\|_{\infty}<+\infty
. The
Bienaymé-Chebyshev inequality gives,

{\mathbb{P}}(f-{\mathbb{E}}f\geq x)\leq\inf_{t>0}e^{-tx}{\mathbb{E}}e^{t(f-{\mathbb{E}}f)}=e^{-{x^{2}}/{2\|\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f\|^{2}_{\infty}}},

(9)

for all
x>0
.
Finally, to remove the boundedness assumption, approximate
f
by

f_{n}=f\mathbbm{1}_{|f|<n}+|n|\mathbbm{1}_{|f|\geq n}.

(10)

Then
|f_{n}(x)-f_{n}(y)|\leq|f(x)-f(y)|
,
\|\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f\|_{\infty}
,
while
{\mathbb{E}}f_{n}\to{\mathbb{E}}f
.
\Box

Remark 2.
(i) In the inequality (
1
), we then are
often left with the problem of estimating
\|\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f\|^{2}_{\infty}
.
A generic estimate is obtained via the weak second moment of
X
, i.e., via

\|\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f\|^{2}_{\infty}\leq\lambda^{*}\|\nabla f\|^{2}_{\infty},

(11)

where
\lambda^{*}
is the maximal eigenvalue of
\Sigma
:

\lambda^{*}=\sup_{\|x\|=1}\langle\Sigma x,x\rangle=\sup_{\|x\|=1}{\mathbb{E}}|\langle X,x\rangle|^{2}.

(12)

(ii) In some instances, e.g., for some particular classes of functions, the weak
moment of order two of
X
can be replaced by the strong second moment, i.e., by

\sigma^{*2}=\max_{i=1,\dots,d}\sigma_{i,i}=\max_{i=1,\dots,d}\mathop{\rm Var}X_{i}
.
A case at hand is
f(x)=\max_{1\leq i\leq d}x_{i}
.
Indeed, then,

\langle\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f(x),\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f(x)\rangle=\sum^{d}_{i=1}\sum^{d}_{j=1}\sigma_{i,j}\frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x_{j}}=\sum^{d}_{i=1}\sigma_{i,i}\left(\frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x_{i}}\right)^{2},

(13)

since for
i\neq j
,
{\partial f}/{\partial x_{i}}
and
{\partial f}/{\partial x_{j}}

live on disjoint sets. Now (
13
) is itself upper bounded by

\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\sigma_{i,i}\sum^{d}_{i=1}\left(\frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x_{i}}\right)^{2}\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\sigma_{i,i}=\sigma^{*2},

(14)

since
\sum^{d}_{i=1}\left({\partial f}/{\partial x_{i}}\right)^{2}\leq 1
.
There are a few ways to obtain this last fact: For example,
if
e=(1,\dots,1)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\max(x+te)=(\max x)+t
. Hence by the chain rule,

1=d\max(x+te)/dt=\sum^{d}_{i=1}{\partial\max x}/{\partial x_{i}}
.

Another way to show (
13
) is to note that

|\max(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})-\max(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})|\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq d}|x_{i}-y_{i}|\leq\sqrt{\sum^{d}_{i=1}(x_{i}-y_{i})^{2}},

(15)

and then to appeal to the fact that if
f\in\mathop{\rm Lip}(1)
, with respect to the Euclidean
norm, then (Rademacher Theorem)
\sum^{d}_{i=1}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}\right)^{2}\leq 1
.

(iii) With a bit more effort, it is possible to get an improved version of the
Gaussian concentration inequality, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(f(X)-{\mathbb{E}}f(X)\geq x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{{\mathbb{E}}(f-{\mathbb{E}}f)^{+}}{x}e^{-x^{2}/2\|\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f\|^{2}_{\infty}}

(16)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sqrt{2}x}\|\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f\|_{\infty}e^{-x^{2}/2\|\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f\|^{2}_{\infty}},

(17)

for all
x>0
.

(iv) The term
\|\Sigma^{1/2}\nabla f\|^{2}_{\infty}
, appears in (
1
)
because of the use of the
Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Different types of upper bounds occur by applying, instead,
Young’s inequality, potentially leading to improve concentration results for special
classes of functions.

Proof of Lemma 1
The idea of the proof is to interpolate between the independent
case,
\alpha=0
and the ”fully dependent identical” case
\alpha=1
. Here is what we mean:

In
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
, we form the vector
\begin{pmatrix}X_{\alpha}\\
Y_{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}
,

0\leq\alpha\leq 1
, which is Gaussian with mean vector

\alpha\begin{pmatrix}\mu\\
\mu\end{pmatrix}+(1-\alpha)\begin{pmatrix}\mu\\
\mu\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mu\\
\mu\end{pmatrix}

and covariance matrix
\alpha\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma&\Sigma\\
\Sigma&\Sigma\end{pmatrix}+(1-\alpha)\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma&0\\
0&\Sigma\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma&\alpha\Sigma\\
\alpha\Sigma&\Sigma\end{pmatrix}
.
So for
\alpha=0
the vector
\begin{pmatrix}X_{0}\\
Y_{0}\end{pmatrix}
is normal with

X_{0}
and
Y_{0}
independent and
X_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{{\cal L}}}Y_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{{\cal L}}}X
,
while for
\alpha=1
,
\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}\\
Y_{1}\end{pmatrix}
is a Gaussian vector on

{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
with
X_{1}=Y_{1}
and
X_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{{\cal L}}}Y_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{{\cal L}}}X
.
Hence if
{\mathbb{E}}_{\alpha}
denotes the expectation with respect to the Gaussian
measure corresponding to
\begin{pmatrix}X_{\alpha}\\
Y_{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(f(X)g(X))

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{1}(f\otimes g)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}f(X){\mathbb{E}}g(X)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{0}(f\otimes g),

(18)

where

\displaystyle f\otimes g:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle(x,y)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow(f\otimes g)(xy)=f(x)g(y).

Now the characteristic function of
\begin{pmatrix}X_{0}\\
Y_{0}\end{pmatrix}
is given by

\varphi_{0}(t,s):=\varphi_{(X_{0},Y_{0})}(t,s)=\varphi_{X}(t)\varphi_{X}(s),

(19)

where
\varphi_{X}
is the characteristic function of
X
, i.e.,

\varphi_{X}(t)=e^{i\langle\mu,t\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle\Sigma t,t\rangle}
.
Now, the characteristic
function of
\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}\\
Y_{1}\end{pmatrix}
is given by

\varphi_{1}(t,s):=\varphi_{(X_{1},Y_{1})}(t,s)=\varphi_{X}(t+s).

(20)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\varphi_{(X_{\alpha},Y_{\alpha})}(t,s)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}e^{i\langle(t,s),(X_{\alpha},Y_{\alpha})\rangle}=\left[\varphi_{(X_{1},Y_{1})}(t,s)\right]^{\alpha}\left[\varphi_{(X_{0},Y_{0})}(t,s)\right]^{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle=\varphi^{\alpha}_{X}(t+s)\varphi^{1-\alpha}_{X}(t)\varphi^{1-\alpha}_{X}(s).

(21)

So using the notation
\varphi_{\alpha}(t,s)
,
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
, to prove
(
3
) note that

\varphi_{1}(t,s)-\varphi_{0}(t,s)=\int^{1}_{0}\frac{d}{d\alpha}\varphi_{\alpha}(t,s)d\alpha,

(22)

and that

\frac{d}{d\alpha}\varphi_{\alpha}(t,s)=-\langle\Sigma t,s\rangle\varphi_{\alpha}(t,s)={\mathbb{E}}\langle\Sigma\nabla f(X_{\alpha}),\nabla g(Y_{\alpha})\rangle,

(23)

for the functions

f(x)=e^{i\langle t,x\rangle}
and
g(x)=e^{i\langle s,x\rangle}
.
Plugging (
23
) into (
22
) just gives (
3
) for such
f
and
g
.
Then by bilinearity, the equality (
3
) extends to trigonometric
polynomial
\sum^{N}_{i=1}a_{j}e^{i\langle t_{j},x\rangle}
and

g(x)=\sum^{N}_{j=1}b_{j}e^{i\langle s_{j},x\rangle}
from there “by density” to the
class of functions described in the lemma.
\Box
