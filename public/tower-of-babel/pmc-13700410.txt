Family caregivers’ experiences with mHealth and related digital health support for people living with dementia: a qualitative evidence synthesis using JBI meta-aggregation
Authors: Hui Gao, Shulin Sang, Ling Chen, Fan Qin, Wenting Qin, Lianzhao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo synthesize qualitative evidence on the perceived experiences, barriers, and support needs of family caregivers of people living with dementia when using mHealth and related digital health support, and to clarify acceptability, usability, feasibility, and implementation considerations for dementia home care.MethodsPubMed, Web of Science, PsycINFO, Embase, CINAHL, Cochrane Library, CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and SinoMed were searched from database inception to 17 June 2025. Qualitative studies and qualitative components of mixed-methods studies were eligible if they reported extractable findings on family caregivers’ experiences of using mHealth or related digital health support for dementia care. Study quality was appraised with the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research. Findings were synthesized using JBI meta-aggregation, and confidence in the synthesized findings was assessed with the CERQual approach.ResultsTwenty-four studies were included. Fifty-five findings were aggregated into eight categories and three synthesized findings: caregivers perceived mHealth and related digital health support as convenient, informative, and emotionally supportive (CERQual: moderate to high); caregivers reported device-related, technical, and contextual barriers that limited use (CERQual: moderate to high); and caregivers expressed needs for integrated functions, personalized content, professional support, and stage-specific care guidance (CERQual: high). Confidence was mainly limited by incomplete researcher reflexivity reporting, heterogeneity of digital support formats, contextual differences across countries, and incomplete reporting of caregiver type or dementia stage in some studies.ConclusionQualitative evidence suggests that caregivers generally perceive mHealth and related digital health support as useful for information access, emotional reassurance, and care coordination, but sustained use is constrained by usability problems, digital literacy barriers, insufficient personalization, and uneven professional integration. These findings inform caregiver-centered design and implementation requirements; they do not establish clinical effectiveness or superiority over usual care.Keywords: Dementia, Family caregivers, mHealth, Digital health, Qualitative evidence synthesis, Meta-aggregation

Background

Dementia is a major global public health challenge, and China accounts for a large proportion of older adults living with dementia [1, 2]. Family caregivers provide most day-to-day support for people living with dementia, particularly in settings where long-term care services remain limited. This caregiving role is associated with emotional strain, reduced quality of life, care-skill uncertainty, and financial and time pressures [3–6]. As dementia progresses, caregivers need practical, accessible, and trustworthy support that can respond to changing symptoms, behavioral challenges, and care decisions.

Mobile health (mHealth), commonly understood as the use of mobile and wireless technologies to support health practice, has been proposed as one way to improve access to caregiver support [7]. In dementia care, however, the evidence base often extends beyond strict mobile applications and includes web-based platforms, telehealth, online psychoeducation, digital communities, tablet-based reminiscence programs, artificial-intelligence chatbots, and broader information and communication technology services [8–34]. To avoid overstating the scope of narrowly defined mHealth, this review uses the term mHealth and related digital health support when referring to the whole evidence base, while using mHealth more narrowly for mobile-device or mobile-application interventions.

Existing reviews have summarized assistive technologies, technology-delivered psychosocial interventions, telehealth, mHealth applications, broader technological interventions, psychoeducational interventions in Asian caregiver contexts, and the support needs of dementia caregivers [8, 34–44]. A recent qualitative meta-synthesis focused specifically on supportive mobile apps [34], whereas other reviews mainly addressed intervention effectiveness, app content, technology-based counseling, structured psychoeducation, or broad caregiver experiences [35–44]. These reviews provide important background, but several gaps remain. First, intervention-focused and psychoeducation-focused reviews often emphasize feasibility, effectiveness, or technology design rather than caregivers’ qualitative experiences. Second, app-focused reviews may exclude web-based, telehealth, online, ICT-supported, or hybrid digital support formats that caregivers encounter in practice. Third, caregiver-need syntheses may not distinguish perceived usefulness, usability barriers, functional expectations, and implementation requirements across digital formats. Fourth, the transferability of findings across countries, care systems, and technology types remains uncertain. A qualitative evidence synthesis can address these gaps by aggregating caregivers’ reported experiences while preserving the distinction between perceived benefits and objectively demonstrated clinical effects.

This study aimed to synthesize qualitative evidence on caregivers’ perceived benefits, barriers, needs, and implementation expectations regarding mHealth and related digital health support in dementia home care. The review was designed to inform acceptability, usability, feasibility, adherence-related considerations, and implementation requirements, rather than to determine clinical effectiveness or superiority over usual care.

Methods

Study design

This review followed methodological guidance for qualitative systematic reviews and JBI meta-aggregation [45, 46]. Reporting was guided by the Enhancing Transparency in Reporting the Synthesis of Qualitative Research (ENTREQ) statement and by PRISMA 2020 flow-reporting principles for study selection [47, 48]. The review protocol was not prospectively registered. To reduce selective-reporting risk despite the absence of prospective registration, the eligibility criteria and extraction fields used for this review were finalized before formal study selection and were not changed in response to the synthesized findings. JBI appraisal procedures, category aggregation, and CERQual judgments were documented in the review working files and cross-checked by multiple reviewers. Category development was conducted after finding extraction and before final synthesis wording, so the categories were derived from extracted findings rather than from a prespecified desired conclusion; the absence of prospective registration remains acknowledged as a methodological limitation.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted by the author review team, without librarian-mediated searching, in PubMed, Web of Science, PsycINFO, Embase, CINAHL, Cochrane Library, CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and SinoMed from database inception to 17 June 2025. These sources were selected to cover major international biomedical, nursing, psychology, multidisciplinary, evidence-synthesis, and Chinese biomedical literature relevant to dementia caregiving and digital health. The search focused on peer-reviewed journal literature and database-indexed academic records; the exclusion of reviews, conference-only or non-original records, duplicate publications, records outside the caregiver-dementia-digital support topic, and records without eligible qualitative findings is reported under Eligibility criteria.

The strategy was rebuilt around five concept blocks: dementia or cognitive impairment, family or informal caregivers, mHealth and related digital health support, qualitative methods, and caregiver experiences or needs. The digital-support block included mobile health, mobile applications, smartphone-based support, eHealth, digital health, telehealth, telemedicine, online or web-based programs, internet platforms, WeChat, and information and communication technology support. The qualitative-experience block included qualitative research, interviews, focus groups, phenomenology, ethnography, grounded theory, thematic analysis, content analysis, experience, perception, attitude, need, acceptability, usability, barriers, facilitators, and preferences.

Chinese and English strategies used equivalent conceptual blocks but were adapted to database-specific controlled vocabulary, field tags, and search syntax. The original search identified 2065 database records before deduplication: VIP (n = 81), Wanfang (n = 15), CNKI (n = 21), SinoMed (n = 6), CINAHL (n = 76), Embase (n = 434), Cochrane Library (n = 165), PubMed (n = 323), Web of Science (n = 939), and PsycINFO (n = 5). The full database-specific strategies and original database-level counts are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were developed using the PICo/PICOS logic appropriate for qualitative evidence synthesis. Participants were family or informal caregivers of people living with dementia. The phenomenon of interest was caregivers’ attitudes, expectations, experiences, perceived usefulness, barriers, and needs when using mHealth or related digital health support during home or community-based dementia care. Eligible designs were qualitative studies and qualitative components of mixed-methods studies that reported extractable qualitative findings. For mixed-methods studies, only qualitative findings, qualitative themes, or author-level paraphrases grounded in participant data and relevant to the phenomenon of interest were synthesized; quantitative outcome data were not pooled or reinterpreted as qualitative evidence. Exclusion criteria were review articles, conference-only reports without sufficient qualitative data, non-Chinese or non-English publications, duplicate publications or overlapping reports from the same study population, records outside the caregiver-dementia-digital support topic, and records whose findings were not relevant to caregivers’ experiences, needs, barriers, or perceived usefulness of mHealth or related digital support. Because this review focused on caregivers’ qualitative experiences rather than patient-level clinical characteristics, no age restriction was applied to the people living with dementia.

Study screening and data extraction

Two trained researchers (FQ and WTQ) independently screened titles and abstracts, assessed full texts, and extracted study information using Microsoft Excel-based standardized review spreadsheets. Disagreements were resolved by discussion and, when necessary, by consultation with a third investigator (LC). Extracted information included author, year, country or region, study design, participants, digital support type, target care problem, intervention or technology features, caregiver and dementia context, phenomenon of interest, and main qualitative findings. For Chinese-language studies, extraction was first conducted in the source language and then translated or summarized in English for synthesis; bilingual checking was used to preserve the meaning of participant-grounded concepts, author-reported themes, and culturally specific terms. For English-language studies, extracted concepts were compared with the Chinese synthesis categories to ensure that equivalent concepts were not separated solely because of language differences.

At full-text screening, exclusion reasons were recorded against the review topic and phenomenon of interest. In the PRISMA flow used for the revised manuscript, all 24 full-text exclusions were classified as topic mismatch; no reports were recorded as not retrieved. No qualitative findings were inferred from titles or abstracts alone, and no findings from excluded reports were used in the synthesis.

Quality appraisal

Two trained researchers (FQ and WTQ) independently appraised methodological quality using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research [45], and disagreements were checked with LC before consensus grading. The checklist addresses congruity among philosophical perspective, methodology, research question, data collection, data analysis, interpretation, researcher reflexivity, representation of participants, ethics approval, and conclusions. Each item was rated Yes, No, or Unclear. Overall quality grades were interpreted as follows: Grade A was assigned when all 10 items were rated Yes; Grade B was assigned when 7–9 items were rated Yes and no single limitation was judged to undermine the credibility of the study findings; and Grade C was assigned when fewer than 7 items were rated Yes or when multiple core criteria were rated No or Unclear. Studies were not excluded solely because of moderate limitations; instead, appraisal findings were used to qualify synthesis confidence and interpretation.

Meta-aggregation and CERQual assessment

Following JBI meta-aggregation procedures, findings were extracted from participant-grounded data, author-reported findings, or clearly stated qualitative results from the included studies and reported in the audit trail as author-level paraphrases. Findings were retained only when they were supported by participant data or by an explicitly reported qualitative theme in the primary study. Unsupported author commentary, quantitative outcome statements, and background statements not grounded in qualitative data were not treated as findings. Where the support for a finding was unclear, the item was treated cautiously and was not used to generate a stronger synthesized claim. Findings with similar meanings were grouped inductively into categories within the JBI meta-aggregation framework, and categories were then aggregated into synthesized findings. The synthesis was managed through structured extraction and aggregation tables rather than automated qualitative-coding software. To reduce reinterpretation, the synthesis stayed close to participant language and primary-study author interpretations and did not attempt to generate a new explanatory theory. Category development and synthesized findings were cross-checked by at least two reviewers (HG and SLS), with disagreements resolved by consensus with LC and LZY. A synthesis audit trail linking extracted findings, categories, and synthesized findings is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Confidence in each synthesized finding was assessed using CERQual by HG and SLS and checked against the JBI appraisal record, contributing studies, and adequacy of data; CERQual considers methodological limitations, relevance, coherence, and adequacy of data [49]. Methodological limitations were informed by the JBI appraisal, relevance by the match between contributing studies and the review question, coherence by the consistency of category meanings across studies, and adequacy by the richness and quantity of supporting data. CERQual judgments were used to qualify the certainty and transferability of conclusions rather than to convert qualitative findings into claims of objective effectiveness.

Meta-aggregation findings

A total of 55 findings were extracted from the included studies, grouped into eight categories, and aggregated into three synthesized findings. The following sections report caregivers’ perceived experiences and needs. They should not be read as evidence that digital support objectively improves clinical outcomes or is superior to usual care. Supplementary Table S2 shows the audit trail used to connect extracted findings to categories and synthesized findings; the revised audit trail uses full-text-supported author-level paraphrases rather than unsupported direct quotations.

Synthesized finding 1: caregivers perceived mHealth and related digital health support as convenient, informative, and emotionally supportive

Caregivers reported that digital support could make professional or peer support easier to access, especially when travel, appointment burden, or time constraints limited in-person services. Some caregivers described being able to ask questions at convenient times, obtain rapid responses, receive home-based support, or book appointments through digital links. These reports suggest perceived access benefits, not objective evidence of improved clinical outcomes [10, 13, 15, 17–19, 21–33].

Caregivers also perceived digital tools as useful sources of dementia-related information. Some participants reported that platforms helped them understand dementia, recognize care needs, reassess disease stage, or feel more confident about care decisions. This category was supported by studies that described educational content, recommendation systems, web-based toolkits, and online programs [10, 21, 25, 27, 30–33].

Several studies described emotional reassurance and social support. Caregivers reported feeling less alone when they could communicate with other caregivers, receive feedback, or share experiences. Some participants described digital support as a way to express fear, anxiety, or frustration and to receive emotional validation. These findings support perceived psychological support and social connection, while not proving mental-health effectiveness [17–19, 25–28, 31].

Synthesized finding 2: caregivers reported device-related, operational, and contextual barriers

Caregivers described barriers related to device design, platform usability, information burden, and unclear next steps. Some reported difficulty entering applications, slow or unsmooth consultation processes, repetitive content, interfaces perceived as impersonal or insufficiently responsive to caregivers’ emotional needs, and uncertainty about whether monitoring data were being recorded. Others perceived digital support as additional work rather than relief when the technology did not provide practical guidance [14, 16, 20, 21, 23–27, 29, 31].

Operational barriers were especially salient for older caregivers or caregivers with limited digital literacy. Participants described low self-efficacy, technical anxiety, lack of tutorials, and concern about making errors. These barriers were not isolated from design issues: unfamiliar technology could make poor navigation more stressful, and unclear platform feedback could reinforce caregivers’ sense of incompetence [14, 16, 20, 21, 23–27, 29, 31].

Synthesized finding 3: caregivers expressed needs for integrated, personalized, and professionally supported services

Caregivers expressed a need for expanded functions, including searchable dementia service directories, integrated care resources, psychological-support channels, professional consultation, practical resource referrals, and clearer information classification. Some participants wanted one-stop systems that could connect educational materials, care plans, community resources, and professional support [11, 12, 21, 29–31, 33].

Caregivers also wanted personalized and stage-specific content. Several participants described the need for guidance tailored to early, middle, or late dementia stages, individualized symptom changes, and care-plan feedback after implementation. These findings indicate that caregivers valued dynamic and context-sensitive support rather than generic information dissemination alone [11, 12, 21, 29–31, 33].

Discussion

Principal findings

This JBI meta-aggregation synthesized qualitative evidence from 24 studies on family caregivers’ experiences with mHealth and related digital health support in dementia care. Three linked findings emerged. First, caregivers often perceived digital support as convenient, informative, and emotionally reassuring. Second, sustained use was constrained by device-related barriers, low digital confidence, unclear navigation, repetitive or poorly tailored content, and lack of practical feedback. Third, caregivers wanted integrated, personalized, stage-specific, and professionally supported services. These findings concern perceived experiences and implementation requirements; they do not establish clinical effectiveness or superiority over usual care.

Acceptability and perceived usefulness

Caregivers’ positive accounts mainly reflected acceptability and perceived usefulness. Digital support was valued when it reduced practical barriers to information, enabled timely communication, supported peer connection, or helped caregivers feel more prepared for care tasks. This is consistent with the broader literature suggesting that dementia-caregiver technologies and psychoeducational interventions may be useful when they address concrete support needs and fit existing caregiving routines [8, 9, 34, 37, 44]. The present review extends app-focused qualitative evidence by including web-based, telehealth, online, ICT-supported, tablet-based, recommendation-system, and chatbot-related formats [10–34]. However, perceived usefulness should not be conflated with demonstrated effectiveness. Qualitative evidence can explain why caregivers may accept or reject digital support, but it cannot determine whether a tool improves patient outcomes, caregiver burden, or health-service utilization.

Digital-format heterogeneity also limits how specifically the synthesized findings can be applied to any single technology type. Chatbot and recommender-system studies mainly informed acceptability, information access, and interface-response concerns; telehealth and ICT-supported service studies contributed evidence on service continuity and remote communication; online courses and web toolkits contributed evidence on psychoeducation, signposting, and structured learning; and tablet-based reminiscence studies contributed evidence on engagement and relationship-centered support. The same three synthesized findings were visible across formats, but the evidence does not show that all formats produce the same barriers or support needs. Therefore, technology-specific implementation should be guided by the contributing modality, target care problem, caregiver digital literacy, and local service setting.

Usability and digital inclusion

The barriers identified in this synthesis highlight the importance of usability and digital inclusion. Caregivers described difficulty starting applications, navigating repeated content, understanding platform functions, and trusting whether monitoring or feedback systems were working. These problems are particularly important for older caregivers and caregivers with lower digital literacy, especially where technology anxiety, wearable-device use, aging-design needs, or digital poverty may affect digital-health uptake [50–53]. Age-friendly design, clear onboarding, transparent charging information, concise content organization, privacy-aware communication, and responsive technical support are therefore implementation requirements grounded in caregivers’ reported barriers. Specific design features should be justified by dementia-caregiver evidence rather than by unrelated disease populations.

Feasibility, adherence, and implementation requirements

The findings also suggest that feasibility and adherence depend on more than initial interest. Caregivers wanted integrated resources, professional confirmation, practical next-step guidance, and personalized content that changes with disease stage and care context. Interventions limited to generic information may be perceived as less useful over time. Future development should therefore distinguish acceptability, usability, feasibility, adherence, implementation, and effectiveness. Qualitative findings can inform the first five domains, while effectiveness requires comparative trials, pragmatic implementation studies, or other outcome-focused designs [54, 55]. This distinction is important because several included studies evaluated early-stage development, acceptability, or user experience rather than sustained implementation in routine care.

Transferability to Chinese dementia care

Most included studies were conducted outside mainland China, and this limits direct transferability to Chinese dementia care. Caregiving in China is shaped by family-centered decision-making, informal family responsibility for day-to-day care, uneven access to long-term care and community dementia services, high reliance on familiar communication platforms such as WeChat, differences between urban and rural service access, and variable trust in online services [50, 52, 53, 56, 57]. For synthesized finding 1, perceived convenience and emotional reassurance may be facilitated by familiar mobile communication infrastructure, but only if platforms are connected to trusted local services. For synthesized finding 2, usability, digital-literacy, privacy, and trust barriers may be amplified among older or rural caregivers and in settings with fragmented community referral pathways. For synthesized finding 3, needs for personalized content, professional support, and stage-specific guidance are especially relevant where family caregivers carry major decision-making responsibilities with limited specialist access. Asian evidence on psychoeducational interventions for dementia caregivers supports the importance of structured caregiver education, but it does not establish that every digital format in this review is clinically effective in Chinese settings [44]. Therefore, Chinese implementation should be evaluated through locally conducted usability, feasibility, qualitative, and effectiveness studies involving caregivers, people living with dementia when appropriate, clinicians, and community-service providers, including locally adapted caregiver-support systems such as iSupport [58].

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this review is that it aggregates recent qualitative evidence, including several studies from 2023 to 2025 and emerging formats such as chatbot, recommendation-system, telehealth, and online support programs. Another strength is the explicit reporting of the original database-specific counts and deduplicated PRISMA screening flow, which improves transparency about study identification and eligibility assessment. The JBI meta-aggregation approach is appropriate for preserving participants’ reported experiences and translating them into practical categories for service design and implementation.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the included studies covered heterogeneous technologies, including mobile applications, telehealth, web platforms, online courses, ICT-supported services, chatbot/recommender systems, and tablet-based programs; this heterogeneity limits the specificity of conclusions about mHealth alone and means that findings should be interpreted as cross-format digital-support evidence rather than app-specific evidence. Second, caregiver type, dementia subtype, dementia severity, and technology-validation stage were not consistently reported in the baseline extraction, limiting subgroup interpretation. Third, most evidence came from Western or high-income settings, which limits direct applicability to China. Fourth, the revised PRISMA flow records the 24 full-text exclusions as topic mismatch rather than reporting more granular exclusion subcategories; no findings from excluded reports were synthesized. Fifth, the review was not prospectively registered; however, eligibility criteria, extraction fields, JBI appraisal, category aggregation, and CERQual judgments were documented and cross-checked to reduce selective reinterpretation, and category aggregation was performed from extracted findings rather than from a predetermined preferred conclusion. Sixth, although the ten original search sources covered major international and Chinese databases, additional grey-literature sources or later indexing updates might identify further records. Finally, because this review synthesized qualitative evidence, the findings should be interpreted as perceived experiences, barriers, and needs rather than proof of clinical effectiveness.

Implications for practice and research

For practice and design, digital dementia-caregiver support should prioritize clear navigation, trustworthy information, caregiver-centered onboarding, practical next-step guidance, privacy-aware communication, and channels for professional or peer support. Personalization should be based on dementia stage, caregiving tasks, caregiver digital literacy, and local service availability. For research, future studies should report technology features, implementation setting, caregiver characteristics, dementia stage, validation status, and qualitative analytic procedures in greater detail. Comparative effectiveness, cost, reimbursement, and long-term adherence questions should be addressed through appropriate quantitative or implementation-study designs rather than inferred from qualitative experience alone.

Conclusion

This meta-aggregation synthesized qualitative evidence from 24 studies to clarify family caregivers’ experiences of using mHealth and related digital health support in dementia care. Caregivers often perceived these tools as convenient, informative, and emotionally supportive, but they also reported usability problems, digital-literacy barriers, insufficient personalization, and lack of practical professional guidance. The findings support the development of caregiver-centered, age-friendly, stage-specific, and locally integrated digital support. They should be interpreted as evidence about acceptability, perceived usefulness, barriers, and implementation needs, not as evidence that digital support is clinically effective or superior to usual care.
