Resolution of life- and limb-threatening pyoderma gangrenosum with spesolimab
Authors: Anshu Jonnalagadda, Michael L Frisoli, Amar D Desai, Harjinder Chowdhary, William Damsky, Jeffrey R Gehlhausen
Published: 2026

Introduction

Pyoderma gangrenosum (PG) is a rare and often difficult-to-manage inflammatory skin condition that typically presents with rapidly enlarging, painful ulcers with violaceous, undermined borders. While the pathogenesis remains incompletely understood, research points toward an overactive immune response, potentially involving interleukin-36 (IL-36) signaling and its role in activating neutrophils.1,2 Spesolimab, a monoclonal antibody that blocks the IL-36 receptor and is approved for generalized pustular psoriasis, has recently been used in a few PG cases with encouraging results.3 Here, we present a patient with severe, refractory ulcerative PG at risk for amputation who achieved complete healing with spesolimab therapy.

Case report

A 57-year-old patient with a history of Sjögren syndrome and Jo1-positive antisynthetase syndrome (with myositis and interstitial lung disease) presented to Dermatology clinic with a 2-month history of worsening, painful ulcers on both lower legs (Fig 1). The lesions initially appeared as small “bug bite–like” papules on the lower legs before rapidly progressing to ulcerations. At the time the ulcers started, the patient was on azathioprine 175 mg daily (later reduced to 100 mg due to anemia) and prednisone 7.5 mg daily. On exam, there were 4 large ulcers with undermined edges. The surrounding skin was intact, with mildly violaceous discoloration but without significant erythema or drainage.

Initial presentation: Clinical photograph of the left lower leg showing a large ulceration with undermined violaceous borders.

Punch biopsy findings were consistent with PG, demonstrating epidermal ulceration with a dense dermal neutrophilic infiltrate and marked dermal edema; special stains were negative for micro-organisms. Tissue cultures for bacteria, fungi, acid-fast bacilli, and Nocardia were negative. The patient's PARACELSUS score was 15, consistent with a diagnosis of pyoderma gangrenosum (score >10 indicative of high likelihood).4 The diagnosis was further supported by the Maverakis consensus diagnostic criteria, as the patient met the major criterion of a neutrophilic infiltrate on biopsy along with several minor criteria, including characteristic undermined ulcer borders, the presence of multiple anterior lower-leg ulcers, and an initial presentation with ‘bug bite–like’ papules that later progressed to ulcerations.5 Hematologic evaluation, including SPEP/IFE, flow cytometry, and bone marrow biopsy, showed no evidence of lymphoid or myeloid malignancy, marrow infiltration, dysplastic changes, or marrow failure. Additional workup revealed no evidence inflammatory bowel disease (colonoscopy normal, fecal calprotectin negative) or inflammatory arthritis (RF/CCP negative). The patient’s prednisone dose was increased from 7.5 mg to 40 mg daily, and topical high-potency corticosteroids were applied to ulcer edges. Azathioprine was continued at 100 mg daily after dose reduction for anemia. This regimen was continued for approximately 4 weeks prior to the subsequent follow-up visit.

After prednisone was increased to 40 mg daily, the patient experienced some initial improvement during the first several days of treatment. However, by the following month, the ulcers had increased in both diameter and depth and superficial wound cultures grew methicillin resistant Staphylococcus aureus, vancomycin resistant enterococcus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Fig 2).

Progression: Clinical photographs demonstrating ulcer enlargement and worsening depth, with evidence of superinfection prior to initiation of spesolimab therapy. These images highlight the progression despite high-dose corticosteroids and antibiotics.

The patient was admitted for a 5-day hospital course and treated with IV vancomycin and piperacillin–tazobactam, then transitioned to oral ciprofloxacin and linezolid for outpatient therapy. Prednisone was increased to 80 mg daily on Day 1, but this did not lead to meaningful improvement. Given the severity and progression, along with immunohistochemical staining that showed IL-36 expression at the ulcer edge (Fig 3), spesolimab was initiated on Day 4 (900 mg IV infusion, followed by a subcutaneous dose 8 weeks later). Within 48 hours of the infusion, the patient reported marked improvement in pain and drainage.

IL-36 immunohistochemistry: Punch biopsy from the ulcer edge stained for IL-36, revealing strong immunohistochemical positivity in keratinocytes and within the inflammatory infiltrate.

Over the following weeks after the initial intravenous dose, the ulcers steadily improved, with noticeable decreases in size, healthier granulation tissue, less drainage, and reduced pain. At 8 weeks, when the patient received the second subcutaneous dose, further healing was seen, and this progress continued for another 6 weeks without any adverse effects. During this period, prednisone was tapered to 40 mg daily, and azathioprine was maintained at 100 mg daily. At 14 weeks, the patient demonstrated marked clinical improvement, with near-complete re-epithelialization, significant reduction in ulcer size, and resolution of surrounding inflammation (Fig 4). At this time, a third subcutaneous dose of spesolimab was given as treatment was transitioned to a maintenance regimen of every 4 weeks. The patient’s Sjogren’s syndrome and Jo-1 antibody–associated interstitial lung disease and myositis remained clinically stable during treatment with spesolimab.

Post-treatment improvement: Clinical photographs before and after initiation of spesolimab, showing marked reduction in ulcer size and near complete re-epithelialization by week 14.

Discussion

IL-36 is increasingly recognized as a key signal in neutrophil-rich skin conditions. It can be produced by keratinocytes, dendritic cells, and neutrophils and drives inflammation by recruiting more neutrophils and activating NF-κB and mitogen activated protein kinase pathways.1,6 Elevated IL-36 expression has been found in PG lesions, supporting the rationale for targeting the IL-36 receptor.7 In addition to IL-36, other upstream molecules activate the NF-κB pathway, which in turn increases pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1, IL-8, and TNF-α.8 These cytokines have established roles in PG pathogenesis, with TNF-α inhibitors like infliximab and adalimumab showing efficacy in case series, and IL-1 blockade with anakinra demonstrating response in some refractory cases.8 This shared pathway is targeted by several psoriasis biologics which could hold therapeutic potential in PG and related neutrophilic dermatoses.9,10

Spesolimab has been effective in generalized pustular psoriasis and is now being reported in small numbers of PG cases.3,4 In our case, the patient’s ulcers improved rapidly—within weeks—despite high-dose corticosteroids, azathioprine, and recent superficial wound colonization with methicillin resistant Staphylococcus aureus and vancomycin resistant enterococcus, suggesting a potent and specific effect of IL-36 antagonism in severe, treatment-refractory PG. The patient also tolerated spesolimab well, in line with earlier safety reports. Several limitations warrant consideration: (1) concurrent immunosuppression makes attribution of improvement to spesolimab alone difficult; (2) the natural history of PG may sometimes include spontaneous remission; (3) our follow-up period cannot assess long-term durability or optimal maintenance dosing. Even so, spesolimab may be a valuable option for patients with severe, treatment-resistant PG and documented IL-36 overexpression.
