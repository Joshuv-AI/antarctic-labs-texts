Do locking-free finite element schemes lock for holey Reissner-Mindlin plates with mixed boundary conditions? Note: The second author acknowledge that this material is based upon work supported by the National Science Foundation under Award No. DMS-2201487. Journal: Computer Methods in Applied Mechanics and Engineering
Mark Ainsworth, Charles Parker

Abstract
We revisit finite element discretizations of the Reissner-Mindlin plate in the case of non-simply connected (holey) domains with mixed boundary conditions. Guided by the de Rham complex, we develop conditions under which schemes deliver locking-free, optimal rates of convergence. We naturally recover the typical assumptions arising for clamped, simply supported plates. More importantly, we also see new conditions arise naturally from the presence of holes in the domain or in the case of mixed boundary conditions. We show that, fortunately, many of the existing popularly used schemes do , in fact, satisfy all of the conditions, and thus are locking-free.

In the current work, we revisit the classical Reissner-Mindlin (RM) plate
model. The literature on finite element approximation for RM is enormous and
one may reasonably question what, if anything, new can be said about finite
element schemes for RM? While RM is of interest in its own right as a
computational model for thin elastic plates, part of the interest in the
problem arises from the fact that it constitutes a coupled system of elliptic
partial differential equations in which there are two distinct scales
corresponding to the

As such, the model may be viewed as a prototype for more complex multiscale
problems. The presence of two distinct scales leads to difficulties in the
numerical discretization in the form of locking whereby a finite element scheme
converges, if it converges at all, at a sub-optimal rate until the mesh size

often predates the widespread adoption of ideas from finite element exterior

While this certainly simplifies the analysis,
practical applications where one might wish to use the RM model are more likely
to involve all types of boundary condition including stress-free, simply
supported, and clamped on complicated domains with holes. The mention of domains
with holes raises warning flags for any analysis that is based on exact
sequences, discrete or otherwise. In particular, the structure of the continuous
exact sequence on domains with holes is complicated by the presence of
mathematical structures known as harmonic forms which have an entirely
different structure from those that appear in the simply connected case. These
are discussed in detail later in the manuscript but, for present purposes,
their presence means that arguments valid in the simply connected case are no
longer necessarily valid in the more general case of domains with holes.
Equally well, when mixed boundary conditions are prescribed, similar
considerations apply and again, arguments that are used in the case of pure
clamping and no longer necessarily valid in the case of mixed boundary
conditions. This raises the danger whereby schemes developed and analyzed
in the case of simply connected domains with pure clamping may not perform
as expected on practical problems.

In the current work, we revisit finite element discretizations of the RM plate
in the case of non-simply connected domains with mixed boundary conditions.
Our approach starts from exact sequences and the de Rham diagram. Firstly, we
discuss the problem of developing schemes for RM at a rather intuitive level
guided by the de Rham sequence. One outcome of this is that one sees the
typical assumptions, which form the starting point for many existing works,
materialize naturally from intuitive arguments based on exact sequences. More
importantly, one also sees
new
conditions arise naturally from the
presence of discrete harmonic forms in the case of domains with holes or in the
case of mixed boundary conditions. Respecting these new conditions is necessary
if one wants to have locking-free schemes or if one wants to be able to use the
existing schemes with confidence for such problems. These conditions are absent
from existing analyses with the result that, as mentioned above, one cannot be
sure that the schemes will be valid for practical problems.

Having derived the new conditions following heuristic arguments guided by exact
sequences, we then provide rigorous mathematical analyses showing that the
conditions are sufficient to deliver locking-free, optimal rates of convergence
provides that the conditions are satisfied. This naturally leads to the
question of whether existing schemes satisfy the full set of conditions. The
good news, though hardly obvious at a glance, is that many of the existing
popularly used schemes
do
, in fact, satisfy the full set of conditions.
While the analysis needed to arrive at this conclusion is rather involved, the
outcome should be what one expects given that the schemes have certainly been
used by practitioners to approximate problems on domains with holes and mixed
boundary conditions without reports of failure. In a sense, the fact that many
existing schemes do in fact satisfy the full set of conditions may be
attributed to good fortune. However, it is more the case that existing schemes
were constructed to respect the exact sequence property in the special case of
simply connected domains with pure clamping, which focused the choice of method
to the extent that the resulting schemes do indeed satisfy the full set of
conditions identified here.

In the Reissner-Mindlin model, the deflection

\displaystyle\bm{\Xi}(v,\bm{\psi})=\grad v-\bm{\psi}\qquad\forall(v,\bm{\psi})\in H^{1}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)\times\bm{\Theta}_{\Gamma}(\Omega).

\displaystyle\leq C_{K}\|\varepsilon(\bm{\psi})\|\qquad

\displaystyle\forall\bm{\psi}\in\bm{\Theta}_{\Gamma}(\Omega).

2.2
Heuristic explanation of locking

Shear locking arises from the interplay between the variational formulation of
the problem and the nature of the finite dimensional spaces

\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma}
. More specifically, the
fact that the solution
(w_{h},\bm{\theta}_{h})
of

eq.

2.12
minimizes the energy
eq.

2.3

\displaystyle(w_{h},\bm{\theta}_{h})=\argmin_{(v,\bm{\psi})\in\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma}}\frac{1}{2}a(\bm{\psi},\bm{\psi})+\frac{\lambda}{2t^{2}}\|\bm{\Xi}(v,\bm{\psi})\|^{2}-F(v)-G(\bm{\psi})

means that if
t\ll 1
, then the first two terms balance only if

\|\bm{\Xi}(w_{h},\bm{\theta}_{h})\|=\mathcal{O}(t)
. In turn, this means that,
for all practical purposes, when
t\ll 1
the minimizer must satisfy

\bm{\Xi}(w_{h},\bm{\theta}_{h})=\bm{0}
or
\bm{\theta}_{h}=\grad w_{h}
. The
fact that
\grad w_{h}
may be discontinuous while
\bm{\theta}_{h}
is continuous
results in the minimizers becoming more occupied with maintaining compatibility
than with approximating the true deflections. Consequently, the resulting errors
are larger than what would be attained from the pure approximation error
appearing in
eq.

2.13
.

This effect can be exhibited analytically by decomposing the solution into two
components – one independent of
t
and a
t
-dependent remainder as follows.
Let
(z_{h},\bm{\zeta}_{h})\in\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma}
denote the solution to the “
t=0
limit" problem

\displaystyle(z_{h},\bm{\zeta}_{h})=\argmin_{(v,\bm{\psi})\in\ker\bm{\Xi}^{h}}\frac{1}{2}a(\bm{\psi},\bm{\psi})-F(v)-G(\bm{\psi}),

(2.14)

where
\ker\bm{\Xi}^{h}
is the restriction of the kernel of
\bm{\Xi}
to

\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma}
; i.e.

\displaystyle\ker\bm{\Xi}

\displaystyle:=\{(v,\bm{\psi})\in H^{1}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)\times\bm{\Theta}_{\Gamma}(\Omega):\bm{\Xi}(v,\bm{\psi})\equiv\bm{0}\},

and

\displaystyle\ker\bm{\Xi}^{h}

\displaystyle:=\ker\bm{\Xi}\cap\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma}.

Then,
(w_{h},\bm{\theta}_{h})=(u_{h},\bm{\phi}_{h})+(z_{h},\bm{\zeta}_{h})
, where

\displaystyle(u_{h},\bm{\phi}_{h})=\argmin_{(v,\bm{\psi})\in\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma}}\frac{1}{2}a(\bm{\psi},\bm{\psi})+\frac{\lambda}{2t^{2}}\|\bm{\Xi}(v,\bm{\psi})\|^{2}+a(\bm{\zeta}_{h},\bm{\psi})-F(v)-G(\bm{\psi}),

and so
\|\bm{\Xi}(u_{h},\bm{\phi}_{h})\|=\|\bm{\Xi}(w_{h},\bm{\theta}_{h})\|=\mathcal{O}(t)
.

The same arguments applied to the true solution
(w,\bm{\theta})
of

eq.

2.5
show that

\displaystyle(w,\bm{\theta})=(u,\bm{\phi})+(z,\bm{\zeta}),

where
(u,\bm{\phi})\in H^{1}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)\times\bm{\Theta}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
satisfies
\|\bm{\Xi}(u,\bm{\phi})\|=\mathcal{O}(t)
and
(z,\bm{\zeta})\in\ker\bm{\Xi}
is independent of
t
.
Thus, if the finite element solution is to provide uniform in
t

approximability, then the space
\ker\bm{\Xi}^{h}
should provide a quasi-optimal
approximation to
(z,\bm{\zeta})\in\ker\bm{\Xi}
; i.e.

\inf_{(z_{h},\bm{\zeta}_{h})\in\ker\bm{\Xi}^{h}}\left(\|z-z_{h}\|_{1}+\|\bm{\zeta}-\bm{\zeta}_{h}\|_{1}\right)\\
\leq C\inf_{(v_{h},\bm{\psi}_{h})\in\mathbb{W}_{\Gamma}^{h}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}_{\Gamma}^{h}}\left(\|z-v_{h}\|_{1}+\|\bm{\zeta}-\bm{\psi}_{h}\|_{1}\right).

(2.15)

This property means that the subspace
\ker\bm{\Xi}^{h}
is able to approximate
functions belonging to
\ker\bm{\Xi}
to the same order as the full space

\mathbb{W}_{\Gamma}^{h}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}_{\Gamma}^{h}
.

Unfortunately, estimate
eq.

2.15
fails to hold for many common
choices of finite element spaces. For instance
Falk2008
, suppose that

\mathbb{W}^{h}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}
consist of continuous, piecewise linear
polynomials. Then, the condition
\bm{\Xi}(z_{h},\bm{\zeta}_{h})\equiv\bm{0}

means that
\bm{\zeta}_{h}
is piecewise constant, and hence constant, since

\bm{\zeta}_{h}
is continuous. However, Korn’s inequality

eq.

2.7b
and Poincaré’s inequality

eq.

2.7a
then show that
\bm{\zeta}_{h}\equiv\bm{0}
and

z_{h}\equiv 0
. Thus,
\bm{\Xi}(z_{h},\bm{\zeta}_{h})\equiv\bm{0}
if and only
if both
z_{h}
and
\bm{\zeta}_{h}
vanish meaning that
eq.

2.15

fails. In the limit
t\to 0
, these results mean that the resulting finite
element approximation
(w_{h},\bm{\theta}_{h})
to

eq.

2.12

also satisfies
w_{h}\equiv 0
and
\bm{\theta}_{h}\equiv\bm{0}
which
corresponds to the plate remaining undeflected or
locked
. The same
issues occur with higher order elements to a lesser extent but still manifest
themselves as suboptimal rates of convergence.

2.3
Alleviating locking with reduction operators

The standard approach to alleviate shear locking is to relax the constraint

\bm{\Xi}(z_{h},\bm{\zeta}_{h})\equiv\bm{0}
. One way in which to do this is
to introduce a linear
reduction
operator
\bm{R}
and define a new
operator
\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}
formally by the rule

\displaystyle\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}(v,\bm{\psi})=\bm{R}(\grad v-\bm{\psi})\qquad\forall(v,\bm{\psi})\in H^{1}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)\times\bm{\Theta}_{\Gamma}(\Omega).

(2.16)

Replacing
\bm{\Xi}
in the variational problem
eq.

2.3
with

\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}
results in a modified energy

\displaystyle J_{t,\bm{R}}(w,\bm{\theta}):=\frac{1}{2}a(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta})+\frac{\lambda}{2t^{2}}\|\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}(w,\bm{\theta})\|^{2}-F(w)-G(\bm{\theta})

(2.17)

in which the Kirchhoff-Love term
\|\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}(w,\bm{\theta})\|^{2}

is weakened. The goal is to balance the choice of
\bm{R}
so that the set

\displaystyle\ker\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}^{h}:=\{(v,\bm{\psi})\in\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma}:\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}(v,\bm{\psi})\equiv\bm{0}\}

(2.18)

has optimal approximation properties while not compromising the integrity of the
underlying physical model through replacing
J_{t}
with the modified energy
J_{t,\bm{R}}
. The corresponding variational problem reads: Find
(w_{h},\bm{\theta}_{h})\in\mathbb{W}_{\Gamma}^{h}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}_{\Gamma}^{h}
such
that

\displaystyle B_{t,\bm{R}}(w_{h},\bm{\theta}_{h};v,\bm{\psi})=F(v)+G(\bm{\psi})\qquad\forall(v,\bm{\psi})\in\mathbb{W}_{\Gamma}^{h}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}_{\Gamma}^{h},

(2.19)

where the reduced bilinear form is given by

\displaystyle B_{t,\bm{R}}(w,\bm{\theta};v,\bm{\psi})

\displaystyle:=a(\bm{\theta},\bm{\psi})+\lambda t^{-2}(\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}(w,\bm{\theta}),\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}(v,\bm{\psi})).

(2.20)

3
Application of the de Rham complex to Reissner-Mindlin discretization

In this section, we show that the de Rham complex provides a systematic approach
to the construction of reduction operators
\bm{R}
. We start by collecting some
key properties of the de Rham complex that will be used in the sequel.

3.1
Brief résumé on the de Rham complex

The rotation operator
\rot:\bm{H}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
is defined by the rule
\rot\bm{\theta}:=\partial_{x}\theta_{2}-\partial_{y}\theta_{1}
, where the space

\displaystyle\bm{H}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega):=\{\bm{\gamma}\in\bm{L}^{2}(\Omega):\rot\bm{\gamma}\in L^{2}(\Omega)\}

is equipped with inner product and norm defined by

\displaystyle(\bm{\gamma},\bm{\eta})_{\rot}:=(\bm{\gamma},\bm{\eta})+(\rot\bm{\gamma},\rot\bm{\eta})\quad\text{and}\quad\|\bm{\gamma}\|_{\rot}^{2}:=(\bm{\gamma},\bm{\gamma})_{\rot}\qquad\forall\bm{\gamma},\bm{\eta}\in\bm{H}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega).

Vector fields in
\bm{H}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega)
have a well-defined tangential component on the boundary
in the sense that the tangential trace operator
\bm{H}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega)\ni\bm{\gamma}\to\bm{t}\cdot\bm{\gamma}|_{\Gamma}\in H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
defines a
continuous mapping
[
GiraultRaviart86
, p. 27 Theorem 2.5]
. We may therefore
define the subspace

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{\Gamma}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega):=\left\{\bm{\gamma}\in\bm{H}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega):\int_{\Gamma}(\bm{t}\cdot\bm{\gamma})v\,\mathrm{d}{s}=0\quad\forall v\in H^{1}(\Omega):v|_{\Gamma_{f}}\equiv 0\right\}

consisting of vector fields with vanishing tangential trace on
\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{f}
, where we use
\int_{\Gamma}
to also denote the
H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)\times H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
duality pairing. Finally, we define

\displaystyle L^{2}_{\Gamma}(\Omega):=\{q\in L^{2}(\Omega):(q,1)=0\text{ if }|\Gamma_{f}|=0\}.

(3.1)

These spaces, together with
H^{1}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
, form a
complex

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{1}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bm{H}_{\Gamma}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\grad}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rot}

(3.2)

in which the image of each operator is contained in the kernel of the next
operator in the sequence; e.g.
\grad H^{1}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)\subset\bm{H}_{\Gamma}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega)
,

\rot\grad v\equiv 0
for all
v\in H^{1}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
, and the operator

\rot:\bm{H}_{\Gamma}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega)\to L^{2}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
is surjective
[
ErnGuerII21
, Propositions
51.3 & 51.6]
. Moreover, in the special case of a simply connected
plate with
\Gamma_{f}
connected, the image of each operator is precisely the
kernel of the following operator (see e.g.
[
Licht17
, p. 1111-1112 Example 9]

or
lemma

4.1
below), and the complex
eq.

3.2

is then said to be
exact
. However, if the domain is not simply connected
or if
\Gamma_{f}
is not connected, then
\grad H^{1}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
is a proper
subspace of
\ker\rot\bm{H}_{\Gamma}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega)
and, as a result, the space

\displaystyle\bm{\mathfrak{H}}_{\Gamma}(\Omega):=\{\bm{\mathfrak{h}}\in\bm{H}_{\Gamma}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega):\rot\bm{\mathfrak{h}}\equiv 0\text{ and }(\bm{\mathfrak{h}},\grad w)=0\ \forall w\in H^{1}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)\}

(3.3)

is nontrivial. This means that there exist non-zero vector fields, known as

harmonic forms
, that have vanishing rotation and which are orthogonal to

\grad H^{1}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
.

In order to see the relevance of the de Rham sequence to the discretization of
the Reissner-Mindlin model, we consider spaces
\mathbb{W}_{\Gamma}^{h}\subset H_{\Gamma}^{1}(\Omega)
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}_{\Gamma}^{h}\subset\bm{H}_{\Gamma}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega)
, and

\mathbb{Q}^{h}_{\Gamma}\subset L^{2}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
for which the following
discrete analogue of
eq.

3.2
holds:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{W}_{\Gamma}^{h}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}^{h}_{\Gamma}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{Q}^{h}_{\Gamma}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\grad}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rot}

(3.4)

The discrete analogue of the harmonic forms is defined to be

\displaystyle\bm{\mathfrak{H}}^{h}_{\Gamma}:=\{\bm{\mathfrak{h}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}^{h}_{\Gamma}:\rot\bm{\mathfrak{h}}\equiv 0\text{ and }(\bm{\mathfrak{h}},\grad w)=0\ \forall w\in\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}\},

(3.5)

and the discrete sequence
eq.

3.4
is exact if and
only if
\bm{\mathfrak{H}}^{h}_{\Gamma}=\{\bm{0}\}
and
\rot\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}^{h}_{\Gamma}=\mathbb{Q}^{h}_{\Gamma}
.

The sequences
eq.

3.2
and
eq.

3.4
can
be often related to one another via bounded, linear projection operators

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathbb{W}}:H^{1}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma},\quad\bm{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}}:\bm{H}_{\Gamma}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega)\to\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}^{h}_{\Gamma},\quad\text{and}\quad\Pi_{\mathbb{Q}}:L^{2}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{Q}^{h}_{\Gamma}

such that the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{1}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bm{H}_{\Gamma}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}_{\Gamma}^{h}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{Q}_{\Gamma}^{h},}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\grad}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Pi_{\mathbb{W}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rot}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bm{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Pi_{\mathbb{Q}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\grad}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rot}

(3.6)

i.e.
\grad\Pi_{\mathbb{W}}=\bm{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}}\grad
and
\rot\bm{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}}=\Pi_{\mathbb{Q}}\rot
. Many examples of spaces
and operators that satisfy
eq.

3.6
are known in the
literature (see e.g.
Arn18
,
BoffiBrezziFortin13
,
Dem07
).

3.2
Application to Reissner-Mindlin discretization

The Galerkin discretization of the Reissner-Mindlin model requires the selection
of subspaces
\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}\subset H^{1}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
and

\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma}\subset\bm{\Theta}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)\subset\bm{H}_{\Gamma}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega)
with which to approximate the displacement
w
and rotation

\bm{\theta}
. We have already seen that the approximation properties of the
subspace

\displaystyle\{(v_{h},\bm{\psi}_{h})\in\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma}:\bm{\Xi}(v_{h},\bm{\psi}_{h})\equiv\bm{0}\}

is responsible for locking. Re-examining this constraint in light of the discrete
sequence
eq.

3.4
shows that the equality

\bm{\Xi}(v_{h},\bm{\psi}_{h})\equiv\bm{0}\iff\bm{\psi}_{h}=\grad v_{h}

should be interpreted by viewing
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}_{\Gamma}^{h}
as a subspace of

\bm{H}_{\Gamma}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega)
as distinct from the
H^{1}
-conforming space

\bm{\Theta}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
. In other words, the constraint
\bm{\Xi}(v,\bm{\psi})\equiv\bm{0}
at the continuous level should be viewed at the
discrete level through the lens of the projection operator

\bm{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}}
that appears in the commuting diagram

eq.

3.6
, and the constraint
\bm{\Xi}(v,\bm{\psi})=\grad v-\bm{\psi}\equiv\bm{0}
should itself be discretized as

\bm{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}}(\grad v_{h}-\bm{\psi}_{h})\equiv\bm{0}
. The fact
that
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma}
is not necessarily a subspace of

\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}^{h}_{\Gamma}
means that the condition

\displaystyle\bm{0}\equiv\bm{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}}(\grad v_{h}-\bm{\psi}_{h})=\grad v_{h}-\bm{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}}\bm{\psi}_{h}

is more relaxed than the pointwise condition
\grad v_{h}-\bm{\psi}_{h}\equiv\bm{0}
. This suggests defining an operator
\bm{\Xi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}}
by the
rule
\bm{\Xi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}}(v_{h},\bm{\psi}_{h})=\bm{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}}(\grad v_{h}-\bm{\psi}_{h})
where the commuting
projection
\bm{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}}
naturally takes the role of the reduction
operator
\bm{R}
which we seek.

The only snag with this approach stems from the fact that in order to implement

eq.

2.19
using standard finite element sub-assembly
techniques, one needs to explicitly compute the action of
\bm{R}
element by
element. Unfortunately, the projection operator
\bm{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}}
, in
most cases, cannot be locally defined owing to the fact that the traces of
functions in
\bm{H}_{\Gamma}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega)
are not well-defined on individual edges which
precludes computing the action of
\bm{R}
separately on each element.

However, a careful examination
eq.

2.19
reveals that the
action of the reduction operator is in fact only required for functions belonging
to the smoother space
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}_{\Gamma}^{h}+\grad\mathbb{W}_{\Gamma}^{h}

rather than
\bm{H}_{\Gamma}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega)
. Such functions have more regularity than just

\bm{H}_{\Gamma}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega)
which means one can define reduction operators in terms of the
traces on individual edges. As such, the action of
the operator can, at least in principle, be computed element by element.
Fortunately, for most choices of spaces forming a complex

eq.

3.4
, there exist appropriate locally-defined
interpolation operators (defined on the smoother spaces). In particular, there
typically exist bounded linear projection operators

\displaystyle T:H^{2}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)+\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}\to\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{R}:\bm{\Theta}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)+\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}^{h}_{\Gamma}\to\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}^{h}_{\Gamma}

(3.7)

such that the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{2}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bm{\Theta}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}_{\Gamma}^{h}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{Q}_{\Gamma}^{h},}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\grad}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rot}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bm{R}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\grad}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rot}

(3.8)

where
P:L^{2}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{Q}_{\Gamma}^{h}
is the
L^{2}
-projection
operator

\displaystyle(Pr,q)=(r,q)\qquad\forall q\in\mathbb{Q}^{h}_{\Gamma},\ \forall r\in L^{2}(\Omega)

(3.9)

and

\displaystyle H^{2}_{\Gamma}(\Omega):=\{w\in H^{2}(\Omega):w|_{\Gamma_{c}\cup\Gamma_{s}}=0\text{ and }\partial_{n}w|_{\Gamma_{c}}=0\}.

Strictly speaking, the operator
\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}
defined in

eq.

2.16
is not valid on all of
H^{1}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)\times\bm{\Theta}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
. However, thanks to the discrete de Rham
complex property together with the projection property in

eq.

3.12
, there holds

\displaystyle\bm{R}\grad w=\grad w\qquad\forall w\in\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}.

Consequently, without affecting the discrete scheme

eq.

2.19
or the space
\ker\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}^{h}
, we
may redefine the operator
\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}(v,\bm{\psi}):=\grad v-\bm{R}\bm{\psi}\qquad\forall(v,\bm{\psi})\in H^{1}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)\times\bm{\Theta}_{\Gamma}(\Omega).

(3.10)

The operator
\bm{R}
is also typically bounded on

\bm{\Theta}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
: There exits
M_{\bm{R}}>0
independent of
h

such that

\displaystyle\|\bm{R}\bm{\psi}\|_{\rot}\leq M_{\bm{R}}\|\bm{\psi}\|_{1}\qquad\forall\bm{\psi}\in\bm{\Theta}_{\Gamma}(\Omega).

(3.11)

In summary, we have the following conditions:

The spaces
\mathbb{W}_{\Gamma}^{h}\subset H_{\Gamma}^{1}(\Omega)
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}_{\Gamma}^{h}\subset\bm{H}_{\Gamma}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega)
and

\mathbb{Q}^{h}_{\Gamma}\subset L^{2}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
and linear projection
operators
\bm{R}:\bm{\Theta}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)+\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}^{h}_{\Gamma}\to\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}^{h}_{\Gamma}
and
T:H^{2}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)+\mathbb{W}_{\Gamma}^{h}\to\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}
are chosen so that

eq.

3.8
commutes and the bottom row forms a discrete
complex.

(3.12)

There exists
M_{\bm{R}}>0
independent of

h
such that
eq.

3.11
holds.

(3.13)

Remark 1
.

The identity
\rot\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}^{h}_{\Gamma}=\mathbb{Q}^{h}_{\Gamma}
is a
direct consequence of the commuting diagram property

eq.

3.12
: For
q\in\mathbb{Q}^{h}_{\Gamma}
, there
exists
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
such that
\rot\bm{\theta}=q

[
AinCP23KirchI
, Lemma A.3]
. As a result, a vector
field
of the form
\bm{\gamma}:=\bm{R}\bm{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}^{h}_{\Gamma}
satisfies

\displaystyle\rot\bm{R}\bm{\theta}=P\rot\bm{\theta}=Pq=q.

3.3
Choosing the rotation space
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma}

Earlier we argued that, in order to avoid locking, the space
\ker\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}^{h}
should satisfy the following quasi-optimal approximation
property:

\inf_{(z_{h},\bm{\zeta}_{h})\in\ker\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}^{h}}\left(\|z-z_{h}\|_{1}+\|\bm{\zeta}-\bm{\zeta}_{h}\|_{1}\right)\\
\leq C\inf_{(v_{h},\bm{\psi}_{h})\in\mathbb{W}_{\Gamma}^{h}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}_{\Gamma}^{h}}\left(\|z-v_{h}\|_{1}+\|\bm{\zeta}-\bm{\psi}_{h}\|_{1}\right),

(3.14)

for all
(z,\bm{\zeta})\in\ker\bm{\Xi}
. It is not always easy to see
directly whether
eq.

3.14
is satisfied, so we develop an
alternative criterion that can be used to ensure this is the case. Firstly,
notice that if the inf-sup condition

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\eta}\in\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}^{h}_{\Gamma}\\
\bm{\eta}\neq\bm{0}\end{subarray}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(v,\bm{\psi})\in\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma}\\
(v,\bm{\psi})\neq(0,\bm{0})\end{subarray}}\frac{(\bm{\eta},\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}(v,\bm{\psi}))_{\rot}}{(\|v\|_{1}+\|\bm{\psi}\|_{1})\|\bm{\eta}\|_{\rot}}\geq\beta_{\bm{R}}

(3.15)

is satisfied with
\beta_{\bm{R}}>0
independent of
h
, then the following
analogue of
eq.

3.14
holds for all
(z,\bm{\zeta})\in\ker\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}
:

\inf_{(z_{h},\bm{\zeta}_{h})\in\ker\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}^{h}}\left(\|z-z_{h}\|_{1}+\|\bm{\zeta}-\bm{\zeta}_{h}\|_{1}\right)\\
\leq\frac{CM_{\bm{R}}}{\beta_{\bm{R}}}\inf_{(v_{h},\bm{\psi}_{h})\in\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma}}\left(\|z-v_{h}\|_{1}+\|\bm{\zeta}-\bm{\psi}_{h}\|_{1}\right).

(3.16)

Here, we used
[
BoffiBrezziFortin13
, Proposition 5.1.3]
along with the
estimate

\displaystyle|(\bm{\eta},\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}(v,\bm{\psi}))_{\rot}|\leq\|\bm{\eta}\|_{\rot}\left(\|\grad v\|+\|\bm{R}\bm{\psi}\|_{\rot}\right)\leq CM_{\bm{R}}\|\bm{\eta}\|_{\rot}\left(\|v\|_{1}+\|\bm{\psi}\|\right)

which holds for all
\bm{\eta}\in\bm{H}_{\Gamma}(\mathrm{rot};\Omega)
,
v\in H^{1}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
,
and
\bm{\psi}\in\bm{\Theta}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
. As it stands,

eq.

3.16
applies to the approximation of
(z,\bm{\zeta})\in\ker\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}
, whereas
eq.

3.14
relates to
(z,\bm{\zeta})\in\ker\bm{\Xi}
. Nevertheless
eq.

3.16
implies
that a variant of
eq.

3.14
holds provided the right hand side
is augmented with an additional term accounting for the error incurred through
using the reduction operator in place of the identity:

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that
eq.

3.12
,
eq.

3.13
, and
the
inf-sup condition
eq.

3.15
hold. Then, for all
(z,\bm{\zeta})\in\ker\bm{\Xi}
, there holds

\inf_{(z_{h},\bm{\zeta}_{h})\in\ker\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}^{h}}\left(\|z-z_{h}\|_{1}+\|\bm{\zeta}-\bm{\zeta}_{h}\|_{1}\right)\\
\leq\frac{CM_{\bm{R}}}{\beta_{\bm{R}}}\inf_{(v_{h},\bm{\psi}_{h})\in\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma}}\left(\|z-v_{h}\|_{1}+\|\bm{\zeta}-\bm{\psi}_{h}\|_{1}\right)+2C_{P}\|\bm{\zeta}-\bm{R}\bm{\zeta}\|.

(3.17)

Proof.

Let
(z,\bm{\zeta})\in\ker\bm{\Xi}
be given. Then,
\grad z=\bm{\zeta}
and so
z\in H^{2}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
. Thanks to

eq.

3.12
,
\grad Tz=\bm{R}\grad z
, and so
(Tz,\bm{\zeta})\in\ker\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}
. Thus, the triangle inequality and

eq.

3.16
give

\displaystyle\inf_{(z_{h},\bm{\zeta}_{h})\in\ker\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}}\left(\|z-z_{h}\|_{1}+\|\bm{\zeta}-\bm{\zeta}_{h}\|_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\|z-Tz\|_{1}+\inf_{(z_{h},\bm{\zeta}_{h})\in\ker\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}}\left(\|Tz-z_{h}\|_{1}+\|\bm{\zeta}-\bm{\zeta}_{h}\|_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\|z-Tz\|_{1}+\frac{CM_{\bm{R}}}{\beta_{\bm{R}}}\inf_{(v_{h},\bm{\psi}_{h})\in\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma}}\left(\|Tz-v_{h}\|_{1}+\|\bm{\zeta}-\bm{\psi}_{h}\|_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 2\|z-Tz\|_{1}+\frac{CM_{\bm{R}}}{\beta_{\bm{R}}}\inf_{(v_{h},\bm{\psi}_{h})\in\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma}}\left(\|z-v_{h}\|_{1}+\|\bm{\zeta}-\bm{\psi}_{h}\|_{1}\right).

Poincaré’s inequality
eq.

2.7a
and

eq.

3.12
gives

\displaystyle\|z-Tz\|_{1}\leq C_{P}\|\grad z-T\grad z\|=C_{P}\|\bm{\zeta}-\bm{R}\bm{\zeta}\|.

∎

The above results show that, in order for the scheme to satisfy the
quasi-optimality approximation property
eq.

3.17
, it
is sufficient that the inf-sup condition
eq.

3.15
holds. It
may not be immediately clear that
eq.

3.15
constitutes an
improvement over
eq.

3.17
. However, examining the
inf-sup condition more closely, we see that it requires that the spaces

\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma}
, and

\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}^{h}_{\Gamma}
and the operator
\bm{R}
be chosen so that

\displaystyle\image\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}^{h}:=\{\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}(v,\bm{\psi}):(v,\bm{\psi})\in\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma}\}=\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}^{h}_{\Gamma},

(3.18)

which, in turn, can be reduced to more familiar conditions as follows. One
necessary condition for
eq.

3.18
is that
\rot\image\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}^{h}=\rot\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}^{h}_{\Gamma}=\mathbb{Q}^{h}_{\Gamma}
,
where the last equality follows from
remark

1
. In
turn, using the commuting diagram
eq.

3.8
, we require that

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{h}_{\Gamma}=\rot\image\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}^{h}=\rot\grad\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}+\rot\bm{R}\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma}=P\rot\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma},

or equivalently, the following inf-sup condition must hold:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\text{\parbox{276.00105pt}{There exists $\beta_{\rot}>0$ independent
of $h$ such that}}\\
&\qquad\qquad\inf_{0\neq q\in\mathbb{Q}^{h}_{\Gamma}}\sup_{\bm{0}\neq\bm{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma}}\frac{(\rot\bm{\theta},q)}{\|\bm{\theta}\|_{1}\|q\|}>\beta_{\rot}.\end{aligned}

(3.19)

Condition
eq.

3.19
is nothing more (up to a
rotation on
\bm{\theta}
) than the familiar inf-sup, or Babuška-Brezzi,
condition commonly seen in the mixed discretization of the Stokes equations.
Here, as we have just shown, the condition is a natural consequence of the
quasi-optimal approximation condition
eq.

3.17
. A
second necessary condition for
eq.

3.18
to hold is that the rot-free
functions in
\image\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}^{h}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}^{h}_{\Gamma}
should
coincide. Thanks to the complex property
eq.

3.12
, there holds

\displaystyle\{\bm{\gamma}\in\image\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}^{h}:\rot\bm{\gamma}\equiv 0\}

\displaystyle=\grad\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}+\{\bm{R}\bm{\theta}:\bm{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma},\ \rot\bm{R}\bm{\theta}\equiv 0\},

\displaystyle\{\bm{\gamma}\in\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}^{h}_{\Gamma}:\rot\bm{\gamma}\equiv 0\}

\displaystyle=\grad\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}\oplus^{\perp}\bm{\mathfrak{H}}^{h}_{\Gamma},

where
\bm{\mathfrak{H}}^{h}_{\Gamma}
is the space of discrete harmonic forms

eq.

3.5
and
\oplus^{\perp}
indicates that the
direct sum is orthogonal in
\bm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
. If we define
\bm{H}:\bm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\to\bm{\mathfrak{H}}^{h}_{\Gamma}
to be the
L^{2}
-projection operator

\displaystyle(\bm{H}\bm{\gamma},\bm{\mathfrak{g}})=(\bm{\gamma},\bm{\mathfrak{g}})\qquad\forall\bm{\mathfrak{g}}\in\bm{\mathfrak{H}}^{h}_{\Gamma},\ \forall\bm{\gamma}\in\bm{L}^{2}(\Omega),

(3.20)

then the rot-free functions in
\image\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}
and

\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}^{h}_{\Gamma}
coincide if and only if

\displaystyle\bm{H}\{\bm{R}\bm{\theta}:\bm{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma},\ \rot\bm{R}\bm{\theta}\equiv 0\}=\bm{\mathfrak{H}}^{h}_{\Gamma},

(3.21)

or, equivalently, a second inf-sup condition must hold:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\text{\parbox{276.00105pt}{There exists $\beta_{\bm{\mathfrak{H}}}>0$
independent of $h$ such that}}\\
&\qquad\qquad\inf_{0\neq\bm{\mathfrak{h}}\in\bm{\mathfrak{H}}^{h}_{\Gamma}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{0}\neq\bm{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma}\\
\rot\bm{R}\bm{\theta}\equiv 0\end{subarray}}\frac{(\bm{R}\bm{\theta},\bm{\mathfrak{h}})}{\|\bm{\theta}\|_{1}\|\bm{\mathfrak{h}}\|}>\beta_{\bm{\mathfrak{H}}}.\end{aligned}

(3.22)

Lemma

3.2
below confirms that conditions

eq.

3.19
and

eq.

3.22
are equivalent to

eq.

3.15
, but as we shall see shortly, are easier to handle.
The inf-sup condition
eq.

3.19
has appeared
previously in the literature associated with locking-free methods for
Reissner-Mindlin plates
Brezzi89
,
Brezzi1991MITC
,
Peisker92
and has been the
subject of
investigation in the setting of incompressible flow. By way of contrast, despite
the fact that
eq.

3.22
also arises naturally
from the quasi-optimal approximation property

eq.

3.17
, it seems to have not been recognized in the
literature hitherto. One reason for this discrepancy may be that the majority of
articles dealing with Reissner-Mindlin plates only concern themselves with simply
connected domains and pure clamping conditions for which there are no nontrivial
harmonic forms (meaning that
eq.

3.22
is
vacuous).

The next result shows that the above conditions are equivalent to the inf-sup
condition
eq.

3.15
:

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose that
eqs.

3.12
and
3.13
hold. Then, the
inf-sup condition
eq.

3.15
holds if and only if
conditions

eqs.

3.19
and
3.22

hold. Moreover, the inf-sup constants satisfy

\displaystyle\beta_{\bm{R}}\geq\frac{C\beta_{\rot}\beta_{\bm{\mathfrak{H}}}}{M_{\bm{R}}^{2}},\quad\beta_{\rot}\geq\frac{C\beta_{\bm{R}}}{M_{\bm{R}}^{2}},\quad\text{and}\quad\beta_{\bm{\mathfrak{H}}}\geq\beta_{\bm{R}}.

(3.23)

The proof of
lemma

3.2
appears in

appendix

A
.

Our quest for locking-free schemes has led to four conditions

eqs.

3.12
,
3.13
,
3.19
, and
3.22
.
In fact, in the ensuing analysis, it will turn out that we shall not require the
operator
T
in the diagram
eq.

3.8
. Likewise, we shall
only need for
\bm{R}
to be

As mentioned earlier, harmonic forms consist of non-trivial vector fields with

Harmonic forms fall into two categories. The first type can be written as the

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\Gamma}(\Omega):=\{w\in H^{1}(\Omega):w|_{\Gamma_{cs}^{(1)}}=0\text{ and }w|_{\Gamma_{cs}^{(i)}}\in\mathbb{R},\ 2\leq i\leq N_{cs}\},

Suppose that

3.15

Proof.

Let
\bm{\gamma}\in\boldsymbol{\mathbb{U}}^{h}_{\Gamma}

3.29

7.1

Theorem 7.1

Suppose that
(C2)

\displaystyle a(\bm{\theta}_{h},\bm{\psi})+\langle\bm{\gamma}_{h},\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{R}}(v,\bm{\psi})\rangle

\displaystyle=F(v)+G(\bm{\psi})\qquad

\displaystyle\forall(v,\bm{\psi})\in\mathbb{W}^{h}_{\Gamma}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbb{V}}^{h}_{\Gamma},

(7.5a)

satisfy

.
Taking

and subtracting the second equation

If

, then the first equation

gives

\displaystyle\beta\|p\|_{Q}\leq\sup_{0\neq v\in V}\frac{b(v,p)}{\|v\|_{V}}=\sup_{0\neq v\in V}\frac{F(v)-a(u,v)}{\|v\|_{V}}\leq\|F\|_{{V}^{\prime}}+\|a\|\|u\|_{V},

(C.4)

and so

\displaystyle|G_{1}(p)|\leq\beta^{-1}\|G_{1}\|_{{Q}^{\prime}}\left(\|F\|_{{V}^{\prime}}+\|a\|\|u\|_{V}\right)\leq\epsilon\|a\|^{2}\|u\|_{V}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|F\|_{{V}^{\prime}}^{2}+\frac{2}{\epsilon\beta^{2}}\|G_{1}\|_{{Q}^{\prime}}^{2},

where

is to be chosen. Alternatively, we have

\displaystyle|G_{1}(p)|\leq\|G_{1}\|_{{W}^{\prime}}\|p\|_{W}\leq\frac{4}{t^{2}}\|G_{1}\|_{{W}^{\prime}}+\frac{t^{2}}{4}\|p\|_{W}\leq\frac{4C_{W}^{2}}{t^{2}}\|G_{1}\|_{{Q}^{\prime}}^{2}+\frac{t^{2}}{4}\|p\|_{W},

which then gives the following for

:

\displaystyle|G_{1}(p)|\leq\epsilon\|a\|^{2}\|u\|_{V}^{2}+\frac{t^{2}}{4}\|p\|_{W}+\frac{1}{2}\|F\|_{{V}^{\prime}}^{2}+\frac{8C_{W}^{2}}{\epsilon(\beta+t)^{2}}\|G_{1}\|_{{Q}^{\prime}}^{2},

where we used

\displaystyle\min\left\{\frac{1}{\beta},\frac{1}{t}\right\}=\frac{1}{\max\{\beta,t\}}\leq\frac{2}{\beta+t}

Thanks to the bound

\displaystyle|G_{2}(p)|\leq\|G_{2}\|_{{W}^{\prime}}\|p\|_{W}\leq\frac{4}{t^{2}}\|G_{2}\|_{{W}^{\prime}}^{2}+\frac{t^{2}}{4}\|p\|_{W}^{2},

we obtain

\displaystyle a(u,u)+\frac{t^{2}}{2}\|p\|_{W}^{2}\leq 2\epsilon\|a\|^{2}\|u\|_{V}^{2}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\|F\|_{{V}^{\prime}}^{2}+\frac{8C_{W}^{2}}{\epsilon(\beta+t)^{2}}\|G_{1}\|_{{Q}^{\prime}}^{2}+\frac{4}{t^{2}}\|G_{2}\|_{{W}^{\prime}}^{2}.

The second equation
eq.

C.1b
means that

, where
\mathfrak{R}_{W}:{W}^{\prime}\to W
is the Riesz operator, and so

\displaystyle\|Bu\|_{{W}^{\prime}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|Bu-G_{1}-G_{2}\|_{{W}^{\prime}}^{2}+2\|G_{1}+G_{2}\|_{{W}^{\prime}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2t^{-2}\|Bu-G_{1}-G_{2}\|_{{W}^{\prime}}^{2}+4\|G_{1}\|_{{W}^{\prime}}^{2}+4\|G_{2}\|_{{W}^{\prime}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2t^{2}\|p\|_{W}^{2}+4C_{W}^{2}\|G_{1}\|_{{Q}^{\prime}}^{2}+4\|G_{2}\|_{{W}^{\prime}}^{2}.

Collecting results then gives

a(u,u)+\|Bu\|_{{W}^{\prime}}^{2}+t^{2}\|p\|_{W}^{2}\\
\leq 12\epsilon\|a\|^{2}\|u\|_{V}^{2}+C\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\|F\|_{{V}^{\prime}}^{2}+\frac{C_{W}^{2}}{\epsilon(\beta+t)^{2}}\|G_{1}\|_{{Q}^{\prime}}^{2}+\frac{1}{t^{2}}\|G_{2}\|_{{W}^{\prime}}^{2}\right).

Applying
eq.

C.2
and choosing

, we obtain

\displaystyle\|u\|_{V}+t\|p\|_{W}\leq C\frac{\|a\|}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\left(\|F\|_{{V}^{\prime}}+\frac{C_{W}}{\beta+t}\|G_{1}\|_{{Q}^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{t}\|G_{2}\|_{{W}^{\prime}}\right).

Inequality
eq.

C.3
now follows from

eq.

C.4
and that

\displaystyle\frac{\beta+t}{C_{W}}\|p\|_{Q}\leq\beta\|p\|_{Q}+t\|p\|_{W}.

∎
