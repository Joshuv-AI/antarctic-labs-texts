Impact of three mammalian milk-derived fortifiers on morbidity and mortality in preterm infants: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Yueqing Feng, Yan Gao, Yue Gu, Yujie Liu, Yunyun Xu, Shenmei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFortification of breast milk with milk-derived fortifiers is essential for the growth and developmental outcomes of preterm infants, yet the ideal fortifier remains uncertain. This network meta-analysis assessed the effects of bovine milk-derived fortifier (BMF), human milk-derived fortifier (HMF), and donkey milk-derived fortifier (DMF) on morbidity and mortality rates of preterm infants within neonatal intensive care units (NICUs).MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive search of multiple databases, including MEDLINE, CINAHL, PubMed, CNKI, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials. The risk of bias for each study was evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool.ResultsTen studies involving 545 preterm infants were identified for qualitative descriptive analysis. network meta-analysis was conducted, incorporating data from five studies and three different types of milk-based fortifiers. The network meta-analysis suggested that HMF may be associated with a lower incidence of necrotizing enterocolitis. Compared with HMF, BMF was associated with an increased risk of necrotizing enterocolitis (RR = 2.33, 95% CI [1.22–4.44]). Cumulative ranking probabilities indicated that HMF appeared to be the most favourable fortifier for reducing both necrotizing enterocolitis incidence and mortality. DMF might represent a potential option for reducing the risk of intraventricular haemorrhage and neonatal sepsis.ConclusionHMF may be associated with a reduced incidence of necrotizing enterocolitis, whereas DMF may offer potential anti-inflammatory benefits, particularly for high-risk populations susceptible to intraventricular haemorrhage. This study provides direct and comparative evidence of the associations between different animal-derived fortifiers and morbidity and mortality in preterm infants. It provides a preliminary evaluation of DMF, for which limited evidence previously existed, and offers insight into the potential mechanisms that connect milk composition to clinical outcomes. However, these findings are primarily based on a limited number of studies with small sample sizes. Further randomized controlled trials are necessary to validate these observations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13006-025-00801-w.

Background

The global incidence of preterm birth has been consistently increasing in recent years. Preterm birth remains a significant public health concern worldwide, with approximately 15 million preterm infants born annually. Among these, nearly 1 million succumb to complications related to prematurity, including necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), intraventricular haemorrhage (IVH), and neonatal sepsis, and preterm birth remains the leading direct contributor to neonatal mortality and long-term disability, particularly in low- and middle-income nations [1–6]. Due to insufficient intrauterine development, preterm infants face numerous challenges after birth, particularly with respect to immune function, neurodevelopment, and the development of vital organs [7]. Proper health management and nutritional support for preterm infants have become crucial research topics in the field of neonatology. Breast milk from mothers or donated human milk provides preterm infants with optimal nutrients, significantly promoting their growth and development while also reducing the incidence of diseases and mortality [8].

However, certain studies suggest that breast milk alone may not be sufficient to meet the elevated nutritional requirements of preterm infants, particularly with respect to protein and mineral intake [9]. Therefore, both maternal and donated breast milk require fortification to meet the nutritional demands necessary for the growth and development of preterm infants. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) advises the fortification of human milk as a standard practice for preterm infants [10]. Breast milk supplements can provide additional nutrients when nutrients in breast milk are insufficient, helping to reduce the gap in nutritional requirements [11]. Compared with unfortified breast milk, fortified breast milk significantly improves growth outcomes at discharge without increasing adverse reactions [12]. Different milk-derived fortifiers may vary in their nutritional composition and effectiveness. Currently, BMF and HMF are the primary safe options for fortifying breast milk [13–16]. BMF is the most widely used, with a relatively comprehensive nutritional profile [17, 18]. Fortifiers derived from human milk exhibit excellent tolerance and efficiently fulfil the nutritional requirements of preterm infants. When mixed with human, maternal, or donor milk, these fortifiers significantly increase protein and micronutrient contents, ensuring that nutritional standards are met. As a result, they offer healthcare providers a viable alternative to traditional BMF. Additionally, some studies suggest that DMF has biological effects similar to those of human milk and contributes positively to the growth and development of preterm infants [19, 20].

The similarity in protein profiles between donkey milk and human breast milk, in terms of both relative abundance and primary structure, influences the protein utilization in preterm infants and leads to distinct metabolic responses [21, 22]. Moreover, compared with other animal milks, donkey milk is known for its low allergenicity, ease of digestion and absorption. It is the closest to human milk, making it an excellent alternative. Breast milk fortifiers derived from donkey milk could therefore serve as an emerging option [23–26]. Numerous studies have shown that breast milk supplements containing trace elements, macronutrients, vitamins, lipids, proteins, etc., can compensate for insufficient nutrients in the breast milk of premature infants [27–30]. However, the application of HMF and alternative milk-based fortifiers (such as BMF and DMF) has raised controversies regarding their relationship with the incidence and mortality of conditions such as NEC, despite improving growth and developmental outcomes. For example, a study by Premkumar MH indicated that there is insufficient evidence to assess the superiority of BMF over HMF [31]. Research by O’Connor suggested that BMF and HMF did not affect the morbidity or mortality of premature infants. In preterm infants receiving exclusive breastfeeding, a significant gap remains in direct and indirect evidence regarding the comparative effectiveness of milk-based fortifiers [32]. Furthermore, there is a lack of evidence evaluating the specific impact of BMF, HMF, and DMF on morbidity and mortality of premature infants, as well as other growth and developmental outcomes. In particular, evidence-based research on DMF related to premature or very-low-birth-weight infants is lacking.

Network meta-analysis was implemented in this study to determine the most effective milk-based fortifiers for reducing morbidity and mortality among preterm infants, thereby filling a significant knowledge gap. Network meta-analysis enables both direct and indirect comparisons of multiple interventions, even when some have not been directly assessed in certain studies [33, 34]. To our knowledge, no prior study has employed an network meta-analysis to evaluate the comparative effectiveness of different milk-based fortifiers on the morbidity and mortality of premature infants. This review, therefore, aims to assess both established and emerging milk-based fortifiers, evaluating the relative effectiveness and the certainty of evidence regarding the ability of BMF, HMF, and DMF to improve outcomes in preterm infants.

Methods

Registration

This study is registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, ID CRD42025642432). It adheres to the Systematic Reviews involving meta-analysis network (PRISMA-NMA) guidelines for network meta-analysis [33].

Study inclusion and exclusion standards

The study inclusion and exclusion standards were established according to the five key pillars of the PICOS framework, which include P (population), I (interv-ention), C (comparison), O (outcome), and S (study design).

Inclusion criteria

Population

This study focused on premature and low-birth-weight infants who were provided with breast milk, maternal milk, donor milk, or a combination of these and who received fortifiers derived from human, bovine or donkey milk. Preterm infants with a gestational age of ≤ 32 weeks and a birth weight of ≤ 1500 g.

Interventions

The primary interventions were breast milk fortified only with BMF, HMF, or DMF (maternal milk, donor milk, or both). The target level of milk intake did not vary.

Comparison

BMF vs. HMF, BMF vs. DMF, and HMF vs. DMF (indirect comparison).

Outcomes

Primary outcome

Incidence of NEC and mortality.

Secondary outcome

Incidence of IVH and neonatal sepsis. Additional outcomes included growth and developmental outcomes, neurodevelopmental outcomes, gastroesophageal reflux (GER) and metabolomics analysis.

At least one of the outcomes mentioned above must be included in the study.

Study design

Randomized clinical trials published in English or Chinese that focused on breast milk fortifiers were included.

Exclusion standards

Animal studies, expert opinions, incomplete articles, course summaries, grey literature, abstracts, editorials, review articles, books, scientific event reports, and studies without accessible full texts were excluded.

Databases and search strategy

We searched the following databases: Embase, MEDLINE, CINAHL, PubMed, SinoMed, CNKI, and JBI. A research strategy was developed utilizing both subject headings and free-text terms. Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” facilitated the combination of these terms. The research was executed according to the specific strategies for each database (refer to Supplementary Material 1) with the coverage extending from database inception to April 1, 2025.

Screening of literature and data extraction

Two researchers independently performed the literature screening and data extraction using EndNote 21 reference management software. In cases of discrepancies, discussions were held to resolve them, with a third researcher consulted when consensus could not be reached. After the relevant data were identified, the following information was extracted and organized into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet: author names, journal, country of origin, publication year, sample size, gestational age (GA) in weeks, interventions, primary outcomes, and other significant adverse events.

Risk of bias and quality of evidence

The methodological quality of the studies included in this review was evaluated by two independent reviewers utilizing the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool RoB 2.0 [35]. The assessment covered five major aspects, including the randomization process, deviation from intended intervention, missing outcome data, measurement of outcome, and selection of reported results. In cases of disagreements, a third team member was consulted to resolve discrepancies through discussion until a consensus was achieved. Each study’s overall risk of bias was classified as low risk, some concern, or high risk.

Statistical methods

We used Stata 15 software to process the data and perform the network meta-analysis utilizing the Network and Mvmeta packages within the frequentist framework. We constructed an evidence network and assessed the ranking probabilities of various interventions by using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA), thereby comparing their effects on morbidity and mortality. A funnel plot (comparison-adjusted plot) was created to evaluate publication bias, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Symmetry around the effect estimate line would indicate no publication bias or minor study effects.

When closed loops were established among interventions, an inconsistency test was necessary to assess the agreement between direct and indirect comparison results. However, the network structure in this study formed a straightforward chain, consisting of comparisons between BMF and DMF, as well as between BMF and HMF, but a direct comparison between HMF and DMF was lacking. All included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were selected to ensure a focus on consistent target populations, uniform interventions, and identical outcome measures, thereby minimizing heterogeneity arising from variations in these factors. The distribution of significant potential effect modifiers—including but not limited to baseline patient characteristics and intervention details—was meticulously examined across studies to ensure adequate similarity in key characteristics.

For the primary outcomes in this study, dichotomous variables were measured using the risk ratio (RR) as the effect size. The combined RRs with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated to compare the effects of different treatments. Statistical analyses for each primary outcome were conducted using Stata 15 with the metan package. Heterogeneity was assessed through the Cochran’s Q test and I² statistic.

Results

Search results

As of April 1, 2025, the initial literature search yielded 14,724 articles. Using EndNote 21 software, 7064 duplicate articles were excluded. Meta-analyses, systematic reviews, reviews (450), and animal studies (3) were excluded. After screening abstracts and titles, 5378 articles were excluded. Additionally, expert consensus papers (28), guidelines, conference papers (1672), and protocols (108) were excluded. Following a thorough review of the articles, 10 were excluded because they did not satisfy the established study criteria, and one was excluded because it did not report any of the predefined outcome indicators for RCTs. Ultimately, 10 RCTs were included in the analysis [19, 22, 32, 36–43], with 5 studies [19, 22, 38, 40, 43] derived from the same trial (Registration Number: ISRCTN70022881) reporting additional outcomes for subgroups of premature infants or different outcomes at 18 months of follow-up. Two other studies were from the same trial (Registration Number: NCT02137473) [32, 41] and reported different outcomes for subgroups of premature infants. To avoid participant duplication, only the most comprehensive primary article from the same trial was included in the network meta-analysis when the same outcome was reported. If substudies reported additional independent outcomes, such as follow-up or extended analyses, they were included in the qualitative synthesis of the systematic review.

To prevent double-counting, the baseline population of the network meta-analysis was based on the initial cohort. The total sample size was 546 participants, including 156 from the ISRCTN70022881 trial and 127 from the NCT02137473 trial. The three remaining independent studies involved a total of 263 participants (Fig. 1).

SUCRA ranking probabilities

To identify potentially optimal interventions among different fortifiers for reducing NEC, mortality, IVH and neonatal sepsis in preterm infants, cumulative ranking probability curves were generated. The surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) offers a quantitative assessment of the likelihood that each intervention is the most effective. A higher SUCRA value, approaching 100%, indicates a greater probability that the corresponding intervention is ranked as the most effective option. In contrast, a lower SUCRA value, near 0%, suggests that the intervention is more likely to be among the least effective (Fig. 4).

Based on cumulative ranking probabilities, HMF was most likely to be the optimal intervention for reducing NEC incidence. SUCRA rankings: HMF (86.4%) > DMF (37.7%) > BMF (25.9%), suggesting HMF has the highest potential efficacy, followed by DMF, with BMF being the least effective. In terms of the probability of being best (PrBest), HMF (73.2%) demonstrated a significantly higher probability than DMF (26.5%) and BMF (0.3%), further supporting the potential superior efficacy of HMF (Table 6).

Publication bias

Funnel plots (comparison-adjusted) were generated to assess publication bias for these outcomes. The NEC, mortality, IVH, neonatal sepsis and comparison-adjusted plot showed a symmetrical distribution of effect sizes (log RR) around zero for all comparisons, indicating no significant publication bias. Global heterogeneity assessment of NEC yielded I² = 0% and a p value = 0.794. Global heterogeneity assessment of mortality resulted in I² = 0% and a p value = 0.819. Global heterogeneity assessment of IVH yielded I² = 27.1% and p = 0.254. Global heterogeneity testing of neonatal sepsis revealed I² = 28.4% and p value = 0.232. (refer to Supplementary Material 2).

Discussion

Nutritional management of preterm infants remains a critical concern in the NICU. Nutritional fortifiers are widely used in clinical medicine, the food industry, and public health [44, 45]. This review summarizes the existing evidence regarding the use of milk-based fortifiers to mitigate morbidity and mortality in preterm infants. Although the preliminary results provide some valuable insights, they must be interpreted within the context of certain limitations. Research on milk-based fortifiers, particularly regarding the use of DMF, is limited, and sample populations in these studies are relatively small. To strengthen the validity of the results, we restricted the inclusion criteria to only those infants who were exclusively breastfed, including either maternal or donated breast milk, to avoid the confounding effects of formula feeding. In one included study, Sullivan reported the occurrence of NEC when fortifiers were added to varying amounts of enteral breast milk (40 mL/kg/d and 100 mL/kg/d, with HMF) [37]. We performed a meta-analysis of the effect size, obtaining a relative risk (RR) of 0.322 (0.089–1.164). Despite the heterogeneity test showing no significant difference in effect sizes between HM40 and HM100 (I²=0%), the limited sample size and potential clinical significance of dosage differences prompted us to treat HM40 and HM100 as separate nodes in the network meta-analysis to avoid information bias.

In this network meta-analysis, the comparison between BMF and HMF regarding the incidence of NEC resulted in a risk ratio, demonstrating a significant benefit of HMF in reducing NEC occurrence in preterm infants. This outcome is consistent with the findings of Huston [46]. Although other network meta-analyses have shown no statistically significant differences between HMF and DMF or between BMF and DMF in terms of morbidity rates for NEC, IVH, neonatal sepsis, and mortality, the limited number of studies on DMF and small sample sizes (N = 31 in one study) may have contributed to insufficient statistical power to detect small yet clinically meaningful differences between the groups [42]. Nevertheless, these results still offer valuable insights for clinical practice.

Although no statistically significant differences were found, clinical decision-making should take into account additional evidence, such as SUCRA rankings, to form a comprehensive judgement rather than relying solely on p values. We have presented a SUCRA ranking plot to explain clinical priorities. In terms of improving the incidence of NEC, the effectiveness of the three fortifiers may be ranked as follows: HMF > DMF > BMF. Similarly, in terms of reducing mortality, the effectiveness may be ranked as follows: HMF > DMF > BMF. In terms of reducing the incidence and mortality of NEC, compared with other types of fortifiers, HMF may be associated with more favourable outcomes. This potential advantage may be due to its specific nutritional composition, including high concentrations of polyunsaturated fatty acids and immunomodulatory bioactive components [47], which could positively impact survival rates in preterm infants. DMF exhibited lower effect sizes than HMF; however, the wide confidence intervals in the comparisons with HMF suggest significant uncertainty. Conversely, BMF was linked to a comparatively higher risk in this analysis. However, in terms of improving IVH incidence, the effectiveness of the three fortifiers may be ranked as follows: DMF > BMF > HMF. Compared with other fortifiers, DMF may provide superior benefits for infants at risk of IVH, likely due to its pharmacological mechanisms, including anti-inflammatory and vascular protective effects [48, 49]. In contrast, HMF appears to have a more limited potential impact on decreasing IVH risk. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, and a thorough assessment of the risk‒benefit profile is recommended prior to clinical application, as only three studies on this outcome were included, and the sample sizes were small. Future research should verify the potential advantages of donkey milk through standardized dosing studies and explore the mechanism underlying its anti-inflammatory properties. Regarding neonatal sepsis, the efficacy of the three fortifiers may be ranked as follows: DMF > BMF > HMF. DMF may represent an optimal option and serve as a viable alternative in settings where HMF is unavailable. In contrast, the effect estimates associated with HMF indicate that its impact may be more limited than that of other interventions. Therefore, a careful evaluation of its risk–benefit profile is recommended prior to clinical application, especially in high-risk neonatal populations.

The potential advantages of HMF may be linked to natural immunoglobulins in human milk (e.g., sIgA), lactoferrin, and low allergenicity, which could reduce infection risk by modulating the gut microbiota and immune responses [50]. In contrast, BMF, while offering a higher protein content, has a greater casein ratio, which may result in poor digestive tolerance, an increased risk of feeding intolerance, and a higher likelihood of allergic reactions, given that casein is the primary allergenic component in bovine milk [48, 51]. The potential biological effects of donkey milk align with the SUCRA ranking results for DMF in preventing IVH and neonatal sepsis. The significantly superior efficacy of DMF in reducing IVH and neonatal sepsis is likely related to its immune-regulating properties. The whey protein content of donkey milk is similar to that of human milk, and its unique fatty acid composition may offer protective effects through antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antiaging, antimicrobial, and anticancer biological activities [49, 52]. Some studies suggest that donkey milk could serve as a new nutritional food or a potential alternative to breast milk or formula [53, 54]. For infants with cow’s milk protein allergy (CMPA), donkey milk may also be a suitable alternative [51, 54].

For infants with limited access to human milk, DMF may be considered an alternative feeding option; however, its safety profile and cost-effectiveness require further evaluation. Given the limited number of trials included in this analysis, any clinical recommendations derived from these findings should be interpreted with caution. These results should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. Clinical decision-making must be informed by a comprehensive evaluation of local conditions, including resource availability, regulatory approval status, and economic viability. DMF may be considered a potential option only if it is accessible, complies with local regulations, and is economically sustainable. Under optimal circumstances where HMF is available and financially feasible, it remains the preferred option. In settings where HMF is unavailable, DMF may represent an alternative worth exploring, although its clinical effects are associated with considerable uncertainty and thus require careful consideration in practice. Regarding BMF, clinicians should diligently evaluate its potential risks, particularly in high-risk infant populations, when other options are limited. Clinical decision-making should consider individual risks, such as allergy history, gut maturity, and resource availability, to avoid reliance on a single indicator.

In terms of other outcomes, our systematic review revealed that an additional analysis of the “Fortilat” trial by Cresi, F [43] demonstrated a significantly lower incidence of multichannel intraluminal impedance-gastroesophageal reflux (MII-GER) in the DMF group than in the BMF group. The median incidence in the DMF group was 2.02 (1.95–3.26), while that in the BMF group was 4.82 (2.84–5.94), which was a statistically significant difference (p = 0.036). In a metabolomics study, Giribaldi, M [22], reported that the urinary excretion of galactose was significantly greater in the DMF group than in the BMF group (p < 0.05), consistent with the higher lactose content in DMF. In contrast, urinary levels of carnitine, choline, lysine, and leucine were elevated in the BMF group (p < 0.05), which may be related to the metabolic differences in bovine milk protein hydrolysis products. Peila, C [38] reported no significant differences in growth or ontogenic progression at 18 months of corrected age (auxological outcomes) between the two groups (head circumference: BMF group − 0.066 vs. DMF group − 0.132, p = 0.758; weight: BMF group − 0.955 vs. DMF group − 0.997, p = 0.833) and emphasized that the DMF group had significantly fewer feeding intolerance events, suggesting a short-term clinical advantage. In another study by Peila, C [38], a neurodevelopmental follow-up of 103 infants from the “Fortilat” trial using the Griffiths-II scale revealed similar overall development quotients (GQ) between the two groups (BMF group 101.6 vs. DMF group 101.2, p = 0.430), with no difference in the incidence of mild or severe neurological impairments. Notably, a higher proportion of infants in the DMF group were small for gestational age (SGA), but this did not affect their neurological outcomes. Hopperton, K. E [41] performed a neurodevelopmental assessment at 18 months using the Bayley-III scale and revealed no statistically detectable disparities between the two groups in cognitive, language, or motor domains (all p > 0.05). The study highlighted that although HMF had a higher cost, no neurodevelopmental advantage was observed.

Limitations

The limited sample sizes in the included studies may have affected the statistical power of the analysis. The network meta-analysis assumes network consistency and transitivity; however, some indirect comparison paths may lead to model bias due to insufficient data. Results were primarily based on data from high-income countries, and their applicability to preterm infants in low- and middle-income regions requires further validation. Future research should focus on multicentre RCTs to directly assess the long-term efficacy and safety of HMF and DMF. Additionally, mechanistic investigations and targeted clinical studies should be conducted to optimize fortification strategies. Concurrently, a comprehensive evaluation of resource availability, regulatory frameworks, and cost-effectiveness is crucial for advancing personalized and precision nutritional approaches in the NICU.

Conclusion

HMF and DMF are likely to offer significant potential for addressing various complications in preterm infants. Specifically, HMF has advantages over other fortifiers in reducing the incidence of NEC and mortality rates. In contrast, DMF appears to be more effective in decreasing the occurrence of IVH and neonatal sepsis. Although DMF exhibited a more modest effect size than HMF in preventing NEC, no statistically significant difference was identified between the two interventions. In resource-limited settings or in situations where HMF is unavailable, DMF may serve as an alternative option; however, its application should be guided by a thorough and adaptive risk–benefit analysis tailored to the specific clinical characteristics of each infant. These findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. Nevertheless, our results may provide preliminary evidence-based insights for the development of individualized nutritional strategies for preterm infants. Additionally, we establish a foundation for further investigation into the mechanisms that connect milk composition with clinical outcomes, thereby informing future nutritional management practices involving milk fortifiers.
