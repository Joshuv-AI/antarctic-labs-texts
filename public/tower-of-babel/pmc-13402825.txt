Choroidal structure as predictor of macular changes and visual outcomes after panretinal photocoagulation in eyes with very severe NPDR and early PDR without center-involving DME
Authors: Esmaeil Asadi Khameneh, Mohammadreza Mehrabi Bahar, Nastaran Abbasian, Baharan Rahmani Chianeh, Alireza Khodabandeh, Elias Khalili Pour, Hamid Riazi-Esfahani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPanretinal photocoagulation (PRP) is widely used for advanced diabetic retinopathy, yet its effects on retinal and choroidal architecture remain incompletely characterized. This prospective observational study evaluated retinal and choroidal structural changes after PRP in 39 eyes of 21 patients with very severe non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) or early proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) without center-involving diabetic macular edema (DME) using enhanced depth imaging optical coherence tomography (EDI-OCT) at baseline and at 1 and 3 months post-treatment. Quantitative parameters included foveal and whole macular thickness, subfoveal choroidal thickness (SFCT), and choroidal vascularity index (CVI), while intraretinal fluid (IRF) and hyperreflective foci (HF) were evaluated by masked graders. Foveal thickness increased significantly at 1 month (p = 0.017) and 3 months (p = 0.032), and whole macular thickness increased at 1 month (p < 0.001), with stable visual acuity. SFCT increase at 1 month was associated with retinal thickening (B = 0.154, p = 0.008). CVI elevation at 1 month correlated with visual improvement (B = − 0.015, p = 0.013), whereas CVI increase at 3 months was associated with visual decline (B = 0.019, p = 0.007). SFCT increase at 3 months was significantly associated with IRF formation (p = 0.016), indicating that EDI-OCT-derived choroidal metrics may serve as predictive biomarkers of post-PRP macular structural and functional responses.Keywords: Diabetic retinopathy, Panretinal photocoagulation, Choroidal vascularity index(CVI), Subfoveal choroidal thickness (SFCT), Macular thicknessSubject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Medical research

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a leading cause of visual impairment among the working-age population worldwide1,2. The progression from severe non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) to proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) represents a critical stage in disease evolution, necessitating timely intervention to prevent irreversible vision loss. Panretinal photocoagulation (PRP) remains the gold-standard treatment for PDR, effectively reducing the risk of vitreous hemorrhage and tractional retinal detachment2. Although PRP effectively reduces the risk of vision-threatening complications in PDR, several clinical and imaging studies have demonstrated that PRP may induce transient macular thickening and alterations in retinal function, even in eyes without center-involving diabetic macular edema (DME)3. These changes are thought to result from inflammatory mediator release, blood–retinal barrier disruption, and hemodynamic redistribution following laser treatment3–5.

Enhanced depth optical coherence tomography (EDI-OCT) enables visualization of both retinal and choroidal structures. Quantitative parameters such as subfoveal choroidal thickness (SFCT) and choroidal vascularity index (CVI) have emerged as potential biomarkers of microvascular health and inflammation in diabetic eyes6,7. Nevertheless, the dynamic response of these biomarkers to PRP and their relationship with macular changes and visual acuity remains poorly understood.

Despite increasing recognition of choroidal involvement in diabetic retinopathy, it remains unclear whether short-term dynamic changes in choroidal structure after PRP are merely epiphenomena or whether they are associated with subsequent macular structural alterations and functional outcomes. In particular, the prognostic significance of early post-PRP changes in SFCT and CVI has not been well established in eyes without center-involving DME.

Therefore, the present study aimed to determine whether postoperative changes in choroidal parameters following PRP are associated with macular thickening, intraretinal fluid (IRF), hyperreflective foci (HF), and best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA). By clarifying these relationships, we sought to better understand the clinical relevance of choroidal remodeling after PRP.

Methods

This observational prospective study included 39 eyes from 21 patients diagnosed with very severe NPDR or early PDR without center-involving DME and was approved by the local institutional review board (IR.TUMS.FARABIH.REC.1402.024). Written informed consents were obtained from the patients. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

From February 2023 to November 2024, patients with very severe nonproliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) or early proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) without center-involving diabetic macular edema (DME) on optical coherence tomography (OCT) who were candidates for panretinal photocoagulation (PRP) were enrolled. Very severe NPDR was defined as the presence of at least two of the following: intraretinal hemorrhages or microaneurysms in all four quadrants, venous beading in two or more quadrants, or intraretinal microvascular abnormalities (IRMA) in at least one quadrant. Eyes with proliferative changes not meeting the criteria for high-risk PDR (HR-PDR) based on ETDRS classification were considered to have early PDR8. In cases with uncertain severity grading, fluorescein angiography was performed to better detect the presence, location, and extent of neovascularization.

Patients with very severe NPDR and early PDR were intentionally selected to evaluate choroidal and macular responses at a stage where PRP is clinically indicated but before the development of high-risk proliferative features or tractional complications. Studying this relatively homogeneous treatment window allowed assessment of PRP-induced microvascular and inflammatory changes while minimizing confounding effects from advanced ischemia or prior extensive retinal damage.

Eligible patients were required to have a BCVA between 0.1(20/25) and 1.0 (20/200) logarithm of minimum angle of resolution (LogMAR). Patients with central retinal thickness > 320 μm within the 1-mm ETDRS circle on OCT with BCVA less than 0.1 LogMAR (20/25) or with cystic changes at the foveal center were excluded.

Exclusion criteria included prior intravitreal anti-VEGF injections, previous PRP, glaucoma, uveitis, tractional retinal detachment, vitreomacular traction, retinal vascular diseases other than diabetic retinopathy, corneal opacity, uncontrolled diabetes (HbA1c > 8%), uncontrolled systemic hypertension (> 140/80 mmHg with or without medication), and any prior intraocular surgery other than cataract surgery. Patients with refractive error more than ± 3.0 diopters (spherical equivalent) were also excluded.

All subjects underwent comprehensive ophthalmic examinations, including BCVA assessment using a Snellen chart, with results recorded as LogMAR at baseline, 1 month, and 3 months after PRP. Slit-lamp biomicroscopy and dilated fundus examination were performed in all cases, and intraocular pressure was measured using Goldmann applanation tonometry.

PRP was performed by a single masked ophthalmologist using an Ellex Integre Pro Scan laser photocoagulator (Adelaide, Australia), following the guidelines of the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study Research Group. Treatment was delivered over two to three consecutive sessions under topical anesthesia at one-week intervals, using an argon laser (532 nm) with a 500 μm spot size to apply 1000–1200 evenly distributed gray-white burns across all four retinal quadrants delivered using a Volk SuperQuad contact lens. Patients were re-evaluated at 1 and 3 months after PRP to determine the need for additional treatment. Those with BCVA worse than 20/25 and central macular thickness greater than 320 μm were considered candidates for intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy.

Enhanced-depth imaging optical coherence tomography (EDI-OCT) was performed using the RTVue XR 100 Avanti system (Optovue, Inc., Fremont, United States) with a scan rate of 70,000 A-scans per second. All scans were acquired in EDI mode to optimize visualization of the choroidoscleral junction. A high-density horizontal line scan protocol (12 mm length, centered on the fovea) was used, consisting of a single enhanced-depth horizontal raster B-scan through the fovea with averaging to improve signal-to-noise ratio. To minimize the effect of diurnal variation in choroidal profile, all EDI-OCT scans were acquired between 8:00 and 11:00 a.m. Low-quality scans in which the choroidoscleral junction was indistinct were excluded.

Image analysis was performed using ImageJ (Fiji; available at http://imagej.nih.gov/ij). After calibrating the image scale, choroidal thickness—from Bruch’s membrane to the choroidoscleral junction—was manually measured at the fovea center using the software’s caliper tool. OCT image binarization and segmentation were performed following the protocol by Sonoda et al.9 Images were first converted to 8-bit format. The total choroidal area (TCA) was selected from the temporal optic disc margin extending 8 mm temporally using the polygonal selection tool and set as the region of interest (ROI). Three representative luminal regions were sampled, and their mean pixel intensity was subtracted from the image. Binarization was then applied using Niblack’s local threshold method to differentiate luminal (dark pixels) and stromal (light pixels) areas. The binarized images were reconverted to RGB format, and the luminal area (LA) was quantified using the color threshold tool; the stromal area (SA) was calculated as the difference between TCA and LA. Measurements were performed on the foveal raster. The choroidal vascularity index (CVI) was defined as the proportion of luminal area to total choroidal area (CVI = LA / TCA). (Fig. 1) In the original report by Sonoda et al., this parameter was described as the luminal-to-choroidal (L/C) ratio9. In subsequent literature, the same metric has been widely referred to as the CVI. For consistency with contemporary terminology, we use the term CVI throughout this manuscript.

Representative foveal enhanced-depth imaging optical coherence tomography (EDI-OCT) B-scan from a patient with early proliferative diabetic retinopathy, acquired using the RTVue XR 100 Avanti system (Optovue, Inc., Fremont, CA, USA). Choroidal thickness was manually measured using FIJI software. The choroidal boundaries were delineated with a continuous yellow line (A), and the image was subsequently binarized using the Niblack thresholding method (B). In this patient, the total choroidal area was 7.509 mm² and the choroidal luminal area was 4.995 mm². The choroidal vascularity index (CVI) was then calculated to be 66.5% (C).

All manual segmentations, including sclerochoroidal junction delineation and choroidal thickness measurements, were performed by an expert grader (EKH) and independently verified by another grader (HRE); discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Inter-rater reliability for SFCT and CVI measurements was evaluated using the absolute agreement model of the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) on twenty EDI-OCT images independently segmented by two mentioned graders in a previously published study. The ICC values were 0.988 (95% CI, 0.970–0.995) for SFCT and 0.969 (95% CI, 0.918–0.988) for CVI, indicating excellent agreement10.

The presence of IRF and HF was independently assessed by two masked vitreoretinal specialists (H.R. and E.A.), with disagreements resolved by a third specialist (E.K.).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported using means, standard deviations, medians, and ranges. Longitudinal changes in continuous outcomes (best-corrected visual acuity, foveal thickness, whole macular thickness, subfoveal choroidal thickness, and choroidal vascularity index) were assessed using linear mixed models (LMM). Absolute values of total choroidal area, luminal area, and stromal area were measured during image binarization; however, because CVI is mathematically derived from these parameters, including them simultaneously in regression models would introduce collinearity. Therefore, statistical analyses focused on SFCT and CVI as representative structural and vascular metrics. For binary outcomes (presence of intraretinal fluid and hyperreflective foci), generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) with a logit link function were employed to estimate odds ratios. All mixed models accounted for repeated measures within eyes and inter-eye correlations by including random intercepts for patients and eyes nested within patients, and were adjusted for age and sex. Beta coefficients from linear mixed models represent mean differences per unit change in the predictor, while those from GLMM represent log-odds (or exponentiated odds ratios when presented as such). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27).

Results

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 39 eyes from 21 patients (11 male, 10 female) with a mean age of 61.2 ± 8.6 years (range, 47–78 years) were included. Baseline mean BCVA was 0.57 ± 0.26 LogMAR. Mean baseline foveal thickness was 250.5 ± 29.8 μm, whole macular thickness 316.9 ± 24.4 μm, SFCT 250.0 ± 57.9 μm, and CVI 68.8 ± 3.1%. During the 3-month follow-up, none of the eyes with very severe NPDR progressed to PDR. All eyes with early PDR showed regression within 1 month, and no additional PRP treatments were required throughout the study period.

Longitudinal changes in retinal and choroidal parameters

Foveal thickness increased significantly compared with baseline at both 1 month (285.9 ± 47.0 μm, p = 0.017) and 3 months (289.2 ± 64.2 μm, p = 0.032). Whole macular thickness increased significantly at 1 month (342.8 ± 31.7 μm, p < 0.001), but the increase at 3 months was not statistically significant (p = 0.071). No significant change in BCVA was observed over the follow-up period (p = 0.142). SFCT and CVI also remained stable with no significant changes over time (Table 1; Fig. 2). During the 3-month follow-up period, none of the included eyes developed center-involving DME requiring intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy. according to predefined retreatment criteria.

At 1 month post-PRP, an increase in subfoveal choroidal thickness (SFCT) was significantly associated with greater whole macular thickening change(β = 0.154 μm per µm increase in SFCT; 95% CI 0.041–0.266; p = 0.008). An increase in choroidal vascularity index (CVI) at 1 month was associated with improvement in best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) (β = −0.015 LogMAR per 1% increase in CVI; 95% CI − 0.027 to − 0.003; p = 0.013).

At 3 months, greater increases in SFCT from baseline were associated with worsening BCVA (β = 0.001 LogMAR per µm increase in SFCT; 95% CI 0.001–0.002; p = 0.001) and higher odds of intraretinal fluid (IRF) development (OR = 1.009 per µm increase in SFCT; 95% CI 1.002–1.017; p = 0.016). Similarly, greater increases in CVI at 3 months were associated with worsening BCVA (β = 0.019 LogMAR per 1% increase in CVI; 95% CI 0.005–0.032; p = 0.007).

No significant associations were found between baseline SFCT or CVI and subsequent changes in retinal thickness, BCVA, IRF, or hyperreflective foci (HF).

Discussion

This study investigated the temporal relationships among retinal thickness, choroidal structural changes, and visual function in eyes undergoing PRP for very severe NPDR or early PDR without center-involving DME. Several important findings emerge from our analysis. First, both foveal and whole retinal thicknesses showed a transient increase after PRP, particularly at the 1-month follow-up. This aligns with previous reports that PRP can induce localized retinal edema, potentially due to increased inflammatory cytokine activity or breakdown of the blood-retinal barrier.

Second, although overall choroidal metrics did not significantly change over time, their associations with retinal structure and visual acuity were clinically relevant. Notably, an increase in subfoveal choroidal thickness at 1 month was significantly associated with retinal thickening. This suggests that choroidal expansion may contribute to post-PRP macular edema, possibly reflecting increased choroidal vascular permeability or stromal inflammation. Histological studies of photocoagulated regions have shown destruction of the outer retina, including the photoreceptor layer and RPE, along with complete obliteration of the choriocapillaris11. However, larger choroidal vessels remain intact within laser-treated areas12. It has therefore been proposed that the reduced blood flow in coagulated regions may be redistributed toward adjacent areas with intact choriocapillaris in the macula13. In current study, Interestingly, CVI showed a dual association with visual acuity: an early increase in CVI was correlated with visual improvement, whereas continued elevation at 3 months was associated with worse BCVA. This temporal pattern may indicate that initial choroidal perfusion recovery supports retinal function, while persistent vascular engorgement or stromal remodeling might compromise photoreceptor integrity. Although the beta coefficients relating CVI changes to BCVA appear numerically small (e.g., β = 0.019 LogMAR per 1% increase in CVI), a 5% sustained increase in CVI would correspond to approximately 0.095 LogMAR (nearly one Snellen line). Therefore, while modest at the individual percentage level, larger cumulative changes in choroidal vascularity may have clinically meaningful visual implications. Additionally, the association of IRF at 3 months with increases in subfoveal choroidal thickness suggests that PRP-induced changes in choroidal hemodynamics may lead to fluid shifts and exudation, even in the absence of center-involving DME.

Our findings are partially consistent with, but also distinct from, prior studies. Okamoto et al. reported a significant reduction in SFCT at 1 and 3 months after PRP, with no significant change in BCVA14. Kim et al. similarly observed a significant decrease in CVI, luminal area, and stromal area, as well as reduced SFCT at 3, 6, and 12 months post-PRP15. In contrast, Mirshahi et al. found that eyes with severe NPDR demonstrated increased choroidal, luminal, and stromal areas at 6 months, while PDR eyes exhibited a reverse trend6. They also reported that CVI increased shortly after PRP in severe NPDR and then returned to baseline by 6 months, whereas in PDR, CVI showed a consistent decrease16. These discrepancies may be explained by differences in duration of diabetic retinopathy, disease stage, PRP parameters, follow-up duration, or methods used for choroidal quantification. For example, in advanced stages of diabetic retinopathy, marked vascular hyalinization has been documented17. Consequently, the associated extensive choroidal alterations—including vascular occlusions, severe ischemia, and possible dysregulation of choroidal autoregulation—may reduce the capacity for blood flow redistribution and vascular remodeling after PRP18.

Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of choroidal health in determining retinal response and functional outcomes after PRP. EDI-OCT-derived biomarkers such as CVI and SFCT may serve as valuable tools for risk stratification and monitoring in patients undergoing PRP.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small and the follow-up duration was short; larger studies with extended follow-up are needed to confirm these findings. This prospective observational study included 39 eyes from 21 patients, representing a moderate sample size for evaluating short-term choroidal and retinal changes following PRP. Prior studies assessing post-PRP choroidal parameters have employed comparable cohorts, including Kim et al. (2020; 65 eyes) and Mirshahi et al. (2022; 39 eyes stratified by retinopathy stage), and demonstrated statistically significant changes in subfoveal choroidal thickness (SFCT) and choroidal vascularity index (CVI)6,15.

Retrospective power estimations based on effect sizes reported in these studies—such as mean SFCT reductions of approximately 20 μm with paired standard deviations of 30–50 μm, or CVI changes of 1–1.5% with standard deviations around 3%—suggest that a sample size of approximately 19–49 eyes would provide 80% power to detect moderate effects in primary outcomes like SFCT using paired analyses. Detection of smaller changes in CVI would require larger samples (up to ~ 96 eyes). Accordingly, the present cohort appears adequately powered to detect clinically meaningful associations between choroidal parameters and post-PRP macular thickening or intraretinal fluid development, while acknowledging that larger samples may be necessary to identify subtler or stage-specific effects. Second, evaluating patients with very severe NPDR, and early PDR as a single group may have introduced heterogeneity, as treatment response could differ across disease stages. Although both very severe NPDR and early PDR represent clinically appropriate stages for PRP, inflammatory response and cytokine release following laser treatment may differ according to retinopathy severity. The relatively modest sample size precluded subgroup analysis. Future larger studies stratifying by disease stage may clarify stage-specific choroidal responses and prognostic implications. Third, our study did not evaluate potential changes in participants’ systemic health status. However, substantial alterations are unlikely within the relatively short follow-up period, and the within-subject analytical approach likely mitigated residual confounding. Forth, although volumetric assessment of choroidal vascularity across the entire macular region represents the most robust method for evaluating CVI in both physiological and pathological states, our analyses were limited to foveal horizontal B-scans. While Agrawal et al. showed that a single subfoveal scan can represent the posterior pole in healthy eyes, this has not been validated in diabetic retinopathy19. Therefore, extrapolation of these findings from the foveal slab to the whole macular area should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, visual acuity is likely more closely related to the integrity of the choriocapillaris rather than global choroidal metrics. Because CVI and SFCT represent composite structural parameters encompassing larger choroidal vessels and stroma, subtle alterations confined to the choriocapillaris layer may not be fully captured. Future studies using choriocapillaris flow metrics may provide more precise insights into microvascular–functional relationships. Finally, the manual calculation of choroidal indices is not standardized across studies, and thus, Comparisons with other reports should be made with caution.

This study demonstrates that PRP in eyes with very severe NPDR, or early PDR without center-involving DME induces macular thickening without significant visual decline. Subfoveal choroidal thickening and increased choroidal vascularity were associated with intraretinal fluid and visual acuity changes, suggesting a role for choroidal hemodynamics and inflammation in post-PRP macular response. EDI-OCT-derived choroidal biomarkers may have utility in predicting and monitoring macular status after PRP, even in the absence of overt DME.
