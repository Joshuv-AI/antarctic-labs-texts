Trunk rotation and hand-elbow-shoulder axis disruption increase the risk of humeral shaft fractures during arm wrestling: a video analysis study
Authors: Erman Öğümsöğütlü, Seçkin Özcan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArm wrestling is a globally popular sport that can lead to serious orthopaedic injuries, particularly humeral shaft fractures. Despite their importance, the mechanisms and positional risk factors underlying these injuries have not yet been clearly defined. This study aimed to investigate whether specific body positions are associated with an increased risk of humeral shaft fractures.MethodsThis study was designed as a retrospective video-based observational comparative study. Seventy-four publicly available videos of arm wrestling matches resulting in humeral shaft fractures were independently evaluated by two orthopaedic specialists. In each video, the exact moment of fracture was identified, and the body positions of injured competitors were compared with those of their non-injured opponents. Variables including sex of competitors, side, match position (standing or sitting), time to fracture, and positions of the trunk, elbow, and wrist were recorded and statistically analyzed.ResultsA total of 148 competitors (74 with fractures and 74 non-injured opponents) were included in the analysis. Fractures occurred in the defense zone in 46 competitors (62.2%) and in the attack zone in 28 competitors (37.8%). Fracture occurrence was significantly associated with the angular zone of injury (p = 0.032). Fractures were more frequent among competitors who rotated their trunk toward the direction of attack (p < 0.001). Misalignment of the wrist, elbow, and shoulder and posterior positioning of the wrist relative to the shoulder were observed in 81.1% of competitors sustaining a fracture and were significantly associated with fracture occurrence (p < 0.001).ConclusionsThe primary finding of this study was that trunk rotation, particularly toward the direction of attack, and disruption of the hand–elbow–shoulder axis were associated with fracture occurrence and may reflect increased torsional loading of the humerus. In addition, fractures were more frequently observed in defensive positions. These findings may help athletes participate in arm wrestling competitions more safely by increasing awareness of high-risk body positions.

Introduction

The origin of arm wrestling, also known as “wrist wrestling,” can be traced back to ancient Egypt, as documented in hieroglyphs [1]. Arm wrestling is commonly observed in informal settings, such as playgrounds and social venues, as well as among amateur and professional athletes. In this sport, two competitors—either standing or seated—place their flexed elbows on a horizontal surface, grasp each other’s hands, and attempt to pull the opponent’s arm toward one side of the table.

Arm wrestling requires minimal equipment, as the presence of two competitors alone is sufficient to conduct a match. Formal arm wrestling competitions began in the 1950s. Today, professional and amateur tournaments are governed and standardized by official bodies such as the United States Armwrestling Federation (USAF) and World Armwrestling Federation (WAF).

Although arm wrestling is widely perceived as a harmless activity, it is not without risk, as it can lead to serious injuries in athletes, such as soft tissue ruptures, joint dislocations, and bone fractures. Humeral shaft fractures are the most frequently encountered injury pattern [2, 3]. Humeral fractures account for approximately 5–8% of all fractures of the upper extremity, whereas humeral shaft fractures account for approximately 1–3% of all orthopaedic injuries [4, 5]. The etiological factors associated with humeral fractures vary according to patient age; in younger individuals, high-energy mechanisms such as traffic accidents, falls from height, sports-related trauma, direct rotational forces, and forceful muscular contractions are the predominant causes. In contrast, in older individuals, humeral fractures are more commonly associated with low-energy trauma, such as simple falls, often in the context of osteoporotic bone fragility [6–8]. With the growing popularity of arm wrestling in recent years, humeral fractures associated with this sport have gained increasing clinical importance. Several risk factors have been identified for arm wrestling-related humeral fractures, such as rapid and forceful contraction of the arm, exposure of the humeral shaft to torsional force, and incorrect positioning of the upper extremity and body [3, 9–11].

This study analyzed publicly available arm wrestling videos on YouTube to identify potential positional risk factors for injury by comparing different arm and body positions and other relevant variables. We hypothesized that defensive positioning, trunk rotation, and disruption of the hand–elbow–shoulder axis would be associated with fracture occurrence.

Methods

This study was designed as a retrospective video-based observational comparative study. Videos were identified through a detailed search of the YouTube video database using the keywords “arm wrestling,” “arm fracture,” and “bone fracture” on December 15, 2024. To minimize potential personalization bias, the search was conducted under standardized conditions independent of prior user activity. Following screening based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 74 eligible videos were included in the analysis. Videos were included if they (1) demonstrated an arm-wrestling match in which a humeral fracture clearly occurred during the competition; (2) captured the entire match continuously from the initiation of the contest to the moment of fracture, without interruption or editing; (3) had sufficient visual resolution and frame stability to allow accurate observation of the athletes’ upper extremities and body posture; and (4) featured both competitors positioned uniformly (i.e., standing or sitting) with both hands, forearms, elbows, and trunks clearly visible throughout the sequence. Videos were included only if they demonstrated a sudden rotational deformity of the arm during competition, a visual feature that has been widely described in the literature as characteristic of acute humeral shaft fracture in arm wrestling.

Videos were excluded if they (1) failed to display the full course of the match; (2) were recorded from an angle that obscured the position of the arm, hand, or trunk; (3) contained significant visual obstructions or motion blur preventing adequate kinematic assessment; or (4) had poor visual quality. Additionally, duplicate recordings of the same event uploaded by different users were identified, and only the highest-quality version was retained for analysis.

All eligible videos that met the inclusion criteria were subjected to a detailed analysis. In each video, the injured competitor was considered the case, whereas the opposing competitor without a fracture served as the comparator for positional analysis. Upper extremity and trunk positions were recorded for each competitor.

Body position analysis

All videos were independently evaluated by two orthopaedic specialists. In cases of disagreement, the videos were jointly reviewed, and a consensus was reached through discussion. Each reviewer assessed the competitors’ trunk, elbow, and wrist positions at the exact moment of the fracture (referred to as the “injury frame”) according to a predefined checklist (Table 1). The moment of fracture was defined as the frame in which a sudden angular deformity of the humerus was first visually observed. For each video, data were collected regarding the competitor’s sex, side, whether the match was played standing or sitting, time to fracture, positions of the trunk, elbow, and wrist in the injury frame, and whether strength was drawn from the contralateral hand. All included cases demonstrated humeral shaft fractures as visually identifiable primary injuries. The presence of associated soft tissue or additional injuries could not be reliably determined from the videos and was therefore not analyzed. Also, fracture classification based on anatomical location was not performed, as the primary objective of the study was to evaluate body position at the moment of injury rather than fracture morphology.

Predefined checklist for positional assessment

Body position variables were assessed according to predefined criteria to ensure consistent evaluation. Accordingly, elbow flexion was recorded as “narrow” or “wide” depending on whether it was less than or greater than 90°, respectively. Competitors’ trunks were categorized as “flexion” or “extension” based on whether the trunk was positioned forward or backward relative to the neutral position. Only competitors with their trunks facing straight ahead were recorded as “no rotation”. Trunk rotation was defined as any visible axial rotation of the torso away from the neutral forward-facing position. Trunk tilt was defined as lateral deviation of the torso toward the direction of attack or defense relative to the neutral upright position. Videos in which competitors tilted their bodies toward the direction of attack or defense were recorded. Competitors’ wrist positions were categorized as “flexion”, “neutral”, or “extension”. Videos featuring competitors drawing strength from their contralateral hands in the direction of the attack were recorded. Videos demonstrating misalignment of the wrist, elbow, and shoulder, with the wrist positioned posterior to the shoulder, were also noted. Hand–elbow–shoulder axis alignment was considered disrupted when these anatomical landmarks were not collinear in the injury frame, particularly when the wrist was positioned posterior to the shoulder. Identification of fracture zones

Videos were further categorized according to predefined 45-degree angular zones in which fractures developed. Accordingly, videos in which fractures developed within angular ranges of 0–45° and 45–90° in the attack direction and 45–90° and 0–45° in the defense direction were classified as “Zone 1”, “Zone 2”, “Zone 3” and “Zone 4”, respectively (Fig. 2). For each video, the zone where the bone fracture occurred, the time elapsed in the respective zone until the development of the fracture, and the number of positional transitions between zones were recorded. Fractures were categorized according to the athlete’s position at the time of injury using predefined zones; Zone 1 and Zone 2 were defined as the attack position, whereas Zone 3 and Zone 4 were defined as the defence position. Ethical considerations

No ethical permission was required as all videos accessed were publicly available, relevant data were treated confidentially, and no athletes’ personal information was accessed [12]. All individuals included in the videos were not identifiable, and no personal data were collected or reported. All sportspersons presented in the figures were de-identified, and no identifiable personal information is shown.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were statistically analyzed using SPSS 22.0 (Statistical Product and Service Solutions for Windows, version 22.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, U.S., 2013) software package. The results of the statistical analyses were expressed using descriptive statistics, i.e., mean ± standard deviation values for numerical variables and frequencies (n) and percentage (%) values for categorical variables. Intergroup comparisons were conducted using the chi-square, Kruskal-Wallis, and Mann-Whitney U tests. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for key categorical variables to quantify the strength of associations with fracture occurrence. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses.

Results

A total of 148 competitors were identified across the 74 eligible videos. Of these, 134 (90.5%) were male and 14 (9.5%) were female. Eighteen (24.3%) matches were conducted on the left arm and 56 (75.7%) on the right arm. Fifty matches (67.6%) were performed in the standing position, and 24 (32.4%) in the sitting position. The mean duration of the matches was 5.08 s (1–20). The mean time elapsed in the fracture zone was 2.09 s (1–9) and the mean number of transitions between angular zones was 0.73 (0–6). Fractures occurred more frequently in the defense zone (46 competitors, 62.2%) than in the attack zone (28 competitors, 37.8%). With respect to angular distribution, fractures were most frequently observed in Zone 3, followed by Zone 2, while they were less common in Zones 1 and 4.

For positional comparisons, the injured competitors were compared with their opposing, noninjured counterparts within the same videos. No significant differences were observed between fracture occurrence and sex, side, match position, elbow angle, wrist position, trunk flexion/extension, trunk tilt, or use of the contralateral hand (all p > 0.05). However, a significant relationship was observed between fracture development and the angular zone in which the fracture occurred (p = 0.032). The number of fractures occurring in the defense zone was significantly higher than that in the attack zone (p = 0.003; OR 2.70, 95% CI 1.39–5.25). Additionally, trunk rotation was significantly associated with fracture occurrence (p < 0.001; OR 3.70, 95% CI 1.86–7.35), with a higher proportion of fractures observed among competitors who rotated their trunk toward the direction of attack. Misalignment of the wrist, elbow, and shoulder axes and posterior positioning of the wrist relative to the shoulder were also significantly associated with fracture occurrence (p < 0.001; OR 18.37, 95% CI 8.07–41.82). Discussion

The primary finding of this study was that trunk rotation and disruption of the hand–elbow–shoulder axis were associated with fracture occurrence and may reflect increased torsional loading of the humerus. Moreover, fractures were more frequently observed when competitors were in defensive positions, suggesting that this posture may represent a potential risk factor for fracture occurrence.

From an epidemiological perspective, sports-related injuries account for approximately 5–7% of all humeral shaft fractures; however, these data are typically reported as a single category without distinction between specific sports types or contact characteristics. Moreover, there are no well-established epidemiological data in the literature regarding the incidence of humeral shaft fractures specifically associated with arm wrestling [13, 14].

Humeral fractures are the most common type of injuries encountered in arm wrestling competitions [15–18]. The biomechanical mechanisms underlying the occurrence of these fractures were defined by Brismar and Spangen and were confirmed by Ogawa and colleagues [14, 19, 20]. In a standard arm-wrestling match, both competitors maintain their elbows fixed on a flat surface while attempting to overpower each other, and the shoulder is flexed at approximately 45°. During this maneuver, the humerus is subjected to internal rotational forces due to concentric contraction of the pectoralis major, latissimus dorsi, subscapularis, and teres major muscles. Concurrently, the elbow remains in a flexed and fixed position with the biceps brachii, brachioradialis, and brachialis muscles. The wrist is initially in a semi-supinated position, with the flexors and pronators undergoing isometric contraction.

Biomechanically, the humerus behaves as a hollow cylindrical structure exposed to a bending moment, axial compression, and torsional strain. The shoulder joint of an athlete in a defensive position attempts to resist the external rotational force generated by the opponent’s attack, thereby increasing torsional stress along the humeral shaft and predisposing the bone to fracture [2].

Previous studies in the literature investigating susceptibility to arm wrestling–related fractures have examined various predisposing factors, including sex, the side of the affected arm, adequacy of muscle warm-up, anabolic steroid use, and alcohol consumption [2, 3, 14, 21]. In contrast, the present study focused on the relationship between body position and fracture occurrence using detailed video analysis.

In the attack position, concentric muscle contractions occur around the shoulder joint, causing progressive internal rotation. Additionally, pronators and flexors undergo concentric contractions. In the defense position, there is eccentric contraction of the internal rotators, flexors, and pronators surrounding the shoulder. In the majority of cases analyzed (62.2%), the athlete was in a defensive position, indicating that eccentric contraction in the defense position may represent a potential risk factor for humeral fracture development.

Previous studies have reported that torsional loads generated by movement alone may be sufficient for fracture development [14]. Similarly, the present study demonstrated a significant positive association between trunk rotation and fracture occurrence. Furthermore, in 81.1% of the athletes with fractures, the hand, elbow, and shoulder were not aligned on the same axis, indicating a strong relationship between axis disruption and fracture occurrence. This misalignment, which may occur with trunk rotation or when the wrist is positioned posterior to the shoulder, may expose the arm to increased torsional loading. These findings highlight the importance of maintaining proper upper limb alignment during arm wrestling.

We found that the trunk position, whether in flexion or extension, was not significantly associated with fracture occurrence. This finding can be attributed to the fact that the trunk in a flexed or extended position does not cause any changes in the hand-elbow-shoulder axis. Similarly, we did not find any significant relationship between wrist position and fracture development. The lack of a significant association between most positional variables and match duration or time spent in the fracture zone suggests that temporal factors alone may not adequately explain fracture occurrence.

Video analysis studies have become increasingly popular in recent years because they provide a more detailed assessment of athletes’ movement patterns. Such analyses have been widely utilized to investigate various sports-related injuries, including those involving the ankle, anterior cruciate ligament, hamstring, achilles tendon, and adductor longus [22–30]. These studies, conducted across multiple sports such as football, basketball, handball, American football, and judo, provide valuable insights that can guide athletes and coaches in injury prevention and health protection strategies.

It is well established that athletes are at risk of sustaining humeral fractures during arm wrestling. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the underlying injury mechanisms is essential for developing safe training practices and preventive strategies for this sport. Studies analyzing the relationship between body position and humeral fracture development during arm wrestling are limited.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis was limited to humeral fractures captured in publicly available video recordings; therefore, injuries that were not recorded or shared online were not included. Second, the athletes’ competitive level and experience were unknown, precluding analysis of participant-related factors. Third, information regarding treatment methods and return-to-sport outcomes was not available; however, this was not within the primary scope of the study, which focused on positional risk factors at the moment of injury. Additionally, variations in video quality, camera angle, and resolution may have affected the accuracy of positional assessments. Furthermore, potential confounding factors such as differences in competitor strength, experience level, fatigue, warm-up status, and possible alcohol or anabolic steroid use could not be assessed due to the observational design, and the study may also be subject to observer and selection bias. Due to the dynamic nature of the YouTube algorithm, search results may vary depending on region, language, and personalization; therefore, the search strategy may not be fully reproducible.

Conclusions

High-risk movement patterns, particularly trunk rotation and disruption of the hand–elbow–shoulder axis, were associated with increased fracture occurrence. Most fractures occurred in defensive positions, suggesting that this posture may represent a high-risk scenario. Increased awareness of high-risk body positions may help athletes adopt safer techniques and reduce the risk of injury during arm wrestling.
