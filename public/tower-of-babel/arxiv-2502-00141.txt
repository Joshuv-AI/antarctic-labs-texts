Bianchi Modular Forms over Imaginary Quadratic Fields with arbitrary class group Thanks: Last updated: August 24, 2026
John Cremona, Kalani Thalagoda, Dan Yasaki

Abstract
. Let K K be an imaginary quadratic field and let 𝒪 K \mathcal{O}_{K} be its ring of integers. For an integral ideal 𝔫 \mathfrak{n} of 𝒪 K \mathcal{O}_{K} , let Γ 0 ​ ( 𝔫 ) \Gamma_{0}({\mathfrak{n}}) be the congruence subgroup of level 𝔫 {\mathfrak{n}} consisting of matrices in GL 2 ⁡ ( 𝒪 K ) \operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K}) that are upper triangular mod 𝔫 {\mathfrak{n}} . In this paper, we discuss techniques to compute the space of Bianchi modular forms of level Γ 0 ​ ( 𝔫 ) \Gamma_{0}({\mathfrak{n}}) as a Hecke module in the case where K K has arbitrary class group. Our algorithms and computations extend and complement that carried out for fields of class number 1 1 , 2 2 , and 3 3 by the first author, and by his students Bygott and Lingham in unpublished theses. We also give an overview of the results obtained for a wide range of imaginary quadratic fields, which are tabulated in the L-functions and modular forms database ( LMFDB ).

Introduction

The goal of this paper is to study Bianchi modular forms over arbitrary imaginary quadratic fields from a computational perspective. We describe an algorithm, and two independent implementations, to compute Bianchi modular forms and their Hecke eigensystems over general imaginary quadratic fields, extending the computations done by the first author and several of his students

The aforementioned work exploits the connection between Bianchi modular forms and the homology of certain quotients of the hyperbolic
3
-space
\mathbb{H}_{3}

We note that while both methods involve first finding a tessellation of

For Hecke operator computations, the second method uses the reduction theory introduced by Gunnells

Using the two methods just described, we have two independent implementations of algorithms to compute spaces of Bianchi modular forms, and the action of the Hecke algebra on them, for arbitrary imaginary quadratic fields; in both cases there is a precomputation stage which only needs to be carried out once for each field (computing the tessellation data), then the rational homology

Remark 2.1
.

An newform can admit at most one self-twist, even when the class group admits more than one quadratic character. This follows from the existence of Galois representations attached to such forms. (It is known that these Bianchi modular forms have associated

\ell
-adic Galois representations
\rho_{F,\ell}
. These were first
constructed by Taylor
et al.
in
[
HST93
]
,

[
Tay94
]
with subsequent results by Berger and Harcos
in
[
BH07
]
.) The reason (as explained to the first author by Frank Calegari) is essentially that if a newform had two distinct self-twists, then the associated projective Galois representation would have finite image, contradicting known facts about these representations.

A fundamental result of Kurčanov
[
Kur78
, Theorem 1]
is that the complex cohomology (and hence also the complex homology) of the quotient space
\widetilde{X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})}
is isomorphic to
S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur}}
, the space of all cuspidal Bianchi modular forms on
\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
with unramified character. More precisely, integration of harmonic differentials along closed paths induces a perfect dual pairing between these two complex vector spaces, which respects the Hecke action on both sides. Using the Hecke operators
T_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}}
, we can cut out a subspace of this homology space whose complexification is isomorphic to the space of Bianchi modular forms with trivial, or any other unramified, character.

For computational purposes, it is more convenient to compute only the homology of the principal part of the quotient space, that is,
H_{1}(X_{0}(\mathfrak{n}),\mathbb{Q})=H_{1}(\mathbb{H}_{3}^{*}/\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n}),\mathbb{Q})
. On this smaller principal space, the full Hecke algebra
\mathbb{T}
does not act, as an operator
T\in\mathbb{T}_{c}
maps
H_{1}(X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})^{(c^{\prime})},\mathbb{Q})
to
H_{1}(X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})^{(c^{\prime}c^{-1})},\mathbb{Q})
. Only the principal subalgebra
\mathbb{T}_{1}
acts on the principal homology
H_{1}(X_{0}(\mathfrak{n}),\mathbb{Q})
itself. The principal operators include
T_{\mathfrak{a}}
for principal ideals
\mathfrak{a}
coprime to
\mathfrak{n}
and
T_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}}
for
\mathfrak{a}
coprime to
\mathfrak{n}
with
\mathfrak{a}^{2}
principal, and more generally
T_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}}T_{\mathfrak{b}}
where
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{b}
are coprime to
\mathfrak{n}
and
\mathfrak{a}^{2}\mathfrak{b}
is principal. For explicit formulas for these, we refer to the first author’s paper
[
Cre26
, §4]
, which builds on special cases developed by Aranes
[
Ara10
]
, Bygott
[
Byg98
]
, Lingham
[
Lin05
]
, and Whitley
[
Whi90
]
. The approach used there is to view Bianchi modular forms as functions on

2.1.1.
Atkin-Lehner operators

As well as the Hecke operators
T_{\mathfrak{a}}
and
T_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}}
, we also have Atkin-Lehner operators
W_{\mathfrak{q}}
operating on
S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur}}
for each
\mathfrak{q}\mid\mathfrak{n}
such that
\mathfrak{q}
and
\mathfrak{q}^{-1}\mathfrak{n}
are coprime. Such divisors of the level are called
exact divisors
, and we write
\mathfrak{q}\mid\mid\mathfrak{n}
. In general, we have
W_{\mathfrak{q}}^{2}=T_{\mathfrak{q},\mathfrak{q}}
(by
[
Cre26

), so their restrictions to the trivial subspace

they are (as in the classical case) involutions, forming an elementary abelian
2
-group of rank equal to the number of prime divisors of
\mathfrak{n}
. This group is generated by the
W_{\mathfrak{q}}
for
\mathfrak{q}
an exact prime power divisor of
\mathfrak{n}
. Also restricting to
S(\mathfrak{n})
, Atkin-Lehner operators commute with Hecke operators
T_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}}
and
T_{\mathfrak{a}}
for
\mathfrak{a}
coprime to
\mathfrak{n}
by
[
Cre26
, Prop.3.5]
, so take eigenforms in
S(\mathfrak{n})
to eigenforms; in particular, every eigenform for the Hecke algebra
\mathbb{T}
with trivial character is also an eigenform for each Atkin-Lehner operator
W_{\mathfrak{q}}
with eigenvalue
\pm 1
. Twisting takes eigenforms to eigenforms; as with the
T_{\mathfrak{a}}
operators, twisting an eigenform with trivial character by an unramified quadratic character
\psi
multiplies the
W_{\mathfrak{q}}
-eigenvalue by
\psi(\mathfrak{q})
.

We will not consider here the action of Atkin-Lehner operators on forms with nontrivial character, where in general (as in the case of classical modular forms, see
[
AL78
]
) they map eigenforms to different eigenforms, and one is led to defining their “pseudo-eigenvalues” instead of eigenvalues.

Proposition 2.1
.

For each integral ideal
\mathfrak{n}

C(\mathfrak{n})
.

Proof.

Suppose that

. Since

In our algorithm, for each level
\mathfrak{n}
we determine the set

of “eligible” self-twist characters for eigenforms at level

As with Hecke operators, the Atkin-Lehner operator

is principal. In order to compute all Atkin-Lehner eigenvalues, we will also use the principal operators
T_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}}W_{\mathfrak{q}}
(when

is principal), and

(when

Example 2.2
.

2.1.2.
Newforms and oldforms

Let
\mathfrak{m}
and
\mathfrak{n}
be two levels, with
\mathfrak{m}\mid\mathfrak{n}
,
\mathfrak{m}\not=\mathfrak{n}
. For each ideal divisor
\mathfrak{d}
of
\mathfrak{m}^{-1}\mathfrak{n}
, there is an operator
A_{\mathfrak{d}}\colon S(\mathfrak{m})^{\text{ur}}\to S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur}}
, which commutes with the Hecke action of Hecke operators
T_{\mathfrak{a}}
(for
\mathfrak{a}
coprime to
\mathfrak{n}
) and
T_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}}
(see
[
Cre26
, §4.4]
). These are the analogues for adelic Bianchi modular forms of the classical maps taking a function
f(\tau)
on the upper half-plane to
f(d\tau)
, where
d
is a positive divisor of the quotient of two levels. These maps take eigenforms to eigenforms, with the same eigenvalues away from the level. The images of an eigenform
G\in S(\mathfrak{m})^{\text{ur}}
for distinct divisors
\mathfrak{d}
are linearly independent, and hence span a subspace of
S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur}}
called the
oldclass
associated to
G
, whose dimension is therefore equal to
\sigma_{0}(\mathfrak{m}^{-1}\mathfrak{n})
, where
\sigma_{0}(\mathfrak{a})
denotes the number of ideal divisors of an integral ideal
\mathfrak{a}
. The
oldspace
at level
\mathfrak{n}
is the sum of all the oldclasses as
\mathfrak{m}
ranges over all levels
\mathfrak{m}\mid\mathfrak{n}
,
\mathfrak{m}\not=\mathfrak{n}
. The
newspace

S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur},\text{new}}
is the orthogonal complement of the oldspace with respect to an inner product on
S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur}}
which generalises the classical Peterssen inner product (for a definition of this inner product for adelic Bianchi modular forms, see Miyake
[
Miy71
]
). Recall that we define a newform to be an eigenform in the new subspace. We summarise here some key facts from Miyake’s paper, noting that Miyake deals with forms with arbitrary character (of conductor not dividing the level), and defines an eigenform at level
\mathfrak{n}
to be a form which is an eigenvector for all
T_{\mathfrak{p}}
and also for all the adjoint operators
T_{\mathfrak{p}}^{*}
; for the subspace
S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur}}
, this is equivalent to being an eigenvector for all
T_{\mathfrak{p}}
and all
T_{\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{p}}
for
\mathfrak{p}\nmid\mathfrak{n}
, since
T_{\mathfrak{a}}^{*}=T_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}T_{\mathfrak{a}}
(see
[
Cre26
, §3.4.2]
).

Theorem 2.3
.

(1)

The Hecke algebra
\mathbb{T}
is commutative; its restriction to
S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur},\text{new}}
is semisimple. Each Hecke operator is diagonalisable. Hence
S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur}}
has a basis of eigenforms, and
S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur},new}
has a basis of newforms (see
[
Miy71
, Theorem 1]
).

(2)

For every eigenform
F\in S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur}}
, there is a newform
G\in S(\mathfrak{m})^{\text{ur},\text{new}}
for some level
\mathfrak{m}\mid\mathfrak{n}
with the same Hecke eigenvalues
T_{\mathfrak{p}}
for
\mathfrak{p}\nmid\mathfrak{n}
as
F
, so that either
F
is itself a newform, or, if
\mathfrak{m}\not=\mathfrak{n}
,
F
is in the oldclass associated to
G
(see
[
Miy71
, Corollary to Theorem 1]
).

(3)

If newforms
F_{j}\in S(\mathfrak{n}_{j})^{\text{ur},\text{new}}
for
j=1,2
have the same eigenvalues for
T_{\mathfrak{p}}
for almost all primes
\mathfrak{p}
, then
\mathfrak{n}_{1}=\mathfrak{n}_{2}
and
F_{1}=F_{2}
(see
[
Miy71
, Theorem 2]
).

Hence the multiplicity at level
\mathfrak{n}
of the eigensystem associated to an eigenform
G\in S(\mathfrak{m})^{\text{ur},\text{new}}
is
\sigma_{0}(\mathfrak{m}^{-1}\mathfrak{n})
.
This leads to an expression for the dimensions of the newspaces
S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur},\text{new}}
in terms of the dimensions of the full spaces
S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur}}
:

(1)

\dim S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur},\text{new}}=\dim S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur}}-\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\mid\mathfrak{n},\mathfrak{m}\not=\mathfrak{n}}\sigma_{0}(\mathfrak{m}^{-1}\mathfrak{n})\dim S(\mathfrak{m})^{\text{ur},\text{new}}.

When we consider the dimensions of the projections from
S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur}}
to the principal component, the corresponding formula requires some adjustment to take into account eigenforms with self-twist; this phenomenon was first remarked in Bygott’s thesis
[
Byg98
]
. The reason is as follows. The maps
A_{\mathfrak{d}}
have class
[\mathfrak{d}]
, in the sense that if
G\in S(\mathfrak{m})^{\text{ur}}
and
A_{\mathfrak{d}}(G)=F\in S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur}}
, (where
\mathfrak{m}\mid\mathfrak{n}
and
\mathfrak{d}\mid\mathfrak{m}^{-1}\mathfrak{n}
), writing
F=\sum_{c}F^{(c)}
and
G=\sum_{c}G^{(c)}
we have
A_{\mathfrak{d}}(G^{(c)})=F^{([\mathfrak{d}]c)}
. In most cases, a new eigenform
G
will have all components
G^{(c)}
non-zero (see Proposition
3.6
below). If this were always the case, then a formula similar to (
1
) would still hold for the dimensions of the principal components. However, when
G
is a new eigenform with self-twist by the unramified quadratic character
\psi
, its components
G^{(c)}
satisfy
G^{(c)}\not=0
if and only if
\psi(c)=+1
; in particular, the principal component
G^{(1)}
is nonzero. Now, when applying the operator
A_{\mathfrak{d}}
, we see (Corollary
3.7
) that the principal component of
A_{\mathfrak{d}}(G)
is nonzero if and only if
\psi(\mathfrak{d})=+1
. Hence, the contribution of
G
to the principal oldspace dimension at level
\mathfrak{n}
is not
\sigma_{0}(\mathfrak{m}^{-1}\mathfrak{n})
, the total number of divisors of
\mathfrak{m}^{-1}\mathfrak{n}
, but is instead the possibly smaller number of divisors
\mathfrak{d}
such that
\psi(\mathfrak{d})=+1
. For example, if
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{p}
with
\mathfrak{p}
prime, then we expect the dimension of the oldspace at level
\mathfrak{n}
associated to a new eigenform at level
\mathfrak{m}
to be
2
, the number of ideal divisors of
\mathfrak{p}
, but if
G
admits self-twist by
\psi
and
\psi(\mathfrak{p})=-1
, then the multiplicity is only
1
. This observation can be used to prove that a newform admits a self-twist, by computing the dimension of the associated oldspace at a suitably chosen higher level.

In summary:

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
G\in S(\mathfrak{m})^{\text{ur},\text{new}}
be a newform, let
\mathfrak{n}
be a multiple of
\mathfrak{m}
, and
V\subseteq S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur}}
be the oldclass associated to
G
at level
\mathfrak{n}
, and let
V^{(1)}
be the projection of
V
to the principal component. Then

(1)

\dim V=\sigma_{0}(\mathfrak{m}^{-1}\mathfrak{n})=\sum_{\mathfrak{d}\mid\mathfrak{m}^{-1}\mathfrak{n}}1
.

(2)

(a)

If
G
admits no self-twist then
\dim V^{(1)}=\dim V
.

(b)

If
G
admits self-twist by the character
\psi
then

\dim V^{(1)}=\#\{\mathfrak{d}\mid\mathfrak{m}^{-1}\mathfrak{n}\mid\psi(\mathfrak{d})=+1\}=\sum_{\mathfrak{d}\mid\mathfrak{m}^{-1}\mathfrak{n}}(1+\psi(\mathfrak{d}))/2.

This makes it impossible to give a simple formula such as (
1
) for the new dimensions of the principal components in general, when
h_{K}
is even. It is only possible in certain special cases, for example when
\psi(\mathfrak{p})=+1
for all unramified quadratic characters
\psi
and all primes
\mathfrak{p}\mid\mathfrak{n}
. Otherwise, we can only compute the dimension of the principal component of
S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur},\text{new}}
when we know not just the dimensions
\dim S(\mathfrak{m})^{\text{ur},\text{new}}
for proper divisors
\mathfrak{m}
(that is, the total number of newforms at level
\mathfrak{m}
), but also, in each case, the number of newforms which have self-twist by each unramified quadratic character
\psi
.

3.
Eigenforms and Eigensystems

Our implementations rely on the fact that knowing only the principal homology, and the action on this of principal Hecke operators, suffices to determine the complete set of Hecke eigensystems for the full Hecke algebra
\mathbb{T}
acting on the full space with all
h_{K}
components. That this is the case (and even in a more general setting than just Bianchi modular forms) is a main result of
[
Cre26
]
:

Theorem 3.1

(Theorem 4.1 of
[
Cre26
]
)
.

Two eigenvalue systems
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
of the same level

\mathfrak{n}
have the same restriction to the subalgebra
\mathbb{T}_{1}
of principal
operators if and only if
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{1}\otimes\psi
for some unramified
character
\psi
(that is, for some character of the ideal class group
\operatorname{Cl}_{K}
).
The characters
\chi_{1}
,
\chi_{2}
of
\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}

satisfy
\chi_{2}=\chi_{1}\psi^{2}
, and hence
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}

have the same character if any only if
\psi
is quadratic.

Hence, knowledge of the eigenvalues of only the principal operators is enough, in principle, to characterize a full eigensystem, up to unramified twist. There remains the algorithmic question of actually computing the eigenvalues of non-principal operators (for one, and hence all, of this family of twists) from those of the principal operators. A solution to this was sketched in
[
Cre26
, Remark 6]
, and we give further details here, as this is an important part of our algorithm. We express the algorithm in terms of eigensystems, but it can also be seen as a method to recover an eigenform (up to twist) from its principal component.

This process is very much simpler over fields with odd class number, and we describe this first, before describing the general situation, where the genus group
\operatorname{Cl}_{K}/\operatorname{Cl}_{K}^{2}
plays a role. This approach for fields of odd class number was first used in Lingham’s thesis
[
Lin05
]
.

The odd class number case

Suppose that the class number
h_{K}
is odd. Then every element of the class group is a square, every unramified character is a square, and there are no unramified quadratic characters. It follows that each space
S(\mathfrak{n},\chi)
is isomorphic to
S(\mathfrak{n})
; in particular,
\dim S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur}}=h_{K}\dim S(\mathfrak{n})
. Taking
\psi
to be a character with
\psi^{2}=\chi
, twisting by
\psi
gives an isomorphism
S(\mathfrak{n})\to S(\mathfrak{n},\chi)
. In other words, every eigenform in
S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur}}
may be obtained from an eigenform with trivial character by twisting. Secondly, when the ideal class
c
is a square, then the twisted group
\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})^{(c)}
is actually conjugate by a matrix
M\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}(K)
to the principal group
\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
. (Essentially,
M
is a matrix inducing the isomorphism
\mathfrak{a}^{2}\oplus\mathcal{O}_{K}\cong\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{a}
for a suitable ideal
\mathfrak{a}
with
c=[\mathfrak{a}]^{2}
.) It follows that
M
induces an isomorphism
H_{1}(X_{0}(\mathfrak{n})^{(c)},\mathbb{Q})\cong H_{1}(X_{0}(\mathfrak{n}),\mathbb{Q})
. Hence all these spaces have the same dimension (as
\mathbb{Q}
-vector spaces), and, by Kurčanov’s theorem, this dimension is equal to
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}S(\mathfrak{n})
. Moreover, we can compute the action of every
T_{\mathfrak{b}}
on
H_{1}(X_{0}(\mathfrak{n}),\mathbb{Q})
(for
\mathfrak{b}
coprime to
\mathfrak{n}
) by computing that of the principal operator
T_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}}T_{\mathfrak{b}}
, where
\mathfrak{a}
is an ideal coprime to
\mathfrak{n}
such that
\mathfrak{a}^{2}\mathfrak{b}
is principal, using the formula for such operators given in
[
Cre26
]
. Then the eigenvalues of
T_{\mathfrak{b}}
on
S(\mathfrak{n})
may be determined, using the fact that the only principal operator of the form
T_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}}
is the identity. On
H_{1}(X_{0}(\mathfrak{n}),\mathbb{Q})
, therefore, the eigenvalue of
T_{\mathfrak{b}}
(on the full eigensystem with trivial character) is the same as that of the principal operator
T_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}}T_{\mathfrak{b}}
.

3.1.
Hecke eigensystems

Hecke operators commute and are diagonalizable, and thus are simultaneously diagonalizable. Hence the space
S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur}}
has a basis consisting of eigenforms for all
T\in\mathbb{T}
. To each such eigenform we associate a
Hecke eigensystem
. As already noted, the operator
T_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}}
only depends on the ideal class
[\mathfrak{a}]
, so
T_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}}
is the identity when
\mathfrak{a}
is principal.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
F\in S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur}}
be an eigenform, that is, a simultaneous eigenvector for all operators in the Hecke algebra
\mathbb{T}
. The function
\lambda_{F}\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{C}
, defined by

T(F)=\lambda_{F}(T)F

for each
T\in\mathbb{T}
, is a ring homomorphism called the
Hecke eigensystem
associated to
F
.
For each integral ideal
\mathfrak{a}
coprime to the level
\mathfrak{n}
, we define
\alpha_{F}(\mathfrak{a})=\lambda_{F}(T_{\mathfrak{a}})
and
\chi_{F}(\mathfrak{a})=\lambda_{F}(T_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}})
, so that (by definition)
F\in S(\mathfrak{n},\chi_{F})
, and
\chi_{F}
is constant on ideal classes, so may be identified with a character of the ideal class group. It will be convenient to set
\chi_{F}(\mathfrak{a})=0
for ideals
\mathfrak{a}
which are not coprime to the level.

The
Hecke field
of
F
, denoted
k_{F}
, is the number field generated by the eigenvalues
\lambda(T)
for
T\in\mathbb{T}
. Since the adjoint of
T_{\mathfrak{a}}
is
T_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}T_{\mathfrak{a}}
, the eigenvalues of principal operators
T_{\mathfrak{a}}
are all real, and moreover, if
F\in S(\mathfrak{n})
then the Hecke field is totally real.

Since
\mathbb{T}
is generated by
T_{\mathfrak{a}}
and
T_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}}
for
\mathfrak{a}
coprime to
\mathfrak{n}
, the eigensystem is uniquely determined by the values
\alpha_{F}(\mathfrak{a})
and
\chi_{F}(\mathfrak{a})
; to emphasize this we will also write the eigensystem
\lambda_{F}
associated to
F
as a pair
\lambda_{F}=(\alpha_{F},\chi_{F})
. The Hecke field is generated by
\alpha_{F}(\mathfrak{a})
and
\lambda_{F}(\mathfrak{a})
for
\mathfrak{a}
coprime to
\mathfrak{n}
; so, in particular, if
F\in S(\mathfrak{n},\chi)
then
k_{F}
contains the cyclotomic field
\mathbb{Q}(\chi)
.

While the functions
\lambda_{F}
and
\chi_{F}
are totally multiplicative, the function
\alpha_{F}
is only multiplicative.

Theorem 3.3
.

The eigensystem
\lambda_{F}=(\alpha_{F},\chi_{F})
associated to a Hecke eigenform
F\in S(\mathfrak{n})^{\text{ur}}
satisfies the following:

•

If
\mathfrak{a}
,
\mathfrak{b}
are integral ideals, then

\chi_{F}(\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b})=\chi_{F}(\mathfrak{a})\chi_{F}(\mathfrak{b}).

•

If
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{b}
are coprime integral ideals, then

\alpha_{F}(\mathfrak{a})\alpha_{F}(\mathfrak{b})=\alpha_{F}(\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}).

•

If
\mathfrak{p}
is a prime ideal, then for all
n\geq 1
we have

\alpha_{F}(\mathfrak{p}^{n})\alpha_{F}(\mathfrak{p})=\alpha_{F}(\mathfrak{p}^{n+1})+\n(\mathfrak{p})\alpha_{F}(\mathfrak{p}^{n-1})\chi_{F}(\mathfrak{p}).

(Here we have used the convention that
\chi_{F}(\mathfrak{p})=0
if
\mathfrak{p}\mid\mathfrak{n}
.)

Since for a normalised eigenform
F
the
\mathfrak{a}
-Fourier coefficient is equal to the eigenvalue
\alpha_{F}(\mathfrak{a})
, it follows that these coefficients satisfy the same multiplicative relations as above.

Definition 3.4
.

For each character
\psi
of the class group, and each eigensystem
\lambda:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{C}
, the
twist

\lambda\otimes\psi
is defined by

•

(\lambda\otimes\psi)(T_{\mathfrak{a}})=\psi([\mathfrak{a}])\lambda(T_{\mathfrak{a}})
;

•

(\lambda\otimes\psi)(T_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}})=\psi^{2}([\mathfrak{a}])\lambda(T_{\mathfrak{a}})
.

Proposition 3.5
.

If
\lambda=(\lambda_{F},\chi_{F})
is the eigensystem associated to the newform
F\in S(\mathfrak{n},\chi_{F})
, the twisted form
F\otimes\psi
is also an eigenform, with eigensystem
\lambda\otimes\psi=(\lambda_{F}\psi,\chi_{F}\psi^{2})=(\lambda_{F\otimes\psi},\chi_{F\otimes\psi})
.

Proof.

Write
F=\sum_{c}F^{(c)}
, so that
F\otimes\psi=\sum_{c}\psi(c)F^{(c)}
.

First consider
T=T_{\mathfrak{a}}
. This maps
F^{(c)}
to the
([\mathfrak{a}]^{-1}c)
-component of
T(F)
, which is
\lambda_{F}(\mathfrak{a})F^{([\mathfrak{a}]^{-1}c)}
. Hence

\displaystyle T(F\otimes\psi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c}\psi(c)T(F^{(c)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{c}\psi(c)\lambda_{F}(\mathfrak{a})F^{([\mathfrak{a}]^{-1}c)}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{F}(\mathfrak{a})\psi([\mathfrak{a}])\sum_{c}\psi([\mathfrak{a}]^{-1}c)F^{([\mathfrak{a}]^{-1}c)}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{F}(\mathfrak{a})\psi([\mathfrak{a}])\sum_{c}\psi(c)F^{(c)}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{F}(\mathfrak{a})\psi([\mathfrak{a}])(F\otimes\psi).

The case of
T=T_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}}
is similar, using
T_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}}(F^{(c)})=\chi_{F}(\mathfrak{a})F^{([\mathfrak{a}]^{-2}c)}
.
∎

We call the set of Hecke eigensystems obtained by twisting a system
(\lambda,\chi)
by the characters of the class group the
twist orbit
of
(\lambda,\chi)
, and the set of characters in the twist orbit the
character orbit
of the Hecke eigensystem. The character orbit is one complete coset of the subgroup of squares of class group characters, so has size
|\operatorname{Cl}_{K}^{2}|
; the size of the twist orbit is usually equal to
h_{K}
, but if the eigensystem admits self-twist by a quadratic character
\psi
, the size of the twist orbit is only
h_{K}/2
. In this self-twist case, for each
\mathfrak{a}
, we have
\lambda(\mathfrak{a})\psi(\mathfrak{a})=\lambda(\mathfrak{a})
, and hence
\lambda(\mathfrak{a})=0
for all ideals
\mathfrak{a}
in half of the ideal classes, namely those on which
\psi
takes the value
-1
. We use this to help identify Hecke eigensystems with self-twist, for if
\alpha_{F}(\mathfrak{a})\not=0
then only the possible self-twist characters
\psi
are those with
\psi(\mathfrak{a})=+1
, if any.

The set of ideal classes
c
for which there exists a Hecke operator
T\in\mathbb{T}_{c}
with
\lambda(T)\not=0
clearly forms a subgroup of
\operatorname{Cl}_{K}
, which always contains
\operatorname{Cl}_{K}^{2}
since
\lambda(T_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}})=\chi(\mathfrak{a})\not=0
for all
\mathfrak{a}
coprime to
\mathfrak{n}
. Denote this subgroup by
H_{0}(\lambda)
, or by
H_{0}(F)
when
\lambda=\lambda_{F}
. Usually (and always when
h_{K}
is odd), this subgroup is the whole of
\operatorname{Cl}_{K}
, but if
\lambda
admits self-twist by the unramified quadratic character
\psi
, we have
H_{0}(\lambda)=\ker\psi
, a subgroup of index
2
. Recall from Proposition
2.1
that for each level
\mathfrak{n}
, the set
C(\mathfrak{n})
of eligible self-twist characters may be a proper subset of the set of all unramified quadratic characters. If, for a given eigensystem, there are ideals
\mathfrak{a}
(coprime to the level) whose ideal classes generate
\operatorname{Cl}_{K}/\operatorname{Cl}_{K}^{2}
, such that
\alpha(\mathfrak{a})\not=0
, then we know that the eigensystem admits no self-twist.

On the other hand, determining rigorously that an eigensystem does admit a self-twist (when
C(\mathfrak{n})\not=\emptyset
) is more problematical algorithmically, since even if we find that for some
\psi\in C(\mathfrak{n})
,
\lambda(T_{\mathfrak{p}})=0
for many primes
\mathfrak{p}
such that
\psi([\mathfrak{p}])=-1
, this does not—without further work—imply that
\lambda(T_{\mathfrak{p}})=0
for all such primes. One method to do this is to use oldform multiplicities, using the observation before Proposition
2.4
; however, this involves working at higher levels, so is inconvenient in practice (and we have not implemented this).

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
F=\sum_{c}F^{(c)}\in S(\mathfrak{n},\chi)^{\text{new}}
be a newform.

(1)

If
F
does not admit self-twist, then every component
F^{(c)}
is nonzero.

(2)

If
F
admits self-twist by the quadratic character
\psi
, then

3.3.
Recovering a full eigensystem from a homological eigensystem

We now describe a procedure to compute one complete Hecke eigensystem
\lambda=(\alpha,\chi)
which matches a given homological eigensystem
\lambda_{f}
at level
\mathfrak{n}
. The procedure has the following form.

Input:

For any principal operator
T
, the eigenvalue
\lambda_{f}(T)
.

Output:

(1)

For any ideal
\mathfrak{a}
coprime to
\mathfrak{n}

\chi(\mathfrak{a})

(2)

For any prime
\mathfrak{p}\nmid\mathfrak{n}

\alpha(\mathfrak{p})

(3)

When

\mathfrak{q}\mid\mid\mathfrak{n}

\varepsilon(\mathfrak{q})

Since we can only handle a finite amount of data in practice, we regard the input as an oracle which can supply the eigenvalue

By Theorem

, the eigensystem

is only be determined up to unramified twist, but if we find one such eigensystem then we can find the others by twisting, so it suffices to find one. In particular, when the restriction of
\chi
to
\operatorname{Cl}_{K}[2]
is trivial, so that

is a square, we may choose

\chi

\alpha(\mathfrak{p})
for primes
\mathfrak{p}
which do not divide the level. Recall that the quotient

of

we call its

3.3.1.
Outline of the procedure

(1)

Determine possible candidates for the character
\chi

(2)

Determine the values
\alpha(\mathfrak{p})
for (a finite set of) prime ideals

(a)

\alpha(\mathfrak{p})
for principal primes (those with trivial ideal class);

(b)

\alpha(\mathfrak{p})
for primes whose ideal class is square (those whose genus class is trivial);

(c)

\alpha(\mathfrak{p})^{2}
for primes in non-square classes (those whose genus class is non-trivial);

(d)

with
\mathfrak{p}

(3)

If
\chi

for prime powers

The same procedure works in the case of odd class number, in which case steps 2(c,d) are not needed as the genus group is trivial.

There are two different Galois actions on the set of new Hecke eigenforms for the field

Secondly, since

\tau\circ\lambda
is
\tau\circ\chi
. The multiplicative Hecke relations for
\lambda=(\alpha,\chi)
and
\lambda\circ\psi=(\psi\alpha,\psi^{2}\chi)
give

\alpha(\mathfrak{p})^{2}-\alpha(\mathfrak{p}^{2})=\chi(\mathfrak{p})\n(\mathfrak{p})

and

\psi(\mathfrak{p})^{2}\alpha(\mathfrak{p})^{2}-\psi(\mathfrak{p}^{2})\alpha(\mathfrak{p}^{2})=\psi(\mathfrak{p})^{2}\chi(\mathfrak{p})\n(\mathfrak{p}).

Applying
\tau
to the first equation and using the hypothesis gives

\psi(\mathfrak{p})^{2}\alpha(\mathfrak{p})^{2}-\psi(\mathfrak{p})^{2}\alpha(\mathfrak{p}^{2})=\tau(\chi(\mathfrak{p}))\n(\mathfrak{p}).

Comparing the right-hand sides gives the result.
∎

3.5.
The Hecke field and principal subfield

We end this section with some remarks on the relation between the Hecke field
k_{F}
and its subfield, the principal Hecke field
k_{f}
. As already observed,
k_{f}
is totally real, and
k_{F}
contains
\mathbb{Q}(\chi)
where
\chi
is the character of
F
.

First consider the case where
F
, and hence also
f
, has trivial character. From our algorithmic procedure we see that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{F}(T)
lie in the principal Hecke field
k_{f}
for all operators
T
in square classes, not just those which are principal. (This is also true when
F
has quadratic character, as it only uses the fact that the character values are rational.) When the class number is odd, therefore, we have
k_{F}=k_{f}
. In general, for
T
whose genus class is non-trivial, we only have
\lambda_{F}(T)^{2}\in k_{f}
, where the class of
\lambda_{F}(T)^{2}
in
k_{f}^{*}/(k_{f}^{*})^{2}
only depends on the genus class of
T
; this is because if
T^{\prime}
has the same genus class as
T
then
TT^{\prime}
has trivial genus class, so
\lambda(TT^{\prime})\in k_{f}
. Hence
k_{F}=k_{f}(\sqrt{r_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{r_{m}})
for some
m\geq 0
, where
r_{1},\dots,r_{m}
are multiplicatively independent modulo squares, and where
m
is at most the
2
-rank of the class group; if
F
admits a self-twist then
m
is at most one less than this. For example, when the class group has cyclic
2
-primary part,
m\in\{0,1\}
; that is, either
k_{F}=k_{f}
or
k_{F}=k_{f}(\sqrt{r})
for some
r\in k_{f}^{*}
which is not a square in
k_{f}
. We will see examples of both these possibilities with
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-17})
in the next section.

When the class number is odd, the character of
f
is necessarily trivial, so that one lift, say
F_{0}
, of the homological eigensystem also has trivial character with
k_{F_{0}}=k_{f}
, while all the other lifts are twists
F=F\otimes\chi
, so that
k_{F}=k_{f}(\chi)
, the extension of
k_{f}
obtained by adjoining the values of the twisting character
\chi
.

The case where
f
has non-trivial character is more complicated; rather than attempt a general description here, we will only give details in the case where the class group is cyclic of order
4
, as is relevant for the examples in the next section.

4.
Results and Tables for

For this section, we fix the imaginary quadratic field

) to refer to integral ideals of

The class group

4.1.
Summary of scope

There are 305 ideals
\mathfrak{n}
of norm less than or equal to 200. Of these, 244 have nontrivial homology

, and hence nontrivial spaces

4.2.
Tessellations

To compute the homology

, we require a tessellation of

with a
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
action. Here we summarize details about two such tessellations, as we use in our two independent implementations.

The first tessellation comes from the theory of perfect Hermitian forms and their minimal vectors. For our field, there are
12
perfect forms up to
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
-equivalence. The resulting tessellation of

by ideal polyhedra has
12

\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
-orbits of polyhedra, consisting of
5
tetrahedra,
2
triangular prisms,
3
square pyramids, one hexagonal cap, and one truncated tetrahedron. The
1
-homology is generated by the directed edges of these; in this case, we have
18

\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
-orbits of edges. The faces of the polyhedra give homology relations among these generators. In this case, there are
29

\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
-orbits of faces, including
22
triangles,
6
squares, and one hexagon, giving
3
-,
4
-, and
6
-term relations respectively. Note that for a given level, we may not need all of the
\Gamma_{0}(\mathfrak{n})
-classes of these edges and faces to be included in our complex. There may be non-orientable edges and faces that come from the existence of orientation-reversing elements in the stabilizer groups of the polyhedra.

The second tessellation was constructed using a method based on Swan’s algorithm. This has
13

\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
-orbits of polyhedra:
6
tetrahedra,
2
cubes,
2
triangular prisms, one hexagonal cap, one truncated tetrahedron, and one square pyramid. There are
14

\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
-orbits of edges which generate the

-homology, and

orbits of faces giving edge-relations, similar to the Voronoi tessellation, in this case consisting of

4.3.
Homological and full eigensystems and Hecke fields over
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-17})

We discuss here in more detail for the field
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-17})
the relation between the principal Hecke field
k_{f}
and the full Hecke fields
k_{F}
of the lifts of a homological newform
f
to full eigenforms
F
.

At each level
\mathfrak{n}
, we decompose the rational new subspace
H
of the homology
H_{1}(X_{0}(\mathfrak{n}),\mathbb{Q})
as a module under the action of the (rational) Hecke algebra. The irreducible submodules of
H
correspond to Galois orbits of homological eigensystems. Under the action of the principal involution operator
T_{\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{p}}
(where
\mathfrak{p}
is any prime in class
c^{2}
coprime to the level),
H
decomposes into two eigenspaces which we denote
H^{+}
and
H^{-}
, with eigenvalue
+1
and
-1
respectively. Each of these may further decompose under the action of the full principal Hecke algebra
\mathbb{T}_{1}
, and each irreducible component of dimension
d
in
H^{+}
or
H^{-}
corresponds to a Galois orbit of
d
homological newforms
f
, each with principal Hecke field
k_{f}
a totally real number field of degree
d
; the Hecke fields of the Galois conjugates are isomorphic (conjugate) number fields.

Consider first the case of an irreducible component of
H^{+}
of dimension
d
, with associated homological eigensystem
f
of dimension
d
and principal Hecke field
k_{f}
, of degree
d
and totally real. This lifts to full eigensystems
F_{j}
for
j=0,1,2,3
: we first lift to
F_{0}
with trivial character
\chi_{0}
and then twist, setting
F_{j}=F_{0}\otimes\chi_{j}
, so that
F_{j}
has character
\chi_{2j}
. That is,
F_{0}
and its quadratic twist
F_{2}
have trivial character
\chi_{0}
, while
F_{1}
and its quadratic twist
F_{3}
have character
\chi_{2}
. In general, these four are distinct, but if
F_{0}
has inner twist then (by definition)
F_{2}=F_{0}
and also
F_{1}=F_{3}
.

As for the Hecke fields, from our general discussion, we know that either
k_{F_{0}}=k_{f}
, or
k_{F_{0}}=k_{f}(\sqrt{r})
for some non-square
r\in k_{f}
. In the first case (which includes the case of self-twist), the lifts of
f
and its Galois conjugates to full eigensystems
F_{0},F_{2}
with characters
\chi_{0}
fall into two Galois orbits (or just one in the self-twist case), each of size
d
. In the second case, these lifts form a single Galois orbit of size
2d
, with full Hecke field
k_{f}(\sqrt{r})
of degree
2d
. Moreover, since the Hecke field of an eigensystem with trivial character is necessarily totally real, we see that the element
r\in k_{f}
is totally positive. The Hecke field of
F_{2}
is
k_{f}(\sqrt{-1})
in the first case, unless there is self-twist, when
k_{F_{2}}=k_{f}
, since the factor of
\sqrt{-1}
only appears as a factor for eigenvalues of operators in classes
c
and
c^{3}
, which are all zero in this case. In the second case, we have
k_{F_{2}}=k_{f}(\sqrt{-r})
; there is one Galois orbit of size
2d
. (Since
r
is totally positive,
-r
is certainly not a square in the totally real field
k_{f}
.) Note that in the second case all the full eigensystems have inner twist by
(\sigma,\chi_{2})
where
\sigma
is the non-trivial automorphism of
\Gal(k_{f}(\sqrt{r})/k_{f})
. Note also that all the eigenvalues are either elements of the principal field
k_{f}
, or products of such elements and either
\sqrt{r}
,
\sqrt{-1}
, or
\sqrt{-r}
.

Now consider the lifts of a
d
-dimensional homological eigensystem
f
associated to an irreducible component of
H^{-}
. Its lifts will have characters
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{3}
, two of each unless they have self-twist. Since these characters are Galois conjugate (switching
i=\sqrt{-1}
to
-i
), we only need consider the lifts with character
\chi_{1}
, as those with character
\chi_{3}
will be Galois conjugates of these, as well as being their twists. Let
F
be one lift to a full eigensystem with character
\chi_{1}
. The eigenvalues for principal operators lie in
k_{f}
. Those of operators in class
c^{2}
are in
k_{f}(i)
, being multiples of elements of
k_{f}
by
i
: for example, if
\mathfrak{p}
is a prime in class
c^{2}
, then taking
\mathfrak{a}
in class
c
, we see that
T_{\mathfrak{p}}T_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}}
is principal, so has eigenvalue in
k_{f}
, and the eigenvalue of
T_{\mathfrak{p}}
is obtained by dividing by
\chi_{1}(\mathfrak{a})=i
. So the full Hecke field certainly contains
k_{f}(i)
. Next, for primes
\mathfrak{p}
in class
c
we see similarly that
\alpha(\mathfrak{p})^{2}=2ri
with
r\in k_{f}
(the factor of
2
is to simplify the formulas which follow), so
\alpha(\mathfrak{p})=\pm\sqrt{2ri}=\pm\sqrt{r}(1+i)
. So as before, we have two cases, according to whether
r
is or is not a square in
k_{f}
. In the first case, the full Hecke field is
k_{f}(i)
, while in the second case it is the biquadratic extension
k_{f}(i,\sqrt{r})
. The lifts with character
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{3}
form two Galois orbits of size
2d
each in the first case, and a single orbit of size
4d
in the second case; in both cases, half of the Galois conjugates in each orbit have character
\chi_{1}
and half
\chi_{2}
. All the eigenvalues
\alpha(\mathfrak{p})
are products of an element of
k_{f}
by either
1
or
i
, or (in the second case)
\sqrt{r}
, or
\sqrt{r}(1+i)
. Examples of both possibilities may be seen at level
\mathfrak{n}=(4)=16.1
: see Example
4.2
below.

Example 4.1
.

The first non-trivial Hecke eigensystem occurs at level
\mathfrak{p}_{2.1}=\langle 2,1+\omega\rangle
. The excerpt from Table
1
corresponding to this level is given below.

\begin{array}[]{c c c c c c }\hline\cr\hline\cr\mathfrak{n}&nd&H^{+}&H^{-}&\chi_{0}&\chi_{1},\chi_{3}\\
\hline\cr 2.1&4&[1]&-&[2]&-\\
\hline\cr\hline\cr\end{array}

At this level, there is only one homological eigenform
f
. Since the homology is one-dimensional, the principal eigenvalues
\lambda_{f}(T)
of
f
are rational, so the principal Hecke field
k_{f}
is
\mathbb{Q}
.

Let
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{p}_{13.1}=\langle 13,3+\omega\rangle
. Then
\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Cl}_{K}^{2}
, and the eigenvalue of the principal Hecke operator
T_{\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{p}}
is
1
. Hence
H=H^{+}
, while
H^{-}=0
. Therefore, the character orbit of
f
is
\{\chi_{0},\chi_{2}\}
. We fix the character to be the trivial character
\chi=\chi_{0}
to identify the full Hecke eigensystem
(\alpha,\chi)
for one newform
F_{0}
.

The full eigensystems
F_{0},F_{2}
in the twist orbit which have trivial character have full Hecke field
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})
while
F_{1},F_{3}
have character
\chi_{2}
and full Hecke field
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-2})
, as indicated by the entry
[2]
in the
\chi_{0}
column.

The first primes not dividing the level are the two primes above 3,
\mathfrak{p}_{3.1}=\langle 3,4+\omega\rangle\in c
and
\mathfrak{p}_{3.2}=\langle 3,2+\omega\rangle\in c^{3}
. The operator
T=T_{\mathfrak{p}_{3.1},\mathfrak{p}_{3.1}}T_{\mathfrak{p}_{3.1}^{2}}
is principal and has eigenvalue
\lambda_{f}(T)=5
, so that
\alpha(\mathfrak{p}_{3.1}^{2})=5
and hence
\alpha(\mathfrak{p}_{3.1})^{2}=5+\chi(\mathfrak{p}_{3.1})\n(\mathfrak{p}_{3.1})=8
. As this is nonzero, we may arbitrarily fix a sign for the square root, taking
\alpha(\mathfrak{p}_{3.1})=2\sqrt{2}
. At this point, we have made two choices and thus determined the full eigensystem
(\alpha,\chi)
uniquely; there is no self-twist, so the twist orbit of
F_{0}
consists of four distinct newforms
F_{i}=F\otimes\chi_{i}
for
0\leq i\leq 3
.

To compute
\alpha(\mathfrak{p}_{3.2})
we compute the principal operator
T=T_{\mathfrak{p}_{3.1}\mathfrak{p}_{3.2}}
, which has eigenvalue
\lambda_{f}(T)=-8
, obtaining
\alpha(\mathfrak{p}_{3.2})=-8/(2\sqrt{2})=-2\sqrt{2}
. Similarly, for all other primes
\mathfrak{p}
in class
c^{3}
we can obtain
\alpha(\mathfrak{p})
from the eigenvalue of the principal operator
T=T_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{p}_{3.1}}
as
\alpha(\mathfrak{p})=\lambda_{f}(T)/\alpha(\mathfrak{p}_{3.1})=\lambda_{f}(T)/(2\sqrt{2})
. For primes
\mathfrak{p}
in class
c
we use the principal operator
T=T_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{p}_{3.2}}
to obtain
\alpha(\mathfrak{p})=\lambda_{f}(T)/\alpha(\mathfrak{p}_{3.2})=\lambda_{f}(T)/(-2\sqrt{2})
. Since all the principal operators have rational eigenvalues, we see that all the non-principal ones have eigenvalues in
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})
; in fact they are all rational multiples of
\sqrt{2}
.

For primes in class
c^{2}

\alpha(\mathfrak{p})=\lambda_{f}(T)
. For example, for
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{p}_{13.1}
we find that
\lambda_{f}(T_{\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{p}}T_{\mathfrak{p}})=-2
, giving
\alpha(\mathfrak{p})=-2
.

Since the level is prime, there is just one Atkin-Lehner eigenvalue
\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{q}}
for
\mathfrak{q}=\mathfrak{n}
. Taking
\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{p}_{3.1}

\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{q}}(F_{0})=-1

\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{q}}(F_{2})=-1

One readily checks that the elliptic curve
E
has the same values for the trace of Frobenius
a_{\mathfrak{p}}(E)
at each of these primes, and one can in principle carry out the same check for any finite number of primes. In order to show that
E
is modular, it is necessary in principal to establish that
E
and the newform
F
have equal
a_{\mathfrak{p}}
for all (or at least all but finitely many) primes
\mathfrak{p}
, as this implies that their
\ell
-adic Galois representations are isomorphic for one, and hence all primes
\ell
, and that their
L
-functions are equal.

We now explain how to carry out the algorithm of
[
DGP10
, Algorithm 2.2]
. This involves determining a finite set of primes
\mathfrak{p}
such that the equality of the trace of Frobenius
a_{\mathfrak{p}}(E)
and the Hecke eigenvalue
a_{\mathfrak{p}}(F)
for the primes in this set is sufficient to imply their equality for all primes. We refer the reader to
[
DGP10
, Algorithm 2.2]
for more details of the algorithm.

(1)

First we chose two prime ideals
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{p}_{2.1}
and
\mathfrak{p}_{3.1}
. These have nonzero trace
a_{\mathfrak{p}}
, and in each case the prime
2
has inertia degree
1
in the extension
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha_{\mathfrak{p}})
, where
\alpha_{\mathfrak{p}}
is a root of the Frobenius polynomial
x^{2}-a_{\mathfrak{p}}x+\n(\mathfrak{p})
, as required by the algorithm. At these primes, we have
a_{\mathfrak{p}_{2.1}}(E)=-1
and
a_{\mathfrak{p}_{3.1}}(E)=-2
, in agreement with the Hecke eigenvalues.

(2)

For the relevant modulus
\mathfrak{n}_{F}=\mathfrak{p}_{2.1}^{5}\mathfrak{p}_{7.1}\mathfrak{p}_{7.2}\mathfrak{p}_{17.1}
, the ray class group is

\operatorname{Cl}_{K}(\mathfrak{n}_{F})\simeq C_{2}\times C_{2}\times C_{2}\times C_{24}\times C_{96}.

(3)

There are 31 index two subgroups of
\operatorname{Cl}_{K}(\mathfrak{n}_{F})
. For each of these and the full group, we consider the corresponding quadratic (or trivial) extension
L
of
K
. We compute the modulus
\mathfrak{n}_{L}
and the corresponding ray class group
\operatorname{Cl}_{K}(\mathfrak{n}_{L})
. For each of these ray class groups, we select a set of generators
\{\chi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
of the group of cubic characters.
For each of these generators, we pick a set of primes
\mathfrak{q}_{j}
such that

\langle\log(\chi_{1}(\mathfrak{q}_{j})),...,\log(\chi_{n}(\mathfrak{q}_{j}))\rangle_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}=(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})^{n}

for some
n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
. In our example, we only needed to use the primes above
3
and
23
. The eigenvalues for these primes are

a_{\mathfrak{p}_{3.1}}(F)=a_{\mathfrak{p}_{3.2}}(F)=-2,\quad a_{\mathfrak{p}_{23.1}}(F)=0,\quad\text{and}\quad a_{\mathfrak{p}_{23.2}}(F)=8,

which are all even, as required. Thus, we proceeded to the next step.

(4)

For this step, we need a basis of quadratic characters
\{\chi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of
\operatorname{Cl}_{K}(\mathfrak{n}_{F})
, together with a set of prime ideal
\{\mathfrak{p}_{i}\}
coprime to
\mathfrak{n}_{F}
, such that

\{\log(\chi_{1}(\mathfrak{q}_{j}),\log(\chi_{2}(\mathfrak{q}_{j}),\dots,\log(\chi_{n}(\mathfrak{q}_{j})\}_{j=1}^{2^{n}-1}=(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{n}\backslash\{0\}.

Here
n=5
, and it suffices to use the primes above the following rational primes:

\{5,11,13,19,31,53,79,89,107,131,149,157,167,257,281,457,593,1721\}.

For each of these, we check that the Hecke eigenvalues are equal to the trace of Frobenius of
E
, and we move on to the final step.

(5)

In the last step of algorithm, we need to check if the local
L
-factors of
E
and
F
are equal at primes dividing
2{\mathfrak{n}}(E){\mathfrak{n}}(F)\overline{{\mathfrak{n}}(F)}\Delta(K)
. These are

\{\mathfrak{p}_{2.1},\mathfrak{p}_{7.1},\mathfrak{p}_{7.2},\mathfrak{p}_{17.1}\}.

We have already checked that the Hecke eigenvalues agree with the trace of Frobenius at all these except
\mathfrak{p}_{7.2}
, the unique prime dividing the level. On the modular form side, the prime
\mathfrak{p}_{7.2}
has Atkin Lehner eigenvalue
+1
. On the elliptic curve side,
E
has non-split multiplicative reduction at
\mathfrak{p}_{7.2}
, and thus
a_{\mathfrak{p}_{7.2}}(E)=+1
, in agreement. So both
L
-functions have the same Euler factor at
\mathfrak{p}_{7.2}
.

This completes the proof of the modularity of the elliptic curve
E
.

Remark 4.1
.

As pointed out in the introduction, the results of
[
CN25
]
do not immediately imply that all elliptic curves over
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-17})
are modular. However, they do give sufficient conditions in terms of the mod-
3
and mod-
5
Galois representations, which are easily checked in individual cases. For this elliptic curve
E
, the mod-
\ell
Galois representations are surjective for all odd primes
\ell
(see the curve’s home page
2.0.68.1.7.2-a.2
for this information), which suffices, and gives a simpler way to establish modularity which was not available when this example was first studied.

5.
Results for other fields and future work

In the previous section we have only given detailed results for one field,
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-17})
, whose class group is different from that of other imaginary quadratic fields for which any detailed computations have been published, to illustrate the general method. However, our code works (at least in principle) over an arbitrary imaginary quadratic field, and we plan to produce more systematic data about newforms and their Hecke fields in the near future, data which we will publish via the
LMFDB
. To date, we have at least some data for all
763
fields of absolute discriminant up to
2500
, which includes all fields of class number up to
5
except
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-2683})
, with the largest class number being
63
(for
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-2351})
) and the largest
2
-rank being
3
(for 21 fields). For each of these fields, we have complete dimension data for all levels
\mathfrak{n}
with norm up to a bound depending on the field, but we have only identified and computed eigenvalues for ‘rational’ newforms
F
, that is, those with Hecke Field
k_{F}=\mathbb{Q}
. All this data for rational Bianchi newforms may be found in the
LMFDB
.
