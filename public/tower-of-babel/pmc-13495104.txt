Mid-term Electrical Remodelling in Surface ECG Following Percutaneous Patent Foramen Ovale Closure
Authors: Jingpu Yue, Gengxu He, Tong Yao, Yuanzhi Luo, Guanli Liang, Qiang Ji, Lei Zhao, Hong Geng, Kun Zuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Controversy exists around AF risk after patent foramen ovale (PFO) closure. This study characterised dynamic ECG changes over 6 months after Amplatzer closure, focusing on electrical remodelling.Methods:In all, 246 PFO patients underwent serial ECGs (P wave dispersion [PWD], PR interval, QRS, corrected QT interval [QTc]) and echocardiography before PFO closure and 24 hours and 1, 3 and 6 months after closure. Temporal changes were analysed. Subgroup analyses evaluated ECG changes according to comorbidity (migraine or cerebral infarction).Results:PWD increased significantly at 24 hours and peaked at 1 month, remaining elevated compared with baseline at 6 months (p<0.001). Of all patients, 5.3% had PWD ≥40 ms. The PR interval was significantly shortened after PFO closure, with this persisting at 6 months (p<0.001). AF occurred in six (2.4%) patients and supraventricular tachycardia occurred in eight (3.3%). QRS and QTc were stable. With regard to cardiac structure/function, no significant echocardiographic changes were seen from before to after PFO closure. In subgroups analyses, PR shortening was greater in patients with migraine than in those with cerebral infarction (p<0.001).Conclusion:PFO closure induced a transient elevation in PWD and sustained PR shortening. Under limited spot ECG monitoring, no clinically significant arrhythmias or structural changes were detected. However, extended surveillance is warranted to evaluate potential late-onset arrhythmogenic sequelae.

The patent foramen ovale (PFO) has emerged as a prominent research focus in structural heart disease. Major clinical complications associated with a PFO include cerebral infarction and migraine. In populations with cryptogenic stroke, percutaneous PFO closure has established a clinical consensus for the secondary prevention of recurrent cerebral ischaemic events.1–3 Current evidence demonstrates that this interventional approach significantly reduces the risk of stroke recurrence by eliminating the pathophysiological basis of right-to-left shunting, with its therapeutic efficacy and preventive value endorsed in international guidelines.4–6 International consensus recommends percutaneous closure as the primary treatment for eligible cryptogenic stroke patients with PFO to effectively mitigate the risk of recurrent stroke.1–3 Moreover, Apostolos et al. reported that PFO closure is clearly associated with the occurrence of postoperative AF.7

P wave dispersion (PWD) and correct QT interval (QTc) dispersion (QTcd), recognised as predictors of arrhythmia, serve as prognostic indicators of morbidity and mortality.8 The predictive value of P wave duration and PWD derived from 12-lead ECG for paroxysmal AF (PAF) in patients with structurally normal hearts has been well documented.9 Extended P wave duration and increased PWD have been identified as reliable predictors of AF onset, with prolonged intra- and interatrial conduction times being the greatest predictive markers of idiopathic AF.10

Although P wave parameters correlate with atrial arrhythmogenesis, prolongation of QTcd reflects heterogeneity in ventricular repolarisation, which is closely associated with a heightened risk of ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death in patients with chronic heart failure, diabetes or pulmonary arterial hypertension.11,12 Notably, QTcd has been proposed as an electrical remodelling indicator after PFO closure reflecting ventricular structural adaptation.11,12 This suggests that reductions in PWD and QTcd may confer protective effects against atrial and/or ventricular arrhythmogenesis.13–15 The novel Amplatzer PFO Occluder (Abbott Medical), a self-expanding nitinol double-disc device, exhibits superior closure performance.16,17 The aim of this study was to investigate changes in ECG parameters and their clinical implications after PFO closure using this next-generation occluder.

Methods

This retrospective single-centre study was conducted in the Department of Thoracic and Cardiovascular Surgery of the First Affiliated Hospital of Hebei North University. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent for the PFO closure procedure was obtained from all patients. For the retrospective analysis of medical records and ECG data, the institutional ethics committee approved a waiver of written informed consent.

Initially, 272 patients (age range 16–75 years) who underwent percutaneous PFO closure between November 2020 and November 2023 were enrolled in the study (Figure 1). Of these patients, 26 were excluded due to incomplete follow-up data, resulting in a final cohort of 246 patients. Other exclusion criteria were age <16 or >75 years, pre-existing AF or other arrhythmias, a history of coronary artery disease or valvular heart disease and concomitant congenital heart defects (e.g. atrial septal defects and ventricular septal defects). All patients underwent standardised assessments, including 12-lead surface ECGs and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) 1 day before the procedure and 24 hours and 1, 3 and 6 months after the procedure. Baseline characteristics and clinical history were extracted from electronic medical records.

Percutaneous closure was performed under local anaesthesia with fluoroscopic and TTE guidance. PFO size was assessed using colour Doppler echocardiography and agitated saline contrast (bubble study). Following standard aseptic preparation of the right groin area, local anaesthesia with lidocaine was administered. A 6-Fr sheath was then introduced into the right femoral vein. Under fluoroscopic guidance, a 6 Fr MPA2 catheter was advanced to the superior vena cava over a hydrophilic guidewire. In the left anterior oblique 45° projection, the catheter was retracted to the right atrium and manoeuvred into the left atrium and left superior pulmonary vein. A stiff guidewire was subsequently positioned and the delivery sheath was advanced into the left atrium.

The Amplatzer PFO occluder was deployed by first releasing the left atrial disc under TTE monitoring. The delivery system was then gently retracted to position the right atrial disc. A traction test was performed to confirm device stability. Real-time TTE was used to evaluate the occluder’s position, morphology and absence of pericardial effusion. Following confirmation of appropriate device placement, the occluder was fully released, and post-deployment TTE reassessment was performed before the delivery sheath was withdrawn. The puncture site was covered with a compression bandage, and the patient was transferred to the recovery ward.

Baseline Characteristics

As noted above, standard 12-lead surface ECGs were recorded before surgery and 24 hours and 1, 3 and 6 months after the intervention. All ECGs (baseline and follow-up assessments) were acquired at a paper speed of 25 mm/s and calibrated to 1 mV/cm. However, measurements were obtained through electronic magnification and callipers to enhance precision. Parameters obtained from the 12-lead ECGs were the P wave duration, PR interval, QRS voltage, QRS duration, QTc interval and QTcd.

The P wave duration was defined as the interval from the onset of the P wave (the junction of the isoelectric line with the initial upward/downward deflection) to its offset (the junction of the terminal P wave deflection with the isoelectric line). PWD was calculated as the difference between the maximum and minimum P wave durations across all 12 ECG leads.18

The PR interval was measured from the onset of the P wave to the initiation of the QRS complex, with the measurement based on lead II or V5. QRS voltage was determined as the vertical distance from the nadir of the Q wave to the peak of the R wave, with the measurement based on lead II or V5. QRS duration was defined as the maximum interval from the onset of the Q wave to the end of the S wave, with the measurement based on lead II or V5.

The QT interval was defined as the distance from the onset of the uniform QRS complex (the earliest QRS across all leads serving as the starting point for the measurement) to the lead-specific T wave endpoint (defined as the point when the descending T wave limb returns to the isoelectric line, determined by drawing a tangent along the steepest portion of the descending T wave limb, with the point where this tangent intersects the isoelectric line marking the end of the T wave). Bazett’s formula (QTc=QT/√RR) was used to calculate QTc. To calculate QTcd, the maximum and minimum QTc intervals among all measurable leads were identified, and the minimum QTc interval was subtracted from the maximum QTc interval.

Echocardiographic examinations were performed at the same time points as the 12-lead ECGs, namely before surgery and 24 hours and 1, 3 and 6 months after the intervention. Echocardiography was used to measure the interventricular septum (IVS), left ventricular posterior wall (LVPW), left ventricle (LV), left ventricle ejection fraction (LVEF), right ventricle (RV), right ventricular end-diastolic diameter/left ventricular end-diastolic diameter ratio and left atrial diameter (LAD).

Statistical analyses were performed using a linear mixed-effects model in SPSS version 27.0 (IBM) to evaluate changes in PWD, PR interval, QRS voltage, QRS duration, QTc and QTcd over time. The model incorporated subject-specific random intercepts to account for the correlation among repeated measurements. Fixed effects included time point (as a categorical variable), age, sex, baseline heart rate, hypertension and diabetes status. For time variables exhibiting significant main effects, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Tukey method to control the Type I error rate. The significance level was set at α=0.05.

To evaluate intra-operator variability, 20 randomly selected ECGs and echocardiograms were re-measured by the same operator (JY) at two different time points, at least 2 weeks apart. Inter-operator variability was assessed by having two independent operators measure the same 20 sets of images and ECGs. Variability was expressed as the mean absolute difference divided by the mean value of the two measurements, expressed as a percentage. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated using a two-way random effects model with absolute agreement to assess both intra- and inter-operator reproducibility. An ICC value >0.75 was considered good agreement.

Results

In all, 246 patients (96 [39.0%] men, 150 [61.0%] women) were enrolled in the study. The mean (± SD) age of the cohort was 49.3 ± 15.6 years (median 52.0 [interquartile range 39.0–62.0] years). Comorbid conditions included cerebral infarction (198; 80.5%) and refractory migraine (48; 19.5%; Table 1). All patients received the Amplatzer PFO occluder, with 243 (98.8%) implanted with a 25 mm device (9-PFO-025) and 3 (1.2%) implanted with a 35 mm device (9-PFO-035) due to concomitant atrial septal aneurysm.

Linear mixed-model analysis revealed a significant main effect of time on PWD after adjusting for age, sex, baseline heart rate, hypertension and diabetes (F4,413.22=227.96, p<0.001). Post hoc Tukey tests (significance level set at α=0.05) demonstrated that, compared with baseline, PWD significantly increased by 4.7 ms (95% CI [4.08–5.30]; p<0.001) at 24 hours after the procedure, peaked 1 month after the procedure with an increase of 10.3 ms (95% CI [9.57–10.98]; p<0.001) and remained elevated at 6 months by 3.8 ms (95% CI [3.27–4.38]; p<0.001; Figure 2A) . Similarly, time exhibited a significant main effect on the PR interval (F4,508.69=17.78, p<0.001). Post hoc Tukey tests indicated an immediate shortening of 5.0 ms (95% CI [3.30–6.62]; p<0.001) in the PR interval on postoperative day 1 compared with baseline, which persisted until 6 months after the procedure, with a shortening of the PR interval of 4.3 ms (95% CI [2.83 to 5.84]; p<0.001). Notably, 13 (5.3%) patients had PWD ≥40 ms at the 1-month follow-up. (Figure 2B).

No other factors were found to have a significant effect on PWD or the PR interval. QRS duration, QRS voltage, QTc and QTcd remained stable throughout the study period without significant changes (p>0.05; Figures 2C–2F). AF occurred in six (2.4%) patients and supraventricular tachycardia occurred in eight (3.3%). Of these, 14 patients, 12 (85.7%) were men older than 60 years. Subgroup analyses stratified by sex and comorbidities revealed no significant sex-specific differences in PWD or PR interval dynamics (Figures 3A and 3B). However, the reduction in the PR interval was greater in the group with refractory migraine than in the group with cerebral infarction. There were no significant differences in PWD changes among the different subgroups.

On the basis of echocardiographic parameters, percutaneous PFO closure did not result in changes in the cardiac ultrasound data (Table 2).

For ECG parameters, the inter- and intra-operator variability was 4.8% and 4.3%, respectively, whereas for echocardiographic data, variability for all measurements was <5%. The ICCs for intra-observer reproducibility demonstrated good agreement between repeated ECG and echocardiographic measurements, with ICC values ranging from 0.85 to 0.90. The ICCs for interobserver reproducibility ranged from 0.75 to 0.87.

Discussion

PFO, the most prevalent congenital cardiac structural anomaly, has a prevalence rate of 25–30% in the adult population.19,20 Accumulating evidence supports potential associations between PFO and clinical entities such as cryptogenic stroke, migraine, decompression sickness and positional hypoxaemia through distinct pathophysiological mechanisms.21–23

Percutaneous PFO closure has been established as a standard of care for the secondary prevention of cryptogenic stroke recurrence, supported by a compelling evidence base derived from multicentre randomised controlled trials.1–3 Notably, although the therapeutic utility of percutaneous PFO closure in the management of migraine remains controversial, systematic reviews have demonstrated that 14–85% of patients report significant symptomatic improvement in migraine episodes after the procedure.24 These findings align with the 80% remission rate observed in our cohort, underscoring the importance of large-scale prospective studies to validate the clinical efficacy of percutaneous PFO closure for the modulation of neurological symptoms.

The current body of evidence on atrial arrhythmogenic risk after percutaneous closure demonstrates considerable heterogeneity. Several RCTs have reported statistically significant differences in the incidence of new-onset AF after the procedure. For example, Wolfrum et al. conducted a meta-analysis of five RCTs (n=3,440 cryptogenic stroke patients with PFO), revealing that PFO closure significantly reduced the risk of stroke recurrence (HR 0.41; 95% CI [0.24–0.68]) without increasing the incidence of major adverse events (p=0.13), although they did demonstrate a trend towards elevated short-term AF risk (OR 1.53; 95% CI [0.97–2.42]).25

These findings corroborate data from the RESPECT trial, a multicentre RCT (n=980) that demonstrated a higher incidence of AF in the Amplatzer device group than in the medical therapy group (3.0% versus 0.6%; p=0.02), albeit without fatal consequences.26

In contrast, other investigations report differing conclusions. For example, Johnson et al. performed a retrospective cohort analysis of 610 patients undergoing percutaneous closure (PFO: 384; atrial septal defect: 184; complex defects: 42), reporting an overall 5.2% (32/610) incidence of arrhythmia at the 4-month follow-up, with clinically significant atrioventricular block requiring intervention observed in only 0.3% (2/610) of patients.27 Notably, supraventricular tachyarrhythmias (AF: 22 patients; atrial flutter: seven patients) were the predominant subtype (90.6%), primarily exhibiting a self-limiting course.

Leclercq et al. prospectively evaluated 62 patients with PFO closure through systematic ambulatory ECG monitoring (three 24-hour ambulatory ECG sessions within 6 months after the procedure).28 The incidence of AF in that study was 4.8% AF, with all events occurring within the first postoperative month as paroxysmal episodes devoid of adverse outcomes.28 That study also revealed that 85.7% of supraventricular arrhythmias clustered within the initial 4-week postoperative period, highlighting a critical time-sensitive window for perioperative electrical surveillance.

The observed heterogeneity across studies may be attributable to methodological variations (RCTs versus observational designs), differences in follow-up duration (median 6 months to 5 years), device-specific mechanical interactions (differences in atrial tissue stimulation across closure systems) and surveillance sensitivity (routine ECG versus systematic ambulatory monitoring protocols).

In the present retrospective cohort study, we analysed 246 patients undergoing percutaneous PFO closure, with systematic evaluation of changes in surface ECG. The key findings are as follows. First, postprocedural PWD exhibited significant temporal variations, peaking at the 1-month follow-up (mean ± SD increase 20.9 ± 3.1 ms from baseline; p<0.001). Although partial regression occurred by 6 months, PWD values remained substantially elevated compared with preoperative levels (p<0.001). Of note, 13 (5.3%) patients developed clinically significant PWD ≥40 ms, which is a validated predictor of AF development.10 This subgroup had a mean age of 51.3 years, with only one patient having migraine as a comorbidity. AF occurred in six (2.4%) cases and supraventricular tachycardia occurred in eight (3.3%). Second, significant PR shortening emerged within 24 hours after the procedure (mean ± SD 152.3 ± 8.5 ms versus 147.9 ± 10.2 ms; p<0.001), which persisted throughout the 6-month observation period (p<0.001). This electrical modification suggests potential device-induced mechanical tension that alters intra-atrial conduction properties. Third, no statistically significant changes were observed in the QRS duration or QTc interval across the entire follow-up period.

We also evaluated postoperative outcomes according to occluder size. Kibler et al. documented a case of complete atrioventricular block following PFO closure using a 35 mm device.29 In a related study, Cheesa et al. examined individualised device size selection for atrial septal defect closure and summarised the subsequent complications, including arrhythmias, device embolisation, erosion and thrombosis.30 Based on the findings reported in these studies, we conclude that the occluder discs, particularly the larger right atrial disc, can cause mechanical irritation or friction against the atrial wall, potentially creating new ectopic foci or re-entry circuits. After implantation, the device exerts substantial mechanical compression on adjacent cardiac structures, particularly the atrioventricular node and its surrounding regions within the interatrial septum, leading to localised tissue oedema and inflammation. Consequently, larger occluders are associated with an elevated risk of arrhythmias, such as atrial arrhythmias, conduction block and ST-T segment changes, due to increased mechanical stimulation of the atrial wall.

The fact that nickel allergy can cause arrhythmia is worthy of attention. Specifically, in the recent study of Apostolos et al., which randomised 96 PFO patients to receive either an Amplatzer or Gore Cardioform septal occluder, 28 patients (29.2%) exhibited nickel hypersensitivity.31 In that study, the incidence of device syndrome (patient-reported symptoms including chest pain, palpitations, migraine, dyspnoea and rash) was significantly higher among those with than without nickel hypersensitivity (71.4% versus 0.6%; p<0.001).31 Specifically, new-onset or worsening migraine and palpitations were more common in patients with nickel hypersensitivity. The study indicated that nickel hypersensitivity was associated with a 10.5-fold increase in the odds of device syndrome, although the incidence of device syndrome was similar between the two device types.31 Apostolos et al. demonstrated that patients with nickel hypersensitivity are at a significantly higher risk of device syndrome after PFO closure.31

Regarding the potential mechanisms underlying nickel hypersensitivity-induced arrhythmias, Apostolos et al. subsequently proposed a plausible explanation: the frame of PFO occluders is composed of a nickel–titanium alloy (nitinol) and a considerable amount of nickel is released into the bloodstream before complete device endothelialisation (which typically takes 1–3 months).32 Nickel-induced inflammation may exert pro-arrhythmic effects. Furthermore, the peak incidence of supraventricular arrhythmias coincides with the period of elevated nickel concentrations in the blood. The proportion of patients developing supraventricular arrhythmias after PFO closure is similar to the proportion of individuals with nickel hypersensitivity in the general population. Moreover, the incidence of supraventricular arrhythmias declines after 3 months, which may be related to the completion of device endothelialisation.

Animal studies have outlined a triphasic remodelling process following implantation of an atrial septal closure device: an acute phase (1–5 days) characterised by platelet/fibrin deposition, a subacute phase (7–10 days) marked by the start of endothelialisation and a chronic phase (≥3 months) in which there is complete endothelial coverage.33-34 This dynamic healing cascade involves spatiotemporal remodelling of both inflammatory and biomechanical microenvironments, which may modulate anisotropic parameters of atrial myocardial electrical conduction, thereby mediating electrical remodelling phenomena, such as changes in PWD and PR interval.

The implantation of an occluder exerts continuous mechanical stimulation/compression on the atrial septum, thereby inducing local inflammation and fibrosis, coupled with autonomic nerve stimulation. This leads to atrial electrophysiological remodelling (shortened refractory period and slowed conduction) and the formation of ectopic foci, ultimately providing a pathological basis for the occurrence of AF (particularly through re-entrant mechanisms).

However, our findings suggest that these transient electrical alterations do not translate into clinically significant atrial arrhythmogenic risk, aligning with a previous report of low postoperative AF incidence (<5%).35 Notably, device-specific arrhythmogenic profiles emerge from comparative analyses: the CardioSEAL/Starflex occluder group exhibited significantly higher AF rates (4.2–4.5%) than contemporary devices, indicating that technological refinements may enhance electrical safety.36 Furthermore, advanced age (>60 years) and diabetes have been identified as independent risk factors for de novo postoperative AF, underscoring the need for personalised risk stratification, which includes characterisation of temporal changes in ECG parameters after PFO closure, providing novel insights into device-associated cardiac remodelling mechanisms.37

According to the study of Musto et al., which compared the therapeutic outcomes of the Gore Cardioform occluder and the Amplatzer ASO occluder, the Gore Cardioform occluder may have an advantage in achieving complete closure (i.e. reducing residual shunt), particularly in patients with complex anatomical structures such as atrial septal aneurysm.38 The soft disc design of the Gore Cardioform occluder may be more compatible with highly mobile atrial septal aneurysms, allowing for tighter apposition. In contrast, the Amplatzer ASO occluder may offer quicker and simpler operation but comes with a slightly higher risk of residual shunt. During an average follow-up period of approximately 2 years, no recurrent ischaemic strokes or transient ischaemic attacks were observed in either patient group.38 This indicates that, provided effective closure is achieved, both devices offer excellent protection against stroke recurrence.

Another study by Gevorgyan Fleming et al. compared several commonly used occluders (Gore Cardioform, Amplatzer cribriform, Helex, Amplatzer ASO, CardioSEAL and Amplatzer PFO occluders).39 In that study, AF was more common with the Cardioform occluder (13%) and less frequent with the Helex (4%) or Amplatzer PFO (4%) occluders.39 Regardless, all the occluders demonstrated good closure performance.39 In summary, an ideal device should combine both a low residual shunt rate and a low incidence of complications.

The augmented PR interval variability observed after PFO closure in patients with migraine necessitates exploration of the potential underlying neurocardiac mechanisms, as detailed below.

Inherent Autonomic Dysregulation in Patients with Migraine

Migraine pathophysiology is intrinsically linked to autonomic imbalance, typified by sympathetic hyperactivity and/or parasympathetic dysmodulation. This aberrant neurovegetative state may directly modulate the conduction properties of the atrioventricular node via catecholaminergic signalling pathways.40

Periprocedural Neuroinflammatory Cross-talk

The acute surgical stress response transiently activates sympathetic outflow, potentially reducing PR intervals, while subsequent parasympathetic rebound during the recovery phases may prolong conduction times. The impaired autonomic adaptability of patients with migraine likely exacerbates these electrical oscillations.41

Serotonergic Homeostasis Perturbations

Migraine pathogenesis involves 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) flux through trigeminovascular interactions. Post-closure attenuation of right-to-left shunting may alter systemic 5-HT bioavailability by reducing pulmonary bypass of vasoactive amines. Given that patients with migraine have heightened 5-HT receptor sensitivity, such metabolic shifts could dysregulate cardiac conduction via central/peripheral serotonergic pathways (e.g. the gut–brain–heart axis).42-43

Limitations

Although this study systematically characterised short- and intermediate-term electrical remodelling after PFO closure, several limitations warrant consideration. First, this study lacks systematic long-term cardiac rhythm monitoring, which means that asymptomatic or paroxysmal arrhythmic events may not have been detected. Second, as a single-centre observational study without a control group receiving drug therapy or no intervention, natural fluctuations in the PR interval and other confounding factors cannot be fully excluded. Therefore, definitive conclusions regarding the long-term electrical safety of PFO closure still require studies with a larger sample size and longer follow-up duration, as well as controlled prospective studies, for confirmation. Third, the measurements in this study were based on ECGs recorded at a standard paper speed of 25 mm/s. Although good reproducibility was ensured through electronic magnification and stringent standardised protocols, this remains an aspect that could be improved in future studies (e.g. by using a recording speed of 50 mm/s or using digital acquisition with higher sampling rates for enhanced measurement precision). Fourth, underpowered subgroup analyses, particularly in the migraine cohort (n=48), precluded definitive conclusions regarding phenotype-specific responses. Finally, the pathophysiological mechanisms linking device implantation to AF remain incompletely understood, highlighting a critical knowledge gap that requires mechanistic investigation through integrated electrical and molecular profiling.

In future, longitudinal studies with an extended follow-up (≥5 years) are needed to delineate the late arrhythmogenic trajectories. Multimodal imaging protocols, combining cardiac MRI atrial strain analysis with high-density electrical mapping, are needed to deconstruct electromechanical coupling mechanisms. Finally, studies comparing the effectiveness of specific devices using standardised arrhythmia monitoring endpoints are needed.

Conclusion

Percutaneous PFO closure induces characteristic electrical remodelling, manifesting as phasic alterations in PWD and a sustained shortening of the PR interval. A transient elevation in PWD was observed during the 1-month post-procedural period, with PWD remaining significantly elevated relative to baseline at the 6-month follow-up despite partial resolution. Conversely, the reduction in the PR interval persisted throughout the observation period. Under limited spot ECG monitoring, no severe bradyarrhythmia or atrioventricular block events associated with PFO closure were observed over the follow-up period in this study, although extended surveillance is warranted to evaluate potential late-onset arrhythmogenic sequelae.
