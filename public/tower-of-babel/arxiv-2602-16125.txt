On the Power of Source Screening for Learning Shared Feature Extractors
Leo (Muxing) Wang, Connor Mclaughlin, Lili Su

Abstract
Learning with shared representation is widely recognized as an effective way to separate commonalities from heterogeneity across various heterogeneous sources. Most existing work includes all related data sources via simultaneously training a common feature extractor and source-specific heads. It is well understood that data sources with low relevance or poor quality may hinder representation learning. In this paper, we further dive into the question of which data sources should be learned jointly by focusing on the traditionally deemed “good” collection of sources, in which individual sources have similar relevance and qualities with respect to the true underlying common structure. Towards tractability, we focus on the linear setting where sources share a low-dimensional subspace. We find that source screening can play a central role in statistically optimal subspace estimation. We show that, for a broad class of problem instances, training on a carefully selected subset of sources suffices to achieve minimax optimality, even when a substantial portion of data is discarded. We formalize the notion of an informative subpopulation, develop algorithms and practical heuristics for identifying such subsets, and validate their effectiveness through both theoretical analysis and empirical evaluations on synthetic and real-world datasets.

Contributions.

Our main contributions are:

\bullet

We show that, for a class of problem instances, a state-of-the-art subspace estimator achieves minimax statistical optimality when trained on a suitably chosen subset of sources, even if a large fraction of sources is discarded.

\bullet

We formalize the notion of a desired subpopulation of sources and prove that training exclusively on such a subpopulation attains the minimax statistical optimality.

\bullet

We develop an efficient algorithm that provably identifies a good subpopulation in the genie-aided setting, and propose principled heuristics for subpopulation selection in the absence of gene information.

\bullet

We empirically validate the effectiveness of data source pre-screening and our proposed methods on both synthetic and real-world datasets.

4.1

Potentials of Source Screening

In this section, through theoretical derivation and numerical illustration, we provide insights into the potential of source/client screening to improve statistical rates – despite discarding a large portion of the data – and to enhance the accuracy of the learned subspaces.

4.2

On the Fundamentals of Source Screening

We devote this section to exploring the fundamental question: For any given collection of local head configuration
\alpha_{i}^{\star}
, does there exist a sufficiently large subpopulation of sources
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq[M]
such that
\kappa\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{C}}}\alpha_{i}^{\star}(\alpha_{i}^{\star})^{\top}\right)=\Theta(1)?

4.2.1

Desired Sub-population and Existence

Before turning to algorithmic or statistical considerations, we first examine the existence of a desired sub-population. Understanding when and why such a sub-population exists is essential for interpreting the guarantees of our framework.

We begin with a couple of concrete examples to build intuition and motivate a formal definition of a desired sub-population of clients.
Recall from the motivating example in Section
4.1
that
\beta_{1}=\frac{m_{1}}{N}
and
\beta_{k}=\frac{m_{k}}{N}
, i.e., they are the fractions of clients whose underlying truth is
\alpha_{1}^{\star}
and
\alpha_{k}^{\star}
, respectively.
Let
{\mathcal{S}}_{\ell}\subseteq\{i:\alpha_{i}^{\star}=\widehat{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell}\}
such that
|{\mathcal{S}}_{\ell}|=\beta_{k}M
for
\ell=1,\cdots,k
. Those subsets exist because

|\{i:\alpha_{i}^{\star}=\widehat{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell}\}|\geq|\{i:\alpha_{i}^{\star}=\widehat{\alpha}^{\star}_{k}\}|=\beta_{k}M
.
Let
{\mathcal{S}}=\cup_{\ell=1}^{k}{\mathcal{S}}_{\ell}
, and
A=[\alpha_{1}^{\star},\cdots,\alpha_{M}^{\star}]
.
It is easy to see that

\kappa\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{S}}}\alpha_{i}^{\star}(\alpha_{i}^{\star})^{\top}\right)=\Theta(1),

and

|{\mathcal{S}}|=k\beta_{k}M=k\lambda_{\min}(AA^{\top}),
where
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{A})
represents the smallest eigenvalue of any matrix
\mathbf{A}
.
From our analysis in Section
4.1
, we know that training on the data kept on this subpopulation of clients with the split-local averaging algorithm in
(
34
)
is minimax optimal.

Similarly, when
\alpha_{i}^{\star}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}{\mathcal{N}}(\bf{0},I)
, from
(
34
)
, we know that with high probability

\kappa\left(\sum_{i\in[M]}\alpha_{i}^{\star}(\alpha_{i}^{\star})^{\top}\right)=\Theta(1),

and

|{\mathcal{S}}|=\Theta\left(k\lambda_{\min}(AA^{\top})\right)=\Theta\left(k\frac{M}{k}\right)=\Theta(M).

Furthermore, training on the data kept on all clients is minimax optimal.
Hence, we provide a formal notion of a desired subpopulation as follows.
As a gentle entry point to this line of investigation, yet without losing much generality, we assume matrix
A
is standardized, i.e., each column has norm 1
(
47
)

Definition 2

(Admissible subpopulation)
.

Let
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k\times M}
be a standardized matrix such that its columns span
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}

{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq[M]

•
\kappa\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{S}}}\alpha_{i}^{\star}(\alpha_{i}^{\star})^{\top}\right)=\Theta(1)

•

Theorem 2

(Minimax Statistical Optimal)

Let
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq[M]
be a given sub-population of clients that satisfies the two conditions in Definition

When

In particular, the optimal rate can be achieved via the split sample averaging algorithm in
(
34
)
.

Intuitively, this result shows that as long as there exists a sufficiently “good” sub-population of clients, training exclusively on this group is already statistically optimal, despite disregarding the data on other clients.
In fact, restricting attention to such a carefully chosen subset does not incur any statistical loss; rather, it can lead to a significant statistical gain by mitigating bias introduced by less informative or misaligned clients.
It turns out that when
\lambda_{\min}(AA^{\top})=O(1)
, a good subpopulation as per Definition

Theorem 3
.

Let
A=[\alpha_{1}^{\star},\cdots,\alpha_{M}^{\star}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k\times M}

Suppose that
\|A\|^{2}\lesssim\frac{M}{k}

When

\kappa\big(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{S}}}\alpha_{i}^{\star}(\alpha_{i}^{\star})^{\top}\big)=\Theta(1),~~\text{and}~~|{\mathcal{S}}|=\Omega(k\lambda_{\min}(AA^{\top})).

It is worth noting that the condition
\|A\|^{2}\lesssim\frac{M}{k}
does not imply the condition number of
A
is small. In fact, its condition number can be arbitrarily bad. To see this, consider the example in which

In this example,
\kappa(A)=\sqrt{\frac{\frac{2M}{k}-\epsilon}{\epsilon}}

Before presenting the proof of Theorem
3

The stable rank of a matrix is defined as

\displaystyle\text{st.rank}(A):=\|A\|^{2}_{F}/\|A\|^{2}.

(7)

Intuitively, the stable rank measures the effective “energy spread” of a matrix. Unlike the traditional rank, it tells you how many “significant directions” the matrix really has, in a way that is robust to small perturbations.
In the special case where each of the columns of
A
has unit norm, it holds that
\|A\|^{2}_{F}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|\alpha_{i}^{*}\|^{2}=M.

Furthermore, if all columns are identical, then
\|A\|^{2}=M
.
In contrast, when the columns approximately evenly cover the
k

\|A\|^{2}\approx\frac{M}{k}
.
In general,
\text{st.rank}(A)\leq\text{rank}(A)
.
In addition, let
A_{{\mathcal{S}}_{0}}
, where
{\mathcal{S}}_{0}\subseteq[M]
, denote the submatrix of

that contains only the collection of columns in
{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
.

4.3

Empirical Subpopulation Search

Algorithm
1
relies on (1) the stable rank of the inaccessible matrix
A
and (2)
\lambda_{\min}\left(AA^{\top}\right)
in determining the size of the randomly selected columns. In practice,
A
is not given. In this section, we present practical heuristics for algorithm design that circumvent the need for this information.

Let
\bar{z_{i}}=\frac{2}{n_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}/2}y_{ij}x_{ij}
, and
\widetilde{{z_{i}}}=\frac{2}{n_{i}}\sum_{j=n_{i}/2+1}^{n_{i}}y_{ij}x_{ij}
.
Let
Z=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{M}n_{i}\bar{z}_{i}\widetilde{z}_{i}^{\top}
.
Next, we discuss the connection between matrix
A
,
B^{\star}A
,
D
, and
Z
.
We know

\mathbb{E}\left[Z\right]=B^{\star}D(B^{\star})^{\top}.

Note that the nonzero eigenvalues of
\mathbb{E}\left[Z\right]
are identical to those of matrix
D
. Formally,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}\left(D\right)=\lambda_{\min}^{+}\left(B^{\star}D(B^{\star})^{\top}\right)=\lambda_{\min}^{+}\left({\mathbb{E}}[Z]\right),

where
\lambda_{\min}^{+}
denotes the smallest non-zero eigenvalue.
In addition, the eigenvectors of
\mathbb{E}\left[Z\right]
are
\left\{B^{\star}\bm{v}_{\ell}\right\}_{\ell=1}^{k}
, where
\left\{\bm{v}_{\ell}\right\}_{\ell=1}^{k}
are the eigenvectors of
D
.

Note that if we define the scaled version
\widetilde{A}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left[\sqrt{n}\alpha_{1}^{\star},\cdots,\sqrt{n}\alpha_{M}^{\star}\right]
, we have
D=\widetilde{A}\widetilde{A}^{\top}
, and

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}\left(\widetilde{A}\widetilde{A}^{\top}\right)=\lambda_{\min}^{+}\left({\mathbb{E}}[Z]\right).

Despite this connection, we are still not able to directly work on
B^{*}\widetilde{A}
as Algorithm
1
requires the unscaled matrix
A
. Instead, we work with the unscaled version of the matrix
Z
, i.e.,
\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bar{z}_{i}\widetilde{z}_{i}^{\top}
. Let
\widehat{Z}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bar{z}_{i}\widetilde{z}_{i}^{\top}.
Then,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\widehat{Z}]=\sum_{i=1}^{M}{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{z}_{i}]{\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{z}_{i}^{\top}]=B^{\star}AA^{\top}(B^{\star})^{\top}.

By the same logic as in the scaled case, the nonzero eigenvalues of
{\mathbb{E}}[\widehat{Z}]
are identical to those of matrix
AA^{\top}
. Thus, we have

\lambda_{\min}\left(AA^{\top}\right)=\lambda_{\min}^{+}\left(B^{\star}AA^{\top}(B^{\star})^{\top}\right)=\lambda_{\min}^{+}\left({\mathbb{E}}[\widehat{Z}]\right).

In addition, we can prove
\text{st.rank}(B^{*}A)=\text{st.rank}(A).
The proof is deferred to Section
I.1
. Then, we can develop an empirical version of Algorithm
1
,
which replaces
A
by
(\bar{Z},\widetilde{Z})
.
Under standard concentration assumptions on the local sample size
n
,

\widehat{Z}
concentrates around its expectation in operator norm, and

\mathrm{st.rank}(\bar{Z}+\widetilde{Z})
serves as a consistent proxy for

\mathrm{st.rank}(A)
.
The resulting empirical procedure is formally stated in
Algorithm

, which can be found in Appendix

Numerical Experiments

We validate the efficacy of the data source pre-screening and our proposed method in a more practically relevant distributed learning context through a series of experiments on synthetic regression tasks and real-world classification benchmarks.
These evaluations aim to demonstrate the resilience of our methods across diverse data regimes (e.g., data dimensionality and inherent correlation structures) and system regimes (such as population scalability) when integrated into real-world distributed learning systems.

We compare our client selection methods to several baselines: full population training (no screening), random subsampling, and power-of-choice selection
(
9
)
. For the latter, we perform selection once following an initial round of full population training. To ensure parity across methods, we first apply our screening procedure and then constrain all baseline methods to select an equivalent number of clients. Additionally, in the clustered synthetic setting, we evaluate a balanced baseline that subsamples clients according to their ground-truth cluster assignments to ensure equal representation across groups. Each algorithm is evaluated based on the performance of downstream estimators on to the selected clients, averaged over 20 random seeds.

Conclusion

We studied the problem of learning shared linear subspaces from heterogeneous data sources.
Our theoretical analysis establishes that, under mild regularity conditions, a balanced subcollection of sources can achieve minimax optimal rates and outperform full data utilization.
Our experiments on confirm that our method consistently improves estimation accuracy across both linear and non-linear regimes. A promising direction for future work involves extending our theoretical analysis in empirical settings.

Impact Statement

We do not anticipate direct negative societal or ethical consequences. While the practical impact of data pre-screening depends on the application context, our results suggest that thoughtful source selection can, in fact, promote more equitable learning outcomes by counteracting imbalance rather than reinforcing it. There are many other potential societal consequences of our work, none which we feel must be
specifically highlighted here.
