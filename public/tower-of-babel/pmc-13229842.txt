DYRK1A and Parkinson’s disease, facts and hypotheses
Authors: Laurent Meijer, Mattias F Lindberg, Gaëlle Hogrel, Bernard Khor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe dual-specificity, tyrosine phosphorylation-regulated kinase 1A (DYRK1A) is intensively studied because of its implication in numerous human diseases (Down syndrome, Alzheimer’s disease, type 2 diabetes, myocardial infarction, various cancers and leukaemia, etc.). Several GWAS studies have identified DYRK1A as a risk factor for Parkinson’s disease (PD). DYRK1A indeed phosphorylates at least 20 proteins clearly involved in PD: AMPH, CASP9, DYN1, FOXO, GSK3B, MAP1B, MAPT, MEF2D, NFAT, TP53, PRKN, PLK2, RABs, RCAN1, SEPT4, SNCA, STAT3, SYNJ1, TOM70, WASL. Several other proteins involved in PD interact with DYRK1A: calpains, DSCAM, REST/NRSF, 14-3-3. DYRK1A is involved in axonal transport, neural stem cells proliferation and differentiation, and neuroinflammation. A few DYRK1A inhibitors have been tested on PD models, generally showing protective effects.The overall picture provided by this comprehensive review on the links between DYRK1A and PD advocates for more fundamental studies to understand how DYRK1A participates to the onset and development of PD and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), two closely related disorders. It also encourages the evaluation of well-characterized pharmacological modulators of DYRK1A as therapeutic approaches to various aspects of PD and DLB.Keywords: DYRK1A, Parkinson's disease, Dementia with Lewy bodies, kinase inhibitors

Introduction

By catalysing the phosphorylation of serine, threonine and tyrosine residues in proteins, protein kinases (2.5% of the genome) probably constitute the main intracellular regulators of cell life and physiology. Many human diseases are associated with abnormal kinase activities and distorted phosphorylation levels of specific proteins. As a consequence, kinases have become the first therapeutic target for the pharmaceutical industry, leading to 94 FDA-approved kinase inhibitors (review in (Roskoski, 2026)). Quite a scientific journey since the discovery of protein kinases in the ‘50s by Edmond H. Fisher and Edwin G. Krebs (Fischer and Krebs, 1955)!

The aim of this article is to review, as comprehensively as possible, all the published data linking the protein kinase DYRK1A (dual specificity, tyrosine phosphorylation-regulated kinase 1A) with Parkinson’s disease (PD) and Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB). The reasons for writing this extensive review were (1) the growing interest for DYRK1A dysregulation in multiple CNS diseases, (2) the increased number of investigations on DYRK1A as a clinical therapeutic drug target (and on pharmacological inhibitors), (3) the appearance of DYRK1A as a risk factor for PD in GWAS and (4) the large body of data (scattered, sometimes solid, sometimes weak, but quite diverse) converging towards a role of DYRK1A at multiple steps of the onset and development of PD and DLB. Collectively the results presented here advocate for the investigation of DYRK1A as a potential therapeutic target for PD and DLB.

Parkinson’s disease: prevalence, environmental & genetic factors, molecular mechanisms

PD, the second most common neurodegenerative disease, affects 6.1 million people worldwide (2016), a number which has more than doubled between 1990 and 2016 and is expected to reach 12.9 million by 2040 (Ben-Shlomo et al., 2024; GBD, 2016; Ou et al., 2021). PD is now the fastest growing neurological disease, with an extremely high societal and economical cost (Yang et al., 2020).

PD is characterized by the specific, progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons from the substantia nigra pars compacta (SN) and the gradual appearance of Lewy bodies (LBs) and Lewy neurites (LNs), the pathological hallmarks of PD. LBs are intracellular aggregates of misfolded α-synuclein (SNCA) and other proteins. Other ‘synucleinopathies’ include the closely related DLB (reviews in ((Goddard et al., 2024; Guerreiro et al., 2018; Scholz et al., 2025; Zarkali et al., 2025)) and multiple system atrophy (MSA) (Sailer et al., 2016).

Depletion of dopamine in the nigro-striatal circuits leads to locomotor disorders (bradykinesia, vocal symptoms, rigidity, postural instability, tremors, walking or gait difficulties, dystonia) and nonmotor symptoms (depression, anxiety, cognitive impairment, sense of smell alteration, sweating, melanoma, gastrointestinal symptoms, joint pain). When motor symptoms appear, 50-60% of dopaminergic neurons have degenerated, and dopamine levels have been depleted by 70-80%.

Both environmental and genetic factors contribute to the onset and development of PD and DLB (Blauwendraat et al., 2020; Goddard et al., 2024; Pang et al., 2019) (Fig. 1). Most PD cases are sporadic, probably linked to the exposure to a variety of exogenous, environmental factors, many of which being of anthropogenic origin (Fig. 1). Environmental factors (De Miranda et al., 2022; Dorsey et al., 2025; Goldman, 2014; Pang et al., 2019; Paul et al., 2023; Payami et al., 2023; Shan et al., 2023) comprise: (1) natural, plant-derived toxins such as cycasin, L-BMAA and annonacin, which are linked to endemic parkinsonism (Menšíková et al., 2023), (2) insecticides (e.g. chlorpyrifos) (Sheikh and Sheikh, 2020), piscicides (e.g. rotenone) (Ibarra-Gutiérrez et al., 2023; Tanner et al., 2011), pesticides (e.g. paraquat, organochlorines) (Paul et al., 2023; See et al., 2022; Tanner et al., 2011), herbicides (e.g. agent orange) (de la Monte and Goel, 2022), glyphosate (Bloem and Boonstra, 2023; Caballero et al., 2018; Costas-Ferreira et al., 2022; Eriguchi et al., 2019; Pu et al., 2020), fungicides (e.g. Maneb, Zineb) (Liu et al., 2022a), solvents (e.g. trichlorethylene, hexane) (Dorsey et al., 2023; Pezzoli et al., 1990), (3) metals (Pyatha et al., 2022) such as copper (Fu et al., 2025b), iron (Mochizuki et al., 2020), lead (Weisskopf et al., 2010), manganese (Lucchini and Tieu, 2023), mercury (Torrey and Simmons, 2023), or zinc (Tamura et al., 2022; Teil et al., 2022), (4) air pollution (particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10)) (Chauhan et al., 2025; Cole-Hunter et al., 2023; Costa et al., 2020; Cristaldi et al., 2022; Flood-Garibay et al., 2023; Gialluisi et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2020b; Zhang et al., 2025c), anionic nanoplastic contaminants (Liu et al., 2023b). Other contributing/inducing factors include aging (Collier et al., 2017), traumatic brain injury (Bruce et al., 2023; Marras et al., 2014), gut microbiome (Feng et al., 2024; Wallen et al., 2022), pathogenic viral infections (Agostini et al., 2021; Bu et al., 2015; Jang et al., 2009; Jasemi et al., 2022; Linard et al., 2022; Zorina et al., 2023), 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) produced by oxidation of dopamine (Burbulla et al., 2017; Kostrzewa, 2022), and experimental laboratory reagents such as 1-methyl-4-phenylpyridinium (MPP+), the conversion product of 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP) by monoamine oxidases (note the structural similarity of MPP+ and paraquat) (Ballard et al., 1985; McKnight and Hack, 2020; Narmashiri et al., 2022; Thirugnanam and Santhakumar, 2022), which are widely used to trigger PD pathology in cellular and animal models, respectively.

Familial forms of PD account for 5-10% of all PD cases, they are due to inheritance of rare mutations in a few genes (Fig. 1) (Deng et al., 2018; Lim et al., 2024; Pang et al., 2019). In addition, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified at least 134 genomic loci which contribute to PD susceptibility (Blauwendraat et al., 2020; Chang et al., 2017; Global Parkinson’s Genetics Program (GP2) and Leonard, 2025; Grenn et al., 2020; Nalls et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2023). 27 genes have been reported to be associated with either autosomal dominant (ATXN2, CHCHD2, DNAJC13, EIF4G1, GBA, GCH1, GIGYF2, HTRA2, LRRK2, RIC3, SNCA, TMEM230, VPS35, UCHL1,) or autosomal recessive inheritance (ATP13A2, DJ-1, DNAJC6, FBXO7, PINK1, PLA2G6, PODXL, PRKN, PTRHD1, SPG11, SYNJ1, VPS13C) or an X-linked transmission (RAB39B) (review in Lunati et al., 2018). The most studied PD genes are shown in Fig. 1. GWAS studies in DLB have identified several genes of interest including APOE, BIN1, GBA, MAPT, SNCA, SNCB, TMEM175 (Chia et al., 2021; Goddard et al., 2024; Sanghvi et al., 2020).

The targets and molecular mechanisms of action of environmental factors linked to PD, and the functions of the rare mutations in parkinsonism-inducing genes and risk factors identified by GWAS have allowed to pinpoint some of the cellular mechanisms at stake during the onset and development of PD. Pre-synaptic functions, mitochondrial quality control and mitophagy, inositol pathway, lysosomal and trafficking functions in neurons are key targets commonly disrupted across the different forms of PD (Nguyen et al., 2019). Many genes linked to PD (such as DJ-1, DNAJC6, LRRK2, PINK1, PRKN, SH3GL2, SNCA, SYNJ1) encode proteins involved in synaptic vesicle endocytosis (review in (Schirinzi et al., 2016; Zou et al., 2021)). Dysfunction of in this process precede the loss of dopaminergic neurons. Many environmental intoxicants involved in PD act on mitochondria (Helley et al., 2017).

Dual-specificity, tyrosine phosphorylation-regulated kinase 1A (DYRK1A)

Among the 538 kinases of the human kinome, the dual-specificity, tyrosine phosphorylation-regulated kinases (DYRKs) family comprises 5 members: DYRK1A, DYRK1B, DYRK2, DYRK3, DYRK4 (review in (Lindberg and Meijer, 2021)). DYRKs belong to the CMGC group which also includes cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAP kinases), glycogen synthase kinases (GSKs) and Cdc2-like kinases (CLKs). DYRK1A has attracted considerable attention because of its implication in many central nervous system (CNS) human diseases (Down syndrome (DS), Alzheimer’s disease (AD), etc.) and non-CNS diseases (various infectious diseases, myocardial infarction, diabetes, various solid cancers and leukaemia, etc. (reviews in (de Souza et al., 2023; Deboever et al., 2022; Gehlot et al., 2024; Karati et al., 2025; Lindberg and Meijer, 2021; Liu et al., 2022d; Murphy et al., 2024a; Yang et al., 2023)).

DYRK1A gene, transcripts and kinase isoforms

The DYRK1A gene (160,786 bases) is located on chromosome 21 in the so-called Down Syndrome Critical Region (DSCR) at 21q22.13 (genomic coordinates: chr21:37,365,573-37,526,358) (Song et al., 1996). The gene can potentially lead to 7 transcript variants which translate into 5 possible isoforms (Fig. 2). Their molecular weights are 85.6, 84.6, 66.1, 60.3 and 81.6 kDa, respectively. Western blots of crude cell/tissue extracts with anti-DYRK1A antibodies usually reveal 3-4 bands in the 80-85 kDa range. In addition DYRK1A can undergo proteolytic cleavage leading to low molecular weight forms (LMW-DYRK1A) (∼50 kDa) which exhibit enhanced catalytic activity and stability (Jin et al., 2015a; Moreau et al., 2022; Souchet et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2023a; Hogrel et al., unpublished) (see below).

Structural organization, post-translational modifications and interactors of DYRK1A

The structure of DYRK1A (UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot: Q13627) comprises a central catalytic domain flanked by N-terminal (NTD) and C-terminal (CTD) domains (Fig. 2).

The NTD comprises a binding site for the scaffolding protein DCAF7 (DDB1 and CUL4 Associated Factor 7) (= WDR68, WD40-repeat protein 68), a first nuclear localization signal (NLS1), and a DYRK homology box signature (DH).

The catalytic domain is characterized by an autophosphorylation site (Ser97) (Himpel et al., 2001; Kii et al., 2016; Miyazaki et al., 2022), a conserved lysine which is essential for catalytic activity (Lys188) (mutation to an Arginine (K188R) leads to a kinase-inactive protein), a phosphorylation site (Ser258) required for activity (Lee et al., 2021b) (this site is dephosphorylated by the PPM1B phosphatase), an activating autophosphorylation site (Tyr321) (Himpel et al., 2001; Kentrup et al., 1996; Lochhead et al., 2005; Soundararajan et al., 2013) (mutation to a Phenylalanine (Y321F) leads to a kinase-inactive protein), a proline (Pro380) which should be hydroxylated by the PHD1 prolyl hydroxylase to allow the activating tyrosine autophosphorylation (Lee et al., 2020b), a second nuclear localization signal (NLS2) and an undefined site for binding FAM53C (family with sequence similarity 53 member C), a factor which anchors DYRK1A in the cytoplasm and suppresses its kinase activity (Hammond et al., 2024; Miyata and Nishida, 2023). SPRED1 and SPRED2 bind to the kinase domain of DYRK1A, inhibiting DYRK1A kinase activity towards Tau and STAT3 (by competition with substrate binding) (Li et al., 2010). SPREDs (Sprouty-related protein with an EVH1 (enabled/vasodilatator-stimulated phosphoprotein homology-1) domain) are known as negative regulators of Ras-MAPK signal transduction (Lorenzo and McCormick, 2020).

The CTD contains a PEST sequence (enriched in proline, glutamic acid, serine, threonine) providing a signal for degradation, a 14-3-3 binding domain (Alvarez et al., 2007; Kim et al., 2004), a phosphorylation site (Ser520) required for 14-3-3 binding (Alvarez et al., 2007), a stretch of 13 consecutive histidines unique to DYRK1A and absent from all other DYRKs, responsible for the targeting of DYRK1A to nuclear speckles (Alvarez et al., 2003) and binding of polyphosphates (polyP) (Neville et al., 2023) (this natural His-tag allows the purification of DYRK1A by immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC)). 86 human proteins containing stretches of 5 or more histidines have been identified (Salichs et al., 2009)) and a serine/threonine-rich domain (S/T). SPRY2 (Sprouty 2), a negative regulator of multiple receptor tyrosine kinases, interacts with the polyhistidine stretch of DYRK1A, and both proteins colocalize in synaptosomal plasma membranes (Aranda et al., 2008). Furthermore, SPRY2 is phosphorylated on Thr75 by DYRK1A.

The binding site for FAM117A and FAM117B (family with sequence similarity member 117A and 117B), two other frequently reported interactors (Ananthapadmanabhan et al., 2023; Courraud et al., 2025; Guard et al., 2019; Menon et al., 2019; Varjosalo et al., 2013) (Deau et al., 20261) (see MAP1B and PLK2 sections), has not been identified.

DYRK1A expression in body, brain, substantia nigra

DYRK1A mRNA is expressed in all tissues with low specificity (https://www.gtexportal.org/home/gene/DYRK1A). Based on the Human Protein Atlas (single-cell variation in mRNA expression) DYRK1A mRNA has low human brain regional specificity. It is enriched in the following CNS cell types: microglial cells, inhibitory neurons, excitatory neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocyte precursor cells, oligodendrocytes (https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000157540-DYRK1A/single+cell) (Karlsson et al., 2021). The GPL1261 data shows that DYRK1A mRNA is highly expressed in dopaminergic neurons.

At the protein expression level, the ProteomicsDB database shows that protein levels of DYRK1A protein expression do not show any particular tissue specificity (https://www.ProteomicsDB.org) (Samaras et al., 2020).

The Allan Brain Atlas (https://portal.brain-map.org/gene-expression) provides information on the expression of DYRK1A in human and mouse brains. DYRK1A shows a widespread expression pattern in adult mice, and it is present in the SN (Martí et al., 2003). DYRK1A expression was investigated by immunofluorescence in the human brain (Wegiel et al., 2004): “In the substantia nigra, Mnb1/Dyrk1A was almost undetectable in melanin-positive neurons in pars compacta and reticulata. However, in all adults, numerous immunopositive corpora amylacea were found in variable amounts. Their number was strikingly high (more than 50 per test field) in the pars reticulata in some adults starting from the age of 33 years… The amount of Mnb/Dyrk1A-positive corpora amylacea increased in subependymal, subpial and perivascular locations in aged people as well as in CA1 sector of the cornu ammonis, fimbria hippocampi, molecular layer of the dentate gyrus, caudate nucleus, thalamus, cerebellar molecular and granule cell layer, and in the dentate nucleus in cerebellum. In the majority of aged people, the pars reticulata in the substantia nigra was very rich in Mnb/Dyrk1A positive corpora amylacea…. Increase in the number of Mnb/Dyrk1A-positive astrocytes in all brain subdivisions examined in aged people and accumulation of numerous corpora amylacea in such structures as the substantia nigra (known to be affected by age associated neuronal loss) [23,33,36] may suggest that Mnb/ Dyrk1A is involved in aging of both neurons and astrocytes”. These experiments clearly need to be repeated. To our knowledge, the expression pattern, and levels of DYRK1A, and its two major regulators, DCAF7 and FAM53C, have not been studied in brain samples from PD and DLB patients.

A recent study reports an increased level of DYRK1A in the plasma of PD patients versus healthy controls (402.00 + 56.38 ng/mL versus 320.95 + 50.93 ng/mL, respectively), correlating with the plasma and erythrocyte levels of SNCA and phosphorylated SNCA (Cai et al., 2025).

DYRK1A intracellular distribution

The Protein Human Atlas project localizes DYRK1A to nucleoplasm, nuclear speckles and cytosol based on immunofluorescence staining of the human cervical cancer cell line SiHa. Initial studies showed that DYRK1A is a nuclear protein, a consequence of its two NLS (Becker et al., 1998). It accumulates at the level of nuclear splicing speckles, a consequence of a sequence motif located C-terminal to the kinase domain and comprising the poly-histidine stretch (Alvarez et al., 2003; Salichs et al., 2009).

Subcellular fractionation of human and mouse brain reveals that almost 80% of DYRK1A is associated with the cytoskeleton in complex with filamentous actin, neurofilaments, and tubulin while the remaining 20% is distributed between the cytosolic and nuclear fractions (Kaczmarski et al., 2014). Notably, DYRK1A is particularly enriched in synaptic fractions (Martí et al., 2003). In the transgenic mouse model Tg(Dyrk1a) that overexpresses DYRK1A, a ∼1.5-fold increase of the kinase is detected in the cytosol and synaptosomes but not in the nuclear fraction compared to the wild-type (WT) brain fractions (Nguyen et al., 2018b). A large fraction of DYRK1A is associated with filamentous actin, neurofilaments, and tubulin (Dowjat et al., 2012, 2007; Kaczmarski et al., 2014). The DYRK1A inhibitory FAM53C protein contributes to the cytosolic localisation of DYRK1A (Miyata and Nishida, 2023). Nuclear DYRK1A promotes nuclear F-actin assembly, supporting double-strand break repair (Li et al., 2024). This effect is dependent on Ser482 phosphorylation of actin nucleator spire homolog 1 (SPIR1) by DYRK1A (Li et al., 2024). In contrast, DYRK1A negatively regulates cytoplasmic actin polymerization through modulation/phosphorylation of GLI1, ABLIM1/2 and N-WASP (neural Wiskott-Alrich syndrome protein) (see section on WASL)(Elagib et al., 2022; Park et al., 2012; Schneider et al., 2015).

Genetic evidence for the implication of DYRK1A in Parkinson’s disease

Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) - polymorphism

Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) refers to the modification of a single nucleotide at a genomic locus (coding or non-coding region), found in a population at a significant frequency (>1%). It is the most frequent type of mutations in the human genome, occurring once every several hundred base pairs. These variations can impact promoter activity and thus gene expression, as well as modify conformation, stability and localization of both mRNAs and proteins, ultimately playing a role in the development of diseases (review in (Shastry, 2009)).

The National Health Institute library of SNPs (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp) reports (as of March 3, 2026) 73,692 SNPs in DYRK1A, 135 known as benign, 444 as likely benign, 56 as likely pathogenic and 143 as pathogenic. Some have been linked with PD (Fig. 3).

Although over twenty-seven gene mutations have been reported to cause PD, they only account for around 10-15% of all PD cases. To better understand the aetiology of PD, many GWAS have been undertaken in large numbers of patients and matching controls with the aim of drawing a map of genetic variability at a very fine scale and to identify genes that predispose to or protect from PD.

One of the first large GWAS (37,688 PD cases, 18,618 proxy-cases and 1,417,791 controls) highlights the contribution of 90 variants within 78 genetic regions to genetic susceptibility to PD, that could explain up to 22% of the disorder's heritability in individuals of European ancestry (Chang et al., 2017; Nalls et al., 2019). rs2248244 (position 37,480,059) is one of those variants and DYRK1A was nominated as the likeliest candidate gene at this locus (Grenn et al., 2020). rs2248244 was later also found to be associated with risk of sporadic PD also in a Chinese population (Zheng et al., 2021). rs11701722-T (position 37,398,228) is also associated with PD and DYRK1A maps to this locus (Grenn et al., 2020; Smeland et al., 2021). rs8126696 (position 37,358,422) and rs2835740 (position 37,436,771) at DYRK1A were initially proposed as a risk marker for AD (Kimura et al., 2007a), but subsequent studies in diverse larger populations did not consistently reproduce these findings (Li et al., 2008; Reiman et al., 2007; Vázquez-Higuera et al., 2009). The association between the presence of rs8126696 and an elevated risk for SNCA dementias (PD and DLB) is however found in a pilot study in a European population, particularly among individuals with the TT genotype (Jones et al., 2012). An association of the DYRK1A rs8126696T allele with the age of onset of PD is also seen in 297 Chinese patients (Fan et al., 2016). An additional study in the Chinese Han population identifies the rs8126696T SNP as a possible risk factor for developing sporadic PD, especially in men, whereas rs2835740T/C polymorphism tends to be protective (Cen et al., 2016). Interestingly, interaction between the DYRK1A rs8126696 CT genotype and the ApoE ε4 (+) haplotype is a protective factor for amnestic mild cognitive impairment (aMCI) in a Chinese population (Liu et al., 2012). rs8126696 with CDE2AP is associated with an increased risk for PD and PD with dementia in a Chinese cohort (Fang et al., 2018). A more recent GWAS study carried out by the Global Parkinson’s Genetics Program (GP2; http://gp2.org/) in European populations with 63,555 cases, 17,700 proxy cases with a family history of PD, and 1,746,386 controls, identified 134 risk loci among which DYRK1A was confirmed (variant rs11701836; position 37,519,947) (Global Parkinson’s Genetics Program (GP2) and Leonard, 2025). Another recent GWAS study carried out with 49,049 PD cases, 18,618 proxy cases (first-degree relative with PD) and 2,458,063 neurologically-healthy controls, confirms DYRK1A as a risk locus (rs2248244) (Kim et al., 2024b).

Due to linkage disequilibrium (non-random association of alleles at different loci within a population), it is often difficult to identify the causal SNP(s) (Pasaniuc and Price, 2017). Since SNPs can act on very distant genes (Brodie et al., 2016), it is also difficult to know the exact consequences on proteins, in terms of expression level, activity, localization, interactions with partner proteins, etc... Especially as their roles may vary from one tissue to another. Tools to validate the consequences of a given SNP are needed to improve the accuracy of SNP-trait associations.

Fine-mapping of GWAS-derived PD susceptibility loci investigated several DYRK1A SNPs (Schilder and Raj, 2022). Although not a lead GWAS SNP, rs11088398 (position 37,452,657) is identified as a potentially causal SNP since it is located within a neuron-specific enhancer and has direct interactions with the upstream promoter of DYRK1A. In contrast, rs2248244 does not fall within any brain cell type-specific or regulatory region (Schilder and Raj, 2022). A PD GWAS locus browser was developed to provide help in the prioritization of PD risk loci and causal genes (https://github.com/neurogenetics/GWAS_locus_browser) (Grenn et al., 2020). The DYRK1A-associated risk variant rs2248244 was used to exemplify the potential of this platform (Grenn et al., 2020). The polygenic priority score (PoPS) allows prioritization of genetic risk factors from GWAS data (Lange et al., 2024). Its use with one of the largest PD GWAS (Nalls et al., 2019) allowed the prioritization of 46 PD genes, among which DYRK1A is presented as one of the 6 promising drug targets for PD (Lange et al., 2024).

In brief, several single nucleotide polymorphism variants in the DYRK1A gene have been shown to be linked to PD. Large GWAS studies have identified DYRK1A as a risk factor gene locus for PD and suggest that this kinase represents a promising therapeutic drug target.

DYRK1A regulation by miRNAs

Micro-RNAs (miRNAs) are small, 20-24 nucleotide-long, non-coding mRNA molecules that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally by binding to the 3’ untranslated region (3’UTR) of target messenger RNAs (mRNAs), thereby blocking or favouring their translation or promoting their degradation. The human genome probably encodes several tens of thousands of miRNAs and each miRNA targets an average of 100-200 genes (Gao et al., 2025a). Many miRNAs are upregulated or downregulated in plasma, serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from PD patients and may be involved in the progression of PD. miRNAs have been linked to SNCA aggregation, modulation of neuroinflammation, mitochondrial functions, oxidative stress, autophagy, etc. (reviews in (Aaluri et al., 2024; Guévremont et al., 2023; Nies et al., 2021; Saadh et al., 2024; Shaheen et al., 2024; Zheng and Chen, 2025)). Some miRNAs appear as neuroprotective while others favour the development of PD.

According to the miRDB database (www.miRDB.org), DYRK1A mRNA is predicted to be the target of 356 miRNAs (Chen and Wang, 2020), including 83 miRNAs with a target prediction score >50 and less than 1,000 predicted targets. Several miRNAs have been demonstrated to interfere with DYRK1A expression in a multitude of settings (Fig. 4). miR-26a-5p is expressed in the APP (swe)/PS1 (ΔE9) (amyloid precursor protein /presenilin 1) transgenic mouse model of AD, and inhibits the expression of the DYRK1A mRNA (Liu et al., 2020). The NFAT transcription factor mediates miR-199b-5p expression which in turn targets the DYRK1A mRNA and downregulates the expression of the kinase, thereby promoting calcineurin/NFAT signalling in an auto-amplification loop (da Costa Martins et al., 2010; Duygu et al., 2017). miR-192-5p attenuates cerebral injury in an acute model of ischemic stroke (MCAO, middle cerebral artery occlusion) and reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines. It binds the DYRK1A mRNA 3’UTR and downregulates DYRK1A expression (He et al., 2023). miR-192-5p is consistently upregulated in plasma and downregulated in postmortem frontal gyrus of PD patients and plasmatic miR-192-5p was identified as one of the two best predictors for cognitive decline in PD (Guévremont et al., 2026). Similarly miR-221-3p negatively regulates DYRK1A expression, reduces high glucose-induced inflammatory response and increases wound healing in diabetic foot ulcer (Hu et al., 2024). Long non-coding RNA myocardial infarction associated transcript (LncRNA MIAT) regulates miR-221-3p (Lang et al., 2022). miR-211-5p is downregulated in the hippocampal dentate gyrus of a rat model of depression, leading to upregulation of its DYRK1A target (Li et al., 2021). This miRNA is downregulated in the hippocampus of an attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) model and confirmed to regulate DYRK1A (Tian et al., 2019). Among the numerous miRNA targets of p53, p63, p73, miR-1246 is a negative regulator DYRK1A, thereby probably modulating the DYRK1A/NFAT pathway in cancer and in DS (Liao et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2022b; Zhang et al., 2011b). The level of miR-1246 is decreased in severe active alopecia areata (AA) patients compared to healthy individuals, with a subsequent increase in DYRK1A levels (Qi et al., 2023). miR-215-5p, which is aberrantly reduced in psoriasis compared to normal skin, inhibits keratinocyte proliferation in a psoriasis-like inflammation model through the downregulation of DYRK1A expression and inhibition of the EGFR signalling pathway (Liu et al., 2021a). miR-215-5p also displays neuroprotective effects in a chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) -induced depression rat model by inhibition of DYRK1A/ASK1 (apoptosis signal regulating kinase 1)/JNK (C-Jun N-terminal kinases) signalling in the CA1 hippocampus region (Shen et al., 2021). Both miR-298 and miR-369-3p downregulate DYRK1A leading to radiosensitivity in colorectal cancer cells (Shen et al., 2024a; Zou et al., 2018). Intracellular tumour-derived miR-378a-3p in bone-metastatic prostate cancer downregulates DYRK1A levels which promotes NFATc1-dependent osteolysis (Wang et al., 2022a).

Micro-RNAs regulating DYRK1A mRNA. Among the numerous potential miRNAs targeting the DYRK1A mRNA, 11 have been validated by in vitro/in vivo assays. These include three miRNAs (in bold) which have been found to play a role in DYRK1A expression in human PD and in animal or cellular models of PD, miR-204-5p, miR-221-3p, miR-1286. miR-204-5p activates DYRK1A expression and shows neurotoxic effects. miR-221-3p inhibits DYRK1A expression and is neuroprotective. miR-1246 inhibits DYRK1A expression. In addition, miR-1246 is predicted to impinge on the expression of FAM53C, a natural modulator/inhibitor of DYRK1A. Long non-coding RNAs (NORAD, MIAT, SNHG1, HOTAIR) inhibit the activity of some of these miRNAs. Wildtype DJ-1, but not mutated DJ-1, positively modulates miR-221-3p. miRNAs in black: inhibition of DYRK1A expression. miRNAs in green: activation of DYRK1A expression.

Three miRNAs, one activating DYRK1A mRNA expression and neurotoxic (miR-204-5p), the two others inhibiting DYRK1A mRNA expression and neuroprotective (miR-221-3p, miR-1246), have been investigated in the context of PD.

miR-204-5p is upregulated in the serum from sporadic PD patients compared to healthy subjects. miR-204-5p is also upregulated in serum and SN of MPTP-treated mice, leading to increased DYRK1A expression in the SN (Chiu et al., 2019). DYRK1A mRNA has three binding sites for miR-204-5p which upregulates both DYRK1A mRNA and protein expression in SH-SY5Y cells. Knock-down of DYRK1A expression and pharmacological inhibition of DYRK1A by harmine (see below) attenuate the neurotoxic effects of miR-204-5p in SH-SY5Y and in primary cultured dopaminergic neurons (Chiu et al., 2019). miR-204-5p is upregulated in an MPP+-treated differentiated PC12 cell model of PD (Talepoor Ardakani et al., 2019). Non-coding RNA activated by DNA damage (NORAD) has been reported as neuroprotective against MPP+-induced damage in neuroblastoma cells (Song et al., 2019). This may be explained by the fact that NORAD acts as a miR-204-5p sponge (Zhou et al., 2020). There are 1114 predicted targets for miR-204-5p in the miRDB database (DYRK1A target score: 87; rank: 241/1114).

Aberrant downregulation of serum miR-221-3p is considered as a biomarker for early PD (Guévremont et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2021; Yao et al., 2023) and as protective in PD (Asci et al., 2013; Li et al., 2018; Oh et al., 2018; Zamanian et al., 2024). miR-221-3p supports neuronal survival and protects from oxidative stress. A study investigated the effects of LncRNA MIAT on MPTP-induced inflammation and oxidative stress in mice (Lang et al., 2022). MPTP and MPP+, respectively in mice brain and in cultured MN9D dopaminergic neuronal cells, trigger increased MIAT expression and inhibition of miR-221-3p expression. MIAT downregulation protects from MPP+ -induced apoptosis and oxidative stress. The proposed mechanism involves direct binding of miR-221-3p to MIAT. Similarly, the LncRNAs SNHG1 (small nucleolar RNA host gene 1) (Qian et al., 2019) and HOTAIR (HOX transcript antisense RNA) (Lang et al., 2020) are upregulated in cellular and animal models of PD and negatively regulate miR-221-3p. Loss-of-function mutations in the DJ-1 gene are linked to autosomal recessive inherited PD. DJ-1 knockdown in SH-SY5Y cells and in mice leads to reduced miR-221 expression (Oh et al., 2018). The mutated form of DJ-1 is unable to modulate miR-221 expression. Adenovirus-mediated overexpression of miR-221-3p in 6-OHDA-treated mice reduces the loss of dopaminergic neurons, increases antioxidant capacities in SN, downregulates apoptosis and improves locomotor functions (Yao et al., 2023). There are 615 predicted targets for miR-221-3p in the miRDB database (DYRK1A target score: 52; rank: 581/615).

A comprehensive miRNome-wide profiling of α-synuclein-overexpressing Lund human mesencephalic neurons (LUHMES) reveals that miR-1246 is down-regulated compared to matching GFP-overexpressing LUHMES (Findeiss et al., 2021) (among 23 altered miRNAs: 11 are downregulated & 12 are upregulated). There are 407 predicted targets for miR-1246 in the miRDB database (DYRK1A target score: 83; rank: 40/407). Interestingly, the first predicted target of miR-1246 is FAM53C, a natural DYRK1A interactor guiding DYRK1A intracellular localization and a potential inhibitor (FAM53C target score: 99; rank: 1/407). The impact of miR-1246 on FAM53C expression and DYRK1A activity is unknown.

The MIR155HG gene is located on chromosome 21 (Hsa21). It encodes miR-155-5p, a micro-RNA which is upregulated in numerous pathological processes including DS, cancer, leukaemia, immunity, neuroinflammation, neurodegeneration (reviews in (Elton et al., 2013; Juźwik et al., 2019)). Several articles point to the involvement of miR-155-5p in neuroinflammation associated with PD (Caggiu et al., 2018; Feng et al., 2019; Guo et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2023b; Lee et al., 2023; Lv et al., 2020). A comprehensive mouse brain miRNA atlas recently identified a sex-independent, age-related microglial driven increase in miR-155-5p (Engel et al., 2025). Computational analyses identify miR-155-5p as a potential regulator of DYRK1A expression (Sinha and Yadav, 2026). This potentially interesting result requires experimental validation.

In brief, the expression of multiple micro-RNAs is altered in PD, providing a complex PD micro-RNA signature. Numerous miRNAs target the expression of DYRK1A, three of which are linked to PD, globally favouring an increased expression and activity of DYRK1A. miRNA-204-5p activates DYRK1A mRNA, is neurotoxic and is overexpressed in PD models and patients. miR-221-3p inhibits DYRK1A mRNA, is neuroprotective and is downregulated in PD models and patients. miR-1246 inhibits DYRK1A mRNA and is downregulated in a cell model of PD. miR-1246 may also inhibit (?) FAM53C mRNA, leading to the downregulation of the expression of this DYRK1A modulator. It should be reminded that these three miRNAs target many other mRNAs besides the DYRK1A mRNA.

DYRK1A phosphorylates key players in Parkinson’s disease

Over 50 substrates have been described for DYRK1A (Atas-Ozcan et al., 2021). Many are associated with synapses and with AD. At least 20 key players in PD (and DLB) are phosphorylated by DYRK1A: AMPH, DYN1, CASP9, FOXO, GSK3β, MAP1B, MAPT, MEF2D, NFAT, TP53, PRKN, PLK2, RABs, RCAN1, SEPT4, SYNJ1, SNCA, STAT3, TOM70, WASL.

Amphiphysin I (AMPH) - dynamin I (DYN1)

Amphiphysin I and dynamin I interact and play a key role in clathrin-mediated endocytosis of synaptic vesicles (reviews in (Tomizawa et al., 2003; Wu et al., 2009)).

Amphiphysin I (695 amino acids, 76.3 kDa) is found predominantly in the nervous system at the presynaptic level (reviews in (Wigge and McMahon, 1998; Wu et al., 2009)). Amphiphysin comprises 5 domains (Fig. 5): (1) a BAR (Bin/Amphiphysin/Rvs) domain, (2) a proline-rich domain (PRD), (3) a clathrin adaptor protein complex 2 (AP-2) -binding domain (CLAP), (4) an alternative splicing region (AS), (5) a C-terminal src homology 3 (SH3) domain. The primary function of amphiphysin is to recruit dynamin to sites of clathrin-mediated endocytosis, to stimulate its GTPase activity and to facilitate membrane curvature. The N-terminal domain binds amphiphysin II, CDK5, phospholipase D and lipid membranes (phosphatidylinositol (4,5) bisphosphate (PI(4,5)P2)). The PRD binds endophilin. The CLAP region binds clathrin and AP-2. The C-terminal SH3 domain binds to proline-rich domains of dynamin, synaptojanin and cain (calcineurin inhibitor).

Amphiphysin I: structural organization, phosphorylation sites and consequences of phosphorylation. AS, alternative splicing region; BAR, Bin/Amphiphysin/Rvs domain, CLAP, clathrin adaptor protein-2 (AP-2) binding domain; PRD, proline-rich domain; SH3, src homology 3 domain.

Among the three mammalian dynamin isoforms, dynamin I (864 amino acids, 97.4 kDa) is solely expressed in the brain (reviews in (Khurana and Pucadyil, 2023; Laiman et al., 2023; Schmid and Frolov, 2011)). Dynamin I comprises 5 domains (Fig. 6) (Ford et al., 2011): (1) a GTPase domain, (2) a middle domain (stalk), (3) a pleckstrin homology domain (PH) (binding PI(4,5)P2), (4) a GTPase effector domain (GED) and (5) a PRD. Binding of phosphoinositides leads to increased GTPase activity.

Dynamin I: structural organization, phosphorylation sites and consequences of phosphorylation. GED, GTPase effector domain; PH, pleckstrin homology domain; PRD, proline-rich domain.

The involvement of dynamin I and amphiphysin I in PD stems from two major sets of data:

(1) Endocytic trafficking is dysfunctional in PD (review in (Ng and Cao, 2024; Singh and Muqit, 2020)). Several disease models derived from PD-associated mutations (DNAJ6, SNJ1) that alter endocytic pathway phenocopy the PD pathology. Other proteins involved in clathrin-mediated endocytosis (cyclin G-associated kinase (GAK), endophilin A1, SAC2 (phosphoinositide 4-phosphatase), and synaptotagmin-11) have been identified in GWAS studies as PD risk factors. Key regulators like amphiphysin I and dynamin I are thus essential.

(2) Leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2) interacts with dynamin I (Stafa et al., 2014). This interaction is downregulated in the presence of the LRRK2-G2019S mutation (Connor-Robson et al., 2019). Dopaminergic neurons derived from LRRK2-R1441G patients show decreased levels of synaptic vesicle endocytosis proteins (including auxilin, AP-2, endophilin A1, synaptojanin 1, and dynamin I) (Nguyen and Krainc, 2018). LRRK2 phosphorylates endophilin A on Ser75, impeding synaptic vesicle endocytosis (Matta et al., 2012).

Both proteins are phosphorylated at multiple sites by several kinases (Fig. 6). Amphiphysin I is phosphorylated by various kinases (reviews in (Craft et al., 2008; Wu et al., 2009)): CDK5/p25 (Ser262, Ser272, Ser276, Ser285, Thr310) (Floyd et al., 2001; Tomizawa et al., 2003), CDKL5 (cyclin-dependent kinase-like 5) (Ser285, Ser293) (Katayama et al., 2016), CSNK2 (casein kinase 2) (Thr350, Thr387) (Döring et al., 2006), DYRK1A (Ser293) (Murakami et al., 2012, 2006). Amphiphysin I Ser293 phosphorylation regulates endophilin A1 binding and synaptic vesicle endocytosis (Kontaxi et al., 2023). DYRK1A phosphorylates amphiphysin I on Ser293, leading to inhibition of endophilin binding (Murakami et al., 2006).

Dynamin I is phosphorylated by various kinases (review in (Smillie and Cousin, 2005)): CDK5/p25 (Ser774, Ser778, Ser780) (Tan et al., 2003; Tomizawa et al., 2003), DYRK1A (Ser778, Ser795, Ser851, Ser857) (Chen-Hwang et al., 2002; Huang et al., 2004a; Liu et al., 2017b), ERK2 (extracellular signal -regulated kinase 2) (undetermined site) (Earnest et al., 1996), GSK3 (Ser 774) (Ferreira et al., 2021), and PKC (protein kinase C) (Ser795) (Powell et al., 2000; Robinson et al., 1993). Endophilin A1 binds to two distinct sites on Dynamin I: phosphobox-1 (containing Ser774, Ser778) and phosphobox-2 (containing Ser851, Ser857). Dephosphorylation of the dynamin 1xA splice variant at Ser851 and Ser857 favours binding to endophilin A1, mediating ultra-fast endocytosis (Imoto et al., 2024). DYRK1A phosphorylates several other clathrin coated vesicle -associated proteins (Murakami et al., 2012). DYRK1A phosphorylates dynamin I, thereby modulating binding to the SH3 domain of amphiphysin I, to endophilin 1 and to GRB2 (growth factor receptor-bound protein 2) (Chen-Hwang et al., 2002). Ser778, Ser795, and, mostly, Ser857 in dynamin I are phosphorylated by DYRK1A (Huang et al., 2004a). Ser857 phosphorylation reduces binding of amphiphysin while it enhances GRB2 binding (Huang et al., 2004a).

In brief, amphiphysin I and dynamin I are key regulators of clathrin-mediated endocytosis of synaptic vesicles which is compromised in PD. Both proteins are phosphorylated by DYRK1A, resulting in the modulation of interaction with other partners. The effects of DYRK1A inhibition are unknown.

Caspase-9 (CASP9)

The human cysteine-dependent, aspartate-specific proteases family of caspases comprises 12 members (reviews in (Kesavardhana et al., 2020; Nadendla et al., 2025; Sahoo et al., 2023)). They are usually classified as apoptotic caspases (caspase -3, -6, -7, -8, -9), inflammatory caspases (caspase-1, -4, -5, -12) and less characterized caspases (-2, -10, -14). They play a multitude of roles including in cell death, development, innate immunity, autophagy, and numerous diseases.

Caspase -9 (416 amino acids, 46.2 kDa) is an initiator caspase, its main function is to activate downstream executioner caspases (-3, -7, -8) (reviews in (Avrutsky and Troy, 2021; Li et al., 2017)). Caspase-9 is activated by mitochondrial cytochrome c released in response to various apoptotic signals. Cytochrome c induces the oligomerization of APAF-1 (apoptotic peptidase activating factor 1) and the recruitment of pro-caspase-9 into the multimeric apoptosome complex. APAF-1 induced dimerization of pro-caspase-9 leads to autocatalytic processing and activation of the proteolytic activity. Caspase-9 is also involved in non-apoptotic functions. Caspase-9 function is required for normal CNS development. It is implied in multiple diseases (Huntington’s disease, AD, retinal neuropathy, lumbar disc disease, myasthenic disorders, immune and cardiovascular pathologies, etc.) (review in (Avrutsky and Troy, 2021)). Caspase-9 comprises two main domains: (1) an N-terminal caspase activation recruitment domain (CARD) (binding to APAF-1), and (2) a catalytic domain (large and small domains separated by an inter-subunit linker) (Fig. 7).

Caspase-9: structural organization, phosphorylation sites and consequences of phosphorylation. DYRK1A phosphorylates Thr125, leading to caspase-9 inactivation. Most phosphorylations results in caspase-9 inactivation. Apaf-1, apoptotic peptidase activating factor 1; CARD, caspase activation recruitment domain.

The involvement of caspase-9 in PD stems from three sets of data:

(1) cytochrome C release, and activation of caspase-9 and downstream caspase-3 and -8, are observed in the SN of MPTP-treated mice (Viswanath et al., 2001). These effects are attenuated in mice overexpressing the general caspase inhibitor p35, thereby providing some resistance to MPTP. These results are confirmed in PC12 cells and mesencephalic dopaminergic cells exposed to MPP+. Activated caspases-9 and -8 are observed in dopaminergic nigral neurons from MPTP-treated mice, as well as from late-onset sporadic PD patients (Viswanath et al., 2001).

(2) Adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated SNCA overexpression in rat SN leads to caspase-9 activation and ∼50% loss of dopaminergic neurons (Yamada et al., 2004). In contrast loss of dopaminergic neurons in a 6-OHDA rat model of PD does not seem to involve the activation of caspase -9 and -3 (Ebert et al., 2008).

(3) multiple examples show that neuroprotective effects of various compounds in PD models are associated with reduced caspase-9 activation (see (Liang et al., 2024; Salama et al., 2020) for examples; review in (Erekat, 2018)).

Cytochrome C-dependent activation of caspase-9, and downstream caspases may contribute to dopaminergic apoptotic cell death in PD, along with other cell death mechanisms (reviews in (Erekat, 2018; Pedrão et al., 2024)).

Caspase-9 is phosphorylated by numerous kinases, leading in essentially all cases in caspase-9 inactivation (reviews in (Allan and Clarke, 2009; La and Pr, 2009)) (Fig. 7). The phosphorylating kinases comprise c-ABL (Abelson kinase) (Tyr397) (Serrano et al., 2017), AKT (?) (Ser196) (Fujita et al., 1999), CDK1/cyclin B1 (Thr125) (Allan and Clarke, 2007), CSNK2 (casein kinase 2) (Ser348) (McDonnell et al., 2008), DYRK1A (Thr125) (Laguna et al., 2008; Seifert et al., 2008; Seifert and Clarke, 2009), ERK1/2 (Thr125) (Allan et al., 2003; Martin et al., 2008), PKA (cAMP-dependent protein kinase) (Ser99, Ser183, Ser195) (Martin et al., 2005; Serrano and Hardy, 2018), PKC-ζ (Ser144) (Brady et al., 2005), polo-like kinase 1 (PLK1) (Ser196) (Liao et al., 2022).

DYRK1A phosphorylates caspase-9 on Thr125 and inhibits its auto-processing activation (Laguna et al., 2008; Seifert et al., 2008). This phosphorylation is prevented by siRNA directed against DYRK1A but not against DYRK1B, and by the DYRK1A inhibitor harmine (Seifert et al., 2008). Caspase-9 phosphorylation by DYRK1A occurs in the nucleus. Caspase-9 phosphorylation protects retina cells from apoptosis, and this regulation may be altered in DS patients, who frequently show visual impairments (Laguna et al., 2008). Harmine increases the number of caspase-9 and -3 positive retinal cells. Triplication of the Dyrk1a gene in mice provided some protection against apoptosis in the retina and increased retinal cell numbers (Laguna et al., 2008).

In brief, activation of caspase-9 is associated with PD. DYRK1A phosphorylates caspase-9 on Thr215, leading to inactivation of the protease, and reduced activity of the downstream effector caspases. DYRK1A inhibition is expected to increase caspase-9 catalytic activity. It should be noted that this site is phosphorylated by other kinases.

Forkhead box proteins O (FOXO)

The family of FOXO (Forkhead box proteins O) transcription factors comprises 4 members in humans: FOXO1 (FKHR (forkhead in rhabdomyosarcoma), 655 amino acids, 69.7 kDa), FOXO3 (FKHRL1, 673 amino acids, 71.3 kDa), FOXO4 (505 amino acids, 53.7 kDa), FOXO6 (492 amino acids, 50.6 kDa) (reviews in (Asadi et al., 2025; Calissi et al., 2021; Gui and Burgering, 2022; Orea-Soufi et al., 2022; Santos et al., 2023)). The four FOXO sequences are highly divergent except for (1) a central forkhead/DNA-binding domain, (2) nuclear localization and nuclear export signals (NLS, NES), (3) a C-terminal transactivation domain (TAD) and (4) three short conserved regions (CR1, CR2, CR3) (Fig. 8). FOXOs are subject to numerous post-translational modifications including phosphorylations (see below). The forkhead box recognizes two different DNA-response elements, DAF-16 binding element (DBE) and insulin-responsive element (IRE) (reviews in (Asadi et al., 2025; Calissi et al., 2021; Gui and Burgering, 2022; Orea-Soufi et al., 2022; Santos et al., 2023)).

FOXO1: structural organization, phosphorylation sites and consequences of phosphorylation. DYRK1A phosphorylates FOXO1 on Ser329, leading to nuclear exclusion/cytoplasmic retention, and down-regulation of FOXO1 transcriptional activity. DYRK1B phosphorylates FOXO1 on Ser329 and on Thr467 and Ser468. DBD, DNA-binding domain; FHD, forkhead domain; NES, nuclear export signal; NLS, nuclear localization signal; TAD, transactivation domain. Blue lines: conserved regions CR1 (8-35), CR2 (294-314), CR3 (596-633).

FOXOs are involved in multiple cellular homeostasis functions including oxidative stress response, mitochondrial functions, cell proliferation, autophagy, DNA damage repair, stem cell maintenance, genomic stability, senescence, longevity, etc.

FOXOs are involved in neuronal cell functions and in neurodegenerative diseases (reviews in (Asadi et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2022b; Orea-Soufi et al., 2022; Santo and Paik, 2018)). The involvement of FOXOs in PD is supported by several sets of data:

(1) overexpression of constitutively active FOXO3 in rat SN leads to apoptotic loss of dopaminergic neurons while high dose of a dominant-negative FOXO3 form induces oxidative damage (Pino et al., 2014). Inhibition of FOXO3-mediated transcription has protective effects in dopaminergic neurons overexpressing human SNCA (Pino et al., 2014). Moderate FOXO3 expression is protective against SNCA accumulation, it contributes to the clearance of soluble SNCA. FOXO3 thus plays a dual role in SN neuronal survival, displaying both pro-apoptotic and protective functions depending on its expression level.

(2) Enhanced FOXO1 expression and increased transcription of FOXO1-dependent genes are observed in the prefrontal cortex of PD patients (Dumitriu et al., 2012). FOXO3 (Su et al., 2009) and FOXO1 (Miki et al., 2018) are found in LBs in PD and DLB brain tissue.

(3) LRRK2 directly phosphorylates FOXO1 on Ser319 and activates its transcriptional activity, especially triggering the production of the pro-apoptotic protein BIM (Kanao et al., 2010). Nitric oxide -activated cyclic GMP (guanosine monophosphate) -dependent kinase II (cGKII or PRKG2 or PKG2 guanosine monophosphate) also phosphorylates FOXO1 at the same site and enhances its transcriptional activity, leading to neurodegeneration (Kanao et al., 2012). A kinase-resistant mutant (S319A) of FOXO in Drosophila suppresses neurotoxicity induced by co-expression of FOXO and DG2, a Drosophila cGK (Kanao et al., 2012). In contrast, in Drosophila, LRRK2 appears to phosphorylate (Ser473) and activate AKT which phosphorylates and inhibits FOXO1 (Chuang et al., 2014).

(4) FOXO3a regulates the expression of PINK1 (phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN)-induced putative kinase 1) (LoF mutations of this pro-survival factor are associated with familial forms of PD) (Mei et al., 2009). Similarly, DJ-1 (LoF mutations lead to early-onset PD) binds to FOXO3 and directly interacts with the PINK1 gene promoter to stimulate its transcriptional activity (Requejo-Aguilar et al., 2015). PINK1 plays a role in detecting mitochondrial damage, then initiating a signalling cascade leading to activation of Parkin, and induction of mitophagy (reviews in (Basak and Holzbaur, 2025a; Nguyen et al., 2016)).

(5) Drosophila FOXO mediates the mitochondrial and dopaminergic neuron protecting signal induced by sirtuin Sir2 (silent information regulator 2) and rescues PINK1 null mutants (Koh et al., 2012).

(6) Point mutations in FBXO7 lead to deficiencies in ubiquitin ligase activity and cause an autosomal recessive form of early-onset PD (Table in Fig. 1). FBXO7 triggers a caspase 8-mediated proteolysis of FOXO4 and reduces the transcription activity of FOXO4 (Lee et al., 2021a). Treatment of dopaminergic MN9D cells by 6-OHDA greatly reduces FOXO4 levels through the action of FBXO7 and caspase 8, leading to neuronal cell death. The PD-associated FBXO7-T22M mutant further increases the instability of FOXO4.

(7) The sirtuin SIRT1 deacetylase is downregulated in mice overexpressing human A53T SNCA, leading to increased FOXO1 acetylation and upregulated expression of monoamine oxidase A (MAO-A), eventually enhancing depressive-like behaviour (Li et al., 2020d).

FOXOs are phosphorylated by numerous kinases, leading to either cytoplasmic or nuclear localization and thus, respectively, to inhibition or activation of transcriptional activity (reviews in (Orea-Soufi et al., 2022; Santos et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2011)) (Fig. 8). Phosphorylation at one site most probably influences phosphorylation at neighbouring sites.

Kinases that promote nuclear exclusion/ cytoplasmic retention and downregulate of transcriptional activity of FOXO1, comprise: AKT (Thr24, Ser256, Ser319) (Thr32, Ser253, Ser315 in FOXO3) (downstream of PI3K (phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase)) (Brunet et al., 1999; Rena et al., 1999) (phosphorylation leads to association with 14-3-3 and cytoplasmic retention), CDK2 (Ser249) (Huang et al., 2006), CDK4 (Ser329) (Lu et al., 2014b), CDK5 (Ser249) (Zhou et al., 2015), CSNK1 (casein kinase 1) (Ser322, Ser325) (Rena et al., 2004, 2002), DYRK1A (Ser329) (Bhansali et al., 2021; Woods et al., 2001b), DYRK1B (Ser329) (Wongchang et al., 2023), NLK (Nemo-like kinase) (some or all of: Ser 287, Ser 298, Ser 329, Ser 383, Ser 394, Ser402, Ser 416, Ser 418) (Ji et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2010), PLK1 (Ser75, Ser218, Ser329) (Moore et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2014).

Kinases that promote nuclear import/ cytoplasmic exclusion and upregulate of transcriptional activity of FOXO1, comprise: AMPK (5'-AMP-activated protein kinase) (Ser22, Ser383, Thr649) (Saline et al., 2019; Yun et al., 2014), CDK1 (Ser249) (Yuan et al., 2008), DYRK1B (Thr467, Ser468) (Li et al., 2025a), ERK/p38 MAPK (p38 mitogen-activated kinase) (Ser249, Ser287, Ser298, Ser329, Ser416, Ser418, Ser432, Ser470, Ser478) (Asada et al., 2007), LRRK2 (Ser319) (Kanao et al., 2010), MST1 (mammalian sterile20-like kinase) (Ser212) (Ser209 in FOXO3) (Lehtinen et al., 2006) (phosphorylation disrupts binding to 14-3-3, leading to cytoplasmic exclusion), PKA (Thr24, Ser256, Ser319) (Lee et al., 2011a), PKG2 (Ser319) (Kanao et al., 2012).

DYRK1A phosphorylates FOXO1 on Ser329 (Bhansali et al., 2021; Woods et al., 2001b), promoting its cytoplasmic accumulation, thus inhibiting its transcriptional activity. This site is conserved in FOXO3 and FOXO4, but not in FOXO6. Interestingly, a kinase-independent effect on FOXO1-dependent glucose-6-phophatase gene expression was demonstrated with a kinase-dead DYRK1A mutant and with a S329A point mutation of the DYRK1A phosphorylation site in FOXO1 (von Groote-Bidlingmaier et al., 2003). Minibrain (Mnb), the DYRK1A homolog gene in Drosophila was initially identified in a RNAi screen as a dFOXO regulator, among 26 other kinases (Mattila et al., 2008). MBK-1, the DYRK1A ortholog in Caenorhabditis elegans, phosphorylates DAF-16, the FOXO ortholog, at Ser326, the amino acid corresponding to Ser329 in human FOXO (Mack et al., 2017). Loss of mbk-1 still allows the nuclear localization of DAF-16, reduces the expression of several DAF-16 target genes and shortens the half-life of mutant and WT (to a lesser extent) animals (Mack et al., 2017). In liver cells, DYRK1B phosphorylates FOXO1 on Thr467 and Ser468, leading to nuclear accumulation and transcription activation (Li et al., 2025a). These sites are conserved in FOXO3 and FOXO4, but not in FOXO6.

AZ191, a DYRK1B inhibitor, leads to FOXO1 cytoplasmic retention, reduces FOXO1-dependent transcription and improves glucose homeostasis (Li et al., 2025b). In contrast, the DYRK1B inhibitor AZ-DYRK1B-33 prevents FOXO1 phosphorylation on Ser329 in CD4 T cells, thereby enhancing FOXO1 transcriptional activity (Wongchang et al., 2023). The CLK2/DYRK1A inhibitor Lorecivivint (see ref. (Lindberg et al., 2023a) for full selectivity characteristics) inhibits FOXO1 phosphorylation in chondrocytes, leading to its nuclear accumulation (Deshmukh et al., 2019). Harmine, a DYRK1A/monoamine oxidase dual inhibitor (Bartholome et al., 2010; Jimenez et al., 2022; Machado et al., 2020), and AnnH75 (Jimenez et al., 2022), a more DYRK1A selective inhibitor (Fig. 31), induce FOXO3 nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity.

Selected pharmacological inhibitors of DYRK1A. Some of the most potent, selective and best characterized inhibitors are shown (see (Lindberg et al., 2023a) for references and comparative analysis). Iso-Leucettinib-21 is a negative control, kinase-inactive isomer of Leucettinib-21 (Lindberg et al., 2023b). IC50 values towards DYRK1A are indicated (from (Lindberg et al., 2023a)).

In brief, although clearly involved in PD, the different FOXO transcription factors appear to display both protective or deleterious effects. FOXO-activating and -inactivating phosphorylations by multiple kinases add another level of complexity. DYRK1A and DYRK1B phosphorylate FOXO1 on Ser329 (a site conserved in FOXO3 and FOXO4), leading to nuclear exclusion and inhibition of FOXO1 transcriptional activity. In contrast DYRK1B phosphorylates FOXO1 on Thr467 and Ser468 (both sites conserved in FOXO3 and FOXO4), leading to nuclear accumulation and increased FOXO3 transcriptional activity. It is thus difficult to predict the outcome of pharmacological DYRK1A/1B inhibition in terms of FOXO nuclear/cytoplasmic localization and transcriptional activities.

Glycogen synthase kinase 3 β (GSK3B)

Glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β) is a Ser/Thr kinase belonging to the CGMC group of kinases (review in (Beurel et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2023a)). Alternative splicing leads to either GSK3β1 (420 amino acids, 46.7 kDa) or GSK3β2 (433 amino acids, 48.0 kDa), the latter being primarily expressed in the CNS (Schaffer et al., 2003).

GSK3β contains (1) an N-terminal lobe (mostly β-sheets) and (2) a C-terminal lobe (α-helices), comprising the glycine-rich loop (G) (P-loop) (ATP-binding pocket), a binding domain (BD) (for substrates and protein complexes), and a C-terminal extension (Dajani et al., 2001) (Fig. 9). The ATP-binding pocket is located between the two lobes. Phosphorylation of Tyr216 leads to kinase activation. In contrast Ser9 phosphorylation is inhibitory, the Ser9-phosphorylated N-terminal domain acting as an inhibitory pseudo-substrate. PINK1 activates AKT through PI3K (Akundi et al., 2012; Furlong et al., 2019), leading to an indirect inhibition of GSK3β. GSK3β is also regulated by interactions with docking proteins such as axin (Dajani et al., 2003; Gavagan et al., 2023; Primot et al., 2000) or 14-3-3 (Agarwal-Mawal et al., 2003). In many instances GSK3β only phosphorylates substrates which have been pre-phosphorylated 4 amino acids C-terminal to the GSK3β phosphorylation site by so-called priming kinases, such as CSKN1, CSNK2, CDK5, JNK, MAPKs, PKA, DYRK1A and DYRK2 (review in (Beurel et al., 2015)). For instance, phosphorylation of Thr212 by DYRK1A/DYRK2 primes Tau for phosphorylation by GSK3 at Ser208 (and Thr205, Ser202, Ser199) (Liu et al., 2008a; Woods et al., 2001a). Similar examples are MAP1B (Scales et al., 2009) (p-Ser1392 (DYRK1A) → p-Ser1388 (GSK3)) or RCAN1 (Jung et al., 2011a) (p-Ser112 (DYRK1A) → p-Ser108 (GSK3)).

GSK3β2: structural organization, phosphorylation sites and consequences of phosphorylation. LRRK2 interacts with and activates GSK3, but independently of LRRK2’s catalytic activity. DYRK1A phosphorylates Thr356, leading to GSK3β inhibition. BD, binding domain; DFG, activation loop (-D200FG-); G, glycine-rich loop (P-loop) (-G63NGSFGVV-); Nt, N-terminal domain; The 13 amino acid insert in GSK3β2 compared to GSK3β1 is indicated.

The involvement of GSK3β in PD is supported by multiple studies (reviews in (Albeely et al., 2018; Demuro et al., 2021; Golpich et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2025b; Samim Khan et al., 2023; Turkistani et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023a)). The pro-oxidative stress, pro-apoptotic, and pro-inflammatory effects of GSK3β have stimulated an active search for GSK3 inhibitors and their evaluation against PD and many other CNS indications (reviews in (Arciniegas Ruiz and Eldar-Finkelman, 2021; Duda et al., 2018; King et al., 2014; Meijer et al., 2004; Morales-García et al., 2013; Turkistani et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2022)).

(1) Substrates of GSK3 which are relevant to PD include β-catenin (Gamit et al., 2023; Marchetti, 2018), HtrA2 (high temperature requirement A2, a mitochondrial serine protease) (Bose et al., 2021; Nam et al., 2023), MEF2D (myocyte enhancer factor 2D) (Hu et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2009), NRF2 (nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2) (Soni and Kumar, 2022), NURR1 (nuclear receptor related factor 1) (García-Yagüe et al., 2021), septin-4 (Koch et al., 2015), synphilin-1 (Avraham et al., 2005; Szargel et al., 2008; Tanji et al., 2003), SNCA (Credle et al., 2015), and Tau (Chakraborty et al., 2024). NURR1, a transcription factor involved in the differentiation and survival of dopaminergic neurons (Al-Nusaif et al., 2022; Dong et al., 2016; Gamit et al., 2023), is down-regulated by SNCA through GSK3 activation. Indeed NURR1 phosphorylation by GSK3 leads to its proteasomal degradation (García-Yagüe et al., 2021). LRRK2 interacts with and activates GSK3, but independently of LRRK2’s catalytic activity (Kawakami et al., 2014; Lin et al., 2010). GSK3 triggers mitochondrial dysfunctions and oxidative stress probably through phosphorylation at Ser344 and Ser347 (with potential priming by an unknown kinase (DYRK1A?) at Ser351 and/or Ser356) (Hayes et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2021b) and subsequent inhibition of the anti-oxidant transcription factor NRF2 (Armagan et al., 2019; Soni and Kumar, 2022). GSK3 is negatively regulated by PINK1 through phosphorylation and activation of PI3K (Akundi et al., 2012; Furlong et al., 2019). GSK3β activates the NF-κB/NLRP3 (nucleotide-binding domain leucine-rich repeat containing receptor 3) inflammasome pathway, contributing to neuronal injury (review in (Khot et al., 2022; Turkistani et al., 2024)).

(2) The ubiquitin ligase SCFFbxo7 (SCF: Skp1, Cullin, F-box complex) is associated with PD. The F-box protein FBXO7 binds to PINK1 and Parkin at the mitochondrial outer membrane to induce mitophagy of dysfunctional mitochondria. Point mutations in FBXO7 (PARK15), leading to deficiencies in ubiquitin ligase activity, are linked to early onset PD (Table in Fig. 1) (reviews in (Deng et al., 2013; Joseph et al., 2018; Zhong et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2018b)). The SCFFbxo7 ubiquitin ligase promotes ubiquitination of GSK3β, leading to a decrease of GSK3β activity (Teixeira et al., 2016).

(3) In vitro. GSK3 mediates dopaminergic cell death induced by various agents as shown in the following few examples. GSK3 is activated and enriched in nuclear fractions in SH-SY5Y cells expressing SNCA and dopamine transporter (DAT), and in mesencephalic neurons treated with MPP+ (Duka et al., 2009). GSK3 inhibitors (lithium, TDZD-8) protect these SH-SY5Y cells from MPP+ -induced damage, SNCA aggregation and cell death (Duka et al., 2009). Similarly, treatment of SH-SY5Y by 6-OHDA leads to GSK3β dephosphorylation on Ser9 and phosphorylation on Tyr216 and cell death, which is prevented by GSK3 inhibitors (Chen et al., 2004). GSK3 inhibitors (lithium, kenpaullone) or GSK3β siRNA protect cultured primary neurons from MPP+-induced cell death (Petit-Paitel et al., 2009). Inhibition of GSK3β decreases SNCA production while PLK2 inhibition and LRRK2 increase α-synuclein in a GSK3β-dependent manner (Kofoed et al., 2020).

(4) In vivo. Similarly, GSK3 appears to mediate the death of dopaminergic neurons in animals exposed to MPTP, as illustrated by the following examples. Administration of MPTP to WT (Wang et al., 2007) or SNCA-overexpressing mice, but not in SNCA-knockout (KO) mice (Duka et al., 2009), leads to increased Tyr216-phosphorylated (activated) GSK3, but not total GSK3, in the striatum. Similarly, Ser9-dephosphorylated (disinhibited) GSK3 increases in dopaminergic neurons from mice treated with MPTP (Li et al., 2020b). Administration of GSK3 inhibitors (indirubin-3’-oxime, AR-A014418) to MPTP-treated mice prevents dopaminergic neurons loss, attenuates the decrease in dopamine level, and corrects motor deficits (Wang et al., 2007). Dopaminergic neuron-specific GSK3B null (GSK3BΔDat) mice, but not GSK3AΔDat mice, show significant resistance to MPTP insult (Li et al., 2020b). The GSK3 inhibitor Tideglusib prevents dopaminergic neurodegeneration and improves motor symptoms in MPTP-treated mice (Li et al., 2020b). Similarly, constitutively active GSK3β (GSK3β-S9A) mice show progressive age-dependent impairments of motor function, loss of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) -positive dopaminergic neurons accompanied by increased phosphorylation of SNCA (Ser129) and Tau (Ser396/404), deterioration of the SN and decreased dopamine production (Credle et al., 2015). Injection of 6-OHDA in rat induces long-term GSK3β activation (Tyr216 phosphorylation) and reduces neural stem cells self-renewal, proliferation and differentiation, migration of neurons and dendritic numbers, length and arborization (Hernandez-Baltazar et al., 2013; Singh et al., 2018), but increases glial differentiation. These effects are counteracted by the administration of the GSK3 inhibitor SB216763 (Singh et al., 2018). Levodopa-induced dyskynesia (LID) is associated with increased expression and reduced Ser9 phosphorylation of GSK3β. GSK3 inhibition by TDZD8 reduces the LID severity in 6-OHDA treated rats (Xie et al., 2016). Altogether the available in vitro and in vivo data suggest that inhibition of GSK3 protect dopaminergic neurons.

(5) GSK3 is present in the cytoplasm of neurons located in the SN and the locus ceruleus of PD patients (Nagao and Hayashi, 2009). pTyr216-GSK3 is increased in the striata of patients with PD relative to age-matched controls (Duka et al., 2009; Wills et al., 2010). Increased GSK3β is observed in peripheral blood lymphocytes from PD patients (Armentero et al., 2011). The link between GSK3β polymorphism and PD risk remains unclear (Kwok et al., 2005; Yuan et al., 2013).

GSK3β is phosphorylated on multiple sites (reviewed in (Krishnankutty et al., 2017; Meijer et al., 2004)) (Fig. 9). GSK3β is phosphorylated by the following kinases: AKT (PKB) (Ser9) (Cross et al., 1995), DYRK1A (Thr356) (Song et al., 2015), ERK (Thr43) (Ding et al., 2005), GSK3β (Tyr216) (Cole et al., 2004; Hughes et al., 1993; Lochhead et al., 2006), MEK1/2 (mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase 1/2) (Tyr216) (Takahashi-Yanaga et al., 2004), PKA (Ser9) (Fang et al., 2000), PKC (Ser9) (Christian et al., 2002), PKCζ (Ser147) (Tejeda-Muñoz et al., 2015), PLK2 (Ser9) (Li et al., 2014a; Lim et al., 2015), PYK2 (Tyr216) (Hartigan et al., 2001; Sayas et al., 2006), p38MAPK (p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase) (Thr390) (Thornton et al., 2008), p70S6K (Ser9) (Sutherland et al., 1993), p90RSK (ribosomal S6 kinase) (Ser9) (Ding et al., 2005), and c-SRC (Tyr216) (Goc et al., 2014).

DYRK1A interacts with and partially inactivates GSK3β by phosphorylation on Thr356 (a phosphorylation defective mutant (T356A) shows reduced GSK3β activity, while a phosphorylation mimic mutant (T356E) displays enhanced GSK3β activity) (Song et al., 2015).

In brief, GSK3β activity globally favours PD. DYRK1A phosphorylates GSK3β on Thr356, leading to partial inhibition of GSK3β’s catalytic activity. In contrast, DYRK1A phosphorylates numerous substrates, favouring their subsequent phosphorylation by GSK3β. DYRK1A inhibition is thus expected to increase GSK3β activity (direct positive effect), or to decrease the phosphorylation by GSK3β of substrates requiring a priming phosphorylation (indirect negative effect). The resulting effects of pharmacological inhibition of DYRK1A may thus depend on the DYRK1A/GSK3 substrate (primed vs. non-primed).

Microtubule-associated protein 1B (MAP1B)

Microtubule-associated protein 1B (MAP1B or MAP5) (2,468 amino acids; 270.6 kDa) is predominantly expressed in neurons where it plays key role in the neuronal cytoskeleton, stabilization of microtubules, neuronal differentiation, axonal and dendritic growth and guidance, axon branching (Ziak et al., 2024) (reviews in (Halpain and Dehmelt, 2006; Riederer, 2007; Villarroel-Campos and Gonzalez-Billault, 2014; Yang et al., 2012)). MAP1B is downstream of multiple neuronal signalling pathways.

MAP1B is first synthesized and then cleaved to an N-terminal heavy chain (HC) and a C-terminal light chain (LC1). Both HC and LC1 comprise an actin-binding domain (ABD) and a a microtubule-binding domain (MBD). The HC contains a microtubule assembly helping site (MTA) (Fig. 10).

LRRK2 binds to and phosphorylates LC1. MAP1B binds to α-synuclein and DJ-1. The Thr1932 site is massively dephosphorylated in the brain of PINK1 KO + A53T-SNCA mice. MAP1B interacts with α-synuclein and DJ-1. ABD, actin-binding domain; HC, heavy chain; LC1, light chain; MBD, microtubule-binding domain; MTA, microtubule assembly helping site.

The involvement of MAP1B in PD and DLB is supported by several sets of data:

(1) MAP1B has been identified as a component of brainstem and cortical LBs. It binds to the CTD of both monomers and aggregates α-synuclein (Gai et al., 1996; Jensen et al., 2000). The MAP1B binding site is located in the CTD of SNCA.

(2) LRRK2 interacts with the light chain (LC1) of MAP1B, resulting in the inhibition of LRRK2 kinase activity (Chan et al., 2014b). Pathogenic LRRK2 mutants (R1441C, G2019S and I2020T) exhibit reduced expression of LC1, but restoring LC1 levels rescues associated with these LRRK2 mutants (Chan et al., 2014b). LRRK2 phosphorylates LC1 (Chan et al., 2014b). Futsch, the Drosophila MAP1B homolog is phosphorylated and negatively regulated by LRRK2 at the pre-synaptic level (Islam et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2010).

(3) Pharmacological inhibition of LRRK2 (by FL090) in a MPTP mouse model and in A53T-SNCA transgenic mice leads to upregulation of MAP1B expression (Chen et al., 2023). Reduced expression of LRRK2 leads to enhanced expression of MAP1B. MAP1B overexpression partially protects N2a cells from MPP+-induced toxicity. The protective effect of LRRK2 inhibition by FL090 is lost in MAP1B-KO HEK-293 cells (Chen et al., 2023).

(4) DJ-1 binds the LC1 fragment of MAP1B and prevents its aggregation (Wang et al., 2011). The DJ-1 LoF L199P mutant or a DJ-1 KO, in cell cultures, no longer prevent MAP1B aggregation. DJ-1 KO mice show more insoluble MAP1B-LC1 than their WT counterparts.

(5) Osteopontin (OPN) is described as a protective factor in PD. Its expression is reduced in surviving SN dopaminergic neurons in PD. OPN protects against MPP+-induced cell death and 6-OHDA-induced cell loss in rat. A yeast two-hybrid screen identified interactions between OPN and MAP1A and MAP1B, which were confirmed in rat brain using affinity pull-down assays (Long et al., 2012).

(6) The double mutant (PINK1 KO + overexpressed A53T-SNCA) PD mouse model displays severe locomotor deficits and reduced lifespan. A phosphoproteomics analysis carried out in brain hemispheres of this model and age/sex-matched WT mice show a striking reduction in the phosphorylation of MAP1B at Thr1928 (corresponding to Thr1932 in humans) (Auburger et al., 2019).

(7) A9-type dopaminergic neurons, derived from human embryonic stem cells (hESCs), either harbouring the A53T-SNCA mutation or isogenic WT controls, were compared in terms of gene expression profiles. The expression profile observed in A53T-SNCA neurons is consistent with the expression profile seen in the SN of PD patients. Both are enriched in antioxidant response elements (ARE) regulatory motifs related to microtubule binding processes and axonal projections. Early α-synuclein accumulation results in loss of NRF2 phosphorylation, reduced NRF2 nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity, and, consequently, in reduced ARE action at the MAP1B gene locus (Czaniecki et al., 2019). Forced expression of MAP1B by NRF2 overexpression rescues axodendritic deficits in PD neurons. Interestingly the DYRK1A interactor FAM117B competes with NRF2 for binding to Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1), reduces NRF2 degradation and activates the NRF2 signaling pathway (Xun et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2023b). The link between DYRK1A, FAM117B, and NRF2 in PD certainly deserves further investigation.

MAP1B is heavily phosphorylated (at least 25 phospho-sites (Scales et al., 2009)) (Fig. 10) by a large number of kinases (review in (Riederer, 2007; Tymanskyj et al., 2010)), including CDK5, CSNK2, DYRK1A (Ser1400), GSK3β (Ser1265, Thr1270 (Ser1260 and Thr1265 in mice) (non-primed phosphorylations (Laks et al., 2018; Trivedi et al., 2005)), Ser1396 (Ser1391 in mice) (primed by prior phosphorylation by DYRK1A (Scales et al., 2009) on Ser1400 (Ser1395 in mice)), JNK, NLK, and certainly other non-identified kinases. Most phosphorylation sites have not been attributed to a specific kinase.

DYRK1A phosphorylates MAP1B on Ser1400, allowing the subsequent phosphorylation at Ser1396 by GSK3β as demonstrated with the poorly selective DYRK1A inhibitor epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) or DYRK1A knock-down with shRNA (Scales et al., 2009). DYRK1A knock-down in cultured cortical neurons alters microtubule stability and disrupts the formation of neurites (Scales et al., 2009). Phosphorylation of MAP1B by DYRK1A reduces its association with actin, while only mildly altering its binding to microtubules (Tan et al., 2024).

In brief, MAP1B is a key regulator of axodendritic growth and guidance. DYRK1A acts as a priming kinase by phosphorylating MAP1B at Ser1400, allowing subsequent phosphorylation at Ser1396 by GSK3β. DYRK1A inhibition is expected to favour the interaction between MAP1B and actin, to modify cytoskeletal dynamics and to reduce MAP1B Ser1396 phosphorylation by GSK3β.

Microtubule-associated protein Tau (MAPT)

The microtubule-associated protein tau (MAPT) gene encodes Tau (758 amino acids; 78.9 kDa), a key regulator of tubulin assembly, cytoskeleton stability, axonal transport, and synaptic functions (reviews in (Aranda-Abreu et al., 2025; Montalto and Ricciarelli, 2023; Parra Bravo et al., 2024)). Tau comprises four domains: (1) an NTD, (2) a proline-rich domain, (3) a microtubule-binding domain (MTBD) (comprising 3 or 4 conserved repeats of 18 amino acids resulting from exons 9 to 12) and (4) a C-terminal assembly region (Fig. 11, bottom).

MAPT (Tau): structure, phosphorylation sites and consequences of phosphorylation. Top. Alternative splicing leads to 6 different isoforms. Exon 10 encodes repeat R2. DYRK1A favours the exclusion of exon 10 and, consequently, the expression of 3R-Tau over 4R-Tau. Bottom. The 2N4R-Tau isoform is represented. DYRK1A phosphorylates Thr212, leading to decreased microtubule binding. LRRK2 activates GSK3 and favours the interaction of CDK5 with Tau. Not all Tau phosphorylating kinases and not all phosphorylation sites are indicated. CHIP, C-terminus of Hsc70 interacting protein (one of the major E3 ubiquitin ligases responsible for Tau degradation); Ub, ubiquitin.

In the central nervous system, alternative splicing of MAPT leads to six major Tau isoforms ranging from 352 (37 kDa) to 441 (46 kDa) amino acids (reviews in (Corsi et al., 2022; Rösler et al., 2019)) (Fig. 11, top). Alternative splicing of exons 2 and 3 determines whether the NTD contains the N2 and/or N1 domains or none. Exclusion or inclusion of exon 10 generates either the short (3R, 3 repeats) or long (4R, 4 repeats) forms of Tau (Fig. 11, top) (Qian and Liu, 2014).

DYRK1A phosphorylates splicing factors (such as alternative splicing factor (ASF), serine-arginine-rich protein 55 (SRp55), SC35, 9G8), thereby regulating the alternative splicing of Tau at the level of exon 10 (Ding et al., 2012; Kadri et al., 2015; Qian et al., 2011; Shi et al., 2008; Yin et al., 2012a; Yin et al., 2017). Increased DYRK1A expression/activity leads to exon 10 exclusion, resulting in increased expression of the 3R-Tau form compared to 4R-Tau (Wegiel et al., 2011; Yin et al., 2017). Conversely, DYRK1A inhibition favours exon 10 inclusion and therefore a reduced 3R/4R ratio. A few other kinases regulate exon 10 exclusion/inclusion: PKA (Gu et al., 2012; Shi et al., 2011), SRPKs (serine/arginine protein kinases) (Nikolakaki et al., 2001), CSNK1E (Chen et al., 2017), GSK3 (Chen et al., 2010a) and CLKs (Glatz et al., 2006; Hartmann et al., 2001), the latter being often inhibited by DYRK1A inhibitors (Lindberg et al., 2023a).

The Tau protein has been extremely studied since aggregation of hyperphosphorylated Tau as intracellular “tau tangles” represents a hallmark of AD and other “tauopathies” (reviews in (Alquezar et al., 2020; Aranda-Abreu et al., 2025; Chakraborty et al., 2024; Montalto and Ricciarelli, 2023; Parra Bravo et al., 2024; Rawat et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2025; Xia et al., 2021)). Imbalance in the expression of 3R and 4R-Tau isoforms leading to an increased 3R/4R ratio also contributes to neurodegenerative pathologies (reviews in (Corsi et al., 2022; Silva et al., 2025)). Tau has become a major therapeutic target (reviews in (Shen et al., 2025; Xia et al., 2021)) and phospho-Thr217-Tau is the major diagnostic biomarker of AD ((Kac et al., 2024), review in (Therriault et al., 2025)) and DS-associated AD (Kac et al., 2025). Phospho-Thr212-Tau is also a solid diagnostic biomarker of sporadic and DS-associated AD (Kac et al., 2025).

Several links between Tau and PD or DLB have been established (reviews in (Pan et al., 2021; Wray and Lewis, 2010; Zhang et al., 2018)):

(1) GWAS studies have identified MAPT as a risk factor for PD (Cisterna-Garcia et al., 2025; Edwards et al., 2010; Nalls et al., 2019; Satake et al., 2009; Senkevich et al., 2023; Simón-Sánchez et al., 2009; UK Parkinson’s Disease Consortium et al., 2011).

(2) extracellular release from diseased neurons, and uptake of pathogenic forms of Tau, known as Tau seeding/spreading activity (TSA), plays a key role in neuropathologies (review in (Liu et al., 2025a)). Monomeric Tau uptake is driven by two mechanisms, low-density lipoprotein LRP1 and LRRK2 (Evans et al., 2025). LRRK2 regulates the endocytosis of monomeric MAPT but also of SNCA. The pathogenic LRRK2 G2019S mutant triggers increased uptake of monomeric and fibrillar Tau and SNCA and perturbed endolysosomal system (Evans et al., 2025), and enhances the neuronal transmission of P301S-Tau in mouse brain (Nguyen et al., 2018a).

(3) LRRK2 is known to activate GSK3β (Kawakami et al., 2014; Lin et al., 2010), a well-characterized Tau phosphorylating kinase. Although Tau is phosphorylated by LRRK2 at several sites in vitro (see below) (Bailey et al., 2013; Hamm et al., 2015; Kawakami et al., 2012; Moszczynski et al., 2017), whether Tau is a physiological substrate for LRRK2 remains unclear (unlikely?). LRRK2 and its G2019S mutant form bind very strongly to Tau favouring its phosphorylation by CDK5. LRRK2 genetic or pharmacological inhibition does not prevent Tau phosphorylation by CDK5, suggesting that LRRK2 acts, independently of its kinase activity, as a scaffolding protein to facilitate CDK5-dependent Tau phosphorylation (mostly on Ser396, Ser404) (Shanley et al., 2015). Treatment of a mouse tauopathy model (hippocampus-injected vector expressing P301L-Tau) with a pharmacological LRRK2 inhibitor (JZ1.40) leads to reduced phosphorylation of RAB10 (Thr73) and Tau (Thr181, Ser202/Thr205) and improves synaptic plasticity and cognitive functions (Castro-Sánchez et al., 2020).

(4) Tau and SNCA interact directly, accelerating both Tau and SNCA aggregation and spreading in PD (Badiola et al., 2011; Dasari et al., 2019; Giasson et al., 2003; Pan et al., 2022). There is evidence for colocalization of Tau and SNCA in LBs in PD patients (Arima et al., 1999; Ishizawa et al., 2003; Ornelas et al., 2020). Increased Tau phosphorylation is observed in the striatum of the transgenic SNCA A53T mutant PD mouse model (Haggerty et al., 2011; Wills et al., 2011). Deletion of Tau reduces the propagation of SNCA pathology in mice injected with SNCA fibrils and improves motor functions in SNCA A53T mice (Pan et al., 2022). Tau deletion in TgA53T mice corrects synaptic and memory deficits (Singh et al., 2019; Vermilyea et al., 2022). However other reports show that Tau deletion does not alter SNCA pathology (Gratuze et al., 2019; Morris et al., 2011; Stoyka et al., 2021) (see detailed discussion in (Pan et al., 2022)).

(5) MPP+ and MPTP induce Tau hyperphosphorylation (Ser396/Ser404) in vitro (SH-SY5Y) (Duka and Sidhu, 2006) and in vivo (Duka et al., 2006), respectively, in a GSK3β- and SNCA-dependent manner.

(6) Nigrostriatal Tau pathology (phosphorylation of Ser 205/Ser207 (AT8 antibodies), and Ser396/Ser404 (PHF-1)) is associated with loss of dopaminergic neurons and of TH-positive neurons in patients with mild motor deficits (parkinsonism without clinical diagnosis of PD nor SNCA aggregates and LBs) (Chu et al., 2024). Tau pathology and dopaminergic degeneration thus appear to precede SNCA pathology. Elevated tauopathy, limited to the striatum, is observed in the brain of PD patients with or without dementia (Wills et al., 2010).

(7) Familial PD-causing LRRK2 mutations are accompanied by intraneuronal Tau aggregation (Henderson et al., 2019; Lubben et al., 2024). The pathogenic LRRK2 I2020T mutation is associated with Tau hyperphosphorylation (Ujiie et al., 2012). The link between LRRK2 and tauopathies is reviewed in (Araki et al., 2018; Herbst et al., 2022). CSF Tau levels may contribute to the diagnosis of PD subtypes (review in (Pan et al., 2021)).

(8) More than 50 mutations in MAPT have been described which lead to frontotemporal dementia with parkinsonism linked to chromosome 17 (FTDP-17) (reviews in (Kimura et al., 2014; Strang et al., 2019)). Most mutations are in exons 9-13.

(9) The H1 haplotype of MAPT is associated with DLB (Cervera-Carles et al., 2016; Colom-Cadena et al., 2013; Labbé et al., 2016; Orme et al., 2020).

Tau is heavily phosphorylated (at least 85 phospho-sites, many of which are associated with AD) by a large number of kinases (reviews in (Montalto and Ricciarelli, 2023; Xia et al., 2021)), including (non-exclusive list of kinases and sites) c-ABL (Tyr310, Tyr394) (Ait-Bouziad et al., 2020; Tremblay et al., 2010), BRSK1 and BRSK2 (brain-selective kinases) (Ser262) (Bendzunas et al., 2025), CaMK2 (Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase 2) (Ser262, Ser356, Ser409, Ser416), CDK5 (Ser202, Thr205, Thr217, Ser235, Ser396, Ser404) (Fu et al., 2025a; Kimura et al., 2014), CSNK1 (>40 residues), DYRK1A (Thr212 and probably other sites) (Azorsa et al., 2010; Coutadeur et al., 2015; Kimura et al., 2007b; Lindberg et al., 2023a; Liu et al., 2007, Liu et al., 2008; Nguyen et al., 2018b; Ryoo et al., 2007; Tahtouh et al., 2012; Woods et al., 2001a), FYN (Tyr18) (Lee et al., 2004b), GSK3β (Ser199, Ser202, Thr231, Ser396, Ser400, Ser404, Ser413) (Chakraborty et al., 2024), LRRK2 (Thr149, Thr153, Thr175, Thr181, Ser202, Thr205 ?) (Bailey et al., 2013; Hamm et al., 2015; Kawakami et al., 2012; Moszczynski et al., 2017), MARK2 (microtubule-affinity regulating kinase 2) (Ser416) (Nadel et al., 2024), MARK4 (Ser262) (Gu et al., 2013), NUAK1/2 (novel (nua) kinase 1) (Ser386) (Taylor et al., 2024), PKA (Ser214 (Sadik et al., 2009), Ser202/Thr205, Ser396), PKG (Thr111, Thr212, Ser214 (Sadik et al., 2009), Thr245, Ser235, Ser356, Ser409/Ser412, Ser409), SGK1 (serum-/glucocorticoid-regulated kinase 1) (Ser214) (Elahi et al., 2021; Saleem et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2006), TYK2 (tyrosine-protein kinase 2) (Tyr29) (Kim et al., 2024a) (Fig. 11, bottom). Most phosphorylations are associated with a loss of microtubule binding and subsequent aggregation. Tau undergoes a multitude of other post-translational modifications (acetylation, glycation, glycosylation, methylation, nitration, polyamination, sumoylation, ubiquitylation, proteolysis) (review in (Alquezar et al., 2020)).

DYRK1A phosphorylates Tau on several sites (Liu et al., 2008b; Tahtouh et al., 2022), the best characterized being Thr212 (Azorsa et al., 2010; Coutadeur et al., 2015; Kimura et al., 2007b; Lindberg et al., 2023a; Liu et al., 2007, Liu et al., 2008; Nguyen et al., 2018b; Ryoo et al., 2007; Tahtouh et al., 2012; Woods et al., 2001a). Thr212 phosphorylation primes Tau for further phosphorylation by GSK3 at Ser208 (Woods et al., 2001a). Thr212 phosphorylation is inhibited in vitro and in vivo by a multitude of pharmacological inhibitors of DYRK1A (Chen et al., 2024; Lindberg et al., 2023a, Lindberg et al., 2023b; Souchet et al., 2022; Tahtouh et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2024). Phosphorylation in this proline-rich region inhibits microtubule-binding and microtubule stability, and promotes Tau aggregation (Liu et al., 2007). Overexpression of DYRK1A stabilizes the Tau mRNA and enhances Tau expression in a kinase-independent manner, involving DYRK1A’s CTD (Qian et al., 2013). Thr212-phosphorylated Tau is a plasma biomarker of sporadic and DS-associated AD (Kac et al., 2025).

In brief, GWAS have identified MAPT as a risk factor for PD. Tau is linked to several proteins involved in PD (LRRK1, SNCA, GSK3β). DYRK1A impinges on two aspects of Tau physiology involved in neurodegenerative diseases: (1) alternative splicing and (2) hyperphosphosphorylation-induced aggregation. (1) Through the regulation of Tau mRNA alternative splicing, DYRK1A increases the 3R-Tau/4R-Tau ratio, a marker associated with neurodegeneration. (2) DYRK1A phosphorylates Thr212 (and other sites), thereby priming Tau for phosphorylation of Ser208 by GSK3, reducing microtubule stability and favouring Tau aggregation. Phospho-Thr212-tau is a biomarker of DS and AD. Pharmacological inhibition of DYRK1A decreases the 3R-Tau/4R-Tau ratio, and reduces Tau phosphorylation and aggregation.

Myocyte enhancer factor 2D (MEF2D)

MEF2A-D (Myocyte Enhancer Factors 2A-D) are four highly conserved transcription factors playing key roles in muscle and neuronal development, cell survival, immune response, angiogenesis, etc. (reviews in (Dietrich, 2013; Flavell et al., 2008; Rashid et al., 2014)). MEF2s are involved in neuronal cell survival, synaptogenesis, axonal outgrowth and memory (Dietrich, 2013; Flavell et al., 2008; Rashid et al., 2014). All four MEF2s contain (1) a highly conserved MADS box and (2) an MEF2 domain on the N-terminal side, which are implicated in homo/hetero-dimerization and DNA-binding. The C-terminal TAD is highly divergent among the four MEF2s. Multiple signaling pathways (MAPK, Wnt, PI3K/Akt, Ca++ signaling, etc.) regulate MEF2s expression.

MEF2D (521 amino acids, 55.9 kDa) is the most studied in the context of PD where it appears to play a pro-survival role. The link between MEF2D and PD is supported by results obtained in in vivo and in vitro models and from human data (reviews in (Dietrich, 2013; She and Mao, 2011; Yang and Mao, 2010)).

(1) MEF2D is expressed in neuronal mitochondria, where it regulates the expression of NADH dehydrogenase 6 (ND6), an essential component of the oxidative phosphorylation complex I (She et al., 2011). Inactivation of MEF2D transcriptional activity results in decreased complex I activity, increased H2O2 level, reduced ATP level, and sensitization of neurons to stress-induced death. Rotenone and MPP+ reduce mitochondrial MEF2D and cell viability in the dopaminergic neuronal cell line SN4741 (She et al., 2011).

(2) In vitro. Exposure of cultured SN4741 cells to 6-OHDA leads to MEF2D oxidation and autophagy-mediated reduction of its levels (Gao et al., 2014a). MPP+ triggers a decrease of the MEF2D mRNA half-life and protein level in SN4741 (Wang et al., 2014). Bis(3)-cognitin (a dimeric version of the acetylcholinesterase inhibitor tacrine) increases MEF2D levels in SN4171 cells and attenuates MPP+ or α-synuclein -induced toxicity (Yao et al., 2012). This protective effect is mediated at least in part by the down-regulation of GSK3β. Similarly, polydatin (Cao et al., 2021), chrysin (Guo et al., 2016) or indirubin-3’-oxime downregulate GSK3β (Hu et al., 2015), increase MEF2D levels and reduce neurotoxicity induced, respectively, by MPP+ (primary cerebellar granule neurons) (polydatin, chrysin) and 6-OHDA (PC12 cells) (indirubin-3’-oxime), respectively.

(3) In vivo. Exposure of mice to MPTP leads to a reduction of MEF2D mRNA and protein levels in SN dopaminergic neurons (Wang et al., 2014). MPTP triggers a calpain-mediated truncation of the CDK5 activator p35 to p25 in mouse SN (Smith et al., 2004, 2003). This results in CDK5/p25 activation and MEF2D Ser444 phosphorylation, leading to MEF2D inactivation and dopaminergic neuron death (Smith et al., 2006). AAV-mediated expression of a S444A non-phosphorylable MEF2D mutant provides neuroprotection towards MPTP (Smith et al., 2006). Bis(3)-cognitin (Yao et al., 2012) or the tetramethylpyrazine-derived T-006 (Chen et al., 2020) increase MEF2D level, prevent loss of TH in SN dopaminergic neurons and improve behavioral abilities in MPTP-treated mice or MPTP-treated rats, respectively. The anti-cancer agent SU4312 provides protection against MPTP-induced neurotoxicity in vitro and in vivo models of PD through activation of MEF2D and inhibition of MAO-B (Guo et al., 2017).

(4) MEF2D levels are increased (Yang et al., 2009) or decreased (immunochemistry) (Chu et al., 2011) in brains of PD patients and SNCA transgenic mice. An oxidized form of MEF2D is observed in the brains of rodent PD model and of PD patients (Gao et al., 2014a). Mitochondrial MEF2D and ND6 protein are reduced in the brains of MPTP-treated mice as well as in the brains of PD patients compared to age-matched controls (She et al., 2011).

Several phosphorylation sites have been described in MEF2D (Fig. 12) (review in (Yin et al., 2012b)). MEF2D is phosphorylated by ATM (ataxia telangiectasia mutated) (Thr259, Ser275, Ser294, Ser314) (Chan et al., 2014a), CaMK4 (undefined sites) (Blaeser et al., 2000), CDK4/cyclin D1 (Ser98, Ser110) and CDK6/cyclin D3 (cell division conditions) (Anders et al., 2011; Di Giorgio et al., 2015), c-SRC (Tyr131, Tyr333, Tyr337) (Xia et al., 2023), ERK5/BMK1 (extracellular signal-regulated kinase 5 / big mitogen-activated kinase 1) (Ser179) (Kato et al., 2000), CDK5/p25 (Ser444) (Gong et al., 2003; Mount et al., 2013; Rashidian et al., 2009; Smith et al., 2006; Tang et al., 2005), DYRK1A (Ser251) (Wang et al., 2021a), GSK3β (Thr145, Ser149, Ser153) (Hu et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2009), PKA (Ser121, Ser190) (Du et al., 2008; Salma and McDermott, 2012), and p38 MAPK (p38α and p38δ) (Ser444 in innate immune responses (Escós et al., 2023) and Thr308, Thr315 in muscle (Rampalli et al., 2007)). Phosphorylation by ATM, CaMK4 and ERK5 favors the transcriptional activity of MEF2D, while phosphorylation by c-SRC, CDK5, GSK3, and PKA and p38 MAPK induces a decline in MEF2D transcriptional activity. Phosphorylation by CKDK4/cyclin D1 leads to MEF2D ubiquitinylation and degradation. MEF2D phosphorylation by CDK5, PKA and GSK3 lead to neuronal apoptosis, while phosphorylation by ERK2 promotes neuronal survival.

MEF2D: structural organization, phosphorylation sites and consequences of phosphorylation. The N-terminal 30 amino acids sequence contains a mitochondria-localization signal. M, MEF2 domain; MADS, MADS box (a conserved sequence motif encoding the DNA-binding MADS domain (minichromosome maintenance 1, agamous, deficiens, serum response factor)).

DYRK1A phosphorylates MEF2D at Ser251, leading to an increased expression of MEF2D in the cytoplasm vs. nucleus and to a decrease in its transcriptional activity (Wang et al., 2021a). Non-phosphorylable S251A MEF2D mutant displays enhanced transcriptional activity, while the phospho-mimic S251D MEF2D mutant shows reduced transcriptional activity (Wang et al., 2021a). MEF2D upregulates DYRK1A expression, by specifically activating transcription of the DYRK1A isoform 5 gene (Wang et al., 2017), mediated by a MEF2D responsive element located in the DYRK1A promoter region (-268 to -254 bp)

In brief, MEF2D transcriptional activity appears to play a protective role in PD. DYRK1A phosphorylates MEF2D on Ser251, leading to decreased transcriptional activity. Inhibition of MEF2D phosphorylation by DYRK1A is thus expected to have a protective effect towards PD.

Nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT)

Nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT) is a family of 5 transcription factors (NFATc1, NFATc2, NFATc3, NFATc4, NFAT5) whose activation is controlled by the calcineurin phosphatase.

NFATc1 (the canonical sequence) (943 amino acids, 101.2 kDa) is constituted of four domains (Fig. 13, bottom): (1) an N-terminal transcriptional activation domain (TAD-A), (2) a regulatory domain (R) which comprises a calcineurin-binding site, the serine-rich gatekeeper region (SRR), 3 regions of Ser-Pro repeats (SP1-3), a nuclear export signal (NES) and a nuclear localization signal (NLS), and where over 20 phosphorylation sites are located, (3) a Rel similarity domain (RSD), allowing DNA-binding, and (4) a C-terminal transcriptional activation domain (TAD-B).

NFATc1: structural organization, phosphorylation sites and consequences of phosphorylation. Top, NFAT pathway. NFAT is kept in the cytoplasm by phosphorylation by various kinases, mainly DYRK1A and GSK3β. It is thus inactive as a transcription factor. NFAT is dephosphorylated by the calcineurin phosphatase, leading to its nuclear accumulation and transcriptional activity. Calcineurin is activated either by Calcium-mediated activation of calmodulin, or by-calcium mediated activation of calpains which generate a constitutively active, calmodulin-independent calcineurin truncated form (Δ-calcineurin). RCAN1 (DSCR1, Down syndrome candidate region 1 gene) is a negative regulator of calcineurin which is regulated by various kinases (see RCAN1 section). Bottom, NFAT phosphorylation. LRRK2 phosphorylates NFATc2 on Thr483, Thr733, and Thr893 (conserved residues in NFATc1: Thr501, Thr756 and Thr912, respectively) and on Thr862 and Thr870 (not conserved in NFATc1). TAD-A, TAD-B, transcriptional activation domains A and B; *, calcineurin-binding sites (118-123; 384-398); Unlabelled Image, NES, nuclear exclusion signal (310-321; 924-933); Unlabelled Image, NLS, nuclear localization signal (265-267; 682-684); RSD, Rel similarity domain (containing the DNA-binding domain); 1, 2, 3, Serine-Proline repeats SP1 (205-221), SP2 (235-251), SP3 (284-300); SRR, serine-rich gatekeeper region.

In the resting state NFAT is heavily phosphorylated in the regulatory domain, leading to NES exposure and 14-3-3 binding, resulting in NFAT retention in the cytoplasm (Fig. 13, top). Upon stimulation leading to increased intracellular Ca++ levels, NFAT binds to and is dephosphorylated by calcineurin in the SRR and SP repeats. Calcineurin is activated either by Ca++/calmodulin or by Ca++ -activated calpains (which renders calcineurin constitutively active). This results in NES masking and NLS unmasking, and translocation of NFAT to the nucleus where, in conjunction with AP1, GATA, MEF2, it acts as a transcription factor (reviews in (Hogan et al., 2003; Holmberg et al., 2002; Kipanyula et al., 2016; Mackiewicz et al., 2023; Minami, 2014)). NFAT has been crystallized in complex with calcineurin (Takeuchi et al., 2007), with DNA (Stroud and Chen, 2003) and with HIV-1 LTR κB element (Giffin et al., 2003).

The involvement of NFAT in PD stems from three sets of data:

(1) The links between NFAT, RCAN1, calcineurin and PD (see section on RCAN1).

(2) LRRK2 and NFAT. A LRRK2-NFATc2 pathway is associated with microglial neuroinflammation (Kim et al., 2020a; Wang et al., 2021b). In the context of Crohn’s disease, LRKK2 functions as a negative regulator of NFAT, maintaining it in the cytoplasm through its interaction with noncoding RNA repressor of NFAT (NRON). This effect is independent of the catalytic activity of LRRK2 (Jabri and Barreiro, 2011; Liu et al., 2011). Similarly, active LRRK2 reduces NFATc1 translocation to the nucleus and participates to SNCA pathology. LRRK2 inactivation leads to nuclear translocation of NFATc1 and reduces SNCA pathology (Feng et al., 2023).

Other studies show that LRRK2 favours nuclear translocation and activation of NFATc2 (Kim et al., 2020a; Takagawa et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2021b). Neuron-released SNCA, through Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2), increases LRRK2 phosphorylation (Ser935, Ser955) and activity in microglia, resulting in enhanced expression, phosphorylation (5 sites) and nuclear translocation of NFATc2, cytokine/chemokine gene expression (TNF- α, IL-1β, IL-6, etc.), neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration (Kim et al., 2020a). Genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition of LRRK2 (by HG-10-102-01) reduces NFATc2 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, reduces neuroinflammation, prevents neuronal cell death and improves motor functions in a rodent α-synucleopathy model (Kim et al., 2020a; Wang et al., 2021b). LRRK2 phosphorylates NFATc2 on Thr483 (close to the DNA binding surface) and on Thr733, Thr862, Thr870 and Thr893, in the CTD associated with NFATc2 translocation. High levels of NFATc2 are observed in brains of PD/DLB patients and in an SNCA transgenic mouse model of PD. Administration of the HG-10-102-01 LRRK2 inhibitor to this mouse model reduces glial activation, normalizes NFATc2 level and corrects motor behaviour deficits (Kim et al., 2020a). NFAT nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity is increased by transfection of LRRK2 or LRRK2 G2019S in HEK-293 cells (Takagawa et al., 2018). Interferon-γ synergizes with active LRRK2 G2019S to reduce nuclear NFAT3 in human iPSCs (induced pluripotent stem cells) -derived dopaminergic neurons and microglia (Panagiotakopoulou et al., 2020). The neurotoxin acrylamide triggers LRRK2 activation, NFATc2 nuclear translocation and neuroinflammation (Yan et al., 2023). Inhibition of the LRRK2-NFAT2c thus appears as a potential therapeutic target for PD and DLB.

(3) SNCA oligomers induce NFAT nuclear translocation and activation in N2a dopaminergic neurons, leading to reduced expression of synapsin 1 and to synaptic loss (Sant’Anna et al., 2023). Transfection of HEK-293 with WT or SNCA A53T missense mutation triggers calcineurin activation and NFATc1 and NFATc3 nuclear translocation (Luo et al., 2014). The calcium ionophore ionomycin triggers NFATc3 nuclear translocation. A53T-SNCA transgenic mice show increased midbrain and dopaminergic nuclear levels of NFATc3 and decreased cytoplasmic levels, compared to WT animals (Luo et al., 2014). Calcineurin inhibition by cyclosporine in A53T-SNCA dopaminergic neuronal cell cultures blocks NFATc3 nuclear translocation and improves cell survival (Luo et al., 2014). SNCA induces the nuclear translocation of NFATc1 and NFATc3 in HEK-293 cells (Luo et al., 2014).

NFATc1 is phosphorylated by various kinases (reviews in (Holmberg et al., 2002; Kennedy et al., 2023; Porter et al., 2000)): CDK1/cyclin B (osteoblasts) (Ser263 in mouse i.e. Ser 261human) (Kim et al., 2020b), COT (mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase 8, MAP3K8) Fig. 13, bottom) (unidentified sites in N-terminal region) (Kuroda et al., 2012), CSNK1 (unidentified sites in the SSR-1 region) (Okamura et al., 2004), CSNK2 (Ser 187) (Porter et al., 2000), DYRK1A (Ser261, Ser278, Ser403, Ser409) (Liu et al., 2017a), ERK/ERK5 (Ser172) (Porter et al., 2000; Shin et al., 2008), GSK3 (unidentified sites in SP repeats) (Beals et al., 1997; Neal and Clipstone, 2001; Sheridan et al., 2002), IKKε (inhibitor of nuclear factor κB kinase subunit epsilon) (major site: Ser161) (Zhang et al., 2016), JNK1 (Ser117, Ser172) (Chow et al., 2000; Porter et al., 2000), LRRK2 (Thr483, Thr733, Thr862, Thr870, Thr893 in NFATc2) (Kim et al., 2020a), MLK3 (mixed lineage kinase 3) (unidentified sites) (Kumar et al., 2020), PIM1 (Ser151,Ser153, Thr154, Ser245, Ser256, Ser269, Thr338, Thr339) (Eerola et al., 2019; Rainio et al., 2002), PKA (Ser 245, Ser269, Ser294) (Sheridan et al., 2002), PKCξ (unidentified sites in N-terminal region) (San-Antonio et al., 2002), VRK2 (vaccinia-related kinase 2) (Ser32) (Vázquez-Cedeira and Lazo, 2012).

DYRK1A phosphorylates NFAT on several sites near the SP3 motif area (Ser261, Ser278), priming for phosphorylation in the SP2 and SRR motifs by GSK3 and CSNK1, respectively, and at Ser403, Ser409 (Arron et al., 2006; Epstein, 2006; Gwack et al., 2006a; Liu et al., 2017a). DYRK1A promotes NFAT nuclear export and cytoplasmic localization in numerous models (Drosophila (Gwack et al., 2006b), Nile tilapia spleen lymphocytes (Wei et al., 2020), plasminogen activation system (Stefos et al., 2013), cardiomyocytes (da Costa Martins et al., 2010; Grebe et al., 2011; Kuhn et al., 2009), pancreatic β-cell proliferation (Wang et al., 2015), HIV-1 transcription (Booiman et al., 2015), etc.). Increased dosage of DYRK1A and RCAN1 (both genes located in chromosome 21) prevents NFAT nuclear localization, and thus reduce its transcriptional activity (Arron et al., 2006). NFATc1 phosphorylation by DYRK1A also enhances its stability (Liu et al., 2017a). DYRK1A inhibition leads to NFAT accumulation in the nucleus and transcriptional activity (see for ex: (da Costa Martins et al., 2010; Shaw et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2015; Wei et al., 2020)).

In brief, activation of the transcription factor NFAT is associated with PD and regulated by key PD factors such as LRRK2 and SNCA. DYRK1A (and other kinases) phosphorylates NFAT, thereby inducing its export out of the nucleus, maintaining it in the cytoplasm and thus inhibiting its transcriptional activity. Inhibition of DYRK1A promotes NFAT retention in the nucleus and favours its transcriptional activity.

p53 (TP53)

p53 (393 amino acids; 43.7 kDa) and its homologs p63 (680 amino acids; 76.8 kDa) and p73 (636 amino acids; 69.6 kDa) are key transcription activators regulating DNA repair, apoptotic cell death triggered by various stress events, autophagy, cell survival, senescence, and cell cycle (review in (Osterburg and Dötsch, 2022; Yogosawa and Yoshida, 2018)). p53 comprises (1) two N-terminal independent transactivation domains (TAD1 and TAD2), (2) an NES, a Proline-rich domain (PRD), (3) a central DNA binding domain (DBD), (4) an NLS, (5) an oligomerization domain (Tetramerization, TET), (6) an NES, and (7) a CTD involved in the negative regulation of DNA binding of the central domain (REG) (Fig. 14).

p53: structure, phosphorylation sites and consequences of phosphorylation. DYRK1A phosphorylates p53 on Ser15, leading to increased p53 transcriptional activity. DYRK1A and DYRK3 phosphorylate SIRT1, leading to p53 deacetylation. p53 induces the expression of miRNA-1246 which has a negative effect on DYRKK1A expression (Fig. 4). DBD, DNA binding domain; NES, nuclear export signal; NLS, nuclear localization signal; PRD, Proline-rich domain; REG, regulatory domain for DNA binding; TAD1, TAD2, transactivation domain; TET, tetramerization domain.

The link between p53 and PD is mostly based on the involvement of p53 in the generic induction of apoptosis in response to neurodegenerative oxidative stress, protein aggregates and mitochondrial dysfunction (reviews in (Alves da Costa and Checler, 2011; Checler and Alves da Costa, 2014; Luo et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024a; Wolfrum et al., 2022)).

(1) 6-OHDA induces p53 phosphorylation on Ser15, leading to p53’s transcriptional activity and apoptosis in dopaminergic neurons derived from embryonic stem cells (Nair, 2006). Dopaminergic cell death induced by 6-OHDA is reduced by p53 siRNAs (Biswas et al., 2005). The p53 inhibitor pifithrin-α prolongates the survival of dopaminergic neural grafts in a 6-OHDA-lesioned rat model of PD (Chou et al., 2011). These results are confirmed with other neurotoxins: neurons from p53-KO mice are resistant to MPTP (Trimmer et al., 1996). p53-induced Bax contributes to rotenone-induced neuron degeneration in a cell and rat model of PD (Wu et al., 2013).

(2) Parkin, but not parkin mutants inducing familial PD, represses the expression of p53 (da Costa et al., 2009). Parkin prevents 6-OHDA-induced caspase-3 activation in a p53-dependent manner. Loss of parkin function, whether due to depletion or missense/deletion mutations, leads to increased p53 expression (da Costa et al., 2009). Furthermore, parkin is a p53 target gene (Viotti et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2011a).

(3) DJ-1 also regulates the transcriptional activity of p53, either negatively (Fan et al., 2008; Ottolini et al., 2013) or positively (Shinbo et al., 2005).

(4) LRRK2 phosphorylates p53 on Thr304 and Thr377 in vitro and in cell cultures, leading to p53 nuclear accumulation and induction of p21CIP1 expression, a critical factor in senescence (Ho et al., 2015). The G2019S mutant of LRRK2, causative of familial PD, or rotenone induce increased LRRK2 kinase activity and promote cellular senescence via phosphorylated p53-mediated p21CIP1 induction (Ho et al., 2021, 2019).

p53 is heavily phosphorylated by a large number of kinases (reviews in (DeHart et al., 2014; MacLaine and Hupp, 2011; Yogosawa and Yoshida, 2018)) comprising AMPK (Ser15) (Imamura et al., 2001; Jones et al., 2005), ATM (Ser15) (Banin et al., 1998; Canman et al., 1998), ATR (Ataxia telangiectasia and Rad3 related) (Ser15) (Tibbetts et al., 1999), AURKA (Aurora kinase A) (Ser315) (Katayama et al., 2004), AURKB (Ser183, Ser269, Thr284) (Wu et al., 2011), CDK2/cyclin A (Ser315) (Luciani et al., 2000), CDK5 (Ser15, Ser33, Ser46) (Lee et al., 2007, p. 53), CDK7/cyclin H (Ser33) (Ko et al., 1997), CHK1 (checkpoint kinase 1) (Ser366, Thr387) (Ou et al., 2005), CHK2 (Ser20) (Pabla et al., 2008), CSNK1 (Thr18, Ser20) (Dumaz et al., 1999; Venerando et al., 2010), CSNK2 (Ser392) (Keller et al., 2001), DYRK1A (Ser15) (Park et al., 2010), DYRK2 (Ser46) (Taira et al., 2007), GRK5 (G protein-coupled receptor kinase 5) (Thr55) (Chen et al., 2010b), HIPK4 (homeodomain interacting protein kinase 4) (Ser9) (Arai et al., 2007), HIPK2 (Ser46) (D’Orazi et al., 2002), JNK (Thr81) (Buschmann et al., 2001), LRRK2 (Thr304, Thr377) (Ho et al., 2017, 2015, p. 53), NUAK1 (Ser15, Ser392) (Hou et al., 2011); review in (Cullen et al., 2025)), p38 MAPK (Ser15, Ser33, Ser46) (Bulavin et al., 1999; Kim et al., 2002), PKCδ (Ser46) (Yoshida et al., 2006), PLK3 (Ser20) (Li et al., 2005b, p. 3), PRAK (p38-regulated/activated protein kinase) (Ser37) (Sun et al., 2007), VRK1 (Thr18) (Lopez-Borges and Lazo, 2000), and VRK2 (Thr18) (Blanco et al., 2006).

DYRK1A phosphorylates p53 on Ser15 in vitro and in immortalized rat embryonic hippocampal progenitor H19-7 cells, leading to increased transcriptional activity of p53, increased expression of p53 target genes such as p21CIP1 and attenuation of neuronal proliferation (Park et al., 2010). Ser15-p53 is phosphorylated by other kinases such as AMPK, ATM, ATR, CDK5, NUAK1 and p38, resulting in the activation of p53’s transcriptional activity.

In response to DNA damage, p53 induces the expression of miR-1246 (Liao et al., 2012), which leads to the downregulation of DYRK1A expression with, as a consequence, nuclear retention of NFATc1 and induction of apoptosis (Zhang et al., 2011b).

DYRK1A also acts indirectly on p53, as both DYRK1A and DYRK3 phosphorylate SIRT1 on Thr522, resulting in increased deacetylase activity, deacetylation of p53, inactivation of p53-induced apoptosis and enhanced cell survival (Guo et al., 2010).

Following exposure to genotoxic stress, DYRK2 is targeted to the nucleus and phosphorylates p53 on Ser46, thereby inducing the expression of the p53AIP1 proapoptotic factor, resulting in the apoptotic response to DNA damage (Taira et al., 2007).

In brief, p53 participates to apoptosis in dopaminergic neurons in response to environmental stress, and its inhibition appears protective. DYRK1A phosphorylates p53 on Ser15, contributing to enhance p53’s transcriptional activity. Therefore, inhibiting DYRK1A is expected to reduce p53’s pro-apoptotic effects, potentially providing neuroprotection.

Parkin (PRKN)

Parkin (PRKN) (465 amino acids, 51.6 kDa) is a member of the RING-in-Between-RING (RBR) (RING, really interesting new gene) family of E3 ubiquitin ligases (reviews in (Checler and Alves da Costa, 2022; Connelly et al., 2023; Gundogdu et al., 2021; Seirafi et al., 2015; Sherawat et al., 2026; Stevens et al., 2023; Trempe and Gehring, 2023)). Parkin comprises six domains (Fig. 15): (1) a ubiquitin-like domain (UBL), (2-5) the four zinc-binding domains RING0, RING1 (which binds the E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme), IBR (In-Between-RINGs), and RING2 (the catalytic site for ubiquitin transfer), and (6) a repressor element (REP). Under basal conditions, parkin is kept inactive in the cytoplasm by a dual mechanism: RING0 binds and blocks the catalytic cysteine of RING2, and both UBL and REP prevent binding of RING1 to the E2-ligase (Trempe and Gehring, 2023). Activation of parkin is triggered by recruitment to the mitochondrial surface through a PINK1-phosphorylated ubiquitin, followed by phosphorylation of Ser65 in the UBL domain of parkin by PINK1 (Sauvé et al., 2018). RING1 then binds the E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme while RING2, containing parkin’s Cys431 catalytic site, mediates ubiquitin transfer from E2 to substrates. Parkin Ser9 phosphorylation by CaMK2 occurs in a neuronal activity-dependent manner, leading to its PINK1-independent activation (Song et al., 2023). Active parkin ubiquitinates synaptojanin, leading to its interaction with endophilin A1 and synaptic vesicle recycling (Song et al., 2023) (Fig. 15).

Parkin: structural organization, phosphorylation sites and consequences of phosphorylation. Parkin E3 ligase activity is stimulated by phosphorylation at Ser9 by AMPK, CaMK2, at Ser65 by PINK1 and at Ser108 by ULK1. Parkin’s activity is inhibited by phosphorylation at Ser131 by CDK5, DYRK1A, PKA, p38MAPK and at Tyr143 by c-ABL. IBR, In-between-RING; RING, Really Interesting New Gene; R, Repressor of parkin; RG2, Ring2; UBL, Ubiquitin-like domain.

In conjunction with the PINK1 kinase, parkin is involved in mitochondrial quality control and induction of mitophagy, the selective autophagy process for damaged, depolarized mitochondria (Connelly et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023a; Pickrell et al., 2015; Pickrell and Youle, 2015; Tanaka, 2020; Zhou et al., 2023a). The two-step activation of parkin by mitochondria-bound PINK1 derepresses parkin which can ubiquitinate mitochondrial surface proteins, leading to their degradation and elimination of the mitochondria. Parkin protects dopaminergic neurons from mitochondrial damage (Pickrell et al., 2015). Parkin is also involved in mitochondria-derived vesicle formation, cellular metabolism, calcium homeostasis, mitochondrial DNA maintenance, mitochondrial biogenesis, apoptosis induction, and inflammation processes (review in (Pereira et al., 2023)).

PRKN was one of the first genes to be identified to cause autosomal recessive familial PD: homozygous mutations are responsible for early-onset autosomal recessive Parkinsonism while heterozygous mutations are responsible for late-onset sporadic PD (reviews in (Kitada et al., 1998; Pereira et al., 2023; Stevens et al., 2023; Torii et al., 2020; Trempe and Gehring, 2023)). PRKN mutations (over 130 described) are responsible for the second most frequent genetic form of PD. These mutations result in loss-of-function and disrupt recycling of synaptic vesicles in dopaminergic neurons (Song et al., 2023).

Several phosphorylation sites have been described in parkin (Fig. 15) (reviews in (Chakraborty et al., 2017; Sauvé et al., 2018)): parkin is phosphorylated by c-ABL (Tyr143) (Imam et al., 2011; Karuppagounder et al., 2014; Ko et al., 2010), AMPK (Ser9) (Lee et al., 2019b), CaMK2 (Ser9, Ser198) (Song et al., 2023), PKA (Ser101, Ser131, Ser136) (Yamamoto et al., 2005), CDK5/p25 (Ser131) (Avraham et al., 2007; Rubio de la Torre et al., 2009; Yamamoto et al., 2005), CSNK1 (Ser101, Ser127, Ser378) (Rubio de la Torre et al., 2009; Yamamoto et al., 2005), DYRK1A (Ser131) (Im and Chung, 2015), p38 MAPK (Ser131) (Chen et al., 2018), PINK1 (Ser65, Thr175, Thr217) (Kazlauskaite et al., 2014, Kazlauskaite et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2008b; Kondapalli et al., 2012; McWilliams et al., 2018; Sha et al., 2010; Shiba-Fukushima et al., 2012, Shiba-Fukushima et al., 2014b), and Unc-51-like kinase 1 (ULK1) (Ser108) (Fujita et al., 2022; Hung et al., 2021).

DYRK1A phosphorylates parkin at Ser131, leading to reduction of parkin/ E2 ubiquitin conjugating enzyme interaction, and consequently to inhibition of the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity (Im and Chung, 2015). Parkin phosphorylation by DYRK1A prevents its neuroprotective activity in 6-OHDA-induced toxicity in SH-SY5Y cells (Im and Chung, 2015). The parkin 226-290 sequence is critical for interaction with DYRK1A, while the DYRK1A 317-416 region is critical for interaction with parkin (Im and Chung, 2015). DYRK1A thus appears as a negative effector of parkin.

In brief, parkin is a demonstrated key player in PD. Parkin phosphorylation at Ser131 by DYRK1A inhibits its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity. As a result, pharmacological inhibition of DYRK1A holds promise for restoring parkin’s functions.

Polo-like kinase 2 (PLK2)

The polo-like kinase family comprises 5 members, PLK1 to PLK5 (reviews in (de Cárcer et al., 2011; Liu, 2015; Strebhardt, 2010)). PLKs share 1 or 2 polo-box domains (PBD), a conserved domain involved in autoinhibition and phosphorylation-dependent binding to substrates (Elia et al., 2003). Although PLKs, PLK1 in particular, are mostly studied for their functions in regulating the cell cycle, PLK2 and PLK3 also appear to play a role in the CNS (Seeburg et al., 2005). The implication of PLKs in cancers and other diseases has generated considerable interest in the development of selective PLK inhibitors (review in (Bian et al., 2024)).

PLK2 (685 amino acids; 78.2 kDa), also known as serum-inducible kinase (SNK), comprises (1) an N-terminal kinase domain and (2) two C-terminal PDBs (review in (Zhang et al., 2022a)) (Fig. 16). PLK2 is implicated in centriole duplication, and thus in cell cycle control. It appears to act as a tumour-suppressor in p53-dependent stress-response.

PLK2 phosphorylates DCTN1, leading to inhibition of autophagosome-lysosome fusion and reduced degradation of SNCA oligomers. PLK2 is highly expressed in the CNS (cortex, hippocampus, occipital, temporal and parietal lobes, putamen and trigeminal ganglion). It regulates synaptic plasticity, neuronal cell differentiation (Draghetti et al., 2009) and participates to neuronal protection and homeostasis following high neuronal activity (Seeburg et al., 2005). PLK2 binds the post-synaptic spine-associated RapGAP (SPAR) through its PBDs, following SPAR priming phosphorylation on Ser1328 by CDK5 (Seeburg et al., 2008). PLK2 then phosphorylates SPAR, leading to SPAR degradation, providing a molecular pathway for synaptic plasticity homeostasis during chronically elevated activity (Ang et al., 2008) (Fig. 16). SPAR promotes dendritic spines growth, and its degradation leads to depletion of PSD-95 (postsynaptic density 95) and loss of mature dendritic spines and synapses (Lee et al., 2011b; Pak and Sheng, 2003). PLK2 inhibition affects the pathology in AD mouse models in a sex-dependent manner (Lee et al., 2019a; Martínez-Drudis et al., 2024, 2022). Learning-induced plasticity at mossy fiber-CA3 synapses during contextual memory formation is mediated by Neuronal PAS Domain Protein 4 (NPAS4)-induced PLK2 expression (Weng et al., 2018).

There are solid yet complex links between PLK2 and PD. Although most of the literature points towards a neurotoxic, pro-PD role of PLK2, and beneficial effects of PLK2 inhibition, a few articles report some protective roles of PLK2.

(1) PLK2 expression is upregulated in the midbrain of aged monkeys, correlating with increased SNCA Ser219 phosphorylation (McCormack et al., 2012), as observed in other animal models of synaptopathies (Mbefo et al., 2010). Incubation of SNCA with brain extracts from older Cynomolgus monkeys and in LB pathology -susceptible brain regions (striatum and hippocampus) leads to higher amounts of phosphorylated and oligomeric SNCA, compared to extracts from younger monkeys and non-LB- susceptible regions (cerebellum and occipital cortex). These age-dependent differences correlate with increased PLK2 levels and decreased phosphatase 2A levels. MPTP-treated Cynomolgus monkeys show increased PLK2 expression (Li et al., 2020c). Increased PLK2 expression is seen in an insulin-resistance model of PD (Hong et al., 2020). Iron-induced oxidative stress in SH-SY5Y cells and in rats is accompanied by increased expression of PLK2 and CSNK2, leading to SNCA Ser219 phosphorylation (Wang et al., 2019b).

(2) PLK2 phosphorylates SNCA on its best characterized phosphorylation site, Ser129. Phospho-Ser219 SNCA is indeed a hallmark of LBs (>90%), while soluble, physiological SNCA is only transiently phosphorylated (<4%) (Anderson et al., 2006; Fujiwara et al., 2002; Kawahata et al., 2022; Oueslati, 2016; Salvi et al., 2012). The role of Ser129 phosphorylation in SNCA aggregation and in neurodegeneration is still under debate and contested (see, for example ref. (Karampetsou et al., 2017; McFarland et al., 2009; Oueslati, 2016)). Although quite a few kinases phosphorylate Ser129 (see Fig. 21), PLK2 is considered as the major kinase phosphorylating this site (Aubele et al., 2013; Bergeron et al., 2014; Inglis et al., 2009; Mbefo et al., 2010; Salvi et al., 2012; Waxman and Giasson, 2011). Genetic deletion of PLK2 reduces SNCA Ser129 phosphorylation in presynaptic terminals but not in LBs, but provides some neuroprotection, suggesting that other targets may be involved (Weston et al., 2021). Pharmacological inhibition of PLK2 (human dopaminergic cell cultures, organotypic mouse hippocampal slices, SNCA transgenic mice) reduces Ser219 phosphorylation of nuclear, soluble SNCA (Elfarrash et al., 2021). In contrast, PLK2 inhibition neither blocks Ser219 phosphorylation of aggregated SNCA nor prevents or reverses the formation of aggregates. Conversely, overexpression of PLK2 or PLK3 in rat SN, leading to enhanced SNCA Ser219 phosphorylation, does not induce nigral dopaminergic cell death, nor accumulation of SNCA aggregates (Buck et al., 2015). Overexpression of PLK2 in a genetic rat model of PD (overexpressed SNCA) enhances SNCA Ser219 phosphorylation and degradation (Oueslati et al., 2013).

Synaptojanin 1: structural organization, phosphorylation sites and consequences of phosphorylation. Synaptojanin’s interaction with endophilin A1 is stimulated by ubiquitination by Parkin and is inhibited by phosphorylation at Thr1205 by LRRK2. Synaptojanin’s 5’-phosphatase activity is activated by phosphorylation at Ser1084 by DYRK1A and inhibited by phosphorylation at Ser1144 by CDK5, and at Tyr1018, 1019 and 1172 by EphB2 RTK. ADBE, activity-dependent bulk endocytosis; CME, clathrin-mediated endocytosis; PRD, proline-rich domain; RRM, RNA recognition motif; SAC1, suppressor of actin 1.

(3) In vitro phosphorylation of SNCA by PLK2 leads to increased aggregation rate and seeding properties compared to non-phosphorylated SNCA (Chaturvedi et al., 2023). PLK2 modulates SNCA aggregation in yeast and mammalian cells (Basso et al., 2013).

PLK2 overexpression promotes SNCA aggregation in vitro independently of Ser219 phosphorylation, while genetic or pharmacological (by TC-S7005) inhibition of PLK2 attenuates SNCA aggregation and neurotoxicity (Zhang et al., 2025a). In vivo, genetic silencing of PLK2 in the striatum of mice injected with preformed SNCA fibrils (PFFs) represses SNCA aggregation, prevents dopamine neuron loss and alleviates motor dysfunction (Zhang et al., 2025a).

Overexpression of PLK2 (and PLK3) triggers the ubiquitin-dependent autophagic degradation of SNCA through direct binding and Ser219 phosphorylation (Dahmene et al., 2017; Oueslati et al., 2013). In contrast, another article reports that overexpressed PLK2 decreases SNCA independently of SNCA Ser129 phosphorylation (Kofoed et al., 2017). Inhibition of PLK2 (compound 37) increases SNCA level in cell models and mouse brains. PLK2 regulates the transcription of SNCA mRNA (Kofoed et al., 2017). In another setting - rat enteric nervous system and enteric neurons - PLK2 inhibition also leads to increased SNCA secretion (Pinard et al., 2025).

(4) Dynactin 1 is a dynein-binding motor protein which mediates retrograde transport of various cargoes (including autophagosomes and lysosomes) and dendritogenesis (reviews in (Li et al., 2014b; Olenick and Holzbaur, 2019; Schroer, 2004; Tempes et al., 2020)). MAPT (prominent 4R-Tau forms are expressed) and mutations in the dynactin subunit 1 (DCNT1) gene are involved in progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), an atypical form of parkinsonism (review in (Wen et al., 2021)). DCTN1 mutations are also involved in Perry syndrome (PS), a rare hereditary neurodegenerative disease characterized by autosomal dominant parkinsonism, and other disorders (Dulski et al., 2021; Konno et al., 2017).

PLK2-mediated phosphorylation disrupts the autophagic flux, probably through the phosphorylation of DCTN1 (p150Glued) on Ser1098, which leads to impaired organelle movement, in particular impaired autophagosome-lysosome fusion (Zhang et al., 2025a). The non-phosphorylable S1098A DCTN1 mutant promotes autophagosome-lysosome fusion and reduces SNCA pathology. Dynactin knockdown leads to SNCA aggregation by blocking autophagy in a zebrafish PD model (Wu et al., 2025b). PLK2 thus appears to reduce the autophagic degradation of oligomeric SNCA, through DCTN1 phosphorylation -mediated restriction of autophagosome-lysosome fusion, thereby contributing to pathology (Fig. 16).

(5) Lipopolysaccharides (LPS) (endotoxins) trigger the rapid expression of PLK2 in microglial cells (Cheng et al., 2024). PLK2 phosphorylates HSP90α (heat shock protein 90 α) on Ser252 and Ser263 resulting in activation of the IKKβ/NF-κB (inhibitor of nuclear factor κB kinase subunit beta / nuclear factor-κ B) pathway, thereby promoting microglial activation. Knockdown or inhibition of HSP90α abolishes PLK2-mediated activation of NF-κB’s transcriptional activity and microglial activation. Knockdown or inhibition of PLK2 suppresses LPS-induced microglial activation (reduction of proinflammatory factors). Specific PLK2 KO in microglial cells in a mouse PD model (intracranial LPS injection) reduces neuroinflammation, dopaminergic neuron loss and motor deficits.

To our knowledge, PLK2 has only been reported to be phosphorylated by DYRK1A (Ser358) (Tan et al., 2023) and by PLK2 itself (Thr298, Ser301, Ser302, Ser324, Ser389, Thr416, Ser500, Ser591, Ser593) (Rozeboom and Pak, 2012). The autophosphorylation sites are implicated in PLK2 protein stability and catalytic activity. DYRK1A-mediated PLK2 phosphorylation on Ser358 leads to increased stability of PLK2, as well as increased catalytic activity as seen by increased phosphorylation of α-synuclein on Ser129 (Tan et al., 2023). In colorectal cancer, FAM117B stimulates the DYRK1A/PLK2 pathway (Yu et al., 2026). FAM117B has been found, in numerous studies and in various tissues, as an interactor of DYRK1A (Ananthapadmanabhan et al., 2023; Courraud et al., 2025; Guard et al., 2019; Varjosalo et al., 2013; Yu et al., 2026). FAM117B has been identified, in GWAS studies, as a candidate gene associated with autism spectrum disorders or learning disability (Roberts et al., 2014) and cerebral small vessel disease (Chung et al., 2019) and, in a transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS), as overexpressed in lacunar stroke (Traylor et al., 2021). FAM117B is expressed in endothelial cells, astrocytes, and neurons (Chung et al., 2019). Whether FAM117B is a general activator of DYRK1A remains to be studied.

In brief, increased PLK2 expression is significantly elevated during the onset and progression of PD. Although PLK2 phosphorylates SNCA on Ser219, this phosphorylation may not play a central role in the loss of dopaminergic cells. In contrast, phosphorylation of dynactin subunit 1 and HSP90α by PLK2 may respectively contribute to SNCA aggregation and microglial activation mediated by the IKKβ/NF-κB pathway. DYRK1A phosphorylates PLK2 on Ser358, enhancing its stability and activity. Inhibiting DYRK1A could therefore reduce PLK2 activity and auto-catalytic activation, potentially leading to reduced accumulation of oligomeric SNCA and mitigated microglia-induced neuroinflammation.

Ras-related proteins in brain (Rabs)

The family of Ras-related proteins in brain (Rabs) comprises about 70 proteins (reviews in (Homma et al., 2021; Parray, 2025; Pylypenko et al., 2018)). They are small GTPases (200-250 amino acids; ∼ 25 kDa) that regulate intracellular vesicle transport and fusion, neurotransmitter release, formation of primary cilia and other cellular functions.

The NTD carries the GTPase activity, while the CTD serves, through its prenylated cysteines (Farnsworth et al., 1994; Pereira-Leal et al., 2001), to anchor the protein in membranes. Specific Rabs are targeted to specific membranes (mitochondria, lysosomes, endoplasmic reticulum (ER), Golgi, etc.). Rabs either bind GTP or GDP, leading to major conformational modifications at regions known as switch-1 and switch-2 (Fig. 17). The binding of GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs) increases GTP hydrolysis, thereby favouring the formation of Rab-GDP complexes, while the binding of guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs) triggers the release of GDP, thereby promoting GTP binding. Binding of GDP-dissociation inhibitors (GDIs) allows the recycling of Rabs between membrane compartments.

Rabs: structure and phosphorylation sites. DYRK1A phosphorylates Rab1A and Rab3B on a conserved residue (Thr75 and Thr86, respectively), located in the Switch-2 domain. This highly conserved site ((Steger et al., 2016)) (box: sequences of Rabs cited in the text, phosphosite in green) is phosphorylated by LRRK2 in several Rabs, strongly modifying their interaction with various effectors. Prenylation (geranylgeranylation) at the C-terminal cysteines ensures binding of Rabs to membranes. SW-1, Switch-1 domain; SW-2, Switch-2 domain.

The involvement of Rabs in PD is supported by numerous reports (reviews in (Bellucci et al., 2022; Bentley-DeSousa et al., 2025; He et al., 2025; Martínez-Menárguez et al., 2021)):

(1) Rab GTPases are physiological substrates of LRRK2 on a conserved Thr or Ser residue (reviews in (Chatterjee et al., 2025; Malik et al., 2021; Pfeffer, 2023; Twellsieck and Boecker, 2025)) (Fig. 17). Rab10 and Rab12 were first identified as LRRK2 substrates, phosphorylated in the switch-2 region, at Thr73 and Ser106, respectively (Steger et al., 2016). The highly conserved corresponding Thr and Ser residues of other Rabs, including Rab3A, 3B, 3C, 3D, Rab8A, 8B, Rab29 (Rab7L1), and Rab35, can also be phosphorylated by LRRK2 (Steger et al., 2017) (Fig. 17). Pathogenic variants of LRRK2 increase the phosphorylation of Rabs (Berndsen et al., 2019; Steger et al., 2016). Rab35, and a subset of other Rabs, are phosphorylated by LRRK2, this phosphorylation (on Thr72) triggering substantial degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in vivo (Jeong et al., 2018).

(2) Rab12 binds to the N-terminal domain (Armadillo) of LRRK2, enhancing the kinase activity of LRRK2 towards Rab10 phosphorylation on Thr73 (Dhekne et al., 2023; Vides et al., 2022). Rab12 is actively recruited to damaged lysosomes, leading to local and LRRK2-dependent Rab10 phosphorylation (Wang et al., 2023b). Similarly, Rab29 recruits LRRK2 to the Golgi network and stimulates its phosphorylating activity towards Rab10 Thr73 (Purlyte et al., 2018). Pathogenic LRRK2 mutants are more readily recruited and activated by Rab29 than WT LRRK2.

(3) Mutations in the Rab29 gene (PARK16 locus) have been identified as risk factors for PD (Liu et al., 2018; MacLeod et al., 2013). A pathogenic Rab32 Ser71Arg variant activates LRRK2 kinase and causes autosomal dominant PD (Gustavsson et al., 2024). Other Rab alterations leading to PD and other forms of parkinsonism are reviewed in (Bellucci et al., 2022).

(4) Rab7A is phosphorylated at Ser72 by TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) (TANK, TRAF-associated NF-κB activator), a kinase involved in PINK1-PARKIN-mediated mitophagy (Heo et al., 2018; Lai et al., 2015). Rab8A, 8B and 13 are phosphorylated at a highly conserved residue (Ser111) by an unidentified kinase in response to PINK1 activation (Lai et al., 2015).

(5) Rab3A-D regulate synaptic vesicles and lysosomes trafficking, neuronal transmission and autophagy and play a key role in PD (review in (He et al., 2025)). Phosphorylation of Rab3A by LRRK2 impairs interaction with and formation of the MADD (MAP kinase activating death domain protein) and kinesin motor proteins KIF1A/1Bβ complex (kinesin family member 1A/1Bβ), therefore disrupting anterograde synaptic vesicle precursor transport (Dou et al., 2024, 2023). Rab3 phosphorylation on Thr86 by gain-of-function pathogenic mutants of LRRK2 impairs trafficking of synaptic vesicles (Dou et al., 2024). Rab3 phosphorylation on Thr86 is aberrantly increased in LRRK2G2019S mouse brain striatal synaptosome fractions and this phosphorylation is reduced when mice are treated with the LRRK2 inhibitor MLi-2 (Chen et al., 2023). Rab3A phosphorylation decreases binding to the RIM1/2 (Rab-3-interacting molecule 1/2) effector proteins, disrupting dopamine release.

(6) Rab1 plays a critical role in ER-to-Golgi vesicular transport, a process which is altered in PD (review in (Martínez-Menárguez et al., 2021)). Rab1 overexpression in cellular and animal models of PD protects from SNCA-induced dopaminergic neuron loss (Cooper et al., 2006). Results obtained in a cell-free system show that SNCA inhibits ER-to-Golgi trafficking and that Rab1, Rab3A and Rab8A suppress SNCA-induced toxicity in vitro (Gitler et al., 2008). Rab1A overexpression in an SNCA-based rat PD model improves motor behaviour (Coune et al., 2011). These latter beneficial effects, requiring Rab1A prenylation, correlate with improved ER-to-Golgi vesicle trafficking transport and reduction of Golgi fragmentation in nigral dopaminergic neurons. The N-arylbenzimidazole NAB2 stimulates ER-to-Golgi trafficking and is neuroprotective in various cellular models of PD (Chung et al., 2013; Hatstat et al., 2021; Tardiff et al., 2013) . The NAB2 target was recently identified as the Rab1A-GDP complex (Hatstat et al., 2022). NAB2 treatment or Rab1A overexpression protect from SNCA-induced toxicity (Cooper et al., 2006; Gitler et al., 2008; Hatstat et al., 2022).

(7) Rab3a interacts with SNCA (Chen et al., 2013), synapsin I (Giovedì et al., 2004) and synaptotagmin (Wang et al., 2022d) on synaptic vesicles.

Rab GTPases are phosphorylated by multiple kinases (review in (Homma et al., 2021; Waschbüsch and Khan, 2020; Xu et al., 2021)), in particular in the conserved Thr/Ser site located in the switch-2 domain (Fig. 17). Phosphorylation at this site impairs the interaction of Rabs with GEFs and GAPs, but favours the binding of other effector proteins (review in (Xu et al., 2021)). The kinases that phosphorylate the two Rabs (Rab1A and Rab3) described as DYRK1A substrates comprise (Fig. 17): CDK1 (Ser194, Thr195? in Rab1A) (Bailly et al., 1991), DYRK1A (Thr75 in Rab1A; Thr54, Thr86 in Rab3A) (Chatterjee et al., 2025; Z. Wang et al., 2023), LRRK1 (Thr86 in Rab3A) (Chatterjee et al., 2025), LRRK2 (Thr75 in Rab1A, Thr86 in Rab3A) (Chatterjee et al., 2025; Steger et al., 2017), MST1 (Thr54, Thr86 in Rab3A) (Chatterjee et al., 2025), TAK1 (TGFβ-activated kinase 1) (Thr75 in Rab1A) (Levin et al., 2016). Many more Rabs are phosphorylated by many more kinases. The phosphorylation sites appear to be quite conserved.

DYRK1A was initially described to be a Rab-phosphorylating kinase in a phosphoproteomics study carried out in an ascidian model of notochord development (Wang et al., 2023c). Rab1A and Rab3A are phosphorylated by DYRK1A in vitro on Thr75 and Thr54/Thr86, respectively. The effects of Rab phosphorylation by DYRK1A are unknown. Yet, Rab1A Thr75 and Rab3A Thr86 correspond to the conserved LRRK2 phosphorylation site. Interestingly, expression of a third DYRK1A gene copy in mice leads to transcriptional reduction in several genes encoding presynaptic proteins including Rab3A and its interactor RIM1, and to the inhibition of an NMDA (N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor)-independent, presynaptic form of long-term potentiation (LTP) (Lepagnol-Bestel et al., 2025).

In brief, LRRK2 phosphorylates Rabs on a highly conserved residue, leading to impaired transport of synaptic vesicles and to compromised ER-to-Golgi trafficking. DYRK1A can phosphorylate the very same conserved site (and other sites), thereby somewhat mimicking LRRK2’s action on Rabs. Inhibiting DYRK1A inhibition is therefore expected to help mitigate dysregulated vesicle transport, as well as the resulting abnormalities in neurotransmission and autophagic.

Regulator of calcineurin 1 (RCAN1)

RCAN1 (regulator of calcineurin 1) (full length RCAN1.1L: 252 amino acids, 28.1 kDa; alternative splicing variant RCAN1.1S: 197 amino acids, 22.8 kDa), previously known as DSCR1 (Down syndrome candidate region 1 gene), MCIP1 (modulatory calcineurin-interacting protein 1), Adapt78, or calcipressin 1 (Davies et al., 2007), is the most studied of the RCAN three -member family (reviews in (Lee and Ahnn, 2020; Minami, 2014; Peiris and Keating, 2018; Serrano-Candelas et al., 2014)). RCAN1 inhibits the activity of the Ca++/calmodulin-modulated calcineurin phosphatase, which regulates the nuclear translocation and activity of the NFAT transcription factor (see section on NFAT).

RCAN1 and calcineurin have been co-crystallized, revealing the details of their interaction (Li et al., 2020e). RCAN1 comprises two domains, (1) an N-terminal RNA-recognition domain and (2) a C-terminal calcineurin-binding domain (Fig. 18). The interaction between RCAN1 and calcineurin occurs at four distinct domains sites: a putative dimerization domain containing an amphipathic leucine repeat (LAVP), a central Ser-Pro (SP) repeat domain (SPPASPP), a C-terminal calcineurin-binding domain (PSVVVH) and an inhibitory domain (TRPP).

RCAN1: structural organization, phosphorylation sites and consequences of phosphorylation. RRM, RNA-recognition domain, which binds mRNA. LAPP [96-99], binding at interface of calcineurin A and B; SPPASPP [108-114] motif, acting as a pseudo-substrate of calcineurin; PSVVVH motif [154-159], binding to catalytic domain of calcineurin; TRRP motif [186-189], binding to calcineurin at the substrate recruitment of the active site. Amino acids are numbered according to human RCAN1 isoform 2 (P53805-2) which is highly expressed in the brain and nervous system.

RCAN1 has been extensively studied in numerous physiological settings, in particular in the nervous system, where it regulates bidirectional synaptic plasticity, i.e. both LTP and long-term depression (LTD), learning and memory (Dudilot et al., 2020; Hoeffer et al., 2007; Martin et al., 2012). RCAN1 has been implicated in conditions such as DS (Martin et al., 2012; Park et al., 2009), AD (Asai et al., 2016; Cook et al., 2005; Harris et al., 2007), Huntington disease (Lee et al., 2024b), cardiovascular diseases (Wang et al., 2020a), cancer (Lao et al., 2022), type 2 diabetes (Peiris et al., 2012), immunity (Han et al., 2017) (calcineurin is the target of the immunosuppressive drugs FK506 (Tacrolimus) and Cyclosporin A), etc. RCAN1 participates to mitochondrial structural and functional integrity.

The link between RCAN1 and PD/DLB stems from the following observations:

(1) LRRK2 binds to and phosphorylates RCAN1, leading to reduced RCAN1/calcineurin binding, increased calcineurin activity and stimulation of IL-1β-mediated inflammatory signalling (Han et al., 2017).

(2) RCAN1 regulates the activity of calcineurin and NFAT (Fig. 13, top), two key factors involved in the toxicity of SNCA (see below).

(3) RCAN1 expression is elevated in brain tissue (temporal cortex) of patients with AD and DLB (Malakooti et al., 2019).

RCAN1 is phosphorylated by CSNK2 (Ser143) (Martínez-Høyer et al., 2013), DYRK1A (Ser112, Thr192) (Jung et al., 2011b; Song et al., 2013), ERK5/BMK1 activated by MEK5 (Ser112) (Abbasi et al., 2006), GSK3 (Ser108) (Hilioti et al., 2004; Vega et al., 2002), LRRK2 (unidentified sites in the CTD) (Han et al., 2017), MEK1 (Ser108, Ser112) (Kim et al., 2022), NIK (NF-κB-inducing kinase) (unidentified sites in the CTD [90-197]) (Lee et al., 2008), PKA (Ser93) (Dudilot et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2015, 2012), p38α MAP kinase (Ser93, Ser108, Ser112, Thr124, Thr153, Thr192) (Li et al., 2020e; Ma et al., 2012a), TAK1 (Ser94, Ser136) (Liu et al., 2009).

DYRK1A directly interacts with and phosphorylates RCAN1 on Ser112 and Thr192 (Jung et al., 2011b; Song et al., 2013). Phosphorylation of Ser112 primes RCAN1 for phosphorylation by GSK3β at Ser108, which in principle should lead to increased calcineurin activity. Yet there is no difference in terms of calcineurin activity in cells expressing WT RCAN1 or S112A-RCAN1 (Jung et al., 2011b). In contrast, phosphorylation of Thr192 enhances RCAN1 stability and favours binding to and inhibition of calcineurin, leading to reduced NFAT transcriptional activity and increased Tau phosphorylation (Thr212, Ser404) (Jung et al., 2011b). RCAN1 phosphorylation on Th192 promotes RCAN1 aggregation (Song et al., 2013). DYRK1A thus increases Thr212-Tau phosphorylation by direct phosphorylation (see above) and by inhibiting the calcineurin phosphatase which dephosphorylates this site (Jung et al., 2011b). DYRK1A inhibitors are thus expected to activate the calcineurin-NFAT pathway. This was observed with the inhibitor proINDY in a zebrafish model (Tucker Edmister and Creton, 2022). Increased dosage of DYRK1A and RCAN1 (both encoded by genes located on chromosome 21) reduce the transcriptional activity of NFAT, thereby delaying neuronal differentiation of neural progenitors (Kurabayashi and Sanada, 2013).

In brief, the activity of RCAN1 appears to be associated with PD. DYRK1A phosphorylates RCAN1 on Thr192, leading to increased binding to calcineurin, decreased calcineurin activity and reduced downstream NFAT signaling. DYRK1A also phosphorylates RCAN1 on Ser112, thereby priming it for phosphorylation by GSK3β at Ser108, which could lead to release of active calcineurin. However, RCAN1 appears to be dominantly regulated by Thr192 phosphorylation. DYRK1A inhibitors are thus expected to lead to reduced Thr192-phosphorylation, reduced RCAN1 activity, subsequent increased calcineurin activity, and enhanced NFAT-dependent transcription.

Septin-4 (SEPT4)

Septins, a family of 13 membrane-interacting GTP-binding proteins, are considered as the fourth element of the cytoskeleton, along with actin filaments, microtubules and intermediate filaments. Septins assemble in oligomers in a combinatorial fashion. They interact with the actin and microtubule cytoskeleton and play a role in cytoskeletal organization, cytokinesis, membrane dynamics, exocytosis, cell polarity, and cell shape control (reviews in (Benoit et al., 2023; Mostowy and Cossart, 2012; Sharma and Menon, 2023; Spiliotis and Nakos, 2021)). Altered expression and mislocalization of septins contribute to synaptic dysfunction in multiple neurodegenerative diseases (review in (Marttinen et al., 2015)).

Septins comprise four main domains: (1) a variable NTD containing the SIAH-binding motif (SIAH, seven in absentia homologue E3 ubiquitin ligase), (2) a polybasic region (P), (3) the guanine nucleotide-binding domain (or G-domain) and (4) a CTD comprising a coiled coil domain which allows interaction with other septins (Fig. 19).

Septin 4: structural organization and phosphorylation sites. Ser68 (present in mouse, not in human) and Ser107 are putative phosphorylation sites for DYRK1A (not formally identified), and Ser 100 and Ser107 are phosphorylated by GSK3β. These sites are present in the splice variant ARTS as Ser81 and Ser88, respectively. ARTS, Apoptosis-related protein in the TGF-β signalling pathway; CC, Coiled coil domain; G1 (residues 151-159 in septin 4), P-loop, interacts with the phosphate of GTP; G2 (182-186) (Switch I), contains a conserved threonine residue critical for GTP hydrolysis; G3 (208-212) (Switch II), binds Mg++, essential for coordination of GTP hydrolysis; G4 (289-293), binds the guanine base of GTP; P, polybasic region (135-141), binds phosphatidylinositides (phosphatidyl-inositol 4,5-bisphosphate and phosphatidyl-inositol 3,4,5-trisphosphate) (Zhang et al., 1999); S, SIAH-binding motif (SIAH, seven in absentia homologue E3 ubiquitin ligase); SUE, Septin Unique Element; X, unique 27-amino-acid ARTS C-terminal XIAP binding domain (X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis protein).

Septin-4 (SEPT4) (478 amino acids, 54.9 kDa) appears to be involved in PD:

(1) Septin-4 is found in SNCA cytoplasmic inclusions seen in the substantia nigra pars compacta from postmortem PD and DLB brains (Ihara et al., 2007, 2003; Shehadeh et al., 2009). Septin-4 colocalizes with SNCA and is downregulated in the striatum of PD patients (Ageta-Ishihara et al., 2013). In healthy brain septin-4 is present in presynaptic terminals of dopamine neurons (Ageta-Ishihara et al., 2013). Septin-4 binds to SNCA in brain and in cultured cells (Ihara et al., 2003) and this binding prevents septin-4 oligomerization (Ageta-Ishihara et al., 2013). Co-expression of SNCA and septin-4 accelerates cell death induced by the proteasome inhibitor, lactacystin (Ihara et al., 2003).

(2) A short splice variant of Sept4, known as ARTS (apoptosis-related protein in the TGF-β signalling pathway), localizes to the mitochondrial outer membrane and is involved in cell death signalling (Larisch et al., 2000; Mandel-Gutfreund et al., 2011). ARTS levels are upregulated in SH-SY5Y undergoing apoptosis and in dopaminergic neurons from 6-OHDA treated rat brains (Kemeny et al., 2012). Although the E3 ubiquitin ligase parkin binds both ARTS and the non-apoptotic, full-size variant of septin-4, it only ubiquitinates and degrades ARTS. Overexpression of parkin protects from ARTS-induced cell death. Mutant, inactive parkin leads to increased levels of ARTS and apoptosis (Kemeny et al., 2012). Septin-4 accumulates in Drosophila parkin mutant brains (Muñoz-Soriano et al., 2012). Parkin may thus protect neurons from cell death by regulating the level of ARTS.

(3) Septin-4-/- mice show decreased dopamine content in the striatum and attenuated dopamine transmission as well as reduced TH (the rate-limiting factor in dopamine synthesis) and DAT. TH and DAT levels are corrected when these mice are crossed with mice overexpressing septin-4. Furthermore crossing of transgenic mice expressing human SNCAA53T (mutant protein responsible for familial PD) with Septin-4-/- mice exacerbates SNCA aggregates (with enhanced Ser129-phosphorylated SNCA), neuropathology, and locomotor deterioration (Ageta-Ishihara et al., 2013). Increased septin-4 expression results in reduced SNCAA53T phosphorylation on Ser129 (Ageta-Ishihara et al., 2013). Septin-4 thus appears to prevent SNCA phosphorylation at the Ser129 site. The similar hypo-dopaminergic phenotypes seen in Septin-4-/- mice and in SNCA-/- mice (Abeliovich et al., 2000) support the functional convergence of the two proteins.

(4) transgenic mice expressing the full-length septin-4 variant in the brain show consistent attenuation of voluntary activities and altered social behaviours, but no neurodegeneration (Ageta-Ishihara et al., 2013).

(5) Septin-4 is phosphorylated by GSK3β (Ser100, Ser107) (Koch et al., 2015).

A search for DYRK1A (kinase domain) partners using a yeast two-hybrid screen led to the identification of a Septin-4 splice variant (Sitz et al., 2008). The DYRK1A/septin-4 interaction was confirmed in mammalian cells by co-immunoprecipitation. HEK-293-expressed septin-4 is phosphorylated in vitro by recombinant DYRK1A. Septin-4 (but not septin-5) is phosphorylated when both DYRK1A and septin-4 (or septin-5) are coexpressed in HEK-293 cells. The DYRK1A inhibitor harmine prevents septin-4 phosphorylation. DYRK1A and septin-4 coexpress and co-localize in mouse brain neurons. Two putative DYRK1A consensus phosphorylation sites are located in the NTD of septin-4 (Ser68 (mouse only) and Ser107) (Sitz et al., 2008). These sites are present in the ARTS variant. The exact phosphorylation site by DYRK1A is still to be identified. The effects of septin-4 phosphorylation by DYRK1A on interaction with SNCA, oligomerization, or intracellular distribution are unknown. Since septin-4 is phosphorylated by GSK3β (Koch et al., 2015), it is possible that DYRK1A acts again as a priming kinase, i.e. allowing GSK3β to phosphorylate septin-4 on Ser100 when Ser107 has been pre-phosphorylated by DYRK1A.

In brief, septin-4 is a substrate for Parkin. Septin-4 appears to play an important role in regulating dopamine content and reducing SNCA Ser129 phosphorylation. In contrast the septin-4 splice variant ARTS plays a role in cell death signalling. DYRK1A interacts with and phosphorylates septin-4 (possibly ARTS), with unknown consequences.

Signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3)

The signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) protein (770 amino acids, 88.1 kDa) belongs to the seven-member (STAT1-4, 5A, 5B, 6) family of STAT transcription factors that mediate many aspects of cellular immunity, proliferation, apoptosis and differentiation. (reviews in (Godugu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024b)).

The structure of STAT3 comprises an NTD followed by a coiled coil domain, a DNA-binding domain (containing an NLS), a linker, an SH2 domain, and a C-terminal TAD (Fig. 20). Alternative splicing leads to the STAT3-β isoform where the last 55 amino acids are replaced by 7 unique residues and where Ser727 (see below) is missing (Maritano et al., 2004). Binding of cytokines (IL-6, 11, 27, 31) or growth factors (EGF, FGF, TGF-β, retinoic acid) to their receptors leads to the recruitment of STAT3 through the SH2 domain. This is followed by phosphorylation by Janus kinases (JAK1-3 (JAK, just another kinase) and TYK2 on Tyr707 (Agashe et al., 2022), and Ser727 phosphorylation by other kinases (see below), thereby promoting homodimerization (or heterodimerization with other STATs), nuclear translocation of STAT dimers and induction of transcription of specific genes. In addition, STAT3 intervenes in a non-canonical pathway, playing a non-transcriptional role in mitochondrial electron transport chain and in the ER.

STAT3: structural organization, phosphorylation sites and consequences of phosphorylation. Phosphorylation at Tyr705 and Ser727 triggers STAT dimerization, nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity. STAT3 expression is inhibited by the PD-relevant miR-124, itself being sponged and inhibited by circHIPK3. SH2, Src homology 2 domain; TAD, transactivation domain.

The links between STAT3 and PD/DLB are numerous. Most reports support the idea that both neuroinflammation (see ‘DYRK1A and neuroinflammation’ section) and neuronal degeneration is associated with STAT3 phosphorylation, nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity.

(1) STAT3 and NF-κB mediate FYN kinase-driven dopaminergic neurons degeneration, microglia activation and inflammatory cytokine production in a zebrafish model (Siddiqui et al., 2024).

(2) Dopaminergic neurotoxins induce cell death and neuroinflammatory responses through nuclear translocation of STAT3 and NF-κB (review in (Nabavi et al., 2019)). Consequently, STAT3 inhibition by numerous pharmacological molecules provides neuroprotection and reduced neuroinflammation. A few examples: Apamin (an inhibitor of small-conductivity Ca++-activated K+ channel) protects SH-SY5Y cells and rat embryo primary mesencephalic dopaminergic neurons from MPP+-induced degeneration by decreasing Ca++ overload and indirectly reducing STAT3 and NF-κB translocation (Park et al., 2022). β-Hydroxybutyrate downregulates STAT3-mediated NLRP3 inflammosome activation in vitro (MPP+) and in vivo (MPTP) (Jiang et al., 2022). Similar results have been reported with echinacoside (Yang et al., 2022) and narirutin (Gao et al., 2025b), etc.

(3) miR-124 is one of the most abundantly expressed micro-RNA in the brain, it appears to play a neuroprotective role in PD through various pathways. The potential use of miR-124 as a biomarker and a therapeutic is being evaluated ((Esteves et al., 2024, 2022; Zhang et al., 2022b; Zhang et al., 2024); review in (Angelopoulou et al., 2019)). miR-124 is downregulated in a multitude of 6-OHDA, MPP+ and MPTP -induced models of PD as well as in PD patients (review in (Angelopoulou et al., 2019)). For example, miR-124-3p expression is decreased while STAT3 expression is increased in SH-SY5Y cells exposed to MPP+ (Geng et al., 2017). Overexpression of miR-124-3p reduces STAT3 expression and attenuates the effects of MPP+. STAT3 is a direct target of miR-124-3p (Geng et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2023c). miR-124 also inhibits STAT3 in glioma cancer stem cells and the therapeutic effect of miR-124 is mediated by CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Wei et al., 2013) (see ‘DYRK1A and neuroinflammation’ section). The circular RNA homeodomain interacting protein kinase 3 (circHIPK3) sponges and inhibits miR-124 (Zheng et al., 2016). CircHIPK3 is increased in serum and CSF from PD patients compared to matching healthy controls, while the levels of miR-124 is reduced (Zhang et al., 2023c). Similarly, LPS treatment of BV2 cells leads to increased circHIPK3, reduced miR-124, and enhanced expression of STAT3 and inflammatory factors (Zhang et al., 2023c). The neuroinflammatory effects of circHIPK3 are mediated by a miR-124-dependent STAT3 pathway.

(4) In vitro exposure of microglial cells and macrophages to SNCA leads to activation of the JAK/STAT pathway (including STAT1 and STAT3 phosphorylation), and JAK1/2 inhibition by AZD1480 leads to reduced STAT1/STAT3 activation and prevents inflammatory gene expression (Qin et al., 2016). In vivo, AAV2-mediated overexpression of SNCA in the SN of rats leads to neuroinflammation (microglial activation, macrophage and CD4+ T cell infiltration) and neurodegeneration, both being prevented by AZD1480 (Qin et al., 2016). Similar results are obtained following intra-striatum injection of SNCA oligomers: increased interleukin 6 cytokine family signal transducer (IL6ST), a component of the interleukin receptor leading to JAK2/JAK3 activation, increased STAT3 phosphorylation and activation, and subsequent increased expression of hypoxia-inducible factor HIF-1α, and enhanced ferroptosis (Zhang et al., 2025b).

Over 80 post-translational modifications of STAT3 have been described. STAT3 is phosphorylated at more than 41 sites (reviews in (Chen et al., 2025; Diallo and Herrera, 2022)), Tyr705 and Ser727 being, by far, the most studied sites (Decker and Kovarik, 2000; Wen et al., 1995). Only a few kinases phosphorylating STAT3 have been identified (Fig. 20): breast tumour kinase (BRK)/ protein tyrosine kinase 6 (PTK6) (undefined site) (Liu et al., 2006), DYRK1A (Ser727) (Bhansali et al., 2021; Khor et al., 2015; Kurabayashi et al., 2015; Li et al., 2010; Malueg et al., 2025; Matsuo et al., 2001; Wiechmann et al., 2003), ERK1 & ERK2 (Ser727) (Plaza-Menacho et al., 2007), GSK3α/β (Thr714, Ser727) (Waitkus et al., 2014), JAK1, JAK2 & JAK3 (Tyr705) (review in (Agashe et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2025)), interleukin-1 receptor associated kinase (IRAK1) (Ser727) (Huang et al., 2004b), JNK (Ser727) (Lim and Cao, 1999), lymphocyte cell-specific protein-tyrosine kinase (LCK)/FYN (Tyr705) (Qin et al., 2024b), PKCε (Ser727) (Aziz et al., 2007), P38 (Ser727) (Xu et al., 2003), SRC (Tyr14, Tyr45, Tyr68, Tyr705) (Diallo and Herrera, 2022; Huang et al., 2018), TBK1 (Thr754) (Hsia et al., 2017), tropomyosin receptor kinase A (TRKA) (Ser727) (Ng et al., 2006), TYK2 (Tyr640, Tyr705) (Mori et al., 2017), zipper-interacting protein kinase (ZIPK) (Ser727) (Sato et al., 2005).

DYRK1A phosphorylates STAT3 on Ser727, triggering STAT3 nuclear transfer and transcriptional activation in various situations such as B cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (Bhansali et al., 2021), astrogliosis in a DS mouse model (Kurabayashi et al., 2015), pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (Lan et al., 2024), non-small cell lung cancer cells (Li et al., 2019), CD4+ T cells (Malueg et al., 2025). In all these examples, pharmacological inhibition, gene silencing (shRNA) or genetic inactivation or depletion of DYRK1A attenuate or inhibit STAT3 transcriptional activation.

In brief, STAT3 transcriptional activity is involved in PD-associated neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration. STAT3 is phosphorylated on Ser727 by DYRK1A (and other kinases), and on Tyr705, leading to homo- or hetero-dimerization, nuclear translocation and transcriptional activation. Pharmacological inhibition of DYRK1A reduces or blocks STAT3 transcriptional activity.

Synaptojanin 1 (SYNJ1)

Synaptojanin 1 (SYNJ1) (1612 amino acids, 173 kDa) is a polyphosphate-5-phosphatase which is highly expressed in presynaptic neurons terminals (Choudhry et al., 2021; Woscholski et al., 1997). It comprises four domains (Fig. 21): (1) a Sac1 (suppressor of actin 1) -like domain which dephosphorylates PI(3)P (phosphatidylinositol-3-phosphate), PI(4)P (phosphatidylinositol-4-phosphate), and PI(3,5)P2 (phosphatidylinositol-3,5-bisphosphate), (2) a 5’-phosphoinositol phosphatase which dephosphorylates PI(4,5)P2 (phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate), PI(3,4,5)P3 (phosphatidylinositol-3,4,5-trisphosphate), IP3 (inositol trisphosphate) and IP4 (inositol tetrakisphosphate) (Kontaxi et al., 2023), (3) an RNA recognition motif (RRM) and (4) a PRD which binds various endocytosis-related proteins (endophilin A1, amphiphysins, intersectin) (Choudhry et al., 2021; Woscholski et al., 1997). There are two splicing variants for synaptojanin 1, leading to a 145 kDa isoform, mostly localized in the brain, and a 173 kDa isoform widely distributed in the body. Synaptojanin 1 dual phosphatase activity is essential for synaptic vesicles recycling (clathrin-mediated endocytosis) and neurotransmitter release (reviews in (Wolfes and Dean, 2020; Choudhry et al., 2021; Cunningham and Moore, 2020; Mani et al., 2007; Paesmans et al., 2020; Rizo, 2022; Rothman et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022c)). Since the SYNJ1 gene is located on chromosome 21, synaptojanin 1 is overexpressed in the brains of DS patients, leading to the enlargement of early endosomes (Arai et al., 2002; Cossec et al., 2012; Hwang et al., 2022). Ts65Dn mice DS model or transgenic mice overexpressing synaptojanin 1 exhibit reduced PI(4,5)P2 levels and show learning deficits (Voronov et al., 2008).

Several observations point to a role of synaptojanin 1 in PD:

(1) Several SYNJ1 LoF mutations cause early-onset, autosomal recessive parkinsonism (Ben Romdhan et al., 2018; Bouhouche et al., 2017; Drouet and Lesage, 2014; Hong et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2015; Kirola et al., 2016; Krebs et al., 2013; Ng et al., 2023; Quadri et al., 2013; Rauschendorf et al., 2017; Lesage et al., 2021). One of the main synaptojanin 1 mutations, R258Q, blocks PI(3)P and PI(4)P dephosphorylation by the Sac1 domain, but does not alter the 5’-phosphatase activity (Krebs et al., 2013). This mutation affects ciliary properties in iPSC-derived dopaminergic neurons (Mohd Rafiq et al., 2024). Mice carrying this mutation show early onset PD-like motor deficits, accumulation of clathrin-coated intermediates at presynaptic terminals, decreased synaptic vesicles, synaptic endocytic dysfunction and abnormalities of nigral dopaminergic neurons (Cao et al., 2017; Krebs et al., 2013). Synaptojanin 1 haploinsufficiency in mice induces dopamine neuron vulnerability and SNCA accumulation (Pan et al., 2020).

(2) Synaptojanin-1 phosphorylation by LRRK2 at Thr1205 is reported to disrupt its interaction with endophilin A1 which is required for synaptic vesicle endocytosis (Pan et al., 2017).

(3) Upon phosphorylation by CaMK2, Parkin recruits synaptojanin 1 for ubiquitination, resulting in its binding to endophilin A1 and synaptic vesicle endocytosis (Song et al., 2023). Mutant parkin leads to dysregulated synaptic vesicle recycling.

Several phosphorylation sites have been described in synaptojanin 1 (Fig. 21): synaptojanin 1 is phosphorylated by CDK5 (Ser1144) (Lee et al., 2004a, 2004b), CDK16/cyclin Y (undetermined site) (Shehata et al., 2019), DYRK1A (Ser1029 in Drosophila (Ser1084 in humans)) (Adayev et al., 2006; Chen et al., 2014; Geng et al., 2016; Peng et al., 2021), Ephrin B2 receptor tyrosine kinase (EphB2 RTK) (Tyr1018, Tyr1059, Tyr1172) (Irie et al., 2005), LRRK2 (Thr1173, Thr1205) (Islam et al., 2016; Pan et al., 2017), and the neuron-specific p21-activated kinase 5 (PAK5) (Ser1291) (Strochlic et al., 2012).

DYRK1A phosphorylates synaptojanin 1 at multiple sites (Adayev et al., 2006; Chen et al., 2014), including Ser1029 in Drosophila (Ser1084 in humans) (Geng et al., 2016; Peng et al., 2021). Phosphorylation leads to enhanced 5’-phosphoinositol phosphatase activity (in contrast to phosphorylation by CDK5 and EphB2 RTK), decreased interaction with endophilin and facilitates active synaptic vesicle endocytosis (exo/endo recycling pool (ECP) but at the expense of reserve vesicle pool (RP) recycling (Chen et al., 2014; Geng et al., 2016). Dephosphorylated synaptojanin 1 maintains RP vesicle endocytosis. Phospho-null SynjS1029A mutants show no increased 5’-phosphoinositol phosphatase activity, but increased interaction with endophilin, and are defective in endocytosis of the active recycling pool of synaptic vesicles. In contrast, phospho-mimetic SynjS1029E mutants display high 5’-phosphoinositol phosphatase activity and reduced interaction with endophilin, compared to SynjS1029E (Geng et al., 2016). Mutants of mnb, the Drosophila DYRK1A homolog, show altered synaptic growth and morphology and impaired synaptic vesicle endocytosis which can be rescued by overexpression of Synj (Chen et al., 2014).

Endocytosis and refilling of synaptic vesicles with neurotransmitter at the presynaptic level is an essential part of neurotransmission (reviews in (Chanaday et al., 2019; Mochida, 2022; Saheki and De Camilli, 2012)). Synaptic vesicles are recovered by four different mechanisms, depending of the synaptic activity or synapse type: (1) fusion pore closure (‘kiss-and-run’), (2) ultrafast endocytosis, (3) clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) (under mild synaptic activity) and (4) activity-dependent bulk endocytosis (ADBE) (under intense synaptic activity). CME occurs during mild neuronal activity, while ADBE occurs during intense neuronal activity. Mnb (DYRK1A) and calcineurin coordinate ADBE through, respectively, synaptojanin 1 phosphorylation and dephosphorylation (Peng et al., 2021). Mnb facilitates CME while it inhibits ADBE. Ca++-activated calcineurin, by dephosphorylating the site phosphorylated by Mnb, promotes ADBE. Since the interaction of synapojanin1 and endophilin is involved in ultrafast endocytosis (Watanabe et al., 2018), phosphorylation of synaptojanin 1 by DYRK1A may also be involved in this synaptic vesicle endocytosis process.

In brief, the presynaptic protein synaptojanin 1 plays a key role in endocytosis of synaptic vesicles. Its phosphorylation by DYRK1A (Ser1084) leads to increased 5’-phosphoinositol phosphatase activity, inhibition of association with the endocytosis protein endophilin-A and suppression of activity-dependent bulk endocytosis (ADBE). During intense neuronal activity, calcineurin dephosphorylates synaptojanin 1, leading to reduced 5’-phosphoinositol phosphatase activity, enhanced association with endophilin-A, stimulated ADBE and increased synaptic plasticity. DYRK1A inhibition is expected to mimic these effects.

α-Synuclein (SNCA)

α-Synuclein (SNCA) (140 amino acids, 14.5 kDa) is an intrinsically unfolded and soluble protein which comprises three domains (Fig. 22): (1) an amphipathic NTD (AMP), thought to mediate interactions of SNCA with lipid membranes, (2) a central, hydrophobic, non-amyloid-β component (NAC) which folds in β-sheets leading to SNCA aggregation as fibrils, and (3) a flexible, acidic, proline-rich CTD (ACID), thought to mediate protein/protein interactions, Ca++ binding and binding to synaptic membranes (reviews in (Bernal-Conde et al., 2019; Burré, 2015; Burré et al., 2018; Calabresi et al., 2023a; Galkin et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2014; Oliveira et al., 2021)). SNCA is subject to numerous post-translational modifications including acetylation, glycation, glycosylation, nitration, phosphorylation, ubiquitination, sumoylation and proteolytic truncation (reviews in (Brembati et al., 2023; Burré et al., 2018; Manzanza et al., 2021; Yoo et al., 2022)). It exists as soluble, membrane-bound and aggregated forms (oligomers, fibrils and larger aggregates in LBs) (review in (Graves et al., 2023)).

SNCA: structural organization, phosphorylation sites and consequences of phosphorylation. Phosphorylation at Tyr39 by c-ABL inhibits autophagic degradation of SNCA. Phosphorylation at Thr64 by CSNK1 leads to formation of small SNCA oligomers and toxicity. Phosphorylation at Tyr125 by FYN, c-SRC or SYK primes SNCA for further phosphorylation by CSNK1 (and probably other kinases). Phosphorylation of Ser129 contributes to either fibril formation and aggregation in LBs, but may also lead to synaptic functions and neuroprotection. Phosphorylation of Ser87 has different outcomes on SNCA aggregation, depending on molecular forms. GCIs, glial cytoplasmic inclusions SNCA isolated from (from multiple system atrophy patients); DLB, SNCA from DLB patients.

SNCA was first identified in cholinergic synaptic vesicles purified from the electric organ of the Pacific electric ray Torpedo californica (Maroteaux et al., 1988). SNCA is predominantly expressed in the brain. It mainly localizes to presynaptic terminals where it may play a role in neurotransmitter release and synaptic plasticity (Burré et al., 2018). SNCA’s physiological and pathological functions are not completely understood: it is involved in synaptic activity, plasticity and vesicle pool maintenance and trafficking, neurotransmitter release, dopamine metabolism, learning, etc. SNCA also binds to mitochondria, contributing to both normal functions and pathological effects (review and discussion in (Bernal-Conde et al., 2019; Espay et al., 2025b; Espay and Lees, 2025a)).

SNCA was the first genetic factor identified to be associated with familial PD (Polymeropoulos et al., 1997). Several missense mutations or gene duplications or triplications lead to early-onset PD. SNCA has been linked to a wide variety of neurodegenerative diseases2, collectively known as “synucleinopathies”. SNCA accumulates in LBs, the cytoplasmic inclusions characteristic of sporadic PD and DLB. Mutations and gene dosage changes affect SNCA aggregation and are observed in autosomal dominant forms of familial PD (review in (Bernal-Conde et al., 2019; Burré, 2015; Burré et al., 2018; Graves et al., 2023; So and Watts, 2023)).

Despites the abundance of literature presenting SNCA as the sole driving force of PD, we should be cautious and consider the physiological role of SNCA as a solid possibility. For example, SNCA is part of the innate immune system (review in (Marogianni et al., 2020)). The aggregated forms of SNCA may also be considered as either a consequence rather than a cause of PD, being either secondary or even playing protective functions. The notion of “toxic SNCA fibrils” is used everywhere in the literature and it is indeed seen in different experimental settings, rather than a solidly established fact in humans.

Several phosphorylation sites have been described in SNCA (Fig. 22) (reviews in (Brembati et al., 2023; Kawahata et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2014; Manzanza et al., 2021; Yoo et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023d)). SNCA is phosphorylated by c-ABL(Tyr39, and Tyr125 to a lesser extent) (Brahmachari et al., 2017, 2016; Hebron et al., 2013; Mahul-Mellier et al., 2014; Zhao et al., 2020), CSNK1 (Ser87 (Waxman and Giasson, 2008), Thr64 (Matsui et al., 2023), and Ser129, following priming by Tyr125 phosphorylation (Kosten et al., 2014; Okochi et al., 2000; Torii et al., 2023)), CSNK2 (Tyr125, Ser129, Tyr136) (Ishii et al., 2007; Okochi et al., 2000; Sano et al., 2021; Takahashi et al., 2007; Waxman and Giasson, 2008), death-associated protein kinase 1 (DAPK1) (Ser129) (Shin and Chung, 2020), DYRK1A (Ser87) (Kim et al., 2006; Yong et al., 2023), FYN (Tyr125) (Ellis et al., 2001; Kosten et al., 2014; Nakamura et al., 2001), GRK1, 2, 3, 5, 6 (Ser129) (Arawaka et al., 2006; Hara et al., 2013; Pronin et al., 2000; Sakamoto et al., 2009; Sato et al., 2011; Wu et al., 2020), GSK3β (Ser129) (Credle et al., 2015), inflammation-associated protein kinase R (PKR) (or EIF2AK2, eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2-alpha kinase 2) (Ser129) (Reimer et al., 2022, 2018), LRRK2 and G2019S-LRRK2 (the most common LRRK2 mutation) (Ser129) (Qing et al., 2009), MAP kinase-interacting kinase 2A (MNK2A) (Ser129) (Zhang et al., 2015), PLKs (Ser129) (Aubele et al., 2013; Bergeron et al., 2014; Buck et al., 2015; Dahmene et al., 2017; Elfarrash et al., 2021; Inglis et al., 2009; Kofoed et al., 2017; Kosten et al., 2014; Mbefo et al., 2010; Oueslati et al., 2013; Waxman and Giasson, 2011; Weston et al., 2021), c-SRC (Tyr125) (Ellis et al., 2001), and spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK) (Tyr125, Tyr133, Tyr136) (Kosten et al., 2014; Negro et al., 2002). Additional phosphorylation sites have been recently identified: Ser42, Thr72, Thr75, Thr81 (Zhang et al., 2023d). In vitro phosphorylation of SNCA shows, besides the major Ser129 phosphorylation, modifications at Ser42 and Ser87 by PLK2 and Ser 87 by GRK4 (Chaturvedi et al., 2023).

Ser129 is the most abundant and most studied phosphorylation site in SNCA (review in (Oueslati, 2016)). 90% of the SNCA present in LBs is Ser129-phosphorylated but less than 4% of physiological SNCA is Ser129-phosphorylated (Anderson et al., 2006; Fujiwara et al., 2002). This specific phosphorylation is elevated in the brains of individuals with PD, it is associated with fibril formation and synucleinopathy in PD (Anderson et al., 2006; Fujiwara et al., 2002; Okochi et al., 2000; Samuel et al., 2016; Sato et al., 2011) and in DLB (Colom-Cadena et al., 2017; Walker et al., 2013). Ser129 phosphorylation also acts as a physiological regulator of neuronal activity at the level of synapses (Ramalingam et al., 2023b, 2023a). Ser129 phosphorylation (for example by PLK2 (Oueslati et al., 2013)) also promotes SNCA degradation in presynaptic terminals, but not in LBs (Weston et al., 2021), and may protect from formation of SNCA fibrils (Ghanem et al., 2022). Ser129 phosphorylation modulates membrane binding, interactions of SNCA with other proteins and metals, and regulates its subcellular localization (review in (Oueslati, 2016)). Ser129 phosphorylation of membrane-bound SNCA by GRKs increases DAT function and intracellular dopamine levels (Hara et al., 2013). Whether Ser129 phosphorylation promotes or inhibits SNCA toxicity in vivo remains controversial (Oueslati, 2016). Whether Ser129-phosphorylated SNCA is a normal physiological event (Espay et al., 2025b; Espay and Lees, 2025a; Parra-Rivas et al., 2023; Ramalingam et al., 2023c), a biomarker of pathology and/or also a cause of the disease remains under debate. A dramatic illustration of the 3C dilemma (coincidence, cause or consequence?), faced by researchers investigating biomarkers or so-called hallmarks associated with a disease…

Ser87 is the second major phosphorylation site in soluble and aggregated SNCA (Kim et al., 2006; Okochi et al., 2000; Zhang et al., 2023d). Ser87 is located in the NAC region, which is essential for fibril formation and aggregation. This site is one of the few sites found in human SNCA which are not conserved in mouse and rat SNCA (Paleologou et al., 2010). Phosphorylation of Ser87 is enhanced in human brains from patients with synucleinopathies and AD (Paleologou et al., 2010; Shujing Zhang et al., 2023d). Phospho-Ser87-SNCA is mostly associated with membranes (Paleologou et al., 2010). The phosphorylation mimetic Ser87Glu SNCA impinges on the formation of fibrils formation and oligomerization (Paleologou et al., 2010; Waxman and Giasson, 2008; Zhang et al., 2023d). More precisely AAV-mediated expression of soluble Ser87Glu SNCA in rat neurons has no effects on the seeding ability of PFFs, reduces the seeding ability of SNCA isolated from glial cytoplasmic inclusions (GCIs) from MSA patients) and increases the seeding ability of SNCA prepared from DLB brains (Zhang et al., 2023d). Similar experiments of AAV-mediated transfection of WT, Ser87Ala and Ser87Glu SNCA in the SN of Wistar rats reveal that WT and the non-phosphorylable (Ser87Ala) mutant SNCA, but not the Ser87Glu phosphomimetic mutant, induce aggregation, loss of dopaminergic neurons, fibre pathology and motor symptoms (Oueslati et al., 2012). Lentiviral-mediated overexpression of parkin and SNCA in rat striatum leads to a reduction of SNCA expression and phosphorylation at Ser87 and Ser129, and protect against SNCA-induced toxicity and inflammation (Khandelwal et al., 2010).

The phosphorylation of SNCA by DYRK1A was described in immortalized rat hippocampal neuronal (H19-7) cells transfected with SNCA (Kim et al., 2006). Transfection of kinase-dead DYRK1A or DYRK1A siRNA prevents SNCA phosphorylation and aggregation. The phosphorylation site was identified as Ser87, a site which is also phosphorylated by CSNK1 (Okochi et al., 2000). Aggregates of DYRK1A-phosphorylated SNCA are morphologically different and more neurotoxic than aggregates of non-phosphorylated α-synuclein (Kim et al., 2006). These encouraging results need to be confirmed with pharmacological inhibitors of DYRK1A. Since Ser87 is absent from mouse and rat SNCA, any effect of DYRK1A knockdown or pharmacological inhibition in rodent models originates from an indirect (or unknown) effect, for example through PLK2 (see below).

DYRK1A phosphorylates PLK2 on Ser358, leading to its increased stability and increased activity (Tan et al., 2023) (see PLK2 section). This effect may thus contribute indirectly to enhanced SNCA phosphorylation on Ser129. Indeed co-transfection of HEK-293 cells with SNCA, DYRK1A and PLK2 vectors leads to increased SNCA Ser129 phosphorylation compared to transfection with SNCA and PLK2 vectors or with SNCA and DYRK1A vectors (Tan et al., 2023).

In brief, SNCA phosphorylation and aggregation is a major hallmark of PD and other “synucleopathies”. DYRK1A phosphorylates SNCA on Ser87, a site present in human, but not conserved in rodents. Through phosphorylation of PLK2 on Ser358 and subsequent activation, DYRK1A indirectly contributes to the phosphorylation of SNCA on Ser129, a major marker of PD. Genetic inhibition of SNCA phosphorylation at the DYRK1A site appears to modulate the properties of SNCA according to experimental models, but the effects of pharmacological inhibitors remain unknown.

Translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 70 (TOM70)

Translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 70 (gene: TOMM70; protein: TOM70) (608 amino acids, 67.5 kDa) (reviews in (den Brave et al., 2024b, 2024a; Kreimendahl and Rassow, 2020; Pfanner et al., 2025; Sayyed and Mahalakshmi, 2022)) is one of the seven subunits of the TOM complex which allows the uptake of newly synthesized, chaperone-bound proteins into mitochondria (Opaliński et al., 2018). TOM70 is anchored in the outer membrane of mitochondria. It consists of 26 α-helices forming 11 repeating units known as tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) domains (Wu and Sha, 2006). The structure of TOM70 comprises (1) a hydrophobic NTD which provides the mitochondrial anchor (T, transmembrane domain), (2) a clamp domain (chaperone acceptor for Hsp70 and Hsp90), (3) a core domain (binding site for the precursor proteins to be imported) and (4) a CTD (Fig. 23).

TOM70: structural organization, phosphorylation site and consequences of phosphorylation. DYRK1A (and DYRK1B) phosphorylates TOM70 on Ser91. T, transmembrane domain.

Besides its protein transport function towards the inside of the mitochondria, TOM70 promotes Ca++ transfer from ER to mitochondria through its association with inositol-1,4,5-triphosphate receptor type 3 (IP3R3) (Filadi et al., 2018). TOM70 is also the direct target of the anti-apoptotic protein MCL1 (Young et al., 2003). TOM70 plays a role in antiviral signalling (review in (Kreimendahl and Rassow, 2020)): it binds the mitochondrial antiviral-signalling protein (MAVS), a major sensor of viral infections in innate immunity, leading to activation of antiviral gene transcription. The SARS-Cov2 Orf9b protein, suspected to contribute to immune evasion, binds to TOM70 (Gao et al., 2021; Lenhard et al., 2023).

Mitochondrial dysfunctions clearly contribute to PD (reviews in (Buck and Sanders, 2025; Lucchesi et al., 2025; Soman et al., 2025)) and DLB (review in (Spano et al., 2015)). The link between TOM70 and PD is supported by several key results:

(1) TOM70 and TOM20 participate to the steady-state mitochondrial import of PINK1 (Kato et al., 2013; Maruszczak et al., 2022; Raimi et al., 2024). The PINK1 precursor (65 kDa) is first imported to the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) followed by transfer to the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) and processing to a 52 kDa mature protein, and finally by proteasomal degradation. When mitochondria are damaged, PINK1 accumulates in the OMM in complex with the TOM machinery (through direct interaction with TOM20) (Eldeeb et al., 2024). PINK1 phosphorylates both polyubiquitin and the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin (Ordureau et al., 2014; Sauvé et al., 2018; Shiba-Fukushima et al., 2014a, 2012) (Fig. 14), leading to Parkin-dependent mitophagy ((Lechado-Terradas et al., 2022; Yoshii et al., 2011); review in (Agarwal and Muqit, 2022; Basak and Holzbaur, 2025b)). LoF mutations in PINK1 cause autosomal recessive early onset PD. Mutant PINK1 do not interact with the translocase, thus preventing mitochondrial quality control and mitophagy (Eldeeb et al., 2024). The interactions sites of TOM70 with PINK1 have been mapped to a multitude of contact sites in the CTD of TOM70 (Fig. 20) (Raimi et al., 2024).

(2) TOM70 binds to Parkin (Bertolin et al., 2013; Sarraf et al., 2013). PD-causing Parkin mutations weaken or disrupt interactions between Parkin and TOM70 and TOM40 (Bertolin et al., 2013).

Although several phosphosites have been identified in various mitochondrial phosphoproteomics studies (Gerbeth et al., 2013), TOM70 is known to be phosphorylated only by DYRK1A and DYRK1B (Ser91) (Marada et al., 2024; Walter et al., 2021). Phosphorylation of Ser91 (Ser94 in mouse) was wrongly attributed to CSNK2α (Latorre-Muro et al., 2021; Marada et al., 2024).

TOM70 phosphorylation by DYRK1A facilitates the interaction of TOM70 with the core TOM translocase complex, allowing the initiation of the carrier import of precursor proteins (Walter et al., 2021). DYRK1A inhibition (by INDY inhibitor) results in a strong decrease in import, in impaired mitochondrial structure, in reduced respiration rate and in reduced cell growth. This effect is mitigated by a protective transcriptional response which leads to the increased expression of TOM70, TOM20, TOM22, DYRK1A and DYRK1B (Walter et al., 2021).

In brief, by modulating the phosphorylation of TOM70 at Ser91, DYRK1A acts as a surveillance kinase for mitochondrial protein biogenesis, a key element in the transport of precursor protein into mitochondria. Inhibition of DYRK1A impacts the interaction of TOM70 with the TOM carrier machinery, an effect which is mitigated by the overexpression of DYRK1A, DYRK1B, TOM70, TOM20 and TOM22.

WASP-like actin nucleation promoting factor (WASL)

WASL (WASP-like actin nucleation promoting factor), also known as N-WASP (Neural Wiskott–Aldrich syndrome protein) (505 amino acids, 54.8 kDa), is a ubiquitously expressed member of the WASP proteins family that, along with the WASP-interacting protein WIP and actin-related protein Arp2/3, regulates cortical actin dynamics as downstream effectors of the Rac1 and Cdc42 Rho family GTPases (reviews in (Alekhina et al., 2017; Alonso-Eiras and Anton, 2025; Carlier et al., 1999; Kramer et al., 2022; Moreau et al., 2000)).

The structure of WASL (Fig. 24) comprises: an NTD WASP homology 1 (WH1) domain, a GTPase binding domain (GBD), the Cdc42/Rac interactive binding motif (CRIB), a PRD, a CTD WWCA (two WASP homology [WH2-1, WH2-2] elements, a central, and an acidic domain) also known as the VCA domain (verprolin homology, cofilin homology, and acidic region). In the basal state, WASL is autoinhibited. By binding the CRIB motif, Cdc42 relieves this inhibition, allowing binding to the Arp2/3 complex, opening the way to actin polymerization. WASL binds numerous proteins such as WIP, Cdc42, actin or IQGAP1 (IQ motif containing GTPase activating protein 1) (Le Clainche et al., 2007).

WASL: structural organization, phosphorylation site and consequences of phosphorylation. DYRK1A phosphorylates WASL on Thr199, Thr205, Thr262. G, GTPase binding domain (or CRIB, Cdc42/Rac interactive binding motif); PRD, proline rich domain; VCA, verprolin homology, cofilin homology, and acidic region (VCA) domain WIP, WASP-interacting protein; WH, WASP homology domain; WWCA, two WASP homology (W, WH2-1 and WH2-2), central (c), and acidic (a) domain.

WASL mutations are associated with a rare form of early-onset familial PD (Kumar et al., 2021). A study of SNCA interactome revealed the presence of actin and some of its regulators, including WASL (Jackson et al., 2024). WASL expression promotes SNCA and synapsin 1 condensates and WASL deficiency promotes SNCA toxicity and mitochondrial defects in C. elegans.

WASP proteins are phosphorylated by numerous kinases (review in (Kramer et al., 2022)). In particular, WASL is phosphorylated by various kinases: c-ABL (Tyr256) (Burton et al., 2005), CSNK2 (Ser480, Ser481, i.e. Ser484, Ser485 in human) (Galovic et al., 2011), DYRK1A (Thr196, Thr202, Thr259 in rat, i.e. Thr199, Thr205, Thr262 in human) (Park et al., 2012), FAK (focal adhesion kinase) (Tyr256) (Wu et al., 2004b), FYN (Tyr253 in rat, i.e. Tyr 256 in human) (Suetsugu et al., 2002), and PAK4 (p21-activated protein kinase 4) (Ser484, Ser485) (Zhao et al., 2017).

DYRK1A phosphorylates the GTPase binding domain of rat WASL (Thr196, Thr202, Thr259, corresponding to Thr199, Thr205, and Thr262 in human) (Park et al., 2012). This leads to the intramolecular interaction of the VCA domain of WASL with the GTPase-binding domain, leading to folding of the protein (Kim et al., 2000; Prehoda et al., 2000) and inhibition of Arp2/3-complex-mediated actin polymerization (Park et al., 2012). Knockdown of endogenous DYRK1A (with shRNAs) promotes actin cytoskeleton and filopodia formation in a WASL-dependent manner (Park et al., 2012). Overexpression of an WASL phospho-mimetic mutant with the T196E, T202E, and T259E triple substitutions (3TE) inhibits dendritic spine formation in primary hippocampal neurons while a phospho-deficient mutant with the T196A, T202A, and T259A triple substitutions (3TA) leads to significant increase in dendritic spine formation (Park et al., 2012).

In brief, WASL mutations are associated with early onset familial PD. DYRK1A-mediated phosphorylation of WASL results in WASL intra-molecular folding, inhibition of Cdc42 binding, prevention of actin polymerization, and inhibition of dendritic spine formation of CNS neurons. DYRK1A inhibitors are thus expected to favour dendritic spine formation.

Other proteins linking DYRK1A to Parkinson’s disease

Calpains (CLPNs)

Calpains are a family of 15 calcium-activated cysteine proteases. The most studied calpains, calpain-1 and calpain-2, require micromolar or millimolar Ca++ levels for activation, respectively, and are also regulated by other factors (phosphorylation, intracellular localization) (review in (Dókus et al., 2020; Ono et al., 2016; Spinozzi et al., 2021)). Altered Ca++ homeostasis results in uncontrolled calpain activation as shown in many models. The association of enhanced calpain activity with human diseases, CNS diseases in particular (Metwally et al., 2021), has stimulated the search for pharmacological inhibitors of these proteases (reviews in (Dókus et al., 2020; Donkor, 2020; Ono et al., 2016)). Only two calpain inhibitors, Alicapistat (ABT-957) (AD) (Lon et al., 2019) and BLD-2660 (COVID-19) (Apaydın et al., 2021), have entered clinical trials.

There is rather clear evidence showing that calpains are involved in PD, suggesting that calpain inhibition should be considered in the treatment of synucleopathies (reviews in (Gao et al., 2022; Knaryan, 2023; Meerman et al., 2023; Shams et al., 2019)):

(1) calpain inhibitors reduce neuronal death in vitro. The calpain inhibitors SJA6017 and the calpeptin analogue SNJ-1945 protect spinal motoneurons or SH-SY5Y cells from the neurotoxic effects of MPP+, rotenone or paraquat (Knaryan et al., 2014; Ramirez-Moreno et al., 2019; Samantaray et al., 2020, 2008). Rotenone triggers calpain activation, prior to caspase 3 activation and apoptosis (Chen et al., 2006).

(2) in vivo treatment of experimental PD models by neurotoxins or environmental toxins induces calpain activation, correlating with dysregulation of Ca++ homeostasis, accumulation of reactive oxygen species, disruption of the blood brain barrier, glial and T cells activation resulting in neuroinflammation, and neuronal death (Meerman et al., 2023; Shams et al., 2019). For example, rotenone administration to Lewis rats leads to activation and overexpression of both calpain-1 and -2 in the SN, correlating with neuronal death (Zaman et al., 2022). Treatment with calpeptin provides neuroprotection in vivo (Zaman et al., 2022). Similarly, treatment of rats with 6-OHDA leads to calpain activation in both caudate-putamen and SN. Adenovirus-mediated overexpression of calpastatin, an endogenous calpain inhibitor, provides neuroprotection (Grant et al., 2009). Calpeptin, calpain inhibitor MDL-28170 or calpastatin reduce dopamine neuron death in MPTP-treated mice (Crocker et al., 2003; Samantaray et al., 2015). SNCA transgenic mice treated with calpain inhibitors (Gabadur, Neurodur) show reduced SNCA deposition, reduced neurodegeneration and improved activity performance (Hassen et al., 2018).

(3) calpain activation is involved in the production of pro-inflammatory mediators in BV2 mouse microglia cells challenged with LPS, as shown with calpeptin, as well as in human microglia-SV40 glial cells, as shown with calpain-2 siRNAs (Zaman et al., 2025). Similarly, MPTP-treated mice show elevated cytokines and chemokines (Zaman et al., 2025). Calpain inhibition by calpeptin in these MPTP-treated animals reduces the levels of proinflammatory mediators and of the inflammasome NLRP3, and attenuates astrocyte activation and neuroinflammation (Zaman et al., 2025).

(4) plasma calpain activity in PD patients is increased 5.1 times compared to plasma calpain activity from healthy individuals (Muruzheva et al., 2021), with higher calpain activity observed in advanced stage PD compared to early PD stage. Calpain-2 expression is also increased in the mesencephalon of patients with PD (Mouatt-Prigent et al., 1996). Calpain-cleaved spectrin is higher in nigral neurons in postmortem human tissues from PD patients than from corresponding controls (Crocker et al., 2003). A SNCA fragment derived from calpain-1 -mediated cleavage is upregulated in serum in patients with PD (Holm Nielsen et al., 2025).

(5) miR-124, a microRNA associated with PD (see section on STAT3), targets and inhibits the translation of calpains (Jegga et al., 2011; Xu et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2019). MPTP-treated mice show a decreased expression of miR-124 in the SN (Kanagaraj et al., 2014). Similarly, miR-124 levels are decreased in MN9D dopaminergic neurons treated with MPP iodide. Consequently, the expression of calpains 1 and 2 is increased in both mice and cell models following exposure to MPTP and MPP iodide, respectively, leading to increased cleavage of the CDK5-activating p35 to p25 (Kanagaraj et al., 2014).

(6) More than 100 substrates of calpains have been described, including several proteins known to be involved in PD (Fig. 25), such as:

Effects of calpains on PD-relevant proteins. A large number of PD-relevant proteins are cleaved by calpains resulting in modifications of their biological properties.

- Amphiphysin I (see above) is involved in clathrin-mediated endocytosis of synaptic vesicles (see above). Its truncation by calpains prevents binding to dynamin I and leads to inhibition of vesicle endocytosis during neural hyperexcitation (Wu et al., 2007).

- Calcineurin is a Ca++/calmodulin-dependent phosphatase constituted of a 60 kDa catalytic subunit and a 19 kDa regulatory subunit. Calcineurin is highly expressed in neurons and appears to be involved in PD (review in (Bohush et al., 2021)). SNCA interacts with calcineurin (Shi et al., 2018). It activates the calcineurin/NFAT pathway in vitro and in mice (Luo et al., 2014; Sant’Anna et al., 2023). Genetic or pharmacological (FK506/FKBP12) inhibition of calmodulin/calcineurin protects from SNCA (Caraveo et al., 2017, 2014) and dopamine or MPTP -induced toxicities (Mondal et al., 2023). In vitro truncation by calpain converts calcineurin to an active, calmodulin-independent form (Lakshmikuttyamma et al., 2004; Tallant et al., 1988). Calpain 1 -mediated calcineurin truncation and activation is observed in AD brains (Liu et al., 2005) and during excitotoxicity (Wu et al., 2004a).

- GSK3β (see above) is activated by calpain-1 -mediated cleavage (Goñi-Oliver et al., 2009, 2007), both in vitro (Jin et al., 2017, 2015b) and in vivo following intraperitoneal or intra-amygdala injections of kainic acid (KA) in rodents (Jin et al., 2015b; Jurado-Arjona et al., 2015). GSK3 truncation by calpain is inhibited by phosphorylation of Ser9 and Thr390 (Ser389 in mouse) (Ma et al., 2012b). GSK3 is negatively regulated by PINK1 (Akundi et al., 2012; Furlong et al., 2019). GSK3 phosphorylates septin-4 on two sites (Koch et al., 2015).

- LIMK1 (LIM domain kinase 1): LIM kinases (1 and 2) are key regulators of the actin cytoskeleton (phosphorylation of the actin-depolymerizing factor, cofilin) and of gene expression (interaction with the cAMP-response element-binding protein (CREB) and phosphorylation and inhibition of NURR1 (Sacchetti et al., 2006)).They are involved in spine regulation, synaptic plasticity, memory and several brain diseases, including AD and PD (reviews in (Ben Zablah et al., 2021; Namme et al., 2021; Villalonga et al., 2023)). Parkin interacts with and ubiquitinates LIMK1, thereby reducing cofilin phosphorylation and assembly of actin filaments, leading to spines and synaptic function through a LIMK1-dependent mechanism (Lim et al., 2007). Truncation of LIMK1 by calpain-2 leads to LIMK1 activation followed by cofilin phosphorylation (Rodríguez-Fernández et al., 2021).

- MAP1B (see above) is cleaved by calpain-10, a susceptibility factor for type 2 diabetes, in vitro and in vivo, leading to the generation of heavy chain (HC) and light chain (LC) forms of MAP1B (Hatta et al., 2018). The full-length MAP1B dominantly binds to actin but not to microtubules. HC and LC form heterodimers, a requirement for microtubule binding. Calpain-10 thus regulates actin dynamics through MAP1B truncation. The light chain of MAP1B binds LRRK2 (Chan et al., 2014b).

- MAPT (Tau) (see above) is cleaved by calpain, leading to a 17 kDa neurotoxic Tau fragment observed in various tauopathies, including FTDP-17 (Ferreira and Bigio, 2011). Truncation of Tau favours its aggregation (Gu et al., 2020) and calpain activation precedes both Tau phosphorylation and synaptic dysfunction in AD (Kurbatskaya et al., 2016).

- MEF2D (see above), a protective transcription factor, is degraded by calpain, thereby favouring excitotoxicity (Wei et al., 2012).

- p35 (see above), the regulator of CDK5, is cleaved by calpain to a LMW form, p25. CDK5/p25 is active and much more stable than CDK5/p35 (Lee et al., 2000; Pao and Tsai, 2021). Furthermore p25, but not p35, binds to and activates GSK3β (Chow et al., 2014). Aberrant CDK5 activation by p25 has been observed in both AD and PD (Smith et al., 2006). Calpain-dependent activation of CDK5 may lead to phosphorylation and reduced activity of peroxiredoxin 2 (PRX2), a thiol-dependent peroxidase, in the MPTP toxicity model and in PD (Qu et al., 2007). Calpain-mediated activation of CDK5/p35 may play a role in dopaminergic neuron death through phosphorylation and inactivation of the transcription factor MEF2D (Smith et al., 2006).

- Parkin (see above): treatment of rat primary neuronal cultures with oligomycin, rotenone or the Ca++ ionophore A23187, results in the truncation of parkin through calpain activity, presumably at Gln71 and Ala134 (Wang et al., 2019a). Phosphorylation of parkin at Ser65 by PINK1 reduces cleavage by calpain (Wang et al., 2019a).

- α-synuclein (see above) truncation by calpains (in addition to neurosin (kallikrein-6), cathepsin D, matrix metalloproteinases) has been widely investigated (reviews in (Bluhm et al., 2021; Nuber and Selkoe, 2016; Suthar and Lee, 2023)). SNCA fragments are observed in both PD and DLB brains (Dufty et al., 2007; Li et al., 2005a). SNCA truncation by calpains in vitro leads to altered aggregation (Dufty et al., 2007; Mishizen-Eberz et al., 2005, 2003; Wang et al., 2016). Calpain inhibitors reduce SNCA deposition in animal models of PD and DLB (Hassen et al., 2018). The Parkinson’s disease inducer pesticide paraquat increases calpain activity and shifts SNCA tetramers to monomers which are truncated by calpains, favouring the formation of oligomers, associated with neurotoxicity and disease (Nuber and Selkoe, 2023). Calpastatin reduces SNCA processing, aggregation, and synaptic impairment in A30P-SNCA transgenic mice (Diepenbroek et al., 2014). Calpain inhibition prevents manganese-induced SNCA oligomerization in brain slices (Xu et al., 2015) and in rats (Ivleva et al., 2022). Extracellular SNCA induces calpain-dependent overactivation of CDK5 in vitro (Czapski et al., 2013). Calpains are key regulators of SNCA C-terminal cleavage, leading to its packaging as fibrils in LB-like inclusions (Mahul-Mellier et al., 2025).

- TP53 (see above) regulates cell death in response to stress signals. In addition to degradation by the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, p53 is cleaved by calpain (Gonen et al., 1997; Kubbutat and Vousden, 1997; Pariat et al., 1997; Qin et al., 2010). Calpain-mediated truncation of p53 is involved in semaphorin 3A -induced axonal growth regulation (Qin et al., 2010).

- Tyrosine hydroxylase (TH), the rate-limiting enzyme involved in dopamine synthesis, is cleaved by calpain, leading to its activation (Kiuchi et al., 1991).

DYRK1A has been described as a substrate of calpain-1 in vitro and in vivo (KA-induced excitotoxicity and 5xFAD mice) (Jin et al., 2015a; Zhang et al., 2023a). Truncated, LMW-DYRK1A forms (around 50 kDa) have been observed in AD brain samples in correlation with calpain activation (Jin et al., 2015a; Souchet et al., 2019). Truncation of full-length DYRK1A (FL-DYRK1A) to LMW-DYRK1A (N-terminal fragment, containing the catalytic domain) has several consequences: delocalization from nuclear speckles, modification in substrate affinity, enhanced catalytic activity (increased Vmax) (Hogrel et al., unpublished), hyperphosphorylation and aggregation of Tau, promotion of Tau exon 10 exclusion, increased phosphorylation of alternative splicing factor 1 (ASF1) which mediates the expression of Bcl-xS, the pro-apoptotic form of Bcl-x, and increased apoptosis (Jin et al., 2015a; Zhang et al., 2023a). The C-terminal fragment of DYRK1A may have some transcription factor activity (Ouyang et al., 2023). Inhibition of DYRK1A by Leucettine L41 (Fig. 31) protects DYRK1A from truncation (Souchet et al., 2019), suggesting that DYRK1A inhibitors may have a dual action: direct inhibition of the catalytic activity and inhibition of DYRK1A over-activation by calpain-induced truncation. It is thus quite possible that, in parallel to other substrates, Ca++-induced calpain activation observed in PD may lead to DYRK1A truncation and activation.

We have observed that FAM53C, a natural DYRK1A inhibitory protein, is very sensitive to calpain-mediated degradation (Hogrel et al., unpublished). By reducing FAM53C level, calpain activation in PD may thus indirectly increase DYRK1A catalytic activity.

In brief, there is clear evidence for activation of calpains in PD and the implication of several of its cleaved substrates (such as SNCA, parkin, GSK3β, calcineurin, etc.) in PD. Calpain-mediated truncation of DYRK1A to LMW-DYRK1A leads to a 2-fold more active kinase. Whether DYRK1A truncation and subsequent activation occurs in PD remains to be investigated.

Down syndrome cell adhesion molecule (DSCAM)

The Down syndrome cell adhesion molecule (DSCAM) (2,012 amino acids; 222.3 kDa), and the closely related DSCAML1 (2,053 amino acids; 224.5 kDa), are transmembrane proteins belonging to the immunoglobulin superfamily of cell adhesion molecules. They are highly expressed in developing neural tissue. DSCAM exits in human as three isoforms. In Drosophila, alternative splicing leads to 38,016 DSCAM isoforms (Neves et al., 2004). DSCAM and DSCAML1 can homo-dimerize. DSCAM and DSCAML1 are truncated by γ-secretase, thereby generating intracellular domains (ICDs) which are imported to the nucleus via a conserved NLS (Sachse et al., 2019). Expression of DSCAM or DSCAML1 ICDs alter the expression of genes involved in neuronal differentiation, apoptosis, and synapse formation and function. Expression of ICDs in primary cortical neurons leads to impaired neurite growth. Increased expression of DSCAM or the DSCAM ICD leads to decreased synapse numbers in primary hippocampal neurons (Sachse et al., 2019).

DSCAM plays numerous functions in the CNS (reviews in (Lemieux et al., 2021; Xiong et al., 2025)), including neuronal development, regulation of dendrite arborization and spine formation, axonal guidance, neuronal self-avoidance (essential for the development of neural circuits) (Matthews et al., 2007), chemoattraction and repulsion, modulation of GABAergic synaptic development (Liu et al., 2023a), congenital heart disease (with COL6A2), etc. The DSCAM gene is located in the DSCR of Hsa21. Consequently DSCAM is overexpressed in DS and this overexpression contributes to intellectual disability associated with DS (review in (Hergenreder et al., 2024)). DSCAM abnormal expression is linked to autism spectrum disorder (Lim et al., 2021; Neff et al., 2024) and possibly to AD (Xiong et al., 2025) .

The possible involvement of DSCAM in PD is supported by the following data.

(1) DSCAM appears as one the 134 PD risk factors reported in the latest GWAS study (variant rs4816690; position 40,577,012) (GP2; http://gp2.org/) (Global Parkinson’s Genetics Program (GP2) and Leonard, 2025). GWAS also identified DSCAM as a gene influencing age-of-onset of AD (Li et al., 2023b).

(2) Although this has not been studied in a PD context, DSCAM is involved in the development of neural control of movement and locomotion (review in (Lemieux et al., 2021)).

The use of the MAPPIT technique (mammalian protein-protein interaction trap) with DSCAM and DSCAML1 ICDs, allowed to identify DYRK1A and DYRK1B as binding partners (Sachse et al., 2019). The CTD, but not the N-terminal or catalytic domains, of DYR1A/1B mediates the interaction with DSCAM/DSCAML1.

A large-scale yeast two-hybrid screen using 72 proteins encoded by the human chromosome 21 as baits and a human adult brain cDNA library confirmed a potent interaction between the intracellular domains of DSCAM or DSCAML1 and DYRK1A (Viard et al., 2022). The DSCAM domain interacting with DYRK1A was identified as 94 amino acids stretch located between positions 1,823 and 1,907. This domain was confirmed in an interaction screen between Mnb, the Drosophila DYRK1A homolog, and DSCAM4 (Viard et al., 2022). The DSCAM-DYRK1A interaction was also demonstrated by co-immunoprecipitation using a synaptosome fraction from adult mouse cortex.

Whether DYRK1A phosphorylates DSCAM/DSCAML1 remains unknown, despite the existence of a putative DYRK1A substrate motif in the ICDs (Sachse et al., 2019).

In brief, DSCAM has been identified as a risk factor for PD in GWAS. The intracellular domains of DSCAM/ DSCAML1 (resulting from the action of γ-secretase) is imported in the nucleus where they drive signals inhibiting synapse formation. The intracellular domains of DSCAM/DSCAML1 interact with the C-terminal domains of DYRK1A and DYRK1B.

Restrictive element-1 silencing transcription factor (REST) / neuron-restrictive silencing factor (NRSF)

Restrictive element-1 silencing transcription factor (REST)/neuron-restrictive silencing factor (NRSF) (full length: 1097 amino acids, 121.9 kDa) (reviews in (Ballas et al., 2005; Bruce et al., 2004; Hwang and Zukin, 2018; Jin et al., 2023a; Schoenherr and Anderson, 1995)) comprises an N-terminal repressor domain, a DNA-binding domain (8 zinc fingers), and a C-terminal repressor domain. REST/NRSF alternative splicing may lead to more than 45 mRNAs. REST/NRSF binds to the neuron-restrictive silencer element (NRSE or RE1) (found in >3,000 genes), recruits multiple co-repressors (including coREST) and acts as a transcriptional repressor for hundreds of genes in non-neuronal tissues and in neural stem cells. It acts as a negative regulator of neurogenesis and a regulator of glutamate uptake in astrocytes and inflammation (Nassar et al., 2024). REST/NRSF silencing leads to neuronal differentiation. REST/NRSF is involved in neuronal survival and synaptic plasticity. REST/NRSF is elevated during aging, associated with cognitive preservation and longevity, and dysregulated in neurodegenerative disorders (Zullo et al., 2019). REST/NRSF is reduced in mild cognitive impairment and almost absent in patients with AD (Lu et al., 2014a). REST/NRSF is induced in aging brain and mediates a neurodegeneration checkpoint response with multiple targets that may repress cell death associated genes and protect against the onset of AD (Aron et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2014a).

The involvement of REST/NRSF in PD stems from eight sets of data from cells to human:

(1) The promoter of the TH gene contains an NSRE (Kim et al., 2008a) and REST/NRSF upregulates TH expression (Pajarillo et al., 2020). Similarly, the promoter of the gene encoding ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydroxylase L1 (UCHL1), which is potentially implicated in PD (Buneeva and Medvedev, 2024), is also a REST/NRSF target (Barrachina et al., 2007).

(2) MPP+ induces mRNA and protein expression of REST/NRSF and REST4 (a neuron-specific truncated form) in SH-SY5Y cells (Yu et al., 2009), and leads to their intracellular redistribution resulting in reduced expression of REST/NRSF target genes. REST/NRSF KO in SH-SY5Y cells results in mitochondrial dysfunction and impairs mitophagy, while REST/NRSF overexpression attenuates mitochondrial toxicity and morphology disruption. REST/NRSF is thus protective, its absence making dopaminergic neurons more vulnerable to SNCA (Ryan et al., 2021).

(3) REST/NRSF cKO (conditional KO) mice show higher sensitivity to MPTP in terms of locomotor impairments, TH level reduction, loss of dopaminergic neurons in the SN and inflammation (Yu et al., 2013). Trichostatin reverses the MPTP-induced dopaminergic neurotoxicity and subsequent motor ability in WT mice, but not in neuronal REST/NRSF cKO mice (Suo et al., 2015). Brain-specific REST/NRSF deficiency aggravates dopaminergic neurons degeneration, impairs neurogenesis, and increases motor defects in MPTP-treated mice (Huang et al., 2019). Astrocyte-specific REST/NRSF cKO mice show activation of astrocytes and striatal microglial cells, and increased dopaminergic cell degeneration (loss of TH-positive cells) following exposure to MPTP (Li et al., 2020a).

(4) A SNCA-overexpressing BAC-transgenic mouse model of PD has been used to investigate the role of REST/NRSF (Janezic et al., 2013; Ryan et al., 2021). Reduced REST/NRSF expression in SN dopaminergic neurons (but not in adjacent GABAergic cells) is associated with mitochondrial dysfunctions (Ryan et al., 2021).

(5) REST/NRSF protects dopaminergic neurons from manganese-induced excitotoxicity (manganism is closely related to PD) through the induced expression of excitatory amino acid transporter 2 (EAAT2) in astrocytes as shown in astrocyte/neuron co-cultures (Pajarillo et al., 2021). REST/NRSF upregulates TH expression in dopaminergic neurons and directly protects them from manganese-induced oxidative stress and inflammation as shown in cell cultures (Pajarillo et al., 2020) and in REST/NRSF cKO mice (Pajarillo et al., 2024). Manganese decreases REST/NRSF mRNA and protein levels in the SN, and induces oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysregulation, apoptosis, dopaminergic neuron degeneration, and alters locomotor activity and coordination. These effects are further decreased in REST/NRSF-cKO mice. REST/NRSF restoration attenuates manganese-induced neurotoxicity (Pajarillo et al., 2024). REST/NRSF regulates many neuroprotective genes counteracting manganese-induced detrimental effects.

(6) Gene expression profiling in the striatum and SN from MPTP-treated Cynomolgus monkeys point to the down-regulation of REST/NRSF target genes in the striatum (Ohnuki et al., 2010).

(7) REST/NRSF is present in LBs but reduced in aged dopaminergic neurons nuclei in brains of PD and DLB patients, in contrast to REST/NRSF accumulation in aged dopaminergic neurons nuclei from healthy controls (Kawamura et al., 2019). REST/NRSF levels are reduced in dopaminergic neurons derived from pluripotent cells from a patient carrying an α-synuclein triplication mutation (Ryan et al., 2021).

(8) Missense, nonsense and splice mutations in the DJ-1 (PARK7) gene are associated with reduced DJ-1 protein expression, mitochondrial dysfunctions and autosomal recessive early onset familial PD (Lv et al., 2025). The DJ-1 promoter contains a REST/NRSF binding sequence. An 18 bp ins/del/dup polymorphism in the DJ-1 promoter has been associated with early PD. 18 bp DJ-1 promoter variants (deletion or duplication) prevent REST/NRSF binding and down-regulate DJ-1 expression (Pal et al., 2023).

A reduced (∼ -50%) REST/NRSF expression is observed in undifferentiated embryonic stem cells to adult trisomic mice brains (3 DS models). Silencing of the third copy of DYRK1A rescues NRSF/REST levels. DYRK1A overexpression and increased kinase activity negatively controls REST/NSRF mRNA levels and expression. REST/NRSF expression is very sensitive to DYRK1A level and activity, both DYRK1A over- and under-expression resulting in REST/NRSF down-regulation and perturbation of the pluripotency network and neuronal number and morphology (Fig. 26 A). (Canzonetta et al., 2008). DYRK1A interacts with the REST/NRSF SWI (switch mutants)/SNF (sucrose non-fermenting) chromatin remodelling complex (Fig. 26 B). DYRK1A overexpression induces a deregulation of multiple REST/NRSF target genes. This deregulation leads to impairment of dendritic growth and complexity, a characteristic neuronal phenotypic change in DS (Lepagnol-Bestel et al., 2009).

REST/NRSF – DYRK1A interactions. A. DYRK1A imbalance (over- or underexpression) leads to a reduction of REST/NSRF expression. B. DYRK1A interacts with REST/NRSF – SWI/SNF chromatin remodelling complex and deregulates the expression of multiple NRSE-dependent genes. C. REST activates the expression of DYRK1A by binding to an NSRE domain in the DYRK1A gene promoter. In contrast DYRK1A dosage imbalance negatively affects REST/NRSF expression and stability.

In addition REST/NRSF activates DYRK1A transcription via a NRSE located in the DYRK1A gene promoter Lu et al., 2011. DYRK1A imbalance reduces REST/NRSF expression and transcriptional activity, leading to a negative DYRK1A expression feedback loop. Reduced DYRK1A triggers REST/NRSF ubiquitination and ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, and, consequently, reduced REST/NSRSF transcriptional activity (Fig. 26 C).

REST is phosphorylated by CSNK1 at Ser1013, Ser1024, Ser1027, and Ser1030 (Kaneko et al., 2014) and by ERK1/2 at Ser861 and Ser864 (Nesti et al., 2014), leading to β-TrCP-mediated ubiquitination and degradation. Whether DYRK1A phosphorylates REST/NRSF is unknown.

In brief, REST/NRSF plays a neuroprotective role in dopaminergic neurons by regulating the expression of a multitude of genes through a NRSE domain located on their promoter. DYRK1A imbalance reduces REST/NRSF expression and transcriptional activity, leading to a negative DYRK1A expression feedback loop. Normalization of DYRK1A expression/activity is expected to enhanced expression of REST/NRSF and to contribute to neuroprotection. The potential impact of pharmacological inhibition of DYRK1A on REST/NRSF expression and activity is unknown.

14-3-3

The family of 14-3-3 proteins comprises 7 members: α/β, γ, ε, δ/ζ, η, θ, and σ (respective gene names: YWHAB, YWHAG, YWHAE, YWHAZ, YWHAH, YWHAQ, and SFN) (245-255 amino acids; 27.7-29.2 kDa) (reviews in (Berg et al., 2003a; Low et al., 2024; Obsilova and Obsil, 2022; Pair and Yacoubian, 2021; Panda et al., 2024; Segal et al., 2023)) (Fig. 27). 14-3-3 are phosphorylated at Ser58/59, Ser184/186, Ser/Thr232 residues (14-3-3α and δ represent phosphorylated 14-3-3 β and ζ, respectively). 14-3-3 proteins were identified over 50 years ago as very abundant proteins in the brain (∼ 1% of total soluble proteins) (Berg et al., 2003a; Moore, 1972). Each 14-3-3 isoform contains 9 anti-parallel α-helices and forms homo- and heterodimers with other 14-3-3 isoforms, resulting in ‘VV’-shaped amphipathic pockets acting as binding sites for other proteins. These highly conserved and highly abundant proteins indeed interact with over 1,200 different proteins, usually via phospho-Ser/Thr binding modules in the “client” protein (Segal et al., 2023). 14-3-3 proteins regulate the folding, activity, protein interaction, localization, stability of their interacting partners. Consequently, 14-3-3 proteins impinge on protein dephosphorylation, signal transduction, cell cycle, cell death, transcription, protein trafficking, cytoskeletal organization, stress response, etc. Dysregulation of 14-3-3 expression is associated with multiple disorders, including cancers, neurodegenerative diseases (AD, PD, Creutzfeldt-Jacob, polyglutamine repeat diseases), glaucoma, cystic fibrosis, viral infections, and others.

Interactions of 14-3-3 with PD actors and DYRK1A. PD-relevant 14-3-3 interactors are shown with the phospho-site involved in the interaction with 14-3-3. Left, specific 14-3-3 isoforms. Right, global or multiple 14-3-3 isoforms binding.

The involvement of 14-3-3 in PD and DLB stems from a large set of data from cells to humans (review in (Giusto et al., 2021)):

(1) 14-3-3 proteins bind to major proteins involved in PD (Fig. 27):

- 14-3-3 proteins interact with tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) through the pSer19 site, favoring TH dephosphorylation on Ser31 and Ser40 and thereby its activation (Ghorbani et al., 2020, 2016; Kleppe et al., 2014).

- 14-3-3η, but not other isoforms, binds parkin and inhibits its ubiquitin ligase activity (Sato et al., 2006). Whether parkin needs to be phosphorylated to interact with 14-3-3η is unknown. 14-3-3η does not bind parkin with mutations leading to autosomal recessive juvenile parkinsonism (R42P, K161N, T240R). SNCA prevents 14-3-3η from binding to and inhibiting parkin. Parkin is associated with 14-3-3η in LBs.

- 14-3-3 proteins bind to LRRK2 at several phosphorylation sites including Ser860, Ser910, Ser935, Ser955, Ser973, Ser1444, and Thr2524, thereby maintaining LRRK2 in an inactive form in the cytoplasm (Dzamko et al., 2010; Lavalley et al., 2016; Manschwetus et al., 2020; Nichols et al., 2010; Sluchanko, 2020; Stevers et al., 2017). Inhibition of mutant (G2019S), overactive LRRK2 by inhibitors results in dephosphorylation of Ser910, Ser935, loss of 14-3-3 binding, increased ubiquitination and degradation of LRRK2 (Dzamko et al., 2010). Some of the most pathogenic mutations of LRRK2 (R1441C, R1441G, R1441H, Y1699C, I2020T) are associated with reduced phosphorylation at Ser910 and Ser935, leading to disrupted interaction with 14-3-3 proteins, and accumulation of LRRK2 in cytoplasmic inclusion bodies, a hallmark of PD (Dzamko et al., 2010; Nichols et al., 2010; Zhao et al., 2015). Similarly, LRRK2 R1441C/G/H mutations prevent phosphorylation on Ser1444 by PKA and interaction with 14-3-3 (Muda et al., 2014). Phosphorylation of 14-3-3γ on Ser59 by p21-activated protein kinase 6 (PAK6) prevents the binding of 14-3-3 to LRRK2 (Civiero et al., 2017). 14-3-3 proteins thus appear as major negative regulators of LRRK2 activation.

- 14-3-3 is found associated with SNCA in LBs of patients with PD and DLB (Berg et al., 2003b; Chakraborty and Patra, 2024; Kawamoto et al., 2002; Ubl et al., 2002; Wakabayashi et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2025a). Reduced levels of soluble 14-3-3s in temporal cortices of PD and DLB patients are associated with increased levels of insoluble, phosphorylated 14-3-3 (McFerrin et al., 2017). Overexpression of SNCA in human primary neurons leads to the formation of a soluble SNCA/14-3-3 complex and dopaminergic toxicity (Xu et al., 2002). SNCA toxicity is reduced by overexpression of 14-3-3η and θ (Plotegher et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2018). SNCA A30P and A53T mutants, responsible for familial PD, are unable to bind 14-3-3η nor to activate parkin (Sato et al., 2006). 14-3-3 η and θ appear as protective agents against protein aggregation (Heesink et al., 2024; Plotegher et al., 2014; Slone et al., 2015, 2011; Wang et al., 2018). In contrast, Ser232-phosphorylated 14-3-3θ exacerbates SNCA aggregation (Wang et al., 2025a). Ser232-phosphorylated 14-3-3 is found in human PD and DLB cortical brain lysates insoluble fractions (McFerrin et al., 2017; Slone et al., 2015), and in a model of rotenone-treated neuroblastoma cells (Slone et al., 2015). Phosphorylation of 14-3-3θ disrupts its interaction with SNCA and blocks its protective effects (Wang et al., 2025a).

- 14-3-3β binds the protein deglycase DJ-1 (Parkinsonism-associated deglycase), a cytoprotective protein with pleiotropic functions (Bonifati et al., 2003). LoF mutations in DJ-1 are linked to autosomal recessive PD (Skou et al., 2024). Interaction between 14-3-3β and DJ-1 leads to DJ-1 sequestration in the cytosol (HEK-293 cells) or mitochondria (neurons).

- 14-3-3ζ binds to GSK3β favouring binding to and phosphorylation of Tau (Agarwal-Mawal et al., 2003). This interaction requires the phosphorylation of GSK3β at Ser9 (Gao et al., 2014b; Yuan et al., 2004).

- 14-3-3 binds to FOXO3a, a transcription factor associated with PD, inducing its nuclear export, and thus reducing its transcriptional activity (Liu et al., 2024; Mathivanan et al., 2022; Tzivion et al., 2011). This binding requires phosphorylation of FOXO3a on Thr32, Ser 253 and Ser315 (Mathivanan et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2010). Similar results have been observed with FOXO1 (Thr24, Ser256, Ser319).

- 14-3-3 binds to the ubiquitin E3 ligase NEDD4-2 (neural precursor cell expressed developmentally downregulated protein 4) through the pSer342 and pSer448 sites, thereby protecting it from dephosphorylation and preventing its interaction with and inhibition of epithelial Na+ channels (ENaC) (Joshi et al., 2022; Nagaki et al., 2006; Pohl et al., 2021). NEDD4-2 mediates the ubiquitination of glutamate transporters in MPP+-treated astrocytes and in MPTP-treated mice (Zhang et al., 2017). NEDD4-2 knock-down stabilizes the transporters, reduces astrogliosis and reactive microgliosis and attenuates movement disorders in PD mice (Zhang et al., 2017). NEDD4 promotes SNCA ubiquitination and degradation (Tofaris et al., 2011) (review in (Conway et al., 2022)).

- different human fetal and AD brain 14-3-3 isoforms bind MAPT (Tau) (review in (Betters et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2019)). Binding of 14-3-3ζ promotes dissociation of Tau phosphorylated on Ser214/Ser324 (by PKA/PKG (Sadik et al., 2009)) from microtubules and reduces aggregation and condensation (Hochmair et al., 2025).

(2) 14-3-3 overexpression in neurotoxin, MPTP and LRRK2 animal models of PD provides neuroprotection or recovery, while 14-3-3 inhibition promotes or exacerbates toxicity (Ding et al., 2015; Lavalley et al., 2016; Li et al., 2011; Slone et al., 2011; Yacoubian et al., 2010). 14-3-3γ haploinsufficiency in mice (resulting in a ∼50% reduction of 14-3-3γ level in the striatum and SN) leads to reduced phosphorylated TH, decreased dopamine levels and reduced DAT expression, reduced LRRK2 phosphorylation at Ser910, increased astrogliosis and PD-related behavioural deficits including impaired motor coordination (Cho et al., 2023).

(3) Enhanced phosphorylation of 14-3-3θ at Ser232 (by CSNK1?) is observed in several PD models (rotenone, MPP+, α-synuclein overexpression) and in human PD and DLB brains, in association with reduced solubility, reduced neuroprotective effect of 14-3-3 and increased pathological severity (McFerrin et al., 2017; Slone et al., 2015). This phosphorylation exacerbates SNCA aggregation and toxicity (Wang et al., 2025a). 14-3-3 phosphorylation at Ser232 thus appears to contribute to the neurodegenerative process of PD. The phosphomimetic Ser232Asp 14-3-3θ accelerates SNCA aggregation (Wang et al., 2025b) and does not protect in neurotoxin and mutant LRRK2 models (Pattanayak et al., 2024). Ser232 phosphorylation of 14-3-3θ reduces binding of proteins key to vesicle trafficking and reduces axonal transport of acidic vesicles (Pair et al., 2025).

(4) 14-3-3θ overexpression decreases SNCA oligomerization, internalization and seeding (Wang et al., 2025a). The non-phosphorylable S232A 14-3-3θ protects while the phosphomimetic S232D 14-3-3θ fails to protect against SNCA (Wang et al., 2025a). 14-3-3θ overexpression rescues social dominance deficits induced in mice by preformed SNCA fibril model and delays SNCA aggregation (Underwood et al., 2021). The 14-3-3 inhibitor difopein accelerates SNCA aggregation, neuronal loss and behavioural deficits (Underwood et al., 2021).

DYRK1A and 14-3-3η were identified to interact through a yeast two hybrid screen (Kim et al., 2004), confirming the interaction of their respective yeast orthologues, Yak1p and Bmh1/2p (Moriya et al., 2001). The DYRK1A/14-3-3ε complex was demonstrated by pull-down experiments. 14-3-3 increases DYRK1A catalytic activity (Kim et al., 2004).

Another yeast two hybrid screen, validated by GST pull-down assays, confirmed the interaction between DYRK1A and 14-3-3β (Alvarez et al., 2007). 14-3-3β binding requires the intramolecular autophosphorylation of DYRK1A on Ser520, a site located near the PEST region in the CTD of the kinase. Another unidentified site in the N-terminal domain is also required for 14-3-3 binding (Alvarez et al., 2007; Kim et al., 2004). 14-3-3β binding increases the catalytic activity of DYRK1A by a 2-fold factor. Pharmacological inhibition of DYRK1A is thus expected to reduce DYRK1A catalytic activity both directly (competition with ATP) and indirectly (reduction of 14-3-3 binding).

Flagged 14-3-3 isoforms have been expressed individually in HEK-293 cell lines and an affinity purification method coupled to mass spectrometry was used to identify interactors: DYRK1A was confirmed as a partner for 14-3-3 isoforms β, γ, ε, ζ, and η (Segal et al., 2023). Two other proteomics studies have shown that DYRK1A binds to 14-3-3γ (Jin et al., 2004) and σ (Benzinger et al., 2005). Whether DYRK1A/14-3-3 binding favours the kinase stability and/or the interaction with key PD actors is unknown. FAM53C binds six out of the seven 14-3-3 isoforms (Segal et al., 2023).

In brief, 14-3-3 proteins binds to different key actors of PD pathogenesis (Parkin, LRRK2, SNCA, Dj-1, etc.), globally leading to their inhibition and to neuroprotection. Phosphorylation of 14-3-3θ on Ser232 is associated with reduced axonal transport and PD or DLB. DYRK1A autophosphorylation on Ser520 promotes binding of 14-3-3, leading to increased kinase activity. Pharmacological inhibition of DYRK1A is expected to reduce DYRK1A catalytic activity directly (competition with ATP) and indirectly (reduction of 14-3-3 binding).

Miscellaneous

DYRK1A and particulate matter

There is a clear correlation between exposure to air pollution particulate matter (PM2.5, PM10) and PD, DLB, and other neurodegenerative diseases (reviews in (Chauhan et al., 2025; Cole-Hunter et al., 2023; Costa et al., 2020; Cristaldi et al., 2022; Flood-Garibay et al., 2023; Gialluisi et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2020b; Zhang et al., 2025c)). Oxidative stress, inflammation, DNA damage, ER stress, autophagy, mitophagy, apoptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis and epigenetic modifications are the main proposed mechanisms underlying the toxicity of PM (review in (Li et al., 2022)). The molecular transduction mechanisms of PM in the CNS are poorly known, neuroinflammation and glucocorticoid receptor (GR) signalling are common pathways for neurotoxicity induced by PM2.5 and ultrafine particles (review in (Qin et al., 2024a)).

Long-term exposure of rats to PM2.5 increases rotenone-induced dopaminergic neuron loss and leads to hypermethylation of CpG sites, leading to down-regulation of PINK1 and DJ-1 gene expression (Du et al., 2025). These effects of PM2.5 on PINK1 and DJ-1 hypermethylation and subsequent downregulation of gene expression are also seen in SH-SY5Y cell cultures (Du et al., 2025). These results confirm similar results obtained with rotenone- and PM2.5- treated mice and PC12 cells (Wang et al., 2021cY et al., 2021).

There is very limited, but potentially interesting data, suggesting a link between PM and DYRK1A. A study of the association between indoor air pollution (PM10) and neurodevelopmental delay revealed 29 differentially methylated CpG sites and 4 gene regions, including DYRK1A, as potential mediators (Feil et al., 2023). Exposure of mice to diesel exhaust PM for 10 weeks led to increased levels of pS199-Tau, DYRK1A, Aβ42 but not SNCA in their cerebral cortex (Lucio COSTA, personal communication & unpublished data) (Costa et al., 2020).

DYRK1A and inorganic polyphosphates

Inorganic polyphosphates (polyP) are linear polyanionic polymers from 3 to thousands of orthophosphate units (Pi) linked by high-energy phosphoanhydride bonds. PolyP are found in abundance from bacteria to human cells and have a likely role in the origin and survival of species. They are found both in the extracellular space and intracellularly (plasma membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, mitochondria, and endo-lysosomes in neurons). Although PolyP have been mostly studied in eukaryotes, they play a role, in mammals, in signalling pathways, transcriptional activity, actomyosin activity, inflammation, platelets and blood coagulation, bone formation, and pathologies ranging from cancer to neurodegeneration (reviews in (Bondy-Chorney et al., 2020; Kus et al., 2022; McCarthy et al., 2025; Neville et al., 2024a; Rai and Jakob, 2025; Rao et al., 2009; Schoeppe et al., 2024)). PolyP are present in the brain and associated with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), AD and PD (reviews in (Borden et al., 2021; Garcés et al., 2024; Montilla et al., 2025)). PolyP are released from astrocytes in a Ca++-dependent manner and mediate signalling between astrocytes through the activation of P2Y1 purinergic receptors (Angelova et al., 2018; Holmström et al., 2013).

PolyP accelerate the formation of SNCA fibrils in vitro. Extracellular polyP prevent the interaction of SNCA fibrils with cell membranes and their uptake into neuronal cells. Consequently they protect neuronal cells from SNCA toxicity (Cremers et al., 2016; Lempart et al., 2019). PolyP interact with α-synuclein via a lysine-rich pocket (Lys43, Lys45, His50) which harbours disease-associated mutations (E46K, G51D, A53T) (Huettemann et al., 2024). Interestingly, an age-associated polyP decline has been reported in mammalian brains (Lorenz et al., 1997) and in the J20 mouse model of AD (Cremers et al., 2016).

PolyP bind non-covalently to lysine repeats (Azevedo et al., 2024, 2015; Neville et al., 2024a, Neville et al., 2024b) and to polyhistidine stretches -bearing proteins (Neville et al., 2023). Among the 86 human proteins with 5 or more consecutive histidine residues (Salichs et al., 2009), DYRK1A contains a 13-histidine stretch in its CTD which is required for localization in nuclear speckles (Alvarez et al., 2003; Lu et al., 2018; Salichs et al., 2009). Nuclear speckles are phase-separated compartments enriched with transcription and splicing factors (Alvarez et al., 2003; Lu et al., 2018). PolyP bind to DYRK1A in an ionic, non-covalent and histidine repeat region -dependent manner (Neville et al., 2023). Binding of polyP to DYRK1A leads to (1) a shift in DYRK1A electrophoretic mobility, (2) an inhibition of DYRK1A localization in nuclear speckles, (3) an inhibition of the ability of DYRK1A to phosphorylate the CTD of the RPB1 subunit of human RNA polymerase II in cell cultures (Neville et al., 2023) (Fig. 28). We tested various forms of polyP (100 μM) on the in vitro catalytic activity of DYRK1A (measured with a peptide substrate) and could not find any inhibitory activity. Whether polyP interact with DYRK1A in vivo and in the PD context remains to be investigated.

Interaction between polyphosphates and DYRK1A and SNCA. DYRK1A accumulates in nuclear speckles where it phosphorylates various substrates, particularly the CTD of the RPB1 subunit of human RNA polymerase II. PolyP prevent DYRK1A activity and nuclear speckle localization. PolyP interact with SNCA, favouring fibril formation but inhibiting neuronal uptake and preventing toxicity.

DYRK1A and axonal transport

Axonal transport comprises anterograde transport (kinesin-mediated) of molecules and organelles from the cell body to synapses and retrograde transport (dynein/dynactin-mediated) of organelles from the presynaptic terminal to the soma. Axonal transport dysregulation is reported in neurodegenerative diseases, including PD and DLB (reviews in (Berth and Lloyd, 2023; Manne et al., 2025; Twellsieck and Boecker, 2025)). PD-causing mutations of LRRK2 are associated with defects in axonal transport of synaptic vesicle precursors, mitochondria and autophagosomes (review in (Twellsieck and Boecker, 2025)).

Phosphorylation of 14-3-3θ on Ser232 leads to reduced axonal transport and is associated with PD and DLB (McFerrin et al., 2017; Pair et al., 2025). Axonal transport is regulated by multiple kinases including AKT, AMPK/p38MAPK (Yang et al., 2025), CDK5, CSNK1, CSNK2, ERK1/2GSK3β, JNK, LRRK2 (Twellsieck and Boecker, 2025), PINK1, PKC (reviews in (Berth and Lloyd, 2023; Gibbs et al., 2015)). PFFs disrupt axonal anterograde transport via the activation of AMPK/p38MAPK pathway (Yang et al., 2025).

DYRK1A differentially regulates the intraneuronal trafficking of APP and transport of vesicles. Harmine or overexpression of the kinase-dead K188R mutant of DYRK1A reduces APP vesicle density in axons while, in contrast, DYRK1A overexpression increases axonal APP vesicle density and retrograde velocity (Fernandez Bessone et al., 2022). Increased retrograde transport and release of synaptic vesicles is seen in neurons derived from human trisomy 21 iPSCs, which is rescued by normalization of DYRK1A copy number (Wu et al., 2022).

DYRK1A, neural progenitors and stem cells

Heterozygote mutations in the DYRK1A gene (leading to a dysfunctional allele) (DYRK1A syndrome), or increased dosage of DYRK1A (due to triplication in Trisomy 21/DS), both lead to alterations in brain development and to intellectual developmental disorders (IDD) (review in (Johnson et al., 2025a)). DYRK1A gene dosage is thus critical for neuronal development and functions.

Depending on the cellular context, DYRK1A has been described as a positive or negative regulator of cell division and as a tumour promoter or suppressor (Chen et al., 2021; Fernández-Martínez et al., 2015; Litovchick et al., 2011; Rammohan et al., 2022; Yabut et al., 2010). DYRK1A inhibition stimulates the proliferation of pancreatic β-cells (in the context of diabetes) ((Bertrand et al., 2025) and references therein) and cardiomyocytes (in the context of myocardial infarct) (Murillo et al., 2025; Young et al., 2022). We did not observe any major change in the proliferation rate of DYRK1A-KO, DYRK1A-KD (kinase dead), DYRK1A-overexpressing SH-SY5Y cells compared to WT SH-SY5Y cells (Lindberg et al., unpublished).

DYRK1A (and other DYRKs, SRPKs, CLKs) regulates neural progenitors and stem cells, both at the level of cell division (cyclin D1, p27kip1 and STAT3 phosphorylation) and of neural differentiation (reviews in (Hogg and Findlay, 2023; Kurabayashi et al., 2019)). DYRK1A negatively regulates self-renewal of glioblastoma stem cells through the inactivation of the CDK5-SOX2 pathway (Chen et al., 2021). In general, overexpression of DYRK1A attenuates neuronal proliferation and promotes differentiation (Hämmerle et al., 2011; Mazur-Kolecka et al., 2012; Najas et al., 2015; Park et al., 2010, Yabut et al., 2010) and loss/inhibition of DYRK1A favours neural proliferation and cell death (Hämmerle et al., 2011; Hibaoui et al., 2014; Soppa et al., 2014). DYRK1A overexpression in embryonic mouse neocortex inhibits neural cell proliferation and promotes neuronal differentiation (Yabut et al., 2010). Repression of DYRK1A (genetically or with the inhibitor harmine) promotes the conversion of ESCs to trophoblast stem cells and thus the transition from pluripotency to totipotency in murine embryogenesis (Zhang et al., 2023b). In contrast, DYRK1A inactivation by siRNAs reduces the proliferation of human neural stem cells (NSCs) in culture (Courraud et al., 2025). Pharmacological inhibition (ID-8) or genetic knock-down of DYRK1A blocks neural specification of human pluripotent stem cells, mimicking DYRK1A haploinsufficiency (Bellmaine et al., 2017). Isogenic cell lines based on DS-specific pluripotent stem cells show that DYRK1A plays a dose-dependent role in promoting proliferation of astrocyte precursors (Kawatani et al., 2021). In the Ts1Cje mouse model of DS, differentiation of cortical progenitor cells into astrocytes is promoted by DYRK1A and mediated by Ser727 phosphorylation and transcriptional activation of STAT3 (Kurabayashi et al., 2015).

Neurogenesis from NSCs declines with age. Quiescence of NSCs appears to be regulated by DYRK1A. The combined overexpression of the PLAGL2 (pleimorphic adenoma gene-like 2) zinc finger transcription factor and inhibition of the Dyrk1a gene expression (by shRNA) in aged mouse brain, rejuvenates hippocampal NSCs, i.e. enhance their proliferative and neurogenic potential. These aged NSCs proliferate and produce neurons, behaving like young NSCs, leading to improved cognitive functions in aged animals (spatial learning and memory, novel object recognition) (Kaise et al., 2022). The Plagl2 induction / Dyrk1a inhibition combination leads to up-regulation of embryonic-high genes and down-regulation of aged-high genes.

The biological age (based on IgG-glycans) of adults with DS is 18.4-19.1 years older than in chronologically matched healthy controls (Murray et al., 2023). This shift is associated with increased DNA damage (increase in γH2AX (Ser139-phosphorylated H2A.X variant histone) foci, a marker of DNA double-strand breaks) and decreased Lamin B1 (a cellular senescence marker). Experiments ran on human IPSCs and in cerebral organoids demonstrate that increased levels of DYRK1A are sufficient and necessary to cause unrepaired DNA damage and progeroid status (Murray et al., 2023). Genetic reduction of DYRK1A expression or pharmacological DYRK1A inhibition (ID8, harmine) improve DNA repair (reduction of γH2AX foci) and restore Lamin B1 level (Murray et al., 2023).

The use of Dyrk1a+/- mice and mice expressing one additional copy of Dyrk1a (mBACtgDyrk1a) shows that DYRK1A plays a protective role towards dopaminergic neurons from caspase 9-mediated apoptosis during early embryogenesis. mBACtgDyrk1a mice are significantly less affected by MPTP than their WT counterparts (Barallobre et al., 2014).

Dopaminergic progenitors derived from hESCs or iPSCs transplanted in putamen of PD patients are now explored as a therapeutic approach for PD ((Chang et al., 2025; Sawamoto et al., 2025; Tabar et al., 2025; Takahashi et al., 2025); review in (Chang and Lee, 2025; Moon et al., 2023)). Although several technical issues (donor cell stress during in vitro preparation, survival of donor cells following transplantation, immune reaction, host SNCA pathology, etc.) are still to be addressed, the first results are rather encouraging. It might be interesting to investigate how DYRK1A /DYRK1A inhibition impinges on hESCs/iPSCs proliferation and differentiation in vitro and in immune reactions in vivo. It is possible that DYRK1A inhibitors may favour cell division and differentiation as they do in other contexts (diabetes ((Bertrand et al., 2025) and references therein), myocardial infarction (Murillo et al., 2025; Young et al., 2022), embryogenesis (see above) and thus be used in the culture and expansion of donor cells prior to transplantation. The anti-inflammatory properties of DYRK1A inhibitors (see below) may be useful after transplantation.

Altogether, the data on the links between DYRK1A and stem cells are conflicting, suggesting a different impact of DYRK1A (and its inhibitors) on neuronal stem cells proliferation and differentiation according to cell type, developmental stage (embryonic vs. adult), evaluation conditions (cell culture vs. organisms). Nevertheless, although DYRK1A inhibitors have the general tendency to favour stem cells proliferation, they do not appear to trigger the development of cancers. DYRK1A inhibitors may be of potential use in the culture and expansion of dopaminergic progenitors designed for in vivo transplantation.

DYRK1A and neuroinflammation

Several lines of evidence point to neuroinflammation as a key driver of the pathophysiology of PD. Studies suggesting that greater neuroinflammation is linked to elevated risk of dementia in patients with PD highlight the importance of better understanding the pathobiology of neuroinflammation in PD (Kouli et al., 2024; Tylutka et al., 2024) and DLB (Amin et al., 2020; Vrillon et al., 2024). Inflammation broadly involves both innate and adaptive immune cells. Particular attention has been paid to the contribution of innate immune responses that may be driven by antigens including but not limited to SNCA (Calabresi et al., 2023b; Furusawa-Nishii et al., 2025; Oludipe et al., 2025). Post-mortem studies reveal large increases in activated microglia in the brains of patients with PD, notably including the SN (Croisier et al., 2005; Imamura et al., 2003; McGeer et al., 1988). This is corroborated by in vivo PET imaging (Gerhard et al., 2006; Lavisse et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022c; Zhang and Gao, 2022).

Microglia are key innate immune effector cells of the CNS and are initial responders, activated by conserved disease-associated molecular patterns (Colonna and Butovsky, 2017). Specific to PD, the innate immune receptors TLR2 and TLR4 (Toll-like receptors) can be engaged by SNCA (Fellner et al., 2013; Kim et al., 2013). Microglial activation leads to production of cytokines including IL-6 (interleukin 6), IL-1β, TNFα (tumor necrosis factor α), IFNγ and IL-2 (Fig. 29). Elevated levels of these cytokines in both serum and CSF of patients with PD support excessive microglial activation (Nagatsu et al., 2000; Schröder et al., 2018). Astrocytes derived from PD patients iPSCs also show increased expression of inflammatory genes including IL-6 production compared to those derived from iPSCs from healthy controls (Pons-Espinal et al., 2024). Dopaminergic neuronal cell death is mediated by expression and activation of IL-6 receptors and downstream activation of STAT3, while anti-IL-6 receptor antibodies prevent neuronal death, pointing to the importance of IL-6 in PD pathobiology. Further, SN neurons overexpressing IL-6 receptors and reactive astrocytes overexpressing IL-6 are detected in PD brain tissues (Pons-Espinal et al., 2024).

DYRK1A and neuroinflammation. SNCA activates the NF-κB and STAT3 pathway in microglia. Consequently, the transcription of several genes involved in inflammation is triggered leading to the production of cytokines. One of them, IL-6, acts on naïve CD4+ T cells to stimulate the STAT pathways which trigger the pro-inflammatory T17 differentiation pathway. DYRK1A is involved in STAT3 phosphorylation in both microglia and CD4+ T cells. In addition, DYRK1A phosphorylates and activates gp130, a key component of the IL-6 receptor. IL, interleukin; MYD88, myeloid differentiation primary response 88; TF, transcription factors.

Although the role of DYRK1A in microglial biology remains to be fully elucidated, phosphorylation of STAT3 on Ser727 is crucial for full inflammatory response to TLR4 (Balic et al., 2020). DYRK1A phosphorylates STAT3 Ser727 (Bhansali et al., 2021; Wiechmann et al., 2003), suggesting how DYRK1A could modulate inflammatory activity of microglia. Early studies showing that small molecule inhibition of DYRK1A can mitigate LPS-TLR4 signalling in a microglial cell line support potential translational relevance of this pathway (Ju et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2020a). DYRK1A inhibitors suppress LPS-induced inflammatory pathways (Lee et al., 2020a; Zeng et al., 2026).

A major outcome of innate immune activation is initiation of the adaptive immune response. CD4+ and CD8+ T cells are key adaptive immune cells of interest in PD. CD8+ T cells, which release cytotoxic granzymes and perforins upon recognition of cognate MHCI (major histocompatibility complex I) + peptide complexes, are found in the SN even at the early stages of PD (Galiano-Landeira et al., 2020). CD4+ T cells are key regulators of immune homeostasis, acting by controlled differentiation into either pro- (e.g., Th17, Th1) or anti- (e.g. Treg) inflammatory lineages. This differentiation is regulated by the cytokine milieu during activation and differentiation upon encounter of cognate MHCII+ peptide complexes. Emerging data supports increased frequency of pro-inflammatory Th17 cells in the peripheral blood and in the midbrain of patients with PD (Jiang et al., 2025; Sommer et al., 2018a; Storelli et al., 2019). Similarly, T cells have been found adjacent to LBs and dopaminergic neurons in postmortem DLB brains (Gate et al., 2021). In vitro assays showing T cells induce death of human iPSC-derived midbrain neurons in an IL-17-dependent manner support a pathogenic role of Th17 cells in PD (Sommer et al., 2018b). SNCA stimulates T cell clonal expansion, activation and IL-17A expression (Gate et al., 2021). These data point to the importance of T cells, particularly Th17 cells, in the pathobiology of PD and DLB.

Both genetic and pharmacological evidence point to the importance of DYRK1A in regulating Th17 differentiation (Kim et al., 2023; Malueg et al., 2025; Sommer et al., 2018b). Both conditional deletion of Dyrk1a and natural variation in Dyrk1a expression are linked to corresponding variation in Th17 differentiation, highlighting DYRK1A as an analogue regulator of Th17 differentiation (Malueg et al., 2025). IL-6 is the key cytokine driving Th17 differentiation (Fig. 29). Increased IL-6 likely contributes to the increased number of Th17 cells in patients with PD (Korn and Hiltensperger, 2021). IL-6 signals by binding the IL-6 receptor, leading to phosphorylation of STAT3 (Tyr705), STAT1 (Tyr701) and STAT5 (Tyr694) (Fig. 29). DYRK1A regulates STAT3 phosphorylation on Tyr705 at least in part by phosphorylating the regulatory Ser727 residue (Bhansali et al., 2021; Wiechmann et al., 2003). Recent studies showing that DYRK1A does not use a similar mechanism to regulate either STAT1 Tyr701 or STAT5 Tyr694 phosphorylation led to the recognition that DYRK1A also regulates phosphorylation of the IL-6 receptor subunit gp130 (Malueg et al., 2025). This highlights the multilayered manners by which DYRK1A regulates IL-6 signalling and Th17 differentiation. Studies showing that small molecule inhibitors of DYRK1A all inhibit pro-inflammatory Th17 differentiation highlight DYRK1A as a therapeutically tractable drug target candidate (Khor et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2023; Malueg et al., 2025). Although the role of DYRK1A in CD8+ T cell biology remains understudied, DYRK1A is known to regulate signalling of NFAT (see section on NFAT), a central transcription factor in T cell biology, highlighting potential avenues of future research (Arron et al., 2006; Gwack et al., 2006a; Khor et al., 2015).

In brief, neuroinflammation is a key driver of PD. Results point to the importance of IL-6 and Th17 cells in the pathobiology of PD and DLB. DYRK1A drives pro-inflammatory processes in both the innate and adaptive immune systems, suggesting that DYRK1A inhibitors may exert anti-inflammatory activity and mitigate PD pathobiology.

Pharmacological inhibitors of DYRK1A and Parkinson’s disease models

Protein kinase inhibitors in Parkinson’s disease and dementia with Lewy bodies

Multiple strategies are being developed to identify new therapeutic drugs for PD and DLB (Abdelnour et al., 2023a; Kaila et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2024a; McFarthing et al., 2024; Menozzi and Schapira, 2025; Stocchi et al., 2024; Tenchov et al., 2025; Tolea et al., 2023). Since 2013, 166 distinct products representing 146 distinct active molecules, mapped to 52 pharmacological targets, have been or are being tested in clinical trials for PD (review in (Machado et al., 2026)). Given the success of kinase inhibitors as therapeutic drugs (review in (Roskoski, 2026)) - the 100th small-molecule kinase inhibitor was approved by the FDA in 2025 (Mullard, 2025)-, it is not surprising that these enzymes have attracted substantial work as potential targets for PD and DLB (reviews in (Kaila et al., 2025; Krahn et al., 2020; Mansour et al., 2023; Mansour and El-Khatib, 2023; Raza et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025a)) (Fig. 30).

Protein kinases as therapeutic targets in PD or DLB. Kinase gene mutations or SNPs associated with PD, GWAS identified kinase targets, Michael J. Fox “Targets to Therapies” (T2T) initiative, potential kinases targets, kinase inhibitors currently in clinical trial in PD or DLB.

Five genes encoding protein kinases show SNPs or mutations associated with PD: DYRK1A, GAK, LRRK2, PINK1, PRKRA (review in (Raza et al., 2025)). LRRK2 (reviews in (Bai et al., 2025; Morez et al., 2024)) has received most of the attention in terms of development of inhibitors. Very few inhibitors of GAK and PINK1 have been developed for PD and DLB, and none for PRKRA (Raza et al., 2025).

The latest GWAS highlighted 141 PD risk factors (Kim et al., 2024b; Nalls et al., 2019). These include 8 protein kinases: AAK1 (AP2 associated kinase 1), AKT3, CaMK2D, DYRK1A, FYN, LRRK2, STK39 (serine/threonine kinase 39) and WNK1 (lysine deficient protein kinase 1) (Fig. 30).

Wu et al. (Wu et al., 2025a) recently reviewed the involvement of protein kinases in neurodegenerative diseases. For PD, these authors list c-ABL, AKT, CSNK, ERK1/2, GAK, GRK5, GSK3β, JNK, LRRK2, MAP2K, MAP3K, PINK1, PLK, and p38. A review on “Kinases control of regulated cell death revealing druggable targets for PD” lists 11 protein kinases as potential targets in this indication (Mansour et al., 2023): AMPK, ASK1, ATM, CDKs, DAPK1, LRRK2, MLK, MLKL (mixed lineage kinase domain like pseudokinase), PKC, RIPK1 (receptor-interacting protein kinase 1), and RIPK3.

The Michael J. Fox Foundation « Target to Therapies » initiative recently selected 59 targets of interest (comprising 5 kinases, CDK5, CSNK2B, DYRK1A, EIF2AK1 (eukaryotic translation initiation factor α kinase 1), and FYN), from which a final list of 21 (only one kinase, CDK5) was chosen (Vaiana et al., 2025).

The latest review on PD drug therapies in the clinical trial pipeline records 136 ongoing trials (McFarthing et al., 2024). Seven drugs target 3 different protein kinases: c-ABL (FB-418, Radotinib, K0706 or Vodobatinib, IkT-148009), LRRK2 (FB-418, BIIB122, BIIB094) and), ROCK (Rho kinase) (Fasudil) (Fig. 30) (see also (Prakash et al., 2021)).

The latest review on DLB drug therapies in the clinical trial pipeline records 25 agents in 40 ongoing trials (Abdelnour et al., 2023b). Four drugs target 3 different protein kinases: p38α kinase (Neflamapimod), c-ABL (Nilotinib, Vodobatinib), and c-SRC (Bosutinib) (Fig. 30).

Nilotinib, Vodobatinib, Bosutinib and Radotinib have no effects (tested up to 10 μM) on DYRK1A or DYRK1B (unpublished results).

Pharmacological inhibitors of DYRK1A

Given the importance of DYRK1A in CNS and non-CNS diseases, numerous inhibitors have been designed and characterized (reviews in (de Souza et al., 2023; Gehlot et al., 2024; Kokkorakis et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2022c; Nguyen et al., 2017); see detailed review and comparative study in (Lindberg et al., 2023a)). Most DYRK1A inhibitors inhibit DYRK1B, and the cdc2-like kinases CLK1 and CLK4. A small selection of the best characterized, most used and/or selective inhibitors is shown in Fig. 31. Several inhibitors have been co-crystallized with DYRK1A and most have been modelled in the DYRK1A structure ((Soundararajan et al., 2013), reviews in (Lindberg et al., 2023a; Lindberg and Meijer, 2021)). With the exception of the PROTAC (proteolysis targeting chimera) inhibitor DYR684 (Wilms et al., 2024), all inhibitors described so far are acting by reversible competition at the ATP binding site. It is anticipated that inhibitors acting through other mechanisms will be described soon: covalent, irreversible inhibitors, allosteric modulators, bivalent binders (ATP binding site + allosteric site), PROTACs, etc. The recent discovery of FAM53C as a natural protein inhibitor of DYRK1A ((Hammond et al., 2024; Miyata and Nishida, 2023)) may lead to the development of another class of DYRK1A inhibitors (peptidomimetics). To our knowledge only 11 DYRK1A inhibitors are/have been in clinical trials (Fig. 31) (Meijer et al., 2024): Abemaciclib, CC-401 (JNK-401), Cirtuvivint (SM08502), epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), FRTX-02 (VRN024219; BBI-02), GSK-626616, Harmine, Leucettinib-21, Lorecivivint (SM04690), Silmitarsertib (CX-4945), and SM07883. Four of them, optimized on other therapeutics targets, were later found to inhibit DYRK1A (Lindberg et al., 2023a).

In brief, DYRK1A is attracting increasing interest as a druggable therapeutic target for a large diversity of clinical indications. Several highly optimized, low molecular weight pharmacological inhibitors have entered early clinical trials. Essentially all inhibitors target the ATP-binding pocket of the kinase. Following safety phase 1 clinical trials, DYRK1A inhibitors should soon be available for phase 2 efficacy studies in the PD and DLB context.

Effects of pharmacological inhibitors of DYRK1A on Parkinson’s disease models

The effects of DYRK1A inhibitors on cellular/animal PD models have been reported in a small number of studies, with poorly characterized inhibitors (in terms of potency and selectivity) (Aboushady et al., 2021; AlNajjar et al., 2022; Araldi and Hwang, 2023). The reported DYRK1A inhibitors 6-hydroxybenzothiazole urea derivatives 24b (Aboushady et al., 2021), b1 and b20 (AlNajjar et al., 2022) partially inhibit SNCA aggregation (IC50: 19.2, 10.5 and 7.8 μM, respectively). Compounds b1 and b20 prevent SNCA-induced cytotoxicity in SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells (AlNajjar et al., 2022). This is not observed with harmine, suggesting that the effects of b1 and b20 are unrelated to DYRK1A inhibition. Compounds 24b, b1 and harmine partially protect SH-SY5Y cells against 6-OHDA-induced death (Aboushady et al., 2021; AlNajjar et al., 2022). Recently, the effects of harmine was evaluated in a MPTP-induced mouse model of PD (Cai et al., 2025). MPTP triggers an increased expression of DYRK1A in the midbrain, paralleled by decreased TH immunofluorescence in the striatum and midbrain, and correction of the expression of several mitochondrial proteins (VDAC1, TIM23, TOM20). Treatment of a mouse model of PD with harmine triggers a dose-dependent correction of behavioural deficits, paralleled by a decreased expression of DYRK1A and SNCA expression, an increase in the number of TH-positive cells and a reduction in apoptosis (Cai et al., 2025). Similarly, MN9D cells exposed to MPP+ show increased DYRK1A expression. DYRK1A knockdown attenuates the MPP+-induced apoptosis, while DYRK1A overexpression exacerbates MPP+-induced cell death (Cai et al., 2025). AAV-mediated silencing of the Dyrk1a gene in the SN of mice leads to apparent protection from the effects of MPTP injection, reduced α-synuclein expression and Ser129 phosphorylation, reduced inflammation (IL-1β, IL-6, COX2, TNF-α), improved motor activity, and reduced dopaminergic neurons apoptosis (Yong et al., 2023).

The green tea extract -derived polyphenol EGCG (Fig. 31) is a weak and unselective, but frequently used DYRK1A inhibitor (see reviews in (Lindberg et al., 2023a)). The pleiotropic actions of EGCG have encouraged numerous studies in cellular and animal models of PD to evaluate its potential as a drug candidate (reviews in (Caruana and Vassallo, 2015; Kalu and Ray, 2025; Payne et al., 2022; Sergi, 2022; Wang et al., 2022d)). Neuroprotective effects of EGCG have been observed in MPTP-, 6-OHDA- or rotenone- induced PD mouse models (for ex. see (Choi et al., 2002; Tseng et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2018a); reviews in (Payne et al., 2022; Sergi, 2022; Wang et al., 2022d)). These beneficial effects can possibly be attributed to protection against oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, neuroinflammation, ferroptosis, to modulation of dopamine production and to inhibition of SNCA aggregation. Similar protective effects are seen with cell cultures exposed to MPP+, 6-OHDA, paraquat, etc. EGCG prevents α-synuclein aggregation (Xu et al., 2016), oxidized EGCG remodels SNCA fibrils into non-toxic aggregates (Gonçalves et al., 2023). A series of fluorinated polyphenol derivatives of EGCG was recently synthesized with improved action on DYRK1A and improved bioavailability: compound 1c (73 nM IC50 on DYRK1A vs 220 nM for EGCG) corrected motricity impairments in the MPTP-induced mouse model of PD (Araldi and Hwang, 2023). A recent study reports the neuroprotective effect of EGCG in a mouse model of PD (stereotaxic injection of preformed SNCA fibrils in the striatum): motor impairments were significantly decreased on a long term basis, and degeneration of TH-immuno-positive neurons, accumulation of phosphorylated SNCA and proinflammatory cytokines were reduced (Shen et al., 2024b).

In brief, given the limited or unknown selectivity of the investigated “DYRK1A inhibitory” compounds, it is not possible, at this stage, to attribute definitively their beneficial effects in PD models to specific inhibition of DYRK1A. Clearly more work is required with more potent, more selective, and better characterized DYRK1A inhibitors, complemented by kinase-inactive analogs and genetic modulations of the DYRK1A gene expression.

The case of Harmine & Parkinson’s disease

In a recent comparative study of DYRKs/CLKs inhibitors (Lindberg et al., 2023a), we described harmine (Fig. 31) as follows: “harmine is a natural product found in many plant species such as Peganum harmala, along with various metabolites/analogs (harmaline, harmane, harmol, etc…) (Brierley and Davidson, 2012; Grabher et al., 2012); (review in (Zhang et al., 2020)). Harmine was initially identified as a DYRK1A inhibitor (Adayev et al., 2011; Bain et al., 2007; Frost et al., 2011; Göckler et al., 2009; Grabher et al., 2012; Tahtouh et al., 2012). It inhibits other DYRKs and CLKs and interacts also with CDK8, CDK9 and CSNK1G3 (Ball et al., 2020). It is a very potent inhibitor of monoamine oxidase MAO-A (Bálint et al., 2017; Reniers et al., 2011). Harmine is also an antagonist of the NMDA receptor (Du et al., 1997). It inhibits histone deacetylase and interacts with DNA (Lu et al., 2022). It has been co-crystallized with DYRK1A (Ogawa et al., 2010) and MAO-A (Son et al., 2008). Harmine and the chemically related natural product Bauerine C (Huber et al., 2012) have inspired the synthesis of many analogs and derivatives with improved selectivity, such as AnnH75 (Fig. 31) (Rüben et al., 2015; Wurzlbauer et al., 2020) and others. In our assays, harmine was a modest DYRK1A and DYRK1B inhibitor (IC50 (radioactivity assay): 192 and 293 nM, respectively; IC50 (luminescence assay): 62 and 94 nM, respectively). It was also active on CLK1 and CLK4 (IC50: 242 and 102 nM, respectively) (see (Lindberg et al., 2023a) for details). Although multi-target (kinases and non-kinases), harmine has been used in many studies investigating the role of DYRK1A in physiological processes” (Lindberg et al., 2023a).

In early 1928, Banisterine, a hallucinogenic compound prepared from the South American vine, Banisteria caapi, was reported to improve motor control, and to have potential use to treat extrapyramidal disorders associated with rigor and akinesia (Beringer and Wilmanns, 1929; Lewin, 1928a; Lewin, 1928b) (reviews in (Djamshidian et al., 2016; Foley, 2003; Sanchez-Ramos, 1991)). Banisterine turned out to be identical to harmine (Wolfes and Ivers, 1928) 3. The beneficial effects were initially attributed to inhibition of MAO. Banisterine /harmine was tested in multiple clinical trials against parkinsonism in the late 1920’s and early 1930’s (reviews in (Djamshidian et al., 2016; Foley, 2003)), and harmine (extracted from plants) was even commercialized by Merck in late 1928 for ‘postencephalitic parkinsonism and paralysis agitans’ in capsules, suppository, and as an injectable solution form. Although initially encouraging, but unrealistically supported by the media, the clinical benefits were variable and short lasting. The drug was finally abandoned after a few years. The safety of harmine has recently been evaluated in a phase 1 clinical trial: no serious adverse events were observed (Ables et al., 2024). B. caapi extracts or harmine provide mild benefits in a marmoset MPTP-induced PD model (Fisher et al., 2018). In-silico studies predicted harmine and harmaline as drug candidates for the treatment of PD (Banerjee et al., 2021). Both DYRK1A knock-down and harmine treatment attenuate the miR-204-5p-induced (miR-204-5p activates DYRK1A expression, see Fig. 4) increase in Ser219-phosphorylated SNCA, Ser202/Thr205-phosphorylated Tau, ER stress, autophagy impairment and JNK-dependent apoptosis in SH-SY5Y cells and primary cultured dopaminergic neurons (Chiu et al., 2019). Harmine (Cai et al., 2019) promotes the degradation of SNCA in a cellular model through a PKA-dependent proteasomal pathway. Similarly, harmol (also inhibiting DYRK1A (Tarpley et al., 2021)) triggers SNCA degradation in cells expressing the SNCA A53T mutant by regulating the autophagy-lysosome pathway and reduces motor impairment in SNCA A53T mice (Xu et al., 2022). Harmine hydrochloride reduces oligokinesia and rigidity in the MPTP-induced PD mouse model (Nurmaganbetov et al., 2019).

On the other hand, β-carboline alkaloids are known as neurotoxins inducing tremor (Louis et al., 2014). Interestingly, a few articles have reported elevated levels of the harmine-related harmane and norharmanin in blood and CSF of PD patients (Kuhn et al., 1996, 1995b, 1995a; Louis et al., 2014). In a study involving 113 PD cases and 101 controls, the plasma levels of harmane were found to be doubled in the PD population (Louis et al., 2014). β-carboline alkaloids appear to be both neuroprotective and neurotoxic. This data on beneficial and neurotoxic effects of β-carbolines, and their presence in the plasma and CSF of PD patients, is confusing but maybe ‘hiding’ some interesting potential. Individual β-carbolines and their metabolites need to be investigated in more detail.

In brief, harmine was evaluated in the late 1920’s and early 1930’s in PD, and even commercialized despites mixed results. This was long before it was identified as a DYRK1A inhibitor (among other targets). Harmine and related compounds globally show some neuroprotective effects in various PD models.

In summary, the global therapeutic success of kinase inhibitors calls for the investigation of the role of kinases in the onset and development of PD and DLB, for their evaluation as potential therapeutic targets and for the multi-parametric optimization of their pharmacological inhibitors. Some kinases have been selected as potentially promising targets, and a few inhibitors are already evaluated in phase 1 and phase 2 clinical trials. DYRK1A certainly deserves similar attention in these indications.

Conclusions and future steps

Facts and hypotheses

This review aims at presenting an extensive view on all available results linking the DYRK1A kinase to PD or DLB. A summary is shown in Fig. 32. Some facts, confirmed by different independent groups, are solidly established, while other results are weaker, some even questionable, and deserve further confirmation. Altogether, these data apparently support the involvement of DYRK1A in PD/DLB and the beneficial effects of its pharmacological modulation. Furthermore, they match with the identification of DYRK1A as a risk factor for PD in GWAS studies. Despites the heterogeneity of the data and our confessed ‘DYRK1A-centric’ view, we believe that the current knowledge on DYRK1A and PD/DLB is sufficiently encouraging to justify further investigations on (1) the precise role of DYRK1A, and its regulators, in the onset and development of PD and DLB, and (2) the potential of pharmacological inhibitors of DYRK1A as treatments for various aspects PD and DLB, including neurodegeneration, synaptic dysfunctions, neuroinflammation, neural regeneration, motor and cognitive dysfunctions.

A “DYRK1A-centric” view of PD. DYRK1A phosphorylates 20 proteins (grey) which play an established role in PD. DYRK1A interacts with a few proteins known to be involved in PD (14-3-3, CALPN, DSCAM, NRSF2). DYRK1A binds to several regulators (DCAF7, FAM53C, FAM117A/B, SPRED1/2, SPRY2). These interactions impinge on dopaminergic neurons survival/cell death, axonal transport, synaptic functions, and neuroinflammation.

PD provides yet another example of the 3C dilemma (coincidence, cause or consequence?) faced by researchers in their efforts to decipher the molecular mechanisms leading to diseases. Is any suspected player just a coincidence, the real cause or a mere consequence? This is no trivial question. Even very accepted “usual suspects” such as SNCA and LRRK2 (Gatto et al., 2026) most probably play key physiological roles, and may not represent the ideal therapeutic targets put forward by many (but not all!). So far, pharmacological approaches directed towards these targets have not yielded any significant therapeutic efficacy. This situation reminds of the AD situation where the main actors, APP, amyloid-β42, and Tau, although clearly implicated in the disease, have not yet provided major, undisputed therapeutic benefits, despites considerable investments (see discussion in (Espay et al., 2025a, 2023)). Confusion of pathology (markers) and disease (inducers) has confused both PD and AD fields (Espay and Lees, 2025b).

Genetic mutations on specific genes leading to early onset PD point to the involvement of the corresponding gene products to their disease triggering role, but provide modest clues on their molecular mechanisms of action. Similarly, GWAS results highlight factors that impinge on the development of the disease, but do not provide much information on how these factors actually act.

This is somewhat the situation we face with DYRK1A. Although most of the results reported here point towards the pathogenic implication of DYRK1A in PD, a formal demonstration is lacking, and a protective role in some instances may not be fully excluded. Is DYRK1A activity abnormally regulated in PD (upregulation or down-regulation of expression and activity, abnormal intracellular localization, abnormal expression/activation of its regulators)? Is this abnormal regulation of DYRK1A a simple coincidence, a real cause, or a mere consequence? As a consequence or a coincidence, DYRK1A might be useful as a pathology biomarker to accompany the development of therapeutic drugs and to stratify patients in clinical trials. If DYRK1A is demonstrated to be causal to PD, it may become a therapeutic target open to the development of activity modulators.

In many aspects DYRK1A appears as a homeostasis modulator. This is illustrated by the negative consequences of both overexpression (DS) and downregulation (DYRK1A syndrome) (reviews in (Atas-Ozcan et al., 2021; Johnson et al., 2025b; Meijer et al., 2024; Murphy et al., 2024b)). Normalization of the DYRK1A activity, rather than full inhibition, is probably what is essential to correct neuronal deficits. This is the case seen in Down syndrome, where normalization of DYRK1A activity leads to correction of cognitive deficits associated with overexpression and overactivity of DYRK1A (Meijer et al., 2024; Nguyen et al., 2018b). Normalization of DYRK1A activity is thus the objective.

DYRK1A in Parkinson’s disease and dementia with Lewy bodies: what’s next?

(1) Fundamental studies

In terms of functional, mechanistic studies, there are numerous, well-characterized cellular and animal models of PD/DLB available to investigate the implication of DYRK1A in the onset and development of many aspects of PD and DLB. The 3C dilemma will always be in the background of such investigations. A key question is the status of DYRK1A expression and activity at the onset and during development of PD/DLB. Is DYRK1A catalytic activity transiently or permanently overactivated, and, if so, what are the initiating events of this abnormal activation? Alternatively, is it downregulated under some conditions? Is DYRK1A over/underexpressed at the mRNA level or are post-translational modifications (phosphorylation, truncation by calpains?) responsible for transiently or stably increased/decreased catalytic activity? Are DYRK1A regulators (DCAF7, FAM53C, FAM117, etc.) dysregulated and through which mechanisms? Is there an abnormal change in DYRK1A intracellular distribution? How do PD-associated micro-RNAs regulate the expression of DYRK1A, its interactors and its substrates? All these questions can be addressed at various levels: molecular, cellular, animal and human.

At the molecular level, DYRK1A can be studied in terms of alternative splicing isoforms, mRNA and protein expression and kinase catalytic activity (either in crude extracts or following enrichment by affinity chromatography (IMAC, immunoprecipitation, immobilized inhibitors)). There are multiple post-translational modifications (Fig. 2) and interacting proteins (DCAF7, FAM53C, FAM117A/B, SPRED1/2, SPRY2, etc.) (reviewed in Deau et al., submitted) which impinge on DYRK1A intracellular localization, catalytic activity, substrate specificity. Furthermore, the expression of DYRK1A is regulated by a series of miRNAs which clearly deserve more investigations (Fig. 4). Finally, the catalytic activity of DYRK1A can also be assessed, indirectly, at the level of specific phosphorylation sites of its substrates (at least 20 PD/DLB-relevant proteins are phosphorylated by DYRK1A (Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7, Fig. 8, Fig. 9, Fig. 10, Fig. 11, Fig. 12, Fig. 13, Fig. 14, Fig. 15, Fig. 16, Fig. 17, Fig. 18, Fig. 19, Fig. 20, Fig. 21, Fig. 22, Fig. 23, Fig. 24, Fig. 32)). All these parameters could be studied in cellular and animal models of PD/DLB, and many of them in human tissues.

At the cellular level, DYRK1A has been localized in different sub-cellular compartments, membranes, cytoplasm, nucleus, nuclear speckles, and cytoskeleton. It is also enriched in synaptic fractions. The intracellular distribution of DYRK1A and its binding partners in the nervous system clearly deserves more studies, both under healthy conditions and in PD/DLB situations, in cell and animal models, as well as in human PD/DLB tissues. The regulation of DYRK1A expression in cells by transcription factors and miRNAs deserves more investigations.

At the animal level, rodents provide a large diversity of models which, to some extent, reproduce various aspects of PD/DLB: neurotoxic agents (MPTP, 6-OHDA, bacterial LPS, etc.), injected SNCA PFFs, viral transfection of disease-relevant mutant genes, and various genetic models (transgenic, knockin, knockout, conditional) (reviews in (Boschen et al., 2025; Dawson and Dawson, 2025; Morales-García, 2025; Reinares-Sebastián et al., 2025; Sturchio et al., 2024)). However, one should be careful with extrapolations: the translational potential of animal models for predicting molecular mechanisms and therapeutic efficacy in humans has its limitations (Loewa et al., 2023; Sturchio et al., 2024). A striking example of divergence is provided by the SNCA phosphorylation site by DYRK1A, Ser87, which is present in human but not conserved in rodents (Paleologou et al., 2010). Studies carried out with human-derived material (cells derived from iPSCs, organoids, one or multi-organs-on-chip, bioengineered tissue models, etc.), or rodents ‘genetically humanized’ for the specific gene under study (Bury et al., 2025), should therefore be encouraged.

At the human level, little is known about the expression and activity of DYRK1A (and its interactors and regulators) in the human brain, whether young or aged, healthy or from PD/DLB patients. There is a strong need for detailed analysis of DYRK1A and its regulators in human brain samples during the complete human life span and during the development of PD or DLB. PD is a family of pathologies, and DYRK1A may be involved in different, but not all subsets. Finally, these studies should not be limited to the brain.

(2) Applied research: development of therapeutic DYRK1A inhibitors

The development of a DYRK1A inhibitor as a PD/DLB therapeutic drug implies the optimization and full characterization of potent and selective inhibitors with excellent pharmacological properties. A solid proof-of-concept demonstration of beneficial effects in relevant animal models of PD/DLB is required. A detailed characterization and demonstration of the drug’s innocuity in several animals is also required. The phase 1 clinical studies should first demonstrate safety and bioavailability, and show favourable pharmacokinetics. They should be accompanied by the identification of biomarkers (preferably plasmatic) which allow both the demonstration of target engagement and a clear pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics correlation. The challenge of translating efficacy in animal models to efficacy in people with PD/DLB has been highlighted recently (Reinares-Sebastián et al., 2025; Teil and Huot, 2026).

In conclusion, we believe that the data reviewed in this article and the development of pharmacological inhibitors of DYRK1A (in the context of other therapeutic indications) call for more investment of human and financial resources in the fundamental study of DYRK1A in PD and DLB and pharmacological investigations on DYRK1A inhibition as a therapeutic approach to these devastating diseases.

Word of caution

Although we have done our best possible efforts to cross-check the reported results, one should remain aware of the very unfortunate recent increase in the number of fake paper-mill produced articles, unreproducible results and very poor-quality research. We hope we did not contribute to spread some of these articles.
