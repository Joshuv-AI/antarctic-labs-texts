Modeling the Peste des Petits Ruminants (PPR) disease transmission dynamics with impacts of vaccination and restocking in small ruminant population in Amhara region, Ethiopia
Authors: Yibekal Walle, Joseph YT Mugisha, Dawit Melese, Haileyesus Tessema
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeste des Petits Ruminants (PPR) is a highly contagious transboundary viral disease of small ruminants with significant economic implications caused by the Peste des Petits Ruminants virus. This study employs mathematical modeling to investigate the impact of imperfect PPR vaccines and restocked small ruminants on the transmission dynamics of PPR. A deterministic mathematical model is developed by incorporating vaccinated and restocked subpopulations into the classical SEIR model. The influence of infected animals introduced through restocking on vaccination efficacy in preventing PPR spread is examined. The global dynamics of equilibrium points in the model are analyzed using the Lyapunov-LaSalle invariance principle. Parameter values for numerical simulations are estimated based on monthly PPR data from the Amhara regional state in Ethiopia, obtained from the Ministry of Agriculture. The basic reproduction number (Rv) is calculated to assess the level of PPR in the small ruminant population, and sensitivity analysis of parameters is performed on Rv. The analytical and numerical results reveal that infected restocked small ruminants significantly facilitate the spread of PPR in the population. Moreover, even with high efficacy vaccination, the system exhibits a unique asymptotically stable endemic equilibrium. These findings emphasize that appropriate vaccination alone is insufficient to control and eradicate PPR in the region. Implementing strict movement restrictions and biosecurity measures are necessary. These findings provide valuable insights for national policymakers in achieving the regional and national targets for PPR eradication by 2027.Keywords: Global stability, PPR disease, Vaccination efficacy, Restocking

Introduction

Peste des petits ruminants (PPR) is a highly contagious non-zoonotic infection caused by the Peste des Petits Ruminants virus, which belongs to the genus Morbillivirus in the family Paramyxoviridae [1]. Primarily affecting goats and sheep, PPR is closely related to the bovine and buffalo rinderpest virus, as well as the human measles virus [2], [3]. Clinical symptoms of PPR include high fever (pyrexia), conjunctivitis, oculo-nasal discharges, necrotizing and erosive stomatitis, diarrhea, and bronchopneumonia, which can ultimately lead to the death of the animal or recovery from the disease [4]. With a high transmission rate, PPR can have a devastating impact on small ruminant populations, often resulting in morbidity and mortality rates close to 100% in naive populations [3]. Currently safe and effective live attenuated vaccine is available against the PPR virus. The PPR vaccine is administered to susceptible small ruminants, such as sheep and goats, through injection or intranasal administration. Vaccination aims to induce active immunity in animals, stimulating the production of antibodies that can neutralize the virus and protect against future infections [5], [2], [6].

The spread of Peste des petits ruminants occurs through close contact between infected animals, with the virus shed in secretions (such as nasal and salivary) and excretions (such as feces) of infected animals. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), PPR affects approximately 30 million animals across 70 countries worldwide, with 60% of these countries situated in Africa and the remaining 40% in the Middle East and Asia [7].

First described in 1942 in Côte d'Ivoire, West Africa, PPR has now become widespread throughout Asia and most of Africa, posing a major constraint on small ruminant production and welfare in these regions. This disease presents a risk to the food security and livelihoods of the world's poorest people, as sheep and goats are often valuable resources for them [3]. In Ethiopia, PPR was first clinically suspected in 1977 in a goat herd in the Afar region, and subsequent evidence of its presence was reported in 1984 and confirmed in 1991 [8], [9], [6]. Despite efforts by organizations like the FAO and the World Organisation for Animal Health (OIE) to control PPR in several countries, including Ethiopia, the disease continues to spread throughout Africa and Asia, burdening livestock keepers and veterinary resources in affected areas. In the Amhara regional state of Ethiopia, retrospective data analysis from 2010 to 2014 revealed a high occurrence of PPR, with a total of 63 outbreaks, 3,978 cases, and 919 deaths reported [9]. This data highlights the significance and widespread nature of PPR in the Amhara region [9], [5].

Several risk factors contribute to the spread of the PPR virus into disease-free regions. These factors include herd size, mixed herds of susceptible animals (goats and sheep), the introduction of animals of unknown origin without proper health guarantees or quarantine periods, the return of unsold animals from markets, religious festivals leading to increased trade movements and the establishment of slaughter centers, as well as the trading, loaning, and gifting of animals [3], [7], [10]. These activities, collectively known as restocking, can inadvertently introduce infected animals into previously disease-free areas, resulting in the rapid spread of PPR and subsequent outbreaks [9], [11].

Given the transboundary nature of PPR [12], it is crucial to incorporate restocking cases into the analysis to understand their impact on PPR transmission dynamics, develop effective prevention mechanisms, and assess whether restocking of small ruminants will affect the implementation of PPR vaccines. Furthermore, it is important to evaluate the current control measures in place and their effectiveness in controlling or eradicating the disease.

In the existing literature, several statistical studies on Peste des Petits Ruminants (PPR) have been conducted. These studies primarily focus on epidemiological aspects, spatial distribution, and risk factors, employing statistical tools such as logistic regression models and univariate or multivariate analyses. Notably, the authors of the study [2] explored the spatial distribution of PPR in Ethiopia and examined the risk factors associated with positive serological status. The results indicated that the circulation of PPR Virus (PPRV) is highly heterogeneous. In their national serological survey, a significant sample size was collected in the Amhara region, comprising 43.9% of the total samples. Research presented in [6] provided insights into the prevalence and distribution of PPR in the Amhara region through cross-sectional and retrospective studies aimed at estimating seroprevalence, identifying risk factors (including species, sex, agro-ecology, and study location), and assessing the distribution of PPR. Their findings revealed that PPR is endemic in the area, with PPR seropositivity and disease outbreaks correlating with identified risk factors. The study underscored the necessity for continuous surveillance and vaccination efforts to mitigate the disease's impact. A similar study [13] was conducted in Afar region (adjacent of Amhara region), focusing on the seroprevalence and associated risk factors of PPR in selected districts. The results indicated a higher seroprevalence of PPR virus infection, confirming that PPR is endemic in that region. These studies highlight the prevalence and circulation of the disease in the respective areas. However, to evaluate the effectiveness of control measures in relation to other risk factors associated with the restocking of small ruminants, the present study adopts a mathematical epidemiology modeling approach based on the findings of these statistical investigations and prevalence data of the disease that reported the last nineteen years in the region.

Mathematical epidemiological modeling is a widely used tool for simulating, predicting disease behavior, evaluating control, and creating potential scenarios to estimate the effects of contagious disease outbreaks, such as Peste des Petits Ruminants (PPR), Anthrax [14], Brucellosis [15], and Foot-and-Mouth Disease (FMD) [16], in populations of domesticated animals. Policymakers can leverage the data generated from these scenarios to implement disease control strategies and plan for early and ongoing efforts in disease management and eradication [7]. However, there have been comparatively few mathematical epidemiological studies [3], [7], [17] focused specifically on modeling PPR, with respect to other infectious animal diseases. Notably, the study by Fournie et al. [3] demonstrated the potential for disease transmission and the possible coverage of vaccination to eradicate PPR in two agro-ecological zones (highland and lowland areas) of Ethiopia, employing a meta population model that integrates both stochastic and deterministic approaches within the SIR framework. To our knowledge, no mathematical or statistical study has specifically examined how the restocking of small ruminants affects the dynamics of PPR and the interplay between restocking and vaccination.

This article aims to model the transmission dynamics of PPR, considering the impact of restocking on small ruminant populations, and evaluate the effectiveness of vaccination in suppressing the spread of PPR in the Amhara region of Ethiopia. We incorporate a restocked class into a classical SVEIR deterministic compartmental model of PPR to investigate the impact of restocking on disease transmission dynamics. The model takes into account factors such as the introduction of animals of unknown origin and trade movements. By simulating different scenarios, policymakers can assess the effectiveness of control measures and vaccination strategies in managing and eradicating PPR.

Discussion and conclusion

Peste des petits ruminants (PPR) is a significant endemic disease of small ruminants in Ethiopia, caused by the PPR virus. Despite the availability of a lifetime immunity-providing vaccine, PPR outbreaks continue to spread in Asia and Africa, indicating that control and eradication efforts can be improved by considering various risk factors. In this study, we incorporated a restocked group into a deterministic SEVIR epidemic model to assess the impact of restocking on PPR dynamics. This model, like other epidemiological models of infectious animal diseases, allows us to simulate disease spread and evaluate control strategies effectively.

Our analytical and numerical results revealed critical insights into the persistence of PPR in the Amhara region. In the scenario without the influx of infective animals due to restocking, we observed that the basic reproduction number (Rv), estimated at 1.0518, indicated the endemicity of the disease in the region. When Rv<1, the disease-free equilibrium was globally asymptotically stable, while the endemic equilibrium was locally stable when Rv>1. In numerical simulation, we demonstrate that high vaccine efficacy could potentially lead to disease control or eradication without the complicating factor of restocking dynamics. In contrast to the scenario that included restocking, we investigated the existence and global asymptotic stability of a unique endemic equilibrium point. This scenario highlighted that the influx of susceptible and infected animals from surrounding regions has a significant impact on disease dynamics. Even with ideal coverage and efficacy of the vaccine, the introduction of PPRV through restocking could maintain the disease within the population, emphasizing that vaccination efforts alone may not be sufficient to control the outbreak.

The findings suggest that while improving vaccination efficacy and coverage is crucial, external influx of infection due to restocking poses a substantial challenge to controlling PPR. This aligns with previous studies [3], which indicate that the Amhara region, characterized as a highland or sedentary agro-ecological zone, interacts with adjacent lowland areas that serve as reservoirs for the PPR virus. Socioeconomic practices that facilitate animal movements between these areas further complicate disease control efforts. Our results advocate for enhanced biosecurity measures and prerestocking evaluations to mitigate the risks associated with the introduction of infected animals.

Furthermore, evidence from previous statistical seroprevalence studies [6], [2] suggests the circulation of Peste des Petits Ruminants (PPR) in the region. Despite differences in methodological techniques, both investigations support our findings on disease epidemiology, associated risk factors, and prevention strategies. Our mathematical modeling approach improves this understanding by predicting disease trends and simulating the intricate interaction of vaccination efforts and risk factors such as restocking. This comprehensive paradigm helps to provide an expanded perspective of PPR transmission and control within the current epidemiological picture capable of keeping the outbreak under control.

Despite these findings, our study has limitations, notably in terms of the availability of relevant data. Future studies should include a broader spectrum of risk factors that influence PPR dynamics, as well as strong data sets for more extensive modeling. Furthermore, investigating optimal control problems using different intervention strategies will be critical for efficient disease management.

In conclusion, effective strategies are critical for achieving herd immunity and strengthening PPR control measures in the region. By implementing strong vaccination programs, completing rigorous pre-restocking evaluations, and improving biosecurity measures, we can considerably limit the impact of restocking on PPR dynamics. Future research should investigate a larger range of risk factors and effective control measures to further eradicate PPR and ensure the health of small ruminant populations in the region and at the national level.
