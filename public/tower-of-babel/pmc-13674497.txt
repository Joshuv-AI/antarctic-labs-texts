A caprine mammary infection model for highly pathogenic avian influenza H5N1
Authors: Lauren Aubrey, Nathalie Berube, Natalia Pessoa, Cynthia Swan, M. Afzal Javed, Claire Bertens, Erin Scruten, Rob Stevens, Carla Norleen, Kyla Cutts, Yanyun Huang, Yohannes Berhane, Bryce Warner, Antonio Facciuolo, Yan Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) H5N1 virus spillover into dairy cows represents the emergence of a mammalian host capable of sustaining a panzootic and creates a transmission pathway to humans. HPAI research involving dairy cows is constrained by the space requirements needed to house large animals in high biocontainment. A small ruminant model would enable larger sample sizes while reducing costs, improving safety, and lowering logistical barriers to conduct research. Here, we establish dairy goats as a model for HPAI mammary infection. Intramammary inoculation with HPAI H5N1 genotype D1.1 and genotype B3.13 resulted in acute mastitis, decreased milk yield, and high virus shedding in milk. Parenteral vaccination with an inactivated virus induced antigen-specific antibodies in blood and milk, reduced clinical disease, limited virus shedding in milk, and mitigated milk production losses. HPAI H5N1 disease dynamics in dairy cows can be recapitulated in dairy goats, providing a relevant model for investigating pathogenesis and for supporting the development of control strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) H5Nx clade 2.3.4.4b has spread globally since 2020, resulting in extensive mortality in wild birds and commercial poultry (1–3). During its circulation, this clade has undergone extensive reassortment and expanded into an unusually broad host range, including many mammalian species (2, 4, 5). In 2024, HPAI H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b genotype B3.13 was detected in dairy cows in the USA, introduced through a single transmission event from wild birds, followed by widespread cow-to-cow transmission within dairy herds in the USA (6). Historically, cattle were not affected by influenza A virus as evidenced by the rare occurrence of natural infections (7, 8). Since the initial HPAI H5N1 detection, three separate introductions to dairy cattle in the USA have been detected, all of clade 2.3.4.4b genotype D1.1, the dominant genotype in North American flyways (9, 10). Genotype B3.13 emerged in North America through reassortment between Eurasian 2.3.4.4.b lineage A1 viruses and circulating low pathogenic avian influenza (LPAI), incorporating four segments from lineage A1 (PA, HA, NA, and M) and four segments from LPAI. Similarly, genotype D1.1 comprises four 2.3.4.4b A3 lineage segments (HA, PB1, M, and NS) and four from North American LPAI (11). To date, H5N1 has been confirmed in 1088 dairy cattle herds in 19 US states (12). Nearly all of these detections have been associated with genotype B3.13, whereas the genotype D1.1 spillovers have been reported in a small number of herds and were quickly contained (10). Experimental studies show that the virus primarily replicates in the bovine mammary gland, leading to acute mastitis and shedding of infectious virus in milk (13, 14). HPAI H5N1 outbreaks in dairy herds have major economic impacts for producers due to decreased milk yield that can persist for up to 2 months postinfection, increased culling due to reproductive issues and losses in productivity, and herd-replacement costs (14). HPAI H5N1 was likely introduced to other US dairy herds due to transport of infected cattle (6). Cow-to-cow transmission is thought to occur through the use of contaminated milking equipment, although this has not been recapitulated under experimental settings and additional transmission routes could be involved (6, 15). HPAI H5N1–infected dairy cows have been responsible for further transmission to nearby commercial poultry flocks, barn cats, humans working in close proximity to infected cows, and wildlife such as raccoons and birds, demonstrating that dairy cows can both sustain a panzootic and act as a new transmission pathway for HPAI H5N1 to humans (6, 13). In humans, previous HPAI H5N1 infections acquired from wild birds have been associated with high mortality rates; however, the 41 reported human cases linked to cattle exposure all resulted in mild respiratory-like illness and conjunctivitis (16–18). This increased circulation of HPAI in mammals always raises concerns of mammalian adaptations or reassortment with seasonal influenza viruses that can increase fitness and enable transmission in humans, potentially leading to a serious public health threat (19).

We previously showed that experimental H5N1 infection of dairy cows induced natural immunity, which included virus-specific antibodies in milk that neutralized high titers of H5N1 and conferred protection against reinfection (20). Natural immunity in dairy cows persists for at least 1 year, as evidenced by protection against homologous experimental challenge, and is associated with neutralizing antibodies in milk (21, 22). These observations conceptually support that vaccines can be developed that elicit durable and protective immune responses in the udder (23).

Conducting HPAI research in cows requires high biocontainment agricultural facilities, and the physical space requirements for these large ruminants exclude many laboratories from performing HPAI studies in the natural host. Goats are a small ruminant that are susceptible to HPAI and have mammary gland architecture comparable to dairy cows, therefore representing a potential surrogate model (24–27). In both species, immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) antibodies are transferred transepithelially from the blood to the milk and peripherally derived lymphocytes traffic to the mammary gland (28, 29). This supports that parenteral vaccination can generate immune responses that protect the mammary gland. In two separate studies, parenteral vaccination of dairy cows with either an mRNA, DNA, or inactivated virus vaccine induced serum and milk neutralizing antibodies, and no infectious virus was detected in milk following homologous intramammary challenge (30, 31). However, these studies did not report whether vaccination protected cows from milk production losses after challenge or measure viral RNA to confirm the absence of virus in milk. Further refinement of H5N1 vaccine strategies will benefit from a small ruminant surrogate model to support larger sample sizes and provides advantages in animal handling, costs, and safety when working with this high consequence pathogen.

Here, we establish dairy goats as a relevant model for HPAI H5N1 mammary infection, bridging a major research gap between large ruminant animals and conventional laboratory models (i.e., mice and ferrets), which differ substantially in their mammary gland architecture. This model provides a biologically relevant species to interrogate existing and emerging H5Nx virus pathogenesis, host immune responses, and countermeasures.

RESULTS

Clinical observations following inoculation with H5N1 genotype D1.1

Three lactating goats were inoculated with 104 median tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) of H5N1 genotype D1.1 virus in the right mammary gland through the teat canal; the left gland was untreated (Fig. 1A). Goat #2 was euthanized at 4 days postinoculation (DPI) [day 4 of study (D4)] to examine disease pathology, viral tissue distribution, and viral load. Goats #1 and #3 had elevated rectal temperatures at 2 DPI (D2) that returned to baseline by 5 DPI (D5), whereas rectal temperatures in goat #2 remained unchanged (Fig. 1B). Goats did not show any behavioral changes or clinical signs of respiratory distress after inoculation. Milk yield decreased by 50 to 70% in all three goats by 3 DPI (D3). Milk yield in goat #2 recovered completely by 4 DPI (D4). The other two goats rebounded to 80 to 100%, relative to day 0, by 2 to 3 weeks postinoculation (Fig. 1C). At 3 DPI (D3), right gland milk from goats #1 and #3 was discolored; no other clinical signs of mastitis nor changes in milk quality were noted throughout the remainder of the study (Fig. 1D and fig. S1).

Viral tissue distribution and histopathology following inoculation with H5N1 genotype D1.1

Goat #2 was euthanized at 4 DPI (D4) to determine viral tissue distribution. Viral RNA was greatest in the right mammary gland (Ct = 17.7, 1.6 × 103 TCID50 equivalent/mg tissue) and notably lower in all other organs tested (Ct > 26; table S1).

Histological examination of goat #2 revealed left and right teat cisterns with intact epithelium without substantial lesions (Fig. 1E). The left mammary gland demonstrated normal alveolar architecture consistent with unremarkable histology. The right mammary gland exhibited multifocal neutrophilic infiltration within the stroma but predominantly in the alveolar lumen, accompanied by necrotic cellular debris in the lumen and attenuation of luminal epithelial cells with vacuolation. On the sections examined, 20% of the areas were affected. At 19 DPI (D19), goats #1 and #3 were euthanized and histologically showed no active cellular damage in any of the mammary gland sections examined. Mild changes indicative of healing were noted in the right mammary gland and squamous metaplasia of the right teat cistern epithelium. Viral RNA was detected in the left mammary gland, spleen, kidney, and lung of goat #1 with Ct values of >32 (table S2). Viral RNA was not detected in goat #3 except in the right mammary gland (Ct = 26.0, 1.8 × 102 TCID50 equivalent/mg).

Virus shedding in milk and antibody response following inoculation with H5N1 genotype D1.1

Milk was collected at regular intervals to measure viral RNA and infectious virus titers. Viral RNA was detected in the milk from the inoculated right gland starting at 1 DPI (D1), peaking at 2 DPI (D2) (106 to 107 TCID50 equivalent/ml) and steadily declined to 2 × 102 TCID50 equivalent/ml by the end of the study (19 DPI) (D19) (Fig. 2A). In the left gland milk, viral RNA was not detected above the predetermined limit of detection (20). In the right gland milk, infectious virus was recovered starting at 2 DPI (D2) (107 to 108 TCID50/ml), with titers declining each day and reaching undetectable levels by 11 DPI (D11) (Fig. 2B). No infectious virus was recovered from the left gland milk. No viral RNA was detected in nasal swabs, oral swabs, feces or blood following inoculation (Ct > 35.7) (table S3).

Together, these data show that H5N1 genotype D1.1 infection remained primarily localized to the inoculated gland and stimulated systemic and local antibody responses.

Viral tissue distribution and histopathology following genotype B3.13 inoculation

Goat #4 received neither formulated vaccine nor adjuvant and was inoculated with 104 TCID50 of genotype B3.13 virus in the right gland via the teat canal. This goat was euthanized at 5 DPI (D5) to examine disease pathology and viral tissue distribution (Fig. 3A). Goat #4 was pyrexic and showed signs of mastitis starting at 2 DPI (D2) (Fig. 3B). Viral RNA was detected in all tissues tested (Ct = 8.8 to 29.8; Fig. 3C). RNA levels were the highest in the uninoculated left (Ct = 8.8, 4.0 × 106 TCID50/mg equivalent) and inoculated right mammary glands (Ct = 12.4, 4.6 × 105 TCID50/mg equivalent). Moderate RNA levels were detected in the supramammary lymph node, mediastinal lymph node, liver, and lung (Ct values of 19.5 to 20.5; 2 × 103 to 6 × 103 TCID50 equivalent). Viral RNA was also detected in mesenteric lymph node (Ct = 23.5, 1.91 × 10 TCID50/mg equivalent), spleen (Ct = 23.3, 2.17 × 10 TCID50/mg equivalent), kidney (Ct = 25.3, 6.5 TCID50/mg equivalent), and bladder (Ct = 28.4, 1.01 TCID50/mg equivalent). In the right gland milk, viral RNA was detected starting at 1 DPI (D1), peaking at 4.2 × 107 TCID50 equivalents at 3 DPI (D3) (Fig. 3D). In the left gland milk, viral RNA levels were low up until 3 DPI (D3) but increased to 3 × 105 TCID50/ml equivalent at 5 DPI (D5) (Fig. 3D). Goat #4 had similar levels of infectious virus in right gland milk (109 TCID50/ml) at 2 DPI (D2) compared with the ADJ goats (Figs. 3E and 5F). Responses to vaccination with inactivated virus vaccine

Dairy goats received either two doses (D0 and D21) of the inactivated vaccine (VACC group, n = 3) or adjuvant alone (ADJ group, n = 3) (Fig. 4A). Milk yield was measured daily. There were no significant differences in daily milk yields between ADJ goats and VACC groups following either the first or second dose (Fig. 4B). VN antibodies were detected in serum of the VACC goats following the booster dose (mean titer of 40) (Fig. 4C) but were not detected in milk of either ADJ or VACC goats before challenge (Fig. 4, D and E). A competitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA; IDEXX Influenza A) was used to measure NP-specific antibodies and revealed that VACC goats seroconverted after the second dose (Fig. 4F). Inactivated vaccine did not generate detectable NP-specific antibodies in milk before challenge (Fig. 4, G and H). We measured genotype B3.13 H5-specific IgG antibodies in serum, revealing VACC goats had mean titers of 200 and 946 after the first and second doses, respectively (Fig. 4I). In milk, H5-specific IgG antibodies were detected in all VACC goats after the second vaccine dose (mean titer of 389 and 305, in left and right gland milk, respectively) (Fig. 4, J and K). These data demonstrate that parenteral vaccination induced detectable levels of VN, anti-NP, and anti-H5 antibodies in serum, in addition to anti-H5 IgG in milk before challenge.

Clinical observations, virus shedding, and antibody response following challenge with H5N1 genotype B3.13

At D35 post–primary vaccination, all goats were challenged by inoculating with 104 TCID50 of genotype B3.13 virus in the right gland via the teat canal. At 2 days postchallenge (DPC; D37), only one ADJ goat showed signs of depression; none of the goats showed clinical signs of respiratory illness. The ADJ goats were pyrexic at 2 DPC (D37) with rectal temperatures returning to normal by 7 DPC (D42); rectal temperatures for VACC goats remained normal throughout the study (Fig. 5A). Between 2 to 3 DPC (D37 to D38), all ADJ goats developed clinical mastitis, resolving by 8 DPC (D43), whereas VACC goats did not develop mastitis following challenge (Fig. 5B). In the ADJ goats, right gland milk was abnormal in color and consistency (fig. S2). Two of the ADJ goats ceased milk production in the right gland by 11 DPC (D46); for the other ADJ goat, color and consistency returned to normal by 13 DPC (D48) in the right gland milk. Left gland milk in ADJ goats was normal in color and consistency throughout, as was the milk from both left and right glands of VACC goats (fig. S2). At 0 DPC (D35), the milk yield between ADJ and VACC goats was not different (Fig. 5C). After challenge, the mean milk yield per day in the ADJ goats was 0.26 kg/day, which was significantly (P < 0.05) lower than before challenge, with a maximum reduction of 84% at 4 DPC (D39) relative to 0 DPC (D35) (Figs. 4B and 5D) (P < 0.05). In the VACC goats, the mean milk yield per day postchallenge was 0.55 kg/day, which was not significantly (P > 0.05) different when compared with that at 0 DPC (D35), with a maximum drop in milk yield of 50% at 6 DPC (D41) (Figs. 4B and 5D). Together, these data show that vaccination partially protected goats from clinical signs of acute mastitis and milk production losses.

Following challenge, viral RNA was detected in the right gland milk of both VACC and ADJ goats starting at 1 DPC (D36) (Fig 5E). RNA levels were higher in the ADJ goats, peaking at a mean of 4.5 × 107 TCID50/ml equivalent at 2 DPC (D37), then gradually declined until 19 DPC (D54), where levels remained between 10 and 103 TCID50/ml equivalents for the rest of the study (Fig. 5E). Given that two of the three ADJ goats ceased milk production in the right gland by 11 DPC (D46), measurements with right gland milk were based on one ADJ goat for the remainder of the study. The VACC group showed lower RNA levels, peaking a mean titer of 6.3 × 105 TCID50/ml equivalent on 3 DPC (D38) in the right gland milk and similarly decreased to 102 TCID50/ml equivalent by 17 DPC (D52). Viral RNA in the left glands remained consistently below or near the limit of detection throughout the study (Fig. 5E). Infectious virus titers were consistent with the viral RNA levels in ADJ goats, peaking at 6.8 × 107 TCID50/ml at 2 DPC (D37) and declining to undetectable levels by 11 DPC (D46) (Fig. 5F). In contrast, VACC goats had no detectable infectious virus in right gland milk until 3 DPC (D38), at which time the mean titer was 8.8 × 103 TCID50/ml (viral RNA mean titer of 6 × 105 TCID50/ml). VACC goats also shed infectious virus in right gland milk at 5 DPC (D40) (102 TCID50/ml), but, by 7 DPC (D41), no infectious virus was recovered. At all time points, left gland milk of both ADJ and VACC goats was negative for infectious virus (Fig. 5F). This indicates that vaccine-induced immunity partially protected against viral replication and shedding of infectious virus in milk.

Goats were euthanized at 38 DPC (D73) to examine disease pathology. Histologically, the left teat cisterns of ADJ goats were generally unremarkable; in contrast, squamous metaplasia was commonly observed in the right teat cistern affecting ∼90% of the examined epithelial surface (Fig. 6). In VACC goats, the right teat cisterns were generally unremarkable, with left teat cisterns occasionally demonstrating squamous metaplasia affecting more than 50% of the examined epithelial surface (Fig. 6). Both ADJ and VACC goat left mammary glands appeared normal. The right mammary gland of VACC goats was generally unremarkable with less than 10% of the examined tissue showing multifocal lymphocytic infiltrates. By contrast, ADJ goats showed diffuse alveolar collapse, and multifocal lymphoid aggregates involving upward of 30% of the examined tissues.

Oral swab samples and blood were largely negative by reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), with only intermittent high-Ct values (Ct > 34.5). Nasal swab samples showed intermittent low-level viral RNA from 3 to 19 DPC (D38 to D54), with the lowest Ct values at 7 DPC (D41) in three goats (two ADJ and one VACC; Ct < 32) (table S4).

Serum VN antibody titers increased in VACC goats after challenge, from a prechallenge mean titer of 40 to a mean titer of 640 at D63. Serum VN antibody titers in ADJ goats were lower than the VACC goats at D63 (mean titer of 80) (Fig. 4C). All three VACC goats had VN antibody titers in the left gland milk (mean of 40), whereas one of the three ADJ goats had a detectable VN antibody titer (10) after challenge, indicating vaccination enhanced antibody titers in milk from the unchallenged gland (Fig. 4D). In right gland milk, mean titers in the ADJ goats (40) were similar to VACC goats (53) (Fig. 4E). NP-specific antibodies were detected in both serum and milk of VACC and ADJ goats following challenge (Fig. 4, F to H). H5-specific IgG titers were similar after challenge in the serum of VACC (mean titer of 10,785) and ADJ (mean titer of 5172) goats (Fig. 4I). In the left gland milk, mean H5-specific IgG titers increased to a mean of 1385 in the ADJ goats and 1286 in the VACC goats (Fig. 4J). In right gland milk, VACC goats had similar titers to the ADJ goat (mean titer of 4300 and 7600, respectively) (Fig. 4K). Infection alone induced VN, anti-NP, and anti-H5 antibodies in the serum and milk from both glands to similar levels as observed in VACC goats.

Secondary challenge with genotype B3.13

To address whether infection can generate protective immunity against homologous virus as we first reported in dairy cows (20), ADJ and VACC goats were rechallenged with genotype B3.13 virus at 28 DPC (D63) in the uninoculated left mammary gland. There was no change in rectal temperature, no signs of clinical mastitis, or changes in milk quality or yield following secondary challenge in either VACC or ADJ.

Ten days post–secondary challenge (D73), goats were euthanized, and tissues were collected. Low levels of viral RNA were detected in the left mammary gland, right mammary gland, supramammary lymph node, mesenteric lymph node, liver, spleen, kidney, ileum, and lung of both VACC and ADJ goats (Ct > 29.1) (table S5).

After secondary challenge, left gland milk VN antibody titers were comparable between VACC and ADJ goats (Fig. 4D). In right gland milk, VN antibody titers were similarly comparable between VACC and the one ADJ goat that continued producing milk (Fig. 4E). Together, these data show that infection-induced immunity in goats confers complete protection against reinfection as noted in dairy cows.

DISCUSSION

HPAI H5N1 has emerged as a novel mammary infection in dairy cows. The physical size of these animals is a major barrier for many laboratories to conduct HPAI research. This proof-of-concept study demonstrates that dairy goats represent a relevant model for HPAI H5N1 mammary infection, given their susceptibility to both D1.1 and B3.13 genotypes and their recapitulation of clinical and virological features similar to those observed in dairy cows (13, 19, 20). Field reports indicate that genotype D1.1 infection in dairy cows causes milder or subclinical disease compared with genotype B3.13 (32). Our preliminary observations suggest that this might be similar in dairy goats. Genotype D1.1 infection resulted in less severe mastitis, a smaller impact on milk yield, no observable changes in milk quality, and a more pronounced rebound in milk production (Fig. 1). The genotype D1.1 virus used in this study was isolated from Canada goose and has avian associated PB2 signatures 627E and 701D but lacks the mammalian-adaptive PB2 M631L substitution that is present in the genotype B3.13 bovine H5N1 isolate. Despite these genetic differences, viral shedding in milk was comparable between the genotypes, indicating that disease severity was influenced by factors beyond viral replication. This likely includes differences in other viral proteins that are known to contribute to pathogenicity, such as NS1, PB1-F2, and PA-X (33–35). Viremia was not evident for either genotype, although low levels of viral RNA were sporadically detected in blood. Genotype D1.1 remained localized to the inoculated right mammary gland with the one goat sacrificed at 4 DPI (D4), revealing that there was minimal spread to extramammary tissues and lymph nodes; by contrast, genotype B3.13 infection was associated with high viral RNA levels in multiple extramammary tissues in the one goat sacrificed at 5 DPC (D5) (table S1 and Fig. 3C). These observations are based on one animal per genotype at a single time point, warranting further validation to determine whether dissemination dynamics is a unique feature for these genotypes. Generally, dissemination of the virus to uninoculated mammary glands is rare in experimental intramammary H5N1 infection of cows, which is similarly observed in all goats, with exception of one goat in which genotype B3.13 infection was detected in both the inoculated and uninoculated mammary gland (Fig. 3, D and E) (19–21). Although the exact cause is unclear, possible explanations include exposure during animal handling, contamination during milking, or virus dissemination within the goat. This model facilitates HPAI H5N1 pathogenesis research in the mammary gland and offers a platform to investigate emerging and existing HPAI H5Nx strains of both avian and mammalian origin.

Parenteral administration of the inactivated virus formulated with Montanide ISA 201 adjuvant had no negative effect on milk production in dairy goats. Overall, the vaccine conferred partial protection by reducing the severity of clinical mastitis and significantly limiting milk production losses following challenge (Fig. 5, A, B, and D). Immunization reduced the onset, duration, and amount of infectious virus shedding in milk when compared with the adjuvant alone group (Fig. 5F). We noted a discordance in infectious virus titers and viral RNA in milk of VACC goats, but not ADJ goats (Fig. 5, E and F). Viral titers converted from viral RNA levels were greater than infectious virus recovered in the VACC goats (Fig. 5, E and F), suggesting that H5N1 was replicating in the tissue, but either was subsequently bound by neutralizing antibodies in milk or that immune-mediated clearance of infected cells resulted in shedding of viral debris, including nucleic acids, into milk. Immunization induced VN and NP-specific antibodies as well as H5-specific IgG antibodies, although VN and NP-specific antibodies were undetectable in milk before challenge (Fig. 4). After challenge, VN titers were similar in right and left gland milk of VACC goats, whereas ADJ goats had lower titers in the uninoculated left gland milk compared with right gland milk (Fig. 4, D and E). Together, these data indicate that vaccination was safe and primed immune responses that mitigated clinical disease but did not confer sterilizing immunity. Our vaccine virus contained the hemagglutinin (HA; H5) and neuraminidase (NA; N1) proteins from the genotype B3.13 virus, but the internal protein-encoding genes were derived from the PR8 strain, which supports high yields of the vaccine virus in embryonated eggs. As immunodominant T cell epitopes are associated with internal influenza proteins, this mismatch with the genotype B3.13 strain might have limited T cell immunity and could be one reason why vaccination did not achieve complete protective immunity (36). Vaccine delivery route, together with dose and adjuvant optimization, requires further evaluation to determine which factors limited protective immunity in the udder. The dairy goat model established herein provides a platform for HPAI H5Nx vaccine development, screening, and assessing protective efficacy. In addition to improving vaccine design, consideration of cross-protection is necessary given the circulation of the two different H5N1 genotypes in cows. Natural immunity provides limited cross-protection in dairy cows, thus multivalent vaccine approaches are likely needed (21, 22). We propose that the dairy goat model can also be used to evaluate multivalent vaccine approaches given their susceptibility to both genotypes. The smaller stature of dairy goats, relative to dairy cows, allows for greater occupancy within high biocontainment agricultural rooms enabling researchers to use larger group sizes needed for robust group-level comparisons.

Inactivated, DNA and mRNA H5N1 vaccines have previously been tested in cows. These vaccines prevented shedding of infectious virus in milk, although, in the absence of viral RNA detection, it is unclear whether sterilizing immunity was achieved (30, 31). Our data emphasize the importance of measuring both infectious virus and viral RNA to provide a complete picture of replication dynamics.

In this study, infection-induced immunity in ADJ goats conferred protection against reinfection, consistent with our observations in dairy cows (20). Reexposing ADJ goats to H5N1 by inoculating into the left mammary gland did not result in clinical mastitis, milk production losses, or viral shedding; neither viral RNA nor infectious virus was detected. Before reexposing ADJ goats to H5N1, VN antibodies were not readily detected in left gland milk, yet all goats were protected from reinfection. However, anti-H5 IgG antibodies were detected in left gland milk of all ADJ goats, confirming the presence of antigen-specific antibodies in milk before rechallenge. This suggests that even low levels of antibodies correlate with protective immunity, similar to our observations in dairy cows, where low milk VN titers of 1:10 were associated with protection (20). Cell-mediated immunity in the udder likely plays an important role in immune protection, but to what extent remains to be determined. Defining the contribution of T cell–mediated responses to H5N1 infection is warranted to provide a complete understanding of host immunity.

The emergence of HPAI H5N1 virus in dairy cows reveals a new mammalian host and tissue tropism that can trigger an avian influenza panzootic and introduces a novel pathway for zoonotic transmission to humans. This ongoing pandemic threat underscores the need for preparedness strategies that block cross-species transmission. Relevant H5N1 mammary infection models, such as the goat model, will become critically important for addressing key knowledge gaps and supporting the development of effective countermeasures to combat this public health threat.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cells and viruses

Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells [American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), no. CRL-2936] were maintained in minimal essential medium (MEM) (Gibco, 11095080) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 16000-044). Human embryonic kidney–293T (HEK-293T) cells (ATCC, no. CRL-3216) were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco, 11965092) supplemented with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 16000-044). Both cell types were incubated at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator.

The HPAI H5N1 genotype D1.1 isolate A/Canada Goose/ON/2025, referred to here as genotype D1.1 virus, was obtained from National Centre for Foreign Animal Disease, Canada. The HPAI H5N1 B3.13 genotype isolate A/dairy cattle/Texas/24-008749-002/2024, referred to here as genotype B3.13 virus, was obtained from the US Department of Agriculture. Both viruses were propagated at a multiplicity of infection of 0.001 on MDCK cells until complete cytopathic effects (CPEs) were observed. Viral growth medium consisted of MEM supplemented with 0.2% bovine serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich, A7030) with l-[(toluene-4-sulphonamido)-2-phenyl] ethyl chloromethyl ketone (1 μg/ml)–trypsin.

Generation of vaccine virus by reverse genetics

The sequences encoding HA and NA genes from the B3.13 H5N1 virus were cloned individually into pHW2000 plasmids. The multiple basic amino acids in the HA protein cleavage site were removed. The HA and NA plasmids, together with the six plasmids representing the rest of the gene segments from A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (PR8), were cotransfected into HEK-293T and MDCK cells to rescue the PR8 + B3.13 (6 + 2) virus (vaccine virus) using reverse genetics (37). Three days posttransfection, supernatant was harvested and used to inoculate 11-day-old embryonated chicken eggs, obtained from the University of Saskatchewan Poultry Teaching and Research Unit. At 3 DPI, allantoic fluid was harvested and inactivated with β-propiolactone at a final concentration of 0.1%, using our validated protocol (38). The inactivated virus was diluted in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Cytiva, SH30256.01) and prepared as a W/O/W vaccine emulsion using, MONTANIDE ISA 201 VG adjuvant (Seppic, Air Liquide Healthcare) following the manufacturer’s directions. One vaccine dose consisted of 1-ml total volume [55% adjuvant:45% antigen in PBS (v/v)] containing 1 × 106 TCID50.

Biosafety and ethics statement

All work involving infectious HPAI H5N1 virus was performed in containment level 3 (CL3) laboratories and CL3 large animal facilities at the Vaccine and Infectious Disease Organization (VIDO). Work was completed in compliance with the Canadian Biosafety Standard, third edition, under the authority of the Public Health Agency of Canada and the Canadian Food Inspection Agency. All animal experiments were completed at the University of Saskatchewan following guidelines provided by the Canadian Council on Animal Care and approved by the University Animal Care Committee (protocol no. 20240042).

Animals

Ten Alpine dairy goats were sourced from a farm in Saskatchewan, Canada, and housed at the CL3 large animal facility at VIDO. Goats were fed an ad libitum diet consisting of alfalfa timothy hay cube pelleted ration (dairy ration, 16% protein) and water. Goats, milked once daily, were brought onto a milking stanchion (Sydell Inc.), and visible debris was removed from each teat using a WypAll general-purpose wipe saturated with an iodine-based disinfectant. Teats were manually stripped two to three times to allow milk let-down, followed by an additional wipe with the iodine disinfectant before sample collection. Milk from left and right glands was then manually stripped into individual specimen cups for sampling followed by the use of a portable milking machine to complete milk collection. The collection vessel containing the milk was weighed, and milk weights for each goat were recorded. One milking machine was dedicated to the vaccinated goat cohort and the other to the unvaccinated cohort. Individual claws on the machine were paired with designated teats (i.e., left and right) so that the claw was used on the same teat at every milking. After each daily milking, the machines were disinfected with hot water, detergent then sanitizer (12% sodium hypochlorite diluted to 200 parts per million). Gloves worn by personnel were disinfected with Prevail after handling the inoculated gland.

Study design: Genotype D1.1 virus inoculation

Three goats were acclimated for 7 days before viral challenge. Age and parity of all goats can be found in table S6. All three goats were inoculated in the right mammary gland with 104 TCID50 of HPAI H5N1 genotype D1.1 in MEM medium (Fig. 1A). The virus inoculum was back titrated to confirm dose. After milking, 1 ml of the viral inoculum was drawn into a sterile 3-ml syringe fitted with a sterile teat cannula. The teat orifice was disinfected using isopropyl alcohol wipes before infusion. The teat cannula was inserted its full length before depositing the solution into the teat canal. Firm pressure was applied to the base of the teat while the cannula was withdrawn and the teat was massaged upward to facilitate movement of the inoculum into the teat cistern. Before the study was initiated, one goat was preselected to be euthanized at 4 DPI (D4) during the period of peak viral shedding in milk, which had previously been identified in dairy cows (20). The remaining two goats were euthanized at 19 DPI (D19) by intravenous injection with Dorminal (20 ml per 45 kg of body weight; Rafter 8 Products Inc.) after resolution of infectious virus shedding in milk.

Study design: Vaccination, B3.13 virus challenge, and rechallenge

Seven goats were acclimated for 7 days. At D0 (prime) and D21 (boost), three goats were vaccinated subcutaneously in the neck with inactivated vaccine virus formulated with Montanide ISA 201 VG adjuvant (VACC group, n = 3), or with adjuvant alone (ADJ group, n = 3) (Fig. 4A). Groups were balanced in terms of age and parity of goats (table S6). One goat (goat #4) received neither vaccine nor adjuvant. On D35, all seven goats were challenged with 104 TCID50 of genotype B3.13 virus in the right mammary gland, as described in the “Study design: Genotype D1.1 virus inoculation” section. Five DPC, at study day 40, goat #4 was euthanized for tissue collection during the period of peak viral shedding in milk. At 28 DPC (D63), all remaining goats were rechallenged with 104 TCID50 of genotype B3.13 virus in the left mammary gland, as described above. The virus inoculum was back titrated to confirm dose. Goats were monitored for 10 days post–secondary challenge and were euthanized at D73, as described in the “Study design: Genotype D1.1 virus inoculation” section. Ten days was chosen for euthanasia after secondary challenge as it corresponds to the period during which infectious viral shedding was observed in cattle after inoculation with this B3.13 genotype strain, ensuring that any shedding following rechallenge would be captured.

Sample collection

Goats were monitored daily by veterinarians or veterinary staff. Rectal temperatures were measured, and goats were clinically scored for respiration, depression, and mastitis (table S7). Foremilk was manually stripped from each gland before milking at regular intervals postinoculation for the quantification of infectious virus and viral RNA. Milk from both glands of each goat was collected together and weighed, and daily milk yield was recorded. Blood, feces, nasal swabs, and oral swabs were collected at regular intervals for quantification of viral RNA, as well as serum for measurement of antibody titers. As feces were completely negative for viral RNA during the genotype D1.1 inoculation study (see the “Study design: Genotype D1.1 virus inoculation” section), feces were not collected in the B3.13 study (see the “Study design: Genotype D1.1 virus inoculation” section).

Viral load titration by TCID50 assay

MDCK cells (2.5 × 104 cells per well) were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator overnight. The following day, whole milk was serially diluted tenfold in MEM and added to the plate in triplicate. The plate was incubated for 1 hour at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator, and, then, the inoculum was replaced with viral growth medium supplemented with antibiotic-antimycotic (Gibco, 15240062). CPE was documented at 48 hours postinfection (hpi), and the TCID50 titer was calculated using the Spearman-Kärber algorithm.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Milk and blood were diluted 1:4 in PBS. Nasal swabs and oral swabs were placed into 1 ml of MEM with antibiotic-antimycotic upon collection. Swabs were vortexed for 30 s and centrifuged at 1600g for 10 min at 4°C, and, then, the supernatant was collected. Each fluid (140 μl) was then added to 560 μl of Buffer AVL, and, then, RNA was extracted using the QIAamp Viral RNA Kit as per the manufacturer’s directions (QIAGEN, 52904).

Tissue was placed in RNAlater (Thermo Fisher Scientific, AM7020) upon collection. Tissue or feces were each homogenized in 600 μl of Buffer RLT (QIAGEN, 79216) using a TissueLyser II, set to 30 Hz for 5 min, and then centrifuged at 5000g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected, and a volume corresponding to 30 mg of starting tissue or feces was transferred to fresh buffer RLT to a total of 600 μl. RNA was extracted using the QIAGEN RNeasy Mini Kit as directed by the manufacturer (QIAGEN, 74104).

Quantification of viral RNA was performed by RT-qPCR using the Luna Universal One-Step RT-qPCR Kit (New England Biolabs, E3005). Reactions were prepared in duplicate with 5 μl of RNA template and a primer probe set targeting the influenza A virus M gene. Forward primer sequence: 5′-GGCCCCCTCAAAGCCGA-3′; reverse primer sequence: 5′-CGTCTACGYTGCAGTCC-3′; probe sequence: 5′ (FAM)-TCACTGGGCACGGTGAGCGT-3′ (MGBNFQ) (IDT) (20). Standard curves were generated by extracting RNA from tissue, blood, or MEM spiked with a known titer of H5N1 virus. Standard curves were generated for each RT-qPCR plate and used to interpolate TCID50 equivalents from Ct values.

Preparation of milk and serum for antibody measurement

Rennet from Mucor miehei (0.15 μg/ml) was added to milk samples and incubated at 37°C for 5 min. Centrifugation of the samples at 1300g for 5 min resulted in three fractions: the top fraction consisting of fat, a soluble whey fraction, and a pellet consisting of curdled milk. The top fat fraction was removed carefully with a pipette, and the soluble fraction was collected. The whey was heat inactivated at 60°C for 30 min before use in virus neutralization assays and ELISAs (39). Serum was heat inactivated at 56°C for 30 min for use in virus neutralization assays and heat-treated at 60°C for 30 min for use in ELISAs.

Virus neutralization assay

MDCK cells (2.5 × 104 cells per well) were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated overnight in a 5% CO2 incubator set to 37°C. The following day, serum or whey samples in quadruplicate were serially diluted twofold in MEM. Samples were mixed with an equal volume of H5N1 virus diluted to 2 × 103 TCID50/ml. Genotype D1.1 virus was used in the virus neutralization assays for the genotype D1.1 inoculation study described in the “Study design: Genotype D1.1 virus inoculation” section, and genotype B3.13 virus was used in virus neutralization assays for the genotype B3.13 vaccination and challenge study described in the “Study design: Genotype D1.1 virus inoculation” section. Medium on the MDCK cells was replaced with 100 μl of whey or serum mixed with virus, totaling 100 TCID50 of virus per well. CPE was recorded at 48 hpi, and the VN antibody titer was defined as the highest serum or whey dilution that prevented CPE in at least two of the four wells.

IDEXX ELISA and HA ELISA

The IDEXX Influenza A Ab test (Influenza A Ab, IDEXX) was conducted per the manufacturer’s guidelines using serum or whey starting at a dilution of 1:10. A sample-to-positive ratio of <0.6 was defined as positive.

Soluble HA from A/dairy cattle/Texas/24-008749-002/2024 (genotype B3.13) was expressed in HEK-293T cells and purified as previously described (40). The HA coding sequence was cloned into episomal expression vector pEB4.3 (accession no. MG182339). The polybasic cleavage site between HA1 and HA2 (RERRK) was replaced with a threonine residue to prevent cleavage during protein expression.

Immulon 2HB 96-well microtiter plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 3655) were coated with HA protein (2 μg/ml) diluted in sodium carbonate buffer (pH 9.6) and incubated overnight at 4°C. Plates were washed with tris-buffered saline (TBS) supplemented with 0.05% v/v Tween 20 (TBS-T) and blocked with 0.1% gelatin from porcine skin (Sigma-Aldrich, G2625) in TBS-T for 1 hour at room temperature (RT). Fourfold serial dilutions of serum (starting at 1:100) or milk (starting at 1:10) in TBS-T and 0.1% gelatin from porcine skin were added to duplicate wells and incubated at RT for 1 hour. For detection of IgG antibodies, Reserve-AP–labeled, rabbit anti-goat IgG (1:10,000 in diluent; KPL catalog no. 5220−0384) was added to each well and incubated for 1 hour at RT. Each well was reacted with 100 μl of p-nitrophenyl phosphate (Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. N3254) diluted in diethanolamine buffer at a concentration of 1 mg/ml. Reactions were allowed to develop until positive samples reached an absorbance of greater than 2.0 measured at 405 nm, with a reference at 490 nm, using a SpectraMax Plus 384 Reader (Molecular Devices). Antibody titers were determined using the reciprocal of the highest dilution that resulted in an absorbance value greater than the means + 3 SDs of the absorbance value from preimmune samples.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Tissue samples were immediately submerged in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 7 days and transferred to fresh 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 24 hours before transfer out of the CL3 laboratory (20). Tissue embedding, sectioning, and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed by Prairie Diagnostic Services and images acquired using an Olympus Virtual Slide Scanning Microscope (Olympus-Life Science) completed by the WCVM Imaging Centre (University of Saskatchewan).

Data presentation and statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 11 was used for data visualization. Images were created using BioRender.com. Daily milk yield data were analyzed both within individual phases (postprime, postboost, and postchallenge) and across phases by averaging daily milk yield per goat within each phase. Generalized linear mixed models (GLIMMIX; SAS v9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) were used for all milk yield analyses. Milk yield was modeled as a repeated measure on goat, applying a first-order autoregressive covariance structure, which yielded the lowest Akaike information criterion corrected (AICc). Fixed effects included treatment (VACC versus ADJ), time (day or phase), and their interaction. Denominator degrees of freedom for tests of fixed effects were adjusted using the Kenward-Roger correction, and a Newton-Raphson optimization algorithm with ridging was requested using the NLOPTIONS statement. Multiple means comparisons were adjusted using the Tukey-Kramer method. Variance attributed to individual goats was captured by the repeated measures term; therefore, the random effect of goat was removed from the model. Normality of studentized residuals was visually assessed to confirm model assumptions. Milk yield recorded on the day before primary vaccination (D0) did not differ among treatments (P = 0.76); however, it was included as a covariate in both models (P < 0.01). Parity was evaluated but excluded as it neither improved model fit according to AICc nor altered interpretation of results. Significance was declared when P ≤ 0.05.
