Stein’s method for Fréchet approximation: a regularly varying functions approach
Paul Mansanarez, Guillaume Poly, Yvik Swan

Abstract
We develop a variant of Stein’s method of comparison of generators to bound the Kolmogorov, total variation, and Wasserstein-1 distances between distributions on the real line. Our discrepancy is expressed in terms of the ratio of reverse hazard rates; it therefore remains tractable even when density derivatives are intractable. Our main application concerns the approximation of normalized extremes by Fréchet laws. In this setting, the new discrepancy provides a quantitative measure of distributional proximity in terms of the average regular variation at infinity of the underlying cumulative distribution function. We illustrate the approach through explicit computations for maxima of Pareto, Cauchy, and Burr XII distributions. Keywords : Stein’s method, Extreme value theory, Regularly varying functions. MSC 2020 subject classifications : 60G70, 26A12, 60F05, 60F99.

Introduction

The
Fréchet law
is a continuous probability measure on the
real line with cumulative distribution function (cdf) and density
(pdf) given by

\Phi_{\alpha}(x)=e^{-x^{-\alpha}}\,\mathbb{I}_{(0,\infty)}(x),\quad\phi_{\alpha}(x)=\alpha x^{-(1+\alpha)}e^{-x^{-\alpha}}\,\mathbb{I}_{(0,\infty)}(x),

where
\alpha>0
(
\mathbb{I}_{A}
is the indicator function of the
set
A
). It is one of the three
generalized extreme value
(GEV) laws
, which are the only possible limits for suitably
normalized maxima of independent and identically distributed real
random variables. The two others are the
Gumbel
and

Weibull
laws.

Convergence of extremes towards a Fréchet (or, more generally,
towards a GEV) is a well-studied problem. We say that a cdf
F

belongs to the
domain of attraction
of
\Phi_{\alpha}
, and
write
F\in\mathrm{DA}(\alpha)
, when there exist real sequences

(a_{n})
and
(b_{n})
such that

F_{n}(x):=\Bigl(F\bigl(a_{n}(x-b_{n})\bigr)\Bigr)^{n}\longrightarrow\Phi_{\alpha}(x)\mbox{ as }n\to\infty.

(1)

The Fréchet domain of attraction is completely determined by the
variation of
F
at infinity:
F\in\mathrm{DA}(\alpha)
if and only
if
F(x)<1
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1-F(tx)}{1-F(t)}=x^{-\alpha}\mbox{
for all }x>0;

(2)

in this case we say
1-F
is regularly varying at infinity with index

-\alpha
, and write
1-F\in\mathrm{RV}_{-\alpha}
. Furthermore one
can take
a_{n}\sim F^{\leftarrow}(1-1/n)
(here
F^{\leftarrow}
is the
generalized inverse, a.k.a. quantile function of
F
) and

b_{n}=0
. See Appendix
A
for definitions and
references as well as an overview of the Karamata theory which
underpins these developments.

The dependence on the sample size in the approximation in
(
1
) has also been studied in the literature. Rates of
convergence in the Kolmogorov distance – denoted

\mathrm{Kol}(F_{n},\Phi_{\alpha})
(see below for a definition) –
and in the total variation distance – denoted

\mathrm{TV}(F_{n},\Phi_{\alpha})
– are relatively easy to obtain,
e.g. from
[
14
]
or
[
12
, Section
2.4]
. In addition, more refined results involving
second-order variation conditions are available in
[

12
,
4

, which not only provide convergence
rates but also identify the limiting constants via certain
non-trivial functionals depending on the underlying distribution

F\in\mathrm{DA}(\alpha)
. However, these results can be
technically demanding to apply (in a forthcoming work

Among the many available methods for obtaining quantitative fixed-
n

Stein’s method
has emerged in recent years as
one of the most effective. While its applicability and effectiveness
have been demonstrated across a wide range of approximation problems

[

, which
applies a version of the Stein–Chen Poisson approximation,

[

[

[

is effective but only in relatively simple settings, as it relies on

, i.e. derivatives of the

limitation is precisely where the present work intervenes.

In this paper, we build on the Stein-operator framework of

[
11
]
to develop a new perspective on the generator
comparison method that avoids derivatives of densities altogether.
Beyond its computational advantages, our approach also enhances
interpretability: our main result connects the distributional proximity
in (
1
) to a quantitative measure of the regular variation at
infinity of the cdf
F
, namely, to a quantitative counterpart
of (
2
).

To state our results we start with some notations and definitions.
First of all, if
F
is a cdf on the real line and
h
is a test
function, we denote

F(h)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}h(x)\,dF(x)

the expectation of
h
under
F
(provided the integral exists and is
finite). Next, given two cdfs
P,Q
, the
integral probability
metrics
from
P
to
Q
are the functionals

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(P,Q\mid\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}|(P-Q)(h)|,

where
\mathcal{H}
is a well-chosen class of test functions. The
Kolmogorov distance corresponds to
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{K}
the
collection of indicators
\mathbb{I}_{(-\infty,z]}
of half lines on

\mathbb{R}
, the total variation distance to

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{T}
the collection of indicators

\mathbb{I}_{B}
of Borel sets on
\mathbb{R}
. We will also consider
the Wasserstein-1 (a.k.a. Kantorovitch-Rubinstein) distance

\mathrm{Wass}(P,Q)
, which is obtained with

\mathcal{H}=\mathrm{Lip}(1)
the set of Lipschitz functions on

\mathbb{R}
with Lipschitz constant 1.

Throughout the paper we will restrict our attention to cdfs
satisfying the following assumption.

Assumption 0:

There exist a starting mass

f_{0}\geq 0
, a left endpoint
c_{F}\in[-\infty,+\infty)
, and a
density-like function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
integrable, such that the cumulative distribution function
F
is
given by

F(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x<c_{F},\\[6.0pt]
f_{0}+\displaystyle\int_{c_{F}}^{x}f(u)\,{\rm d}u,&x\geq c_{F}.\end{cases}

(3)

Moreover,
f
is continuous and strictly positive on

(c_{F},+\infty)
.

Note how Assumption 0 guarantees that the distribution has
no mass below
c_{F}
, may have a point mass of size
f_{0}
at
x=c_{F}
,
and has continuous and strictly positive density
f
on

(c_{F},+\infty)
. In particular, this ensures that
F
is strictly
increasing in its right tail, has no mass at infinity (i.e.,

\lim_{x\to+\infty}F(x)=1
) and also
F(x)<1
for all

x<+\infty
.

Given
F
a cdf satisfying Assumption 0,
we define

r_{F}(x)=\frac{f(x)}{F(x)}\mathbb{I}_{(c_{F},+\infty)}(x)={(\log F)^{\prime}(x)}\mathbb{I}_{(c_{F},+\infty)}(x);

this function is sometimes referred to as the
reverse hazard
rate function
of
F
. Finally, we recall the standard notation
\overline{F}(x)=1-F(x)

the survival function.

Our main abstract results, obtained through a version of Stein’s
method which we shall detail in Section
2
,
read as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
P,Q
be two cdfs satisfying
Assumption 0, with pdfs
p,q
and supports
[c_{P},+\infty)
and

[c_{Q},+\infty)
, respectively. Suppose that
P
has no atom
(i.e.
p_{0}=0
) and that
c_{Q}\geq c_{P}\geq-\infty
. Introduce the

Stein discrepancy

\Delta(Q\mid P):=\int_{c_{Q}}^{+\infty}\left|1-\frac{r_{P}(x)}{r_{Q}(x)}\right|q(x)\,{\rm d}x.

(4)

Then

\mathrm{Kol}(P,Q)\leq\Delta(Q\mid P)+q_{0}\mbox{ and }\mathrm{TV}(P,Q)\leq 2\Delta(Q\mid P)+q_{0}

(5)

with
q_{0}=Q(\left\{c_{Q}\right\})
the starting mass of
Q
.

Theorem 1.2
.

In addition to the assumptions
from Theorem
1.1
, suppose also that
P,Q
have finite
mean, that
c_{P}\geq 0
and that
q_{0}=0
. Set

\Delta_{w}(Q\mid P):=\int_{c_{Q}}^{+\infty}x\left|1-\frac{r_{P}(x)}{r_{Q}(x)}\right|q(x)\,{\rm d}x.

(6)

Then

\mathrm{Wass}(P,Q)\leq 2\mu\Delta(Q\mid P)+3\Delta_{w}(Q\mid P)

(7)

(
\mu
is the mean of
P
).

The bounds in (
5
) and (
7
) can be applied for the
comparison of any two distributions satisfying Assumption 0, but our
purpose is to use them towards the quantitative approximation of the
distribution of a maximum by a Fréchet distribution. The Fréchet
reverse hazard rate is

r_{\Phi_{\alpha}}(x)=\frac{\alpha}{x^{\alpha+1}}\mathbb{I}_{(0,+\infty)}(x).

Now let
F
be a cdf satisfying Assumption 0 and consider
F_{n}
as
defined in equation (
1
), with
b_{n}=0
. Clearly
F_{n}
also
satisfies Assumption 0, with support lower bounded by

c_{F_{n}}=a_{n}^{-1}c_{F}
and reverse hazard rate

r_{F_{n}}(x)=na_{n}r_{F}(a_{n}x).

If
c_{F}\leq 0
, the Fréchet distribution has the smallest support so
we take
Q=\Phi_{\alpha}
and
P=F_{n}
in (
4
) so that

\Delta(\Phi_{\alpha}\mid F_{n})=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left|1-\frac{x^{\alpha+1}}{\alpha}na_{n}{r_{F}(a_{n}x)}\right|\phi_{\alpha}(x)\,{\rm d}x.

(8)

If
c_{F}\geq 0
then the situation is reversed: we take
Q=F_{n}
and

P=\Phi_{\alpha}
in (
4
) and the Stein discrepancy
becomes

\Delta(F_{n}\mid\Phi_{\alpha})=\int_{c_{F}/a_{n}}^{+\infty}\left|1-\frac{\alpha}{x^{\alpha+1}}\frac{1}{na_{n}r_{F}(a_{n}x)}\right|f_{n}(x)\,{\rm d}x.

(9)

The corresponding expressions for (
6
) are easy to deduce
from here.

The Stein discrepancies (
8
) and
(
9
) have a natural interpretation in the
extreme value context. Indeed, in order for the convergence in
(
1
) to occur with
a_{n}=F^{\leftarrow}(1-1/n)
and

b_{n}=0
, classical Karamata theory (see
Appendix
A
for an overview) indicates that
we must have

na_{n}r_{F}(a_{n}x)\approx na_{n}r_{F}(a_{n})x^{-\alpha-1}\approx\alpha x^{-\alpha-1}\mbox{ when }n\mbox{ is large}.

(10)

The discrepancies measure by how far (
10
) holds true, on
average. They can thus be viewed as quantitative proxies of the fact
that
F
belongs to the domain of attraction of
\Phi_{\alpha}
. In
fact, as our next result shows, convergence of our Stein discrepancy
to 0 captures convergence to Fréchet exactly; we provide a proof
in Section
3
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
F\in\mathrm{DA}(\alpha)
satisfy Assumption 0. Let
F_{n}
be
defined through (
1
) with
b_{n}=0
and
(a_{n})_{n}
a sequence
of positive real numbers.

•

If
c_{F}\leq 0
and if
r_{F}
is bounded on finite intervals of

\mathbb{R}_{+}
, then
\Delta(\Phi_{\alpha}\mid F_{n})\to 0
as soon as

(a_{n})_{n}
satisfies
n(1-F(a_{n})){\longrightarrow}1
. If

\alpha>1
then so does
\Delta_{w}(\Phi_{\alpha}\mid F_{n})
.

•

If
c_{F}\geq 0
and if
1/r_{F}
is bounded on finite intervals
of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, then
\Delta(F_{n}\mid\Phi_{\alpha})\to 0
if

a_{n}=F^{\leftarrow}(1-1/n)
. If
\alpha>1
, then so does
\Delta_{w}(F_{n}\mid\Phi_{\alpha})
.

We conclude this introduction with some applications.

Example 1.4

(Maxima of independent Pareto)
.

If

F(x)=(1-x^{-\alpha})\mathbb{I}_{(1,\infty)}(x)
is the Pareto
cdf then
a_{n}=n^{1/\alpha}
and with

F_{n}(x)=(F(n^{1/\alpha}x))^{n}
we can apply
Theorem
1.1
with
Q=F_{n}
and
P=\Phi_{\alpha}
(the
choice is dictated by the supports). Immediate computations yield:

\Delta(F_{n}\mid\Phi_{\alpha})=\frac{1}{n}\int_{n^{-1/\alpha}}^{\infty}x^{-\alpha}f_{n}(x)\,{\rm d}x=\frac{1}{n+1}

which is the correct and known rate of convergence in the Kolmogorov
and total variation distances for this simple problem. We also have,
for
\alpha>1
,

\Delta_{w}(F_{n}\mid\Phi_{\alpha})=\frac{1}{n}\int_{n^{-1/\alpha}}^{\infty}x^{1-\alpha}f_{n}(x)\,{\rm d}x=\frac{n^{-1/\alpha}\Gamma(n+1)}{\Gamma(2+n-1/\alpha)}\Gamma(2-1/\alpha)\approx\frac{\Gamma(2-1/\alpha)}{n}.

Hence the rate of convergence in Wasserstein is also bounded by

O(1/n)
; this is confirmed by numerical evaluations.
Exactly the
same conclusions hold, for instance, in case
F
is the log-logistic
cdf
F(x)=x^{\alpha}/(1+x^{\alpha})\mathbb{I}_{(0,\infty)}(x)
.

Example 1.5

(Comparison of Fréchet)
.

When
P,Q
are both Fréchet distributions, our discrepancies can be computed
explicitly, yielding (if
\beta>\alpha
):

\displaystyle\Delta(\Phi_{\alpha}\mid\Phi_{\beta})

\displaystyle=1-2e^{-\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{-\alpha+\beta}}}-\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\alpha}\right)+\frac{2\beta}{\alpha}\Gamma\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha},\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{-\alpha+\beta}}\right)

\displaystyle\Delta_{w}(\Phi_{\alpha}\mid\Phi_{\beta})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Gamma\left(-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)+\beta\,\Gamma\left(\frac{-1+\beta}{\alpha}\right)+2\alpha\,\Gamma\left(\frac{-1+\alpha}{\alpha},\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{-\alpha+\beta}}\right)-2\beta\,\Gamma\left(\frac{-1+\beta}{\alpha},\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{-\alpha+\beta}}\right)}{\alpha},\mbox{ if }\alpha>1

with
\Gamma(x)
the gamma function and
\Gamma(x,y)
the incomplete
gamma function. A (much nicer and sharper) upper bound on the
Kolmogorov distance between two Fréchet distributions is provided in

[
12
, Lemma 2.11]
; this bound however is not valid
for the total variation or the Wasserstein distance whereas ours is
valid in both these cases (and numerical evaluations indicate that our
bound is even of the correct order).

Example 1.6

(Maxima of independent Cauchy)
.

Let
X_{n}:=\frac{\pi}{n}\max(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
with

Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
i.i.d. standard Cauchy variables with
density
f(x)=({\pi(1+x^{2})})^{-1}.
The Cauchy cdf is in

\mathrm{DA}(1)
, so we consider

\Delta(\Phi_{1}\mid F_{n})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|1-\frac{x^{2}n^{2}}{(\pi^{2}+n^{2}x^{2})\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\pi}\arctan\left(\frac{nx}{\pi}\right)\right)}\right|\frac{e^{-1/x}}{x^{2}}\,dx

to bound the Kolmogorov and total variation distances (the expression

\Delta_{w}
could be dealt with similarly to bound the Wasserstein
distance). After the change of variables
u=({nx})^{-1}
, this
discrepancy can be rewritten

\displaystyle\Delta(\Phi_{1}\mid F_{n})=n\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|1-R(u)\right|e^{-nu}\,du=\mathbb{E}\left[|1-R(E_{n})|\right]

where

E_{n}\sim\mathrm{Exp}(n)
and

R(u):=\frac{1}{(1+\pi^{2}u^{2})\left(1-\frac{1}{\pi}\arctan(\pi u)\right)}.

One checks that
\lim_{u\to 0^{+}}R(u)=1
,
1-R(u)\sim-u
for
u

close to 0,
\lim_{u\to+\infty}R(u)=0
and
R(u)<1
for

u\geq 1
. Thus there exists a unique
z_{0}\in(0,1)
such
that
R(z_{0})=1
(numerical resolution gives

z_{0}\approx 0.109
). The function
R
is smooth, and integration
by parts yields:

n\int_{0}^{+\infty}|1-R(u)|e^{-nu}du=\frac{2}{n}R^{\prime}(z_{0})e^{-nz_{0}}+\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\int_{z_{0}}^{+\infty}R^{\prime\prime}(u)e^{-nu}du-\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{z_{0}}R^{\prime\prime}(u)e^{-nu}du.

Since

\frac{1}{n}\left|\int_{0}^{z_{0}}R^{\prime\prime}(u)e^{-nu}\,{\rm d}u\right|\leq||R^{\prime\prime}||_{{}_{\infty,[0,z_{0}]}}\frac{1}{n^{2}}(1-e^{-nz_{0}})

and

\frac{1}{n}\left|\int_{z_{0}}^{+\infty}R^{\prime\prime}(u)e^{-nu}\,du\right|\leq\frac{e^{-nz_{0}}}{n}\int_{z_{0}}^{+\infty}|R^{\prime\prime}(u)|\,du

it follows that there exists universal constants
C,D
such that

\Delta(\Phi_{1}\mid F_{n})\leq\frac{C}{n}+\frac{D}{n^{2}}.

Numerical estimation provides
C\leq 1.832
and
D\leq 1.965
. Similar
conclusions hold also for
\Delta_{w}(\Phi_{1}\mid F_{n})
, with different
constants.

Example 1.7

(Maxima of independent Burr XII)
.

Let

X_{n}=n^{-1/(\alpha\tau)}\max(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
with

Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}

,

\displaystyle\Delta(\Phi_{\alpha\tau}\mid F_{n})

2
Stein’s method and a proof of Theorems
1.1
and

1.2

Let
P
be a cdf on
\mathbb{R}
satisfying Assumption 0, having no
atoms (i.e.
p_{0}=0
in (
3
)). We follow the blueprint from

[
11
]
to set up “a Stein’s method for
P
”, in three
steps.

Step 1: A Stein operator for

P
.
The
canonical Stein operator
for
P
is the linear
operator

\varphi\mapsto\mathcal{T}_{p}\varphi:={(p\varphi)^{\prime}}/{p}\mathbb{I}_{(c_{P},+\infty)}
acting on the set of functions

\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
p\varphi
is
differentiable on
(c_{P},+\infty)
. The
Stein class
for

\mathcal{T}_{p}
is the collection
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T}_{p})
of functions

\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that (i)
p\varphi
is
differentiable on
(c_{P},+\infty)
, (ii)
(p\varphi)^{\prime}
is integrable
on
(c_{P},+\infty)
, and (iii)

\lim_{x\to c_{P}^{+}}p(x)\varphi(x)=\lim_{x\to+\infty}p(x)\varphi(x)
. The pair

(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T}_{p}),\mathcal{T}_{p})
is the
canonical
Stein pair
for
P
. Conditions (i) and (ii) are equivalent to
requiring that
(p\varphi)
is absolutely continuous on every compact
subinterval of
(c_{P},+\infty)
, which we denote

(p\varphi)\in\mathrm{AC}_{\mathrm{loc}}((c_{P},+\infty))
. In
particular these conditions guarantee that
P(\mathcal{T}_{p}\varphi)=0
for
all
\varphi\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T}_{p})
. This last fact characterizes
P
,
under certain additional conditions (see e.g.
[
11
]
).

Typically
(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T}_{p}),\mathcal{T}_{p})
is not
tractable, and it is best to work with a modified operator

\mathcal{A}_{p}\cdot:=\mathcal{T}_{p}\left(m\,\cdot\right)
for
m
a
well-chosen function. Such an operator is called a

reparameterization
. In the present paper we choose
m=P/p
,
so that the reparameterized operator we use is

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{p}\varphi

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{T}_{p}\left(\frac{P}{p}\,\varphi\right)=\frac{(P\varphi)^{\prime}}{p}\mathbb{I}_{(c_{P},+\infty)}=\left(\frac{P}{p}\varphi^{\prime}+\varphi\right)\mathbb{I}_{(c_{P},+\infty)}.

(11)

We associate to
\mathcal{A}_{p}
the reparameterized Stein class

Suppose

Proof.

In the case of Item

7

U^{\leftarrow}
its generalized inverse function, defined
by

\displaystyle U^{\leftarrow}(t):=\inf\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mid U(x)>t\big\}.

Whenever
U
is increasing, one has
U^{\leftarrow}=U^{-1}
. As
mentioned in the main text, being in a domain of attraction of the
Fréchet distribution of parameter
\alpha
is related to knowing
whether the survival function
1-F
belongs to the class of
regularly varying functions of parameter
-\alpha
.

Definition A.1

(B.1.1,
[
6
]
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a Lebesgue measurable
function, eventually positive and let
\rho
be in
\mathbb{R}
. One says
that
f
is
regularly varying of index
\rho
if

\underset{t\rightarrow+\infty}{\lim}\,\frac{f(tx)}{f(t)}=x^{\rho},

for every
x
in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
. One denotes by
\operatorname{RV}_{\rho}
the set of
all regularly varying functions of index
\rho
.

Remark A.2
.

The function
f
above only needs to be defined on a neighborhood of

+\infty
. In that case, one can always extend it by
0
on the
remainder of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

The connection between regular variation and the Fréchet DA is summarized in the next result.

Proposition A.3

(Theorem 1.2.1.1,
[
6
]
)
.

Let
X
be a random variable with cumulative distribution function

F
and let
\alpha
be a positive real number. Then
X
is in the
domain of attraction of
\Phi_{\alpha}
if and only if for every

x\in\mathbb{R}
,
F(x)<1
and
1-F\in\operatorname{RV}_{-\alpha}
. Furthermore, one
can choose
a_{n}=F^{\leftarrow}(1-1/n)
and
b_{n}=0
.

The following result, known as Karamata’s theorem, is key.

Theorem A.4

(B.1.5,
[
6
]
)
.

Let
f\in RV_{\alpha}
for
some
\alpha
in
\mathbb{R}
. Then there exists
t_{0}>0
such that
f
is
positive and locally bounded on
[t_{0},+\infty[
. If
\alpha>-1

then

\underset{t\rightarrow+\infty}{\lim}\,\frac{tf(t)}{\int_{0}^{t}f(s)\,ds}=\alpha+1.

(22)

If
\alpha\leq-1
and
\int_{0}^{+\infty}f(s)\,ds<+\infty
, then

\underset{t\rightarrow+\infty}{\lim}\,\frac{tf(t)}{\int_{t}^{+\infty}f(s)\,ds}=-\alpha-1.

(23)

Conversely, if (
22
) holds for
\rho>-1
then

f\in\operatorname{RV}_{\rho}
, and if (
23
) holds for
\rho<-1

then
f\in\operatorname{RV}_{\rho}
.

Let
f
be a continuous density function such that
f
is in
\operatorname{RV}_{-1-\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
. Then by Theorem
A.4
, one gets

\displaystyle\underset{t\rightarrow+\infty}{\lim}\,\frac{tf(t)}{1-F(t)}=-(-1-\alpha)-1=\alpha,

(24)

where
F(t):=\int_{-\infty}^{t}f(u)\,du
is the cumulative
distribution function associated to
f
.

Proposition A.5
.

Let
f
be a density function on
\mathbb{R}
, belonging to

\operatorname{RV}_{-1-\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
. Let
F
be its cumulative
distribution function and set
L:=f/F
. Then
L
belongs to

\operatorname{RV}_{-1-\alpha}
.

Proof.

Indeed, since
\underset{u\rightarrow+\infty}{\lim}\,F(u)=1
and
f\in\operatorname{RV}_{-1-\alpha}
, one gets, for
x>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{L(tx)}{L(t)}=\frac{f(tx)}{f(t)}\frac{F(t)}{F(tx)}\underset{t\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}x^{-1-\alpha}.

(25)

∎

Proposition A.6

(B.1.9,7,
[
6
]
)
.

If
f
is
RV_{\rho}
with
\rho\leq 0
and is bounded on finite
intervals of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, then for every
\delta>0
and every

\xi>0
, there exists
c>0
and
t_{0}>0
such that for
t\geq t_{0}

and
0<x\leq\xi
,

\frac{f(tx)}{f(t)}\leq c_{\delta,\xi}x^{\rho-\delta}.

That means that for
0<\varepsilon<1
, there exists
t_{0}>0
and

M_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

x^{1+\alpha}\frac{f(tx)}{f(t)}\leq M_{\varepsilon}x^{-\varepsilon},

(26)

for every
0<x\leq 1
and
t\geq t_{0}
.

Proposition A.7

(B.1.10,
[
6
]
)
.

Let

f
be in
\operatorname{RV}_{\alpha}
. Then for every
\delta>0
and

\varepsilon>0
, there exists
t_{0}>0
such that for every
t>0
and
every
x>0
such that
tx\geq t_{0}
,

\left|\frac{f(tx)}{f(t)}-x^{\alpha}\right|\leq\varepsilon\max(x^{\alpha+\delta},x^{\alpha-\delta}).

Proposition A.8

(B.1.9,4,
[
6
]
)
.

Let
\rho<-1
and let
f
be a continuous probability density
function in
\operatorname{RV}_{\rho}
. Then its cumulative distribution function

F
satisfies
1-F\in\operatorname{RV}_{\rho+1}
.

Proposition A.9
.

Let
F
be a cumulative distribution function on
\mathbb{R}
such that

1-F
belongs in
\operatorname{RV}_{-\alpha}
and
F(x)<1
for every
x>0
, with

\alpha>0
. Then the function

U:=\left(\frac{1}{1-F}\right)^{\leftarrow}
belongs in

\operatorname{RV}_{1/\alpha}
.

Proof.

Since
1-F\in\operatorname{RV}_{-\alpha}
, for all
x>0
, one has

\displaystyle\underset{t\rightarrow+\infty}{\lim}\,\frac{1-F(t)}{1-F(tx)}=\underset{t\rightarrow+\infty}{\lim}\,\left(\frac{1-F(tx)}{1-F(t)}\right)^{-1}=\left(\frac{1}{x^{\alpha}}\right)^{-1}=x^{\alpha},

that is,
1/(1-F)\in\operatorname{RV}_{\alpha}
. Then, since
\alpha>0
and

1/(1-F)
is increasing, by
[
6
, Proposition
B.1.9,9]
,
U
belongs to
\operatorname{RV}_{1/\alpha}
.
∎

Proposition A.10
.

Let
F
be a cumulative distribution function on
\mathbb{R}
such that

1-F
belongs in
\operatorname{RV}_{-\alpha}
and
F(x)<1
for every
x>0
,
with
\alpha>0
. Suppose that the support of
F
is
[a,+\infty[

with
a>0
. Denote by
U
the function
\left(\frac{1}{1-F}\right)^{\leftarrow}
. Then
1/U
belongs in
\operatorname{RV}_{-1/\alpha}
.

Proof.

Since
\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{1-F(t)}=+\infty
, one has
\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}U(t)=+\infty
. By
[
6
, Proposition B.1.9,2]
, one deduces
1/U\in\operatorname{RV}_{-1/\alpha}
.
∎
