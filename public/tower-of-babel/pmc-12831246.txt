A comparison of McGrath video laryngoscope versus macintosh laryngoscope for intubation undergoing general anesthesia: a meta-analysis
Authors: Min Wang, Qiqi Lin, Yibin Tao, Li Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnexpected difficult intubation during general anesthesia can significantly increase patient morbidity and mortality. The McGRATH videolaryngoscope is recognized for its ability to enhance laryngeal visibility in patients with both difficult and normal airways. This study aims to compare the efficacy of McGrath videolaryngoscopes and Macintosh laryngoscope in adult patients under general anesthesia, utilizing data from prospective randomized trials.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was performed across databases including PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase. Data on the success rate of the first intubation attempt, intubation time, and glottic visualization during tracheal intubation were extracted from the identified studies. The data were analyzed using Stata 15 software. The risk of bias was assessed using the five domains of the Cochrane Collaborations tool.ResultsNineteen articles met our inclusion criteria. The McGrath laryngoscope demonstrated superior glottic visualization compared to the Macintosh laryngoscope (RR, 1.47; 95% CI, 1.14 to 1.89). However, there were no significant differences between the McGrath and Macintosh laryngoscopes in terms of intubation time (SMD, 0.35; 95% CI, -0.14 to 0.85) and the success rate of the first intubation attempt (RR, 1.01; 95% CI, 0.96 to 1.07). Additionally, there were no statistically significant differences in the incidence of sore throat between the two laryngoscopes (RR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.71 to 1.42).ConclusionsOur study indicates that the McGrath videolaryngoscope provides superior glottic visualization compared to the Macintosh laryngoscope. Background

The videolaryngoscope is an intubation device that integrates video technology into the laryngoscope to improve airway visualization [1]. Among various types of videolaryngoscopes, the McGrath MAC® videolaryngoscope by Aircraft Medical is highly favored for its lightweight design and the ability to be rapidly and hygienically prepared through the replacement of disposable blades [2, 3]. Furthermore, research has demonstrated that the McGrath MAC videolaryngoscope achieved the highest first-attempt success rate for tracheal intubation among six different videolaryngoscopes [4]. The McGrath videolaryngoscope features a high-definition display that provides real-time images of the patient’s throat and glottic area, aiding healthcare professionals in accurately observing and locating the tracheal entrance during intubation. Its lightweight and compact design makes the McGrath videolaryngoscope highly versatile for use in various medical settings, including emergency and operating rooms.

In recent years, numerous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses have compared the effectiveness of the McGrath videolaryngoscope and the direct Macintosh laryngoscope. These studies evaluated the devices in terms of tracheal intubation success rate, intubation time, and quality of glottic visualization. Some studies have reported that the McGrath videolaryngoscope has a higher success rate compared to the Macintosh laryngoscope [5, 6], while others have found the opposite [7]. Additionally, research indicates that the intubation time with the McGrath videolaryngoscope is shorter in normal airways [8, 9]; however, it is longer in patients with an immobilized cervical spine or obstetric patients [10, 11]. Furthermore, some studies have shown that the McGrath videolaryngoscope provides a better view of the glottis compared to the Macintosh laryngoscope [12, 13], although other studies have reported no significant difference between the two groups [14]. Previous meta-analyses have shown similar tracheal intubation success rates between the two laryngoscopes, with the McGrath videolaryngoscope requiring longer intubation times [15]. However, another meta-analysis, which included only five studies, did not analyze intubation times or glottic visualization for the McGrath [16]. Recently, several studies comparing the McGrath and Macintosh laryngoscopes have reported no significant differences in intubation times between the two groups [9, 17]. Therefore, this study evaluates the efficacy of the McGrath videolaryngoscope versus the Macintosh direct laryngoscope for intubation under general anesthesia, aiming to provide clinical insights for effective airway management.

Methods

The meta-analysis was crafted in adherence to the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature inquiry was executed across multiple databases, namely PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science, up until the final search date of May 30, 2024. The search methodology encompassed a fusion of free text and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms within the databases, incorporating terms such as “McGrath” (or McGrath MAC, videolaryngoscope) and “Macintosh” (or direct laryngoscope). Additionally, a manual examination of references cited in pertinent reports and reviews was executed. No language constraints were imposed during the search process.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The studies incorporated into this meta-analysis adhered to the following inclusion criteria: (1) Inclusion of patients aged 18 years or older who underwent surgical procedures under general anesthesia; (2) Clinical trials that evaluated the efficacy of McGrath video laryngoscopy versus Macintosh laryngoscopy techniques; (3) Evaluation of outcomes pertaining to the success rate of the first intubation attempt, intubation duration, glottic visualization, and occurrences of adverse events. Conversely, the exclusion criteria comprised: (1) Involvement of pediatric patients, manikin trials, or cadaver studies; (2) Non-randomized controlled trials; (3) Literature reviews; (4) Abstracts from conferences; and (5) Case reports.

Data extraction

Two authors conducted an independent assessment of each article to ascertain its eligibility based on the predefined inclusion criteria. Any discrepancies in the interpretation of values or the assignment of analyses were amicably resolved through third author. Data were systematically extracted onto a standardized data extraction sheet. The data extracted encompassed the following elements: (1) The characteristics of the studies included, such as authorship, groups (MG/MC), age, participant count, sex, body mass index, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) status, and type of surgery; (2) an evaluation of the risk of bias; and (3) measures of outcomes.

Moreover, two evaluators independently assessed trial bias risk utilizing the five domains outlined in the Cochrane Collaboration’s tool. Should discord arise between these evaluators, a final determination was reached through consensus involving a senior author.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was performed utilizing Stata 15 software. Aggregate effect estimations for categorical variables were presented as risk ratios (RR) alongside a 95% confidence interval (CI), while for continuous variables, they were expressed as standardized mean differences (SMD) with a corresponding 95% CI. Heterogeneity was evaluated through both the p-value and I2 statistics. A fixed-effect model was employed for meta-analysis when p > 0.05 and I2 < 50%. Conversely, a random-effects model was utilized if p < 0.05 and I2 > 50%. Sensitivity analysis was conducted by sequentially excluding individual studies to assess the robustness of the results. Publication bias was investigated using Egger’s test.

Results

Searching result

The initial literature search yielded a total of 4808 records. Upon removal of duplicate entries, the number of articles was reduced to 1925. Subsequent screening of titles and abstracts resulted in 29 articles meeting the criteria for full-text review. After a thorough examination of the full texts, 10 studies were deemed ineligible, leaving 19 studies to be incorporated into this meta-analysis [7, 9, 10, 13, 14, 17–28]. First intubation attempt success rate

Eleven studies documented the first intubation attempt success rate. There was no heterogeneity among the studies (I2 = 42.3%, p = 0.076), so the utilization of a fixed effects model for analytical purposes. The pooled data revealed no statistically significant differences in the first-attempt success rates between the McGrath and Macintosh laryngoscopes (RR, 1.01; 95% CI, 0.96 to 1.07; Fig. 3A).

A Forest plot of the first intubation attempt success rate for intubation undergoing general anesthesia using the McGrath compared with the Macintosh laryngoscope. B Forest plot of intubation time for intubation undergoing general anesthesia using the McGrath compared with the Macintosh laryngoscope

Intubation time

Sixteen studies assessed the intubation time associated with the McGrath and Macintosh laryngoscopes. Given the substantial heterogeneity among the pooled studies (I2 = 94.1%, p < 0.001), a random-effects model was employed for the analysis. The combined data unveiled no statistically significant distinctions in the intubation time between the McGrath and Macintosh laryngoscopes (SMD, 0.35; 95% CI, −0.14 to 0.85; Fig. 3B).

Glottic view

The glottic view based on the Cormack–Lehane grading system was reported in nine studies. A ‘good view’ was defined according to the Cormack–Lehane classification as grade 1. Owing to considerable heterogeneity observed across the aggregated studies (I2 = 84.5%, p < 0.001), a random-effects model was utilized for the analysis. The findings revealed that the rate of achieving a Cormack–Lehane classification 1 was significantly higher with the McGrath videolaryngoscope compared to the Macintosh laryngoscope (RR, 1.47; 95% CI, 1.14 to 1.89; Fig. 4A).

A Forest plot of glottic visualization (Cormack–Lehane classification 1) for intubation undergoing general anesthesia using the McGrath compared with the Macintosh laryngoscope. B Forest plot of sore throat for intubation undergoing general anesthesia using the McGrath compared with the Macintosh laryngoscope

Sore throat

A total of five studies evaluated the incidence of sore throat associated with the McGrath and Macintosh laryngoscopes. As there was no significant heterogeneity observed among the studies (I2 = 20.1%, p = 0.287), a fixed-effects model was applied for the analysis. The pooled data revealed no statistically significant differences in the incidence of sore throat between the McGrath and Macintosh laryngoscopes (RR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.71 to 1.42; Fig. 4B).

Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

A publication bias assessment was conducted on the outcomes. The findings indicated that Egger’s test yielded all p-value greater than 0.05, signifying the absence of publication bias (Fig. 5). To investigate potential sources of heterogeneity, a sensitivity analysis of the outcome measures was carried out to assess the aggregated effect by systematically excluding individual studies. The outcomes demonstrated robust stability in the combined results.

Discussion

Difficult intubation, which may be encountered unexpectedly under general anesthesia, is a life-threatening event that increases the morbidity and mortality of anesthetized patients during surgery [29]. The escalating challenges in airway management have led to an increased reliance on videolaryngoscopy. Despite its perceived advantages over direct laryngoscopy for tracheal intubation, the application of videolaryngoscopy continues to be a subject of debate [15]. Our meta-analysis indicates that under general anesthesia, the McGrath videolaryngoscope provides better glottic visualization compared to the Macintosh laryngoscope. There is no significant difference between the two in terms of tracheal intubation success rates and intubation times.

Our results indicate that there is no significant difference in the first-attempt intubation success rates between the McGrath and Macintosh laryngoscopes. This suggests that, overall, both devices perform similarly in terms of first-attempt success rates. This finding is consistent with the study by Sansone et al., who reported similar success rates of 72.0% for the McGrath and 68.4% for the Macintosh laryngoscope [30]. In contrast, Kriege et al. found that the first-pass intubation success rate was significantly higher in the McGrath group than the Macintosh group [31]. These discrepancies may be attributed to variations in study design, patient populations, operator experience, and clinical settings. Operator experience and skill level are critical factors influencing intubation success. Research has shown that the video-assisted guidance provided by the McGrath videolaryngoscope offers clearer glottic visualization, which is particularly beneficial for less experienced operators [32]. Conversely, experienced anesthesiologists may achieve high success rates using the traditional Macintosh laryngoscope. Therefore, future research should focus on stratifying operator expertise to further elucidate the optimal performance of each laryngoscope in different contexts.

The Cormack–Lehane classification system is a standardized method used in anesthesiology to assess the degree of glottic exposure during laryngoscopy [33]. Our study results indicate that the rate of Grade 1 in the Cormack–Lehane classification system increased in the McGrath group compared to the Macintosh group, suggesting that the McGrath videolaryngoscope provides better visualization of the glottis. This finding is consistent with the study by Lewis et al., which demonstrated a statistically significant increase in the number of laryngoscopies achieving a Cormack and Lehane grade 1 view with videolaryngoscopes compared to Macintosh laryngoscopes, along with a decrease in the number of grade 3 and 4 views [16]. This improvement can be attributed to the digital camera on the tip of the McGrath laryngoscope, which allows practitioners to access the glottis more intuitively, offering a wider field of view and reducing the need for alignment with the line of sight. However, the effectiveness of the Cormack–Lehane classification system is questioned when using the McGrath videolaryngoscopes. A good view of the glottis on the screen does not guarantee easy tracheal intubation, as the indirect view may impair hand-eye coordination [34]. Therefore, practitioners should possess good hand-eye coordination and sufficient experience to effectively perform video laryngoscopy.

Additionally, our study found no significant difference in intubation times between the McGrath videolaryngoscope and the traditional Macintosh laryngoscope. This contrasts with the findings of Ho et al. and Hoshijima et al. Ho et al. reported significant decreases in intubation times in the McGrath group compared to the Macintosh group [35], whereas Hoshijima et al. found that the McGrath required longer intubation times than the Macintosh [15]. Although some studies have shown that videolaryngoscopes, including the McGrath, may provide better glottic visualization or higher first-attempt success rates in specific scenarios, such as difficult airways, the intubation time has not significantly decreased [36]. These discrepancies may be due to differences in study designs, patient population diversity, and operator experience levels. Future research should further explore the efficacy of different types of laryngoscopes in special patient populations.

Postoperative sore throat is considered one of the primary complaints following tracheal intubation. In this study, we found no statistically significant differences in the incidence of the sore throat between the McGrath videolaryngoscope and the traditional Macintosh laryngoscope.

This study has several limitations. First, the included studies exhibit variations in design, strategies, and outcome measures, which may lead to measurement bias. Second, there is clinical and methodological heterogeneity among the included studies, stemming from differences in research methods, such as the diversity of patient populations (e.g., varying BMI levels, comorbidities), the skill levels of laryngoscopists (e.g., experienced anesthesiologists or critical care attendings versus trainees or infrequent intubators such as emergency physicians), and anesthesia techniques. Third, the lack of stratified reporting in the original studies precluded subgroup analyses to explore potential differences in outcomes across clinical contexts (e.g., elective surgeries versus critical care/emergency department settings) or operator expertise. Fourth, the included studies did not systematically incorporate preoperative airway assessment tools such as the Wilson risk sum score, modified Mallampati classification, or Upper Lip Bite Test (ULBT) to stratify patients’ risk of difficult intubation. Lastly, since most of the included studies involve elective surgery patients, we are unable to evaluate the efficacy of the two laryngoscopy methods across different types of surgeries or urgent clinical scenarios.

Conclusion

In summary, our analysis demonstrates that the McGrath videolaryngoscope provides superior glottic visualization compared to the Macintosh laryngoscope in adult patients under general anesthesia. There are no significant differences between the two devices in terms of first-attempt tracheal intubation success rates and intubation times. Further high-quality trials are needed to emphasize and confirm the benefits of the McGrath videolaryngoscope in routine anesthesia practice.
