On computational schemes for the Magnus expansion of the in-medium similarity renormalization group
Matthias Heinz

Abstract
The in-medium similarity renormalization group (IMSRG) is a popular many-body method used for computations of nuclei. It solves the many-body Schrödinger equation through a continuous unitary transformation of the many-body Hamiltonian. The IMSRG transformation is typically truncated at the normal-ordered two-body level, the IMSRG(2), but recently several approaches have been developed to capture the effects of normal-ordered three-body operators, the IMSRG(3). In particular, a factorized approximation to the IMSRG(3) proposes to capture the leading effects of three-body operators at the same computational cost as the IMSRG(2) approximation. This approach often employs an approximate scheme for solving the IMSRG equations, the so-called hunter-gatherer scheme. In this work, I study the uncertainty associated with this scheme. I find that the hunter-gatherer scheme differs by up to 7 ​ MeV 7\,\mathrm{MeV} for ground-state energies and 0.5 ​ MeV 0.5\,\mathrm{MeV} for excitation energies from standard IMSRG(2) approaches. These differences are in some cases comparable to the expected size of IMSRG(3) corrections.

For such predictions, it is essential to understand and quantify all uncertainties, e.g., due to truncations in the effective field theory or in the many-body method.

The in-medium similarity renormalization group (IMSRG)
Tsukiyama et al. 2011
;
Hergert et al. 2016
;
Stroberg et al. 2017
;
Stroberg et al. 2019
is a systematically improvable many-body method
that performs a unitary transformation of the Hamiltonian

H(s)=U(s)\,H\,U^{\dagger}(s)

(1)

by solving the flow equation

\frac{dH(s)}{ds}=[\eta(s),H(s)]

(2)

from
s=0
towards
\infty
.
The generator
\eta(s)
is chosen to generate the desired unitary transformation:
One either decouples the ground state from its excitations in the single-reference IMSRG
Tsukiyama et al. 2011
,
or one decouples a core and valence space from the rest of the Hilbert space in the valence-space IMSRG (VS-IMSRG)
Stroberg et al. 2017
.

The IMSRG is typically solved in the IMSRG(2) approximation,
where all operators are truncated at the normal-ordered two-body level.
This approximation may be systematically improved by including normal-ordered three-body operators,
the IMSRG(3)
Heinz et al. 2021
;
Stroberg et al. 2024
;
Heinz et al. 2025b
.
IMSRG(3) calculations are several orders of magnitude more expensive than IMSRG(2) calculations,
making them far from routine.
Still, the uncertainty due to the IMSRG(2) must be quantified for a complete uncertainty estimate.
To this end, Ref.
He and Stroberg 2024
introduced a factorized approximation to the IMSRG(3),
the IMSRG(3f
2
).
The IMSRG(3f
2
) captures leading IMSRG(3) corrections at the same computational cost as the IMSRG(2),
providing an accessible way to obtain more precise IMSRG predictions
and quantify the many-body method uncertainty.

The IMSRG(3f
2
) typically uses an approximate computational scheme for the IMSRG(2),
the hunter-gatherer scheme.
In this article, I study and quantify the uncertainty associated with the hunter-gatherer scheme.

Magnus expansion schemes.—

The IMSRG may be solved by directly integrating Eq. (
2
) from
s=0
towards
\infty
.
Consistently transforming other operators to evaluate their expectation values or transition matrix elements requires solving additional flow equations in parallel:

\frac{dO(s)}{ds}=[\eta(s),O(s)]\,,

(3)

for a given operator
O
.
This makes direct integration of Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
) prohibitively expensive when considering more than just a few operators.
The Magnus expansion
Morris et al. 2015

directly parametrizes the unitary transformation

U(s)=e^{\Omega(s)}

(4)

with the anti-Hermitian Magnus operator
\Omega
.
One instead solves a flow equation for the Magnus operator:

\frac{d\Omega(s)}{ds}=\eta(s)+\frac{1}{2}[\Omega(s),\eta(s)]+\frac{1}{12}\big[\Omega(s),[\Omega(s),\eta(s)]\big]+\dots.

(5)

The transformed Hamiltonian is then computed using the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff (BCH) formula

H(s)=H+[\Omega(s),H]+\frac{1}{2}\big[\Omega(s),[\Omega(s),H]\big]+\dots,

(6)

and any other operator may be transformed in the same way.
The Magnus expansion has the clear benefit that the unitary transformation is solved for only once
and then easily applied to any other operator.

An important subtlety is the role of the many-body approximation.
The Magnus approach and the direct integration of the flow equation (
2
) (hereafter referred to as the flow equation approach)
are exactly equivalent only when the Magnus expansion in Eq. (
5
) converges
and the commutators in Eqs. (
2
), (
5
), and (
6
) are evaluated exactly.
In practice, all commutators are truncated.
In the IMSRG(2), this truncation is at the normal-ordered two-body level.
As a result, the Magnus approach and the flow equation approach
will differ at the level of the normal-ordered two-body approximation.

To mitigate this,
a modified computational scheme for the Magnus approach has been standardized
Stroberg et al. 2024
,
referred to here as the split Magnus approach.
The unitary transformation is split into small successive transformations:

\displaystyle U(s)

\displaystyle=U_{N+1}(s-s_{N})\cdots U_{2}(s_{2}-s_{1})U_{1}(s_{1})

\displaystyle=e^{\Omega_{N+1}(s-s_{N})}\cdots e^{\Omega_{2}(s_{2}-s_{1})}e^{\Omega_{1}(s_{1})}\,.

(7)

The first transformation on the right transforms from
s=0
to
s_{1}
,
the next transforms from
s=s_{1}
to
s_{2}
,
and so on.
The splitting is decided based on the Frobenius norm
of
\Omega_{i}(s-s_{i-1})
;
we choose a splitting threshold
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{split}}

and once we reach
s=s_{i}
where

||\Omega_{i}(s-s_{i-1})||\geq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{split}}

we save
\Omega_{i}(s_{i}-s_{i-1})

and start a new unitary transformation from
s=s_{i}
,

U_{i+1}(s-s_{i})=e^{\Omega_{i+1}(s-s_{i})}
.

This approach has a few benefits.
First, the norm of
\Omega_{i}
remains small,
so the nested commutator expansions in Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
)
converge quickly.
This reduces number of commutators evaluated to solve the IMSRG,
reducing the computational cost.
Second,
one can see that as
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{split}}\to 0

it approaches the flow equation solution.
Each
\Omega_{i}
performs an infinitesimal transformation of the Hamiltonian.
The integration from
s_{i}
to
s_{i}+\Delta s
in Eq. (
5
) becomes
\frac{d\Omega_{i+1}(s-s_{i})}{ds}=\eta(s_{i})
,
yielding
\Omega_{i+1}(\Delta s)=\Delta s\,\eta(s_{i})
.
Plugging this into Eq. (
6
) gives

H(s_{i}+\Delta s)=H(s_{i})+\Delta s[\eta(s_{i}),H(s_{i})]+\mathcal{O}[(\Delta s)^{2}]\,.

(8)

It is clear that this is a systematic way to approximate the flow equation approach using the Magnus expansion
at the price of storing several Magnus operators.
In practice, one chooses a value for
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{split}}

so that the IMSRG transformation is close to the
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{split}}\to 0
limit
but the number of Magnus operators stored remains moderate (e.g.,
<50
).
To store fewer Magnus operators,
one can increase the value for
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{split}}
.

The hunter-gatherer scheme avoids storing many Magnus operators by handling the splitting differently
Stroberg et al. 2024
.
The unitary transformation is split in two parts,

U(s)=e^{\Omega_{H}(s)}e^{\Omega_{G}(s)}\,,

(9)

the hunter parametrized by
\Omega_{H}(s)
,
which remains small,
and the gatherer parametrized by
\Omega_{G}(s)
.
When
||\Omega_{H}(s)||\geq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{split}}
,
the hunter is absorbed into the gatherer using the BCH expansion
and is set to 0.
One then continues to solve the flow equation for the Magnus operator until the the splitting threshold is reached.
The unitary transformation remains compact, requiring only two Magnus operators.

The hunter-gatherer uncertainty.—

I compare the hunter-gatherer and split Magnus schemes in IMSRG(2) and VS-IMSRG(2) calculations of the ground-state energy and charge radius of
{}^{48}\mathrm{Ca}
in Fig.
2
.
Throughout, I use the 1.8/2.0 (EM) Hamiltonian
Hebeler et al. 2011
,
employ a model space of 13 major harmonic oscillator shells with
\hbar\omega=16\,\mathrm{MeV}

and start from a Hartree-Fock reference state.
Three-body forces are truncated with
E_{\mathrm{3max}}=24

Miyagi 2023

Only the method of solving the IMSRG(2) equations
and the splitting parameter
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{split}}
(where relevant) are varied.

On the left, I consider single-reference IMSRG calculations.
The results obtained by solving the IMSRG(2) via direct integration of the flow equation are indicated by black stars.
In blue, I show results obtained using the split Magnus approach for
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{split}}=2.0

Overall, in all cases, the flow equation result is well approximated,
with, e.g., the ground-state energy differing only by

For smaller
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{split}}

This is not the case for the hunter-gatherer approach, which approximates the Magnus approach without splitting.
For all
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{split}}

the predicted ground-state energies and charge radii differ visibly from the flow equation predictions.
In this case,
the deviations of
\sim 300\,\mathrm{keV}

and

\sim 3\,\mathrm{MeV}

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{split}}
.
In Fig.

E(s)

Recall that

In black, I show

The split Magnus approach in blue (with
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{split}}=0.25

The smaller dots indicate the integration steps,

In this approach the splitting of the Magnus operator does not lead to discontinuities in
E(s)

or cause any other problems during the integration.
In the hunter-gatherer approach,

E(s)
jumps when the Magnus operator is split
and the hunter is absorbed into the gatherer.
The new gatherer fails to generate the same transformation as the previous hunter and gatherer combined,
producing a discontinuity in the energy after the split.
This jump can be large:
For instance for
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{split}}=2.0
,
the final split at
s=4.7
causes
E(s)
to suddenly decrease by
200\,\mathrm{keV}
.
The accumulated error from these jumps produces the difference between the flow equation and hunter-gatherer approaches.

We also see that for larger values of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{split}}
the hunter-gatherer approach is sensitive to where the splitting of the Magnus operator actually occurs.
For
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{split}}=1.0
,
the last split of the Magnus operator occurs around

s\to\infty

For
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{split}}=2.0

before the last split at

after the split it jumps to be significantly lower.
This is in contrast to the split Magnus approach,
which is only weakly sensitive to the value of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{split}}
and systematically converges to the flow equation result.
The hunter-gatherer scheme becomes more reliable for small values of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{split}}
,
consistent with the findings of Ref.
He and Stroberg 2024

Single-reference IMSRG(2) calculations are reliably approximated by both the split Magnus and hunter-gatherer approaches.

Conclusion.—

I studied the hunter-gatherer scheme for solving the IMSRG(2) equations,
which underlies the improved IMSRG(3f
2
) approximation,
seeking to understanding differences between this scheme and standard approaches (direct integration of the flow equation and a split Magnus expansion scheme).
I find that the hunter-gatherer scheme is very reliable for single-reference IMSRG(2) calculations,
differing very little from standard schemes.
For valence-space IMSRG(2) calculations,
differences between the hunter-gatherer and split Magnus schemes are larger.
In some cases, these differences are comparable to other uncertainties related to the IMSRG(2) approximation,
e.g., differences between IMSRG(2) and VS-IMSRG(2) calculations for
{}^{48}\mathrm{Ca}
.
These differences are similar in size to expected IMSRG(3) corrections,
especially for calculations involving larger valence spaces.
The IMSRG(3f
2
) remains valuable for more precise calculations and uncertainty quantification,
but the uncertainty of the hunter-gatherer scheme should be quantified when it is employed.
Improved approximations should reduce this uncertainty,
as it is related to the normal-ordered two-body truncation of the IMSRG(2) commutators.
