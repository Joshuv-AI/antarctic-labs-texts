Local-in-time existence of strong solutions to a quasi-incompressible Cahn–Hilliard–Navier–Stokes system
Mingwen Fei, Xiang Fei, Daozhi Han, Yadong Liu

Abstract
. We analyze a quasi-incompressible Cahn–Hilliard–Navier–Stokes system (qCHNS) for two-phase flows with unmatched densities. The order parameter is the volume fraction difference of the two fluids, while mass-averaged velocity is adopted. This leads to a quasi-incompressible model where the pressure also enters the equation of the chemical potential. We establish local existence and uniqueness of strong solutions by the Banach fixed point theorem and the maximal regularity theory.

1.
Introduction

In this article we consider the quasi-incompressible Cahn–Hilliard–Navier–Stokes system (qCHNS) for two-phase flows with mismatched densities
[
23
,
33
,
34
,
37
]
in a smooth bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{u})+\mathrm{div}\left(\rho\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u}\right)=\mathrm{div}\big(S(\phi,\mathbb{D}\mathbf{u})\big)-(\nabla p+\phi\nabla\mu)-\rho\mathbf{k},\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$},

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}=\alpha\Delta\mu_{p},\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$},

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi+\mathrm{div}(\phi\mathbf{u})=\Delta\mu_{p},\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$},

(1.1c)

\displaystyle\mu=f(\phi)-\Delta\phi,\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$},

(1.1d)

\displaystyle\mu_{p}=\mu+\alpha p,\;\rho=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\phi+1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$}.

(1.1e)

Here
Q_{T}=\Omega\times(0,T)
, the matrix
\mathbf{I}
denotes the identity matrix and the vector
\mathbf{k}
stands for the (fixed) unit vector pointing upwards. The unknowns
(\phi,\mathbf{u},p):Q_{T}\to\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}
are the order parameter (volume fraction difference), the mass-averaged (barycentric) mixture velocity and the pressure respectively.
The scalar
\mu
represents the chemical potential, with
f=F^{\prime}
, where the function
F(\phi)=\frac{1}{4}(1-\phi^{2})^{2}
is the potential energy for the interface. Note that the constant
\varepsilon\coloneqq\frac{\rho_{2}}{\rho_{1}}-1
measures how large are the variance of the two densities
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}>0
for each pure phase. Thus, without loss of generality, it is assumed to be negative (
\rho_{2}<\rho_{1}
). By this, the ratio
\alpha\coloneqq-\frac{\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}}{\rho_{2}+\rho_{1}}=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2+\varepsilon}
is positive.
Moreover, the matrix
S(\phi,\mathbb{D}\mathbf{u})\coloneqq 2\eta(\phi)\mathbb{D}(\mathbf{u})-\frac{2}{3}\eta(\phi)(\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u})\mathbf{I}
denotes the Newtonian stress tensor with the symmetric gradient
\mathbb{D}(\mathbf{u})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla\mathbf{u}^{T})
, where

\eta(\phi)=\frac{\nu-1}{2}\phi+\frac{\nu+1}{2}
is the viscosity of the mixture and

\nu>0
stands for the constant viscosity ratio of the two fluids. Note for simplicity we have set
the rest of the dimensionaless parameters to be unity.

Following
[
3
]
we equip this system with the following initial-boundary conditions

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{u}=0,\quad\text{on $S_{T}$},

(1.2a)

\displaystyle\nabla\phi\cdot\mathbf{n}=\nabla\mu_{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}=(\mathbf{n}\cdot S(\phi,\mathbb{D}\mathbf{u}))_{\tau}+(a(\phi)\mathbf{u})_{\tau}=0,\quad\text{on $S_{T}$ },

(1.2b)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{u},\phi)|_{t=0}=(\mathbf{u}_{0},\phi_{0}),\quad\text{in }\Omega,

(1.2c)

where
S_{T}=\partial\Omega\times(0,T)
and
a(\cdot):\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
denote the friction parameter on the boundary,
\mathbf{n}
represent the exterior normal at the boundary of

\Omega
,
(\cdot)_{\tau}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n})\cdot
denotes the tangent projection on
\partial\Omega
. Note that the boundary condition (
1.2b
) for the velocity is the Navier-slip boundary condition.
Eqs. (
1.1b
) and (
1.1c
) imply

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\mathrm{div}(\rho\mathbf{u})=0.

(1.3)

Then by the standard formal test procedure for smooth solutions, i.e., multiplying (
1.1a
), (
1.1c
), and (
1.1d
) with
\mathbf{u}
,
\mu
, and
\partial_{t}\phi
respectively together with integrating by parts, the total energy fulfills

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E(\mathbf{u},\phi)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\eta(\phi)\left(2\mathbb{D}(\mathbf{u}):\mathbb{D}(\mathbf{u})-\frac{2}{3}(\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u})^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

(1.4)

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\mu_{p}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\partial\Omega}a(\phi)|\mathbf{u}_{\tau}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(1.5)

where the total energy
E
is defined as

\displaystyle E(\mathbf{u},\phi)=\int_{\Omega}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\rho|\mathbf{u}|^{2}+\rho z\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\Big(F(\phi)+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x,

with
\nabla z=\mathbf{k}
.

The qCHNS system is a diffuse interface model for two-phase flows of mismatched densities. Formally setting
\alpha=0
, one recovers the celebrated model H for two-phase flows with matched densities, cf.
[
24
,
27
]
. See
[
10
,
22
]
for recent reviews of diffuse interface models for multi-phase flows. There exist several different diffuse interface models for the case of mismatched densities, depending on how the mixture velocity is defined and which order parameter is utilized. A typical example is the thermodynamically consistent model (with unmatched density) derived by J. Lowengrub and L. Truskinovsky in
[
29
]
, where the mixture velocity is mass-averaged (conservation of momentum) and the order parameter is the concentration difference. The mass-averaged velocity is no longer divergence-free inside the diffusive interface, hence the model is a quasi-incompressible model. Moreover, the quasi-incompressibility introduces a pressure term in the chemical potential equation, cf.(
1.1e
), which entails a strong coupling between the Navier-Stokes equations and the Cahn-Hilliard equations. Recently, by using balance laws of the individual constituents and the Coleman–Noll procedure, M. Shokrpour Roudbari et al.
[
34
]
derived the qCHNS system (
1.1
). The usage of volume fraction difference as the order parameter in (
1.1
) greatly simplified the quasi-incompressible model, which makes it more amenable for numerical computation.
An important observation of M. Shokrpour Roudbari et al.
[
34
, Remark 2.9 & 2.10]
is that, up to the definition of the mobility and the definition of mass fluxes, the models in
[
10
,
33
,
34
]
are all equivalent. A unified derivation and comparison of known diffuse interface
models from the physical point of view is presented in
[
37
]
. On the other hand, based on a volume-averaged velocity, H. Abels, H. Garcke and G. Grün
[
8
]
firstly derived a thermodynamically
consistent diffuse interface model with different densities, termed as the AGG model. Since the mixture velocity is derived from volume averaging, the AGG model is an incompressible model–closely resembling the model H. We also refer the interested readers to
[
10
,
13
,
14
,
21
,
33
,
37
]
for other diffuse interface systems
modeling two-phase flows with unmatched densities.

For the AGG model, the existence of global weak solutions was established in
[
5
]
with the logarithmic potential
F
and a positive mobility
m
, and the case of degenerate mobility was addressed in
[
6
]
. The local existence of strong solutions was proved in
[
9
]
by the Hilbert monotone operator theory and the maximal
L^{p}
-regularity theory for fourth-order parabolic equations. Later on, the strong well-posedness and stability result of the model with logarithmic potential
in two-dimensional and three-dimensional cases were established by Giorgini with
L^{2}
-energy method
[
19
,
20
]
. Recently, H. Abels, H. Garcke and A. Giorgini
[
7
]
gave the regularity properties and long time behavior of weak solutions. There were lots of extensions of this model in different aspects.
Concerning moving contact line problem, C.G. Gal, M. Grasselli and H. Wu
[
18
]
proved the existence of global weak solutions of above system with dynamic boundary conditions and logarithmic potential. In
[
15
]
, S. Frigeri derived a nonlocal version of above model and proved existence of global dissipative weak solutions under the case of singular
double-well potentials and non degenerate mobilities. S. Frigeri later turned from the study of non degenerate mobility to the physically more relevant situation of degenerate mobility and proved existence
of global weak solutions in
[
16
]
. Recently, the global well-posedness and convergence to equilibrium of the nonlocal model was justified in
[
17
]
. In
[
4
]
, weak solutions of a non-Newtonian variant of the AGG model was considered.

The theoretical results on quasi-incompressible diffuse interface models are rather limited. Since the model is quasi-incompressible (compressible), the regularity of pressure is low
[
2
]
. Note that the pressure enters the chemical potential equation (
1.1e
). In this case, the pressure does not enjoy any integrability
a priori
and hence one cannot use the standard technique for Cahn–Hilliard equation with elliptic estimates, due to which the logarithmic potential could not be included. For the Lowengrub–Truskinovsky model, global existence of weak solution is proven in
[
2
]
where the energy law yields the
W^{1,p},p>d
estimate of the order parameter. Local in-time existence of strong solution is established in
[
3
]
.

The aim of this article is to show local existence of strong solution to the qCHNS model which differs significantly from the Lowengrub-Truskinovsky model. Following the approach in
[
3
]
, we linearize the system and show that the linear operator is an isomorphism between certain Hilbert spaces, making use of the maximal regularity theory of parabolic systems and properties of traces of anisotropic fractional Sobolev spaces(cf.
[
32
]
). Then we argue that the nonlinear terms are Lipschitz continuous with a small constants afforded by a small final time
T
. Unlike the quasi-incompressible model of Lowengrub-Truskinovsky studied by Abels in
[
3
]
, there is a higher-order term
\mathrm{div}(\phi\mathbf{u})
(precisely
\phi\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}
) in presence from the equation of order parameter, cf. (
3.5
). This higher order term poses substantial difficulty in current analysis framework. To overcome this challenge, we additionally impose a smallness assumption on the initial data
\phi_{0}
.
The following assumption is maintained throughout.

Assumption 1.1
.

We assume
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a smooth bounded domain,
\eta,a\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,
0<\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\eta(t)\leq\eta(s)\leq\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\eta(t)<+\infty
,
0<\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}a(t)\leq a(s)\leq\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}a(t)<+\infty
.

The main result is summarized as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, and
\mathbf{\phi}_{0}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
such that
|\mathbf{\phi}_{0}|<\varepsilon_{0}

a.e. in

\Omega
for a sufficiently small constant
\varepsilon_{0}
. Then there exists
T_{0}>0
such that for any
0<T\leq T_{0}
, the system (
1.1
) equipped with boundary conditions (
1.2
) admits a unique strong solution
(\mathbf{u},\phi)
fulfilling

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}\in H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\phi\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle|\phi|\leq\frac{1}{2},\ \rho\geq\frac{1}{4}.

Moreover, there is a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))}+\|\partial_{t}\phi\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C(\|\phi_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}).

Remark 1.2
.

For the case
d=2
, the same conclusion can be obtained by applying the same method as
d=3
.

Remark 1.3
.

Here we get
|\mathbf{\phi}|\leq\frac{1}{2}
for simplicity. In fact the general result can be written as
|\mathbf{\phi}|<C<1
and
\rho\geq C_{0}>0
, cf. Remark
3.1
.

The structure of this article is as follows. In section
2
, we recall some classical results and preliminary lemmas. In order to prove the main result, we shall divide the system into the linear part and nonlinear part to apply the Banach fixed point theorem. Section
3
is devoted to the proof of the main result Theorem

2.3.
The Helmholtz Decomposition

Let

\Omega
be a bounded sufficiently smooth domain. We say that the Helmholtz decomposition for

exists whenever

can be decomposed into

\displaystyle L^{p}(\Omega)=L_{\sigma}^{p}(\Omega)\oplus G_{p}(\Omega),

where

\displaystyle G_{p}(\Omega):=\{\mathbf{u}\in L^{p}(\Omega):\mathbf{u}=\nabla\pi\>\ \text{for some}\>\ \pi\in W_{loc}^{1,p}(\Omega)\},

and

as its null space is called the Helmholtz projection. The existence of the Helmholtz projection for

is very strongly linked with the following weak Neumann problem: given

satisfying

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega}(\nabla\pi-\mathbf{f})\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x=0,\varphi\in W_{(0)}^{1,p}(\Omega).

For more details, one refers to
[
36
,
26
]
.

.

Let

-boundary,

Lemma 2.2
.

-boundary, for

,

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section we prove the main result Theorem
1.1
, i.e., the existence and uniqueness of strong solutions to (
1.1
) equipped with boundary conditions and initial condition(
1.2
) for a short time. For convenience of computations, we reformulate the system by eliminating the pressure
p
and the chemical potential
\mu
. Note that

\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}=\alpha\Delta\mu_{p},\ \text{in }\Omega;\ \nabla\mu_{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0,\ \text{on }\partial\Omega.

We get
\mu_{p}=\tfrac{1}{\alpha}G(\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u})
if
G=\Delta_{N}^{-1}
is defined by solving the following weak Neumann Laplace equation

\displaystyle\Delta G(g)

\displaystyle=g,\quad\text{in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\partial_{n}G(g)

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}g\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=0,

where
g=\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}
. We then have
P_{\sigma}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}-\nabla G(g)
.

Hence, we rewrite (
1.1
) as follows in
Q_{T}

\displaystyle\rho\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+\rho\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}-\mathrm{div}S(\phi,\mathbb{D}\mathbf{u})+(\tfrac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\nabla G(\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u})+(\phi-\tfrac{1}{\alpha})\nabla(f(\phi)-\Delta\phi))+\rho\mathbf{k}=0,

(3.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi+\mathrm{div}(\phi\mathbf{u})=\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u},

(3.1b)

with the following boundary conditions and initial condition

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0,\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),

(3.1c)

\displaystyle\nabla\phi\cdot\mathbf{n}=\nabla G(\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{n}=0,\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),

(3.1d)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{n}\cdot S(\phi,\mathbb{D}\mathbf{u}))_{\tau}+(a(\phi)\mathbf{u})_{\tau}=0,\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),

(3.1e)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{u},\phi)|_{t=0}=(\mathbf{u}_{0},\phi_{0}),\quad\text{in }\Omega.

(3.1f)

Before the main argument, we reduce the initial data to be vanishing via temporal trace operator. Namely, one shall choose
\tilde{\phi}\in H^{1}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega)\cap H_{N}^{2}(\Omega))
be such that
\tilde{\phi}|_{t=0}=\phi_{0}
.

To this end, we rewrite the system (
3.1
) as follows

L(\phi)\binom{\mathbf{u}}{\phi^{\prime}}=F(\mathbf{u},\phi),

(3.2)

where the operator
L(\phi):X_{T}\rightarrow Y_{T}
is defined by

L(\phi)\binom{\mathbf{u}}{\phi^{\prime}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}-\mathrm{div}(\tfrac{1}{\rho_{0}}S(\phi,\mathbb{D}\mathbf{u}))+h(\phi,\phi^{\prime})\\
\partial_{t}\phi^{\prime}-\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}\\
(\mathbf{n}\cdot S(\phi,\mathbb{D}(P_{\sigma}\mathbf{u})))_{\tau}+(a(\phi)P_{\sigma}\mathbf{u})_{\tau}\big|_{\partial\Omega}\\
(\mathbf{u},\phi^{\prime})|_{t=0}\\
\end{array}\right),

(3.3)

for
h(\phi,\phi^{\prime}):=\nabla(\mathrm{div}(\tfrac{1}{\rho_{0}}(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}-\phi)\nabla\phi^{\prime}))
and
\phi^{\prime}=\phi-\tilde{\phi}
. Moreover, we define the nonlinear part
F(\mathbf{u},\phi):X_{T}\rightarrow Y_{T}
as

F(\mathbf{u},\phi)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}F_{1}(\mathbf{u},\phi)\\
F_{2}(\mathbf{u},\phi)\\
F_{3}(\mathbf{u},\phi)\\
(\mathbf{u}_{0},\phi^{\prime}_{0})\\
\end{array}\right),

where

\displaystyle F_{1}(\mathbf{u},\phi)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}-\frac{1}{\rho(\phi)}(\phi-\tfrac{1}{\alpha})\nabla(f(\phi))-\tfrac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla G(\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u})+\mathbf{k}+\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla(\nabla(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}-\phi)\cdot\nabla\phi)

\displaystyle\quad-\nabla\frac{1}{\rho}\cdot S(\phi,\mathbb{D}\mathbf{u})+\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}-\phi)\Delta\phi+\nabla(\nabla\frac{1}{\rho}\cdot((\tfrac{1}{\alpha}-\phi)\nabla\phi^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\quad-(\nabla\frac{1}{\rho})\mathrm{div}((\phi-\tfrac{1}{\alpha})\nabla\phi))+\nabla(\frac{1}{\rho}\mathrm{div}((\phi-\tfrac{1}{\alpha})\nabla\tilde{\phi})))-\nabla\mathrm{div}((\frac{1}{\rho}-\tfrac{1}{\rho_{0}})(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}-\phi)\nabla\phi^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{div}((\frac{1}{\rho}-\tfrac{1}{\rho_{0}})S(\phi,\mathbb{D}\mathbf{u})),

\displaystyle F_{2}(\mathbf{u},\phi)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{t}\tilde{\phi}-\mathrm{div}(\phi\mathbf{u}),

\displaystyle F_{3}(\mathbf{u},\phi)

\displaystyle=-(\mathbf{n}\cdot S(\phi,\nabla^{2}G(\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}))_{\tau}-(a(\phi)\nabla G(\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u})))_{\tau}\big|_{\partial\Omega}.

Here the spaces are defined as
X_{T}=X_{T}^{1}\times X_{T}^{2}

and

\displaystyle X_{T}^{1}=\{\mathbf{u}\in H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)):\mathbf{u}|_{\partial\Omega}=0\},

\displaystyle X_{T}^{2}=\{\phi\in H^{1}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega)):\mathbf{n}\cdot\nabla\phi|_{\partial\Omega}=0\},

\displaystyle Y_{T}=L^{2}(Q_{T})\times L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{(0)}(\Omega))\times\{\mathbf{a}\in H^{\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2}}(S_{T}):\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{a}=0\}\times H^{1}_{n}(\Omega)\times H^{2}_{N}(\Omega),

with
H^{\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2}}(S_{T})=H^{\frac{1}{4}}(0,T;L^{2}(\partial\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega))
. The norms of above spaces are given by

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}\|_{X_{T}^{1}}=\|(\partial_{t}u,\mathbf{u},\nabla\mathbf{u},\nabla^{2}\mathbf{u})\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})},

\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{X_{T}^{2}}=\|(\phi,\partial_{t}\phi,\nabla\phi,\nabla^{2}\phi,\nabla^{3}\phi)\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})},

\displaystyle\|(f,g,\mathbf{a},\mathbf{u}_{0},\phi_{0}^{\prime})\|_{Y_{T}}=\|(f,\nabla g)\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}+\|\mathbf{a}\|_{H^{\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2}}(S_{T})}+\|\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{H^{1}}+\|\phi_{0}^{\prime}\|_{H^{2}}.

Now we begin to construct strong solutions to the linearized system (
3.2
), and prove the corresponding operator is isomorphism between the proper spaces. By means of the contraction mapping principle, the proof of uniqueness and existence can be obtained for a small time

Proof.

Firstly, we frequently use the following estimate

\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C\|\phi\|_{H^{1}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C\|\phi\|_{X_{T}^{2}},

,

, the following result also holds if

\displaystyle\|f(\phi_{1})-f(\phi_{2})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\leq\|f(\phi_{1})-f(\phi_{2})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C\|\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}\|_{X_{T}^{2}}.

(3.4)

We divide the proof of this proposition into three parts to proceed.

Estimation of

Starting with Hölder’s inequality, using Lemma

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}_{1}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{u}_{2}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}_{2}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{u}_{1}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{u}_{2}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}_{1}+\mathbf{u}_{2}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{u}_{2}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}_{2}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{u}_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{6}(\Omega))}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{1}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{3}(\Omega))}+\|\mathbf{u}_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{6}(\Omega))}{\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{1}-\nabla\mathbf{u}_{2}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{3}(\Omega))}}

\displaystyle\leq CT^{\frac{1}{8}}\|\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{u}_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{1}\|_{L^{4}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{1}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{6}(\Omega))}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+CT^{\frac{1}{4}}\|\mathbf{u}_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{1}-\nabla\mathbf{u}_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{1}-\nabla\mathbf{u}_{2}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{6}(\Omega))}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C(R)T^{\frac{1}{4}}\|\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{u}_{2}\|_{X_{T}^{1}}.

Next, by Remark
3.1
and Lemma

, we can show

\displaystyle\quad\|\tfrac{1}{\rho(\phi_{1})}\tfrac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\nabla G(\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}_{1})-\tfrac{1}{\rho(\phi_{2})}\tfrac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\nabla G(\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}_{2})\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\quad\leq CT^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\tfrac{1}{\rho(\phi_{1})}-\tfrac{1}{\rho(\phi_{2})}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{6}(\Omega))}\|\nabla G(\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}_{1})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{3}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\qquad+CT^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\tfrac{1}{\rho(\phi_{2})}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{6}(\Omega))}\|\nabla G(\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}_{1})-\nabla G(\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}_{2})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{3}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\quad\leq C(R)T^{\frac{1}{2}}(\|\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}\|_{X_{T}^{2}}+\|\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{u}_{2}\|_{X_{T}^{1}}).

By Remark
3.1
and Lemma
B.4
,
one similarly deduces

\displaystyle\|\tfrac{1}{\rho(\phi_{1})}(\phi_{1}-\tfrac{1}{\alpha})\nabla(f(\phi_{1}))-\tfrac{1}{\rho(\phi_{2})}(\phi_{2}-\tfrac{1}{\alpha})\nabla(f(\phi_{2}))\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle=\|\tfrac{1}{\rho(\phi_{1})}(\phi_{1}-\tfrac{1}{\alpha})\nabla(f(\phi_{1}))-\tfrac{1}{\rho(\phi_{2})}(\phi_{1}-\tfrac{1}{\alpha})\nabla(f(\phi_{1}))

\displaystyle\quad+\tfrac{1}{\rho(\phi_{2})}(\phi_{1}-\tfrac{1}{\alpha})\nabla(f(\phi_{1}))-\tfrac{1}{\rho(\phi_{2})}(\phi_{2}-\tfrac{1}{\alpha})\nabla(f(\phi_{1}))

\displaystyle\quad+\|\tfrac{1}{\rho(\phi_{2})}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\|(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\|\nabla(f(\phi_{1}))\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}
