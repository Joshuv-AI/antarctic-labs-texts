An evaluation of peripheral nerve damage that may develop following intraneural bupivacaine, dexmedetomidine, and lidocaine injections: multifaceted analysis in an experimental model
Authors: İrem ATEŞ, Esra Laloğlu, Serkan Yildirim, Şeyma Çoruh, Basri Pür, Sevilay Özmen, Nergis Ulaş, Metin Ki̇li̇çli̇oğlu, Emir Şahan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the histopathological, electrophysiological, functional, biochemical, and inflammatory changes occurring in peripheral nerves following erroneous intraneural injections of bupivacaine, dexmedetomidine, and lidocaine, and to compare the neurotoxic potentials of these drugs.MethodsThirty-two adult male Sprague-Dawley rats were divided into four groups. The first received intraneural injection of 0.2 ml saline solution to the sciatic nerve. The second received intraneural injection 0.2 ml of 0.5% bupivacaine, the third 20 µg/kg dexmedetomidine diluted to 0.2 ml with normal saline, and the fourth 0.2 ml of 2% lidocaine, all via a 30gauge tuberculin syringe. Sciatic functional index (SFI) and electromyography (EMG) measurements were performed on all groups on days 1, 7, and 14 postoperatively. On the 14th day, the rats were sacrificed by the intraperitoneal administration of high-dose 50 mg/kg sodium thiopental. Following intracardiac blood collection for biochemical analysis, the nerve tissues were removed for histomorphological, immunohistochemical, immunofluorescence examination.ResultsMultimodal analyses revealed that the intraneural injection of bupivacaine, lidocaine, and dexmedetomidine caused varying degrees of nerve damage. Bupivacaine and lidocaine exhibited similar effects in terms of electrophysiological and motor function loss, and bupivacaine caused greater inflammation than lidocaine. Considering the entirety of the electrophysiological, histomorphological, immunohistochemical, and immunofluorescence evaluations, dexmedetomidine emerged as the least neurotoxic and most regenerative anesthetic agent.ConclusionAll intraneural injections can cause varying degrees of nerve damage. Dexmedetomidine showed the least damage compared with bupivacaine and lidocaine within 14 days. Dexmedetomidine may be a safer option as an adjuvant agent.Keywords: Intraneural injection, Bupivacaine, Lidocaine, Dexmedetomidine, Experimental study, Neuroprotection

Introduction

Regional anesthesia, and especially peripheral nerve block (PNB) techniques, is a reliable option in terms of both perioperative anesthesia and postoperative analgesia. Rapid advances in ultrasonography (USG) technology are causing further improvements in the safety and effectiveness of PNB, and permit simultaneous visualization of the target nerve, the position of the block needle, the spread of local anesthetic (LA), and the surrounding anatomical structures [1, 2]. The anesthetic agent needs to be applied as closely as possible to the nerve. However, even the most experienced anesthetists can accidentally inject LA intraneurally and give rise to peripheral nerve damage. While these injuries usually consist of reversible, asymptomatic, subclinical or mild mononeuropathies, they can sometimes result in severe neuropathic pain and neurological sequelae. Neuropathic pain is difficult to treat, and the results are generally unsatisfactory [3]. The histopathological and immunohistochemical examination of peripheral nerves following intraneural injections in humans is not possible. This has increased the need for animal experiments to shows sciatic nerve injury following intraneural injection [4].

The intraneural administration of LAs not only constitutes a risk of mechanical injury, but also exposes the nerves to higher concentrations of LA and thus increases the neurotoxic effects associated with the anesthetic applied. LAs block neuronal voltage gated sodium channels in a reversible manner, thus reversibly blocking nerve impulse propagation [5]. All LAs cause direct chemical nerve damage in a dose-dependent manner [6]. Bupivacaine is an amide-type LA that has been widely used for nerve blockage for many years. Toxic effects on neurons have been observed [7]. Lidocaine is another amide-type LA frequently employed for regional anesthesia and nerve blocks. It is frequently used as an anesthetic agent in regional anesthesia due to its anti-arrhythmic and postoperative analgesic effects and, like all other LAs, it also possesses anti-inflammatory characteristics [8, 9]. Dexmedetomidine is an α2 adrenergic agonist with analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects, and is a popular drug used as an adjuvant in combination with LAs in PNB [10].

The purpose of this experimental study was to evaluate and compare potential motor dysfunction, histopathological changes, and inflammation in the peripheral nerve resulting from the intraneural injection of bupivacaine, lidocaine, and dexmedetomidine as a complication of PNB.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and consent to participation

The animal experiments and procedures were compatible with the national guidelines regarding the use and care of laboratory animals. The study was approved by the local animal experimentation ethics committee of Atatürk University (HADYEK no. E-42190979-050.01.04-2300142218, dated 2023-05-03). It also complied with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Study design and setting

Thirty-two male Sprague-Dawley aged 2–3 months and weighing 200–250 g were used in the study. Animals were provided by the Atatürk University Medical Experimental Application and Research Center (ATADEM), and the experiment was conducted in the center’s laboratory. All animal procedures and experiments were performed according to national guidelines for laboratory animals’ use and care. These were randomized to ensure that the average weights of all groups would be similar to one other. The animals were housed in cages containing eight animals each, with ad libitum access to chow and water. The room was maintained at 18–210 C and 40–50% humidity, with a 12 h–12 h day-night lighting cycle. The animals were randomly assigned to four equal groups, none being excluded from the analysis.

Group 1(Control):0.2 ml 0.9% saline.

Group 2: 0.2 ml of 0.5% bupivacaine.

Group 3: 0.2 ml dexmedetomidine (20 µg/kg, diluted to 0.2 ml with saline).

Group 4: 0.2 ml of 2% lidocaine.

For the surgical procedure, 30 mg/kg sodium thiopental (Ibrahim Ethem ULUGAY A.S., Istanbul, Türkiye) was first administered intraperitoneally. Following the induction of anesthesia, all rats’ right thighs were shaved and wiped with povidone iodine. The sciatic nerve was then explored under sterile conditions by dissecting the right gluteal region sciatic nerve tract between the vastus lateralis and hamstring muscles under a microscope. Following dissection, the rats in Group 1 (the control group) received intraneural 0.2 ml saline solution to the sciatic nerve, while groups 2, 3, and 4 (the drug injection groups) received intraneural injections of bupivacaine (Marcaine 0.5% vial, 5 mg/ml, AstraZeneca, Istanbul, Türkiye), dexmedetomidine (Semotidin 200 µg/2 ml, İstanbul, Türkiye), and lidocaine (Arıtmal 2%, Istanbul, Türkiye).The volumes and concentrations of the drugs administered were based on previously published reported LA studies and major PNBs [11–13]. The injections were performed using a 30gauge needle (BD PrecisionGlide, Becton-Dickinson, product no. 305106, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) with a finger depressor. The same injection method was applied by the same operator for all animals in order to minimize needle-related injury. An angle of 30° was selected for nerve puncturing and advancement. Intrafascicular injections were confirmed with the direct observation of symmetrical fusiform nerve swelling. One the intraneural injections had been completed, the layers were closed in an appropriate manner, and the wounds were disinfected and dressed with povidone iodine. High dose sodium thiopental (50 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally to sacrifice the rats on the 14th postoperative day of the study. After the animals were observed to be anesthetized and unconscious, intracardiac blood was taken for biochemical analysis and then nerve tissues were removed. All animals were euthanized in conformity with standard ethical protocols.

Electrophysiological evaluation

Electromyography (EMG) was performed on all animals on days 1, 7, and 14 after the surgical procedures. Thiopental sodium was injected via the intraperitoneal route for the purpose of ensuring minimal disruption to the EMG recordings, with all rats inhaling sevoflurane before each EMG procedure.

Electrophysiological examinations were conducted using a Cadwell Cascade Elite electroneuromonitorization device. The postoperative neuro-monitoring parameters consisted of a filtering range of 1–3 k Hz, a sweeping time of 10 ms/div, a stimulus time of 0.3 ms, and a stimulus frequency of 2.71/s. Twisted stainless steel needle electrodes with 13 mm copper wires were carefully advanced into the gastrocnemius muscle, with reference electrodes being installed in the piriformis muscle. Motor impulses were recorded by inserting the electrodes into muscles innervated by the particular nerves.

The researchers carrying out the measurements were blinded to the group assignments. Various parameters were calculated, including compound muscle action potentials deriving from sciatic nerve stimulation, the action potential transmission velocity between consecutive impulses, and changes occurring in the amplitude and area of compound action potentials. Impulse data obtained from the experimental right leg and the reference left leg were used to calculate the experimental limb/reference limb ratio. These data were also employed in subsequent statistical analyses.

Footprint analysis and functional evaluation

Sciatic functional index (SFI) analyses were conducted on postoperative days 1, 7, and 14, by means a closed racetrack with dimensions of 8.2 × 42 × 12 cm. The test involved staining the rats’ hind legs with blue ink, after which they walked along the track.

At the end of the walking test, millimetric scales were employed to yield hıghly accurate measurements based on the animals’ footprints. The parameters evaluated included the following distances:

Between the heel and the third phalanx (print length, PL).

Between the heel and the third phalanx (print length, PL).

Between the first and fifth phalanges (toe spread, TS).

Between the first and fifth phalanges (toe spread, TS).

Between the second and fourth phalanges (intermediate toe spread, IT).

Between the second and fourth phalanges (intermediate toe spread, IT).

Values were calculated for both the experimental limb (E) and for the contralateral, normal limb (N).

The SFI was calculated using the f formula.

SFI =-38.3(EPL-NPL)/NPL + 109.5(ETS-NTS)/NTS + 13.3(EIT-NIT/NIT-8.8 (E: experimental side, N: normal side).

The evaluations were scored between 0 and 100, a value of 100 indicating complete loss of nerve function, and a value of approximately 0 as representing normal nerve function [14].

Biochemistry

The biochemists responsible for calculating the measurements were blinded to the study groups. On day 14 of the study, following administration of a high dose of sodium thiopental and confirmation of anesthesia, 5 mL of intracardiac blood was collected from the rats for biochemical analysis and transferred into biochemistry tubes. Sera were obtained by centrifuging all blood samples at 4500 rpm for 7 min. The serum specimens obtained were stored at -80 C until the day of analysis. Serum interleukin-1 beta (IL-1 beta), tumor necrosis factor (TNF-α), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and ciliary neurotrophic factor (CNTF) levels were measured using the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) with Rat IL-1 beta Elisa (Cat No. E0119Ra, BTLAB, China), Rat TNF-alpha Elisa (Cat No. E0764Ra, BTLAB, China), Rat IL-10 Elisa (Cat No. E0108Ra, BTLAB, China), and Rat CNTF Elisa (Cat No. E1202Hu, BTLAB, China) kits in line with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Histopathological examination

At the conclusion of the evaluation, the collected tissue specimens were fixed for 48 h in 10% formaldehyde solution, subjected to routine tissue processing, and embedded in paraffin blocks. Section 4 μm in thickness were taken from each specimen. The preparates to be subjected to histopathological investigation were stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE) and examined under a light microscope (Olympus BX 51, Japan). The sections were evaluated on the basis of their histopathological features as none (-), mild (+), moderate (++), and severe (+++).

Immunohistochemical examination

For immunoperoxidase examination, the tissue samples were placed onto adhesive (poly-L-lysine) slides, deparaffinized, and dehydrated. Endogenous peroxidase was next inactivated in 3% H2O2 for 10 min. The tissues were then boiled in 1% antigen retrieval solution (citrate buffer (pH + 6.1) 100X) and left to cool at room temperature. The specimens were next left to incubate with protein block for 5 min in order to prevent non-specific background staining. Primary antibody, nerve growth factor (NGF), and transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) (Cat no. A281812, BS23061941) dilution ratio: 1/100, US) were then dripped onto the tissue and incubated in line with the instructions for use. 3–3’ diaminobenzidine (DAB) was used as the chromogen in the tissues. The stained sections were examined under a light microscope (Zeiss AXIO, Germany).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted on Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 25 and GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 software. Levene’s test and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test were applied to assess the homogeneity of the variances and normality of the variables, respectively. One-way ANOVA was applied for variance analysis, followed by the Tukey test as post hoc for normally distributed variables (biochemical analyses). Intergroup comparisons of non-normally distributed variables (SFI, EMG, and histopathology results) were conducted with the Kruskal‒Wallis test, followed by the Mann-Whitney U post hoc test.

Histopathological evaluation was performed using a blinded pathology method, the assessments being performed independently by two pathologists. The analysis involved five regions being identified and photographed from tissue samples from all rats. The average value of the pathological findings observed in these regions was adopted as the representative figure for each group. p values < 0.05 were regarded as significant, and the data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Discussion

The electrophysiological, functional, histopathological and biochemical analyses in this experimental study showed that all three drugs caused some degree of neurotoxicity when administered intraneurally. However, significant differences were observed between them in terms of severity and regenerative outcomes.

The use of USG-guided PNB to provide analgesia and anesthesia is considered safer than blind techniques based on anatomical points, such as paresthesia and neurostimulation, which were performed until recently, although various complications can still occur. Iatrogenic intraneural injections are one such complication [4]. The mechanism of the damage that develops following intraneural injections, the scope of the sequelae, and the incidence are still not fully understood [15]. They can sometimes lead to no damage, or sometimes to permanent injury [16, 17]. This mechanical trauma is probably affected by numerous factors, including the nerve anatomy, the shape and size of the block needle, injection pressure, and chemical and neurotoxic effects of the anesthetics, the dose of anesthetic applied, and the experience of the anesthetist [5]. The damages observed in the saline injection group in our study, although not very significant, are likely related to mechanical trauma from needle insertion or local pressure generated during injection rather than chemical injury.

Local anesthetics are known to be neurotoxic. However, there are inconsistencies among studies concerning which exhibits the greatest neurotoxic effect. Different published studies have reported differing LA toxicity rankings. While some have suggested that lidocaine is more neurotoxic than bupivacaine at equipotent concentrations, others have proposed that bupivacaine causes greater neuronal damage than lidocaine [5, 18–20]. However, others have reported no difference between LAs in terms of toxicity, and the subject is therefore still controversial [21, 22]. In the present multi-faceted study, the two LAs’ neurotoxic effects were close to one another, and dexmedetomidine was less harmful than both those two LAs. There are studies showing that dexmedetomidine exhibits a neuroprotective effect, rather than a neurotoxic one like those two LAs, and that it even ameliorates nerve damage and inflammation caused by bupivacaine and lidocaine [6, 23, 24].

The essential aim in peripheral nerve regeneration is functional recovery. One widely used method for evaluating such recovery is to evaluate a mouse’s walking pattern by recording its footprints [25]. The EMG and SFI tests performed up to postoperative day 14 for the assessment of functional damage revealed a faster and more effective nerve recovery process in the dexmedetomidine group than in the other injected groups. Bupivacaine and lidocaine caused more pronounced functional impairment, but gradually exhibited similar levels of recovery.

Neurotrophic factors are a family of polypeptides that neurons require for survival and that constitute the most important stimulators of axon growth and regeneration. CNTF, subjected to biochemical analysis in this study, is an important member of this family, with neuroprotective effects [26]. One striking finding of this study was the absence of a significant increase in CNTF levels in any of the intraneural drug injection groups. This suggests the presence of some degree of regenerative activity in nerve repair, irrespective of the agent employed post-injury. Two other markers that shape the regenerative process by exhibiting neuroprotective effects are TGF-β and NGF [27, 28]. The present study shows some degree of nerve damage in the drug-injected groups, and that a regenerative process commenced at the same time in all three groups with NGF and TGF-β expression. However, the neurotrophic factor expression observed in the dexmedetomidine group shows that functional and electrophysiological recovery and regenerative signals may be more effective in this group. Alpha-2 adrenergic agonism can reduce excitotoxicity and increase neurotrophic factor expression, and this may account for the regenerative effects observed in the dexmedetomidine group [29].

A powerful inflammatory response develops following nerve trauma. TNF-α and IL-1β are known mediators of neuroinflammation and proinflammatory cytokines associated with the degree of neuronal damage. LA allow a decrease in the release of TNF-α through their potent anti-inflammatory properties [30]. Serum TNF-α and IL-1β levels at the end of this study were significantly higher in all the injection groups than in the control group. The highest cytokine levels were observed in the bupivacaine group, and the lowest in the dexmedetomidine group. Levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 were also lower in the dexmedetomidine group. Similarly to LAs, dexmedetomidine is also an anti-inflammatory drug, and appeared to be more effective in this study. These findings are compatible with previous studies emphasizing the anti-inflammatory characteristics of dexmedetomidine when employed as an adjuvant in PNB [31, 32].

Significant histopathological changes occur both proximal and distal to the affected region and with neuroinflammation. Degenerative changes encapsulating morphological, chemical, and functional alterations following nerve fiber damage are collectively known as WD [33]. The histopathological analysis in this study included WD, necrosis, and inflammation. Our histopathological findings were consistent with the neurotoxic profiles of the anesthetic drugs. Similarly to the current research, Sen et al. reported that only axonal damage was histopathologically more severe in their lidocaine group compared to a bupivacaine group when the two were injected intraneurally [11]. Faber et al. examined sciatic nerves under an electron microscope following intraneural injection and reported that bupivacaine caused greater damage than lidocaine [20]. Wiesmann et al. investigated whether the intraneural injection of a test dose of bupivacaine with a crystalloid solution was capable of causing histological changes. Those authors reported mild myelin changes and mild inflammation in all nerve specimens following intraneural injection compared to a control group, irrespective of the substance administered [34]. The more pronounced nerve damage in the present study compared to that research may be due to our use of greater amounts of LA.

This study has a number of limitations. First, although rats represent a useful model for examining nerve injury, their anatomical and physiological characteristics differ from those of humans, and we are therefore unable to draw any clinical conclusions. Second, only a single dose and concentration were examined for each drug. Therefore, further long-term studies are needed to investigate drug response relationships and observe late inflammation. The baseline values of EMG and SFI analyses before injection could have been recorded and a sham group could have been formed without injection. Whether intraneural injection can also lead to neuropathic might also have been evaluated within the scope of the study.

In conclusion, this study shows that the intraneural injection of bupivacaine, lidocaine, and dexmedetomidine causes varying degrees of nerve damage, and the dexmedetomidine is the least neurotoxic and most regenerative of the three. These findings suggest that dexmedetomidine could represent a safe alternative as an adjuvant. Another important characteristic of dexmedetomidine, its analgesic effects, should also not be ignored. More clinical and experimental data are now needed in order for future research to demonstrate the long-term effects of dexmedetomidine in peripheral nerve block techniques.
