DADA-EV: domain-adaptive diffusion autoencoder for estimating tissue- and cell-type-specific origin in extracellular vesicle transcriptomes
Authors: Shuilin Liao, Haoxiang Yang, Shuting Xiao, Zhiwen Li, Qi Zhu, Le Li, Shanghui Lu, Yong Liang, Ming Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTracing the tissue and cell-type origins of extracellular vesicles (EVs) in blood is critical for liquid biopsy and precision medicine, yet existing deconvolution methods remain limited by the need for labor-intensive reference signatures and poor adaptability to distribution shifts between tissue/cell-type datasets and EV transcriptomes. We introduce DADA-EV (Domain-Adaptive Diffusion Autoencoder for EVs), a hybrid deep learning framework that combines an autoencoder backbone with a generative simulation module and adversarial domain adaptation. DADA-EV features three key innovations: (1) a reference-free design that eliminates reliance on predefined signatures; (2) cross-domain generalization by aligning feature distributions between source (tissue/cell-type) and target (EV) domain; and (3) reduced dependence on source data during target-domain training. Extensive evaluations on pseudo-EV data show that DADA-EV consistently outperforms existing approaches, yielding accurate fraction estimates across diverse tissues and gene sets. Validation using in vitro cell-line mixtures further confirms its reliability in resolving complex compositions, demonstrating high sensitivity in detecting low-abundance targets. Applied to real EV transcriptomes, it reveals tissue- and cell-type heterogeneity across patient groups. In summary, DADA-EV provides a robust, reference-free, and generalizable solution for EV origin tracing, with strong potential to advance diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment monitoring via liquid biopsy.

Introduction

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are nanoscale, membrane-bound particles secreted by most eukaryotic cells, carrying diverse biomolecules such as proteins, lipids, metabolites, and RNAs [1–3]. They mediate intercellular communication and regulate processes ranging from immune responses to tumor progression [4]. Plasma-derived EVs, in particular, encapsulate signals from diverse tissues, thereby reflecting the body’s physiological or pathological state [5–7]. This makes EVs a promising, minimally invasive source for molecular profiling in precision medicine, where tracing their tissue or cell origins could aid diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment monitoring [8, 9].

In the broader field of transcriptomics, computational deconvolution has evolved significantly to resolve cellular heterogeneity [10–14]. Mainstream approaches predominantly rely on machine learning (ML) algorithms, such as Support Vector Regression (e.g., CIBERSORT [10]) and non-negative least squares (e.g., MuSiC [15]). These methods operate on the core concept that bulk gene expression is a linear combination of cell-type-specific signatures, requiring curated marker gene lists or reference matrices to utilize regression models for estimating cell proportions. While effective for tissues, their accuracy is highly dependent on the quality and completeness of the reference signature matrix [16]. To overcome the reliance on static references, deep learning (DL) strategies have recently gained traction. Methods like Scaden [17] and TAPE [15] employ deep neural networks and autoencoder architectures to infer cell fractions. By training on large-scale simulated datasets, these reference-free approaches can capture complex, non-linear relationships within gene expression data, offering improved robustness over traditional linear models.

Despite these advancements, few deconvolution methods are specifically tailored for EVs, and directly applying existing tissue-based algorithms presents significant challenges. Among the limited EV-specific efforts, EV-origin [5] represents a pioneering approach. However, it remains a reference-based method that assumes a linear relationship between cellular and EV transcriptomes, leaving it vulnerable to reference bias. Conversely, while general DL methods like TAPE [15] eliminate the need for references, they fail to account for the inherent biological disparity between cellular (source) and EV (target) domains. EV transcriptomes differ substantially from their cells of origin due to selective RNA packaging, distinct RNA biotype enrichment, and variations in RNA stability [2, 18]. This results in a ‘domain shift’, a distributional difference between the training data (cell/tissue RNA-seq) and the inference data (EV transcriptomes). Current methods lack formal domain adaptation mechanisms to bridge this gap, leading to biased predictions when cell-trained models are applied to EV data.

To address these challenges, we developed Domain-Adaptive Diffusion Autoencoder for EVs (DADA-EV), a novel deconvolution framework specifically designed to bridge the gap between tissue- or cell-type data and EV transcriptomes. DADA-EV integrates a deep autoencoder backbone with a diffusion-based generative module and adversarial domain adaptation strategies. By leveraging simulated data derived from tissue and cell transcriptomes, our method effectively transfers knowledge from the cellular domain to the EV domain. The key contributions of this study are summarized as follows:

(1) Novel framework design: we propose DADA-EV, the first deep learning-based deconvolution model that explicitly incorporates domain adaptation to handle the biological discrepancy between tissue or cellular sources and EV targets.

Method

Datasets and preprocess

Data collection and domain definition

We downloaded RNA-seq data of different tissues from two data sources: the GTEx Portal (https://gtexportal.org) and the HPA (https://www.proteinatlas.org). From both datasets, we downloaded the transcript per million data. Specifically, GTEx includes data from 31 tissue types and 48 231 tissue samples. HPA includes data from 39 tissue types and 187 human tissue samples. We selected only the 23 tissue types that are common to both datasets (Table S1). In this study, we treated GTEx data as source-domain data and HPA data as target-domain data.

Two PBMC single cell (scRNA-seq) datasets were obtained from 10× Genomics (https://support.10xgenomics.com/single-cell-gene-expression/datasets), where they were identified as ‘6k PBMCs from a Healthy Donor’ and ‘Donor C Frozen PBMCs’ , respectively. Here, we used the 6 k PBMCs dataset as the source-domain data and the Donor C dataset as the target-domain data.

Real plasma EV RNA-seq data were obtained from exoRBase 2.0 (http://www.exoRBase.org) [3]. Five categories were included, each with more than 100 samples. In total, 664 samples were selected for deconvolution (Table S2), specifically including 118 samples for healthy state (Healthy), 130 samples for benign disease (Benign), 140 samples for breast cancer (BRCA), 112 samples for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and 164 samples for pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD). In relation to the source-domain data used for training, the real EV data for inferring was considered as the target-domain data.

(c) Signal-dependent Dropout (Zero-Inflation)

Due to the low abundance, limited copy numbers, and heterogeneous distribution of nucleic acid targets within EVs, coupled with the limited sensitivity of isolation and detection platforms, some genuine molecules may fall below the limit of detection [23]. This results in the ‘dropout’ phenomenon, where genes are present but fail to be detected. Similar zero-inflation phenomena have been extensively studied in single-cell RNA-seq data [24–26].

Rather than assuming random or uniform dropout, previous studies have shown that dropout events arise from a combination of low transcript abundance and limited capture efficiency, leading to a strong dependence between detection probability and underlying expression level. In particular, genes with low expression are more likely to be missed during reverse transcription and amplification, resulting in higher dropout rates. This motivates the use of a signal-dependent probabilistic model in our study.

Data preprocessing and normalization

To ensure optimal performance and convergence of the deep learning model, we implemented a standardized preprocessing pipeline adapted from the TAPE framework [15]. First, we addressed the high dynamic range of transcriptomic data by performing a log-transformation. Specifically, we calculated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\mathit{\log}}_2\left(x+1\right)$\end{document} for every entry \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $x$\end{document} in the expression matrix (for both simulated and real bulk data). This transformation stabilizes variance and prevents numerical issues associated with zero values.

Subsequently, to align the feature distributions and facilitate gradient descent during training, we applied feature scaling. We normalized the expression data linearly into the range [0, 1]. This step prevents genes with inherently high absolute expression levels from dominating the loss function. It is important to note that identical preprocessing steps were applied to the source-domain (simulated) and target-domain (real) data to maintain consistency for the domain adaptation task.

Architecture of DADA-EV

Inspired by the explicit mitigation of distribution shifts in the scpDeconv model [27], we proposed DADA-EV, a domain-adaptive deep learning framework designed to explicitly mitigate distribution shifts in deconvolution without relying on original reference data during the adaptation phase. The overall model architecture is shown in Fig. 1. It contains three stages: (1) Pre-training for source-domain; (2) Domain-adversarial training and deconvolution; (3) Inference for target-domain.

The overall architecture of DADA-EV. DADA-EV contains three stages: (1) Pre-training for source-domain, where the model learns latent representations from source-domain pseudo-EV data, jointly optimizing reconstruction and fraction prediction; a diffusion generator is trained to produce source-like embeddings from noise. (2) Domain-adversarial training and deconvolution, where source and target distributions are aligned via a discriminator with gradient reversal. (3) Inference for target-domain, where the trained encoder, decoder, and predictor are applied to target-domain pseudo-EV or real EV data to reconstruct expression profiles and estimate proportions.

We adopt a Residual Network (ResNet) backbone to effectively capture complex data distributions. The main framework of the diffusion generator is shown in Fig. S1. The goal is to learn how to reverse the corruption process caused by adding noise over time, using a framework adapted from the diffusion probabilistic model [29]. Module network structure

The detailed architecture and hyperparameters for all modules are summarized in Table S3.

The encoder maps the high-dimensional gene expression input \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{in}}$\end{document} into a low-dimensional latent embedding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{256}$\end{document}. It consists of three fully connected (FC) layers. The first layer maps input to 1024 dimensions followed by batch normalization and SiLU activation. The second layer compresses features to 512 dimensions, utilizing layer normalization, SiLU activation, and dropout for regularization. A final linear layer maps the features to the latent dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${d}_z=256$\end{document}.

The first layer maps the latent features to 512 dimensions followed by CELU activation. The second layer expands features to 1024 dimensions, utilizing CELU activation. The final linear layer projects the features back to the original gene expression input dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${d}_{in}$\end{document} for reconstruction.

The predictor operates on the latent vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $z$\end{document} to estimate cell type fractions. It is composed of three FC layers: the first maps the latent input to 128 dimensions, and the second compresses it to 64 dimensions. Both layers utilize CELU activation and dropout for regularization. The final linear layer projects the features to an output dimension corresponding to the number of tissue or cell types. The raw output is subsequently processed by ReLU activation and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${L}_1$\end{document} normalization to ensure the predicted fractions are non-negative and sum to one.

To align the feature distributions across domains, the discriminator takes the latent vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $z$\end{document} as input. It comprises three FC layers. The first layer maps the latent input to 128 dimensions, followed by CELU activation and dropout. The second layer compresses the features to 64 dimensions, utilizing CELU activation and dropout. The final linear layer yields a scalar output, which is passed through a sigmoid activation function to predict the probability of the domain source.

The noise prediction network \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\epsilon}_{\theta }$\end{document}within diffusion generator module estimates the noise component added to the latent data, conditioning on the noisy state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${h}_k$\end{document} and the timestep \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $k$\end{document}. The architecture incorporates a time embedding module and a residual backbone. First, the timestep \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $k$\end{document} is mapped to sinusoidal positional encodings (128 dimensions) and processed by a multi-layer perceptron consisting of a linear layer, SiLU activation, and a second linear layer to produce a 256-dimensional time embedding. In the main branch, the noisy input \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${h}_k$\end{document}is projected to a 512-dimensional feature space via an initial linear layer. This is followed by three stacked time-aware residual blocks, which process the features while integrating the time embedding to modulate the signal. The final linear layer projects the 512-dimensional features back to the original latent dimension to yield the predicted noise.

In vitro controlled EV mixture experiments

To establish in vitro benchmarks with ground-truth compositions, we generated synthetic EV mixtures derived from a diverse panel of human cell lines. This panel included pancreatic ductal epithelial cells (hTERT-HPNE), normal liver cells (L02), human embryonic kidney cells (293 T), and bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS-2B). Detailed procedures regarding vitro cell culture, the isolation of EVs from both cell culture and sample mixing are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

EVs were individually isolated from the conditioned media of each cell line and quantified using Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis. To create defined mixtures, purified EVs were combined based on strict particle count ratios. We designed three distinct mixtures to simulate various biological scenarios. Mixed EV samples were then subjected to RNA extraction and transcriptome sequencing (see Supplementary Methods).

Results

Validation of the diffusion module performance using simulated cross-database scenarios

We first analyzed the distributional characteristics of real EV expression data, observing that it follows a long-tailed distribution heavily populated with zero values (zero-inflated, Fig. S2A). However, given the lack of ground truth regarding tissue/cell proportions in real EV data, we leveraged two external databases (GTEx and HPA) to simulate a domain adaptation task. Specifically, one dataset served as the source data and the other as the target data to mimic the process of a model trained on simulated data (source) predicting real EV data (target) (Fig. S2B).

Firstly, to evaluate the robustness of our simulation framework, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by systematically varying key parameters, including the variance of selective packaging \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\sigma}_{pack}$\end{document}, the degree of overdispersion \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${k}_{disp}$\end{document}, and the dropout strength \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\lambda$\end{document}. The distributional similarity between simulated and real EV data was quantified using the Wasserstein distance (WD), where smaller WD values indicate higher similarity (Fig. S2C).

The parameter ranges were set as follows: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\sigma}_{pack}=\{0.4,0.6,0.8,1.2, 1.6\}$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${k}_{disp}=\left\{0.6,0.8,1.5,2.5,2.7\right\}$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\lambda =\left\{0.5,0.8,1.5,2,2.5\right\}$\end{document}. Our results revealed the following trends: (i) Larger \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\sigma}_{\mathrm{pack}}$\end{document} values yielded smaller WD, indicating greater similarity to real data. This suggests that higher inter-gene variability more closely recapitulates the heterogeneity observed in real EV expression profiles. (ii) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${k}_{\mathrm{disp}}$\end{document} controls the global dispersion and tail behavior of the expression distribution. Larger \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${k}_{\mathrm{disp}}$\end{document} values led to larger WD (poorer similarity), whereas smaller \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${k}_{\mathrm{disp}}$\end{document} values generated heavier-tailed distributions that better matched real EV data. This indicates that simulating heavy-tailed expression distributions is critical for faithfully reproducing real data characteristics. (iii) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\lambda$\end{document} determines the sparsity and zero-inflation level of the simulated data. Larger \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\lambda$\end{document} values resulted in smaller WD, indicating that stronger zero inflation improves similarity to real EV data. Notably, when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\lambda$\end{document} increased from 1.0 to 1.5, WD exhibited a sharp decline, suggesting the existence of a critical threshold beyond which zero inflation becomes essential for capturing the sparsity pattern of real EV data.

Using this simulation pipeline, we adopted two distinct strategies to generate datasets representing different distributional characteristics: (i) Zero-inflated / Long-tailed Distribution (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\sigma}_{pack}$\end{document}=1.2, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${k}_{disp}$\end{document}=0.8, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\lambda =2$\end{document}): This configuration mimics high-noise data characterized by severe sparsity and heavy skewness. (ii) Gaussian Distribution (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\sigma}_{pack}$\end{document}=0.6, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${k}_{disp}$\end{document}=2.5, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\lambda =0.8$\end{document}): This configuration represents a more centered, symmetric distribution with moderate noise.

This process yields synthetic data that retains the ground-truth tissue- or cell-type proportions while introducing the distributional shifts, sparsity, and non-linear distortions characteristic of real EV transcriptomics.

We designed three simulation scenarios (Fig. S2D): (1) Scene 1, where real EV data follows a zero-heavy long-tailed distribution, while both GTEx pseudo-EV and HPA pseudo-EV follow normal distributions; (2) Scene 2, where real EV and HPA pseudo-EV follow zero-heavy long-tailed distributions, while GTEx pseudo-EV follows a normal distribution; and (3) Scene 3, where real EV, GTEx pseudo-EV, and HPA pseudo-EV all exhibit zero-heavy long-tailed distributions.

To evaluate the performance of the data generated by the diffusion generator module across these distinct distributions, we calculated the WD between the generated samples and the original data samples. We compared our approach against two simpler latent modeling techniques: Simple Gaussian and Gaussian Mixture Model. The results showed that, whether dealing with normal distributions or more complex long-tailed distributions, the diffusion method achieved smaller distances compared to the other two methods (Fig. S2E), demonstrating the superior capability of the diffusion model in accurately capturing and reconstructing complex data distributions.

Furthermore, among the three constructed scenarios, we found that prediction performance was highest in Scene 1 and lowest in Scene 3 (Fig. S2F). Notably, across all three scenarios, the performance of the diffusion model remained consistent with baselines using original data as input. In contrast, the performance of the alternative methods degraded significantly in the complex Scene 3. These findings further demonstrate the superiority and robustness of using diffusion to implement source-free domain adaptation.

On the HPA set, we further compared the full DADA-EV model with a variant lacking the diffusion generator (DADA_noDF), in which stage 2 embeddings were obtained directly from source-domain expression data via the encoder (see Methods). Cross-domain evaluation showed that Stage1AE and SIG suffered substantial performance loss, whereas DADA-EV and DADA_noDF maintained performance at the same level as in the source domain, achieving higher CCC and lower MAE (Fig. 2B). Remarkably, DADA-EV achieved an average MAE of 0.02 and CCC of 0.83 for fraction prediction, comparable to DADA_noDF (MAE 0.02, CCC 0.81), demonstrating that diffusion-generated embeddings are essentially indistinguishable in effectiveness from encoder-derived source embeddings.

To assess the impact of domain adaptation at specific tissue level, we compared the deconvolution performance of the four methods across 23 tissues. The largest performance differences were observed in the Small Intestine, Skin, Prostate, and Pancreas (Fig. 2C).

As shown in Fig. 2D, the WD decreased (e.g., from 0.838 to 0.541), indicating that the domain shift was effectively mitigated. This result is further corroborated by t-SNE visualization, which shows that the initially disjoint source and target embeddings became well-aligned and overlapped following our GRL-based adaptation. Together, these metrics validate the effectiveness of our proposed strategy.

To rigorously validate the superiority of our approach against established baselines, we compared DADA-EV with three representative domain adaptation techniques: DeepCORAL [30], CoGAN [31], and ADDA [32]. As illustrated in Fig. 2E, DADA-EV consistently outperformed all baselines, followed by DeepCORAL and ADDA, while CoGAN exhibited the lowest performance. However, its poor deconvolution performance suggests that such excessive alignment forced distinct tissue-type features to collapse, leading to negative transfer. Conversely, ADDA showed limited alignment capabilities. DADA-EV achieved a balanced reduction in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $Diff\_ WD$\end{document} while maintaining the highest prediction accuracy. This indicates that by integrating adversarial learning with reconstruction-based regularization, our strategy successfully mitigates domain shifts while preserving the intrinsic biological manifold structure required for accurate deconvolution.

DADA-EV outperforms existing deconvolution methods

To assess the efficacy of our approach, we performed a comprehensive comparison between DADA-EV and other established deconvolution methods: TAPE [15], Scaden [17], EV-origin [5], CIBERSORT [10], Bisque [12], DeconRNASeq [13], MuSiC [14], and DWLS [33], and DestVI [34] (Supplementary Methods). To ensure a fair and consistent comparison, we unified the input feature space by constructing a pre-selected gene expression profile (GEP) using the TSS-DEP-MCN approach (Supplementary Methods) [5, 35]. These selected genes served as input features for DADA-EV and as the signature matrix for all competing methods.

Before evaluating deconvolution performance, we verified whether DADA-EV captures biologically meaningful tissue signatures. For the source domain data prior to domain adaptation, we utilized the latent space obtained from the autoencoder. Specifically, we derived a signature matrix directly from the Stage1AE and compared it with the manual constructed tissue-specific gene expression profile via TSS-DEP-MCN method (referred to as the manual method). This manual benchmark was rigorously created using tissue specificity scoring and condition number optimization (detailed in Supplementary Methods). Visually, the autoencoder-derived matrix exhibited distinct tissue-specific patterns highly similar to the manual baseline (Fig. S3A). Quantitatively, correlation analysis confirmed a strong correspondence between the learned signatures and the manual profiles (Fig. S3B). This suggests that baseline autoencoder effectively captures intrinsic tissue identity with higher specificity. Building on these robust tissue representations, our model excelled in the source domain validation: Stage1AE achieved the lowest MAE (average 0.011) and the highest CCC (average 0.916) on the domain-free GTEx test data (source domain data), surpassing the second-best method, Scaden (Fig. 3A, Table S4).

When introducing domain shifts (HPA data), the performance of most methods declined. Notably, DADA-EV achieved the most substantial gains in this cross-domain scenario, significantly outperforming all competitors. Its CCC on the HPA dataset (0.751) exceeded that of Scaden (0.649) by a margin of 0.1 (Fig. 3B, Table S4), demonstrating that DADA-EV delivers state-of-the-art (SOTA) accuracy in handling distributional shifts.

To further dissect these results, we conducted a stratified analysis of estimation accuracy across individual tissue. DADA-EV consistently achieved higher CCC scores across the majority of tissue types in both datasets (Fig. 3C). Crucially, breakdown analysis reveals that DADA-EV excels in identifying tissues where other methods struggle, such as Adipose Tissue and Cervix Uteri in both GTEx and HPA, and Ovary in HPA. This highlights the model’s versatility: it maintains high specificity for major, complex contributors (e.g., Adipose Tissue) while simultaneously exhibiting the sensitivity required to detect minority contributors whose signals are typically masked in bulk mixtures.

Accurate deconvolution across diverse gene variance levels and tissue contexts

To evaluate the effectiveness and reliability of DADA-EV under varying conditions, we systematically assessed its performance across different gene variance levels, gene set sizes, numbers of tissue types, and ratio configurations. This comprehensive analysis enabled us to probe the robustness and adaptability of the model under diverse simulated scenarios.

Under various conditions of gene variance levels and gene set sizes (Fig. S4, see Supplementary Methods), DADA-EV consistently demonstrated strong deconvolution performance in domain adaptation (Fig. 4A). Across different variance levels and gene set sizes, the model achieved an overall mean MAE of 0.03 and CCC of 0.96 for reconstructed expression profiles, and a mean MAE of 0.02 and CCC of 0.76 for fraction prediction. Notably, genes with low variance were predicted with the highest accuracy and reliability, as indicated by the lowest MAE and highest CCC values. Within each variance group, performance improved as the number of genes increased, with both low- and high-variance groups showing reduced MAE and increased CCC. This pattern is likely to reflect the design of DADA-EV: low-variance genes provide stable, domain-invariant signals that are easier to align in the latent space, while larger gene sets enhance the encoder’s capacity to capture discriminative features, thereby improving reconstruction and fraction estimation.

Effective deconvolution performance of DADA-EV in synthetic cell-line EV mixtures

To validate the deconvolution accuracy of DADA-EV, we conducted in vitro mixture experiments using EVs derived from four distinct cell lines representing major organs: hTERT-HPNE (pancreas), L02 (liver), 293 T (kidney), and BEAS-2B (lung). We designed three specific mixing scenarios to simulate varying biological complexities (Fig. 7A): a Balanced set (Mix1) to assess baseline identification when sources contribute equally; a Heterogeneity set (Mix2) containing low-abundance targets (~5%) to evaluate sensitivity; and a Gradient set (Mix3) with varying proportions to test the model’s dynamic quantification range.

The predicted fractions of cell-type-derived EVs are shown in Fig. 7B. Visual reconstruction of the sample compositions (as shown in the stacked bar plots, Fig. 7C) confirms that DADA-EV effectively recovers the major proportion profiles of complex samples.

We further dissected the model’s performance across specific biological scenarios (Mix1–Mix3, Fig. 7D). The model achieved its most robust performance in the Heterogeneity scenario (Mix2), which simulates rare cell population detection, with a Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) of 0.91. Notably, DADA-EV accurately resolved the low-abundance hTERT-HPNE targets (~5%) from the dominant background, confirming its high sensitivity for trace signal detection. While the Balanced (Mix1) and Gradient (Mix3) scenarios showed moderate deviation from the ground truth, this behavior is largely attributable to a systematic bias revealed in the sample reconstruction (Fig. 7E). Specifically, the model tends to overestimate 293 T-derived signals while underestimating contributions from L02 or hTERT-HPNE. This inherent bias skews the relative proportions in identical-proportional (Mix1) or gradient (Mix3) settings, yet the model remains effective at identifying the presence of distinct cellular components.

Overall, DADA-EV demonstrated strong concordance with the ground truth, achieving a PCC of 0.66 across all samples (Fig. 7F). This correlation indicates that the model reliably captures cell type-specific EV signatures within mixed populations, suggesting DADA-EV is a robust tool for dissecting cellular origins in complex EV mixtures.

Discussion and conclusion

In the development of liquid biopsy traceability models, only a few prior studies [5, 37–39] have been reported. These works mainly relied on existing algorithmic frameworks, adapting them by modifying signature gene-based matrices for EV or tissue/cell deconvolution. However, none of them made specific algorithmic adjustments to account for the differences between liquid biopsy data and tissue/cell-source data. In contrast, we developed DADA-EV, which represents a significant advancement by integrating diffusion-based generation and domain adaptation. Unlike previous methods, DADA-EV does not depend on predefined signature matrices, which is a major limitation of conventional approaches, and bridges the distributional gap between tissue/cell-type and EV transcriptomes. This enables accurate estimation of tissue- and cell-type-specific contributions and provides a generalizable strategy applicable to other cross-domain biomedical integration tasks, such as multi-omics fusion or cross-platform single-cell analysis.

Diffusion module is capable of learning the full data distribution and progressively reconstructing signals from noisy observations, making them particularly suitable for handling complex and highly heterogeneous EV transcriptomic data. Unlike conventional latent-variable models that impose simplified distributional assumptions, diffusion models can flexibly capture non-Gaussian, long-tailed, and multimodal distributions, which are commonly observed in EV-derived RNA profiles. Combined with the results of DADA-EV in Fig. S2F and Fig. 2B and the consistency with baselines using original data as input, these findings collectively demonstrate that the diffusion module successfully captures the intrinsic latent distribution of the source domain, allowing the model to discard original reference data without compromising performance. While diffusion model effectively captures complex data distributions, it cannot inherently guarantee cross-domain consistency. In our task, the discrepancy between tissue/cell-derived profiles and EV-derived profiles is not merely a simple distribution shift, but involves nonlinear distortions, signal-dependent dropout, and compositional effects. To address this, we incorporate adversarial domain adaptation to explicitly enforce alignment between tissue/cell and EV representations. The adversarial objective encourages the learned latent space to be domain-invariant, thereby enabling accurate transfer of tissue/cell-type-specific signatures across domains.

Hence, the key advantage of our design lies in the complementary roles of the two components: (i) The diffusion module models the data generation process, enabling accurate reconstruction of complex, noisy, and incomplete transcriptomic signals. (ii) The adversarial module enforces cross-domain alignment, ensuring that the learned representations are transferable between tissue and EV domains. Such a design is also supported by recent studies in other domains. For example, in computer vision, Zhang et al. [40] used diffusion to generate target-domain samples while leveraging domain classifiers for alignment. In the biomedical domain, Yang et al. [41] integrated diffusion-based generation with adversarial learning to bridge the gap between simulated and real medical images. These studies collectively suggest that combining generative modeling with adversarial alignment is particularly effective in scenarios with large domain gaps.

When using deep learning-based deconvolution models like Scaden [17] and TAPE [15], an important preprocessing step is to filter out genes with low variance, as the model may not predict fractions effectively otherwise. Models tend to perform better when the number of genes is limited to around 10,000. One likely reason for this is that less variable genes often contain a significant amount of noise. However, in our DADA-EV, we achieved good deconvolution results across varying levels of variance, whether with a smaller subset of genes or the full set. Importantly, using all genes (MAE 0.018, CCC 0.828) achieved better than gene-selected settings (MAE 0.022, CCC 0.751) while eliminating the need for labor-intensive gene selection. This flexibility enhances usability and broad applicability. Additionally, the diffusion generator by synthesizing source-like embeddings from noise, reduces dependence on reference data and enabling effective training even with scarce or low-quality references, which might be a notable advantage in contexts such as rare diseases or underrepresented tissues.

Our plasma EV analysis supports existing evidence that hematopoietic cells, particularly platelets and leukocytes, are dominant contributors to the circulating EV pool [42, 43]. Although these populations are relatively minor in single-cell datasets, DADA-EV correctly identifies them as major EV sources. Furthermore, disease-specific alterations inferred by DADA-EV align with previous reports. For example, reduced dendritic cells in breast cancer are consistent with findings that dysfunctional DCs impair immune surveillance and promote metastasis [44], and that cDC1 infiltration is preferentially enriched in triple-negative breast cancer [45]. Similarly, reduced B cells in HCC agree with evidence that B cell subsets are significantly decreased in tumor tissues compared to adjacent live [46], and that memory B cell percentages decline during HCC progression [47]. Finally, increased monocytes in pancreatic cancer reflect clinical observations linking elevated monocyte levels to tumor aggressiveness and poor prognosis [48], as well as enrichment of complement-high monocytes and tumor-associated macrophages [49]. Together, these consistencies confirm that DADA-EV not only achieves technical improvements but also yields biologically credible insights into EV origins.

In the field of liquid biopsy, several studies have explored tissue-of-origin inference using alternative omics signals. For example, transcription start site coverage patterns and methylation atlases have been successfully applied to cell-free DNA for tissue tracing [38, 50]. However, for EVs, which are membrane-bound particles carrying diverse molecular cargo including DNA, RNA, methylation signatures, and proteins, current tissue deconvolution approaches are almost exclusively based on transcriptomic data [5]. The DADA-EV framework provides a potentially generalizable strategy for cross-domain biomedical inference. Its diffusion-based generative module and domain adaptation design are, in principle, compatible with other omics layers in EVs, provided that appropriate tissue- or cell-type-specific reference atlases are available. Looking forward, we anticipate that extending DADA-EV to multi-omics settings will be particularly valuable. Integrating deconvolution results across different molecular layers may enable cross-validation and yield a more comprehensive and robust understanding of EV tissue/cell-of-origin. In addition, a promising direction is dual layer deconvolution, where DADA-EV could first resolve tissue-level origins and then further dissect the cellular composition within each tissue.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, a fundamental challenge in liquid biopsy is the lack of absolute ground truth for the tissue-of-origin composition of circulating EVs in humans. Unlike synthetic mixtures with predefined proportions, it remains technically infeasible to isolate and quantify EVs from every organ within a patient, making direct validation of model predictions impossible. Second, the current framework does not explicitly account for inter-individual variability. Differences in EV secretion patterns, physiological states, and disease contexts across individuals may introduce biases that are not fully captured by reference atlases. Third, our evaluation is based on bulk EV transcriptomic profiles, which represent averaged signals across heterogeneous EV populations. This limits the resolution of the model, as it cannot capture variability at the level of individual EVs or distinct EV subpopulations.

Looking ahead, we envision DADA-EV as a versatile tool with broad clinical applications. First, it may provide valuable insights into the diagnosis and assessment of systemic diseases. Many conditions, such as metabolic syndrome and diabetes, involve coordinated dysfunction across multiple organs rather than isolated organ pathology. By quantifying shifts in the tissue-of-origin composition of EVs, DADA-EV could reveal the relative contributions of different organs during disease progression. For example, an increase in adipose-derived EVs indicates inter-organ communication and metabolic imbalance. These EVs carry bioactive molecules that can reprogram distal tissue metabolism and promote insulin resistance and macrophage activation in obesity [51, 52]. Second, DADA-EV holds promise for evaluating metastatic potential and predicting metastatic sites in cancer. Tumor-derived EVs often prepare pre-metastatic niches in specific target organs such as lung, liver and brain [53]. Thus, an EV tissue-origin profile enriched not only for lung-derived EVs but also for liver-derived EVs in a lung cancer patient may signal heightened metastatic risk. Additionally, quantitative EV tracing could help infer the primary tumor site in cases of cancer of unknown primary. Third, DADA-EV could facilitate early monitoring and precise management of treatment. For example, Shi et al. [54] analyzed the cell types present in plasma EVs from individuals with metastatic melanoma and used this information to create a tool for tracking tumor/non-tumor ratios in response to treatment receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors. Therefore, performing transcriptome deconvolution on EVs to pinpoint the originating cell types opens a new avenue for monitoring tumor persistence and immune activation.

We anticipate that DADA-EV will provide a versatile framework for liquid biopsy, enabling systemic disease evaluation, metastasis risk prediction, and treatment monitoring. More broadly, its ability to bridge EV biology with clinical translation may help advance precision medicine.
