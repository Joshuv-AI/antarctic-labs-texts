Non-local-in-time structure formation and sixth-order galaxy bias


Abstract
We present a systematic construction of the non-local-in-time galaxy bias expansion in the Effective Field Theory of Large-Scale Structure. In order to fully capture time non-locality up to sixth order, we must take into account that every field can contribute non-locally from a separate time in the past. Starting from the general non-local-in-time expression for the galaxy overdensity, we explicitly compute the complete sixth-order basis of bias operators at leading order in spatial derivatives, finding 57 independent biases, compared to 46 in the corresponding local-in-time expansion. As previously found at fifth order, this difference implies that higher-order clustering statistics can distinguish between local- and non-local-in-time galaxy formation, and thus are sensitive, at a single redshift, to the formation time of galaxies. Along the way, we obtain recursion relations for the perturbative kernels and show that they satisfy specific multi-leg soft limits when the sum of a subset of the external momenta goes to zero.

Introduction, notation, and conclusion

In the weakly non-linear regime, correlations of density fluctuations observed in the Large-Scale Structure (LSS) of the universe can be predicted perturbatively within the framework of the Effective Field Theory (EFT) of Large-Scale Structure
[
1
,
2
]
. In this approach, the dynamics of long-wavelength perturbations are reproduced through a systematic expansion in both the amplitude of fluctuations and their spatial derivatives relative to the non-linear scale, which is roughly
10
Mpc today. The effects of short-distance physics on large-scale clustering are captured by a set of EFT coefficients, which, from the long-wavelength point of view, are a priori unknown but can be determined directly by comparison with cosmological data. Overall, this approach ensures that our theory of gravitational clustering is consistent with the basic symmetries of General Relativity and provides a controlled framework to compute large-scale observables.

A central ingredient in modeling LSS is the description of galaxies as biased tracers of the underlying dark-matter distribution (for early examples, see

Within the EFT of LSS, dark matter and baryons are treated as effective fluids whose long-wavelength densities and momenta evolve according to coarse-grained equations of motion, while the galaxy density is represented as a composite operator depending on these long-wavelength fields. This perspective leads naturally to the bias expansion, a Taylor series in dark-matter fluctuations and their spatial derivatives, capturing how galaxy formation is affected by the local gravitational environment. As discussed originally in

There is, however, no such hierarchy of scales for the time dependence: the time scale of the modes that are integrated out is about the same as the time scale of the full history of the universe. This makes galaxy clustering, and the EFT of LSS in general, local in space, but non-local in time.

This extremely powerful approach has enabled robust analyses of galaxy clustering data, with impressive results. Diffeomorphisms and the fluid trajectory

Ultimately, the equations governing our system are determined by General Relativity, and as such, they are covariant under diffeomorphisms. For the non-relativistic limit in the Newtonian approximation (which is what is relevant for galaxy clustering), a useful gauge is the so-called Newtonian gauge, where the scalar part of the metric is
5
5

5

If anisotropic stress is zero, we have
\Phi=\Psi
from the Einstein equations. Even though anisotropic stress can be generated in the EFT of LSS, it is a relativistic correction.

ds^{2}=-(1+2\Phi)dt^{2}+a(t)^{2}(1-2\Psi)d\vec{x}^{2}\ .

(2.1)

Once we are in the Newtonian gauge, there is a subset of diffeomorphisms that, up to relativistic corrections, keep us in the Newtonian gauge. These are the so-called residual large diffeomorphisms
[
56
,
57
,

,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\tilde{x}^{i}=x^{i}+n^{i}(t)\ ,\quad\tilde{t}=t+a(t)^{2}\vec{n}(t)\cdot\vec{x}\ ,\quad\tilde{v}^{i}(\tilde{x}^{j},t)=v^{i}(x^{j},t)+a(t)\dot{n}^{i}(t)\ ,\\
&\tilde{\Phi}(\tilde{x}^{i},t)=\Phi(x^{i},t)-a(t)^{2}(\ddot{n}^{i}(t)+2H(t)\dot{n}^{i}(t))x^{i}\ ,\quad\tilde{\delta}(\tilde{x}^{j},t)=\delta(x^{j},t)\ ,\end{split}

(2.2)

for a generic time-dependent
\vec{n}(t)
. The above transformations for
\delta
,
\vec{v}
, and
\Phi
can be straightforwardly derived from the usual diffeomorphism transformation rules with respect to the change of coordinates in Eq. (
2.2
). Since the transformation Eq. (
2.2
) keeps us in the Newtonian gauge, the equations of motion are invariant under Eq. (
2.2
).

We point out that Eq. (
2.2
) is not a Galilean transformation
[
59
]
(which would have
\vec{n}(t)=\vec{u}\,t
for a constant velocity
\vec{u}
). Galilean transformations are indeed a symmetry of our system, but Eq. (
2.2
) is a consequence of diffeomorphism invariance, and so is inherited from General Relativity. For brevity, we refer to Eq. (
2.2
) as an NRNGP (non-relativistic Newtonian-gauge preserving) transformation. Already just looking at the particular subset of diffeomorphisms given by Eq. (
2.2
) shows that that clustering can only depend on the velocity field with at least one spatial derivative, and the gravitational potential with at least two spatial derivatives (which of course one can deduce by looking at the full diffeomorphisms). For the consequences of the NRNGP transformation to the case of two fluids, such as dark matter and baryons, see
[
48
]
.

We can use Eq. (
2.2
) to define the transformation of a generic NRNGP scalar
\phi
as

\tilde{\phi}(\vec{x}+\vec{n}(t),t)=\phi(\vec{x},t)\ .

(2.3)

In particular, we see that
r^{ij}\propto\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\Phi
and
p^{ij}\propto\partial_{i}v^{j}
are both scalars under Eq. (
2.2
). Note that the coordinate change in
t
is almost always a relativistic correction, but is used to derive the transformation of
\Phi
, for example (which comes from the normal transformation of
g_{\mu\nu}
under diffeomorphisms). Therefore, when it is irrelevant for the non-relativistic, Newtonian limit, we will leave off the transformation of the time coordinate, as we did in Eq. (
2.3

In this work, we are building the non-local-in-time bias expansion, so our main concern is that our non-local-in-time expression for
\delta_{g}(\vec{x},t)
is a scalar under Eq. (
2.2
). To ensure this, a crucial object is the fluid trajectory which is given by

\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t)=\vec{x}+\int_{t_{\rm in}}^{t}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{a(t^{\prime})}\vec{v}\left(\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t^{\prime}),t^{\prime}\right)\ ,

(2.4)

and satisfies

\frac{d}{dt}\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t)=\frac{1}{a(t)}\vec{v}(\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t),t)\ .

(2.5)

Physically, the expression
\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t)
means that one starts at position
\vec{x}
at time
t_{\rm in}
, and then one follows the velocity field
\vec{v}
up to time
t

The fluid trajectory satisfies an intuitive composition rule

\vec{x}_{\rm fl}\left(\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t_{1}),t_{1},t\right)=\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t)\ .

(2.6)

Eq. (
2.6
) is simply the statement that if one starts at point
\vec{x}
at time
t_{\rm in}
, follows the fluid up to time
t_{1}
, and then follows the fluid from time
t_{1}
to time
t

\vec{x}
at
t_{\rm in}
directly to
t
.
In particular, by plugging
t=t_{\rm in}
in Eq. (
2.6
), we also have the inverse relationship

\vec{x}_{\rm fl}\left(\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t_{1}),t_{1},t_{\rm in}\right)=\vec{x}\ ,

(2.7)

since
\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t_{\rm in})=\vec{x}
, as can be seen in Eq. (
2.4

To rigorously prove these rules, we first look at Eq. (
2.6
), since Eq. (
2.7

2.6

2.6

. The left-hand side is
\vec{x}_{\rm fl}\left(\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t_{1}),t_{1},t_{1}\right)

\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t_{1})

t_{1}
to
t_{1}
in the definition Eq. (

\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t_{1})

. Now we show that they satisfy the same first-order differential equation. The differential equation for the right-hand side of Eq. (
2.6
) is just Eq. (
2.5
). To find the differential equation for the left-hand side of Eq. (
2.6
), we first replace

with
t_{1}
in Eq. (
2.5
), and then we plug in
\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t_{1})
for
\vec{x}
in Eq. (
2.5
). This gives

\frac{d}{dt}\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t_{1}),t_{1},t)=\frac{1}{a(t)}\vec{v}(\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t_{1}),t_{1},t),t)\ ,

which indeed is the same equation for

\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t)

Another useful property is the derivative of the fluid trajectory with respect to the starting time. Let us start with Eq. (
2.6

\vec{x}_{\rm fl}\left(\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t),t,t^{\prime}\right)=\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t^{\prime})\ .

(2.9)

Since the right-hand side is independent of the intermediate point

\frac{d}{dt}\vec{x}_{\rm fl}\left(\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t),t,t^{\prime}\right)=0\ ,

which gives

\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{y},t,t^{\prime})+\frac{d}{dt}x_{\rm fl}^{j}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t)\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{j}}\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{y},t,t^{\prime})\right]\Big|_{\vec{y}=\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t)}=0\ ,

(2.11)

and then using Eq. (
2.5
), this gives

\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{y},t,t^{\prime})+\frac{1}{a(t)}v^{j}(\vec{y},t)\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{j}}\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{y},t,t^{\prime})\right]\Big|_{\vec{y}=\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t)}=0\ .

(2.12)

Now, since we can take the initial time to be whatever we want, we can take
t_{\rm in}=t
, and then we simply have

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t,t^{\prime})+\frac{1}{a(t)}v^{j}(\vec{x},t)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t,t^{\prime})=0\ ,

(2.13)

for all

(2.14)

2.13

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt^{\prime}}f(\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t^{\prime},t),t)&=\frac{d\,x^{j}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t^{\prime},t)}{dt^{\prime}}\frac{\partial f(\vec{y},t)}{\partial y^{j}}\Bigg|_{\vec{y}=\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t^{\prime},t)}\\

(2.15)

where in the last line we used the chain rule.

We can now derive how the fluid trajectory transforms under an NRNGP transformation. After an NRNGP transformation, the new fluid trajectory
\vec{\tilde{x}}_{\rm fl}
should be the path traced by following the new velocity
\vec{\tilde{v}}
, i.e.,

\frac{d}{dt}\vec{\tilde{x}}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t)=\frac{1}{a(t)}\vec{\tilde{v}}(\vec{\tilde{x}}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t),t)\ .

(2.16)

Expressing this equation in terms of the old fields must be equivalent to the original equation Eq. (
2.5
). To start our manipulations, let us first evaluate Eq. (
2.16
) at
\vec{x}\rightarrow\vec{x}+\vec{n}(t_{\rm in})

\frac{d}{dt}\vec{\tilde{x}}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x}+\vec{n}(t_{\rm in}),t_{\rm in},t)=\frac{1}{a(t)}\vec{\tilde{v}}(\vec{\tilde{x}}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x}+\vec{n}(t_{\rm in}),t_{\rm in},t),t)\ ,

(2.17)

and then use the transformation law Eq. (
2.2
) for
\vec{v}
to get

\frac{d}{dt}\vec{\tilde{x}}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x}+\vec{n}(t_{\rm in}),t_{\rm in},t)=\frac{1}{a(t)}\left(\vec{v}(\vec{\tilde{x}}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x}+\vec{n}(t_{\rm in}),t_{\rm in},t)-\vec{n}(t),t)+a(t)\dot{\vec{n}}(t)\right)\ .

(2.18)

We want to solve for
\vec{\tilde{x}}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x}+\vec{n}(t_{\rm in}),t_{\rm in},t)
in terms of
\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t)
such that Eq. (
2.18

2.5

\vec{\tilde{x}}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t_{\rm in})=\vec{x}

\vec{\tilde{x}}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x}+\vec{n}(t_{\rm in}),t_{\rm in},t)=\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t)+\vec{n}(t)\ ,

(2.19)

which gives us the transformation law for
\vec{x}_{\rm fl}
. To see this, we first show that Eq. (
2.19
) is indeed a solution. First, plug Eq. (
2.19
) into Eq. (
2.18
) to replace
\vec{\tilde{x}}_{\rm fl}
for
\vec{x}_{\rm fl}
to get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\left[\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t)+\vec{n}(t)\right]&=\frac{1}{a(t)}\left(\vec{v}(\left[\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t)+\vec{n}(t)\right]-\vec{n}(t),t)+a(t)\dot{\vec{n}}(t)\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{a(t)}\vec{v}(\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t),t)+\dot{\vec{n}}(t)\ ,\end{split}

(2.20)

and then cancel the
\dot{\vec{n}}(t)
on both sides to recover Eq. (

) by adding an arbitrary function

which we will show must be zero. First of all, in order to have
\vec{\tilde{x}}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t_{\rm in})=\vec{x}

\vec{g}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t_{\rm in})=0
. Then, following manipulations similar to Eq. (

), we find that the equation for

\frac{d}{dt}\vec{g}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t)=\frac{1}{a(t)}\left(\vec{v}(\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t)+\vec{g}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t),t)-\vec{v}(\vec{x}_{\rm fl}(\vec{x},t_{\rm in},t),t)\right)\ ,

(2.21)

and since

, Eq. (

, so we must have

for all

at the very end, or we start with the shift

Using the above NRNGP transformation properties, we can also derive how NRNGP scalars transform when evaluated along the fluid trajectory. For an NRNGP scalar

3
Non-local-in-time structure formation

We now give a general constructive method to compute all non-local-in-time kernels for the galaxy bias. First we specialize the expansion of

8

The goal of this section is to find all of the potentially independent functional forms that can appear; later, we will explicitly solve for degeneracies of these functions at sixth order to find the minimal non-local-in-time basis (i.e. bias expansion). \left[\mathcal{O}_{m}\big|_{\vec{x}_{\rm fl}}(\vec{x},t;t_{1},\dots,t_{m})\right]^{(\ell)}
. Now, to match with Eq. (
3.15
), we want to swap the order of the sums to get

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{d}{dt}\left[\mathcal{O}_{m}\big|_{\vec{x}_{\rm fl}}(\vec{x},t;t_{1},\dots,t_{m})\right]^{(n)}\\
&\quad\quad=\frac{\dot{D}(t)}{D(t)}\sum_{\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}\}_{\leq n-1}}\left[\prod_{a=1}^{m}\left(\frac{D(t_{a})}{D(t)}\right)^{\alpha_{a}}\right]\sum_{\ell=\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{m}}^{n-1}\frac{\partial_{i}\theta^{(n-\ell)}(\vec{x},t)}{\partial^{2}}\partial_{i}\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{(\ell)}_{\mathcal{O}_{m};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}(\vec{x},t)\ .\end{split}

(3.20)

Since this has to be equal to Eq. (
3.15
) for arbitrary times
t_{a}
, we can match powers of
D(t_{a})
, which gives

\left(n-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\right)\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{(n)}_{\mathcal{O}_{m};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}(\vec{x},t)=\sum_{\ell=\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{m}}^{n-1}\frac{\partial_{i}\theta^{(n-\ell)}(\vec{x},t)}{\partial^{2}}\partial_{i}\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{(\ell)}_{\mathcal{O}_{m};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}(\vec{x},t)\ ,

(3.21)

for
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}<n
. So, all of the kernels with
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}<n
are in this way determined by lower-order kernels.

This leave us to find the kernels with
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}=n
. Looking back at the definition of the kernels Eq. (
3.10
), we see that these are actually the simplest ones to form. In Eq. (
3.10
), we sum over all the tuples
(n_{1},\dots,n_{m})
such that
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{m}=n
and
n_{a}\geq\alpha_{a}
for
a=1,\dots,m
. For the terms with
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}=n
, the only possibility is to have
n_{a}=\alpha_{a}
for
a=1,\dots,m
, so there is only one term in the sum

\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{(n)}_{\mathcal{O}_{m};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}(\vec{x},t)\Big|_{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}=n}=\Delta_{\mathcal{O}_{m}}[i_{1},j_{1},\dots,i_{m},j_{m}]\prod_{a=1}^{m}[\mathbb{C}_{(\mathcal{O}_{m},a),\alpha_{a}}^{i_{a}j_{a}}]^{(\alpha_{a})}(\vec{x},t)\ .

(3.22)

As an example of this relation, consider the operator
\mathcal{O}_{2}=r^{ij}p^{ij}
. For
n=4
, there are three terms with
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=4
, i.e.
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})=(3,1),(2,2),(1,3)
. Using Eq. (
3.22
), these are simply given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{(4)}_{[rp];3,1}=[\mathbb{C}^{ij}_{r,3}]^{(3)}[\mathbb{C}^{ji}_{p,1}]^{(1)}\ ,\quad\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{(4)}_{[rp];2,2}=[\mathbb{C}^{ij}_{r,2}]^{(2)}[\mathbb{C}^{ji}_{p,2}]^{(2)}\ ,\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{(4)}_{[rp];1,3}=[\mathbb{C}^{ij}_{r,1}]^{(1)}[\mathbb{C}^{ji}_{p,3}]^{(3)}\ .\end{split}

(3.23)

Thus, we have been lead to a much more efficient way to compute all of the perturbative kernels. For an operator
\mathcal{O}_{m}
at order
n
, all of the kernels
\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{(n)}_{\mathcal{O}_{m};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}<n
can be determined from lower order kernels using Eq. (
3.21
), and all of the kernels with
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}=n
can be formed using the simple formula Eq. (
3.22
). An additional relationship satisfied by our kernels is the equal-time completeness relation

\mathcal{O}_{m}^{(n)}(\vec{x},t)=\sum_{\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}\}_{\leq n}}\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{(n)}_{\mathcal{O}_{m};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}(\vec{x},t)\ ,

(3.24)

which can be easily obtained by setting
t_{1}=\cdots=t_{m}=t
in Eq. (
3.14
), where
\mathcal{O}_{m}^{(n)}
is the local-in-time contraction, Eq. (
3.7
), of
r^{ij}
and
p^{ij}
at
n
-th order in terms of the standard
F_{i}
and
G_{i}
kernels.

4.2
p
-particle soft limits

In this section, we show that our kernels, and ultimately the non-local-in-time galaxy overdensity
\delta_{g}
, satisfy a series of multi-leg soft limits (which we call
p
-particle soft limits) that arise when the sum of a subset of the external momenta goes to zero. These limits were pointed out in
[
51
]
, and here we explicitly show that they are satisfied by our non-local-in-time construction (in the EdS approximation with no velocity vorticity). We provide an explicit and different proof from
[
51
]
, which allows us to give a precise procedure for taking the
p
-particle soft limits.

Here we give a brief sketch of our proof, which is presented in detail in App.
D
. We find it easiest to first work at the level of the fields in position space, as opposed to the kernels in Fourier space, since we have all of the expressions explicitly in terms of
\delta
and
\theta
, as will become evident below. We start by noticing that, ultimately, the expression for the perturbations of any scalar
X
, including the galaxy overdensity
\delta_{g}
and
\delta
and
\theta
themselves, can be written as a function of the fields as

X^{(n)}\left[\partial_{i},\delta^{ij}_{K},\left\{\frac{\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\delta^{(a)}}{\partial^{2}},\frac{\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\theta^{(a)}}{\partial^{2}},\frac{\partial_{i}\theta^{(a)}}{\partial^{2}}\right\}_{a=1,\dots,n}\right]\ ,

(4.10)

as it follows from the iterative solution for the kernels.
For the fields
\delta
and
\theta
, this form is obvious from the perturbative solutions to the equations of motion Eq. (
D.2
) (although only perturbations up to
a=n-1
appear), while for general non-local-in-time scalars, it is a consequence of the fluid expansion. In terms of lower order fields, each of the building blocks of Eq. (
4.10
) are not necessarily made simply of products of the fields shown in Eq. (
4.10
), but rather there can be non-local operators acting on products of the perturbations, as in, for example

\frac{\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\delta^{(4)}}{\partial^{2}}\supset\frac{\partial_{i}\partial_{j}}{\partial^{2}}\left(\theta^{(1)}\partial_{k}\delta^{(1)}\frac{\partial_{k}\theta^{(2)}}{\partial^{2}}\right)\ .

(4.11)

The
p
-particle soft limit is roughly the limit when a sum of
p
external momenta goes to zero, i.e.
\vec{q}_{1}+\dots+\vec{q}_{p}\rightarrow 0
(although we make this more precise below). From Eq. (
4.10
), we see that in this limit, there is a special term,
\partial_{i}\theta^{(p)}/\partial^{2}
, that would appear to have a pole going like
\sim\mathcal{O}(|\vec{q}_{1;p}|^{-1})
, but because of dark-matter mass and momentum conservation, we have
\theta^{(p)}\sim\mathcal{O}(|\vec{q}_{1;p}|^{2})
, so in fact there is no actual pole. This term is still special, though, because it is the only one that is allowed to appear with a
\partial_{i}/\partial^{2}
, i.e. with only one spatial derivative above the Laplacian. So, since we have
X^{(n)}
directly in terms of the fields, we can simply explicitly track all of the terms with
\partial_{i}\theta^{(p)}/\partial^{2}
through the perturbative solution. In field theoretic language, this corresponds to taking the limit
\vec{q}_{1;p}\rightarrow 0
while at the same time keeping
\theta^{(p)}

off shell
, that is, being ignorant about the explicit solution for
\theta^{(p)}
from the equations of motion.

We can now give our definition of the
p
-particle soft limit (which we denote as
\underset{p}{\rightarrow}
) in this setup. First, we explicitly collect all of the terms with
\partial_{i}\theta^{(p)}/\partial^{2}
in Eq. (
4.10
). Then we send the total momentum of
\theta^{(p)}
to zero, which at the level of the fields means that we can pull
\partial_{i}\theta^{(p)}/\partial^{2}
out of other derivatives, for example

\partial_{j}\left(\partial_{i}\delta^{(a)}\frac{\partial_{i}\theta^{(p)}}{\partial^{2}}\right)\approx\frac{\partial_{i}\theta^{(p)}}{\partial^{2}}\partial_{j}\partial_{i}\delta^{(a)}\ .

(4.12)

In App.
D.1
, using this definition and the dark-matter equations of motion, we first show that the dark-matter fields satisfy

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\delta^{(n)}\underset{p}{\rightarrow}\frac{1}{p}\partial_{i}\delta^{(n-p)}\frac{\partial_{i}\theta^{(p)}}{\partial^{2}}\ ,\quad\text{and}\quad\theta^{(n)}\underset{p}{\rightarrow}\frac{1}{p}\partial_{i}\theta^{(n-p)}\frac{\partial_{i}\theta^{(p)}}{\partial^{2}}\ ,\end{split}

(4.13)

for
1\leq p\leq n-1
. In App.
D.2
, we use this to prove some intermediate results about the fluid expanded kernels
\mathbb{C}_{\delta,\alpha}^{(n)}
and
\mathbb{C}_{\theta,\alpha}^{(n)}
. In App.
D.3
we show that the non-local-in-time kernels that have
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}<n
satisfy

\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{(n)}_{\mathcal{O}_{m};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}\underset{p}{\rightarrow}\frac{1}{p}\partial_{i}\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{(n-p)}_{\mathcal{O}_{m};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}\frac{\partial_{i}\theta^{(p)}}{\partial^{2}}\ ,

(4.14)

for
1\leq p\leq n-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}
, and

\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{(n)}_{\mathcal{O}_{m};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}\Big|_{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}=n}\underset{p}{\rightarrow}0\ .

\epsilon^{ijk}\epsilon^{abk}=\delta^{ia}\delta^{jb}-\delta^{ib}\delta^{ja}

Thus, the linear equation for the rescaled velocity vorticity is

which is solved by

(B.6)

In this EdS approximation, this scales as

, which should be compared to

, which scales as

. Thus, any vorticity in the initial conditions will decay with respect to the growing modes like

\frac{a}{f}\omega^{i}_{(2)}{}^{\prime}+\left(\frac{3\Omega_{\rm m}}{2f^{2}}-1\right)\omega^{i}_{(2)}=-\epsilon^{ijk}\partial_{j}\left(\epsilon^{klm}\left(\frac{\partial_{l}\theta^{(1)}}{\partial^{2}}-\epsilon^{lnp}\frac{\partial_{n}\omega^{p}_{(1)}}{\partial^{2}}\right)\omega^{m}_{(1)}\right)\ ,

(B.7)

so that
\omega^{i}_{(2)}
is also zero. This obviously continues to all higher orders if the stress tensor is ignored.

18

This is the familiar statement that vorticity is not generated in standard perturbation theory.
However, as pointed out in
[
71
]
, and as can be seen from Eq. (
B.3
), vorticity is generated by the stress tensor
\tau^{ij}
in the EFT of LSS. Indeed, it was shown in
[
13
]
that in order to renormalize the one-loop bispectrum, even for dark matter without redshift space distortions, vorticity
needs
to be generated. Although it is generated at second order, it does not contribute to
\delta
or
\theta
until third order, as can be seen in Eq. (
B.3
). In
[
8
]
it was shown that one must include this contribution from the vorticity to consistently renormalize the two-loop dark-matter power spectrum. See also
[
79
]
for a study of vorticity in perturbation theory.

Since we do not compute the stochastic contributions, we focus on the response counterterms in the stress tensor. Looking at the vorticity equation in Eq. (
B.3
), we see that the contribution from the first-order stress tensor is zero since
\tau^{kl}_{(1)}\sim c_{1}\delta^{kl}_{K}\partial^{2}\Phi^{(1)}+c_{2}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\Phi^{(1)}
and
\epsilon^{ijk}\partial_{j}\partial_{l}\left(c_{1}\delta^{kl}_{K}\partial^{2}\Phi^{(1)}+c_{2}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\Phi^{(1)}\right)=0
. Next we look at the second-order terms. The terms proportional to
\omega^{m}
are zero since
\omega_{(1)}^{m}=0
. One possible second-order contribution comes from expanding
1/(1+\delta)
and is proportional to

\displaystyle\begin{split}-\epsilon^{ijk}\partial_{j}(\delta^{(1)}\partial_{l}\tau^{kl}_{(1)})&\propto-\epsilon^{ijk}\partial_{j}(\partial^{2}\Phi^{(1)}\partial_{l}(c_{1}\delta^{kl}_{K}\partial^{2}\Phi^{(1)}+c_{2}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\Phi^{(1)}))\\
&=-(c_{1}+c_{2})\epsilon^{ijk}\partial_{j}(\partial^{2}\Phi^{(1)}\partial_{k}\partial^{2}\Phi^{(1)})\\
&=-\frac{(c_{1}+c_{2})}{2}\epsilon^{ijk}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}(\partial^{2}\Phi^{(1)}\partial^{2}\Phi^{(1)})\ ,\end{split}

(B.8)

which is zero. So, the first non-zero contribution comes from the second-order stress tensor which in the EdS approximation can be written as

\tau^{ij}_{(2)}(\vec{x},a)=\frac{f^{2}H^{2}\bar{\rho}}{k_{\rm NL}^{2}}D(a)^{4}\tilde{\tau}^{ij}_{(2)}(\vec{x})\ ,

(B.9)

so that in the EdS approximation, writing
\omega^{i}_{(2)}(\vec{x},a)=D(a)^{4}\tilde{\omega}^{i}_{(2)}(\vec{x})
, we have

\tilde{\omega}^{i}_{(2)}(\vec{x})=\frac{2}{9k_{\rm NL}^{2}}\epsilon^{ijk}\partial_{j}\partial_{l}\tilde{\tau}^{kl}_{(2)}(\vec{x})\ .

(B.10)

Thus, the vorticity is a higher-derivative term, suppressed by
k_{\rm NL}^{2}
. The fact that the vorticity is generated with at least two spatial derivatives continues to higher orders, as can be readily seen from the last equation in Eq. (
B.3
). Now, the velocity building block of the bias expansion is

p^{ij}=\frac{\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\theta}{\partial^{2}}-\epsilon^{jkl}\frac{\partial_{i}\partial_{k}\omega^{l}}{\partial^{2}}\ ,

(B.11)

but since we only consider the bias expansion at zeroth order in spatial derivatives in this work, we see that we can ignore the vorticity for that purpose, in which case
p^{ij}
is symmetric. However, when considering higher-derivative bias, these terms may need to be included.

Dark-matter

-particle soft limits in fluid basis

As shown in

In this appendix, we will show that
\mathbb{C}_{\delta,n}^{(n)}
(i.e. with
\alpha=n

-particle soft limits, i.e.

for all

To do this, we will have to do an induction inside of an induction. Ultimately, we will assume that

p
-particle soft limits for

\mathbb{C}_{\delta,n}^{(n)}
has no
p
-particle soft limits. The base case

is

, which obviously has no
p
-particle soft limits. Now, we start by assuming that
\mathbb{C}_{\delta,n^{\prime}}^{(n^{\prime})}

-particle soft limits for

. Then we take the
p
-particle soft limit of Eq. (
D.24
), for

\delta^{(n)}=\mathbb{C}_{\delta,n}^{(n)}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{C}_{\delta,\alpha}^{(n)}\ ,

(D.27)

and focus on the sum, which we will show already satisfies all of the limits of the previous section, so that
\mathbb{C}_{\delta,n}^{(n)}
must have no
p
-particle soft limits. So we now consider the kernels
\mathbb{C}_{\delta,\alpha}^{(n)}
with
\alpha<n

First, we notice that, for
n^{\prime}<n
,

\mathbb{C}_{\delta,\alpha}^{(n^{\prime})}\underset{p}{\rightarrow}0\ ,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for}\hskip 10.00002ptn^{\prime}-\alpha<p\ .

(D.28)

To see this, consider Eq. (
D.25
) for fixed
\alpha
and
n^{\prime}<n

for which
\mathbb{C}_{\delta,\alpha}^{(n^{\prime})}
has a
p
-particle soft limit. Considering that
\alpha\leq n^{\prime}
in Eq. (
D.28
), starting with
n^{\prime}=\alpha+1
, we have

\mathbb{C}_{\delta,\alpha}^{(\alpha+1)}=\partial_{i}\mathbb{C}_{\delta,\alpha}^{(\alpha)}\frac{\partial_{i}\theta^{(1)}}{\partial^{2}}\ ,

(D.29)

which, since
\mathbb{C}_{\delta,\alpha}^{(\alpha)}

D.28
). Next, consider

Since
\mathbb{C}_{\delta,\alpha}^{(\alpha)}

p
-particle soft limit, and
\mathbb{C}_{\delta,\alpha}^{(\alpha+1)}
has no
p
-particle soft limit for

\mathbb{C}_{\delta,\alpha}^{(\alpha+2)}

\ell

\mathbb{C}_{\delta,\alpha}^{(\ell)}

D.28

Now we want to show that

\mathbb{C}_{\delta,\alpha}^{(n)}\underset{p}{\rightarrow}\frac{1}{p}\partial_{i}\mathbb{C}_{\delta,\alpha}^{(n-p)}\frac{\partial_{i}\theta^{(p)}}{\partial^{2}}\ ,

for

and

)). To prove Eq. (

\mathbb{C}_{\delta,\alpha}^{(n)}\underset{n-\alpha}{\rightarrow}\frac{1}{p}\partial_{i}\mathbb{C}_{\delta,\alpha}^{(n-p)}\frac{\partial_{i}\theta^{(p)}}{\partial^{2}}\ ,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for}\hskip 10.00002ptp=n-\alpha\ .

Now we assume that Eq. (

D.3
General non-local-in-time
p
-particle soft limits

In this section, we ultimately want to show that

\delta^{(n)}_{g}\underset{p}{\rightarrow}\frac{1}{p}\partial_{i}\delta^{(n-p)}_{g}\frac{\partial_{i}\theta^{(p)}}{\partial^{2}}\ ,

(D.35)

for
1\leq p\leq n-1
. We will do this by starting with Eq. (
3.9
) and writing it as

\delta^{(n)}_{g}=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\sum_{\{\mathcal{O}_{m}\}}\sum_{\alpha=m}^{n}\sum_{\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}\}_{\alpha}}c_{\mathcal{O}_{m};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{(n)}_{\mathcal{O}_{m};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}\ ,

(D.36)

where
\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}\}_{\alpha}
is the set of all tuples
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})
with
\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{m}=\alpha
. Using Eq. (
3.22
), we can see that

\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{(n)}_{\mathcal{O}_{m};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}\Big|_{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}=n}\underset{p}{\rightarrow}0\ ,

(D.37)

because it is a product of factors from the
\delta
or
\theta
fluid expansion with
n=\alpha
, and in the previous section we showed that they all have no
p
-particle soft limits. Thus, we can focus on the terms of Eq. (
D.36
) with
m\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\leq n-1
, which we will now show satisfy

\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{(n)}_{\mathcal{O}_{m};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}\underset{p}{\rightarrow}\frac{1}{p}\partial_{i}\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{(n-p)}_{\mathcal{O}_{m};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}\frac{\partial_{i}\theta^{(p)}}{\partial^{2}}\ ,

(D.38)

for
1\leq p\leq n-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}
.

First, we notice that the recursion relation for
\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{(n)}_{\mathcal{O}_{m};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}
in Eq. (
3.21
) is exactly the same form as the recursion relation for
\mathbb{C}_{\delta,\alpha}^{(n)}
in Eq. (
D.25
) identifying
\alpha\rightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}
. The only difference is that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\geq m
, while in Eq. (
D.25
),
\alpha\geq 1
. Thus, anything we derived from the recursion relation Eq. (
D.25
) for
\mathbb{C}_{\delta,\alpha}^{(n)}
, we can analogously derive from the recursion relation Eq. (
3.21
) for
\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{(n)}_{\mathcal{O}_{m};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}
, as long as the proof did not depend on the value of
\alpha
. Thus, we immediately have

\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{(n)}_{\mathcal{O}_{m};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}\underset{p}{\rightarrow}0\ ,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for}\hskip 10.00002ptn-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}<p\ ,

(D.39)

since the proof of Eq. (
D.28
) did not depend on the value of
\alpha
. Next, using this, we can run through the exact manipulations as in Eq. (
D.33
) (but identifying
\alpha\rightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}
), which also did not depend on the value of
\alpha
. Thus, we have proven Eq. (
D.38
).

Given the results above, we can now take the
p
-particle limit of
\delta_{g}^{(n)}
to get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\delta^{(n)}_{g}&\underset{p}{\rightarrow}\sum_{m=1}^{n-p}\sum_{\{\mathcal{O}_{m}\}}\sum_{\alpha=m}^{n-p}\sum_{\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}\}_{\alpha}}c_{\mathcal{O}_{m};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}\frac{1}{p}\partial_{i}\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{(n-p)}_{\mathcal{O}_{m};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}\frac{\partial_{i}\theta^{(p)}}{\partial^{2}}\\
&=\frac{1}{p}\partial_{i}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{n-p}\sum_{\{\mathcal{O}_{m}\}}\sum_{\alpha=m}^{n-p}\sum_{\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}\}_{\alpha}}c_{\mathcal{O}_{m};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{(n-p)}_{\mathcal{O}_{m};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}\right)\frac{\partial_{i}\theta^{(p)}}{\partial^{2}}\\
&=\frac{1}{p}\partial_{i}\delta_{g}^{(n-p)}\frac{\partial_{i}\theta^{(p)}}{\partial^{2}}\ .\end{split}

(D.40)

In the first line, the sum over
\alpha
goes from
m
to
n-p
because of Eq. (
D.39
), and to go to the third line, we used Eq. (
D.36
) with
n\rightarrow n-p
. Thus, we have proven Eq. (
D.35
) for
1\leq p\leq n-1
.
