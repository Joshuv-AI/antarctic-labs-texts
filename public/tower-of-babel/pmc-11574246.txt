A study on the effect of Hypericum perforatum L. extract on vanadium toxicity in Allium cepa L.
Authors: Tuğçe Kalefetoğlu Macar, Oksal Macar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe growth of industrialization growth the risk of vanadium (V) contamination. The objective of this study was to examine the impact of 200 µg L− 1 VCI3 -induced toxicity as well as the potential protective effect of 187.5 mg L− 1 and 375 mg L− 1Hypericum perforatum (H. perforatum) extracts against this toxicity on the Allium cepa (A. cepa) model organism. For this purpose, a series of investigations were conducted on the growth physiology alterations (germination percentage, root elongation, weight gain), cytogenetic alterations (mitotic index, micronucleus, chromosomal aberrations), biochemical alterations (malondialdehyde, superoxide dismutase, catalase) and defects in meristematic tissue in A. cepa. In addition, the phenolic compound content of H. perforatum extract was determined by the LC/MS-MS method. V application negatively affected all the investigated parameters and caused a serious phytotoxic and genotoxic effect as well as oxidative stress in A. cepa. Conversely, no statistical difference was observed between the parameters of the groups treated with H. perforatum extract and those of the control group. The administration of H. perforatum extract combined with V resulted in a notable enhancement in germination percentage, root elongation, weight gain, mitotic index value, chlorophyll a level and chlorophyll b level. Additionally, it led to a reduction in micronucleus and chromosomal aberrations frequencies, as well as meristematic tissue defects. Furthermore, LC/MS-MS analysis demonstrated that H. perforatum extract contains phenolic compounds, including catechin, epicatechin, quercetin, oleuropein and rutin, which confer antioxidant properties to the extract. The study provided clear evidence that H. perforatum extract attenuates the toxic effects of V in A. cepa, which can be attributed to its high content of bioactive phenols. The findings of the study indicate that H. perforatum extract may serve as a protective natural agent for daily use against heavy metal toxicity.Keywords: Allium cepa L., Antioxidant, Genotoxicity, Hypericum perforatum L., Phenolic compound, VanadiumSubject terms: Plant genetics, Biochemistry, DNA damage and repair

Introduction

Metal pollution became a global problem that originated in mining areas, industries and the agricultural sector1. Vanadium (V) is of particular importance and concern, given that its concentration in soil, air and water is increasing on a daily basis as a consequence of human activities. In 1831, Nils Gabriel Sefstrom was the first to recognize V as a unique metallic element and name it “Vanadis”, referring to the Norse goddess of beauty2. It is the fifth most prevalent transition element in the world’s crust. Apart from natural sources such as volcanoes, forest fires, continental dust, and sea salt spray, the major sources of V are petrochemical, steel, phosphorite processing, and fossil fuel combustion industries, along with mining, metallurgy, and galvanizing3. As a consequence of the growing industrial utilization of V, the discharge of V into the natural environment is also rising4. V is a toxic pollutant that is harmful to living organisms, including microorganisms, plants, and animals, when present in high concentrations5. Although V is regarded as an essential element, it is categorized as a potentially toxic element by the United States Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA)6. The consumption of food containing elevated levels of V can lead to lesions in the kidneys, liver, bones, spleen, and nervous system7. Furthermore, V has been linked to a number of adverse effects in humans, including eye and skin irritation, emphysema, pain, pneumonia, cardiovascular diseases and cognitive impairment6. Low doses of V have been demonstrated to have favorable effects on plant growth, such as the increased synthesis of sugars, amino acids and chlorophyll. Conversely, elevated levels of V in the soil have been found to induce chlorosis and growth retardation in plants8. The mechanism of V toxicity is closely associated with oxidative stress, which is caused by the excessive accumulation of V-derived reactive oxygen species (ROS)9.

Hypericum perforatum L. (St. John’s wort) (H. perforatum), a member of the Hypericaceae family and the genus Hypericum, is a perennial shrub with heights of 1–3 feet10. It is originally native to Madeira, Europe, India, North Africa, China, West Asia and the Azores, and nowadays spread throughout the world,. Although it is traditionally used in the treatment of anxiety, wound healing, lung conditions, urinary tract infections, hysteria, sciatica, hemorrhages, mild gastrointestinal discomfort and infections in the Mediterranean region11, it is currently most commonly preferred as an antidepressant12. Extracts of this plant, which has a long history of use for various purposes, have been demonstrated to possess significant antioxidant capacity13. Furthermore, research has demonstrated that extracts derived from H. perforatum exhibit antifungal, anti-mycobacterial, anti-inflammatory, and anti-viral properties14. The medicinal properties of H. perforatum are attributed to its bioactive components, including hypericin, hyperforin, phenolics and flavonoids15. Naphthodianthrones such as hypericin and hyperforin are noted for their anticancer properties and therapeutic potential against neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease12. Additionally, the antioxidant properties of H. perforatum extract reduced the toxicity associated with chromium exposure in mice16, suggesting that it may be effective against other heavy metal-induced toxicities such as V.

Biological assays are commonly utilized for detecting the genotoxicity of pollutants. The Allium cepa (A. cepa) test is one of the most favored in vivo experimental models used to assess potential genotoxicity due to its low cost and rapid development, as well as its high correlation with mammalian test systems17,18. This test enables the identification of diverse genetic endpoints associated with the cytotoxic, clastogenic, genotoxic and aneugenic effects of toxic substances19.

Given the proven role of ROS in V-induced toxicity20, dietary plant antioxidants have the potential to reduce V-induced toxicity via their ability to regulate the innate antioxidant system and alleviate oxidative stress. From this point of view, H. perforatum, known for its antioxidant properties, is one of the possible medicinal plants that can be used against V toxicity. This study aimed to investigate the effect of V-induced toxicity and whether H. perforatum has a mitigating effect against this toxicity using a multifaceted approach on A. cepa.

Materials and methods

A. cepa bulbs (2n = 16), which are preferred as a eukaryotic indicator organism as test material, were obtained from a local market in Giresun. The test chemical was Vanadium (III) Chloride (VCI3-CAS No: 7718-98-1-25 G), a product of Merck Company. In the experiments, a commercial H. perforatum L. extract containing 0.3% hypericin (Balen, Ankara, Turkey) was used.

Experimental design

Six groups, one of which was a control, were established to examine the effects of V and H. perforatum extract. Each set consisted of 50 similarly sized A. cepa bulbs. The germination percentage was determined using whole 50 bulbs, while the length and weight gain of 10 randomly selected bulbs were assessed. After physiological measurements of onion roots, they were used in other tests. Prior to immersion in the respective solutions for a period of three days in the dark at 25 °C, the bulbs were weighed. The dose of V used in the experimental studies was selected as 200 µg L− 1, as mentioned in a previous study by Kaya et al.21. In this study, this dose was considered the highest dose that allowed A. cepa bulbs to be examined without causing mortality The daily suggested dose by the producer and half of this dose were used to calculate the dosages of H. perforatum extract. The experimental groups and their doses are shown in Table 1. The bulbs were randomly divided into six groups and each group consisting of 50 bulbs. Similar-sized bulbs were then placed in sterile glass beakers with their disc stems in contact with respective solutions. Subsequently, bulbs were stored at room temperature and in the dark for three days. The solutions in the beakers were replaced with fresh solutions daily. To ascertain the chlorophyll pigment analysis, the identical experimental procedure was conducted for a period of six days.

Investigation of growth physiology alterations

To calculate the germination percentage, out of a total of 50 bulbs (n = 50) in each group, those with an additional root length exceeding 1 cm were considered “germinated”. The percentage of germination was determined with the assistance of Eq. 1, as proposed by Atik et al.22.

To calculate the average weight gain in the groups, the weights of ten randomly selected bulbs (n = 10) were measured before and after the experiment. Similarly, the lengths of ten additional roots of 10 randomly selected bulbs from each group were measured21.

Investigation of cytogenetic alterations

The cytogenetic alterations were evaluated by using the same slides for the assessment of alterations in the levels of micronucleus and chromosomal aberrations along with the mitotic index score23. At the end of the third day, 1 cm pieces of root tips were fixed in Clarke’s fluid (ethanol/glacial acetic acid: 3/1) for two hours. Thereafter, the root tips were subjected to hydrolysis in 1 N HCl in a hot water bath at 60 °C for ten minutes. The root tips were stained with an acetocarmine (1%) solution for 24 h prior to examination under a microscope (Irmeco, IM-450 TI)24. The slides were prepared using the standard squash preparation method. Changes in micronucleus and CA frequencies were assessed by examining 100 cells in each of ten slides (n = 10) prepared from each group (1000 cells in total). Changes in mitotic index score were calculated using Eq. 2 by examining 1000 cells (total 10,000 cells) in each of ten slides (n = 10) prepared from each group.

Investigation of biochemical alterations

The amount of malondialdehyde (MDA), as well as the activities of catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD), were measured to determine the oxidative stress generated by V. The MDA level is a reliable indicator of oxidative stress-induced lipid peroxidation, while the SOD and CAT enzymes are parts of the antioxidant defense system that fights oxidative stress.

For the assessment of MDA levels in roots, 0.5 g of root tissue was homogenized in 1 mL trichloroacetic acid (TCA) solution (5%) in an ice bath using a cold mortar and pestle. It was then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 ºC. Equal amounts of TCA (20%), thiobarbituric acid (0.5%), and obtained supernatant were combined, and the mixture was heated to boiling for 25 min. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min after the reaction was halted by moving it to an ice bath25. Spectrophotometric measurement was used to determine the absorbance of the reaction mixture’s supernatant at 532 nm. The outcomes of the MDA level analyses were displayed as µM g− 1 Fresh Roots Weight (FRW)26,27.

The A. cepa roots homogenate used for the determination of SOD and CAT enzyme activities were prepared in the same way. In this method, 0.25 g of fresh root samples were washed with distilled water and then mechanically homogenized in 2.5 mL of monosodium phosphate buffer (50 mM / pH 7.8). The supernatant obtained by centrifugation of the homogenate at 9,600 rpm for 20 min was used as extract to assess SOD and CAT activities28. To measure SOD activity, a reaction medium comprising 1.5 mL of sodium phosphate buffer (50 mM / pH 7.8), 0.3 mL of methionine (130 mM), 0.3 mL of nitroblue tetrazolium chloride (750 µM), 0.3 mL of EDTA-Na2 (0.1 mM), 0.3 mL of riboflavin (20 µM), 0.01 mL of insoluble polyvinylpyrrolidone (4%), 0.28 mL of de-ionized water, and distilled water was mixed with 0.01 mL of the extract to measure. To initiate the enzymatic reaction, the glass tube containing the mixture was positioned in front of two 15-watt fluorescent lamps for 10 min. Subsequently, the tubes were left in the dark for a further 10 min to allow the reaction to reach completion. SOD activity was then expressed as U mg− 1 FRW after the solution’s absorbance was measured at 560 nm29. To measure CAT activity, a reaction mixture including 1.5 mL of monosodium phosphate buffer (50 mM / pH 7.8), 1.0 mL of distilled water, and 0.3 mL of hydrogen peroxide (0.1 M) was mixed with 0.2 mL of extract. The absorbance at 240 nm was fallowed to assess the catalytic activity, which was then represented as OD240 nm min g− 1 FRW30.

To ascertain the chlorophyll pigment content, 0.2 g of A. cepa leaves were harvested at the conclusion of the sixth day and the samples were cooled to 4 °C. The cooled samples were then crushed in 5 mL of 80% acetone with the assistance of a mortar and pestle. Subsequently, the crushed sample was transferred to a glass tube and stored in the refrigerator in the dark for one week. Then after, the stored sample was treated once more with 5 mL of 80% acetone, shaken vigorously and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min. The absorbance of the obtained supernatant was determined at 645 and 663 nm using a spectrophotometer31. The concentrations of chlorophyll a (Eq. 3) and chlorophyll b (Eq. 4) were then determined using the equations provided by Witham et al.32.

Investigation of defects in meristematic tissue

The root samples from each group were washed in distilled water in order to remove any debris that may interfere with the observations. The cross-sections were obtained manually by a razor blade. The samples were then stained with a drop of 1% methylene blue and placed between a slide and a coverslip, where they were examined under a research microscope (Irmeco, IM-450 TI). The extent of defects was determined according to a pre-established classification system, which categorized the observed damage as healthy, slightly damaged, severely damaged or extremely damaged27.

Determination of phenolic composition of H. Perforatum

The composition of the phenolic compounds in the H. perforatum was determined by liquid chromatography - tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis. One gram of the sample was dissolved in a mixture of methanol and dichloromethane solutions (4:1). The solution was filtered through a 0.45 μm sterile syringe. The resulting filtrate was subjected to analysis of phenolic matter using LC-MS/MS (Thermo Scientific). The analysis was conducted using an ODS Hypersil 4.6*250 mm column. The elution gradient comprised two mobile phases: mobile phase A (water with 0.1% formic acid) and mobile phase B (methanol). The gradient program was fixed as follows: 0–1 min, 0% B; 1–22 min, 95% B; 22–25 min, 95% B; 25–30 min, 100% B33,34. The total evaluation time was 34 min, including conditioning time. The LC-MS/MS analysis was conducted at HUBTUAM-Hitit University.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained from this study were analyzed using the SPSS Statistics V 23.0 (2015) software. The data were evaluated using one-way ANOVA and the Duncan test at a significance level of p < 0.05. The variations in the results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results and discussion

As illustrated in Table 2, the individual and collective impacts of V and H. perforatum extracts on the physiological parameters of A. cepa root tip cells were evaluated. A. cepa bulbs are characterized by a reduced disc stem. Germination was deemed to have occurred when adventitious roots emerging from this region reached a length of 1 cm. A 100% germination rate was observed in the Hyper 1 and Hyper 2 groups following the application of H. perforatum extracts, which was the same as the control group. Similarly, the root length and weight gain of the Hyper 1 and Hyper 2 groups were found to be statistically similar to that of the control. Therefore, the application doses of H. perforatum did not result in any observable physiological toxicity in A. cepa. Conversely, treatment with V resulted in a 70% reduction in the germination percentage of A. cepa bulbs. In addition, the root elongation and weight gain in the V group exhibited a reduction of 60.5% and 61.9%, respectively, in comparison to the control group (Table 2). Our findings align with those of previous studies that have demonstrated the inhibitory effects of V exposure on plant growth and development in rice35, tomato36, Medicago sativa37 and Cicer arietinum38. According to Chongkid et al.39, V given to the root media accumulated primarily in the roots and least in the seeds. Plant roots can absorb a number of heavy metals, including V, using the same transporters that facilitate the absorption of essential plant nutrients7. The presence of these metals in the root growth medium may limit the uptake of essential minerals and water, which are vital for optimal growth. Additionally, it has been established that V serves to inhibit the activity of plasma membrane hydrogen ions in transferring the ATPase, which is a need for the uptake of specific minerals by root cells40. According to Altaf et al.20, the reduction in root length due to V stress may occur as a result of mitotic cell proliferation, which may inhibit root tip development. Furthermore, elevated levels of V stress can trigger an ionic imbalance, which affects plant growth and development by disrupting the function of these ions in metabolic pathways41. The observed growth suppression in A. cepa may also be attributed to the initiation of oxidative stress associated with the accumulation of ROS. The administration of H. perforatum extract in combination with V resulted in the alleviation of V-induced physiological damage. The dose of H. perforatum extract in the mixture was related to the degree of recovery. The germination rates observed in the VHyper 1 and VHyper 2 groups were 76% and 84%, respectively (Table 2). The root lengths of the VHyper 1 and VHyper 2 groups were 1.3 and 1.6 times that of the V group, respectively. Moreover, weight gain in the VHyper 2 group was approximately 1.9 times that in the V group. Previous research indicates that a number of natural extracts can mitigate the growth-inhibiting effects of various heavy metals, including V42–44. Rajak et al.45 stated that the antioxidant compounds such as essential oils, vitamins and phenolic compounds found in fruits and vegetables have scavenging and chelating capabilities, which may render them a viable alternative to synthetic chelating agents in the treatment of metal toxicity. The present study is the first to demonstrate the protective capacity of H. perforatum extract against the growth-restrictive effect of V.

Mitotic index, micronucleus formation and chromosomal aberrations frequency analyses were performed in order to ascertain the protective efficacy of H. perforatum extract against V-induced genotoxicity in A. cepa (Table 3; Fig. 1). The administration of both doses of H. perforatum extract did not result in any observable genetic damage (Table 3). Indeed, the values for mitotic index, micronucleus and chromosomal aberrations in the Hyper 1 and Hyper 2 groups were not statistically different from those in the control group (p > 0.05). Conversely, mitotic index, which was determined as 880 ± 19.2 in the control group, decreased to 595 ± 12.8 in the V-treated group. Although micronucleus (Fig. 1a) was absent in the control, Hyper 1 and Hyper 2 groups, it increased notably in the V group. Chromosomal aberrations identified in the V group were ranked according to the degree of their accumulation as fragment (Fig. 1b), vagrant chromosome (Fig. 1c), unequal distribution of chromatin (Fig. 1d), sticky chromosome (Fig. 1e), bridge (Fig. 1f) and reverse polarization (Fig. 1g) (Table 3). The ability of V to reduce the mitotic index and to induce the accumulation of micronucleus and chromosomal aberrations in the cells of the root tip of A. cepa has already been demonstrated20. Similar results have also been observed in mice46 and Lens culinaris47. The ratio of mitotic cells to total cells is known as mitotic index. According to Álvarez-Barrera et al.46, the capacity of V compounds to modify the activity of several enzymes, including protein kinases, phosphatases, ATPases and ribonucleases, may be connected to the decline in mitotic index. Since mitotic index is an indicator of cell proliferation, V-induced growth arrest (Table 3) seemed to be associated with suppression of mitosis. It has been suggested that V stress suppresses cell division47 and is a threat to DNA structure through DNA fragmentation48. V induces double-strand breaks in DNA molecules, and the primary processes leading to chromosomal aberrations production are related to the repair mechanisms involved in these breaks. Indeed, if DNA synthesis takes place before the break in the DNA molecule is repaired, visible chromosomal aberrations occur46. Furthermore, V ions have the ability to attach to DNA by interacting with the N-7 atoms of adenine and guanine bases as well as PO2 groups. They may also react with hydrogen peroxide to create hydroxyl radicals, which are capable of attacking DNA21. Indeed, heavy metals are inducers of oxidative stress. Therefore, V can be said to cause chromosomal lesions due to this damage to the DNA. Exposure to clastogens or aneugens, activation of oxidative stress, genetic abnormalities in DNA repair genes or cell cycle checkpoints, lack of vital cofactors in DNA metabolism and chromosomal separation are among the potential causes of micronucleus development49. The formation of micronucleus and nuclear buds is associated with fragments or whole chromosomes that are unable to enter daughter nuclei after telophase. The disorganization of the spindle apparatus is the underlying cause of vagrant chromosomes, which subsequently give rise to the accumulation of unequally distributed chromatins. The formation of vagrant chromosomes is based on the poleward movement of a chromosome in front of its associated chromosome set50. A sticky chromosome is a sign that chemicals may interact lethally with chromosomal DNA. DNA depolymerization, partial nucleoprotein degradation, enhanced DNA condensation, connections between chromatids and/or chromosomes and increased nucleoprotein synthesis can all cause stickiness during DNA replication51. The primary source of chromosomal bridges are chromosomes that break and reassemble during anaphase or sticky chromosomes that cannot be separated50. According to Salim et al.52, the mutations produced by mutagens may have damaged genes, depolymerized microfilaments or interfered with the mechanism that controls spindle formation, which might account for the changed polarity. V has been shown to delay the cell cycle during the G1-S phase but has no effect on cell survival53. This delay can result in intracellular oxidative stress, which can break DNA double helix and phosphorylate histone proteins. It is also linked to a drop in the mRNA and protein levels of cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases53,54. It is possible to explain the V-induced accumulation of chromosomal aberrations and the reduction in mitotic index in A. cepa as a consequence of these effects.

The data indicated that, in both the VHyper 1 and VHyper 2 groups, the incidence of mitotic index was statistically higher than that observed in the V group (p < 0.05) (Table 3). Conversely, the incidence of micronucleus and chromosomal aberrations was statistically lower in the VHyper 1 and VHyper 2 groups than in the V group (p < 0.05). The suppressive effect of H. perforatum on genotoxicity increased with the dose of the herbal extract in the mixture of V + H. perforatum. The simultaneous increases in root elongation, weight gain and mitotic index in these groups compared to the V group also showed that the V-induced growth restriction in A. cepa was associated with a decrease in mitotic activity. The DNA protective effect of H. perforatum extracts has been previously investigated on pBR322 plasmid DNA isolated from Escherichia coli in the presence of ultraviolet radiation and hydrogen peroxide55. Additionally, it has been demonstrated that water extracts of H. perforatum possess protective properties against alkylating oxidative DNA damage induced by methyl-methanesulfonate in HT29 cells56. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that H. perforatum has the capacity to reduce the levels of micronucleus and chromosomal aberrations in Triticum aestivum caryopsis subjected to X-ray radiation57. Nevertheless, our work is the first to show that H. perforatum extract can support the maintenance of genomic integrity against V toxicity in A. cepa. The observed antigenotoxic effect may be attributed to the antioxidant properties of the H. perforatum extract, which may act by scavenging free radicals and/or chelating metal ions. It has been suggested that the high antioxidant activity of Hypericum extracts may be attributed to their rich composition of phenolic compounds, including rutin and quercetin56. Furthermore, it has been pointed out that the protective effect of herbal extracts may be achieved indirectly by modulating the activities of enzymes that control the production of toxic by-products of lipid peroxidation57.

In addition to SOD and CAT enzyme activities, MDA, chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b levels were analyzed to monitor the biochemical changes in A. cepa root tip cells exposed to H. perforatum extract, V and the mixture of the extract with V (Table 4). The differences between the enzyme activities (SOD and CAT) of the Hyper 1 and Hyper 2 groups treated with H. perforatum and those of the control group were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Similarly, there was no statistically significant difference between the MDA levels of these three groups (p > 0.05). These parameters are important indicators of the oxidative stress in the cells. According to our results, application doses of H. perforatum did not induce oxidative damage in A. cepa root tip meristem cells when applied alone. In contrast, V administration triggered a notable accumulation of MDA as well as a striking elevation of SOD and CAT activities (p < 0.05) (Table 4). The mean MDA level in this group was 3.1 times that of the control group. Furthermore, the mean SOD and CAT activities in the V group were 1.9 and 2.8 times those of the control group, respectively. Heavy metals have the potential to cause genomic instability, inducing ROS-related oxidative stress, leading to DNA damage, cellular metabolic collapse and subsequent cell death58,59. Oxidative damage, as evidenced by protein oxidation, DNA oxidation and lipid peroxidation, has been documented in organisms exposed to V compounds60,61. As observed in our study, it has been previously demonstrated that V causes an increase in SOD and CAT activities accompanied by an increase in MDA in Oryza sativa20,]A. cepa21 and Capsicum annuum62. The capacity of plants to withstand environmental stressors and regulate the overproduction of ROS is significantly influenced by their antioxidant defense system62. SOD and CAT are integral components of the enzymatic part of this system. SOD facilitates the elimination of the superoxide radical, while CAT plays a crucial role in the removal of the hydrogen peroxide radical63. Additionally, MDA is frequently employed as a marker to gauge the degree of peroxidation damage to polyunsaturated membrane lipids1. Here we report that V is an agent which simultaneously causes both oxidative stress and genotoxicity in A. cepa (Tables 3 and 4). Our findings indicate that exposure to V resulted in a notable reduction in chlorophyll a (57.1%) and chlorophyll b (72.1%) levels in comparison to the control group (p < 0.05) (Table 4). Similar to our findings, it has been previously demonstrated that V causes a decline in chlorophyll contents in Cicer arietinum48, Capsicum annuum62, Ipomoea aquatica64 and Brassica chinensis65. Leaf chlorosis and necrosis due to loss of chlorophyll along with growth inhibition and oxidative stress are among the indicators of toxicity due to elevated V levels in plants1. The formation of visible chlorosis in the presence of V may be a consequence of the inhibition of chlorophyll biosynthesis and/or the degradation of chlorophyll66. It is emphasized that alterations in membrane permeability and ROS production represent the primary mechanisms underlying the reduction of chlorophyll pigment levels in response to V-induced stress62.

Since oxidative stress is the main mechanism underlying V-induced toxicity, natural substances with antioxidant virtues are becoming increasingly popular as low-cost and safe V countermeasures60. The levels of SOD and CAT activity, as well as the MDA content, were found to be lower in the VHyper 1 and VHyper 2 groups in comparison to the V group (p < 0.05) (Table 4). Therefore, H. perforatum extract was effective in alleviating V-induced oxidative stress, with greater efficacy observed at higher doses. In the VHyper 1 and VHyper 2 groups, H. perforatum extract also supported the increase in chlorophyll pigments, which were found to be at the lowest levels in the V-treated group (Table 4). The power of H. perforatum extract in attenuating V-induced oxidative imbalance was demonstrated for the first time in this study. Nevertheless, the research conducted by Savici et al.16 indicated that H. perforatum extract was effective in reducing chromium-related toxicity in Wistar rats, owing to its antioxidant properties. It has also been reported that H. perforatum extract not only affects the activities of antioxidant enzymes in rats exposed to rotenone but also alters the gene expression of these enzymes, thus suppressing oxidative stress67. The greatest phytochemical substances found in the plant are phenolic compounds, which it produces on its own as a defense mechanism to encourage development under adverse circumstances. The antioxidant qualities of H. perforatum are guaranteed by the flavonoids’ capacity to scavenge ROS or to perform actions like chelation, blockage of singlet oxygen or hydrogen donation16. Indeed, the pharmacological treatment of V poisoning has been described to be based on the principle of chelation, which involves the binding of metal ions to form a stable, water-soluble complex with low toxicity60. In addition to the aforementioned virtues, it has been reported that the active components of H. perforatum exhibit potential antioxidant activity through the inhibition of lipid peroxidation and the stimulation of signal transducers68. One of the mechanisms through which phenolic compounds exert their antioxidant activity is by forming a synergistic effect with other antioxidants69. It is possible that there is such a synergistic relationship between phenolics in the composition of H. perforatum and antioxidants such as SOD and CAT activated in A. cepa cells in the suppression of V-induced oxidative stress.

Table 5; Fig. 2 illustrate the effects of V and H. perforatum extracts on root meristem tissues. The root tip meristem tissues of the control and H. perforatum extract-treated groups (Hyper 1 and Hyper 2) exhibited no observable defects. Figure 2a-d illustrates the morphology of various cellular components under normal conditions, including epidermis cells (Fig. 2a), cell nucleus (Fig. 2b), cortex cells (Fig. 2c), and conduction tissue (Fig. 2d). However, treatment with 200 µg/L VCl3 resulted in the emergence of several distinct damage types such as epidermal cell damage (Fig. 2e), flattened cell nucleus (Fig. 2f), giant nucleus (Fig. 2g), binuclear cells (Fig. 2h), cortex cell damage (Fig. 2j), cortex cell wall thickening (Fig. 2k) and conduction tissue thickening (Fig. 2l). Similar defects caused by V in the meristematic tissue of A. cepa roots have been reported in other studies21,70. Altaf et al.20 reported that V leads to the induction of structural defects in rice roots. V accumulation is higher in the root parts of the plants rather than in other plant parts7, which is the main reason for the severity of the defects observed in the study. The observed nuclear deformations, namely flattened nucleus, giant nucleus and binuclear cells, were other indicators of genotoxicity as a consequence of V-induced toxicity, as revealed in this study. Damage to DNA from oxidative stress and cell membrane rupture can both result in flattened cell nuclei71, while binuclear cells are a reflection of inhibited cytokinesis at any cell cycle checkpoint72. When plants are exposed to elevated levels of V, they tend to immobilize it in the roots prior to reduction to the tetravalent form1. Accordingly, the thickening of the cortex cell wall and conduction tissue may be adaptations to form a barrier to prevent the upward movement of V to the upper parts of the plant, or they may be indicative of V entrapment within the cell walls. The damage observed in epidermal and cortex cells may be a consequence of damage to cell membranes, which is one of the primary effects of V toxicity, as evidenced by the results of MDA analysis in this study.

In the VHyper 1 and VHyper 2 groups, the severity of the observed meristematic tissue defects was significantly reduced by the combined application of V and H. perforatum extract. The declines were more noticeable in the VHyper 2 group, where there was a higher dosage of H. perforatum extract applied. In this group, epidermal cell damage, cortex cell damage and cortex cell wall thickening damage types decreased to minor level while flattened nucleus, giant nucleus, binuclear cell and conduction tissue thickening damage types were completely prevented. This study is the first to demonstrate the protective role of H. perforatum against V-induced meristematic tissue defects. However, various studies have indicated that plant extracts may have the potential to partially repair tissue and cellular disorders in non-target organisms exposed to heavy metals44,73. The high antioxidant content of H. perforatum extract alleviated V-induced oxidative stress and improved cell membranes, which may be related to the ability of this extract to reduce meristematic tissue defects in A. cepa.

Table 6; Fig. 3 provide a representation of the phenolic compound composition of the H. perforatum extract. The most abundant phenolic compounds in the extract were identified as catechin, epicatechin, quercetin, oleuropein, rutin, p-coumaric acid, rosmarinic acid, ferulic acid, vanillin, caffeic acid, 4-OH benzoic acid, salicylic acid, naringenin and syringaldehyde, according to their abundance (Table 6). Our findings largely coincided with the findings of many studies on the phenolic composition of H. perforatum in the literature74,75. The chemical composition of plants grown in different regions is typically influenced by genetic factors. However, additional factors, including altitude, plant parts used, harvest time, solvent used in extraction, pH of the medium, pressure, temperature and light exposure, can also affect observed differences76. The more ROS present in a cell, the more DNA damage is promoted. These defects in the DNA need to be treated urgently. Our data showed that all these phenolics, which are individually metal chelators, membrane stabilizers, DNA shields, oxidative stress suppressors and protectors of biological pathways, were uniquely combined to contribute to a large extent to the neutralization of V toxicity by H. perforetum. It was observed that both catechin and epicatechin reduced ROS-induced single-strand breaks and base damage in a plasmid DNA system77. Huang et al.78 showed that catechin treatment played an active role in the scavenging of free radicals, the up-regulation of SOD and CAT enzyme activities and the increased expression of DNA repair enzymes. They also react via a single electron transfer and subsequent deprotonation with peroxyl radicals in phospholipid bilayers to protect cellular membranes79. Epicatechin was reported to be effective in suppressing cytotoxicity in HepG2 cells by promoting cell cycle progression and attenuating cellular apoptosis and DNA damage80. The high concentration of quercetin found in H. perforatum extract (Table 6) could also help to reduce the damage caused by V to A. cepa cells. Indeed, quercetin has the capacity to modify metabolic pathways involved in antioxidant defense. It also has the ability to scavenge ROS and inhibit lipid peroxidation79. Quercetin has also been shown to protect genetic integrity by stimulating the expression of DNA repair enzymes81. Rutin is a quercetin glycoside and has been reported to reduce methotrexate-induced chromosomal aberrations in mice82. The role of rutin in preventing oxidative, chromosomal and DNA damage has been associated with its ability to suppress oxidative stress83. Spectral shift analysis showed that oleuropein mixed with DNA tends to intercalate between the base pairs to strengthen the helix structure84. In addition to suppressing ROS production, oleuropein has been reported to increase the levels and activities of many antioxidant enzymes, including SOD and CAT85. In addition to hypericin, whose antigenotoxic properties were previously demonstrated by de Souza et al.86 in V79 cells, the phenolic compounds present in H. perforatum extract appear to function to reduce the genotoxic effect of V on A. cepa. H. perforatum is a highly potent antioxidant, attributable to the considerable number of bioactive compounds it contains and the high phenol content demonstrated in this study. Because of its strong antioxidant content, H. perforatum extracts have great promise for treating oxidative stress brought on by harmful chemicals and illnesses. From this perspective, it can be posited that H. perforatum extract may be employed as a protective supplement against heavy metals and other environmental pollutants that are known to induce oxidative stress, in addition to its conventional medical applications. On the other hand, Suljić et al.87 mentioned that Hypericum perforatum has the potential to remediate soil contaminated with heavy metals. This capacity may be attributed to the combined effect of the chelate and antioxidant biochemical arsenal, which is effective in resisting heavy metal toxicity.

Conclusion

The increasing industrialization of the modern world has led to increased exposure to toxic levels of heavy metals, including V. This growing health concern has increased interest in plant extracts with high antioxidant potential, such as H. perforatum, as a potential therapeutic option. The aim of this study was to investigate the toxic effects of V on A. cepa along with the potential protective benefits of H. perforatum extract against these adverse effects in a comprehensive manner. The administration of 187.5 mg L− 1 and 375 mg L− 1 of H. perforatum extracts did not result in any toxic effects in A. cepa. Conversely, the administration of 200 µg L− 1 VCI3 led to a significant decline in growth, increased oxidative stress, elevated levels of cytotoxicity, adverse effects on biochemical parameters and the disruption of root meristem tissue in A. cepa. The findings of this investigation also demonstrated that the administration of H. perforatum extract in conjunction with V resulted in a reduction in the toxicity parameters induced by V in A. cepa. This protective effect was facilitated by the well-known potent antioxidant properties of H. perforatum extract, which were associated with the rich bioactive phenol composition exhibited in the study. H. perforatum, which has been used for a considerable period of time and its products widely commercialized, demonstrates considerable untapped potential against heavy metal toxicity. In the context of an increasing risk of heavy metal toxicity, H. perforatum extract may be considered a suitable option for the general public as a safe, natural product. In addition, as a robust plant with the capacity to flourish in diverse environmental conditions which contains many bioactive compounds, H. perforatum represents a promising option for the remediation of soil contaminated with heavy metals. Further investigations into the potential medical applications of H. perforatum would undoubtedly benefit from the solid foundation provided by this research.
