Precision and efficacy in congenital cataract surgery: a retrospective study of the 27-gauge vitrectomy system
Authors: Maura Mancini, Alessandro Meduri, Claudio Brancato, Giovanni William Oliverio, Laura De Luca, Alessandra Mancini, Paola Palino, Pasquale Aragona, Antonino Pioppo, Gregorio Lo Giudice
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the precision, safety, and efficacy of congenital cataract surgery performed using the 27-gauge vitrectomy system in infants.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed 44 eyes from 22 pediatric patients (aged 3–5 months) with bilateral congenital cataracts. The following parameters were assessed: intraocular pressure, corneal diameters, axial length, and the occurrence of intraoperative and postoperative complications. Visual function was evaluated based on visual behavior in response to various stimuli. All patients were followed for a minimum of 6 months.ResultsA significant reduction in intraocular pressure was observed (from 15.8 ± 5.7 mmHg preoperatively to 12.2 ± 1.1 mmHg postoperatively, p = 4.64 × 10⁻7). The average duration of the surgical procedure was 30 ± 5 min. Postoperative complications were minimal and transient, including conjunctival hyperemia in 30% of eyes (resolved within one week) and mild anterior chamber reaction in 12% of eyes. No significant complications, such as retinal detachment or glaucoma, were observed during follow-up.ConclusionThe use of the 27-gauge vitrectomy system for congenital cataract surgery in infants provides high surgical precision, low complication rates, and effective postoperative outcomes. Further prospective studies with longer follow-up are needed to confirm the long-term safety and efficacy of this approach.

Background

Congenital cataract is one of the leading causes of preventable childhood blindness, with an estimated global prevalence of 1–6 cases per 10,000 live births, depending on geographic and socioeconomic factors [1]. Although rare, blindness associated with congenital cataracts remains a significant concern due to its profound psychological and socioeconomic repercussions if untreated or if treatment is delayed. Obstruction of the visual axis during neurosensory development, caused by congenital cataract, can lead to irreversible deprivation amblyopia in the absence of timely intervention [2]. However, visual deficits in affected children are not solely due to untreated cataracts but may also result from surgical complications or associated structural anomalies. As such, congenital cataract surgery should be performed by experienced surgeons within a multidisciplinary team that includes expert pediatric anesthesiology, medical, and nursing support for postoperative care. Although several steps in adult cataract surgery can be adapted for pediatric cataract surgery, the pediatric eye differs significantly from the adult eye. The elasticity of the anterior capsule and the soft, sticky nature of the lens material make capsulorhexis and cataract removal more prolonged and challenging. Additionally, the thin and elastic sclera, combined with positive vitreous pressure in pediatric eyes, can result in repeated shallowing of the anterior chamber during surgery [3]. Over the past decades, advancements in surgical techniques have revolutionized the management of this condition, with the introduction of smaller-gauge instrumentation offering a potential paradigm shift in terms of safety, precision, and postoperative outcomes. The 27-gauge pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) system represents one of the most recent innovations in micro-incisional cataract surgery. Compared to traditional 23- and 25-gauge systems, the 27-gauge system offers enhanced maneuverability and reduces tissue trauma due to its ultra-thin instrumentation [4]. This is particularly advantageous in pediatric populations, where anatomical constraints and heightened postoperative inflammatory responses pose significant challenges [5]. Despite its theoretical benefits, evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of 27-gauge vitrectomy in the context of congenital cataract surgery remains limited. Existing literature has primarily focused on its application in adult vitreoretinal procedures, with pediatric studies being scarce [6]. Given the unique anatomical and physiological characteristics of pediatric eyes, further investigations are required to validate the utility of this technique in younger patients.

This retrospective study aims to evaluate the precision, efficacy, and safety profile of 27-gauge vitrectomy in the surgical management of congenital cataracts. By analyzing clinical outcomes, intraoperative parameters, and postoperative complications, this study seeks to clarify the potential role of this advanced surgical modality in optimizing visual rehabilitation for pediatric patients.

Methods

This retrospective study was conducted at the Ophthalmology Complex Operating Unit (UOC) of the National High Specialization Healthcare Company (ARNAS) Civico "Di Cristina e Benfratelli" in Palermo, Italy. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The retrospective analysis of medical and surgical records covered the period from January 2021 to December 2024. A total of 44 eyes from 22 children aged 3 to 5 months with bilateral congenital cataracts requiring surgical intervention were included in this study. Written informed consent for the processing of personal data was obtained for all patients, signed by their parents or legal guardians. The inclusion criteria for the study were: Diagnosis of bilateral congenital cataract; Need for surgical intervention to prevent deprivation amblyopia; Absence of major ocular pathologies (e.g., microphthalmia, coloboma, anterior segment dysgenesis); Willingness to participate in a postoperative follow-up period of at least 6 months; Signed informed consent from parents or legal guardians for the collection and analysis of clinical data. The exclusion criteria were: Unilateral cataract; Complex ocular anomalies (e.g., severe microphthalmia, aniridia, advanced retinopathy of prematurity); Previous ocular surgeries; Severe systemic diseases (e.g., Down syndrome with significant systemic involvement, major congenital malformations) that could interfere with follow-up or postoperative evaluation; Inability to complete the required postoperative follow-up visits. The collected data included: birth and medical history, family history, cataract morphology, metabolic and infection screening, and genetic analysis. Preoperative ophthalmological data, obtained during a visit under general anesthesia, included: evaluation of the anterior segment (cornea, anterior chamber, lens, and lens capsule) using a surgical microscope; applanation tonometry with the Perkins tonometer; measurement of corneal diameters (vertical and horizontal) with a surgical caliper; axial length measurement using A-scan ultrasonography; fundus evaluation using binocular indirect ophthalmoscopy; and B-scan ultrasonography. All cataract surgeries were performed under general anesthesia by a single surgeon (G.L.G.) using the STELLARIS ELITE PhacoVitrectomy platform (Bausch & Lomb, Vaughan, Canada) with the 27G vitrectomy kit, including the 27G cannula system and 27G Bi-Blade cutter. Surgical visualization was achieved using the ZEISS ARTEVO 800 ophthalmic surgical microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany), supplemented by the ZEISS ARTEVO® 800 3D visualization system.

Anterior capsulorhexis, posterior capsulorhexis, lens aspiration, and anterior hyaloid vitrectomy were all performed using the 27G vitrectomy cutter with the following standardized parameters: cut rate set at 800 cuts per minute (cpm) and vacuum up to 400 mmHg (aspiration flow 10–15 cc/min, infusion pressure ~ 25 mmHg) for all cases and for all these surgical steps.

We chose a moderate cut rate (800 cpm) for both capsulorhexis and vitrectomy steps to ensure maximum control, minimize turbulence in the anterior chamber, and reduce the risk of capsule tears or damage to intraocular tissues in these very young patients. Incisions were closed with hydrosealing, and a subconjunctival dexamethasone injection was administered at the end of the procedure. All patients were left aphakic.

The standard postoperative eye drop regimen included combined netilmicin/dexamethasone (six times daily for one week, gradually tapered over six weeks) and tropicamide twice daily for only two weeks. During surgery, anterior chamber stability, leakage from corneal incisions, iris prolapse, and vitreous prolapse were assessed. Postoperative follow-ups were conducted on the first day, first week, and then at 1-, 3-, and 6-months post-surgery. Postoperative data, collected during a visit under general anesthesia one month after surgery, included: evaluation of the anterior segment (cornea, anterior chamber) using a surgical microscope; applanation tonometry with the Perkins tonometer; fundus evaluation using binocular indirect ophthalmoscopy; B-scan ultrasonography; and assessment of complications related to surgery, such as conjunctival hyperemia at the incision site, corneal edema, anterior chamber reaction, pupil shape (regular/irregular), anterior or posterior synechiae. Visual acuity, both preoperative and postoperative, was assessed by observing the children’s visual behavior in response to various stimuli.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the mean values and standard deviations of all numerical variables, including axial length, corneal diameters, and intraocular pressure (IOP). Frequencies and percentages were calculated for categorical variables, such as the etiology and morphology of congenital cataracts, as well as the incidence of intraoperative and postoperative complications.To explore potential relationships among continuous variables, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. For comparisons of preoperative and postoperative IOP, a paired t-test was performed to assess the effectiveness of the surgical technique in reducing intraocular pressure. Independent t-tests were used to compare axial length and corneal diameters between male and female patients. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 20.

Results

This retrospective study included 22 pediatric patients (44 eyes) with bilateral congenital cataracts. Among these patients, 12 (54.5%) were female and 10 (45.5%) were male, corresponding to 24 eyes from female patients (54.5%) and 20 eyes from male patients (45.5%). The patients' age ranged from 3 to 5 months (mean age). All patients had bilateral congenital cataracts.

The etiology of congenital cataracts in this cohort was classified as presumably idiopathic in 11 patients (50%) and familial in 11 patients (50%), based on clinical history and available genetic data. The morphology of congenital cataracts in this cohort was classified as nuclear in approximately 60% of cases (26 eyes) and polar in the remaining 40% (18 eyes). The mean axial length in this cohort was approximately 17.88 ± 1.44 mm. The mean axial length in cataractous eyes was slightly greater in male patients (18.2 ± 1.66 mm, n = 16 eyes) than in female patients (17.89 ± 1.56 mm, n = 28 eyes).

The mean preoperative IOP was 15.8 ± 5,7 mmHg, decreasing to 12.2 ± 1.1 mmHg postoperatively.A paired t-test was used to compare preoperative and postoperative IOP: t = 5.929; p = 4.64 × 10⁻7 (highly significant). The mean horizontal corneal diameter in this cohort of 44 eyes was 10.05 ± 0.34 mm, ranging between 9.8 and 10.3 mm. The mean horizontal diameter was 10.12 ± 0.31 mm in boys (n = 16 eyes) and 10.00 ± 0.35 mm in girls (n = 28 eyes).

The mean vertical corneal diameter was 9.6 ± 0.38 mm, ranging between 9.2 and 10.0 mm. The mean vertical diameter was 9.65 ± 0.36 mm in boys and 9.58 ± 0.39 mm in girls.

Fundus examination and B-scan ultrasonography ruled out any associated posterior segment pathologies in all patients. The macula and optic disc appeared normal, and no retinal peripheral lesions were detected.

The average duration of the surgical procedure was 30 ± 5 min.

Intraoperatively, the anterior chamber remained stable in all patients, with no iris or vitreous prolapse observed. All patients remained aphakic after surgery. On postoperative day 1, conjunctival congestion and hyperemia were observed in 13 eyes (30%), while a mild anterior chamber reaction was noted in 5 eyes (12%). Both conditions resolved completely within one week after surgery. Transient postoperative intraocular pressure (IOP) elevation was observed in 2 eyes (5%).Other significant postoperative complications, such as inflammatory membranes, irregular pupils, posterior/anterior synechiae, and corneal neovascularization, were not observed during the entire follow-up period. Additionally, there were no cases of retinal detachment or secondary glaucoma.In this age group (3–5 months), it is not possible to obtain a precise measurement of visual acuity due to the patients’ limited ability to cooperate with standard tests. Therefore, instead of quantitative visual acuity assessment, we evaluated visual function based on visual behavior and the infants’ responses to various stimuli, such as following a light source, tracking moving objects, or reacting to visual targets. This approach is widely accepted and represents the standard method for assessing visual function in infants of this age.

Discussion

The results of this retrospective study demonstrate the efficacy and safety of 27-gauge vitrectomy in the surgical management of congenital cataracts in pediatric patients. This innovative technique offers numerous advantages over traditional procedures using larger gauge instruments (23G and 25G), significantly improving visual outcomes and reducing the risk of intra- and postoperative complications.

The use of the 27G vitrectomy cutter enabled more controlled and precise capsulorhexis due to the enhanced maneuverability of the instruments and the reduced force exerted on the capsule. This eliminated instances of capsulorhexis extension, one of the most common risks associated with congenital cataract surgery. These findings are consistent with those reported in the literature by Liu et al. (2012) [7] and Chan et al. (2023) [8], which emphasize the importance of smaller gauge instruments in enhancing surgical precision and minimizing damage to surrounding tissues.

The mean duration of the surgery was significantly reduced compared to previous techniques, with a lower usage of balanced salt solution (BSS). This limited fluid recirculation within the anterior chamber, preserving the integrity of ocular structures and reducing the risk of postoperative corneal edema, as corroborated by Memon et al. (2016) [9] and Cao et al. (2019) [10].

The smaller incisions associated with the 27G technique contributed to better intraocular pressure (IOP) stability during surgery. This aspect is particularly relevant in pediatric patients, where managing IOP is complex due to the anatomical structure of the eye. Previous studies, such as Rastogi et al. (2018) [11], highlight that the use of smaller gauge instruments reduces IOP fluctuations, improving intraoperative safety.

Unlike traditional techniques, this method did not demonstrate significant conjunctival hyperemia beyond the first postoperative day. This outcome, attributable to the minimal incisions and the absence of sutures, aligns with studies by Chee and Lam (2009) [12] and Chan et al. (2023) [8], which reported a rapid resolution of inflammatory reactions in patients treated with small-gauge instruments.

No patients experienced persistent IOP elevation during the postoperative period, a result that reinforces the notion that the 27G technique is less traumatic to internal ocular structures. These findings are consistent with those reported by Hong et al. (2009) [13], who noted a low rate of postoperative complications related to intraocular pressure.

A notable aspect of this technique is the absence of induced astigmatism, attributable to the reduced size of corneal incisions and the lack of sutures. Although the objective assessment of astigmatism in neonates is challenging, long-term follow-up may confirm these findings. Previous studies, such as those by Li et al. (2014) [14], have already suggested that smaller incisions significantly decrease the risk of postoperative astigmatism.

In our series, we observed an absence of early postoperative inflammation, pupil irregularity, and wound site problems—complications that are frequently encountered in infants. Importantly, despite not using sutures at the corneal entry sites, no problems with corneal incisions were observed in any case. This emphasizes the safety of this minimally invasive approach. The absence of sutures not only reduces the risk of infection and local irritation, but also eliminates the need for a second procedure under general anesthesia for suture removal, sparing infants additional risk and discomfort. Moreover, avoiding sutures may facilitate an earlier start of refractive rehabilitation, potentially leading to better visual outcomes.

This minimally invasive surgical approach likely contributes to the lower incidence of postoperative inflammation and wound-related complications observed in our cohort. These advantages should be further highlighted and investigated in future studies, as they represent a significant benefit for pediatric patients undergoing cataract surgery.

The corneal incisions created with 27G instruments did not require sutures for closure, providing an additional advantage by reducing surgical time and the risk of suture-related complications, such as infections or local irritation. This minimally invasive approach has also been well-documented by Meier et al. (2010) [15].

No significant intraoperative complications, such as iris or vitreous prolapse or anterior chamber instability, were observed, underscoring the reliability of the technique. Furthermore, the absence of severe long-term complications, such as secondary glaucoma or retinal detachment, confirms the high safety profile of this procedure. The study's limitations include its retrospective nature and the inability to accurately assess visual acuity in neonates, relying instead on the observation of visual behavior. This study is a retrospective, non-comparative analysis, which represents a limitation. Therefore, future prospective and controlled studies with longer follow-up periods and objective visual acuity assessments are needed to further confirm the long-term efficacy and safety of this surgical approach.

Conclusions

This retrospective study demonstrates that 27-gauge vitrectomy is a precise, effective, and safe surgical approach for the management of congenital cataracts in pediatric patients. Its numerous advantages, including enhanced surgical precision, reduced tissue trauma, lower incidence of complications, and improved postoperative recovery, make it a valuable option for improving visual outcomes and quality of life in children affected by this condition. This technique represents a promising option for congenital cataract surgery, offering several advantages over traditional methods using larger gauge instruments. Future prospective studies with longer follow-up periods and objective visual acuity assessments are needed to further confirm the long-term efficacy and safety of this surgical approach.
