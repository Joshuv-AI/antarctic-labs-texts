Abstract
. We initiate the study of the effective content of K K -theory for C ∗ \mathrm{C}^{*} -algebras. We prove that there are computable functors which associate, to a computably enumerable presentation of a C ∗ \mathrm{C}^{*} -algebra 𝐀 \mathbf{A} , computably enumerable presentations of the abelian groups K 0 ​ ( 𝐀 ) K_{0}(\mathbf{A}) and K 1 ​ ( 𝐀 ) K_{1}(\mathbf{A}) . When 𝐀 \mathbf{A} is stably finite, we show that the positive cone of K 0 ​ ( 𝐀 ) K_{0}(\mathbf{A}) is computably enumerable. We strengthen the results in the case that 𝐀 \mathbf{A} is a UHF algebra by showing that the aforementioned presentation of K 0 ​ ( 𝐀 ) K_{0}(\mathbf{A}) is actually computable. In the UHF case, we also show that 𝐀 \mathbf{A} has a computable presentation precisely when K 0 ​ ( 𝐀 ) K_{0}(\mathbf{A}) has a computable presentation, which in turn is equivalent to the supernatural number of 𝐀 \mathbf{A} being lower semicomputable; we give an example that shows that this latter equivalence cannot be improved to requiring that the supernatural number of 𝐀 \mathbf{A} is computable. Finally, we prove that every UHF algebra is computably categorical.



Introduction

One of the most important invariants attached to a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
is the
K
-theory of
\mathbf{A}
, which consists of a partially
ordered abelian group
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})
and another abelian group
K_{1}(\mathbf{A})
. Initially arising in algebraic topology,
K
-theory has played a
central role in the classification program for
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras. With hindsight, Glimm’s classification of Uniformly Hyperfinite (UHF)
algebras
[
9
]
in terms of supernatural numbers can be viewed as showing that if
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
are UHF algebras, then

\mathbf{A}\cong\mathbf{B}
as
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras if and only if
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})\cong K_{0}(\mathbf{B})
as abelian groups. Elliott then classified
Approximately Finite-Dimensional (AF) algebras by their
ordered

K_{0}
groups. This result launched a line of research in classifying
increasingly general classes of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras through
K
-theoretic data, culminating in the recent proof
[
3
]
that a
very wide class of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras (namely, the simple, separable, nuclear, unital,
\mathcal{Z}
-stable algebras satisfying the Universal Coefficient
Theorem) are classifiable by data built on their
K
-theory.

1

It may seem puzzling that this large set of conditions yields a very large class of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras. However, it is known that many different methods for constructing
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras give rise to algebras that do satisfy the hypotheses of the classification theorem; see
[
13
]
for a survey of the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra classification program.

In this paper, we address the question of whether the
K
-theory of a separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra can be effectively computed. Our main results along these lines involve the notions of presentations of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras and groups. By a presentation
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
of
\mathbf{A}
, we mean a countable sequence from
\mathbf{A}
that generates
\mathbf{A}
as a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra (so only separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras have presentations); the presentation is computable if there is an algorithm such that, upon input a
*
-polynomial
p(\vec{x})
with coefficients from
\mathbb{Q}(i)
and no constant term, a tuple
\vec{a}
from the aforementioned countable sequence, and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, returns
q\in\mathbb{Q}^{>0}
such
|\|p(\vec{a})\|-q|<2^{-k}
. If instead the algorithm only returns a computable sequence of upper bounds converging to
\|p(\vec{a})\|
, then we say that the presentation is computably enumerable. The definition of a presentation of a group is slightly more involved; see Subsection
2.2
for the precise definition as well as what it means for a presentation of a group to be computable or computably enumerable. (We note that the latter condition coincides with the notion of a recursive presentation of a group from algorithmic group theory.)

Our main results are the following:

Main Theorem 1
.

There is a computable functor
K_{0}
so that
K_{0}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
is a computably enumerable presentation of the abelian group
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})
whenever
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is a computably enumerable presentation of a unital separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
. Moreover, if
\mathbf{A}
is stably finite, then the positive cone of
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})
is a computably enumerable subset of
K_{0}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
.

Main Theorem 2
.

There is a computable functor
K_{1}
so that
K_{1}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
is a computably enumerable presentation of the abelian group
K_{1}(\mathbf{A})

whenever
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is a computably enumerable presentation of a unital separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
.

Presently, we do not know if
K_{0}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
is computable provided
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is computable. In the case of UHF algebras (for which computably enumerable presentations are automatically computable), we do indeed obtain stronger results.

Main Theorem 3
.

If
\mathbf{A}
is a UHF algebra with a computable presentation
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
, then
K_{0}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
is a computable presentation of
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})
and the positive cone
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})^{+}
is a computable subset of
K_{0}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
.

UHF algebras can be classified either by their
K_{0}
groups or by their
supernatural number
(see Section
4
below for the definition).

Main Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a UHF algebra. The following are equivalent:

(1)

The
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
has a computable presentation.

(2)

The abelian group
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})
has a computable presentation.

(3)

The abelian group
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})
has a computably enumerable presentation.

(4)

The supernatural number of
\mathbf{A}
is lower semicomputable.

Here, the supernatural number is lower semicomputable if, roughly speaking, it can be computably approximated from below; see Definition
4.16
for a more precise statement. One might have expected that a UHF algebra is computably presentable if and only if its supernatural number is in fact computable. However, we give an example of a lower semicomputable supernatural number with Turing degree
\mathbf{0}^{\prime\prime}
(and so, in particular, is not computable); by our theorem above, the associated UHF algebra is computably presentable.

Finally, we prove the following:

Main Theorem 5
.

Every UHF algebra is computably categorical. That is, any two computable presentations of a given UHF algebra are computably isomorphic.

1.
Preliminaries

1.1.
Presentations of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra. A
presentation
of
\mathbf{A}
is a sequence
(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots)
of points in
\mathbf{A}
such that the *-subalgebra of
\mathbf{A}
generated by
\{a_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}
is dense in
\mathbf{A}
. The points
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots
are called the
special points
of the presentation, while the points of the form
p(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n})
, where
p(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
is a
*
-polynomial with coefficients from
\mathbb{Q}(i)
with no constant term (as
n
varies), are called the
rational points
of the presentation.

We note that this definition of “presentation” is not the same as a presentation by generators and relations, but there are connections between the two notions (see
[
10
, Subsection 2.2]
).

Throughout this paper, unless explicitly stated otherwise,
\mathbf{A}
denotes a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra (although at some points we will consider
nonunital algebras).
For a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
denotes a presentation of
\mathbf{A}
.

Throughout, we always consider
\mathbb{C}
as equipped with its
standard presentation
whose special points are
\mathbb{Q}(i)
.

By standard techniques (see, for example,
[
2
, Section 2]
), we can obtain an effective (but typically non-injective) list of the rational points of a presentation of a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra. When we say, for example, that an algorithm takes a rational point
q
of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
as input, we really mean that we have fixed an effective list
(q_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of rational points of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
and the algorithm takes input an index
i
for which
q=q_{i}
.

By the Church-Turing thesis, every algorithm can be represented as a Turing machine. An
index
(or
code
) of an
algorithm is a natural number that codes a representation of the algorithm as a Turing machine.

Definition 1.2
.

Fix a presentation
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
.

(1)

\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is
computable
if there is an algorithm that, given a rational point
q
and a
k\in\mathbb{N}
, outputs a rational number
r
such that
\left|\|q\|-r\right|<2^{-k}
; an index of such an algorithm is an
index
of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
.

(2)

\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is
left c.e.
if there is an algorithm that, given a rational point
q
, enumerates an increasing sequence
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of rational numbers such that
\lim_{n}r_{n}=\|q\|
; an index of such an algorithm is a

left c.e. index
of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
.

(3)

\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is
right c.e.
if there is an algorithm that, given a rational point
q
, enumerates a decreasing sequence
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of rational numbers such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}r_{n}=\|q\|
; an index of such an algorithm is a

right c.e. index
of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
.

It is immediate from the definitions that a presentation is computable if and only if it is both left and right c.e. Fox
[
7
, Theorem 3.14]
has shown that if
\mathbf{A}
is simple, then every right c.e. presentation of
\mathbf{A}
is computable.
In the sequel, we shall refer to right-c.e. presentations simply as
c.e. presentations
; for some justification behind this decision, see
[
7
, Theorem 3.3]
.

With each presentation of
\mathbf{A}
, there is an associated class of computable points. These are defined as follows.

Definition 1.3
.

An element
a\in\mathbf{A}
is a
computable point of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
if there is an algorithm such that, given
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
returns a rational point
b
of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
such that
\|a-b\|<2^{-k}
; an index of such an algorithm is a

\mathbf{A}^{\#}
-index for
a
.

In other words, a computable point of
A^{\#}
is one that can be effectively approximated by rational points of

\mathbf{A}^{\#}
with arbitarily good precision.

Similarly, with each presentation of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
, there is an associated class of computable sequences.

Definition 1.4
.

A sequence
(p_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of points in
\mathbf{A}
is a
computable sequence of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
if
there is an algorithm that, given any
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
, computes a rational vector
\rho
of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}

so that
\|\rho-p_{n}\|<2^{-k}
. An index of such an algorithm is a
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
-index of

(p_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

We regard the collection of presentations of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
algebras as a category in which the morphisms are the
computable
*
-homomorphisms. These maps are defined as follows.

Definition 1.5
.

A bounded linear map
\phi:\mathbf{A}_{0}\to\mathbf{A}_{1}
is a
computable map from
\mathbf{A}_{0}^{\#}
to
\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\#}
if there is an algorithm that,
given a rational point
a
of
\mathbf{A}_{0}^{\#}
and a
k\in\mathbb{N}
, computes a rational point
b
of
\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\#}
such that

\|\phi(a)-b\|<2^{-k}
;
an index of such an algorithm is an
(A_{0}^{\#},A_{1}^{\#})
-index
of
\phi
.

Put another way,
\phi
is computable if the
\phi
-image of every rational point of
\mathbf{A}_{0}^{\#}
is a computable point of
\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\#}

whose index can be computed from the rational point of
\mathbf{A}_{0}^{\#}
.
It is easily verified that the inverse of a computable
*
-isomorphism is computable as well.
We note that there is a general definition of a computable map between presentations of metric structures;
when the function is a bounded linear map between normed spaces, the general definition specializes to the definition given here.

Suppose
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
are
\mathrm{C}^{*}
algebra. Then
\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
algebra, where the operations are defined
coordinate-wise and the norm of

is
\max\{\|a\|,\|b\|\}
.
Given presentations
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
and
\mathbf{B}^{\#}
, we define
\mathbf{A}^{\#}\times\mathbf{B}^{\#}
to be the presentation of

\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}
whose special points are the points formed by pairing rational points of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
and
\mathbf{B}^{\#}
.
Here, we view these points as enumerated by
\mathbb{N}
by fixing some computable bijection between

\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{N}^{2}
. Note that
\mathbf{A}^{\#}\times\mathbf{B}^{\#}
is in fact the product in the category of presentations of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
algebras. If
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
and
\mathbf{B}^{\#}
are computable (resp. c.e.), then it is readily verified that
\mathbf{A}^{\#}\times\mathbf{B}^{\#}
is also computable (resp. c.e.).

1.2.
Matrix amplifications

The definition of
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})
involves projections in matrix algebras over
\mathbf{A}
, so we must consider how to produce a (computable) presentation of
M_{n}(\mathbf{A})
from a (computable) presentation of
\mathbf{A}
. We begin by fixing some notations. Throughout, we fix a positive integer
n
.

First, we let
(E^{n}_{r,s}(\mathbf{A}))_{r,s\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}
denote
the standard system of matrix units for
M_{n}(\mathbf{A})
, that is,
E^{n}_{r,s}(\mathbf{A})
is the element of
M_{n}(\mathbf{A})
whose
(r,s)

1_{\mathbf{A}}
and with all other entries
0
; when
\mathbf{A}=\mathbb{C}
, we simply write
E^{n}_{r,s}
for the matrix units of
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

Throughout this paper, we let
\|\cdot\|
denote the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-norm on
M_{n}(\mathbf{A})
. Occasionally, we will have the need to consider other norms on
M_{n}(\mathbf{A})
. For
A=(a_{r,s})_{r,s\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}\in M_{n}(\mathbf{A})
, we set
\|A\|_{\max}=\max_{r,s}\|a_{r,s}\|
and
\|A\|_{1}=\sum_{r,s}\|a_{r,s}\|
. It is well-known that

\|A\|_{\max}\leq\|A\|\leq\|A\|_{1}
and thus

\frac{1}{n^{2}}\|A\|_{1}\leq\|A\|
.

We let
()^{3}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}^{3}
denote a computable bijection with component functions
()^{3}_{0}
,
()^{3}_{1}
,
()^{3}_{2}
, so
(k)^{3}=((k)^{3}_{0},(k)^{3}_{1},(k)^{3}_{2}))
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

For
m
a nonzero integer, let
\mbox{rem}(m,n)

denote the remainder when
m
is divided by
n
. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, set:

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(k)^{3}_{0}

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(n,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\mbox{rem}((k)^{3}_{1},n)

\displaystyle\phi_{2}(n,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\mbox{rem}((k)^{3}_{2},n).

We are finally ready to define the presentation on
M_{n}(\mathbf{A})
induced by a presentation on
\mathbf{A}
.

Definition 1.6
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is a presentation of
\mathbf{A}
and let
v_{m}
denote the
m
-th special point of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
. We define

to be the presentation of

whose

-th special point is
v_{\phi_{0}(k)}E^{n}_{\phi_{1}(n,k),\phi_{2}(n,k)}(\mathbf{A})
.

Since

,
it is straightforward to verify that

is indeed a presentation of

. However, we have a much stronger result.

Proposition 1.7

If
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is c.e. (resp. computable), then

is also c.e. (resp. computable).

Proof.

Set
\mathbf{A}_{1}=M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathbf{A}
.
Let
\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\#}
be the presentation of
\mathbf{A}_{1}
whose

-th distinguished

-th distinguished point of the standard presentation of

(defined as in Definition

)
and the

\mathbf{A}^{\#}

By

1.3.
C.e. open and c.e. closed sets

Certain subsets of
\mathbf{A}
with particular computability properties will play significant roles for us. First, by a
rational open ball
of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
we mean an open ball in
\mathbf{A}
with rational radius and centered at a rational point of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
.

Definition 1.8
.

If
C\subseteq\mathbf{A}
is closed, we say that
C
is a
c.e.-closed subset of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
if
the set of all open rational balls of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
that intersect
C
is c.e.

Definition 1.9
.

If
U\subseteq\mathbf{A}
is open, we say that
U
is a
c.e.-open set of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
if
there is a c.e. set
\mathcal{S}
of rational open balls so that

U=\bigcup\mathcal{S}
.

The import of a set
U
being c.e.-open is that, provided the presentation is c.e., if a computable point
a
belongs to
U
, then an effective search will eventually lead us to this fact. Indeed, one must simply search for a rational open ball
B(x;r)
in the c.e. set
\mathcal{S}
from the definition and a rational point
b
such that
\|a-b\|<\epsilon
for rational
\epsilon
small enough so that
\|x-b\|<r-\epsilon
.

By an index of a c.e. open set we mean an index for the c.e. set of open balls whose union comprises the set; the index of a c.e. closed set is defined analogously.

The following fact is probably folklore; we include a proof for the sake of the reader. In the following proof (as well as later in the
paper), it will be useful to observe that if
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is a c.e. presentation, then the subset relation between rational open balls of

\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is also c.e. In fact, this holds whenever
\mathbf{A}
is a vector space, because in that case
B(\rho_{0},r_{0})\subseteq B(\rho_{1},r_{1})
if and only if
\|\rho_{0}-\rho_{1}\|+r_{0}<r_{1}
.

Lemma 1.10
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is a c.e. presentation. Further suppose that
U
is a c.e. open subset of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
and
C
is a c.e. closed
subset of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
such that
U\cap C\not=\emptyset
. Then
U\cap C
contains a computable point. Moreover, an index of this computable
point can be computed from indices of
U
and
C
.

Proof.

First search for the first rational ball
B_{0}
of radius less than
1
that is contained in
U
and intersects
C
. Then search for the first
rational ball
B_{1}
of radius less than
1/2
contained in
B_{0}
that intersects
C
. Continuing in this manner, the centers of these balls
converge to a point of
C
that is necessarily computable.
∎

In continuous model theory, the definable sets in metric structures are characterized by a condition known as
weak stability

(see
[
6
, Definition 3.2.4]
). Many proofs that sets are c.e.-closed can be described in terms of a computable version of
weak stability. The relevant notion of computable weak stability was isolated by Fox, Goldbring, and Hart
[
8
, Definition 5]
. We recall it here (in a slighlty different guise) and take the opportunity to show that computable weak stability
always gives rise to c.e.-closed sets.

Definition 1.11
.

Suppose that
\vec{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
is a tuple of variables,
C_{1},\ldots,C_{N}\in\mathbb{N}
,
and
p_{1}(\vec{x}),\ldots,p_{M}(\vec{x})
are rational
*
-polynomials. Consider the set

\mathcal{R}=\{p_{i}(\vec{x})=0\ :\ i=1,\ldots,M\}\cup\{\|x_{j}\|\leq C_{j}\ :\ j=1,\ldots,N\}

of relations.

(1)

For a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
algebra
\mathbf{B}
and
w_{1},\ldots,w_{N}\in\mathbf{B}
,
let
\mathcal{R}^{\mathbf{B}}
denote the quantity

\max(\{\|p_{j}(\vec{w})\|\ :\ j\in\{1,\ldots,M\}\}\cup\{\|w_{j}\|-C_{j}\ :j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\}

and write
\mathbf{B}\models\mathcal{R}(\vec{w})
if

p_{j}(\vec{w})=0
for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
and
\|w_{j}\|\leq C_{j}
for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.

(2)

A function
g:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
is a
modulus of weak stability
for

\mathcal{R}
provided that, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, every
\mathrm{C}^{*}
algebra
\mathbf{B}
,
and every
w_{1},\ldots,w_{N}\in\mathbf{B}
, if
\mathcal{R}^{\mathbf{B}}(\vec{w})<2^{-g(k)}
,
then there exists
z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}\in\mathbf{B}
so that

\max_{j}\|w_{j}-z_{j}\|<2^{-k}
and
\mathcal{B}\models\mathcal{R}(\vec{z})
.

(3)

We say that
\mathcal{R}
is
weakly stable
if it has a modulus of weak stability and
computably weakly stable
if it has a computable modulus of weak stability.

Theorem 1.12
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is a c.e. presentation and suppose that
E\subseteq\mathbf{A}^{N}
is defined by a computably weakly stable set of
relations. Then
E
is a c.e.-closed subset of
(\mathbf{A}^{\#})^{N}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{R}=\{p_{i}(\vec{x})=0\ :\ i=1,\ldots,M\}\cup\{\|x_{j}\|\leq C_{j}\ :\ j=1,\ldots,N\}

be a computably weakly stable set of relations that defines
E
.
Fix a computable modulus of weak stability
g
for
\mathcal{R}
.

Let
S_{0}
be the set of all rational balls of
(\mathbf{A}^{\#})^{N}
of the form
B(\vec{a};2^{-k})
,
where
\max_{i}\|p_{i}(\vec{a})\|<2^{-g(k)}
and
\|a_{j}\|<C_{j}+2^{-g(k)}
for all
j=1,\ldots,N
.
It is clear that
S_{0}
is a c.e. set.

We now note that, for every
\vec{u}\in E
and every
\epsilon>0
, there is a ball
B(\vec{a};2^{-k})\in S_{0}

such that
u\in B(\vec{a};r)
and
2^{-k}<\epsilon
. To see this, choose
k\in\mathbb{N}
so that

2^{-k}<\epsilon
.
Using the continuity of the polynomials
p_{i}(\vec{x})
, we may choose a tuple of rational vectors
\vec{a}

sufficiently close to
\vec{u}
so that
\|\vec{u}-\vec{a}\|<2^{-k}
,
\max_{i}\|p_{i}(\vec{a})\|<2^{-g(k)}
,
and
\max_{j}\|a_{j}\|-C_{j}<2^{-g(k)}
. Then
B(\vec{a};2^{-k})
is as required.

The theorem now follows by noticing that for a rational open ball
B
of
(\mathbf{A}^{N})^{\#}
,
B
contains an element of
E
if and only if
B

includes an element of
S_{0}
. Indeed, first suppose that
B
contains the element
\vec{e}
of
E
and set

\epsilon=d(p,\mathbf{A}^{N}\setminus B)
. By the previous paragraph, there is
B^{\prime}\in S_{0}
so that
\vec{e}\in B^{\prime}
and

\mbox{diam}(B^{\prime})<\epsilon
, whence
B^{\prime}\subseteq B
. In the other direction, suppose that
B
includes
the element
B(\vec{a};r)
of
S_{0}
. Then by the definitions of
g
and
S_{0}
, there is
\vec{e}\in E
such that

\|\vec{a}-\vec{e}\|<r
. Hence,
\vec{e}\in B(\vec{a};r)\subseteq B
.
∎

Of particular importance for us are the relations defining projections and systems of matrix units, both of which are shown to be given by computably weakly stable relations in
[
8
, Examples 12]
. We therefore have the following two corollaries.

Corollary 1.13
.

If
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is a c.e. presentation of a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
, then the set of all projections in
\mathbf{A}
is a c.e.-closed subset of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
.

A matrix
(a_{r,s})_{r,s}\in M_{n}(\mathbf{A})
is an

n\times n
system of matrix units for
\mathbf{A}
if

a_{r,s}^{*}=a_{s,r}
and if
a_{r,s}a_{r^{\prime},s^{\prime}}=\delta_{s,r^{\prime}}a_{r,s^{\prime}}
.
We may also regard an
n\times n
system of
matrix units as a point in
\mathbf{A}^{n^{2}}
.

Corollary 1.14
.

If
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is a c.e. presentation of a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
, then for every positive integer
n
,
the set of all
n\times n
systems of
matrix units of
\mathbf{A}
is a c.e.-closed subset of
(\mathbf{A}^{\#})^{n^{2}}
and of

M_{n}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is a c.e. presentation of a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
, and
let
E
denote the set of
n\times n
systems of matrix units for
\mathbf{A}
.
That
E
is a c.e. closed subset of
(\mathbf{A}^{\#})^{n^{2}}
follows directly now from
the aforementioned result of
[
8
]
while the fact that it is a c.e. closed subset of
M_{n}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})

follows from the inequalities
\|A\|_{\max}\leq\|A\|\leq\|A\|_{1}
.
∎

In Section
4
, we will need the following variant of the previous corollary:

Corollary 1.15
.

If
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is a c.e. presentation of a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
, then for every positive integer
n
,
the set of all
n\times n
systems
(a_{r,s})_{r,s}
of
matrix units of
\mathbf{A}
for which
\sum_{r=1}^{n}a_{rr}=1_{\mathbf{A}}
is a c.e.-closed subset of
(\mathbf{A}^{\#})^{n^{2}}
and of

M_{n}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be the relations obtained from the above relations for matrix units with the additional relation
\sum_{r=1}^{n}x_{rr}=1
. Fix
\epsilon>0
and suppose that
\mathcal{R}^{\mathbf{A}}(a_{r,s})<\delta
for
\delta>0
to be specified shortly. If
\delta
is sufficiently small, by the previous corollary, there are actual matrix units

such that

for all

. Note that the

is a projection. Moreover,

∎

2.
Computing
K_{0}

The goal of this section is to establish the existence of a computable functor that takes a c.e. presentation of a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
and produces a c.e. presentation of
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})
.

2.1.
Murray-von Neumann equivalence

We begin by understanding the complexity of the Murray-von Neumann equivalence relation on projections in matrix amplifications of
\mathbf{A}
. We let
P_{n}(\mathbf{A})
denote the set of all projections in
M_{n}(\mathbf{A})
, and set
P_{<\omega}(\mathbf{A})=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}P_{n}(\mathbf{A})
. For
p,q\in P_{<\omega}(\mathbf{A})
, we set

p\oplus q=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}p&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&q\\
\end{array}\right),

and note that
p\oplus q\in P_{<\omega}(\mathbf{A})
. Recall that
p\in P_{m}(\mathbf{A})
and
q\in P_{n}(\mathbf{A})
are
Murray von-Neumann equivalent
, denoted
p\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}q
, if there exists
v\in M_{m,n}(\mathbf{A})
so that
p=vv^{*}
and
q=v^{*}v
. We let
[p]_{\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}}
denote the Murray-von Neumann equivalence class of
p
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is a c.e. presentation of
\mathbf{A}
. Then there is a c.e.-open subset
U
of
M_{n}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})\times M_{n}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
such that, for all
p,q\in P_{n}(\mathbf{A})
, one has
(p,q)\in U
if and only if
p\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}q
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the set of all pairs
(B,B^{\prime})
of rational open balls of

M_{n}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
for which there exists a sequence
B_{0},\ldots,B_{k}
of rational open balls of

M_{4n}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

\sup\{\|a-b\|\ :a\in B_{j}\ \text{ and }\ b\in B_{j+1}\}<1
for all
j=0,\ldots,k-1
.

(2)

B_{j}\cap P_{4n}(\mathbf{A})\neq\emptyset
for all
j=0,\ldots,k
.

(3)

For all
a\in B
and
b\in B_{0}
,

\|((a\oplus\mathbf{0}_{n})\oplus\mathbf{0}_{2n})-b\|<1
.

(4)

For all
a\in B_{k}
and
b\in B^{\prime}
,

\|a-((b\oplus\mathbf{0}_{n})\oplus\mathbf{0}_{2n})\|<1
.

By Corollary
1.13
,
P_{4n}(\mathbf{A})
is a c.e.-closed subset of
M_{4n}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
, whence it follows that

\mathcal{S}
is c.e. Set
U=\bigcup\{B\times B^{\prime}\ :\ (B,B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}\}
.
If
(B,B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}
, then
B\times B^{\prime}
is a c.e.-open subset of
M_{n}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})\times M_{n}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
. It follows that
U

is a c.e.-open subset of
M_{4n}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
.

We first show that, if
(p,q)\in U\cap(P_{n}(\mathbf{A})\times P_{n}(\mathbf{A}))
,
then
p\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}q
. To see this, suppose
(p,q)\in U\cap(P_{n}(\mathbf{A})\times P_{n}(\mathbf{A}))
. Take rational open balls
B,B^{\prime},B_{0}
,
\ldots
,
B_{k}

of
M_{4n}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
satisfying the above conditions with

p\in B
and
q\in B^{\prime}
.
For each
j=1,\ldots,k
, fix
p_{j}\in B_{j}\cap P_{4n}(\mathbf{A})
. Then,

\|(p\oplus\mathbf{0}_{n})\oplus\mathbf{0}_{2n}-p_{0}\|<1
,
\|(q\oplus\mathbf{0}_{n})\oplus\mathbf{0}_{2n}-p_{k}\|<1
, and

\|p_{j}-p_{j+1}\|<1
for
j=0,\ldots,k-1
. We may now use
[
12
, Propositions 2.2.4 and 2.2.7]
to conclude that

(p\oplus\mathbf{0}_{n})\oplus\mathbf{0}_{2n}\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}p_{0}\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}p_{1}\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}\cdots\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}p_{k}\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}(q\oplus\mathbf{0}_{n})\oplus\mathbf{0}_{2n}.

By
[
12
, Proposition 2.3.2.i]
,
p\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}(p\oplus\mathbf{0}_{n})\oplus\mathbf{0}_{2n}
and

q\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}(q\oplus\mathbf{0}_{n})\oplus\mathbf{0}_{2n}
, whence the claim follows.

Conversely, suppose that
p,q\in P_{n}(\mathbf{A})
are such that

p\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}q
. We wish to show that
(p,q)\in U
. Set
p^{\prime}=(p\oplus\mathbf{0}_{n})\oplus\mathbf{0}_{2n}
, and set

q^{\prime}=(q\oplus\mathbf{0}_{n})\oplus\mathbf{0}_{2n}
. Note that
p^{\prime}\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}q^{\prime}
. By
[
12
, Proposition 2.2.8]
,

p^{\prime}
is homotopic to
q^{\prime}
in
P_{4n}(\mathbf{A})
; that is, there is a continuous function

f:[0,1]\rightarrow P_{4n}(\mathbf{A})
with
f(0)=p^{\prime}
and
f(1)=q^{\prime}
.
Since
[0,1]
is compact,
f
is uniformly continuous. Thus, there exist

p_{0},\ldots,p_{k}\in\mbox{ran}(f)
so that
\|p_{j}-p_{j+1}\|<1
for
j=1,\ldots,k-1

and
\max\{\|p^{\prime}-p_{0}\|,\|q^{\prime}-p_{k}\|\}<1
.
The existence of the required rational open balls needed to show that
(p,q)\in U
now follows from continuity.
∎

We note that the proof of Theorem
2.1
is uniform. Namely, it provides an algorithm
that, given an index for a presentation
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
, computes an index for the c.e.-open set
U
given in the conclusion of the theorem.

Corollary 2.2
.

If
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is c.e., then there is a c.e. subset
Q\subseteq\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
such that,
for all
e,e^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
, if
e
and
e^{\prime}
are
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
-indices of projections
p
and
p^{\prime}
respectively, then

p\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}p^{\prime}
if and only if
Q(e,e^{\prime})
. Furthermore, an index of
Q
can be computed from an index of

\mathbf{A}^{\#}
.

Proof.

Let
\rho_{n}
denote the
n
-th rational point of
M_{n}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
and let
U
be a c.e. open set of
M_{n}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})\times M_{n}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
that satisfies the conclusion of
Theorem
2.1
. The desired set
Q
is the set of those pairs
(e,e^{\prime})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
for which there exist
s,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
(B,B^{\prime})\in U
so that

\phi_{e,s}(k)\downarrow
,
\phi_{e^{\prime},s}(k)\downarrow
,
B(\rho_{\phi_{e}(k)};2^{-k})\subseteq B
, and

B(\rho_{\phi_{e^{\prime}}(k)};2^{-k})\subseteq B^{\prime}
.
∎

2.2.
The Murray-von Neumann semigroup

From now on, let
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A})
denote the set of Murray-von Neumann equivalence classes of elements of
\mathcal{P}_{<\omega}(\mathbf{A})
. The operation
\oplus
induces an abelian semigroup operation on
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A})
, which we continue to denote by
\oplus
.

Our goal in this subsection is to show how the presentation
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
induces a presentation of
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A})
.
First, we must make precise what we mean by a presentation of
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A})
.

Fix a countably infinite set
X=\{x_{0},x_{1},\ldots\}
of indeterminates
and let
D_{\omega}
and
F_{\omega}
denote the free semigroup and free group generated by
X
respectively.
We implicitly view both
D_{\omega}
and
F_{\omega}
as equipped with a computable bijection with
\mathbb{N}
,
whence it makes sense to speak of, say, c.e. and computable subsets of
D_{\omega}
and
F_{\omega}
.
The following definitions are taken from algorithmic group theory.

Definition 2.3
.

If
S
is a semigroup, then a
presentation of
S
is a pair
(S,\nu)
, where
\nu
is a semigroup epimorphism of
D_{\omega}
onto
S
.
If
G
is a group, then a
presentation of
G
is a pair
(G,\nu)
, where
\nu
is a group epimorphism of
F_{\omega}
onto
S
.

As with
\mathrm{C}^{*}
algebras, if
S
is a semigroup or a group, then we use expressions such as
S^{\#}
and
S^{\dagger}
to denote
presentations of
S
.

We introduce some terminology associated with presentations of groups and semigroups.
Suppose
S
is a semigroup or group and
S^{\#}=(S,\nu)
is a presentation of
S
.
If
\nu(w)=a
, then we call
w
an
S^{\#}
-label
of
a
. The
kernel
of
S^{\#}
, denoted
\ker(\nu)
, is the set
of all pairs
(w,w^{\prime})
so that
w
and
w^{\prime}
are
S^{\#}
-labels of the same element of
S
.

Definition 2.4
.

Suppose
S^{\#}
is a presentation of a group or semigroup.
We say that
S^{\#}
is
c.e.
(resp.
computable
) if its kernel is c.e. (resp. computable).

We remark that in algorithmic group theory, c.e. presentations are called
recursive
and
computable presentations are called
decidable
.

Definition 2.5
.

Suppose
S_{0}^{\#}
and
S_{1}^{\#}
are (semi)group presentations. A function
f:S_{0}\rightarrow S_{1}
is a
computable map from
S_{0}^{\#}
to
S_{1}^{\#}
if there is a computable map

F
so that, for every
a\in S_{0}
and every
S_{0}^{\#}
-label
w
of
a
,
F(w)
is an
S_{1}^{\#}
-label of
f(a)
.

To be clear, in the previous definition,
S_{0}
could be a semigroup while
S_{1}
is a group (or vice-versa)

Definition 2.6
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is a presentation of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A})^{\#}
is a presentation of
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A})
. We say that
\mathbf{A}^{\#}

computably supports

\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A})^{\#}
if there is an algorithm that, given
w\in D_{\omega}
, computes
n,e\in\mathbb{N}
so that
e
is an
M_{n}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
-index of
a projection
p\in M_{n}(\mathbf{A})
for which
w
is a
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A})^{\#}
-label of
[p]_{\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}}
.

Proposition 2.7
.

If
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is c.e., then, up to computable isomorphism, there is at most one

\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A})
that is computably supported by

.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A})^{\#}
and
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A})^{\dagger}
are

\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A})
that are computably supported by
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
.
We define a function

as follows.
Given

so that
e_{w}

is a
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A})^{\#}

By Corollary

to be this

is computable. Moreover, by construction,

witnesses that the identity map on

is a computable isomorphism of

\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A})^{\dagger}
.

Proposition 2.8
.

If
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is c.e. and

is a positive integer,

-computable sequence

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}
denote the set of all rational open balls

for which

By Lemma

, for each

, we may compute an

.

.
∎

The proof of Proposition

If
\mathbf{A}^{\#}

Suppose

We define the semigroup epimorphism

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{w}+n_{w^{\prime}}

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(w)\oplus\mathbf{0}_{n_{w^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle q^{\prime}

,

.

If

\mathcal{D}(\psi):\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A}_{0})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A}_{1})

\mathcal{D}(\psi)([p]_{\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}})=[\psi_{n}(p)]_{\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}}

.

.

Suppose
\mathbf{A}_{0}^{\#}
and
\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\#}
are c.e. and
\psi
is a computable

\ast

\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A}_{0}^{\#})
to

Given

n

-index

Once again, the statement of Theorem

As previously remarked, we view presentations of unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras as forming a category; we also consider the full subcategories of c.e. and computable presentations of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras. Similarly, we have the category of (semi)group presentations, where the morphisms are the computable homomorphisms; we analogously can consider the full subcategories of c.e. and computable presentations of (semi)groups. Our work in this section can be summarized as follows.

Corollary 2.11
.

\mathcal{D}
is a computable functor from the category of c.e. presentations of unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras to the category of c.e. semigroup presentations.

In this corollary, when we say that
\mathcal{D}
is a computable functor, we mean that there is an algorithm that, given an index
of an object or morphism, computes an index for its
\mathcal{D}
-image. We will use the word computable in the same way for the other functors defined later in this paper.

2.3.
The
K_{0}
group

For a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
,
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})
is defined by taking the Grothendieck group of the Murray-von Neumann semigroup
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A})
. Consequently, we begin this section by recalling the definition of the Grothendieck functor
\mathcal{G}
from the category of abelian semigroups to the category of
abelian groups.

Fix an abelian semigroup
S
and consider the equivalence relation
\sim
on
S\times S
given by
(a,b)\sim(c,d)
if and only if there exists
z\in S
so that

a+d+z=c+b+z
. Let
\mathcal{G}(S)=(S\times S)/\sim
denote the quotient space. Then the semigroup operation on
S\times S
(defined coordinatewise) induces an abelian group operation on
\mathcal{G}(S)
, where the additive inverse of the equivalence class of
(a,b)
is the equivalence class of
(b,a)
. We call
\mathcal{G}(S)
the
Grothendieck group of the semigroup
S
. We let
\pi:S\times S\to\mathcal{G}(S)
denote the natural quotient map. There is also a map
\gamma_{S}:S\rightarrow\mathcal{G}(S)
given by setting
\gamma_{S}(a)=\pi(a,b)
, where
b\in S
is any fixed element. (The definition of
\gamma_{S}
does not depend on the choice of
b\in S
.) The reader should be warned that
\gamma_{S}
is not injective in general; in fact,
\gamma_{S}
is injective precisely when
S
is a cancellative semigroup. Every element of
\mathcal{G}(S)
can be written as a difference
\gamma_{S}(a)-\gamma_{S}(b)
for some
a,b\in S
.

The Grothendieck group
\mathcal{G}(S)
satisfies the following universal property: if
\phi:S\rightarrow H
is a semigroup morphism from an abelian semigroup to an abelian group, then there is a unique group homomorphism

\psi_{\phi}:\mathcal{G}(S)\to H
so that
\psi_{\phi}\circ\gamma_{S}=\phi
. A consequence of this fact is that
\mathcal{G}
is actually a functor, that is, given a semigroup morphism
\phi:S_{0}\to S_{1}
, there is a unique group morphism
\mathcal{G}(\phi):\mathcal{G}(S_{0})\to\mathcal{G}(S_{1})
such that
\mathcal{G}(\phi)\circ\gamma_{S_{0}}=\gamma_{S_{1}}\circ\phi
.

We now seek to effective this construction.

Definition 2.12
.

Let
S^{\#}
be a presentation of an abelian semigroup and let
\mathcal{G}(S)^{\#}
be a presentation of
\mathcal{G}(S)
.
We say that
\mathcal{G}(S)^{\#}
is
S^{\#}
-universal
if it satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

\gamma_{S}
is a computable map from
S^{\#}
to
\mathcal{G}(S)^{\#}
.

(2)

Whenever
H^{\#}
is a c.e. presentation of an abelian group and
\phi
is a computable semigroup morphism from

S^{\#}
to
H^{\#}
, then
\psi_{\phi}
is a computable map from
\mathcal{G}(S)^{\#}
to
H^{\#}
and, moreover, an
index of
\psi_{\phi}
can be computed from an index of
\phi
.

It is straightforward to show that if there is an
S^{\#}
-universal presentation of
\mathcal{G}(S)
, then this presentation is unique
up to computable isomorphism.

Definition 2.13
.

If
S^{\#}
is a (semi)group presentation, then
X\subseteq S
is said to be a
c.e. subset of
S^{\#}
if
the set of all
S^{\#}
-labels of elements of
S
is c.e.

Proposition 2.14
.

If
S^{\#}
is a c.e. presentation of an abelian semigroup, then there is a (necessarily unique) c.e. presentation

\mathcal{G}(S^{\#})
of
\mathcal{G}(S)
that is
S^{\#}
-universal.

Proof.

We first claim that there is a c.e. presentation
(S\times S)^{\#}
of the semigroup
S\times S
that satisfies the following conditions.

(1)

The projection functions are computable maps from
(S\times S)^{\#}
to
S^{\#}
.

(2)

For each
a\in S
, the injection maps
b\mapsto(a,b)
and
b\mapsto(b,a)

are computable maps from
S^{\#}
to
(S\times S)^{\#}
.

To see this, write
S^{\#}=(S,\nu)
. Fix a computable bijection
\tau=(\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
of
\mathbb{N}
onto

(\mathbb{N}^{<\omega}\times\mathbb{N}^{<\omega})\setminus\{(\emptyset,\emptyset)\}
.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\{0,1\}
, let
\sigma_{k}(x_{n})=\prod_{j<|\tau_{k}(n)|}x_{\tau_{k}(j)}
.
Set
\sigma=(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1})
.
Then, for each
w\in D_{\omega}
, let
\phi(w)=\prod_{j<|w|}\sigma(w_{j})
.
It follows that
(D_{\omega}\times D_{\omega},\phi)
is a computable presentation of

D_{\omega}\times D_{\omega}
.
Let
\phi_{0}
,
\phi_{1}
be the component maps of
\phi
.
Set
\nu_{1}(w)=(\nu\phi_{0}(w),\nu\phi_{1}(w))
, and set
(S\times S)^{\#}=(S\times S,\nu_{1})
.
It follows that
(S\times S)^{\#}
has the required properties. Note also that the Grothendieck equivalence relation
\sim
is a c.e. subset of
(S\times S)^{\#}
.

We next claim that there is a c.e. presentation

\mathcal{G}(S^{\#})
so that the canonical epimorphism
\pi:S\times S\to\mathcal{G}(S)
is a computable map from

(S\times S)^{\#}
to
\mathcal{G}(S^{\#})
. Let
\nu_{1}
be the labeling map of
(S\times S)^{\#}
and set
\nu_{2}=\pi\nu_{1}
. Extend
\nu_{2}
to
F_{\omega}
by defining

\nu_{2}(x_{n}^{-1})=\nu_{2}(x_{n})^{-1}
and
\nu_{2}(\mathbf{1})=\nu_{2}(x_{0}x_{0}^{-1})
, and set
\mathcal{G}(S^{\#})=(\mathcal{G},\nu_{2})
.
Then
\mathcal{G}(S^{\#})
has the required properties.
Note also that, since
\sim
is a c.e. subset of
(S\times S)^{\#}
,
\mathcal{G}(S)^{\#}
is a c.e. presentation of
\mathcal{G}(S)
.

We now argue for the
S^{\#}
-universality of
\mathcal{G}(S^{\#})
.
Let
a_{0}
be the element of
S
whose
S^{\#}
-label is the indeterminate
x_{0}
. We have,

\gamma_{S}(a)=[(a,a_{0})]_{\sim}
for all
s\in S
. Thus, by the computability of the
injections and the canonical epimorphism,
\gamma_{S}
is a computable map from
S^{\#}
to
\mathcal{G}(S^{\#})
.
Suppose now that
H^{\#}
is a c.e. presentation of an abelian group, and suppose
\phi
is a computable
semigroup morphism from
S^{\#}
to
H^{\#}
.
Suppose
g_{0}\in F_{\omega}
is a
\mathcal{G}(S^{\#})
-label of
[(a,b)]_{\sim}
.
By using the canonical epimorphism, we can compute a
(S\times S)^{\#}
-label
w_{1}

of a pair
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
so that

(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\sim(a,b)
. By using the projection maps, we can compute

S^{\#}
-labels of
a^{\prime}
and
b^{\prime}
.
We then compute an
H^{\#}
-label of
\phi(a^{\prime})-\phi(b^{\prime})=\psi_{\phi}([(a,b)]_{\sim})
.
∎

The statement of Proposition
2.14
holds uniformly in the sense that, from an index of
S^{\#}
, one can compute
indices for
\mathcal{G}(S^{\#})
and
\gamma_{S}
.

Corollary 2.15
.

\mathcal{G}
is a computable functor from the category of c.e. presentations of abelian semigroups to the category of
c.e. presentations of abelian groups.

Proof.

It only remains to show that
\mathcal{G}
uniformly transforms computable semigroup homomorphisms into
comptuable group homomorphisms. Suppose

\phi
is a computable semigroup homomorphism from
S_{0}^{\#}
to
S_{1}^{\#}
.
From a
\mathcal{G}(S_{0}^{\#})
-label of
g\in\mathcal{G}(S_{0})
, one can compute
S_{0}^{\#}
-labels of
a,b\in S_{0}
so that

g=\gamma_{S_{0}}(a)-\gamma_{S_{0}}(b)
. Consequently, we can then compute a
\mathcal{G}(S_{1}^{\#})
-label of

\mathcal{G}(\phi)(g)=\gamma_{S_{1}}(\phi(a))-\gamma_{S_{1}}(\phi(b))
.
∎

Given a c.e. presentation
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
, we set
K_{0}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})=(\mathcal{G}\circ\mathcal{D})(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
.

Corollary 2.16
.

K_{0}
is a computable functor from the category of c.e. presentations of unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras to the
category of c.e. presentations of abelian groups.

The previous corollary raises the following natural question.

Question 2.17
.

If
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is computable, is
K_{0}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
also computable? More generally, if
\mathbf{A}
is computably presentable, is
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})
also computably presentable?

We will see that in the case of UHF algebras, the previous question has a positive answer. In order to prove that result, we will require the following:

Proposition 2.18
.

Suppose that
S^{\#}
is a computable presentation of a
cancellative
abelian semigroup
S
. Then
\mathcal{G}(S^{\#})
is also computable.

Proof.

Suppose
x_{1},x_{2}\in F_{\omega}
are
\mathcal{G}(S^{\#})
-labels for
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathcal{G}(S)
. Since
\gamma_{S}
is a computable map from
S^{\#}
to
\mathcal{G}(S^{\#})
, we can effectively search for
y_{i,j}\in D_{\omega}
which are
S^{\#}
-labels for
b_{i,j}\in\mathcal{G}(S)
satisfying
a_{i}=\gamma_{S}(b_{i,1})-\gamma_{S}(b_{i,2})
. We then have that
a_{1}=a_{2}
if and only if
\gamma_{S}(b_{1,1})+\gamma_{S}(b_{2,2})=\gamma_{S}(b_{1,2})+\gamma_{S}(b_{2,1})
, which, since
S
is cancellative, is equivalent to
b_{1,1}+b_{2,2}=b_{1,2}+b_{2,1}
; since
S^{\#}
is computable, this can indeed be decided.
∎

Recall that an
ordered abelian group
is a pair
(G,G^{+})
, where
G
is a group and
G^{+}
is a subset of
G
, called the
positive cone of
G
, that satisfy the following three properties.

(1)

G^{+}+G^{+}\subseteq G^{+}
.

(2)

G^{+}\cap(-G^{+})=\{0\}
.

(3)

G=G^{+}-G^{+}
.

If
\mathbf{A}
is stably finite, then
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})
is an ordered abelian group with positive cone
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})^{+}=\{\gamma_{\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A})}([p]_{\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}})\ :\ p\in\mathcal{P}_{<\omega}(\mathbf{A})\}
.

Proposition 2.19
.

If
\mathbf{A}
is stably finite and
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is c.e.,
then
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})^{+}
is a c.e. subset of
K_{0}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
.

Proof.

For simplicity, set
S=\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A})
, so
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})^{+}=\mbox{ran}(\gamma_{S})
.
Write
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})=(\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A}),\nu_{0})
and

K_{0}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})=(K_{0}(\mathbf{A}),\nu_{1})
. Then by the computable universality of
K_{0}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
,
there is a computable

F:D_{\omega}\rightarrow F_{\omega}
so that
\gamma_{S}\nu_{0}=\nu_{1}F
.
A direct computation shows that

\nu_{1}^{-1}[\mbox{ran}(\gamma_{S})]=\{w\in F_{\omega}\ :\ (w,F(w^{\prime}))\in\ker(\nu_{1})\text{ for some }w^{\prime}\in F_{\omega}\}.

Since
\ker(\nu_{1})
is c.e. and
F
is computable, we see that
\nu_{1}^{-1}[\mbox{ran}(\gamma_{S})]
is
c.e., whence
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})^{+}
is a c.e. subset of
K_{0}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
.
∎

Corollary 2.20
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{A}
is stably finite and
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})
is linearly ordered, that is,
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})=K_{0}(\mathbf{A})^{+}\cup(-K_{0}(\mathbf{A})^{+})
. Then for any c.e. presentation
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
of
\mathbf{A}
,
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})^{+}
is a computable subset of
K_{0}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
.

Proof.

This follows from Proposition
2.19
together with the easy observation that, for any presentation
G^{\#}
of of an abelian group, the map
x\mapsto-x
is a computable map from
G^{\#}
to itself.
∎

2.4.
Non-unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras

In this section, we deviate from our convention and do not assume that
\mathbf{A}
is necessarily unital. Recall that the
unitization

\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}
of
\mathbf{A}
is the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra with underlying set
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}=\{(a,\alpha)\ :\ a\in A,\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\}
, with addition and scalar multiplication given coordinatewise, and multiplication given by

(a,\alpha)\cdot(b,\beta)=(ab+\beta a+\alpha b,\alpha\beta)
. Equipped with these operations,
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}
is a unital *-algebra
with unit
(0,1)
. One has *-algebra homomorphisms
\iota:\mathbf{A}\to\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}
and
\pi:\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}\to\mathbb{C}
given by

\iota(a)=(a,0)
and
\pi(a,\alpha)=\alpha
. Note that
\iota(\mathbf{A})
is an ideal of
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}
, which we usually identify with

\mathbf{A}
itself.
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}
is in fact a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra under the norm

\|x\|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}}=\max(|\pi(x)|,\sup\{\|ax\|_{\mathbf{A}}\ :\ a\in A,\|a\|\leq 1\})
.
Note that when
\mathbf{A}
is unital, then
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}
is isomorphic to
\mathbf{A}\oplus\mathbb{C}
.

If
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is a presentation of
\mathbf{A}
, then one obtains a presentation
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\#}
on
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}
by declaring that the
\langle m,k\rangle
-th special point of
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\#}
is the pair
(a,\alpha)
, where
a
is the
m
-special point of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
and
\alpha
is the
k
-th special point of the standard presentation of
\mathbb{C}
. It follows immediately that if
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is a left-c.e. presentation, then so is
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\#}
.

An alternate description of the unitization in terms of universal
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras will prove useful. Namely,
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}
is the universal
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra generated by
\mathbf{A}
and an additional indeterminate
x
subject to all of the relations satisfied by
\mathbf{A}
together with the relations stating that
x
is a contraction that serves as a unit for the algebra. In particular, if
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is a presentation of
\mathbf{A}
, then in the previous sentence, one may replace the statement “all of the relations satisfied by
\mathbf{A}
” by the statement “all rational relations
\|p(\vec{a})\|\leq r
” satisfied by the special points of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
. In this way, one can equip
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}
with the corresponding universal presentation
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\#,u}
(see
[
10
, Subsection 2.2]
), which is easily seen to be computably isomorphic to the previously defined presentation
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\#}
.

Proposition 2.21
.

If
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is a c.e. (resp. computable) presentation of
\mathbf{A}
, then the induced presentation on
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\#}
is also c.e. (resp. computable). Moreover, an index for
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\#}
can be computed from an index for
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
.

Proof.

The statement for c.e. presentations follows immediately from
[
7
, Theorem 3.3]
. The statement for computable presentations follows from the statement for right-c.e. presentations and the corresponding statement for left-c.e. presentations mentioned above.
∎

Given a *-algebra homomorphism
\phi:\mathbf{A}_{0}\to\mathbf{A}_{1}
, we obtain a *-algebra homomorphism
\widetilde{\phi}:\widetilde{\mathbf{A}_{0}}\to\widetilde{\mathbf{A}_{1}}
given by
\widetilde{\phi}(a,\alpha)=(\phi(a),\alpha)
. The following fact is clear.

Proposition 2.22
.

If
\phi:\mathbf{A}_{0}^{\#}\to\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\#}
is a computable *-algebra homomorphism, then so is
\widetilde{\phi}:\widetilde{\mathbf{A}_{0}}^{\#}\to\widetilde{\mathbf{A}_{1}}^{\#}
.

Corollary 2.23
.

The unitization functor from the category of c.e. (resp. computable) presentations of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras to the category of c.e. (resp. computable presentations) of unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras is computable.

For
\mathbf{A}
not necessarily unital,
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})
is defined to be the kernel of the map
K_{0}(\pi):K_{0}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}})\to K_{0}(\mathbb{C})
. In order to equip

with a c.e. presentation, we need a couple of preliminary lemmas. It is quite possible that these lemmas are folklore in the algorithmic group theory community, but since we could not find exact statements of them in the literature, we provide proofs for the convenience of the reader.

Lemma 2.24
.

If
\phi:G_{0}^{\#}\to G_{1}^{\#}
is a computable map of group presentations and

is c.e., then
\ker(\phi)
is a c.e. subset of

. Moreover, an index for
\ker(\phi)

\phi

Proof.

Suppose that
G_{i}^{\#}=(G_{i},\nu_{i})
,

, and fix
w^{\prime}\in F_{\omega}
such that
\nu_{1}(w)=0
. Take also a computable function

witnessing that
\phi
is computable. Then
\nu_{0}^{-1}[\ker(\phi)]=\{w\in F_{\omega}\ :\ (f(w),w^{\prime})\in\ker(\nu_{1})\}
. Since

is computable and
\ker(\nu_{1})
is c.e., we see that
\nu_{0}^{-1}[\ker(\phi)]
is a c.e. subset of
F_{\omega}
, whence
\ker(\phi)
is a c.e. subset of
G_{0}^{\#}
.
∎

Lemma 2.25
.

If
G^{\#}
is a c.e. group presentation and

, then there is a c.e. presentation
H^{\#}
of

H^{\#}\to G^{\#}
. Moreover, an index for
H^{\#}
can be computed from an index for
G^{\#}
and an index for
H
as a c.e. set.

Proof.

Let
U
be the set of all
G^{\#}
-labels of elements of
S
. Then
U
is c.e. and
U
is a subgroup of
F_{\omega}
.
Let
(w_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be an effective enumeration of
U
. Set
\nu_{0}(x_{n})=w_{n}
, extend
\nu_{0}
to an epimorphism of

F_{\omega}
onto
U
, and set
U^{\#}=(U,\nu_{0})
. Then
U^{\#}
is a c.e. presentation of
U
. Define
H^{\#}
by declaring

w
to be an
H^{\#}
-label of
a\in H
if
w
is a
U^{\#}
-label of a
G^{\#}
-label of
a
. It follows that

H^{\#}
has all the required properties.
∎

Given a c.e. presentation
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
of
\mathbf{A}
, define
K_{0}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
to be the c.e. presentation of
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})=\ker(\pi)
induced by the c.e. presentation of
K_{0}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\#})
, where
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\#}

\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}
induced by the presentation
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
.

Lemma 2.26
.

Suppose that
\phi:\mathbf{A}_{0}^{\#}\to\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\#}
is a computable *-homomorphism. Then the induced map
K_{0}(\phi):K_{0}(\mathbf{A}_{0})\to K_{0}(\mathbf{A}_{1})
is a computable map from
K_{0}(\mathbf{A}_{0}^{\#})
to
K_{0}(\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\#})
. Moreover, an index for
K_{0}(\phi)
can be computed from indices for
\phi
,
A_{0}^{\#}
, and
A_{1}^{\#}
.

Proof.

Note that
K_{0}(\phi)
is the restriction of
K_{0}(\widetilde{\phi}):K_{0}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}_{0}})\to K_{0}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}_{1}})
to
K_{0}(\mathbf{A}_{0})
.
The inclusion map is a computable map from
K_{0}(\mathbf{A}_{i}^{\#})
to
K_{0}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}_{i}}^{\#})
. The result then follows from the fact that

K_{0}(\phi)
is a computable map of
K_{0}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}_{0}}^{\#})
to
K_{0}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}_{1}}^{\#})
.
Since all of the assertions in this proof
have already been show to be uniform, so is the conclusion of the lemma.
∎

Putting all of the pieces from this subsection together, we obtain the following.

Corollary 2.27
.

K_{0}
is a computable functor from the category of c.e. presentations of (not necessarily unital)
\mathrm{C}^{*}
algebras to the category of c.e. presentations of groups.

3.
Computing
K_{1}

Having established that c.e. presentations of
\mathbf{A}
give rise to c.e. presentations of
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})
, it is natural to turn our attention next to
K_{1}(\mathbf{A})
. Fortunately, our work on
K_{0}
will allow us to accomplish this quite quickly. In this section, we also abandon our convention that all
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras are assumed to be unital.

Recall that for any
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
, the
suspension of
\mathbf{A}
is the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
C_{0}(0,1)\otimes\mathbf{A}
; we denote
this algebra by
S\mathbf{A}
. It follows that

S\mathbf{A}\cong\{f\in C([0,1],\mathbf{A})\ :\ f(0)=f(1)=0\}
where the isomorphism is given by the mapping

f\otimes a\mapsto(t\mapsto f(t)a)
. Note that
S\mathbf{A}
is not unital.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is a c.e. (resp. computable) presentation, then there is an induced presentation
S\mathbf{A}^{\#}
of
S\mathbf{A}
that is also c.e. (resp. computable). Moreover, an index for
S\mathbf{A}^{\#}
can be computed from an index for
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
.

Proof.

We begin by noting that
C_{0}(0,1)
has a computable presentation
C_{0}(0,1)^{\#}
, namely the ones whose special points are the rational polygonal curves
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
so that

\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)=0
. Equip
S\mathbf{A}^{\#}
with the presentation whose
\langle m,k\rangle
th special point is
f_{m}\otimes a_{k}
, where
f_{m}
and
a_{k}
are the
m
th rational point of
C_{0}(0,1)^{\#}
and
k
th rational point of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
respectively. By
[
10
, Lemmas 2.4 and 2.5]
, we have that
S\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is c.e. when
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is c.e. If
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is actually computable, then
S\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is also left-c.e. (and hence computable) using the formula

\left\|\sum_{i}f_{i}\otimes a_{i}\right\|=\sup_{t\in(0,1)}\left\|\sum_{i}f_{i}(t)a_{i}\right\|

and the fact that
C_{0}(0,1)^{\#}
is
evaluative
in the sense of
[
11
]
, that is, that the map
(f,t)\mapsto(f,t):(C_{0}(0,1)\times(0,1))^{\#}\to\mathbb{C}^{\#}
is computable, where
(0,1)
and
\mathbb{C}
are given their standard presentations.
∎

The suspension operation is actually an endofunctor on the category of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras: given a *-homomorphism
\phi:\mathbf{A}_{0}\to\mathbf{A}_{1}
, one gets another *-homomorphism
S\phi:S\mathbf{A}_{0}\to S\mathbf{A}_{1}
given by
S(\phi)(\sum_{i}f_{i}\otimes a_{i})=\sum_{i}f_{i}\otimes\phi(a_{i})
. The following lemma is immediate:

Lemma 3.2
.

If
\phi:\mathbf{A}_{0}^{\#}\to\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\#}
is computable, then so is
S\phi:S\mathbf{A}_{0}^{\#}\to\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\#}
. Moreover, an index for
S\phi
can be computed from indices for
\mathbf{A}_{0}^{\#},\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\#}
, and
\phi
.

We let
S
denote the endofunctor on both the category of computable presentations of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
algebras and its full subcategory of c.e. presentations, which associates to each c.e. (resp. computable) presentation
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
the c.e. (resp. computable) presentation
S\mathbf{A}^{\#}
as in Lemma
3.1
. We have just shown the following.

Corollary 3.3
.

S
is a computable functor.

The reason for introducing the suspension functor is that one may define
K_{1}(\mathbf{A})
as
K_{1}(\mathbf{A})=K_{0}(S\mathbf{A})
. Consequently, we may define the functor
K_{1}=K_{0}\circ S
. As a composition of two computable functors is computable, we obtain the following.

Theorem 3.4
.

The functor
K_{1}
is a computable functor from the category of c.e. presentations of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras to the category of c.e. presentations of groups.

Our definition of
K_{1}(\mathbf{A})
is perhaps not the usual one, but rather is equivalent to the usual definition only via a nontrivial theorem
(see
[
12
, Theorem 10.1.3]
). The usual definition of
K_{1}(\mathbf{A})
is as the set of unitaries in matrix
amplifications of
\mathbf{A}
modulo homotopy equivalence, with the group operation being induced by the operation
\oplus
introduced above. It
would be interesting to see if perhaps one can show that this definition of
K_{1}
leads to a computable functor which is computably naturally
equivalent to the functor just defined here.

4.
UHF algebras

We now restrict our our attention to UHF algebras. Recall that a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
is called
uniformly hyperfinite
(or
UHF
) if there is a sequence of matrix algebras
(M_{n_{k}}(\mathbb{C}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and a sequence of unital embeddings
\iota_{k}:M_{n_{k}}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n_{k+1}}(\mathbb{C})
so that
\mathbf{A}
is isomorphic to
\displaystyle{\lim_{\rightarrow}(M_{n_{k}}(\mathbb{C}),\iota_{k})}
. We recall that there is a unital embedding from
M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
to
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
if and only if
m
divides
n
. If
m
divides
n
, then all such embeddings are unitarily conjugate to the canonical embedding
\mathcal{E}_{m,n}
that takes a matrix
A
in
M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
to the block-diagonal matrix whose diagonal consists of
n/m
copies of
A
. More formally,

\mathcal{E}_{m,n}(A)=\sum_{\ell<n/m}\sum_{r,s\in\{1,\ldots,m\}}a_{r,s}e^{(n)}_{r+\ell m,s+\ell m}.

As such, we frequently suppress mention of the embeddings and refer only to the sequence of matrix algebras involved in constructing a UHF algebra. It is well-known that each UHF algebra
\mathbf{A}
has a unique tracial state, which we denote by
\tau_{\mathbf{A}}
, given as the direct limit of the unique traces on the various matrix algebras.

It is well-known that UHF algebras are simple. Consequently, by the result of Fox mentioned in Section
1
, any c.e. presentation of a UHF algebra is automatically computable. In a sequel in preparation, we will show that this statement holds for the wider class of AF algebras (simple or otherwise).

4.1.
K
-theory and supernatural numbers

In
[
9
]
, Glimm classified UHF algebras up to isomorphism by an invariant known as a
supernatural number
. This invariant is intimately related to the
K_{0}
group of the algebra. We recall the definitions and basic properties. Throughout,
\mathbb{P}
denotes the set of prime numbers.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\displaystyle{\mathbf{A}\cong\lim_{\rightarrow}M_{n_{k}}(\mathbb{C})}
be a UHF algebra. The
supernatural number
of
\mathbf{A}
is the function
\epsilon_{\mathbf{A}}:\mathbb{P}\to\{0,1,\ldots,\infty\}
defined by

\epsilon_{\mathbf{A}}(p)=\sup\{m\in\mathbb{N}:p^{m}\text{ divides }n_{k}\text{ for some $k$}\}.

The supernatural number
\epsilon_{\mathbf{A}}
is often expressed as a formal product
\prod_{p\in\mathbb{P}}p^{n_{p}}
where
n_{p}=\epsilon_{\mathbf{A}}(p)
. However, for our purposes it is usually more convenient to think of it as a function, so that we may apply computability-theoretic terminology to it directly.

More generally, any function
\epsilon:\mathbb{P}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
is referred to as a supernatural number. It is easy to see that every supernatural number is the supernatural number of a UHF algebra (which is then necessarily unique up to isomorphism).

Although the definition of
\epsilon_{\mathbf{A}}
appears to depend on the choice of sequence of matrix algebras whose limit is
\mathbf{A}
, this is not in fact the case.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a UHF algebra and fix a prime
p\in\mathbb{P}
. If
m\in\mathbb{N}
is such that there is a unital embedding of
M_{p^{m}}(\mathbb{C})
into
\mathbf{A}
, then
m\leq\epsilon_{A}(p)
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{A}
is UHF, we can express it as
\mathbf{A}\cong\lim_{\rightarrow}M_{n_{k}}(\mathbb{C})
. Suppose that
\phi:M_{p^{m}}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbf{A}
is a unital embedding and set
\sigma=\tau_{\mathbf{A}}\circ\phi
. Then
\sigma
is a tracial state on
M_{p^{m}}(\mathbb{C})
and hence is the usual normalized trace.

Let
q\in M_{p^{m}}(\mathbb{C})
be a rank
1
projection. Thus,
\sigma(q)=p^{-m}
. Since
\phi(q)
is a projection in
\mathbf{A}
, there exists
k
and a projection
q^{\prime}\in M_{n_{k}}
such that
\phi(q)
is unitarily conjugate (in
\mathbf{A}
) to
q^{\prime}
. In particular,
\tau_{\mathbf{A}}(q^{\prime})=\tau_{\mathbf{A}}(\phi(q))=\sigma(q)=p^{-m}
. Thus the rank of
q^{\prime}
is
\tau_{\mathbf{A}}(q^{\prime})n_{k}=p^{-m}n_{k}
. Hence,
p^{m}
divides
n_{k}
and
m\leq\epsilon_{\mathbf{A}}(p)
.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

For every UHF algebra
\mathbf{A}
and every
p\in\mathbb{P}
, we have

\epsilon_{\mathbf{A}}(p)=\sup\{m\in\mathbb{N}:\text{ there is a unital embedding of $M_{p^{m}}(\mathbb{C})$ into $\mathbf{A}$}\}.

The supernatural number can be viewed as an encoding of the ordered group
(K_{0}(\mathbf{A}),K_{0}(\mathbf{A})^{+},[\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}]_{0})
, as we now recall. For proofs of these claims, see
[
12
, Lemma 7.4.4.ii and Theorem 7.4.5.i]
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a UHF algebra with supernatural number
\epsilon_{\mathbf{A}}
. Let
\mathbb{Q}(\epsilon_{\mathbf{A}})
be the subgroup of
(\mathbb{Q},+)
generated by numbers of the form
p^{-m}
, where
p
is prime and
m\leq\epsilon_{\mathbf{A}}(p)
. Then
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})\cong\mathbb{Q}(\epsilon_{\mathbf{A}})
via an isomorphism that sends the
K_{0}
-class of
\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}
to the rational number
1
and sends
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})^{+}
to
\mathbb{Q}(\epsilon_{\mathbf{A}})^{+}
.

On the other hand, every UHF algebra
\mathbf{A}
has
K_{1}(\mathbf{A})=0
(see
[
12
, Example 8.1.8 and Proposition 8.2.7]
). Thus supernatural numbers form a complete description of
K
-theory for UHF algebras.

4.2.
Computable UHF certificates

Many different sequences of matrix algebras (and connecting maps) give rise to the same UHF algebra. For computability purposes, it will be useful for us to have access not just to a UHF algebra, but also to data on how to construct the UHF algebra as a direct limit of matrix algebras.
To this end, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 4.4
.

A
UHF certificate
for a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
is a sequence
(n_{j},\psi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
that satisfies the following
conditions:

(1)

(n_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of positive integers.

(2)

\psi_{j}:M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbf{A}
is a unital embedding.

(3)

\mbox{ran}(\psi_{j})\subseteq\mbox{ran}(\psi_{j+1})
.

(4)

\mathbf{A}=\overline{\bigcup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\mbox{ran}(\psi_{j})}
.

The UHF certificate is a
computable UHF certificate
of the presentation
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
if the sequence
(n_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is computable and the maps
\psi_{j}
are computable maps of
M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C})
(with its standard presentation) into
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
, uniformly in
j
.

It is clear that a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
is UHF if and only if it has a UHF certificate. Our next goal is to show that if
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is a computable presentation of a UHF algebra
\mathbf{A}
, then
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
has a computable UHF certificate. Note that the identity matrix of
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is a rational point of the standard presentation of
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, and hence if
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
has a computable UHF certificate, then
\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}
must be a computable point of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
. In fact, our construction of a computable UHF certificate for
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
will use
\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}
as its starting point, so we first prove that
\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}
is a computable point of every computable presentation of a UHF algebra. This result actually holds in greater generality.

Proposition 4.5
.

If
\mathbf{A}
is stably finite and
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is computable, then

\mathbf{1}_{A}
is a computable point of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
is a presentation of
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A})
, there is a

\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
-label
w_{0}\in D_{\omega}
for
[\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}]_{\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}}
.
Since
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
computably supports
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
, from
w_{0}
it is possible to compute
n
and an
M_{n}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
-index
of a projection
p\in P_{n}(\mathbf{A})
so that
p\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}
.

Next note that there is a computable projection
p^{\prime}
of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
so that
p^{\prime}\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}p
. Indeed, by Theorem
2.1
, there is a pair
(B_{0},B_{1})
of rational open balls
of
M_{n}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
so that
\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}\oplus\mathbf{0}_{n-1}\in B_{0}
,
p\in B_{1}
, and
q_{0}\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}q_{1}

whenever
(q_{0},q_{1})\in(B_{0}\times B_{1})\cap P_{n}(\mathbf{A})^{2}
. Take a rational open ball

B_{2}
of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
so that
\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}\in B_{2}
and

q\oplus\mathbf{0}_{n-1}\in B_{0}
whenever
q\in B_{2}
. Since
P_{1}(\mathbf{A})
is a c.e. closed set of

\mathbf{A}^{\#}
, and since
B_{2}\cap P_{1}(\mathbf{A})\neq\emptyset
, Lemma
1.10
implies that
B_{2}\cap P_{1}(\mathbf{A})
contains
a computable point
p^{\prime}
of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
. Thus,
p^{\prime}\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}p^{\prime}\oplus\mathbf{0}_{n-1}\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}p
.

Since
\mathbf{A}
is stably finite and
p^{\prime}\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}p\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}1_{\mathbf{A}}
, we have that
p^{\prime}=1_{\mathbf{A}}
,as desired.
∎

Note that the proof of the previous proposition is
not
uniform as it requires one to fix a label for the Murray-von Neumann equivalence class of the identity. In fact, the uniform version of the statement is not true, as was shown by the third author in
[
11
, Theorem 5.3]
.

We next show how to extend a matrix algebra in
\mathbf{A}
to a larger one. We begin by recalling a result of Glimm (see
[
5
, Lemma III.3.2]
), which we immediately strengthen in the lemma that follows it.

Fact 4.6
.

For all
\epsilon>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a positive real number

\delta=\delta(\epsilon,n)
such that, whenever
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
are
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-subalgebras of a
unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathbf{D}
with
\dim(A)\leq n
and such that
\mathbf{A}
has a system of matrix
units
(e_{r,s})_{r,s}
satisfying
d(e_{r,s},\mathbf{B})<\delta
, then there is a unitary

U\in C^{*}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
with
\|U-1_{\mathbf{D}}\|<\epsilon
for which
U\mathbf{A}U^{*}\subseteq\mathbf{B}
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{A}
is a UHF algebra, that
\mathbb{M}\subseteq\mathbf{A}
is a finite dimensional
subalgebra, and that
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\in\mathbf{A}
. Then for every
\epsilon>0
, there is a

\mathrm{C}^{*}
-subalgebra
\mathbf{B}
of
\mathbf{A}
containing
\mathbb{M}
such that
\mathbf{B}\cong M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
for some
n
and for which
d(v_{i},\mathbf{B})<\epsilon
for all
i=1,\ldots,k
.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon^{\prime}
be small enough so that for
(\epsilon^{\prime 2}+2\epsilon^{\prime})(\|v_{i}\|)<\epsilon/2
for all
i=1,\ldots,k
.
Set
\delta=\delta(\epsilon^{\prime},\dim(M))
as in Fact
4.6
, and let
\delta^{\prime}=\min\{\delta,\epsilon^{\prime},\epsilon/2\}
. Let
(e_{r,s})_{r,s}
be a system of matrix units for
\mathbb{M}
. Since
\mathbf{A}
is UHF, there is an algebra
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbf{A}
such that
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}\cong M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
for some
n
,

d(e_{r,s},\mathbb{B}^{\prime})<\delta^{\prime}
for all
r,s
, and
d(v_{i},B^{\prime})<\delta^{\prime}
for all
i=1,\ldots,k
. By the choice of
\delta^{\prime}
, there is a unitary
U
in
C^{*}(\mathbb{M},\mathbb{B}^{\prime})
such that
\|U-1_{\mathbf{A}}\|<\epsilon^{\prime}
and
U\mathbb{M}U^{*}\subseteq\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
. Set
\mathbf{B}=U^{*}\mathbb{B}^{\prime}U
. Then

\mathbb{M}\subseteq\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{B}\cong\mathbf{B}^{\prime}\cong M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

For any
v\in\mathbf{A}
, we have

\displaystyle\|v-U^{*}vU\|

\displaystyle=\|(U-1_{\mathbf{A}})^{*}v(U-1_{\mathbf{A}})+(U-1_{\mathbf{A}})^{*}v+v(U-1_{\mathbf{A}})\|

\displaystyle\leq(\|U-1_{\mathbf{A}}\|^{2}+2\|U-1_{\mathbf{A}}\|)\|v\|

\displaystyle\leq(\epsilon^{\prime 2}+2\epsilon^{\prime})\|v\|.

In particular, for each
i=1,\ldots,k
, the choice of
\epsilon^{\prime}
gives
\|v_{i}-U^{*}v_{i}U\|<\epsilon/2
. Now for each
i=1,\ldots,k
, and each
b\in\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\|v_{i}-U^{*}bU\|

\displaystyle=\|v_{i}-U^{*}(b-v_{i})U-U^{*}v_{i}U\|

\displaystyle\leq\|v_{i}-U^{*}v_{i}U\|+\|b-v_{i}\|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon/2+d(v_{i},\mathbb{B}^{\prime})

\displaystyle<\epsilon.

Thus
d(v_{i},\mathbb{B})<\epsilon
for all
i=1,\ldots,k
as required.
∎

Our next step is a computable version of the previous lemma.

Lemma 4.8
.

Suppose
\mathbf{A}
is UHF and
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is computable. Assume
\phi
is a unital embedding of

M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
into
\mathbf{A}
and is a computable map from
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
into
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
.
Then for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and every finite set
F
of rational vectors of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
, there exists

n^{\prime}>n
with
n\mid n^{\prime}
and a unital embedding
\psi
of
M_{n^{\prime}}(\mathbb{C})
into
\mathbf{A}
that satisfy the following conditions.

(1)

\psi
is a computable map from
M_{n^{\prime}}(\mathbb{C})
to
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
.

(2)

For all
\rho\in F
,
d(\rho,\mbox{ran}(\psi))<2^{-k}
.

(3)

For all
r,s\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,

\|\phi(e_{r,s}^{(n)})-\sum_{j<n^{\prime}/n}\psi(e^{(n^{\prime})}_{r+jn,s+jn})\|<2^{-k}.

Proof.

Set
\mathbf{A}_{0}=\mbox{ran}(\phi)
and fix a positive integer
m
. Let
C_{m}
denote
the set of all
(mn)\times(mn)
systems of matrix units in
M_{mn}(\mathbf{A})
. By Corollary
1.15
,
C_{m}
is a c.e. closed subset of
M_{mn}(\mathbf{A})^{\#}
uniformly in

m
. Let
U^{1}_{m}
denote the set of all

A\in M_{mn}(\mathbf{A})
so that

d(\rho,\langle a_{r,s}\rangle_{r,s})<2^{-k}
for each
\rho\in F
, where
\langle a_{r,s}\rangle_{r,s}
denotes the subalgebra of
\mathbf{A}
generated by the
a_{r,s}
’s.
Let
U^{2}_{m}
denote the set of all
A\in M_{mn}(\mathbf{A})
so that,
for all
r,s\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, one has

\|\phi(e^{(n)}_{r,s})-\sum_{j<m}a_{r+jn,s+jn}\|<2^{-k}.

Note that both
U^{1}_{m}
and
U^{2}_{m}
are c.e. open sets of
M_{mn}(\mathbf{A})^{\#}
uniformly in
m
, whence so is

U^{1}_{m}\cap U^{2}_{m}
.

We claim that there is a positive integer
m
so that
C_{m}\cap U^{1}_{m}\cap U^{2}_{m}\neq\emptyset
. To see this, note that, by Lemma
4.7
, there is an
n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
and a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra

\mathbf{B}\cong M_{n^{\prime}}(\mathbb{C})
so that
\mathbf{A}_{0}\subseteq\mathbf{B}\subseteq\mathbf{A}
and so that

d(\rho,\mathbf{B})<2^{-k}
for all
\rho\in F
. Let
\psi_{0}
be a
*
-isomorphism
of
M_{n^{\prime}}(\mathbb{C})
onto
\mathbf{B}
. Thus,
\psi_{0}^{-1}\circ\phi:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n^{\prime}}(\mathbb{C})
is a unital embedding. Consequently, there is a unitary
U\in M_{n^{\prime}}(\mathbb{C})
so that

\psi_{0}^{-1}(\phi(A))=U\mathcal{E}_{n,n^{\prime}}(A)U^{*}
for all
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Now define
\psi_{1}:M_{n^{\prime}}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbf{A}
by
\psi_{1}(A)=\psi_{0}(UAU^{*})
. Set
a_{r,s}=\psi_{1}(e^{(n^{\prime})}_{r,s})
, and let
A
be the matrix with entries
a_{r,s}
. It suffices to show
A\in C_{m}\cap U^{1}_{m}\cap U^{2}_{m}
. Since
\psi_{1}
is an embedding, we have
A\in C_{m}
.
Since we have
\langle a_{r,s}\rangle_{r,s}=\mathbf{B}
, it follows that
A\in U^{1}_{m}
. Finally, since

\phi(e^{(n)}_{r,s})=\psi_{1}(\mathcal{E}_{n,n^{\prime}}(e^{(n)}_{r,s}))=\sum_{j<m}a_{r+jn,s+jn}
, we have
A\in U^{2}_{m}
, proving the claim.

Take
m
as in the previous paragraph and set
n^{\prime}=mn
. By Lemma
1.10
, there is a computable point
A
belonging to

C_{m}\cap U^{1}_{m}\cap U^{2}_{m}
. Let
\psi
be the unital embedding of

M_{n^{\prime}}(\mathbb{C})
into
\mathbf{A}
for which
\psi(e^{(n^{\prime})}_{r,s})=a_{r,s}
.
It follows that
\psi
satisfies (
1
) through
(
3
).
∎

Once again, the proof of Lemma
4.8
shows that the conclusion holds uniformly in codes for
A^{\#}
and
\phi
.

We now arrive at the key theorem of this subsection.

Theorem 4.9
.

Suppose
\mathbf{A}
is UHF and
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is computable.
Then
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
has a computable UHF certificate

(n_{j},\psi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
so that
\psi_{j+1}^{-1}\psi_{j}=\mathcal{E}_{n_{j},n_{j+1}}
.

Proof.

We define a computable sequence
(n_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
of positive integers with
n_{j}\mid n_{j+1}
and a monotonic nondecreasing sequence
(\mathbf{A}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
for which

\mathbf{A}=\overline{\bigcup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbf{A}_{n_{j}}}
. At the same time, for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
,
we will also define a
*
-isomorphism
\psi_{j}
of
M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C})
onto
\mathbf{A}_{j}
that is a computable map from
M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C})
to
\mathbf{A}^{\#}

uniformly in
j
.

These subalgebras and maps will be built by a sequence of approximations.
More precisely, for each
j,t\in\mathbb{N}
with
j\leq t
, we build an approximation

\mathbf{A}_{j,t}
of
\mathbf{A}_{j}
. This is acccomplished by defining an
n_{j}\times n_{j}
system

G^{(j,t)}=(g^{(j,t)}_{r,s})_{r,s}
of matrix units and setting

\mathbf{A}_{j,t}=\langle g^{(j,t)}_{r,s}\rangle_{r,s}
. Along the way, we will ensure the following three conditions.

•

1_{\mathbf{A}}=\sum_{r}g^{(j,t)}_{r,r}
.

•

G^{(j,t)}
is a computable point of
M_{n_{j}}(\mathbf{A})
uniformly in
j,t
.

•

(G^{(j,t)})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
is a strongly Cauchy sequence of
M_{n_{j}}(\mathbf{A})^{\#}
.
That is,
\|G^{(j,t)}-G^{(j,t+1)}\|<2^{-t}
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
.

By the last item, we may set
G^{(j)}=\lim_{t}G^{(j,t)}
. Writing
G^{(j)}=(g^{(j)}_{r,s})
, we then define
\mathbf{A}_{j}=\langle g^{(j)}_{r,s}\rangle_{r,s}
.
When
j\leq t
, we define
\psi_{j,t}:M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbf{A}_{j,t}
by
\psi_{j,t}(e^{(n_{j})}_{r,s})=g^{(j,t)}_{r,s}

and then define
\psi_{j}:M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbf{A}_{j}
by
\psi_{j}=\lim_{t}\psi_{j,t}
.

An auxiliary sequence to be constructed during the construction is an array
(\alpha^{(n,t)}_{r,s})_{r,s}
of
scalars from
\mathbb{Q}(i)
whenever
n<t
.

Let
(\rho_{n})
denote an effective enumeration of the rational points of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
.
There are two key invariants to be maintained at each stage of this construction.

(INV-1)

\|\rho_{n}-\sum_{r,s}\alpha^{(n,t^{\prime})}_{r,s}g^{(t^{\prime},t)}_{r,s}\|<2^{-t^{\prime}}

when
n<t^{\prime}\leq t
.

(INV-2)

g^{(j,t)}_{r,s}=\sum_{\ell<n_{j+1}/n_{j}}g^{(j+1,t)}_{r+\ell n_{j},s+\ell n_{j}}
when
j+1\leq t
.

At each stage of the construction, we assume the invariants are satisfied at all prior stages.

Stage 0:
We define
n_{0}
to be
1
and
g^{(0,0)}_{1,1}=\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}
.

Stage
\mathbf{t}+1
:
We begin by setting
n=n_{t}
,
\phi=\psi_{t,t}
,
F=\{\rho_{0},\ldots,\rho_{t}\}
, and

k_{t+1}=\log_{2}\left(2^{t+1}n_{t}\prod_{n<t^{\prime}\leq t}\frac{n_{t}n_{t^{\prime}}(\|\rho_{n}\|+2^{-t^{\prime}}+1)}{2^{-t^{\prime}}-\|\rho_{n}-\sum_{r,s}\alpha^{(n,t^{\prime})}_{r,s}g^{(t^{\prime},t)}_{r,s}\|}\right).

By Lemma
4.8
, there exists
n_{t+1}>n_{t}
with
n_{t}\mid n_{t+1}
and a unital embedding
\psi_{t+1,t+1}
of

M_{n_{t+1}}(\mathbb{C})
into
\mathbf{A}
that satisfy the following three conditions.

(1)

\psi_{t+1,t+1}
is a computable map of
M_{n_{t+1}}(\mathbb{C})
to
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
.

(2)

For all
n\leq t
,
d(\rho_{n},\mbox{ran}(\psi_{t+1,t+1}))<2^{-k_{t+1}}
.

(3)

For all
r,s\in\{1,\ldots,n_{t}\}
,

\|g_{r,s}^{(t,t)}-\sum_{j<n_{t+1}/n_{t}}\psi_{t+1,t+1}(e^{(n_{t+1})}_{r+jn_{t},s+jn_{t}})\|<2^{-k_{t+1}}.

We set
g_{r,s}^{(t+1,t+1)}=\psi_{t+1,t+1}(e^{(n_{t+1})}_{r,s})
.
For each
n\leq t
, compute an array
(\alpha^{(n,t+1)}_{r,s})_{r,s}
of
scalars from
\mathbb{Q}(i)
so that

\|\rho_{n}-\sum_{r,s}\alpha^{(n,t+1)}_{r,s}g_{r,s}^{(t+1,t+1)}\|<2^{-(t+1)}.

For
j\leq t
, by “backwards recursion”, we set

g^{(j,t+1)}_{r,s}=\sum_{\ell<n_{j+1}/n_{j}}g^{(j+1,t+1)}_{r+\ell n_{j},s+\ell n_{j}}.

We first claim that for every
t\in\mathbb{N}
,

\|g^{(j,t)}_{r,s}-g^{(j,t+1)}_{r,s}\|\leq\frac{n_{t}}{n_{j}}2^{-k_{t+1}}

whenever
j\leq t
and
r,s\in\{1,\ldots n_{j}\}
.
For, let
t\in\mathbb{N}
. We proceed by ‘backward induction’ on
j
. The base case

j=t
holds from (3) above and the definition of
g_{r,s}^{(t,t+1)}
. Now suppose
j<t
. By construction,

\displaystyle g_{r,s}^{(j,t)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell<n_{j+1}/n_{j}}g^{(j+1,t)}_{r+\ell n_{j},s+\ell n_{j}}\text{ and }

\displaystyle g_{r,s}^{(j,t+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell<n_{j+1}/n_{j}}g^{(j+1,t+1)}_{r+\ell n_{j},s+\ell n_{j}}

By the inductive hypothesis,

\|g^{(j,t)}_{r,s}-g^{(j,t+1)}_{r,s}\|\leq\frac{n_{j+1}}{n_{j}}\frac{n_{t}}{n_{j+1}}2^{-k_{t+1}}=\frac{n_{t}}{n_{j}}2^{-k_{t+1}}

as required.

We now show that the invariants are satisfied at every stage
t
.
We first note that the invariants are vacuously true when
t=0
.
Suppose the invariants hold at stage
t
. We now show they hold at stage
t+1
.
(
INV-2
) holds by construction.
Suppose
n<t^{\prime}\leq t+1
. We need to show

\|\rho_{n}-\sum_{r,s}\alpha^{(n,t^{\prime})}_{r,s}g^{(t^{\prime},t+1)}_{r,s}\|<2^{-t^{\prime}}.

If
t^{\prime}=t+1
, then this is by construction. Suppose
t^{\prime}\leq t
.
On the one hand,

\|\rho_{n}-\sum_{r,s}\alpha^{(n,t^{\prime})}_{r,s}g^{(t^{\prime},t+1)}_{r,s}\|\leq\|\rho_{n}-\sum_{r,s}\alpha^{(n,t^{\prime})}_{r,s}g^{(t^{\prime},t)}_{r,s}\|+\sum_{r,s}|\alpha_{r,s}|\|g^{(t^{\prime},t+1)}_{r,s}-g^{(t^{\prime},t)}_{r,s}\|.

By what has just been shown,

\|g^{(t^{\prime},t)}_{r,s}-g^{(t^{\prime},t+1)}_{r,s}\|\leq\frac{n_{t}}{n_{t^{\prime}}}2^{-k_{t+1}}.

We also have that

|\alpha^{(n,t^{\prime})}_{r,s}|\leq\|\sum_{r,s}\alpha^{(n,t^{\prime})}_{r,s}e^{(n_{t^{\prime}})}_{r,s}\|=\|\sum_{r,s}\alpha^{(n,t^{\prime})}_{r,s}g^{(t^{\prime},t+1)}_{r,s}\|\leq\|\rho_{n}\|+2^{-t^{\prime}}.

Thus,

\sum_{r,s}|\alpha^{(n,t^{\prime})}_{r,s}|\|g^{(t^{\prime},t+1)}_{r,s}-g^{(t^{\prime},t)}_{r,s}\|\leq(n_{t^{\prime}})^{2}(\|\rho_{n}\|+2^{-t^{\prime}})\frac{n_{t}}{n_{t^{\prime}}}2^{-k_{t+1}}=n_{t}n_{t^{\prime}}(\|\rho_{n}\|+2^{-t^{\prime}})2^{-k_{t+1}}.

By the definition of
k_{t+1}
,

n_{t}n_{t^{\prime}}(\|\rho_{n}\|+2^{-t^{\prime}})2^{-k_{t+1}}<2^{-t^{\prime}}-\|\rho_{n}-\sum_{r,s}\alpha^{(n,t^{\prime})}_{r,s}g^{(t^{\prime},t)}_{r,s}\|.

Hence, the invariants are maintained at all stages of the construction.

The only thing that remains to be verified is that
\mathbf{A}=\overline{\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbf{A}_{n}}
. To see this, note that

d(\rho_{n},\mathbf{A}_{t})\leq 2^{-t}
whenever
n<t
.
Thus, every rational vector of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
belongs to
\overline{\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbf{A}_{n}}
, which clearly suffices.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

If
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is a computable presentation of the UHF algebra
\mathbf{A}
and
(n_{j},\psi_{j})
is a computable UHF certificate of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
, then it is clear that one may compute, for every rational
\epsilon>0
and rational point
y
of
A^{\#}
, an integer
j
and a rational point
x
of
M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C})
so that
\|\psi_{j}(x)-y\|<\epsilon
. In other words, the union of the ranges of the
\psi_{j}

is a presentation of the UHF algebra
\mathbf{A}
for which there is a computable UHF certificate of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
satisfying this extra computable density property, then
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is readily seen to be computable.

4.3.
Computability of
K
-theory

We saw in Corollary
2.16
that if
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is a computable presentation of a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
, then
K_{0}(\mathbf{A}^{\#})
is a c.e. presentation of
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})
. Moreover, Proposition
2.19
implies that if
\mathbf{A}
is UHF, then the positive cone

is a c.e. subset of

(since UHF algebras are stably finite). We now show that for UHF algebras, the situation is better, in that

is actually a computable presentation of

and the positive cone

is a computable subset of

.

Theorem 4.11
.

Suppose
\mathbf{A}
is a UHF algebra. If
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
is computable, then
the tracial state
\tau_{\mathbf{A}}
of
\mathbf{A}
is a computable map from
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
to
\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.9
, there is a computable UHF certificate
(n_{j},\psi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
for
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
.
For each
j\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathbf{A}_{j}=\mbox{ran}(\psi_{j})
and let
\tau_{j}
be the tracial state of
M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C})
. Note that the maps
\tau_{j}
are computable, uniformly in
j
.

Given a rational open ball
B(\rho;r)
of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
, we compute a rational open disk

D(\zeta;3r)\subseteq\mathbb{C}
as follows. First, we compute
j
and a rational point

so that

, whence it follows that

|\tau_{j}(A)-\tau(\rho)|=|\tau\psi_{j}(A)-\tau(\rho)|<r.

Next, since
\tau_{j}
is computable, we can compute

so that
|\zeta-\tau_{j}(A)|<r
.
As a result, for each

Thus,
\tau
is a computable map of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
into
\mathbb{C}
.
∎

Definition 4.12
.

Suppose
\tau
is a trace on a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
. We say that
(\mathbf{A},\tau)
is
factor-like
provided that for any two projections
p,q\in P_{<\omega}(\mathbf{A})
,
p\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}q
if and only if
\tau(p)=\tau(q)
.

The nomenclature stems from the fact that the conclusion of the definition holds for tracial factors. Note that
\tau(p)=\tau(q)
always holds when
p\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}q
, whence the import of the definition is the statement that projections of the same trace are Murray-von Neumann equivalent.

The following is probably well-known, but since we could not find it explicitly stated in the literature, we include a proof.

Lemma 4.13
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{A}
is UHF and
\tau_{\mathbf{A}}
is its unique tracial state. Then
(\mathbf{A},\tau_{\mathbf{A}})
is factor-like.

Proof.

Since
M_{n}(\mathbf{A})
is UHF as well, it suffices to consider the case that
p,q\in P(\mathbf{A})
are such that
\tau_{\mathbf{A}}(p)=\tau_{\mathbf{A}}(q)
. Take a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-subalgebra
\mathbf{B}\subseteq\mathbf{A}
such that
\mathbf{B}\cong M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
for some
n
and which contains projections
p^{\prime}
and
q^{\prime}
such that
\|p-p^{\prime}\|,\|q-q^{\prime}\|<1/2
. Thus,
p\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}p^{\prime}
,
q\sim_{\operatorname{mvn}}q^{\prime}
, and consequently

\tau_{\mathbf{A}}(p^{\prime})=\tau_{\mathbf{A}}(q^{\prime})
. However,
\tau_{\mathbf{A}}
agrees with the usual trace on
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
(once
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is identified with
\mathbf{B}
), and it is well-known that matrix algebras equipped with their usual trace are factor-like (in fact, they are factors!).
∎

The following is immediate from the definitions.

.

is a presentation such that

to

.

If
\mathbf{A}^{\#}

.

We now turn to the connection with supernatural numbers. The following definition is
an adaptation of a standard concept from computable analysis.

Definition 4.16
.

A supernatural number
\epsilon
is
lower semicomputable
if
it is the pointwise limit of a uniformly computable
sequence
(h_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
of functions from
\mathbb{P}
into
\mathbb{N}

so that
h_{j}(p)\leq h_{j+1}(p)
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in\mathbb{P}
.

We are now prepared to demonstrate the relations between computability properties of
\mathbf{A}
,
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})
, and
\epsilon_{\mathbf{A}}
.

Theorem 4.17
.

Suppose
\mathbf{A}
is a UHF algebra. The following are equivalent.

(1)

\mathbf{A}
has a computable presentation.

(2)

K_{0}(\mathbf{A})
has a computable presentation.

(3)

K_{0}(\mathbf{A})
has a c.e. presentation.

(4)

The supernatural number of
\mathbf{A}
is lower semicomputable.

Proof.

Theorem
4.15
already states that
(

) implies (

).
It is definitional that (

) implies (

).
For (

)
\Rightarrow
(

), set

, and let

be a c.e. presentation of

.
Since
\mathbf{A}
is UHF, we may assume

in that, from the presentation

, we may form a c.e. presentation of

\mathbb{Q}(\epsilon_{\mathbf{A}})
that has the same index.
Fix a

-label

of

and set

By Corollary
4.3
, to show that

is c.e.
Let

. Fix a prime

By the definition of

Finally, suppose that

to

Let

be the inductive limit of

.
∎

It follows from the previous theorem that
\mathbf{0^{\prime\prime}}
computes the supernatural number of every

\mathrm{C}^{*}
algebra.
We now give an example of a supernatural number that achieves this upper bound.
Let

be the increasing enumeration of

.
For each

, let

and set

.

4.4.
Computable categoricity

Recall that a structure is called
computably categorical
if every two computable presentations of the structure are computably isomorphic. We conclude this paper by showing that all UHF algebras enjoy this property.

Theorem 4.18
.

Every UHF algebra is computably categorical.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathbf{A}
is UHF and suppose that
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
and
\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}
are computable presentations of
\mathbf{A}
. By Theorem
4.9
,
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
has a computable UHF certificate

(m_{j},\phi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying
\phi_{j+1}^{-1}\phi_{j}=\mathcal{E}_{m_{j},m_{j+1}}
.
Similarly,
\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}
has a computable UHF certificate

(n_{j},\psi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying
\psi_{j+1}^{-1}\psi_{j}=\mathcal{E}_{n_{j},n_{j+1}}
.
Set
\mathbf{A}_{j}=\mbox{ran}(\phi_{j})
and
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}_{j}=\mbox{ran}(\psi_{j})
.

We define sequences
(k_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\ell_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
of positive integers
by simultaneous recursion as follows. Set
k_{0}=0
and let
\ell_{j}
be the smallest number greater than
\max\{\ell_{j^{\prime}}\ :\ j^{\prime}<j\}

for which
m_{k_{j}}|n_{\ell_{j}}
.
Similarly, let
k_{j+1}
be the smallest number greater than
\max\{\ell_{k^{\prime}}\ :\ j^{\prime}\leq j\}
for which

.

Now define
\gamma_{j}:\mathbf{A}_{k_{j}}\to\mathbf{A}^{\prime}_{l_{j}}
and
\delta_{j}:\mathbf{A}^{\prime}_{l_{j}}\to\mathbf{A}_{k_{j+1}}
by setting

\displaystyle\gamma_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi_{\ell_{j}}\mathcal{E}_{m_{k_{j}},n_{\ell_{j}}}\phi_{k_{j}}^{-1}\mbox{, and}

\displaystyle\delta_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{k_{j+1}}\mathcal{E}_{n_{\ell_{j}},m_{k_{j+1}}}\psi^{-1}_{\ell_{j}}.

Note that
\delta_{j}\gamma_{j}:\mathbf{A}_{k_{j}}\to\mathbf{A}_{k_{j+1}}
is the inclusion map
\iota_{k_{j},k_{j+1}}
and likewise that
\gamma_{j+1}\delta_{j}:\mathbf{A}^{\prime}_{\ell_{j}}\to\mathbf{A}^{\prime}_{\ell_{j+1}}
is the inclusion map
\iota^{\prime}_{\ell_{j},\ell_{j+1}}
. In particular,
\gamma_{j+1}
extends
\gamma_{j}
and
\delta_{j+1}
extends
\delta_{j}
.

Let
\gamma=\bigcup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\gamma_{j}
, and let
\delta=\bigcup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\delta_{j}
.
Since
\gamma_{j}
and
\delta_{j}
are embeddings, they are
1
-Lipschitz.
It follows that
\gamma
has a unique continuous extension
\overline{\gamma}
to

\mathbf{A}
and that
\delta
has a unique continuous extension
\overline{\delta}
to

\mathbf{A}
. Since each
\gamma_{j}
and
\delta_{j}
is an embedding, it follows that the extensions
\overline{\gamma}
and
\overline{\delta}
are
*
-homomorphisms. As we have already seen that
\delta_{j}\gamma_{j}=\iota_{k_{j},k_{j+1}}
and
\gamma_{j+1}\delta_{j}=\iota^{\prime}_{\ell_{j},\ell_{j+1}}
, it follows that
\overline{\gamma}=\overline{\delta}^{-1}
. In particular,
\overline{\gamma}
and
\overline{\delta}
are each automorphisms of
\mathbf{A}
.

It remains to show that
\overline{\gamma}
is a computable map from
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
to
\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}
and
\overline{\delta}
is a computable map from
\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}
to
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
. As the two proofs are similar, we will only establish the former claim.

Let
\rho
be a rational point of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
and fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Search for
j\in\mathbb{N}
and a rational point
\rho^{\prime}
of

M_{m_{k_{j}}}(\mathbb{C})
so that
\|\phi_{k_{j}}(\rho^{\prime})-\rho\|<2^{-(k+1)}
. Since
\mathbf{A}=\overline{\bigcup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbf{A}_{j}}

and
\lim_{j}k_{j}=\infty
,
this search must terminate.
Since
\overline{\gamma}
is
1
-Lipschitz, we have

\|\overline{\gamma}(\rho)-\overline{\gamma}(\phi_{k_{j}}(\rho^{\prime}))\|<2^{-(k+1)}.

Moreover, we have

\overline{\gamma}(\phi_{k_{j}}(\rho^{\prime}))=\gamma_{j}(\phi_{k_{j}}(\rho^{\prime}))=\psi_{\ell_{j}}\mathcal{E}_{k_{j},\ell_{j}}(\rho^{\prime}).

The point
\psi_{\ell_{j}}\mathcal{E}_{k_{j},\ell_{j}}(\rho^{\prime})
is a computable point of
\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}
uniformly in
j
and
\rho^{\prime}
.
Thus, we may compute a rational point
\rho^{\prime\prime}
of
\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}
so that

\|\rho^{\prime\prime}-\overline{\gamma}(\phi_{k_{j}}(\rho^{\prime}))\|<2^{-(k+1)}
. Thus,

\|\overline{\gamma}(\rho)-\rho^{\prime\prime}\|<2^{-k}
as desired.
∎

Appendix A
The functor
K_{0}
in general

It is naturally desirable to extend
K_{0}
to a “computable” functor from the category of presentations of unital
C^{*}
-algebras (computable or otherwise)
to the category of semigroup presentations. However, for any reasonable definition of what it means for a functor of this sort to be computable,
it seems unlikely that such a thing exists. Indeed, if it did, it would map computable presentations of unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras to computable presentations of abelian groups and we believe it is not the case that computable presentability of
\mathbf{A}
implies the computable presentability of
K_{0}(\mathbf{A})
(although we have no counterexample at
present). Consequently, we propose a weaker condition than computability of the functor for which our methods do naturally extend.

We consider three categories of presentations:

(1)

The category of presentations of semigroups. In this category, the objects are the
semigroup presentations and the morphisms are the computable homomorphisms between semigroup presentations.

(2)

The category of presentations of groups. In this category, the objects are the group presentations
and the morphisms are the computable maps between group presentations.

(3)

The category of presentations of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
algebras. In this category, the objects are the
presentations of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
algebras and the morphisms are the computable
*
-homomorphisms between presentations.

Our definition of computability for functors between these categories is based on
names and name transformations which we define now.

Definition A.1
.

A
name
is a pair
(e,Y)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})
.

Definition A.2
.

A
name transformation
is a map
F:\mathbb{N}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})\rightarrow\mathbb{N}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})

so that, whenever
F(e,X_{0})=(e^{\prime}_{0},Y_{0})
and
F(e,X_{1})=(e_{1}^{\prime},Y_{1})
, then
e_{0}^{\prime}=e_{1}^{\prime}
.

In the case of semigroup and group presentations, we consider three kinds of names.

Definition A.3
.

Suppose
J^{\#}
is a semigroup presentation or a group presentation and let
(e,X)
be a name.

(1)

(e,X)
is a
purely positive name
for
J^{\#}
if
the kernel of
J^{\#}
is
W_{e}^{X}
.

(2)

(e,X)
is a
purely negative name
for
J^{\#}
if
the kernel of
J^{\#}
is the complement of
W_{e}^{X}
.

(3)

(e,X)
is an
exact name
for
J^{\#}
if
the kernel of
J^{\#}
is computed by
\phi_{e}^{X}
.

We analogously define three kinds of names for
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras.

Definition A.4
.

Let
(e,X)
be a name and
let
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
be a presentation of a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
algebra.

(1)

(e,X)
is a
purely positive name
for
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
if,
for every rational point
\rho
of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
,
W_{\phi_{e}(\rho)}^{X}

is the left Dedekind cut of
\|\rho\|
.

(2)

(e,X)
is a
purely negative name
for
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
if,
for every rational point
\rho
of
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
,
W_{\phi_{e}(\rho)}^{X}

is the right Dedekind cut of
\|\rho\|
.

(3)

(e,X)
is an
exact name
for
\mathbf{A}^{\#}
if

\phi_{e}^{X}
computes the norm of
\mathbf{A}
on the rational points of

\mathbf{A}^{\#}
.

Rather than a single concept of a computable functor, we now propose three concepts based on the kinds of
names used for the objects in the domain and range.

Definition A.5
.

Suppose that, for each
j\in\{0,1\}
,
\mathcal{C}_{j}
is one of the three categories listed above.
Suppose
F
is a functor from
\mathcal{C}_{0}
to
\mathcal{C}_{1}
.

(1)

F
is
lower semicomputable
if there is a computable name transformation
\Phi
so that

\Phi(\eta)
is a purely positive name of
F(J^{\#})
whenever
\eta
is an exact name of
J^{\#}
.

(2)

F
is
upper semicomputable
if there is a computable name transformation
\Phi
so that

\Phi(\eta)
is a purely negative name of
F(J^{\#})
whenever
\eta
is an exact name of
J^{\#}
.

(3)

F
is
strongly lower semicomputable
if there is a computable name transformation
\Phi
so that

\Phi(\eta)
is a purely positive name of
F(J^{\#})
whenever
\eta
is a purely positive name of
J^{\#}
.

In each of the above cases, we also require that, from an index of a morphism
\phi
from

J_{0}^{\#}
to
J_{1}^{\#}
, it is possible to compute an index of
F(\phi)
.

In computable analysis, a sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of reals is lower semicomputable if, from

n\in\mathbb{N}
, it is possible to compute an enumeration of the left Dedekind cut of
a_{n}
.
Upper semicomputability of a sequence of reals is defined similarly. These concepts motivate
the choice of terminology in the above definition.

-algebra presentations

(2)

(3)

\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra presentations to the
