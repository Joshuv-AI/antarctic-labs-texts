TeO2-modified SiO2-B2O3-Al2O3 glasses with enhanced dielectric constant and dissipation factor for advanced semiconductor packaging applications
Authors: Linganna Kadathala, Yong-Tak Ryu, Young-Ouk Park, Won-Taek Han, Bok Hyeon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recently, glass has emerged as a core substrate material for next-generation semiconductor packaging applications compared with organic substrates because of its low dielectric property, low coefficients of thermal expansion, and excellent scalability. In this study, we present new TeO2-modified alkaline-earth alumino-borosilicate glasses with enhanced dielectric and thermomechanical properties for advanced packaging applications. The effect of TeO2 content on structural, thermomechanical, and dielectric properties of the glasses was systematically investigated. FTIR and XPS studies revealed enhanced polymerization of the glass network with increasing TeO2 addition up to 2.0 mol%, as evidenced by a reduction in the Q1/Q4 ratio. By substituting TeO2 for SiO2 up to 2.0 mol%, the dielectric constant (Dk) decreased from 4.582 to 4.458 at 40 GHz, but increased to 4.537 for further increases in TeO2 (3.0 mol%). The dissipation factor (Df) also decreased from 3.93 to 3.73(⋅10−3) at 40 GHz upon TeO2 substitution. Additionally, the incorporation of TeO2 reduced the thermal expansion coefficient of the alumino-borosilicate glasses from 3.47 to 2.99(⋅10− 6/K). These findings demonstrate that the TeO2-modified alumino-borosilicate glasses are promising alternative core substrate materials for advanced semiconductor packaging, with the potential to mitigate the limitations of organic substrates at high frequencies in AI and high-performance computing applications.

Introduction

The demand for high-performance computing (HPC) and memory capacity has grown rapidly in recent years, driven by the proliferation of data-intensive applications such as data centers, artificial intelligence (AI), autonomous driving, and graphics processing1–6. Simultaneously, the adoption of multiple frequency bands in the millimeter-wave (mm-wave) spectrum has intensified concerns related to signal attenuation, particularly for high-speed 5G/6G telecommunications7–12. For the past six decades, the system-on-chip (SoC) paradigm driven by Moore’s law has enabled the scaling and integration of all the system components into a single chip1,3,5. However, as Moore’s law slowing down, the SoC strategy faces several challenges, including an increase in the cost per square millimeter of transistors and a minimal increase in transistor performance and computer performance5. This has led to a shift towards system-on-package (SoP) architectures, which address the limitations by integrating the heterogeneous components into a high-level assembly, thereby enabling the evolution of high density and high signal performance electronic systems for the aforementioned emerging applications1,3. The benefits of the SoP integration depend on the development of high performance interposer materials and their interfaces1,3. These materials must be fully compatible with existing packaging processes to enable reliable and cost-effective manufacturing1–6. Furthermore, highly integrated SoP architectures require diverse sets of materials capable of satisfying stringent electrical, thermomechanical, and mechanical properties1. Various types of interposer materials such as Al2O3-based ceramics, silicon, epoxy-based mold solutions, polymers, and printed circuit board (PCB) laminates have been used in semiconductor chips for integrated circuits6. However, they exhibit considerable disadvantages as substrate materials for 5G and 6G electronic packaging, particularly at high frequencies.

Recently, glass has emerged as a promising core substrate material for advanced semiconductor packaging applications, such as AI, computing, and autonomous driving9–16. This is owing to its exceptional dielectric, thermomechanical, and mechanical properties, including a low dielectric constant (Dk) and dissipation factor (Df), low thermal expansion coefficient (CTE) to match package components, and high elastic modulus, thereby allowing better scalability and the assembly of larger chiplets compared to current silicon and organic substrates9–16. A distinctive property of glass is its low Dk, which minimizes signal propagation delays and crosstalk between adjacent interconnects. It also reduces the capacitive coupling between adjacent interconnects, allowing faster signal transmission and improved overall performance6,9,12. In addition, the low Df of glass compensates for the intrinsically higher attenuation at high frequencies, ensuring an acceptable propagation loss through devices6,9,12. Glass substrates can also tolerate higher temperatures, providing chip architects with greater flexibility in defining the design rules for power delivery and signal routing14,15. This facilitates the seamless integration of interconnects as well as the embedding of inductors and capacitors into the glass at higher temperatures. Furthermore, their ultralow flatness enhances the depth of focusing during high-precision laser through-glass-vias processing, resulting in a higher density in the substrate15. Therefore, glass substrates with excellent electrical performance, thermal and mechanical stability, and flatness are being actively developed as next-generation materials to replace polymer substrates in advanced semiconductor packaging1–16. As operating frequencies extend into the mm-wave range, the advantages of glass have become increasingly important8–12.

To use glass as a core substrate material for advanced semiconductor packaging applications, particularly in high-frequency and high-speed electronic packaging, the prerequisite parameters are a low Dk (≤ 5 at 10 GHz), Df (≤ 0.004 at 10 GHz), glass transition temperature (Tg: ≤ 600 °C), and CTE (≤ 4.0⋅10−6/K at 25–300 °C). To date, fused silica has demonstrated the lowest Dk (3.8 at 10 GHz) and Df (0.06⋅10−3 at 10 GHz) values6,16. However, this glass composition requires specialized processing techniques involving high melting temperatures6,16. Alternative glass products, such as Schott Borofloat 33 (Dk = 4.4 and Df = 7.5⋅10−3 at 10 GHz), Schott AF32 eco (Dk = 5.1 and Df = 6.1⋅10−3 at 10 GHz), Schott D263 T eco (Dk = 6.4 and Df = 12.7⋅10−3 at 10 GHz), AGY L-glass (Dk = 4.8 and Df = 3.0⋅10−3 at 10 GHz), and E-glass (Dk = 6.9 and Df = 7.0⋅10−3 at 10 GHz), have been developed for high-frequency substrate applications6,16. However, they are unsuitable for high-frequency electronic packaging, due to their relatively high Dk and Df values. Among the various oxide glass matrices, the SiO2-B2O3-Al2O3glass system has been extensively studied for a wide range of applications, including PCB laminates17–21, liquid crystal display panels22,23, nuclear-waste immobilization24,25, and solid-oxide fuel cells26,27, because of its promising characteristics, such as a low Dk and Df, low CTE, high Young’s modulus, and so on. Nevertheless, this glass system exhibits relatively high Dk, Df, Tg, and CTE values, limiting its suitability as a core substrate material for advanced packaging applications17,18. Thus, optimizing both the dielectric (Dk, Df) and thermomechanical (Tg, Ts, and CTE) properties of this glass system has attracted significant attention for advanced semiconductor packaging applications.

Among the network forming oxides, tellurium dioxide (TeO2) has received considerable attention for glass core substrate applications due to its low melting and glass transition temperatures, as well as its unique structural characteristics28–43. The basic structural units of TeO2, are the TeO3 trigonal pyramid, TeO4 trigonal bipyramid, and distorted TeO3+δ pyramid, which are responsible for its characteristic optical and thermal properties37,38. Over the past few decades, TeO2-based glasses have been extensively investigated for their spectroscopic and non-linear optical characteristics, enabling a wide range of applications, such as light amplification, optical switching, bio-sensing, and radiation shielding28–43. Recently, the impact of TeO2 on the structural, physical, and optical properties of borosilicate glasses has been demonstrated for radiation shielding and biological applications44,45. In Ref44., the maximum TeO2 content incorporated into borosilicate glasses was limited to 0.2 mol%, whereas Ref45. reported a TeO2 content level up to 6.0 wt% (∼3.0 mol%). To the best of our knowledge, no prior studies have reported the preparation and characterization of TeO2-modified SiO2-B2O3-Al2O3-MgO-CaO glasses for use as glass core substrate materials in advanced semiconductor packaging applications at high frequencies (5–50 GHz).

In this study, we attempt to improve the dielectric properties (Dk, Df) at 5–50 GHz, as well as the thermomechanical properties (Tg, Ts, CTE), of an alumino-borosilicate glass system containing alkaline earth oxides by incorporating TeO2 at various concentrations ranging from 0 to 3.0 mol% for use as glass core substrate materials in advanced semiconductor packaging applications. The impact of TeO2 on the structural, thermomechanical, and dielectric properties of alumino-borosilicate glasses containing alkaline-earth oxides, including the glass-transition and softening temperatures, and CTE was systematically investigated.

Materials and methods

Glass fabrication

TeO2-modified alumino-borosilicate glasses with chemical compositions of (59.5−x)SiO2−22B2O3−9Al2O3−6MgO-3CaO-0.1Na2O-0.4SnO2-xTeO2, where x = 0, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, and 3.00 mol% (hereafter referred to as GDMC-Te), were prepared using a melt-quenching technique. Batch compositions were designed with the replacement of SiO2 by TeO2. The glasses with the chemical compositions are listed in Table 1. Reagent grade high-purity SiO2 (99.9%, Kojundo, Japan), Al2O3 (99.9%, Kojundo, Japan), B2O3 (99.9%, Kojundo, Japan), TeO2 (99.9%, Kojundo, Japan), CaCO3 (99.99%, Kojundo, Japan), MgCO3 (99.9%, Kojundo, Japan), Na2CO3 (99.0%, Kojundo, Japan), and SnO2 (99.9%, Kojundo, Japan), were used as precursors for the preparation of the glasses. The chemical constituents, especially Na2CO3 and SnO2, were added to the batch composition as fluxing agents to reduce the viscosity and remove bubbles within the glass melt during the melting process. Each batch composition was prepared with a weight of 200 g. First, raw materials from each batch were ground in a ball mill for 12 h to obtain a homogenous powder. Before melting each batch composition at the maximum temperature, the following processes were performed: (1) dehydration of the batch constituent oxides at 400 °C to remove water, and (2) decarbonization of MgCO3, CaCO3, and Na2CO3 at 900 °C to release CO2 gas. After calcination, the batch composition was melted at 1620 °C for 3 h in a platinum crucible in a box furnace. The liquid melt was then poured onto a preheated brass mold, and subsequently the glass samples were annealed at 600 °C for 3 h to avoid thermal shock and improve thermal stability. A re-melting process was performed for each glass to improve its homogeneity. Finally, the annealed glass samples were cut and polished to different sizes (5⋅5⋅10 mm3 and 80·90·0.4 mm3) to measure their thermomechanical and dielectric properties.

TeO2 was incorporated into this alumino-borosilicate glass system in the range of 0 to 3.0 mol% to improve the dielectric properties while maintaining sufficient thermomechanical stability. It should be noted that crystallization was observed when the TeO2 content exceeded 3.0 mol%. Although volatilization of TeO2 can occur during melting at 1620 °C, the retained TeO2 concentration in the glasses still increases progressively with increasing nominal TeO2 content, as evidenced by the increasing Te 3 d XPS signal (will be discussed later). Therefore, the observed variations in properties (thermal, thermomechanical, and dielectric) are attributed to the structural changes rather than volatilization effects.

Characterization

The infrared (IR) absorption spectra were measured using an attenuated total reflection-Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (ATR-FTIR; Vertex 70v, Bruker, Germany) equipped with a DLaTGS (Deuterated Lanthanum Alpha Alanine-doped TriGlycine Sulfate) detector. Measurements were carried out on a bulk sample (size: 10⋅10⋅1 mm3) under vacuum conditions in ATR mode using a diamond crystal. Spectra were collected over the wavenumber range of 4000–600 cm⁻¹ with a resolution of 2 cm⁻¹, and 64 scans were accumulated for each measurement to improve the signal-to-noise ratio. This approach differs from conventional IR studies that employ KBr chemical as a carrier matrix, where a small amount of glass powder (about mg) is doped. The use of bulk samples in ATR mode enhances the reliability and accuracy of the FTIR data.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements of the glasses were performed using a spectrometer (NEXSA G2, Thermo Scientific, USA) equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (hν = 1486.6 eV) and concentric hemispherical analyzer. No surface preparation was performed prior to analysis. XPS survey spectra of the glasses were measured in the binding energy range of 0 to 1400 eV at a resolution of 1.0 eV. XPS scans of the constituent elements in the glass were acquired at a resolution of 0.1 eV. The acquisition time for the glass sample was 1.0 min 40.5 s and 10 scans were obtained. The spectral deconvolution was carried-out using Avantage XPS software with Gaussian–Lorentzian peak fitting after smart background subtraction. The peak positions of the constituent elements were determined with reference to the carbon contamination peak (C1s) located at a binding energy of 284.8 eV.

The physical properties (e.g. density) of the glasses were measured using a densimeter (MD-300 S, Alfa Mirage, Japan) based on the Archimedes’ principle. The density of each glass sample was measured at least three times to avoid measurement errors, and the standard deviation of the values was within ±0.003 g/cm3.

The thermal characterization of the glasses was performed by a simultaneous thermal analyzer (STA 409, Netzsch, Germany) in the range of 25–1400 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. The thermomechanical characterization of the glasses was performed using thermomechanical analyzer (TMA; TMA 402 C, Netzsch, Germany) in the range of −10 to 900 °C at a scan rate of 5 °C/min. Glass samples with dimensions of 5⋅5⋅10 mm3 were used for this measurement. The dielectric properties (Dk, Df) of the glasses were measured using a split-post dielectric resonator (QWED, Poland) in the frequency range of 1 to 10 GHz, Fabry–Pérot open resonator (QWED, Poland) in the frequency range of 20 to 50 GHz, and vector network analyzer (MS4647B, Anritsu, Japan). Polished glass samples with dimensions of 80·90·0.4 mm3 were used for the measurements.

Results and discussion

Structural analysis – FTIR spectra

Figure 1A shows the FTIR spectra of the TeO2-modified alumino-borosilicate glasses for different TeO2 concentrations varying from 0 to 3.0 mol%, overlaid on the spectrum of the GDMC-Te0.0 glass. The spectra were normalized to the spectral maximum, as all glasses had a maximum intensity in the same band (∼1055 cm− 1). The spectra broadly show consistent band patterns in this glass series, with only shifts in peak positions and intensities upon TeO2 substitution for SiO2. The spectra can be distinguished into three distinct frequency regions: the low-frequency region (600–850 cm− 1), mid-frequency region (850–1250 cm− 1), and high-frequency region (1250–1600 cm− 1). Bands were assigned based on the FTIR and Raman bands previously reported for alumino-borosilicate and borosilicate glasses containing alkali and alkaline earth oxides46–51. As shown in Fig. 1A, the low-frequency region (600–850 cm− 1) corresponds to the mixed vibrational modes of Si−O−Si, [BO3], [AlO4], and [TeO4/TeO3] units46–51. Bands in the mid-frequency region (850–1250 cm− 1) can be attributed to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibration modes of the silicate network (850–1250 cm−1). The prominent [SiO4] tetrahedral band in the 850–1250 cm−1 region is represented by the Qn species, where n = 1, 2, 3, and 4 corresponds to the number of bridging oxygens (BOs) coordinated to the silicon46–51. The bands in the high-frequency region (1250–1600 cm−1) correspond to the stretching vibrations of the B–O bonds in the chain-type metaborate groups and boroxol rings46–51.

Figure 1B−F show the deconvoluted FTIR absorption spectra of the glasses using a Gaussian function to quantify the variations in the relative proportions of the different structural units. Table 2 presents the peak positions and relative areas of the deconvoluted peaks in the GDMC-Te glasses with various TeO2 concentrations. Spectral deconvolution analysis is discussed based on the low-frequency (600–850 cm− 1), mid-frequency (850–1250 cm− 1), and high-frequency (1250–1600 cm− 1) regions. In the low-frequency region (600–850 cm− 1), the spectra of the GDMC-Te glasses were fitted with two peaks located at approximately 679 cm− 1 and 789 cm− 1. The band located around 679 cm−1 corresponds to the rocking vibrational modes of Si−O−Si, the bending vibrational modes of B−O bonds in [BO3], and the asymmetric or coupled symmetric vibrations of Te−O bonds in [TeO4/TeO3], whereas the band around 789 cm−1 attributes to the asymmetric vibrations of Al−O bonds in [AlO4] and the stretching vibrational modes of [BO3] in boroxol rings46–51. The peak at approximately 679 cm− 1 increased from 8.24% to 9.24%, whereas the peak at approximately 789 cm− 1 decreased from 3.56% to 2.50% with increasing TeO2 content from 0 to 3.0 mol% in the GDMC-Te glasses. The band in the 600–700 cm–1 region corresponds to the vibrations of Te−O bonds38,44. The observed increase in intensity within this region with increasing TeO2 concentration indicates the successful incorporation of TeO2 into the studied glass compositions. The mid-frequency region (850–1250 cm− 1) was deconvoluted into three peaks representing the asymmetric stretching vibrational modes of Q1, Q3, and Q4 units within the [SiO4] tetrahedra at ∼919, ∼1052, and ∼1146 cm− 1, respectively46–51. It should be noted the Q1 (∼919 cm− 1), Q3 (∼1052 cm− 1), and Q4 (∼1146 cm− 1) units within the [SiO4] tetrahedra, do not show any frequency shift upon TeO2 substitution in the glass compositions, however, the intensity of the Qn units changes. The observed variations in peak intensity, without corresponding frequency shifts, may indicate variations in the relative populations of Qⁿ species, although definitive assignment requires complementary techniques. The Q1 peak (∼919 cm− 1) decreased from 10.72% to 8.60%, the Q3 peak (∼1052 cm− 1) changed from 41.06% to 42.38%, and the Q4 peak (∼1146 cm− 1) increased from 12.73% to 14.60% with increasing TeO2 up to 2.0 mol% and then decreased to 13.47% with a further increase in TeO2 (3.0 mol%). Figure 1G shows the changes in the relative areas of the Q1, Q3, and Q4 units as a function of TeO2. The Q1/Q4 ratio, derived from the deconvoluted peaks, decreased from 0.84 to 0.59 with increasing TeO2 from 0 to 2.0 mol% and then increased to 0.64 for a further increase in TeO2, as shown in Fig. 1H. Based on the spectral deconvolution of the 850–1250 cm−1 region, substituting the TeO2 up to 2.0 mol% for SiO2 causes the polymerization of the silicate network, as evidenced by an increase in the peak area corresponding to Q4 (∼1146 cm− 1) and a decrease in the area of the Q1 (∼ 919 cm− 1) peak in the GDMC-Te glasses. The increase in network connectivity with increasing TeO2 content may be explained by the substitution of TeO2 for SiO2, that is, the substitution of one [TeO4] trigonal bipyramid for one [SiO4] tetrahedron. This implies that the replacement of SiO2 with TeO2 introduces more Si-O-Te bond units into the alumino-borosilicate glass network44,45. Thus, the incorporation of TeO2 corresponds to an increasing degree of polymerization in the studied glasses.

Bands in the high-frequency region (1250–1500 cm− 1) were fitted with two peaks at approximately 1315 and 1364 cm− 1, corresponding to the symmetric stretching vibrations of metaborate [BO3] units46–51. The relative areas of the peaks at 1315 cm− 1 significantly increased from 1.47% to 15.38%, and at 1364 cm− 1 predominantly decreased from 22.22% to 8.42%. In addition, the peaks initially observed at 1315 and 1364 cm− 1 shifted to 1354 cm− 1 and 1421 cm− 1 as the TeO2 content in the glasses increased from 0 to 3.0 mol%, respectively. Spectral deconvolution clearly reveals a high wavenumber shift in high-frequency region peaks (~ 1315 cm− 1 and ~ 1364 cm− 1) with the substitution of TeO2 for SiO2. The shift in the high-frequency region is attributed to an increase in the bond strength around the B-O− harmonic oscillator, which forms chain-type metaborate [BO3] structural units because of the increased force constant resulting may be from the mixing of SiO2 and TeO244. This suggests that the observed transformations reflect changes in the local boron environment upon TeO2 substitution.

It should be noted that the formation of NBOs on [SiO4] tetrahedra is investigated in the studied TeO2-doped alumino-borosilicate glasses, despite the fact that the network modifier content (MgO + CaO: 9.0 mol%) is equal to the Al2O3 content (9.0 mol%). In general, negatively charged [AlO4] tetrahedra are preferentially charge-compensated by modifier cations, such as Na+, Ca2+, and Mg2+, which would typically limit NBO formation on the silicate network. However, the observed generation of NBOs on Si (Q1, Q2, Q3 units) suggests a deviation from this expected behavior. This can be attributed to the relatively higher CFS of Mg2+ compared to Na+ and Ca2+, which favors the formation of NBOs within the silicate network49,51. These findings are consistent with previous studies in the literature46,48,49,51–53. For instance, Demirok et al.46 investigated MgO-CaO-Al2O3-SiO2 glasses with and without boron using FTIR-ATR spectroscopy and statistical modeling. Their study clearly identified Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4 units within [SiO4] tetrahedra through spectral deconvolution in aluminoborosilicate glasses containing alkaline earth oxides. Notably, the modifier content (MgO + CaO: 20−25 wt%) is comparable to the Al2O3 content (15−25 wt%), which aligns with the compositional design shown in the present work. The deconvolution methodology and interpretation used in their study are consistent with those applied here. Logrado et al.48 also investigated the effect of ionic potential of the network modifier cations on structure and properties of aluminoborosilicate glasses using solid-state NMR spectroscopy. Their results showed that cations with high ionic potential, such as Mg2+, decrease the fraction of four-fold coordinated boron [BO4] in the glass network. Tuheen and Du51 examined the influence of modifier cation field strength (CFS) on the structures of magnesium oxide (MgO)-containing aluminoborosilicate glasses through molecular dynamic (MD) simulations. They demonstrated that higher CFS cations such as Mg2+ reduce the fraction of fourfold coordinated boron [BO4], indicating that such cations promote NBO formation and are less effective in charge compensation in the silicate network. These findings from MD simulations are compared with the results from NMR and Raman spectroscopy studies49. Cui et al.52 examined the effects of Al2O3/SiO2 and B2O3 substitution on the structure and properties of alkali-free aluminoborosilicate glasses containing alkaline-earth oxides. They reported the formation of NBO, as evidenced by O 1 s XPS analysis, and a decrease in [BO4] concentration, as revealed by 11B MAS-NMR, despite the fact that the modifier content was lower than that of Al2O3.

Based on these established studies and our combined experimental results, it can be concluded that the presence of NBOs on Si (Qⁿ species) occurs in the present TeO₂-modified alumino-borosilicate glasses across the investigated composition range. The NBO/BO ratio decreases with increasing TeO2 content up to 2.0 mol%, indicating progressive network polymerization upon TeO2 substitution. This trend is further corroborated by XPS analysis (will be discussed later), consistent with the FTIR structural results.

Structural analysis – XPS

XPS measures the intrinsic binding energy, atomic compositions, oxidation states, and bonding structures of an atomic orbitals, which change depending on the chemical environment52–55. In this study, XPS was used to investigate the effect of TeO2 on the ratio of NBOs to BOs in alumino-borosilicate glasses containing alkaline earth oxides. Figure 2A shows the XPS survey spectra of the GDMC-Te glasses for different TeO2 concentrations within the binding energy (BE) range of 0–1400 eV. The core-level peaks corresponding to each element were identified and marked in the spectra. The survey spectra revealed Si2p, B1s, Al2p, O1s, Mg1s, Ca2p, Na1s, Sn3d, and Te3d peaks along with a C1s signal caused by surface contamination. Furthermore, the high-resolution C1s spectrum showed no additional peaks or shoulders at higher BE values (> 286 eV), indicating that the C1s signal corresponds to C–C bonding, with no signs of hydrocarbon, carbonate, or carbide formation. Thus, the adventitious C1s peak (284.6 eV) was used as a reference to determine the BE values of the glass constituent elements. The peaks at approximately 103, 193, 74, 532, 1304, 348, 1072, 487, and 578 eV correspond to the Si2p, B1s, Al2p, O1s, Mg1s, Ca2p, Na1s, Sn3d, and Te3d photoelectrons, respectively.

High-resolution O1s spectra were acquired for all glass samples to interpret the bonding environment of BO and NBO in the GDMC-Te glass system52–54. The O1s XPS spectra of the GDMC-Te glasses for different TeO2 concentrations varying from 0 to 3.0 mol% are depicted in Fig. 2B. A peak was observed within the BE range of 526–540 eV and centered at approximately 531 eV. Figure 2C−G display the deconvoluted O1s spectra of all the samples. The spectral deconvolution methodology and its interpretation used in this study are consistent with those reported in the literature52–54. The initial peak parameters, including the BE, full width at half maximum, and peak height, were iteratively optimized until the χ-square value remained unchanged. The spectral fitting was performed in an unconstrained manner. XPS has proven to be a powerful technique for quantifying BO and NBO atoms in glasses and has been successfully applied to the study of many oxide glasses52–54. Peaks at higher and lower BE sides of the O 1 s signal are attributed to BO and NBO components, respectively52–54. In the multicomponent glasses, such as those in the present study, it is very difficult to further deconvolute the BO component into distinct oxide environments, such as Si–O, B–O, Al–O, and Te–O, as the validity of the separation by three or more components cannot be evaluated52–55. Therefore, further separation of the O 1 s signal is not justified. The relative amount of each oxygen type was assumed to be proportional to the area under its respective peak. The low BE peak at approximately 531 eV and high BE peak at approximately 532 eV were assigned to the NBO and BO atoms, respectively. The BEs, relative peak areas, and NBO/BO ratios of the GDMC-Te glasses with various TeO2 concentrations are listed in Table 3. The substitution of TeO2 for SiO2 in the glass system changed the bonding between the mobile alkaline ions and their charge-balancing centers (e.g., NBO), and therefore, the fractions of BO and NBO. Relative changes in the total amount of NBO in the glass network were evaluated using the ratio of the NBO and BO peak areas. The NBO/BO ratio decreased from 0.276 to 0.075 upon TeO2 substitution up to 2.0 mol% and then increased to 0.198 for a further increase in TeO2 (3.0 mol%) in the glasses, as shown in Fig. 2H. This trend indicates that the TeO2 substitution for SiO2 up to 2.0 mol% promotes network connectivity in the alumino-borosilicate glass system, which is consistent with the results of FTIR analysis. High-resolution Te 3 d XPS spectra were measured for all glass samples with varying TeO2 concentrations. Physical properties

The density of glass is generally explained by the relationship between the mass and radius of the constituent atoms within its network. It measures how tightly atoms and structural units are packed in a glass network56–58. Fig 3 shows the dependence of density (ρ) and molar volume (Vm) with respect to the TeO2 in the GDMC-Te glasses. Table 4 presents the ρ and Vm values of the GDMC-Te glasses for various concentrations of TeO2 in the range of 0–3.0 mol%. Upon the incorporation of TeO2 into the alumino-borosilicate glass, the ρ and Vm values increased from 2.288 to 2.363 g/cm3 and 28.411 to 28.773 cm3/mol, respectively. The increase in ρ is attributed to the replacement of the lighter SiO2 (molar mass = 60.08 g/mol) by heavier TeO2 (molar mass = 159.61 g/mol) in the GDMC-Te glasses39,44. Vm was calculated using the measured glass densities and molar masses. A decrease in Vm is expected when heavier TeO2 replaces the lighter SiO2 in the GDMC-Te glasses, owing to the corresponding increase in ρ. Although Vm is generally inversely proportional to ρ, this relationship is not always followed in glass systems53. The observed opposite behavior can be explained as follows: the high atomic weight of TeO2 increases the molecular weight of the glass more strongly than the corresponding increase in ρ. Thus, the fraction of the molecular weight of the glass and consequently the Vm is still increasing56,58. The atomic packing densities (Cg) were also calculated for all glass compositions using the molar fractions of constituent oxides in the nominal compositions and their respective ionic radii, as reported by Shannon in Ref59. Since the ionic radii depend on the coordination numbers of the constituent atoms, appropriate assumptions were made: coordination numbers of 2 for O, 4 for Si, 3 for B, 4 for Al, 6 for Mg, 6 for Ca, 6 for Na, 4 for Sn, and 4 for Te were adopted, corresponding to ionic radii of 1.35, 0.26, 0.02, 0.39, 0.72, 1.00, 1.02, 0.69, and 0.52 Å, respectively. The Cg was calculated using the following formula56,58:

Thermal and thermomechanical properties

Figure 4A shows the differential thermal analysis (DTA) traces of TeO2-modified alumino-borosilicate glasses with various concentrations of TeO2. Characteristic thermal properties, including the glass transition temperature (Tg), onset crystallization temperature (Tx), and thermal stability (ΔT), were derived from these traces. The Tg and Tx values were taken from the onset points of the endothermic and exothermic events, respectively, in the DTA curves measured during the first heating cycle21,31,38,53,57. Figure 4B shows the variations in Tg, Tx, and ΔT as a function of TeO2 in the GDMC-Te glasses. Table 5 summarizes the Tg, Tx, and ΔT values of these glasses. As shown in Fig. 4B, upon the incorporation of TeO2 in the range of 0–3.0 mol% into the alumino-borosilicate glass system, both Tg and Tx decreased from 672 to 658 °C and from 1060 to 815 °C, respectively. Generally, Tg is predominantly governed by both the glass network connectivity and its average bond strength21,57. The observed decrease in Tg when TeO2 replaces SiO2 is caused by differences in the energies of the chemical bonds. Te−O bonds (285 kJ/mol) are weaker than Si-O bonds (443 kJ/mol)60. The observed Tg values for the GDMC-Te glasses are within the range required for glass substrates in advanced semiconductor packaging applications.

The stability parameter ΔT is calculated from the difference between the Tg and Tx values, i.e., ΔT = Tx −Tg21,31,38,53. It measures the thermal resistance of the glass against crystallization during reheating21,31,38,53. Higher values of ΔT (> 100 °C) typically correspond to a higher thermal stability and glass-forming tendency21,31,38,53. In this study, the ΔT value of the glasses decreased steadily from 388 to 157 °C upon the incorporation of TeO2 in the range of 0–3.0 mol%. The decrease in ΔT with the substitution of TeO2 for SiO2 is attributed to a large difference in the melting temperatures of TeO2 (733 °C) and SiO2 (1700 °C). The GDMC-Te glasses showed a thermal stability greater than 100 °C, indicating their suitability for packaging applications.

The CTE is an important property to consider when utilizing oxide glasses in composite structures, such as PCB laminates, heterogeneous integration, and 3D electronic packaging, to prevent thermal damage due to mismatched thermal behaviors between the composite materials6,13,15. In particular, it is a key factor in reducing the chip warpage in advanced semiconductor packages13,15. The CTE of glass is not only influenced by the temperature but also by the structure of the glass network, including the degree of polymerization, type of structural units, and role of different cations as network formers or modifiers61. Figure 5A shows the TMA curves of the GDMC-Te glasses for various TeO2 concentrations. The dilatometric glass transition temperature (Tdg), softening temperature (Tds), and CTE values were determined from the TMA curves, as listed in Table 6. The variations in the Tdg, Tds, and CTE values as functions of TeO2 are shown in Fig. 5B. The values of Tdg and Tds decreased from 691 to 665 °C and from 816 to 787 °C, respectively, with the progressive substitution of TeO2 for SiO2 in the range of 0–3.0 mol%. The observed steady decrease in Tdg and Tds with the progressive substitution of TeO2 for SiO2 is attributed to the larger radius of Te4+ and, consequently, the weaker Te−O bonds (285 kJ/mol) compared with the stronger Si-O bonds (443 kJ/mol)60. The CTE reached a minimum of 2.99 (⋅10−6/K) at 1.0 mol% TeO2 and then increased to 3.26 (⋅10−6/K) at 3.0 mol% over the temperature range of 25–300 °C. The decrease in the CTE of the glasses with increasing TeO2 content is attributed to the enhanced network connectivity, as evidenced by the FTIR and XPS analyses. Specifically, the increase in the area of the Q4 peak (∼1146 cm− 1) is associated with BOs, and the corresponding decrease in the Q1 peak (∼ 919 cm− 1), indicative of NBOs, suggests the strengthening of the glass network in the GDMC-Te system upon TeO2 substitution. The glass containing 1.0 mol% TeO2 exhibited the lowest CTE among the investigated compositions. Notably, the CTE of this GDMC-Te1.0 glass (2.99⋅10−6/K) was close to that of silicon (2.6 ⋅10−6/K)62. In addition, the TeO2-modified GDMC glasses exhibited lower CTE values than those of the commercial glasses, including Schott Borofloat 33 (3.29 ⋅10−6/K)63, Schott AF32 (3.2 ⋅10−6/K)64, Schott D263T (7.2 ⋅10−6/K)65, L-glass (3.9 ⋅10−6/K)66, and E-glass (5.4 ⋅10−6/K)67, as listed in Table 6 and shown in Fig. 5C. These features ensure their suitability as glass core substrate materials for advanced semiconductor packaging applications.

Dielectric and polarizability properties at GHz frequency

The dielectric properties (Dk, Df) of the TeO2-modified alumino-borosilicate glasses were measured in the frequency range of 5–50 GHz and are plotted in Fig. 6. A frequency dispersion of the real part of the permittivity (Dk) was observed for all the studied glasses, which is typical for dielectric materials9–11,68,69. This behavior is attributed to the reduced ability of the dipoles within the glass structure to align with a rapidly oscillating electric field at higher frequencies70,71. The imaginary part of the permittivity, known as the dissipation factor or loss tangent (Df), is plotted in Fig. 6B. As shown in Fig. 6B, the Df increased with increasing frequency for all the compositions. This trend is attributed to the dielectric polarization mechanisms within the glass network, such as the movement of parts of the glass network structure or ion migration9–11,68,69. Figures 6C and D show the variations in Dk and Df as a function of TeO2 varying from 0 to 3.0 mol% in the alumino-borosilicate glasses. Table 7 lists the Dk and Df values of the GDMC-Te glasses for different concentrations of TeO2. Notably, the Dk values decreased with increasing TeO2 content up to 2.0 mol%, but increased when TeO2 was further increased to 3.0 mol% at the high frequencies of 5, 10, 28, and 40 GHz, while Df values remained nearly constant with increasing TeO2concentration across all measured frequencies. The observed behavior is interpreted using the Clausius-Mossotti Eq68,69,72, which links the dielectric constant (εr, Dk) to the dielectric polarizability (αD) and molar volume (Vm) of the glass. αD reflects the sum of the polarizabilities of the constituent oxide ions, whereas Vm affects the density of the polarizable units per unit volume. The Clausius-Mossotti (CM) equation is given by,

where Vm is the molar volume (Å3), αD is the total dielectric polarizability, MW is the molar mass, ρ is the density of glass, and NA is Avogadro’s number (6.023⋅1023 mol− 1). Shannon72 reported a comprehensive compilation of the polarizability data for various ions. Among the chemical elements, the B3+ ion stands out with the lowest polarizability of 0.05 Å3, while Si4+, Al3+, Te4+, Sn4+, and O2− exhibit values of 0.87, 0.79, 5.23, and 2.01 Å3, respectively72. Typical glass network modifiers, such as Na, Mg, and Ca, possess polarizability values of 1.8, 1.32, and 3.16 Å3, respectively72. Dk would typically be expected to increase with TeO2 substitution because Te4+ (5.23 Å3) has a much higher cation polarizability than Si4+ (0.87 Å3)72; however, the opposite behavior was observed in the studied glasses. Thus, the observed Dk with composition is explained by a structural investigation. Structural analyses (FTIR and XPS) revealed a reduction in the NBO/BO ratio with TeO2 the addition of up to 2.0 mol%, indicating enhanced network connectivity in the alumino-borosilicate glass network. This increased connectivity reduced the overall polarizability, thereby leading to an observed decrease in Dk. In addition, an increase in molar volume (Vm) and a corresponding decrease in atomic packing density (Cg) were observed with increasing TeO2 content (see Fig. 3). This results in a lower density of polarizable ions per unit volume, thereby further contributing to the reduction in Dk. The reduction in Dk with the addition of TeO2 in the glasses can also be explained by the shift toward higher wavenumbers in the high-frequency region (1300–1500 cm−1) of the FTIR spectra. This shift indicates an increase in the bond strength around the B-O− harmonic oscillator, revealing the rigid chain-type metaborate structures [BO3] within the glass network, which in turn contributes a decrease in Dk. The Df reached a minimum at 1.0 mol% TeO2 and then increased for further addition of TeO2 at all measured frequencies (5, 10, and 40 GHz), except for 28 GHz. Notably, the present GDMC-Te glasses exhibit lower Dk and Df values than those of commercial glasses, such as Schott Borofloat 33 (Dk = 4.4 and Df = 7.5⋅10−3 at 10 GHz), Schott AF32 eco (Dk = 5.1 and Df = 6.1⋅10−3 at 10 GHz), Schott D263 T eco (Dk = 6.4 and Df = 12.7⋅10−3 at 10 GHz), AGY L-glass (Dk = 4.8 and Df = 3.0⋅10−3 at 10 GHz), and E-glass (Dk = 6.9 and Df = 7.0⋅10−3 at 10 GHz), as listed in Table 7 and illustrated in Fig. 6E and F. These lower dielectric properties make the GDMC-Te glasses promising candidates for substrate applications, particularly in high-frequency and high-speed electronic packaging, where reduced signal delay, minimized dielectric loss, and improved signal integrity are essential.

Conclusions

TeO2-modified alumino-borosilicate glasses were successfully fabricated by a conventional melt-quenching technique. The effect of replacing SiO2 with TeO2 on the structural, thermal, thermomechanical, and dielectric properties at 5–50 GHz of the glasses were demonstrated. The glass system exhibited significant improvements in Dk and Df with the incorporation of TeO2. The Dk values decreased from 4.582 to 4.458 at 40 GHz with the substitution of TeO2 for SiO2 up to 2.0 mol% and then increased to 4.537 for further increases in TeO2 (3.0 mol%). The Df reached a minimum at 1.0 mol% TeO2, but increased with further additions of TeO2 at all measured frequencies of 5, 10, and 40 GHz except for 28 GHz. The structural investigations using ATR-FTIR and XPS supported the improvements in dielectric properties with respect to the chemical composition. Structural studies revealed that substituting TeO2 for SiO2 up to 2.0 mol% in the GDMC-Te glass system caused the polymerization of the silicate network, as evidenced by a decrease in the Q1/Q4 ratio, that is, a decrease in the NBOs with respect to the BOs in the glass network. The CTE reached a minimum of 2.99 (⋅10−6/K) at 1.0 mol% TeO2 and then increased to 3.26 (⋅10−6/K) at 3.0 mol% over the temperature range of 25–300 °C. The obtained CTE values were lower than those of commercial glasses, while being close to that of silicon (2.6⋅10− 6/K). Notably, the Dk and Df values at 10 GHz were lower than those of commercial glasses, such as Schott Borofloat 33 (Dk = 4.4 and Df = 7.5⋅10−3 at 10 GHz), Schott AF32 eco (Dk = 5.1 and Df = 6.1⋅10−3 at 10 GHz), Schott D263 T eco (Dk = 6.4 and Df = 12.7⋅10−3 at 10 GHz), AGY L-glass (Dk = 4.8 and Df = 3.0⋅10−3 at 10 GHz), and E-glass (Dk = 6.9 and Df = 7.0⋅10−3 at 10 GHz). These results indicate that the TeO2-modified alumino-borosilicate glasses with enhanced dielectric properties and CTE are promising glass core substrate materials for advanced semiconductor packaging applications in the frequency range of 5–50 GHz.
