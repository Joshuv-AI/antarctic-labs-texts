Clinical agreement and physiological relevance of the zero-heat-flux temperature measurement in trauma patients during operating room resuscitation
Authors: Jiyoung Lee, Hyoeun Ahn, Hye-min Sohn, Seung Ho Song, In Kyong Yi

Background

Hypothermia is common and occurs early in trauma patients. Previous reports have showed that hypothermia at the time of admission is associated with mortality [1, 2]. The prevalence of hypothermia is as high as 29% on EMS arrival, therefore routine early measurement and continuous monitoring of temperature is recommended [3]. The gold standard for core temperature measurement is pulmonary artery, however, its use is limited due to the invasiveness. Esophageal temperature is known to correlate well with pulmonary artery temperature and is considered a standard method for pre-hospital measurement of core temperature [4]. However, applying esophageal temperature is difficult in patients without intubation and in those with orofacial injury or when esophageal injury is suspected. Tympanic contact temperature is another alternative method, but it could be affected by ambient temperature and carries a potential risk of tympanic membrane injury [5].

Zero-heat-flux(ZHF) temperature monitoring devices were introduced 50 years ago but have recently begun to enter the market with upgraded function. The 3 M™ Bair Hugger™ Temperature Monitoring System (Solventum, Maplewood, MN, USA), one such device, is simple and non-invasive, as it is applied by attaching a sensor to the forehead [6]. In addition, this method of temperature monitoring has the advantage of being applicable in patients without intubation. Verheyden et al. reported that the 3 M™ Bair Hugger™ Temperature Monitoring system correlated better with pulmonary artery temperature than nasopharyngeal, bladder, and rectal measurements in patients who underwent off-pump coronary artery bypass surgery [7]. Other studies have also shown that the 3 M™ Bair Hugger™ Temperature Monitoring System provides clinically acceptable accuracy and precision [6, 8, 9]. Therefore, its application in trauma patients may be useful and can also be easily extended to the pre-hospital stage. However, no previous studies have evaluated the use of this device in the extreme settings of trauma patients.

The purpose of this study was to investigate the correlation between esophageal temperature and ZHF temperature measured by the 3 M™ Bair Hugger™ Temperature Monitoring System in trauma patients who underwent emergency surgery.

Methods

Patients

The current retrospective study protocols were approved by Ajou Hospital Institutional Review Board (AJOUIRB-MDB-2021-557) and also registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05770830). Written informed consent was waived. Trauma patients aged 15–80 years who were concurrently applied with the esophageal temperature probe and 3 M™ Bair Hugger™ Temperature Monitoring System (ZHF temperature) during operating room resuscitation from Jan 1st, 2021 to Oct 31st 2021 in Level 1 trauma center were included.

Data collection

Demographic data, laboratory data, and trauma-related data, including injury severity score, initial Glasgow coma scale, and revised trauma score, were extracted from electrical medical records and KTAS (Korean triage and acuity scale) registry.

Hemodynamic data and temperature data was extracted from the anesthesia records which are saved automatically every 5 min.

Missing values in which even one of the two temperatures is excluded. If the missing values account for more than 50% of the total number of measurements in one case, the case was excluded from the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Agreement between ZHF temperature and esophageal temperature was evaluated using Bland–Altman analysis with correction for repeated measurements within subjects. The concordance between the two measurements was further assessed using Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient, also corrected for repeated measurements. Additional analyses were performed after excluding extreme data (esophageal temperature < 34 °C), as these values may obscure overall agreement trends and are associated with critical clinical conditions.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to compare the mean difference (Tempₑₛₒ − TempZHF) across clinical factors such as survival status, lactate change, open abdomen or chest, brain injury, and activation of a massive transfusion protocol.

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range], as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using [R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) / or SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA)].

The esophageal temperature was considered as standard method and ranged from 30.3 to 37.8℃ in this study population. The bias calculated by Bland-Altman analysis was − 0.22 ℃ (95% limits of agreement [LOA], -1.30 to 0.85 ℃ (p < 0.001), which means the esophageal temperature was 0.22 ℃ lower than the ZHF temperature. (Fig. 1). Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient with correction for repeated measurements was 0.759 (95% confidence interval, 0.674–0.825). In summary, the two methods showed moderate agreement, indicating that they were not interchangeable. However, the overall precision and stability across a wide temperature range suggest that the ZHF temperature method may suitable for trend monitoring under limited circumstances.

To further evaluate performance under different thermal conditions, data were stratified by esophageal temperature (≥ 34 °C vs. < 34 °C). After excluding extreme hypothermic data (esophageal temperature < 34 °C), 1,591 paired measurements were analyzed. The mean bias was − 0.23 ± 0.45 °C (p < 0.001), and the 95% LOA were − 1.11 °C to 0.65 °C (Fig. 2). Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient with correction for repeated measurements was 0.846 (95% confidence interval, 0.716–0.864). Excluding extremely low temperature values (< 34 °C) improved agreement and slightly narrowed the confidence interval of Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient, suggesting enhanced precision.

Subgroup analyses were performed to identify factors affecting the difference between esophageal and ZHF temperatures (Table 2). The mean temperature difference (Tempₑₛₒ – Tempₛₖiₙ) was significantly larger in patients who died compared with survivors (–0.43 ± 0.49 °C vs. − 0.25 ± 0.53 °C; p < 0.001). Lactate improvement status did not significantly influence the temperature difference (p = 0.261). Patients who underwent open abdominal surgery showed a greater mean difference than those without (–0.33 ± 0.57 °C vs. − 0.15 ± 0.40 °C; p < 0.001). In contrast, patients who underwent open chest surgery exhibited smaller bias values (–0.17 ± 0.51 °C) compared with those without (–0.28 ± 0.53 °C; p = 0.023). In patients with brain hemorrhage or open head wounds, the ZHF temperature tended to be lower than the esophageal temperature, showing a positive mean difference (0.02 ± 0.42 °C and 0.11 ± 0.31 °C, respectively; both p < 0.001**). Patients with activation of a massive transfusion protocol showed only a modest increase in bias (–0.31 ± 0.61 °C) compared with those without (–0.25 ± 0.47 °C; p = 0.037), indicating that the difference between the two temperature measurements widened under massive transfusion conditions.

Discussion

This is the first study to compare ZHF temperature measured by the 3 M™ Bair Hugger™ Temperature Monitoring System and esophageal temperature in trauma patients who underwent operating room resuscitation.

Mortality in severely injured patients increases with the degree of hypothermia [10]. In particular, because the prognosis of hypothermia is poor in trauma patients, hypothermia in trauma patients is classified more strictly than the traditional classification of hypothermia: mild (36–34 °C), moderate (34–32 °C), or severe (below 32 °C) [11]. Therefore, it is important to measure body temperature to prevent hypothermia in trauma patients.

According to previous studies, the 3 M™ Bair Hugger™ Temperature Monitoring System provides good accuracy and precision for continuous core temperature measurement during the perioperative phase [6, 12] as well as in targeted temperature management (TTM) following cardiac arrest [13, 14]. In our study, the mean bias of the esophageal thermometer and the 3 M™ Bair Hugger™ Temperature Monitoring System did not exceed 0.5 °C as considered clinically important in the previous studies, which is considered the threshold for clinical acceptability in previous reports [15, 16]. This finding indicates that the accuracy of the 3 M™ Bair Hugger™ Temperature Monitoring System is clinically acceptable and comparable to results from prior studies [8, 9, 13]. However, the precision as measured by the Bland–Altman analysis 95% limits of agreement of ± 1.0337℃ did not meet to commonly accepted criterion of ± 0.5 °C for “good precision” [17]. Our findings are consistent with a recent meta-analysis of the 3 M™ Bair Hugger™ Temperature Monitoring System, which similarly showed high accuracy but relatively wide limits of agreement of about ± 1 °C [18]. In our study, factors associated with a greater difference between the two temperature measurements included poorer clinical condition and the requirement for massive transfusion. In addition, open abdominal surgery may have increased exposure of the esophageal probe to ambient temperature, and head open trauma may also have affected the ZHF temperature measured at the forehead.

The limited precision of the 3 M™ Bair Hugger™ Temperature Monitoring System is related to the intrinsic characteristics of the method [18]. The 3 M™ Bair Hugger™ Temperature Monitoring System uses the zero-heat-flux method, which includes thermal insulator sensor with servo-controlled heating on the surrounding skin - it makes zero-heat-flux region of isothermal tunnel from the deep tissue to skin. When the heat flow between the skin and the deep tissue stops, the temperature between the skin and the deep tissue becomes identical. Therefore, although this 3 M™ Bair Hugger™ Temperature Monitoring System attaches to the skin, it measures core body temperature [19]. However, this equilibrium process requires a certain amount of time to establish, and thus the system may respond more slowly to rapid or transient changes in core temperature. This delayed response could have a greater impact in trauma patients, whose core temperature often fluctuates rapidly due to massive bleeding, fluid resuscitation, and exposure during surgery.

Because the lower esophagus and the left atrium are anatomically close, esophageal temperature measured in the lower third of the esophagus reflects core body temperature well [20, 21]. However, inserting an esophageal probe carries potential risks such as mucosal injury, bleeding, or arrhythmia, particularly in trauma patients with coagulopathy or severe hypothermia [22–27]. In addition, esophageal temperature monitoring is not feasible in conscious patients or in those without a definitive airway, which limits its applicability during the initial resuscitation phase. In contrast, the 3 M™ Bair Hugger™ Temperature Monitoring System provides a non-invasive and convenient alternative that continuously measures core temperature from the forehead skin [28]. Therefore, the 3 M™ Bair Hugger™ Temperature Monitoring System may offer a safer and more practical option for continuous core temperature monitoring in trauma patients, especially during operating room resuscitation or in prehospital emergency settings.

In our data, the ZHF temperature measured was generally higher than the esophageal temperature, and we observed that the discrepancy tended to widen in patients with poorer clinical conditions. The ZHF method determines core temperature by applying controlled heat to the skin until thermal equilibrium is achieved between the skin surface and underlying tissues. However, this equilibrium relies on adequate peripheral perfusion to ensure consistent heat transfer. In critically ill or hemodynamically unstable patients, impaired skin blood flow or redistribution of circulation may delay or disrupt this equilibrium, resulting in greater discrepancies between skin and esophageal temperatures. In our study, patients who required massive transfusion or those who eventually died after surgery showed a tendency toward larger temperature differences, suggesting that the zero-heat-flux method may become less reliable under compromised circulatory conditions. A similar tendency was also observed in patients undergoing open abdominal procedures, in whom greater temperature differences were noted. Nevertheless, this widening temperature gap itself could serve as a physiological marker of deteriorating perfusion or thermal imbalance, rather than merely a measurement error. However, this relationship cannot be conclusively determined from the present study and should be interpreted with caution.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective study with a relatively small number of patients, which may limit the generalizability of the results. In addition, some subgroup analyses included a limited number of patients, which may have reduced the statistical robustness of those findings. Second, although esophageal temperature was used as the reference standard, it may not perfectly represent true core temperature because the probe depth or position could have varied among patients. Third, we did not evaluate the temporal trend or response time of the 3 M™ Bair Hugger™ Temperature Monitoring System in relation to rapid changes in body temperature. Future prospective studies with larger populations are warranted to validate our findings and to assess the dynamic accuracy of this system under varying thermal conditions.

In conclusion, although the 3 M™ Bair Hugger™ Temperature Monitoring System has some limitations in precision, it demonstrates clinically acceptable accuracy and offers the advantages of being continuous, non-invasive, and easy to use. Therefore, it may serve as a reliable alternative method for monitoring core body temperature in trauma patients undergoing operating room resuscitation.
