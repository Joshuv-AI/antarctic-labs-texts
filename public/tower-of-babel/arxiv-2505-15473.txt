Fast and robust detection of single Rydberg excitations in mesoscopic ensembles
Sven Schmidt, Aaron Thielmann, Thomas Niederprüm, Herwig Ott

Abstract
We propose a novel non-destructive method for the detection of single Rydberg excitations in a mesoscopic ensemble. The protocol achieves high fidelities on a microsecond timescale and is robust against changes in the probe laser frequency. The technique relies on optical pumping in Autler Townes configuration, whose efficiency is controlled by the presence/absence of a Rydberg excitation. Taking rubidium atoms as an example, we give realistic estimates for the achievable fidelities and parameters. However, our protocol can be transferred to any other atomic species which features multiple stable states. Our protocol is applicable in quantum simulation and quantum information processing with mesoscopic ensembles requiring fast and high fidelity Rydberg state detection.



I
Introduction

Due to their outstanding properties in terms of scalability and controllability, tweezer arrays of neutral atoms emerged as one of the prime candidates for quantum simulation and quantum information processing in past years
Barredo et al. 2016
;
Norcia et al. 2018
;
Cooper et al. 2018
.
The building principle is based on the generation of arbitrary configurations of optical tweezers with single site resolution.
Although separated by several micrometers, excitations to high lying Rydberg states can be used to mediate tunable interactions between the traps
Jaksch et al. 2000
;
Saffman et al. 2010
, making use of both, well isolated sites and versatile couplings.
Based on this concept, groundbreaking progress such as the simulation of quantum spin models with hundreds of atoms
Ebadi et al. 2021
;
Scholl et al. 2021
, realization of high fidelity quantum gates
Levine et al. 2019
;
Graham et al. 2019
;
Levine et al. 2018
;
Evered et al. 2023
and implementation of quantum algorithms
Graham et al. 2022
;
Bluvstein et al. 2022
has already been demonstrated, with recent findings underlining its scalability by achieving arrays of a few thousand sites
Pause et al. 2024
;
Manetsch et al. 2024
.
Although most commonly loaded with a single atom per site, ensembles with a few (hundred) atoms
Wang et al. 2020
;
Gosar et al. 2022
in each tweezer can exploit collective excitations
Ebert et al. 2015
;
Spong et al. 2021
, speeding up the preparation and detection
Xu et al. 2021
.
While several suggestions for fast detection in the
\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
timescale have already been made, they either lack a sufficient spatial resolution
Bergschneider et al. 2018
, or require large ensembles of hundreds of atoms and specialized detection setups with near single photon sensitivity
Xu et al. 2021
.
We aim to bridge the gap between the well established methods of fluorescence imaging of single atoms on the
\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
scale
Madjarov et al. 2020
;
Tao et al. 2024
and those fast techniques by amplifying the signal of a single excitation by a surrounding ensemble, reducing the state read out time and, optionally, storing its information in the ensemble.

Our method is designed for small atomic ensembles and employs a two-photon transition from the ground to the Rydberg state in Autler–Townes configuration.
In the presence of a Rydberg excitation in the sample, optical pumping of the sample into a second hyperfine ground state is activated.
This leads to a very fast projective non-destructive measurement of the Rydberg excitation, which is mapped to the ground state population on a
\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
scale.
The population of the ground state is then read out immediately afterwards or later on.
This protocol is fast, provides high imaging fidelity and even allows for the detection of multiple subsequent Rydberg excitations in the same sample.

II
Basic principle

For the imaging protocol to work, we require an atomic species with at least two (meta-)stable states while we limit the following discussion to hyperfine ground states. The sample is initially prepared in one of the ground states
\ket{\uparrow}
(Fig.
1(a)
).
In order to detect a Rydberg state
\ket{\mathrm{r}}
in the atomic sample, we couple the ground state to an auxiliary Rydberg state
\ket{\mathrm{r}^{*}}
via an intermediate state
\ket{\mathrm{e}}
in a two-photon transition.
The coupling is realized in Autler-Townes configuration, i.e. the coupling on the upper transition to the Rydberg state is much stronger than the radiative decay rate of the intermediate state.
When both light fields are resonant, the Autler-Townes splitting of the intermediate state strongly suppresses scattering of the probe laser
Autler and Townes 1955
.
Consequently, optical pumping to a second hyperfine ground state
\ket{\downarrow}
is strongly suppressed.
In the presence of a Rydberg excitation
\ket{\mathrm{r}}
in the sample (control atom) the energy of the auxiliary Rydberg state
\ket{\mathrm{r}^{*}}
is shifted due to the dipole–dipole interaction and the Autler–Townes condition breaks down.
Optical pumping is now active and ground state population is quickly transferred to the state
\ket{\downarrow}
.

\Delta E_{\pm}=-\frac{\hbar\delta_{c}}{2}\pm\frac{\hbar\sqrt{\Omega_{c}^{2}+\delta_{c}^{2}}}{2}\,.

(1)

In the limit of vanishing detuning, this results in the well known Autler-Townes splitting

\Delta E_{\pm}=\pm\frac{\hbar\Omega_{c}}{2}

(2)

and the atoms in the sample witness an effective detuning

\left|\Delta_{p}\right|=\frac{\Omega_{c}}{2}

(3)

on the probing transition
\ket{\uparrow}\rightarrow\ket{\mathrm{e}}
.
For large
\Omega_{c}
, the initial state of the atom is preserved as scattering on the probe transition is strongly suppressed (Fig.
2(a)
).
If, however, the control atom is in state
\ket{\mathrm{r}}
, the situation is different.
Due to their huge polarizability, Rydberg atoms experience a strong dipole-dipole or van der Waals interaction
V_{\mathrm{RyRy}}
, which shifts the energy of
\ket{\mathrm{r}^{*}}

Reinhard et al. 2007
;
Singer et al. 2005
;
Comparat and Pillet 2010
and increases the detuning
\delta_{c}\propto V_{\mathrm{RyRy}}
.
For
\delta_{c}\gg\Omega_{c}
, Eq. (
1
) is approximated by

\Delta E_{\pm}=-\frac{\hbar\delta_{c}}{2}\pm\left(\frac{\hbar\delta_{c}}{2}+\frac{\hbar\Omega_{c}^{2}}{4\delta_{c}}\right),

(4)

resulting in a small detuning

\left|\Delta_{p}\right|=\frac{\Omega_{c}^{2}}{4\delta_{c}}\ll\frac{\Omega_{c}}{2}

(5)

for one of the eigenstates. Consequently, the transfer to
\ket{\downarrow}
is enhanced (Fig.
2(b)
).

While this simplified two-level consideration explains the basic principle, the multi-level structure of the atom leads to a much more involved situation as multiple couplings and competing interactions have to be considered
Bender et al. 2024
.
Furthermore, the energy shift due to
V_{\mathrm{RyRy}}
leading to the coupling laser detuning
\delta_{c}
cannot always be assumed to be independent of the Rabi coupling, as for sufficiently strong
\Omega_{c}
both interactions compete with each other, leading to modifications of the Rydberg-Rydberg potential.
For a more in-detail calculation bases on the simplified two-level model, we refer to Appendix 1.

III
Full model

Hamiltonian and chosen basis sets

Taking into account these aspects, we extend the discussion to the more realistic case of a multi-level system with respect to the intermediate state and the Rydberg states.
To this end, we introduce all interactions and the appropriate basis as a starting point for the diagonalization of the system Hamiltonian.

As we consider the probing to be weak compared to all other interactions and couplings, we don’t model it but rather include the probing of the states
\ket{\uparrow}
and
\ket{\downarrow}
in a second step as a perturbation.
A sketch of the level system is shown in Fig.
3(a)
.
We include two Rydberg manifolds
\left\{\ket{\mathrm{r}^{*}}\!\right\}
and
\left\{\ket{\mathrm{r}}\!\right\}
to realistically model the Rydberg-Rydberg interaction
V_{\mathrm{RyRy}}
.
In addition, all states within
\left\{\ket{\mathrm{e}}\!\right\}
and their internal interaction
V_{\mathrm{int}}
are included as well.
Finally, we neglect multi-particle effects between the atoms in the sample, which go beyond the Rydberg blockade physics.
It is now sufficient to consider only the control atom and one additional atom, which is representative for each atom in the ensemble. We therefore restrict the Hilbert space to a two-particle product basis

\ket{\Psi}=\ket{\psi}_{\mathrm{C}}\otimes\,\ket{\psi}_{\mathrm{S}}\,,

(6)

where
\ket{\psi}_{\mathrm{C}}(\ket{\psi}_{\mathrm{S}})
represents the state of the control (sample) atom. As the control atom is either in the Rydberg state
\ket{\mathrm{r}}
or does not play a role, the intermediate state manifold
\{\ket{\mathrm{e}}\!\}
is only taken into account for the sample atom.

With these assumptions, the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ATI}}
consists of three terms, describing the involved couplings:

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ATI}}\left(R\right)=\hat{H}_{\mathrm{RyRy}}\left(R\right)+\,\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Rabi}}+\,\hat{H}_{\mathrm{HFS}}\,.

(7)

The Rydberg-Rydberg Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{RyRy}}\left(R\right)
is based on the dipole-dipole interaction
V_{\mathrm{RyRy}}
, which couples the Rydberg states
\left\{\ket{\mathrm{r}}\!\right\}
and
\left\{\ket{\mathrm{r}^{*}}\!\right\}
of the control and the ensemble atom, respectively. It explicitly depends on the separation
R
of the two atoms. In the following, we drop this dependence in the notation for the sake of simplicity. We, however, point out it’s importance when considering the finite extent of the sample and the relative distances of the atoms.
We set up the Hamiltonian following Ref.
Wolf and Birgeneau 1968
, including only dipolar interactions.
As for the dipole-dipole interaction, the fine structure basis of both atoms is a good basis, defined by quantum numbers
n,l,j
,
m_{j}
and
s
:

\ket{\Psi}=\ket{\psi}_{\mathrm{C}}\otimes\,\ket{\psi}_{\mathrm{S}}=\ket{n,l,j,m_{j}}_{\mathrm{C}}\otimes\,\ket{n,l,j,m_{j}}_{\mathrm{S}}.

(8)

In the same basis, the coupling between
\left\{\ket{\mathrm{r}^{(*)}}\!\right\}
and
\left\{\ket{\mathrm{e}}\!\right\}
is described by the Rabi Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Rabi}}
, whose expectation value is given by
\Omega_{c}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\braket{\mathrm{r}^{(*)}|\,\mathbf{d}\kern-4.25003pt\hat{\phantom{\mathbf{d}}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{E}}\,|\mathrm{e}}
, where
\mathbf{d}\kern-4.25003pt\hat{\phantom{\mathbf{d}}}
is the dipole operator and
\hat{\mathbf{E}}
the electric field of the coupling light.
The Hamiltonian separates

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Rabi}}=\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Rabi,\mathrm{C}}}\otimes\,\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Rabi,\mathrm{S}}}

(9)

and if
\left\{\ket{\mathrm{r}^{*}}\!\right\}
and
\left\{\ket{\mathrm{r}}\!\right\}
are energetically very different states, the coupling only acts on the sample atom and we can rewrite

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Rabi}}=\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{C}}\otimes\,\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Rabi,\mathrm{S}}}.

(10)

While the hyperfine interaction for Rydberg states can be neglected
Li et al. 2003
;
Cardman and Raithel 2022
;
S. et al. 2022
, it has to be included in the manifold
\left\{\ket{\mathrm{e}}\!\right\}
as for lower lying states the associated energy shifts are comparable to
\Omega_{c}
.
The hyperfine structure Hamiltonian

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{HFS}}=\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{C}}\otimes\,\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{HFS}}^{\{\ket{\mathrm{e}}\}}\,\mathbf{\hat{I}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{J}}

(11)

again only acts on the sample atom
\ket{\psi}_{\mathrm{S}}
.
To this end, the nuclear spin
I
and its projection
m_{I}
of the sample atom need to be included, extending Eq. (
8
) to

\ket{\Psi}=\ket{n,l,j,m_{j}}_{\mathrm{C}}\otimes\,\ket{n,l,j,m_{j},I,m_{I}}_{\mathrm{S}}.

(12)

In this basis, the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ATI}}(R)
takes the form presented in Fig.
3(b)
and is diagonalized to obtain the level structure that can be addressed by the probing laser to perform optical pumping.

Probing

As a second step, we now consider the scattering of the (weak) probing laser on the level system resulting from the above diagonalization.
The primary quantity of interest in this process for our proposal is the sample transfer probability
\mathcal{P}
. It is defined as the probability of transferring an atom of the sample from
\ket{\uparrow}
to
\ket{\downarrow}
and will in the following be calculated.

To simplify the calculation, we first transform the eigenstates of
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ATI}}
to the hyperfine structure as we naturally describe the ground states in the basis
\ket{\psi}_{\mathrm{G}}=\ket{n,j,l,F,m_{F}}
.
The manifolds
\left\{\ket{\mathrm{r}}\!\right\}
and
\left\{\ket{\mathrm{r}^{*}}\!\right\}
do not need to be transformed.

Since we are only interested in the ground state population of the sample atoms, irrespective of the control atom, we need to trace out the latter state.
To this end, we set up the density matrix
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{CS}}^{\left(\varphi\right)}=\ket{\phi_{\varphi}}\bra{\phi_{\varphi}}
for each eigenstate
\ket{\phi_{\varphi}}
resulting from the diagonalization and take the partial trace over the control atom:

\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{S}}^{\left(\varphi\right)}=\mathrm{tr}_{\mathrm{C}}\left(\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{CS}}^{\left(\varphi\right)}\right).

(13)

Diagonalizing the density matrix provides a basis set
\left\{\ket{\Theta_{\vartheta}}\!\right\}
with eigenvalues
\left\{\theta_{\vartheta}\right\}
, in which the coupling of the probe field can be treated conveniently.

For each ground state
g\equiv\ket{\psi_{g}}_{\mathrm{G}}
and basis state
\vartheta\equiv\ket{\Theta_{\vartheta}}
, we determine the scattering rate according to

R_{\mathrm{sc}}^{\left(g,\vartheta\right)}\,=\,\frac{\Gamma}{2}\,\frac{2\left(\Omega^{\left(g,\vartheta\right)}\!/\Gamma\right)^{2}}{1+2\left(\Omega^{\left(g,\vartheta\right)}\!/\Gamma\right)^{2}+\left(2\Delta_{p}/\Gamma\right)^{2}}\,.

(14)

Here,
\Omega^{\left(g,\vartheta\right)}\,=\,{}_{\mathrm{G}}\!\braket{\psi_{g}|\,\mathbf{d}\kern-4.25003pt\hat{\phantom{\mathbf{d}}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{E}}\,|\Theta_{\vartheta}}
is the Rabi coupling between the states
g
and
\vartheta
,
\Gamma
is the natural linewidth of the
\ket{\uparrow}\rightarrow\ket{\mathrm{e}}
transition and the effective probe laser detuning
\Delta_{p}
is given by the difference in the photon energy and the energy of the eigenstate
\ket{\phi_{\varphi}}
.
Summing up the contributions of the different
\ket{\Theta_{\vartheta}}
results in

R_{\mathrm{sc}}^{\left(g,\varphi\right)}\,=\,\sum_{\vartheta}\left|\theta_{\vartheta}\right|^{2}R_{\mathrm{sc}}^{\left(g,\vartheta\right)}.

(15)

In the limit of weak probing, the scattering rates of the individual eigenstates
\ket{\phi_{\varphi}}
for a given ground state
\ket{\psi_{g}}_{\mathrm{G}}
can be summed up in a single effective scattering rate

R_{\mathrm{sc}}^{\left(g\right)}\,=\,\sum_{\varphi}R_{\mathrm{sc}}^{\left(g,\varphi\right)}\,.

(16)

The above expression is the basis for the calculation of the transfer probability. Note again, that it implicitly depends on
R
.

The number of photons scattered by an atom is then given by

N^{\left(g\right)}\,=\,R_{\mathrm{sc}}^{\left(g\right)}\cdot t_{p}\,,

(17)

where
t_{p}
is the duration of the probe pulse. To access the transfer probability we define the branching ratio
B
, which describes the probability to end up in
\ket{\downarrow}
after one scattering event.
After the probing time
t_{p}
, we find

P_{\mathrm{tr}}^{\left(g\right)}\,=\,1-\left(1-B\right)^{N^{\left(g\right)}}\,,

(18)

for a given initial state
\ket{\uparrow}
, with probability
p^{\left(g\right)}
and
\sum_{g}p^{\left(g\right)}=1
. The weighted transfer probability is then given by

P_{\mathrm{tr}}\,=\,\sum_{g}p^{\left(g\right)}P_{\mathrm{tr}}^{\left(g\right)}\,.

(19)

Note that due to the
R
-dependence of the eigenstates, this probability is still dependent on the internuclear separation of control and sample atom.
Therefore, the transfer probability of the whole sample

\mathcal{P}\,=\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho_{R}\left(R\right)P_{\mathrm{tr}}\left(R\right)\,\mathrm{d}R.

(20)

takes this fact into account by introducing the internuclear distance probability density
\rho_{R}
.
It depends on the trapping potential and the temperature of the sample. In the following, we assume a harmonic trapping potential and temperature of
50\,\mathrm{\mu K}
which is typical after a dark MOT loading into the tweezer potential. Further details on the sample properties can be found in the Appendix 3.

IV
Numerical simulations

The fidelity of the imaging protocol is determined by the difference between the transfer probability Eq. (
20
) with and without Rydberg excitation present in the ensemble.
In the following we will assess this Rydberg imaging fidelity in a realistic scenario and investigate its dependence on the systems parameters.

Choice of involved atomic states

The presented imaging scheme is very versatile and can be tailored to different needs.
Finding optimal parameters, on the other hand, is specific to the concrete application and depends on many aspects like the atomic species, the experimental implementation, the available laser systems, the number of atoms, the size of the sample, and the purpose of the experiment.

Here, we focus on mesoscopic ensembles of rubidium atoms in optical tweezers.
We consider the two hyperfine ground state manifolds of rubidium
\left\{\ket{\uparrow}\!\right\}=\ket{5\text{S}_{1/2};\,F=2,\,m_{F}}
and
\left\{\ket{\downarrow}\!\right\}=\ket{5\text{S}_{1/2};\,F=1,\,m_{F}}
.
As intermediate state, we chose the 6P state,
\left\{\ket{\mathrm{e}}\right\}=\ket{6\text{P}_{3/2};\,F,\,m_{f}}
state. Within this manifold, we near resonantly address the transition
\ket{5\text{S}_{1/2};\,F=2}\rightarrow\ket{6\text{P}_{3/2};F=2}
.
For a given Rydberg state
\ket{\mathrm{r}}
, which is to be detected, the auxiliary state
\ket{\mathrm{r}^{*}}
needs to be a different Rydberg state in order to prevent a fast map down and loss of the excitation due to the light coupling.
Furthermore, in order to ensure strong interactions across the whole sample, we make use of Förster resonances
Förster 1948
;
Walker and Saffman 2008
;
Wu et al. 2023
, substantially increasing
V_{\mathrm{RyRy}}
while maintaining large Rabi couplings.
Specifically, we choose the

\ket{39\text{S}_{1/2}}\otimes\ket{38\text{S}_{1/2}}\leftrightarrow\ket{38\text{P}_{3/2}}\otimes\ket{38\text{P}_{3/2}}

resonance with a Förster defect of only
\Delta_{\mathrm{F}}=2\pi\times 4.3\,\mathrm{MHz}
.
While the proposed imaging scheme generally works with any pair of Rydberg states, it obviously benefits from the strong enhancement of the interaction at the Förster resonance.
For a detailed discussion of the state and parameter choice see Appendix 4.

To sufficiently describe the Rydberg interaction, several other close by Rydberg states need to be includes, as due to their energetic proximity, they still have a non vanishing influence on the shifts of the Rydberg states of interest (see Appendix 2).

Experimental parameters

Besides the number of included basis states, there are many more parameters to be chosen.

While not being exhaustive by any means, we chose in the following experimental settings which are frequently encountered in experiments with optical tweezers and allow us at the same time to illustrate the basic dependencies of the imaging protocol.

q_{c}
and detuning
\delta_{c}

\Omega_{c}

\ket{6\text{P}_{3/2};\,F=2,\,m_{F}}

We consider only

The ones which form the continuation of the previously discussed states approaching

It is especially in the intermediate distance regime, where the effect of the coupling increases the effective detuning and thus changes the scattering rate.

The opposite scaling of the performance indicators
\mathcal{P}
and requires to find an optimal trade-off that depends on the application and the experimental setup at hand.
For larger atom numbers

To benchmark the ATI protocol, we calculate the detection infidelities at the parameters marked by triangles in Fig.
6
and vary the number of sample atoms
N_{\mathrm{at}}
and the duration of the probe pulse
t_{p}
.
To this end, we include the Poissonian distribution of both, the number of transferred atoms and the number of photons scattered by the probing light.
The Poisson distribution for the number of transferred atoms is characterized by a mean value depending on
\mathcal{P}
and
N_{\mathrm{at}}
.
For the subsequent detection, we assume a fluorescence imaging protocol with
\bar{n}_{\mathrm{ph}}=10
detected photons per atom.
This relatively small number of detected photons accounts for the loss of atoms in a dense sample due to light-assisted collisions
Fuhrmanek et al. 2012
during the fluorescence image and can be considered a conservative assumption to find a lower bound for the fidelities.
It is furthermore crucial to take into account the decay of the control excitation that can happen at random times within the duration of the probe pulse.
A detailed discussion of the modelling of the fidelity is found in Appendix 5.

The obtained scaling of the infidelities are shown in Fig.
7
for varied coupling strengths and atom numbers.
It reveals that the infidelity decreases for larger samples.
This is expected since the probing is a single particle effect and thus the absolute number of transferred atoms scales with the number of atoms in the ensemble, irrespective of the control atom being in the Rydberg state or not.
Analyzing the scaling with the coupling strength, we find that moderate couplings (which also maximize
\mathcal{P}
) are favorable as they show generally smaller infidelities,
The coupling must, however, be chosen large enough that the transfer ratio remains sufficiently large.
For an easily discriminable transfer ratio
\ratio>10
this is given for all parameters analyzed in Fig.
7
.

The fidelities can even be improved by using adequately chosen probe pulse durations as depicted in the lower panel of Fig.
7
.
In general longer probe times allow for more scattering events and thus more atoms to be transferred.
On the other hand longer probe times also make a decay of the Rydberg atom during the probe pulse more likely.
These contrary effects lead to the existence of an optimal probing time that depends on the coupling strength and the number of atoms in the ensemble.
In accordance with the previous results, the optimal probe time gets reduced for increasing
N_{\mathrm{at}}
, to the point where pulses of only a few
\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
are ideally suited to map the state of the control atom to the surrounding sample, underlining the potential of the protocol to very rapidly measure and store the state of a Rydberg atom.

In comparison to Rydberg imaging techniques based on the breakdown of the EIT condition
Xu et al. 2021
, the presented ATI scheme acts on similar timescales but surpasses the achievable fidelities already at relatively small sample sizes of only
N_{\mathrm{at}}>10
.
To obtain the same imaging fidelity the EIT-based protocol requires much larger atom numbers
N_{\mathrm{at}}\approx 400
and suffers from spectroscopically narrow EIT feature.

VI
Discussion

Exploiting the competition of Rydberg-Rydberg interaction and external Rabi coupling, our novell detection scheme for individual Rydberg excitations encodes the state of the control atom in the surrounding sample within only a few
\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
.
At this time the information is stored in a stable state and preserved for later readout.
In addition to the swiftness of the protocol, atoms only need to scatter a few to no photons, preventing losses af sample atoms due to radiative heating or molecule formation.
For the parameters discussed within this proposal, we show robustness against external fluctuations offsetting the probe laser detuning such as residual light shifts.
The detection fidelities are promising, well surpassing state of the art EIT methods at much smaller atom numbers, making it ideally suited for small mesoscopic ensembles.
Furthermore, no specialized detection equipment such as APDs with single photon sensitivity is required but the concept is readily implemented in systems with fluorescence imaging setups.
Our scheme is, however, not limited to fluorescence imaging in tweezer setups but can be combined with every state-resolved imaging of high density samples with a resolution on the length scale of
$1\text{\,}$-$2\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
.
This enables its application for example in quantum gas microscopes
Gross and Bakr 2021
where in an optical lattice with
\lambda_{\mathrm{lattice}}=$1064\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}$
25 atoms are located in a
$2.5\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$\times$2.5\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
region and can be image state selectively.

The method can even be extended to multi-species systems as the Rydberg-Rydberg interaction is not limited to within a single species.
Infidelities can be further decreased by optimizing experimentally easy accessible parameters such as the coupling strength
\Omega_{c}
.
Increasing the principle quantum number of the involved Rydberg states, we predict an increased interaction radius due to the increasing strength of the Rydberg-Rydberg interaction.
At this point, the control atom and sample could even be spatially separated to reduce their mutual influence when the coupling is not applied.
All aspects show the potential of the ATI scheme for state detection in modern day tweezer platforms for quantum simulation with small mesoscopic ensembles.

I.2
Transfer ratio

Taking this result, we have access to the detuning of the coupling laser

(25)

where

the probability to end up in the
\ket{\downarrow}

\ket{\mathrm{e}}

Weighting the transfer probabilities with the pair distance probability density (see Appendix

II
Full model

Supplementary to the discussion the main paper, this section aims to further elaborate the theoretical description of the full ATI model with its states, the Hamiltonian and the subsequent probing.
In the following we will use the pair index reference for the pair states introduced in Section 3 but without explicitly referencing the according subspace if not necessary:

\begin{split}\ket{\Psi_{ik}}&=\ket{\psi_{i}}_{\mathrm{C}}\otimes\,\ket{\psi_{k}}_{\mathrm{S}}\\
&=\ket{n_{i},l_{i},j_{i},m_{j,i}}_{\mathrm{C}}\otimes\ket{n_{k},l_{k},j_{k},m_{j,k},I_{k},m_{I,k}}_{\mathrm{S}}\\
&=\ket{\psi_{i},\psi_{k}}\,.\end{split}

(26)

II.1
Hamiltonian and diagonalization

Following the ordering already used in the main text, we start with the Rydberg-Rydberg interaction Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{RyRy}}
and only consider dipolar interactions.
In explicit form it is given by

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{RyRy}}=\sum_{\ket{\mathrm{r}_{i}},\ket{\mathrm{r}_{m}}}\sum_{\ket{\mathrm{r}_{k}^{*}},\ket{\mathrm{r}_{n}^{*}}}\ket{\mathrm{r}_{i},\mathrm{r}_{k}^{*}}\braket{\mathrm{r}_{i}|\mathbf{d}\kern-4.25003pt\hat{\phantom{\mathbf{d}}}_{\mathrm{C}}|\mathrm{r}_{m}}\braket{\mathrm{r}_{k}^{*}|\mathbf{d}\kern-4.25003pt\hat{\phantom{\mathbf{d}}}_{\mathrm{S}}|\mathrm{r}_{n}}\bra{\mathrm{r}_{m},\mathrm{r}_{n}^{*}}\,,

(27)

where
\mathbf{d}\kern-4.25003pt\hat{\phantom{\mathbf{d}}}_{\mathrm{C;S}}
is the dipole operator in the respective space.
As this interaction couples pair state components of both, control and sample atom, it is unique in the sense that it is the only part of the model that acts on both subspaces.
Additionally,
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{RyRy}}
acts only on pair states where both sub states are of Rydberg character, leaving every state with
\ket{\mathrm{e}}
character untouched.
For the extension of the subspace
\ket{\psi}_{\mathrm{C}}
we neglect the additional quantum numbers
I
and
m_{I}
of the corresponding
\ket{\psi}_{\mathrm{S}}
states as they are irrelevant for the Rydberg-Rydberg interaction.
Equally, the states of
\left\{\ket{\mathrm{r}}\right\}
are included in
\left\{\ket{\mathrm{r}^{*}}\right\}
and extended to the basis of
\ket{\psi}_{\mathrm{S}}
.

The second contribution to the Hamiltonian is the pairwise Rabi coupling of states in
\left\{\ket{\mathrm{e}}\right\}
and
\left\{\ket{\mathrm{r}^{*}}\right\}
by the coupling field.
It factorizes as only states in the sample atom subspace are affected and can thus be written as

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Rabi}}=\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{C}}\otimes\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Rabi,\mathrm{S}}}

(28)

with

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Rabi,\mathrm{S}}}=-\sum_{\ket{\mathrm{r}_{i}}}\sum_{\ket{\mathrm{r}_{k}^{*}}}\sum_{\ket{\mathrm{e}_{n}}}\ket{\mathrm{r}_{i},\mathrm{e}_{n}}\braket{\mathrm{e}_{n}|\mathbf{d}\kern-4.25003pt\hat{\phantom{\mathbf{d}}}\mathbf{\hat{E}}|\mathrm{r}_{k}^{*}}\bra{\mathrm{r}_{i},\mathrm{r}_{k}^{*}}+h.c.\,,

(29)

\mathbf{d}\kern-4.25003pt\hat{\phantom{\mathbf{d}}}
being the dipole operator and
\mathbf{\hat{E}}
the directional amplitude of the coupling field.
Pair states with pure Rydberg character are not subjected to this interaction, as are two pair states where the sample atom is in an
\left\{\ket{\mathrm{e}}\right\}
state in both cases.

Lastly, the hyperfine interaction for non-Rydberg states needs to be included in the sample subspace.
It is given by

\begin{split}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{HFS}}&=\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{C}}\otimes\hat{H}_{\mathrm{HFS,\mathrm{S}}}\\
&=\sum_{\ket{\mathrm{r}_{i}}}\sum_{\ket{\mathrm{e}_{k}},\ket{\mathrm{e}_{n}}}A_{\mathrm{HFS}}\ket{\mathrm{r}_{i},\mathrm{e}_{k}}\braket{\mathrm{e}_{k}|\mathbf{\hat{I}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{J}}|\mathrm{e}_{n}}\bra{\mathrm{r}_{i},\mathrm{e}_{n}}\,,\end{split}

(30)

where
A_{\mathrm{HFS}}
is the hyperfine structure constant of the
\left\{\ket{\mathrm{e}}\right\}
manifold.
As described in the main text, the ATI Hamiltonian is given by the sum of those three components and transferred to the frame rotating with the coupling field frequency
\omega_{c}
before the diagonalization.
A rotating wave approximation is applied, keeping only the low frequency components of the Hamiltonian.
Diagonalization of
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ATI}}
yields the eigenstates as superpositions of the base states

\ket{\tilde{\phi}_{\tilde{\varphi}}}=\sum_{i,k}{a}_{ik}^{\left(\tilde{\varphi}\right)}\ket{\psi_{i},\psi_{k}}

(31)

where the admixture of each base state to the new eigenstate is given by the prefactor
{a}_{ik}^{\left(\tilde{\varphi}\right)}=\braket{\tilde{\phi}_{\tilde{\varphi}}|\Psi_{i,k}}
fulfilling both

\sum_{i,k}\left|{a}_{ik}^{\left(\tilde{\varphi}\right)}\right|^{2}=\sum_{\tilde{\varphi}}\left|{a}_{ik}^{\left(\tilde{\varphi}\right)}\right|^{2}=1\,.

(32)

Using this tuple indexing of the base states, the admixtures for a given eigenstate
\ket{\tilde{\phi}}
can be written in matrix representation, where the base vectors
\ket{\psi_{i}}_{\mathrm{C}}
(
\ket{\psi_{k}}_{\mathrm{S}}
) form the rows (columns) of the
n_{\mathrm{c}}\times n_{\mathrm{S}}
matrix, depicted in Fig.
3
, whose size is given by the dimensionality of the two Hilbert subspaces
n_{\mathrm{C}}=\mathrm{dim}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{C}}\right),n_{\mathrm{S}}=\mathrm{dim}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\right)
.

(a)

(b)

II.2
Probing the system

In a first step, we aim to change the base representation of our system, since the ground states
\left\{\ket{\uparrow},\ket{\downarrow}\right\}
are low lying states for which a description in the hyperfine basis is preferable, the Hamiltonian on the other hand was formulated in a fine structure basis.
Due to the orthonormality of the two bases, the transformation is orthogonal.
This implies that the eigenenergies are preserved under a basis transformation and the eigenstates in the hyperfine base are simply given by the transformed eigenstates of the fine structure basis.
The transformation is given by the well-known Clebsch-Gordan decomposition of the fine structure states

\ket{j,m_{j},I,m_{I}}=\sum_{F,m_{F}}\ket{F,m_{F}}\braket{F,m_{F}|j,m_{j},I,m_{I}}\,.

(33)

Since we only probe the
\left\{\ket{\mathrm{e}}\right\}
state manifold, all basis states
\ket{\psi_{i}}_{\mathrm{C}}
are unaffected, making the transformation
T_{\mathrm{HFS}}^{\mathrm{FS}}
separable:

T_{\mathrm{HFS}}^{\mathrm{FS}}=\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{C}}\otimes T_{\mathrm{HFS,S}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\,,

(34)

with

\begin{split}T_{\mathrm{HFS,S}}^{\mathrm{FS}}=&\sum_{\ket{\mathrm{e}_{k}^{\mathrm{HFS}}}}\sum_{\ket{\mathrm{e}_{n}}}\ket{\mathrm{e}_{k}^{\mathrm{HFS}}}\braket{F_{k},m_{F,k}|j_{n},m_{j,n},I_{n},m_{I,n}}\bra{\mathrm{e}_{n}}\\
&\hskip 50.00008pt+\sum_{\ket{\mathrm{r}_{k}^{*}}}\sum_{\ket{\mathrm{r}_{n}^{*}}}\ket{\mathrm{r}_{k}^{*}}\bra{\mathrm{r}_{n}^{*}}\,.\end{split}

(35)

Making use of the coefficient matrix, changing the base is now a simple matrix multiplication

\left({A}^{\left(\varphi\right)}\right)^{T}=T_{\mathrm{HFS}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left({A}^{\left(\tilde{\varphi}\right)}\right)^{T}\,,

(36)

or using the orthogonality of the transformation matrix

{A}^{\left(\varphi\right)}=\left(T_{\mathrm{HFS}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left({A}^{\left(\tilde{\varphi}\right)}\right)^{T}\right)^{T}={A}^{\left(\tilde{\varphi}\right)}\left(T_{\mathrm{HFS}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\right)^{-1}\,,

(37)

where the admixture of the hyperfine structure base states to the transformed eigenstates
\ket{\phi_{\varphi}}
is again given by the coefficients of
{A}^{\left(\varphi\right)}
.

For the probing, we are only interested in the state of the sample atom and need to trace out the state of the control atom.
To this end, the reduced density matrix with respect to the sample atom subspace
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}

\begin{split}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{S}}^{\left(\varphi\right)}&=\mathrm{tr}_{\mathrm{C}}\left(\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{CS}}^{\left(\varphi\right)}\right)=\mathrm{tr}_{\mathrm{C}}\left(\ket{\phi_{\varphi}}\bra{\phi_{\varphi}}\right)\\
&=\sum_{i}\left(\bra{\mathrm{r}_{i}}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{S}}\right)\ket{\phi_{\varphi}}\bra{\phi_{\varphi}}\left(\ket{\mathrm{r}_{i}}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{S}}\right)\end{split}

(38)

can be calculated by multiplying the coefficient matrix with its transposed

\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{S}}^{\left(\varphi\right)}=\left({A}^{\left(\varphi\right)}\right)^{T}{A}^{\left(\varphi\right)}

(39)

and has the size
n_{\mathrm{S}}\times n_{\mathrm{S}}
.
Therefore we get
n_{\mathrm{S}}
eigenbasis vectors
\ket{\Theta_{\vartheta}}
when diagonalizing the reduced density matrix with the same amount of eigenvalues
\theta_{\vartheta}
.
The eigenvalues fulfill the normalization condition

\sum_{\vartheta}\left|\theta_{\vartheta}\right|^{2}=1\,,

(40)

since by definition
\mathrm{tr}_{\mathrm{S}}\left(\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{S}}^{2}\right)=1
and diagonalization is a trace preserving operation.
How to get to the transfer probability
\mathcal{P}
from this point is described in the main text.
For our study we assume the eigenenergies and admixtures to be constant for
R\leq R_{0}=0.2\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
and keep the values fixed to those calculated at
R=R_{0}
where the system starts to enter the regime at which the Rydberg-Rydberg interaction leads to rather complex spectra due to avoided crossings and the emergence the increasing effect of higher multipole interactions.

III
Internuclear distance probability density

As the Rydberg-Rydberg interaction is depends on the distance between the two involved particles also all quantities in our calculations carry this pair distance dependence.
In an ensemble the distance between randomly picked pairs of particles is determined by the internuclear distance probability density.
Thus, the sample transfer probability
\mathcal{P}
to be calculated has to take into account the internuclear distance probability density in the ensemble under study.
This density is influenced by external and experimental parameters such as the the trap geometry and the temperature
T
of the atoms.
Within this work, we assume the trap to be well approximated by a harmonic potential.
It can thus be described by the trap frequencies
\left(\omega_{x},\omega_{y},\omega_{z}\right)
in the three spatial directions, which, in an optical tweezer, depend on external parameters such as the wavelength
\lambda
and power
P_{t}
of the trapping laser as well as its waist
w_{0}
at the position of the atoms.
In this case, the atomic density distribution of a thermal ensemble is given by

n\left(R,T\right)=n_{0}\exp\left({-\left(\frac{x^{2}}{2\sigma_{x}^{2}}+\frac{y^{2}}{2\sigma_{y}^{2}}+\frac{z^{2}}{2\sigma_{z}^{2}}\right)}\right)\,,

(41)

where the width of the sample
\sigma_{i}
in the according direction given by

\sigma_{i}=\sqrt{\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{m\omega_{i}^{2}}}\,.

(42)

From this density, it can be shown that the internuclear distance probability density (short IDPD) can be analytically calculated to be
Tu and Fischbach 2002

\rho_{R}\left(R,T\right)=\frac{R^{2}}{2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}\sigma_{z}}\mathrm{exp}\left(-\frac{R^{2}}{4\left(\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}\sigma_{z}\right)^{2/3}}\right)\,.

(43)

Weighting the transfer probability
P_{\mathrm{tr}}\left(R\right)
by the IDPD, the transfer probability of all atoms in the sample is

\mathcal{P}=\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{\mathrm{tr}}\left(R\right)\rho_{R}\left(R,T\right)\mathrm{d}R\,.

(44)

IV
Numerical parameters

The ATI scheme presented in this study is very versatile and can be realized for a large variety of atomic species, internal states and external parameters.
Naturally, we have to restrict our numerical analysis of the model to a certain set of parameters.
Here, we motivate the particular choice of parameters we took.

IV.1
Atomic Species and States

Due to the large abundance of tweezer experiments working with
{}^{87}\mathrm{Rb}
, we settled for this element.
Within
\hphantom{{}^{\text{87}}_{\text{}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{87}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{87}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{87}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{87}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\text{Rb}
the implementation of
\left\{\ket{\uparrow}\right\}
and
\left\{\ket{\downarrow}\right\}
as the two hyperfine ground state manifolds
5S_{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{F}=1
and
5S_{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{F}=2
is straight forward since the information on the control excitation can be stored in a long-lived state and preserved for later detection.
The choice of the intermediate state, to which the probe transition couples, i.e.
\ket{\mathrm{e}}
, has a lot more variability.
We chose the
\left\{\ket{\mathrm{e}}\right\}=\ket{6\text{P}_{3/2};F,m_{f}}
manifold, as it can easily be addressed by a
\lambda_{p}=420\,$\mathrm{nm}$
laser in a well-established two-photon excitation scheme to the Rydberg state.
Within this set of states, we probe close to the
F=2
states which provides a decent dipole matrix elements from the
\ket{5\text{S}_{1/2};\,F=2,m_{F}}
states while still having a finite branching ratio
B=0.5
to the
\left\{\ket{\downarrow}\right\}
manifold.
By the nature of two-photon transitions from an
S
ground state, Rydberg
S
and
D
states are possible candidates for the choice of the
\ket{\mathrm{r}^{*}}
state.
As discussed in the main text, the two Rydberg states
\ket{\mathrm{r}}
and
\ket{\mathrm{r}^{*}}
have to differ, as otherwise the possible excitation of the control atom will be rapidly mapped down to the ground state by the coupling field.
Typically larger principle quantum numbers of the Rydberg states provide larger interaction strength.
At the same time, however, the optical coupling strength reduces due to the reducing dipole matrix element for the coupling from the intermediate state.
A favorable situation in this respect is created by low-lying Förster resonances, where the resonant energy transfer between (almost) degenerate Rydberg pairstates substantially increases the interaction strength
V_{\mathrm{RyRy}}
.
The use of Förster resonances is also possible in systems with differing atomic species of control and sample atoms, as they are not restricted to single atomic species
Beterov and Saffman 2015
;
Ireland et al. 2024
;
Otto et al. 2020
.
To demonstrate the capabilities of the imaging scheme at relatively low principle quantum numbers, where the influence of external fields is minimized, we decide to use the state pair
\ket{39\text{S}_{1/2}}\otimes\ket{38\text{S}_{1/2}}
which is close to the resonance

\ket{39\text{S}_{1/2}}\otimes\ket{38\text{S}_{1/2}}\leftrightarrow\ket{38\text{P}_{3/2}}\otimes\ket{38\text{P}_{3/2}}.

Using higher lying resonances such as

\ket{67S_{1/2}}\otimes\ket{69S_{1/2}}\leftrightarrow\ket{67P_{1/2}}\otimes\ket{68P_{3/2}}

with an even reduced Förster defect
\Delta_{\mathrm{F}}=2\pi\times 2.83\,$\mathrm{MHz}$
and increased
C_{3}
and
C_{6}
interaction coefficients, we assume even better contrasts.

IV.2
Coupling Transition

While the Rydberg-Rydberg interaction and the hyperfine interaction are fixed quantities for a given choice of atomic species and states, the parameters of the coupling field can be varied externally.
The coupling strength is described by the Rabi frequency
\Omega_{c}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\braket{\mathrm{r}^{*}|\mathbf{d}\kern-4.25003pt\hat{\phantom{\mathbf{d}}}\hat{\mathbf{E}}|\mathrm{e}}
which is set by the polarization
q_{c}
and the the electric field
\hat{\mathbf{E}}
of the coupling light.
For a fixed polarization and light field strength, the coupling is still not constant as a function of
R
, as the overlap of the different
\ket{6\text{P}_{3/2};F=2,m_{F}}
states with the two
\ket{38\text{S}_{1/2},m_{j}=\pm\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
states varies due to the changing
m_{j}
composition of the former for varying
m_{F}
.
In order to still quantify the coupling field by a single quantity, we choose the weakest of the non-zero couplings as a reference to label our parameter sets.

IV.3
Probing Transition

For the probing of the ATI system, the properties of the probe field as well as the properties of the sample influence the outcome.
In our analysis, we keep the sample parameters fixed and concentrate on the more easily variable parameters of the probe field.
The duration of the probe pulse is set to
15\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}$
which corresponds to half the lifetime
\tau_{\mathrm{Ryd}}^{\mathrm{bb}}=32.27\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}$
of the
\ket{39\text{S}_{1/2}}
state of the control atom in a black-body environment of
T_{\mathrm{env}}=300$\mathrm{K}$
.
To further restrict the number of varied parameters, we set the probe polarization to
q_{p}=+1
and do not vary the probing strength.
Additionally, we keep the duration
t_{p}
and strength
\Omega_{p}
fixed because their influence on
\mathcal{P}
is rather straight forward.
An increase/decrease in either of them increases/decreases the exponent in Eq. (22) (main text) and thereby the transfer probability.
Whilst a variation in
t_{p}
leads to a linear variation of the exponent, a change in the coupling strength leads to a variation approximately proportional to
\nicefrac{{\Omega_{p}^{2}}}{{\left(1+\Omega_{p}^{2}\right)}}
as it changes the Rabi coupling strength in Eq. (18) (main text) which shows the mentioned scaling in Eq. (17) (main text).
The detuning
\Delta_{p}
on the other hand has a non-trivial influence, as it leads to an more (in)efficient coupling to different eigenstates in the eigenspectra depicted in Fig. 5, hence why we focus on this variation in the frame of this proposal.

The probing strength
\Omega_{p}
needs to fulfill certain two criteria.
It needs to be small compared to the other interactions such as
V_{\mathrm{RyRy}},\Omega_{c}
and
V_{\mathrm{HFS}}
to be able to treat it as a perturbation, i.e. separately from the diagonalization of the system, and the probing transition needs to not be saturated, as otherwise Eq. (20) (main text) breaks down.
In the lights of these conditions, we choose
\Omega_{p}=2\pi\times 1\,$\mathrm{MHz}$
in order to stay quantitative, ensuring both conditions to be satisfied.

V
Fidelity

To calculate the detection fidelity of the ATI scheme, we numerically model the photon scattering of the probe transition on a the sample.
We aim to obtain histograms for the probability to detect a certain number of probe photons for the two different states of the control atom
By comparing these two histograms, we extract the fidelity for detecting the state of the control atom.
In the modelling, we include three random processes: i) the temporally exponentially decaying probability for the control atom to be in the Rydberg state ii) the Poissonian process of transferring an atom into the
\ket{\mathrm{g}}
state under probe light illumination and iii) the Poissonian process of photon scattering of imaging light on the atoms previously transferred into the
\ket{\mathrm{g}}
state.

To calculate the fidelities, we use the results presented in Fig. 6 from which we deduce an effective scattering rate

R^{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{\ln\left(1-\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{ml}}\right)}{t_{p}\,\ln\left(B\right)}

(45)

that averages over the different scattering channels in the multilevel system.

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{ml}}
denotes the quantity to be calculated in Section 11.
We determine this effective scattering rate for both cases, the excited control atom and the control atom in the ground state.
Using this effective rate, the transfer for different times
t_{0}
and decay times
t_{d}\leq t_{0}
of the control excitation is modeled similar to the method described in the main text

\mathcal{P}=1-\left(1-B\right)^{N_{\mathrm{ph}}}

(46)

but with the number of scattered photons
N_{\mathrm{ph}}
given by

N_{\mathrm{ph}}\left(t_{0},t_{d}\right)=\begin{cases}R^{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot t_{d}+R_{\mathrm{noRyd}}^{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\left(t_{0}-t_{d}\right),&\text{if }\ket{\psi}_{\mathrm{C}}\in\left\{\ket{\mathrm{r}^{*}}\right\}\\
R_{\mathrm{noRyd}}^{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot t_{0},&\text{else}\end{cases}\,,

(47)

where for each probe time
t_{0}
, we calculate the photon number for a whole range of incrementally changing decay times.
For
t_{0}<10\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}$
,
5\,$\mathrm{ns}$
intervals are used, for longer times we use
25\,$\mathrm{ns}$
steps.

V.1
State Transfer

Having accessed the transfer probabilities
\mathcal{P}\left(t_{0},t_{d}\right)
, the probability distribution of transferred atoms starting with a sample of
N_{\mathrm{at}}
atoms is described by a Poisson distribution

\mathrm{Pois}\left(n_{\mathrm{at}},N_{\mathrm{at}},t_{0},t_{d}\right)=\mathrm{Pois}\left(n_{\mathrm{at}},\mu=N_{\mathrm{at}}\cdot\mathcal{P}\left(t_{0},t_{d}\right)\right)\,,

(48)

where
n_{\mathrm{at}}
is the number of transferred atoms.
As a detection method, we use the statistics of a simple fluorescence imaging protocol for which we assume to detect on average
\bar{n}_{\mathrm{ph}}=10
photons per atom with a probability distribution also modeled by a Poisson distribution, i.e. the probability of detecting
n_{\mathrm{ph}}
photons is in the case of
n_{\mathrm{at}}
atoms is given by
\mathrm{Pois}(n_{\mathrm{ph}},\mu=n_{\mathrm{at}}\cdot\bar{n}_{\mathrm{ph}})
.
Combining the two distributions, we get the probability of detecting a given photon number
n_{\mathrm{ph}}
by

\mathcal{N}\left(n_{\mathrm{ph}},\bar{n}_{\mathrm{ph}},N_{\mathrm{at}},t_{0},t_{d}\right)=\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{at}}+\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{bg}}\,,

(49)

where

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{at}}=\sum_{n_{\mathrm{at}}\geq 1}\mathrm{Pois}&\left(n_{\mathrm{at}},\mu=N_{\mathrm{at}}\cdot\mathcal{P}\left(t_{0},t_{d}\right)\right)\\
&\cdot\mathrm{Pois}(n_{\mathrm{ph}},\mu=n_{\mathrm{at}}\cdot\bar{n}_{\mathrm{ph}})\end{split}

(50)

accounts for the situation where an atom is transferred and

\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{bg}}=\mathrm{Pois}\left(n_{\mathrm{ph}},\mu=5\right)

(51)

is used to model the background noise in the detection.

V.2
Control Atom Decay

If the control atom is in its ground state, we only get a single distribution
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{noRyd}}=\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{noRyd}}\left(n_{\mathrm{ph}},\bar{n}_{\mathrm{ph}},N_{\mathrm{at}},t_{0}\right)
, as the number of photons scattered in the duration of the probe pulse
N_{\mathrm{ph}}
is independent of the time of loss of the control excitation
t_{d}
since we use the second case in Eq. (
47
).
With initial excitation, the situation complicates, since the detection probability depends on
t_{d}
.
In this case, the different photon distributions are weighted by the probability to lose the excitation at a given time described by the deviation of the survival probability resulting in an exponential continuous random variable

\mathrm{Exp}\left(t,t_{0}\right)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\tau_{\mathrm{Ryd}}}\,e^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{Ryd}}},&t<t_{0}\\
P\left(t_{0}\right),&t=t_{0}\\
0,&t>t_{0}\end{cases}\,,

(52)

\tau_{\mathrm{Ryd}}
being the lifetime of the excitation and
P\left(t_{0}\right)
chosen such that
\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{Exp}\left(t,t_{0}\right)\mathrm{d}t=1
.
The photon histogram is therefore given by

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}=&\mathcal{N}\left(n_{\mathrm{ph}},\bar{n}_{\mathrm{ph}},N_{\mathrm{at}},t_{0}\right)\\
&=\int_{t_{d}}\mathrm{Exp}\left(t=t_{d}\right)\cdot\mathcal{N}\left(n_{\mathrm{ph}},\bar{n}_{\mathrm{ph}},N_{\mathrm{at}},t_{0},t_{d}\right)\mathrm{d}t_{d}\end{split}

(53)

The two photon number distributions now help us calculate different fidelities.
The easiest accessible is the fidelity of correctly identifying the state of the control atom independently of the state itself.
This fidelity is simply given by the overlap integral of the two distributions

\mathcal{F}\left(N_{\mathrm{at}},t_{0}\right)=\int_{n_{\mathrm{ph}}}\sqrt{\mathcal{N}\cdot\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{noRyd}}}\,\mathrm{d}n_{\mathrm{ph}}\,,

(54)

the square root normalizing the overlap as for probabilities
\int P^{2}\neq 1
for a normalized probability distribution
P
.
If one is interested in only correctly identifying one of the states, it is best to use a threshold, splitting the histogram into two parts.
To find the threshold, the function

f\left(n_{\mathrm{th}}\right)=\sum_{n_{\mathrm{ph}}\leq n_{\mathrm{th}}}\mathcal{N}+\sum_{n_{\mathrm{ph}}\geq n_{\mathrm{th}}}\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{noRyd}}

(55)

is minimized with respect to
n_{\mathrm{th}}
.
With this threshold, all probabilities to correctly/falsely detect the initially prepared state can be calculated by summing the corresponding photon distribution in the two intervals whose one boundary is given by
n_{\mathrm{th}}
.
In the main text, we take the average fidelity to correctly identify the state as a figure of merit, i.e.

\mathcal{F}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathrm{P}\left(\ket{\mathrm{r}},\ket{\mathrm{r}}\right)+\mathrm{P}\left(\ket{\mathrm{g}},\ket{\mathrm{g}}\right)\right]\,,

(56)

where
\mathrm{P}\left(x,y\right)
is the probability of preparing
x
and detecting
y
.
Exemplarily, the different values for a probe time of
t_{0}=15\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}$
, a Rabi coupling of
\Omega_{c}=2\pi\times 31\,$\mathrm{MHz}$
and three different atom numbers are given in Tab.
1
.
