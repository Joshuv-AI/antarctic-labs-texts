Consensus Recommendations for Patients with Complex Dispositions: A Modified Delphi Study Among the Strengthening Essential Emergency Departments (SEED)
Authors: Kathleen M Joseph, Jennifer Whitfield, Leah Warner, Alexandria Davis, Christian Arbelaez, Greg Buehler, Reed Caldwell, Jeffery Metzger, Carrie Shumway, Lisa Vogel, Benjamin von Schweinitz, Marissa Wolfe, Brian J Yun, Stephen J Wolf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSome emergency department (ED) patients have complex needs requiring hospitalization but lack acute conditions that meet inpatient admission criteria. Although common, these cases have received little national attention. We convened emergency medicine experts to propose definitions, barriers, and calls to action for this population.MethodsWe conducted a modified Delphi study using iterative online surveys and an inperson meeting. Panelists were recruited from Strengthening Essential EDs (SEED), a consortium of 15 United States (US) academic safety-net hospitals. Panelists were asked to rank agreement with survey items on a 5-point Likert scale, with elimination of items that failed to reach consensus (defined as <3.75) after each round. Between rounds, we integrated all panelist suggestions and distributed anonymous controlled feedback. The inperson discussion used a modified Liberating Structures framework to elicit feedback, which was used to edit items in real time.ResultsIn total, 35 of 67 (52.2%) panelists from 12 of 15 (80%) institutions attended the inperson discussion, and 22 panelists from 13 institutions participated in ≥1 Delphi survey. The panel reached consensus on a single term and definition—recommending “patients with complex dispositions” defined as “a patient who requires hospitalization due to complex disposition planning needs and identified barriers to safe discharge.” The panel also reached consensus on 5 key barriers and 6 calls to action for future work.ConclusionsWe recommend the term “patients with complex dispositions” to describe patients who require hospitalization due to identified barriers to safe discharge. This term, along with the proposed definition, identified barriers, and calls to action, should be considered and evaluated by a larger and more diverse stakeholder group in the future. We further encourage future work by multidisciplinary teams to evaluate and address this issue in the US health care system.Keywords: social determinants of health, modified Delphi, nonmedical admissions, geriatrics, emergency department operations

The Bottom Line

Although many patients present to emergency departments (EDs) with complex social needs, a lack of attention to this population in the United States has resulted in wide variation in definitions and care. Through a modified Delphi consensus process, a panel of academic, safety-net ED leaders propose redefining these cases as “patients with complex dispositions,” thus recognizing the social barriers that limit safe discharge. They also identified 5 current barriers to optimal care and 6 calls to action for future work. This consensus study represents a first step toward identifying and defining this patient population and the need for future work to improve care.

Introduction

Background

“Nonmedical admissions” and “social admissions” are nondiagnostic terms used to identify patients who are hospitalized without an acute medical condition identified as amenable to reversibility or rehabilitation.1 Rather, the patient’s social circumstances are felt to be the primary drivers for hospitalization. Many of these patients present to the emergency department (ED) lacking adequate social support or family members who can assist with their needs. These patients are often older, have neurocognitive impairments, are medically complex, live alone, and have a history of falls and rehospitalizations.2

Most scientific literature and commentary on “nonmedical admissions” comes from Canada and Europe. Unfortunately, this area has received little attention in the United States (US), likely due to wide variation in how institutions define and manage admission criteria for patients who present for “social reasons”. For example, some institutions place these patients in observation units awaiting placement at postacute care facilities. At other institutions, it is expected that patients board in the ED for extended periods before meeting criteria for an inpatient bed.

Importance

EDs and emergency clinicians are faced with a complex and seemingly impossible conundrum: to create an efficient system in which care can be provided to those with acute medical needs while simultaneously caring for a vulnerable patient population requiring nonacute care that has historically been understudied and undertreated.

Goals of This Investigation

We recognized the paucity of evidence, policy, and consensus regarding patients with nonacute medical complaints who present to the ED. Thus, as an initial step to address this issue, we assembled a group of experts from Strengthening Essential Emergency Departments (SEED), a consortium comprised of US safety-net academic EDs, to reach consensus on (1) definitions and terminology, (2) systemic and patient-level barriers, and (3) calls to action to improve the care of this patient population. We propose that this consensus is foundational for larger discussions that incorporate more varied viewpoints on this topic.

Methods

Study Design

We performed a modified Delphi study with structured, iterative online surveys and a 3 hour inperson meeting. The study was conducted between February and July 2025 and directed by a steering committee (SJW, KMJ, JW), all of whom are authors on this study and have collective expertise in Delphi and qualitative methods, social emergency medicine, and ED operations. This study is reported according to ACCORD (ACcurate COnsensus Reporting Document) guidelines.3 It was reviewed and approved as an exempt study by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board.

Panelists and Recruitment

Panelists were recruited from Strengthening Essential Emergency Departments (SEED), a consortium comprised of 15 academic safety-net hospitals with representatives from nursing, physician, and administrative leadership for participation in an annual conference. SEED has convened 3 times since 2023 to address priority issues affecting the nation’s EDs. A complete list of SEED hospital members is included in the Supplementary Material. Representative leaders were designated by their respective hospital, and included, but were not limited to, directors of nursing, nurse managers, departmental chairs, vice-chairs, medical directors, directors of finance, and directors of operations. SEED representatives were invited to participate in the online surveys and the inperson discussion. Patient and public feedback were not solicited.

Given competing administrative and clinical demands and the fact that the study was not incentivized, we anticipated low response rates to surveys and targeted a minimum of 11 respondents based on prior literature on modified Delphi methods, which recommends a sample size of 10 to 30 participants.4,5 For all modified Delphi methods, we followed best practices as outlined by Diamond et al., 2014.5 For online surveys (Supplementary Material), participants were recruited through electronic mail sent at least 3 weeks before closure of each survey. Participants received at least 2 reminder e-mails to complete the survey.

Round 0: Item generation (February to March 2025)

An initial online survey (Google Forms; Google LLC, Menlo Park, CA) invited panelists to suggest definitions, terminology, challenges and barriers, and calls to action for patients presenting for nonacute medical needs. Respondents were asked to state, via free-text boxes, how they defined “social” or “nonmedical” admissions, current processes at their institution, and the impact of nonacute medical visits at both patient and institutional levels. Responses were used by the steering committee to develop the first modified Delphi questionnaire.

Round 1: Modified Delphi (March to April 2025)

In Round 1 (R1), panelists were invited to complete an online survey rating their level of agreement on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly agree to 5 = strongly disagree) for proposed items created from Round 0 (R0). Panelists were invited to suggest changes to any of the proposed items and suggest new items through free text boxes. If participants responded to the survey more than once, only their first response was used for analysis. Mean agreement, standard deviation (SD), percent agreement (defined as percent of panelists that agreed or strongly agreed), and 95% confidence interval for each item were calculated using Excel Version 16.1 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA). Between rounds, all free text responses by panelists, including all proposed changes and new items, were incorporated into subsequent Delphi rounds verbatim or with minimal edits necessary for appropriate grammar and syntax. Any statements that did not meet the minimum requirement for consensus, defined as a mean score ≥3.75,5 were removed between rounds. We felt that the incorporation of panelist edits and suggested items was paramount and provided ample opportunity for deliberation—thus we chose to eliminate items that did not reach the consensus threshold after R1 to reduce ambiguity and complexity of items, while adding suggested panelist items to hone language and prompt discussion.

Round 2: Modified Delphi (May – June 2025)

Panelists received anonymized and summarized results of R1 in keeping with anonymous controlled feedback. In Round 2 (R2), panelists were again asked to rate their level of agreement on a 5-point Likert scale. The same consensus criteria and methods described in R1 were used.

Inperson discussion: June 10, 2025

An inperson discussion was hosted in Denver, Colorado as part of a 2-day SEED conference. Facilitators presented a summary of existing published literature and the results of R0 though R2. Items that reached consensus in R2 were shown on a screen and the wording of each was explicitly discussed and refined by the group in a stepwise manner. To encourage participation by all panelists, panelists were asked to first discuss each item in pairs, then in small groups, then as an assembly as a whole (a modification of the Liberating Structures 1-2-4-All method).6,7 The goal of this method is to allow all panelists to participate equally and to limit the effects of unequal power dynamics. Discussion was audio recorded (Zoom Communications Inc., San Jose, CA), but feedback was incorporated in real time. Approval to include any new or refined items in R3 was obtained using a show of hands requiring a simple majority.

Round 3: Modified Delphi (June - July 2025)

Round 3 (R3) was not preplanned but occurred because of the results of the inperson discussion in which a majority of panelists voted to consider and revise final items in an additional online survey. The same methods described for R1 and R2 were used. Following this round, the consensus findings were disseminated to SEED panelists for final approval, which was obtained through electronic mail correspondence.

Results

A total of 67 panelists from 15 institutions were invited to participate through either online surveys or in person discussion. Characteristics of participants for each Delphi round are described in Table 1 and eTable 1. Due to travel restrictions during the summer of 2025, 3 institutions were unable to send representatives to the inperson discussion. Additionally, individual scheduling conflicts precluded some designated representatives from attending. As a result, there were some representatives sent by their respective institutions to participate in the conference, and thus in the inperson discussion, who were not initially recruited for participation in the online survey (n = 8/67, 11.9%). This was not a preplanned recruitment strategy, but rather the result of working within the confines of an existing consortium and ongoing conference. In total, 35 (52.2%) panelists from 12 (80%) institutions attended the inperson discussion, whereas 22 (32.8%) panelists from 13 (86.7%) institutions participated in at least one of the Delphi surveys. Round 0: Item Generation

Eleven panelists (16.4%) responded to R0. The steering committee edited and consolidated free text responses from panelists into items. For example, one panelist responded to the question, “How do you define ‘social’ or ‘nonmedical’ admissions?” with the following: “requiring [an] overnight stay in the hospital as ED discharge [is] unsafe because of social concerns, such as lack of safe housing or chronic medical conditions prohibiting safe discharge.” The steering committee used this and other similar responses to craft one proposed definition: “Admission or prolonged ED stay due to barriers in ensuring timely access to outpatient resources necessary to ensure patient's wellbeing” and the associated term “PUTD (Patient unsafe to discharge).” A similar process was undertaken by the steering committee to generate as many items as necessary to incorporate all panelist responses.

Round 1: Modified Delphi

Eleven panelists (16.4%) responded to this survey. One of the 4 proposed definitions fell below the consensus threshold and was removed from subsequent rounds. Panelists recommended 4 changes and 2 additional definitions. All 4 proposed terms fell below the consensus threshold, but 5 new terms were proposed by panelists. In addition, 4 of the 9 proposed barriers fell below the consensus threshold; panelists proposed 2 additional barriers and suggested 3 changes. None of the 5 proposed calls to action fell below the consensus threshold, but panelists proposed 2 changes and 3 new calls to action. All panelist suggestions were incorporated iteratively between rounds. For example, when a panelist suggested, “social determinants of health as drivers of some of these admissions”, this was incorporated into definition items that met consensus criteria in R2.

Round 2: Modified Delphi

Eight panelists (11.9%) responded to this survey. Six of the 8 proposed definitions fell below the consensus threshold with 1 suggested change. Notably, the 2 definitions that met the consensus threshold were very similar, with only 1 word difference (“admission” versus “hospitalization”). Four of the 5 proposed terms fell below the consensus threshold, but 4 new terms were proposed. None of the 13 barriers fell below the consensus threshold, but there were 2 suggested changes and 3 new proposed barriers. None of the 9 calls to action fell below the consensus threshold, but 1 new call to action was proposed.

Inperson Discussion

Thirty-five panelists attended and participated in the 3-hour inperson discussion. During the discussion, the proposed items were edited and revised based on panelist feedback in real time. For example, in R2 the term “Patient Without Safe Discharge (PAWS)” had a mean score of 4.25 and 100% agreement by panelists. However, during the inperson discussion multiple groups voiced that although they had approved of “PAWS” in the R2 survey, upon further reflection and discussion, they no longer felt it was an appropriate term and could be belittling to patients. Instead, panelists voiced greater excitement for the newly proposed term “Patient with complex disposition” and a refined, but similar acronym, “Patient with Unsafe Discharge (PaUSD)”. Additionally, the panelists felt that the number of barriers and calls to action enumerated were too many. Therefore, these topic areas were consolidated and revised in real time, with each newly proposed item reaching greater than 50% approval by a show of hands. Although we anticipated reaching consensus during this discussion, due to the number of revisions proposed by panelists, the group agreed by majority vote to proceed to a third online Delphi round.

Round 3: Modified Delphi

Eleven participants (16.4%) responded to this survey. One of 2 definitions and one of the 2 terms met the consensus threshold. All 5 barriers and all 6 calls to action met the consensus threshold. The SEED panelists approved of the final consensus items described herein:

Limitations

Although we aimed to recruit a diverse group of experts through SEED, there is inherent selection bias in our decision to recruit panelists from a group of academic, safety-net hospital representatives. It is possible that our findings may have differed if experts practicing in nonacademic or forprofit institutions had been recruited. This, therefore, limits the generalizability of these findings. Furthermore, ideally consensus on this broad topic would involve additional stakeholders including patients, family members, case managers, social workers, community health workers, and others. Unfortunately, recruitment of these additional stakeholders was beyond the scope of this research. However, we anticipate that our findings can be used to germinate further discussion with a wider and more representative stakeholder group. Additionally, we did have low response rates to the online surveys (sample sizes ranged from 8 to 11), likely due to the lack of incentivization and competing professional demands. This low participation further limits the generalizability of these findings. Furthermore, the majority of panelists responding to online surveys were male and physicians; although, we believe that the robust inperson discussion with 35 attendees and use of Liberating Structures methods to encourage participation from all attendees help mitigate these limitations. Despite the use of a Liberating Structures framework, it is possible that power dynamics between and within groups contributed to unequal participation. Finally, ideally within Delphi methodology, the recruited panelists would be stable throughout the consensus process. Unfortunately, panelists sporadically responded to online surveys, resulting in an inconsistent panel across rounds. Additionally, because this consensus process was pursued within an existing consortium and conference, the inperson discussion introduced 8 new panelists who had not previously participated in the online surveys. This was an unplanned deviation from the recruitment protocol. However, we feel that these additional panelists still met the qualifications as experts, as they were selected by their respective institutions as representative leaders for the conference in lieu of the original representatives who were unable to attend.

Discussion

Despite the prevalence of patients with complex social needs but apparent low or absent medical acuity that require hospitalization, there is a lack of consensus on how to define and care for this population in a safe, compassionate, patient-centered, and cost-effective way. To our knowledge, this is the first consensus article on this topic. The SEED panel’s collective expertise provides valuable insight into this issue; safety-net EDs across the US are frequently tasked with navigating challenging situations involving these vulnerable patients. We anticipate that the proposed definition, terms, barriers, and calls to action agreed upon by this group can be used to generate future work with multidisciplinary teams and patients to determine appropriate terminology and advocate for reform.

Using a modified Delphi approach, the SEED group recommended using the term "patient with complex disposition" rather than the terms previously used in the scientific and nonscientific literature, including “social admit”, “acopia,” or “nonmedical admission”. We believe that this term better encapsulates the multifactorial circumstances driving ED visits and subsequent prolonged hospitalizations. Furthermore, this terminology omits the pejorative language of “social admits" or “acopia” and avoids the implication that these patients do not have comorbid or occult medical conditions contributing to their presentation. In fact, among United Kingdom patients triaged as a “social visit,” 30% ultimately were diagnosed with sepsis, and 6% were diagnosed with a new malignancy, suggesting that many of these patients do have underlying medical complaints.8 This shift in terminology is an essential first step in reframing policy and should be considered by larger stakeholder groups for evaluation and possible adoption.

The panelists also identified 5 primary barriers and challenges in managing this population within the health care system. These barriers are complex and span multiple levels of health care delivery and health policy. First, the lack of standardized legal and payor requirements, as well as the absence of institutional or national admission guidelines, create substantial variation in care and operational approaches. For example, within our own group of experts, some panelists reported that patients were required to board in their ED for over 72 hours prior to receiving a hospital bed, whereas other hospitals had robust multidisciplinary teams that coordinated clinical care and disposition for these patients. Prolonged ED boarding is particularly problematic in older adults, as they are at increased risk of developing delirium.9 Additionally, patients with prolonged ED boarding times have increased inhospital mortality, especially among patients who require assistance with activities of daily living.10

Additionally, hospitalizations that fail to meet payor criteria for medical necessity, but are, according to the clinical team, necessary for the safety of that patient result in inconsistent and nonuniform reimbursement for complex care coordination. Panelists believed that this results in deprioritization of these patients and subsequently hampers hospitals from providing high-quality care. Thus, by failing to offer appropriate compensation, payor systems actively discourage the care of those patients who are most isolated, vulnerable, and in need of resources.

The panelists also recognized that there is a dearth of alternative inpatient and community resources to care for these patients in lieu of hospitalization. The US faces a scarcity of postacute care and long-term care facilities due to an aging population, labor shortages, economic instability, and geographic lack of access.11 Moreover, the infrastructure to support aging in place is fragmented and underfunded.12 By the time many of these patients present to the hospital, they simply have nowhere else to go. Ultimately, the inability to address the complex needs of these patients in a comprehensive and coordinated way negatively impacts patient care and contributes to significant moral injury and burnout for the clinicians caring for them.

In light of these challenges, the SEED panel advocated for multidisciplinary, systems-level changes. Health care organizations, regulatory entities, and payors should collaborate to create standardized, transparent, and simplified policies addressing the care of patients with complex dispositions. Elimination of arbitrary time and other logistical requirements, such as length-of-stay thresholds, would enable more efficient and patient-centered care. Process and outcome measures specific to this population should be developed, evaluated, and revised to recognize the unique needs of these patients, thus removing the incentive to prematurely discharge or board these patients in the ED. Finally, there should be an incentivized, effective, and timely process to improve access to alternative dispositions, such as medical respite, skilled nursing, and long-term care facilities. Prioritizing access to alternative dispositions allows both social and medical needs to be addressed in a more cost-effective and patient-centered way.

These consensus findings emphasize that the stigmatizing language historically used to define patients with complex dispositions reflects the inadequate care these patients receive. Additionally, barriers to caring for these patients are multifactorial and are disseminated throughout our health care system, and as such, require innovative, multidisciplinary solutions. Future research should evaluate the proposed definition, term, barriers, and calls to action with a wider range of stakeholders, including patients, family members, case managers, social workers, and community health workers. Finally, future work should evaluate interventions to improve the ED care of patients with complex dispositions, including safety outcomes, cost-effectiveness, patient experience, and clinician wellness.

In conclusion, we recommend the term “patients with complex dispositions” to describe patients who require hospitalization due to identified barriers to safe discharge. This term, along with the proposed definition, identified barriers, and calls to action, should be considered and evaluated by a larger and more diverse stakeholder group in the future. We further encourage future work by multidisciplinary teams to evaluate and address this issue in the US health care system.
