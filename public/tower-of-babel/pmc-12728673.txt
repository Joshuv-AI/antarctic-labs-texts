Insulin‐Related Consumables Should not be Ignored While Pooling Insulin Purchases: Experience From China
Authors: Jingya Zhang, Haoran Li, Ning Zhang, Ying Mao, Bin Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA series of measures to implement national volume‐based procurement (NVBP) and follow‐on NVBP in China have significantly reduced insulin prices and increased patient affordability. However, NVBP may lead to a higher burden of insulin‐related consumables (such as injection pens and needles), which might discourage patients from using insulin in the pooled list and increase the risk of needle reuse. This article emphasizes that it is essential NVBP be implemented for both drugs and consumables, which will contribute to the achievement of universal insulin access.Keywords: China, insulin, insulin‐related consumables, procurement

Introduction

In many low‐ and middle‐income countries, insulin affordability remains a significant barrier to treatment [1]. China, as a major global pharmaceutical market, implemented an insulin‐specific national volume‐based procurement (NVBP) in November 2018, which was an important measure for improving insulin accessibility because of the high political commitment [2]. The NVBP resulted in a reduction in insulin prices. Therefore, in March 2024, the government implemented follow‐on NVBP for insulin. This article aims to analyze recent progress, potential dilemmas, and outlook for future prospects, with a view to informing other nations in their efforts to improve insulin accessibility and align with the World Health Organization's Global Action Plan for universal insulin access.

Recent Progress

The insulin‐specific NVBP in China, which began in 2021, has resulted in a remarkable 42.08% decrease in insulin prices, significantly improving affordability and accessibility [3]. Following this success, the government chose to continue with follow‐on procurement, further increasing the volume and variety of insulin procured, especially third‐generation insulin, because of the therapeutic advantages it affords.

The prices of the procured insulin continued to fall, and affordability further improved after follow‐on NVBP. As shown in Table 1, the median purchase price per defined daily dose for second‐generation insulin was $0.48, a 15.79% decrease in the price after NVBP. The median purchase price for third‐generation insulin was $0.72 per defined daily dose, indicating a price decrease (21.74%) greater than that in the case of second‐generation insulin. The estimated median affordability of second‐generation insulin increased from wages for 1.04 days to that for 0.87 days and that for third‐generation insulin increased from wages for 0.65 days to that for 0.59 days.

The price of third‐generation insulin in the NVBP decreased more than that of second‐generation insulin. After NVBP, the prices of second‐ and third‐generation insulin reduced by 40.63% and 44.58%, respectively. After follow‐on NVBP, the prices further reduced by 15.79% and 21.74%, respectively. This means that the price gap between third‐ and second‐generation insulin became smaller, contributing to the wider use of safer third‐generation products and facilitating the iterative upgrading of insulin regimens.

Potential Dilemmas

Despite the positive impact of NVBP on insulin prices, there is concern that the increased cost of insulin‐related consumables, such as injection pens and needles, could undermine patient adherence and increase the risk of needle reuse. In terms of practice, after the NVBP in 2021, Gan & Lee Pharmaceuticals and Tonghua Dongbao had the largest shares of third‐ and second‐generation insulin, respectively. As shown in Figure 1, the two companies' gross margins for insulin biologics in 2022 and 2023 significantly declined, down 32.62% and 4.35%, respectively, compared with those in 2020 and 2021, which also confirms the NVBP progress mentioned above. However, it is very noteworthy that the gross margins of insulin‐related consumables of the two companies showed significant improvements in the same period, with 25.75% and 17.41% improvements in 2023 compared with 2020, respectively.

We must realize that a drop in insulin prices does not fully mean increased accessibility to the medication. Insulin fills, pens, and needles are essential for standard insulin injection procedures. Before the NVBP, pharmaceutical companies often included free or discounted injection pens and needles with insulin purchases. However, after the NVBP, these promotional offers ceased, and injection pens and needles became additional patient costs that cannot be ignored.

Because the vast majority of insulin injection pens can only be paired with the same brand of insulin, when free injection pens are not provided with the purchased insulin [4], an additional financial burden is created for patients. Considering that injection pens and insulin are complementary commodities, there is even a risk that pharmaceutical companies may increase the price of injection pens to offset lower insulin profits. If the insulin used by patients before NVBP is not on the procurement list, the additional expense of insulin pens is likely to be one of the reasons that constrain them from using the purchased insulin.

Moreover, increasing needle costs may exacerbate needle reuse. Before the implementation of the NVBP in China, the situation of non‐standard injection in patients with diabetes was not optimistic, especially because of frequent needle reuse, and studies [5] have shown the high cost of pen needles to be the main reason. NVBP amplifies patients' perceptions of the financial burden of needles, especially in the case of some patients with mild diabetes who only need to inject a small amount of insulin per day. The cost of injectable needles is becoming increasingly impossible to ignore because this expense may even be close to the drug expense as insulin prices drop.

Globally, more than half of all patients reuse needles [6] and access to adequate and safe insulin delivery devices remains problematic [7], particularly in many low‐ and middle‐income countries, with costs being a key internal driver. These unregulated injection behaviors are quite dangerous and can lead to problems such as lipohypertrophy, pain, and infection [8]. Transport costs may prevent poor and sick individuals from accessing healthcare services [9], and the costs of insulin‐related consumables are likely to be key to bridging the last mile of insulin accessibility for low‐income sick individuals. Therefore, we must sound the alarm about the cost of insulin‐related consumables.

Future Prospects

To address these challenges, China must strengthen its NVBP for insulin and related consumables. The existing NVBP mechanism should be used to reduce insulin prices and expand procurement variety. Related consumables (insulin injection pens, needles, and blood sugar test strips) should be gradually included in the NVBP list. The inclusion of injection pens can enhance patient adherence to pooled insulin. The inclusion of needles could help reduce risky injection behaviors, such as needle reuse. Price reductions in blood glucose test strips could help increase blood glucose monitoring, which is the key to preventing and treating diabetes [10].

Such explorations have already been performed in some provincial‐level administrative regions in China. For example, Jiangxi Province, located in southeast China, conducted the first pooled procurement of medical consumables, including insulin pen syringes, pen needles, and blood glucose test strips, in January 2024. As a result, the average price of these consumables dropped by more than 50%; thus, the initiative is worthy of emulation elsewhere. Other countries with high insulin burdens could try to apply these experiences and establish pooled purchasing mechanisms to promote insulin supply security [11].

Conclusion

Nationally pooled procurement has the potential to reduce insulin prices and improve accessibility. However, to achieve long‐term success, it is imperative to consider the costs of insulin‐related consumables and integrate them into NVBP strategies. By doing so, we can ensure that the benefits of the NVBP extend beyond reductions in insulin prices to encompass the full spectrum of insulin treatment, thereby contributing to the global effort toward universal insulin access.
