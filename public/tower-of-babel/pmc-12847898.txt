Multi-omics dissection of SNP-mediated immunometabolic signatures in Alzheimer’s disease reveals a novel individual predictive model
Authors: Ji Wu, Xueyang Wang, Qing Tian, Chengliang Yin, Dandan Gao, Xiaoyang Ai, Xi Yang, Tingting Xiao, Yijia Gao, Fanggang He, Jianjuan Ke, Wenxin Yao, Xiaobo Feng, Dan He, Ling Yu, Jiewen Zhang, Ying Yu, Nanxiang Xiong, Lei-Lei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
While genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have implicated immune and metabolic pathways in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), their specific cellular impacts remain unclear. To address this, we employed bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization to identify single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-mediated, AD-associated immunometabolic signatures, which revealed both positively and negatively correlated immune cell types and metabolic pathways. Integrated single-cell omics analysis further delineated distinct astrocyte subpopulations in patient brains: one enriched for Glutamate-glutamine uptake and metabolism was positively associated with AD, while another characterized by Amino acid metabolism and transport was negatively associated. In peripheral blood, mononuclear cells (PBMCs) primarily displayed AD-negative metabolic signatures accompanied by downregulated immune responses. Leveraging these findings, we developed and optimized a blood transcriptome-based AD prediction model on a gene set derived from blood immune cells that is negatively associated with AD, using multiple machine learning approaches. This model is applicable to both European and Asian populations, enables pre-symptomatic detection for familial AD, effectively discriminates AD from other neurodegenerative disorders, and is readily accessible for clinical implementation. Our study provides novel evidence underscoring the critical role of immunometabolism in AD and delivers a practical predictive tool suitable for large-scale, routine population screening.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by gradual declines in memory, cognitive function, and the ability to perform daily activities. With the accelerating aging of the global population, the incidence of AD has risen steadily, making it a major public health concern worldwide involvement1–3. The lack of effective treatments exacerbates chronic psychological stress in aging populations. Despite decades of research, the fundamental cause of sporadic AD remains elusive. Prevailing hypotheses—including amyloid cascade, tau pathology, neuroinflammation, and microbial4–8, describe pathological mechanisms but fail to identify root causes or preclinical risk factors beyond aging. While familial Alzheimer’s disease (FAD) is predominantly caused by deterministic mutations (e.g., in APP, PSEN1/2), recent research has refocused on the polygenic architecture of sporadic AD, particularly through genome-wide association studies (GWAS)9,10.

GWAS have emerged as a cornerstone for deciphering the genetic architecture of complex diseases11,12. Large-scale initiatives such as the International HapMap Project have enabled million-variant GWAS, empowering methods like Mendelian randomization (MR) to strengthen causal inference in disease genetics. Elucidating the genetic determinants of AD susceptibility could provide mechanistic insights into disease etiology, facilitate predictive modeling with enhanced prospective validity, and improve clinical precision.

Recently, GWAS has successfully identified key AD-risk loci such as APOE ε4, TREM2, and CD33, which show significant enrichment in immune regulation and metabolic pathways9,10, underscoring the pivotal role of neuro-immune crosstalk in AD pathogenesis. Emerging evidence highlights immunometabolic reprogramming as a critical nexus linking genetic risk to AD pathology13. Metabolomic and single-cell multi-omics analyses reveal that the APOE ε4 allele exacerbates neuroinflammation by altering lipid metabolism in immune cells, while TREM2 mutations disrupt microglial energy homeostasis14,15. These findings establish immunometabolic dysregulation as a new paradigm in AD research. However, key questions remain unresolved: (1) How to systematically decode the regulatory networks connecting genetic variants, immunometabolic dysfunction, and neurodegeneration; (2) How to integrate cross-omics data to pinpoint disease-driving mechanisms and advance novel therapeutic and diagnostic/predictive strategies.

In this study, we leveraged GWAS data from AD, immune cell profiles, and plasma metabolites to identify genomic-level associations between immunometabolic dysregulation and AD pathogenesis. We identified that immunometabolic signatures derived from single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) encompass both positive/risk and negative/protective associations with AD. Through single-nucleus RNA sequencing we demonstrated that astrocyte subpopulations exhibit distinct metabolic signatures with bidirectional associations to AD, while peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) primarily displayed protective metabolic signatures, suggesting their potential as predictive biomarkers for AD. We developed a pre-diagnostic/predictive model based on blood transcriptome data, which, after evaluation and optimization, was implemented into a user-friendly web tool for individual-level risk prediction in clinical settings.

Results

Implication of SNP-mediated immunometabolic signatures in AD

This study aims to determine whether AD-associated SNPs directly induce immunometabolic alterations, thereby contributing to AD pathogenesis and progression. Employing three GWAS datasets, encompassing immune cell traits, plasma metabolites and AD risk, we performed bidirectional two-sample MR analysis across 731 immune phenotypes and 1,400 metabolites to identify SNP-mediated associations between immunometabolic traits and AD (Fig. 1 the gray panel). We then delineated the cellular distribution and functional effects of AD-associated immunometabolic signatures linked by SNPs through integrated single-cell profiling of both brain tissue and peripheral blood (Fig. 1 blue and orange panels). Prior to MR analysis, SNPs were pre-filtered (Supplementary SF1, Supplementary Table 3), resulting in a final set of 20,921,626 SNPs derived from a cohort of 39,106 AD patients and 46,828 normal controls used for association testing (Fig. 2a, e). For the MR sensitivity analysis, only SNPs meeting genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸, Supplementary Table 1) and demonstrating strong association with AD were retained to evaluate the influence of specific genetic variants on AD risk. Using the positive IVW method, the genetic signature of immune cells associated with AD are shown in Supplementary SF2 and Supplementary Tables 2 and 6. Volcano plot results revealed 46 immune cells were positively associated with AD (acted as AD-risk factors), while 28 immune cells were negatively associated with AD (acted as AD-protective factors) (Fig. 2b). Further classification analysis showed that AD-positive/risk factors were enriched for lymphoid cells (32/46, ~70%), largely in a mature functional state (with activated state, mature state and memory state, total: 26/46, 56.7%); conversely, AD-negative/protective factors were enriched with resting lymphoid cell (7/28, 25%) and activated myeloid cells (8/28, 28.6%) (Fig. 2c, d). This suggests that an activated lymphoid state is associated with increased AD risk while resting one is negatively associated with AD. For plasma metabolites, the IVW method identified 96 metabolites associated with AD (Fig. 2f, Supplementary SF3, Supplementary Tables 7 and 11). KEGG analysis revealed that 44 metabolites were positively associated with AD (acted as AD-risk factors), predominantly enriched in Astrocytic glutamate-glutamine uptake and metabolism (Risk P-I) and Neurotransmitter uptake and metabolism in glial cells (Risk P-II) (Fig. 2g). Conversely, 52 metabolites were negatively associated with AD (acted as AD-protective factors), enriched in Metabolism of amino acids and derivatives (Protective P-I), Amino acid metabolism (Protective P-II), Transport of inorganic cations/anions and amino acids/oligopeptides (Protective P-III), Arginine and proline metabolism (Protective P-IV) and Amino acid transport across the plasm membrane (Protective P-V) (Fig. 2h, i). Sensitivity analyses further confirmed the reliability of immune cells or metabolite associations, with details on instrumental variable strength (Supplementary Tables 3, 8), heterogeneity (Supplementary Tables 4, 9), and pleiotropy (Supplementary Tables 5, 10) provided in the supplementary materials. Meanwhile, Supplementary SF4 displays the Generalised summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (GSMR) results for the immune cell and metabolite associations shown in Fig. 2b, f. The analysis demonstrated that whereas both “CD11c on monocytes” and metabolite X-24757 show a positive causal relationship with AD, the immunophenotype “CD45 on CD33- HLA DR + “ and kynurenine levels are both significantly negatively correlated with it. This consistency with the MR estimates reinforces the robustness of these causal inferences. The dual role of astrocytic metabolic signatures associated with AD

We next sought to determine if SNPs mediated immunometabolic signatures influences the brain (Fig. 3a). Single-cell transcriptome analysis was performed on the prefrontal cortex of AD patients and normal controls (NCs). Following quality control, a total of 227,671 high-quality cells were retained for analysis (Fig. 3a, Supplementary SF5). Based on the expression of marker genes, we identified eight major cell types: excitatory neuron, inhibitory neuron, OPC (oligodendrocyte precursor cell), oligodendrocyte, astrocyte, microglia, fibroblast and endothelial cell (Supplementary SF5 and Fig. 3b, c). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the AD risk metabolic pathways (Risk P-I and Risk P-II) were predominantly expressed in astrocytes (Fig. 3d), while the AD protective pathways (Protective P-II, P-III and P-IV) were also located in astrocytes (Fig. 3e). Given that Risk P-I and Risk P-II are defined by the same gene set, we therefore consolidated them into a single pathway, labeled Risk P-I/II. These astrocytes were then classified into high- and low-metabolism groups based on the expression level of the metabolic pathways, and they were spatially segregated on the UMAP (Fig. 3f), indicating that AD-risk and protective factors are harbored by separate cellular groups within astrocytes. We then identified the differentially expressed genes between the high- and low-metabolism groups within the same metabolic pathway as shown in Fig. 3g. GO enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes in the for high-metabolism astrocyte subpopulations revealed a notable overlap in the upregulated biological processes (Fig. 3h). A plausible explanation for this overlap is that while the risk-related pathways specifically involve the uptake and metabolism of glutamate, the protective pathways are associated with broader amino acid metabolism, including the metabolism and transport of arginine and proline. These results suggest that the metabolic state of astrocytes is critically involved in AD pathogenesis, functioning as a key regulatory switch. PBMCs exhibit AD-protective signatures with attenuated immune responses

To investigate whether immune cells exhibit SNP-mediated metabolic features associated with AD, we analyzed single-cell data derived from blood samples of AD patients and NCs. Following quality control, a total of 256,206 cells were retained (Fig. 4a and Supplementary SF6). Major cell populations including T cell, NK cell, B cell, monocyte, plasma cell and platelet were identified based on the expression level of marker genes, with their relative abundances were quantified (Fig. 4b–d and Supplementary SF6). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the previously identified AD risk metabolic pathways (Risk P-Ⅰ and Risk P-Ⅱ) were predominantly expressed in platelets (Fig. 4e). However, due to the low abundance of platelets (0.1%) in the population, further investigation of SNP-mediated AD-risk metabolic pathways was not pursued at the blood level. In contrast, the AD-protective pathway (Protective P-Ⅰ) showed elevated expression in T cel, NK cells, B cell and monocyte (Fig. 4f), suggesting that the metabolic state of these four major immune cell types is negatively correlated with AD pathogenesis. Their functional status may thus represent a protective factor against the disease. Subsequently, we stratified the immune cells into high- and low-metabolism groups according to the average expression level of the Protective P-Ⅰ pathway (Fig. 4g), and get four sets of differentially expressed genes as shown in Fig. 4h. GO enrichment analysis showed that in the high-metabolism group, the up-regulated genes were primarily involved in ribosomal assembly, cytoplasmic translation and peptide metabolic processes, consistent with the biological functions linked to the Protective P-Ⅰ pathway. Surprisingly, within these cell populations predominantly carrying AD protective metabolic pathway, the down-regulated genes were all associated with their respective immune response (Fig. 4i). This data suggests that the immune cells exhibiting a protective state, and either their immune responses are attenuated or they are remaining inactive. Collectively, these results indicate that the SNP-mediated metabolic features observed in PBMCs are negatively correlated with AD. SNP-mediated immunometabolic signatures in PBMC contribute to AD prediction models

Given that these SNP-mediated, AD-negative associated metabolic features are enriched in the vast majority of PBMCs, we proposed that PBMCs can form a disease prediction model characterized by protective factors. To mitigate the influence of ethnic background on the predictive model, we integrated and harmonized blood transcriptome data from both European (GSE63060 and GSE63061) and Asian populations (GSE239454 and Chinese Self) (Supplementary SF8 and Fig. 5c). The Chinese Self dataset, generated in this study, includes 18F-FDG PET/CT results from AD patients (Supplementary SF7). Importantly, no statistically significant age difference was observed between the AD and NC groups in both Chinese cohort (Supplementary SF7). The combined transcriptome data were then randomly split into a Train set (70%) and Test sets (30%) as illustrated in Fig. 5c. Then 113 machine learning algorithms were applied to identify the optimal prediction conditions using the Training set and three Test sets for further validation (Fig. 5c). The “glmBoost+RF” model, based on the P-PI-Blood B signature demonstrated optimal performance, with the area under the curve (AUC) of the Train set achieving 1.000 and strong predictive accuracy in the Test sets (AUC > 0.833) (Fig. 5d, e). Further evaluation using decision curve analysis (DCA) and calibration plots indicated the model’s strong clinical utility and predictive power (Fig. 5f, g). This model incorporates six core genes: HELZ, KLF6, RPL36AL, RPS27A, CHD9 and LCOR, all of which showed differential expression in blood transcriptomes between AD patients and NCs (Fig. 5h). Furthermore, analysis of brain tissue samples from public databases revealed that CHD9, KLF6 and LCOR were also upregulated in AD brains (Supplementary SF10), indicating partial consistency with that observed in blood. Subsequent Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis elucidated expression of KLF6 and CHD9 as key bidirectional switches: high expression levels promoted positive predictions, whereas low expression drove negative outcomes (Fig. 5i). Furthermore, the force plot delineated feature contributions for an individual sample, revealing CHD9 (expression = 8.2, SHAP value = −0.141) as the strongest negative influencer, followed by KLF6 (expression = 9.9, SHAP value = −0.105) as the second strongest, consistent with their overall mechanistic roles (Fig. 5j). In subsequent experiments, we will utilize the expression levels of these six model genes in blood transcriptomes to evaluate model specificity (6-gene model) and to further refine the predictive framework. Together, our blood transcriptome-based AD model demonstrated robust, clinically actionable prediction accuracy, combining strong validity in both European and Asian populations.

Validation of early detection in a familial AD mouse model

Although our AD prediction model is based on SNP-mediated immunometabolic signatures derived from sporadic AD patients, it may also be applicable to familial AD. Then we tested the prediction (6-gene model) with a cohort of 9-month-old WT and 5 × FAD mice. Prior to blood collection, water maze experiments were performed to assess learning and memory, following protocol from16 (Fig. 6a, b). The 9-month-old 5 × FAD mice displayed significantly longer escape latencies, fewer target crossings, and slightly reduced time spent in the target zone, although they moved faster, indicating impaired learning and memory (Fig. 6b, c). Immunofluorescence staining revealed substantial amyloid deposition in the 9-month-old 5 × FAD mice brains (Fig. 6d). Thus, we test their transcriptome in the predict model and get the AUC = 0.870 and much higher than the one by each single gene (Fig. 6e, f). Then we want to know if this model could predict familial AD at a much earlier stage. We also tested it in 2.5-month-old 5 × FAD mice, who showed minimal memory impairment and limited amyloid deposition (Fig. 6g–i). Unexpectedly, the prediction model still performed well with AUC = 0.812 (Fig. 6k, l). Specificity and discriminative power of the AD prediction model

Since certain neurodegenerative disorders, such as Parkinson’s disease (PD) and frontotemporal dementia (FTD), sharing overlapping clinical symptom with AD, we evaluated the specificity of our model by testing its ability to distinguish AD from these related conditions. We download the transcriptome data from peripheral blood samples of patients with PD or FTD, and test them in the 6-gene model after standardized preprocessing (Supplementary SF11). ROC analysis yielded an AUC of 0.849 (95% CI: 0.812–0.884) in AD patients (Fig. 7a), compared to 0.584 (95% CI: 0.521–0.645) in PD (Fig. 7b) and 0.577 (95% CI: 0.539–0.617) in FTD patients (Fig. 7c), with no overlap in confidence intervals between AD and either neurodegenerative disorder. Additionally, we also investigated whether metabolic abnormalities, such as obesity (OB, BMI > 30), could interfere with the model’s predictive performance. When applied to the blood transcriptome of a standardized obesity cohort (BMI > 30) (Supplementary SF11), the model achieved an AUC of 0.603 (95% CI: 0.542–0.666) (Fig. 7d), with no overlap in confidence intervals compared to the AD cohort. Clinical translation and web tool development for individual prediction

To further optimize the model and enhance its clinical applicability, we incorporated the six identified model genes along with age and gender as independent predictors to construct a nomogram (Fig. 8a, b). The nomogram enables users to estimate AD risk by assigning points for each predictor and projecting the total score onto a risk probability axis (Fig. 8a, b). The AUC of this nomogram is 0.851 (Fig. 8c), slightly increased than only performed with the six genes (Fig. 7a). Here, the expression of RPL36AL still demonstrated the strongest individual predictive performance (AUC = 0.800) (Fig. 8c). Both decision curve analysis and calibration curves confirmed the high reliability of the model (Fig. 8d). We then selected predictors with significant contributions: age and expression levels of CHD9, KLF6, and RPL36AL to reconstruct a refined model (Fig. 8e, f). The optimized model achieved an AUC of 0.848 (Fig. 8g), which was highly comparable to the original model. Its high reliability was further confirmed by both decision curve analysis and calibration curves (Fig. 8h). An online tool for AD risk prediction has been finally deployed at [https://jw-ad.shinyapps.io/JW_AD_DynNomapp/], allowing users to evaluate AD risk by inputting gene expression levels. Discussion

GWAS combined with Mendelian randomization provides powerful insights into genetic disease susceptibility11,12, yet challenges remain, such as limited tissue-specific resolution. Integrating single-cell sequencing with hybrid statistical models offers a promising solution. Here, by merging GWAS, scRNA-seq, and transcriptomics in AD, we linked AD-associated SNPs to immune and metabolic dysregulation at cellular levels. Our findings provide new evidence supporting the critical role of astrocytes in AD pathogenesis, while also revealing a negative correlation between the metabolic state of peripheral immune cells and the disease.

Astrocytes are crucial for maintaining brain homeostasis. Under pathological conditions, they become activated and proliferate, a state in which they may contribute to neurodegeneration through either a loss of normal functions or a gain of toxic functions17,18. A recent single-nucleus RNA sequencing study of 628,943 astrocytes from 32 donors delineated their roles in AD progression and classified them into five major subtypes19. The proportions of homeostatic, intermediate, and reactive astrocytes varied primarily along a spatial axis throughout AD pathogenesis. In contrast, a subtype enriched for trophic factors decreased gradually during pathology, while another subtype characterized by glucose metabolism, metallothionein, and extracellular matrix-related gene expression increased progressively, peaking in late-stage AD19.

Two additional studies, covering 48 individuals and 437 older adults respectively, also found that specific astrocyte subpopulations are associated with how tau pathology contributes to cognitive decline in AD20,21. Those all fully illustrate the complex and dynamic involvement of astrocytes during the development of AD. Our study distinguished astrocyte subpopulations based on their metabolic pathway activities correlated with AD, identifying both protective and risk-associated groups. Thereby, it provides novel evidence highlighting the complex, multifaceted role of astrocytes in AD pathogenesis.

While Mendelian randomization established causal links between immune cells and AD and indicated that activated lymphocytes predominantly contributed to positive associations with AD, it still lacked cellular specificity (Fig. 2c, d). These findings highlight the need for experimental studies to directly dissect the functional roles of these AD-associated immune cell subtypes (Supplementary SF2). From another perspective, analysis of the AD-negative/protective signature (mainly involved amino acid metabolism and transport) revealed an enrichment of these pathways across all major PBMC cell types. Amino acids serve as vital nutrients for immune cells, supporting organ development, tissue homeostasis, and immune responses22. Notably, branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), glutamine, tryptophan, and methionine modulate T cell proliferation, differentiation, and anti-tumor immunity22. Similarly, B cell relies on leucine, glutamine, glutamate, and GABA for proliferation, differentiation, and anti-tumor function, largely via mTOR signaling22. Within this framework, our findings indicate that the AD-negative correlation in immune cells is indicative of a healthy state. Interestingly, they also exhibited downregulation in immune response, further pointing to a potential mechanism where an AD-protective state coincides with reduced immune reactivity. Thus, future experiments need to address whether a causal relationship exists between this low-activation immune phenotype and AD progression.

Blood transcriptome analysis has emerged as a critical tool for disease prediction due to its non-invasive sampling and dynamic monitoring capabilities23,24, with clinical applications for various diseases23,25,26. A key limitation of blood transcriptome-based neurodegenerative disease models27 is their capture of established disease states rather than preclinical susceptibility, suggesting they reflect consequences with limited early intervention utility. However, our predictive model is remarkable not only to predict sporadic AD patients but also for familial AD mice, even prior symptoms or pathological progression, demonstrating the truly prospective ability. Besides, it also indicates that familial and sporadic AD exhibit a convergent effect, underscoring the multifactorial and complex nature of the disease’s etiology. Also to be note, current other prediction models are predominantly developed using population-level data, failing to fully capture individual variations28. Our online nomogram overcomes these limitations by enabling precise individual-level risk assessment, enabling precise individual-level risk assessment. Nonetheless, our model could discriminate AD from related disorders with stability against transient health fluctuations, thus significantly broaden the potential applications. Its low cost and non-invasive nature make it suitable as a universal screening tool for healthcare providers, even as part of routine check-ups that allow for repeated testing.

This study has several limitations. First, the GWAS data were derived exclusively from European populations, which may overlook ancestry-specific genetic factors relevant to other groups. Second, the feature genes used in our predictive model may vary with sample size and population heterogeneity, highlighting the need for larger and more diverse datasets. Third, while the model performs well in familial AD mice, its applicability to human familial AD remains unvalidated. Fourth, longitudinal studies are required to verify whether asymptomatic high-risk individuals identified by the model eventually develop AD. Fifth, while our analysis supports a causal role of SNP-mediated immunometabolic signatures with AD, the direct biological pathways linking these signatures—or peripheral gene expression features—to brain pathology remain unestablished. Limited by data availability, we were unable to incorporate several recently published, larger-scale human single-cell AD cohorts20,21 into our validation. A promising future direction lies in integrating these datasets to clarify how SNP-mediated immunometabolic traits interact with astrocyte states in AD pathogenesis. Finally, our modeling and optimization consistently identified age as the predominant risk factor in AD. Yet, this study does not explain how aging modulates the SNP-mediated immunometabolic profile—a congenital set of traits that may interact with the aging process to trigger late-onset disease. In subsequent work, we aim to integrate dynamic cellular trajectories of brain aging and dementia21 to experimentally validate this interplay.

In summary, we identified both risk and protective SNPs for Alzheimer’s disease through Mendelian randomization analysis of GWAS data encompassing AD, immune cell profiles, and plasma metabolites. Integrated single-cell sequencing revealed distinct immunometabolic signatures, with specific astrocyte subpopulations in the brain showing either positive or negative associations with AD, while peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) primarily exhibited protective metabolic profiles. Leveraging these findings, we developed a blood-based AD prediction model using machine learning that demonstrates multiple advantages: validated applicability in European and Asian populations, pre-symptomatic detection capability for familial AD, specificity unaffected by metabolic diseases, and clinical accessibility for individualized assessment. Our study not only provides novel evidence supporting the crucial role of immunometabolism in AD but also delivers a practical predictive tool suitable for large-scale population screening.

Methods

Genome-wide association study data acquisition

GWAS data for AD patients were obtained from the MRC-IEU GWAS summary datasets repository (URL: https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/), provided by Bellenguez et al. 29. This dataset comprises 39,106 clinically or pathologically confirmed AD cases and 46,828 normal controls (NCs), with a total of 20,921,626 SNPs included. It represents a large-scale, high-statistical-power AD GWAS to date. To enrich the genetic landscape, additional summary statistics from 731 immune cell-related GWAS (IDs GCST90001391 to GCST90002121) were sourced from the GWAS Catalog30. Furthermore, plasma metabolite GWAS data, comprising 8299 individuals with 1,091 metabolites and 309 metabolite ratios, were downloaded from a peer-reviewed publication11. All datasets are from European populations, ensuring consistency in the analyses.

Mendelian randomization analysis

SNPs serving as instrumental variables (IVs) were selected based on established genetic associations reported in prior MR studies investigating immune cell phenotypes31,32. Only SNPs meeting genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−8) and demonstrating robust association with exposure variables were included. The causal relationship between immune cell phenotypes and AD outcomes was estimated using the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method under a fixed-effects model. This approach provides an unbiased causal estimate when all IVs satisfy the MR assumptions (relevance, independence, and exclusion restriction). To validate the robustness of causal inferences, the following sensitivity analyses were conducted: 1) Heterogeneity Assessment: Cochran’s Q statistic was computed to evaluate between-SNP heterogeneity. A non-significant Q-value (P > 0.05) indicated absence of substantial heterogeneity. 2) Horizontal Pleiotropy Evaluation: The MR-Egger regression method was applied to test for directional pleiotropy. The intercept term’s significance (P > 0.05) confirmed no evidence of unbalanced pleiotropic effects.

Generalised summary-data-based Mendelian randomization

To investigate the causal effects of immune cells and plasma metabolites on AD, we performed Generalised summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (GSMR) using the dedicated R package. The analysis applied a multi-SNP model to GWAS summary statistics to test for causality between phenotypes33, with significance defined as P < 0.05.

Transcriptomic data acquisition

Multiple transcriptomic data from AD patients were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (URL: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/info/datasets.html). The datasets were listed as the below. 1) Blood transcriptome data from AD patients and NCs: GSE63060 (European: 104 NCs and 145 AD patients), GSE63061 (European:135 NCs and 139 AD patients), GSE239454 (Chinese: 15 NCs and 11 AD patients). 2) Human blood single-cell data: GSE226602 (22 NCs and 28 AD patients). 3) Human prefrontal cortex single-cell data: GSE157827 (9 NCs and 12 AD patients) and GSE167494 (5 NCs and 10 AD patients). 4) Transcriptome data of human brain tissues: GSE138260 (19 NCs and 17 AD patients), GSE29378 (32 NCs and 31 AD patients), GSE37263 (8 NCs and 8 AD patients) and GSE5281 (74 NCs and 87 AD patients). 5) Blood transcriptome data from human patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), Obesity (OB, BMI > 30) and the countered NC: GSE6613 (55 NCs and 50 PD patients), GSE57475 (49 NCs and 93 PD patients), GSE72267 (19 NCs and 40 PD patients), GSE99039 (89 NCs and 132 PD patients), GSE140829 (249 NCs and 204 FTD patients), GSE140830 (139 NCs and 139 FTD patients), GSE109597 (60 NCs and 24 OBs), GSE110551 (78 NCs and 78 OB) and GSE181339 (24 NCs and 48 OB). All datasets were subjected to standardized preprocessing prior to analysis.

Metabolite enrichment analysis (MSEA) using MetaboAnalyst 6.0

MSEA was performed using the MetaboAnalyst 6.0 platform, utilizing the Over-representation Analysis (ORA) module to identify significantly associated metabolite sets. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Small Molecule Pathway Database (SMPDB) were used for pathway-based enrichment34.

Peripheral blood collection and transcriptome sequencing (Chinese Self)

AD patients were recruited from Renmin Hospital, Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University, and Henan Provincial People’s Hospital, while NCs were recruited from the community and Henan Provincial People’s Hospital. All NCs are supposed normal control without diagnosis of AD in the hospital. All AD patients met the NINCDS-ADRDA diagnostic criteria35. Exclusion criteria included claustrophobia, inability to undergo neurocognitive assessments, or other dementia types. Clinical assessments, including MRI and cognitive function tests (Montreal Cognitive Assessment [MOCA] and Mini-Mental State Examination [MMSE]), were conducted. MRI typically revealed bilateral hippocampal and cerebral atrophy, while 18 F FDG-PET/CT scans showed reduced glucose metabolism in several brain regions (Supplementary SF7). A total of 30 NCs and 44 PET-confirmed AD patients were included. Blood was collected following a 12-hour fast, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) isolated via Ficoll (Solarbio, China) separation, and samples processed for immune cell transcriptome sequencing (BGI, Beijing Genomics Institution, China). Data were homogenized to remove batch effects and this data set were named: Chinese Self (Supplementary SF8) to be used in machine learning.

Ethics approval and consent statement

All blood samples were collected from participants, including community-based healthy controls and Alzheimer’s disease patients, with written informed consent obtained from the participants or their legal guardians. The study protocol was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuhan University (Approval No.: WHU-LFMD-IRB2024011) and the Ethics Committee of Zhengzhou University People’s Hospital (No. HNEECKY-2024-217). To ensure participant confidentiality, all data were anonymized; no personally identifiable information (e.g., names or identifiers) will be disclosed in publications—only coded identifiers will be used.

Single-cell sequencing analysis

Expression profiles were processed using the Seurat package, with steps including gene exclusion, normalization, scaling, Principal Components Analysis (PCA), and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). The optimal number of Principal Components (PCs) was determined using an Elbow Plot. UMAP analysis provided insights into spatial relationships between clusters. Cluster annotation focused on cell populations implicated in AD pathogenesis, and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) examined metabolite pathway expression patterns. Specific cell populations were classified into high- and low-metabolism groups, and expression differences were analyzed.

Machine learning for constructing an AD prediction model and validation

The GSE239454 dataset and the Chinese Self were first randomly split at a 7:3 ratio, with the 70% portion synthesized to form the Chinese Train set cohort and the 30% portion serving as the Chinese Test set cohort. Meanwhile, the transcriptomic datasets of European (GSE63060 and GSE63061) were also randomly divided into Train and Test sets at a 7:3 ratio to combined with the Chinese cohort to generate to final Train set (Supplementary SF8). The training set was then normalized using the Biobase and GEOquery packages in R. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified in single-cell subpopulations (high-metabolism vs. low-metabolism) were cross-validated in the generated bulk cohort, while differential expression analysis of blood transcriptomes between AD patients and NCs was performed using “limma”. We then retained the overlapping DEGs from the two above as input features (Fig. 5a, b) to perform machine learning. A total of 113 machine learning algorithms, including Random Forest, support vector machines, logistic regression, and XGBoost, were employed to develop the prediction model. Each algorithm utilized nested cross-validation and hyperparameter tuning to prevent overfitting. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, with predictive accuracy quantified by the area under the curve (AUC). Ultimately, a heatmap of ROC values for all algorithms was generated, from which the model with the highest average ROC value was selected as the optimal model. Clinical utility was assessed via DCA and calibration plots, and a nomogram was developed to visually represent prediction outcomes. To further enhance the interpretability of the machine learning models, we applied Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP), a game-theoretic approach that quantifies the contribution of each feature to individual predictions. SHAP values were calculated using the “shap” package in Python, which provides both global and local explanations.

Adaptation of the model to familial AD animal models

5 × FAD mice and age-matched wild type (WT) mice were housed under conditions of free access to water and food, maintained at a constant temperature of 24 °C, relative humidity of approximately 60%, and a 12-hour light/12-h dark cycle in Shulaibao Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Morris Water Maze behavioral assays were conducted to assess learning and memory in 9- and 2.5-month-old mice, following the instructions reported by Zheng et al. 16. Post six days of training, performance of these mice was evaluated by removing the hidden platform and placing the mice in the water from the opposite quadrant. Their swimming behavior was monitored for 60 s, to record the first arrival time to the target area, the number of platform crossings, and the swimming path recorded using a camera tracking system. Post-behavioral assessment, mice were euthanized, blood processed for immune cell transcriptome sequencing (BGI, Beijing Genomics Institution, China), and brains sectioned for immunofluorescence staining. Antibodies against Aβ (Abcam, ab201060, USA) and Alexa Fluor® 488 anti-β-Amyloid, 1-16 Antibody (6E10, BioLegend, 803013, UK) were employed to validate the pathology in 5 × FAD mice. The staining results were captured using Andor BC43 (Oxford Instrument, UK).

Adaptability evaluation of the model for other neurodegenerative diseases and metabolic diseases

The AD prediction model was applied to PD, FTD and OB, with performance assessed using ROC analysis and AUC calculation. Comparative AUC values helped evaluate the model’s effectiveness across different diseases.

Statistical methods

This study integrated Mendelian randomization, single-cell transcriptomics, and machine learning to build disease risk prediction models. Analyses were conducted in R (v4.4.2), with code publicly available. GWAS data were stringently quality-controlled, excluding low-call-rate SNPs ( < 95%), heterozygosity outliers, and population stratification outliers. For MR, instrumental variables were selected at P < 5×10⁻⁸, clumped for independence (r² < 0.001, window = 10,000 kb), and subjected to MR Steiger directionality testing. Weak instrument bias was assessed using F-statistics (F > 10). IVW was the primary MR method, supplemented by MR-Egger, weighted median, and MR-PRESSO. Sensitivity analyses included Cochran’s Q, MR-Egger intercept, and MR-PRESSO global tests. Results are reported as β, SE, OR, and 95% CI, with FDR correction for multiple testing. Batch effects in transcriptomic data were corrected using ComBat. Single-cell data were processed via Seurat (normalization, HVG selection, Harmony integration) and clustered with Louvain algorithm followed by marker-based annotation. Model performance was evaluated by AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, with optimism-corrected AUC derived from 1000 bootstrap replicates. Significance was defined as two-sided P < 0.05 or FDR < 0.05.
