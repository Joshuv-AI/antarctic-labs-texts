Integrated light spectrum and growth regulators improved the growth of saffron daughter corms by regulating carbohydrate metabolism in a novel two-stage cropping system
Authors: Mostafa Eftekhari, Majid Ghorbani Javid, Nazim S. Gruda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSaffron, a valuable spice and industrial crop used in food, pharma, and cosmetics, is propagated only vegetatively via corms due to triploid male sterility. Corms are the primary storage organs and determine both the yield and quality of daughter corms. However, the optimal growing conditions for producing large, heavy daughter corms remain unclear.Materials and methodsHere, we examined the effects of light spectra and plant growth regulators on photosynthetic pigments, starch accumulation, and daughter corm production in a two-stage system, with reproductive growth occurring indoors followed by outdoor maturation. The plants were grown under four light spectra: white, red, blue, and a combination of half red and half blue. Two plant growth regulators, gibberellic acid and gamma-aminobutyric acid, were applied during the reproductive stage of the plant. After flowering, the mother corms were directly transplanted to the field for the development of daughter corms.ResultsThe combination of red and blue light with gamma-aminobutyric acid produced the heaviest daughter corms, reaching 8.7 g dry weight, and the highest starch accumulation in the corms reached 383 milligrams per gram fresh weight. Red and blue light combined with gibberellic acid increased the leaf starch concentration to 926 milligrams per gram of fresh weight. A higher pigment content and larger leaf area support greater photosynthetic performance, preserve carbohydrate reserves in mother corms, and ultimately favor the production of heavier daughter corms.ConclusionsThese results provide practical insights for optimizing saffron propagation in controlled-environment and field systems, directly contributing to industrial-scale production and value chain efficiency. Future research should test dynamic light spectra and optimized regulatory strategies to refine corm development and strengthen commercial and industrial saffron production.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09687-0.

Background

The increasing awareness of the harm caused by chemicals and the side effects of synthetic drugs has directed global attention to medicinal plants [1–3]. Medicinal plants not only contribute to human health and well-being but also serve as valuable raw materials for the food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic industries, accounting for a significant share of the global economy [4]. Saffron, often called “Red Gold”, is a triploid plant propagated vegetatively through corms [5]. The dried stigmas of saffron flowers, the most expensive spice in the world, are extensively used not only in gastronomy but also in industrial applications, such as natural colorants, functional ingredients, and high-value bioactive compounds for pharmaceutical and nutraceutical products. Its stigmas are particularly rich in secondary metabolites with antioxidant properties [6, 7], linking saffron production directly to industrial product development and global value chains.

In traditional saffron-producing areas, cultivation methods have remained unchanged since ancient times, requiring numerous laborers for flower harvesting and stigma separation. Furthermore, over the past five decades, climate change has led to a gradual decline in saffron flower yields [8, 9]. Improving stigma production without increasing labor costs or relying on unpredictable climate conditions is needed [10] and can be achieved through the development of sustainable agroecosystem approaches.

Controlled-environment agriculture (CEA) can increase medicinal plant production by overcoming the limitations of conventional practices [11]. Compared with that in the field, the crocin concentration in the stigma was greater in the CEA [12, 13]. It offers substantial potential for regulated production, targeting high stigma yield and corm quality [14], enhancing stigma quality, and reducing labor expenses associated with flower harvesting [9, 15]. For example, flower production in soilless systems is 6.6 times greater than that in soil fields [16]. The enhanced flower and stigma production in CEA primarily stems from precise microclimate regulation [13]. In addition, the two-stage planting system, in which flowering occurs under controlled environmental conditions followed by field cultivation of daughter corms, has gained wide acceptance for its ability to improve stigma yield and quality [17, 18]. However, while stigma production benefits from this system, the yield of daughter corms is often reduced [17, 18]. Since the size and weight of daughter corms directly determine flower and stigma yield during the following harvest and act as the central reserve for new plant growth, corm production remains the key component of saffron vegetative growth. Nevertheless, the mechanisms underlying the reduction in daughter corm yield have not been thoroughly investigated to date. However, the physiological mechanisms behind reduced daughter corm production under these conditions remain poorly understood. Thus, CEA offers the opportunity to refine growing conditions, particularly for producing large daughter corms [19].

Additional lighting is used to increase plant growth, quality, and crop yield in CEA systems [20]. A combination of red and blue lights improves the photosynthetic rate and the accumulation of total carbohydrates, starch, and sucrose. Moradi et al. [21, 22] and Eftekhari et al. [23] also reported the effective role of light wavelength on the quantity and quality of saffron stigmas produced in soilless culture. Light-emitting diode (LED) technology enables precise manipulation of light spectra in the CEA, increasing crop quality, productivity, and energy efficiency [24]. Nevertheless, to date, only a limited number of studies have investigated whether light quality regulates carbohydrate metabolism in saffron corms [21, 25]. Comprehending the fundamental physiological basis is crucial for addressing daughter corm yield loss in two-stage planting systems.

In addition to light quality, plant growth regulators (PGRs) constitute another set of signals that regulate plant growth and development. Gibberellic acid (GA3) has been frequently used in saffron and other medicinal plants to stimulate growth and development [1, 26, 27]. It facilitates the hydrolysis of storage reserves and increases carbohydrate content by increasing α-amylase activity [28]. GA3 has been shown to increase flower and leaf development and to improve corm propagation and saffron yield [29]. The application of GABA to saffron corms has been shown to increase flower production and stigma yield [23].

Despite advances in CEA, saffron cultivation still faces significant challenges. While this approach has improved flowering, stigma yield, and quality compared with traditional field methods, it has not solved the persistent problem of reduced daughter corm production. Since daughter corms determine the future yield and sustainability of saffron cultivation, this limitation is critical. Light quality is one of the most important environmental factors that can be precisely managed in CEA systems and influences photosynthesis, carbohydrate accumulation, and assimilate partitioning. Likewise, PGRs play central roles in regulating source–sink relationships, carbohydrate metabolism, and storage organ development. Because daughter corm growth depends on both carbohydrate availability (source activity) and sink strength, light and hormonal signals are expected to interact in regulating corm formation and development. Emerging evidence from other crops suggests that light signaling and hormonal pathways jointly influence plant growth and carbon allocation, indicating that their combined manipulation may have synergistic effects on daughter corm production. However, the interactive effects of light quality and PGRs on carbohydrate metabolism and daughter corm development in saffron remain poorly understood. Addressing these gaps is essential for developing more sustainable and productive saffron cultivation strategies under protected environments.

The objective of this study was therefore to investigate whether specific light spectra, in combination with PGRs, can increase photosynthetic activity, regulate carbohydrate use in mother corms, and ultimately increase the size and weight of daughter corms. We hypothesized that (i) optimal light quality combined with PGRs would stimulate leaf development and improve photosynthetic performance; (ii) increased photosynthetic activity would reduce the breakdown of storage carbohydrates in mother corms during the reproductive stage; and (iii) these processes would favor greater starch accumulation and lead to the production of larger, heavier daughter corms in the field.

Methods

Plant materials

The corms used in this research were purchased from Qayen, South Khorasan, a significant saffron-producing region in Iran. The corms were pulled from the soil in June and spent the summer (from June to September) in a controlled environment at 60% relative humidity, 25 °C, and in darkness [31]. To prevent results from being influenced by corm weight, 10–12 g mother corms were selected for the study.

Experimental design and plant management

The study was set up as a two-factor factorial experiment in a randomized complete block design (RCBD) in the phytotron, with three replicates. The phytotron maintained a relative humidity of 80%, a day/night temperature of 20 °C/15°C, and a photoperiod of 8 h of daylight and 16 h of darkness throughout the plant growth phase [17]. Two plant growth regulator (PGR) treatments (GA₃ and GABA) and a non-treated control (tap water) were used as experimental factors, along with four light qualities. The LED lamps provided irradiance, including red (R, peak wavelength at 660 nm), blue (B, peak wavelength at 465 nm), white (W, 400–700 nm), and a combination of red and blue lights (RB, 50% R: 50% B) (Fig. 1). Throughout the plant growth phase, a consistent distance of 50 cm was maintained between the LED lamps and the plant canopy. Irradiance was regularly monitored by a PAR-FluorPen FP 100-MAX (Photon Systems Instruments, Drasov, Czech Republic).

In early October, undamaged corms with no fungal infection and a main bud length of 2–3 cm were treated in the phytotron by spraying GABA at 25 µM and GA3 at 1 mM, as previous studies have reported maximum growth at these concentrations [29, 32]. A total of 300 corms were used for each treatment. The corms were placed directly on metal mesh shelves. They received no water or nutrients, as saffron corms possess sufficient stored reserves to support growth during the flowering phase under controlled-environment conditions. Furthermore, irrigation and nutrient application were intentionally omitted to minimize potential confounding effects and ensure that observed responses could be attributed primarily to the light-quality and PGR treatments. At the end of phytotron growth in mid-November, all lateral buds were removed, leaving only two apical buds intact, after which the corms were directly transplanted to the open field.

Climatic conditions during the experimental period and soil physicochemical properties of the experimental site are presented in Table 1. Each plot was 3 m × 1 m and 0.5 m apart. To achieve a plant density of 33 corms m− 2, the rows were set 20 cm apart. Before sowing, basal fertilizers, including urea, triple superphosphate, and potassium sulfate, were applied at rates of 50, 150, and 100 kg ha− 1, respectively. Throughout the growth period, all the plots received adequate irrigation and were manually weeded.

Plant sampling

In the phytotron experiment, the flowers were harvested before the petals opened, and the stigma was separated manually. To demonstrate the effects of light exposure on plant growth, leaf morphological and photosynthetic traits were recorded after the plants had been grown in the field for 90 days. Five plants from each replication were sampled in early February. The plants were immediately divided into roots and leaves to record their fresh weight. The roots and leaves were oven-dried at 80 °C for 72 h to a constant weight and weighed. Then, all the leaves were placed upside down on white paper for photography, allowing the width, length, and area of the leaves to be measured using the WinDIAS Leaf Image Analysis System with healthy-area and green-thresholding parameters. The root length of the longest root was measured using a digital caliper or ruler, and the number of leaves was counted manually.

Determination of the photosynthetic pigment concentration

The fresh leaf samples were ground in liquid nitrogen, and 100 mg of the resulting powder was extracted in 10 mL of 80% (v/v) aqueous acetone at 25 °C in the dark for 24 h. The absorbance of the extract was measured at wavelengths of 663, 645, and 470 nm via a UV‒Vis spectrophotometer (UV-2501PC, Shimadzu, Japan) to determine the photosynthetic pigments of chlorophyll a (Chl a), chlorophyll b (Chl b), and carotenoids, respectively [33].

Determination of starch concentration

We determined the starch concentration in leaves and daughter corms. The total soluble carbohydrate content was measured via the Anthrone procedure [34]. Fresh samples were ground in liquid nitrogen, and 200 mg of tissue was blended with 7 mL of ethanol (70% w/v) for 5 min on ice and then centrifuged for 10 min at 6700 rpm at 4 °C. After 200 µL of the alcohol extract was mixed with one mL of an Anthrone solution (comprising 500 mg Anthrone reagent, 250 mL H2SO4 95%, and 12.5 mL distilled water), the absorbance was read via spectrophotometry (PowerWave™ XS Microplate Reader, BioTek Instruments, Inc., Winooski, VT, USA) at 625 nm, which was based on the method of Van Doorn [35]. The starch concentration was also determined using a sugar‒anthrone‒sulfuric acid reaction, as described by McCready et al. [36].

Determination of daughter corm number and weight

At maturity, daughter corms were dug up, and their number and fresh weight were recorded immediately. The daughter corms were weighed again after drying under the same conditions.

Statistical analysis

The data were sorted using Excel. Analysis of variance was performed using the SAS statistical software package (version 9.4) [37], and significant differences among treatments were evaluated using Duncan’s multiple range test (p ≤ 0.05). Linear relationships among variables were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient, a parametric statistic that measures the association between two quantitative, continuous variables and is suitable for interval- or ratio-scale data when both variables are normally distributed [38]. Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) was then performed using the Ward method and Euclidean distance. The figures were drawn via Origin (Origin 2025, USA). The correlation matrix heatmap and the clustered heatmap were generated via the R programming language (version 4.5.1) [39].

Compared with those treated with GA3 or the control (without PGRs), plants treated with GABA presented increased Chl a, Chl a + b, Chl a/b, and carotenoid concentrations; GA3 also increased pigment contents relative to those of the control but to a smaller extent than GABA did, and the magnitude of the increase in pigment content varied with light quality (Fig. 2a, c, d, e). Under all light conditions, the leaves in the GABA and control treatments presented the highest and lowest carotenoid concentrations, respectively (Fig. 2e). The carotenoid concentration in leaves in the control treatment decreased sharply but showed almost no difference among the four light quality treatments (Fig. 2e). Across light qualities, the total chlorophyll concentration (Chl a + b) followed trends similar to the trends of the Chl a concentration, with GABA generally providing the most substantial gains (Fig. 2c). GA3 and the control typically produced smaller pigment gains than did GABA, with variation across light qualities.

With the use of GABA under combined light, the concentrations of photosynthetic pigments (Chl a, Chl b, and carotenoids) increased significantly, especially the Chl a concentration (Fig. 2a, b, e). In the combined light treatment, GABA increased the concentrations of Chl a, Chl b, and carotenoids by up to 542.9%, 194.4%, and 135.3%, respectively, compared with those in the control treatment, and GA3 increased these concentrations by up to 385.7%, 138.9%, and 123.5%, respectively (Fig. 2a, b, e). Under both white light and combined light, the leaf Chl b concentration significantly increased with the use of PGRs, peaking when the PGR was GABA (Fig. 2b). In contrast, under monochromatic red light, the Chl b concentration in leaves was 1.5% greater in the GA3 treatment than in the GABA treatment. Interestingly, under blue light, the leaf Chl b concentration was equivalent between the GA3 and GABA treatments (0.065 mg g− 1 fresh weight) (Fig. 2b). The ratio of chlorophyll a to b (Chl a/b) in the RB+GABA treatment was the highest (4.3) (Fig. 2d).

Starch concentration in leaves

The effects of the various treatments on the starch concentration in the leaves significantly differed (Table 4).

The highest starch concentration in leaves was observed under combined light (Fig. 4b). Across all PGR treatments, white light had the lowest starch concentration in the leaves (Fig. 4b).

Regardless of the light quality treatment, the starch concentration in the leaves decreased from GA3 to GABA (Fig. 4b). Interestingly, the control treatment further enhanced the decomposition of starch in the leaves (Fig. 4b).

From GABA to GA3, the starch concentrations in leaves under white, blue, red, and combined B&R lights increased by 0.1%, 0.6%, 1.2%, and 1.7%, respectively (Fig. 4b). In the GABA treatment, the concentration of storage carbohydrates in the leaves did not significantly differ among the W, B, and R light quality treatments but was 3.5%, 2.7%, and 1.8% lower than that in the RB treatment, respectively (Fig. 4b). Additionally, saffron leaves treated with RB+GA3 presented the highest starch concentration (925.7 mg g− 1 fresh weight) (Fig. 4b).

For leaf production, there was a significant difference between the two PGRs under a given light quality (Fig. 3a). The fresh weight of the leaves in the control treatment decreased sharply but was not different among the four light qualities. The reduced weight of the leaves in the control treatment was due mainly to the decreased number of leaves. Irrespective of the light quality treatment, the use of PGRs increased the number of leaves, which in turn increased their weight (Fig. 3a-c). Compared with the control treatment, the PGR treatment also significantly increased the length of the leaves. Compared with the GA3 treatment, both the GABA treatment and the control resulted in decreases in leaf number, fresh weight, and leaf area 90 days after transplanting (Fig. 3a, b, e). The control treatment decreased the root dry weight, with no difference among the light qualities (Fig. 3g).

The highest leaf fresh weight was observed in the RB+GA3 treatment, with a value of 8.69 g (corm)−1 (Fig. 3b). In addition, the fresh weight of leaves decreased by 14.1% and 17.9% when the plants were shifted from combined light to red light in the GA3 and GABA treatments, respectively; however, no significant difference was observed between the combined and red light conditions (Fig. 3b). In the GA3 treatment, light quality had no effect on the dry weight of the leaves (Fig. 3c). The longest leaves were observed in the RB+GA3 treatment (34.8 cm). Between the GA3 and GABA treatments under white light, the use of PGRs did not affect leaf length (Fig. 3d). In terms of leaf area, the application of GA3 did not significantly differ among the light qualities of W, B, and RB. Nevertheless, the leaf area decreased substantially under R light (Fig. 3e). The fresh and dry weights of the roots in the RB+GA3 treatment were the highest (6.93 and 0.80 mg (corm)−1, respectively). However, there was no marked difference in root weight under RB light between the GA3 and GABA treatments (Fig. 3f, g). Neither the leaf area nor the root dry weight substantially differed between the RB+GA3 and RB+GABA treatments (Fig. 3e, g). Furthermore, no significant difference in root length was observed between the B+GABA and RB+GA3 treatments (Fig. 3h). Compared with the control treatment, GA3 increased root length under white, blue, red, and combined light conditions by up to 78.9%, 95.3%, 26.3%, and 102.4%, respectively. Similarly, GABA increased root length by 56.9%, 131.3%, 58.4%, and 69.9%, respectively (Fig. 3h).

The effects of the various treatments on the starch concentration in the mother corms were significantly different (p ≤ 0.01) (Table 4).

Discussion

Light quality drives saffron physiology and leaf carbon status and shapes leaf starch accumulation

In soilless cultivation under water deficit conditions, maintaining high photosynthetic activity is essential for robust growth and development [40]. Light quality is the primary driver of pigment accumulation, with specific light regimes maximizing pigment pools. Studies have demonstrated that light quality affects photomorphogenesis, photosynthesis capacity, and carbon fixation [41–43]. These results are in accordance with the results of our study, where combined light increased the photosynthetic pigments and starch concentrations in leaves (Figs. 2a-e and 4b). These findings suggest that the enhanced accumulation of photosynthetic pigments and starch under combined light treatments may be associated with the complementary effects of red and blue wavelengths on photosynthetic performance and carbon assimilation. Previous studies have shown that red and blue lights are perceived by different photoreceptors and can influence pathways related to pigment biosynthesis and carbohydrate metabolism [44, 45]. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms were not investigated in the present study.

Further using PGRs increased the total chlorophyll concentration of saffron across all light qualities in our study (Fig. 2c). Among the PGR treatments, GABA generally increased pigment content more than did GA3 or the control, and this effect was amplified under favorable light conditions. Bhatnagar et al. [46] noted a positive relationship between GABA supplementation and pigments production, and this supports the result of this study. In some cases (e.g., Chl b under red light), GA3 outperformed GABA, indicating that the interaction can reverse which PGR is most effective depending on the light (Fig. 2b). The ratio of chlorophyll a-to-b in leaves was found to increase with the use of PGRs, especially GABA (Fig. 2d).

Significant Light×PGR interactions were observed for several pigments, indicating that the magnitude of PGR effects depended on light quality. Combining red and blue lights and using PGRs produced the largest pigment pools (Fig. 2c, e), suggesting a synergistic effect of light quality and PGRs on photosynthetic pigment synthesis, which subsequently increased the starch concentration in leaves (Fig. 4b). The total chlorophyll concentration increased further when the two light qualities were combined, and the Chl a + b concentration was increased by the application of GABA (Fig. 2c). In addition, the starch concentration in leaves was greater in the RB+GA3 treatment than in the other treatments (Fig. 4b).

Light-driven canopy development modulated by PGRs

The physiological effects of different light qualities and PGRs were reflected in the plant characteristics, leaves, and roots of saffron (Table 3). A combination of blue and red lights in a controlled environment before transplanting increased carbon fixation (Fig. 4b), increasing leaf length and weight (Fig. 3b-d) and then minimizing the degradation of storage carbohydrate in mother corms. These observations align with findings in other species; for example, rice (Oryza sativa L.), cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.), and tomato (Lycopersicon esculentum L.) grown under a combination of R and B light had a higher rate of net photosynthetic rate and improved parameters of plant growth [47–49]. A possible explanation could be that saffron leaves may adapt better to a combination of blue and red lights; however, understanding the underlying mechanisms requires additional research. Combined light quality also facilitated leaf area development (Fig. 3e), providing further evidence to support the view. Plants grown under monochromatic blue LEDs exhibited the lowest morphological characteristics of leaves such as the number of leaves, leaf fresh weight, length, and area (Fig. 3a, b, d, e), indicating negative effects of blue light on above-ground elongation, leading to compact plants with shorter leaves. Blue light has been shown to inhibit vertical growth by promoting compact morphology, a response supported by findings from Metallo et al. [50], Moradi et al. [21], and Naznin and Lefsrud [51]. Yang et al. [52] reported that blue light spectra upregulate negative-GA signaling components, resulting in compact plants without any morphological abnormalities. This reduction in elongation under blue light reflects its role in suppressing internode extension and enhancing structural rigidity.

Contrary to the above-mentioned observations, the results of the current study demonstrated the positive effects of blue light on the length of roots (Fig. 3h), which is in line with findings by Moradi et al. [21], who reported the longest root in monochromatic blue-light-grown plants. It has also been reported that cryptochromes and phototropins regulate root elongation and development [53], with cytoplasmic cryptochrome1 promoting root elongation by modulating auxin efflux, whereas phototropin suppresses the growth and number of lateral buds. While a larger root mass typically reflects improved plant vigor, nutrient uptake efficiency, and environmental adaptability, from a commercial saffron-production perspective, a lower root-to-corm ratio is more desirable, as it indicates a greater allocation of biomass to the harvestable corm organ. The data in this study indicated that while PGRs may play a role in modulating root biomass, their effect is likely secondary to the dominant influence of light spectra. This is evidenced by the increase in root fresh weight with combining red and blue wavelengths (Fig. 3f), a response that is consistent with previous reports showing enhanced root biomass under a 50% blue + 50% red LED treatment [21], possibly because of the increased translocation of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) from the shoots to the roots [54, 55]. Root dry weight followed a similar trend to root fresh weight (Fig. 3g). The results of the present study provide further evidence that RB is an optimal light quality for saffron growth in this two-stage cropping system.

As expected, plant growth showed a positive response to PGRs, indicating that the experimental conditions were appropriate for assessing how plant growth and carbohydrate metabolism in both mother corms and leaves respond to different PGRs. We found that there was no difference in the fresh and dry weights of leaves and roots, respectively, when the plants were grown under the control treatment (Fig. 3b, g). The highest root weight occurred in plants treated with GA3 (Fig. 3f, g). GA3 application reportedly promotes root development through its influence on accelerating the assimilation and translocation of auxins [56, 57], suggesting that PGRs may have a secondary but complementary role in root biomass modulation. The GA3-treated plants also exhibited the highest morphological characteristics of leaves such as the number of leaves, leaf fresh weight, length, and area (Fig. 3a, b, d, e). These findings are consistent with the role of GA3 in promoting internode elongation and stimulating leaf growth [58].

Combining red and blue lights and using PGRs improved leaf characteristics (Fig. 3a-e). Compared with the GABA treatment, the GA3 treatment increased the number of leaves and elevated the leaf dry weight under white light (Fig. 3a, c) to levels almost as high as those in the R+GABA treatment. The intermediate leaf length recorded under the B+GA3 treatment suggests a counteracting effect where GA3 promotes leaf elongation while blue light reduces it (Fig. 3d). The leaf area of the RB+GA3 and RB+GABA plants did not differ (Fig. 3e). The observed trends indicate that the application of PGRs and RB light are optimal for maximizing leaf expansion and photosynthetic efficiency. These findings are consistent with the importance of balanced light spectra in enhancing photosynthetic efficiency and the role of PGRs in regulating plant morphology.

Mother corms treated with RB+GABA in the phytotron increased daughter corm size in the open field via source‒sink dynamics

The optimal lighting conditions for saffron cultivation in a two-stage cropping system must consider not only saffron growth but also the yield of flowers, the concentration of apocarotenoids in the stigma, and even the yield of daughter corms. In the present study, the number of daughter corms and their fresh and dry weights changed in response to different light qualities. The combined light increased the starch concentration in the mother corms (Fig. 4a).

Furthermore, the number of daughter corms at maturity was greater in saffron treated with red light than in those exposed to white, blue, or combined light (Fig. 5a). This result aligns with a previous study reporting the maximum number of daughter corms under pure red light [21] and may be attributed to red light-mediated inhibition of apical dominance in corm buds and the consequent outgrowth of lateral buds through phytochrome signaling. Yang et al. [52] have also demonstrated that the red light promotes branching in Norway spruce (Picea abies L.) plants by suppressing auxin metabolism. However, this finding did not necessarily indicate that red light was the optimal light quality for saffron growth. Significant increases in leaf area (Fig. 3e) and the size of daughter corms (Fig. 5b, c) were achieved by increasing the carbohydrate content in the mother corm. These results are consistent with previous studies in which the high carbohydrate content in the mother corm increased the leaf area [25, 59].

The results of this study demonstrate that combined red and blue light spectra significantly (p ≤ 0.05) increase daughter corm weight (Fig. 5b, c), which aligns with the findings of Moradi et al. [21] and Zhou et al. [25], who highlighted that combined red and blue lights often leads to larger new corms due to increased carbohydrate accumulation in mother corms, resulting in heavier daughter corms.

Moreover, the number of leaves, leaf weight, and leaf length under the combined light were greater than those under the other light qualities (Fig. 3a-d), providing further evidence that a high carbohydrate content in the mother corm stimulates leaf growth under field conditions. The data reported here are consistent with the observations that under combined B&R lights, the Chl a + b, Chl a/b, leaf dry weight, leaf area, and daughter corm yield and weight increased [25]. However, the above parameters increased further when the ratio of R to B was 3:2. These findings imply that the size of daughter corms is influenced by light quality during the reproductive stage in the two-stage cropping system.

The number of daughter corms at the mature stage decreased sharply with the application of PGRs under all four light regimes (Fig. 5a), potentially due to a reduction in new lateral buds because of the induced apical dominance. In addition to the number of corms, light quality and PGRs also influenced corm size (Fig. 5b, c). Producing a large number of small daughter corms under red light in the control treatment may indicate a bias toward allocating resources to meristem initiation rather than enlargement. With the application of PGRs, saffron plants appeared to prioritize producing fewer but larger daughter corms, possibly as an adaptive response to ensure subsequent growth. These findings underscore the critical role of PGRs in efficient corm production while highlighting the secondary influence of light qualities, particularly when considered in interaction with PGRs treatments.

Compared with daughter corms under the RB+GABA treatment, the daughter corms under the other light qualities and PGR treatments presented greater numbers but lower weights (Fig. S1), which demonstrated that mother corm reserves (rather than leaf-captured carbon and root-absorbed nutrients) were the primary source supporting daughter corm growth during the vegetative stage. In addition, under the RB+GABA treatment, the starch concentration in the mother corms was greater than that in the mother corms under the other treatments (Fig. 4a). Since the plants in this study were rootless and did not uptake any nutrients from the environment before being transplanted to the field, this further supports the hypothesis that mother corms are the primary source of reserves for the growth of daughter corms. Compared with those in the RB+GABA treatment, in the other light qualities and PGR treatments, the starch concentration in the mother corms was at least 1.9% lower (Fig. 4a). However, whether or not the light quality and PGRs during the reproductive stage in a two-stage cropping system decrease the metabolism of storage carbohydrate in mother corms remains unclear. Additional work is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms involved.

The results of the experiments reported here clearly revealed that the mother corms under combined light and PGRs showed increased carbohydrate content. In the control treatment, the starch concentration decreased under all four light qualities. Nevertheless, white light stimulated further reduction (Fig. 4a). Among the GABA-treated plants, the starch concentration in the mother corms grown under combined light was more than 50% greater than that in those grown under white light (Fig. 4a). Under both blue light and combined light, the fresh and dry weights of the daughter corms significantly increased with the use of PGRs, reaching a maximum when the PGR was GABA (Fig. 5b, c). Based on these observations, combined light and PGRs can stimulate leaf expansion, which may have contributed to increased carbon fixation, possibly through enhanced photosynthesis. The greater starch concentration in leaves (as the sink organ) inhibited the metabolism of reserves in mother corms (as the source organ) via feedback, which increased the production of large-sized daughter corms. In contrast, combining red and blue lights and using PGRs decreased the number of daughter corms (Fig. 5a). Compared with the RB+GA3 treatment, the RB+GABA treatment resulted in a 1.9% greater starch concentration and a 16.2% lower number of daughter corms (Figs. 4a and 5a). This observation indicated that the high starch concentration in leaves directly suppressed starch transport from corms to leaves and therefore indirectly inhibited starch degradation. As the formation of underground storage organs in saffron is affected by starch synthesis, source‒sink dynamics may play a signaling role in starch synthesis and degradation. During the period of daughter corm growth, increased leaf growth and increased photosynthetic capacity increase the starch concentration in leaves while reducing sink demand, thereby inhibiting carbohydrate degradation in mother corms and improving the production of large-sized daughter corms. This hypothesis is entirely consistent with the data obtained in the present study (Fig. 8). The integration of RB+PGRs yields complementary effects: combined red and blue light enhances overall growth pathways, and PGRs directly regulate carbohydrate biosynthesis and degradation in saffron corms.

The suppression of reserve metabolism in mother corms by PGRs and combined light quality may not be solely mediated by the previously mentioned mechanisms; additional systemic signals may also contribute to this inhibitory effect. For example, gibberellin (GA) is well-documented for its role in inducing starch degradation in plants, and low light intensity and a reduced red-to-far-red light ratio have been shown to stimulate GA biosynthesis [55, 60, 61]. Conversely, blue light has been found to reduce GA biosynthesis by maintaining DELLA protein stability [62, 63]. A previous experimental study also demonstrated that GA3 priming of saffron corms before cultivation elevates soluble carbohydrate levels in the corms by inducing starch degradation [27]. Therefore, additional research is needed to clarify the role of GA in the light-dependent regulation of starch synthesis and degradation in saffron corms.

Correlations between vegetative traits and reproductive success

In saffron, the size of daughter corms is positively correlated with the size of the mother corm [64, 65]. The high daughter corm size was related mainly to the high carbohydrate content in the mother corm, whereby (1) more carbohydrates were allocated directly to daughter corms [66], and (2) improving leaf and root growth enhanced carbon fixation [67]. Therefore, in this study, the saffron plants treated with GABA and the combined light during the reproductive stage presented greater carbohydrate contents in mother corms (Fig. 4a). On the basis of these findings, we hypothesized that optimal light quality and PGR treatment during the reproductive stage could increase leaf growth and, subsequently, the growth of daughter corms during the vegetative stage in the field.

The photosynthetic pigment content is likely one of the most critical factors in saffron vegetative growth. In our study, daughter corm weight was significantly and positively correlated with the photosynthetic pigment content and leaf area, as well as with the leaf and mother corm starch concentrations (Fig. 6). These findings suggest that the improvement in the pigment pools in the leaves may enhance saffron leaf starch by boosting photosynthetic rates, thereby improving carbon availability for leaf growth and corm development. These physiological gains serve as a carbon source that supports a relatively large leaf area, which likely promotes photosynthesis and, in parallel, further preserves reserves in the mother corms. Limited depletion of reserves in the mother corms, coinciding with greater source capacity, ultimately led to the development of larger, heavier daughter corms with greater potential for growth and flowering in the subsequent year. Importantly, these findings support the mechanistic pathway proposed in the hypotheses.

Among all the parameters studied in this research, all but the number of daughter corms exhibited a positive correlation with the fresh and dry weights of the daughter corms (Fig. 6). These results suggest that, in saffron cropping systems aimed at increasing daughter corm size, particular attention should be given to the relationships between these traits—especially the concentration of photosynthetic pigments and the weight of daughter corms.

As shown in Fig. 6, the correlations between the number of daughter corms and all other traits were negative, all of which were significant, indicating that higher values of pigment content, leaf starch, leaf area, and mother corm starch are associated with allocating assimilates to fewer but larger daughter corms. These relationships suggest that increases in storage carbohydrates are linked to the production of heavier daughter corms. However, this occurs at the expense of the number of daughter corms, highlighting a trade-off between corm size and corm proliferation.

Multivariate assessment of plant responses to different treatments

Among the treatments, RB+GA3 and RB+GABA were closely associated. These two treatments had the most significant positive impacts on most growth parameters, as previously shown (Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5). The HC analysis placed them in a single group (Fig. 7). Enhanced pigment pools and increased leaf starch in these two treatments increased leaf length and area, significantly improving the biomass of the new corms. In contrast, the number of daughter corms formed an independent cluster, reflecting its negative correlation with all other traits. The four control treatments stand out distinctly, indicating significantly inferior performance across most parameters. Finally, the clustered heatmap effectively grouped the variables and treatments.

The functional relationships among growth parameters indicate that PGRs and combined blue and red light play a critical role in optimizing canopy structure and quality. The combination of blue and red light enhances the expression of crucial genes encoding chlorophyll and carotenoid pigment-related enzymes, leading to increased pigment accumulation [68], enhanced light absorption, and improved photosynthesis [69]. Pigment accumulation increases under combined B&R light, leading to greater leaf starch and larger leaf area, thereby contributing to increased daughter corms weight. PGRs increase leaf elongation, likely by inhibiting starch metabolism in mother corms, possibly through increased photosynthetic capacity. Furthermore, optimal light quality increased starch biosynthesis, which serves as both a carbon substrate for leaf growth and a signal of decreased carbon translocation from corms to leaves and of starch degradation. The increased reserve content in mother corms, combined with a well-developed canopy, results in the production of large-sized daughter corms in a two-stage cropping system (Fig. 8).

Because molecular, hormonal, and gene-expression analyses were not performed, the mechanisms underlying the observed responses to light quality and PGR treatments remain speculative and warrant further investigation.

Conclusions

The optimal light quality for saffron growth in this study was a spectrum of 50% blue and 50% red. When combined with PGRs during the reproductive stage, this treatment minimized carbohydrate decomposition in the mother corms and increased the production of larger daughter corms. The integration of light quality and PGRs influences carbohydrate utilization in mother corms by enhancing chlorophyll synthesis, photosynthesis, leaf starch accumulation, and leaf area. The higher starch content of leaves served both as an indicator of the plant’s growth status and as a carbon source supporting leaf development, while preserving reserves in the mother corms that were ultimately redirected toward daughter corm formation.

The application of 50% red and 50% blue light together with the utilization of PGRs increased the production of large daughter corms by inhibiting the allocation of reserves from mother corms to leaves at the seedling stage. Producing large daughter corms is crucial for sustaining saffron cultivation, since their nutrient reserves directly determine flower and stigma yields in the following year. This study advances the understanding of how light quality and PGRs regulate reserve metabolism and daughter corm development. By optimizing these factors during the reproductive stage, the production of daughter corms can be increased, offering a practical strategy for more productive and sustainable saffron cultivation in two-stage cropping systems. Future research should focus on testing dynamic light regimes and refined combinations of PGRs to optimize daughter corm development further and ensure sustainable commercial saffron production.

From a practical perspective, the results suggest that the combined red–blue LED treatment, particularly when integrated with GA₃ and GABA applications, can be used in two-stage saffron production systems to enhance daughter corm growth and carbohydrate accumulation. Growers adopting controlled-environment saffron cultivation may benefit from optimizing light spectra and PGR management to improve the production of vigorous daughter corms for subsequent cultivation cycles.
