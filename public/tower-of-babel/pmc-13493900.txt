Association between carbohydrate quality index and cardiovascular disease: Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study
Authors: Zahra Gaeini, Parvin Mirmiran, Fereidoun Azizi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile emerging evidence from Western populations suggests that higher carbohydrate quality (assessed by indices incorporating fiber, glycemic index, whole grains, and solid carbohydrate sources) is inversely associated with cardiovascular risk factors, prospective evidence from Middle Eastern populations regarding the association between carbohydrate quality and cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains limited and inconsistent. We included 2,648 participants free of CVD, from the participants of the 3th phase of Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS). Dietary intake was assessed at baseline (2006–2008) using a validated 168-item food frequency questionnaire. Carbohydrate quality index (CQI) was calculated based on four components: dietary fiber intake, dietary glycemic index, whole grain to total grain ratio, and solid carbohydrate to total carbohydrate ratio. The CQI and its components were calculated and categorized into tertiles. Incident CVD was defined as coronary heart disease, stroke, or cardiovascular death occurring during follow-up. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with sequential adjustment for demographics, lifestyle factors, dietary factors, and potential mediators (BMI, diabetes, hypertension). Sensitivity analyses were conducted by (1) omitting the whole grain ratio from the CQI to yield a 3-component Modified CQI, and (2) constructing a Population-Adapted Index (PAI) replacing whole grain with legume intake. During a median follow-up of 10.6 years, 171 CVD cases (6.5%) were identified. After multivariable adjustment, no significant association was observed between CQI and incident CVD (T3 vs. T1: HR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.57–1.21). Individual CQI components also showed null associations. Sensitivity analyses using a Modified CQI (without whole grain) and a Population-Adapted Index (incorporating legume intake) yielded consistent null associations (Modified CQI T3 vs. T1: HR = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.39–1.13; PAI T3 vs. T1: HR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.57–1.50, fully adjusted). In this prospective study of Iranian adults, we observed no statistically significant association between CQI and incident CVD. Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD), remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, and dietary factors play an important role in its development1. Among dietary components, carbohydrates represent the primary energy source in most populations, yet their relationship with cardio-metabolic health remains complex and controversial2. Recent nutritional epidemiology has shifted focus from carbohydrate quantity to quality, recognizing that metabolic effects vary substantially based on source, processing, and associated nutrients3,4. Previous Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS) analyses examining carbohydrate intake and metabolic outcomes have reported inconsistent findings, highlighting the need for more comprehensive measures of carbohydrate quality5–7.

The carbohydrate quality index (CQI) was developed to capture multiple dimensions of carbohydrate quality simultaneously8. This index incorporates four components: dietary fiber intake, dietary glycemic index (GI), the ratio of whole grains to total grains, and the ratio of solid carbohydrates to total carbohydrates8,9. Higher CQI scores reflect greater consumption of fiber-rich, low-glycemic, whole-grain, and solid carbohydrate sources8. Several studies have reported inverse associations between higher CQI and cardio-metabolic risk factors, including obesity, metabolic syndrome, and glycemic abnormalities8–11. However, evidence regarding the association between CQI and incident CVD remains limited and inconsistent8–12. In the SUN cohort, higher dietary carbohydrate quality was associated with lower risk of cardiovascular events13, although findings across populations have not been uniform.

Most available evidence on CQI originates from Western populations, whereas prospective data from Middle Eastern populations are scarce. Dietary patterns in Iran differ substantially from Western dietary patterns, particularly regarding staple carbohydrate sources such as refined flatbreads, rice, and traditional grain products14,15. These differences may influence the applicability of CQI in this population and may affect the relationship between carbohydrate quality and CVD risk.

Therefore, we aimed to prospectively investigate the association between CQI and its individual components with incident CVD among Iranian adults participating in the TLGS. We hypothesized that higher CQI scores would be associated with lower risk of incident CVD.

Methods

Study design and population

This prospective cohort study was conducted within the framework of the TLGS, an ongoing population-based cohort initiated in 1999 to investigate the prevalence and incidence of non-communicable diseases and their risk factors among residents of district 13 of Tehran, Iran16. The TLGS follows participants at approximately 3-year intervals with standardized examinations including questionnaires, anthropometric measurements, and biochemical assessments.

For the present analysis, we used data from the third examination (2006–2008) as baseline. Participants were included if they were aged ≥ 19 years (n = 10,092), had complete dietary, anthropometric and biochemical data (n = 2963), and were free of the CVD at baseline (n = 2807). Participants with missing or implausible dietary data (total energy intake < 800 kcal/day or > 4,200 kcal/day), or who lost to follow-up were excluded. Finally, we included 2,648 CVD-free participants at baseline, who were followed through subsequent examination cycles until March 2018, with median follow-up periods of 10.6 years (Fig. 1).

Dietary assessment

Dietary intake at baseline was assessed by trained dietitians using a validated 168-item semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ). The FFQ which used in TLGS has been validated against multiple 24-h dietary recalls in this population, showing reasonable agreement for major nutrients (correlation coefficients: 0.60 for carbohydrates, 0.58 for protein, 0.52 for fat) and good reproducibility (intra-class correlation coefficients: 0.65–0.78 for macronutrients). However, as with all FFQs, measurement error and recall bias remains inherent limitations15,17. The FFQ asked participants to report their usual frequency of consumption of each food item during the previous year, with standard portion sizes defined for each item. Portion sizes were converted to grams per day using household measures. Nutrient intakes were calculated by multiplying the frequency and portion size of each food item by its nutrient content using the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) food composition database, supplemented by the Iranian food composition table for local food items when available.

Carbohydrate quality index (CQI) calculation

The CQI was calculated based on four components:1 dietary fiber intake,2 dietary glycemic index (GI)3 the ratio of whole grains to total grains, and4 the ratio of solid carbohydrates to total carbohydrates8,18. Each component was scored and summed to create the overall CQI score.

Dietary fiber intake (g/day): Total dietary fiber intake was calculated from all fiber sources reported in the FFQ. Participants were ranked by fiber intake and assigned scores based on tertiles, with higher fiber intake receiving higher scores.

Dietary fiber intake (g/day): Total dietary fiber intake was calculated from all fiber sources reported in the FFQ. Participants were ranked by fiber intake and assigned scores based on tertiles, with higher fiber intake receiving higher scores.

Dietary glycemic index: The overall dietary GI was calculated as the weighted average of the GI values of all carbohydrate-containing foods consumed, using the following formula:Dietary GI = Σ (GI of food i × carbohydrate content of food i × frequency of consumption of food i) / Σ(carbohydrate content of food i × frequency of consumption of food i), where the sum is taken over all carbohydrate-containing foods reported in the FFQ.GI values were assigned to each food item using published international GI tables19, with glucose as the reference food (GI = 100). Lower dietary GI received higher scores in the CQI calculation.

Whole grain to total grain ratio: Whole grain intake included all whole grain products such as whole wheat bread, whole grain barley, oats, and traditional Iranian whole grain products. Whole grain to total grain ratio: Whole grain intake included all whole grain products such as whole wheat bread, whole grain barley, oats, and traditional Iranian whole grain products. Total grain intake included both whole and refined grain products. The ratio was calculated as:

Whole grain ratio = whole grain intake (g/day) / total grain intake (g/day).

Higher ratios (greater whole grain consumption relative to total grains) received higher scores.

Solid carbohydrate to total carbohydrate ratio: Solid carbohydrates were defined as carbohydrates from solid and semi-solid food sources (e.g., bread, rice, fruits, and vegetables), while liquid carbohydrates included carbohydrates from beverages (e.g., fruit juices, sugar-sweetened beverages, milk). The ratio was calculated as:Solid carbohydrate ratio = solid carbohydrate intake (g/day) / total carbohydrate intake (g/day).Higher ratios (greater proportion of carbohydrates from solid sources) received higher scores.

Solid carbohydrate to total carbohydrate ratio: Solid carbohydrates were defined as carbohydrates from solid and semi-solid food sources (e.g., bread, rice, fruits, and vegetables), while liquid carbohydrates included carbohydrates from beverages (e.g., fruit juices, sugar-sweetened beverages, milk). The ratio was calculated as:

Solid carbohydrate ratio = solid carbohydrate intake (g/day) / total carbohydrate intake (g/day).

Higher ratios (greater proportion of carbohydrates from solid sources) received higher scores.

For each of the four components, participants were divided into tertiles, and scores of 1, 2, or 3 were assigned to the lowest, middle, and highest tertiles, respectively (with reversed scoring for dietary GI, where lower values received higher scores). The four component scores were then summed to create the overall CQI, with a possible range of 4 to 12, where higher scores indicate better carbohydrate quality.

Covariate assessment

Socioeconomic and demographic variables were collected through standardized questionnaires administered by trained interviewers. Weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using a digital scale, with participants wearing light clothing and no shoes. Height was measured to the nearest 0.5 cm using a stadiometer. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Waist circumference (WC) was measured at the midpoint between the lowest rib and the iliac crest to the nearest 0.5 cm using a non-elastic tape.

Systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP and DBP) were measured twice on the right arm after a 15-min rest in a seated position using a standardized mercury sphygmomanometer, calibrated by the Iranian Institute of Standards and Industrial Research20. The mean of the two measurements was used for analysis. Physical activity was assessed using the Modifiable Activity Questionnaire (MAQ), which evaluates the frequency and duration of various leisure-time and occupational physical activities over the past year. Physical activity levels were expressed as metabolic equivalent task (MET) minutes per week. Low physical activity was defined as < 600 MET-minutes per week according to standard criteria21. The validity and reliability of the Persian version of the MAQ have been previously established22.

Venous blood samples were collected after 12–14 h of overnight fasting. Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and serum lipids were determined by enzymatic colorimetric assays: FPG with glucose oxidase, triglycerides (TG) with glycerol phosphate oxidase, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) with phosphotungstic acid. All biochemical analyses were performed at the TLGS research laboratory, using Pars Azmoon kits (Pars Azmoon Inc., Tehran, Iran) and a Selectra 2 auto-analyzer (Vital Scientific, Spankeren, The Netherlands). Coefficients of variation for inter- and intra-assay measurements were consistently below 5% during both baseline and follow-up phases.

Outcome ascertainment

Incident CVD was defined as the first occurrence of coronary heart disease (CHD), cerebrovascular accident (stroke), or cardiovascular death during follow-up. CHD included cases of definite myocardial infarction (MI) diagnosed by cardiac enzymes and electrocardiographic findings, probable MI diagnosed by electrocardiographic findings plus cardiac symptoms or by cardiac enzymes plus cardiac symptoms, and unstable angina pectoris diagnosed by symptoms. Stroke was defined as a new neurological deficit lasting > 24 h, confirmed by a neurologist. Cardiovascular death included deaths attributed to CHD or stroke. All suspected CVD events were adjudicated by an outcome committee of physicians using medical records, electrocardiograms, cardiac biomarkers, and imaging studies according to standard criteria23.

Incident type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) was defined according to the American Diabetes Association criteria as:1 fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L), or2 2-h post-challenge plasma glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) during a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test, or 3 use of glucose-lowering medications (oral hypoglycemic agents or insulin)24.

Hypertension (HTN) was defined as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive medications 25.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of participants were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and as percentages for categorical variables. Differences in baseline characteristics between participants who developed CVD versus those who remained free of CVD were assessed using independent t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables.

Participants were categorized into tertiles based on the distribution of CQI and each of its four components (fiber intake, dietary GI, whole grain ratio, and solid carbohydrate ratio). The lowest tertile (T1) was used as the reference category in all analyses. P for trend was calculated by assigning the median value of each tertile to participants within that tertile and modeling this variable as a continuous term in the Cox regression models. For the main analyses, CQI was categorized into tertiles, and additionally modeled as a continuous variable (per SD increase), to facilitate interpretation. The tertile-based approach was chosen for consistency with prior CQI literature and to facilitate clinical interpretation.

Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for incident CVD across tertiles of CQI and its components. Component analyses were conducted separately for each CQI component (fiber intake, glycemic index, whole grain ratio, solid carbohydrate ratio) using the same sequential modeling approach. Each component was examined independently without mutual adjustment for other components to avoid potential collider bias and to assess the independent contribution of each dimension of carbohydrate quality. Person-time was calculated from the baseline examination (2006–2008) until the date of outcome occurrence, death, loss to follow-up, or the end of follow-up (2018), whichever came first. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals and was satisfied for all models.

We constructed sequential multivariable models with increasing levels of adjustment:

Crude model: Unadjusted associations.

Model 1: Adjusted for age, sex, smoking status, and physical activity.

Model 2: Additionally, adjusted for total energy intake, total fat intake, and saturated fat intake. Adjustment for total energy intake in the same model as CQI ensures that the CQI coefficient reflects variation in carbohydrate quality at a constant level of total energy intake, effectively isolating quality from quantity. This model represents the total effect of CQI on CVD risk, independent of total caloric intake and fat intake, but excluding potential mediators on the causal pathway.

Model 3: Additionally, adjusted for BMI, T2DM, and HTN status. Because these variables may lie on the causal pathway between carbohydrate quality and CVD (e.g., CQI → improved glycemic control → reduced diabetes → reduced CVD), Model 3 may represent over-adjustment and should be interpreted as a direct effect estimate.

To explicitly verify the independence of CQI from total carbohydrate quantity, two additional sensitivity analyses were performed:

Model 4a: Model 3 additionally adjusted for total carbohydrate intake (g/day), directly isolating the effect of carbohydrate quality from absolute carbohydrate quantity.

Model 4b: Model 3 additionally adjusted for carbohydrate intake as a percentage of total energy (%E), providing an alternative operationalization of carbohydrate quantity.

Furthermore, to address the potential limited discriminatory contribution of the whole grain ratio component in this Iranian population — where whole grain consumption is low (mean whole grain ratio: 0.07 ± 0.11) — we conducted two pre-specified sensitivity analyses. First, we recomputed the CQI excluding the whole grain ratio component, yielding a modified 3-component index (dietary fiber intake + dietary glycemic index + solid carbohydrate ratio; score range: 3–9) hereafter referred to as “Modified CQI.” Second, we constructed a population-adapted carbohydrate quality index (PAI) by replacing the whole grain ratio with a legume intake score. Legume intake was defined as the sum of daily servings of lentils, chickpeas, and kidney/white beans — the most commonly consumed legumes in the TLGS population — and scored in tertiles1–3. The PAI was thus computed as: dietary fiber score + glycemic index score + legume score + solid carbohydrate ratio score (range: 4–12). Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to both modified indices using the same sequential covariate adjustment as the primary analysis (Models 1–3).

Mediation analysis

To formally quantify the direct and indirect effects of CQI on CVD incidence, we conducted a causal mediation analysis using the regression-based product-of-coefficients approach within a counterfactual framework26,27. We hypothesized that the association between CQI and CVD may be partially mediated through three biologically plausible intermediate variables: BMI, incident T2DM, and incident HTN. These variables represent established mechanistic pathways linking dietary carbohydrate quality to CVD outcomes.

The total effect (TE) of CQI on CVD was estimated from a Cox proportional hazards model adjusted for baseline confounders only (age, sex, smoking status, physical activity, total energy intake, total fat intake, and saturated fat intake), equivalent to Model 2. The natural direct effect (NDE) was estimated from the fully adjusted model additionally including all three mediators (equivalent to Model 3). The natural indirect effect (NIE) was computed as the difference between the total and direct effects on the log-hazard scale (NIE = log(HR_TE) − log(HR_NDE)), and exponentiated to obtain HR_NIE. Mediator-specific NIEs were estimated using the product-of-coefficients method: for each mediator, the effect of CQI on the mediator (α) was estimated using linear regression (BMI) or logistic regression (T2DM, HTN), and multiplied by the effect of the mediator on CVD (β) from the fully adjusted Cox model. The proportion mediated was calculated as NIE/TE × 100 on the log scale. Bootstrap resampling (200 iterations) was used to derive 95% confidence intervals for all mediation estimates.

Post-hoc statistical power was calculated using the Schoenfeld (1983) formula for Cox proportional hazards models13. For the continuous CQI analysis (per 1 standard deviation [SD] increment), with 159 incident CVD events and a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05, the study had 80% power to detect a minimum hazard ratio of 0.801 (a ≥ 20% reduction in CVD risk per SD increment in CQI) and 90% power to detect HR ≤ 0.773. For the tertile comparison (T3 vs T1), based on 123 events in the T1 and T3 subgroups, the study had 80% power to detect HR ≤ 0.603 and 90% power to detect HR ≤ 0.557. The power curve across a range of plausible hazard ratios is presented in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Causal mediation analysis was performed using the mediation package in R (version 4.x, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), while all other statistical analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) software (version 20; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with P-values < 0.05 being considered significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The study included 2,648 participants free of CVD at baseline. During a median follow-up of 10.6 years (interquartile range: 10.0–11.2 years), 171 CVD cases (6.5%) were identified, corresponding to an overall incidence rate of 6.1 per 1000 person-years. Incidence rates across CQI tertiles were: T1: 6.8 per 1000 person-years; T2: 5.9 per 1000 person-years; T3: 5.7 per 1000 person-years, showing no clear gradient. The mean CQI score was 8.01 ± 1.64 (range: 4–12), with tertile cut points at ≤ 7, 7–9, and ≥ 9. The mean values for CQI components were as follows: dietary fiber 37.6 ± 20.9 g/day (coefficient of variation [CV] = 56%), dietary glycemic index 60.5 ± 5.3 (CV = 9%), whole grain ratio 0.06 ± 0.11 (CV = 183%; standard deviation exceeding the mean, indicating that the vast majority of values were near zero with minimal between-person variability), and solid carbohydrate ratio 0.08 ± 0.69. The extremely low and homogeneous whole grain ratio reflects the predominance of refined white flatbreads (lavash, sangak, barbari) as staple grain sources in the Iranian diet, with limited consumption of Western-style whole grain products.

Baseline characteristics of participants according to incident CVD are presented in Table 1. Participants who developed CVD during follow-up were significantly older (56.37 ± 10.20 vs. 37.99 ± 13.10 years, P-value < 0.05), had higher BMI (28.35 ± 4.42 vs. 26.82 ± 4.88 kg/m2, P-value < 0.05), greater WC (97.50 ± 10.60 vs. 88.68 ± 13.29 cm, P-value < 0.05), and higher SBP (126.8 ± 20.98 vs. 110.4 ± 15.60 mmHg, P-value < 0.05) and DBP (79.25 ± 11.49 vs. 72.95 ± 10.34 mmHg, P-value < 0.05) compared to those who remained free of CVD. A higher proportion of CVD cases were male (65.5% vs. 42.9%, P-value < 0.05), current smokers (18.1% vs. 12.0%, P-value < 0.05), with T2DM (18.8% vs. 5.5%, P-value < 0.05) and HTN (37.9% vs. 10.3%, P-value < 0.05). However, no significant differences were observed in total energy intake, carbohydrate intake, protein intake, or total fat intake between those who developed CVD and those who did not.

To explicitly isolate the effect of carbohydrate quality from carbohydrate quantity, we additionally adjusted for total carbohydrate intake — both in absolute terms (Model 4a: HR per SD = 0.940, 95% CI: 0.807–1.096, p = 0.430) and as a percentage of total energy (Model 4b: HR per SD = 0.946, 95% CI: 0.811–1.103, p = 0.477). These estimates were virtually identical to those from Model 3 (HR = 0.949), confirming that the CQI–CVD association is independent of total carbohydrate quantity (Supplementary Table 1).

Notably, the solid carbohydrate ratio in the highest tertile showed a point estimate suggesting potential protection (HR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.46–1.05, Model 3), though the confidence interval included the null. This borderline finding may warrant further investigation in larger samples.

To assess potential non-linear associations, CQI was modeled as a continuous variable (per SD increase: HR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.83–1.10, Model 3), suggesting no evidence of non-linear associations. These findings indicate that the null results were consistent across different modeling approaches and were not artifacts of categorization.

Mediation analysis

The formal causal mediation analysis revealed that the total effect of CQI on CVD incidence per 1-SD increment was HR = 0.948 (95% CI: 0.832–1.098), consistent with our Model 2 estimate. The natural direct effect (NDE), representing the effect of CQI independent of the three mediators, was HR = 0.942 (95% CI: 0.835–1.082), virtually identical to the total effect. The total natural indirect effect (NIE) was HR = 1.007 (95% CI: 0.975–1.045), indicating that BMI, incident T2DM, and incident HTN together mediated a negligible and non-significant proportion of the CQI–CVD association. Mediator-specific analyses showed that the NIE via BMI (HR = 1.004, 95% CI: 0.998–1.013) and via HTN (HR = 1.041, 95% CI: 0.917–1.326) were non-significant. The NIE via incident diabetes was nominally significant (HR = 1.182, 95% CI: 1.004–1.445), though this likely reflects residual confounding rather than true mediation, given the direction of the estimate. The near-equivalence of the NDE and TE confirms that the inclusion of these variables in Model 3 did not result in meaningful over-adjustment. Full mediation results are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Sensitivity analysis

Results of the two sensitivity analyses are presented in Supplementary Table 3. When the whole grain ratio component was completely omitted from the CQI (Modified CQI; 3 components: fiber + GI + solid CHO), the null association with CVD incidence was maintained across all models (T3 vs. T1: HR = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.39–1.13, p-for-trend = 0.184 in Model 3). Similarly, the population-adapted index (PAI), in which legume intake replaced the whole grain ratio, showed no significant association with CVD incidence (T3 vs. T1: HR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.57–1.50, p-for-trend = 0.851 in Model 3). These results were consistent across crude and all adjusted models. The direction and magnitude of associations for the Modified CQI were broadly similar to the original CQI, while the PAI showed attenuated point estimates with wider confidence intervals, likely reflecting the lower between-individual variability in legume intake in this population (mean ± SD: 0.23 ± 0.25 servings/day).

Age-stratified analyses (< 50 vs. ≥ 50 years) and formal interaction testing revealed no significant CQI × age interaction (p-interaction > 0.05), with null associations consistent across strata (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

In this prospective analysis of 2,648 Iranian adults followed for a median of 10.6 years, we found no significant associations between the CQI or its individual components and incident cardiovascular disease. While null findings require careful interpretation, several methodological and contextual factors may explain these results and warrant consideration.

Among individual CQI components, the solid carbohydrate ratio showed the strongest (though non-significant) inverse association with CVD. This component distinguishes solid from liquid carbohydrate sources and was designed to capture the adverse effects of sugar-sweetened beverages. The borderline protective association may reflect the importance of limiting liquid sugars in this population, though the wide confidence interval reflects limited statistical power. Although sugar-sweetened beverage consumption in Iranian adults may be lower than that reported in many Western populations, variation in liquid carbohydrate intake may still reflect broader differences in dietary patterns and consumption of processed foods. Therefore, the observed inverse trend for the solid-to-total carbohydrate ratio may indicate potential metabolic benefits of obtaining carbohydrates predominantly from solid food sources rather than beverages. Future studies with larger sample sizes may clarify whether this component has particular relevance for CVD prevention in Middle Eastern populations.

Interpretation of null findings in context

The lack of significant associations between CQI and CVD outcomes in our study contrasts with some, but not all, prior research. A recent systematic review concluded that while qualitative evidence suggests inverse associations between CQI and cardio-metabolic risk factors, quantitative evidence remains insufficient and inconsistent across populations and study designs8. Several well-conducted prospective cohort studies have similarly reported null or inconsistent findings for composite CQI and hard cardio-metabolic endpoints28,29. For example, a large prospective analysis from the China Health and Nutrition Survey found U-shaped associations for glycemic index and carbohydrate-to-fiber ratios with incident T2DM, with CQI showing associations only in certain subgroups28. Similarly, while the UK Biobank demonstrated consistent inverse associations for fiber and benefits of substituting refined grains with whole grains for CVD risk, composite CQI-style indices have produced more variable results across different studies and populations30,31. Notably, Zazpe et al. reported a significant inverse association between a dietary carbohydrate index and CVD incidence in the SUN cohort (a Western Mediterranean population), suggesting that population-specific dietary patterns may modulate the strength of this association13.

A critical methodological consideration in studies of carbohydrate quality is the potential confounding by carbohydrate quantity. Since higher CQI scores in our cohort were associated with higher absolute carbohydrate intake (T1: 288 g/day vs. T3: 388 g/day) and a higher carbohydrate energy percentage (T1: 55.5% vs. T3: 60.9%), failure to account for quantity could conflate quality effects with quantity effects. Our primary models addressed this by adjusting for total energy intake in Model 2, which mathematically constrains the CQI coefficient to reflect quality variation at constant energy intake. To provide explicit confirmation, we additionally adjusted for total carbohydrate intake (g/day) and carbohydrate percentage of energy in Models 4a and 4b, respectively. The CQI effect estimate remained essentially unchanged across all four adjusted models, providing strong evidence that the observed association — and its statistical non-significance — reflects the independent biological effect of carbohydrate quality and is not attributable to differences in carbohydrate quantity between tertiles.

When a well-powered, methodologically rigorous cohort yields null associations, interpretation should systematically consider measurement issues, statistical power, biological plausibility, and contextual factors that may differ across populations. Below, we discuss several potential explanations for our null findings and place them in the broader context of nutritional epidemiology.

Measurement error and dietary assessment limitations

Dietary assessment in observational studies is inherently subject to measurement error, which typically attenuates associations toward the null32. In TLGS, as in most large cohort studies, dietary intakes were assessed using a semi-quantitative FFQ administered at baseline. While the TLGS FFQ has been validated against multiple dietary recalls and biomarkers in this population17, FFQs remain imperfect instruments for capturing habitual intake, particularly for components of the CQI.

Several specific measurement challenges are relevant to CQI assessment. First, dietary fiber intake is notoriously difficult to measure accurately via FFQ, as fiber content varies substantially across food preparation methods and sources that may not be fully captured in food composition databases33. Second, assigning glycemic index values to reported foods requires assumptions about food varieties, preparation methods, and combinations, introducing considerable error, especially in populations with diverse traditional cooking practices8,28. Third, the whole grain to total grain ratio depends on accurate classification of grain products as “whole” versus "refined," which varies by cultural context—traditional Iranian breads and coarse grains differ markedly from Western whole-grain products, potentially misclassifying the biological exposure captured by this component30,34. Fourth, the solid carbohydrate to total carbohydrate ratio aims to distinguish liquid sugars from solid starches, but FFQs may not adequately capture all sources of added sugars in beverages and processed foods31.

Furthermore, dietary intake was measured only at baseline in our study, and dietary patterns likely changed over the follow-up periods of 10.6 years. This represents a critical limitation, as single-point dietary assessment introduces substantial non-differential misclassification that typically biases associations toward the null. Repeated dietary measurements would reduce misclassification and better capture long-term habitual intake35. The combination of these measurement limitations would be expected to bias our results toward the null and reduce statistical power to detect modest associations.

The discussion acknowledges measurement error, but quantitative correction methods (e.g., regression calibration) would require validation sub-study data with repeated dietary measurements or objective biomarkers that were not available for this analysis. While the TLGS FFQ has been validated against multiple dietary recalls and biomarkers in this population, FFQs remain imperfect instruments with substantial random and systematic error. This error typically biases effect estimates toward the null (regression dilution bias). Given that our study already reports null findings, it is critical to recognize that measurement error may have substantially attenuated true associations. Future studies incorporating repeated dietary assessments and objective biomarkers (e.g., plasma alkylresorcinols for whole grains, urinary sugars for added sugar intake) would enable quantitative bias correction and provide more accurate effect estimates.

Insufficient exposure contrast and population-specific dietary patterns

A critical consideration in null findings is whether the population has sufficient variability in the exposure of interest to detect associations35. If the range of CQI scores in our TLGS sample was narrow, or if most participants consumed similar types and amounts of carbohydrates, statistical power to detect associations would be reduced.

Middle Eastern dietary patterns differ substantially from Western and East Asian patterns that dominate the carbohydrate quality literature15. In urban Tehran, refined wheat bread (particularly white flatbreads such as lavash) is a dietary staple, contributing a large proportion of daily carbohydrate intake14. Whole grain consumption in Iran may be lower and qualitatively different from Western whole-grain products, potentially limiting the exposure contrast for the whole grain ratio component of CQI. Indeed, our component analysis showed that the whole grain ratio was not significantly associated with any outcome after adjustment (HR for T3 vs. T1 = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.59–1.28; p-trend = 0.458), consistent with insufficient variability to detect an effect even if one exists.

This limited exposure contrast raises fundamental concerns about the “construct validity” of the CQI in this population. Construct validity refers to the degree to which an instrument measures the theoretical construct it purports to measure26,27,36. In response to the concerns regarding the construct validity of the whole grain ratio in this population, we conducted two sensitivity analyses. The modified CQI (excluding whole grain) and the population-adapted index (incorporating legume intake) both yielded null associations with CVD incidence, consistent with the primary analysis. These findings suggest that the null results are not attributable solely to the poor discriminatory power of the whole grain component, but rather reflect a broader pattern of non-significant associations between carbohydrate quality and CVD risk in this Iranian cohort. The legume-based PAI, while theoretically more appropriate for a Middle Eastern dietary context — given that legumes such as lentils, chickpeas, and beans are traditional staples — also failed to uncover significant associations, possibly due to insufficient statistical power, limited exposure contrast in legume intake, or true null effects in this population. These sensitivity analyses strengthen the robustness of our primary findings and are consistent with the interpretation that methodological limitations (limited power, single dietary assessment) rather than index misspecification are the primary explanation for the null results.

Additionally, sources of dietary carbohydrates in Iran include substantial amounts of rice, refined breads, and traditional sweets, with lower consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages compared to Western populations14. The solid carbohydrate component of CQI was designed primarily to capture the adverse effects of liquid sugars (e.g., sodas, fruit juices), which may be less relevant in populations with lower beverage-derived sugar intake31. Conversely, if liquid sugar intake is already low in the population, this component may show little variation and contribute minimally to the overall CQI score.

On the other hand, the 18-year age difference between CVD cases and non-cases represents a major source of confounding. While we adjusted for age as a continuous variable in all models, residual confounding by age-related factors (e.g., cohort effects, cumulative exposures, biological aging processes) cannot be excluded. The strong age gradient may have overwhelmed more modest dietary effects, contributing to null findings.

Biological mechanisms and null composite associations

From a biological perspective, the individual CQI components are hypothesized to influence cardio-metabolic health through several mechanisms. Dietary fiber, particularly soluble fiber, improves glycemic control by slowing carbohydrate absorption, enhances satiety, promotes beneficial gut microbiota, and reduces cholesterol absorption37,38. Whole grains contain fiber, vitamins, minerals, and bioactive compounds (e.g., polyphenols, lignans) that may reduce inflammation, improve insulin sensitivity, and favorably modulate lipid metabolism30. Low glycemic index foods produce smaller postprandial glucose and insulin excursions, potentially reducing beta-cell stress and insulin resistance over time39. Solid carbohydrates, compared to liquid sugars, promote greater satiety and less rapid glucose absorption, reducing over-consumption and metabolic dysregulation40,41.

However, these mechanisms may not operate uniformly across all populations and dietary contexts. Inter-individual variability in glycemic response to identical foods is substantial and influenced by genetic factors, gut microbiome composition, physical activity, and food matrix effects42. If the TLGS population exhibits high inter-individual variability in metabolic responses to carbohydrate quality, population-level associations may be attenuated. Additionally, if other dietary components (e.g., saturated fat, sodium, overall energy balance) or non-dietary factors (e.g., physical inactivity, genetic predisposition) are stronger determinants of CVD risk in this population, the independent contribution of carbohydrate quality may be modest and difficult to detect.

Furthermore, the composite CQI assumes that its four components have additive effects on cardio-metabolic health. However, interactions among components, or between components and other dietary factors, may be non-additive. For example, the metabolic impact of glycemic index may depend on the amount and type of fat consumed with carbohydrates, or on overall dietary fiber intake43. If such interactions are important, a simple additive CQI score may not adequately capture the true relationship between carbohydrate quality and CVD risk.

To further address the concern of potential over-adjustment in Model 3, we conducted a formal causal mediation analysis decomposing the total effect of CQI into its direct and indirect components. The results demonstrated that BMI, incident T2DM, and incident HTN collectively mediated a negligible proportion of the CQI–CVD association, with the NIE being small and statistically non-significant. The near-identical total effect and natural direct effect provide formal statistical evidence that adjusting for these variables in Model 3 did not meaningfully attenuate the CQI estimate through over-adjustment. These findings are consistent with the broader literature suggesting that the cardio-metabolic pathways through which dietary carbohydrate quality influences CVD risk are complex and multifactorial, and are not fully captured by single intermediate variables such as BMI or glycemic status36,44. Notably, the nominally significant NIE via incident diabetes warrants cautious interpretation: in this Iranian cohort, higher CQI scores were not associated with lower diabetes incidence in the fully adjusted model, suggesting that the metabolic pathways through which carbohydrate quality influences CVD may differ from those previously described in Western populations—possibly reflecting the distinct carbohydrate food matrix (predominantly refined flatbreads) consumed in this population.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths. First, TLGS is a well-established prospective cohort with standardized data collection, validated dietary assessment tools, and adjudicated CVD outcomes16. Second, our median follow-up periods were substantial (10.6 years), allowing for adequate time for disease incidence. Third, we carefully adjusted for multiple potential confounders, including demographic, lifestyle, and dietary factors. Fifth, prospective data from a Middle Eastern cohort with distinct dietary patterns adds to the evidence base on carbohydrate quality across diverse populations.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, a key limitation of the present study is the restricted statistical power for detecting subtle associations between CQI and CVD incidence. With 159 incident CVD events over a median follow-up of 10.6 years, the study had adequate power (80%) to detect a hazard ratio of ≤ 0.801 per standard deviation increment in CQI (continuous analysis), corresponding to a ≥ 20% risk reduction. However, for the tertile analysis (T3 vs T1), the study had only approximately 51–67% power to detect the observed hazard ratio of 0.69, as reflected in the wide 95% confidence interval (0.43–1.09) crossing the null. The minimum detectable hazard ratio at 80% power for the tertile comparison was 0.603, indicating that the study was adequately powered only for large effect sizes (≥ 40% risk reduction) in this analysis. Consequently, we cannot definitively rule out a Type II error for the tertile-based analysis; the observed inverse trend (p-trend = 0.130) may reflect a true moderate protective association that the current sample size lacked sufficient power to confirm. Future studies with larger sample sizes or longer follow-up periods, particularly in Middle Eastern populations, are warranted to clarify the dose–response relationship between carbohydrate quality and CVD risk. Second, dietary assessment via FFQ is subject to measurement error, and dietary intake was assessed only at baseline. Consequently, regression dilution bias due to within-person changes in dietary intake over time may have attenuated the observed associations toward the null. Repeated measurements of CQI during follow-up would provide a more accurate assessment of long-term dietary exposure. Third, although socioeconomic status (SES)–including household income, educational attainment, and occupational class–is a recognized upstream driver of both dietary quality and cardiovascular outcomes, these variables were deliberately excluded from our Cox proportional hazards models. This decision was governed by a strict methodological constraint: with only 171 incident CVD events, the recommended minimum of 10 events per variable (EPV) for Cox regression45 permitted inclusion of a maximum of approximately 17 covariates. Priority was therefore assigned to established, biologically proximal confounders (age, sex, BMI, physical activity, total energy and fats intake, smoking status, T2DM and HTN), in accordance with the pre-specified analytical plan. Inclusion of additional variables such as SES indicators, alcohol consumption, or kidney function (eGFR) would have exceeded this EPV threshold, risking model overfitting, parameter instability, and inflated standard errors. Nevertheless, residual confounding by these excluded factors cannot be excluded. With respect to SES specifically, the dual pathways through which it may influence our findings are well-established: in Iranian populations, higher educational attainment is associated with substantially better diet quality46, while lower SES predicts markedly higher cardiovascular mortality in Tehran47 and globally48. Consequently, if lower-SES participants in our cohort had simultaneously poorer carbohydrate quality and higher CVD risk, unmeasured SES could attenuate the observed diet-CVD association toward the null–consistent with the direction of our findings. It is worth noting, however, that TLGS participants were recruited from district 13 of Tehran, a relatively urban, middle-class neighborhood, which may reduce–though not eliminate–SES heterogeneity compared to nationally representative samples. Future studies with larger event numbers should incorporate comprehensive SES measures to formally disentangle the independent contribution of carbohydrate quality from socioeconomic pathways.

Moreover, the substantial age difference between CVD cases and non-cases may have contributed to residual confounding despite multivariable adjustment. Given the strong influence of age on cardiovascular risk, age-related factors and cumulative lifetime exposures may have overshadowed relatively modest dietary effects, potentially attenuating associations toward the null. Fourth, a substantial proportion of initially eligible participants were excluded because of incomplete dietary data. This exclusion may have introduced selection bias if participants with complete dietary assessments differed systematically from those excluded in terms of dietary habits, health status, or socioeconomic characteristics. Consequently, the generalizability of our findings to the broader TLGS population may be limited. Fifth, our findings are specific to an urban Iranian population and may not generalize to other Middle Eastern populations or to rural Iranian populations with different dietary patterns. Sixth, age-stratified analyses and interaction testing were performed and yielded no significant effect modification (p-interaction > 0.05); however, limited event numbers within strata reduce statistical power to detect subgroup differences, and residual confounding by age-related factors cannot be fully excluded. Seventh, the CQI used in our study was based on a standardized definition from the literature8, but alternative formulations or population-specific weighting of components might yield different results.

Clinical and public health implications

Our null findings have several important clinical implications. First, carbohydrate quality indices developed in Western populations may not directly translate to CVD risk prediction in Iranian adults consuming traditional Middle Eastern diets, suggesting caution in applying Western- derived recommendations without considering population-specific contexts. Second, the absence of associations does not imply carbohydrate quality is unimportant; rather, the specific CQI formulation may not optimally capture relevant dimensions in the Iranian dietary context. Individual healthy carbohydrate choices—such as increasing fiber from legumes and vegetables, choosing traditional whole grain breads, and limiting added sugars—remain evidence-based recommendations supported by mechanistic studies49. Third, our findings highlight the importance of comprehensive dietary assessment beyond single indices. In the Iranian context, other dietary factors (types of fats, sodium intake, and overall dietary patterns emphasizing vegetables, fruits, legumes, and fish) may be more important modifiable CVD risk factors than carbohydrate quality alone. Clinicians should adopt holistic dietary counseling addressing multiple components simultaneously. Finally, Iranian dietary guidelines should be informed by local evidence and consider traditional food sources and culinary practices rather than direct extrapolation from Western studies. Improving overall diet quality, including carbohydrate quality, remains a reasonable goal as part of comprehensive cardiovascular risk reduction strategies15.

Conclusions

In this prospective cohort study of Iranian adults, we observed no statistically significant association between the CQI and incident CVD. However, the findings should be interpreted cautiously because limited statistical power and measurement error may have attenuated modest associations. Therefore, the present results should be considered inconclusive rather than evidence of absence of association. Larger prospective studies with repeated dietary assessments are needed to clarify the role of carbohydrate quality in cardiovascular disease prevention in Middle Eastern populations.
