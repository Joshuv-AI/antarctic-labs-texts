Transformers are almost optimal metalearners for linear classification
Roey Magen Weizmann Institute of Science roey.magen@weizmann.ac.il Gal VardiWeizmann Institute of Science gal.vardi@weizmann.ac.il

Abstract
Transformers have demonstrated impressive in-context learning (ICL) capabilities, raising the question of whether they can serve as metalearners that adapt to new tasks using only a small number of in-context examples, without any further training. While recent theoretical work has studied transformers’ ability to perform ICL, most of these analyses do not address the formal metalearning setting, where the objective is to solve a collection of related tasks more efficiently than would be possible by solving each task individually. In this paper, we provide the first theoretical analysis showing that a simplified transformer architecture trained via gradient descent can act as a near-optimal metalearner in a linear classification setting. We consider a natural family of tasks where each task corresponds to a class-conditional Gaussian mixture model, with the mean vectors lying in a shared k k -dimensional subspace of ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} . After training on a sufficient number of such tasks, we show that the transformer can generalize to a new task using only O ~ ​ ( k / R ~ 4 ) \widetilde{O}(k/\widetilde{R}^{4}) in-context examples, where R ~ \widetilde{R} denotes the signal strength at test time. This performance (almost) matches that of an optimal learner that knows exactly the shared subspace and significantly outperforms any learner that only has access to the in-context data, which requires Ω ⁡ ( d / R ~ 4 ) \Omega(d/\widetilde{R}^{4}) examples to generalize. Importantly, our bounds on the number of training tasks and examples per task needed to achieve this result are independent of the ambient dimension d d .



1
Introduction

Transformer-based models are the dominant architecture in both natural language processing (NLP) and computer vision. Since their introduction by
Vaswani et al. 2017
, transformers have been scaled to produce remarkable advances in language modeling
[
2
]
, image classification
[
3
]
, and multimodal learning
[
4
]
. Their strength lies in their ability to model complex dependencies through attention mechanisms and to generalize across diverse tasks with minimal task-specific supervision.

One of the most intriguing emergent capabilities of large transformer models is
in-context learning
(ICL). In ICL, a model is given a short sequence of input-output pairs (called a
prompt
) from a particular (possibly new) task, and is asked to make predictions on test examples from that task without any explicit parameter updates. This ability to rapidly adapt to new tasks from a small number of examples, solely by conditioning on the prompt, has been observed in large language models
[
5
]
,
and is central to the ongoing shift toward prompt-based learning paradigms.

The ICL phenomenon is closely connected to the broader framework of
metalearning
, or “learning to learn”
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
, which has been widely studied before. In metalearning, a learner is trained to perform well across a distribution of related tasks, thereby acquiring representations that allow for rapid adaptation to new tasks. It is often helpful to think of tasks as corresponding to individual users. For instance, generating personalized email completions. While each user provides limited task-specific data, such as writing style and personal preferences, there is a rich shared structure across users that can be exploited. Metalearning aims to leverage this structure to improve performance on each task beyond what would be possible if learned independently.

In this paper, we consider binary classification, where we model each task as a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over labeled examples
({\bm{x}},y)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{\pm 1\}
. During training, the learner
has access to
B
datasets, where the
j
-th dataset consists of
N
samples drawn i.i.d from task
\mathcal{D}_{j}
. We further assume that each task labeled by a classifier that relies on a common
representation

h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k}
, where typically
k
is smaller than the ambient dimension
d
. For each task
\mathcal{D}
, there is a classifier
f_{\mathcal{D}}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}
such that
f_{\mathcal{D}}\circ h
has high accuracy on
\mathcal{D}
. Our interest lies in studying families of tasks for which knowing this shared representation
h
substantially reduces the number of samples required to learn each task separately.

Metalearning Objective.
Assuming that the tasks
\mathcal{D}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{D}_{B}
are themselves drawn i.i.d. from an unknown metadistribution
\Omega
, the goal is to output a representation
\hat{h}
that can be specialized to a new unseen task
\mathcal{D}\sim\textit{$\Omega$}
. In the modern view, the model is first trained on tasks
\mathcal{D}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{D}_{B}
, each task contain
N
samples. Then, at test time, the learner is given
M
in-context labeled samples drawn i.i.d. from some new task
\mathcal{D}
, and needs to classify a new sample from
\mathcal{D}
, without further training.
To evaluate performance, mostly the required number of in-context labeled samples
M
that required to ensure (with high probability) small error, we consider two benchmark baselines:

•

Single-task optimal learner:
An optimal algorithm, in terms of the number of required samples, that has access only to samples from the new task
\mathcal{D}
.

•

Optimal learner with access to the ground-truth representation:
An optimal algorithm that has access both to samples of the new task and to the ground-truth representation
h
.

The above discussion naturally motivates the study of transformers from the lens of metalearning. In particular, we are interested in understanding whether, and under what conditions, transformer architectures can act as (optimal)
metalearners
. Specifically,

Can a transformer outperform a
single-task optimal learner
(that only have access to the in-context data), and potentially approach the performance of an
optimal learner with access to the ground-truth representation
?

1.1
Our contributation

To address the question of whether transformers can serve as effective
metalearners
, we analyze their behavior in the well-studied Gaussian mixture framework. In this setting, each task is a random instance of a class-conditional Gaussian mixture model in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with identical spherical covariance and opposite means. The task-specific signal vector
{\bm{\mu}}
is sampled from a shared low-dimensional subspace of dimension
k\leq d
. Formally, for some semi-orthogonal matrix
{\bm{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
and signal strength
R>0
, each task
\tau=1,\dots,B
is defined by:

\displaystyle{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}{\bm{P}}\cdot\mathsf{Unif}(R\cdot{\mathbb{S}}^{k-1}),

\displaystyle y_{\tau,i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{Unif}(\{\pm 1\}),\ \ \ \ \

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}_{\tau,i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{N}({\mathbf{0}},{\bm{I}}_{d}),

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{\tau,i}=y_{\tau,i}{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}+{\bm{z}}_{\tau_{i}}.

At test time, in-context examples are also drawn from a class-conditional Gaussian mixture model, but potentially with a different signal-to-noise ratio determined by a test-time signal strength
\tilde{R}
. That is, the meta-distribution may shift between training and testing. We consider a simplified transformer with linear attention, a setup similar to many prior works on ICL
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
. The transformer is trained via gradient descent (GD) on the logistic or exponential loss over the above random linear classification tasks.

Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We prove that if the transformer is trained on a sufficiently large number of tasks, then the number of in-context samples required at test time to achieve a small constant error on a new task (without parameters update) is
\tilde{O}(k/\tilde{R}^{4})
, where
\tilde{R}
denote the test-time signal strength. In contrast, any single-task learner, which has access only to samples from the new task, require at least
\Omega(d/\tilde{R}^{4})
samples (see our discussion in Remark
2.1
on the information-theoretic lower bound by
Giraud and Verzelen 2019
). To our knowledge, this is the first theoretical result establishing such a guarantee for metalearning with transformers in a linear classification setting.

•

Using the lower bound from
Giraud and Verzelen 2019
, we show that even an
optimal learner with access to the ground-truth representation
, one that knows the true shared low-dimensional subspace
{\bm{P}}
, requires at least
\Omega(k/\tilde{R}^{4})
samples to achieve a small constant error. This implies that transformers, in our setting, are nearly optimal metalearners for linear classification.

•

Our analysis also yields a characterization of the number of pretraining tasks required to generalize effectively at test time. Specifically, we derive an explicit relationship between the number of tasks
B
and the signal strength
R
, which controls the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) during training. We find that it is sufficient to train the transformer on
B=(k/\text{SNR}^{2})
tasks and
N=O(1/\text{SNR}^{2})\lor 1
samples per task, to (almost) match the performance of an optimal metalearner, without any dependence on the ambient dimension
d
.

•

Finally, while
Frei and Vardi 2024
analyze a similar setting without assuming a shared representation (i.e., they assumed
k=d
) and require a strong assumption
R=\Omega(\sqrt{d})
, we show that it suffices to have
R=\tilde{\Omega}(1)
for achieving in-context generalization, even when
k=d
. We note that a single-task optimal learner needs only
O(1)
samples when the signal strength already equals
\Theta(d^{1/4})
, whereas it is information-theoretically impossible to achieve small error when the signal strength is
o(1)
, regardless of how many samples are available (see again Remark
2.1
).
Thus, learning a Gaussian mixture is challenging in the regime where the signal strength is between
\Omega(1)
and
O(d^{1/4})
, and we cover the case where both
R
and
\tilde{R}
are in this regime.

1.2
Related Work

In context learning.

Following the initial experiments of
Garg et al. 2022
, which demonstrated empirically that transformers can perform in-context learning of various function classes, such as linear functions, two-layer neural networks, and decision trees,
a number of works have explored what types of algorithms are implemented by trained transformers.
Akyürek et al. 2022
,
Bai et al. 2023
,
Von Oswald et al. 2023
provided expressivity results showing that transformers can implement a wide range of in-context algorithms such as least squares, ridge regression, Lasso and gradient descent on two-layer neural networks.
Wies et al. 2023
provided a PAC framework for in-context learnability, and established finite sample complexity guarantees.

Huang et al. 2023
investigated the training dynamics of a one-layer transformer with softmax attention trained by GD in a regression setting. Focusing on linear regression,
Wu et al. 2023
established a statistical task complexity bound.
Ahn et al. 2023
and
Mahankali et al. 2023
demonstrated that a one-layer transformer minimizing the pre-training loss effectively implements a single step of gradient descent.
Zhang et al. 2024
additionally developed guarantees for the convergence of (non-convex) gradient flow dynamics.

In the linear
classification
setting,
Shen et al. 2024
showed that a linear transformer trained via gradient descent is equivalent to the optimal logistic regressor, whenever the number of training tasks
B
, the number of samples per task
N
, and the test prompt length
M
, are all tend to infinity.
Li et al. 2025
showed that a single-layer linear attention model can learn the optimal binary classifier under the squared loss, with a focus on semi-supervised learning. The work most closely related to ours is
Frei and Vardi 2024
, who studied the behavior of linear transformers via an analysis of the implicit regularization of gradient descent (similar to our approach). As we already mentioned, they analyzed a setting with a strong signal
R=\Omega(\sqrt{d})
while we allow
R=\tilde{\Omega}(1)
. Moreover, we emphasize that none of the above papers addresses metalearning in the sense studied in our work.

Metalearning.

There is a large body of research on metalearning, often associated with related concepts or alternative names such as multitask learning, transfer learning, learning to learn, and few-shot learning (See
Thrun and Pratt 1998
for an early overview).

Baxter 2000
provided distribution-free sample complexity bounds for metalearning.
A long line of works
[
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
has developed computationally efficient metalearning algorithms, such as MAML (Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning) under various settings, primarily for regression tasks.
Several works consider metalearning with a shared low-dimensional linear representation, which resembles our setting, albeit their metalearners are not related to transformers
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
7
,
34
,
29
]
.
To our knowledge, the only work that explores a form of metalearning in transformers is
Oko et al. 2024
, which studies a linear transformer architecture augmented with a nonlinear MLP layer. For target functions of the form
f({\bm{x}})=\sigma(\langle{\bm{\mu}},{\bm{x}}\rangle)
, where
{\bm{\mu}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
lies in a
k
-dimensional subspace, they show that the model can learn in-context with a prompt length that scales only with
k
. The key differences from our work are: First, their focus is on regression rather than classification. Second, they employ a somewhat artificial two-step optimization procedure – first applying gradient descent on the MLP layer, and only afterwards performing empirical risk minimization (ERM) on the attention layer. In contrast, we consider standard end-to-end gradient descent. Third, they require that the number of tasks and samples during training scales with the ambient dimension
d
.

Implicit Regularization in Transformers.

Our theoretical analysis begins by examining the
implicit regularization
induced by gradient descent in transformer models. We refer readers to the survey by
Vardi 2023
for a broader overview. The convex linear transformer architecture we study is linear in the vectorized parameters, which, following
Soudry et al. 2018
, implies that gradient descent converges in direction to the max-margin classifier. More general transformer architectures are non-convex, but many subclasses exhibit parameter homogeneity and thus converge (in direction) to Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) points of max-margin solutions
[
38
,
39
]
. Another line of work investigates the implicit bias of gradient descent in softmax-based transformers
[
40
,
41
,
42
,
43
]
, typically under stronger assumptions about the structure of the training data.

Preliminaries

Notations.
We use bold-face letters to denote vectors and matrices, and let
[n]
be shorthand for
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. Let
{\bm{I}}_{d}
be the
d\times d
identity matrix, and let
{\mathbf{0}}_{d}
(or just
{\mathbf{0}}
, if
d
is clear from the context) denote the zero vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We let
\left\|\cdot\right\|
denote the Euclidean norm. The Frobenius norm of a matrix is denoted
\left\|{\bm{W}}\right\|_{F}
. We use
a\lor b=\max(a,b)
and

. We use standard big-Oh notation, with
\Theta(\cdot),\Omega(\cdot),O(\cdot)

2.1
Data Generation Setting

We consider the following metadistribution during training:

Assumption 2.1

(training-time task distribution)
.

Fix some
k\leq d
and let
{\bm{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
be a semi-orthogonal matrix, i.e.
{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{P}}={\bm{I}}_{k}
. Let
B,N\geq 1
and signal strength
R>0
. For any task
\tau\in[B]
, the input-label pairs
({\bm{x}}_{\tau,i},y_{\tau,i})_{i=1}^{N+1}
in task
\tau
satisfy the following:

1.

Let
{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}^{\prime}
be sampled i.i.d from the distribution
\mathsf{Unif}(R\cdot\mathbb{{\mathbb{S}}}^{k-1})
, i.e., the uniform distribution on the sphere of radius
R
in
k
dimensions.

2.

Set
{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}={\bm{P}}{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}^{\prime}
to be the isometric embedding of
{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
under
{\bm{P}}
.

3.

Let
{\bm{z}}_{\tau,i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{N}({\mathbf{0}},{\bm{I}}_{d})
, and
y_{\tau,i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{Unif}(\{\pm 1\})
, where
{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}
,
{\bm{z}}_{\tau,i}
and
y_{\tau,i}
are mutually independent.

4.

Conditioned on the task parameter
{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}
, set
{\bm{x}}_{\tau,i}:=y_{\tau,i}{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}+{\bm{z}}_{\tau,i}
.

Thus, the above assumption states that each pretraining task is a class-conditional Gaussian mixture, with two opposite Gaussians, where the direction of the cluster means for each task is drawn randomly from a
k
-dimensional subspace in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Next, we introduce the test-time distribution, which may generalize the pretraining distribution by allowing in-context examples to have a different
cluster mean size and sample size (denoted by
\tilde{R}
and
M+1
)
than those during training (
R
and
N+1
).

Assumption 2.2

(test-time task distribution)
.

Let
M\geq 1
be the number of in-context examples and
\tilde{R}>0
be the signal strength during test time. The input-label pairs
({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})_{i=1}^{M+1}
in the test task satisfy the following:

1.

Let
{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime}
be sampled from the distribution
\mathsf{Unif}(\tilde{R}\cdot\mathbb{{\mathbb{S}}}^{k-1})
, i.e., the uniform distribution on the sphere of radius
\tilde{R}
in
k
dimensions.

2.

Set
{\bm{\mu}}={\bm{P}}{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime}
, where
{\bm{P}}
is from assumption
2.1
, be the isometric embedding of
{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

3.

Let
{\bm{z}}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{N}({\mathbf{0}},{\bm{I}}_{d})
, and
y_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{Unif}(\{\pm 1\})
, where
{\bm{\mu}}
,
{\bm{z}}_{i}
and
y_{i}
are mutually independent.

4.

Conditioned on the task parameter
{\bm{\mu}}
, set
{\bm{x}}_{i}:=y_{i}{\bm{\mu}}+{\bm{z}}_{i}
.

Since the cluster means in our training and test distributions have norms
R
and
\tilde{R}
, and the deviation from the cluster means has a standard Gaussian distribution and hence norm of roughly
\sqrt{d}
, we call the ratios
\frac{R}{\sqrt{d}}
and
\frac{\tilde{R}}{\sqrt{d}}
the
signal-to-noise ratios
(SNR for short).

2.2
Attention Model & Tokenization

In our setting, each example is a task that consists of a sequence of
({\bm{x}},y)
pairs. In order to encode such a task and provide it as an input to the transformer (i.e., to tokenize it), we use the following embedding matrix:

{\bm{E}}=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{x}}_{1}&{\bm{x}}_{2}&\cdots&{\bm{x}}_{N}&{\bm{x}}_{N+1}\\
y_{1}&y_{2}&\cdots&y_{N}&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times(N+1)}.

(1)

That is, each of the
N+1
examples is given in a separate column, and for the
(N+1)
-th example we do not encode the label and place
0
instead. The single-head transformer with softmax attention
[
1
]
is parametrized by query, key, and value matrices:
{\bm{W}}^{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{e}\times d_{e}}
,
{\bm{W}}^{K},{\bm{W}}^{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}\times d_{e}}
. Then, softmax attention is defined by

\displaystyle f({\bm{E}};{\bm{W}}^{K},{\bm{W}}^{Q},{\bm{W}}^{V})={\bm{E}}+{\bm{W}}^{V}{\bm{E}}\cdot\mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{({\bm{W}}^{K}{\bm{E}})^{\top}{\bm{W}}^{Q}{\bm{E}}}{\rho}\right),

(2)

where
\rho
is a fixed normalization that may depend on
N
and
d_{e}
, but is not learned. We focus on
linear transformers
, where the softmax is replaced with the identity function. Following many prior works (e.g.,
Von Oswald et al. 2023
,
Zhang et al. 2024
,
Ahn et al. 2023
), we consider a parameterization where the key and query matrices
{\bm{W}}^{K},{\bm{W}}^{Q}
are merged into
{\bm{W}}^{KQ}:=({\bm{W}}^{K})^{\top}{\bm{W}}^{Q}
. Our objective is to use the first
N
columns of Eq.
1
to predict
{\bm{x}}_{N+1}
.
Similar to prior works, we consider a convex parameterization of the linear transformer, obtained by fixing some of the parameters to
0
or
1
(see details in Appendix
A
), which results in the following prediction for the label of
{\bm{x}}_{N+1}
:

\hat{y}({\bm{E}};{\bm{W}})=\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}{\bm{x}}_{i}\right)^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}_{N+1}.

(3)

Here, the trained parameters are
{\bm{W}}
. We note that the model in Eq.
3
has become a common toy model for analyzing in-context learning both for both regression
[
10
,
9
]
and classification
[
11
,
12
]
.

2.3
Gradient Descent & Implicit Bias

Given a task
\tau
, we define the embedding matrix
{\bm{E}}_{\tau}
using the labeled examples
({\bm{x}}_{\tau,i},y_{\tau_{i}})_{i=1}^{N+1}
from Assumption
2.1
, tokenized according to Eq.
1
. We consider linear transformers (Eq.
3
) trained to minimize the prediction loss on the final token
{\bm{x}}_{\tau,N+1}
, with ground-true label
y_{\tau,N+1}
. Formally, for a training dataset
\{({\bm{E}}_{\tau},y_{\tau,N+1})\}_{\tau=1}^{B}
, we define the empirical loss:

\mathcal{L}({\bm{W}}):=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{\tau=1}^{B}\ell\big(y_{\tau,N+1}\cdot\hat{y}({\bm{E}}_{\tau};{\bm{W}})\big)),

where
\hat{y}(E;W)
is the prediction function from Eq.
3
and
\ell
is either the logistic loss
\ell(z)=\log(1+\exp(-z))
or the exponential loss
\ell(z)=\exp(-z)
.
We train on this objective using gradient descent:
{\bm{W}}_{t+1}={\bm{W}}_{t}-\alpha\nabla\mathcal{L}({\bm{W}}_{t}),
where
\alpha>0
is a fixed learning rate. Since
\hat{y}({\bm{E}}_{\tau};{\bm{W}})
is linear in
{\bm{W}}
, gradient descent has an implicit bias towards maximum-margin solutions, as formalized in the following theorem:

Theorem 2.3

(
Soudry et al. 2018
)
.

Let
{\bm{W}}_{\text{MM}}
denote the solution to the max-margin problem:

{\bm{W}}_{\text{MM}}:=\argmin_{\bm{U}}\|{\bm{U}}\|_{F}^{2}\text{ s.t. }\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{\tau,i}{\bm{x}}_{\tau,i}\right)^{\top}{\bm{U}}y_{\tau,N+1}{\bm{x}}_{\tau,N+1}\geq 1,\,\forall\tau=1,\dots,B.

(4)

If the above
problem is feasible
and the learning rate
\alpha
is sufficiently small, then
{\bm{W}}_{t}
converges in direction to
{\bm{W}}_{\text{MM}}
, that is
\nicefrac{{{\bm{W}}_{t}}}{{\|{\bm{W}}_{t}\|}}\to c{\bm{W}}_{\text{MM}}
, as
t\rightarrow\infty
, for some constant
c>0
.

Thus, the max-margin solution
{\bm{W}}_{\text{MM}}
characterizes the asymptotic behavior of gradient descent for our model. In the remainder of this work, we analyze the ability of this max-margin solution to perform in-context learning and metalearning.

2.4
Information-Theoretic Lower Bounds for Single-Task and Metalearners

We begin by characterizing the minimax test error for algorithms that only have access to the
M
labeled examples from the test task
({\bm{x}}_{1},y_{1}),\dots,({\bm{x}}_{M},y_{M})
, but not to the underlying subspace
{\bm{P}}
, and need to predict the label of
{\bm{x}}_{M+1}
. This models the performance of an
optimal single-task learner
. Importantly, this lower bound holds even when
{\bm{P}}
, as defined in Assumption
2.1
, is drawn uniformly at random from the space of all semi-orthogonal matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
. We emphasize that our main generalization result (Theorem
3.1
) applies in the worst-case setting—i.e., it holds for any fixed
{\bm{P}}
. However, when
{\bm{P}}
is sampled uniformly at random, the induced task mean
{\bm{\mu}}
becomes uniformly distributed on the sphere of radius
\tilde{R}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this setting, we recover the following lower bound:

Remark 2.1

(
Giraud and Verzelen 2019
, Appendix B)
.

The minimax test error for Gaussian classification with identical spherical covariance and opposite means, as defined in Assumption
2.2
with k = d, is at least
c\cdot\exp\left(-c^{\prime}\cdot\min\left\{R^{2},\frac{MR^{4}}{d}\right\}\right),

for some absolute constants
c,c^{\prime}>0
. In particular, when
R=\Omega(1)
, the number of samples required to achieve small constant error must satisfy
M=\Omega(d/R^{4})
. While for
\tilde{R}=o(1)
it is impossible to learn with small error.

We now consider the setting where the learner is granted full access to the underlying subspace
{\bm{P}}
, in addition to the in-context labeled examples and the test point
({\bm{x}}_{1},y_{1}),\dots,({\bm{x}}_{M},y_{M}),{\bm{x}}_{M+1}
. This models the performance of an
optimal learner that knows the shared representation
.

Remark 2.2
.

Consider the same Gaussian classification model, where the mean vectors lie in a low-dimensional subspace
{\bm{P}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, as described in Assumption
2.2
with
k\leq d
. Then, the minimax test error for any algorithm with access to
{\bm{P}}
is at least
c\cdot\exp\left(-c^{\prime}\cdot\min\left\{R^{2},\frac{MR^{4}}{k}\right\}\right)
, for some absolute constants
c,c^{\prime}>0
.

The proof of this remark follows by a direct reduction to the bounds established by
Giraud and Verzelen 2019
and is included in the appendix for completeness.

3
Main Result

We make the following assumptions:

Assumption A
.

Let
\delta>0
be a desired probability of failure. There exists a sufficiently large universal constant
C
(independent in
d,B,k,N
and
\delta
), such that the following conditions hold:

(A1)

The signal strength
R
satisfies:
C\log(B/\delta)\leq R^{2}\leq\frac{d}{C\log^{2}(B/\delta)}

(A2)

Dimension
d
should be sufficiently large:
d\geq C\log^{4}(B/\delta)
.

(A3)

Number of samples per task
N
should be sufficiently large:
N\geq C(d/R^{2})\lor 1

Assumption
(A1)
provides explicit bounds on the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). We emphasize that when
R=o(1)
, learning with small error is information-theoretically impossible (see Remark
2.1
). In contrast, when

\displaystyle\leq 6\exp\left(-\frac{c}{\log^{2}(B/\delta)}\cdot\left(1\wedge\sqrt{\frac{BR^{2}}{dk}}\right)\cdot\left(\sqrt{k}\wedge\tilde{R}\wedge\sqrt{\frac{M\tilde{R}^{4}}{k}}\right)\right)

•

•

In fact, a trained transformer is almost an optimal metalearner: given enough tasks during training, it suffices to have

•

A common assumption in the metalearning literature is that the training and test tasks are drawn from the same metadistribution. In our setting, this corresponds to the case where

Let
{\bm{W}}:={\bm{W}}_{\text{MM}}

{\bm{W}}:=\argmin\{\|{\bm{U}}\|_{F}^{2}:\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}^{\top}{\bm{U}}y_{\tau}{\bm{x}}_{\tau}\geq 1,\,\forall\tau=1,\dots,B\}.

(5)

where
\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{\tau,i}{\bm{x}}_{\tau,i}
, and
({\bm{x}}_{\tau},y_{\tau}):=({\bm{x}}_{\tau,N+1},y_{\tau,N+1})
. For notational simplicity let us denote

({\bm{x}}_{M+1},y_{M+1})=({\bm{x}},y)

\mathbb{P}(\sign(\hat{y}({\bm{E}};{\bm{W}}))\neq y)=\mathbb{P}(\hat{\bm{\mu}}{\bm{W}}y{\bm{x}}<0)
. Using the identity
\sum_{i=1}^{M}y_{i}{\bm{x}}_{i}={\bm{\mu}}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}y_{i}{\bm{z}}_{i}
and by properties of the Gausian, we get:
\hat{\bm{\mu}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}{\bm{\mu}}+M^{-1/2}{\bm{z}}
and

{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{N}({\mathbf{0}},{\bm{I}}_{d})

3
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\hat{y}({\bm{E}};{\bm{W}})\neq y_{M+1})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Big(\left({\bm{\mu}}+M^{-1/2}{\bm{z}}\right)^{\top}{\bm{W}}({\bm{\mu}}+{\bm{z}}^{\prime})<0\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Big({\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{\mu}}<-{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{z}}^{\prime}-M^{-1/2}{\bm{z}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{\mu}}-M^{-1/2}{\bm{z}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big({\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{\mu}}<\left|{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\right|+M^{-1/2}\left|{\bm{z}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{\mu}}\right|+\left|M^{-1/2}{\bm{z}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\right|\Big)

(6)

Recall that
{\bm{\mu}}={\bm{P}}{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime}
, where

{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{Unif}(R\cdot\mathbb{{\mathbb{S}}}^{k-1})

\displaystyle{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{\mu}}\geq\frac{\tilde{R}^{2}}{k}\tr({\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{P}})-\tilde{O}\left(\frac{\tilde{R}\left\|{\bm{W}}\right\|_{F}^{2}}{k}\right),

\displaystyle\left|{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\right|\leq\tilde{O}\left(\frac{\tilde{R}\left\|{\bm{W}}\right\|_{F}}{\sqrt{k}}\right),\ \ \ \left|{\bm{z}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\right|\leq\tilde{O}\left(\left\|{\bm{W}}\right\|_{F}\right)

(7)

Then, our goal becomes:
(i)
Establish a lower bound on

is large and positive.
(ii)
Establish an upper bound on
\|{\bm{W}}\|_{F}
.
Assuming the number of tasks

is large enough, we can show that

and
\left\|{\bm{W}}\right\|_{F}=O(\sqrt{k}/R^{2})
. Substituting these bounds into Eq.

, yields the desired conclusion.

Lower bound on
\tr({\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{P}})
. Using the KKT conditions for the max-margin optimization problem and the fact that
\nabla\hat{y}({\bm{E}}_{\tau};{\bm{W}})=\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}{\bm{x}}_{\tau}^{\top}
, we obtain that there exist
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{B}\geq 0
such that

{\bm{W}}=\sum_{\tau=1}^{B}\lambda_{\tau}y_{\tau}\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}{\bm{x}}_{\tau}^{\top},

(8)

We first show that
\tr({\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{P}})\gtrsim R^{2}\cdot\sum_{\tau=1}^{B}\lambda_{\tau}
, which means it suffices to lower bound

{\bm{W}}
(Eq.
8

5

\tau\in[B]:

\displaystyle 1\leq\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}^{\top}\left(\sum_{q=1}^{B}\lambda_{q}y_{q}\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q}^{\top}\right)y_{\tau}{\bm{x}}_{\tau}=\lambda_{\tau}\|\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}\|^{2}\|{\bm{x}}_{\tau}\|^{2}+\sum_{q:\ q\neq\tau}\lambda_{q}\langle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}\rangle\langle y_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q},{\bm{x}}_{\tau}y_{\tau}\rangle.

Averaging over
\tau

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{B}\sum_{\tau=1}^{B}\lambda_{\tau}\|\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}\|^{2}\|{\bm{x}}_{\tau}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{B}\sum_{q=1}^{B}\lambda_{q}\sum_{\tau:\tau\neq q}\langle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}\rangle\langle y_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q},{\bm{x}}_{\tau}y_{\tau}\rangle

To derive a lower bound on

(up to scaling) that satisfies the constraints of the max-margin problem. Since
{\bm{W}}
is the minimum Frobenius norm matrix that separates all training examples, this implies that

{\bm{\mu}}
lies in a low-dimensional subspace, a natural candidate for

is the projection matrix
{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}^{\top}

is small (i.e.,

{\bm{U}}:=\theta\cdot\sum_{q=1}^{B}y_{q}\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q}^{\top}

, compared to
Frei and Vardi 2024

Remark 4.1
.

Interestingly, our analysis indicates that
{\bm{W}}_{\text{MM}}
exhibits properties similar to those of the projection matrix
{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}^{\top}
, up to a scaling factor of
R^{2}
. Specifically, letting
{\bm{U}}:={\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}^{\top}
, we observe that
\left\|{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{U}}{\bm{P}}\right\|_{F}=\sqrt{k}
and
\tr({\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{U}}{\bm{P}})=k
. If the matrix
{\bm{W}}
defined by the learning rule in
3
indeed corresponds to this projection, then the transformer effectively carries out the following procedure: it first projects the data onto the ground-truth subspace; next, it performs maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) of the signal
{\bm{\mu}}
by averaging the in-context examples (cf. Example 9.11 in
[
44
]
); and finally, it uses this estimate for prediction.
Since this is the Bayes classifier under a Gaussian prior (cf.
Giraud and Verzelen 2019
),
this procedure gives an
optimal learner with access to the ground-truth representation
.

6
Conclusion and Future Direction

We study the sample complexity of metalearning for the Gaussian mixture framework using a pretrained linear transformer. By analyzing gradient descent, we establish a generalization bound that provably competes with any metalearner and outperforms any algorithm that only has access to the in-context examples. Importantly, our bounds do not depend on the ambient dimension, highlighting the transformer’s ability to leverage low-dimensional task structure efficiently.

Our findings underscore the potential of transformers to extract shared representations across diverse but related tasks. This opens several future directions, and encourages extending the metalearning analysis to additional data distributions and transformer architectures, such as deep and multi-head softmax attention.

Moreover, our proof suggests that a trained transformer is closely related to a specific optimal learner
with access to the ground-truth representation, namely to a learner that first projects the data onto the ground-truth subspace, and then performs maximum likelihood estimation (see Remark
4.1
). However, it remains open whether the transformer can exactly mimic this procedure as the number of training tasks approaches infinity.

Finally, although our results indicate that transformers can implement effective metalearning using only a relatively small number of tasks and examples per task during training, independent of the ambient dimension
d
, an interesting open question is to precisely characterize the minimal sample requirements for successful metalearning. In particular, it remains unclear how many tasks and examples per task are sufficient for training an optimal metalearner, what the exact tradeoff is between the number of tasks and examples per task, and how these requirements may differ between transformer-based architectures and more general metalearning frameworks.

\displaystyle+\left|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{q\in[B]:q\neq\tau}\langle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}\rangle\langle{\bm{z}}_{q},{\bm{z}}_{\tau}\rangle\right|.

(34)

Next, we analyze each of the above terms seperatly. Regarding the first term,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{q\in[B]:q\neq\tau}\langle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}\rangle\langle{\bm{\mu}}_{q},{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}\rangle\right|\leq\max_{\tau,q}\left|\langle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}\rangle\right|\left|\langle{\bm{\mu}}_{q},{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}\rangle\right|\leq c_{0}\frac{R^{4}}{k}\log^{2}(B/\delta).

(35)

where the last inequality holds by the first part (third item) of this Lemma and Eq.
12
.
Regarding the second term of Eq.
34
, let’s fix
\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}
and
{\bm{\mu}}_{q}
and observe that
\langle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}\rangle\langle{\bm{\mu}}_{q},{\bm{z}}_{1}\rangle,\dots,\langle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}\rangle\langle{\bm{\mu}}_{q},{\bm{z}}_{B}\rangle
are independent random variables. We emphasize that we currently treat only
{\bm{z}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{z}}_{B}
as random variables, while the other terms are fixed. In other words, the following analysis holds for any realization of
\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}
and
{\bm{\mu}}_{q}
, conditioning on the events of the first part (Eqs.
23
-
29
). By General Hoeffding’s inequality (Thm. 2.6.3 from
Vershynin 2018
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{q\in[B]:q\neq\tau}\langle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}\rangle\langle{\bm{\mu}}_{q},{\bm{z}}_{\tau}\rangle\right|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{ct^{2}B^{2}}{K^{2}\sum_{q:q\neq\tau}\langle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}\rangle^{2}}\right),

where
c
is some constant and
K:=\max_{q:q\neq\tau}\left\|\langle{\bm{\mu}}_{q},{\bm{z}}_{\tau}\rangle\right\|_{\psi_{2}}
. By properties of the Gaussian distribution we have that
\langle{\bm{\mu}}_{q},{\bm{z}}_{\tau}\rangle\sim\mathsf{N}({\mathbf{0}},\left\|{\bm{\mu}}_{q}\right\|^{2})
, which means that
\left\|\langle{\bm{\mu}}_{q},{\bm{z}}_{\tau}\rangle\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=c\left\|{\bm{\mu}}_{q}\right\|=cR
, for some constant
c
. Moreover, by the first part of this lemma (item 3) we have that
\sum_{q:q\neq\tau}\langle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}\rangle^{2}\leq c_{0}BR^{4}\log^{2}(B/\delta)/k
. By choosing
t=\sqrt{R^{6}\log(B/\delta)\log^{2}(2/\delta)/cBk}
, we obtain that with probability at least
1-\delta
, for any
\tau\in[B]
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{q\in[B]:q\neq\tau}\langle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}\rangle\langle{\bm{\mu}}_{q},{\bm{z}}_{\tau}\rangle\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{R^{6}\log^{2}(B/\delta)\log(2/\delta)}{cBk}}\leq\frac{1}{c\cdot\sqrt{c_{B}d}}\cdot\frac{R^{4}}{k}\cdot\log^{2}(B/\delta),

(36)

where the last inequality holds by definition of
B
. The same bound also holds for the third term in Eq.
34
. Regarding the last term of Eq.
34
, we can use again the General Hoeffding’s inequality to obtain,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{q\in[B]:q\neq\tau}\langle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}\rangle\langle{\bm{z}}_{q},{\bm{z}}_{\tau}\rangle\right|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{ct^{2}B^{2}}{K^{2}\sum_{q:q\neq\tau}\langle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}\rangle^{2}}\right),

where now
K:=\max_{q:q\neq\tau}\left\|\langle{\bm{z}}_{q},{\bm{z}}_{\tau}\rangle\right\|_{\psi_{2}}
. Now we have that
\langle{\bm{z}}_{q},{\bm{z}}_{\tau}\rangle\sim\mathsf{N}({\mathbf{0}},\left\|{\bm{z}}_{\tau}\right\|^{2})
, which means that
\left\|\langle{\bm{z}}_{q},{\bm{z}}_{\tau}\rangle\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\left\|{\bm{z}}_{\tau}\right\|\leq 2c\sqrt{d}
. By choosing
t=\sqrt{dR^{4}\log^{2}(B/\delta)\log(2/\delta)/cBk}
, we obtain that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{q\in[B]:q\neq\tau}\langle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}\rangle\langle{\bm{z}}_{q},{\bm{z}}_{\tau}\rangle\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{dR^{4}\log^{2}(B/\delta)\log(2/\delta)}{cBk}}\leq\frac{1}{cR\cdot\sqrt{c_{B}}}\cdot\frac{R^{4}}{k}\log^{2}(B/\delta),

(37)

where the last inequality holds by definition of
B
. Substituting Eqs.
35
,
36
and
37
into Eq.
34
, and observe that
1\lor 1/c_{B}\geq 1/R\sqrt{c_{B}}\lor 1/\sqrt{dc_{B}}
, for any
R,d=\Omega(1)
(Assumptions
(A2)
and
(A3)
), Eq.
31
follows. We note that by union bound, all the events of this lemma occur with probability at least
1-5\delta
.
∎

Our results will require this event to hold, so we introduce the following to allow us to refer to it in later lemmas:

Definition C.1
.

Let us say that a good run occurs if the events of Lemma

hold. By that lemma, this happens with probability at least

over the draws of the training sets.

C.3
Analysis of
{\bm{W}}_{\text{MM}}

Next, we introduce the following lemma regarding the max-margin solution
{\bm{W}}:={\bm{W}}_{\text{MM}}
, as defined in Eq.
4
:

Lemma C.4
.

On a good run and for
C>1
sufficiently large under Assumption
A
, the max-margin solution
{\bm{W}}
of Problem
4
,

{\bm{W}}=\sum_{\tau=1}^{B}\lambda_{\tau}y_{\tau}\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}{\bm{x}}_{\tau}^{\top},

is such that the
\lambda_{\tau}\geq 0
satisfy the following:

\sum_{\tau=1}^{B}\lambda_{\tau}\geq c\cdot\frac{(c_{B}\land 1)}{\log^{2}(B/\delta)}\cdot\frac{k}{R^{4}},

where
c_{0}>1
is some constant.
Further, we have the inequalities

\displaystyle\|{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{P}}\|_{F}\land\|{\bm{W}}\|_{F}\leq(1\land\sqrt{c_{B}})\cdot\frac{\sqrt{k}}{R^{2}},

and

\tr({\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{P}})\geq c\cdot\frac{(c_{B}\land 1)}{\log^{2}(B/\delta)}\cdot\frac{k}{R^{2}},

for some constant
c
. We recall that
B=c_{B}\cdot dk/R^{2}
(Assumption
C.1
).

Proof.

In this part,
c
and
c_{0}
represent some constants that can change from line to line.

•

\tr({\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{P}})
lower bound.
By the feasibility conditions of the max-margin problem, we have for any
\tau\in[B]
,

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\leq y_{\tau}\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}^{\top}\left(\sum_{q=1}^{B}\lambda_{q}y_{q}\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q}^{\top}\right)y_{\tau}{\bm{x}}_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\sum_{q=1}^{B}\lambda_{q}\langle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}\rangle\langle y_{\tau}{\bm{x}}_{\tau},y_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q}\rangle

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\tau}\|\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}\|^{2}\|{\bm{x}}_{\tau}\|^{2}+\sum_{q:\ q\neq\tau}\lambda_{q}\langle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}\rangle\langle y_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q},{\bm{x}}_{\tau}y_{\tau}\rangle.

The above equation also holds if we average over
\tau
i.e.

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{B}\sum_{\tau=1}^{B}\lambda_{\tau}\|\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}\|^{2}\|{\bm{x}}_{\tau}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{B}\sum_{\tau=1}^{B}\sum_{q:\ q\neq\tau}\lambda_{q}\langle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}\rangle\langle y_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q},{\bm{x}}_{\tau}y_{\tau}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{\tau=1}^{B}\lambda_{\tau}\|\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}\|^{2}\|{\bm{x}}_{\tau}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{B}\sum_{q=1}^{B}\lambda_{q}\sum_{\tau:\tau\neq q}\langle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}\rangle\langle y_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q},{\bm{x}}_{\tau}y_{\tau}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\tau=1}^{B}\lambda_{\tau}\frac{\|\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}\|^{2}\|{\bm{x}}_{\tau}\|^{2}}{B}+\sum_{q=1}^{B}\lambda_{q}\cdot\left|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{\tau:\tau\neq q}\langle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}\rangle\langle y_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q},{\bm{x}}_{\tau}y_{\tau}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2R^{4}}{c_{B}k}\sum_{\tau=1}^{B}\lambda_{\tau}+c\left(\frac{1}{c_{B}}\lor 1\right)\frac{R^{4}}{k}\log^{2}(B/\delta)\sum_{q=1}^{B}\lambda_{q},

where the last inequality holds by the second part of Lemma
C.3
. We can conclude that

\sum_{\tau=1}^{B}\lambda_{\tau}\geq c\cdot\frac{(c_{B}\land 1)}{\log^{2}(B/\delta)}\cdot\frac{k}{R^{4}},

which proves the first part of the lemma. The last part of the Lemma regarding
\tr({\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{P}})
following directly from the displayed Equation and Lemma
C.3
(last item).

•

\left\|{\bm{W}}\right\|_{F}
upper bound
. We first derive an upper bound on
\left\|{\bm{W}}\right\|_{F}
by showing the existence of some matrix
{\bm{U}}
(up to some scaling) that satisfies the constraints of the max-margin problem.
Frei and Vardi 2024
used
{\bm{U}}={\bm{I}}_{d}
, so a natural candidate in our case (where the signal
{\bm{\mu}}
is sampled from some low-dimensional subspace) is the projection matrix
{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}^{\top}
. Let
{\bm{U}}:={\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}^{\top}
to be the projection matrix into the columns of
{\bm{P}}
. We first derive an upper bound on
\left\|{\bm{W}}\right\|_{F}
by showing that the matrix
{\bm{U}}
(up to some scaling) satisfies the constraints of the max-margin problem (Problem
4
). Indeed, by Lemma
C.3
(last item), for any
\tau\in[B]
,

\displaystyle\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{\tau}^{\top}{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}^{\top}y_{\tau}{\bm{x}}_{\tau}=\tilde{\Theta}(R^{2}).

Thus the matrix
{\bm{U}}/R^{2}
separates the training data with a margin of at least
1
for every sample. Since W is the minimum Frobenius norm matrix which separates all of the training data with margin
1
,this implies

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{W}}\right\|_{F}\leq\left\|{\bm{U}}/R^{2}\right\|_{F}=\frac{\sqrt{k}}{R^{2}}.

(38)

By Remark
D.3
the same bound also holds for
\|{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{P}}\|_{F}
.

•

\left\|{\bm{W}}\right\|_{F}
upper bound, when number of tasks
B\leq dk
.
The above approach does not yield a tight bound when the number of tasks is small. Instead, we use a different approach and define

\displaystyle{\bm{U}}:=\theta\cdot\sum_{q=1}^{B}y_{q}\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q}^{\top}.

(39)

The idea is to choose specific value for
\theta
and show that for this specific value,
{\bm{U}}
satisfies the constraints of the max-margin problem, then by definition of
{\bm{W}}
(Thm.
2.3
) we can upper bound
\left\|{\bm{W}}\right\|_{F}
by
\left\|{\bm{U}}\right\|_{F}
. We start with a thechnical calculation that will be usefull later in the proof. Since
good run
holds, by Lemma
C.3
(last part), if
B\leq dk
, then for any
\tau\in[B]
we have,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{q:q\neq\tau}\langle\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{\tau},\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q}\rangle\cdot\langle{\bm{x}}_{q},{\bm{x}}_{\tau}\rangle\right|\leq c_{0}\cdot\sqrt{B}\cdot\frac{R^{2}\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{k}}\cdot\log^{2}(B/\delta).

(40)

Next, we move to show that
{\bm{U}}
(Eq.
39
) satisfies the constraints of the max-margin problem. Indeed, for any
\tau\in[B]
,

\displaystyle y_{\tau}\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}^{\top}{\bm{U}}{\bm{x}}_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\theta\cdot\left\|\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{\tau}\right\|^{2}\left\|{\bm{x}}_{\tau}\right\|^{2}+\theta\cdot\sum_{q:q\neq\tau}\langle\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{\tau},\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q}\rangle\langle y_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q},y_{\tau}{\bm{x}}_{\tau}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\theta\cdot\left\|\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{\tau}\right\|^{2}\left\|{\bm{x}}_{\tau}\right\|^{2}-\theta\cdot\left|\sum_{q:q\neq\tau}\langle\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{\tau},\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q}\rangle\langle y_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q},y_{\tau}{\bm{x}}_{\tau}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\geq}\theta\cdot\frac{R^{2}d}{2}-\theta\cdot c_{0}\sqrt{B}\frac{R^{2}\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{k}}\cdot\log^{2}(B/\delta)

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{\geq}1.

(41)

Inequality
(i)
uses Lemma
C.3
and Eq.
40
. Inequality
(ii)
holds by choosing
\theta:=3/(R^{2}d)
and for small enough
B
i.e.
B\leq kd/(C\log^{4}(B/\delta)
. Thus, the matrix
{\bm{U}}
(with
\theta=3/(R^{2}d)
) separates the training data with margin at least
1
for every sample. By Lemma
D.2
we have that

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{U}}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\theta^{2}\cdot\sum_{q=1}^{B}\left\|\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q}\right\|^{2}\left\|{\bm{x}}_{q}\right\|^{2}+\theta^{2}\cdot\sum_{q\neq\ell}\langle\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q},\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{\ell}\rangle\cdot\langle y_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q},y_{\ell}{\bm{x}}_{\ell}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\theta^{2}\cdot\sum_{q=1}^{B}\left\|\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q}\right\|^{2}\left\|{\bm{x}}_{q}\right\|^{2}+\theta^{2}\cdot\sum_{q=1}^{B}\left|\sum_{\ell:\ell\neq q}\langle\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q},\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{\ell}\rangle\cdot\langle y_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q},y_{\ell}{\bm{x}}_{\ell}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\leq}\left(\frac{9}{R^{4}d^{2}}\right)B\left(\frac{3R^{2}d}{2}\right)-\left(\frac{9}{R^{4}d^{2}}\right)B\cdot\left(c_{0}\sqrt{B}\cdot\left(\frac{R^{2}\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{k}}\right)\cdot\log^{2}(B/\delta)\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{\leq}\left(\frac{9}{R^{4}d^{2}}\right)B\cdot\left(3R^{2}d\right).

(42)

Inequality
(i)
uses Lemma
C.3
, Eq.
40
and
\theta=3/(R^{2}d)
. Inequality
(ii)
holds for small enough
B
i.e. whenever

\displaystyle d\geq 2c_{0}\sqrt{B}(\sqrt{d}/\sqrt{k})\log^{2}(B/\delta)\implies B\leq\frac{2c_{0}dk}{\log^{4}(B/\delta)}.

Since
{\bm{W}}
is the minimum Frobenius norm matrix which separates all of the training data with margin
1
, and together with Eq.
42
we have that

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{W}}\right\|_{F}\leq\left\|{\bm{U}}\right\|_{F}\leq\frac{6\sqrt{B}}{\sqrt{d}R}=6\sqrt{c_{B}}\frac{\sqrt{k}}{R^{2}}.

(43)

Combine with Eq.
38
, this proves the upper bound of
\|{\bm{W}}\|_{F}
.
By Remark
D.3
the same bound also holds for
\|{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{P}}\|_{F}
.

∎

C.4
Concentration inequalities of quadratic forms

In this section, we derive concentration inequalities for quadratic forms, which can be viewed as variants of the Hanson–Wright inequality, tailored to the following terms:

•

{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{\mu}}
, where
{\bm{\mu}}={\bm{P}}{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime}
for some matrix
{\bm{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
and
{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime}\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\tilde{R}\cdot\mathbb{S}^{k-1})
.

•

{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{z}}
, where
{\bm{z}}\sim\mathsf{N}({\mathbf{0}},{\bm{I}}_{d})
.

•

{\bm{z}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{z}}
, where
{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\sim\mathsf{N}({\mathbf{0}},{\bm{I}}_{d})
.

{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{\mu}}
:

Lemma C.5

(Hanson-Wright for uniform on the sphere of a subspace)
.

Let
\tilde{R}>0
and
{\bm{W}}\in\tilde{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
be a matrix. If
{\bm{\mu}}={\bm{P}}{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime}
for some matrix
{\bm{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
and
{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime}\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\tilde{R}\cdot\mathbb{S}^{k-1})
, then for any
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{\mu}}-\frac{\tilde{R}^{2}}{k}\tr({\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{P}})\right|\geq t\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\left|{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{\mu}}-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{\mu}}]\right|\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-c\min\left(\frac{t^{2}k^{2}}{\tilde{R}^{4}\|{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{P}}\|_{F}^{2}},\frac{tk}{\tilde{R}^{2}\|{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{P}}\|_{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-c\min\left(\frac{t^{2}k^{2}}{\tilde{R}^{4}\|{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{P}}\|_{F}^{2}},\frac{tk}{\tilde{R}^{2}\|{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{P}}\|_{F}}\right)\right),

where
c>0
is some constant. We also can conclude that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{\mu}}-\frac{\tilde{R}^{2}}{k}\tr({\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{P}})\right|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{ctk}{\tilde{R}^{2}\|{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{P}}\|_{F}}\right)

.

Proof.

Write
{\bm{Q}}:={\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{P}}
and observe that
{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{\mu}}={\bm{\mu}}^{\prime\top}{\bm{Q}}{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime}
for
{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime}\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\tilde{R}\cdot\mathbb{S}^{k-1})
. Then the first inequality follows directly from Lemma C.2 in
Frei and Vardi 2024
. The second inequality holds since
\left\|{\bm{W}}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|{\bm{W}}\right\|_{F}
for any matrix
{\bm{W}}
. Regarding the last part, write
a:=td/\tilde{R}^{2}\|{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{P}}\|_{F}
, if
a\geq 1
, we have that
\exp(-\min(a,a^{2}))\leq\exp(-a)
. If
a\leq 1,
the above bound still holds for small enough
c
and since
\mathbb{P}(\cdot)\leq 1
is a trivial inequality.
∎

{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{z}}
:

Lemma C.6
.

Let
{\bm{\mu}}={\bm{P}}{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime}
for some semi-orthogonal matrix
{\bm{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
and
{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime}\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\tilde{R}\cdot\mathbb{S}^{k-1})
. Moreover, let
{\bm{g}}\sim\mathsf{N}({\mathbf{0}},{\bm{I}}_{d})
(independent of
{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime}
) and let
{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a matrix. There is an absolute constant
c>0
such that for any
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(|{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{g}}|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{ct\sqrt{k}}{\tilde{R}\|{\bm{W}}\|_{F}}\right).

Proof.

By Remark
D.1
,
{\bm{\mu}}
has sub-Gaussian norm at most
cR/\sqrt{k}
for some constant
c>0
. From this point, the proof follows similarly to the proof of Lemma C.4 from
Frei and Vardi 2024
, and we provide it here for completeness. Observe that
\|{\bm{g}}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq c
, for some absolute constant
c>0
. By
Vershynin 2018
, this implies that for independent
{\bm{g}}_{1},{\bm{g}}_{2}\sim\mathsf{N}({\mathbf{0}},{\bm{I}}_{d})
and any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\beta\frac{\sqrt{k}}{\tilde{R}}{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{g}}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\exp\left(c_{1}\beta{\bm{g}}_{1}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{g}}_{2}\right).

Then using the moment-generating function of Gaussian chaos
[
46
, Lemma 6.2.2]
, for
\beta
satisfying
|\beta|\leq c_{2}/\|{\bm{W}}\|_{2}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\beta\frac{\sqrt{k}}{\tilde{R}}{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{g}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\exp\left(c_{1}\beta{\bm{g}}_{1}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{g}}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(c_{3}\beta^{2}\|{\bm{W}}\|_{F}^{2}).

That is, the random variable
\tilde{R}^{-1}\sqrt{k}{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{g}}
is mean-zero and has sub-exponential norm at most
\max(c_{2}^{-1},c_{3})\|{\bm{W}}\|_{F}
. There is therefore a constant
c_{4}>0
such that for any
u\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}(|\tilde{R}^{-1}\sqrt{k}{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{g}}|\geq u)=\mathbb{P}\left(|{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{g}}|\geq\frac{\tilde{R}u}{\sqrt{k}}\right)\leq 2\exp(-cu/\|{\bm{W}}\|_{F}).

Setting
u=t\sqrt{k}/\tilde{R}
we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{g}}|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{ct\sqrt{k}}{\tilde{R}\|{\bm{W}}\|_{F}}\right)

∎

{\bm{z}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{z}}
:

Lemma C.7

(Lemma C5 from
Frei and Vardi 2024
)
.

Let
{\bm{\zeta}},{\bm{\zeta}}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{N}({\mathbf{0}},{\bm{I}}_{d})
and let
{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a matrix. There is a constant
c>0
such that for all
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|{\bm{\zeta}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{\zeta}}^{\prime}|\geq t)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{ct}{\|{\bm{W}}\|_{F}}\right).

C.5
Proof of Thm.
3.1

For notational simplicity let us denote
{\bm{W}}={\bm{W}}_{\text{MM}}
,
\hat{\bm{\mu}}:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}y_{i}{\bm{x}}_{i}
, and let us drop the
M+1
subscript so that we denote
({\bm{x}}_{M+1},y_{M+1})=({\bm{x}},y)
. We recall that
B=c_{B}\cdot dk/R^{2}
(Assumption
C.1
), and let’s denote
\rho
as the quantity

\rho:=c\cdot\frac{(c_{B}\land 1)}{\log^{2}(B/\delta)}\in(0,1).

Then the test error is given by the probability of the event,

\{\sign(\hat{y}(E;W))\neq y_{M+1}\}=\{\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}y{\bm{x}}\leq 0\}.

By standard properties of the Gaussian, we have for
{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{N}({\mathbf{0}},{\bm{I}}_{d})
,

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\mu}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}{\bm{\mu}}+M^{-1/2}{\bm{z}},

\displaystyle\tilde{y}{\bm{x}}

Lemma D.2
.

Let
{\bm{W}}=\sum_{q=1}^{B}y_{q}\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}{\bm{x}}^{\top}
. Then

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{W}}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{q=1}^{B}\left\|\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q}\right\|^{2}\left\|{\bm{x}}_{q}\right\|^{2}+\sum_{q\neq\ell}\langle\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q},\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{\ell}\rangle\cdot\langle y_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q},y_{\ell}{\bm{x}}_{\ell}\rangle

Proof.

\displaystyle{W}_{i,j}=\sum_{q=1}^{B}y_{q}(\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q}^{\top})_{i,j}

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{W}}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i,j}\left(\sum_{q=1}^{B}y_{q}(\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q}^{\top})_{i,j}\right)^{2}=\sum_{i,j}\left(\sum_{q,\ell}y_{q}(\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q}^{\top})_{i,j}y_{\ell}(\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{\ell}{\bm{x}}_{\ell}^{\top})_{i,j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\left(\sum_{q=1}^{B}(\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q}^{\top})_{i,j}^{2}+\sum_{q\neq\ell}y_{q}y_{\ell}(\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q}^{\top})_{i,j}(\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{\ell}{\bm{x}}_{\ell}^{\top})_{i,j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q=1}^{B}\left\|\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\sum_{q\neq\ell}y_{q}y_{\ell}\langle\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q}^{\top},\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{\ell}{\bm{x}}_{\ell}^{\top}\rangle_{F}

Recall that
\left\|A\right\|_{F}^{2}=tr(A^{\top}A),\langle A,B\rangle_{F}=tr(A^{T}B),tr(A^{\top}B)=tr(BA^{\top})
, which means that
\left\|\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\left\|\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q}\right\|^{2}\left\|{\bm{x}}_{q}\right\|^{2}
and
\langle\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q}{\bm{x}}_{q}^{\top},\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{\ell}{\bm{x}}_{\ell}^{\top}\rangle_{F}=\langle\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{q},\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{\ell}\rangle\cdot\langle{\bm{x}}_{q},{\bm{x}}_{\ell}\rangle
. Substituting that into the displayed equation gives us the desired result.
∎

.
