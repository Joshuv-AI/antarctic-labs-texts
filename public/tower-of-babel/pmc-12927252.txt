Examining streetscape visuals and emotional responses through social media and street view image analysis
Authors: Xueyan Yang, Jie Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStreets are important spaces for everyday activities, and the street environment can impact quality of life. This study investigates the impact of streetscape visuals on public emotions in the Beishan Street and Nanshan Road historic districts surrounding West Lake in Hangzhou.MethodsEmotional perceptions were analyzed through a textual study of relevant posts on Sina Weibo, categorizing them into positive or negative sentiments. Simultaneously, Baidu Map Street View Images (SVIs) of the area were processed using the DeepLabV3 + Network model to semantically segment them into 19 elements, followed by the calculation of seven visual space indicators.ResultsCorrelation analysis between these street view elements, visual space indicators, and the emotional content of the posts revealed that multiple factors within the SVIs are significantly associated with public emotions. For instance, elements such as Rider and visual space indicators like Enclosure are positively correlated with emotions, while elements like Traffic Light and visual space indicators such as Openness show negative correlations. These emotional impacts vary depending on the specific building types present in the streetscape.ConclusionsThe findings underscore the association between streetscape visuals and public emotions and demonstrate that social media platforms provide substantial data for studying these effects. This research offers valuable insights for urban planners and managers to understand resident and tourist preferences, providing an objective foundation for enhancing urban streetscape quality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12942-025-00440-8.Keywords: Street view image (SVI), Sina weibo microblog, Semantic segmentation, Visual space indicator, Historic district

Introduction

Streetscapes and public experience

Streets are vital spaces for residents’ daily activities and tourist visits, serving various purposes such as transportation, recreation, and social interaction [1]. The street environment plays a significant role in shaping human emotional states [2] and influencing their physical and mental health [3]. Positive neighborhood environments can increase people’s physical activity [4], including children’s outdoor activities [5]. High-quality street space plays an important role in promoting urban vitality and improving the well-being of residents [6], whereas a poor design can lead to psychological stress for residents [7]. Interdisciplinary scholars and practitioners have therefore explored how to enhance daily experiences by improving the built environment [8]. In recent years, governments have expanded their focus beyond the functional aspects of street spaces, emphasizing beautification and promoting street liveliness [9].

The contribution of social media data to public participation

In urban environmental management, public perception and participation are now recognized as central to landscape decision-making [10]. The Historic Urban Landscape (HUL) approach proposed by UNESCO in 2011 emphasizes community engagement to integrate local knowledge and skills into planning processes [11]. The earlier Historic Landscape Characterization (HLC) methodology also included public input in three of its eight core evaluation dimensions [12]. However, most of the studies on public participation have concentrated on social sustainability aspects rather than on enhancing public satisfaction in historic districts [13]. The evaluations of residents [14] and their psychological perceptions [15] regarding urban environments are commonly assessed through questionnaires. For instance, Wang et al. [9] collected scores from volunteers with knowledge of history and culture on their perceptions of street scenes of the Binjiang District in Hangzhou, China. Similarly, Chen et al. [16] employed a questionnaire to investigate tourists’ perceptions of visual, auditory, tactile, and olfactory factors of the landscape environment of Hangzhou Gardens in Hangzhou, China. While widely used, such self-reported, awareness-based methods are vulnerable to response bias, as participants’ awareness of being observed may influence their answers [17]. This corresponds to active data collection, in which respondents’ conscious participation can restrict the authenticity of emotional expression. As technology advances, it is now possible to measure the differences in psychological state between individuals in different environments through brain waves, body temperature, skin conductance levels, and other human physiological indicators [18]. However, as with questionnaires, measuring physiological indicators yields a limited number of samples, which is time-consuming and inefficient.

Passive data collection via social media has been recognized as more reflective of real-life emotional states than active data collection [19]. Individuals posting on social media platforms are typically unaware that their data may be used for research, allowing their emotional expressions to be more spontaneous and authentic. Scholars have characterized social media as a valuable source for data collection. For example, Kumar et al. [20] collected images posted on social media that may refer to a cultural heritage site and automatically categorized whether they represent damage, significantly reducing the manual effort required to locate and analyze photos of damaged heritage. Chen et al. [21] utilized deep learning-based photo recognition to classify social media images into five types of cultural photos within historical districts. These approaches effectively address the limitations of traditional questionnaire-based data collection, such as delayed responses, and improve the timeliness and relevance of policy recommendations [21]. Based on the Sina Weibo microblog platform, one of the most popular Chinese microblogging platforms, this study analyzes the textual content of user posts to assess the positive and negative emotional states of residents regarding their neighborhood environments.

Street view image (SVI)

Streets serve as essential access points to the visual landscape [22]. Geographic information systems (GIS) and remote sensing techniques have been widely used to extract streetscape features from data, such as parcel boundaries [1] and visual accessibility [23], in large-scale analysis at the city level. However, in reality, people typically experience landscapes from a ground-level perspective [24], which is not captured by traditional remote sensing methods that focus on aerial views [1]. The emergence of Street View Images (SVIs) with an eye-level perspective provides a reliable data source for urban studies [25]. Compared with the 2D view, the 3D view is more conducive to healthy urban design and development [26]. SVIs from platforms such as Google Maps [14, 27, 28], Baidu Maps [1, 9, 29], and Tencent Maps [6, 30, 31] are well-suited for urban environment analysis. They enhance urban land use classification accuracy [32], facilitate the identification of sidewalk characteristics [14], and even support socio-economic studies [33]. Additionally, SVIs facilitate the calculation of the Green View Index [34], pedestrian counts [35], and the identification of obstacles, such as missing curb ramps on sidewalks [36].

The correlation between street view image (SVI) and public emotion

This study investigates human perception of the environment by integrating social media data with SVIs. The concept of perception has its origins in psychological research. Perception represents a process of mental activity that occurs when an external object has a visual or mental impact on an individual. According to phenomenology, perception engages multiple senses throughout the body [37], and over 80% of information from the external world is believed to be acquired through visual senses [38]. Advances in computer vision and machine learning have enabled the extensive application of diverse big data to investigate human perception of urban environments [33]. The Place Pulse 2.0 dataset developed by MIT Media Lab is one of the earliest attempts to quantify subjective urban perception through crowdsourced pairwise comparisons across six dimensions: safe, beautiful, depressing, lively, wealthy, and boring [39]. It has since been widely used to evaluate visual impressions of urban environments across different cultural and geographic contexts. For example, Zhang et al. [40] quantified the connections between visual elements and human perceptions in Shanghai and Beijing. Song et al. [41] compared the consistency and discrepancy between subjective and objective measurements of streetscape perception. Similarly, Chen et al. [42] assessed greenery around streets using Baidu panoramic images in Sanya City, China, while Dai et al. [31] used Tencent panoramic images to assess associations between urban visual space indicators, such as Greenness and Enclosure, and residents’ psychological perceptions in Wuhan’s central streets. Verhoeven et al. [43] proved that adolescents preferred to ride along residential streets rather than busy arterials. Shen and Karimi [44] utilized social media check-in data to explore spatial and temporal preferences in urban areas. This study quantifies the positive and negative emotional states of both residents and visitors based on Weibo posts and evaluates the streetscape environment of the study area using SVIs. The correlation between individuals’ emotional states and the street environment is subsequently analyzed.

Study area and data sources

Study area

This study focuses on the Beishan Street and Nanshan Road historic districts surrounding West Lake in Hangzhou, China (Fig. 1). Hangzhou is a historically significant city, having served as the capital during the Wu-Yue Dynasty of the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms (893–978) and the Southern Song Dynasty (1127–1279) [45]. The city is recognized for its rich cultural heritage [46]. The Beishan Street historic district was listed as a historic and cultural district of Zhejiang Province in 2016 [47]. In 2024, Nanshan Road was designated as one of the first historic character areas in Hangzhou. These districts are home to a large number of historical buildings from the Republic of China period, which are considered highly representative and historically valuable [48], and are frequently mentioned by the public on social media platforms. The districts are also located near the West Lake Scenic Spots, a UNESCO World Heritage Site recognized for its cultural landscape [49]. They exhibits a typical linear spatial structure, characterized by clear directionality and strong visual continuity. Flanked by a serene lake, historic districts offers diverse and abundant natural scenery [50]. Historical buildings are distributed along the street, forming a unique visual corridor. Welcoming approximately 20 million visitors annually, the West Lake attracts a lot of tourists [51], making the visual quality of the streetscape increasingly important, and also driving the growth of the surrounding restaurant and hotel industries [52], which have attracted widespread attention on social media.

Study area

Situated in a subtropical monsoon climate zone, Hangzhou is characterized by abundant vegetation [50]. Over the past decade, the city has experienced extensive urban infrastructure expansion, particularly in preparation for the 19th Asian Games held in 2022. As of 2020, Hangzhou’s built-up area green space rate reached 36.7%, while the green coverage rate was 40.29%, and the coverage rate of the park’s green space service radius has reached 90% [53]. Beishan Street spans a total of 3,381 m, and Nanshan Road extends 4,736 m, with its streetscape observed along primary, secondary, and tertiary roads. Points were established at approximately 50-meter intervals to facilitate observation, resulting in a total of 366 points for streetscape image collection.

Data sources

Sina Weibo, one of China’s leading microblogging platforms, had 598 million monthly active users and 257 million daily active users as of 2023 [54]. The platform allows users to send and receive short character-limited posts and retrieve text using keyword-based searches within a defined date range [55]. This study utilized Sina Weibo to collect posts from January 1, 2012, to December 31, 2023, using keywords such as “Beishan Street”, “Nanshan Street” and the names of historic buildings, restaurants, and hotels within the district. The historic buildings were identified from lists published on the websites of the State Administration of Cultural Heritage [56], National Government Services [57], Zhejiang Provincial Bureau of Cultural Heritage [58], and Zhejiang Government Services [59]. Furthermore, lists of restaurant and hotel buildings were identified based on functional coordinates labeled with Point of Interest (POI) data from the Baidu Map [60].

Building data of the 3D city model was sourced from the Baidu Maps open platform [61], while street data were obtained from OpenStreetMap (OSM) [62]. Topographic data was acquired from a Digital Elevation Model (DEM) provided by the Geospatial Data Cloud [63]. OSM streets were further used to generate observation point coordinates, facilitating the collection of SVIs. Crowdsourced mapping services, accessed via an Application Programming Interface (API), served as a source of big data for this research. The API enabled the acquisition of 3,462 SVIs from the Baidu Maps open platform [61], with each image having a resolution of 960 × 720 pixels.

Method

The methodology of this study is divided into three steps. First, the text from Sina Weibo posts is retrieved and analyzed for sentiment. Second, the SVIs in the study area are obtained via the Baidu Map API, followed by semantic segmentation. Finally, the sentiments expressed in the Weibo posts are correlated with the street view elements at corresponding locations to explore their influence on the emotional states of residents and tourists (Fig. 2).

The study workflow of correlation analysis between sentiment and streetscape

Social media text access and sentiment analysis

The text from Sina Weibo posts was accessed using the Sina Weibo API and a web crawler tool. Weibo, considered a significant Chinese media platform in 2010 and 2011 [64], served as the data source. Due to the limited initial data availability, the study defines the investigation period from 2012 to 2023. Keywords for retrieval were categorized into two groups: one using “Beishan Street” or “Nanshan Road” directly and the other comprising the names of historic buildings, restaurants, and hotels within the Beishan Street and Nanshan Road historic districts. A total of posts were retrieved, including 15,964 posts with the keyword “Beishan Street” or “Nanshan Road” and 48,924 posts with names of historic buildings, restaurants, and hotels [65]. The dataset was curated based on relevance, completeness, and data quality, ensuring that only valid samples were included in the analysis.

Sentiment analysis was conducted using Statistical Product and Service Solutions (SPSS) [66]. The SPSS sentiment dictionary incorporates 130,000 words compiled from sources such as BosonNLP, National Taiwan University, Tsinghua University, and the China National Knowledge Internet (CNKI). SPSSAU, a web-based statistical analysis tool, provides two sentiment analysis approaches: word-based and post-based. Word-based analysis evaluates sentiments derived from multiple keywords across posts, while post-based analysis assesses emotions within individual posts. Sentiment analysis calculates sentiment scores using a sentiment dictionary, with the resulting scores compressed between − 1 and 1. Positive sentiment scores indicate positive emotions, negative scores reflect negative emotions, and scores near zero denote neutrality (Table 1).

Street view acquisition and semantic segmentation

Street data for the study area were sourced from OSM. To ensure each street was represented by a single line, the data were simplified through merging and other adjustments. Primary, secondary, and tertiary roads were selected as the observation targets. Observation points were established at approximately 50-meter intervals along these streets, with coordinates generated using GIS. The SVIs corresponding to these observation points were then obtained from the Baidu Map open platform using the Baidu Map API.

The SVIs underwent semantic segmentation, a pixel-level image recognition technique where each pixel is assigned a category label (Fig. 3). It can help intelligent systems understand natural scenes, and this technology has broad application prospects in the fields of mobile robots, unmanned driving, image segmentation, object tracking, intelligent security, and so on [67]. For this study, the DeepLabV3 + Network model, introduced by Chen et al. [68] in 2018, was used for semantic segmentation [69]. Compared to the Full Convolutional Network (FCN), the DeepLabV3 + Network can control the resolution of the output feature map through dilated convolution to realize the balance between accuracy and running time [70]. Before application, the DeepLabV3 + model was trained using the Cityscapes Dataset [71], designed for urban street scene segmentation. The trained model classified the SVIs into 19 categories: Road, Sidewalk, Building, Wall, Fence, Pole, Traffic Light, Traffic Sign, Vegetation, Terrain, Sky, Person, Rider, Car, Truck, Bus, Train, Motorcycle, and Bicycle. The pixel percentages of each segmented street view element were then calculated.

As an example, a four-directional SVI of an observation point on Beishan Street in March 2015 is shown (Fig. 4). These directional images were combined into a 360-degree panoramic view for analysis. The calculated percentages of street view elements reflect the 360-degree panorama, not individual directional SVIs. It is worth noting that image recognition automatically infers the tree canopy outline of trees in leafy summer based on the tree branches of deciduous trees in winter (Fig. 4). As a result, the season of the SVIs does not affect the results of the analysis.

Human perception of urban landscape refers to their cognitive judgments of urban scenes, such as streets, parks, and buildings. Physical attributes of urban settings, such as vegetation amount, and their quality, such as openness, determine human perception, which further affects the quality of the urban environment [26]. To evaluate the perceptions of residents and tourists, seven visual space indicators were calculated based on the 19 street view elements obtained from the semantic segmentation of the SVIs. These indicators are Greenness, Openness, Enclosure, Traffic Flow, Motorization, Walkability, and Imageability. These indicators were calculated based on the pixel percentages of elements in the SVIs, and are therefore entirely objective. Although objective indicators cannot replace subjective perceptions, the study by Qiu et al. [72] has demonstrated that they can reflect subjective perceptions to a considerable extent. They were selected based on both theory and existing research. Openness and Enclosure, for example, are classic spatial perception measures rooted in environmental psychology and urban design [73]. Prior studies have shown that these indicators effectively reflect the relationship between visual streetscape features and public emotions [7, 9, 74]. Combined, they offer a comprehensive view of the visual and traffic characteristics of street environments and help explain emotional responses found in social media data.

Relevance of social media emotions and SVIs

To investigate the relationship between street view elements and public perception, this study employed both Pearson product-moment correlation [81] and Spearman rank correlation [82] analysis, elucidating the impact of the street environment on the emotional states of residents and tourists. The combination of the two correlation analyses allows for a comprehensive examination of linear and nonlinear relationships between street view elements, visual space indicators, and human emotions [9]. Given the relatively small sample size of this study, Spearman rank correlation was primarily used to enhance the robustness of the analysis. To further validate the correlation results, normality tests were conducted on the sentiment values from Weibo posts, street view elements, and visual space indicators. For variables that met the criteria for normal distribution, Pearson correlation was additionally applied as a supplementary analysis. It is worth noting that normality tests are often stringent and difficult to satisfy in practice. Nevertheless, when the absolute value of skewness is less than 2 and the absolute value of kurtosis (proper) is less than 7, the distribution can be regarded as approximately normal, even if it does not meet the strict criteria for perfect normality [83]. This threshold is commonly accepted in applied research for determining the suitability of parametric statistical methods [84].

Based on these two correlation methods, this study examined the relationships between street view indicators and four sentiment variables: sentiment score based on posts, proportion of positive emotions based on posts, sentiment score based on words, and proportion of positive emotions based on words, resulting in a total of eight sets of correlation results. To enhance the interpretability and credibility of the findings, a visual factor was considered significantly correlated if it showed statistical significance in at least two of the eight results, and was further analyzed.

Results and discussion

Changes in the last decade

Public emotions related to Beishan street and Nanshan Road on social media

Analyzing posts related to Beishan Street and Nanshan Road from 2012 to 2023 on Weibo reveals that 93.63% of posts expressed positive or moderately positive emotions (Fig. 6), reflecting high satisfaction levels among residents and tourists. However, word-based sentiment analysis showed a slightly lower satisfaction rate of 73.23%, attributed to the presence of negative words in overall positive posts. For instance, users may describe an enjoyable trip while mentioning minor inconveniences. Relevant studies have indicated that sentiment analysis of social media texts is often affected by semantic ambiguity, rhetorical expressions, and contextual factors, which may lead to discrepancies from real-world situations [85].

Results of textual analysis of study area related posts between 2012 to 2023

Words related to the 2019 novel coronavirus (2019-nCoV) outbreak from 2020 to 2022 were excluded from the analysis. Frequent positive words associated with Beishan Street and Nanshan Road included West Lake, Scenic Area, Willow Tree, Park, City, Culture, Life and Fallen Leaves. In the descriptions given by residents and visitors alike, the street was characterized as scenic and agreeable. In contrast, common negative words included Road Section, Time, Power Outage, Construction, Lane and Occupy. As a result of urban development, the street widths of Beishan Street and Nanshan Road are no longer adequate to meet the transportation needs of the citizens, preventing expansion due to the proximity of historic buildings to the north and West Lake to the south, as well as by the urban built-up area along the southern side of Nanshan Road. Consequently, municipal authorities enforce traffic restrictions during weekends, holidays, and major events, such as taxi prohibitions and bus stop suspensions. These measures, along with congestion, were identified as primary contributors to negative public sentiment.

As shown in Fig. 6, sentiment scores reveal a more significant shift over time compared to the proportion of positive and negative posts, making them a clearer indicator of emotional trends related to Beishan Street and Nanshan Road on social media. Post-based analysis indicates that the proportion of positive and negative emotions remained relatively stable from 2012 to 2023, with minor fluctuations. In contrast, word-based analysis shows a slight decrease in positive emotions and an increase in negative emotions in 2023 compared to 2012. This phenomenon is related to the manner in which individuals have been expressing themselves on the Internet in recent years. The use of pejorative language for praise or criticism has become increasingly popular, that is, the opposite of ironic criticism (i.e., mock positive evaluation of negative circumstances) is emerging, namely ironic praise (i.e., mock negative evaluation of positive circumstances) [86]. Such expressions, often termed “sick sentences” in written expression, are widely used on the Internet.

A Weibo post dated January 9, 2023, exemplifies this phenomenon: “The greatest luxury of today was to walk around West Lake, from Shuguang Road to Curved Yard and Lotus Pool in Summer, to Beishan Street, and then return back along Shuguang Road. Due to the worry about being late, I had to give up the idea of taking a walk on Nanshan Road. My eyes greedily looked at the beautiful scenery of West Lake. Because of the worry about being late, I did not even dare to get out of the car to stay and take a closer look. From now on, I will only remember the happy things and let the unhappy things melt in the sun and dissipate in the wind. Today, I thought of two meaningless but happy little things: flying a kite on the White Causeway and boating on the West Lake, to be realized next time.”

The textual analysis of the passage identified 19 positive or moderately positive words, including today, West Lake, Beishan, street, eyes, look, beautiful scenery, stay, closer, happy, let, melt, in the sun, dissipate, in the wind, little things, boating, realize, and next time. Conversely, 11 negative or moderately negative words were also found, such as luxury, walk, return back, worry, late, give up, idea, greedily, get off, meaningless, and matter. However, the analysis assigned the post a sentiment score of 1, indicating a 100% positive emotional state. This example highlights how negative words can be used to express highly positive emotions. For instance, the word “greedily,” traditionally a pejorative term associated with excessive desire for food or wealth, is used in this context to emphasize the depth of the author’s admiration for West Lake’s beauty. This nuanced use of language underscores the richness of emotional expression in the analyzed post.

Furthermore, it is noteworthy that word-based sentiment scores showed a significant decline in 2020, reaching their lowest point before gradually recovering in 2021. This trend was influenced by the 2019-nCoV outbreak. As a leading social media platform with over 500 million users, Weibo facilitated widespread access to news, discussions, opinion exchanges [55], and feedback during this period [87]. In the early stages of the outbreak in late 2019, online discourse was characterized by messages of positivity and encouragement. The study of Pan et al. [88] indicated that during the early stages of the pandemic, expressions of Positive Energy expression accounted for a relatively high proportion. However, as the pandemic persisted, negative emotions became increasingly prevalent in online postings, particularly by 2020, the second year after the outbreak. Many negative words during this period were directly associated with the outbreak. The study of Pan et al. [88] also indicated that after the initial success in pandemic prevention and control, there was an increase in “Sad” emotions. In our study, to ensure accurate results, it was necessary to exclude the influence of this specific event from the study.

Street view images (SVIs) of study area

Observation points were established at 50-meter intervals along Beishan Street and Nanshan Road’s main roads, resulting in 366 observation points. Historical SVIs were obtained from Baidu Maps for the years 2014, 2015, 2016, 2017, 2019, 2020, and 2022. At each time point, four-directional images were collected at each observation point, yielding over 3,000 valid street view samples in total. This ensured temporal and spatial representativeness in the calculation of visual space indicators. Semantic segmentation was then applied to these SVIs. The mean percentage of street view elements for each observation point across different time points was calculated, accounting for data availability. While most observation points did not have data from all years, the vast majority included data from at least two years. Averaging image data across different years and seasons also helps to reduce potential errors caused by the variability of dynamic indicators such as Rider, Person, and Bus. Although these dynamic elements are indeed influenced to some extent by seasonal changes and specific events, they were retained as important indicators of pedestrian density and spatial vitality.

The analysis revealed that vegetation dominates the street view interface in study area, accounting for 55.3% (Table 3). This proportion is significantly higher than the average vegetation coverage observed in the streets of the Binjiang District (10.1%), one of the central areas of Hangzhou, and even exceeds the maximum recorded vegetation coverage of 53.8% in that district [9]. It is evident that the Vegetation coverage of study area is considerably higher than that observed in other areas within the city center of Hangzhou. Other prominent street view elements include Building (13.29%), Road (11.01%), and Sky (3.98%) (Table 3). Notably, the sky percentage is ranked fourth, which is not an exceptionally high percentage and is considerably lower than the Binjiang District average of 34.1% [9]. Subsequently, the street view element proportion of Terrain, Car, Sidewalk, Wall, and Fence accounts for 2.92%, 2.38%, 1.71%, 1.26%, and 0.95%, respectively. The average percentage of the remaining street view elements, such as Traffic Lights and Poles, is less than 0.5% (Table 3).

Percentage of each street view element in study area

The visual space indicators of Greenness, Openness, Enclosure, Traffic Flow, Motorization, Walkability, and Imageability were calculated for each observation point in study area using the formulas provided in Sect. 3.2. By selecting the years with the most extensive street view data, the average values of the seven visual space indicators were calculated to examine the trends in streetscape changes in study area over the past decade (Fig. 7). Due to the limited number of available SVIs in 2019 and 2020, these two years were not included in the annual comparison shown in Fig. 7, but their data were still incorporated into the overall mean calculations to enhance the temporal robustness of visual indicator analysis.

Spatial distribution and temporal trends of seven visual space indicators

The results for Greenness, Openness, and Enclosure indicate that the overall Openness of study area is relatively low. Openness, calculated from the sky percentage, averages 3.98%, considerably lower than Greenness, which averages 55.35%. The greenness indicator has remained stable over the past decade, at approximately 50%, reflecting a well-maintained ecological environment. Multiple studies have demonstrated the important role of visible greenery in mental health, including attention restoration and stress relief [89]. Vegetation and sky can be considered highly correlated factors [7]. The streets within the study area, particularly Beishan Street, exhibit excessive vegetation growth along the route, which substantially reduces sky visibility. Consequently, the high Greenness percentage results in a pronounced Enclosure effect, with an average of 57.50% and a maximum of 92.48%. The Enclosure indicator has remained high (around 60–75%) throughout the past decade, showing an opposite trend to Openness. Observation points with higher Openness are predominantly located in two types of areas: in front of large hotels, such as the Westlake Villa Hotel, Shangri-La Hotel, and North China Hotel, where circular driveways and sparse tree planting provide unobstructed views; and at intersections where vehicle crossings disrupt the continuous canopy of large trees, creating open vistas.

The calculations for Traffic Flow, Motorization, and Walkability reveal that the mean Motorization percentage (12.01%) is slightly more than twice the mean Walkability percentage (4.04%). Considering that motor vehicles occupy more space than pedestrians, this suggests a higher number of pedestrians than vehicles on streets in study area. Due to road congestion, some visitors prefer to leave the historic district on foot before using public transport, such as buses or cabs. Over the past decade, Traffic Flow has increased, Motorization has remained relatively stable, while Walkability has declined. This trend may reflect the rising popularity of cycling, particularly after 2017. Between 2016 and 2017, the rapid rise of the sharing economy led to an explosive growth of dockless shared bicycles in many Chinese cities, including Hangzhou. These bicycles could be parked anywhere and unlocked easily via QR code [90]. In the same year, Hangzhou’s citywide ban on motorcycles further encouraged the use of bicycles and e-bikes [91]. These dual drivers of policy and market have led to the increasing popularity of shared micro-mobility, aligning with global development trends [92]. Based on textual analysis of Weibo posts, the disorderly parking of shared bicycles and the failure of electric bicycles to follow traffic rules may be a source of public dissatisfaction. Spatially, Traffic Flow, Motorization, Walkability, and Imageability are significantly higher on primary roads along West Lake compared to branch roads. Primary roads are more heavily trafficked, while branch roads experience lighter traffic.

Relationship between public perception and street view of historic, restaurant, and hotel buildings

The buildings in the Beishan Street and Nanshan Road historic districts are primarily ancient temples, residences of contemporary celebrities, and luxury hotels, including 48 buildings of various levels of historic preservation [93]. These buildings serve as exhibition halls promoting history and culture or operate as restaurants and hotels to accommodate tourists visiting West Lake. For this study, three categories of buildings were selected: historic buildings, restaurants, and hotels. The names of the selected buildings were used as keywords to retrieve Weibo posts for emotional analysis, aiming to explore the relationship between the emotional content of these posts and the visual environment surrounding the corresponding buildings.

To further examine whether the emotional expressions in Weibo posts are primarily influenced by the visual environment, the terms “Beishan Street” and “Nanshan Road” and the names of selected historic buildings, restaurants, and hotels were used as search queries. We then extracted high-frequency words from the retrieved posts (Fig. 8). The results show that the most frequently mentioned words are related to scenic landscapes, cultural activities, and historical events, such as “West Lake”, “Scenic Spot”, and “Park”. Terms related to transportation, such as “Road Section”, “Intersection”, and “Construction”, also appeared frequently, whereas words directly associated with food or accommodation quality, such as ‘Booking’ or ‘Taste’, were much less common. This finding suggests that in Beishan Street and Nanshan Road, historic districts adjacent to West Lake, visitors typically choose restaurants or hotels due more to their surrounding landscape environment than to service quality. Consequently, emotional expressions in social media posts are predominantly shaped by the district’s visual characteristics, with relatively limited interference from internal commercial factors. This outcome reinforces the validity and credibility of the correlation analysis between visual space indicators and public emotions.

High-frequency words extracted from Weibo posts containing “Beishan Street”, “Nanshan Road” and names of historic buildings, restaurants, and hotels

The corresponding street view of each building was determined based on continuous observation points near the building visible from its interior. Therefore, the emotions associated with each building were not compared to a single SVI, but rather to a group of consecutive SVIs selected based on spatial visibility. The size of each SVI set varied depending on the building’s location, but typically included around ten observation points. As illustrated in Fig. 9, this process involved extracting multiple vertex points outlining the building and analyzing their viewsheds using the 3D city model. The visual fields of all vertices were superimposed (illustrated in blue in the figure, with darker shades indicating visibility by a greater number of vertices), then it is possible to obtain a total visual field of the exterior seen from any interior of the building. The street view for a building was defined by the continuous observation points within its visual field in close proximity to the building. For example, if the visual field of a building included the first through fourth and sixth through seventh observation points, only the first four were selected as its corresponding street view, as the field was interrupted at the fifth point. Observation points beyond the sixth were excluded. This correlation method linked buildings with their corresponding street views. Emotional perceptions from Weibo posts were then correlated with visual space indicators derived from segmenting the SVIs into 19 street view elements and seven visual space indicators.

Correspondence between buildings and SVIs

Historic buildings

The results of the analysis of Weibo posts related to historical buildings indicate that the posts published by netizens about the historical buildings in study area are dominated by positive emotional content. For several buildings, the proportion of positive emotion posts reaches 100% (Fig. 10). Post-based and word-based sentiment analysis shows that 90.83% and 82.88% of posts, respectively, express positive emotions (Fig. 10). These findings suggest that the historic buildings in Beishan Street and Nanshan Road are highly appreciated by the public and receive substantial positive feedback online. While most historic buildings exhibit similar positive sentiment levels, notable exceptions exist. The Xinxin Hotel stands out with significantly negative evaluations compared to other historic buildings. Unlike most historic buildings, which primarily operate as tourist attractions, the Xinxin Hotel continues to function as a hotel. Negative feedback about the Xinxin Hotel mainly stems from dissatisfied tourists citing poor service. This is the primary reason for the significantly inferior evaluation of the Xinxin Hotel compared to other historic buildings.

Textual analysis results of posts related to historic buildings

Positive high-frequency words associated with historic buildings include Leifeng Pagoda, West Lake, Video, Scenic Area, Park, Scenic Spot, Fahai, Su Causeway, Bai Suzhen, Ten Scenes, and Tourism. From these words, it can be surmised that both the landscape of attractions and their cultural significance of the study area are a significant draw for tourists. Among these, Fahai, Bai Suzhen, and Leifeng Pagoda are related to traditional Chinese mythological stories. Furthermore, words such as Yue Fei and national hero also appear frequently, indicating the spirit of patriotism and the heritage of history and culture evoke a sense of respect among the populace. Conversely, negative high-frequency words include Time, Hour, Night, Bus, and Pour Out, with several terms related to temporal aspects, suggesting that transportation issues may contribute to public dissatisfaction. However, the negative high-frequency words sniveling, wretchedness, and cemetery are associated with the historical event of Yue Fei’s tomb, as well as with Yue Fei, national heroes, and the Southern Song Dynasty, which are included in the positive high-frequency words. This site, the final resting place of Yue Fei, a national hero of the Southern Song Dynasty (1127–1279), is a powerful symbol of national pride. Yue Fei’s accomplishments are integral to Chinese cultural education, as his story is included in elementary school curricula [94].

Based on the combined results of Spearman and Pearson correlation analyses (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5), a high visual percentage of Sidewalk, Rider, Car, Bus, and Motorcycle and a high Enclosure, Motorization, and Traffic Flow visual space indicator in the street view of historic buildings show a positive correlation with public emotions, with Enclosure demonstrating a more statistically significant relationship (Fig. 11). These traffic-related visual elements typically represent high street vitality and accessibility, in which the public is more likely to express positive emotions. Generally, open outdoor environments with lower Enclosure levels are associated with greater relaxation and positive emotions [78], whereas more enclosed spaces can create a sense of oppression, leading to negative emotions [7]. However, existing studies have shown that streetscapes with higher levels of complex, pleasing, and historical ambience from buildings elicit more positive perceptions, whereas modern and monotonous exteriors often evoke holistic feelings of being boring and depressing [95]. Therefore, we speculate that historic buildings, shaped by accumulated cultural and historical events, foster a solemn atmosphere that aligns. Environments with a higher Enclosure index appear to be more compatible with the atmosphere of historic buildings, allowing for a heightened sense of the gravity of history. Such spaces offer privacy and evoke a stronger sense of belonging, which encourages individuals to engage in reflective contemplation on historical matters.

Correlation of emotions of posts related to historic buildings with street view

Conversely, the high visual proportion of visual space indicators of Imageability in the street view of historic buildings, are associated with negative public emotions (Fig. 11). Imageability refers to elements in the streetscape visual environment such as traffic signs and other visually distinctive features, and the presence of visual disturbances may prevent individuals from fully connecting with the historical atmosphere, leading to negative emotions. In different urban spatial usage scenarios, different users require different types of street facilities [96]. Based on the findings of this study, we speculate that visitors to historic buildings often seek a quiet environment conducive to deep reflection, and modern elements such as Poles, which belong to the monotonous appearance of contemporary urban settings [95], clash with the historical and cultural context, diminishing the overall visitor experience.

Restaurant buildings

The analysis of Weibo posts related to restaurant buildings in study area shows predominantly positive sentiments, although the proportion of negative emotions is notably higher compared to historic buildings. Positive sentiment posts accounted for 85.41% and 82.18% in post-based and word-based analyses, respectively (Fig. 12). However, three out of eleven restaurants recorded negative sentiment levels exceeding 50%. Posts expressing dissatisfaction with the environment accounted for over half of the total for these restaurants, indicating significant discontent among patrons. One noteworthy case is Le Villa Restaurant, which exhibited the highest proportion of negative sentiment. Much of this negativity stems from posts related to the 2019-nCoV outbreak, as discussed in Sect. 4.1. As a niche fine-dining establishment with a limited clientele, the concentration of posts referencing the 2019-nCoV disproportionately increased its negative sentiment percentage. The results of the word-level analysis indicate that the proportion of positive words for this restaurant is within the normal range, suggesting that the relatively high proportion of negative posts may not be attributable to public dissatisfaction. In contrast, the other two restaurants with high negative emotions, Shangri-La Hotel and Xinxin Hotel, cater to a larger customer base, making their results less influenced by specific factors.

Textual analysis results of posts related to restaurant buildings

Positive high-frequency words associated with restaurant buildings include Lakeside, Manor, Town, Scenic Area, Tourism, Garden, Wedding, Teahouse, Culture, and Life, reflecting that in a tourism context—especially within scenic areas—restaurant buildings are closely related to landscape, cultural atmosphere, and social activities [97]. Restaurants around West Lake may thus be perceived by residents and tourists as important venues carrying local culture and lifestyle. Conversely, apart from terms associated with the 2019-nCoV outbreak, negative high-frequency words include Bus, Adjustment, Time, Afternoon, and Address, reflecting the public’s negative emotions regarding transportation, time management, and locational information. These negative sentiments may largely stem from inconvenient public transport connections, limited travel time, or unclear spatial orientation [98], indicating that the negative experiences are primarily associated with external transportation systems and activity organization, rather than the service quality or spatial characteristics of the restaurant buildings themselves. A detailed correlation analysis between Weibo sentiment and SVIs was conducted to explore the relationship between transportation inconvenience and the sentiments expressed on Weibo (Fig. 13).

Correlation of emotions of posts related to restaurant buildings with street view

Based on the combined results of Spearman and Pearson correlation analyses (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7), a high visual percentage of Roads in the street view of restaurant buildings is associated with positive public emotions (Fig. 13). A high visual percentage of Roads and Sidewalks in the SVIs near a restaurant may indicate that the street in front of the restaurant is relatively spacious and thus provides a comfortable walking environment, which is typically evaluated more positively by pedestrians. Existing studies have shown that wide sidewalks and well-designed public space facilities, such as public seating, help enhance pedestrian comfort and promote both street food and community interaction [99]. Previous research has indicated that providing more travel options within urban space facilitates residents’ sense of identity and promotes public life in neighborhoods [100]. The openness of street-facing restaurant spaces and their accessibility often correspond to higher levels of positive emotional expression. Furthermore, high-frequency words related to restaurant buildings include Bus, adjustment, and Time. It can be speculated that the public is discontent with temporary restrictions and traffic pattern changes near restaurants, as these can disrupt plans despite the general acceptance of dining in high-traffic areas. Further research is needed to understand these contextual factors more accurately.

Conversely, a high visual percentage of Traffic Light, Terrain, Car, and Trunk, along with the visual space indicators Greenness and Traffic Flow, may evoke negative emotions (Fig. 13). The public is more likely to experience negative feelings in spaces with dense traffic signals, undulating terrain, or high levels of motor vehicle interference. The presence of uneven terrain can pose challenges for pedestrians and drivers, while disruptions to the visual continuity of the streetscape negatively affect the overall experience in the street space. Current studies suggest that terrain can lead to mobility difficulties, particularly for elderly or mobility-impaired individuals [79], thereby reducing pedestrian satisfaction [101]. However, unlike the undulating hills commonly referenced in other studies, the “terrain” identified in this research primarily refers to steps or elevation differences, which may contribute to potential negative experiences in urban street spaces. In addition, the simultaneous presence of vegetation and traffic flow also suggests that excessive visual obstruction or dynamic interference may reduce the visual comfort and perceived safety of restaurant spaces.

Hotel buildings

The analysis of Weibo posts related to hotel buildings in study area reveals predominantly positive sentiments, with a slightly higher proportion of positive emotions compared to restaurant buildings. The proportion of positive posts based on post-based and word-based analysis was 79.84% and 86.66%, respectively (Fig. 14). The two hotels with the highest percentage of negative posts are the Shangri-La Hotel and Xinxin Hotel, consistent with the findings for restaurant buildings. These two hotels are large-scale integrated hotels which serve as both restaurants and accommodations.

Textual analysis results of posts related to hotel buildings

Positive high-frequency words associated with hotel buildings include Manor, Tourism, Garden, Wedding, Four Seasons, By the West Lake, Restaurant, and Travel. These terms show certain similarities with the analysis of restaurant buildings, both reflecting the public’s positive perceptions of the landscape, recreational atmosphere, and ceremonial experiences. This suggests that the natural scenery around West Lake may contribute to the positive emotional experiences of visitors. Conversely, apart from terms related to the 2019-nCoV outbreak, negative high-frequency words include Public Transport, Time, Sector, and Arrangement, which are similar to the sources of negative emotions observed in restaurant buildings, primarily related to inconvenient transportation organization and time management. This indicates that in areas of high scenic value, negative emotions are more likely to stem from deficiencies in external environmental support systems rather than from negative perceptions of the space itself.

Based on the combined results of Spearman and Pearson correlation analyses (Supplementary Tables 8 and 9), the street view elements of Roads, Sidewalks, Traffic Light, and Motorcycles, along with the visual space indicators of Openness and Motorization, may be associated with negative emotions in the context of hotel buildings (Fig. 15). These elements are often associated with heavy traffic, noise pollution, and reduced privacy, all of which detract from the relaxation and comfort expected in hotel accommodation. For example, noise can disrupt sleep, and there is both objective and subjective evidence showing that noise leads to sleep disturbances [102], which can lead to negative sentiments. Similarly, Song et al. [103] study showed that people’s accommodation preferences are related to noise and traffic intensity. In general, there is a common perception that the sky and roads have a stress-relieving effect [7] and that people feel better in open spaces [104]. Nevertheless, the correlation analysis reveals that for hotel buildings, excessive Openness may correlate with negative emotions. Privacy is a key concern for hotel guests [105]. Rest and sleep, which are typically conducted in private settings, are hindered by overly open spaces, which can instill unease, undermining the desired comfort and tranquility of a hotel stay.

Comparison of the effects of SVIs on human emotions across different types of buildings

Higher proportion of positive posts related to historic buildings

The analysis reveals that the proportion of positive emotions expressed in social media posts about historic buildings surpasses those related to restaurant and hotel buildings, whether analyzed based on posts or words. From one perspective, historic buildings evoke cultural identity and pride. Conversely, expectations for environmental quality are higher when people are consuming, particularly in restaurants and hotels. This aligns with findings by Fan et al. [106], who observed that expressions of anger among users correlate more strongly than expressions of joy or sadness. Satisfied tourists may choose not to leave reviews, whereas dissatisfied individuals are more likely to post negative feedback not only on booking platforms but also on other social media channels to warn potential visitors.

The analysis also demonstrates that the proportion of positive sentiment related to historic buildings is higher when analyzed based on posts than on words. In contrast, the opposite pattern is observed for hotel buildings. This indicates that netizens predominantly express positive emotions when discussing historic buildings. However, due to the heightened emotional weight of historical terminology, word-based analysis captures a slightly higher percentage of negative emotions. Conversely, hotels are primarily associated with tourism and leisure. Even in cases of dissatisfaction, the language used tends to be milder and more pleasant, reflecting the lighter context of these activities.

Similar and opposite effects on human emotions across different types of Building landscapes

The analysis of SVIs reveals similarities in the effects of certain street view elements on human emotions across different building types. Notably, Traffic Lights negatively impact emotions in two of the three building categories. It suggests that these street view elements could elicit significant negative sentiment from residents and tourists. The presence of Traffic lights is often indicative of intersections. The Beishan Street and Nanshan Road are narrow and heavily trafficked by tourists. The concentration of pedestrians, bicycles, electric bicycles, and motor vehicles at intersections results in a noisy and chaotic environment.

Conversely, certain street view elements have opposing effects on human emotions depending on the building type. For instance, the high proportion of Sidewalks, Cars, and Motorcycles in SVIs, along with a high visual space indicator of Motorization, positively influences emotions for historic buildings but negatively impacts emotions for restaurant and hotel buildings. This difference is also closely related to the functional attributes of the buildings. Historic buildings, often serving as tourist attractions, lead the public to perceive street traffic and pedestrian flow as indicators of vitality and openness, resulting in a positive emotional association with motorized elements in this context. In contrast, in spaces such as restaurants and hotels, which emphasize quietness and privacy, motorized elements are more likely to be associated with negative emotions.

Strengths and limitations

This study employed a passive and unobtrusive data collection method [19] to construct a methodological framework that integrates emotion data from Sina Weibo users with semantic segmentation of SVIs, aiming to explore the associations between public emotions and visual elements in historic districts. Based on this framework, the Beishan Street and Nanshan Road historic district adjacent to West Lake was selected as the study area, revealing the emotional perception characteristics of residents and tourists in a culturally historic district. The visual factors identified through the analysis, such as Greenness, Enclosure, Motorization, and Traffic Flow, are associated with public emotions and may serve as references for future renewal and revitalization of historic districts.

However, it should be noted that this study only reveals correlations between visual elements and public emotions, but does not imply causality. Emotional responses may be influenced by multiple factors simultaneously, such as weather conditions or unexpected events, with streetscape visuals being only one of them. Moreover, due to the limited sample size, this study has not yet conducted multivariate modeling, such as multiple linear regression. The results of current correlation analysis should therefore be regarded as exploratory findings aimed at identifying potential key visual factors, rather than definitive conclusions.

This study utilized Sina Weibo big data to examine sentiments expressed by residents and tourists regarding Beishan Street, Nanshan Road and their buildings. This analysis was conducted using SPSS Text Analysis for Survey. While this analysis focused on binary positive and negative sentiments, emotions are multifaceted. For example, sentiments such as wealth, safety, and beauty are related yet distinct [9]. Future research will aim to develop a more nuanced classification of social media posts. Moreover, online social media users tend to be younger, limiting representation. According to statistics, 27% of Sina Weibo users are aged 18–24, 42% are aged 25–34, and 31% are aged 35 and above. In addition, a higher proportion of users reside in urban areas [107]. The perception of landscape visuals varies across different groups. For example, cultural perception differences have been observed between local residents and tourists [21]. In this study, guests and passersby may perceive the same landscape visuals differently. To gain a deeper understanding, further studies should segment populations to analyze differences in perceptions.

In this study, the proportion of 19 street view elements and seven visual space indicators in street view vision is calculated based on the Baidu map SVI using the DeepLabV3 + Network model semantic segmentation technique. With extensive SVI coverage of urban streets, a substantial repository of image data is available for urban landscape research. The advancement of machine learning technology offers a scientific foundation for the quantification of streetscapes [33]. The ready availability and extensive coverage of SVI data significantly reduce the financial burden on researchers. However, the current average accuracy of semantic segmentation is approximately 90% [14, 36], and the potential impact of segmentation errors on results should not be overlooked.

Conclusion

This study explored the significant correlations between street environments and human emotions using SVI data and social media content in the context of the Beishan Street and Nanshan Road historic districts. Historic districts, as tangible embodiments of cultural heritage, play a vital role in preserving urban memory and fostering residents’ cultural recognition. In recent years, these districts have gained popularity as important tourist destinations. Streetscapes with a stronger historical ambiance from buildings elicited more positive perceptions [95]. The findings reveal that individuals generally exhibit more favorable sentiments toward historic buildings compared to restaurants and hotels. The analysis identified that multiple street view elements and visual space indicators exert a positive or negative influence on people’s emotions, with varying impacts observed across different building types within the built environment. Positive influences included Sidewalk, Rider, Car, Bus, Motorcycle, Enclosure, Motorization, and Traffic Flow for historic buildings, and Roads for restaurant buildings environments. Conversely, negative influences were observed for Imageability in historic buildings; Traffic Light, Terrain, Car, Trucks, Greenness, and Traffic Flow in restaurant buildings; and Roads, Sidewalks, Traffic Lights, Motorcycles, Openness, and Motorization in hotel environments. The impact of these street view elements and visual space indicators on emotional response provides a valuable basis for optimizing urban landscapes.
