Topological Levinson’s theorem and corrections at thresholds: the full picture in a quasi-1D example
T. T. Nguyen, D. Parra, S. Richard

Abstract
Various threshold effects are investigated on a discrete quasi-1D scattering system. In particular, one of these effects is to add corrections to Levinson’s theorem. We explain how these corrections are due to the opening or to the closing of channels of scattering, and how these contributions can be computed as partial winding numbers on newly introduced operators. Embedded thresholds, thresholds associated with changes of spectral multiplicity, and doubly degenerate thresholds are exhibited and analyzed. Most of the investigations are of an analytical nature, but the final equalities rely on a C ∗ C^{*} -algebraic framework.

Introduction

Levinson’s theorem is a relation between the number of bound states of a quantum
mechanical system and an expression arising from the scattering part of the system.
The original formulation by N. Levinson in
[
29
]
was given in the context of
a Schrödinger operator with a spherically symmetric potential.
It has then been extended and refined in numerous papers and in various contexts.
A common feature of these results is the appearance of correction terms
and of a regularization process on the scattering side of the equality.
Regularizations are often related to
the infinite dimension of all fixed energy subspaces, and can
be disregarded by considering quasi-1D systems.
On the other hand, corrections are usually related
to thresholds at the endpoints of the spectrum of the underlying
unperturbed operator, and are explicitly computed on an ad hoc basis.
Since it is impossible to mention all paper containing a Levinson’s theorem
with correction terms,
let us just mention two review papers
[
30
,
33
]
which contain several
earlier references.

In the discrete setting,
[
20
]
is one of the earliest works on Levinson’s theorem.
For more general graphs, let us mention
[
9
,
12
,
13
]
,
while for recent results in the quasi-one dimensional setting include
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
21
]
. In higher dimension,
[
37
]
contains an extension of Levinson’s theorem based on the notion of surface states. However, none of the systems considered in these references
possess embedded thresholds, and hence the only effect considered is of endpoint thresholds.
It is only in the recent publication
[
5
]
that systems with embedded thresholds
were considered for the first time, with their importance for Levinson’s theorem being highlighted.
However, even in this reference some restrictions were imposed, leading only to a subfamily of threshold’s behaviors. The current manuscript removes all restrictions, provides the full picture, and exhibits new phenomena.

Let us be more concrete about the content of the paper and its specificity.
The model we consider has been introduced and partially studied in
[
31
]
.
In this reference it appears as parts of a discrete model of scattering theory in a half-space,
see also
[
10
,
11
,
18
,
19
,
22
,
34
,
35
]
for systems leading to a similar decomposition.
Independently, the model corresponds to a magnetic Laplace operators acting on the graph
\mathbb{N}\times(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
, corresponding to a discrete half-cylinder, see Figure
1
and Remark
2.1
.

Figure 1:
Discrete cylinder with perturbations located on its first layer.
The direction of the magnetic field is indicated by the arrow, with an intensity
parametrized by
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
.

The magnetic field is constant in the
\mathbb{N}
direction, and is parameterized by
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
.
We refer to
[
14
,
28
]
for seminal references on discrete magnetic operators.
Perturbations are then added on the boundary of the system, namely
they are supported on
\{0\}\times(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
.
These systems have changes of multiplicity in the continuous spectrum,
and accordingly embedded thresholds. The set
of thresholds is defined by the set
\{\lambda^{\theta}_{j}\pm 2\}_{j=1}^{N}
,
where
\{\lambda^{\theta}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
correspond to the eigenvalues of a magnetic Laplace operator on
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
.
These thresholds naturally lead to the structure of a Hilbert space with fibers of finite and locally constant dimension, in which most of the analysis has to be performed.
This setup is precisely introduced in Section
2.1
, where also basics spectral
and scattering results are recalled from
[
31
]
.

Levinson’s theorem is then obtained as an index theorem in scattering theory.
The general framework has been exhibited in
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
, and then summarized in the review paper
[
33
]
. However, all examples presented in these references
were based on systems in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and not in a discrete setting.
The key idea is to construct a
C^{*}
-algebraic setting which accommodates one of the wave operators (no need to consider both). Once in a suitable
C^{*}
-algebra, its Fredholm index can be evaluated by its image in a sub-algebra of the Calkin algebra. More precisely, by looking at the image of the wave operators in a quotient algebra (obtained by quotienting it with the ideal of compact operators) one obtains a unitary operator.
This operator consists of several pieces, among which the main one is the scatting operator
S^{\theta}
.
The other pieces can be interpreted as operators of opening of new channels of scattering, or operators of closing of channels of scattering. All of them are located at thresholds, and the their precise form depend on the nature of the thresholds. Note that the scattering operator is fibered in the energy variable, while these newly introduced operator are fibered in a conjugate variable. Once a winding number
is suitably applied to this family of operators, the already mentioned Fredholm index is recovered, and the equality of these two quantities is precisely Levinson’s theorem.

In the paper
[
5
]
, the intensity of the magnetic field was restricted to
(0,\pi)
.
As a consequence, all internal thresholds correspond to a change by
\pm 1
of the spectral multiplicity:
+1
for the opening of a new channel of scattering,
-1
for the closing of a channel of scattering. At these thresholds, the scattering matrix restricted to this specific channel can only take two values:
\pm 1
, the value
-1
being generic while the value
1
being exceptional. This situation was fully investigated in the mentioned reference
and led to a Levinson’s theorem of the form:

\mathrm{Var}\big(\lambda\to\det S^{\theta}(\lambda)\big)+N-\frac{\#\big\{j\mid\mathfrak{s}^{\theta}_{jj}(\lambda^{\theta}_{j}\pm 2)=1\big\}}{2}=\#\sigma_{\rm p}(H^{\theta})

(1.1)

where
\mathfrak{s}_{jj}^{\theta}(\lambda_{j}^{\theta}\pm 2)
corresponds to a distinguished entry of the scattering matrix at the threshold energy
\lambda_{j}^{\theta}\pm 2
, and where
\mathrm{Var}\big(\lambda\mapsto\det S^{\theta}(\lambda)\big)

is the total variation of the argument of the piecewise continuous function
\lambda\mapsto\det S^{\theta}(\lambda)
.
On the r.h.s. the expression
\#\sigma_{\rm p}(H^{\theta})

corresponds to the number of bound states of the perturbed system.
Let us emphasize that the second and third term on the l.h.s. of the above equality
are obtained as the partial winding number (or equivalently the partial variation of the argument) of the newly exhibited and already mentioned fibered operators of opening or of closing of a channel of scattering.

In the present paper, we consider arbitrary intensity of the magnetic field in
[0,2\pi)
. This leads to two main new phenomena. Firstly, for
\theta\in\{0,\pi\}
the change of spectral
multiplicity belongs to
\{-2,-1,1,2\}
, depending on the thresholds. The change of multiplicity
\pm 2
leads to new operators of opening or closing of channels of scattering
which are described in Section
3
and the main result read then

\mathrm{Var}\big(\lambda\to\det S^{\theta}(\lambda)\big)+N-\tfrac{1}{2}C=\#\sigma_{\rm p}(H^{\theta}),

(1.2)

where

C:=\#\big\{k\mid\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}^{\theta}_{k}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\theta}_{k}\pm 2)=1\big\}+2\#\big\{k\mid\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}^{\theta}_{k}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\theta}_{k}\pm 2)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\big\}+\#\Big\{k\mid\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}^{\theta}_{k}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\theta}_{k}\pm 2)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
\overline{b}&-a\end{smallmatrix}\right)\Big\}.

where
\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}_{k}^{\theta}(\tilde{\lambda}_{k}^{\theta}\pm 2)
corresponds now to the distinguished part of the scattering matrix at the threshold energy
\tilde{\lambda}_{k}^{\theta}\pm 2
. Note that a new indexation had to be introduced since
some eigenvalues
\lambda^{\theta}_{j}
are now equal, while they are all distinct for

\theta\not\in\{0,\pi\}
. Again all correction terms in the factor
C

are explicitly computed as partial winding numbers of some fibered operators.

The second new phenomenon has been called the
intricate case
and appears
only for
\theta=0
for very specific perturbations. This special situation was mentioned in
[
31
]
under the name
degenerate case
because it was not possible to treat it at that time. The name has been changed because it corresponds to a surprising new effect, and a precise definition is given in Definition
2.7
.
When
\theta=0
and
N
is even,
the point
0
in the spectrum of the unperturbed operator
corresponds to a doubly degenerate threshold: it is the closing of a channel of scattering, and simultaneously the opening of a new channel of scattering.
In this specific situation and for very specific potential (with at least
N/2
trivial values) a term in the wave operators which is compact for all other perturbations, all other
\theta
and
N
, is suddenly no more compact. As a consequence, this term provides a new contribution when one considers the image of the wave operators through the ideal of compact operators.
At the algebraic level, the
C^{*}
-algebra introduced in Section
2.2
whose quotient is carefully studied in Section
3
is suddenly no more sufficient.
An extended framework has to be introduced for this specific situation, and lead to a surprising connection between energies
-4
,
0
, and
4
.

This new situation is thoroughly studied in Section
4
,
firstly for
N=2
and then for a general even number
N
.
These investigations impose the use of a larger algebra whose construction is borrowed
from
[
15
, Sec. V.7]
and which was used for the first time in this context in
[
4
]
.
Once in this framework, the topological version of Levinson’s theorem appears quite naturally, and in this context it reads:

\mathrm{Var}\big(\lambda\to\det S^{0}(\lambda)\big)+N-\tfrac{1}{2}C-\tfrac{1}{2}=\#\sigma_{\rm p}(H^{0}),

(1.3)

where

C:=\#\big\{k\mid\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}^{0}_{k}(\tilde{\lambda}^{0}_{k}\pm 2)=1\big\}+2\#\big\{k\mid\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}^{0}_{k}(\tilde{\lambda}^{0}_{k}\pm 2)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\big\}+\#\Big\{k\mid\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}^{0}_{k}(\tilde{\lambda}^{0}_{k}\pm 2)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
\overline{b}&-a\end{smallmatrix}\right)\Big\}.

The additional factor
\frac{1}{2}
in the above formula is due to the intricate connection
between energies
-4
,
0
, and
4
. Let us mention another special effect of the doubly degenerate threshold at
0

in the intricate case:
even if the closure of one channel of scattering and simultaneously the opening of a new channel of scattering correspond to two changes of
\pm 1
for the spectral multiplicity, the scattering matrix restricted to these specific channels do not take the values
\pm 1
, as it could be anticipated,
it takes the values
\pm i
. When computing
the partial winding number of the corresponding operator, this provides the difference of factor
\frac{1}{2}
.

In summary, this paper completes and provides a full picture of the partial results obtained
in
[
5
]
and
[
31
]
. The correction terms appearing in Levinson’s theorem
are now fully explained in all possible cases, and appear as partial winding numbers of new operators.
Once all of them are taken into account, the topological nature of Levinson’s theorem is restored.

2
Model and existing results

In this section, we introduce the quantum system and give a rather complete description of the results obtained in
[
31
]
. Part of the algebraic framework provided in
[
5
]

is also recalled. We present this material for arbitrary
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
.

2.1
Spectral and scattering theory

The following exposition is partially based on the short review paper
[
32
]
.
We set
\mathbb{N}:=\{0,1,2,\dots\}
. In the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})

we consider the discrete Neumann
adjacency operator whose action on
\phi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
is described by

\big(\Delta_{\mathrm{N}}\;\!\phi\big)(n)=\begin{cases}2^{1/2}\;\!\phi(1)&\hbox{if $n=0$}\\
2^{1/2}\;\!\phi(0)+\phi(2)&\hbox{if $n=1$}\\
\phi(n+1)+\phi(n-1)&\hbox{if $n\geq 2$.}\end{cases}

For any fixed
N\in\mathbb{N}
with
N\geq 2
and for any fixed
\theta\in[0,2\pi)

we also consider the
N\times N
Hermitian matrix

A^{\theta}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&\cdots&0&\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-i\theta}\\
1&0&1&\ddots&&0\\
0&1&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&1&0\\
0&&\ddots&1&0&1\\
\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{i\theta}&0&\cdots&0&1&0\end{pmatrix}.

Note that in the special case
N=2
, this matrix takes the form

A^{\theta}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1+\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-i\theta}\\
1+\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{i\theta}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right).

These two ingredients lead to the self-adjoint operator
H_{0}(\theta)

acting on
\ell^{2}\big(\mathbb{N};\mathbb{C}^{N}\big)
as

H_{0}(\theta):=\Delta_{\mathrm{N}}\otimes 1_{N}+A^{\theta}

with
1_{N}
the
N\times N
identity matrix.

This operator can be viewed as a discrete magnetic adjacency operator, see Remark
2.1
. It is also the one which appears in a direct integral decomposition of
an operator acting on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z})
through a Bloch-Floquet
transformation, see
[
31
, Sec. 2]
. We do not emphasize this decomposition
in the present work, but concentrate on each individual operator living on the fibers
of a direct integral.

The perturbed operator
H(\theta)
describing the discrete quantum model is then given by

H(\theta):=H_{0}(\theta)+V,

where
V
is the multiplication operator by a nonzero, matrix-valued function
with support on
\{0\}\in\mathbb{N}
. In other words, there exists a nonzero function

v:\{1,\dots,N\}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for
\psi\in\ell^{2}\big(\mathbb{N};\mathbb{C}^{N}\big)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}

one has

\big(H(\theta)\psi\big)_{j}(n)=\big(H_{0}(\theta)\psi\big)_{j}(n)+\delta_{0,n}\;\!v(j)\;\!\psi_{j}(0),

with
\delta_{0,n}
the Kronecker delta function.

Remark 2.1
.

H_{0}(\theta)
can be interpreted as a magnetic adjacency operator on the Cayley graph of the semi-group
\mathbb{N}\times(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
with respect to a magnetic field constant in the
\mathbb{N}
direction. In particular, it corresponds to choosing a magnetic potential supported only on edges of the form
\big((n,N),(n,1)\big)
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In this representation, the perturbation
V
corresponds
to a multiplicative perturbation with support on
\{0\}\times(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
.

We now set

\mathfrak{h}:=\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits\big([0,\pi),\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{\pi};\mathbb{C}^{N}\big)

and define a transformation in the
\mathbb{N}
-variable

\mathcal{G}:\ell^{2}\big(\mathbb{N};\mathbb{C}^{N}\big)\to\mathfrak{h}

given for
\psi\in\ell^{2}\big(\mathbb{N};\mathbb{C}^{N}\big)_{\rm fin}
(the finitely supported functions),
\omega\in[0,\pi)
, and
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
by

\big(\mathcal{G}\psi\big)_{j}(\omega):=2^{1/2}\sum_{n\geq 1}\cos(n\;\!\omega)\psi_{j}(n)+\psi_{j}(0).

The transformation
\mathcal{G}
extends to a unitary operator from

\ell^{2}\big(\mathbb{N};\mathbb{C}^{N}\big)
to
\mathfrak{h}
, which we denote by the same
symbol, and a direct computation gives the equality

\mathcal{G}\;\!H_{0}(\theta)\;\!\mathcal{G}^{*}=H^{\theta}_{0}\quad\hbox{with}\quad H^{\theta}_{0}:=2\cos(\Omega)\otimes 1_{N}+A^{\theta}

with the operator
2\cos(\Omega)
of multiplication by the function

[0,\pi)\ni\omega\mapsto 2\cos(\omega)\in\mathbb{R}
.

Through the same unitary transformation, the operator
H(\theta)

is unitarily equivalent to

H^{\theta}:=2\cos(\Omega)\otimes 1_{N}+A^{\theta}+\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\nolimits(v)P_{0}

with

\big(\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\nolimits(v)\;\!\mathfrak{f}\big)_{j}:=v(j)\;\!\mathfrak{f}_{j}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\big(P_{0}\;\!\mathfrak{f}\big)_{j}:=\int_{0}^{\pi}\mathfrak{f}_{j}(\omega)\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{\pi}

for
\mathfrak{f}\in\mathfrak{h}
,
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.
Observe that the term
\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\nolimits(v)P_{0}
corresponds to a finite rank
perturbation, and therefore
H^{\theta}
and
H_{0}^{\theta}

differ only by a finite rank operator.

Let us now move to spectral results.
A direct inspection shows that the matrix
A^{\theta}
has eigenvalues

\lambda_{j}^{\theta}:=2\cos\left(\frac{\theta+2\pi\;\!j}{N}\right),\quad j\in\{1,\dots,N\},

(2.1)

with corresponding eigenvectors
\xi_{j}^{\theta}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
having components

\big(\xi_{j}^{\theta}\big)_{k}:=\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{i(\theta+2\pi j)k/N}
for
j,k\in\{1,\dots,N\}
. Using the
notation
\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}\equiv\tfrac{1}{N}|\xi^{\theta}_{j}\rangle\langle\xi^{\theta}_{j}|
for the orthogonal projection associated to
\xi_{j}^{\theta}
, one has

A^{\theta}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{j}^{\theta}\;\!\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}
.

The next step consists in exhibiting the spectral representation of
H_{0}^{\theta}
.
For that purpose, we first define the sets

I_{j}^{\theta}:=\big(\lambda_{j}^{\theta}-2,\lambda_{j}^{\theta}+2\big)\quad\hbox{and}\quad I^{\theta}:=\cup_{j=1}^{N}I^{\theta}_{j},

with
\lambda_{j}^{\theta}
the eigenvalues of
A^{\theta}
.
Also, we consider for
\lambda\in I^{\theta}
the fiber Hilbert space

\mathscr{H}^{\theta}(\lambda):=\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\nolimits\big\{\mathcal{P}^{\theta}_{j}\mathbb{C}^{N}\mid\hbox{$j\in\{1,\dots,N\}$ such that
$\lambda\in I_{j}^{\theta}$}\big\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{N},

and the corresponding direct integral Hilbert space

\mathscr{H}^{\theta}:=\int_{I^{\theta}}^{\oplus}\mathscr{H}^{\theta}(\lambda)\;\!\mathrm{d}\lambda.

(2.2)

Then, the map
\mathscr{F}^{\theta}:\mathfrak{h}\to\mathscr{H}^{\theta}

acting on
\mathfrak{f}\in\mathfrak{h}
and for a.e.
\lambda\in I^{\theta}
as

\big(\mathscr{F}^{\theta}\mathfrak{f}\big)(\lambda):=\pi^{-1/2}\sum_{\{j\mid\lambda\in I_{j}^{\theta}\}}\big(4-\big(\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}\big)^{2}\big)^{-1/4}\;\!\mathcal{P}^{\theta}_{j}\mathfrak{f}\left(\arccos\left(\tfrac{\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}}{2}\right)\right).

is unitary.
In addition,
\mathscr{F}^{\theta}
diagonalises the Hamiltonian
H_{0}^{\theta}
, namely for all

\zeta\in\mathscr{H}^{\theta}
and a.e.
\lambda\in I^{\theta}
one has

\big(\mathscr{F}^{\theta}H_{0}^{\theta}\;\!(\mathscr{F}^{\theta})^{*}\zeta\big)(\lambda)=\lambda\;\!\zeta(\lambda)=\big(X^{\theta}\zeta\big)(\lambda),

with
X^{\theta}
the (bounded) operator of multiplication by the variable in

\mathscr{H}^{\theta}
.
One infers that

H_{0}^{\theta}
has purely absolutely continuous spectrum equal to

\textstyle\sigma(H_{0}^{\theta})=\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{Ran}}\nolimits(X^{\theta})}=\overline{I^{\theta}}=\big[\big(\min_{j}\lambda_{j}^{\theta}\big)-2,\big(\max_{j}\lambda_{j}^{\theta}\big)+2\big]\subset[-4,4].

Note that we use the notation
\overline{I}
for the closure of a set
I\subset\mathbb{R}
.
We also set
\sigma_{\rm p}(H^{\theta})
for the point spectrum of
H^{\theta}
.

The spectral representation of
H^{\theta}_{0}
leads also naturally to the
notion of thresholds: these real values correspond to a change
of multiplicity of the spectrum.
Clearly, the set
\mathcal{T}^{\theta}
of thresholds for the operator
H_{0}^{\theta}

is given by

\mathcal{T}^{\theta}:=\big\{\lambda_{j}^{\theta}\pm 2\mid j\in\{1,\dots,N\}\big\}.

The main spectral result for
H^{\theta}
has been presented in
[
31
, Prop. 1.2]
,
and will be recalled when necessary. About scattering theory, since the difference
H^{\theta}-H^{\theta}_{0}
is a finite rank
operator we observe that the wave operators

W_{\pm}^{\theta}:=\slim_{t\to\pm\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{itH^{\theta}}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH^{\theta}_{0}}

(2.3)

exist and are complete, see
[
23
, Thm. X.4.4]
.
As a consequence of the completeness, the scattering operator

S^{\theta}:=(W_{+}^{\theta})^{*}W_{-}^{\theta}

is a unitary operator in
\mathfrak{h}
commuting with
H^{\theta}_{0}
, and thus
S^{\theta}
is
decomposable in the spectral representation of
H^{\theta}_{0}
, that is
for
\zeta\in\mathscr{H}^{\theta}
and a.e.
\lambda\in I^{\theta}
, one has

\big(\mathscr{F}^{\theta}S^{\theta}(\mathscr{F}^{\theta})^{*}\zeta\big)(\lambda)=S^{\theta}(\lambda)\zeta(\lambda),

with the scattering matrix
S^{\theta}(\lambda)
a unitary operator in
\mathscr{H}^{\theta}(\lambda)
.
For simplicity, we shall often just write
S^{\theta}(X^{\theta})
for the operator

\mathscr{F}^{\theta}S^{\theta}(\mathscr{F}^{\theta})^{*}
since this operator corresponds to a matrix-valued multiplication operator.

Finally, for
j,j^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,N\}
and for

\lambda\in\big(I^{\theta}_{j}\cap I^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}\big)\setminus\big(\mathcal{T}^{\theta}\cup\sigma_{\rm p}(H^{\theta})\big)

let us define the channel scattering matrix

S^{\theta}(\lambda)_{j,j^{\prime}}:=\mathcal{P}^{\theta}_{j}S^{\theta}(\lambda)\mathcal{P}^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}

and consider the map

\big(I^{\theta}_{j}\cap I^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}\big)\setminus\big(\mathcal{T}^{\theta}\cup\sigma_{\rm p}(H^{\theta})\big)\ni\lambda\mapsto S^{\theta}(\lambda)_{j,j^{\prime}}\in\mathscr{B}\big(\mathcal{P}_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}\mathbb{C}^{N};\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}\mathbb{C}^{N}\big).

The continuity of the scattering matrix at embedded eigenvalues has been shown in

[
31
, Thm. 3.10]
, while its behavior at thresholds has been studied in

[
31
, Thm. 3.9]
.
This latter result can be summarized as follows: For each
\lambda\in\mathcal{T}^{\theta}
,
a channel can already be opened at the
energy
\lambda
(in which case the existence and the equality of the
limits from the right and from the left is proved), it can open at the energy
\lambda
(in
which case only the existence of the limit from the right is proved), or it can
close at the energy
\lambda
(in which case only the existence of the
limit from the left is proved).

2.2
C*-algebras

We now briefly sketch the algebraic framework which played a crucial role in
[
5
]

for getting an index theorem in scattering theory.
In this reference, the case
\theta\in(0,\pi)
was thoroughly investigated
(and it applies to
\theta\in(\pi,2\pi)
as well).
In this section we generalize the construction for
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
.
We firstly exhibit an algebra
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}
of multiplication operators acting on
\mathscr{H}^{\theta}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}\subset\mathscr{B}\big(\mathscr{H}^{\theta}\big)
with
a\in\mathcal{A}^{\theta}
if for any
\zeta\in\mathscr{H}^{\theta}
and
\lambda\in I^{\theta}
one has

\big[a\zeta\big](\lambda)\equiv\big[a(X^{\theta})\zeta\big](\lambda)=a(\lambda)\zeta(\lambda)

and for
j,j^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,N\}
the maps

I_{j}^{\theta}\cap I_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}\ni\lambda\mapsto a_{j,j^{\prime}}(\lambda):=\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}a(\lambda)\mathcal{P}_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}\in\mathscr{B}\big(\mathscr{H}^{\theta}(\lambda)\big)\subset\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{C}^{N})

(2.4)

belongs to
C_{0}\big(I_{j}^{\theta}\cap I_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta};\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{C}^{N})\big)
if
\lambda_{j}^{\theta}\neq\lambda_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}
while it belongs to
C\big(\overline{I_{j}^{\theta}};\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{C}^{N})\big)
if
\lambda_{j}^{\theta}=\lambda_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}
.

In other words, the function defined in (
2.4
)
is a continuous function vanishing at the boundaries of the intersection
I_{j}^{\theta}\cap I_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}
when these two intervals are not equal, and having limits at the boundaries of these intervals when they coincide.

Remark 2.3
.

In the sequel, we shall use the notation
a_{j,j^{\prime}}
for
\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}a\mathcal{P}_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}

even if this notation is slightly misleading. Indeed,
a_{j,j^{\prime}}(\lambda)
exists if and only if

\lambda\in\overline{I_{j}^{\theta}\cap I_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}}

If
\lambda\not\in\overline{I_{j}^{\theta}}

More precisely, with the standard bra-ket notation, we shall write

a_{j,j^{\prime}}=\mathfrak{a}_{j,j^{\prime}}\otimes\;\!\tfrac{1}{N}|\xi^{\theta}_{j}\rangle\langle\xi^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}|

\lambda_{j}^{\theta}=\lambda_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}

Thus, with these notations and for any

a(\lambda)=\sum_{\{j,j^{\prime}\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j},\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}\}}\mathfrak{a}_{j,j^{\prime}}(\lambda)\otimes\;\!\tfrac{1}{N}|\xi^{\theta}_{j}\rangle\langle\xi^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}|

By the above abuse of notation, we shall conveniently write

2.3
Formulas for the wave operators

So far, all values of
\theta
have been treated similarly.
However, there are two main differences between the case
\theta\in\{0,\pi\}
and the
generic case
\theta\in(0,2\pi)\setminus\{\pi\}
. Firstly, the multiplicity of the eigenvalues

\lambda^{\theta}_{j}
is no more always
1
, and secondly the explicit formulas for the wave operators are, in a very special case for
\theta=0
, slightly more involved.
For now, we shall disregard this intricate case and come back to it later.
However, in order to define it, we need additional notations.
The material of this section is borrowed from
[
31
, Sec. 3–5]
.

We firstly decompose the diagonal matrix
\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\nolimits(v)
, with entries
v(1),\dots,v(N)
on the diagonal, as a product

\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\nolimits(v)=\mathfrak{u}\;\!\mathfrak{v}^{2},

where
\mathfrak{v}:=|\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\nolimits(v)|^{1/2}
and
\mathfrak{u}:=\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}\nolimits\big(\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\nolimits(v)\big)
is the diagonal matrix
with components

\mathfrak{u}_{j,j}=\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}\nolimits\big(\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\nolimits(v)\big)_{j,j}=\begin{cases}+1&\hbox{if $v(j)\geq 0$}\\
-1&\hbox{if $v(j)<0$,}\end{cases}\quad j\in\{1,\dots,N\}.

We also introduce the bounded operator
G:\mathfrak{h}\to\mathbb{C}^{N}
defined by

G=\mathfrak{v}\gamma_{0}
with

\gamma_{0}:\mathfrak{h}\to\mathbb{C}^{N}
given by

(\gamma_{0}\mathfrak{f})_{j}:=\int_{0}^{\pi}\mathfrak{f}_{j}(\omega)\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{\pi},\quad\mathfrak{f}\in\mathfrak{h},~j\in\{1,\dots,N\}.

Its adjoint
G^{*}:\mathbb{C}^{N}\to\mathfrak{h}
satisfies

(G^{*}\xi)_{j}(\omega):=|v(j)|^{1/2}\xi_{j}
for

\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
,
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, and
\omega\in[0,\pi)
.
For the resolvent, we set
R_{0}^{\theta}(z):=(H_{0}^{\theta}-z)^{-1}
for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
.

From the stationary approach of scattering theory, one readily infers that
for suitable
\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{g}\in\mathfrak{h}

\big\langle\big(W_{-}^{\theta}-1\big)\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{g}\big\rangle_{\mathfrak{h}}=-\int_{\sigma(H_{0}^{\theta})}\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\big\langle G^{*}M^{\theta}(\lambda+i\varepsilon)G\delta_{\varepsilon}\big(H_{0}^{\theta}-\lambda\big)\mathfrak{f},R_{0}^{\theta}(\lambda-i\varepsilon)\mathfrak{g}\big\rangle_{\mathfrak{h}}\;\!\mathrm{d}\lambda,

with

M^{\theta}(z):=\big(\mathfrak{u}+GR_{0}^{\theta}(z)G^{*}\big)^{-1},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}

and

\delta_{\varepsilon}\big(H_{0}^{\theta}-\lambda\big):=\frac{\pi^{-1}\varepsilon}{(H_{0}^{\theta}-\lambda)^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}

with
\varepsilon>0
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
In order to derive an expression for the operator

\big(W_{-}^{\theta}-1\big)
in the spectral representation of
H_{0}^{\theta}
we define the set

\textstyle\mathscr{D}^{\theta}:=\left\{\zeta\in\mathscr{H}^{\theta}\mid\zeta=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\zeta_{j},~\zeta_{j}\in C^{\infty}_{\rm c}\big(I^{\theta}_{j}\setminus\big(\mathcal{T}^{\theta}\cup\sigma_{\rm p}(H^{\theta})\big);\mathcal{P}^{\theta}_{j}\mathbb{C}^{N}\big)\right\},

which is dense in
\mathscr{H}^{\theta}
because
\mathcal{T}^{\theta}
is countable and

\sigma_{\rm p}(H^{\theta})
is closed and of Lebesgue measure
0
.
Then, it is shown in the mentioned reference that for

\zeta,\zeta^{\prime}\in\mathscr{D}^{\theta}
one has

\displaystyle\big\langle\mathscr{F}^{\theta}\big(W_{-}^{\theta}-1\big)(\mathscr{F}^{\theta})^{*}\zeta^{\prime},\zeta\big\rangle_{\mathscr{H}^{\theta}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{I^{\theta}_{j}}\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\left\langle\mathfrak{v}M^{\theta}(\lambda+i\varepsilon)\mathfrak{v}\;\!\gamma_{0}(\mathscr{F}^{\theta})^{*}\delta_{\varepsilon}\big(X^{\theta}-\lambda\big)\zeta^{\prime},\int_{I_{j}^{\theta}}\frac{\beta_{j}^{\theta}(\mu)^{-1}}{\mu-\lambda+i\varepsilon}\;\!\zeta_{j}(\mu)\;\!\mathrm{d}\mu\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{N}}\mathrm{d}\lambda

(2.9)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{\sigma(H^{\theta}_{0})\setminus I^{\theta}_{j}}\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\left\langle\mathfrak{v}M^{\theta}(\lambda+i\varepsilon)\mathfrak{v}\;\!\gamma_{0}(\mathscr{F}^{\theta})^{*}\delta_{\varepsilon}\big(X^{\theta}-\lambda\big)\zeta^{\prime},\int_{I_{j}^{\theta}}\frac{\beta_{j}^{\theta}(\mu)^{-1}}{\mu-\lambda+i\varepsilon}\;\!\zeta_{j}(\mu)\;\!\mathrm{d}\mu\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{N}}\mathrm{d}\lambda

(2.10)

with the positive numbers

\beta_{j}^{\theta}(z):=\big|\big(z-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}\big)^{2}-4\big|^{1/4},\quad j\in\{1,\dots,N\},~z\in\mathbb{C}.

Based on these expressions, one of the main results of
[
31
]
has been to provide
a rather explicit formula for
W_{-}^{\theta}
. Before exhibiting them,
we still need to introduce the precise definition of the intricate case.
As emphasized in
[
31
, Eq. 1.16]
this situation takes place only for very specific potentials.
This can be easily observed from the following definition, since
\big(\xi^{0}_{N}\big)_{k}=1
for any
k\in\{1,\dots,N\}
,
while
\big(\xi^{0}_{N/2}\big)_{k}=(-1)^{k}
.

Definition 2.7
.

The
intricate case
stands for the case where
\theta=0
,

N\in 2\mathbb{N}
, and
\mathfrak{v}\xi_{N}^{0}
and
\mathfrak{v}\xi^{0}_{N/2}
are linearly dependent.

We now use again the unitary transformation introduced in (
2.6
)
and (
2.7
), and the operators
X
and
D
introduced right after.
By using these notations, it has been shown in
[
31
, Sec. 4.4]
that for any

\theta\in[0,2\pi)
the equality

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}^{\theta}\big(W_{-}^{\theta}-1\big)(\mathscr{F}^{\theta})^{*}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathcal{V}^{\theta})^{*}\big\{\big(1-\tanh(\pi D)-i\cosh(\pi D)^{-1}\tanh(X)\big)\otimes 1_{N}\big\}\mathcal{V}^{\theta}\big(S^{\theta}(X^{\theta})-1\big)+K^{\theta}

(2.11)

holds, with
K^{\theta}\in\mathscr{K}(\mathscr{H}^{\theta})
in the non-intricate cases, and

Observe that this statement leads directly to the next statement:

For any

, but not in the intricate case,

belongs to

3
Quotient algebra and topological outcomes

The
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathscr{E}^{\theta}
was introduced in (
2.8
),
and the affiliation of the wave
operator to this algebra was precisely stated in Proposition
2.8
.
The computation of the quotient of this algebra by the ideal of compact
operators was performed in
[
5
]
only for
\theta\in(0,\pi)
.
The result remains valid for
\theta\in(\pi,2\pi)
but is not valid for the
special cases
\theta\in\{0,\pi\}
. Note that this difference is due to the multiplicity of some eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}^{\theta}
, as explained below.
In this section, we extend the results of
[
5
]
to arbitrary
\theta
.

3.1
Quotient algebra

Our first task is to compute the quotient of the algebra
\mathscr{E}^{\theta}
by the set of compact operators. This construction was exhibited in
[
5
, Sec. 3]
for
\theta\in(0,\pi)
, and our current aim is to extend it to arbitrary
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
. The main difficulty in the extension is that for
\theta\in\{0,\pi\}
, some eigenvalues of
A^{\theta}
, as given in (
2.1
),
are of multiplicity
2
, while they are always of multiplicity
1
for
\theta\not\in\{0,\pi\}
.
Namely, for
\theta\not\in\{0,\pi\}
, the eigenvalues
\lambda^{\theta}_{j}
can be uniquely ordered by increasing order. For example, for
\theta\in(0,\pi)
and for
N
even, we have

\lambda^{\theta}_{\frac{N}{2}}<\lambda^{\theta}_{\frac{N}{2}-1}<\lambda^{\theta}_{\frac{N}{2}+1}<\lambda^{\theta}_{\frac{N}{2}-2}<\lambda^{\theta}_{\frac{N}{2}+2}<\ldots<\lambda^{\theta}_{1}<\lambda^{\theta}_{N-1}<\lambda^{\theta}_{N},

while for
N
odd we have

\lambda^{\theta}_{\frac{N-1}{2}}<\lambda^{\theta}_{\frac{N+1}{2}}<\lambda^{\theta}_{\frac{N-1}{2}-1}<\lambda^{\theta}_{\frac{N+1}{2}+1}<\lambda^{\theta}_{\frac{N-1}{2}-2}<\ldots<\lambda^{\theta}_{1}<\lambda^{\theta}_{N-1}<\lambda^{\theta}_{N}.

A similar unique ordering holds for
\theta\in(\pi,2\pi)
. On the other hand, for
\theta=0
and
N
even we have

-2=\lambda^{0}_{\frac{N}{2}}<\lambda^{0}_{\frac{N}{2}+1}=\lambda^{0}_{\frac{N}{2}-1}<\lambda^{0}_{\frac{N}{2}+2}=\lambda^{0}_{\frac{N}{2}-2}<\ldots<\lambda_{1}^{0}=\lambda^{0}_{N-1}<\lambda^{0}_{N}=2,

while for
N
odd we have

-2<\lambda^{0}_{\frac{N-1}{2}}=\lambda^{0}_{\frac{N+1}{2}}<\lambda^{0}_{\frac{N-3}{2}}=\lambda^{0}_{\frac{N+3}{2}}<\ldots<\lambda_{1}^{0}=\lambda^{0}_{N-1}<\lambda^{0}_{N}=2.

Accordingly, for
\theta=\pi
and
N
even we have

-2<\lambda^{\pi}_{\frac{N}{2}}=\lambda^{\pi}_{\frac{N}{2}-1}<\lambda^{\pi}_{\frac{N}{2}+1}=\lambda^{\pi}_{\frac{N}{2}-2}<\ldots<\lambda_{1}^{\pi}=\lambda^{\pi}_{N-2}<\lambda^{\pi}_{N}=\lambda^{\pi}_{N-1}<2,

while for
N
odd we have

-2=\lambda^{\pi}_{\frac{N-1}{2}}<\lambda^{\pi}_{\frac{N-1}{2}-1}=\lambda^{\pi}_{\frac{N-1}{2}+1}<\ldots<\lambda^{\pi}_{N-2}=\lambda_{1}^{\pi}<\lambda^{\pi}_{N-1}=\lambda^{\pi}_{N}<2.

Thus, if we denote by
M
the number of distinct eigenvalues in the set

\{\lambda^{\theta}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
, then one has

M=N
for
\theta\not\in\{0,\pi\}
, while
M=\frac{N}{2}+1
for
\theta=0
and
N
even,

M=\frac{N+1}{2}
for
\theta\in\{0,\pi\}
and
N
odd, and
M=\frac{N}{2}
for
\theta=\pi
and
N
even.

We are now interested in computing the quotient algebra
\mathcal{Q}^{\theta}:=\mathscr{E}^{\theta}/\mathscr{K}\big(\mathscr{H}^{\theta}\big)
.
As a consequence of
[
5
, Lem. 3.9]
on the compactness of commutators
[\eta(D^{\theta}),a]
, we can focus on elements of the form
\eta(D^{\theta})a

with
\eta\in C_{0}\big([-\infty,+\infty)\big)
and
a\in\mathcal{A}^{\theta}

(introduced in Definition
2.2
).
The starting point is the decomposition

\eta(D^{\theta})\;\!a=\sum_{j,j^{\prime}}\big(\eta(D^{\theta}_{j})\;\!\mathfrak{a}_{j,j^{\prime}}\big)\otimes\;\!\tfrac{1}{N}|\xi^{\theta}_{j}\rangle\langle\xi^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}|

(3.1)

which follows from (
2.5
).
We can then deal with operators of the form

\eta(D^{\theta}_{j})\,\!\mathfrak{a}_{j,j^{\prime}}:\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(I^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}})\to\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(I^{\theta}_{j})
.
However, since
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits\mathfrak{a}_{j,j^{\prime}}\subset\overline{I_{j}^{\theta}\cap I_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}}
, it is
simpler to look at this operator in
\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(I_{j}^{\theta})
.
Thus, if we set
q_{j}:\mathscr{B}\big(\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(I_{j}^{\theta})\big)\to\mathscr{B}\big(\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(I_{j}^{\theta})\big)/\mathscr{K}\big(\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(I_{j}^{\theta})\big)
then the image of
\eta(D^{\theta}_{j})\,\!\mathfrak{a}_{j,j^{\prime}}
through

q_{j}
falls into two distinct situations.

(i)

If
I_{j}^{\theta}\neq I^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}
, then

q_{j}\big(\eta(D^{\theta}_{j})\;\!\mathfrak{a}_{j,j^{\prime}}\big)=\eta(-\infty)\;\!\mathfrak{a}_{j,j^{\prime}}\in C_{0}\big(I^{\theta}_{j}\cap I^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}\big),

(3.2)

(ii)

If
I_{j}^{\theta}=I_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}
(meaning
\lambda^{\theta}_{j}=\lambda^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}})
, then

\displaystyle\begin{split}q_{j}\big(\eta(D^{\theta}_{j})\;\!\mathfrak{a}_{j,j^{\prime}}\big)&=\Big(\eta\;\!\mathfrak{a}_{j,j^{\prime}}(\lambda_{j}^{\theta}-2),\ \eta(-\infty)\;\!\mathfrak{a}_{j,j^{\prime}},\ \eta\;\!\mathfrak{a}_{j,j^{\prime}}(\lambda_{j}^{\theta}+2)\Big)\\
&\qquad\in C_{0}\big((+\infty,-\infty]\big)\oplus C\big(\overline{I^{\theta}_{j}}\big)\oplus C_{0}\big([-\infty,+\infty)\big).\end{split}

(3.3)

These statement can be obtained as in the proof of
[
5
, Lem. 3.8]
by looking
at these operators in
\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R})
through the conjugation by
\mathcal{V}_{j}^{\theta}
. Then, one ends up with operators of the form
\eta(D)\varphi(X)
for
\eta\in C_{0}\big([-\infty,+\infty)\big)

and for
\varphi\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R})
in the first case, and
\varphi\in C\big([-\infty,+\infty]\big)

in the second case. The image of such operators by the quotient map (defined by the compact operators) have been extensively studied in
[
33
, Sec. 4.4]
, from which we infer the results presented above.

Remark 3.1
.

Observe that in the first component of (
3.3
), the interval
[-\infty,+\infty)
has been oriented in the reverse direction. The reason is that the function
introduced in (
3.3
) can be seen as a continuous function on the union of the three intervals

(+\infty,-\infty]\cup\overline{I^{\theta}_{j}}\cup[-\infty,+\infty)

once their endpoints are correctly identified. This observation and this trick will be used several times in the sequel.
Note also that (
3.2
) could be expressed as (
3.3
) by considering
the triple
\big(0,\;\!\eta(-\infty)\mathfrak{a}_{j,j^{\prime}},\;\!0\big)
.

In the next statement we collect the results obtained so far.
However, in order to provide a unified statement for all
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
and for arbitrary
N
, some notations have to be slightly updated.
More precisely, recall that for
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
one has

\lambda_{j}^{\theta}:=2\cos\big(\frac{\theta+2\pi\;\!j}{N}\big)
.
For fixed
\theta
, let us now denote by
\tilde{\lambda}^{\theta}_{1}<\tilde{\lambda}^{\theta}_{2}<\ldots<\tilde{\lambda}^{\theta}_{M}

these
M
distinct values.
As already mentioned, their multiplicities are generically
1
, but can be
2
when
\theta=0
or
\theta=\pi
.
Accordingly, we define the orthogonal projection
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}^{\theta}_{k}
(based on the projections
\mathcal{P}^{\theta}_{j}
corresponding to the eigenvalue(s)
\tilde{\lambda}^{\theta}_{k}=\lambda_{j}^{\theta}
) which is
one dimensional if the corresponding eigenvalue is of multiplicity
1
, and two dimensional when
\lambda_{j}^{\theta}
is of multiplicity
2
.
Finally for
k\in\{1,\dots,M\}
we set

{{\tilde{\mathscr{H}}}^{\theta}}_{k}:=\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\nolimits\big\{\tilde{\mathcal{P}}^{\theta}_{i}\mathbb{C}^{N}\mid i\leq k\big\}.

In order to understand the next statement,
let us mention that a very specific feature appears in the special case
\theta=0

and
N
even. Indeed, in this case
-2
and
2
are eigenvalues of the matrix
A^{0}
,
namely
\lambda^{0}_{N/2}=-2
and
\lambda^{0}_{N}=2

Lemma 4.4
.

Let

satisfying

Proof.

By the colinearity of the two vectors one infers that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{v}\mathcal{P}^{0}_{N}\mathfrak{v}+i\mathfrak{v}\mathcal{P}^{0}_{N/2}\mathfrak{v}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\Big(\big|\mathfrak{v}\xi_{N}^{0}\big\rangle\big\langle\mathfrak{v}\xi_{N}^{0}\big|+i\big|\mathfrak{v}\xi_{N/2}^{0}\big\rangle\big\langle\mathfrak{v}\xi_{N/2}^{0}\big|\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}(1+i)\big|\mathfrak{v}\xi_{N}^{0}\big\rangle\big\langle\mathfrak{v}\xi_{N}^{0}\big|

As a consequence, one has

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2}\mathcal{P}^{0}_{N/2}\mathfrak{v}I_{0}(0)^{-1}\mathfrak{v}\mathcal{P}^{0}_{N}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\big|\xi_{N/2}^{0}\big\rangle\big\langle\mathfrak{v}\xi_{N/2}^{0}\big|\frac{2N}{\|v\|_{\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits}^{2}(1+i)}\Big(\big|\widehat{\mathfrak{v}\xi_{N}^{0}}\big\rangle\big\langle\widehat{\mathfrak{v}\xi_{N}^{0}}\big|\Big)^{-1}\big|\mathfrak{v}\xi_{N}^{0}\big\rangle\big\langle\xi_{N}^{0}\big|

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}(1-i)\alpha\frac{1}{N}\big|\xi_{N/2}^{0}\big\rangle\big\langle\widehat{\mathfrak{v}\xi_{N}^{0}}\big|\Big(\big|\widehat{\mathfrak{v}\xi_{N}^{0}}\big\rangle\big\langle\widehat{\mathfrak{v}\xi_{N}^{0}}\big|\Big)^{-1}\big|\widehat{\mathfrak{v}\xi_{N}^{0}}\big\rangle\big\langle\xi_{N}^{0}\big|

as expected.
∎

4.3
Even / odd representation and Cordes algebra

In this section we develop some preliminaries for the
C^{*}
-algebraic framework
fully developed in the subsequent sections. As already emphasized, the main difference with the
cases already studied in
[
5
]
is the non-compactness
of the terms
A_{N/2,N}
and
A_{N,N/2}
exhibited in the previous section.
Not only these two terms are not compact, but in addition they are not only defined
by functions of
X
and
D
, see (
4.12
) and (
4.13
): they also contain
the operator
I
which can not be simply expressed in terms of
X
and
D
.
In order to accommodate this new operator, the framework has to be slightly enlarged:
We need to consider one more unitary
transformation, namely the decomposition into even and odd functions on
\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R})
.
It is then easily observed that the operator
I
acts in a very simple form on even and on odd functions.

Let us consider

\mathcal{U}:\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R})\to\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathbb{C}^{2})
given by

\mathcal{U}f:=\sqrt{2}\begin{pmatrix}f_{\rm e}\\
f_{\rm o}\end{pmatrix}\quad{\rm and}\quad\big[\mathcal{U}^{*}\big(\begin{smallmatrix}f_{1}\\
f_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\big)\big](x):=\textstyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big[f_{1}(|x|)+\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}\nolimits(x)f_{2}(|x|)\big],

for
f\in\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R})
,
\big(\begin{smallmatrix}f_{1}\\
f_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\big)\in\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathbb{C}^{2})
, and
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
Here
f_{\rm e},f_{\rm o}
denote the even and the odd part of
f
.
Then, one observes that if
m
is a function on
\mathbb{R}

\mathcal{U}m(X)\mathcal{U}^{*}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}m_{\rm e}(L)~&m_{\rm o}(L)\\
m_{\rm o}(L)~&m_{\rm e}(L)\end{smallmatrix}\right),

where
L
stands for the multiplication operator by the variable in
\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.

In order to consider
\mathcal{U}m(D)\mathcal{U}^{*}
, let us denote by
\mathscr{F}_{1}
the usual unitary Fourier transform
in
\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R})
, and let
\mathscr{F}_{\rm N}
,
\mathscr{F}_{\rm D}
be the unitary cosine and sine transforms on
\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, respectively. The subscripts
\rm N
and
\rm D
are related to the Neumann Laplacian and the Dirichlet Laplacian in
\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, which are diagonalised by
\mathscr{F}_{\rm N}
and
\mathscr{F}_{\rm D}
, respectively.
Note also that these operators correspond to their own inverse.
It is then easily checked that

\mathcal{U}\mathscr{F}_{1}\mathcal{U}^{*}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathscr{F}_{\rm N}&0\\
0&-i\mathscr{F}_{\rm D}\end{smallmatrix}\right).

In addition, by a straightforward computation one gets

\mathcal{U}m(D)\mathcal{U}^{*}=\mathcal{U}\mathscr{F}_{1}^{*}m(X)\mathscr{F}_{1}\mathcal{U}^{*}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathscr{F}_{\rm N}m_{\rm e}(L)\mathscr{F}_{\rm N}&-i\mathscr{F}_{\rm N}m_{\rm o}(L)\mathscr{F}_{\rm D}\\
i\mathscr{F}_{\rm D}m_{\rm o}(L)\mathscr{F}_{\rm N}&\mathscr{F}_{\rm D}m_{\rm e}(L)\mathscr{F}_{\rm D}\end{smallmatrix}\right).

For the final step, let us introduce the unitary group of dilation in
\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R})

by
[U_{t}f](x)=\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{t/2}f\big(\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{t}x\big)
for any
f\in\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R})
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and
t\in\mathbb{R}
, whose generator is denoted by
A
. Similarly, the dilation group in
\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is defined by

[U_{t}^{+}f](\ell)=\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{t/2}f\big(\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{t}\ell\big)
for any
f\in\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R}_{+})
,
\ell\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and
t\in\mathbb{R}
, with generator denoted by
A_{+}
.
Then one easily observes that

\mathcal{U}m(A)\mathcal{U}^{*}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}m(A_{+})&0\\
0&m(A_{+})\end{smallmatrix}\right).

In addition, the following equality holds:

i\mathscr{F}_{\rm N}\mathscr{F}_{\rm D}=-\tanh(\pi A_{+})+i\cosh(\pi A_{+})^{-1}=:\phi(A_{+}).

We refer for example to
[
16
, Prop. 4.13]
for a proof of the above equality.
We also stress that
|\phi|=1
, which will be used implicitly in the following computations.
Then, if we define the Neumann Laplacian by
-\Delta:=\mathscr{F}_{\rm N}L^{2}\mathscr{F}_{\rm N}
,
we end up with

\mathcal{U}m(D)\mathcal{U}^{*}=\mathcal{U}\mathscr{F}_{1}^{*}m(X)\mathscr{F}_{1}\mathcal{U}^{*}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}m_{\rm e}\big(\sqrt{-\Delta}\big)&-m_{\rm o}\big(\sqrt{-\Delta}\big)\phi(A_{+})\\
-\overline{\phi}(A_{+})m_{\rm o}\big(\sqrt{-\Delta}\big)&\ \overline{\phi}(A_{+})m_{\rm e}\big(\sqrt{-\Delta}\big)\phi(A_{+})\end{smallmatrix}\right).

(4.14)

The next step is to look at the image of the operators
A_{N/2,N/2}
,
A_{N/2,N}
,
A_{N,N/2}
, and
A_{N,N}
inside this new representation. The resulting expressions are rather long,
but the computation is easy, once we take into account that the commutators between these operators are compact. Indeed, it is shown in
[
15
, Sec. 5.5–5.7]

that any commutator between
a(A_{+})
,
b(L)
, and
c(-\Delta)
belongs to
\mathscr{K}\big(\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R}_{+})\big)
if
a\in C\big([-\infty,+\infty]\big)
and
b,c\in C\big([0,+\infty]\big)
.
Thus, we present the results modulo compact operators, which means
that the sign
\cong
corresponds to an equality modulo
\mathscr{K}\big(\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathbb{C}^{2})\big)
.
One gets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}\;\!A_{N/2,N/2}\;\!\mathcal{U}^{*}

\displaystyle\cong\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&\tanh(\pi\sqrt{-\Delta})\phi(A_{+})-i\mathop{\mathrm{sech}}\nolimits(\pi\sqrt{-\Delta})\tanh(L)\\
\overline{\phi}(A_{+})\tanh(\pi\sqrt{-\Delta})-i\mathop{\mathrm{sech}}\nolimits(\pi\sqrt{-\Delta})\tanh(L)&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\Big[\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathfrak{s}^{0}_{N/2,N/2}(-2+2\tanh(\cdot))_{\rm e}(L)&\ \mathfrak{s}^{0}_{N/2,N/2}(-2+2\tanh(\cdot))_{\rm o}(L)\\
\mathfrak{s}^{0}_{N/2,N/2}(-2+2\tanh(\cdot))_{\rm o}(L)&\ \mathfrak{s}^{0}_{N/2,N/2}(-2+2\tanh(\cdot))_{\rm e}(L)\end{smallmatrix}\right)-\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\Big],

(4.15)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}\;\!A_{N,N}\;\!\mathcal{U}^{*}

\displaystyle\cong\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&\tanh(\pi\sqrt{-\Delta})\phi(A_{+})-i\mathop{\mathrm{sech}}\nolimits(\pi\sqrt{-\Delta})\tanh(L)\\
\overline{\phi}(A_{+})\tanh(\pi\sqrt{-\Delta})-i\mathop{\mathrm{sech}}\nolimits(\pi\sqrt{-\Delta})\tanh(L)&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\Big[\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathfrak{s}^{0}_{N,N}(2+2\tanh(\cdot))_{\rm e}(L)&\ \mathfrak{s}^{0}_{N,N}(2+2\tanh(\cdot))_{\rm o}(L)\\
\mathfrak{s}^{0}_{N,N}(2+2\tanh(\cdot))_{\rm o}(L)&\ \mathfrak{s}^{0}_{N,N}(2+2\tanh(\cdot))_{\rm e}(L)\end{smallmatrix}\right)-\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\Big].

(4.16)

Similarly, one has

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}\;\!A_{N/2,N}\;\!\mathcal{U}^{*}

\displaystyle\cong\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\overline{\psi}_{\rm e}\big(\sqrt{-\Delta}\big)&-\overline{\psi}_{\rm o}\big(\sqrt{-\Delta}\big)\phi(A_{+})\\
-\overline{\phi}(A_{+})\overline{\psi}_{\rm o}\big(\sqrt{-\Delta}\big)&\ \overline{\psi}_{\rm e}\big(\sqrt{-\Delta}\big)\end{smallmatrix}\right)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}(m_{N/2,N})_{\rm e}(L)~&(m_{N/2,N})_{\rm o}(L)\\
(m_{N/2,N})_{\rm o}(L)~&(m_{N/2,N})_{\rm e}(L)\end{smallmatrix}\right)

(4.17)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}\;\!A_{N,N/2}\;\!\mathcal{U}^{*}

\displaystyle\cong-\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\overline{\psi}_{\rm e}\big(\sqrt{-\Delta}\big)&-\overline{\psi}_{\rm o}\big(\sqrt{-\Delta}\big)\phi(A_{+})\\
-\overline{\phi}(A_{+})\overline{\psi}_{\rm o}\big(\sqrt{-\Delta}\big)&\ \overline{\psi}_{\rm e}\big(\sqrt{-\Delta}\big)\end{smallmatrix}\right)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}(m_{N,N/2})_{\rm e}(L)~&(m_{N,N/2})_{\rm o}(L)\\
(m_{N,N/2})_{\rm o}(L)~&(m_{N,N/2})_{\rm e}(L)\end{smallmatrix}\right)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right).

(4.18)

Let us now recall a construction of Cordes.
In
[
15
, Sec. V.7]
, the following
C^{*}
-subalgebra
of
\mathscr{B}\big(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\big)
is introduced:

\mathscr{E}_{\mathrm{C}}:=C^{*}\Big(a_{i}(A_{+})b_{i}(L)c_{i}(-\Delta)\mid a_{i}\in C\big([-\infty,+\infty]\big),\ b_{i},c_{i}\in C\big([0,+\infty]\big)\Big).

It is then shown in
[
15
, Thm. V.7.3]
that the quotient algebra
\mathscr{E}_{\mathrm{C}}/\mathscr{K}\big(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\big)
is isomorphic to
C(\hexagon)
, the set of continuous functions defined on the edges of a hexagon
see Figure
3
.
For an operator of the form

a(A_{+})\;\!b(L)\;\!c\big(\sqrt{-\Delta}\big)\in\mathscr{E}_{\mathrm{C}}
,
its image in the quotient algebra takes the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\Gamma^{1}(\ell):=a(-\infty)\;\!b(\ell)\;\!c(+\infty),\qquad\ell\in[0,+\infty],\\
&\Gamma^{2}(\xi):=a(-\infty)\;\!b(+\infty)\;\!c(\xi),\qquad\xi\in[+\infty,0],\\
&\Gamma^{3}(s):=a(s)\;\!b(+\infty)\;\!c(0),\qquad s\in[-\infty,+\infty],\\

\Gamma=(\Gamma^{1},\Gamma^{2},\Gamma^{3},\Gamma^{4},\Gamma^{5},\Gamma^{6})

\mathop{\mathrm{ind}}\nolimits\big([\Gamma]_{1}\big)=[1-(\mathcal{W}^{0}_{-})^{*}\mathcal{W}_{-}^{0}]_{0}-[1-\mathcal{W}_{-}^{0}(\mathcal{W}_{-}^{0})^{*}]_{0}=-[\mathcal{E}_{\rm p}(H^{0})]_{0},

through the unitary conjugation by the unit defined by

Let us emphasize that the equality (

K_{1}

of quantities related to scattering theory, and the equivalence class in
K_{0}
of the projection
on the bound states of
H^{0}

The standard formulation of Levinson’s theorem is an equality between numbers.
Thus, our last task is to extract a numerical equality from (

K_{1}
class of the scattering matrix with the Chern character of a suitable spectral triple, as in
[
2

. For this specific case, we proceed in a more elementary way by using the determinant and winding number directly.
Thus, on
M_{4}\big(\mathscr{K}(\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R}_{+}))\big)

C\big(\hexagon;M_{4}(\mathbb{C})\big)

the winding number of the pointwise determinant is the correct notion to be used.

When computing the winding number, and pairing the equality (

Theorem 4.10

For

\#\sigma_{\rm p}(H^{0})=\mathrm{Var}\big(\lambda\to\det S^{0}(\lambda)\big)+\begin{cases}2&\hbox{ in case (i),}\\
3/2&\hbox{ in case (ii)--(iv),}\end{cases}

In the previous theorem, only the case

\mathrm{Var}\big(\lambda\to\det S^{0}(\lambda)\big)+2-\frac{\#\big\{k\mid\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}^{0}_{k}(\lambda^{0}_{k}\pm 2)=1\big\}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}=\#\sigma_{\rm p}(H^{0}).

(4.27)

4.5

Lemma 4.12

For any

and

\iota\psi(D^{0}_{N})\varphi(X^{0})\otimes\tfrac{1}{N}|\xi^{0}_{N/2}\rangle\langle\xi^{0}_{N}|

\mathscr{E}^{0}_{2}+\mathscr{K}(\mathscr{H}^{0})

Proof.

This easy proof is omitted but is based on the following observation: For any

\varphi_{-}\in C_{0}\big([-4,0)\big)

\iota\psi(D^{0}_{N})\varphi(X^{0})\varphi_{+}(X^{0})\qquad\hbox{ and }\qquad\varphi_{-}(X^{0})\iota\psi(D^{0}_{N})\varphi(X^{0})

\varphi_{+}(X^{0})\psi(D^{0}_{N})\varphi(X^{0})\iota^{*}\qquad\hbox{ and }\qquad\psi(D^{0}_{N})\varphi(X^{0})\iota^{*}\varphi_{-}(X^{0})

\mathscr{K}\big(\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(-4,0),\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(0,4)\big)

Lemma 4.13
.

When
\mathfrak{v}\xi^{0}_{N/2}
and

are linearly independent,

\mathfrak{s}^{0}_{N/2,N/2}(0_{-})=-1

\mathfrak{s}^{0}_{N/2,N/2}(0_{-})=-i

\mathfrak{s}^{0}_{N,N}(0_{+})=i

On the other hand, Lemma

3.4

Here, we shall use that the determinant of the function

Lemma 4.14

For

The case when

\alpha\in\{-1,1\}

\mathfrak{v}\xi^{0}_{N/2}=\alpha\mathfrak{v}\xi^{0}_{N}

For
\alpha^{2}=1

\mathfrak{s}^{0}_{N/2,N/2}(0_{-})=-i

\mathfrak{s}^{0}_{N,N}(0_{+})=i

4cd+(b+a)(f-e)=-i\eta_{+}(\xi),
