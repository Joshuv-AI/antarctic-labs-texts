Failure Modes of Screw Fixation in Slipped Capital Femoral Epiphysis: A Proposed Life-Cycle Classification
Authors: Graydon Dietrich, Zayne Browning, Jerard Tambunan, Ryan Dieter, Kurt Soloniuk, Dewayne Weaver
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Screw fixation is the standard surgical treatment for slipped capital femoral epiphysis. Screw-related adverse events, including implant breakage, joint penetration, fixation failure, growth-mediated construct change, removal failure, and postremoval fracture, are inconsistently reported throughout the pediatric orthopaedic literature. In this current concept review, we propose a practical, life-cycle classification encompassing implant insertion, in vivo fixation, growth and remodeling, implant removal, and postremoval follow-up. Reported screw-related failures cluster into four practical evidence domains: placement-related risk, construct-longevity failure, mechanical implant failure, and removal/postremoval complications. These domains are organized into seven chronological life-cycle categories. A four-domain synthesis mapped to a seven-stage life-cycle classification provides a practical framework and shared vocabulary for preoperative counseling, implant selection, postoperative surveillance, removal planning, and standardized reporting.Key Concepts(1)Screw-related failures after pediatric proximal femoral fixation are heterogeneous and can occur at any point in the implant life cycle, from insertion through long-term postremoval follow-up.(2)Reported failures can be grouped into four practical evidence domains: placement-related risk, construct-longevity failure, mechanical implant failure, and removal/postremoval complications, which map to seven chronological life-cycle categories.(3)Construct-level decisions, including single- versus two-screw fixation, screw-thread configuration, and implant material, entail different trade-offs across the insertion, fixation, growth, and removal stages.(4)Because pediatric proximal femoral fixation is performed in a growing skeleton, the relationship among the screw, physis, epiphysis, and femoral neck may change over time, warranting continued surveillance even after technically successful fixation.(5)Implant removal is a distinct failure window; removal-related and postremoval complications should be anticipated during preoperative counseling rather than assumed to be benign.

Introduction

Screw fixation is the standard surgical treatment for slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE), one of the most common hip disorders in adolescents. The incidence of SCFE has been reported at approximately 2-10 per 100,000 children, depending on geography, era, and case ascertainment [1,2]. Additionally, epidemiologic literature links SCFE with obesity and increasing body mass index [1,2].

The screw construct appears straightforward, but failure modes are heterogeneous. Reported events include malposition and joint penetration, slip progression, screw breakage, growth-mediated construct changes, difficult removal, and postremoval subtrochanteric fracture [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. These events are reported in case reports, retrospective series, technical notes, and biomechanical studies, often with varying definitions and denominators.

The existing SCFE classifications are structured around the slip rather than the implant. SCFE severity is measured using the Southwick angle and classified as mild (grade I), moderate (grade II), or severe (grade III) [9,10]. The temporal grading system is based on symptom duration and onset (acute, chronic, acute-on-chronic), while stability is classified using Loder’s system as stable versus unstable based on the ability to bear weight, with instability associated with potential osteonecrosis [11]. Complications are currently reported by screw-related events, clinical outcomes, or surgical technique. None of the current SCFE classifications organize implant failures by the implant life cycle, leaving these events without a shared vocabulary.

A targeted narrative review was conducted in accordance with the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles [12]. The objective was to synthesize representative literature on screw-related adverse events following pediatric proximal femoral fixation for SCFE and to propose a practical seven-stage life-cycle classification system.

Approximately 30 manuscripts formed the initial evidence base. Additional articles were identified through citation chaining and targeted searches to ensure that each proposed life-cycle category was adequately supported. The evidence base was heterogeneous and included in vitro biomechanical studies, retrospective clinical series, technical reports, and case reports. Study quality was appraised qualitatively rather than using a formal risk-of-bias tool. Each source was characterized by its design and level of evidence and weighted accordingly in the synthesis. Findings were organized chronologically and conceptually to develop a seven-stage classification system that reflects the implant life cycle from insertion through postremoval follow-up. Consistent with narrative review methodology, sources were selected for their relevance to the implant life cycle and interpreted through the clinical and conceptual perspective of the author team.

This review organizes the available evidence into four practical domains—placement risk, construct-longevity failure, mechanical implant failure, and removal/postremoval complications—and maps these domains onto a 7-stage implant life-cycle classification. This review therefore proposes a novel life-cycle classification system for screw-related failure following pediatric proximal femoral fixation.

Failure modes by life-cycle stage

The following sections use the 7-stage structure as a practical reporting framework. The categories should not be interpreted as equally represented in the literature; the available evidence is strongest for placement, biomechanical fixation, and removal-related complications and weaker for pure mechanical failure and postremoval complications.

Intraoperative insertion-related failure

Malposition is a key insertion-related failure because the initial screw or pin position can influence fixation behavior and articular risk. In an early retrospective study of 80 SCFE patients, Stambough [3] reported that pin placement and number were associated with complication patterns and that a more inferior (varus-oriented) epiphyseal pin position was associated with fewer complications in that series.

Intraarticular penetration remains an important insertion-related concern. Fluoroscopic techniques, such as the approach-withdraw assessment, are used to better define screw-tip position and avoid articular penetration. The technique, first described by Moseley [13] and later incorporated into percutaneous fixation protocols by Morrissy [14], relies on rotating the hip or C-arm under live fluoroscopy to obtain images in which the screw tip approaches and then recedes from the subchondral bone; the closest point of this arc defines the minimum screw-tip-to-subchondral-bone distance [15].

Rooks et al. [16] described a complementary fluoroscopic protocol for systematically evaluating all four quadrants of the femoral head with dynamic imaging and limb repositioning. They proposed maintaining a minimum distance of 8 mm, approximately one-third of the femoral head radius, between the fixation pin and the subchondral bone as a practical working margin.

Even with modern fluoroscopy, the true proximity of the screw tip to the articular surface may be underappreciated. In a postoperative computed tomography (CT) study of 33 hips, Senthi et al. [10] found that anteroposterior radiographs overestimated the screw-tip-to-subchondral-bone distance compared with CT and that some screws were closer to or even penetrated the subchondral bone despite appearing satisfactory on radiographs. In a subsequent cadaveric SCFE model, Heffernan [17] similarly demonstrated that both standard fluoroscopy and intraoperative CT overestimated the screw-tip-to-subchondral-bone distance compared with direct anatomic measurement. Together, these studies indicate that intraoperative imaging may underestimate articular proximity and the risk of penetration [10,17]. This finding bears directly on older safe-margin recommendations. The historical 8-mm threshold proposed by Rooks was itself derived from fluoroscopic assessment. Because fluoroscopy overestimates the screw-tip-to-subchondral-bone distance, a screw judged to sit 8 mm from the joint on intraoperative imaging may in fact lie considerably closer. The older guidance may therefore be less protective than what its numerical precision suggests. Screw-tip position that appears acceptable during surgery warrants cautious interpretation.

Guidewire-related complications have also been reported; Ilizaliturri et al. [18] described arthroscopic retrieval of a broken guidewire fragment after cannulated screw fixation for SCFE. The evidence in this category is descriptive and predominantly level IV or lower. The data are drawn from retrospective series, technical descriptions, and cadaveric or imaging studies rather than comparative clinical trials.

Early in vivo fixation failure

Early in vivo fixation failure is defined as the construct’s inability to maintain fixation before physeal closure or fracture union. It includes slip progression, recurrent displacement, loss of purchase, loosening, and early construct insufficiency.

In stable SCFE, slip progression has been linked to inadequate thread purchase. Carney reported slip progression after in situ single-screw fixation and emphasized the importance of epiphyseal thread engagement [4]. Makarewich [19] similarly evaluated factors affecting slip progression and suggested that adequate screw diameter and thread engagement across the physis are important protective factors. Additionally, Upasani et al. [20] provided biomechanical support for this concept, showing that single-screw SCFE fixation stability depends on thread distribution across the physis, with greatest strength and stiffness when approximately 40%-60% of the threads engage the epiphysis. Beyond thread engagement and screw diameter, the screw insertion starting point may also affect construct durability. In a consecutive series of 49 patients (53 hips) treated with single-screw in situ fixation, Hagiwara [21] observed slip progression in over half of the cohort, with clinically meaningful progression exceeding 5° in approximately one quarter. Their analysis identified a more lateral femoral entry point as a favorable prognostic factor, adding insertion-site selection to the list of modifiable technical variables that influence whether a single-screw construct maintains its hold across the physis.

Biomechanical studies have examined whether unstable or higher-shear slips require different constructs, reaching conflicting conclusions for reasons that appear largely methodological. Karol et al. [22] created slips in paired bovine femurs and loaded them to failure, finding that two-screw fixation yielded only a 33% increase in stiffness and that resistance to further slip was not proportional to screw number. Neither construct approached the stiffness of the intact physis, and on that basis, they recommended single-screw fixation. Kishan et al. [23], testing an unstable model in immature porcine femora under physiologic loading, found two-screw constructs to be 66% stiffer and 66% stronger than a single screw, with the advantage emerging only beyond 2 mm of femoral head displacement. Schmitz et al. [24], using cyclic shear loading in a displaced porcine model, observed only a nonsignificant trend favoring two screws and concluded that within displacement groups, a second screw did not significantly increase stability. The apparent conflict therefore reflects differences in model system, loading protocol, and displacement, rather than a settled mechanical answer. Taken together, the evidence suggests that any benefit of a second screw is modest and inconsistently demonstrated, and all three groups note that added stability must be weighed against the increased risk of inadvertent joint penetration. All of this evidence remains in vitro and has not been validated clinically.

The Miyanji study [25] showed in vitro that fully and partially threaded screws did not differ significantly in stability in their model; this paper is treated as biomechanical evidence, not clinical removal-failure evidence. Lastly, Dragoni et al. [26] further supported the importance of screw-thread configuration in SCFE fixation by comparing 16-mm threaded, 32-mm threaded, and fully threaded screws in a biomechanical model. However, their study evaluates how thread length and screw design influence construct stability rather than reporting clinical removal or long-term failure outcomes. For this category, most of the evidence is from in vitro biomechanical studies and level IV retrospective series. There are very little, if any, prospective comparative data (level I-II) that exist.

Late growth-mediated construct change

Continued skeletal growth after fixation can progressively alter implant position relative to the physis and epiphysis. Sailhan [27] evaluated continued hip growth after fixation of SCFE with a single cannulated screw with proximal threading and reported measurable femoral neck growth after fixation. Falciglia [28] evaluated fixation intended to reduce the risk of femoroacetabular impingement while allowing remodeling. Sleth et al. [29] evaluated persistent capital femoral epiphyseal growth after screw fixation and found that growth may persist despite fixation in selected patients.

The available evidence supports growth-mediated construct change as a clinically relevant phenomenon but does not support a stable estimate of the incidence of failure of growth-preserving constructs. This category is based on a small number of level IV retrospective and observational clinical series.

Mechanical implant failure

Mechanical implant failure refers to the physical failure of the screw itself, including breakage, bending, or fatigue failure. The available clinical evidence in this category is limited and largely based on case reports.

Slone et al. [5] reported mechanical failure of single cannulated screw fixation in unstable SCFE. Jacobson [6] reported cannulated screw failure with recurrent SCFE displacement and described management of the broken construct. Because the available literature lacks a large incidence study of pure in vivo screw breakage, this review does not assign a stable population incidence to category IV.

Biomechanical studies offer limited and inconsistent insights into whether single-screw constructs are more vulnerable in unstable or high-shear settings, and none of these studies examined implant breakage as an outcome [[22], [23], [24]]. Clinical evidence is limited to level V case reports and is supported only indirectly by biomechanical data.

Articular or biologic screw-position complication

Articular or biologic screw-position complications include joint penetration, chondral injury, or changes in the screw-joint relationship after femoral head collapse or remodeling. These events may arise from initial screw placement or from later changes in proximal femoral anatomy. These complications overlap conceptually with late growth-mediated change (category III), and the two are distinguished by mechanism. Category III describes geometric change driven primarily by continued skeletal growth, such as the epiphysis growing off the screw or femoral neck shortening. Category V describes complications arising from the interaction between implant position and the joint, such as articular penetration, chondral injury, or impingement, regardless of whether growth contributed. Category V is further distinguished from category I by timing and mechanism. Category I captures the intraoperative act of malposition at the time of insertion, whereas category V captures the later articular consequences of screw position, which may manifest only after remodeling, collapse, or continued weight-bearing.

Articular complications after in situ fixation arise from the relationship between the implant and the joint, and this relationship can be compromised at the screw tip or the screw head. Postoperative imaging studies demonstrate that screw-tip position is often closer to the articular surface than what intraoperative assessment suggests, with unrecognized subchondral penetration identified on CT in screws that appeared satisfactory on radiographs [10,16,17]. At the opposite end of the construct, the screw head itself can cause mechanical conflict with the acetabulum.

Beyond late articular changes associated with femoral head collapse, the screw construct itself can be a mechanical source of femoroacetabular impingement, independent of the residual slip deformity. In a cadaveric study, Goodwin et al. [30] found that the screw head abutted the acetabulum at 70° of hip flexion in a moderate slip model and at 50° of flexion in a severe slip model, both representing functionally relevant restrictions that could limit activities of daily living and contribute to anterior groin pain even after technically successful fixation. Notably, screw heads positioned lateral to the intertrochanteric line on anteroposterior radiographs were unlikely to impinge, suggesting that entry-point selection can mitigate this implant-specific articular risk [30].

Together, these findings establish that the screw-joint relationship is a distinct source of articular risk after SCFE fixation, at both the screw tip and the screw head. Evidence in this category comes from imaging and cadaveric studies rather than from comparative clinical series. Category V therefore encompasses both growth-related positional change, in which a shifting screw-joint relationship follows femoral head collapse or remodeling, and purely mechanical screw-joint conflict. The screw-head impingement demonstrated by Goodwin et al. [30] represents the latter, occurring independently of any growth-related contribution.

Removal-related failure

Implant removal is a distinct failure window. Removal-related problems include screw stripping, broken or retained implants, difficult extraction, prolonged operative time, and the need for specialized extraction techniques.

Raney et al. reported that implant removal in children carries a meaningful risk of complications and should not be assumed to be benign [31]. Ilchmann and Parsch [7] reported removal complications after cannulated titanium screw fixation for SCFE, including prolonged removal, extensive chiseling, incomplete removal, and postremoval subtrochanteric fractures. Lee [32] similarly identified titanium devices as poorly suited for removal in SCFE due to removal difficulty. Crandall [33] reported complications associated with second operations for pin removal after SCFE fixation.

These studies support classifying difficult extraction as a removal-related failure mode. This classification is supported primarily by level IV clinical series and technical notes, with a biomechanical simulation as a supplement.

Postremoval complication

Successful screw removal does not necessarily end the implant’s life cycle. Postremoval complications include refracture, subtrochanteric fracture, wound issues, infection, persistent pain, and progression of deformity if removal occurs before the physis is reliably closed.

Postremoval subtrochanteric fractures may be directly related to the extraction process. Ilchmann and Parsch [7] reported two subtrochanteric fractures occurring at 5 and 7 weeks after removal of cannulated titanium screws (Asnis III; Stryker, Mahwah, NJ, USA) used for SCFE fixation. In their series of 27 patients (43 screws), the hexagonal Allen sockets stripped under the torque required for reverse-cutting extraction, necessitating extensive chiseling of the lateral cortex in 11 patients and resulting in incomplete removal in seven. The cortical damage sustained during these salvage extraction maneuvers likely weakened the lateral femoral cortex and contributed to the subsequent fractures, illustrating how removal-related difficulty (category VI) can directly predispose to postremoval complications (category VII), particularly when titanium osseointegration and implant design increase the mechanical demands of extraction.

The stress-riser mechanism underlying postremoval subtrochanteric fracture was first highlighted by Karr and Schwab [34], who reported fractures after multiple-pin proximal femoral fixation and emphasized that pin tracts create significant cortical stress concentrations in the lateral subtrochanteric region. Canale et al. [35] subsequently provided the only denominator-based incidence estimate in the SCFE literature, reporting subtrochanteric fractures in three of 204 in situ pinnings performed over a 10-year period (1.4%), with a fourth case referred from another institution. Notably, all four fractures propagated through unused drill holes in the lateral femoral cortex rather than through the occupied screw tract. Together, these studies identify a specific and potentially preventable mechanism: cortical defects created at the time of initial fixation, whether from abandoned drill holes, multiple pin sites, or aggressive extraction, serve as stress risers that may determine both the site and the risk of fracture after implant removal. The available literature supports a postremoval fracture risk after SCFE fixation but does not support a broader incidence claim across all pediatric screw removal. Evidence here is limited to level IV-V case series and reports, with a single retrospective series providing the only incidence estimate.

Proposed life-cycle classification

Based on the aforementioned synthesis, screw-related failures after pediatric proximal femoral fixation can be grouped into seven chronological life-cycle categories. These categories are defined by timing and mechanism rather than by anatomic location alone. The system is intended to support preoperative counseling, implant selection, postoperative surveillance, removal planning, and standardized reporting. Clinical applications

The classification is intended as a clinical tool rather than a purely descriptive framework. By separating the dominant mechanism of failure from the chronological stage at which it occurs, it provides surgeons with a shared vocabulary for five practical tasks: preoperative counseling, implant selection, postoperative surveillance, removal planning, and standardized reporting.

Preoperative counseling

Rather than grouping screw-related adverse events under a generic “complication” label, the seven-stage framework allows surgeons to describe risks to patients and families by timing and mechanism: intraoperative placement events, early loss of fixation, late growth-mediated change, implant fatigue, articular sequelae, removal difficulty, and postremoval events. Stage-specific framing is especially relevant in pediatric SCFE, where the implant must function during continued skeletal growth and many patients will undergo a second procedure for implant removal.

Implant selection

The four-domain framework highlights construct-level trade-offs that are not always apparent when complications are reported in aggregate. Whether a second screw meaningfully increases construct stability remains unsettled across biomechanical models, and it adds a second insertion site with additional articular risk [[22], [23], [24]]. A partially threaded screw configuration offers theoretical insertion advantages but has been associated with greater extraction effort in a bone-simulation model [37]. Titanium implants have been described as poorly suited for later removal in SCFE because of removal difficulty [7,32]. These statements remain evidence-limited and construct-specific rather than universal rules, but the classification encourages surgeons to weigh insertion- and fixation-stage benefits against removal- and postremoval-stage risks when choosing implants.

Postoperative surveillance

Mapping the seven categories to a follow-up timeline supports tailored imaging and clinical review. Early postoperative imaging focuses on placement and fixation maintenance (categories I-II); intermediate follow-up addresses growth-mediated changes in the construct in skeletally immature patients (category III); and longer-term review addresses articular sequelae, including late changes in the screw-joint relationship after femoral head collapse or remodeling (category V). Recognizing that proximal femoral growth may continue after fixation in selected patients [27,29] provides a rationale for periodic radiographic review in younger patients rather than treating fixation as a one-time event.

Removal planning

The classification treats implant removal as a distinct failure window rather than a routine endpoint. Preoperative planning for removal can incorporate construct-specific extraction risk, informed by reports of stripping, broken or retained implants, prolonged operative time, and the need for special extraction techniques [7,32,33,36,37]. Anticipating these issues supports informed consent for revision instrumentation, hollow-mill or overdrilling techniques, and the possibility of incomplete removal. Postremoval counseling can address stress-riser concerns and the risk of subtrochanteric or peri-implant fracture, particularly when removal has required extensive bone work or has left multiple drill holes [7,8]; precise, evidence-based activity restrictions require further study.

Standardized reporting

Inconsistent definitions and denominators are a recurring challenge in this literature. Reporting screw-related events by the life-cycle stage in which they occur, rather than by anatomic location or by lumping all events as “complications,” would enable more direct comparisons across case reports, retrospective series, technical notes, and biomechanical studies. Stage-based reporting also makes evidence gaps explicit, such as the limited clinical incidence data for pure mechanical implant failure (category IV) and for postremoval complications (category VII).

Illustrative application

The framework can be applied retrospectively to published cases to illustrate how it organizes a single patient’s course. In the series reported by Ilchmann and Parsch [7], a patient underwent attempted removal of a cannulated titanium screw. The hexagonal socket stripped under the torque required for extraction, necessitating extensive chiseling of the lateral cortex, a category VI–related failure. Seven weeks later, the patient sustained a subtrochanteric fracture through the weakened cortex, a category VII postremoval complication [7]. Under conventional reporting, both events would likely be recorded together as a single “complication of implant removal.” Stage-based classification instead separates the mechanical difficulty of extraction from its downstream structural consequence, making explicit that the fracture was a predictable sequela of cortical damage rather than an independent event. This distinction is not merely descriptive; it identifies extraction difficulty as the modifiable point in the sequence and supports both preoperative counseling regarding fracture risk and consideration of implant material at the time of index fixation.

Limitations

This is a targeted narrative review intended as a clinical synthesis rather than a systematic review or meta-analysis. No Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses–style search, duplicate screening, risk-of-bias assessment, or pooled incidence estimation was performed. The evidence base is heterogeneous, comprising retrospective series, biomechanical studies, technical notes, and case reports. Several life-cycle categories, particularly pure mechanical implant failure (category IV) and postremoval complications (category VII), are based on limited clinical evidence rather than on stable incidence estimates. The relative clinical weight of categories should not be inferred from the volume of available literature alone.

The proposed life-cycle classification is unvalidated. Formal interobserver reliability testing, which would establish whether independent raters consistently assign the same complication to the same category, has not been performed and is the essential next step before the framework can be recommended for routine use. Some complications also span more than one stage, such as an insertion-related malposition that later results in articular penetration, or a removal-related failure that results in a postremoval fracture. We regard this as a property of the implant life cycle rather than a flaw in the framework since a central aim of stage-based classification is to make such progressions explicit rather than to force each event into a single mutually exclusive category. Whether this feature aids or hinders consistent classification is itself an empirical question for future reliability work. The classification is therefore offered as a starting framework for discussion and future study, not as a finalized clinical tool.

Summary

Screw-related failures after pediatric proximal femoral fixation are heterogeneous and span the entire implant life cycle. A four-domain synthesis mapped to a seven-stage classification organizes the available evidence into a clinically useful framework that supports preoperative counseling, implant selection, postoperative surveillance, removal planning, and standardized reporting. The classification is offered as a starting point and requires further validation.
