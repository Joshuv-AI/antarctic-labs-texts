Plastome comparison and phylogenomic analysis of threatened wild Egyptian Capparis taxa
Authors: Norhan Amar, Heba A. M. AbdAlla, Qing-Feng Wang, Mohamed Abd. S. El Zayat, Hager R. Belal, Sheng-Wei Wang, Tao Wan, Mohamed Hamdy Amar, Jun-Nan Wan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCapparis is one of the largest genera in the Capparaceae family, it is currently classified as comprising threatened wild plants in Egypt. The taxonomy of Capparis remains ambiguous due to complex classifications, overlapping morphological traits, and regional variability. Herein, we present, for the first time, the complete plastome genome sequences of five threatened Capparis taxa from Egypt, all belonging to Capparis spinosa, including one subspecies and several varieties. Together with 13 additional plastomes in the Capparaceae family from public databases, we conducted detailed structural and phylogenetic analyses to explore taxonomic relationships and enhance the molecular understanding of this complex genus.ResultsThe results revealed significant variations in tetrad structure among the Egyptian taxa, particularly at the IR/LSC/SSC junctions, as well as unique gene rearrangements. SSR repeats and codon usage bias may also contribute to shaping plastome diversity. Both mutational forces and natural selection contributed to genome size variation at low taxonomic levels. Phylogenomic analysis strongly supported the taxonomic relationships among Egyptian Capparis taxa and their relatives, shedding light on evolutionary divergence within the genus. Divergence time estimates suggested that the crown node age of Capparaceae originated in the mid-Miocene, approximately 18.62 million years ago (Mya), while the divergence of Egyptian Capparis genotypes occurred around 1.7 Mya during the Pleistocene. Capparis spinosa var. deserti and Capparis spinosa var. herbacea exhibited highly similar plastome organization structures, supporting a close evolutionary relationship between these two wild varieties.ConclusionsThis comparative plastome genome approach served as an effective tool for studying plant lineage evolution, taxonomy, and developing genetic markers for accurate identification of Capparis taxa and the evolution of the Capparaceae family.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12338-7.

Introduction

The genus Capparis is one of the largest genera in the family Capparaceae, comprising of approximately 142 recognized species of small trees, shrubs and climbers. These species are dispersed throughout tropical and subtropical regions in the Old World, including Mediterranean region, Africa and central Asia [1, 2]. Plants within this genus exhibit remarkable ecological adaptability, thriving in diverse habitats ranging from arid deserts and high-altitude mountainous zones to densely vegetated forests. Some certain species within the genus have been traditionally employed for medicinal purposes due to the pharmacological efficacy based on its abundance of various bioactive constituents such as natural antioxidants (flavonoids, rutin), natural sugars, alkaloids, terpenoids, vitamins and minerals, and antimicrobial agents [3]. Accurately identifying Capparis taxa is necessary for the long-term use and conservation of biological resources.

In Egypt, Capparis taxa are among the most important traditional medicinal plants, which offer a wide array of health-promoting qualities. These taxa are distributed across three geographic zones, included the Sinai Peninsula, the North-western coast of Egypt, and Southern Upper Egypt (specifically the Halayeb and Shalateen triangle) [4]. Some of that taxa are under immediate threat and currently classified as threatened wild plants in Egypt, particularly considering their extreme habitat and the impacts of global climate changes [5]. While not formally assessed on the IUCN Red List, several Capparis taxa are considered regionally threatened within Egypt’s arid and semi-arid zones. Moreover, Capparis spinosa, although widespread across the Mediterranean and of economic importance, is subject to significant pressures globally including overharvesting, grazing, habitat disturbance from tourism and development, and increasing drought frequency [6]. Several Capparis taxa, including Capparis spinosa subsp. cartilaginea and four related varieties, have been identified as rare or locally declining in Egypt due to habitat fragmentation, overgrazing, and increasing aridity [5, 7]. These threats are particularly pronounced in ecologically sensitive areas such as South Sinai and the Gebel Elba region, where these taxa are naturally distributed [8]. These combined threats underscore the urgent need for regional conservation strategies and molecular studies to clarify taxonomic boundaries and support the sustainable conservation and utilization of Capparis germplasm in Egypt.

However, the genus’s extensive distribution pattern and significant morphological variation have long led to contentious subgeneric taxonomic treatments. Several taxonomic treatments of the genus Capparis have been proposed, ranging from early works by de Candolle [9] and Boissier [10], to regional treatments by Zohary [11] and Jacobs [1], and more recent Mediterranean-wide revisions by Fici [12, 13] and Melzi et al. [14] (Table 1). Zohary [11] emphasizes Mediterranean and West Asian taxa but omits certain varieties recognized locally in Egypt. Similarly, Jacobs [1] sectional reorganization is global in scope and does not reflect the infraspecific diversity recorded in Egypt. More recent floristic revisions [13, 15] recognize two subspecies in the Mediterranean-Central Asia and four in Africa, Asia, and Oceania. These studies reveal high morphological and genetic variability in C. spinosa and show that one of varieties of C. spinosa is now reduced to subspecies for example, C. spinosa subsp. spinosa var. aegyptia is widely treated as C. spinosa subsp. aegyptia [14, 16–19]. Within Egypt, both Täckholm [20] and Boulos [21] provided detailed taxonomic treatments, with Boulos offering the most updated and widely used reference. We acknowledge that these large-scale revisions provide updated nomenclature and alternative classifications. Nevertheless, because our study focuses on threatened wild Egyptian Capparis taxa, we follow the treatment of Boulos [21] and the recent taxonomic revisions [14, 15], which consolidates Egyptian records into three species (Capparis decidua, Capparis cartilaginea, and C. spinosa) with four varieties of C. spinosa (var. spinosa, var. inermis, var. canescens, and var. deserti) and we treat C. cartilaginea as C. spinosa subsp. cartilaginea (Decne.) Maire & Weiller. This approach ensures consistency with Egyptian floristic and conservation literature while recognizing that broader Mediterranean and paleotropical taxonomic frameworks may apply different concepts. Moreover, Boulos’ treatment also serves as the reference standard in Egyptian conservation legislation, and limited molecular data for local populations reinforce the need to maintain this framework. It is noteworthy that Boulos [21] treated C. spinosa var. aegyptia as a synonym of C. spinosa var. spinosa, whereas more recent databases [2] and recent molecular population-level studies [22, 23] represented it as distinct. The considerable morphological variability of C. spinosa, shaped by geographical differentiation, environmental plasticity, and wide distribution, has been well documented [12, 13, 15, 24].

The ambiguous taxonomic classification of Capparis taxa was due to significant challenges of limited data, conflicting classifications, and frequent reclassifications and placement [5]. Traditional approaches, which primarily focus on leaf anatomy, pollen morphology, and seed morphology, have proven insufficient to resolve taxonomic discrepancies [25]. Adopting molecular systematic methods, such as DNA sequencing, can help achieve a more robust classification. This multidisciplinary approach combining traditional taxonomic methods with molecular genomic tools will clarify the taxonomy and reconstruct the phylogenetic relationship of Capparis taxa in Egypt, to aid in their conservation and management efforts.

During the last two decades, researchers have adopted several molecular approaches using different DNA markers or barcoding, to resolve the taxonomy problem within Capparis genus, such as RAPD [26, 27] AFLP [28] and ISSRs [29]. However, most of the markers have some limitations in accurately resolving taxonomic relationships within Capparis genus due to their low resolution [30, 31]. Plastome genome assembly, and comparative genomics barcoding can be effective in resolving taxonomic relationships in many lineages, it may not have enough genetic variation to accurately distinguish closely related taxa such as those in the genus Capparis.

In contrast, plastome genome analysis offers higher resolution and greater accuracy in resolving phylogenetic relationships and taxonomic distinctions, particularly at lower taxonomic levels [32]. Thanks to the rise of high-throughput sequencing and advances in bioinformatics, resequencing, plastome genome assembly, and comparative genomics are now powerful methods [33]. The plastome genome’s conservation and unique qualities make it an important asset for enhancing species identification accuracy and plant evolution [34]. The scrutiny of plastome genome sequences provides a valuable means of detecting sequence variations and identifying regions with a heightened incidence of mutation hotspots regions [35], which will contribute to taxonomic and systematic research of Capparis taxa.

Hence, to address these uncertainties and provide a clearer understanding of Capparis taxa in Egypt, there is an urgent need for comprehensive research utilizing molecular genomic tools. Considering the documented deficiency of the initial plastome genome in Egyptian Capparis taxa, we report for the first time a new assembly of the completed plastome of five threatened wild Egyptian Capparis taxa, including Capparis spinosa subsp. cartilaginea, C. spinosa var. aegyptia, C. spinosa var. canescens, C. spinosa var. deserti, and C. spinosa var. inermis. Using these newly five assembled plastomes alongside publicly available NCBI data of 13 species and variates of Capparaceae family, we performed comprehensive comparative analyses to investigate [1], the evolutionary patterns of the IR boundaries revealing structural variations and notable divergence hotspots regions [2], repetitive sequences, codon usage patterns and parity rules [3], the phylogenomic framework and divergence time estimates, and evolutionary relationships to address taxonomic questions and taxa boundaries, particularly C. spinosa and its varieties.

Although differences among Capparaceae genera are known, few plastome level studies have systematically examined the structural and phylogenomic distinctions across this group. This study provides valuable molecular insights and baseline plastome data that contribute to resolving taxonomic ambiguities of the Capparis taxa in Egypt, resulting background information for the local conservation on the Capparis taxa in Egypt. Although this study can’t resolve the global taxonomy of Capparis, we attempt to offer a foundation for future integrative taxonomic work that combines broader molecular, ecological, and distributional data. By situating our treatment within this regional context while referencing broader Mediterranean and international revisions, we aim to complement rather than replace large-scale taxonomic perspectives.

Materials and methods

Plant material and genome sequencing

The plant material used in this study was collected and identified based on morphological characteristics by Mahmoud El Sayd Ali and Mohamed Abd. S. El Zayat, experts from the Regional Plants Gene Bank in Sinai, affiliated with the Desert Research Center in Cairo, Egypt. Plant identification was performed using morphological keys from Boulos [21] and Tackholm [20], in conjunction with more recent revisions of the Capparis group (supplementary Table S19) [5, 13, 15]. The material was sourced from specific geographical locations in the Marsa Matrouh and South Sinai governorates of Egypt, as presented in Table 2; Fig. 1. All taxonomic identifications and nomenclature followed the classification system proposed by Boulos [21] and the recent taxonomic revisions [14, 15]. No special permissions were required for the collection, as it was conducted by the Regional Plants Gene Bank in Sinai, Desert Research Center in Cairo, Egypt. Fresh young leaves of C. spinosa var. inermis and C. spinosa var. deserti were collected from Matrouh & Siwa rood of North-western coast and the rocky ridges of coastal western desert, respectively, Marsa Matrouh governorate. C. spinosa var. aegyptia, C. spinosa subsp. cartilaginea, and C. spinosa var. canescens were collected from the Saint Katherine Protectorate (SKP) in South Sinai governorate. Voucher specimens of the five Egyptian Capparis taxa analyzed in this study were deposited in the herbarium of the Regional Plants Gene Bank, Sinai (RGBS), a local institutional herbarium affiliated with the CAIH herbarium of Desert Research Center (DRC), Cairo, Egypt. While RGBS it is not internationally registered yet, it serves as a recognized local depository for regional flora, particularly those collected from arid and semi-arid zones of Egypt. Plant Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (DP305-03, Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China) was used to extract genomic DNA from 100 mg of fresh leaves. Three to five DNAs replicates were utilized for each sample. The concentration and quality of the DNA samples were verified using a spectrophotometer (Nanodrop 2000, Thermo Fischer, USA). Both the stock and diluted portions were stored at − 80 °C until use to ensure their stability and integrity of experiments.

Whole genome sequencing was performed by Annoroad Gene Technology Co., Ltd, (Beijing, China) using the BGI sequencing platform to sequence the total DNA of the five Capparis samples, facilitating the comprehensive analysis of genetic variations and providing new insights into the evolutionary relationships among the studied taxa. After sequencing, the raw data underwent a quality control process to ensure its reliability and accuracy. We utilized FastQC v 0.12 [36] for trimming the adapters and filtering out low-quality reads before further analysis. Subsequently, the cleaned data was ready for further bioinformatics processing and interpretation.

Plastome genome assembly and annotation

The clean plastome genomes were assembled using Get Organelle ver. 1.7.4 [37], to extract the plastome genome sequence from the raw sequencing data. The annotation was conducted using CPGAVAS2 online tool (http://47.96.249.172:16019/analyzer/home) [38] and PGA software [39], the process involved comparing the assembled plastome genomes to the reference genome of C. decidua (GenBank accession NC_053386) as a reference, facilitating the identification of genes, coding regions, and other genomic features within the plastome. We employed Geneious v. 8.0.2 [40] and GeSeq online tool (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/geseq.html) [41] for examining and adjusting the genomes. Additionally, CPGview (http://www.1kmpg.cn/cpgview/) [42] was used to visualize the results. After rigorous curation, the plastome genome sequence and annotation file were submitted to NCBI.

Comparative analysis of plastome genome sequence

The plastome genome sequences of 18 plastome samples underwent thorough examination. This comprehensive analysis involved a detailed comparison of the Inverted Repeat (IR), Large Single-Copy region (LSC), and Small Single-Copy region (SSC), including the boundary details. The study provided insights into the structural variations and evolutionary relationships among the taxa. This comparison was conducted using the IRscope, online tool, (irscope.shinyapps.io/irapp/) [43], for mapping the IR boundary contrast figure. We compared the plastome genomes of the five Egyptian Capparis taxa (C. spinosa var. deserti, C. spinosa var. inermis, C. spinosa subsp. cartilaginea, C. spinosa var. canescens and C. spinosa var. aegyptia) with other thirteen taxa using mVISITA online tool [44], the annotation of C. decidua was used as a reference in the Shuffle-LAGAN mode [45].

Plastome genome repeats

To understand the genetic structure and evolutionary patterns of taxa, we identified four types of repetitive sequences (palindromic, forward, complement, and reverse) in 18 Capparaceae taxa using REPuter (https://bibiserv.cebitec.uni-bielefeld.de/reputer/) (Retrieved on 26 June 2024) [46, 47] with the following parameters: minimum repeat size = 30 bp and Hamming distance limit = 3 [48]. For Simple Sequence Repeat (SSR) detection, we employed MISA.pl (v1.0) [49], to ensure precise identification of it. We set the minimum counts for mono-nucleotide, di-nucleotide, tri-nucleotide, tetra-nucleotide, penta-nucleotide, and hexa-nucleotide repeats at 10, 5, 4, 3, 3, and 3, respectively [50].

Relative synonymous codon usage and parity rule (PR2) analysis

Codon deviation suggests that each amino acid can be denoted by a range of one to six codons [51]. Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) delineates the variances in the frequency of usage among synonymous codons. We utilized Phylosuite version 1.2.2 [52] to generate the codon bias ratio. To investigate the effects of mutation and natural selection on the codon usage of genes, we employed Parity rule 2 plot analysis (A3/(A3 + T3) values against (G3/(G3 + C3) values in the ordinate. We conducted PR2 analysis to evaluate nucleotide usage bias in the coding genes for the complete plastome of our five Capparis taxa with the other thirteen Capparaceae taxa downloaded from NCBI database [53]. Nucleotide composition (A, T, C, G) at synonymous third-codon positions was calculated using the MEGA v11.0.13 software [54].

Phylogenomic analysis

The phylogenetic analysis was conducted based on the complete plastome genome sequences of eighteen Capparaceae taxa, with two outgroup species of Malvaceae (Gossypium stocksii and Hibiscus cannabinus). All sequences were aligned using MAFFT program [55] with default settings. A maximum parsimony analysis of the phylogenetic tree was performed using Gblocks [56] combined with PhyloSuite software [52], using tree bisection and reconnection branch swapping with IQtrees enabled, saving a maximum of 1000 trees per replication. Missing characters were treated as gaps. The bootstrap analysis confidence based on 1000 replicates. MrBayes version 3.2.6 [57] was used to conduct Bayesian analysis, and maximum likelihood phylogenies were inferred using IQ-TREE [58] under the TVM + R2 + F model for 5000 ultrafast bootstraps [59] and the Shimodaira–Hasegawa-like approximate likelihood-ratio test [60] for selecting the appropriate model.

Divergence time Estimation

The divergence time of Capparaceae taxa was estimated by the BEAST v1.8.0 software [61]. We imported comparison results from MAFFT to BEAUTi v2, and selected GTR + Gamma as nucleotide substitution model [62]. Empirical mode was set as basic frequency, and the strict molecular clock model was selected. The ‘Yule Model’ was used as the system tree model, and we set the number of interactions and sampling in the Makov chain Monte Carlo algorithm to 3,000,000 and 1000, respectively. Finally, the result was imported into Tracer v 1.7.1 software (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/tracer/). The effective sample size was greater than 200, suggesting that the results were robust. Tree Annotator program (https://beast.community/index.html) was used to preform optimal tree merging [63]. Burn-in level was set to 10%, the optimal tree was saved as the result, and FigTree v1.4.4 software [64] was used to view phylogenetic trees with different time estimates.

Results

Characteristics of the five Capparis plastome genomes

In this study, we present the assembled sequence of the complete plastome genomes of five Capparis taxa including C. spinosa subsp. cartilaginea (PP706108), C. spinosa var. aegyptia (PP706109), C. spinosa var. canescens (PP706110), C. spinosa var. deserti (PP706111), and C. spinosa var. inermis (PP706112).

The complete plastome genome of the five Capparis taxa range from 157,574 to 159,115 bp in length, and protein coding genes ranged from 77,074 to 78,972 bp, comparable to 48.9% to 49.6% of the total genome length. They exhibit a standard quadripartite structure, featuring a pair of inverted repeat regions (IR) spanning 26,395 to 31,477 bp. These repeats are separated by a large single copy region (LSC) ranging from 86,041 to 86,643 bp, and small single copy region (SSC) being from 9,636 to 18,747 bp (Table 3; Fig. 2).

Comparative analysis of IR contraction

The plastome genome sequences of 13 available Capparaceae taxa downloaded from NCBI were included in our analysis in addition to the five newly sequenced Egyptian Capparis taxa, in order to investigate changes in the inverted repeats (IR) sequence. To explore these variances, we conducted an analysis of the LSC, SSC, and IR boundary structures within the 18 Capparaceae taxa. Variations in the expansion or contraction of the IR exhibit distinct characteristics across different plant species. Among all, the C. versicolor and Maerua crassifolia recorded the lowest length of IR regions being 26,141 bp and 26,294 bp, respectively. While, C. spinosa var. deserti showed the highest length of IR regions harboring 31,477 bp, as same as that of C. s var. herbacea. Whilst the rest of the four Egyptian Capparis taxa C. spinosa subsp. cartilaginea, C. spinosa var. inermis, C. spinosa var. aegyptia and C. spinosa var. canescens had similar lengths of IR with a slight difference, being of 26,381 bp, 26,394 bp, 26,395 bp and 26,395 bp, respectively (Table 2).

In details, the junction between LSC/IRb referred as JLB was identified to lie in the gene region of rps19, except for C. versicolor. The length of rps19 ranges from 168 to 213 bp in the LSC region and from 66 to 108 bp in the IRb region. Likewise, rps19 gene was absent in the junction of IRa/LSC denoted as JLAs of 11 taxa, including all Egyptian Capparis taxa. Only in both C. spinosa var. herbacea and C. spinosa var. deserti, our analysis revealed that the two genes ndhE and psaC are exclusively located within the junctions of the IRa/SSC and IRb/SSC regions, respectively (Fig. 3).

Simple sequence repeats (SSRs)

The present study investigated the appearance and types of SSRs among 18 plastome genomes of Capparaceae taxa (Fig. 5B and C). Results revealing a diverse array of SSR repeat types spanning mono to hexa-nucleotide motifs. On average, the highest and the lowest total SSRs number were recorded in both C. spinosa var. herbacea & C. spinosa var. deserti and Maerua oblongifolia & Maerua crassifolia being 370, 360 and 160, 140, respectively. In details, the total SSRs number discovered in the five Egyptian Capparis taxa are as follows: 370 (C. spinosa var. deserti), 299 (C. spinosa subsp. cartilaginea), 296 (C. spinosa var. canescens), and 287 (C. spinosa var. aegyptia) and 270 (C. spinosa var. inermis) (Fig. 5B).

The occurrence of A/T repetitions was more frequent, while only one instance of C/G repeats was found the five newly sequenced Capparis taxa. (Fig. 5C). The dinucleotide repeat AA/TT was the most common among the 18 taxa, while AC/GT was found exclusively in Cadaba glandulosa. Furthermore, we observed five trinucleotide motifs (AAA/TTT, AAG/CTT, AAT/ATT, ACT/AGT, CCC/GGG), ten tetra-nucleotide (AAAA/TTTT, AAAC/GTTT, AAAG/CTTT, AAAT/ATTT, AACG/CGTT, AACT/AGTT, AATT/AATT, AGAT/ATCT, ATAT/ATAT, CCCC/GGGG) and twelve penta-nucleotides (Fig. 5C). Nevertheless, no hexa-nucleotide repeats found in C. decidua, C. versicolor, M. crassifolia, M. oblongifolia, and C. religiosa, and C. unilocularis.

Plastome phylogenomic analysis

A phylogenetic maximum likelihood (ML) tree was constructed based on the 63-protein coding sequence from complete plastome sequences of 18 Capparaceae taxa, with two outgroup species from the Malvaceae family (Fig. 8, supplementary Table S13). In general, three confirmed main clades were identified in Capparaceae with a notable bootstrap value of 100. Overall, Clade I includes all accessions from the genus Capparis comprising three species, with C. spinosa represented by one subspecies and seven varieties. while Clade II comprises all species from the genera Cadaba and Maerua, whereas, Clade III encompasses species belonging to the Crateva genus. In detail, three confirmed sub-clades were identified within the Clade I, which can help to clearly distinguish among the five Egyptian Capparis taxa.

Fig. 8Phylogenetic topology for whole plastome genomes of 18 Capparaceae taxa and two outgroups based on whole plastome genome, used maximum likelihood analysis with bootstrap estimated branches

Phylogenetic topology for whole plastome genomes of 18 Capparaceae taxa and two outgroups based on whole plastome genome, used maximum likelihood analysis with bootstrap estimated branches

Within Clade I, that is the genus Capparis, all plastome sequences from C. spinosa and its varieties along with C. spinosa subsp. cartilaginea, form a monophyletic clade, sharing a sister relationship with C. decidua and C. versicolor in the broader caper clade. The five Egyptian Capparis taxa belong to this monophyletic group, which comprises several well-supported subclades. One sub-clade comprised C. spinosa var. aegyptia, var. canescens, var. inermis, and var. ovata. A second sub-clade included C. spinosa var. deserti and var. herbacea, which were closely related and shared the largest plastome sizes among the analyzed taxa. Meanwhile, the nominal C. spinosa and C. spinosa var. spinosa clustered with in a third sub-clade. Both our plastome evidence and recent taxonomic revisions support C. spinosa subsp. cartilaginea placement at the subspecies rank. This phylogenetic topology suggests possible cryptic divergence within C. spinosa s.l. (sensu lato) and raises questions about the taxonomic boundaries among these taxa.

Divergence time estimation

Evolutionary divergence dating time was estimated using the integration of 63 coding sequences (CDS) extracted from the plastome genome (Fig. 9, supplementary Table S13). The divergence time of the Capparaceae family (node 1) was approximated ~ 18.62 million years ago (Ma), during the Miocene epoch. The divergence time of Cadaba, Maerua, and Capparis genus (node 2) was estimated to be around ~ 3.72 Ma, corresponding to the Pliocene (PI) epoch. Results, indicated that the Capparis genus, with exception of C. versicolor, diverged approximately ~ 1.70 Ma, closely associated with the Pleistocene (P) epoch (node 3). The divergence of C. spinosa subsp. cartilaginea and the C. spinosa varieties occurred during the Pleistocene epoch, indicating relatively recent diversification. Suggesting that the Pleistocene epoch is probably a major driving factor in its genetic differentiation (Fig. 9).

Discussion

Plastome structures reveal taxonomic relationships

Capparis genus is a xerophytic shrub belonging to the Capparaceae family, with a widespread distribution across the Mediterranean, and discoveries from an Old-World Palaeolithic site in Egypt suggested consumption of this genus dates back to 17,000 years ago [66, 67]. This genus is well adapted in arid or semi-arid regions (such as Eastern Mediterranean countries, the Arabic peninsula, and the Middle East) [15]. The genus Capparis exhibits great morphological variation driven by phenotypic plasticity, eco-geographical variances, topographical shifts, and hybridization processes [68, 69]. Such variation reflects the complex organization of wild caper forms [7]. Due to the complexity of variation, the taxonomic relationships within the genus Capparis are still unresolved and are under discussion.

Recent surveys highlight that the Capparis genus in Egypt is under threat as never before, and perhaps on the verge of extinction, mainly due to the desertification induced by global climate change [70]. Urgent conservation and restoration strategies, informed by clear taxonomic resolution and evolutionary insights, are needed to safeguard these taxa. Reliance on vegetative morphology in Egypt has caused taxonomic confusion, misidentifications, and broad classification errors [4, 71]. Only a few studies providing molecular insights into Capparis taxonomy and systematics [4, 26, 28, 29, 72] have been conducted in the past two decades, and none have focused on Egyptian caper plastomes. To fill this gap, we evaluated the initial full plastome configuration of the five wild Capparis taxa from Egypt. The plastome genome contains a wealth of genetic information, both coding and noncoding regions, offering comprehensive view of evolutionary relationships and systematic revision among taxa at low taxonomic levels [73–76].

Among the 18 Capparaceae taxa, the plastomes of C. spinosa var. herbacea and C. spinosa var. deserti had the largest in size (159,216 bp and 159,115 bp, respectively), while C. versicolor exhibited the smallest plastome size (155,051 bp). Notably, both C. spinosa var. herbacea and C. spinosa var. deserti also had the longest inverted repeat (IR) regions. The dynamic processes of expansion and contraction in IR regions significantly contribute to plastome size diversification and have been pivotal in land plant evolution [77, 78]. In addition, both taxa (C. spinosa var. herbacea and C. spinosa var. deserti) also shared a unique combination of genes (e.g., ndhD, psaC, ndhE, rps15, and ndhH) at the IR boundary regions, which were absent in the remaining 16 Capparaceae taxa examined. Furthermore, the repeated sequence patterns and coding gene content were highly similar between C. spinosa var. herbacea and C. spinosa var. deserti, further supporting a close phylogenetic relationship. These genomic similarities may reflect a shared evolutionary origin and provide molecular evidence for taxonomic reevaluation of their classification [79, 80].

Exploring repeats and codon usage bias

Long dispersed repeats are essential for the rearrangement and sequence divergence of the plastome, including forward, palindromic, reverse, and complement, and play a key role in genomic studies. In the five Egyptian Capparis taxa, repeat sizes exhibited a specific pattern of size divergence. Specifically, C. spinosa var. aegyptia, C. spinosa var. canescens, and C. spinosa var. inermis predominantly exhibited repeats with a similar pattern. However, the patterns observed in the other two taxa differed from it, which aligns with findings previously reported in flowering plants and is supported by our phylogenetic results [81]. This pattern may be attributed to evolutionary significance, potentially linked to the ecological or genetic distinctiveness of each taxon.

In contrast, simple sequence repeats (SSRs) are abundant in plastomes and serve as highly variable molecular markers at low taxonomic levels, making them valuable for systematic, evolutionary, and population structure analyses [78, 81]. Herein, we identified 270–370 polymorphic SSR loci across the five Egyptian plastomes, composed of mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotide repeats, with AA/TT dinucleotides predominating among the 18 taxa, which is consistent with the previous reported in land plants [82, 83]. Moreover, C/G mononucleotide repeats occurred only in these five Egyptian taxa, similar to recent reports in Annonaceae species [82] and angiosperms plastomes [84]. Melzi, La Bella [14] reported that SSR distribution in C. spinosa varies by location, type, and number, highlighting SSRs as efficient tools for evolutionary and phylogenetic studies in Capparaceae. These genic SSRs and long repeat sequences define diverged hotspot regions with variable mutation rates and offer candidate markers for DNA barcoding, phylogenetics, and evolutionary studies in Capparaceae [51, 81].

The coding sequences of plastomes are evolutionarily conserved with minimal change [74]. An analysis of codon usage in the plastome coding sequences (CDS) of all Egyptian Capparis taxa revealed UUA (Leu2), AGA (Arg), and GCU (Ala) as the most frequent codons, except in C. spinosa var. deserti where AAC (Asn), UUA (Leu2), and GCU (Ala) predominated. The variation in codon usage is driven by GC content, a key factor shaping genomic organization through evolution [75]. The plastome exhibits high AT content, especially at the third codon position, underscoring nucleotide composition’s role in genomic structure [76]. Using the PR2 bias plot, the study of AT and GC bias across 35 coding sequences provided further insight into the selection and mutational forces influencing the evolution of the plastome in the Capparaceae family. In the PR2 bias plot, if the values of G3/(G3 + C3) and A3/(A3 + T3) are equal to 1, codon bias is solely determined by base mutation pressure. Conversely, if both values are 0, natural selection is the exclusive influencing factor [77]. Accordingly, our results suggest that in the Capparaceae family, both base mutation pressure and natural selection are at play with a possible bias towards G and T nucleotides at the third codon position, as indicated by A3/(A3 + T3) values less than 0.5 and G3/(G3 + C3) values greater than 0.5. Collectively, codon usage bias is a crucial evolutionary feature across diverse genomes, shaped by multiple biological factors including gene expression, gene length, tRNA abundance, mutation bias, and GC composition [78].

Plastome phylogenomic analysis

Examination of the complete plastome genome holds significant promise in addressing diverse issues within a certain lineage [85, 86]. To our knowledge, no previous studies have reported plastome sequences for the five wilds Egyptian Capparis taxa examined here. We evaluated plastome sequences for taxonomic classification and phylogenetic analysis of Egyptian Capparis and related members of Capparaceae. Our phylogenetic analysis offers robust evidence for the monophyly of each of the four genera in the Capparaceae family, especially for the Capparis genus, whose complex evolutionary history has long complicated its taxonomy. Earlier taxonomic studies suggested that C. spinosa var. aegyptia is synonyms to C. spinosa var. deserti [2]. However, our phylogeny places C. spinosa var. aegyptia and C. spinosa var. deserti in different sub-clades. Herein, our findings reveal greater molecular than morphological divergence between these taxa. This pattern aligns with recent systematic revision in Egypt, which indicated that they are independent varieties with close relationships [4, 14, 17]. Furthermore, in our plastome-based phylogenetic tree, C. spinosa var. spinosa is not recovered as closely related to C. spinosa var. aegyptia, supporting their recognition as separate entities. This result contrasts with Boulos [21] synonymizing of C. spinosa var. aegyptia under C. spinosa var. spinosa but is consistent with earlier molecular population-level studies [22], which also differentiated between the two varieties.

C. spinosa var. deserti is clearly distinguished from other Capparis taxa in Egypt, and may warrant classification as an independent variety. This concept has gained support from genomic studies [84, 87]. Unexpectedly, our phylogenomic analysis revealed high similarity between C. spinosa var. deserti and C. spinosa var. herbacea, despite C. spinosa var. herbacea is distributed in Turkey, Central Asia, and western China [79], while C. spinosa var. deserti is a wild form distributed in arid North-western coast of Egypt, differing slightly in flower and pollen coloration (Fig. 1; Table 2). This difference may reflect local adaptation in the Mediterranean area, which can be mediated by climate factors influencing pigmentation [7].

The C. spinosa group represents a complex of taxa with broad distribution across North Africa, the Middle East, and southern Europe [80]. The Mediterranean region is widely regarded as both the historical and recent center of Capparis speciation, functioning as the primary center of origin for C. spinosa. It also remains the predominant focus on the thousand-years history of caper cultivation, with most agricultural practices centered exclusively on C. spinosa [28]. Historical records indicate that caper cultivation was extensively documented by Roman authors between the 1 st and 4th centuries A.D. In modern times, Turkish caper production predominantly relies on wild populations of Capparis sicula subsp. sicula and C. spinosa var. herbacea [88]. Despite long-standing human utilization, caper domestication remains incomplete, with most commercial production still based on wild genetic resources. Our plastome phylogenomic results revealed a close genetic similarity between C. spinosa var. deserti and C. spinosa var. herbacea, including shared plastome size, inverted repeat (IR) expansions, and conserved gene content. These findings suggest a close evolutionary relationship, which may reflect shared ancestry or convergent adaptation to arid environments. The draft genome of C. spinosa var. herbacea has been published, not only firstly offering a valuable insight into genome diversity, genetic structure, speciation, and the evolution of the Capparaceae family [72], but also making it more practicable for further research based on plastome genomes. Our findings highlighted the close relationship between C. spinosa var. herbacea and the Egypt native taxon C. spinosa var. deserti, expanding the application of the published genomic data in Capparis plant resource conservation and utilization.

C. spinosa subsp. cartilaginea clusters with other taxa within C. spinosa, rather than forming a distinct phylogenetic lineage. This phylogenetic result supports recent taxonomic revisions that treat it as a subspecies rather than an independent species [14, 15]. Evidence suggests that hybridization, coupled with ecological adaptations, may have facilitated the diversification of Mediterranean Capparis taxa, with genetic data supporting the derivation of multiple varieties from these two ancestral lineages [24]. Similar findings emerged from recent molecular reclassification of Capparis [14, 15, 80], which confirmed that C. spinosa subsp. cartilaginea and C. spinosa from India and Saudi Arabia, form a sister clade with strong support (94/0.95).

Recent population genetic analyses by Melzi, La Bella [14], based on EST-SSR markers, reported significant genetic divergence between C. spinosa var. aegyptia and C. spinosa var. canescens, and suggested that C. spinosa var. aegyptia could be elevated to subspecies rank due to its genetic distinctiveness. Their findings emphasized high polymorphism within the C. spinosa group and clear separation among Mediterranean varieties. In contrast, our plastome-based phylogenomic analysis recovered C. spinosa var. aegyptia and var. canescens as sister clades with strong bootstrap support (BS >90%), indicating, suggesting more recent maternal lineage divergence. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the molecular markers used: nuclear EST-SSRs, which are biparentally inherited, highly variable, and reflect recombination and recent gene flow. Plastome genomes, which are maternally inherited, more conserved, and primarily reflect deeper maternal lineage history, are more sensitive than nuclear EST-SSRs to various evolutionary processes, making them useful for studying plant evolution [89–91]. Thus, while our plastome results suggest close evolutionary relationships at the maternal level, they do not contradict Melzi et al.‘s nuclear findings but rather complement them by highlighting different evolutionary layers.

Importantly, our plastome phylogenomic analysis also aligns with broader findings, indicating a monophyletic lineage formed by C. spinosa subsp. cartilaginea and C. spinosa within the genus, which supports the view that various Mediterranean taxa including the Egyptian varieties likely stem from these ancestral forms. Combined plastome and nuclear data reveal complex lineage differentiation within the C. spinosa complex, emphasizing the need for integrative genomic approaches to resolve its taxonomic framework. However, hybridization, gene flow between taxa, and environmental pressures may have contributed to the genetic diversity observed in Mediterranean caper plants.

Divergence time estimation

The divergence time tree will lead to a broader understanding of the interplay of the change in the biosphere with the diversity of species on Earth [92]. To date, there are limited published studies that attempt to put the diversification of Capparis within a temporal context, based on a divergence time analysis of Capparaceae using plastome genome data. A study indicated that Capparaceae has a strong African ancestry with two subsequent dispersal events occurring between Oligocene and Miocene epochs, leading to the spread of the family to Australasia and to the New World [93]. Another study reported that wood fossil of Capparis was described from Germany and dated to 17–16.3 Mya [94]. The results from these two studies might support an over-land migration during the evolutionary history of Capparis taxa. According to the divergence time estimation conducted in this study, the crown node [1] of Capparaceae estimated here being approximately ~ 18.62 Mya, was close to a recent study reported by Wang et al., [72] and Cardinal-McTeague et al., [93]. However, other recent efforts by Mercado-Gómez [95] and AbdAlla et al., [96], proposed slightly younger estimates for the crown diversification age, ranging between ~ 16–11 Mya and ~ 13.45 Mya, respectively. Our findings further reveal that the primary divergence event within the Egyptian Capparis occurred during the Pleistocene (P) epoch (approximately ~ 1.70 Mya). This relatively recent diversification suggests that the climatic and environmental fluctuations of the Pleistocene played a significant role in driving the genetic differentiation and evolutionary history of these taxa. Evidences suggested that the age differences estimated for Capparaceae are affected by molecular data selection, taxon sampling, calibration point setting, and different methods of selection [93, 97–99].

Conclusion

Capparis is a threatened genus in Egypt, with complex biology and taxonomy. In this study, we present the first comprehensive plastome analysis of five wilds Egyptian Capparis taxa, together with 13 published plastomes, to investigate genomic variation and resolve infrageneric relationships. We document structural variation at the IR/LSC/SSC boundaries, gene rearrangements, and identify several highly variable regions suitable for DNA barcoding and evolutionary inference. PR2 bias analysis indicates that both mutational pressure and natural selection have driven plastome evolution in Capparis. The results supported the recognition of C. spinosa var. deserti as a distinct from other Capparis taxa in Egypt, and highlighted the close affinity between C. spinosa subsp. cartilaginea and C. spinosa. The divergence time of the five Egyptian taxa was estimated at ~ 1.7 Mya in Pleistocene. This regional study will contribute to the future taxonomic researches on the charming genus Capparis, by providing both the new plastome data and the phylogenomic results.

Limitation

Our study was limited by the sample size and restricted geographic scope within Egypt, a consequence of the threatened status of the wild Egyptian population. While this focus prevents broad generalization to the entire Mediterranean or global distribution, it enabled a detailed plastome-level and phylogenomic assessment of Egyptian Capparis taxa. Future studies incorporating a wider range of Capparis variants from across the Mediterranean and beyond would provide a broader, more reliable understanding of the genus and help resolve its long-standing taxonomic issues.
