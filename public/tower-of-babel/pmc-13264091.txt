Septic cardiomyopathy: Fact or fiction?
Authors: Alyssa Naimi, Mario Rodriguez, Yusuf Kamran Qadeer, Hafeez Ul Hassan Virk, Muzamil Khawaja, WH Wilson Tang, Markus Strauss, Chayakrit Krittanawong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeptic cardiomyopathy (SCM) represents a dynamic form of myocardial dysfunction occurring in the setting of sepsis. Unlike chronic ischemic or structural heart disease, SCM arises acutely due to a complex interplay of inflammatory, metabolic, endothelial, and microvascular mechanisms and often, though not always, demonstrates reversibility within days.6˒8˒9 Despite increasing recognition, SCM remains poorly defined due to the absence of standardized diagnostic criteria and substantial overlap with other acute cardiomyopathies, including Takotsubo cardiomyopathy and myocarditis.10 Current diagnostic approaches rely primarily on echocardiography, strain imaging, biomarkers, and clinical trajectory, while emerging modalities such as cardiac magnetic resonance imaging may provide additional insight into myocardial inflammation, edema, and fibrosis. Management remains largely supportive and centered on treatment of the underlying septic process, with ongoing uncertainty regarding targeted heart failure therapies and adjunctive interventions. This review summarizes current understanding of SCM pathophysiology, diagnosis, imaging findings, and management while critically examining whether SCM represents a distinct disease entity or part of a broader stress-inflammatory cardiomyopathy spectrum.ObjectivesThe objectives include: proposing a broad definition and description of septic cardiomyopathy, providing a comprehensive literature review on current diagnostic and management options available for septic cardiomyopathy, and describing the limitations and gaps in this diagnosis and possible direction of future studies.Keywords: Septic cardiomyopathy, Sepsis, Cardiomyopathy, Cardiac inflammation

Intro: definition of sepsis and septic cardiomyopathy

Sepsis is a life-threatening condition caused by a dysregulated host response to infection and remains a major contributor to morbidity and mortality worldwide [3], [4]. Cardiovascular dysfunction is a frequent complication of sepsis, with septic cardiomyopathy (SCM), also referred to as sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy (SICM), representing an increasingly recognized but incompletely understood manifestation of sepsis-associated organ dysfunction [6], [7]. SCM is generally characterized by acute myocardial impairment occurring during sepsis, often involving reduced ventricular contractility, altered hemodynamics, and variable recovery following resolution of the underlying illness [8], [9].

Despite decades of investigation, no universally accepted definition of SCM exists. Findings commonly associated with SCM, including reduced left ventricular ejection fraction, ventricular dilation, biomarker elevation, and reversibility, lack specificity and may overlap with Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, type 2 myocardial infarction, and pre-existing heart failure [10]. Additionally, myocardial dysfunction during sepsis is highly dynamic and influenced by loading conditions, vasoplegia, catecholamine exposure, and systemic inflammation, complicating diagnosis in critically ill patients [6], [8], [13].

The proposed mechanisms underlying SCM are multifactorial and include inflammatory cytokine signaling, endothelial dysfunction, mitochondrial injury, oxidative stress, glycocalyx disruption, and microvascular abnormalities [8], [13], [14], [15]. Advances in echocardiography, strain imaging, and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging have improved characterization of myocardial involvement in sepsis; however, no pathognomonic imaging or biomarker profile has been established.

This review summarizes current evidence regarding the pathophysiology, diagnosis, imaging findings, and management of SCM while critically examining the ongoing debate surrounding whether SCM represents a distinct disease entity or part of a broader stress-inflammatory cardiomyopathy spectrum.

Risk factors

There are some known risk factors associated with septic cardiomyopathy including: younger age, higher lactate levels, male sex, history of heart failure [10], [11], [12].

Pathophysiology of septic cardiomyopathy

The pathophysiology of septic cardiomyopathy (SCM) reflects a complex interplay of inflammatory, metabolic, cellular, and vascular derangements that collectively impair myocardial performance. It was initially thought that septic cardiomyopathy (SCM) was a consequence of myocardial ischemia, nevertheless this hypothesis has been ruled out. Unlike ischemic heart disease or structural cardiomyopathies, SCM arises acutely in the setting of systemic infection and immune dysregulation, often without direct myocardial infection or coronary artery occlusion. It is now understood as a distinct clinical and biological entity with diverse contributing mechanisms.

Common cardiac manifestations in sepsis

•Mitochondrial dysfunction: One of the earliest and most well-established features of SCM is mitochondrial dysfunction, which impairs cardiomyocyte energy production. Inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 disrupt mitochondrial respiration, leading to reduced ATP synthesis, increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane potential. These changes interfere with excitation–contraction coupling, ultimately leading to reduced contractility. Electron microscopy in animal models and human autopsies has confirmed the presence of swollen, fragmented mitochondria in septic myocardium, accompanied by disorganized cristae and oxidative stress markers. [49], [50], [54]

•Altered calcium homeostasis: Another key component is calcium handling dysfunction. Sepsis alters calcium homeostasis within cardiomyocytes through impaired sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase (SERCA2a) activity and increased intracellular sodium via the Na+/H+ exchanger. These changes lead to impaired calcium reuptake and abnormal contractility, a hallmark of septic myocardial depression.

•Inflammatory cytokines: At the molecular level, the immune response plays a central role. Systemic inflammation in sepsis activates Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and downstream NF-κB signaling, resulting in widespread cytokine release. Transcriptomic and bioinformatic analyses have identified several hub genes implicated in SCM, including IL6ST, JAK3, MMP9, PRF1, and SERPINE1, which regulate immune cell trafficking, cytokine signaling, and apoptosis. [51] Key transcription factors such as STAT1, STAT3, RELA, and NF-κB1 orchestrate these gene networks, mediating both pro-inflammatory and compensatory anti-inflammatory responses. These processes are reflected in immune phenotypes such as the Sepsis Response Signature (SRS1), characterized by T-cell exhaustion and low HLA-DR expression. [55]

Thus, while mitochondrial impairment, myocardial edema, and biventricular involvement are common across septic states, SCM emerges from the convergence of these processes with maladaptive calcium handling, iNOS overexpression, targeted immune gene activation, and endothelial disruption via heparanase. These pathophysiological mechanisms contribute not only to the clinical manifestations of SCM but also to its variable course and prognosis. In survivors, resolution of inflammation and recovery of mitochondrial function often result in near-complete reversibility of dysfunction within 7–10 days. In contrast, persistent inflammation, ongoing fibrosis, or secondary myocardial insults can lead to chronic cardiac impairment or death.

Diagnosis

Diagnosing septic cardiomyopathy (SCM) remains complex due to its heterogeneous presentation, overlap with other forms of myocardial dysfunction, and lack of standardized criteria. Recognition depends on clinical suspicion, imaging findings, and exclusion of alternative etiologies.

Cardiac Magnetic Resonance Imaging (CMR): Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) has emerged as a powerful tool for evaluating myocardial involvement in sepsis, offering the ability to assess both cardiac function and underlying tissue characteristics in a single modality. In contrast to echocardiography, which primarily provides functional information, CMR enables detailed evaluation of myocardial composition through techniques such as T1 and T2 mapping, extracellular volume (ECV) quantification, and late gadolinium enhancement (LGE).

Within the broader context of inflammatory cardiomyopathies, CMR is well established as a central diagnostic modality. The Lake Louise Criteria incorporate imaging markers of myocardial injury—namely edema, hyperemia, and fibrosis—to support the diagnosis of myocarditis [1]. While this framework has improved diagnostic consistency, it is important to recognize that even in myocarditis, these findings are not entirely specific and must be interpreted alongside clinical and laboratory data. [59]

In septic cardiomyopathy (SCM), the application of CMR is more complex, as no standardized imaging profile has been established. Available data suggest that myocardial changes in sepsis are variable and evolve over time. In the acute phase, CMR may demonstrate features consistent with diffuse myocardial involvement, including increased T2 values indicative of edema, as well as elevated native T1 and ECV reflecting interstitial expansion and inflammatory activity. [60] These findings suggest overlap with inflammatory cardiomyopathies, although they lack specificity for SCM.

A key distinction from myocarditis lies in the pattern of irreversible injury. In acute SCM, LGE is typically absent or minimal, indicating that focal necrosis or fibrosis is not a dominant feature early in the disease course [60]. However, this pattern appears to change over time. In patients evaluated after recovery from septic shock, imaging studies have demonstrated increased native T1 values and a higher prevalence of non-ischemic LGE, findings consistent with residual myocardial remodeling or fibrosis [61]. This temporal shift underscores the dynamic nature of myocardial injury in sepsis and highlights the importance of timing when interpreting imaging findings.

CMR findings also reflect the clinical overlap between SCM and other stress-related cardiomyopathies. For example, Takotsubo-like patterns of regional dysfunction have been described in patients with septic shock, suggesting that similar pathophysiologic mechanisms may be involved [60]. At the same time, classic imaging features used to distinguish myocarditis—such as subepicardial or mid-wall LGE—or Takotsubo cardiomyopathy—characterized by regional wall motion abnormalities with minimal fibrosis—are not consistently present in SCM [59]. This lack of a reproducible imaging signature reinforces the challenge of defining SCM as a discrete entity based on imaging alone.

Despite these advantages, the clinical application of CMR in sepsis is limited. The need for patient transport, continuous monitoring, and hemodynamic stability makes its use challenging in critically ill populations [60]. Consequently, CMR is most often reserved for cases in which diagnostic uncertainty persists or when differentiation from alternative etiologies, such as myocarditis or stress cardiomyopathy, may alter management.

Overall, while CMR provides valuable insight into myocardial involvement in sepsis, its findings are best understood as part of a broader clinical assessment. Current evidence supports the view that SCM represents a heterogeneous and evolving myocardial response to systemic inflammation, rather than a condition defined by a single or pathognomonic imaging pattern.

PA catheter

Pulmonary artery catheter device provides useful information that can help guide management in the setting of SCM. However, due to the invasive nature of the device, this makes it difficult for widespread use and therefore may not be the gold standard for diagnosis. This device allows data like cardiac output and cardiac filling pressures to be obtained by measuring mixed venous blood oxygen saturation (SvO2). PA catheter may be useful to decide whether to initiate a patient on inotropes or not, considering a SvO2 value of <65 to 70% to start inotropes in patients with low cardiac output and high PA occlusion pressure [31].

Diagnostic challenges

The primary challenge in defining SCM lies in distinguishing it from new-onset or exacerbated heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) that occurs during sepsis. Left ventricular dysfunction alone is insufficient to diagnose SCM, given that many patients with sepsis have pre-existing cardiac disease or may develop supply-demand mismatch.

Our proposed diagnostic features aim to address this ambiguity by incorporating findings that reflect the distinct pathophysiology and clinical trajectory of SCM:

•Biventricular involvement and transient LV dilation: Unlike typical ischemic HFrEF, SCM often shows biventricular systolic dysfunction with transient LV cavity enlargement that normalizes within days to a week in survivors.

•Reversibility: While not required for diagnosis, recovery of function within 7–10 days is typical and suggests SCM over persistent or progressive HFrEF.

Septic cardiomyopathy remains difficult to diagnose because no single imaging modality or biomarker provides sufficient specificity. Echocardiography frequently demonstrates abnormalities such as reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), right ventricular dysfunction, or impaired strain, but these findings are not unique to SCM and may also be observed in Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, viral myocarditis, or ischemic injury. Biomarkers such as troponin and BNP are similarly nonspecific, as they may be elevated in a wide range of critical illness states. A large retrospective cohort study by Huang et al. (Crit Care Med, 2020) evaluated more than 400 patients with sepsis and septic shock who underwent echocardiography within the first 24 h of ICU admission. The investigators found that approximately one-third of patients had reduced LVEF, yet this finding was not independently associated with increased in-hospital mortality after adjustment for severity of illness. Interestingly, patients with reduced LVEF often had higher illness severity and required more intensive support, but many experienced subsequent recovery of systolic function [58]. These results highlight two key diagnostic challenges: first, that echocardiographic findings of reduced systolic function in sepsis are common but not specific to SCM, and second, that reversibility of dysfunction, though characteristic of SCM, cannot reliably be confirmed at the time of diagnosis. Together, these observations emphasize that SCM is best considered a diagnosis of exclusion, recognized when cardiac dysfunction arises in temporal association with sepsis and generally tracks with the illness course, rather than on the basis of any pathognomonic imaging or biomarker feature.

Is septic cardiomyoapthy a distinct entity?

Whether septic cardiomyopathy represents a distinct disease entity or one phenotype within a broader spectrum of stress- and inflammation-mediated myocardial dysfunction remains unresolved. Traditionally, SCM has been described as acute, often reversible myocardial dysfunction occurring during sepsis, characterized by global systolic impairment, ventricular dilatation, and recovery over days to weeks. However, these features are not specific to SCM. Reversibility is also a defining feature of Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, inflammatory myocardial dysfunction may occur in myocarditis, and reduced LVEF during sepsis may reflect pre-existing heart failure, type 2 myocardial infarction, or loading-condition changes rather than a sepsis-specific cardiomyopathy.

Several observations support SCM as a clinically meaningful phenotype. Cardiac dysfunction often develops in close temporal association with sepsis, may parallel the severity of the septic illness, and is linked to inflammatory, metabolic, endothelial, and microvascular pathways that are central to sepsis biology. CMR studies further suggest that acute SCM may demonstrate myocardial edema and interstitial expansion, while post-sepsis cardiomyopathy may show residual remodeling or fibrosis. These findings support the concept that sepsis can produce a recognizable pattern of myocardial injury.

At the same time, available evidence does not establish SCM as a discrete disease with pathognomonic diagnostic criteria. Echocardiographic abnormalities, biomarker elevations, strain impairment, and CMR findings overlap substantially with Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, and ischemic injury. Additionally, SCM studies vary widely in timing of assessment, imaging criteria, inclusion of patients with pre-existing cardiovascular disease, and definitions of recovery. This heterogeneity limits comparison across studies and makes it difficult to determine whether SCM is a single entity or a collection of related phenotypes.

A more practical framework may be to view SCM as part of a broader stress-inflammatory cardiomyopathy spectrum, in which sepsis acts as a systemic trigger for myocardial dysfunction through inflammatory signaling, catecholamine exposure, microvascular injury, and metabolic dysregulation. Within this spectrum, some patients may manifest a predominantly global septic myocardial depression, others may develop Takotsubo-like regional dysfunction, and others may have myocarditis-like inflammatory injury. Until validated diagnostic criteria, imaging signatures, or biomarker profiles are established, SCM should be regarded as a clinically useful but biologically heterogeneous syndrome rather than a fully defined disease entity.

Management

At present time, there is no definitely established guidelines regarding the treatment of SCM. The mainstay of the management of SCM is supportive and includes: fluid expansion, hemodynamic support, antibiotics directed towards source of infection. However, there are many promising potential treatment options that will be explored in this section.

Step 1: identify sepsis and assess for myocardial involvement

Diagnosis begins with confirming sepsis and assessing for cardiac dysfunction via echocardiography (LVEF, GLS, RV function, LV dilation) and biomarkers (troponin, BNP, inflammatory cytokines). Cardiac dysfunction during sepsis should prompt consideration of SCM, particularly when reversible features or biventricular involvement are present.

Step 2: initiate fluid resuscitation

In patients with sepsis, fluid management is crucial to avoid tissue hypoperfusion. The mechanism through which fluids play an important role managing patients with sepsis lies in the Frank-Starling, mechanism, increasing preload increases stroke volume thus optimizing cardiac output; early recognition of sepsis is paramount to start fluids in an early phase to guarantee optimal tissue perfusion, however the SOAP study, in a follow up of 3147 patients demonstrated that aggressive resuscitation or a positive fluid balance is associated with an increased risk of death [37]. The fluid of choice is crystalloids [32]. Many different fluids have been proposed to be routinely used for sepsis, but data supporting other fluids than crystalloids is lacking. According to recent guidelines fluid management should begin with an initial 30 mL/kg of crystalloids within the first 3 h [32]. Echocardiography plays an important role monitoring patients with SCM while receiving fluids, echocardiogram can help to differentiate volume overloaded patients from hypovolemic ones.

Step 3: begin vasopressors if hypotension persists

Vasopressor use is suggested for fluid unresponsive or hemodynamically unstable patients. First line vasopressors in the management of SCM are catecholamines. Norepinephrine has been proposed on the management of fluid unresponsive SCM. Norepinephrine has alpha and beta-1 effects but has a stronger adrenergic profile which comes in handy when treating distributive shock, however increasing afterload can cause a drop in cardiac output that can unmask cardiac dysfunction; while epinephrine and dopamine have a stronger alpha than beta effects, but their use has been associated with cardiac arrhythmias 32.

Step 4: add inotropes for persistent low cardiac output or hypoperfusion

Evidence of use of inodilators for sepsis or septic shock with myocardial dysfunction is based on physiological data. In patients with signs of hypoperfusion despite resuscitation measures, inotropes may improve hemodynamic profile and reduce mortality [33]. However the decision to treat SCM with inotropes cannot be taken lightly, considering that an optimal volume status must be ensured.

Dobutamine: The drug of choice for SCM. Its use is approved in patients with myocardial dysfunction and low cardiac output [32]. May cause an increased myocardial demand and vasodilation.

Levosimendan: Increases calcium effects on myocardial cells thus improving contraction. The supporting data for levosimendan in the context of SCM is controversial. Some authors found it may decrease mortality versus standard inotropics, however the LeoPARDS trial found no difference in mortality in patients under levosimendan vs standard care.

Milrinone: Most of the studies supporting its use in septic shock are in pediatric patients [34], [35]. In experimental endotoxemic hamster models milrinone showed arteriolar vasoconstriction attenuation and improvement of functional capillary density [36]; however, because of its vasodilatory effects milrinone is not recommended for SCM.

Step 5: heart rate control and beta blockade

Persistent supraventricular tachycardia is a common finding in the intensive care unit and may contribute to myocardial oxygen demand–supply mismatch. The hypothesis that controlling excessive heart rate could benefit patients with septic shock or SCM has led to trials investigating selective beta-blockade:

Step 8: Consider investigational and experimental therapies

Melatonin has shown promise in mouse models by preserving mitochondrial function and reducing cardiac stress. Schistosoma japonicum-derived cystatin reduced inflammation and cardiac dysfunction in preclinical models. Heparanase inhibitors may mitigate endothelial damage and fibrosis. Gene therapy targeting immune or endothelial pathways is under exploration, though not yet applicable to clinical care.

Step 9: reassess for recovery and consider delayed GDMT

Beyond conventional management strategies, several emerging therapies have shown promise in preclinical studies for mitigating myocardial injury and inflammation in septic cardiomyopathy. The following agents represent potential future directions for targeted intervention:

•Melatonin: Melatonin has been studied in mouse models and been shown to attenuate septic myocardial injury. Step 10: Monitor closely and adapt care as the clinical picture evolves.

Repeat echocardiography, including strain imaging, helps track myocardial recovery. Persistent dysfunction should prompt evaluation for alternate or overlapping diagnoses (e.g., Takotsubo, myocarditis), while rapid recovery is more consistent with SCM.

In summary, SCM management begins with standard sepsis care—fluid resuscitation, vasopressors, and inotropes—and expands into individualized strategies that consider myocardial physiology, recovery trajectory, and emerging therapies. While definitive guidelines are lacking, early recognition and targeted management may improve outcomes.

Additional treatment considerations: Goal Directed Medical Therapy (GDMT)

SCM differs from classic heart failure (HF) in several important ways that influence its management. Septic cardiomyopathy is often reversible, therefore initiation of GDMT is a question for consideration. Though retrospectively, recovery may be able to differentiate from septic cardiomyopathy vs ischemic cardiomyopathy, it is unclear in the acute setting therefore current guidelines on early initiation of GDMT are not available. However, the transient nature of SCM in many cases makes earlier GDMT unnecessary of even harmful in the acute setting therefore it is typically deferred until stabilization. These therapies may be considered during convalescence if persistent myocardial dysfunction is observed, but optimal timing is undefined.

Key management controversies in septic cardiomyopathy

Despite advances in supportive care for sepsis and septic shock, several management questions specific to septic cardiomyopathy (SCM) remain unresolved. One major challenge is determining whether SCM represents a transient physiologic response to sepsis or a form of myocardial injury requiring heart failure–directed therapy. Although ventricular dysfunction in SCM is often reversible, reversibility alone does not distinguish SCM from Takotsubo cardiomyopathy or other stress-related cardiomyopathies and cannot be confirmed at initial presentation [27], [28]. Additionally, excessive fluid administration may worsen ventricular dysfunction and contribute to adverse outcomes, emphasizing the importance of repeated hemodynamic reassessment rather than fixed resuscitation strategies [37].

Another ongoing controversy involves catecholamine exposure. Vasopressors and inotropes remain central to septic shock management, yet excessive adrenergic stimulation may also contribute to myocardial injury, tachyarrhythmias, and stress-related cardiomyopathy [17], [18], [19], [38]. This has led to growing interest in heart rate control and selective beta-blockade in septic shock and SCM. While physiologic and observational studies suggested potential hemodynamic benefit, evidence remains inconsistent, and routine beta-blocker use in SCM is not currently recommended [38], [40], [41]. Similarly, the optimal timing and role of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) remain unclear, particularly given concerns that beta-blockers or afterload-reducing therapies may worsen hemodynamics during acute septic vasoplegia [28], [31].

The role of immunomodulatory therapies also remains uncertain. Corticosteroids are used in refractory septic shock but are not specifically indicated for SCM, and evidence supporting targeted anti-inflammatory therapy for septic myocardial dysfunction remains limited [32]. Emerging imaging and molecular studies further suggest that SCM may represent a heterogeneous syndrome rather than a single disease entity, with overlapping inflammatory, catecholamine-mediated, and microvascular mechanisms shared with Takotsubo cardiomyopathy and myocarditis [28]. This evolving understanding raises the possibility that future therapies may need to be individualized according to specific inflammatory or stress-mediated phenotypes.

Current challenge

Current challenges in the recognition of SCM remain the lack of a universal definition and therefore specific guidelines on diagnostic criteria or management. Though small trials have been performed, there is a lack of large randomized control trials for septic cardiomyopathy. In light of this, it is of remarkable importance to have a high index of suspicion early on in sepsis as to aid physicians in prompt treatment. Fluid therapy, hemodynamic support, and treatment of the underlying cause remain the mainstay in treatment. New studies are required to determine whether or not new therapies can be routinely useful.

Future directions

Despite increasing recognition of septic cardiomyopathy (SCM), major gaps remain in its definition, diagnosis, and management. Future efforts should focus on establishing standardized diagnostic criteria that account for biventricular dysfunction, strain imaging, and immune profiling, rather than relying solely on LVEF or reversibility. Biomarkers and transcriptomic signatures may help refine diagnosis and guide therapy, while advanced imaging like GLS and RV strain could improve early detection and risk stratification. Therapeutically, questions persist around the optimal timing of guideline-directed medical therapy, the role of heart rate control, and the utility of immunomodulatory strategies. Targeted approaches, such as heparanase inhibition and mitochondrial protection, represent promising experimental avenues but require further investigation in SCM-specific clinical trials.

Conclusion

Septic cardiomyopathy represents a clinically significant but often underrecognized manifestation of sepsis-related organ dysfunction. It is characterized by dynamic and heterogeneous cardiac impairment that frequently tracks the inflammatory and hemodynamic course of sepsis, rather than conforming to traditional heart failure paradigms. While reversibility is common, it is not universal, and the diagnosis remains one of exclusion—relying on imaging, biomarkers, and clinical context. Current management is largely supportive, with growing interest in tailored approaches that reflect the underlying immune and metabolic disturbances. Continued research is needed to define SCM more precisely, identify reliable diagnostic markers, and determine whether specific therapeutic strategies can improve outcomes beyond standard sepsis care. Advancing this field will require both mechanistic insights and targeted clinical trials designed around the unique biology and clinical behavior of SCM.
