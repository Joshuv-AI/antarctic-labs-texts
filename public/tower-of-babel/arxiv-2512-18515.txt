The Narrow Corridor of Stable Solutions in an Extended Osipov–Lanchester Model with Constant Total Population
Sergey Salishev

Abstract
This paper considers a modification of the classical Osipov–Lanchester model in which the total population of the two forces N = R + B N=R+B is preserved over time. It is shown that the dynamics of the ratio y = R / B y=R/B reduce to the Riccati equation y ˙ = α ​ y 2 − β \dot{y}=\alpha y^{2}-\beta , which admits a complete analytical study. The main result is that asymptotically stable invariant sets in the positive quadrant R , B ≥ 0 R,B\geq 0 exist exactly in three sign cases of ( α , β ) (\alpha,\beta) : (i) α < 0 , β < 0 \alpha<0,\beta<0 (stable interior equilibrium), (ii) α = 0 , β < 0 \alpha=0,\beta<0 (the face B = 0 B=0 is stable), (iii) α < 0 , β = 0 \alpha<0,\beta=0 (the face R = 0 R=0 is stable). For α > 0 \alpha>0 or β > 0 \beta>0 the solutions reach the boundaries of applicability of the model in finite time. Moreover, α < 0 , β < 0 \alpha<0,\beta<0 corresponds to exponential growth of solutions in the original system. Passing to a model perturbed in α ⁡ ( t ) , β ⁡ ( t ) \alpha(t),\beta(t) requires buffer dynamics repelling from the axes to preserve stability of the solution.



1
Introduction

The intuitive illustration of confrontation and cooperation between the state and society in the book “The Narrow Corridor”
[
1
]
presumes the existence of a stable balance of forces, as well as the possibility of transitions to extreme regimes. The book contains a large number of historical examples; however, it does not pose the question of mathematical formalization and qualitative investigation of models. It is interesting to compare Acemoglu’s intuitive dynamics with a simple mathematical model exhibiting the same qualitative behavior. It is known that the classical Osipov–Lanchester competition model with parameters
\alpha>0,\beta>0
describes the mutual attrition of two forces and has no stable solutions
[
2
,
3
]
. This raises the question of extending the Osipov–Lanchester model to all values of
\alpha,\beta
that, without essential changes of behavior in the region
\alpha>0,\beta>0
, would contain stable solutions in the region
\alpha\leq 0
or
\beta\leq 0
, corresponding to cooperative regimes in Acemoglu’s intuitive dynamics. That would be in the spirit of V. Arnold’s view on soft models in the social sciences
[
4
]
.

An extended Osipov–Lanchester model with a constant total population is proposed

\dot{R}=-\beta B+\mu R,\qquad\dot{B}=-\alpha R+\mu B,\qquad N=R+B=\text{const}.

(1)

The passage from the absolute variables
(R,B)
to the dimensionless shares

r=R/N
,
b=B/N
with
N=R+B
is a standard normalization in population and
competition dynamics: it separates
composition
(how the total is split
between the two sides) from
scale
(the overall magnitude of the system).
The impact of scale is not discarded: once the relative dynamics is understood,
absolute growth/decay can be analyzed separately in the original system; see Theorem
5

for the resulting “cooperation premium” in absolute magnitudes.

The existing literature contains several mathematical formalizations of confrontational interaction between the state and society: extensions of Lanchester equations to irregular conflicts
[
5
,
6
]
, dynamic protest models
[
7
]
, the structural-demographic theory of macro-social cycles
[
8
]
.

The form of equation (
1
) is typical for models with growth or reinforcements in differential games with nonzero-sum
[
9
,
10
]
, but
\mu
usually corresponds to the influence of external factors and is not state-dependent.

Without loss of generality, the system with
N=R+B=1
can be regarded as a generalized replicator with a state-dependent payoff matrix (a special case with a fixed payoff matrix is the predator-prey Lotka–Volterra system
[
11
]
). Unlike the more general treatments of models of this type in
[
12
,
13
]
, an explicit reduction to the Riccati equation provides a complete analysis of the behavior of solutions for all parameter values
[
14
]
.

In view of the above, a simple closed system with a constant sum and an explicit classification of stable regimes appears useful
and, to the author’s knowledge, has not been published in this form. The novelty is that a single model preserves the Osipov–Lanchester structure, remains analytically tractable, and covers both confrontational and cooperative interaction regimes, matching Acemoglu’s intuitive dynamics of the
“narrow corridor”
[
1
]
.
In particular, the “narrowness” is made explicit in Theorem
4
, which derives a closed-form stability
condition and error bound for the ratio dynamics under bounded time-varying perturbations of
\alpha
and
\beta
. Throughout the paper,
political-economic terms such as “technological progress” are used as interpretive labels for mathematically defined regimes, rather than
as normative or empirical claims.

Main technical results:

1.

It is proved (Theorem
2
) that for
R,B\geq 0
and constant
N=R{+}B
asymptotically stable invariant
sets exist only when
\alpha\leq 0,\ \beta\leq 0
, and in this case:

(a)

for
\alpha<0,\beta<0
there exists a unique interior asymptotically stable equilibrium point;

(b)

for
\alpha=0,\beta<0
the boundary
B=0
becomes stable;

(c)

for
\alpha<0,\beta=0
the boundary
R=0
becomes stable.

In all other cases the solutions reach the boundaries
R=0
or
B=0
in finite time, which corresponds to the classical Osipov–Lanchester formulation.

2.

When passing to the model perturbed in
\alpha(t),\beta(t)
, it is necessary to have buffer dynamics repelling from the axes in order for stability to hold (Theorem
4
).

3.

It is proved that for
\alpha<0,\beta<0
and unbounded resources the solutions of the Osipov–Lanchester system grow asymptotically faster than in the other cases (Theorem
5
).

2
Problem Statement and Relation to the Classical Osipov–Lanchester Equations

Consider the original Osipov–Lanchester model:

\dot{R}=-\beta B,\qquad\dot{B}=-\alpha R.

(2)

This model only makes sense for parameters
\alpha>0,\beta>0
, and in this parameter region it has no stable solutions
[
4
]
: trajectories in the positive orthant reach the coordinate axes in finite time (mutual attrition).

Extending the model to arbitrary signs of
\alpha
and
\beta
leads to the emergence of exponentially growing solutions. To bound the growth of the solutions, the analysis passes to relative values of the population sizes. For this purpose the sum and shares are introduced

N=R+B,\qquad r=\frac{R}{N},\qquad b=\frac{B}{N},\qquad r+b=1.

Then

\dot{N}=\dot{R}+\dot{B}=-\beta B-\alpha R=-N\big(\alpha r+\beta b\big).

(3)

Denote

\mu:=\alpha r+\beta b\quad\Rightarrow\quad\dot{N}=-\mu\,N.

(4)

Derivatives of the shares (quotient rule):

\displaystyle\dot{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{R}}{N}-r\,\frac{\dot{N}}{N}=\frac{-\beta B}{N}-r(-\mu)=-\beta\,b+\mu\,r,

(5)

\displaystyle\dot{b}

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{B}}{N}-b\,\frac{\dot{N}}{N}=\frac{-\alpha R}{N}-b(-\mu)=-\alpha\,r+\mu\,b.

(6)

The equivalent system in variables
(r,b,N)
takes the form

\dot{r}=-\beta b+\mu r,\qquad\dot{b}=-\alpha r+\mu b,\qquad\dot{N}=-\mu N,\qquad\mu=\alpha r+\beta b,\ \ r+b=1.

(7)

This system is rewritten in the following generalized form

\dot{R}=-\beta B+\mu R,\qquad\dot{B}=-\alpha R+\mu B,\qquad N=R+B=\text{const},

where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
are interaction parameters, and
\mu(t)
is chosen so as to preserve the sum
N
. From
\dot{N}=\dot{R}+\dot{B}=0
it follows that

\mu(t)=\frac{\alpha R+\beta B}{N}.

(8)

Here
\mu
appears in two closely related but conceptually different ways.
In the original model (
2
) the normalization
N=R+B
yields the

induced
scale rate
\mu:=\alpha r+\beta b
through
\dot{N}=-\mu N

(
7
).
In the constant-sum extension (
1
) the construction instead
chooses
the
time-dependent term
\mu(t)
according to (
8
) so as to enforce

\dot{N}=0
and thus obtain a closed dynamics on the simplex of shares.
Equivalently,
\mu(t)
acts as a balancing (normalizing) feedback that cancels
the scale drift produced by the raw interaction terms and isolates the dynamics
of the relative variables
x=R/N
(or
y=R/B
).
If this balancing term is removed, i.e. if one sets
\mu\equiv 0
in
(
1
), then (
1
) reduces to the classical
Osipov–Lanchester system (
2
).

It is convenient to switch to the share
x=\frac{R}{N}\in[0,1]
and the ratio
y=\frac{R}{B}=\frac{x}{1-x}
(for
B>0
). Then from (
1
), (
8
) one obtains

\dot{x}=(\alpha-\beta)x^{2}+2\beta x-\beta,

(9)

and for the ratio the Riccati equation follows

\dot{y}=\alpha y^{2}-\beta.

(10)

Lemma 1

(Dynamics of the share and the ratio)
.

Consider the system (
1
)

\dot{R}=-\beta B+\mu R,\qquad\dot{B}=-\alpha R+\mu B,\qquad N=R+B=\text{const},

with
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mu(t)
given by (
8
)

\mu(t)=\frac{\alpha R+\beta B}{N}.

Define the share
x=\frac{R}{N}\in[0,1]
and, for
B>0
, the ratio

y=\frac{R}{B}=\frac{x}{1-x}
. Then
x
satisfies (
9
)

\dot{x}=(\alpha-\beta)x^{2}+2\beta x-\beta,

and
y
satisfies the Riccati equation (
10
)

\dot{y}=\alpha y^{2}-\beta.

Proof.

Since
N=R+B
is constant, it follows that

x=\frac{R}{N}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\dot{x}=\frac{\dot{R}}{N}.

Using (
1
) gives

\dot{x}=\frac{-\beta B+\mu R}{N}=-\beta\,\frac{B}{N}+\mu\,\frac{R}{N}.

(11)

Because
x=\frac{R}{N}
and
R+B=N
, it follows that

\frac{R}{N}=x,\qquad\frac{B}{N}=1-x.

Hence (
11
) becomes

\dot{x}=-\beta(1-x)+\mu x.

(12)

Next
\mu
from (
8
) is rewritten in terms of
x
:

\mu=\frac{\alpha R+\beta B}{N}=\alpha\frac{R}{N}+\beta\frac{B}{N}=\alpha x+\beta(1-x)=\beta+(\alpha-\beta)x.

Substituting this expression for
\mu
into (
12
) yields

\dot{x}=-\beta(1-x)+x\bigl[\beta+(\alpha-\beta)x\bigr]=-\beta+\beta x+\beta x+(\alpha-\beta)x^{2}.

Collecting like terms yields

\dot{x}=(\alpha-\beta)x^{2}+2\beta x-\beta,

which is exactly (
9
).

For
y
, the definition
y=\frac{R}{B}
(with
B>0
) is used. Differentiating,

\dot{y}=\frac{\dot{R}\,B-R\,\dot{B}}{B^{2}}.

Substituting
\dot{R}
and
\dot{B}
from (
1
),

\dot{R}=-\beta B+\mu R,\qquad\dot{B}=-\alpha R+\mu B,

gives

\displaystyle\dot{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-\beta B+\mu R)B-R(-\alpha R+\mu B)}{B^{2}}=\frac{-\beta B^{2}+\mu RB+\alpha R^{2}-\mu RB}{B^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha R^{2}-\beta B^{2}}{B^{2}}=\alpha\left(\frac{R}{B}\right)^{2}-\beta=\alpha y^{2}-\beta,

which is (
10
). This completes the proof.
∎

3
Solution via the Riccati Equation and Stability Analysis

3.1
Explicit solutions

Equation (
10
) can be integrated in elementary functions in all sign configurations of
\alpha,\beta
.

Theorem 1

(Solutions for
y=R/B
)
.

Let
y(0)=y_{0}>0
and consider

\dot{y}=\alpha y^{2}-\beta.

Introduce the shorthand

\kappa:=\sqrt{|\alpha\beta|},\qquad\rho:=\sqrt{\Bigl|\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\Bigr|},

(with
\rho
used only when
\alpha\neq 0
).

1.

If
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
, then on the maximal interval of existence

y(t)=\rho\,\coth\!\Big(\operatorname{artanh}\!\big(\rho/y_{0}\big)-\kappa t\Big).

(13)

2.

If
\alpha<0
and
\beta<0
, then

y(t)=\rho\,\tanh\!\Big(\operatorname{artanh}\!\big(y_{0}/\rho\big)+\kappa t\Big).

(14)

3.

If
\alpha>0
and
\beta<0
, then (on its maximal interval of existence, up to the first pole of
\tan
)

y(t)=\rho\,\tan\!\Big(\arctan\!\big(y_{0}/\rho\big)+\kappa t\Big).

(15)

4.

If
\alpha<0
and
\beta>0
, then (on its maximal interval of existence, until the first time the argument reaches
0
)

y(t)=\rho\,\tan\!\Big(\arctan\!\big(y_{0}/\rho\big)-\kappa t\Big).

(16)

5.

If
\alpha=0
, then
y(t)=y_{0}-\beta t
; if
\beta=0
, then

y(t)=\frac{y_{0}}{1-\alpha y_{0}t}.

Proof.

Set
y(t)=\dfrac{R(t)}{B(t)}
and assume that
B(t)>0
on the time interval under
consideration (for trajectories of system (
1
) in the orthant

R,B\geq 0
this is true as long as the solution has not reached the face
B=0
).
Applying Lemma
1
yields the autonomous Riccati equation

\dot{y}=\alpha y^{2}-\beta,\qquad y(0)=y_{0}>0,

which integrates in elementary functions. All sign cases are considered.

1.

Case
\alpha>0,\ \beta>0
.

Since
\alpha y^{2}-\beta=\alpha\,(y^{2}-\rho^{2})
, one has

\int\frac{dy}{\alpha y^{2}-\beta}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int\frac{dy}{y^{2}-\rho^{2}}=\frac{1}{2\alpha\rho}\ln\Bigl|\frac{y-\rho}{y+\rho}\Bigr|=t+C.

Exponentiating and solving for
y
yields the standard hyperbolic form

y(t)=\rho\,\coth(\kappa t+s_{0})

for some constant
s_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. Using the initial condition
y(0)=y_{0}
gives

\coth(s_{0})=\frac{y_{0}}{\rho}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\tanh(s_{0})=\frac{\rho}{y_{0}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad s_{0}=\operatorname{artanh}\!\Bigl(\frac{\rho}{y_{0}}\Bigr).

Substituting
s_{0}
and using the identity
\coth(u)=\coth(-u)
with a sign change
in the constant, the representation used in (
13
) is obtained:

y(t)=\rho\,\coth\!\Bigl(\operatorname{artanh}\!\bigl(\rho/y_{0}\bigr)-\kappa t\Bigr).

2.

Case
\alpha<0,\ \beta<0
.

Write
\alpha=-a
,
\beta=-b
with
a,b>0
. Then

\dot{y}=-ay^{2}+b=b-ay^{2}=b\Bigl(1-\frac{y^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\Bigr).

Separating variables gives

\int\frac{dy}{b-ay^{2}}=\frac{1}{b}\int\frac{dy}{1-(y/\rho)^{2}}=\frac{\rho}{b}\,\operatorname{artanh}\!\Bigl(\frac{y}{\rho}\Bigr)=t+C.

Since
\rho/b=1/\kappa
, it follows that

\operatorname{artanh}\!\Bigl(\frac{y(t)}{\rho}\Bigr)=\kappa t+s_{0},\qquad s_{0}=\operatorname{artanh}\!\Bigl(\frac{y_{0}}{\rho}\Bigr),

hence (
14
):

y(t)=\rho\,\tanh\!\Bigl(\operatorname{artanh}\!\bigl(y_{0}/\rho\bigr)+\kappa t\Bigr).

3.

Case
\alpha>0,\ \beta<0
.

Write
\beta=-b
with
b>0
. Then

\dot{y}=\alpha y^{2}+b=b\Bigl(1+\frac{y^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\Bigr).

Separating variables gives

\int\frac{dy}{\alpha y^{2}+b}=\frac{1}{b}\int\frac{dy}{1+(y/\rho)^{2}}=\frac{\rho}{b}\,\arctan\!\Bigl(\frac{y}{\rho}\Bigr)=t+C.

Since
\rho/b=1/\kappa
, it follows that

\arctan\!\Bigl(\frac{y(t)}{\rho}\Bigr)=\kappa t+s_{0},\qquad s_{0}=\arctan\!\Bigl(\frac{y_{0}}{\rho}\Bigr),

and therefore

y(t)=\rho\,\tan\!\Bigl(\arctan\!\bigl(y_{0}/\rho\bigr)+\kappa t\Bigr),

which is (
15
). The solution exists until the first pole of
\tan
.

4.

Case
\alpha<0,\ \beta>0
.

Write
\alpha=-a
with
a>0
. Then

\dot{y}=-ay^{2}-\beta=-\beta\Bigl(1+\frac{y^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\Bigr).

Separating variables gives

\int\frac{dy}{ay^{2}+\beta}=\frac{1}{\beta}\int\frac{dy}{1+(y/\rho)^{2}}=\frac{\rho}{\beta}\,\arctan\!\Bigl(\frac{y}{\rho}\Bigr)=-t+C.

Since
\rho/\beta=1/\kappa
, it follows that

\arctan\!\Bigl(\frac{y(t)}{\rho}\Bigr)=s_{0}-\kappa t,\qquad s_{0}=\arctan\!\Bigl(\frac{y_{0}}{\rho}\Bigr),

and therefore

y(t)=\rho\,\tan\!\Bigl(\arctan\!\bigl(y_{0}/\rho\bigr)-\kappa t\Bigr),

which is (
16
). Here
y(t)
decreases and can reach
0
in finite time,
which corresponds to the original trajectory hitting the face
R=0
.

5.

Case
\alpha=0
.

Then
\dot{y}=-\beta
and
y(t)=y_{0}-\beta t
.

6.

Case
\beta=0
.

Then
\dot{y}=\alpha y^{2}
and solving
\frac{dy}{y^{2}}=\alpha\,dt
yields

y(t)=\dfrac{y_{0}}{1-\alpha y_{0}t}
.

In all cases the solution is unique and satisfies
y(0)=y_{0}
.
∎

3.2
Classification of stable invariant sets

Interior equilibrium points are given by the roots of
\alpha y^{2}-\beta=0
. Their existence and stability, as well as the invariance of the domain
R,B\geq 0
, are described in the following theorem.

Theorem 2

(Classification of stable solutions)
.

Consider system (
1
) for
R,B\geq 0
,
N>0
.

1.

The domain
R,B\geq 0
is forward invariant if and only if
\alpha\leq 0
and
\beta\leq 0
. Indeed, from (
9
) it follows that
\dot{x}|_{x=0}=-\beta
and
\dot{x}|_{x=1}=\alpha
.

2.

An interior equilibrium exists if and only if
\alpha\beta>0
, and it is equal to

y_{*}=\sqrt{\beta/\alpha},\qquad x_{*}=\frac{y_{*}}{1+y_{*}}=\frac{\sqrt{|\beta|}}{\sqrt{|\alpha|}+\sqrt{|\beta|}}.

Its linear stability is determined by the sign of
g^{\prime}(y_{*})=2\alpha y_{*}
:

(a)

for
\alpha<0,\ \beta<0
the equilibrium point is asymptotically stable;

(b)

for
\alpha>0,\ \beta>0
the equilibrium point is unstable (repelling).

3.

In the degenerate cases
\alpha=0,\ \beta<0
(respectively
\beta=0,\ \alpha<0
) the set of equilibria coincides with the face
B\equiv 0
(respectively
R\equiv 0
), and this face is asymptotically stable.

4.

In all other cases (at least one of the parameters is
>0
) interior equilibria are absent or unstable, the domain
R,B\geq 0
is not invariant, and the solution reaches the boundary
R=0
or
B=0
in finite time (the times follow from (
13
), (
15
), (
16
)).

Proof.

Switch to the variable
x=\dfrac{R}{N}\in[0,1]
, where
N=R+B>0
is fixed. Then the equation (
9
) follows

\dot{x}=(\alpha-\beta)x^{2}+2\beta x-\beta,

and on the boundaries

\dot{x}\big|_{x=0}=-\beta,\qquad\dot{x}\big|_{x=1}=\alpha.

1.

The domain
R,B\geq 0
(that is,
x\in[0,1]
) is forward invariant if and only if the vector field on both boundaries points inward along the interval, namely

\dot{x}|_{x=0}\geq 0\iff-\beta\geq 0\iff\beta\leq 0,\qquad\dot{x}|_{x=1}\leq 0\iff\alpha\leq 0.

This yields the necessary and sufficient condition
\alpha\leq 0
,
\beta\leq 0
.

2.

Interior stationary points satisfy
\dot{x}=0
or, equivalently, the stationarity of
y=\dfrac{R}{B}
:

0=\alpha y^{2}-\beta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y_{*}=\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\quad(\alpha\beta>0).

Hence

x_{*}=\frac{y_{*}}{1+y_{*}}=\frac{\sqrt{|\beta|}}{\sqrt{|\alpha|}+\sqrt{|\beta|}}.

Linearizing the equation for
y
in a neighborhood of
y_{*}
yields

\dot{e}=(\alpha(2y_{*}))\,e+o(e)=2\alpha y_{*}e+o(e),

so the equilibrium attracts if and only if
2\alpha y_{*}<0
, that is, for
\alpha<0
and, automatically,
\beta<0
.

3.

If
\alpha=0
,
\beta<0
, then from (
9
)

\dot{x}=2\beta x-\beta=-\beta(1-2x),

and the equilibrium
x=0
(face
B=0
) attracts all solutions from
(0,1]
. Similarly, for
\beta=0
,
\alpha<0
the attracting face is
R=0
.

4.

If at least one of the parameters is positive, then at least on one of the boundaries the vector field points outward. Then either
x(t)
leaves the interval
[0,1]
, or (when
\alpha\beta>0
and
\alpha>0,\beta>0
) the solution for
y
according to Theorem
1
reaches infinity/zero in finite time, which in terms of
(R,B)
means reaching the face
R=0
or
B=0
. In both cases invariance is absent.

∎

4
Interpretation of the Corridor Narrowness

Acemoglu’s intuitive dynamics claim that the stable corridor is narrow, whereas our model yields an entire quadrant of stable solutions.
In practice
\alpha,\beta
vary over time, so the focus is on conditions for structural stability with respect to parameter changes
[
15
]
. The model is therefore modified.

Goal.

Guarantee invariance of
R>0,\ B>0
(i.e.
x(t)\in(0,1)
) under unknown dynamics of
a,b
.

Model.

Set
N=R+B>0
,
x=R/N\in[0,1]
. Then

\dot{x}=(a-b)x^{2}+2bx-b,\qquad\dot{a}=\phi(t),\quad\dot{b}=\psi(t).

(17)

Lemma 2

(Invariance)
.

If
a(t)\leq 0
and
b(t)\leq 0
for all
t\geq 0
, then the interval
[0,1]
is forward invariant for (
17
):

f(0)=-b(t)\geq 0
,
f(1)=a(t)\leq 0
.

Proof.

Let
x(t)
be a solution of (
17
) such that
x(0)\in[0,1]
. Suppose the contrary: there exists a first moment
t^{*}>0
when
x(t)
leaves the interval
[0,1]
.

1.

If
x(t^{*})=0
and the exit is “downwards”, then
\dot{x}(t^{*})<0
must hold. But

\dot{x}(t^{*})=f(t^{*},0)=-b(t^{*})\geq 0,

which contradicts the assumption.

2.

If
x(t^{*})=1
and the exit is “upwards”, then
\dot{x}(t^{*})>0
must hold. But

\dot{x}(t^{*})=f(t^{*},1)=a(t^{*})\leq 0,

which is also impossible.

Therefore the first exit cannot occur, and
x(t)\in[0,1]
for all
t\geq 0
.
∎

Theorem 3

(Sufficient conditions on the dynamics of
a,b
)
.

Let
a,b\in C^{1}([0,\infty))
,
a(0),b(0)\in[-1,0]
, and for all
t\geq 0

a(t)=0\ \Rightarrow\ \dot{a}(t)\leq 0,\qquad a(t)=-1\ \Rightarrow\ \dot{a}(t)\geq 0,

b(t)=0\ \Rightarrow\ \dot{b}(t)\leq 0,\qquad b(t)=-1\ \Rightarrow\ \dot{b}(t)\geq 0.

Then
a(t),b(t)\in[-1,0]
for all
t\geq 0
. In particular, by Lemma
2
, if
x(0)\in[0,1]

it follows that
x(t)\in[0,1]
for all
t\geq 0
, that is, the trajectory of the original system satisfies

R(t)>0,\quad B(t)>0,\qquad\forall t\geq 0.

Proof.

Consider the function
a(\cdot)
; the reasoning for
b(\cdot)
is symmetric. Assume that the set
[-1,0]
is not forward invariant for
a(\cdot)
. Then there exists a first moment
t^{*}>0
such that
a(t)\in[-1,0]
for
t<t^{*}
and
a(t^{*})\notin[-1,0]
.
By continuity
a(t^{*})
can be either
0
or
-1
.

1.

Let
a(t^{*})=0
and
a
try to exit into
(0,\infty)
. For the exit
\dot{a}(t^{*})>0
would be required, but by the assumption of the theorem
\dot{a}\leq 0
when
a=0
. Contradiction.

2.

Let
a(t^{*})=-1
and
a
try to exit into
(-\infty,-1)
. For this
\dot{a}(t^{*})<0
would be required, whereas by assumption
\dot{a}\geq 0
when
a=-1
. Contradiction.

Hence
a(t)\in[-1,0]
for all
t\geq 0
. Similarly
b(t)\in[-1,0]
for all
t\geq 0
. Then by Lemma
2
the vector field (
17
) on the interval
[0,1]
points inward at both boundaries, and thus every trajectory with
x(0)\in[0,1]
remains in
[0,1]
for all
t\geq 0
. This means
R(t)>0
,
B(t)>0
.
∎

Corollary 1

(Buffer dynamics)
.

Suppose there exist numbers
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\eta>0
such that for all
t\geq 0
the following “buffer” conditions hold:

\displaystyle a\in[-\delta,0]\Rightarrow\dot{a}\leq-\eta,\qquad a\in[-1,-1+\delta]\Rightarrow\dot{a}\geq\eta,

\displaystyle b\in[-\delta,0]\Rightarrow\dot{b}\leq-\eta,\qquad b\in[-1,-1+\delta]\Rightarrow\dot{b}\geq\eta.

If
a(0),b(0)\in[-1+\delta,-\delta]
, then
a(t),b(t)\in[-1+\delta,-\delta]
for all
t\geq 0
. In particular, there exists
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\delta)\in(0,1/2)
such that for every solution
x(t)
of equation (
17
) with
x(0)\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
it holds that
x(t)\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
for all
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

By the assumption on
a(t)
and
b(t)
for all
t\geq 0
the signs of the right-hand sides at the points
x=\varepsilon
and
x=1-\varepsilon
agree with the requirements of Theorem
3
. Therefore all the hypotheses of that theorem are satisfied for the interval
[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
, and it can be applied directly. The theorem then guarantees that the interval
[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
is forward invariant.
∎

Lemma 3

(Differential form of Gronwall’s inequality
[
15
]
)
.

Let
u\colon[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
be absolutely continuous and suppose that
for almost all
t\in[0,T]
,

\dot{u}(t)\;\leq\;\alpha(t)\,u(t)+\beta(t),

where
\alpha,\beta\in L^{1}([0,T])
. Then for all
t\in[0,T]
,

u(t)\;\leq\;u(0)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\int_{0}^{t}\alpha(s)\,ds\Bigr)+\int_{0}^{t}\exp\!\Bigl(\int_{s}^{t}\alpha(\tau)\,d\tau\Bigr)\,\beta(s)\,ds.

Theorem 4

(Structural “narrowness” of the corridor under parameter perturbations)
.

Assume that in system (
1
) the parameters have the form

\alpha(t)=-a+\delta a(t),\qquad\beta(t)=-b+\delta b(t),

where
a,b>0
are constants, and the perturbations are bounded:
|\delta a(t)|\leq\bar{a}
,
|\delta b(t)|\leq\bar{b}
for all
t\geq 0
.
Let
y=R/B
and
y_{*}=\sqrt{b/a}
; then the unperturbed dynamics is
\dot{y}=-ay^{2}+b
.
Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
and introduce the target “buffer” for the variable
y
, corresponding to the buffer for the share
x\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
:

\underline{y}_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon},\qquad\overline{y}_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon},\qquad Y_{\varepsilon}:=[\,\underline{y}_{\varepsilon},\overline{y}_{\varepsilon}\,].

Let

m_{\varepsilon}:=\min\{\,y_{*}-\underline{y}_{\varepsilon},\ \overline{y}_{\varepsilon}-y_{*}\,\}>0,\qquad M_{\varepsilon}:=\max_{y\in Y_{\varepsilon}}y^{2}=\overline{y}_{\varepsilon}^{\,2}.

If the “corridor” inequality

2\sqrt{ab}\,m_{\varepsilon}\;>\;\bar{a}\,M_{\varepsilon}+\bar{b}

(18)

holds, then for any solution with
y(0)\in Y_{\varepsilon}
it follows that
y(t)\in Y_{\varepsilon}
for all
t\geq 0
, and the deviation

e(t):=y(t)-y_{*}

satisfies the estimate

|e(t)|\;\leq\;e^{-2\sqrt{ab}\,t}\,|e(0)|+\frac{\bar{a}\,M_{\varepsilon}+\bar{b}}{2\sqrt{ab}}\bigl(1-e^{-2\sqrt{ab}\,t}\bigr),\qquad t\geq 0.

(19)

In particular, its steady-state part does not exceed
\dfrac{\bar{a}\,M_{\varepsilon}+\bar{b}}{2\sqrt{ab}}<m_{\varepsilon}
, so that the share

x(t)=\dfrac{y(t)}{1+y(t)}

remains in
[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
for all
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

Substitute the perturbed parameters into the equation for the ratio
y=R/B
. This gives

\dot{y}=\alpha(t)y^{2}-\beta(t)=\big(-a+\delta a(t)\big)y^{2}-\big(-b+\delta b(t)\big)=-ay^{2}+b+u(t),

where

u(t):=-\delta a(t)\,y^{2}+\delta b(t).

Let
y_{*}=\sqrt{b/a}
be the unperturbed equilibrium and introduce the deviation
e:=y-y_{*}
. Then

\dot{e}=-a\big((y_{*}+e)^{2}-y_{*}^{\,2}\big)+u(t)=-a(2y_{*}e+e^{2})+u(t)=-2\sqrt{ab}\,e-ae^{2}+u(t).

Note that
-ae^{2}\leq 0
, therefore

\dot{e}\leq-2\sqrt{ab}\,e+u(t).

(20)

Next the perturbation bound is calculated. By the definition of
Y_{\varepsilon}
the relation
y(t)\in Y_{\varepsilon}\subset(0,\infty)
holds and, consequently,

|u(t)|\leq|\delta a(t)|\,\max_{y\in Y_{\varepsilon}}y^{2}+|\delta b(t)|\leq\bar{a}\,M_{\varepsilon}+\bar{b}.

Applying to inequality (
20
) the Gronwall’s inequality
3
for the comparable linear system

\dot{z}=-2\sqrt{ab}\,z+(\bar{a}M_{\varepsilon}+\bar{b}),

yields the estimate

|e(t)|\leq\theta\,|e(0)|+(1-\theta)\frac{\bar{a}M_{\varepsilon}+\bar{b}}{2\sqrt{ab}},\quad t\geq 0,

(21)

where

\theta:=e^{-2\sqrt{ab}\,t}

that is, (
19
).

It remains to show that the trajectory cannot leave
Y_{\varepsilon}
. Assume the contrary: let
t^{*}
be the first moment when
y(t)
reaches the boundary of
Y_{\varepsilon}
. For
t<t^{*}
the solution lies in
Y_{\varepsilon}
, hence the previous estimate is valid on
[0,t^{*}]
.
Since
y(0)\in Y_{\varepsilon}
, it follows that
|e(0)|\leq m_{\varepsilon}
, and the corridor condition (
18
) gives

\frac{\bar{a}M_{\varepsilon}+\bar{b}}{2\sqrt{ab}}<m_{\varepsilon}.

Therefore from (
21
) it follows that
|e(t^{*})|<m_{\varepsilon}
, i.e.
y(t^{*})
remains inside
Y_{\varepsilon}
. The resulting contradiction shows that
y(t)\in Y_{\varepsilon}
for all
t\geq 0
.
Finally, from the relation
x=\dfrac{y}{1+y}
and the choice of
Y_{\varepsilon}
it follows that
x(t)\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
.
∎

Corollary 2

(Explicit “corridor” inequality)
.

Condition (
18
) can be rewritten in terms of
\varepsilon
and
(a,b)
:

m_{\varepsilon}=\min\!\Big\{\sqrt{\tfrac{b}{a}}-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon},\ \tfrac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}-\sqrt{\tfrac{b}{a}}\Big\},\quad M_{\varepsilon}=\Big(\tfrac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\Big)^{\!2},

and the “narrowness” of the corridor is given by the requirement

\sqrt{ab}\;>\;\frac{\bar{a}\,\big(\tfrac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\big)^{2}+\bar{b}}{2\,m_{\varepsilon}\,}.

The smaller
\sqrt{ab}
(that is, the weaker the mutual cooperation), the narrower the admissible range of perturbations
(\bar{a},\bar{b})
for preserving the buffer
x\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
.

Proof.

Substituting

\underline{y}_{\varepsilon}=\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon},\ \overline{y}_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}

into the definitions of
m_{\varepsilon}
and
M_{\varepsilon}
in Theorem
4
yields the expressions indicated in the statement.
Plugging them into (
18
) gives the claimed explicit inequality.
∎

5
Exponential Absolute Growth under Mutual Cooperation

The model also allows evaluation of the emergent effect that arises in the system under mutual cooperation.

Theorem 5

(Exponential cooperation premium)
.

Consider the original linear Osipov–Lanchester system

\dot{R}=-\beta B,\qquad\dot{B}=-\alpha R,\quad z(t)=\begin{pmatrix}R(t)\\
B(t)\end{pmatrix},\quad S=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\beta\\
-\alpha&0\end{pmatrix}.

Then the following holds.

1.

(Explicit solution)
The solution satisfies
z(t)=e^{tS}z(0)
, and
S^{2}=\alpha\beta\,I
, hence

e^{tS}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\cosh\!\big(\sqrt{\alpha\beta}\,t\big)\,I+\dfrac{\sinh\!\big(\sqrt{\alpha\beta}\,t\big)}{\sqrt{\alpha\beta}}\,S,&\alpha\beta>0,\\[5.69054pt]
I+tS,&\alpha\beta=0.\end{cases}

2.

(Exponential growth/decay)
For
\alpha\beta>0

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\|z(t)\|=\sqrt{\alpha\beta},

that is, the magnitude of the solution grows/decays with exponent
\sqrt{\alpha\beta}
.

3.

(Boundary case)
If
\alpha=0
or
\beta=0
, then
S^{2}=0
and
e^{tS}=I+tS
, whence the solution grows at most linearly.

4.

(Comparison)
If
(\alpha,\beta)
and
(\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta})
are such that
\alpha\beta>0
and
\tilde{\alpha}\tilde{\beta}=0
, then for any initial conditions
z(0)\neq 0

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\frac{\|z_{\alpha,\beta}(t)\|}{\|z_{\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta}}(t)\|}=\sqrt{\alpha\beta}>0,

so the cooperative case is asymptotically “stronger” than the linear one.

Proof.

The matrix
S
has the form

S=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\beta\\
-\alpha&0\end{pmatrix}

and it is easy to check that

S^{2}=\alpha\beta\,I.

Then the power series for the exponential splits into even and odd parts:

e^{tS}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}S^{2k}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}S^{2k+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha\beta)^{k}t^{2k}}{(2k)!}I+\frac{S}{\sqrt{\alpha\beta}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha\beta)^{k}t^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}.

For
\alpha\beta>0
these are precisely the formulas for
\cosh
and
\sinh
, yielding item (1). For
\alpha\beta=0
only the first two terms remain, giving
e^{tS}=I+tS
.

For item (2), note that when
\alpha\beta>0
the eigenvalues of
S
are
\pm\sqrt{\alpha\beta}
, so the solution decomposes over eigenvectors and behaves as
\exp(\pm\sqrt{\alpha\beta}\,t)
in modulus, which yields the indicated growth rate.

Items (3) and (4) follow directly from the explicit formula and the comparison of exponential and linear growth.
∎

The possibility of exponential solutions is retrospectively explained by technological progress, which for several centuries has ensured exponential growth of resources.

6
Conclusion

A closed extension of the Osipov–Lanchester model to all parameter values
\alpha,\beta
with a constant total population
N
is presented that describes both confrontational and cooperative regimes. A simple reduction to the Riccati equation provides a complete analytical study and a rigorous classification of stable invariant sets. This is a qualitative minimal model; empirical calibration is outside the scope of this work.

The political-economic terminology used throughout follows Acemoglu’s “Narrow Corridor” as an interpretive framework and is intended as
a descriptive mapping of mathematically defined regimes, not as a normative or empirical claim.
If
R
is identified as the “strength of the state” and
B
as the “strength of society”, then for
\alpha\leq 0,\,\beta\leq 0
the magnitudes
|\alpha|
and
|\beta|
can be interpreted as the state’s “inclusiveness” and society’s “patriotism”, respectively, in Acemoglu’s terminology
[
16
]
.
Then the three stable regimes of Theorem
2
are interpreted as:

1.

\alpha<0,\beta<0
— stable balance (“democracy”);

2.

\alpha=0,\beta<0
— the stable edge
B=0
(“autocracy”);

3.

\alpha<0,\beta=0
— the stable edge
R=0
(“anarchy”).

The term ‘autocracy’ is used as a broad label for state-dominant regimes, which includes the ‘despotic Leviathan’ in Acemoglu and Robinson’s terminology. The term ’democracy’ refers to any form of stable cooperation between the state and society. This is consistent with Acemoglu’s intuition of the “narrow corridor”
[
1
]
and illustrates the absence of an “end of history” in the sense of Fukuyama
[
17
]
. A practically important consequence is that stable balance is possible only under mutually cooperative interaction (
\alpha,\beta<0
in the chosen parameterization); extreme regimes become attractors when one of the channels degenerates, and confrontation leads beyond the model’s applicability (Figure
1
).

Furthermore, when passing to the system perturbed in
\alpha(t),\beta(t)
, buffer dynamics (Corollary
1
) repelling from the axes are required to preserve stable solutions, which can be interpreted as a formal analogue of Acemoglu’s intuitive “narrowness” of the stability corridor. This underscores the institutional importance of power rotation mechanisms and mutual constraints as safeguards of cooperation.

It is also shown by Theorem
5
that for
\alpha<0,\beta<0
the solution of the system in absolute population values exhibits exponential growth, whereas the other two solutions grow only polynomially. However, this is possible only under exponential growth of available resources. This may explain both the practical advantages of remaining in the corridor when resources are abundant and the strong connection between “democracy” and technological progress; when the benefits weaken, the incentive to remain in the corridor diminishes.
