On density analogs of Hindman’s finite sums theorem
By Felipe Hernández and Ioannis Kousek and Tristán Radić

Abstract
For any set A A of natural numbers with positive upper Banach density, we show the existence of an infinite set B B and sequences ( t k ) k ∈ ℕ , ( s k ) k ∈ ℕ (t_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}},(s_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}} of natural numbers such that { ∑ n ∈ F n : F ⊂ B + s k , 1 ≤ | F | ≤ k } ⊂ A − t k \left\{\sum_{n\in F}n:F\subset B+s_{k},1\leq|F|\leq k\right\}\subset A-t_{k} , for every k ∈ ℕ k\in\mathbb{N} . This strengthens the density finite sums theorem of Kra, Moreira, Richter, and Robertson. We further show, given such a set A A , the existence of an infinite set B B and a sequence ( t k ) k ∈ ℕ (t_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}} of natural numbers such that { ∑ n ∈ F n : F ⊂ B , | F | = k } ⊂ A − t k \left\{\sum_{n\in F}n:F\subset B,|F|=k\right\}\subset A-t_{k} , for every k ∈ ℕ k\in\mathbb{N} . As a corollary, we obtain a sequence ( B n ) n ∈ ℕ (B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}} of infinite sets of natural numbers such that B 1 + ⋯ + B k ⊂ A B_{1}+\cdots+B_{k}\subset A , for every k ∈ ℕ k\in\mathbb{N} . We also establish the optimality of our main theorems by providing counterexamples to potential further generalizations, and thereby addressing questions of the aforementioned authors in the context of density analogs to Hindman’s finite sums theorem.



1.  Introduction

1.1.  Main results

In 1974, Hindman
[
14
]
proved the celebrated finite sums theorem, showing that for any finite partition of the natural numbers,
\mathbb{N}=C_{1}\cup C_{2}\cup\cdots\cup C_{k}
, there exists an infinite set
B\subset\mathbb{N}
such that

\Big\{\sum_{n\in F}n\colon F\subset B,\ 1\leq|F|<\infty\Big\}\subset C_{i},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for some }i=1,\ldots,k.

(1)

Erdős
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
was interested in
extending Hindman’s result to a density analog, namely by finding infinite
sumset patterns similar to those in (
1
), in any set
A\subset\mathbb{N}
whose upper (Banach) density,

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{*}(A)=\limsup_{N-M\to\infty}\frac{|A\cap\{M,M+1,\ldots,N-1\}|}{N-M},

is positive. Due to parity obstructions – consider, for instance, the odd numbers – shifting the finite sumsets in (
1
) is necessary. An example of Straus
[
13
, Theorem
11.6
]
showing that a single shift of these patterns cannot always be found in sets of positive density, rendered unclear what could potentially be a density analog of Hindman’s theorem.

A series of breakthroughs in the last decade
[
5
,
15
,
22
,
20
,
23
,
24
]
revealed that infinite sumset patterns of increasing complexities can be found in sets of positive upper Banach density. These results
verified conjectures predicted by Erdős and gave rise to further natural questions and conjectures (most of which were formulated in
[
21
]
). In particular, using structure theory of multiple ergodic averages, Kra, Moreira, Richter and Roberson proved the following truncated density version of Hindman’s theorem.

Theorem KMRR

(
[
23
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{N}
be a set with
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{*}(A)>0
. Then, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists an infinite set
B_{k}\subset\mathbb{N}
and an integer
t_{k}\geq 0
such that

\bigg\{\sum_{n\in F}n\colon\ F\subset B_{k}\text{ with }1\leq|F|\leq k\bigg\}\subset A-t_{k}.

Again, Straus’s example shows that one
cannot choose the set
B_{k}
and the shift
t_{k}
independently of
k
.
However, the same authors asked if a sequence of shifts to the dense sets would allow for an unbounded number of summands generated by a fixed infinite set.

Question 1.1

(
[
21
, Question 2.11]
)
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{N}
be a set with
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{*}(A)>0
. Does there exist an infinite set
B\subset\mathbb{N}
and a sequence of shifts
(t_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{N}
such that

\bigg\{\sum_{n\in F}n\colon\ F\subset B\text{ with }1\leq|F|\leq k\bigg\}\subset A-t_{k},\ \text{for every}\ k\in\mathbb{N}\ ?

We answer Question
1.1

in the negative (see
2.2
) by constructing a set of density
arbitrarily close to
1
for which even a weaker version of the conclusion
fails. However, our main results, Theorems
A
and
B
, extend

Theorem KMRR

(
[
23
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.
in two different directions, achieving in each case a certain degree of independence between the infinite set
B
and the number of summands
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Theorem A
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{N}
be a set with
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{*}(A)>0
and
\ell\geq 0
an integer. Then, there exist an infinite set
B_{\ell}\subset\mathbb{N}
and a sequence
(t_{\ell,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{N}
, such that

\bigg\{\sum_{n\in F}n\colon\ F\subset B_{\ell}\text{ with }k\leq|F|\leq k+\ell\bigg\}\subset A-t_{\ell,k},\ \text{for every}\ k\in\mathbb{N}.

In Section
2
, we show that

A
is optimal in the sense that one cannot choose the set

B_{\ell}
independently of
\ell
.
We also show that the sequence of shifts
(t_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
cannot, in general, be bounded. Moreover, by the proof of
A
, we can choose the sequence
(t_{\ell,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
to be increasing.

Theorem B
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{N}
be a set with
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{*}(A)>0
. Then, there exist an infinite set
B
and sequences
(t_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}},(s_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{N}
such that

\bigg\{\sum_{n\in F}n\colon\ F\subset B+s_{k}\text{ with }1\leq|F|\leq k\bigg\}\subset A-t_{k},\ \text{for every}\ k\in\mathbb{N}.

Letting
B_{k}=B+s_{k}
, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we see that the strength of this result over
Theorem KMRR

(
[
23
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.
is that the sets
(B_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are shifts of a single infinite set
B\subset\mathbb{N}
. Our construction in Section
2
shows that one cannot in general remove the shift sequences
(t_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}},(s_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, as well as the necessity of them being unbounded. The second auxiliary sequence in
B
, as opposed to merely shifting the dense set, is naturally introduced to overcome the further local obstructions that arise in answering
1.1
. These results are the first to guarantee sumsets
with arbitrarily large numbers of summands generated by
fixed infinite sets, contained in sets of positive density. Furthermore, we prove a combination of the conclusions of Theorems
A

and
B
(see
6.8
for more details).

The main result of
[
22
]
shows that for any

k\in\mathbb{N}
, any set of positive density contains a sumset of the form

B_{1}+\cdots+B_{k}
, where
B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}
are infinite. As a corollary of our
main theorems, we strengthen this by removing the dependence of the infinite sets on
k
. This
positively answers
[
22
, Question 8.1]
and
verifies
[
21
, Conjecture 2.10]
.

Corollary C
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{N}
be a set with
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{*}(A)>0
. Then, there exists an infinite sequence
(B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of infinite sets of natural numbers such that

B_{1}+B_{2}+\cdots+B_{k}\subset A,\ \text{for every}\ k\in\mathbb{N}.

We remark that
A
for
\ell=0
already implies

C
and the details can be found in

Section

3.2
. Similarly,
B

also implies
C
.

1.2.  Dynamical reformulation and key ingredients

In this section, we summarize our approach to prove
A
and
B
, highlighting the new tools developed to address them. We make use of notation and terminology that is later defined in
Section

1.3
and
Section

3.1
.

Inspired by the aforementioned literature
[
12
,
15
,
20
,
22
,
23
]
, after using Furstenberg’s correspondence principle, we reinterpret the combinatorial statements into an ergodic language.
Concerning
A
, the dynamical result that implies it is the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic measure preserving system with a point
a\in X
such that
\mu\left(\overline{\{T^{n}a:n\in\mathbb{N}\}}\right)=1
. Let also
\ell\geq 0
be fixed and
E\subset X
be an open set with
\mu(E)>0
. Then, there exist an infinite set
B\subset\mathbb{N}
and sequence
(t_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{N}
such that

T^{\left(t_{k}+b_{1}+\cdots+b_{i}\right)}a\in E,

(2)

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, all
k\leq i\leq k+\ell
, and
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{i}
distinct elements in
B
.

To prove a similar dynamical statement, the authors of
[
23
]
introduce a family of measures on finite product spaces, which they call
progressive measures
(see
4.5
). The positivity of finite cartesian products of open sets with respect to such measures is used to recover
Theorem KMRR

(
[
23
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.
. However, since we are looking for sumset patterns with an unbounded number of summands, we need to deal with infinite cartesian products of open sets and in general, these sets have zero measure with respect to any useful measure.

We then first adapt the definition of
progressive measures to measures on
X^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and build one, denoted by
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}(X^{\mathbb{N}_{0}})
, by lifting a natural measure
\xi
on
Z^{\mathbb{N}}_{\infty}
, where
(Z_{\infty},m,T)
is the infinite-step pronilfactor. This is a natural approach, and it turns out that the marginals
of this measure
\sigma
in the first
k+1
coordinates do not necessarily coincide with the measures constructed in
[
23
]
by lifting measures from the
k
-step pronilfactor
Z_{k-1}
(see
Appendix

A
for relevant examples).
One can actually recover the proof of
Theorem KMRR

(
[
23
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.
by considering
\sigma
lifted from
Z_{\infty}
, but for this result the original measures of
[
23
]
give an optimal control.

Another obstacle is that we cannot obtain our main results from statements about convergence of orbits in dynamical systems. In previous works, the introduction of Erdős progressions (see
[
23
, Definition 2.1]
) was critical for obtaining sumset-type configurations; see, for instance,
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
18
,
19
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
. In contrast, the use of Erdős progressions could only lead to weaker versions of Theorems
A
and
B
, in particular versions with nested infinite sets, as we explain in
2.8
. Instead, expanding on the ideas already present in
[
12
]
, we construct the sumsets inductively, using properties of the measure
\sigma
.

For the following discussion and to illustrate the properties of
\sigma
, fix
k=4
, and denote by
\sigma_{4}
the projection on the first
5
coordinates. We use that the first marginal of
\sigma
is the Dirac mass of the predetermined point
a\in X
in
1.2
and the following:

1.

An
initialization property
; for an open set
E\subset X
, if
\mu(E)>0
, then

\sigma_{4}(X\times T^{-t}E\times T^{-t}E\times T^{-t}E\times T^{-t}E)>0,

for some
t\in\mathbb{N}
(see
5.1
).

2.

Left-progressiveness
(see
4.5
); if
U_{1},U_{2},U_{3}\subset X
are open sets and

\sigma_{4}(X\times U_{1}\times U_{2}\times U_{3}\times X)>0,
then

\sigma_{4}(T^{-n}U_{1}\times(U_{1}\cap T^{-n}U_{2})\times(U_{2}\cap T^{-n}U_{3})\times U_{3}\times X)>0,

for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

3.

Right-progressiveness
(see
4.5
); if
U_{1},U_{2},U_{3}\subset X
are open sets and

\sigma_{4}(X\times U_{1}\times U_{2}\times U_{3}\times X)>0,
then

\sigma_{4}(X\times U_{1}\times(U_{2}\cap T^{-n}U_{1})\times(U_{3}\cap T^{-n}U_{2})\cap T^{-n}U_{3})>0,

for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

The first two properties were already exploited in
[
23
]
, where
the second was called
progressiveness
. This notion is not sufficient
for our purposes, and we refer to it as left-progressiveness, because we
need to exploit its symmetrical version described in
3
.
It is this last property that enables us to enlarge the distance to the first
coordinate, which ultimately allows us to increase the number of summands in the
sumsets of
1.2
.

To put things into perspective, for an open set
E\subset X
with
\sigma_{4}(X\times X\times E\times X\times X)>0
,
a first application of left-progressiveness allows us to find
b_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\sigma_{4}(X\times T^{-b_{1}}E\times E\times X\times X)>0,

(3)

and with a second application of the same property, we find
b_{2}>b_{1}
such that

\sigma_{4}(T^{-(b_{1}+b_{2})}E\times\left(T^{-b_{1}}E\cap T^{-b_{2}}E\right)\times E\times X\times X)>0.

Since the first marginal of
\sigma
is
\delta_{a}
, we get that
T^{b_{1}+b_{2}}a\in E
. Continuing this process, we get a sumset
\{b_{i}+b_{j}:b_{i},b_{j}\in B\text{ distinct}\}
inside the set of return times
\{n\in\mathbb{N}:T^{n}a\in E\}
of
a
to
E
. However, if we apply right-progressiveness beforehand in (
3
), we find
t\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\sigma_{4}(X\times T^{-b_{1}}E\times(E\cap T^{-t-b_{1}}E)\times T^{-t}E\times X)>0.

In particular,
\sigma_{4}(X\times X\times T^{-b_{1}}\tilde{E}\times\tilde{E}\times X)>0
where
\tilde{E}=T^{-t}E
and
b_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
is the same as in (
3
). Therefore applying left-progressiveness multiple times as before, we find that not only
\{b_{i}+b_{j}:b_{i},b_{j}\in B\text{ distinct}\}
is in the set
\{n\in\mathbb{N}:T^{n}a\in E\}
, but also
t+\{b_{i}+b_{j}+b_{m}:b_{i},b_{j},b_{m}\in B\text{ distinct}\}
.

The previous discussion gives a sense of how to obtain
1.2
(and therefore
A
) from the initialization property, left-progressiveness, and right-progressiveness. Nevertheless, when it comes to
B
, this method is insufficient. The dynamical reformulation of this theorem is the following.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic measure preserving system with a point
a\in X
such that
\mu\left(\overline{\{T^{n}a:n\in\mathbb{N}\}}\right)=1
. Let also
E\subset X
be an open set with
\mu(E)>0
. Then, there exist an infinite set
B\subset\mathbb{N}
and sequences
(s_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}},(t_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{N}
such that

T^{\left(t_{k}+is_{k}+b_{1}+\cdots+b_{i}\right)}a\in E,

(4)

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, all
1\leq i\leq k
, and
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{i}
distinct elements in
B
.

In order to prove
1.3
we need to use an additional
property of our measure
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}(X^{\mathbb{N}_{0}})

which we term
multiple
right-progressiveness
. This notion is derived from appropriated double ergodic averages. To prove it, we first show an analog of Furstenberg’s multiple recurrence theorem and then that for these related ergodic averages, the infinite-step pronilfactor is characteristic; the details can be found in
Section

6
.

We refer to
6.4
for more details, but when, for instance,
k=4
, multiple right-progressiveness implies that for open sets
U_{1},U_{2}\subset X
, if

\sigma_{4}(X\times U_{1}\times U_{2}\times X\times X)>0,

then there are
t,s\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\sigma_{4}(X\times U_{1}\times(U_{2}\cap T^{-(t+s)}U_{1})\times(T^{-(t+s)}U_{2}\cap T^{-(t+2s)}U_{1})\times(T^{-(t+2s)}U_{2}\cap T^{-(t+3s)}U_{1}))>0.

This property recovers both right-progressiveness and the initialization property (see
Remark

6.5
). Multiple right-progressiveness not only serves to translate sets to the right, but it also allows us to consider an increasing number of patterns with increasing length, by paying the cost of adding an extra shift
s\in\mathbb{N}
, which corresponds to the second shift appearing in
B
.

1.3.  Notation

By
\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{R}
we denote, respectively, the integers,
nonnegative integers, positive integers (natural numbers), and the reals.

Given a measure space
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)
, a measurable space

(Y,\mathcal{C})
and a measurable function
F:X\to Y
, we denote by

F\mu
the push-forward of
\mu
under
F
. That is,
F\mu(C)=\mu(F^{-1}C)
,
for any
C\in\mathcal{C}
. Moreover, given a measurable transformation
T:X\to X
and some integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we let
T^{n}
denote the composition
T\circ T\circ\cdots\circ T
taken
n
-times and we sometimes write
T^{n}f
instead of
f\circ T^{n}
, where
f:X\to\mathbb{C}
is a measurable map. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
we write
\tilde{T}
for the map
T\times T^{2}\times\cdots\times T^{k}
, and
T_{\Delta}
for the map
T^{\otimes k}=T\times T\times\cdots\times T
, where the product is taken
k
-times, omitting the dependence on
k
which is clear from the context.

If
S=\emptyset
we let
T^{-S}E=X
. Also, if
S\subset\mathbb{N}
is nonempty and
E\subset X
, we denote

T^{-S}E=\bigcap_{n\in S}T^{-n}E.

In addition, for a finite set

F\subset\mathbb{N}
we define
F^{\oplus 0}=\{0\}
, and for
i\in\mathbb{N}

F^{\oplus i}=\begin{cases}\{f_{1}+\cdots+f_{i}:f_{1},\ldots,f_{i}\in F\text{ distinct}\}&\text{ if }i\leq|F|\\
\emptyset&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}.

Notice that, under this convention,
\emptyset^{\oplus 0}=\{0\}
. The motivation for introducing such notation is that if
A\subset\mathbb{N}
,
a\in X
and
E\subset X
are as in
3.1
below, then for any nonempty set
S\subset\mathbb{N}
,
a\in T^{-S}E
if and only if
S\subset A
.

2.  Negative results and optimality of the main theorems

In
[
21
]
, several possible results
serving the purpose of a potential analog to Hindman’s finite sums theorem
were conjectured or asked. We begin with a weaker version of
1.1
stated in the introduction.

Question 2.1

(
[
21
, Question 2.12]
)
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{N}
with
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{*}(A)>0
. Does there exist a nested sequence of infinite sets
B_{1}\supset B_{2}\supset B_{3}\supset\dots
, where
B_{k+1}
is cofinite in
B_{k}
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and a sequence of shifts
(t_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
it holds that

\bigg\{\sum_{n\in F}n:F\subset B_{k}\ \text{with}\ 1\leq|F|\leq k\bigg\}\subset A-t_{k}\ ?

Note that a positive answer to
1.1
would clearly
imply a positive answer to
2.1
. But one could – ostensibly – weaken these statements even further and in the setting of
2.1
simply ask for a nested sequence of infinite sets
B_{1}\supset B_{2}\supset B_{3}\supset\dots
, without demanding they be cofinite in
B_{1}
. We remark that in fact this last statement is equivalent to that of
2.1
and a proof of this equivalence is included in
2.5
below. The next result, which follows by a modification of Straus’s example mentioned in the introduction, disproves this statement.

Proposition 2.2
.

Given any
\epsilon>0
there exists a set
A\subset\mathbb{N}
with
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{*}(A)>1-\epsilon
such that for any sequence
(k_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, for any nested sequence
B_{1}\supset B_{2}\supset\ldots
, of infinite sets, and any sequence
(t_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, it does not hold that

\bigg\{\sum_{n\in F}n:F\subset B_{i}\ \text{with}\ k_{i}\leq|F|\leq k_{i}+i\bigg\}\subset A-t_{i},\ \text{for every}\ i\in\mathbb{N}.

Before proving
2.2
, we list some
consequences.

Remark 2.3
.

The set
A\subset\mathbb{N}
provided by
2.2
is such that:

1.

Taking
k_{i}=1
for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
, then for any nested sequence
B_{1}\supset B_{2}\supset B_{3}\supset\dots
, of infinite sets and any sequence of shifts
(t_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, it does not hold that

\bigg\{\sum_{n\in F}n:F\subset B_{k}\ \text{with}\ 1\leq|F|\leq k\bigg\}\subset A-t_{k},\ \text{for every}\ k\in\mathbb{N}.

In view of the discussion prior to the statement of
2.2
, this answers Questions
1.1
and
2.1
. Note that it also shows the necessity of the auxiliary shift-sequence

(s_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in the statement of
B
.

2.

If we let
k_{i}=i
for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
, then for any infinite set
B\subset\mathbb{N}
and any sequence
(t_{k,\ell})_{k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
it does not hold that

\bigg\{\sum_{n\in F}n:F\subset B\ \text{with}\ k\leq|F|\leq k+\ell\bigg\}\subset A-t_{k,\ell},

for each
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. This shows that
A
cannot be
improved in the sense of allowing the infinite set
B
to be independent of
\ell
.

Proof of
2.2
.

Let
\epsilon>0
be fixed and consider an increasing sequence of prime numbers
(p_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n/p_{n}<\epsilon.
Then, we define the set

A=\mathbb{N}\setminus\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\bigcup_{j=0}^{n-1}p_{n}\mathbb{N}+j\right).

(5)

By the choice of
(p_{n})
it readily follows that
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{*}(A)>1-\epsilon
. Now, assume there exists an infinite sequence of sets
B_{1}\supset B_{2}\supset B_{3}\supset\dots
and a sequence of shifts
(t_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
such that for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
it holds that

\bigg\{\sum_{n\in F}n:F\subset B_{i}\ \text{with}\ k_{i}\leq|F|\leq k_{i}+i\bigg\}\subset A-t_{i}.

Notice that, if
r>i
, the fact that
B_{r}\subset B_{i}
implies

\bigg\{\sum_{n\in F}n:F\subset B_{r}\ \text{with}\ k_{i}\leq|F|\leq k_{i}+i\bigg\}\subset A-t_{i}.

Fix
i,m,n,r\in\mathbb{N}
with
t_{i}<n<p_{n}<r<m
and
i<r<m
so that
B_{m}\subset B_{r}\subset B_{i}
.

Assume first that there is an infinite set
\tilde{B}_{m}\subset B_{m}\cap p_{n}\mathbb{N}
. In particular,

\bigg\{\sum_{n\in F}n:F\subset\tilde{B}_{m}\ \text{with}\ |F|=k_{i}\bigg\}\subset p_{n}\mathbb{N}.

Then, by assumption and the above
discussion, it must be the case that

\bigg\{\sum_{n\in F}n:F\subset\tilde{B}_{m}\ \text{with}\ |F|=k_{i}\bigg\}+t_{i}\subset A.

However,
t_{i}<n
and thus

\bigg\{\sum_{n\in F}n:F\subset\tilde{B}_{m}\ \text{with}\ |F|=k_{i}\bigg\}+t_{i}\subset\bigcup_{j=0}^{n-1}p_{n}\mathbb{N}+j\subset\mathbb{N}\setminus A,

which is a contradiction.

Therefore, by the pigeonhole principle, we may assume there exists
c_{n}\in\{1,\ldots,p_{n}-1\}
and an infinite set
\tilde{B}_{m}\subset B_{m}\cap(p_{n}\mathbb{N}+c_{n})
. As before, the preceding discussion implies that

\bigg\{\sum_{n\in F}n:F\subset\tilde{B}_{m}\ \text{with}\ k_{r}\leq|F|\leq k_{r}+r\bigg\}\subset A-t_{r}.

Now,
p_{n}
is a prime and

, hence

is a generator in
\mathbb{Z}_{p_{n}}
. As

this means that there exists some

such that

We have thus established that

\bigg\{\sum_{n\in F}n:F\subset\tilde{B}_{m}\ \text{with}\ |F|=d\bigg\}+t_{r}\subset A\cap\left(p_{n}\mathbb{N}+dc_{n}+t_{r}\right)\subset A\cap p_{n}\mathbb{N}=\emptyset,

which is again a contradiction.
∎

Proposition 2.4
.

There exists a set

with

such that the statement of

fails if either of the sequences

or

is bounded.

Remark
.

The analogous is true for the statement of

Proof.

Take

To see that

Remark 2.6
.

The same proof can be performed for other types of sumsets results. In the present work, our “positive” results are proven in non-nested versions, that is, the strongest possible. We do not know, however, if

We finish this section by showing that infinite analogs of Erdős progressions (see

Definition 2.7

.

Let

∎

3.  Dynamical interpretation

3.1.  Classical terminology

If
X
is a compact metric space and
T:X\to X
is a homeomorphism, we call

(X,T)
a
topological system
. In this context, if
\mu
is a Borel
probability measure that is invariant under
T
, i.e.,
T\mu=\mu
,
we call
(X,\mu,T)
a
measure preserving system
or a

dynamical system
or simply a
system
. A system is

ergodic
if
T^{-1}A=A
implies that
\mu(A)\in\{0,1\},
for any Borel set
A\subset X
.

A Følner sequence
\Phi=(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{N}
is a sequence of nonempty finite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
which are asymptotically invariant under any shift, that is,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{|\Phi_{N}\cap(\Phi_{N}+t)|}{|\Phi_{N}|}=1,

for every
t\in\mathbb{N}
. Observe that a set
A\subset\mathbb{N}
has positive upper Banach density if and only if there is a Følner sequence
\Phi=(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{|A\cap\Phi_{N}|}{|\Phi_{N}|}>0.

Given a measure preserving system
(X,\mu,T)
, and a Følner sequence

\Phi=(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{N}
, we say that a point
a\in X
is generic
for
\mu
along the Følner sequence
\Phi
, and we write
a\in\gen(\mu,\Phi)
, if

\mu=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\delta_{T^{n}a},

where the limit is in the weak* topology and by
\delta_{x}
we denote the Dirac mass at
x\in X
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
and
(Y,\nu,S)
be measure preserving systems. We say that
(Y,\nu,S)
(or simply
Y
) is a
factor
of
(X,\mu,T)
(or simply
X
) if there exists a measurable map
\pi\colon X\to Y
, called a
factor map
, such that
\pi\mu=\nu
and
\pi\circ T=S\circ\pi
, up to null-sets. In this case
X
is called an
extension
of
Y
.
If, in addition, the function
\pi\colon X\to Y
is continuous and
surjective, then it is called a
continuous factor map
. In an abuse of notation we often use the same letter for the transformation of a factor and its extension, that is, we write
(Y,\nu,T)
. Given a factor map
\pi:X\to Y
and a function
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
,
we let
\mathbb{E}(f|Y)
denote the conditional expectation

\mathbb{E}(f|\pi^{-1}\mathcal{B}(Y))
, where
\mathcal{B}(Y)
is the Borel

\sigma
-algebra on
Y
.

Let
(X_{j},\mu_{j},T_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of measure preserving systems endowed with factor maps
\pi_{j,j+1}:X_{j+1}\to X_{j}
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
. An
inverse limit
of this sequence of systems is the unique system
(X,\mu,T)
(up to isomorphism) endowed with the factor maps
\pi_{j}:X\to X_{j}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(i)

\pi_{j+1}=\pi_{j,j+1}\circ\pi_{j}
, for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
and

(ii)

For
1\leq p<\infty
,
\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\{f\circ\pi_{i}:f\in L^{p}(\mu_{i})\}
is dense in
L^{p}(\mu)
.

A similar definition applies for topological dynamical systems (replacing factor maps by continuous factor maps and integrable functions by continuous ones). For existence and alternative definitions of inverse limits we refer the reader to
[
17
, Chapter 6.2]
.

3.2.  Translation to dynamics

The proofs of our main combinatorial

begin by translating them to statements in ergodic theory. A fundamental step in order to facilitate

]
.
For a proof of this version, we refer the reader to

Proposition 3.1

We show the deduction of our main combinatorial results, Theorems

The proof that Theorem

Proof that

Let

4.  Progressive measures and infinite-step nilsystems

4.1.  Pronilfactors

As in
[
22
]
and
[
23
]
,
(also
[
15
]
and

[
20
]
for specific use of the Kronecker factor)
we use measures on pronilfactors to generate the infinite sumsets.
A
k
-step nilsystem
(X,\mu,T)=(G/\Gamma,\mu,T)
is a dynamical system given by a
k
-step nilpotent Lie group
G
, with
\Gamma
being a co-compact discrete subgroup,
T\colon X\to X
denoting the left translation by a fixed element of
G
and
\mu
being the Haar measure of
X
. For the
k
-step nilsystem
(X,\mu,T)
the properties of ergodicity, minimality, transitivity and unique ergodicity are equivalent. Moreover, if
x\in X
is any point, the orbit closure
\overline{\{T^{n}x\colon n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}
is a minimal and uniquely ergodic
k
-step subnilsystem. An inverse limit (in the measure theoretic sense) of
k
-step nilsystems is called a
k
-step pronilsystem.
All the above properties are preserved under inverse limits of
k
-step nilsystems, so they also hold for
k
-step pronilsystems. For these classical facts and other details we refer the reader to
[
17
, Chapter 11 and Chapter 13]
.

Definition 4.1
.

A system
(Z,m,T)
is called an
infinite-step pronilsystem
if it is
an inverse limit of a sequence
\left((Z_{k},m_{k},T)\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, where each
(Z_{k},m_{k},T)
is a
k
-step pronilsystem.

Infinite-step pronilsystems share many properties with finite-step pronilsystems; some of those, which are exploited in this paper, are listed in the end of this subsection.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic measure preserving system. Then, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
(X,\mu,T)
has a maximal factor isomorphic to a
k
-step pronilsystem, called the
k
-step pronilfactor
(see
[
17
, Chapter 16]
). We shall denote this factor by
(Z_{k},m_{k},T)
. The inverse limit of
(Z_{k},m_{k},T)
is the
infinite-step pronilfactor of

X
and we denote it by
(Z_{\infty},m,T)
. The infinite-step pronilfactor of
X
coincides with the maximal factor of
X
isomorphic to an infinite-step pronilsystem. In their seminal paper
[
16
]
, Host and Kra showed that it is possible to describe these factors by studying uniformity seminorms, classically referred to as Gowers-Host-Kra seminorms.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic system and
f\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
. The
k
-uniformity seminorms of
f
,
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{U^{k}(X,\mu,T)}
, are defined inductively as follows:

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{U^{0}(X,\mu,T)}=\int fd\mu

and

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{U^{k+1}(X,\mu,T)}^{2^{k+1}}=\lim_{H\to\infty}\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h\leq H}\left\lVert f\cdot\overline{T^{h}f}\right\rVert_{U^{k}(X,\mu,T)}^{2^{k}}.

(8)

Remark
.

The function
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{U^{k}(X,\mu,T)}
defines a seminorm when
k\geq 1
. The fact that the limit in (
8
) exists is proved in
[
16
]
by iterative applications of the mean ergodic theorem. In the same paper, it is also shown that
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{U^{k+1}(X,\mu,T)}
is actually a norm for
k
-step pronilsystems.

The main theorem in
[
16
]
shows that the
k
-step pronilfactor,
Z_{k}
, of an ergodic system
(X,\mu,T)
is characterized by the property

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{U^{k+1}(X,\mu,T)}=0\iff\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f\mid Z_{k})=0,

for any
f\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
. Similarly, for
Z_{\infty}
we have the following characterization.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic system and
(Z_{\infty},m,T)
denote its infinite-step pronilfactor. For any
f\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
we have that

\lVert f-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f\mid Z_{\infty})\rVert_{U^{k}(X,\mu,T)}=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }k\in\mathbb{N}.

Conversely, if
h\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
is such that
\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{U^{k}(X,\mu,T)}=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then
\mathbb{E}(h|Z_{\infty})=0
.

Proof.

By the definition of
Z_{\infty}
, we have
\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f\mid Z_{\infty})=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f\mid Z_{s})
in
L^{2}(\mu)
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
fixed,

\lVert f-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f\mid Z_{s})\rVert_{U^{k}(X,\mu,T)}\leq\lVert f-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f\mid Z_{s})\rVert_{U^{s+1}(X,\mu,T)}=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }s\geq k.

Thus
\displaystyle\lVert f-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f\mid Z_{\infty})\rVert_{U^{k}(X,\mu,T)}=\lim_{s\to\infty}\lVert f-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f\mid Z_{s})\rVert_{U^{k}(X,\mu,T)}=0
.

For the converse, assume
\mathbb{E}(h|Z_{\infty})\neq 0
in
L^{2}(\mu)
. Then, since
\mathbb{E}(h|Z_{\infty})=\lim_{s\to\infty}\mathbb{E}(h|Z_{s})
in
L^{2}(\mu)
, there must exist
k\in\mathbb{N}
so that
\mathbb{E}(h|Z_{k})\neq 0
in
L^{2}(\mu)
. However this contradicts the assumption that
\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{U^{k+1}(X,\mu,T)}=0
.
∎

A property that we use throughout the article is that of unique ergodicity of
orbit closures in infinite-step pronilsystems.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
(Z,m,T)
be an infinite-step pronilsystem,
d\geq 1
and
\Phi=(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
a Følner sequence in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Then, for every
k\geq 2
, any
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in Z
and any non-trivial
S_{i}=T^{a_{i,1}}\times T^{a_{i},2}\times\cdots\times T^{a_{i,k}}
for
i=1,\ldots,d
, the measure

\rho=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})\in\Phi_{N}}(S_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots S_{d}^{n_{d}})\delta_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})},

where the limit is in the weak* topology, is well-defined. Moreover
\rho
is independent from the Følner sequence
\Phi
.

Proof.

Let
Z_{s}
be the
s
-step pronilfactor of
Z
with factor map
\pi_{s}\colon Z\to Z_{s}
. It follows by
[
16
]
that
\pi_{k}\colon Z\to Z_{s}
is continuous. Let
\Omega_{s}
be the orbit closure of
\bm{(}\pi_{s}(x_{1}),\pi_{s}(x_{2}),\ldots,\pi_{s}(x_{k}))\in Z_{s}^{k}
under the
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-action given by the maps
S_{1},\ldots,S_{d}
. By properties of pronilsystems,
(\Omega_{s},S_{1},\ldots,S_{d})
is minimal and uniquely ergodic. If
\Omega_{\infty}
is the orbit closure of
\bm{(}x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})\in Z^{k}
under the
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-action given by the maps
S_{1},\ldots,S_{d}
, then
\Omega_{\infty}
is the inverse limit of
(\Omega_{s})_{s\in\mathbb{N}}
and therefore is minimal and uniquely ergodic which concludes the proposition.
∎

4.2.  Progressive measures

We now define the notion of progressiveness for measures on
finite (and infinite) product spaces. This property can be
viewed as a variation of recurrence. Later, we introduce a more general property, which can analogously be viewed
as variation of multiple recurrence. These properties are the key ingredients that allow us to prove our main dynamical theorems.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
(X,T)
be a topological system and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We say that a Borel probability measure
\tau\in\mathcal{M}(X^{k+1})
is
left-progressive
if for all open sets
U_{1},\ldots,U_{k}\subset X
with

\tau(X\times U_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{k})>0,

there exist infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\tau((X\times U_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{k})\cap(T\times\cdots\times T)^{-n}(U_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{k}\times X))>0.

We say that
\tau
as above is
right-progressive
if for all open sets
U_{1},\ldots,U_{k-1}\subset X
with

\tau(X\times U_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{k-1}\times X)>0,

there exist infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\tau((X\times U_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{k-1}\times X)\cap(T\times\cdots\times T)^{-n}(X\times X\times U_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{k-1}))>0.

Finally, a measure is
progressive
if it is both left- and right-progressive.

For some measures on product spaces that we consider,
the first marginal has a distinguished role from the rest.
As a useful convention we index such measures starting
from the
0
-th coordinate.

Remark
.

In
[
23
, Definition 3.1]
, the authors introduced a notion of progressive measures that coincides with what we refer to as left-progressive.

We naturally extend this notion to measures
\tau\in\mathcal{M}(X^{\mathbb{N}_{0}})
.

Definition 4.6
.

Let
(X,T)
be a topological system. We say that a Borel probability measure
\tau\in\mathcal{M}(X^{\mathbb{N}_{0}})
is
progressive
if
P_{k}\tau
is progressive for all
k\geq 2
, where
P_{k}\colon X^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}\to X^{k+1}
is the projection onto the first
k+1
coordinates (including the
0
-th coordinate).

We now proceed to define a Borel measure
\sigma
on the infinite product
space
X^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, with base space coming from a given measure preserving
system
(X,\mu,T)
, which turn out to be progressive. In order to
achieve this we first define a measure
\xi
on
Z_{\infty}^{\mathbb{N}}
, where
(Z_{\infty},m,T)

is the infinite-step pronilfactor of
(X,\mu,T)
and then lift this measure
on
X^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. We remark that the definition of
\sigma
crucially depends
on a single predetermined point
a\in X
. However, the factor map
\pi:X\to Z_{\infty}
, which is used to lift
\xi
to
\sigma
, is a priori only measurable and thus defined almost everywhere (hence, potentially, not on
a
). Fortunately, we can assume that the factor map is actually continuous, a manoeuvre known among aficionados that was also exploited in
[
23
]
. This reduction is formally proven at the end of this section.

Definition 4.7
.

We say that
(X,\mu,T)
has a
topological infinite-step pronilfactor
if there is a continuous factor map
\pi_{\infty}:X\to Z_{\infty}
, where
(Z_{\infty},m,T)
denotes the infinite-step pronilfactor of
(X,\mu,T)
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic system with topological infinite-step
pronilfactor
(Z_{\infty},m,T)
. Let also
\Phi=(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
be
a Følner sequence in
\mathbb{N}
and
a\in\gen(\mu,\Phi)
. Similarly to
[
23
]
, our goal is to construct a measure
\xi\in\mathcal{M}(Z_{\infty}^{\mathbb{N}})
given by

\xi=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\delta_{T^{in}\pi_{\infty}(a)},

(9)

where the limit (assuming it exists) is in the weak* topology, then lift it and build a progressive measure
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}(X^{\mathbb{N}_{0}})
(see (
10
)). For that we first need to ensure that the limit in (
9
) is well-defined.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
(Z,m,T)
be an infinite-step pronilsystem,
a\in Z
and
\Phi=(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
a Følner sequence in
\mathbb{N}
. Then for every
k\geq 2
, the measure

\xi_{k}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\delta_{T^{n}a}\times\delta_{T^{2n}a}\times\cdots\times\delta_{T^{kn}a}

is well-defined. Moreover
\xi_{k}
is independent from the Følner sequence
\Phi
.

Proof.

This is a corollary of
4.4
with
d=1
and
S_{1}=T\times T^{2}\times\cdots\times T^{k}
.
∎

By a classical approximation argument we deduce that the limit in (
9
) exists.

Corollary 4.9
.

The measure given by (
9
) is well-defined.

Proof.

This follows at once from
Lemma

4.8
using the definition of weak* convergence, the observation that
P_{k}^{*}\xi_{n}=\xi_{k}
, for each
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
, with
k\leq n
, where
P_{k}^{*}
denotes the projection onto the first
k
coordinates, and the fact that
\{f_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes f_{k}:k\in\mathbb{N},f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in C(Z_{\infty})\}
is densely embedded in
C(Z_{\infty}^{\mathbb{N}})
.
∎

With that we can define the Borel probability measure
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}(X^{\mathbb{N}_{0}})
through the use of Kolmogorov’s extension theorem as follows.

Definition 4.10
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the measure
\sigma_{k}\in\mathcal{M}(X^{k+1})
via the formula

\int_{X^{k+1}}f_{0}\otimes f_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes f_{k}\ \mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma_{k}=f_{0}(a)\int_{Z^{k}_{\infty}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f_{1}\mid Z_{\infty})\otimes\cdots\otimes\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f_{k}\mid Z_{\infty})\ \mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}P_{k}^{*}\xi,

(10)

for all

, where
P_{k}^{*}
denotes the projection onto the coordinates
\{1,\ldots,k\}
(excluding the
0
-th coordinate). It follows by Stone-Weierstrass’ theorem that each

is well-defined via (

satisfies the compatibility condition of Kolmogorov’s extension theorem, so we denote by

the unique extension of this sequence.

Remark
.

By definition, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}

, where
P_{k}
denotes the projection onto the first
k+1
coordinates, including the
0
-th coordinate. It follows immediately from the definition that

and for each

and the

Theorem 6.1

18
) holds for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
and the proof is complete.
∎

It is possible to find a set
B\subset\mathbb{N}
that satisfies the statements of both
A
and
B
at the same time. As
Fig.

1
and
Fig.

2
show, the proofs of these theorems only differ in the iterative application of multiple right-progressiveness to find the shifts
t_{k},s_{k}
for
B
, and right-progressiveness to find the shift
t_{k}
for
A
. Thus, we can use both multiple-right progressiveness and right progressiveness, in addition to left-progressiveness, to have, in each iteration, three shifts
t_{k},t_{k}^{\prime}
and
s_{k}
such that

\displaystyle\sigma_{k+\ell+1}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\Bigg(\bigcap_{i=1}^{k+1}\prod_{j=0}^{k+\ell+1}\bigcap_{h=\max(j,i)}^{i+\ell}T^{-\left(t_{i}^{\prime}+B_{k}^{\oplus h-j}\right)}E\Bigg)\cap

\displaystyle\Bigg(\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\Bigg(\prod_{j=0}^{k+1}\bigcap_{\ell=\max(j,1)}^{i+1}T^{-\left(t_{i}+\ell s_{i}+B_{k}^{\oplus\ell-j}\right)}E\Bigg)\times X^{\ell}\Bigg)\Bigg]>0,

which would imply that the same infinite set
B\subset\mathbb{N}
satisfies the conclusions of
B
and
A
simultaneously. Thus, we obtain the following result.

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{N}
be a set with
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{*}(A)>0
and
\ell\geq 0
an integer. Then, there exist an infinite set
B\subset\mathbb{N}
and sequences
(\tilde{t}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
,
(t_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
,
(s_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{N}
such that

\bigg\{\sum_{n\in F}n\colon\ F\subset B\text{ with }k\leq|F|\leq k+\ell\bigg\}\subset A-\tilde{t}_{k},\ \text{for every}\ k\in\mathbb{N},

and

\bigg\{\sum_{n\in F}n\colon\ F\subset B+s_{k}\text{ with }1\leq|F|\leq k\bigg\}\subset A-t_{k},\ \text{for every}\ k\in\mathbb{N},

simultaneously.

Appendix A
Progressive measures, averages and seminorms

A.1.  Progressive measures coming from finite-step nilsystems are distinct

For an ergodic system
(X,\mu,T)
with continuous pronilfactors, the progressive measures
\sigma^{Z_{k}}
for
k\geq 1
are defined in
[
23
]
as follows: Consider
a\in\gen(\mu,\Phi)
and
Z_{k}
the
k
-step pronilfactor with continuous factor map
\pi_{k}\colon X\to Z_{k}
. Define
\xi^{Z_{k}}\in\mathcal{M}(Z_{k}^{k})
to be the Haar measure of the orbit closure of
(\pi_{k}(a),\ldots,\pi_{k}(a))
under the transformation
T\times\cdots\times T^{k}
and for
f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in C(X)
the measure
\sigma^{Z_{k}}\in\mathcal{M}(X^{k+1})
is defined via

\int_{X^{k+1}}f_{0}\otimes f_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes f_{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma^{Z_{k}}=f_{0}(a)\int_{Z_{k}^{k}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f_{1}\mid Z_{k})\otimes\cdots\otimes\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f_{k}\mid Z_{k})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi^{Z_{k}}.

(21)

Notice that even though it is not explicit in the notation, the measure
\sigma^{Z_{k}}
depends on the choice of the point
a\in\gen(\mu,\Phi)
.

In this appendix we want to illustrate that there are cases where if
P_{k+1}\colon X^{k+2}\to X^{k+1}
is the projection to the first
k+1
coordinates, then
P_{k+1}\sigma^{Z_{k+1}}
and
\sigma^{Z_{k}}
are distinct (see also
[
2
, Example 3.19]
).

Fix
s\geq 2
and let
X=\mathbb{T}^{s}
and
T\colon X\to X
be the affine transformation given by
T(x_{1},\ldots,x_{s})=(x_{1}+\alpha,x_{2}+2x_{1}+\alpha,\ldots,x_{s}+sx_{s-1}+\binom{s}{2}x_{s-2}+\cdots+sx_{1}+\alpha)
. This transformation has been repeatedly used in the literature; see, for example,
[
10
, Proposition 3.11]
. In particular if
a=(0,\ldots,0)
then
T^{n}a=(n\alpha,n^{2}\alpha,\ldots,n^{s}\alpha)

Notice that the factors
Z_{k}(X)
for
k\leq s
in this case are the systems
Z_{k}=\mathbb{T}^{k}
with factor maps
\pi_{k}\colon X\to Z_{k}
given by projecting onto the first
k
coordinates. Moreover, for every
k\leq s
,
\sigma^{Z_{k+1}}
is the Haar measure on the manifold

\Omega_{k}=\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{c}v_{0}\\
v_{1}\\
\vdots\\
v_{k+1}\end{array}\right)\in(\mathbb{T}^{s})^{k+2}\middle|\begin{array}[]{c}v_{0}=a,\\
v_{r}=(rt_{1},r^{2}t_{2},\ldots,(r+1)^{r}t_{k},u_{r,1},\ldots,u_{r,s+1-k}),\\
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\in\mathbb{T}\quad\text{and}\\
\quad u_{r,1},\ldots,u_{r,s+1-k}\in\mathbb{T}\quad\text{for }r=1,\dots k+1\end{array}\right\}

where we recall that
a=(0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{T}^{s}=X
.
For instance, when
s=3
,

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}

\displaystyle=\{(0,0,0,\ t,u,v,\ 2t,w,w^{\prime})\mid t,u,v,w,w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}\}\subset X^{1+2}

\displaystyle\Omega_{2}

\displaystyle=\{(0,0,0,\ t,r,u,\ 2t,4r,v,\ 3t,9r,w)\mid t,r,u,v,w\in\mathbb{T}\}\subset X^{2+2}

\displaystyle\Omega_{3}

\displaystyle=\{(0,0,0,\ t,r,s,\ 2t,4r,8s,\ 3t,9r,27s,\ 4t,16r,64s)\mid t,r,s\in\mathbb{T}\}\subset X^{3+2}

Notice that
P_{k}(\Omega_{k})
, the image of
\Omega_{k}
under the projection
P_{k}\colon X^{k+2}\to X^{k+1}
is strictly contained in
\Omega_{k-1}
, so in particular they are distinct. Since Haar measures of nilmanifold are fully supported, one concludes that the measures
P_{k+1}\sigma^{Z_{k}}
and
\sigma^{Z_{k-1}}
are distinct for all
k\leq s
.

For the same reason, in this example, the measures
\sigma^{Z_{k}}
are not ergodic under the action of
Id\times T\times\cdots\times T^{k}
for
k<s
. In fact, the nilmanifold
P_{s,k}(\Omega_{s})
, that is the image of
\Omega_{s}
under the projection map
P_{s,k}\colon X^{s+2}\to X^{k+2}
to the first
k+2
coordinates, is a minimal component of
\Omega_{k}
for the transformation
Id\times T\times\cdots\times T^{k}
. This shows that
\Omega_{k}
itself is not minimal for
Id\times T\times\cdots\times T^{k}
and hence its Haar measure
\sigma^{Z_{k}}
is not ergodic.

A.2.  Double average for seminorm control

Here we use the same family of examples to prove that the seminorms control is only guaranteed in the double averaging version of
Lemma

6.6
. More precisely, we build an example where if one consider a single average, the
L^{2}
norm is no longer controlled by the seminorms of
f_{1},f_{2}
.

Consider
X=\mathbb{T}^{3}
with the transformation
T\colon\mathbb{T}^{3}\to\mathbb{T}^{3}
given by
(x,y,z)\mapsto(x+\alpha,y+2x+\alpha,z+3y+3x+\alpha)
for
\alpha
irrational. Let also
\bm{0}=(0,0,0)
and

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle=\overline{Orb_{T\times T^{2}}(\bm{0},\bm{0})}=\{(t,s,r,2t,4s,9r)\mid t,s,r\in\mathbb{T}\}\subset X^{2}

with its Haar measure
\xi
. Then,
(\Omega,\xi,T\times T^{2})
is a minimal and ergodic system. Notice that, for
F\in C(\Omega)
and
(t_{0},s_{0},r_{0},2t_{0},4s_{0},9r_{0})\in\Omega
, the conditional expectation onto
W=Z_{2}(\Omega,\xi,T\times T^{2})
is given by

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(F\mid W)(t_{0},s_{0},r_{0},2t_{0},4s_{0},9r_{0})=\int_{\mathbb{T}}F(t_{0},s_{0},r,2t_{0},4s_{0},9r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(r).

In particular if
f_{1},f_{2}\in C(\mathbb{T}^{3})
are given by
f_{1}=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{T}^{2}\times(0,1/9^{2})}+1
and
f_{2}=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{T}^{2}\times(1/9,2/9)}+1
we have that

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f_{1}\mid Z_{2})=

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{T}^{2}\times(0,1/9^{2})}\mid Z_{2})+1=1+1/9^{2}

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f_{2}\mid Z_{2})=

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{T}^{2}\times(1/9,2/9)}\mid Z_{2})+1=1+1/9

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f_{1}\otimes f_{2}\mid W)=

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{T}^{2}\times(0,1/9^{2})}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{T}^{2}\times(1/9,2/9)}\mid W)+\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{T}^{2}\times(0,1/9^{2})}+\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{T}^{2}\times(1/9,2/9)}\mid Z_{2})+1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0+1/9^{2}+1/9+1

In particular
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f_{1}\otimes f_{2}\mid W)-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f_{1}\mid Z_{2})\otimes\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f_{2}\mid Z_{2})\neq 0
. Moreover, we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n\leq N}(T^{n}f_{1}\cdot T^{2n}f_{1})\otimes(T^{n}f_{2}\cdot T^{2n}f_{2})\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\xi)}

is controlled by

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n\leq N}\tilde{T}^{4n}(f_{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1})\cdot\tilde{T}^{2n}(f_{1}\otimes f_{2})\cdot\tilde{T}^{n}(\mathbbm{1}\otimes f_{2})\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\xi)},

by similar arguments as in the proof of
Lemma

6.6
, where here
\tilde{T}=T\times T^{2}
. By
[
16
]
we can replace it by

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n\leq N}\tilde{T}^{4n}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f_{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1}\mid W)\cdot\tilde{T}^{2n}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f_{1}\otimes f_{2}\mid W)\cdot\tilde{T}^{n}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(\mathbbm{1}\otimes f_{2}\mid W)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\xi)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1/9^{3}+1)(1/9^{3}+1/9+1)(1/9+1)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n\leq N}\tilde{T}^{4n}(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f_{1}\mid Z_{2})\otimes\mathbbm{1})\cdot\tilde{T}^{2n}(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f_{1}\mid Z_{2})\otimes\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f_{2}\mid Z_{2}))\cdot\tilde{T}^{n}(\mathbbm{1}\otimes\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(f_{2}\mid Z_{2}))\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\xi)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1/9^{3}+1)^{2}(1/9+1)^{2}.
