Unveiling the molecular and immune heterogeneity across distinct disease activity states of systemic lupus erythematosus through integrative bulk and single-cell transcriptomics
Authors: Chen Shen, Mingzhe Guo, Jian Huang, Huiwen Zheng, Gang Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a complex autoimmune disease characterised by substantial variability in clinical presentations and disease severity. This study aims to investigate the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying distinct SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI)-defined disease states of SLE and to identify potential prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets associated with heightened inflammatory activity.MethodsWe analysed bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing datasets from SLE patients. Patients were stratified into clinically distinct SLEDAI-defined disease states using established SLEDAI thresholds, which served as operational surrogates of inflammatory disease burden. Differentially expressed genes and gene co-expression networks were analysed to identify severity-associated hub genes. The immune microenvironment and intercellular communication were evaluated using CIBERSORTx and CellChat algorithms, respectively.ResultsDifferential expression and network analyses identified five key hub genes (KLRK1, IL2RB, RAG1, SH2D1B and NCR1) strongly correlated with SLE disease activity states, which were used to develop a five-gene SLE severity (SLEsev) score. The SLEsev score was significantly higher in high-activity/severe SLE (sSLE) compared with low-activity/mild SLE (mSLE) (p<0.001), which showed a consistent trend in an independent validation cohort. Immune profiling revealed that mSLE was enriched in regulatory T cells (Tregs) and memory B cells, whereas sSLE exhibited elevated levels of CD8+T cells, natural killer cells and monocytes (p<0.05). Single-cell analysis demonstrated that sSLE possessed a more complex cellular communication network (311 vs 283 inferred interactions) and greater overall interaction strength than mSLE, with prominent pro-inflammatory signalling pathways such as IFN-II, GAS and CCL playing a key role in sSLE.ConclusionsThis study unveils the profound immunological and transcriptomic dysregulation underlying SLE severity. The identified five-gene SLEsev score serves as a potential biomarker for disease stratification, while the hyperactive intercellular signalling networks highlight specific molecular targets for modulating immune responses in severe SLE.Keywords: Systemic Lupus Erythematosus, Autoimmune Diseases, Severity of Illness Index, Autoimmunity

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a systemic autoimmune disease characterised by a complex interplay of immune cells, cytokines and signalling pathways, leading to a wide range of clinical manifestations.1 2 Recent epidemiological data indicate a continuous increase in the global prevalence of SLE over time.3 A hallmark of the disease is the generation of a wide variety of autoantibodies.4 Clinically, lupus follows an unpredictable pattern of flares and remissions, with cumulative damage over time greatly impacting quality of life and impairing organ function.5 SLE can lead to various complications, with lupus nephritis being the most common and severe organ manifestation.6 It significantly elevates both morbidity and mortality in affected patients.

Dysregulation of the adaptive immune system is a key factor in SLE pathogenesis, where abnormal T and B cell activation, self-antigen exposure and defective apoptotic pathways lead to increased autoantibody production.7 Through complement involvement, SLE can trigger inflammation and tissue necrosis, or antibodies may directly bind to tissue antigens, causing cell destruction and resulting in multisystem damage.8 9 Additionally, pro-inflammatory cytokine production in monocytes/macrophages and type I interferon (IFN) production in dendritic cells (DCs) promote inflammation in SLE.10 11 Targeting these interconnected components shows potential for therapeutic intervention, making it crucial to understand these pathways for developing effective treatments that modulate immune responses in SLE.

SLE poses a major social and public health challenge, as available treatments and multidisciplinary care can only alleviate symptoms and delay disease progression, without offering a full cure.12 As a result, biomarkers, particularly immunological ones, have become important tools for improving SLE diagnosis and assessing its underlying pathophysiological processes, ultimately aiming to enhance disease management.12

This study aims to explore the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying distinct SLE activity-associated inflammatory states. By integrating bulk and single-cell transcriptomic analyses, we seek to identify molecular signatures, characterise immune microenvironment alterations and decipher intercellular communication networks that distinguish mild (mSLE) from severe (sSLE) disease. Ultimately, this study aims to uncover the immunopathology driving heightened inflammatory burden and highlight potential molecular targets for personalised therapeutic intervention.

Methods

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research, as this study relied exclusively on anonymised, publicly available transcriptomic datasets.

Data sources

Primary bulk RNA-seq expression data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (accession number: GSE228066,13 containing transcriptome data from the peripheral blood of 45 female SLE patients, with gene expression levels analysed as FPKM and subsequently log-transformed as log2(FPKM+1). Disease activity was evaluated using the SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI).14 While SLEDAI is fundamentally an index of current disease activity rather than cumulative irreversible severity (damage), acute high disease activity frequently correlates with severe systemic organ involvement and profound inflammatory flares. Following established precedents in translational transcriptomic research, we used a stringent SLEDAI cut-off as an operational surrogate to define the magnitude of the active inflammatory state. Based on SLEDAI scores, patients were operationally stratified into low-activity/mild SLE (mSLE, SLEDAI≤9) and high-activity/severe SLE (sSLE, SLEDAI>9), according to previously reported SLEDAI-based clinical activity stratification criteria.15

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were acquired from the GEO database (accession number: GSE135779),16 comprising 75 569 cells derived from adult peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). Data from the original study authors, comprising seven SLE patients and five healthy controls (HCs), were used for the downstream single-cell analysis.

To evaluate the reproducibility of our findings, we downloaded an independent SLE dataset (accession number: GSE242721)17 from the GEO database. This validation dataset includes transcriptomic profiles of peripheral blood samples from SLE patients with detailed clinical information, including SLEDAI scores.

Differential expression and gene set enrichment analysis

The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by the R package ‘limma’ (v3.56.2) to compare mSLE and sSLE in the primary bulk RNA-seq dataset. DEGs were defined as genes with a p.adj<0.05 and |log2FC|>1. The p.adj values were calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction method. The R package ‘clusterProfiler’18 (v4.8.1) was employed to analyse DEGs for enrichment in Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways.

Construction of gene co-expression network related to SLE severity

The R package ‘WGCNA’ (v1.72.5) was employed to build a weighted gene co-expression network. To reduce noise and avoid false associations, transcripts with similar expression patterns were clustered using the ‘blockwiseModules’ function with networkType = ‘signed’ and TOMType = ‘signed’. Modules showing a strong positive correlation with the group (Pearson’s R>0.8, p<0.01) were identified as potential co-expression modules.

Development of PPI network and identification of hub genes

We ranked the DEGs by log2FC in descending order and selected the top 200 significant DEGs. These genes were intersected with the previously identified co-expression modules. The intersecting genes were used to construct a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network through the STRING v12.019 database, and the network was visualised using Cytoscape software.20 The Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) plug-in tool in Cytoscape was subsequently used to mine the PPI network for highly interconnected modules. In this study, the MCODE analysis parameters were set as: degree cut-off=2, node score cut-off=0.2, k-core=2.

Calculation of the SLEsev score

Based on the identified key hub genes comprising the core module, an SLE severity gene signature was defined. To quantify the absolute enrichment of this specific signature within individual samples, the R package ‘GSVA’ (v1.48.2) was used to perform single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA). Briefly, the ssGSEA algorithm rank-ordered all genes within a single sample based on their absolute expression levels and then computed an empirical cumulative distribution function to evaluate the relative over-representation of our hub genes towards the top of the ranked list. The resulting integrated enrichment score for each sample was formally designated as the SLE severity (SLEsev) score.

Evaluation of cell infiltration proportions of SLE microenvironment

CIBERSORTx21 was applied to estimate the cellular abundance based on the blood transcriptome profiles. Gene expression data were uploaded to the web portal using the LM22 signature matrix for immune cell profiling. The relationship between cellular infiltration and gene expression was evaluated, with a Spearman’s |R|>0.3 and p.adj<0.05 (BH correction) considered statistically significant.

Single-cell RNA-seq data analysis

The scRNA-seq dataset was analysed using the R package ‘Seurat’ (v5.1.0). All cells expressing <200 genes were removed, as well as cells that contained <500 unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) or >20% mitochondrial counts. The default parameters of Seurat were applied unless mentioned otherwise. Briefly, for the clustering of all cell types, the top 3000 highly variable genes (HGVs) were identified. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was employed to reduce dimensionality. The selection of principal components (PCs) was based on the following criteria: (1) PCs with eigenvalues greater than 1; (2) cumulative variance explained exceeding 80%; and (3) identifying the ‘elbow point’ on a scree plot. The selected 30 PCs were used as input for UMAP clustering. To remove batch effects between samples, the R package ‘harmony’ (v1.2.0) was used to correct for inter-patient variability. Single-cell scores were performed with the ‘AddModuleScore’ function provided by the ‘Seurat’ package.

Pseudobulk analysis

To assess celltype-specific transcriptional changes, we performed a pseudobulk analysis using the ‘muscat’ (v1.16.0) R package. Single-cell RNA-sequencing data were aggregated by summing gene expression counts within each sample-cell type combination using the ‘aggregateData()’ function, with grouping based on sample ID and cell type. The resulting pseudobulk expression profiles were then used to calculate SLEsev scores for each sample-cell type combination using ssGSEA in the ‘GSVA’ (v1.48.2) R package.

Inference of cell-cell interactions

The R package ‘CellChat’22 (v2.1.2) was used to infer intercellular communication networks from scRNA-seq data. Default parameters were applied for the analysis, and interaction probabilities were calculated to assess the strength and frequency of communication between the immune cell populations in both sSLE and mSLE.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software. Continuous and categorical variables were compared using the two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test and Fisher’s exact test, respectively. Correlations were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation. To adjust for immune cell compositional shifts (total NK cells, CD8+T cells and monocytes) in our cohort (n=45), Firth’s penalised likelihood logistic regression was employed to assess the independent association between the SLEsev score and disease state (sSLE vs mSLE). A sensitivity analysis further incorporated Treg proportions. Unadjusted and adjusted ORs with 95% profile likelihood CIs were calculated. A P value or adjusted P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Transcriptome perturbations and biological mechanism differences in mild and severe SLE

A total of 45 patients were included, comprising 23 patients in the sSLE group and 22 patients in the mSLE group (figure 1A). The age distribution showed that the median age was similar across groups, although the sSLE group exhibited a slightly broader range of ages. Additionally, the gender distribution was comparable between the two groups, with both groups predominantly female. BMI1 (log2FC=4.06, p.adj=3.29e-10) and CA14 (log2FC=3.49, p.adj=1.44e-23) were significantly upregulated in sSLE compared with mSLE.

GO analysis indicated that upregulated DEGs in sSLE were associated with myeloid cell differentiation, Ras protein signal transduction, and cell-matrix adhesion (figure 1C). In contrast, downregulated DEGs were associated with critical metabolic processes, including cellular respiration, oxidative phosphorylation and other pathways related to energy metabolism (figure 1D). KEGG results further showed that upregulated genes in sSLE were enriched in focal adhesion and Hippo signalling pathways (online supplemental figure S1A), while downregulated genes were also enriched in lipid and atherosclerosis, oxidative phosphorylation and IL-17 signalling pathway (online supplemental figure S1B).

Identification of key genes associated with SLE severity phenotypes

The turquoise module was strongly and positively correlated with sSLE (figure 2A; Pearson’s R=0.89, p=2e-16), yielding 187 DEGs correlated with SLE severity. From MCODE PPI network analysis, we pinpointed five key hub genes (KLRK1, IL2RB, RAG1, SH2D1B and NCR1) that were strongly associated with SLE severity (figure 2B). These five hub genes displayed significant positive correlations with each other, suggesting that they may function together to influence SLE severity (figure 2C). Functional enrichment analysis indicated that these genes are involved in critical immune pathways, such as lymphocyte-mediated immunity and natural killer cell-mediated immunity (figure 2D).

By calculating scores of the five hub genes (SLEsev scores), we found that SLEsev scores were significantly higher in sSLE compared with mSLE (figure 2E; p=2.3e-05, two-sided Wilcoxon’s rank-sum test). Moreover, there was a positive correlation between SLEsev scores and SLEDAI scores (figure 2F). To further validate the utility of these hub genes as biomarkers, we stratified SLE patients into high (SLEhi) and low (SLElo) groups based on the median SLEsev scores. Fisher’s exact test demonstrated that the SLEhi group had a significantly higher proportion of sSLE patients, while the SLElo group contained more mSLE patients (figure 2G; p=7.96e-05, two-sided Fisher’s exact test). Furthermore, evaluation in an independent cohort (GSE242721) demonstrated a generally consistent trend between SLEsev score groups and SLEDAI-defined clinical activity categories (online supplemental figure S2). However, the Fisher’s exact test did not reach statistical significance (p=0.294), likely reflecting the limited sample size and cohort heterogeneity. Therefore, this external dataset provides supportive but not definitive evidence for the reproducibility of the SLEsev score.

Heterogeneous immunological microenvironment across different severity level SLE patients

Immune infiltration analysis revealed a heterogeneous microenvironment across SLE patients (figure 3A). In sSLE, the proportions of M0 macrophages, resting CD4 memory T cells and resting NK cells were significantly higher compared with mSLE (figure 3B). The expression of KLRK1 showed a strong positive correlation with the proportion of CD8+ T cells, suggesting its role in regulating cytotoxic immune responses in sSLE. On the other hand, the expression of IL2RB was negatively correlated with the proportion of memory B cells, indicating a potential mechanism by which IL2RB influences B cell regulation in SLE. (figure 3C,D). Higher SLEsev scores were positively associated with increased levels of CD8+ T cells, resting CD4+memory T cells and NK cells. In contrast, the proportion of Tregs was negatively correlated with SLEsev scores, suggesting that immune regulation is reduced in more severe phenotypes of SLE (figure 3E). The correlation analysis revealed that SLEsev scores demonstrated stronger and more distinct associations with immune cell abundances compared with SLEDAI scores, particularly for key cell types such as resting NK cells and monocytes (online supplemental figure S3).

To account for potential confounding by immune cell compositional shifts, we performed Firth’s penalised logistic regression. The SLEsev score was significantly associated with sSLE in the unadjusted model (OR=4.94, 95% CI 2.15 to 14.57, p=2.66e-05). Importantly, this association remained highly significant after adjusting for the proportions of total NK cells, CD8+ T cells and monocytes (adjusted OR=11.37, 95% CI 2.64 to 88.53, p=3.19e-04). A sensitivity analysis further incorporating Tregs yielded consistent results (adjusted OR=6.04, 95% CI 1.47 to 41.97, p=0.0103). Thus, the SLEsev score maintains independent predictive value for severe SLE beyond major cellular abundance shifts (online supplemental table S1).

Characterisation of immune microenvironment heterogeneity in SLE at the single-cell level

We annotated nine main cell clusters after correcting batch effects, including CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), natural killer cells (NK cells), CD14+ monocytes, CD16+monocytes, DCs, plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs) and B cells (figure 4A,B and online supplemental figure S4). Compared with sSLE, the proportions of Tregs and B cells were significantly higher in mSLE, suggesting an immune-regulatory and suppressive environment in mSLE (figure 4C,D). However, the proportion of NK cells was significantly higher in sSLE, indicating a potential role of these cells in driving the heightened immune response observed in sSLE.

In sSLE, the SLEsev scores in Tregs and monocytes (CD14+ and CD16+ monocytes) were lower compared with mSLE, while the scores in NK cells were higher (figure 4E). Interestingly, a similar trend was also observed in the pseudobulk analysis, providing additional support for the cell-type-specific patterns observed in the single-cell analysis, where Tregs and monocytes exhibited reduced SLEsev scores in sSLE, whereas NK cells demonstrated increased activity, highlighting the immune dysregulation associated with severe SLE phenotypes (online supplemental figure S5).

Distinct intercellular interactions across SLE severity levels

Compared with mSLE, sSLE exhibited a higher number of inferred cell-cell interactions (figures 5A and 311 vs 283) and greater overall interaction strength (figure 5B, 12.623 vs 10.533). In sSLE, key immune cell types, such as CD8+ T cells and CD14+ monocytes, showed an increased number of interactions and higher interaction strength compared with mSLE (figure 5C). However, interactions involving Tregs were more prominent in mSLE, highlighting the distinct immune network dynamics between mSLE and sSLE (figure 5D).

In mSLE, DCs, CD16+ monocytes and pDCs exhibited higher interaction strengths, indicating their prominent roles in the milder disease phenotype (figure 5E). In contrast, sSLE showed not only the continued dominance of CD16+ monocytes and DCs but also a significant increase in the interaction strength of CD8+ T cells, NK cells and Tregs, indicating a more active and possibly dysregulated immune response (figure 5F). In sSLE, signalling pathways such as IFN-II, GAS, IGFBP and CCL showed a predominant increase in information flow, reflecting a more aggressive signalling network (figure 5G). However, mSLE exhibited relatively higher information flow through pathways like CD40 and MIF, indicative of a less intense immune response. In mSLE, signalling pathways such as MIF, GALACTIN and ANNEXIN were prominent, particularly in interactions with DCs and CD16+ monocytes. In sSLE, there was a noticeable increase in signalling strength involving IFN-II, GAS and CCL, especially in interactions with pDCs and CD8+ T cells. The results highlighted that CD14+ monocytes engaged in differential interactions with various immune cells, including NK cells, B cells and DCs. Notably, interactions involving CD16+monocytes and pDCs were more pronounced in sSLE.

In sSLE, there was a significantly higher communication probability between MIF and its receptors (CD74+CXCR4 and CD74+CD44), particularly in interactions involving Tregs, CD14/16+ monocytes and NK cells (figure 5H). Additionally, IL16 and IFN-γ signalling pathways showed increased interaction probabilities in sSLE, particularly between Tregs-DCs and CD8+ T cells-CD14/16+ monocytes.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the molecular and cellular differences between mSLE and sSLE. BMI-1, upregulated in sSLE, has been reported to promote autoreactive antibody-secreting cells (ASCs) in autoimmune diseases, making it a potential therapeutic target for antibody-mediated autoimmune conditions.23 Similarly, CA14, known for its role in regulating acid-base balance, is intricately involved in inflammatory responses and tissue damage.24 These results are consistent with the known pathophysiology of SLE, where immune dysregulation plays a central role.25 26 In addition, gene set enrichment analyses further highlighted that DEGs upregulated in sSLE are involved in myeloid cell differentiation, Ras protein signal transduction and focal adhesion pathways, suggesting a more aggressive immune response in these patients, whereas downregulated DEGs were enriched in oxidative phosphorylation and lipid metabolism pathways, indicating potential metabolic disturbances.

The co-expression network identified 187 DEGs as being associated with SLE severity. Five hub genes (KLRK1, IL2RB, RAG1, SH2D1B and NCR1) were identified as key molecular features associated with high disease activity states and inflammatory burden in SLE, particularly through lymphocyte and natural killer cell-mediated immunity.27–31 The strong positive correlation between these hub genes suggests a coordinated effort in driving the severe disease phenotype, further emphasising the roles of immune dysregulation in sSLE. Importantly, these genes are unlikely to merely recapitulate the clinical SLEDAI score itself; rather, they may reflect the underlying biological processes and immune perturbations that characterise highly active disease states. Moreover, the significant correlation between SLEsev scores and SLEDAI scores supports their potential as biomarkers for the molecular stratification of SLE activity states, offering a more precise approach to managing SLE patients.

Our investigation revealed distinct differences in immune cell composition. sSLE patients exhibited higher proportions of M0 macrophages, resting CD4 memory T cells and resting NK cells, whereas mSLE patients had higher proportions of memory B cells and Tregs. A decrease in the number or function of Treg cells is a major factor in their imbalance.32 33 These findings suggested that sSLE is associated with a more pro-inflammatory immune environment, characterised by increased innate immune activity and reduced regulatory mechanisms, which may contribute to the chronic inflammation observed in sSLE.34 35

Single-cell analysis confirmed that the immune microenvironment is more heterogeneous in sSLE patients, with Tregs and monocytes displaying lower SLEsev scores while NK cells showed higher scores. The analysis of cell-cell interactions further elucidated the complex communication networks present in SLE. sSLE exhibited a higher number and strength of inferred cell-cell interactions compared with mSLE, particularly involving CD8+T cells and CD14+monocytes. The signalling network analysis revealed that sSLE is characterised by a shift towards more aggressive signalling pathways, such as IFN-II, GAS and CCL, particularly in interactions involving pDCs and CD8+T cells. Conversely, mSLE was associated with higher overall information flow in pathways like CD40 and MIF, which are linked to immune regulation.36–38 Interestingly, while global MIF signalling was prominent in mSLE, specific ligand-receptor interactions were highly pronounced in sSLE, such as MIF with its receptors (CD74+CXCR4 and CD74+CD44), alongside IL16 and IFN-γ, underscoring the importance of precise cellular communication in driving immune dysregulation.

From a clinical perspective, conventional serological biomarkers such as anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) antibodies and complement levels (C3/C4) remain widely used for monitoring SLE disease activity and treatment response. However, these markers primarily reflect selected aspects of humoral immune dysregulation and may not fully capture the complex cellular interactions and transcriptomic heterogeneity underlying different disease activity states. In contrast, the SLEsev score integrates the coordinated expression of multiple immune-related genes associated with NK-cell and lymphocyte-mediated immune responses. Therefore, the SLEsev score should currently be considered a complementary molecular indicator that may provide additional information beyond conventional serological biomarkers. Future prospective studies incorporating anti-dsDNA titers, complement levels and standardised clinical outcomes will be necessary to determine the incremental clinical value of the SLEsev score and to assess whether it offers advantages over existing biomarkers.

Although this study offers a comprehensive analysis, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the modest sample size and reliance on public datasets may limit the generalisability of our findings. Notably, while the independent validation cohort (GSE242721) showed a consistent trend, it did not reach statistical significance (p=0.294), indicating that the SLEsev score requires recalibration in larger, prospective cohorts before clinical translation. Second, the limited number of donors in our scRNA-seq cohort restricts the statistical power for definitive donor-level and pseudobulk inferences; thus, our intercellular communication findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Third, although our Firth logistic regression confirmed the independent predictive value of the SLEsev score, bulk transcriptomic differences may still partially reflect unmeasured immune cell compositional shifts rather than purely cell-intrinsic reprogramming. Finally, as an in silico study, the lack of protein-level data and in vivo functional experiments restricts causal conclusions. Future large-scale, multi-centre prospective cohorts and experimental models are urgently warranted to validate these hub genes and transition them into clinical application.

In conclusion, this study developed the SLEsev score based on five key hub genes (KLRK1, IL2RB, RAG1, SH2D1B and NCR1), offering a novel molecular tool for stratification of SLE activity-associated inflammatory states. Furthermore, our analysis revealed distinct immune microenvironment heterogeneity, demonstrating that severe SLE is driven by heightened innate immune activation, metabolic downregulation and a highly complex, dysregulated intercellular communication network dominated by aggressive pro-inflammatory signalling (eg, IFN-II, GAS and CCL). The identification of specific DEGs, immune cell imbalances and cell-cell interactions that distinguish mSLE from sSLE not only enhances our understanding of the disease pathology but also offers potential biomarkers and candidate therapeutic targets for managing SLE patients. Ultimately, these findings provide new insights into the biological heterogeneity underlying clinically distinct SLE activity states and support the development of personalised therapeutic strategies targeting specific immune pathways.
