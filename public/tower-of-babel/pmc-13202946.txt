A novel tulathromycin oil suspension injection: preparation, characterization, and pharmacokinetics in goats
Authors: Ziyang Li, Yawen Zhong, Yifei Xiang, Chengzhi Liu, Chaojun Wei, Yan Ren, Zhengmin Liang, Jiakang He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tulathromycin (TUL), a semisynthetic macrolide antibiotic, is effective against respiratory tract infections across diverse animal species and is widely used for their prevention and treatment in veterinary medicine. However, the clinical application of TUL is currently limited by the sole availability of an injectable formulation, which may restrict its broader clinical adoption. In the present study, an injection of a TUL oil suspension with 10% drug loading was prepared using the colloid mill dispersion method, and corresponding pharmacokinetic studies were conducted in goats. The resulting preparation was obtained as a milky white suspension whose sedimentation volume ratio, redispersibility, syringeability and injectability, and pH value conformed to the technical standards of the Ministry of Agriculture of the People’s Republic of China. In vitro release studies revealed that the TUL oil suspension exhibited a slower release rate compared with the commercially available injection. Furthermore, in vivo pharmacokinetic studies in goats demonstrated that it significantly delayed absorption (Tmax: 2.33 ± 0.52 h vs. 0.58 ± 0.13 h, P < 0.01) and increased AUC0−∞ by 50.44% (14382.53 ± 1654.23 h·ng/mL vs. 9567.98 ± 1166.02 h·ng/mL, P < 0.01), corresponding to a relative bioavailability of 150.44%. CL was reduced by 37.93% (P < 0.01), and T1/2 was prolonged by 12.64 h (83.32 h vs. 70.68 h). These results highlight the therapeutic potential of this novel formulation, which, with its demonstrated sustained-release profile and enhanced bioavailability, presents a commercially viable and therapeutically promising option against respiratory infections in goats.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05445-4.

Introduction

Mycoplasma ovipneumoniae is a significant respiratory pathogen in global sheep and goat production, primarily causing chronic and debilitating pneumonia. It can establish colonization in the respiratory tract of adult goats and is transmitted via environmental respiratory secretions [1]. Under modern intensive farming, M. ovipneumoniae readily triggers herd outbreaks and commonly establishes co-infections with other pathogens, provoking severe respiratory disease complexes. Infection in adult goats leads to reduced weight gain and compromised carcass quality, thereby diminishing overall production performance [2]. In lambs, infection can cause a significant surge in mortality and may precipitate pneumonia outbreaks with mortality rates reaching 100%, resulting in substantial economic losses [3, 4]. Control and eradication of M. ovipneumoniae are complicated by its lack of a cell wall and its ability to induce long-term carriage states. Although antibiotics effective against mycoplasmas, such as tylosin [5], tilmicosin [6], and tetracyclines [7], remain the primary treatment for M. ovipneumoniae, their use raises public health concerns related to antimicrobial resistance and drug residues.

Tulathromycin (TUL) is a semisynthetic, long-acting macrolide antibiotic used in veterinary medicine [8]. TUL distributes preferentially to alveolar macrophages and neutrophils, where it is slowly released, achieving significantly higher concentrations in lung tissue than in plasma [9]. It exhibits broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, an extended elimination half-life (T1/2), and enables single-dose clinical cure [10]. These characteristics make TUL the drug of choice for treating respiratory infections in livestock, especially those associated with mycoplasma pathogens [11–13]. Traditional macrolides such as tylosin and tilmicosin have short T1/2 and often require multiple or extended dosing, which can accelerate antimicrobial resistance [14, 15]. In contrast, TUL has been widely adopted in veterinary medicine since its introduction in 2002 due to its superior pharmacokinetics (PK).

However, TUL delivery presents notable challenges. Its highly alkaline nature, substantial molecular weight, and strong charge impede effective penetration of the intestinal barrier, rendering oral administration impractical [16]. Given these limitations, subcutaneous (SC) injection is currently the preferred route because of its operational simplicity. The release rate of a drug from the injection site is a critical determinant of its systemic bioavailability and overall pharmacokinetic profile [17]. To date, the conventional TUL formulation on the market is an aqueous solution, exemplified by the commercial product Draxxin® [18]. These solutions are characterized by rapid absorption from the injection site and a quick onset of action, yet their efficacy is compromised by a lack of sustained-release properties. This typically results in a short elimination half-life and thus restricts the full exploitation of its bioavailability.

For prolonged therapeutic effect, several advanced drug delivery systems, including polymeric microspheres and nanoparticles, have been investigated for veterinary use [19, 20]. Although these systems can offer excellent sustained-release profiles, their translation to commercial products is often impeded by drawbacks such as multi-step preparation procedures, the use of potentially toxic organic solvents, difficulties in large-scale manufacturing, and relatively high costs [21–23]. These limitations are particularly challenging in the veterinary pharmaceutical industry, where cost-effectiveness and scalability are paramount. Given these considerations, oil-based suspensions emerge as a pragmatic and attractive platform for sustained drug delivery [24, 25]. Upon SC administration, the oil vehicle forms a hydrophobic depot at the injection site [26]. The release of the active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) is then governed by its partitioning from the oil phase into the surrounding biological fluids, a process that inherently provides a sustained-release effect without the need for complex polymer matrices [27–29]. Compared to microspheres or nanoparticles, oil suspensions offer distinct advantages, including a simpler and more robust manufacturing process (e.g., colloid milling), the use of pharmaceutically acceptable and generally regarded as safe (GRAS) excipients, and lower production costs. While such systems have been successfully employed to increase the bioavailability of other veterinary drugs, their application for enhancing TUL delivery in goats has not been thoroughly investigated.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to develop a novel TUL oil suspension and evaluate its pharmacokinetic profile in goats. We hypothesized that, compared with the commercial aqueous solution, this oil suspension would slow TUL release from the injection site, leading to a prolonged absorption phase, extended elimination half-life, and enhanced relative bioavailability. To test this hypothesis, a TUL oil suspension was prepared using a colloid mill dispersion technique. The physicochemical properties of the suspension, including morphology, drug content, and stability, were systematically characterized. Furthermore, its release profile and relative bioavailability were assessed using in vitro release studies and an in vivo pharmacokinetic study in goats. This study aims to provide a clinically viable, sustained-release TUL formulation for controlling respiratory infections in goats.

Materials and methods

Materials

The TUL standard (purity 98.3%) was purchased from the China Institute of Veterinary Drug Control (Beijing, China). Pure TUL (purity 99.9%) and TUL injection were provided by Amicogen (China) Biopharm Co., Ltd. (Jining, Shandong, China). Glycerol diacetate, hydrogenated castor oil, polysorbate 80, vitamin E, and other pharmaceutical excipients were obtained from Jiangxi Alpha Hi-tech Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Pingxiang, Jiangxi, China). Acetonitrile, methanol, and formic acid were HPLC grade, while all other chemical reagents used in the HPLC, dissolution, and pharmacokinetic analyses were analytical grade.

Selection of dispersing medium and excipients

The preparation of suspensions typically requires an API, a dispersion medium, and excipients such as suspending agents, wetting agents, and antioxidants. First, on the basis of evaluations of the sedimentation volume ratio, redispersibility, and syringeability, suitable excipients for TUL oil suspensions were selected via single-factor experiments. The excipients evaluated included dispersion media (medium chain triglycerides, glycerol diacetate, ethyl oleate, and soybean oil for injection), suspending agents (hydrogenated castor oil, aluminium monostearate, methylcellulose, and sodium carboxymethylcellulose), wetting agents (polysorbate 80, span 80, and polyethylene glycol), and antioxidants (propyl gallate, sodium thiosulfate, and vitamin E). An excipient was considered favorable if it yielded a sedimentation volume ratio ≥ 0.9, required ≤ 2 inversions for redispersibility, and allowed smooth passage through a 22G needle without blockage.

Following single-factor experiments, a three-factor, three-level orthogonal experimental design was employed to determine the optimal amounts of excipients (Table S1). The factors investigated were A: hydrogenated castor oil (0.5%, 1.0%, and 1.5% w/v), B: polysorbate 80 (0.50%, 0.75%, and 1.00% v/v), and C: vitamin E (0.5%, 1.0%, and 1.5% v/v).

Preparation of the TUL oil suspension

The TUL oil suspension was prepared by dispersion using a JM-65 colloid mill (Shanghai Duoyuan Machinery Equipment Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Specifically, 200 mL of glycerol diacetate was preheated to 80 °C, followed by the addition of 5 g of hydrogenated castor oil under continuous stirring until a homogeneous solution (Solution A) was formed. Next, 800 mL of glycerol diacetate was transferred into a colloid mill for circulating grinding. After 5 mL of polysorbate 80 and 0.50 mL of vitamin E were added to the colloid mill and ground for 1 min, Solution A and 100 g of pure TUL were added sequentially. The mixture was subjected to intermittent grinding cycles (30 min of grinding at 2900 r/min followed by 10 min of cooling), and this cycle was repeated twice. This intermittent regimen was employed to dissipate frictional heat generated during high-shear milling, thereby preventing thermal degradation of the heat-sensitive drug substance and maintaining the physical stability of the suspension. Finally, the homogenized oil suspension was collected, packaged into vials, sealed, and sterilized by gamma irradiation at 25 kGy. In other words, a 10% drug-loaded, homogeneous, milky-white TUL oil suspension was obtained, with no aggregates or extraneous materials observed upon visual inspection.

Drug content determination

The drug content in the suspension was quantified using a Shimadzu LC-2050 high-performance liquid chromatography system (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Chromatographic separation was achieved on an Excsep HPH-C18 reversed-phase column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm; Onetwo Chromatography Inc., Zhejiang, China) with detection at 205 nm. The phosphate buffer solution was prepared by dissolving 0.544 g of KH₂PO₄ and 5.572 g of K₂HPO₄ in 600 mL of ultrapure water. The mobile phase consisted of this buffer solution and acetonitrile (35:75, v/v) at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. Analysis was performed at 40 °C with an injection volume of 20 µL and a run time of 20 min. All content determination data were calculated using TUL as the standard.

Characterisation of the TUL oil suspension

Microscopic analysis

A 2 µL aliquot of the homogenized TUL oil suspension was applied to a slide, covered with a coverslip, and examined using an Olympus BX53F2 biological microscope (Evident Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) to assess its physical stability under accelerated conditions—specifically, storage at 25 °C/65% relative humidity (RH) and 40 °C/75% RH for 0, 1, 2, 3, and 6 months. Digital images were captured with an MH20 microscope camera (Fooyue Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) connected to Capture V2.3 software, and the particle size distribution was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Settling volume ratios

After the homogenized TUL oil suspension was allowed to settle for 3 h, the sediment height-to-initial suspension height ratio was determined to calculate the sedimentation volume ratio.

Redispersibility

50 mL of TUL oil suspension was transferred to a stoppered measuring cylinder and allowed to settle for 24 h to reach equilibrium. The sample was vertically inverted and returned to its original position at a rate of one full 180° cycle per second. The minimum number of cycles required to achieve visual homogeneity was recorded.

Syringeability and injectability

Syringeability was assessed by withdrawing 5 mL of homogenized TUL oil suspension under a constant force using syringes fitted with 20G, 21G, 22G, and 23G needles, and the time required for complete withdrawal was recorded. The suspension was then discharged at a constant flow rate to assess injectability. During this process, the smoothness of the suspension flowing through the needle was observed. If there was no obvious stalling, blockage, or interruption of fluid flow and if the injection pressure was uniform, then the needle was determined to have good patency.

pH

The pH of the suspension samples was measured using a calibrated Starter3100F pH meter (OHAUS, New Jersey, USA).

In vitro release testing (IVRT)

The paddle method and pH 6.8 phosphate buffer were selected according to the Chinese Veterinary Pharmacopoeia guidelines for IVRT of sustained-release formulations. This medium is widely employed for comparative in vitro release studies of parenteral dosage forms [30], enabling a direct comparison of the release profiles between the novel oil suspension and the commercial product under standardized conditions. Specifically, the paddle blades were uniformly positioned at a fixed distance from the bottom of the vessel, and the apparatus was preheated to 37 °C with the rotation speed set at 100 rpm. When the system temperature stabilized at 37 °C, 995 mL of phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) was added to the dissolution vessel. Upon initiating rotation, 5 mL of TUL oil suspension and 5 mL of commercial TUL injection were simultaneously injected at a controlled rate into the midpoint between the paddle tip and medium surface, maintaining identical injection heights and speeds for both samples. Samples were withdrawn at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h with immediate replenishment of equal volumes of temperature-equilibrated dissolution medium. Following filtration through 0.45 μm nylon membrane filters, the drug content was quantified using the validated HPLC method.

Stability testing

In accordance with the Chinese Veterinary Pharmacopoeia, the stability of the TUL oil suspension was assessed in sealed glass vials. The preparation was stored under conditions of high humidity (90% ± 5% RH), high temperature (60 °C), and strong light (4500 ± 500 lx) for 10 days, and the drug content, particle size and pH were measured on days 0, 5, and 10. In addition, long-term and accelerated stability tests were conducted over six months under conditions of 25 °C/65% RH and 40 °C/75% RH, respectively, and the drug content, particle size and pH were measured at 0, 1, 2, 3, and 6 months.

PK of the TUL oil suspension in goats

Experimental design

Twelve Nubian black goats (half were male, and half were female) with body weights (BWs) of 40 ± 5 kg were kindly provided by Guangxi Henglang Agriculture and Animal Husbandry Technology Co., Ltd. (Baise, Guangxi, China). All the animals were confirmed to be disease free and maintained under standardized conditions: ambient temperature 22–26 °C, relative humidity 40–60%, and 12 h light/dark cycles. Throughout the study, the goats were fed antibiotic-free commercial pellet feed ad libitum with free access to water. All experimental procedures received prior approval from the Guangxi University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC Protocol No. GXU-2025-019).

Following a one-week acclimatization period, the 12 goats were randomly assigned to two groups (TUL injection and TUL oil suspension, n = 6 per group) using a random number table. Each group contained three male and three female goats. The animals were fasted for 12 h prior to drug administration but allowed free access to water. Goats in groups TUL injection and TUL oil suspension received a single SC injection of TUL injection and TUL oil suspension, respectively, at a dose of 2.5 mg/kg BW (Fig. 1A, Table S2). This dosage was chosen to match the clinically recommended dose of the commercial TUL formulation [10, 31, 32], enabling a direct comparison of pharmacokinetic profiles between the novel suspension and the existing product.

Blood sample collection

The intensive sampling schedule (35 time points over 504 h) was employed to precisely capture the rapid absorption phase of the TUL injection and the extended elimination phase of the TUL oil suspension. Serial blood samples were collected via the jugular vein from all the study animals at predose (0 h) and 34 postdose time points (0.17, 0.25, 0.33, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144, 168, 180, 192, 216, 240, 264, 288, 312, 336, 360, 384, 408, 432, 456, 480, and 504 h) using sodium heparin anticoagulant tubes. Following collection, the blood was centrifuged at 2000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant samples were aliquoted into cryovials and stored at -80 °C until bioanalysis.

Plasma sample processing

Plasma aliquots (250 µL) were combined with 500 µL of acetonitrile in 1.5 mL polypropylene microcentrifuge tubes. The mixture was vortex-mixed for 2 min and then centrifuged at 10,000×g for 10 min to allow complete protein precipitation. Following filtration through a 0.22 μm nylon membrane, the supernatant was transferred into labelled UPLC vials and stored in an autosampler tray until chromatographic analysis.

UPLC-MS/MS analytical procedures

The analytes were determined using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II liquid chromatography system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA) in combination with an Agilent 6470 triple quadrupole MS system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA).

Chromatographic separation was performed using a Phenomenex SuperLu C18 column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.8 μm; Guangzhou FLM Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, Guangdong, China). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid aqueous solution) and solvent B (methanol). The gradient elution procedure was as follows: 0 min (20% B), 0.5 min (20% B), 2 min (100% B), 4.5 min (100% B), 4.6 min (20% B), and 7 min (20% B). Other detection conditions included a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min, a column temperature of 40 ℃, and a sample injection volume of 2 µL.

The ESI source of the mass spectrometer was in positive ion mode, and quantification was performed using multiple reaction monitoring. To facilitate efficient isolation and quantification of TUL, the mass spectrometric conditions were optimized as follows: the sheath gas temperature was set at 250 °C, the sheath gas flow was 11 L/min, the nozzle voltage was 500 V, the nebulizer pressure was 45 psi, the capillary voltage was 2500 V, the drying gas flow was 5 L/min, and the drying gas temperature was 300 °C. Compound-specific MS/MS optimization parameters (see Table S3) were applied to the analytes.

Preparation of calibration standard solutions and quality control (QC) solutions

A stock solution of TUL was prepared at a concentration of 500 µg/mL in acetonitrile. TUL solutions of 12,500, 6250, 2500, 1250, 500, 250, 100, 50, 20, and 10 ng/mL were prepared by serial dilution in acetonitrile. Next, 20 µL of the aforementioned TUL solutions was added to 230 µL of blank goat plasma, resulting in plasma concentrations of 1000, 500, 200, 100, 50, 20, 10, 5, 2, and 1 ng/mL. The plasma TUL concentrations were linear over the range of 1-1000 ng/mL, with the equation of y = 15.917x + 5.098 and a correlation coefficient of 0.9994 (Figure S1).

Three different concentrations of the QC samples, low (2 ng/mL), medium (200 ng/mL), and high (750 ng/mL), were prepared in the same way. The relative standard deviations (RSDs) for intraday precision were 6.31%, 4.07%, and 1.69%, and those for interday precision were 3.95%, 4.42%, and 2.94%, as measured over three consecutive days using the QC samples. The mean recovery of TUL in plasma was 103.34% ± 6.06%. The limit of detection (LOD) was 0.3 ng/mL, and the limit of quantification (LOQ) was 1.0 ng/mL. The stability of TUL in plasma demonstrated RSDs ranging from 1.37% to 8.52%, confirming excellent stability with no significant degradation.

Statistical analysis

Pharmacokinetic parameters (Tmax, Cmax, T1/2, AUC0−∞, Vd, CL and MRT0−∞) were calculated using Phoenix WinNonlin 8.3.5 software (Certara, Inc.‌, Delaware, USA) via noncompartmental model analysis. Statistical comparisons between the two groups were conducted using an independent samples t-test with GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA). Prior to analysis, normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Optimization of the TUL oil suspension

Single-factor experiment

API–excipient compatibility is critical for suspension stability. Among the dispersion media evaluated, glycerol diacetate performed best, with a sedimentation volume ratio of 0.90 and redispersibility within 2 inversions (Table S4). Hydrogenated castor oil outperformed other suspending agents (aluminium monostearate, methylcellulose, and sodium carboxymethylcellulose), achieving an “excellent” rating with a sedimentation volume ratio of 0.98, redispersibility within 1 inversion, and smooth passage through a 22G needle (Table S5). Polysorbate 80 was an effective wetting agent, yielding optimal redispersibility (score of 1) and sedimentation volume ratio (0.98). The resulting suspension also showed excellent injectability, passing smoothly through a 22G needle (Table S6). Vitamin E effectively stabilized the suspension as an antioxidant. After 7 days at 60 °C, the vitamin E-containing formulation showed minimal drug degradation (0.09% loss), outperforming the control (8.73% loss) and formulations with propyl gallate (0.39% loss) or sodium thiosulfate (2.71% loss) (Table S7). In summary, the optimal TUL oil suspension formulation comprised glycerol diacetate (dispersing medium), hydrogenated castor oil (suspending agent), polysorbate 80 (wetting agent), and vitamin E (antioxidant).

Orthogonal testing

To enable quantitative comparison across formulations, a comprehensive score (Y) was calculated for each candidate excipient based on two key physical stability parameters: sedimentation volume ratio (F) and redispersibility (N). F reflects the degree of flocculation and ranges from 0 to 1, with values closer to 1 indicating a well-flocculated, stable suspension that resists caking. N represents the number of manual inversions required to rehomogenize a settled suspension; lower N values indicate easier redispersion and better practical usability. Since these two parameters capture complementary aspects of suspension stability—F reflects equilibrium sediment structure, while N reflects the energy required for reuse—both were considered equally important in the optimization process. To combine them into a single “larger-is-better” score suitable for orthogonal analysis, we transformed N using its reciprocal (1/N), which also ranges from 0 to 1. The comprehensive score was then calculated as Y = F + 1/N. This scoring system assigns equal weight (maximum 1 point each) to both stability parameters, with a maximum possible Y of 2.

As shown in Table 1, the orthogonal test results demonstrated that the influence hierarchy of the three formulation factors was A > C > B, corresponding to hydrogenated castor oil > vitamin E > polysorbate 80. Analysis of variance results (Table 2) revealed a significant effect for hydrogenated castor oil (P < 0.05) but no significant effects for polysorbate 80 or vitamin E (P > 0.05). This finding identifies the content ratio of hydrogenated castor oil as the most critical factor influencing the stability of the TUL oil suspension. On the basis of clinical-scale production feasibility and manufacturing cost considerations, the optimal excipient ratio for the TUL oil suspension formulation was ultimately selected to be A1B1C1. This corresponds to 0.5% w/v hydrogenated castor oil, 0.50% v/v polysorbate 80, and 0.050% v/v vitamin E.

Results of the examination of the TUL oil suspension

Particle size distribution of the TUL oil suspension

The particle size distribution of the freshly prepared TUL oil suspension ranged from 0.53 to 1.63 μm, with a mean particle size of 0.98 μm (Fig. 1B). Further examination of the microscopic morphology of the TUL oil suspension stored for 6 months under conditions of 25 °C/65% RH and 40 °C/75% RH was conducted (Fig. 1C, D). The suspension maintained a uniform particle distribution throughout 6 months of storage at 25 °C/65% RH (Fig. 1C). These results indicate that the suspension is stable at room temperature. However, after 6 months at 40 °C/75% RH, particle aggregation was observed (Fig. 1D). These results demonstrate that TUL oil suspensions exhibit reduced stability under elevated-temperature conditions.

Stability

As shown in Table 4, the labelled amount, particle size, and pH of the TUL oil suspension were monitored under conditions of high humidity, high temperature, and strong light. The quality of the TUL oil suspension did not show statistically significant changes under high humidity conditions. Under high temperature and strong light conditions, the TUL content exhibited a slight decreasing trend, with specific reductions of 0.66% and 0.81%, respectively. The particle size and pH remained stable, with no significant changes observed.

As shown in Table 5, the labelled amount, particle size, and pH of the TUL oil suspension were summarized under long-term (25 °C/65% RH) and accelerated (40 °C/75% RH) conditions. After 6 months of storage under long-term and accelerated conditions, the drug content of the suspension showed a slow decreasing trend, with average reductions of 1.62% and 2.41%, respectively, yet remained within an acceptable range. The pH remained stable, indicating good buffering capacity of the suspension. However, under accelerated conditions, the particle size increased noticeably over time, suggesting that high temperature and humidity may induce particle aggregation or crystal growth, which warrants attention during storage and use. Overall, the TUL oil suspension demonstrated relatively stable quality under normal storage conditions, but prolonged exposure to high temperature and humidity should be avoided to maintain its physical and chemical stability.

Discussion

Through careful screening of the dispersing medium and excipients, a TUL oil suspension was successfully developed. The selection and optimization of a suitable dispersing medium constitute critical steps in the preparation of stable, effective, and safe suspensions [33, 34]. Glycerol diacetate is designated by the FDA as a GRAS substance, exhibiting good biocompatibility, low toxicity, minimal local irritation, and efficient metabolic clearance [35]. It is metabolized to glycerol and acetic acid—both endogenous metabolic intermediates—which are extensively utilized in pharmaceutical, food, and cosmetic applications [36]. Suspending agents function by increasing the viscosity of the dispersion medium, decelerating drug particle sedimentation, and maintaining suspension stability [37, 38]. In this study, hydrogenated castor oil enables sustained drug release and improves stability in suspension formulations, while also offering excellent biocompatibility and safety. Wetting agents facilitate the effective dispersion of drug particles, laying the foundation for the preparation of uniform, stable, and readily redispersible suspensions [39]. By reducing solid-liquid interfacial tension, polysorbate 80 ensures the uniform dispersion and prevents the agglomeration of TUL. It also acts as a compound emulsifier with hydrogenated castor oil and glycerol diacetate, synergizing to enhance suspension stability. Antioxidants are highly effective at inhibiting the oxidative degradation of API and critical excipients, thus maintaining the chemical stability of suspensions [40, 41]. Vitamin E (α-tocopherol) is a naturally occurring, fat-soluble, chain-breaking antioxidant extensively employed in food, cosmetic, and pharmaceutical products to inhibit lipid peroxidation and prevent rancidity [42, 43]. It exhibits low toxicity and excellent lipid compatibility, and facilitates dispersion in lipid-based systems [44]. Therefore, the TUL oil suspension, comprising glycerol diacetate (dispersing medium), hydrogenated castor oil (suspending agent), polysorbate 80 (wetting agent), and Vitamin E (antioxidant), exhibits excellent compatibility and safety. Its quality fully conforms with the technical standards of the Ministry of Agriculture of the People’s Republic of China.

The physical and chemical stability of the TUL oil suspension was systematically evaluated under various storage conditions. Under long-term storage at 25 °C/65% RH, the suspension maintained excellent stability over 6 months, with minimal drug degradation (1.62%), stable pH, and no significant particle aggregation. These results indicate that the oil-based matrix effectively preserves drug integrity under recommended storage conditions. However, under accelerated conditions (40 °C/75% RH), a gradual increase in particle size was observed after 6 months, accompanied by visible aggregation on microscopic examination. This temperature-induced instability is consistent with previous reports on oil-based suspensions and is likely attributable to increased molecular mobility and enhanced particle–particle collisions at elevated temperatures, promoting Ostwald ripening or agglomeration [45]. The concomitant slight decline in drug content (2.41% over six months) suggests that thermal stress may also facilitate chemical degradation pathways, such as hydrolysis or oxidation of the macrolide structure [46]. Under high-humidity conditions (90% ± 5% RH), all quality parameters remained largely unaffected, likely due to the hermetic sealing of the primary packaging, which effectively prevented moisture ingress. In contrast, exposure to high temperature (60 °C) and strong light (4500 ± 500 lx) resulted in modest drug degradation (0.66% and 0.81%, respectively), while particle size and pH remained stable (Table 4). The photosensitivity of TUL may be attributed to its conjugated diene system, which can absorb photon energy and initiate degradation [47, 48]. Regarding matrix effects, the oil vehicle (glycerol diacetate) and excipients (hydrogenated castor oil, polysorbate 80, vitamin E) likely contribute to the observed stability profile in several ways. The hydrophobic nature of the oil matrix limits water penetration, thereby reducing the risk of hydrolytic degradation of TUL. Furthermore, the inclusion of vitamin E as an antioxidant effectively inhibited oxidative degradation, as evidenced by the minimal drug loss compared with formulations lacking antioxidant protection (Table S7). Nevertheless, the present study did not systematically investigate drug–excipient interactions or the oxidative stability of the oil phase itself (e.g., peroxide formation over time). Future studies employing techniques such as Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) or accelerated stability modeling could provide deeper insights into these matrix effects. Overall, the TUL oil suspension demonstrates robust stability under normal storage conditions, but prolonged exposure to elevated temperatures or intense light should be avoided. These findings underscore the importance of appropriate storage and transport conditions for commercial deployment.

The pharmacokinetic profile of the TUL oil suspension differed markedly from that of the commercial injection, consistent with its sustained-release design. The significantly prolonged Tmax (2.33 ± 0.52 h vs. 0.58 ± 0.13 h, P < 0.01) indicates that the oil vehicle successfully delayed drug absorption from the injection site. This delay is attributable to the formation of a hydrophobic depot, from which TUL must partition into the surrounding aqueous tissue fluid before entering the systemic circulation [26–29]. The slower absorption rate was further corroborated by the in vitro release data (Fig. 2), where the oil suspension exhibited a markedly extended release profile compared with the rapid-dissolving commercial injection. Although the Cmax of the oil suspension was numerically lower than that of the injection (843.69 ± 94.68 vs. 996.28 ± 221.92 ng/mL), this difference did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05). Nevertheless, this trend toward a reduced peak concentration aligns with the expected behavior of sustained-release formulations, where the initial burst release is moderated to prolong therapeutic coverage and potentially mitigate peak-related toxicity. The most striking finding was the significant increase in systemic exposure, with AUC0−∞ elevated by 50.44% (14382.53 ± 1654.23 vs. 9567.98 ± 1166.02 h·ng/mL, P < 0.01), corresponding to a relative bioavailability of 150.44%. This enhancement likely results from the sustained-release properties of the oil matrix, which prolongs the residence time of TUL at the absorption site and reduces the fraction of drug cleared before reaching the systemic circulation [49]. The concomitant 37.93% reduction in clearance (CL) (P < 0.05) supports this interpretation, as slower absorption effectively flip-flops the pharmacokinetic profile, making the terminal phase reflect absorption rather than elimination. The T1/2 was prolonged by 12.64 h (83.32 ± 22.18 vs. 70.68 ± 48.41 h), consistent with the extended drug release from the oil depot. This prolongation is clinically meaningful, as it may allow for extended dosing intervals and improved compliance in veterinary practice.

Comparison with previous TUL formulations in goats further contextualizes our findings. Pharmacokinetic parameters of the commercial TUL injection in this study (Tmax: 0.58 ± 0.13 h; Cmax: 996.28 ± 221.92 ng/mL; T1/2: 70.68 ± 48.41 h; AUC0−∞: 9567.98 ± 1166.02 h·ng/mL) are in good agreement with values reported by Clothier et al. [50] (Cmax: 987.8 ± 226.9 ng/mL; T1/2: 59.1 ± 7.6 h; AUC0−∞: 10580 ± 2743 h·ng/mL) and Young et al. [51] (Tmax: 0.40 ± 0.26 h; Cmax: 633 ± 300 ng/mL; T1/2: 110 ± 19.9 h; AUC0−∞: 12500 ± 2020 h·ng/mL), confirming the validity of our experimental model. In contrast, the oil suspension developed herein achieved a substantially longer Tmax (2.33 ± 0.52 h) and higher AUC0−∞ (14382.53 ± 1654.23 h·ng/mL), underscoring the success of the sustained-release strategy. The relative bioavailability of 150.44% compared with the commercial injection represents a substantial improvement, positioning this novel formulation as a promising candidate for clinical deployment.

Importantly, throughout the 21-day post-administration observation period, no adverse reactions or signs of systemic toxicity were observed in any of the goats receiving either the TUL injection or the TUL oil suspension at a dose of 2.5 mg/kg BW. All animals maintained normal behavior, appetite, and body condition, indicating good clinical tolerability of both formulations at the tested dose. However, the absence of a dedicated evaluation of local tolerance at the injection site represents a gap in the safety assessment of this novel oily formulation. SC administration of oil-based depots can occasionally elicit tissue reactions such as inflammation or granuloma formation, which may not be apparent through clinical observation alone. Future investigations should address this by incorporating histopathological examination of injection-site tissues to confirm its biocompatibility.

As a macrolide antibiotic, TUL is effective against respiratory diseases caused by bacterial and mycoplasmal infections, primarily due to its preferential accumulation in lung tissue [9, 52]. However, TUL is not suitable for oral administration, and the only formulation currently available on the market is a water-soluble injectable solution. In contrast to aqueous solutions which undergo rapid release and absorption in the body, the oil suspension formulation has a distinct sustained-release effect. This property not only mitigates peak concentration-related toxicity but also prolongs the duration of therapeutic exposure. Consequently, in this study, TUL was engineered as an oil suspension formulation to intentionally increase its relative bioavailability. Comparative pharmacokinetic evaluation in goats demonstrated analogous disposition kinetics between the TUL oil suspension and the commercial TUL injection. The oil suspension formulation, however, was accompanied by an effectively extended elimination half-life and enhanced relative bioavailability following SC administration. These improved pharmacokinetic properties could translate into significant clinical advantages, such as reduced dosing frequency and a lower risk of inducing antimicrobial resistance.

Conclusion

In summary, a 10% drug-loaded TUL oil suspension was successfully prepared via the colloid mill dispersion method. It is applicable for the treatment, prevention, and control of respiratory infections in goats. The TUL oil suspension exhibited excellent chemical and physical stability, with a particle size ranging from 0.53 to 1.63 μm and an average of 0.98 μm. In vitro release tests demonstrated that the formulation was superior to its commercially available injectable counterparts in terms of sustained release. Pharmacokinetic studies demonstrated that the product exhibited pharmacokinetic behaviour comparable to that of its commercially available injectable counterparts but outperformed them in drug absorption in goats. Specifically, the TUL oil suspension showed an extended elimination half-life of 12.64 h and a 150.44% increase in relative bioavailability. Overall, this study introduces a promising new formulation for the treatment of caprine respiratory infections. While the present pharmacokinetic data are encouraging, the therapeutic efficacy of this formulation warrants further validation through specific pharmacodynamic studies and extensive field trials.
