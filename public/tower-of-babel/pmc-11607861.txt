Health workers’ knowledge and practices toward vaccine cold chain management and its associated factors in a resource-limited setting of Sheger, Oromia, Ethiopia: a multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Lucha Geneti, Seifadin Ahmed Shallo, Mecha Aboma Yebasa, Derese Bekele Daba
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCold chain management is a continuous and cohesive process of preserving vaccines to ensure their availability and potency. However, the quality control of vaccines has been neglected in resource-limited areas such as Ethiopia. Adequate levels of health workers’ knowledge and practices in vaccine cold chain management are important to maintain the potency of vaccines and achieve the intended vaccine end goal.ObjectiveTo assess the level of knowledge and practices of health workers toward vaccine cold chain management and associated factors in resource-limited settings in Ethiopia.MethodsAn institution-based cross-sectional survey was conducted from May to June 2023 in eight sub-cities of Sheger City, Oromia Region, Ethiopia. 255 health workers were selected via simple random sampling from 17 health centers and 17 health posts. Data were collected through a self-administered questionnaire and entered in to EPI DATA version 3.1 and exported to SPSS version 26.0 software for analysis. Binary and multiple logistic regression analyses were carried out to identify factors associated with the knowledge and practices of health workers toward vaccine cold chain management. A variable with a p value < 0.25 was considered a candidate for further regression analysis, whereas a variable with a 95% CI and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultA total of 255 respondents participated, for a 100% response rate. The overall magnitude of good knowledge and practice of vaccine cold chain management was 58% (95% CI: 52.2%--64.3%) and 52.2% (95% CI: 46.3-58.4%), respectively. Receiving pre-service training (AOR = 8.2; 95% CI: (2.93–22.99), availability of SOP/guidelines (AOR = 6.55; 95% CI: (2.0–21.36) and receiving supportive supervision (AOR = 4.2; 95% CI: (1.67–10.59)) were significantly associated with good knowledge of cold chain management. The availability of SOP/guidelines (AOR = 2.13; 95% CI: (1.22–3.7)) and the knowledge of health workers (AOR = 2.32; 95% CI: (1.11–4.8) were significantly associated with good cold chain management practices.ConclusionThere are gaps in knowledge and practice concerning cold chain management. There is an urgent need to improve the knowledge and practice of health workers in cold chain management in the newly organized but resource-limited administrative structure (Sheger) in Ethiopia.Keywords: Cold chain management, Knowledge, Practices, Vaccine

Introduction

Background

Vaccines remain among the most effective tools in public health for preventing infectious diseases and saving lives. Over the last 50 years, vaccines have saved an estimated 150 million lives globally, which is equivalent to 6 lives every minute for a year [1]. Effective vaccine management (EVM) is an initiative endorsed by the WHO/UNICEF to regulate and maintain valid vaccine production, transportation, storage, and utilization by member states. For full public health potential, not only vaccination but also an appropriate and high-quality delivery system is critical [2].

The cold chain is a set of rules and procedures that ensure the proper storage and distribution of vaccines to health services from the national to the local level [3]. It is interconnected with refrigeration equipment that allows vaccines to be stored at recommended temperatures to maintain their potency. It consists of a series of storage and transport links, all of which are designed to keep the vaccine at the recommended temperature (+ 20C to + 80C for refrigerator vaccines) and (-150C to -250C for freezer vaccines) [4].

Vaccines are natural biological products, so they are susceptible to heat, light, and freezing, which requires only storage within the recommended temperature ranges [5]. The cold chain management system is the backbone for the success of the Expanded Program of Immunization (EPI) [6]; thus, there is a crucial need to pay attention to the condition of the vaccine cold system to ensure a reduction in childhood deaths due to vaccine-preventable diseases [3, 4]. The administration of vaccines with improved potency and proven safety in a well-maintained cold chain system is one of the areas of public health focus in developing regions of the world [7]. Moreover, WHO and partner agencies developed EVM; a global initiative aimed at improving countries’ vaccine supply chain management practice through the provision of standardized tools to assess, monitor and improve the supply chain [8].

International organizations such as the WHO and UNICEF have made significant investments through initiatives such as the effective vaccine management (EVM) framework. These investments aim to increase the capacity and efficiency of the vaccine cold chain system. The GAVI, the vaccine alliance, has supported the procurement and deployment of cold chain equipment, including solar-powered refrigerators and data loggers, to improve vaccine storage and monitoring. The Ethiopian government has also invested in infrastructure development, training health workers, and maintenance of cold chain equipment [9]. In Ethiopia, the implementation of EVM focuses on improving various aspects of vaccine supply chain management, such as cold chain equipment, temperature monitoring, stock management, and distribution systems. However, studies are still indicating gaps in vaccine logistics supply chain management practices, like in storing vaccines and monitoring their temperature [10]. In further detail, recent studies are exhibiting the challenges as shortages caused by poor forecasting and delays in requesting supplies, lack of functional refrigerators due to insufficient skilled technicians and spare parts, and the absence of reliable backup power in health centers. Vaccine wastage is common, attributed to forecasting errors, negligence, and cold chain issues. The system has not yet adopted digital logistics solutions and is overwhelmed by frequent outbreak responses [10, 11]. for children under 5 years.

The Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) is designed to protect against vaccine-preventable diseases. In Ethiopia, immunization is provided primarily for children under 5 years with a schedule at birth (BCG and Oral polio vaccine 0-OPV-0); at the sixth week (Pentavalent 1, OPV1, Pneumococcal conjugate vaccine 1- PCV-1, Rotavirus 1), at tenth week (Penta 2, OPV 2, PCV 2, and Rota 2), at fourteenth weeks (Penta 3, OPV 3, PCV 3 and inactivated polio vaccine -IPV); at the ninth months (Measles 1), and twelfth months (Measles 2) as well as Vitamin A supplement at sixth and twelve months [12]. Various professionals are operating cold chain systems of the above vaccines. These include but are not limited to pharmacists, Nurses, medical doctors, EPI focal persons, health extension professionals (HEWs), logistics officers, and so on. These professionals receive on- and off-job training on different occasions [3, 4, 12].

According to a WHO report, significant proportions of vaccine products reach their destination with declining potency [9]. Subsequent studies also indicated that difficulty in managing the cold chain is a problem for both developing and developed countries [17]. It is believed that overheating is more common in developing countries, whereas accidental exposure to freezing temperatures is more likely in developed countries [13]. A study conducted in 440 developed and developing countries revealed that 33.3% of vaccinated individuals in lower-income countries and 37.1% of vaccinated individuals in wealthier countries were exposed to temperatures below the recommended ranges [14]. A study conducted in health facilities in three African countries revealed that 16.6% of health facilities were not in the recommended temperature range [15].

The knowledge and practice of health workers are crucial to delivering the vaccine to the client with maintained potency and safety. Although various reports exist concerning good knowledge and practice of cold chain management globally, systematic reviews in Ethiopia have indicated that only half (49.92%: 95% CI (48.06– 51.79)) of primary health care employees have good knowledge [16], whereas only a few (27.48%: 95% CI (25.70–29.26)) have good practices toward cold chain management. Several factors have contributed to the improvement of cold chain management, including but not limited to receiving training, supportive supervision, and education levels among health workers [10, 11]. In addition, logistic materials and tools for monitoring storage temperatures (thermometers, vaccine vial monitoring, and temperature logging charts, among others) play a significant role. These findings can help encourage health workers to obtain practical knowledge that has been acquired, hence supporting effective and efficient immunization [18].

Despite the demonstrable evidence of the preventive and protective ability of vaccines in reducing the incidence of vaccine-preventable diseases, several significant disease outbreaks have been recorded in developing countries [19]. Several reports from developing countries have shown that health workers seem to be overwhelmingly concerned with increasing vaccination coverage only. The quality of vaccine cold chain management, which is equally important for achieving the ultimate goal of disease reduction, has been neglected. Suboptimal seroconversion rates and outbreaks of vaccine-preventable diseases are attributed to the loss of vaccine potency either during transportation or storage. In some settings, vaccine-preventable disease outbreaks have been linked to poor vaccine management [19].

This study was carried out to assess the level of health workers’ knowledge of and practices related to vaccine cold chain management as well as the contributing factors among frontline primary health care (PHC) unit employees, thus serving as a guide for identifying areas in which to focus intervention mechanisms. Previous studies have focused mainly on health facilities, and their samples have focused only on EPIs [20, 21]; however, the large number of actors involved in vaccine cold chain management are those who are storing, transporting, and delivering the vaccine to end users. Therefore, this study investigated the knowledge and practices of PHC unit actors such as nurses, midwives, and public health professionals toward cold chain management and the contributing factors for better management of cold chain systems in a newly organized administrative but resource-limited setting in Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study design, area, and period

A multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted in public health facilities in Sheger City, Oromia, Ethiopia, which is a newly organized administrative structure [22]. The administrative center of Sheger City is in Finfine, the capital of Ethiopia. It is bordered on the South, by South West Shewa Zone, on the West, by the West Shewa Zone, on the North, by the North Shewa Zone, and on the East, by the East Shewa Zone. The city has 12 sub-cities and 36 districts. In Sheger City, there is one hospital, Twenty- four health centers, and thirty-four health posts. Governmental health facilities provide services by using a total of 1,149 health professionals of various categories serving more than 750,000 people [23]. Among the functioning health centers and health posts, 17 health centers and 17 health posts were included in the study. The study was conducted from May 01 to June 01, 2023 (Fig. 1).

Population

Source population

All health workers engaging in vaccine cold chain management in public health facilities in Sheger City.

Sample population

Primary healthcare facilities found in Sheger City and providing EPI services.

Study population

Health workers engaged in vaccine cold chain management (nurses, midwives, health officers, pharmacists/druggists, and health extension workers) from the sampled primary healthcare facilities.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Health workers involved in vaccine cold chain management with more than six months of working experience.

Exclusion criteria

Health workers who were on annual leave and students who were on internships/practical attachments were excluded.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

The sample size was calculated via single population proportion formulas considering parameters such as 95% confidence interval (CI), 5% α, appropriate knowledge (54.6%), and practice (53.8%) from previous studies [24, 25], with sample sizes of 380 and 372. Furthermore, the sample size for the second objective (contributing factors for good knowledge and practice) was calculated with Epi Info 7 software via the double population proportion formula with the following assumptions: 95% confidence interval, power of 80%, proportion of individuals exposed to the unexposed ratio of 1:1 and ORs of other contributing factors. The results were compared with the sample sizes for the first and second objectives, although they were smaller than the first objective. The maximum sample size (380) was treated with Yate’s correction formula and a 15% nonresponse rate, resulting in a minimum sample size of 255.

The calculated sample sizes for all objectives were compared, and the largest sample size considering the first objective (255) was taken as the final sample size.

Sampling technique

A stratified sampling technique with a proportional allocation of sample size was employed for 30% of the primary health care facilities that are found at the Sheger City administration, resulting in a selection of 8 out of 12 sub-cities, 17 out of 24 HCs, and 17 out of 34 health posts, each of which was selected via a simple random sampling technique. Among each of the selected health facilities, healthcare providers who were rendering services were proportionally selected until the final sample size (255) was achieved.

Study variables

The current status of health workers’ knowledge of and practices related to cold chain management were considered as dependent variables. A binary variable was created from composite variables for both the knowledge and practice of health professionals and two models were fitted while the predictor variables were determined. The variables of socio-demographic characteristics, availability of facility infrastructure, training, supportive supervision, availability of cold chain equipment, and cold chain management SOP/guidelines were considered independent variables.

Operational definitions

Knowledge about cold chain management

Fourteen questions were used, and those health professionals who scored less than or equal to 10 questions (less than 75% of the questions) correctly were considered to have inadequate knowledge. Those who answered greater than or equal to 11 (≥ 75%) questions were classified as having good knowledge of vaccine cold chain management. Overall knowledge was analyzed by creating a new additional variable with “1” for good knowledge and “0” for poor/inadequate knowledge [20].

Practice on cold chain management

The questions used for assessing practice were Twelve questions and those respondents who scored less than 10 (less than 80% of the questions) were considered as having poor practice and those who answered greater than or equal to 10 (≥ 80%) were classified as having good practice of vaccine cold chain management. Overall, all practices were analyzed by creating a new additional variable with “1” for good practices and “0” for poor practices. The EVM initiative encourages countries to achieve or exceed an 80% score for all EVM indicators [26].

Data collection tools and techniques

The data were collected via a pretested, self-administered structured questionnaire. The questionnaire was adapted from the WHO and WHO-UNICEF-effective vaccine management assessment tools [27] and other studies [24, 28–31]. The English version of the questionnaire was translated into the Afan Oromo language (the dominant spoken language in the study area) and returned to English to check its conceptual equivalence. The Afan Oromo version of the questionnaire was pretested in a non-sampled health facility to assess its clarity and consistency accordingly.

An observational checklist was prepared to assess the availability and status of the refrigerator, the adequacy of cold chain equipment, and the logistics of the health facilities. To reduce inter-observer bias, a discussion on the checklist was held for half a day with the experts. The observation was performed by three pharmacy experts who have sufficient training and experience in cold chain management.

Data quality control and management

Training was given for the data collectors to become familiar with the data collection tool. During training, the objective of the study and the method of data collection were discussed. The questionnaire was pretested on 5% of the sample size at another health facility one week before the actual data collection, and the feedback was used to modify the questionnaires. Each day, the collected data were checked for completeness and consistency by supervisors.

Data processing and analysis plan

The data were coded and entered into EPI-DATA version 3.1, exported to SPSS version 26.0, and cleaned to check for completeness and missing values. The logical and consistent errors identified during data entry were corrected after the revision of the completed questionnaire. Descriptive statistics such as frequencies and summary statistics were used to describe the relevant variables in the study population to summarize the data, and the results were presented via frequency tables and charts. Associations between dependent and independent variables were assessed via a binary logistic regression model with crude and adjusted odds ratios at 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for knowledge of and practices related to vaccine cold chain management. Multi-collinearity, interaction, and model fitness tests were employed before fitting the model.

Concerning training provided to employees of the PHC unit of Sheger City, fewer than half (43.1%) of them received pre-service training, whereas 60.8% received in-service training while discharging their responsibility as frontline providers of the EPI. Only 86 (33.7%) health workers knew about the contingency plan in the case of power failure. The overall adequate knowledge score was 58.0% (95% CI: 52.2%--64.3%) (Table 2).

Health workers’ cold chain management practices

Vaccine cold chain management practice indicators have been used for assessment. Vaccines (OPV and freeze-dried vaccines (measles, BCG, and other conjugate vaccines) with diluents were stored in a proper compartment for placement that was a top shelf of the ILR, and freeze-sensitive vaccines (DTP, DT, Td, TT, Hib, HPV, and rotavirus) were stored on middle or lower shelves in 71.4% of health facilities.

With respect to refrigerator temperature readings, at the time of data collection, 90.2% of facilities had a temperature reading within the normal range of (+ 2 °C to + 8 °C), but 9.8% of facilities’ refrigerator temperature readings were outside the recommended range.

Fewer than half (45.9%) of the health facilities record twice-daily manual temperatures for every vaccine refrigerator and vaccines stored on the basis of first expire first-out (FEFO) in 62% of health facilities.

In addition, in 53.7% of the facilities, the refrigerators did not have an alarm that was activated when the temperature exceeded 8°C and fell below 2°C. The refrigerators were situated in a well-ventilated area, away from sunlight and heat only in (48.2%) of the facilities, and in only 53.7% of the health facilities, functional thermometers/fridge tags were placed inside every ILR functional thermometer. The overall percentage of vaccine cold chain management practices was 52.2% (95% CI: (46.3-58.4%) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the level of knowledge and practices of health care workers toward vaccine cold chain management and the contributing factors for good knowledge and practices among those who are working in the vicinity of the newly organized administrative structure of Sheger city, Oromia, Ethiopia.

This study revealed that good knowledge of vaccine cold chain management was 58% (95% CI: 52.2%--64.3%) which is nearly consistent with previous studies conducted in the Dalocha District of Silt’e Zone, Ethiopia (54%) [25], the Amhara region, Ethiopia (56%) [32], the Bale Zone, the Oromia region, Ethiopia (54.6%) [33], the Guji Zone, the Oromia region, Ethiopia (53.58%) [34], and central Ethiopia (56%) [19]. Similar findings might be achieved because the compared studies were conducted in Ethiopia, where all share a similar work structure led by similar guidelines and administrative structures.

However, good knowledge of vaccine cold chain management score was greater than the findings from the Gojam zone, Amhara region, Ethiopia (38.3%) [35], Gurage Zone (51.3%) [31], Tigray region, northern Ethiopia (48%) [30] and Ibadan, Oyo State, Nigeria (43%) [36]. This difference might be explained by the fact that the majority of the employees were pooled into the newly organized structure from different facilities that were formerly implementing vaccine cold chain management. Furthermore, these findings might be attributed to differences in prior training experience, supportive supervision and socio-demographic characteristics such as education and experience.

In contrast, good knowledge of vaccine cold chain management score was lower than those of other studies conducted in the Wolaita Zone, Ethiopia (75%) [37], Malaysia (78.7%), and Ghana (68.6%) [38]. The difference in the results may be due to differences in operational definitions, the characteristics of the health workers in the study area (experience and exposure to pre-service training), the professional categories of the study participants, the geographical location of the study site (remote areas are sometimes major focus of aid organizations that provide training and support), and the timing of the study (the current study was conducted in a newly organized but previously scattered administratively area).

The current study also revealed that only half 52.2% (95% CI: 46.3-58.4%) of the employees had good cold chain management practices. This good practice is nearly consistent with previous studies conducted in the Oromia Special Zone (48.8%) [32], East Gojam Zone of the Amhara Region (58.3%) [35], Tigray Region, Northern Ethiopia (46%) [30], and Bale Zone, Oromia Region (45.4%) [33]. This consistent finding might be attributed to the educational background of the employees, training time and frequency, and years of experience in that in the Ethiopian context, all PHCU employees spent two to four years learning theories and practical aspects of medical discipline followed by placement in the work environment without special training.

The results of good practice score was also lower than those of studies conducted in the Wolaita Zone, Ethiopia (61%) [37] Ibadan, Oyo State, Nigeria (66.1%) [36], and another study involving primary health care workers in Nigeria (77.2%) [39]. The difference could be due to the variation in the study population (socio-demographic and administrative difference), geographical and sociocultural differences among studies (studies in Ethiopia as compared to studies in Nigeria), and operational definitions.

The availability of workplace equipment and infrastructure impacts the performance of workers and the quality of services they render. The infrastructure of vaccine cold chain equipment is important for enhancing proper vaccine cold chain management. The goal of the WHO-UNICEF Effective Vaccine Management (EVM) initiative strongly recommends that all vaccine cold chain management should fulfill at least 80% of each criterion of the WHO standards. Vaccine stores should have a reliable electricity supply, with an automatic standby power supply in the event of main failure. The storage capacity of refrigerators should be sufficient to accommodate peak-level stock requirements, sufficient cold boxes and vaccine carriers. Adequate supplies of spare parts and storage rooms should be clean, inspected regularly and locked when not in use [26].

This study identified gaps related to the availability of vaccine storage equipment and infrastructures and was found to be unacceptable in Sheger city. The number of health facilities using electricity as their primary power source was not sufficient (64.7%). The availability of necessary spare parts and the ability to conduct cold room maintenance were very low (38.2%). The majority of the health facilities did not have separate room for vaccine storage (38.2%), and their storage capacities were insufficient to accommodate all the vaccines (50%), the availability of functional generators for backup services (32.3%), refrigerator cleanness (64.7%), secured windows and doors (41.2%), the availability of at least two vaccine carriers and 8 ice packs (73.5%), and the availability of freezers and cold boxes (58.8); all the findings did not meet at least 80% of each criterion.

With respect to the factors associated with knowledge, pre-service training on vaccine cold chain management, the availability of vaccine cold chain management SOP/guidelines, and regular supportive supervision of vaccine cold chain management were significant predictor variables for good knowledge among health workers. The odds of having good knowledge among workers who had received pre-service training on cold chain management were almost eight times greater (AOR = 8.2; 95% CI: (2.93–22.99)) than those who did not receive pre-service training. This finding was supported by studies conducted in the West Guji Zone, southern Ethiopia [40], Oromia Special Zone [32]. This can be explained by the fact that pre-service and focused trading on cold chain management enhances the knowledge that is acquired at colleges and universities. Furthermore, those who miss or do not understand the concept of cold chain management theoretically will have the chance to see and appreciate it practically during pre-service training.

The availability of vaccine cold chain management SOP/guidelines also significantly contributed to the good knowledge of PHCU employees, as health facilities with SOP/ guidelines/ materials had almost seven times greater knowledge of employees (AOR = 6.55; 95% CI: (2-21.36)) than did PHCUs without SOP. This finding was supported by studies from the Dalocha District of Silt’e Zone [25] and West Guji Zone, Oromia [40]. This is justified by the fact that the presence of SOP/Guidelines in health facilities helps professionals easily obtain access to refer to it when needed as well as to prepare materials while delivering training.

The presence of supportive supervision in the cold chain and vaccine management also plays a significant role in health professionals’ knowledge in that health professionals who are working in the presence of supportive supervision had an almost fourfold greater chance of having good knowledge than those who did not have supportive supervision (AOR = 4.2; 95% CI: (1.67–10.59)). This is because the presence of supportive supervision creates spare time to think about and attract the attention/ curiosity of health professionals regarding cold chain management activities so that they are not criticized by supervisors.

With respect to factors associated with good practices of cold chain management, the availability of vaccine cold chain management SOP/guidelines (AOR = 2.13; 95% CI: 11.22–3.7) and health workers’ vaccine cold chain management knowledge were significant predictor variables of vaccine management practices (AOR = 2.32; 95% CI: 1.11–4.85). Health workers who had good cold chain management knowledge were two times more likely to practice cold chain management than health workers with poor knowledge. Compared with those who had no familiarity with the EPI guidelines or SOP, health practitioners who used the EPI guidelines were two times more likely to have satisfactory practices of cold chain management [37].

The findings of this study are consistent with those of a study conducted in the Gojam Amhara Region, in which health workers who had sufficient knowledge were 2 times more likely to have proper cold chain management practices than those who did not [35]. A systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted among health professionals in Ethiopia, who had good knowledge of vaccine cold chain management and were 2.27 times more likely to have satisfactory practices of cold chain management [7]. This finding is similar to those of previous studies, which revealed that health workers who had good cold chain management knowledge were 3.02 times more likely to practice cold chain management than healthcare workers with poor knowledge [37].

The success of cold chain and vaccine management depends on the knowledge of cold chain handlers and the availability of EPI guidelines. In this study, having good knowledge of vaccine cold chain management and the availability of EPI guidelines were significantly associated with good vaccine cold chain management practices.

A possible explanation is that if health workers have appropriate knowledge and vaccine cold chain management guidelines (SOPs), they may have good motivation and feel responsibility and accountability to practice proper vaccine cold chain management. Health workers who have appropriate knowledge and availability of EPI guidelines will also improve their practices.

Finally, although not distinguished by associations, the presence of facility infrastructure and cold chain equipment for cold chain management also indirectly affects the practice of vaccine cold chain management. The availability of workplaces (separate refrigerator rooms), equipment, and infrastructure has impacted the performance of workers and the quality of vaccines.

Strengths and limitations

Although studies have been conducted on the level of health workers’ knowledge of and practices related to the vaccine cold chain, almost all of the previously conducted studies in Ethiopia focused on health facilities and only EPI focal persons; however, this study included health workers engaging in the vaccine cold chain management.

Owing to time constraints, the current study presented the current level of knowledge and practice of cold chain management among employees of PHCUs. The other limitation of this study is that only quantitative findings were generated; therefore, the details of this problem are lacking. Furthermore, the study did not consider the time since last pre-service training though the presence of pre-service training mostly contribute for good knowledge. Recall and social desirability bias might obscure the real findings of the study.

Conclusion

The results of the study indicated that health workers have a lower level of knowledge and practices regarding vaccine cold chain management than expected. The EVM initiative encourages countries to achieve or exceed an 80% score for all EVM indicators. Pre service training, the availability of vaccine cold chain management SOPs/guidelines, and regular supportive supervision of vaccine cold chain management were significant predictor variables for good knowledge among health workers. The availability of vaccine cold chain management SOPs/guidelines and health workers’ vaccine cold chain management knowledge were significant predictor variables for good practices. Providing regular technical support and training on vaccine cold chain management will improve the knowledge and practice of vaccinators and vaccine handlers.
