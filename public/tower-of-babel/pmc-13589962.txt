Risk of Cancer After a Hospital Contact for Dyspnea: A Nationwide Danish Cohort Study
Authors: Usama Sikandar, Søren Korsgaard Martiny, Dóra Körmendiné Farkas, Eyal Oren, Hans Erik Bøtker, Henrik Toft Sørensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeDyspnea may be a symptom of undiagnosed cancer, but this association remains poorly understood. We examined cancer incidence among patients with a hospital contact for dyspnea compared with the general population.Patients and MethodsUsing nationwide Danish registries, we identified all patients with a hospital contact for dyspnea during 1994–2022 and followed them until a cancer diagnosis, emigration, death, or December 31, 2022. We computed cumulative cancer incidence treating death as a competing event, and standardized incidence ratios (SIR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), as the observed number of cancers relative to the expected number based on national cancer incidence rates.ResultsAmong 266,044 patients with dyspnea, 27,884 subsequent cancers were observed. The 1-year cumulative incidence of any cancer was 3.8%, rising to 12.7% at 10 years. At one year, cumulative incidence was highest for lung (1.4%), gastrointestinal (0.6%), hematological (0.5%), and prostate (0.5%) cancers. The 1-year SIR for any cancer was 3.17 (95% CI, 3.11–3.23), elevated across all cancer types, and highest for pleural (17.57; 95% CI, 15.32–20.05), lung (9.34; 95% CI, 9.05–9.64), and hematological (4.17; 95% CI, 3.94–4.41) cancers. Beyond one year, cancer incidence remained elevated (SIR, 1.14; 95% CI, 1.12–1.16), particularly for lung cancer (SIR, 1.56; 95% CI, 1.50–1.61).ConclusionPatients with a hospital contact for dyspnea had a markedly higher incidence of thoracic and non-thoracic cancers than the general population, particularly during the first year of follow-up. These findings support consideration of an underlying cancer across organ systems as part of the diagnostic evaluation of patients presenting with dyspnea.

Introduction

Dyspnea is a common symptom, affecting up to 50% of patients admitted to hospital and one in four patients referred to outpatient specialist clinics.1 While frequently associated with benign cardiopulmonary, neuromuscular, or psychogenic conditions, dyspnea may also be a symptom of an as-yet undetected cancer.2–4 Dyspnea is reported as a presenting symptom in 6–8% of patients with newly diagnosed cancer,3,5 with particularly high prevalence in patients with lung cancer (up to 56%), mesothelioma (45%), and hematological cancers (5–15%).2–4

In the primary care setting, England’s National Institute for Health and Care Excellence lists dyspnea as a symptom that may warrant urgent cancer referral when it occurs in combination with specific clinical features and risk factors. In particular, evaluation for lymphoma is recommended when dyspnea occurs alongside lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly, and evaluation for lung cancer or mesothelioma is recommended in patients over 40 years of age with unexplained dyspnea, especially in the presence of risk factors such as smoking, asbestos exposure, or other associated respiratory or constitutional symptoms.6

To our knowledge, no previous study has investigated outcomes in a nationwide cohort to determine potential links between dyspnea and short- and long-term risk of cancer across a broad range of cancer types. We hypothesized that patients with a hospital contact for dyspnea would have a higher incidence of thoracic and non-thoracic cancers than the general population. To investigate this hypothesis, we conducted a nationwide cohort study in Denmark.

Materials and Methods

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (see Online Supplemental Material, Appendix S1).7

Setting

The Danish healthcare system provides tax-supported healthcare to all residents of Denmark, ensuring free access to general practitioner and hospital care.8 General practitioners act as gatekeepers to most specialist care and coordinate the initial evaluation and referral of patients with suspected cancer.9 The Danish diagnostic guidelines provide different routes according to the clinical presentation: patients with symptoms suggestive of a specific malignancy may be referred to standardized organ-specific fast-track cancer pathways, whereas patients with serious non-specific symptoms may be referred to hospital-based diagnostic centers for expedited evaluation.9 For patients with less specific but clinically concerning symptoms, general practitioners may also obtain rapid access to selected diagnostic investigations.9 Together, these pathways facilitate coordinated diagnostic evaluation across primary and secondary care.

Contacts with the healthcare system are recorded in national registries covering the entire population.8 Individual-level data linkage among various Danish administrative and health registries is possible using the unique ten-digit Civil Registration Number assigned to each Danish resident at birth or upon immigration.10

Design and Data Sources

We conducted a nationwide population-based cohort study in Denmark between January 1, 1994, and December 31, 2022. We used the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) code R06.0 to identify all patients with a first-time inpatient, outpatient, or emergency department hospital contact for dyspnea registered in the Danish National Patient Registry covering all Danish hospitals.11 We included patients with a primary or secondary diagnosis of dyspnea and excluded patients with a history of cancer recorded in the Danish Cancer Registry.12 Primary diagnoses indicate the main reason for a hospital contact, while optional secondary diagnoses represent diagnoses supplementing the primary diagnosis.

The date of hospital contact for dyspnea served as the index date. Patients were followed from their index date until a diagnosis of incident cancer, death, emigration, or December 31, 2022, whichever came first. The end of follow-up was set at December 31, 2022, as this was the latest date for which complete registry data were available at the time of the study.

To obtain information on incident cancers, we linked our study cohort to the Danish Cancer Registry.12 We obtained information on all cancers other than non-melanoma skin cancer and categorized them into head and neck, gastrointestinal, thoracic (including lung, pleural, and other cancers), breast, female reproductive organs and genitalia, male reproductive organs and genitalia, urological, neurological, hematological, and all other cancers.

Details on the data sources are provided in the online supplemental material (Appendix S2),10–20 with a full list of ICD-10 codes for cancer identification and categorization provided in Table S1.

Covariates

From the Danish National Patient Registry, we obtained information on selected comorbidities commonly associated with dyspnea and, for descriptive purposes, on imaging procedures performed during diagnostic evaluation. Definitions and categorizations are provided in the online supplemental material (Appendix S2), with complete code lists provided in Table S1.

Statistical Analyses

We summarized covariates using medians and interquartile ranges (Q1, Q3) for continuous variables and proportions for categorical variables.

As a measure of absolute cancer risk, we computed cumulative incidences of any cancer and site-specific cancers using the Aalen-Johansen estimator, treating death as a competing event.21 We used the standardized incidence ratio (SIR) as a measure of the relative risk of a cancer diagnosis for patients with dyspnea compared to the general population.22 The SIR was computed as the observed number of cancers in patients with dyspnea divided by the expected number of cancers. The expected number of cancers was obtained by multiplying the number of person-years at risk in our dyspnea cohort by national incidence rates of cancer according to strata defined by sex, 5-year age groups, and 5-year calendar year groups. Exact 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used when the observed number of cancers was <10; otherwise, Byar’s approximation was applied, assuming a Poisson distribution.22 SIRs were calculated for any cancer and for site-specific cancers. Because a hospital contact for dyspnea may be related to undiagnosed cancer, we computed SIRs separately during short-term follow-up (0 months to 1 year), long-term follow-up (>1 year), and total follow-up time. Additional analyses by finer time intervals and subgroups are described in the online supplemental material (Appendix S2).

We also estimated the number of patients needed to undergo diagnostic workup to detect one excess cancer (NND) as the reciprocal of the absolute excess risk, assuming that cancers diagnosed within 6 months of a hospital visit for dyspnea were already present at the time of the visit.23 NNDs were estimated overall and by age group and cancer site.

Data analysis was conducted from May 2024 to May 2025 using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, North Carolina, US).

Results

We identified 266,044 individuals with a first-time hospital contact for dyspnea. The median age was 65.4 (Q1–Q3: 49.4–77.0) years and 47.0% were men (Table 1). Among all hospital contacts, 32.1% were inpatient clinic visits, 47.4% were outpatient clinic visits, and 20.5% were emergency department visits. Dyspnea was recorded as the primary diagnosis for 86.3% of patients. The most prevalent comorbidities were cardiovascular diseases (51.3%), chronic pulmonary diseases (26.8%), and obesity (10.0%). Imaging data from 2002 to 2022 (N = 252,811) showed that diagnostic workups included chest X-rays in 66.6% of patients, chest ultrasounds in 38.3%, and chest computed tomography (CT) scans in 21.1%. Overall Cancer Risk

We identified 27,884 new cancer cases in the study cohort over a median follow-up of 3.8 years. The absolute risk of any cancer was 3.8% at 1 year, 8.4% at 5 years, 12.7% at 10 years, and 18.8% at 20 years, primarily driven by lung, prostate, breast, and colorectal cancers. A detailed breakdown of the absolute risks for any cancer and site-specific cancers in different follow-up intervals is provided in Table S2. The SIR for any cancer over the entire follow-up period was 1.47 (95% CI, 1.45–1.49; Table 2). Short-Term Cancer Risk

Within the first year after a hospital contact for dyspnea, 9,824 cancers (vs 3,101 expected) were diagnosed among the 266,044 patients with dyspnea. Most cancers (6,651/9,824; 67.7%) were diagnosed within the first 3 months after a hospital contact for dyspnea, corresponding to an absolute risk of 2.5%, and yielding a SIR of 7.82 (95% CI, 7.64–8.01). While the 1-year absolute risk of cancer was driven mainly by lung (1.4%), gastrointestinal (0.6%), hematological (0.5%), and prostate cancers (0.5%), SIRs were elevated for all cancer types. The 1-year SIR for any cancer was 3.17 (95% CI, 3.11–3.23; Table 2).

We found particularly high SIRs for pleural (17.57; 95% CI, 15.32–20.05) and lung cancers (9.34; 95% CI, 9.05–9.64). The 1-year SIR for hematological cancer also was markedly elevated at 4.17 (95% CI, 3.94–4.41; Table 2). Specifically, the SIRs were 6.66 (95% CI, 4.84–8.95) for Hodgkin lymphoma, 3.92 (95% CI, 3.36–4.55) for multiple myeloma, 3.80 (95% CI, 3.45–4.18) for non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and 3.55 (95% CI, 3.16–3.98) for leukemia (Table S3). For head and neck cancer, gastrointestinal cancer, cancer of the female reproductive organs and genitalia, and urological cancer, the SIR at 1 year was roughly doubled (Table 2). Among these cancers, 1-year SIRs were notably high for ovarian cancer (4.33; 95% CI, 3.73–5.00), liver cancer (4.56; 95% CI, 3.93–5.28), kidney cancer (3.85; 95% CI, 3.39–4.36), laryngeal cancer (3.61; 95% CI, 2.82–4.56), thyroid cancer (3.41; 95% CI, 2.61–4.37), and esophageal cancer (3.03; 95% CI, 2.53–3.59; Table S3).

Stratified analyses showed consistently elevated 1-year SIRs across all subgroups defined by age and sex, calendar period, hospital contact type, and diagnosis type (Table 3). Increased 1-year SIRs were observed in patients both with and without dyspnea-associated comorbidities (Table 4). Assuming diagnostic sensitivity of 100%, 42 patients with dyspnea would need to undergo a diagnostic workup to detect one excess cancer within 6 months of follow-up, corresponding to an absolute excess risk of 2.38% (95% CI, 2.33–2.44%). The absolute excess risk was highest for lung cancer (1.20%, NND = 83) and hematological cancer (0.34%, NND = 290; Table S4). In age-stratified analyses, the absolute excess risk was highest among patients aged ≥70 years (3.57%, NND = 28) and among those aged 50–69 years (2.5%, NND = 40). When assessing age-specific absolute excess risk for lung, pleural, and hematological cancers, the risk increased with age, peaking for lung cancer in patients aged ≥70 years (1.78%, NND = 56) and 50–69 years (1.35%, NND = 74; Table S5).

Long-Term Cancer Risk

After the first year of follow-up, 18,060 cancers were observed (vs 15,843 expected) among patients with a hospital contact for dyspnea, corresponding to a SIR of 1.14 (95% CI, 1.12–1.16). The SIR after one year of follow-up was highest for lung cancer (1.56, 95% CI: 1.50–1.61) and remained elevated for urological, head and neck, hematological, neurological, gastrointestinal, and breast cancers (Table 2).

Stratified analyses showed elevated SIRs after the first year of follow-up across all subgroups, except for pediatric patients (SIR: 0.95; 95% CI, 0.61–1.41; Table 3). Increased SIRs after the first year of follow-up were observed in patients both with and without dyspnea-associated comorbidities (Table 4).

Discussion

We observed elevated cancer risk among patients with a hospital contact for dyspnea compared to the general population. During the first year of follow-up, the risk of any cancer was elevated in patients with dyspnea. As expected, the short-term association was particularly pronounced for lung, pleural, and hematological cancers. Notably, however, markedly elevated risks were also observed for head and neck, gastrointestinal, urological, and gynecological cancers. Beyond the first year of follow-up, the relative risk of any cancer remained elevated, with risk particularly increased for lung cancer (56% above expected).

Comparison to Previous Studies

Previous studies have examined the diagnostic value of dyspnea for undiagnosed cancer in primary care, focusing mainly on lung and hematological malignancies, reflecting the designation of dyspnea as an alarm feature for these cancers in current referral guidelines. Studies from general practice in the United Kingdom reported low positive predictive values (< 1%) for lung and hematological cancers when dyspnea was not accompanied by other alarm symptoms or clinical features.24,25 A nationwide Danish general population survey corroborated these findings for lung cancer, reporting similarly very low predictive value of dyspnea, even among patients who had consulted a general practitioner for an alarm symptom.26 Recently, a study of more than 55,000 patients diagnosed with incident cancer in England in 2018 mapped presenting symptoms based on information provided by general practitioners. In this case series, patients presenting with dyspnea were most frequently diagnosed with lung cancer, followed by mesothelioma, colorectal cancer, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and ovarian cancer.4 However, as the authors noted, the case series design precluded any inference about the predictive value of dyspnea for cancer.

In contrast, our nationwide cohort study, spanning nearly three decades, examined cancer incidence among patients with a hospital contact for dyspnea relative to the general population and provided both absolute and relative risk estimates across all major cancer sites. Our findings demonstrated that dyspnea was associated with markedly increased risks of both thoracic and non-thoracic malignancies in the hospital setting.

Potential Pathophysiological Explanations

Several pathophysiological mechanisms may explain the association observed between dyspnea and cancer. In thoracic cancers, such as lung and pleural cancers, tumors can impair respiratory function by invading the lung parenchyma, obstructing airways, encasing the lung, causing pleural effusion, or compressing major vessels and the heart.27 Hematological cancers may contribute to dyspnea through mass effect (eg, mediastinal lymphoma)28 and systemic mechanisms, including anemia,29 coagulation disorders that increase the risk of pulmonary embolism and hemorrhages,30,31 and immunosuppression that predisposes to respiratory infections.30

Importantly, these mechanisms are not confined to thoracic or hematological cancers. Solid tumors outside the thoracic cavity—such as head and neck, gastrointestinal, gynecological, and urological cancers—especially in advanced stages, also can lead to dyspnea, either directly by invading respiratory structures32 or indirectly through complications such as organomegaly,27 ascites,33 malignant effusions,34,35 pulmonary embolism,31 respiratory infections,36 and cancer-related anemia (particularly common in gastrointestinal malignancies, eg, colorectal cancer).37,38 These multifactorial pathways, while not exhaustive, provide plausible pathophysiological explanations for the associations observed across cancer sites.

Limitations

Our cohort study has limitations. Despite its strengths of being large and population-based, with virtually no losses to follow-up, Danish registry data lack clinical detail. As well, the validity of dyspnea coding in the Danish National Patient Registry has not been examined specifically, though coding of respiratory diseases has high validity.15,17,19 Importantly, dyspnea is a subjective symptom perceived only by the patient experiencing it.1 Although questionnaires and scales exist to assess its quality and severity,39 and physicians often invoke certain signs (eg, tachypnea, use of accessory muscles, and intercostal retractions) as evidence of respiratory distress,1 registration of dyspnea in the Danish National Patient Registry is likely inconsistent.

As cancer diagnosis often involves a prolonged diagnostic workup, patients presenting with dyspnea due to an undiagnosed malignancy initially may be assigned a symptom-based code for dyspnea. In contrast, patients whose dyspnea can be readily attributed to a specific condition (eg, heart failure) are more likely to receive a disease-specific code rather than a symptom-based code. Such differential coding may result in dyspnea diagnoses being recorded only for a selective subset of patients, potentially resulting in misclassification that would bias the observed associations toward underestimation.23

Heightened diagnostic efforts may explain some of the short-term cancer risk, particularly for thoracic and hematological malignancies. Chest imaging and routine blood tests, commonly performed during the evaluation of dyspnea, may uncover occult malignancies by revealing thoracic cancer or hematological abnormalities that prompt further investigation. During long-term follow-up, detection bias may still contribute to the observed cancer risk if patients with dyspnea undergo more intensive diagnostic approaches than the general population. Such differential diagnostic ascertainment could arise if factors that increase clinical suspicion of cancer, such as a history of smoking, are more prevalent among patients with dyspnea than in the general population and lower the threshold for diagnostic evaluation. Separately, even in the absence of differences in diagnostic ascertainment, the elevated long-term cancer risk may partly reflect shared risk factors (eg, occupational and lifestyle exposures including smoking) associated with both dyspnea and cancer. As information on these risk factors was unavailable, we could not determine the extent to which they contributed to the observed excess cancer risk.

The old age distribution of our cohort may have contributed to the observed absolute cancer risks. However, age is unlikely to explain the elevated relative risks, as expected cancer counts were based on age-specific national incidence rates and elevated SIRs were observed across age groups.

Clinical and Research Implications

In our hospital-based cohort, the number of diagnostic workups required to detect one excess cancer case was lowest for lung and hematological malignancies. This finding is consistent with the evidence underlying current referral guidelines for suspected cancer, which prioritize evaluation for thoracic and hematological malignancies in patients presenting with dyspnea. However, the elevated risks observed across all cancer sites examined in our study—particularly gastrointestinal, urological, and gynecological cancers—underscore the importance of adopting a broader diagnostic perspective and maintaining vigilance not only for thoracic and hematological cancers but also non-thoracic malignancies. Nevertheless, the appropriate level of non-thoracic cancer investigation in patients presenting with dyspnea, including any potential role of whole-body imaging, cannot be determined from our findings. Furthermore, in our cohort, most cancers detected within the first year of follow-up were likely present at the time of hospital contact for dyspnea and identifying some of these cancers would have required an extensive diagnostic evaluation. Because our study did not determine whether dyspnea occurred at an early or advanced stage of the underlying cancer, it remains uncertain whether earlier detection would have influenced clinical outcomes. This concern is supported by a previous study showing that approximately half of patients with undiagnosed cancer who presented with dyspnea were ultimately diagnosed with advanced stage.3 Future studies incorporating clinical information on the character, severity, temporal evolution, and accompanying symptoms of dyspnea may further refine risk stratification and support more targeted recommendations for diagnostic workups.

Notably, in our study, the risk of cancer—particularly smoking-related cancers such as lung cancer—remained elevated beyond the first year of follow-up. Although we lacked data on occupational and lifestyle exposures, including smoking, these factors likely contribute to the observed cancer risks.40–42 Thus, prioritizing early diagnostic efforts and clinical vigilance in individuals with dyspnea and a history of relevant risk factors may be of value. Further research is needed to clarify the contribution of these factors to cancer risk among individuals presenting with dyspnea.

Lastly, it is important to emphasize that dyspnea in patients with undiagnosed cancer may also stem from underlying comorbidities, potentially obscuring its recognition as a symptom of an as-yet undetected malignancy. Our stratified analyses showed that patients with dyspnea and a prior diagnosis of common comorbid conditions associated with dyspnea had nearly a threefold increased risk of cancer compared to the general population. A previous study showed that among patients with lung cancer who presented with dyspnea and a history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), dyspnea was often misattributed to COPD, leading to delayed cancer diagnosis.43 The importance of clinical surveillance of lung cancer in patients with COPD is reflected in current Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease guidelines, which recommend that eligible patients undergo annual low-dose CT screening according to established lung cancer screening criteria.44 These findings underscore the importance of considering the possibility of an undiagnosed malignancy in patients with dyspnea and comorbidities—particularly when dyspnea persists despite appropriate treatment of the known condition.43

Conclusion

In this population-based study, a hospital contact for dyspnea was associated with an elevated short-term risk of cancer. This risk was most pronounced for lung, pleural, and hematological cancers, but extended to several non-thoracic cancers, particularly gastrointestinal, urological, and gynecological malignancies. The elevated risk of cancer, especially lung cancer, persisted beyond the first year of follow-up. These findings indicate that cancer diagnoses following a hospital contact for dyspnea encompass both thoracic and non-thoracic sites, supporting the need for clinical vigilance and the consideration of an underlying cancer during diagnostic evaluation.
