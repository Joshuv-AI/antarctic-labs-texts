Feasibility of Decentralised Response‐Guided Albuminuria Monitoring to Optimise Empagliflozin and Finerenone Therapy in Type 2 Diabetes and Chronic Kidney Disease
Authors: Jelle M. Beernink, Gozewijn D. Laverman, Hiddo J. L. Heerspink
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo assess the feasibility of a decentralised response‐guided trial and to characterise urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (UACR) responses to empagliflozin and finerenone in type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease (CKD).Materials and MethodsAdults with type 2 diabetes, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) ≥ 25 mL/min/1.73 m2, and UACR > 4.5 mg/mmol (> 40 mg/g) were enrolled in a decentralised response‐guided trial. Participants completed study procedures remotely and received empagliflozin 10 mg once daily for 3 weeks. Based on predefined UACR response thresholds during empagliflozin treatment, participants entered a 4‐week second treatment phase with continuation of empagliflozin, add‐on finerenone or substitution with finerenone. Primary outcomes were feasibility measures, including participant‐reported acceptability, sample delivery and completion of home measurements. Exploratory outcomes included changes in UACR.ResultsEleven participants completed the study (mean [SD] age 69.6 [6.1] years; mean eGFR 61.1 [24.8] mL/min/1.73 m2; median [IQR] UACR 246.9 mg/g [102.8–457.7]). All predefined feasibility criteria were met: urine and capillary blood sample delivery rates were 100% and 98.8%, respectively; home blood pressure and body weight completion rates were 91.3% and 98.6%. During empagliflozin monotherapy, UACR changed by −18.7% overall, after which nine participants (81.8%) initiated empagliflozin–finerenone combination therapy and 2 (18.2%) switched to finerenone monotherapy. UACR was further reduced by 40.1% after finerenone addition and by 34.0% after finerenone substitution, relative to the end of empagliflozin monotherapy. No serious adverse events or hyperkalaemia events were observed during treatment with empagliflozin, finerenone, or their combination.ConclusionsA decentralised response‐guided trial was feasible and safe, supporting further evaluation of this approach for individualised optimisation of kidney‐protective therapy in type 2 diabetes and CKD. Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06094920.

Introduction

People with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease (CKD) are at increased risk of kidney and cardiovascular events [1]. Over the past decade, major advances in pharmacotherapy have transformed the management of diabetic kidney disease [2]. In particular, sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and the non‐steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (nsMRA) finerenone have demonstrated robust kidney and cardiovascular protective effects in large randomised controlled trials [3, 4, 5]. Consequently, international endocrinology, nephrology and cardiology guidelines now recommend both drug classes as foundational therapies for patients with type 2 diabetes and CKD [6, 7, 8].

Multiple studies have demonstrated that both SGLT2 inhibitors and finerenone reduce albuminuria [9, 10]. Reductions in albuminuria during treatment with these agents explain a substantial proportion of their long‐term kidney‐protective effects [11, 12]. Early albuminuria responses can therefore be used to assess treatment efficacy in slowing progressive kidney function loss. When albuminuria reduction is insufficient with either SGLT2 inhibitors or finerenone, combination therapy may be considered, as residual albuminuria is associated with persistent risk of kidney failure and cardiovascular outcomes [13, 14, 15, 16].

Recent evidence from the CONFIDENCE trial demonstrated that simultaneous initiation of empagliflozin and finerenone leads to rapid and additive reductions in albuminuria compared with either agent alone, with an acceptable short‐term safety profile [17]. These findings support the clinical rationale for early combination therapy in appropriate patients. However, CONFIDENCE did not address whether repeated albuminuria measurements can be performed remotely or used to guide treatment optimisation. This remains relevant because therapy is often initiated sequentially in routine clinical practice, and delayed reassessment of albuminuria may postpone treatment intensification in patients with residual albuminuria risk [18, 19, 20]. Decentralised clinical trial methodologies provide a practical framework to evaluate whether repeated remote monitoring can enable earlier identification of suboptimal responses and support timely, response‐guided treatment optimisation while reducing participant burden and enhancing study feasibility [21, 22].

In this context, the present study was designed to evaluate the feasibility of a decentralised response‐guided trial assessing individual responses to empagliflozin and, in cases of suboptimal response, response‐guided addition or substitution with finerenone in patients with type 2 diabetes and CKD.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This was a Phase IV, investigator‐initiated, single‐centre, prospective, open‐label, decentralised study of empagliflozin with response‐guided continuation of empagliflozin, addition of finerenone or substitution with finerenone in persons with type 2 diabetes and CKD recruited from Dutch primary and secondary healthcare settings. The trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Institutional review board and independent ethics committee approval were obtained before study initiation, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. The trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06094920).

Participants

Eligible participants were adults with type 2 diabetes and CKD, defined as a urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (UACR) > 4.5 mg/mmol (> 40 mg/g) and ≤ 300 mg/mmol (≤ 2655 mg/g) and an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) ≥ 25 mL/min/1.73 m2. The lower UACR threshold was selected to include participants with albuminuria above the conventional 30 mg/g threshold, ensuring sufficient residual albuminuria to assess individual treatment responses while maintaining recruitment feasibility. All participants were required to receive stable treatment with an angiotensin‐converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB), unless not tolerated, and to be able and willing to provide written informed consent. Key exclusion criteria included a diagnosis of type 1 diabetes, current treatment with an SGLT2 inhibitor or MRA, serum potassium levels > 5.0 mmol/L after repeat testing and an inability to perform home measurements or use digital technologies.

Intervention

After a 1‐week run‐in period to establish baseline measurements, all participants initiated treatment with empagliflozin at a dose of 10 mg once daily for 3 weeks. During the trial, changes in UACR were assessed weekly using first‐morning void urine samples collected on three consecutive days. The albuminuria response after the first 2 weeks of empagliflozin monotherapy was used to guide allocation to the subsequent treatment strategy. All participants completed a third week of empagliflozin monotherapy to allow for logistical arrangements before initiation of the second treatment phase. After this third week, participants entered the 4‐week second treatment phase according to their response category: (1) continuation of empagliflozin monotherapy in participants with a UACR reduction > 30% and residual UACR < 30 mg/g; (2) continuation of empagliflozin with add‐on finerenone in participants with a UACR reduction > 0% to ≤ 30% or > 30% with residual UACR > 30 mg/g; or (3) discontinuation of empagliflozin with substitution by finerenone in participants with no UACR reduction or a UACR increase. Finerenone was initiated at 20 mg once daily in participants with an eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and at 10 mg once daily in those with an eGFR of 25 to < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2.

Study Procedures

The study followed a decentralised design. After an on‐site screening visit, all subsequent study procedures were conducted remotely by participants in their homes. During the decentralised baseline visit, participants received detailed instructions and training for all home‐based procedures.

First‐morning void urine samples were collected using the PeeSpot device on Days −3, −2 and −1 of the run‐in period and on Days 5, 6 and 7 of each treatment week. The PeeSpot device is a validated tool for the collection and preservation of small urine volumes [23, 24]. Participants collected urine samples on three consecutive days during each 1‐week study period. After collection, samples were stored in the participant's refrigerators and dispatched within 3 days. For shipment, PeeSpot devices were placed in a safety bag and sent to the central laboratory using a biological materials envelope. Urine samples were analysed fresh on the day of arrival at the central laboratory.

Capillary blood samples were collected using contact‐activated finger‐prick lancets and BD Microtainer lithium‐heparin plasma tubes on Days −7 and −1 of the run‐in period and on Days 1 and 7 of each treatment week. Samples were mailed to the local laboratory, where analyses were performed using a Cobas 8000 analyser for measurement of creatinine and cystatin C. A venous blood sample was collected at study initiation (Day −7 of the run‐in period), alongside the capillary sample. In addition, venous potassium was measured 1 week after initiation of finerenone for safety monitoring, as reliable potassium assessment is not feasible in capillary blood. Follow‐up and treatment decisions based on potassium results were performed in accordance with local clinical guidelines. Fasting plasma glucose was measured at the same time points as capillary blood sampling. Participants who already possessed a suitable glucose metre were allowed to use their own device, whereas participants without a suitable device were provided with an Accu‐Chek Instant glucose meter (Roche Diabetes Care GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) and corresponding test strips. Participants manually recorded plasma glucose results in a paper diary.

Blood pressure was measured using the Withings BPM Connect (Withings, Issy‐les‐Moulineaux, France), a clinically validated Wi‐Fi–enabled blood pressure monitor, and body weight was measured using the Withings Body (Withings, Issy‐les‐Moulineaux, France). Participants connected both devices to the corresponding mobile application, with values automatically transferred to a secure digital platform. Blood pressure measurements were performed during the run‐in period on Days −7, −3, −2 and −1, and during each treatment week on Days 1, 3, 6 and 7. Participants received standardised instructions for home blood pressure measurements in accordance with clinical guidelines and were instructed to perform three consecutive seated measurements at each time point [25, 26]. Body weight measurements were performed at the same scheduled time points as blood pressure measurements, with an additional measurement on Day 5 of each treatment week. Participants received standardised instructions to consistently measure body weight under identical conditions, preferably in the morning, before food intake and under comparable clothing conditions.

Participants' perspectives on the feasibility and acceptability of participating in a decentralised clinical trial were assessed at the end of the study using a questionnaire. This questionnaire consisted of items derived from the validated Telehealth Usability Questionnaire and supplemented with additional questions used in previous studies evaluating the feasibility of decentralised clinical trials [27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32]. The questionnaire comprised seven items addressing experiences with digital technologies and home‐based measurements, scored on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from ‘completely disagree’ (score 1) to ‘completely agree’ (score 5).

Outcomes

Primary outcomes included participants' perspectives on remote trial participation, as assessed using the previously described questionnaire, the delivery and analysability rates of first‐morning void urine and capillary blood samples, the completion rates of blood pressure and body weight measurements and treatment adherence. Exploratory outcomes included changes in UACR, eGFR, body weight, blood pressure and fasting plasma glucose from baseline to the end of the empagliflozin monotherapy and response‐guided treatment phase. Additional analyses assessed concordance between treatment decisions based on week 2 and week 3 UACR responses and within‐individual UACR variability at baseline and after 2 weeks of empagliflozin. Lastly, we evaluated the correlation between venous and capillary blood measurements obtained on Day −7.

Statistical Analysis

Because this was a pilot feasibility study, the sample size was based on feasibility considerations rather than a formal power calculation. All analyses were prespecified as descriptive, and no formal hypothesis testing was performed. Feasibility outcomes, including questionnaire responses, delivery and analysability rates of urine and capillary blood samples, and completion rates of blood pressure and body weight measurements, were summarised descriptively using counts, percentages and mean scores, as appropriate. Treatment adherence, overall and by study drug, was assessed by pill count and calculated as the proportion of prescribed doses taken during each study phase and summarised as mean percentages with SDs. The agreement between venous and capillary blood measurements obtained on Day −7 was assessed using Pearson correlation analysis.

Acceptability of the decentralised trial design was evaluated against predefined feasibility criteria based on results from our previous decentralised trial [33]. These criteria included delivery and analysability rates of ≥ 90% for urine and capillary blood samples, performance rates of ≥ 90% for blood pressure and body weight measurements and a treatment adherence rate of ≥ 90%. Responses to the participants' perspectives questionnaire were summarised by calculating mean item scores on a 5‐point Likert scale. Mean scores were interpreted using predefined categories adapted from a previous study (1.00–1.80 strongly disagree; 1.81–2.60 disagree; 2.61–3.40 neither agree nor disagree; 3.41–4.20 agree; 4.21–5.00 strongly agree) [34]. An acceptability threshold of a mean score ≥ 3.41 (‘agree’) was applied to each questionnaire item.

To determine changes from baseline in UACR during each treatment phase, baseline values were defined as the geometric mean of three first‐morning void urine samples obtained during the run‐in period on Days −3, −2 and −1, and on‐treatment values as the geometric mean of three consecutive samples collected on Days 5, 6 and 7. Because UACR was skewed, analyses were performed on the logarithmic scale. End‐of‐phase to baseline ratios were back‐transformed and expressed as percentage change from baseline, with additive and substitutive effects reported relative to the end of empagliflozin monotherapy. Within‐individual variability of UACR was assessed at baseline and after 2 weeks of empagliflozin monotherapy using three consecutive first‐morning void urine samples collected within each week. Variability was quantified as the within‐person coefficient of variation, calculated from the SD of log‐transformed UACR values and back‐transformed to the original scale using √(exp(σ 2) − 1), where σ 2 represents the within‐person variance on the logarithmic scale. Within‐person coefficients were summarised as median (IQR). All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Between 29 July 2024, and 5 June 2025, 12 participants were screened, of whom 11 initiated treatment and completed the study after 1 participant withdrew during the run‐in period. The study population had a mean (SD) age of 69.6 (6.1) years (median [IQR] 71 [68–74]), and all participants were male (Table 1). Most participants were White (75%), with the remainder identifying as Asian (25%). The mean (SD) eGFR was 61.1 (24.8) mL/min/1.73 m2 (median [IQR] 57.5 [44.5–96.0]). Median UACR was 246.9 mg/g (IQR 102.8–457.7). All participants were receiving background treatment with an ACE inhibitor or ARB.

Feasibility of Remote Data Collection

All predefined acceptability criteria for decentralised trial conduct were met. All planned first‐morning void urine samples were successfully delivered to the central laboratory and were eligible for analysis (309 of 309, 100%) and 169 of 171 planned capillary blood samples (98.8%) were delivered and analysable. Of the 400 scheduled home blood pressure measurements, 365 (91.3%) were completed, and body weight measurements were completed in 483 of 490 scheduled instances (98.6%).

Participants reported high usability of all home‐based measurement devices. Based on predefined interpretation thresholds, mean questionnaire scores indicated strong agreement regarding ease of use for the urine collection device (mean score 4.82), finger‐prick blood sampling procedure (4.73), blood pressure monitor (5.00) and body analysis scale (4.90). All devices exceeded the predefined acceptability threshold of a mean score ≥ 3.41.

Treatment adherence was high across the study. Mean (SD) overall adherence was 99.8% (0.78). Mean adherence to empagliflozin was 99.6% (1.23), and mean adherence to finerenone was 100%, with all participants meeting the ≥ 90% adherence threshold for both medications.

To evaluate the reliability of capillary blood sampling, venous and capillary blood samples were collected concurrently at a single time point during the run‐in period. Strong correlations were observed between venous and capillary measurements for creatinine (r = 0.99; p < 0.001), cystatin C (r = 0.99; p < 0.001) and fasting plasma glucose (r = 0.85; p < 0.001).

Albuminuria Response During Empagliflozin Monotherapy and Treatment Allocation

At week 2 of empagliflozin treatment, the geometric mean UACR change from baseline (prior to empagliflozin monotherapy initiation) was −18.7% overall, with individual responses ranging from −42.1% to 32.4% (Figure 1). Based on predefined week 2 UACR response thresholds, nine participants (81.8%) initiated empagliflozin–finerenone combination therapy and 2 (18.2%) switched to finerenone monotherapy; none continued empagliflozin monotherapy. At week 2, the geometric mean UACR change was −26.9% (range: −42.1% to −14.2%) among participants later assigned to combination therapy, compared with +31.1% (individual responses: +29.9% and +32.4%) among those later switched to finerenone monotherapy.

After an additional week of empagliflozin monotherapy, the geometric mean UACR change from baseline in the total population was −14.0%, with individual responses ranging from −50.6% to +45.3%. In 10 participants (90.9%), the treatment decision based on the week 2 response was concordant with the decision that would have been made based on the week 3 response. Median within‐individual UACR variability was 13.6% (IQR 9.6–20.2) at baseline and 16.8% (7.9–23.2) after 2 weeks of empagliflozin monotherapy.

Albuminuria Response During the Second Treatment Phase

Relative to the end of empagliflozin monotherapy, the addition of finerenone resulted in an additional 40.1% reduction in geometric mean UACR among participants allocated to empagliflozin–finerenone combination therapy (Figure 2). Among participants switched to finerenone monotherapy, geometric mean UACR changed by −34.0% relative to the end of empagliflozin monotherapy. From baseline, before empagliflozin initiation, to the end of the second treatment phase, geometric mean UACR changed by −53.2% in participants allocated to empagliflozin–finerenone combination therapy, with individual responses ranging from −72.5% to −11.6%. In participants switched to finerenone monotherapy, geometric mean UACR changed by −12.7% from baseline, with individual responses of −25.1% and +1.7%.

From baseline to the end of the second treatment phase, among participants receiving empagliflozin–finerenone combination therapy, mean eGFR changed by −9.3 mL/min/1.73 m2 (range −24.0 to 0.0; Figure 3a), body weight by −1.6 kg (−2.7 to −0.8; Figure 3b), systolic blood pressure by −17.6 mmHg (−50.2 to −3.4; Figure 3c), diastolic blood pressure by −10.7 mmHg (−21.8 to +0.9; Figure 3d) and fasting plasma glucose by −1.4 mmol/L (−6.1 to 0.9). Over the same period, fasting plasma glucose changed by −0.4 and +0.6 mmol/l, respectively.

Adverse Events

No investigator‐reported treatment‐emergent events of hyperkalaemia, defined as any serum potassium concentration > 5.5 mmol/L, were observed during the study. No serious adverse events occurred during treatment with empagliflozin, finerenone or their combination.

Discussion

In this decentralised response‐guided trial, repeated home‐based urine sampling proved feasible and acceptable, supporting decentralised trial conduct in people with type 2 diabetes and CKD. Within this setting, we demonstrated that early albuminuria responses to empagliflozin can guide individualised treatment optimisation. Empagliflozin treatment resulted in a population‐level reduction in UACR, accompanied by substantial interindividual variability. In participants with a suboptimal albuminuria response, response‐guided addition or substitution with finerenone achieved clinically meaningful reductions in albuminuria and was well tolerated.

These findings should be interpreted within the context of the rapidly evolving therapeutic landscape of diabetic kidney disease [2]. Current management is increasingly conceptualised as foundational risk factor control combined with four pharmacologic pillars: renin–angiotensin system inhibition, SGLT2 inhibition, glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonism and non‐steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonism [35]. Although international guidelines now recommend early initiation of multiple pillars, implementation in routine practice often remains sequential and delayed, in part because treatment escalation is rarely guided by frequent reassessment of kidney risk markers such as albuminuria [18]. Our study directly addresses this gap by demonstrating the feasibility of response‐guided, sequential optimisation based on short‐term albuminuria responses.

The magnitude of albuminuria reduction observed with empagliflozin–finerenone combination therapy in this study is directionally consistent with accumulating mechanistic, preclinical and clinical evidence supporting additive or synergistic effects of SGLT2 inhibitors and nsMRAs [35]. Animal models of cardiorenal disease have demonstrated that low‐dose combination therapy produces additive reductions in blood pressure, proteinuria, fibrosis and mortality compared with either agent alone, supporting distinct and complementary mechanisms of action [36]. A clinical crossover study combining the SGLT2 inhibitor dapagliflozin with the steroidal MRA eplerenone similarly demonstrated an additive reduction in albuminuria. Albuminuria decreased by 19.6% during dapagliflozin monotherapy and by 53.0% from baseline during combination therapy, effects that were remarkably similar in magnitude to those observed in the present study [37]. Larger outcome trials and subgroup analyses further suggest that finerenone retains cardiorenal benefits irrespective of background SGLT2 inhibitor use, with signals of reduced hyperkalaemia risk when both agents are combined [38, 39]. The CONFIDENCE trial further strengthens this concept by demonstrating that simultaneous initiation of empagliflozin and finerenone resulted in a 32% greater reduction in UACR compared with empagliflozin alone, supporting early initiation of combination therapy in appropriate patients [17]. This is directionally consistent with the UACR reduction observed after response‐guided finerenone addition in the present study.

Whereas CONFIDENCE evaluated simultaneous initiation in participants assigned to combination therapy, treatment is often implemented sequentially in routine clinical practice [20]. The present study therefore addressed a complementary question by evaluating whether early individual UACR responses to empagliflozin can be assessed remotely and used to guide subsequent treatment optimisation. In this context, our findings demonstrate the feasibility of decentralised albuminuria monitoring as a tool to inform timely, response‐guided treatment optimisation. They also support further evaluation of individualised approaches to implementing SGLT2 inhibitor and nsMRA therapy across different clinical strategies and study designs.

The protocol‐defined substitution of empagliflozin with finerenone in participants without an early albuminuria response was intended to characterise individual albuminuria responses to finerenone within this feasibility study. It should not be regarded as a clinical recommendation to discontinue SGLT2 inhibitor therapy, nor should the absence of an albuminuria response to empagliflozin be considered treatment failure. SGLT2 inhibitors provide broad cardiorenal benefits that extend beyond albuminuria reduction, including effects on heart failure, kidney function decline and mortality [40]. Rather than serving as a criterion for discontinuation, albuminuria response may help identify patients in whom add‐on therapy with finerenone could be considered while continuing SGLT2 inhibitor therapy. This approach aligns with emerging concepts of precision medicine in CKD care, in which therapy is tailored according to individual risk profiles and dynamic treatment responses rather than static baseline characteristics alone. In the future, response‐guided treatment approaches will likely increasingly employ multibiomarker tools that integrate albuminuria with other efficacy and safety markers to provide a more comprehensive estimate of individual treatment benefit [41].

From a methodological perspective, this study further supports decentralised clinical trials as a practical platform for individualised treatment optimisation and builds on our previous decentralised trial, which demonstrated high feasibility and acceptability of remote data collection [33]. Consistent with those findings, all predefined acceptability criteria were met in the present study, with high completion rates for urine sampling, capillary blood sampling, blood pressure and body weight measurements. As digital health tools and remote monitoring technologies continue to advance, such approaches may enable more frequent assessment of treatment response, reduce clinical inertia and facilitate earlier optimisation across multiple therapeutic pillars of diabetic kidney disease.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The small sample size reflects the individual‐level, response‐guided design of this study, which enabled detailed within‐participant evaluation of treatment response and optimisation rather than between‐group comparisons. Consequently, formal comparisons between treatment strategies were limited. In addition, the relatively high baseline albuminuria levels in this cohort should be considered when interpreting treatment allocation, as participants with higher baseline UACR are less likely to meet the criterion for continuation of empagliflozin monotherapy. The short treatment duration precludes inference regarding long‐term kidney or cardiovascular outcomes. The study population was entirely male and lacked broader demographic diversity; this was not a deliberate design choice but reflects the small sample and source population. Nevertheless, the absence of female participants is an important limitation and limits applicability to the broader population of people with type 2 diabetes and CKD. Future larger studies should specifically address sex‐balanced recruitment to ensure that the feasibility, acceptability and treatment‐response findings are evaluated in both female and male participants. Notably, the study was successfully conducted in an older population with a mean age of nearly 70 years and across varying educational backgrounds, supporting the feasibility of decentralised approaches in groups often underrepresented in digital health research. Finally, although decentralised trial conduct was highly successful in this setting, feasibility may vary across healthcare systems and populations with differing access to digital infrastructure. The costs of repeated home‐based UACR monitoring should also be considered. In the present study, the additional material cost, including the PeeSpot device, mailing envelope and laboratory analysis, was approximately 8 euros per sample set. Formal cost‐effectiveness evaluation was beyond the scope of this feasibility study. Future analyses should consider direct sampling costs alongside potential savings in patient travel, parking, productivity losses and clinician and nursing time. Additional monitoring costs may be partly offset by more efficient use of combination therapy and timely treatment intensification in patients with a suboptimal response.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that a decentralised, response‐guided approach using repeated home‐based albuminuria assessments is feasible and can support individualised sequencing of SGLT2 inhibitors and nsMRAs in people with type 2 diabetes and CKD. Within the evolving multi‐pillar treatment paradigm, response‐guided strategies may represent a practical step towards more personalised, efficient and timely optimisation of kidney‐protective therapy.
