On the Detectability of LLM-Generated Text: What Exactly Is LLM-Generated Text?
Mingmeng Geng, Thierry Poibeau

Abstract
With the widespread use of large language models (LLMs), many researchers have turned their attention to detecting text generated by them. However, there is no consistent or precise definition of their target, namely “LLM-generated text”. Differences in usage scenarios and the diversity of LLMs further increase the difficulty of detection. What is commonly regarded as the detecting target usually represents only a subset of the text that LLMs can potentially produce. Human edits to LLM outputs, together with the subtle influences that LLMs exert on their users, are blurring the line between LLM-generated and human-written text. Existing benchmarks and evaluation approaches do not adequately address the various conditions in real-world detector applications. Hence, the numerical results of detectors are often misunderstood, and their significance is diminishing. Therefore, detectors remain useful under specific conditions, but their results should be interpreted only as references rather than decisive indicators.



1
Introduction

The rapid development of large language models (LLMs) has led to a rise of LLM-generated text observed across various fields, such as academia
(
Liang et al. 2024
;
Geng and Trotta 2024
)
and Wikipedia
(
Brooks et al. 2024
;
Huang et al. 2025
)
. The use of AI may also be associated with negative judgments (e.g., low-quality works and lack of creativity), which has become a social phenomenon
(
Sarkar 2025
)
. Thus, the detection of LLM-generated text is not merely a technical matter, but also a social issue.

A lot of detectors have been proposed and studied
(
Yang et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2025
)
, while various issues and challenges have also been examined
(
Tang et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2025
;
Fraser et al. 2024
;
Abdali et al. 2024
)
. The reliability of the detectors has also been widely discussed, for example, their robustness against attacks and their use in different situations
(
Sadasivan et al. 2023
;
Ghosal et al. 2023
;
Chakraborty et al. 2024
)
. Similar to
Chakraborty et al. 2024
’s paper, we explore the following question:

Is it possible to detect the LLM-generated text in practice?

Researchers often say that the text generated by LLMs is different from that written by humans
(
Pu and Demberg 2023
;
Muñoz-Ortiz et al. 2024
;
Reinhart et al. 2025
)
. The indistinguishability between LLM-generated and human-written text is one of big challenges for LLMs
(
Kaddour et al. 2023
)
. However, a basic problem has not been adequately addressed in current literature:

What exactly is “LLM-generated text”?

A simpler and more direct example is that the output of LLMs will vary when given different prompts. Just as different people can write in various styles
(
Zhu and Jurgens 2021
)
, different LLMs generate text in different styles
(
Rosenfeld and Lazebnik 2024
;
Sun et al. 2025
)
.

The broad and vague definitions of LLM-generated text have resulted in inconsistent benchmarks and evaluations, which, in turn, has created new ethical issues and challenges in practical use. If we take into account the diversity of LLMs and human-in-the-loop, the situation becomes even more intricate, and few detectors have tried to distinguish them
(
Cheng et al. 2025b
)
. It has shown that humans possess the capacity to identify LLM-generated text with reasonable accuracy
(
Russell et al. 2025
)
, and the coevolution between humans and LLMs may have occurred
(
Geng and Trotta 2025
)
. Empirical studies have consistently demonstrated that different LLMs exhibit distinct stylistic patterns fingerprints, which could even be used for classifiction
(
McGovern et al. 2024
;
Sun et al. 2025
;
Bitton et al. 2025
)
. The continuous evolution and adjustment of watermarking and attacks are also related to its definition, adding further complexity to the issue of detectability. LLMs may be fully capable of generating text that is nearly indistinguishable from human-written content in the future.

Hence, in many practical cases, we think that it is not possible to accurately detect LLM-generated text. We will discuss the detectability of LLM-generated text in detail in the following sections.

Apart from slight differences in terminology, the definition of “LLM-generated text” in most papers is quite broad, meaning the text can be produced in many ways using LLMs, like paraphrasing, translation, or generating long text from simple prompts. Theoretically, the difference between “LLM-generated text” and “human-written text” lies in how they are produced, as implied by the names. But in practice, we can only evaluate them based on their final output i.e., the text, where a considerable overlap exists between them.

what is commonly regarded as the detecting target usually represents only a subset of the text that LLMs can potentially produce
, and it’s likely the kind that corresponds to the most common and direct prompts. For instance, many detectors are trained on text generated by LLMs, which cannot represent all possibilities. Consequently, their detection capabilities are constrained. While different parameters can be set for various types of cases

Hans et al. 2024

, such configurations can hardly cover all possible scenarios.

)
. We can even say that, LLM-generated text has now integrated into all areas of life, mixing with human writing.

;

3
Background

Probably most people became aware of LLMs after the release of ChatGPT, but the research on detecting text generated by language models had started before that. For example,
Gehrmann et al. 2019
proposed the GLTR (Giant Language model Test Room) tool to detect whether text was generated by models, with experiments involving GPT-2
(
Radford et al. 2019
)
and BERT
(
Devlin et al. 2019
)
.
Zellers et al. 2019
developed the Grover model to detect AI-generated fake news. Even GPT-3
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
continued to face skepticism regarding its text-generation capabilities
(
Bender et al. 2021
)
, making the detectors’ performance unsurprising. Another pioneering work by
Ippolito et al. 2019
demonstrated that humans have already encountered some difficulties in identifying texts generated by GPT-2. Later,
Clark et al. 2021
found that untrained people at the time were not very good at recognizing text produced by GPT-3, and
Wahle et al. 2022
noticed the similar situation for machine-paraphrased plagiarism.

The rapid development and spread of LLMs has drawn significant attention from researchers to the detection of LLM-generated text, and diverse methods have been proposed
(
Wu et al. 2025
)
: DetectGPT
(
Mitchell et al. 2023
)
, Fast-DetectGPT
(
Bao et al. 2023
)
, DetectLLM
(
Su et al. 2023
)
, LLMDet
(
Wu et al. 2023
)
, DeID-GPT
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
and some others in 2023
(
Dugan et al. 2023
;
Zhu et al. 2023
)
; Binoculars
(
Hans et al. 2024
)
, TOCSIN
(
Ma and Wang 2024
)
, Dpic
(
Yu et al. 2024b
)
, Text Fluoroscopy
(
Yu et al. 2024a
)
in 2024, some more detecting methods in 2025
(
Zhou et al. 2025
;
Zeng et al. 2025
)
. The examples listed above are illustrative, and the actual number of detectors is much larger.

These detection methods can be classified into many categories according to different criteria. For instance,
Abdali et al. 2024
classifies them as supervised methods, zero-shot methods, retrieval-based methods, watermarking methods, discriminating features.
Wu et al. 2025
mainly examines them through the lens of watermarking techniques, statistics-based detectors, neural-based detectors, and human-assisted methods. Specialized detectors have been developed, for instance, targeting tweets
(
Gambini et al. 2022
;
Kumarage et al. 2023
)
, journalistic news articles
(
Bhattacharjee et al. 2023
)
and Wikipedia
(
Quaremba et al. 2025
)
. Additionally, the detection of LLM-generated text is not limited to English
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
. Detectors that are used in other languages have also been brought up, such as French
(
Antoun et al. 2023a
)
, Japanese
(
Zaitsu and Jin 2023
)
, Chinese
(
Wang et al. 2024a
)
.

There are other ways to categorize the detectors. For example, most studies only think about binary classification, and detectors with multi-category cases have not been comprehensively explored. In spite of the wide range of methods utilized by these detectors, the absence of universal benchmarks and different application scenarios limits a meaningful comparison
(
Dugan et al. 2024
)
. Although these detection techniques performed well earlier on certain datasets, the ongoing progress of LLMs also makes detection harder
(
Wu et al. 2025
)
.

Researchers have not yet reached full agreement on the detectability of LLM-generated text. For instance,
Chakraborty et al. 2024
claim in their position paper: “
Despite ongoing debate about the feasibility of such differentiation, we present evidence supporting its consistent achievability, except when human and machine text distributions are indistinguishable across their entire support. Drawing from information theory, we argue that as machine-generated text approximates human-like quality, the sample size needed for detection increases.
” While
Zhang et al. 2024b
argue that "
We believe that the issue of AI-generated text detection remains an unresolved challenge. As LLMs become increasingly powerful and humans become more proficient in using them, it is even less likely to detect AI text in the future.
"
Nicks et al. 2023
“
advise against continued reliance on LLM-generated text detectors
”, and
Ganie 2025
try to show that “
perfect AI detection is mathematically impossible
”.

The key disagreement among researchers may not be technical in nature, but rather stems from differing perspectives on human intervention and usage scenarios. The assumptions made by different researchers on this issue are not the same. Take watermarking studies as an example, if people edit the generated text (which is simple to do), the watermark’s reliability may be greatly weakened
(
Dathathri et al. 2024
)
.

4
Evaluation and Benchmark

Now, we will focus on a relatively simple scenario, i.e., we consider that
“LLM-generated text” refers to the content produced by LLMs through normal prompts
.

As outlined earlier, there have always been many doubts about the effectiveness of these detectors
(
Sadasivan et al. 2023
;
Weber-Wulff et al. 2023
)
. The sensitivity of detectors and the problem of false positives have been widely criticized and examined in various papers
(
Weber-Wulff et al. 2023
;
Walters 2023
;
Kar et al. 2024
;
Tufts et al. 2024
)
. By way of illustration, a detection accuracy of 85% necessarily implies a 15% error rate, which may include both false positives and false negatives.
Rashidi et al. 2023
find that the AI text detector erroneously identified up to 8% of the known real abstracts as AI-generated text, and
Giray 2024
states that false positives disproportionately affect non-native English speakers and scholars with distinctive writing styles.

Studies indicate that the detectability of texts depends on the LLM used for text generation
(
Antoun et al. 2023b
)
. For instance,
Wu et al. 2024
point out that the Binoculars
(
Hans et al. 2024
)
only achieved a 55.15% AUROC in detecting texts generated by Claude, while for texts generated by several other models, it reached at least 88%. Given the diversity of usage scenarios and the variety of LLMs, such results are to be expected and no existing LLM-detection system is infallible.

Besides, most studies focus on the binary classification problem of determining whether a given text is generated by LLMs. While some detection methods could achieve good results on given datasets, the scenario becomes more much complicated in real-world settings. To exemplify, people could edit LLM-generated text or mix it with human written text, which has also attracted considerable attention
(
Zhang et al. 2024a
;
Abassy et al. 2024
;
Kumar et al. 2025
;
Kadiyala et al. 2025
)
. Human contribution also affects detection results
(
Gehring and Paaßen 2025
)
. Only a small number of researchers have tried to identify specific roles of LLM in content generation
(
Cheng et al. 2025b
)
, and no universally accepted approaches have been established.

Another challenge in establishing benchmarks for detecting LLM-generated text is that LLMs are continuously evolving, and their characteristics do not remain the same. To cite an example,
(
Liyanage et al. 2022
)
create their benchmark with GPT-2, which should be quite differently from the current advanced LLMs. Although LLMs were compared to stochastic parrot
(
Bender et al. 2021
)
a couple of years ago, their capabilities are gradually being recognized
(
Srivastava et al. 2022
)
, and now their competencies have reached or even surpassed those of human experts in various fields. This leads us to speculate that future LLM-generated texts could surpass current versions in human resemblance. The persona effect could be a good example
(
Hu and Collier 2024
;
Li and Wan 2025
;
Gao et al. 2025
)
.

Some researchers have shown that detectors may more easily flag text from older and smaller models
Elkhatat et al. 2023
;
Saha and Feizi 2025
. The performance of detection systems varies with the application scenario; for instance, they tend to be less effective when dealing with short texts
(
Gameiro et al. 2024
;
Shi et al. 2024
)
. More LLMs were employed in subsequent benchmark construction
(
Wang et al. 2024b
;
He et al. 2024
;
Cornelius et al. 2024
)
, but the number of prompts and scenarios used was limited. Some recent benchmarks
(
Tao et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2024
)
have incorporated a broader range of scenarios, and their impact and effectiveness remain to be seen. There is a similar issue with datasets. For example,
Gritsai et al. 2024
found that all analyzed datasets fail in one or another of their methods and do not allow to reliably estimate AI detectors.

Therefore, their adoption has not yet become as widespread as other well-known LLM benchmarks, such as GLUE
(
Wang et al. 2018
)
and MMLU
(
Hendrycks et al. 2020
)
. Although these benchmarks have also faced some criticism
(
Hadi et al. 2023
)
, there is still no highly universal benchmark to detect LLM-generated texts. Researchers have also started developing shared benchmarks to tackle this problem
(
Dugan et al. 2024
)
. Nevertheless, due to the diversity of usage scenarios and the continuous updates of LLMs, a gold-standard benchmark is hard to realize, may even remain permanently absent.

5
Attack, Watermarking, and Evolution

The brittleness of these tools is demonstrated by many realistic scenarios, including various attacks. An early study has shown that while humans can reliably detect poetry produced by GPT-2, but they struggle to accurately recognize it after human selection
(
Köbis and Mossink 2021
)
. If post-generation modifications are taken into account, the detection process should become more challenging. For example, although some recent systems claim to maintain performance when the LLM-generated text is lightly modified, empirical evidence shows that detection accuracy tends to decline as the extent of human revision increases
(
Lau and Zubiaga 2024
)
.

Studies on how to attack these detectors are also being conducted in different views, such as paraphrasing
(
Sadasivan et al. 2023
;
Krishna et al. 2023
)
, adversarial prompting
(
Tufts et al. 2024
;
Cheng et al. 2025a
)
, and other forms of authorship obfuscation
(
Macko et al. 2024
)
.
Tufts et al. 2024
think that adversarial attacks can easily bypass these detectors, and balancing high sensitivity with a reasonable true positive rate remains challenging.
Dubois et al. 2025
found that even minor adjustments to decoding parameters (such as temperature and top-p) can severely impair detector accuracy. The text produced by the fine-tuned model could also be more challenging to detect
(
Dawkins et al. 2025
)
. Some subtle differences could also affect the detectors’ results, such as a single space
(
Cai and Cui 2023
)
or some minor perturbations
(
Zhou et al. 2024
)
.

To address the detection problem at its source, researchers have already begun experimenting with watermarking methods, which has shown good effectiveness in simulation
(
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023
)
and may also be an ethical necessity
(
Grinbaum and Adomaitis 2022
)
. Diverse watermarking techniques have been proposed, in theory
(
Christ et al. 2024
)
and in practice
(
Dathathri et al. 2024
)
. In addition, some researchers have pointed out the potential risk of watermark stealing
(
Jovanović et al. 2024
)
, and studies on adversarial attacks against watermarking are ongoing
(
Xie et al. 2025
)
.

While there are specific solutions for certain attacks
(
Huang et al. 2024
;
Fang et al. 2025
)
, in practice, there are numerous methods of attack. Consequently, the tools’ applicability to real-world use cases remains limited. This limitation exacerbates the concerns raised in the first point, as reliance on imperfect systems in nuanced or ambiguous situations increases the likelihood of erroneous judgments.

And some of these difficulties are simply unavoidable, not merely temporary technical challenges. As noted by several researchers, humans can learn to detect AI-generated texts
(
Milička et al. 2025
)
and can become an accurate and robust detector of LLM-generated text
(
Russell et al. 2025
)
. When LLMs become more widely used and people may learn from their outputs, the difference between them may get further smaller. We can find more cases of coevolution
(
Pedreschi et al. 2024
;
Geng and Trotta 2025
)
between human and machines. Therefore, the gap between LLM-generated and human-written text is expected to narrow further, but we think that they will not become entirely identical.

Consequently, in the future, detecting LLM-generated text may become less important, particularly in terms of numerical interpretation. While the hybrid human-in-the-loop model is a possible option for detectors, it should be regarded as one way to mitigate the problem, not a perfect solution.

6
Ethical Considerations

The social impact of of LLMs has already been considered
(
Solaiman et al. 2019
)
. People began discussing their positive impact shortly after its emergence
(
Kasneci et al. 2023
)
. LLMs can play a valuable role in numerous contexts, such as enhancing productivity
(
Noy and Zhang 2023
)
. LLMs can help bridge linguistic divides by enabling non-native speakers to produce coherent and idiomatic texts in English or other target languages, thereby supporting greater inclusivity in academic and professional communication. Non-native English speakers have to put in more effort as scientists, and there has been discrimination in the past
(
Amano et al. 2023
;
Lepp and Smith 2025
)
. Automatic editing methods have shown promise in improving alignment between LLM-generated and human-written text
(
Chakrabarty et al. 2024
)
. LLMs may have the potential to serve as effective tutors in English Education
(
Ye et al. 2025
)
.

However, text generated with LLMs is frequently the subject of criticism
(
Sarkar 2025
)
, particularly due to concerns about academic dishonesty, plagiarism, and fraud, which has led to the development of various detection tools
(
Wahle et al. 2022
;
Wu et al. 2025
;
Iofinova et al. 2025
)
. As we have discussed, the definition of LLM-generated text is broad and lacks consistency. In some cases, the LLM acts as a language aid, and the substantive intellectual work remains the responsibility of human authors. It is therefore natural to ask the following question:

Should we use these detectors?

In addition to the technical challenges we have previously discussed, the use of detectors requires consideration of possible ethical risks. A non-native speaker might use an LLM to translate, rephrase, or refine a manuscript that is otherwise the product of original research. But experiments show that certain detectors may exhibit bias against non-native English writers
(
Liang et al. 2023
)
or against certain demographic groups
(
Kadoma et al. 2025
)
. The analyses from
Li and Wan 2025
reveal that all the detectors they tested are highly sensitive to CEFR level and language environment. Signs of language discrimination and resistance have also been noted in recently
(
Lepp and Smith 2025
)
.

As such, current detectors are far from perfect and may never achieve perfection in the future either. Accusing someone of misconduct based on an imperfect tool can lead to unjust outcomes, reputational damage, and institutional distrust. Therefore, even detectors with relatively high accuracy present significant risks when used for evaluative or disciplinary purposes. With LLMs being so widely used in academia
(
Eger et al. 2025
;
Russell et al. 2025
)
, detecting AI-generated text must be handled with extreme care.

In addition to the detection and policing of LLM-generated text, it may also be useful to advocate for transparency regarding their use
(
Hosseini et al. 2023
)
and promote AI literacy
(
Cardon et al. 2023
)
. In academic publishing, for example, it is increasingly common to disclose how LLMs have assisted in drafting, editing, or rephrasing portions of a manuscript. Clear guidelines and disclosures can thus help normalize the ethical integration of LLMs into scholarly workflows without undermining academic integrity.

If LLMs are applied properly and people assess detection tools reasonably, their positive influence can be greatly amplified. Those familiar with LLMs and detectors are aware of the potential issues, but the public tends to be easily drawn to these numbers and the brief conclusion. The mechanism behind the language style of LLMs is currently unclear
(
Juzek and Ward 2024
)
. The lack of detector interpretability represents another concern
(
Ji et al. 2024
)
, severely limiting the ability to provide transparent explanations to the public.

8
Conclusions

We must highlight that a major source of difficulty is that LLM-generated text lacks a unified and clear definition. Moreover, due to the various influences of LLMs on humans, human-written text is likely to converge toward the LLM-generated text. The detection of LLM-generated text can be achieved under multiple assumptions, but in reality, these assumptions are often difficult to satisfy. The abuse and misuse of these detectors can create many risks.

The detectors usually struggle to assess the proportion, function, or ethical significance of LLM contributions in a given text. As a result, the development and deployment of LLM-generated text detectors raise serious concerns, not only due to technical limitations but also because they risk enforcing overly simplistic and potentially unjust frameworks for evaluating authorship and intellectual responsibility.

The numerical effectiveness of LLM-generated text detectors is declining. On the one hand, human may be influenced by LLMs and may create text resembling LLM-generated text. One the other hand, people may also adapt their language to bypass LLM detection tools
(
Geng and Trotta 2025
)
. Detection efforts should target substantive content (e.g., fact-checking) rather than linguistic characteristics
(
Schuster et al. 2020
)
.

Therefore, while these detectors can be useful under specific conditions, their results should be used only as a reference with great caution and not as a decisive indicator. For instance, when interpreting the detection results of LLM-generated text, it is necessary to explicitly state the assumptions and prerequisites.
