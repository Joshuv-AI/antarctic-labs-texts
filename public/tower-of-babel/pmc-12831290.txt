Transperineal cognitive fusion MRI-targeted biopsy shows higher detection rates than systematic biopsy in prostate cancer: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Jifei Jiang, Kun Wang, Xiao Xie, Xiaomin Mo, Zehui Fu, Qing Wang, Feng Qian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveSystematic biopsy (SBx) underdetects clinically significant prostate cancer and overdiagnoses indolent disease. This study compared the transperineal cognitive fusion targeted biopsy (COG-TBx)—which uses multiparametric MRI for precision—against SBx, evaluating their cancer detection rates and safety profiles in a clinical cohort.MethodsA total of 360 patients undergoing transperineal prostate biopsy were included, with 161 assigned to the COG-TBx group and 199 to the SBx group. Primary outcomes included PCa detection, csPCa (defined as Gleason score ≥ 3 + 4 or ISUP Level ≥ 2), and 30-day complication rates. Secondary outcomes comprised cancer detection rate per core and pathological characteristics. Statistical analyses involved χ² tests, t-tests, and multivariable logistic regression.ResultsBaseline characteristics were comparable between groups, though prostate volume was larger in the COG-TBx group (52.0 ml vs. 46.0 ml, P = 0.024). The COG-TBx group demonstrated significantly higher PCa (52.2% vs. 41.2%, P = 0.034) and csPCa detection rates (44.7% vs. 25.6%, P < 0.05), as well as a higher cancer detection rate per needle (28.5% vs. 19.3%, P = 0.003). The overall 30-day complication rate was slightly higher in the COG-TBx group (13.0% vs. 7.0%, P > 0.05), without statistical significance. Multivariate analysis identified COG-TBx (OR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.15–3.41, P = 0.013) and PSA level (OR = 1.12 per ng/mL, 95% CI: 1.03–1.22, P = 0.008) as independent predictors of csPCa detection.ConclusionTransperineal COG-TBx is associated with higher detection rates for prostate cancer and clinically significant cancer compared to SBx. Although associated with a slightly higher complication rate, the difference was not significant. The overall benefit supports the clinical adoption of COG-TBx.

Introduction

Systematic biopsy (SBx) has traditionally been the gold standard for diagnosing prostate cancer, but its clinical application has significant limitations [1, 2]. Although 12-core systematic biopsy has largely replaced the traditional sextant biopsy, its random sampling strategy still cannot overcome the diagnostic bias caused by prostate tumor heterogeneity [3]. A long-term cohort study in Denmark involving 37,231 patients showed that the standardized incidence ratio of prostate cancer after an initial negative SBx was as high as 5.2 [4], suggesting that a significant number of clinically significant cancers may be missed, particularly for tumors in the anterior prostate or small tumors in large prostates, where SBx sensitivity is significantly inadequate. Meanwhile, the issue of overdiagnosis is equally prominent: the STHLM 3 MRI screening trial demonstrated that traditional PSA-based screening combined with SBx leads to the detection of a large number of indolent cancers (Gleason score 6), while the use of PSA density-optimized MRI reduces overdiagnosis by 17%, further confirming the limitations of SBx in distinguishing clinically significant from non-significant cancers [5]. This contradiction of coexisting underdiagnosis and overdiagnosis has prompted the academic community to seek more precise biopsy techniques.

Cognitive Fusion Targeted Biopsy (COG-TBx) leverages precise localization based on multi-parameter MRI (mpMRI) to provide a theoretical solution for overcoming the limitations of SBx [6, 7]. By fusing MRI-identified suspicious areas (PI-RADS score ≥ 3) with real-time ultrasound images, COG-TBx enables targeted sampling of high-risk lesions [8]. Studies have confirmed that for PI-RADS 5 lesions, COG-TBx achieves a clinically significant cancer detection rate of up to 90%, significantly outperforming the random sampling efficacy of SBx [9]. However, there is a notable gap in the existing evidence: most studies have focused on the transrectal approach, while the transperineally approach, as a lower-risk alternative (0% vs. 1.6% infection rate), lacks sufficient independent cohort comparison data with SBx [10]. Although recent studies show no statistically significant difference in the clinically significant cancer detection rate between transperineally and transrectal MRI-guided targeted biopsy (48.6% vs. 47.2%), these analyses did not specifically target the COG-TBx technique, and there is a lack of direct comparison with SBx in the same clinical setting, particularly regarding performance differences in biopsy screening or active surveillance populations [11–13].

This study aims to systematically evaluate the diagnostic efficacy and safety differences between COG-TBx and traditional SBx via the perineal approach through an independent cohort comparison. The primary study endpoints include overall prostate cancer (PCa) detection rate, clinically significant prostate cancer (CsPCa, defined as Gleason score ≥ 3 + 4), and 30-day complication incidence rate. By focusing on the specific technical scenario of the transperineal approach, this study fill the evidence gaps, clarifying whether COG-TBx can improve the accuracy of clinically significant cancer detection while reducing the risk of overdiagnosis associated with unnecessary biopsies, and validating its potential advantages in infection control. The study results has provided high-level evidence for optimizing prostate cancer biopsy strategies, particularly offering more specific practical guidance for transperineal biopsy techniques.

Materials and methods

Study and design

This study is a single-center prospective cohort study conducted at a tertiary referral center in China, spanning from April 2024 to June 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of our institution (Ethics Approval Number: [(2025) Tech No. 007]), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Patient selection

Inclusion criteria: Patients meeting any of the following conditions were included in the study: ① Age ≥ 45 years and serum PSA level ≥ 4.0 ng/mL; ② Age ≥ 45 years and a suspicious palpable nodule on DRE regardless of PSA level. Exclusion Criteria: ① Previously diagnosed prostate cancer; ② MRI contraindications (e.g., metal implants, claustrophobia); ③ Severe coagulation disorders (INR > 1.5 or platelet count < 100 × 10⁹/L); ④ Acute urinary tract infection or prostatitis; ⑤ Inability to cooperate with biopsy procedures.

Patients were divided into two groups based on biopsy method: the COG-TBx group (transperineal cognitive fusion targeted biopsy) and the SBx group (transperineal systematic biopsy). The assignment to the COG-TBx or SBx group was non-randomized and reflected real-world clinical practice. The decision was based on a combination of pre-biopsy mpMRI findings (the presence of a targetable lesion was a prerequisite for COG-TBx), clinician recommendation, and informed patient preference after detailed discussion of the benefits and risks of each approach.

Preoperative assessment

All patients undergo the following preoperative tests: ① Serum PSA testing (chemiluminescence method, detection limit 0.01 ng/mL); ② mpMRI examination using an MRI scanner ([Siemens Magneton Skyra 3 T device]), including sequences such as T2WI, DWI, and DCE-MRI. All images are independently evaluated by two radiologists with more than 5 years of experience in prostate imaging. Any discrepancies in PI-RADS v2.1 scoring are resolved by a third senior radiologist. The mpMRI must be performed within 1 month prior to prostate biopsy; ③ Transrectal ultrasound (TRUS) measurement of prostate volume (formula: length × width × height × 0.52).

Biopsy procedure

No prophylactic antibiotics were administered preoperatively to any patient. All patients received a uniform prophylactic medication regimen after surgery: a single intravenous infusion of the hemostatic drug tranexamic acid (1 g), and a single oral anti-infective drug levofloxacin (500 mg). The biopsy procedure was performed in an ultrasound operating room by two sonographers with over one year of experience in standard transperineal prostate biopsy and familiarity with prostate MRI imaging. Anesthesia was administered via perineal local infiltration anesthesia (2% lidocaine, 10 mL). COG-TBx Group: For lesions with a preoperative mpMRI PI-RADS score ≥ 3, transperineal midline needle placement was performed under real-time ultrasound guidance using cognitive fusion technology (offline image registration of ultrasound and MRI). An 18G automatic biopsy gun (22 mm core length) was used to place 3 needles per target lesion. The total number of cores obtained per patient was therefore variable and dependent on the number of lesions with a PI-RADS score ≥ 3. The SBx group underwent 12 systematic needles, sampling according to prostate anatomical zones (base, body, apex; 2 needles per side), using the same equipment as the COG-TBx group. Postoperatively, all patients were instructed to increase fluid intake and avoid strenuous exercise for one week.

Outcome measures

Primary Outcomes: ① PCa Detection Rate: Proportion of patients with pathologically confirmed prostate cancer; ②CsPCa Detection Rate : Proportion of patients with pathologically confirmed Gleason score ≥ 3 + 4 (ISUP Level ≥ 2) or maximum tumor diameter ≥ 6 mm (according to International Society of Urological Pathology standards); ③30-day complication rate: Record any of the following complications that occur within 30 days after biopsy: Infection: Defined as postoperative fever (body temperature > 38 °C) requiring medical intervention. Hematuria: Defined as postoperative visible blood in the urine. Hematoma: Defined as a hematoma in the perineum or around the rectum confirmed by clinical examination or imaging. Secondary outcome indicators: ① Cancer detection rate per specimen (cancer detection rate per core).

Pathological assessment

All biopsy specimens were reviewed by two urological pathologists (with > 10 years of experience) in a double-blind manner, recording the Gleason score, tumor length, number of positive cores, and presence of ductal carcinoma in situ. In cases of diagnostic discrepancy, the final result was determined through review by the pathology committee.

Statistical analysis

Analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 software. Sample size calculation was based on the primary outcome CsPCa: assuming a CsPCa of 25% in the SBx group and 40% in the COG-TBx group, with α = 0.05 (two-sided) and β = 0.2, each group required at least 156 cases. Considering a 10% loss to follow-up, the final plan was to include 360 cases.

Preliminary analysis: continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (normal distribution) or median (interquartile range), and intergroup comparisons were performed using t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests; categorical variables were expressed as frequency (percentage), and comparisons were performed using χ² tests or Fisher’s exact probability tests.

To control for potential confounding bias caused by non-random grouping, we conducted 1:1 propensity score matching as a sensitivity analysis. The matching variables included age, PSA level, prostate volume, the highest PI-RADS score (as an ordinal categorical variable, divided into 3 points, 4 points, and 5 points), biopsy type (initial biopsy vs. repeat biopsy), and operator experience (operated by physicians with more than 100 punctures vs. others). The propensity score was calculated using Logistic regression, and matching was performed using the nearest neighbor method with a cutoff value of 0.02. Post-matching, the primary outcome indicators were compared using paired t-test/Wilcoxon signed-rank test (for continuous variables) and McNemar test/paired χ² test (for categorical variables). The balance of all covariates before and after matching was evaluated using standardized mean difference (SMD), and an SMD < 0.1 was considered as good balance between groups.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent factors influencing CsPCa (adjusted for age, PSA, and prostate volume). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient flow and baseline characteristics

This study screened 395 patients from April 2024 to June 2025, with 360 eligible participants ultimately enrolled, including 161 in the COG-TBx group and 199 in the SBx group (Fig. 1). Exclusion criteria primarily included MRI contraindications (n = 10), coagulation disorders (n = 12), and refusal of biopsy (n = 18). Baseline characteristics were generally balanced between the two groups, as detailed in Table 1. There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of median age, serum PSA levels and highest PI-RADS score (P > 0.05). Notably, the prostate volume was slightly larger in the COG-TBx group (46.0 ml vs. 52.0 ml), with the difference reaching statistical significance (P = 0.024).

Complication rates

The 30-day overall complication rate was slightly higher in the COG-TBx group than in the SBx group (13.0% vs. 7.0%, P > 0.05). The infection rate was higher in the COG-TBx group than in the SBx group (4.3% vs. 2.5%), but the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.370). All the recorded infections were mild to moderate in severity and were relieved after oral or short-term intravenous antibiotic treatment. No severe infections, sepsis, or infection-related readmission cases occurred during the study period. There were no significant differences between the two groups in the incidence of hematuria and hematoma (P > 0.05 for both). The specific distribution of complications is shown in Table 2.

Secondary outcome measures

The cancer detection rate per needle was significantly higher in the COG-TBx group than in the SBx group (28.5% vs. 19.3%, P = 0.003) (Table 2).

Pathological feature analysis

There was a significant difference in the distribution of Gleason scores between the two groups of confirmed patients (P = 0.002). In the COG-TBx group, the proportion of Gleason scores of 3 + 4 or higher was 44.7% (72/161), higher than the 25.6% (51/199) in the SBx group. (Table 2).

Propensity scores matching analysis

After conducting 1:1 propensity score matching considering age, PSA, prostate volume, highest PI-RADS score, biopsy type, and operator experience, 115 pairs of patients were successfully matched. After the matching, both groups achieved good balance in all included covariates (all SMD < 0.1, Table 3). In this more balanced cohort, the overall PCa detection rate in the COG-TBx group (52.2% vs. 39.1%, P = 0.032) and the csPCa detection rate (43.5% vs. 21.7%, P = 0.011) were still significantly higher than those in the SBx group. The advantage in the per-puncture detection rate (28.8% vs. 18.7%, P = 0.002) remained unchanged. The inter-group difference in complication occurrence rate was still not statistically significant (12.2% vs. 8.7%, P = 0.372). This analysis confirmed that even after controlling for a wider range of clinically relevant factors, the diagnostic advantage of COG-TBx remained independent and robust.

Multivariate regression analysis

To identify the independent predictors of csPCa, we established a multivariate model. In addition to the grouping variables, this model also adjusted for age, PSA level, prostate volume and highest PI-RADS score. The results showed that the biopsy method (COG-TBx vs. SBx), PSA level and the PI-RADS score were independent predictors of csPCa. Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that biopsy method (COG-TBx vs. SBx: OR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.15–3.41, P = 0.013), PSA (per 1 ng/ml increase) (OR = 1.12 per ng/mL, 95% CI: 1.03–1.22, P = 0.008) and the PI-RADS score (OR = 1.82 per point increase, 95% CI: 1.45–2.29, P < 0.001) were independent predictors of clinically significant cancer detection (Table 4).

Discussion

This prospective study demonstrated that the transperineal COG-TBx approach was associated with significantly higher overall prostate cancer detection rates, clinically significant prostate cancer detection rates, and cancer detection rates per core compared to transperineal SBx. These diagnostic advantages were maintained despite the COG-TBx group having significantly larger prostate volumes at baseline (52.0 mL vs. 46.0 mL, P = 0.024), a clinical scenario known to challenge systematic biopsy due to sampling density issues. The consistent superiority of COG-TBx after propensity score matching balancing for prostate volume and other covariates (csPCa detection: 45.2% vs. 29.6%, P = 0.011) further supports its particular value in patients with larger prostates. Although the COG-TBx group showed a numerically higher 30-day complication rate (13.0% vs. 7.0%), this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.092) and did not diminish its diagnostic superiority. These findings contribute meaningful evidence supporting the clinical adoption of COG-TBx, with special relevance for patients with larger prostate volumes where traditional systematic biopsy may be suboptimal.

The overall PCa (52.2% vs. 41.2%) and CsPCa (44.7% vs. 25.6%) in the COG-TBx group were significantly higher than those in the SBx group, consistent with the previously reported advantages of targeted biopsy [14, 15]. Notably, the COG-TBx group had larger prostate volumes (52.0 ml vs. 46.0 ml, P = 0.024) in this study, and SBx may lead to missed diagnoses in larger prostates due to insufficient sampling density, suggesting that COG-TBx may overcome this limitation through precise targeting. This result extends the conclusions of previous studies—even in cases of larger prostate volumes, MRI-guided targeted biopsy can maintain high diagnostic efficacy, potentially due to the focused sampling of suspicious lesions enabled by cognitive fusion technology, reducing the blindness of random sampling [16].

In terms of CsPCa, the advantage of the COG-TBx group (44.7% vs. 25.6%) was statistically significant (P < 0.05) despite the small numerical difference. Combined with the significant difference in the proportion of Gleason scores ≥ 3 + 4 in pathological feature analysis (P = 0.002), this indicates that COG-TBx not only improves cancer detection rates but also effectively identifies high-risk tumors with clinical intervention value. This result supports the theoretical advantage of targeted biopsy in reducing overdiagnosis while lowering the risk of missed diagnoses.

In this study, the cancer detection rate per needle for COG-TBx (28.5%) was significantly higher than that for SBx (19.3%), consistent with the reported efficacy of targeted biopsy in international multicenter studies (25%−35%), further validating the high efficiency of targeted sampling [17]. However, unlike previous studies, the overall complication rate at 30 days in the COG-TBx group (13.0%) was slightly higher than that in the SBx group (7.0%), particularly the infection rate (4.3% vs. 2.5%), significantly higher than the range reported in Rai et al.‘s 2021 systematic review for transperineal biopsy (0–1.6%) [12]. Though this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.092), but the trend is noteworthy [18]. This might be related to the relatively broad definition of infection we used (including any cases with a body temperature >38 °C and those treated with antibiotics). Additionally, our antibiotic prophylaxis protocol (providing a single dose only after surgery) differs from some of the recommended preoperative administration protocols in guidelines, which could also be an influencing factor. However, all infections were effectively controlled, and there were no serious sequelae, confirming the safety advantage of the perineal approach. This phenomenon also may be related to the needle path adjustments required for precise sampling or the more frequent punctures in the COG-TBx group [19]. However, it is worth noting that the difference between the two mainly stems from mild, self-limiting hematuria and hematoma, while in the more concerning infectious complications (fever), the absolute difference is small (4.3% vs. 2.5%) and not statistically significant. Future studies should include a larger sample size to more accurately assess the potential differences in safety between the two techniques [20, 21]. Compared with the recently published PREVENT trial, the perineal infection rate reported in this study was significantly lower than the findings observed in our study [10]. This suggests that other confounding factors may be present in the current infection prevention protocols or population characteristics of this study. Further optimization of perioperative management and validation of its applicability are warranted. Additionally, this study found that the prostate volume was significantly larger in the COG-TBx group (P = 0.024), differing from the balanced baseline design in most studies [22, 23]. The observed trend towards a higher overall complication rate in the COG-TBx group, despite a comparable or lower total number of cores in some patients, may be attributable to the larger prostate volumes in this group and the inherent technical complexity of targeted sampling. Precise needle placement for anterior or apical lesions often requires traversing more tissue and may involve concentrated sampling within a specific region, potentially increasing local tissue trauma compared to the standardized, distributed pattern of systematic biopsy. This “imbalance” actually highlights the clinical utility of COG-TBx-in clinical practice, patients with large prostate volumes often face diagnostic challenges due to insufficient SBx sampling, yet this study shows that COG-TBx maintains its advantage even in such populations, providing guidance for real-world applications.

The strengths of this study include: a large sample size (n = 360) encompassing patients with diverse clinical characteristics; second, a strict definition of CsPCa (Gleason ≥ 3 + 4), combined with multivariate regression analysis to control for confounding factors (e.g., PSA levels), enhancing the reliability of the results; and, for the first time, a focus on the impact of prostate volume differences on biopsy efficacy, providing evidence for individualized treatment selection. However, this study also has limitations: firstly, its non-randomized design and the self-assignment of patients based on personal preferences and clinical doctor’s recommendations inevitably introduce the risk of selection bias. Although we used propensity score matching and multivariate regression analysis to try to control for known confounding variables, we could not rule out the residual confounding caused by unmeasured factors (such as detailed family history, the interval between MRI and biopsy, or the patient’s comorbidity index). Secondly, this is a study conducted in a single center. Our operational procedures, patient population, and regional infection prevention strategies may limit the generalizability of the research results. In particular, the infection rate observed in this study requires further validation with multi-center data. Finally, the follow-up period of this study is relatively short, and it is unable to assess the differences in long-term tumor control (such as biochemical recurrence rate) between the two biopsy strategies. Despite these limitations, through a prospective design, a large sample size, and statistical correction for key variables (including operator experience), we believe that this study provides strong real-world evidence for the clinical value of perineal cognitive fusion targeted biopsy. In addition, this study adopted a “group comparison” design, assigning patients to different biopsy strategy groups. Although this design cannot conduct direct comparisons at the individual level like “combined biopsy’’ (where all patients undergo targeted and systemic biopsies simultaneously), it is more in line with the real clinical decision-making scenario, where doctors and patients need to choose an initial biopsy path based on the available information. It can answer the practical question of ‘for a suspected prostate cancer patient seeking medical attention, which biopsy strategy should be initially selected?‘. Moreover, this design avoids the problems caused by combined biopsy, such as excessive number of biopsy needles, prolonged surgery time, and increased risks of related complications.

Based on the results of this study, COG-TBx can be used as the preferred biopsy strategy for patients with large prostates, as its precise targeting capability can compensate for the insufficient sampling of SBx in this population. Future studies could explore whether optimizing the perioperative antibiotic regimen, for instance by adjusting it based on regional resistance patterns, could further minimize the already low infection risk associated with the transperineal approach. Future research should focus on: first, it is necessary to conduct multicenter randomized controlled trials with long-term patient follow-up, focusing on evaluating differences between COG-TBx and SBx in long-term oncological endpoints such as biochemical recurrence rate, metastasis incidence, and tumor-specific survival. Additionally, a systematic comparison should be made of infection prevention strategies—such as antibiotic regimens optimized based on regional resistance patterns—to assess their effectiveness in controlling infections following transperineal biopsy. Second, an individualized decision model should be developed based on prostate volume, MRI PI-RADS scores, and biopsy strategies to assist clinicians in selecting the most appropriate biopsy approach for different patients. Finally, further exploration of novel technologies such as AI-assisted cognitive fusion localization or robot-assisted transperineal biopsy platforms is warranted to reduce needle counts and procedure-related complications while maintaining detection efficacy. Accumulating long-term follow-up data will be crucial for validating the long-term efficacy and safety of these emerging techniques.

Conclusion

In summary, the transperineal COG-TBx approach was associated with superior detection rates for prostate cancer and clinically significant cancer compared to SBx, and this association was consistent in patients with larger prostates. Although the incidence of complications is slightly higher, it does not reach a significant difference. The overall benefit-risk ratio supports its clinical application.
