Effects of bio-slurry and chemical fertilizer on soil fertility and productivity of bread wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) in Northwestern Ethiopia
Authors: Zerubabel Moges Amare, Fenta Assefa, Teferi Alem, Yezbie Kassa, Hunachew Andualem, Dessie Tibebe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDeclining soil fertility and rising mineral fertilizer costs severely constrain wheat production in Ethiopia, threatening food security. Bio-slurry from biogas digesters offers a potential low-cost organic amendment, but limited field evidence exists on its integrated use with chemical fertilizers.MethodsA two-year field experiment (2023/2024–2024/2025) was conducted at the University of Gondar Shinta Research Farm, northwestern Ethiopia. Six treatments: control (no amendment), 100% chemical fertilizer (CF: 100 kg N ha⁻¹ as urea + 100 kg NPS ha⁻¹), 75% CF + 25% bio-slurry (BS), 50% CF + 50% BS, 25% CF + 75% BS, and 100% BS (10 t ha⁻¹ dry weight) were arranged in a randomized complete block design with three replications. Wheat agronomic parameters, grain yield, soil physicochemical properties, and heavy metal concentrations (Pb, Cd, Cr) were analyzed using standard methods.ResultsIntegrated BS and CF applications significantly improved all agronomic parameters compared to sole CF or control (p < 0.05). The 25% CF + 75% BS treatment produced the highest aboveground dry biomass (9.5–11.1 t ha⁻¹) and grain yield (2.8–4.4 t ha⁻¹), representing 40–60% increases over sole CF. Post-harvest soil analysis revealed significant increases (p < 0.05) in pH (from 5.6 to 8.0), organic carbon (from 1.8% to 15.8%), organic matter (from 3.2% to 27.5%), total nitrogen (from 1.0% to 2.2%), and available phosphorus (from 1.4% to 1.9%) under BS-amended treatments. Heavy metals (Pb, Cd, Cr) in both soil and wheat grain remained below detection limits, confirming food safety.ConclusionPartial substitution of chemical fertilizer with bio-slurry at 25% CF + 75% BS enhances wheat productivity and soil fertility while reducing mineral fertilizer dependence by 75%. This integrated nutrient management strategy offers a sustainable, economically viable approach for smallholder farmers in Ethiopian highlands, supporting circular economy principles through organic waste recycling.

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is one of the most important cereal crops grown in Ethiopia and across Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), with over 10 million hectares under production [1]. Following South Africa, Ethiopia is the second-largest wheat producer in Africa [2]. Despite its strategic importance for food security, wheat productivity in Ethiopia remains low. This is mainly attributed to several interrelated constraints, including depletion of soil fertility and soil acidity [3–5], continuous nutrient off-take by crops, mono-cropping in major wheat-growing areas, insufficient supply of improved seed varieties, improper fertilizer use, and low fertilizer use efficiency [6].

Ethiopia’s agricultural development strategy emphasizes increasing smallholder productivity while simultaneously reducing natural resource degradation [7]. The application of chemical fertilizers has been a key approach for improving crop yields, farm income, and overall agricultural productivity [5, 6, 8]. However, growing concerns related to the cost, environmental impact, and long-term sustainability of chemical fertilizers have encouraged interest in alternative nutrient management strategies. Organic fertilizers are often favored because they are environmentally friendly, relatively simple to apply, economically accessible, and socially acceptable [9]. In line with the principles of sustainable agriculture, reduced reliance on external inputs and improved utilization of locally available resources are increasingly promoted [10]. The application of organic amendments like compost and bio-slurry have been shown to significantly enhance crop productivity while improving soil fertility and biological activity [11]. Comparative studies evaluating chemical fertilizers, organic fertilizers, and their integrated application consistently report high yield performance under organic and integrated nutrient management systems [12].

The use of chemical fertilizer is not only cost-intensive but also associated with potential risks to human health and the environment [13]. Therefore, the identification and promotion of safer and more affordable alternatives, such as compost and bio-slurry, are strongly encouraged [14]. The shift toward organic nutrient sources is particularly attractive when low-cost materials are locally available, such as the bio-slurry generated from biogas digesters. At the University of Gondar, a pilot-scale biogas digester provides a readily accessible source of bio-slurry that could substantially reduce farmers’ dependence on expensive mineral fertilizers. Nevertheless, widespread adoption of bio-slurry by farmers and agricultural stakeholders requires robust scientific evidence demonstrating its agronomic benefits, particularly its effect on crop yield improvement [15].

Declining soil fertility is a global challenge driven by nutrient mining, salinity buildup, soil erosion, and intensive land use [16]. At the same time, increased application of chemical fertilizers under intensified agricultural systems has resulted in adverse environmental consequences, prompting global efforts to mitigate the negative impacts of chemical-based farming [17]. In Ethiopia, wheat production predominantly depends on di-ammonium phosphate (DAP) and urea. Nevertheless, many smallholder farmers cannot afford the recommended application rates of these fertilizers. Moreover, these fertilizers often fail to supply a balanced spectrum of macro- and micronutrients required for optimal crop growth. Due to economic constraints, farmers frequently apply suboptimal fertilizer rates, which limits yield potential [18]. As a result, supplementing mineral fertilizers with organic amendments has become increasingly important to enhance soil fertility through improvements in soil microbial biomass, nutrient cycling, and overall soil health [19].

Integrated application of chemical fertilizers and organic amendments offers a promising approach to overcoming these limitations. Despite the potential benefits of bio-slurry for sustainable agriculture, several critical research gaps remain unaddressed in Ethiopian wheat systems. First, limited field-based evidence exists on the independent and combined effects of bio-slurry and chemical fertilizers specifically under highland conditions (> 2000 m a.s.l.), where wheat is predominantly cultivated. Second, no systematic studies have determined the optimal substitution rate of chemical fertilizer with bio-slurry for maximizing bread wheat productivity in this agroecology. Third, the food safety implications, particularly heavy metal accumulation in wheat grain from bio-slurry application remain unknown, creating potential barriers to farmer adoption. Fourth, practical knowledge regarding integrated bio-slurry use is limited among smallholder farmers and agricultural extension agents, hindering technology transfer.

To address these gaps, this study was guided by the following research questions:

Therefore, the objective of this study was to conduct a two-year field experiment to evaluate the effects of sole and integrated application of bio-slurry and chemical fertilizer on wheat agronomic performance, soil physicochemical properties, and nutrient status. We hypothesized that partial substitution of chemical fertilizer with bio-slurry would enhance wheat productivity and improve soil fertility without compromising environmental and food safety. This study aims to provide scientific evidence to support integrated nutrient management strategies for sustainable wheat production in Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

The experiment was conducted at the Shinta Horticultural Research and Demonstration site, College of Agriculture and Environmental Science, University of Gondar, central Gondar zone, Ethiopia in the main cropping season from June to December in 2023/24 to 2024/25. The experimental field is located at 12′0 36′ 0”N latitude, 37′0 28′0″E longitude, and an altitude of 2,133 m.a.s.l. The soil of the experimental field is clay loam with a pH of 6.1 [20, 21]. The experimental area has a unimodal rainfall distribution pattern with the main rainy season with a total mean annual rainfall of 1240 mm [22] (Fig. 1).

Experimental treatments, design and procedures

The experiment was conducted over two consecutive cropping years (2023/2024 and 2024/2025) using RCBD with three replications and six treatments. The treatments were T1=Control (untreated), T2=100% CF, T3 = 75% CF + 25% BS, T4= 50% CF + 50% BS, T5 = 25% CF + 75% BS and T6=100% BS. The total experimental area was 164 m2, comprised of 18 plots. The gross plot size was 3 m×2 m (6 m2), with a spacing of 20 cm between rows (10 rows per plot), 50 cm between plots, and 1 m between blocks. The experimental treatments were randomly assigned to plots within each block.

The land was initially prepared using the local oxen-drawn plough, known as ‘Maresha’. The field was plowed three times: the first in early May, the second on June 1st, and the final plowing on July 6th in both years. Following plowing, the land was leveled and smoothed using manual human labor and hand tools. The amendment (initial soil preparation/incorporation) was conducted around the same date [11]. The tested crop variety was “Boro” bread wheat (a variety widely grown by smallholder farmers in the study area), which was sourced from the Gondar Agricultural Research Center (GARC) [23].

The fertilizer sources used were dry bio-slurry (DBS) and chemical fertilizer (CF). The bio-slurry was the byproduct of an anaerobic biogas digester installed at the University of Gondar Shinta agricultural research farm, resulting from the co-digestion of soke plant and cow dung. The bio-slurry was weighed according to the treatment plan and incorporated into the experimental field 21 days before sowing in both years [11]. The appropriate ratio of Urea (as part of the CF treatment) was incorporated later as a side dressing during the tillering stage. Standard recommended rates were followed for fertilizer application to study the integrated effect of chemical fertilizers and bio-slurry on wheat crop and soil health, as described by reference [6].

Apparatus and instruments

Samples of soil, bio-slurry, wheat and other materials & equipment were used in this investigation. Samples of soil, bio-slurry and wheat were placed within labeled zippered polyethylene (PE) bags, ensuring their protection from potential contamination, and promptly taken to the laboratory for subsequent analysis as described by reference [24]. The instruments such as, analytical balance (JA203H, 200 g, China), Rapid moisture and protein analyzer (IM9500, India), beakers (Boron silicate China), desiccators (CG-8272-R250, China), crucible (RO, 5200, India), Hot plate (SH3, STERILINE LTD, UK), Universal oven (AC-366 A, India), pH meter (H160, Hach, Germany), conductivity meter (Orion Model 145, USA), muffle furnace (BIBBY, R0000, UK), thermometer (Straight H=150 mm BSPP 1/2, US), funnel, electric grinder (IAK–WERKE, Germany), instruments such as FAAS (Buck Scientific, 210VGP, USA), Kjeldahl apparatus (KI-26, Gerhardt, Germany), were employed.

Chemicals and reagents

The experiment employed de-ionized water and all of the chemicals and reagents were analytical grade. Sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 98%; India), potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7, 99.8%, India), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30%; Okhla industrial area, India), nitric acid (HNO3, 69–70%; Loba Chemie, Mumbai, India) and hydrochloric acid (HCl, 70%, Blulux laboratories, Haryana, India), were employed to digest the sample matrix.

Optimization of metals digestion procedure

The wet acid digestion procedure was systematically optimized to ensure the efficient extraction of free metal ions from the organic matrix of the wheat grains, following the methodology described in reference [25]. This method, which utilizes the oxidative power of a HNO3 and H2O2 acid mixture, is a widely recognized technique for decomposing complex biological samples [26]. The optimization involved varying three critical parameters: reagent volume ratio, digestion temperature, and digestion time. The success criterion for complete digestion was a clear and colorless resulting solution, which signifies the full mineralization of the organic matter. A series of two trials were conducted using different HNO3: H2O2 ratios, temperatures, and time intervals. The outcomes of these trials were evaluated based on the resulting solution’s visual clarity, digest stability, and minimal reagent use. The primary goal of the trials was to identify digestion conditions that achieved complete mineralization while maximizing time and resource efficiency. The optimized digestion condition was successfully determined to be a 3:2 volume ratio of HNO3 (69–70%) to H2O2 (30%), a digestion temperature of 120℃, and a total digestion time of 2.5 h (two and a half hours) [27]. This specific set of conditions provided consistent, reproducible results with clear solutions, making them highly suitable for trace metal determination using Flame Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy (FAAS), which confirms alignment with established methods [26].

Analytical procedures

Physicochemical analysis

The pH of soil samples was determined by the FAO protocol of the soil-water suspension system with a 1:2.5 w/v ratio [28], using the digital pH meter. Electrical conductivity (EC) of the soil samples was determined by the FAO protocol [29]. Soil samples were dissolved with deionized water with a 1:5 w/v ratio and measured by an EC meter after 1 h of continuous shaking. The moisture content of the soil sample was determined by the gravimetric method allows the quantification of the soil moisture content based on the loss of weight (mass) due to the loss of water by heating the soil to a temperature of 105 °C ± 5 °C until constant mass is achieved. Soil moisture is expressed as a percentage of the mass of water in a given soil mass (e.g., grams of water per 100 g of oven-dry soil) [30]. Before analyzing air-dry moisture content, the sample has been pre-treated by drying to a constant mass in an oven at 30 °C. Digestion of samples for metal analysis

Wheat samples were digested according to the method described in [26] with minor modifications (optimized digestion procedure). Briefly, 1.0 g of the finely powdered wheat sample was transferred into a 100 mL conical flask, followed by the addition of 5 mL of a HNO3 (69–70%), HClO4 (70%) mixture (3:2, v/v). The mixture was digested at 120 °C for 2:30 h. on a hot plate [31]. For bio-slurry, 1.0 g of dried sample was digested in a 100 mL conical flask using 7 mL of an acid mixture consisting of 5 mL HNO3 (69–70%) and 2 mL HClO4 (70%). Digestion was carried out on a hot plate at 160 °C for 2 h under a fume hood until a clear solution was obtained. Soil samples were digested following FAO guidelines [32]. Precisely 1.0 g of air-dried and 2 mm-sieved soil sample was placed in a 100 mL conical flask and treated with 10 mL of 37% HCl and 5 mL of 65% HNO3. The mixture was allowed to stand overnight without heating. Subsequently, 3 mL of HClO4 (70%) was added, and the mixture was heated gradually at 100–180 °C for 4 h until dryness. After cooling, 25 mL of 5% HCl was added, reheated for 10 min. The digested wheat, bio-slurry, and soil samples were cooled at room temperature, followed by added around 20 mL of de-ionized water. Finally, filter and adjust very precisely to 50 mL with deionized water in a volumetric flask and store at 4 °C refrigerator until analysis is made. Macro and trace metal concentrations (Ca, Mg, K, Zn, Fe, Cu, Mn, Ni, Cr, and Pb) in soil, bio-slurry and wheat samples were determined using Flame Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometry (FAAS). Blank samples were digested and analyzed following the same procedure to ensure analytical accuracy and quality control.

Nutrient analysis in wheat

Total nitrogen (TN) determination of the sample was computed using the Kjeldahl Method [35]. Precisely, 10 ml of an aliquot was placed on the Kjeldahl ammonium distillation device, and 10 ml of a 40% (NaOH) solution was added. The flask was then quickly connected to the distillation apparatus, as per reference [36].

Phosphorus was determined by combining 10 ml of digested liquid and 10 ml of coloured reagent (Molybdate Vanadate solution) were combined with in a 50 ml volumetric flask, and 30 ml of de-ionized water. The levels of phosphorus were examined using a Double-beam UV-Vis spectrophotometer at 420 nm wavelength according to the method described by reference [37]. C/N was calculated using the ratio b/n percentage of nitrogen, and percentage of carbon.

Organic carbon in wheat sample was measured using established colorimetric techniques [38].

Sulfur (S) content was determined from wheat extracts using the mono-calcium phosphate extraction method, and quantified by spectrophotometry at 440 nm, following the procedure of [39].

Data collected and measurement

All parameters related to wheat yield and growths were recorded. For above ground biomass yield and grain yield, data were collected from the net plot area (1.6 m × 2.6 m) to eliminate border effects [6]. The harvested above-ground plant material was subsequently dried in an oven at 70 ℃ for 72 hours. Straw yield was calculated by taking the difference between the total above-ground biomass and the grain yield for the respective treatment. Several individual plant parameters, including plant height, effective tillers, total tillers, spike length, and non-effective tiller were recorded from ten randomly tagged plants selected from the central rows of each plot at the physiological stage. Spike length (SL) was specifically measured as the average length from the base to the tip (excluding awns) of the 10 random samples. The number of tillers and number of seeds per spike were also determined by counting the plants within a 1 m2 area of each plot [24]. Number of seeds per spike, straw yield, harvested index, grain yield, above ground biomass yield and thousand seed weight (TSW) were measured at the maturation date. Thousand seed weight (TSW) was measured by counting one thousand seeds sampled randomly from the total grain harvest of the experimental plot and weighing them using a sensitive electronic balance as adopted from reference [40].

Analytical method validation

The performance of the digestion and analytical methods employed for soil and dry wheat samples was rigorously evaluated by assessing key parameters: detection limits (LOD), accuracy, precision, and sensitivity [31]. Accuracy was established by spiking the samples with known standard solutions, while precision was determined using the percent relative standard deviation (%RSD) calculated from the values obtained through triplicate tests on a given sample for a specific metal. The Limit of Detection (LOD) was determined by measuring three blank samples (digested and analyzed in triplicate alongside each sample) [41], and was computed by dividing the slope of the calibration equation by the average blank signal plus three times its standard deviation (LOD = 10 × SD). Finally, the Limit of Quantification (LOQ) was set as three times the LOD (LOQ = 3 × LOD) [42]. This systematic validation ensured the generation of reliable and high-quality data.

Precision and accuracy

The precision and accuracy of the analytical method used for determining heavy metals in the soil and wheat samples were accurately validated using the matrix spike recovery analysis technique as described by reference [6]. Examination of the validation data confirmed the method’s high performance: the achieved recovery values ranged from 95.5% to 105%, while the corresponding percentage relative standard deviations (%RSD) remained consistently below 5.56% across all samples. These matrix spike recovery values align favorably with the established benchmark of 80% to 120% for robust recovery assessments [43]. The notably high percentage of recovery achieved in this study substantiates the method’s accuracy and reliability in precisely determining metal concentrations within both the soil and wheat samples, which were the focal subjects of the analysis [26].

Statistical analysis

The Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was performed for each measured parameter in different seasons using the General Linear Model (GLM) procedure within the Statistical Analysis System (SAS) software version 9.4 [44]. To differentiate between treatment means when a significant F-test was obtained, the Least Significant Difference (LSD) test was used. Significant differences between treatments were identified at the 5% significance level (p < 0.05). Additionally, Pearson’s simple linear correlation coefficient values were computed using the mean values to examine the relationships among the agronomic components.

The mean soil pH was 6.13 ± 0.38, indicating a slightly acidic condition. Moisture content differed significantly (p < 0.05) between soil (6 ± 0.5%) and bio-slurry (7.1 ± 0.2%). Organic matter content was markedly higher in bio-slurry (23.7 ± 2%) than in soil (3.4 ± 1%). Electrical conductivity was also higher in bio-slurry (34 ± 0.1 µS cm⁻¹) compared with soil (30.9 ± 0.5 µS cm⁻¹). Total nitrogen, available phosphorus, and organic carbon were higher in bio-slurry, while available sulfur was slightly higher in soil.

The concentrations of metals in pre-experimental soil followed the order Fe > Mg > Ca > Mn > Ni > Zn > Cu (Table 1). Cadmium (Cd), Lead (Pb), and Chromium (Cr) were below detection limits, while Nickel (Ni) concentration was relatively high (89.4 ± 3 mg kg⁻¹) (Table 3).

Effects of bio-slurry and Chemical fertilizer on metal concentrations in wheat grain

The analysis of macro and micro metal content in the wheat grain samples (Table 8) revealed substantial variability across treatments, which is primarily influenced by the type and rate of fertilizer applied. Significant difference was observed in Calcium (Ca) concentrations, ranging from 7.6 ± 0.15 to 11.9 ± 0.5 mg/kg, and wide variations were seen in Iron (Fe) and Manganese (Mn), ranging from 213.6 ± 4.7 to 301.1 ± 5.9 mg/kg and 121.9 ± 2.1 to 167.9 ± 2.63 mg/kg, respectively (Table 8).

Across all treatments, the mean concentrations for key metals were identified as: Ca (10.1 ± 0.31 mg/kg), Mg (35.7 ± 0.4 mg/kg), K (44.2 ± 1.2 mg/kg), Fe (247.5 ± 6.1 mg/kg), Mn (139.0 ± 2.12 mg/kg), Zn (15.7 ± 0.52 mg/kg), Cu (16.8 ± 0.43 mg/kg), and Ni (20.3 ± 3.8 mg/kg). Notably, the highest concentrations of essential metals (Zn, Cu, Fe, and Mn) in the wheat tissues were obtained from the T6 (100% bio-slurry) application, while the untreated control (T1) and sole chemical fertilizer (T2) treatments yielded the lowest concentrations.

Discussion

The significant increase in soil pH observed in bio-slurry-amended plots (T3–T6) aligns with the well-documented liming effect of organic amendments. The observed increase in soil pH under bio-slurry treatments aligns with the liming effect theory, whereby organic amendments release basic cations (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺) during decomposition while consuming H⁺ through decarboxylation of organic acids [50]. This mechanism can be exemplified by the reaction: R-COOH + OH⁻ → R-COO⁻ + H₂O, which effectively neutralizes soil acidity. In contrast, the acidifying effect of sole chemical fertilizer (T2: pH 5.6) follows the nitrification theory: NH₄⁺ + 2O₂ → NO₃⁻ + 2 H⁺ + H₂O, where each mole of ammonium nitrified produces two moles of H⁺, explaining the lower pH under 100% CF treatment [51].

Bio-slurry typically contains alkaline compounds and basic cations (e.g., Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺) that neutralize exchangeable acidity, while microbial decomposition releases OH⁻ ions through decarboxylation reactions [50]. In contrast, the acidifying effect of sole chemical fertilizer (T2) is attributed to the nitrification of ammonium-based urea, which releases H⁺ ions, a common phenomenon in intensive cropping systems [51]. The rise in pH under integrated treatments is agronomically beneficial in acidic highland soils of Ethiopia, as it enhances phosphorus availability and reduces aluminum toxicity [52, 50].

The substantial increases in organic carbon (OC) and organic matter (OM) in plots receiving bio-slurry reflect the direct input of carbon-rich organic materials. The substantial increase in soil organic carbon (from 2.3% to 15.8%) under 100% BS reflects the humification theory, where partial decomposition of organic residues leads to formation of stable humic substances resistant to further mineralization. According to humification theory, the humification coefficient for anaerobically digested materials ranges from 0.3 to 0.5, meaning 30–50% of applied organic carbon can persist in soil. This explains why even a single season of bio-slurry application (10 t ha⁻¹) produced measurable increases in soil OC.

The highest OC (15.8%) and OM (27.5%) under T6 (100% BS) can be attributed to the large quantity of stable organic compounds added, which are not fully mineralized within a single cropping season. Such improvements in soil organic matter are critical for long-term soil health, as they enhance aggregate stability, water-holding capacity, and cation exchange capacity, thereby improving the soil’s resilience to degradation [11]. Notably, even the integrated treatments (e.g., T5: 25% CF + 75% BS) resulted in OC and OM levels comparable to sole BS, indicating that partial substitution does not compromise soil organic matter buildup.

The enhancement in total nitrogen (TN) and available phosphorus (Av. P) under integrated treatments (T4, T5, T6) demonstrates a synergistic nutrient supply mechanism. Chemical fertilizers provide readily available N and P, meeting the crop’s immediate demand, while bio-slurry contributes to a slow-release nutrient pool through microbial mineralization. Additionally, organic acids released during bio-slurry decomposition can solubilize fixed soil phosphorus, increasing its availability [53]. This integrated approach effectively addresses both short-term crop needs and long-term soil fertility, making it a sustainable alternative to sole mineral fertilization, particularly in resource-limited smallholder systems [12, 50].

The superior grain protein content and total nitrogen recorded under T5 (25% CF + 75% BS) and T4 (50% CF + 50% BS) treatments underscore the role of balanced and sustained nitrogen supply in wheat quality. Nitrogen is a key component of amino acids and proteins, and its availability during grain filling directly influences protein synthesis [54]. The combination of inorganic N (quick release) and organic N (slow release) likely ensured a prolonged N supply, reducing losses and improving nitrogen use efficiency. Similar findings have been reported in integrated nutrient management studies on wheat and other cereals [54, 55].

The relatively lower protein content under sole CF (T2) may be attributed to potential N losses through leaching or volatilization, especially in the absence of organic matter to improve nutrient retention. Conversely, the lower protein under sole BS (T6) could reflect slower N mineralization rates, insufficient to meet the high N demand of wheat during critical growth stages. This highlights the importance of complementarity between organic and inorganic sources for optimizing both yield and grain quality [51, 55].

The positive correlation between grain yield and protein content (implied through N uptake) further supports the idea that integrated management enhances both productivity and nutritional value. These results align with previous studies in Ethiopia and similar agro-ecologies, where combined organic-inorganic fertilization improved wheat grain quality without compromising yield [11, 56].

The absence of detectable Cd, Pb, and Cr in both soil and grain across all treatments indicates that the bio-slurry used in this study was low in toxic heavy metals, complying with international safety standards for organic fertilizers (Table 4) [48, 57]. This is a critical finding for food safety and farmer adoption, as contamination from organic amendments is a common concern. The baseline soil analysis revealed a high Nickel (Ni) concentration (89.4 ± 3 mg/kg), exceeding international agricultural thresholds.

This enrichment is likely lithogenic, originating from the weathering of basaltic parent materials typical of the Amhara Region’s volcanic plateau. Since these levels were recorded pre-experiment, they indicate, historical application of contaminated amendments, or atmospheric deposition from industrial activities, natural geogenic background levels enrichment rather than anthropogenic contamination. Regarding potential implications, while total Ni is high, its bioavailability is often limited by the soil’s alkaline pH and high organic matter, which promote metal complexation. Although no phytotoxicity was observed in the wheat crop, the long-term impact of bio-slurry on Ni solubility warrants ongoing monitoring to ensure food safety.

Notably, post-harvest soil Ni levels decreased under most treatments (Table 8), particularly in plots receiving bio-slurry (T5, T6). This reduction could be due to Ni immobilization through complexation with organic matter or adsorption onto Fe/Mn oxides enhanced by organic amendments [58]. Importantly, Ni concentrations in wheat grain remained below the FAO/WHO permissible limit (67 mg/kg), indicating low phytoavailability and minimal risk to human health under the conditions of this study.

These findings suggest that the application of clean, well-characterized bio-slurry does not exacerbate heavy metal accumulation in soil or crops. Nevertheless, regular monitoring of both organic amendments and soil metal concentrations is recommended to ensure long-term safety, especially in regions with unknown contamination histories [58].

The superior performance of the 25% CF + 75% BS treatment across almost all agronomic parameters plant height, tiller number, spike length, seeds per spike, thousand seed weight, and grain yield demonstrates a clear synergistic effect. The superior performance of 25% CF + 75% BS (47% yield increase over sole CF) can be explained by the nutrient complementarity theory. Chemical fertilizers provide immediately available nutrients (NH4⁺, NO3⁻, and H2PO4⁻) that meet the crop’s rapid early growth demand, while bio-slurry contributes to a slow-release nutrient pool through microbial mineralization. This creates a “two-phase” nutrient supply pattern: an initial pulse from CF followed by sustained release from BS, matching the sigmoidal nutrient uptake curve of wheat. For example, wheat requires approximately 40% of its total nitrogen during stem elongation and booting stages; the CF component supplies this demand while mineralized organic N supports grain filling [59, 60]. The combined application likely improved root development and nutrient uptake efficiency through better soil physical conditions (aeration, structure) and enhanced microbial activity [56, 61]. Furthermore, the gradual nutrient release from bio-slurry may have better matched crop demand, reducing losses and improving fertilizer use efficiency [62, 63].

The significant positive correlations among yield components (Table 11) reinforce the idea that integrated management enhances overall crop vigor and productivity. For instance, the strong correlation between grain yield and biological yield (r = 0.89∗∗∗r = 0.89∗∗∗) indicates that improvements in vegetative growth directly translate into higher grain production. Similarly, the correlation between effective tillers and grain yield (r = 0.78∗∗∗r = 0.78∗∗∗) underscores the importance of optimizing tiller fertility through balanced nutrition.

These results are consistent with previous studies in Ethiopia and South Asia, where integrated use of biogas slurry and mineral fertilizers increased wheat yields by 20–40% compared to sole chemical fertilization [11, 55]. The harvest index (HI) was also highest under T5, indicating efficient translocation of assimilates to the grain, a key goal in cereal production.

From a practical perspective, the 25% CF + 75% BS treatment offers a viable pathway to reduce mineral fertilizer use by 75% while maintaining or even improving wheat productivity and soil health. This has important economic implications for smallholder farmers in Ethiopia, where fertilizer costs are a major constraint [6]. Moreover, the use of locally available bio-slurry from biogas digesters promotes nutrient recycling and reduces dependence on external inputs, aligning with principles of circular economy and sustainable intensification [9].

The improvement in soil organic matter and nutrient retention under integrated management also contributes to climate resilience by enhancing soil water storage and reducing nutrient leaching. This is particularly relevant in rain fed wheat systems of the Ethiopian highlands, where moisture stress and soil degradation are common [50].

While this study demonstrates the practical field-level benefits of integrated bio-slurry and chemical fertilizer application on wheat productivity and soil health, the findings open avenues for more mechanistic investigations. The organic matter in bio-slurry, largely composed of complex carbohydrates, represents a valuable carbon source that could be further optimized. Future research could explore pre-treatment methods to enhance the biodegradability and nutrient release kinetics of such organic amendments, drawing on techniques applied to other cellulosic waste streams [64]. Furthermore, the observed improvements in crop performance under integrated management may involve the activation of intrinsic plant stress-tolerance pathways. Investigating whether improved soil conditions from bio-slurry integration up regulate the expression of key plant protective proteins, such as heat shock proteins known to confer abiotic stress tolerance in wheat, would provide a valuable molecular perspective to complement these agronomic results [65].

Conclusions

The results of this study demonstrate that an integrated nutrient management approach using a 25% Chemical Fertilizer (CF) and 75% Bio-slurry (BS) ratio is the optimal strategy for wheat production. This specific combination provides a dual benefit by maximizing grain yield and agronomic traits such as spike length, thousand-seed weight and tiller number; while simultaneously revitalizing soil health. Post-harvest soil analysis confirmed significant improvements (p < 0.05) in soil pH, organic matter, and organic carbon, effectively reversing the soil fertility decline common in the Ethiopian highlands.

Furthermore, this integrated approach allows for a 75% reduction in chemical fertilizer use, offering a more economically viable and environmentally sustainable alternative for farmers. The observed decline in trace metal levels over time further underscores the safety of this method. Based on these findings, the 25% CF + 75% BS treatment performing best across two years and highly recommended for adoption as a sustainable practice to enhance wheat productivity and ensure long-term soil stability in the region.
