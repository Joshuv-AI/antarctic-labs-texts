Renormalization of massless matter fields in the ( 𝟏 , 𝟎 ) ⊕ ( 𝟎 , 𝟏 ) (1,0)\oplus(0,1) representation. arXiv: 1234.56789
Armando de la C. Rangel-Pantoja, M. Napsuciale, Carlos A. Vaquera-Araujo

Abstract
We study the one-loop renormalization of massless matter fields in the ( 1 , 0 ) ⊕ ( 0 , 1 ) (1,0)\oplus(0,1) representation of the Restricted Lorentz Group. The model is constructed with the most general parity-invariant kinetic term and includes all the independent dimension-4 parity-invariant self-interactions available in this representation. Its free Lagrangian reproduces the massless limit of three theories previously studied in the literature: the Joos-Weinberg, the Shay-Good/Hammer-McDonald-Pursey, and the Klein-Gordon-like one, as particular cases. The model is found to be renormalizable.



1
Introduction

According to Wigner’s work
WIGNER19899
, one-particle states are defined by their mass
m
and spin
j
, for
m\neq 0
, or by their helicity
h
, for
m=0
. In quantum field theory, one-particle states arise from the action of creation Hilbert-space operators on the vacuum state. Likewise, quantum fields are Hilbert-space operators built as linear superpositions of one-particle state creation and annihilation operators with coefficients belonging to a representation of the proper ortochronus Lorentz group
SO^{+}(1,3)
, also known as the Restricted Lorentz Group (RLG)
WeinbergQFT
. The irreducible representations (irreps) of the RLG are labeled by two
SU(2)
numbers
(a,b)
.
In general, the
(a,b)
representation contains multiple spin sectors
j=a+b,\cdots,|a-b|
; among them, one single value has to be picked out, through the addition of restrictions, in the form of equations of motion (EOMs), or as auxiliary equations.
In particular, the irreps
(j,0)
and
(0,j)
have one single spin sector, and thus fields
constructed from these irreps have well-defined spin
j
regardless of the EOM they satisfy. Although there exists an infinite number of RLG irreps and hence an arbitrary number of quantum fields that can be defined out of them, the Standard Model (SM) uses only a few: the scalar
(0,0)
, with
j=0
, for the Higgs; the spinors
(\frac{1}{2},0)
and
(0,\frac{1}{2})
, with
j=1/2
, for leptons and quarks; and the vector
(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
, with
j=1
, for gauge bosons.
We will refer to them as standard representations, for simplicity.
In view of this, one way to probe physics beyond the SM is to survey fields in non-standard representations; in particular, high spin fields (
j>1
) all belong to this class.

The construction of quantum field theories for high spin values or in non-standard RLG representations has been a long-standing problem. Some of the historically most
popular
approaches addressing it are those due to Dirac-Fierz-Pauli
Dirac:1936tg
;
Fierz:1939zz
;
Fierz:1939ix
; Bhabha-Majorana
Bhabha:1945zz
;
Majorana:1932chs
; and Joos-Weinberg
Joos:1962qq
;
Weinberg:1964cn
;
AnySpinII
. Recently, a novel general first-principles formalism for deducing free EOMs for fields in any representation of the RLG was proposed in
Napsuciale:2022usn
. This formalism relies solely on the symmetry group of a free particle: the full Poincaré group (i.e., including parity and time reversal). It asserts that a complete set of commuting observables (CSCO) characterizing its quantum state must include the Hamiltonian, the Casimir operators of the full Poincaré group, the generators of the Cartan subalgebra, and the Hermitian discrete symmetry operators that commute with all the above. Including these last operators is the key feature of this approach; in particular, the parity operator yields a kinematical restriction that turns out to be the EOM.
Thus, it reproduces the conventional EOMs for the standard representations: the Dirac equation for the
(\frac{1}{2},0)\oplus(0,\frac{1}{2})
and the Proca and Maxwell equations for the
(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
in the massive and massless case, respectively; in addition, it reveals that, in the latter representation, the gauge symmetry arising in the massless case has its fundamental origin in the parity symmetry. We will refer to this latter approach as the
Poincaré
formalism, for simplicity. Among all these different approaches for constructing fields in non-standard representations, both the Joos-Weinberg and Poincaré formalisms can be applied to a (massive or massless) field belonging to the
(1,0)\oplus(0,1)
representation of the RLG, and lead to slightly different EOMs that have been studied since decades ago
Joos:1962qq
;
Weinberg:1964cn
;
AnySpinII
;
Shay:1968iq
;
Hammer
;
Tucker
;
Napsuciale:2015kua
;
Kalb:1974yc
;
Chizhov:2011
;
Kaul:1977am
;
Townsend:1979hd
. Their corresponding quantum field theories for the massive cases, however, are not exempt from issues: while the former’s propagator has an unphysical pole at
p^{2}=-m^{2}
, the latter’s is ultraviolet(UV)-divergent. Interestingly, the propagators for the massless cases do not exhibit either of these issues. This is our main motivation to study these fields.

On the other hand, the EOMs for fields in
(1,0)\oplus(0,1)
are second-order; as a consequence, the field has mass-dimension one. This fact enables the definition of dimension-4 self-interactions and opens the possibility of having renormalizable self-interacting theories. Among the various massless theories that can be defined, the renormalization group tools can be used to determine which of them can support a renormalizable self-interacting theory. This has led us to study the renormalization of self-interacting massless fields in this representation.

In this article, we study the one-loop renormalization of self-interacting massless matter fields in the
(1,0)\oplus(0,1)
representation. A general model is defined using the most general parity-invariant kinetic term and all the independent parity-invariant dimension-4 self-interactions available in this representation. The general kinetic term reproduces the free massless dynamics that arise in the Joos-Weinberg and Poincaré formalisms as well as the Klein-Gordon-like one, as particular cases. The article is structured as follows: in section
2
, we review the basics of this representation and deduce the free massive and massless EOMs for fields on it; in section
3
, we present the model; in section
4
, the renormalization procedure of the model is performed; and finally, in section
5
, we end with conclusions.

2
Fields in
(1,0)\oplus(0,1)

A field in the
(1,0)\oplus(0,1)
representation has six complex components, three coming from each irrep:
(1,0)
and
(0,1)
. It can be formulated and studied both as a complex six-component column vector
\Psi_{a}
(
a=1,\cdots,6
)
Joos:1962qq
;
Weinberg:1964cn
;
AnySpinII
;
Shay:1968iq
;
Hammer
;
Tucker
;
Napsuciale:2015kua
or as a complex second-rank antisymmetric Lorentz tensor
B_{\mu\nu}

(\mu,\nu=0,\cdots,3)

Kalb:1974yc
;
Chizhov:2011
;
Kaul:1977am
;
Townsend:1979hd
, and there exists a map translating between these two languages where each vector index
a
corresponds to a pair of antisymmetric internal Lorentz indices
\mu\nu

Napsuciale:2022usn
. In this work we adopt the former notation.
A covariant basis for operators in this representation was developed in
Selim
. This basis comprises six operators
\{1,\chi,S^{\mu\nu},\chi S^{\mu\nu},M^{\mu\nu},C^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\}
: the unit operator
1
, the chirality operator
\chi
, two second-rank symmetric tensors
S^{\mu\nu}
(analogous to
\gamma^{\mu}
) and
\chi S^{\mu\nu}
, the Lorentz generators
M^{\mu\nu}
, and a fourth-rank tensor
C^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
with symmetries
C^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}=-C^{\nu\mu\rho\sigma}=-C^{\mu\nu\sigma\rho}
and
C^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}=C^{\rho\sigma\mu\nu}
, having null traces and satisfying the Bianchi identity.
The use of this basis makes the mathematical structure of this representation transparent and facilitates the handling of theories constructed from it.

Fields with mass
m\neq 0
have spin-1, and the theories studied in the literature can be readily retrieved, along the lines described in
Ecker:1988te
;
Napsuciale:2013yda
, by writing down the most general parity-invariant free massive Lagrangian in terms of the basis operators and demanding the location of the propagator’s pole to be at
p^{2}=m^{2}
.
The most general parity-invariant free massive Lagrangian is

\mathcal{L}=\partial^{\mu}\bar{\Psi}\left(\alpha g_{\mu\nu}+\beta S_{\mu\nu}+igM_{\mu\nu}\right)\partial^{\nu}\Psi-m^{2}\bar{\Psi}\Psi,

(1)

where
\bar{\Psi}=\Psi^{\dagger}S^{00}
is the dual field, and
\alpha,\beta
are arbitrary real-valued coefficients normalized as
|\alpha|+|\beta|=1
to get proper normalization of the Lagrangian. Its propagator is

i\Gamma_{ab}(p)=i\frac{\left(\alpha p^{2}-m^{2}\right)1_{ab}-\beta p_{\mu}p_{\nu}(S^{\mu\nu})_{ab}}{\left[(\alpha-\beta)p^{2}-m^{2}\right]\left[(\alpha+\beta)p^{2}-m^{2}\right]},

(2)

which has a two-pole structure. To get the physical pole at
p^{2}=m^{2}
the coefficients must be chosen as: i)
\alpha=1,\beta=0
, ii)
\alpha=0,\beta=1
or iii)
\alpha=\pm\beta=1/2
.
For i) we get a Klein-Gordon-like theory, with propagator

i\Gamma_{ab}(p)=i\frac{1_{ab}}{p^{2}-m^{2}+i\epsilon};

(3)

this theory and its interactions were studied in
Rangel-Pantoja:2025nkm
;
Delgado-Acosta:2013nia
;
Delgado-Acosta:2012dxv
;
Rivero-Acosta2020
.
For ii) we get the Joos-Weinberg theory
Joos:1962qq
;
Weinberg:1964cn
, whose propagator is

i\Gamma_{ab}(p)=i\frac{m^{2}1_{ab}+p_{\mu}p_{\nu}(S^{\mu\nu})_{ab}}{\left[p^{2}+m^{2}\right]\left[p^{2}-m^{2}\right]},

(4)

and its interactions were studied in
Eeg:1972us
;
Eeg:1972uwa
;
Eeg1973
;
Chizhov:2011
. Finally, for iii) with
\beta=1/2
we get the Shay-Good/Hammer-McDonald-Pursey

theory
Shay:1968iq

Hammer
,
whose propagator is

i\Gamma_{ab}(p)=i\frac{\left(-p^{2}+2m^{2}\right)1_{ab}+p_{\mu}p_{\nu}(S^{\mu\nu})_{ab}}{2m^{2}\left[p^{2}-m^{2}+i\epsilon\right]},

(5)

and its interactions were studied in
Prabhakaran:1973pr
;
Napsuciale:2015kua
;
Chizhov:2011
. This latter theory is the one arising from the Poincaré formalism in
Napsuciale:2022usn
, where it was found that the case
\beta=1/2
corresponds to the positive-parity field construction, while the case
\beta=-1/2
corresponds to the negative-parity one; it is also shown there that, when written in the tensor language, it becomes the Kalb-Ramond theory
Kalb:1974yc
.
Inspection of propagators (
3
)–(
5
) allows us to gain insight into the features of the theories we are dealing with.
For theory i) we have a conventional Klein-Gordon-like propagator (
3
), that is
1/p^{2}
-order and a single pole at
p^{2}=m^{2}
; in fact, the one-loop renormalization of its dimension-4 self-interactions and electrodynamics was demonstrated in
Rivero-Acosta2020
.
For theory ii) we have propagator (
4
), which is
1/p^{2}
-order and less, but has an additional and awkward unphysical pole located at
p^{2}=-m^{2}
.
For theory iii) we have propagator (
5
), which has a single pole at
p^{2}=m^{2}
but is zero-order and less in
p^{2}
, implying that it renders amplitude integrals divergent in the UV region (
p^{2}\gg m^{2}
) and thus leads to a non-renormalizable theory (it suffers from the same problem as the Proca massive vector field).

In contrast, the massless theories do not have any of those problems. The most general parity-invariant free massless Lagrangian is

\mathcal{L}=\partial^{\mu}\bar{\Psi}\left(\alpha g_{\mu\nu}+\beta S_{\mu\nu}+igM_{\mu\nu}\right)\partial^{\nu}\Psi,

(6)

and its propagator becomes

i\Gamma_{ab}(p)=\frac{i}{\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}}\frac{\alpha p^{2}1_{ab}-\beta p_{\mu}p_{\nu}(S^{\mu\nu})_{ab}}{p^{4}+i\epsilon},

(7)

which is
1/p^{2}
-order, making it well-behaved in the UV region, and no longer has an unphysical pole. Notice, however, that it’s undefined for
|\alpha|=|\beta|
, which is precisely the massless case for theory iii). As explained in
Napsuciale:2022usn
, this is related to the emergence of a gauge symmetry, implemented as
\Psi\rightarrow\Psi^{\prime}=\Psi+(\alpha g_{\mu\nu}-\beta S_{\mu\nu})\partial^{\mu}\partial^{\nu}\Phi
(which in the tensor language becomes the gauge symmetry of the massless Kalb-Ramond field
Kalb:1974yc
), and, to get a well-defined propagator, it’s necessary to add the gauge-fixing term

\mathcal{L}_{\text{gf}}=\frac{1}{\xi}\partial^{\mu}\bar{\Psi}\left(\alpha g_{\mu\nu}-\beta S_{\mu\nu}\right)\partial^{\nu}\Psi,\qquad\qquad\text{for }\alpha=\pm\beta=\frac{1}{2},

(8)

where
\xi
is the gauge parameter. With this additional term, the Lagrangian for the massless theory iii) becomes

\mathcal{L}=\partial^{\mu}\bar{\Psi}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{\xi}\right)g_{\mu\nu}\pm\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{\xi}\right)S_{\mu\nu}+igM_{\mu\nu}\right]\partial^{\nu}\Psi,

(9)

which has the form of (
6
) with
\alpha=(1/2)(1+1/\xi)
and
\beta=\pm(1/2)(1-1/\xi)
satisfying now
|\alpha|\neq|\beta|
for finite
\xi
, and hence its propagator is still a particular case of (
7
).
From these observations, we can conclude that the propagator (
7
) is the most general one for massless fields in this representation and doesn’t suffer from the problems that the propagators of massive theories have.

At this point, it’s important to note that the gauge symmetry arising in the massless theory iii) has led to interpreting this field not as a matter field but as a gauge one. This distinction is further explained in
Chizhov:2011
, using tensor language, where the matter and gauge theories presented there correspond to ii) and iii), respectively. Nevertheless, the study of theory iii), with
m\neq 0
, as a matter field has yielded intriguing results when proposed as dark matter and gave rise to a new candidate called tensor dark matter (TDM)
Hernandez-Arellano:2018sen
;
Hernandez-Arellano:2019qgd
;
Hernandez-Arellano:2021bpt
.

From the Lagrangian (
6
), we see that the field’s mass dimension is one. This fact enables for the formulation of dimension-4 self-interactions and makes possible the construction of power-counting renormalizable self-interacting theories. In the next section, we construct the model we work with: a general parity-invariant massless one that includes all the independent dimension-4 self-interactions available in this representation.

3
The model

The free Lagrangian is given by (
6
). The term involving
igM_{\mu\nu}
becomes relevant for gauge interactions, see for example
Rivero-Acosta2020
; however, for self-interactions it’s just an insignificant boundary term, and so we will omit it in the following. The dimension-4 self-interactions are quartic interactions of the form
(\bar{\Psi}A\Psi)(\bar{\Psi}B\Psi)
, where
A,B
are operators of the covariant basis
\{1,\chi,S^{\mu\nu},\chi S^{\mu\nu},M^{\mu\nu},C^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\}
.
By deducing the corresponding Fierz identities it is shown that, among the parity-invariant, the independent ones are just four:
(\bar{\Psi}\Psi)^{2},(\bar{\Psi}\chi\Psi)^{2},(\bar{\Psi}S^{\mu\nu}\Psi)^{2},(\bar{\Psi}M^{\mu\nu}\Psi)^{2}
. Thus, the most general parity-invariant massless model with dimension-4 self-interactions is

\mathcal{L}=\partial^{\mu}\bar{\Psi}\left(\alpha g_{\mu\nu}+\beta S_{\mu\nu}\right)\partial^{\nu}\Psi+\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}(\bar{\Psi}\Psi)^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}(\bar{\Psi}\chi\Psi)^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{3}}{2}(\bar{\Psi}S^{\mu\nu}\Psi)^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{4}}{2}(\bar{\Psi}M^{\mu\nu}\Psi)^{2},

(10)

where
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4}
are coupling parameters.
Here it’s important to notice that, even though the kinetic term in (
10
) has theory iii), in (
9
), as a particular case and so its propagator, the introduced self-interactions explicitly break its gauge symmetry. This may bring inconsistencies in the propagating degrees of freedom of its interacting theory; to avoid this possible issue, we restrict to the study of theories i) and ii) from now on. 9

To implement DR, we first have to extend the basis
\{1,\chi,S^{\mu\nu},\chi S^{\mu\nu},M^{\mu\nu},C^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\}
to dimension
D
. Initially, this task seems challenging because its Lie and Jordan algebras are very complicated (much more than those of the Dirac basis), as can be explicitly seen in
Selim
; for example, the anticommutator
\{S^{\mu\nu},S^{\rho\sigma}\}
is given as

\{S_{\mu\nu},S_{\rho\sigma}\}=\frac{4}{3}\left(g_{\mu\rho}g_{\nu\sigma}+g_{\mu\sigma}g_{\nu\rho}-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}g_{\rho\sigma}\right)-\frac{1}{6}\left(C_{\mu\rho\nu\sigma}+C_{\mu\sigma\nu\rho}\right),

(16)

depending not only on
g_{\mu\nu}
, but also on
C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
. Thus, if we define the
D
-dimensional
S^{\mu\nu}
by demanding fulfillment of the algebra (
16
) (as is done for the Dirac gamma matrices in the context of DR
Peskin:1995ev
), all the results coming from it will involve the
D
-dimensional
C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
as well, and so this approach gets hard to follow. Fortunately, in the tensor language all of the basis operators are expressed in terms of the metric tensor
g_{\mu\nu}
and the Levi-Civita symbol
\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
, as
Napsuciale:2013yda

\displaystyle 1_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(g_{\alpha\gamma}g_{\beta\delta}-g_{\alpha\delta}g_{\beta\gamma}\right),

(17)

\displaystyle\chi_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{2}\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta},

(18)

\displaystyle(S_{\mu\nu})_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}=

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}1_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}-g_{\mu\gamma}1_{\alpha\beta\nu\delta}-g_{\mu\delta}1_{\alpha\beta\gamma\nu}-g_{\nu\gamma}1_{\alpha\beta\mu\delta}-g_{\nu\delta}1_{\alpha\beta\gamma\mu},

(19)

\displaystyle(M_{\mu\nu})_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}=

\displaystyle-i(g_{\mu\gamma}1_{\alpha\beta\nu\delta}+g_{\mu\delta}1_{\alpha\beta\gamma\nu}-g_{\gamma\nu}1_{\alpha\beta\mu\delta}-g_{\delta\nu}1_{\alpha\beta\gamma\mu}),

(20)

\displaystyle(C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma})_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}=

\displaystyle 321_{\alpha\gamma\mu\nu}1_{\delta\beta\rho\sigma}-321_{\alpha\gamma\rho\sigma}1_{\beta\delta\mu\nu}+6g_{\alpha\gamma}X_{\beta\delta\rho\sigma\mu\nu}+6g_{\beta\delta}X_{\alpha\gamma\rho\sigma\mu\nu}

(21)

\displaystyle+81_{\alpha\beta\delta\gamma}1_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu}+161_{\alpha\beta\rho\sigma}1_{\delta\gamma\mu\nu}+161_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu}1_{\delta\gamma\rho\sigma}-161_{\alpha\delta\rho\sigma}1_{\beta\gamma\mu\nu}

\displaystyle-161_{\alpha\delta\mu\nu}1_{\beta\gamma\rho\sigma},

where

X_{\alpha\gamma\rho\sigma\mu\nu}=2g_{\alpha\rho}1_{\mu\nu\gamma\sigma}-2g_{\alpha\sigma}1_{\mu\nu\gamma\rho}-2g_{\alpha\mu}1_{\nu\gamma\rho\sigma}+2g_{\alpha\nu}1_{\mu\gamma\rho\sigma}.

(22)

Thus, this language provides a straightforward way to construct the
D
-dimensional extension of the basis: simply replace the
4
-dimensional metric tensor
g_{\mu\nu}
with the
D
-dimensional one and follow a prescription for the Levi-Civita symbol
\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
. In fact, for the self-interacting theory (
10
), it’s not even necessary to worry about
\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
because the term involving it, in the Feynman rule (
3
), can be written in terms of
g_{\mu\nu}
as well by using the identity

\chi_{ab}\chi_{cd}\overset{\cdot}{=}\chi_{a_{1}a_{2}b_{1}b_{2}}\chi_{c_{1}c_{2}d_{1}d_{2}}=\frac{i^{2}}{4}\varepsilon_{a_{1}a_{2}b_{1}b_{2}}\varepsilon_{c_{1}c_{2}d_{1}d_{2}}=\frac{1}{4}\text{Det}\begin{pmatrix}g_{a_{1}c_{1}}&g_{a_{1}c_{2}}&g_{a_{1}d_{1}}&g_{a_{1}d_{2}}\\
g_{a_{2}c_{1}}&g_{a_{2}c_{2}}&g_{a_{2}d_{1}}&g_{a_{2}d_{2}}\\
g_{b_{1}c_{1}}&g_{b_{1}c_{2}}&g_{b_{1}d_{1}}&g_{b_{1}d_{2}}\\
g_{b_{2}c_{1}}&g_{b_{2}c_{2}}&g_{b_{2}d_{1}}&g_{b_{2}d_{2}}\\
\end{pmatrix},

(23)

where
\{(a_{1},a_{2}),(b_{1},b_{2}),(c_{1},c_{2}),(d_{1},d_{2})\}
are the internal Lorentz indices corresponding to the vector ones
\{a,b,c,d\}
, respectively; and hence, in this case, it all comes down to the metric tensor.

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\delta_{Z_{2}}}=

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\delta_{Z_{4}}}=

5
Summary and conclusions

The EOMs for fields belonging to the
(1,0)\oplus(0,1)
representation of the RLG, studied in the literature, are three: i) a Klein-Gordon-like one, ii) the Joos-Weinberg, and iii) the Shay-Good/Hammer-McDonald-Pursey. For
m\neq 0
, the propagator of ii) has an awkward unphysical pole at
p^{2}=-m^{2}
while that of iii) is divergent in the UV region (
p^{2}\gg m^{2}
). For
m=0
, however, the corresponding propagators have none of these issues. On the other hand, the mass-dimension of these fields is one, and, as a consequence, it’s possible to define dimension-4 self-interactions; this fact opens the possibility of having renormalizable self-interacting theories. This led us to study the renormalization of the corresponding self-interacting massless theories.

We constructed the most general self-interacting parity-invariant massless model for fields in this representation, whose free part has theories i), ii), and iii) as particular cases, aiming to study the renormalization of all of them at once. However, these interactions explicitly break the gauge symmetry that arises in the free massless case of theory iii), and so we restricted ourselves to retrieving theories i) and ii). This general model has two superficially divergent diagrams: the self-energy and the vertex. Both were successfully renormalized in the MS scheme with appropriate choices for the counterterms. For theory i), we found three independent renormalizable subtheories, while for ii), it turned out that all of the independent self-interactions are necessary for its renormalization. The beta functions were calculated, and their fixed points identified. In particular, for i) we found that
\beta_{\lambda_{1}}
has no fixed points at all, while there are six independent non-trivial common fixed points for
\beta_{\lambda_{2}},\beta_{\lambda_{3}},\beta_{\lambda_{4}}
; for ii), although each beta function has fixed points, there are no non-trivial common solutions for the four of them; interestingly, a curious solution emerged at which
\beta_{\lambda_{3}}
and
\beta_{\lambda_{4}}
vanish, while
\beta_{\lambda_{1}}
and
\beta_{\lambda_{2}}
are the negative of each other. These results indicate that the model studied in this work is a rich theoretical laboratory for renormalization group analisys.
