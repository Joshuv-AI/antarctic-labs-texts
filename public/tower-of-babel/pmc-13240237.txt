Bilateral Thalamic Diffuse Midline Glioma, H3K27M‐Altered, in a 3‐Year‐Old Boy
Authors: Ammir Abuzahra, Bessan N Abuzahra, Ruba Atawneh, Siwar Abu Sakout, Raya Abu Ayyash, Jana Dwaik, Mohammad Y Nairokh, Nasim Abukaresh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiffuse midline glioma, H3 K27‐altered, is an aggressive pediatric brain tumor that can mimic non‐neoplastic conditions when located outside the brainstem. We describe a 3‐year‐old boy who presented with progressive ocular misalignment and left‐sided weakness without systemic symptoms. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated symmetric bilateral thalamic T2/FLAIR hyperintense lesions without contrast enhancement or diffusion restriction, initially raising suspicion for inflammatory or metabolic disease. Cerebrospinal fluid and metabolic investigations were unremarkable. Due to diagnostic uncertainty, surgical resection of the dominant lesion was performed. Histopathology revealed an infiltrative high‐grade astrocytic tumor with loss of H3K27me3 and positive H3K27M immunostaining, confirming diffuse midline glioma, H3 K27‐altered (WHO grade IV). Postoperatively, the patient developed transient contralateral weakness but remained neurologically stable at 3‐month follow‐up and was referred for radiochemotherapy. This case highlights that bilateral thalamic lesions in children may represent diffuse midline glioma even when imaging lacks typical malignant features. Early biopsy should be considered in unexplained bilateral thalamic lesions to prevent diagnostic delay and initiate appropriate oncologic management.Keywords: bilateral thalamic glioma, diffuse midline glioma, H3 K27‐altered, pediatric brain tumor, WHO CNS classification

Introduction

Diffuse midline glioma (DMG), H3 K27‐altered, is a highly aggressive WHO grade IV pediatric tumor arising in midline central nervous system structures [1]. Although most commonly located in the pons, thalamic involvement can occur and may present a diagnostic challenge [2]. Bilateral thalamic lesions in children are frequently attributed to metabolic, inflammatory, or infectious etiologies, especially when imaging lacks contrast enhancement [3]. Consequently, neoplastic causes may initially be overlooked. Early identification is crucial because DMG carries a poor prognosis and requires prompt multidisciplinary management and family counseling [2]. We describe a rare case of DMG, H3 K27‐altered presenting with bilateral thalamic lesions in a very young child, and review the relevant literature.

Case History/Examination

Clinical History

A 3‐year‐old boy with unremarkable past history presented with bilateral ocular squint and progressive weakness of the left arm and leg over two weeks. He had no headaches, fever, or systemic symptoms. Neurological exam showed mild left hemiparesis and left cranial nerve VI palsy; the rest of the exam was normal. No history of infection or trauma was reported. The sequence of clinical events from symptom onset to follow‐up is summarized in Table 1.

Imaging

Brain MRI (1.5 T) revealed bilateral thalamic and basal ganglia masses that were sharply delineated and T2/FLAIR hyperintense (Figure 1). There was no peritumoral edema. On T1‐weighted imaging, these lesions were hypointense and showed no contrast enhancement. Diffusion‐weighted imaging demonstrated no focal restriction (Figure 2), arguing against acute infarction or abscess. Given the bilateral symmetry and lack of enhancement, imaging differential included diffuse glioma, metabolic, and inflammatory processes (ADEM, deep venous infarcts). However, CSF studies were negative, and there was no evidence of metabolic derangement. The MRI findings (non‐enhancing, expansile thalamic lesions) are consistent with DMG. Of note, prior series have shown that about half of pediatric thalamic H3K27M tumors enhance and half do not, and imaging cannot reliably distinguish H3‐mutant from wildtype DMGs. Thus, surgical biopsy/resection was pursued for definitive diagnosis.

Axial diffusion‐weighted MRI (b = 1000 s/mm2) shows no areas of restricted diffusion within the thalamic lesions, consistent with neoplastic (rather than acute infarctive) etiology.

Surgery and Pathology

The patient underwent a left parieto‐occipital craniotomy with gross total resection of the dominant left thalamic lesion. Intraoperative frozen section confirmed a high‐grade glial tumor. Histology showed a densely infiltrative astrocytic neoplasm with pleomorphic nuclei, brisk mitotic activity, and focal microvascular proliferation, but no necrosis. Immunohistochemistry was positive for H3 K27M mutant protein and showed global loss of H3K27me3 in tumor nuclei. The tumor was negative for IDH1/2 and BRAF V600E mutations. These findings established the diagnosis of Diffuse Midline Glioma, H3 K27‐altered (H3K27M mutation), WHO Grade IV. By definition, this entity is uniformly high‐grade. Stereotactic biopsy was considered; however, resection was favored due to the accessible tumor component and progressive neurological deficit.

Postoperative Course

After surgery, the patient's left‐sided weakness initially improved to near normal strength. However, on postoperative day 3 he developed new right hemiparesis, most likely related to perioperative edema affecting the contralateral corticospinal pathways. He underwent supportive management and short‐term neurological rehabilitation with gradual clinical stabilization. No surgical complications such as hemorrhage, infection, or hydrocephalus were observed.

Following histopathological confirmation, the case was discussed in a multidisciplinary neuro‐oncology board. Because diffuse midline glioma is not surgically curable, adjuvant therapy was recommended. The patient was planned for focal external beam radiotherapy directed to the tumor region followed by temozolomide‐based chemotherapy according to the institutional pediatric high‐grade glioma protocol. Surgical intervention was performed primarily to obtain tissue diagnosis and reduce mass effect rather than as definitive curative treatment. The family received detailed counseling regarding the disease course and prognosis.

At 3‐month follow‐up after initiation of adjuvant therapy, the patient remained neurologically stable with mild residual right‐sided weakness.

Conclusion

Non‐enhancing bilateral thalamic lesions in children should not be assumed benign or inflammatory. Early biopsy with H3K27 testing is critical to establish the diagnosis of diffuse midline glioma and guide management and counseling.

Discussion

The main diagnostic challenge in this case was the symmetric non‐enhancing appearance, which strongly suggested inflammatory or metabolic disease. This frequently leads to delayed biopsy in pediatric patients. Our case demonstrates that the absence of contrast enhancement does not exclude high‐grade glioma, particularly in midline locations.

Diffuse midline gliomas with H3 K27‐alteration are notoriously aggressive pediatric brain tumors. The 2021 WHO CNS classification now categorizes “Diffuse midline glioma, H3 K27‐altered” as a distinct high‐grade type, replacing the 2016 “H3 K27M‐mutant” terminology [2]. This reflects new knowledge that, in addition to H3F3A/HIST1H3B mutations, overexpression of EZHIP or EGFR alterations can similarly inactivate PRC2 methylation and define this entity [1]. H3 K27‐altered DMGs are assigned WHO Grade IV regardless of histology. They most commonly present in the pons (DIPG), but also occur in the thalamus, spinal cord, and other midline sites [4].

Diffuse midline gliomas are increasingly understood within the context of molecular neuro‐oncology, where epigenetic dysregulation plays a central role. Recent evidence highlights the involvement of ligand‐gated ion channels in glioma biology, particularly their contribution to tumor proliferation, neuronal–tumor interactions, and microenvironmental signaling. Alterations in glutamate receptor signaling and other ligand‐gated pathways may promote tumor growth and invasiveness, representing a potential therapeutic vulnerability [5].

In parallel, the emergence of targeted therapies in the molecular era has reshaped the management landscape of high‐grade gliomas. Advances in understanding the epigenetic and genetic drivers of diffuse midline glioma, including H3K27 alterations, have led to the development of novel agents such as ONC201 and other pathway‐specific therapies. Although outcomes remain poor, these targeted approaches offer promising avenues for future treatment and highlight the importance of molecular characterization in guiding therapy [6].

Bilateral bithalamic presentation in a child is rare. In a large series of thalamic DMG, only ~30% were pediatric, and bilateral cases were a minority [4]. Notably, the genomic profile of pediatric bithalamic gliomas often differs: recent studies showed that EGFR mutations (especially exon 20 insertions) are frequent in these tumors, while H3K27M mutations are uncommon [1]. Demonstrated that pediatric bithalamic gliomas are epigenetically distinct and harbor EGFR alterations, rarely exhibiting H3 mutations [3]. Similarly, Xu et al. reported that standard bithalamic gliomas have frequent EGFR mutations and seldom H3K27M, whereas cases with both alterations may represent a novel subtype [4]. Our case, by contrast, was an H3K27‐altered DMG (tested positive for H3F3A K27M by IHC) without known EGFR mutation data, underscoring the heterogeneity in this region.

Imaging of DMGs is non‐specific. Pediatric H3K27M tumors can be solid or infiltrative with variable enhancement [7]. In our patient, the lesions were nonenhancing on MRI. This pattern has been described in DMGs centered in the thalamus or cerebellum [7]. However, imaging alone cannot distinguish H3 status [4]. For example, Aboian et al. found that about half of thalamic H3K27M‐mutant gliomas showed enhancement, whereas others did not, and neither enhancement nor diffusion restriction reliably predicted mutation status. Thus definitive diagnosis relies on pathology [7].

From a therapeutic standpoint, maximal safe surgical resection remains controversial for deep midline gliomas. Studies suggest resection may extend survival for low‐grade thalamic tumors, but its benefit in high‐grade DMG is unclear [1]. In this case a gross total resection was achieved, which at least provided tissue diagnosis. Postoperatively, the patient will receive cranial irradiation with chemotherapy. Radiotherapy has been shown to modestly prolong survival in DMG [2]. Unfortunately, survival for H3 K27‐altered DMG is generally < 1 year [1], despite multimodal therapy. Research into targeted agents (e.g., ONC201) and immunotherapy is ongoing, but no standard effective systemic treatment exists to date.

This case also emphasizes the necessity of molecular testing in pediatric thalamic tumors. According to WHO CNS5, any midline diffuse glioma must be tested for H3 K27 mutation and H3K27me3 status [1]. Identification of H3 K27‐alteration immediately assigns WHO Grade IV and carries prognostic and potentially therapeutic implications [3].

Clinically, any unexplained bilateral thalamic lesion in a child should prompt early neurosurgical consultation and molecular testing rather than prolonged empirical treatment. Early diagnosis allows timely oncologic referral and avoids unnecessary therapies.
