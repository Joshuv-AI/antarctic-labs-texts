An observation of the relation between the cyst on magnetic resonance imaging at bare-area of humeral head and the adhesive capsulitis of the shoulder
Authors: Luning Sun, Chao Lu, Tongyue Ji, Xiaozhong Ma, Yao Huang, Guangyi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate whether bare-area cysts of the humeral head, detected on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), are associated with adhesive capsulitis and to evaluate their diagnostic value compared with other imaging features.MethodsThis retrospective study included 138 patients with adhesive capsulitis and 114 control subjects identified from hospital records (February 2021-May 2024). Only patients with primary idiopathic adhesive capsulitis were included in this study. Patients with secondary adhesive capsulitis related to trauma, surgery, rotator cuff tear, or other identifiable shoulder pathologies were excluded. The diagnosis of adhesive capsulitis was established based on clinical evaluation by experienced orthopedic specialists, including progressive shoulder pain and significant restriction of both active and passive range of motion. In particular, loss of external rotation at the side was considered a key diagnostic feature. Range of motion limitation was evaluated by comparison with the contralateral shoulder, and internal rotation was assessed using vertebral level measurements. MRI examinations were assessed for the presence, size, and signal characteristics of bare-area cysts. Two musculoskeletal radiologists independently reviewed the images. Interobserver agreement was analyzed using intraclass correlation coefficients for continuous variables and Cohen κ for categorical variables. Binary logistic regression identified predictors of adhesive capsulitis, reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Receiver operating characteristic analysis was used to evaluate diagnostic performance, including area under the curve, optimal cutoffs, sensitivity, and specificity. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation.ResultsBare area cysts were significantly more common and larger in the adhesive capsulitis group (mean size, 13.3 ± 15.1 mm) than in controls (1.8 ± 4.7 mm, P < .001). Bare area hyperintensity was also more frequent (109 vs. 29, P < .001). Multivariable logistic regression analysis including age and sex as covariates demonstrated that bare area cyst size remained independently associated with adhesive capsulitis (OR: 1.27; 95% CI: 1.17–1.37; P < .001). Female sex was also independently associated with adhesive capsulitis (OR: 3.10; 95% CI: 1.57–6.13; P = .001), whereas age showed a significant association (OR: 1.05; 95% CI: 1.03–1.08; P < .001). Receiver operating characteristic analysis demonstrated good diagnostic performance, with an area under the curve of 0.837 for cyst size and 0.773 for bare-area hyperintensity.ConclusionBare area cysts of the humeral head, particularly when enlarged or hyperintense, represent a useful MRI marker for adhesive capsulitis. Recognition of this feature may enhance diagnostic accuracy and facilitate differentiation from other shoulder disorders.Keywords: Shoulder, Bone cysts, Adhesive capsulitis, Frozen shoulder, Bare area cyst, Magnetic resonance imaging

Adhesive capsulitis of the shoulder,12 commonly termed frozen shoulder, is a musculoskeletal disorder characterized by persistent pain and a marked reduction in both active and passive movements of the glenohumeral joint. The pathogenesis of the disease remains uncertain. Variable factors that affect its prevalence include women, trauma, age, diabetes mellitus, prolonged immobilization, thyroid disease, stroke, myocardial infarction, and autoimmune disorders.1,2 In addition, other shoulder conditions such as calcific tendonitis, bursitis, and rotator cuff tears may present with overlapping manifestations, potentially leading to diagnostic uncertainty.16 Accurate diagnosis can be challenging due to the overlapping clinical symptoms among various shoulder conditions. In cases where clinical findings are atypical, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is instrumental in pinpointing adhesive capsulitis.9,13,17 For the purposes of the present research, cystic changes involving the proximal humerus were grouped into 4 categories: anterior greater tuberosity (GT), posterior GT, lesser tuberosity, and bare area (BA).6,7,11 These cysts, often situated at the attachment sites of the supraspinatus and infraspinatus tendons, are not considered degenerative changes. By contrast, cysts occurring in anterior regions are frequently linked to subscapularis tendon abnormalities.14 Of note, pseudocystic lesions are frequently detected in the posterosuperior bare area of the humeral head4,10 and may communicate with the joint cavity.15,21 This region, positioned posterolaterally between the posterior capsule insertion and the articular surface margin, should not be confused with a cartilage defect on axial imaging. True cartilage defects are typically located medial to the bare area and are more often related to rotator cuff tears or aging.20,23 Similar cartilage defects may also be observed in Hill–Sachs lesions associated with traumatic anterior shoulder instability.

No prior studies have investigated humeral head BA cysts as an abnormal finding in shoulder adhesive capsulitis. The present study aimed to determine whether these cysts could serve as diagnostic indicators of adhesive capsulitis, independent of concomitant rotator cuff tears.

Methods

The institutional review board approved this retrospective cohort investigation. Data were collected from existing hospital records between February 2021 and May 2024. Informed consent was waived as this study involved secondary analysis of anonymized historical clinical and imaging data.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Adhesive capsulitis patients

We retrospectively identified 374 patients who underwent shoulder MRI for shoulder pain between February 2021 and May 2024. Their clinical records were reviewed, and the preliminary diagnosis of adhesive capsulitis was established according to patient history and clinical findings. The diagnostic definition required limitation of passive movement exceeding 30° in at least 2 directions, progressively worsening shoulder pain persisting for more than 1 month with greater severity at rest, and normal radiographic findings.1,13,18,24 Of the 374 patients, 124 were excluded due to bilateral shoulder restriction, tears of the rotator cuff, calcium-deposit tendinopathy, rheumatoid disease, advanced degenerative arthritis, or labral pathology. The final cohort comprised 138 patients (47 men and 90 women; mean age, 51 years; range, 30-73 years), whose MRI scans were analyzed.

Control group

The control group consisted of 114 participants (70 men and 43 women; mean age, 42 years; range, 11-77 years) who demonstrated normal glenohumeral morphology on shoulder MRI. These individuals underwent shoulder MRI for evaluation of shoulder pain during the same time interval as the adhesive capsulitis cohort. Their diagnoses included various shoulder disorders, such as calcific tendinitis, synovitis, biceps tendinitis, and joint effusion. However, none of these patients met the diagnostic criteria for adhesive capsulitis. In addition, none showed limitation of shoulder motion or a previous history of adhesive capsulitis or rotator cuff pathology.

Clinical assessment

All participants, including both patients and controls, underwent a standardized pre-MRI shoulder assessment performed by an experienced clinician. The passive range of motion was evaluated using a universal goniometer, with measurements obtained for external rotation, forward flexion, and abduction. For external rotation, assessments were conducted with the shoulder fixed at 0° abduction and the elbow bent to 90°. Internal rotation was assessed by identifying the highest vertebral level the participant's thumb could reach. Forward flexion was quantified as the maximum angle formed between the arm and trunk during elevation. Abduction was measured as the greatest arm–trunk angle achieved during lateral arm elevation.

Magnetic resonance image acquisition

Magnetic resonance imaging protocol

All patients and controls underwent imaging according to a standardized MRI protocol. Two scanners were used: a 3.0-T system (Magnetom Verio, Siemens Healthcare) and a 1.5-T system (Magnetom Aera, Siemens Health care). During acquisition, patients were positioned supine with their arms placed alongside the trunk. T2-weighted imaging was carried out with a repetition time of 3,000 ms and an echo time of 48 ms, while axial oblique sections were obtained at a thickness of 3 mm.

Interobserver and intraobserver agreement assessment

Two board-certified musculoskeletal radiologists (R1 with 10 years of experience and R2 with 8 years of experience), blinded to clinical diagnoses and group assignments, independently evaluated all MRI images. For each case, the radiologists assessed whether BA cysts were present and determined their maximum diameter on axial T2-weighted images. To assess intraobserver reliability, a randomly selected subset of 30% of the images (n = 42) was re-evaluated by both radiologists after a 2-week interval.

Statistical analysis of agreement

To evaluate observer consistency, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was applied to cyst size, treated as a continuous variable. The presence of cysts, regarded as categorical data, was examined with Cohen kappa (κ). ICC values were derived through a two-way random-effects model with absolute concordance. For interpretation, values < 0.50 indicated poor reliability, scores between 0.50 and 0.75 suggested moderate consistency, 0.75-0.90 were regarded as good, and those exceeding 0.90 were considered excellent. Kappa outcomes were interpreted as follows: values below 0.20 were classified as slight, 0.21-0.40 as fair, 0.41-0.60 as moderate, 0.61-0.80 as substantial, and any value above 0.80 as nearly perfect agreement.

Evaluation of humeral head BA on magnetic resonance imaging

Following established literature,6,11 BA cysts were identified relative to the infraspinatus tendon attachment site. Detection was conducted using MRI axial T2-weighted images. When a BA cyst was visible, a reference line was drawn from the apex of the posterior glenoid to the center of the humeral head to standardize the measurement of cyst size at the bare area. Quantitative analysis

For quantitative evaluation, measurements were obtained from MRI images magnified fourfold. The BA cyst dimension was assessed using T2-weighted, fat-suppressed oblique axial views (Fig. 1B). All results were documented in millimeters.

Qualitative analysis

Dorsolateral vascular channels in the proximal humerus were evaluated to distinguish them from cysts associated with partial tears of the supraspinatus and infraspinatus tendons.8 In addition, the presence or absence of dorsolateral vascular channels and bone marrow edema was systematically assessed.

Statistical analysis

Differences in demographic data and MRI characteristics were evaluated across the adhesive capsulitis and control groups. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to explore predictive factors. Receiver operating characteristic analysis was used to estimate diagnostic indices, including sensitivity, specificity, and threshold values. Statistical calculations were completed using SPSS software, version 26 (IBM Corp, IL, USA).

Results

Univariate analysis

Univariate analysis showed that both BA cyst size and the presence of BA hyperintensity were significantly associated with adhesive capsulitis (Table I). The average cyst size in the adhesive capsulitis group was 13.34 ± 15.07 mm, which was markedly greater than that observed in the control group (1.75 ± 4.66 mm; P< .001). In addition, BA hyperintensity was detected more frequently among adhesive capsulitis patients (109 of 138 cases) compared with controls (29 of 114 cases), showing a highly significant difference (P< .001).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis

Multivariable logistic regression analysis including age and sex as covariates demonstrated that BA cyst size remained independently associated with adhesive capsulitis (odds ratio [OR]: 1.27; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.17–1.37; P < .001). Female sex was also independently associated with adhesive capsulitis (OR: 3.10; 95% CI: 1.57–6.13; P = .001), whereas age showed a significant association (OR: 1.05; 95% CI: 1.03–1.08; P < .001). Receiver operating characteristic analysis

Receiver operating characteristic analysis showed that the size of BA cysts provided significant discriminatory power for identifying adhesive capsulitis, with an area under the curve [AUC] of 0.837. The best threshold was 2.5 mm, yielding a sensitivity of 80.3% and a specificity of 77.7%. A comprehensive overview of the cutoff value and AUC is presented in Table III and Figure 2.

Discussion

Characteristic findings of adhesive capsulitis can be well demonstrated on MRI. These include hypertrophy of the coracohumeral ligament, narrowing of the axillary recess, and pathological changes at the rotator interval capsule. In addition, the subcoracoid fat triangle often becomes obscured. Moreover, features such as increased T2 signal intensity and contrast enhancement of the joint capsule further support the diagnosis of adhesive capsulitis.5

In our study on humeral head cysts, anterior GT cysts near the supraspinatus insertion and posterior GT cysts near the infraspinatus insertion were linked to tears of the supraspinatus and infraspinatus tendons.7 The underlying mechanisms remain uncertain. Cysts of the lesser tuberosity have occasionally been correlated with subscapularis tendon tears.3,19,22 Previous studies suggest that BA cysts may develop in association with rotator cuff pathology or age-related degenerative alterations.3,8 However, our findings indicate that BA cysts are associated not only with rotator cuff injuries but also with adhesive capsulitis.

The interobserver reliability was good for cyst size (ICC = 0.824) and almost perfect for the assessment of bare-area hyperintensity (κ = 0.831), indicating excellent reproducibility of the measurements. These results align with prior studies validating MRI for cystic lesion assessment.6 The strong reproducibility underscores that BA cysts can be reliably quantified, supporting their clinical utility as a diagnostic marker.

Previous studies on humeral head cysts have predominantly focused on those associated with superior rotator cuff pathologies.3 These cysts have been hypothesized to result from direct contact between bare bone and synovial fluid. Histological studies describe these cystic lesions as small depressions noted on gross inspection and pseudocystic cavities bordered by fibroconnective collagen tissue, connected to joint spaces without evidence of degenerative changes in adjacent osteochondral structures.8 Such cysts are usually located outside and superior to the GT of the humeral head. Our findings suggest that BA cysts may also be associated with adhesive capsulitis in addition to previously described rotator cuff pathologies. The size of cysts at bare-area and the presence of hyperintensity were identified as significant diagnostic markers for adhesive capsulitis, achieving high diagnostic performance (AUC: 0.837). This suggests that BA cysts may serve as complementary diagnostic indicators, particularly in cases where clinical findings are inconclusive. The exact mechanism underlying BA cyst formation in adhesive capsulitis remains unclear. One possible explanation is that capsular inflammation and fibrosis associated with adhesive capsulitis may alter the biomechanical environment of the glenohumeral joint, resulting in increased stress and synovial fluid infiltration at the bare area of the humeral head. BA cysts may also provide additional imaging clues in patients presenting with shoulder pain before significant stiffness has developed.

Our study also demonstrated that these cysts can occur in relatively younger patients without rotator cuff tears. In the adhesive capsulitis group, the mean age was 51 years, and the youngest patient with a cyst was 30 year old, suggesting that BA cysts may have an association with adhesive capsulitis independent of rotator cuff pathology. This finding highlights the need for further exploration into the pathogenesis of these cysts, particularly in patients without coexisting rotator cuff pathology.

Despite these promising findings, our study has several limitations. However, this study did not include a comprehensive comparison across a wide spectrum of shoulder pathologies. Therefore, the specificity of BA cysts for adhesive capsulitis requires further investigation in future studies including other shoulder disorders. In addition, this study is subject to limitations inherent to retrospective designs, including potential selection bias from incomplete medical records and the inability to establish causality due to the lack of longitudinal follow-up, preventing confirmation of communication between BA cysts and the joint space. Second, although patients with confirmed rotator cuff tears were excluded during the patient selection process, this study did not include a separate comparison group of patients with isolated rotator cuff disorders. Therefore, the specificity of BA cysts for adhesive capsulitis requires further investigation, limiting the ability to establish whether these cysts are exclusively associated with adhesive capsulitis. Third, arthroscopic validation of MRI findings was not performed. In addition, the study focused solely on BA cysts, excluding an analysis of other cystic lesions in asymptomatic shoulders. We also need to further observe the occurrence of cysts in patients with rotator cuff tears with and without adhesive capsulitis to further confirm that there is a greater correlation between cysts and adhesive capsulitis.

Conclusion

BA cysts, defined by their size and abnormal hyperintensity, represent useful imaging markers for shoulder adhesive capsulitis. These results emphasize the clinical significance of MRI-identified BA cysts in distinguishing adhesive capsulitis, particularly in scenarios where symptoms overlap with other shoulder disorders. Although the control group was significantly younger than the adhesive capsulitis group, multivariable logistic regression analysis demonstrated that cyst size remained independently associated with adhesive capsulitis after adjusting for age and sex. Age itself was also significantly associated with adhesive capsulitis, suggesting that both factors may contribute to the observed relationship.
