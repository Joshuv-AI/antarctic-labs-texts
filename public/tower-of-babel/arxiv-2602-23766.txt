UniFAR: A Unified Facet-Aware Retrieval Framework for Scientific Documents
Zheng Dou, Zhao Zhang, Deqing Wang, Yikun Ban, Fuzhen Zhuang

Abstract
Existing scientific document retrieval (SDR) methods primarily rely on document-centric representations learned from inter-document relationships for document-document (doc-doc) retrieval. However, the rise of LLMs and RAG has shifted SDR toward question-driven retrieval, where documents are retrieved in response to natural-language questions (q-doc). This change has led to systematic mismatches between document-centric models and question-driven retrieval, including (1) input granularity (long documents vs. short questions), (2) semantic focus (scientific discourse structure vs. specific question intent), and (3) training signals (citation-based similarity vs. question-oriented relevance). To this end, we propose UniFAR, a Unified Facet-Aware Retrieval framework to jointly support doc-doc and q-doc SDR within a single architecture. UniFAR reconciles granularity differences through adaptive multi-granularity aggregation, aligns document structure with question intent via learnable facet anchors, and unifies doc–doc and q–doc supervision through joint training. Experimental results show that UniFAR consistently outperforms prior methods across multiple retrieval tasks and base models, confirming its effectiveness and generality.

Introduction

With the rapid growth of scientific publications worldwide, scientific document retrieval (SDR) has become essential for effective knowledge discovery and scientific innovation. Dense retrieval
(
12
)
has emerged as the dominant paradigm in information retrieval, shifting the focus from lexical to semantic matching by representing queries and documents in a shared embedding space. Models like Contriever
(
10
)
and E5
(
23
)
demonstrate strong performance on general-domain benchmarks
(
14
)
. However, their lack of explicit adaptation to the scientific domain limits their ability in SDR tasks.

To address this, a line of work has extended dense retrieval to scientific literature by exploiting inter-document relationships to capture scholarly context and structural semantics beyond isolated texts. Representative methods learn document embeddings under citation-informed supervision to reflect scientific relatedness
(
5
;
18
)
, while others incorporate finer-grained signals from co-citation contexts
(
15
)
or structured discourse components
(
8
)
to enable more nuanced semantic alignment.
By relying on inter-document relationships, these methods fundamentally follow a
document-centric
paradigm, where representations are optimized to retrieve documents given a query document (doc-doc retrieval).

The development of large language models (LLMs) and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has introduced new retrieval settings centered on natural-language queries
(
13
;
9
)
. Recent studies have explored the use of LLMs and RAG for scientific literature, where SDR serves as a key interface between users, language models, and large-scale scientific corpora
(
2
;
6
)
. Beyond traditional doc-doc retrieval, SDR is increasingly required to support
question-driven
retrieval where scientific documents are retrieved in response to a natural-language question (q-doc retrieval).

This leads to systematic mismatches between document-centric retrievers and question-driven retrieval along three dimensions.
(1) Input granularity
: scientific documents are typically long and structured, whereas user questions are short and compressed, making document-level representations insufficient for fine-grained question matching
(
4
)
.

(2) Semantic focus
: document-centric modeling emphasizes discourse structure and scientific roles (e.g., background, method, result) shared across papers
(
8
)
, whereas question-driven retrieval is guided by user intent and specific information needs, causing representations optimized for document similarity to overlook intent-relevant contents
(
7
)
.

(3) Training signals
: scientific retrievers are typically trained with citation-based supervision
(
5
;
18
)
rather than explicitly optimizing question-oriented relevance, leading to a mismatch between training objectives and question-driven retrieval needs.

Overall, document-centric approaches are effective at modeling scientific relationships and structure, but exhibit systematic mismatches in question-driven retrieval.
This motivates the central goal of our work:
Can we unify both doc-doc and q-doc retrieval for scientific literature, retaining document-level relational structure while resolving mismatches in question-driven retrieval?

In this paper, we propose UniFAR, a
Uni
fied
F
acet-
A
ware
R
etrieval framework for scientific literature that bridges document-centric modeling and question-driven retrieval within a single architecture. UniFAR combines adaptive multi-granularity aggregation, facet-anchored semantic alignment, and joint doc-doc and q-doc contrastive training to address mismatches in input granularity, semantic focus, and training signals. Our main contributions are threefold:

· Unified Framework for SDR.
We unify doc-doc and q-doc retrieval for scientific literature within a single framework (UniFAR) that incorporates multi-granularity representation and aggregation as well as learnable facet-level semantic anchors.

· Facet-Aware Joint Training.
We propose a facet-aware joint training strategy that integrates doc-doc and q-doc supervision within a shared semantic space, enabling unified learning for both retrieval settings.

· Empirical Evaluation.
Extensive experiments across multiple tasks and base models show that UniFAR consistently outperforms prior methods, confirming its effectiveness and generality.

Related Work

Document-Centric.
A dominant paradigm in scientific document retrieval focuses on modeling relevance through inter-document relationships to capture scholarly context and structural semantics.
Representative work such as SPECTER
(
5
)
learns document embeddings via citation-informed contrastive learning to supervise doc-doc similarity.
Building on this paradigm, SciNCL
(
18
)
replaces binary citation supervision with neighborhood contrastive learning over citation graph embeddings, enabling graded similarity signals and more informative positive and negative sampling.
Multi-vector models
(
15
)
with textual guidance exploit co-citation sentences to supervise aspect-level embeddings, allowing document similarity to be computed through fine-grained semantic alignment.
FLeW
(
8
)
constructs facet-specific document training triplets based on citation intent and frequency, explicitly modeling scientific discourse structure via facet-level representations aligned with background, method, and result.
While these methods effectively enrich document representations using citation-derived signals, they remain confined to a document-centric paradigm with limited optimization for user questions.

Question-Driven.

With the growing adoption of LLM-based systems and RAG, scientific document retrieval (SDR) has become increasingly question-driven.
SciRepEval
(
20
)
categorizes downstream scientific tasks to explicitly include query-based retrieval, and introduces task-aware learning to train task-specific modules. MixGR
(
4
)
integrates complementary similarity signals computed at different granularities, enabling more robust matching between complex scientific queries and long-form documents. FaBle
(
7
)
targets faceted query-by-example retrieval by constructing facet-conditioned training data through decomposition and recomposition, allowing retrieval to be guided by specific semantic aspects. To improve question supervision, CCQGen
(
11
)
generates concept-covering query sets for better approximation on user information needs over scientific documents.
Despite these advances, question-driven approaches in SDR typically remain separately modeled and supervised, rather than jointly optimizing doc-doc and q-doc retrieval within a unified framework.

3
UniFAR Framework

Figure 1:
The architecture of the UniFAR framework. Scientific documents and questions are formatted as a unified sequence of segmented sentences and encoded by the
Replaceable Base Model (gray)
into token-level and sentence-level embeddings.
Facet Aggregation Module (blue)
builds on learnable facet anchors to produce input-specific facet queries, which guide the aggregation through multi-granularity attention to generate facet-level embeddings. Facet embeddings are used to compute a similarity matrix between query and candidate for each facet, supporting facet-aware retrieval.

In this section, we introduce the proposed UniFAR framework in detail.
As illustrated in Figure
1
, UniFAR consists of two components: a replaceable base model for unified contextual embeddings, and a facet-level aggregation module with multi-granularity attention and learnable facet-level semantic anchors for addressing mismatches in input granularity and semantic focus.

3.1
Input Encoding and Representation

To achieve unified modeling for both scientific documents and scientific questions, UniFAR adopts a consistent input structure and a replaceable base encoder for multi-granularity representation.

Input Format.
All inputs are organized into a unified structure of
Sentence Sequence
, where each sentence is explicitly separated by the special token
[SEP]
. As shown in Figure
1
, an input can take
either
of the following forms: a scientific document with
Title
and
d
sentences, represented as:
\textit{Input}_{\textit{d}}=\textit{Title}~\texttt{[SEP]}~S_{1}~\texttt{[SEP]}~S_{2}~\cdots~\texttt{[SEP]}~S_{d}
,
or
a scientific question (or a scientific document without
Title
) with
q
sentences, represented as:
\textit{Input}_{\textit{q}}=\texttt{[CLS]}~S_{1}~\texttt{[SEP]}~S_{2}~\cdots~\texttt{[SEP]}~S_{q}
. This unified format preserves sentence-level structure and enables consistent encoding across input types.

Multi-Granularity Representation.
As shown in Figure
1
(gray), UniFAR employs a
replaceable
base model: given an input sequence
\textit{Input}_{\textit{{d/q}}}
with
L
tokens, it produces contextualized token embeddings:

\mathbf{T}_{1},\mathbf{T}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{T}_{L}=\text{BASE}(\textit{Input})
.
Each
\mathbf{T}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{h}
corresponds to a token embedding, including special tokens such as
[CLS]
and
[SEP]
.
To further obtain sentence-level representations, mean pooling is applied over token embeddings within each sentence:

\mathbf{S}=\{\mathbf{S}_{1},\mathbf{S}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{S}_{s}\},\quad\mathbf{S}_{i}=\text{MeanPool}(\mathbf{T}_{S_{i}})

where
\mathbf{T}_{S_{i}}
denotes the set of token embeddings belonging to the
i
-th sentence separated by
[SEP]
. For scientific documents, the title is treated as an additional sentence whose representation
\mathbf{S}_{\textit{title}}\in\mathbb{R}^{h}
is also included in
\mathbf{S}
. Thus, the base model encodes each input into token-level and sentence-level embeddings, providing multi-granularity representations for subsequent facet-level aggregation.

3.2
Facet-Level Aggregation

This module introduces learnable facet anchors to derive input-specific facet queries, guiding multi-granularity attention over sentence-level and token-level representations.
A dynamic selector further determines the appropriate aggregation level according to input granularity, yielding final facet-specific embeddings.

Facet Anchors and Input-Specific Queries.

We introduce a set of
learnable facet anchors

\mathbf{A}_{facet}=\{\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{N_{facet}}\}
,
where each
\mathbf{a}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{h}
represents a latent semantic prototype for one facet. By serving as shared semantic reference points, these anchors induce a facet-aware shared representation space where scientific documents and questions are jointly represented. As shown in Figure
1
(blue, left), UniFAR projects either the title embedding
\mathbf{S}_{\textit{title}}
from scientific documents or the
[CLS]
token embedding
\mathbf{T}_{[\text{CLS}]}
from scientific questions into a contextual vector
\mathbf{E}_{input}\in\mathbb{R}^{h}
that captures input semantics.
The input-specific facet queries
\mathbf{Q}_{facet}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{facet}\times h}
are then generated by concatenating
\mathbf{E}_{input}
with each facet anchor
\mathbf{a}_{i}\in\mathbf{A}_{facet}
and transforming them through a lightweight MLP followed by residual connection and layer normalization:

\mathbf{Q}_{facet}=\text{Add\&Norm}\!\left(\text{MLP}\!\left([\,\mathbf{A}_{facet};\mathbf{E}_{input}\,]\right)\right).

By conditioning learnable facet anchors on contextual input embeddings, UniFAR generates
input-specific
facet queries which guides the subsequent multi-granularity attention aggregation to produce facet-level representations.

Multi-Granularity Attention Aggregation.
Given the input-specific facet queries
\mathbf{Q}_{facet}
, UniFAR aggregates token-level and sentence-level embeddings through a multi-granularity attention mechanism, as shown in Figure
1
(blue, right). Specifically, the sentence embeddings
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\times h}
from mean pooling and the token embeddings
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times h}
from the base model serve as key–value pairs for two multi-head attention branches:

\mathbf{E}_{facet}=\begin{cases}\text{MHA}_{sent}(\mathbf{Q}_{facet},\mathbf{S},\mathbf{S}),|\mathcal{S}|\geq N_{facet}\\
\text{MHA}_{tok}(\mathbf{Q}_{facet},\mathbf{T},\mathbf{T}),|\mathcal{S}|<N_{facet}\end{cases}

where
|\mathcal{S}|
denotes the number of sentences in the input sequence. Sentence-level attention is reliable only when the input contains a sufficient number of sentences to support facet discrimination (i.e.,
|\mathcal{S}|\geq N_{facet}
). Short inputs, such as scientific questions or brief document segments, lack enough sentence units to ensure stable facet-specific alignment, making token-level attention a more appropriate and fine-grained alternative. Thus, the selector dynamically chooses between sentence-level and token-level outputs depending on
|\mathcal{S}|
, ensuring effective facet representation across varying input lengths of scientific documents and questions.

3.3
Facet-Aware Retrieval

UniFAR supports unified retrieval by treating the input
query
as either a query document or a scientific question, while the
candidate
refers to a scientific document in the corpus. As shown at the top of Figure
1
, the facet aggregation module produces facet-level embeddings for the input query
q
and the candidate document
c
, denoted as
\{\mathbf{e}_{q}^{(f)}\}_{f=1}^{N_{\text{facet}}}
and
\{\mathbf{e}_{c}^{(f)}\}_{f=1}^{N_{\text{facet}}}
, respectively. Their relevance is modeled as a facet similarity matrix
\mathbf{M}_{q,c}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{facet}}\times N_{\text{facet}}}
, where each entry is computed as
\mathbf{M}_{q,c}^{(i,j)}=\operatorname{sim}\!\left(\mathbf{e}_{q}^{(i)},\mathbf{e}_{c}^{(j)}\right)
.

This formulation decomposes retrieval relevance into fine-grained facet interactions, enabling facet-aware retrieval. Different calculation strategies over the facet similarity matrix can be applied to support diverse retrieval demands.

4
Facet-Aware Joint Training

Figure 2:
The facet-aware joint training strategy of UniFAR.
Facet-Aware Training Unit (FTU, left)
is constructed via LLM-based facet labeling and question generation, containing a query document, its facet-specific positives and negatives, and facet-aware scientific questions. FTUs are then forwarded through
UniFAR (middle)
as described in Section
3
to produce attention maps and facet-level document/question embeddings for
Joint Optimization (right)
. Three objectives: (1)
\mathcal{L}_{\textit{DD}}
for doc–doc alignment, (2)
\mathcal{L}_{\textit{QD}}
for q–doc retrieval, and (3)
\mathcal{L}_{\textit{KL}}
for facet-sentence attention consistency, are jointly optimized through backpropagation to update all trainable components of UniFAR.

We propose a facet-aware joint training strategy for UniFAR framework to address the mismatch in training signals between citation-based document similarity and question-oriented relevance.
Figure
2
shows the overall workflow.

4.1
Training Data Construction

Unlike prior methods
(
5
;
18
;
7
)
that mainly rely on document–document data, we construct training data that jointly incorporate labeled scientific documents and facet-specific scientific questions, as illustrated on the left of Figure
2
.

We begin by constructing the
Documents
set from the facet-specific triplets in FLeW
(
8
)
, which follow a three-facet structure for scientific documents:
background (
bg
)
,
method (
mt
)
and
result (
rs
)
. In FLeW, each query document
d_{q}
appears in multiple independent triplets of the form
(d_{q},d^{(f)}_{+},d^{(f)}_{-})
, where
f\in\{bg,mt,rs\}
and each triplet contains exactly one positive–negative document pair for facet
f
. To avoid redundancy and expose richer facet-level relations, we merge all triplets sharing the same query document
d_{q}
into a single query-centered group, resulting in facet-specific sets
\mathcal{D}^{(f)}_{\text{pos}}=\{d^{(f)}_{+}\}
and
\mathcal{D}^{(f)}_{\text{neg}}=\{d^{(f)}_{-}\}
for each facet
f
. Unlike FLeW, which generates coarse text spans for facet segmentation, we obtain fine-grained sentence-level facet labels by prompting an LLM to classify each sentence into one of the three facets
f\in\{bg,mt,rs\}
, producing fully labeled scientific documents. To construct the
Questions
set, we prompt the LLM with the query document
d_{q}
and its facet-positive documents
\mathcal{D}^{(f)}_{\text{pos}}
, instructing it to generate one scientific question
q^{(f)}
for each facet.

The labeled documents and generated questions are then grouped by their shared query document to construct unified instances. Each instance forms a Facet-Aware Training Unit (FTU), consisting of the query document
d_{q}
, facet-specific positive and negative document sets
\mathcal{D}_{\textit{pos}}^{(f)}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\textit{neg}}^{(f)}
, and three facet-aware questions
q^{(f)}
:

\text{FTU}=\{d_{q},\mathcal{D}_{\textit{pos}}^{(f)},\mathcal{D}_{\textit{neg}}^{(f)},q^{(f)}\mid f\in\{bg,mt,rs\}\}.

We release the constructed FTUs publicly to facilitate future research. The prompting templates used for sentence labeling and question generation are provided in Appendix
C
, and detail statistics of the constructed data are provided in Appendix
B
.

4.2
Forward and Optimization

Each FTU is fed into UniFAR for forward computation, where all inputs are encoded by the replaceable base encode (Section
3.1
) and then passed through the facet aggregation module (Section
3.2
). As illustrated on the right of Figure
2
, this forward process produces facet-level embeddings for documents and questions, along with facet–sentence attention maps generated by multi-head attention.

Instead of the triplet margin loss
19
used in existing methods
5
;
18
;
8
;
7
, we adopt the widely used InfoNCE loss
(
17
)
as the contrastive learning objective. Given an anchor
\mathbf{a}
, a set of positive embeddings
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathbf{p}_{i}\}
and a set of negative embeddings
\mathcal{N}=\{\mathbf{n}_{m}\}
, the loss function is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{CL}}=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum\limits_{p_{i}\in\mathcal{P}}\log\frac{e^{\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{p}_{i})/\tau}}{\sum\limits_{p_{j}\in\mathcal{P}}e^{\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{p}_{j})/\tau}+\sum\limits_{n_{m}\in\mathcal{N}}e^{\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{n}_{m})/\tau}}

where
\mathrm{sim}(\cdot)
denotes a similarity function and
\tau
is the temperature hyperparameter.

This contrastive objective is jointly applied to doc-doc and q-doc retrieval under each facet, encouraging documents and questions to share a unified semantic space.
For each facet
f
, the two losses are defined as:

\scalebox{0.84}{$\mathcal{L}^{(f)}_{\text{DD}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{CL}}\big(\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{d}_{q},\;\mathcal{P}=\{\mathbf{d}^{(f)}_{\textit{+}}\},\;\mathcal{N}=\{\mathbf{d}^{(f)}_{-}\}\big)$},

\mathcal{L}^{(f)}_{\text{QD}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{CL}}\big(\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{q}^{(f)},\;\mathcal{P}=\{\mathbf{d}_{q}\}\cup\{\mathbf{d}^{(f)}_{\textit{+}}\},\;\mathcal{N}=\{\mathbf{d}^{(f)}_{-}\}\big)

where
\mathbf{d}_{q}
is the query document embedding,
\mathbf{d}^{(f)}_{\textit{+}}
and
\mathbf{d}^{(f)}_{-}
are the facet-specific positive and negative document embeddings, and
\mathbf{q}^{(f)}
is the facet-aware question embedding.

To explicitly supervise facet-aware aggregation, we further introduce a KL-divergence loss that aligns the predicted facet attention with annotated facet labels by sentence. For each document
d
, UniFAR produces a facet–sentence attention matrix
\mathbf{A}
(Figure
2
, right). Given the gold facet–sentence alignment matrix
\mathbf{G}
derived from facet labeling (Section
4.1
), we normalize both matrices row-wise to obtain probability distributions
\tilde{\mathbf{A}},\tilde{\mathbf{G}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{F}^{(d)}|\times|\mathcal{S}^{(d)}|}
. The loss function is then defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{KL}}^{(d)}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}^{(d)}|}\sum\limits_{f\in\mathcal{F}^{(d)}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}^{(d)}|}\sum\limits_{s_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{(d)}}\tilde{G}_{f,s_{i}}\log\!\frac{\tilde{G}_{f,s_{i}}}{\tilde{A}_{f,s_{i}}}.

The total training objective integrates the facet-level contrastive losses and the doc-level KL-loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|}\sum\limits_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\Big(\mathcal{L}_{\text{DD}}^{(f)}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{QD}}^{(f)}\Big)+\lambda\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum\limits_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{(d)}

where
\mathcal{F}=\{\textit{background},\textit{method},\textit{result}\}
and
\lambda
is a weighting coefficient. Details in Figure
2
show that
\mathcal{L}_{\textit{KL}}
is detached from the base model, and gradients from
\mathcal{L}_{\textit{QD}}
are restricted to the question branch to preserve document representations.

Experiments

5.1
Experimental Setup

Base Models.
To evaluate the adaptability of UniFAR with different base models, we adopt three representative models.
SciBERT

(
3
)
is a domain-specific encoder pretrained on scientific corpora, serving as a standard baseline for scientific document understanding.
Contriever

(
10
)
is an unsupervised dense retriever trained via large-scale contrastive learning on general-domain text, representing domain-agnostic models.
MPNet

(
21
)
combines masked and permuted language modeling, providing strong general-purpose representations.

Baseline Methods.
To ensure fair comparison across different backbone encoders, we group all baselines by their underlying base models. Most prior methods are built on
SciBERT
for its domain specificity, including the citation-driven models SPECTER
(
5
)
and SciNCL
(
18
)
, the co-citation-based models CoSentBERT and Aspire
(
15
)
, as well as the facet-aware approaches FaBle
(
7
)
and FLeW
(
8
)
. For the
Contriever
group, we include OpenScholar Retriever (OSRetriever)
(
2
)
, which adapts contrastive training to scientific document chunks for domain alignment.

Benchmarks and Metrics.
We evaluate UniFAR on both question–document (q-doc) and document–document (doc-doc) retrieval settings. For the q–doc retrieval, we evaluate on
DORIS-MAE

(
22
)
, a multi-level benchmark featuring diverse queries at different semantic granularities. For doc-doc retrieval, we use
CSFCube

(
16
)
, a facet-level benchmark where each query paper is annotated with three facets consistent with UniFAR’s facet schema. For evaluation, we use Recall@5, R-Precision, and MAP from
DORIS-MAE
; and report nDCG
%20
and MAP for
CSFCube
following prior works
(
15
;
7
)
. Detailed descriptions of benchmarks and implementation settings are further provided in Appendix
D
and
A
.

Facet-Aware Retrieval Strategies.
Based on the facet similarity matrix (Section
3.3
), UniFAR uses
different strategies across retrieval tasks. For
DORIS-MAE
(q-doc), we compute the final similarity by averaging the diagonal entries of the matrix, providing an overall relevance across facets for multi-level queries.
For
CSFCube
(doc-doc), we compute relevance using the facet-wise diagonal similarities, evaluating each facet independently under the benchmark’s fixed facet schema.

Analysis

As shown in Table
3
, we conduct ablation analyses to examine how multi-granularity aggregation, learnable facet anchors, and joint training objectives in UniFAR address mismatches in input granularity, semantic focus, and training signals.

Multi-Granularity Aggregation.
Removing sentence-level attention (w/o
\text{MHA}_{sent}
) causes a larger drop on CSFCube (doc-doc), where long scientific documents benefit from sentence-level semantic structure for facet aggregation. Conversely, removing token-level attention (w/o
\text{MHA}_{tok}
) leads to greater degradation on DORIS-MAE (q-doc), where short queries depend more heavily on fine-grained attention over individual tokens for facet-relevant evidence. These degradation patterns indicate that combining sentence-level and token-level attention with adaptive granularity selection is essential for maintaining robust facet embeddings across different retrieval tasks.

Learnable Facet Anchors.
Freezing the anchors (Freeze
\mathbf{A}_{facet}
) yields mild degradation across both benchmarks, indicating that static anchors initialized by facet words are still useful for structuring facet queries. As the only structural source of facet-specific differentiation, fully removing the anchors (w/o
\mathbf{A}_{facet}
) causes all facet queries to collapse into the same vector, yielding identical facet embeddings. This collapse severely weakens facet disentanglement and leads to notable performance degradation on both benchmarks. Removing the input-specific refinement (w/o
\mathbf{E}_{input}
) also reduces performance, suggesting that anchors alone are insufficient and adapting them to each input is essential for capturing facet-specific nuances.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we present UniFAR, a unified facet-aware retrieval framework for scientific literature that jointly supports document-document and question-document retrieval.
By integrating adaptive multi-granularity aggregation, learnable facet anchors, and facet-aware joint training, UniFAR reconciles systematic mismatches between doc-doc and q-doc retrieval, including input granularity, semantic focus, and training signals.
Extensive experiments across multiple benchmarks and base models demonstrate that UniFAR consistently outperforms prior approaches in both retrieval settings. Further ablation analyses confirm the contributions of key components and training objectives.
We wish UniFAR can provide a fundamental and flexible framework for future scientific knowledge discovery based on LLMs and RAG.

Limitations

In this work, the number of facets in UniFAR is fixed during training and shared across retrieval tasks for simplicity and stability. Nevertheless, the proposed framework itself is not restricted to a specific facet cardinality, and can naturally support different choices of
N_{facet}
by adjusting the number of learnable facet anchors. Dynamically adapting the number or granularity of facets to different retrieval scenarios, query complexities, or domain characteristics may further improve flexibility and retrieval effectiveness.
Future work will explore leveraging facet-level representations for adaptive retrieval augmentation and knowledge grounding in scientific domain, extending the framework toward more interpretable and generalizable retrieval systems.

Appendix D
Benchmark Details

DORIS-MAE

(
22
)
is designed to evaluate scientific document retrieval under complex, multi-intent information needs. Each query is a human-authored, long-form scientific question that is hierarchically decomposed into semantically distinct aspects and sub-aspects, enabling fine-grained relevance assessment beyond single-sentence queries. This multi-level structure substantially increases query diversity and difficulty, and exposes the limitations of retrievers trained on simple or single-aspect queries. By explicitly modeling aspect-level relevance and supporting both full queries and sub-queries, DORIS-MAE provides a challenging and realistic benchmark for question–document retrieval in scientific literature, making it well suited for evaluating UniFAR’s ability to handle question-driven retrieval with complex semantic structure.

CSFCube

(
16
)
is designed for faceted query-by-example retrieval in scientific literature, where each query consists of a scientific paper paired with a specific facet of interest. The dataset is constructed through expert annotation following TREC-style pooling and adjudication, producing graded relevance judgments with high inter-annotator agreement. By explicitly associating relevance with rhetorical facets of scientific discourse, CSFCube enables evaluation of document–document similarity at the facet level rather than treating documents as monolithic units. This design makes CSFCube a principled benchmark for assessing whether retrieval models can capture fine-grained, facet-specific semantic alignment between scientific documents, directly matching the doc-doc setting targeted by UniFAR.

LitSearch

(
1
)
constructs retrieval questions from in-line citation contexts and then refines them through expert validation, producing information needs that closely mirror how researchers formulate queries during literature exploration. It contains both human-written and rigorously validated LLM-generated questions, and evaluates retrieval over full papers rather than abstracts. These characteristics make LitSearch a demanding and practically oriented benchmark, providing a complementary setting for evaluating the generality of UniFAR.

To illustrate the interpretability of facet-level aggregation in UniFAR, we present a case study on a randomly selected sample from LitSearch

In Figure

Background

This pattern indicates that UniFAR identifies these early tokens as defining the conceptual frame of the question. The
Method
facet exhibits localized and moderate activations in the middle portion of the question, primarily around mentions of model or technical procedures such as “transformer language models” and “generation”. The
Result

In Figures

Background

These attention patterns show that UniFAR effectively disentangles background, method, and result information in both the question and the retrieved documents, yielding facet embeddings that correspond to distinct components of scientific discourse. The facet-specific signals track introductory context, procedural detail, and empirical findings aligned with the common structure of scientific writing. This alignment indicates that UniFAR’s multi-granularity aggregation does not diffuse attention uniformly but selectively focuses on segments that carry facet-relevant evidence. By grounding attention with facet anchors, UniFAR produces interpretable and stable facet embeddings that reflect semantic structure and question intent in realistic scientific document retrieval scenarios.

Scientific Document 1

Title:

Generating Informative Conclusions for Argumentative Texts

Abstract:

The purpose of an argumentative text is to support a certain conclusion. (S0)

Yet, they are often omitted, expecting readers to infer them rather. (S1)

While appropriate when reading an individual text, this rhetorical device limits accessibility when browsing many texts (e.g., on a search engine or on social media). (S2)

In these scenarios, an explicit conclusion makes for a good candidate summary of an argumentative text. (S3)

This is especially true if the conclusion is informative, emphasizing specific concepts from the text. (S4)

With this paper we introduce the task of generating informative conclusions: First, Webis-ConcluGen-21 is compiled, a large-scale corpus of 136,996 samples of argumentative texts and their conclusions. (S5)

Second, two paradigms for conclusion generation are investigated; one extractive, the other abstractive in nature. (S6)

The latter exploits argumentative knowledge that augment the data via control codes and finetuning the BART model on several subsets of the corpus. (S7)

Third, insights are provided into the suitability of our corpus for the task, the differences between the two generation paradigms, the trade-off between informativeness and conciseness, and the impact of encoding argumentative knowledge. (S8)

The corpus, code, and the trained models are publicly available. (S9)

Scientific Document 2

Title:

Explainable Unsupervised Argument Similarity Rating with Abstract Meaning Representation and Conclusion Generation

Abstract:

When assessing the similarity of arguments, researchers typically use approaches that do not provide interpretable evidence or justifications for their ratings. (S0)

Hence, the features that determine argument similarity remain elusive. (S1)

We address this issue by introducing novel argument similarity metrics that aim at high performance and explainability. (S2)

We show that Abstract Meaning Representation (AMR) graphs can be useful for representing arguments, and that novel AMR graph metrics can offer explanations for argument similarity ratings. (S3)

We start from the hypothesis that similar premises often lead to similar conclusions and extend an approach for AMR-based argument similarity rating by estimating, in addition, the similarity of conclusions that we automatically infer from the arguments used as premises. (S4)

We show that AMR similarity metrics make argument similarity judgements more interpretable and may even support argument quality judgements. (S5)

Our approach provides significant performance improvements over strong baselines in a fully unsupervised setting. (S6)

Finally, we make first steps to address the problem of reference-less evaluation of argumentative conclusion generations. (S7)

Title:

Abstract:

The premises of an argument give evidence or other reasons to support a conclusion. (S0)

Our best model variant achieves an F1-score of 0.885, outperforming the previous state-of-the-art and being on par with human experts. (S6)

While manual evaluation reveals the quality of the generated conclusions, their impact remains low ultimately. (S7)
