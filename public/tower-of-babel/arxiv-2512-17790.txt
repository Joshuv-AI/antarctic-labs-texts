Introduction

Ramsey theory is a central subject in combinatorics which has very recently seen a number of impressive developments. Tracing its origins to the work of Frank Ramsey in 1930
[
29
]
, the (classical) Ramsey number of a graph
G
is the minimum
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that any red-blue colouring of the edges of the complete graph
K_{n}
contains a monochromatic copy of
G
. Ramsey proved that
R(G)
exists for all
G
. Erdős and Szekeres
[
19
]
showed that
R(K_{k})\leq 4^{k}
, a result which was only improved by subexponential terms until the recent breakthrough of Campos, Griffiths, Morris and Sahasrabudhe
[
7
]
and the subsequent optimization of their ideas by Gupta, Ndiaye, Norin and Wei
[
22
]
to
R(K_{k})\leq 3.8^{k}
for all sufficiently large
k
. In the other direction, the lower bound of
R(K_{k})\geq 2^{k/2}
by Erdős
[
20
]
initiated the
probabilistic method
in combinatorics.

We refer the reader to the classical monograph of Graham, Rothschild and Spencer
[
21
]
and the more recent survey of Conlon, Fox and Sudakov
[
16
]
. We mention that other very recent results in Ramsey theory have also arisen from the use of (or indeed have driven the development of) powerful and varied tools in combinatorics, such as algebraic constructions
[
28
]
, random graphs
[
24
,
27
]
and nibble tools
[
8
]
for off-diagonal Ramsey numbers, and hypergraph containers
[
4
]
for induced Ramsey numbers.

In this work we are interested in a sub-area of Ramsey theory called
zero-sum
Ramsey theory. This has its origin in the celebrated Erdős-Ginzburg-Ziv theorem
[
18
]
, stating that given a sequence of
2m-1
elements in
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
, there exists a subsequence of
m
elements summing to zero. Motivated by this result, the following notion was introduced by Bialostocki and Dierker
[
5
]
and by Caro
[
9
]
.

Definition 1.1
.

For a (finite) graph
G
and a finite abelian group
\Gamma
such that
|\Gamma|
divides
e(G)
, the
zero-sum Ramsey number
of
G
over
\Gamma
, denoted by
R(G,\Gamma)
, is the minimum integer
t
such that for any edge-colouring
c:E(K_{t})\rightarrow\Gamma
, there exists a zero-sum copy of
G
in
K_{t}
, i.e. a copy of
G
so that
\sum_{e\in E(G)}c(e)=0_{\Gamma}
.

It is easy to see that
R(G,\Gamma)\leq R_{|\Gamma|}(G)
, where
R_{r}(G)
represents the
r
–colour (classical) Ramsey number of
G
, and therefore
R(G,\Gamma)
is finite. A monochromatic colouring of
E(K_{n})
with a non-zero element of
\Gamma
also shows that the requirement for
|\Gamma|
to divide
e(G)
is necessary for this quantity to be finite. In the following exposition of results, we always implicitly assume that
|\Gamma|
divides
e(G)
.

Exact values of
R(G,\Gamma)
are known only in very few cases. Caro
[
10
]
determined the exact value of
R(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
for all graphs
G
and recently Alvarado, Colucci and Parente
[
3
]
determined
R(F,\mathbb{Z}_{3})
for all forests
F
. Alvarado, Colucci, Parente and Souza
[
2
]
determined
R(F,\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2})
for all odd forests
F
and
R(C_{4},\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{d})
for all
d\geq 2
.

In terms of general bounds, Alon and Caro
[
1
]
proved that
R(K_{n},\mathbb{Z}_{k})\leq n+c(k)
. Note, however, that zero-sum Ramsey numbers are not monotone under graph containment (unlike their classical counterparts): for example,
R(C_{4},\mathbb{Z}_{2})=4
but
R(2K_{2},\mathbb{Z}_{2})=5
, where
2K_{2}
is a matching of size
2
. It therefore does not follow from Alon and Caro’s result that zero-sum Ramsey numbers of all graphs are small. In the opposite direction, if
k=e(G)
then
R(G,\mathbb{Z}_{k})\geq R(G)

[
5
,
9
]
. We refer the reader to the survey of Caro
[
11
]
for a (somewhat dated) general overview of the field.

The majority of work on zero-sum Ramsey theory has so far focused on cyclic groups
\mathbb{Z}_{k}
, with a few notable exceptions such as
[
2
]
where the groups
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{d}
were investigated, or
[
12
]
and
[
6
]
and the references therein, which provide upper bounds for the existence of
some
zero-sum cycle, without any control over its length, over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{d}
and over general groups of odd order, respectively. Our main theorem is the first we are aware of that establishes a general upper bound for a fixed graph over
any
finite abelian group
\Gamma
; namely, we prove that the zero-sum Ramsey number of any bounded-degree graph is linear.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Delta
be a positive integer. Then there exists a constant
C=C(\Delta)
such that for any graph
G
with maximum degree
\Delta
and any finite abelian group
\Gamma_{0}
such that
|\Gamma_{0}|
divides
e(G)
, we have

R(G,\Gamma_{0})\leq C\cdot v(G).

Note that it suffices to prove
Theorem

1.2
for
|\Gamma_{0}|=e(G)
, since in general we have
\Gamma_{0}\leqslant\Gamma_{0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{e(G)/|\Gamma_{0}|}
and the latter is a group of order precisely
e(G)
. It also clearly suffices to consider graphs without isolated vertices, in which case
v(G)
and
e(G)
are at most a factor of
\Delta
apart. We henceforth assume that
|\Gamma_{0}|=e(G)=n
and prove the above theorem with an upper bound of the form
C\cdot n
.

As mentioned above, we know that
R(G,\Gamma_{0})\leq R_{n}(G)
, the
n
-colour classical Ramsey number of
G
. This upper bound is in general much larger than linear in
n
. It is worth noting, however, that if we instead fix the number of colours to be
r
, say, then
R_{r}(G)
is indeed linear in
n
.

Theorem 1.3

(Chvátal, Ródl, Szemerédi and Trotter
[
13
]
)
.

Let
r,\Delta\geq 2
be positive integers. Then there exists a constant
C^{\prime}=C^{\prime}(r,\Delta)
such that for any graph
G
with maximum degree
\Delta
, we have

R_{r}(G)\leq C^{\prime}\cdot v(G).

The constant
C^{\prime}
obtained in
[
13
]
has a tower-type dependency on
\Delta
, owing to the use of the Szemerédi regularity lemma. For
r=2
, the best known upper bound of
C^{\prime}(\Delta,2)\leq 2^{c\Delta\log\Delta}
for some absolute constant
c>0
is due to Conlon, Fox and Sudakov
[
15
]
– see also the survey
[
16
]
for a history of this problem; this is generally believed to not be tight, and the situation for
r\geq 3
is even less clear. In our proof of
Theorem

1.2
, however, we make use of
Theorem

1.3
(for general

), and hence the value of
C
as a function of
\Delta
depends on the value of
C^{\prime}
in
Theorem

1.3
. Due to this, we do not make an attempt to optimize our arguments for the value of

throughout the paper, and our proof yields
C(\Delta)\leq\Delta^{42\Delta^{6}}\cdot C^{\prime}(r,\Delta)
for
r=200\Delta^{12}
.

Note.
Very recently and independently, Colucci and D’Emidio
[
14

proved the linear upper bound
R(F,\mathbb{Z}_{p})\leq v(F)+9p
for all forests

with

1.1
Terminology and notation

Given an abelian group

Given

Given

, and

3.

The Cauchy-Davenport theorem only applies in

. We instead use a result due to Kneser (

If

Algebra basics

We now take stock of some of the elementary algebraic tools we will require. First and foremost, we will be making extensive use of the classification theorem of finite abelian groups.

Theorem 3.1

(Fundamental theorem of finite abelian groups
[
17
]
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be an abelian group of order
n>1
and let the unique factorization of
n
into distinct prime powers be

n=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}p_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots p_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}.

Then

1.

\Gamma\cong A_{1}\times A_{2}\times\cdots\times A_{k}
for some groups
A_{i}
with
|A_{i}|=p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
, and

2.

for each
A\in\{A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{k}\}
with
|A|=p^{\alpha}
, we have

A\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\beta_{1}}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\beta_{2}}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\beta_{\ell}}}

for some
\beta_{1}\geq\beta_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\beta_{\ell}\geq 1
satisfying

\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}+\cdots+\beta_{\ell}=\alpha\text{ (where $\ell$ and $\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{\ell}$ depend on $i$)}
.

Moreover, as
{\mathbb{Z}}_{mn}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
if and only if
m
and
n
are coprime, it follows that each finite abelian group also has a unique factorization
\Gamma\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{m_{1}}\times\cdots\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{m_{k}^{\prime}}
where
m_{i}|m_{i+1}
for each
1\leq i<k^{\prime}
. This decomposition is called an
invariant factor decomposition
.

Next, we will need the following generalization of the Cauchy-Davenport theorem.

Theorem 3.2

(Kneser’s Theorem
[
25
]
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a finite abelian group and
A,B\subseteq\Gamma
. Then
|A+B|\geq\min\{|\Gamma|,|A|+|B|-1\}
or
A+B
contains a coset of some nontrivial subgroup of
\Gamma
.

In our proof of
Theorem

1.2
, we will use the above to generate cosets
r_{1}+H_{1}\subset\Gamma_{0}
,
r_{2}+H_{2}\subset\Gamma_{0}/H_{1}
, and so on. We then need the following simple lemma to put these pieces back together to obtain
\Gamma_{0}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Given
H
a subgroup of
\Gamma
,
r\in\Gamma
, and two sets
A=r+H
and
B
satisfying
|B/H|=|\Gamma/H|
, we have
A+B=\Gamma
.

Proof.

Given
g\in\Gamma
, there exists some element of
g-r+H
in
B
, i.e. it is the case that
b=g-r+h
for some
b\in B
and
h\in H
. We also know that
a=r-h\in A
since
-h\in H
, and so
a+b=g-r+h+r-h=g
, as desired.
∎

Hence, a tuple with a smaller

With this definition, we can give a general overview of our algorithm. It is divided into three phases. First, we begin by pointing out that we can assume there is no monochromatic copy of
G

. This fact will become useful in Phase 1.

In Phase 0, we complete the setup. We look for a
\kappa

\sigma

, we will either go to Phase 1 or straight to Phase 2. In both cases, the nature of
\mathcal{C}
will inform how we look for gadgets.

In Phase 1, we begin by taking the largest class of vertices
\mathcal{C}_{i}\coloneqq\{x\in R:\mathcal{C}(x)=i\}
and setting that to be our new set of vertices (updating the colouring appropriately), thereby returning us to a state the looks a lot like the trivial well-behaved case (but losing up to a constant factor on the size of
R
). Then, we will realize blueprint pairs from
K
at multiplicity
\beta

of
\Gamma
. We will then preform an operation that will reduce our colouring to one on
\Gamma/H
, and proceed to realize blueprint pairs with this colouring. This will repeat until we reach a group
\Gamma/H^{\prime}
with size smaller than
|\Gamma_{0}|/\alpha
, at which point we move on to Phase 2.

In Phase 2, much like in Phase 1, we will realize blueprint pairs from
K
, but now it will be at multiplicity
2
. The rest is exactly the same as Phase 1: we find a coset, quotient our operating group by it, and proceed, until we have reached the trivial group.

Then, to finish, we will take our set of functions

and extend them uniformly so that each embeds a copy of
G
into
R_{0}
. We will aim to conclude that for each

.

Lemma 9.2
.

Suppose that

and

are positive integers such that

,

, and a vertex colouring

|I|\geq|X|/(\lambda-1)^{d+d^{\prime}}-d-d^{\prime}

for all

Proof.

First, if
|X|\leq d+d^{\prime}
then setting

the lemma is vacuously true. Otherwise, fix sets

and

in

so that

. Since

does not contain a
(d^{\prime},\lambda)
–gadget, it follows that for each

we have

Denote by

the most popular colour in the set above, so that there exist at least
(|X|-(d+d^{\prime}-m-2))/(\lambda-1)

such that

.

Similarly, note that for each
1\leq i<d

|\{c(u_{i}w^{\prime}V):w^{\prime}\in X\setminus(U\cup V)\}|\leq\lambda-1

holds and analogously define

to be the most popular colour in the above set.

Then there exists a set of vertices

with

such that for all

, we have

and

.

Further, let us fix a vertex

and observe that

and

both take at most
\lambda-1
colours across all

, and so we can again restrict to the most popular colours (denoted by

and

, respectively) to obtain a set

of size

such that for all

,

and

. Further, let us define

Then observe that for all

\displaystyle c(ww^{\prime})

\displaystyle=c(ww^{\prime})+c(w^{\prime}U)-c(w^{\prime}U)-(d-1)\cdot c(w^{\prime}V)+(d-1)\cdot c(w^{\prime}V)

\displaystyle=g_{1}-q_{0}+(d-1)\cdot c(w^{\prime}V)

\displaystyle=g_{1}-q_{0}+(d-1)\cdot q_{1}-(d-1)\cdot c(ww^{\prime})

.

(recalling that

takes values in
\Gamma/H^{\prime}

Letting

constant, we then have that

of

Conclusion and further work

In this paper we considered zero-sum Ramsey numbers in the case where
|\Gamma_{0}|=e(G)
. Note that
Theorem

1.2
immediately gives the same bound
R(G,\Gamma)\leq Cn
whenever
|\Gamma|
divides
e(G)
, where
n=e(G)
. However, more interestingly, we can observe that the value of
C
should decrease in the case of
N\coloneqq e(G)/|\Gamma|
large. Let us give an informal overview of how the computations in our proof change in this case. Note that if
m_{1}<n
(with
m_{1}
as in
1Embedding-Algorithm

), then we run Phase 1 of the algorithm in our proof for fewer iterations. Further, recall how we computed
\alpha
in
Section

4
: we required
|K|\cdot 2\alpha\geq|\Gamma_{0}|
. In this case, we can divide both
\alpha
and
\beta
by
N
(to get new
\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta^{\prime}
to be used in the algorithm) which implies that the new constant needed is about
C\cdot\frac{C^{\prime}(\alpha\beta/N^{2},\Delta)}{C^{\prime}(\alpha\beta,\Delta)}\cdot\frac{1}{N^{2\Delta+1}\cdot\Delta^{(2\beta)(N-1)/N}}
(see the calculations at the end of
Section

9.1
). That is, we run our algorithm until
\alpha/N=1
, and so we never enter Phase 1 of the algorithm. This means that we do not require
Theorem

1.3
, and then the new constant that replaces
C
is determined entirely by
Corollary

8.2
and is on the order of
\Delta^{3}
. We choose not to present all the formal details here.

It is natural to try and extend our result to other graph classes, and try to prove linear upper bounds for more general graphs than just those of bounded degree. Recall, however, that if
n=e(G)
then
R(G,\mathbb{Z}_{n})\geq R(G)
, and so we cannot expect to obtain linear zero-sum Ramsey numbers for graphs that have too large classical Ramsey numbers. A natural family to consider is that of graphs of bounded degeneracy, and here a celebrated result of Lee
[
26
]
shows that
R(G)
is indeed linear for such graphs
G
. We expect the same to be true for
R(G,\Gamma_{0})
.

Conjecture 1
.

Let
d\geq 2
be an integer. Then there exists a constant
C=C(d)
such that for any graph
G
with degeneracy at most
d
and
n
edges, and any finite abelian group
\Gamma_{0}
of order dividing
n
, we have
R(G,\Gamma_{0})\leq C\cdot n
.

Note that the only point in our proof where we used the assumption of bounded degree is in
Section

4
, where our construction of blueprint pairs that we used in the rest of the proof leveraged the fact that these blueprint pairs each only use a small number of vertices. Therefore, if one could prove a statement akin to
Proposition

4.2
for
d
-degenerate graphs, a positive resolution to
1
would then follow using the same strategy as in our current work. We also note that
1
–degenerate graphs are precisely forests, and so a very recent result of Colucci and D’Emidio
[
14
]
resolves
1
for
d=1
,
\Gamma_{0}=\mathbb{Z}_{p}
and

sufficiently large.

Finally, we note that we have studied zero-sum Ramsey numbers of bounded degree graphs under the assumption that the group is abelian. It is natural to ask a similar question for non-abelian groups. Given a graph
G
with an ordering
\sigma=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
of its edges and a finite group

, write

for the smallest positive integer

(if it exists) such that any edge-colouring

contains a copy of

.

Problem 2
.

Given an integer
\Delta\geq 2
, does there exist a constant

The non-abelian case immediately provides many significant complications. A fundamental issue in this case is that we can no longer perform basic translations of our colouring. Namely, if
