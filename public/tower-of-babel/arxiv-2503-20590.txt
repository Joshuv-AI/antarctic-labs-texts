Dual-Issue Execution of Mixed Integer and Floating-Point Workloads on Energy-Efficient In-Order RISC-V Cores
Luca Colagrande, Luca Benini

Abstract
To meet the computational requirements of modern workloads under tight energy constraints, general-purpose accelerator architectures have to integrate an ever-increasing number of extremely area- and energy-efficient processing elements (PEs). In this context, single-issue in-order cores are commonplace, but lean dual-issue cores could boost PE IPC, especially for the common case of mixed integer and floating-point workloads. We develop the COPIFT methodology and RISC-V ISA extensions to enable low-cost and flexible dual-issue execution of mixed integer and floating-point instruction sequences. On such kernels, our methodology achieves speedups of 1.47x, reaching a peak 1.75 instructions per cycle, and 1.37x energy improvements on average, over optimized RV32G baselines.



I
Introduction

With the end of Dennard scaling, energy-efficiency has become a primary constraint in the design of high-performance computer architectures
[
1
]
.
Simultaneously, the rapid growth of
Machine Learning (ML)
models, requiring immense computational resources for training and inference, are dramatically escalating the demand for high-performance computing power
[
2
]
.

To meet this challenge, modern high-performance architectures integrate an ever-increasing number of cores, designed to meet extreme area- and energy-efficiency targets to enable large-scale replication
[
3
]
.
In-order single-issue cores with shallow pipelines are favoured over power- and resource-hungry deeply-pipelined out-of-order processors.

Within the context of massively-parallel general-purpose accelerators, few architectures propose cores with limited multiple-issue capabilities.
Nvidia’s Turing architecture, for instance, implements concurrent execution of FP32 and INT32 operations
[
4
]
, allowing the
Floating-Point (FP)
math datapath to remain active during the execution of non-
FP
-math instructions.
According to the authors, this results in a 36% average increase in throughput across several gaming workloads.
Unfortunately, due to the proprietary nature of the architecture, implementation details and their area and power impact remain undisclosed.

A similar form of dual-issue execution was implemented in the Snitch processor
[
5
]
, coupling a tiny streamlined single-issue in-order RV32I control core and a 64-bit SIMD-capable
Floating Point Unit (FPU)
.
Instructions are fetched and issued by the control core, and offloaded to the
FPU
as required.
To support dual-issue execution, the
Floating Point Subsystem (FPSS)
integrates a small instruction buffer to cache small
FP
loop bodies, the
Floating Point Repetition (FREP)
buffer.

FP
instructions are first issued by the integer core, and cached in the
FREP
buffer.
In the remaining loop iterations, the
FREP
sequencer can independently issue instructions, while the integer core is free to fetch, and potentially execute, successive instructions. Its authors refer to this scheme as
pseudo dual-issue execution
.

This approach implies the following constraints:

•

the
FP
loop body must be small enough to fit entirely in the
FREP
buffer;

•

the programmer or compiler need to explicitly mark the
FP
loop for
FREP
execution in software;

•

there can be no dependencies between the
FP
and integer threads.

The latter constraint follows from the asynchronous execution of the two threads, between which no ordering guarantees are enforced.
While this enables Snitch’s dual-issue capabilities at a very low power and area cost, it limits flexibility.
In particular, it hinders the execution of computations featuring a mix of interdependent integer and FP instructions.
We refer to these as mixed integer and
FP
instruction sequences.

In this paper, we present
COPIFT
, a generic method to develop
C
o-
O
perative
P
arallel
I
nteger and
F
loating-point
T
hreads in Snitch, overcoming the limitation imposed by pseudo dual-issue, and enabling low-cost dual-issue execution of mixed integer and
FP
instruction sequences.

To summarize our contributions, we:

•

Present COPIFT, a generic methodology to enable sustained dual-issue execution of mixed integer and
FP
instruction sequences in Snitch, a state-of-the-art open-source RISC-V processor.

•

Develop a set of
Instruction Set Architecture (ISA)
extensions to broaden the range of applications suited for pseudo dual-issue execution, in conjunction with the COPIFT method.

•

Implement COPIFT-accelerated codes for selected mixed integer and
FP
instruction sequences, achieving average speedups of 1.47
\times
over optimized RV32G baselines, and a peak
IPC
of 1.75.

•

Present a first account of the energy-efficiency of pseudo multiple-issue execution schemes, demonstrating average energy reductions of 1.37
\times
with COPIFT, over optimized RV32G baselines.

Our implementation is fully open source and performance experiments are reproducible using free software.

Akin to other
ISAs
, RISC-V defines distinct
Register Files (RFs)
for the RV32I base integer
ISA
and the “D” standard extension
[
6
]
. As a result, integer and
FP
instructions operate mostly on independent sets of registers.
This fundamental characteristic allows us to define two threads with independent state, comprising respectively the integer
RF
and RV32I instructions and the
FP

RF
and “D” extension instructions.
Several notable
FP
instructions, however, also require access to the integer
RF
, consuming operands from integer source registers (
FP
load, store, conversion and move instructions) or producing results into integer destination registers (
FP
comparisons).
Such instructions conflict with the independent thread abstraction, precluding pseudo-dual issue execution.

Partly relaxing this constraint, Snitch implements
Stream Semantic Registers (SSRs)

[
7
]
, a custom
ISA
extension to stream data from memory directly into the
RF
, eliminating explicit load and store instructions.

SSRs
support any
affine stream
or memory access pattern which can be described as an affine function of the induction variables of a four-dimensional loop nest.
Extending the
SSR
mechanism,
Indirection Stream Semantic Registers (ISSRs)

[
8
]
, further provide support for
indirect streams
or arbitrary memory access patterns, provided a list of relative addresses encoding the pattern.

SSRs
enable memory accesses without introducing dependencies on the integer
RF
, solving only a particular instance of the problem.
Conversely, our methodology tackles any form of interdependency between integer and
FP
threads.

Consider the code in
Figure

1
, computing the exponential of an array of single-precision
FP
numbers.
Compiling this code for the RV32G architecture results in the mix of integer and
FP
instructions shown in
Figure

1
.
Dependencies between these instructions are marked with red and blue boxes in the consuming and producing instructions, respectively.
These can be classified into:

Type 1

Dynamic memory dependencies, from
FP
load-stores with dynamically-computed addresses.

Type 2

Static memory dependencies, from
FP
load-stores with statically-determined addresses.

In the most general case, a third type may be present:

Type 3

Register dependencies, from
FP
conversion, move and comparison instructions.

Due to these dependencies, we cannot trivially partition integer and
FP
instructions for pseudo dual-issue execution.
The COPIFT method addresses the problem by eliminating or increasing the distance between dependent instructions.
It involves the following steps:

Step 1

Construct a
Data Flow Graph (DFG)
from the RISC-V assembly and identify all dependencies between integer and
FP
instructions.

Step 2

Partition the
DFG
on the identified edges, such that there exists an acyclic precedence-relation among the subgraphs, and the number of edges between them is minimized.

Step 3

Reorder the instructions according to the subgraph ordering established in
Item

2
.

Step 4

Apply loop tiling to block the data, and loop fission to reschedule the subgraph computations on each block.

Step 5

Apply software pipelining and multiple buffering to overlap subgraph computations.

Step 6

Map all
FP
load-store instructions to
SSRs
.

Step 7

Map all
FP
loops to
FREP
loops, and reorder them to preceed the integer loop.

To clarify each step, we walk through how to apply COPIFT to the exponential kernel in
Figure

1
.

Figure

1(a)

Item

Following

2

This alone results in two subgraphs for which a precedence-relation cannot be established.
We therefore further partition the graph, cutting edge

We obtain three subgraphs, each defining a phase of the computation with clear ordering requirements w.r.t. the others:
FP

This allows us to reorder the instructions in consecutive groups of integer-only or

The resulting code would resemble

Each phase is now applied to an entire block of inputs, before passing on a block of intermediate results to the next phase.
Implicitly, this step requires allocating block-sized buffers for each variable associated to an edge between subgraphs.
Additionally, all inter-phase communication originally through the

To reduce spill communication overheads and memory requirements, it is important to minimize the number of edges between subgraphs in

Item

II-B

COPIFT ISA Extensions

FP
conversion and comparison instructions are critical for mixed integer and
FP
workloads.
To support these, we can alter their semantics under
FREP
operation, to operate entirely on the
FP

RF
.
Communication between the integer and
FP

RFs
is spilled to memory, through the use of additional load-store instructions, decoupling it from the computation, so the target instructions can find all operands directly in the
FP

RF
.
This technique can be combined with
SSRs
, to implicitly encode the
FP
load-store operations.

Albeit not strictly necessary, we chose to develop custom instructions to carry the altered semantics, for each of the relevant “D” extension instructions, i.e.
fcvt.w[u].d
,
fcvt.d.w[u]
,
feq.d
,
flt.d
,
fle.d
and
fclass.d
.
We copy the original encodings, allocating the new instructions in the
custom-1
opcode space
[
6
]
.

III
Results

We implement a Snitch cluster hosting one compute core in GlobalFoundries’ 12LP+ FinFET technology using Fusion Compiler, with a target clock frequency of 1 GHz.
Our extensions introduce negligible area and timing overheads, within the margin of synthesis process variability.

All experiments are conducted at the target frequency in cycle-accurate RTL simulations using QuestaSim 2023.4.
Switching activities are extracted from post-layout simulations, and used for power estimation in PrimeTime, assuming typical operating conditions of 25 °C and 0.8 V supply voltage.

III-A

Performance Evaluation

To evaluate the benefits of the COPIFT method and
ISA
extensions, we implement a set of mixed integer and
FP
workloads with diverse characteristics, extracted from two different domains:

•

Hit and miss Monte Carlo integration methods
[
11
]
, evaluating two different integration problems and pseudo-random number generators. Integer instructions are typically employed in pseudo-random number generation, while the integration is performed in double-precision
FP
arithmetic.

•

Transcendental function evaluation on a vector of single-precision
FP
numbers. Reference implementations are taken from the GNU C Library
[
12
]
v2.40, and involve integer instructions for bit-manipulation and double-precision
FP
arithmetic to evaluate approximating polynomials.

The latter in particular, applied to the exponential function, is the main component of softmax operations, which consume a considerable fraction of cycles in modern
Large Language Models (LLMs)

[
13
]
.

We implement Snitch-optimized RV32G-compliant baseline codes and COPIFT-accelerated variants for each kernel, compiled using a custom toolchain for Snitch based on LLVM 18.1.4 with
-
O3
optimization.

Table

I
lists all of the evaluated kernels, with their unique characteristics.
As the table shows, the number of integer and
FP
instructions can vary as a result of applying the COPIFT method, as e.g. integer load-stores are introduced in
Item

4
and
FP
load-stores are eliminated in
Item

6
.
These easily measurable kernel characteristics can be used to estimate the speedup potential of the COPIFT method.
In fact, assuming similar IPCs in the integer and
FP
threads, the speedup
S
can be approximated as a function of the number of integer and
FP
instructions:

\begin{split}S\coloneqq\frac{t^{\text{base}}_{\text{int}}+t^{\text{base}}_{\text{fp}}}{\max(t^{\text{copift}}_{\text{int}},t^{\text{copift}}_{\text{fp}})}\approx\frac{n^{\text{base}}_{\text{int}}+n^{\text{base}}_{\text{fp}}}{\max(n^{\text{copift}}_{\text{int}},n^{\text{copift}}_{\text{fp}})}=S^{\prime}\end{split}

(1)

Similarly, we can approximate the
IPC
improvement
I
:

\begin{split}I\coloneqq S*\frac{n^{\text{copift}}_{\text{int}}+n^{\text{copift}}_{\text{fp}}}{n^{\text{base}}_{\text{int}}+n^{\text{base}}_{\text{fp}}}\approx\frac{n^{\text{copift}}_{\text{int}}+n^{\text{copift}}_{\text{fp}}}{\max(n^{\text{copift}}_{\text{int}},n^{\text{copift}}_{\text{fp}})}=I^{\prime}\end{split}

(2)

If the number of instructions does not differ significantly between the two code variants, we can further approximate the speedup as a function of the number of instructions in the original implementation alone:

\begin{split}S^{\prime}\approx\frac{n^{\text{base}}_{\text{int}}+n^{\text{base}}_{\text{fp}}}{\max(n^{\text{base}}_{\text{int}},n^{\text{base}}_{\text{fp}})}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{=}}1+\frac{\min(n^{\text{base}}_{\text{int}},n^{\text{base}}_{\text{fp}})}{\max(n^{\text{base}}_{\text{int}},n^{\text{base}}_{\text{fp}})}=\\
&=1+\mathrm{TI}=S^{\prime\prime}=I^{\prime\prime}\end{split}

(3)

Intuitively, this shows how the speedup approximately depends on the
thread imbalance (TI)
, as defined above.

IV
Related Work

To issue multiple instructions in parallel while preserving program correctness, multiple-issue processors must be able to identify dependencies within an instruction stream.
Multiple-issue capabilities are often built into out-of-order processors, leveraging the facilities which enable out-of-order execution, such as reservation stations, register renaming and reorder buffers, which lead to high area and power overheads.
On the other hand, in-order multiple-issue processors can be implemented in two flavours: statically-scheduled superscalar processors and VLIW processors
[
14
]
.
In-order superscalar processors integrate logic to identify hazards between instructions in the issue packet and those in execution, while VLIW processors offload part of the job to the compiler, in charge of packing independent instructions into bundles of a predetermined size, at the cost of increased code size.

Few recent works in the literature look into the area- and energy-efficient design of dual-issue in-order RISC processors.
All of these target dual-issue execution of arbitrary instruction pairs, with minor restrictions; as a consequence, they require a minimum of four read and two write (4R2W) ports into the
RF
.
Lozano et al.
[
15
]
design a novel latency-optimized 4R2W-port SRAM-based
RF
implementation for soft FPGA cores, yet their implementation incurs a 4
\times
cost in physical RAM blocks compared to the single-issue baseline, which also accounts for the majority of the +18% power increase.
Similarly, Kra et al.
[
16
]
implement a dual-issue in-order RISC-V core based on CV32E40P, duplicating instruction decode, issue and execution stages and integrating a 5R3W-port
RF
with cross-forwarding support from all write to all read ports, for a total 60% area increase.
Patsidis et al.
[
17
]
claim to further push the area and energy efficiency limits of dual-issue RISC-V cores, by restricting dual-issue to 16-bit compressed instructions, fitting within a single 32-bit instruction fetch port.
They extend the
RF
with extra read ports, and a partitioned design to harvest the energy benefits from locality of reference to eight commonly-addressed registers by compressed instructions.

Wygrzvwalski et al.
[
18
]
present a pseudo dual-issue mechanism for small loop bodies, which resembles the Snitch implementation.
A separate instruction buffer is allocated as a source of instructions for the second issue slot.
Differently from Snitch, they rely on a banked
RF
design to prevent structural hazards on the
RF
ports, restricting parallel execution to pairs of instructions accessing different register banks.
Conversely, Snitch targets pseudo dual-issue execution of integer and
FP
instructions, leveraging the separate
RFs
available by
ISA
design.
Unfortunately, Wygrzvwalski’s design is targeted to a specific application, limiting their investigation to a rather simple code featuring two independent threads operating in lockstep, without diving into the intricacies required to support more complex general-purpose scenarios.

In contrast to these works, we presented a novel methodology enabling dual-issue execution of mixed integer and
FP
instruction sequences in an area- and energy-efficient processor, with virtually no area overhead due to our extensions.
This is possible by leveraging Snitch’s
SSR
and
FREP
extensions, which extend beyond the scope of dual-issue execution, enabling the core’s high
FPU
utilization.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we presented COPIFT, a generic methodology to enable sustained dual-issue execution of mixed integer and floating-point instruction sequences in Snitch, a state-of-the-art open-source RISC-V processor.
We further developed a set of
ISA
extensions to broaden the range of applications which can leverage our methodology.
To evaluate the benefits of COPIFT-enabled dual-issue execution, we implemented optimized RV32G baseline codes for a variety of mixed integer and floating-point workloads on Snitch, and compared these to COPIFT-optimized variants leveraging our
ISA
extensions.
We measured speedups of 1.47
\times
on average and a peak
IPC
of 1.75, proving COPIFT’s effectiveness in enabling sustained dual-issue execution.
We further compared the energy-efficiency of baseline and COPIFT-accelerated codes.
Despite a small 1.07
\times
average increase in power consumption, COPIFT proves beneficial in overall energy terms, resulting in 1.37
\times
average energy savings on the measured benchmarks, and a peak 1.93
\times
saving on the
exp
kernel, proving that effective dual-issue execution is possible on area- and energy-efficient in-order cores.
