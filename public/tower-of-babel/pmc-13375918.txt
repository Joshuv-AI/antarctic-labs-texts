Preprocedural New York Heart Association Class IV Independently Predicts Mortality After Effective Edge-To-Edge Repair for Primary Mitral Regurgitation

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn patients with primary mitral regurgitation (MR) (PMR), advanced symptoms classified as New York Heart Association (NYHA) class IV are typically driven by valve deterioration. Transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (mitral valve transcatheter edge-to-edge repair [M-TEER]) reliably achieves effective reduction of mitral regurgitation in high-risk patients. However, whether or not preprocedural NYHA class IV continues to predict outcomes after successful MR reduction remains unclear.MethodsThe Outcomes of Patients tReated wIth Mitral Transcatheter Edge-to-edge Repair for Primary Mitral Regurgitation Registry includes PMR patients undergoing M-TEER at 27 international sites between 2009 and 2023. Clinical outcomes were compared between patients in NYHA class IV and those in NYHA class II/III.ResultsA total of 2528 patients were included (median age 82 years [interquartile range (IQR) 76–85], 45.9% female), and 19.7% presented in NYHA class IV. Baseline left ventricular ejection fraction and right ventricular function did not differ significantly between groups. Procedural success (residual MR ≤ 1+: 66.7% vs. 64.6%, p = 0.55) and 2-year rehospitalization rates (17.9% vs. 20.4%, log-rank p = 0.78) were comparable. In contrast, 2-year all-cause mortality was higher in NYHA class IV patients (31.8% vs. 21.1%, log-rank p < 0.0001). Multivariable Cox regression confirmed preprocedural NYHA class IV as an independent predictor of mortality (hazard ratio [HR] 1.75; 95% CI 1.32–2.31; p < 0.001).ConclusionsIn PMR, preprocedural NYHA class IV remains an independent predictor of all-cause mortality after effective MR reduction with M-TEER, despite comparable postprocedural results.

Introduction

Primary mitral regurgitation (MR) (PMR) is associated with progressive heart failure, frequent hospitalizations, and increased mortality.1,2 Symptom severity is routinely assessed using the New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification, which has consistently demonstrated prognostic value across cardiovascular disease.3,4 In patients with PMR, advanced symptoms corresponding to NYHA class IV are typically driven by valve deterioration rather than by end-stage myocardial dysfunction.

Surgical valve repair or replacement is the standard treatment for severe PMR.5,6 However, many patients are deemed unsuitable candidates for surgical repair or replacement due to factors such advanced age and comorbidities, including renal failure.7,8 In such cases, mitral valve (MV) transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) (M-TEER) has emerged as a safe and effective alternative for treating symptomatic PMR in patients at high surgical risk.4,9, 10, 11 Thus, in patients with PMR whose NYHA IV status is primarily valvular in origin, M-TEER should, in principle, provide sufficient treatment to restore outcomes comparable to less symptomatic patients. Nevertheless, whether or not preprocedural NYHA class IV continues to confer prognostic risk despite effective MR reduction remains uncertain. Existing evidence is limited to small studies or heterogeneous populations, and the prognostic implications of NYHA class IV in patients undergoing M-TEER for PMR have not been systematically investigated.12, 13, 14

One may reasonably assume that patients diagnosed with PMR and NYHA class IV constitute a distinct cohort. Given that PMR likely serves as the primary etiological factor leading to NYHA class IV status in these individuals, efficacious treatment modalities may yield comparable clinical outcomes as patients presenting lower NYHA classes. This study investigates the impact of preprocedural NYHA class IV status on outcomes following M-TEER in patients with PMR.

Material and Methods

Study Population and Data Collection

This retrospective analysis used data from the investigator-initiated, noninterventional, international multicenter Outcomes of Patients tReated wIth Mitral Transcatheter Edge-to-edge Repair for Primary Mitral Regurgitation (PRIME-MR) Registry (ClinicalTrials.gov Unique Identifier: NCT05332782). The registry includes consecutive symptomatic patients with relevant PMR who underwent M-TEER between 2009 and 2023 at 27 centers in Germany, Denmark, Switzerland, Italy, France, Portugal, the United Kingdom, Canada, and the United States. Ethical approval was obtained from local ethics committees, and the study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Patients with adequate baseline NYHA class data and available outcomes information were considered eligible for inclusion. Data collection and statistical analysis were carried out at the Center for Population Health Innovation, University Heart & Vascular Center Hamburg, University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany. All patients were deemed unsuitable for MV surgery by the local interdisciplinary heart team.

During follow-up, each center evaluated survival status, rehospitalization(s), NYHA classification, and echocardiographic findings through clinical visits, phone consultations, and medical record reviews. M-TEER was performed using MitraClip (Abbott Medical, Santa Clara, California) or PASCAL (Edwards Lifescience, Irvine, California), following current guidelines.4

Echocardiographic Assessment

Transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography were conducted by experienced physicians at each site to assess MR severity, morphology, and associated parameters. PMR was defined as MR due to abnormal MV anatomy (e.g., leaflet prolapse, flail leaflet, or calcification). MR severity was evaluated using a 4-grade approach in accordance with current guidelines15,16 assessing parameters such as effective regurgitant orifice area (EROA), regurgitant volume, MV orifice area (assessed via 3-dimensional echocardiography), and the lengths of anterior and posterior mitral leaflets.

Baseline echocardiography included the evaluation of left ventricular (LV) end-diastolic and end-systolic with systolic function assessed using LV ejection fraction (LVEF) (Simpson’s biplane method). Right ventricular function was assessed by tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), and right ventricular pressure was estimated by systolic pulmonary artery pressure. Tricuspid regurgitation (TR) severity was evaluated on a 5-grade scale.17

Intraprocedural and Postprocedural Assessment

M-TEER, performed under general anesthesia, was evaluated for procedural duration, the number and iterations of devices used, and intraprocedural MV pressure gradient. Postprocedural and 1-year follow-up assessments included residual MR (rMR). Defined according to Mitral Valve Academic Research Consortium criteria, technical success required successful device delivery, correct positioning, absence of intraprocedural mortality, and no reintervention or emergency surgery related to the device or access site.18,19

Definition of Subgroups and Endpoints

The study cohort was classified according to preprocedural NYHA class (NYHA class II/III vs. NYHA class IV). The primary endpoint was defined as all-cause mortality at 2 years. Secondary endpoints included rehospitalization for heart failure at 2 years, postprocedural efficacy defined as rMR at discharge ≤1+, and improvement in NYHA class at 1-year follow-up.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using R (version 4.0.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD or median with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. To assess differences between groups, Mann–Whitney U tests were used for continuous variables, whereas the Chi-square test was used for categorical variables.

The median follow-up was estimated by the Kaplan–Meier potential follow-up estimator. Event rates and cumulative incidence curves were also calculated based on the Kaplan–Meier estimator. To compare survival between groups, log-rank test were evaluated. Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to identify independent predictors of all endpoints, with adjustment for potential confounders. Centers were considered as strata in regression analysis. The results were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CI. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were 2-sided.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Among the 3083 patients in the PRIME-MR register, 2528 patients (median age 82 years, IQR 76–85, 45.9% female) were eligible for inclusion. Patients in NYHA class IV had significantly higher periprocedural risk compared to those in NYHA class II/III. Patients with NYHA class IV presented with higher median equivalent furosemide doses (NYHA IV: 80 mg [IQR 40–120] vs. NYHA II/III: 40 mg [IQR 20–80]; p < 0.001). Procedural Data and Complication Rates

Procedural characteristics are summarized in Table 3. M-TEER procedures were shorter in NYHA class IV group (68.0 minutes [IQR 43.0–112.0] vs. 80.0 minutes [IQR 55.0–115.0]; p < 0.001). Technical success was high and comparable between groups (NYHA IV: 92.0% [n = 346] vs. NYHA II/III: 93.4% [n = 1740]; p = 0.37). The most frequent complications were minor bleeding (NYHA IV: 4.0% [n = 17] vs. NYHA II/III: 3.8% [n = 67]; p = 1.00) and minor access site vascular complications (NYHA IV: 4.3% [n = 21] vs. NYHA II/III: 4.4% [n = 84]; p = 1.00). Intraprocedural mortality was rare with no significant difference between groups (NYHA IV: 1.0% [n = 5] vs. NYHA II/III: 0.6% [n = 12]; p = 0.48). Postprocedural Results

Postprocedural efficacy, defined as rMR ≤1+ at discharge, was achieved in the majority of patients, regardless of preprocedural NYHA class (NYHA IV: 66.7% [n = 436] vs. NYHA II/III: 64.6% [n = 1217]; p = 0.55). The prevalence of severe postprocedural rMR remained low overall (3.5% [n = 77]), with a significantly higher occurrence in the NYHA class IV subgroup (NYHA IV: 6.0% [n = 26] vs. NYHA II/III: 2.9% [n = 51]; p < 0.01). Analogously, this analysis indicates an elevated mean postprocedural MV pressure gradient in NYHA class IV (NYHA IV: 3.5 mmHg ± 1.6 vs. NYHA II/III: 3.3 ± 1.6; p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

This analysis compares baseline characteristics and the prognostic impact of preprocedural NYHA class IV with NYHA class II/III in patients undergoing M-TEER for PMR based on data from the international, multicenter PRIME-MR Registry.

Key Findings

1.Despite elevated NT-proBNP levels and a higher prevalence of severe MR in patients with preprocedural NYHA class IV, baseline characteristics were largely comparable, minimizing potential confounding related to patient demographics.2.Patients with preprocedural NYHA class IV exhibited a significantly higher incidence of all-cause mortality at 2 years.3.Comparable complication rates, postprocedural efficacy, functional improvement, and rehospitalization rates suggest that M-TEER is a feasible treatment option for patients presenting with preprocedural NYHA class IV.

Baseline Characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the present analysis underscore the high-risk nature of NYHA class IV patients, marked by elevated periprocedural surgical risk scores, high median levels of baseline NT-proBNP, and a high prevalence of comorbidities such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and coronary artery disease. Given that NYHA class IV signifies more severe clinical symptoms and hence a more deteriorated disease stage of PMR, the fact that these patients more frequently present with severe mitral regurgitation and are prone to prior heart failure hospitalizations is unsurprising. However, acknowledging the absence of disparities in the subgroups regarding right ventricular and LV function, as measured by LVEF and TAPSE, is imperative. Both are important predictors and, therefore, potential confounders for clinical outcomes in M-TEER procedures.20, 21, 22

Interestingly, despite the higher prevalence of severe MR in NYHA class IV patients, EROA showed no significant difference, likely due to the influence of valve morphology on regurgitant volume calculations, a known limitation in both PMR and secondary MR.4,23

Lately, the prospective EXPAND trial also identified a higher risk profile in NYHA class IV patients compared to those in lower classes.24 However, unlike the more heterogeneous EXPAND cohort, which included patients with both primary and secondary MR, our study focused exclusively on PMR. This distinction in MR etiology likely accounts for the comparatively worse outcomes observed in EXPAND relative to PRIME-MR.

Procedural and Clinical Outcomes

Periprocedural complication rates were notably low, particularly regarding periprocedural mortality and major complications, reinforcing the feasibility of M-TEER as a treatment for high-risk patients with resting dyspnea. The PRIME-MR analysis demonstrated high efficacy of M-TEER across NYHA classes, with over two-thirds of patients achieving significant MR reduction at discharge (≤1+), consistent with findings from the EXPAND study. Given that MR reduction remains a critical determinant of improved survival, these results underscore the importance of procedural success across all NYHA classes.25,26

Moreover, our analysis reveals substantial improvement in functional outcomes, reflected in comparable NYHA class distributions at 1-year follow-up, regardless of preprocedural NYHA classification.

However, the adverse prognostic impact of NYHA class IV has been previously documented, particularly in the TRAMI and COAPT analyses, which demonstrated increased mortality associated with higher NYHA classes.27,28 Notably, the COAPT trial exclusively enrolled patients with secondary MR, whereas the TRAMI registry included patients with primary and secondary MR. Consistent with these findings, our analysis reports a 31.8% 2-year mortality rate among NYHA class IV patients, aligning with the 1-year mortality observed in the EXPAND subanalysis for patients with PMR.24 Variations in all-cause mortality rates across studies may reflect baseline differences in patient populations.

In patients undergoing MV surgical repair for PMR, David et al. analyzed the impact of baseline symptomatic status on long-term outcomes. Although this cohort had a low surgical risk (overall Society of Thoracic Surgeons risk score of 1.5%), in contrast to the higher-risk PRIME-MR population, long-term survival of NYHA class IV was below the expected survival for the general population.29

Interestingly, despite the elevated mortality observed in NYHA class IV patients, heart failure rehospitalization rates were similar to those in NYHA class II/III, suggesting that M-TEER effectively addresses PMR, whereas preprocedural NYHA class IV likely signifies later disease stages contributing to increased mortality. Thus, M-TEER emerges as a feasible and beneficial treatment option for high-risk PMR patients with resting dyspnea.

Implications for Future Clinical Practice

According to the presented data, patients with PMR leading to NYHA class IV represent a high-risk cohort and face higher mortality after 2 years. In preprocedural planning, Ben-Shoshan et al.22 identified patients with advanced symptoms as constituting a distinct group, which might not profit from M-TEER despite procedural success. Our findings underscore the critical need for enhanced monitoring and patient follow-up within this specific cohort.

According to current guidelines,5,6 M-TEER received a class IIa recommendation for severely symptomatic patients (NYHA III/IV) with high surgical risk. The currently enrolling prospective randomized trials MITRA-HR30 and REPAIR-MR31 aim to determine the effectiveness and superiority of safety for M-TEER compared to conventional MV surgery in patients with PMR and moderate or high surgical risk. However, patient enrollment in these studies has been challenging, as many potential candidates are excluded due to unacceptably high surgical risk. Although awaiting significant insights from these studies, our findings contribute to the expanding evidence of M-TEER as a viable option for managing high-risk patients presenting with NYHA class IV.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered and primarily derive from the retrospective and nonrandomized nature of this analysis. The lack of centralized adjudication for clinical and echocardiographic data may affect the reliability of these measures. Therefore, presented echocardiographic data and clinical information, including NYHA class, could be liable to interobserver variability and must be interpreted with caution. In addition, clinical symptoms used to allocate NYHA class could be influenced by other clinical diseases and not exclusively by mitral regurgitation. Finally, information about lactate levels and use of inotropes were not available for the patients in our study, restraining potential requirement and use of advanced therapies.

Conclusion

In this large, international cohort of high-risk PMR patients, preprocedural NYHA class IV status was independently associated with significantly higher mortality at 2 years following M-TEER. Nevertheless, similar efficacy, functional improvement, and rehospitalization rates support the feasibility of M-TEER as a treatment option for this high-risk cohort. These findings underscore the importance for individualized follow-up of NYHA class IV patients following M-TEER and highlight the need for further prospective studies to optimize long-term outcomes in this population.
