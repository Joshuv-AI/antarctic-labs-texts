Comparative survey of eating and lifestyle habits in Italian school age children using Nutripiatto as an educational tool
Authors: Chiara Spiezia, Claudia Di Rosa, Martina Monticone, Greta Lattanzi, Francesca La Farina, Laura De Gara, Yeganeh Manon Khazrai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nutripiatto, a Mediterranean diet - based educational tool combining a colorful plate and recipe guide, was evaluated in 781 children (6–10 years) from Lazio, Piedmont, and Sicily, divided in age related-groups (A and B). Eating habits were assessed at baseline (T0) and after 2 months (T1) using a non-validate caregiver completed FFQ; 525 children (67%) completed both assessments. At T1, children showed greater awareness of portion sizes and food frequencies. In Group A, portion size awareness improved in Sicily (vegetable drinks, whole grain cereals) and Piedmont (cereals, yogurt, potatoes, meat). In both regions, children also increased their frequency of consumption of vegetables and fruit, while reducing red pizza, cold cuts, and meat in Piedmont, and French fries, crisps and cold cuts in Sicily. In Lazio, children increased their intake of crackers and fish while reducing French fries. In Group B, children in Piedmont increased the consumption of vegetables and fruit, in Lazio nuts while in Sicily they reduced crisps. Across both groups, water intake increased in Lazio and Sicily. Overall, regional dietary patterns persisted, characterized by higher processed and protein-rich foods (Lazio), greater bread and water intake (Sicily), and higher fruit and vegetable consumption (Piedmont). Despite the short duration and reliance on caregiver reports, Nutripiatto may appear to be a feasible, engaging school-based tool supporting healthier eating, warranting larger controlled studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26302-8.

Introduction

Childhood overweight and obesity, defined as excess body fat accumulation that may impair health, represent major public health concerns worldwide1. In Italy, OKkio alla Salute 20232 reports that nearly 30% of primary school children have excess body weight, with higher prevalence in southern regions. These conditions are linked to early-onset complications that can persist into adulthood, including hepatic steatosis (NAFLD/MAFLD)3,4, cardiometabolic disorders, and psychosocial difficulties5,6. The progressive shift from traditional Mediterranean dietary patterns toward more Westernized eating habits—characterized by higher consumption of ultra-processed foods and lower intake of fruits, vegetables, legumes, and whole grains—has contributed to this trend7–9. National surveillance data confirm that over half of Italian children consume sweet snacks more than three times per week, 11% skip breakfast daily, and more than one-third have nutritionally inadequate breakfasts2.

Schools are recognized as strategic settings for preventive interventions, offering structured opportunities to deliver evidence-based nutrition education to children and families. The World Health Organization (WHO) underscores the potential of school-based programs to support children’s physical growth, cognitive development, and academic success through healthier diets and lifestyles10. At the European level, the Horizon 2020 Science and Technology in childhood Obesity Policy (STOP) project emphasized the need for multilevel strategies ranging from food labelling and healthier school meals to marketing restrictions to foster balanced diets and physical activity11.

Within this framework, visual plate models have been developed as practical tools to promote portion awareness and food balance. Originating in adult nutrition education12 the plate model concept has been incorporated into several national initiatives such as the UK Eatwell Guide13, and the US Healthy Eating Plate14, and culturally adapted for different populations.

Evidence from systematic and scoping reviews suggests that portion-control plates, when integrated into broader educational programs, can enhance dietary knowledge and, in some cases, reported eating behaviors among children15–18.

Nutripiatto is an Italian, Mediterranean-oriented plate model specifically designed for children aged 4–12 years. It combines a visual plate with age-appropriate educational materials to foster portion awareness and balanced meals consistent with Mediterranean principles19 (Supplementary Figure S1, image of the plate). The model translates nutritional recommendations into an engaging, color-coded, and developmentally tailored format that promotes both self-regulation and family involvement, in line with the educational values of the Mediterranean diet20.

A previous pilot study conducted in preschool settings demonstrated improved dietary knowledge and behaviors following its use21. However, its applicability and short-term effects in primary school children across different Italian regions have not yet been assessed. The present study therefore aimed to evaluate changes in dietary habits and lifestyle behaviors following the implementation of Nutripiatto in a large, geographically diverse sample of Italian primary school children, and to explore potential regional differences in dietary patterns. This work extends prior research by applying a standardized protocol across multiple regions and age groups, providing a broader perspective on the educational value of plate-based nutrition tools in the school setting.

Methods

Study protocol

This prospective observational before-and-after study, with no control group or randomization, was conducted between March and May 2023 and involved children aged 6–10 years attending primary schools in three Italian regions: Lazio (Rome), Sicily (Messina), and Piedmont (Asti). Baseline (T0) measurements were used as an internal comparator, with each participant acting as their own control.

The intervention lasted two months and included four school-based nutrition education sessions delivered by trained nutritionists or educators (two per month). (Fig. 1):

Participant recruitment

Schools were selected based on voluntary participation in nutritional education initiatives promoted by the Campus Bio-Medico University of Rome. School principals were contacted by email and telephone and subsequently invited to an online meeting in which the aims and methodology of the Nutripiatto project were illustrated. Once participation was confirmed, eligible families were invited to in-person meetings in February held in school settings (e.g., after parent-teacher meetings). During these sessions, researchers explained the study’s objectives, procedures, and requirements. Parents or caregivers provided written informed consent for their children to be included in the study.

Nutripiatto kit

Nutripiatto is a nutrition education tool designed for children aged 4 to 12 years. It consists of a physical plate (26 cm in diameter) divided into sections and that can be considered an adaptation for children of the Harvard Healthy Eating Plate. The central portion of the plate is divided into three coloured segments: green for vegetables (half the plate), red for protein foods (one-quarter), and yellow for carbohydrate-containing foods (one-quarter). The edge of the plate features icons emphasizing physical activity, hydration, consumption of extra virgin olive oil, and daily fruit intake (see Supplementary Figure S1).

The Nutripiatto kit includes the plate, and four downloadable recipe guides available on the Nutripiatto website24:

The recipes adhere to portion sizes and consumption frequencies recommended by Dietary Reference Values of Nutrients and Energy for Italian population (LARN) and the Mediterranean Diet model19,25. They are based on Italian culinary traditions and children’s preferences to promote a positive relationship with food26–28.

Food frequency questionnaire (FFQ)

Children’s dietary habits were assessed using a non-validated Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) previously developed and published by Lattanzi et al. The questionnaire was designed for research purposes, drawing on items from established surveillance systems such as the WHO Childhood Obesity Surveillance Initiative (COSI) and the Italian OKkio alla Salute (ISS)2,29, and was subsequently adapted to the study context and objectives. It combined elements from these tools with additional questions created specifically for the Nutripiatto evaluation. The FFQ was administered at two time points—baseline (T0) and post-intervention (T1)—and completed by parents or caregivers through self-administration. While this approach may be subject to recall or social desirability bias, it allowed large-scale, non-invasive data collection in the school setting.

The questionnaire consisted of 26 core items, organized into four thematic sections:

Ethics statement

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Campus Bio-Medico University of Rome for research involving human subjects (approval code: 131/21). Informed consent was obtained from the parents and/or legal guardians of all minor participants involved in the study, in accordance with applicable ethical standards and institutional guidelines.

Statistical analysis

The questionnaire included predominantly categorical variables (e.g., yes/no responses on physical activity and food portion size and consumption), as well as ordinal variables indicating frequency of food intake (e.g., from 0 times per day = 0 to everyday = 7 except for eggs and fruits that were calculated as number of eggs or fruits/week) and beverages, seasoning (never = 0; always = 1; sometimes = 2) and snacks (never = 0, once a day = 1; often = 2; sometimes = 3) enabling a detailed assessment of children’s dietary habits, lifestyle behaviours, and caregivers’ perceptions of changes after the intervention.

The descriptive analysis was conducted including mean ± standard deviation (SD), absolute frequencies, and percentages calculated according to variable type and distribution. For beverages, seasonings and snacks values are reported as median.

The Chi-square test was used to evaluate changes in the number of children consuming the correct portion size (small portion size for Group A and medium portion size for Group B, respectively) between T0 and T1 for each region. Result

Study population

At the beginning of the study (T0), 781 participants were recruited from primary schools. Of these, 525 participants (67%) completed the study at T1, comprising 269 boys (51%) and 256 girls (49%). The drop-out rate was 33% (254 children) primarily due to parents failing to complete the questionnaires at both T0 and T1.

These children were following specific diets for medical reasons—such as allergies (to certain fruits and mollusks), lactose intolerance, overweight, insulin resistance, type 1 diabetes, or hypercholesterolemia. However, they were not excluded from the analysis, as these dietary choices did not entail the complete exclusion of entire food groups.

Family meals

In both groups (A and B) and across all three regions, dinner was the meal “always” consumed at home, with the highest percentages observed in Sicily (96% and 95% for Groups A and B, respectively), followed by Lazio (91% and 88%) and Piedmont (90% and 89%), with only minimal differences between the latter two regions. Breakfast was most frequently consumed together with the family in Piedmont (58% and 60% for Groups A and B, respectively) compared to Lazio (57% and 38%) and Sicily (55% and 53%). Lunch was the meal least frequently eaten at home in Lazio and Piedmont, where many children had lunch at the school canteen. In contrast, in Sicily, the majority of children (80%) reported always having lunch at home.

Predominant figure in meal Preparation

In all three regions, the mother was the predominant figure in meal preparation, especially for dinner. This was particularly evident in Sicily (92% and 91% for Groups A and B, respectively) and in Lazio (90% in Group B).

In Piedmont, 40% of Group A participants reported that a third person (“other”)—neither the mother nor the father—prepared meals, which was higher than in the other regions. Fathers were more involved in preparing lunch and dinner in Group B of Piedmont, accounting for 10% and 18%, respectively, which was more than in the other regions.

Section III: frequency of consumption of seasonings, drinks and snack

Seasonings

This section assessed the consumption of seasonings, including extra virgin olive oil, seed oil, butter, margarine, cream, and lard. A significant reduction in the frequency of consumption was observed in Piedmont for both Group A and Group B across all items, except margarine (p < 0.05). Conversely, in Group B in Lazio, a significant increase in seed oil consumption was observed (p = 0.0391). No significant differences were observed in Sicily in both group A and group B from baseline to T1.

Beverages

This section assessed the consumption of beverages, including fruit juice, milkshakes, orange juice, sugary drinks, tea, and light beverages.

Between T0 and T1, a significant reduction in fruit juice consumption was observed only in Group A in Piedmont (p = 0.0450).

Regarding daily water intake, no significant changes were observed in Piedmont in either Group A or Group B between T0 and T1. In contrast, in Lazio, a significant increase in the number of glasses of water consumed per day was observed in both Group A (from 6.62 ± 2.93 to 7.48 ± 3.68, p = 0.0276) and Group B (from 6.66 ± 3.42 to 7.35 ± 3.73, p = 0.0264). In Sicily, a highly significant increase in daily water consumption was observed in both Group A (from 6.27 ± 2.39 to 8.05 ± 2.57, p < 0.0001) and Group B (from 6.93 ± 2.76 to 8.87 ± 3.06, p < 0.0001).

Children in Group B from Sicily consistently reported higher water consumption compared to those from the other two regions. At T1, their intake was significantly higher than in both Lazio (p < 0.0001) and Piedmont (p = 0.0070).

No significant differences in water consumption were observed among the three regions in Group A.

Snacks

This section evaluated the consumption of snacks, including dark chocolate, candies, and chewing gum. Group A in Piedmont and Sicily showed changes in candy consumption, but with opposite trends: in Piedmont, consumption significantly increased (p = 0.0312), whereas in Sicily, it significantly decreased (p = 0.0079). Additionally, in Group B in Sicily, chewing gum consumption also showed a significant decrease (p = 0.0098). No other significant changes were observed in both groups in the three regions.

Details regarding beverages, seasonings and snacks are reported in Tables S9 and S10 in Supplementary Materials.

Comparing beverages, seasonings and snacks consumption across the three regions ant both time (T0 and T1) no significant differences were observed (p > 0.9999 for all comparisons, partial η2 = 0.00).

Section IV: effectiveness of “Nutripiatto”

The final section of the questionnaire, present only at T1, evaluated the effectiveness of the Nutripiatto. Despite the absence of statistically significant differences, the tool appeared to be equally effective in the three regions as parents reported improvements in their children’s lifestyles (an increasing trend in consumption of whole grains, vegetables, and water, and a reducing trend in protein portions and sedentary behaviour).

In fact, in Group A, the effectiveness of Nutripiatto as a visual guide was observed in 96% of children in Sicily, 92% in Piedmont and 88% in Lazio, while in Group B the rates were 97% in Sicily, 94% in Piedmont and 69% in Lazio.

Moreover, in Group A, the consumption of low-refined grains increased in 61% of children in Piedmont, 25% in Lazio, and 21% in Sicily while in Group B, the percentages were 56% in Piedmont, 55% in Lazio, and 33% in Sicily. Additionally, a reduction in meat and fish portions after using Nutripiatto was observed. In Group A, the percentages were 52% in Piedmont, 30% in Sicily and 25% in Lazio while in Group B, the percentages were 63% in Lazio, 49% in Piedmont and 46% in Sicily.

Regarding the consumption of vegetables, in Group A, 83% of children in Piedmont, 61% in Lazio, and 60% in Sicily increased their consumption. In Group B, the percentages were 81% in Piedmont, 79% in Lazio, and 59% in Sicily.

Furthermore, most children increased their water consumption, with percentages of 86% in Piedmont, 75% in Sicily and 57% in Lazio in Group A, and 77% in Sicily, 76% in Piedmont, and 71% in Lazio in Group B.

Lastly, the majority of children demonstrated increased physical activity following the use of Nutripiatto. In Group A, the percentages of children who reduced sedentarity were 80% in Piedmont, 66% in Sicily and 61% in Lazio, while in Group B, 73% in Lazio and 66% both in Piedmont and in Sicily.

Discussion

This study, conducted across three Italian regions, provides exploratory evidence that Nutripiatto may support healthier dietary habits among primary school children over a two-month period. The most notable improvements were observed in vegetable, water, and whole grain consumption, particularly among younger children, who appeared more receptive to playful and visually engaging activities. Since half of the plate is dedicated to vegetables, this visual cue may have contributed to the increased intake of this food group, consistent with national dietary recommendations. The color-coded layout and proportional food representation are designed to make mealtimes more interactive, encouraging children to “self-measure” portions using their hands (fist, palm, fingers, fingertips)34,35 and to involve their families in meal preparation and table-setting. However, these improvements did not reach statistical significance in all areas and should therefore be viewed as exploratory, indicating emerging trends that warrant confirmation in future controlled studies.

By adopting a proportional plate model, Nutripiatto aligns with established international tools such as the US MyPlate36,37, the Kid’s Healthy Eating Plate38, the UK “Eat Well Guide”13 and the Colombian “Mi Plato, Un Arcoíris divertido de Sabores”39. Previous systematic reviews have shown that plate-based visual tools, when integrated into school curricula, can enhance children’s nutritional awareness and encourage gradual behavioral change15,16,18.

Within this framework, Nutripiatto may represent a culturally adapted model that translates international dietary guidelines into an engaging and age-appropriate format for Italian children and families. Its proportional and color-coded design may act as a visual reinforcement of key nutritional messages, complementing broader educational strategies.

At T1, Group A children showed the clearest improvements, particularly in Sicily and Piedmont, where understanding of portion sizes and consumption of vegetables and fruit increased, accompanied by reduced intake of pizza, cold cuts, and fried foods. In Lazio, children increased their consumption of crackers and fish reducing French fries. Group B children exhibited fewer changes, with modest increases in fruit, vegetables, and nuts. Across both groups, water intake improved, particularly in Lazio and Sicily. When comparing regions, significant differences emerged in the frequency of consumption across several food categories. In general, in group A, carbohydrate-rich foods such as pizza, soups, potatoes, and French fries were consumed more frequently in Lazio, while bread intake was higher in Sicily. Regarding protein-rich foods, children in Lazio showed higher consumption of meat, cold cuts, and fresh cheeses, whereas Piedmont children reported relatively higher intake of fruit and vegetables. In Group B, regional variation persisted, with higher consumption of protein-dense and processed foods in Lazio, while Piedmont confirmed the same trend observed in Group A, with higher fruit and vegetable intake. Sicilian children showed the highest higher water intake across time points.

These results reflect the well-known North–South gradient in Italy, whereby children from northern regions generally display healthier eating patterns and greater adherence to the Mediterranean diet compared to those from central and southern regions40. Such differences underscore the importance of tailoring nutrition education programs to local food cultures and socioeconomic contexts. The regional variations observed in this study—higher consumption of processed and protein-rich foods in Lazio, greater fruit and vegetable intake in Piedmont, and higher water and bread consumption in Sicily—support this interpretation and are consistent with data from the national surveillance system OKkio alla Salute (2023). Importantly, the comparison across regions did not reveal significant differences in portion sizes, although within-region analyses suggested progressive, positive shifts in dietary habits.

These findings also expand on earlier preschool evidence21 showing that playful and proportion-based educational approaches are particularly effective among younger children, who tend to be more responsive to visual and experiential learning41. The absence of significant portion-size changes in this study may be related to the short follow-up period and the limited reinforcement at home. In contrast, older children (Group B) appeared less responsive overall, possibly due to more entrenched eating habits, reduced parental supervision, and stronger individual food preferences, underscoring the importance of age-specific strategies and continuous family engagement.

The study also explored physical activity habits, revealing generally high levels of sports participation, with regional and age-related variations. In Lazio, younger children were less active than their peers in Piedmont and Sicily, while older children in Lazio reported the highest activity levels. The more evident dietary than physical activity improvements likely reflect the food-centered nature of Nutripiatto. Although the plate’s rim includes a sneaker icon as a reminder to move, its message remains limited. Integrating Nutripiatto with physical activity materials, developed after the study, could promote a more comprehensive lifestyle approach combining healthy eating and movement, in line with emerging frameworks that incorporate gamified and multidisciplinary education20,42,43 .

Beyond nutritional education, integrating Nutripiatto into broader lifestyle interventions could further enhance its impact. Recent evidence highlights the potential of digital, gamified, and interactive learning tools to promote health literacy and sustained behavior change in children42,44. Studies have shown that serious games and immersive digital platforms can increase motivation, enjoyment, and adherence to healthy eating programs, while self-monitoring applications and feedback systems improve self-regulation and goal achievement in dietary and physical activity behaviors45. In this perspective, combining analog tools such as Nutripiatto with digital or game-based resources, for example, mobile apps or interactive classroom modules, may amplify children’s engagement and reinforce nutritional messages over time45–47. Such hybrid, multidimensional approaches align with recent international trends in nutrition and physical activity education, emphasizing experiential learning, digital innovation, and family participation45.

The results of the present study are consistent with other national initiatives. For instance, the Italian program Frutta e Verdura nelle Scuole48, funded by the European Union and implemented by the Ministries of Agriculture, Education, and Health, promotes fruit and vegetable consumption and awareness of healthy eating through experiential learning. Similarly, the Giocampus Program in Parma49 involved 8,165 children aged 8–11 in structured nutritional lessons using age-specific educational games, confirming the value of “learning through play” approaches such as Nutripiatto in shaping children’s food choices. The ZOOM8 study, a nationally representative survey of Italian children aged 8–9, further highlighted suboptimal adherence to the Mediterranean diet and the influence of family and environmental factors50. These observations reinforce the importance of school- and family-based interventions targeting both knowledge and behavior.

The study’s strengths include its large and regionally diverse sample and the active involvement of both families and schools, which enhance the generalizability of its observations. In contrast, important limitations must be acknowledged: a 33% attrition rate, the use of a non-validated, caregiver-reported FFQ, and the short two-month follow-up may have introduced bias or limited the detection of sustained effects. Furthermore, the before–after design without a control group precludes causal inference and limits generalizability.

Despite these constraints, the study reflects the real-world application of Nutripiatto in routine school practice and contributes valuable insights for scaling up nutrition education initiatives in Italy.

Conclusion

This exploratory, real-world study supports the feasibility and potential of Nutripiatto as a practical educational tool for promoting healthier eating habits among Italian primary school children. However, improvements did not reach statistical significance across all categories and should therefore be considered exploratory, offering hypothesis-generating insights for future research.

The large sample size, regional comparative approach, and integration of families and schools strengthen the ecological validity of this study. Nevertheless, the real-world educational setting required prioritizing feasibility over methodological stringency, and the results should be regarded as preliminary, providing insight into how such tools may function across regions and inform future controlled studies.

Future research should include longer follow-up, validated instruments, and a control group to assess the long-term effectiveness of such tools. Integrating Nutripiatto with digital, interactive, and gamified resources, as well as with physical activity and family-based programs, may represent a promising direction to foster sustainable lifestyle changes from early childhood.
