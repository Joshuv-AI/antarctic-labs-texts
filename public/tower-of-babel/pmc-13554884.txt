Finding NEMO (Nuanced Effective Medical Options) for Personalized Cholesterol Management
Authors: Yakubu Bene-Alhasan, Layla A Abushamat, Vijay Nambi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: dyslipidemias, genetic disorders, primary prevention

Randomized controlled trials provide evidence of population-level response to therapies; however, individual responses may vary considerably. In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Pan et al1 report an intriguing response to lipid lowering therapy (LLT) in a 34-year-old woman with genetically confirmed heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (HeFH) due to the low-density lipoprotein-receptor (LDL-R) Afrikaner-2 variant. The patient, who was on statin and ezetimibe therapies, exhibited a suboptimal low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-C) reduction on evolocumab followed by a dramatic reduction on inclisiran, achieving an LDL-C of 73.4 mg/dL, a nadir not attained on any previous combination of therapies. Furthermore, the response was unusual given that typically proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibition (PCSK9i) with monoclonal antibodies has resulted in greater LDL-C reductions compared with PCSK9 modulation with small interfering RNA (inclisiran).2 The clinicians appropriately attempted to exclude the most common explanations for PCSK9i failure, including nonadherence, injection technique errors, and injection-site reactions, and advance a mechanistically plausible hypothesis: that inclisiran's intracellular silencing of hepatic PCSK9 messenger RNA may confer a pharmacological advantage in LDL-R recycling defects that extracellularly acting monoclonal antibodies cannot replicate. Although there are limited data/reports to support this hypothesis and, therefore, it warrants prospective evaluation, the varied responses to LLT in this patient also compel a broader reflection on 2 fundamental sources of variability in response to LLT in clinical practice: differences in response between individuals, and differences within the same individual over time.

Population-level trials have reported the mean LDL-C reductions of LLT as approximately 15% to 20% for ezetimibe and bempedoic acid, 45% to 50% for high-intensity statins, 50% to 60% for PCSK9 monoclonal antibodies, and approximately 50% for inclisiran. These mean LDL-C reductions represent central tendencies (mean), which implies that some individuals will have had a greater and others a lesser LDL-C reduction relative to the reported mean LDL-C reduction. These reported mean reductions therefore obscure considerable individual-level heterogeneity that are typically captured by waterfall plots. Analyses have demonstrated that statin dose-response varies considerably.3,4 For PCSK9i, the distribution appears tighter: waterfall plots from FOURIER (Further Cardiovascular Outcomes Research with PCSK9 Inhibition in Subjects with Elevated Risk) suggest that more than 90% of patients achieve at least a 50% LDL-C reduction; however, 0.5% of patients did not experience any reduction in LDL-C after a year of use.5 The determinants of heterogeneity in responses across drug classes could be related to any number of factors including adherence, background therapy intensity, injection-site absorption kinetics (including lipohypertrophy from repeated injections) for injectable agents, gastrointestinal absorption kinetics for oral agents, and the underlying genetic architecture of an individual. Genetic variants of HeFH have been shown to be associated with differential response to LLT, for instance Afrikaner-2 (mutation noted in this patient) null carriers have been shown to have greater response to simvastatin than defective carriers.6 Polymorphisms in other genes have been shown to modulate response to statins and ezetimibe.7 Response to PCSK9i in HeFH has generally been similar regardless of genotype, although available evidence is based on functional receptor activity classification rather than specific variant type, and a meta-analysis of PCSK9-targeting therapies in genotyped HeFH found no statistically significant heterogeneity across genetic variants or between monoclonal antibodies and small interfering RNA.8

However, before attributing the greater LDL-C reduction to a variant-specific pharmacological advantage of inclisiran, alternative explanations deserve consideration. Adherence to previous therapies was self-reported, whereas inclisiran was clinic-administered by subcutaneous injection under direct observation. Although the patient had compelling reasons to remain adherent to medications (given family history), medication adherence or improper technique of injection as a contributor to attenuated previous responses cannot be entirely excluded. For example, with ezetimibe, there was an initial reduction in LDL-C of 45% (greater than expected) but based on the provided figure, LDL-C increased while ezetimibe was continued. Similarly, after initially starting evolocumab, the LDL-C increases and then decreases, which are all atypical responses that cannot be completely explained.

Intraindividual fluctuation of LDL-C over time independent of medication changes represents another issue that merits attention. Typically, when there are sudden increases in LDL-C clinicians should examine the status of the patient’s thyroid, evaluate if there has been the development of significant proteinuria, review the patient’s diet, and examine medication changes. In the current report, several physiological modulators plausibly contributed to this patient's LDL-C trajectory, of which thyroid status is the most compelling. As shown in Figure 2 of the case report, the LDL-C nadir on inclisiran coincided with the lowest recorded thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), while periods of rising TSH earlier in the patient's course tracked with worsening hypercholesterolemia despite stable or intensified therapy. This association is mechanistically coherent on multiple levels: hypothyroidism suppresses hepatic LDL-R expression, impairs receptor recycling, and reduces lipoprotein lipase activity, each directly counteracting LLT efficacy; in addition, TSH directly upregulates hepatic PCSK9 expression9 via the sterol regulatory element-binding protein pathway, providing a specific mechanistic link between thyroid status and the efficacy of PCSK9-targeting therapies beyond general LDL-R effects. Improving thyroid function therefore may restore these pathways and may have substantially amplified the apparent inclisiran response. Progressive weight gain and subsequent loss on semaglutide represent an additional modulator: obesity accelerates very low density lipoprotein production and low-density lipoprotein turnover, whereas the metabolic improvements accompanying glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonist therapy extend to broader effects on hepatic lipid metabolism that are difficult to quantify in isolation, beyond the modest 3% to 5% direct LDL-C reduction attributed to this drug class. Taken together, inclisiran was introduced into a metabolic milieu of low TSH and weight reduction, which may have all contributed to its success.

The presented patient and challenges in reducing her LDL-C emphasize the importance and nuances in finding effective personalized medical options in lowering cholesterol. In addition, the case emphasizes why serial lipid measurements and measuring lipids after changing or starting therapies are important to ensure adequate and expected LDL-C lowering is achieved. We are living in an era in which several options are available to reduce LDL-C and several others continue to emerge. With combination therapy, we can lower LDL-C to recommended targets in most patients. Challenging patients will require additional evaluations for plausible explanations of their less-than-expected response as well as personalized management strategies to achieve their individualized cholesterol targets.10
