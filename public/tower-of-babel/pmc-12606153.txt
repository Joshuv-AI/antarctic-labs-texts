Deep learning and multi-objective optimization for real-time occupancy-based energy control in smart buildings
Authors: Shaoqi Zhang, Cheng Kong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Forecasting room utilization based on indoor environmental conditions offers a novel approach, which improves energy efficiency and also delivers the personalized indoor comfort. This study investigates whether parameters such as CO2 concentration, illumination, humidity, and temperature which can reliably predict the room occupancy. This work introduces a new deep learning-augmented predictive energy modeling (DL-PEM) framework combined with multi-objective particle swarm optimization (MOPSO) for real-time, occupancy-based energy management in intelligent buildings. In contrast to conventional linear predictive or rule-based systems, DL-PEM utilizes a deep feedforward neural network (DNN) that can extract non-linear relationships between indoor environmental variables (CO2, lighting, humidity, temperature) and occupation patterns. Using a Pareto-optimal approach to manage trade-offs, this real-time system minimizes energy consumption (kWh), reduces CO2 concentration (ppm), and maximizes the occupant thermal comfort index. It does this by adjusting adaptive HVAC and lighting control via MOPSO. The combined framework illustrates enhanced adaptability under changing conditions and scalability towards wider deployment. Experimental results indicate that the method proposed here attains 99.8% accuracy and at most 85% optimization efficiency, outperforming KNN, DT, AO-ANN, and even LSTM baselines for prediction and control tasks. Empirical evaluation using real building data demonstrates that the proposed DL-PEM-MOPSO framework significantly outperforms traditional models, enhances decision-making transparency, and offers a scalable, future-ready solution for smart building energy management. It improves occupancy data analysis and adapts to seasonal fluctuations, which also optimizes the thermal comfort and enables the accurate power demand forecasting, and enhances overall energy utilization.

Introduction

The rapid growth in global energy demand, coupled with the need to mitigate climate change, has brought more emphasis on sustainable building operations and energy-efficient management practices. Traditional building energy management systems rely upon fixed schedules or feedback approaches that do not take into account dynamic occupancy. Also varying environmental conditions, and consequently lead to inefficient energy use and compromised comfort levels. While artificial intelligence-based methods of room occupancy prediction based on variables like carbon dioxide concentration and light have been explored in recent studies. Most of these methods are plagued by the nature of their application focusing narrowly on a limited set of features, being non-adaptive in varying environments, and offering limited support for multi-objective optimization. These concerns have been reiterated in recent papers, which have argued that past AI-based occupancy and HVAC control models are not generalizable across heterogeneous building environments and do not strike a good balance between competing tasks. Arun et al.1 demonstrated, for instance, that although deep learning techniques and the Internet of Things can be utilized to improve tracking in existing buildings, conventional algorithms are still very context-sensitive and unadaptable. Similarly, Long2 highlighted that conventional AI-based methods for designing building envelopes have a tendency to restrict itself to a small number of features, which renders them less robust in dynamic settings. Verma et al.3 also contrasted several data-driven optimization strategies and stressed that the majority of current methods attain high prediction accuracy but are lacking in integrated multi-objective optimization, thus limiting their real-world efficacy. Together, these investigations reinforce the limitations of previous AI methods and confirm the need for systems that are expandable, flexible, and optimization-based, such as the one described in this research. Most of the current AI-based occupancy control approaches lack adaptability and do not address multi-objective trade-offs, which restricts their usage in real-time building environments1–3.

Sophisticated methods in occupancy-controlled control and HVAC optimization have also come to the forefront of the need to employ real-time environmental and occupant behavior information for dynamic building control. The authors Abdel-Razek et al.4 and Arun et al.1 indicate the possibility of AI-based models in controlling thermal comfort as well as occupant prediction for energy efficiency enhancement. Long2 and Azizi et al.5 discuss optimization techniques that dynamically adapt control parameters based on deep learning for smart buildings. To confirm our strategy, we compare our DL-PEM-MOPSO framework with other established multi-objective optimization algorithms, such as NSGA-II, MOEA/D, and SPEA26, which are traditionally applied in smart building contexts in balancing energy use against occupant comfort. These publications establish the validity of hybrid approaches that merge data-driven forecasting with evolutionary optimisation as the basis of reliable, scalable building control systems. Integrating deep learning neural network forecasting with multi-objective optimisation, our model follows and expands current state-of-the-art developments.

Nearly 70% of the global population will live in cities by 2050, as is well known1,4. The astonishing rate of urbanization is not going down. Therefore, adapting to the present situation rather than reducing its negative impacts is the best course of action for interested citizens and politicians2,6. Technologies has been applied to diagnose, disrupt, resolve, and sustain these negative consequences in order to lessen the enormous environmental concerns that urbanization will bring about. These efforts can be more integrated and take the shape of smart cities, or they can be independent projects. Despite its new definition, the effort focused on technology and city administration as distinct fields rather than as components of a larger whole8.

Energy efficiency is an economical and environmental concern, not only a technical one pertaining to engineering or construction. Especially in the expanding cities of developing and growing sectors, massive structures have been on the rise; the power system in these areas is prone to collapsing under the strain of mounting need9. Heat zone impacts are exacerbated by ineffective high-rise structures, which also raise energy expenses and waste resources more generally. Therefore, energy-efficient exterior design is about the economic sustainability of cities and extends beyond engineering and material science. To reduce the significant environmental issues that development will have, technologies have been utilized to determine, interfere with, remedy, and sustain these adverse consequences, whether as stand-alone initiatives or as part of a more connected one like a smart city.

Although both kinds of heat pump technologies can significantly reduce energy consumption, especially for warmth, in comparison to conventional HVAC systems, these systems’ performance varies depending on the temperature, the kind of establishing, and the system architecture10. Because they are inexpensive and simple to install, ASHP systems may be a viable option for retrofitting existing structures11. However, ASHP performance can be significantly impacted by variations in the outside air temperatures, which can result in a higher operating energy requirement than GSHP efficiency. In regions when the outside temperatures fall below the realistic bounds of an ASHP, these consequences may be particularly severe12. As an alternative, GSHP systems utilize the comparatively consistent temperatures that are kept below 1 to 3 m underground, which leads to more reliable and effective operation13–15. GSHP systems may need a higher initial financial outlay than traditional heating systems because of the significant groundwork required, such as the digging of trenches or boreholes16.

Numerous machine learning classification algorithms were used to investigate the predictive power of light, temperature, humidity, and CO2 sensor data for occupancy in a room environment. Recent predictions suggest that precise occupancy detection estimation in buildings could result in energy savings of between 30 and 42%5,17,18. Using information gathered by an office building’s CO2 sensor and additional synthetic data obtained using a computational approach for CO2 structure with randomly assigned occupant actions. The majority of current systems struggle with limited environmental parameter scope, lack of flexibility to different building settings, and lack of multi-objective trade-off handling, despite the potential of AI in occupancy-driven building management. These systems frequently fall short of striking the ideal balance between occupant comfort and energy economy. Furthermore, a lot of models are rule-based and unexplainable, which makes them challenging to understand and extrapolate. This study aims to fill these gaps by developing a hybrid framework that combines Multi-Objective Particle Swarm Optimization (MOPSO) and Deep Learning-based Predictive Energy Modeling (DL-PEM). With the use of explainable AI technologies like SHAP, this system aims to provide model interpretability while simultaneously optimizing energy consumption and occupant comfort in real-time. The suggested approach might run into issues like overfitting in building-specific installations, increasing processing demands for real-time learning and optimization, and dependence on high-quality sensor data.

While prior studies have combined optimization algorithms with predictive modeling for smart buildings, most approaches have limitations in either real-time adaptability or interpretability. Our framework advances the state of the art by integrating deep neural network–based predictive energy modeling with MOPSO to simultaneously minimize energy consumption and maximize thermal comfort in real time. In contrast to conventional approaches like NSGA-II or MOEA/D, which have greater computational overhead and no native explainability. Our proposed method exploits the use of SHAP analysis for decision transparency and cross-dataset validation for robustness across multiple building scenarios. Proposed methodology

The proposed method involves using Internet of Things (IoT) sensors and smart meters incorporated into the building’s structures to collect dataset. Next, it pre-processes the heterogeneous data streams, such as building usage patterns, weather patterns, material thermal characteristics, and real-time energy use measurements. A Deep Learning-based Predictive Energy Model (DL-PEM), which use deep neural networks to forecast future consumption of energy and thermal demand across a range of usage trends and climatic variables at a high temporal resolution, is then used to process the data inputs. The DL-PEM predictions are used as functional inputs in Multi-Objective Particle Swarm Optimization (MOPSO), the technique most suited to managing concurrent goals. MOPSO may be capable to offer the finest operational designs and layouts that reduce energy consumption and environmental effects while increasing occupant effectiveness and satisfaction. Lastly, Explainable AI tool like SHAP is used for making model decisions. In Fig. 1 shows the architectural structure of suggested work.

Architecture of suggested work.

Next, this data is input into the Data Pre-processing module where a number of important cleaning operations are done, specifically, the dataset’s normalization, noise filtering, and missing value imputation are performed. Following this, the Value Extraction Phase of the Feature Selection process is done using SHAP explainable AI techniques as illustrated in one of the SHAP plots where the most important features associated with energy consumption and thermal comfort are extracted and selected. These features are passed to a DL-PEM (Predictive Energy Modeling based on Deep Learning) which is dedicated to the prediction of energy demand and indoor climate parameters. The produced forecasts are then passed on to the MOPSO (Multi-Objective Particle Swarm Optimization) module where they are utilized to dynamically adjust control parameters such as air conditioning and lighting and at the same time energy conservation and occupant comfort are maintained at balance. Lastly, the output from MOPSO is given into Control, evaluation and explainability module it helps to evaluate the building system.

Dataset collection and occupancy labeling

Initially, the data is collected by using IoT sensors and smart meters which is incorporated in sustainable building. With the help of these sensors, it collects temperature, humidity, light, CO2 and room occupancy. In this work, it uses Kaggle dataset for evaluation (https://www.kaggle.com/sachinsharma1123/room-occupancy (accessed on 2 May 2022))4. The dataset was used to test and experiment with the categorization strategies. The dataset’s properties include carbon dioxide (CO2), light, temperature, humidity, humidity ratio, and residence position (0 for vacant, 1 for engaged).

These environmental parameters were selected for their high correlation with people and low deployment complexity for smart building contexts. Unlike motion or door sensors, not included in the Kaggle dataset, CO2 and light levels give a cumulative indication of occupancy and can be monitored passively without invading privacy. These parameters have been found to be robust in various existing occupancy modeling research and are suitable for scalable, real-time application.

This data collection includes six columns, 2665 rows, and six sections, including Temperature: measured in Celsius humidity: the proportion of relative humidity, Lux (light), ppm (parts per million) of CO2, measured in kilos of water vapour per kilogram of air, the humidity ratio is a number that is generated from temperatures and humidity ratio. One indicates occupied status, while zero indicates unoccupied status.

The provided occupancy tags in the dataset were obtained with the assistance of motion detection sensors (specifically, PIR sensors) that recorded human movement within the room in question. Instead of manually annotated or threshold values, the binary labels—0 for unoccupied and 1 for occupied were generated by a physical sensor in real time. By guaranteeing the accuracy and consistency of the occupancy categorization, this fundamental truth gathering technique provides a solid foundation for verifying the model and training.

Dataset pre-processing

An effective DL algorithm often differs from an average one based on its capacity to clean a dataset. Among the most difficult problems in purifying information is identifying and handling outliers. Measurements that vary significantly from the remaining portions of the dataset are known as outliers. Failure to eliminate anomalies from the dataset will result in poor performance from even the strongest DL methods. Outliers may have a detrimental effect on the initial validation of the DL algorithm, resulting in a decrease in accuracy. The dataset includes outlier values of characteristics that have been eliminated utilizing anomaly rejections with the standard deviation and mean, as mentioned earlier in the statistical evaluation of the database.

Handling missing or corrupted sensor data

In real-time smart building infrastructures, sensor data may be compromised by transient loss of connectivity, sensor drift, or hardware failure, leading to missing or anomalous values. For this purpose, a strong fallback and imputation strategy was incorporated in the data preprocessing workflow. More specifically, missing data values are detected based on time-window consistency checks and processed utilizing a two-tier system. First, the short-term missing values are filled up with rolling mean imputation to preserve temporal continuity. Second, for longer gaps or individual sensor failures, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) interpolation against correlated environmental factors (e.g., temperature and CO2) is applied. To negate sensor drift or spikes in noise, outlier detection with z-score thresholding is also performed, and outlier readings are removed or smoothed out by applying smoothing filters. In case of failure in imputation or loss of sensor data reliability, the model triggers a rule-based fallback situation that is based on previous tendencies andervative default values for HVAC and illumination control. This ensures uninterrupted operation of the DL-PEM-MOPSO system under partial failure conditions of sensors.

Deep Learning-based Predictive Energy Modeling (DL-PEM) is a data-driven method that uses predictive modeling of building energy demand by learning intricate interdependencies among indoor environmental parameters and occupancy patterns. In essence, DL-PEM employs deep neural networks (DNNs) as high-dimensional sensor data, e.g., temperature, humidity, CO2 concentration, lighting level, and occupancy status, and generate accurate, non-linear energy demand predictions. DL-PEM differs from conventional rule-based or linear predictive models in detecting fine-grained temporal and spatial interdependencies in environmental dynamics, enabling it to learn and generalize across various building scenarios and usage patterns. The deep learning architecture, which consists of multiple hidden layers with activation functions such as ReLU, allows the system to learn and generalize across varied conditions and scenarios. The version iteratively learns from actual-time sensor inputs and adjusts its inner weights through backpropagation to lessen prediction mistakes. Consequently, DL-PEM is a clever engine within clever building systems, generating pretty accurate power forecasts that may be used to inform adaptive manage strategies for HVAC, lighting fixtures, and ventilation and thereby understand energy efficiency and occupant comfort.

To provide interpretability of the deep learning outputs, we utilized SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values to determine and visualize the most impactful features. The SHAP summary plot indicates that CO2 level, illumination levels, and temperature had the most significant positive or negative effect on occupancy prediction and energy consumption predictions. SHAP values provide fine-grained understanding of how every sensor input is a factor in a particular prediction and hence facilitate the understanding and confidence in model behavior in real-time by facility managers. An example SHAP force plot further depicts single prediction explanation, and traceability and wise operational decisions are facilitated.

The new DL-PEM model is realized as a deep feedforward neural network trained for predictive energy modeling on high-dimensional indoor environmental data. Three hidden layers with 64, 128 and 64 neurons each comprise the network, along with an input layer. The ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit) activation function is used in each hidden layer to inject non-linearity. To avoid overfitting, a dropout layer with a dropout rate of 0.3 is added following every hidden layer. The output layer employs a sigmoid activation to generate probabilistic occupancy predictions. The model is optimized with the Adam optimizer for a learning rate of 0.001, a batch size of 32, and for 100 training epochs. Training was performed on 75% of the data while leaving 25% for testing. The architecture was empirically optimized for optimal trade-off between accuracy and computational cost.

The first step in the procedure is to arrange a collection of particles, each of which represents a potential solution in the form of building control parameters like air flow rates, light levels, or temperature points. Each particle is assigned a position and speed that guide its motion over the answer space. By predicting energy consumption and heat comfort according to its parameters, the DL-PEM model verifies each particle. These tests serve as the particle’s “fitness” for two conflicting objectives: reducing energy use and increasing occupant comfort.

Every particle keeps track of its own top solution (pbest), while a global top solution (gbest) comes from an outside collection of non-dominated (Pareto-optimal) answers. The process updates the particles’ speeds and spots over and over using a mix of momentum personal know-how, and group wisdom. After each update, DL-PEM checks how well each particle’s new spot performs. If a particle finds a better spot in terms of performance or variety, it updates its personal best.

We implemented a crowding distance-based diversity preservation strategy in the MOPSO algorithm that was used to maintain a diverse and representative set of solutions across the Pareto front. The crowding distance is the relative density of solutions around any specific particle in objective space, and the algorithm prefers the solutions that occupy less crowded regions. This makes sure the chosen solutions cover the whole range of trade-offs between the competing goals in our case, maximum thermal comfort and minimum energy consumption rather than being concentrated in one area. By assigning equal priority to dominance and distribution, this methodology prevents premature convergence to a limited number of solutions and helps in constructing a more comprehensive Pareto front. Empirical experiments demonstrated that MOPSO convergence towards a stable Pareto front from 80 to 100 iterations was typical, balancing exploration and exploitation. This range was considered sufficient enough to find optimal solutions without spending too much computational time and to be responsive enough for real-time adaptation in decision-making control for smart buildings.

Discussion

Its extensibility to a variety of smart building environments is confirmed by the DL-PEM-MOPSO framework’s reproducibility using two different occupancy datasets. The model’s deploy ability to multiple application scenarios is confirmed by the verification. The framework’s modularity further supports its application in multi-zone building or groups of buildings with different HVAC characteristics, allowing real-time dynamic control at scale.

The uniform performance on both Kaggle and UCI datasets indicates the scalability and adaptability of the suggested framework. The attainment of nearly identical accuracy and F1-scores on two disparate datasets assures that the model generalizes very well to unseen building situations, thus resolving issues of overfitting and enhancing its applicability to real-world settings.

Deployment scalability in multi zone and campus setting

The DL-PEM-MOPSO model is architected with a modular design that allows for deployment in multi-zone buildings or campus-scale settings with varied HVAC usage patterns. Any given zone can be classified as an independent system, with the DL-PEM block being trained on zone-level sensor inputs. This enables localized energy prediction according to occupancy and thermal behaviors in the zone. In large buildings or campus networks, the MOPSO algorithm may be hierarchically installed with a high-level controller controlling zone-level optimizers, or distributed. Transfer learning techniques can also be used to reuse trained DL-PEM models for other different similar zones or buildings to reduce the training cost and enhance scalability. This flexibility helps to support the framework’s generalization and practicability in real-world applications over smart city infrastructures and diverse building ecosystems.

Conclusion

In summary, this study suggests a hybrid solution combining Deep Learning-enhanced Predictive Energy Modeling (DL-PEM) and Multi-Objective Particle Swarm Optimization (MOPSO) to provide real-time, occupancy-based energy optimization in smart buildings. The system demonstrates excellent predictive potential with 99.8% accuracy, 99.1% precision, and a thermal comfort score of 0.92 compared to traditional machine learning and optimization methods. Major contributions are the integration of explainable AI (SHAP) for transparency, dynamic control of HVAC and lighting from environmental conditions, and effective handling of trade-offs between energy use and comfort. Furthermore, SHAP helps to clarify model decisions, building trust and enabling facility managers to better understand the system. This approach promotes a rapid, data-driven method for building management that supports the objectives of environmental sustainability and the broader vision of smart cities. This results in significant energy savings and improved comfort from heating demonstrating the model’s effectiveness and potential to evolve for real-time building management. A key advantage of this approach is its capacity to manipulate two objectives such as reducing energy consumption and ensuring user comfort through explainable AI and adaptive control. But the system might face limits in how much computing it needs, relying on top-notch sensor info, and maybe fitting too well to specific building setups. Despite its good performance, the proposed approach has many practical limitations. Although the simulation is based on high-quality sensor information, prediction effectiveness may be impacted by interference from malfunctioning sensors or issues with validation. Furthermore, real-time deep learning and optimization computational requirements can be problematic to implement in resource-scarce settings. Overfitting of the model also occurs when used with building-specific datasets without transfer learning. In order to improve generality across various building segments, future studies will examine edge-based deployment utilizing light simulations, integrating with clean energy sources, and more advanced domain adaption techniques. Future work can explore by adding green energy sources, using Transfer learning at the edge for quicker responses learning that transfers to make the model work well across different buildings. Nonetheless, achieving these future directions comes with significant technical challenges. For example, edge deployment of DL-PEM models is hampered by limited resources, available memory, and real-time inference latency, which could call for model compression or light-weight models. Analogously, transfer learning between various buildings has to overcome adaptation issues, e.g., avoiding negative transfer whenever the source and target domains have different occupancy dynamics or sensor settings. For campus-level scalability, interworking across diverse IoT infrastructures as well as handling communication overhead between multiple zones pose additional challenges. Overcoming these challenges will be important to realize the proposed framework into large-scale, viable deployments.
