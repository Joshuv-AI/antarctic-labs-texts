A case report of post-arthroscopic knee infection caused by Capnocytophaga sputigena in an anemic patient
Authors: Yuzhu Wang, Yiqun Ma, Jiayong Yi, Xiaoyu Li, Yunchao Shao, Lu Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCapnocytophaga sputigena (C. sputigena), a species of bacteria resident in the human oral cavity. Here, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a post-arthroscopic knee infection caused by this organism in an anemic patient. We described the clinical characteristics, therapeutic intervention and outcome associated with knee joint infection induced by this bacterium. Furthermore, the treatment approaches and challenges in managing this particular pathogen were explored.Case presentationA 58-year-old female patient presented with a knee infection following arthroscopy, caused by C. sputigena. The species C. sputigena was confirmed on the basis of the results of the joint fluid culture and metagenomics next-generation sequencing (mNGS). The infection was treated with arthroscopic debridement, in addition to intravenous and intra-articular meropenem irrigation. The initial therapy with meropenem resulted in clinical improvement, and was subsequently de-escalated to amoxicillin-clavulanate potassium. Following a period of observation, the patient was discharged, as her condition was stable.ConclusionsThe case of a post-arthroscopic knee infection caused by C. sputigena in an anemic patient resulted in surgeons developing a more profound clinical understanding of infections induced by the bacterium. The combination of arthroscopic debridement with meropenem therapy (subsequently followed by de-escalation to amoxicillin-clavulanate potassium) yielded favourable clinical outcomes, thereby establishing a practical reference point for the management and prognosis of such infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13507-z.Keywords: Capnocytophaga sputigena, Knee joint, Infection, Arthroscopy

Background

Capnocytophaga, a group of facultatively anaerobic gram-negative bacteria that are classified within the Flavobacteriaceae family, commonly colonizes the oral cavities of humans and animals. Nine species have been identified. Specifically, C. sputigena, C. gingivalis, C. granulosa, C. haemolytica, C. leadbetteri, C. ochracea, and C. genospecies AHN8471 belong to the microbiome of the human oral cavity, while C. canimorsus and C. cynodegmi belong to the oral microbiota of dogs and cats [1]. Such infections are usually rare. However, it should be noted that these organisms have the capacity to act as causative agents of infection in patients with both immunocompromised and immunocompetent states [2–6]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first documented case of C. sputigena-caused post-arthroscopic knee joint infection in an anemic patient with no history of animal bite or splenectomy, as reported in the English-language scientific literature. This may provide a valuable reference point for the diagnosis and treatment of infections caused by the bacterium.

Case presentation

A 58-year-old female (height 156 cm, weight 48 kg) presented with a history of left knee soreness that had been persisting for a period of one year. Prior to this admission, the patient underwent arthroscopic surgery at a different hospital one month prior, due to exacerbated left knee swelling and pain. However, after hospital discharge, she developed progressive swelling, stiffness, and limited motion of the left knee, accompanied by intermittent fever, with a maximum temperature of 37.9℃. She was admitted to Zhongshan Hospital Fudan University with a suspected postoperative left knee infection for further treatment. The patient has suffered from anaemia for a period of six years. Six years prior to the present, the patient exhibited a haemoglobin level of 89 g/L. The level of haemoglobin demonstrated the stability over a period of six years without obvious progression. The results of the bone marrow aspiration performed two years prior had revealed no abnormalities. Moreover, there is no documented history of known food or drug allergies. The patient exhibited no history of immune-related systemic diseases or long-term use of immunosuppressive drugs. In addition, the patient had undergone the insertion of six dental implants and had two carious lesions, despite maintaining good daily oral hygiene. On admission (hospital day 1), a physical examination was conducted, which revealed the following vital signs: a temperature of 36.3 °C, a pulse rate of 68 beats per minute, a respiratory rate of 18 breaths per minute, and a blood pressure of 104/65 mmHg. Physical examination revealed a surgical scar over the left knee with local skin warmth. The magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the left knee showed infrapatellar fat pad injury, anterior cruciate ligament injury, and meniscal injury, accompanied by effusion and soft tissue oedema in the suprapatellar and subcutaneous regions, consistent with inflammatory/infective changes. Laboratory tests conducted on admission showed that the complete blood count exhibited a white blood cell (WBC) count of 4.74 × 10⁹/L (reference 3.5-9.5), with a neutrophil percentage of 79.9% (reference 40.0-75.0), a haemoglobin level of 49 g/L, and a platelet count of 105 × 10⁹/L (reference 125-350). Furthermore, the high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) level was recorded at 123.9 mg/L (reference 0.0-3.0), while the activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) was determined to be 34.5 s (reference 25-31.3), the D-dimer level was 2.47 mg/L (0.00-0.80), serum albumin was 31 g/L (reference 35-55), fasting blood glucose was 5.6 mmol/L (reference 3.9-6.1), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was 160 U/L (reference 109–245), total bilirubin (TBil) was 11.4 µmol/L (reference 3.4–20.4), and direct bilirubin (DBil) was 6.8 µmol/L (reference 0.0–6.8). A preliminary diagnosis of left knee postoperative infection and anaemia was made. On hospital day 3, ultrasound-guided percutaneous catheter drainage of the left knee was performed, yielding pale yellow purulent fluid. No antibiotics were administered to the patient prior to sample collection. Metagenomics next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of the joint drainage fluid sample identified C. sputigena (41,626 reads; relative abundance 89.03%). On hospital day 5, culture of joint drainage fluid was positive for C. sputigena, while the results of blood cultures were negative. Following the etiological diagnosis, targeted intravenous antibiotic therapy with meropenem was initiated. On hospital day 7, antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) by disk diffusion method yielded the following inhibition zone diameters: amikacin (10 mm), gentamicin (25 mm), tobramycin (6 mm), ampicillin/sulbactam (26 mm), piperacillin (30 mm), piperacillin/tazobactam (26 mm), ceftazidime (6 mm), cefepime (25 mm), imipenem (30 mm), meropenem (25 mm), aztreonam (6 mm), ciprofloxacin (15 mm), levofloxacin (11 mm), minocycline (25 mm), ceftazidime/avibactam (28 mm), and cefoperazone/sulbactam (25 mm). Of note, the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) does not define interpretive breakpoints for C. sputigena. On hospital day 9, laboratory markers improved: WBC 3.68 × 10⁹/L, neutrophils 74.3%, hs‑CRP 36.9 mg/L, hemoglobin 61 g/L, indicating a favorable response to meropenem. Consequently, the current anti-infective regimen was maintained. On hospital day 10, arthroscopic debridement was performed (Fig. 1), and meropenem irrigation was administered postoperatively. At this time, the albumin level was 37 g/L (reference 35-55). Following two weeks of intravenous meropenem and daily joint irrigation, the treatment regimen was de-escalated to oral amoxicillin-clavulanate potassium (0.75 g, three times daily) on hospital day 24. A repeat joint fluid culture, collected on hospital day 19 was negative following a 15-day incubation period. The patient exhibited signs of reduced swelling and pain, and was discharged following the manifestation of an amelioration in her condition on hospital day 30 and continued oral amoxicillin‑clavulanate potassium after discharge. Ten days after discharge, the patient exhibited significantly improved haemoglobin and albumin levels of 85 g/L and 43 g/L, respectively, when compared with the levels present at the time of admission. Discussion and conclusions

Although C. sputigena is generally considered to be a commensal organism, it has been observed to manifest with systemic complications, including but not limited to, bacteremia and chorioamnionitis [5–7]. In order to more fully comprehend the potential of the therapy to treat infections caused by C. sputigena, a literature search was conducted, which revealed 32 studies that had previously been documented in the extant literature on human patients [2–33] (Supplementary Table S1). Although the involvement of C. canimorsus in knee infections had been previously described [34], detailed therapeutic management was not reported. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of C. sputigena-caused post-arthroscopic knee joint infection in an anemic patient, and we herein detail its specific clinical treatment course.

C. sputigena is a facultative anaerobic gram‑negative bacillus that commonly colonizes the human oral cavity and is regarded as part of the normal commensal flora. Consequently, when isolated from clinical samples, particularly in non‑sterile sites, it is frequently interpreted as colonization or incidental contamination. Nevertheless, the preponderance of evidence in the present case unequivocally substantiates its role as a genuine pathogenic organism, thereby contradicting the possibility of it being a mere colonizer or contaminant. Firstly, C. sputigena was identified in a consistent manner by both joint fluid culture and mNGS at the species level. This significantly reduces the likelihood of exogenous contamination during specimen collection or processing. The utilization of mNGS technology has been demonstrated to exhibit elevated levels of sensitivity and specificity across a range of clinical specimens, with particular efficacy observed in the analysis of orthopaedic puncture fluid, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid samples [35]. Although the implementation of mNGS technology enhanced the precision of infection diagnosis, traditional bacterial culture techniques are widely regarded as the gold standard for diagnosis. Secondly, the patient exhibited typical clinical features of infectious arthritis, including persistent joint swelling, severe local pain, and limited mobility. Thirdly, considerable elevations in peripheral blood leukocyte count, neutrophil percentage, and hs-CRP were observed, which were consistent with acute inflammation. Furthermore, the patient’s clinical symptoms and inflammatory markers demonstrated a substantial improvement following targeted antimicrobial therapy. When considered as a whole, the consistent microbiological identification, congruent clinical manifestations, heightened inflammatory indicators, and positive response to antibiotic treatment collectively substantiate the hypothesis that C. sputigena was the causative agent of infectious bone and joint in this patient.

In the present case, arthroscopic surgery was performed, which revealed the presence of a substantial quantity of purulent exudate within the joint, and extensive hyperplasia of the synovium and injury to the cartilage (Fig. 1). However, it is important to note that the severe cartilage and subchondral bone destruction observed in this case was likely attributable to a combination of the virulence of C. sputigena, delayed diagnosis and treatment, and persistent infection associated with residual suture material, rather than solely the exceptional destructive capacity of the pathogen. Two objectives are identified in the treatment of bone- and joint-localised C. sputigena infections: firstly, to achieve sufficient antibacterial activity and, secondly, to ensure adequate joint or bone diffusion. In general, susceptible antibiotics should be administered for a period of 4–6 weeks. The precise duration is contingent on numerous factors, including bacterial virulence, the clinical response to treatment, the surgical approach adopted, and the extent to which surgical debridement is performed. However, following the acquisition of bacterial culture and antimicrobial susceptibility results, it is imperative to meticulously evaluate the necessity for modification of the initial regimen, particularly when the initial antimicrobial therapy has attained a favourable clinical response. The therapeutic regimen consisting of prolonged intravenous meropenem and intra-articular irrigation may be considered relatively aggressive. Imipenem-cilastatin, clindamycin, or combinations of β-lactamase inhibitors have been shown to be efficacious in cases of Capnocytophaga species infection [36]. The majority of antibiotics, including amoxicillin, piperacillin-tazobactam, carbapenems, cloxacillin, cephalosporins, aztreonam, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, doxycycline, vancomycin, linezolid, daptomycin, clindamycin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, fosfomycin, rifampin, dalbavancin, and oritavancin, exhibited effective penetration into bone and joint tissues [37]. However, it should be noted that certain isolates have been observed to produce β-lactamases, which have been shown to render these medications ineffective. As specified in the 53rd Chinese translation version of the Sanford Guide to Antimicrobial Therapy (2023), amoxicillin-clavulanate and piperacillin-tazobactam are recommended as first-line therapeutic drugs for infections caused by Capnocytophaga species. Furthermore, carbapenems, cefuroxime, and third-generation cephalosporins are designated as second-line treatment options [38]. At the time of admission, the causative pathogen remained unidentified, and the patient exhibited severe acute septic arthritis and extensive joint injury. The selection of meropenem was determined by the patient’s clinical condition and local drug availability: injectable amoxicillin-clavulanate was not available at the institution, and piperacillin-tazobactam required provisional procurement at that time. Consequently, the decision was taken to administer antimicrobial therapy with meropenem in order to treat bone and joint infections. Following definitive identification of C. sputigena, the most appropriate subsequent course of action would have been to transition to a narrower-spectrum agent, such as amoxicillin-clavulanate or clindamycin, in accordance with the findings of preceding studies and the established clinical guidelines. However, due to severe joint destruction, slow improvement of inflammatory markers, and the requirement for arthroscopic debridement, meropenem was continued to ensure satisfactory antimicrobial penetration into joint tissues and to minimize the risk of treatment failure or recurrent infection. This case demonstrates that, while narrow-spectrum agents are preferred for confirmed C. sputigena infection, individualised therapy based on disease severity, imaging findings and clinical response remains crucial in managing septic bone and joint infection.

The most common aetiology of septic arthritis is hematogenous infection, followed by infections secondary to joint trauma, surgery, and puncture. Irrespective of the antimicrobial regimen, it is imperative that surgical drainage or joint debridement should be promptly performed upon the suspicion of septic arthritis. Intrathecal injection alone is not generally employed; however, continuous antibiotic infusion combined with sustained joint drainage is considered an effective therapeutic approach [39]. In the present case, despite the fact that meropenem, when administered systemically, displays excellent efficacy in terms of penetration into synovial fluid, local therapy is not routinely required. However, in the perioperative period following arthroscopic debridement, intra-articular meropenem irrigation was administered as an adjunctive measure in response to a severe and rapidly progressive joint infection [40]. The clinical assessments demonstrated that the antibacterial treatment regimen yielded favorable therapeutic outcomes. Upon discharge, the patient was transitioned to sequential therapy with amoxicillin-clavulanate potassium.

Three aspects merit consideration in the presented case. First, clinical studies have been published on Capnocytophaga species infections. However, given the absence of randomized studies, a consensus remains elusive regarding the choice of antibiotics and the duration of treatment. Second, as no standardised clinical breakpoints have been established by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) or European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) for C. sputigena, antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed by disk diffusion, and results were reported as inhibition zone diameters (mm). The interpretation process was informed by extant literature and expert recommendations. It is imperative to note that the aforementioned susceptibility data is intended for qualitative reference only and does not constitute a definitive classification of susceptible or resistant categories. Third, although C. sputigena is generally considered to be a commensal organism, it may also be the causative organism in patients with both immunocompromised and immunocompetent states. Thus, it is imperative that surgeons raise awareness of this bacterial infection.

In conclusion, an anemic patient was diagnosed with a knee joint infection caused by C. sputigena following knee arthroscopy. Surgeons should take into account the possibility that C. sputigena may also be the causative organism in immunocompromised patients, with the exception of those who are severely ill and neutropenic. Furthermore, in order to select the most appropriate antimicrobial agents against C. sputigena, it is urgently required to establish the interpretive breakpoints for antimicrobial susceptibility testing.
