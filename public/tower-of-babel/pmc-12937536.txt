Immune-related deubiquitylation spectrum of microsatellite stability colorectal cancer reveals USP7 as a potential immunotherapeutic target
Authors: Xiaomao Yin, Jinran Wu, Mi-die Xu, Tongguan Tian, Lin Zhu, Jiexuan Wang, Xuan Dai, Xin Yang, Jingjing Qian, Wenqiang Wang, Liangchen Zhu, Zekun Zhao, Kai Xu, Yanping Xu, Xinxing Li, Zhiqian Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in microsatellite stable colorectal cancer (MSS CRC) remains limited, highlighting an urgent need for predictive biomarkers. Through multi-omics analysis, we identified two novel MSS CRC subtypes, termed DUB-H and DUB-L. The DUB-L subtype exhibited an inflamed tumor immune microenvironment, a superior response to immune therapy, and better recurrence-free survival (RFS) compared to DUB-H. The classifier gene USP7 was selected as a gene of interest due to its specific expression profile, which is highly expressed in MSS CRC but not in microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) tumors, and strongly correlated with suppressed immune infiltration. Large-scale clinical analyses confirmed associations between high USP7 expression, microsatellite stability, specific consensus molecular subtypes (CMS), and unfavorable prognosis. Single-cell analysis and multiplex immunofluorescence validated an immune-desert phenotype in USP7-high MSS tumors. Mechanistically, USP7 knockdown in MSS CRC cells enhances the secretion of T-cell-recruiting chemokines (CXCL9/10/11), promoting CD8⁺ T cell recruitment and cytotoxicity in vitro. In vivo experiments demonstrated that USP7 blockade enhanced the efficacy of anti-PD-1 treatment in MSS CRC models by remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment, increasing infiltration and function of CD8⁺ T and NK cells. Consistently, low USP7 expression is associated with a better response to anti-PD-1 therapy. Overall, we propose a novel DUB-based classification system for MSS CRC and demonstrate that targeting USP7 may overcome immunotherapy resistance by converting immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” ones.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12943-025-02502-8.Keywords: USP7, Microsatellite stable colorectal cancer, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. Approximately 5–15% of CRCs exhibit a deficient-mismatch-repair (dMMR) or microsatellite instability (MSI) phenotype, while the remaining are proficient-MMR (pMMR) or microsatellite stability (MSS) [2]. The development of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has revolutionized anticancer therapies in recent years, involving the use of the checkpoint inhibitors targeting programmed cell death 1 (PD-1), programmed cell death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA4) [3]. However, not all CRC patients benefit from ICB treatment. PD-1 antibodies (pembrolizumab and nivolumab) were approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for second-line treatment of dMMR/MSI-H CRC in 2017 [4]. Nevertheless, the prevailing view was that the monotherapy (e.g., pembrolizumab) was ineffective for pMMR/MSS CRC patients [5]. Lack of immune cell infiltration and low tumor mutation burden (TMB) have been suggested as mechanisms of immune resistance [4]. Therefore, to overcome these problems, several new combination therapies in pMMR/MSS CRC are currently being investigated. Chalabi et al. reported that 27% (4 out of 15) of pMMR CRC patients showed pathological responses, with three major pathological responses (≤ 10% residual viable tumor) and one partial response under combination therapy with ipilimumab and nivolumab [6]. A randomized phase II study also found that compared with best supportive care alone, MSS CRC patients treated with durvalumab and tremelimumab combinations had significantly improved overall survival (OS), especially those with higher TMB [7]. In addition, a recent phase Ib clinical trial study found that when MSS CRC patients were treated with nivolumab combined with regorafenib, the objective response rate reached 33% (8 out of 24) [8]. These studies suggest that combination with other drugs with different mechanisms of action can potentially overcome resistance. Therefore, a better understanding of the resistance mechanism to improve the efficacy of ICB in pMMR/MSS CRC are urgently needed.

To better clarify this resistance mechanism, the classification of tumors into ‘cold’ and ‘hot’ categories has increasingly advocated [9]. The characteristics of hot tumors are by immune effector cell infiltration and immune activation status, which could induce effective antitumor immunity [10]. In contrast, cold tumors are characterized by insufficient numbers of tumor-infiltrating immune cells and immunosuppressive phenotype, such as low expression of antigen processing and presentation machinery (APM) and tumor mutation burden (TMB), acting together to antagonize the effectiveness of immunotherapy [11–13]. This binary classification has become a recognized concept for predicting the ICB response. Accordingly, MSI CRC shows higher levels of TILs and TMB and higher expression of immune checkpoints [14]. In contrast, MSS CRC is considered to have a less inflamed immune microenvironment. Therefore, dMMR/MSI CRC has achieved more compelling clinical data for ICB treatment compared with pMMR/MSS CRC. However, the underlying molecular mechanism distinguishing ‘cold’ and ‘hot’ MSS CRC remains unclear.

Results

Identification of immune related deubiquitinases (IR-DUBs) in MSS CRC

The overall design of this study was displayed in Fig. 1A. According to the standards of cancer immunotherapy [15], molecular targets should meet two conditions for potential immunotherapy: high expression in tumor cells and inhibition of immune cells infiltration. Therefore, we first analyzed the expression patterns of DUBs between MSS tumors and peritumor samples in TCGA-COAD&READ cohort. Based on the Limma algorithm, 53 up-regulated and 13 down-regulated DUBs were determined after setting |log2FC|> 1 and adjusted P value < 0.05 (Fig. 1B). Then, infiltrated immune cells in MSS samples were identified using the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm and systematically correlated with differentially expressed DUBs. To avoid bias caused by the analytical algorithm, seven additional algorithms, including CIBERSORT, TIMER, xCell, MCP-counter, quanTIseq, EPIC and TIP were also used to calculate immune cell infiltration. Since antitumor immunity is mainly mediated by CD8⁺ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, DUBs that showed significant correlations with infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells and NK cells, and were validated across three or more algorithms, were classified as immune-related DUBs (IR-DUBs) (Table S1).

Finally, we identified 16 DUBs that exhibited a negative correlation with tumor-infiltrating immune cells in MSS colorectal tumors, while 6 DUBs showed a positive correlation within the same tumor type (Fig. 1C). The expression patterns of these 22 DUBs in MSS tumor versus peritumor tissues are presented in Figure S1A. Notably, most negatively correlated DUBs (NC-DUBs)—except USP30—were upregulated in MSS tumors. In contrast, positively correlated DUBs (PC-DUBs) were generally downregulated compared to peritumor tissues. To prevent potential bias caused by the single cohort, four additional cohorts (GSE39582, GSE37892, GSE35896, GSE26682) derived from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) were employed to substantiate the result from TCGA cohort. Immune cell infiltration levels in MSS tumors were again estimated using the ssGSEA algorithm. Consistent with the initial results, NC-DUBs maintained a negative correlation with the vast majority of immune cells, whereas PC-DUBs showed positive correlations across all four validation cohorts (Figure S1B–E).

To explore the mechanism underlying the expression patterns of IR-DUBs and their immune correlations in MSS tumors, we compared the mutation profiles of IR-DUBs between MSS and MSI-H tumors. In MSI-H tumors, NC-DUBs exhibited higher mutation frequencies than PC-DUBs in MSS tumors, with missense mutations being the most common type. A similar trend was observed among PC-DUBs. Furthermore, frameshift deletions were more frequent in MSI-H compared to MSS tumors. Overall, both NC-DUBs and PC-DUBs showed elevated mutation rates in MSI-H tumors (Fig. 1D). Notable IR-DUBs with significantly increased mutation frequencies in MSI-H tumors included USP7 (16% vs 3%, P = 5.29e-04), USP54 (10% vs 1%, P = 1.09e-03), and USP10 (9% vs 2%, P = 9.92e-03), among others (Fig. 1E). It is well established that MSI-H colorectal tumors display a more inflamed tumor microenvironment than MSS tumors. The differential mutation profiles of IR-DUBs between subtypes may help explain this immunogenic discrepancy.

The IR-DUBs spectrum can distinguish MSS CRC into two subtypes with disparate immune status and prognosis

It has been reported that a subset of MSS patients may also benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) or combination therapies [6, 7]. However, the absence of reliable predictive biomarkers continues to hinder clinical decision-making regarding immunotherapy. Given the strong association between IR-DUBs and the immune microenvironment in MSS CRC, we hypothesized that IR-DUB expression could help identify a more immunologically active subset within MSS tumors. Based on established criteria for immunotherapy responsiveness, we selected NC-DUBs to delineate tumor subtypes. Five DUBs (USP7, USP10, USP36, USP40, BRCC3) showing the strongest correlations were included in a consensus clustering algorithm (Table S2). This approach identified two distinct subtypes (designated DUB-H and DUB-L) among MSS samples in the TCGA cohort at the optimal k value (Figure S2A). To validate the robustness of the classification results, four additional GEO cohorts (GSE39582, GSE37892, GSE26682, GSE35896) were employed to implement consensus cluster using the DUB classifier. Reassuringly, the classifier consistently partitioned MSS samples into two discrete subtypes across all validation cohorts (Figure S2B-E). DUB-L samples exhibited a lower expression level of DUBs which concur to MSI-H samples (Fig. 2A, Figure S3A-D). Notably, across four GEO cohorts, the DUB-H cluster contained a higher proportion of CMS2 tumors compared to the DUB-L cluster, although this trend was not observed in the TCGA cohort.

Immunologically, DUB-L samples showed significantly higher immune scores than DUB-H samples (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, the T cell-inflamed gene expression profile (GEP)—a recognized predictor of response to immune checkpoint blockade—was also significantly elevated in DUB-L compared to DUB-H samples (Fig. 2C). Both immune score and T cell-inflamed GEP were higher in MSI-H samples than in either MSS subtype. Consistent increases in these indicators within DUB-L MSS tumors were further validated across all four GEO cohorts. The notable elevations of indicator levels observed in MSI-H tumors were also evident in the GEO cohorts, with the exception of GSE35896, which might be attributed to the limited number of cases (Figure S3E–H). Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) score can also predict immune response to ICIs, higher TIDE scores are associated with reduced immunotherapy benefits and poorer prognosis [16]. In our analysis, the TIDE score was significantly elevated in the DUB-H cluster compared to the DUB-L cluster, while no significant difference was observed between the DUB-H and MSI-H groups (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, DUB-L tumors demonstrated increased infiltration of multiple immune cell types—including CD8⁺ T cells, NK cells, and dendritic cells—relative to DUB-H tumors in both the TCGA cohort and the four GEO validation cohorts (Fig. 2D and Figure S4A). Notably, when applying a TMB cutoff of > 20 mut/Mb, DUB-L MSS CRC patients showed significantly higher tumor mutational burden compared to MSI-H patients (Figure S4B). In summary, the DUB classifier proved robust across multiple independent cohorts. The DUB-L subtype of MSS CRC is characterized by a more immunologically active tumor microenvironment and higher TMB, suggesting that this subgroup is more likely to benefit from immunotherapy.

Additionally, DUB-L samples were associated with significantly longer recurrence-free survival (RFS) compared to DUB-H samples in both the TCGA cohort and GEO cohorts (GSE39582 and GSE37892) (Fig. 2F, Figure S4C). MSI-H tumors also showed improved survival outcomes relative to MSS tumors. Multivariate regression analysis confirmed that both MSI-H status and the DUB-L subtype within MSS tumors served as independent protective factors, even after adjusting for TNM stage (Fig. 2G, Figure S4D). To investigate the underlying mechanisms distinguishing DUB-L and DUB-H tumors, we performed gene set variation analysis (GSVA). The results revealed enhanced activation of antitumor immune pathways—such as cytotoxic T-lymphocyte (CTL), natural killer T (NKT) cell, and cytokine signaling—in both DUB-L and MSI-H tumors (Fig. 2H). The upregulation of the programmed death-1 (PD-1) pathway in DUB-L MSS tumors may further contribute to a more responsive immune microenvironment. Conversely, DUB-H tumors exhibited upregulation of cell cycle-related pathways and processes implicated in colorectal carcinogenesis and progression, including p53, RAS, and PI3K/AKT signaling (Fig. 2I, Figure S4E). Notably, angiogenesis and mismatch repair pathways were also enriched in DUB-H tumors. Consistent with this, expression of angiogenesis-related genes was significantly elevated in DUB-H compared to DUB-L samples (Figure S4F). In conclusion, the DUB-based classifier identifies a distinct subset of MSS CRC patients with an immune-rich tumor microenvironment, higher TMB, and improved survival, who may represent optimal candidates for immunotherapy.

Deubiquitinase USP7 promotes a non-inflamed tumor microenvironment in MSS CRC

Among the NC-DUBs, USP7 demonstrated the most distinct mutation profile between MSS and MSI-H colorectal cancers (Fig. 1E). It was upregulated in MSS tumors and contributed prominently to DUB-based cluster identification (Fig. 2A). These findings prompted further investigation into whether USP7 could represent a potential target for immunotherapy in MSS CRC. Analysis of TCGA and GEO cohorts (GSE39582, GSE26682, GSE37892) revealed a significant negative correlation between USP7 expression and immune cell infiltration levels in MSS tumors (Fig. 3A–B). In the TCGA cohort, USP7 expression was markedly higher in tumor tissues compared to matched peritumoral samples (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, USP7 levels were significantly elevated in the DUB-H subgroup relative to both DUB-L and MSI-H groups, with no notable difference observed between the latter two—a trend consistently validated across all cohorts (Fig. 3D, Figure S5A). Notably, USP7 upregulation was statistically significant specifically in MSS tumors, but not in MSI-H samples (Fig. 3E–F). This tumor-specific over expression was further confirmed in paired clinical samples from our institution using qPCR and Western blot (Fig. 3G, Figure S5E).

Furthermore, USP7 expression showed a significant negative correlation with immune scores in both TCGA and GEO cohorts (Fig. 3I, Figure S5B). Functional enrichment analysis indicated that tumors with low USP7 expression were associated with immune-related processes such as leukocyte-mediated cytotoxicity, T cell proliferation and activation, as well as antigen processing and presentation. In contrast, tumors with high USP7 expression were enriched in cell cycle and DNA metabolism-related activities, including DNA replication, sister chromatid segregation, and regulation of DNA repair (Figure S5C–D). GSEA further supported these findings, revealing enhanced activity in interferon-γ signaling and allograft rejection pathways in tumors with low USP7 expression (Fig. 3H). Since antitumor immunity can be depicted as a stepwise process known as the Cancer-Immunity Cycle [17]. We evaluated the correlation between USP7 and each step. USP7 expression was negatively correlated with most steps of this cycle, particularly the infiltration of immune cells into tumor tissues. It also showed negative associations with established immune signatures, including MHC class I/II, interferon-γ, and interleukin response signatures (Fig. 3J). Further analysis incorporating immunostimulators, MHC molecules, chemokines, and interleukins confirmed that USP7 expression was negatively correlated with the majority of these immune factors across both TCGA and GEO cohorts (Figure S5F). Additionally, low USP7 expression was significantly associated with higher microsatellite instability (MSI) scores in colorectal cancer (Fig. 3K). In summary, USP7 is specifically upregulated in MSS CRC compared to MSI-H CRC and may promote a non-inflamed tumor microenvironment, potentially by suppressing multiple stages of the cancer–immunity cycle, particularly the infiltration of immune cells into tumor tissues.

USP7 is significantly correlated with microsatellite status, molecular subtypes, mutation landscape and prognosis of CRC

Given the specific upregulation of USP7 in MSS CRC and its inverse association with MSI scores, we sought to further investigate the relationship between USP7 expression and microsatellite status. To enhance the robustness of our findings, we integrated eight GEO datasets with the TCGA cohort, totaling 1447 MSS and 323 MSI-H CRC cases. These were grouped into four analytical cohorts: TCGA, GSE39582, Pooled cohort 1 (GSE13067, GSE13294, GSE18088, GSE75316), and Pooled cohort 2 (GSE26682, GSE35896, GSE39084). Batch effects from dataset integration were effectively mitigated in the pooled cohorts (Figure S6A, B). Details of these datasets is provided in Table S3. As illustrated in Fig. 4A, MSS tumors exhibited a markedly higher proportion of high USP7 expression samples compared to MSI-H tumors (with frequencies of 28% vs 9% in TCGA, 43% vs 9% in GSE39582, 65% vs 21% in Pooled cohort 1, 56% vs 10% in Pooled cohort 2). We further evaluated USP7 expression within the consensus molecular subtype (CMS) framework, which classifies CRC into four transcriptome-based subgroups: CMS1 (MSI/immune), CMS2 (canonical), CMS3 (metabolic), and CMS4 (mesenchymal). USP7 expression was lowest in CMS1 tumors and highest in CMS2 tumors, a pattern consistent across all cohorts (Fig. 4B). Consistent with our previous clustering results, the USP7 high-expression samples was predominantly enriched in the DUB-H cluster, while the USP7 low-expression samples aligned more closely with the immunologically active DUB-L and MSI-H clusters (Fig. 4C). Since MSS tumors are characterized by intact mismatch repair (MMR) function, reflected by positive staining of four MMR proteins (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2), we assessed the correlation between USP7 and these genes. USP7 showed significant positive correlations with all four MMR genes (MLH1: R = 0.43; MSH2: R = 0.54; MSH6: R = 0.45; PMS2: R = 0.51; all P < 0.0001) (Figure S7F), further supporting a strong link between USP7 expression and microsatellite stability in CRC.

Additionally, the mutation landscape revealed distinct profiles between USP7 high- and low-expression groups (Figure S7A–B). BRAF-mutant CRC, which is enriched in MSI-H tumors and associated with improved response to immunotherapy [18], showed significantly lower USP7 expression compared to BRAF wild-type tumors in both TCGA and GSE39582 datasets. Conversely, TP53 mutations—more frequent in MSS CRC—were accompanied by elevated USP7 expression relative to TP53 wild-type cases in both cohorts. In contrast, no significant difference in USP7 expression was observed between KRAS-mutated and KRAS wild-type tumors. Furthermore, USP7 expression was significantly reduced in tumors carrying mutations in POLE or MUC16 (Figure S7C–D)-both of which have been proposed as predictive biomarkers for favorable immunotherapy response in CRC [19, 20].

To validate the findings from public database analyses, we utilized two independent local cohorts to evaluate USP7 expression in CRC samples using microarray and immunohistochemistry (IHC). Detailed clinicopathological characteristics of these cohorts are provided in Tables S4 and S5. IHC staining confirmed that USP7 was predominantly localized in tumor epithelial cells (Fig. 4D). In both local cohorts, USP7 expression was significantly elevated in tumor tissues relative to matched peritumoral tissues. Furthermore, USP7 levels were markedly higher in MSS CRC compared to both MSI-H tumors and peritumoral samples, whereas no significant difference was observed in the latter two (Fig. 4E). These findings substantiate the strong correlation between USP7 and microsatellite status and confirm the specific expression of USP7 in MSS CRC (Fig. 3E-F). Given that proximal CRC tumors contain a higher proportion of MSI-H cases—a known indicator for immunotherapy eligibility [21], we also examined USP7 expression by tumor location. USP7 levels were significantly lower in proximal compared to distal CRC (Fig. 4F), a pattern consistently reproduced in the TCGA and GSE39582 datasets (Figure S7E). Most importantly, among MSS CRC patients, those with low USP7 expression exhibited significantly improved overall survival (Fig. 4G).

Overall, the specific upregulation of USP7 in MSS CRC is confirmed at the protein level by IHC, and its expression is significantly associated with microsatellite stability, proficient mismatch repair functionality, and distinct molecular subtypes such as CMS2. Lower USP7 expression correlates with BRAF/POLE/MUC16 mutations, proximal tumor location, and improved overall survival, suggesting USP7 may serve as a potential regulator of immunotherapy sensitivity in MSS CRC.

The combination of single-cell analysis and multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) confirms an immune-desert phenotype in MSS tumors with high USP7 expression

A negative correlation between USP7 and immune activity in the tumor microenvironment was initially identified through bulk RNA-seq analysis and subsequently confirmed using single-cell RNA sequencing. For this purpose, we analyzed MSS tumors from the GSE132465 dataset with the highest and lowest USP7 expression levels (Fig. 5A). Cell types were annotated based on an established classification system [22]. t-SNE visualization revealed a pronounced enrichment of immune cells—including CD8⁺ T cells, CD4⁺ T cells, myeloid cells, and plasma cells—in samples with low USP7 expression. In contrast, epithelial cells were predominantly enriched in samples with high USP7 expression (Fig. 5B, C). Cell communication analysis further demonstrated that low-USP7 samples exhibited stronger interactions with CD8⁺ T cells, CD4⁺ T cells, and myeloid cells than high-USP7 samples (Fig. 5D). Outgoing signaling patterns also markedly differed between the two groups: in low-USP7 samples, CD8⁺ T cells, CD4⁺ T cells, and myeloid cells showed upregulation of CCL signaling, while epithelial cells displayed downregulation of VEGF signaling (Fig. 5E).

To validate the single-cell findings, multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) was performed on the TMA from the local cohort to assess immune cell infiltration. In MSS colorectal cancers, tumors with low USP7 expression showed higher levels of immune cell infiltration compared to those with high USP7 expression (Fig. 5F). Significant negative correlations were observed between the positive staining area of USP7 and proportions of CD3+CD8+ T cells (R = −0.61, P = 1.7e-05), CD3−CD56+ NK cells (R = −0.42, P = 5.1e-03), and GZMB+ cells (R = −0.73, P = 2.3e-08) in MSS CRC tumors (Fig. 5H). MSI-H tumors exhibited a greater abundance of CD3+CD8+ T cells compared to MSS tumors. Similarly, proportions of CD3−CD56+ NK cells and GZMB+ cells were significantly higher in MSI-H tumors than in USP7-high MSS tumors. In contrast, no significant differences in these immune subsets were observed between MSI-H tumors and USP7-low MSS tumors (Fig. 5I). Representative images comparing MSS (USP7-high) and MSI-H tumors are presented in Fig. 5G. Furthermore, it was noted that the tumor stromal regions were enriched with a higher density of immune cells, whereas USP7 staining was predominantly localized in the tumor epithelium. This spatial exclusivity was more significant in MSS tumors with high USP7 expression (Fig. 5F, G). Collectively, the integration of single-cell analysis and mIF data supports that MSS tumors with high USP7 expression display an immune-desert phenotype.

USP7 knockdown enhances secretion of pro-inflammatory chemokines in MSS-type CRC cells.

Immunohistochemistry, multiplex immunofluorescence of tissue microarrays combining with single-cell analyses revealed that USP7 is highly expressed in tumor epithelial cells, but shows low expression in immune cell-enriched stromal regions. This finding raised a key question: does high USP7 expression in tumor cells suppress immune cell infiltration by modulating tumor immunogenicity or chemokine secretion? To investigate, we knocked down USP7 in the human MSS-type CRC cell line SW480 using short hairpin RNA (shUSP7) and analyzed the secretion levels of 29 chemokines and cytokines via a multiplex assay. Results showed that USP7 knockdown significantly increased the secretion of multiple chemokines compared to the control (shNC) group (Fig. 6A). Among these chemokines, CXCL9, CXCL10 and CXCL11-key chemokines responsible for recruiting immune cells, especially CD8⁺ T cells [23, 24]-were robustly upregulated.

We next validated the expression of these three chemokines using two independent shUSP7 (#1 and #2) in two human MSS CRC cell lines (SW480 and SW1116) [25]. Given that the stable expression of CXCL9/10/11 depends on IFN-γ signaling [26, 27], and considering our bioinformatic analysis indicating enhanced IFN-γ response in USP7-low MSS CRC tumors (Fig. 3H), we stimulated cancer cells with low-dose IFN-γ to mimic inflammatory conditions in the tumor microenvironment. qPCR analysis confirmed that USP7 knockdown significantly elevated the mRNA expression of CXCL9, CXCL10 and CXCL11 upon IFN-γ stimulation in SW480 cells (Fig. 6B), the same result was observed in SW1116 cells (Figure S8A). Subsequently, ELISA results confirmed that USP7 knockdown combined with IFN-γ stimulation significantly increased the protein secretion of CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 of SW480 and SW1116 cells (Fig. 6C, Figure S8B). To explore the underlying mechanism, we performed RNA-seq analysis of USP7-knockdown and control SW480 cells (Fig. 6D). Gene Ontology (GO) and pathway enrichment analyses revealed significant enrichment of interferon response pathway and activation of T cell-mediated immune pathway in USP7-knockdown cells (Fig. 6E-F), consistent with both experimental phenotypes and bioinformatic predictions.

We further evaluated the effect of USP7 knockdown on immune cell recruitment and function using in vitro co-culture systems. CD8⁺ T cells were isolated from healthy donor Peripheral blood mononuclear cell(PBMCs) by positive selection and activated by CD3/CD28/CD2 antibodies and IL-2 [28] (Fig. 6G). Tumor cells were subjected to either genetic knockdown (shUSP7#2) or pharmacologic inhibition (P5091) to model USP7 targeting. Transwell migration assays showed that USP7 knockdown or P5091 inhibition significantly enhanced the recruitment of CD8⁺ T cells by MSS CRC cells (Fig. 6H, Figure S8E). Flow cytometry further revealed that CD8+ T cells co-cultured with USP7-deficient tumor cells displayed increased production of IFN-γ, granzyme B (GZMB), and TNF-α (Fig. 6I-J; Figure S8C–D, H–I), indicating enhanced cytotoxic function. In T cell-mediated tumor cell-killing assay, USP7 knockdown or inhibition also markedly increased T cell-mediated killing of tumor cells (Fig. 6K-L, Figure S8F-G). In summary, these results demonstrate that knockdown or inhibition of USP7 in MSS CRC cells promotes the secretion of chemokines, especially CXCL9/10/11, thereby enhancing the recruitment, activation, and cytotoxicity of CD 8+ T cells.

Targeting USP7 enhances the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy in MSS CRC

Based on the above findings that USP7 loss enhances chemokine secretion and CD8+ T cells recruitment and cytotoxicity, we next assessed whether targeting USP7 could convert MSS CRC from an immunologically “cold” to “hot” tumor and thereby sensitize it to anti–PD-1 therapy. We established a USP7-knockdown CT26 mouse MSS CRC cell line and subcutaneously inoculated mice with either control or knockdown cells, followed by anti–PD-1 antibody treatment (Fig. 7A). Results showed that both USP7 knockdown and anti–PD-1 monotherapy significantly suppressed tumor growth, while the combination therapy exhibited the strongest synergistic anti-tumor effect (Fig. 7B-D), indicating that USP7 knockdown enhances the efficacy of anti–PD-1 therapy in MSS CRC. No significant differences in body weight were observed among the groups (Fig. 7E). In survival experiment, the combination group showed the longest survival (Fig. 7F-G).

To investigate immunological mechanisms, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes were analyzed by flow cytometry. To evaluate the clinical translational potential of targeting USP7, we further tested the USP7 inhibitor P5091 in combination with anti–PD-1 therapy in the CT26 model (Fig. 8A). Mirroring the genetic knockdown results, P5091 combined with anti–PD-1 significantly inhibited tumor growth and extended survival without affecting body weight (Fig. 8B–G). Flow cytometry confirmed that the combination group had highest proportions of CD8⁺ T cells, an increased CD8⁺/CD4⁺ T cell ratio, and more NK cell infiltration in the TME (Fig. 8H-I, K-M). Further analysis revealed that P5091 primarily drove the increase in the CD11b⁺CD27⁻ NK cell subset (Figure S9B-C). IHC staining corroborated that the combination group had the highest infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells and NK cells, along with elevated expression of GZMB and IFN-γ (Fig. 8J, N; Figure S9D-I), suggesting enhanced immune cell infiltration and function.

In peripheral immune, spleen morphology showed no significant differences across groups (Figure S10A-B). Flow cytometry of splenic immune cells indicated that both P5091 and anti–PD-1 monotherapy increased the CD8⁺/CD4⁺ T cell ratio, with the combination being most effective (Figure S10D, G). Furthermore, splenic NK cell proportions were significantly increased (Figure S10E, H), accompanied by a shift in NK subsets from CD11b⁺CD27⁻ to CD11b⁺CD27⁺ (Figure S10F, I-J), indicating that P5091 systemically modulates immune cell composition and functional differentiation in peripheral organs. We also validated the effect of P5091 in the MSI-H CRC model MC38, where it inhibited tumor growth and enhanced immune cell infiltration, though to a lesser extent than in the MSS model (Figure S9J–N).

In conclusion, both genetic knockdown and pharmacological inhibition of USP7 synergize with anti–PD-1 therapy to effectively remodel the tumor immune microenvironment, converting immunologically “cold” MSS CRC into “hot” tumors, providing strong preclinical evidence for future clinical combinations.

USP7 and immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) response

Given that high USP7 expression contributes to an immune-desert microenvironment in MSS CRC, we hypothesized that reduced USP7 expression may be associated with improved response to ICIs. To preliminarily assess the clinical relevance of USP7 expression in ICB response, we analyzed a small cohort of 10 patients with advanced MSS CRC who received anti-PD-1 therapy. No-responder includes patients with both progressive disease (PD) and stable disease (SD) according to RECIST 1.1 criteria. Detailed information of this cohort was listed in Table S6. Pre-treatment tumor samples obtained via biopsy or surgery were subjected to USP7 IHC staining.

Representative IHC images (Fig. 9A) and quantitative analysis (Fig. 9B) revealed that non-responders to anti-PD-1 therapy exhibited significantly higher USP7 expression, characterized by stronger staining intensity and higher positivity rates, whereas responders showed lower USP7 expression levels. Patients with low USP7 expression also had extended survival time (Fig. 9C). This result is consistent with our preclinical findings that USP7 overexpression suppresses anti-tumor immunity, suggesting that low USP7 expression may predict better response to ICIs.

Methods

Data acquisition and processing

1900 CRC patients (1577 MSS patients and 323 MSI-H patients) from 10 independent cohorts were enrolled in this study: Colon and rectal adenocarcinoma (COAD&READ) from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). GSE39582, GSE37892, GSE35896, GSE26682, GSE13067, GSE13294, GSE18088, GSE75316, GSE39084 from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Detailed information of these cohorts was showed in Table S3. Among them, three datasets (TCGA, GSE39582, GSE37892) contained the follow-up information.

The RNA-seq raw read count from the TCGA database was converted to transcripts per kilobase million (TPM). Data from the GEO database was all retrieved from the Affymetrix GPL570 platform (Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array). R package “affy” was used to conduct combination of CEL files, RMA background correction and data normalization [29]. The ‘sva’ R package was used to remove batch effects for Pooled cohort 1 (GSE13067, GSE13294, GSE18088, GSE75316), and Pooled cohort 2 (GSE26682, GSE35896, GSE39084) [30]. Principal components analysis (PCA) was used to detect the results by ‘prcomp’ R function.

Calculation of immune cell infiltration in tumor microenvironment

The single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was conducted to calculate infiltrated immune cells in the MSS tumor microenvironment by “GSVA” package [31]. The gene set for each immune cell was obtained from the previous study [32]. Seven other algorithms including CIBERSORT, TIMER, xCell, MCP-counter, quanTIseq, EPIC and TIP were further performed to verify the stability and robustness of the ssGSEA results. Among these algorithms, TIMER, xCell, MCP-counter,, EPIC and quanTIseq algorithms were performed using “deconvolute” function of the “immunedeconv”R package. For CIBERSORT, LM22 was downloaded, an annotated gene set, to define the 22 immune cell subtypes from the CIBERSORT web portal (http://cibersort.stanford.edu/). For TIP, RNA-seq matrix were uploaded to infer the proportion of various tumor-infiltrating immune cells (http://biocc.hrbmu.edu.cn/TIP/).

Other immunological characteristics of tumor microenvironment

The ESTIMATE algorithm was employed to infer the immune score, stroma score and estimate score of tumors using the "immunedeconv" R package. The TIDE algorithm was utilized to evaluate the response probability of individuals to immunotherapy by uploading gene matrix to the website (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/). Patients with lower TIDE scores exhibited superior responses. T cell-inflamed gene expression profile (GEP) was a robust predictive biomarker for PD-1 treatment which was used in our study [33]. Seven steps of cancer immune cycles were determined by the web-based analytical platform (http://biocc.hrbmu.edu.cn/TIP/). The immune related signatures were obtained from Thorsson V’s work [34]. MHC receptors, chemokines, immunostimulators and interleukins were identified based on previous studies [32].

Classification and characterization of DUB subtypes

The “ConsensusClusterPlus” R package was used for subtype identification based on the expression matrix [35]. Five DUBs with strongest correlations were incorporated to perform unsupervised clustering in the TCGA cohort. The k-means clustering method was used with 500 iterations. The optimal k was evaluated using the PAC algorithm. PCA plot was used to detect differences in expression between clusters. The remaining four cohorts were also used to recapitulate the two subtypes after applying the same algorithm.

Somatic variants analysis

Somatic mutation data were downloaded from TCGA. The "maftools" package was employed for the analysis of somatic variants. The mutation landscape of IR-DUBs was demonstrated between MSS and MSI-H CRC samples. Subsequently, the "mafCompare" function was utilized for the identification of significantly mutated DUBs between MSS and MSI-H samples. The overall mutation status was illustrated in USP7 high- and low-expression groups.

Single cell analysis

10 × UMI count data of GSE132465 was download from GEO, the count matrixes were converted into Seurat object by “Seurat” R package(version 4.4.0). Cells were regarded as low-quality cells with gene numbers less than 300, mitochondrion-derived UMI counts over 20%, erythrocyte-derived UMI count over 5% and filtered out these cells. Then the count matrixes were normalized using “NormalizeData” function and scaled using “ScaleData” function. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using “RunPCA” function and ran t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (tSNE) based on “pca” reduction using “RunTSNE” function. Finally, the main cells clusters were identified using “FindNeighbors” and “FindClusters” function (res = 1.0) and visualized the cells clusters based on tSNE plot. Clusters were annotated using the annotation files given in the GEO portal.

Cell chat analysis

The “CellChat” R package(version 4.4.0) was used to perform cell chat analysis [36]. According to the official workflow, the normalized expression data and cell group information into CellChat. Receptor-ligand interactions were screened using the “CellChatDB.human” database as a reference.

“computeCommunProb”,“filterCommunication”,“computeCommunProbPathway” and “aggregateNet” functions were used to figure out the potential ligand-receptor interactions between high/low USP7 expression subgroups. All parameters of the functions used above were set to default.

Functional analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes, Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) were performed on differently expressed genes between USP7 high and low group using R package “clusterProfiler”. The adjusted p-value < 0.05 was used to identify significant GO, GSEA and KEGG terms. Gene Set Variant Analysis (GSVA) was conducted on DUB subgroups using R package “GSVA” [31]. The gene set “c2.all.v2023.2.Hs.symbols.gmt” downloaded from the Molecular Signatures Database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/) was chosen as the reference gene set of GSEA and GSVA.

Cell culture, transfection, virus infection

CRC cell lines (SW480, SW1116, CT26 and MC38) were obtained from the cell bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. SW480 and CT26 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium. SW1116 and MC38 cells were cultured in DMEM medium. All media contained 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S), and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. shUSP7 and negative control plasmids were purchased from MiaoLing Plasmid Platform (Cat: P48326, P65930 and P54187). For transient transfection, cells were transfected with plasmids using Lipofectamin 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat:11,668,019). For stable expression, the lentiviral vector containing mouse USP7 shRNA (5’-CCTGCAATGTTAGATAATGAA-3’), together with the packing plasmids were transfected into CT26 cells, Cells were subjected to puromycin selection before the experiments were performed.

RNA-sequencing and qRT–PCR

Total RNA was extracted from USP7 knockdown or negative control SW480 cells and subsequently sent for RNA-sequencing, which was carried out by Honsunbio Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). For qRT–PCR, total RNA was extracted using the RNA extraction kit (Cat: 15596026CN, EZB), and cDNA was synthesized with SuperScript II Reverse Transcriptase (Cat: R222-01, Vazyme). Relative quantification of RNA levels was conducted using the SYBR Green Master Mix Kit (Cat: Q341-02, Vazyme) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The CT values were normalized to GAPDH, and the relative mRNA expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. All gene primers used are listed in Table S7.

Western blot

The western blot was performed according to standard protocol. Briefly, after collecting protein lysates in 2% SDS, concentration of each sample was measured by BCA method. Protein lysate was further heated with 5 × Native Gel Sample Loading Buffer (Cat: WB3002, New Cell Molecular Biotech) at 100℃ for 20 min. Afterwards, they were separated on the 7.5% SDS-PAGE gel (Cat: P2012, New Cell Molecular Biotech). Next, the proteins were transferred to the NC membrane, the membrane was blocked and then treated with USP7 antibody (Cat: ab264422, Abcam) and target protein bands were detected by an enhanced chemiluminescence system (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Chalfont).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF)

The TMA were prepared according to the method reported by Matthew et al. [37]. and IHC was performed previously described [38]. Primary anti-USP7 antibodies (Abcam, Cat: ab264422; Proteintech, Cat: 66,514–1-Ig) was used for IHC staining. The positive area was determined by ImageJ software (https://imagej.nih.gov/). Multiplex IF of the TMA was performed previously described [39]. Primary antibodies were as follows: anti-CD3 (Zuochengbio, Cat: ZA0053, 1:100 dilution), anti-CD8 (Zuochengbio, Cat: ZA0055, 1:100 dilution), anti-CD56 (Zuochengbio, Cat: ZA0272, 1:100 dilution), anti-GZMB (Zuochengbio, Cat: ZA0485, 1:100 dilution). Each slide was scanned with the PANNORAMIC MIDI (3DHISTECH). Multispectral images were evaluated using Visiopharm analysis platform (VISIOPHARM, Denmark). Individual cells were identified using the DAPI nucleus staining. CD8+ T cell: CD3(+) CD8(+), NK cell: CD3(-) CD56(+) were quantified and their percentages were calculated. Functions of CD8+ T cells and NK cells were evaluated by GZMB.

Cytokines and chemokines detection in cell culture supernatants

Cell culture supernatants were collected from 6-well plates and centrifuged. The levels of 29 cytokines and chemokines—including CCL2, CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, CCL7, CCL8, CCL11, CCL20, CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-7, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12P40, IL-12P70, IL-15, IL-17α, IL-18, IL-31, TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IFN-α—were simultaneously quantified using the XMplex Human 29-Plex Custom Panel kit (XMBIOTECH Biotechnology, Cat: XMplex01029) and the XMplex-01033 detection platform (XMBIOTECH Biotechnology) following the manufacturer’s protocols. Fluorescence intensity data were log₂-transformed and scaled, and the results are presented as a heatmap.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Cell culture supernatants were collected from 6-well plates and centrifuged. The protein levels of CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 were quantified using human ELISA kits (Elabscience; Cat: E-EL-H6062, E-EL-H0050, E-EL-H0051) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and the concentrations of each chemokine were calculated based on the corresponding standard curves derived from the optical density (OD) values.

CD8+ T cell migration assay

Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from healthy donors' blood samples using Lymphoprep™ (StemCell Technologies, Cat: 18,061) density gradient centrifugation. All donors provided written informed consent prior to participation. CD8⁺ T cells were subsequently purified from PBMCs through magnetic bead-based positive selection with the Human CD8⁺ T Cell Isolation Kit (StemCell Technologies, Cat: 17,953). The isolated CD8⁺ T cells were maintained in ImmunoCult™-XF T Cell Expansion Medium (StemCell Technologies, Cat: 10,981) and activated using ImmunoCult™ Human CD3/CD28/CD2 T Cell Activator (StemCell Technologies, Cat: 10,970) in combination with recombinant human IL-2. To evaluate T cell migration, a 24-well transwell system (Corning) with 6.5 mm diameter inserts and 3.0 μm pore polycarbonate membranes was employed. SW480 and SW1116 cell lines—either transfected, pretreated with P5091 (10 μM, Cat: T6925, Topscience), or negative control variants—were placed in the lower chamber, while the activated CD8⁺ T cells were seeded in the upper chamber. After 6 h of incubation, migrated T cells in the lower chamber were collected and quantified. This study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji University.

T cell–mediated tumor cell–killing assay

PBMCs were isolated from healthy donors following a standard protocol, with written informed consent obtained from each individual prior to enrollment. The PBMCs were cultured in ImmunoCult™-XF T Cell Expansion Medium and activated for one week using ImmunoCult™ Human CD3/CD28/CD2 T Cell Activator along with recombinant human IL-2 to generate activated T cells. Meanwhile, SW480 and SW1116 cell lines—either transfected, pretreated with P5091, or serving as negative controls—were seeded into 24-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. These tumor cells were then co-cultured with the activated T cells for 12 h. After co-culture, non-adherent and dead cells were removed by washing with PBS. The remaining adherent cancer cells were stained with crystal violet, and cell viability was quantified by measuring the optical density (OD) at 570 nm using a microplate reader.

In vivo experiments

BALB/c and C57BL/6 mice (4–6 weeks) were randomly divided into each group (n = 7 per group). Approximately 1*106 CT26 cells were subcutaneously injected into the flank of BALB/c mice (5*105 MC38 cells into the C57BL/6 mice). After the diameter of tumor reaches 5 mm, mice were treated with solvents, P5091 or anti-mouse PD-1 mab (100 μg/mouse/3d, Bioxcell, Cat:BE0146) by intraperitoneal injection. Mice body weights and tumor volumes were measured every other day. The mice were euthanized and their tumors were collected after the last treatment cycle or on the day of tumor volume ≥ 2000 mm3, tumor diameter ≥ 2 cm, or on appearance of tumor ulceration. The tumors were harvested, weighed, photographed, and subsequently divided into two portions. One portion was fixed with 4% formaldehyde for immunohistochemical analysis, while the other was dissociated for flow cytometry analysis.

Flow cytometry analysis

Mice were anesthetized and perfused with ice-cold PBS to clear blood components. Subcutaneous tumors were then excised and dissociated into single-cell suspensions using a Tumor Dissociation Kit (RWD Life Science, Cat: DHTE-5001) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Splenocytes were isolated by mechanically dissociating the spleen using a 5 mL syringe plunger against a 70 μm cell strainer in RPMI-1640 medium. Erythrocytes were lysed with RBC lysis buffer (Beyotime, Cat: C3702). Co-cultured Human CD8+ T cells were collected and washed by PBS containing 2% FBS. Single-cell suspensions were stained with the following fluorescently conjugated antibodies: PE/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD45 antibody (BioLegend, Cat:103,107), FITC anti-mouse CD3 antibody (BioLegend, Cat:100,227), APC anti-mouse CD4 antibody (BioLegend, Cat:100,412), PE anti-mouse CD8a antibody (BioLegend, Cat:100,708), PE anti-mouse NKp46 Antibody (BioLegend, Cat:137,604), Brilliant Violet 421™ anti-mouse CD27 Antibody (BioLegend, Cat:124,223), Alexa Fluor® 647 anti-mouse CD11b Antibody (BioLegend, Cat:101,220). Surface staining was performed by incubating the cell-antibody mixture for 1 h at 37 °C. A transcription factor Staining Buffer Set (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat:00–5523) was used to fix and permeabilize cells. Intracellular markers were stained for 50 min with APC anti-human/mouse GZMB antibody (BioLegend, Cat: 372,204), APC anti-mouse IFN-γ antibody (BioLegend, Cat: 505,810), APC anti-mouse TNF-α antibody (BioLegend, Cat: 506,308), APC anti-human IFN-γ antibody (BioLegend, Cat: 502,512), APC anti-human TNF-α antibody (BioLegend, Cat: 502,913). After staining, cells were washed with PBS, resuspended in PBS containing 2% FBS, and analyzed on a BD Fortessa flow cytometer. Data processing was performed using FlowJo software (Version 10.8.1).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.0). Correlations between variables were assessed using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation coefficients, depending on the distribution of the data. For normally distributed data, group comparisons were conducted using Student’s t-test; non-normally distributed data were analyzed with the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-squared test. Kaplan–Meier analysis was performed to plot survival curves, and the statistical significance of differences between groups was determined using the log-rank test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels are denoted as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Discussion

The relatively immune-quiescent tumor microenvironment in MSS CRC contributes to its ineffective response to immunotherapy. This has prompted the exploration of novel combination treatment strategies. In this study, we identified a subset of deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs)—designated immune-related DUBs (IR-DUBs)—that are strongly correlated with the immune landscape of MSS CRC, as determined through multiple immune deconvolution algorithms applied across various CRC cohorts (Fig. 1). Using these IR-DUBs, we classified MSS CRC into two subtypes: DUB-H and DUB-L. Patients in the DUB-L group exhibited a more immunologically active tumor microenvironment, increased potential responsiveness to immunotherapy, and longer RFS compared to those in the DUB-H group (Fig. 2).Among these IR-DUBs, USP7 was selected for further investigation due to its distinct expression pattern—showing significant upregulation in MSS tumors but not in MSI-H counterparts—and its strong negative correlation with immune infiltration. Large-scale multi-cohort analysis confirmed that USP7 expression is closely associated with microsatellite status and consensus molecular subtypes (CMS) in CRC (Figs. 3 and 4). Single-cell transcriptomics and multiplex immunohistochemistry further validated that high USP7 expression correlates with an immune-desert phenotype in MSS CRC (Fig. 5). Mechanistically, inhibition of USP7 in MSS CRC cell lines enhanced the secretion of T-cell-recruiting chemokines, particularly CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11, leading to increased recruitment and activation of CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 6). In vivo, USP7 knockdown or pharmacological inhibition synergized with anti–PD-1 therapy in a CT26 mouse model, promoting CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and enhancing their cytotoxic function (Figs. 7 and 8). Importantly, low USP7 expression was associated with improved response to anti–PD-1 therapy in a clinical cohort of MSS CRC patients (Fig. 9). Collectively, these results identify USP7 as a key regulator of immune evasion in MSS CRC and support its therapeutic targeting as a promising strategy for enhancing immunotherapy efficacy in this immunologically cold tumor subtype.

Although it is widely accepted that MSS CRC patients are unlikely to respond to immunotherapy, a subset of MSS patients may still benefit from immunotherapy [6]. Researchers are increasingly focused on identifying potential immunotherapy responders among MSS CRC patients. A recent study demonstrated that MSS patients with or without chromosome 20q amplification represent two distinct subtypes of MSS CRC, characterized by different mutation profiles and immune cell infiltrations [40]. Furthermore, POLE/POLD1 mutations, which are mutually exclusive with MSI mutations, are frequently found in MSS CRC patients. Several studies have shown that pembrolizumab exhibits promising efficacy in MSS metastatic CRC with POLE/POLD1 mutations [41, 42]. According to the Consensus Molecular Subtypes, tumors classified as CMS1 and CMS4 exhibit heightened immune cell infiltration and may benefit from immunotherapy. However, their TIME characteristics differ: CMS1 tumors displayed an immune-activated state, while CMS4 tumors exhibited an immune-suppressed state. A clinical trial study combining BRAF and EGFR inhibitors with nivolumab as the treatment for CMS1 MSS CRC patient with a BRAF-V600E mutation resulted in an enhanced PD-1 antibody response rate [43], indicating that ICI combination therapy may be more effective in MSS CRC patients with the CMS1 subtype. Notably, only 16% of CRC patients with high TMB are classified as MSI-H, while the remaining 84% are MSS. This suggests that MSS CRC patients with high TMB may also benefit from ICI treatment [44, 45].

However, research study identifying the molecular subtypes of MSS CRC remains limited. In our study, MSS CRC samples were categorized into two classes with differing immune features based on IR-DUBs. The DUB-L class exhibited significant enrichment of immune-related signatures, including immune score, T-cell inflamed GEP, TIDE score, CD8+ T cells and NK cells infiltration. In DUB-L MSS CRC tumors, immune response pathways such as CTL, TCYTOTOXIC and MHC pathways were activated, whereas DUB-H MSS CRC displayed activation of signaling pathways involved in tumorigenesis and angiogenesis, correlating with significantly inferior recurrence-free survival rate. Our findings confirmed the existence of “hot” immune microenvironment in MSS CRC, which is relevant for improved ICIs response.

The reversible process of ubiquitination and de-ubiquitination, regulated by ubiquitin E3 ligases and DUBs, plays a crucial role in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment. Recent studies have shown that the dysregulation of DUBs can lead to the immune evasion or suppression within tumors [46]. For example, Xiao et al. reported that USP5 inhibition in T cells increased effector cytokine production and reduced tumor growth in mice. Additionally, USP5 inhibition combined with anti-PD-1/CTLA-4 therapy demonstrated additive tumor-suppressive effect in mice [47]. Xiong et al. found that USP8 inhibition increased PD-L1 protein levels by enhancing its ubiquitination. The combination of USP8 inhibitor with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade significantly activated infiltrating CD8+ T cells, suppressing tumor growth and improving survival in murine tumor models [48]. Furthermore, Tian et al. discovered that FBXO38 interacts with and ubiquitylates FGL1. Depletion of FBXO38 markedly increased FGL1 abundance, enhancing CD8+ T cell infiltrations in non-small cell lung cancer [49]. While these studies underscore the broad importance of ubiquitination dynamics in immune regulation, our work specifically delineates the central role of USP7 in establishing the non-inflamed phenotype characteristic of MSS CRC, a context where effective therapeutic strategies are urgently needed. Furthermore, unlike USP8 which upregulates PD-L1, USP7's mechanism appears to primarily involve suppressing T-cell chemotaxis, revealing a distinct mode of action.

USP7 has been shown to be overexpressed in several cancer types including CRC [50], aligning with our research findings. As the first identified p53-interacting protein, USP7 stabilizes p53 through direct deubiquitylation, inducing p53-dependent tumor suppression and apoptosis [51]. Recent studies highlighted USP7’s role in regulating the stability of intracellular proteins involved in the suppression of anti-tumor immune responses. For instance, Dai et.al demonstrated that USP7 is a crucial and highly expressed gene in the maintenance of M2 macrophages. USP7 inhibition caused phenotypic and functional changes in M2 macrophages, promoting the proliferation of IFN-γ-expressing CD8+ T in vitro [52]. Wang et al. found that USP7 directly interacts with PD-L1, and its abrogation reduced PD-L1/PD-1 interactions, sensitizing cancer cells to T-cell medicated killing both in vitro and in vivo [53]. The comprehensive roles of USP7 in the TIME and immunotherapy of MSS CRC remain to be elucidated.

We selected USP7 from the classifier for in-depth investigation based on the following compelling reasons. First and foremost, USP7 exhibited the most significant differential mutation frequency between MSI-H and MSS CRC (16% vs 3%, P = 5.29e−04) (Fig. 1E). It was specifically upregulated in MSS tumors, with no notable expression difference between MSI-H and adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 3C–F), highlighting its subtype-specific role. Second, USP7 demonstrated a robust and highly consistent negative correlation with antitumor immunity across all cohorts. USP7 showed strongest associations with impaired immune cell infiltration (CD8⁺ T cells, NK cells), reduced immune scores, and suppressed immune signatures (e.g., MHC, IFN-γ, T cell-inflamed GEP) (Fig. 3A–B, I–J), suggesting a central role in mediating immune exclusion in MSS CRC. Third, USP7 exhibited strong clinical relevance. Low USP7 expression was significantly associated with improved overall survival in MSS CRC patients (Fig. 4G), supporting its utility as a prognostic biomarker and reinforcing its biological importance. We also considered the functional clarity and context specificity of USP7 relative to other candidates. For instance, while USP36 is known to promote proliferation across cancers by stabilizing oncoproteins, its role in immune regulation remains poorly documented [54]. Similarly, USP10 has been reported to exhibit both immune-suppressive and immune-favorable roles depending on cancer type—such as degrading B7-H4 to enhance antibody efficacy in breast cancer, while correlating positively with CD8⁺ T cells and dendritic cells in pancreatic, lung, and liver cancers [55, 56]. This functional ambiguity contrasts with the clear and consistent immune-inhibitory profile of USP7 observed in our CRC cohorts. Therefore, based on its distinct mutation profile, consistent immune-suppressive correlations and clinical prognostic value, we identified USP7 as the most compelling candidate for further functional validation.

Given the challenges posed by the immunosuppressive microenvironment nature of MSS CRC, to achieve meaningful clinical benefits through the use of a single ICI has proven difficult. Consequently, researchers had explored combination therapies. A phase II clinical trial showed that the administration of anti-PD-L1 and anti-CTLA-4 inhibitors in refractory metastatic CRC (mCRC) patients (92% MSS) resulted in a median overall survival extension of 2 months compared to optimal supportive therapy. However, no improvement was observed in progression-free survival (PFS) or overall response rate (ORR), with the latter reaching only 1% [7]. VEGF/VEGFR inhibitors show promise in reducing tumor angiogenesis and promoting normalization, which could enhance the infiltration of effective T cells while decreasing the infiltration of immune suppressive cells such as M2 macrophages and Tregs [57]. The combination of multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) that primarily inhibit VEGFR with PD-1 antibody represents one of the most promising combination therapies under investigation. The REGONIVO study reported that the combination of nivolumab with low-dose regorafenib markedly increased ORR in MSS mCRC to 33.3% [8]. Various TKIs and PD-1/PD-L1 monoclonal antibody combination regimens have since been adopted as subsequent lines of mCRC treatment [58–60]. Our study employed single-cell analysis and multiplex immunofluorescence, revealing that high USP7 expression in MSS CRC negatively correlates with immune cell infiltration (Fig. 5). Inflamed tumors, characterized by CD4+ and CD8+ T cell infiltration, exhibited a greater responsiveness to immunotherapy. We found genetic knockdown or pharmacological inhibition of USP7 in MSS CRC cells promotes the secretion of chemokines, especially CXCL9/10/11, thereby enhancing the recruitment, activation, and cytotoxicity of CD 8+ T cells (Fig. 6). This suggests that inhibiting USP7 may serve as a viable strategy for combination therapy with ICIs in the treatment of MSS CRC. Furthermore, we explored whether USP7 inhibition could enhance the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy. Combining USP7 blockade with anti-PD-1 therapy not only improves tumor suppression but also fosters a more robust immune response compared to monotherapy (Figs. 7 and 8), offering a promising avenue for future therapeutic interventions in MSS CRC patients.

There are some limitations in our research. First, the predictive value of our DUB classifier and USP7 was established retrospectively. Prospective validation in large, multi-center trials is essential to confirm its utility in stratifying patients for immunotherapy. Second, the functional mechanisms by which USP7 regulates chemokine expression, particularly the CXCL9/10/11 axis, were not fully elucidated, future studies are needed to identify the direct targets of USP7 that mediate its immune-recruitment function in MSS CRC. Third, although we demonstrated compelling efficacy of USP7 inhibition in combination with anti–PD-1 in syngeneic mouse models, the translational potential of this strategy requires further evaluation. Given the reported roles of USP7 in neurodevelopment and hematopoiesis [61–63], detailed toxicology and pharmacokinetic studies are essential prior to clinical application to determine the safety profile and identify the applicable patient population. Finally, the preliminary clinical correlation between low USP7 expression and improved anti-PD-1 response, while encouraging, is based on a very small sample size. This observation requires confirmation in a larger cohort with standardized treatment to draw definitive conclusions about its utility as a clinical biomarker.

In summary, we established a molecular typing based on immune-related DUBs, effectively distinguishing MSS CRC into two subgroups with distinctive immune statuses and survival prognoses. Notably, USP7 plays a significant role in shaping the immunosuppressive microenvironment of MSS CRC. Targeting USP7 has the potential to convert MSS-CRC from “cold” tumor to “hot” tumor, thereby improving the efficacy of immunotherapy.
