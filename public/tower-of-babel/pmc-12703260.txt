Lung cancer risk assessment by prediction model: a global perspective
Authors: Longyao Zhang, Xiang Wang, Qiuyuan Chen, Mengsheng Zhao, Can Ju, David C Christiani, Feng Chen, Ruyang Zhang, Yongyue Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNumerous lung cancer risk prediction models have been developed and validated worldwide. It is imperative to offer a comprehensive overview and comparative analysis of their performances.MethodsWe conducted an extensive literature search to identify studies developing and/or validating lung cancer risk prediction models. Then we summarised and compared the external performance of these models, focusing on discriminative accuracy (C-index) and calibration performance (E:O ratio).ResultsAfter an initial screening of 10 210 articles, 35 studies on 21 distinct prediction models were identified, which used 42 different types of predictors spanning seven categories. Notable performance variations were observed in external validations. In North American cohorts, the C-index ranged from 0.60 to 0.87, with E:O ratios from 0.62 to 3.70. Among the European cohorts, the Trøndelag health study HUNT and CanPredict exhibited C-indices surpassing 0.870. Conversely, the Bach, lung cancer risk assessment tool (LCRAT), prostate, lung, colorectal and ovarian cancer screening (PLCO)m2012 and PLCOall2014 performed poorly in electronic health records of the Qresearch database subgroup, with C-indices falling below 0.60. PLCOm2012 reached the best E:O ratio of 1.00 (95% CI: 0.93 to 1.08) in the UK Biobank subgroup. In Asian cohorts, the C-index ranged from 0.54 to 0.87. Only three models, Korean Men, LCRAT and Liverpool lung project incidence risk model (LLPi), achieved a C-index exceeding 0.80. LCRAT demonstrated the best calibration, while Hoggart performed the worst.ConclusionsPerformance of lung cancer risk prediction models, despite being well developed and validated, varies in diverse populations. Significant regional imbalance persists in the development of these models. Rigorous external validation or recalibration study in the target population is crucial in accordance with the guidance prior to model implementation.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42022324602.Keywords: Lung Cancer

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

This study demonstrated that, even for the lung cancer risk prediction models that are well developed and rigorously validated, their performance tends to diverge across diverse populations during validation. Introduction

Lung cancer is a leading worldwide cancer, accounting for 11.4% of the total cancer incidence and 18.0% of cancer-related deaths.1 The US National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) demonstrated that screening high-risk individuals with low-dose CT (LDCT) reduced lung cancer mortality by 20%.2 The US Preventive Services Task Force 2021 (USPSTF2021) recommends annual screening for lung cancer with LDCT for adults aged 50–80 years with a smoking history ≥20 pack-years, meanwhile current smoking individuals or former smoking individuals who ceased smoking within ≤15 years.3 Despite the expanded screening eligibility, these criteria may not fully identify all high-risk individuals. Growing evidence suggests that using individually calculated risk scores from lung cancer risk prediction models might be a more effective approach for screening selection.4

In the past two decades, numerous multivariable lung cancer risk prediction models have been successfully developed and validated globally.5 For reliability and generalisability, models ought to be implemented in wider populations that differ from development samples. Model performance in terms of calibration and discrimination may vary according to modelling methodology, risk factors included or population applied to. Hence, conducting systematic reviews to extract and evaluate model performance across multiple studies is crucial for comprehensive assessment.

Therefore, we conducted a systematic review with two aims. First, to offer a concise and comprehensive overview of lung cancer risk prediction models globally that had been externally validated. Second, to understand the performance of these models across diverse populations, delving into their potential for future application and calibration.

Methods

We designed this systematic review in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic review and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement.6 A protocol for this study was published with the PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews), under registration number CRD42022324602.

Search strategy and selection criteria

The literature was systematically searched through PubMed, Embase and Web of Science from database inception to 15 November 2023. The combination of the following key search terms was used for each database: “lung cancer”, “risk”, “prediction”, “model” and “valid”. The full search strategy is available in online supplemental file 1. Meanwhile, a manual search for reference lists of relevant articles was performed for further eligible studies.

Broad inclusion criteria for prediction modelling studies were used to include all possible models for predicting the risk of developing lung cancer in participants. All studies in the English language that met the following criteria were included: (1) studies designed to develop the new risk prediction models but without external validation in original studies; (2) studies developing and externally validating the same prediction models; (3) studies designed to develop the new risk prediction models and compare with the previously developed comparable models; (4) updating the previously developed models; (5) studies designed to validate the existing risk prediction models.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) newly developed models without external validation either in the original model development study or the following studies designed to validate the existing models; (2) models incorporating lung nodule characteristics extracted from LDCT images; (3) conference abstracts, letters to editors, reviews and study protocols, in addition to other non-original studies were excluded.

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted independently by two investigators (LZ, QC) and assessed by the other two investigators (MZ, CJ). Disagreements were resolved by consensus. From included articles, we extracted information on first author, publication year, derived or validated model name and validation data sets information. For all eligible models, we extracted information on the following domains: study information, model characteristics, model performance and validation. Full information on the extracted data was provided in table 1 and online supplemental file 1.

To evaluate the predictive performance, measures of discrimination and calibration were extracted from the original publication. Common measures of discrimination are the discrimination ability (C-index) and area under the curve (AUC) which typically ranges from 0.50 to 1.00. Calibration is usually assessed with calibration plots and the observed/expected ratio (E:O ratio). Detailed information was provided in the online supplemental file 1.

Quality assessment

For each prediction model, the prediction model risk of bias assessment tool (PROBAST) was used to assess risk of bias (ROB), or potential for inflated model performance estimates due to shortcomings in design/analysis.7 This tool evaluated ROB in four domains: participants, predictors, outcome and statistical analysis. Each domain was rated as high, low or unclear ROB. Overall ROB was judged as low if all domains were low risk, high if any domain was high risk and otherwise unclear risk.

Results

Model selection and characteristics

Search by PRISMA guidance yielded 10 210 studies, of which 296 were screened the full text, and 35 studies were included (figure 1). Of these, 21 studies were designed to develop lung cancer risk prediction models, of which 3 studies proposed new models without external validation, 5 studies developed and externally validated the proposed model, 6 studies upgraded the previously developed models and the other 7 studies developed new models while comparing with the existing ones. The other 14 studies validated these existing models independently (online supplemental file 1). Finally, 21 lung cancer risk prediction models with external validations were identified.

PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) flowchart of study inclusions and exclusions.

All 21 risk prediction models were applicable to ever smoking individuals; 13 of them were tailored for general population, including never smoking individuals. A total of 19 (90.48%) were developed in prospective cohorts and only 2 were trained from case-control studies. There were 9, 7 and 5 models for European, North American and Asian populations, respectively. All these models underwent internal validation, of which nine were tested in non-randomly split data sets, and the remaining models were tested in either randomly split data sets (n=3), or by cross-validation (n=4) or bootstrapping (n=5) approaches. Sample sizes of model development sets ranged from 1736 to 12 917 662, with lung cancer cases from 237 to 73 380.

Of note, 11 models were developed using Cox regression, 7 were by logistic regression, 1 was by Weibull regression; and the other two were developed by machine learning methods including eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and AutoPrognosis (table 1).

Evaluation for risk of bias

According to the PROBAST checklist, nine models had high overall ROB (figure 2), all of which were evaluated to have high ROB for the analysis domain. Details of domain evaluation are provided in online supplemental file 1.

PROBAST (Prediction Model Risk Of Bias Assessment Tool) risk of bias for all included models (n=21).

Predictors

The 21 prediction models included, aimed at general populations or specific subpopulations, were derived from 28 individual models (figure 3, online supplemental file 1). Numbers of predictors ranged from a minimal of 2 (Spitz model for never smoking individuals) to a maximum of 18 (OWL+ as an extension of optimized early warning model for lung cancer risk (OWL) model), with an interquartile of 6–11 predictors per model. Overall, 42 unique predictors fell into seven categories according to their definition or functionality: (1) cancer history; (2) demographics; (3) environmental exposures; (4) laboratory and functional measurements; (5) medical activity; (6) medical history and symptoms; and (7) smoking. Details were provided in online supplemental file 1.

External validation data sets of the models

All models in this study underwent external validation. Each included model reported discrimination in external validation sets and reported at least one AUC or C-index. The prostate, lung, colorectal and ovarian cancer screening (PLCO)m2012, Liverpool lung project V3 (LLPv3) and Bach models were the most frequently validated externally. Sample size in the external data sets ranged from 4 051 to 1 287 502 individuals, with lung cancer cases varying from 99 to 11 509. Regarding calibration, seven models had no E:O ratio reported in external validation studies. The maximum sample size for validation sets regarding E:O ratio evaluation varied from 25 653 to 3 69 650, with the number of lung cancer cases varying from 503 to 11 590 (online supplemental file 1).

Model performance in external validation

Stratified by populations

In the North American population, 13 models had C-index ranged from 0.60 to 0.87 in external validation. The PLCO family models, OWL, LCRAT and LLPv3 showed high C-index (>0.80) in the PLCO trial subgroup. However, the PLCO2011, PLCOm2012 and LLPv3 models performed poorly in the PLCO trial under different inclusion and exclusion criteria (C-index, 0.65–0.67). For calibration, models showed a clear difference in E:O ratio ranging from 0.62 to 3.70. The UCL-I and Bach models demonstrated satisfactory calibration in the PLCO Trial chest radiography arm (PLCO-CXR) and The National Institutes of Health and the American Association of Retired Persons data sets, with E:O ratios of 1.00 (95% CI: 0.92 to 1.07) and 1.00 (95% CI: 0.98 to 1.02), respectively (figures4 5, online supplemental file 1).

In the European population, 14 models had C-index values in the external validation that ranged from 0.53 to 0.89. The HUNT and CanPredict models demonstrated excellent C-index (>0.87) in the Cohort of Norway and Clinical Practice Research Datalink Gold database (CPRD GOLD), respectively. The Bach, LCRAT, PLCOm2012 and PLCOall2014 models performed poorly in electronic health records of the Qresearch database subgroup (C-index <0.6). For calibration, the PLCOm2012 model reached the best E:O ratio of 1.00 (95% CI: 0.93 to 1.08) in UK Biobank (UKB) current smoking individuals. Conversely, the Hoggart model showed overprediction in the Generations Study (E:O ratio, 16.10; 95% CI: 6.69 to 38.60) (figures4 5, online supplemental file 1).

In the Asian population, 14 models had C-index values from 0.54 to 0.87. Three models, including the Korean Men model in the Korean National Health Corporation enrolled from 1996 to 1997 data set, LCRAT and LLPi in the Korea National Health Insurance Service, achieved well performance (C-index >0.80). LLP (C-index, 0.56; 95% CI: 0.50 to 0.69) and LLPi (0.57; 95% CI: 0.50 to 0.71) models showed poor performance in Whole Life Cycle of Cancer Screening ever smoking individuals. For calibration, LCRAT had the best calibration in the China National Lung Cancer Screening programme ever smoker subgroup (E:O ratio, 1.01; 95% CI: 0.51 to 1.51); while the Hoggart model showed the worst calibration in this data set (0.41; 95% CI: 0.38 to 1.39) (figures4 5, online supplemental file 1).

Stratified by smoking status

For the general population, nine models had C-index values ranging from 0.66 to 0.89; of these, seven models, excluding Spitz and Bach models, had well performance (C-index >0.8). The CanPredict model showed notable C-index in the GOLD-Men subgroup (C-index, 0.878; 95% CI: 0.875 to 0.881). As for calibration, only the PLCOall2014, OWL and LLPv3 models reported E:O ratio in the general population, ranging from 0.66 to 1.33 (figures4 5, online supplemental file 1).

In ever smoking individuals, all models reported C-index values that varied from 0.54 to 0.84. OWL model showed outstanding discrimination with C-index of 0.84 (95% CI: 0.81 to 0.87) in UKB Scotland and Wales area (UKB-SW). Regarding the E:O ratio, 14 models were validated in external data sets yielding values from 0.41 to 16.10, with both the lowest and highest values attributed to the Hoggart model. Conversely, OWL, PLCOm2012, UCI-I, Bach, LCRAT, Pittsburgh Predictor and Spitz models exhibited good calibration ability in some external data sets (E:O ratio ranging between 0.80 and 1.20) (figures4 5, online supplemental file 1).

For never smoking individuals, six models were validated in this specific subpopulation. PLCOall2014, LLPv3 and OWL models showed robust C-index in the PLCO trial (C-index >0.80). OWL also demonstrated satisfactory calibration in UKB-SW (E:O ratio, 1.02; 95% CI: 0.85 to 1.27) (figures4 5, online supplemental file 1).

Discussion

This systematic review employed rigorous criteria to exclusively include externally validated prediction models. We provided an in-depth description of the selected models along with validation studies of these established models, summarising their performance across subgroups in external validation cohorts, to offer a precise assessment of their predictive capabilities.

Predictors incorporated in the models

Models incorporating easily obtainable predictors

Most included models leveraged easily obtainable predictors such as demographics, cancer history and smoking-related factors, which are key modifiable risk factors for lung cancer.8 These factors, like age and personal history of cancer, can be quickly gathered via interview or questionnaire and are commonly found in clinical practice and primary care records, facilitating their integration into prediction models.

Models incorporating lung function measurements

In this study, only two models incorporated laboratory and lung functional measurements. Forced expiratory volume in 1 second, which had the association with lung cancer incidence and mortality,9 only appeared in the OWL+ model. Muller et al developed and internally validated a prediction model incorporating spirometry data from a large UK prospective cohort, modestly enhancing discriminatory power.10 In addition, a study conducted in Canada with 2596 individuals having smoking exposure over 20 pack-years found that incorporating lung function improved model discrimination (AUC: 0.77, 95% CI: 0.73 to 0.81) over the base model (AUC: 0.72, 95% CI: 0.67 to 0.77) (comparison p=0.001) in internal validation.11 Assessing how lung function parameters enhance lung cancer risk prediction models is crucial.

Models incorporating biomarkers

Recent advancements in biomarker discovery have produced novel and effective means for personalised lung cancer risk assessment. Biomarkers involve identifying abnormal levels of specific molecules (eg, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), proteins or hormones) in blood, body fluids or tissues, signalling disease progression.12 While some biomarkers have been integrated into risk prediction models, most of these models lack external validation.

K Gould et al developed a machine learning model incorporating distinctive laboratory variables, including white blood cell, platelet count and red cell distribution width. This model exhibited an AUC of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.84 to 0.87), surpassing the PLCOm2012 model’s AUC of 0.79 (95% CI: 0.77 to 0.81) (comparison p value<0.001) in an internal validation set.13 This study underscored the potential of routine laboratory measures as biomarkers for lung cancer risk.

Routine clinical and laboratory data can be complemented with genetic variants in predictive models. Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have identified hundreds of SNPs linked to lung cancer across diverse ethnicities since 2008.14 Spitz et al enhanced their original model (Spitz) by incorporating DNA repair capacity and bleomycin sensitivity markers, boosting the AUC from 0.67 (95% CI: 0.63 to 0.71) to 0.70 (95% CI: 0.66 to 0.74) for former smoking individuals, and from 0.68 (95% CI: 0.64 to 0.72) to 0.73 (95% CI: 0.69 to 0.77) for current smoking individuals, respectively, in threefold cross-validation (comparison p=0.006, p=0.0048, respectively).15 Similarly, incorporating SNPs in seizure 6-like improved the LLP model’s AUC from 0.72 to 0.75 in internal validation (comparison p=0.01).16 In our study, OWL+, which includes genetic indicators, significantly improved lung cancer risk discrimination. A polygenic risk score (PRS) effectively quantifies the cumulative effect of multiple risk-associated variants. Dai et al have shown for the first time in a Chinese population that GWAS-derived PRS can identify subpopulations at high risk for lung cancer, beyond traditional predictors like smoking.17

In a proof-of-principle study, Guida et al showed that a four-marker protein panel (4MP) comprising cancer antigen 125, carcinoembryonic antigen, cytokeratin-19 fragment and the precursor form of surfactant protein B could improve lung cancer risk assessment, achieving an AUC of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.76 to 0.90) in internal validation.18 Fahrmann et al further showed that combining 4MP with PLCOm2012 yields further improved performance compared with PLCOm2012 alone and outperformed USPSTF2013 and USPSTF2021 criteria by increasing sensitivity by 11.9% and 9.9%, and specificity by 12.9% and 6.9%, respectively.19 Additionally, the 4MP validated its utility in predicting lung cancer-specific mortality risk and distinguishing benign from malignant indeterminate pulmonary nodules.20

Biomarker-based risk stratification can be independently implemented, offering ease and speed in interpreting a single blood test compared with compiling extensive patient information. However, lung cancer’s complex mechanisms suggest that combining biomarkers with other crucial risk factors may provide higher accuracy than relying solely on biomarkers.21 A biomarker-informed model usually yields moderate performance improvements over questionnaire-based models, hinting at undiscovered significant biomarkers for specific subpopulations and complex interactions between biomarkers and other factors. Thorough evaluation of the cost-effectiveness of such models is necessary.

Models incorporating environmental exposure predictors

Environmental exposure predictors have occupational-related, indoor and outdoor tobacco smoking exposure but lack air pollution predictors. Among the five models, one considered asbestos exposure, and another included the occupational hazardous substance exposure. Previous epidemiological studies have linked air pollution to lung cancer mortality,22 with PM pollution classified as a group 1 carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer.23 Huang et al revealed that high genetic risk combined with high air pollution exposure synergistically increased lung cancer risk, and 15–32% of lung cancer risk was attributed to the underlying interactions.24 These studies offered a fresh perspective on the possibility of incorporating a blend of air pollution and genetic factors as predictors.

Risk prediction model versus categorical screening strategies

Current lung cancer screening strategies mainly target individuals by age and smoking history.3 Despite proving superiority over current guidelines, prediction models are rarely recommended or used in screening programmes. The Targeted Lung Health Checks screening programme uses the PLCOm2012 and LLPv2 models to select high-risk participants undergoing LDCT screening. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines endorse the use of risk prediction models in the USA.25 Meza et al assessed the potential effects of screening with eligibility criteria based on three prediction models (PLCOm2012, LCRAT and Bach models), which indicated that risk model-based strategies led to higher numbers of deaths averted and overdiagnosed lung cancer cases and to lower numbers of screenings per person and radiation-related lung cancer deaths. Similarly, Ten Haaf et al demonstrated that risk model-based strategies reduced lung cancer mortality more effectively and efficiently compared with USPSTF2013 recommendations.26 Toumazis et al proved that the PLCOm2012 model yielded more quality-adjusted life-years and cost savings than USPSTF2021.27 However, these comparative studies overlook potential implementation challenges associated with risk model–based screening or whether such challenges may vary by setting or among different demographic groups. Moreover, current evidence is mainly retrospective. Ongoing implementation studies and trials are expected to provide further insights into the effectiveness and feasibility of risk model-based screening in clinical settings. Cost-effectiveness should also be evaluated by health economic modelling, which is currently under-represented in existing lung cancer risk prediction models.28

Populations covered by the model

The majority of the included models originate from Western hemisphere populations, particularly the USA and the UK, with the NLST and PLCO cohorts in the USA frequently used for both model development and validation. Despite alarmingly high incidence of lung cancer in low- and middle-income countries/regions, model building and validation in Asia and Africa remains limited. Etzel et al developed a risk prediction model in 2008 tailored for the African-American population.29 Among five Asian models (JPHC, Korean Men, China NCC-LCm2021, LCRS and Shanghai-LCM) included in our study, external validations are relatively scarce. Western models generally perform poorly in Asian populations, potentially attributable to the notable differences in genetic susceptibility, diet, alcohol consumption, physical activity, air pollution and occupational/environmental exposures.30 Notably, the distinct smoking patterns and tobacco-related factors in the Asian population, characterised by low intensity, late initiation, bidi use and extremely low prevalence of female smoking, could shape unique and Asian-specific underlying lung cancer risk.31 Gandini et al found that in Asian countries such as China, Japan and Korea, the lung cancer risk among smoking individuals was about four times among never smoking individuals. By contrast, the risk of lung cancer in African-American smoking individuals was 10 times higher than in never smoking individuals. Notably, over 40% Asian lung cancer cases occur in never smoking individuals,32 possibly due to higher exposure to indoor and ambient air pollution and secondhand smoke.33 These disparities highlight the need for region-specific model development, validation, recalibration and localisation in Africa and Asia.

Model development strategy

Existing models are developed using two main approaches: developing new models using known predictors in large-scale cohorts (such as OWL and CanPredict), or refining existing models like LLP, PLCO and HUNT. Prospective cohorts are ideal for constructing predictive models, whereas conventional case-control studies, though efficient for identifying predictors, are not optimal due to inability to determine absolute risk.34 Only Spitz and LLP models derived from case-control studies.

Model performance metrics may be artificially inflated if based solely on the original sample used for model training. Internal validation techniques can mitigate this optimism. More than half of these models adopted a split-sample approach, despite its inefficiency.35 External validation is crucial for assessing overfitting and generalisability, with no internal validation methods being a substitute.

Model performance in external validation

Classical models such as Bach, Spitz and LLP have served as benchmarks for model improvement, frequently used for validation and comparison. PLCO series models, LLPv3 and LCRAT have undergone multiple external validations yielding mixed results. The latest models, OWL and CanPredict, demonstrated significant performance, particularly OWL in North American and various smoking status populations. The CanPredict model showed outstanding discrimination in the general population but declined in the CPRD database, with an AUC below 0.70. Models derived from Asian countries, such as China NCC-LCm2021, LCRS and Shanghai-LCM, have unclear predictive accuracy and generalisability due to poor reporting performance statistics, which also warrants validation in other populations.

Indeed, it is impossible to definitively prove that all models are valid. A significant issue with external validation studies is that they frequently rely on small, localised data sets. Model performance consistency hinges on population characteristics similarity between validation and development sets, including age range, smoking status and race. Furthermore, the quality and integrity of these validation studies significantly influence model performance. Over time, participants’ measurements and baseline risk factors may change, causing calibration drift and heterogeneity. Therefore, multiple validation studies are necessary to assess model generalisability. To ensure model reliability and transportability, researchers must scrutinise model adequacy and report the model’s predictive performance across clinical settings, populations and subgroups. Design and report of high-quality external validation studies should adhere to certain standards or guidelines,36 such as the extension to the TRIPOD+AI (Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis+artificial intelligence).37

Model implementation in medical practice

The ease of implementation of a model is a critical consideration, depending on the modelling methodology used. In this study, most included models built by conventional statistical methods, which had explicit modelling assumptions, features and complexities, but these methods could not be scaled to very large data sets.38 OWL and UCL-I models, powered by machine learning algorithms, exhibited superior performance. Notably, the UCL-I model, using ensemble machine learning with three predictors (age, smoking duration and pack-years), matched the predictive prowess and clinical relevance of existing models. Machine learning and AI models excel in capturing non-linear and interaction effects of predictors, offering more precise outcomes than traditional statistical methods when dealing with high-dimensional and heterogeneous data. In contrast, machine learning models, heavily reliant on computers, are often perceived as black boxes, lacking transparency and reproducibility due to their inherent complexity. Thus, model developers should release the code and provide detailed documentation on model fitting and data usage, as exemplified by the UCL-I development team. This transparency fosters independent validation and seamless integration into clinical workflows.39 Furthermore, AI models must undergo rigorous evaluation before deployment in medical practice.

Future research perspective

Given model performance heterogeneity arising from different populations, target settings or changing baseline risks, high-quality external validation is crucial for ongoing evaluation. Routinely monitoring performance and model updates are increasingly important.

Increasing availability of data and growing popularity of AI will further drive lung cancer risk prediction model development. Both enhancing model capabilities and facilitating model implementation merit thorough evaluation. Moreover, incorporating biomarkers in specific subpopulations and examining their interactions with other risk factors probably boosts model predictive accuracy.

Regional imbalance persists in the development of lung cancer risk prediction models. Recognising the need for an update or recalibration of the model across different target populations is crucial. Adhering to harmonised guidance when developing or validating the model would enhance study quality, transparency and reproducibility.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, while the E:O ratio offers a comprehensive evaluation of model calibration, including estimates of calibration slope or other relevant metrics would improve the assessment. Second, due to the wide range of cohorts involved and the high heterogeneity among the populations within these cohorts, the limited validation metrics available hinder more precise evaluation and comparison of model performance. Third, our study restricted the language of studies to English, which may partially explain regional imbalance in model development as well as regional model performance variations.

Conclusions

Performance of lung cancer risk prediction models, despite being well developed and validated, varies in diverse populations. Significant regional imbalance persists in the development of these models. Rigorous external validation or recalibration study in the target population is crucial in accordance with the guidance prior to model implementation.
