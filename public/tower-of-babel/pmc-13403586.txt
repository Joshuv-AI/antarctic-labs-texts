Transcriptomic profiling of DYT-TOR1A patient-derived iPSC reveals dysregulation in extracellular matrix, lipid metabolism, and Chr22q11.23
Authors: Núria Setó-Salvia, Henry Houlden, Thomas T. Warner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDYT-TOR1A is an early-onset generalised movement disorder characterised by involuntary muscle contractions, leading to abnormal postures and repetitive movements. The trinucleotide GAG in-frame deletion (ΔGAG) in the TOR1A gene is the most common Mendelian form of dystonia. The TOR1A gene encodes for TorsinA protein, involved in several molecular functions, including chaperone activity, protein quality control, synaptic vesicle recycling, vesicular trafficking, protein folding, homeostasis of the nuclear envelope integrity, and lipid metabolism. There is increasing evidence that DYT-TOR1A dystonia is a neurodevelopmental disorder, beginning in the period where structural and functional abnormalities manifest early in physiological maturation. Recent publications have highlighted the importance of the extracellular matrix and lipid metabolism in dystonia, processes important to cellular neurodevelopment. In this study we aimed to elucidate the gene expression profiles of mutant DYT-TOR1A patient-derived iPSCs to identify key pathways and differentially expressed proteins involved in the early stages of neurodevelopment.ResultsOf the 39,065 genes analysed, 1,322 were found to be significantly dysregulated (p < 0.05). For the purposes of this study, we focus on the 36 most significantly dysregulated genes (q < 0.05) and discuss their biological relevance. We identified 28 upregulated and 8 downregulated genes in human DYT-TOR1A patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). Most of the encoded proteins constitute fundamental components of extracellular matrix and lipid metabolism, suggesting impairments related to the microenvironment for cell growth, and cell differentiation, membrane fluidity, receptor trafficking, as well as neurodevelopment. In addition, we identified changes in the expression of genes coding for nuclear proteins, suggesting dysregulation in transcription factors involved in development, particularly in the forebrain and hippocampus. Interestingly, a high proportion of dysregulated genes are localised on Chromosome 22q11.23.ConclusionsOur study in iPSC-derived from DYT-TOR1A patients shows a transcriptomic profile, which validates previous candidate genes highlighted in other animal models of dystonia. We believe that our results will help elucidate early mechanisms in neurodevelopment of DYT-TOR1A dystonia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12920-025-02249-7.

Background

DYT-TOR1A dystonia is an early-onset generalised form of dystonia caused by a mutation in the TOR1A gene. Typically, DYT-TOR1A dystonia presents in childhood, affecting the limbs (usually legs) and progresses to involve the trunk and upper limbs. It is characterised by intermittent muscle contractions leading to abnormal postures and repetitive movements. An increasing number of studies have discovered the dysfunction of the circuitry involving sensorimotor, basal ganglia and cerebellar function [1], in the absence of neurodegeneration or cell loss.

Mutations in the TOR1A gene, particularly an in-frame deletion of the GAG trinucleotide (ΔGAG), represent the most common Mendelian form of dystonia [2]. The TOR1A gene encodes TorsinA protein, an ATPase associated with different cellular activities (AAA+), located in the inner lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and contiguous perinuclear space of the nuclear envelope (NE). It also can shuttle between the ER lumen and NE. TorsinA is involved in several molecular functions, including chaperone activity, protein quality control, vesicular trafficking, synaptic vesicle recycling, protein folding, homeostasis of the nuclear envelope integrity, and lipid metabolism [3–12].

There is increasing evidence that DYT-TOR1A dystonia is a neurodevelopmental disorder [13, 14]. Several studies in animal models have demonstrated that Tor1a knockout mice have disrupted neural development, including abnormal nuclear envelope morphology and impaired cellular migration [15, 16]. Imaging studies in DYT-TOR1A knock-in mice have also revealed structural abnormalities in white matter and altered connectivity in motor and non-motor networks, consistent with a developmental aetiology [17]. Furthermore, several animal models have shown that TorsinA is expressed during critical periods of brain development, linking its role in neurodevelopmental processes [18–20]. However, the absence of TorsinA during early development leads to lasting changes in neural circuitry that are not corrected by later restoration of the protein [21].

Recent studies of the molecular functions of TorsinA have focused on 2 new areas, lipid metabolism and the extracellular matrix (ECM). For example in Drosophila, the modulation of lipid content and peroxidation levels, including the lipocalin, Glaz (Glial Lazarillo), improved neuronal health and mitochondrial function in flies, suggesting a role of lipid dysregulation in development [22]. ​ Other studies of TorsinA in Drosophila have shown the essential role of the lipin family in fat body development and triacylglycerol storage [23], with lipins 1 and 2 associated with TOR1A in the central nervous system (CNS) [24]. In addition, defects in the lipin family have been shown to cause neurological deficits in Drosophila, such as abnormal neuromuscular junction morphology, suggesting evidence of defective lipid metabolism and neurological disease [25]. On the other hand, research in mouse models of Tor1A dystonia has also shown elevated levels of lipin phosphatidic acid phosphatase enzyme activity [13], showing that the increased protein in both human patient-derived neurons and Tor1a mutant mouse brains affected lipid metabolism. Genetic reduction of lipin1 activity in DYT-TOR1A knock-in mouse models resulted in improved survival and reduced neurodegeneration, supporting a pathogenic role for dysregulated lipid metabolism in dystonia and highlighting lipin1 as a potential therapeutic target [26]. ​In addition, the human models of iPSC-derived dopaminergic neurons in DYT-TOR1A patients have shown altered lipin enzyme activity, suggesting again that lipid metabolism in DYT-TOR1A dystonia is crucial [13].

Lipid metabolism, which contributes not only to the continuous lipid turnover and protein–lipid interactions in the NE membrane [27, 28] but also affects the function and stability of the ECM, involved in the synthesis, secretion, remodulation and signalling between the intracellular and extracellular microenvironment [29, 30], as well as during development and neurogenesis [31–35], is essential for cellular homeostasis. One of the main functions of the ECM is to act as scaffold that facilitates cell-matrix interactions, which are crucial for various physiological processes in muscle tissue. In skeletal muscle, the ECM creates a stable microenvironment that supports cell adhesion, migration, proliferation, and differentiation [36]. Additionally, ECM can remodel enzymes such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which are regulated by lipid-derived signals and membrane composition, with dysregulated lipid metabolism often contributing to pathological ECM defects, including fibrosis and altered tissue architecture [37]. Lipid metabolism also influences ECM composition through the synthesis of membrane phospholipids and cholesterol, which are essential for the secretion and structural assembly of collagen and elastin [38]. During neurodevelopment, lipid-ECM interactions are particularly relevant, with guiding of processes like cell migration and synaptic organization via lipid-mediated modulation of ECM-dependent signalling pathways [35, 39, 40]. In summary, these recent research findings point to a potential role for lipid metabolism, not only in neurogenesis and development, but also dystonia.

The study of human DYT-TOR1A patient derived- iPSCs could help us understand the effects of mutant DYT-TOR1A on gene expression at early stages of development. To unravel the molecular underpinnings of these changes in gene expression and pathways, RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) has become a powerful tool, providing comprehensive insights into gene expression profiles associated with neurodevelopment and dystonia. To date, several studies have used RNA-seq in mouse models to investigate the role of DYT-TOR1A [41] and transcriptome expression, as well as in other types of dystonia, like DYT-THAP1 [42–44] and DYT-SGCE [45]. Gene expression analyses in human brain tissue have highlighted cellular and molecular pathways, including psychiatric comorbidities which are also affected in dystonia [46]. The majority of studies of the pathogenesis of DYT-TOR1A and RNA-seq experiments have used animal models. However, the data on RNA-seq in human DYT-TOR1A cells is very limited. One of the reasons is the lack of cell models and the low penetrance of the disease. In this study, we reprogrammed fibroblasts from DYT-TOR1A (ΔGAG) patients into iPSCs and analysed the RNA-seq transcriptomic profiling. With this study of human DYT-TOR1A iPSCs we aimed to gain insights into changes in gene expression and potential dysregulated pathways during early developmental stages in neurogenesis.

Generation of fibroblast lines

Primary fibroblasts from DYT-TOR1A patients and controls were generated from 3-mm skin punch biopsies. Written informed consent to participate was obtained from all the participants and the parents/legal guardians of minors. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Joint Research Ethics Committee of the National Hospital for Neurology and Neurosurgery and the Institute of Neurology (London), under the reference number (Ref 22/NE/0080). In this project, we had access to samples from 5 DYT-TOR1A symptomatic patients and 5 control cell lines (Table 1).

Reprogramming of fibroblasts into induced pluripotent stem cells

Fibroblasts were reprogrammed into iPSCs by episomal plasmids of OCT4/p53, SOX2/KLF4, and L-MYC/LIN28A as described previously [47]. Transfected fibroblasts were transferred in DMEM (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium) (Invitrogen) for 24 h, and subsequently the cells were maintained with Essential 8 medium until colonies with iPSC morphology were observed after approximately 5 weeks in culture. All iPSCs lines were reprogrammed at the same time and in parallel. New colonies were manually picked for each cell line and transferred in a new plate previously coated with Geltrex (Gibco). The new iPSC colonies were expanded for further characterisation. Initial karyotype tests were performed in several colonies using the hPSC Genetic Analysis Kit (STEMCELL Technologies) and subsequently, the full karyotyping of the candidate iPSC colonies from controls and patients was performed by Cell Guidance Systems, UK. Three control iPSC cell lines: RB001A (C1), BioN (C2), and UKK (C3) purchased through Public Health England, Department of Health, over the European Collection of Cell Cultures (ECACC), were previously fully characterised and karyotyped. All the induced pluripotent stem cells in this study were fully characterized using immunocytochemistry for pluripotency markers SOX2 (Millipore Sigma AB5603 1:200) and SSEA4 (Biolegend 1:250) at early passages. In addition, RNA expression of OCT3/4, NANOG and SSEA4 were also assessed in all iPSCs lines and compared with neurons cell lines to show the differences in pluripotency. Finally, the full karyotype and Sanger sequencing was performed with SourceBio (Nottingham) with all the iPSCs for the confirming presence or absence of the ΔGAG deletion in the TOR1A gene (Supplementary Figures).

RNA extraction

Total RNA was extracted from 3 different passages of all iPSCs lines using TRIzol™ (Life Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were tested for concentration and quality control in the lab using DeNovix DS-11 FX + Spectrophotometer for the ratios: A260/A280, A260/A230 and RIN/DIN before analysis. Subsequently, samples were sent to Novogene for RNA-seq.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

In brief, mRNA was isolated from total RNA using oligo(dT) magnetic beads and then fragmented into approximately 200-base segments. These short mRNA fragments served as templates for cDNA synthesis, followed by PCR amplification to build the RNA library. Sequencing of the cDNA library was performed using Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Homo sapiens) by Novogene Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Raw RNA-seq reads were assessed for quality using base-call Phred scores and reads with average Q scores < 30 were not included. The cleaned reads were then aligned to the human genome (GRCh38).

Raw read counts were used for differential expression analysis of three biological replicates using DESeq2 and pairwise comparisons between groups were performed using the DESeq2 R package (1.16.1). The resulting P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg’s approach for controlling false discovery rate. Genes with an adjusted P value (q-value) < 0.05 were assigned as differentially expressed (DEGs) regardless of the direction of fold change. To gain biological insights from the RNA-seq data, functional and pathway analysis was performed, Gene Ontology (GO) dot chart and KEGG pathway analysis were also used.

Software

Differentially expressed genes were analysed with QIAGEN’s Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) (version 24.0.2) to identify potential direct molecular interactions, enriched canonical pathways, and predictions in upstream regulators and their activation states, including associated gene changes with diseases/functions to build gene interaction networks. The values used were log2(FoldChange) ≥ 0 for upregulated genes, and log2(FoldChange) ≤ 0 for downregulated genes, and p-value < 0.05. For statistical analysis and graphs for normalized values, Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism 10 were used.

Data Availability

The data generated in this study have been deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession number GSE302557: (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE302557).

Results

A total of 39,065 genes were analysed, of which 1,322 genes were significantly dysregulated (p < 0.05). A heatmap illustration from the top 100 genes with lowest p-values is also presented (Fig.1B). However, in this paper we have selected only the most significantly 36 dysregulated genes (q < 0.05) with the aim of shortlisting from more than 1,000 candidates (Fig. 2). The list includes novel genes and pseudogenes, of which interactions and functional roles are yet to be experimentally confirmed. The classification between up- and down regulated genes is shown in Fig. 2A, as well as the representation of the chromosomal location, where chromosome 22 had the highest number of dysregulated genes in our analysis (Fig. 2B).

Extracellular matrix and lipid pathways in DYT-TOR1A iPSC patients

36 most dysregulated genes in DYT-TOR1A patient iPSCs were identified, mainly associated with the ECM and lipid metabolism. The upregulated genes identified in DYT-TOR1A patient-derived iPSCs were SPOCK3 (q-value = 1.49E-06; log2FoldChange = 3.31), DPEP3 (q-value = 0.0094; log2FoldChange = 2.37), PLA2G2A (q-value = 0.049; log2FoldChange = 2.12) and SMIM3 (q-value = 0.0022; log2FoldChange = 0.91).

The SPOCK3 gene, also known as CWCV and Kazal like domains proteoglycan 3, encodes the Testican-3 protein, that is involved in many steps of neurogenesis. To date, Spock3/Testican-3 has been associated with brain development in mouse model, demonstrating structural abnormalities in the corpus callosum and cortical axonal tracts, accompanied by behavioural changes, such as decreased anxiety-like behaviour and lowered sociability [48]. However, there is no supporting evidence of the role of Testican-3 protein and dystonia. Similarly, with the DPEP3 gene, which encodes for dipeptidase 3 protein, a membrane-bound glycoprotein from the family of dipeptidases, a specific functional role remains to be described. However, possible function, with localisation in the cell membrane, is involved in hydrolytic metabolism of different dipeptides. The SMIM3 gene encodes for small integral membrane protein 3, however, its role is not fully understood. The high expression of this protein has been associated to acute myeloid leukemia (AML), suggesting that it regulates ion channels in neuronal differentiation and transmembrane transport functions due to encoding a small integral membrane protein likely involved in membrane-associated processes such as ion flux and signal transduction, which are key mechanisms in neurodevelopment [49].

In contrast, the downregulated genes identified in DYT-TOR1A patient-derived iPSCs were PCDHGA3 (q-value = 0.049; log2FoldChange = −2.12), NALCN (q-value = 0.027; log2FoldChange = −2.05), RIPOR2 (q-value = 6.5E-04; log2FoldChange = −1.22), and SLC47A1 (q-value = 0.035; log2FoldChange = −0.93).

Interestingly, the most significantly downregulated gene implicates the ECM. The PCDHGA3 encodes for protocadherin gamma-A3, a calcium-dependent cell-adhesion protein, also potentially involved in the establishment and maintenance of specific neuronal connections. Interestingly, in a whole-exome sequencing analysis in blepharospasm, a type of focal dystonia characterized by involuntary orbicularis oculi spasms, a patient was found with a variant in PCDHGA3 gene [50].

In addition, NALCN gene, which encodes a sodium leak channel localised in the cell membrane, is involved in influencing neuronal excitability and signalling. NALCN interacts with members of the Src family of tyrosine kinases (SFKs) and its link with dystonia was established few years ago with the identification of variants in isolated cervical dystonia [51, 52], developmental and intellectual impairment with hypotonia [53] and Parkinson’s diseases [54]. Subsequent studies have also identified variants in NALCN gene as causative of infantile neuroaxonal dystrophy (INAD) [55]. It is still unknown how variants in this gene affects the mechanisms in early stages of neurodevelopment; however, pathways involved in sodium channel and lipid metabolism, cell membrane dynamics, and signalling could trigger a specific pathway in neuronal development in dystonia.

The RIPOR2 gene, encodes for Rho family-interacting cell polarisation regulator 2 protein and is involved in several cellular processes, including cell polarisation, intracellular signalling, and cytoskeletal regulation. RIPOR2 gene, also known as FAM65B, is involved in the modulation of sorting nexins, located in the plasma membrane [56]. In addition, it has been associated with negative regulation of T cell proliferation, migration and polarity involved in the immune system [57], and mutations in FAM65B are also involved in hearing loss by impairing membrane localization and phospholipid binding [58]. To date, there is no supporting evidence of an association between RIPOR2 and dystonia. Similarly, with SLC47A1 gene, which encodes for multidrug and toxin extrusion protein 1, acts as a multidrug and toxin extrusion (MATE) transporter and facilitates the efflux of various substrates across cellular membranes. SLC47A1 is localised in the cell membrane and is expressed in different tissues, mostly the adrenal gland and liver. SLC47A1 is also present at the brain blood barrier (BBB), a dynamic and complex interface between the blood and the central nervous system that regulates brain homeostasis [59]. The role of SLC47A1 could be involved in the efflux of organic cations and cationic drugs [60] but, its association with dystonia is still unknown.

PLA2G2A upregulation in DYT-TOR1A patients leads to a disrupted lipid metabolism

Interestingly, with the genes dysregulated in DYT-TOR1A patient-derived iPSCs, we identified upregulated PLA2G2A gene, which encodes the phospholipase A2 Group IIA membrane associated protein (sPLA2-IIA). The sPLA2-IIA is a secreted extracellular protein, also found in the cell membrane and mitochondrial outer membrane, involved in lipid remodelling of cellular membranes and generation of lipid mediators. Even though this protein is expressed only at low levels in brain, its role in dystonia could be relevant during development. RNA-seq data shows that PLA2G2A upregulation causes impaired metabolism of arachidonic, linoleic and α-linoleic acids, and ether lipid pathways in DYT-TOR1A patients. (Table 3).

The fatty acid metabolites have an important role in synaptic signalling, neuronal firming, neurotransmitter released, and neuronal gene expression, among other properties [61].

In addition, the association between PLA2G6, one of the PLA2G2A metabolites, and dystonia-parkinsonism (PARK14; 612953) and INAD [64] is well known, suggesting a potential indirect effect of this pathway in a different manner for DYT-TOR1A dystonia. Nuclear proteins and transcription factors in DYT-TOR1A patients

Previous studies have identified dysfunction of the nuclear envelope (NE) associated with DYT-TOR1A dystonia [4]. Mutant TorsinA protein causes disrupted nuclear-cytoskeletal interactions and defective nuclear membrane integrity [16, 69]. Interestingly, we have identified four upregulated genes expressed in the nucleus in DYT-TOR1A patients-derived iPSC.

The LHX5 gene (q-value = 0.001; log2FoldChange = 3.80), encodes for LIM Homeobox 5 protein Lhx5, a transcription factor crucial for central nervous system in development. The LIM Homeobox 5 protein Lhx5 regulates neuronal differentiation and migration, and particularly plays an essential role in the forebrain [70], forming the Cajal-Retzius cells [71], and posterior hypothalamus [72, 73]. In mice, Lhx5 is essential for cell proliferation and neuronal differentiation during hippocampal development [74, 75]. Previous studies in mouse models have demonstrated that Lhx5 is co-expressed in multiple interneuron subtypes in the developing spinal cord, including early-born dI4 and dI6 inhibitory interneurons [76]. Lhx5 is also crucial for maintaining the expression of transcription factors such as Pax2, Pax5, and Pax8, which are essential for establishing and maintaining the GABAergic inhibitory neurotransmitter phenotype in these neurons [76, 77]. In addition, the LHX5-AS1 gene (q-value = 0.028; log2FoldChange = 4.15), which is also upregulated, is a long non-coding RNA and complementary to LHX5 mRNA, which could by duplex formation, potentially block LHX5 translation into protein. During early human brain development, LHX5-AS1 RNA and Lhx5 protein exhibit similar expression patterns, suggesting that LHX5-AS1 may play a regulatory role in modulating Lhx5 protein levels [78]. LHX5 is expressed in cerebellum during development, particularly in Purkinje cells soon after cells migrate out of the cerebellar ventricular zone [79]. Despite the critical roles of LHX5 and LHX5-AS1 in neural development, there is no current evidence directly associating mutations or dysregulation of LHX5 or LHX5-AS1 and dystonia.

Another upregulated gene is TYW1B (q-value = 5.40E-13; log2FoldChange = 2.90), which encodes for the tRNA-yW synthesising 1 homolog B protein. The tRNA-yW, is expressed in the cellular nucleus (Human Protein Atlas), and it is involved in the biosynthesis of wybutosine, a hypermodified guanosine found in phenylalanine tRNA. This protein plays a crucial role in stabilising codon-anticodon interactions during ribosomal decoding, maintaining the reading frame during protein synthesis. Mutations in TYW1B can cause primary microcephaly and problems in motion and cognition by hindering neuronal proliferation and migration, demonstrating in mouse brains malfunctioning in translation of proteins involved in cell cycling [80]. However, there is no scientific evidence in the association between TYW1B and dystonia, it suggests potential indirect associations through shared biological pathways, in which disruptions in tRNA modification and subsequent neuronal differentiation defects were found in patients with cerebral palsy and intellectual disability [80].

Another upregulated coding-protein gene expressed in the nucleus of the DYT-TOR1A patients, was the RAMACL (q-value = 5.41E-05; log2FoldChange = 2.65). This gene encodes for the RNA guanine-N7 methyltransferase-activating subunit-like protein, which is involved in RNA 5’-cap (guanine-N7)-methylation. There is very limited information regarding this protein as well as its interactions with other proteins or diseases. It belongs to the RAM family (Ras and Rab interactor-like proteins), a group of proteins that interact with small GTPases, playing a role in intracellular trafficking, signal transduction, and possibly other cellular processes, and it has been involved in RNA-binding activities, but it is more generally related to GTPase regulation. One of the possible interactions with RAMACL is the RAF proto-oncogene serine/threonine-protein kinase (RAF1) [81], a serine/threonine-protein kinase key factor in regulating connection between membrane-bound Ras GTPases and the MAPK/ERK signalling pathway. RAF1 is also involved in cell fate, proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, survival, and oncogenic transformation. It is worth mentioning that the MAPK/ERK signalling pathway has been associated with the pathophysiology of L-DOPA-induced dyskinesia (LID) [82, 83], and the existent indirect link between RAMACL dysregulation and MAPK/ERK signalling could be a pathway for future consideration.

The Chr22q11.23 locus as a possible expression modulator in DYT-TOR1A dystonia

Interestingly, previous studies have shown an association between chromosome 22q11.2 (Chr22q11.2) and dystonia in patients with deletion syndrome [84, 89]. However our results show clustering of dysregulated genes in DYT-TOR1A patient-derived iPSCs on chromosome 22q11.23 (Chr22q11.23). These include: AP000350.6 (q-value = 0.00023; log2FoldChange = 3.4), AP000350.2 (q-value = 0.049; log2FoldChange = 3.55), MIF-AS1 (q-value = 3.45E-08; log2FoldChange = 2.5), DDTL (q-value = 0.038; log2FoldChange = −0.9), GSTT2B (q-value = 0.0013; log2FoldChange = −1.36), and SUSD2 (q-value = 4.17E-10; log2FoldChange = 2.27).

The DDTL gene encodes for the D-dopachrome tautomerase-like protein, which is localised in the cytoplasm. Curiously, different studies have identified specific DDTL copy number variants (CNV) in patients with epilepsy and intellectual disability, also emphasising the high change of methylation in this locus [90, 91]. The D-dopachrome tautomerase-like protein belongs to the macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) family, which includes proteins like D-dopachrome decarboxylase (DDT) [92].

In addition to the DDTL coding-protein, we identified 3 non-coding genes related to DDTL. The AP000350.6 gene is a long non-coding RNA which is highly expressed in brain (cerebellar hemisphere and cerebellum), among other tissues (GTEx Analysis Release V10), however the function of this novel transcript is still not known. In addition, the AP000360.2 pseudogene, also known as KLHL5 pseudogene 1 (KLHL5P1) has a predicted function as KLHL5 gene, enabling protein-protein interactions. A recent study has suggested that AP000350.6 and AP000360.2 genes are predicted regulators of DDT and MIF gene through post-transcriptional mechanisms [93]. The MIF-AS1, classified as MIF antisense RNA 1, was also identified in DYT-TOR1A patient-derived iPSCs. Based on previous data, the AP000350.6, AP000360.2 and MIF-AS1 non-coding genes could also regulate the expression of DDT-like protein, potentially affecting on the downregulation of the DDTL expression (Fig. 5).

Additionally, we identified the GSTT2B gene, encoding for the glutathione S-transferase theta-2B protein. Its function is to reduce glutathione to a wide number of exogenous and endogenous hydrophobic electrophiles. Recently, a genome-wide association study (GWAS) in UK Biobank participants identified genetic loci for 19 circulating fatty acid (FA) traits. This included significant loci for traits related to polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), and saturated fatty acids (SFAs), including different loci, respectively. Interestingly, a novel locus for total FA was identified around GSTT2B, GSTT2 and GSTT1 genes, suggesting a possible role in lipid metabolism [94]. Alongside GSTT2B, the genes MIF-AS1, DDTL, and SUSD2 may be subject to co-ordinated transcriptional regulation, as observed in a patient with fucosidosis [95]. Lastly, the identification of the SUSD2 gene, encoding for sushi domain-containing protein 2, a type I transmembrane protein, is characterized by several functional domains frequently found in molecules involved in cell–cell and cell–matrix adhesion, migration, and immune modulation [96]. The role of GSTT2B and SUSD2 in the ECM and dystonia is still unknown, however, studies of the possible interaction with ECM-degrading enzymes (metalloproteinases) or ECM-binding proteins to modulate ECM integrity should be considered in the future research in dystonia.

Overall, the ECM and lipid metabolism, with the gene expression in locus 22q11.23 and possible methylation events could play a crucial role during neurodevelopment in DYT-TOR1A dystonia. Further studies should be considered, as a potential regulatory event affecting specific biological functions of proteins and additional research is also needed to elucidate its precise roles in human neurogenesis and dystonia.

Relevant non-coding findings in DYT-TOR1A patients

In this study, we also identified dysregulated non-coding genes with the DYT-TOR1A patient-derived iPSCs. However, their functions and interactions are yet to be ascertained. The two most strongly upregulated genes in this study regarding log2FoldChange values were CYP4F29P (q-value = 6.71E-22; log2FoldChange = 26.05), and ANKRD20A11P (q-value = 0.049; log2FoldChange = 7.86), which are classified as unprocessed pseudogenes.

The CYP4F29P gene is a cytochrome P450 family 4 subfamily F member 29 pseudogene. The cytochrome P450 proteins are monooxygenases that catalyse several reactions also involved in the metabolism and synthesis of lipids. Even though CYP4F29P is generally considered non-functional and does not produce an active enzyme, expression data indicates that CYP4F29P is overexpressed in specific tissues, mostly in oesophagus mucosa, as well as the nucleus accumbens and caudate (basal ganglia) in the brain, adipose visceral, and subcutaneous tissue (GTEx Analysis Release V10). An epigenome-wide association study tested different methylation sites in skeletal muscle mass (SMM) patients and discovered replicated signals in CYP4F29P methylation, suggesting its involvement in potential muscle functions and the high likelihood of methylation [97]. The second most strongly upregulated gene was the ANKRD20A11P (q-value = 0.049; log2FoldChange = 7.86), also a pseudogene located on chromosome 21, at the same cytogenic band as CYP4F29P. This gene, also known as C21orf81, is a member of the ankyrin repeat domain 20 family and shows a very similar pattern of gene expression in bulk tissue as CYP4F29P (GTEx Analysis Release V10). Both genes are primate-specific pseudogenes lacking known functional analogues in other animal models. In a study of CpG methylation with 250 probes, corresponding to 111 genes, was analysed within metastable epiallele regions and measured in placental tissue, showed that ANKRD20A11P gene had increased methylation between ages one and two, suggesting a key role in development [98]. In summary, the most strongly upregulated genes identified in the DYT-TOR1A iPSCs suggests a potential role of CpG methylation in this region indicating possible regulatory changes during development. Further investigations are required to explore the genes within cytogenic band Chr21q11.2 and its implications and associations with DYT-TOR1A developmental dystonia.

In addition, we identified the following non-coding genes: AL592494.3 (q-value = 0.010; log2FoldChange =−1.16), MTND1P23 (q-value = 4.45E-07; log2FoldChange = 5.86), AC016769.3 (q-value = 3.4E-04; log2FoldChange = 3.39), AL589740.1 (q-value = 0.049; log2FoldChange = 2.57), RHOXF1-AS1 (q-value = 0.0027; log2FoldChange = 2.26), and XACT (q-value = 0.017; log2FoldChange = 1.52), AC233723.2 (q-value = 0.004; log2FoldChange = −2.43), MED15P5 (q-value = 0.001; log2FoldChange = 3.89), AC092821.1 (q-value = 0.005; log2FoldChange = 3.23), AC016769.2 (q-value = 0.0009; log2FoldChange = 3.05), AC005520.2 (q-value = 0.047; log2FoldChange = 1.99), AC007325.2 (q-value = 0.001; log2FoldChange = 1.51), CASC9 (q-value = 0.021; log2FoldChange = 1.46), FTLP14 (q-value = 0.049; log2FoldChange = 1.37), SUGT1P1 (q-value = 0.009; log2FoldChange = 1.25), LINC02253 (q-value = 0.010; log2FoldChange = 1.13). Some of these genes are expressed in different tissues and brain regions (GTEx Analysis Release V10). Nevertheless, further studies of their roles are required in order to draw any conclusions on their functional relevance.

Discussion

Our study identified potentially key dysregulation of gene expression in DYT-TOR1A patient-derived iPSCs, strongly suggesting early dysregulation in the ECM organization, lipid metabolism, and expression of genes clustered on Chr22q11.23. These findings suggest that neurodevelopmental disruptions in these pathways and mechanisms may underlie the pathogenesis of dystonia. We identified 36 dysregulated candidate genes in DYT-TOR1A patient cells, suggesting multiple molecular or cellular events that may result in impaired ECM function and altered lipid metabolism in early developmental stages. Previous research has established the role of ECM dysfunction in dystonia. Dysregulation in the ECM in DYT-TOR1A motor neurons (MN) impairs neurite outgrowth, branching, and synapse formation, thereby compromising neuronal connectivity, network stability, and plasticity [99]. In addition, laminins are essential ECM components and interact with synaptic proteins critical for cell–cell communication and plasticity, as well as their interaction with lipids [100]. For instance, mutations in the Lamb1 gene, encoding the laminin β1 subunit, result in dystonia-like hindlimb movements and postures in mouse models [101]. Additionally, ECM disruption has been involved in DYT-SGCE myoclonus-dystonia [102]. Sarcoglycan family members, which form part of the dystrophin–glycoprotein complex (DGC), are located at the ECM–cytoskeleton interface and mutations are associated with muscular dystrophies [103, 104].

During brain development, dynamic changes in ECM composition create evolving stiffness gradients that provide crucial biomechanical cues for neuronal proliferation, differentiation, and survival [105–108]. Disruption of neuronal mechanosensing [109] and striatal cholinergic neurons [110] within these ECM contexts could contribute to DYT-TOR1A dystonia pathogenesis during neurodevelopment. In summary, our findings involving dysregulated genes in ECM suggest a potential impairment in the formation of precise neuronal connections, potentially leading to dysregulated neural circuit assembly and function. Altogether, our data suggest that ECM malfunction in DYT-TOR1A iPSCs may begin early in development, accompanied by alterations in lipid metabolism. Nevertheless, our results in iPSC, corresponding to a very early embryonic stage of development, may not necessarily reflect neuronal dysregulation. It is possible that dysregulation at early developmental stages occurs within a specific temporal window, which may later imply different types of abnormalities during subsequent stages of development [14, 21].

Genes related to lipid membrane organisation were also dysregulated in iPSC DYT-TOR1A patients. Lipid metabolism has been previously associated with torsin function in Drosophila and mouse models. Torsin is required for adipose tissue development, and suppression of lipin, a key lipid-metabolising enzyme, disrupts lipid homeostasis [24]. Although our findings do not directly implicate lipin metabolism, potential indirect interactions cannot be excluded. Jung and collaborators showed that lpin-1 knockdown in C. elegans altered fatty acid composition, notably increasing levels of the ω−6 polyunsaturated fatty acids, linoleic acid and arachidonic acid—opposite to changes observed with glucose intake [111]. These results suggest that lipin may influence PUFA biosynthesis, which could play a modulatory role in lipid metabolism. Our results support the notion of disruption of lipid metabolism in DYT-TOR1A dystonia, particularly involving fatty acid-related processes. The PLA2G2A gene upregulation in DYT-TOR1A patients, also with the increased expression of PLA2G6 gene, suggest a possible direct association between these 2 genes at early stages of development. This preliminary data should be taken in consideration as, even though the aim of this study is not proposing a detailed mechanistic explanation for our findings, we hope to point the directions of future studies in dystonia and where further studies are needed to elucidate the specific lipid-related mechanisms contributing to dystonia pathophysiology.

Strikingly, nearly 17% of the dysregulated genes identified in DYT-TOR1A patient iPSCs are located on chromosome 22q11.23. Our findings suggest that expression modulators in this locus could play a key role during early stages of dystonia. These genes may be particularly sensitive to epigenetic modifications, such as altered methylation patterns, which could influence the expression in development of nearby genes and contribute to disease susceptibility.

While iPSC technology offers a powerful tool for disease modelling, it also presents several limitations. One major concern is the incomplete erasure of epigenetic memory during reprogramming, which may influence gene expression and differentiation potential [112, 113]. Moreover, iPSC lines from the same individual—or between individuals—can exhibit substantial gene expression heterogeneity, affecting the reproducibility of results [114]. This variability is especially relevant in neurodevelopmental studies, where subtle changes in gene regulation can yield significant phenotypic differences [115, 116]. Such variability may complicate efforts to model complex disorders like dystonia, which may involve specific, stage-dependent pathway dysregulation during development. Further research involving more DYT-TOR1A patient samples and functional assays is required to validate these findings.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that genes involved in ECM organization, lipid metabolism, and those clustered-on chromosome 22q11.23 are differentially expressed in iPSCs-derived from DYT-TOR1A dystonia patients compared to controls. These dysregulated pathways, previously studied in animal models of dystonia, have been shown to be active during early neurodevelopment, suggesting a conserved mechanism underlying disease pathogenesis [117, 118]. This study provides novel evidence supporting the role of early molecular disturbances in cell adhesion, lipid homeostasis, and possible epigenetic modulation in the aetiology of dystonia. While iPSC technology offers a powerful platform for uncovering disease mechanisms, future work should validate these findings in complementary models and assess the functional consequences of the affected pathways. Altogether, our results advance the understanding of DYT-TOR1A dystonia as a neurodevelopmental disorder and identify candidate genes and molecular targets for future investigation and therapeutic targets.
