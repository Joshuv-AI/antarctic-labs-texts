Antibiotic resistance in E.coli isolated from mint in local markets in Hebron, Palestine
Authors: Omama Shabana, Ali AlKoumi, Ashraf Abdeen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe growing concerns about antibiotic resistant bacteria have emerged as a significant global health threat, raising concerns about leafy vegetables including mint as potential reservoirs for resistant strains. This study investigated antibiotic resistance in the fecal contamination indicator E.coli, isolated from locally cultivated mint in Hebron, Palestine.MethodsA total of 14 mint samples were collected from grocery markets across the central, western, and southern regions of Hebron between January and March 2024. E.coli identification was performed using biochemical testing and polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and antibiotic susceptibility testing was done through Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method.Results13 isolates were confirmed to be E.coli through biochemical and molecular analyses (PCR). These were subsequently tested for phenotypic resistance through Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method. Among the 13 isolates, 53.9% (95% CI, 26.8–81%) and 46.2% (95% CI, 19.2–74.9%) were resistant to tetracycline and chloramphenicol, respectively. Notably, 15.4% (95% CI, 4.3–42.2%) of the isolates showed possible heteroresistance to fluoroquinolones, with one isolate displaying resistance to both tetracycline and chloramphenicol (7.7%, 95% CI, 1.4–33.3%). Intermediate resistance to orally administered cefuroxime was observed in 61.5% (95% CI, 35.1–88%) of the isolates. Furthermore, 23.1% (95% CI, 8.2–50.1%) of the isolates were classified as MDR strains based on Magiorakos et al. (2012) MDR classification criteria.ConclusionThis study is considered the first to demonstrate the presence of antibiotic resistance in mint plant in Palestine. Our preliminary results align with international findings, add to the existing local epidemiological data, and highlight the need to study the extent of antibiotic resistance in clinically significant bacteria contaminating fresh produce in Palestine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05277-0.

Background

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR), often described as a “silent pandemic,” develops in both pathogenic and commensal bacteria [1, 2]. This marks a possible return to the pre-antibiotic era, where routine procedures such as surgeries and dental treatments carried fatal infection risks [3, 4]. The Global Research on Antimicrobial Resistance (GRAM) revealed that 4.71 million AMR-associated deaths were documented in 2021, projecting at least further 92 million deaths between 2025 and 2050 [5].

Beyond AMR in human and animal reservoirs, accumulating evidence links resistant bacteria to foodborne diseases, highlighting the need for systematic screening of plant food varieties [6–8]. This was overlooked until the routine consumption of fresh produce became a global trend owing to its nutritional value [9]. The associated production pressure may explain the accumulating evidence of resistant bacteria-associated foodborne outbreaks [6–8]. Although bacterial contamination is evident throughout the entire crop supply chain, it predominates during the pre-harvest stage [10–13]. This is driven by the utilization of wastewater for irrigation [11], inadequately processed animal manure as an organic fertilizer [12], and the cross-reactivity of resistance genes against pesticides applied to combat crop pests, which adds to the resistance repertoire of plant bacterial commensals and other acquired bacterial species [10].

According to the Global Antimicrobial Surveillance System (GLASS) by the World Health Organization (WHO), E.coli is among the most frequently detected resistant bacteria in individuals diagnosed with bacterial infections across 22 countries including several neighboring countries [14]. This bacterium is commonly used to detect fecal contamination in food products, as it is a major intestinal commensal in warm-blooded animals [15]. Edible plants have frequently been documented as reservoirs for E.coli. It has been isolated from various types of fresh produce at all stages of the supply chain worldwide [16–20]. Although E.coli has been isolated from a broad range of vegetables, leafy green vegetables are among the most frequently screened produce because of their higher consumption rate in raw or minimally cooked forms. Multidrug resistance (MDR) refers to the acquired non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antibiotic classes [21]. Multiple studies have frequently reported high levels of MDR in E.coli isolated from fresh produce [16, 17, 20, 22]. This highlights the risks posed to vegetable consumers, especially those who consume leafy green varieties.

Palestine faces a serious and worsening AMR burden, as shown by the increase in the total AMR rate from 31.9% in 2020 to 37% in 2023 [23], due to unfully regulated antibiotic consumption in human and veterinary medicine [24]. Consequently, combating AMR is a top priority of the National Action Plan. In 2020, the Palestinian Ministry of Health reported the resistance patterns of E.coli and Salmonella spp. isolated from clinical specimens, as well as the resistance profiles of gram-negative bacteria isolated from animals. E.coli exhibited high resistance rates to ampicillin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, nalidixic acid, and tetracycline [25]. Another study analysed 10,000 clinical isolates of clinically significant bacteria isolated at the laboratories of governmental hospitals in the West Bank of Palestine. It showed that 22.8% of E.coli isolates were MDR, while 58.3% of extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing (ESBL) E.coli were MDR, reflecting co-resistance patterns commonly associated with ESBL genes [24]. Other studies have reported high AMR rates of different bacterial species from clinical samples including ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, and carbapenems [26–28]. However, there is a lack of data on the AMR status of clinically significant bacteria isolated from fresh produce. Furthermore, there is limited evidence regarding the extent of bacterial contamination in vegetables that are commonly consumed in Palestine. To our knowledge, the only study examining bacterial contamination of plants is a master’s thesis conducted by Najajreh et al. [29]. The study identified E.coli along with other clinically significant bacteria isolated from parsley as well as other bacterial species in mint, two widely consumed raw ingredients in Palestinian cuisine [29]. Therefore, collecting preliminary data on fecal contamination and AMR in commonly consumed raw leafy plants in Palestine is crucial. This study was conducted to screen for antibiotic resistance in E.coli isolated from locally cultivated raw mint from grocery stores in Hebron, Palestine.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and processing

A total of 14 locally cultivated mint samples were aseptically collected from grocery stores in Hebron (Hebron H1, Hebron H2, Dura, and Aldahriyyah) between January and March 2024, with each geographical location represented by three to five samples collected from different stores. The samples were transported in ice-cooled insulator box, stored at 4 °C in the case of delayed analysis, and analysed within 24 h of sampling.

Isolation of presumptive E.coli from the samples

An average of 25 g of mint edible parts (leaf and stem) was immersed in 225 mL of nutrient broth and incubated at 37 ± 2 °C. After 24 h of incubation, 1 mL of the turbid broth was transferred into 9 mL of lauryl sulphate tryptose broth (LSTB) and incubated at 37 °C for up to 24 h to selectively enrich coliform bacteria. Subsequent subculturing on LSTB was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Eventually, 10 µL of turbid, gas production-positive, and indole-positive LSTB (incubated at 44 °C) was streaked in duplicate on a MacConkey agar plate from the fecal coliform tube. Positive indole production at 44 °C was considered indicative of E.coli isolation.

Biochemical tests for the primary identification of E.coli

The primary identification of E.coli was performed using triple sugar iron (TSI), sulphide indole motility (SIM), and citrate utilization tests. Testing was performed for at least four colonies with typical E.coli morphology for each sampling location, with a total of 28 presumptive colonies tested.

Molecular confirmation of E.coli using the uidA primers

After biochemical identification, the identity of the positive isolates was confirmed using colony PCR. Primers specific to the E.coli marker β-glucuronidase (uidA) gene were used to amplify a 166 bp conserved sequence of the gene in 97% of E.coli strains: forward primer(FP): 5′tatggaatttcgccgatttt3′, reverse primer (RP): 5′tgtttgcctccctgctgcgg3′ [30]. Each sample reaction contained a single biochemically positive E.coli colony suspended in a mixture of 0.4 µM of each primer and 12.5 µL of PCR master mix (2X) in a 25 µL reaction volume. E.coli (ATCC 53323) was used as a positive control, and genomic DNA was extracted using a silica gel-based spin column. A no-template PCR reaction was used as a negative control. The PCR conditions were as follows: 10 min at 95 °C; 35 cycles [30 Sect. 95 °C, 30 Sect. 55.2 °C, 20 Sect. 72 °C] and 15 min final extension at 72 °C. Thereafter, 5 µL of the PCR reaction was mixed with loading dye (10x) in a 20 µL final volume, 6 µL of the mix was loaded into the gel, separated on 2.5% agarose gel stained with gel red nucleic acid stain under 80 V for one hour in Tris-acetic acid EDTA (TAE) buffer, and visualized using a Gel Doc XR system (Bio-Rad).

Antibiotic sensitivity testing

All PCR-confirmed E.coli isolates were screened for antibiotic resistance using the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method. Testing was performed in a certified clinical microbiology laboratory operating under routine internal quality control and quality assurance protocols, based on the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100 manual [31], and based on local treatment protocols and antibiotic panel availability. Antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed in duplicate on two separate days to evaluate inter-day reproducibility.

Bacterial suspensions equivalent to the 0.5 McFarland standard were prepared from fresh cultures (up to 24 h old ), spread on Muller-Hinton agar, and screened for antibiotic resistance. The tested antibiotics were amoxicillin/clavulanate (30 µg/10 µg), cefotaxime (30 µg), cefuroxime (30 µg), ceftazidime (30 µg), meropenem (10 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), nitrofurantoin (300 µg), tetracycline (30 µg), chloramphenicol (30 µg), cefoxitin (30 µg), and levofloxacin (5 µg). The antibiotics used covered the following MDR strain screening tests: Double Disc Synergy test for ESBL production [32], carbapenemase production [31], and AmpC β-lactamase expression [33]. After 16–18 h of incubation in an ambient air incubator at 37 ± 2 °C, the zones of inhibition were measured in millimeters and the results were reported as sensitive, intermediate, resistant, or heteroresistant based on CLSI breakpoints. A bacterial isolate was considered non-susceptible to an antibiotic when it was resistant or intermediate. MDR was defined as acquired non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial classes, as per the standardised criteria proposed by Magiorakos et al. [21]. MDR classification was further supported by the aforementioned screening tests for ESBL, AmpC β-lactamase, and carbapenemase expression.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistical analysis was performed. Antibiotic non-susceptibility proportions including resistance, and intermediate resistance, were calculated as percentages of the total confirmed isolates (n = 13), as well as the proportions of putative heteroresistance and MDR. The 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the reported proportions were calculated using the Wald method. For proportions near 0%, the Wilson score interval method was applied, as it provides more reliable estimates for extreme or nearly extreme percentages with a small sample size. Finally, the Fisher’s exact test was performed to analyse significant differences between sampling areas and antibiotic types; P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significant results were tested for correlation using Spearman’s ranking test. It should be noted that this study has a limited statistical power, and all statistical results should therefore be interpreted as preliminary.

Results and discussion

Isolation of E.coli from mint in grocery markets

A total of 14 locally cultivated mint samples were collected from local grocery markets in Hebron, representing its central, western, and southern areas. Subsequently, post-LSTB enrichment MacConkey plates had presumptive E.coli colonies that showed either the rough or the smooth typical E.coli colony morphology, as shown in Fig. 1. Collectively, 13 isolates from all sampling areas were positive for the E.coli biochemical profile (A/A with gas production (TSI), indole-positive and motile (SIM), and citrate-negative (Simmon’s citrate test)).

Subsequently, pure cultures from the same biochemically positive colonies were used to test for the presence of a 166 bp conserved sequence in the β-glucuronidase (uidA) gene through colony PCR, and all were positive for the gene. As shown in Fig. 2, the positive control (E.coli ATCC 53323) showed the 166 bp band, while the negative control showed no bands. Compared to biochemical tests, molecular testing offers more convenient, straightforward, cost-effective, and accurate platforms for identifying organisms at the strain level [34]. For the purpose of this study, colony PCR enabled us to identify and confirm E.coli at the species level without the need for DNA extraction using one of the conventional methods, increasing the throughput without a considerable increase in the required cost [35, 36].

The majority of current studies have evaluated the antimicrobial activity of mint essential oils [37, 38]. Nevertheless, evidence from few available studies suggests that this plant is a potential reservoir for clinically significant bacteria, including E.coli, despite its potent antibacterial and antifungal activity [20, 22, 29, 39]. The results of this study showed fecal contamination in mint isolated from the four sampling regions through isolating the universal fecal indicator, E.coli [15]. Similar results were reported in a previous study conducted in Palestine by Najajreh et al. [29], where they identified endophytic Pseudomonas spp., Enterobacter spp., and Klebsiella pneumoniae strains in mint and E.coli, Enterobacter spp., and Salmonella enterica in parsley from Hebron and Bethlehem Cities in Palestine [29]. Our results align with the reported findings worldwide, where E.coli was isolated from vegetables throughout its supply chain in Lebanon [16], UAE [19], Tunisia [18], South Africa [40], Ethiopia [41], India [22], China [42], UK [43], and USA [44].

Given the global evidence of fecal contamination of edible plants, it is imperative to unravel the underlying causes of this phenomenon. Multiple studies have strongly linked plant-based food contamination with the use of contaminated water for irrigation, inadequately processed manure, non-optimal processing, and the presentation of goods under unhygienic conditions [11, 12, 45]. Overall, these potential sources of contamination highlight the necessity of conducting extensive investigations to determine the most likely cause of our findings in future studies.

Antibiotic resistance in E.coli

Antibiotic resistance screening was conducted for the 13 confirmed E.coli isolates using the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method, and the results are listed in Supplementary Table 1. All susceptibility results were reproducible across the two independent testing sessions conducted on separate days. Non-susceptibility was detected for tetracycline, chloramphenicol, and cefuroxime antibiotics, while the 13 isolates were fully susceptible to amoxicillin/clavulanate, cefotaxime, ceftazidime, meropenem, gentamicin, nitrofurantoin, and cefoxitin. According to the data in Table 1, 53.9% (95% CI:26.8-81.0%) and 46.2% (95% CI: 19.2-74.9%) of the isolates were resistant to tetracycline and chloramphenicol, respectively. Fisher’s exact test showed a significant association between tetracycline and chloramphenicol resistance (p-value=0.005), further supported by a strong positive Spearman’s rank correlation (rho=0.842, p-value<0.001). Notably, putative heteroresistance (HR) to levofloxacin (15.4%, 95% CI: 4.3-42.2%) and chloramphenicol and tetracycline (7.7%, 95% CI: 1.4-33.3%) was observed (Table 1 and Figure 3). Heteroresistance is defined as the coexistence of susceptible and resistant cell populations within an apparently clonal population [46]. Furthermore, 61.5% (95% CI: 35.1-88.0%) of the isolates exhibited intermediate resistance to orally administered cefuroxime.

Based on the standardised definition of MDR by Magiorakos et al. [21], 23.1% (95% CI:8.2-50.1%) of the isolates were classified as MDR. Putative heteroresistance was not included in our MDR classification because it did not meet the criteria without confirmatory testing. Additionally, none of the 13 isolates tested positive for ESBL, AmpC β-lactamase, or carbapenemase expression. Although MDR strains were detected in Hebron H1 sampling area, no significant associations were detected between the sampling area and the MDR status (p-value=1.000) or overall non-susceptibility status (p-value=0.640). This is most likely attributable to the limited number of isolates from each sampling area.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to report the emergence of antibiotic resistance and multidrug resistance in clinically significant bacteria isolated from edible leafy plants in Palestine.

One of our key findings is the putative heteroresistance observed in 3/13 E.coli isolates. Heteroresistance is defined as the coexistence of sensitive and resistant cells in a clonal population and is considered a transitional state to full resistance [47]. While our results are limited by the lack of confirmatory test results such as population analysis profiling (PAP), to our knowledge, this is the first report of potential heteroresistance in Palestine. This phenomenon has been reported internationally for E.coli for several antibiotics including colistin, polymyxin B, and carbapenems, although reports involving the antibiotics tested in this study are limited [47, 48]. Scientists have reported multiple mechanisms for HR emergence including (1)tandem amplification of resistance genes, (2)increased resistance plasmid copy number, (3)transposition of resistance genes into a cryptic plasmid, and (4) mutations in antibiotic target sites [48]. In terms of its effect on treatment efficacy, HR has been shown to result in treatment failure due to the selection of resistant subpopulations and the transition into full resistance after antibiotic therapy; however, the results are inconclusive [49]. Here, we report putative heteroresistance to levofloxacin, tetracycline, and chloramphenicol. This is particularly relevant as levofloxacin is one of the most commonly prescribed empirical therapies in Palestine for a wide range of infections including lower respiratory tract infections [28]. Levofloxacin resistance in E.coli is primarily mediated by mutations in DNA gyrase (gyrA and gyrB) and topoisomerase II genes (parC and parE), as well as efflux pump overexpression through the AcrAB-Tolc system and quinolone-resistance genes (qnr). Critically, these resistance mechanisms confer cross-resistance to fluoroquinolones and other antibiotic classes [50]. The putative heteroresistance observed in our isolates may represent a broader fluoroquinolone resistance. Fluoroquinolone resistance is strongly emerging in Palestine, as per a recent study, where levofloxacin and ciprofloxacin resistance was evident [28].

Of special notice is our relatively high resistance rates for tetracycline (53.9%, 95% CI:26.8–81.0) and chloramphenicol (46.2%, 95% CI: 19.2–74.9). In Palestine, tetracycline is rarely prescribed in clinical settings; rather, it is commonly used in veterinary practice, including as a growth promoter [51]. This may indicate potential fecal contamination arising from the application of inadequately processed manure or from the existence of animal farms near agricultural landscapes, which is often observed in Palestine. Tetracycline resistance has been frequently reported in veterinary research and, to a lesser extent, in human medicine research [52, 53]. This is commonly mediated by the resistance genes tetA and tetB, which encode efflux pumps [54]. Other resistance mechanisms include ribosomal protection, enzymatic deactivation, target site modification, and alterations in proton motive force-dependent efflux pump activity, which increase antibiotic cellular extrusion rates [52, 55]. In contrast, chloramphenicol is banned for use in food-producing animals and is infrequently prescribed in human medicine because of its potential toxicity. Based on the strong positive correlation between tetracycline and chloramphenicol resistance (Spearman’s rho = 0.842, p-value < 0.001), we hypothesize that the high reported resistance rate, characterized by the co-occurrence of tetracycline and chloramphenicol resistance, may reflect shared resistance mechanisms, such as proton motive force-dependent manner, multidrug extrusion transporters [55, 56], or co-selection of genetically linked resistance determinants [56]. This hypothesis is further supported by previous results showing that chloramphenicol resistance genes including catA, catB, cmlA, and floR, may be associated with mobile genetic elements or resistance plasmids and may co-occur with tetracycline resistance genes [56, 57]. Testing larger samples, resistance confirmation by genetic testing, and co-selection confirmation are critical for comprehensively evaluating our hypothesis.

In addition, our results showed a 61.5% (95% CI: 35.1–88.0%) intermediate resistance rate to orally administrated cefuroxime, another commonly prescribed antibiotic for treating multiple gram-positive and gram-negative bacterial infections, especially urinary tract infections (UTIs) in Palestine [28]. The CLSI states that intermediate resistance may represent a buffer zone and may result in lower antibiotic levels in the blood and tissues than in cases of susceptible isolates [31]. Although cefuroxime has a wide pharmacotoxicity margin, it has highly variable, food-dependent bioavailability, restricting its oral dose adjustment and administration in patients with isolates exhibiting reduced susceptibility [58]. The reported results raise concerns because patients consuming mint contaminated with such isolates may carry E.coli strains for which standard cefuroxime treatment may prove inadequate. This may contribute to treatment failure in community-acquired infections, especially in Palestine, where, despite huge efforts, irrational prescriptions and over-the-counter availability of antibiotics, along with the reliance on empirical therapy rather than precision therapy, are still evident [24, 59]. Cefuroxime resistance mechanisms in E.coli involve a complex interplay between efflux pumps overexpression, reduced outer membrane permeability, and upregulated AmpC β-lactamase activity [60].

Another critical concern is the detection of multidrug resistance in 23.1% (95% CI:8.2–50.1%) of the isolates based on the Magiorakos et al. [21] definition, while all of them tested negative for ESBL, AmpC β-lactamase, and carbapenemase expression. Collectively, these findings add another layer of concern, as these strains may act as reservoirs of transmissible resistance genes (38, 39) and may increase the risk of treatment failure during infections (38–40). Overall, our preliminary results are in alignment with a recently published study on the prevalence of MDR in clinically significant bacteria isolated from clinical samples in Palestine, which reported that 22.8% of non-ESBL-producing E.coli were MDR [24].

Palestinian Ministry of Health reports on antibiotic resistance provide further insights into the national resistance situation. In a report published in 2020, it was found that, in alignment with the global literature, E.coli is the major causative agent of urinary tract infections in Palestine, with the highest resistance rates against ampicillin (65%), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (37%), nalidixic acid (37%), and nitrofurantoin (29%). Whereas, low resistance levels were against amoxicillin/ clavulanic acid (12.2%), ciprofloxacin (17.2%), cefotaxime (11.1%), ceftriaxone (11.1%). This explains the common implementation of the latter antibiotics in the therapeutic plans of Palestinian patients [28]. Other studies conducted by the private sector add to the resistance prevalence estimates in Palestine including the previously mentioned levofloxacin and carbapenems resistance [27, 28]. The continuous transmission of bacteria carrying our reported phenotypic resistance indicates a potential risk of treatment failure of overall therapeutic classes rather than the particularly tested agent, even for isolates with intermediate resistance (38–40). For the animal sector, the resistance patterns for bacteria isolated from animal sources were as follows: tetracycline (100%), ampicillin (83.33%), amoxicillin (83.33%), kanamycin (80.3%), ciprofloxacin (72.72%), neomycin (69.70%), nitrofurantoin (18.18%), and cephalexin (12.12%) [25]. This further supports our assumption of the dominance of fecal contamination from animal-source sources.

Globally, research on antibiotic resistance in clinically significant bacteria isolated from mint is limited, and the results are usually incomparable because of the use of different antibiotic panels for sensitivity testing. One study conducted by Priyanka et al. [22] on mint collected from different market types in Bangladesh found that E.coli was exclusively isolated from local market samples. Over 90% of the isolates were MDR. However, they exhibited lower resistance rates towards tetracycline (39.13%) and chloramphenicol (34.78%) than our isolates [22].

E.coli isolates from other types of vegetables exhibited various MDR rates as well as resistance levels to tetracycline and chloramphenicol. Similar tetracycline resistance levels have been reported in isolates from various supermarket vegetables in Tunisia (~ 60%) [18], cabbage farms in Ghana (63.64%) [61], and vegetables throughout the supply chain in Lebanon (41.7%) [16]. Lower tetracycline resistance levels have been reported for vegetables from local markets and supermarkets in the UAE (20%) [19] and from retail markets in Ethiopia (23.9%) [17].

Comparable chloramphenicol resistance trends have been reported in E.coli isolates from lettuce (46.7%) and cabbage (63.64%) in Ghanaian farms [61] and in various vegetables (26.1%) collected from local Ethiopian markets [17]. In contrast, markedly higher resistance levels to tetracycline (90%), chloamphenicol (86.6%), and cefuroxime (93.3%) were found in E.coli isolates from lettuce farms in Ghana [62]. In terms of MDR rates, the results were as follows: 60%, in Lebanon [16]; >50%in Tunisia [18]; and 13.8%in the UAE [19].

All in all, our results provide the first preliminary data on the antibiotic resistance profiles of a clinically significant microbial reservoir in a frequently consumed edible plant in Hebron, Palestine. Nevertheless, these findings are subject to several limitations. First, the small sample size was caused by the inability to include the entire Hebron region owing to the challenging access to all regions. Second, the analysed samples were grown during the winter season, when the scarcity of water for the agricultural sector is less pronounced. This might have resulted in lower-than-usual levels of resistant E.coli strains, which may explain our lower MDR rates compared to those reported in other countries. Third, antibiotic susceptibility testing was limited to the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method, without molecular confirmation of resistance genes or confirmatory testing for putative heteroresistance. Furthermore, formal quality control strains were not employed during the susceptibility testing period, because of resource constraints and the nature of the study, which involved environmental isolates analysed under routine diagnostic laboratory conditions. Fourth, this study aimed to screen for antibiotic resistance in E.coli at the most common direct interface with consumers, that is, grocery markets. However, testing for fecal contamination and resistance in mint from farm to grocery markets will help identify the potential sources of the reported bacterial contamination. Genotyping of the isolates would also support the investigation of the origin of the isolates, whether human or animal.

Future work should include the following: (1)microbiological analysis of a larger number of mint samples from all over the city throughout its supply chain during the four seasons, as it will provide us with the source of contamination. (2)Performing antibiotic susceptibility testing using phenotypic and genetic methods against a more comprehensive antibiotic panel, along with including standard QC strains in the testing protocol. (3)This is along with the microbiological assessment of the irrigation water, soil, and organic fertilizers utilized during mint cultivation.

Conclusion

Our study is the first to document contamination by antibiotic-resistant and MDR strains of E.coli in locally cultivated mint collected from grocery stores in Hebron, Palestine. The reported heteroresistance to levofloxacin, tetracycline, and chloramphenicol, as well as non-susceptibility to cefuroxime, tetracycline, and chloramphenicol, provides insights into another important source of antibiotic resistance dissemination. Our preliminary results spot the light on the effects of the unwise and unfully regulated antibiotic consumption in the medical and veterinary sectors. Although limited by the small sample size and the phenotypic nature of the resistance results, the results enrich the local and regional data repository and should provide the basis for more comprehensive research, supported by the Palestinian Ministries of Health and Agriculture. Testing larger sample numbers from wider areas of Hebron across the four seasons would aid in building a comprehensive picture of the condition. Screening and subsequent risk assessment of the microbiological quality of plant-based foods are essential for formulating contemporary laws that support a One Health approach.
