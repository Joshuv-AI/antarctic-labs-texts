Association between olfactory function and metabolic syndrome in bipolar disorder patients: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Huiqian Yuan, Yingying Li, Xianlin Liu, Langjun Su, Qiping Li, Chunhong Yang, Chao Chen, Chunyang Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlfactory function is closely related to mood and the endocrine system. However, the role of olfactory function in bipolar disorder combined with metabolic syndrome remains unclear. The purpose of this study was to explore the associations among olfactory function, tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), and metabolic syndrome and its components in patients with bipolar disorder.MethodsNinety-six bipolar disorder patients were divided into two groups with and without metabolic syndrome. We also included 46 healthy controls. Olfactory function was assessed using the Sniffin’ Sticks test. Blood samples were collected to measure metabolic indicators and serum TNF-α levels.ResultsSignificant differences in olfactory function were found among the three groups. Compared with the healthy controls, the bipolar disorder without metabolic syndrome group showed poorer olfactory identification ability (P < 0.001) and the bipolar disorder with metabolic syndrome group showed impaired olfactory sensitivity (P = 0.003) and olfactory identification (P < 0.001). Moreover, the bipolar disorder with metabolic syndrome group had poorer olfactory identification ability than the bipolar disorder without metabolic syndrome group (P = 0.015). Both bipolar disorder groups showed lower TNF-α levels than healthy controls. However, there was no significant difference between the two patient groups. Correlation analysis showed that, in the bipolar disorder with metabolic syndrome group, olfactory identification was negatively correlated with systolic blood pressure (r = − 0.424, P = 0.031), and serum TNF-α level was negatively correlated with body mass index (BMI; r = − 0.398, P = 0.049), triglyceride (r = − 0.503, P = 0.010), total cholesterol (r = − 0.491, P = 0.013), low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (r = − 0.491, P = 0.013), and high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (r = − 0.454, P = 0.023).ConclusionsThe olfactory identification ability of patients with bipolar disorder is worse than that of healthy controls, and the occurrence of metabolic syndrome will further aggravate the olfactory identification impairment of those patients. Furthermore, there may be a stronger link between serum TNF-α level and multiple metabolic indicators in bipolar disorder patients with metabolic syndrome than in bipolar disorder patients without metabolic syndrome.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-024-06164-6.Keywords: Bipolar disorder, Metabolic syndrome, Olfactory function, Tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α)

Introduction

Bipolar disorder (BD)and metabolic syndrome (MS)

BD is a serious mental illness that is characterized by alternating or mixed episodes of mania and depression and has high prevalence, disability, and mortality rates. The average life expectancy of BD patients is 15–20 years less than that of the general population; moreover, in BD patients, cardiovascular disease is a common cause of death and also increases the risk of death [1]. MS is a set of metabolically related risk factors, including abdominal obesity, abnormal blood glucose, dyslipidemia, and hypertension, which directly contribute to the development of cardiovascular disease. Various psychiatric disorders, including major depressive disorder, BD, schizophrenia, anxiety disorder, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, and post-traumatic stress disorder, can increase the risk of MS [2]. Studies have shown that the prevalence of MS in BD patients is 37.3%, almost twice that of the general population [3]. The high comorbidity of both is related to the common pathophysiological mechanism, including alterations in neural pathways in the brain, dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, and chronic inflammatory responses [4].

BD and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α)

TNF-α, as one of the most important pro-inflammatory cytokines, also plays an important role in the pathological mechanism of BD. Evidence shows that there are changes in a variety of inflammatory factors in BD patients, especially in acute mood episodes (depressive episodes, manic episodes and mixed states), the levels of Interleukin-6 (IL-6) and TNF-α are significantly increased in BD patients, but only the levels of IL-6 are significantly increased in bipolar remission, and the levels of TNF-α in bipolar remission are not significantly different from the control group [5, 6]. This suggests that TNF-α may serve as a marker for mood episodes in patients with BD.

BD and olfaction

Smell can regulate emotions, behavior, and interpersonal relationships, which may be related to the transmission of neuronal signals produced by odors to the higher cortical areas and limbic system involved in mood and cognition [7]. A growing number of studies have found that olfactory function impairment is common in BD patients, involving olfactory sensitivity (OS) and olfactory identification (OI). According to a study by Li et al., OI impairment exists in BD patients regardless of whether they are experiencing a manic episode, depressive episode, or bipolar remission period, but OS impairment is only observed in the acute onset period of BD patients [8]. It is suggested that OI may be a trait marker of BD, and OS may be a state marker of BD. Kazour et al. conducted two studies on olfactory function in bipolar depression patients and bipolar mania patients respectively, and found that compared with the healthy control group, there was no significant difference in OI between the bipolar depressive episode and the remission phase, and there were defects in OI between the bipolar manic episode group and the bipolar remission group. This suggests that OI in BD may be related to the type of mood episodes [9, 10].

Metabolism and olfaction

It is currently widely believed that olfaction plays an important role in triggering appetite and the control of food intake, so changes in olfactory function may affect eating behavior, which in turn leads to weight gain. On the other hand, it has been found that olfaction is regulated by the changes in various hormones and cytokines in the body [11], and is also affected by MS and obesity [12]. Human studies have also revealed that the olfactory system is closely related to the endocrine system, and chronic metabolic disorders can lead to changes in olfactory function via protein expression alterations in olfactory-related regions [11]. In obese people, the volume of olfactory bulbs is generally reduced and the olfactory function is decreased [13], which may be caused by metabolic changes and obesity-related hormonal [14].

Metabolism and TNF-α

Obesity and metabolic disorders are very common in patients with BD and MS, and chronic inflammation is a common state. Lamers et al. reported that in patients with depressive episodes, increased appetite was positively correlated with the number of MS components and TNF-α levels, and increased appetite may be a key linking depressive symptoms, MS, and inflammatory markers [15], and the role of smell behind it deserves further discussion. Whether there is a relationship between inflammatory factors and olfaction in this population will be further explored in this study.

BD combined with MS can result in poorer clinical outcomes, longer disease duration, poorer overall function, poorer treatment response, and more rapid cycling of episodes than BD without MS [16]. Therefore, MS should be monitored in BD patients to assess physical burden and long-term prognosis. To date, no comprehensive studies have focused on MS, olfactory function, and cytokines in BD patients. Therefore, from the perspective of olfactory function and inflammatory factors in BD, our study aimed to identify new indicators for the early recognition and screening of BD patients with MS. In addition, we aimed to further explore the relationships among MS, the olfactory system, and immune inflammation in BD patients to provide empirical support for the pathophysiological mechanism hypothesis of BD.

Methods

Participants

Patients were recruited from all eligible BD patients who had been admitted to the Psychiatric Department of Shunde WuZhongpei Memorial Hospital, Foshan City, from August 1 to August 31, 2022. In total, 96 patients were enrolled and divided into either the MS (n = 26) or non-MS (NMS; n = 70) group according to whether they met the diagnostic criteria for MS at the time of enrollment. The healthy control (HC) group (n = 46) comprised healthy hospital staff matched for sociodemographic characteristics. Possible influencing factors, including sex, age, education level and smoking status, which may affect the participants’ olfactory function, were considered and matched among the three groups.

The inclusion criteria for the BD groups were as follows: met the US Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, diagnostic criteria for BD; aged 18–60 years; an educational level of primary school education or above; Han Chinese.

For the diagnosis of MS, we used the definitions of the 2017 Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Type 2 Diabetes for the following three factors: abdominal obesity (i.e., central obesity), which was defined as a waist circumference of ≥ 90 cm for men and ≥ 85 cm for women; hyperglycemia, which was defined as fasting plasma glucose (FPG) of ≥ 6.1 mmol/L or blood glucose of ≥ 7.8 mmol/L 2 h after glucose load and/or undergoing treatment for diabetes; hypertension, which was defined as a blood pressure of ≥ 130/85 mmHg and/or undergoing treatment for hypertension; fasting triglycerides (TG) of ≥ 1.70 mmol/L; fasting high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-C) of < 1.04 mmol/L [17].

The inclusion criteria for the HC group were good health, no mental illness, a Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAMD)-24 score of ≤ 7 [18], a Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) score of ≤ 7 [19], aged 18–60 years, educational level of primary school or above, and Han Chinese.

Participants were excluded if they had a history of head and neck trauma or nasal surgery, a history of neurological disorders (e.g., epilepsy and stroke), mental retardation, any medical diseases that could affect olfactory function (e.g., nasal polyps, common cold, allergies, obesity and thyroid disease), a history of alcohol or drug abuse or dependence in the past year, or if they were unable to complete the olfactory examination.

This study was carried out in accordance with the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki) for experiments involving humans and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Shunde WuZhongpei Memorial Hospital, Foshan City (the approval number: 202211). Participation was voluntary, and all participants or family members of the patients provided written informed consent.

Study assessments

This study was a cross-sectional survey conducted between August 1 and August 31, 2022. During this period, all eligible BD patients were admitted to the Psychiatric Department of Shunde WuZhongpei Memorial Hospital, Foshan City, and were examined individually. We collected demographic (i.e., sex, age, educational level, and smoking status), clinical diagnosis, age of onset, disease course, and medication information of the patient group.

Symptoms of the current depressive episode were assessed using the HAMD-24, current manic symptoms were assessed using the YMRS, current anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Hamilton Rating Scale for Anxiety (HAMA) [20], and social function was assessed using the Global Assessment Function (GAF) [21].

Olfactory function was assessed using the Sniffin’ Sticks test, which consists of OS and OI tests. OS and OI involve the peripheral and central mechanisms of olfaction, respectively. OS represents the ability to smell the lowest concentration of odors, and OI represents the ability to identify specific odors, with a focus on the extraction of previously perceived odor memories. The OS test consists of 48 olfactory sticks, divided into groups of three, for a total of 16 groups. Each group contains two blank odorless sticks and one olfactory stick with n-butanol. After familiarization with the smell of the olfactory stick, participants were exposed to the olfactory stick with the lowest concentration. If they provided an incorrect answer, they were then exposed to the next higher concentration. If the correct answer was provided twice for a given concentration, participants were exposed to the next lower concentration using the “step procedure method.” After switching between concentrations seven times, the average of the last four step-reversal points was recorded as the olfactory threshold score. A higher OS score indicated greater OS. The OI test consists of 16 sticks with different scents. For each scent, participants selected one answer from four options, and one point was awarded for every correctly identified smell, for a maximum score of 16. A higher OI score indicated greater OI ability [22].

Serum TNF-α level measurement: In the enrolled participants, 5 ml of venous blood was drawn on an empty stomach in the morning, which was naturally coagulated at room temperature for 10–20 min and centrifuged for about 20 min (2000–3000 revolutions per minute). Carefully collect the supernatant and store it in the refrigerator at -40℃ for later use. We used a double antibody sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. The kit was produced by Shanghai Renjie Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm wavelength by MultiskanMK3, and TNF-α level was measured by standard curve.

For the MS-related indicators, we used a standard measuring tape to assess height, a corrected electronic scale to measure weight, and an electronic sphygmomanometer that met international standards to assess blood pressure. Blood samples were analyzed using fully automated biochemical instruments to determine FPG, total cholesterol (TC), TG, low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-C), and HDL-C.

Statistics

Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS25.0 statistical software. Data conforming to a normal distribution are expressed as means and standard deviations. Comparisons between two groups were performed using an independent samples t-test, and comparisons among multiple groups were performed using a one-way analysis of variance. Non-normally distributed data are expressed as medians (lower quartiles and upper quartiles). Comparisons between two groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test, and comparisons among multiple groups were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted if significant differences were found. P-values were corrected using the Bonferroni method. Count data are expressed as frequencies, groups were compared using the chi-square test. Pearson (for normally distributed data) and Spearman (for non-normally distributed data) correlation analyses were used to examine correlations, and an α of 0.05 was used for two-sided tests. P < 0.05 indicated a statistical significance.

Results

Comparisons of demographic data and clinical features among the three groups

There were no significant differences in age, sex, smoking, or years of education among the three groups (P > 0.05). The median disease duration of the MS group was 17.5 years, which was significantly higher than that of the NMS group (P = 0.021). There was no significant difference in the age of initial onset between the MS and NMS groups (P > 0.05). Compared with the NMS group, the MS group had higher body weight, waist circumference, BMI, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, FPG, and TG, but lower HDL-C levels (P < 0.05; Table 1).

Discussion

Changes in olfactory function in BD patients

The objective of this study was to explore the associations between olfactory function and MS in BD patients. We found that BD patients had poorer OI ability than HC group; furthermore, BD patients with MS had poorer OI ability than those without MS. This finding is similar to the results of a study on olfaction conducted in a large population in Korea, which found that olfactory dysfunction is associated with MS in Korean women [23]. Notably, antihypertensive drugs, which are commonly taken by BD patients with concomitant MS, can cause olfactory dysfunction [24]. This may have contributed to the poorer olfactory function of the MS group. Jovanovic et al. proposed a bi-directional relationship between olfaction and metabolism, where on the one hand, olfactory information exchange is involved in the regulation of food intake, energy expenditure, and fat metabolism, while on the other hand, chronic neurodegenerative diseases, aging, and high-calorie diets can result in a progressive loss of olfactory function [25]. Recently, it was proposed that BD is a chronic neurodegenerative disease. Zovetti et al. conducted a neuroimaging study on the neurodegenerative process of BD and found that BD is associated with age-related structural gray matter volume reduction in areas such as the amygdala [26], yet these regions highly overlap with regions that regulate olfactory function. In summary, BD, MS and olfactory function are related to each other. We also found that the OI score of BD patients was lower than that of HC group, regardless of the presence of MS. This indicated that OI ability was impaired in our BD patients, which is consistent with previous studies [8, 9, 27]. Taken together, our results suggest that OI dysfunction could serve as an early biological indicator of BD, and that the presence of MS was related to more severe OI dysfunction in BD patients.

Factors influencing olfactory function in BD patient

The correlations between olfactory function and demographic and clinical data in BD patients showed that in patients without MS, OS was positively correlated with years of education, and OI was negatively correlated with age. OI is a semantic memory task that refers to an individual’s general knowledge or experience of a particular odor [28]. Therefore, OI is thought to be linked to age-related OI deficits. Seo et al. conducted a study on olfactory function in 127 older adults aged 65–89 years and found that older and less educated people have poorer olfactory function and cognitive performance. Moreover, the study found that deficits in olfactory function in older adults were associated with declines in cognitive function [29], which is strong evidence that the link between olfactory function and cognitive function is associated with age.

Our exploration of the correlations between olfactory function and clinical features and social functioning in BD patients showed that OS and the GAF score were positively correlated in BD patients without MS; that is, patients with better social functioning had higher OS than those with poorer social functioning. Studies have shown that sense of smell plays a key role in social communication. The associations, emotions, and plasticity of smell facilitate the gathering and transmission of social information in society, and are thus, closely related to human evolution [30]. Indeed, Hardy et al. found that high OS was related to higher levels of employment and social functioning [31], which is consistent with our findings.

In regard to the correlation between olfactory function and metabolic indicators in patients with BD, we revealed that OI was inversely correlated with systolic blood pressure in BD patients with MS; that is, patients with poorer OI ability had higher systolic blood pressure. Gouveri et al. reported that patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and hypertension have significantly lower OS and OI ability than patients with type 2 diabetes but no hypertension and proposed that both type 2 diabetes and hypertension are associated with olfactory dysfunction [32]. Similarly, Catamo et al. reported that 65% of patients with type 2 diabetes and hypertension had OI impairment compared with only 18% of type 2 diabetes patients without hypertension [33]. The results of these studies are consistent with our findings. In addition, a longitudinal study of 5190 Chinese adults with normal baseline blood pressure found significantly elevated systolic blood pressure in those with an altered sense of smell and taste at the 2-year follow-up [34]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the effects of olfactory function in BD patients are multifaceted and that olfactory function may play an important role in metabolic pathways.

Changes in serum TNF-α levels in BD patients

Increasing evidence shows the involvement of neuroinflammation and immune dysfunction in major psychiatric disorders, such as schizophrenia, BD, and major depression [35]. Although the causal relationship between the pathological mechanism underlying BD and the immune inflammatory process is unclear, several studies have reported patients with BD have a higher prevalence of comorbid immunoinflammatory diseases (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus, autoimmune thyroiditis, rheumatoid arthritis, obesity, atherosclerosis, and type 2 diabetes) [36]. This result suggests that immune inflammation not only occurs as an incidental phenomenon in the pathophysiological process of BD but also has a bi-directional relationship with BD. Immune dysfunction can also lead to hypercortisolemia and metabolic dysfunction due to the activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis. Additionally, hypercortisolemia has a variety of effects on endocrine and metabolic systems, including weight gain, insulin resistance, and hypothyroidism, all of which are strongly associated with the development of BD and MS [37, 38]. We found that serum TNF-α levels were lower in BD patients than in HC group, regardless of the presence of MS. However, this finding is inconsistent with most recent studies, which have shown that serum TNF-α levels are significantly higher during manic, depressive, or mixed states in patients with BD than in HC group [39, 40]. A meta-analysis reported similar results although it found no elevated serum TNF-α levels in BD patients who had achieved remission [6]. In our study, serum TNF-α levels of BD patients were lower than those of HC group, and the specific reasons may be complicated, which may be partly related to the widespread use and inflammation-reversing effects of Medications (including mood stabilizers, antipsychotics, etc.), and may also be partly related to the disease stage of the enrolled BD patients. Studies have shown that lithium, as one of the most commonly used mood stabilizers, has anti-inflammatory effects. Fernandes et al. used olanzapine to activate macrophages in vitro and observed that macrophage proliferation and TNF-α expression levels were significantly increased 72 h later. However, after continued exposure to lithium salt, they noted a decrease in the TNF-α expression level in macrophages and an increase in the interleukin-10 expression level [41]. This suggests that lithium salt partially reverses the inflammatory response via the interaction between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory factors. In addition, serum TNF-α is not necessarily elevated in euthymic BD patients. Previous studies have shown that TNF-α also plays an important role in neuroprotection and repair [42] and that TNF-α levels significantly increase in BD patients during the acute phase of a manic or depressive episode but decrease as symptoms improve and over time. According to the scale scores in our study, quite a few BD patients were in remission or close to remission, which may partly explain the inconsistency between the results of serum TNF-α in our study and those in previous studies.

Factors influencing serum TNF-α level in BD patients

To date, the pathological mechanism underlying the relationship between MS and BD has not been elucidated. Nevertheless, the mechanism is likely related primarily to genetics, psychotropic drugs, depressive symptoms, insulin resistance, abnormal lipid metabolism, inflammatory response, and stress, among which inflammation in BD and MS has been studied extensively. However, few studies have included BD patients with MS to explore the relationship between serum TNF-α levels and metabolic indicators. We revealed that in BD patients with MS, serum TNF-α level was negatively correlated with BMI and TC, TG, LDL-C, and HDL-C levels. Although we established that serum TNF-α level of BD patients with MS is associated with numerous metabolic markers, determining a causal relationship requires longitudinal studies. Evidence has suggested that local inflammation of the olfactory epithelium is associated with olfactory dysfunction, although it remains unclear whether it is acute or chronic systemic inflammation that causes olfactory dysfunction. Tognetti et al. found that in inflammatory diseases, chronic systemic inflammation may be associated with olfactory function [43]. Indeed, a systemic chronic low-grade inflammatory response is a cross-diagnostic feature of BD and MS. However, we observed a positive correlation between serum TNF-α level and OI ability in BD patients without MS only, which may be because the pathophysiological mechanisms of olfactory function and serum TNF-α are complex and have numerous influencing factors. Moreover, these mechanisms may be independent of each other, and the relationship between the two may not be linear.

Our findings of the relationship between BD and MS proposed OI ability as an early predictor of MS in patients with BD and provided a new perspective for early diagnosis and intervention of BD with MS. However, our study has several limitations. The number of patients in the MS group was small and was not balanced with the sample size of the other two groups. We plan to conduct larger and more rigorously designed studies in the future to confirm the correlations among olfactory function, MS, and the pathogenesis of BD. The Sniffin’ Sticks test used in this study is a subjective olfactory detection method that relies primarily on subjects’ self-reported olfactory function, which inevitably results in some variation in performance. In the future, the olfactory function of BD patients could be studied by combining electrophysiological or neuroimaging methods. Additionally, we only assessed serum TNF-α as an inflammatory indicator, which can be affected by physical diseases, drug use, and other inflammatory indicators. In the future, we plan to conduct large-scale prospective studies on pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines in BD patients with follow-up assessments to further explore changes in serum cytokines in BD patients with MS.

Conclusion

In this study, we found that BD patients have poorer OI than HC group. The presence of MS was related to more severe OI dysfunction in BD patients. In addition, serum TNF-α level was associated with multiple metabolic indicators in exclusively BD patients with MS, although no linear relationship with olfactory function was observed. Further studies using larger sample sizes and a more rigorous study design are needed to verify this association.
