How coastal resilience emerges
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As climate change accelerates and pressures on coastal systems intensify, understanding coastal resilience is becoming as important as understanding the hazards themselves.

Few environments are shaped by the interaction of natural and human influences as profoundly as coastal zones. They are highly sensitive to sea level rise, high-energy storms, and erosional processes that alter sediment supply and coastal dynamics. At the same time, more than 40% of the global population lives within 100 km of the coast, a proportion that continues to grow1. Many coastlines are therefore shaped not only by climatic forcing, but also by land-use change, urbanisation, and infrastructure development. Together, these interacting pressures are increasing the risk of flooding, erosion, habitat loss, and damage to infrastructure in coastal regions where people, economic activity, and ecosystems are densely concentrated.

In response, coastal resilience has become an increasingly important scientific and societal objective. Broadly defined, resilience refers to the capacity of coastal systems, including physical landscapes, ecosystems, and human communities, to absorb disturbances, adapt to changing conditions, and recover from impacts while maintaining essential functions. Yet resilience is not a fixed property of coastal systems. Instead, it is continually shaped by interactions between physical processes, ecosystems, and human activities. Coastal hazards do not act in isolation. The impacts they produce depend on interactions among environmental change, landscape dynamics, ecosystems, and human activities. In many densely populated regions, for example, land subsidence can accelerate relative sea-level rise and amplify flood risk, increasing exposure and reducing the time available for adaptation2. Human activities such as dredging, land reclamation, and coastal development can further alter coastal processes, influencing how shorelines respond to changing environmental conditions. Together, these interacting drivers help explain why similar hazards can produce very different outcomes across coastal settings.

Because resilience emerges from these interactions, there is no single pathway to achieving it. Responses range from hard-engineering interventions to nature-based and hybrid approaches, each offering advantages and limitations depending on local environmental and societal conditions. Coastal ecosystems are a particularly important component of resilience because they can reduce hazard impacts while supporting biodiversity and other ecosystem services3. Increasingly, combinations of natural and engineered approaches, supported by adaptive planning and risk-informed governance, are being explored as more flexible strategies for managing coastal risk, with growing evidence that hybrid approaches can deliver both adaptation and environmental benefits4.

However, resilience is neither static nor guaranteed. The effectiveness of resilience-building approaches depends not only on their design and implementation, but also on the capacity of the physical and ecological systems on which they rely to continue functioning as environmental conditions change. Coastal ecosystems provide a clear example. While they play a critical role in buffering hazards and sustaining livelihoods, they are themselves vulnerable to sea level rise, extreme events, and human disturbance. Recent evidence suggests that in some regions rates of ecosystem degradation are accelerating, with extreme events increasingly contributing to habitat loss5. At the same time, long-term assessments from other coastal settings reveal substantial losses of ecosystems that contribute to coastal protection, driven by development, land-use change, and erosion6. Together, these findings highlight that the natural systems sustaining coastal resilience are themselves being reshaped by the same environmental and societal pressures from which they are expected to provide protection.

The temporal dimension of coastal change further complicates resilience planning. Decisions taken today on carbon emissions, land use, and infrastructure commit coastlines to trajectories of risk that may extend beyond conventional planning horizons. While projections of coastal flooding often show comparable levels of exposure by the end of this century across a range of scenarios, divergence becomes much greater over longer timescales, particularly under continued warming and potential ice-sheet instability7. In some settings, the long-term challenge may extend beyond maintaining existing flood protection. As sea levels continue to rise, adaptation may increasingly require combinations of protection, accommodation, and managed retreat, particularly where physical limits to adaptation are approached7.

At Nature Communications, we present a cross-journal collection of studies that, taken together, suggest that coastal resilience cannot be understood as a fixed property that can simply be strengthened through individual interventions. Instead, resilience emerges from interactions between physical processes, ecosystems, and human activities operating across scales, making it more dynamic and, in some cases, more fragile than is often assumed. Climate change is accelerating these interactions and reshaping the conditions that enable both natural and human systems to adapt. Understanding coastal resilience therefore requires moving beyond individual hazards or adaptation measures to consider the coupled processes that shape coastal futures.

The scientific basis for prioritising coastal resilience is well established. What has changed is the pace, scale, and persistence of the pressures now reshaping coastal systems. Understanding how resilience can be maintained, enhanced, or reconfigured under these conditions is essential for managing future coastal risk. Addressing this challenge will require integrated approaches that bridge disciplines and scales, and that consider not only how to protect coastlines, but also how to navigate inevitable change in a way that sustains both human and natural systems.
